Regression analysis studies how the mean of a response variable depends on one or more explanatory variables. The name goes back to Galton's observation of regression toward the mean, and its mathematical starting point is the **conditional expectation** of a **bivariate normal distribution**; in practice, however, the explanatory variables are usually fixed by the design or simply observed, and the question becomes how to model, estimate, test and use a regression function from data. The linear model answers it with a mean that is linear in the parameters and with random errors around it. Three questions run through the whole subject: how to estimate the parameters and judge their precision, how to check whether the assumptions behind every $p$-value and interval actually hold, and what to do when they fail. This subject corresponds to the course Linear Regression Analysis.

The chapters follow one main line. After a prelude on the history of regression and on what precedes any analysis (Chapter 1), Chapters 2–4 develop the **simple linear regression model** with one predictor: estimation by **least squares** and by maximum likelihood; inference on the slope and the intercept with the **Gauss–Markov theorem**, joint inference and power; and the use of the fitted line through confidence and prediction intervals, together with the **analysis of variance** and the **general linear test**, which compares a full and a reduced model. Chapters 5 and 6 turn to **regression diagnostics** and **remedial measures**: graphical and formal checks of linearity, constant variance, normality and independence based on residuals, the distinction between outliers, leverage and influence, and transformations such as Box–Cox. Chapter 7 rewrites everything in matrix form, with a **design matrix**, least squares as an **orthogonal projection** and sums of squares as **quadratic forms**, and thereby passes to the **multiple linear regression model**; Chapter 8 works through a complete example and states the distribution theory of the normal linear model. Chapter 9 introduces **extra sums of squares**, which turn the general linear test into a test of any group of coefficients, together with partial correlation and standardized regression. Chapter 10 traces the difficulties of multiple regression to **multicollinearity** and extends the model by **polynomial regression** and interaction terms; Chapter 11 treats model selection by criteria such as Mallows' $C_p$, AIC, BIC and PRESS, and diagnostics for several predictors. Chapter 12 completes the remedies (**weighted least squares**, ridge regression and the Lasso, robust and nonparametric regression, the **bootstrap**) and begins single-factor analysis of variance with the **cell means model** and the **factor effects model**; Chapter 13 writes one-way ANOVA as a regression and adds multiple comparisons and contrasts; Chapter 14 ends with two-way ANOVA, where the **interaction effect** of two factors appears, and with diagnostics and remedies for ANOVA models.

This subject applies the normal-theory inference of [statistical inference](/3.2) (sampling distributions, the $t$- and $F$-tests, confidence intervals) to one model throughout, and its matrix form rests on the projections, ranks and quadratic forms of [linear algebra](/2.1). [Multivariate statistical analysis](/3.4) revisits the same linear model in matrix form, with regularization and principal component regression, while the computations and the bootstrap reappear in [statistical computing and software](/3.5), and the rank-based alternatives to the $F$-test in [nonparametric statistics](/3.6). The analysis of variance at the end of the course is the starting point of the [design and analysis of experiments](/3.8), which asks how data should be collected so that these models can answer the question; and the warning that a regression coefficient measures association conditional on the other variables, not an effect, as Simpson's paradox shows, leads to [causal inference](/3.9).
