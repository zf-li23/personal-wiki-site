Questions of cause and effect guide decisions: whether a drug helps a group of patients, whether a policy improved the air of a city. Each asks what would happen if we did one thing rather than another. Association answers a different question, since it lets us predict one variable from another, and changing a variable need not change the variables it predicts. Causal inference is the part of statistics that makes the step from data to statements about the consequences of actions precise. It works in two frameworks. The potential outcomes framework of Neyman and Rubin starts from a given treatment and defines its effect on a unit as a comparison of **potential outcomes**; since only one of them is ever observed, which is the **fundamental problem of causal inference**, causal inference becomes a missing-data problem, and what can be learned depends on the **assignment mechanism** that decided who was treated. The graphical framework of Pearl starts from a system of variables, represents its causal structure by a **directed acyclic graph**, and asks which effects of an **intervention** can be computed from data on the system observed without intervention. This subject corresponds to the course Introduction to Causal Inference.

The first chapter introduces units, treatments, potential outcomes, the **stable unit treatment value assumption** and causal estimands, and Chapter 2 makes the assignment mechanism a mathematical object and classifies designs by it. Chapters 3 to 7 study the **randomized experiment**, where the mechanism is known because the researcher chose it, through four approaches of increasing reliance on models. **Fisher's exact p-value** tests the sharp null hypothesis of no effect on any unit, using only the randomization. Neyman's difference in means estimates the **average treatment effect** without bias, with a conservative variance estimate. Regression on the treatment indicator and covariates acquires a causal meaning from the randomization even when the linear model is false. Model-based imputation treats the missing outcomes as unknowns in a Bayesian model and handles any estimand in the same way. Chapter 7 adapts all four to the **stratified randomized experiment** and the **paired randomized experiment**. Chapters 8 to 10 turn to the **observational study**, under the assumption of **unconfoundedness**. Its key tool is the **propensity score**, the coarsest function of the covariates that balances them between the groups. The study is designed before any outcome is seen, by assessing balance and overlap, trimming, blocking and **matching**, so that it imitates a stratified or paired experiment; only then are blocking, weighting and matching estimators applied to the outcomes. Chapters 11 to 13 build Pearl's framework: **Bayesian networks** and **d-separation**, the **causal Bayesian network** with its notion of intervention, and the **ladder of causation** from association through intervention to counterfactuals; the **backdoor criterion**, the **front-door criterion** and the **do-calculus** decide when an interventional distribution is identified from observational data; and **structure learning** estimates the graph itself, which the data determine only up to **Markov equivalence**. Chapter 14 brings the two frameworks together for noncompliance: when a confounder is unobserved, an **instrumental variable** still identifies the **complier average causal effect**. Chapter 15 reviews the course and introduces newer directions, among them **rerandomization**, **negative controls**, **principal stratification**, **mediation analysis**, the **surrogate paradox** and the connections with machine learning.

The ideas grew out of the [design of experiments](/3.8), where **randomization** was first made the basis of inference, and the stratified and paired experiments are the causal counterparts of its blocked and paired designs. Fisher's exact p-value is a **permutation test** of [nonparametric statistics](/3.6) justified by the actual randomization; the regression results rely on least squares and the robust variance estimator of [linear regression analysis](/3.3); the model-based approach applies [Bayesian statistics](/3.7); and conditional independence, on which d-separation rests, comes from [probability theory](/3.1). Bayesian networks originated in [artificial intelligence](/4.5.1), and the learning of causal structure meets [machine learning](/4.6) and the inference of [gene regulatory networks](/7.9.11). In biomedical research, **confounding**, observational designs and clinical trials are treated from the applied side in [biostatistics](/7.7.15).

I regard this as a very advanced course, because it asks a question that the other courses deliberately leave aside. Their methods describe distributions and the associations within them; causal inference asks what would happen under a change that was never observed, and so it has to say precisely which assumptions connect the data to that question. It draws on almost everything that comes before it, experimental design, permutation tests, regression, Bayesian inference and graph theory, and it adds a new layer on top: before choosing an estimator, one has to state the estimand, the assignment mechanism and the assumptions that make the estimand identifiable. What I find most attractive is that this layer is a matter of logic rather than of technique. Several apparent paradoxes, such as Lord's paradox and **Simpson's paradox**, dissolve once the causal question is written down explicitly, and the two frameworks, developed in different communities with different languages, turn out to express the same conditions, unconfoundedness on one side and the backdoor criterion on the other. For anyone who wants to use data to support decisions rather than only to make predictions, this is the theory that says when that is possible.
