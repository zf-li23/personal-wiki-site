On paper a statistical method is a formula: an estimator, a likelihood to be maximized, an integral that defines a posterior expectation. On a computer it becomes an algorithm that works with finitely many bits, finite memory and finite time. Statistical computing studies this passage from formula to algorithm for the basic mathematical problems on which every statistical method rests: how numbers are stored and how arithmetic on them goes wrong; how linear systems, least squares problems and eigenvalue problems are actually solved; how a likelihood or another objective function is optimized by iteration; and how a deterministic machine produces random numbers and uses them to approximate distributions and integrals that cannot be computed analytically. Two criteria run through all of these problems. One is efficiency, measured by operation counts, by the order and rate of convergence of an iteration, or by the variance obtained per unit of computing time. The other is error: rounding error and its propagation, the conditioning of the problem and the stability of the algorithm, the gap between an iterate and the solution, and the Monte Carlo error of a simulation. This subject corresponds to the course Statistical Computing and Software, in which all computations are carried out in R.

The chapters move from the deterministic to the stochastic. Chapter 1 starts at the lowest level, with **floating-point numbers** in the IEEE 754 standard, **machine epsilon** and rounding errors, and introduces the two questions that every later chapter asks again: is the problem well-conditioned, and is the algorithm backward stable? Chapter 2 describes R as a tool for computing, with the emphasis on how it represents data and on **vectorization**. Chapter 3 revisits the factorizations of linear algebra as algorithms, from Gaussian elimination with pivoting and the Cholesky factorization through three ways of computing a **QR decomposition** to the **singular value decomposition** and the **power method**, counting the flops of each and following the rounding errors through it. Chapters 4 and 5 treat numerical optimization, mostly in the service of **maximum likelihood estimation**: Chapter 4 sets up the framework of iterative methods and covers methods that need few or no derivatives (golden-section search, the **Nelder–Mead method**, bisection, fixed-point and coordinate iterations, **Brent's method**), and Chapter 5 covers methods that exploit derivatives, namely **Newton's method** and its statistical form **Fisher scoring**, **quasi-Newton methods**, **gradient descent** and the **conjugate gradient method**. Chapter 6 develops the **EM algorithm**, which maximizes a likelihood with latent variables by a sequence of easy problems. The last three chapters turn to simulation: the **bootstrap** (Chapter 7) quantifies the uncertainty of an estimator by resampling the data; Chapter 8 shows how uniform pseudorandom numbers are generated and transformed into draws from other distributions by the inverse transform, **rejection sampling** and transformations; and Chapter 9 uses these draws for Monte Carlo integration and **importance sampling**, and then combines them with Markov chains into **Markov chain Monte Carlo**, with the **Metropolis–Hastings algorithm** and the **Gibbs sampler**.

The course draws on almost every earlier part of this section. The theory of the matrix factorizations comes from [linear algebra](/2.1), and Taylor expansions from [calculus](/2.2) underlie the analysis of conditioning and of Newton-type methods. The likelihoods being maximized are those of [statistical inference](/3.2), Fisher scoring for generalized linear models is the iteratively reweighted least squares of [linear regression analysis](/3.3), and the EM algorithm for Gaussian mixtures was first met in [multivariate statistical analysis](/3.4); the **Monte Carlo method** and the inverse transform were introduced in [probability theory](/3.1). In the other direction, resampling methods continue in [nonparametric statistics](/3.6), and the MCMC samplers and variational methods are the computational core of [Bayesian statistics](/3.7). On the side of computer science, the floating-point formats are part of [computer organization](/4.9), operation counts are the complexity analysis of [data structures and algorithms](/4.3), and gradient descent, stochastic gradients and the EM algorithm are basic tools of [machine learning](/4.7) and [artificial neural networks](/4.8).

This course is hard. It is the only four-credit course in the statistics minor, and following the lectures completely really requires a background in linear algebra at the level of Selected Topics in Advanced Linear Algebra or beyond, the material of the later chapters of [linear algebra](/2.1). But it is also a superb course. It takes apart the basic mathematical and computational problems (numbers, linear algebra, optimization, random numbers and stochastic simulation) and shows how each of them actually happens inside a computer, and the measurement of efficiency and error runs through it from the first chapter to the last. Formulas that look finished in other courses turn out to hide real choices: which factorization to use, when an iteration can be trusted, how many simulated draws are enough. For students who will develop computational tools themselves, rather than only call them, this is exactly the knowledge that is needed.

The lecture slides form a system of their own. Instead of following one textbook, they integrate the parts of several good textbooks that the instructor considers the best, and the result is broader than any single one of them. The textbooks listed above are therefore useful mainly for checking details and for further reading, not as a substitute for the course.
