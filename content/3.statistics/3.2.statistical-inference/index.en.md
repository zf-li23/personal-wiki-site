Statistical inference reasons from a sample to the population that produced it. Probability theory ([3.1](/3.1)) starts from a completely specified model and deduces how data behave; inference starts from the data, assumes only that the distribution belongs to some family, and asks what can be said about the unknown parameter. Because the sample is random, every conclusion is uncertain, and the central task of the subject is to quantify that uncertainty: by the bias, variance and mean squared error of an estimator, by the confidence level of an interval, by the significance level and power of a test, or, in the Bayesian approach, by the posterior distribution itself. This subject corresponds to the course Statistical Inference and also covers the statistics part of Probability and Mathematical Statistics.

The chapters follow one main line. Chapter 1 fixes the vocabulary: population and sample, the simple random sample, the statistical model as a family of distributions indexed by a parameter, the **statistic**, and the **empirical distribution function**. Chapter 2 develops the tools used in the rest of the course: the **sampling distributions** of normal samples, where the sample mean and the sample variance are independent and lead to the $\chi^2$, $t$ and $F$ distributions; **exponential families**; and **sufficient statistics** and complete statistics, which reduce the data without losing information about the parameter. Chapters 3–5 are the three classical types of inference. Point estimation (Chapter 3) sets criteria for comparing estimators (unbiasedness, efficiency, mean squared error, consistency and asymptotic normality), constructs estimators by the method of moments and by **maximum likelihood estimation**, and finds the uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator; it ends with regression, kernel density estimation and sparse estimation in high dimensions. Interval estimation (Chapter 4) constructs a **confidence interval** mainly by the pivotal quantity method, exactly for normal and other populations and approximately for large samples. Parametric **hypothesis testing** (Chapter 5) introduces the two types of error, power and $p$-values, the classical tests for normal and other populations, the **likelihood ratio test**, uniformly most powerful tests via the Neyman–Pearson lemma, the duality between tests and confidence intervals, and tests in linear models. The last two topic chapters leave the classical parametric setting: nonparametric tests (Chapter 6) make no assumption on the form of the distribution and are based on signs, ranks and the empirical distribution function, including goodness-of-fit tests, contingency tables and tests of normality; **Bayesian inference** (Chapter 7) treats the parameter as a random variable and draws every conclusion from the posterior distribution, and closes with statistical decision theory. Chapter 8 reviews the course.

This subject relies on [probability theory](/3.1) throughout: the $\chi^2$, $t$ and $F$ distributions and the order statistics come from the distributions of functions of random vectors, and the consistency and asymptotic normality of estimators come from the law of large numbers, the central limit theorem, Slutsky's theorem and the delta method. Its language of normal-theory inference is the starting point of the later courses: [linear regression analysis](/3.3) applies the $t$ and $F$ tests to linear models; [multivariate statistical analysis](/3.4) replaces the $t$ statistic by **Hotelling's T² statistic** and the $\chi^2$ distribution by the Wishart distribution; and Chapters 6 and 7 are entry points to [nonparametric statistics](/3.6) and [Bayesian statistics](/3.7). Maximum likelihood, kernel density estimation and sparse estimation are also basic methods of [machine learning](/4.6).

Compared with Elementary Probability Theory, this course did not give me the sense of being much more advanced than the statistics part of Probability and Mathematical Statistics. Most of the teaching and assessment concentrated on normal populations; the requirements on nonparametric methods were very light, and Bayesian methods were not taught at all. Even so, the language of normal-theory inference developed here, with its sampling distributions, pivotal quantities, likelihood ratios and the duality between tests and intervals, is used far more than what the introductory course offers as the basis of the later statistics courses: regression, the analysis of variance and multivariate analysis are all written in it. For this reason the course is still well suited to being a required course.
