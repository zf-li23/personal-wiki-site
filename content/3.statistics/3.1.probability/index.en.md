Probability theory studies the quantitative laws of random phenomena: given a completely specified probability model, it computes the probabilities of events and the distributions and numerical characteristics of random variables, and it studies the deterministic regularities that emerge when many random influences add up. It has four central problems: how to describe a random experiment rigorously; how to characterize the distributions of random variables and random vectors, and of functions of them; how to summarize a distribution by a few numbers such as the expectation and the variance; and how sequences of random variables behave in the limit. This subject corresponds to the course Elementary Probability Theory and also covers the probability part of Probability and Mathematical Statistics. It is developed within Kolmogorov's axiomatic framework; compared with the latter course its definitions are more rigorous, its distributions and tools are richer, and its demands on the limit theorems are higher.

The chapters follow one main line. Chapters 1 and 2 build the axiomatic framework starting from probability models. Classical and geometric models are the most basic ones, and Bertrand's paradox shows that "equally likely" must be made precise first. Once events are restricted to a σ-algebra, a probability is a function on it with **countable additivity**, and the three together form a **probability space**; countable additivity yields the continuity of probability and the Borel–Cantelli lemma, while conditional probability, the law of total probability and Bayes' formula describe how probabilities are updated when new information arrives. Chapters 3–7 turn the outcomes of an experiment into numbers: Chapter 3 introduces independence of events and of random variables; Chapter 4 presents the common discrete and continuous distributions together with the random experiments they come from; Chapter 5 characterizes every random variable by its distribution function and finds the distributions of functions of a random variable; Chapters 6 and 7 extend this to the joint, marginal and conditional distributions of random vectors and use the convolution formula and change of variables to find the distributions of functions of random vectors, which yields the $\chi^2$, $t$ and $F$ distributions of statistics and the order statistics. Chapters 8–10 deal with numerical characteristics: expectation, variance and moments; probability inequalities such as those of Markov, Chebyshev and Jensen; **conditional expectation** with the law of iterated expectations and the law of total variance; and covariance, the correlation coefficient and the best linear predictor. The probability generating function, the moment generating function and the **characteristic function** of Chapter 11 transform a distribution into a function, so that the convolution of independent summands becomes a product; they are the main tool for proving the limit theorems. Chapters 12–14 study limits of sequences of random variables: the **law of large numbers** states that the sample mean converges to the expectation, and the **central limit theorem** states that the standardized sum converges in distribution to the standard normal distribution; Chapter 14 then compares the strength of the various modes of convergence systematically and gives the continuous mapping theorem, Slutsky's theorem and the delta method, tools used again and again in statistics. Chapter 15 generalizes the normal distribution to the **multivariate normal distribution**, and Chapter 16 summarizes the whole course in a few tables.

The mathematical language of probability theory comes from [calculus](/2.2) and [linear algebra](/2.1): distribution functions, densities and expectations are integrals; distributions of functions of random vectors are computed with multiple integrals and Jacobian determinants; the **covariance matrix** of a random vector is positive semidefinite, and the derivations for the multivariate normal distribution rely on matrix algebra; and the characteristic function is the **Fourier transform** of a distribution. This subject is the common foundation of all the other courses in statistics. In [statistical inference](/3.2), sampling distributions and the consistency and asymptotic normality of estimators come directly from the $\chi^2$, $t$ and $F$ distributions and the limit theorems developed here; the multivariate normal distribution is the starting point of [linear regression analysis](/3.3) and [multivariate statistical analysis](/3.4); the **Monte Carlo method** rests on the law of large numbers and grows into a whole toolbox of stochastic simulation in [statistical computing and software](/3.5); and Bayes' formula is the starting point of [Bayesian statistics](/3.7). Beyond statistics, the models of [machine learning](/4.7) are mostly formulated as probability distributions, and the tests and models of [biostatistics](/7.8) and [bioinformatics](/7.9) are built on the concepts introduced here.

In my view, this course treats its material much more thoroughly than the probability part of Probability and Mathematical Statistics. First, its definitions are more rigorous: events, probabilities and random variables are all defined from axioms, and questions that the introductory course passes over, such as whether every subset of the sample space can be assigned a probability, receive a definite answer here. Second, it asks much more about distributions other than the normal: it is not enough to remember the formulas for the binomial, Poisson and normal distributions; one has to understand which experiment each of the geometric, negative binomial, hypergeometric, exponential, Gamma and Beta distributions comes from, and how they are related to one another. The course also introduces very useful tools such as the characteristic function, which turns problems that are otherwise laborious, such as finding the distribution of a sum of independent variables or proving the central limit theorem, into operations on functions. Its demands on the laws of large numbers are higher as well: one has to distinguish the weak and the strong law, convergence in probability and almost sure convergence, and the conditions of the different versions due to Markov, Khinchin and others. Precisely because it lays a solid foundation for the language of the later statistics courses, I strongly recommend Elementary Probability Theory as the first course for anyone trying out the statistics minor.
