The previous chapter, [3.1.1](/3.1.1), described a random experiment by "a sample space plus a probability satisfying the axioms", but left a question open: can every subset of the sample space be assigned a probability? This chapter answers it first. Events are restricted to a family of subsets closed under countable set operations (an event field), a probability is a measure defined on that family, and the three together form a probability space, the core of Kolmogorov's axiomatic system. The first consequence of countable additivity is the continuity of probability, which allows us to discuss limits of sequences of events and leads to the Borel–Cantelli lemma. The second half of the chapter introduces conditional probability: how to update our beliefs about events once new information is obtained. The multiplication rule, the law of total probability and Bayes' theorem, all derived from conditional probability, are the three most frequently used tools for computing probabilities. The next chapter, [3.1.3](/3.1.3), uses conditional probability to define independence and introduces random variables.

## 2.1. Probability Spaces

### 2.1.1. The Problem

Recall the geometric model of Example 1.2.6: $\Omega=[0,1]^2$ and $\mathbb{P}(A)$ is the area of $A$. Every single point $\{(x,y)\}$ has area zero, while $\Omega=\bigcup_{(x,y)\in\Omega}\{(x,y)\}$. Applying additivity to this union would give

$$
1=\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=\sum_{(x,y)\in\Omega}\mathbb{P}(\{(x,y)\})=0,
$$
a contradiction. The error is that this is a union of uncountably many events, while additivity holds only for countably many. The example also shows that an event of probability 1 need not be $\Omega$: $\mathbb{P}\big((x,y)\ne(0,0)\big)=1$, yet $(0,0)$ can still occur.

The deeper question is whether every subset of the sample space can be assigned a probability. For uncountable sample spaces the answer is no (Example 2.1.5). We must therefore first specify which subsets count as events; this is the role of the event field.

### 2.1.2. Event Fields

**Definition 2.1.1 (σ-Algebra)** Let $\Omega$ be a sample space and $\mathcal{F}$ a family of subsets of $\Omega$. If $\mathcal{F}$ satisfies the following three conditions, $\mathcal{F}$ is called an event field on $\Omega$, or a **σ-algebra** (also **σ-field**); the elements of $\mathcal{F}$ are called events, and $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$ is called a **measurable space**:

1. $\Omega\in\mathcal{F}$;
2. if $A\in\mathcal{F}$, then $A^{\mathsf c}\in\mathcal{F}$;
3. if $A_n\in\mathcal{F}$ for $n=1,2,\dots$, then $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\in\mathcal{F}$.

**Remark 2.1.1** (1) Every event in $\mathcal{F}$ can be assigned a probability; $\mathcal{F}$ delimits the range of all events of interest. A subset of $\Omega$ is not necessarily an event: only the elements of $\mathcal{F}$ are called events. (2) By definition, $\varnothing=\Omega^{\mathsf c}\in\mathcal{F}$; by De Morgan's laws, $\bigcap_nA_n=(\bigcup_nA_n^{\mathsf c})^{\mathsf c}\in\mathcal{F}$; taking $A_{n+1}=A_{n+2}=\cdots=\varnothing$ shows that finite unions and intersections also lie in $\mathcal{F}$, and $A\setminus B=A\cap B^{\mathsf c}\in\mathcal{F}$. Thus $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under countably many intersections, unions, complements and differences, including the limit operations on sequences of events in the next section. (3) Whether the probability of a given event in $\mathcal{F}$ can actually be computed depends on what is known; sometimes an event is so complicated that it can only be approximated by events whose probabilities are easy to compute, or bounded above and below by inequalities.

Many different event fields can be built on a fixed $\Omega$.

**Example 2.1.1** (1) $\mathcal{F}=\{\Omega,\varnothing\}$ is the trivial σ-algebra and also the smallest σ-algebra. (2) The **power set** $\mathcal{P}(\Omega)$ (also written $2^\Omega$), consisting of all subsets of $\Omega$, is the largest σ-algebra. (3) For $A\subset\Omega$, $\{\Omega,\varnothing,A,A^{\mathsf c}\}$ is the smallest σ-algebra containing $A$.

In general, the intersection of any collection of σ-algebras is again a σ-algebra (check the definition condition by condition). Hence for any family $\mathcal{A}$ of subsets of $\Omega$, the intersection of all σ-algebras containing $\mathcal{A}$ is the smallest σ-algebra containing $\mathcal{A}$; it is called the σ-algebra generated by $\mathcal{A}$ and denoted $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$. Example 2.1.1 (3) is $\sigma(\{A\})$.

**Example 2.1.2** Let $A,B\subset\Omega$. The four sets $AB$, $AB^{\mathsf c}$, $A^{\mathsf c}B$, $A^{\mathsf c}B^{\mathsf c}$ are pairwise disjoint with union $\Omega$, and $\sigma(\{A,B\})$ consists exactly of the unions of some (possibly none) of them. When all four sets are nonempty, $\sigma(\{A,B\})$ has $2^4=16$ elements:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma(\{A,B\})=\{&\Omega,\ \varnothing,\ A,\ A^{\mathsf c},\ B,\ B^{\mathsf c},\ AB,\ AB^{\mathsf c},\ A^{\mathsf c}B,\ A^{\mathsf c}B^{\mathsf c},\\
&A\cup B,\ A^{\mathsf c}\cup B,\ A\cup B^{\mathsf c},\ A^{\mathsf c}\cup B^{\mathsf c},\ AB\cup A^{\mathsf c}B^{\mathsf c},\ AB^{\mathsf c}\cup A^{\mathsf c}B\}.
\end{aligned}
$$
If some of the four sets are empty (for example $B=A^{\mathsf c}$, or $B\subset A$), the number of elements decreases accordingly.

**Example 2.1.3** The union of two σ-algebras need not be a σ-algebra. Let $\Omega=\{a,b,c\}$, $\mathcal{F}=\{\Omega,\varnothing,\{a\},\{b,c\}\}$ and $\mathcal{G}=\{\Omega,\varnothing,\{c\},\{a,b\}\}$. Then $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}=\{\Omega,\varnothing,\{a\},\{c\},\{a,b\},\{b,c\}\}$, but $\{a\}\cup\{c\}=\{a,c\}\notin\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}$. (If $\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{G}$, then $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}$ is of course a σ-algebra.) To verify that a family is a σ-algebra one usually checks the definition; to refute it one usually finds an operation under which it is not closed.

### 2.1.3. Probability Measures and Probability Spaces

**Definition 2.1.2 (Probability Measure)** Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$ be a measurable space and $\mathbb{P}$ a function defined on $\mathcal{F}$. If $\mathbb{P}$ satisfies the following three conditions, $\mathbb{P}$ is called a **probability measure** on $\mathcal{F}$, or simply a probability, and $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ is called a **probability space**:

1. (Nonnegativity) $\mathbb{P}(A)\ge0$ for every $A\in\mathcal{F}$;
2. (Normalization) $\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1$;
3. (Countable additivity) for pairwise disjoint events $A_1,A_2,\dots$ in $\mathcal{F}$,
$$
\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\Big)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\mathbb{P}(A_n).
$$

Distinguish carefully between the event field $\mathcal{F}$, the measurable space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F})$ and the probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$: the same $\Omega$ can carry different event fields, and the same measurable space can carry different probabilities. All properties derived from the axioms in [3.1.1](/3.1.1) hold on a probability space; the proper formulation reads, for instance, "if $A\in\mathcal{F}$, then $\mathbb{P}(A^{\mathsf c})=1-\mathbb{P}(A)$".

**Example 2.1.4** (1) Tossing a coin: $\Omega=\{H,T\}$, $\mathcal{F}=\{\Omega,\varnothing,\{H\},\{T\}\}$, $\mathbb{P}(\{H\})=0.6$, $\mathbb{P}(\{T\})=0.4$.

(2) Rolling a die: $\Omega=\{1,\dots,6\}$, $\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{P}(\Omega)$, $\mathbb{P}(\{i\})=1/6$; for every $A\in\mathcal{F}$, $\mathbb{P}(A)=\#A/6$.

(3) Toss a coin repeatedly (with probability $p\in(0,1)$ of heads on each toss) until a head appears: $\Omega=\{T^nH\colon n\ge0\}\cup\{T^\infty\}$, where $T^nH$ means $n$ tails followed by a head and $T^\infty$ means that a head never appears. Since $\mathbb{P}(\{T^nH\})=(1-p)^np$ and, by countable additivity, $\sum_{n\ge0}(1-p)^np=1$, we get $\mathbb{P}(\{T^\infty\})=0$; this also follows from the continuity of the next section, $\mathbb{P}(\{T^\infty\})=\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-p)^n=0$.

If $\Omega$ is countable, assign to each sample point a probability $p_\omega\ge0$ with $\sum_\omega p_\omega=1$ and set $\mathbb{P}(A)=\sum_{\omega\in A}p_\omega$; then every subset of $\Omega$ receives a probability, and we may take $\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{P}(\Omega)$. If $\Omega$ is uncountable, there may be subsets to which no reasonable probability can be assigned, and such subsets cannot be events.

**Example 2.1.5 (A Non-Measurable Set)** On $\Omega=[-2,2]$, the geometric probability $\mathbb{P}(A)=\operatorname{length}(A)/4$ cannot be defined on all subsets so as to be both countably additive and translation invariant. For $x,y\in[0,1]$, define $x\sim y$ if and only if $x-y\in\mathbb{Q}$. This is an equivalence relation, which partitions $[0,1]$ into disjoint equivalence classes. By the axiom of choice there is a set $E\subset[0,1]$ containing exactly one element of each equivalence class. Enumerate $[-1,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}$ as $r_1,r_2,\dots$ and let $E_n=r_n+E$.

1. $E_m\cap E_n=\varnothing$ for $m\ne n$: if $x\in E_m\cap E_n$, then $x-r_m$ and $x-r_n$ are two distinct elements of $E$ whose difference $r_n-r_m$ is rational, so they belong to the same equivalence class, contradicting the construction of $E$.
2. $[0,1]\subset\bigcup_nE_n\subset[-1,2]$: for $x\in[0,1]$, take the $y\in E$ equivalent to $x$; then $x-y\in[-1,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}$, that is, $x-y=r_n$ for some $n$, and $x\in E_n$. The second inclusion is obvious.

If $\mathbb{P}(E)$ were defined, translation invariance would give $\mathbb{P}(E_n)=\mathbb{P}(E)$, and monotonicity and countable additivity would give

$$
0<\frac14=\mathbb{P}([0,1])\le\sum_{n=1}^\infty\mathbb{P}(E)\le\mathbb{P}([-1,2])=\frac34.
$$
The right inequality forces $\mathbb{P}(E)=0$, while the left one requires $\mathbb{P}(E)>0$, a contradiction. So $E$ cannot be assigned a probability. The geometric models used in practice take $\mathcal{F}$ to be the σ-algebra generated by all intervals, whose members are called *Borel sets*, or the larger family of Lebesgue measurable sets.

**Definition 2.1.3 (Almost Surely)** If $A\in\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathbb{P}(A)=1$, we say that $A$ occurs with probability 1, or **almost surely** (almost everywhere), abbreviated a.s.

## 2.2. Continuity of Probability

### 2.2.1. Monotone Sequences of Events

A sequence of events $\{A_n\}$ is called increasing if $A_1\subset A_2\subset\cdots$ and decreasing if $A_1\supset A_2\supset\cdots$; both are called monotone sequences of events. For an increasing sequence define $\lim_{n\to\infty}A_n=\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n$, and for a decreasing sequence define $\lim_{n\to\infty}A_n=\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n$. This is analogous to the limit of a monotone sequence of numbers.

**Theorem 2.2.1 (Continuity of Probability)** Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and $A_n,B_n\in\mathcal{F}$.

1. If $\{A_n\}$ is increasing, then $\mathbb{P}(\lim_{n\to\infty}A_n)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(A_n)$;
2. If $\{B_n\}$ is decreasing, then $\mathbb{P}(\lim_{n\to\infty}B_n)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_n)$.

**Proof** (1) Let $A_0=\varnothing$. By monotonicity, $\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i=\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty(A_i\setminus A_{i-1})$, where the terms on the right are pairwise disjoint and $\mathbb{P}(A_i\setminus A_{i-1})=\mathbb{P}(A_i)-\mathbb{P}(A_{i-1})$. By countable additivity,

$$
\mathbb{P}\Big(\lim_{n\to\infty}A_n\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^\infty\big[\mathbb{P}(A_i)-\mathbb{P}(A_{i-1})\big]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^n\big[\mathbb{P}(A_i)-\mathbb{P}(A_{i-1})\big]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(A_n).
$$

(2) $\{B_n^{\mathsf c}\}$ is increasing, and by De Morgan's laws $(\bigcap_nB_n)^{\mathsf c}=\bigcup_nB_n^{\mathsf c}$. By (1),

$$
1-\mathbb{P}\Big(\lim_{n\to\infty}B_n\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\lim_{n\to\infty}B_n^{\mathsf c}\Big)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_n^{\mathsf c})=1-\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_n).\qquad\square
$$

Conversely, if a set function that is nonnegative, normalized and finitely additive has the continuity property (1), then it is countably additive: for disjoint $A_i$, apply (1) to $C_n=\bigcup_{i\le n}A_i$. Continuity and countable additivity are therefore equivalent requirements.

### 2.2.2. Limits Superior and Inferior of Events

**Definition 2.2.1 (Limits Superior and Inferior of Events)** Let $\{A_n\}$ be a sequence of events. The event

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n=\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty\bigcup_{k=n}^\infty A_k=\{\omega\in\Omega\colon\omega\text{ belongs to infinitely many }A_k\}
$$
is called the **limit superior** of $\{A_n\}$, and

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty}A_n=\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty\bigcap_{k=n}^\infty A_k=\{\omega\in\Omega\colon\omega\text{ belongs to all but finitely many }A_k\}
$$
is called the **limit inferior** of $\{A_n\}$. If $\liminf_nA_n=\limsup_nA_n$, the limit of the sequence $\{A_n\}$ is said to exist and is denoted $\lim_{n\to\infty}A_n$.

The limit superior is often read "$A_n$ occurs infinitely often" ($A_n$ i.o.), and the limit inferior "$A_n$ occurs eventually always". Clearly $\liminf_nA_n\subset\limsup_nA_n$, and $(\limsup_nA_n)^{\mathsf c}=\liminf_nA_n^{\mathsf c}$. For monotone sequences, both limits coincide with the limit defined in Section 2.2.1.

**Theorem 2.2.2** For every sequence of events $\{A_n\}$,

$$
\mathbb{P}\Big(\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n\Big)\ge\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(A_n),\qquad\mathbb{P}\Big(\liminf_{n\to\infty}A_n\Big)\le\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(A_n).
$$

**Proof** Let $B_n=\bigcup_{k=n}^\infty A_k$. Then $\{B_n\}$ is decreasing and $\lim_nB_n=\limsup_nA_n$, so by continuity $\mathbb{P}(\limsup_nA_n)=\lim_n\mathbb{P}(B_n)$. By monotonicity, $\mathbb{P}(B_n)\ge\mathbb{P}(A_k)$ for all $k\ge n$, that is, $\mathbb{P}(B_n)\ge\sup_{k\ge n}\mathbb{P}(A_k)$. Letting $n\to\infty$ (the right side is decreasing in $n$, so its limit exists) gives the first inequality. The second follows in the same way from $C_n=\bigcap_{k=n}^\infty A_k$. $\square$

### 2.2.3. The Borel–Cantelli Lemma

**Theorem 2.2.3 (Borel–Cantelli Lemma)** Let $\{A_n\}$ be a sequence of events.

1. If $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\mathbb{P}(A_n)<\infty$, then $\mathbb{P}(\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n)=0$;
2. If the $A_n$ are mutually independent and $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\mathbb{P}(A_n)=\infty$, then $\mathbb{P}(\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n)=1$.

**Proof** (1) For each $n$, $\limsup_kA_k\subset\bigcup_{k=n}^\infty A_k$. By monotonicity and countable subadditivity, $\mathbb{P}(\limsup_kA_k)\le\sum_{k=n}^\infty\mathbb{P}(A_k)$, and the tail of a convergent series tends to zero as $n\to\infty$. (2) This part uses the independence of the sequence; see Theorem 3.1.2 in [3.1.3](/3.1.3). $\square$

Part (1) requires no independence assumption at all and is the basic tool for proving that statements hold almost surely (such as the strong law of large numbers, see [3.1.12](/3.1.12)).

**Example 2.2.1** Toss a fair coin infinitely many times (the sample space consists of all infinite sequences of heads and tails), and let $H_n$ be the event that the $n$-th toss is a head. The tosses are independent, $\mathbb{P}(H_n)=1/2$ and $\sum_n\mathbb{P}(H_n)=\infty$. By part (2) of the Borel–Cantelli lemma, $\mathbb{P}(\limsup_nH_n)=1$: with probability 1, infinitely many heads appear. Can it happen that from some toss on every toss is a head? By the same argument $\mathbb{P}(\limsup_nH_n^{\mathsf c})=1$, so

$$
\mathbb{P}\Big(\liminf_{n\to\infty}H_n\Big)=1-\mathbb{P}\Big(\limsup_{n\to\infty}H_n^{\mathsf c}\Big)=0,
$$
which is almost impossible. On the other hand, if the $n$-th toss is a head with probability $1/n^2$, then $\sum_n1/n^2<\infty$, and by (1) only finitely many heads appear with probability 1.

## 2.3. Conditional Probability

### 2.3.1. Definition

How should we update our knowledge of an event once new information is obtained?

**Example 2.3.1** Draw a card at random from a deck of 52. Given that the card is a club, what is the probability that it is a 5? Let $A$ be the event "the card is a club" and $B$ the event "the card is a 5". Without the condition, $\mathbb{P}(A)=13/52$ and $\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=1/52$. Once we know that $A$ has occurred, the only possible outcomes are the 13 clubs, which are still equally likely, so the required probability is $1/13$, written $\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)=1/13$. Note that

$$
\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)=\frac1{13}=\frac{1/52}{13/52}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(A)}.
$$

In general, given an experiment, a sample space and a probability, if we know that an event $A$ has occurred and want to know how likely an event $B$ is, we construct a new probability that takes the information that $A$ occurred into account.

**Definition 2.3.1 (Conditional Probability)** Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and $A,B\in\mathcal{F}$ with $\mathbb{P}(A)>0$. The quantity

$$
\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(A)}
$$
is called the **conditional probability** of $B$ given $A$.

When $\mathbb{P}(A)=0$ the conditional probability is undefined (it is sometimes set, as needed, to an arbitrary constant in $[0,1]$). Conditioning on events of probability zero will be handled with densities in the conditional distributions of [3.1.9](/3.1.9).

**Example 2.3.2** Roll a fair four-sided die twice; the 16 outcomes are equally likely, and $X$, $Y$ are the two results. Find $\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)$, where $A=\{\max(X,Y)=m\}$ and $B=\{\min(X,Y)=2\}$. We have $B=\{(2,2),(2,3),(2,4),(3,2),(4,2)\}$, $\#B=5$, and $\#(A\cap B)$ equals $0,1,2,2$ for $m=1,2,3,4$. Hence $\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)$ is $0$ for $m=1$, $1/5$ for $m=2$ and $2/5$ for $m=3,4$.

### 2.3.2. Conditional Probability Is a Probability Measure

**Theorem 2.3.1** Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and $A\in\mathcal{F}$ with $\mathbb{P}(A)>0$. Then

1. $\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)\ge0$ for every $B\in\mathcal{F}$;
2. $\mathbb{P}(\Omega\mid A)=1$;
3. for every sequence of pairwise disjoint events $\{B_i\}$, $\mathbb{P}\big(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty B_i\mid A\big)=\sum_{i=1}^\infty\mathbb{P}(B_i\mid A)$.

**Proof** (1) and (2) follow directly from the definition. (3) The events $A\cap B_i$ are pairwise disjoint, so by countable additivity $\mathbb{P}(A\cap\bigcup_iB_i)=\mathbb{P}(\bigcup_i(A\cap B_i))=\sum_i\mathbb{P}(A\cap B_i)$; divide both sides by $\mathbb{P}(A)$. $\square$

Writing $\mathbb{P}_A(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A)$, the triple $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_A)$ is again a probability space, so all properties of probability in [3.1.1](/3.1.1) hold for conditional probabilities, for example $\mathbb{P}(B^{\mathsf c}\mid A)=1-\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)$. Since $\mathbb{P}(A\mid A)=1$, the conditional probability is entirely concentrated on $A$; we may also discard the outcomes outside $A$ and regard $(A,\ A\cap\mathcal{F},\ \mathbb{P}_A)$ as a new probability space, where $A\cap\mathcal{F}=\{A\cap B\colon B\in\mathcal{F}\}$. For example, in the classical model $\Omega=\{1,2,3\}$ with $A=\{1,2\}$, we have $\mathbb{P}(\{2\})=1/3$, while $\mathbb{P}_A(\{2\})=1/2$ and $\mathbb{P}_A(\{3\})=0$.

### 2.3.3. Conditional versus Unconditional Probability

There is no fixed order between $\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)$ and $\mathbb{P}(B)$: if $B\subset A$, then $\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)=\mathbb{P}(B)/\mathbb{P}(A)\ge\mathbb{P}(B)$; if $A\cap B=\varnothing$, then $\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)=0\le\mathbb{P}(B)$. The more specific the information in the condition, the greater its possible effect on the conclusion; the next two examples show that the conditioning event must be written down carefully.

**Example 2.3.3 (The Two-Child Problem)** A family has two children; the four outcomes of the sample space $\{(G,G),(G,B),(B,G),(B,B)\}$ (listed by age, $G$ for a girl and $B$ for a boy) are equally likely.

1. Given that the elder child is a girl, the probability that both are girls: the conditioning event is $\{(G,G),(G,B)\}$, and the probability is $1/2$.
2. Ask the father "Do you have at least one daughter?" and he answers "yes". The conditioning event is $\{(G,G),(G,B),(B,G)\}$, and the probability is $1/3$.
3. Ask the father "Do you have at least one daughter named Lilia?" and he answers "yes". Suppose each girl is independently named Lilia with a small probability $a$, and no boy has this name. Let $L$ be the event "at least one daughter is named Lilia" and $G_2$ the event "both children are girls". Each child is a girl named Lilia with probability $a/2$, so $\mathbb{P}(L)=1-(1-a/2)^2=a-a^2/4$; if both are girls, at least one is named Lilia with probability $1-(1-a)^2=2a-a^2$, so $\mathbb{P}(G_2\cap L)=\frac14(2a-a^2)$. Therefore
$$
\mathbb{P}(G_2\mid L)=\frac{(2a-a^2)/4}{a-a^2/4}=\frac{2-a}{4-a}\approx\frac12\quad(a\to0).
$$
A rare name is specific information about one particular child, and its effect is close to "specifying which child", so the answer changes from $1/3$ to nearly $1/2$.

**Example 2.3.4** Toss a coin twice. Is the probability that both tosses are heads given that the first is a head at least as large as the probability that both are heads given that at least one is a head? Let $A$ and $B$ be the events that the first and second tosses are heads. Then

$$
\mathbb{P}(A\cap B\mid A)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(A)}\ge\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(A\cup B)}=\mathbb{P}(A\cap B\mid A\cup B),
$$
whether or not the coin is fair (for a fair coin the two values are $1/2$ and $1/3$). More generally, if $B\subset C$ and $A\cap B=A\cap C$, then $\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)\ge\mathbb{P}(A\mid C)$.

## 2.4. The Multiplication Rule

### 2.4.1. The Multiplication Rule and Tree Diagrams

Many probability models are built from conditional probabilities: first give the probabilities of the first stage, then the conditional probabilities of the second stage given the result of the first, and so on.

**Example 2.4.1** A radar monitors the airspace above it for aircraft. Let $A$ be the event "an aircraft is present" and $B$ the event "the radar raises an alarm". Suppose $\mathbb{P}(A)=0.05$, $\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)=0.99$ and $\mathbb{P}(B\mid A^{\mathsf c})=0.10$. Then $\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)=0.0495$, $\mathbb{P}(B)=0.05\times0.99+0.95\times0.10=0.1445$, and the probability that an aircraft is actually present when the radar raises an alarm is $\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=0.0495/0.1445\approx0.34$.

**Theorem 2.4.1 (Multiplication Rule)** Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and $A_1,\dots,A_n\in\mathcal{F}$ with $\mathbb{P}(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{n-1})>0$. Then

$$
\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^nA_i\Big)=\mathbb{P}(A_1)\prod_{i=2}^n\mathbb{P}(A_i\mid A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{i-1}).
$$

**Proof** By monotonicity, $\mathbb{P}(A_1)\ge\mathbb{P}(A_1\cap A_2)\ge\cdots\ge\mathbb{P}(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{n-1})>0$, so all the conditional probabilities are defined. Writing each conditional probability as a ratio of two probabilities, the product on the right telescopes to $\mathbb{P}(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_n)$. $\square$

Since the events can be taken in any order, the multiplication rule is really $n!$ formulas; the key to a problem is choosing an order in which the conditional probabilities are easy to compute. The multiplication rule corresponds to the following procedure on a sequential tree diagram:

1. Draw the tree so that the events of interest are at the ends (leaves). Each node on a path from the root to a leaf represents an event, and the event of interest is the result of the events along the path occurring in turn;
2. Write the corresponding conditional probability on each branch;
3. The probability of the event at a leaf is the product of the conditional probabilities on the branches along the path.

For example, a statistical language model writes the probability of a sentence as a product of word-by-word conditional probabilities: $\mathbb{P}(\text{Where are we going})=\mathbb{P}(\text{Where})\,\mathbb{P}(\text{are}\mid\text{Where})\,\mathbb{P}(\text{we}\mid\text{Where are})\,\mathbb{P}(\text{going}\mid\text{Where are we})$.

### 2.4.2. The Matching Problem

**Example 2.4.2 (The Matching Problem)** Someone writes $n$ letters and $n$ envelopes and puts the letters into the envelopes at random (one letter per envelope). Find (1) the probability $q_0(n)$ that no letter is in its correct envelope; (2) the probability $q_r(n)$ that exactly $r$ letters are in their correct envelopes.

(1) Let $A_i$ be the event that letter $i$ is in its correct envelope. By the multiplication rule, $\mathbb{P}(A_i)=\frac1n$ and, for $i<j$, $\mathbb{P}(A_iA_j)=\mathbb{P}(A_i)\mathbb{P}(A_j\mid A_i)=\frac1n\cdot\frac1{n-1}=\frac{(n-2)!}{n!}$; in general, the probability that $k$ specified letters are all correct is $\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}$. By the inclusion–exclusion identity (Property 1.3.6 in [3.1.1](/3.1.1)),

$$
\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^nA_i\Big)=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k-1}\binom nk\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k!}=1-\frac1{2!}+\frac1{3!}-\cdots+\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n!},
$$
so

$$
q_0(n)=1-\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^nA_i\Big)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}\ \longrightarrow\ \mathrm{e}^{-1}=0.36787944\cdots\quad(n\to\infty).
$$
For large $n$, $q_0(n)\approx0.37$, almost independent of $n$.

(2) The probability that $r$ specified letters (say the first $r$) are all correct equals the probability of drawing, without replacement from $n$ balls with distinct numbers, exactly the numbers $1,\dots,r$ in order in the first $r$ draws, namely $\frac1{n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)}$; given this, the probability that the remaining $n-r$ letters are all wrong is $q_0(n-r)$ by (1). Multiplying by the number $\binom nr$ of ways to choose the $r$ letters gives

$$
q_r(n)=\binom nr\frac{1}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{n-r}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}=\frac1{r!}\sum_{k=0}^{n-r}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1}}{r!}.
$$
The limit is exactly the Poisson distribution with parameter 1 of [3.1.4](/3.1.4).

## 2.5. The Law of Total Probability

### 2.5.1. The Theorem

To compute the probability of a complicated event, one often splits the sample space into cases according to some "cause", computes the probability in each case and then takes a weighted sum.

**Theorem 2.5.1 (Law of Total Probability)** Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and let $A_1,\dots,A_n\in\mathcal{F}$ be a **partition** of $\Omega$ ($\bigcup_{i=1}^nA_i=\Omega$ and $A_i\cap A_j=\varnothing$ for $i\ne j$) with $\mathbb{P}(A_i)>0$. Then for every $B\in\mathcal{F}$,

$$
\mathbb{P}(B)=\mathbb{P}(A_1)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A_1)+\cdots+\mathbb{P}(A_n)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A_n).
$$

**Proof** $B=\bigcup_{i=1}^n(B\cap A_i)$, a union of pairwise disjoint events. By finite additivity and the multiplication rule, $\mathbb{P}(B)=\sum_i\mathbb{P}(B\cap A_i)=\sum_i\mathbb{P}(A_i)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A_i)$. $\square$

In particular, if $0<\mathbb{P}(A)<1$, then $\mathbb{P}(B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)+\mathbb{P}(A^{\mathsf c})\mathbb{P}(B\mid A^{\mathsf c})$. By countable additivity, $n$ in the theorem may be replaced by $\infty$ (a countable partition). The key to applying the law is to find a suitable partition, which usually comes from the context of the problem: on a tree diagram, the law of total probability simply adds the probabilities of all leaves leading to $B$.

### 2.5.2. Applications

**Example 2.5.1** An urn contains $n$ white balls and $m$ black balls, which are drawn one after another without replacement. Find the probability that the $k$-th draw gives a black ball ($1\le k\le n+m$). Let $A_k$ be the event "the $k$-th draw is black".

Method 1 (counting): label the balls and draw $k$ of the $n+m$ balls in order, in $P_{n+m}^k$ ways; there are $m$ choices for a black ball in position $k$, and the first $k-1$ positions hold an arrangement of $k-1$ of the remaining $n+m-1$ balls, in $P_{n+m-1}^{k-1}$ ways. So $\mathbb{P}(A_k)=\dfrac{mP_{n+m-1}^{k-1}}{P_{n+m}^k}=\dfrac m{n+m}$.

Method 2 (induction and the law of total probability): we prove by induction on the number of draws the statement "whatever the initial numbers of black and white balls, the probability that the $k$-th draw is black equals the initial proportion of black balls". The case $k=1$ is obvious. Assume the statement holds for $k$, and partition according to the result of the first draw:

$$
\mathbb{P}(A_{k+1})=\frac m{n+m}\mathbb{P}(A_{k+1}\mid A_1)+\frac n{n+m}\mathbb{P}(A_{k+1}\mid A_1^{\mathsf c}).
$$
$\mathbb{P}(A_{k+1}\mid A_1)$ is the probability that the $k$-th draw from an urn with $m-1$ black and $n$ white balls is black, which equals $\frac{m-1}{n+m-1}$ by the induction hypothesis; likewise $\mathbb{P}(A_{k+1}\mid A_1^{\mathsf c})=\frac m{n+m-1}$. Substituting gives $\mathbb{P}(A_{k+1})=\frac{m(m-1)+nm}{(n+m)(n+m-1)}=\frac m{n+m}$.

Conclusion (drawing lots is fair regardless of order): in sampling without replacement, every draw gives a black ball with probability equal to the initial proportion of black balls.

**Example 2.5.2 (Pólya's Urn)** A Pólya urn contains $b$ black and $r$ red balls. At each step a ball is drawn at random and returned together with $c$ additional balls of the same color. Let $A_k$ be the event "the $k$-th draw is black". The same inductive argument as in Method 2 of Example 2.5.1 (partition by the first draw and apply the induction hypothesis to an urn with $b+c$ black or $r+c$ red balls) shows that $\mathbb{P}(A_k)=\frac b{b+r}$ for all $k$:

$$
\mathbb{P}(A_{k+1})=\frac b{b+r}\cdot\frac{b+c}{b+r+c}+\frac r{b+r}\cdot\frac b{b+r+c}=\frac b{b+r}.
$$
By the multiplication rule, the probability that the first two draws are both black is $\frac b{b+r}\cdot\frac{b+c}{b+r+c}$. The case $c=-1$ is sampling without replacement and $c=0$ is sampling with replacement. Pólya's urn is a simple model of contagion: each "infection" makes the next event of the same kind more likely.

**Example 2.5.3 (Surveys on Sensitive Questions)** When surveying the proportion $p$ of households affected by domestic violence (or other sensitive questions, such as doping), respondents are often unwilling to answer truthfully. The randomized response technique works as follows. An urn contains red balls in proportion $p_0$ and white balls in proportion $q_0=1-p_0$. The respondent draws a ball at random, looks at it and returns it; if it is red, the respondent answers truthfully the question "Is there domestic violence in your household?", and if it is white, gives the opposite answer, writing only "yes" or "no" on an anonymous questionnaire. Nobody knows which ball the respondent drew. Let $p_1$ be the probability that a household answers "yes", let $B$ denote the answer "yes" and $A$ the drawing of a red ball. By the law of total probability,

$$
p_1=\mathbb{P}(B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)+\mathbb{P}(A^{\mathsf c})\mathbb{P}(B\mid A^{\mathsf c})=p_0p+q_0(1-p)=q_0+(p_0-q_0)p.
$$
Hence, as long as $p_0\ne q_0$, $p=\dfrac{p_1-q_0}{p_0-q_0}$. In practice $p_1$ is unknown; if $k$ of $n$ surveyed households answer "yes", $p_1$ can be estimated by $\hat p_1=k/n$ and hence $p$ by $\hat p=\dfrac{\hat p_1-q_0}{p_0-q_0}$. For example, with 30 red and 50 white balls in the urn and 195 "yes" answers among 320 households, $p_0=3/8$, $q_0=5/8$ and

$$
\hat p=\frac{195/320-5/8}{3/8-5/8}=6.25\%.
$$
The larger $\lvert p_0-q_0\rvert$, the more reliable the estimate (the error is amplified less by the factor $1/\lvert p_0-q_0\rvert$), but the less acceptable the scheme is to respondents.

**Example 2.5.4 (A Strategy for Switching Envelopes)** Two envelopes each contain a positive whole amount of money (in yuan); the amounts differ but are unknown (regarded as unknown constants). You pick an envelope at random and, after seeing the amount, may decide whether to switch. Strategy: toss a fair coin repeatedly, let $N$ be the number of tosses needed for the first head and set $X=N+\frac12$; switch if the amount seen is less than $X$. Let the two amounts be $a>b$. On seeing the amount $x$, you switch with probability $f(x)=\mathbb{P}(N\ge x)=(1/2)^{x-1}$, which is strictly decreasing in $x$. Partitioning according to which envelope was picked first, the probability of ending with the larger amount $a$ is

$$
\frac12\big(1-f(a)\big)+\frac12f(b)=\frac12+\frac12\big(f(b)-f(a)\big)>\frac12.
$$
Any strictly decreasing switching probability $f$ has this effect: whenever the random threshold falls between $b$ and $a$, the strategy always makes the right decision.

## 2.6. Bayes' Theorem

### 2.6.1. The Theorem

The law of total probability reasons from "causes" to "effects". Conversely, when we know that an effect $B$ has occurred and want to revise our beliefs about the possible causes $A_i$, we need to compute $\mathbb{P}(A_i\mid B)$. For example, $B$ may be "an imaging examination reveals a shadow", with possible causes $A_1,A_2,A_3$: a malignant tumor, a benign tumor and other causes.

**Theorem 2.6.1 (Bayes' Theorem)** Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ be a probability space, $A_1,\dots,A_n$ a partition of $\Omega$ with $\mathbb{P}(A_i)>0$, and $B\in\mathcal{F}$ with $\mathbb{P}(B)>0$. Then

$$
\mathbb{P}(A_i\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A_i)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbb{P}(A_j)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A_j)},\qquad i=1,\dots,n.
$$
In particular, if $0<\mathbb{P}(A)<1$, then

$$
\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)}{\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)+\mathbb{P}(A^{\mathsf c})\mathbb{P}(B\mid A^{\mathsf c})}.
$$

**Proof** By the definition of conditional probability and the multiplication rule, $\mathbb{P}(A_i\mid B)=\mathbb{P}(A_i\cap B)/\mathbb{P}(B)=\mathbb{P}(A_i)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A_i)/\mathbb{P}(B)$; expand the denominator by the law of total probability. $\square$

$\mathbb{P}(A_i)$, the belief in the cause $A_i$ before the information $B$ is obtained, is called the prior probability; $\mathbb{P}(A_i\mid B)$, the belief after the information is obtained, is called the posterior probability. Bayes' theorem says that the posterior probability is proportional to the product of the prior probability and the probability of observing $B$ under that cause. If the information arrives in two steps (first $B_1$, then $B_2$), we may use the posterior after the first step as the prior for the second and apply Bayes' theorem again; the result is the same as conditioning on $B_1\cap B_2$ at once, so the order in which information arrives does not matter.

### 2.6.2. The False-Positive Puzzle

**Example 2.6.1 (The False-Positive Puzzle)** A rare disease has prevalence $0.001$ in a certain population. A test has accuracy $0.95$: a patient tests positive with probability $0.95$, and a healthy person tests negative with probability $0.95$ as well. A person drawn at random from the population tests positive. What is the probability that this person has the disease? Let $A$ be the event "the person has the disease" and $B$ the event "the test is positive". By Bayes' theorem,

$$
\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\frac{0.001\times0.95}{0.001\times0.95+0.999\times0.05}=0.01866\cdots\approx1.87\%.
$$
Even after a positive test, the probability of having the disease is below 2%. The reason is that the prevalence is very low while the test is not accurate enough: among a thousand people there is about 1 patient, but about 50 healthy people are wrongly classified as positive. The curves in the figure show that the posterior probability is determined mainly by the prevalence and by the specificity of the test.

Bayes' theorem is the starting point of Bayesian statistics (see [3.2.7](/3.2.7)), which treats unknown parameters as random and obtains a posterior distribution from a prior distribution and the data. It also underlies many other models, such as Bayesian networks (which represent conditional dependence between variables by a directed graph) and forecasts of epidemic spread.

## Summary

| Topic | Key points |
|---|---|
| Probability space | The event field $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under countable intersections, unions and complements; a probability measure is nonnegative, normalized and countably additive; uncountable sample spaces contain subsets that cannot be assigned a probability |
| Continuity | For monotone sequences, $\mathbb{P}(\lim A_n)=\lim\mathbb{P}(A_n)$; the limit superior means "occurs infinitely often", the limit inferior "occurs eventually always"; the Borel–Cantelli lemma |
| Conditional probability | $\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)=\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)/\mathbb{P}(A)$; $\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid A)$ is again a probability measure |
| Three formulas | Multiplication rule (tree diagrams), law of total probability (from causes to effects), Bayes' theorem (from effects back to causes) |

When meeting a new concept, it helps to compare it with familiar ones (limits of sequences of numbers and limits of sequences of events) and to take a complicated definition apart with simple examples (the limits superior and inferior of events). In computations, break a complicated problem into small simple ones, and use experience to choose the order of conditioning and the partition of the sample space that make the computation easiest.
