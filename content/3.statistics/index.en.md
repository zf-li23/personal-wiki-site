Statistics studies how to draw reliable conclusions from data that are subject to randomness, and how to quantify the uncertainty of those conclusions. Its language is probability theory: probability theory starts from a known model and deduces the behavior of data, while statistical inference runs the other way, from observed data to the model that produced them. This section contains nine courses: [probability theory](/3.1), [statistical inference](/3.2), [linear regression analysis](/3.3), [multivariate statistical analysis](/3.4), [statistical computing and software](/3.5), [nonparametric statistics](/3.6), [Bayesian statistics](/3.7), [design and analysis of experiments](/3.8) and [causal inference](/3.9). The first two are the theoretical foundation; the third and fourth extend inference to relations among several variables; and the remaining five branch out in different directions: computation, relaxing distributional assumptions, a different paradigm of inference, the way data are generated, and causal relations. Together they roughly make up the curriculum of the statistics minor.

The courses are ordered by their dependencies. Probability theory provides random variables, distributions, numerical characteristics, the limit theorems and the **multivariate normal distribution**. Statistical inference builds on it the general framework of point estimation, interval estimation and **hypothesis testing**; the sampling distributions of normal populations and the $t$ and $F$ tests are the language that every later course uses again and again. Linear regression analysis applies inference to the question of how a response depends on several explanatory variables; **least squares**, regression diagnostics and the **analysis of variance** all take shape there. Multivariate statistical analysis then replaces a single variable by a random vector and uses the multivariate normal distribution and the **spectral decomposition** of the covariance matrix to study inference on mean vectors, dimension reduction, classification and clustering. Statistical computing and software discusses how these methods are implemented on a computer and how stochastic simulation answers questions that analytic methods cannot, so it comes after regression and multivariate analysis. Nonparametric statistics relaxes the assumptions on the form of the population distribution, and Bayesian statistics treats parameters as random variables and makes inferences from the posterior distribution; both extend the classical parametric methods of statistical inference, whose Chapters 6 and 7 already introduce them. Design and analysis of experiments studies how to arrange an experiment so that its data can answer the questions of interest, and its analytical tools are linear models and the analysis of variance. Causal inference comes last: it asks how to distinguish association from causation in experimental and observational data, and it draws on almost all of the preceding courses.

The mathematical foundation of statistics is [linear algebra](/2.1) and [calculus](/2.2): probabilities, densities and expectations are integrals, and nearly every formula of regression and multivariate statistics is written with vectors and matrices. Statistics shares a large number of methods with [machine learning](/4.6): regression, regularization, principal component analysis, discrimination and clustering are basic material on both sides, and the difference lies mainly in whether the emphasis is on inference or on prediction. In [biology](/7), [biostatistics](/7.8) and [bioinformatics](/7.9) use these tools directly, from comparisons between experimental groups, to the control of the **false discovery rate** when thousands of genes are tested at once, to dimension reduction and clustering of high-dimensional omics data.

Many science and engineering programs require a course in Probability and Mathematical Statistics, of which about three fifths is probability and two fifths is mathematical statistics. Its axiomatic framework, however, is not rigorous, and the material it covers is rather shallow. I had received some training in statistics while preparing for the biology olympiad, so the course left me with the feeling that I neither learned anything new nor was taught how to solve problems correctly in a thorough way. My main minor is computer science, but those courses are somewhat too low-level and basic for research practice in biology, so I studied statistics at the same time. The programs I followed were mainly the statistics minor and the Data Thinking and Practice certificate; the latter is essentially a reduced version of the minor, with only Statistical Inference and Linear Regression Analysis required. In fact, the courses I took already meet nearly all the graduation requirements of the statistics minor, except for the thesis.

Apart from Statistical Inference, these courses were taught by two instructors, four courses each, and they form two lines. Wanlu Deng leans toward probabilistic modeling and latent structure, teaching Elementary Probability Theory, Multivariate Statistical Analysis, Introduction to Bayesian Statistics and Introduction to Causal Inference, in a rather calm style. Zaiying Zhou leans toward data modeling and computation, teaching Linear Regression Analysis, Statistical Computing and Software, Introduction to Nonparametric Statistics and Design and Analysis of Experiments, in a rather enthusiastic style. Both are very popular with students and explain things very clearly, and as long as one studies seriously, there is no need to worry about harsh grading. On the whole, the curriculum of the statistics minor is now very complete: it starts from the axioms of probability, passes through inference, regression and multivariate analysis, and extends all the way to computation, Bayesian methods and causal inference. Training in biology tells us which questions to study; statistics tells us how to reach conclusions that hold up from data full of noise. This makes it well suited to biology students who want to do quantitative research and need to build a second body of knowledge.
