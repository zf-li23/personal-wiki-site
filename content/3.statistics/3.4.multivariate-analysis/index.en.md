Almost every data set collected in practice records several variables on each individual, and these variables are usually correlated. Multivariate statistical analysis studies such data jointly rather than one variable at a time. Its questions concern different aspects of a **random vector**: where its mean lies and whether two groups differ in their means; in which directions it varies most and whether a few combinations or latent factors explain that variation; how two sets of variables are associated; and how to assign observations to known groups or discover unknown ones. Two tools run through the whole subject: the **multivariate normal distribution**, which plays the role that the normal distribution plays for one variable, and the eigendecomposition and singular value decomposition of matrices, to which almost every method reduces. This subject corresponds to the course Multivariate Statistical Analysis.

The chapters fall into two halves. The first half is classical inference under normality. Chapter 1 organizes multivariate data, measures distance between observations by the Mahalanobis distance, and collects the matrix tools of the course; Chapter 2 introduces the sample mean vector and sample covariance matrix and develops the multivariate normal distribution and its estimation; Chapters 3 and 4 generalize the $t$ test to **Hotelling's T² statistic**, whose companion is the **Wishart distribution**, and use it to test one mean vector, compare two, and build confidence ellipsoids and simultaneous confidence intervals, together with large-sample procedures and checks of the assumptions. The second half develops tools that exploit the structure of multivariate data. **Principal component analysis** (Chapter 5) replaces correlated variables by a few uncorrelated linear combinations and is the **spectral decomposition** of the covariance matrix; Chapter 6 views it through the **singular value decomposition** and extends it to **multidimensional scaling**, **kernel PCA** and t-SNE. Chapter 7 treats the linear regression model in matrix form, with regularization and model selection. **Factor analysis** (Chapter 8) explains covariances by a few latent factors, and **canonical correlation analysis** (Chapter 9) studies the association between two sets of variables. The last two methods concern groups: **discriminant analysis** (Chapter 10) learns a classification rule from labeled observations, and **cluster analysis** (Chapter 11) finds groups without labels, by hierarchical clustering, $k$-means or Gaussian mixtures fitted by the **EM algorithm**. Chapter 12 reviews the methods side by side and introduces extensions such as the **false discovery rate**, correspondence analysis, sparse PCA and network analysis.

This subject brings together several earlier ones. The multivariate normal distribution and the covariance matrix come from [probability theory](/3.1) ([3.1.15](/3.1.15)); the tests, confidence regions and likelihood ratios generalize those of [statistical inference](/3.2); and principal components, canonical correlations and discriminant directions are all optimizations of a **Rayleigh quotient**, solved by the spectral decomposition of [linear algebra](/2.1). Regression is treated in detail in [linear regression analysis](/3.3). Principal component analysis, discriminant analysis and clustering are also the basic unsupervised and supervised methods of [machine learning](/4.6), and dimension reduction and clustering of high-dimensional data are routine steps in [bioinformatics](/7.9).

Probability theory and statistical inference still only let us discuss a population with a single variable, whereas the data we face in actual research mostly have a multivariate structure. Besides classical statistics under the normal assumption, which occupies the first half of the course, the multivariate nature of the data makes it possible to develop many tools of an applied character, represented by principal component analysis in the second half. These tools need no response variable and often no distributional assumption, and they are usually the first things one does with a new data set: reducing its dimension, looking at its structure, and checking whether it falls into groups. For this reason I consider this course the one with the widest application in data analysis.
