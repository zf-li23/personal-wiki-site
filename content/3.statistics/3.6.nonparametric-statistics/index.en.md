Parametric inference assumes that the data come from a family of distributions known up to a few parameters, usually the normal family, and derives the sampling distributions of its statistics from that assumption. Nonparametric statistics asks how much of inference survives without it. Its central question is where a null distribution can come from when the shape of the population is unknown, and three answers recur: from rearrangements of the observed data, which is the idea of the **permutation test**; from **ranks** and other statistics whose distribution is the same for every continuous population; and from counts of events whose probability is fixed by the null hypothesis, which lead to the binomial distribution. Two cautions accompany every method. "**Distribution-free**" does not mean "assumption-free": independence, symmetry, a common shape or **exchangeability** under the null hypothesis are still required, and a method is only as good as these assumptions. And freedom from a model is paid for with information, so the price has to be measured. A second, more recent part of the subject, made practical by cheap computation, aims less at testing than at describing a data set (its center, its density, the trend in a scatter plot) without forcing it into a parametric family. This subject corresponds to the course Introduction to Nonparametric Statistics.

Chapter 1 builds the permutation test and shows that exchangeability, not normality, is what makes it exact. Chapter 2 collects the tools that turn the permutation idea into tabulated procedures: **order statistics**, ranks, **U-statistics**, and the **empirical distribution function** with the **Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality**, which gives a confidence band valid for every population and every sample size. Chapters 3 to 5 then work through the one-sample location problem completely. The **binomial test** is exact for any sample size and underlies the **sign test**; the **Wilcoxon signed-rank test** uses more of the data at the price of symmetry; inverting both tests gives confidence intervals whose endpoints are order statistics or **Walsh averages**, and point estimates such as the **Hodges–Lehmann estimator**; and the **asymptotic relative efficiency** of Pitman turns the question of which test to use into a number that depends only on the shape of the population. Chapter 6 tests a whole distribution, explaining the **Kolmogorov–Smirnov test** through Donsker's theorem and the **Brownian bridge** and repairing it when parameters are estimated; Chapter 7 reduces further one-sample questions (quantiles, trend, randomness of order, directions on a circle) to binomial or combinatorial counts. The following chapters move from one sample to many: paired samples and rank correlation (Chapter 8); two independent samples (Chapter 9), where the central lesson is that the **Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test** tests $\mathbb{P}(X>Y)=1/2$ and compares medians only under a shift model, which motivates the **Brunner–Munzel test**; dispersion, the **Smirnov test** and **quantile regression** (Chapter 10); and several independent or blocked samples, with the **Kruskal–Wallis test** and the **Friedman test** as rank versions of the analysis of variance (Chapter 11). Chapter 12 reads contingency tables the same way, with exact conditional tests such as **Fisher's exact test**, the **Mantel–Haenszel test** for stratified tables and **log-linear models** for multi-way tables. Chapters 13 and 14 survey the modern part: robust estimation, measured by the **influence function** and the **breakdown point**; **kernel density estimation**; and **nonparametric regression**, ordered by the strength of the constraint on the regression function, from the **Theil–Sen estimator** and **isotonic regression** to kernel smoothers, **local polynomial regression** and **smoothing splines**, all governed by the same trade-off between bias and variance. Chapter 15 rearranges the methods by the question a data analyst starts from and names the few ideas from which almost all of them are built.

The course continues several earlier ones. Order statistics come from [probability theory](/3.1); the empirical distribution function, the **Glivenko–Cantelli theorem**, kernel density estimation and the first nonparametric tests were introduced in [statistical inference](/3.2), whose $t$, $F$ and likelihood ratio tests are the parametric benchmarks against which efficiency is measured. Nonparametric regression is placed within regression modeling in [linear regression analysis](/3.3), and the **bootstrap** of [statistical computing](/3.5) is the resampling counterpart of the permutation test. The parametric analyses of paired, several-sample and blocked data are developed in [design and analysis of experiments](/3.8), and the same permutation reasoning, justified by **randomization** rather than by exchangeable sampling, becomes the Fisher test of [causal inference](/3.9). In applications, the rank tests and exact tests are standard tools of [biostatistics](/7.7.8), and the local smoothers reappear as nonparametric methods in [machine learning](/4.6.4).

I regard this course as an advanced one for a simple reason: its value can only be judged against the parametric theory it departs from. One has to know exactly what the $t$ test assumes and what it delivers before one can see what a rank test gives up and what it gains, and the comparison itself requires asymptotic tools, from Pitman efficiency to the weak convergence of the empirical process, that go beyond a first course in inference. What makes it interesting is the attitude behind it. Instead of adopting a model because it is convenient, the subject asks how little needs to be assumed for a conclusion to be valid, and it states the remaining assumptions explicitly. The answers are often better than one might expect: the Wilcoxon tests lose at most about $14\%$ of efficiency against the $t$ test for any symmetric continuous shift family and can gain a great deal when the tails are heavy, so caution is cheap. A second lesson seems to me equally valuable beyond this course: a test answers the question that its statistic is sensitive to, not the question its name suggests, as the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test shows. I have not yet completed the course, and this section is organized from the course content.
