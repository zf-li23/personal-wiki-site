Complex analysis studies functions of a complex variable that are differentiable in the complex sense, the **analytic functions**. The definition looks like the one from one-variable calculus, but requiring the same limit of the difference quotient from every direction in the plane has far-reaching consequences, and the subject is organized around three questions. The first is what complex differentiability forces: an analytic function is infinitely differentiable, equals the sum of its power series near every point, and is determined on a whole region by its values on a small piece of it. The second is how such functions integrate: an integral around a closed curve depends only on the singularities enclosed, so it reduces to a finite sum of residues. The third is how they map regions: an analytic function with nonzero derivative preserves angles, and this lets us transplant problems from complicated plane domains to a disk or a half-plane. This subject corresponds to the engineering mathematics course "Introduction to Complex Analysis" taken by engineering departments. Like the other engineering mathematics courses it is taught as a selection of topics oriented toward computation, with requirements well below those of a mathematics department; the chapters cover the standard core of an introductory course, with conformal mappings as the final topic.

The chapters follow one main line. Chapter 1 prepares the stage: the algebra and geometry of complex numbers, the multivalued argument, the point at infinity and the Riemann sphere, and the topology of plane sets and curves. Chapter 2 defines the complex derivative and reduces it to the Cauchy–Riemann equations, which say that the linear approximation of an analytic map is a rotation followed by a scaling, and which make the real and imaginary parts harmonic conjugates of each other. Chapter 3 extends the exponential, logarithm, powers and trigonometric functions to complex arguments; the periodicity of $\mathrm{e}^z$ makes its inverses multivalued, and choosing branches on cut regions becomes a basic skill. Chapter 4 is the heart of the course: Cauchy's integral theorem and Cauchy's integral formula, from which follow infinite differentiability, Liouville's theorem, the fundamental theorem of algebra and the maximum modulus principle. Chapter 5 expands the Cauchy kernel into geometric series to obtain Taylor series in disks and Laurent series in annuli, and derives the isolation of zeros and the identity theorem. Chapter 6 classifies isolated singularities, including the point at infinity, by their Laurent series and proves the residue theorem. Chapter 7 turns the residue theorem into methods: real integrals over $[0,2\pi]$ and over the real line, Jordan's lemma and Fourier-type integrals, principal values, integrands with branch points, the summation of series, and, through the logarithmic derivative, the argument principle and Rouché's theorem for counting zeros. Chapter 8 takes the geometric view: Möbius transformations and mappings by elementary functions, the Schwarz lemma, the Riemann mapping theorem, and the solution of Laplace's equation in plane domains by conformal mapping. Two ideas run through the whole subject: a local condition (complex differentiability) yields global conclusions, and one formula, Cauchy's integral formula, is the engine behind nearly every theorem.

Complex analysis builds directly on [calculus](/2.2): a contour integral is a line integral in disguise, so Cauchy's theorem first comes out of Green's theorem, and the radius of convergence of a real Taylor series, which is often puzzling on the real line, is explained by the location of complex singularities. From [linear algebra](/2.1) it takes the complex numbers themselves and the observation that multiplication by a complex number is a special $2\times 2$ real matrix; the Cauchy–Riemann equations say precisely that the Jacobian matrix has this form. In [ordinary differential equations](/2.4), the radius of convergence of a power series solution is determined by the complex singular points of the coefficients. [Partial differential equations](/2.5) use this subject constantly: Fourier and Laplace transforms are computed and inverted by contour integrals and residues, harmonic functions in the plane are real parts of analytic functions, and conformal mapping is the classical method for two-dimensional potential problems. The characteristic function of a probability distribution is a Fourier transform, and its inversion formulas are statements of the same kind ([3.1](/3.1)). Finally, the stability of an equilibrium of a linear model, such as those in [Systems Biology](/7.9) and [Ecology](/7.19), is decided by whether the roots of a characteristic polynomial lie in the left half-plane, and the argument principle answers such questions without computing the roots; the Nyquist criterion of control theory rests on the same principle.

I value this subject mainly as a toolkit that replaces computation by structure. A real integral that numerical quadrature can only approximate is obtained exactly from a few residues; a potential problem on an awkward domain becomes a problem on the disk after one well-chosen mapping; and whether a characteristic equation has roots in the right half-plane, which is what stability depends on, can be decided by watching how an image curve winds around the origin, without solving anything. These are the elegant tools that go beyond brute-force simulation: they give exact statements and qualitative properties instead of a single numerical answer. Since the course asks for little beyond calculus and its methods reappear in partial differential equations, probability and signal processing, I think it is well worth taking for students in engineering and computational fields.
