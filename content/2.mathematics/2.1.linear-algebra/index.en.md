Linear algebra studies vectors, matrices and linear maps. It has two central problems. The first is to solve a system of linear equations $A\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{b}$: whether it has a solution, how many solutions it has, and, when it has none, which vector comes closest. The second is to choose a suitable basis for a linear map so that its matrix becomes as simple as possible; this is the problem of normal forms of matrices under equivalence, similarity, congruence, orthogonal similarity and unitary similarity. This subject combines two courses. The first eight chapters follow the textbook Introduction to Linear Algebra and the course Linear Algebra, starting from matrices in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and moving gradually toward abstraction. Chapters 9–13 form the generalization part, corresponding mainly to Selected Topics in Advanced Linear Algebra: they place the theory over general fields and in complex inner product spaces, and complete the treatment of the Jordan canonical form, matrix functions, the spectral theorem and bilinear forms.

The chapters follow one main line. Chapters 1–3 revolve around linear systems: elimination gives a standard procedure for solving them; writing elimination as multiplication on the left by elementary matrices leads to inverse matrices and the LU decomposition; and subspaces, dimension and rank then describe the structure of the solution set, which is a particular solution plus the null space, while the dimensions of the four fundamental subspaces are completely determined by the rank. Chapter 4 introduces inner products and uses orthogonal projections and the QR decomposition to solve the least squares problem when a system has no solution; the determinant of Chapter 5 condenses invertibility into a single number; Chapter 6 is led by iterating a matrix to eigenvalues and diagonalization by similarity; Chapter 7 shows that a real symmetric matrix can always be diagonalized by an orthogonal matrix, and derives from this quadratic forms, positive definite matrices and the singular value decomposition. Chapter 8 is the turning point: it abstracts $\mathbb{R}^n$ into general vector spaces, and once a basis is fixed, linear maps turn back into matrices, so that the earlier results become properties of the linear maps themselves and "simplifying a matrix" becomes "choosing a good basis". The remaining chapters continue along this path: the theory of complex numbers and polynomials in Chapter 9 provides the tools for proving the Jordan canonical form in Chapter 10; Chapter 11 uses the Jordan form to define matrix functions and to solve linear systems of differential equations with constant coefficients; Chapters 12 and 13 prove the spectral theorem for normal operators on unitary spaces and discuss Hermitian matrices, the polar decomposition, bilinear forms and tensors. Two methods run through the whole subject: introducing an equivalence relation, reducing a matrix to a normal form by equivalence transformations and reading off the invariants from the normal form; and breaking a problem down into standard problems that can be solved mechanically (elimination, Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization, finding the roots of a characteristic polynomial, the Euclidean algorithm), while comparing the costs of different algorithms.

Linear algebra is connected with almost every later course. Its abstract definitions agree with those of vector spaces over a field and of groups in discrete mathematics ([1.3](/1.3)); the core of multivariable calculus ([2.2](/2.2)) is local linearization, and deciding whether a critical point is an extremum depends on whether the Hessian matrix is positive definite; the solutions of linear systems of differential equations with constant coefficients are given by the matrix exponential ([2.4](/2.4)). In statistics, the covariance matrix of a random vector is positive semidefinite ([3.1](/3.1)), linear regression rests on least squares and orthogonal projection ([3.3](/3.3)), principal component analysis is the spectral decomposition of a covariance matrix ([3.4](/3.4)), and the LU, QR and Cholesky decompositions and the singular value decomposition are the standard algorithms of [statistical computing](/3.5).

Linear algebra is a foundational course that almost every student of science and engineering takes, and people's experiences of it differ widely. To me it is a subject with a very clear logic and therefore easy to get into: most of the problems it raises have standardized solutions, and every step, from elimination to diagonalization, can be written as a definite computational procedure. This is not because linear algebra was designed for computers (its core took shape long before electronic computers existed) but because its methods are algorithmic by nature, which makes it a natural fit for computers, and numerical linear algebra grew up together with them. This algorithmic character is even more evident in Selected Topics in Advanced Linear Algebra, a required course for the Department of Computer Science and some engineering departments. There we must not only compute but also pay attention to how the efficiency of solving the same problem is improved from one algorithm to another, for example by factoring once and substituting many times, or by updating an inverse cheaply with the Sherman–Morrison formula; and to how a problem can be broken down into classical problems that can be solved in a standardized, automatic way, for example how deciding whether a matrix is diagonalizable reduces to computing a single greatest common divisor, and how computing a matrix function reduces to solving a linear system. As for why people's experiences differ, I think the difference comes mostly from the abstraction that begins in Chapter 8: the same result must be translated from the language of matrices into the language of linear maps and bases, and for this step there is no ready-made computational procedure to rely on.

Linear algebra has an enormous range of applications in research. Together with calculus, it forms the mathematical language needed for the later study of probability and mathematical statistics, and beyond that the foundation of multivariate statistics: as soon as there is more than one variable, almost every formula has to be written with vectors and matrices. The course on statistical computing showed me that many statistical methods, once implemented on a computer, come down to one of the matrix factorizations of this subject; and in machine learning, vectors and higher-order tensors are likewise the basis on which problems are discussed.
