Partial differential equations relate an unknown function of several variables to its partial derivatives. Almost every continuum model takes this form: the displacement of a string, the temperature in a body, the concentration of a chemical, the density of a population spread over space. The subject has three central problems. The first is formulation: deriving the equation from a conservation law and deciding which initial and boundary conditions make a problem well-posed, with a solution that exists, is unique and depends continuously on the data. The second is solution: for the classical linear equations, finding explicit formulas by characteristics, by separation of variables, by integral transforms and by Green's functions. The third is qualitative behavior: the three types of second-order equations, hyperbolic, parabolic and elliptic, behave in fundamentally different ways, with finite propagation speed for waves, instantaneous smoothing for diffusion, and maximum principles for equilibria. This subject corresponds to the engineering mathematics course "Introduction to Partial Differential Equations" taken by engineering departments, taught as a selection of topics oriented toward computation, with requirements well below those of a mathematics department. The chapters cover first-order equations and the classical theory of the wave, heat and Laplace equations, and treat the Fourier transform with some care, since calculus courses state its formulas without saying when they hold.

The chapters follow one main line. Chapter 1 introduces the vocabulary, derives the three classical equations, states Hadamard's notion of well-posedness, and classifies second-order linear equations. Chapter 2 solves first-order equations by the method of characteristics, reducing them to ordinary differential equations along curves; for the Burgers equation the characteristics cross and a shock forms, and Charpit's method handles fully nonlinear equations. Chapter 3 solves the wave equation on the line by d'Alembert's formula, treats boundaries by reflection and sources by Duhamel's principle, proves uniqueness by energy conservation, and contrasts Kirchhoff's formula in three dimensions with Poisson's formula in two, where Huygens' principle fails. Chapters 4 and 5 are separation of variables on bounded regions: the boundary conditions turn the separated equations into eigenvalue problems, the solution is expanded in eigenfunctions (Fourier series on intervals, Bessel functions and Legendre polynomials in cylinders and balls), and Sturm–Liouville theory justifies the expansions. Chapter 6 passes from series to integrals on unbounded regions: the Fourier transform turns differentiation into multiplication, so that linear equations with constant coefficients become ordinary differential equations and solutions appear as convolutions; the Laplace transform handles initial value problems and is inverted by residues. Chapter 7 studies the heat equation through its kernel, the maximum principle and Duhamel's principle, interprets diffusion as the limit of random walks, and introduces reaction-diffusion equations. Chapter 8 studies the Laplace equation: Green's identities and the fundamental solution, the mean value property and the maximum principle, Green's functions constructed by the method of images, conformal mapping in the plane, and Dirichlet's principle, which characterizes the solution as the minimizer of an energy. Two patterns recur throughout: a solution is decomposed into modes (eigenfunctions or plane waves) that evolve independently, or it is written as an integral of the data against a kernel (the heat kernel, the Poisson kernel, a Green's function).

This subject draws on all the earlier courses in mathematics. From [calculus](/2.2) it takes the divergence theorem, which turns conservation laws into equations and yields Green's identities, and Fourier series. From [ordinary differential equations](/2.4) it takes characteristics as integral curves of a vector field, the special functions and their orthogonality, Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue problems, and the calculus of variations behind Dirichlet's principle. [Complex analysis](/2.3) supplies the contour integrals used to compute and invert transforms and conformal mapping for plane potential problems. [Linear algebra](/2.1) supplies the point of view: an eigenfunction expansion is the infinite-dimensional analogue of diagonalizing a symmetric matrix by an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. In probability ([3.1](/3.1)), the heat kernel is the normal density whose variance grows linearly in time, which is the link between diffusion and Brownian motion. In physics, the equations of [electromagnetism](/5.3) and the Schrödinger equation of [quantum physics](/5.5) are partial differential equations, and separation of variables in spherical coordinates leads to spherical harmonics. In [biology](/7), reaction-diffusion equations describe the invasion fronts of spreading populations ([Ecology](/7.19)) and the Turing patterns of morphogenesis ([Developmental Biology](/7.5)).

Partial differential equations are where models of living systems acquire space: once concentrations or populations vary across a tissue or a habitat, a system of ordinary differential equations is no longer enough. Any such model can be discretized and simulated, but I think the value of this course lies in what the analysis tells us before any simulation. It tells us whether a problem makes sense at all (the backward heat equation is ill-posed, so a simulation of it can only blow up), how fast an invasion front travels, and whether a uniform state loses stability to spatial patterns, a condition read off from a dispersion relation computed mode by mode with the Fourier transform. These are the elegant tools that go beyond brute-force simulation and give us properties that hold for whole classes of parameters. For students in computational biology and engineering, I recommend taking this course after ordinary differential equations and together with, or after, complex analysis.
