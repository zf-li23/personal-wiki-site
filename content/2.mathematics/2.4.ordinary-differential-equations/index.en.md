Ordinary differential equations are equations involving an unknown function of one variable and its derivatives. The subject has three central problems. The first is solving: which equations have solutions that can be written in terms of elementary functions and integrals. The second is existence and uniqueness: most equations cannot be solved explicitly, so we ask whether a solution exists, whether it is determined uniquely by its initial value, how far it can be continued, and how it depends on initial values and parameters. The third is to understand the properties of solutions without solving: whether an equilibrium is stable, whether solutions oscillate, and where trajectories eventually go. Chapters [2.2.15](/2.2.15) and [2.2.16](/2.2.16) of calculus have already covered, with an emphasis on computation, the elementary integration methods, linear equations and linear systems with constant coefficients. This subject does not repeat the routine methods and examples; instead it reorganizes the material as a rigorous mathematical theory, supplies proofs of the theorems that were only stated there, and treats theory and topics that calculus does not touch. It corresponds to the engineering mathematics course "Ordinary Differential Equations" taken by engineering departments, which is taught as a selection of topics. The chapters follow the skeleton of the textbook, and include material the course did not cover: power series solutions, stability and qualitative theory, and the general Sturm–Liouville theory.

The chapters follow one main line. Chapter 1 fixes the basic language and gives a geometric interpretation of first-order equations through direction fields. Chapters 2 and 3 are the elementary integration methods: exact equations and integrating factors are the common starting point, separable equations, first-order linear equations, Bernoulli equations and Riccati equations are all reduced to them by changes of variables, and the Riccati equation shows the limits of integration by quadratures. Chapters 4 and 5 are the core of the theory: Picard iteration gives local existence and uniqueness under a Lipschitz condition, Peano's theorem guarantees existence under continuity alone, the continuation theorem extends local solutions to the boundary of the domain, and comparison theorems are used to estimate the maximal interval of existence. Chapter 6 studies singular solutions, where uniqueness breaks down; geometrically they are envelopes of families of integral curves. Chapter 7 extends the results for first-order equations to systems in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and proves that solutions depend continuously and differentiably on initial values and parameters. Chapters 8 and 9 are the linear theory: the solutions of a linear system form a vector space, the variation of constants formula solves the nonhomogeneous equation, the solutions of a system with constant coefficients are given by the matrix exponential $\mathrm{e}^{tA}$, and linear equations of order $n$ are treated as a special case of systems. Chapter 10 solves equations with variable coefficients by power series and the Frobenius method, leading to Legendre polynomials and Bessel functions. Chapters 11 and 12 are qualitative theory: Lyapunov stability, linear approximation and Lyapunov functions, planar autonomous systems and limit cycles; the Sturm comparison theorem studies the zeros of solutions of second-order equations, and the Sturm–Liouville theory yields eigenvalues and expansions in orthogonal eigenfunctions. The calculus of variations in Chapter 13 reduces extremum problems for functionals to the Euler–Lagrange equation, and Chapter 14 uses first integrals to generalize the general solution of a first-order equation to higher dimensions and discusses the integrability of Pfaffian equations.

Ordinary differential equations are connected with many other subjects. Their linear theory rests on [linear algebra](/2.1): the solution space of a linear system is an instance of the theory of vector spaces, solving a system with constant coefficients reduces to the matrix exponential, computing $\mathrm{e}^{tA}$ uses the Jordan canonical form, and stability is decided by the real parts of the eigenvalues of the coefficient matrix. Power series solutions are best understood in the complex domain: the radius of convergence of a power series solution of an equation with analytic coefficients is determined by the location of the singular points, which belongs to [complex analysis](/2.3). The method of separation of variables in [partial differential equations](/2.5) reduces an equation to a family of boundary value problems for ordinary differential equations; this is exactly how the Legendre and Bessel equations arise, and the Sturm–Liouville theory justifies the eigenfunction expansions. First-order linear partial differential equations, in turn, are solved by first integrals and characteristics. In [mathematical modeling in biology](/7.4), models of populations, chemical reactions and signal transduction are almost all systems of ordinary differential equations, and stability analysis of equilibria, phase planes and limit cycles are the basic tools for understanding them (see [7.4.4](/7.4.4)).

I am particularly interested in ordinary differential equations because they are the main basic tool of mathematical modeling in biology. When we characterize the behavior of a dynamical system, basic notions such as equilibrium, stability and oscillation are all defined by the theory of ordinary differential equations; and biological systems, because of their network structure, are often quite complex systems that call for careful study. With ordinary differential equations we are able to simulate certain parts of a biological system at a fine level of detail, such as metabolic kinetics, the processes of cellular signal transduction, and changes in population size. Most of these models are nonlinear and have no closed-form solutions, so what actually answers our questions are precisely the theorems that determine the behavior of solutions without solving the equations.

Compared with the treatment in calculus, this subject is mathematically more rigorous: solutions that are taken for granted in calculus must here first be shown to exist, to be unique, and to extend to a certain interval. The cases and special situations it discusses are also far richer: uniqueness may fail and singular solutions appear, solutions may blow up in finite time, and linearization may be unable to decide stability. Together with complex analysis, ordinary differential equations also form the foundation for studying partial differential equations.
