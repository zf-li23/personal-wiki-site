The mathematics section contains five subjects: [linear algebra](/2.1), [calculus](/2.2), [complex analysis](/2.3), [ordinary differential equations](/2.4) and [partial differential equations](/2.5). The first two are foundational courses taken by almost every student of science and engineering. Linear algebra studies linear structure, and its core is linear systems and normal forms of matrices; calculus studies the local change and the global accumulation of functions, and its core is limits, derivatives and integrals. The two meet in multivariable differentiation: the differential of a function of several variables is a linear map, and classifying an extremum comes down to whether a quadratic form is positive definite. The remaining material comes from four engineering mathematics courses offered to engineering departments at Tsinghua: Selected Topics in Advanced Linear Algebra (merged into Chapters 9–13 of linear algebra), Introduction to Complex Analysis, Ordinary Differential Equations, and Introduction to Partial Differential Equations. They are oriented toward computation and taught as a selection of topics, with lower requirements than the corresponding courses for mathematics students, but they provide the analytical tools that later subjects use most often.

These four engineering mathematics courses each have their own role, and they come in a definite order. Advanced linear algebra extends the theory to general fields and complex inner product spaces and establishes the **Jordan canonical form**, matrix functions and the spectral theorem. Complex analysis carries calculus over to the complex plane and provides Cauchy's integral formula, residues, the computation of integral transforms and **conformal mappings**. Ordinary differential equations study dynamics in one variable: whether solutions exist, whether they are unique, how they depend on initial values, and whether equilibria are stable; the solutions of linear systems are given by the **matrix exponential**, which draws directly on advanced linear algebra. Partial differential equations build on all of the above: separation of variables reduces them to eigenvalue problems for ordinary differential equations, an eigenfunction expansion is the infinite-dimensional analogue of diagonalizing a symmetric matrix by an orthogonal matrix, inverting the **Fourier transform** and the **Laplace transform** uses residues from complex analysis, and **harmonic functions** in the plane are the real parts of **analytic functions**. The recommended order is therefore: first linear algebra and calculus, then complex analysis and ordinary differential equations (which can be taken in parallel), and finally partial differential equations.

These subjects are a common foundation for many later ones. In [statistics](/3), probabilities, densities and expectations are integrals; nearly every formula of multivariate statistics is written with vectors and matrices; principal component analysis is the spectral decomposition of a covariance matrix; the characteristic function of a distribution is a Fourier transform; and the fundamental solution of the heat equation is the normal density. In [computer science](/4), the models of [machine learning](/4.7) are expressed with vectors and matrices and trained by gradients and optimization. In [physics](/5), equations of motion are ordinary differential equations, and the equations of electromagnetic fields and of quantum mechanics are partial differential equations. In [biology](/7), [mathematical modeling in biology](/7.4) uses these tools directly: models of populations, chemical reactions and signal transduction are systems of ordinary differential equations ([7.4.4](/7.4.4)), and invasion fronts and Turing patterns are described by **reaction-diffusion equations** ([7.4.5](/7.4.5)).

In my view, linear algebra and calculus form the foundation for every later course, in statistics, computer science and beyond: as soon as there is more than one variable, formulas must be written with vectors and matrices, and as soon as change and accumulation are involved, derivatives and integrals are needed. The engineering mathematics courses, in turn, give us elegant tools that free us from relying solely on brute-force simulation by computer. A simulation computes one trajectory for one given set of parameters, whereas theory gives properties that hold for a whole class of parameters: whether an equilibrium is stable, whether a system oscillates, how fast a front advances, and under what conditions a uniform state loses stability and forms a spatial pattern. Living systems are complex networks, and these tools cannot fully characterize them, but they attempt to provide some valuable properties for characterizing living systems, and this is exactly what we hope to gain when we study them: knowing what to simulate, and how to interpret the results of a simulation.

Computational biology, one might say, also has a strong engineering character: it builds models, designs computations, and uses models to predict and explain experiments. For this reason I strongly recommend taking the four engineering mathematics courses: Selected Topics in Advanced Linear Algebra, Introduction to Complex Analysis, Ordinary Differential Equations, and Introduction to Partial Differential Equations.
