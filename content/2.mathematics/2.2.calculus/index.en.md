Calculus studies the local change and the global accumulation of functions. It has two central problems. The first is, given a function, to find its rate of change at a point: the derivative and the differential, which approximate a curved object locally by a linear function. The second is, given a rate of change or a density, to find the total amount: the integral, obtained by partitioning a quantity, approximating, summing and passing to the limit. Both rest on the notion of limit, and the fundamental theorem of calculus shows that they are inverse operations. This subject combines two courses: the first nine chapters correspond to Calculus B1, covering single-variable calculus and series; Chapters 10–16 correspond to Calculus B2, covering multivariable calculus and ordinary differential equations. Some engineering departments take Calculus A1 and A2 instead, where A1 covers single-variable calculus and ordinary differential equations and A2 covers series and multivariable calculus. The two levels cover almost the same material; the A-level examinations are harder and contain more proofs, while the B level places more weight on computation. This subject follows the B-level order, which is also the chapter order of the textbook: series come right after single-variable integration, and ordinary differential equations come last, where they can draw directly on the tools of multivariable calculus and linear algebra.

The chapters follow one main line. Chapter 1 reviews real numbers and functions; the supremum principle expresses the fact that the real numbers have "no gaps" and is the source of every existence theorem for limits. Chapters 2 and 3 develop limits of sequences and functions in the $\varepsilon$-$N$ language and use the completeness of the real numbers to prove the basic properties of continuous functions on closed intervals. Chapters 4 and 5 are differential calculus: the definition of the derivative and the rules of differentiation make it possible to differentiate every elementary function mechanically, the mean value theorems connect the local information carried by derivatives with the global behavior of function values, and Taylor's formula pushes linear approximation to arbitrary order. Chapters 6 and 7 are integral calculus: the indefinite integral is the inverse of differentiation and has no general algorithm; the definite integral arises from computing areas, masses and work, the fundamental theorem of calculus reduces it to finding antiderivatives, and improper integrals extend integration to infinite intervals and unbounded functions. Chapters 8 and 9 extend summation to infinitely many terms: the convergence tests for series of nonnegative terms correspond almost one to one with those for improper integrals, the key notion for series of functions is uniform convergence, and power series and Fourier series represent functions by polynomials and by trigonometric functions, respectively. Chapters 10–14 carry the theory over to several variables: the differential of a multivariable function is a linear map whose matrix is the Jacobian matrix, and the type of an extremum is decided by whether the Hessian matrix is positive definite; multiple integrals, line integrals and surface integrals all follow the same scheme of partitioning, approximating, summing and passing to the limit, and are computed as iterated integrals or through parametrizations; the theorems of Green, Gauss and Stokes all state that "the integral of a derivative over a region equals an integral over its boundary", generalizing the fundamental theorem of calculus to higher dimensions, and the gradient, divergence and curl provide a classification of vector fields. Chapters 15 and 16 are ordinary differential equations: the elementary integration methods for first-order equations are organized around changes of variables, the solution set of a linear equation is a vector space, solving an equation with constant coefficients reduces to finding the roots of its characteristic equation, and the solutions of a linear system are given by the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of its coefficient matrix. Only the ordinary differential equations of the calculus courses are covered here; the general theory, including existence, continuation and stability of solutions, will be developed in detail in [Ordinary Differential Equations](/2.4).

Calculus is the common language of many later subjects. It is closely intertwined with [linear algebra](/2.1): multivariable differentiation is local linearization, the second-derivative test uses quadratic forms, the structure of the solutions of linear differential equations is the theory of vector spaces, and solving systems with constant coefficients uses eigenvalues and the matrix exponential. In [probability theory](/3.1), distribution functions, densities and expectations are all integrals; joint distributions and the distributions of functions of random vectors are computed with multiple integrals; the Gamma and Beta distributions are built on the Gamma and Beta functions of this subject; and the various modes of convergence of sequences of random variables rest on limits of sequences. Power series and Green's theorem are the starting points of Taylor expansion and of Cauchy's integral theorem in [complex analysis](/2.3). In physics, the equations of motion of [mechanics](/5.1) are ordinary differential equations, and the integral and differential forms of Maxwell's equations in [electromagnetism](/5.3) are converted into each other precisely by the theorems of Gauss and Stokes; ordinary differential equations in biology appear in [Systems Biology](/7.9).

Compared with linear algebra, I find calculus a subject that is not so easily standardized. Most problems in linear algebra have fixed algorithms, and every step from elimination to diagonalization can be written as a definite procedure; calculus, by contrast, studies situations closer to the real world, without an idealized setting such as linearity, and deals with functions of endlessly varied forms. Differentiation can still be carried out mechanically, but finding antiderivatives cannot: a tiny change in the form of a function can make the difficulty of integrating it differ enormously, and the antiderivative may not even be expressible in terms of elementary functions. So it is not only proofs: many routine computations also depend on choosing the right substitution or spotting the right differential, and such flashes of inspiration are hard to reduce to fixed rules in advance.

Yet even if one does not aim to be able to compute every problem, mastering the ideas of calculus (describing infinite processes by limits, treating local behavior by linear approximation, and treating global quantities by partitioning, summing and passing to the limit), together with its notation and a few basic computational methods, already provides the foundation for understanding probability theory and physics, subjects that are closer to the real world. The laws of these subjects are almost all written in terms of derivatives and integrals.
