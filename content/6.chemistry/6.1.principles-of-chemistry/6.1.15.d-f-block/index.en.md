The d-block elements (Groups 3–12) form the large block of metals in the middle of the periodic table, and the f-block elements (the lanthanoids and actinoids) are the two rows inserted into the sixth and seventh periods. Unlike those of the s and p blocks, their additional electrons enter the penultimate $(n-1)\mathrm{d}$ subshell or the antepenultimate $(n-2)\mathrm{f}$ subshell while the outermost shell hardly changes, so the properties change much more gradually across a period; the d and f electrons also bring properties rare among the main-group elements: several oxidation states and a rich redox chemistry, colored compounds, paramagnetism and a very strong tendency to form complexes. This chapter first discusses the general features of the d block (Section 15.1), then introduces the representative elements of the first transition series group by group (Sections 15.2–15.8), and finally the lanthanoids (Section 15.9). Three groups of tools from the earlier chapters are used here extensively: crystal field theory of complexes ([6.1.5](/6.1.5)) explains colors, magnetism and the stability of certain oxidation states; Latimer and Frost diagrams ([6.1.12](/6.1.12)) summarize redox behavior; and coordination and precipitation equilibria ([6.1.9](/6.1.9), [6.1.10](/6.1.10)) explain the identification and separation of ions. The magnetism of the lanthanoids requires the term symbols of atoms ([5.5.4](/5.5.4)). The bioinorganic chemistry of metalloenzymes, hemoglobin and so on is covered in chemical biology ([6.6](/6.6)) and biochemistry ([7.1](/7.1)), and the bonding theories of coordination compounds in [Structural Chemistry](/6.2).

## 15.1. General Features of the d-Block Elements

### 15.1.1. Valence Configurations and Physical Properties

The valence configuration of the d-block elements is $(n-1)\mathrm{d}^{1\text{–}10}\,n\mathrm{s}^{1\text{–}2}$ (Pd is $4\mathrm{d}^{10}\,5\mathrm{s}^0$). By the IUPAC definition, an element whose atom or common ions have an incompletely filled d subshell is a **transition element** ([6.1.2](/6.1.2)); Zn, Cd and Hg of Group 12 ($\mathrm{d}^{10}$) belong to the d block but are not transition elements in the strict sense.

The d-block elements are all metals and, compared with the main-group elements, have the following characteristics:

- Most of the metals of high melting and boiling points, hardness and density are concentrated in this block. The $(n-1)$d electrons also take part in metallic bonding; within a period the number of electrons involved in bonding is largest near Group 6, where the melting point reaches its maximum (panel b of the figure below). The metal with the highest melting point is tungsten ($3422\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), the hardest is chromium (Mohs hardness about 8.5) and the densest is osmium ($22.59\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$; iridium, $22.56$, is almost the same).
- They are good electrical and thermal conductors and are ductile and malleable; Ag, Cu and Au are the best conductors.
- Many of the elements have several oxidation states and a rich redox chemistry (Section 15.1.3).
- Most compounds are colored, and many are paramagnetic (Section 15.1.4).
- They have a strong tendency to form complexes, both classical complexes and organometallic compounds with ligands such as CO and alkenes ([6.1.5](/6.1.5)).

### 15.1.2. Atomic Radii and the Lanthanide Contraction

From left to right across a period the atomic radii and the radii of ions of the same charge of the d-block elements generally decrease, but only slightly (the added d electrons are in the penultimate shell and screen the outermost electrons strongly, [6.1.2](/6.1.2#sec-2.4.1)), and the radii increase slightly near $\mathrm{d}^{10}$. Down a group the radius increases from the fourth to the fifth period, whereas the fifth and sixth periods are almost equal; this is the result of the **lanthanide contraction** ([Definition 2.4.2](/6.1.2#def-2.4.2)): the metallic radii of Zr and Hf are $160$ and $159\,\mathrm{pm}$, those of Nb and Ta are both $146\,\mathrm{pm}$, and those of Mo and W are also almost the same.

Two consequences follow. First, the elements of the second and third transition series in the same group, and their compounds, have similar structures and extremely similar properties; they occur together in nature and are very difficult to separate. Hf was not discovered in zirconium ores until 1923, and the zirconium used as fuel cladding in nuclear reactors must be freed of Hf to a very low level (Zr barely absorbs neutrons, Hf absorbs them strongly). Second, the atomic radii of the third transition series equal those of the second while the masses are much larger, so the densities of Os, Ir and Pt ($21$–$22.6\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$) are far greater than those of Ru, Rh and Pd ($12$–$12.4$).

### 15.1.3. Oxidation States

![(a) Oxidation states of the 3d elements: large filled circles are the most common states, small open circles less common ones. (b) Melting points of the 3d, 4d and 5d metals against group number](./plots/d-block-oxidation-states.py)

The oxidation states of the first transition series follow these rules (panel a of the figure above):

1. The highest oxidation state of the elements of Groups 3–7 equals the group number ($\ce{Sc^3+}$, $\ce{TiO2}$, $\ce{V2O5}$, $\ce{CrO4^2-}$, $\ce{MnO4-}$), that is, all the valence electrons can take part in bonding; after Group 8 the highest oxidation state is lower than the group number (Fe up to +6, Co and Ni up to +4).
2. From left to right across a period the ability to form high oxidation states decreases: with more than five d electrons it is unlikely that all of them take part in bonding, compounds of high oxidation state are extremely strong oxidizing agents ($\ce{FeO4^2-}$), and +2 gradually becomes the most common oxidation state.
3. Down a group the high oxidation states become more and more stable, exactly opposite to the inert-pair effect of the p block: Cr(VI) is a strong oxidizing agent, whereas Mo(VI) and W(VI) are very stable; Fe reaches only +6 whereas Os forms $\ce{OsO4}$ (+8); Ni(IV) is rare whereas Pt(IV) is common. The reason is that the 4d and 5d orbitals extend farther and bond more strongly with the ligands.
4. In **metal carbonyls** such as $\ce{Ni(CO)4}$ and $\ce{Fe(CO)5}$, the metal can be in the oxidation state zero or even negative.

### 15.1.4. Color and Coordination Ability

The ions and compounds of the d-block elements are mostly colored, for two reasons. One is d–d transitions: the d orbitals split in the ligand field (**crystal field theory**), and electrons make transitions between the split d orbitals and absorb visible light ([6.1.5](/6.1.5#sec-5.5.7)); ions with $\mathrm{d}^0$ and $\mathrm{d}^{10}$ configurations ($\ce{Sc^3+}$, $\ce{Ti^4+}$, $\ce{Zn^2+}$, $\ce{Cu+}$, $\ce{Ag+}$) have no d–d transitions and are usually colorless. The other is charge-transfer transitions, in which an electron is transferred from a ligand (such as $\ce{O^2-}$) to the metal; the colors of the $\mathrm{d}^0$ ions $\ce{MnO4-}$ (purple), $\ce{CrO4^2-}$ (yellow), $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ (orange) and of $\ce{V2O5}$ (orange-red) come from such transitions, whose absorption is much stronger than that of d–d transitions.

| Ion (hydrated) | $\ce{Ti^3+}$ | $\ce{V^3+}$ | $\ce{V^2+}$ | $\ce{Cr^3+}$ | $\ce{Cr^2+}$ | $\ce{Mn^2+}$ | $\ce{Fe^2+}$ | $\ce{Fe^3+}$ | $\ce{Co^2+}$ | $\ce{Ni^2+}$ | $\ce{Cu^2+}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of d electrons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 5 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
| Color | violet | green | violet | violet (green with $\ce{Cl-}$) | blue | pale pink | pale green | pale violet (yellow after hydrolysis) | pink | green | blue |

The colors of high-spin $\mathrm{d}^5$ $\ce{Mn^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$ are very pale, because all their d–d transitions require a change of electron spin, are "spin-forbidden" and absorb extremely weakly. The ions of the d block have empty $(n-1)\mathrm{d}$, $n\mathrm{s}$ and $n\mathrm{p}$ orbitals, small radii and high charges and are strongly polarizing, hence their strong ability to form complexes; compounds with unpaired d electrons are paramagnetic, and the number of unpaired electrons can be obtained from the **effective magnetic moment** ([6.1.5](/6.1.5#sec-5.3)).

### 15.1.5. Redox Behavior from Frost Diagrams

The figure below shows the **Frost diagrams** of the first-row transition elements in acidic solution (pH 0); for how to read them see [6.1.12](/6.1.12) and [6.1.14](/6.1.14#sec-14.1.3). The dashed line has the slope of the $\ce{O2}/\ce{H2O}$ couple ($1.229\,\mathrm{V}$): if the line segment of a couple is steeper than it, the species of higher oxidation state can (thermodynamically) oxidize water to $\ce{O2}$; if the slope of the segment is negative and below the slope of $\ce{H+}/\ce{H2}$ (zero), the species of lower oxidation state can reduce $\ce{H+}$ to $\ce{H2}$.

![Frost diagrams of the first-row transition elements at pH 0 (E° from the CRC Handbook; Mn as in 6.1.12). The dashed line has the slope of the O2/H2O couple](./plots/d-block-frost.py)

The following can be read from the figure:

- The most stable oxidation state of each element (the lowest point): V(III), Cr(III), Mn(II), Fe(II), Co(II), Ni(II) and Zn(II); the lowest point of Cu is the element, so Cu does not dissolve in nonoxidizing acids.
- From left to right the species of high oxidation state "rise" higher and higher: $\ce{VO2+}$ is only a moderate oxidizing agent, $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ and $\ce{MnO4-}$ are strong oxidizing agents, and $\ce{FeO4^2-}$, $\ce{Co^3+}$ and $\ce{NiO2}$ can oxidize water and are unstable in water.
- The segments of the low oxidation states $\ce{V^2+}$ and $\ce{Cr^2+}$ have negative slopes, and these are strong reducing agents that can reduce water.
- $\ce{Mn^3+}$ and $\ce{MnO4^2-}$ lie above the lines joining their neighbors and disproportionate in acid; $\ce{Cu+}$ lies above the line joining Cu and $\ce{Cu^2+}$ and disproportionates in aqueous solution (Section 15.7).

## 15.2. The Titanium Group

Titanium (Ti), zirconium (Zr) and hafnium (Hf) are in Group 4 (Group IVB in older Chinese textbooks), with the valence configuration $(n-1)\mathrm{d}^2\,n\mathrm{s}^2$; the most stable oxidation state is +4. Titanium has a low density ($4.51\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$) and high strength, is covered with a dense passivating film of $\ce{TiO2}$, resists corrosion and is compatible with human tissue; it is widely used in aerospace, chemical equipment and artificial joints. Ti(III) is a reducing agent ($E^\circ(\ce{TiO^2+}/\ce{Ti^3+}) \approx 0.1\,\mathrm{V}$), and violet $\ce{[Ti(H2O)6]^3+}$ is a classic example of the d–d transition of a $\mathrm{d}^1$ ion ([6.1.5](/6.1.5)).

$\ce{TiO2}$ has three crystalline forms at ordinary temperature: rutile, anatase and brookite. The most important, rutile, belongs to the tetragonal system, with coordination number 6 for Ti and 3 for O (the rutile structure, [6.1.4](/6.1.4#sec-4.3.3)). It does not dissolve in water or dilute acids but dissolves in hot concentrated sulfuric acid and in hydrofluoric acid, and fusion with alkaline earth carbonates gives titanates:

$$
\ce{TiO2 + H2SO4 -> TiOSO4 + H2O}, \qquad \ce{TiO2 + 6HF -> H2[TiF6] + 2H2O}, \qquad \ce{TiO2 + BaCO3 ->[\text{molten}] BaTiO3 + CO2}.
$$
Barium titanate is a ferroelectric material of the perovskite type and is used to make capacitors and piezoelectric elements.

There are two industrial processes for $\ce{TiO2}$:

| | Sulfate process | Chloride process |
|---|---|---|
| Raw material | ilmenite $\ce{FeTiO3}$ | rutile or high-titanium slag |
| Reactions | $\ce{FeTiO3 + 2H2SO4 -> TiOSO4 + FeSO4 + 2H2O}$; $\ce{TiOSO4 + 2H2O -> TiO2*H2O v + H2SO4}$ (hydrolysis), followed by calcination | $\ce{TiO2 + 2C + 2Cl2 ->[\Delta] TiCl4 + 2CO}$; after purification by distillation, gas-phase oxidation $\ce{TiCl4 + O2 -> TiO2 + 2Cl2}$ ($\ce{Cl2}$ is recycled) |
| Characteristics | long process, produces large amounts of waste acid and iron(II) sulfate | pure product, little waste, now the mainstream process |

Pure $\ce{TiO2}$, commonly called titanium white, has a high refractive index (about 2.7 for rutile) and strong hiding power and is nontoxic; it is the most important white pigment, has replaced toxic white lead, and is also used as a filler and a matting agent. Anatase $\ce{TiO2}$ is a semiconductor (band gap about $3.2\,\mathrm{eV}$); it absorbs ultraviolet light to produce electrons and holes and can photocatalytically decompose organic compounds and water.

$\ce{TiCl4}$ is a colorless liquid (melting point $-24\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, boiling point $136\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) with a pungent odor and is a covalent compound. It hydrolyzes very readily and fumes in moist air, which can be used to make smoke screens: $\ce{TiCl4 + 2H2O -> TiO2 + 4HCl}$. $\ce{TiCl4}$ can be reduced to $\ce{TiCl3}$ ($\ce{2TiCl4 + H2 -> 2TiCl3 + 2HCl}$, $\ce{2TiCl4 + Zn -> 2TiCl3 + ZnCl2}$); industrially it is reduced to metallic titanium by magnesium in an inert atmosphere (the Kroll process): $\ce{TiCl4 + 2Mg ->[\Delta] Ti + 2MgCl2}$.

## 15.3. The Vanadium Group

Vanadium (V), niobium (Nb) and tantalum (Ta) are in Group 5 (Group VB in older Chinese textbooks), with the valence configuration $(n-1)\mathrm{d}^3\,n\mathrm{s}^2$ and the highest oxidation state +5; they are rare elements. Vanadium is used mainly to make vanadium steel (a small amount of V greatly raises the strength and toughness of steel).

The Latimer diagram of vanadium in acidic medium (V):

$$
\ce{VO2+ ->[0.991] VO^2+ ->[0.337] V^3+ ->[-0.255] V^2+ ->[-1.175] V}.
$$
The ions of the various oxidation states have characteristic colors and can be interconverted with suitable oxidizing and reducing agents:

| Oxidation state | +5 | +4 | +3 | +2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Species | $\ce{VO2+}$ | $\ce{VO^2+}$ (vanadyl ion) | $\ce{V^3+}$ | $\ce{V^2+}$ |
| Color | pale yellow | blue | green | violet |
| Reducing agent used to reach the next lower state | $\ce{Fe^2+}$, oxalic acid | $\ce{Ti^3+}$ | Zn | — |

When zinc granules are added to an acidic solution of a vanadate, the color changes yellow → blue → green → violet are observed in turn (Zn, $E^\circ = -0.762\,\mathrm{V}$, can reduce as far as $\ce{V^2+}$); conversely, $\ce{MnO4-}$ oxidizes them stepwise back to $\ce{VO2+}$.

**Example 15.3.1** Why can $\ce{Fe^2+}$ reduce $\ce{VO2+}$ only to $\ce{VO^2+}$? What use is this?

Solution. $E^\circ(\ce{Fe^3+}/\ce{Fe^2+}) = 0.771\,\mathrm{V}$ lies between $E^\circ(\ce{VO2+}/\ce{VO^2+}) = 0.991\,\mathrm{V}$ and $E^\circ(\ce{VO^2+}/\ce{V^3+}) = 0.337\,\mathrm{V}$, so $\ce{VO2+ + Fe^2+ + 2H+ -> VO^2+ + Fe^3+ + H2O}$ can proceed, whereas $\ce{VO^2+}$ is not reduced further. The reaction stops quantitatively at V(IV), so vanadium can be titrated with a standard $\ce{Fe^2+}$ solution (or V(IV) can be titrated back to V(V) with $\ce{KMnO4}$); this is the redox volumetric analysis of vanadium ([Analytical Chemistry](/6.4)). Oxalic acid acts in the same way: $\ce{2VO2+ + H2C2O4 + 2H+ ->[\Delta] 2VO^2+ + 2CO2 ^ + 2H2O}$. $\square$

Vanadium pentoxide $\ce{V2O5}$ is an orange-red powder ($\mathrm{d}^0$, the color arises from charge transfer), toxic and slightly soluble in water. V(V) has no d electrons and $\ce{V2O5}$ is a **diamagnetic substance**, whereas VO, $\ce{V2O3}$ and $\ce{VO2}$ have unpaired d electrons and are **paramagnetic substances**. $\ce{V2O5}$ is an amphoteric oxide that is mainly acidic and has some oxidizing power:

$$
\ce{V2O5 + 6NaOH -> 2Na3VO4 + 3H2O}, \qquad \ce{V2O5 + H2SO4 -> (VO2)2SO4 + H2O}, \qquad \ce{V2O5 + 6HCl -> 2VOCl2 + Cl2 ^ + 3H2O}.
$$
$\ce{V2O5}$ is the catalyst for the contact process for sulfuric acid ($\ce{2SO2 + O2 <=> 2SO3}$, about $420$–$600\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, **heterogeneous catalysis**, [6.1.7](/6.1.7)): $\ce{V2O5}$ first oxidizes $\ce{SO2}$ and is itself reduced to V(IV), which is then oxidized back to V(V) by $\ce{O2}$, so that vanadium cycles between the two oxidation states.

Vanadates and polyvanadates. The vanadates include metavanadates $\ce{M^IVO3}$ and orthovanadates $\ce{M^I3VO4}$. The simple orthovanadate ion $\ce{VO4^3-}$ exists only in strongly basic solution. When acid is added to an orthovanadate solution, as the pH falls $\ce{VO4^3-}$ is protonated stepwise and condenses with loss of water to polyvanadate ions of increasing degree of polymerization (isopolyanions, [6.1.14](/6.1.14#sec-14.1.4)); the atomic ratio V : O becomes larger and larger, and in strong acid $\ce{VO2+}$ is finally formed:

| pH (approx.) | ≥ 13 | 12–10.6 | about 9 | 8–3 | about 2 | < 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main species | $\ce{VO4^3-}$ | $\ce{V2O7^4-}$ | $\ce{V3O9^3-}$ | $\ce{V10O28^6-}$ (orange) | $\ce{V2O5}\cdot x\ce{H2O}$ precipitate | $\ce{VO2+}$ |
| V : O | 1 : 4 | 1 : 3.5 | 1 : 3 | 1 : 2.8 | 1 : 2.5 | 1 : 2 |

This "acidification–condensation" is a common property of the oxoanions of V, Cr, Mo and W.

## 15.4. The Chromium Group

Chromium (Cr), molybdenum (Mo) and tungsten (W) are in Group 6 (Group VIB in older Chinese textbooks); the valence configuration is $(n-1)\mathrm{d}^5\,n\mathrm{s}^1$ for Cr and Mo and $5\mathrm{d}^4\,6\mathrm{s}^2$ for W. Chromium is hard and corrosion-resistant and is used for electroplating and for making stainless steel (with more than 10.5% Cr, a dense **passivation** film of $\ce{Cr2O3}$ forms on the surface).

The Latimer diagram of chromium (V):

$$
\text{acidic: }\ \ce{Cr2O7^2- ->[1.36] Cr^3+ ->[-0.407] Cr^2+ ->[-0.913] Cr}, \qquad E^\circ(\ce{Cr^3+}/\ce{Cr}) = -0.744\,\mathrm{V};
$$

$$
\text{basic: }\ \ce{CrO4^2- ->[-0.13] Cr(OH)3 ->[-1.1] Cr(OH)2 ->[-1.4] Cr}.
$$
The common oxidation states are +6 ($\ce{CrO3}$ dark red, $\ce{CrO4^2-}$ yellow, $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ orange), +3 ($\ce{Cr^3+}$; $\ce{Cr2O3}$ green; $\ce{Cr(OH)3}$ gray-green and amphoteric) and +2 ($\ce{Cr^2+}$ blue, a strong reducing agent).

### 15.4.1. Chromate and Dichromate

Potassium dichromate $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ forms orange-red crystals. In aqueous solution there is an equilibrium between yellow chromate and orange-red dichromate:

$$
\ce{2CrO4^2- + 2H+ <=> Cr2O7^2- + H2O}, \qquad K^\circ = 1.2\times10^{14}.
$$
More precisely, it comprises two steps: $\ce{CrO4^2- + H+ <=> HCrO4-}$ ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} \approx 6.5$) and $\ce{2HCrO4- <=> Cr2O7^2- + H2O}$ ($K \approx 12$). Acid shifts the equilibrium to the right and the solution changes from yellow to orange; base shifts it to the left. Because the second step is a dimerization, the distribution of Cr(VI) depends not only on pH but also on the total concentration (figure below): above pH 8 it is almost entirely $\ce{CrO4^2-}$; in acidic solution, at $0.1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ and $\ce{HCrO4-}$ account for about half each, whereas at $10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ the main species is $\ce{HCrO4-}$. The distribution is calculated in the same way as the distribution coefficients of [6.1.9](/6.1.9), except that the mass balance contains an additional quadratic term.

![Fractions of Cr(VI) present as CrO4^2−, HCrO4^− and Cr2O7^2− (counted as two Cr) against pH, for total chromium 0.10 mol/L (solid) and 0.0010 mol/L (dashed); pKa(HCrO4^−) = 6.5, overall K = 1.2 × 10^14](./plots/chromate-distribution.py)

Chromates are generally less soluble than dichromates ($K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\ce{Ag2CrO4}) = 1.1\times10^{-12}$, $K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\ce{Ag2Cr2O7}) = 2.0\times10^{-7}$), so when $\ce{Ag+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$ or $\ce{Pb^2+}$ is added to a dichromate solution, chromates precipitate (the precipitation shifts the above equilibrium to the left):

$$
\ce{4Ag+ + Cr2O7^2- + H2O -> 2Ag2CrO4(s, brick red) + 2H+},
$$

$$
\ce{2Ba^2+ + Cr2O7^2- + H2O -> 2BaCrO4(s, lemon yellow) + 2H+}, \qquad \ce{2Pb^2+ + Cr2O7^2- + H2O -> 2PbCrO4(s, yellow) + 2H+}.
$$
$\ce{PbCrO4}$ is the pigment "chrome yellow"; the formation of $\ce{Ag2CrO4}$ indicates the end point in the Mohr method for determining $\ce{Cl-}$ ([Analytical Chemistry](/6.4)).

### 15.4.2. Interconversion of Cr(III) and Cr(VI)

From the Latimer diagram, Cr(III) is very difficult to oxidize in acid ($E^\circ(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}/\ce{Cr^3+}) = 1.36\,\mathrm{V}$) but much easier in base ($E^\circ(\ce{CrO4^2-}/\ce{Cr(OH)3}) = -0.13\,\mathrm{V}$). Conversely, Cr(VI) is a strong oxidizing agent in acid:

| Conversion | Reaction | Remark |
|---|---|---|
| Cr(III) → Cr(VI), acidic | $\ce{2Cr^3+ + 3S2O8^2- + 7H2O ->[Ag+] Cr2O7^2- + 6SO4^2- + 14H+}$ | requires the strongest oxidizing agents |
| Cr(III) → Cr(VI), basic | $\ce{2[Cr(OH)4]- + 3H2O2 + 2OH- -> 2CrO4^2- + 8H2O}$ | $\ce{H2O2}$ suffices; identification of Cr(III) |
| Cr(III) → Cr(II) | $\ce{2Cr^3+ + Zn -> 2Cr^2+ (blue) + Zn^2+}$ | air must be excluded |
| Cr(VI) → Cr(III) | $\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 3SO3^2- + 8H+ -> 2Cr^3+ + 3SO4^2- + 4H2O}$ | treatment of chromium-containing wastewater |
| | $\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 3H2S + 8H+ -> 2Cr^3+ + 3S v + 7H2O}$ | |
| | $\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 6I- + 14H+ -> 2Cr^3+ + 3I2 + 7H2O}$ | iodometry |
| | $\ce{K2Cr2O7 + 14HCl(conc) -> 2KCl + 2CrCl3 + 3Cl2 ^ + 7H2O}$ | reacts only with concentrated hydrochloric acid ($1.36 \approx 1.358\,\mathrm{V}$) |
| | $\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 6Fe^2+ + 14H+ -> 2Cr^3+ + 6Fe^3+ + 7H2O}$ | determination of iron by dichromate; $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ is a **primary standard** |
| | $\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 3Sn^2+ + 14H+ -> 2Cr^3+ + 3Sn^4+ + 7H2O}$ | |

**Example 15.4.1** Compare the standard electrode potentials of the $\ce{M^3+}/\ce{M^2+}$ couples of the first transition series and explain why $\ce{Cr^3+}$ both gains and loses electrons with difficulty.

| Couple | $\ce{V^3+}/\ce{V^2+}$ | $\ce{Cr^3+}/\ce{Cr^2+}$ | $\ce{Mn^3+}/\ce{Mn^2+}$ | $\ce{Fe^3+}/\ce{Fe^2+}$ | $\ce{Co^3+}/\ce{Co^2+}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $E^\circ$ / V | −0.255 | −0.407 | 1.51 | 0.771 | 1.92 |
| d electrons of $\ce{M^3+}$ | $\mathrm{d}^2$ | $\mathrm{d}^3$ | $\mathrm{d}^4$ | $\mathrm{d}^5$ | $\mathrm{d}^6$ |

Solution. The more negative $E^\circ$ is, the more difficult it is to reduce $\ce{M^3+}$. The value for $\ce{Cr^3+}$ is the most negative, so it gains electrons with the greatest difficulty; at the same time, oxidizing Cr(III) to Cr(VI) in acid also requires $1.36\,\mathrm{V}$. The reason is that the $\mathrm{d}^3$ configuration of $\ce{Cr^3+}$ is $t_{2g}^3$ in an octahedral field: each of the three electrons occupies one $t_{2g}$ orbital, and the **crystal field stabilization energy** is $-1.2\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}$ ($-12\,Dq$), the largest of the high-spin configurations (the same as for $\mathrm{d}^8$). Adding an electron requires entering an $e_g$ orbital and removing one loses stabilization energy, so $\ce{Cr^3+}$ is particularly stable; it is also kinetically inert, with very slow ligand exchange ([6.1.5](/6.1.5#sec-5.5.6)). In contrast, $\ce{Ti^3+}$ and $\ce{V^3+}$ lose electrons easily and are reducing agents; $\ce{Mn^3+}$ (becoming the half-filled $\mathrm{d}^5$ is very favorable) and $\ce{Co^3+}$ gain electrons easily and are oxidizing agents, and $\ce{Mn^3+}$ and $\ce{Co^3+}$ can even oxidize water. $\square$

A mixture of saturated potassium dichromate solution and concentrated sulfuric acid is called chromic acid cleaning solution; it is strongly oxidizing and was formerly used to clean glassware in laboratories. Cr(VI) compounds are toxic and carcinogenic and pollute the environment, so chromic acid cleaning solution is no longer recommended; alkaline cleaning solutions, persulfate cleaning solutions or special detergents are used instead, and wastewater containing Cr(VI) must first be reduced to Cr(III) and then precipitated.

The +6 oxidation state of molybdenum and tungsten is very stable, and $\ce{MoO3}$ and $\ce{WO3}$ are only weakly oxidizing. Their oxoanions condense in acid to isopolyanions ($\ce{[Mo7O24]^6-}$ and so on) and heteropolyanions ($\ce{[PMo12O40]^3-}$, [6.1.14](/6.1.14#sec-14.1.4)), which is characteristic of the chemistry of molybdenum and tungsten.

## 15.5. The Manganese Group

Manganese (Mn), technetium (Tc) and rhenium (Re) are in Group 7 (Group VIIB in older Chinese textbooks). The valence configuration of Mn is $3\mathrm{d}^5\,4\mathrm{s}^2$, and all the oxidation states from +2 to +7 occur. The Latimer diagram of manganese (V; for the Frost diagram see [6.1.12](/6.1.12)):

$$
\text{acidic: }\ \ce{MnO4- ->[0.56] MnO4^2- ->[2.26] MnO2 ->[0.95] Mn^3+ ->[1.51] Mn^2+ ->[-1.18] Mn}, \qquad E^\circ(\ce{MnO4-}/\ce{Mn^2+}) = 1.51,\ E^\circ(\ce{MnO2}/\ce{Mn^2+}) = 1.23;
$$

$$
\text{basic: }\ \ce{MnO4- ->[0.56] MnO4^2- ->[0.60] MnO2 ->[-0.20] Mn(OH)3 ->[0.10] Mn(OH)2 ->[-1.56] Mn}.
$$
From it, disproportionation and comproportionation can be judged:

$$
\ce{3MnO4^2- + 4H+ -> 2MnO4- + MnO2 v + 2H2O}, \qquad \ce{2Mn^3+ + 2H2O -> Mn^2+ + MnO2 v + 4H+},
$$

$$
\ce{2MnO4- + 3Mn^2+ + 2H2O -> 5MnO2 v + 4H+}.
$$
The first two are **disproportionation** reactions (for $\ce{MnO4^2-}$ and $\ce{Mn^3+}$ the potential on the right is larger than that on the left), and the third is a **comproportionation** reaction ($\ce{MnO2}$ lies below the line joining $\ce{MnO4-}$ and $\ce{Mn^2+}$). Hence, in titrations with $\ce{KMnO4}$, a local excess of titrant gives brown $\ce{MnO2}$.

### 15.5.1. Compounds of Mn(II)

Mn(II) is the most stable oxidation state of manganese in acid (half-filled $\mathrm{d}^5$). Among the Mn(II) salts, apart from $\ce{MnCO3}$, $\ce{Mn3(PO4)2}$, MnS (flesh-colored) and $\ce{MnC2O4}$, which are sparingly soluble, the salts of strong acids are all soluble in water. Under basic conditions the reducing power of Mn(II) is pronounced: white $\ce{Mn(OH)2}$ is rapidly oxidized by air to brown $\ce{MnO(OH)2}$:

$$
\ce{2Mn(OH)2 + O2 -> 2MnO(OH)2}.
$$
This reaction is the basis of the Winkler method for determining dissolved oxygen in water. Under acidic conditions only very strong oxidizing agents can oxidize $\ce{Mn^2+}$ to purple-red $\ce{MnO4-}$, which is used to identify $\ce{Mn^2+}$ ($\ce{Mn^2+}$ must be present in small amount, otherwise the comproportionation above occurs):

| Oxidizing agent | Reaction |
|---|---|
| $\ce{NaBiO3}$ ($\ce{HNO3}$ medium) | $\ce{2Mn^2+ + 5NaBiO3 + 14H+ -> 2MnO4- + 5Bi^3+ + 5Na+ + 7H2O}$ |
| $\ce{S2O8^2-}$ ($\ce{Ag+}$ catalysis) | $\ce{2Mn^2+ + 5S2O8^2- + 8H2O -> 2MnO4- + 10SO4^2- + 16H+}$ |
| $\ce{H5IO6}$ | $\ce{2Mn^2+ + 5H5IO6 -> 2MnO4- + 5IO3- + 11H+ + 7H2O}$ |
| $\ce{PbO2}$ | $\ce{2Mn^2+ + 5PbO2 + 4H+ -> 2MnO4- + 5Pb^2+ + 2H2O}$ |

All four oxidizing agents are among the strongest of the p block or contain peroxide bonds; the first two reflect the inert-pair effect of Bi(V) and the strong oxidizing power of the peroxide bond ([6.1.14](/6.1.14)).

### 15.5.2. Compounds of Mn(IV)

$\ce{MnO2}$ is a black powder that is insoluble in water and dilute acids. It is in an intermediate oxidation state: it is a strong oxidizing agent in acidic medium ($E^\circ(\ce{MnO2}/\ce{Mn^2+}) = 1.23\,\mathrm{V}$), and in basic medium in the presence of an oxidizing agent and on heating it can be oxidized to manganate ($E^\circ_{\mathrm{B}}(\ce{MnO4^2-}/\ce{MnO2}) = 0.60\,\mathrm{V}$):

$$
\ce{MnO2 + 4HCl(conc) ->[\Delta] MnCl2 + Cl2 ^ + 2H2O}, \qquad \ce{2MnO2 + 2H2SO4(conc) ->[\Delta] 2MnSO4 + O2 ^ + 2H2O};
$$

$$
\ce{2MnO2 + 4KOH + O2 ->[\text{molten}] 2K2MnO4 + 2H2O}, \qquad \ce{3MnO2 + 6KOH + KClO3 ->[\text{molten}] 3K2MnO4 + KCl + 3H2O}.
$$
The first reaction is the laboratory method for preparing chlorine: $E^\circ(\ce{MnO2}/\ce{Mn^2+}) = 1.23\,\mathrm{V}$ is smaller than $E^\circ(\ce{Cl2}/\ce{Cl-}) = 1.36\,\mathrm{V}$, so concentrated hydrochloric acid and heating are required (Nernst equation). $\ce{MnO2}$ is also the catalyst for the decomposition of $\ce{H2O2}$ and $\ce{KClO3}$ and the positive electrode material of zinc–manganese batteries ([6.1.12](/6.1.12)).

### 15.5.3. Compounds of Mn(VI) and Mn(VII)

Potassium manganate $\ce{K2MnO4}$ forms dark green crystals; it is stable only in strong base and disproportionates in acidic, neutral and even weakly basic solutions, so it is mainly an intermediate in the preparation of potassium permanganate:

$$
\ce{3MnO4^2- + 4H+ -> 2MnO4- + MnO2 v + 2H2O}\ \ (\text{or pass in } \ce{CO2}), \qquad \ce{2K2MnO4 + Cl2 -> 2KMnO4 + 2KCl}.
$$
Industrially electrolytic oxidation is used: at the anode $\ce{MnO4^2- -> MnO4- + e-}$ and at the cathode $\ce{H2}$ is evolved, with the overall reaction $\ce{2K2MnO4 + 2H2O -> 2KMnO4 + 2KOH + H2 ^}$.

Potassium permanganate $\ce{KMnO4}$ forms purple-black crystals that decompose in light and on heating ($\ce{2KMnO4 ->[\Delta] K2MnO4 + MnO2 + O2 ^}$), so it is stored in brown bottles. It is the most commonly used strong oxidizing agent, and the reduction product depends on the medium:

| Medium | Reduction product | Example (reducing agent $\ce{SO3^2-}$) |
|---|---|---|
| Acidic | $\ce{Mn^2+}$ (almost colorless), $E^\circ = 1.51\,\mathrm{V}$ | $\ce{2MnO4- + 5SO3^2- + 6H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 5SO4^2- + 3H2O}$ |
| Neutral or weakly basic | $\ce{MnO2}$ (brown-black precipitate), $E^\circ_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.59\,\mathrm{V}$ | $\ce{2MnO4- + 3SO3^2- + H2O -> 2MnO2 v + 3SO4^2- + 2OH-}$ |
| Strongly basic | $\ce{MnO4^2-}$ (green), $E^\circ = 0.56\,\mathrm{V}$ | $\ce{2MnO4- + SO3^2- + 2OH- -> 2MnO4^2- + SO4^2- + H2O}$ |

Permanganate titration is one of the main methods of redox titration, and the color of $\ce{MnO4-}$ itself indicates the end point ([Analytical Chemistry](/6.4)).

## 15.6. The Iron Triad

The first-row transition elements of Groups 8, 9 and 10, iron (Fe), cobalt (Co) and nickel (Ni), have similar properties and are collectively called the iron triad; Ru, Rh, Pd, Os, Ir and Pt of the second and third transition series are collectively called the *platinum group metals*, which are chemically unreactive and are important catalysts (purification of automobile exhaust, hydrogenation, fuel cell electrodes). Fe, Co and Ni are all **ferromagnetic substances** and are also the most important alloying elements.

### 15.6.1. Redox Behavior

The Latimer diagrams of the iron triad (V):

$$
\text{acidic: }\ \ce{FeO4^2- ->[2.20] Fe^3+ ->[0.771] Fe^2+ ->[-0.447] Fe}, \quad \ce{Co^3+ ->[1.92] Co^2+ ->[-0.28] Co}, \quad \ce{NiO2 ->[1.68] Ni^2+ ->[-0.257] Ni};
$$

$$
\text{basic: }\ \ce{Fe(OH)3 ->[-0.56] Fe(OH)2 ->[-0.877] Fe}, \quad \ce{CoO(OH) ->[0.17] Co(OH)2 ->[-0.73] Co}, \quad \ce{NiO(OH) ->[0.49] Ni(OH)2 ->[-0.72] Ni}.
$$
Comparison with the $\ce{O2}$ couples ($E^\circ_{\mathrm{A}} = 1.229\,\mathrm{V}$, $E^\circ_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.401\,\mathrm{V}$) shows:

- In acidic solution $\ce{Fe^2+}$, $\ce{Co^2+}$ and $\ce{Ni^2+}$ are the lowest points on the Frost diagram. $\ce{Co^3+}$ ($1.92\,\mathrm{V}$) can oxidize water and is unstable in water (although its ammine complex ions are very stable, [6.1.11](/6.1.11)); $\ce{Fe^3+}$ is stable in water; $\ce{Fe^2+}$ can thermodynamically be oxidized by air, but slowly, so iron nails and acid are added when $\ce{FeSO4}$ solutions are prepared.
- In basic solution $\ce{Fe(OH)2}$ is very easily oxidized by air ($-0.56 < 0.401$), $\ce{Co(OH)2}$ is oxidized slowly ($0.17 < 0.401$), and $\ce{Ni(OH)2}$ is not oxidized by air ($0.49 > 0.401$); strong oxidizing agents such as NaClO and $\ce{Br2}$ are required. The reducing power of $\ce{Fe(OH)2}$, $\ce{Co(OH)2}$ and $\ce{Ni(OH)2}$ therefore decreases in this order, and the oxidizing power of Fe(III), Co(III) and Ni(III) increases.

### 15.6.2. Oxides and Hydroxides

| | Fe | Co | Ni |
|---|---|---|---|
| +2 oxide | FeO black | CoO gray-green | NiO green |
| +3 oxide or oxide hydroxide | $\ce{Fe2O3}$ red-brown; $\ce{Fe3O4}$ black ($\ce{Fe^{II}Fe^{III}2O4}$) | CoO(OH) brown-black | NiO(OH) black |
| +2 hydroxide | $\ce{Fe(OH)2}$ white | $\ce{Co(OH)2}$ pink | $\ce{Ni(OH)2}$ apple green |
| +3 hydroxide | $\ce{Fe(OH)3}$ red-brown (in fact $\ce{FeO(OH)}$ and $\ce{Fe2O3}\cdot x\ce{H2O}$) | exists as CoO(OH) | exists as NiO(OH) |

The $\ce{Co2O3}$, $\ce{Ni2O3}$, $\ce{Co(OH)3}$ and $\ce{Ni(OH)3}$ often written in textbooks are not definite compounds; what is actually obtained are the oxide hydroxides CoO(OH) and NiO(OH) and their hydrates. These oxides and hydroxides are predominantly basic and dissolve in acids to give salts. On addition of base to solutions of $\mathrm{M}^{2+}$:

$$
\ce{Fe^2+ + 2OH- -> Fe(OH)2 v}\ (\text{white, in the absence of } \ce{O2}), \qquad \ce{4Fe(OH)2 + O2 + 2H2O -> 4Fe(OH)3}\ (\text{white} \to \text{gray-green} \to \text{red-brown});
$$
$\ce{Co^2+}$ first gives the blue basic salt $\ce{Co(OH)Cl}$, which turns into pink $\ce{Co(OH)2}$ when base is in excess and slowly turns into brown CoO(OH) in air; $\ce{Ni(OH)2}$ is stable in air and is oxidized by NaClO to black NiO(OH): $\ce{2Ni(OH)2 + ClO- -> 2NiO(OH) + Cl- + H2O}$.

The difference in oxidizing power of Fe(III), Co(III) and Ni(III) shows in their reactions with hydrochloric acid:

$$
\ce{Fe2O3 + 6HCl -> 2FeCl3 + 3H2O}, \qquad \ce{2CoO(OH) + 6HCl(conc) -> 2CoCl2 + Cl2 ^ + 4H2O}\ (\text{the same for NiO(OH)}).
$$
The strong oxidizing power of NiO(OH) is exploited in the positive electrodes of nickel–cadmium and nickel–metal hydride batteries ([6.1.12](/6.1.12)).

## 15.7. The Copper Group

Copper (Cu), silver (Ag) and gold (Au) are in Group 11 (Group IB in older Chinese textbooks), with the valence configuration $(n-1)\mathrm{d}^{10}\,n\mathrm{s}^1$. Like the alkali metals they have a single s electron, but the penultimate shell is an 18-electron shell and the effective nuclear charge is large, so the ionization energies are high and the metals are unreactive (Cu and Ag do not dissolve in nonoxidizing acids, Au dissolves only in aqua regia, [6.1.14](/6.1.14#sec-14.4.3)), and they can lose some d electrons to show the oxidation states +2 and +3.

### 15.7.1. Relative Stability of Cu(I) and Cu(II)

Which of Cu(I) and Cu(II) is more stable depends on the conditions.

(1) In aqueous solution Cu(I) is unstable and disproportionates to Cu(II) and Cu. The Latimer diagram of copper:

$$
\ce{Cu^2+ ->[0.153] Cu+ ->[0.521] Cu},
$$
the potential on the right is larger than that on the left, so $\ce{2Cu+ <=> Cu^2+ + Cu}$ with $\lg K^\circ = (0.521 - 0.153)/0.05916 = 6.22$, $K^\circ = 1.7\times10^6$. $\ce{Cu2O}$ disproportionates when it dissolves in dilute sulfuric acid: $\ce{Cu2O + H2SO4 -> CuSO4 + Cu + H2O}$. Although the second ionization energy of $\ce{Cu+}$ is large (a filled $\mathrm{d}^{10}$ shell), the more highly charged and smaller $\ce{Cu^2+}$ has a much larger hydration enthalpy than $\ce{Cu+}$ (about $1500\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ more exothermic), which is enough to compensate.

(2) In the presence of precipitating or complexing agents the stability of Cu(I) is greatly enhanced. Formation of sparingly soluble CuCl ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 1.7\times10^{-7}$) or CuI ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 1.3\times10^{-12}$), or of stable complex ions, greatly reduces the concentration of $\ce{Cu+}$. By the Nernst equation, $E^\circ(\ce{CuX}/\ce{Cu}) = E^\circ(\ce{Cu+}/\ce{Cu}) + 0.05916\lg K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and $E^\circ(\ce{Cu^2+}/\ce{CuX}) = E^\circ(\ce{Cu^2+}/\ce{Cu+}) - 0.05916\lg K_{\mathrm{sp}}$:

| System | $E^\circ(\ce{Cu(II)}/\ce{Cu(I)})$ / V | $E^\circ(\ce{Cu(I)}/\ce{Cu})$ / V | Does Cu(I) disproportionate? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aqueous solution | 0.153 | 0.521 | yes |
| $\ce{Cl-}$ (formation of CuCl) | 0.55 | 0.12 | no |
| $\ce{I-}$ (formation of CuI) | 0.86 | −0.185 | no |

Hence $\ce{Cu^2+}$ can oxidize $\ce{I-}$ ($0.86 > 0.54\,\mathrm{V}$): $\ce{2Cu^2+ + 4I- -> 2CuI v + I2}$, which is the basis of the iodometric determination of copper; and Cu and $\ce{Cu^2+}$ comproportionate in concentrated hydrochloric acid:

$$
\ce{Cu + Cu^2+ + 4Cl- ->[\Delta] 2[CuCl2]-}\ (\text{muddy yellow}), \qquad \text{on dilution } \ce{[CuCl2]- -> CuCl v (white) + Cl-}.
$$
$\ce{Cu2O + 2HCl -> 2CuCl v + H2O}$ likewise no longer involves disproportionation. Cu(I) complexes are mostly two-coordinate and linear ($\ce{[CuCl2]-}$, $\ce{[Cu(NH3)2]+}$, $\ce{[Cu(CN)2]-}$), and there are also four-coordinate tetrahedral ones ($\ce{[Cu(CN)4]^3-}$).

(3) In the solid state at high temperature Cu(I) is more stable than Cu(II):

$$
\ce{Cu2(OH)2CO3 ->[200\,^\circ C] 2CuO (brown-black) + CO2 + H2O}, \qquad \ce{4CuO ->[> 1000\,^\circ C] 2Cu2O (dark red) + O2}, \qquad \ce{2CuCl2 ->[990\,^\circ C] 2CuCl + Cl2}.
$$
All these decompositions release gas, so $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}S^\circ_{\mathrm{m}} > 0$, and at a sufficiently high temperature $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G^\circ_{\mathrm{m}} = \Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H^\circ_{\mathrm{m}} - T\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}S^\circ_{\mathrm{m}}$ becomes negative ([6.1.6](/6.1.6#sec-6.4.9)); in solids there is also no hydration enthalpy to favor the highly charged $\ce{Cu^2+}$. $\ce{Cu2O}$ decomposes to Cu and $\ce{O2}$ only at about $1800\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

### 15.7.2. Reactions of Ag(I)

Almost all silver compounds are in the +1 state. The main reactions of $\ce{Ag+}$:

| Reagent | Reaction | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| NaOH | $\ce{2Ag+ + 2OH- -> Ag2O v + H2O}$ | brown precipitate (AgOH is unstable and dehydrates immediately) |
| Aqueous ammonia (small amount → excess) | $\ce{2Ag+ + 2NH3 + H2O -> Ag2O v + 2NH4+}$; $\ce{Ag2O + 4NH3 + H2O -> 2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + 2OH-}$ | precipitate first, then dissolution |
| $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ | $\ce{4Ag+ + Cr2O7^2- + H2O -> 2Ag2CrO4 v + 2H+}$; the precipitate dissolves in aqueous ammonia: $\ce{Ag2CrO4 + 4NH3 -> 2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + CrO4^2-}$ | brick-red precipitate |
| $\ce{S2O3^2-}$ (small amount) | $\ce{2Ag+ + S2O3^2- -> Ag2S2O3 v}$, $\ce{Ag2S2O3 + H2O -> Ag2S v + H2SO4}$ | white → yellow → brown → black, used to identify $\ce{S2O3^2-}$ |
| $\ce{S2O3^2-}$ (excess) | $\ce{Ag+ + 2S2O3^2- -> [Ag(S2O3)2]^3-}$ | the precipitate dissolves (photographic fixing, [6.1.14](/6.1.14#sec-14.3.4)) |

The dissolution of silver halides in aqueous ammonia, sodium thiosulfate and cyanide solutions depends on the relative magnitudes of $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and the stability constants of the complex ions ([6.1.10](/6.1.10)).

Ammoniacal silver solution (Tollens' reagent) is reduced by aldehydes and deposits a bright silver mirror on clean glass (the silver mirror reaction), which is used to test for the aldehyde group and to make mirrors:

$$
\ce{2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + HCHO + 3OH- -> HCOO- + 2Ag v + 4NH3 + 2H2O},
$$

$$
\ce{2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + C6H12O6 + 3OH- -> C6H11O7- + 2Ag v + 4NH3 + 2H2O}.
$$
Glucose is oxidized to gluconic acid, which is present in basic solution as gluconate $\ce{C6H11O7-}$. Ammoniacal silver solution must be prepared freshly: on long standing it deposits explosive silver nitride.

Gold does not dissolve in any single acid but dissolves in cyanide solution in the presence of $\ce{O2}$, which is the main modern method of gold extraction ($\ce{4Au + 8CN- + O2 + 2H2O -> 4[Au(CN)2]- + 4OH-}$): coordination lowers $E^\circ$ from $1.69\,\mathrm{V}$ ($\ce{Au+}/\ce{Au}$) to $-0.60\,\mathrm{V}$ ($\ce{[Au(CN)2]-}/\ce{Au}$), below the electrode potential of $\ce{O2}$.

## 15.8. The Zinc Group

Zinc (Zn), cadmium (Cd) and mercury (Hg) are in Group 12 (Group IIB in older Chinese textbooks), with the valence configuration $(n-1)\mathrm{d}^{10}\,n\mathrm{s}^2$; the d subshell is full and the elements show only the oxidation state +2 (Hg also shows +1 in $\ce{Hg2^2+}$). Their ions are colorless ($\mathrm{d}^{10}$), and the colors of their compounds arise from charge transfer or polarization (CdS yellow, HgS red or black, $\ce{HgI2}$ red).

### 15.8.1. The Elements

The elements of the zinc group have low melting points: Zn $419.5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, Cd $321\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, Hg $-38.8\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Mercury is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature: because of the inert-pair effect and relativistic effects the 6s electrons are bound very tightly and hardly take part in metallic bonding. In nature zinc occurs as sphalerite ZnS and mercury as cinnabar HgS. Zinc is used mainly for galvanized steel sheet (zinc is more reactive than iron and, if the coating is damaged, still protects the iron as an anode) and for brass (a Cu–Zn **alloy**). $\ce{Zn(OH)2}$ is an amphoteric hydroxide that dissolves in strong base to give $\ce{[Zn(OH)4]^2-}$ and in aqueous ammonia to give $\ce{[Zn(NH3)4]^2+}$. Cadmium is toxic (itai-itai disease), and nickel–cadmium batteries have gradually been phased out.

**Definition 15.8.1 (Amalgam)** An alloy of mercury with another metal is called an **amalgam**, such as sodium amalgam Na–Hg, gold amalgam Au–Hg and silver amalgam Ag–Hg.

Mercury dissolves many metals; sodium amalgam is a mild reducing agent, and silver amalgam was long used for dental fillings. Amalgamation was formerly used to extract precious metals such as gold and silver from ores, with the mercury then distilled off by heating. Mercury vapor and mercury compounds are toxic, and organomercury compounds (methylmercury) accumulate along the food chain (Minamata disease); gold extraction by amalgamation is still one of the main sources of mercury pollution, and mercury-containing products and processes are being phased out internationally under the Minamata Convention (2013).

### 15.8.2. Compounds of Mercury

Halides of mercury:

| | $\ce{HgCl2}$ (corrosive sublimate) | $\ce{Hg2Cl2}$ (calomel) |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | linear covalent molecule Cl–Hg–Cl | linear molecule Cl–Hg–Hg–Cl containing an Hg–Hg bond |
| Properties | sublimes readily, soluble in water but present in water mainly as molecules (a weak electrolyte) | sparingly soluble in water ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 1.4\times10^{-18}$), decomposes in light to $\ce{HgCl2}$ and Hg |
| Toxicity | highly toxic | toxic (less toxic than $\ce{HgCl2}$ because of its low solubility) |
| Use | disinfectant (now seldom used) | **calomel electrode** ([6.1.11](/6.1.11)) |

$\ce{HgBr2}$ and $\ce{HgI2}$ are covalent molecules like $\ce{HgCl2}$, whereas $\ce{HgF2}$ is an ionic compound. The nitrates $\ce{Hg(NO3)2}$ and $\ce{Hg2(NO3)2}$ are ionic compounds that dissolve readily in water, and nitric acid is added to suppress hydrolysis when they are prepared.

The Latimer diagram of mercury:

$$
\ce{Hg^2+ ->[0.920] Hg2^2+ ->[0.797] Hg}.
$$
The potential on the right is smaller than that on the left, so $\ce{Hg2^2+}$ does not disproportionate in aqueous solution, in contrast to $\ce{Cu+}$; instead $\ce{Hg^2+}$ and Hg comproportionate: $\ce{Hg^2+ + Hg <=> Hg2^2+}$, $\lg K^\circ = (0.920 - 0.797)/0.05916 = 2.08$, $K^\circ = 1.2\times10^2$. The stability of $\ce{Hg2^2+}$ comes from the Hg–Hg σ bond formed by two 6s electrons. However, when $\ce{Hg^2+}$ is removed in quantity by precipitation or complexation, the equilibrium shifts to the left and $\ce{Hg2^2+}$ disproportionates:

$$
\ce{Hg2^2+ + 2OH- -> HgO v + Hg v + H2O}, \qquad \ce{Hg2^2+ + S^2- -> HgS v + Hg v}, \qquad \ce{Hg2Cl2 + 2NH3 -> Hg(NH2)Cl v (white) + Hg v (black) + NH4Cl}.
$$
The last reaction gives a gray-black mixture and is used to identify $\ce{Hg2^2+}$.

## 15.9. The f-Block Elements

### 15.9.1. Lanthanoids and Rare-Earth Elements

The f-block elements comprise the 15 **lanthanoids** (Ln, La–Lu, $Z = 57$–$71$) of the sixth period and the 15 **actinoids** (Ac–Lr) of the seventh period. All the actinoids are radioactive; Th and U are nuclear fuels, and they are not discussed here.

**Definition 15.9.1 (Rare-Earth Element)** The 17 elements comprising scandium (Sc), yttrium (Y) and the 15 lanthanoids are collectively called the **rare-earth elements** (RE).

Sc, Y and the lanthanoids are all in Group 3 and have similar outer-shell structures; the radius of $\ce{Y^3+}$ ($90\,\mathrm{pm}$) is close to that of $\ce{Ho^3+}$, and in minerals Y always occurs together with the lanthanoids. La–Eu are usually called the light rare earths (cerium group) and Gd–Lu and Y the heavy rare earths (yttrium group). The "rare earths" are not rare (the crustal abundance of Ce is comparable to that of Cu) but are dispersed and difficult to separate: the properties of the lanthanoids are extremely similar, and the ionic radii of adjacent elements differ by only about $1\,\mathrm{pm}$. Modern industry separates the rare earths by ion exchange and multistage solvent extraction: a complexing agent forms more stable complexes with the smaller (heavy rare-earth) ions, and the small differences in stability constants are exploited through many extraction stages (the theory of cascade extraction established by the Chinese chemist Xu Guangxian made the large-scale production of high-purity rare earths possible). The rare earths are key materials of modern technology: permanent magnets, phosphors, lasers and fiber amplifiers, catalysts and hydrogen storage alloys ([6.1.13](/6.1.13)).

### 15.9.2. Valence Structure and Oxidation States

The valence configuration of the lanthanoids is $4\mathrm{f}^{n}\,6\mathrm{s}^2$ or $4\mathrm{f}^{n-1}\,5\mathrm{d}^1\,6\mathrm{s}^2$ (La $5\mathrm{d}^1\,6\mathrm{s}^2$, Ce $4\mathrm{f}^1\,5\mathrm{d}^1\,6\mathrm{s}^2$, Gd $4\mathrm{f}^7\,5\mathrm{d}^1\,6\mathrm{s}^2$, Lu $4\mathrm{f}^{14}\,5\mathrm{d}^1\,6\mathrm{s}^2$, the others $4\mathrm{f}^n\,6\mathrm{s}^2$); the configurations of $\ce{Ln^3+}$ are all $[\ce{Xe}]\,4\mathrm{f}^{0\text{–}14}$. The 4f orbitals lie close to the nucleus and are well screened by the outer $5\mathrm{s}^2\,5\mathrm{p}^6$ electrons, so they hardly take part in bonding: although the numbers of 4f electrons differ, they influence the chemical properties of the lanthanoids very little, and the elements are very similar; the effect of the ligand field on the 4f levels (about $100\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) is also two orders of magnitude smaller than on the 3d levels (about $10^4\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$).

The sum of the first, second and third ionization energies of the lanthanoids is rather low (about $3455$ for La and $3886\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for Lu, compared with $5230$ for Cr of the first transition series), and +3 is the common characteristic oxidation state. Because half-filled and filled 4f subshells are particularly stable, a few elements have other relatively stable oxidation states:

- Eu ($\ce{Eu^2+}$ is $4\mathrm{f}^7$) and Yb ($\ce{Yb^2+}$ is $4\mathrm{f}^{14}$) have a relatively stable +2 state, and their third ionization energies are the largest among the lanthanoids (panel b of the figure below);
- Ce ($\ce{Ce^4+}$ is $4\mathrm{f}^0$, that is, the [Xe] configuration) has a stable +4 state, and Tb ($\ce{Tb^4+}$ is $4\mathrm{f}^7$) can also form +4 compounds.

The lanthanide contraction is evident in panel a of the figure: the metallic radius decreases from $187.7\,\mathrm{pm}$ for La to $173.4\,\mathrm{pm}$ for Lu (about $14\,\mathrm{pm}$ in all), and the radius of $\ce{Ln^3+}$ (coordination number 6) decreases from $103\,\mathrm{pm}$ to $86\,\mathrm{pm}$ (about $17\,\mathrm{pm}$ in all), with adjacent elements differing by about $1\,\mathrm{pm}$ ([6.1.2](/6.1.2#def-2.4.2)). The metallic radii of Eu and Yb are anomalously large: in the metals they contribute only two electrons to metallic bonding (the ion cores are the $\ce{Ln^2+}$ ions with $4\mathrm{f}^7$ and $4\mathrm{f}^{14}$), so the metallic bonding is weaker and the melting points and densities are also anomalously low. Many properties (stability constants of complexes, hydration enthalpies of ions) show a break at Gd ($4\mathrm{f}^7$), called the gadolinium break.

### 15.9.3. Colors of the Ions

The colors of $\ce{Ln^3+}$ arise from f–f transitions. The 4f orbitals are shielded and interact only weakly with the ligands, so the absorption bands are very narrow (like the lines of atomic spectra) and weak, and the colors hardly change with the ligand. The rule for the colors is that ions with $4\mathrm{f}^n$ and $4\mathrm{f}^{14-n}$ electrons have the same or similar colors, since $n$ electrons and $n$ "holes" have the same term structure. With $\ce{Gd^3+}$ ($4\mathrm{f}^7$) as the center, the color sequence from $\ce{La^3+}$ to $\ce{Gd^3+}$ is repeated in going from $\ce{Lu^3+}$ to $\ce{Gd^3+}$:

| $4\mathrm{f}^n$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ion | $\ce{La^3+}$ | $\ce{Ce^3+}$ | $\ce{Pr^3+}$ | $\ce{Nd^3+}$ | $\ce{Pm^3+}$ | $\ce{Sm^3+}$ | $\ce{Eu^3+}$ | $\ce{Gd^3+}$ |
| Color | colorless | colorless | green | pale violet | pink | yellow | nearly colorless | colorless |
| $4\mathrm{f}^{14-n}$ | 14 | 13 | 12 | 11 | 10 | 9 | 8 | 7 |
| Ion | $\ce{Lu^3+}$ | $\ce{Yb^3+}$ | $\ce{Tm^3+}$ | $\ce{Er^3+}$ | $\ce{Ho^3+}$ | $\ce{Dy^3+}$ | $\ce{Tb^3+}$ | $\ce{Gd^3+}$ |
| Color | colorless | colorless | pale green | pink | yellow | yellow | nearly colorless | colorless |

The sharp f–f transitions make rare-earth ions excellent luminescence centers: the red light of $\ce{Eu^3+}$ and the green light of $\ce{Tb^3+}$ are used in phosphors and displays, yttrium aluminum garnet doped with $\ce{Nd^3+}$ (Nd:YAG) is a commonly used solid-state laser, and optical fibers doped with $\ce{Er^3+}$ amplify the $1.55\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ optical signal used in communications.

### 15.9.4. Standard Electrode Potentials and the Elements

$E^\circ(\ce{Ln^3+}/\ce{Ln})$ ranges from $-2.38\,\mathrm{V}$ (La) to $-2.28\,\mathrm{V}$ (Lu) (Eu −1.99, Yb −2.19). The lanthanoid metals are fairly strong reducing agents, second only to the alkali and alkaline earth metals and close to Mg ($-2.37\,\mathrm{V}$); La is the most reactive, and the reactivity decreases slightly with increasing atomic number. Properties of the elements:

- They are silvery white, relatively soft, ductile and malleable but of low tensile strength, becoming gradually harder with increasing atomic number; the density and melting point generally increase with atomic number except for Eu and Yb.
- All except Yb and Lu are quite strongly paramagnetic; Gd is a ferromagnetic substance below $293\,\mathrm{K}$ (its **Curie temperature**).
- They are slowly oxidized in air, react slowly with cold water and faster with hot water to release hydrogen, and are therefore stored under kerosene; they dissolve readily in dilute acids and do not react with bases.
- The ignition temperatures of the light rare-earth metals are low, and they throw sparks on friction; cerium–iron alloy is used as flint.

### 15.9.5. Magnetism of the Ions

The magnetism of the lanthanoids differs from that of the d-block transition elements. The d orbitals of d-block ions interact strongly with the ligand field and the orbital angular momentum is almost completely "quenched", so the magnetic moment comes mainly from the spin of the unpaired electrons and can be estimated with the spin-only formula $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}} = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$ ([6.1.5](/6.1.5#sec-5.3)). The 4f electrons are shielded, the ligand field is weak, the orbital angular momentum is not quenched, and in addition the **spin–orbit coupling** of the heavy atoms is very strong (level splittings of more than $10^3\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, far greater than $k_{\mathrm{B}}T \approx 200\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$), so the contributions of both spin and orbit must be considered: the total angular momentum quantum number $J$ is a good quantum number, and the magnetic moment is

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}} = g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}, \qquad g_J = 1 + \frac{J(J+1) + S(S+1) - L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)},
$$
where $g_J$ is called the *Landé g-factor* and $S$, $L$ and $J$ are the quantum numbers of the ground **term symbol** $^{2S+1}L_J$ of the ion ([5.5.4](/5.5.4#sec-4.2.4)). For $J = 0$, $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0$; for spin only ($L = 0$), $g_J = 2$ and $J = S$, and the formula reduces to the spin-only formula.

The ground term is determined by **Hund's rules**; for equivalent electrons the complete statement has three rules: (1) the total spin $S$ is a maximum; (2) subject to that, the total orbital quantum number $L$ is a maximum; (3) $J = \lvert L - S\rvert$ for a subshell less than half full and $J = L + S$ for one more than half full.

**Example 15.9.1** Find the ground terms and effective magnetic moments of $\ce{Nd^3+}$ ($4\mathrm{f}^3$) and $\ce{Dy^3+}$ ($4\mathrm{f}^9$) and compare them with the spin-only formula.

Solution. The f orbitals have $m_l = 3, 2, 1, 0, -1, -2, -3$. The three electrons of $\ce{Nd^3+}$ have parallel spins and occupy $m_l = 3, 2, 1$ in turn: $S = \frac{3}{2}$ and $L = 6$ (denoted I); the subshell is less than half full, so $J = L - S = \frac{9}{2}$, and the ground term is $^4\mathrm{I}_{9/2}$.

$$
g_J = 1 + \frac{\frac{9}{2}\cdot\frac{11}{2} + \frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{5}{2} - 6\cdot7}{2\cdot\frac{9}{2}\cdot\frac{11}{2}} = 1 - \frac{13.5}{49.5} = 0.727, \qquad \mu_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.727\sqrt{24.75}\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}} = 3.62\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}.
$$
Of the nine electrons of $\ce{Dy^3+}$, seven with spin up fill the seven orbitals and the other two with spin down occupy $m_l = 3, 2$: $S = \frac{5}{2}$ and $L = 5$ (H); the subshell is more than half full, so $J = L + S = \frac{15}{2}$, the ground term is $^6\mathrm{H}_{15/2}$, $g_J = \frac{4}{3}$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{4}{3}\sqrt{63.75}\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}} = 10.65\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$.

The experimental values are about $3.5$ and $10.5\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$, respectively, in agreement with the calculated ones; the spin-only formula gives $\sqrt{3\cdot5} = 3.87$ and $\sqrt{5\cdot7} = 5.92\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$, almost a factor of two too small for $\ce{Dy^3+}$. $\square$

Panel c of the figure above shows the calculated and experimental values for all the $\ce{Ln^3+}$ ions: the magnetic moment has two peaks as a function of the number of 4f electrons, and the maximum is not at $\ce{Gd^3+}$, which has the most unpaired electrons ($7.9\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$, $L = 0$), but at $\ce{Dy^3+}$ and $\ce{Ho^3+}$ (about $10.6\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$), where the orbital and spin magnetic moments add; in the first half $J = L - S$ and the two partly cancel. The experimental values for $\ce{Sm^3+}$ and $\ce{Eu^3+}$ deviate from the calculated ones (the ground state $^7\mathrm{F}_0$ of $\ce{Eu^3+}$ has zero moment, whereas about $3.4\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$ is observed) because the first excited $J$ levels lie so close to the ground state that they are appreciably populated at room temperature.

Rare-earth permanent magnets exploit precisely the unquenched orbital angular momentum of the 4f electrons: it makes the magnetic moments tend strongly to align along a certain direction of the crystal (magnetocrystalline anisotropy), so the magnet is difficult to demagnetize. Permanent magnet materials have developed from alnico alloys and Pt–Co alloys through $\ce{SmCo5}$ in the late 1960s and $\ce{Sm2Co17}$ in the 1970s to $\ce{Nd2Fe14B}$ (neodymium magnets), invented in 1984, which are the strongest permanent magnets at present and are used in electric motors, wind turbines and hard disks.

### 15.9.6. Compounds of the Lanthanoids

Compounds of Ln(III):

- $\ce{Ln2O3}$ is a basic oxide, sparingly soluble in water and bases, readily soluble in strong acids, and absorbs $\ce{CO2}$ from the air; it has a high melting point and can be used as a refractory material.
- The basicity of $\ce{Ln(OH)3}$ is close to that of the alkaline earth hydroxides, but the solubility is much smaller; from La to Lu the ionic radius decreases and the **ionic potential** increases, so the basicity gradually weakens and the solubility gradually decreases. Fractional precipitation was one of the early methods for separating the rare earths.
- $\ce{LnCl3}\cdot x\ce{H2O}$ ($x = 6$ or 7) is soluble and deliquescent; anhydrous $\ce{LnCl3}$ has a high melting point and conducts electricity when molten, and electrolysis of the molten chlorides gives the rare-earth metals. As for $\ce{AlCl3}$ ([6.1.14](/6.1.14#sec-14.6.5)), anhydrous chlorides cannot be prepared by heating the hydrated chlorides because of hydrolysis: $\ce{LnCl3 + H2O -> LnOCl + 2HCl}$; they are dehydrated in a stream of HCl or by heating with $\ce{NH4Cl}$.
- The oxalates $\ce{Ln2(C2O4)3}$, fluorides, carbonates and phosphates are sparingly soluble. The oxalates also precipitate from acidic solution and are used to separate the rare earths from other metals; ignition gives the oxides.

Compounds of Ln(IV). Ce, Pr, Nd, Tb and Dy can all form +4 compounds, but only Ce(IV), which can exist both in aqueous solution and in solids, is important. $\ce{CeO2}$ (fluorite structure) is a pale yellow solid that does not dissolve in acids or bases and is a strong oxidizing agent (it dissolves on reduction by $\ce{H2O2}$); it is the oxygen-storage component of the three-way catalyst for automobile exhaust (oxygen is released and taken up reversibly between $\ce{CeO2}$ and $\ce{CeO_{2-x}}$) and is also a polishing powder for glass. $E^\circ(\ce{Ce^4+}/\ce{Ce^3+}) = 1.72\,\mathrm{V}$, but the oxidizing power of Ce(IV) depends strongly on the medium: in $1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ $\ce{H2SO4}$, $\ce{Ce^4+}$ is coordinated by $\ce{SO4^2-}$ and the formal potential drops to $1.44\,\mathrm{V}$ (about 1.70 in $\ce{HClO4}$ and 1.61 in $\ce{HNO3}$). $\ce{Ce(SO4)2*2H2O}$ is the most stable Ce(IV) salt, dissolves readily in water and is a strong oxidizing agent in acidic solution. In redox processes Ce(IV) is converted directly to Ce(III) with transfer of a single electron and no intermediates, so the reactions are fast and quantitative and the solutions are also stable, which is why it is often used in titrimetric analysis (cerimetry, [Analytical Chemistry](/6.4)).

The chemistry of the d-block elements is determined by the d electrons: the number of d electrons determines the range and stability of the oxidation states and the colors and magnetism of the complexes. The 4f electrons of the f-block elements are buried deep inside, which makes the lanthanoids resemble one another but preserves the atom-like spectra and magnetism.

## Questions

1. Why is $\ce{Fe^3+}$ stable in acidic solution whereas $\ce{Co^3+}$ is not? Why is $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^3+}$ nevertheless very stable?
   Answer: $E^\circ(\ce{Co^3+}/\ce{Co^2+}) = 1.92\,\mathrm{V}$ is larger than $1.229\,\mathrm{V}$, so $\ce{Co^3+}$ can oxidize water, whereas $E^\circ(\ce{Fe^3+}/\ce{Fe^2+}) = 0.771\,\mathrm{V}$ cannot. $\ce{NH3}$ is a strong-field ligand, the low-spin $t_{2g}^6$ configuration of $\ce{Co^3+}$ has a large crystal field stabilization energy, and $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^3+}$ is far more stable than $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}$, which lowers the electrode potential to about $0.1\,\mathrm{V}$.
2. Why do a white precipitate and a brown solution form when KI is added to a $\ce{CuSO4}$ solution?
   Answer: $\ce{2Cu^2+ + 4I- -> 2CuI v + I2}$ gives white CuI and $\ce{I2}$ (which forms brown $\ce{I3-}$ with excess $\ce{I-}$); the low solubility of CuI raises $E^\circ(\ce{Cu^2+}/\ce{CuI})$ to $0.86\,\mathrm{V}$, which is larger than $E^\circ(\ce{I2}/\ce{I-})$.
3. Why are Zr and Hf, and Nb and Ta, difficult to separate? Why are the lanthanoids even more difficult to separate?
   Answer: In the former case the lanthanide contraction makes the radii of the fifth- and sixth-period elements of the same group almost equal; within the lanthanoids, adjacent elements differ in electron configuration by only one 4f electron, which hardly takes part in bonding, and the radii differ by only about $1\,\mathrm{pm}$, so the differences in chemical properties are even smaller.
4. Why does $\ce{Gd^3+} $, which has the most unpaired electrons, not have the largest magnetic moment among the lanthanoid ions?
   Answer: For $\ce{Gd^3+}$ ($4\mathrm{f}^7$) $L = 0$, and the magnetic moment comes from spin alone ($7.9\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$); for the ions in the second half of the series $J = L + S$, the orbital and spin magnetic moments add, and $\ce{Dy^3+}$ and $\ce{Ho^3+}$ reach about $10.6\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$.
