Chemistry studies the composition, structure, properties and transformations of matter. Its central concern is how atoms combine into molecules and condensed matter, and how molecules in turn convert into one another. Unlike physics, which starts from a few universal laws, chemistry faces hundreds of millions of concrete substances built from a little over a hundred elements: it must explain with unifying principles why each substance has the properties it has, and it must also answer how to identify these substances and how to synthesize them. This discipline collects six courses: [Inorganic Chemistry](/6.1), [Structural Chemistry](/6.2), [Physical Chemistry](/6.3), [Analytical Chemistry](/6.4), [Organic Chemistry](/6.5) and [Chemical Biology](/6.6). The core questions are these: what is the nature of the **chemical bond**, and how does the structure of a molecule determine its properties; whether a reaction can occur, how far it proceeds and how fast; how to determine what is in a sample, how much of it, and with what structure; and how to observe and manipulate life processes by chemical means.

The order of the six courses is a line from principles to applications. Inorganic chemistry corresponds to the course "Principles of Chemistry": it first describes the structure of matter with elementary theories of atomic structure, chemical bonding and crystal structure, then introduces chemical thermodynamics, kinetics and the four equilibria in solution, and finally uses these principles to go through the elements of the periodic table region by region; it is the common starting point of all the other courses. Structural chemistry is the bridge from quantum physics to chemistry: atomic orbitals and **molecular orbitals**, theories of the chemical bond, molecular symmetry and point groups, crystal structure and molecular spectra put the qualitative picture of structure from inorganic chemistry on the foundation of quantum mechanics. Physical chemistry makes the principles of chemistry rigorous and quantitative: starting from the laws of thermodynamics, it uses the **chemical potential** to derive the theories of solutions, chemical equilibrium and phase equilibrium, uses statistical thermodynamics to compute macroscopic properties from molecular energy levels, and then treats electrochemistry, reaction kinetics, nonequilibrium thermodynamics and interfacial chemistry. Analytical chemistry turns the principles of equilibrium and spectroscopy into methods of measurement: chemical analysis, represented by titration; instrumental analysis, represented by spectroscopy, electrochemistry, chromatography and mass spectrometry; and the data treatment that assesses whether a result is reliable. Organic chemistry studies the compounds of carbon, with the **reaction mechanism** as its main line, reducing thousands of reactions to a few patterns of electron flow, and ends with the chemistry of sugars, amino acids, peptides and nucleic acids. Chemical biology studies life by chemical methods and is divided into four parts, chemical analysis, genetic manipulation, chemical reconstitution and active molecules, which treat how to measure, modify and intervene in biomolecules and cells with chemical tools.

The theoretical foundation of chemistry comes from [Physics](/5): [Thermal Physics](/5.2) is the starting point of chemical thermodynamics, [Quantum Physics](/5.5) explains atomic structure and chemical bonding, and [Electromagnetism](/5.3) and [Optics](/5.4) provide the principles of electrochemistry and spectroscopic methods. The mathematics it uses is equally broad: the relations of chemical thermodynamics are calculations with total differentials and partial derivatives from [Calculus](/2.2), the rate equations of reaction mechanisms are [ordinary differential equations](/2.4), the Hückel molecular orbital method is an **eigenvalue** problem from [Linear Algebra](/2.1), and the symmetry operations of a molecule form a **group** in the sense of [Discrete Mathematics](/1.3); statistical thermodynamics uses [Probability Theory](/3.1), the tests and confidence intervals for analytical data come from [Statistical Inference](/3.2), and numerical calculations of phase diagrams, kinetic curves and molecular orbitals are done with [Scientific Computing with Python](/4.4). Chemistry is in turn the molecular language of [Biology](/7): enzyme kinetics, bioenergetics and the structure of biomolecules in [Biochemistry](/7.1) rest on physical and organic chemistry, [Biophysics](/7.6) uses the thermodynamics, electrochemistry and spectroscopy found here, and the buffer solutions, electrophoresis, chromatography, fluorescence and mass spectrometry in molecular and cell biology experiments are all methods of analytical chemistry.

The position of chemistry within a biology curriculum is somewhat awkward. Unlike physics, it does not enjoy the status of general education that is required of almost every science and engineering department; only students in the combined chemistry-and-biology admission category, which groups the two majors together, and students of medicine have to take it, and putting the two majors into one category is more of a historical arrangement. In the actual course of studying biological sciences, hardly any specialized course becomes impossible to follow for lack of chemical knowledge; even when it does, it is usually only a few specific points, which can be made up in a targeted way, and these points are not covered in chemistry classes anyway.

Still, since chemistry remains a required course for all biology majors in China, I think it should be viewed as follows: we need not confine ourselves to immediate usefulness, because the value of a course lies not in how much knowledge can be applied right away, but in letting a discipline enter one's own field of knowledge, so that at some later moment it may inspire a direction of research. I once heard a case study of this kind: a senior student in biology, whose chemistry grades were in fact not good, worked in neuroscience, yet developed a tissue-clearing technique with superb chemical skill; the chemistry involved was quite deep, far beyond what the few courses required of biology majors could cover, and he did a great deal of investigation and screening for it. Chemistry is also the subject I have learned least well myself, but I still keep it in this knowledge base, and, following the curriculum of the chemical biology major, I have raised many courses from the B level to the H level. Here H stands for honor, and can also be understood as hard; but it is precisely these more in-depth courses, because they touch the principles behind the phenomena, that have more connections with biology than the B-level courses that biology majors are required to take. Chemistry may now seem to me a "useless learning"; but perhaps some day in the future I will recall a concept from some chemistry class of my undergraduate years and, following it, come up with a new idea.
