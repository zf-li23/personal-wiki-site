Analytical chemistry answers four questions about a substance: what it contains, how much it contains, where these components are, and in what structure or form they exist. The products of a synthesis are identified by analysis, the quality of drugs, food, environmental and clinical samples is checked by analysis, and many discoveries in the life sciences have come from new analytical methods. Every measurement carries error, so analytical chemistry is equally concerned with one more question: how reliable is an analytical result. This discipline corresponds to the course "Analytical Chemistry (for Chemical Biology)", offered for the chemical biology major, which, besides classical chemical analysis and instrumental analysis, introduces more of the analysis of biomolecules such as proteins and nucleic acids.

Chapter 1 gives an overview of the tasks and methods of analytical chemistry and establishes how analytical data are evaluated and treated: errors, significant figures, significance tests and calibration curves. Chapters 2–5 are chemical analysis: the four kinds of **titration** (acid–base, complexation, precipitation and redox) and gravimetric analysis, with emphasis on how to treat solution equilibria rigorously in the presence of side reactions, choose indicators and estimate titration errors. Chapter 6 is electroanalytical chemistry: conductance, potentiometry and ion-selective electrodes, coulometry, voltammetry and electrochemical sensors. Chapters 7–12 are spectroscopic analysis: first an overview of the components common to spectroscopic instruments, then atomic emission, atomic absorption and X-ray spectroscopy, molecular ultraviolet–visible absorption, fluorescence and chemiluminescence (including fluorescence imaging and fluorescent proteins), infrared and Raman spectroscopy, and finally **nuclear magnetic resonance**. Chapters 13–14 are separation methods: the plate theory and rate theory of **chromatography**, gas chromatography, high-performance liquid chromatography, and the chromatographic separation and electrophoresis of biological macromolecules. Chapter 15 is **mass spectrometry**, from ionization methods and mass analyzers to proteomics.

The principles of analytical chemistry come from the preceding courses: titration is based on the four equilibria in [Inorganic Chemistry](/6.1), electroanalytical chemistry on the electrochemistry in [Physical Chemistry](/6.3), the energy levels and transitions of spectroscopic methods on [Quantum Physics](/5.5) and [Structural Chemistry](/6.2), and the optical elements in instruments on [Optics](/5.4); the tests and confidence intervals in data treatment are applications of [Statistical Inference](/3.2). The methods of this discipline are used repeatedly later: [Organic Chemistry](/6.5) determines molecular structures with ultraviolet, infrared, nuclear magnetic resonance and mass spectrometry and uses chromatography to separate and purify products, and the chemical analysis part of [Chemical Biology](/6.6) takes sequencing, mass spectrometry and imaging as its themes. In [Biology](/7), buffer solutions, electrophoresis and chromatography are everyday operations in biochemistry experiments, fluorescence microscopy and fluorescent probes are the main means of observing molecules in cell biology, and mass spectrometry is the core tool of **proteomics** and metabolomics.

I have not taken this course, but it is required for the chemical biology major, its demands are far higher than those of the analytical chemistry for the chemistry major, and it is also valuable as groundwork for organic chemistry and chemical biology. It seems to give more coverage specifically to the analysis of biomolecules: physiological buffer solutions, fluorescent probes and fluorescent proteins, the chromatography and electrophoresis of proteins and nucleic acids, and biological mass spectrometry each have dedicated space in the course. In my view, however, this is still limited: it remains mainly a course on traditional instrumental analysis, in which most of the space is spent on explaining the principle, construction and quantitative use of each instrument, while sequencing, single-cell and high-throughput analytical techniques, which are used more and more in life science research, are not its focus. From this point of view it is more like a well-laid foundation; analytical techniques truly oriented toward the life sciences will only be developed in chemical biology.
