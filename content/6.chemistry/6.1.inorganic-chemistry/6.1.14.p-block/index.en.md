The p block (Groups 13–18, $n\mathrm{s}^2\,n\mathrm{p}^{1\text{–}6}$) is the richest part of descriptive chemistry: it contains all the nonmetals (except H), almost all the metalloids and a number of "poor metals", and the character of the elements changes gradually from metallic at the lower left to typical nonmetallic at the upper right. This chapter first discusses the trends common to all the groups (Section 14.1): the special features of the second-period elements, oxidation states and the inert-pair effect, reading oxidizing and reducing behavior from Frost diagrams, and the nomenclature and acidity of oxoacids. We then proceed group by group, from the typical nonmetals of the halogens (Section 14.2) through the chalcogens (Section 14.3), the pnictogens (Section 14.4) and the carbon group (Section 14.5) to the electron-deficient boron group (Section 14.6). Many principles of the earlier chapters are applied here: molecular structure (hybridization, VSEPR, molecular orbitals and delocalized π bonds in [6.1.3](/6.1.3)), crystal structure ([6.1.4](/6.1.4)), the four types of equilibria ([6.1.8](/6.1.8)–[6.1.10](/6.1.10)), and above all electrode potentials and Latimer diagrams ([6.1.11](/6.1.11), [6.1.12](/6.1.12)). For each group we first give a table of general properties and then describe the substances one by one; similar reactions are collected in tables and only representative equations are kept. The s block is covered in [6.1.13](/6.1.13), and the d and f blocks in [6.1.15](/6.1.15). The organic chemistry of p-block elements (hydroboration, organosilicon and organophosphorus compounds) belongs to [Organic Chemistry](/6.5), and the symmetry of oxoanion structures to [Structural Chemistry](/6.2).

## 14.1. An Overview of the p-Block Elements

### 14.1.1. General Properties and the Special Features of the Second Period

The p-block elements of one group have the same valence configuration, and the highest oxidation state is generally equal to the group number minus 10 (+3 for Group 13, +4 for Group 14, …, +7 for Group 17, except for F and O). Common oxidation states often differ by 2, because valence electrons take part in bonding, or do not, in pairs.

The second-period elements B, C, N, O and F differ greatly from the other members of their groups:

- The atoms are small and highly electronegative and have only four valence orbitals, 2s and 2p, so the maximum coordination number is 4 ($\ce{BF4-}$, $\ce{NH4+}$); molecules such as $\ce{NCl5}$ and $\ce{OF4}$ do not exist. Elements from the third period on can have 5, 6 or even 7 ligand atoms ($\ce{PCl5}$, $\ce{SF6}$, $\ce{IF7}$, $\ce{[SiF6]^2-}$). The bonding of such hypervalent molecules does not require d orbitals and is described with three-center four-electron bonds ([6.1.3](/6.1.3#sec-3.4.5)).
- Because the atoms are small, the sideways overlap of p orbitals is good, and stable π bonds and multiple bonds form easily: $\ce{N2}$ (the $\ce{N#N}$ bond enthalpy is $945\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, much larger than $3 \times 160$ for three N–N single bonds), $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ are all small molecules. For the heavier members of the group the π bonds are weak and it is preferable to form more σ single bonds, so phosphorus exists as $\ce{P4}$ (P–P $201$, whereas $\ce{P#P}$ is only $489\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$), sulfur as $\ce{S8}$, and $\ce{SiO2}$ is a network solid whereas $\ce{CO2}$ is molecular.
- Conversely, the N–N, O–O and F–F single bonds are unusually weak ($160$, $146$ and $159\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, compared with $201$, $266$ and $242$ for P–P, S–S and Cl–Cl): the atoms are so small that the lone pairs on adjacent atoms repel each other.

### 14.1.2. The Inert-Pair Effect

The heavy elements of Groups 13–15 share a tendency: the lower oxidation state is more stable than the group oxidation state. In aqueous solution Tl exists mainly as $\ce{Tl+}$, Pb as $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and Bi as $\ce{Bi^3+}$, whereas $\ce{Tl^3+}$, $\ce{PbO2}$ and $\ce{NaBiO3}$ are all strong oxidizing agents ($E^\circ(\ce{Tl^3+}/\ce{Tl+}) = 1.25\,\mathrm{V}$, $E^\circ(\ce{PbO2}/\ce{Pb^2+}) = 1.455\,\mathrm{V}$). For the lighter elements of the groups the opposite holds: $\ce{Al^3+}$ is stable, $\ce{Sn^2+}$ is a reducing agent, and compounds of Ge(II) are even less stable.

**Definition 14.1.1 (Inert-Pair Effect)** The phenomenon that the $6\mathrm{s}^2$ electron pair of the sixth-period p-block elements (Tl, Pb, Bi, Po) takes part in bonding only reluctantly, so that oxidation states two units below the group oxidation state (+1, +2, +3) are more stable, is called the **inert-pair effect**.

There are two reasons. First, the 6s electrons are particularly difficult to remove: because of their penetration they pass through the poorly screening 4f and 5d shells and experience a large **effective nuclear charge**; in addition, relativistic effects contract the 6s orbital and lower its energy ([Structural Chemistry](/6.2)). The sum $I_1 + I_2 + I_3$ for Group 13 does not decrease monotonically:

| | B | Al | Ga | In | Tl |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $I_1 + I_2 + I_3$ / $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ | 6887 | 5139 | 5521 | 5083 | 5438 |

The value is larger for Ga than for Al (the poorly screening 3d shell is filled) and larger for Tl than for In (the 4f and 5d shells are filled), an effect of the same kind as the lanthanide contraction of [6.1.2](/6.1.2). Second, the orbitals of heavy atoms are large and diffuse and form weak covalent bonds, so the energy released by forming two more bonds does not compensate for the energy needed to remove two more s electrons.

### 14.1.3. Redox Behavior and Frost Diagrams

The p-block elements have many oxidation states, and redox behavior is at the heart of their chemistry. If the oxidation states of an element in a given medium are plotted according to its **Latimer diagram** ([6.1.12](/6.1.12)) as a **Frost diagram** (ordinate $NE^\circ$, that is, $-\Delta G^\circ/F$ of the species relative to the element; abscissa the oxidation number $N$), the following can be read at a glance:

- the species at the lowest point is the most stable thermodynamically;
- the slope of the line joining two species is the standard electrode potential of that couple; the greater the slope, the more strongly oxidizing the species of higher oxidation state;
- a species lying above the line joining its two neighbors disproportionates; one lying below it is formed by comproportionation of the neighbors.

![Frost diagrams of p-block elements at pH 0, with chlorine also at pH 14: (a) halogens, (b) chlorine in acid and base, (c) sulfur and oxygen, (d) nitrogen and phosphorus. E° values from the CRC Handbook](./plots/p-block-frost.py)

Many conclusions of the following sections can be read from the figure. In acid, the high oxidation states of the halogens and of $\ce{NO3-}$ have large slopes (strong oxidizing agents), and $\ce{X-}$, $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{NH4+}$ are the lowest points of their elements. In acid $\ce{Cl2}$ lies below the line joining $\ce{Cl-}$ and HClO, whereas in base it lies above the line joining $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{ClO-}$, so chlorine disproportionates in base (Section 14.2.2). $\ce{H2O2}$, $\ce{S2O3^2-}$, $\ce{HNO2}$ and $\ce{NH3OH+}$ all lie above the lines joining their neighbors and disproportionate. All the oxidation states of phosphorus lie below the zero line; $\ce{H3PO4}$ is the most stable and $\ce{H3PO2}$ and $\ce{H3PO3}$ are strong reducing agents (Section 14.4.4).

### 14.1.4. Nomenclature of Oxoacids

**Definition 14.1.2 (Oxoacid)** An acid whose molecules contain oxygen, with the dissociable hydrogen atoms bonded to oxygen (as O–H), is called an **oxoacid**.

An oxoacid can be written as $\ce{(HO)_kXO_m}$: the central atom X carries $k$ hydroxyl groups and $m$ non-hydroxyl oxygen atoms. The nomenclature rules for p-block oxoacids are as follows:

| Rule | Basis | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| hypo-, -ous, -ic, per- | oxidation state of the central atom from low to high | HClO hypochlorous acid (+1), $\ce{HClO2}$ chlorous acid (+3), $\ce{HClO3}$ chloric acid (+5), $\ce{HClO4}$ perchloric acid (+7) |
| ortho-, meta-, pyro-, poly- | degree of dehydration of acids of the same oxidation state | $\ce{H3PO4}$ orthophosphoric acid, $\ce{HPO3}$ metaphosphoric acid ($\ce{H3PO4}$ minus one $\ce{H2O}$), $\ce{H4P2O7}$ pyrophosphoric acid (two $\ce{H3PO4}$ minus one $\ce{H2O}$), $\ce{H5P3O10}$ triphosphoric acid |
| di-, tri-, tetrathionic (polythionic acids) | more than one acid-forming atom, directly bonded to each other | $\ce{H2S2O6}$ dithionic acid, $\ce{H2S4O6}$ tetrathionic acid |
| thio- | an O of the oxoacid is replaced by S | $\ce{H2S2O3}$ thiosulfuric acid |
| peroxo- | contains a peroxide bond –O–O– | $\ce{H2SO5}$ peroxomonosulfuric acid, $\ce{H2S2O8}$ peroxodisulfuric acid |

**Definition 14.1.3** An acid formed by the condensation (dehydration) of two or more molecules of the same oxoacid is called an **isopolyacid**, such as $\ce{H2Cr2O7}$ (dichromic acid) and $\ce{H4P2O7}$; an acid formed by condensation of molecules of different oxoacids is called a **heteropolyacid**, such as 12-molybdophosphoric acid $\ce{H3[PMo12O40]}$ (formed from $\ce{H3PO4}$ and 12 $\ce{H2MoO4}$).

The oxoanions of V, Cr, Mo, W and others condense stepwise to isopolyanions on acidification ([6.1.15](/6.1.15)); the ammonium salt of the heteropolyanion $\ce{[PMo12O40]^3-}$ is a yellow precipitate used to identify phosphate (Section 14.4.4). The structures of several oxoacids:

```smiles
OP(O)(=O)OP(=O)(O)O H4P2O7
OS(=O)(=O)O H2SO4
OS(=O)(=O)OO H2SO5
OS(=O)(=O)OOS(=O)(=O)O H2S2O8
```

### 14.1.5. Acidity of Oxoacids: Pauling's Rules

The acidity of an oxoacid is determined mainly by the number $m$ of non-hydroxyl oxygen atoms. Non-hydroxyl oxygen atoms attract electrons, so that the electrons of the O–H bond shift toward O, and they also spread the negative charge of the anion over more O atoms (the conjugate base is more stable). Pauling summarized two empirical rules:

1. The first dissociation constant of $\ce{(HO)_kXO_m}$ is $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a1}} \approx 7 - 5m$: $m = 0$ gives a very weak acid ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} \approx 7$), $m = 1$ a moderately strong acid ($\approx 2$), $m = 2$ a strong acid ($\approx -3$), and $m = 3$ a very strong acid ($\approx -8$). Some textbooks write $8 - 5m$; both are estimates good to the order of ±1.
2. For a polyprotic acid the $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ increases by about 5 with each successive dissociation ($\ce{H3PO4}$: 2.16, 7.21, 12.32; $\ce{H2SO3}$: 1.86, 7.2).

![First pKa of p-block oxoacids against the number of non-hydroxyl oxygen atoms m, compared with Pauling's rule pKa1 ≈ 7 − 5m. Open circles: estimated values of very strong acids](./plots/oxoacid-pka.py)

Pauling's rules are most useful for deducing structure from acidity. If $\ce{H3PO3}$ and $\ce{H3PO2}$ were $\ce{P(OH)3}$ and $\ce{HP(OH)2}$, they should be very weak acids like $\ce{As(OH)3}$ ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} = 9.2$); in fact their $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a1}}$ values are about 1.3 and 1.2, in agreement with $m = 1$. This shows that their structures are $\ce{HPO(OH)2}$ and $\ce{H2PO(OH)}$: each has one P=O and one or two H atoms bonded directly to P, which cannot dissociate, so phosphorous acid is diprotic and hypophosphorous acid is monoprotic (Section 14.4.4). Likewise, the $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} = 9.27$ of $\ce{H3BO3}$ agrees with $m = 0$, but it is not a protic acid but a **Lewis acid** (Section 14.6.4).

## 14.2. The Halogens

### 14.2.1. Overview

**Definition 14.2.1** The elements of Group 17, fluorine (F), chlorine (Cl), bromine (Br), iodine (I) and astatine (At), are collectively called the **halogens** ("salt formers").

At is radioactive and is not discussed below.

| | F | Cl | Br | I |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Valence configuration | $2\mathrm{s}^2\,2\mathrm{p}^5$ | $3\mathrm{s}^2\,3\mathrm{p}^5$ | $4\mathrm{s}^2\,4\mathrm{p}^5$ | $5\mathrm{s}^2\,5\mathrm{p}^5$ |
| Common oxidation states | −1 | −1, +1, +3, +5, +7 | −1, +1, +5, +7 | −1, +1, +5, +7 |
| Covalent radius / pm | 64 | 99 | 114 | 133 |
| $\ce{X-}$ radius / pm | 133 | 181 | 196 | 220 |
| First ionization energy / $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ | 1681 | 1251 | 1140 | 1008 |
| Electron affinity / $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ | 328 | 349 | 325 | 295 |
| Electronegativity | 3.98 | 3.16 | 2.96 | 2.66 |
| X–X bond enthalpy / $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ | 159 | 242 | 193 | 151 |
| $E^\circ(\ce{X2}/\ce{X-})$ / V | 2.866 | 1.358 | 1.066 | 0.5355 |
| Element (room temperature) | pale yellow gas | yellow-green gas | red-brown liquid | purple-black solid (violet vapor) |

A halogen atom lacks one electron of the noble-gas structure, and the halogens have the highest electronegativities of their periods; the elements are diatomic molecules and are all oxidizing agents. F, the most electronegative element, shows only the oxidation state −1 in compounds; the other halogens can show positive oxidation states when combined with the more electronegative O and F.

Anomaly of F. Its electron affinity is smaller than that of Cl, and the F–F bond is much weaker than the Cl–Cl bond because the atoms are so small (Section 14.1.1). Nevertheless $\ce{F2}$ is the strongest oxidizing agent.

**Example 14.2.1** Use a thermochemical cycle to explain the order of $E^\circ(\ce{X2}/\ce{X-})$: $\ce{F2} > \ce{Cl2} > \ce{Br2} > \ce{I2}$.

Solution. We split the half-reaction $\ce{1/2 X2(\text{standard state}) + e- -> X-(aq)}$ into three steps: dissociation to gaseous atoms (for $\ce{Br2(l)}$ and $\ce{I2(s)}$ including vaporization or sublimation), electron attachment to the atom ($-E_{\mathrm{ea}}$), and hydration of $\ce{X-}$. The enthalpy changes of the steps are ($\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$; the hydration enthalpies are commonly used approximate values):

| | F | Cl | Br | I |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{1/2 X2 -> X(g)}$ | 79 | 121 | 112 | 107 |
| $\ce{X(g) + e- -> X-(g)}$ | −328 | −349 | −325 | −295 |
| $\ce{X-(g) -> X-(aq)}$ | −510 | −367 | −336 | −291 |
| Sum | −759 | −595 | −549 | −479 |

The more negative the sum, the easier the electron uptake and the larger $E^\circ$; the order agrees with experiment. The electron affinity of F is $21\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ smaller than that of Cl, but its dissociation enthalpy is $42$ smaller and its hydration enthalpy $143\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ larger, and the latter two terms are decisive. From the enthalpy difference, the potential difference between $\ce{F2}$ and $\ce{Cl2}$ is estimated to be about $164/96.5 = 1.7\,\mathrm{V}$, compared with the actual $1.51\,\mathrm{V}$; the discrepancy comes from the entropy changes neglected here (small ions lose more entropy on hydration). $\square$

### 14.2.2. Chemical Properties of the Elements

The oxidizing power decreases in the order $\ce{F2} > \ce{Cl2} > \ce{Br2} > \ce{I2}$, and the preceding halogen displaces the following ones from their salts ($\ce{Cl2 + 2Br- -> 2Cl- + Br2}$). $\ce{F2}$ combines directly with almost all the elements (including Xe) and explodes with $\ce{H2}$ even in the dark at low temperature.

There are two types of reaction with water:

| Type | Reaction | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Oxidation of water | $\ce{2X2 + 2H2O -> 4HX + O2}$ | $\ce{F2}$ reacts violently; for $\ce{Cl2}$ it is thermodynamically possible ($1.358\,\mathrm{V} > 1.229\,\mathrm{V}$) but very slow and requires light; $\ce{Br2}$ reacts extremely slowly; for $\ce{I2}$ the reverse reaction proceeds: $\ce{4HI + O2 -> 2I2 + 2H2O}$ |
| Disproportionation | $\ce{X2 + H2O <=> H+ + X- + HXO}$ | $K^\circ(\ce{Cl2}) = 4.2\times10^{-4}$, $K^\circ(\ce{Br2}) = 7.2\times10^{-9}$, $K^\circ(\ce{I2}) = 2.0\times10^{-13}$ ($25\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) |

**Example 14.2.2** At $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the total concentration of dissolved chlorine in saturated chlorine water is about $0.090\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and that in saturated bromine water about $0.21\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. Estimate the fraction that has disproportionated.

Solution. Let $x$ be the concentration disproportionated, so that $[\ce{H+}] = [\ce{Cl-}] = [\ce{HClO}] = x$ and $x^3/(0.090 - x) = 4.2\times10^{-4}$. Iteration or numerical root finding gives $x = 0.030\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$: about one third of the chlorine has disproportionated. For bromine water, $x^3 = 7.2\times10^{-9} \times 0.21$ gives $x = 1.1\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, only about 0.5%. Chlorine water therefore contains, besides $\ce{Cl2}$, a considerable amount of HClO (to which its bleaching and disinfecting action is due), whereas bromine and iodine water consist mainly of the free elements. $\square$

Base shifts the disproportionation equilibrium strongly to the right, and the halogens dissolve in base by rapid disproportionation. There are two modes of disproportionation, and the product depends on the halogen and the temperature because the $\ce{XO-}$ formed itself disproportionates further ($\ce{3XO- -> 2X- + XO3-}$):

$$
\ce{X2 + 2OH- -> X- + XO- + H2O}, \qquad \ce{3X2 + 6OH- -> 5X- + XO3- + 3H2O}.
$$

| Halogen | Room temperature | Heated (about $70\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) | Around $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Cl2}$ | $\ce{ClO-}$ | $\ce{ClO3-}$ | $\ce{ClO-}$ |
| $\ce{Br2}$ | $\ce{BrO3-}$ | $\ce{BrO3-}$ | $\ce{BrO-}$ |
| $\ce{I2}$ | $\ce{IO3-}$ | $\ce{IO3-}$ | $\ce{IO3-}$ |

The disproportionation of $\ce{XO-}$ is always thermodynamically favorable, and the differences lie in the rate: $\ce{ClO-}$ disproportionates very slowly at room temperature, $\ce{BrO-}$ fairly quickly above $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and $\ce{IO-}$ very quickly at any temperature. Passing $\ce{Cl2}$ into cold NaOH solution therefore gives sodium hypochlorite (the active ingredient of household bleach), and passing it into hot KOH solution gives potassium chlorate.

### 14.2.3. Oxoacids of the Halogens and Their Salts

In the halogen oxoacids HXO, $\ce{HXO2}$, $\ce{HXO3}$ and $\ce{HXO4}$, X is $\mathrm{sp}^3$ hybridized (four valence electron pairs in VSEPR terms) and carries 3, 2, 1 and 0 lone pairs, respectively, so the anions are linear, bent, trigonal pyramidal and regular tetrahedral. The known acids are HClO, $\ce{HClO2}$, $\ce{HClO3}$, $\ce{HClO4}$, HBrO, $\ce{HBrO3}$, $\ce{HBrO4}$, HIO, $\ce{HIO3}$ and $\ce{H5IO6}$; of these only $\ce{HClO4}$, $\ce{HIO3}$ and $\ce{H5IO6}$ can be obtained as pure substances, and the others exist only in solution. The I atom is large and can accommodate six O atoms around it, so periodic acid exists mainly as orthoperiodic acid $\ce{H5IO6}$ (octahedral), which loses water to give metaperiodic acid $\ce{HIO4}$.

```smiles
OCl HClO
OCl=O HClO2
OCl(=O)=O HClO3
OCl(=O)(=O)=O HClO4
OI(O)(O)(O)(O)=O H5IO6
```

The hypohalous acids are all weak acids ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$: HClO 7.53, HBrO 8.6, HIO 10.6); they are very unstable and are strong oxidizing agents ($E^\circ(\ce{HXO}/\ce{X-})$: 1.482, 1.331, 0.987 V):

$$
\ce{2HClO ->[\text{light}] 2HCl + O2 ^}, \qquad \ce{3HClO ->[\Delta] HClO3 + 2HCl}.
$$
Hypochlorites are more stable than hypochlorous acid. The action of $\ce{Cl2}$ on cold slaked lime gives bleaching powder, $\ce{2Cl2 + 2Ca(OH)2 -> Ca(ClO)2 + CaCl2 + 2H2O}$ (the commercial product is a mixture of $\ce{Ca(ClO)2}$, $\ce{CaCl2}$, $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ and so on); its active ingredient $\ce{Ca(ClO)2}$ absorbs $\ce{CO2}$ from the air to give HClO, which does the bleaching.

The halic acids $\ce{HClO3}$ and $\ce{HBrO3}$ are strong acids and $\ce{HIO3}$ is a moderately strong acid ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} = 0.78$). The stability of the anions increases in the order $\ce{ClO3-} < \ce{BrO3-} < \ce{IO3-}$, and iodic acid is the only halic acid obtainable as crystals. In acid they are strong oxidizing agents ($E^\circ(\ce{XO3-}/\ce{X2})$: 1.47, 1.48, 1.20 V). The magnitudes of the potentials allow preparative reactions to be devised:

$$
\ce{2ClO3- + I2 -> 2IO3- + Cl2}, \qquad \ce{5Cl2 + I2 + 6H2O -> 2HIO3 + 10HCl}.
$$
Potassium chlorate decomposes on heating: with $\ce{MnO2}$ as a catalyst it releases oxygen ($\ce{2KClO3 -> 2KCl + 3O2}$), and without a catalyst it disproportionates to perchlorate at about $400\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ ($\ce{4KClO3 -> 3KClO4 + KCl}$).

The perhalic acids are all strong oxidizing agents. $\ce{HClO4}$ is the strongest common inorganic acid; when concentrated and hot it is an extremely powerful oxidizing agent (it can explode on contact with organic matter), but in dilute solution it is a very weak oxidizing agent for kinetic reasons, and $\ce{ClO4-}$ is one of the least coordinating anions. $\ce{HBrO4}$ was not prepared until 1968, and $E^\circ(\ce{BrO4-}/\ce{BrO3-}) = 1.85\,\mathrm{V}$ is larger than those of Cl and I (fourth-period elements reach their highest oxidation state with difficulty; arsenic acid and selenic acid are also particularly strongly oxidizing). $\ce{H5IO6}$ is a weak acid and oxidizes $\ce{Mn^2+}$ to $\ce{MnO4-}$ in acid:

$$
\ce{5H5IO6 + 2Mn^2+ -> 2MnO4- + 5IO3- + 11H+ + 7H2O}.
$$

The general trend is that, for the oxoacids of one element, the higher the oxidation state, the stronger the acidity (Section 14.1.5), the higher the thermal stability and the weaker the oxidizing power (measured by reaction rate): $\ce{HClO} > \ce{HClO2} > \ce{HClO3} > \ce{HClO4}$.

Identification in a mixed solution of $\ce{I-}$ and $\ce{Br-}$. Add $\ce{CCl4}$ and then chlorine water dropwise with shaking. $\ce{I-}$ is oxidized first, and the $\ce{CCl4}$ layer turns purple-red ($\ce{I2}$). On further addition of chlorine water, $\ce{I2}$ is oxidized to colorless $\ce{HIO3}$ and the purple-red color disappears. Then $\ce{Br-}$ is oxidized, the $\ce{CCl4}$ layer turns orange-yellow ($\ce{Br2}$), and the color does not disappear on adding more chlorine water. This sequence is determined entirely by the electrode potentials:

$$
E^\circ(\ce{I2}/\ce{I-}) = 0.54 < E^\circ(\ce{Br2}/\ce{Br-}) = 1.07 < E^\circ(\ce{IO3-}/\ce{I2}) = 1.20 < E^\circ(\ce{Cl2}/\ce{Cl-}) = 1.36 < E^\circ(\ce{BrO3-}/\ce{Br2}) = 1.48\ \mathrm{V}.
$$
$\ce{Cl2}$ cannot oxidize $\ce{Br2}$ to $\ce{BrO3-}$ ($1.36 < 1.48$); this succeeds only with concentrated chlorine water, and then only barely. In base the situation is different: $E^\circ(\ce{ClO-}/\ce{Cl-}) = 0.81\,\mathrm{V}$ is greater than $E^\circ(\ce{BrO3-}/\ce{Br-}) = 0.61\,\mathrm{V}$, so hypochlorite can oxidize $\ce{Br-}$: $\ce{3ClO- + Br- -> BrO3- + 3Cl-}$. All half-reactions of oxoanions involve $\ce{H+}$, so according to the **Nernst equation** their oxidizing power in acid is always much greater than in base ([6.1.11](/6.1.11)).

## 14.3. The Chalcogens

### 14.3.1. Overview

Group 16 (Group VIA in older Chinese textbooks) comprises oxygen (O), sulfur (S), selenium (Se), tellurium (Te) and polonium (Po). O and S are typical nonmetals, Se and Te are metalloids (Se is a **semiconductor** used in photocells and electrostatic copying), and Po is a radioactive metal. O and S occur as the elements or in minerals (oxides, sulfides, sulfates), and Se and Te occur together in the sulfide ores of the heavy metals.

| | O | S | Se | Te |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Valence configuration | $2\mathrm{s}^2\,2\mathrm{p}^4$ | $3\mathrm{s}^2\,3\mathrm{p}^4$ | $4\mathrm{s}^2\,4\mathrm{p}^4$ | $5\mathrm{s}^2\,5\mathrm{p}^4$ |
| Common oxidation states | −2 (−1) | −2, +4, +6 | −2, +4, +6 | −2, +4, +6 |
| Covalent radius / pm | 66 | 104 | 117 | 137 |
| First ionization energy / $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ | 1314 | 1000 | 941 | 869 |
| Electron affinity / $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ | 141 | 200 | 195 | 190 |
| Electronegativity | 3.44 | 2.58 | 2.55 | 2.10 |

The electronegativity of O is second only to that of F, and O is always in a negative oxidation state except in its compounds with F ($\ce{OF2}$); S, Se and Te can show +4 and +6. S is second only to C in its ability to form chains (S–S bonds), forming $\ce{S8}$, polysulfide ions $\ce{S_x^2-}$, polythionic acids and so on.

### 14.3.2. Oxygen and Hydrogen Peroxide

**Definition 14.3.1** Several forms of an element that differ in structure or properties are called **allotropes** of one another.

Oxygen has two allotropes, $\ce{O2}$ and ozone $\ce{O3}$ (bent, with a delocalized π bond $\Pi_3^4$; a strong oxidizing agent; the stratospheric ozone layer absorbs ultraviolet radiation). The molecular orbital configuration of $\ce{O2}$ is

$$
\mathrm{KK}\,(\sigma_{2\mathrm{s}})^2(\sigma^*_{2\mathrm{s}})^2(\sigma_{2\mathrm{p}})^2(\pi_{2\mathrm{p}})^4(\pi^*_{2\mathrm{p}})^2,
$$
the **bond order** is 2, and the two $\pi^*$ electrons have parallel spins, so $\ce{O2}$ is a **paramagnetic substance** ([6.1.3](/6.1.3#sec-3.6.3)). The oxidizing power of $\ce{O2}$ depends on the acidity:

$$
\ce{O2 + 4H+ + 4e- -> 2H2O}, \quad E^\circ_{\mathrm{A}} = 1.229\,\mathrm{V}; \qquad \ce{O2 + 2H2O + 4e- -> 4OH-}, \quad E^\circ_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.401\,\mathrm{V}.
$$
The two differ by $14 \times 0.05916 = 0.828\,\mathrm{V}$ (Nernst equation; the potential–pH diagram of water is given in [6.1.12](/6.1.12)).

The hydrogen peroxide ($\ce{H2O2}$) molecule contains an O–O single bond ($147.5\,\mathrm{pm}$), and the two O–H bonds are not in one plane but, like a half-open book, form a dihedral angle of about $111^\circ$ (gas phase). Its properties reflect the –OH and –O–O– groups:

(1) Weak acidity. $\ce{H2O2 <=> H+ + HO2-}$, $K_{\mathrm{a1}} = 2.2\times10^{-12}$ ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a1}} = 11.65$); with bases it forms peroxides: $\ce{H2O2 + Ba(OH)2 -> BaO2 + 2H2O}$.

(2) Instability. $\ce{2H2O2(l) -> 2H2O(l) + O2(g)}$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H^\circ_{\mathrm{m}} = -196\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. On the Frost diagram $\ce{H2O2}$ lies above the line joining $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ and is thermodynamically unstable. Pure $\ce{H2O2}$ decomposes very slowly, but $\ce{MnO2}$, heavy metal ions, light and catalase all make it decompose rapidly (**catalysts**, [6.1.7](/6.1.7)); it is therefore stored in brown plastic bottles with stabilizers added.

(3) Redox behavior. The Latimer diagram (V):

$$
\text{acidic: }\ \ce{O2 ->[0.695] H2O2 ->[1.776] H2O}; \qquad \text{basic: }\ \ce{HO2- + H2O + 2e- -> 3OH-}, \quad E^\circ_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.878\,\mathrm{V}.
$$

| Role | Reaction | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Oxidizing agent (acidic) | $\ce{H2O2 + 2Fe^2+ + 2H+ -> 2Fe^3+ + 2H2O}$ | |
| | $\ce{H2O2 + 2I- + 2H+ -> I2 + 2H2O}$ | test for $\ce{H2O2}$ |
| | $\ce{4H2O2 + PbS(s, black) -> PbSO4(s, white) + 4H2O}$ | restoring old paintings whose lead white pigment was blackened by $\ce{H2S}$ |
| Oxidizing agent (basic) | $\ce{3H2O2 + 2[Cr(OH)4]- + 2OH- -> 2CrO4^2- + 8H2O}$ | identification of Cr(III) |
| Reducing agent | $\ce{5H2O2 + 2MnO4- + 6H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 5O2 ^ + 8H2O}$ | determination of $\ce{H2O2}$ content |
| | $\ce{H2O2 + Cl2 -> 2HCl + O2}$ | removal of residual chlorine |

$\ce{H2O2}$ is strongly oxidizing and weakly reducing; its products are $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{O2}$, which introduce no impurities, so it is a "green" oxidizing agent and disinfectant.

### 14.3.3. Elemental Sulfur, Hydrogen Sulfide and Sulfides

The most stable form of elemental sulfur is the $\ce{S8}$ molecule: eight S atoms (approximately $\mathrm{sp}^3$ hybridized, S–S–S angle about $108^\circ$) joined in a crown-shaped ring.

```smiles
S1SSSSSSS1 S8
```

The allotropes of sulfur:

| | Orthorhombic sulfur (α-sulfur) | Monoclinic sulfur (β-sulfur) | Plastic sulfur |
|---|---|---|---|
| Composition | $\ce{S8}$ rings | $\ce{S8}$ rings | long chains $\ce{S_n}$ |
| Density / $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ | 2.07 | 1.96 | — |
| Color | yellow | pale yellow | brownish yellow |
| Range of stability | below $95.3\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ | $95.3\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to the melting point (about $119\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) | metastable |

$$
\ce{S(orthorhombic) <=>[95.3\,^\circ C] S(monoclinic)}.
$$
Natural sulfur and sulfur at room temperature are orthorhombic. Above about $160\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the $\ce{S8}$ rings in liquid sulfur open and polymerize to long chains, and the viscosity increases sharply. If molten sulfur at about $200\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is poured into cold water and cooled rapidly, the long chains have no time to close into rings again and elastic plastic sulfur is obtained, which gradually reverts to orthorhombic sulfur on standing.

Hydrogen sulfide ($\ce{H2S}$) is a highly toxic gas with the smell of rotten eggs; its aqueous solution is a weak diprotic acid ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a1}} \approx 7.0$; the dissociation of $\ce{HS-}$ is extremely weak, reliable determinations of $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a2}}$ give about 17–19, and the values $10^{-13}$–$10^{-14}$ commonly given in tables are outdated, see [6.1.9](/6.1.9)). The Latimer diagram of sulfur in acidic medium (V):

$$
\ce{SO4^2- ->[0.172] H2SO3 ->[0.449] S ->[0.142] H2S}.
$$
$\ce{H2S}$ is a commonly used reducing agent, and the product depends on the strength of the oxidizing agent: with oxidizing agents of moderate strength S is formed, and with strong (or excess) oxidizing agents $\ce{SO4^2-}$ is formed:

| Oxidizing agent | Reaction |
|---|---|
| $\ce{Fe^3+}$ | $\ce{H2S + 2Fe^3+ -> S v + 2Fe^2+ + 2H+}$ |
| $\ce{SO2}$ | $\ce{2H2S + SO2 -> 3S + 2H2O}$ |
| $\ce{MnO4-}$ (small amount) | $\ce{5H2S + 2MnO4- + 6H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 5S v + 8H2O}$ |
| $\ce{MnO4-}$ (excess) | $\ce{5H2S + 8MnO4- + 14H+ -> 8Mn^2+ + 5SO4^2- + 12H2O}$ |
| $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{Br2}$ (excess) | $\ce{H2S + 4X2 + 4H2O -> H2SO4 + 8HX}$ |

Most metal sulfides are sparingly soluble and colored, and most are black; the exceptions must be remembered: ZnS is white, MnS flesh-colored, CdS yellow, SnS brown, $\ce{SnS2}$ yellow, $\ce{As2S3}$ and $\ce{As2S5}$ yellow, and $\ce{Sb2S3}$ and $\ce{Sb2S5}$ orange. $\ce{Al2S3}$ and $\ce{Cr2S3}$ are completely hydrolyzed in water and cannot be prepared by precipitation: $\ce{2Al^3+ + 3S^2- + 6H2O -> 2Al(OH)3 v + 3H2S ^}$.

The solubilities of sulfides differ greatly, which is the basis for the separation of metal ions in qualitative analysis (the quantitative treatment uses $K_{\mathrm{spa}}$, [6.1.10](/6.1.10)). According to the reagent needed for dissolution they fall into four groups:

| Dissolution conditions | Sulfides | Reaction | Principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dilute hydrochloric acid ($0.3\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$) | MnS, FeS, ZnS | $\ce{MS + 2H+ -> M^2+ + H2S ^}$ | relatively large solubility product; binding of $\ce{S^2-}$ by $\ce{H+}$ suffices |
| Concentrated hydrochloric acid | PbS, CdS, SnS, $\ce{SnS2}$, $\ce{Bi2S3}$ | $\ce{PbS + 4HCl -> H2[PbCl4] + H2S ^}$, $\ce{SnS2 + 6HCl -> H2[SnCl6] + 2H2S ^}$ | acid dissolution plus coordination by $\ce{Cl-}$ |
| Nitric acid | CuS, $\ce{Ag2S}$ | $\ce{3CuS + 8HNO3 -> 3Cu(NO3)2 + 3S v + 2NO ^ + 4H2O}$ | oxidation of $\ce{S^2-}$ to S |
| Aqua regia | HgS | $\ce{3HgS + 2HNO3 + 12HCl -> 3H2[HgCl4] + 3S v + 2NO ^ + 4H2O}$ | oxidation plus coordination |

Compounds containing polysulfide ions $\ce{S_x^2-}$ are called *polysulfides*, such as $\ce{Na2S_x}$ and $\ce{(NH4)2S_x}$ ($x = 2$–$6$); the polysulfide ions are zigzag chains of S atoms, and their solutions are yellow to red-brown. They are obtained by dissolving sulfur in $\ce{Na2S}$ solution: $\ce{Na2S + (x-1)S -> Na2S_x}$. Polysulfides decompose with acids: $\ce{S_x^2- + 2H+ -> H2S ^ + (x-1)S v}$. They are oxidizing and can dissolve SnS: $\ce{SnS + S2^2- -> SnS3^2-}$; they are also reducing, and pyrite $\ce{FeS2}$ (which contains $\ce{S2^2-}$) is oxidized on roasting: $\ce{3FeS2 + 8O2 -> Fe3O4 + 6SO2}$ (one of the sources of feed gas for the sulfuric acid industry).

### 14.3.4. Oxides and Oxoacids of Sulfur

$\ce{SO2}$ is a bent molecule: S is $\mathrm{sp}^2$ hybridized, $\angle\ce{OSO} = 119.5^\circ$, the S–O bond length is $143.2\,\mathrm{pm}$, and the molecule contains a delocalized π bond $\Pi_3^4$; it is a colorless, pungent gas that is very soluble in water. $\ce{SO3}$ (gas) is a trigonal planar molecule with $\angle\ce{OSO} = 120^\circ$, S–O $143\,\mathrm{pm}$, and contains $\Pi_4^6$; in the solid it polymerizes to a cyclic trimer or long chains. The S–O bonds are much shorter than single bonds and have considerable double-bond character, which is described here with **delocalized π bonds** ([6.1.3](/6.1.3#sec-3.4.4)).

The aqueous solution of $\ce{SO2}$ is called sulfurous acid; it contains mainly hydrated $\ce{SO2}$ and $\ce{HSO3-}$, with very few free $\ce{H2SO3}$ molecules, and it is a moderately strong diprotic acid ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a1}} = 1.86$). S(IV) is in an intermediate oxidation state and is both an oxidizing and a reducing agent, mainly the latter, and it is more strongly reducing in base:

$$
E^\circ_{\mathrm{A}}(\ce{SO4^2-}/\ce{H2SO3}) = 0.172\,\mathrm{V}, \qquad E^\circ_{\mathrm{B}}(\ce{SO4^2-}/\ce{SO3^2-}) = -0.93\,\mathrm{V};
$$

$$
\ce{H2SO3 + 2H2S -> 3S v + 3H2O}\ (\text{oxidizing}), \qquad \ce{H2SO3 + I2 + H2O -> H2SO4 + 2HI}\ (\text{reducing}).
$$

Sulfuric acid is a strong diprotic acid (second stage $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a2}} = 1.99$). Concentrated sulfuric acid (98%) is strongly hygroscopic and dehydrating (it chars sucrose and paper) and is strongly oxidizing when hot; the extent to which it is reduced depends on the strength of the reducing agent:

| Reducing agent | Reaction | Product from sulfuric acid |
|---|---|---|
| Unreactive metals | $\ce{Cu + 2H2SO4(conc) ->[\Delta] CuSO4 + SO2 ^ + 2H2O}$ | $\ce{SO2}$ |
| Reactive metals | $\ce{Zn + 2H2SO4(conc) -> ZnSO4 + SO2 ^ + 2H2O}$, $\ce{3Zn + 4H2SO4(conc) -> 3ZnSO4 + S v + 4H2O}$, $\ce{4Zn + 5H2SO4(conc) -> 4ZnSO4 + H2S ^ + 4H2O}$ | $\ce{SO2}$, S or $\ce{H2S}$; the extent of reduction increases as the acid becomes more dilute |
| Nonmetals | $\ce{C + 2H2SO4(conc) ->[\Delta] CO2 ^ + 2SO2 ^ + 2H2O}$, $\ce{S + 2H2SO4(conc) ->[\Delta] 3SO2 ^ + 2H2O}$, $\ce{2P + 5H2SO4(conc) ->[\Delta] 2H3PO4 + 5SO2 ^ + 2H2O}$ | $\ce{SO2}$ |

**Definition 14.3.2 (Passivation)** The phenomenon that a metal in contact with a strongly oxidizing acid (cold concentrated sulfuric or nitric acid) forms a dense protective oxide film on its surface and is no longer corroded is called **passivation**.

Fe, Al and Cr are passivated by cold concentrated sulfuric and nitric acids, so concentrated sulfuric acid can be stored and transported in iron tanks. The corrosion resistance of stainless steel and aluminum ware also comes from a passive film on the surface ([6.1.12](/6.1.12)).

Sodium thiosulfate $\ce{Na2S2O3*5H2O}$ ("hypo") can be regarded as $\ce{SO4^2-}$ with one O replaced by S and is tetrahedral; it is prepared by boiling $\ce{Na2SO3}$ solution with sulfur powder: $\ce{Na2SO3 + S -> Na2S2O3}$. Its properties:

| Property | Reaction | Remark |
|---|---|---|
| Decomposition by acid | $\ce{S2O3^2- + 2H+ -> S v + SO2 ^ + H2O}$ | disproportionation; on the Frost diagram $\ce{S2O3^2-}$ lies above the line joining S and $\ce{H2SO3}$ |
| Moderate reducing agent | $\ce{2S2O3^2- + I2 -> S4O6^2- + 2I-}$ | forms tetrathionate; used in iodometry ([Analytical Chemistry](/6.4)) |
| With strong oxidizing agents | $\ce{S2O3^2- + 4Cl2 + 5H2O -> 2SO4^2- + 8Cl- + 10H+}$ | used as a dechlorinating agent in the textile industry |
| Coordination | $\ce{Ag+ + 2S2O3^2- -> [Ag(S2O3)2]^3-}$ | photographic fixing: dissolves unexposed AgBr |

```smiles
[O-]S(=O)(=S)[O-] S2O3^2-
```

Peroxodisulfuric acid can be regarded as $\ce{H2O2}$ with both H atoms replaced by $\ce{-SO3H}$ (for the structure see Section 14.1.4); it contains an –O–O– bond, S remains +6, and the peroxide bond is responsible for the oxidizing action. Its salts $\ce{K2S2O8}$ and $\ce{(NH4)2S2O8}$ are among the strongest oxidizing agents ($E^\circ(\ce{S2O8^2-}/\ce{SO4^2-}) = 2.010\,\mathrm{V}$) and, catalyzed by $\ce{Ag+}$, oxidize $\ce{Mn^2+}$ to $\ce{MnO4-}$ (identification of $\ce{Mn^2+}$):

$$
\ce{2Mn^2+ + 5S2O8^2- + 8H2O ->[Ag+] 2MnO4- + 10SO4^2- + 16H+}.
$$
Peroxodisulfates have low thermal stability: $\ce{2K2S2O8 ->[\Delta] 2K2SO4 + 2SO3 + O2}$.

## 14.4. The Pnictogens

### 14.4.1. Overview

Group 15 (Group VA in older Chinese textbooks) comprises nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), arsenic (As), antimony (Sb) and bismuth (Bi), with the valence configuration $n\mathrm{s}^2\,n\mathrm{p}^3$. N and P are nonmetals, As and Sb are metalloids, and Bi is a metal.

| | N | P | As | Sb | Bi |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Covalent radius / pm | 70 | 110 | 121 | 141 | 152 |
| First ionization energy / $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ | 1402 | 1012 | 947 | 834 | 703 |
| Electronegativity | 3.04 | 2.19 | 2.18 | 2.05 | 2.02 |
| Common oxidation states | −3 to +5 | −3, +3, +5 | −3, +3, +5 | +3, +5 | +3 (+5) |

The stability of the +5 oxidation state first decreases, then increases and then decreases again down the group: $\ce{HNO3}$ is a strong oxidizing agent, $\ce{H3PO4}$ is very stable, arsenic acid is somewhat oxidizing, and Bi(V), because of the inert-pair effect, is an extremely strong oxidizing agent ($\ce{NaBiO3}$ oxidizes $\ce{Mn^2+}$ to $\ce{MnO4-}$, [6.1.15](/6.1.15)).

### 14.4.2. Nitrogen, Ammonia and Ammonium Salts

The molecular orbital configuration of $\ce{N2}$ is $\mathrm{KK}\,(\sigma_{2\mathrm{s}})^2(\sigma^*_{2\mathrm{s}})^2(\pi_{2\mathrm{p}})^4(\sigma_{2\mathrm{p}})^2$ (because of 2s–2p interaction, $\sigma_{2\mathrm{p}}$ lies above $\pi_{2\mathrm{p}}$, [6.1.3](/6.1.3#sec-3.6.3)), and the bond order is 3. The bond enthalpy of $\ce{N#N}$ ($945\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$) is very large and so is the HOMO–LUMO gap, so $\ce{N2}$ is extremely unreactive at room temperature. Its conversion to compounds (nitrogen fixation) relies industrially on catalytic ammonia synthesis at high temperature and pressure ([6.1.8](/6.1.8)), and in nature on nitrogenase.

$\ce{NH3}$ is a trigonal pyramidal molecule (N is unequally $\mathrm{sp}^3$ hybridized); hydrogen bonding between the molecules makes it easy to liquefy (boiling point $-33\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), and it is extremely soluble in water. The lone pair on N makes it a Brønsted base ($\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH-}$, $K_{\mathrm{b}} = 1.8\times10^{-5}$) and a **Lewis base** (forming ammine complex ions such as $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4]^2+}$ and $\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]+}$). The $\ce{NH4+}$ ion in ammonium salts is similar in size to $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Rb+}$, and ammonium salts are often isomorphous with the corresponding potassium salts and have similar solubilities. Ammonium salts decompose on heating, and the products depend on the anion: ammonium salts of volatile acids decompose to $\ce{NH3}$ and the acid ($\ce{NH4Cl -> NH3 + HCl}$), whereas ammonium salts of oxidizing acids undergo redox reactions ($\ce{NH4NO3 ->[\Delta] N2O + 2H2O}$, and at higher temperatures $\ce{2NH4NO3 -> 2N2 + O2 + 4H2O}$, which can be explosive).

Identification of $\ce{NH4+}$: on heating with NaOH, the $\ce{NH3}$ released turns moist red litmus paper blue; alternatively, with Nessler's reagent (a KOH solution of $\ce{K2[HgI4]}$) a red-brown precipitate forms:

$$
\ce{NH4+ + 2[HgI4]^2- + 4OH- -> [OHg2NH2]I v + 7I- + 3H2O}.
$$

### 14.4.3. Oxides and Oxoacids of Nitrogen

Nitrogen forms oxides in the oxidation states +1 to +5: $\ce{N2O}$, NO, $\ce{N2O3}$, $\ce{NO2}$/$\ce{N2O4}$ and $\ce{N2O5}$. $\ce{NO2}$ is a toxic red-brown gas with a characteristic odor; the molecule is bent and contains a delocalized π bond $\Pi_3^3$ with a single electron. Because of the unpaired electron it dimerizes readily to colorless $\ce{N2O4}$ (exothermic, favored at low temperature, [6.1.8](/6.1.8)): below $-11\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ it is solid, colorless $\ce{N2O4}$, and above $140\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ it is almost entirely $\ce{NO2}$. The reactions of $\ce{NO2}$ with water and with base are both disproportionations:

$$
\ce{3NO2 + H2O -> 2HNO3 + NO}, \qquad \ce{2NO2 + 2NaOH -> NaNO3 + NaNO2 + H2O}.
$$

Nitrous acid is an unstable weak acid ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} = 3.25$) that exists only in cold dilute solution; on decomposition the solution first turns blue ($\ce{N2O3}$):

$$
\ce{2HNO2 <=> H2O + N2O3 <=> H2O + NO + NO2}.
$$
Nitrites are relatively stable, and almost all are colorless and soluble ($\ce{AgNO2}$ is pale yellow and slightly soluble). Nitrites are toxic and, under acidic conditions, can form carcinogenic nitrosamines with amines. N(III) is in an intermediate oxidation state ($E^\circ_{\mathrm{A}}(\ce{HNO2}/\ce{NO}) = 0.983\,\mathrm{V}$, $E^\circ_{\mathrm{A}}(\ce{NO3-}/\ce{HNO2}) = 0.934\,\mathrm{V}$) and is mainly oxidizing in acid, but it behaves as a reducing agent toward strong oxidizing agents:

$$
\ce{2NO2- + 2I- + 4H+ -> 2NO ^ + I2 + 2H2O}, \qquad \ce{NO2- + Fe^2+ + 2H+ -> NO ^ + Fe^3+ + H2O},
$$

$$
\ce{5NO2- + 2MnO4- + 6H+ -> 5NO3- + 2Mn^2+ + 3H2O}.
$$

The nitric acid molecule is planar with $\mathrm{sp}^2$ hybridized N; $\ce{NO3-}$ is trigonal planar and contains $\Pi_4^6$. Pure nitric acid is a colorless liquid (density $1.51\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$), and commercial concentrated nitric acid contains about 68% $\ce{HNO3}$ (density $1.4\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$). Nitric acid decomposes in light or on heating ($\ce{4HNO3 -> 4NO2 + O2 + 2H2O}$), and dissolved $\ce{NO2}$ makes concentrated nitric acid yellow, so it is stored in brown bottles.

The strong oxidizing power of nitric acid comes from N(V). It oxidizes most nonmetals to the corresponding oxoacids of high oxidation state and is itself usually reduced to NO:

$$
\ce{3P + 5HNO3 + 2H2O -> 3H3PO4 + 5NO ^}, \quad \ce{S + 2HNO3 -> H2SO4 + 2NO ^}, \quad \ce{C + 4HNO3(conc) -> CO2 ^ + 4NO2 ^ + 2H2O}.
$$
All metals except a few such as Au and Pt dissolve in nitric acid. The extent to which nitric acid is reduced depends on its concentration and on the reactivity of the metal: the more dilute the acid and the more reactive the metal, the lower the oxidation state to which nitric acid is reduced (in practice mixtures are often obtained; the table gives the main products):

| Metal | Nitric acid | Reaction | Main reduction product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cu | concentrated | $\ce{Cu + 4HNO3 -> Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO2 ^ + 2H2O}$ | $\ce{NO2}$ |
| Cu | dilute | $\ce{3Cu + 8HNO3 -> 3Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO ^ + 4H2O}$ | NO |
| Zn | concentrated | $\ce{Zn + 4HNO3 -> Zn(NO3)2 + 2NO2 ^ + 2H2O}$ | $\ce{NO2}$ |
| Zn | dilute (about 1:2) | $\ce{3Zn + 8HNO3 -> 3Zn(NO3)2 + 2NO ^ + 4H2O}$ | NO |
| Zn | more dilute (about $2\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$) | $\ce{4Zn + 10HNO3 -> 4Zn(NO3)2 + N2O ^ + 5H2O}$ | $\ce{N2O}$ |
| Zn | very dilute (about 1:10) | $\ce{4Zn + 10HNO3 -> 4Zn(NO3)2 + NH4NO3 + 3H2O}$ | $\ce{NH4+}$ |

That the product from concentrated nitric acid is $\ce{NO2}$ rather than NO is not because concentrated nitric acid is "less oxidizing": in concentrated nitric acid the NO formed is oxidized by $\ce{HNO3}$ ($\ce{NO + 2HNO3 <=> 3NO2 + H2O}$, with the equilibrium lying further to the right the more concentrated the acid), and the reaction is also much faster in the concentrated acid. Cold concentrated nitric acid causes the **passivation** of Fe, Al and Cr.

**Definition 14.4.1 (Aqua Regia)** A solution prepared by mixing concentrated nitric acid and concentrated hydrochloric acid in a volume ratio of 1:3 is called **aqua regia**.

Aqua regia dissolves Au and Pt, which neither concentrated nitric acid nor concentrated hydrochloric acid dissolves alone:

$$
\ce{Au + HNO3 + 4HCl -> H[AuCl4] + NO ^ + 2H2O}, \qquad \ce{3Pt + 4HNO3 + 18HCl -> 3H2[PtCl6] + 4NO ^ + 8H2O}.
$$
The principle is "oxidation plus coordination": $\ce{HNO3}$ (and the NOCl and $\ce{Cl2}$ it forms with HCl) provides the oxidizing power, and the large amount of $\ce{Cl-}$ forms the stable $\ce{[AuCl4]-}$ with $\ce{Au^3+}$, lowering the electrode potential from $E^\circ(\ce{Au^3+}/\ce{Au}) = 1.50\,\mathrm{V}$ to $E^\circ(\ce{[AuCl4]-}/\ce{Au}) = 1.00\,\mathrm{V}$, below the potential of nitric acid (the effect of coordination on electrode potentials is discussed in [6.1.11](/6.1.11)).

Nitrates are almost all soluble in water; in aqueous solution they are oxidizing only under acidic conditions, whereas the solids are strong oxidizing agents at high temperature ($\ce{KNO3}$ in black powder). Nitrates have low thermal stability, and the decomposition products depend on the reactivity of the metal:

| Metal | Example | Products |
|---|---|---|
| More reactive than Mg (except Li) | $\ce{2NaNO3 ->[\Delta] 2NaNO2 + O2 ^}$ | nitrite + $\ce{O2}$ |
| Mg to Cu (and Li) | $\ce{2Pb(NO3)2 ->[\Delta] 2PbO + 4NO2 ^ + O2 ^}$, $\ce{2Cu(NO3)2 ->[\Delta] 2CuO + 4NO2 ^ + O2 ^}$ | metal oxide + $\ce{NO2}$ + $\ce{O2}$ |
| Less reactive than Cu | $\ce{2AgNO3 ->[\Delta] 2Ag + 2NO2 ^ + O2 ^}$ | metal + $\ce{NO2}$ + $\ce{O2}$ |

Nitrites of reactive metals are stable; nitrites of metals of intermediate reactivity are unstable whereas the oxides are stable; the oxides of unreactive metals also decompose.

### 14.4.4. Phosphorus and Its Compounds

Phosphorus has several allotropes:

| | White phosphorus | Red phosphorus | Black phosphorus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structure | tetrahedral $\ce{P4}$ molecules | chain polymer formed by ring opening of $\ce{P4}$ | layered, similar to graphite |
| Properties | reactive, ignites spontaneously in air at about $40\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, stored under water; soluble in $\ce{CS2}$; highly toxic | more stable, ignites only at a fairly high temperature, insoluble in organic solvents, nontoxic | the most stable, metallic luster, a semiconductor |
| Preparation | — | heating white phosphorus in the absence of air (about $250$–$400\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) | heating white phosphorus under high pressure |

White phosphorus is reactive because the P–P–P angle in $\ce{P4}$ is only $60^\circ$, much smaller than the angle between p orbitals, so the bonds are highly strained. White phosphorus is not the most stable form of phosphorus, but it is the defined reference state of the element ($\Delta_{\mathrm{f}}H^\circ = 0$, [6.1.6](/6.1.6)) because its composition and structure are the best defined.

Oxides of phosphorus. Phosphorus burns in a limited supply of oxygen to give $\ce{P4O6}$ and in an ample supply of oxygen to give $\ce{P4O10}$ (further oxidation of $\ce{P4O6}$ also gives $\ce{P4O10}$). $\ce{P4O6}$ has an O atom inserted in the middle of each edge of the $\ce{P4}$ tetrahedron; $\ce{P4O10}$ has in addition a terminal O on each P (figure above). $\ce{P4O6}$ is a white, volatile, waxy crystalline solid that dissolves readily in organic solvents. $\ce{P4O10}$ is a white, snowflake-like crystalline solid with an extremely strong affinity for water; it is one of the strongest drying agents and can even remove the "structural water" from $\ce{H2SO4}$ and $\ce{HNO3}$:

| Reaction | Remark |
|---|---|
| $\ce{P4O10 + 6H2O -> 4H3PO4}$ | stepwise hydration via metaphosphoric and pyrophosphoric acids |
| $\ce{P4O10 + 6H2SO4 -> 6SO3 + 4H3PO4}$, $\ce{P4O10 + 12HNO3 -> 6N2O5 + 4H3PO4}$ | dehydration of oxoacids to anhydrides |

Oxoacids of phosphorus (the structures are deduced from Pauling's rules, Section 14.1.5):

$$
\ce{H3PO4 ->[-0.276] H3PO3 ->[-0.499] H3PO2 ->[-0.508] P ->[-0.063] PH3},
$$

$$

Phosphate esters and pyrophosphate bonds form the backbone of energy and genetic material in living organisms (ATP, DNA, [Biology](/7)).

## 14.5. The Carbon Group

### 14.5.1. Overview

Group 14 (Group IVA in older Chinese textbooks) comprises carbon (C), silicon (Si), germanium (Ge), tin (Sn) and lead (Pb), with the valence configuration $n\mathrm{s}^2\,n\mathrm{p}^2$.

| | C | Si | Ge | Sn | Pb |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity | 2.55 | 1.90 | 2.01 | 1.96 | 2.33 |
| Oxidation states | −4, +2, +4 | +4 (+2) | +4, (+2) | +2, +4 | +2, (+4) |
| Maximum coordination number | 4 | 6 | 6 | 6 | 6 |
| Element | network solid (diamond), layered (graphite), molecular (fullerenes) | network solid, semiconductor | network solid, semiconductor | metal (gray tin is a network solid) | metal |

Down the group the stability of the +4 state decreases and that of +2 increases, and Pb is mainly in the +2 state (inert-pair effect). C has the greatest ability to form chains (C–C $346$, C–H $413\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$), which gives rise to the vast field of organic chemistry. Si–Si ($222$) is weak whereas Si–O ($452\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$) is strong, so silicon in nature always occurs as silica and silicates with an Si–O framework, which make up most of the Earth's crust.

### 14.5.2. Elemental Carbon

The allotropes of carbon:

| | Diamond | Graphite | Fullerene ($\ce{C60}$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hybridization | $\mathrm{sp}^3$ | $\mathrm{sp}^2$ | between $\mathrm{sp}^2$ and $\mathrm{sp}^3$ |
| Structure | each C tetrahedrally bonded to four C, C–C $154\,\mathrm{pm}$, network solid | layers of planar hexagonal nets, C–C $142\,\mathrm{pm}$ within the layer, interlayer spacing $335\,\mathrm{pm}$ | cage molecule composed of 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons |
| Properties | greatest hardness, highest melting point, excellent thermal conductor, insulator | soft, metallic luster, conducts along the layers, lubricant | soluble in benzene, toluene and so on, giving purple-red solutions |

Graphite has delocalized π electrons within the layers and only intermolecular forces between the layers; it is a mixed-type crystal ([6.1.4](/6.1.4#sec-4.5.2)). At ordinary temperature and pressure graphite is more stable than diamond ($\ce{C(graphite) -> C(diamond)}$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H^\circ_{\mathrm{m}} = 1.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G^\circ_{\mathrm{m}} = 2.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$); diamond persists because the activation energy of the conversion is extremely high. Synthetic diamond is made at high temperature and pressure (diamond has the higher density, so pressure favors it, [6.1.8](/6.1.8)).

**Definition 14.5.1 (Fullerene)** A closed cage molecule composed of carbon atoms and bounded by 12 pentagons and a number of hexagons is called a **fullerene**.

The most typical is $\ce{C60}$ (discovered in 1985 by Kroto, Curl and Smalley, Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1996), which has the shape of a soccer ball (a truncated icosahedron): 60 vertices, 90 edges, 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons, each C atom belonging to two hexagons and one pentagon. The three σ bond angles of a C atom add up to $348^\circ$ (average $116^\circ$), and the hybridization lies between the $\mathrm{sp}^2$ of graphite and the $\mathrm{sp}^3$ of diamond (about $\mathrm{sp}^{2.28}$). There are two different C–C bond lengths: $139\,\mathrm{pm}$ for the bond shared by two hexagons and $145\,\mathrm{pm}$ for the bond shared by a pentagon and a hexagon. There are also larger fullerenes such as $\ce{C70}$.

Why are there exactly 12 pentagons? Regard a fullerene as a convex polyhedron; its numbers of vertices, edges and faces satisfy [Euler's formula](/1.4.4#thm-4.1.1) of graph theory, $V - E + F = 2$. With $p$ pentagons and $h$ hexagons, three edges at every vertex and each edge belonging to two faces,

$$
2E = 3V = 5p + 6h, \qquad F = p + h.
$$
Substituting into Euler's formula gives $\frac{2E}{3} - E + p + h = 2$, that is, $p + h - \frac{5p + 6h}{6} = 2$, so that $p = 12$ independently of $h$. For $\ce{C60}$ $h = 20$, and for $\ce{C70}$ $h = 25$.

A single layer of graphite is called *graphene*, a two-dimensional crystal one atom thick, with high strength and excellent electrical and thermal conductivity; it was obtained in 2004 by Geim and Novoselov by peeling with adhesive tape (Nobel Prize in Physics 2010). A tube obtained by rolling up graphene is called a *carbon nanotube*, discovered by Iijima in 1991.

### 14.5.3. Oxides of Carbon and Carbonates

Carbon monoxide is isoelectronic with $\ce{N2}$, and there is a triple bond between C and O: one σ bond, one π bond and one π **coordinate bond** in which O supplies the electron pair (the Lewis structure is $\ce{^{-}:C#O:^{+}}$, and C carries a negative **formal charge**). The high electronegativity of O draws the electrons toward O, whereas the coordinate bond draws them toward C; the two nearly cancel, so the dipole moment of CO is very small ($0.11\,\mathrm{D}$, with the C end slightly negative). The molecular orbitals of CO are given in [6.1.3](/6.1.3#sec-3.6.5): the HOMO is a σ orbital concentrated mainly on C and the LUMO is $\pi^*$, so CO coordinates to metals through the C end and accepts **π back-bonding** from the metal, forming **metal carbonyls** ([6.1.5](/6.1.5)). For the same reason CO binds to Fe(II) in hemoglobin about 200 times more strongly than $\ce{O2}$, which makes CO highly toxic. CO is an important reducing agent in metallurgy ($\ce{Fe2O3 + 3CO -> 2Fe + 3CO2}$).

Carbon dioxide is the linear molecule O=C=O (C is $\mathrm{sp}$ hybridized). Besides the two σ bonds there are two mutually perpendicular delocalized π bonds $\Pi_3^4$, and the C–O bond length of $116\,\mathrm{pm}$ is shorter than that of a typical C=O double bond (about $122\,\mathrm{pm}$). After $\ce{CO2}$ dissolves in water, most of it is present as the hydrated molecule $\ce{CO2(aq)}$ and only about 0.2% forms $\ce{H2CO3}$. The apparent $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a1}} = 6.35$, calculated for the total dissolved $\ce{CO2}$, is to be compared with a $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ of about 3.6 for the $\ce{H2CO3}$ molecule itself, which agrees with Pauling's rule ($m = 1$). $\ce{CO3^2-}$ is trigonal planar, C is $\mathrm{sp}^2$ hybridized and the ion contains $\Pi_4^6$.

The trends in the thermal stability of carbonates ($\ce{H2CO3} < \ce{MHCO3} < \ce{M2CO3}$; increasing down a group; the carbonates of transition metals and p-block metals are unstable) were explained by ionic polarization in [6.1.4](/6.1.4#sec-4.3.5). The $\ce{CO3^2-}$ in solutions of soluble carbonates hydrolyzes and makes them basic ($K_{\mathrm{b1}} = K_{\mathrm{w}}/K_{\mathrm{a2}} = 2.1\times10^{-4}$), so when $\ce{Na2CO3}$ is added to a metal ion solution, the precipitate obtained depends on the relative solubilities of the carbonate and the hydroxide:

| Case | Ions | Reaction | Precipitate |
|---|---|---|---|
| The hydroxide is much less soluble than the carbonate | $\ce{Al^3+}$, $\ce{Cr^3+}$, $\ce{Fe^3+}$ | $\ce{2Al^3+ + 3CO3^2- + 3H2O -> 2Al(OH)3 v + 3CO2 ^}$ | hydroxide |
| The carbonate is less soluble than the hydroxide | $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$ | $\ce{Ca^2+ + CO3^2- -> CaCO3 v}$ | carbonate |
| The two have similar solubilities | $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Cu^2+}$, $\ce{Zn^2+}$ | $\ce{2Cu^2+ + 2CO3^2- + H2O -> Cu2(OH)2CO3 v + CO2 ^}$ | basic carbonate |

### 14.5.4. Silicon and Its Compounds

The structures of the silanes $\ce{Si_nH_{2n+2}}$ are similar to those of the alkanes, but they are far less stable than the alkanes. The electronegativity of Si (1.90) is smaller than that of H (2.20), so H in the Si–H bond carries a partial negative charge, and the Si atom can be attacked by nucleophiles; silanes are therefore reactive:

| Property | Reaction |
|---|---|
| Spontaneously flammable in air | $\ce{SiH4 + 2O2 -> SiO2 + 2H2O}$ |
| Strongly reducing | $\ce{SiH4 + 2KMnO4 -> 2MnO2 v + K2SiO3 + H2O + H2 ^}$ |
| Hydrolysis (catalyzed by traces of base) | $\ce{SiH4} + (n+2)\ce{H2O -> SiO2}\cdot n\ce{H2O} + 4\ce{H2 ^}$ |
| Low thermal stability | $\ce{SiH4 ->[500\,^\circ C] Si + 2H2}$ |

The last reaction is one of the methods of depositing thin films of high-purity silicon on substrates for the manufacture of **semiconductor** devices ([5.5.6](/5.5.6)).

Silica occurs in crystalline and amorphous forms. In the crystals each Si forms a silicon–oxygen tetrahedron $\ce{SiO4}$ with four O atoms through $\mathrm{sp}^3$ hybridization, and each O joins two tetrahedra to form a three-dimensional network solid; different ways of linking the tetrahedra give quartz, tridymite and cristobalite (each with α and β modifications). The natural crystal is quartz; pure, transparent quartz is called rock crystal, and varieties containing impurities include amethyst and agate. Amorphous forms include quartz glass, diatomaceous earth and flint. $\ce{CO2}$ is molecular whereas $\ce{SiO2}$ is a network solid because Si forms π bonds with difficulty (Section 14.1.1): two Si–O single bonds ($2\times452$) are more stable than one Si=O double bond.

$\ce{SiO2}$ is chemically very stable and does not react with acids other than hydrofluoric acid: $\ce{SiO2 + 4HF -> SiF4 ^ + 2H2O}$ (with excess HF, $\ce{H2SiF6}$ is formed), which is used to etch glass, so hydrofluoric acid must be stored in plastic bottles. It is an acidic oxide and reacts with hot strong base solutions or molten sodium carbonate to give silicates:

$$
\ce{SiO2 + 2NaOH -> Na2SiO3 + H2O}, \qquad \ce{SiO2 + Na2CO3 ->[\text{molten}] Na2SiO3 + CO2 ^}.
$$
Glass bottles containing base solutions therefore need rubber stoppers.

Silicic acids and *silicates*. The composition of silicic acid varies with the conditions and is commonly written $x\ce{SiO2}\cdot y\ce{H2O}$: orthosilicic acid $\ce{H4SiO4}$ ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a1}} = 9.8$), metasilicic acid $\ce{H2SiO3}$, and polysilicic acids. Soluble silicates and acids give a silicic acid gel, which on dehydration gives porous silica gel, used as a drying agent and as a catalyst support. The aqueous solution of $\ce{Na2O}\cdot n\ce{SiO2}$ is called water glass and is used as an adhesive and a fireproof coating. Oxo salts with the silicon–oxygen tetrahedron as the basic structural unit are called silicates; natural silicates are classified according to the way in which the tetrahedra share vertices into isolated, chain, layer (mica, clay) and framework types, and the composition is usually written in the form of oxides, as for kaolin $\ce{Al2O3*2SiO2*2H2O}$. When part of the Si in a framework is replaced by Al, the framework becomes negatively charged and is balanced by cations such as $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$; these are the aluminosilicates (feldspars, zeolites).

**Definition 14.5.2 (Molecular Sieve)** A porous crystal with uniform channels and cavities whose pore size is comparable to molecular size (about $0.3$–$1\,\mathrm{nm}$) and which adsorbs molecules selectively according to their size (mainly natural and synthetic aluminosilicates, that is, zeolites) is called a **molecular sieve**.

An example is the zeolite $\ce{Na2O*Al2O3*2SiO2}\cdot n\ce{H2O}$. Molecular sieves are used for drying gases and liquids and for separating molecules (such as straight-chain alkanes from hydrocarbons), and are the main catalysts of petroleum cracking; the cations in the channels are exchangeable, which is used for softening hard water.

### 14.5.5. Tin and Lead

Oxides of tin and lead: SnO is black and unstable (easily oxidized); $\ce{SnO2}$ is white (cassiterite, rutile structure); PbO is yellow or orange-red (two modifications); $\ce{PbO2}$ is brown (rutile structure); $\ce{Pb3O4}$ (red lead, bright red) can be regarded as $\ce{2PbO*PbO2}$, a mixed oxide of Pb(II) and Pb(IV); $\ce{Pb2O3}$ (orange) can be regarded as $\ce{PbO*PbO2}$. On heating, $\ce{PbO2}$ gradually loses oxygen and ends up as PbO via $\ce{Pb2O3}$ and $\ce{Pb3O4}$: $\ce{3PbO2 ->[\Delta] Pb3O4 + O2 ^}$, $\ce{2Pb3O4 ->[\Delta] 6PbO + O2 ^}$.

The inert-pair effect appears clearly between tin and lead (Latimer diagrams, V):

$$
\text{acidic: }\ \ce{Sn^4+ ->[0.151] Sn^2+ ->[-0.138] Sn}, \qquad \ce{PbO2 ->[1.455] Pb^2+ ->[-0.126] Pb};
$$

$$
\text{basic: }\ \ce{[Sn(OH)6]^2- ->[-0.93] [Sn(OH)4]^2- ->[-0.91] Sn}, \qquad \ce{PbO2 ->[0.247] PbO ->[-0.580] Pb}.
$$
Sn(II) is a reducing agent (stronger in basic medium), and Pb(IV) is a strong oxidizing agent (stronger in acidic medium):

| | Reaction | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Reducing action of Sn(II) | $\ce{Sn^2+ + 2HgCl2 + 4Cl- -> Hg2Cl2(s, white) + [SnCl6]^2-}$ | |
| | $\ce{Sn^2+ + Hg2Cl2 + 4Cl- -> 2Hg(l, black) + [SnCl6]^2-}$ | mutual identification of $\ce{Sn^2+}$ and $\ce{Hg^2+}$ |
| | $\ce{Sn^2+ + 2Fe^3+ -> Sn^4+ + 2Fe^2+}$ | prereduction of $\ce{Fe^3+}$ in the determination of iron |
| | $\ce{2Bi(OH)3 + 3[Sn(OH)4]^2- -> 2Bi(s, black) + 3[Sn(OH)6]^2-}$ | identification of $\ce{Bi^3+}$ |
| Oxidizing action of Pb(IV) | $\ce{Pb(OH)2 + NaClO -> PbO2 + NaCl + H2O}$ | Pb(IV) forms more readily under basic conditions |
| | $\ce{5PbO2 + 2Mn^2+ + 4H+ -> 2MnO4- + 5Pb^2+ + 2H2O}$ | identification of $\ce{Mn^2+}$ ($\ce{HNO3}$ medium) |
| | $\ce{PbO2 + 4HCl(conc) -> PbCl2 + Cl2 ^ + 2H2O}$ | |
| | $\ce{2PbO2 + 2H2SO4 ->[\Delta] 2PbSO4 + O2 ^ + 2H2O}$ | |

$\ce{SnCl2}$ solutions hydrolyze readily ($\ce{SnCl2 + H2O <=> Sn(OH)Cl v + HCl}$) and are readily oxidized by air, so hydrochloric acid and tin granules are added when they are prepared ($\ce{Sn^4+ + Sn -> 2Sn^2+}$). $\ce{PbO2}$ and Pb are the two electrodes of the lead–acid battery ([6.1.12](/6.1.12)). Lead and its compounds are toxic, and leaded gasoline, lead pigments and lead solders have gradually been phased out.

## 14.6. The Boron Group

### 14.6.1. Overview

Group 13 (Group IIIA in older Chinese textbooks) comprises boron (B), aluminum (Al), gallium (Ga), indium (In) and thallium (Tl), with the valence configuration $n\mathrm{s}^2\,n\mathrm{p}^1$. They have only three valence electrons but four valence orbitals (one s and three p); an atom with fewer valence electrons than valence orbitals is called an electron-deficient atom.

**Definition 14.6.1 (Electron-Deficient Compound)** A compound in which the number of bonding electron pairs of the central atom is smaller than the number of its valence orbitals is called an **electron-deficient compound**, such as $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{BCl3}$, $\ce{AlCl3}$ and $\ce{B2H6}$.

An electron-deficient compound has an empty valence orbital and therefore behaves in three ways: it accepts an electron pair as a **Lewis acid** ($\ce{BF3 + NH3 -> F3B<-NH3}$, $\ce{BF3 + F- -> BF4-}$); it polymerizes ($\ce{Al2Cl6}$); and it forms multicenter bonds ($\ce{B2H6}$).

| | B | Al | Ga | In | Tl |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity | 2.04 | 1.61 | 1.81 | 1.78 | 1.62 |
| Common oxidation states | +3 | +3 | +3 | +1, +3 | +1 (+3) |
| Maximum coordination number | 4 ($\ce{HBF4}$) | 6 ($\ce{Na3AlF6}$) | 6 | 6 | 6 |

B is a nonmetal and the others are metals. The electronegativity and $I_1 + I_2 + I_3$ of Ga are anomalously larger than those of Al, and the +1 state of Tl is the most stable ($\ce{Tl+}$ resembles $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Ag+}$), see Section 14.1.2. B and Si lie on a diagonal and have similar properties (the elements are network solids, the oxoacids are weak acids, the halides hydrolyze readily and the hydrides ignite spontaneously).

### 14.6.2. The Elements Boron and Gallium

Elemental boron exists as amorphous boron (a brown powder of high chemical reactivity) and crystalline boron (black-gray, very hard, with very high melting and boiling points; a semiconductor). The structural unit of crystalline boron is the regular icosahedron $\ce{B12}$ of 12 B atoms (as in α-rhombohedral boron), and the icosahedra are joined by covalent bonds into a network solid.

Gallium melts at only $29.8\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (it melts in the hand) but boils at about $2200\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, the widest liquid range of all the elements, so that gallium in a quartz tube can serve as a thermometer for high temperatures; like water, gallium expands on solidifying. GaAs and GaN are important compound semiconductors (light-emitting diodes, lasers).

### 14.6.3. Boranes and the Three-Center Two-Electron Bond

The hydrides of boron are called *boranes* and are mainly of two classes, $\ce{B_nH_{n+4}}$ and $\ce{B_nH_{n+6}}$, such as $\ce{B2H6}$ and $\ce{B5H9}$, and $\ce{B4H10}$ and $\ce{B5H11}$. The simplest, $\ce{BH3}$, cannot exist stably and dimerizes to diborane $\ce{B2H6}$.

$\ce{B2H6}$ is a typical electron-deficient compound: the two B and six H atoms share 12 valence electrons but have 14 valence orbitals ($2\times4 + 6\times1$), and classical two-center two-electron bonds would require 14 electrons (7 bonds). In the experimental structure (figure in the previous section) the two B and four terminal H atoms lie in one plane, with normal B–H bond lengths of $119\,\mathrm{pm}$; the other two H atoms lie above and below this plane, each joining two B atoms to form a B–H–B bridge, with B–H bond lengths of $133\,\mathrm{pm}$, longer than normal. Each B is approximately $\mathrm{sp}^3$ hybridized and uses two hybrid orbitals to form normal σ bonds with the terminal H atoms; the remaining two hybrid orbitals (one with one electron, the other empty) take part in the two B–H–B bridges.

**Definition 14.6.2 (Three-Center Two-Electron Bond)** A chemical bond in which three atoms share one electron pair is called a **three-center two-electron bond** (3c–2e bond). The B–H–B bridge bonds in $\ce{B2H6}$ are three-center two-electron bonds, also called hydrogen bridge bonds.

In terms of **molecular orbital** theory, the $\mathrm{sp}^3$ hybrid orbitals of the two B atoms and the 1s orbital of the bridging H combine linearly to give three molecular orbitals, which in order of increasing energy are bonding, nonbonding and antibonding; one electron pair occupies the bonding orbital and holds the three atoms together. The bond order of each B–H is only 1/2, so the bridge bonds are longer and weaker. Three-center bonds allow electron-deficient atoms to link more atoms with fewer electrons and underlie the structures of boranes, metal alkyls and many clusters. Compare $\ce{Al2Cl6}$: the bridging Cl uses its own lone pair to form an ordinary **coordinate bond** (two-center two-electron) with the other Al, so there are four electrons in the bridge, and it is not an electron-deficient three-center bond.

Diborane is a highly toxic colorless gas and is chemically very reactive:

| Property | Reaction |
|---|---|
| Ignites spontaneously in air, releasing a great deal of heat (once studied as a high-energy fuel) | $\ce{B2H6(g) + 3O2(g) -> B2O3(s) + 3H2O(g)}$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H^\circ_{\mathrm{m}} = -2035\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ |
| Hydrolysis | $\ce{B2H6(g) + 6H2O(l) -> 2H3BO3(s) + 6H2(g)}$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H^\circ_{\mathrm{m}} = -510\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ |
| Adducts with Lewis bases | $\ce{B2H6 + 2CO -> 2H3B<-CO}$, $\ce{2NaH + B2H6 -> 2NaBH4}$ |
| Hydroboration | addition to alkenes, used in organic synthesis ([Organic Chemistry](/6.5)) |

Boron compounds burn with a green flame, which can be used to identify boron.

### 14.6.4. Oxygen Compounds of Boron

B is oxophilic and the B–O bond is very strong; the oxides, oxoacids and salts of boron are all very stable, and in nature boron occurs entirely as oxygen compounds.

$\ce{B2O3}$ (boric anhydride) reacts with water to give successively metaboric acid and (ortho)boric acid:

$$
\ce{B2O3 <=>[+H2O][-H2O] 2HBO2 <=>[+2H2O][-2H2O] 2H3BO3}.
$$
Molten $\ce{B2O3}$ dissolves many metal oxides to give metaborate glasses of characteristic colors (the borax bead test): $\ce{B2O3 + CuO -> Cu(BO2)2}$ (blue), $\ce{B2O3 + NiO -> Ni(BO2)2}$ (brown).

Boric acid $\ce{H3BO3}$, that is, $\ce{B(OH)3}$: B is $\mathrm{sp}^2$ hybridized and the molecule is trigonal planar; the molecules are joined by hydrogen bonds into layers, with only intermolecular forces between the layers, so boric acid forms flaky crystals with a slippery feel.

```smiles
OB(O)O H3BO3
O[B-]12O[B-](O)(OB(O)O1)OB(O)O2 [B4O5(OH)4]^2-
```

Boric acid is a weak monoprotic acid ($K_{\mathrm{a}} = 5.4\times10^{-10}$, $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} = 9.27$), but it does not donate a proton of its own: the empty p orbital of B accepts the lone pair of $\ce{OH-}$ from water (**Lewis acid**):

$$
\ce{B(OH)3 + H2O <=> [B(OH)4]- + H+}.
$$
On addition of polyols such as glycerol and mannitol, these form stable chelates with $\ce{[B(OH)4]-}$, shifting the equilibrium to the right and greatly increasing the acidity of boric acid, so that it can be titrated directly with NaOH ([Analytical Chemistry](/6.4)).

Borax is conventionally written $\ce{Na2B4O7*10H2O}$, but its actual composition is $\ce{Na2[B4O5(OH)4]*8H2O}$: the anion consists of two $\ce{BO4}$ tetrahedra and two $\ce{BO3}$ triangles linked together (the structural formula above). Its properties:

(1) Hydrolysis gives a basic solution that constitutes a buffer:

$$
\ce{[B4O5(OH)4]^2- + 5H2O <=> 2H3BO3 + 2[B(OH)4]-}.
$$
In the solution the amounts of $\ce{H3BO3}$ and $\ce{[B(OH)4]-}$ are equal, and according to the **Henderson–Hasselbalch equation** $\mathrm{pH} \approx \mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} = 9.27$. With activities taken into account, a $0.01\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ borax solution has pH 9.18 at $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and is one of the standard **buffer solutions** used to calibrate pH meters ([6.1.9](/6.1.9)).

(2) Reaction with acids gives boric acid: $\ce{Na2B4O7 + H2SO4 + 5H2O -> 4H3BO3 + Na2SO4}$.

(3) Dehydration on heating: $\ce{Na2B4O7*10H2O ->[\text{ca. } 380\,^\circ C] Na2B4O7 ->[\text{ca. } 880\,^\circ C] Na2B4O7(\text{glass})}$.

(4) Borax bead test: molten borax and metal oxides form double salts of characteristic colors, for example $\ce{Na2B4O7 + CoO -> Co(BO2)2*2NaBO2}$ (blue) and $\ce{Na2B4O7 + NiO -> Ni(BO2)2*2NaBO2}$ (brown). Borax is also used as a flux in welding metals (it dissolves the surface oxides).

### 14.6.5. Compounds of Aluminum

Al is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust. Aluminum is a reactive metal, but the dense oxide film on its surface makes it stable in air and water and passivated in cold concentrated nitric acid; it is an amphoteric metal and dissolves in both acids and strong bases: $\ce{2Al + 2NaOH + 6H2O -> 2Na[Al(OH)4] + 3H2 ^}$. $\ce{Al(OH)3}$ is an amphoteric hydroxide (it forms $\ce{[Al(OH)4]-}$ in base, [6.1.10](/6.1.10)).

Aluminum oxide has two main modifications. In α-$\ce{Al2O3}$ (corundum), $\ce{O^2-}$ forms a hexagonal close packing and $\ce{Al^3+}$ occupies two thirds of the octahedral holes; it is very hard (Mohs 9), has a high melting point and is insoluble in water, acids and bases. Natural corundum crystals show brilliant colors owing to small amounts of impurities: ruby contains $\ce{Cr^3+}$ (d–d transitions of the $\mathrm{d}^3$ ion, **crystal field theory**, [6.1.5](/6.1.5)), the blue of sapphire comes from a charge-transfer transition between neighboring $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Ti^4+}$ ($\ce{Fe^2+ + Ti^4+ -> Fe^3+ + Ti^3+}$), and yellow sapphire contains $\ce{Fe^3+}$. γ-$\ce{Al2O3}$ (activated alumina) is obtained by dehydrating $\ce{Al(OH)3}$ at a lower temperature; it dissolves in acids and bases, has a large specific surface area and strong adsorption, is used as an adsorbent and catalyst support, and is converted to the α form above about $1000\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

Aluminum halides. $\ce{AlF3}$ is an ionic compound with a high melting point (sublimes at about $1290\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$); the covalent character increases through $\ce{AlCl3}$, $\ce{AlBr3}$ and $\ce{AlI3}$, which have low melting points, are volatile and dissolve in organic solvents (ionic polarization, [6.1.4](/6.1.4#sec-4.3.5)). $\ce{AlCl3}$ is an electron-deficient compound that exists in the gas phase (below about $200\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) as the dimer $\ce{Al2Cl6}$: each Al is bonded to four Cl atoms in an approximately $\mathrm{sp}^3$ hybridized fashion, and two Cl atoms bridge the two Al atoms (for the nature of the bridge bonds, see Section 14.6.3). Anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$ is an important Lewis acid catalyst (Friedel–Crafts reactions, [Organic Chemistry](/6.5)).

$\ce{AlCl3}$ hydrolyzes strongly in water and fumes in moist air (HCl): $\ce{AlCl3 + 3H2O -> Al(OH)3 + 3HCl}$. Heating $\ce{AlCl3*6H2O}$ therefore does not give anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$ but only $\ce{Al(OH)3}$ and $\ce{Al2O3}$; anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$ must be prepared by dry methods:

$$
\ce{2Al + 3Cl2(g) ->[\Delta] 2AlCl3}, \qquad \ce{2Al + 6HCl(g) ->[\Delta] 2AlCl3 + 3H2}, \qquad \ce{Al2O3 + 3C + 3Cl2 ->[\Delta] 2AlCl3 + 3CO}.
$$

Down the p block the nonmetallic character decreases and the metallic character increases, and lower oxidation states become more stable; the second-period elements differ markedly from the other members of their groups because their atoms are small. The redox behavior of an element can be read uniformly from its Frost diagram, the acidity of oxoacids estimated with Pauling's rules, and the dissolution and precipitation of sparingly soluble substances calculated from solubility products and coordination equilibria: descriptive chemistry is a synthesis of the principles of the earlier chapters.

## Questions

1. Why is $\ce{H3PO3}$ a diprotic acid and $\ce{H3PO4}$ a triprotic acid? Why are their $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a1}}$ values similar?

   **Answer** The structure of $\ce{H3PO3}$ is $\ce{HPO(OH)2}$; it has only two O–H groups, and the H on P does not dissociate. Both acids have only one non-hydroxyl oxygen ($m = 1$), so by Pauling's rules both have $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a1}}$ of about 2.

2. Use electrode potentials to explain why $\ce{MnO2}$ reacts only with concentrated hydrochloric acid to give chlorine.

   **Answer** $E^\circ(\ce{MnO2}/\ce{Mn^2+}) = 1.23\,\mathrm{V}$ is smaller than $E^\circ(\ce{Cl2}/\ce{Cl-}) = 1.36\,\mathrm{V}$, so the reaction does not proceed under standard conditions. In concentrated hydrochloric acid the concentrations of both $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ are large; by the Nernst equation the former raises the potential of the $\ce{MnO2}$ couple and the latter lowers that of the $\ce{Cl2}$ couple, and heating and the prompt escape of $\ce{Cl2}$ allow the reaction to proceed.

3. $\ce{B2H6}$ and $\ce{Al2Cl6}$ both have bridge bonds. How do they differ?

   **Answer** The B–H–B bridge in $\ce{B2H6}$ is a three-center two-electron bond (the bridging H has no lone pair), whereas the Al–Cl–Al bridge in $\ce{Al2Cl6}$ consists of two ordinary two-center two-electron bonds formed by the bridging Cl with its lone pairs (one of them a coordinate bond).

4. Why must hydrofluoric acid and concentrated NaOH solution not be stored in glass containers (or closed with glass stoppers)?

   **Answer** $\ce{SiO2 + 4HF -> SiF4 + 2H2O}$; $\ce{SiO2 + 2NaOH -> Na2SiO3 + H2O}$, and the sodium silicate formed cements the stopper to the neck of the bottle.
