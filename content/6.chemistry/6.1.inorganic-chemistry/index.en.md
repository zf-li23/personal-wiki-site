Inorganic chemistry studies the composition, structure, properties and reactions of the elements and their compounds (other than hydrocarbons and their derivatives). This discipline corresponds to the course "Principles of Chemistry": its first half presents the basic principles shared by all of chemistry, and its second half uses these principles to discuss the elements of the periodic table. There are three core questions: what binds atoms into molecules and crystals, and how structure determines the properties of a substance; how much heat a chemical reaction releases, whether it can proceed spontaneously, how far it goes and how fast; and why elements in the same group have similar properties while each group nevertheless has its own character.

The chapters fall into three parts. Chapters 1–5 deal with the structure of matter: starting from the three states of aggregation (gases, liquids and solids), they review the quantum mechanical model of the atom and explain electron configurations and the periodic law by shielding and penetration; they then introduce several theories of the chemical bond, namely ionic bonding, valence bond theory with **hybrid orbitals**, valence shell electron pair repulsion and molecular orbital theory, followed by the packing of crystals, lattice enthalpy and intermolecular forces; finally the structural theories are applied to **coordination compounds**, whose colors and magnetism are explained by crystal field theory. Chapters 6–12 deal with the principles of chemical reactions: chemical thermodynamics uses enthalpy, entropy and Gibbs energy to judge the heat effect and direction of a reaction; chemical kinetics discusses rate equations, activation energy and reaction mechanisms; chemical equilibrium links thermodynamic data to equilibrium composition through the reaction isotherm. Then come the four equilibria in solution, acid–base and complexation equilibria, precipitation–dissolution equilibria and redox equilibria, the last of which leads to the **electrode potential**, the Nernst equation and electrochemistry. Chapters 13–15 are descriptive chemistry of the elements: going through the s-, p-, d- and f-blocks group by group, they present the elements and their important compounds and use the earlier principles (periodicity of ionization energy and radius, ionic polarization, elemental potential diagrams and Frost diagrams, crystal field theory) to explain their properties instead of merely listing facts.

Much of this material already has a foundation in physics: the kinetic theory of gases and the laws of thermodynamics are in [Thermal Physics](/5.2), and the hydrogen atom, quantum numbers and the Pauli principle are in [Quantum Physics](/5.5); here they are only reviewed, with the emphasis on their use in chemistry. This discipline is also the common starting point of the other chemistry courses: [Structural Chemistry](/6.2) puts the atomic structure, chemical bonding and crystal structure treated here on the foundation of quantum mechanics, [Physical Chemistry](/6.3) makes chemical thermodynamics, chemical equilibrium and kinetics rigorous, the four kinds of titration in [Analytical Chemistry](/6.4) are built on the four equilibria here, and [Organic Chemistry](/6.5) analyzes the structures and reactions of organic molecules with hybrid orbitals, resonance and acid–base theory. In [Biology](/7), heme and chlorophyll are both coordination complexes, the pH of body fluids is maintained by buffer solutions, and ions such as $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and the transition metals in metalloenzymes all have to be understood with the chemistry of the elements treated here.

This is the chemistry we have been studying since middle school, and the most familiar one: acids and bases, precipitation, redox and the properties of the elements already appear in school textbooks, and the first book that a chemistry olympiad contestant reads is usually about exactly these things. University inorganic chemistry is not a simple repetition: many facts that one had to memorize in school can here be explained by principles, such as why a certain oxidation state is stable, why compounds of transition metals are colored, and why one reaction can proceed while another cannot. The course title "Principles of Chemistry" also hints at its role: it is the foundation for studying all the other branches, and structural chemistry, physical chemistry, analytical chemistry and organic chemistry each start from some part of it and then go deeper.
