From this chapter on we turn to descriptive inorganic chemistry: following the blocks of the periodic table, we survey the elements and their important compounds group by group and explain their properties with the principles of the earlier chapters. The s block comprises Groups 1 and 2 ($n\mathrm{s}^{1\text{–}2}$) together with hydrogen. These are the most typical metals, and their chemistry is determined almost entirely by the ease with which they lose their s electrons to form cations with a noble-gas configuration. The main tools of this chapter are therefore the periodicity of **ionization energy** and radius ([6.1.2](/6.1.2)), ionic crystals and **ionic polarization** ([6.1.4](/6.1.4)), thermochemical cycles ([6.1.6](/6.1.6)) and **standard electrode potentials** ([6.1.11](/6.1.11)). Hydrogen occupies a special position; it is treated separately in Section 13.2, together with the hydrides of the other blocks. The spectra of the alkali metals are discussed in [5.5.4](/5.5.4#sec-4.2.5); the roles of $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ in living organisms belong to biology ([7](/7)). The p-, d- and f-block elements are treated in [6.1.14](/6.1.14) and [6.1.15](/6.1.15).

## 13.1. The Alkali and Alkaline Earth Metals

### 13.1.1. Overview

**Definition 13.1.1** The elements of Group 1 other than hydrogen, namely lithium (Li), sodium (Na), potassium (K), rubidium (Rb), cesium (Cs) and francium (Fr), are called **alkali metals**; the elements of Group 2, namely beryllium (Be), magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), strontium (Sr), barium (Ba) and radium (Ra), are called **alkaline earth metals**.

"Alkali" refers to the strongly basic character of their hydroxides, and "earth" is the old name for refractory metal oxides (CaO, MgO). Fr and Ra are radioactive and are not discussed below. By the current IUPAC definition, Be and Mg are also alkaline earth metals, although their properties differ markedly from those of Ca, Sr and Ba (Section 13.1.5).

The trends in the table can all be understood from the electron configurations. An alkali metal has a single valence electron; its $I_1$ is small but $I_2$ is very large (an electron must now be removed from a noble-gas core), so the metals are always found in the +1 state in compounds. For the alkaline earth metals $I_3$ is likewise very large, and the oxidation state is always +2. Alkali metal atoms are the largest in their periods, and the metallic bonding provided by a single valence electron is weak; the metals are therefore soft, of low density (Li, Na and K float on water) and low melting point (Cs melts at only $28.4\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$). The alkaline earth metals have two valence electrons and smaller radii, so their metallic bonding is much stronger and their melting points, hardness and densities are higher (panel a of the figure below). The flame colors arise from valence-electron transitions in the atoms; for example, the yellow light of Na is the $3\mathrm{p} \to 3\mathrm{s}$ transition (the 589 nm doublet). Flame colors are used to identify these elements.

### 13.1.2. Chemical Properties of the Elements

The alkali and alkaline earth metals are among the strongest reducing agents. They combine directly or indirectly with the more electronegative nonmetals ($\ce{O2}$, S, $\ce{N2}$, the halogens, $\ce{H2}$) and also reduce water.

Reaction with oxygen. When the metals burn in air, among the alkali metals only Li gives the normal oxide $\ce{Li2O}$; Na gives the peroxide $\ce{Na2O2}$, and K, Rb and Cs give the superoxides $\ce{MO2}$. The alkaline earth metals give the normal oxides $\ce{MO}$, and Ba also forms $\ce{BaO2}$ in oxygen under pressure.

**Definition 13.1.2** A compound containing the peroxide ion $\ce{O2^2-}$ ($\ce{[O-O]^2-}$) is called a **peroxide**; a compound containing the superoxide ion $\ce{O2-}$ is called a **superoxide**.

In the **molecular orbital** picture ([Example 3.6.1](/6.1.3#ex-3.6.1)), the $\pi^*_{2\mathrm{p}}$ orbitals of $\ce{O2^2-}$ are completely filled, so its **bond order** is 1, the O–O bond length is about $149\,\mathrm{pm}$ and the ion is diamagnetic; $\ce{O2-}$ has one unpaired electron, a bond order of 1.5 and is paramagnetic ($\ce{KO2}$ is orange-yellow). Why do the heavier alkali metals tend to form such large anions as $\ce{O2^2-}$ and $\ce{O2-}$? The decomposition of peroxides and superoxides, $\ce{M2O2 -> M2O + 1/2 O2}$, follows the same logic as that of carbonates ([6.1.4](/6.1.4#sec-4.3.5)). The small $\ce{Li+}$, with its strong **polarizing power**, distorts $\ce{O2^2-}$ and "pulls" one O atom toward itself; moreover, the **lattice enthalpy** gained on replacing a large anion by the small $\ce{O^2-}$ is much greater for a small cation. Hence $\ce{Li2O2}$ is unstable whereas $\ce{Li2O}$ is stable. The larger the cation, the weaker these two effects, and the more stable the compound of the large anion.

Peroxides and superoxides are strong oxidizing agents and release oxygen on reaction with water or $\ce{CO2}$, which makes them useful as oxygen sources in submarines, spacecraft and breathing apparatus:

| Reaction | Remark |
|---|---|
| $\ce{Na2O2 + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2O2}$ | the $\ce{H2O2}$ formed decomposes further to give $\ce{O2}$ |
| $\ce{2Na2O2 + 2CO2 -> 2Na2CO3 + O2}$ | absorbs $\ce{CO2}$ and releases $\ce{O2}$ |
| $\ce{2KO2 + 2H2O -> 2KOH + H2O2 + O2}$ | a superoxide reacting with water |
| $\ce{4KO2 + 2CO2 -> 2K2CO3 + 3O2}$ | 1.5 mol $\ce{O2}$ is released per mole of $\ce{CO2}$ absorbed |

Reaction with nitrogen. Li and Mg combine directly with $\ce{N2}$ ($\ce{6Li + N2 -> 2Li3N}$, $\ce{3Mg + N2 -> Mg3N2}$), which is why the product of burning Mg in air contains some nitride. Mg also burns in $\ce{CO2}$ ($\ce{2Mg + CO2 -> 2MgO + C}$), so a magnesium fire cannot be extinguished with a $\ce{CO2}$ extinguisher.

Reaction with water. The alkali metals react violently with cold water; among the alkaline earth metals, Ca, Sr and Ba react with cold water, Mg reacts only slowly with hot water (the surface layer of $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$ is sparingly soluble), and Be does not react with water:

$$
\ce{2M + 2H2O -> 2MOH + H2 ^}, \qquad \ce{M + 2H2O -> M(OH)2 + H2 ^}.
$$
Although Li has the most negative standard electrode potential, its reaction with water is much milder than those of Na and K. The electrode potential only indicates the tendency of a reaction (thermodynamics); how fast it proceeds is a matter of kinetics. Li has a relatively high melting point ($180.5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), so it does not melt into a ball with a larger contact area during the reaction, and the LiOH formed is also less soluble.

**Example 13.1.1** The ionization energy of Li is the largest among the alkali metals. Why, then, is $E^\circ(\ce{Li+}/\ce{Li})$ the most negative?

Solution. $E^\circ(\ce{M+}/\ce{M})$ corresponds to the reaction $\ce{M(s) + H+(aq) -> M+(aq) + 1/2 H2(g)}$ with $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G^\circ_{\mathrm{m}} = -FE^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = FE^\circ(\ce{M+}/\ce{M})$ (where $E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = E^\circ(\ce{H+}/\ce{H2}) - E^\circ(\ce{M+}/\ce{M})$). Using **Hess's law**, we split the metal side into three steps: sublimation $\ce{M(s) -> M(g)}$, ionization $\ce{M(g) -> M+(g) + e-}$ and hydration $\ce{M+(g) -> M+(aq)}$; the steps on the hydrogen side are the same for all the metals. Neglecting entropy changes, we approximate the Gibbs energy changes by the enthalpy changes (in $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$; the hydration enthalpies are commonly used approximate values):

| | Li | Na | K | Rb | Cs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sublimation enthalpy $\Delta_{\mathrm{sub}}H^\circ$ | 159 | 107 | 89 | 81 | 77 |
| First ionization energy $I_1$ | 520 | 496 | 419 | 403 | 376 |
| Hydration enthalpy $\Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}}H^\circ(\ce{M+})$ | −519 | −404 | −321 | −296 | −271 |
| Sum | 160 | 199 | 187 | 188 | 182 |

The smaller the sum, the easier the process $\ce{M -> M+(aq)}$ and the more negative $E^\circ$. The $\ce{Li+}$ ion is the smallest and has the strongest ion–dipole interaction with water molecules; its hydration enthalpy is about $115\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ more exothermic than that of $\ce{Na+}$, enough to offset the larger sublimation enthalpy and ionization energy. The sums for Li and Na differ by $39\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, equivalent to $39/96.5 = 0.40\,\mathrm{V}$, in reasonable agreement with the actual difference of $0.33\,\mathrm{V}$. The sums for K, Rb and Cs are close, and so are their $E^\circ$ values; the remaining differences come from the entropy changes neglected here. $\square$

### 13.1.3. Hydroxides

The oxides (except BeO) react with water to form hydroxides: $\ce{M2O + H2O -> 2MOH}$, $\ce{MO + H2O -> M(OH)2}$.

Solubility. The hydroxides of the alkali metals are all very soluble in water and dissolve with the release of much heat (LiOH is less soluble). The hydroxides of the alkaline earth metals are far less soluble, and their solubility increases with atomic number: $\ce{Be(OH)2}$ is practically insoluble (**solubility product** about $7\times10^{-22}$), $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$ is sparingly soluble ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 5.6\times10^{-12}$), $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ is slightly soluble (about $0.02\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$), and the solubilities of $\ce{Sr(OH)2}$ and $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$ are about $0.07$ and $0.2\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ ($20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$).

Basicity. The alkali metal hydroxides are all strong bases. Among the alkaline earth metals, $\ce{Be(OH)2}$ is amphoteric (it dissolves in both acids and strong bases: $\ce{Be(OH)2 + 2OH- -> [Be(OH)4]^2-}$), $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$ is a moderately strong base, and $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, $\ce{Sr(OH)2}$ and $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$ are strong bases. Basicity increases down a group.

A hydroxide $\ce{R-O-H}$ can dissociate in water in two ways: by cleavage of the R–O bond (basic dissociation, releasing $\ce{OH-}$) or by cleavage of the O–H bond (acidic dissociation, releasing $\ce{H+}$). Which one prevails depends on how strongly $\ce{R^{n+}}$ attracts O. The higher the charge and the smaller the radius of $\ce{R^{n+}}$, the more strongly it draws the electrons of O toward itself, the weaker the O–H bond becomes, and the more the acidic dissociation is favored. This qualitative picture can be quantified by an empirical parameter:

**Definition 13.1.3 (Ionic Potential)** The ratio $\phi = Z/r$ of the charge number $Z$ of a cation to its radius $r$ is called the **ionic potential**.

With $r$ in picometers, empirically a hydroxide is basic if $\sqrt{\phi} < 0.22$, amphoteric if $0.22 < \sqrt{\phi} < 0.32$ and acidic if $\sqrt{\phi} > 0.32$. For example (Shannon radii), $\ce{Na+}$: $\sqrt{1/102} = 0.10$; $\ce{Mg^2+}$: $\sqrt{2/72} = 0.17$; $\ce{Be^2+}$ (coordination number 4): $\sqrt{2/27} = 0.27$; $\ce{Al^3+}$: $\sqrt{3/54} = 0.24$; $\ce{B^3+}$ (coordination number 4): $\sqrt{3/11} = 0.52$. These correspond to a strong base, a moderately strong base, two amphoteric hydroxides and an acid ($\ce{B(OH)3}$), in agreement with experiment. The ionic potential is also a rough measure of **polarizing power**. It ignores the electron configuration of the cation ([6.1.4](/6.1.4#sec-4.3.5)), applies only to cations with a noble-gas configuration, and is unreliable for borderline cases.

### 13.1.4. Salts

The halides, nitrates, sulfates, carbonates, phosphates and so on of the s-block elements are overwhelmingly **ionic crystals**: they have high melting points, conduct electricity when molten and dissociate completely in water.

Melting points. As the figure above (panel b) shows, the melting points of the alkali metal chlorides decrease from NaCl to CsCl, because the larger ionic radius reduces the lattice enthalpy. The melting points of the alkaline earth metal chlorides, in contrast, increase from $\ce{BeCl2}$ ($399\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) to $\ce{BaCl2}$ ($961\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), the opposite trend: $\ce{Be^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$ are strongly polarizing and the bonds have a large covalent contribution ([6.1.4](/6.1.4#sec-4.3.5)); $\ce{BeCl2}$ is essentially a covalent compound. The melting point of LiCl ($610\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) is lower than that of NaCl, which is also an effect of the polarization by $\ce{Li+}$.

Solubility. Most alkali metal salts are soluble; the few sparingly soluble ones are often used for identification. For Li they are LiF, $\ce{Li2CO3}$ and $\ce{Li3PO4}$; for Na, sodium hexahydroxoantimonate(V) $\ce{Na[Sb(OH)6]}$ (white); for K, Rb and Cs, $\ce{KClO4}$, $\ce{K2[PtCl6]}$, potassium tetraphenylborate $\ce{K[B(C6H5)4]}$ and potassium sodium hexanitritocobaltate(III) $\ce{K2Na[Co(NO2)6]}$ (yellow). The carbonates, oxalates, phosphates and fluorides ($\ce{CaF2}$, $K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 3.45\times10^{-11}$) of the alkaline earth metals are sparingly soluble. The solubilities of the sulfates and chromates decrease down the group: $\ce{MgSO4}$ is very soluble, $\ce{CaSO4}$ is slightly soluble ($K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 4.9\times10^{-5}$), and $\ce{SrSO4}$ ($3.4\times10^{-7}$) and $\ce{BaSO4}$ ($1.08\times10^{-10}$) are sparingly soluble, the opposite of the order for the hydroxides. A useful rule of thumb is that a salt is soluble when the cation and anion differ greatly in size (the large anion $\ce{SO4^2-}$ with the small cation $\ce{Mg^2+}$, the small anion $\ce{OH-}$ with the large cation $\ce{Ba^2+}$) and sparingly soluble when they are similar in size. Dissolution is the outcome of a competition between the lattice enthalpy and the hydration enthalpies of the ions; small ions have large hydration enthalpies, and when the sizes are very different the lattice enthalpy is not large either, which favors dissolution.

Thermal stability. The trends in the decomposition temperatures of carbonates (they increase down a group; for the same period $\ce{M2CO3} > \ce{MCO3}$; $\ce{BeCO3}$ decomposes at around $100\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) were explained by ionic polarization in [6.1.4](/6.1.4#sec-4.3.5). Nitrates are less stable thermally, and the decomposition products also depend on the polarizing power of the cation:

| Nitrate | Decomposition reaction | Products |
|---|---|---|
| Na, K, Rb, Cs | $\ce{2NaNO3 ->[\Delta] 2NaNO2 + O2 ^}$ | nitrite + $\ce{O2}$ |
| Li, Mg and the alkaline earth metals (high temperature) | $\ce{4LiNO3 ->[\Delta] 2Li2O + 4NO2 ^ + O2 ^}$, $\ce{2Mg(NO3)2 ->[\Delta] 2MgO + 4NO2 ^ + O2 ^}$ | oxide + $\ce{NO2}$ + $\ce{O2}$ |

The decomposition of the nitrates of other metals is discussed in [6.1.14](/6.1.14#sec-14.4.3).

### 13.1.5. The Anomalous Behavior of Lithium and Beryllium; the Diagonal Relationship

The atoms and ions of the second-period elements are exceptionally small and have only four valence orbitals, 2s and 2p; their properties often differ from those of the other members of their groups.

The anomalous behavior of lithium stems from the very small radius and very strong polarizing power of $\ce{Li+}$:

- Some lithium salts have covalent character, with lower melting points and solubility in organic solvents (LiCl dissolves in ethanol); LiF, $\ce{Li2CO3}$ and $\ce{Li3PO4}$ are sparingly soluble.
- Lithium forms only the normal oxide and combines directly with $\ce{N2}$; LiOH, $\ce{Li2CO3}$ and $\ce{LiNO3}$ decompose to $\ce{Li2O}$ on heating.
- $\ce{Li+}$ is the most strongly hydrated, so its hydrated ion has the largest radius among the alkali metal ions (about $380\,\mathrm{pm}$, compared with about $330\,\mathrm{pm}$ for $\ce{Cs+}$) and it migrates most slowly in water (panel d of the figure above); $E^\circ(\ce{Li+}/\ce{Li}) = -3.040\,\mathrm{V}$ is the most negative (Example 13.1.1).

The anomalous behavior of beryllium has the same origin, and $\ce{Be^2+}$ is in addition more highly charged:

- $\ce{BeCl2}$ is a covalent compound that dissolves in organic solvents. In the gas phase it exists as linear monomeric $\ce{Cl-Be-Cl}$ molecules (at higher temperatures) or as dimers; in the solid it forms long chains linked by chloride bridges.
- $\ce{Be(OH)2}$ is amphoteric (Section 13.1.3).
- Beryllium does not form a peroxide, and its compounds decompose at low temperatures.
- Its maximum coordination number is 4 (only the 2s and 2p orbitals are available), and in aqueous solution the ion is the tetrahedral $\ce{[Be(H2O)4]^2+}$.
- Beryllium and its compounds are highly toxic; inhaling the dust can cause berylliosis.

**Definition 13.1.4 (Diagonal Relationship)** Two elements located diagonally from upper left to lower right in the periodic table (Li and Mg, Be and Al, B and Si) show many similarities in the properties of the elements and their compounds. This empirical rule is called the **diagonal relationship**.

```text
Li   Be   B    C
  ↘    ↘    ↘
Na   Mg   Al   Si
```

From left to right the ionic charge increases and the polarizing power increases; from top to bottom the ionic radius increases and the polarizing power decreases. A step along the diagonal approximately cancels the two effects, so the two ions have similar polarizing power and electronegativity (Li 0.98 and Mg 1.31, Be 1.57 and Al 1.61, B 2.04 and Si 1.90). It is only an empirical rule and is most pronounced in the upper left corner of the periodic table:

| Li and Mg | Be and Al |
|---|---|
| Burn in oxygen to give the normal oxide; both combine directly with $\ce{N2}$ | The oxides and hydroxides are amphoteric |
| The hydroxide is a moderately strong base of limited solubility and decomposes to the oxide on heating | The anhydrous chlorides $\ce{BeCl2}$ and $\ce{AlCl3}$ are covalent, sublime easily and dissolve in organic solvents |
| The fluoride, carbonate and phosphate are sparingly soluble | Both are passivated by cold concentrated nitric acid ([6.1.14](/6.1.14)) |
| The chloride dissolves in organic solvents; the hydrated chloride hydrolyzes on heating | BeO and $\ce{Al2O3}$ are both hard and have high melting points |
| The carbonate decomposes to the oxide on heating | The salts are readily hydrolyzed |

The similarity between B and Si (both elements are covalent network solids, both have weakly acidic oxoacids, both have halides that hydrolyze readily and both have hydrides that ignite spontaneously) is discussed in [6.1.14](/6.1.14).

## 13.2. Hydrogen

### 13.2.1. Isotopes of Hydrogen and Its Position in the Periodic Table

Hydrogen has three **isotopes**: protium $^1\mathrm{H}$ (H, abundance 99.98%), deuterium $^2\mathrm{H}$ (D, about 0.015%) and radioactive tritium $^3\mathrm{H}$ (T, β decay, half-life 12.3 years). The isotopes have almost identical chemical properties, but the mass of D is twice that of H, the largest relative difference among all the elements, so isotope effects are especially pronounced for hydrogen. Heavy water $\ce{D2O}$ melts at $3.82\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and boils at $101.4\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$; breaking a C–D bond is several times slower than breaking a C–H bond (the zero-point energy is lower, [5.5.5](/5.5.5)). This difference in reaction rate caused by isotopic substitution is called the *kinetic isotope effect* and can be used to decide whether breaking the bond concerned occurs in the rate-determining step of a mechanism ([6.1.7](/6.1.7)). $\ce{D2O}$ is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors, and deuterated reagents are used in nuclear magnetic resonance and isotopic tracing.

A hydrogen atom has only one electron ($1\mathrm{s}^1$), and there are several views of its position in the periodic table:

- Like the alkali metals it has one valence electron and is often placed at the head of Group 1. But its $I_1 = 1312\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ is much larger than that of Li ($520$), so it loses its electron with difficulty and usually shares an electron pair with other atoms to form covalent bonds. $\ce{H+}$ is the bare proton, with a radius of only about $10^{-3}\,\mathrm{pm}$ and an extremely strong polarizing power; it cannot exist on its own in solution but is always present as $\ce{H3O+}$ and similar species.
- Like the halogens it lacks one electron of the noble-gas structure and can form $\ce{H-}$ (radius about $140$–$150\,\mathrm{pm}$, comparable to $133\,\mathrm{pm}$ for $\ce{F-}$). But its electron affinity is only $73\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ (for F, $328$), and it gains an electron only in reactions with the most reactive metals.
- Its electronegativity (2.20) and half-filled valence shell also resemble those of C in Group 14.

The properties of hydrogen are therefore unique, which stems from its unique atomic structure: an exceptionally small atomic radius, a single electron without screening by inner electrons, and an intermediate electronegativity.

The bond enthalpy of $\ce{H2}$ is large ($436\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$), and the molecule is unreactive at room temperature; it is a good reducing agent only at high temperature or in the presence of a catalyst ($\ce{CuO + H2 ->[\Delta] Cu + H2O}$). Industrially, hydrogen comes mainly from steam reforming of natural gas ($\ce{CH4 + H2O -> CO + 3H2}$, followed by the water–gas shift reaction $\ce{CO + H2O -> CO2 + H2}$) and, to a smaller extent, from the electrolysis of water ([6.1.12](/6.1.12)).

### 13.2.2. Hydrides

**Definition 13.2.1 (Hydride)** A binary compound of hydrogen with another element is called a **hydride**.

By the type of bonding, hydrides fall into three classes, which correspond roughly to the position of the element in the periodic table (figure below): reactive metals of very low electronegativity form ionic hydrides, the p-block nonmetals form molecular (covalent) hydrides, and many d- and f-block metals form metallic hydrides. The hydrides of Be, Mg, Al, Cu and Zn are covalent polymers and are of an intermediate type. The metals of Groups 6–11 (except Pd) generally do not form stable binary hydrides; this region is called the "hydride gap".

![Types of binary hydrides across the periodic table (lanthanoids omitted): ionic hydrides of the s-block metals, molecular hydrides of the p-block, metallic hydrides of groups 3–5 and Pd, intermediate polymeric hydrides in between, and the hydride gap](./plots/hydride-types.py)

Molecular hydrides. The p-block elements (except the noble gases, In and Tl) form molecules in which they are bound to hydrogen by **covalent bonds**, such as HX, $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{PH3}$, $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{SiH4}$ and $\ce{B2H6}$. The molecules are held together by intermolecular forces, so the melting and boiling points are low; HF, $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{NH3}$ form **hydrogen bonds** between molecules and have anomalously high boiling points ([6.1.4](/6.1.4#sec-4.4.4)). Their behavior toward water differs greatly:

| Hydride | Behavior toward water | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{GeH4}$, $\ce{SnH4}$, $\ce{PH3}$, $\ce{AsH3}$, $\ce{SbH3}$ | no reaction | E–H bond of low polarity |
| $\ce{B2H6}$, $\ce{SiH4}$ | hydrolyze with release of $\ce{H2}$: $\ce{SiH4 + 2H2O -> SiO2 + 4H2 ^}$ | B (2.04) and Si (1.90) are less electronegative than H, so H carries a partial negative charge and combines with the positively charged H of water to give $\ce{H2}$ |
| $\ce{NH3}$ | dissolves and acts as a weak base: $\ce{NH3 + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH-}$ | the lone pair on N accepts a proton |
| $\ce{H2S}$, $\ce{H2Se}$, $\ce{H2Te}$, HF | dissolve and dissociate partially, weakly acidic | |
| HCl, HBr, HI | dissociate completely in water, strong acids | |

Across a period from left to right the electronegativity of E increases, the E–H bond becomes more polar, and the hydrides change from basic ($\ce{NH3}$) through neutral ($\ce{H2O}$) to acidic (HF). Down a group the E–H bond becomes longer and its bond enthalpy decreases (H–F $570$, H–Cl $432$, H–Br $366$, H–I $298\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$) and the acidity increases, so HF is a weak acid ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} \approx 3.2$) while HI is the strongest of the hydrohalic acids. The thermal stability and the reducing power of the hydrides also follow the bond enthalpies: down a group the thermal stability decreases and the reducing power increases ($\ce{HI}$ and $\ce{H2S}$ are commonly used reducing agents, [6.1.14](/6.1.14)).

Ionic hydrides. The s-block metals (except Be and Mg) combine directly with $\ce{H2}$ on heating to give ionic hydrides containing $\ce{H-}$, such as LiH, NaH and $\ce{CaH2}$. They are white crystals (the alkali metal hydrides have the NaCl structure) of rather low thermal stability, and most decompose before melting. On electrolysis of molten LiH, $\ce{H2}$ is released at the anode, which shows that $\ce{H-}$ is present. The properties of $\ce{H-}$ are as follows:

- It is a strong reducing agent, $E^\circ(\ce{H2}/\ce{H-}) = -2.23\,\mathrm{V}$, and at high temperature reduces metals from their compounds: $\ce{4NaH + TiCl4 -> Ti + 4NaCl + 2H2 ^}$.
- It is an extremely strong base and reacts with water (and with anything more acidic than $\ce{H2}$, such as $\ce{NH3}$), releasing $\ce{H2}$: $\ce{NaH + H2O -> NaOH + H2 ^}$, $\ce{H- + NH3 -> NH2- + H2 ^}$. For this reason $\ce{CaH2}$ is used as a drying agent for organic solvents and as a field source of hydrogen ($\ce{CaH2 + 2H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + 2H2 ^}$).
- It acts as a **ligand** and forms complex hydrides:

  $$
  \ce{4LiH + AlCl3 ->[\text{anhydrous ether}] Li[AlH4] + 3LiCl}, \qquad \ce{2NaH + B2H6 -> 2NaBH4}.
  $$
  The ions $\ce{AlH4-}$ and $\ce{BH4-}$ in lithium aluminum hydride $\ce{LiAlH4}$ and sodium borohydride $\ce{NaBH4}$ are tetrahedral, and these compounds are the most commonly used reducing agents in organic synthesis ([Organic Chemistry](/6.5)).

Metallic hydrides. The metals of Groups 3–5 (Sc, Y, La, Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta), Pd and most of the f-block metals absorb hydrogen to form metallic hydrides. The H atoms occupy the interstices of the metal lattice (such as the **octahedral holes** of the face-centered cubic lattice of Pd). Consequently:

- they largely retain the appearance and properties of the metal, with metallic luster and electrical conductivity, the conductivity varying with the hydrogen content;
- their density is lower than that of the parent metal (the lattice expands);
- the composition is often nonstoichiometric, as in $\ce{TiH_{1.7}}$ and $\ce{PdH_x}$ ($x$ reaches about 0.7 at most at room temperature);
- some of the metals absorb and release hydrogen reversibly.

At room temperature Pd absorbs hundreds of times its own volume of $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{Pd + \frac{x}{2} H2 <=> PdH_x}$, and releases it again on heating or reduction of pressure; H diffuses rapidly in Pd, so a heated palladium membrane is permeable only to hydrogen, which is used to prepare high-purity hydrogen. U forms $\ce{UH3}$ with $\ce{H2}$ at about $250\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, which decomposes at higher temperatures to release pure hydrogen.

**Definition 13.2.2 (Hydrogen Storage Material)** A material that can reversibly absorb and release large amounts of hydrogen at moderate temperature and pressure is called a **hydrogen storage material**.

A typical example is $\ce{LaNi5}$:

$$
\ce{LaNi5 + 3H2 <=>[\text{(2–3)} \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}][\text{gentle heating or reduced pressure}] LaNi5H6}.
$$
The hydrogen content of $\ce{LaNi5H6}$ per unit volume is even higher than that of liquid hydrogen (about $71\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$); hydrogen is absorbed and released at close to atmospheric pressure and quickly, and the material can be reused many times. Such alloys serve as the negative electrode material of nickel–metal hydride **secondary cells** ([6.1.12](/6.1.12)). Their drawback is a gravimetric hydrogen density of only about 1.4%, too heavy for use in automobiles; research on hydrogen storage materials aims at combining high volumetric and gravimetric densities (complex hydrides of light metals, $\ce{MgH2}$ and so on).

## Summary

| | Alkali metals | Alkaline earth metals | Hydrogen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Valence electrons and oxidation states | $n\mathrm{s}^1$, +1 | $n\mathrm{s}^2$, +2 | $1\mathrm{s}^1$, +1, −1 |
| Elements | soft, light, low melting points, the strongest reducing agents | harder, higher melting points, strong reducing agents | $\ce{H2}$, large bond enthalpy, unreactive at room temperature |
| With oxygen | Li normal oxide, Na peroxide, K–Cs superoxides | normal oxides ($\ce{BaO2}$) | $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{H2O2}$ |
| Hydroxides | strong bases, soluble | Be amphoteric, Mg moderately strong base, Ca–Ba strong bases; solubility increases down the group | — |
| Salts | mostly soluble; carbonates stable | sulfates and carbonates mostly sparingly soluble; stability of carbonates increases down the group | ionic, molecular and metallic hydrides |
| Special features | Li resembles Mg | Be resembles Al | unique position |

Only two explanations run through this chapter. The smaller the ion and the higher its charge, the stronger its polarizing power, its hydration and its lattice enthalpy. Thermodynamic quantities (electrode potentials, solubilities, decomposition temperatures) result from the competition among several energy terms, which must be compared term by term with a thermochemical cycle rather than judged from a single one (Example 13.1.1).

## Questions

1. Why can a fire of sodium or magnesium not be extinguished with a $\ce{CO2}$ extinguisher? What should be used instead?
   Answer: The $\ce{Na2O2}$ formed when Na burns reacts with $\ce{CO2}$ to release $\ce{O2}$, and Mg burns in $\ce{CO2}$ ($\ce{2Mg + CO2 -> 2MgO + C}$); water cannot be used either (it reacts to release $\ce{H2}$). The fire should be smothered with dry sand or a special metal-fire extinguishing agent.
2. $\ce{BeCl2}$ and $\ce{BaCl2}$ are both chlorides of Group 2 elements. Why does the former have a low melting point and dissolve in organic solvents, whereas the latter is a typical ionic crystal?
   Answer: $\ce{Be^2+}$ has a very small radius and a high charge, so its polarizing power is very strong and it distorts $\ce{Cl-}$ strongly; the Be–Cl bond is predominantly covalent. $\ce{Ba^2+}$ has a large radius and a weak polarizing power, so the bond is essentially ionic.
3. What are the oxidation numbers of H in NaH and in HCl? What happens when NaH is added to water?
   Answer: −1 and +1, respectively. The $\ce{H-}$ in NaH and the $\ce{H+}$ of water comproportionate to give $\ce{H2}$: $\ce{NaH + H2O -> NaOH + H2 ^}$.
