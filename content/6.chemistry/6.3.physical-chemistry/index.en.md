Physical chemistry uses the principles and methods of physics to study the general laws of chemical change. It has three pillars: thermodynamics answers whether a process can occur and how far it goes, kinetics answers how fast it proceeds and by what pathway, and structural theory answers where the properties of a molecule come from; statistical mechanics connects the first and the third by computing macroscopic thermodynamic properties from molecular energy levels. Structural theory forms a discipline of its own, [Structural Chemistry](/6.2), so this discipline discusses thermodynamics, statistical thermodynamics, electrochemistry, kinetics and interfacial chemistry.

The main line of the chapters is as follows. Chapters 1–9 are chemical thermodynamics: the first law and thermochemistry, the second law and entropy, from which the entropy criterion is used to construct the Helmholtz energy and the **Gibbs energy** and to establish the mathematical structure of thermodynamics; then **partial molar quantities** and the chemical potential are introduced and used to treat in a unified way ideal solutions, the **activity** in real solutions, chemical equilibrium and phase equilibrium, with the phase rule and phase diagrams as the subject of the last two chapters. Chapters 10–11 are statistical thermodynamics: the Boltzmann distribution and the **partition function** give the thermodynamic functions of ideal gases, explain how heat capacity varies with temperature, and yield entropies and equilibrium constants from spectroscopic data alone. Chapters 12–14 are electrochemistry: the conduction of electrolyte solutions and ionic strength, the thermodynamics of cells at zero current and the Nernst equation, and polarization and the kinetics of electrode processes when current flows. Chapters 15–17 are chemical kinetics: rate equations and approximation methods for complex reactions, collision theory and transition state theory, and reactions in solution, photochemistry and catalysis (including enzyme catalysis). Chapter 18 extends thermodynamics to nonequilibrium systems, from entropy production and the Onsager reciprocal relations to dissipative structures and chemical oscillations. Chapters 19–21 are interfaces and colloids: surface tension and curved interfaces, adsorption and heterogeneous catalysis, and colloids and macromolecular solutions.

Physical chemistry starts from [Thermal Physics](/5.2) in physics and [Inorganic Chemistry](/6.1) in chemistry: the laws of thermodynamics are established in the former, and the framework of chemical thermodynamics, equilibrium and kinetics is given in the latter; here they are made rigorous and quantitative. The mathematics of thermodynamics is the calculus of total differentials and **partial derivatives** from [Calculus](/2.2), the kinetics of complex reactions is a system of [ordinary differential equations](/2.4), diffusion and nonequilibrium transport require [partial differential equations](/2.5), statistical thermodynamics uses [Probability Theory](/3.1) and the molecular energy levels of [Quantum Physics](/5.5), and phase diagrams and kinetic curves are computed numerically with [Scientific Computing with Python](/4.4). Within chemistry, the electroanalytical and spectroscopic methods of [Analytical Chemistry](/6.4) have the electrochemistry and photochemistry treated here as their principles, and [Organic Chemistry](/6.5) uses the thermodynamics and kinetics here to discuss the energetics of reactions. Physical chemistry has especially many connections with [Biology](/7): the water potential in plant physiology is the chemical potential of water (relative to pure water) divided by the partial molar volume of water, and osmosis is described by the **osmotic pressure** of dilute solutions ([6.3.5](/6.3.5#sec-5.3.4)); the action of pulmonary surfactant comes from the Young–Laplace equation for curved interfaces ([6.3.19](/6.3.19#sec-19.2.1)); the **Michaelis–Menten equation** is the starting point of the kinetics of enzymatic reactions ([6.3.17](/6.3.17#sec-17.3.5)); the Nernst equation gives the equilibrium potential of a single ion across a membrane, the starting point for understanding the resting potential of a cell ([6.3.13](/6.3.13#sec-13.9.2)), the Donnan equilibrium explains the distribution of ions on the two sides of a charged macromolecule ([6.3.21](/6.3.21#sec-21.9.5)), and active transport is a coupling of free energies ([6.3.18](/6.3.18#sec-18.2.5)); and phase separation in regular solutions ([6.3.9](/6.3.9#sec-9.3.2)) is the starting point for understanding membraneless organelles in cells. These topics reappear in courses on [Biochemistry](/7.1), [Biophysics](/7.6) and physiology.

Physical chemistry is in fact the chemistry course most closely related to the theoretical side of the biological sciences that I like best. The water potential of cells, the surfactant of the alveoli, the kinetics of enzymatic reactions, the Nernst equation and phase separation, the most quantitative concepts in biology, all rest on the principles of physical chemistry; once one understands chemical potential, interfacial thermodynamics and electrochemical equilibrium, they are no longer separate conclusions to be memorized but applications of one and the same theory on different occasions. Unfortunately the instructors hardly ever connect these biological processes in their lectures, let alone go into them deeply. When I mention this knowledge on the biology pages later, I hope to analyze it genuinely with the theory found here, rather than stopping at a qualitative description.
