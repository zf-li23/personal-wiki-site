The carbon–carbon π bond is the most common and most reactive functional group in organic chemistry. The preceding chapters dealt with substitution and elimination at saturated carbon ([6.5.8](/6.5.8), [6.5.9](/6.5.9)): elimination produces alkenes, and in this chapter we turn to the reverse process, addition to alkenes, alkynes and conjugated dienes. The π electrons are high in energy and far from the nuclei, so they are good electron donors, and the typical reaction of an unsaturated hydrocarbon is addition of an **electrophile**. Other reactions include radical addition, oxidation, catalytic hydrogenation, carbene addition and polymerization, and terminal alkynes show characteristic behavior of their own, namely acidity and nucleophilic addition. The tools used here come from earlier chapters: the structure of the π bond and **frontier orbitals** ([6.1.3](/6.1.3), [6.5.2](/6.5.2)), the stability of **carbocations** and radicals ([6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.3.2), [6.5.7](/6.5.7#sec-7.3)), and reaction energy diagrams with kinetic versus thermodynamic control ([6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.9.2)). Concerted reactions between conjugated dienes and π systems (the **Diels–Alder reaction** and others) are left to the next chapter, [6.5.12](/6.5.12), on **pericyclic reactions**.

## 11.1. Structure of Unsaturated Hydrocarbons and Frontier Orbitals

### 11.1.1. Alkenes and Alkynes

Alkenes contain a C=C double bond, and acyclic monoalkenes have the general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$; alkynes contain a C≡C triple bond and have the general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n-2}}$. The double-bond carbons of ethene bond through $\mathrm{sp^2}$ **hybrid orbitals**: three $\mathrm{sp^2}$ orbitals form σ bonds with bond angles of about $120^\circ$ (the measured $\angle\mathrm{HCH}$ is about $117^\circ$), and the unhybridized p orbitals overlap sideways to form the **pi bond**; all six atoms lie in one plane. The carbons of ethyne are sp hybridized and the molecule is linear; two mutually perpendicular sets of p orbitals form two π bonds, so the π electron density is distributed cylindrically around the bond axis.

| | Ethane | Ethene | Ethyne |
|---|---|---|---|
| C–C bond length / pm | 154 | 134 | 120 |
| Mean C–C bond enthalpy / $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ | 346 | 610 | 835 |
| C–H bond length / pm | 109 | 108.7 | 106.1 |
| Hybridization of carbon, s character | $\mathrm{sp^3}$, 25% | $\mathrm{sp^2}$, 33% | sp, 50% |

The bond enthalpies are taken from the table in [6.1.3](/6.1.3). The bond enthalpy of the π bond is therefore about $610 - 346 \approx 264\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, clearly weaker than that of a σ bond; the π bond is the source of the reactivity of unsaturated hydrocarbons. Rotation about a double bond is not free, which is why alkenes show Z/E isomerism ([6.5.5](/6.5.5#sec-5.1.3)); thermal cis–trans isomerization of ethene must surmount a barrier of about $270\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, the cost of breaking the π bond completely at a twist of $90^\circ$. Alkynes are linear and have no cis–trans isomers. The C–H bond becomes shorter as the s character of the carbon increases: an s orbital lies close to the nucleus, so the more s character a hybrid orbital has, the closer its electrons are to the carbon nucleus, the shorter the bond, and the more electronegative the carbon. This is also why the C–H bond of a terminal alkyne is acidic (Section 11.12.2).

### 11.1.2. Dienes

According to the relative positions of the two double bonds, dienes fall into three classes:

- Cumulated dienes (allenes), $\ce{H2C=C=CH2}$: the central carbon is sp hybridized, the two π bonds are perpendicular to each other, and so are the planes of the two terminal $\ce{CH2}$ groups; hence $\ce{RCH=C=CHR}$ shows **axial chirality** ([6.5.5](/6.5.5#sec-5.5.1)).
- Conjugated dienes, $\ce{H2C=CH-CH=CH2}$: the p orbitals of the four $\mathrm{sp^2}$ carbons overlap continuously to form a **delocalized pi bond**.
- Isolated dienes, $\ce{H2C=CH-CH2-CH=CH2}$: the two double bonds are separated by an $\mathrm{sp^3}$ carbon, and the properties are those of simple alkenes.

The C2–C3 bond of buta-1,3-diene is $147\,\mathrm{pm}$ long, shorter than an ordinary C–C single bond (the $\mathrm{sp^2}$–$\mathrm{sp^2}$ σ bond is itself short, and there is a little π overlap). Rotation about it gives two planar **conformations**: s-cis, with the two double bonds on the same side of the single bond, and s-trans, with them on opposite sides. The s-trans conformer, free of repulsion between the inner hydrogens on C1 and C4, is more stable by about $12\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and predominates at room temperature; the s-cis conformer is in fact slightly twisted into a gauche form, and the barrier between them is low. The **Diels–Alder reaction** can proceed only through the s-cis conformation ([6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.4.3)), so the conformational preference of a diene bears directly on its reactivity.

### 11.1.3. Frontier Orbitals

The reactions of unsaturated hydrocarbons are governed first of all by their frontier orbitals (for the general concept of **frontier orbitals** see [6.1.3](/6.1.3); for Hückel orbitals of π systems see [6.5.2](/6.5.2#sec-2.2)):

- The **highest occupied molecular orbital** of an alkene is the π orbital, much higher in energy than the σ orbitals, and it is the target of electrophilic attack. The **lowest unoccupied molecular orbital** is the π* orbital, which is high in energy, so simple alkenes hardly accept nucleophiles; only alkenes that carry electron-withdrawing groups (α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds, [6.5.17](/6.5.17#sec-17.6)) undergo **nucleophilic addition**.
- Alkyl groups are electron donors and raise the π orbital (the ionization energies of propene and 2-methylpropene decrease in turn), so polysubstituted alkenes undergo electrophilic addition faster.
- The π orbital of ethyne lies about $0.9\,\mathrm{eV}$ below that of ethene (sp carbon is more electronegative), so electrophilic addition to alkynes is slower than to alkenes. The π* orbital of ethyne (its electron affinity) is, on the contrary, slightly higher than that of ethene, yet nucleophilic addition to alkynes is easier than to alkenes; the reason lies on the product side: the lone pair of the vinyl anion that is formed occupies an $\mathrm{sp^2}$ orbital (Section 11.12.5).
- Conjugation raises the HOMO and lowers the LUMO (Hückel result, [6.5.2](/6.5.2#sec-2.2)). The HOMO–LUMO gap of buta-1,3-diene is much smaller than that of ethene: conjugated dienes absorb at longer wavelengths in the ultraviolet ([6.4.9](/6.4.9)) and are more reactive toward both electrophiles and pericyclic reactions.

![Frontier orbital energies estimated from experiment (Koopmans approximation: the HOMO energy is taken as the negative of the first vertical ionization energy, the LUMO energy as the electron attachment energy from electron transmission spectra): ethyne, ethene, propene, 2-methylpropene and buta-1,3-diene. Both alkyl substitution and conjugation raise the HOMO](./plots/pi-frontier-levels.py)

## 11.2. Thermodynamics and Types of Addition Reactions

### 11.2.1. Thermodynamics of Addition

An addition reaction breaks one π bond and one σ bond of the reagent and forms two new σ bonds; it is usually exothermic. A rough estimate with **bond enthalpies** for $\ce{CH2=CH2 + HBr -> CH3CH2Br}$ breaks the π bond (about 264) and H–Br (366) and forms C–H (413) and C–Br (about 285):

$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H \approx (264 + 366) - (413 + 285) = -68\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
$$

Two molecules combine into one, so the entropy decreases. For the hydrogenation of ethene, the CRC standard enthalpies of formation and standard entropies ($\ce{C2H4}$: $52.4\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, $219.3\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}$; $\ce{C2H6}$: $-84.0$, $229.2$; $\ce{H2}$: $0$, $130.7$) give $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H^\circ = -136.4\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}S^\circ = -120.8\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}$, so that

$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G^\circ = \Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H^\circ - T\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}S^\circ = -100.4\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \quad (298\,\mathrm{K}).
$$

The term $-T\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}S^\circ$ grows with temperature, and above $T = \Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H^\circ/\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}S^\circ \approx 1100\,\mathrm{K}$ the reverse reaction (dehydrogenation) predominates. The statement "addition is exothermic and therefore always favorable" is thus imprecise: low temperatures favor addition and high temperatures favor the reverse reaction, elimination ([6.5.9](/6.5.9)); industrially, ethene is made at high temperature by dehydrogenation and cracking of ethane (Section 11.13). Nor does thermodynamic favorability guarantee that a reaction occurs: ethene and $\ce{H2}$ do not react in the absence of a catalyst.

| Radical addition | Radicals ($\ce{Br.}$, $\ce{RS.}$) | Addition of HBr in the presence of peroxides, thiol–ene reaction | 11.7 |

## 11.3. Mechanism of Electrophilic Addition and Markovnikov's Rule

### 11.3.1. The Two-Step Mechanism

**Definition 11.3.1 (Electrophilic Addition)** An electrophile $\ce{E+}$ (or the positive end of a dipole or induced dipole) first interacts with the π bond to give a cationic intermediate, and a nucleophile $\ce{Nu-}$ then combines with the intermediate, so that E and Nu are added to the two originally unsaturated carbons. This reaction is called **electrophilic addition**.

$$
\ce{C=C + E-Nu -> [E-C-C^+] + Nu- -> E-C-C-Nu}
$$

The first step forms the cation and is rate-determining; in the second step the cation combines rapidly with the nucleophile. There are three types of intermediate, which determine the regioselectivity and stereochemistry of the addition:

| Intermediate | Examples | Regioselectivity | Stereochemistry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open carbocation | HX, $\ce{H3O+}$ | Markovnikov | Not selective (both syn and anti addition), rearrangement possible |
| Bridged onium ion | $\ce{Br2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{Hg(OAc)2}$, $\ce{PhSeCl}$ | The nucleophile attacks the more substituted carbon | Anti addition |
| No intermediate (concerted, four-center) | $\ce{BH3}$ | B adds to the less substituted carbon | Syn addition |

![Electrophilic addition of HBr to propene: the π bond takes up a proton to give a secondary carbocation (slow), and bromide then combines with the carbocation (fast)](./plots/hx-addition.py)

### 11.3.2. Markovnikov's Rule

**Definition 11.3.2 (Markovnikov's Rule)** In the ionic addition of a polar reagent such as a hydrogen halide to an unsymmetrical alkene, the hydrogen atom adds to the double-bond carbon that bears more hydrogens, and the halogen or other group adds to the carbon that bears fewer hydrogens. This empirical regularity is called **Markovnikov's rule** (1870).

The rule reflects the fact that the first step produces the more stable carbocation. The stability order of carbocations is tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl, which arises from **hyperconjugation** and polarization effects ([6.5.2](/6.5.2#sec-2.6), [6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.3.2)); by the **Hammond postulate** ([6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.10)), the transition state of the endothermic protonation step is late and resembles the carbocation, so the transition state leading to the more stable carbocation is also lower in energy. The modern statement is therefore that the electrophile adds at the position that makes the cationic intermediate most stable. This formulation is more general than "hydrogen adds to the carbon that has more hydrogens":

- Electron-withdrawing groups destabilize an adjacent carbocation: $\ce{CF3CH=CH2 + HBr -> CF3CH2CH2Br}$ looks "anti-Markovnikov" taken literally, but follows the same principle in terms of carbocation stability (the proton adds to the carbon next to $\ce{CF3}$, so that the positive charge is farther from $\ce{CF3}$).
- Substituents with lone pairs ($\ce{-OR}$, $\ce{-NR2}$, halogens) stabilize an adjacent cation by conjugation: the enol ether $\ce{CH2=CH-OEt}$ is protonated on $\ce{CH2}$ to give an oxonium ion.
- Additions that do not proceed through cations (radical addition, Section 11.7; hydroboration, Section 11.4.6) have different regioselectivity.

![Two pathways for the addition of HBr to propene (schematic): the pathway through the secondary carbocation has the lower transition state and is the main pathway](./plots/markovnikov-energy.py)

### 11.3.3. Stereochemistry and Rearrangement

An open carbocation is planar and the nucleophile can attack from either face, so additions by the carbocation mechanism show no stereoselectivity: a new stereogenic center is formed as the **racemate**, and cyclic alkenes give mixtures of syn and anti addition products. Carbocations also undergo 1,2-hydride or 1,2-alkyl shifts to give more stable cations ([6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.3.3)): addition of HCl to 3-methylbut-1-ene gives, besides the normal product 2-chloro-3-methylbutane, a considerable amount of the rearranged product 2-chloro-2-methylbutane.

```smiles
C=CC(C)C>>CC(Cl)C(C)C.CC(C)(Cl)CC
```

![Carbocation rearrangement in electrophilic addition: 3-methylbut-1-ene and HCl. The π bond takes the proton at the CH₂ end, giving the secondary cation (Markovnikov orientation); chloride can capture it directly (2-chloro-3-methylbutane), or the hydrogen on the neighboring tertiary carbon can first move with its electron pair (1,2-hydride shift) to give the more stable tertiary cation, which gives the rearranged product 2-chloro-2-methylbutane](./plots/hcl-rearrangement.py)

A cation can also be captured by an intramolecular π bond to form a new C–C bond: the carbocation generated by loss of a leaving group attacks a double bond in the ring (the orientation still obeys Markovnikov's rule) and is then trapped by water, building a bicyclic skeleton in a single step (Question 2). In the biosynthesis of steroids, 2,3-oxidosqualene is converted by a cyclase through a series of such cationic cyclizations into lanosterol, and four rings are formed in one enzymatic reaction.

### 11.3.4. Reactivity

The rate-determining step of electrophilic addition is the transfer of π electrons to the electrophile, so:

- Electronic effects: electron-donating groups raise the HOMO and stabilize the cation and so accelerate the reaction; electron-withdrawing groups slow it. $\ce{CH3CH=CH2} > \ce{ClCH2CH=CH2} > \ce{Cl2CHCH=CH2} > \ce{Cl3CCH=CH2}$; polyhalogenated and polycyano alkenes such as tetrafluoroethene and tetracyanoethene hardly undergo electrophilic addition and instead react readily with nucleophiles.
- Steric hindrance: the electrophile must approach from above or below the π bond, and a double bond surrounded by four bulky groups (such as ethene bearing four tert-butyl groups) reacts with difficulty.
- Reagent: the reactivity of HX is $\ce{HI} > \ce{HBr} > \ce{HCl} > \ce{HF}$, the same as the order of acidity.

## 11.4. Electrophilic Addition to Alkenes

### 11.4.1. Addition of Hydrogen Halides and Acid-Catalyzed Hydration

Alkenes add HX to give alkyl halides (Markovnikov orientation); HX gas is usually passed into a nonpolar solvent or acetic acid. In the presence of a strong acid catalyst such as dilute sulfuric or phosphoric acid, alkenes add water to give alcohols (acid-catalyzed hydration), by the same mechanism as the addition of HX: protonation gives a carbocation, water attacks, and a proton is lost.

```smiles
CC(C)=C.O>>CC(C)(C)O
```

Hydration is reversible, and the reverse reaction is the acid-catalyzed dehydration of alcohols ([6.5.9](/6.5.9#sec-9.8.1)). The position of equilibrium is governed by Le Chatelier's principle: a large excess of water and a lower temperature favor the alcohol, whereas concentrated acid, heating and distillation of the alkene favor dehydration. Industrially, ethanol is made by gas-phase hydration of ethene with steam over a phosphoric acid–diatomaceous earth catalyst (about $300\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $7\,\mathrm{MPa}$); the conversion per pass is only about 5%, and unreacted ethene is recycled. Acid-catalyzed hydration is accompanied by rearrangements, so in the laboratory the following two methods, which do not involve open carbocations, are more commonly used: oxymercuration (giving the Markovnikov alcohol) and hydroboration–oxidation (giving the anti-Markovnikov alcohol).

### 11.4.2. Addition of Halogens and Onium Ions

Alkenes add $\ce{Br2}$ rapidly in $\ce{CH2Cl2}$ or $\ce{CCl4}$, and the red-brown color of bromine disappears, which can be used as a test for unsaturation. The addition is found to be anti: cyclohexene gives only trans-1,2-dibromocyclohexane (as the **racemate**), (Z)-but-2-ene gives racemic (2R,3R)/(2S,3S)-2,3-dibromobutane, and (E)-but-2-ene gives the **meso compound** (2R,3S)-2,3-dibromobutane. An open carbocation cannot explain this **stereospecificity**, and the following intermediate was therefore proposed:

**Definition 11.4.1 (Bromonium Ion)** A bromine molecule is polarized by the π electrons of the alkene, and the proximal bromine uses a lone pair to bond to both double-bond carbons at once, giving a positively charged three-membered ring intermediate called a **bromonium ion**; analogous three-membered cations containing chlorine or iodine are collectively called halonium ions.

Bromide can attack only from the back side of the bromine bridge (like **SN2**, [6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.4.1)), and ring opening gives the anti addition product:

![Bromination of cyclohexene: the π bond attacks bromine to give a bromonium ion, and bromide opens it from the back side to give racemic trans-1,2-dibromocyclohexane](./plots/bromination.py)

```smiles
C/C=C\C.BrBr>>C[C@@H](Br)[C@@H](C)Br (±)
C/C=C/C.BrBr>>C[C@@H](Br)[C@H](C)Br meso
```

Direct evidence for the bromonium ion came from Olah, who observed the ethylenebromonium ion in a superacid medium ($\ce{SbF5}$–$\ce{SO2}$, $-60\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$): the four hydrogens are equivalent, with a $^1\mathrm{H}$ **chemical shift** $\delta$ 5.53, a $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ shift $\delta$ 73.8 and $^1J_{\mathrm{CH}} = 185\,\mathrm{Hz}$ (for the meaning of chemical shifts and coupling constants see [6.4.12](/6.4.12)). In an open carbocation the $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ shift of the cationic carbon lies above $\delta$ 300 (about $\delta$ 335 for the tert-butyl cation), so the observed $\delta$ 73.8 shows that the positive charge resides mainly on bromine and that the two carbons are equivalent through the bromine bridge. In 1985 Brown and coworkers determined the X-ray crystal structure of the bromonium ion of adamantylideneadamantane, which is so crowded that it cannot open.

The halogens differ: chlorine is small and weakly polarizable, so the bridge is weaker and more positive charge falls on carbon, especially for substrates such as phenyl-substituted alkenes that can stabilize a cation, and chlorination is less stereoselectively anti; the addition of iodine is reversible and the diiodides are unstable; fluorine reacts violently with alkenes and cannot be controlled.

Opening of onium ions derived from cyclohexene follows the rule of trans-diaxial opening (the Fürst–Plattner rule): the nucleophile attacks antiperiplanar to the leaving bond and directly gives a **chair conformation** in which both new bonds are **axial bonds**, which only afterwards undergoes a **ring flip** to the more stable diequatorial conformation ([6.5.4](/6.5.4#sec-4.5.1)). In rings whose conformation is locked (such as 4-tert-butylcyclohexene), this rule decides which trans isomer is formed.

### 11.4.3. Halohydrins

In bromination in the presence of water ($\ce{Br2/H2O}$, or more conveniently NBS in aqueous DMSO), the solvent water competes with bromide in attacking the bromonium ion; water is present in high concentration, and the main products are β-halo alcohols. The regioselectivity is that "OH adds to the more substituted carbon": in an unsymmetrical bromonium ion the C–Br bond to the more substituted carbon is longer and carries more positive charge, so the transition state has more carbocation character, and water attacks this carbon preferentially. This agrees with the Markovnikov orientation of the carbocation mechanism, although the stereochemistry is still anti.

```smiles
CC1=CCCCC1>>C[C@]1(O)CCCC[C@@H]1Br (±)
```

![Bromohydrin formation from 1-methylcyclohexene in water: the π bond attacks bromine to give the bromonium ion; water, present in large excess, attacks the more substituted carbon (whose C–Br bond is longer and carries more positive charge) from the face opposite to the bridging bromine, and loss of a proton gives the trans bromohydrin with OH on the tertiary carbon (racemic)](./plots/halohydrin.py)

Halohydrins undergo intramolecular SN2 reaction under base to give **epoxides** ([6.5.10](/6.5.10#sec-10.2.2)).

### 11.4.4. Oxymercuration–Demercuration

Reaction of an alkene with mercury(II) acetate in water–THF, followed by reduction with $\ce{NaBH4}$, gives the alcohol with Markovnikov orientation; this is called oxymercuration–demercuration. The electrophile $\ce{AcOHg+}$ forms a bridged mercurinium ion with the alkene, water attacks the more substituted carbon from the back side (anti), and loss of a proton gives an organomercury compound; $\ce{NaBH4}$ then replaces the C–Hg bond with a C–H bond. This last step proceeds through radicals and is not stereospecific. Compared with acid-catalyzed hydration, the mercurinium ion is not an open carbocation, no rearrangement takes place, and the conditions are mild; using an alcohol in place of water (alkoxymercuration) gives ethers. Mercury compounds are highly toxic, so the method is now seldom used, and Markovnikov alcohols are mostly prepared by other methods.

![Oxymercuration–demercuration of 2-methylpropene: water opens the mercurinium ion at the tertiary carbon, and after loss of a proton, demercuration with $\ce{NaBH4}$ gives tert-butyl alcohol](./plots/oxymercuration.py)

An intramolecular hydroxy group can likewise attack the mercurinium ion to form a cyclic ether (Question 3).

### 11.4.5. Sulfonium and Selenonium Ions

Sulfenyl chloride $\ce{PhSCl}$ and benzeneselenenyl chloride $\ce{PhSeCl}$ form three-membered episulfonium and episelenonium ions with alkenes, which the nucleophile opens from the back side, again giving anti addition. The value of selenium lies in the subsequent reaction: after oxidation of the adduct to the selenoxide, syn elimination through a five-membered transition state occurs already at room temperature, losing $\ce{PhSeOH}$ and generating a new double bond (the same type as the Cope elimination in [6.5.23](/6.5.23#sec-23.8.4)). The sulfur atoms of cysteine and methionine in proteins can also act as nucleophiles and open such onium ions, which is the chemical basis for the modification of proteins by some electrophilic drugs and halogenating reagents.

![Selenoxide syn elimination: after the PhSe group of the addition product has been oxidized (H₂O₂ or mCPBA) to the selenoxide, the selenoxide oxygen takes a hydrogen on the neighboring carbon through a five-membered cyclic transition state; the C–H bond becomes the new π bond, the C–Se bond breaks toward selenium, and PhSeOH is released at room temperature. The H and the Se leave from the same side (syn elimination)](./plots/selenoxide-elimination.py)

### 11.4.6. Hydroboration–Oxidation

**Definition 11.4.2 (Hydroboration)** The concerted addition of a B–H bond of a borane across a C=C or C≡C bond is called **hydroboration**: boron adds to the less substituted carbon and hydrogen to the more substituted carbon, and both add to the same face of the π bond.

Borane $\ce{BH3}$ exists as the dimer diborane $\ce{B2H6}$, in which the two borons are joined by two three-center two-electron B–H–B bonds; in the laboratory its THF complex $\ce{BH3*THF}$ is used. Boron has only six valence electrons and an empty p orbital, so it is a **Lewis acid**. The electronegativity of B (2.04) is lower than that of H (2.20), so the hydrogen of B–H carries a partial negative charge and the boron end is the electrophilic center. The reaction takes place in a single step through a four-center transition state: the π electrons attack the empty orbital of boron while H, carrying a pair of electrons, transfers to the other carbon. In the transition state the C–B bond forms ahead of the C–H bond, and the more substituted carbon carries partial positive charge; this agrees with the steric effect (boron and its substituents are large and add preferentially to the less hindered carbon), and both place boron on the less substituted carbon.

![Hydroboration (top): propene and $\ce{BH3}$ add in one step through a four-center transition state, with B adding to the terminal carbon; oxidation (bottom): hydroperoxide anion adds to boron, an alkyl group migrates with its electron pair from B to O with retention of configuration, and hydrolysis gives the alcohol](./plots/hydroboration.py)

All three B–H bonds of $\ce{BH3}$ can add, giving a trialkylborane. In alkaline hydrogen peroxide, $\ce{HOO-}$ adds to boron, and an alkyl group migrates from B to O with expulsion of $\ce{HO-}$, with retention of configuration at carbon; after three repetitions, hydrolysis gives the alcohol. The overall result is anti-Markovnikov, syn hydration, complementary to acid-catalyzed hydration and oxymercuration, and a good method for converting terminal alkenes into primary alcohols:

```smiles
CC1=CCCC1>>C[C@@H]1CCC[C@H]1O (±)
```

1-Methylcyclopentene gives trans-2-methylcyclopentanol (H and OH add on the same face, so OH and methyl are trans). Bulky boranes (dicyclohexylborane, 9-BBN, diisopinocampheylborane $\ce{Ipc2BH}$) are more regioselective, and chiral boranes also allow asymmetric hydroboration ([6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.4.3)). H. C. Brown received the 1979 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for organoboron chemistry.

## 11.5. Electrophilic Addition to Alkynes

Addition of hydrogen halides: alkynes add 1 equivalent of HX to give a haloalkene (Markovnikov orientation), and a further equivalent gives the geminal dihalide (both halogens on the same carbon: the cation formed in the second protonation is stabilized by a lone pair on the halogen already present). The intermediate is the vinyl cation $\ce{R-C+=CH2}$, whose positive charge is on an sp carbon and which is far less stable than an alkyl carbocation; the actual addition often proceeds through a termolecular transition state (the triple bond interacts simultaneously with HX and a second HX or $\ce{X-}$). The reverse reaction, double elimination from a geminal or vicinal dihalide, is a method for preparing alkynes (Section 11.13).

Addition of halogens: 1 equivalent of $\ce{Br2}$ adds mainly anti to give the (E)-1,2-dibromoalkene, and an excess gives the tetrabromide. Alkynes decolorize bromine water more slowly than alkenes do.

Hydration: alkynes add water under $\ce{HgSO4}$–$\ce{H2SO4}$ catalysis, first giving an enol, which rapidly isomerizes to the carbonyl compound. Terminal alkynes give methyl ketones by Markovnikov orientation, and ethyne gives acetaldehyde (historically the industrial route to acetaldehyde); unsymmetrical internal alkynes give mixtures of two ketones. Mercury-free catalysts such as gold(I) complexes are now often used.

```smiles
CCCCC#C.O>>CCCCC(C)=O
```

![Conversion of the enol formed by hydration of an alkyne into a ketone under acid catalysis: C=C is protonated at the $\ce{CH2}$ end to give an oxonium ion (a lone pair on oxygen stabilizes the positive charge), and the O–H proton is then lost](./plots/enol-tautomerization.py)

An enol and a ketone differ only in the position of one proton, and this kind of isomerism is called **tautomerism**; for simple aldehydes and ketones the equilibrium lies almost entirely on the keto side ([6.5.21](/6.5.21#sec-21.2.1)).

Hydroboration: terminal alkynes add a bulky borane (dicyclohexylborane, 9-BBN) once, with boron adding to the terminal carbon; oxidation gives an enol, which tautomerizes to the aldehyde. Hydration of a terminal alkyne ($\ce{Hg^2+}$) thus gives the methyl ketone, and hydroboration–oxidation gives the aldehyde; the two are complementary. If the hydroboration product is protonolyzed with acetic acid (without oxidation), the cis alkene is obtained.

Why electrophilic addition to alkynes is slower than to alkenes: the vinyl cation is unstable (positive charge on an sp carbon), and the HOMO of an alkyne is lower in energy (Section 11.1.3). However, once an alkyne is coordinated as a ligand to a soft Lewis acid such as $\ce{Hg^2+}$ or $\ce{Au+}$, it is readily attacked by nucleophiles, which is why modern alkyne chemistry makes extensive use of π-acid catalysis.

## 11.6. Addition to Conjugated Dienes

### 11.6.1. 1,2-Addition and 1,4-Addition

Buta-1,3-diene adds 1 equivalent of HBr or $\ce{Br2}$ to give two products. The proton adds to C1, and the allyl cation formed is a hybrid of two **resonance structures** with the positive charge distributed over C2 and C4; $\ce{Br-}$ attacks C2 or C4:

**Definition 11.6.1 (1,2-Addition and 1,4-Addition)** When a conjugated diene $\ce{C1=C2-C3=C4}$ adds a reagent E–Nu, addition of E and Nu to the adjacent carbons C1 and C2 is called **1,2-addition**; addition to the terminal carbons C1 and C4, with formation of a new double bond between C2 and C3, is called **1,4-addition** (also called conjugate addition, a name more commonly used for α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds, [6.5.17](/6.5.17)).

![Addition of HBr to buta-1,3-diene: protonation at C1 gives an allyl cation (two resonance structures); bromide attacking C2 gives the 1,2-addition product and attacking C4 gives the 1,4-addition product](./plots/diene-addition.py)

The proton always adds to a terminal carbon: addition to C2 would give only a primary carbocation that is not stabilized by conjugation.

### 11.6.2. Kinetic Control and Thermodynamic Control

The product ratio depends on temperature:

| Conditions | 1,2-Addition product (3-bromobut-1-ene) | 1,4-Addition product (1-bromobut-2-ene) |
|---|---|---|
| $-80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ | about 80% | about 20% |
| $40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ | about 15% | about 85% |
| Low-temperature product heated to $40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ | about 15% | about 85% |

At low temperature the 1,2-product forms faster: in the allyl cation C2 is a secondary carbon and carries more of the positive charge, and the $\ce{Br-}$ that has just left C1–H is, as an ion pair, closer to C2. The 1,4-product is a disubstituted internal alkene and is thermodynamically more stable. At low temperature neither product can revert, and the ratio is set by the ratio of the rate constants of the two pathways (**kinetic control**); at $40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the allylic bromides can dissociate again into an **ion pair** (SN1-type ionization, [6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.4.2)), the products equilibrate, and the ratio is set by the free-energy difference (**thermodynamic control**); the mixture obtained at low temperature likewise changes to the same equilibrium composition on heating. The general theory of **kinetic control** and **thermodynamic control** is given in [6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.9.2); this is a system of parallel reversible reactions, and the solution of the rate equations is described in [6.3.15](/6.3.15).

![Top: schematic free-energy profile for the two products formed from the allyl cation: the transition state to the 1,2-product is lower, and the 1,4-product is more stable. Bottom: solving the first-order linear rate equations with a set of model barriers (Eyring rate constants): at $-80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the fraction of 1,4-product stays at the kinetic ratio, and at $+40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ it approaches the equilibrium ratio over several hours](./plots/diene-control.py)

## 11.7. Radical Addition

### 11.7.1. The Peroxide Effect

Pure alkenes add HBr according to Markovnikov's rule; in the presence of peroxides or light, however, HBr adds with the opposite orientation to give the 1-bromoalkane. Kharasch discovered in 1933 that the cause is the small amount of peroxide formed by air oxidation in commercial alkenes, a phenomenon called the *peroxide effect*. It is a **chain reaction** of **radicals** (the general theory of chain reactions is given in [6.3.15](/6.3.15)):

![Radical chain addition of HBr to propene in the presence of peroxides: homolysis of the peroxide initiates the chain; a Br atom adds to the terminal carbon to give the more stable secondary radical, which abstracts a hydrogen atom from HBr and regenerates a Br atom. Fishhook arrows represent the movement of single electrons](./plots/radical-hbr.py)

The reason for the orientation: the first propagation step is the addition of $\ce{Br.}$ to the double bond, and it adds to the terminal carbon to give the more stable secondary radical (the stability order of radicals is the same as for carbocations, tertiary > secondary > primary, [6.5.7](/6.5.7#sec-7.3)), and the terminal carbon is also less hindered. What decides the orientation is "which species adds first": in ionic addition it is $\ce{H+}$, in radical addition it is $\ce{Br.}$, so the two orientations are opposite, but both generate the more stable intermediate.

### 11.7.2. Why Only HBr

Both propagation steps must be exothermic (or nearly thermoneutral) for the chain to propagate rapidly. Estimating with mean bond enthalpies (π bond about 264, C–H 413, H–X see [6.1.13](/6.1.13), C–X as the mean bond enthalpy):

| X | ① $\ce{X. + C=C -> X-C-C.}$: $\Delta H \approx D(\pi) - D(\ce{C-X})$ | ② $\ce{C. + H-X -> C-H + X.}$: $\Delta H \approx D(\ce{H-X}) - D(\ce{C-H})$ |
|---|---|---|
| Cl | $264 - 327 = -63$ | $432 - 413 = +19$ |
| Br | $264 - 285 = -21$ | $366 - 413 = -47$ |
| I | $264 - 213 = +51$ | $298 - 413 = -115$ |

(Units: $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.) For HCl the second step is endothermic: the H–Cl bond is too strong, and chain propagation is very slow. For HI the first step is endothermic: the C–I bond is too weak, and an iodine atom that adds falls off again quickly. Only for HBr are both steps exothermic, so the peroxide effect is observed only for HBr.

### 11.7.3. Other Radical Additions

Thiols add to alkenes with anti-Markovnikov orientation under the action of photoinitiators or radical initiators to give sulfides (the thiol–ene reaction): the S–H bond is weak (about $365\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$), the two steps of $\ce{RS.}$ addition and hydrogen transfer are both fast, and the reaction is quantitative and tolerant of oxygen and water. It is used as a "click" reaction for crosslinking polymers, for hydrogels, and for the modification of biomolecules. $\ce{BrCCl3}$ and $\ce{CCl4}$ also add to alkenes in the presence of initiators (Kharasch addition), with $\ce{.CCl3}$ adding to the terminal carbon.

## 11.8. Oxidation

### 11.8.1. Epoxidation

A peroxy acid $\ce{RCO3H}$ (meta-chloroperoxybenzoic acid, mCPBA, or peroxyacetic acid) transfers an oxygen atom to an alkene to give an **epoxide** (a three-membered cyclic ether, [6.5.10](/6.5.10#sec-10.1.1)). The reaction proceeds through a concerted "butterfly" transition state: the π electrons attack the outer oxygen of the peroxy acid, the weak O–O bond breaks, the O–H proton is transferred to the carbonyl oxygen, and the two C–O bonds form on the same face.

![Epoxidation of an alkene by a peroxy acid (Bartlett butterfly mechanism): the movement of four electron pairs is completed in one step, and (Z)-but-2-ene gives cis-2,3-dimethyloxirane](./plots/epoxidation.py)

- Stereospecific: cis alkenes give cis epoxides and trans alkenes give trans epoxides; the configuration of existing stereogenic centers is unchanged. When the two faces of the double bond differ in steric hindrance, the peroxy acid attacks from the less hindered face and gives unequal amounts of diastereomers.
- Electronic effect: the oxygen of the peroxy acid is electrophilic (the alkene HOMO interacts with the O–O σ* orbital), so polysubstituted, electron-rich double bonds react faster, and when a molecule contains several double bonds the most electron-rich one is epoxidized selectively.
- Epoxides open in acid or base with water attacking from the back side, giving trans vicinal diols: epoxidation followed by hydrolysis is an anti dihydroxylation, complementary to the syn dihydroxylation below.

Allylic alcohols undergo highly enantioselective epoxidation with $\ce{Ti(O-\mathit{i}Pr)4}$, diethyl tartrate and tert-butyl hydroperoxide (Sharpless asymmetric epoxidation, part of the 2001 Nobel Prize in Chemistry; for **asymmetric synthesis** see [6.5.5](/6.5.5#sec-5.8.2)).

### 11.8.2. Syn Dihydroxylation

$\ce{OsO4}$ undergoes a concerted [3+2] cycloaddition with alkenes to give a cyclic osmate ester, which is hydrolyzed to the syn vicinal diol. $\ce{OsO4}$ is expensive, highly toxic and volatile, so in practice only a catalytic amount is used, with N-methylmorpholine N-oxide (NMO) reoxidizing Os(VI) to Os(VIII) (the Upjohn procedure); adding chiral cinchona alkaloid ligands allows asymmetric dihydroxylation (Sharpless). Cold, dilute, basic $\ce{KMnO4}$ also gives syn diols through an analogous cyclic manganate ester, with loss of the purple color and precipitation of brown $\ce{MnO2}$, which can be used as a test for double bonds; however, $\ce{KMnO4}$ readily oxidizes the diol further with cleavage, and the yields are poor.

![[3+2] cycloaddition of $\ce{OsO4}$ gives a cyclic osmate ester, which is hydrolyzed to cis-cyclohexane-1,2-diol; NMO allows the osmium to be recycled](./plots/osmylation.py)

### 11.8.3. Ozonolysis

**Definition 11.8.1 (Ozonolysis)** The reaction of an alkene with ozone in which the C=C double bond is cleaved completely to give two carbonyl compounds (after reductive work-up) is called **ozonolysis**.

The reaction is carried out by passing $\ce{O3}$ into $\ce{CH2Cl2}$ or methanol at $-78\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and the appearance of the pale blue color of ozone in the solution indicates that the reaction is complete. The Criegee mechanism has three steps: ozone (a 1,3-dipole, [6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.4.5)) undergoes **1,3-dipolar cycloaddition** with the alkene to give the unstable primary ozonide (1,2,3-trioxolane); this fragments by a retro-[3+2] reaction into a carbonyl compound and a carbonyl oxide (the Criegee intermediate); the two recombine by a second [3+2] cycloaddition with the opposite orientation to give the secondary ozonide (1,2,4-trioxolane). Isotope labeling and crossover experiments (ozonolysis of two different alkenes together gives crossed ozonides) support this mechanism.

![The Criegee mechanism of ozonolysis: 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition, retro-[3+2] fragmentation, second [3+2] cycloaddition, and finally reduction with $\ce{Me2S}$](./plots/ozonolysis.py)

Ozonides are explosive and are generally not isolated but worked up directly:

| Work-up | Product |
|---|---|
| $\ce{Me2S}$, or Zn/AcOH (reductive) | Aldehydes, ketones |
| $\ce{H2O2}$ (oxidative) | Carboxylic acids (from $\ce{=CHR}$), ketones |
| $\ce{NaBH4}$ | Alcohols |

```smiles
CC1=CCCCC1>>CC(=O)CCCCC=O
```

Ozonolysis of a cycloalkene gives an acyclic dicarbonyl compound. Deducing the position of the original double bond from the products was once an important means of determining the structures of natural products ([6.5.6](/6.5.6)). Hot concentrated $\ce{KMnO4}$ likewise cleaves double bonds but oxidizes the aldehydes to carboxylic acids, and a terminal $\ce{=CH2}$ becomes $\ce{CO2}$; $\ce{OsO4}$ (catalytic) combined with $\ce{NaIO4}$ (Lemieux–Johnson oxidation) first dihydroxylates and then cleaves the vicinal diol ([6.5.10](/6.5.10#sec-10.7)), giving the same aldehydes and ketones as ozonolysis.

### 11.8.4. Allylic Oxidation with Selenium Dioxide

$\ce{SeO2}$ (Riley oxidation) oxidizes an allylic C–H bond to the allylic alcohol, with the double bond remaining in position; in trisubstituted alkenes the trans methyl group is oxidized selectively, and with $t$-BuOOH as the co-oxidant only a catalytic amount of $\ce{SeO2}$ is needed. The mechanism (Sharpless) consists of two pericyclic steps: $\ce{SeO2}$ acts as an enophile in an ene reaction with the alkene to give an allylic seleninic acid, which transfers oxygen to the original allylic carbon by a [2,3]-σ shift, returning the double bond to its original position; hydrolysis gives the alcohol (for the ene reaction and the [2,3]-σ shift see [6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.7)). $\ce{SeO2}$ also oxidizes benzylic C–H bonds and the α-$\ce{CH2}$ of carbonyl groups ([6.5.17](/6.5.17#sec-17.5.3)). Catalytic asymmetric allylic functionalization of alkenes through the same ene/[2,3] pathway with chiral selenium or sulfur catalysts is a recent research direction.

```smiles
CC(C)=CC>>OC/C(C)=C/C
```

### 11.8.5. Single-Electron Oxidation

Alkenes can also lose one electron to give **radical cations**. A visible-light-excited acridinium photocatalyst (the excited state has a very high reduction potential) abstracts an electron from an electron-rich alkene such as styrene, a nucleophile adds to the less substituted carbon of the radical cation, and **hydrogen atom transfer** then gives the anti-Markovnikov addition product; Nicewicz and coworkers (*Nat. Chem.* 2014) used this method to achieve the anti-Markovnikov addition of mineral acids to styrenes. The general principles of **photoredox catalysis** are given in [6.5.31](/6.5.31#sec-31.5).

## 11.9. Catalytic Hydrogenation

Alkenes add $\ce{H2}$ over metal catalysts such as Pt, Pd (Pd/C) and Ni (Raney Ni) to give alkanes. This is **heterogeneous catalysis** ([6.3.20](/6.3.20)): $\ce{H2}$ dissociates on the metal surface by **chemisorption** into H atoms, the alkene is adsorbed on the surface through its π electrons, the two H atoms are transferred in turn to the double-bond carbons (Horiuti–Polanyi mechanism), and the product desorbs.

- Syn addition: both H atoms add from the same face of the alkene that is adsorbed on the surface; 1,2-dimethylcyclohexene gives mainly cis-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane (70%–85%; the small amount of trans product arises from stepwise hydrogenation and double-bond migration on the surface).
- Addition from the less hindered face: 2,3-dimethylnorbornene is adsorbed from the exo side (the face away from the one-carbon bridge), the two hydrogens add from the exo face, and the two methyl groups are pushed to the endo side.
- Rate: double bonds that are less hindered and adsorb easily react faster, ethene > monosubstituted > disubstituted > trisubstituted > tetrasubstituted, so the less substituted double bond in a molecule can be hydrogenated selectively.

```smiles
CC1=C(C)CCCC1>>C[C@@H]1CCCC[C@@H]1C cis
```

Heats of hydrogenation ($-\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H$) compare the energies of isomeric alkenes that give the same alkane and are the experimental basis for judging alkene stability: polysubstituted alkenes, trans alkenes and conjugated dienes are more stable ([6.5.2](/6.5.2#sec-2.6), [6.5.9](/6.5.9#sec-9.1)). Homogeneous hydrogenation catalyzed in solution by rhodium and ruthenium complexes (**Wilkinson's catalyst**, asymmetric hydrogenation) is treated in [6.5.29](/6.5.29#sec-29.2). A syn reduction that does not use $\ce{H2}$ is carried out with diimide $\ce{HN=NH}$ (generated in situ by oxidation of hydrazine), which transfers both H atoms simultaneously to the double bond through a cyclic transition state.

## 11.10. Carbenes and Cyclopropanation

### 11.10.1. Structure of Carbenes

**Definition 11.10.1 (Carbene)** A neutral reactive intermediate $\ce{R2C:}$ in which a carbon atom is bonded to only two atoms or groups and has only six valence electrons is called a **carbene**. A carbene whose two nonbonding electrons are paired in the same orbital is a singlet carbene, and one whose electrons occupy two different orbitals with parallel spins is a triplet carbene.

- **Singlet** carbene: the two nonbonding electrons are paired in a roughly $\mathrm{sp^2}$ orbital, and there is also an empty p orbital; the structure is bent ($\ce{CH2}$ about $102^\circ$). It resembles a carbocation (empty p orbital, electrophilic) and also a carbanion (lone pair).
- **Triplet** carbene: the two electrons occupy a σ orbital and a p orbital, like a diradical. Triplet $\ce{CH2}$ is also bent (about $136^\circ$, determined by EPR) and not a linear, sp-hybridized structure.

The ground state of methylene $\ce{CH2}$ is the triplet, with the singlet about $38\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ higher (Hund's rule: one electron in each of two orbitals of similar energy with parallel spins). Substituents with lone pairs, such as halogens, oxygen and nitrogen, donate electrons into the empty p orbital of the singlet and make the singlet the ground state: dichlorocarbene and dibromocarbene are singlets. In N-heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs), the lone pairs of the two nitrogens strongly stabilize the empty p orbital, and with large protecting substituents they can be isolated as stable crystals (Arduengo, 1991; 1,3-di(adamantan-1-yl)imidazol-2-ylidene, melting point $240$–$241\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$); they are now important ligands in transition-metal catalysis and organocatalysts ([6.5.28](/6.5.28#sec-28.5.4)). The reactivity of carbenes is roughly $\ce{H2C:} > \ce{ROOC-CH:} > \ce{PhCH:} > \ce{ClCH:} > \ce{Cl2C:}$.

| | Singlet | Triplet |
|---|---|---|
| Electrons | Two nonbonding electrons paired | Two unpaired electrons with parallel spins |
| With (Z)-but-2-ene | One-step concerted addition, giving only the cis cyclopropane (stereospecific) | Two steps through a diradical, giving both cis and trans cyclopropanes |
| C–H insertion | One-step insertion, low selectivity | Hydrogen abstraction followed by recombination, tertiary C–H > secondary > primary |
| Generation | Direct photolysis or thermolysis of diazo compounds in the liquid phase | Photolysis in the presence of a triplet sensitizer, or collisional deactivation in the gas phase |

A triplet carbene first forms one C–C bond with the alkene to give a triplet 1,3-diradical, which can close the ring only after the spin of one electron has flipped (**intersystem crossing**, [6.3.17](/6.3.17)); during this time the single bond can rotate, and the stereospecificity is lost.

### 11.10.2. Generation of Carbenes

- Decomposition of **diazo compounds** and **ketenes**: $\ce{CH2N2 ->[h\nu] :CH2 + N2}$, $\ce{CH2=C=O ->[h\nu] :CH2 + CO}$. **Diazomethane** is toxic and explosive ([6.5.24](/6.5.24#sec-24.5.1)). Diazo esters give metal carbenes under $\ce{Rh2(OAc)4}$ or copper catalysis; these are far more selective than free carbenes and are the main modern method of cyclopropanation.
- α-Elimination: chloroform loses a proton in strong base, and the trichloromethyl anion loses $\ce{Cl-}$ from the same carbon to give dichlorocarbene; a **quaternary ammonium salt** is often added as a **phase-transfer catalyst** in 50% aqueous NaOH ([6.5.23](/6.5.23#sec-23.7.2)). Alkyl halides and butyllithium can also undergo α-elimination, giving lithium carbenoids.

![Chloroform forms singlet dichlorocarbene by α-elimination in base (top), which adds to (Z)-but-2-ene in one step to give only cis-1,1-dichloro-2,3-dimethylcyclopropane (bottom)](./plots/carbene.py)

### 11.10.3. Cyclopropanation and Other Reactions

**Definition 11.10.2 (Cyclopropanation)** The reaction in which a one-carbon unit (a carbene or carbenoid) adds to a C=C double bond to give a cyclopropane is called **cyclopropanation**.

- The Simmons–Smith reaction: $\ce{CH2I2}$ and zinc–copper couple Zn(Cu) (or $\ce{Et2Zn}$, the Furukawa modification) give the carbenoid $\ce{ICH2ZnI}$, which is not a free carbene but transfers $\ce{CH2}$ to the alkene through a concerted "butterfly" transition state; the reaction is stereospecific, has few side reactions and does not require dangerous diazomethane. The hydroxy group of an allylic alcohol coordinates to zinc, so that $\ce{CH2}$ adds on the same face as the hydroxy group (directing effect).
- Addition to alkynes gives cyclopropenes; addition to benzene gives norcaradiene, which opens to cycloheptatriene (electrocyclization, [6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.3.5)).
- C–H insertion: singlet carbenes insert into C–H bonds, as in the reaction of $\ce{:CCl2}$ with propane; intramolecular C–H insertion of metal carbenes is a useful way to construct rings.

```smiles
C=CCO>>OCC1CC1
```

## 11.11. Addition Polymerization

**Definition 11.11.1 (Addition Polymerization)** A polymerization in which unsaturated monomers are joined into a macromolecule by repeated addition of an active center (radical, cation, anion or metal–carbon bond) to double bonds, without loss of a small molecule, is called **addition polymerization** (also called chain-growth polymerization).

| Mode | Initiator or catalyst | Suitable monomers | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radical polymerization | Peroxides, azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN), light | Most vinyl monomers | Low-density polyethylene, polystyrene, poly(vinyl chloride), poly(methyl methacrylate) |
| Cationic polymerization | Lewis acids such as $\ce{BF3}$/$\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{AlCl3}$ | Alkenes bearing electron-donating groups (isobutene, vinyl ethers) | Polyisobutylene, butyl rubber |
| Anionic polymerization | BuLi, $\ce{NaNH2}$, even water and weak bases | Alkenes bearing electron-withdrawing groups (acrylonitrile, acrylates, α-cyanoacrylates) | Superglue (ethyl α-cyanoacrylate, initiated by traces of moisture in air) |
| Coordination polymerization | Ziegler–Natta catalysts, metallocenes | Ethene, propene | High-density polyethylene, isotactic polypropylene |

The mechanism of radical polymerization is a chain reaction: a radical from the decomposition of the initiator adds to a monomer, the new radical adds to the next monomer (propagation), and chains terminate when two chain radicals couple or disproportionate; the propagation step always forms the more stable radical, so monomers join head to tail ($\ce{-CH2-CHX-CH2-CHX-}$). The requirements of cationic and anionic polymerization are consistent with the electronic effects of Section 11.3.4: a cationic active center needs electron-donating groups to stabilize it, and an anionic one needs electron-withdrawing groups. In the absence of impurities, anionic polymerization has no termination step (Szwarc's "living polymerization"); the chain end remains active throughout, so a second monomer can be added subsequently to prepare block copolymers, and the molar mass distribution of the product is very narrow (dispersity close to 1, [6.3.21](/6.3.21)).

**Definition 11.11.2 (Ziegler–Natta Catalyst)** A catalyst composed of a transition-metal halide (such as $\ce{TiCl4}$) and an alkylaluminum (such as $\ce{AlEt3}$) that can polymerize ethene and propene by coordination at ordinary pressure and low temperature is called a **Ziegler–Natta catalyst**.

The monomer first coordinates to a vacant site on titanium and then inserts into the Ti–C bond (the Cossee mechanism; for **migratory insertion** see [6.5.28](/6.5.28#sec-28.4.4) and [6.5.29](/6.5.29#sec-29.8)). Compared with the high-temperature, high-pressure radical process, it gives high-density polyethylene with very little branching and can control the tacticity of polypropylene: isotactic, with all methyl groups on one side of the main chain; syndiotactic, with them alternating on the two sides; and atactic, with random placement. Isotactic polypropylene is crystalline and heat-resistant and is a commodity plastic, whereas atactic polypropylene is a sticky, soft amorphous material. Ziegler and Natta received the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work, and most of the world's polyolefins are still produced with such catalysts (and their successors, **metallocene** catalysts).

## 11.12. Reduction of Alkynes and Reactions of Terminal Alkynes

### 11.12.1. Reduction of Alkynes

| Method | Product | Stereochemistry |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{H2}$, Pt or Pd/C | Alkane | The alkene intermediate cannot be stopped |
| $\ce{H2}$, Lindlar catalyst | cis Alkene | Two H added to the same face |
| Na or Li, liquid ammonia | trans Alkene | Through a vinyl anion |
| Hydroboration, then protonolysis with AcOH | cis Alkene | B and H added to the same face |

Alkynes adsorb on Pd more strongly than alkenes do. The Lindlar catalyst (Pd/$\ce{CaCO3}$, partially poisoned with lead acetate and quinoline) stops hydrogenation at the alkene stage: the more strongly adsorbed alkyne displaces the alkene that is formed from the surface, and poisoning lowers the activity of the catalyst toward alkenes.

Dissolving-metal reduction: sodium transfers an electron to the π* orbital of the alkyne to give a radical anion; the trans configuration, with the two alkyl groups far apart, is the more stable, and protonation by liquid ammonia gives a vinyl radical, which takes up another electron to become a vinyl anion; a second protonation gives the (E)-alkene. Terminal alkynes cannot be reduced in this way (they are deprotonated by sodium amide to the acetylide).

![Mechanism of reduction of an internal alkyne by sodium in liquid ammonia: two single-electron transfers and two protonations, with the intermediates adopting the configuration in which the two alkyl groups are trans, giving the (E)-alkene](./plots/alkyne-reduction.py)

```smiles
CCCC#CCCC>>CCC/C=C\CCC Lindlar
CCCC#CCCC>>CCC/C=C/CCC Na/NH3
```

Propargylic alcohols can also be reduced by $\ce{LiAlH4}$ to (E)-allylic alcohols: hydride is delivered to the triple bond anti, by intramolecular direction through the aluminum alkoxide.

### 11.12.2. Acidity of Terminal Alkynes

The C–H bond of a terminal alkyne $\ce{RC#C-H}$ is weakly acidic, far more so than the C–H bonds of alkenes and alkanes: the order of acidity is $\ce{CH3CH3} < \ce{CH2=CH2} < \ce{NH3} < \ce{HC#CH} <$ alcohols, water. The reason is that the lone pair of the acetylide anion occupies an sp orbital with 50% s character, which is close to the nucleus and low in energy (Section 11.1.1). For numerical values see the unified table in [6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.1.4): the $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ of ethyne is about 25 (an estimate on the aqueous scale), of ethene about 44 and of ethane about 50; in DMSO, phenylacetylene is 28.8 and $\ce{NH3}$ is 41 (Bordwell). Consequently:

- The strong bases $\ce{NaNH2}$, $n$-BuLi, NaH and Grignard reagents deprotonate terminal alkynes completely;
- Aqueous NaOH and sodium alkoxides cannot shift the equilibrium appreciably toward the acetylide: water and alcohols are much more acidic than terminal alkynes;
- Terminal alkynes form white silver acetylide precipitates with $\ce{Ag(NH3)2+}$, and ethyne forms red-brown copper(I) acetylide with $\ce{Cu(NH3)2+}$, which allows terminal alkynes to be identified and distinguished from internal alkynes. Dry heavy-metal acetylides explode on heating or impact and must be destroyed with dilute nitric acid after use.

### 11.12.3. Acetylides as Nucleophiles

Acetylide anions are strong nucleophiles (and strong bases) and form new C–C bonds with primary alkyl halides by the **SN2** reaction ([6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.2.2)); this is a common way to lengthen a carbon chain. With secondary and tertiary alkyl halides, **E2** elimination mainly occurs ([6.5.9](/6.5.9#sec-9.6)). Ethyne can be alkylated twice in succession. Acetylide anions also add to the carbonyl group of aldehydes and ketones to give propargylic alcohols ([6.5.16](/6.5.16#sec-16.7.2)).

```smiles
CCCC#C.CI>>CCCC#CC
```

### 11.12.4. Isomerization of Alkynes

In strong base a triple bond can migrate along the carbon chain, and the direction depends on the strength of the base:

- Strong base ($\ce{NaNH2}$, $150\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ in mineral oil): an internal alkyne moves to the terminal position. The mechanism is a series of propargylic deprotonations and reprotonations, with stepwise migration through allene intermediates; as soon as the triple bond reaches the chain end, the terminal alkyne is immediately deprotonated by $\ce{NaNH2}$ to the sodium acetylide, a step that is effectively irreversible and pulls the equilibrium toward the terminal alkyne, which is obtained after hydrolysis. Potassium 3-aminopropylamide (KAPA) can move a triple bond from the middle of a long chain all the way to the end at room temperature in a "zipper" fashion (the alkyne zipper reaction).
- Weak base (KOH/ethanol, heating): a terminal alkyne moves to an internal position. A weak base cannot deprotonate the terminal alkyne completely, the isomerization is reversible, and the product ratio is determined thermodynamically, with the more substituted internal alkyne being more stable (a disubstituted triple bond has greater hyperconjugative stabilization).

### 11.12.5. Nucleophilic Addition

Alkynes accept nucleophiles more readily than alkenes do: the lone pair of the vinyl anion formed after addition is in an $\mathrm{sp^2}$ orbital (greater s character) and is more stable than the alkyl anion formed by addition to an alkene. Ethyne adds alcohols under KOH catalysis to give vinyl ethers (the Reppe process), adds carboxylic acids in the presence of a catalyst to give vinyl esters (vinyl acetate, the precursor of poly(vinyl alcohol)), and adds HCN to give acrylonitrile. The nucleophile attacks the less hindered carbon, and addition is generally anti. Activated alkynes bearing carbonyl or ester groups (propiolates, ynones) are readily attacked by thiols and amines (Michael type, [6.5.17](/6.5.17#sec-17.6.3)), and the thiol–yne addition is used to conjugate biomolecules.

### 11.12.6. Oligomerization and Polymerization of Alkynes

Ethyne trimerizes to benzene under Ni or Co complex catalysis ([2+2+2] cycloaddition, [6.5.29](/6.5.29#sec-29.6.2)), tetramerizes to cyclooctatetraene under $\ce{Ni(CN)2}$ catalysis (Reppe), and dimerizes to vinylacetylene with $\ce{CuCl}$–$\ce{NH4Cl}$. Ethyne polymerizes to polyacetylene under $\ce{Ti(OBu)4}$–$\ce{AlEt3}$ catalysis: in Shirakawa's laboratory a catalyst concentration a thousand times too high was accidentally used, and a silvery film of trans-polyacetylene was obtained; in collaboration with MacDiarmid and Heeger he found that doping with iodine raises its conductivity by about seven orders of magnitude, to the metallic range. The three shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for "the discovery and development of conductive polymers".

## 11.13. Preparation of Alkenes and Alkynes

## Summary

The three mechanisms of electrophilic addition (Definition 11.3.1):

| Intermediate | Reagents | Regioselectivity | Stereochemistry | Rearrangement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carbocation | HX, $\ce{H3O+}$ | Markovnikov (Definition 11.3.2) | None | Yes |
| Onium ion (Definition 11.4.1) | $\ce{X2}$, $\ce{X2/H2O}$, $\ce{Hg(OAc)2}$, $\ce{PhSeCl}$ | Nucleophile attacks the more substituted carbon | Anti | No |
| Concerted, four-center | $\ce{BH3}$ (Definition 11.4.2) | B on the less substituted carbon | Syn | No |

Overview of the reactions of alkenes:

| Reaction | Reagents | Product | Regiochemistry | Stereochemistry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Addition of HX | HX | Alkyl halide | Markovnikov | None |
| Addition of HBr (peroxides) | HBr, ROOR | 1-Bromoalkane | Anti-Markovnikov | None |
| Acid-catalyzed hydration | $\ce{H3O+}$ | Alcohol | Markovnikov | None (rearrangement possible) |
| Oxymercuration–demercuration | $\ce{Hg(OAc)2}$, $\ce{H2O}$; $\ce{NaBH4}$ | Alcohol | Markovnikov | No rearrangement |
| Hydroboration–oxidation | $\ce{BH3}$; $\ce{H2O2}$, $\ce{OH-}$ | Alcohol | Anti-Markovnikov | Syn |
| Halogenation | $\ce{Br2}$ | Vicinal dihalide | — | Anti |
| Halohydrin formation | $\ce{Br2/H2O}$ or NBS/$\ce{H2O}$ | β-Halo alcohol | OH on the more substituted carbon | Anti |
| Epoxidation | mCPBA | Epoxide | — | Syn (retention) |
| Dihydroxylation | $\ce{OsO4}$ (cat.)/NMO, cold $\ce{KMnO4}$ | syn Diol | — | Syn |
| Ozonolysis | $\ce{O3}$; $\ce{Me2S}$ (Definition 11.8.1) | Two carbonyl compounds | — | — |
| Catalytic hydrogenation | $\ce{H2}$, Pd/C | Alkane | — | Syn |
| Cyclopropanation | $\ce{CH2I2}$/Zn(Cu), $\ce{:CCl2}$ (Definitions 11.10.1, 11.10.2) | Cyclopropane | — | Syn (retention) |

Alkynes: electrophilic addition is slower than for alkenes (the vinyl cation is unstable); hydration ($\ce{Hg^2+}$) gives methyl ketones and hydroboration–oxidation gives aldehydes; Lindlar hydrogenation gives cis alkenes and Na/$\ce{NH3}$ gives trans alkenes; the C–H bond of a terminal alkyne is acidic (sp carbon), and acetylides lengthen the carbon chain by SN2 reaction with primary alkyl halides; strong bases move the triple bond to the chain end and weak bases move it toward the middle of the chain; alkynes undergo nucleophilic addition more readily than alkenes.

Conjugated dienes: 1,2-addition and 1,4-addition (Definition 11.6.1) proceed through the same allyl cation; at low temperature kinetic control gives mainly the 1,2-product, and at higher temperature thermodynamic control gives mainly the more stable 1,4-product.

A unified explanation of selectivity: the more stable the intermediate (cation, radical), the lower the transition state leading to it (Hammond postulate); the stereospecificity of concerted reactions arises from simultaneous formation of new bonds on the same face. Addition polymerization (Definition 11.11.1) is the repetition of the same kind of addition, and Ziegler–Natta catalysts (Definition 11.11.2) control the tacticity of the polymer.

## Questions

1. The C–H bond lengths of ethene and ethyne are $108.7$ and $106.1\,\mathrm{pm}$, respectively. Explain the difference and relate it to the acidity of terminal alkynes.

   **Answer** The carbon of ethene bonds to H through an $\mathrm{sp^2}$ orbital (33% s character) and that of ethyne through an sp orbital (50%). An s orbital lies closer to the nucleus than a p orbital, so the greater the s character, the smaller the hybrid orbital and the closer it is to the carbon nucleus, the shorter the C–H bond, and the more electronegative the carbon appears. For the same reason, the lone pair of the acetylide anion occupies an sp orbital with 50% s character, close to the nucleus and low in energy, so the acetylide anion is relatively stable and the C–H bond of a terminal alkyne is much more acidic than those of alkenes and alkanes.

2. In a cyclooctene ring, C1 bears a methanesulfonyloxy group (OMs), C5=C6 is a double bond, and C6 bears a methyl group. Solvolysis in an aqueous solvent gives a tertiary alcohol with a bicyclo[3.3.0]octane skeleton (OH and methyl on the same carbon). Write the mechanism, and explain why the cation at C1 bonds to C5 rather than to C6.

   **Answer** Loss of OMs gives a secondary carbocation at C1 (SN1-type ionization). The C5=C6 bond lies in a transannular position relative to it, and the π electrons attack the cation to form a transannular C–C bond. If the C1–C5 bond forms, the two new rings are C1–C2–C3–C4–C5 and C1–C5–C6–C7–C8, two fused five-membered rings that are almost strain-free, and the positive charge remains on C6, which bears the methyl group, as a tertiary carbocation. If the C1–C6 bond forms, a bicyclo[4.2.0]octane skeleton with a six-membered and a four-membered ring results, and the positive charge remains on C5 as only a secondary carbocation. Both factors (the stability of the cation, i.e., the Markovnikov principle, and ring strain) favor C1–C5 bond formation. Finally, water attacks the tertiary carbocation at C6 and a proton is lost to give the tertiary alcohol.

3. Pent-4-en-1-ol is treated successively with $\ce{Hg(OAc)2}$ (THF–water) and $\ce{NaBH4}$/NaOH to give a cyclic ether. Write each step and state whether the product has a five- or six-membered ring.

   **Answer** (1) $\ce{AcOHg+}$ forms a mercurinium ion with the terminal double bond. (2) The intramolecular hydroxy oxygen attacks the more substituted C4 from the back side of the mercury bridge (Markovnikov orientation; although water is present in large amount, the intramolecular hydroxy group is suitably positioned and reacts much faster), forming a five-membered ring, and loss of a proton gives 2-[(acetoxymercurio)methyl]oxolane. (3) $\ce{NaBH4}$ reduces the C–HgOAc bond to C–H, giving 2-methyloxolane. Attack at C5 would give a six-membered ring (oxane), but that would be attack at the less substituted carbon, which carries less positive charge, and is unfavorable. This is an intramolecular alkoxymercuration: as when an alcohol replaces water, an ether is obtained, and no rearrangement occurs.

4. Write the major product of the reaction of 1-methylcyclohexene with each of the following reagents (state the regiochemistry and stereochemistry): (a) HBr; (b) HBr, peroxides; (c) $\ce{BH3*THF}$, then $\ce{H2O2}$/NaOH; (d) $\ce{Br2}$/$\ce{H2O}$; (e) mCPBA; (f) $\ce{OsO4}$ (cat.)/NMO; (g) $\ce{O3}$, then $\ce{Me2S}$.

   **Answer** (a) 1-Bromo-1-methylcyclohexane (Markovnikov; no new stereogenic center). (b) 1-Bromo-2-methylcyclohexane (anti-Markovnikov), as both cis and trans isomers (radical intermediate). (c) trans-2-Methylcyclohexan-1-ol (racemic; H and OH add syn, OH is on the less substituted carbon and trans to the methyl group). (d) trans-2-Bromo-1-methylcyclohexan-1-ol (racemic; OH on the tertiary carbon, Br and OH trans). (e) 1-Methyl-7-oxabicyclo[4.1.0]heptane (1,2-epoxy-1-methylcyclohexane, racemic). (f) cis-1-Methylcyclohexane-1,2-diol (racemic). (g) 6-Oxoheptanal, $\ce{CH3CO(CH2)4CHO}$.

5. Carry out the following conversions (several steps are allowed): (a) but-1-yne → butanal; (b) but-1-yne → butan-2-one; (c) but-2-yne → racemic (2R,3R)/(2S,3S)-2,3-dibromobutane; (d) ethyne → (Z)-hex-3-ene.

   **Answer** (a) Hydroboration with dicyclohexylborane or 9-BBN, then $\ce{H2O2}$/NaOH; the enol tautomerizes to the aldehyde. (b) $\ce{HgSO4}$/$\ce{H2SO4}$/$\ce{H2O}$ (Markovnikov hydration, methyl ketone). (c) Reduce with $\ce{H2}$/Lindlar catalyst to (Z)-but-2-ene, then add $\ce{Br2}$: anti addition to the cis alkene gives the racemic (2R,3R)/(2S,3S) product. If one first reduced with Na/liquid ammonia to (E)-but-2-ene, anti addition would give the meso (2R,3S) product. (d) $\ce{NaNH2}$, $\ce{CH3CH2Br}$; then $\ce{NaNH2}$, $\ce{CH3CH2Br}$, giving hex-3-yne; $\ce{H2}$/Lindlar catalyst then gives (Z)-hex-3-ene.

6. Which product predominates when buta-1,3-diene reacts with 1 equivalent of $\ce{Br2}$ at low temperature and at a higher temperature? Explain, and predict on which carbon the proton adds when penta-1,3-diene adds 1 equivalent of HCl.

   **Answer** At low temperature the 1,2-addition product 3,4-dibromobut-1-ene predominates (kinetic control: the bridged bromonium ion or allylic-type cation is attacked by bromide faster at C2); at a higher temperature the products can interconvert by dissociation and recombination, and the more stable (E)-1,4-dibromobut-2-ene predominates (thermodynamic control, a disubstituted internal alkene). In penta-1,3-diene $\ce{CH2=CH-CH=CH-CH3}$ the proton adds to C1: the resulting cation $\ce{CH3-CH+-CH=CH-CH3}$ is an allyl cation with a secondary carbon at each end, which is more stable than $\ce{CH2=CH-CH+-CH2CH3}$ obtained by protonation at C4 (in whose other resonance structure the positive charge is on a primary carbon).

7. Why do HCl and HI not show the peroxide effect? Explain with bond enthalpies.

   **Answer** The two propagation steps are $\ce{X. + C=C -> X-C-C.}$ and $\ce{C. + H-X -> C-H + X.}$. With mean bond enthalpies (the table in Section 11.7.2), for HCl the second step is endothermic by about $19\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ (the H–Cl bond is stronger than the C–H bond), propagation is slow, the radical chains are very short, and ionic addition predominates; for HI the first step is endothermic by about $51\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ (the C–I bond is too weak), the iodine atom that has added is quickly lost again, and iodine atoms also readily combine with one another and terminate the chain. Only for HBr are both steps exothermic, so the chain propagates rapidly.

8. What products are obtained when singlet $\ce{CH2}$ and triplet $\ce{CH2}$ each react with (Z)-but-2-ene? How does the product change if diazomethane is decomposed in its triplet state with a photosensitizer?

   **Answer** Singlet carbene adds in one concerted step, with both C–C bonds forming simultaneously, and gives only cis-1,2-dimethylcyclopropane (stereospecific). Triplet carbene first forms one C–C bond to give a triplet 1,3-diradical; before intersystem crossing flips the spin and allows ring closure, the central C–C single bond can rotate freely, so a mixture of cis- and trans-1,2-dimethylcyclopropane is obtained (usually with more of the trans isomer, which is thermodynamically more stable). A triplet photosensitizer (such as benzophenone) transfers energy to diazomethane and makes it give the triplet carbene directly, so the proportion of trans cyclopropane in the product increases and stereospecificity is lost.
