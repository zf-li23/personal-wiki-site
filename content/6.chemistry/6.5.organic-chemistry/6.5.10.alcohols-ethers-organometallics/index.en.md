Alcohols and ethers are compounds with C–O single bonds. They occupy the first level of the oxidation states of carbon ([6.5.1](/6.5.1#sec-1.3.2)); they can be obtained downward by substitution of alkyl halides and oxidized upward to aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids, and so they are the hub of organic synthesis. The two preceding chapters discussed substitution and elimination of alcohols and ethers ([6.5.9](/6.5.9#sec-9.7)); this chapter fills in their structure, nomenclature, physical properties and acidity as a class of compounds and describes the preparation of alcohols and ethers. We then turn to the reactions of alkyl halides with metals. Grignard reagents, organolithium reagents and organocopper reagents turn the electron-poor carbon of an alkyl halide into an electron-rich carbon and so convert the carbon atom from an **electrophile** into a **nucleophile**, which makes them the most important tools for constructing C–C bonds. Finally we discuss the oxidation of alcohols, the cleavage of vicinal diols, the pinacol rearrangement and the ring opening of epoxides.

This chapter uses **nucleophilic substitution** ([6.5.8](/6.5.8)), elimination ([6.5.9](/6.5.9)) and the [pKa table](/6.5.3#sec-3.1.4) of [6.5.3](/6.5.3). The addition of Grignard reagents to aldehydes and ketones is treated in detail in [6.5.16](/6.5.16#sec-16.7.3); the structures of organolithium and organomagnesium compounds and more main-group organometallic chemistry are in [6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.2); the mechanisms of the Swern and Dess–Martin oxidations are in [6.5.26](/6.5.26#sec-26.9.2) and [6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.7.3); and the Claisen rearrangement of aryl allyl ethers is in [6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.5.6).

## 10.1. Alcohols, Phenols, Ethers, Thiols and Epoxides

### 10.1.1. Structure and Classification

A compound with a hydroxy group on an $\mathrm{sp^3}$ carbon is an alcohol ($\ce{R-OH}$), one with a hydroxy group on an aromatic ring is a phenol ($\ce{Ar-OH}$), and one with a hydroxy group on a double-bond carbon is an enol (generally unstable, tautomerizing to an aldehyde or ketone, [6.5.21](/6.5.21#sec-21.2)). A compound with two carbon groups on oxygen is an ether ($\ce{R-O-R'}$), and a three-membered cyclic ether is called an epoxide. Replacing oxygen by sulfur gives thiols ($\ce{R-SH}$) and sulfides ($\ce{R-S-R'}$, [6.5.26](/6.5.26)). According to the number of carbon groups on the carbon bearing the hydroxy group, alcohols are classified as primary ($\ce{RCH2OH}$), secondary ($\ce{R2CHOH}$) and tertiary ($\ce{R3COH}$), as for alkyl halides, and this also determines differences in reactivity (substitution, elimination, oxidation).

The oxygen of an alcohol is $\mathrm{sp^3}$ hybridized with two lone pairs; the C–O–H angle is about $108.5^\circ$ (methanol) and the C–O bond length 143 pm; the C–O–C angle of an ether is about $112^\circ$ (dimethyl ether). The O–H and C–O bonds are both polar, and the dipole moment of an alcohol is about $5.7\times10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m}$ (methanol).

The bond angle in the three-membered ring of an epoxide is about $60^\circ$, and the **ring strain** is about $114\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, comparable to cyclopropane ([6.5.4](/6.5.4#sec-4.4.1)). It is an ether but much more reactive than ordinary ethers, because ring opening releases this strain (Section 10.9).

**Definition 10.1.1 (Epoxide)** A cyclic ether in which an oxygen atom and two adjacent carbon atoms form a three-membered ring is called an **epoxide**; the parent compound is oxirane.

### 10.1.2. Nomenclature

The systematic name of an alcohol is based on the longest chain containing the carbon that bears the hydroxy group, with the suffix "-ol", numbered from the end nearer the hydroxy group; when the hydroxy group is the principal characteristic group its locant must be as low as possible ([6.5.1](/6.5.1#sec-1.4.2)), and the locant is placed before "ol". In cyclic alcohols the carbon bearing the hydroxy group is C1. The configuration of stereocenters is given at the very front of the name.

```smiles
CC(O)CC(C)C 4-methylpentan-2-ol
OC[C@H](Cl)Cc1ccccc1 (R)
CC1(C)CC[C@@H](O)C1 (R)
OCC=C prop-2-en-1-ol
```

They are 4-methylpentan-2-ol, (*R*)-2-chloro-3-phenylpropan-1-ol, (*R*)-3,3-dimethylcyclopentan-1-ol and prop-2-en-1-ol (allyl alcohol). Compounds with two hydroxy groups are diols, such as ethane-1,2-diol (ethylene glycol), and propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol).

Phenols are named on the parent phenol, with the substituents cited in alphabetical order and the carbon bearing the hydroxy group as C1: 2-chloro-4-nitrophenol.

Ethers have two systems of nomenclature: the functional class name lists the two hydrocarbon groups in alphabetical order followed by "ether" (ethyl methyl ether, *tert*-butyl methyl ether, diethyl ether), and the substitutive name treats the smaller alkoxy group as a substituent (methoxyethane, 2-methoxy-2-methylpropane, 1-ethoxypentane); the substitutive name is used for complex ethers. Cyclic ethers have heterocyclic names: oxirane, oxetane, tetrahydrofuran (THF, oxolane), tetrahydropyran (oxane) and 1,4-dioxane. Substituted epoxides are numbered as oxiranes (O is 1): 2-methyloxirane (propylene oxide), 2,2-dimethyloxirane; names of the type "alkene + oxide" (cyclohexene oxide) or the prefix "epoxy" (1,2-epoxypropane) are also common.

Thiols take the suffix "-thiol": ethanethiol, propane-1-thiol.

### 10.1.3. Hydrogen Bonding and Physical Properties

Alcohol molecules associate through O–H···O **hydrogen bonds** (hydrogen-bond energy about $20\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$), and their boiling points are much higher than those of alkanes, alkyl halides and ethers of similar molar mass:

| Compound | Molar mass / $(\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}})$ | Boiling point / $^\circ\mathrm{C}$ | Solubility in water / $(\mathrm{g}/100\,\mathrm{g}$ water, $20$–$25\,^\circ\mathrm{C})$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Propane | 44 | $-42$ | Very small |
| Dimethyl ether | 46 | $-24$ | About 7 (gas) |
| Ethanol | 46 | 78 | Miscible |
| Chloroethane | 64 | 12 | 0.6 |
| Ethanethiol | 62 | 35 | 0.7 |
| Diethyl ether | 74 | 35 | 6.9 |
| Butan-1-ol | 74 | 118 | 7.4 |
| Pentan-1-ol | 88 | 138 | 2.2 |
| Hexan-1-ol | 102 | 157 | 0.59 |
| Octan-1-ol | 130 | 195 | 0.05 |
| Phenol | 94 | 182 | 8.3 |
| Water | 18 | 100 | — |

Ethers can only be hydrogen-bond acceptors and not donors, so there are no hydrogen bonds between ether molecules and the boiling points are close to those of the corresponding alkanes (diethyl ether $35\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, comparable to pentane at $36\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$); but they can form hydrogen bonds with water, and their solubility is comparable to that of alcohols of the same molar mass (diethyl ether and butan-1-ol both dissolve to about 7 g/100 g water). Sulfur is less electronegative, the S–H bond forms hydrogen bonds hardly at all, and the boiling points of thiols are much lower than those of the corresponding alcohols.

The solubility of an alcohol in water depends on the ratio of the hydrophilic hydroxy group to the hydrophobic hydrocarbon group: methanol, ethanol and propanol are miscible with water, butanol dissolves to about 7 g per 100 g of water, the solubility falls rapidly from pentanol on, and hexanol and higher alcohols are essentially insoluble. The octanol–water partition coefficient $\lg P$ is the standard measure of lipophilicity in medicinal chemistry. Phenol dissolves to about 8 g/100 g water at room temperature and is miscible with water above $66\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Polyhydric alcohols (ethylene glycol, glycerol, sugars) are very soluble in water because of their many hydroxy groups.

### 10.1.4. Acidity and Basicity

Alcohols are very weak acids. $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ (water, 25 °C, [6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.1.4)):

| Compound | $\ce{H2O}$ | $\ce{CH3OH}$ | $\ce{CH3CH2OH}$ | $\ce{(CH3)2CHOH}$ | $\ce{(CH3)3COH}$ | $\ce{CF3CH2OH}$ | $\ce{C6H5OH}$ | 4-Nitrophenol | $\ce{CH3CH2SH}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ | 14.00 | 15.5 | 15.9 | about 17 | about 18 | 12.4 | 9.99 | 7.15 | 10.6 |

In aqueous solution the acidity of alcohols decreases as the alkyl group gets larger, mainly because a large alkoxide ion is difficult to solvate; in the gas phase the order is reversed ([6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.3)). Electron-withdrawing groups stabilize the alkoxide through the **inductive effect** and increase the acidity (trifluoroethanol is about 3.5 pK units stronger than ethanol). Phenols are about $10^6$ times more acidic than alcohols: the negative charge of the phenoxide ion is delocalized onto the ortho and para positions of the ring by the **conjugation effect**, and a para nitro group delocalizes it further onto the nitro oxygens. A phenol can therefore be converted completely into a phenoxide by aqueous NaOH, whereas an alcohol cannot; a phenol does not react with $\ce{NaHCO3}$, whereas a carboxylic acid does (Example 3.1.1 of [6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.1.2)). Thiols are about $10^5$ times more acidic than alcohols (the S–H bond is weak, and the sulfur anion is large with dispersed charge), and the thiolate ion is an excellent nucleophile ([6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.7.1)).

To obtain an alkoxide quantitatively one needs a base stronger than the alkoxide, or an active metal:

$$
\ce{2 ROH + 2 Na -> 2 RO- Na+ + H2 ^}, \qquad \ce{ROH + NaH -> RO- Na+ + H2 ^}.
$$
The reaction of sodium with alcohols is gentler than with water and is often used to destroy residual sodium metal. NaH releases hydrogen, making the reaction irreversible, and is the most commonly used method for preparing alkoxides (the $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ of H₂ is about 35). Alcohols are also weak bases: in strong acid they are protonated to alkyloxonium ions $\ce{ROH2+}$ ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ about $-2$), the first step of substitution and elimination of alcohols in acid ([6.5.9](/6.5.9#sec-9.7)). Ethers are likewise protonated by strong acids ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ about $-3.6$) and coordinate through their lone pairs to Lewis acids; for example, $\ce{BF3.OEt2}$ is a common liquid source of $\ce{BF3}$. Coordination of ethers to $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{Li+}$ is the reason why Grignard reagents form and are stable in ethers (Section 10.3).

Crown ethers are macrocyclic polyethers with $\ce{-CH2CH2O-}$ as the repeating unit. The cavity of 18-crown-6 exactly accommodates $\ce{K+}$ (binding constant in methanol about $10^6\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}$); it dissolves salts such as KF and $\ce{KMnO4}$ in nonpolar solvents such as benzene and leaves the anions "naked" and much more nucleophilic ([6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.7.3)). Pedersen received the 1987 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for crown ethers, and Cram and Lehn for host–guest chemistry.

### 10.2.2. Preparation of Ethers: The Williamson Synthesis

**Definition 10.2.1 (Williamson Ether Synthesis)** The method of making ethers by the SN2 reaction of an alkoxide (or phenoxide) anion with a primary alkyl halide (or a sulfonate or dialkyl sulfate) is called the **Williamson ether synthesis** (1850):

$$
\ce{R-O- Na+ + R'-CH2-X -> R-O-CH2R' + NaX}.
$$

It is the most general method for preparing mixed (unsymmetrical) ethers. Because an alkoxide is a strong base, the alkyl halide must be a methyl or primary halide: secondary halides undergo much **E2**, and tertiary halides almost exclusively E2 ([6.5.9](/6.5.9#sec-9.6)). In designing a synthesis, therefore, put the more hindered part on the alkoxide side: *tert*-butyl methyl ether is made from potassium *tert*-butoxide and iodomethane (or dimethyl sulfate), and not from sodium methoxide and *tert*-butyl bromide.

```smiles
CC(C)(C)[O-].CI>>CC(C)(C)OC good
C[O-].CC(C)(C)Br>>C=C(C)C E2 only
```

![Planning a Williamson synthesis of *tert*-butyl methyl ether: top, *tert*-butoxide attacks the unhindered carbon of iodomethane from the back side (SN2) and iodide leaves, giving the ether; bottom, with the roles reversed methoxide cannot reach the tertiary carbon of 2-bromo-2-methylpropane and acts as a base on a β-hydrogen instead (E2), giving 2-methylpropene](./plots/williamson.py)

Aryl ethers are made from a phenoxide and an alkyl halide (anisole from sodium phenoxide and dimethyl sulfate or iodomethane); aryl halides cannot serve as the electrophile ([6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.5.3)). An intramolecular Williamson reaction gives cyclic ethers: halo alcohols close to rings in base, three-membered rings (epoxides) and five- and six-membered rings fastest. This synthesis of epoxides is stereospecific: the alkoxide must attack from the back of the halogen, so *trans*-2-chlorocyclohexanol closes rapidly to cyclohexene oxide in NaOH, whereas the cis isomer cannot close (the hydroxy group and the chlorine are on the same side). Epoxides made from an alkene through a halohydrin followed by ring closure ([6.5.11](/6.5.11#sec-11.4.3)) are the same as those obtained by direct epoxidation with a peroxy acid ([6.5.11](/6.5.11#sec-11.8.1)).

![Intramolecular Williamson reaction: in aqueous NaOH, hydroxide first removes the hydroxy proton of *trans*-2-chlorocyclohexanol; the alkoxide, on the face opposite to chlorine, attacks the neighboring carbon from the back side and chloride leaves (SN2), closing the ring to cyclohexene oxide. In the *cis* isomer the alkoxide and chlorine are on the same face and backside attack is impossible](./plots/halohydrin-epoxide.py)

Intermolecular dehydration of primary alcohols in concentrated sulfuric acid is suitable only for symmetrical ethers ([6.5.9](/6.5.9#sec-9.8.2)); *tert*-butyl ethers can be obtained by acid-catalyzed addition of alcohols to 2-methylpropene (industrially, methyl *tert*-butyl ether, MTBE).

Ethers undergo slow **autoxidation** in air and light ([6.5.7](/6.5.7#sec-7.3.3)), forming hydroperoxides and polymeric peroxides at the $\alpha$-C–H, which can explode on distillation and concentration. Diethyl ether, THF and diisopropyl ether must be tested for peroxides before use (KI–starch paper) and stored with a stabilizer (BHT); diethyl ether also has a low boiling point and a low flash point ($-45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$), and its vapor is heavier than air and spreads along the bench to a source of ignition, making it one of the commonest causes of fires in the laboratory.

## 10.3. Organometallic Compounds and Grignard Reagents

### 10.3.1. Polarity of the C–M Bond

A compound in which carbon is bonded directly to a metal is called an **organometallic compound** (systematic treatment in [6.5.28](/6.5.28#sec-28.1)). The properties of the C–M bond change with the electronegativity of the metal: the Pauling electronegativity of carbon is 2.55, and those of Li, Na, K, Mg, Zn, Cu, Sn and B are 0.98, 0.93, 0.82, 1.31, 1.65, 1.90, 1.96 and 2.04.

| Metal | Example | C–M bond | Reactivity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group 1 (Na, K) | $\ce{C2H5Na}$ | Strongly ionic | Extremely strong bases, hard to control |
| Group 1 (Li) | $\ce{CH3Li}$, $n$-BuLi | Strongly polar covalent bond, aggregated in solution ([6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.2.2)) | Strong bases, strong nucleophiles |
| Group 2 (Mg) | $\ce{CH3MgBr}$ | Polar covalent bond | Strong nucleophiles, strong bases |
| Groups 11 and 12 (Cu, Zn, Cd) | $\ce{(CH3)2CuLi}$, $\ce{Et2Zn}$ | More covalent | Milder, more selective |
| Transition metals | Complexes of Pd, Ni, Rh | Covalent bond, may have $\pi$ coordination | Intermediates in catalytic cycles ([6.5.28](/6.5.28#sec-28.4), [6.5.29](/6.5.29#sec-29.1)) |

The electrons of the C–M bond are polarized toward carbon, which carries a partial negative charge: the electrophilic carbon of an alkyl halide $\ce{R^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-}}$ becomes a nucleophilic carbon in $\ce{R^{\delta-}-M^{\delta+}}$. This reversal of the reactivity polarity of carbon is why organometallic reagents are so important in synthesis: it allows an alkyl halide and a carbonyl compound, both originally electrophiles, to be joined.

### 10.3.2. Preparation of Grignard Reagents

**Definition 10.3.1 (Grignard Reagent)** An organomagnesium halide $\ce{RMgX}$ formed by the reaction of an alkyl halide with magnesium metal in an anhydrous ether solvent (diethyl ether, THF) is called a **Grignard reagent**:

$$
\ce{R-X + Mg ->[\text{dry } Et2O] R-MgX}.
$$

Grignard discovered this reaction in 1900 and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1912 for it. R may be primary, secondary or tertiary alkyl, vinyl or aryl; the reactivity order is RI > RBr > RCl (RF generally does not react). Vinyl and aryl chlorides require THF, which has a higher boiling point ($66\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) and stronger coordinating ability (the lone pair on oxygen is less hindered). The oxide film on the magnesium surface hinders the reaction, and a little $\ce{I2}$ or 1,2-dibromoethane is often added to "initiate" it (the latter reacts with Mg to give ethene and $\ce{MgBr2}$, exposing a fresh surface). The reaction proceeds on the magnesium surface by **single electron transfer**, with alkyl radicals as intermediates ([6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.3.1)): when the Grignard reagent is made from 6-bromohex-1-ene, partly cyclized cyclopentylmethylmagnesium is obtained, the result of radical cyclization.

![Grignard reagent formation goes through radicals: top, an electron from the magnesium surface enters the C–Br σ* orbital, the C–Br bond breaks to an alkyl radical and ·MgBr, and the two combine to the Grignard reagent; bottom, the hex-5-enyl radical from 6-bromohex-1-ene can cyclize (5-exo) before it is captured, so part of the reagent is cyclopentylmethylmagnesium bromide, evidence for the radical intermediate](./plots/grignard-formation.py)

The ether is more than a solvent. Two molecules of ether coordinate to magnesium through the lone pairs on oxygen, making the magnesium in $\ce{RMgX(OEt2)2}$ four-coordinate and stabilizing the reagent; without ether, Grignard reagents form with difficulty. In ether solution Grignard reagents are in a Schlenk equilibrium:

**Definition 10.3.2 (Schlenk Equilibrium)** The equilibrium in ether solution among the organomagnesium halide, the dialkylmagnesium and the magnesium halide,

$$
\ce{2 RMgX <=> R2Mg + MgX2}
$$
is called the **Schlenk equilibrium**.

The position of the equilibrium depends on R, X, the solvent and the concentration; in diethyl ether alkylmagnesium bromides exist mainly as $\ce{RMgX}$. Adding 1,4-dioxane precipitates $\ce{MgX2(dioxane)}$ and pushes the equilibrium toward $\ce{R2Mg}$. By convention the reagent in solution is still written RMgX.

The main side reaction in the preparation is coupling of RMgX formed with unreacted RX (Wurtz-type coupling, Section 10.4), which is especially serious with reactive allyl and benzyl halides:

$$
\ce{R-MgX + R-X -> R-R + MgX2}.
$$
In practice, therefore, the ether solution of the alkyl halide is added slowly dropwise to an excess of magnesium turnings in ether, so that the concentration of RX in the system is always low, rather than adding magnesium to the alkyl halide.

### 10.3.3. Reactions of Grignard Reagents

The carbon of a Grignard reagent is both a strong nucleophile and a strong base.

As a base: reaction with acidic hydrogens. The $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ of alkanes is about 50, much higher than that of almost all compounds containing O–H, N–H, S–H and terminal alkyne C–H, so a Grignard reagent is protonated at once on meeting them to give an alkane ("a strong base gives a weak base", that is, the reaction goes in the direction of the weaker acid):

$$
\ce{R-MgX + H-OH -> R-H + HOMgX}, \quad \ce{R-MgX + R'OH -> R-H + R'OMgX}, \quad \ce{R-MgX + R'C#CH -> R-H + R'C#CMgX}.
$$

![The Grignard reagent as a strong base: top, the C–Mg electron pair takes a proton from water and the O–H electron pair stays on oxygen, giving methane and HOMgBr; bottom, a terminal alkyne (pKa about 25) is deprotonated in the same way by ethylmagnesium bromide, giving ethane and an alkynyl Grignard reagent](./plots/grignard-protonolysis.py)

Relevant $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ values ([6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.1.4)): ethane about 50, ethene about 44, $\ce{NH3}$ about 38 (41 in DMSO), acetylene about 25, ethanol 15.9, water 14.00. Three corollaries follow:

1. Grignard reagents must be prepared and used under strictly anhydrous, alcohol-free conditions, with dry glassware and under $\ce{N2}$ or Ar (the ether vapor at reflux also provides some protection).
2. A molecule cannot contain both an acidic hydrogen and a halogen that can form a Grignard reagent. A Grignard reagent cannot be made directly from 4-bromobutan-1-ol, for example, because the hydroxy group would protonate it; the hydroxy group must first be protected as a **silyl ether** (trimethylsilyl ether, *tert*-butyldimethylsilyl ether, [6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.5.3)) or a tetrahydropyranyl ether, and the protecting group removed after the Grignard reaction (Question 1).
3. The reaction can be put to use: quenching with $\ce{D2O}$ gives a selectively deuterated hydrocarbon $\ce{R-D}$; reaction of a sample with $\ce{CH3MgI}$ and measurement of the volume of methane released determines the number of active hydrogens in a molecule (the Zerewitinoff method); and exchange of a terminal alkyne with $\ce{C2H5MgBr}$ gives an alkynyl Grignard reagent.

As a nucleophile: addition to carbonyl compounds, $\ce{CO2}$ and epoxides. The electron pair of the C–Mg bond attacks an electron-poor carbon and forms a new C–C bond:

![Nucleophilic addition of Grignard reagents: the electrons of the C–Mg bond of methylmagnesium bromide attack the carbonyl carbon of acetone, the π electrons move to oxygen to give a magnesium alkoxide, and acidification gives 2-methylpropan-2-ol; addition to carbon dioxide gives a carboxylate, and acidification gives the carboxylic acid with one more carbon](./plots/grignard-addition.py)

| Electrophile | Product (after acidic hydrolysis) | See |
|---|---|---|
| Formaldehyde | Primary alcohol, one more carbon | [6.5.16](/6.5.16#sec-16.7.3) |
| Other aldehydes | Secondary alcohol | [6.5.16](/6.5.16#sec-16.7.3) |
| Ketones | Tertiary alcohol | [6.5.16](/6.5.16#sec-16.7.3) |
| Esters, acyl chlorides | Tertiary alcohol (two additions) | [6.5.19](/6.5.19) |
| $\ce{CO2}$ (gas or dry ice) | Carboxylic acid, one more carbon | [6.5.18](/6.5.18) |
| Oxirane | Primary alcohol, two more carbons | Section 10.9 |
| Nitriles | Ketone | [6.5.20](/6.5.20) |
| $\ce{O2}$ | Alcohol (through the peroxide ROOMgX) | — |

![A Grignard reagent adds twice to an ester: methylmagnesium bromide adds to the carbonyl carbon of methyl acetate, and the tetrahedral alkoxide collapses, re-forming C=O and expelling methoxide, to give acetone; the ketone is more reactive than the ester and immediately adds a second equivalent of the reagent, and acid work-up gives the tertiary alcohol 2-methylpropan-2-ol](./plots/grignard-ester.py)

![Grignard reagents and nitriles give ketones: the C–Mg electron pair adds to the nitrile carbon of benzonitrile and the C≡N π electrons move onto nitrogen, giving the magnesium salt of an imine; it reacts no further, and acidic hydrolysis (via the iminium ion) gives acetophenone](./plots/grignard-nitrile.py)

The reactions of Grignard reagents with $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ are side reactions in preparation and storage (the reagent should be made fresh and used under inert gas), whereas in synthesis they can be exploited: $\ce{RMgX + CO2}$ is the standard method for preparing a carboxylic acid with one more carbon from an alkyl halide. Coupling of Grignard reagents with reactive alkyl halides (allyl and benzyl halides) can also be used to extend carbon chains, but coupling with ordinary alkyl halides is slow and accompanied by elimination and halogen–magnesium exchange; such couplings are now carried out with copper salts (Section 10.4) or with nickel or palladium catalysis (Kumada coupling, [6.5.29](/6.5.29#sec-29.4.4)).

## 10.4. Organolithium and Organocopper Reagents and the Wurtz Reaction

### 10.4.1. Organolithium Reagents

**Definition 10.4.1 (Organolithium Reagent)** A compound RLi containing a C–Li bond is called an **organolithium reagent**. Alkyl halides react with lithium metal in hydrocarbon or ether solvents to give organolithium compounds:

$$
\ce{R-X + 2 Li -> R-Li + LiX}.
$$

Organolithiums undergo the same types of reaction as Grignard reagents but are more reactive and more strongly basic; they add to hindered ketones and can also remove protons from many weak C–H acids (metalation). *n*-Butyllithium ($n$-BuLi), *sec*-butyllithium and *tert*-butyllithium are sold as solutions in hydrocarbons and are the most commonly used strong bases in the laboratory; **LDA**, formed from them and diisopropylamine, is the standard base for generating **enolates** ([6.5.21](/6.5.21#sec-21.3.2)). The reaction of $n$-BuLi or $t$-BuLi with aryl or vinyl bromides or iodides at low temperature exchanges bromine (iodine) for lithium rapidly (**lithium–halogen exchange**), a common method of making aryllithiums; the aggregation of organolithiums, **directed ortho metalation** and related topics are in [6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.2).

Alkyllithiums react violently and exothermically with oxygen and water in the air, *tert*-butyllithium ignites spontaneously in air, and the solvents are flammable hydrocarbons; they must be transferred under inert gas with dry syringes or double-tipped needles, with dry sand ready for putting out fires.

### 10.4.2. Organocopper Reagents

**Definition 10.4.2 (Gilman Reagent)** The lithium diorganocuprate $\ce{R2CuLi}$ formed by the reaction of two molecules of organolithium with one molecule of a copper(I) halide (usually CuI) in ether at low temperature is called a **Gilman reagent**:

$$
\ce{2 R-Li + CuI ->[Et2O][-78\,^\circ C] R2CuLi + LiI}.
$$

Gilman reagents are softer and milder carbon nucleophiles. They couple with primary alkyl halides, vinyl halides and aryl halides to give cross-coupled hydrocarbons (the Corey–House reaction), which Grignard reagents and organolithiums cannot do:

$$
\ce{R2CuLi + R'-X -> R-R' + RCu + LiX}.
$$
Coupling of vinyl halides retains the geometry of the double bond. Gilman reagents add slowly to aldehydes and ketones, but they react with **acyl chlorides** to give ketones (without further addition, [6.5.19](/6.5.19#sec-19.8)) and undergo **1,4-addition** (**conjugate addition**, [6.5.17](/6.5.17#sec-17.6.4)) to $\alpha,\beta$-unsaturated ketones. For example:

```smiles
CCCCBr.C[Cu-]C.[Li+]>>CCCCC
Br/C=C/CCCC.CC[Cu-]CC.[Li+]>>CC/C=C/CCCC retention
```

![Corey–House coupling of lithium dimethylcuprate with 1-bromobutane: the electron-rich copper(I) center attacks the carbon from the back side (an SN2-like oxidative addition), bromide leaves, and a copper(III) intermediate bearing three alkyl groups forms; reductive elimination joins the methyl and butyl groups, giving pentane and methylcopper](./plots/gilman-coupling.py)

### 10.4.3. The Wurtz Reaction

An alkyl halide treated with sodium metal gives an alkylsodium that couples at once with another molecule of alkyl halide to give an alkane with twice the number of carbon atoms:

$$
\ce{2 R-X + 2 Na -> R-R + 2 NaX}.
$$
This is the Wurtz reaction (1855). It is suitable only for making symmetrical alkanes from a single primary alkyl halide: two different alkyl halides give a mixture of three coupling products, and secondary and tertiary halides mainly eliminate. It is now of mainly historical interest, with a few intramolecular applications (ring closure of 1,3-dibromopropane with zinc or sodium to give cyclopropane); cross-couplings are carried out with Gilman reagents or transition-metal catalysis.

![Wurtz coupling of 1-bromopropane with sodium: sodium transfers one electron to the C–Br bond, which breaks into a propyl radical and NaBr; a second sodium atom reduces the radical to propylsodium, a carbanion equivalent, which attacks a second molecule of 1-bromopropane by SN2 to give hexane](./plots/wurtz.py)

## 10.5. Reduction of Alkyl Halides

Alkyl halides can be reduced to hydrocarbons ($\ce{R-X -> R-H}$), and there are four common kinds of method with different mechanisms:

- Metal hydrides: $\ce{LiAlH4}$ in diethyl ether or THF reduces primary and secondary alkyl halides to alkanes. The H of $\ce{AlH4-}$ displaces the halogen as hydride by SN2: a chiral secondary halide reduced with $\ce{LiAlD4}$ gives the deuterated product with inverted configuration, whereas tertiary halides mainly eliminate. $\ce{LiAlH4}$ reacts violently with water ($\ce{LiAlH4 + 4 H2O -> LiOH + Al(OH)3 + 4 H2}$) and must be handled under anhydrous conditions. The hydride of $\ce{NaBH4}$ is weaker, and in **polar aprotic solvents** such as DMSO it can reduce primary and secondary halides and sulfonates by SN2; in aqueous diglyme it reduces tertiary halides and benzylic halides that ionize readily, the halide first ionizing to a carbocation that is then captured by $\ce{BH4-}$ (SN1 type).
- Radical reduction: tributyltin hydride $\ce{Bu3SnH}$ with the initiator AIBN reduces all kinds of alkyl halides (including tertiary and aryl halides) by a radical chain reaction ([6.5.7](/6.5.7#sec-7.5)). Tin compounds are toxic, and tris(trimethylsilyl)silane is now often used instead.
- Dissolving metals: Na or Li in liquid ammonia; the metal donates an electron (**single electron transfer**), the C–X bond breaks to give a radical, which accepts a second electron to become a carbanion that is protonated.
- Catalytic hydrogenolysis: $\ce{H2}$/Pd cleaves the C–X bond, and benzylic C–X (and C–O and C–N) bonds are especially easily cleaved; this cleavage of single bonds between carbon and a heteroatom by catalytic hydrogenation is called hydrogenolysis. Hydrogenolysis of benzyl ethers is the standard method for removing benzyl protecting groups.

![Reduction of an alkyl halide by a metal hydride (SN2): a deuteride of AlD4⁻ attacks the carbon of (R)-2-bromobutane from the back side as bromide leaves, giving 2-deuteriobutane with inversion of configuration](./plots/lialh4-reduction.py)

![Dissolving-metal reduction (Na or Li in liquid ammonia): the metal donates one electron and the C–X bond breaks into an alkyl radical and halide; a second electron turns the radical into a carbanion, which takes a proton from ammonia](./plots/dissolving-metal.py)

## 10.6. Oxidation of Alcohols

### 10.6.1. Oxidation of Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Alcohols

In the framework of oxidation states of [6.5.1](/6.5.1#sec-1.3.2), the carbon bearing the hydroxy group of an alcohol goes up one level for each two electrons lost (a C–H bond replaced by a C–O bond, or formation of C=O). Primary alcohols are oxidized to aldehydes, which are oxidized further to carboxylic acids; secondary alcohols are oxidized to ketones, which are not readily oxidized further; tertiary alcohols have no hydrogen on the carbon bearing the hydroxy group and are not oxidized under ordinary conditions, and under strongly acidic oxidizing conditions they first dehydrate to alkenes, which are then cleaved at the double bond to give small molecules.

```smiles
CCCO>>CCC=O primary
CCC=O>>CCC(=O)O aldehyde
CC(O)C>>CC(=O)C secondary
```

### 10.6.2. Chromium(VI) Oxidants

The classical oxidants are chromium(VI) compounds:

| Reagent | Composition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Jones reagent | $\ce{CrO3}$ in dilute sulfuric acid, added dropwise in acetone | Primary alcohol → carboxylic acid, secondary alcohol → ketone |
| Dichromates | $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ or $\ce{Na2Cr2O7}$ / $\ce{H2SO4}$ | The same; low-boiling aldehydes can be distilled out as they form |
| Sarett and Collins reagents | $\ce{CrO3.2C5H5N}$ (Sarett: in pyridine; Collins: in dichloromethane) | Primary alcohol → aldehyde, secondary alcohol → ketone |
| PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate) | $\ce{C5H5NH+ CrO3Cl-}$ in dichloromethane | Primary alcohol → aldehyde (Corey, 1975), secondary alcohol → ketone |

The mechanism of chromic acid oxidation has two steps. The alcohol first forms a chromate ester $\ce{R2CH-O-CrO3H}$ with $\ce{H2CrO4}$ (this step is like esterification and does not change the oxidation state of the alcohol carbon); the rate-determining step that follows is an E2-like elimination across C–O:

![The rate-determining step of chromic acid oxidation of propan-2-ol: water acts as a base and removes the hydrogen on the carbinol carbon of the chromate ester, the electron pair of the C–H bond becomes the π bond of C=O, the electron pair of the O–Cr bond moves to chromium, and chromium is reduced from +6 to +4 to give acetone](./plots/chromate-oxidation.py)

With propan-2-ol-2-d, $k_{\mathrm{H}}/k_{\mathrm{D}} \approx 6$ was found (Westheimer), which shows that the C–H bond on the carbinol carbon is broken in the rate-determining step ([6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.11.4)). Cr(IV) then disproportionates and reacts further, finally becoming Cr(III), and the solution changes from orange to blue-green; early breath alcohol tests made use of this color change.

When a primary alcohol is oxidized to an aldehyde in aqueous solution, the aldehyde adds water to give a hydrate (a gem-diol, [6.5.16](/6.5.16#sec-16.5.1)); the hydrate, like the alcohol, forms a chromate ester and is oxidized further, so Jones reagent oxidizes primary alcohols all the way to carboxylic acids; PCC and Collins reagent are used in anhydrous dichloromethane, where no hydrate forms and oxidation stops at the aldehyde.

![Why Jones oxidation does not stop at the aldehyde: in water propanal adds water to give its hydrate (a *gem*-diol); the hydrate forms a chromate ester like an alcohol, and the same E2-like step (water removes the C–H proton, the O–Cr electron pair moves onto chromium) gives propanoic acid. In anhydrous dichloromethane (PCC) no hydrate forms and the oxidation stops at the aldehyde](./plots/hydrate-oxidation.py)

Chromium(VI) compounds are carcinogenic and harmful to the environment and generate large amounts of chromium-containing waste, so they are now seldom used in synthesis. Common alternatives are:

- **Swern oxidation**: DMSO is activated with oxalyl chloride at $-78\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, the alcohol is added, and the mixture is treated with triethylamine; primary alcohol → aldehyde, secondary alcohol → ketone; the conditions are mild, and the by-products are dimethyl sulfide (malodorous), CO and $\ce{CO2}$ (mechanism in [6.5.26](/6.5.26#sec-26.9.2)).
- **Dess–Martin oxidation**: oxidation with the hypervalent iodine reagent DMP at room temperature under neutral conditions ([6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.7.3)).
- TEMPO-catalyzed oxidation: with the stable nitroxide radical TEMPO ([6.5.7](/6.5.7#sec-7.3.4)) as catalyst and NaOCl as terminal oxidant, or with Cu/TEMPO and $\ce{O2}$ from the air (Stahl, 2011), primary alcohols are oxidized selectively to aldehydes.
- Industrially: the vapor of a primary or secondary alcohol is passed over a copper (or silver) catalyst at $250$–$350\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and dehydrogenated to the aldehyde or ketone and $\ce{H2}$; methanol is oxidized over a silver catalyst with air to formaldehyde.
- In living organisms, alcohol dehydrogenase uses $\ce{NAD+}$ as the oxidant and removes the hydrogen from the carbinol carbon as hydride (transferred stereospecifically to C4 of the pyridine ring of $\ce{NAD+}$); ethanol is oxidized in this way to acetaldehyde in the liver ([6.5.25](/6.5.25#sec-25.6.1)).

![Alcohol dehydrogenase oxidizes ethanol with NAD⁺: in the active site ethanol is bound to zinc as the alkoxide; the oxygen lone pair forms the C=O bond while the C–H electron pair moves as a hydride to C4 of the nicotinamide ring of NAD⁺ (added stereospecifically to one face), and the ring π electrons move onto the positive nitrogen, giving acetaldehyde and NADH (a 1,4-dihydropyridine); R is the ADP-ribose part of the coenzyme](./plots/adh-nad.py)

$\ce{KMnO4}$ (basic or acidic) and hot nitric acid can also oxidize alcohols, but with poor selectivity, often cleaving the carbon chain.

## 10.7. Oxidative Cleavage of Vicinal Diols

Vicinal (1,2-) diols react with periodic acid ($\ce{HIO4}$, present in water mainly as $\ce{H5IO6}$) or lead tetraacetate $\ce{Pb(OAc)4}$, and the C–C bond between the two carbons bearing the hydroxy groups is cleaved to give two molecules of carbonyl compound:

$$
\ce{R2C(OH)-C(OH)R'2 + HIO4 -> R2C=O + R'2C=O + HIO3 + H2O}.
$$

Periodic acid first forms a five-membered cyclic periodate ester with the two hydroxy groups (I has oxidation state +7); in the cyclic intermediate the C–C bond and the two O–I bonds break in a concerted way, and iodine is reduced to iodic acid (+5). Formation of the cyclic ester requires that the two hydroxy groups can approach each other: open-chain diols rotate freely and all react; *cis*-cyclohexane-1,2-diol reacts much faster than the trans isomer (which can react only in the diequatorial conformation, in which the two hydroxy groups are gauche); a diol held by a rigid skeleton in a trans arrangement with a torsion angle close to $180^\circ$ does not react. $\alpha$-Hydroxy ketones, 1,2-diketones, $\alpha$-hydroxy aldehydes and 1,2-amino alcohols are also cleaved by periodic acid. Lead tetraacetate is used in organic solvents, goes through a similar cyclic plumbate ester, and can also cleave trans diols, more slowly.

![Periodate cleavage of a vicinal diol (2,3-dimethylbutane-2,3-diol): the two hydroxy groups form a five-membered cyclic periodate ester (iodine +7); in one concerted step the C–C electron pair becomes one C=O π bond, one O–I electron pair moves onto iodine and the other O–I electron pair becomes the second C=O π bond, giving two molecules of acetone and iodate (iodine +5)](./plots/periodate.py)

The reaction is quantitative, with one molecule of $\ce{HIO4}$ consumed for each C–C bond cleaved, and it can be used to deduce the structures of polyols and sugars ([6.5.32](/6.5.32)): the carbon in the middle of three consecutive hydroxy groups is oxidized to formic acid. For example, treatment of hexane-2,3,4-triol with 2 molecules of $\ce{HIO4}$ gives acetaldehyde, formic acid and propanal:

```smiles
CC(O)C(O)C(O)CC>>CC=O.OC=O.CCC=O
```

Dihydroxylation of an alkene ([6.5.11](/6.5.11#sec-11.8.2)) followed by cleavage with periodic acid gives the same result as **ozonolysis** (the Lemieux–Johnson oxidation combines $\ce{OsO4}$ and $\ce{NaIO4}$ in one step).

## 10.8. The Pinacol Rearrangement

**Definition 10.8.1 (Pinacol Rearrangement)** The reaction in which a vicinal diol in acid loses one hydroxy group to give a carbocation, a carbon group (or hydrogen) on the adjacent carbon migrates with its electron pair to the cationic carbon, and the cation formed is stabilized by the oxygen of the other hydroxy group and loses a proton to become an aldehyde or ketone is called the **pinacol rearrangement**.

The name comes from the rearrangement of 2,3-dimethylbutane-2,3-diol (pinacol) in dilute sulfuric acid to 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-one (pinacolone):

![The pinacol rearrangement: the protonated hydroxy group leaves as water to give a tertiary carbocation; a methyl group on the adjacent carbon migrates to the cationic carbon, the positive charge moves to the carbon bearing the hydroxy group and is stabilized by the lone pair of oxygen as a protonated carbonyl (an oxonium ion), and loss of a proton gives pinacolone](./plots/pinacol.py)

The driving force of the rearrangement is formation of a cation stabilized by oxygen (every atom of the oxonium ion has an octet) and of the final C=O bond, so the rearrangement is irreversible even though the carbocation before migration is already tertiary. As in the carbocation rearrangements of [6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.3.3), the migration is a 1,2-shift.

An unsymmetrical vicinal diol offers two choices:

1. Which hydroxy group leaves: the one that gives the more stable carbocation. 2-Methyl-1,1-diphenylpropane-1,2-diol loses in acid the hydroxy group on the carbon bearing the two phenyl groups (giving a diphenylmethyl-type cation), and a methyl group on the other carbon then migrates, giving 3,3-diphenylbutan-2-one.
2. Which group migrates: when the cations are the same, the migratory aptitudes are compared. The migrating group bridges the two carbons in the transition state and bears partial positive charge, so groups that can stabilize positive charge migrate faster. The approximate order is: 4-methoxyphenyl > phenyl > 4-nitrophenyl, aryl > tertiary alkyl > secondary alkyl > ethyl > methyl. The migratory aptitude of hydrogen is hard to state in general; it is often comparable to or larger than that of aryl groups, depending on the substrate and conditions (a systematic discussion of migratory aptitudes is in [6.5.30](/6.5.30)).

![Pinacol rearrangement of an unsymmetrical diol (2-methyl-1,1-diphenylpropane-1,2-diol): the hydroxy group that leaves is the one on the carbon that gives the more stable (diphenylmethyl-type) cation; a methyl group on the neighboring carbon then migrates, assisted by the lone pair of the remaining hydroxy oxygen, and the oxocarbenium ion loses a proton to give 3,3-diphenylbutan-2-one](./plots/pinacol-unsymmetrical.py)

```smiles
CC(C)(O)C(O)(c1ccccc1)c1ccccc1>>CC(=O)C(C)(c1ccccc1)c1ccccc1
OC(c1ccccc1)(c1ccccc1)C(O)(c1ccccc1)c1ccccc1>>O=C(c1ccccc1)C(c1ccccc1)(c1ccccc1)c1ccccc1 benzopinacolone
```

Cyclic vicinal diols show the stereochemical requirement of the migration: the migrating group and the leaving group must be antiperiplanar (the same origin as the requirement of E2, [6.5.9](/6.5.9#sec-9.4.2)). When one hydroxy group of *cis*-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane-1,2-diol leaves from an axial position, the methyl group on the adjacent carbon is also axial and antiperiplanar to it, the methyl group migrates rapidly, and 2,2-dimethylcyclohexanone is obtained. In the stable conformation of the trans isomer both hydroxy groups are axial and anti to each other, and the adjacent carbon has no antiperiplanar group that can migrate; only a ring C–C bond can migrate, in a less favorable conformation, so the reaction is slow and gives the ring-contracted product 1-(1-methylcyclopentyl)ethanone. The semipinacol rearrangement and other stereochemical details are in [6.5.30](/6.5.30).

## 10.9. Ring Opening of Epoxides

The C–O bonds of epoxides are activated by the ring strain of about $114\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$: an alkoxide is normally a very poor leaving group, but release of the ring strain on ring opening makes the reaction thermodynamically very favorable and lowers the energy of the transition state correspondingly. Epoxides therefore differ from ordinary ethers in that they can be opened by nucleophiles under both basic and acidic conditions to give 1,2-difunctional compounds. The regioselectivity under the two conditions is opposite:

![Ring opening of 2,2-dimethyloxirane: under basic conditions methoxide attacks the less hindered CH2 by SN2 to give 1-methoxy-2-methylpropan-2-ol; under acidic conditions, in the protonated epoxide the C–O bond to the tertiary carbon is longer and the positive charge is mostly on the tertiary carbon, and methanol attacks the tertiary carbon to give 2-methoxy-2-methylpropan-1-ol](./plots/epoxide-opening.py)

Basic (or neutral) conditions: direct attack by a strong nucleophile. Strong nucleophiles such as $\ce{HO-}$, $\ce{RO-}$, $\ce{RS-}$, $\ce{N3-}$, $\ce{CN-}$, $\ce{NH3}$ and amines, Grignard reagents, organolithiums and $\ce{LiAlH4}$ attack the less hindered carbon by SN2, and the leaving group is the oxide anion of the ring, which is protonated in the work-up. If the carbon attacked is a stereocenter, its configuration is inverted.

Acidic conditions: attack of a weak nucleophile on the protonated epoxide. The acid first protonates the epoxide oxygen (or coordinates to it as a Lewis acid), weakening the C–O bonds; weak nucleophiles such as $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{ROH}$ and HX then attack. The attack is still from the back (inversion of configuration, giving the trans product), but the transition state has strong carbocation character: the C–O bond to the more substituted carbon lengthens first and the positive charge is mainly on that carbon, so the nucleophile attacks the more substituted carbon ("an SN2 mechanism with SN1 regioselectivity"). When both ring carbons are secondary, the regioselectivity is poor.

Hydrolysis of cyclohexene oxide in dilute acid gives *trans*-cyclohexane-1,2-diol, complementary to the cis diol obtained by dihydroxylation with $\ce{OsO4}$:

```smiles
C1CC[C@H]2O[C@H]2C1.O>>O[C@@H]1CCCC[C@H]1O trans
```

![Acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of cyclohexene oxide: water attacks one ring carbon of the protonated epoxide from the side opposite to the C–O bond, the C–O bond breaks and the ring strain is released, and loss of a proton gives *trans*-cyclohexane-1,2-diol](./plots/epoxide-hydrolysis.py)

Synthetic uses of ring opening:

- Reaction with Grignard reagents or organolithiums gives primary alcohols with two more carbons (from oxirane), the standard "chain + 2" method: for example, propene undergoes allylic chlorination with $\ce{Cl2}$ at $500\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ ([6.5.7](/6.5.7#sec-7.8)) to give 3-chloropropene, which is converted into the Grignard reagent and reacts with oxirane to give pent-4-en-1-ol.
- Reaction with $\ce{NH3}$ and amines gives 1,2-amino alcohols (the skeleton of $\beta$-blockers such as propranolol); reaction with phenoxides and alkoxides gives 2-alkoxy alcohols; reaction with $\ce{N3-}$ gives 2-azido alcohols.
- Opening by hydride: $\ce{LiAlH4}$ is a strongly nucleophilic hydride and attacks the less hindered carbon, giving the more substituted alcohol ("basic" regioselectivity). $\ce{NaBH4}$ alone is too weakly nucleophilic and has difficulty opening epoxides under ordinary conditions; with $\ce{BH3}$ (itself a Lewis acid that coordinates to the epoxide oxygen), or with $\ce{BF3.OEt2}$ added to $\ce{NaBH4}$/$\ce{LiBH4}$, the epoxide is first activated by the Lewis acid, the hydride goes to the more substituted carbon that can better carry positive charge, and the less substituted alcohol results ("acidic" regioselectivity).
- Deoxygenation to alkenes: the phosphorus atom of $\ce{Ph3P}$ or $\ce{Ph2PLi}$ opens the epoxide by SN2 (one inversion), and the alkoxide formed attacks phosphorus to form a four-membered oxaphosphetane, which eliminates $\ce{Ph3P=O}$ with syn stereochemistry (the same as the last step of the **Wittig reaction**, [6.5.16](/6.5.16#sec-16.8.1)). The net result is inversion of the alkene geometry: the epoxide of a cis alkene gives the trans alkene on deoxygenation (the Vedejs method, often $\ce{Ph2PLi}$ followed by $\ce{CH3I}$, [6.5.26](/6.5.26#sec-26.6.5)).

![Chain extension by two carbons: allylmagnesium chloride (from 3-chloropropene) opens oxirane; the C–Mg electron pair attacks a ring CH2 carbon by SN2, the C–O bond breaks with release of the ring strain, and the magnesium alkoxide is protonated on work-up to give pent-4-en-1-ol](./plots/grignard-epoxide.py)

In biology, epoxides are both metabolic intermediates and a source of toxicity. Cytochrome P450 in the liver oxidizes the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon benzo[a]pyrene to the 7,8-diol-9,10-epoxide, whose epoxide carbon is opened by SN2 attack of the amino group of a DNA guanine, forming a covalent adduct, which is the main molecular mechanism of carcinogenesis by smoking ([6.5.15](/6.5.15#sec-15.6.4)); epoxide hydrolases detoxify by attack of an aspartate carboxylate to open the epoxide followed by hydrolysis, similar to the haloalkane dehalogenases of [6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.11).

When aryl allyl ethers are heated to about $200\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ they undergo the **Claisen rearrangement**, in which the allyl group migrates to the position ortho to the phenolic oxygen (to the para position if both ortho positions are occupied); this is a concerted **pericyclic reaction**, treated in [6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.5.6).

## Summary

- Alcohols, phenols, ethers and thiols (Section 10.1): alcohols have high boiling points because of hydrogen bonding; ethers are only hydrogen-bond acceptors; thiols hardly form hydrogen bonds. Solubility falls with increasing alkyl size (butanol about 7 g/100 g water, octanol practically insoluble). Acidity ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ in water): *tert*-butyl alcohol about 18, ethanol 15.9, methanol 15.5, water 14.00, ethanethiol 10.6, phenol 9.99; alkoxides are made with Na or NaH.
- Williamson synthesis (Definition 10.2.1): alkoxide + methyl or primary alkyl halide (SN2), with the more hindered part on the alkoxide side; ring closure of halo alcohols to epoxides (Definition 10.1.1) requires antiperiplanar geometry.
- Organometallic reagents convert an electrophilic carbon into a nucleophilic carbon:

  | Reagent | Preparation | Main reactions |
  |---|---|---|
  | Grignard reagents RMgX (Definition 10.3.1) | RX + Mg, anhydrous ether; Schlenk equilibrium (Definition 10.3.2) | Protonated by acidic hydrogens; add to aldehydes, ketones, esters, $\ce{CO2}$, oxirane |
  | Organolithiums RLi (Definition 10.4.1) | RX + 2 Li; lithium–halogen exchange | More reactive and more strongly basic than RMgX |
  | Gilman reagents $\ce{R2CuLi}$ (Definition 10.4.2) | 2 RLi + CuI | Cross-coupling with alkyl halides; ketones from acyl chlorides; 1,4-addition |
  | Wurtz reaction | 2 RX + 2 Na | Gives only symmetrical alkanes, little used |

- Reduction of alkyl halides (Section 10.5): $\ce{LiAlH4}$ (SN2), $\ce{NaBH4}$ (SN2 or SN1 type), $\ce{Bu3SnH}$ (radical), Na/$\ce{NH3}$ (single electron transfer), $\ce{H2}$/Pd (benzylic hydrogenolysis).
- Oxidation of alcohols (Section 10.6): primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (Jones to the acid; PCC, Swern, DMP and TEMPO stop at the aldehyde), secondary alcohol → ketone, tertiary alcohols not oxidized; the rate-determining step of chromic acid oxidation is an E2-like elimination of the chromate ester with C–H cleavage ($k_{\mathrm{H}}/k_{\mathrm{D}} \approx 6$).
- Cleavage of vicinal diols (Section 10.7): $\ce{HIO4}$ or $\ce{Pb(OAc)4}$ through cyclic esters, cis diols fast. Pinacol rearrangement (Definition 10.8.1): formation of the more stable carbocation, then migration of the group with the greater migratory aptitude that is antiperiplanar to the leaving group, giving an oxygen-stabilized cation and a carbonyl compound.
- Ring opening of epoxides (Section 10.9): under basic conditions strong nucleophiles attack the less substituted carbon (SN2); under acidic conditions weak nucleophiles attack the more substituted carbon (an SN2 with SN1 character); in both the configuration of the carbon attacked is inverted, giving trans products.

## Questions

1. What is wrong with the following synthetic design? How can it be corrected? 4-Bromobutan-1-ol $\xrightarrow{\ce{Mg},\ \text{ether}}$ the corresponding Grignard reagent $\xrightarrow{\ce{CH3CHO}}$ hexane-1,5-diol (after acidification).

   **Answer** The hydroxy group in the molecule is an acidic hydrogen ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ about 16), and as soon as a C–Mg bond forms it is protonated by an intramolecular or intermolecular O–H, so no Grignard reagent is obtained. The hydroxy group must be protected first: convert it to the TBS ether with *tert*-butyldimethylsilyl chloride and imidazole (or to the THP ether with dihydropyran), then react with magnesium and add acetaldehyde, and finally remove the protecting group with $\ce{Bu4N+F-}$ (or dilute acid) to give hexane-1,5-diol.

2. What combinations can be used to synthesize 2-phenylbutan-2-ol by a Grignard reaction? Which is best?

   **Answer** Any one of the three carbon groups of the tertiary alcohol (methyl, ethyl, phenyl) can come from the Grignard reagent: (a) phenylmagnesium bromide + butan-2-one; (b) ethylmagnesium bromide + acetophenone; (c) methylmagnesium iodide + propiophenone (1-phenylpropan-1-one). All three are feasible; the starting materials of (a) and (b) are the cheapest and most readily available, and (b) or (a) is usually chosen. With an ester, methyl benzoate and two equivalents of Grignard reagent could give only a tertiary alcohol with two identical carbon groups (such as 2-phenylpropan-2-ol), which does not apply here.

3. Which is the best method of synthesizing *tert*-butyl methyl ether? (a) sodium methoxide + *tert*-butyl bromide; (b) potassium *tert*-butoxide + iodomethane; (c) methanol and *tert*-butyl alcohol heated in concentrated sulfuric acid; (d) acid-catalyzed addition of methanol to 2-methylpropene.

   **Answer** In the laboratory (b) is best: iodomethane has no $\beta$-hydrogen and can only undergo SN2. In (a) *tert*-butyl bromide with a strong base undergoes almost only E2 and gives 2-methylpropene. (c) gives a mixture of three ethers and 2-methylpropene (*tert*-butyl alcohol dehydrates preferentially). (d) goes through capture of the *tert*-butyl cation by methanol and also gives the target product; this is the industrial method of making MTBE, but it requires an acidic catalyst and pressure.

4. What is obtained when hexane-2,3,4-triol is treated with 2 molecules of $\ce{HIO4}$? What would it show if only acetaldehyde and 2-hydroxybutanal were obtained?

   **Answer** Both adjacent C–C bonds (C2–C3 and C3–C4) are cleaved: C2 becomes acetaldehyde, C3, cleaved on both sides, is oxidized to formic acid, and C4 becomes propanal, that is, acetaldehyde + formic acid + propanal. If only acetaldehyde and 2-hydroxybutanal were obtained, only 1 molecule of $\ce{HIO4}$ would have been consumed and only the C2–C3 bond cleaved; 2-hydroxybutanal is itself an $\alpha$-hydroxy aldehyde, and further periodic acid would cleave it to formic acid and propanal.

5. Explain why *cis*-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane-1,2-diol rearranges rapidly in acid to 2,2-dimethylcyclohexanone, whereas the trans isomer reacts much more slowly and gives mainly 1-(1-methylcyclopentyl)ethanone.

   **Answer** In the pinacol rearrangement the migrating group must be antiperiplanar to the leaving group. In the stable conformation of the cis diol one hydroxy group is axial and one equatorial; when the axial hydroxy group leaves after protonation, the axial methyl group on the adjacent carbon is exactly anti to it and migrates, the positive charge falls on the carbon bearing the equatorial hydroxy group, and loss of a proton gives 2,2-dimethylcyclohexanone. In the preferred conformation of the trans diol both methyl groups are equatorial and both hydroxy groups axial and anti to each other; opposite the leaving hydroxy group there is only the other hydroxy group, which cannot migrate. To leave from an equatorial hydroxy group, the group anti to it is a ring C–C bond, and migration of the ring bond contracts the six-membered ring to a five-membered ring, giving 1-(1-methylcyclopentyl)ethanone, in which the acetyl group and the methyl group are on the same carbon. The ring must first flip into an unfavorable conformation, so the reaction is slow.

6. What are the main products of the reactions of 2,2-dimethyloxirane with (a) $\ce{LiAlH4}$, (b) $\ce{BH3}$·THF, (c) $\ce{CH3MgBr}$, (d) methanol with a little sulfuric acid?

   **Answer** (a) Hydride attacks the $\ce{CH2}$ by SN2 to give 2-methylpropan-2-ol. (b) $\ce{BH3}$ coordinates to oxygen as a Lewis acid, the ring opening has carbocation character, and hydride goes to the tertiary carbon; after hydrolysis 2-methylpropan-1-ol is obtained. (c) The methyl group attacks the $\ce{CH2}$ to give 2-methylbutan-2-ol. (d) After protonation methanol attacks the tertiary carbon to give 2-methoxy-2-methylpropan-1-ol.

7. Design a route to pent-4-en-1-ol from propene.

   **Answer** Propene undergoes radical allylic substitution with $\ce{Cl2}$ at $500\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to give 3-chloropropene; with Mg in dry ether this gives allylmagnesium chloride (added slowly to reduce Wurtz-type coupling); ring-opening addition to oxirane and acidification give $\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH2CH2OH}$, with the carbon chain extended by two carbons.

8. The epoxy alcohol below reacts with thiophenol in aqueous NaOH, yet the sulfur ends up on the terminal carbon that was not originally in the ring, giving a 1-phenylthio-2,3-diol. Draw a reasonable mechanism.

   ```smiles
   CC1(C)OC[C@@H](O1)[C@@H]2O[C@H]2CO
   ```

   **Answer** In base the terminal primary hydroxy group is deprotonated, and the alkoxide attacks the adjacent epoxide carbon from the back (intramolecular SN2, with inversion of configuration), opening the original epoxide and forming a new terminal epoxide between C2 and C1; the original epoxide oxygen becomes an alkoxide on C3. This reversible epoxide "migration" is called the Payne rearrangement. The new terminal epoxide is less hindered, and $\ce{PhS-}$ attacks the terminal $\ce{CH2}$ by SN2; ring opening gives the 1-phenylthio-2,3-diol. The terminal carbon is not a stereocenter, and the configuration of the product is determined by the one inversion in the Payne rearrangement.

9. How could butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol be distinguished by a simple test? How could butan-1-ol and diethyl ether be distinguished?

   **Answer** Use Lucas reagent (concentrated hydrochloric acid with anhydrous $\ce{ZnCl2}$): the tertiary alcohol (2-methylpropan-2-ol) turns turbid at once, the secondary alcohol (butan-2-ol) after a few minutes, and the primary alcohol (butan-1-ol) remains unchanged for a long time at room temperature ([6.5.9](/6.5.9#sec-9.7.1)). Jones reagent can also be used: primary and secondary alcohols turn the orange color blue-green, and the tertiary alcohol does not. Butan-1-ol liberates hydrogen with sodium metal and is oxidized by Jones reagent, whereas diethyl ether does neither.
