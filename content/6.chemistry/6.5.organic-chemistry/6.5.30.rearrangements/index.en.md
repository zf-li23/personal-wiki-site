Earlier chapters have already met many reactions that change the carbon skeleton: **carbocation** rearrangements in **SN1** and **E1** reactions, the **pinacol rearrangement**, the Claisen and **Cope rearrangements**, the **Fries rearrangement**, the **Baeyer–Villiger oxidation**, the **Hofmann rearrangement**, the **Wolff rearrangement**, the **Curtius rearrangement** and the **Schmidt reaction**. This chapter places them in a single framework: an atom or group, with or without its bonding electrons, migrates from one atom of a molecule to another. The terminus of the migration may be electron-deficient (a carbocation, a carbene carbon, an electron-deficient nitrogen or oxygen), electron-rich (a **carbanion**), or a radical center, or the whole process may be a concerted **pericyclic reaction**. Following this classification, the rearrangements treated earlier are only summarized in a table with links to where they were discussed, and the chapter discusses in full those not yet covered: the Wagner–Meerwein, Demjanov, semipinacol, Meinwald, benzilic acid, Beckmann, Lossen and Favorskii rearrangements, the [1,2]-Wittig, Stevens and Sommelet–Hauser rearrangements, the [2,3]-**sigmatropic shifts**, radical rearrangements, and the variants of the Cope and **Claisen rearrangements**.

Rearrangements are powerful tools for constructing complex skeletons. In a single step they can change the size of a ring (ring expansion and contraction), insert nitrogen or oxygen into a carbon chain (Beckmann, Baeyer–Villiger), remove or add one carbon (Hofmann, Arndt–Eistert), or transfer the configuration of a **stereocenter** to a new position ([3,3]- and [2,3]-σ shifts). They are also key steps in biosynthesis: terpenoid and steroid skeletons arise from cascades of carbocation rearrangements, and the biosynthesis of phenylalanine and tyrosine includes a Claisen rearrangement under **enzyme catalysis**. Methods for studying mechanisms (crossover experiments, isotopic labeling) are described in [6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.11.3); the selection rules for pericyclic reactions are given in [6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.6.2).

## 30.1. Classification and Methods of Study

### 30.1.1. The General Course of a Rearrangement

**Definition 30.1.1 (Rearrangement)** A reaction in which an atom or group migrates from one atom of a molecule to another atom of the same molecule, changing the positions of functional groups or the molecular skeleton, is called a **rearrangement** (rearrangement reaction). The migrating atom or group is the migrating group, the atom it leaves is the migration origin, and the atom to which it becomes attached is the migration terminus.

The most common type is the 1,2-shift, in which origin and terminus are adjacent. There are also [2,3]-, [3,3]- and other **sigmatropic shifts**, and more remote migrations such as the 1,5-hydrogen shift. In this chapter the migration always takes place within one molecule; the intermolecular exchange of groups (for example, transesterification) is not counted as a rearrangement. An ionic rearrangement generally proceeds in three steps:

1. Formation of a reactive intermediate or an activated substrate (a **carbocation**, an electron-deficient nitrogen, a **carbanion** or a radical), usually triggered by loss of a **leaving group** or removal of a proton;
2. Migration, which gives a more stable intermediate (or occurs concurrently with the departure of the leaving group);
3. Conversion into a stable product by capture by a **nucleophile**, loss of a proton or **tautomerism**.

The three steps may be partly or wholly merged. In electron-deficient rearrangements, departure of the leaving group and migration are often concerted (Hofmann, Beckmann and Baeyer–Villiger rearrangements); pericyclic rearrangements (Cope, Claisen) take place in one step without an intermediate.

### 30.1.2. Four Classes of Rearrangement

Rearrangements are classified by the electronic state of the migration terminus:

| Type | Terminus | The migrating group carries | Typical reactions | Treated in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electron-deficient rearrangement (nucleophilic or cationic rearrangement) | **Carbocation**, carbene carbon, carbonyl carbon, electron-deficient N or O | The bonding electron pair (migrates as an anion) | 1,2-shifts in carbocations, pinacol, Wolff, benzilic acid, Beckmann, Hofmann, Baeyer–Villiger | 30.2–30.7 |
| Electron-rich rearrangement (electrophilic or anionic rearrangement) | **Carbanion** or lone pair | No electron pair (formally migrates as a cation) | [1,2]-Wittig, Stevens, Favorskii, 1,2-metallate shifts | 30.8 |
| Radical rearrangement | Radical center | One electron | 1,2-Aryl shift, 1,5-hydrogen shift (Barton reaction) | 30.9 |
| Concerted rearrangement | Pericyclic transition state | The bonding electrons move around the ring | [3,3]- and [2,3]-**sigmatropic shifts**, **electrocyclic reactions** | 30.10 and [6.5.12](/6.5.12) |

"Nucleophilic rearrangement" means that the migrating group moves as a **nucleophile** to an electron-deficient center, and "electrophilic rearrangement" that it moves as an **electrophile** to an electron-rich center. These two names are easily confused, so the terms "electron-deficient rearrangement" and "electron-rich rearrangement" are used below. A further special class of concerted rearrangement, in which two σ bonds exchange positions simultaneously (for example, the interchange of the two bromine atoms of a vicinal dibromide), is called a dyotropic rearrangement and is not discussed here.

### 30.1.3. Methods of Study

Three questions dominate mechanistic work on rearrangements: whether the migration is intramolecular or intermolecular; where the migrating group goes and through which intermediates; and whether migration and departure are concerted. The common methods are the following.

- **Crossover experiments** ([6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.11.3)): two structurally similar substrates carrying different labels are allowed to react together, and the product is examined for "crossover products" in which the labels have been exchanged. The Hofmann, Curtius and Beckmann rearrangements give no crossover products, so the migrating group never leaves the molecule; the **Fries rearrangement** gives some crossover products under certain conditions, showing that the acylium ion can leave the original molecule.
- Isotopic labeling: in the **Claisen rearrangement** of allyl phenyl ether, the ¹⁴C-labeled terminal carbon of the allyl group is attached to the ring in the ortho product, which proves a [3,3] shift ([6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.5.6)); the distribution of ¹⁴C in the Favorskii rearrangement proves a symmetric cyclopropanone intermediate (30.8.5); when the Beckmann rearrangement is run in $\ce{H2^{18}O}$, the oxygen of the amide comes from the solvent, showing that the carbonyl oxygen is added by water after the migration.
- Stereochemistry: retention of configuration in the migrating group shows that it remains attached to the molecule throughout the shift; inversion at the terminus shows that migration and departure are concerted and that the migrating group enters from the side opposite the **leaving group**.
- Kinetics and spectroscopy: the dependence of the rate on substituents in the migrating group (Hammett relationship) reveals the charge distribution in the transition state; the **kinetic isotope effect** identifies which bond is broken in the **rate-determining step**; chemically induced dynamic nuclear polarization (CIDNP) and radical traps detect radical pairs (30.8.2).

## 30.2. 1,2-Shifts to Electron-Deficient Centers: A Unified Framework

### 30.2.1. The Terminus and the Driving Force

The common step of electron-deficient rearrangements is as follows. Next to an atom A that has only six valence electrons (or is about to lose a pair of electrons), a group R on the neighboring atom B moves to A, carrying the electron pair of the B–R bond. The nature of A varies:

| Terminus A | How it arises | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbocation** | Departure of a **leaving group** ($\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{OTs-}$), protonation of an alkene, epoxides and **Lewis acids** | Wagner–Meerwein, Demjanov, pinacol, semipinacol, Meinwald |
| Carbene carbon | Loss of $\ce{N2}$ from an $\alpha$-diazo ketone | Wolff |
| Carbonyl carbon | Intrinsically electron-deficient; after migration the π electrons move to oxygen | Benzilic acid rearrangement, $\alpha$-ketol rearrangement |
| Nitrogen | Departure of a leaving group on N ($\ce{Br-}$, $\ce{N2}$, carboxylate, $\ce{H2O}$) | Beckmann, Hofmann, Curtius, Schmidt, Lossen |
| Oxygen | **Heterolysis** of an O–O bond, with loss of carboxylate or water | Baeyer–Villiger, Hock, oxidation of boranes |

The driving force of the migration is the formation of a more stable cation or neutral molecule: a secondary carbocation becomes a tertiary one, a carbocation becomes an oxonium ion stabilized by the lone pair of oxygen (the **pinacol rearrangement**), or strong C=O and C=N bonds and $\ce{N2}$ are formed. The **ring strain** released on expansion of a small ring or contraction of a large one is also an important driving force. Because the **electronegativity** of N and O is greater than that of carbon, a positively charged N or O intermediate is extremely unstable, so for these termini the migration is almost always concerted with departure of the leaving group and no free nitrenium or oxenium ion is formed; a carbocation, in contrast, can be a genuine intermediate.

### 30.2.2. Migratory Aptitude

In an unsymmetrical substrate two or more groups can migrate. Which one does so preferentially?

**Definition 30.2.1 (Migratory Aptitude)** In a given class of rearrangement, the relative ease with which different groups migrate from the origin to the terminus is called their **migratory aptitude**. It is usually measured by the competition between two groups in the same molecule (intramolecular competition) or from the relative rates of different substrates.

Migratory aptitude is not a fixed property of a group; it depends on the type of reaction:

| Type of rearrangement | Approximate order of migratory aptitude | Determining factor |
|---|---|---|
| Electron-deficient carbon (pinacol, Wagner–Meerwein) | 4-Methoxyphenyl > phenyl > 4-chlorophenyl; aryl > tertiary alkyl > secondary alkyl > primary alkyl > methyl; H variable | The migrating group carries partial positive charge in the transition state |
| Electron-deficient oxygen (Baeyer–Villiger) | Tertiary alkyl > secondary alkyl ≈ cyclohexyl > benzyl ≈ phenyl > primary alkyl > methyl ([6.5.17](/6.5.17#sec-17.5.4)) | As above; in addition the group must be antiperiplanar to the O–O bond |
| Electron-deficient nitrogen (Beckmann, Schmidt of ketones) | The group anti to the **leaving group** migrates, largely regardless of electronic character | Stereochemistry (30.5) |
| Electron-deficient nitrogen (Hofmann, Curtius, Lossen) | Only one group can migrate | — |
| Electron-rich carbon ([1,2]-Wittig, Stevens) | Allyl ≈ benzyl > tertiary alkyl > secondary alkyl > primary alkyl > methyl > phenyl | Stability of the migrating group as a radical (30.8) |

The transition state of an electron-deficient rearrangement is a bridged structure with three centers and two electrons: the migrating group is partly bonded to both origin and terminus and carries partial positive charge. A group that can stabilize positive charge therefore migrates faster. Aryl groups are the most typical: their π system approaches the terminus from the back and forms a bridged phenonium ion, resembling a **σ complex** (related to the **neighboring group participation** of [6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.10)), so aryl groups have a high migratory aptitude, which is increased further by electron-donating substituents. Bachmann rearranged symmetrical tetraaryl pinacols in acetic acid and found approximate relative migratory aptitudes of 500 for 4-methoxyphenyl, 15.7 for 4-methylphenyl, 1 for phenyl, 0.7 for 4-chlorophenyl and 0.3 for 2-methoxyphenyl (the steric effect of the ortho substituent actually lowers the value). Alkyl groups follow the order tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl, in agreement with their ability to carry positive charge (**hyperconjugation**).

The migratory aptitude of hydrogen is hard to generalize. In the **pinacol rearrangement** H sometimes migrates faster than aryl, and the barrier to a 1,2-hydride shift is often even lower than that to an alkyl shift, because H has only a 1s orbital and so places little geometric demand on the bridged transition state. The order "aryl > alkyl > H" is therefore only a rough empirical rule.

Migratory aptitude is often masked by two other factors: which **carbocation** forms first (in the pinacol rearrangement the hydroxyl group that leaves first is the one that gives the more stable carbocation, [6.5.10](/6.5.10#sec-10.8)), and conformation (only a group antiperiplanar to the leaving group can migrate, as discussed in the next section).

### 30.2.3. Stereochemistry

1,2-Shifts have three stereochemical characteristics:

1. The configuration of the migrating group is retained. In the transition state the migrating group is bonded to both origin and terminus at once and never leaves the molecule, so if it is a chiral carbon its configuration does not change. This holds for all electron-deficient rearrangements: in the Hofmann, Curtius, Wolff, Baeyer–Villiger and Beckmann rearrangements, the optical purity of a chiral migrating group is fully preserved.
2. When migration is concerted with departure, the migrating group enters the terminus from the side opposite the **leaving group**, and the configuration at the terminus is inverted (equivalent to an intramolecular **SN2 reaction**); the migrating group and leaving group must be **antiperiplanar**. The **pinacol rearrangement** of cyclic vicinal diols illustrates this requirement ([6.5.10](/6.5.10#sec-10.8)).
3. If departure occurs first and gives a planar **carbocation** (corresponding to an **SN1 reaction**), the configuration at the terminus is partly or fully racemized. Slowly migrating groups (for example, alkyl groups) often give a racemic terminus, whereas rapidly migrating groups (aryl groups) migrate during or immediately after departure, and inversion at the terminus still predominates.

Older textbooks often state that "rearrangements always proceed through bridged intermediates". In fact genuine bridged intermediates (nonclassical carbocations, phenonium ions) exist only in particular systems, such as the 2-norbornyl cation and aryl-containing substrates ([6.5.8](/6.5.8#sec-8.10)); in most 1,2-shifts the bridged structure is only a transition state and the intermediate is an ordinary (classical) carbocation.

## 30.3. Carbocation Rearrangements

### 30.3.1. The Wagner–Meerwein Rearrangement

**Definition 30.3.1 (Wagner–Meerwein Rearrangement)** A rearrangement of a carbocation in which an alkyl or aryl group (in a broad sense, also hydrogen) on a carbon adjacent to the cationic carbon migrates to it with its bonding electron pair, producing a new carbocation and thereby changing the carbon skeleton, is called a **Wagner–Meerwein rearrangement**.

The reaction was discovered by Wagner (1899) and Meerwein (1914) in studies of the transformations of terpenes: dehydration of isoborneol in acid gives camphene, in which the skeleton has changed from bornane to camphane.

![Wagner–Meerwein rearrangement: protonated isoborneol loses water to give the secondary 2-bornyl cation; the C1–C6 bond, antiperiplanar to the leaving group, migrates with its electron pair to C2, and the positive charge moves to the methyl-bearing tertiary carbon C1; loss of a proton from the methyl group gives camphene](./plots/wagner-meerwein.py)

The products of **carbocation** rearrangements can be predicted by two empirical rules:

- The positive charge moves to a position where it is better stabilized: primary → secondary → tertiary, or to a **benzylic position**, an allylic position, or the α position of a heteroatom.
- The ring size changes in the direction of lower strain: when the cationic carbon is outside the ring and adjacent to it, migration of a ring C–C bond expands the ring (cyclobutylmethyl → cyclopentyl); when the cationic carbon is in the ring, migration of an exocyclic or endocyclic bond can contract it. Expansion of a four-membered ring to a five-membered ring lowers the **ring strain** from about $110\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ to about $26\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, so it can proceed even when a tertiary carbocation is converted into a secondary one.

In strong acid a substrate may undergo several successive rearrangements, usually each through a tertiary carbocation (except for steps that release ring strain), until the thermodynamically most stable cation is reached or the cation is trapped. Terpene synthases and oxidosqualene cyclases in living organisms exploit such cascades: after protonation, 2,3-oxidosqualene cyclizes to the protosteryl cation, which then undergoes two 1,2-hydride shifts and two 1,2-methyl shifts and loses a proton to give lanosterol, the precursor of cholesterol. The enzyme pocket controls the conformation of the cations and stabilizes them by cation–π interactions with the side chains of aromatic amino acids, so that an achiral chain-like substrate is converted in one step into a tetracyclic skeleton with seven **stereocenters**.

### 30.3.2. The Demjanov and Tiffeneau–Demjanov Rearrangements

Primary amines react with nitrous acid to give alkyldiazonium salts ([6.5.24](/6.5.24#sec-24.3)), which readily lose $\ce{N2}$. Because $\ce{N2}$ is an excellent **leaving group**, departure and migration of an adjacent group can be concerted even when a primary **carbocation** would otherwise be formed.

**Definition 30.3.2 (Demjanov Rearrangement)** The reaction in which a primary amine (especially a cycloalkylmethylamine) reacts with nitrous acid to give an alkyldiazonium salt, which loses $\ce{N2}$ with migration of an adjacent ring bond so that the ring is enlarged (or contracted) by one carbon, and the product is trapped by water as an alcohol, is called the **Demjanov rearrangement** (Demjanov, 1903).

```smiles
NCC1CCC1>>OC1CCCC1.OCC1CCC1
```

Cyclobutylmethylamine gives a mixture of cyclopentanol and unrearranged cyclobutylmethanol, together with alkenes; the substitution and rearrangement products are difficult to separate, which limits the synthetic use of the reaction. If the carbon next to the migration terminus carries a hydroxyl group, after rearrangement the positive charge resides on the carbon bearing the hydroxyl group and is stabilized by the lone pair of oxygen; loss of a proton gives a ketone directly, and the product is a single compound:

**Definition 30.3.3 (Tiffeneau–Demjanov Rearrangement)** The reaction in which a 1-(aminomethyl)cycloalkanol reacts with nitrous acid to give a diazonium salt, which loses $\ce{N2}$ with simultaneous migration of a ring C–C bond to give a cyclic ketone enlarged by one carbon, is called the **Tiffeneau–Demjanov rearrangement** (1937).

![Tiffeneau–Demjanov ring expansion: diazotization of 1-(aminomethyl)cyclohexanol gives an alkyldiazonium salt; as N₂ leaves, a ring C–C bond migrates with its electron pair to the CH₂ carbon, expanding the ring to seven members; the positive charge sits on the carbon bearing the hydroxyl group and is stabilized by oxygen, and loss of a proton gives cycloheptanone](./plots/tiffeneau-demjanov.py)

The substrate is obtained by converting a cyclic ketone into its **cyanohydrin** with HCN (or $\ce{TMSCN}$) and reducing the nitrile with $\ce{LiAlH4}$ ([6.5.16](/6.5.16#sec-16.7.1)), so the Tiffeneau–Demjanov rearrangement is a three-step method for converting a cyclic ketone into one with a ring larger by one carbon, applicable to five- to eight-membered rings. In unsymmetrically substituted cyclic ketones the more highly substituted ring carbon migrates preferentially (**migratory aptitude**). The same ring expansion is now often carried out directly from the cyclic ketone with **diazomethane** or trimethylsilyldiazomethane in the presence of a **Lewis acid** (the diazo carbon adds to the carbonyl group, followed by the same 1,2-shift with loss of $\ce{N2}$).

### 30.3.3. The Pinacol and Semipinacol Rearrangements

In the **pinacol rearrangement** ([6.5.10](/6.5.10#sec-10.8)) one hydroxyl group of a vicinal diol is protonated and leaves as water, a group on the adjacent carbon migrates, the resulting cation is stabilized by the oxygen of the other hydroxyl group, and a ketone is finally obtained. Its two selectivity problems were discussed in 6.5.10: the hydroxyl group that leaves first is the one that gives the more stable **carbocation**, and the group that migrates is the one with high migratory aptitude that is **antiperiplanar** to the **leaving group**.

When both hydroxyl groups can give carbocations of similar stability, the leaving group can be specified chemically: the secondary hydroxyl group is selectively converted into a tosylate (or mesylate) while the tertiary one does not react; on treatment with base or a **Lewis acid** the sulfonate leaves from the secondary carbon, a group on the tertiary carbon migrates, and the hydroxyl group on the tertiary carbon becomes a carbonyl group. Which group leaves first is then determined by the leaving group and not by the stability of the carbocation.

**Definition 30.3.4 (Semipinacol Rearrangement)** A rearrangement in which one carbon carries a hydroxyl group (or its alkoxide or silyl ether) and the adjacent carbon carries a leaving group or a positive charge (a sulfonate, a halogen, a diazonium group, a ring-opened epoxide, or a cation formed by addition of an electrophile to an alkene), and a group on the oxygen-bearing carbon migrates to the adjacent carbon while the oxygen-bearing carbon becomes a carbonyl carbon, is called a **semipinacol rearrangement**.

The type of semipinacol rearrangement is determined by the leaving group:

| Substrate | Trigger | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $\beta$-Amino alcohol | $\ce{HNO2}$ (diazonium salt) | Tiffeneau–Demjanov ring expansion (30.3.2) |
| Monosulfonate of a vicinal diol, $\beta$-halo alcohol | Base, $\ce{Ag+}$ or Lewis acid | Pinacol rearrangement with specified direction of migration |
| 2,3-Epoxy alcohol | Lewis acid ($\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{TiCl4}$) | Formation of a $\beta$-hydroxy ketone (aldol-type product) |
| Allylic alcohol (or its silyl ether) | Activation of the double bond by a halonium ion, a proton or a metal catalyst | Formation of an $\alpha$-quaternary ketone |

The semipinacol rearrangement of small-ring alcohols is a good method for ring expansion: in acid, 1-(hydroxybenzyl)cyclopropanol loses its benzylic hydroxyl group, a C–C bond of the cyclopropane migrates to the benzylic carbon, and the three-membered ring expands to a four-membered ring to give 2-phenylcyclobutanone (Question 3).

```smiles
OC1(C(O)c2ccccc2)CC1>>O=C1CCC1c1ccccc1
```

![Semipinacol ring expansion: the protonated benzylic hydroxyl group leaves as water while a ring C–C bond of the cyclopropanol migrates with its electron pair to the benzylic carbon, expanding the three-membered ring to a four-membered ring; the positive charge sits on the carbon bearing the hydroxyl group, where the lone pair of oxygen turns it into a protonated carbonyl group, and loss of a proton gives 2-phenylcyclobutanone](./plots/semipinacol.py)

If the migration terminus is a **stereocenter**, the stereochemistry follows 30.2.3: when migration is slow the terminus tends to racemize, and when it is fast (for example, aryl) or the conformation is fixed (in cyclic substrates the group antiperiplanar to the leaving group migrates), the configuration at the terminus is inverted. The semipinacol rearrangement can build quaternary carbon centers stereospecifically, and many catalytic asymmetric versions have been developed in recent years.

### 30.3.4. The Meinwald Rearrangement of Epoxides

When an epoxide coordinates to a **Lewis acid**, the more highly substituted C–O bond breaks, and the resulting **carbocation** is adjacent to a carbon bearing $\ce{O-BF3^-}$, which is exactly the substrate of a semipinacol rearrangement.

**Definition 30.3.5 (Meinwald Rearrangement)** The rearrangement in which an epoxide opens under the action of a Lewis acid ($\ce{BF3.OEt2}$, $\ce{MgBr2}$, $\ce{InCl3}$, etc.) or a strong acid, and a hydrogen, alkyl or aryl group on the adjacent carbon migrates to the cationic carbon to give an aldehyde or ketone, is called the **Meinwald rearrangement** (Meinwald, 1963).

![Meinwald rearrangement: the oxygen of trans-stilbene oxide coordinates to BF₃, and one C–O bond breaks to give a benzylic carbocation; the phenyl group on the adjacent carbon migrates with its electron pair to the cationic carbon, a lone pair of oxygen forms the C=O bond, and BF₃ leaves, giving diphenylacetaldehyde](./plots/meinwald.py)

The **regioselectivity** is determined in two steps: the C–O bond that breaks first is the one that gives the more stable carbocation; then, among the groups on the oxygen-bearing carbon, the migrating group is selected by **migratory aptitude** (and conformation). In trans-stilbene oxide the phenyl group migrates and not H, so diphenylacetaldehyde is obtained rather than deoxybenzoin $\ce{PhCH2COPh}$; in 1-methylcyclohexene oxide a ring C–C bond migrates to give the ring-contracted 1-methylcyclopentanecarbaldehyde, whereas H migration gives 2-methylcyclohexanone, and the ratio of the two depends on the Lewis acid. Protic acids in nucleophilic solvents such as water and alcohols generally cause only ring opening (giving vicinal diols or their monoethers, [6.5.10](/6.5.10#sec-10.9)), but under nonnucleophilic conditions (for example, $\ce{HClO4}$ in an inert solvent, or acidic molecular sieves) they can also induce the Meinwald rearrangement.

## 30.4. Migration to a Carbonyl Carbon: The Benzilic Acid Rearrangement

A carbonyl carbon is itself electron-deficient, and the tetrahedral anion formed when a **nucleophile** adds to an adjacent carbonyl group can "push" a group onto the carbonyl carbon:

**Definition 30.4.1 (Benzilic Acid Rearrangement)** The rearrangement in which a 1,2-diketone in strong base ($\ce{NaOH}$, $\ce{KOH}$) adds hydroxide to one carbonyl group, and in the resulting tetrahedral anion a group migrates with its electron pair to the adjacent carbonyl carbon to give the salt of an $\alpha$-hydroxy carboxylic acid, is called the **benzilic acid rearrangement**. The name comes from the conversion of benzil into benzilic acid (2-hydroxy-2,2-diphenylacetic acid) (Liebig, 1838), which is also the earliest recorded rearrangement.

![Benzilic acid rearrangement: hydroxide adds to one carbonyl group of benzil; in the tetrahedral anion a lone pair of oxygen re-forms C=O while a phenyl group migrates with its electron pair to the adjacent carbonyl carbon, and the π electrons of that C=O move to oxygen (rate-determining step); a fast proton transfer gives the benzilate ion](./plots/benzilic-acid.py)

The **rate law** is first order in both the diketone and $\ce{HO-}$: addition of $\ce{HO-}$ is a fast preequilibrium, and the migration is the **rate-determining step**. In the last step the proton transfer converts the carboxylic acid into the carboxylate, which irreversibly drives the reaction toward the product. Choice of base:

- With sodium methoxide or potassium *tert*-butoxide in place of $\ce{NaOH}$, the alkoxide adds to the carbonyl group, and after migration an $\alpha$-hydroxy carboxylic ester is obtained directly (the "benzilic ester rearrangement").
- Alkoxides that have an $\alpha$-H, such as sodium ethoxide and sodium isopropoxide, readily transfer hydride to the highly reactive 1,2-diketone (as in the **MPV reduction**, [6.5.17](/6.5.17#sec-17.2.2)) and reduce it to an $\alpha$-hydroxy ketone, so they are generally not used.
- Acyclic 1,2-diketones that have an $\alpha$-H mainly undergo the **aldol reaction** in strong base ([6.5.22](/6.5.22#sec-22.1)); good substrates are diketones without an enolizable $\alpha$-H (aryl diketones) and cyclic diketones. A cyclic 1,2-diketone contracts by one carbon on rearrangement: cyclohexane-1,2-dione gives 1-hydroxycyclopentanecarboxylic acid.

The $\alpha$-ketol rearrangement is a close relative of the benzilic acid rearrangement: the hydroxyl group of an $\alpha$-hydroxy ketone is deprotonated in base (or activated by a **Lewis acid**), and a group migrates from the oxygen-bearing carbon to the adjacent carbonyl carbon, with the result that the hydroxyl and carbonyl groups exchange positions. The reaction is reversible, and the equilibrium favors the more stable isomer. The rearrangement of 17$\alpha$-hydroxy-20-keto steroids in base to D-homosteroids with a six-membered D ring (17a-ketones) is an example of expansion of a five-membered to a six-membered ring. In living organisms, ketol-acid reductoisomerase (KARI) in the biosynthesis of valine and isoleucine first catalyzes an $\ce{Mg^2+}$-dependent $\alpha$-ketol-type alkyl shift and then reduces the carbonyl group.

![α-Ketol rearrangement (1-acetylcyclopentanol as a model, corresponding to D-ring expansion of a 17α-hydroxy-20-keto steroid): in base the alkoxide oxygen re-forms C=O while a ring C–C bond migrates with its electron pair to the adjacent ketone carbon, and the π electrons of that C=O move to oxygen; the five-membered ring expands to a six-membered ring, the hydroxyl and carbonyl groups exchange positions, and protonation gives 2-hydroxy-2-methylcyclohexanone](./plots/alpha-ketol.py)

Esters of benzilic acid are a structural unit of a class of anticholinergic drugs, for example clidinium (the ester of benzilic acid with a quaternized quinuclidinol).

## 30.5. The Beckmann Rearrangement

### 30.5.1. Reaction and Mechanism

Ketones form oximes with hydroxylamine ([6.5.16](/6.5.16#sec-16.9.2)). The OH of an oxime is a poor **leaving group**, but the N–O bond breaks readily after protonation ($\ce{H2SO4}$, polyphosphoric acid) or conversion to an ester ($\ce{PCl5}$, $\ce{SOCl2}$, TsCl, MsCl).

**Definition 30.5.1 (Beckmann Rearrangement)** The reaction in which a ketoxime (or its sulfonate or phosphate), treated with acid or an activating reagent, undergoes migration of the hydrocarbon group anti to the leaving group on N from carbon to nitrogen with simultaneous departure of the leaving group, and the resulting nitrilium ion is captured by water and tautomerizes to an N-substituted amide, is called the **Beckmann rearrangement** (Beckmann, 1886). Cyclic ketoximes give **lactams**.

![Beckmann rearrangement: in protonated (E)-acetophenone oxime, the phenyl group anti to OH₂⁺ migrates from carbon to the electron-deficient nitrogen as water leaves, giving a nitrilium ion in one step; water adds to the nitrilium carbon, and loss of a proton gives an imidic acid, which tautomerizes to acetanilide](./plots/beckmann.py)

In the Beckmann rearrangement the migration terminus is an electron-deficient nitrogen: when the leaving group departs with the electron pair of the N–O bond, nitrogen is left with only six valence electrons, and the migrating group supplies an electron pair at the same time. The reaction therefore belongs to the same class as the Hofmann and **Curtius rearrangements** (migration to an electron-deficient nitrogen), and not to the rearrangements to oxygen.

### 30.5.2. Stereochemistry

The Beckmann rearrangement has strict stereochemical requirements:

- Anti migration. The group that migrates is always the one anti to the **leaving group**, not the one with the higher migratory aptitude. (*E*)-Acetophenone oxime (phenyl anti to OH) gives acetanilide $\ce{CH3CONHPh}$, and the (*Z*) isomer gives *N*-methylbenzamide $\ce{PhCONHCH3}$. This shows that cleavage of the N–O bond and migration of the anti group are concerted: the migrating group enters nitrogen from the side opposite the leaving group (the same geometry as in **SN2**).
- Retention of configuration in the migrating group. A chiral secondary alkyl group migrates with almost no loss of optical purity (about 99%).
- Isomerization of the oxime. In strong acid the *E* and *Z* isomers of an oxime can interconvert through protonated intermediates; the product ratio then reflects the equilibrium between the two isomers and their respective rates of rearrangement, and mixtures are often obtained. To preserve the original configuration of the oxime, it is first converted under mild conditions to a sulfonate (TsCl/pyridine, MsCl/triethylamine) and then rearranged under neutral or weakly acidic conditions.

In an unsymmetrical ketoxime the bulkier group is usually anti to OH (determined by sterics during oxime formation), so in most cases the bulkier group migrates: 2-methylcyclohexanone oxime gives mainly 7-methylazepan-2-one, with N inserted on the methyl-bearing side.

```smiles
ON=C1CCCCC1>>O=C1CCCCCN1
CC1CCCCC1=NO>>CC1CCCCC(=O)N1
```

### 30.5.3. Applications, Conditions and Side Reactions

In industry, cyclohexanone oxime rearranges in oleum to $\varepsilon$-caprolactam, whose ring-opening polymerization gives nylon 6 ([6.5.25](/6.5.25#sec-25.9.2)). In the traditional process each tonne of caprolactam produced several tonnes of ammonium sulfate as a by-product (formed on neutralizing the sulfuric acid); modern processes catalyze the rearrangement in the gas phase over high-silica MFI zeolite, and cyclohexanone oxime itself is obtained by direct ammoximation of cyclohexanone with ammonia and $\ce{H2O2}$ over titanium silicalite, with almost no inorganic salt produced.

In the laboratory, acid-sensitive substrates are treated with mild modes of activation:

| Mode of activation | **Leaving group** | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| Concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$, polyphosphoric acid, $\ce{HCl}$/$\ce{AcOH}$ (Beckmann mixture) | $\ce{H2O}$ | Classical conditions; the oxime may isomerize |
| $\ce{PCl5}$, $\ce{SOCl2}$ | Phosphorus- or sulfur-containing oxyanions | Anhydrous conditions |
| TsCl/pyridine, MsCl/$\ce{Et3N}$ | $\ce{TsO-}$, $\ce{MsO-}$ | Preserves the oxime configuration; $\ce{Et3N}$ neutralizes HCl |
| Cyanuric chloride, **Lewis acids** (catalytic) | Heteroaryloxide | For the catalytic cycle see Question 2 |

Besides being captured by water, the nitrilium ion can be captured by other **nucleophiles** (alcohols give imidates, amines give amidines, and carbon nucleophiles such as allylsilanes give imines). If the anti group can depart to give a stable **carbocation** (tertiary alkyl, $\alpha$-alkoxyalkyl or $\alpha$-aminoalkyl), Beckmann fragmentation takes place: the N–O bond and the C–C bond break simultaneously to give a nitrile and a carbocation, and the carbocation loses a proton to give an alkene or is captured by a nucleophile. An example is the fragmentation of methyl *tert*-butyl ketoxime:

```smiles
CC(=NO)C(C)(C)C>>CC#N.C=C(C)C
```

![Beckmann fragmentation: in protonated (E)-methyl tert-butyl ketoxime, the group anti to OH₂⁺ is tert-butyl; the C–C bond does not migrate but breaks, and its electron pair forms the third bond of C≡N while water leaves at the same time, giving acetonitrile and the tert-butyl cation, which loses a proton to give 2-methylpropene](./plots/beckmann-fragmentation.py)

Aldoximes generally do not undergo the Beckmann rearrangement but lose water to give nitriles (when the C–H of the aldoxime is anti to the leaving group, this is equivalent to an **E2** elimination). In natural product synthesis the Beckmann rearrangement is often used to insert a nitrogen atom into the middle of a ring, converting carbocyclic skeletons into nitrogen-containing lactam and alkaloid skeletons.

## 30.6. Migration to Electron-Deficient Nitrogen: Degradations via Isocyanates

The **Hofmann rearrangement** ([6.5.20](/6.5.20#sec-20.2)), the **Curtius rearrangement** and the **Schmidt reaction** ([6.5.24](/6.5.24#sec-24.8.3)) all convert a carboxylic acid derivative $\ce{RC(O)X}$ into a primary amine with one carbon fewer: the acyl nitrogen carries a **leaving group**, and as the leaving group departs R migrates from the carbonyl carbon to nitrogen, giving an isocyanate $\ce{R-N=C=O}$, which is then captured by water (giving the amine and $\ce{CO2}$), an alcohol (a carbamate) or an amine (a urea). The fourth member is the Lossen rearrangement.

**Definition 30.6.1 (Lossen Rearrangement)** The reaction in which the oxygen of a hydroxamic acid $\ce{RC(O)NHOH}$ is acylated, sulfonylated or otherwise activated, the N–H proton is then removed by base (or heat), and R migrates from the carbonyl carbon to nitrogen as the carboxylate (or sulfonate) on oxygen leaves, giving an isocyanate, is called the **Lossen rearrangement** (Lossen, 1872).

![Lossen rearrangement: O-acetylbenzohydroxamic acid loses the N–H proton in base; the lone pair of nitrogen forms C=N while the phenyl group migrates with its electron pair from carbon to nitrogen and acetate leaves, giving phenyl isocyanate in one step; the isocyanate reacts with water to give carbamic acid, which loses CO₂ to give aniline](./plots/lossen.py)

Free hydroxamic acids cannot rearrange directly: $\ce{HO-}$ on nitrogen is a very poor leaving group, and protonating it to $\ce{H2O}$ in strong acid would hydrolyze the hydroxamic acid. The OH must first be converted into a good leaving group by acylation with an acyl chloride or anhydride, sulfonylation with a sulfonyl chloride, or in situ activation with reagents such as carbonyldiimidazole or carbodiimides. There are also conditions that use only base and heat without a separate activator; the activating agent is then the isocyanate formed in the reaction, which *O*-carbamoylates the unreacted hydroxamic acid so that it acquires a good leaving group, and the reaction proceeds by **autocatalysis**.

A comparison of the four reactions:

| Reaction | Substrate | Leaving group on nitrogen | Conditions | Features |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hofmann rearrangement | Primary amide $\ce{RCONH2}$ | $\ce{Br-}$ (*N*-bromoamide anion) | $\ce{Br2}$ or NaOCl, NaOH; or **hypervalent iodine reagents** | Amine from the amide in one pot; strongly basic, oxidizing conditions |
| Curtius rearrangement | Acyl azide $\ce{RCON3}$ | $\ce{N2}$ | Heat (neutral); DPPA gives the amine from the carboxylic acid in one pot | Mildest conditions; the isocyanate can be isolated or trapped in situ as a Boc- or Cbz-protected amine |
| Schmidt reaction | $\ce{RCOOH}$ + $\ce{HN3}$ | $\ce{N2}$ | Concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ | One step from the carboxylic acid; strong acid, and $\ce{HN3}$ is highly toxic and explosive |
| Lossen rearrangement | *O*-Activated hydroxamic acid | Carboxylate, sulfonate | Base, heat | No azides or halogens needed; substrate must be activated first |

The four reactions share the following features: the migrating group retains its configuration completely; there are no crossover products; migration is concerted with departure and does not pass through a free acyl nitrene ([6.5.20](/6.5.20#sec-20.2)); migratory aptitude is not an issue because only one group can migrate. All of them can prepare tertiary alkylamines, arylamines and chiral primary amines, which are difficult to obtain by **SN2** methods.

## 30.7. Migration to Electron-Deficient Oxygen

Rearrangements to oxygen require **heterolysis** of an O–O (or O–N) bond:

| Reaction | Substrate | **Leaving group** | Product | See |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Baeyer–Villiger oxidation** | Criegee intermediate from a ketone and a peracid | Carboxylate | Ester, lactone | [6.5.17](/6.5.17#sec-17.5.4) |
| Dakin oxidation | Electron-rich aromatic aldehyde with $\ce{H2O2}$/base | $\ce{HO-}$ | Aryl formate → phenol | [6.5.17](/6.5.17#sec-17.5.4) |
| Hock rearrangement | Tertiary alkyl hydroperoxide (cumene hydroperoxide) with acid | $\ce{H2O}$ | Oxocarbenium ion → phenol and ketone | [6.5.15](/6.5.15#sec-15.9) |
| Oxidation of boranes | Trialkylborane with $\ce{HOO-}$ | $\ce{HO-}$ | Borate ester → alcohol | [6.5.11](/6.5.11#sec-11.4.6), [6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.4.5) |

In all of them the migratory aptitude is determined by the migrating group carrying partial positive charge (tertiary alkyl and aryl groups migrate quickly), the configuration of the migrating group is retained, and the migrating bond must be **antiperiplanar** to the O–O bond that breaks. In the Hock rearrangement the migratory aptitude of phenyl is far greater than that of methyl, so cumene hydroperoxide gives only phenol and acetone, and not acetophenone and methanol.

## 30.8. Rearrangements at Electron-Rich Centers

### 30.8.1. Why 1,2-Shifts to Carbanions Are Difficult

In contrast to **carbocations**, groups next to a **carbanion** rarely undergo 1,2-shifts. In terms of orbitals, the transition state of an electron-deficient 1,2-shift is a three-center, two-electron system (the bonding pair of the migrating group plus the empty orbital at the terminus), analogous to the cyclopropyl cation and a 2-electron system with Hückel **aromaticity**; an electron-rich 1,2-shift is a three-center, four-electron system (with the extra pair at the terminus), and a concerted **suprafacial** migration is symmetry-forbidden by the **Woodward–Hoffmann rules** ($[\sigma 2_{\mathrm{s}} + \omega 2_{\mathrm{s}}]$, four electrons, suprafacial–suprafacial).

Electron-rich 1,2-shifts therefore occur only in two situations:

1. Through a radical pair: the bond between the migration origin and the migrating group undergoes **homolysis** to give a **radical anion** and a radical, which recombine rapidly in the solvent cage ([1,2]-Wittig and Stevens rearrangements). Migratory aptitude then follows radical stability.
2. When the migrating group can accept the electron pair in its own empty orbital or π* orbital, so that the transition state is no longer a four-electron system: the **1,2-metallate rearrangement** in borates (the terminus carries a **leaving group**, [6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.4.5)), the migration of silyl groups from carbon to oxygen (the Brook rearrangement, driven by the strong Si–O bond), and addition–elimination-type migrations of acyl and aryl groups (through a three-membered ring or a cyclic **tetrahedral intermediate**, such as the cyclopropanone of the Favorskii rearrangement and the anionic **Fries rearrangement**).

![[1,2]-Brook rearrangement: the alkoxide oxygen of an α-silyl alcohol attacks its own silicon atom; through a three-membered pentacoordinate silicate the C–Si bond breaks and the trimethylsilyl group is transferred to oxygen, leaving a benzylic carbanion, which is protonated to give a benzyl silyl ether; the strong Si–O bond is the driving force](./plots/brook.py)

In contrast, the [2,3] shift is a pericyclic process with five atoms and six electrons, which is thermally allowed and has a much lower barrier (30.8.4).

### 30.8.2. The [1,2]-Wittig Rearrangement

**Definition 30.8.1 ([1,2]-Wittig Rearrangement)** The rearrangement in which, in the $\alpha$-carbanion of an ether (obtained by removing the C–H proton next to the ether oxygen with an alkyllithium), the other hydrocarbon group on oxygen migrates to the carbanion to give an alkoxide is called the **[1,2]-Wittig rearrangement** (Wittig, 1942). It is a different discovery of the same chemist who found the Wittig reaction (olefination of carbonyl compounds with **ylides**, [6.5.16](/6.5.16#sec-16.8.1)).

![[1,2]-Wittig rearrangement: butyllithium removes a benzylic proton of dibenzyl ether; the O–CH₂Ph bond undergoes homolysis (fishhook arrows) to give a radical pair of a ketyl radical anion and a benzyl radical, which recombine at carbon in the solvent cage to give the lithium salt of 1,2-diphenylethanol](./plots/wittig-12.py)

```smiles
COCc1ccccc1>>CC(O)c1ccccc1
```

Evidence for the radical-pair mechanism includes: CIDNP signals are observed during the reaction; radical traps give trapped products; the migratory aptitude parallels radical stability (allyl ≈ benzyl > tertiary alkyl > secondary alkyl > primary alkyl > methyl > phenyl); and there are small amounts of coupling by-products from radicals that escape the solvent cage. If the migrating carbon is a **stereocenter**, its configuration is largely retained (recombination of the radical pair in the solvent cage is faster than radical inversion), whereas the lithium-bearing carbon terminus is inverted. Practical substrates need a group that stabilizes the **carbanion** at the $\alpha$ position (benzylic, allylic, or adjacent to a carbonyl or alkynyl group), and the migrating group must be able to form a reasonably stable radical.

### 30.8.3. The Stevens and Sommelet–Hauser Rearrangements

Removal of an $\alpha$-C–H of a **quaternary ammonium salt** by base gives an ammonium ylide $\ce{R3N+-CR2^-}$.

**Definition 30.8.2 (Stevens Rearrangement)** The rearrangement in which, in the ylide formed from a quaternary ammonium (or sulfonium) salt by base, a hydrocarbon group on nitrogen (or sulfur) migrates to the adjacent carbanion to give a tertiary amine (or a sulfide) is called the **Stevens rearrangement** (Stevens, 1928).

![Stevens rearrangement: hydroxide removes the acidic proton between the carbonyl group and nitrogen in the benzyldimethyl(phenacyl)ammonium ion to give an ammonium ylide; the N–benzyl bond undergoes homolysis, and the benzyl radical and the radical anion recombine at carbon in the solvent cage to give 2-(dimethylamino)-1,3-diphenylpropan-1-one](./plots/stevens.py)

The Stevens rearrangement has the same (radical-pair) mechanism as the [1,2]-Wittig rearrangement and similar requirements on the substrate:

- The **carbanion** must be stabilized: the ylide carbon is adjacent to a carbonyl, ester, aryl, vinyl or alkynyl group, or is itself a benzylic carbon;
- The migrating group must form a reasonably stable radical: benzyl, allyl, benzhydryl, or $\ce{CH2Z}$ (Z an electron-withdrawing group); simple alkyl groups generally do not migrate (in contrast to the [1,2]-Wittig rearrangement);
- The hydrocarbon groups on nitrogen should preferably have no $\beta$-H, since otherwise **Hofmann elimination** occurs ([6.5.23](/6.5.23#sec-23.8));
- A chiral migrating group largely retains its configuration.

The Stevens rearrangement of sulfonium salts is analogous, and sulfur ylides form more readily than ammonium ylides ([6.5.26](/6.5.26#sec-26.11.2)). In the Stevens rearrangement of spirocyclic quaternary ammonium salts, a bond of one ring migrates to the exocyclic carbanion, converting the spiro ring into a fused (or ring-expanded) amine, which can be used in the synthesis of alkaloid skeletons.

Benzylic ammonium ylides have another pathway:

**Definition 30.8.3 (Sommelet–Hauser Rearrangement)** The rearrangement in which a benzyltrialkylammonium salt in strong base ($\ce{NaNH2}$/liquid ammonia) forms the ylide on an *N*-methyl group, the carbanion attacks the ortho position of the benzene ring, the benzylic C–N bond breaks by a [2,3]-σ shift with temporary loss of aromaticity of the ring, and tautomerization gives an ortho-methylated benzylamine is called the **Sommelet–Hauser rearrangement**.

![Sommelet–Hauser rearrangement: sodium amide in liquid ammonia converts the benzyltrimethylammonium ion into the ylide on a methyl group; the carbanion attacks the ortho position of the ring, the π bonds of the ring shift, and the benzylic C–N bond breaks ([2,3]-σ shift) to give a triene that has lost aromaticity; tautomerization restores the aromatic ring and gives N,N-dimethyl-2-methylbenzylamine](./plots/sommelet-hauser.py)

The benzylic ylide forms most readily (the benzylic C–H is the most acidic), but it can only undergo the Stevens rearrangement; the ylide on the methyl group is present in small concentration but can react through a concerted [2,3] shift with a low barrier. The competition between the two pathways is determined by the conditions: low temperature and $\ce{NaNH2}$/liquid ammonia favor the Sommelet–Hauser rearrangement (the product forms by the concerted pathway), whereas at higher temperatures, or with an organolithium base in an aprotic solvent, the proportion of the Stevens rearrangement increases (**homolysis** requires a higher activation energy). Repeated Sommelet–Hauser rearrangements can introduce methyl groups into the benzene ring one after another.

### 30.8.4. [2,3]-σ Shifts

The [2,3] shift among the **sigmatropic reactions** ([6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.7)): an allyl group is attached to an atom X bearing a lone pair or negative charge (in $\ce{X-Y-C-C=C}$, X carries a negative charge or a lone pair); through a five-membered cyclic transition state, X bonds to the distal carbon of the allyl group, the allyl double bond shifts, and the Y–C bond breaks. With five atoms and six electrons the process is thermally allowed by the **Woodward–Hoffmann rules**, so [2,3] shifts often take place at low temperature, whereas the corresponding [1,2] shift needs a higher temperature and proceeds through a radical pair. This is why the [2,3] shift is easier than the [1,2] shift: one is a symmetry-allowed concerted process, the other is symmetry-forbidden and can proceed only through a high-energy radical pair; it is not that "participation of the π bond lowers the orbital energy".

**Definition 30.8.4 ([2,3]-Wittig Rearrangement)** The reaction in which the $\alpha'$-carbanion of an allyl ether (formed by deprotonation of the oxygen-bearing carbon on the other side of the allyl group) rearranges to a homoallylic alkoxide by a [2,3]-σ shift is called the **[2,3]-Wittig rearrangement**.

The mechanism of the [2,3]-Wittig rearrangement is shown in [6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.7). It proceeds at $-78\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$; the **carbanion** is usually obtained by deprotonation of a propargylic, benzylic or allylic C–H, or by tin–lithium exchange (the Still–Wittig rearrangement: $\ce{Bu3SnCH2OR}$ with BuLi). The five-membered cyclic transition state adopts an **envelope conformation** with the substituents pseudo-equatorial, so the reaction has excellent **stereoselectivity**: the newly formed double bond is mainly *E*, and the chirality of the allyl group (or the configuration of its double bond) is transferred with high selectivity to the two new adjacent **stereocenters**. The allylic double bond shifts in the product (allylic transposition):

```smiles
CC=CCOCC#C>>C=CC(C)C(O)C#C
```

[2,3] shifts of the same kind:

| Atom bearing the negative charge | Atom attached to the allyl group | Substrate | Product | Name |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C | O | $\alpha'$-Carbanion of an allyl ether | Homoallylic alcohol | [2,3]-Wittig rearrangement |
| C | N⁺ | Allylic ammonium ylide | Homoallylic tertiary amine | [2,3]-Stevens rearrangement (including the Sommelet–Hauser rearrangement) |
| C | S⁺ | Allylic sulfonium ylide (often formed from a metal carbene and an allyl sulfide) | Homoallylic sulfide | Doyle–Kirmse reaction |
| O | N⁺ | Allylic tertiary amine *N*-oxide | *O*-Allylhydroxylamine | Meisenheimer rearrangement |
| O | S⁺ | Allylic sulfoxide | Allyl sulfenate | Mislow–Evans rearrangement |
| O | Se⁺ | Allylic selenoxide | Allyl selenenate → allylic alcohol | Second step of $\ce{SeO2}$ allylic oxidation |

The [2,3] shift of ammonium ylides is often used for nitrogen-containing natural products, because it can create a C–C bond and a nitrogen-bearing stereocenter on a ring at the same time; the [2,3]-Wittig rearrangement of carbanions is often used to build homoallylic alcohol units in oxygen-containing natural products.

**Definition 30.8.5 (Mislow–Evans Rearrangement)** The reversible equilibrium between allylic **sulfoxides** and allyl sulfenates established by a [2,3]-σ shift, together with the method that uses a thiophile to convert the allylic sulfoxide into an allylic alcohol, is called the **Mislow–Evans rearrangement**.

![Mislow–Evans rearrangement: the oxygen of an allylic sulfoxide bonds to the distal carbon of the allyl group, the double bond shifts and the C–S bond breaks, establishing through a five-membered cyclic transition state an equilibrium with the allyl sulfenate; trimethyl phosphite acts as a thiophile and cleaves the O–S bond of the sulfenate, pulling the equilibrium toward the allylic alcohol, with transposition of the double bond](./plots/mislow-evans.py)

The equilibrium lies on the side of the sulfoxide ([6.5.26](/6.5.26#sec-26.9.3)); Mislow (1968) used this equilibrium to explain why optically active allylic sulfoxides racemize at room temperature (the chiral sulfur center is lost through the sulfenate), and Evans (1974) turned it into a synthesis of (*E*)-allylic alcohols by adding $\ce{P(OMe)3}$ or diethylamine to trap the sulfenate. In a chiral sulfoxide, the configuration at sulfur is transferred through the five-membered transition state to the new carbon stereocenter, giving an optically active allylic alcohol.

### 30.8.5. The Favorskii Rearrangement

$\alpha$-Halo ketones ([6.5.21](/6.5.21#sec-21.5)) do not undergo simple substitution in base but rearrange their skeleton:

**Definition 30.8.6 (Favorskii Rearrangement)** The rearrangement in which an $\alpha$-halo ketone having an $\alpha'$-H is treated with hydroxide or an alkoxide, the $\alpha'$-carbanion displaces the halogen intramolecularly to give a cyclopropanone, base adds to the carbonyl group of the cyclopropanone, and the three-membered ring opens to give a carboxylic acid (or ester or amide), is called the **Favorskii rearrangement** (Favorskii, 1894). A cyclic $\alpha$-halo ketone contracts by one carbon.

![Favorskii rearrangement. Top: sodium methoxide removes the α′-H of 2-chlorocyclohexanone on the side remote from chlorine, and the carbon of the enolate displaces chloride intramolecularly to give a cyclopropanone (bicyclo[4.1.0]heptan-7-one). Bottom: methoxide adds to the highly strained carbonyl group, the tetrahedral anion re-forms C=O and breaks one bond of the three-membered ring, releasing the more stable carbanion, which takes a proton from methanol to give methyl cyclopentanecarboxylate](./plots/favorskii.py)

Evidence for the cyclopropanone mechanism:

- Isotopic distribution. Loftfield (1951) rearranged 2-chlorocyclohexanone labeled with ¹⁴C equally at the carbonyl carbon and the $\alpha$ carbon; in the product half of the ¹⁴C is on the ester carbonyl carbon and the other half is divided equally between the ring carbon attached to it and the next carbon (one quarter each), which shows that the original C2 and C6 become equivalent in the intermediate; only a symmetric cyclopropanone can explain this.
- Different substrates give the same product. 1-Chloro-3-phenylpropan-2-one and 1-chloro-1-phenylpropan-2-one both give methyl 3-phenylpropanoate with sodium methoxide, because they form the same phenylcyclopropanone.

```smiles
ClCC(=O)Cc1ccccc1>>COC(=O)CCc1ccccc1
CC(=O)C(Cl)c1ccccc1>>COC(=O)CCc1ccccc1
```

**Regioselectivity** of the ring opening: the tetrahedral anion breaks the bond that gives the more stable **carbanion**. Phenylcyclopropanone breaks the bond to the phenyl-bearing carbon to give a benzylic carbanion, so the product is the 3-phenylpropanoate and not the 2-phenylpropanoate; alkyl-substituted cyclopropanones break on the less substituted side (a primary carbanion is more stable than a tertiary one).

$\alpha$-Halo ketones without an $\alpha'$-H (such as $\alpha$-chlorocyclohexyl phenyl ketone) cannot form a cyclopropanone but still rearrange: base adds directly to the carbonyl group, and a group in the tetrahedral anion migrates to the halogen-bearing carbon with simultaneous loss of halide (a 1,2-shift similar to that of the semipinacol rearrangement, with inversion at the terminus). This is called the quasi-Favorskii rearrangement or semibenzilic rearrangement.

![Quasi-Favorskii rearrangement: hydroxide adds to the carbonyl group of 1-chlorocyclohexyl phenyl ketone; in the tetrahedral anion a lone pair of oxygen re-forms C=O while the phenyl group migrates with its electron pair to the chlorine-bearing carbon, and chloride leaves from the back side; proton transfer gives 1-phenylcyclohexanecarboxylate](./plots/quasi-favorskii.py)

Ring contraction by the Favorskii rearrangement is useful in the synthesis of strained rings. In the synthesis of cubane by Eaton and Cole (1964), two Favorskii ring contractions convert a cage-like dibromo diketone into cubanedicarboxylic acid, and **decarboxylation** then gives cubane. Favorskii rearrangement of an $\alpha,\alpha'$-dihalo ketone, with ring opening followed by loss of HX, gives an $\alpha,\beta$-unsaturated ester.

### 30.8.6. The Fries Rearrangement and the Anionic Fries Rearrangement

In the **Fries rearrangement** ([6.5.15](/6.5.15#sec-15.8.3)), a phenolic ester coordinates to $\ce{AlCl3}$, the C–O bond breaks to give an acylium ion and an aluminum phenoxide, and the acylium ion then undergoes **electrophilic aromatic substitution** at the ortho or para position. Strictly speaking it is a transfer of an acylium ion, and crossover experiments show that it is partly an intermolecular process. Low temperature favors the para product (**kinetic control**), and high temperature favors the ortho product chelated to Al (**thermodynamic control**).

The anionic Fries rearrangement (Snieckus) is an example of an electron-rich rearrangement: an *O*-aryl carbamate $\ce{ArOC(O)NEt2}$ is lithiated at the ortho position by *sec*-butyllithium (**directed ortho metalation**, [6.5.27](/6.5.27#sec-27.2.4)); on warming, the aryllithium adds to the adjacent carbamoyl carbonyl group, and through a cyclic **tetrahedral intermediate** the carbamoyl group migrates from oxygen to carbon, giving an *o*-hydroxybenzamide. The outcome resembles that of the Fries rearrangement (an acyl group moves from O to the ortho C), but the **regioselectivity** is determined entirely by the site of lithiation.

![Anionic Fries rearrangement: on warming, the ortho-lithiated O-aryl carbamate (Li⁺ omitted) adds through its aryl carbanion to the adjacent carbamoyl carbonyl group, giving a four-membered cyclic tetrahedral intermediate; the oxygen anion re-forms C=O and the C–O(aryl) bond breaks, so that the carbamoyl group migrates from oxygen to the ortho carbon, giving the phenoxide of an o-hydroxybenzamide](./plots/anionic-fries.py)

## 30.9. Radical Rearrangements

1,2-Shifts of radicals are much rarer than those of **carbocations**, for the same reason as in 30.8.1: in the three-center, three-electron transition state the extra electron occupies an **antibonding orbital**. H and alkyl groups do not undergo radical 1,2-shifts; the groups that can migrate are those with a π system or an empty orbital that can first bond to the radical center to form a cyclic intermediate: aryl, vinyl, acyl, acyloxy, cyano and silyl groups.

The 1,2-aryl shift proceeds through a spirocyclohexadienyl radical intermediate and is called the neophyl rearrangement (Urry and Kharasch, 1944):

![Neophyl rearrangement: the primary 2-methyl-2-phenylpropyl (neophyl) radical adds to the ipso carbon of its own benzene ring (fishhook arrows, one electron each) to give a spirocyclohexadienyl radical; the other bond of the cyclopropane then breaks, the aromatic ring is restored, and the unpaired electron resides on the tertiary carbon: the phenyl group has migrated from C2 to C1](./plots/neophyl.py)

The acyl radical formed from 3-methyl-3-phenylbutanal and di-*tert*-butyl peroxide loses CO to give the neophyl radical; it is captured by a hydrogen atom donor (the aldehyde itself) to give the unrearranged *tert*-butylbenzene, or it first rearranges to the tertiary radical and then abstracts hydrogen to give isobutylbenzene. The ratio of the two products varies with the concentration of the hydrogen atom donor, from which the rate constant of the rearrangement can be measured (of the order of $10^{3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at 25 °C); this is the idea behind the **radical clock** ([6.5.7](/6.5.7#sec-7.5)). Acyl radicals with no migrating group simply lose CO and do not rearrange. Other radical rearrangements:

- 1,2-Acyloxy shift (the Surzur–Tanner rearrangement): in a $\beta$-(acyloxy)alkyl radical the acyloxy group moves from one carbon to the adjacent carbon through a five-membered cyclic transition state; it is used in carbohydrate chemistry for the synthesis of deoxy sugars.
- The Wieland rearrangement (1911): in the triarylmethoxyl radical $\ce{Ar3CO.}$ an aryl group migrates from carbon to oxygen to give the more stable $\ce{Ar2C.-OAr}$.
- Remote migrations and 1,5-hydrogen atom transfer: alkoxyl and nitrogen radicals abstract hydrogen from the $\delta$ carbon through a six-membered cyclic transition state (intramolecular **hydrogen atom transfer**), moving the radical center to an unactivated carbon. In the Barton reaction (1960), photolysis of a nitrite ester RONO gives an alkoxyl radical, and the carbon radical formed after 1,5-hydrogen transfer combines with NO to give a $\delta$-nitroso alcohol (which tautomerizes to the oxime). Barton used it to functionalize the C18 methyl group of corticosterone acetate in one step and so synthesized aldosterone acetate. The related Hofmann–Löffler–Freytag reaction forms pyrrolidines by 1,5-hydrogen abstraction by the nitrogen radical of an *N*-haloamine.

![Barton reaction: photolysis of amyl nitrite homolyzes the O–NO bond (fishhook arrows) to give an alkoxyl radical and NO; the alkoxyl radical abstracts the hydrogen on the δ carbon through a six-membered cyclic transition state (1,5-hydrogen atom transfer) to give a secondary carbon radical, which combines with NO to give a δ-nitroso alcohol that tautomerizes to the oxime](./plots/barton.py)

In recent years, radical 1,2- and remote functional group migrations (aryl, cyano, alkynyl and imino groups moving from carbon to carbon), combined with **photoredox catalysis** ([6.5.31](/6.5.31#sec-31.5)), have become a class of methods for the difunctionalization of alkenes and remote C–H functionalization.

## 30.10. Concerted Rearrangements: Variants of the Cope and Claisen Rearrangements

The orbital analysis of [3,3]-**sigmatropic shifts**, the chair transition state and the basic examples are given in [6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.5.6). Here we discuss the variants that have made them standard synthetic methods.

### 30.10.1. The Anionic Oxy-Cope Rearrangement

The **Cope rearrangement** of a 3-hydroxy-1,5-diene gives an enol, which tautomerizes to an unsaturated carbonyl compound; the latter step makes the reaction irreversible (the oxy-Cope rearrangement, Berson and Jones, 1964), but temperatures above $200\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ are still required.

**Definition 30.10.1 (Anionic Oxy-Cope Rearrangement)** The [3,3]-σ shift of the alkoxide of a 3-hydroxy-1,5-diene (usually the potassium salt, formed with KH and 18-crown-6) at room temperature or slightly above, which gives an enolate and, on work-up, a $\delta,\varepsilon$-unsaturated carbonyl compound, is called the **anionic oxy-Cope rearrangement** (Evans and Golob, 1975).

The alkoxide accelerates the reaction by a factor of $10^{10}$–$10^{17}$. The reason is that the strong electron-donating effect of $\ce{O-}$ weakens the C3–C4 bond (the bond that breaks in the rearrangement) and gives the transition state some enolate character; the acceleration is greatest when the potassium ion is sequestered by the crown ether and the **ion pair** is separated. The product is a stable **enolate**, which can be alkylated in situ. The anionic oxy-Cope rearrangement is often used for ring expansion: rearrangement of a 1,2-divinylcycloalkanol enlarges the ring by four carbons, so a ten-membered ring can be obtained from a six-membered ring in one step.

```smiles
C=CC(C)(O)CC=C>>CC(=O)CCCC=C
```

![Anionic oxy-Cope rearrangement: the potassium salt of 3-methylhexa-1,5-dien-3-ol undergoes a [3,3]-σ shift at room temperature through a chair six-membered transition state (drawn flat; the dashed line is the C1–C6 bond being formed); the C3–C4 bond weakened by the alkoxide breaks and the two π bonds shift to give an enolate; on work-up, protonation and tautomerization give hept-6-en-2-one](./plots/anionic-oxy-cope.py)

### 30.10.2. The Aza-Cope Rearrangement

Replacing one carbon of a 1,5-diene by nitrogen gives an aza-**Cope rearrangement**. The most useful in practice is the cationic version of the 2-aza-Cope rearrangement: the iminium ion $\ce{C=N+}$ formed from a homoallylic amine and an aldehyde takes part in a [3,3] shift, which proceeds at room temperature. Overman combined it with the **Mannich reaction** ([6.5.21](/6.5.21#sec-21.6)) (the aza-Cope–Mannich reaction): the [3,3] shift produces an enol and a new iminium ion, which immediately undergoes an intramolecular Mannich reaction to give an acyl-substituted pyrrolidine in one step. This tandem reaction was the key step of Overman's synthesis of strychnine (1993).

![Aza-Cope–Mannich reaction (simplest acyclic substrate). Left: the iminium ion formed from formaldehyde and 2-hydroxy-2-methylbut-3-enylmethylamine undergoes a cationic 2-aza-Cope rearrangement ([3,3]; the dashed line is the C–C bond being formed) to give an enol and a new iminium ion. Right: the enol attacks the iminium carbon (intramolecular Mannich reaction), closing a pyrrolidine ring, and loss of a proton gives 3-acetyl-1-methylpyrrolidine](./plots/aza-cope-mannich.py)

### 30.10.3. Variants of the Claisen Rearrangement

Allyl vinyl ethers are not easy to prepare, so the variants of the **Claisen rearrangement** all generate an "allyl vinyl ether"-type structure in situ:

| Variant | Generated in situ | Conditions | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Johnson–Claisen | Allylic alcohol + triethyl orthoacetate → ketene acetal | Propionic acid catalyst, about $140\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ | $\gamma,\delta$-Unsaturated ester |
| Eschenmoser–Claisen | Allylic alcohol + *N*,*N*-dimethylacetamide dimethyl acetal → ketene *N*,*O*-acetal | Heat | $\gamma,\delta$-Unsaturated amide |
| Ireland–Claisen | Allyl ester + LDA, $\ce{Me3SiCl}$ → silyl ketene acetal | $-78\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to room temperature | $\gamma,\delta$-Unsaturated carboxylic acid |
| Aromatic Claisen | Allyl aryl ether | About $200\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ | *o*-Allylphenol ([6.5.12](/6.5.12#sec-12.5.6)) |

```smiles
CC(O)C=C.CCOC(C)(OCC)OCC>>CCOC(=O)CC/C=C/C
```

![Johnson–Claisen rearrangement: but-3-en-2-ol and triethyl orthoacetate exchange under acid catalysis with loss of two molecules of ethanol, generating the ketene acetal CH₂=C(OEt)–O–allyl in situ; it undergoes a [3,3]-σ shift through a six-membered (chair) transition state (the dashed line is the C–C bond being formed), the allylic double bond is transposed, and ethyl (E)-hex-4-enoate is obtained](./plots/johnson-claisen.py)

**Definition 30.10.2 (Ireland–Claisen Rearrangement)** The reaction in which an allyl ester is converted by LDA into its ester enolate, which is trapped on oxygen by chlorotrimethylsilane as a silyl ketene acetal, and the latter undergoes a [3,3]-σ shift near room temperature to give the silyl ester of a $\gamma,\delta$-unsaturated carboxylic acid, which is hydrolyzed to the acid, is called the **Ireland–Claisen rearrangement** (Ireland, 1972).

![Ireland–Claisen rearrangement: allyl propionate gives its ester enolate with LDA at −78 °C, and Me₃SiCl traps it on oxygen to give a silyl ketene acetal (an allyl vinyl ether); at room temperature it rearranges through a six-membered [3,3] transition state (the dashed line is the C–C bond being formed) to give trimethylsilyl 2-methylpent-4-enoate, which is hydrolyzed to the carboxylic acid](./plots/ireland-claisen.py)

The advantages of the Ireland–Claisen rearrangement are mild conditions and controllable stereochemistry: in THF, LDA gives mainly the (*E*)-**enolate** (by Ireland's naming convention), and in THF/HMPA (or DMPU) mainly the (*Z*) isomer; the two silyl ketene acetals pass through chair transition states to give the two diastereomeric products (the *anti* and *syn* 2,3-disubstituted pent-4-enoic acids). From the same allyl ester, a change of solvent alone therefore selects the relative configuration of the two newly formed adjacent **stereocenters**.

### 30.10.4. Other Concerted Rearrangements

- The key step of the **Fischer indole synthesis** ([6.5.25](/6.5.25#sec-25.7.2)) is a [3,3]-**sigmatropic shift** of an ene-hydrazine; an *N*-substituted phenylhydrazine gives an *N*-substituted indole; when the carbonyl $\alpha$ position is disubstituted, aromatization is interrupted and a 3*H*-indole (indolenine) is formed.
- Use of **electrocyclic reactions** in synthesis: in Nicolaou's synthesis of endiandric acids (1982), a conjugated tetraene first undergoes an 8π **conrotatory** electrocyclization and then a 6π **disrotatory** electrocyclization, followed by an intramolecular **Diels–Alder reaction**, building four rings and eight **stereocenters** in one operation and mimicking the possible nonenzymatic biosynthesis in plants.
- The **Claisen rearrangement** in living organisms: chorismate mutase rearranges chorismate to prephenate and accelerates the reaction about $10^6$-fold. The enzyme does not change the pericyclic nature of the reaction; it holds the substrate in a reactive conformation close to the transition state and stabilizes, with positively charged residues, the partial negative charge that develops in the transition state (on the ether oxygen).

## 30.11. Research Frontiers

Research on rearrangements is advancing in three directions. The first is catalytic asymmetric rearrangement: chiral Brønsted acids, chiral **Lewis acids** and metal catalysts make the semipinacol and Meinwald rearrangements and [2,3]- and [3,3]-**sigmatropic shifts** enantioselective routes to quaternary carbon **stereocenters**, which are hard to obtain by addition or substitution. The second is biocatalysis: the **carbocation** cascades of terpene synthases are being redesigned by directed evolution and computation to give terpenoid skeletons not found in nature, and designed enzymes (the "pericyclases" that followed Diels–Alderases and Kemp eliminases) are used to study how enzymes accelerate pericyclic rearrangements. The third is the combination of radical functional group migration with **photoredox catalysis** and electrochemistry ([6.5.31](/6.5.31#sec-31.5)), which turns 1,2- and 1,4-aryl migrations and remote 1,5-**hydrogen atom transfer** into selective methods for C–H and alkene functionalization.

## Summary

- Rearrangements (Definition 30.1.1) fall into four classes by the migration terminus: electron-deficient, electron-rich, radical and concerted (30.1.2). Mechanisms are studied by crossover experiments (the presence or absence of crossover products shows whether the process is intramolecular or intermolecular), isotopic labeling, stereochemistry and CIDNP.
- Electron-deficient 1,2-shifts (30.2): the migrating group moves with its electron pair through a bridged transition state with three centers and two electrons; the configuration of the migrating group is retained, and when migration is concerted with departure the configuration at the terminus is inverted and the groups must be **antiperiplanar**; the migratory aptitude (Definition 30.2.1) is generally electron-rich aryl > aryl > tertiary > secondary > primary alkyl > methyl, with H variable, except that the Beckmann rearrangement is controlled by stereochemistry and the [1,2]-Wittig rearrangement by radical stability.

| Rearrangement | Terminus | Product | Key conditions and features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wagner–Meerwein (Definition 30.3.1) | **Carbocation** | Alkenes, alcohols, halides with a changed skeleton | Formation of a more stable cation, release of **ring strain** |
| Demjanov / Tiffeneau–Demjanov (Definitions 30.3.2, 30.3.3) | Carbon that loses $\ce{N2}$ from the diazonium salt | Ring-expanded alcohol / ring-expanded ketone | $\ce{HNO2}$; the latter gives a single product |
| Semipinacol (Definition 30.3.4), Meinwald (Definition 30.3.5) | Cationic carbon next to the oxygen-bearing carbon | Ketones, aldehydes | The **leaving group** specifies the direction of migration; epoxide + **Lewis acid** |
| Benzilic acid (Definition 30.4.1) | Carbonyl carbon | $\alpha$-Hydroxy carboxylic acids | 1,2-Diketone + $\ce{HO-}$; cyclic diketones contract |
| Beckmann (Definition 30.5.1) | Nitrogen | Amides, lactams | Anti group migrates, configuration retained; caprolactam |
| Hofmann, Curtius, Schmidt, Lossen (Definition 30.6.1) | Nitrogen | Isocyanate → amine with one carbon fewer | Leaving group: $\ce{Br-}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{N2}$, carboxylate |
| Baeyer–Villiger, Hock | Oxygen | Esters, phenols | Tertiary alkyl and aryl groups preferred |
| [1,2]-Wittig, Stevens (Definitions 30.8.1, 30.8.2) | **Carbanion** | Alcohols, amines | Radical pair; migratory aptitude follows radical stability |
| [2,3]-Wittig, Mislow–Evans, Sommelet–Hauser (Definitions 30.8.3–30.8.5) | Concerted [2,3] | Homoallylic alcohols, allylic alcohols, *o*-methylbenzylamines | Six electrons, thermally allowed, low temperature; allylic transposition, chirality transfer |
| Favorskii (Definition 30.8.6) | Cyclopropanone | Ring-contracted carboxylic acids, esters | Requires $\alpha'$-H; ring opens to the more stable carbanion |
| Neophyl rearrangement, Barton reaction | Radical | 1,2-Aryl shift, functionalization at the $\delta$ position | H and alkyl groups do not undergo radical 1,2-shifts |
| Anionic oxy-Cope, Ireland–Claisen (Definitions 30.10.1, 30.10.2) | Concerted [3,3] | Unsaturated carbonyl compounds, $\gamma,\delta$-unsaturated acids | Alkoxide acceleration by more than $10^{10}$; **enolate** geometry controls diastereoselectivity |

- Electron-rich 1,2-shifts are three-center, four-electron systems and are symmetry-forbidden; they proceed only through radical pairs or addition–elimination. The [2,3] shift is symmetry-allowed and is therefore much easier than the [1,2] shift (30.8.1, 30.8.4).

## Questions

1. What is the main product of the reaction of 3-bromo-3-methylbutan-2-one with sodium ethoxide in ethanol? Write the mechanism and explain why ethyl 3-methylbutanoate is not formed.

   **Answer** The main product is ethyl 2,2-dimethylpropanoate (ethyl pivalate). Ethoxide removes a proton from the C1 methyl group (the $\alpha'$ position); C1 of the **enolate** displaces bromide on C3 intramolecularly to give 2,2-dimethylcyclopropanone; $\ce{EtO-}$ adds to the carbonyl group of the cyclopropanone, and when the tetrahedral anion opens it breaks the C2–C1 bond (giving a primary **carbanion**, which is more stable than the tertiary carbanion that would result from breaking the C2–C3 bond); the carbanion takes a proton from ethanol. C3 now carries two methyl groups, the methyl group derived from C1, and the ester group, that is, $\ce{(CH3)3CCOOEt}$. Ethyl 3-methylbutanoate would form only if the C2–C3 bond broke, which requires the less stable tertiary carbanion.

2. When benzophenone oxime (1) is treated with TsCl to undergo the Beckmann rearrangement to *N*-phenylbenzamide (2), an intermediate 3, $\ce{Ph2C=N-O-C(Ph)=NPh}$ (the *O*-imidoyl derivative of the oxime), has been isolated. Under acidic conditions 3 can catalyze the conversion of 1 to 2. Propose a plausible catalytic cycle.

   **Answer** The $\ce{-O-C(Ph)=NPh}$ group on the N–O bond of 3 is a good **leaving group** (after departure it is the **conjugate base** of amide 2, stabilized by amide resonance). (1) Intermediate 3 undergoes the Beckmann rearrangement: the phenyl group anti to the leaving group migrates to nitrogen while $\ce{PhC(=NPh)O-}$ leaves, giving the nitrilium ion $\ce{PhC#N+Ph}$; protonation of the departed anion gives product 2. (2) The nitrilium ion is a strong **electrophile** and is attacked by the oxygen of another molecule of oxime 1; loss of a proton regenerates 3. The net result is 1 → 2, with 3 regenerated in every cycle and thus acting as a catalyst; TsCl is needed only at the start to generate the first nitrilium ions. The Beckmann rearrangement with catalytic amounts of an activator such as cyanuric chloride follows the same cycle of **autocatalysis**.

3. 1-(Hydroxy(phenyl)methyl)-2-butylcyclopropan-1-ol gives 2-phenyl-3-butylcyclobutanone with *p*-toluenesulfonic acid ($\ce{CHCl3}$, room temperature) or $\ce{BF3.OEt2}$. Identify which hydroxyl group leaves and which bond migrates, and explain the driving force of the reaction.

   **Answer** The secondary hydroxyl group at the **benzylic position** is protonated (or coordinated to $\ce{BF3}$) and leaves, giving a benzylic **carbocation** stabilized by the benzene ring; a ring C–C bond on the cyclopropanol carbon migrates with its electron pair to this cationic carbon, the three-membered ring expands to a four-membered ring, the positive charge sits on the carbon bearing the hydroxyl group and is stabilized by oxygen as a protonated carbonyl group, and loss of a proton gives the cyclobutanone. This is a semipinacol rearrangement (Definition 30.3.4). The driving force is mainly the formation of the benzylic cation and, finally, of the C=O bond; the **ring strain** of cyclopropane and cyclobutane is similar (about $115$ and $110\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$), so ring expansion itself releases little strain, but the bent C–C bonds of cyclopropane are electron-rich and have a strong migrating ability (they stabilize an adjacent positive charge like π bonds). Whether the ring bond on the butyl-bearing side or on the other side migrates is decided by which bond is **antiperiplanar** to the leaving group, which also decides the relative configuration of phenyl and butyl in the product (the *cis* product mainly).

4. When an $\alpha$-diazo ketone $\ce{RCOCHN2}$ is heated in methanol, methyl phenylacetate is the main product for R = Ph, whereas 1-methoxypropan-2-one is obtained in larger amounts for R = $\ce{CH3}$. Explain the difference.

   **Answer** After loss of $\ce{N2}$, an $\alpha$-diazo ketone has two competing pathways: R migrates to the diazo carbon to give a ketene (the **Wolff rearrangement**; the ketene is trapped by methanol to give $\ce{RCH2COOCH3}$), or the $\alpha$-carbonyl carbene (or its precursor) inserts into the O–H bond of methanol to give $\ce{RCOCH2OCH3}$. The migratory aptitude of phenyl is far greater than that of methyl (in the migration transition state the migrating group carries partial positive charge, and phenyl can stabilize it through a bridged phenonium-type structure), so for R = Ph the Wolff rearrangement predominates; for R = $\ce{CH3}$ migration is slow and the proportion of O–H insertion increases.

5. Write the products of rearrangement of (*E*)- and (*Z*)-2-methylcyclohexanone oxime with TsCl/pyridine followed by weakly acidic aqueous solution. Why do concentrated sulfuric acid rearrangements of the two oximes often give the same mixture of products?

   **Answer** The group anti to OTs migrates. Of the two groups on the C=N carbon, C2 ($\ce{CHCH3}$) ranks higher than C6 ($\ce{CH2}$). In the (*E*)-oxime OH is anti to C2, so C2 migrates and N is inserted between C1 and C2, giving 7-methylazepan-2-one (methyl next to NH), with retention of configuration at the migrating chiral carbon; in the (*Z*)-oxime OH is anti to C6, so C6 migrates, giving 3-methylazepan-2-one (methyl next to the carbonyl group). During oxime formation the OH is generally on the less hindered side, away from the methyl group, that is, the (*E*)-oxime is mainly formed, so the usual main product is 7-methylazepan-2-one. In concentrated sulfuric acid the protonated oxime can interconvert *E* and *Z* by rotation about C=N (through protonated intermediates) or by addition–elimination; the two oximes have equilibrated before rearrangement, and the product ratio is determined by the equilibrium and by the rearrangement rates of the two isomers, independent of the configuration of the starting oxime.

6. (*E*)-Crotyl benzyl ether $\ce{PhCH2OCH2CH=CHCH3}$ reacts with butyllithium at $-78\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to give mainly 2-methyl-1-phenylbut-3-en-1-ol; at higher temperatures 1-phenylpent-3-en-1-ol also appears. State which rearrangement gives each product and why the effect of temperature is so marked.

   **Answer** Butyllithium removes a benzylic proton to give $\ce{PhCH(-)-O-CH2CH=CHCH3}$. In the [2,3]-Wittig rearrangement (Definition 30.8.4) the carbanion bonds to the methyl-bearing distal carbon of the allyl group, the O–$\ce{CH2}$ bond breaks and the double bond moves to the terminal position, giving $\ce{PhCH(OH)CH(CH3)CH=CH2}$, that is, 2-methyl-1-phenylbut-3-en-1-ol (allylic transposition). In the [1,2]-Wittig rearrangement (Definition 30.8.1) the O–$\ce{CH2}$ bond undergoes **homolysis**, and the crotyl radical recombines with carbon through its original $\ce{CH2}$ terminus to give $\ce{PhCH(OH)CH2CH=CHCH3}$, that is, 1-phenylpent-3-en-1-ol (no transposition). The [2,3] shift is a five-atom, six-electron **pericyclic reaction**, thermally allowed with a low barrier, so it proceeds at $-78\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and has good **stereoselectivity**; the concerted pathway of the [1,2] shift is a symmetry-forbidden four-electron process and can proceed only through the radical pair formed by homolysis, which has a high activation energy, so it competes with the [2,3] shift only at higher temperatures.

7. Benzyltrimethylammonium iodide reacts with $\ce{NaNH2}$ in liquid ammonia to give mainly *N*,*N*-dimethyl-2-methylbenzylamine and not the Stevens product $\ce{PhCH(CH3)NMe2}$ of the benzylic ylide. Why, when the benzylic C–H is clearly more acidic than the methyl C–H?

   **Answer** The benzylic ylide and the methyl ylide interconvert rapidly by proton transfer, and the benzylic ylide is present in larger concentration, but it can undergo only the Stevens rearrangement: the migrating group is methyl, and homolysis of the N–$\ce{CH3}$ bond to give a methyl radical has a very high barrier. The methyl ylide, although present in only a small amount, can attack the ortho position of the benzene ring by a concerted, thermally allowed [2,3] shift (the Sommelet–Hauser rearrangement, Definition 30.8.3), which has a much lower barrier; as it is consumed, the equilibrium continually replenishes it. According to the **Curtin–Hammett principle**, the product ratio is determined by the transition-state energies of the two pathways and not by the equilibrium concentrations of the two ylides. At higher temperature, or in an aprotic solvent, the proportion of the Stevens product increases.

8. What is obtained when 3-methylhexa-1,5-dien-3-ol is treated with KH and 18-crown-6 in THF at room temperature and then worked up with dilute acid? What conditions would be needed, and what would result, if the alcohol itself were heated without KH?

   **Answer** The alkoxide undergoes the anionic oxy-Cope rearrangement (Definition 30.10.1): the C3–C4 bond breaks and the C1–C6 bond forms to give the enolate $\ce{CH3C(O-)=CHCH2CH2CH=CH2}$, and protonation and tautomerization give hept-6-en-2-one. The alcohol itself can also undergo the (neutral) oxy-**Cope rearrangement** to the same product, but only on heating at about $200$–$250\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, in competition with retro-ene-type fragmentation (giving acrolein-type fragments); the alkoxide accelerates the [3,3] shift by more than $10^{10}$-fold, so that it is complete at room temperature.

9. 3-Methyl-3-phenylbutanal and a small amount of di-*tert*-butyl peroxide are heated at reflux in benzene, giving a mixture of *tert*-butylbenzene and isobutylbenzene. The higher the concentration of the aldehyde, the higher the proportion of *tert*-butylbenzene. Explain, and describe how the rate constant of the rearrangement of the neophyl radical can be obtained from this.

   **Answer** A *tert*-butoxyl radical abstracts the aldehyde hydrogen to give an acyl radical, which loses CO to give the neophyl radical $\ce{PhC(CH3)2CH2.}$. The neophyl radical has two competing pathways: it abstracts hydrogen directly from the aldehyde (rate $k_{\mathrm{H}}[\text{RCHO}]$) to give *tert*-butylbenzene, or it first undergoes a 1,2-phenyl shift (rate constant $k_{\mathrm{r}}$, independent of concentration) to give the tertiary radical $\ce{(CH3)2C.CH2Ph}$, which then abstracts hydrogen to give isobutylbenzene. Hence $[\text{tert-butylbenzene}]/[\text{isobutylbenzene}] = k_{\mathrm{H}}[\text{RCHO}]/k_{\mathrm{r}}$, and the higher the aldehyde concentration, the more unrearranged product. A plot of the product ratio against aldehyde concentration is a straight line with slope $k_{\mathrm{H}}/k_{\mathrm{r}}$; if $k_{\mathrm{H}}$ is known (the rate constant of hydrogen transfer for similar aldehydes), $k_{\mathrm{r}}$ follows. This is the **radical clock** method ([6.5.7](/6.5.7#sec-7.5)).
