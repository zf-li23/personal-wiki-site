Organic chemistry studies the compounds of carbon: their structures, properties, reactions and synthesis. Carbon forms stable covalent bonds with itself and with elements such as hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, joining into chains, rings and a variety of three-dimensional structures, so the number of organic compounds far exceeds the number of compounds of all other elements combined, and drugs, materials, dyes and the vast majority of the molecules of life belong to this class. The core idea of organic chemistry is that structure determines properties: the distribution of electrons in a molecule and the spatial arrangement of its atoms determine where and in what way the molecule reacts. The core questions of this discipline are: how to describe the structure and stereochemistry of organic molecules; why a reaction occurs, by what pathway it proceeds and which product it gives; and how to combine these reactions to synthesize a target molecule.

This discipline follows the teaching order of Organic Chemistry H(1) and H(2) while also covering the content of Organic Chemistry B. It does not list reactions by class of compound; instead it takes the **reaction mechanism** as its main line and reduces reactions to a few patterns of electron flow. Chapters 1–6 are the foundations: nomenclature and structure, molecular orbitals and electronic effects, organic acids and bases and the energetics of reactions, conformation and configuration, and the determination of structure by spectroscopy. Chapters 7–12 present several basic classes of mechanisms: radical reactions, nucleophilic substitution and elimination at saturated carbon, alcohols, ethers and organometallic reagents, addition to unsaturated hydrocarbons, and **pericyclic reactions**, which proceed through cyclic transition states. Chapters 13–15 are aromatic compounds: the criteria for **aromaticity**, electrophilic and nucleophilic substitution on aromatic rings, benzyne and phenols. Chapters 16–22 revolve around the carbonyl group: nucleophilic addition, reduction and oxidation of aldehydes and ketones, nucleophilic acyl substitution of carboxylic acids and their derivatives, and enolates and carbonyl condensations; this is the largest part of the whole course and the one most suited to being unified by a single set of mechanisms. Chapters 23–29 take up in turn nitrogen-containing compounds and heterocycles, compounds of phosphorus, sulfur and other main-group elements, and organotransition-metal compounds and the reactions they catalyze. Chapters 30–31 are rearrangements and reactions driven by light, electricity and mechanical force. The last two chapters discuss carbohydrates and amino acids, peptides and nucleic acids, with emphasis on their chemical properties: structure, reactions, synthesis and characterization.

Organic chemistry starts from the theory of the chemical bond in [Inorganic Chemistry](/6.1), where **hybrid orbitals**, resonance and acid–base theory become everyday tools for analyzing reactions; it uses the thermodynamics and kinetics of [Physical Chemistry](/6.3) to discuss the direction and rate of reactions, determines structures with the ultraviolet, infrared, nuclear magnetic resonance and mass spectrometry of [Analytical Chemistry](/6.4), and the systematic theory of molecular orbitals and symmetry is in [Structural Chemistry](/6.2). The Hückel molecular orbital method reduces problems of conjugated systems to eigenvalue problems of real symmetric matrices from [Linear Algebra](/2.1), which can be solved in a few lines of code with [Scientific Computing with Python](/4.4). Organic chemistry is a direct prerequisite of [Chemical Biology](/6.6): the chemical reactions used to label, modify and intervene in biomolecules are all built on the mechanisms treated here. In [Biology](/7), the structures and reactions of sugars, amino acids and nucleic acids are the starting point of [Biochemistry](/7.1), which emphasizes how these chemical properties determine the functions of the molecules in life; the catalytic mechanisms of enzymes can likewise be understood with the nucleophilic substitution, nucleophilic acyl substitution and acid–base catalysis found here.

"Is there a chance? Organic won't give you one" is a Chinese pun: *yǒu jīhuì* ("to have a chance") begins with the same syllables as *yǒujī* ("organic"), so the saying works as a rhyming joke about the subject. It reflects the experience of the great majority of biology majors who take Organic Chemistry B. The course covers a very wide range but lacks a clear logic, so there is almost no chance of truly understanding it, and in the end it turns into a contest of memory: students memorize diagrams of reaction mechanisms by rote to get through the exam, and most forget them once it is over. Organic chemistry itself is also truly an enormous discipline: anyone with a friend who was a chemistry olympiad contestant has probably seen them carrying a thick white book. This is also why this discipline is divided into so many chapters, and why the last two chapters also discuss the structures of biomolecules.
