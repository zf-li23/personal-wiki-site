This course studies how data that outlive a single program run are described, queried and kept correct. A **database management system** stands between the applications and the data and makes four promises: a precise description of the data in a **schema**, a declarative language in which users say what they want rather than how to find it, efficient access to data far larger than main memory, and correctness when many users work at once and when the machine crashes. The core questions follow from these promises: how data should be modeled and how a good schema is recognized, how a declarative query is turned into an efficient computation, and how concurrent and failing executions can be made to look serial and atomic. The scope of the chapters follows the course's teaching calendar, and the presentation follows current SQL and PostgreSQL, with SQLite and DuckDB for small runnable examples; one gene-expression database runs through the whole course as its example.

The chapters first look at a database from the outside and then open it up. After the introduction, which sets out the vocabulary and the architecture of a database system, the **relational model** represents all data as relations and answers queries with the operations of **relational algebra**; **SQL** puts the model into practice, first with table definitions and single query blocks, then with subqueries, recursion, window functions, views, access rights and the safe use of SQL from programs. Design comes next: the **entity–relationship model** describes the application domain in diagrams and translates them into relation schemas, and integrity constraints together with the theory of **functional dependencies** and **normalization** give the formal criterion that a schema records every fact once and only legal states at all. From storage onward the view is that of the system's implementer. Files are divided into pages held in a **buffer pool**, an **index** such as the **B+ tree** provides access paths besides the scan, and the query processor chooses, by a cost model and statistics, among the many equivalent plans and operator algorithms for one query. A **transaction** groups operations that must appear to run alone and all or nothing; recovery by **write-ahead logging** makes the second guarantee hold across crashes, and concurrency control by **two-phase locking**, timestamps or **multiversion concurrency control** makes the first hold when transactions interleave. The last chapter reassembles the pieces in the way of the course project, a single-user relational DBMS with its own SQL parser and file storage, and then surveys distributed, NoSQL, analytical and vector databases and the use of databases in data analysis and bioinformatics.

The relational model rests on the sets, relations and logic of [Set Theory](/1.2) and [Mathematical Logic](/1.1). Indexes and query algorithms reuse the B-trees, hash tables and sorting of [Data Structures and Algorithms](/4.3), now measured in disk pages, and readers who know the data frames of [Scientific Computing with Python](/4.4) will recognize joins and grouping. The SQL front end of a DBMS is an application of the grammars and parsers of [Formal Languages and Automata](/4.8). The buffer pool, logging and locks are the database counterparts of the paging, journaling and synchronization of [Operating Systems](/4.11), with the transaction as the new unit of protection. In the other direction, [Software Engineering](/4.14) uses databases as the storage layer of web back ends and of large Internet services, and [Bioinformatics](/7.9) is where most readers of this course will query biological data in practice.

I have not taken this course. In my research I do build databases, but they are usually static, built to present data and at most to offer downloads, far simpler than the relational databases this course is about: there are no concurrent writes, let alone transactions or recovery. I compiled this material because I may later take on building research infrastructure, where a database must be read and written interactively by many users. That is when normalized schema design, indexes and query optimization, and transaction isolation levels stop being textbook concepts and become engineering decisions that have to be made correctly.
