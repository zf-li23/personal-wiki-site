[3.4.10](/3.4.10) briefly introduced the **support vector machine** at the end of its discussion of discriminant analysis: among all hyperplanes that separate two classes, choose the one with the largest margin. This chapter derives it in full. The idea of the support vector machine has three layers: when the two classes are separable, maximizing the margin gives a convex quadratic program; when they are not separable, slack variables are introduced to allow a few errors, which is equivalent to minimizing the hinge loss plus a regularization term; and when a nonlinear boundary is needed, the property that the dual problem contains "only inner products" allows a kernel function to replace the inner product, so that linear classification is carried out in a high-dimensional or even infinite-dimensional feature space without computing the features explicitly. The Lagrange duality and KKT conditions used in the derivation are the basic tools of constrained optimization, and this chapter gives their general form. Finally we introduce the SMO algorithm for solving the dual problem. From the mid-1990s to around 2010, support vector machines were among the most influential classification methods; today they remain a reliable baseline on small and medium-sized tabular and text data, while large-scale perception tasks are dominated by deep learning ([4.5.4](/4.5.4), [4.7](/4.7)).

## 5.1. Statistical Learning and Linear Classification

### 5.1.1. The Framework of Statistical Learning

Simon said: "If a system can improve its performance by executing some process, this is learning." Statistical learning is machine learning in which a computer system improves its performance using data and statistical methods: starting from data, abstract a model of the data, and then use it for prediction and analysis on new data. Its basic setting is:

- an input $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{X}$ and an output $y \in \mathcal{Y}$, with an unknown target function $f\colon \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ (or conditional distribution $\mathbb{P}(y \mid \boldsymbol{x})$);
- a training set $T = \{(\boldsymbol{x}_1, y_1), \dots, (\boldsymbol{x}_N, y_N)\}$ generated by $f$, possibly with noise;
- a hypothesis space $\mathcal{H}$, the set of candidate models; the learning algorithm uses $T$ to choose from $\mathcal{H}$ a $g \approx f$.

A statistical learning method thus consists of three parts: the model (what the hypothesis space is, such as linear functions or decision trees), the strategy (by what criterion the model is chosen), and the algorithm (how to solve the optimization problem given by the strategy).

**Definition 5.1.1 (Empirical Risk Minimization)** Given a **loss function** $L(y, g(\boldsymbol{x}))$, the average loss $R_{\mathrm{emp}}(g) = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N} L(y_i, g(\boldsymbol{x}_i))$ of a model $g$ on the training set is called the empirical risk. The strategy of choosing in the hypothesis space the model that minimizes the empirical risk is called **empirical risk minimization**; the strategy of adding to the empirical risk a regularization term $\lambda J(g)$ that penalizes model complexity and choosing the model that minimizes $R_{\mathrm{emp}}(g) + \lambda J(g)$ is called **structural risk minimization**.

When samples are few and the model is complex, empirical risk minimization easily leads to **overfitting**; structural risk minimization trades off fit against complexity through the regularization term, and **ridge regression** ([3.3.12](/3.3.12)) is an example. We will see later that the soft-margin support vector machine is exactly structural risk minimization with the hinge loss as the loss and $\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2$ as the regularization term. According to whether the training data are labeled, statistical learning is divided into **supervised learning**, **unsupervised learning**, semi-supervised learning, and so on; this chapter and the next are both about supervised classification.

### 5.1.2. Linear Separability and Margins

Consider binary classification: $\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathcal{Y} = \{+1, -1\}$, with $+1$ the positive class and $-1$ the negative class. The hyperplane $\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b = 0$ divides the space into two halves, and the corresponding decision function is $g(\boldsymbol{x}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b)$.

**Definition 5.1.2 (Linear Separability)** If there exists a hyperplane $(\boldsymbol{w}, b)$ such that all positive examples satisfy $\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b > 0$ and all negative examples satisfy $\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b < 0$, that is, $y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b) > 0$ for all $i$, the training set is called **linearly separable**.

When the data are linearly separable, there are infinitely many hyperplanes separating the two classes (the perceptron finds an arbitrary one of them). Intuitively, the hyperplane that is as far as possible from the samples of both classes is the most reliable: a new sample will not be misclassified under a small perturbation. There are two measures of how far a point is from a hyperplane.

**Definition 5.1.3 (Functional Margin and Geometric Margin)** The **functional margin** and **geometric margin** of a hyperplane $(\boldsymbol{w}, b)$ with respect to a sample point $(\boldsymbol{x}_i, y_i)$ are, respectively,

$$
\hat\gamma_i = y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b), \qquad \gamma_i = y_i\Bigl(\frac{\boldsymbol{w}}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + \frac{b}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|}\Bigr) = \frac{\hat\gamma_i}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|}.
$$
The functional margin and geometric margin of the hyperplane with respect to the training set $T$ are $\hat\gamma = \min_i \hat\gamma_i$ and $\gamma = \min_i \gamma_i$, respectively; the geometric margin $\gamma$ is called, for short, the **margin** of the hyperplane with respect to the training set.

When a sample is correctly classified, the geometric margin is the distance from the point to the hyperplane; when it is misclassified, it is the negative of that distance. The size of the functional margin has no meaning: multiplying $(\boldsymbol{w}, b)$ by $2$ leaves the hyperplane unchanged but doubles the functional margin. The geometric margin is unaffected by such scaling, $\gamma = \hat\gamma / \|\boldsymbol{w}\|$.

## 5.2. The Hard-Margin Support Vector Machine

### 5.2.1. Margin Maximization

We look for the separating hyperplane with the largest geometric margin:

$$
\max_{\boldsymbol{w}, b}\ \frac{\hat\gamma}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b) \ge \hat\gamma, \quad i = 1, \dots, N.
$$
Since scaling $(\boldsymbol{w}, b)$ does not change the problem, we may take $\hat\gamma = 1$; and maximizing $1/\|\boldsymbol{w}\|$ is equivalent to minimizing $\frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2$. This gives

**Definition 5.2.1 (Hard-Margin Support Vector Machine)** For a linearly separable training set, solve the convex quadratic program

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{w}, b}\ \frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b) \ge 1, \quad i = 1, \dots, N,
$$
obtain the optimal solution $(\boldsymbol{w}^*, b^*)$, and classify with the separating hyperplane $\boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b^* = 0$ and the decision function $g(\boldsymbol{x}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b^*)$. This model is called the linearly separable support vector machine, or the **hard-margin SVM**. The sample points for which the constraint holds with equality, $y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b^*) = 1$, are called **support vectors**.

The support vectors lie on the two hyperplanes $\boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b^* = \pm 1$, and the band between them has width $2/\|\boldsymbol{w}^*\|$ and is called the margin band. The separating hyperplane is determined only by the support vectors: if a point outside the margin band is moved or deleted, the solution does not change as long as it does not enter the margin band. This differs from LDA, which uses all samples through the means and covariances ([3.4.10](/3.4.10)).

![The maximum-margin separating line (solid) and margin boundaries (dashed) on linearly separable data; the circled points are the support vectors, which lie exactly on the margin boundaries, and the other samples do not affect the solution](./plots/max-margin.py)

**Theorem 5.2.1 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Maximum-Margin Separating Hyperplane)** If the training set is linearly separable (and both classes are nonempty), the optimization problem in Definition 5.2.1 has a unique solution.

**Proof** Existence: when the data are linearly separable, suitably scaling a separating hyperplane satisfies the constraints, so the feasible region is nonempty. The objective $\frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2$ is continuous, and in the sublevel set $\{\|\boldsymbol{w}\| \le r\}$ the feasible $b$ are also bounded (take a positive example $\boldsymbol{x}_p$ and a negative example $\boldsymbol{x}_q$; the constraints give $1 - \boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_p \le b \le -1 - \boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_q$), so the minimum is attained on a compact set. Since both classes are nonempty, $\boldsymbol{w}^* \ne \boldsymbol{0}$ in an optimal solution (when $\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{0}$ the constraints would require $b \ge 1$ and $-b \ge 1$, which is impossible).

Uniqueness: let $(\boldsymbol{w}_1, b_1)$ and $(\boldsymbol{w}_2, b_2)$ both be optimal solutions, with $\|\boldsymbol{w}_1\| = \|\boldsymbol{w}_2\| = c$. The feasible region is convex, so the midpoint $(\boldsymbol{w}, b) = \bigl(\frac{\boldsymbol{w}_1 + \boldsymbol{w}_2}{2}, \frac{b_1 + b_2}{2}\bigr)$ is also feasible, and hence $c \le \|\boldsymbol{w}\| \le \frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}_1\| + \frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}_2\| = c$. Equality in the triangle inequality shows that $\boldsymbol{w}_1 = \lambda \boldsymbol{w}_2$ with $\lambda > 0$, and equal norms force $\lambda = 1$, that is, $\boldsymbol{w}_1 = \boldsymbol{w}_2 = \boldsymbol{w}^*$. Next we show $b_1 = b_2$: for $(\boldsymbol{w}^*, b_1)$, some positive example must make its constraint hold with equality, since otherwise slightly decreasing $b_1$ and then slightly shrinking $(\boldsymbol{w}^*, b_1)$ would remain feasible, contradicting the minimality of $\|\boldsymbol{w}^*\|$; likewise some negative example must make its constraint hold with equality. Then $b_1 = 1 - \min_{y_i = +1}\boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i = -1 - \max_{y_i = -1}\boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i$ is uniquely determined by $\boldsymbol{w}^*$, and the same holds for $b_2$. $\square$

### 5.2.2. Lagrange Duality and KKT Conditions

The hard-margin problem can be solved directly by quadratic programming, but passing to the dual problem has two benefits: the dual problem is easier to solve (§5.5), and it leads naturally to the kernel trick (§5.4). Consider first the general constrained optimization problem (the primal problem):

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\ f_0(\boldsymbol{x}) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f_i(\boldsymbol{x}) \le 0\ (i = 1, \dots, m), \quad h_j(\boldsymbol{x}) = 0\ (j = 1, \dots, p),
$$
with optimal value denoted $p^*$. Following the **method of Lagrange multipliers**, introduce a multiplier for each constraint and define the Lagrangian

$$
L(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta}) = f_0(\boldsymbol{x}) + \sum_{i=1}^{m} \alpha_i f_i(\boldsymbol{x}) + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j h_j(\boldsymbol{x}), \qquad \alpha_i \ge 0.
$$
For a fixed $\boldsymbol{x}$ that satisfies all the constraints, $\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha} \ge \boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{\beta}} L = f_0(\boldsymbol{x})$ (taking $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \boldsymbol{0}$); if it violates some constraint, the corresponding multiplier can be arbitrarily large and $\max L = +\infty$. So the primal problem is equivalent to the min–max problem $p^* = \min_{\boldsymbol{x}} \max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha} \ge \boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{\beta}} L(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta})$. Exchanging the order of min and max gives

**Definition 5.2.2 (Dual Problem)** The function $d(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \inf_{\boldsymbol{x}} L(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta})$ is called the Lagrange dual function. The problem

$$
\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta}}\ d(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta}) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \boldsymbol{\alpha} \ge \boldsymbol{0}
$$
is called the (Lagrange) **dual problem** of the primal problem, and its optimal value is denoted $d^*$.

The dual function is the infimum of a family of affine functions of $(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta})$ and is therefore always concave; whether or not the primal problem is convex, the dual problem is a convex optimization problem.

**Theorem 5.2.2 (Weak Duality)** $d^* \le p^*$.

**Proof** For any feasible $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}$ and any $\boldsymbol{\alpha} \ge \boldsymbol{0}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, we have $\alpha_i f_i(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}) \le 0$ and $h_j(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}) = 0$, so $d(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta}) \le L(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta}) \le f_0(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})$. Taking the supremum over $(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\beta})$ on the left and the infimum over $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}$ on the right gives the claim. $\square$

This is just the general max–min inequality $\max_y \min_x L \le \min_x \max_y L$. When $d^* = p^*$ we say that strong duality holds, and then the primal problem can be solved by solving the dual problem. Strong duality does not hold in general, but for convex problems there is a simple sufficient condition:

**Theorem 5.2.3 (Slater's Condition)** Let $f_0, f_1, \dots, f_m$ be convex functions and $h_j$ affine functions. If there is a strictly feasible point, that is, a point $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}$ with $f_i(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}) < 0$ for all $i$ and $h_j(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}) = 0$ for all $j$, then strong duality holds, and if $d^* > -\infty$ the optimal value of the dual problem is attained. If the inequality constraints are all affine, it suffices that the feasible region be nonempty.

When strong duality holds, the relationship between a primal optimal solution and a dual optimal solution is characterized by the following conditions.

**Definition 5.2.3 (KKT Conditions)** Let $f_i$ and $h_j$ be differentiable. We say that $(\boldsymbol{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*)$ satisfies the **KKT conditions** (Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions) if

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}} L(\boldsymbol{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = \boldsymbol{0} && \text{(stationarity)}\\
& f_i(\boldsymbol{x}^*) \le 0, \quad h_j(\boldsymbol{x}^*) = 0 && \text{(primal feasibility)}\\
& \alpha_i^* \ge 0 && \text{(dual feasibility)}\\
& \alpha_i^* f_i(\boldsymbol{x}^*) = 0 && \text{(complementary slackness)}
\end{aligned}
$$
hold for all $i = 1, \dots, m$ and $j = 1, \dots, p$.

**Theorem 5.2.4** (1) If strong duality holds, $\boldsymbol{x}^*$ is a primal optimal solution, $(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*)$ is a dual optimal solution, and all the functions are differentiable, then they satisfy the KKT conditions. (2) If the primal problem is convex ($f_0, f_i$ convex, $h_j$ affine), then $\boldsymbol{x}^*$ and $(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*)$ satisfying the KKT conditions are primal and dual optimal solutions, respectively, and strong duality holds.

**Proof** (1) By strong duality and the chain of inequalities in the proof of weak duality,

$$
f_0(\boldsymbol{x}^*) = d(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) \le L(\boldsymbol{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = f_0(\boldsymbol{x}^*) + \sum_i \alpha_i^* f_i(\boldsymbol{x}^*) \le f_0(\boldsymbol{x}^*),
$$
so both inequalities are equalities. The second equality gives $\sum_i \alpha_i^* f_i(\boldsymbol{x}^*) = 0$, and since every term is $\le 0$, every term is zero, which is complementary slackness; the first equality says that $\boldsymbol{x}^*$ is a minimizer of $L(\cdot, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*)$, so its gradient is zero. Feasibility is given. (2) In a convex problem $\alpha_i^* \ge 0$ and $L(\cdot, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*)$ is a convex function; a zero gradient shows that $\boldsymbol{x}^*$ is its global minimizer, so $d(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = L(\boldsymbol{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = f_0(\boldsymbol{x}^*)$ (by complementary slackness and primal feasibility). By weak duality, the primal objective value of $\boldsymbol{x}^*$ equals the dual objective value of $(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*)$, and both are optimal. $\square$

The meaning of complementary slackness is: if a constraint is inactive at the optimal solution ($f_i(\boldsymbol{x}^*) < 0$), the corresponding multiplier is 0; if the multiplier is positive, the constraint must hold with equality.

### 5.2.3. The Dual Problem of the Support Vector Machine

Write the constraints of the hard-margin problem as $1 - y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b) \le 0$; the Lagrangian is

$$
L(\boldsymbol{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i\bigl(1 - y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b)\bigr), \qquad \alpha_i \ge 0.
$$
The constraints are affine and the feasible region is nonempty, so strong duality holds by Theorem 5.2.3. To find the dual function, take the partial derivatives with respect to $\boldsymbol{w}$ and $b$ and set them to zero:

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}} L = \boldsymbol{w} - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i \boldsymbol{x}_i = \boldsymbol{0}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i = 0.
$$
(If $\sum_i \alpha_i y_i \ne 0$, $L$ is unbounded below in $b$ and the dual function is $-\infty$, so this is a constraint of the dual problem.) Substituting back into $L$ gives

$$
d(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = -\frac12 \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\boldsymbol{x}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_j) + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i.
$$
Changing the maximization to the minimization of its negative gives the dual problem of the hard-margin support vector machine:

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\boldsymbol{x}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_j) - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i = 0, \quad \alpha_i \ge 0. \tag{5.1}
$$

Let $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*$ be its optimal solution. By the KKT conditions (Theorem 5.2.4):

- stationarity gives $\boldsymbol{w}^* = \sum_i \alpha_i^* y_i \boldsymbol{x}_i$;
- since $\boldsymbol{w}^* \ne \boldsymbol{0}$, at least one $\alpha_j^* > 0$, and by complementary slackness $y_j(\boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_j + b^*) = 1$; multiplying both sides by $y_j$ ($y_j^2 = 1$) gives

$$
b^* = y_j - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i^* y_i (\boldsymbol{x}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_j).
$$

The decision function is therefore

$$
g(\boldsymbol{x}) = \operatorname{sgn}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i^* y_i (\boldsymbol{x}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{x}) + b^*\Bigr).
$$
By complementary slackness, a sample with $\alpha_i^* > 0$ must be a support vector; the converse does not necessarily hold: in degenerate cases, a sample lying exactly on the margin boundary may also have $\alpha_i^* = 0$. So the samples with $\alpha_i^* > 0$ are usually called the support vectors, and they completely determine $\boldsymbol{w}^*$ and $b^*$. Moreover, both training and prediction use only the inner products $\boldsymbol{x}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_j$ between samples, which is the starting point of §5.4.

### 5.2.4. An Example

**Example 5.2.1** The positive examples are $\boldsymbol{x}_1 = (2, 2)$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_2 = (3, 3)$ and the negative example is $\boldsymbol{x}_3 = (0, 1)$. Find the hard-margin support vector machine.

The inner products are $\boldsymbol{x}_1 \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_1 = 8$, $\boldsymbol{x}_1 \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_2 = 12$, $\boldsymbol{x}_1 \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_3 = 2$, $\boldsymbol{x}_2 \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_2 = 18$, $\boldsymbol{x}_2 \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_3 = 3$, $\boldsymbol{x}_3 \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_3 = 1$. The dual problem (5.1) is

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ \frac12\bigl(8\alpha_1^2 + 18\alpha_2^2 + \alpha_3^2 + 24\alpha_1\alpha_2 - 4\alpha_1\alpha_3 - 6\alpha_2\alpha_3\bigr) - \alpha_1 - \alpha_2 - \alpha_3
$$
with constraints $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 - \alpha_3 = 0$ and $\alpha_i \ge 0$. Substituting $\alpha_3 = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2$, the objective becomes

$$
s(\alpha_1, \alpha_2) = \frac52\alpha_1^2 + \frac{13}{2}\alpha_2^2 + 8\alpha_1\alpha_2 - 2\alpha_1 - 2\alpha_2.
$$
Setting the partial derivatives to zero: $5\alpha_1 + 8\alpha_2 = 2$ and $8\alpha_1 + 13\alpha_2 = 2$, whose solution is $\alpha_1 = 10$, $\alpha_2 = -6$, violating $\alpha_2 \ge 0$, so the minimum is on the boundary. For $\alpha_2 = 0$, $s = \frac52\alpha_1^2 - 2\alpha_1$, with minimum $-\frac25$ at $\alpha_1 = \frac25$; for $\alpha_1 = 0$, $s = \frac{13}{2}\alpha_2^2 - 2\alpha_2$, with minimum $-\frac{2}{13} > -\frac25$ at $\alpha_2 = \frac{2}{13}$. Therefore

$$
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^* = \Bigl(\frac25, 0, \frac25\Bigr), \quad \boldsymbol{w}^* = \frac25(2, 2) - \frac25(0, 1) = \Bigl(\frac45, \frac25\Bigr), \quad b^* = 1 - \boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_1 = -\frac75.
$$
The separating hyperplane is $\frac45 x^{(1)} + \frac25 x^{(2)} - \frac75 = 0$, that is, $4x^{(1)} + 2x^{(2)} = 7$, and the support vectors are $\boldsymbol{x}_1$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_3$. Check: $\boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_3 + b^* = -1$ and $\boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_2 + b^* = \frac{11}{5} > 1$; the margin is $1/\|\boldsymbol{w}^*\| = \sqrt5/2$, exactly half the distance between $\boldsymbol{x}_1$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_3$, and $\boldsymbol{w}^*$ has the same direction as $\boldsymbol{x}_1 - \boldsymbol{x}_3 = (2, 1)$. The program in §5.5 gives the same result.

## 5.3. The Soft-Margin Support Vector Machine

### 5.3.1. Slack Variables

Real data are often not linearly separable, or, even when separable, only a very narrow margin can be obtained because of a few noisy points. We allow some samples not to satisfy "functional margin at least 1": introduce a slack variable $\xi_i \ge 0$ for each sample, relax the constraint to $y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i$, and at the same time penalize the total slack in the objective.

**Definition 5.3.1 (Soft-Margin Support Vector Machine)** Solve the convex quadratic program

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\xi}}\ \frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^{N} \xi_i \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i, \quad \xi_i \ge 0, \quad i = 1, \dots, N,
$$
where $C > 0$ is the penalty parameter, and classify with $g(\boldsymbol{x}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b^*)$. This model is called the linear support vector machine, or the **soft-margin SVM**.

A sample with $\xi_i = 0$ lies outside the margin band or on its boundary; a sample with $0 < \xi_i \le 1$ lies inside the margin band but is still correctly classified; a sample with $\xi_i > 1$ is misclassified. So $\sum_i \xi_i$ is an upper bound on the number of training errors. $C$ balances the two objectives: when $C$ is large, violations of the margin are penalized heavily, the margin is narrow, and there are fewer training errors, and $C \to \infty$ is the hard margin; when $C$ is small, the margin is wide and more errors are tolerated. $C$ is a hyperparameter and is usually chosen by **cross-validation**. The soft-margin problem always has feasible solutions (take $\xi_i$ large enough), and it can be shown that $\boldsymbol{w}^*$ is unique while $b^*$ may not be.

![Linear soft-margin support vector machines with C = 0.02, 1, and 100 on two overlapping classes: for small C the margin is wide and there are many support vectors; for large C the margin is narrow and there are few support vectors; the circled points are the support vectors (samples with α > 0)](./plots/soft-margin.py)

### 5.3.2. The Dual Problem

Introduce multipliers $\alpha_i \ge 0$ and $\mu_i \ge 0$ for the two groups of constraints:

$$
L = \frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 + C\sum_i \xi_i + \sum_i \alpha_i\bigl(1 - \xi_i - y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b)\bigr) - \sum_i \mu_i \xi_i.
$$
The partial derivatives with respect to $\boldsymbol{w}$ and $b$ are the same as in the hard-margin case; the partial derivative with respect to $\xi_i$ gives $C - \alpha_i - \mu_i = 0$. After substituting back, all the $\xi_i$ and $\mu_i$ cancel, and the dual objective is exactly the same as in (5.1); the only change is that $\mu_i = C - \alpha_i \ge 0$ gives $\alpha_i \le C$:

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\boldsymbol{x}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_j) - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i = 0, \quad 0 \le \alpha_i \le C. \tag{5.2}
$$

By the KKT conditions, writing $f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{w}^* \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b^*$, complementary slackness $\alpha_i(1 - \xi_i - y_i f(\boldsymbol{x}_i)) = 0$ and $\mu_i \xi_i = (C - \alpha_i)\xi_i = 0$ divides the samples into three classes:

| $\alpha_i^*$ | $\xi_i$ | $y_i f(\boldsymbol{x}_i)$ | Position |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $0$ | $\ge 1$ | Outside the margin band or on its boundary; not a support vector |
| $(0, C)$ | $0$ | $= 1$ | Exactly on the margin boundary (free support vector) |
| $C$ | $1 - y_i f(\boldsymbol{x}_i) \ge 0$ | $\le 1$ | Inside the margin band or misclassified (bounded support vector) |

These conditions are used as the stopping criterion in §5.5. As before, $\boldsymbol{w}^* = \sum_i \alpha_i^* y_i \boldsymbol{x}_i$; $b^*$ is computed from any free support vector ($0 < \alpha_j^* < C$) as $b^* = y_j - \sum_i \alpha_i^* y_i(\boldsymbol{x}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_j)$, and in numerical computation the average over all free support vectors is often taken.

### 5.3.3. Hinge Loss

There is also an equivalent way to write the soft-margin support vector machine, which puts the support vector machine into the framework of structural risk minimization of §5.1.1.

**Definition 5.3.2 (Hinge Loss)** For a label $y \in \{+1, -1\}$ and a real-valued prediction $f(\boldsymbol{x})$, the **hinge loss** is

$$
L_{\mathrm{hinge}}(y, f(\boldsymbol{x})) = \max\bigl(0,\ 1 - y f(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr) = [1 - y f(\boldsymbol{x})]_+.
$$

**Theorem 5.3.1** The soft-margin problem of Definition 5.3.1 is equivalent to the unconstrained problem

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{w}, b}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl[1 - y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b)\bigr]_+ + \lambda\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2, \qquad \lambda = \frac{1}{2C}.
$$

**Proof** For fixed $(\boldsymbol{w}, b)$, the constraints on $\xi_i$ in the soft-margin problem are $\xi_i \ge 1 - y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b)$ and $\xi_i \ge 0$, and the objective is increasing in $\xi_i$, so the optimal $\xi_i = [1 - y_i(\boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_i + b)]_+$. Substituting, the objective becomes $\frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 + C\sum_i [\cdot]_+$, and dividing by $C$ gives the claim. $\square$

The hinge loss is zero when $yf \ge 1$: samples that are correctly classified with enough confidence produce no loss at all, which is why the solution depends only on the support vectors. It is a convex upper bound of the 0-1 loss $\boldsymbol{1}_{\{yf \le 0\}}$. Replacing it by $\log(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-yf})$ gives **logistic regression** with $L_2$ regularization, and replacing it by $(1 - yf)^2$ gives least-squares classification; these losses are all convex surrogates of the 0-1 loss, and they differ in whether they still give a gradient for samples that are "classified very correctly." The unconstrained form also provides another way to solve the problem: applying subgradient descent or **stochastic gradient descent** to it (for example the Pegasos algorithm) is much faster than solving the dual problem for linear problems with many samples.

## 5.4. Kernel Methods

### 5.4.1. Nonlinear Classification and Feature Maps

For some problems no hyperplane in the original space separates the classes well, for example when one class of samples lies inside a circle and the other outside. Then we can first use a transformation $\phi$ to map the data into a new feature space and then use a linear method in the new space. In the plane, take

$$
\phi(\boldsymbol{x}) = \bigl((x^{(1)})^2,\ \sqrt2\, x^{(1)} x^{(2)},\ (x^{(2)})^2\bigr);
$$
the circle $(x^{(1)})^2 + (x^{(2)})^2 = r^2$ becomes the plane $z_1 + z_3 = r^2$ in the new space, and the two classes inside and outside the circle become linearly separable. In general, a quadratic curve in the original space corresponds to a hyperplane in the space of the features $\bigl(1, x^{(1)}, x^{(2)}, (x^{(1)})^2, x^{(1)}x^{(2)}, (x^{(2)})^2\bigr)$; §10.7.2 of [3.4.10](/3.4.10) already used this idea to generalize LDA to quadratic boundaries.

The problem is the dimension: the monomials of degree $d$ in $n$ input dimensions number $\binom{n+d-1}{d}$, which is already over 170,000 for $n = 100$ and $d = 3$, so computing $\phi$ explicitly is expensive, and sometimes the feature space is even infinite-dimensional.

### 5.4.2. The Kernel Trick

In the dual problem (5.2) and in the decision function, samples appear only in the form of inner products. After moving to the feature space, we only need to replace $\boldsymbol{x}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_j$ by $\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_i) \cdot \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_j)$. And this inner product can often be computed directly without going through $\phi$: for the $\phi$ above,

$$
\phi(\boldsymbol{x}) \cdot \phi(\boldsymbol{z}) = (x^{(1)}z^{(1)})^2 + 2x^{(1)}x^{(2)}z^{(1)}z^{(2)} + (x^{(2)}z^{(2)})^2 = (\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{z})^2.
$$
Computing the right side needs only $O(n)$ operations, rather than $O(n^2)$.

**Definition 5.4.1 (Kernel Trick)** In an algorithm that depends only on inner products between samples, replacing the inner product $\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{z}$ by a kernel function $K(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z}) = \phi(\boldsymbol{x}) \cdot \phi(\boldsymbol{z})$, thereby running the algorithm implicitly in the feature space without computing the feature map $\phi$ explicitly, is called the **kernel trick**.

Which $K$ is the inner product of some feature map? The answer is the **positive definite kernel** ([3.4.6](/3.4.6)): $K$ is symmetric, and for any finite set of points $\boldsymbol{x}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{x}_N$ the matrix $[K(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}_j)]$ is positive semidefinite. Every positive definite kernel corresponds to a feature map into a (possibly infinite-dimensional) Hilbert space (Mercer's theorem, or the construction of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space). The positive semidefiniteness of the kernel matrix also guarantees that the dual problem is still a convex quadratic program. Commonly used kernels are:

| Kernel | $K(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})$ | Feature space | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear kernel | $\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{z}$ | The original space | The linear support vector machine |
| **Polynomial kernel** | $(\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{z} + c)^d$, $c \ge 0$ | All monomials of degree at most $d$ | |
| **Gaussian kernel** | $\exp\bigl(-\|\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{z}\|^2 / (2\sigma^2)\bigr)$ | Infinite-dimensional | Also called the RBF kernel; the most commonly used |
| Sigmoid kernel | $\tanh(a\,\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{z} + r)$ | | Generally not positive definite |

Using a kernel function in place of the inner product gives the nonlinear support vector machine: solve

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j K(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}_j) - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i = 0, \quad 0 \le \alpha_i \le C,
$$
choose some $0 < \alpha_j^* < C$, compute $b^* = y_j - \sum_i \alpha_i^* y_i K(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}_j)$, and the decision function is

$$
g(\boldsymbol{x}) = \operatorname{sgn}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i^* y_i K(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}) + b^*\Bigr).
$$
$\boldsymbol{w}^* = \sum_i \alpha_i^* y_i \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_i)$ lives in the feature space and is generally not computed explicitly. With the Gaussian kernel, the decision function is a weighted sum of Gaussian functions centered at the support vectors.

The width $\sigma$ of the Gaussian kernel controls the complexity of the model: when $\sigma$ is large the kernel function varies slowly, each support vector influences a wide range, and the decision boundary is close to linear and may underfit; when $\sigma$ is small each support vector influences only a tiny neighborhood, the boundary can bend around individual samples, the training error tends to zero while generalization is poor, which is overfitting. $\sigma$ and $C$ are chosen together by cross-validation.

![Decision boundaries of Gaussian-kernel support vector machines (C = 10) on two interleaved half-moons: for σ = 3 the boundary is almost a straight line (underfitting); for σ = 0.4 the boundary follows the shape of the data; for σ = 0.05 the boundary bends around every sample, with training accuracy 1 and almost all samples becoming support vectors (overfitting)](./plots/rbf-width.py)

The kernel trick is not limited to support vector machines: **kernel principal component analysis** ([3.4.6](/3.4.6)), kernel ridge regression, and Gaussian processes are all based on the same idea. Its cost is the need for an $N \times N$ kernel matrix; when the number of samples reaches hundreds of thousands or more, both computation and storage become bottlenecks, which is one reason why deep learning has replaced kernel methods on big data; methods such as random features (Rahimi and Recht, 2007) approximate the kernel function with explicit low-dimensional random features and ease this problem.

## 5.5. The SMO Algorithm

The dual problem is a convex quadratic program with a global optimal solution, and general quadratic programming algorithms can solve it; but when the number of samples $N$ is large, the $N \times N$ kernel matrix does not fit in memory and general algorithms are very slow. The sequential minimal optimization (SMO) algorithm proposed by Platt (1998) optimizes only two variables at a time, with the others fixed, and the two-variable subproblem has an analytic solution.

Why two? The equality constraint $\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0$ means that changing only one $\alpha_i$ necessarily violates the constraint, so at least two must be changed at the same time. Suppose $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ are chosen and the others fixed; then

$$
\alpha_1 y_1 + \alpha_2 y_2 = -\sum_{i \ge 3} \alpha_i y_i = \text{constant},
$$
and $(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ can move only on a line segment inside the box $[0, C]^2$, so the problem reduces to minimizing a quadratic function of one variable on an interval. Write $f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i K(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}) + b$, let $E_i = f(\boldsymbol{x}_i) - y_i$ be the prediction error, and let $\eta = K_{11} + K_{22} - 2K_{12}$ ($K_{ij} = K(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}_j)$; for a positive definite kernel $\eta \ge 0$). Differentiating along the segment gives the unconstrained optimal solution

$$
\alpha_2^{\mathrm{new, unc}} = \alpha_2 + \frac{y_2(E_1 - E_2)}{\eta},
$$
which is then clipped to the range $[L, H]$ of the segment's endpoints:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_1 \ne y_2: \quad & L = \max(0, \alpha_2 - \alpha_1), & H &= \min(C, C + \alpha_2 - \alpha_1),\\
y_1 = y_2: \quad & L = \max(0, \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 - C), & H &= \min(C, \alpha_1 + \alpha_2),
\end{aligned}
$$
Then the equality constraint gives $\alpha_1^{\mathrm{new}} = \alpha_1 + y_1 y_2(\alpha_2 - \alpha_2^{\mathrm{new}})$, and $b$ is updated so that the sample whose multiplier lies in $(0, C)$ satisfies $y f(\boldsymbol{x}) = 1$.

Heuristics for choosing the variables: choose as the first variable the sample that violates the KKT conditions (the table of §5.3.2) most severely; choose as the second variable the sample that maximizes $\lvert E_1 - E_2 \rvert$, so that this step makes as large a change as possible; if this choice cannot decrease the objective, try other samples in turn. Each step does not increase the dual objective; the algorithm stops when all samples satisfy the KKT conditions within a tolerance.

```python
import numpy as np

def smo(K, y, C, tol=1e-3, max_iter=10000):
    """Sequential minimal optimization for the SVM dual, given the kernel matrix K."""
    n = len(y)
    alpha, b = np.zeros(n), 0.0
    for it in range(max_iter):
        E = (alpha * y) @ K + b - y                      # prediction errors f(x_i) - y_i
        # KKT violations: alpha_i < C needs y_i f_i >= 1, alpha_i > 0 needs y_i f_i <= 1
        viol = ((y * E < -tol) & (alpha < C)) | ((y * E > tol) & (alpha > 0))
        if not viol.any():
            break                                       # all KKT conditions hold
        i = int(np.argmax(np.where(viol, np.abs(y * E), -1)))   # worst violator
        for j in np.argsort(-np.abs(E - E[i])):        # partner with largest |E_i - E_j| first
            if j == i:
                continue
            if y[i] != y[j]:
                L, H = max(0, alpha[j] - alpha[i]), min(C, C + alpha[j] - alpha[i])
            else:
                L, H = max(0, alpha[i] + alpha[j] - C), min(C, alpha[i] + alpha[j])
            eta = K[i, i] + K[j, j] - 2 * K[i, j]
            if L >= H or eta <= 0:
                continue
            aj = np.clip(alpha[j] + y[j] * (E[i] - E[j]) / eta, L, H)
            if abs(aj - alpha[j]) < 1e-12:
                continue
            ai = alpha[i] + y[i] * y[j] * (alpha[j] - aj)
            # update b so that a multiplier strictly inside (0, C) satisfies y f = 1
            b1 = b - E[i] - y[i] * (ai - alpha[i]) * K[i, i] - y[j] * (aj - alpha[j]) * K[i, j]
            b2 = b - E[j] - y[i] * (ai - alpha[i]) * K[i, j] - y[j] * (aj - alpha[j]) * K[j, j]
            b = b1 if 0 < ai < C else b2 if 0 < aj < C else (b1 + b2) / 2
            alpha[i], alpha[j] = ai, aj
            break
    return alpha, b, it

# Example 5.2.1: hard margin (a very large C).
X = np.array([[2.0, 2.0], [3.0, 3.0], [0.0, 1.0]])
y = np.array([1.0, 1.0, -1.0])
alpha, b, _ = smo(X @ X.T, y, C=1e6)
print("alpha =", np.round(alpha, 4), " w =", np.round((alpha * y) @ X, 4), " b =", round(b, 4))

# Two noisy concentric rings with a Gaussian kernel (sigma = 0.5).
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 200
r = np.r_[rng.normal(1.0, 0.25, n // 2), rng.normal(2.2, 0.25, n // 2)]
t = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, n)
X = np.c_[r * np.cos(t), r * np.sin(t)]
y = np.r_[np.ones(n // 2), -np.ones(n // 2)]
sq = np.sum(X**2, 1)
K = np.exp(-(sq[:, None] + sq[None, :] - 2 * X @ X.T) / (2 * 0.5**2))
alpha, b, iterations = smo(K, y, C=1.0)
pred = np.sign((alpha * y) @ K + b)
print("iterations:", iterations, " support vectors:", int(np.sum(alpha > 1e-8)),
      " training accuracy:", np.mean(pred == y))
```

```text
alpha = [0.4 0.  0.4]  w = [0.8 0.4]  b = -1.4
iterations: 304  support vectors: 61  training accuracy: 0.995
```

For brevity, every step here recomputes all the errors $E$ ($O(N^2)$), while real implementations update them only incrementally. On the ring data, the resulting dual objective value differs from that of a general quadratic programming solver by less than $10^{-4}$.

SMO and its improvements are the core of software such as LIBSVM: Fan, Chen, and Lin (2005) select the working set using second-order information, combined with caching and "shrinking" (temporarily removing variables that are likely to end up at the bounds); Keerthi and Gilbert (2002) proved the convergence of this class of methods. Empirically, the training time of kernel support vector machines lies between $O(N^2)$ and $O(N^3)$. For linear support vector machines, LIBLINEAR's dual coordinate descent and stochastic gradient methods based on the hinge loss can handle millions of samples and features.

## 5.6. Multiclass Classification and Applications

The support vector machine is a binary classifier. There are several ways to combine binary classifiers for a $K$-class problem:

- One-vs-rest: train $K$ classifiers, the $k$-th with class $k$ as positive examples and the rest as negative examples; at prediction time take the class with the largest $f_k(\boldsymbol{x})$.
- One-vs-one: train one classifier for each pair of classes, $K(K-1)/2$ in all, each using only the data of its two classes; at prediction time they vote. LIBSVM uses this method by default.
- Hierarchical methods: first divide all classes into two groups, then divide each group into two, forming a binary tree.

**Example 5.6.1 (Text Classification)** Represent document $j$ as a vector $(w_{1j}, \dots, w_{nj})$ in the vector space model, where $w_{ij}$ is the weight of term $i$ in document $j$; the simplest choice is the term frequency $\mathrm{tf}_{ij}$. The commonly used tf-idf weighting also takes into account the discriminating power of a term: suppose the training set has $N$ documents in all and term $i$ appears in $\mathrm{df}_i$ of them; the inverse document frequency is $\mathrm{idf}_i = \log(N / \mathrm{df}_i)$, and $w_{ij} = \mathrm{tf}_{ij} \cdot \mathrm{idf}_i$: words that appear in all documents ("the," "is") get weights close to zero. Preprocessing includes tokenization, removing stop words, stemming (for English), and feature selection. Text vectors have high dimension (tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands) and are sparse, and the samples are often linearly separable or nearly so, so linear support vector machines work very well on such problems (Joachims, 1998) and remain a strong baseline for text classification. A more common practice today is to encode documents as dense vectors with a pre-trained language model ([4.7.7](/4.7.7)) and train a linear classifier on top, or to have a large language model classify directly.

## Summary

| | Hard margin | Soft margin | Kernel methods |
|---|---|---|---|
| Applies to | Linearly separable data | Nearly linearly separable data | Nonlinear data |
| Primal problem | $\min \frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2$, $y_i f(\boldsymbol{x}_i) \ge 1$ | $\min \frac12\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 + C\sum\xi_i$, $y_i f(\boldsymbol{x}_i) \ge 1 - \xi_i$ | Soft margin in the feature space |
| Dual constraints | $\sum\alpha_i y_i = 0$, $\alpha_i \ge 0$ | $\sum\alpha_i y_i = 0$, $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$ | Same as soft margin |
| Samples in the dual objective | $\boldsymbol{x}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_j$ | $\boldsymbol{x}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_j$ | $K(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}_j)$ |

- The support vector machine chooses the separating hyperplane with the largest geometric margin; the maximum-margin hyperplane exists and is unique (Theorem 5.2.1) and is determined only by the support vectors.
- Lagrange duality always gives a lower bound on the primal optimal value (weak duality); for convex problems satisfying Slater's condition strong duality holds, and the optimal solutions are characterized by the KKT conditions. Complementary slackness shows that samples with $\alpha_i > 0$ are all on the margin boundary (hard margin) or violate the margin (soft margin, $\alpha_i = C$).
- The soft-margin support vector machine is equivalent to structural risk minimization with hinge loss plus $L_2$ regularization; the larger $C$ is, the closer it is to the hard margin.
- The dual problem depends only on inner products; the kernel trick replaces the inner product with a positive definite kernel and implicitly performs linear classification in a high-dimensional feature space; the width of the Gaussian kernel and $C$ control the complexity of the model and are chosen by cross-validation.
- SMO optimizes two multipliers analytically at each step and is the standard training algorithm for kernel support vector machines.
