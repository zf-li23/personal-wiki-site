**Artificial intelligence** studies how to make machines perform tasks that require intelligence: finding routes, playing board games, recognizing images, classifying and judging. This course is an introduction to the field; its aim is not to go deep into any single technique but to give a panorama of the field's problems and methods. It is organized around two routes to intelligence. One formalizes the problem and then searches efficiently for a solution, using human knowledge about the problem; the other writes no rules and lets the machine learn the solution from data. The former is represented by **heuristic search** in a **state space** and by adversarial search, the latter by neural networks, **support vector machines** and **decision trees**. The question that runs through the whole course is where knowledge about a problem comes from, whether it is written by people or learned from data, and what price each choice carries.

The chapters follow, roughly, the history of artificial intelligence. The introduction surveys the field in five periods (symbolic reasoning, knowledge engineering, statistical learning, deep learning, and large models) and points out that each dominant method has a bottleneck it cannot escape: rules, knowledge, features, labeled data and computing power must all be supplied by someone. Two chapters on search follow. Search by a single decision maker centers on the **A\* algorithm**, with a proof of the conditions under which a heuristic function lets the search examine fewer nodes while still guaranteeing an optimal solution; when an opponent is involved, the **minimax algorithm** and **alpha–beta pruning**, together with a hand-designed **evaluation function**, produced Deep Blue, while Go, whose evaluation function is hard to write down, gave rise to **Monte Carlo tree search** based on random simulation. AlphaGo combines tree search with an evaluation learned from data, which leads into the learning part of the course: starting from handwritten digit recognition, we introduce the basic ideas of neural networks, **gradient descent** and **backpropagation**; two further chapters then treat the two most representative methods of classical statistical learning, the support vector machine, built from margin maximization, duality and the **kernel trick**, and the decision tree, which asks questions step by step according to information gain and whose result can be read directly as rules.

This course divides the work with the other two artificial intelligence courses. The methodology of machine learning (the design of learning systems, experiments and evaluation, Bayesian learning, ensemble learning, sequence models) is developed in [Introduction to Machine Learning](/4.6); the in-depth material on neural networks and deep learning (convolutional networks, recurrent networks, Transformers, pre-training and large models) is concentrated in [Artificial Neural Networks](/4.7), and the neural network chapter here is only an introduction. The foundations of search come from state-space search in [Programming Fundamentals](/4.1) and from graph traversal and shortest-path algorithms in [Data Structures and Algorithms](/4.3); support vector machines and decision trees were already sketched from the viewpoint of discriminant analysis in [Multivariate Statistical Analysis](/3.4), and the theory of optimization methods is in [Statistical Computing and Software](/3.5). The applications of the large-model era mentioned in the introduction are put into concrete practice in the chapter [AI-Assisted Programming](/4.2.14) of [Object-Oriented Programming](/4.2).
