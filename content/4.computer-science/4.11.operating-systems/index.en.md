This course studies the layer of software between programs and hardware. A program sees a private processor, a large private memory, files that survive a reboot and devices it can simply read and write; the hardware offers a few cores, a limited physical memory shared by everyone, storage that reads and writes numbered blocks, and devices driven through registers and interrupts. The **operating system** builds the first picture out of the second. Its central questions follow the three themes of the classical treatment in OSTEP: virtualization, how one machine is shared among many programs so that each believes it owns the processor and the memory; concurrency, how several flows of control can cooperate on shared data correctly; and persistence, how data are stored reliably and quickly on devices that may fail or lose power mid-update. Throughout, the course takes the view of a programmer and system user rather than of a kernel developer, with Linux on x86-64 as the running example, and it ends with the practical use of Linux on workstations, servers and clusters, the environment in which computational biology is actually done.

The chapters move from mechanisms inside one machine to the use of many machines. The overview introduces the two privilege modes of the processor and the **system calls** through which programs enter the **kernel**. Virtualizing the processor comes first: the **process** is the unit of execution and protection, created and controlled through `fork`, `exec` and `wait` and run by **limited direct execution** with timer interrupts and context switches; scheduling then decides which process runs next, from the classical policies to the proportional-share schedulers of Linux and scheduling on multiprocessors. Virtualizing memory follows the same pattern of mechanism and policy: **address translation** with multi-level page tables and the TLB gives each process its own **address space**, page replacement decides what stays in RAM, and a user-space allocator divides the pages obtained from the kernel into the blocks requested by `malloc`, with **garbage collection** as the managed alternative. Once several **threads** share one address space, correctness becomes the problem: locks, condition variables and semaphores, their construction from atomic instructions, and the typical bugs, including **deadlock**. Persistence covers the **file system** interface and its implementation with inodes, the Fast File System and journaling, then the devices underneath, hard disks, SSDs and RAID, and the buffering layers of system-level I/O. The last three chapters apply these ideas one level up and put them to work: **virtual machines** and **containers** isolate whole operating systems or groups of processes, the Bash command line makes processes, file descriptors and permissions directly visible, and the final chapter uses all of the above to explain how a cluster is organized and how batch jobs are scheduled with Slurm and automated with workflow managers.

The hardware mechanisms on which the operating system relies, privilege modes, exceptions and the instruction set, are the subject of [Computer Organization](/4.10). The data structures used by schedulers and allocators (queues, **priority queues**, **red-black trees**) come from [Data Structures and Algorithms](/4.3), and the memory model of a C or C++ program, with its stack and heap, from [Programming Fundamentals](/4.1). Python's process pools and the global interpreter lock in [Scientific Computing with Python](/4.4) are the practical side of the processes and threads studied here. The command line, servers and clusters of the last chapters are the working environment for the analyses of [Bioinformatics](/7.9).
