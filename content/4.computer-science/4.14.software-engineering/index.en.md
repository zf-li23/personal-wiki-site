This course is about the difference between a program that works for its author and software that many people build together, that many more people use, and that keeps changing for years. **Software engineering** is the discipline that studies this difference, and its core questions are organizational as much as technical: how a team agrees on what to build and writes it down, how the work of many people is kept consistent, how a system is structured so that it meets its quality goals and stays cheap to change, how one gains confidence that it is correct, and how it is kept running after release. The emphasis throughout is on engineering ideas rather than on the syntax of particular tools. Where tools are needed, the course uses those of current practice: Git for version control, and a web application built with **front-end/back-end separation**, with a React front end and a Django back end, as the one worked example of implementation.

The chapters follow the **software life cycle**. After an introduction that defines the discipline and surveys its body of knowledge as organized by SWEBOK, the **software process** chapter traces the move from the **waterfall model** to **agile software development**, Scrum, Kanban and **DevOps**, and version control adds the tool on which every process relies: Git's data model and commands, branching strategies, **pull requests**, **code review** and **continuous integration**. **Requirements engineering** then turns vague wishes into precise and checkable requirements. **Software architecture** gives the first answer to how they are met, through **quality attributes**, design tactics and classic architectural styles, and the next chapter carries these ideas to distributed Internet services, with microservices, REST and the patterns of large websites for performance, availability and scalability. Three chapters descend to a single web application: HTTP, APIs, authentication and web security, then the React front end as a function of state, and the Django back end with its models, views and API. Code quality and **refactoring** concern code once it is written; two chapters on testing cover the design of test cases by **white-box testing** and **black-box testing**, then automation, **test-driven development** and the levels from unit to acceptance tests. Operations close the cycle with **site reliability engineering**: **service level objectives**, **error budgets**, **monitoring**, incident response and safe releases. Most chapters end with a note on what changes when AI agents do part of the work, and the final chapter brings these notes together as engineering with **coding agents**: specifications, guardrails, **context engineering**, orchestration and human review, with the process by which this wiki is written as its case study.

The course assumes the programming of [Programming Fundamentals](/4.1), where unit tests first appear, and the classes, **design patterns** and AI-assisted programming of [Object-Oriented Programming](/4.2). Its web chapters rest on the protocols of [Computer Networks](/4.12); the back end stores its data in the relational databases of [Database Systems](/4.13), which also treats the indexes, partitioning and transactions that large services rely on; and the containers and clusters of [Operating Systems](/4.11) are the platform on which services are deployed and operated.

Of the computer science courses I have taken, this is the one that taught me the most. In the age of AI-assisted programming I consider it the most important required course in programming: the conventions and ideas it teaches for maintaining a project, version control, requirements documents, layered architecture and testing, make it easy to lay out a project, while the coding itself can be handed to an agent. In my experience agents are especially strong at front-end work, which involves a large amount of code but can almost always be produced once the requirements are stated clearly; the back end needs a more detailed requirements document that spells out every interface and constraint so that each function is implemented correctly. The course project ran through the whole semester and took most of the time I spent on the course. In practice we settled on a division of labor in which whoever worked on the back end could change a small amount of front-end code to align an interface, as long as the front-end developer was told, so that the front end could concentrate on creative design and refinement. Having finished the course, I can comfortably act as the architect of the small programs I write day to day and let AI do the coding.

I also expect the norms of software engineering to become more radical. Some AI companies already let AI take over code review, and that is what truly saves human time. My principle is that being free of problems means working without problems in use, not looking free of problems on inspection. Once a piece of engineering has passed the test of practice, it does not matter whether its code was written and reviewed by people or by AI. This principle rests on exactly what the second half of the course teaches: the test of practice has to be systematic, with automated tests of adequate coverage, acceptance criteria traced to the requirements and monitoring after release; otherwise "works without problems" only means "has not met a problem yet".
