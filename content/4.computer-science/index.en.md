Computer science studies how machines represent information and carry out computation, and how computation is organized into reliable systems and software. Its questions come at three levels: what can be computed and at what cost; how a computer is built up, layer by layer, from logic gates into a system that runs programs; and how people write the problems of a domain as programs and keep those programs correct while many people collaborate on them and maintain them for years. This discipline collects fourteen courses, organized as one foundation and three branches.

The foundation consists of four courses on programming and algorithms. [Programming Fundamentals](/4.1) covers procedural programming, [Object-Oriented Programming](/4.2) how to organize programs with objects and interfaces, [Data Structures and Algorithms](/4.3) how to organize data and analyze the efficiency of algorithms, and [Scientific Computing with Python](/4.4) the numerical and data-handling tools that data analysis depends on. The three branches build on it. The artificial intelligence branch is [Introduction to Artificial Intelligence](/4.5), [Introduction to Machine Learning](/4.6) and [Artificial Neural Networks](/4.7), running from search and reasoning through statistical learning to deep learning and large models. The branch on the theory of computation and systems is [Formal Languages and Automata](/4.8), [Digital Logic Circuits](/4.9), [Computer Organization](/4.10), [Operating Systems](/4.11) and [Computer Networks](/4.12): it starts at the limits of computability and passes through logic gates, the processor and the operating system to machines connected in networks. The software and data branch is [Database Systems](/4.13) and [Software Engineering](/4.14), on how to store and query data reliably and how to turn requirements step by step into software that can be maintained and verified. Software Engineering closes the whole sequence; it draws on almost every course before it.

The mathematical foundation of computer science comes from [Discrete Mathematics](/1): logic, sets and relations, graph theory and algebraic structures are the common language of programs, data structures and formal languages, while machine learning and neural networks also need [Linear Algebra](/2.1), [Calculus](/2.2) and [Probability](/3.1). Computer science meets [Statistics](/3) in machine learning, where the two share regression, dimensionality reduction, classification and clustering, and [Statistical Computing and Software](/3.5) discusses how statistical methods are implemented on computers. For [Biology](/7), computing is the tool of [Bioinformatics](/7.8) and of all large-scale data analysis: sequences, phylogenetic trees and interaction networks are linear, semi-linear and nonlinear data structures, analysis pipelines run on Linux servers and computing clusters, and the results reach other people through databases and web pages.

I took computer science as a minor because I did not want my biological research to focus only on a single molecule or a single mechanism, however meaningful, or even profitable, such research may be; I would rather look for general laws of life. Whatever level such a question comes from, molecular, cellular, organismal or ecological, building a holistic view depends on computational tools. There was another reason. I came to university with a background in the biology olympiad, and to keep me from settling into my comfort zone my parents wanted me to try many things, ideally to study a second discipline systematically; to challenge myself, I chose computer science. For the practical needs of research I later also took [Statistics](/3), but the computer science minor is the one that will actually give me a degree. Even if I were to forget everything I learned in these courses, I am no longer afraid of writing code, and I dare to approach any technology I have never heard of. That courage is what stepping outside my field gave me.

In fact, I remember a good deal of computer science. Programming Fundamentals made me see programming as describing the world in a language a machine can read, and together with Discrete Mathematics it was where I first dared to represent the world I knew with symbols. Object-oriented programming studies how the world is composed: what things there are, what each can do, and how they relate. Data Structures and Algorithms gave me a way of looking at data, and the sequences, phylogenetic trees and interaction networks of computational biology are, in that order, exactly its linear, semi-linear and nonlinear structures. In the age of AI-assisted programming I came to one judgment about these courses: syntax can be learned lightly, but the ideas must be learned thoroughly. My own path through the courses records this change, from writing every line by hand without AI, which I call "old-school programming", to working with agents that were still weak, to the point where, by the time I took Software Engineering, I could act as the architect of the small programs I write day to day and let AI do the coding. Once writing code is no longer the bottleneck, how to decompose a problem, how to design an experiment and how to judge whether a result can be trusted matter all the more. The three branches mean different things to me. Artificial intelligence and software and data are tools I use directly in research. In the systems branch, Formal Languages and Automata showed me how far one can go once something has been formalized, and Operating Systems serves the servers and computing clusters that bioinformatics analysis depends on; the remaining courses were taken mainly to complete the minor, and they show me the foundations these tools rest on.

In the age of AI-assisted programming and learning, even computer science majors may not remember many details once a course is over. My grades are not necessarily higher than theirs, but I always come away with a broad understanding of each course and a lasting impression of it, and that is the point of studying widely. As long as such an impression remains, it keeps shaping my research and my choices: I know that there are things I can perfectly well try, and that they are not obstacles.
