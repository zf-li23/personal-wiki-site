This course studies how a usually infinite set of strings can be described by finitely many rules, and how a machine can decide whether a given string belongs to it. There are two complementary ways to describe a language. One is generation: a set of rewriting rules, a **grammar**, produces exactly the valid sentences. The other is recognition: an abstract machine, an automaton, reads the symbols one at a time and decides to accept or reject. Most theorems of the course show that a certain class of grammars and a certain class of automata describe exactly the same family of languages, and these families form a hierarchy ordered by how much the machine can remember. At the top of the hierarchy the question changes from "how do we describe a language" to "what can algorithms do": which problems have no algorithm at all, and which have one that may not run efficiently.

The chapters climb three levels of the **Chomsky hierarchy**. The introduction defines alphabets, strings and languages, the chapter on grammars sets up the four classes, and the rest of the course proceeds level by level. The lowest level consists of the **regular languages**. They have three equivalent descriptions, algebraic **regular expressions**, **finite automata** with finitely many states, and type-3 grammars, tied together by Kleene's theorem; the pumping lemma and the Myhill–Nerode theorem expose the limits of finite memory, while closure properties, decision algorithms and DFA minimization show how well behaved this family is algorithmically. Adding a stack to a finite automaton gives the **pushdown automaton**, which recognizes exactly the languages generated by **context-free grammars**. Simplifying grammars and bringing them into Chomsky normal form leads to the CKY and Earley parsing algorithms, and the pumping lemma, closure properties and decision properties, parallel to those of regular languages, show what changes at this level: the class is no longer closed under intersection and complement, and equivalence is no longer decidable. Replacing the stack by a tape that can be read and written anywhere gives the **Turing machine**; its many extended and restricted variants are all equivalent to it, and this robustness is the evidence for the **Church–Turing thesis**. The last chapter takes the Turing machine as the definition of computation, uses diagonalization and reduction to prove that the **halting problem** and other problems are undecidable, and gives precise definitions of $\mathrm{P}$, $\mathrm{NP}$ and **NP-complete** problems.

The course is the mathematics behind many practical techniques. Finite automata are the model of lexical analyzers and of **regular expression** engines ([4.2.11](/4.2.11)), and the essence of the KMP string-matching algorithm ([4.3.13](/4.3.13)); context-free grammars and pushdown automata underlie the syntax analysis of compilers ([4.2.2](/4.2.2)). A synchronous sequential circuit in digital logic ([4.9.7](/4.9.7)) is a finite automaton with outputs, and the hidden Markov model ([4.6.8](/4.6.8)) is its probabilistic version. The introduction of [Data Structures and Algorithms](/4.3) gave a first look at Turing machines and at $\mathrm{P}$ and $\mathrm{NP}$; here they are defined rigorously. The proof methods (induction, contradiction, diagonalization) and the language of sets and relations come from [Discrete Mathematics](/1), in particular [Mathematical Logic](/1.1) and [Set Theory](/1.2).
