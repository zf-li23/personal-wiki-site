In computational work a figure is often the first and the last thing done with data: the first, because plotting raw measurements reveals outliers, batch effects, saturated detectors and mislabeled samples that no summary statistic shows; the last, because the conclusions of a paper are communicated and judged largely through its figures. This chapter treats both uses. It starts with Matplotlib, the library on which nearly all Python plotting is built, and its object-oriented interface; then turns to the decisions that make a figure honest and readable (which chart for which data, which colors, how to arrange many panels, which mistakes to avoid); and ends with two higher-level libraries, seaborn for statistical graphics and Plotly for interactive figures. The data come from the tables of [4.4.5](/4.4.5); the statistical displays (histograms, the **boxplot**, **kernel density estimation**, the **scatterplot matrix**) are defined in the statistics courses, and R's graphics system is described in [3.5.2](/3.5.2).

The figures in this chapter are drawn with Matplotlib 3.10 from synthetic data; each is generated by the script shown below it. The seaborn and Plotly examples were run with seaborn 0.13.2 and Plotly 7.1.0 (Matplotlib 3.11.2, pandas 3.0.6).

## 6.1. Matplotlib: Figures and Axes

### 6.1.1. Two Interfaces

Matplotlib offers two ways to draw. The *pyplot* interface (`plt.plot`, `plt.xlabel`, …) imitates MATLAB: it keeps a hidden "current figure" and "current axes" and directs each call to them. It is convenient for a quick look in a notebook, but in scripts with several panels the implicit state makes it hard to know where a call draws. The *object-oriented* interface creates the objects explicitly and calls their methods; it is the recommended style for anything that is kept.

The objects form a tree:

| Object | What it is | Typical methods |
|---|---|---|
| `Figure` | the whole image: size, resolution, background; holds one or more Axes | `subplots`, `subplot_mosaic`, `subfigures`, `colorbar`, `suptitle`, `savefig` |
| `Axes` | one plotting area with its coordinate system: two `Axis` objects, title, legend | `plot`, `scatter`, `bar`, `hist`, `imshow`, `fill_between`, `set_xlabel`, `set_xscale`, `legend` |
| `Axis` | one axis of an Axes: limits, scale, ticks, tick labels | `set_major_locator`, `set_major_formatter` (usually reached through Axes methods) |
| `Artist` | everything drawn: `Line2D`, `Text`, `Patch`, `AxesImage`, … | `set_color`, `set_linewidth`, `set_visible` |

Every drawing method of an Axes returns the artists it created, so they can be modified afterwards. A minimal script in this style:

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

t = np.linspace(0, 24, 49)
od = 1.2 / (1 + 29 * np.exp(-0.45 * t))          # logistic growth curve

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4, 3), layout="constrained")
(line,) = ax.plot(t, od, linewidth=2, label="WT")
ax.set_xlabel("Time (h)")
ax.set_ylabel("OD600")
ax.set_yscale("log")
ax.legend(frameon=False)
fig.savefig("growth.pdf")
```

`plt.subplots` is the only pyplot call needed: it creates the Figure and the Axes and returns them, and from then on the code names the object it changes. The curve is a **logistic growth** model ([2.2.15](/2.2.15)); on a logarithmic axis its exponential phase becomes a straight line whose slope is the growth rate, which is why growth curves are conventionally shown on a log scale.

### 6.1.2. Multi-Panel Figures

A figure in a paper usually combines several panels. `fig.subplots(nrows, ncols, sharex=..., sharey=..., width_ratios=...)` creates a regular grid; `fig.subplot_mosaic("AAB;CCB")` creates panels of different sizes from a text layout, returning a dictionary of Axes keyed by the letters; `fig.subfigures` splits a figure into independent regions with their own layouts. `layout="constrained"` makes Matplotlib reserve space for labels, titles and colorbars so that nothing overlaps, which replaces manual tuning with `subplots_adjust` or the older `tight_layout`.

![A three-panel figure drawn with the object-oriented interface. (A) Growth curves of three strains, mean and a band of one standard deviation over four replicates, on a log scale and labeled directly instead of with a legend. (B) The individual replicates at 12 h with their mean. (C) A heatmap of row z-scores of twelve genes in six samples with a diverging colormap centered at zero; the line separates control from treated samples.](./plots/multi-panel-figure.py)

The script of this figure shows several habits worth copying: all Axes are created at once and named (`ax_a`, `ax_b`, `ax_c`); panel letters are placed with `set_title(loc="left")`; a categorical color is assigned to each strain once and reused in every panel; the lines are labeled directly at their ends, which spares the reader the trip to a legend; the bounds of the colormap are symmetric about zero so that white means "no change".

### 6.1.3. Exporting Figures

| Output | Format | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| line art, plots, text | PDF, SVG, EPS (vector) | scale without loss; text remains editable; set `pdf.fonttype` to `42` so that journals and Illustrator get TrueType fonts instead of Type 3 |
| images, heatmaps with millions of cells, dense scatter plots | PNG, TIFF (raster) at 300–600 dpi | use `rasterized=True` on a single dense artist to keep the rest of a PDF vector |
| web, slides | PNG, SVG | lossy JPEG is unsuitable for plots |

Make the figure in its final printed size (`figsize` in inches: about 3.5 in for one journal column, 7 in for the full width) with font sizes of 7–9 pt, rather than drawing at screen size and shrinking the result: shrinking makes text and lines too thin and inconsistent between panels. For figures that must look alike across a project, collect the settings in a style sheet (`plt.style.use("paper.mplstyle")`) or a `with plt.rc_context({...}):` block, instead of repeating keyword arguments in every call.

## 6.2. Choosing a Chart

The type of chart follows from the question the figure answers and from the kind of variables involved:

| Question | Variables | Charts |
|---|---|---|
| How is one quantity distributed? | one numeric | histogram, **kernel density estimation**, ECDF; for several groups: strip plot or beeswarm (all points), **boxplot**, violin plot |
| How do two quantities relate? | two numeric | scatter plot; hexbin or 2D histogram when points overlap heavily; **scatterplot matrix** for several |
| How does a quantity change along an ordered variable? | numeric vs time, dose, position | line plot, with a band or error bars for the uncertainty |
| How do groups compare? | numeric vs categorical | dot plot of estimates with intervals; points with a box plot; a bar chart only for counts and other quantities that start at zero |
| How large are the parts of a whole? | counts or proportions | stacked bars; a pie chart only for two or three parts |
| What is the structure of a matrix? | numeric matrix | heatmap, with rows and columns ordered by **hierarchical clustering**; a **contour plot** for a smooth function of two variables |
| How do many series behave? | many lines or groups | small multiples (§6.4) |

A bar chart of group means with error bars, still common in biology, is a poor choice for measurements: the bar's area encodes nothing (the bar starts at zero by convention, not because zero is meaningful), the error bars hide the sample size and the shape of the distribution, and very different data produce the same picture.

![Three samples of 30 cells that have exactly the same mean and standard deviation. As bars of means with standard errors (left) they are indistinguishable; shown as points over a box plot (right), sample A is unimodal, sample B consists of two subpopulations, and in sample C two outliers produce the whole spread.](./plots/bar-of-means.py)

Whenever the number of observations is moderate (up to a few hundred per group), show the observations themselves. When error bars are used, state what they are: the standard deviation describes the spread of the data, the standard error of the mean describes the precision of the mean and shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$, and a 95 % confidence interval is roughly twice the standard error. Without the label the reader cannot interpret the figure, since the three differ by a factor that depends on $n$.

## 6.3. Color

### 6.3.1. Colormaps

**Definition 6.3.1 (Colormap)** A **colormap** is a function from an interval of data values, normalized to $[0, 1]$, to colors. A **sequential colormap** varies monotonically in lightness and encodes magnitude; a **diverging colormap** has two sequential arms meeting at a neutral color and encodes deviation from a meaningful center (zero, a ratio of one); a **qualitative colormap** is a set of distinct colors without order, encoding categories.

The eye judges differences of lightness far more reliably than differences of hue. A colormap is *perceptually uniform* if equal steps in the data produce equal perceived color differences; in particular its lightness then changes linearly and monotonically. The "rainbow" colormaps (`jet`, `hsv`), long the default in MATLAB and in Matplotlib before version 2.0, are not: their lightness rises and falls, so that the bright cyan and yellow bands look like features of the data and two different values can have the same lightness.

![The same smooth field (a broad peak, a weaker shoulder and a linear trend) in three colormaps, with the lightness L* of each colormap below. The lightness of jet rises to a plateau and falls again, which creates sharp apparent edges at cyan and yellow where the data are smooth; viridis and cividis increase linearly in lightness and show the field without artifacts.](./plots/colormaps.py)

Matplotlib's default colormap since version 2.0 has been `viridis`, designed to be perceptually uniform, to print well in grayscale and to remain readable for the common forms of color vision deficiency; `cividis` is a variant optimized further for that case. Practical choices:

| Data | Colormap in Matplotlib |
|---|---|
| magnitudes (expression level, density, intensity) | sequential: `viridis`, `cividis`, `magma`; single-hue `Blues`, `Greys` |
| signed deviations (log fold change, z-score, correlation) | diverging: `RdBu_r`, `PuOr`, `berlin`, `vanimo`, with limits symmetric about the center (`vmin=-m, vmax=m`, or `matplotlib.colors.TwoSlopeNorm`) |
| angles, phases, time of day | cyclic: `twilight` |
| categories | qualitative: `tab10`, or a fixed list such as Okabe–Ito; the `petroff10` style sheet (Matplotlib 3.10) supplies an accessible color cycle |

Two further points. A logarithmic quantity spanning several orders of magnitude needs a logarithmic normalization (`norm=LogNorm()`) rather than a different colormap. And a qualitative palette of more than about seven colors is no longer readable: the series should then be grouped, highlighted selectively, or split into small multiples.

### 6.3.2. Color Vision Deficiency

About 8 % of men and 0.5 % of women of Northern European ancestry have a red–green color vision deficiency (deuteranomaly being the most common form). Red and green as the two poles of a heatmap, or as two lines of a plot, are therefore indistinguishable to a substantial part of the readers. The remedies:

- use palettes designed for it: Okabe–Ito for categories, `viridis` and `cividis` for magnitudes, blue–orange or blue–red rather than red–green for diverging data;
- encode identity redundantly: direct labels, marker shapes, line styles, panel positions;
- check the figure with a simulation of color vision deficiency (image editors and browser developer tools provide one) and in grayscale.

## 6.4. Small Multiples

When a figure has to show many series, drawing them all in one panel produces a tangle in which no single series can be followed. The alternative, called *small multiples* by Edward Tufte, is a grid of small panels with identical axes and scales, each showing one series or one subset of the data; because the scales agree, the eye compares the panels by position alone.

![Eight synthetic expression time courses. In one panel with eight colors (left) the patterns are hard to separate. As small multiples with shared axes (right), each gene is highlighted against the others in gray, and the circadian, induced, repressed, transient and flat profiles are recognized at a glance.](./plots/small-multiples.py)

In Matplotlib, small multiples are a grid created with `sharex=True, sharey=True`; seaborn creates them from a long-format table with one argument (`col="gene", col_wrap=4`), and ggplot2 calls them facets. Shared axes are essential: panels with independently scaled axes invite exactly the comparisons that they make invalid. Showing the other series in light gray behind the highlighted one, as above, keeps the context without the clutter.

## 6.5. Common Mistakes

Most misleading figures are not deliberate; they result from defaults and habits. The following list covers the frequent cases.

![Two ways axes mislead. A bar axis starting at 95 % turns a 4 % difference in viability into an apparent fivefold one; with a zero-based axis the difference appears in proportion. Two genes on a dual y axis seem to follow the same curve, but only because each axis was scaled to its own data; indexed to their values at 0 h on one axis, gene A triples while gene B grows by less than half.](./plots/misleading-axes.py)

| Mistake | Why it misleads | Better |
|---|---|---|
| bar axis not starting at zero | bar length is read as the quantity, so a truncated axis exaggerates differences | start bars at zero, or use points, whose position need not be compared with zero |
| dual y axis | the two scales are arbitrary, so any two increasing series can be made to coincide or cross anywhere | two panels with a shared x axis, or both series on one axis after indexing to a common baseline |
| rainbow colormap | non-monotonic lightness creates false boundaries and hides real ones; unreadable with color vision deficiency | a perceptually uniform colormap (§6.3) |
| independent scales in panels meant to be compared | equal heights look like equal values | `sharey=True`, or state clearly that scales differ |
| 3D bars, perspective pies, shadows | perspective distorts lengths and areas | plain 2D marks |
| overplotting (thousands of points as opaque dots) | dense regions saturate and outliers dominate | transparency, smaller markers, hexbin or 2D histograms, density contours |
| unlabeled error bars, missing units, unexplained abbreviations | the figure cannot be interpreted on its own | axis labels with units; the caption states $n$ and what the error bars are |
| a log scale without saying so, or a linear scale for multiplicative data | ratios and growth rates become unreadable | log axes for concentrations, fold changes and growth, labeled as such |

A useful test before publishing a figure is to ask what a reader would conclude from it in ten seconds, and whether that is what the data support.

## 6.6. Statistical Graphics with seaborn

seaborn builds on Matplotlib and pandas: its functions take a long-format DataFrame ([4.4.5](/4.4.5)) and map columns to visual properties (position, color, size, panel), computing the necessary statistics (means, confidence intervals, density estimates, regression fits) on the way. Its classic API has two levels:

- *axes-level* functions (`scatterplot`, `lineplot`, `histplot`, `kdeplot`, `boxplot`, `violinplot`, `stripplot`, `heatmap`) draw into one Matplotlib Axes, passed as `ax=`, so they can be mixed with Matplotlib code in a custom layout;
- *figure-level* functions (`relplot`, `displot`, `catplot`, `lmplot`, and `pairplot` for a **scatterplot matrix**, `clustermap` for a clustered heatmap) create a whole figure, including small multiples via `row=` and `col=`, and return a grid object.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import seaborn as sns

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
t = np.tile(np.arange(0, 25, 2.0), 6)
strain = np.repeat(["WT", "mutA", "mutB"], 26)
rep = np.tile(np.repeat([1, 2], 13), 3)
rate = {"WT": 0.45, "mutA": 0.35, "mutB": 0.25}
od = np.array([1.2 / (1 + 30 * np.exp(-rate[s] * x)) for s, x in zip(strain, t)])
growth = pd.DataFrame({"time_h": t, "strain": strain, "replicate": rep,
                       "od600": od * rng.lognormal(0, 0.05, t.size)})

g = sns.relplot(data=growth, x="time_h", y="od600", hue="strain",
                kind="line", errorbar="sd")
g.set(yscale="log", xlabel="Time (h)", ylabel="OD600")
g.savefig("growth.pdf")
```

Since version 0.12, seaborn also has an *objects interface* (`seaborn.objects`), modeled on the *grammar of graphics* of Leland Wilkinson that also underlies ggplot2 in R: a plot is composed from data, a mapping of variables to visual properties, marks (`Dot`, `Line`, `Bar`, `Area`), statistical transformations (`Agg`, `Est`, `Hist`, `KDE`, `PolyFit`) and scales, each added as a separate layer. The same figure in this style:

```python
import seaborn.objects as so

(so.Plot(growth, x="time_h", y="od600", color="strain")
   .add(so.Dots(alpha=0.4))            # every measurement
   .add(so.Line(), so.Agg())           # mean at each time point
   .scale(y="log")
   .label(x="Time (h)", y="OD600")
   .save("growth-objects.pdf"))
```

The objects interface is still marked experimental in seaborn 0.13 (the current release), but its layered structure is easier to extend than the classic functions. In both styles the result is an ordinary Matplotlib figure, which can be adjusted with the methods of §6.1 before saving.

## 6.7. Interactive Figures with Plotly

For exploring data, an interactive figure that shows the values under the cursor, zooms into a region and hides series on a click can be more useful than any static plot: which sample is the outlier, which gene is that point in a volcano plot. Plotly renders figures in the browser with JavaScript; its high-level module `plotly.express` takes a long-format DataFrame like seaborn:

```python
import plotly.express as px

fig = px.line(growth, x="time_h", y="od600", color="strain",
              line_group="replicate", log_y=True,
              hover_data=["replicate"],
              labels={"time_h": "Time (h)", "od600": "OD600"})
fig.write_html("growth.html", include_plotlyjs="cdn")
```

The resulting HTML file (about 13 kB here, loading the Plotly library from a CDN) opens in any browser and can be shared or embedded in a report; in Jupyter the figure is displayed directly. `plotly.graph_objects` gives full control over traces and layout, and Dash builds web applications around Plotly figures. Plotly 7.0 (August 2026), built on plotly.js 4, removed the old Mapbox-based map traces in favor of MapLibre-based ones and several long-deprecated figure factories, so older examples may need adjustment. Alternatives with a similar purpose are Bokeh and Altair, and for large images and volumes in microscopy, napari.

Interactive figures complement but do not replace static ones: journals print static figures, an interactive figure hides information behind the cursor, and its appearance depends on the viewer's software. A common workflow uses interactive plots to explore and Matplotlib or seaborn to produce the final figure.

## 6.8. A Checklist for Publication-Quality Figures

1. Content: one message per figure; the type of chart fits the question (§6.2); raw data are shown when $n$ is small.
2. Text: every axis has a label with units; fonts are legible at print size (7–9 pt) and consistent across panels; abbreviations are explained in the caption.
3. Uncertainty: error bars and bands are defined in the caption (standard deviation, standard error or confidence interval), together with $n$ and what a replicate is.
4. Scales: bars start at zero; panels that are compared share their scales; logarithmic axes are used for multiplicative quantities and labeled.
5. Color: a perceptually uniform or diverging colormap as appropriate, centered when diverging; red–green avoided; identity also encoded by labels or shapes; the figure still works in grayscale.
6. Layout: panels are aligned and lettered; legends are replaced by direct labels where possible; no 3D effects, shadows or unnecessary gridlines and frames.
7. Export: final size, vector format for line art, raster at 300–600 dpi for images, fonts embedded.
8. Reproducibility: the figure is produced by a script from the data, with fixed random seeds, so it can be regenerated when the data or the reviewers change.

## Summary

- Use Matplotlib's object-oriented interface: create the Figure and Axes with `plt.subplots` or `subplot_mosaic`, then call methods on them; use `layout="constrained"`.
- Choose the chart from the question: distributions, relationships, trends, comparisons, matrices; show the data points when there are few, and avoid bars of means for measurements.
- Use perceptually uniform sequential colormaps for magnitudes, diverging colormaps centered at a meaningful value for deviations, few distinct colors for categories; never a rainbow colormap; design for color vision deficiency.
- Small multiples with shared axes show many series better than one crowded panel.
- Truncated bar axes, dual y axes, inconsistent scales and unlabeled error bars mislead.
- seaborn turns long-format tables into statistical graphics, with small multiples and a grammar-of-graphics objects interface; Plotly produces interactive HTML figures for exploration.
- Produce figures by scripts, at their final size, in vector formats where possible.
