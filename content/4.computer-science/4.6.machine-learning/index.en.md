**Machine learning** studies how programs improve with experience: by Mitchell's definition, a program learns if its performance at a task $T$, as measured by $P$, improves with experience $E$. This course treats the methods and the experimental methodology of classical machine learning and answers two kinds of questions. The first is how to learn: for different data and assumptions, which representation and learning algorithm to choose, ranging from the **naive Bayes classifier**, which rests on probabilistic reasoning, through **k-nearest neighbors**, which builds no explicit model, and clustering, which uses no labels, to **ensemble learning**, which combines several learners, and on to the **hidden Markov model**, which models data with an order. The second is how to know whether the learning is good: data are always finite, so the accuracy on a test set is itself a random variable, and one needs a reliable experimental design, suitable evaluation metrics, and statistical methods for deciding whether a difference is real. In many textbooks the second kind of question occupies a section or two; this course gives it the same weight as the algorithms.

The chapters are organized around the design of a learning system. The first chapter uses the example of a program that learns to play checkers to pose four design questions (which experience to use, which function to learn, how to represent it, and which algorithm to learn it with), and each later chapter is a different answer to them. The second chapter first discusses how to judge an answer: the split into training, validation and test sets, **cross-validation**, metrics for classification and ranking, and the most insidious error, data leakage. The following chapters present different learning methods: Bayesian learning regards learning as updating the probabilities of hypotheses by Bayes' formula; instance-based learning stores only the examples and predicts by analogy according to similarity, and the curse of dimensionality and computational cost lead to kd-trees and approximate nearest neighbor search; unsupervised learning turns to unlabeled data and discusses similarity and the various clustering algorithms; ensemble learning explains why a combination of many learners is more accurate and more stable than a single one, and presents bagging, random forests and **boosting**. The penultimate chapter returns to evaluation and uses confidence intervals and hypothesis tests to answer "what is the true error rate of a hypothesis" and "which of two learning algorithms is better"; the last chapter abandons the assumption that examples are independent and identically distributed, describes sequences with Markov models and hidden Markov models, and solves their inference and decoding problems by **dynamic programming**.

This course divides the work with the other two artificial intelligence courses: search, games, support vector machines and decision trees are taught in [Introduction to Artificial Intelligence](/4.5), while neural networks and deep learning (including recurrent neural networks, Transformers and large language models) are concentrated in [Artificial Neural Networks](/4.7). The statistical theory behind many methods has been covered in statistics, so this course only links to it rather than repeating it, and concentrates on the machine learning viewpoint and practice: Bayes' formula and Markov chains are in [Probability Theory](/3.1); confidence intervals, hypothesis tests and [Bayesian Inference](/3.2.7) are in [Statistical Inference](/3.2); overfitting and nonparametric regression are in [Linear Regression Analysis](/3.3); discriminant analysis, k-means and hierarchical clustering are in [Multivariate Statistical Analysis](/3.4); and the **EM algorithm** and the **bootstrap** are in [Statistical Computing and Software](/3.5). The implementation of kd-trees is in [Data Structures and Algorithms](/4.3), and the idea of dynamic programming is in [Programming Fundamentals](/4.1).
