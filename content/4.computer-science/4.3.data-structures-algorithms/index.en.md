[Programming Fundamentals](/4.1) is about how to devise a solution: recursion, divide and conquer, backtracking, dynamic programming. This course answers two other questions: whether a solution is correct and how much it costs, and how to organize data so that the common operations are all fast enough. The first question needs a machine-independent measure, namely **asymptotic notation** and the methods of complexity analysis, including the master theorem for divide-and-conquer recurrences, **amortized analysis** and expected-case analysis of randomized algorithms; the second question is the subject of the various **data structures**. Each data structure is first presented as an **abstract data type** with an interface, then implemented, and the cost of every operation is proved; between different implementations of the same set of operations there is often a trade-off among access, modification and space.

The main thread of the chapters runs from linear structures to semilinear structures, and then to nonlinear structures. After the introduction builds the tools for analyzing algorithms, the course begins with linear structures: the **vector** wraps an array into an abstract data type that can grow dynamically, and a sorted vector supports binary search in logarithmic time; the **list** links independent nodes by pointers, trading random access for constant-time insertion and deletion; **stacks** and **queues** are restricted sequences that underlie function calls, expression evaluation, backtracking and level-by-level search. A **tree** is not a linear structure, but once a traversal order is fixed it acquires linear features, which is why it is called semilinear: after **binary trees** and their traversals, the **binary search tree** carries binary search over to trees, balanced trees (AVL trees, splay trees, B-trees, red-black trees) keep the height logarithmic, and interval trees, segment trees, kd-trees and others apply search trees to geometric range queries. A graph allows a relation between any two objects and is the most general nonlinear structure for expressing relations: breadth-first and depth-first search turn a graph into a traversal tree, on which algorithms for topological sorting, biconnected components, shortest paths and minimum spanning trees are built. The last chapters discuss several kinds of problems in which data are accessed in other ways: the **dictionary** locates data directly by the key itself through hashing, the **priority queue** cares only about the currently most important element, **string matching** looks for a pattern in a long text, and sorting and selection gather the methods of the earlier chapters on the problems studied most thoroughly in the algorithms curriculum.

This course sits at the center of the prerequisite relations among computer science courses. It builds directly on the algorithmic ideas of [Programming Fundamentals](/4.1) and the classes and templates of [Object-Oriented Programming](/4.2), and the implementation principles of the various C++ standard library containers are all found here. The mathematical theory of graphs comes from [graph theory](/1.4) in discrete mathematics; this course concentrates on how graphs are represented in computers, how algorithms are implemented, and their complexity. The B-tree is the basis of indexes in [Database Systems](/4.13), the call stack and the priority queue in scheduling reappear in [Operating Systems](/4.11), graph traversal is the starting point of the search algorithms in [Introduction to Artificial Intelligence](/4.5), and regular expression matching uses the finite automata of [Formal Languages and Automata](/4.8).

If [Object-Oriented Programming](/4.2) provides a perspective for looking at the world, then this course provides, more concretely, a perspective for looking at data. The perspective I discuss in [graph theory](/1.4) was inspired by this course: data structures can be classified by the relations among their elements into linear (vectors, lists, stacks and queues), semilinear (trees) and nonlinear (graphs). Data in computational biology happen to cover all three. Sequence data are of course linear; comparing, clustering and inferring evolutionary relationships among sequences form trees; and studying interactions between molecules forms graphs. The complexity of the three kinds of structure rises in turn: linear and semilinear structures have a natural traversal order and a recursive decomposition, whereas a general graph has neither, and many basic problems on it are computationally hard. The difficulty of research grows accordingly, which roughly represents the trend of development in bioinformatics.

Similar courses offered for other departments are mostly called "Data Structures and Algorithms", while this course of the computer science department is called only "Data Structures" for historical reasons about naming, although algorithms carry just as much weight in it. It is one of the courses with the heaviest workload that I have taken, but also one of those with the most complete teaching system: every data structure comes with a complete implementation, rigorous complexity proofs and carefully designed programming assignments. Even for those who do not take this course, I recommend trying a similar course with lower requirements. It holds a central position in the prerequisite relations of computer science courses, and with a foundation in data structures and algorithms one can attempt almost any computer science course.
