This course discusses neural networks trained from data, that is, **deep learning**: how networks are built, how their parameters are found, and why the trained models work. Its central questions come in three layers. The first is representation: which network structures capture the characteristics of different kinds of data such as images and language, so that a model can learn their regularities with relatively few parameters. The second is optimization: on a high-dimensional, nonconvex loss surface, how to train a network reliably and quickly with noisy gradients, and how to make it generalize to new data. The third is scale: when a model has hundreds of billions of parameters and training takes thousands of GPUs, how to organize computation and storage, and what new capabilities growth in scale brings. The course runs from the simplest artificial neuron to today's large language models and reasoning models.

The chapters start from basic networks and training methods and move step by step toward large models. The first chapter introduces the development of deep learning and collects the machine learning concepts needed in all later chapters: capacity and generalization, regularization, hyperparameters, and the loss function as a negative log-likelihood. The basic models come next: starting from the **perceptron** and its convergence theorem, the XOR problem leads to the **multilayer perceptron**, **backpropagation** is derived in full and viewed as a special case of reverse-mode automatic differentiation on a computational graph. The next two threads design structures for two kinds of data: the **convolutional neural network** builds locality and translation invariance into the network, developing from LeNet to residual networks; the **recurrent neural network** processes a sequence step by step through a hidden state, the gated structures LSTM and GRU alleviate the difficulty of long-range dependencies, and word embeddings turn words into vectors. Between the two threads is a chapter on training techniques: optimizers, learning rate schedules, normalization and dropout. The **attention mechanism** removes the sequential bottleneck of recurrent networks and yields the **Transformer**, the common architecture of everything that follows. **Pre-training** and **fine-tuning** divide learning into two stages: first learn general representations from massive unlabeled data, then adapt to various tasks with a small amount of data or with prompts. Training and deploying such models requires dedicated infrastructure (mixed precision, data and model parallelism, FlashAttention). The **scaling law**, **alignment** with human preferences, reasoning models trained with verifiable rewards, and new architectures such as the **mixture of experts** and the **state space model** explain how large language models arrived at where they are today. The last part of the course moves from language to multimodality, and then to agent models that can plan on their own and call tools.

This is the most in-depth of the three artificial intelligence courses and covers deep learning in full. The neural network chapter of [Introduction to Artificial Intelligence](/4.5) gives the intuition for neurons, gradient descent and backpropagation, and here they are given in rigorous form; the methodology of classical machine learning (the design of learning systems, experiments and evaluation) and classical sequence models such as Markov models are in [Introduction to Machine Learning](/4.6). The mathematical foundations come from statistics: maximum likelihood estimation is in [Statistical Inference](/3.2), model selection and overfitting in [Linear Regression Analysis](/3.3), floating-point numbers, gradient descent and stochastic gradient descent in [Statistical Computing and Software](/3.5); matrix libraries and numerical computing in Python are in [Scientific Computing with Python](/4.4). The practical use of large language models as **coding agents** is in [AI-Assisted Programming](/4.2.14).
