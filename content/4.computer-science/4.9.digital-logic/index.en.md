This course studies how computation, control and storage are realized with signals that take only two values and with switching networks built from transistors. It sits at the lowest level of computer hardware: the adders, decoders, registers, counters and control state machines of a processor are ultimately combinations of gates and flip-flops. Two central questions run through the course. The first is function: how to turn a requirement given as a truth table or a state diagram systematically into a correct circuit, and make that circuit as simple as possible. The second is physics: real gates have voltage ranges, delays and power consumption, so circuits exhibit hazards and the clock frequency has an upper limit, and a design must remain reliable under these constraints. At the end the course moves from hand wiring to the modern way of designing, in which circuits are written in a hardware description language and implemented on an FPGA.

The chapters lead from circuits without memory to circuits with memory, and then to programmable circuits. After the introduction collects number systems and codes, the chapter on logic algebra recasts **Boolean algebra** in the language of circuit design and gives three minimization methods: algebraic manipulation, Karnaugh maps and the Quine–McCluskey method. The chapter on logic gates, centered on CMOS, shows how ideal logic operations are realized with transistors and introduces the external characteristics of gates: noise margins, fan-out, delay and power. On this basis, the chapter on **combinational logic circuits** gives the general methods of analysis and design, the medium-scale components such as decoders and multiplexers, and the races and hazards caused by gate delays; the chapter on arithmetic circuits starts from the full adder, parallelizes the carry computation by carry lookahead, and ends with the **arithmetic logic unit**. Feedback lets a circuit remember the past: latches and **flip-flops** are the basic storage elements, and their timing parameters and metastability determine whether they sample correctly. Synchronous sequential circuits, built from flip-flops and combinational logic, have the **finite-state machine** as their mathematical model, a design method that leads from function to circuit, and a maximum clock frequency given by the setup-time constraint; counters and registers are the most direct applications of this method. The last chapter follows the development of programmable devices from ROM and PLA to CPLD and FPGA, and introduces **hardware description languages**, mainly SystemVerilog, together with the flow of simulation and synthesis and the principles of synchronous design: the minimization, state assignment and timing analysis done by hand in the earlier chapters are here carried out automatically by tools.

The mathematics of the course comes from [Discrete Mathematics](/1): logic algebra corresponds operation by operation to the connectives of propositional logic ([1.1.2](/1.1.2)) and is the two-element case of Boolean algebra in [Lattice Theory](/1.3.4), and state reduction uses equivalence relations and partitions ([Set Theory](/1.2)). The model of synchronous sequential circuits shares its origin with the finite automata of [Formal Languages and Automata](/4.8) ([4.8.4](/4.8.4)), and state reduction is essentially the same algorithm as DFA minimization. The encoding of numbers and **two's complement** are in [Computer Representation of Numbers](/3.5.1). The course supplies the components of [Computer Organization](/4.10) directly: the arithmetic unit, register file and datapath there are built from the circuits here, and the processor labs are implemented on an FPGA with the methods of this course.
