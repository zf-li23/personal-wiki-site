Quantum physics studies the laws of motion of microscopic particles. At the end of the nineteenth century, classical physics could explain most phenomena in mechanics, thermal physics and electromagnetism, but it never gave the correct law for the frequency distribution of thermal radiation; from Planck's energy quantum hypothesis in 1900 to the establishment of quantum mechanics in the 1920s, physics replaced the classical deterministic picture with an entirely new set of concepts: energy can be quantized, light and matter particles both show **wave–particle duality**, the state of a particle is described by a **wave function**, and the results of measurement can only be predicted probabilistically. This course corresponds to the quantum physics part of University Physics B (2), and its core questions are: where classical theory fails, how the state of a microscopic particle is described and how it evolves in time; and how to use this theory to understand the properties of atoms, molecules and solids.

The first three chapters establish the basic framework of quantum mechanics. Chapter 1 shows the quantum nature of light through blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect and Compton scattering, and introduces the **photon**; Chapter 2 conversely shows the wave nature of matter particles: matter waves are probability waves, the essence of wave behavior is the coherent superposition of probability amplitudes, and the coexistence of particle and wave behavior invalidates the classical notion of trajectory, whose quantitative expression is the **Heisenberg uncertainty relation**; Chapter 3 writes down the **Schrödinger equation** and uses three one-dimensional models, the infinite square well, the potential barrier and the harmonic oscillator, to illustrate energy quantization, zero-point energy and tunneling. The last three chapters apply it to real physical systems. Chapter 4 solves the hydrogen atom, introduces electron spin and identical particles, and explains the electron configurations in the periodic table by the **Pauli exclusion principle**; Chapter 5 discusses applications of atomic and molecular energy levels: X-ray spectra, the stimulated emission underlying the laser, and the vibrational and rotational spectra of molecules; Chapter 6 uses the free electron gas and energy band models to understand electrons in solids, explaining the difference between conductors, semiconductors and insulators and the operation of p-n junctions and semiconductor devices.

Quantum physics uses more mathematics than the earlier courses: the time-independent Schrödinger equation is solved by separation of variables from [Partial Differential Equations](/2.5), the angular solutions of the hydrogen atom are the **spherical harmonics**, energy quantization is an **eigenvalue** problem, and the energy bands of the tight-binding model are the eigenvalues of a tridiagonal matrix from [Linear Algebra](/2.1); $\lvert\Psi\rvert^2$ as a probability density brings [Probability Theory](/3.1) into the fundamental laws of physics. It builds on [Mechanics](/5.1), [Thermal Physics](/5.2), [Electromagnetism](/5.3) and [Optics](/5.4), and also corrects their conclusions at the microscopic scale. Quantum physics is the key to understanding [Chemistry](/6): atomic orbitals, quantum numbers and the periodic table are the starting point of [Inorganic Chemistry](/6.1), chemical bonding and molecular spectra belong to [Physical Chemistry](/6.3), and the various spectroscopic methods are tools of [Analytical Chemistry](/6.4). In [Biology](/7), absorption spectra, fluorescence and nuclear magnetic resonance are common means of studying biological macromolecules ([Biophysics](/7.5)), and the absorption and transfer of energy from light in photosynthesis also have to be described in the language of quanta.

I remember hearing a saying in class: the better you have learned the earlier material, the less you may understand quantum physics. There is some truth in this. Classical physics trains us to believe that a particle has a definite position and velocity at every moment and that the future can be deduced once the initial state is known; quantum theory asks us to give up this picture, since results of measurement can only be predicted probabilistically, and the uncertainty relation is not a limitation of measurement technique but a property of the microscopic state itself. The more accustomed one is to classical deterministic thinking, the more easily one tries to find a classical explanation of "where the particle really is" for quantum phenomena, and falls into confusion. But what has to be put down is the classical picture, not rigor: the mathematics of quantum mechanics is completely deterministic, the Schrödinger equation uniquely determines the evolution of the wave function, and only the results of measurement are probabilistic. The Schrödinger equation here is certainly very helpful for understanding atomic structure in chemistry, as orbitals, quantum numbers and the periodic table all come from the solution of the hydrogen atom and the Pauli exclusion principle; but for the present needs of our major, there are few occasions to use it directly. So this course can be taken as a story course: it tells how physics at the beginning of the twentieth century was pushed out of the classical picture by one experiment after another, and it is very interesting to learn.
