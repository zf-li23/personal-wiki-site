Electromagnetism studies how charges and currents produce electric and magnetic fields, how electromagnetic fields act on charges and currents, how electric and magnetic fields convert into each other, and how electromagnetic fields interact with matter. Unlike mechanics and thermal physics, its protagonist is the field: the vectors $\boldsymbol{E}$ and $\boldsymbol{B}$ defined at every point of space and varying in time, with charged particles serving merely as sources of the field. There are two basic global quantities for describing a vector field, the flux through a surface and the circulation along a closed path, and the basic laws of electromagnetism are written in terms of exactly these two. This course corresponds to the electromagnetism part of University Physics B (2).

The chapters follow the main line "from static to dynamic, from vacuum to matter". Chapters 1 and 2 study the electrostatic field in vacuum: Coulomb's law and the superposition principle give the field strength, Gauss's law describes its flux, and the circulation theorem shows that it is a **conservative vector field**, so that the **electric potential** can be introduced and electrostatic problems reduce to the **Poisson equation**. Chapters 3 and 4 put matter into the field: the electrostatic equilibrium of a conductor is a boundary value problem for the Laplace equation, with the uniqueness theorem and the method of images as tools for solving it; the polarization of dielectrics is described by the polarization and the electric displacement, and the **capacitor** is its most important application. Steady currents in Chapter 5 are the first step from electrostatics to electrodynamics, covering the continuity equation for current, Ohm's law, electromotive force and circuit laws. Chapters 6 to 8 turn to magnetic fields: the **Biot–Savart law** and **Ampère's circuital law** give the magnetic field produced by currents, the **Lorentz force** describes the action of the magnetic field on moving charges, and special relativity shows that electric and magnetic forces are manifestations of the same interaction in different reference frames; magnetic media are treated in complete parallel with dielectrics, while ferromagnets have special properties such as hysteresis. **Electromagnetic induction** in Chapter 9 truly connects electricity and magnetism: a changing magnetic field excites an induced electric field, and the energy of the magnetic field follows from self-inductance. Chapter 10 adds the **displacement current**, writes down **Maxwell's equations**, and derives from them the **electromagnetic wave** traveling at the speed of light, thereby unifying electricity, magnetism and light.

The mathematical language of electromagnetism is the field theory in [Calculus](/2.2): the **divergence**, **curl**, **divergence theorem** and **Stokes' theorem** connect the integral and differential forms of the equations; boundary value problems in electrostatics and the equations of electromagnetic waves belong to [Partial Differential Equations](/2.5), and RC, RL and LC circuits are first- and second-order [ordinary differential equations](/2.4). Within physics, it builds on the potential energy of [Mechanics](/5.1) and on special relativity, and it is the theoretical source of [Optics](/5.4); the magnetism and electrical conductivity of matter are ultimately explained by [Quantum Physics](/5.5). Chemical bonding and intermolecular interactions are in the end electrostatic interactions, and electrochemistry and spectroscopic analysis are also based on electromagnetism ([Chemistry](/6)). In [Biology](/7), a cell membrane can be viewed as a leaky capacitor, and the conduction of nerve impulses can be described by circuit models ([Computational Neuroscience](/7.16)); the electrostatic interactions between biological macromolecules, electrophoresis, and nuclear magnetic resonance and magnetic resonance imaging are all applications of electromagnetism in [Biophysics](/7.6).

Like mechanics, electromagnetism is hard, and more abstract: the objects of mechanics are visible bodies, whereas the protagonist of electromagnetism is the invisible field, and understanding it requires switching back and forth between integral and differential forms while imagining a vector at every point of space. I think the finest part is of course Maxwell's equations: the laws established separately in the first nine chapters combine into one set of equations once the displacement current is added, which not only connects electricity and magnetism but also yields electromagnetic waves and calculates their speed to be exactly the speed of light. Although electromagnetism is the most laborious to study, its exam may be somewhat easier than that of mechanics, and I myself mastered it through a concentrated cram before the exam.
