Optics studies the generation and propagation of light and its interaction with matter, and is usually divided into geometric optics, wave optics and quantum optics. This course corresponds to the optics part of University Physics B (2) and covers wave optics: light is an **electromagnetic wave**, and its wave nature appears in three kinds of phenomena, interference, diffraction and polarization. The core questions are: under what conditions two or more light beams can superpose coherently and how the intensity is distributed after superposition; how light deviates from rectilinear propagation when it meets an obstacle or an aperture, and how this limits the resolving power of optical instruments; and what states of vibration light has as a transverse wave, and how to produce, test and convert them.

The three chapters proceed from the superposition of a finite number of beams, to the superposition of infinitely many wavelets, to the direction of vibration of a transverse wave. Chapter 1 discusses **interference**: starting from Young's double slit, it explains the conditions for coherent light and temporal and spatial coherence, treats phase differences in different media uniformly with the optical path, and then discusses the fringes of equal thickness and equal inclination in thin films and the Michelson interferometer. Chapter 2 discusses **diffraction**: the Huygens–Fresnel principle takes the coherent superposition of infinitely many wavelets on a wavefront as the source of the diffracted field, from which the intensity distributions of single-slit Fraunhofer diffraction and grating diffraction are obtained, the resolution limit set by circular-aperture diffraction is explained, and finally X-ray diffraction is discussed with a crystal as a three-dimensional grating. Chapter 3 discusses **polarization**: it describes linear, elliptical and **circularly polarized light**, introduces several ways of producing polarized light (polarizers, reflection and refraction with the Brewster angle, scattering and crystal birefringence), uses wave plates to convert between polarization states, and discusses the interference of polarized light, artificial birefringence and optical rotation.

Optics is a direct application of [Electromagnetism](/5.3): the wave equation of light comes from Maxwell's equations. Mathematically, the superposition of light is the same as the composition of oscillations of the same frequency in [Mechanics](/5.1), the various polarization states are compositions of two mutually perpendicular simple harmonic motions, and the amplitude distribution of Fraunhofer diffraction is the **Fourier transform** of the aperture function. The wave picture of light is supplemented by the photon picture in [Quantum Physics](/5.5), where the coherence of lasers and X-ray spectra are also discussed. In [Chemistry](/6), optical activity is the mark of chiral molecules, and spectroscopic analysis relies on grating dispersion. In [Biology](/7), the resolution limit of the light microscope is set by diffraction, X-ray crystallography determines the structure of biological macromolecules from diffraction patterns, and the polarizing microscope is used to observe ordered structures such as fibers and crystals ([Biophysics](/7.6)).

Although optics has only three short chapters, it contributes some of the coolest experiments of the entire university physics course, and it has many interesting intersections with biology. Most of the blue and purple colors on animals are structural colors: they arise not from the absorption of pigments but from the interference and scattering of light by micro- and nanoscale structures. The main mechanisms are multilayer thin-film interference ([5.4.1](/5.4.1)) and the coherent scattering of photonic crystals and quasi-ordered structures, while grating-like structures ([5.4.2](/5.4.2)) are only one of the rarer kinds; structural colors often change with viewing angle and have a brilliant metallic luster. Polarization is equally common in the living world ([5.4.3](/5.4.3)): the cuticle of some scarab beetles (such as the genus *Chrysina*) reflects circularly polarized light, the eyes of mantis shrimp can perceive circularly polarized light, and bees and ants navigate by the polarization direction of scattered skylight.
