Mechanics studies the mechanical motion of bodies and how changes of motion relate to the interactions between bodies. It is the foundation of the other branches of physics: the kinetic theory in thermal physics treats molecular collisions with mechanics, charged particles in electromagnetism move according to Newton's laws, and momentum, angular momentum and energy in quantum mechanics are all concepts inherited from mechanics. This course corresponds to the mechanics part of University Physics B (1), and its core questions are four: how to describe motion; how force changes motion; which quantities remain unchanged during motion; and how these laws must be modified when speeds approach the speed of light.

Chapter 1 uses the calculus of vector-valued functions to describe the position, velocity and acceleration of a particle, and discusses how velocities transform between different **reference frames**. Newton's laws of motion in Chapter 2 relate force to acceleration, and solving a motion problem usually reduces to a differential equation; in non-inertial frames, introducing inertial forces lets Newton's laws still be used. Chapters 3 and 4 discuss the cumulative effects of forces: the accumulation of force over time changes momentum, the accumulation of torque over time changes angular momentum, and the accumulation of force over space (work) changes kinetic energy; extended to systems of particles, these give the three conservation laws of momentum, angular momentum and energy, which by **Noether's theorem** correspond to the symmetries of spatial translation, spatial rotation and time translation. Chapter 5 applies these laws to **rigid bodies**: the law of rotation about a fixed axis corresponds term by term to Newton's second law, and **precession** appears when the angular momentum is not parallel to the angular velocity. Chapters 6 and 7 turn to oscillations and waves: **simple harmonic motion** is the most basic oscillation, damped and driven oscillations are described by linear ordinary differential equations, and a wave is the propagation of oscillation in a continuous medium made of countless coupled oscillators; interference, standing waves and the Doppler effect appear here for the first time and prepare the ground for optics. Chapter 8, special relativity, starts from the principle of relativity and the **invariance of the speed of light**, replaces the Galilean transformation with the **Lorentz transformation**, and rewrites the concepts of simultaneity, time and length, as well as the expressions for momentum and energy.

The mathematical tools of mechanics come from [Calculus](/2.2) (vector operations, vector-valued functions and multiple integrals), [Ordinary Differential Equations](/2.4) (equations of motion and of oscillation) and [Partial Differential Equations](/2.5) (d'Alembert's formula and separation of variables for the wave equation); motion that cannot be solved analytically can be integrated with the numerical methods of [Scientific Computing with Python](/4.4). It is the starting point of the later courses: [Thermal Physics](/5.2) applies mechanics to large numbers of molecules, [Electromagnetism](/5.3) uses special relativity when discussing magnetic forces, [Optics](/5.4) is built on the superposition of waves, and the Schrödinger equation in [Quantum Physics](/5.5) plays the role of Newton's second law here. In [Biology](/7), mechanics underlies the study of forces on muscles, bones and cells in [Biophysics](/7.5), and the method of describing the evolution of a system with differential equations in [Mathematical Modeling in Biology](/7.4) first took shape in mechanics.

Mechanics is what makes the other branches of physics discussable, and it is also the course most closely tied to high school physics; but university mechanics adds angular momentum and rigid-body rotation, and the cross product of vectors and the integral for the moment of inertia force many problems that could be solved by plugging into a formula in high school to be analyzed again from the theorems. I think it is the most flexibly examined and therefore the hardest of the courses. In biology, cell differentiation is often compared to a ball rolling down Waddington's epigenetic landscape: valleys stand for stable cell fates, and the branching points of the ridges stand for fate decisions. The metaphor looks like mechanics, but what it borrows is the picture of a potential surface and a dynamical system: the position of the ball represents the state of gene expression in the cell, it moves in the direction of steepest descent of a potential function (a gradient flow), and finally comes to rest at an attractor, a stable **equilibrium point** ([2.4.11](/2.4.11), [7.4.4](/7.4.4)); it has no inertia and does not oscillate back and forth at the bottom of the valley as a real ball would. So the metaphor has little to do with Newtonian mechanics; what really connects them is the idea of reading stable and unstable equilibria off the shape of the **potential energy curve** ([5.1.4](/5.1.4)).
