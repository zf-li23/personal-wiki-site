Thermal physics studies phenomena related to temperature: macroscopically they appear as changes in temperature, pressure, volume and phase, and microscopically they originate in the incessant random motion of a great many molecules. There are two complementary approaches to thermal phenomena. The statistical approach starts from a microscopic model of the molecules and uses probability to obtain macroscopic quantities, which reveals the nature of thermal phenomena; the thermodynamic approach starts from a few macroscopic experimental laws without reference to the microscopic structure of matter, and its conclusions are general and reliable. This course corresponds to the thermal part of University Physics B (1), and its core questions are: what are the microscopic meanings of temperature and pressure, and how are molecular speeds and energies distributed; what laws govern the conversion of energy between heat and work; and why do natural processes always proceed in one direction.

The first two chapters introduce the statistical approach. Chapter 1 establishes the concepts of equilibrium state and temperature and uses Newtonian mechanics and probability to derive the pressure of an **ideal gas**, the microscopic meaning of temperature, the equipartition theorem, the **Maxwell speed distribution** and the **Boltzmann distribution**; Chapter 2 removes the two idealizations of the ideal gas, using the van der Waals equation to describe the deviations of real gases, liquefaction and the critical point, and then obtaining the coefficients of the three transport processes, thermal conduction, viscosity and diffusion, from the mean free path of molecular collisions. The last two chapters turn to the thermodynamic approach. The **first law of thermodynamics** in Chapter 3 is conservation of energy including thermal phenomena: **internal energy** is defined through adiabatic work, heat is then defined from internal energy and work, and with these the work and heat of an ideal gas in various processes are computed, leading to heat capacity, enthalpy and the efficiency of cycles, with the upper bound on efficiency of the **Carnot cycle** preparing for the next chapter. The **second law of thermodynamics** in Chapter 4 gives the direction of natural processes: from the Kelvin and Clausius statements it derives Carnot's theorem and the thermodynamic temperature scale, from the Clausius equality it introduces the state function entropy and writes the second law as the **principle of entropy increase**; finally the **Boltzmann entropy formula** shows that entropy is the logarithm of the number of microstates and that the second law is a statistical law, and the two approaches meet here. The notation and sign conventions of this course (heat and work are both positive when received by the system) agree with physical chemistry.

The distributions and expectations used in the statistical approach come from [Probability Theory](/3.1); the macroscopic laws of transport processes (Fourier's law, Fick's law) are the physical origin of the heat equation in [Partial Differential Equations](/2.5) ([2.5.1](/2.5.1)). Within physics, thermal physics is based on [Mechanics](/5.1), and the Boltzmann distribution is generalized in [Quantum Physics](/5.5) to the Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein distributions. Thermal physics is a direct prerequisite of [Physical Chemistry](/6.3): chemical thermodynamics uses the internal energy, enthalpy and entropy introduced here, together with the Gibbs free energy and chemical potential, to discuss the direction and extent of chemical reactions and phase equilibria. In [Biology](/7), the diffusion of molecules in cells, the transport of substances across membranes, and the discussion of the stability of biological macromolecules in [Biophysics](/7.6) all start from the concepts here.

Ever since I began to express an interest in quantitative biology, my class advisor recommended that I take the more advanced statistical mechanics, but I doubt I will find the time for such a demanding course before I finish my undergraduate studies. The thermal physics here is certainly important: its concepts will appear many times in chemistry courses, and learning it well greatly helps with the physical chemistry that follows. But thermal physics that serves the modeling of living systems needs a language that goes deeper into microscopic mechanisms. University thermal physics takes ideal gases and heat engines as its main objects and studies equilibrium states and quasi-static processes, whereas molecules in a cell work in solution, in a nonequilibrium state that constantly consumes energy. Describing them requires obtaining free energies from microscopic models via the partition function, discussing protein folding and conformational changes with free-energy landscapes, and discussing molecular motors and biochemical reaction networks through the relation between fluctuation and dissipation. All of this is statistical mechanics, and the Boltzmann distribution and Boltzmann entropy formula in this course are exactly its entrance.
