Physics studies the fundamental structure of matter, the general laws of motion, and the interactions between bodies. Its method is to start from a few universal laws and use mathematics to derive conclusions that experiment can test quantitatively. This discipline collects five courses: [Mechanics](/5.1), [Thermal Physics](/5.2), [Electromagnetism](/5.3), [Optics](/5.4) and [Quantum Physics](/5.5), which together form a two-semester university physics sequence. The core questions are these: how force changes the motion of a body and which quantities are conserved during motion; how a system of many particles comes to show macroscopic properties such as temperature and pressure, and why natural processes have a direction; how charges and currents produce fields, and how fields propagate and act on matter; and why the classical deterministic picture fails at the atomic scale and what theory replaces it.

The order of the five courses is also the order in which the concepts of physics unfold. Mechanics starts from the kinematics of a particle, derives the three conservation laws of momentum, angular momentum and energy from Newton's laws, applies them to rigid bodies, oscillations and waves, and finally corrects our view of space and time with special relativity. Thermal physics has two complementary approaches: the kinetic theory of molecules treats the random motion of a great many molecules with mechanics and probability, while thermodynamics starts only from macroscopic experimental laws, using the first law to describe conservation of energy and the second law and **entropy** to describe the direction of processes; the two approaches meet in the Boltzmann entropy formula. In electromagnetism the protagonist changes from particles to fields: from the flux and circulation of the electrostatic field, through conductors, dielectrics, steady currents, magnetic fields and electromagnetic induction, the course arrives at **Maxwell's equations**, which predict electromagnetic waves traveling at the speed of light. Optics then becomes an application of electromagnetism, with interference, diffraction and polarization all manifestations of light as an electromagnetic wave. Quantum physics begins with blackbody radiation and the photoelectric effect, which classical theory could not explain, passes through matter waves and the uncertainty relation to the **Schrödinger equation**, and uses it to explain the structure of atoms, lasers and molecular spectra, and the energy bands of solids. The two "dark clouds" of the late nineteenth century led to relativity and quantum theory respectively, which are exactly the last and the first chapters of the mechanics and quantum physics courses.

Almost all the mathematical tools of physics come from [Mathematics](/2): the **vector fields**, **divergence** and **curl** of [Calculus](/2.2) are the language of electromagnetism; the equations of motion, oscillations and circuits are [ordinary differential equations](/2.4); the wave equation, the heat equation, the Poisson equation and separation of variables come from [partial differential equations](/2.5); and the tight-binding model of energy bands in solids is an eigenvalue problem from [Linear Algebra](/2.1). The Maxwell speed distribution and the statistical interpretation of the wave function use [Probability Theory](/3.1), and equations of motion that cannot be solved analytically can be handled with the numerical methods of [Scientific Computing with Python](/4.4). Physics is in turn the foundation of [Chemistry](/6): thermal physics is the starting point of chemical thermodynamics in [Physical Chemistry](/6.3), and quantum physics explains atomic structure and chemical bonding. In [Biology](/7), [Biophysics](/7.5) studies life directly with the methods of physics, and diffusion and transport, membrane potentials and nerve signals, X-ray diffraction and spectroscopy all use what is learned here; the dynamical models in [Mathematical Modeling in Biology](/7.4) and [Systems Biology](/7.10) continue the physicist's way of describing the evolution of a system with equations.

Compared with high school physics, university physics has one more powerful tool: calculus. In high school many of my classmates were physics olympiad contestants, and when they explained problems to me they would say that here one has to differentiate, there one has to integrate, and sometimes linear algebra came in as well; at university all of this became part of the official curriculum. With integration we can accumulate the effect of a force along a process and go directly from the initial state to the final state; with the language of vector fields our understanding of fields no longer stops at the picture of field lines, especially for **conservative vector fields**: conservative forces and potential energy, the electrostatic field and electric potential all rest on the same mathematical structure, namely that the circulation of the field along any closed path is zero, so that the field can be written as the gradient of a scalar function. These tools appear everywhere in mechanics and electromagnetism, the two main and hardest courses. The five courses felt very different to me. Mechanics is the closest to high school, yet because of rotation and angular momentum it is the most flexibly examined; electromagnetism is the most abstract, and the unification of electricity and magnetism by Maxwell's equations is the finest moment of the whole course; optics has only three short chapters but contributes the coolest demonstration experiments; and quantum physics is like a course that tells the story of the revolution in twentieth-century physics.
