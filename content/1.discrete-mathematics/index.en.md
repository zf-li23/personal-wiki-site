Discrete mathematics studies discrete objects and their structure. Its objects are usually finite or countable in number and separate from one another, rather than varying continuously like points on the real line. Its concern is not limits and continuity but things that can be listed one by one and built up step by step: propositions and inferences, sets and relations, algebraic systems equipped with operations, and graphs made of vertices and edges. Accordingly, this subject is divided into four courses: mathematical logic, set theory, algebraic structures, and graph theory.

The recommended reading order is [Mathematical Logic](/1.1) → [Set Theory](/1.2) → [Algebraic Structures](/1.3) → [Graph Theory](/1.4). Mathematical logic provides a precise language and rules of inference, and makes explicit what counts as a valid argument. Set theory uses this language to build mathematical objects: starting from sets, it defines relations and functions, constructs the natural numbers, integers, rationals, and reals, and compares the sizes of infinite sets. Algebraic structures adds operations to sets and studies the laws shared by systems such as **groups**, **rings**, **fields**, and **lattices**; the notions of **equivalence relation**, **quotient set**, and **partial order** it relies on come directly from set theory. Graph theory studies graphs, which consist of a vertex set and a binary relation on it. It, too, rests on sets and relations, and it connects to linear algebra through the **adjacency matrix** and the **incidence matrix**; since it uses few algebraic tools, it can be studied in parallel with algebraic structures.

Discrete mathematics is one of the theoretical foundations of computer science. **Propositional logic** and **Boolean algebra** are the mathematical model of digital circuits, **resolution** is the starting point of automated theorem proving, finite fields and groups are used in coding theory and cryptography, and trees and graphs are central objects in data structures and algorithm design. For other mathematics courses, set theory provides a common language, algebraic structures lead to abstract algebra and representation theory, and partial orders and lattices appear in topology, measure theory, and the semantics of programming languages. In computational biology, phylogenetic trees, gene regulatory networks, and protein–protein interaction networks are all graphs, and the analysis of sequences and symmetries relies on combinatorial and algebraic methods.

I believe discrete mathematics should be more than a foundational course for computer science students; it deserves a place in general education. Mathematical logic and set theory deal with the foundations of mathematics itself: why an argument is valid, and how an object can be constructed from as few assumptions as possible. Looking at science again from this level makes it clear what premises the forms of reasoning and the concepts we use by default each depend on, and that is valuable training for researchers in any discipline.
