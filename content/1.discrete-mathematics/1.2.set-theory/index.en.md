Set theory is the basic language of modern mathematics. The notion of a set is itself too primitive to be defined rigorously, yet almost every mathematical object can be built out of sets. The course begins with how sets are described, the relations and operations between sets, the laws these operations obey and the cardinality of finite sets, together with a brief introduction to **Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory** and the definition of the **natural numbers** within it. It then defines relations as sets of ordered pairs and studies their representations, operations, properties and closures, as well as three important kinds of relations: equivalence relations, compatibility relations and partial orders. Next, functions are treated as special relations: injections, surjections and bijections, composition and inverses, and the **axiom of choice**, followed by open and closed sets and **fuzzy subsets**. Finally, the integers, the rational numbers and the real numbers are constructed in turn inside set theory; bijections are used to compare the sizes of sets, cardinal numbers and their arithmetic are introduced, and the course closes with **countable sets** and the **continuum hypothesis**.

The thread running through the course can be summarized as "start from sets and define, step by step, the objects mathematics uses every day": **ordered pairs** and **Cartesian products** give relations, special relations give functions, the natural numbers appear as particular sets in the axiomatic system, the integers, rationals and reals are constructed from the natural numbers one stage at a time, and **bijections** extend the counting of finite sets to the **cardinal number** of an arbitrary set.

Set theory supplies conceptual tools for the other courses. **Equivalence relations** and **quotient sets** underlie **cosets**, **quotient groups** and quotient rings in [Algebraic Structures](/1.3); **partial orders** lead to **lattices** and **Boolean algebras**; and relation matrices and relation graphs lead directly to the algebraic representations of graphs in [Graph Theory](/1.4), such as the **adjacency matrix**. In analysis, topology and probability theory, concepts such as open sets, functions, countability and cardinality all take the material of this course as their starting point.

To me, once mathematical logic is in place, set theory reads very much like a gradual definition of "number": from the empty set we obtain the natural numbers, then the integers, the rationals and the reals. Along the way it also defines relations and functions, concepts used in every other mathematics course. While taking the course, I kept following an undercurrent: the relationship between mathematical logic and set theory. On the one hand, the ZF axioms are formulas of **first-order logic**, so set theory can be viewed as a formal theory built on top of logic. On the other hand, the semantics of predicate logic (the **domain of discourse**, interpretations and models) has to be described in terms of sets. Each depends on the other, and sorting out which parts are assumed and which are constructed is, I believe, the key to understanding the foundations of mathematics.
