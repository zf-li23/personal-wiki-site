Graph theory takes graphs as its objects of study: things are represented by vertices, and the relationships between them by directed or undirected edges. This course begins with the basic notions of graphs, vertex degrees, subgraphs, graph operations and isomorphism, together with ways of representing a graph such as the adjacency matrix, the incidence matrix and adjacency lists. It then turns to walks, paths and cycles, covering tests for connectivity, Eulerian circuits and Hamiltonian cycles, the traveling salesman problem, shortest paths, critical paths and the Chinese postman problem. Next it studies trees: incidence, cycle and cut-set matrices are used to characterize and count spanning trees, and Huffman trees, minimum spanning trees and maximum branchings are introduced. After that come planar graphs, Euler's formula, Kuratowski's theorem, dual graphs and graph coloring, followed by matchings in bipartite graphs and network flows, including Hall's theorem, the Hungarian algorithm, the max-flow min-cut theorem and minimum-cost flows. Finally, vertex connectivity, edge connectivity, Whitney's inequality and Menger's theorem give a finer measure of how well connected a graph is.

A distinctive feature of the course is that structural theorems and algorithms carry equal weight. Almost every chapter contains both a theorem characterizing when a certain structure exists (the criterion for Eulerian circuits, Kuratowski's theorem, Hall's theorem, Menger's theorem) and an algorithm that constructs or optimizes it (Dijkstra's algorithm, the algorithms of Kruskal and Prim, the Hungarian algorithm, the Ford–Fulkerson algorithm). The chapters also echo one another: network flows give a unified treatment of the matching problem and of Menger's theorem, and matrix methods reduce the counting of spanning trees to the evaluation of a determinant.

Graph theory is the direct foundation of data structures, algorithm design and operations research, and it is the mathematical language of network science. Formally, a graph is just a vertex set together with a binary relation on it, so the subject rests on the theory of relations from [Set Theory](/1.2). Matrix representations and spectral methods connect it with linear algebra, and automorphism groups of graphs connect it with [Algebraic Structures](/1.3).

Among the courses in discrete mathematics, I find graph theory the most self-contained in its methods: it requires almost no knowledge of groups, rings or other algebraic structures and can be taken on its own. That does not make it unrelated to the other branches, since a graph is itself a relation on a set, and matrix and algebraic methods matter a great deal in graph theory. What I value more is that graph theory lays the groundwork for understanding network science, and networks are everywhere in biology: from gene regulatory networks and cell–cell interaction networks at the microscopic scale to food webs at the macroscopic scale. One important reason why the complexity of biological systems remains so hard to describe is that networks are far more complicated than arrays and trees. The elements of an array lie on a line, and a tree is a hierarchy without cycles; both have natural traversal orders and recursive decompositions, which makes it easy to develop standard statistical and analytical methods for them. A general network, by contrast, contains many cycles and cross-links and has no natural order, many of its basic problems are computationally hard, and feedback loops in a network give rise to nonlinear dynamics. For these reasons I believe graph theory should be taken as a foundational course for computational biology.
