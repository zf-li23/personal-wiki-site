Mathematical logic studies reasoning by mathematical methods: it writes sentences as symbolic formulas and then answers precisely which formulas are true under all circumstances and which conclusions follow from given premises. This course introduces the foundations of propositional logic and first-order predicate logic. The propositional part begins with **propositions**, **logical connectives**, **well-formed formulas**, and **truth tables**; it then treats **logical equivalence**, normal forms, inference, and **resolution**, and finally gives a first introduction to the axiomatization of propositional logic through Russell's axiom system and **Wang's algorithm**. The predicate part introduces **individual terms**, **predicates**, and **quantifiers**, discusses the formalization of natural-language sentences, the validity of formulas, and the **decision problem**, and then extends equivalence, normal forms, and inference to formulas with quantifiers, including **prenex normal form**, **Skolem normal form**, and resolution in predicate logic.

Two views of truth run through the whole course. One is semantic: using truth tables or interpretations, we determine the truth value of a formula under each assignment, and on this basis define **tautologies**, equivalence, and **tautological implication**. The other is syntactic: without mentioning truth at all, we derive formulas mechanically from axioms by rules of transformation, and on this basis define theorems and formal proofs. The **soundness** and **completeness** of an **axiomatic system** for propositional logic show that the two views agree exactly: everything provable is a tautology, and every tautology is provable. The course does not yet cover the axiomatization of predicate logic, first-order theories and their models, or the various logical systems of proof theory.

The language of mathematical logic underlies the later courses: the definitions and axioms of [Set Theory](/1.2) are written in predicate logic, and **Boolean algebra** in [Algebraic Structures](/1.3) is an abstraction of the laws of the propositional connectives. In computer science, normal forms and **functionally complete sets** of connectives correspond to digital circuit design, **resolution** is the basis of automated theorem proving and logic programming, and the satisfiability problem for propositional formulas is one of the central problems of computational complexity theory.

I studied mathematical logic because I wanted to understand what kind of logical language is needed to set up a standard for science: what truth is, and how one decides that a proposition is true. The course gives a clear answer. Within a formal system, "true" can be defined by truth tables or interpretations, "provable" can be defined by axioms and rules of inference, and the completeness theorem connects the two. Whether a proposition is "scientific", however, and whether it can be falsified by experience, are questions for the philosophy of science, and logic alone cannot answer them. What logic does is state a proposition precisely enough that one can tell under what circumstances it would be refuted; in my view, this is exactly the precondition for any discussion of falsifiability.
