Algebraic structures are "sets equipped with operations": we ignore what the elements are and ask only which laws the operations obey, and which properties all systems obeying the same laws share. Building on **sets**, maps and **equivalence relations**, this course first defines algebraic operations and **algebraic systems** and introduces identity elements, inverses, **homomorphisms** and isomorphisms. It then turns to group theory, proceeding from semigroups and monoids to **groups** and covering subgroups, cyclic groups, permutation groups and Cayley's theorem, cosets and Lagrange's theorem, **normal subgroups** and quotient groups, the fundamental homomorphism theorem and direct products of groups. After that come **rings** and **fields**, which have two operations; **lattices** and **Boolean algebras**, which start from an order relation; and **Lie algebras**, vector spaces equipped with a Lie bracket. Finally, **representation theory** realizes groups and Lie algebras as linear transformations of vector spaces.

The chapters follow a single thread of progressively richer structure: one operation that can be "undone" gives a group; two operations linked by the distributive law give rings and fields; the correspondence between an order structure and algebraic operations gives lattices; a bilinear bracket on a vector space gives a Lie algebra; and representation theory turns all of these abstract structures into problems of linear algebra. In every one of these structures, substructures, homomorphisms, kernels and quotient structures recur in the same pattern, and the fundamental homomorphism theorem holds again and again. This is precisely where the power of abstraction in algebra lies.

Algebraic structures are connected with many fields. **Finite fields** and groups underlie error-correcting codes and cryptography; Boolean algebra is both the abstraction of the propositional operations of [mathematical logic](/1.1) and the mathematical model of logic circuits; and groups and their representations are the language of symmetry, with wide applications in physics and chemistry.

> Author's note: The course and its textbook cover only group theory. The chapters on rings and fields, lattices and Boolean algebras, Lie algebras, and representation theory are supplementary material beyond the course, written with suggestions from AI. I added them because abstract algebra is gradually making its way into research in computational biology.

Instructors of Discrete Mathematics (2) teach its topics in different orders: some start with graph theory, others with algebraic structures. I think it is more logical to take algebraic structures right after set theory. The subject rests directly on sets, maps and equivalence relations, and it answers the question of which laws a system of operations must satisfy to be truly workable (closure, associativity, an identity, invertibility); from there it develops, step by step, into the more elaborate structures of abstract algebra. I also suggest thinking about its connections with computer systems while studying it: fixed-width integer arithmetic is really the ring of residue classes modulo $2^n$, string concatenation forms a **monoid**, error-correcting codes and encryption algorithms are built on finite fields, and switching circuits obey the laws of Boolean algebra.
