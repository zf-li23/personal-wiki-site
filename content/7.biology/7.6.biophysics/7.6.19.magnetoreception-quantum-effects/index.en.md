All of chemistry is quantum mechanics, but in most of biology the quantum world is hidden behind thermal averages: bonds have their energies, reactions their rates, and the rest is statistical mechanics. This chapter is about the few places where quantum effects survive in a less trivial form in the warm, wet cell, and about the senses for magnetic fields, where the question of whether they do is still being decided. The geomagnetic field is weak: its energy of interaction with an electron spin is ten million times smaller than the thermal energy (19.1). Organisms nevertheless use it. Magnetotactic bacteria do so with permanent magnets, chains of magnetite crystals whose moment is large enough to align a whole cell (19.2); migratory birds, turtles and fish sense the direction and strength of the field with mechanisms that are only partly known (19.3). The leading explanation of the compass of birds is a quantum effect, the spin dynamics of a photochemically generated radical pair in cryptochrome (19.4). Two other effects are better established: hydrogen tunneling in enzyme reactions (19.5) and the electron tunneling of [7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.4); a third, long-lived coherence in photosynthetic energy transfer, has largely been reinterpreted (19.6). The chapter ends with the magnetic fields that living tissue produces, and how they are measured (19.7). The magnetism of matter is in [5.3.8](/5.3.8), electron spin and the Zeeman effect in [5.5.4](/5.5.4#sec-4.2), magnetic resonance in [7.6.13](/7.6.13), radicals and their EPR spectra in [6.5.7](/6.5.7#sec-7.4); the microbiology of magnetotactic bacteria belongs to [7.11](/7.11) and animal navigation to [7.18](/7.18).

## 19.1. Magnetic Fields and Living Matter

### 19.1.1. Magnitudes

Biologically relevant magnetic flux densities span seventeen orders of magnitude. The geomagnetic field is $25$–$65\,\mu\mathrm{T}$, depending on location (strongest near the magnetic poles), and varies by tens of nanotesla over a day; power lines and household appliances produce $0.1$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{T}$ nearby; clinical MRI scanners $1.5$–$7\,\mathrm{T}$ (research scanners up to $11.7\,\mathrm{T}$); the strongest continuous laboratory fields $45\,\mathrm{T}$. The fields produced by living tissue are tiny: about $50\,\mathrm{pT}$ at the chest from the heart and $10$–$1000\,\mathrm{fT}$ at the scalp from the brain (19.7). Some magnetic senses appear to be disturbed by radio-frequency fields of tens of nanotesla, a thousand times weaker than the static geomagnetic field they detect.

### 19.1.2. Diamagnetism, Paramagnetism and Ferromagnetism

Biological materials respond to magnetic fields like any matter ([5.3.8](/5.3.8)), through their **magnetic susceptibility** $\chi$, the magnetization per unit applied field.

| Response | Origin | Biological examples | Volume susceptibility (SI) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diamagnetic | induced orbital currents, present in all matter | water ($-9.0\times10^{-6}$), lipids, most proteins, oxyhemoglobin and carboxyhemoglobin, tissues | $-10^{-5}$ |
| Paramagnetic | unpaired electron spins aligned by the field against thermal motion | $\ce{O2}$, NO, deoxyhemoglobin (high-spin $\ce{Fe^2+}$, $S = 2$), methemoglobin, hemozoin, ferritin cores, $\ce{Mn^2+}$, $\ce{Gd^3+}$ contrast agents, free radicals | $+10^{-6}$ to $+10^{-3}$ |
| Ferrimagnetic, ferromagnetic | spontaneous ordering of spins in domains | magnetosomes of magnetite ($\ce{Fe3O4}$) or greigite ($\ce{Fe3S4}$), biogenic magnetite in some animal tissues | large, with remanence |

Physiology changes magnetism. Oxyhemoglobin is diamagnetic (low-spin $\ce{Fe^2+}$ bonded to $\ce{O2}$), deoxyhemoglobin paramagnetic, as Pauling and Coryell found in 1936; deoxygenated blood therefore distorts the local field and shortens the transverse relaxation time of the water around it, the contrast mechanism of functional **magnetic resonance imaging** (BOLD, [7.6.13](/7.6.13#sec-13.8)). Malaria parasites digest hemoglobin and store its heme as paramagnetic hemozoin crystals, so infected red cells can be pulled out of blood by a magnetic column.

**Formula 19.1.1 (Magnetic Energy of a Weakly Magnetic Body)** A body of volume $V$ and susceptibility $\chi$ ($\lvert\chi\rvert \ll 1$) in a field $B$ has the energy $U = -\chi VB^2/(2\mu_0)$. A body with anisotropic susceptibility, $\chi_\parallel$ along an axis and $\chi_\perp$ across it, has the orientation-dependent energy

$$
U(\theta) = -\frac{\Delta\chi\,V B^2}{2\mu_0}\cos^2\theta, \qquad \Delta\chi = \chi_\parallel - \chi_\perp. \tag{19.1}
$$

The force on a diamagnetic body in a field gradient is $F = \chi V\nabla(B^2)/(2\mu_0)$, directed towards weaker field. It balances gravity for water when $B\,\mathrm{d}B/\mathrm{d}z = \mu_0\rho g/\lvert\chi\rvert = 1.4\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{T^2\,m^{-1}}$, reached in the bore of a $16\,\mathrm{T}$ resistive magnet: in 1997 Andre Geim and Michael Berry levitated a living frog this way, unharmed. The orientation energy of Eq. (19.1) is negligible for a single molecule ($\Delta\chi$ of a protein corresponds to $\lvert U\rvert \ll k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ even at $20\,\mathrm{T}$), but it adds up in ordered assemblies. With $\Delta\chi \sim 10^{-7}$, the energy exceeds $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ at $10\,\mathrm{T}$ for $V > 2\mu_0k_{\mathrm{B}}T/(\Delta\chi B^2) \approx 10^{-21}\,\mathrm{m^3}$, a cube of $100\,\mathrm{nm}$: membranes, red cells, microtubule bundles and mitotic spindles orient in fields of several tesla, and cleavage planes of frog eggs and human spindles are reoriented at $17$–$27\,\mathrm{T}$. Fields of a few tesla, as in MRI, have no known lasting biological effect.

### 19.1.3. The Energy-Scale Argument

A sense for the geomagnetic field must convert a minute energy into a biological signal.

**Formula 19.1.2 (Zeeman Energy in the Geomagnetic Field)** The Zeeman splitting of a free electron spin in a field $B$ is $g\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B$, with $g = 2.0023$ and the **Bohr magneton** $\mu_{\mathrm{B}} = 5.788\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV\,T^{-1}}$. At $50\,\mu\mathrm{T}$,

$$
g\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B = 5.8\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{eV} = 2.2\times10^{-7}\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T\quad(310\,\mathrm{K}), \qquad \nu = \frac{g\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B}{h} = 1.40\,\mathrm{MHz}. \tag{19.2}
$$
The thermal polarization of the spins is of order $10^{-7}$, and the field changes the equilibrium constant of any reaction by a similar fraction.

The argument rules out every mechanism based on thermodynamic equilibrium: a field that shifts an equilibrium by one part in $10^7$ cannot be sensed by any cell. Two loopholes remain. A permanent magnet made of many aligned spins has a moment $m$ so large that $mB \gg k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ (19.2). Or the field acts not on an equilibrium but on the kinetics of a system that is far from equilibrium and does not have time to thermalize its spins: a radical pair created by light, whose spin evolution in the microseconds before it reacts is coherent and depends on the field direction (19.4).

## 19.2. Magnetotactic Bacteria

### 19.2.1. Magnetosomes

Salvatore Bellini in Pavia (1963) and, independently and with wider impact, Richard Blakemore in Woods Hole (1975) found bacteria in marine and freshwater sediments that swim along magnetic field lines. Blakemore showed by electron microscopy that each cell contains a chain of electron-dense crystals of magnetite.

**Definition 19.2.1 (Magnetosome and Magnetotaxis)** A **magnetosome** is a membrane-bounded, biomineralized crystal of magnetite ($\ce{Fe3O4}$) or greigite ($\ce{Fe3S4}$), typically $35$–$120\,\mathrm{nm}$ in size, formed in a vesicle of the cytoplasmic membrane of magnetotactic bacteria and arranged with others in a chain along an actin-like filament (MamK). **Magnetotaxis** is the passive alignment of a motile cell by the torque that a magnetic field exerts on its magnetic moment, combined with active swimming along the aligned axis.

The size of magnetosomes is not accidental. A **magnetic domain** ([5.3.8](/5.3.8#sec-8.5.1)) costs wall energy, and small crystals are single domains, uniformly magnetized; above roughly $80$–$100\,\mathrm{nm}$ for equidimensional magnetite, splitting into domains lowers the energy and reduces the net moment. Very small crystals are single domains too, but their moment flips between the easy directions by thermal activation.

**Definition 19.2.2 (Superparamagnetism)** A single-domain particle of volume $V$ and anisotropy energy density $K$ reverses its magnetization by thermal activation with the Néel relaxation time $\tau = \tau_0\exp(KV/k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$, $\tau_0 \approx 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}$. When $\tau$ is shorter than the time of observation the particle has no permanent moment and behaves like a strong paramagnet: this is **superparamagnetism**.

For magnetite ($K \approx 10^4\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$) the moment is stable for a second when $KV \approx 21\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$, for a cube of about $20\,\mathrm{nm}$, and for the age of the Earth at $30\,\mathrm{nm}$: the magnetosomes lie exactly in the window between superparamagnetic and multidomain crystals, where the moment per unit of iron is largest and permanent. Ferritin cores ($8\,\mathrm{nm}$, antiferromagnetic ferrihydrite) are far below the window.

**Example 19.2.1 (Moment of a Magnetosome Chain)** Magnetite has a saturation magnetization $M_{\mathrm{s}} = 4.8\times10^5\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$. A $50\,\mathrm{nm}$ cube has $m = M_{\mathrm{s}}V = 4.8\times10^5\times1.25\times10^{-22} = 6.0\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$, and a chain of 20 such crystals, magnetized head to tail by their mutual fields, $1.2\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$. In $50\,\mu\mathrm{T}$ its energy is $mB = 6\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm{J} = 15\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ at $293\,\mathrm{K}$, a hundred million times the Zeeman energy of one electron spin; measured cell moments are $10^{-16}$–$10^{-15}\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$.

### 19.2.2. Alignment and Swimming

A magnetic dipole in thermal equilibrium in a field is aligned on average by an amount that depends only on the ratio of magnetic to thermal energy.

**Theorem 19.2.1 (Langevin Alignment)** A rigid dipole $m$ in a field $B$ at temperature $T$, free to rotate, has the mean alignment

$$
\langle\cos\theta\rangle = \coth\xi - \frac{1}{\xi} \equiv \mathcal{L}(\xi), \qquad \xi = \frac{mB}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}, \tag{19.3}
$$
where $\mathcal{L}$ is the *Langevin function*; $\mathcal{L}(\xi) \approx \xi/3$ for $\xi \ll 1$ and $1 - 1/\xi$ for $\xi \gg 1$.

**Proof** By the **Boltzmann distribution** ([7.6.2](/7.6.2#thm-2.2.1)) the probability of an orientation in the solid angle $\mathrm{d}\Omega = 2\pi\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta$ is proportional to $\mathrm{e}^{\xi\cos\theta}$. With $x = \cos\theta$ the partition function is $Z = \int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{\xi x}\,\mathrm{d}x = 2\sinh\xi/\xi$, and $\langle x\rangle = \partial\ln Z/\partial\xi = \coth\xi - 1/\xi$. $\square$

With $\xi = 15$ a cell is aligned to $\langle\cos\theta\rangle = 0.93$; its swimming velocity along the field is $93\,\%$ of its speed, so magnetotaxis turns a three-dimensional random search for the oxic–anoxic transition zone in the sediment into a one-dimensional one. Cells swim along the field and reverse direction with their flagella when the oxygen concentration tells them they have gone too far (magneto-aerotaxis).

The rotation of a swimming cell after a change of field direction measures its moment. In the overdamped world of bacteria ([7.6.22](/7.6.22)), the magnetic torque $mB\sin\varphi$ is balanced by the rotational drag $f_{\mathrm{r}}\dot\varphi$, so the cell turns towards the field with the time constant $\tau_{\mathrm{B}} = f_{\mathrm{r}}/(mB)$. When the field is reversed, a cell swimming at speed $v$ makes a U-turn of diameter $L = \pi v f_{\mathrm{r}}/(mB)$ in a time $(f_{\mathrm{r}}/mB)\ln(2mB/k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$. For a cell of radius $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in water, $f_{\mathrm{r}} = 8\pi\eta a^3 = 2.5\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s}$; with $m = 1.2\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$ in $50\,\mu\mathrm{T}$, $\tau_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.4\,\mathrm{s}$ and, at $40\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $L = 53\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, as observed.

B: simulated trajectories of cells swimming at 40 μm s⁻¹ along the field, which is reversed at t = 1 s; the cells make U-turns whose diameter L = πv f_r/(mB) is inversely proportional to the moment. C: alignment time, U-turn time and U-turn diameter against the magnetic moment in 50 μT (cell radius 1 μm); the dotted line marks the moment of Example 19.2.1](./plots/magnetotaxis.py)

The direction of swimming is set by the polarity of the chain relative to the flagella. In the Northern Hemisphere most magnetotactic bacteria are north-seeking: they swim parallel to $\boldsymbol{B}$, which there points north and downward, so they swim down into the sediment. In the Southern Hemisphere south-seeking cells dominate, and at the magnetic equator both occur. In a drop on a microscope slide, north-seeking cells accumulate on the side facing geographic north; near a bar magnet they swim along the field lines towards its south pole, where the field lines end. (The statement that they swim towards the north pole of a magnet confuses geographic and magnetic poles: the geographic North Pole is a magnetic south pole.) The inclination of the field, not its horizontal component, is what makes polarity useful: it points the swimming downward.

## 19.3. Magnetoreception in Animals

### 19.3.1. Behavioural Evidence

**Definition 19.3.1 (Magnetoreception)** **Magnetoreception** is the detection of the direction, inclination or intensity of a magnetic field by an organism, used for orientation (a compass sense) or for determining position (a map sense).

Many animals have it: migratory birds and homing pigeons, sea turtles, salmon and eels, spiny lobsters, newts, mole rats, honeybees, ants. Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko discovered in 1972 that European robins, tested in orientation cages in the migratory season, keep their seasonal direction in an artificial field and turn when the field is turned, and found that the compass has unexpected properties:

- Inclination, not polarity: Reversing the vertical component of the field reverses the birds' heading, like reversing the horizontal component; reversing both leaves it unchanged. The bird reads the axis of the field lines and their angle to gravity, poleward being the direction along the axis in which the field dips towards the ground.
- A narrow window of intensity: Birds are disoriented if the field is made $25$–$30\,\%$ stronger or weaker than the local one, but adapt to the new intensity within about an hour.
- Light dependence: The compass works in blue and green light and fails in yellow and red light or in darkness.
- Sensitivity to radio frequencies: Weak oscillating fields in the megahertz range, of nanotesla to microtesla amplitude, disorient the birds (Ritz et al. 2004), and so does the broadband anthropogenic noise of a city, which disappears when the test huts are screened (Engels et al. 2014).

**Definition 19.3.2 (Inclination Compass)** An **inclination compass** determines the poleward direction from the axis of the magnetic field lines and their inclination relative to gravity, independently of the polarity of the field.

![The inclination compass. A: field lines of the geomagnetic dipole and local field vectors at the surface; the inclination I below the horizontal satisfies tan I = 2 tan(latitude); the field points north and downward in the Northern Hemisphere, upward in the Southern, horizontally at the magnetic equator. B: what a bird at 50° N reads: the field axis at I = 67° below the horizontal and the direction of gravity g; poleward is the side along the axis where the field dips down. Reversing only the vertical component (dashed) turns this side into the equatorward one, so the bird reverses its heading, the classic test of an inclination compass](./plots/inclination-compass.py)

The properties are clues. A compass blind to polarity rules out a simple magnet needle (which would read polarity); a dependence on light and on megahertz fields, near the electron Larmor frequency of Eq. (19.2), points to electron spins (19.4). Map senses are different: sea turtles and salmon return to their natal coasts by the intensity and inclination of the field, which vary systematically over the Earth, and these senses work in darkness; they may use magnetite.

### 19.3.2. Mechanisms Proposed

Three physical mechanisms can in principle transduce the geomagnetic field, and a fourth has been proposed and disputed:

1. Magnetite receptors: Single-domain magnetite crystals anchored to mechanosensitive ion channels would exert torques of order $mB$ on them; a chain like that of a bacterium ($15\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$) could gate a channel. Biogenic magnetite has been found in the tissues of several animals, and a magnetite-based map sense is plausible, but no magnetite-based receptor cell has been identified convincingly in any vertebrate. The best-known candidate, iron-rich structures in the upper beak of pigeons interpreted as magnetite-containing dendrites (Fleissner et al. 2003), turned out to be macrophages full of ferritin-like iron (Treiber et al. 2012); claims of $0.2\,\mathrm{pN}$ forces on beak neurons are therefore not supported.
2. Electromagnetic induction: A conductor moving at velocity $\boldsymbol{v}$ through a field experiences the **motional electromotive force** of the **Lorentz force**, an electric field $\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}$ ([5.3.9](/5.3.9#sec-9.2)). For a shark swimming at $1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ in $50\,\mu\mathrm{T}$ it is $50\,\mu\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$; the ampullae of Lorenzini of sharks and rays detect electric fields down to about $0.5\,\mu\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$, a hundred times smaller. Induction is thus a feasible magnetic sense for electroreceptive marine fishes, though not proven to be used. In air (a poor conductor) and for small animals it is not.
3. Radical pairs: Photochemically generated radical pairs whose reaction yields depend on the direction of the field (19.4): the only mechanism that explains the inclination compass, its light dependence and its sensitivity to radio-frequency fields together.
4. MagR: A complex of an iron–sulfur protein (IscA1, renamed MagR) with cryptochrome was reported to align in magnetic fields like a "biocompass" (Qin et al. 2016). Its magnetic moment, even if all its iron were ferromagnetically coupled, is far too small: a rod of a few dozen iron atoms has $mB \sim 10^{-8}\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ in the geomagnetic field (Meister 2016), and the proposal is not accepted.

The same energy argument limits magnetogenetics, the attempt to control engineered neurons with magnetic fields through ferritin-tagged channels: the moment of a ferritin core is too small to produce forces or heating of the reported size, and the published effects are disputed.

## 19.4. The Radical Pair Mechanism

### 19.4.1. Spin-Correlated Radical Pairs

When an excited molecule transfers an electron to a neighbour ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.4)), it creates a pair of **radicals** ([6.3.15](/6.3.15)), each with an unpaired **electron spin**. The two spins inherit the spin state of their precursor: a pair formed from an excited singlet is born in the **singlet state** ($S = 0$), a pair formed from a triplet in a **triplet state** ($S = 1$). Each electron spin precesses in the local magnetic field it feels: the external field plus the fields of the magnetic nuclei of its own radical (the **hyperfine coupling**, [6.5.7](/6.5.7#def-7.4.2)). Because the two radicals have different nuclei, the two spins precess at different rates, and the pair oscillates between singlet and triplet character. Chemistry is spin-selective: a singlet pair can recombine to the ground state, a triplet pair cannot (or gives a different product).

**Definition 19.4.1 (Radical Pair Mechanism)** In the **radical pair mechanism**, a magnetic field changes the yields of the reactions of a spin-correlated radical pair by changing the coherent interconversion of its singlet and triplet states, driven by the Zeeman and hyperfine interactions, before the pair reacts in a spin-selective way.

The mechanism was established in spin chemistry in the 1970s, as the source of magnetic field effects on reaction yields and of the chemically induced nuclear polarization of NMR, and Klaus Schulten proposed in 1978 that it underlies the bird compass; Thorsten Ritz proposed cryptochrome as the receptor in 2000. The physics of the compass follows from comparing time scales. Hyperfine couplings of organic radicals are about $1\,\mathrm{mT}$, precession frequencies of $28\,\mathrm{MHz}$ and periods of tens of nanoseconds. The geomagnetic field ($50\,\mu\mathrm{T}$) gives a Larmor frequency of $1.4\,\mathrm{MHz}$, a period of $0.7\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. For the Earth's field to influence the spin dynamics, the pair must live, and its spins must stay coherent, for a time of order a microsecond.

### 19.4.2. Spin Dynamics and the Singlet Yield

Consider the simplest model that can act as a compass: two electron spins $\hat{\boldsymbol{S}}_1$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{S}}_2$, and one nuclear spin $\hat{\boldsymbol{I}}$ coupled to the first electron by an anisotropic hyperfine tensor $\mathsf{A}$, in a field $\boldsymbol{B}$. Neglecting the exchange and dipolar couplings between the radicals (small at a separation of $1.5$–$2\,\mathrm{nm}$), the spin **Hamiltonian** is

$$
\hat H = g\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\boldsymbol{B}\cdot(\hat{\boldsymbol{S}}_1 + \hat{\boldsymbol{S}}_2) + \hat{\boldsymbol{S}}_1\cdot\mathsf{A}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{I}}. \tag{19.4}
$$
The singlet projection operator is $\hat P_{\mathrm{S}} = \tfrac14 - \hat{\boldsymbol{S}}_1\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{S}}_2$. The pair is born as a singlet with the nuclear spin unpolarized; its spin state is described by a **density operator** ([7.6.13](/7.6.13#def-13.4.1)), $\hat\rho(0) = \hat P_{\mathrm{S}}/M$ with $M$ the number of nuclear spin states. If singlet and triplet pairs both disappear by reaction at rate $k$, the fraction that reacts through the singlet channel is $\Phi_{\mathrm{S}} = k\int_0^\infty\mathrm{e}^{-kt}P_{\mathrm{S}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t$, with $P_{\mathrm{S}}(t) = \operatorname{tr}[\hat P_{\mathrm{S}}\hat\rho(t)]$.

**Theorem 19.4.1 (Singlet Yield)** Let $\lvert m\rangle$ be the eigenstates of $\hat H$ with energies $\hbar\omega_m$, and $\omega_{mn} = \omega_m - \omega_n$. Then

$$
\Phi_{\mathrm{S}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m,n}\lvert\langle m\vert\hat P_{\mathrm{S}}\vert n\rangle\rvert^2\,\frac{k^2}{k^2 + \omega_{mn}^2}. \tag{19.5}
$$
For slow reactions ($k \ll \lvert\omega_{mn}\rvert$ for $m \ne n$) only the terms with $\omega_{mn} = 0$ survive; for fast reactions ($k \gg \lvert\omega_{mn}\rvert$) $\Phi_{\mathrm{S}} \to 1$ and the field has no effect.

**Proof** In the eigenbasis, $\rho_{mn}(t) = \rho_{mn}(0)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{mn}t}$ with $\rho(0) = P_{\mathrm{S}}/M$, so $P_{\mathrm{S}}(t) = \sum_{m,n}(P_{\mathrm{S}})_{nm}\rho_{mn}(t) = M^{-1}\sum_{m,n}\lvert(P_{\mathrm{S}})_{mn}\rvert^2\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{mn}t}$, since $\hat P_{\mathrm{S}}$ is Hermitian. Multiplying by $k\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$ and integrating, $k\int_0^\infty\mathrm{e}^{-(k + \mathrm{i}\omega_{mn})t}\,\mathrm{d}t = k/(k + \mathrm{i}\omega_{mn})$; the terms $(m, n)$ and $(n, m)$ combine to the real part, $k^2/(k^2 + \omega_{mn}^2)$. If $k$ is large, $\Phi_{\mathrm{S}} \to M^{-1}\sum_{m,n}\lvert(P_{\mathrm{S}})_{mn}\rvert^2 = M^{-1}\operatorname{tr}\hat P_{\mathrm{S}}^2 = M^{-1}\operatorname{tr}\hat P_{\mathrm{S}} = 1$. $\square$

The direction of $\boldsymbol{B}$ enters through the eigenstates. With an isotropic hyperfine coupling, $\hat H$ is rotationally symmetric apart from the field, and $\Phi_{\mathrm{S}}$ depends on the field strength (the low-field effect) but not on its direction; with an anisotropic tensor, $\Phi_{\mathrm{S}}$ depends on the angle between $\boldsymbol{B}$ and the hyperfine axes. A radical pair fixed in an oriented molecule is then a chemical compass whose product yield varies with the direction of the field; a sense based on it would read an axis, not a polarity (the Hamiltonian is unchanged when $\boldsymbol{B} \to -\boldsymbol{B}$ together with a rotation of all spins), exactly the inclination compass of 19.3.

![The radical pair mechanism in a minimal model: two electron spins, one coupled to a spin-1/2 nucleus by an axial hyperfine tensor (−0.1, −0.1, 1.75 mT), in 50 μT, born as a singlet. A: singlet probability without recombination, for the field along the hyperfine axis and perpendicular to it: the coherent singlet–triplet oscillation depends on the field direction. B: singlet yield (Theorem 19.4.1) against the angle between field and hyperfine axis for recombination rates of 0.1, 1 and 10 μs⁻¹: the yield varies by up to 15 percentage points. **Example 19.4.1** In the model of the figure the singlet yield at $50\,\mu\mathrm{T}$ varies between $0.25$ and $0.40$ with the field direction when the pair lives $1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ ($k = 10^6\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$), and between $0.37$ and $0.42$ when it lives $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. Real radicals have many magnetic nuclei, which dilute the anisotropy to a few percent; with the dozen or more nuclei of a flavin–tryptophan pair, calculations give directional effects of $0.1$–$5\,\%$. A visual receptor cell with $10^4$–$10^5$ such molecules, read out through a signalling cascade, could detect a few percent, but spin relaxation (fluctuating local fields from molecular motion, [7.6.13](/7.6.13#sec-13.3)) must be slower than about a microsecond, a stringent requirement at body temperature. That a field of $15\,\mathrm{nT}$ at $1.4\,\mathrm{MHz}$ disorients robins is hard to explain even with ideal radical pairs; this remains an open problem.

The mechanism has been demonstrated in vitro. A carotenoid–porphyrin–fullerene triad, in which light creates a radical pair living about a microsecond, showed directional changes of its radical-pair lifetime in fields below $50\,\mu\mathrm{T}$ at $193\,\mathrm{K}$, the first artificial chemical compass sensitive to the geomagnetic field (Maeda et al. 2008).

### 19.4.3. Cryptochrome

**Definition 19.4.2 (Cryptochrome)** **Cryptochromes** are flavoproteins related to DNA photolyases that bind flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and function as blue-light photoreceptors in plants and insects and as components of the circadian clock in animals; on excitation, the FAD is reduced by an electron transferred along a chain of three or four tryptophan residues, creating a radical pair $[\mathrm{FAD}^{\bullet-}\,\mathrm{TrpH}^{\bullet+}]$.

Cryptochromes are the only known vertebrate photoreceptor proteins that form long-lived radical pairs; the retinal of rhodopsin does not. Birds have several, and cryptochrome 4 is the leading candidate: it is expressed in the double cones and long-wavelength cones of the retina, its expression in migratory robins does not follow a circadian rhythm (unlike that of the clock cryptochromes), and recombinant robin cryptochrome 4a forms $[\mathrm{FAD}^{\bullet-}\,\mathrm{TrpH}^{\bullet+}]$ pairs at the end of its tryptophan tetrad whose reactions are measurably affected by magnetic fields of a few millitesla, more strongly than those of the homologous protein of nonmigratory chickens and pigeons (Xu et al. 2021). Effects at geomagnetic strength have not yet been demonstrated on the protein, and nothing is known about how a change of a few percent in the yield of a signalling state would be read out by the visual system, although birds' magnetic compass input is processed in a visual area of the forebrain (cluster N). Behavioural experiments with cryptochrome mutants in *Drosophila* (Gegear et al. 2008) suggested a light-dependent magnetic sense in flies, but a large replication found no evidence of any magnetic response (Bassetto et al. 2023).

## 19.5. Hydrogen Tunneling in Enzymes

### 19.5.1. Semiclassical Isotope Effects

The **tunnel effect** ([5.5.3](/5.5.3#sec-3.3.2)) depends steeply on mass: the decay length of Formula 18.4.1 ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.4.2)) scales as $1/\sqrt m$, so a proton, 1836 times heavier than an electron, tunnels over about $0.05\,\mathrm{nm}$ instead of $1$–$2\,\mathrm{nm}$. That is still the length scale of a hydrogen transfer between donor and acceptor atoms in contact ($0.27$–$0.32\,\mathrm{nm}$ apart, a transfer distance of $0.05$–$0.1\,\mathrm{nm}$). The tool to detect it is the **kinetic isotope effect** (KIE, [6.5.3](/6.5.3#sec-3.11.4)), the ratio of rates for H and D (or T).

**Formula 19.5.1 (Semiclassical Isotope Effect)** If the stretching vibration of the transferred hydrogen (wavenumber $\tilde\nu_{\mathrm{H}}$) is lost in the transition state and nothing else changes, the difference in **zero-point energy** gives

$$
\frac{k_{\mathrm{H}}}{k_{\mathrm{D}}} = \exp\left[\frac{hc(\tilde\nu_{\mathrm{H}} - \tilde\nu_{\mathrm{D}})}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right], \qquad \tilde\nu_{\mathrm{D}} \approx \tilde\nu_{\mathrm{H}}/\sqrt2. \tag{19.6}
$$
For a C–H bond ($2900\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, C–D $2130\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) at $298\,\mathrm{K}$ this is $\mathrm{e}^{1.86} \approx 6.4$; including the bending modes, semiclassical KIEs reach about $7$–$10$ at room temperature, and their Arrhenius prefactors are nearly equal ($A_{\mathrm{H}}/A_{\mathrm{D}} \approx 0.7$–$1.4$).

### 19.5.2. Signatures of Tunneling

Tunneling adds to the rate a contribution that is larger for H than for D, and less dependent on temperature. For a parabolic barrier of imaginary frequency $\omega^\ddagger$, the transmission probability at energy $E$ below the barrier top $V$ is $P(E) = [1 + \mathrm{e}^{2\pi(V - E)/\hbar\omega^\ddagger}]^{-1}$, and averaging over the Boltzmann distribution of energies multiplies the classical rate by a factor $Q > 1$ (Bell 1959; for small corrections Wigner's $Q \approx 1 + (\hbar\omega^\ddagger/k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^2/24$). Because $\omega^\ddagger$ is larger for H, $Q_{\mathrm{H}} > Q_{\mathrm{D}}$. The signatures of tunneling are therefore:

- KIEs larger than the semiclassical limit (above about 10 at room temperature);
- curved Arrhenius plots, flattening at low temperature;
- Arrhenius prefactor ratios $A_{\mathrm{H}}/A_{\mathrm{D}}$ far from one (much below 0.7 in moderate tunneling, much above 1.4 in the deep tunneling regime);
- deviations from the semiclassical relations between H, D and T effects (the Swain–Schaad exponent).

![Kinetic isotope effects of hydrogen transfer. A: Arrhenius plots of k_H and k_D in a transition-state model (barrier 50 kJ mol⁻¹ for H, 4.6 kJ mol⁻¹ higher for D from the zero-point energy) without (dashed) and with tunneling through a parabolic barrier of imaginary frequency 800 cm⁻¹ for H and 800/√2 cm⁻¹ for D. B: the isotope effect against temperature; tunneling raises it above the semiclassical range (shaded) and makes it more temperature dependent. ### 19.5.3. Gated Tunneling in Enzymes

Several enzymes show all these signatures. Soybean lipoxygenase-1, which abstracts a hydrogen atom from a C–H bond of linoleic acid, has a KIE of about $80$ at room temperature, almost independent of temperature (the activation energies of H and D differ by only about $4\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$) and $A_{\mathrm{H}}/A_{\mathrm{D}} \approx 18$ (Knapp, Rickert and Klinman 2002); aromatic amine dehydrogenase and methylamine dehydrogenase show KIEs of $17$–$55$. In these reactions the hydrogen does not pass over a barrier at all; it tunnels from the ground vibrational state of the donor well to that of the acceptor well.

The theory is the same as for electron transfer ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#thm-18.4.1)), with the proton in the role of the electron: the protein and solvent fluctuate until the donor and acceptor proton levels are degenerate (the Marcus factor, with a reorganization energy), and the tunneling probability is the overlap of the proton wave functions, $\propto\exp(-m_{\mathrm{X}}\omega_{\mathrm{X}}d^2/2\hbar)$ for harmonic wells a distance $d$ apart, much smaller for D than for H. A distinctive enzymatic feature is the donor–acceptor distance itself, which is modulated by a "gating" vibration of the active site. If the active site is stiff and holds donor and acceptor close, the distance hardly varies, the KIE is large and independent of temperature; if it is soft, thermal excitation of the gating mode brings the atoms closer at higher temperature, which helps the heavier isotope more, and the KIE becomes smaller and temperature dependent. Mutations of active-site residues that loosen the packing indeed make the KIE of lipoxygenase more temperature dependent. Whether protein motions are "tuned" to promote tunneling, or whether enzymes accelerate reactions chiefly by electrostatic preorganization of the active site with tunneling a modest additional factor, is debated; the computed contribution of tunneling to rate enhancement in most enzymes is a factor of $10$–$1000$, small against the $10^{10}$ or more by which enzymes accelerate reactions overall.

## 19.6. Quantum Coherence in Biology: Claims and Evidence

### 19.6.1. Coherence in Photosynthetic Energy Transfer

In strongly coupled pigment arrays the excitation is delocalized over several molecules as an exciton ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.3.2)), and immediately after a femtosecond excitation the system is in a superposition of exciton states. In 2007 Engel, Fleming and co-workers observed in the two-dimensional electronic spectra of the Fenna–Matthews–Olson (FMO) complex of green sulfur bacteria at $77\,\mathrm{K}$ oscillations ("quantum beats") that lasted more than $600\,\mathrm{fs}$, and later at room temperature, and suggested that the excitation searches for the reaction center coherently, like a quantum walk, and that this makes energy transfer efficient. The claim launched the field of "quantum biology". A decade of work changed its interpretation. Most of the long-lived oscillations are vibrational or vibronic coherences (of the pigments' intramolecular vibrations, mixed with electronic states), not electronic ones; electronic coherence between excitons decays within about $50$–$100\,\mathrm{fs}$, faster than the transfer itself (Duan et al. 2017; Cao et al. 2020). The efficiency of light harvesting, above $95\,\%$, is explained by incoherent hopping between delocalized exciton states, downhill in energy ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.3.3)).

The theory of this debate yielded a lasting insight: noise can help transport.

**Definition 19.6.1 (Environment-Assisted Quantum Transport)** **Environment-assisted quantum transport** is the enhancement of the efficiency of excitation transfer through a network of coupled sites by moderate dephasing from the environment: purely coherent dynamics is hindered by energy mismatches between sites and by destructive interference, which dephasing removes, whereas very strong dephasing freezes the motion (the quantum Zeno effect), so the efficiency is maximal at intermediate dephasing.

![Environment-assisted transport in a model of the FMO complex (seven bacteriochlorophyll sites, site energies and couplings of Adolphs and Renger 2006; Lindblad equation with pure dephasing γ, trapping from site 3 at 1 ps⁻¹, loss at 1 ns⁻¹). A: populations of sites 1–3 after excitation of site 1 without dephasing (solid: coherent beating between the strongly coupled sites 1 and 2) and with γ = 100 cm⁻¹ (dashed: damped, steady flow towards site 3). B: transfer efficiency against dephasing rate for excitation on site 1 or 6: very weak dephasing leaves part of the excitation trapped by energy mismatch, intermediate dephasing of the size found in proteins (shaded) gives the highest efficiency, very strong dephasing slows transfer (quantum Zeno effect)](./plots/fmo-enaqt.py)

The model shows why the protein environment, far from destroying efficient transfer, is part of it, and why the dephasing rates of real proteins at room temperature (of order $100\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, tens of femtoseconds) fall near the optimum. It is not evidence for functional long-lived coherence; it shows that efficiency does not require it.

### 19.6.2. What Counts as Quantum Biology

**Definition 19.6.2 (Quantum Biology)** **Quantum biology** studies biological functions that depend on quantum effects beyond those already contained in molecular structure and chemical kinetics: coherent superpositions, entanglement or tunneling whose consequences survive thermal averaging and decoherence long enough to matter for the function.

By this definition, the strongest cases are tunneling (of electrons in respiration and photosynthesis, [7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.4.2); of protons and hydrogen atoms in enzymes, 19.5) and the spin coherence of radical pairs (19.4); the photoisomerization of rhodopsin, which passes a vibrational wave packet coherently through a conical intersection within $200\,\mathrm{fs}$, is a further well-documented example ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.6.1)). The obstacle in every other case is *decoherence*: interaction with a warm environment destroys phase relations between states that differ in the position of charges or atoms within femtoseconds to picoseconds. Spins are an exception because they interact weakly with their surroundings, which is why the radical pair is the most plausible sensory quantum effect. Claims made outside these cases have not held up. The vibration theory of olfaction, according to which receptors sense the vibrational frequencies of odorants by inelastic electron tunneling (Turin 1996), predicts that deuterated odorants smell different; human receptors expressed in cells respond identically to deuterated and normal musks (Block et al. 2015). Proposals that neural computation is quantum founder on decoherence times of the relevant states in the brain, estimated at $10^{-13}$–$10^{-20}\,\mathrm{s}$ (Tegmark 2000), many orders of magnitude shorter than the millisecond of neural dynamics. Claims of "biophotons" carrying information between cells, from Gurwitsch's "mitogenetic rays" of the 1920s onwards, have not been reproduced.

## 19.7. Biomagnetism

Every current produces a magnetic field (**Ampère's circuital law**), and living tissue carries ionic currents. The fields are weak because the currents are small and partly cancel, but they can be measured outside the body without contact and without the distortion that the poorly conducting skull imposes on electric potentials.

**Formula 19.7.1 (Field of a Current Dipole)** A primary current element $\boldsymbol{Q} = I\boldsymbol{l}$ (current $I$ along a short length $\boldsymbol{l}$, in $\mathrm{A\,m}$) produces at distance $r$ the field

$$
\boldsymbol{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{\boldsymbol{Q}\times\boldsymbol{r}}{r^3}, \qquad B \sim \frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi r^2}. \tag{19.7}
$$
In a spherical conductor, radially oriented current dipoles produce no external field, so magnetoencephalography sees mainly currents tangential to the scalp (in the walls of the sulci).

**Example 19.7.1** A brain response measurable by *magnetoencephalography* (MEG) corresponds to a current dipole of about $10\,\mathrm{nA\,m}$, produced by the synchronous postsynaptic currents in the apical dendrites of some $10^5$ pyramidal neurons (each contributes about $0.2\,\mathrm{pA\,m}$). At $r = 4\,\mathrm{cm}$, Eq. (19.7) gives $B \approx 10^{-7}\times10^{-8}/(1.6\times10^{-3})\,\mathrm{T} = 6\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm{T}$, a few hundred femtotesla, as measured, and $10^8$ times weaker than the geomagnetic field. The heart, with a current dipole of about $1\,\mu\mathrm{A\,m}$, gives $50$–$100\,\mathrm{pT}$ at the chest. Close to a squid giant axon carrying an axial current of a few microamperes during an action potential ([7.6.30](/7.6.30)), $B = \mu_0I/(2\pi r) \approx 1\,\mathrm{nT}$ at $1\,\mathrm{mm}$.

Such fields are measured with superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs, noise $1$–$5\,\mathrm{fT\,Hz^{-1/2}}$, cooled with liquid helium) in magnetically shielded rooms, and increasingly with optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs, $10$–$20\,\mathrm{fT\,Hz^{-1/2}}$), vapour cells of alkali atoms whose spins are polarized by a laser and precess in the field, small enough to be placed on the scalp of a moving subject. Nitrogen–vacancy centres in diamond, electron spins read out optically, have detected the action potentials of single giant axons of worms and squid from outside the animal (Barry et al. 2016) and image magnetic fields with micrometre resolution, including the magnetosomes of individual bacteria. Physics also acts in the other direction: transcranial magnetic stimulation induces currents in the cortex with field pulses of about $1\,\mathrm{T}$ rising in $0.1\,\mathrm{ms}$, a direct application of Faraday's law, used to map and treat the brain. The physics of electrical signals and their sources in tissue is treated in [7.6.30](/7.6.30), magnetic resonance imaging in [7.6.13](/7.6.13#sec-13.8).

## Questions

1. Compute the Zeeman splitting of a free electron in the geomagnetic field ($50\,\mu\mathrm{T}$) in eV and in units of $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ at $310\,\mathrm{K}$, and the fractional thermal polarization of the spins. Why does this rule out magnetoreception based on a magnetically shifted chemical equilibrium?

   **Answer** $g\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B = 2.0023\times5.788\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV\,T^{-1}}\times5\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T} = 5.8\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{eV}$; $k_{\mathrm{B}}T = 26.7\,\mathrm{meV}$, so the ratio is $2.2\times10^{-7}$, and the polarization $\tanh(g\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B/2k_{\mathrm{B}}T) \approx 1.1\times10^{-7}$. Any equilibrium constant changes by a relative amount of the same order. To detect a relative change of $10^{-7}$ in the concentration of a signalling molecule against its Poisson fluctuations, a cell would need more than $10^{14}$ molecules counted in the integration time, impossible. A magnetic sense therefore needs either a large moment ($mB \gtrsim k_{\mathrm{B}}T$) or a system far from equilibrium whose kinetics, not its equilibrium, depends on the field.

2. Estimate the U-turn diameter and the alignment time of a magnetotactic bacterium (radius $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, speed $50\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, moment $5\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$) when a field of $100\,\mu\mathrm{T}$ is reversed. How would you use such measurements to determine the moment?

   **Answer** $f_{\mathrm{r}} = 8\pi\eta a^3 = 2.5\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s}$; $mB = 5\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm{J}$; $\tau_{\mathrm{B}} = f_{\mathrm{r}}/mB = 0.5\,\mathrm{s}$; $L = \pi v\tau_{\mathrm{B}} = \pi\times50\times0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m} = 79\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. Measuring $L$ (or the U-turn time) at several field strengths and plotting against $1/B$ gives a straight line whose slope is $\pi vf_{\mathrm{r}}/m$; with $v$ measured and $f_{\mathrm{r}}$ computed from the cell shape, this yields $m$, which can be compared with the moment calculated from the number and size of magnetosomes in electron micrographs.

3. Why do magnetotactic bacteria make magnetite crystals of $35$–$120\,\mathrm{nm}$ rather than many $10\,\mathrm{nm}$ ones or a single $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ crystal? Use the Néel relaxation time with $K = 10^4\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$.

   **Answer** For a $10\,\mathrm{nm}$ cube, $KV/k_{\mathrm{B}}T = 10^4\times10^{-24}/4\times10^{-21} = 2.5$, so $\tau = 10^{-9}\mathrm{e}^{2.5}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 10\,\mathrm{ns}$: the moment fluctuates freely, and the crystals add up only to a paramagnet with alignment $\approx\xi/3$ (superparamagnetism). For $35\,\mathrm{nm}$, $KV/k_{\mathrm{B}}T = 107$: stable forever. Above about $100\,\mathrm{nm}$ the crystal forms domains whose moments partly cancel, so the moment per iron atom falls. Between the limits, each atom contributes its full saturation moment permanently; the chain adds the moments of crystals in a geometry that keeps them aligned by their mutual fields, giving the largest stable moment for a given investment of iron.

4. A robin at a site where the geomagnetic field has an inclination of $66^\circ$ is placed in a field with the same horizontal component but the vertical component reversed. In which direction does it try to migrate in spring, and why does this experiment distinguish an inclination compass from a polarity compass?

   **Answer** With the vertical component reversed, the field dips downward towards the south; an inclination compass takes the direction in which the field lines dip down as poleward, so the bird heads south instead of north. A polarity compass (a magnet needle) would read the unchanged horizontal component and keep heading north. The robins reverse (Wiltschko and Wiltschko 1972), which shows that they use the axis and inclination of the field lines, not their polarity, consistent with a radical-pair mechanism.

5. Show from Theorem 19.4.1 that the singlet yield is $1$ if the radical pair reacts infinitely fast, and explain physically why a compass needs $k$ smaller than the Larmor frequency $\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}B$. Estimate the minimum lifetime for $B = 50\,\mu\mathrm{T}$.

   **Answer** For $k \to \infty$, every factor $k^2/(k^2 + \omega_{mn}^2) \to 1$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm{S}} = M^{-1}\operatorname{tr}\hat P_{\mathrm{S}}^2 = M^{-1}\operatorname{tr}\hat P_{\mathrm{S}} = 1$: the pair reacts before any singlet–triplet conversion. The field acts through the Zeeman term, which changes the evolution only on its own time scale $1/(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}B)$; if the pair reacts earlier, the evolution it has undergone is determined by the hyperfine terms alone and is independent of the field. $\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}B = 1.76\times10^{11}\times5\times10^{-5} = 8.8\times10^6\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, so the lifetime (and the spin coherence time) must be at least $1/\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}B \approx 0.1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, and in practice about $1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ for a substantial effect (panel C of the radical-pair figure).

6. A shark swims at $2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ perpendicular to the geomagnetic field ($45\,\mu\mathrm{T}$). Compute the induced electric field and the potential difference along a $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ electroreceptor canal, and compare with the threshold of the ampullae of Lorenzini ($0.5\,\mu\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$). Why is this mechanism unavailable to a pigeon?

   **Answer** $E = vB = 2\times4.5\times10^{-5} = 9\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}} = 90\,\mu\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$; along $0.1\,\mathrm{m}$, $9\,\mu\mathrm{V}$. This is 180 times the threshold, so detection is physically feasible, and the signal depends on the heading relative to the field. Induction requires a closed conducting path through a medium in which charges can flow: seawater provides the return path for the current through the canal. Air is an insulator, so a flying bird cannot use induction in this way (an internal conducting loop would be far too small: the voltage scales with its size).

7. Using Formula 19.5.1, compute the semiclassical KIE for breaking an O–H bond ($3600\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) at $300\,\mathrm{K}$. An enzyme shows $k_{\mathrm{H}}/k_{\mathrm{D}} = 50$ with $E_{\mathrm{a,D}} - E_{\mathrm{a,H}} = 2\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. What is $A_{\mathrm{H}}/A_{\mathrm{D}}$, and what does it indicate?

   **Answer** $\tilde\nu_{\mathrm{D}} = 3600/\sqrt2 = 2546\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$; $hc\Delta\tilde\nu/2 = 527\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, i.e. $6.3\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$; $k_{\mathrm{H}}/k_{\mathrm{D}} = \exp(6.3/2.494) = 12.5$. For the enzyme, $k_{\mathrm{H}}/k_{\mathrm{D}} = (A_{\mathrm{H}}/A_{\mathrm{D}})\exp(\Delta E_{\mathrm{a}}/RT)$ with $\exp(2/2.494) = 2.23$, so $A_{\mathrm{H}}/A_{\mathrm{D}} = 50/2.23 = 22$, far above the semiclassical range ($0.7$–$1.4$): the temperature dependence is nearly the same for both isotopes and the isotope effect resides in the prefactor, the signature of ground-state tunneling with a rigid (well-gated) donor–acceptor distance.

8. Estimate the magnetic field $3\,\mathrm{cm}$ from a current dipole of $20\,\mathrm{nA\,m}$ in the brain, and the number of synchronously active pyramidal neurons it represents (about $0.2\,\mathrm{pA\,m}$ each). Why does MEG see mainly activity in the walls of sulci?

   **Answer** $B \approx \mu_0Q/(4\pi r^2) = 10^{-7}\times2\times10^{-8}/(9\times10^{-4}) = 2.2\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 2\,\mathrm{pT}$ (an overestimate: the volume currents in the head reduce the field, and the orientation factor is below one; measured fields are hundreds of femtotesla). $2\times10^{-8}/2\times10^{-13} = 10^5$ neurons. In a spherically symmetric conductor the field of a radial current dipole is exactly cancelled by that of the volume currents it drives, so only tangential dipoles are seen; pyramidal neurons are oriented perpendicular to the cortical surface, which is tangential to the scalp in the walls of the sulci and radial at the crowns of the gyri.

9. (Experiment design) Propose an experiment that would discriminate between a radical-pair compass and a magnetite-based compass in a migratory bird, using oscillating fields and pulses.

   **Answer** Two predictions differ. A radical-pair compass is disrupted by weak radio-frequency fields in the megahertz range (near the Larmor frequency of the electron, $1.3$–$1.4\,\mathrm{MHz}$ in the local field, and broadband noise), with an effect that depends on the angle between the oscillating and static fields, and is unaffected by a brief strong magnetic pulse. A magnetite-based receptor is insensitive to megahertz fields of nanotesla amplitude (the moment cannot follow, and the energy is negligible) but is affected by a strong short pulse ($0.1$–$0.5\,\mathrm{T}$, milliseconds), which remagnetizes single-domain particles and changes the orientation for days. Test orientation in cages under (a) RF fields of different frequencies and orientations, (b) after a strong pulse, and (c) in darkness or under long-wavelength light (radical pairs need short-wavelength light). Experiments of this kind indicate that the compass of robins is RF-sensitive and light-dependent, whereas pulse effects mostly affect the map sense of experienced migrants.

10. (Open question) In the FMO model, transfer efficiency increases when moderate dephasing is added. Explain this using the two-well model of [7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.4.2) with an energy mismatch $\Delta$, and argue whether this result supports or undermines the claim that long-lived quantum coherence is needed for efficient photosynthesis.

    **Answer** Between two sites with mismatch $\Delta \gg H$, coherent dynamics transfers at most $4H^2/(4H^2 + \Delta^2)$ of the population and brings it back: the excitation oscillates without moving on. Dephasing at rate $\gamma$ destroys the phase of this oscillation and turns it into an incoherent hop at rate $\approx 2H^2\gamma/(\Delta^2 + \gamma^2)$, maximal at $\gamma \approx \Delta$; the excitation now moves irreversibly down the network to the trap. Too much dephasing ($\gamma \gg \Delta, H$) gives a rate $\propto H^2/\gamma$ that falls (Zeno). Efficient transport thus results from coherent coupling combined with rapid dephasing, not from long-lived coherence: the optimum lies where coherence decays in tens of femtoseconds, as in real proteins. The result undermines the claim that function needs long-lived coherence, while showing that quantum coupling (delocalization over strongly coupled pigments) is still essential.
