The circuits of the previous chapter had two or three components. Real regulatory and metabolic networks have hundreds to thousands, and two questions arise that do not arise for small circuits. The first is statistical: what is the architecture of such networks as a whole, and does it differ from a random wiring? The second is dynamical: can the behavior of a large network be understood without knowing all its rate constants? This chapter, which follows SH chapter 20, answers both for three systems that have become paradigms. The cell cycle (34.2) is the best-understood regulatory network; it is treated at three resolutions, a two-variable oscillator, a differential-equation model of the yeast cycle and a Boolean network whose global attractor is the cell's resting state. The gap-gene network of the *Drosophila* embryo (34.3) shows the same ideas in development. Metabolism (34.4) is too large and too poorly characterized kinetically for differential equations, and is treated instead by flux balance analysis, which uses only stoichiometry, mass balance and an optimization principle.

The chapter uses the vocabulary of graph theory, degrees, paths, **adjacency matrices** and connectivity ([1.4.1](/1.4.1), [1.4.2](/1.4.2)), the stability theory of [2.4.11](/2.4.11), and the tools of [7.6.33](/7.6.33): nullclines, bistability and **hysteresis**, relaxation oscillators and attractors. The biology of the cell-cycle control system is in [7.3.20](/7.3.20) and [7.3.22](/7.3.22), with a three-variable model of the embryonic oscillator in [7.3.20](/7.3.20#sec-20.6); the segmentation genes of *Drosophila* in [7.5.15](/7.5.15); metabolic pathways, their regulation and **metabolic control analysis** in [7.1.17](/7.1.17). The statistics of the transcriptional network of *E. coli* and its network motifs (feed-forward loops, single-input modules, dense overlapping regulons; SH 20.2.1), the modularity and evolution of networks and their inference from omics data belong to [systems biology](/7.9) and are not treated here. The robustness of the cell-cycle network and of other networks to perturbations of their parameters and wiring is the subject of [7.6.40](/7.6.40), and the proteome cost of metabolism, which explains overflow metabolism, of [7.6.39](/7.6.39).

## 34.1. The Architecture of Complex Networks

### 34.1.1. Five Features

Biological networks of genes, proteins, metabolites, cells and species share several features that distinguish them from engineered circuits and from random graphs (SH 20.1):

| Feature | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nonlinear interactions | responses are sigmoidal, saturating or thresholded, not proportional | cooperative binding, **ultrasensitivity**, switches |
| Self-organization | ordered states and patterns emerge from local interactions without a template | oscillations, spatial patterns ([7.6.36](/7.6.36)), collective motion ([7.6.37](/7.6.37)) |
| Heterogeneity | a few nodes have many links, most have few; links cluster into modules | hubs in protein-interaction networks; metabolic currency molecules (ATP, NADH) |
| Nonequilibrium dynamics | the network is maintained by a flux of free energy and is never at equilibrium | metabolic fluxes, phosphorylation cycles ([7.6.38](/7.6.38)) |
| Multiple scales | interactions on molecular, cellular, tissue and population scales are coupled | gene expression controlling cell mechanics controlling tissue shape |

The heterogeneity can be quantified with a few statistics of graphs.

### 34.1.2. Degree Distributions

**Definition 34.1.1 (Degree Distribution)** The **degree distribution** $P(k)$ of a network is the fraction of its vertices that have degree $k$ (the number of edges at the vertex; for a directed network, separate in- and out-degree distributions are defined).

In the random graph of Erdős and Rényi, each of the $\binom{N}{2}$ pairs of $N$ vertices is joined independently with probability $p$. The degree of a vertex is binomial with mean $\langle k\rangle = p(N - 1)$, and for large $N$ at fixed $\langle k\rangle$ it is Poisson: degrees cluster around the mean and vertices with many more links than average are exponentially rare. Measured networks are different. In protein-interaction networks of yeast (Jeong, Mason, Barabási and Oltvai, 2001), in metabolic networks (Jeong, Tombor, Albert, Oltvai and Barabási, 2000), in the World Wide Web and in citation networks, the degree distribution has a long tail, approximately a power law over one or two decades.

**Definition 34.1.2 (Scale-Free Network)** A **scale-free network** is a network whose degree distribution decays as a power law, $P(k) \propto k^{-\gamma}$ for large $k$, typically with $2 < \gamma < 3$; it has no characteristic degree, and a small number of highly connected vertices, hubs, carry a large fraction of the edges.

Barabási and Albert (1999) showed that two ingredients produce such networks: growth, the addition of new vertices over time, and preferential attachment, new vertices linking to existing ones with probability proportional to their degree. With $m$ links per new vertex, the stationary degree distribution is
$$
P(k) = \frac{2m(m + 1)}{k(k + 1)(k + 2)} \sim 2m^2k^{-3}. \tag{34.1}
$$
In biology, gene duplication followed by divergence is a mechanism of the same kind: a duplicated gene inherits the interaction partners of its parent, so a protein with many partners gains new ones in proportion to their number (Vázquez, Flammini, Maritan and Vespignani, 2003).

To derive Eq. (34.1), let vertices be added one per unit time, each with $m$ edges, so that after $t$ steps the total degree is $2mt$. A vertex of degree $k$ gains an edge from each new vertex with probability $mk/(2mt) = k/(2t)$. The expected number $N_k(t)$ of vertices of degree $k$ then obeys $N_k(t + 1) - N_k(t) = \frac{(k-1)N_{k-1} - kN_k}{2t}$ for $k > m$, plus one new vertex of degree $m$. In the stationary regime $N_k = tP(k)$, so $P(k) = \frac12[(k - 1)P(k - 1) - kP(k)]$, that is $P(k)/P(k - 1) = (k - 1)/(k + 2)$, and the product from $m$ to $k$, with $P(m) = 2/(m + 2)$ from the equation for $k = m$, gives Eq. (34.1).

The power-law description of biological networks must be used with care. The interaction maps are incomplete and biased towards well-studied proteins, a power law fitted over one or two decades is hard to distinguish from other heavy-tailed distributions, and rigorous tests find strong evidence for scale-free structure in only a minority of networks (Broido and Clauset, 2019). The robust conclusion is heterogeneity: degrees vary over orders of magnitude, far more than in a random graph.

### 34.1.3. Clustering and Small Worlds

**Definition 34.1.3 (Clustering Coefficient)** The local **clustering coefficient** of a vertex with $k \geq 2$ neighbors is the fraction of the $\binom{k}{2}$ pairs of its neighbors that are themselves adjacent, $C_i = 2T_i/(k_i(k_i - 1))$ with $T_i$ the number of triangles through $i$; the clustering coefficient $C$ of the network is its average over vertices.

In an undirected graph $T_i = \frac12(A^3)_{ii}$, the number of closed walks of length three from $i$ divided by two ([1.4.2](/1.4.2)). In an Erdős–Rényi graph $C = p = \langle k\rangle/(N - 1)$, which vanishes for large sparse networks; in biological, social and technological networks $C$ is orders of magnitude larger, because interacting partners tend to interact with each other (protein complexes, metabolic modules). At the same time, the mean shortest-path length $L$ between vertices is small, close to that of a random graph, $L \approx \ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$: any metabolite can be converted into almost any other in three or four reactions (Jeong and colleagues, 2000).

**Definition 34.1.4 (Small-World Network)** A **small-world network** is a network whose clustering coefficient is much larger than that of a random graph with the same number of vertices and edges, while its mean shortest-path length is comparable to that of the random graph, growing only logarithmically with the number of vertices.

Watts and Strogatz (1998) showed how little randomness is needed. Starting from a ring in which each vertex is joined to its $k$ nearest neighbors (high clustering, path length $L \approx N/2k$, proportional to $N$), they rewired each edge at random with probability $p$. A few shortcuts suffice to shrink $L$ to its random-graph value while leaving most triangles intact, so there is a wide range of $p$ in which the network is a small world (figure, panel B). In biology, short paths mean that a perturbation can spread through the whole network in a few steps, and clustering means that most interactions are local, within modules; together they are the graph-theoretic expression of the modular yet integrated organization of the cell.

### 34.1.4. Robustness to Failure and Attack

The degree distribution determines how a network falls apart when vertices are removed. Removing vertices at random from a scale-free network rarely hits a hub, and the largest connected component shrinks slowly; removing the hubs first (a targeted attack) fragments it quickly. A random graph, without hubs, responds in the same way to both (Albert, Jeong and Barabási, 2000). In yeast, proteins with many interaction partners were more often essential than poorly connected ones (Jeong and colleagues, 2001), a correlation that later analyses attributed partly to the membership of hubs in essential complexes rather than to their connectivity as such (Zotenko, Mestre, O'Leary and Przytycka, 2008). The structural robustness of a graph is not the same as the functional robustness of the dynamics it supports, which depends on the signs and strengths of the interactions; the latter is treated in 34.2.3 and in [7.6.40](/7.6.40).

## 34.2. The Cell-Cycle Network

### 34.2.1. The Embryonic Oscillator in Two Variables

The early cell cycles of the frog embryo run autonomously, every $25$–$30\,\mathrm{min}$, without checkpoints ([7.3.20](/7.3.20#sec-20.6.1)). The core is cyclin B–Cdk1, which is activated by the continuous synthesis of cyclin and by its own positive feedback through Cdc25 and Wee1, and APC/C–Cdc20, which is activated by Cdk1 with a delay and destroys cyclin B. Ferrell, Tsai and Yang (2011) reduced this to two equations for the active fractions $C$ of Cdk1 and $A$ of APC/C (SH Eq. 20-1):
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \alpha_1 - \beta_1C\,\frac{A^n}{K^n + A^n} + \alpha_3(1 - C)\frac{C^n}{K^n + C^n}, \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \alpha_2(1 - A)\frac{C^n}{K^n + C^n} - \beta_2A.
\end{aligned} \tag{34.2}
$$
The first term is the activation of Cdk1 by cyclin synthesis, the second its inactivation through cyclin destruction by APC/C, the third the positive feedback, compressed into a self-activation; APC/C is activated by Cdk1 and inactivated at a constant rate.

The phase plane explains the dynamics. The APC/C nullcline, $A = \alpha_2H(C)/(\alpha_2H(C) + \beta_2)$ with $H$ the Hill function, is monotonic and sigmoidal. Because of the positive feedback, the Cdk1 nullcline is S-shaped (or N-shaped): over a range of APC/C activity there are three steady states of Cdk1, the outer two stable and the middle one unstable, the bistable switch of Cdk1 ([7.3.20](/7.3.20#sec-20.6.3)). The two nullclines cross once, on the middle branch, at an unstable fixed point. All trajectories enter the unit square $[0, 1]^2$ and stay there, so by the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem ([2.4.11](/2.4.11#thm-11.6.1)) a limit cycle exists around the fixed point: a relaxation-type oscillation in which Cdk1 rises, switches on, activates APC/C, and is switched off ([7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.5.4)). Without the positive feedback ($\alpha_3 = 0$) the Cdk1 nullcline is monotonic and, for these parameters, the fixed point is stable: negative feedback alone gives damped oscillations, the result of the experiments with nonphosphorylatable Cdk1 (Pomerening, Kim and Ferrell, 2005). The positive loop adds robustness: the oscillation persists over a wider range of parameters, its amplitude is nearly independent of the period, and the period can be tuned by the rate of cyclin synthesis (Tsai, Byun, Ferrell and colleagues, 2008).

### 34.2.2. Antagonism and Hysteresis in Yeast

Somatic cell cycles are not free-running clocks but sequences of irreversible transitions gated by growth and by checkpoints. In budding and fission yeast, the cell divides only after it has grown to a critical size, and the core of the size control is a mutual antagonism: B-type cyclin–Cdk (CycB) phosphorylates and inactivates Cdh1, the activator of APC/C that targets cyclin B for destruction (and inhibits the stoichiometric inhibitor Sic1), while Cdh1 destroys cyclin B. Tyson and Novák (2001) wrote this antagonism as
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{CycB}]}{\mathrm{d}t} &= k_1 - (k_2' + k_2''[\mathrm{Cdh1}])[\mathrm{CycB}], \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{Cdh1}]}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \frac{(k_3' + k_3''[\mathrm{Cdc20}_{\mathrm{A}}])(1 - [\mathrm{Cdh1}])}{J_3 + 1 - [\mathrm{Cdh1}]} - \frac{k_4\,m\,[\mathrm{CycB}][\mathrm{Cdh1}]}{J_4 + [\mathrm{Cdh1}]},
\end{aligned} \tag{34.3}
$$
where $m$ is the cell mass, which multiplies the activity of the cyclin because a larger cell contains more cyclin–Cdk per gene; the inactivation of Cdh1 by phosphorylation and its activation by a phosphatase follow Michaelis–Menten kinetics with small Michaelis constants $J_3, J_4$, which makes the response switch-like (**zero-order ultrasensitivity**, [7.1.9](/7.1.9)). The full model adds Cdc20, made in response to CycB and activated with a delay through an intermediary enzyme, and growth of the mass with halving at division.

With Cdc20 inactive, the steady states of Eq. (34.3) as a function of $m$ form an S-shaped curve (figure, panel A): a G1 state with Cdh1 active and CycB low, which exists only for small cells; an S/G2/M state with Cdh1 inactive and CycB high, which exists only above a smaller critical mass; and an unstable branch between them. The cell cycle is a traversal of this hysteresis loop. A newborn cell sits in G1; as it grows, $m$ passes the saddle-node point at which the G1 state disappears (for the parameters of the figure, $m \approx 0.53$), and the cell jumps irreversibly to high CycB: this is **Start**, the commitment to the cycle. Since the S/G2/M state persists for smaller masses, division, which halves $m$, cannot return the cell to G1 by itself. Instead, high CycB activates Cdc20, which activates Cdh1 and destroys CycB, pushing the system back over the other side of the loop, Finish, after which the cell divides and the cycle restarts. Two saddle-node bifurcations of one antagonistic pair thus provide both size control and the irreversibility of the transitions, and the checkpoints act by holding the system on one side of a switch (blocking Cdc20 until all chromosomes are attached, for example). The predictions of this view, the hysteresis of the Cdk switch and its irreversibility, have been tested in yeast and in frog extracts ([7.3.20](/7.3.20#sec-20.6.3)), and the Tyson–Novák models were extended to dozens of variables that reproduced the phenotypes of over a hundred mutants of budding yeast (Chen and colleagues, 2004).

### 34.2.3. A Boolean Network and Its Global Attractor

The differential-equation models require dozens of rate constants, most of them unmeasured. Li, Long, Lu, Ouyang and Tang (2004) asked whether the wiring alone determines the essential behavior. They took the eleven key regulators of the budding-yeast cycle, the cyclins Cln3, Cln1,2, Clb5,6 and Clb1,2, the transcription factors SBF, MBF, Mcm1/SFF and Swi5, the APC/C activators Cdh1 and Cdc20 (with the phosphatase Cdc14), and the inhibitor Sic1, and the 29 known activating and inhibiting interactions among them. Each node is a Boolean variable $S_i \in \{0, 1\}$, and all nodes are updated simultaneously by a threshold rule:
$$
S_i(t + 1) = \begin{cases} 1, & \sum_ja_{ij}S_j(t) > 0, \\ 0, & \sum_ja_{ij}S_j(t) < 0, \\ S_i(t), & \sum_ja_{ij}S_j(t) = 0, \end{cases} \tag{34.4}
$$
with $a_{ij} = +1$ for an activation of $i$ by $j$ and $-1$ for an inhibition, and with nodes that are not inhibited by any other node (Cln3, Cln1,2, Swi5, Cdc20/Cdc14, Mcm1/SFF) decaying to $0$ when their input is zero, which represents their degradation. Cell size enters as a signal that switches on Cln3.

The network has $2^{11} = 2048$ states, and its dynamics can be computed for all of them. Every trajectory ends in one of seven fixed points, and one of them attracts $1764$ states, $86\,\%$ of the state space (figure, panel C): its state, with only Cdh1 and Sic1 on, is the G1 state of a resting cell. The other attractors have small basins (151, 109, 9, 7, 7 and 1 states). Starting from the excited G1 state, with Cln3 switched on by the size signal, the network passes in twelve steps through the sequence of biological phases, the activation of SBF and MBF and of Cln1,2 (late G1), the inactivation of Sic1 and Cdh1, Clb5,6 (S phase), Mcm1 and Clb1,2 (G2 and M), Cdc20 and Swi5, the return of Sic1 and Cdh1 and the exit from mitosis, and back to G1 (panel B). More than that, most trajectories from arbitrary initial states join this sequence early and follow it: the biological pathway is a globally attracting trajectory, a funnel in state space, so that the cycle is completed correctly even after large perturbations of the state.

Li and colleagues then asked how special this is. Among random networks with the same number of nodes and of activating and inhibiting links, few had a fixed point with a basin as large as $86\,\%$, and fewer still had a sequence as attracting as the biological one; and almost all single deletions or additions of links in the real network preserved the large basin and the pathway. The cell-cycle network is thus robust by its architecture: its function does not depend on the precise values of its parameters, and in this sense the network has been designed by evolution for stability. The analysis of this kind of robustness, for the cell cycle and for other networks, is continued in [7.6.40](/7.6.40), and the relation between Boolean and continuous descriptions in [7.5.10](/7.5.10#sec-10.12). Boolean models have since been built for many other networks, from the segment-polarity network of *Drosophila* (Albert and Othmer, 2003) to the decision between growth and apoptosis in mammalian cells, and their attractors compared with the cell types and states observed, which gives a precise meaning to the idea that cell types are attractors of the regulatory network (Kauffman, 1969).

## 34.3. A Developmental Network: The Gap Genes

During the first three hours of *Drosophila* development the embryo is a syncytium: thirteen nuclear divisions produce about $6000$ nuclei at the surface of a common cytoplasm, through which transcription factors diffuse freely (SH 20.2.3; [7.5.14](/7.5.14), [7.5.15](/7.5.15)). The anteroposterior axis is defined by maternal gradients: Bicoid protein, translated from mRNA anchored at the anterior pole, forms an exponential gradient with a length constant of about $20\,\%$ of the embryo length; Nanos protein forms a posterior gradient; maternal *hunchback* and *caudal* mRNAs are translationally regulated by Nanos and Bicoid respectively. These inputs activate the **gap genes**, *hunchback* (*hb*), *Krüppel* (*Kr*), *knirps* (*kni*) and *giant* (*gt*), expressed in broad, partly overlapping domains, whose products regulate the pair-rule genes, whose products in turn regulate the segment-polarity genes, a hierarchy that subdivides the embryo into 14 parasegments.

The gap genes form a network: they repress one another, and the strongest repressions are between the genes whose domains do not overlap, *hb* and *kni*, and *gt* and *Kr*. Mutual repression of this kind makes each pair a toggle switch ([7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.3)), and the network as a whole sharpens the smooth maternal gradients into domains with sharp borders and maintains them. Gap-gene circuit models, systems of reaction–diffusion equations with production terms of the form $\sigma(\sum_jW_{ij}x_j + m_iB + h_i)$ (a sigmoid of a weighted sum of the regulators and of Bicoid), fitted to quantitative expression data, reproduce the domains and their shifts in time (Jaeger and colleagues, 2004), and in the phase space of such models the domain boundaries correspond to transitions between attractors as Bicoid concentration varies along the axis (Manu and colleagues, 2009). The output is remarkably precise: from the combined levels of the four gap proteins at a nucleus, its position along the axis can be decoded with an error of about $1\,\%$ of the embryo length, about one nuclear spacing (Dubuis, Tkačik, Wieschaus, Gregor and Bialek, 2013), close to the limit set by the noise in the gap-gene levels. How much **positional information** a gradient can carry, and the physical limits of reading it, are treated in [7.5.11](/7.5.11) and [7.6.35](/7.6.35).

## 34.4. Metabolic Networks and Flux Balance Analysis

### 34.4.1. Why a Different Approach

The metabolic network of *E. coli* contains more than $2000$ reactions among more than $1000$ metabolites in its current genome-scale reconstructions (iML1515: $2712$ reactions, $1877$ metabolites and $1516$ genes; Monk and colleagues, 2017). Writing kinetic equations for it would require thousands of enzyme parameters, most unknown and many dependent on conditions. Yet the stoichiometry of every reaction is known exactly, and in a growing cell most intracellular metabolites turn over in seconds, far faster than the cell grows, so their concentrations are in a quasi-steady state. Flux balance analysis uses only these two facts and adds an assumption about what the network is for.

### 34.4.2. The Stoichiometric Matrix and Mass Balance

**Definition 34.4.1 (Stoichiometric Matrix)** The **stoichiometric matrix** $S$ of a reaction network with $M$ metabolites and $R$ reactions is the $M \times R$ matrix whose entry $S_{ij}$ is the stoichiometric coefficient of metabolite $i$ in reaction $j$: negative for a substrate, positive for a product, zero if the metabolite does not take part. Exchange of metabolites with the environment (uptake, excretion) and the drain of precursors into biomass are written as additional reactions.

For example, the reaction $\ce{B + 2C -> D}$ contributes a column with $-1$ in the row of B, $-2$ in the row of C and $+1$ in the row of D. With the vector $\boldsymbol{v}$ of reaction rates (fluxes, in $\mathrm{mmol}$ per gram of dry weight per hour), the concentrations $\boldsymbol{c}$ of the metabolites change as
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{c}}{\mathrm{d}t} = S\boldsymbol{v}, \tag{34.5}
$$
and in the quasi-steady state of the internal metabolites
$$
S\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{0}. \tag{34.6}
$$
Equation (34.6) is mass balance: for every internal metabolite, production equals consumption. It defines the null space of $S$, of dimension $R - \operatorname{rank}(S)$ ([2.1](/2.1)), which in genome-scale networks has hundreds of dimensions: mass balance alone leaves the fluxes highly underdetermined. Further constraints are bounds on individual fluxes, $v_j^{\min} \le v_j \le v_j^{\max}$: zero lower bounds for irreversible reactions (set by thermodynamics, [6.3](/6.3)), measured maximal uptake rates of nutrients and oxygen, and enzyme capacities where known. The feasible fluxes form a convex polytope, the intersection of the null space with a box. A steady flux distribution is also a flow in a hypergraph, and Eq. (34.6) is the analog of the conservation law at the vertices of a flow network ([1.4.5](/1.4.5)).

### 34.4.3. Optimization

**Definition 34.4.2 (Flux Balance Analysis)** **Flux balance analysis** predicts the steady-state fluxes of a metabolic network as a solution of the linear program
$$
\max_{\boldsymbol{v}}\ \boldsymbol{c}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{v} \quad \text{subject to} \quad S\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{0}, \quad \boldsymbol{v}^{\min} \le \boldsymbol{v} \le \boldsymbol{v}^{\max}, \tag{34.7}
$$
where the objective $\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{v}$ is a flux that the cell is assumed to maximize, usually the flux of a biomass reaction that drains precursors, cofactors and ATP in the proportions measured in the cell's composition.

A linear objective over a polytope is maximized at a vertex (or on a face), and linear programs with thousands of variables are solved in milliseconds by the simplex or interior-point methods (`scipy.optimize.linprog`, [4.4.4](/4.4.4)). The assumption that bacteria maximize their growth yield is justified by evolution in the conditions in which they are grown, and it is a hypothesis that can fail: in other conditions the relevant objective may be ATP yield, robustness, or a trade-off among several. The dual of the linear program assigns a shadow price to each metabolite, the increase of the objective per unit of additional supply, which identifies the limiting resources.

**Example 34.4.1 (A Toy Model of Central Metabolism)** Consider eight internal metabolites (glucose, pyruvate, ribose 5-phosphate, acetyl-CoA, NADH, NADPH, ATP and oxygen) and eleven reactions: glucose uptake (at most $10$), glycolysis ($\ce{Glc -> 2 Pyr}$ with $2$ ATP and $2$ NADH), the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway ($\mathrm{Glc} \to \mathrm{R5P} + 2\,\mathrm{NADPH}$), pyruvate dehydrogenase ($\mathrm{Pyr} \to \mathrm{AcCoA} + \mathrm{NADH}$), a lumped TCA cycle ($\mathrm{AcCoA} \to 4\,\mathrm{NADH} + \mathrm{ATP}$), oxidative phosphorylation ($\mathrm{NADH} + \frac12\ce{O2} \to 2.5\,\mathrm{ATP}$, [7.1.23](/7.1.23)), oxygen uptake, lactate and acetate excretion, a maintenance ATP demand of at least $1$, and a biomass reaction consuming $0.2$ R5P, $1.5$ pyruvate, $1$ acetyl-CoA, $0.4$ NADPH, $3.5$ NADH and $20$ ATP per unit. ### 34.4.4. Tests and Extensions

The genome-scale predictions of flux balance analysis have been tested in three ways. Edwards and Palsson (2000) predicted, for a reconstruction of *E. coli* central metabolism, which single-gene deletions would abolish growth on glucose; the predictions agreed with the observed phenotypes for $86\,\%$ of the mutants examined. Ibarra, Edwards and Palsson (2002) grew *E. coli* on glycerol, a carbon source on which its initial uptake rates lay below the line of optimality of the phenotype phase plane; after about 700 generations of adaptive evolution, the evolved strains had moved onto the line, as predicted for growth maximization, and different replicate populations reached it by different routes. And measurements of intracellular fluxes with ${}^{13}\mathrm{C}$-labeled substrates have shown that the fluxes of central metabolism are close to the predicted optima in many but not all conditions (Schuetz, Kuepfer and Sauer, 2007).

The failures are instructive. A knockout mutant has not been selected for its new genotype, and its fluxes are better predicted by minimization of metabolic adjustment (MOMA), which seeks the feasible flux distribution closest to the wild-type one, than by re-optimization (Segrè, Vitkup and Church, 2002). Fast-growing *E. coli* excretes acetate even with plenty of oxygen (overflow metabolism), which pure flux balance analysis does not predict because respiration is more efficient per glucose; the explanation is that respiration costs more protein per unit of ATP flux than fermentation, so that under a constraint on the total proteome the cheaper pathway wins at high growth rates (Basan and colleagues, 2015), a constraint on enzyme allocation that is the subject of [7.6.39](/7.6.39). Since an optimum is usually not unique, flux variability analysis computes the range of each flux over all optimal or near-optimal solutions. Regulatory flux balance analysis adds Boolean rules for the expression of enzymes (Covert, Schilling and Palsson, 2001), combining the methods of this section with those of 34.2.3; dynamic flux balance analysis updates the external concentrations between successive linear programs to follow batch growth. Flux balance analysis complements the kinetic picture of [7.1.17](/7.1.17): metabolic control analysis asks how fluxes respond to small changes of enzyme activities near a given state, flux balance analysis which states are possible and which of them is optimal.

## Summary

- Biological networks are nonlinear, self-organizing, heterogeneous, out of equilibrium and multiscale (34.1.1). Their architecture is characterized by the degree distribution (Definition 34.1.1), heavy-tailed and often approximately scale-free (Definition 34.1.2; preferential attachment gives $P(k) \sim 2m^2k^{-3}$, Eq. (34.1)), by a clustering coefficient far above that of random graphs (Definition 34.1.3) and by short paths, the small-world property (Definition 34.1.4); hubs make networks robust to random failure and fragile to targeted attack.

## Questions

1. Show that the mean clustering coefficient of an Erdős–Rényi graph is $p$, and compute $C$ for a random graph with $N = 6000$ proteins and $\langle k\rangle = 6$. Compare with a measured clustering coefficient of about $0.1$ for a protein-interaction network of this size, and explain what the difference means.

   **Answer** Two neighbors of a vertex are adjacent independently of everything else with probability $p$, so $C_i = p$ on average. With $\langle k\rangle = p(N - 1)$, $p = 6/5999 = 0.001$. The measured value is a hundred times larger: interaction partners of a protein tend to interact with each other, because proteins act in complexes and pathways. The network is modular, a property a random graph with the same density lacks entirely; clustering is a signature of the functional organization, not of the density.

2. In the Barabási–Albert model, compute the fraction of vertices with degree at least $k$, and the expected maximal degree in a network of $N$ vertices. Compare with the Erdős–Rényi graph with the same mean degree.

   **Answer** From Eq. (34.1), $\mathbb{P}(K \geq k) = \sum_{j\geq k}2m(m + 1)/(j(j + 1)(j + 2)) = m(m + 1)/(k(k + 1)) \approx m^2/k^2$ (telescoping: $\frac{2}{j(j+1)(j+2)} = \frac{1}{j(j+1)} - \frac{1}{(j+1)(j+2)}$). The largest degree is reached where $N\mathbb{P}(K \geq k) \approx 1$, $k_{\max} \approx m\sqrt N$: for $m = 3$, $N = 20000$, about $420$, as in the figure. In the Erdős–Rényi graph with mean $6$, the Poisson tail gives $N\mathbb{P}(K \geq k) \approx 1$ at $k \approx 18$–$20$. Heavy tails produce hubs whose degree grows with network size.

3. For Eq. (34.2), show that the unit square $[0, 1]^2$ is invariant and that, if the only fixed point inside is unstable, a limit cycle exists. Which hypothesis of the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem would fail for a model with three variables?

   **Answer** On the edges of a slightly enlarged square: on $C = 0$, $\dot C = \alpha_1 > 0$; on $A = 0$, $\dot A \geq 0$; on $A = 1$, $\dot A = -\beta_2 < 0$. On $C = 1$ the feedback term vanishes and $\dot C = \alpha_1 - \beta_1H(A)$, which is positive for small $A$; but for $C = 1 + \varepsilon$ the feedback term $\alpha_3(1 - C)H(C) \approx -\alpha_3\varepsilon$ is negative, and for $\varepsilon > \alpha_1/\alpha_3 \approx 0.03$ it outweighs $\alpha_1$, so the rectangle $[0, 1.04] \times [0, 1]$ is invariant (the model loses its meaning slightly above $C = 1$, but the mathematical argument needs only a bounded invariant region). If the only fixed point inside is unstable, remove a small disk around it: trajectories enter the resulting annulus and stay, and the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem ([2.4.11](/2.4.11#thm-11.6.1)) guarantees a closed orbit. The theorem holds only in the plane: in three dimensions a bounded trajectory without fixed points may be quasi-periodic or chaotic, so the existence of oscillations must be shown otherwise (for example by a Hopf bifurcation or by simulation).

4. Using Eq. (34.3) with $[\mathrm{Cdc20}_{\mathrm{A}}] = 0$, derive the parametric form of the steady-state curve $m([\mathrm{Cdh1}])$ and explain how the size threshold for Start depends on the cyclin synthesis rate $k_1$. What does this predict for a mutant that overexpresses cyclin B?

   **Answer** From $\dot{[\mathrm{CycB}]} = 0$, $[\mathrm{CycB}] = k_1/(k_2' + k_2''H)$ with $H = [\mathrm{Cdh1}]$; from $\dot H = 0$, $m = k_3'(1 - H)(J_4 + H)/[(J_3 + 1 - H)k_4[\mathrm{CycB}]H]$. Since $m \propto 1/[\mathrm{CycB}] \propto 1/k_1$ at fixed $H$, the whole curve scales as $1/k_1$, and so does the critical mass at the saddle-node point (about $0.53$ for $k_1 = 0.04$). Overexpressing cyclin B raises $k_1$ and lowers the size at which cells pass Start, so the mutant divides at a smaller size (and if the lower fold of the S-shaped curve moves to $m < 0$ in effect, cells cannot return to G1 and the cycle fails, as in some cyclin overexpression phenotypes). This is the classic prediction that size at division is set by the ratio of cyclin to its antagonists.

5. Verify two steps of the Boolean trajectory of the yeast cell cycle by hand: from the state with Cln3, Cdh1 and Sic1 on, compute the next two states with Eq. (34.4).

   **Answer** Step 1: MBF and SBF receive $+1$ from Cln3, so they turn on; Cln3 has no input (sum $0$) and degrades; Cdh1 and Sic1 have sum $0$ (their inhibitors are off) and stay on; Clb1,2 receives $-1$ from Cdh1 and $-1$ from Sic1 and stays off; Clb5,6 receives $-1$ from Sic1 and stays off. State: MBF, SBF, Cdh1, Sic1 on. Step 2: SBF activates Cln1,2 ($+1$), MBF activates Clb5,6 ($+1$) but Sic1 inhibits it ($-1$), sum $0$, so Clb5,6 stays off; MBF and SBF stay on (no input); Cdh1 and Sic1 stay on. State: MBF, SBF, Cln1,2, Cdh1, Sic1 on, as in the figure. In step 3, Cln1,2 inhibits Sic1 and Cdh1, which turn off, and in step 4 Clb5,6 is freed (S phase).

6. Write the stoichiometric matrix of the network $\varnothing \xrightarrow{v_1} \mathrm{A}$, $\mathrm{A} \xrightarrow{v_2} \mathrm{B}$, $\mathrm{A} \xrightarrow{v_3} \mathrm{C}$, $\mathrm{B} + \mathrm{C} \xrightarrow{v_4} \mathrm{D}$, $\mathrm{D} \xrightarrow{v_5} \varnothing$ (biomass), with $v_1 \le 10$, find the null space, and solve the flux balance problem of maximizing $v_5$.

   **Answer** With rows A, B, C, D and columns $v_1, \dots, v_5$:
   $$
   S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}.
   $$
   $S\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{0}$ gives $v_2 = v_4$, $v_3 = v_4$, $v_5 = v_4$ and $v_1 = v_2 + v_3 = 2v_4$, so the null space is one-dimensional, spanned by $(2, 1, 1, 1, 1)$ (rank $4$, five reactions). With $v_1 \le 10$ the optimum is $v_5 = 5$, at the vertex $v_1 = 10$. Mass balance alone fixes the flux distribution here because the network has no branches that can be traded off; with an alternative route (for example $\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{C}$ by a second enzyme) the null space would be larger and the objective would choose among the routes.

7. In Example 34.4.1, the optimum uses about $15.9$ units of oxygen for $10$ of glucose. Explain why the line of optimality passes through the origin in the toy model, and what it means if a measured strain grows with an oxygen-to-glucose uptake ratio below this line.

   **Answer** Apart from the small maintenance demand, all constraints are homogeneous: scaling glucose and oxygen uptake by the same factor scales the optimal flux distribution, so the boundary between the regimes is a ray from (nearly) the origin, with slope $1.59$ mol oxygen per mol glucose. A strain below the line uses less oxygen than would be optimal for its glucose uptake and must excrete fermentation products; according to flux balance analysis it is not growing optimally for its uptake rates. Two interpretations: the strain has not adapted to the conditions (as in the glycerol experiments of Ibarra and colleagues, after which evolved strains moved onto the line), or a constraint missing from the model, such as limited respiratory capacity or proteome cost, makes the excretion optimal ([7.6.39](/7.6.39)).

8. Use the toy model of the figure (the code of `fba.py` can be reused) to find the double knockouts that are lethal although neither single knockout is (synthetic lethality). Interpret the result biologically.

   **Answer** At an oxygen limit of $12$, the lethal single knockouts are uptake, glycolysis, PPP, PDH and biomass. Among the remaining reactions (TCA, OxPhos, O₂ uptake, lactate, acetate), the only lethal pairs are OxPhos with lactate and O₂ uptake with lactate: without respiration the only remaining way to reoxidize NADH is lactate excretion, and without either the cell cannot balance its redox. Several other pairs are strongly but not completely deleterious: TCA with acetate reduces growth to $0.93$ (the fermentative rate, since acetyl-CoA can no longer be drained when the TCA cycle is absent and excess NADH must be fermented away), whereas lactate with acetate leaves $90\,\%$ of wild-type growth. Synthetic lethality arises when two pathways can each perform a function that the other cannot (here, the regeneration of NAD⁺), and the computation of all double knockouts is the basis of genome-scale predictions of synthetic lethal pairs, used to find drug targets and to interpret genetic interaction screens ([7.4](/7.4)).

   ```python
   import itertools
   import numpy as np
   from scipy.optimize import linprog
   # S, rxns and col as defined in fba.py

   def growth(knockouts, glc=10.0, o2=12.0):
       bounds = [(0, None)] * len(rxns)
       bounds[col["uptake"]] = (0, glc)
       bounds[col["O2 uptake"]] = (0, o2)
       bounds[col["maintenance"]] = (1, None)
       for r in knockouts:
           bounds[col[r]] = (0, 0)
       c = np.zeros(len(rxns))
       c[col["biomass"]] = -1
       res = linprog(c, A_eq=S, b_eq=np.zeros(S.shape[0]), bounds=bounds, method="highs")
       return -res.fun if res.status == 0 else 0.0

   viable = [r for r in rxns if r not in ("biomass", "maintenance") and growth([r]) > 1e-6]
   for a, b in itertools.combinations(viable, 2):
       print(a, b, round(growth([a, b]), 3))   # 0.0 marks a synthetic lethal pair
   ```

9. (Open question) The yeast Boolean network is robust to most single changes of its wiring. Propose a quantitative measure of robustness for a Boolean network, and design a computational experiment that tests whether robustness was selected for, rather than being a generic property of networks with the same numbers of nodes and links.

   **Answer** Measures: the size of the largest basin relative to $2^N$; the fraction of single-link changes (deletion, addition, sign reversal) that preserve the biological fixed point and the sequence of states of the pathway; the convergence of trajectories (the average number of states that merge into the pathway, or the "flow" towards it). Experiment: generate an ensemble of random networks with the same numbers of nodes and of activating and inhibiting links and the same degree sequence (randomization by swapping link endpoints, which preserves degrees), compute each measure, and compare the real network's value with the ensemble distribution (a z-score or empirical $p$-value). This is what Li and colleagues did; a stricter null model also preserves the self-degradation pattern and requires the random network to have a pathway of the same length. If the real network lies far in the tail, its robustness is unlikely to be generic. The parameter-space analogue for continuous models (the fraction of random parameter sets that produce the function) is the method of [7.6.40](/7.6.40).
