A cell responds to its environment through networks of genes and proteins that activate and repress one another. The previous chapter treated the randomness of individual reactions; this one treats the deterministic dynamics that emerges when reactions are connected into circuits with feedback. Two behaviors recur from bacteria to mammals and are the subject of the chapter: switches, which turn a graded or transient signal into a lasting all-or-none decision, and oscillators, which turn a constant input into a rhythm. Both are properties of the circuit, not of its components, and both can be understood with a small set of tools from the theory of dynamical systems: fixed points and their stability, nullclines and phase planes, and bifurcations. The chapter introduces these tools on the simplest circuits, a single self-regulating gene (33.2), two mutually repressing genes (33.3) and the lac operon (33.4), and then turns to oscillators built from delayed negative feedback and from coupled positive and negative feedback (33.5), to the clocks that time the day (33.6), and to attractor networks as models of memory (33.7).

The stability theory used here, Lyapunov's first method, the classification of planar equilibria, limit cycles, the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem and the Hopf bifurcation, is developed in [2.4.11](/2.4.11), and the topological view of developmental decisions, including the **Waddington landscape** and catastrophe theory, in [7.5.10](/7.5.10). The production–removal equation, the response time and autoregulation of a single gene are in [7.2.22](/7.2.22#sec-22.3), the biology of feedback in signaling (bistable kinase switches, adaptation, Ca²⁺ oscillations) in [7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.4), and the embryonic cell-cycle oscillator in [7.3.20](/7.3.20#sec-20.6); they are used here, not repeated. Larger networks, the cell cycle and metabolism, follow in [7.6.34](/7.6.34). The general theory of network motifs, the synchronization of oscillators and the propagation of noise through circuits belong to [systems biology](/7.9); this chapter treats the physics of specific circuits.

## 33.1. Networks as Dynamical Systems

### 33.1.1. Signed Networks and the Sign of a Loop

A regulatory network is a directed graph whose vertices are molecular species and whose arcs are interactions. Since an interaction either increases or decreases the activity of its target, the network is represented by a signed **adjacency matrix** $M$ ([1.4.1](/1.4.1)), with $M_{ij} = +1$ if $i$ activates $j$, $-1$ if $i$ represses $j$ and $0$ if $i$ does not act on $j$. A path is a sequence of arcs, and its sign is the product of the signs of its arcs: two repressions in series amount to an activation. This is how the lac repressor works: lactose (through allolactose) inactivates the repressor, the repressor blocks the operon, and the net effect of lactose on the operon is positive.

A path that returns to its start is a feedback loop, positive if the product of its signs is $+1$ (an even number of repressions) and negative otherwise. The sign of loops constrains what a network can do, as René Thomas conjectured and as was later proved for differential equations under mild conditions (Thomas, 1981; Soulé, 2003): a positive loop is necessary for multistability, and a negative loop is necessary for sustained oscillations. The rest of this chapter makes the two halves of the rule quantitative. Two routes from a signal to a target, one direct and one through an intermediate, form a feed-forward loop rather than a feedback loop; feed-forward loops filter transient signals or generate pulses, and their systematic analysis as network motifs is in [7.9](/7.9).

### 33.1.2. Three Levels of Description

The same circuit can be modeled at three resolutions (SH 19.2.6): as a **Boolean network** ([1.3.4](/1.3.4)), in which each species is on or off and time advances in steps; as ordinary differential equations for concentrations, in which production rates are Hill functions of the regulators (the gene regulation function of [7.2.22](/7.2.22#sec-22.4)); and as a chemical master equation for copy numbers ([7.6.32](/7.6.32)). Boolean models need no rate constants and scale to large networks; differential equations give thresholds, time scales and bifurcations; master equations give fluctuations and switching rates. The equations of this chapter have the common form
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = f_i(x_1, \dots, x_N) - \gamma_ix_i, \tag{33.1}
$$
a production term that depends on the regulators and a first-order removal by degradation and dilution. For a gene activated by $X$ the production is $f = \beta x^n/(K^n + x^n)$, for a repressed gene $f = \beta/(1 + (x/K)^n)$, with Hill coefficients typically between $1$ and $4$; combinatorial regulation multiplies or adds such factors.

**Example 33.1.1 (A Logic Gate)** The lac operon integrates two signals (SH 19.2.5). Allolactose relieves repression by LacI, and cAMP–CAP, which accumulates when glucose is scarce, recruits RNA polymerase ([7.2.11](/7.2.11)). As a Boolean function the promoter is an AND gate, $\mathrm{lacP} = \mathrm{lac} \land \lnot\mathrm{glu}$: the operon is expressed only when lactose is present and glucose absent. A continuous version multiplies two Hill functions, $f = \beta\,\frac{[\mathrm{cAMP}]}{K_{\mathrm{c}} + [\mathrm{cAMP}]}\,\frac{[\mathrm{allo}]}{K_{\mathrm{a}} + [\mathrm{allo}]}$, which is small if either input is small. Setty, Mayo, Surette and Alon (2003) measured the promoter's output at about a hundred combinations of cAMP and IPTG and found a function intermediate between AND and OR, and showed with a model that a few point mutations in the binding sites could move it towards either; the logic of a promoter is a quantitative property of its binding energies.

## 33.2. One-Dimensional Feedback

### 33.2.1. Fixed Points, Stability and Basins

The simplest feedback is a gene whose product regulates its own promoter. With a single variable, Eq. (33.1) reads $\dot x = F(x) = f(x) - \gamma x$. A fixed point $x^*$ satisfies $F(x^*) = 0$, the intersection of the production curve with the removal line. A small deviation obeys $\dot{\delta x} = F'(x^*)\delta x$, so it decays if $F'(x^*) < 0$ and grows if $F'(x^*) > 0$; graphically, a fixed point is stable if production falls below removal just above it and exceeds removal just below it ([2.4.11](/2.4.11#sec-11.3)).

**Definition 33.2.1 (Attractor and Basin of Attraction)** An **attractor** of a dynamical system is a closed invariant set of states, such as a stable fixed point or a stable **limit cycle**, to which all states in some neighborhood converge as $t \to \infty$. The **basin of attraction** of an attractor is the set of all initial states whose trajectories converge to it.

In one dimension, the basins of the stable fixed points are separated by the unstable ones, which act as thresholds. Positive autoregulation by a dimer, $f(x) = b_0 + Vx^2/(K^2 + x^2)$, gives a sigmoidal production curve that the removal line can cross three times: a low stable state, an unstable threshold and a high stable state (figure, panel A). Cyanobacteria use such a loop when they differentiate nitrogen-fixing heterocysts under nitrogen starvation: the transcription factor HetR, active as a dimer, activates its own gene, and a cell whose HetR level crosses the threshold commits to the heterocyst fate (SH 19.4.1). Autophosphorylating kinases and the autoinducer circuits of bacterial quorum sensing are positive loops of the same kind.

### 33.2.2. The Saddle-Node Bifurcation and Hysteresis

As a parameter changes, fixed points move, and at special values they appear or disappear. For the self-activating gene, increasing the maximal production $V$ raises the sigmoid: at a critical value its middle part touches the removal line from below, and a new pair of fixed points, one stable and one unstable, is born; at a higher value the low stable state collides with the threshold and both vanish.

**Definition 33.2.2 (Saddle-Node Bifurcation)** A **saddle-node bifurcation** is a bifurcation in which a stable and an unstable fixed point approach each other as a parameter $\mu$ varies, merge at the critical value $\mu_{\mathrm{c}}$ and disappear. Near it the dynamics has the normal form $\dot x = \mu - \mu_{\mathrm{c}} + x^2$ (up to signs and rescaling): two fixed points $x = \pm\sqrt{\mu_{\mathrm{c}} - \mu}$ for $\mu < \mu_{\mathrm{c}}$ and none for $\mu > \mu_{\mathrm{c}}$.

Between the two saddle-node points of the self-activating gene, both stable states coexist (**bistability**), and the state a cell occupies depends on its history (**hysteresis**, [7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.4.3)): increasing $V$ from below keeps the cell on the low branch until that branch ends at the upper fold, where it jumps to the high branch; decreasing $V$ again keeps it high until the lower fold (panel B). The two thresholds differ, so a transient stimulus that pushes the cell across the upper fold leaves a permanent memory. Near a saddle-node point the relaxation slows, because $F'(x^*) \to 0$: the return time to the steady state diverges as $\lvert\mu - \mu_{\mathrm{c}}\rvert^{-1/2}$, and, as shown in [7.6.32](/7.6.32#sec-32.6.3), fluctuations grow. Slow recovery from perturbations and growing variance are therefore early warnings of an impending switch, observed in cell populations and ecosystems alike (Dai, Vorselen, Korolev and Gore, 2012).

### 33.2.3. Negative Autoregulation

With negative autoregulation, $f$ decreases with $x$, so $F' = f' - \gamma < 0$ everywhere: there is exactly one fixed point and it is stable. A strongly self-repressing gene holds its level near the repression threshold $K$ whatever the parameters, the basis of homeostasis (panel C): if the degradation rate doubles, an unregulated protein halves, whereas a protein that represses itself with a steep Hill function changes by a fraction of that, the more so the steeper the repression. The same feedback speeds up the response to a step in production and suppresses noise ([7.2.22](/7.2.22#sec-22.6); Eq. (32.17) of [7.6.32](/7.6.32#sec-32.6.3)). In one dimension negative feedback cannot oscillate, since a trajectory on a line cannot turn back without passing through a fixed point; oscillation requires more variables (33.5).

## 33.3. The Toggle Switch

### 33.3.1. Nullclines and Fixed Points

Two genes that repress each other form a positive loop with two repressions. Gardner, Cantor and Collins (2000) built such a circuit in *E. coli* from the lac repressor and a temperature-sensitive λ repressor (and, in a second version, TetR), each expressed from a promoter repressed by the other; a pulse of IPTG or of heat flipped the cells into one state or the other, and the state persisted for many generations after the inducer was removed (the same issue of Nature reported the repressilator, 33.5.3). With the concentrations measured in units of the repressors' dissociation constants and time in units of the protein lifetime, the circuit is
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t} = -u + \frac{\alpha}{1 + v^n}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = -v + \frac{\alpha}{1 + u^n}, \tag{33.2}
$$
where $\alpha$ is the dimensionless promoter strength (maximal production times lifetime divided by the dissociation constant) and $n$ the cooperativity of repression (PBoC 19.3.5).

**Definition 33.3.1 (Genetic Toggle Switch)** A **genetic toggle switch** is a circuit of two genes whose products repress each other's expression, so that the circuit has, for suitable parameters, two stable states, one with each gene on and the other off, and can be flipped between them by transient signals.

The analysis of a planar system begins with its nullclines.

**Definition 33.3.2 (Nullcline)** A **nullcline** of a planar system $\dot x = f(x, y)$, $\dot y = g(x, y)$ is a curve on which one component of the velocity vanishes: the $x$-nullcline is $f(x, y) = 0$, on which trajectories move vertically, and the $y$-nullcline is $g(x, y) = 0$, on which they move horizontally. The fixed points are the intersections of the nullclines.

For Eq. (33.2) the nullclines are $u = \alpha/(1 + v^n)$ and $v = \alpha/(1 + u^n)$, two decreasing curves symmetric about the diagonal. Substituting one into the other gives $u = h(h(u))$ with the decreasing function $h(x) = \alpha/(1 + x^n)$; since $h \circ h$ is increasing and sigmoidal, it can cross the diagonal once or three times. The symmetric fixed point $u = v = s$, with $s(1 + s^n) = \alpha$, always exists. For $n = 2$ the fixed-point condition factorizes as $(u^2 - \alpha u + 1)(u^3 + u - \alpha) = 0$: the cubic gives the symmetric point, and the quadratic gives two asymmetric points $u_\pm = (\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - 4})/2$, $v_\pm = u_\mp$, which exist only for $\alpha > 2$ (PBoC 19.3.5).

### 33.3.2. Linear Stability in the Plane

At a fixed point $(u^*, v^*)$ the linearized system is governed by the **Jacobian matrix**
$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & h'(v^*) \\ h'(u^*) & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}J = -2, \qquad \det J = 1 - h'(u^*)h'(v^*). \tag{33.3}
$$
For a planar system the eigenvalues solve $\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta = 0$ with $\tau = \operatorname{tr}J$ and $\Delta = \det J$: a fixed point is a saddle (unstable) if $\Delta < 0$; stable if $\Delta > 0$ and $\tau < 0$ (a node if $\tau^2 > 4\Delta$, a focus otherwise); unstable if $\Delta > 0$ and $\tau > 0$ ([2.4.11](/2.4.11#sec-11.5.2)). Since $\tau = -2$ for the toggle, everything depends on the sign of $\Delta$, that is on whether the loop gain $h'(u^*)h'(v^*)$, a product of two negative slopes, exceeds one.

**Theorem 33.3.1 (Bistability of the Toggle Switch)** The symmetric fixed point of Eq. (33.2) is stable if $\lvert h'(s)\rvert < 1$ and a saddle if $\lvert h'(s)\rvert > 1$, where $\lvert h'(s)\rvert = ns^n/(1 + s^n)$. The switch is bistable if and only if $n > 1$ and
$$
\alpha > \alpha_{\mathrm{c}} = \frac{n}{n - 1}\,(n - 1)^{-1/n}; \tag{33.4}
$$
$\alpha_{\mathrm{c}} = 2$ for $n = 2$ and $1.19$ for $n = 3$. At $\alpha_{\mathrm{c}}$ the two asymmetric stable states branch off the symmetric state in a pitchfork bifurcation.

**Proof** At the symmetric point $\Delta = 1 - h'(s)^2$, and using $\alpha = s(1 + s^n)$, $\lvert h'(s)\rvert = \alpha ns^{n-1}/(1 + s^n)^2 = ns^n/(1 + s^n)$. This increases with $s$, hence with $\alpha$, from $0$ towards $n$; it can exceed one only if $n > 1$, and equals one at $s^n = 1/(n - 1)$, which gives $\alpha_{\mathrm{c}} = s(1 + s^n)$ as in Eq. (33.4). When the symmetric point becomes a saddle, its unstable direction is the antidiagonal $(1, -1)$ (the eigenvector of $J$ with eigenvalue $-1 + \lvert h'(s)\rvert$), and trajectories leaving it along that direction converge to two new fixed points, by the symmetry $u \leftrightarrow v$ located symmetrically about the diagonal; at these $\lvert h'(u^*)h'(v^*)\rvert < 1$ (one repressor is high and the other repressed far into the flat part of its Hill function), so they are stable. $\square$

**Definition 33.3.3 (Pitchfork Bifurcation)** A **pitchfork bifurcation** is a bifurcation of a system with a symmetry in which a symmetric fixed point loses stability as a parameter crosses a critical value and two new stable fixed points, images of each other under the symmetry, branch off from it; its normal form is $\dot x = \mu x - x^3$.

The pitchfork is a consequence of the exact symmetry of Eq. (33.2). Any difference between the two promoters breaks the symmetry and unfolds the pitchfork into a saddle-node bifurcation, at which a single new pair of fixed points appears away from the original branch (figure, panel C). The diagonal $u = v$ in the bistable regime is the separatrix, the boundary between the two basins of attraction: an inducer that weakens one repressor shifts the nullclines so that the state on one side of the separatrix disappears in a saddle-node bifurcation, and the cell is forced into the other basin, where it stays after the inducer is gone.

### 33.3.3. Mutual Inhibition and Cell Fate

Mutual repression between transcription factors that specify alternative fates is common in development: PU.1 and GATA1 in the choice between myeloid and erythroid fates of blood progenitors, Cdx2 and Oct4 in the first lineage decision of the mammalian embryo, Nanog and Gata6 in the second. The toggle model explains several features of such decisions (SH 19.4.2). A progenitor that expresses both factors at intermediate levels, a "primed" state, corresponds to the symmetric state below the pitchfork; as the strength of the mutual repression (or of the factors' self-activation, which many fate determinants also have) increases during differentiation, the symmetric state becomes unstable and the cell must choose; signals that bias one factor make the choice asymmetric. This sequence of bifurcations is a dynamical reading of the **Waddington landscape**, in which a valley branches into two ([7.5.10](/7.5.10#sec-10.2), [7.3.24](/7.3.24)). Since the decision is made by a loop, it can be reversed by overexpressing the factors of the other side, as in the reprogramming of fibroblasts to pluripotency by Oct4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc ([7.5.36](/7.5.36)), in which lineage specifiers that counteract each other can substitute for the pluripotency factors (Shu and colleagues, 2013).

## 33.4. The lac Operon: Logic and Bistability

### 33.4.1. The Maintenance Effect

In 1957 Novick and Weiner found that *E. coli* cells grown with a non-metabolizable inducer (TMG) responded in an all-or-none way: at intermediate concentrations, individual cells were either fully induced or uninduced, and the fraction of induced cells, not their level, grew with the inducer and with time. Cells that had been induced at a high concentration remained induced when transferred to a "maintenance" concentration at which uninduced cells stayed uninduced for many generations. The explanation is a positive loop (SH 19.4.1): the operon encodes the permease LacY, which imports the inducer, which inactivates the repressor, which increases the expression of LacY. When the inducer is lactose, it is also metabolized by LacZ, which adds a negative loop and makes the behavior graded; with TMG or IPTG the positive loop dominates.

A minimal model takes the expression level $x$ of the operon (in units of full induction, time in units of dilution), an internal inducer concentration proportional to the external one $T$ and to the permease, $y = T(\delta + x)$ (with $\delta$ a small leak by passive uptake, $T$ in units of the repressor's inducer affinity), and repression by LacI relieved by the binding of two inducer molecules:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{1}{1 + \rho/(1 + y^2)} - x, \tag{33.5}
$$
where $\rho$ is the repression factor, the ratio of fully induced to repressed expression, of order $100$–$1000$ for the lac operon ([7.2.11](/7.2.11)). The production term, plotted on logarithmic axes, is a sigmoid between $1/(1 + \rho)$ and $1$, and the removal line crosses it three times over a range of $T$ (figure, panel A). For $\rho = 200$ and $\delta = 0.01$ the system is bistable for $28 < T < 58$: a twofold range of inducer concentration in which the expression of a cell is determined by its history (panel B).

Ozbudak, Thattai, Lim, Shraiman and van Oudenaarden (2004) mapped this hysteresis in single cells. They measured the expression of the operon with a chromosomal GFP reporter as a function of TMG and of the glucose concentration (which, through cAMP–CAP, sets the maximal expression) in cells that had been either fully induced or uninduced before. Over a several-fold range of TMG concentrations the population was bimodal and its state depended on its history, and the boundaries of the bistable region in the plane of TMG and glucose had the cusp shape predicted by a model of the form (33.5). The transitions of single cells across the boundary are the noise-driven switches of [7.6.32](/7.6.32#sec-32.7.2), triggered by rare complete dissociations of the repressor (Choi and colleagues, 2008).

## 33.5. Oscillators

### 33.5.1. Negative Feedback and Delay

A negative loop drives a variable back towards its set point. If the correction arrives late, the variable overshoots, and the loop then drives it back too far in the opposite direction: a thermostat with a slow sensor makes the room oscillate. In a genetic circuit the delays come from the steps between the transcription of a repressor's gene and the action of the protein: transcription, translation, folding, modification, transport into the nucleus. A chain of first-order steps acts as a distributed delay, and the question is how long the chain and how steep the repression must be.

**Definition 33.5.1 (Goodwin Oscillator)** The **Goodwin oscillator** is the model of a gene whose product represses its own transcription through a chain of intermediates,
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_1}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\alpha}{1 + x_N^n} - \gamma_1x_1, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}x_k}{\mathrm{d}t} = x_{k-1} - \gamma_kx_k \quad (k = 2, \dots, N), \tag{33.6}
$$
with $x_1$ the mRNA, $x_2$ the protein and $x_N$ the repressor in its active form (Goodwin, 1965).

**Theorem 33.5.1 (Secant Condition)** For a negative loop of $N$ first-order stages with equal rate constants $\gamma$, the steady state is unstable, and the system oscillates, if the loop gain, the product of the magnitudes of the logarithmic sensitivities around the loop at the steady state, exceeds $\sec(\pi/N)^N$. For unequal rate constants the steady state is stable whenever the gain is below $\sec(\pi/N)^N$, which is therefore a necessary condition for oscillation.

**Proof** (Equal rates.) Write the linearization about the steady state in scaled deviations. Each stage contributes a factor $(\lambda + \gamma)$, and the loop closes with gain $-\gamma^NG$ ($G$ the loop gain, the minus sign the repression), so the characteristic equation is $(\lambda + \gamma)^N = -\gamma^NG$. Its roots are $\lambda = \gamma(G^{1/N}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi(2k+1)/N} - 1)$, and the root with the largest real part has $\operatorname{Re}\lambda = \gamma(G^{1/N}\cos(\pi/N) - 1)$, positive if and only if $G > \sec(\pi/N)^N$. At the threshold a pair of complex conjugate eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis with frequency $\gamma\tan(\pi/N)$, a Hopf bifurcation (33.5.2). The case of unequal rates, in which the equal-rate case is the least stable, is due to Thron (1991) and Sontag (2006). $\square$

For $N = 2$ the threshold is infinite: a two-stage negative loop cannot oscillate, in agreement with the Bendixson criterion ([2.4.11](/2.4.11#sec-11.6.2)). For $N = 3$ the gain must exceed $\sec(\pi/3)^3 = 8$, and since the logarithmic sensitivity of a Hill repression is at most $n$, Goodwin's three-variable model oscillates only for Hill coefficients above $8$ (Griffith, 1968), an unrealistically steep repression (figure in 33.5.2, panel C). Longer chains lower the threshold towards $1$ ($\sec(\pi/N)^N \to 1$ as $N \to \infty$): delay can substitute for cooperativity, and explicit transcriptional delays, nuclear transport and multisite phosphorylation make realistic oscillators of this kind possible, such as the oscillators of somitogenesis ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)). Other ways around the high threshold are saturable (Michaelis–Menten) degradation, which adds nonlinearity, and coupling to positive feedback (33.5.4).

### 33.5.2. The Hopf Bifurcation

In all these cases the oscillation is born when a stable fixed point becomes unstable with a pair of complex eigenvalues crossing the imaginary axis.

**Theorem 33.5.2 (Hopf Bifurcation, Normal Form)** In polar coordinates the normal form of a supercritical Hopf bifurcation is
$$
\dot r = \mu r - r^3, \qquad \dot\theta = \omega + br^2. \tag{33.7}
$$
For $\mu < 0$ the origin is a stable focus. For $\mu > 0$ it is an unstable focus surrounded by a stable limit cycle of radius $\sqrt{\mu}$ and frequency $\omega + b\mu$: the amplitude of the oscillation grows from zero as the square root of the distance from the bifurcation, while its frequency stays near the imaginary part of the eigenvalues at the bifurcation.

**Proof** The radial equation is independent of $\theta$. Its fixed points are $r = 0$ and, for $\mu > 0$, $r = \sqrt{\mu}$; the derivative of $\mu r - r^3$ is $\mu$ at $r = 0$ and $-2\mu$ at $r = \sqrt\mu$, so for $\mu > 0$ the origin repels and the circle $r = \sqrt\mu$ attracts, and on it $\dot\theta = \omega + b\mu$. That a generic system with a pair of eigenvalues crossing the imaginary axis can be transformed into Eq. (33.7) near the bifurcation, with a coefficient of $r^3$ whose sign decides between the supercritical case shown and a subcritical one, is the Hopf theorem ([2.4.11](/2.4.11#sec-11.6.4)). $\square$

The square-root growth of the amplitude distinguishes this soft onset from the subcritical case, in which a large oscillation appears abruptly and coexists with the stable steady state over a range of parameters, with hysteresis. For the planar systems of this chapter, the existence of a limit cycle can also be shown without local analysis: if all trajectories enter a bounded region that contains a single, unstable fixed point, the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem guarantees a closed orbit inside ([2.4.11](/2.4.11#thm-11.6.1)).

### 33.5.3. The Repressilator

**Definition 33.5.2 (Repressilator)** The **repressilator** is a synthetic genetic oscillator of three repressors arranged in a cycle, each repressing the transcription of the next: LacI represses *tetR*, TetR represses *cI*, and λ CI represses *lacI* (Elowitz and Leibler, 2000).

With mRNA concentrations $m_i$ and protein concentrations $p_i$ (in units of the repression threshold) and time in units of the mRNA lifetime,
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}m_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = -m_i + \frac{\alpha}{1 + p_j^n} + \alpha_0, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}p_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\beta(p_i - m_i), \tag{33.8}
$$
where $j$ is the repressor of gene $i$, $\alpha_0$ the leaky expression, $\alpha + \alpha_0$ the maximal expression and $\beta$ the ratio of the mRNA to the protein lifetime. The loop has six stages with two different rates; with $\alpha_0 = 0$ the symmetric steady state $p^* = m^*$ solves $p(1 + p^n) = \alpha$, and the magnitude of the slope of the repression there is $X = np^{*n}/(1 + p^{*n})$. The characteristic equation factorizes into $(\lambda + 1)(\lambda + \beta) = \beta X\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi/3}$ (and a stable real factor), and setting $\lambda = \mathrm{i}y$ gives the boundary of the oscillatory region:
$$
\text{unstable} \iff \frac{(\beta + 1)^2}{\beta} < \frac{3X^2}{4 - 2X} \quad (X < 2), \qquad \text{always unstable for } X \geq 2. \tag{33.9}
$$
The left side is smallest, $4$, when $\beta = 1$, which requires $X > 4/3$; since $X < n$, there is no oscillation for $n = 1$, and for $n = 2$ the steady-state level must exceed $\sqrt{2}$, that is $\alpha > 3\sqrt{2} \approx 4.2$. The design rules are therefore strong promoters, cooperative repression, and similar lifetimes of mRNA and protein (figure, panel B). Elowitz and Leibler used repressors fused to degradation tags to shorten the protein lifetimes, and observed oscillations of a GFP reporter with a period of about $150\,\mathrm{min}$, longer than the cell cycle, in about $40\,\%$ of the cells, with large variability between cells and between periods. Potvin-Trottier, Lord, Vinnicombe and Paulsson (2016) traced most of this variability to the noise of the circuit: removing a protein-degradation bottleneck shared by the repressors and sequestering the reporter's repressor in a sponge plasmid gave oscillations so regular that cells in a colony stayed in phase for dozens of generations without any coupling.

### 33.5.4. Relaxation Oscillators

The most robust biological oscillators couple a fast positive loop, which makes a switch, to a slow negative loop, which flips it back and forth.

**Definition 33.5.3 (Relaxation Oscillator)** A **relaxation oscillator** is an oscillator in which a fast variable with bistable dynamics (an S-shaped nullcline) is driven back and forth across its hysteresis loop by a slow variable that it controls through negative feedback; each cycle consists of slow phases, in which the system creeps along the stable branches of the fast nullcline, separated by fast jumps between them.

A minimal genetic version has an activator $A$ that activates its own gene and that of a repressor $R$, which represses the activator's gene (Barkai and Leibler, 2000; Vilar, Kueh, Barkai and Leibler, 2002; PBoC 19.3.6). If the activator is fast and the repressor slow, at each moment the activator sits on its S-shaped nullcline at the current repressor level. Starting with little repressor the activator is high; the repressor accumulates slowly until the activator's upper branch ends at a fold, where the activator collapses to the lower branch; the repressor then decays slowly until the lower branch ends, and the activator jumps up again (figure). The amplitude is set by the width of the hysteresis loop, not by a delicate balance of gains, and the period mainly by the slow variable's rate: frequency can be tuned over a wide range without loss of amplitude, which is the property of the embryonic cell cycle in which positive feedback on Cdk1 creates the switch and APC/C provides the slow negative loop ([7.3.20](/7.3.20#sec-20.6), and the two-variable model in [7.6.34](/7.6.34)). Synthetic activator–repressor oscillators built from AraC and LacI in *E. coli* oscillated robustly over wide ranges of inducer, with periods tunable from about $15$ to $60\,\mathrm{min}$ (Stricker and colleagues, 2008). The same architecture underlies the action potential ([7.6.30](/7.6.30)), in which the fast positive loop is the regenerative opening of sodium channels.

### 33.5.5. The p53–Mdm2 Oscillator

After DNA double-strand breaks, the tumor suppressor **p53** accumulates in pulses of fixed amplitude and duration, about $5.5\,\mathrm{h}$ apart in human cells, and the number of pulses, not their size, increases with the dose of damage (Lahav and colleagues, 2004; [7.3.22](/7.3.22#sec-22.3.3)). The core is a negative loop: p53 activates the gene of its ubiquitin ligase **Mdm2**, which marks p53 for degradation. A two-variable model of this loop cannot sustain oscillations; including the mRNA of Mdm2 as a third variable, or an explicit delay, provides the lag needed for a Hopf bifurcation, and the stimulus, which inactivates Mdm2 and activates p53 through the kinase ATM, moves the system across it: low damage gives a stable low steady state, intermediate damage sustained oscillations, and very high damage a stable high state (SH 19.5.2). The pulses can be decoded: replacing pulses by sustained p53 with drugs changed the genes expressed and the fate of the cells, from transient arrest towards senescence (Purvis and colleagues, 2012).

## 33.6. Biological Clocks

### 33.6.1. Circadian Rhythms

Most organisms that live in daylight anticipate the day with an internal clock whose free-running period, in constant conditions, is close to but not exactly $24\,\mathrm{h}$ and which is entrained by light and temperature. Three properties distinguish a clock from an arbitrary oscillator: it runs autonomously; its period is compensated against temperature (a $Q_{10}$, the factor by which the period changes for a $10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ rise, close to $1$, whereas biochemical rates typically double or triple); and its phase can be reset by external cues (**entrainment**). In animals, plants and fungi the core is a transcription–translation feedback loop of the delayed negative kind: in *Drosophila* and mammals, the activators CLOCK and BMAL1 (CYCLE in flies) drive the *Per* and *Cry* genes (*per* and *tim* in flies), whose products accumulate in the cytoplasm, are phosphorylated over hours by casein kinases, enter the nucleus and repress their own activators; the slow, multisite phosphorylation supplies the delay that sets the period. Mutations in the kinase sites change the period, as in familial advanced sleep phase syndrome in humans.

### 33.6.2. The KaiABC Oscillator in a Test Tube

The cyanobacterium *Synechococcus elongatus* has a circadian clock that requires no transcription at all. Nakajima and colleagues (2005) mixed the three purified proteins KaiA, KaiB and KaiC with ATP and observed that the phosphorylation of KaiC oscillated with a period of about $24\,\mathrm{h}$, compensated against temperature, for days. KaiC is a hexameric ATPase that autophosphorylates and autodephosphorylates at two sites, S431 and T432, in an ordered cycle (T432 first, then S431; dephosphorylation in the same order); KaiA promotes phosphorylation, and KaiC phosphorylated at S431 binds KaiB, which then sequesters KaiA. The positive element is the stimulation of phosphorylation by KaiA, the delayed negative element the sequestration of KaiA by the KaiB–KaiC complexes that form late in the cycle, and the oscillation of individual hexamers is synchronized in the population by the competition for the limited KaiA (van Zon, Lubensky, Altena and ten Wolde, 2007; Rust, Markson, Lane, Fisher and O'Shea, 2007). The clock is slow because KaiC is a remarkably slow ATPase, hydrolyzing about $15$ ATP per monomer per day (Terauchi and colleagues, 2007), and its ATPase activity is itself temperature compensated. Being driven by ATP hydrolysis, the KaiABC clock is a nonequilibrium machine whose precision has a thermodynamic cost; the relation between the energy dissipated per cycle and the number of coherent oscillations is treated in [7.6.38](/7.6.38).

### 33.6.3. Amplitude and Frequency Coding

Oscillations transmit information in their frequency as well as their level. Ca²⁺ oscillations in hepatocytes and other cells encode the strength of a hormonal stimulus mainly in their frequency, decoded by kinases such as CaMKII and by transcription factors with different activation and inactivation kinetics (the two-pool model and its decoding are in [7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.4.6)). In the language of this chapter, a **calcium oscillation** is a relaxation oscillation: Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release is the fast positive loop, depletion of the stores and slow inactivation of the release channel the negative one, and the stimulus moves the system through a range bounded by two Hopf bifurcations, between which the frequency rises steadily while the amplitude stays nearly constant.

## 33.7. Attractor Networks: The Hopfield Model

Switches store one bit; a network of many mutually interacting units can store many patterns as attractors, the idea behind models of associative memory in the brain and of cell types as attractors of gene regulatory networks (Kauffman, 1969). The clearest example is the network of Hopfield (1982), built on the **Ising model** of [7.6.4](/7.6.4#sec-4.4).

**Definition 33.7.1 (Hopfield Network)** A **Hopfield network** is a network of $N$ binary units $\sigma_i = \pm 1$ with symmetric couplings $J_{ij} = J_{ji}$ ($J_{ii} = 0$), updated asynchronously by $\sigma_i \leftarrow \operatorname{sgn}(h_i)$ with the local field $h_i = \sum_jJ_{ij}\sigma_j$, and with couplings chosen by Hebb's rule to store $K$ patterns $\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(\mu)} \in \{\pm 1\}^N$,
$$
J_{ij} = \frac1N\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\xi_i^{(\mu)}\xi_j^{(\mu)}. \tag{33.10}
$$

Because the couplings are symmetric, each update can only lower the energy $E = -\frac12\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$ (it aligns one spin with its field), so the dynamics descends a landscape and ends in a local minimum: the attractors are fixed points, unlike the cycles of the oscillators above. With the Hebbian couplings the energy is $E = -\frac{N}{2}\sum_\mu m_\mu^2$, where $m_\mu = \frac1N\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(\mu)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is the overlap with pattern $\mu$. If the patterns are random and few, they are nearly orthogonal in high dimension, and each stored pattern (and its negative) is a minimum: a state close to a pattern, a corrupted memory, flows to it. As $K$ grows, the crosstalk between patterns acts as random couplings, and above a critical load $K/N \approx 0.138$ (Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky, 1985) retrieval fails and the network behaves like a spin glass, with many spurious minima and frustration. The model abstracts away most of biology: real neural networks are sparse, asymmetric and modular. But it makes precise two ideas used throughout this course: that a network's states of interest are its attractors and their basins, as for the cell-cycle network of [7.6.34](/7.6.34), and that learning can be the shaping of an energy landscape. The theory of neural network dynamics and learning belongs to [computational neuroscience](/7.16) and to [artificial neural networks](/4.7).

## Questions

1. For the self-activating gene $\dot x = Vx^2/(1 + x^2) - x$ (no basal expression), find the fixed points and their stability as functions of $V$. Why is there no hysteresis loop in this model, and what does a small basal expression change?

   **Answer** $x = 0$ is always a fixed point, with $F'(0) = -1$: stable. Other fixed points solve $Vx/(1 + x^2) = 1$, i.e. $x^2 - Vx + 1 = 0$, $x_\pm = (V \pm \sqrt{V^2 - 4})/2$, which exist for $V \geq 2$ (saddle-node at $V = 2$, $x = 1$); $x_-$ is unstable (the threshold), $x_+$ stable. Since the off state $x = 0$ exists for all $V$, a cell in the off state never switches on by increasing $V$: the right fold of the hysteresis loop is at infinity. Basal expression $b_0 > 0$ lifts the production curve at $x = 0$, so the off state moves to $x \approx b_0$ and collides with the threshold at a finite $V$, creating the second saddle-node point and a closed hysteresis loop (for $b_0 = 0.1$, between $V \approx 1.8$ and $2.6$, figure in 33.2). For too large $b_0$ the bistability disappears altogether (no three intersections for $b_0 \gtrsim 0.2$ here).

2. Derive Eq. (33.4) for $n = 3$ and compute the asymmetric fixed points of the toggle switch for $n = 2$, $\alpha = 3$. Check them against the figure.

   **Answer** For $n = 3$: $s^3 = 1/2$, $s = 0.794$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{c}} = s(1 + s^3) = 0.794 \times 1.5 = 1.19$, equal to $\frac{3}{2}\cdot 2^{-1/3}$. For $n = 2$, $\alpha = 3$: $u_\pm = (3 \pm \sqrt{5})/2 = 2.618$ and $0.382$, with $v = u_\mp$; the symmetric point solves $u^3 + u - 3 = 0$, $u = 1.213$. These are the three intersections in panel B. Check of stability: at $(2.618, 0.382)$, $\lvert h'(v)\rvert = 2\alpha v/(1 + v^2)^2 = 2.29/1.30 = 1.76$ and $\lvert h'(u)\rvert = 15.7/(7.85)^2 = 0.255$, product $0.45 < 1$: stable; at the symmetric point $\lvert h'\rvert = 2s^2/(1 + s^2) = 1.19 > 1$: saddle.

3. Show that a two-variable negative feedback loop $\dot x = f(y) - x$, $\dot y = x - y$ with decreasing $f$ cannot have a limit cycle, and explain why adding a third stage changes this.

   **Answer** The divergence of the vector field is $\partial_x(f(y) - x) + \partial_y(x - y) = -2 < 0$ everywhere, so by Bendixson's criterion ([2.4.11](/2.4.11#sec-11.6.2)) there is no closed orbit. Equivalently, the Jacobian has trace $-2$ and determinant $1 - f'(y^*) > 0$, so the single fixed point is stable for any steepness: with $N = 2$, $\sec(\pi/2)^2 = \infty$. A third stage adds phase lag: each first-order stage delays a sinusoidal perturbation by up to $90°$, and with three stages the total lag can reach $180°$ at a finite frequency, at which the negative feedback becomes positive and, if the gain there exceeds one (loop gain $> 8$ for equal stages), amplifies the oscillation.

4. For the repressilator with $n = 2$ and $\beta = 1$, what is the minimal promoter strength $\alpha$ for oscillation? How does the answer change for $\beta = 0.1$ (proteins ten times longer lived than mRNA)?

   **Answer** At $\beta = 1$, Eq. (33.9) requires $3X^2/(4 - 2X) > 4$, i.e. $3X^2 + 8X - 16 > 0$, $X > 4/3$. With $X = 2p^2/(1 + p^2)$ this gives $p^2 > 2$ and $\alpha = p(1 + p^2) > 3\sqrt2 = 4.24$. At $\beta = 0.1$, $(\beta + 1)^2/\beta = 12.1$, so $3X^2 > 12.1(4 - 2X)$, $3X^2 + 24.2X - 48.4 > 0$, $X > 1.66$; then $2p^2/(1 + p^2) > 1.66$ gives $p^2 > 4.9$, $p > 2.21$, and $\alpha > 13$. Long-lived proteins make the oscillator harder to build, which is why Elowitz and Leibler destabilized their repressors with degradation tags.

5. Estimate the period of the repressilator of Elowitz and Leibler from Theorem 33.5.1, treating the six stages as having equal rates, and compare with the observed period of about $150\,\mathrm{min}$ when mRNA lives about $2\,\mathrm{min}$ and the tagged proteins about $10\,\mathrm{min}$.

   **Answer** Near the Hopf bifurcation, for $N$ equal stages with rate $\gamma$, the frequency is $\gamma\tan(\pi/N)$; for $N = 6$, $\tan(30°) = 0.577$ and the period is $2\pi/(0.577\gamma) = 10.9/\gamma$. The protein stages are rate limiting; with an effective $\gamma \approx 1/(6\,\mathrm{min})$ (between the two lifetimes) the estimate is about $65\,\mathrm{min}$; with only the three protein stages counted ($N = 3$, $\tan 60° = 1.73$, period $3.6/\gamma_p$) about $36\,\mathrm{min}$. Both are shorter than the observed $150\,\mathrm{min}$. Far from the bifurcation (α = 216 is far above threshold) the oscillation is strongly nonlinear and slower, since each repressor must fall by orders of magnitude before the next gene turns on; in addition, dilution by growth sets a floor on the effective decay rate of the proteins. The linear estimate gives the right order of magnitude but not the period, which must be computed from the nonlinear model (panel A of the figure gives a period of about $8.5$ mRNA lifetimes for β = 5).

6. In Eq. (33.5), how would the bistable range change if the permease were replaced by a constitutively expressed copy (so that the internal inducer no longer depends on $x$), and what experiment would test the role of the positive loop?

   **Answer** With $y = cT$ independent of $x$, the production term is a function of $T$ alone, there is a single fixed point $x^* = 1/(1 + \rho/(1 + c^2T^2))$, and the response is graded and history independent: no bistability. Experimentally, deleting *lacY* and supplying the inducer by a mechanism independent of LacY (high concentrations of IPTG enter by diffusion), or expressing LacY from a separate constitutive promoter, should abolish the all-or-none response and the maintenance effect; this is what is observed, and Ozbudak and colleagues confirmed it by measuring a graded response when the feedback was broken. Conversely, tuning the strength of the loop (by the repressor level or the leak δ) moves the boundaries of the bistable region as predicted.

7. A relaxation oscillator spends most of its cycle on the slow branches. Derive an approximate expression for its period in the limit of a very fast activator, for the model of the figure in 33.5.4, given that the repressor relaxes as $\dot R = R_{\max} - R$ when the activator is high ($R_{\max} \approx 120$) and as $\dot R = -R$ when it is low, and the folds of the activator nullcline are at $R_{\mathrm{low}} \approx 7$ and $R_{\mathrm{high}} \approx 83$.

   **Answer** On the high branch, $R$ rises from $R_{\mathrm{low}}$ to $R_{\mathrm{high}}$: $t_1 = \ln[(R_{\max} - R_{\mathrm{low}})/(R_{\max} - R_{\mathrm{high}})] = \ln(113/37) = 1.12$. On the low branch, $R$ decays from $R_{\mathrm{high}}$ to $R_{\mathrm{low}}$: $t_2 = \ln(83/7) = 2.47$. The period is about $3.6$ repressor lifetimes, plus the short jumps; the simulation gives about $4.5$, the difference being mainly the time the repressor needs after each jump to respond (the jump is not instantaneous at the finite speed ratio $30$, and $R$ overshoots the fold value, reaching about $97$). The estimate shows that the period depends logarithmically on the positions of the folds and linearly on the repressor lifetime, which is why such oscillators can be tuned.

8. The KaiABC oscillator runs for days in a test tube with a period of about $24\,\mathrm{h}$. Using the ATP consumption of KaiC (about $15$ ATP per monomer per day, about $20\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ each under the conditions of the experiment), estimate the free energy dissipated per hexamer per cycle and per hour, and comment on why a slow clock need not be energetically expensive.

   **Answer** Per hexamer and cycle: $6 \times 15 = 90$ ATP, about $1800\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T \approx 7\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J}$ (with $k_{\mathrm{B}}T = 4.1\times10^{-21}\,\mathrm{J}$), or about $75\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ per hour, an extremely small power per molecule (of order $10^{-22}\,\mathrm{W}$). The clock is slow because KaiC's ATPase is slow, and the period is set by the chemistry of phosphorylation rather than by a large energy throughput. The thermodynamic uncertainty relations of [7.6.38](/7.6.38) bound the precision of an oscillator by the dissipation per cycle: a few thousand $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ per cycle allow at best tens to hundreds of coherent cycles for an individual hexamer, and the population synchronizes many hexamers, which averages their fluctuations.

9. (Open question) A colleague proposes that a mutual-repression circuit controls a binary fate decision in a stem cell population. Design an experiment, using the theory of 33.2–33.3, that distinguishes a bistable switch from a graded, monostable response that merely looks bimodal because of noise.

   **Answer** Key signatures of bistability are hysteresis and memory. (1) Prepare cells in each state (sort by a reporter of each factor, or induce transiently), then expose both populations to the same intermediate level of the input signal: a bistable switch gives different outcomes depending on the starting state over a range of inputs, a monostable system converges to the same distribution. (2) Apply a transient pulse and remove it: in a bistable system a fraction of cells remains switched for many generations; in a monostable one all relax back with the protein lifetime. (3) Break the loop (express one factor constitutively, or remove the binding site through which it represses the other) and check that bimodality and memory disappear. (4) Measure the relaxation time and the variance as the input approaches a candidate threshold: both should increase near a saddle-node point (33.2.2). Noise-induced bimodality without bistability (for example slow promoter switching, [7.6.32](/7.6.32#sec-32.3.2)) shows neither hysteresis nor long-term memory.
