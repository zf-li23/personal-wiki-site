A biochemical network works in a cell whose components vary. The number of copies of a protein differs from cell to cell by tens of percent ([7.2.22](/7.2.22#sec-22.5)), temperature changes all rates, mutations change binding constants, and the inputs are noisy. Yet many functions are performed with precision: bacteria adapt exactly to attractants, cells divide in the right order, embryos form the same pattern of segments. One explanation would be that every parameter is finely tuned; the other is that the networks are built so that their function does not depend on most parameters. The second is the notion of robustness, and the search for the network designs that make a function robust is one of the main programs of quantitative biology. A related program asks whether the parameters that do matter are set at values that are optimal by some measure, growth, information, energy, and whether the optimum can be predicted from physics.

This chapter collects the concepts and the case studies of both programs, building on systems treated in earlier chapters: the chemotaxis network, whose robust adaptation and its integral-feedback mechanism were derived in [7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.6), together with the topology search of Ma and colleagues ([7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.7)); the Boolean model of the yeast cell cycle ([7.6.34](/7.6.34#sec-34.2.3)); the costs of precision ([7.6.38](/7.6.38)) and the optimal allocation of the bacterial proteome ([7.6.39](/7.6.39)). Here the emphasis is on what the examples have in common: how robustness is defined and measured, how it constrains network architecture, and how to test claims of optimality. The general theory of network design principles, network motifs, **modularity** and the propagation of noise belongs to systems biology ([7.9](/7.9)); the evolution of robustness and its relation to evolvability is taken up from the side of sequences in [7.6.41](/7.6.41).

## 40.1. Measuring Robustness

### 40.1.1. Robust and Fine-Tuned Properties

**Definition 40.1.1 (Robustness)** A property of a biological system is **robust** to a class of perturbations (changes of parameters, of initial conditions, internal noise, environmental changes, mutations) if it is maintained, within a stated tolerance, for all perturbations of the class up to a stated size. A property that is maintained only in a small region of parameter space is *fine-tuned*.

The definition requires two specifications, a property and a class of perturbations: robustness is always robustness of something to something. Barkai and Leibler (1997) made the distinction concrete for chemotaxis. In their model the precision of adaptation, the return of the tumbling frequency to its prestimulus value, is robust to the concentrations of all the proteins of the pathway, whereas the adapted tumbling frequency itself and the adaptation time vary with them; Alon, Surette, Barkai and Leibler (1999) varied the expression of CheR and CheB over a fiftyfold range and confirmed exactly this: precision stayed, the other two properties changed. A robust property is a candidate for a function that has been selected, a fine-tuned one is either unimportant or held in place by additional control.

**Definition 40.1.2 (Logarithmic Sensitivity)** The *logarithmic sensitivity* of a steady-state output $y$ to a parameter $p$ is

$$
S_p^y = \frac{\partial\ln y}{\partial\ln p} = \frac{p}{y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial p},
$$
the relative change of the output per relative change of the parameter. $|S| \ll 1$ means robustness to small changes of $p$; $S = 1$ means proportionality; $|S| > 1$ means amplification.

Logarithmic sensitivities are the **flux control coefficients** and elasticities of **metabolic control analysis** in another context ([7.1.17](/7.1.17)), and they are dimensionless, so that sensitivities to different parameters can be compared. They describe small perturbations; robustness to large ones, such as tenfold changes of a protein level, must be tested by exploring parameter space, as in 40.2.3.

### 40.1.2. Feedback Reduces Sensitivity

The simplest mechanism of robustness is negative feedback. Consider a protein that represses its own synthesis, made at maximal rate $\beta$ and removed at rate $\gamma$ ([7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.2.3)):

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\beta}{1 + (x/K)^n} - \gamma x.
$$

**Formula 40.1.1 (Sensitivity under Negative Autoregulation)** The steady state $x^*$ has sensitivity to the production rate

$$
S_\beta^{x^*} = \frac{1}{1 + n\,u/(1 + u)}, \qquad u = (x^*/K)^n, \tag{40.1}
$$
which falls from $1$ without regulation ($n = 0$ or $x^* \ll K$) to $1/(1 + n)$ when the repression is strong ($x^* \gg K$).

Taking logarithms of the steady-state condition, $\ln\beta - \ln(1 + u) = \ln\gamma + \ln x^*$, and differentiating with respect to $\ln\beta$ gives $1 - \frac{nu}{1 + u}S = S$, which is Eq. (40.1). The result is the biological form of a principle of control engineering: a feedback loop with loop gain $L$ reduces the sensitivity of its output to disturbances inside the loop by the factor $1/(1 + L)$, here $L = nu/(1 + u)$. Measurements in *E. coli* showed that negatively autoregulated genes have narrower distributions of expression than unregulated ones (Becskei and Serrano 2000) and respond faster ([7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.2.3)). Feedback buys robustness to what happens inside the loop, not to the set point itself: the sensitivity of $x^*$ to $K$ is close to one when the repression is strong.

### 40.1.3. Sloppy Parameter Spectra

The robustness of a model's behaviour to its parameters can be examined all at once by the curvature of a cost function. Let a model predict observables $y_k(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, $k = 1, \dots, K$, from parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, and let $J_{kj} = \partial y_k/\partial\ln\theta_j$. Near a parameter set, the change of the prediction under a small change $\delta\ln\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is governed by the matrix $J^{\mathsf T}J$, the **Fisher information matrix** of the model for Gaussian errors ([3.2](/3.2)): its eigenvectors are the combinations of parameters that change the output independently, and its eigenvalues measure how strongly. Gutenkunst, Waterfall, Casey, Brown, Myers and Sethna (2007) computed these spectra for seventeen published models of biochemical networks and found that in every case the eigenvalues were spread roughly evenly over many decades on a logarithmic scale: the models are *sloppy*. A few "stiff" combinations of parameters determine the behaviour; most combinations can be changed by large factors with no visible effect.

Sloppiness has two consequences. First, it explains why the parameters of network models are rarely identifiable from data, even from many data: fitting determines the stiff combinations, and individual rate constants remain uncertain by orders of magnitude, so that a good fit is no evidence that the fitted constants are correct. Second, it suggests that robustness is in part a generic property of models with many parameters and few outputs, and not necessarily a sign of design: the predictions of a sloppy model can be accurate even when its parameters are not, and collective behaviour is insensitive to most microscopic details, as in the renormalization-group picture of physical systems. The examples below go beyond this generic robustness by showing that particular architectures make particular functions robust and that others do not.

### 40.1.4. Robust Fluxes and Dominance

In a metabolic pathway, the flux is controlled jointly by all enzymes, and the flux control coefficients sum to one (the summation theorem, [Theorem 17.4.1](/7.1.17#thm-17.4.1) of 7.1.17). With many enzymes, each has on average a control of order $1/n$, and the flux is robust to large changes of the activity of any one of them. Kacser and Burns (1981) used this to explain why most loss-of-function alleles are recessive: a **heterozygote** with half the activity of one enzyme has nearly the wild-type flux ([7.4.3](/7.4.3#sec-3.2.1)). The robustness here is a consequence of the hyperbolic dependence of flux on enzyme amount, not of selection for robustness: it is an example of the generic robustness of the previous section, and the exceptions, haploinsufficient genes, are those with large control coefficients.

## 40.2. Three Case Studies

### 40.2.1. Exact Adaptation in Chemotaxis

The chemotaxis network of *E. coli* adapts exactly to steps of attractant over five orders of magnitude of concentration, and the precision of adaptation is robust to the levels of its proteins. Barkai and Leibler showed that this follows from two features of the methylation reactions: CheR methylates at a saturated rate, independent of the activity, and CheB demethylates only active receptors. Then the methylation level integrates the deviation of the activity from a fixed set point, $\dot m = V_R - V_B a$ in the simplest form, and the steady state must satisfy $a^* = V_R/V_B$ whatever the ligand concentration (Theorem 35.6.1 of [7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.6.1)). This is **integral feedback** (Definition 35.6.2): Yi, Huang, Simon and Doyle (2000) pointed out that, by the internal model principle of control theory, **integral feedback** is not just sufficient but in a precise sense necessary for robust **perfect adaptation** to step inputs in a linear system, which explains why the same structure has been found in other adapting systems, from calcium homeostasis to osmoregulation in yeast. The set point $a^*$ itself depends on the ratio of the two enzymes' maximal rates and is not robust; the bacterium handles its variability with an ultrasensitive but adaptive motor ([7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.5.3)).

The two lessons are general. A robust property is generally a ratio or a set point fixed by a structural feature of the network (saturation, a conservation law, a balance between two fluxes), not by the value of any rate constant. And the robustness is bought: the integral controller needs a cycle driven by S-adenosylmethionine, and the precision improves exponentially with the free energy dissipated per cycle ([7.6.38](/7.6.38#sec-38.5.3)).

### 40.2.2. The Global Attractor of the Yeast Cell Cycle

Li, Long, Lu, Ouyang and Tang (2004) represented the core regulatory network of the budding-yeast cell cycle by eleven Boolean nodes ([7.6.34](/7.6.34#sec-34.2.3)). Of the $2^{11} = 2048$ possible initial states, $1764$, or $86\,\%$, flow to the stationary G1 state, and the trajectory from the excited G1 state follows the biological sequence of phases, attracting most other trajectories on its way. The network is robust in two senses: to initial conditions (a large **basin of attraction** for the resting state) and to the timing of each step (the sequence is a stable pathway, not a precisely timed one). Random networks with the same numbers of nodes and of positive and negative links rarely have a basin as large or a pathway as attracting, and most single changes of links in the real network preserve these properties. The robustness is therefore a property of the specific wiring, and it is plausibly a target of selection: the cell cycle must complete in the right order despite large fluctuations in the levels and activities of its regulators. Similar conclusions were reached for the cell cycles of fission yeast and *Xenopus* embryos.

### 40.2.3. Parameter Robustness of the Segment-Polarity Network

The **segment-polarity genes** of *Drosophila* maintain the boundaries between parasegments after the transient pair-rule pattern has decayed: a stripe of cells expressing *engrailed* (and secreting Hedgehog) lies next to a stripe expressing *wingless*, and the two stripes maintain each other through signals between neighbouring cells ([7.5](/7.5)). Von Dassow, Meir, Munro and Odell (2000) wrote a model of the known interactions among *engrailed*, *wingless*, *hedgehog*, *patched*, *cubitus interruptus* and their proteins in a row of cells, with 48 parameters whose values were mostly unknown, and asked how many random parameter sets would maintain the pattern.

**Example 40.2.1 (A Network That Works for Almost Any Parameters)** With the interactions that were well established, the model failed for essentially every parameter set tried. After adding two interactions that had been suggested by experiments but not established, they sampled $240\,000$ random parameter sets, each parameter drawn from a range of up to three decades. About $1$ in $200$ sets maintained a **prepattern** of *engrailed* and *wingless* stripes, and about $1$ in $5000$ sharpened and maintained a fuzzier, weaker prepattern; for a typical successful set, each parameter could be changed individually by a factor of $10$ to $100$ without loss of function. For a 48-dimensional space, these fractions are enormous: if each parameter had to lie within a factor of two of a correct value, a random set would succeed with a probability of order $10^{-30}$. The network topology, not the parameter values, performs the function, and the authors called the network a robust developmental module.

Ingolia (2004) analysed the successful parameter sets and found that their common feature was **bistability** of individual cells: the autoregulatory loops make each cell a switch with two stable states, *wingless* on or off and *engrailed* on or off, and the intercellular signals only choose between them. A network whose cells are switches remembers a pattern for a wide range of parameters, because bistability itself is robust (it requires only that the feedback be strong enough), whereas a network in which the pattern is maintained by a balance of graded signals is fine-tuned. The figure shows the same phenomenon in a two-gene caricature.

The robustness has a biological interpretation. Insensitivity to parameters makes the module tolerant of mutations that change rates, which may explain why the segment-polarity network is conserved across insects whose upstream segmentation mechanisms differ greatly; insensitivity to the details of the prepattern tolerates the noise of the transient pair-rule stripes. Other developmental systems show comparable robustness to **morphogen** dosage through mechanisms such as **self-enhanced degradation** and expansion–repression ([7.5.11](/7.5.11#sec-11.6)).

## 40.3. Topology and Function

### 40.3.1. Searching the Space of Networks

If robustness is a property of architecture, one can ask which architectures can perform a given function robustly. The method of Ma, Trusina, El-Samad, Lim and Tang (2009) is to enumerate coarse-grained networks of a few nodes, to sample many parameter sets for each, and to score each topology by its *Q value*, the number of parameter sets (out of, say, $10^4$) that perform the function. For adaptation, all $16\,038$ three-node enzyme networks were scored; the robust ones contain one of two cores, the negative feedback loop with a buffering node and the incoherent feed-forward loop with a proportioner node, each requiring a specific regime of its reactions ([7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.7.1), where the search and the mechanisms are described). When the space is too large to enumerate, three strategies are used: enumerating a subspace with part of the network fixed; random sampling of topologies and parameters, which works because robust topologies are clustered, surrounded by similar topologies that perform the same function; and evolutionary algorithms that mutate, recombine and select networks by their score.

The same approach has been applied to other functions: cell polarization (Chau, Walter, Gerardin, Tang and Lim 2012 found a small set of core topologies and built one in yeast), biochemical oscillation, and adaptation in the presence of input noise. The result is in each case a short list of core motifs that can be combined and decorated, a "periodic table" of designs for a function. Its significance is twofold. It explains why unrelated organisms, with different molecules and evolutionary histories, converge on the same architecture for the same task; and it gives synthetic biology a list of designs to implement.

### 40.3.2. Robustness as Designability

The Q value is a count of parameter sets compatible with a function, and the robust topologies are those with many. The concept is the same as the **designability** of protein structures (Li, Helling, Tang and Wingreen 1996; [7.6.16](/7.6.16)), where the designable folds are those encoded by many sequences: in both cases, a large preimage in the space of underlying variables makes a structure or a function both common in nature and stable against mutations, because most mutations move the system within the preimage. A topology with a high Q value is also more likely to be found by evolution, which samples parameter space through mutation. This connects robustness to evolvability: networks of parameter sets (or of sequences, [7.6.41](/7.6.41)) that share a function allow populations to drift among them while keeping the function, and to reach new functions from their edges (Wagner 2005).

### 40.3.3. Modularity

**Modularity** is the organization of a network into subnetworks that are densely connected internally and sparsely connected to each other, performing separable functions. In protein-interaction networks of yeast, groups of proteins involved in DNA repair, transport or ribosome biogenesis form such communities; in gene-regulatory networks, coexpressed modules share functions; in bacterial genomes, genes of a pathway cluster in operons; in proteins, domains are reusable modules. Modularity is quantified at the level of vertices by the **clustering coefficient** ([7.6.34](/7.6.34#sec-34.1.3)), the fraction of a vertex's neighbours that are connected to each other, and at the level of a partition by the modularity score $Q_{\mathrm{mod}} = \sum_s[e_{ss} - (\sum_te_{st})^2]$ (Newman and Girvan 2004), where $e_{st}$ is the fraction of edges between modules $s$ and $t$, which compares the fraction of edges within modules with the value expected for random wiring with the same degrees.

Why biological networks are modular is debated. Modularity confines the effect of a failure to one module, which is a form of robustness; it may evolve when the goals of selection change in a modular way, as Kashtan and Alon (2005) found in simulated evolution of circuits under goals that alternate between related tasks; and it may be a by-product of evolution by duplication and divergence, which copies existing modules. Hartwell, Hopfield, Leibler and Murray (1999) proposed that functional modules, rather than single molecules, are the natural units for understanding cellular function; the general theory of modules and motifs is developed in systems biology ([7.9](/7.9)).

## 40.4. Optimality

### 40.4.1. Optimality Principles

Robustness asks how a function survives the variation of parameters; optimality asks whether the parameters are set at the best values. Physicists like optimality principles, because a variational principle makes predictions without a mechanism, and biology offers many candidates. Some have been tested quantitatively:

| Quantity optimized | System | Prediction tested | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| growth rate | bacterial proteome allocation; lac expression | the growth laws; evolution to the cost–benefit optimum | [7.6.39](/7.6.39) |
| information transmitted | gap genes of *Drosophila*; signaling pathways | positional information near the physical limit (Dubuis, Tkačik, Wieschaus, Gregor and Bialek 2013); channel capacities of 1–2 bits | [7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.3) |
| power and material cost of flow | branching of blood vessels and airways | **Murray's law** $r_0^3 = r_1^3 + r_2^3$ | [7.5.29](/7.5.29) |
| search time | transcription factors on DNA | the optimal fraction of time spent sliding | [7.6.17](/7.6.17#sec-17.8.2) |
| detection of single photons | rod photoreceptors | quantum efficiency and dark noise near limits | [7.6.18](/7.6.18) |
| accuracy against cost | proofreading, adaptation | error–energy relations | [7.6.38](/7.6.38) |

A claim of optimality has three parts, each of which must be stated: what is optimized (a measure of fitness or a proxy for it), over what set of possible designs (the constraints), and with what evidence (a quantitative prediction that could have failed). **Murray's law** illustrates all three. Its quantity is the power needed to maintain a vessel, the viscous dissipation of the flow plus the metabolic cost of the blood volume; its constraint is Poiseuille flow in a cylindrical tube of radius $r$; and its prediction, that the flow is proportional to $r^3$ so that the cubes of the radii are conserved at each branching, agrees with measurements in many vascular beds above the capillaries ([Theorem 29.5.1](/7.5.29#thm-29.5.1) of 7.5.29).

### 40.4.2. Several Tasks: Pareto Fronts

Organisms do several things at once, and a design that is best for one task is rarely best for another: a beak good for cracking seeds is poor for catching insects, a bacterium that grows fast survives starvation poorly. When fitness depends on performance in several tasks, the relevant optima are those that cannot be improved in one task without loss in another.

**Definition 40.4.1 (Pareto Optimality)** A phenotype $\boldsymbol{x}$ is *Pareto optimal* with respect to performance functions $P_1, \dots, P_k$ if no other phenotype $\boldsymbol{y}$ satisfies $P_i(\boldsymbol{y}) \ge P_i(\boldsymbol{x})$ for all $i$ with strict inequality for at least one. The set of Pareto-optimal phenotypes is the Pareto front.

**Theorem 40.4.1 (Archetypes and Polytopes)** If each performance $P_i$ is a decreasing function of a distance $\|\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{x}_i\|$ from an archetype $\boldsymbol{x}_i$, the same norm for all tasks (Euclidean, or any norm induced by an inner product, after a common linear change of coordinates), then the Pareto front is the convex hull of the archetypes: a segment for two tasks, a triangle for three, a tetrahedron for four (Shoval, Sheftel, Shinar and colleagues 2012).

**Proof** Let $\boldsymbol{x}$ lie outside the convex hull $H$ of the archetypes, and let $\boldsymbol{y}$ be its orthogonal projection onto $H$ in the inner product of the norm. For every point $\boldsymbol{x}_i \in H$, the angle at $\boldsymbol{y}$ between $\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{y}$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_i - \boldsymbol{y}$ is at least $90^\circ$ (the characterization of projections onto convex sets), so $\|\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{x}_i\|^2 \ge \|\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{x}_i\|^2 + \|\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{y}\|^2 > \|\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{x}_i\|^2$. Every archetype is closer to $\boldsymbol{y}$, every performance is higher there, and $\boldsymbol{x}$ is dominated. Conversely, a point $\boldsymbol{y}$ of $H$ is not dominated: any move away from $\boldsymbol{y}$ increases the distance to at least one archetype $\boldsymbol{x}_i$ with positive weight in the representation of $\boldsymbol{y}$ as a convex combination, because the distances cannot all decrease when $\boldsymbol{y}$ is a weighted mean of the $\boldsymbol{x}_i$ (the gradient of $\sum_iw_i\|\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{x}_i\|^2$ vanishes there). $\square$

The theorem turns optimality into a geometric prediction that can be tested without knowing the performance functions: if a set of related phenotypes (species, cell types, gene-expression states of tumors) is shaped by trade-offs among $k$ tasks, the data should fill a polytope with $k$ vertices in trait space, and the vertices, the archetypes, should be specialists for identifiable tasks. Shoval and colleagues found triangles in the beak shapes of Darwin's finches, in the morphology of bats and ants, and in the expression profiles of *E. coli* genes across conditions; later work found polytopes in the gene expression of tumors and of tissues. The prediction is falsifiable: phenotypes shaped by a single task or by drift should fill a cloud, not a polytope, and a statistical test against randomized data is part of the method.

### 40.4.3. The Limits of Optimality Arguments

Optimality arguments have a mixed reputation in biology. Gould and Lewontin (1979) criticized the "adaptationist programme" for telling plausible stories about why every trait is optimal without testing them, and for neglecting the constraints of development, history and physics; Jacob (1977) described evolution as a tinkerer that modifies what exists rather than an engineer who designs from scratch. Both points are correct and both are compatible with the quantitative program of this chapter: a quantitative optimality hypothesis states its objective and constraints, and the tests cited above are tests because their predictions could have failed. The interesting results are often the failures. *E. coli* expresses many proteins far from the level that would maximize growth in the laboratory (for example, it makes motility proteins in rich media); these deviations reveal objectives other than growth, such as preparing for future environments, and they are as informative as the agreements.

A second limit is that robustness and optimality trade against each other and against fragility. Carlson and Doyle (2002) described complex engineered and biological systems as "robust yet fragile": designs optimized to be robust to common perturbations become sensitive to rare ones, as feedback that suppresses disturbances at some frequencies amplifies them at others (a conservation law of control theory, the Bode sensitivity integral), and a network tolerant of random failures can be vulnerable to targeted attacks on its hubs ([7.6.34](/7.6.34#sec-34.1.4)). Robustness is never total; the question is what a system is robust to and what it pays for it, in proteins, in energy, in fragility elsewhere.

## 40.5. Robustness and Evolution

Robust networks hide genetic variation. Mutations that change parameters within the tolerated range have no phenotypic effect and accumulate neutrally, and they can be revealed when the robustness is compromised. Waddington called the buffering of development against genetic and environmental variation **canalization** ([7.5](/7.5)). Rutherford and Lindquist (1998) found that reducing the activity of the chaperone Hsp90 in *Drosophila* revealed a wide range of morphological abnormalities, different in different genetic backgrounds, which could then be selected and became independent of the reduction in Hsp90: a buffering protein acts as a capacitor that stores cryptic variation and releases it under stress. The relation between robustness and evolvability is therefore not a simple opposition. A robust system changes less with each mutation, but it can explore more of genotype space while keeping its function, and the variation it accumulates can become useful when conditions change. The evolutionary dynamics of sequences on such neutral networks, and the biophysical **fitness landscapes** on which they live, are taken up in [7.6.41](/7.6.41).

## Summary

- Robustness is the maintenance of a stated property under a stated class of perturbations (Definition 40.1.1); the logarithmic sensitivity $S = \partial\ln y/\partial\ln p$ (Definition 40.1.2) measures it for small changes.
- Mechanisms and generic sources: negative feedback reduces sensitivity by $1/(1 + L)$, for autoregulation $S = 1/(1 + n)$ at strong repression (Formula 40.1.1); mechanistic models are sloppy, with stiff and sloppy parameter combinations spread over many decades; fluxes of long pathways are insensitive to single enzymes (summation theorem, dominance).
- Case studies: exact adaptation in chemotaxis is robust because methylation is an integral controller (Theorem 35.6.1), with a set point that is not robust; the yeast cell cycle has a global **attractor** and an attracting pathway (86 % of states); the segment-polarity network works for 1 in 200 random parameter sets of 48 dimensions because its cells are bistable switches (Example 40.2.1).
- Topology and function: enumerating networks and scoring them by the number of working parameter sets (Q value) reveals a few core designs for each function; robust topologies are designable and clustered; modularity is measured by **clustering coefficients** and modularity scores, and has several proposed origins.
- Optimality: a claim needs an objective, constraints and a falsifiable prediction (table in 40.4.1, Murray's law); with several tasks, the optima form a Pareto front, the convex hull of archetypes (Definition 40.4.1, Theorem 40.4.1); robustness, optimality and fragility trade against each other.
- Robustness hides variation (**canalization**, Hsp90 as a capacitor) and can promote evolvability.

Compare with an unregulated gene.

   **Answer** $u = 9$, $S = 1/(1 + 2\times9/10) = 1/2.8 = 0.36$. A relative change $\ln1.3 = 0.262$ in $\beta$ changes $\ln x^*$ by about $0.36\times0.262 = 0.094$, a $10\,\%$ increase (an exact solution of $1.3\beta/(1 + u) = \gamma x$ gives $x^*/K = 3.29$, $9.8\,\%$). An unregulated gene would increase by $30\,\%$. Negative autoregulation thus suppresses copy-number and transcription-rate variation about threefold.

2. In the **Barkai–Leibler model**, which properties of chemotaxis are robust to a tenfold overexpression of CheR, and which are not? Explain from the structure of the model.

   **Answer** Robust: the precision of adaptation, because the steady-state activity is fixed by $\dot m = 0$, which with saturated CheR and activity-dependent CheB gives $a^* = f(V_R/V_B)$ independent of the ligand concentration; the return to $a^*$ after any step holds for any $V_R$. Not robust: the adapted activity $a^*$ itself (it increases with $V_R/V_B$, so overexpressing CheR raises the tumbling bias), and the adaptation time (proportional to $1/V_R$ and $1/V_B$). This is what Alon and colleagues observed when they varied CheR expression.

3. Why does the fraction of random parameter sets that work in von Dassow's model (1 in 200) indicate robustness, given that the parameter space has 48 dimensions? Estimate the fraction expected if each parameter had to lie within a factor of two of its correct value, with parameters drawn log-uniformly over three decades.

   **Answer** For each parameter, the probability of falling within a factor of two (a window of $\log_{10}4 = 0.6$ decades, from half to twice) is $0.6/3 = 0.2$; for 48 independent parameters, $0.2^{48} \approx 3\times10^{-34}$. The observed fraction of $5\times10^{-3}$ is thirty orders of magnitude larger: most parameters are nearly irrelevant to the function, and the successful region of parameter space is a large, connected volume, not a point. This is robustness of the network topology.

4. Show that the steady state of the linear pathway in the dominance figure has flux control coefficients that sum to one, and compute the flux of a heterozygote for an enzyme with control coefficient $0.05$ in the wild type.

   **Answer** The flux is $J = \Delta/\sum_ir_i$ with $r_i \propto 1/e_i$, so $C_i = \partial\ln J/\partial\ln e_i = r_i/\sum_jr_j$, and $\sum_iC_i = 1$. Halving $e_i$ doubles $r_i$: $J'/J = \sum r/(\sum r + r_i) = 1/(1 + C_i) = 1/1.05 = 0.952$. The heterozygote has $95\,\%$ of the wild-type flux, which is why it looks wild type (the **null allele** is recessive).

5. Derive Murray's law. Assume Poiseuille flow, a power cost $Q\Delta p$ for viscous dissipation and a metabolic cost $b$ per unit volume of blood per unit time.

   **Answer** For a vessel of length $L$ and radius $r$, $\Delta p = 8\eta LQ/(\pi r^4)$ (**Hagen–Poiseuille law**), so the total cost is $W = 8\eta LQ^2/(\pi r^4) + b\pi r^2L$. Minimizing over $r$: $-32\eta LQ^2/(\pi r^5) + 2b\pi rL = 0$, $Q = \pi r^3\sqrt{b/(16\eta)}$: the optimal flow is proportional to $r^3$. At a branching, conservation of flow $Q_0 = Q_1 + Q_2$ then gives $r_0^3 = r_1^3 + r_2^3$. A consequence is that the wall shear stress, $4\eta Q/(\pi r^3)$, is the same in all optimal vessels, which suggests a mechanism: endothelial cells sense shear and remodel the vessel until it reaches the set value.

6. (Data analysis) You measure two morphological traits for 40 related species and find that they fall within a triangle. What would you do to test whether this reflects a Pareto front of three tasks, and what alternative explanations would you consider?

   **Answer** Test the triangularity statistically: compute the ratio of the area of the minimal enclosing triangle to that of the convex hull of the data, and compare it with the distribution obtained when the traits are shuffled independently among species (which preserves the marginal distributions but destroys correlations); a significantly better fit than in shuffled data supports the triangle. Then identify the species near each vertex and check whether they are specialists for distinct tasks (diets, habitats), and whether species near the middle are generalists. Alternatives: phylogenetic structure (related species are similar; use phylogenetically independent contrasts or tests that account for the tree), a geometric constraint of the measurement (traits that are fractions summing to a constant fill a simplex automatically), and **developmental constraints** that limit the reachable trait combinations without any trade-off in performance.

7. Explain why a sloppy model can make accurate predictions although its parameters are poorly determined. When would its predictions fail?

   **Answer** Data determine the stiff combinations of parameters, those along which the output changes most; if the new prediction depends on the same stiff combinations, it is constrained by the data even though individual parameters are not, because the parameter uncertainty lies mostly along sloppy directions that do not affect the output. Predictions fail when they depend on sloppy combinations of the fitted conditions, for example when an experiment changes one parameter that enters the sloppy directions in a new way (a knockout, a drug that affects one rate), or when the model is extrapolated to conditions that make previously irrelevant parameters important. Gutenkunst and colleagues showed that collective fitting followed by prediction with the whole ensemble of acceptable parameters gives honest error bars, whereas predictions from single best-fit values do not.

8. Give one argument why negative feedback is a robust design and one reason why it can make a system fragile.

   **Answer** Robust: the loop reduces the sensitivity of the output to parameters and disturbances inside the loop by $1/(1 + L)$, so large variations in expression or rates produce small changes in the regulated quantity. Fragile: with delays, strong negative feedback becomes unstable and oscillates (the Hopf bifurcation of delayed negative feedback, [7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.5.1)); by the Bode integral, suppression of disturbances at low frequencies amplifies them at others; and the regulated quantity becomes dependent on the sensor and the set point, whose failure the loop cannot correct.

9. (Open question) Propose a way to distinguish, for a given network, robustness that is a generic property of models with many parameters from robustness that has been selected.

   **Answer** Compare the real network with an ensemble of alternative networks of the same size, the same numbers of activating and inhibiting links and comparable kinetics: if the real network performs its function for a much larger fraction of parameter space than most alternatives (as in the yeast cell-cycle and segment-polarity examples), its robustness is specific, a candidate for selection. Also test robustness of the function against robustness of non-functional properties: generic sloppiness would make all outputs equally insensitive, whereas selected robustness should protect the functional output (adaptation precision) more than non-functional ones (adaptation time). Experimentally, compare the variation of the functional output and of other quantities among natural isolates or after random mutagenesis.

10. Explain the connection between the Q value of a network topology and the **designability** of a protein fold. What does this connection predict about the frequencies of topologies or folds in nature?

    **Answer** Both count the size of a preimage: the Q value counts the parameter sets that realize a function with a given topology, the designability of a fold counts the sequences whose lowest-energy structure is that fold. Large preimages are more likely to be hit by random variation and are more tolerant of mutation, because most mutations move within the preimage. The prediction is that highly designable folds and high-Q topologies should be overrepresented in nature, used for many functions, and evolutionarily stable; designable folds such as the TIM barrel and the Rossmann fold are indeed among the most common, and the adaptive cores found in the topology search recur in unrelated organisms.
