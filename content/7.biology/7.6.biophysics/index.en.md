Biophysics asks what living matter is and how it works, and it expects answers in the language of physics: structures with coordinates, forces and energies with units, probabilities and rates that can be measured and predicted. Its questions come from biology, how a protein recognizes its target, how a motor walks, how a nerve fires, how a bacterium finds food, how a cell keeps time and divides its resources, but its standard of an answer is a model with few parameters that can be calculated and tested. Following the Chinese textbook edited by Yigong Shi, Chao Tang and Hongwei Wang, the course divides the field into three directions by scale and by the kind of law sought. Molecular biophysics, to a large extent structural biology, determines the three-dimensional structures of macromolecules and studies the physics of the methods that determine them. Cell biophysics deals with physical behavior that carries dimensions at the scale of cells: forces in piconewtons, bending rigidities, viscosities, membrane potentials. Quantitative biology, closest to mathematical biology, looks for abstract, often dimensionless regularities that hold across systems, such as the bound that the number of counted molecules places on the precision of a sensor, or the proportion between the growth rate of a bacterium and its ribosome content. The three rest on one foundation, the statistical physics of the cell.

The chapters follow that order. The foundations come first: entropy and free energy, the **Boltzmann distribution** and the **chemical potential**, binding and rates, the **two-state system** that turns out to describe a dozen different switches, the **random walk** and diffusion, and electrostatics in water. Molecular biophysics begins with the architecture of macromolecules and then takes the methods of structural biology one by one as applied physics, calorimetry and hydrodynamics, scattering, X-ray crystallography, optical spectroscopy, single-molecule fluorescence, **nuclear magnetic resonance** and **cryo-electron microscopy**, together with structure prediction and simulation; the molecular physics built on these measurements follows, the folding of proteins and RNA, single-molecule mechanics and recognition, and the interaction of molecules with light and magnetic fields. Cell biophysics starts from the census of a cell and its numbers, and moves through DNA as a polymer, swimming at low Reynolds number, crowding, membranes and their channels, filaments, motors, phase separation, bioelectricity up to the **Hodgkin–Huxley model**, and the mechanics of whole cells. Quantitative biology then treats the cell as a noisy, driven system: the **master equation** and the noise of gene expression, circuits as dynamical systems and larger networks, the **Berg–Purcell limit** of sensing and bacterial chemotaxis, intracellular patterns and waves, **active matter**, the cost of functions that are impossible at equilibrium, the **bacterial growth laws**, **robustness** and design, and the physics of sequences and evolution. The course summary lays the models side by side and shows that a few mathematical structures, Boltzmann weights, master equations, first passage, ratchets, Fourier space, fluctuations used as measurements, feedback and the cost of dissipation, carry most of the subject.

The course builds directly on the other biology courses and goes one level deeper. Enzyme kinetics, membrane transport and chemiosmotic coupling come from [biochemistry](/7.1); transcription, translation and DNA topology from [molecular biology](/7.2); the cytoskeleton, motors, membranes, signaling and size control from [cell biology](/7.3); morphogen gradients, reaction–diffusion and tissue mechanics from [developmental biology](/7.5). The physics is that of [mechanics](/5.1), [thermal physics](/5.2), [electromagnetism](/5.3), [optics](/5.4) and [quantum physics](/5.5); the thermodynamics of solutions comes from [physical chemistry](/6.3) and the spectroscopic methods from [analytical chemistry](/6.4); the mathematics is that of [ordinary differential equations](/2.4), [partial differential equations](/2.5) and [probability](/3.1). In turn, the inference from single-molecule and single-cell data continues in [biostatistics](/7.7), structure prediction, alignment and energy models of binding in [bioinformatics](/7.8), the general theory of networks, motifs and oscillators in [systems biology](/7.9), and the neuron models built on the cable equation in [computational neuroscience](/7.16).

With this course biology becomes, for me, quantitative in earnest. What drew me to the life sciences was the question of what life essentially is, and biophysics is the discipline that tries most directly to answer it. Its present shape in China owes much to two institutions. At Tsinghua, after the department of biology was re-established, the subject grew from two disciplines, biophysics and biochemistry, and the biophysics branch developed mainly into structural biology; it trained a large group of structural biologists, Yigong Shi foremost among them, who now make up a large part of biophysics in China. The School of Life Sciences course on biophysics (30450233) accordingly centers on structural biology. At Peking University most biophysicists work in the Center for Quantitative Biology, founded on Tsung-Dao Lee's proposal after Chao Tang returned to China, whose research is in cell biophysics and quantitative biology. The introduction to biophysics offered by Tsinghua's department of physics (80430692), open to undergraduate and graduate students, follows this second tradition and takes *Physical Biology of the Cell* as its reference; physics students usually find it too easy and biology students too hard, which shows how far it leans toward physics. A third course, on mathematics, physics and chemistry in biology (00450282), supplies the foundations. Together the two universities have built a fairly complete framework of the field, and these pages try to hold both halves: the methods of structural biology at the depth of their physics, and the models of cells at the level of *Physical Biology of the Cell*.

My own view of where the essence of life lies has changed along the same axis. Before I had a philosophy of science to guide me I was a thorough reductionist, and believed that only structural biology led to the essence of life, because a structure seemed the final explanation of a function. I overlooked that life also resides in the coordination between molecules and in the patterns of complex networks: a structure explains how a channel opens, but not why a toggle switch remembers, why a chemotaxis network adapts exactly, or why a bacterium sets its ribosome content in proportion to its growth rate. Once I came to a more holistic view, my interest moved toward quantitative biology, toward the circuits, sensing limits and costs of nonequilibrium function of the last part of this course ([7.6.33](/7.6.33)–[7.6.41](/7.6.41)), where the questions are about what a whole system can and cannot do rather than about one molecule; and beyond it to the still more global view of omics. Biophysics has taught me a great deal, and it remains one of my favorite subjects.
