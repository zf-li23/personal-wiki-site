The **receptors** of [7.1.14](/7.1.14) act through **G proteins** and diffusible **second messengers**. Most growth factors, cytokines and **insulin** use another strategy: the receptor is itself an enzyme, a **protein kinase**, or recruits one, and the signal is carried by phosphorylation of tyrosine, serine and threonine **residues**, which switches the activity of **enzymes** and creates docking sites that assemble signaling complexes. This chapter follows that strategy from the insulin receptor through the MAP kinase and PI 3-kinase pathways to the modular **proteins** that read phosphorylation, and then turns to the receptors that bypass the membrane altogether, the nuclear receptors of steroid and thyroid **hormones**. Signaling outside the animal kingdom uses related and unrelated parts: bacteria transduce signals by histidine–aspartate phosphoryl transfer, and plants combine receptor kinases, histidine kinases inherited from bacteria and hormone-triggered destruction of **repressors**. The last part shows signaling as the control of cell division and death: cyclin-dependent kinases drive the cell cycle, oncogenes and tumor suppressors are mutant signaling **genes**, and apoptosis is a protease cascade. The kinases, phosphatases, **zero-order ultrasensitivity** and **regulatory cascades** of [7.1.9](/7.1.9#sec-9.6) are the elements throughout; the transcriptional mechanisms behind the nuclear responses are treated in molecular biology ([7.2.14](/7.2.14)), the cell biology of the cycle and of apoptosis in [7.3](/7.3), plant hormone physiology in [7.13](/7.13), and the dynamics of signaling networks in [7.9](/7.9).

## 15.1. Receptor Tyrosine Kinases

### 15.1.1. The Insulin Receptor

**Definition 15.1.1 (Receptor Tyrosine Kinase)** A **receptor tyrosine kinase** (RTK) is a plasma membrane receptor with an extracellular ligand-binding domain, a single transmembrane helix and a cytoplasmic protein tyrosine kinase domain. Ligand binding brings two kinase domains together so that each phosphorylates Tyr residues of the other (trans-autophosphorylation), which activates the kinase and creates phosphotyrosine docking sites for downstream proteins.

The human **genome** encodes 58 RTKs in about 20 families, among them the receptors for insulin and insulin-like growth factor, epidermal growth factor (EGFR and its relatives ErbB2–4), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGFR), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGFR), fibroblast growth factors (FGFR) and nerve growth factor (TrkA). Their extracellular **domains** differ with their **ligands**, assembled from immunoglobulin-like, Cys-rich and Leu-rich modules; the cytoplasmic kinase domain is conserved. Most RTKs are monomers that dimerize when the ligand binds.

**Definition 15.1.2 (Insulin Receptor)** The **insulin receptor** (INSR) is a preformed $(\alpha\beta)_2$ dimer of the RTK family: the two extracellular α subunits, linked by disulfide bonds to each other and to the β subunits, bind insulin, and each transmembrane β subunit carries a tyrosine kinase domain.

In the inactive kinase an activation loop lies across the **active site** and is held there by a hydrogen bond between Tyr1162 and Asp1132. Binding of insulin between the α **subunits** moves the two kinase domains together; each β subunit then phosphorylates three Tyr residues (Tyr1158, Tyr1162 and Tyr1163) in the activation loop of the other. The three negatively charged phosphotyrosines move the loop about 3 nm away from the active site, which becomes accessible to ATP and protein **substrates**. Displacement of an activation loop or of an autoinhibitory segment, here by phosphorylation, in **PKA** by **cAMP** ([7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.2.2)), is a common theme of kinase regulation. Among the first substrates of the active receptor are the insulin receptor substrates (IRS1, IRS2), large adaptor proteins with an N-terminal PH domain that binds membrane phosphoinositides, a PTB domain that binds a phosphotyrosine of the receptor, and many Tyr residues that become phosphorylated and serve as docking sites.

### 15.1.2. Phosphotyrosine Docking: SH2 and SH3 Domains

**Definition 15.1.3 (SH2 Domain)** An **SH2 domain** (Src homology 2) is a protein **domain** of about 100 residues that binds a phosphotyrosine and the three or so residues on its C-terminal side in a partner protein: the phosphotyrosine sits in a deep pocket, where its phosphate is bound by two Arg residues, and the residues at positions +1 to +3 fit a specificity pocket that differs between SH2 domains.

Differences in the specificity pocket divide the more than 100 human SH2 domains into subclasses: the SH2 domain of Src prefers pTyr–Glu–Glu–Ile, that of Grb2 pTyr–X–Asn, and those of PLCγ and the phosphatase SHP2 bind longer hydrophobic stretches. The distinction between a **domain**, an independently folded structural unit ([7.1.5](/7.1.5#sec-5.4.2)), and a **motif**, here in the sense of a short linear sequence that is recognized (a phosphorylation site, a **consensus sequence**), is important in signaling: domains are the readers, short **motifs** the sites that are read. The SH3 domain (about 60 residues) is a second widespread module; it binds Pro-rich motifs of the type Pro–X–X–Pro without any phosphorylation.

**Definition 15.1.4 (Adaptor Protein)** An **adaptor protein** is a signaling protein without catalytic activity that links two or more other proteins through its binding domains.

The adaptor Grb2 has one SH2 domain flanked by two SH3 domains. Its SH2 domain binds a phosphotyrosine of IRS1 (or of an activated RTK), and its SH3 domains hold Sos (son of sevenless), a **guanine nucleotide-exchange factor**, which is thereby brought to the plasma membrane next to its substrate, Ras.

### 15.1.3. Ras and the MAP Kinase Cascade

**Definition 15.1.5 (Small G Protein)** A **small G protein** (small GTPase) is a monomeric GTP-binding protein of about 20–25 kDa that, like the α subunit of heterotrimeric G proteins, is active with GTP and inactive with GDP, is switched on by a GEF and off by its own GTPase, accelerated by a GAP ([7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.2.1)). The Ras family regulates growth and differentiation; other families (Rho, Rab, Arf, Ran) regulate the cytoskeleton, vesicle traffic and nuclear transport.

Ras is anchored to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane by a farnesyl group ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.1.6)). Its structure is the prototype of all G proteins: the γ-phosphate of GTP is held by a Lys of the phosphate-binding P loop (Gly-rich, residues 10–17), by Thr35 in the region switch I and by Gly60 in switch II; these two hydrogen bonds act like springs that hold the switches in the conformation recognized by effectors, and hydrolysis of GTP releases them. Ala146 makes a hydrogen bond to the guanine and gives Ras its specificity for GTP over ATP. The GTPase of Ras alone is very slow; a **GAP** inserts an Arg residue (the "arginine finger") into the active site and accelerates hydrolysis about $10^5$-fold.

Ras·GTP binds and activates the protein kinase Raf, the first of three kinases that form a cascade.

**Definition 15.1.6 (MAP Kinase Cascade)** A **MAP kinase cascade** is a series of three protein kinases in which a MAP kinase kinase kinase (MAPKKK, such as Raf) phosphorylates and activates a MAP kinase kinase (MAPKK, such as MEK), a dual-specificity kinase that phosphorylates the MAP kinase (MAPK, such as ERK) on both a Thr and a Tyr residue of the motif Thr–X–Tyr in its activation loop; the doubly phosphorylated MAPK phosphorylates Ser and Thr residues of cytoplasmic and nuclear target proteins.

ERK (extracellular signal-regulated kinase) enters the nucleus and phosphorylates **transcription** factors such as Elk1, which with the serum response factor (SRF) activates immediate early genes such as *FOS*, starting a program of gene expression that leads to growth and division; insulin thereby acts as a growth factor. The name "MAP kinase" was first given to an insulin-stimulated kinase that phosphorylated microtubule-associated protein 2; it now stands for mitogen-activated protein kinase (a mitogen is a signal that induces mitosis). The parallel cascades ending in JNK and p38 respond to stress and inflammatory cytokines, and the yeast mating pathway, with the MAPK Fus3, is the classic genetic model. The three kinases are often held together on a scaffold (15.3.3), and ERK feeds back negatively by phosphorylating Sos and Raf; dual-specificity phosphatases (DUSPs), whose genes ERK induces, inactivate it.

### 15.1.4. Ultrasensitivity of the MAP Kinase Cascade

Why three kinases in a row, if one could phosphorylate the transcription factors directly? A cascade amplifies (14.2.3), offers several points for integration and feedback, and shapes the dose–response relation. Huang and Ferrell (1996) modeled the cascade with Michaelis–Menten kinetics for every kinase and phosphatase, as in the modification cycles of [7.1.9](/7.1.9#sec-9.6.1).

**Formula 15.1.1 (Steady State of a Double-Phosphorylation Cycle)** Let a MAPK (total 1, fractions $m_0$, $m_1$, $m_2$ with zero, one and two phosphates) be phosphorylated distributively by an upstream kinase and dephosphorylated by a phosphatase, both with Michaelis constant $K$ (in units of the total MAPK) and with both forms competing for each enzyme. With $u$ the ratio of the maximal rates of kinase and phosphatase, the steady state satisfies $m_1/m_0 = m_2/m_1 = r$, where

$$
r = u\,\frac{1 + (m_1 + m_2)/K}{1 + (m_0 + m_1)/K}, \qquad m_2 = \frac{r^2}{1 + r + r^2}.
$$

For $K \gg 1$ the enzymes are not saturated, $r = u$, and $m_2 = u^2/(1 + u + u^2)$ is already sigmoid: two independent activating steps behave like a dimer that needs two ligands. Saturation and competition between the singly and doubly phosphorylated forms ($K \lesssim 1$) make the curve steeper still, by the zero-order mechanism of [7.1.9](/7.1.9#sec-9.6.1). Stacking such cycles compounds the steepness: each tier sees an input that is already a steep function of the stimulus. In the model, the apparent **Hill coefficient** ([7.1.7](/7.1.7#sec-7.3.4)) rises from about 1 at the MAPKKK to about 1.7 at the MAPKK and 4.9 at the MAPK, and Ferrell measured a value of about 5 for MAPK activation in *Xenopus* oocyte extracts. A steep, switch-like response turns graded hormone concentrations into decisions, and with positive feedback, which the oocyte has, the switch becomes bistable and irreversible (oocyte maturation is all-or-none); such network properties are taken up in [7.9](/7.9).

B: the sources of steepness in a single tier: a single modification is graded, a distributive double modification is sigmoid even with little saturation (K_m = 10), and saturation with substrate competition (K_m = 1) makes it steeper](./plots/mapk-ultrasensitivity.py)

### 15.1.5. The PI3K–PKB Branch: Glycogen Synthesis and Glucose Uptake

The insulin signal branches at IRS1. Besides Grb2, phosphorylated IRS1 binds, through SH2 domains of its regulatory subunit, the enzyme phosphoinositide 3-kinase.

**Definition 15.1.7 (PI 3-Kinase and PKB)** **Phosphoinositide 3-kinase** (PI3K) phosphorylates the 3-OH of the inositol ring of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP₂), forming phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP₃) in the cytoplasmic leaflet of the plasma membrane. PIP₃ recruits proteins with PH (pleckstrin homology) domains, among them **protein kinase B** (PKB, also called Akt), a Ser/Thr kinase that is activated at the membrane by phosphorylation by PDK1 (on Thr308) and by mTORC2 (on Ser473).

PKB has several targets in the metabolic response to insulin. It phosphorylates **glycogen synthase kinase 3** (GSK3) on Ser9, inactivating it; since active GSK3 phosphorylates and inactivates **glycogen synthase**, insulin thus leaves **glycogen** synthase dephosphorylated and active, and glycogen synthesis rises (only part of the explanation; insulin also activates **protein phosphatase** 1, [7.1.19](/7.1.19)). In muscle and fat, PKB phosphorylates AS160 (TBC1D4), a GAP for Rab proteins, which allows the Rab-dependent movement of GLUT4 vesicles to the plasma membrane and raises glucose uptake ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.4.1)). PKB also promotes cell survival and, through **mTORC1**, protein synthesis and growth ([7.1.29](/7.1.29)).

The branch is switched off by the **lipid** phosphatase PTEN (phosphatase and tensin **homolog**), which removes the 3-phosphate from PIP₃. Loss of PTEN leaves PIP₃ high and PKB constantly active, a constant signal to grow and survive; *PTEN* is one of the most frequently inactivated tumor suppressor genes (15.6.2), and activating **mutations** of PI3K are common in breast and other cancers.

Measurements of the whole phosphoproteome of mouse liver after an insulin injection show the time course of the response: the receptor and IRS proteins are phosphorylated within seconds, **PKC** and Sos within half a minute to a minute, ERK maximally within about 3 min; GLUT4 reaches the plasma membrane in about 15 min, and changes in gene expression take hours. The many RTKs that phosphorylate IRS1, the several IRS proteins and the different SH2 proteins that bind them let one receptor activate several pathways and several receptors share them.

### 15.1.6. Cross Talk and Other Receptor Kinases

Insulin opposes **epinephrine** in most tissues, and the pathways interact directly. The insulin receptor phosphorylates two Tyr residues near the C-terminus of the β₂-adrenergic receptor, and PKB phosphorylates two Ser residues nearby; the receptor is then internalized, and the cell becomes less sensitive to epinephrine. Conversely, the phosphotyrosines of the **GPCR** can recruit Grb2, so that activation of ERK by insulin is severalfold stronger in cells that express the β₂ receptor. **Fatty acids**, **ceramides**, amino acids and **bile acids** also modulate insulin signaling, part of the basis of **insulin resistance** ([7.1.29](/7.1.29)).

Plants have no receptor tyrosine kinases but a much larger family of receptor-like kinases with Ser/Thr specificity: *Arabidopsis* has more than 400 with an extracellular domain and a transmembrane helix, classified by their extracellular domains (leucine-rich repeat, **lectin**, S-domain, wall-associated and others). They perceive hormones (the brassinosteroid receptor BRI1, 15.5.6), **peptides** that coordinate development (CLAVATA1 in the meristem), self-incompatibility signals in pollination (S-locus receptor kinase) and microbial molecules in innate immunity (FLS2, which binds a peptide of bacterial flagellin).

## 15.2. Receptors without Enzyme Activity: JAK–STAT

Cytokine receptors, including those for erythropoietin, growth hormone, prolactin, interferons and many interleukins, have no catalytic domain of their own; they bind a soluble tyrosine kinase.

**Definition 15.2.1 (JAK–STAT Pathway)** In the **JAK–STAT pathway**, a cytokine receptor associated with a Janus kinase (JAK) dimerizes or changes conformation on ligand binding; the two JAKs phosphorylate each other and Tyr residues of the receptor, which then bind the SH2 domains of STAT proteins (signal transducers and activators of transcription); JAK phosphorylates the STATs, which dimerize through reciprocal SH2–phosphotyrosine interactions, expose a nuclear localization signal, enter the nucleus and activate transcription of target genes.

Erythropoietin (EPO), made by the kidney when oxygen is low, binds its receptor on erythroid progenitor cells in the bone marrow; JAK2 and STAT5 then activate genes for survival and proliferation, and more erythrocytes are made. (JAKs are named after Janus, the two-faced Roman god, for their kinase domain and the kinase-like pseudokinase domain beside it, which regulates it.) The pathway is turned off by phosphatases (SHP1) and by SOCS proteins, whose genes STATs induce: a negative feedback. An activating mutation in the pseudokinase domain of JAK2 (Val617Phe) makes the kinase active without EPO and causes polycythemia vera, an overproduction of blood cells treated with the JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib; recombinant EPO is used against anemia (and abused in endurance sport). JAKs also engage the Ras–MAPK pathway, another case of divergence.

## 15.3. Multivalent Adaptor Proteins and Scaffolds

### 15.3.1. Modules That Read Phosphorylation

Two generalizations have emerged from the study of signaling. Protein kinases and phosphatases that act on Tyr, Ser and Thr are central and directly change the activities of very many proteins; and phosphorylation creates **binding sites**, so that the assembly of protein complexes is itself regulated. Most signaling proteins are multivalent: they have several binding modules and can bind several partners at once.

| Module | Binds | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| SH2 | phosphotyrosine + C-terminal flanking residues | Grb2, PI3K p85, PLCγ, Src, STATs, SHP2 |
| PTB | phosphotyrosine in Asn–Pro–X–pTyr (some PTB domains bind unphosphorylated motifs) | IRS1, Shc |
| 14-3-3 proteins | phosphoserine/phosphothreonine in specific motifs | Raf, Bad, Cdc25; the FRET reporter AKAR (15.7.2) |
| FHA, WW, polo-box, BRCT | pThr; pSer/pThr–Pro; pSer/pThr in other contexts | DNA-damage and cell-cycle proteins |
| PH | phosphoinositides (PIP₃, PIP₂) | PKB, PDK1, IRS1, PLCδ |
| SH3 | Pro-rich motifs (Pro–X–X–Pro) | Grb2, Src, PLCγ |
| PDZ | C-terminal sequences of partner proteins | scaffolds at synapses and cell junctions |

In Lehninger's image, protein kinases are the writers, phosphoresidue-binding domains the readers and phosphatases the erasers of a reversible code. Kinases that phosphorylate Tyr create sites for SH2 and PTB domains, and about 37 classical protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) remove them, about half of them receptor-like transmembrane proteins and the others soluble, often with SH2 domains of their own. Many of the regions that interact are intrinsically disordered ([7.1.5](/7.1.5#sec-5.4.3)) and fold only when they bind a partner, which lets one segment bind different partners in different complexes.

### 15.3.2. Autoinhibition: Src and GSK3

The same reading domains are used inside one protein to keep it inactive. The cytoplasmic tyrosine kinase Src has, from the N-terminus, a myristoylated membrane anchor, an SH3 domain, an SH2 domain, the kinase domain and a short C-terminal tail. When Tyr527 in the tail is phosphorylated (by the kinase Csk), the SH2 domain binds it, the SH3 domain binds a Pro-rich linker between SH2 and kinase domains, and the kinase is clamped shut. Dephosphorylation of Tyr527, or the binding of SH2 and SH3 ligands of a partner, opens it; the oncogenic v-Src of Rous sarcoma virus lacks the regulatory tail and is always active. GSK3 is autoinhibited differently: its substrates are "primed" by previous phosphorylation, and their phosphate binds a pocket of the kinase; phosphorylation of Ser9 near the N-terminus of GSK3 (by PKB, 15.1.5) turns the N-terminus into a pseudosubstrate that occupies this pocket.

### 15.3.3. Scaffolds and Localization

**Definition 15.3.1 (Scaffold Protein)** A **scaffold protein** is a noncatalytic protein that binds several components of a signaling pathway (often successive enzymes of a cascade) and so holds them together at a defined place in the cell.

Scaffolds speed reactions between their partners, protect them from cross-reaction with similar proteins of other pathways (insulating the pathway), place them near their substrates and can themselves be regulated. The **AKAPs** of [7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.2.6) are scaffolds for cAMP signaling. In the MAPK cascade of mammals the scaffold KSR binds Raf, MEK and ERK; in the yeast mating pathway the scaffold Ste5 binds the three kinases Ste11, Ste7 and Fus3, and the MAPK Fus3 phosphorylates Ste5, which weakens the activation of the cascade: a negative feedback that converts a sustained stimulus into a transient response, as shown by mutants of Ste5 lacking the phosphorylation sites. Since so many signaling reactions take place at the inner face of the plasma membrane, recruiting proteins there through phospholipid-binding domains and lipid anchors confines them to two dimensions, where collisions are far more frequent. **Membrane rafts** and caveolae ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.2.4)) add a further level: the β-adrenergic receptor is found in rafts together with Gs, **adenylyl cyclase**, PKA and the phosphatase PP2A, and in fibroblasts EGFR is concentrated in caveolae and leaves them, in a kinase-dependent way, when it binds EGF.

### 15.3.4. Integrins: Bidirectional Adhesion Receptors

**Integrins** ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.2.5)) are the sixth class of receptors listed in [7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.1.3). Humans have 24 integrins, formed from 18 α and 8 β subunits; each binds particular proteins of the extracellular matrix (**collagen**, fibronectin, laminin) or counter-receptors on other cells. They signal in both directions. In "inside-out" signaling, cytoplasmic proteins (talin and kindlin) bind the tail of the β subunit, separate the α and β tails and switch the extracellular domains from a bent, low-affinity to an extended, high-affinity state; this is how platelets and leukocytes make their integrins sticky within seconds. In "outside-in" signaling, binding of the matrix clusters integrins and assembles focal adhesions with the actin cytoskeleton and with the kinases FAK and Src, which control cell shape, motility, polarity, survival and differentiation.

## 15.4. Nuclear Receptors

**Steroid hormones** (estrogens, progesterone, androgens, glucocorticoids such as **cortisol**, mineralocorticoids such as aldosterone), the **vitamin** D hormone calcitriol, thyroid hormone and retinoic acid act mainly through receptors inside the cell. These hormones are too hydrophobic to dissolve well in blood and are carried on specific carrier proteins; they cross the plasma membrane by simple diffusion ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.3.2)).

**Definition 15.4.1 (Nuclear Receptor)** A **nuclear receptor** is a ligand-regulated transcription factor of a conserved family (48 members in humans) with, from the N-terminus, a variable region containing an activation function (AF-1), a DNA-binding domain of two zinc fingers (each a Zn²⁺ ion coordinated by four Cys), a hinge, and a ligand-binding domain whose C-terminal helix 12, when hormone is bound, forms a surface for coactivators (AF-2). The receptor binds specific DNA sequences called **hormone response elements** (HREs) in the regulatory regions of target genes, usually as a homodimer or as a heterodimer with the retinoid X receptor (RXR).

HREs consist of two six-base-pair half-sites, one bound by each zinc-finger module, arranged as **palindromes** for the steroid receptors (the glucocorticoid response element AGAACAnnnTGTTCT) or as direct repeats for the RXR heterodimers, whose spacing determines the receptor (3 bp for the vitamin D receptor, 4 for the thyroid hormone receptor, 5 for the retinoic acid receptor: the "3-4-5 rule"). Steroid receptors such as the glucocorticoid receptor are held in the cytoplasm, inactive, by **chaperones** of the Hsp90 family ([7.1.6](/7.1.6)); hormone binding releases them, and they dimerize, enter the nucleus and bind their HREs. The RXR heterodimers sit on **DNA** already without hormone, bound to **corepressors** that recruit **histone deacetylases**; hormone binding moves helix 12, the corepressors are exchanged for **coactivators** with **histone acetyltransferase** activity, and transcription starts (mechanisms in [7.2.14](/7.2.14) and [7.2.15](/7.2.15)). Because the response requires transcription and **translation**, its full effect takes hours to days. (Some steroid effects are faster and act through receptors in the plasma membrane.)

Nuclear receptors are major drug targets. Tamoxifen binds the estrogen receptor and, in breast tissue, prevents the movement of helix 12 that recruits coactivators, so it is an **antagonist** that slows the growth of estrogen-dependent breast cancers (in bone and uterus it acts as a **partial agonist**: a selective estrogen receptor modulator). Mifepristone (RU486) is an antagonist of the progesterone receptor used to terminate early pregnancy and for emergency contraception. Synthetic glucocorticoids (dexamethasone, prednisolone) are powerful anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive drugs, and thiazolidinediones, agonists of the fatty acid receptor PPARγ, improve insulin sensitivity ([7.1.29](/7.1.29)).

## 15.5. Signaling in Bacteria and Plants

### 15.5.1. Two-Component Systems

**Definition 15.5.1 (Two-Component System)** A **two-component system** consists of a sensor histidine kinase, usually a transmembrane protein that autophosphorylates a His residue (with ATP, in trans within its dimer) when its input domain detects a signal, and a cytoplasmic response regulator, to whose receiver domain the phosphoryl group is transferred onto an Asp residue; phosphorylation of the receiver activates the output domain of the regulator, usually a DNA-binding domain that controls transcription.

*E. coli* has about 30 such systems, which sense **osmolarity** (EnvZ–OmpR, which regulates the porins of the outer membrane), phosphate limitation (PhoR–PhoB), nitrogen, oxygen and other conditions; in chemotaxis, the kinase CheA, regulated by chemoreceptors, phosphorylates CheY, whose phosphorylated form binds the flagellar motor and changes its direction of rotation ([7.11](/7.11)). Phosphohistidine and acyl phosphates are high-energy phosphoryl compounds, so the transfer His → Asp is fast and reversible, and many sensor kinases also act as phosphatases of their regulator, which makes the system respond quickly in both directions. In phosphorelays the phosphoryl group passes through additional His and Asp sites (His–Asp–His–Asp) and several inputs can be integrated. Two-component systems are found in bacteria, archaea, fungi and plants, but not in animals, and they are being explored as targets for new antibiotics.

### 15.5.2. Plant Hormones and Their Receptors

Plants coordinate growth and development and respond to light, gravity, water, temperature and pathogens with a set of small molecules: the five classical hormones auxin, cytokinins, gibberellins, ethylene and abscisic acid (ABA), and the brassinosteroids, jasmonic acid, salicylic acid and strigolactones identified later. Their physiological roles are described in plant physiology ([7.13](/7.13)); here we compare the molecular logic of their perception.

| Hormone | Receptor | Receptor type | Key steps | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ethylene | ETR1, ERS1, ETR2, ERS2, EIN4 (ER membrane) | His kinase-like, Cu⁺ **cofactor** | receptors activate CTR1 (Raf-like) ⊣ EIN2; ethylene inactivates receptors | EIN3/EIL1 transcription factors |
| Cytokinins | AHK2, AHK3, AHK4/CRE1 | His kinases, CHASE domain | His → Asp → AHP (His) → Asp of ARR | type-B ARRs (transcription); type-A ARRs feed back negatively |
| Gibberellins | GID1 (soluble) | homolog of **hormone-sensitive lipase** | GA–GID1 binds DELLA; SCF^SLY1 ubiquitinates DELLA | degradation of DELLA repressors |
| Auxin | TIR1/AFB with Aux/IAA (co-receptor) | F-box protein of an SCF ubiquitin ligase | auxin glues TIR1 to Aux/IAA, which is degraded | ARF transcription factors released |
| Brassinosteroids | BRI1 with BAK1 | LRR receptor-like kinase | receptors inhibit BIN2 (GSK3-like) | BZR1/BES1 dephosphorylated, enter nucleus |
| Abscisic acid | PYR/PYL/RCAR (soluble, 14 in *Arabidopsis*) | START-domain proteins | ABA–receptor inhibits PP2C (ABI1/2) | SnRK2 kinases active: ABF transcription factors, SLAC1 anion channel |
| Jasmonate (JA-Ile) | COI1 with JAZ (co-receptor) | F-box protein | JAZ repressors degraded | MYC2 transcription factors |

Three themes recur. Plants use receptor kinases and histidine kinases rather than GPCRs ([7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.2.5)). Several hormones act by triggering the destruction of a transcriptional repressor by the **ubiquitin–proteasome system** (15.5.5). And most pathways are chains of negative regulators, ON by derepression: the hormone inhibits an inhibitor.

### 15.5.3. Ethylene: A Pathway of Negative Regulators

The gas ethylene ($\ce{C2H4}$), produced in all parts of a plant, promotes fruit ripening, leaf and flower senescence and abscission, root hair formation and responses to stress; its effect on plant growth was first described by the Russian student Dimitry Neljubow in 1901. Dark-grown *Arabidopsis* seedlings exposed to ethylene show a "triple response": short, thick hypocotyls and roots and an exaggerated apical hook. Screening for seedlings that ignore ethylene (the *etr*, *ein* mutants) or show the triple response without it (*ctr1*) identified the pathway.

Ethylene binds, with a $\ce{Cu+}$ cofactor, to the transmembrane domain of five related receptors in the ER membrane, which resemble the sensor kinases of two-component systems (ETR1, ERS1 and EIN4 with His kinase activity, ETR2 and ERS2 without; ETR1, ETR2 and EIN4 also have a receiver domain). The genetics revealed an unexpected logic. Loss-of-function mutations in single receptor genes have little effect, but knocking out several receptors together gives a constitutive ethylene response: the receptors are negative regulators, active without ethylene. The dominant ethylene-insensitive mutations (such as *etr1-1*) lock a receptor in its active, signaling state, which ethylene cannot switch off. The active receptors activate CTR1, a protein kinase related to Raf, which phosphorylates the ER membrane protein EIN2 and keeps it inactive. When ethylene inactivates the receptors, CTR1 becomes inactive, EIN2 is no longer phosphorylated, its C-terminal part is cleaved off, enters the nucleus and stabilizes the transcription factors EIN3 and EIL1 (and, in the cytoplasm, represses the translation of **mRNAs** for the F-box proteins EBF1/2 that target EIN3 for degradation). EIN3 then activates the genes of the ethylene response. (Lehninger's earlier editions drew a MAP kinase cascade below CTR1; that branch has not been confirmed, and EIN2 is now known to be the direct substrate.) The *etr1-1* gene expressed in petunia delays flower senescence and fruit ripening, and in practice ethylene action is blocked during the storage of fruit with 1-methylcyclopropene, which binds the receptors.

### 15.5.4. Cytokinins: A His–Asp Phosphorelay

Cytokinins, adenine derivatives that promote cell division in shoots, are perceived by the His kinases AHK2, AHK3 and AHK4 (CRE1), which bind cytokinin through an extracellular CHASE domain. The phosphoryl group passes from the His of the kinase domain to an Asp of the receiver domain of the same receptor, then to a His of the small histidine phosphotransfer proteins (AHPs), which move from the cytoplasm into the nucleus (visible with AHP–GFP fusions), and finally to the Asp of the receiver domain of type-B response regulators (ARRs), transcription factors with a DNA-binding domain; among their targets are the type-A ARRs, which lack a DNA-binding domain and inhibit the pathway (negative feedback). It is a multistep phosphorelay of bacterial type, probably inherited from the cyanobacterial ancestor of chloroplasts.

### 15.5.5. Gibberellins and DELLA: Derepression by Degradation

Gibberellins were discovered as the toxin of the fungus *Gibberella fujikuroi*, which causes the "foolish seedling" (bakanae) disease of rice: infected plants grow so tall that they fall over and are sterile. They turned out to be endogenous hormones that promote stem elongation, seed germination and, in some species, flowering and fruit development; the short peas of Mendel's experiments carry a mutation (*le*) in a GA biosynthetic enzyme (GA 3-oxidase). Their perception uses the ubiquitin system.

**Definition 15.5.2 (Ubiquitination)** **Ubiquitin** is a protein of 76 residues, highly conserved in all eukaryotes, that is attached covalently, through an isopeptide bond between its C-terminal Gly and a Lys side chain, to target proteins. A cascade of three enzymes attaches it: a ubiquitin-activating enzyme (E1) forms a thioester with ubiquitin using ATP, a ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2) carries it, and a ubiquitin ligase (E3), which recognizes the substrate and so confers specificity, transfers it to the target. Chains linked through Lys48 of ubiquitin mark the target for degradation by the 26S **proteasome**; other chains and single ubiquitins have nondegradative signaling functions.

The mechanism of the proteasome and the other roles of ubiquitination belong to molecular biology ([7.2.20](/7.2.20)). The small ubiquitin-related modifier SUMO (about 11 kDa, attached to Lys in the motif ψ–Lys–X–Glu, with ψ a large hydrophobic residue) is added by a similar **enzyme cascade** and changes the localization and interactions of proteins in transcription, chromosome organization, DNA repair, nuclear transport and signaling.

DELLA proteins (named for a conserved Asp–Glu–Leu–Leu–Ala motif at their N-terminus) are nuclear repressors of growth: they bind and inactivate transcription factors such as the PIFs. GA binds its soluble receptor GID1, a protein homologous to hormone-sensitive lipase that has lost its catalytic activity; a lid of GID1 closes over the hormone and creates a surface that binds the DELLA domain. The GA–GID1–DELLA complex is recognized by the F-box protein SLY1 (GID2 in rice) of an SCF-type ubiquitin ligase, DELLA is polyubiquitinated and degraded, and growth genes are derepressed. Mutants make the logic plain. Rice *slr1* (slender) lacks DELLA and grows tall as if saturated with GA. The *Arabidopsis* mutant *gai* has a DELLA protein with a deletion in the DELLA domain that can no longer bind GID1; it is not degraded and represses growth even in the presence of GA, giving a dwarf that is GA-insensitive and genetically dominant.

The semi-dwarf wheat varieties of the Green Revolution, bred by Norman Borlaug (Nobel Peace Prize 1970), carry exactly such mutations: the *Rht-B1b* and *Rht-D1b* alleles encode DELLA proteins with N-terminal truncations that resist GA-induced degradation. Their short, sturdy stems do not fall over (lodge) when heavily fertilized, and more of the plant's resources go into grain. The semi-dwarf rice varieties carry instead a mutation in a GA biosynthetic gene (*sd1*, GA 20-oxidase), and growth retardants that inhibit GA synthesis are used for the same purpose.

### 15.5.6. Auxin, Brassinosteroids and Abscisic Acid

Auxin (indole-3-acetic acid) uses the same strategy as GA with a twist: its receptor is the F-box protein TIR1 (and its relatives, AFB1–5) of an SCF ubiquitin ligase, and auxin binds in a pocket of TIR1 that is completed by the Aux/IAA repressor, acting as a "molecular glue" between the ligase and its substrate. The Aux/IAA proteins are degraded and release the ARF transcription factors. The direction of auxin transport, which underlies tropisms and organ patterning, is set by the polar location of PIN auxin efflux carriers in the plasma membrane, revealed by PIN–GFP fusions (15.7.1). Jasmonate perception by COI1 and JAZ repressors works the same way.

Brassinosteroids, plant steroid hormones, are not perceived by a nuclear receptor as animal steroids are, but by the leucine-rich-repeat receptor kinase BRI1 at the cell surface. Brassinolide binding brings BRI1 together with its co-receptor BAK1; the two kinases phosphorylate each other, release an inhibitory protein (BKI1) and activate the cytoplasmic kinases BSKs and the phosphatase BSU1, which inactivates the GSK3-like kinase BIN2. BIN2, active in the absence of hormone, phosphorylates the transcription factors BZR1 and BES1, keeping them out of the nucleus and inactive; with BIN2 off, they are dephosphorylated by PP2A, enter the nucleus and activate growth genes. The parallel with insulin signaling, where PKB inactivates GSK3 and so activates glycogen synthase (15.1.5), is striking: in both kingdoms a signal activates its targets by inhibiting a constitutively active GSK3.

**Remark 15.5.1** The receptor of abscisic acid, the hormone of stress and dormancy that closes stomata in drought, was long elusive, and several reported receptors did not stand up: an RNA-binding protein (FCA; the report was retracted in 2008), the H subunit of Mg-chelatase (ChlH/ABAR, whose role in ABA perception is not supported by later genetics) and a protein claimed to be a GPCR (GCR2, which turned out to be related to bacterial lanthionine synthetases and not to be required for ABA responses). Older textbooks still list these three. The receptors established since 2009 are the soluble PYR/PYL/RCAR proteins (14 in *Arabidopsis*), members of the START/Bet v 1 family of lipid-binding proteins.

ABA binds in a pocket of a PYR/PYL/RCAR receptor and closes two loops (the "gate" and the "latch") over it. The closed gate forms a surface that binds and inhibits the clade A protein phosphatases 2C (ABI1, ABI2, HAB1), which in the absence of ABA dephosphorylate and inactivate the SnRK2 kinases (SnRK2.2, 2.3 and 2.6, also called OST1). Freed from the phosphatase, the SnRK2s autophosphorylate and phosphorylate the ABF/AREB transcription factors, which activate stress-response genes, and, in guard cells, the anion channel SLAC1, whose opening depolarizes the cell and leads to loss of $\ce{K+}$ and water and closure of the stomatal pore ([7.13](/7.13)). The dominant ABA-insensitive mutants *abi1-1* and *abi2-1*, isolated long before the receptor was known, have phosphatases that are no longer inhibited by the receptors. Again the hormone inhibits an inhibitor. Signaling in salt stress uses related components: the calcium sensor SOS3 (calcineurin B-like protein CBL4, not a **calmodulin**) binds and activates the SnRK3-type kinase SOS2 (CIPK24), which phosphorylates and activates the plasma membrane $\ce{Na+}$/$\ce{H+}$ **antiporter** SOS1, which exports $\ce{Na+}$ from the cell.

## 15.6. The Cell Cycle, Oncogenes and Apoptosis

### 15.6.1. Cyclin-Dependent Kinases Drive the Cell Cycle

The eukaryotic cell cycle has four phases: G1 (gap 1), S (DNA synthesis), G2 and M (mitosis and cytokinesis); a typical animal cell in culture completes it in about 24 hours. Cells that stop dividing leave G1 for a quiescent state, G0, which may last for the lifetime of a differentiated cell such as a hepatocyte or adipocyte; stem cells retain the ability to divide. The order of events is enforced by protein kinases whose activity rises and falls during the cycle.

**Definition 15.6.1 (Cyclin and CDK)** A **cyclin-dependent kinase** (CDK) is a Ser/Thr protein kinase that is active only when bound to a regulatory subunit, a **cyclin**; cyclins are made and destroyed at specific times in the cell cycle, so that particular cyclin–CDK complexes are active in particular phases.

In animal cells cyclin D–CDK4/6 rises during G1 in response to growth factors, cyclin E–CDK2 peaks at the G1/S transition, cyclin A–CDK2 acts in S and G2, and cyclin B–CDK1 in mitosis. Binding of cyclin moves a T loop out of the substrate-binding site of the CDK and brings a catalytic Glu into the active site, raising activity about $10^4$-fold. Four mechanisms regulate CDK activity:

1. synthesis of cyclins (and CDKs), induced by growth factors through transcription factors such as Fos, Jun and Myc;
2. phosphorylation: CDK-activating kinase (CAK, itself cyclin H–CDK7) phosphorylates a Thr of the T loop (Thr160 in CDK2), which holds the loop away from the active site, while Wee1 (and Myt1) phosphorylate Tyr15 (and Thr14), which blocks the binding of ATP; the phosphatase Cdc25 removes these inhibitory phosphates, and since active CDK1 activates Cdc25 and inhibits Wee1, activation of the mitotic CDK is self-reinforcing;
3. degradation of cyclins: active cyclin B–CDK1 activates its own destroyer, the ubiquitin ligase APC/C (anaphase-promoting complex, with Cdc20), which recognizes the "destruction box" of mitotic cyclins and marks them for the proteasome; the resulting negative feedback makes cyclin levels oscillate;
4. CDK inhibitor proteins (CKIs), such as p21 and p27 (Cip/Kip family) and p16 (INK4 family).

CDKs then phosphorylate the proteins that carry out each phase. Phosphorylation of the nuclear lamins causes the nuclear envelope to break down at mitosis; phosphorylation of a regulatory light chain of myosin after division inactivates the contractile ring until the next cytokinesis. A key target in G1 is the retinoblastoma protein: unphosphorylated pRb binds the transcription factor E2F and keeps it inactive; phosphorylation of pRb by cyclin D–CDK4/6 and then cyclin E–CDK2 releases E2F, which activates genes for DNA synthesis (**DNA polymerase** α, **ribonucleotide reductase**, **thymidylate synthase**, [7.1.28](/7.1.28)) and for cyclin E itself, a positive feedback that commits the cell to S phase.

DNA damage stops the cycle at checkpoints. Double-strand breaks, detected by the MRN complex, activate the kinase ATM; single-stranded DNA at stalled **replication forks**, coated with RPA, activates the related kinase ATR. They activate the checkpoint kinases Chk2 and Chk1, which inhibit Cdc25, so that CDKs stay inhibited by Tyr15 phosphorylation and the cell is arrested in G2; and they phosphorylate the transcription factor p53, which is then no longer marked for degradation by its ubiquitin ligase MDM2, accumulates and induces the CDK inhibitor p21, arresting the cell in G1. The cell gains time to repair its DNA ([7.2.8](/7.2.8)); if the damage is too severe, p53 induces apoptosis (15.6.3). The cell biology of the checkpoints is the subject of [7.3](/7.3), and the mathematics of the cell-cycle oscillator that of [7.9](/7.9). Inhibitors of CDK4/6 (palbociclib, ribociclib) are used against hormone receptor–positive breast cancer.

### 15.6.2. Oncogenes and Tumor Suppressor Genes

Cancer arises from a clone of cells that divide without the normal restraints, almost always because of mutations in genes that regulate division, inherited, caused by mutagens and radiation, or arising from errors of **replication**; usually several mutations must accumulate.

**Definition 15.6.2 (Oncogene and Tumor Suppressor Gene)** A **proto-oncogene** is a normal gene whose product promotes cell growth or division, typically a signaling protein; an **oncogene** is a mutated or overexpressed form of it whose product is excessively or constitutively active. A **tumor suppressor gene** encodes a protein that restrains division or promotes the death of damaged cells; tumors arise when both copies are inactivated.

Oncogenes were first found in tumor viruses, which had picked up host proto-oncogenes and mutated them (*v-src* of Rous sarcoma virus, *v-abl* of Abelson leukemia virus); most human oncogenes arise by mutation, amplification or translocation in somatic cells. Their effect is dominant: one mutant allele sends the signal "divide", the car's accelerator stuck to the floor. The products of oncogenes include growth factors, receptors (EGFR with activating mutations in lung cancer; the RTK ErbB2/HER2, whose gene is amplified, up to 100-fold overexpressed, in about a fifth of breast cancers), G proteins (Ras: a mutation at Gly12, Gly13 or Gln61 abolishes its GTPase, and Ras genes are mutated in about a fifth of human cancers, *KRAS* in most pancreatic cancers), protein kinases (BRAF Val600Glu in half of melanomas; the fusion BCR–ABL, produced by the Philadelphia chromosome translocation, in chronic myeloid leukemia) and transcription factors (Myc, Jun, Fos). Mutant tumor suppressors are recessive at the cellular level, the failure of the brakes: *RB1*, *TP53* (mutated in about half of all human cancers and in over 90% of cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas), *PTEN*, *NF1* (a Ras GAP), *APC*, *CDKN2A* (p16). Stability (caretaker) genes such as *ATM*, the *XP* genes of xeroderma pigmentosum, *BRCA1* and *BRCA2* and the **mismatch repair** genes encode DNA repair proteins; their loss raises the mutation rate of all other genes.

**Example 15.6.1** Knudson's "two-hit" hypothesis (1971) explains the two forms of retinoblastoma, a childhood tumor of the retina. If a tumor needs both copies of *RB1* to be inactivated, a child who inherits one defective copy needs only one further somatic mutation in any retinal cell. With a rate $\mu$ of such hits per cell per unit time, the expected number of tumors grows linearly with time, and the probability of remaining tumor-free decays exponentially; these children often develop several tumors, in both eyes, early. A child born with two good copies needs two independent hits in the same cell; the probability per cell grows as $(\mu t)^2$, so tumors are much rarer, single and later. Knudson found that the age distribution of the hereditary cases fits the one-hit and that of the sporadic cases the two-hit curve. People with the inherited form also have a high risk of other cancers later in life, and those who inherit one mutant copy of *TP53* (Li–Fraumeni syndrome) develop multiple cancers early, for the same reason.

The development of colorectal cancer illustrates the accumulation of mutations: inactivation of both copies of *APC* gives a benign polyp (adenoma), which is chromosomally unstable; an activating mutation of *KRAS* (or *BRAF*) enlarges it; mutations in *PI3K*, loss of *SMAD4* and of the region containing *DCC*, and finally loss of *TP53*, give a carcinoma that can invade and metastasize. Removal of polyps found by colonoscopy at the adenoma stage prevents the later stages. Sequencing of thousands of tumors has identified a few hundred driver genes, among many thousands of passenger mutations that occurred at random and confer no growth advantage.

Since many oncogene products are protein kinases, kinase inhibitors have become a major class of cancer drugs. Their difficulty is selectivity: about 500 kinases share the ATP-binding site that most inhibitors occupy. Imatinib (Gleevec), the first great success, binds the ATP site and an adjacent pocket available only in the inactive conformation of ABL kinase, and induces remission in almost all patients with chronic-phase chronic myeloid leukemia; resistance arises from mutations such as Thr315Ile at the "gatekeeper" position, which later drugs overcome. Erlotinib, gefitinib and osimertinib (a covalent inhibitor that reacts with a Cys of the ATP site) target mutant EGFR in lung cancer; vemurafenib BRAF Val600Glu; trametinib binds an allosteric pocket of MEK; sotorasib (2021) binds covalently to the Cys of KRAS Gly12Cys and traps it in the GDP state, the first drug against Ras after decades of attempts. Monoclonal antibodies target the extracellular domains: trastuzumab (Herceptin) binds HER2, cetuximab EGFR, and bevacizumab (Avastin) binds the growth factor VEGF itself rather than its receptor, starving tumors of new blood vessels.

### 15.6.3. Apoptosis

**Definition 15.6.3 (Apoptosis and Caspase)** **Apoptosis** (programmed cell death) is a regulated form of cell death in which the cell shrinks, condenses its chromatin, cleaves its DNA between nucleosomes, exposes phosphatidylserine on its surface and fragments into membrane-bounded apoptotic bodies that are engulfed by phagocytes, without releasing its contents or causing inflammation. It is executed by **caspases**, cysteine proteases that cleave their substrates after Asp residues and are made as inactive zymogens.

Apoptosis shapes the developing embryo (it removes the tissue between the digits, and exactly 131 of the 1090 somatic cells formed during the development of the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans* die by apoptosis, a program whose genes *ced-3*, *ced-4* and *ced-9* were found by H. Robert Horvitz, Nobel Prize 2002); it eliminates self-reactive lymphocytes during their maturation (T cells in the thymus, B cells in the bone marrow), virus-infected cells and cells with irreparable DNA damage; and it removes cells in normal turnover, as in the shedding of the uterine lining. It differs from necrosis, the uncontrolled death of injured cells, which swell and burst and cause inflammation.

The caspases form a **zymogen** cascade ([7.1.9](/7.1.9#sec-9.5.4)) with two entry points.

- Extrinsic pathway: death ligands such as Fas ligand or tumor necrosis factor (TNF) bind death receptors (Fas, TNFR1) with a cytoplasmic death domain of about 80 residues; the death domains recruit the adaptor FADD (directly for Fas, through TRADD for TNFR1), which brings several molecules of procaspase-8 together; the initiator caspase-8 activates itself by mutual cleavage and then cleaves the executioner caspases-3 and -7.
- Intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway: DNA damage (through p53), growth-factor withdrawal or other stress activates BH3-only proteins of the BCL-2 family, which neutralize the antiapoptotic proteins BCL-2 and BCL-XL and activate BAX and BAK; these form pores in the outer mitochondrial membrane, through which **cytochrome** *c* ([7.1.23](/7.1.23)) escapes into the cytosol. Cytochrome *c* binds Apaf-1, which assembles with ATP into a wheel-shaped heptamer, the apoptosome, that activates the initiator procaspase-9; caspase-9 activates caspases-3 and -7.

The pathways are linked: caspase-8 cleaves the BH3-only protein Bid, whose fragment tBid activates the mitochondrial pathway. Executioner caspases cleave hundreds of proteins, among them the inhibitor of a caspase-activated DNase, which then cuts the DNA, the nuclear lamins and the cytoskeleton, and they activate a **scramblase** that exposes phosphatidylserine as an "eat me" signal ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.2.2)). The cell is dismantled in an orderly way, and its components are taken up and reused by its neighbors. Cancer cells often evade apoptosis by overexpressing BCL-2 (in follicular lymphoma, through a translocation) or losing p53; venetoclax, which mimics the BH3 domain and inhibits BCL-2, is used in chronic lymphocytic leukemia.

## 15.7. Methods for Studying Signaling

### 15.7.1. Fluorescent Proteins

The **green fluorescent protein** of the jellyfish *Aequorea victoria* ([6.4.10](/6.4.10#sec-10.5)), which forms its chromophore from three of its own residues without any cofactor, can be fused genetically to any protein and followed in living cells; Osamu Shimomura, Martin Chalfie and Roger Tsien shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2008 for its discovery and development. Enhanced GFP absorbs at 488 nm and emits at 507 nm, and engineered variants cover the spectrum from blue to far red. Fusions reveal where a signaling protein is and how it moves: PIN–GFP fusions show the polar localization of auxin efflux carriers in root cells (15.5.6), AHP–GFP the movement of the phosphorelay protein into the nucleus (15.5.4), and some transcription factors and their regulators enter or leave the nucleus depending on light.

### 15.7.2. FRET Biosensors

Fluorescent proteins also serve as spectroscopic rulers. In **Förster resonance energy transfer** (FRET, [6.4.10](/6.4.10#sec-10.3.4)) an excited donor passes its energy without emission to a nearby acceptor, which then fluoresces at its own, longer wavelength.

**Formula 15.7.1 (FRET Efficiency)** The efficiency of energy transfer between a donor and an acceptor at distance $r$ is

$$
E = \frac{1}{1 + (r/R_0)^6},
$$
where the Förster radius $R_0$, the distance of 50% transfer, depends on the spectral overlap of the pair, their relative orientation and the quantum yield of the donor; for CFP and YFP it is about $4.9\,\mathrm{nm}$.

Because of the sixth power, $E$ falls from 90% to 10% when $r$ increases from $0.69R_0$ to $1.44R_0$: FRET reports changes of a few nanometres, the scale of protein conformational changes and complexes, and the ratio of acceptor to donor emission can be imaged in single living cells. Biosensors built on this principle measure second messengers and enzyme activities in real time and with subcellular resolution.

- cAMP: the first sensor used the R and C subunits of PKA labeled with two fluorophores, whose FRET disappears when cAMP dissociates the **holoenzyme**; unimolecular sensors place the cAMP-binding domain of Epac between CFP and YFP, which separate when cAMP binds. They revealed the localized cAMP signals predicted by the AKAP model ([7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.2.6)).
- PKA activity: in the reporter AKAR, a PKA substrate peptide and a phosphopeptide-binding domain (14-3-3 or FHA) lie between CFP and YFP; phosphorylation by PKA makes the domain bind the peptide, bringing the fluorophores together and raising FRET.
- $\ce{Ca^2+}$: the cameleons contain calmodulin and a calmodulin-binding peptide between two fluorescent proteins.

### 15.7.3. Complementation Assays

Several methods detect protein–protein interactions by the reassembly of a split reporter protein.

- *Bimolecular fluorescence complementation* (BiFC): the N- and C-terminal halves of YFP (or of its brighter relative Venus) are fused to two proteins; neither fragment fluoresces, but if the proteins bind each other the fragments fold into a fluorescent YFP. BiFC shows where in the cell an interaction occurs, but the reassembly is practically irreversible and the chromophore matures slowly, so it does not follow fast changes.
- Luciferase complementation imaging (LCI): the two proteins are fused to the N- and C-terminal halves of firefly luciferase, which, when reassembled, oxidizes luciferin with ATP and $\ce{O2}$ and emits light at about 560 nm (bioluminescence, [6.4.10](/6.4.10#sec-10.6.3)). The complementation is reversible and there is no background from excitation light, so the signal is quantitative; with transient expression in leaves of *Nicotiana benthamiana* it is a standard test of interactions between plant proteins, for example between a small G protein (ROP) and its GEFs.

**Definition 15.7.1 (Yeast Two-Hybrid System)** The **yeast two-hybrid system** detects interactions between two proteins X (the bait) and Y (the prey) by reconstituting a transcription factor in yeast: X is fused to the DNA-binding domain of the activator Gal4, which binds upstream activating sequences in front of reporter genes, and Y to a transcription activation domain; only if X binds Y is the activation domain brought to the promoter, and the reporter genes are expressed.

The method rests on the modular structure of eukaryotic **activators**, whose DNA-binding and **activation domains** work independently ([7.2.14](/7.2.14)). Bait and prey are carried on **plasmids** selected by markers such as *TRP1* and *LEU2* (growth without tryptophan and leucine); interaction is detected by reporters that permit growth without histidine, adenine or uracil (*HIS3*, *ADE2*, *URA3*) or that turn colonies blue on X-gal (*lacZ*). A whole library of preys can be screened against one bait. The interaction between the salt-tolerance proteins SOS3 and SOS2 (15.5.6), for example, was found in this way. Positives must be confirmed by other methods: proteins that activate transcription by themselves give false positives, and membrane proteins, or proteins that need modifications absent in the yeast nucleus, give false negatives.

## Questions

1. A Ras mutant has lost its GTPase activity. How does this affect a cell's response to insulin and to EGF? Why are mutations in the GAP NF1 oncogenic, and why does a dominant-negative Ras that binds GDP but not GTP block the response?

   **Answer** Once activated by Sos, the mutant Ras stays GTP-bound and keeps stimulating Raf; the MAPK cascade is active even after the hormone is gone, and the growth genes stay on: the cell behaves as if it were permanently stimulated. Loss of NF1 removes the GAP that accelerates the slow intrinsic GTPase of normal Ras, so Ras·GTP lasts much longer after each activation, with a similar effect. A Ras that binds only GDP sequesters Sos (it binds the GEF but cannot be released by GTP), so endogenous Ras is not activated and the signal is blocked.

2. Place in the order in which they act after insulin binds its receptor, and say which do not belong to the pathway: MEK, Ras, ERK, **GRK**, Raf, Sos, IRS1, PKA, Grb2, PI3K, PKB, GSK3.

   **Answer** MAPK branch: IRS1 → Grb2 → Sos → Ras → Raf → MEK → ERK. Metabolic branch: IRS1 → PI3K → (PIP₃) → PKB → GSK3 (inhibited). GRK and PKA belong to GPCR signaling, not to insulin signaling ([7.1.14](/7.1.14)).

3. Using Formula 15.1.1 with unsaturated enzymes ($K \gg 1$), show that the fully phosphorylated fraction is $m_2 = u^2/(1 + u + u^2)$ and estimate its apparent **Hill coefficient**. Why does a cascade of three such tiers respond much more steeply than any one of them?

   **Answer** For $K \gg 1$ the denominators are 1 and $r = u$; then $m_0 : m_1 : m_2 = 1 : u : u^2$ and $m_2 = u^2/(1 + u + u^2)$. Solving $m_2 = 0.1$ gives $0.9u^2 - 0.1u - 0.1 = 0$, $u_{10} = 0.393$; $m_2 = 0.9$ gives $0.1u^2 - 0.9u - 0.9 = 0$, $u_{90} = 9.92$; $n_{\mathrm{H}} = \ln81/\ln(9.92/0.393) = 4.39/3.23 \approx 1.36$. In a cascade the input of each tier is the output of the tier above, which is already a steep function of the stimulus; composing sigmoid functions multiplies their logarithmic sensitivities in the transition region, so the overall response is much steeper (Figure in 15.1.4), and saturation of the enzymes adds zero-order ultrasensitivity at each tier.

4. In *Arabidopsis*, single loss-of-function mutations in ethylene receptor genes have little effect, quadruple mutants show a constitutive triple response, and dominant *etr1-1* plants are ethylene-insensitive. *ctr1* mutants show a constitutive response, and *ein2* mutants are completely insensitive. Explain these phenotypes with a model of the pathway, and predict the phenotype of an *ein2 ctr1* double mutant.

   **Answer** The receptors are redundant negative regulators: without ethylene they keep CTR1 active, and CTR1 inhibits EIN2, which is required for the response. Losing one receptor leaves others to keep CTR1 on; losing most of them inactivates CTR1 and releases EIN2, as ethylene does. *etr1-1* encodes a receptor that cannot be switched off by ethylene and keeps CTR1 active even with ethylene, hence dominant insensitivity. Without CTR1, EIN2 is always active (constitutive response); without EIN2, nothing downstream can respond. In the double mutant *ein2* is epistatic: since EIN2 acts downstream of CTR1, *ein2 ctr1* plants are ethylene-insensitive.

5. The *gai* mutation of *Arabidopsis* deletes part of the DELLA domain of a DELLA protein and causes a dominant, GA-insensitive dwarf; a loss-of-function mutation in the rice DELLA gene (*slr1*) gives a slender, overgrown plant. Explain both, and explain why the Green Revolution wheat varieties are short but respond normally to nitrogen fertilizer.

   **Answer** GA–GID1 binds the DELLA domain and recruits SCF^SLY1, which ubiquitinates DELLA for degradation. A DELLA lacking this domain cannot bind GA–GID1 and is not degraded, so it represses growth in the presence of GA (dominant, because the stable repressor acts regardless of the normal copy). Without DELLA, growth is not repressed even without GA, as if the plant were saturated with GA. The *Rht-B1b* and *Rht-D1b* alleles of wheat encode N-terminally truncated DELLAs resistant to GA-induced degradation: stems stay short and sturdy and do not lodge under heavy fertilization, while the plant still uses the nitrogen for grain.

6. Explain why tumors caused by oncogenes are dominant at the cellular level, while those caused by tumor suppressor genes require both copies to be defective. Why do children who inherit one defective copy of *RB1* develop multiple tumors in both eyes, early?

   **Answer** An oncogene product is a hyperactive accelerator: one mutant allele produces enough constitutively active protein to send the "divide" signal, whatever the normal allele does. A tumor suppressor is a brake; one normal allele usually provides enough protein to restrain division, so loss of function needs both alleles to be lost. A child with an inherited mutant *RB1* allele has it in every retinal cell, so only one somatic mutation of the remaining allele, which happens in some of the millions of dividing retinal cells, is needed; several such events are likely, in both eyes, before the retinal cells stop dividing at about age 3 (one-hit kinetics, Example 15.6.1). Sporadic cases need two hits in the same cell and are rare, single and later.

7. Compare the two pathways that activate caspases. What would be the effect of overexpressing BCL-2, and how does venetoclax act? Why does a cell dying by apoptosis not cause inflammation?

   **Answer** Extrinsic: death ligands (FasL, TNF) bind death receptors, whose death domains recruit FADD (through TRADD for TNFR1) and procaspase-8, which activates itself and cleaves caspases-3 and -7. Intrinsic: stress activates BH3-only proteins; BAX and BAK permeabilize the outer mitochondrial membrane; cytochrome *c* binds Apaf-1, forming the apoptosome, which activates caspase-9 and then caspases-3 and -7. Caspase-8 also cleaves Bid, linking the two. Overexpressed BCL-2 binds BH3-only proteins and BAX/BAK, preventing cytochrome *c* release, so the cell survives damage that should kill it (as in follicular lymphoma); venetoclax occupies the BH3-binding groove of BCL-2, freeing the proapoptotic proteins. Apoptotic cells keep their membranes intact, expose phosphatidylserine and are engulfed by phagocytes before they release their contents, so no inflammatory signals are spilled.

8. A FRET sensor has $R_0 = 5.0\,\mathrm{nm}$. In the "off" state the fluorophores are 4.0 nm apart, and binding of the messenger moves them to 6.0 nm. Calculate the FRET efficiency in both states. How would you use FRET to show that **β-arrestin** binds the β-adrenergic receptor in living cells, and what would you expect?

   **Answer** $E(4.0) = 1/(1 + 0.8^6) = 1/(1 + 0.262) = 0.79$; $E(6.0) = 1/(1 + 1.2^6) = 1/(1 + 2.99) = 0.25$: a large change for a 2 nm movement. To show arrestin binding, fuse CFP to the C-terminal (cytoplasmic) tail of the receptor and YFP to β-arrestin, express both, excite CFP at about 433 nm and record the emission at 476 nm (CFP) and 527 nm (YFP). Before stimulation the ratio of YFP to CFP emission is low; after addition of an agonist (and phosphorylation by GRK) arrestin binds the receptor tail, bringing the fluorophores within a few nanometres, and the YFP/CFP ratio rises. Failure may mean that the fluorophores are still too far apart or poorly oriented, or that the fusions disturb binding; controls with a phosphorylation-site mutant of the receptor should show no change.
