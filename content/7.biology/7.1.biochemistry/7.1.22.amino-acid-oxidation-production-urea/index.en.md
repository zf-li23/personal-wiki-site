Amino acids are the last of the three classes of fuel whose oxidation contributes to the energy of the cell. They differ from carbohydrates and fatty acids in two ways that organize this chapter. First, they contain nitrogen. Reduced nitrogen is valuable but toxic as free ammonia, so every degradative pathway begins by removing the α-amino group, always with the help of pyridoxal phosphate, and the amino groups follow a separate route that ends, in most terrestrial vertebrates, in urea. Second, the carbon skeletons are twenty different molecules, broken down by twenty pathways; yet they converge on only six intermediates of central metabolism, and whether a skeleton can make glucose or only ketone bodies depends on which of the six it reaches. The chemistry of the coenzymes involved (PLP, tetrahydrofolate, S-adenosylmethionine, coenzyme B12) is in [7.1.17](/7.1.17#sec-17.2); the **citric acid cycle** into which the skeletons feed is in [7.1.20](/7.1.20), gluconeogenesis and ketogenesis in [7.1.19](/7.1.19) and [7.1.21](/7.1.21), and the synthesis of amino acids, the reverse problem, in [7.1.27](/7.1.27). The structures of the amino acids themselves are in [7.1.3](/7.1.3).

## 22.1. Metabolic Fates of Amino Groups

### 22.1.1. When Amino Acids Are Oxidized

The fraction of metabolic energy obtained from amino acids varies widely: carnivores obtain most of it from protein, herbivores little; most microorganisms can use amino acids as fuel when they are available; plants hardly ever oxidize amino acids for energy, and regulate their amino acid pools to meet the needs of biosynthesis. In humans amino acid catabolism normally provides 10–15% of the energy. Amino acids are oxidized in three situations:

1. during normal protein turnover, when amino acids released by the degradation of cellular proteins are not reused;
2. after a meal rich in protein, when the amino acids absorbed exceed the needs of protein synthesis: amino acids cannot be stored;
3. in starvation or uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, when carbohydrates are unavailable or cannot be used, and body proteins are broken down for fuel and for **gluconeogenesis** ([7.1.29](/7.1.29)).

The amino groups and the carbon skeletons take separate but interconnected pathways. The amino groups are collected as glutamate, glutamine and alanine, delivered to the liver, and excreted; the skeletons, converted to α-keto acids, are oxidized in the citric acid cycle or used for gluconeogenesis and ketogenesis. Four amino acids, glutamate, glutamine, alanine and aspartate, carry and distribute amino groups; they are present at high concentrations in many tissues and are each one step from an intermediate of central metabolism (α-ketoglutarate, pyruvate, oxaloacetate).

Animals excrete excess nitrogen in one of three forms, chosen by the availability of water:

| Excretory form | Animals | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{NH4+}$ (ammonotelic) | most aquatic animals, bony fishes | toxic, but diluted at once in the surrounding water |
| urea (ureotelic) | most terrestrial vertebrates, sharks | nontoxic, very soluble; costs ATP; needs water for excretion |
| uric acid (uricotelic) | birds, reptiles, insects | excreted as a semisolid paste, saving water (and weight in flight) |

In urea and uric acid the carbon atoms are highly oxidized: the organism discards carbon only after extracting most of its energy.

### 22.1.2. Digestion of Dietary Protein

Entry of protein into the stomach stimulates the release of gastrin, which induces the secretion of HCl by parietal cells and of pepsinogen by chief cells. The acidic gastric juice (pH 1.0–2.5) kills most bacteria and denatures proteins, and pepsinogen, a **zymogen**, activates itself at low pH to pepsin, which cleaves long chains into peptides. In the small intestine, secretin from the duodenum induces the pancreas to release bicarbonate, which raises the pH to about 7, and cholecystokinin the release of the pancreatic zymogens: trypsinogen, activated by enteropeptidase of the intestinal cells, and chymotrypsinogen, proelastase and procarboxypeptidases A and B, activated by trypsin ([7.1.9](/7.1.9#sec-9.5.4)). Because the proteases cleave different bonds (pepsin and chymotrypsin at aromatic residues, trypsin after Lys and Arg, elastase after small neutral residues; the table in [7.1.4](/7.1.4#sec-4.3.3)), and carboxypeptidases and the aminopeptidases of the brush border remove residues from the ends, the mixture is degraded efficiently to free amino acids and di- and tripeptides, which are taken up by the intestinal epithelium and carried by the portal blood to the liver. Synthesis as zymogens, and the pancreatic trypsin inhibitor, protect the pancreas from digesting itself; when the pancreatic duct is obstructed, zymogens are activated within the gland and cause acute pancreatitis.

### 22.1.3. Transamination and Pyridoxal Phosphate

The first step in the degradation of most amino acids, once they reach the liver, is the removal of the α-amino group.

**Definition 22.1.1 (Transamination)** **Transamination** is the transfer of the α-amino group of an amino acid to an α-keto acid, usually α-ketoglutarate, which gives the α-keto acid of the first amino acid and glutamate; it is catalyzed by **aminotransferases** (transaminases), which all contain **pyridoxal phosphate** as prosthetic group:

$$
\text{L-amino acid} + \alpha\text{-ketoglutarate} \rightleftharpoons \alpha\text{-keto acid} + \text{L-glutamate}. \tag{22.1}
$$

Transamination involves no net deamination: α-ketoglutarate is aminated as the other amino acid is deaminated. Its equilibrium constant is close to 1 ($\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G'^{\circ} \approx 0$), so the same enzymes serve in the synthesis of amino acids (7.1.27). Most aminotransferases accept many amino acids but are specific for α-ketoglutarate as acceptor; they are named for the amino group donor. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST, also AAT) are abundant in liver, heart and muscle; when these tissues are damaged, the enzymes leak into the blood, and their serum activities (and that of creatine kinase) measure the extent of liver damage or myocardial infarction.

Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP, [7.1.17](/7.1.17#sec-17.2.3)) is bound in the resting enzyme as an **imine** (Schiff base, the internal aldimine) with the ε-amino group of an active-site Lys. The incoming amino acid displaces the Lys by transimination and forms the external aldimine. The protonated pyridine ring, conjugated with the imine, is an electron sink: when the Lys, now a free base, removes the α-hydrogen, the negative charge is delocalized into the ring in the quinonoid intermediate. Reprotonation at C-4′ instead of C-α gives the ketimine, whose hydrolysis releases the α-keto acid and leaves pyridoxamine phosphate (PMP). A second α-keto acid, usually α-ketoglutarate, then binds, and the same steps in reverse transfer the amino group to it and regenerate PLP. The first product leaves before the second substrate binds: aminotransferases are the classic **ping-pong mechanism** ([7.1.9](/7.1.9#sec-9.2.1)), and PMP is the covalent intermediate that carries the amino group between the two half-reactions.

![The first half-reaction of transamination. The external aldimine, the Schiff base of the substrate amino acid with PLP, loses its α-hydrogen to the active-site Lys acting as a base; the electron pair flows through the imine into the protonated pyridine ring, which acts as an electron sink, giving the quinonoid intermediate. Reprotonation at C-4′ (by the Lys, now protonated) gives the ketimine, which is hydrolyzed to the α-keto acid and pyridoxamine phosphate (PMP). The second half-reaction runs the same steps in reverse with α-ketoglutarate, giving glutamate and regenerating PLP. R is the side chain of the amino acid](./plots/plp-transamination.py)

The same external aldimine is the starting point of all PLP reactions at the α, β and γ carbons of amino acids. Which bond to C-α breaks is decided by the enzyme, which orients that bond perpendicular to the conjugated π system (Dunathan's hypothesis, [7.1.17](/7.1.17#sec-17.2.3)): loss of H⁺ leads to transamination or racemization, loss of $\ce{CO2}$ to decarboxylation (biogenic amines, [7.1.27](/7.1.27)), loss of the side chain to aldol-type cleavage (serine hydroxymethyltransferase, 22.3.3); eliminations and replacements at the β and γ carbons (serine dehydratase, cystathionine β-synthase) use the same electron sink.

### 22.1.4. Glutamate Dehydrogenase: Oxidative Deamination

Glutamate formed by transamination in the cytosol of hepatocytes enters the mitochondria, where it gives up its amino group as ammonia.

**Definition 22.1.2 (Oxidative Deamination)** **Oxidative deamination** is the removal of an amino group from an amino acid as $\ce{NH4+}$ with oxidation of the carbon to a keto group; in mammals it is catalyzed mainly by L-glutamate dehydrogenase of the mitochondrial matrix, which uses either $\ce{NAD+}$ or $\ce{NADP+}$:

$$
\text{L-glutamate} + \ce{NAD(P)+} + \ce{H2O} \rightleftharpoons \alpha\text{-ketoglutarate} + \ce{NH4+} + \text{NAD(P)H} + \ce{H+}. \tag{22.2}
$$

The combined action of an aminotransferase and glutamate dehydrogenase, which converts the amino group of most amino acids into free ammonia, is called transdeamination. Glutamate dehydrogenase is an allosteric hexamer at the junction of carbon and nitrogen metabolism: ADP activates it and GTP inhibits it, so that a low energy charge accelerates the oxidation of amino acids; its α-ketoglutarate product can be oxidized as fuel or used for glucose synthesis. Mutations that abolish the inhibition by GTP cause the hyperinsulinism–hyperammonemia syndrome: in pancreatic β cells the overactive enzyme feeds α-ketoglutarate into the citric acid cycle after a protein meal, raises [ATP] and triggers inappropriate insulin secretion and hypoglycemia, while the liver releases excess ammonia. The reaction is reversible, and in the reverse direction (reductive amination) it is one of the routes by which ammonia enters amino acids (7.1.27). A few amino acids are deaminated directly: serine and threonine by PLP-dependent dehydratases (22.3.3), others by flavin-dependent amino acid oxidases.

### 22.1.5. Glutamine Carries Ammonia in the Blood

In extrahepatic tissues, including the brain, free ammonia from nucleotide degradation and other reactions is converted into a nontoxic carrier before it enters the blood: glutamine synthetase adds it to glutamate in two steps, through enzyme-bound γ-glutamyl phosphate, with one ATP:

$$
\text{glutamate} + \ce{NH4+} + \ce{ATP} \to \text{glutamine} + \ce{ADP} + \mathrm{P_i} + \ce{H+}. \tag{22.3}
$$

Glutamine is the most abundant amino acid in the blood. In the liver, the intestine and the kidney, glutaminase in the mitochondria hydrolyzes its amide to glutamate and $\ce{NH4+}$; the intestine uses glutamine as a major fuel and sends ammonia to the liver in the portal blood. In metabolic acidosis the kidney increases its uptake of glutamine and excretes the $\ce{NH4+}$ directly into the urine, where it carries metabolic acids out of the body as ammonium salts, and the α-ketoglutarate left over is metabolized to glucose and bicarbonate, which returns to the blood as base ([7.1.2](/7.1.2)). Glutamine synthetase is found in all organisms; in bacteria and plants it is the main entry point of fixed nitrogen into biomolecules, and its regulation is one of the classics of enzyme control (7.1.27).

### 22.1.6. Alanine Carries Nitrogen from Muscle to Liver

Vigorously working skeletal muscle produces pyruvate from glycolysis and ammonia from the breakdown of protein, in particular from the oxidation of branched-chain amino acids, which occurs mainly in muscle (22.3.7). Alanine aminotransferase transfers amino groups from glutamate to pyruvate, and alanine, rather than glutamine, carries most of the amino groups from muscle to liver.

**Definition 22.1.3 (Glucose–Alanine Cycle)** In the **glucose–alanine cycle**, skeletal muscle transaminates pyruvate to alanine, which travels in the blood to the liver; there alanine aminotransferase returns pyruvate, used for gluconeogenesis, and glutamate, whose amino group is converted to urea; the glucose returns to the muscle.

The cycle carries both nitrogen and carbon. It complements the **Cori cycle** (lactate from muscle to liver, glucose back; [7.1.19](/7.1.19)), and like it moves the energetic burden of gluconeogenesis, and here also of urea synthesis, from the muscle, which devotes its ATP to contraction, to the liver.

### 22.1.7. Ammonia Is Toxic

Plasma ammonia is kept at about 20–40 µM. Ammonia is a weak base: with $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} = 9.25$ at $25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (about 9.1 at $37\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$), the **Henderson–Hasselbalch equation** gives $[\ce{NH3}]/[\ce{NH4+}] = 10^{7.4 - 9.1} \approx 0.02$ in blood: about 98% is $\ce{NH4+}$, which crosses membranes poorly, but the 2% of $\ce{NH3}$ diffuses freely, including across the blood–brain barrier, and is trapped as $\ce{NH4+}$ inside cells. The brain is especially sensitive. Detoxification in the brain consumes α-ketoglutarate (reductive amination) and glutamate (glutamine synthetase, found only in astrocytes), which may deplete the citric acid cycle and the neurotransmitters glutamate and GABA; but this is not enough to explain the toxicity. $\ce{NH4+}$ also competes with $\ce{K+}$ for the $\ce{Na+}/\ce{K+}$-ATPase of astrocytes, extracellular $\ce{K+}$ rises and enters neurons through the $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{K+}$–2$\ce{Cl-}$ cotransporter NKCC1, and the excess $\ce{Cl-}$ makes $\mathrm{GABA_A}$ receptors depolarizing instead of inhibitory, which accounts for the incoordination and seizures of ammonia poisoning. Glutamine accumulating in astrocytes swells them, and the resulting brain edema can be fatal. Hyperammonemia from liver failure (hepatic encephalopathy) or from defects of the urea cycle (22.2.5) causes lethargy, confusion and coma.

## 22.2. Nitrogen Excretion and the Urea Cycle

### 22.2.1. Urea Is Made in Five Steps

Hans Krebs and Kurt Henseleit discovered in 1932, five years before the citric acid cycle, that liver slices make urea from ammonia far faster when ornithine, citrulline or arginine is added, in catalytic amounts: urea is made in a cycle.

**Definition 22.2.1 (Urea Cycle)** The **urea cycle** is the cyclic pathway of the liver, spanning the mitochondrial matrix and the cytosol, in which ornithine carries the carbamoyl group from carbamoyl phosphate and the amino group of aspartate through citrulline and argininosuccinate to arginine, whose hydrolysis releases urea and regenerates ornithine. One nitrogen of urea comes from $\ce{NH4+}$, the other from aspartate, and the carbon from bicarbonate.

**Definition 22.2.2 (Carbamoyl Phosphate)** **Carbamoyl phosphate** ($\ce{H2N-CO-OPO3^2-}$), a mixed anhydride of carbamic and phosphoric acid with a high group transfer potential, is the activated donor of carbamoyl groups; it is made by carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I in the mitochondria of the liver (for urea) and by carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II in the cytosol of all cells (for pyrimidines, [7.1.28](/7.1.28)).

The five enzymes, two in the matrix and three in the cytosol:

| Step | Enzyme | Location | Reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS I) | matrix | $\ce{NH4+}$ + $\ce{HCO3-}$ + 2 ATP → carbamoyl phosphate + 2 ADP + $\mathrm{P_i}$ |
| 1 | ornithine transcarbamoylase (OTC) | matrix | ornithine + carbamoyl phosphate → citrulline + $\mathrm{P_i}$ |
| 2 | argininosuccinate synthetase (ASS) | cytosol | citrulline + aspartate + ATP → argininosuccinate + AMP + $\mathrm{PP_i}$ |
| 3 | argininosuccinate lyase (ASL, argininosuccinase) | cytosol | argininosuccinate ⇌ arginine + fumarate |
| 4 | arginase | cytosol | arginine + $\ce{H2O}$ → urea + ornithine |

CPS I activates bicarbonate and ammonia with two ATP in three steps: ATP phosphorylates bicarbonate to carboxyphosphate; ammonia attacks it, displacing $\mathrm{P_i}$ to give carbamate; a second ATP phosphorylates carbamate to carbamoyl phosphate. OTC transfers the carbamoyl group to the δ-amino group of ornithine, a nonprotein amino acid that plays the role of oxaloacetate in the citric acid cycle: it accepts material at each turn and is regenerated. Citrulline leaves the mitochondrion in exchange for ornithine (the ornithine–citrulline antiporter ORNT1). Argininosuccinate synthetase activates the ureido oxygen of citrulline as citrullyl-AMP, which aspartate displaces; this brings in the second nitrogen. Argininosuccinate lyase eliminates fumarate, the only reversible step of the cycle, and arginase hydrolyzes arginine to urea and ornithine. The enzymes appear to be organized as a metabolon: citrulline, argininosuccinate and arginine are channeled from enzyme to enzyme, and only urea is released into the cytosol. Urea passes into the blood and is excreted by the kidney.

### 22.2.2. The Urea Cycle and the Citric Acid Cycle Are Linked

Fumarate released by argininosuccinate lyase is an intermediate of the citric acid cycle; in the cytosol, fumarase and malate dehydrogenase (cytosolic isozymes) convert it to malate and oxaloacetate, and aspartate aminotransferase regenerates aspartate with an amino group from glutamate. Malate can also enter the mitochondria for the citric acid cycle, and aspartate made from oxaloacetate in the matrix can leave on the glutamate–aspartate antiporter. These reactions, the aspartate–argininosuccinate shunt, connect the fates of the amino groups and of the carbon skeletons; Krebs's two cycles joined this way have been called the "Krebs bicycle". Using aspartate as nitrogen donor may look roundabout, but it is one of the two general ways in which amino groups enter biomolecules (the other being the amide of glutamine; 7.1.27, 7.1.28), and it ties urea synthesis to the malate–aspartate shuttle that brings reducing equivalents into the mitochondria ([7.1.23](/7.1.23#sec-23.3.7)).

In the matrix, carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS I), activated by N-acetylglutamate, forms carbamoyl phosphate from NH₄⁺, bicarbonate and 2 ATP, and ornithine transcarbamoylase (OTC) transfers the carbamoyl group to ornithine. Citrulline is exported in exchange for ornithine (ORNT1). Fumarate is hydrated to malate and oxidized to oxaloacetate with formation of NADH, and oxaloacetate is transaminated back to aspartate (aspartate–argininosuccinate shunt); the net reaction costs four high-energy phosphates](./plots/urea-cycle.py)

### 22.2.3. The Energy Cost

Considered alone, the cycle consumes three ATP and four high-energy phosphate bonds per urea (two ATP → ADP for carbamoyl phosphate, one ATP → AMP + $\mathrm{PP_i}$, with the $\mathrm{PP_i}$ hydrolyzed):

$$
2\,\ce{NH4+} + \ce{HCO3-} + 3\,\ce{ATP^4-} + \ce{H2O} \to \text{urea} + 2\,\ce{ADP^3-} + 4\,\mathrm{P_i^{2-}} + \ce{AMP^2-} + 5\,\ce{H+}, \tag{22.4}
$$
where one of the two nitrogens enters as aspartate made by transamination. The cost is offset by the oxidations that accompany it: the fumarate returned as oxaloacetate yields one NADH in the malate dehydrogenase reaction, and the release of the ammonia by glutamate dehydrogenase another NAD(P)H; each NADH can give about 2.5 ATP in oxidative phosphorylation ([7.1.23](/7.1.23)). In the overall metabolism of an amino acid the urea cycle is therefore nearly energy-neutral, but the nitrogen of urea is lost, and the cost of disposing of it is one reason why protein is a less efficient fuel than carbohydrate and fat (Question 3).

### 22.2.4. Regulation

The flux through the cycle varies with the diet: it rises after high-protein meals and in starvation, when muscle protein provides fuel, and falls on a protein-free diet. Two mechanisms adjust it.

- Slow: the amounts of all five enzymes rise in starvation and on very-high-protein diets, by induction of their synthesis by glucagon and glucocorticoids.
- Fast: CPS I is allosterically activated by N-acetylglutamate, without which it is inactive. N-Acetylglutamate synthase makes it from acetyl-CoA and glutamate, and is activated by arginine. When amino acids are being degraded, the concentrations of glutamate and arginine rise, more N-acetylglutamate is made, and the entry of ammonia into the cycle accelerates. (In plants and bacteria N-acetylglutamate synthase catalyzes the first step of arginine synthesis; in mammals, which lack the rest of that pathway, its role is purely regulatory.)

### 22.2.5. Genetic Defects of the Urea Cycle

Inherited defects of each of the five enzymes and of N-acetylglutamate synthase are known (OTC deficiency, X-linked, is the most common). Affected infants often appear normal at birth and develop hyperammonemia within days, with lethargy, vomiting, hyperventilation and cerebral edema; without treatment they die or suffer brain damage. Because most steps are irreversible, the defective enzyme can usually be identified from the intermediate that accumulates in the blood or urine (citrulline, argininosuccinate, arginine, or orotic acid when carbamoyl phosphate overflows into pyrimidine synthesis in OTC deficiency). A protein-free diet is impossible, because nine amino acids cannot be made by humans:

| Nonessential | Conditionally essential | Essential |
|---|---|---|
| alanine, asparagine, aspartate, glutamate, serine | arginine, cysteine, glutamine, glycine, proline, tyrosine | histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine |

(Lehninger 8e, Table 18-1; conditionally essential amino acids are required in growing children or in illness.) Treatment exploits the biochemistry of the pathway:

- dietary control: protein limited to the requirement, sometimes with essential amino acids supplied as their α-keto acids, which are transaminated with the excess nitrogen;
- benzoate and phenylbutyrate: benzoate is activated to benzoyl-CoA and conjugated with glycine to hippurate; phenylbutyrate is converted by β oxidation to phenylacetate, whose CoA ester is conjugated with glutamine to phenylacetylglutamine; both are excreted, and the glycine and glutamine are made again from ammonia;
- arginine, which becomes essential when the cycle is blocked, is supplemented in deficiencies of OTC, ASS and ASL; in ASL deficiency it lets the cycle run as far as argininosuccinate, which is excreted with two nitrogens; in arginase deficiency, by contrast, arginine must be restricted;
- N-carbamoylglutamate, an analog of N-acetylglutamate that activates CPS I, treats N-acetylglutamate synthase deficiency;
- liver transplantation cures the disease, and gene therapy for OTC deficiency is in clinical trials.

## 22.3. Pathways of Amino Acid Degradation

### 22.3.1. Six Entry Points; Glucogenic and Ketogenic Amino Acids

The twenty degradative pathways converge on six products, all of which enter the citric acid cycle: pyruvate, acetyl-CoA (directly or through acetoacetyl-CoA, which the course slides count as a seventh), α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, fumarate and oxaloacetate. Some amino acids appear in two groups, because different parts of their skeletons reach different entry points.

**Definition 22.3.1 (Glucogenic and Ketogenic Amino Acids)** An amino acid whose carbon skeleton is degraded, wholly or in part, to pyruvate or to an intermediate of the citric acid cycle (α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, fumarate, oxaloacetate) is **glucogenic**: these carbons can be converted to glucose through oxaloacetate and phosphoenolpyruvate. An amino acid degraded, wholly or in part, to acetyl-CoA or acetoacetyl-CoA is **ketogenic**: in the liver these carbons can form ketone bodies, but because the pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction is irreversible, animals cannot convert them into glucose.

| Entry point | Amino acids | Glucogenic or ketogenic |
|---|---|---|
| pyruvate | Ala, Cys, Gly, Ser, Thr, Trp | glucogenic |
| acetyl-CoA, acetoacetyl-CoA | Ile, Leu, Lys, Phe, Thr, Trp, Tyr | ketogenic |
| α-ketoglutarate | Arg, Gln, Glu, His, Pro | glucogenic |
| succinyl-CoA | Ile, Met, Thr, Val | glucogenic |
| fumarate | Phe, Tyr | glucogenic |
| oxaloacetate | Asn, Asp | glucogenic |

Only leucine and lysine are exclusively ketogenic; five (Phe, Tyr, Trp, Thr, Ile) are both; the other thirteen are only glucogenic. In starvation and uncontrolled diabetes the ketogenic amino acids add to the production of **ketone bodies**, and leucine, common in proteins, contributes substantially. (Pyruvate can also give acetyl-CoA, so the amino acids degraded to pyruvate are potentially ketogenic as well.)

![The carbon skeletons of the 20 amino acids enter central metabolism at six points. Pyruvate (A, C, G, S, T, W) is decarboxylated to acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex or carboxylated to oxaloacetate; acetoacetyl-CoA (F, K, L, W, Y) is cleaved by thiolase to acetyl-CoA; acetyl-CoA (I, L, T, W) condenses with oxaloacetate to citrate; α-ketoglutarate (E, H, P, Q, R), succinyl-CoA (I, M, T, V), fumarate (F, Y) and oxaloacetate (D, N) are intermediates of the citric acid cycle. Amino acids are coloured by fate: glucogenic (their carbons can become glucose through oxaloacetate, phosphoenolpyruvate and gluconeogenesis), ketogenic (only acetyl-CoA or acetoacetyl-CoA: ketone bodies or fatty acids) and both; leucine and lysine are the only purely ketogenic amino acids](./plots/amino-acid-skeletons.py)

The pathways look complicated, but they reuse a small set of reactions: transamination (PLP); oxidative decarboxylation of α-keto acids by dehydrogenase complexes like pyruvate dehydrogenase; the sequence of the β oxidation of fatty acids (dehydrogenation with FAD, hydration, dehydrogenation with NAD, thiolysis, [7.1.17](/7.1.17#sec-17.1.3)) for the skeletons of leucine, isoleucine, valine and lysine; carboxylation (biotin); and one-carbon transfers.

### 22.3.2. One-Carbon Carriers and Other Cofactors

Three cofactors carry one-carbon groups at three oxidation levels ([7.1.17](/7.1.17#sec-17.2.4)): biotin carries $\ce{CO2}$, the most oxidized; **tetrahydrofolate** carries intermediate oxidation states on N-5, N-10 or both; and **S-adenosylmethionine** carries methyl groups, the most reduced.

| Oxidation level | One-carbon unit on THF | Sources and uses |
|---|---|---|
| methanol | $N^5$-methyl-THF | made irreversibly by reduction of methylene-THF; used only by methionine synthase |
| formaldehyde | $N^5,N^{10}$-methylene-THF | from serine (serine hydroxymethyltransferase) and glycine (glycine cleavage); used for thymidylate (7.1.28) |
| formate | $N^5,N^{10}$-methenyl-, $N^{10}$-formyl-, $N^5$-formimino-THF | from histidine (formimino), formate; $N^{10}$-formyl-THF for purines (7.1.28) and formylmethionine |

The main source of one-carbon units is the conversion of serine to glycine. The interconversions are reversible except the reduction to $N^5$-methyl-THF.

S-Adenosylmethionine is made from methionine and ATP by methionine adenosyltransferase, an unusual reaction in which the sulfur attacks C-5′ of ATP and releases triphosphate (cleaved to $\mathrm{P_i}$ and $\mathrm{PP_i}$). After donating its methyl group, which is about 1000 times more reactive than that of methyl-THF, it becomes S-adenosylhomocysteine, which is hydrolyzed to adenosine and homocysteine. Methionine synthase remethylates homocysteine with $N^5$-methyl-THF through methylcobalamin, completing the activated methyl cycle. The cycle links two vitamins. In vitamin B12 deficiency (pernicious anemia, from failure to absorb the vitamin), methionine synthase stops, and folate accumulates as $N^5$-methyl-THF, which no other enzyme uses (the "methyl trap"); methylene-THF for thymidylate synthesis runs short and DNA synthesis in the bone marrow fails, causing megaloblastic anemia. Folate alone corrects the anemia but not the neurological damage, which comes from the second $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ enzyme, methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (22.3.6); the two vitamins are therefore given together until the cause is known. Folate deficiency, or defects of the remethylation, raise plasma homocysteine, a risk factor for cardiovascular disease and neural tube defects.

Tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄), a reduced pterin like the one in folate, is not a one-carbon carrier but a reductant of $\ce{O2}$ in the aromatic amino acid hydroxylases (22.3.4).

### 22.3.3. Six Amino Acids Are Degraded to Pyruvate

- Alanine gives pyruvate directly by transamination.
- Serine is converted to pyruvate and $\ce{NH4+}$ in one PLP-dependent step by serine dehydratase, which eliminates the β-hydroxyl group and the α-amino group (an elimination at C-β, then hydrolysis of the resulting enamine–imine).
- Glycine is degraded by three routes. Serine hydroxymethyltransferase (PLP and THF) adds a hydroxymethyl group from methylene-THF to give serine, reversibly. The glycine cleavage system, the major route in animals, oxidizes glycine to $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{NH4+}$ and methylene-THF in the mitochondria; it is a multienzyme complex (proteins P, H, T and L, with PLP, lipoate, THF and FAD/NAD), and its genetic deficiency causes nonketotic hyperglycinemia, with severe neurological damage (glycine is an inhibitory neurotransmitter). D-Amino acid oxidase of the kidney, which detoxifies D-amino acids from bacterial cell walls and cooked food, also oxidizes glycine to glyoxylate, which is oxidized further to oxalate, the main constituent of kidney stones (calcium oxalate).
- Threonine is degraded mainly to succinyl-CoA in humans (22.3.6); a minor route through threonine dehydrogenase gives 2-amino-3-ketobutyrate, then glycine and acetyl-CoA.
- Cysteine loses its sulfur (as sulfite, sulfate or $\ce{H2S}$, by several routes) and is transaminated to pyruvate.
- Tryptophan gives alanine (and hence pyruvate) from its side chain, and acetoacetyl-CoA from the indole ring (22.3.4).

### 22.3.4. Seven Amino Acids Give Acetyl-CoA; Phenylalanine and Its Diseases

Portions of the skeletons of tryptophan, lysine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, leucine, isoleucine and threonine yield acetyl-CoA or acetoacetyl-CoA. The final steps for leucine, lysine and tryptophan resemble β oxidation. Tryptophan breakdown is the most complex of all: the indole ring is opened by tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (liver) or indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (immune cells, which use the resulting depletion of tryptophan and the kynurenine metabolites to suppress T cells, a target in cancer immunotherapy); four of its carbons end in acetoacetyl-CoA. Its intermediates are precursors of nicotinate, from which humans can make part of their NAD (so that pellagra results from a combined lack of niacin and tryptophan), of the neurotransmitter serotonin, and in plants of the growth hormone indole-3-acetate (auxin, [7.1.15](/7.1.15)).

Phenylalanine and tyrosine (nine carbons each) are degraded to fumarate (four carbons) and acetoacetate (four carbons); the ninth carbon is lost as $\ce{CO2}$. The pathway is the best-known source of inborn errors.

1. Phenylalanine hydroxylase (phenylalanine 4-monooxygenase) hydroxylates phenylalanine to tyrosine. It is a mixed-function oxygenase: one atom of $\ce{O2}$ goes to the substrate, the other is reduced to water by tetrahydrobiopterin, which is oxidized to dihydrobiopterin and reduced again by dihydropteridine reductase with NADPH. A hydrogen at C-4 migrates to C-3 (the "NIH shift", discovered at the National Institutes of Health).
2. Tyrosine aminotransferase gives p-hydroxyphenylpyruvate, which p-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase oxidizes, decarboxylates and rearranges to homogentisate.
3. Homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase opens the ring to maleylacetoacetate, which is isomerized to fumarylacetoacetate and hydrolyzed by fumarylacetoacetase to fumarate and acetoacetate.

Tyrosine is also the precursor of dopamine, norepinephrine and epinephrine, of the thyroid hormones and of melanin (7.1.27).

**Definition 22.3.2 (Inborn Error of Metabolism)** An **inborn error of metabolism** is an inherited disease caused by the deficiency of a single enzyme (or transporter or cofactor) of a metabolic pathway, which leads to the accumulation of the substrate of the defective step or its byproducts and to the lack of its product.

The term is Archibald Garrod's (1908), who recognized from family studies that alkaptonuria is inherited as a recessive trait and inferred that it is due to the absence of a single enzyme, the first link between a gene and an enzyme, decades before the one gene–one enzyme hypothesis (7.4).

**Definition 22.3.3 (Phenylketonuria and Alkaptonuria)** **Phenylketonuria** (PKU) is the inborn error of phenylalanine hydroxylase (or, rarely, of the synthesis or regeneration of tetrahydrobiopterin) in which phenylalanine accumulates in the blood and tissues (hyperphenylalaninemia) and is transaminated to phenylpyruvate, which, with phenylacetate and phenyllactate, is excreted in the urine. **Alkaptonuria** is the inborn error of homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase in which homogentisate is excreted and darkens the urine on oxidation.

PKU was discovered in 1934 by Asbjørn Følling, who found that the urine of intellectually disabled siblings turned green with ferric chloride (the test for phenylpyruvate); its musty odor comes from phenylacetate. It occurs in about 1 in 10 000 births in populations of European ancestry. Untreated, high phenylalanine impairs brain development, probably because it competes with other large neutral amino acids for the transporter of the blood–brain barrier and so starves the brain of tyrosine and tryptophan, the precursors of dopamine and serotonin. Newborn screening (a drop of blood, now measured by tandem mass spectrometry) and a diet low in phenylalanine from the first weeks of life prevent the damage; protein must be given as mixtures of amino acids free of phenylalanine, and aspartame (Asp–Phe methyl ester) must be avoided. Some patients with residual enzyme respond to large doses of tetrahydrobiopterin (sapropterin), which stabilizes the mutant enzyme; since 2018, injection of PEGylated phenylalanine ammonia lyase (pegvaliase), a plant and bacterial enzyme that converts phenylalanine to trans-cinnamate and ammonia, allows a less strict diet. The defects of biopterin metabolism also deprive tyrosine and tryptophan hydroxylases of their cofactor, so L-dopa and 5-hydroxytryptophan must be given in addition.

With $K_{\mathrm{M}} = 300\,\mu\mathrm{M}$, $V_{\max} = 57.6\,\mu\mathrm{M\,h^{-1}}$, $k = 0.0067\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$ and $I = 10\,\mu\mathrm{M\,h^{-1}}$, the steady state (found by solving the quadratic, or numerically with `brentq`, [4.4.4](/4.4.4)) is $60\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ for $f = 1$; it is $270\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ for $f = 0.3$, but $860$ for $f = 0.1$ and $1420\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ for $f = 0.01$. Because the hydroxylase saturates, once $fV_{\max}$ falls to the order of $I$ the enzyme cannot keep up, and $F$ rises toward $I/k \approx 1500\,\mu\mathrm{M}$, set by the slow route. Halving the residual activity at high $f$ changes little; at low $f$ the patient is effectively without the enzyme. Lowering $I$ to $2\,\mu\mathrm{M\,h^{-1}}$ brings the steady state with $f = 0.01$ to $260\,\mu\mathrm{M}$, within the treatment target of 120–360 µM; the time constant $1/k \approx 6$ days explains why phenylalanine rises over the first week of life, after the mother's enzyme no longer clears it through the placenta, and why screening on day 2–3 detects the disease before damage.

Alkaptonuria is mild: homogentisate is excreted and its oxidation turns the urine black on standing; in middle age, its polymer deposits in cartilage cause ochronosis and arthritis. Nitisinone, an inhibitor of p-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase, lowers homogentisate. The same drug treats tyrosinemia type I, a severe defect of fumarylacetoacetase in which the accumulated fumarylacetoacetate is converted to toxic succinylacetone that damages liver and kidney: blocking the pathway upstream of the defect, at the price of a diet low in tyrosine and phenylalanine, prevents the toxic products, an instructive application of pathway logic. Albinism results from defects of tyrosinase, which begins the synthesis of melanin.

### 22.3.5. Five Amino Acids Are Converted to α-Ketoglutarate

Proline, glutamate and glutamine have five-carbon skeletons. Proline is oxidized at C-5 to a cyclic Schiff base, which opens by hydrolysis to glutamate γ-semialdehyde, oxidized further to glutamate; glutaminase or any amidotransferase converts glutamine to glutamate; transamination or glutamate dehydrogenase gives α-ketoglutarate. Arginine and histidine have five adjacent carbons and a sixth attached through nitrogen. Arginase converts arginine to ornithine (22.2.1), which is transaminated at C-5 to glutamate γ-semialdehyde. Histidine is deaminated by histidine ammonia lyase to urocanate, which is hydrated and its ring opened to N-formiminoglutamate (FIGLU); the formimino group is transferred to tetrahydrofolate, leaving glutamate. Excretion of FIGLU after a histidine load is a test for folate deficiency.

### 22.3.6. Four Amino Acids Are Converted to Succinyl-CoA

Methionine, isoleucine, threonine and valine all give propionyl-CoA, which is carboxylated (biotin) to D-methylmalonyl-CoA, epimerized, and rearranged to succinyl-CoA by methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, a coenzyme $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ enzyme ([7.1.17](/7.1.17#sec-17.2.5); the same route handles odd-chain fatty acids, [7.1.21](/7.1.21)). Methionine donates its methyl group through S-adenosylmethionine; homocysteine condenses with serine to cystathionine (cystathionine β-synthase, PLP), which is cleaved to cysteine and α-ketobutyrate (so that the sulfur of cysteine comes from methionine), and α-ketobutyrate is oxidatively decarboxylated to propionyl-CoA. Isoleucine is transaminated, oxidatively decarboxylated, and degraded by β-oxidation-like steps to acetyl-CoA and propionyl-CoA; valine, by similar steps, to propionyl-CoA alone. In human tissues threonine is mainly converted by a dehydratase (like serine dehydratase) to α-ketobutyrate and hence to propionyl-CoA.

Deficiency of methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, or of the synthesis of its coenzyme, causes methylmalonic acidemia (about 1 in 50 000 births), with vomiting, convulsions, intellectual disability and, untreated, early death. Lehninger recounts a case that shows the practical importance of this biochemistry: in 1989 a mother in St. Louis was convicted of poisoning her infant son with ethylene glycol on the basis of laboratory analyses; a second son developed the same symptoms and was diagnosed with methylmalonic acidemia, and biochemists who reanalyzed the samples found methylmalonate and no ethylene glycol, which the original assays had confused. She was released in 1991. Homocystinuria, from cystathionine β-synthase deficiency, causes lens dislocation, skeletal abnormalities, thrombosis and intellectual disability; some patients respond to vitamin $\mathrm{B_6}$.

### 22.3.7. Branched-Chain Amino Acids Are Degraded Outside the Liver

Unlike other amino acids, leucine, isoleucine and valine are oxidized mainly in muscle, adipose tissue, kidney and brain, because the liver lacks the branched-chain aminotransferase that starts their degradation; their nitrogen leaves muscle as alanine and glutamine (22.1.6). The resulting α-keto acids are oxidatively decarboxylated to acyl-CoA derivatives by the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase complex, which is homologous to the **pyruvate dehydrogenase complex** and the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex ([7.1.20](/7.1.20)), uses the same five cofactors (TPP, lipoate, CoA, FAD, NAD) and the same mechanism: an enzyme machine that evolved for one reaction was duplicated and adapted to others. Like pyruvate dehydrogenase, it is regulated by phosphorylation: a specific kinase inactivates it when branched-chain amino acids are scarce, and a phosphatase activates it when they are in excess.

**Definition 22.3.4 (Maple Syrup Urine Disease)** **Maple syrup urine disease** (branched-chain ketoaciduria) is the inborn error of the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase complex, in which leucine, isoleucine and valine and their α-keto acids accumulate in the blood and urine, giving the urine the smell of maple syrup.

Untreated, it causes brain edema, convulsions and death in infancy; treatment is a diet that provides just enough of the three amino acids for growth, or liver transplantation, which supplies enough enzyme activity even though muscle is the main site of their oxidation.

### 22.3.8. Asparagine and Aspartate Are Degraded to Oxaloacetate

Asparaginase hydrolyzes asparagine to aspartate, which is transaminated to oxaloacetate (in mammals the oxaloacetate is reduced to malate in the cytosol and enters the mitochondria as malate). Bacterial L-asparaginase is a drug for acute lymphoblastic leukemia: the leukemic cells have little asparagine synthetase and depend on asparagine from the blood, which the enzyme depletes.

## Summary

The pathway card of the urea cycle ([7.1.16](/7.1.16#sec-16.1.3)):

| Field | Urea cycle |
|---|---|
| Location | liver (periportal hepatocytes); CPS I and OTC in the mitochondrial matrix, ASS, ASL and arginase in the cytosol |
| Net reaction | 2 $\ce{NH4+}$ (one as aspartate) + $\ce{HCO3-}$ + 3 ATP + $\ce{H2O}$ → urea + 2 ADP + AMP + 4 $\mathrm{P_i}$ (Eq. (22.4)) |
| Energy account | 4 high-energy phosphates per urea; about 2 × 2.5 ATP recovered from the NADH of malate dehydrogenase and glutamate dehydrogenase |
| Committed and regulated steps | CPS I (committed, irreversible); ASL is the only reversible step |
| Activators | N-acetylglutamate (obligatory activator of CPS I); arginine (activates N-acetylglutamate synthase) |
| Inhibitors | lack of N-acetylglutamate (low acetyl-CoA, glutamate, arginine) |
| Covalent, hormonal and transcriptional control | induction of all five enzymes by glucagon and glucocorticoids in starvation and on high-protein diets |
| Opposing pathway | none; the cycle intersects the citric acid cycle through fumarate and aspartate (Krebs bicycle), CPS II begins pyrimidine synthesis |
| Coenzymes (vitamins) | none in the cycle itself; PLP ($\mathrm{B_6}$) for the aminotransferases that supply aspartate and glutamate |
| Diseases and drugs | deficiencies of all five enzymes and of N-acetylglutamate synthase (OTC deficiency X-linked); benzoate, phenylbutyrate, arginine, N-carbamoylglutamate; hepatic encephalopathy |

## Questions

1. Write the products of transamination with α-ketoglutarate of (a) aspartate, (b) alanine, (c) phenylalanine, (d) glutamate. Why does aspartate aminotransferase have the highest activity among liver aminotransferases?

   **Answer** (a) Oxaloacetate and glutamate; (b) pyruvate and glutamate; (c) phenylpyruvate and glutamate; (d) α-ketoglutarate and glutamate (no net change, an isotope exchange). Aspartate aminotransferase supplies the aspartate that brings the second nitrogen into urea (argininosuccinate synthetase) and runs the malate–aspartate shuttle in both directions; the flux through it must equal the flux of urea synthesis plus that of the shuttle, so the enzyme is present in large excess and keeps glutamate, aspartate and their keto acids near equilibrium.

2. Alanine aminotransferase activity in serum is measured with excess lactate dehydrogenase and NADH, by following the decrease of absorbance at 340 nm. Explain the assay.

   **Answer** ALT converts alanine and α-ketoglutarate to pyruvate and glutamate. Lactate dehydrogenase, in excess, immediately reduces each pyruvate to lactate with one NADH, so the rate of NADH oxidation, followed at 340 nm where NADH absorbs and $\ce{NAD+}$ does not ([7.1.16](/7.1.16#sec-16.5.1)), equals the rate of the transamination. The coupled assay works because the coupling enzyme is not rate-limiting and the indicator reaction is effectively irreversible.

3. Lactate and alanine have carbon skeletons at the same oxidation level. Compare the net ATP yield from the complete oxidation of one lactate and one alanine when the cost of excreting the nitrogen as urea is included (use P/O ratios of 2.5 and 1.5).

   **Answer** Lactate → pyruvate gives one NADH (2.5 ATP); pyruvate → acetyl-CoA one NADH (2.5); the citric acid cycle 3 NADH, 1 $\ce{FADH2}$ and 1 GTP (7.5 + 1.5 + 1 = 10): 15 ATP. Alanine is transaminated to pyruvate (no energy), and the glutamate formed releases its nitrogen as $\ce{NH4+}$ with one NADH (2.5 ATP) in glutamate dehydrogenase, so the skeleton gives 12.5 + 2.5 = 15 ATP. Half a urea (one nitrogen) costs 2 high-energy phosphates, partly recovered: the fumarate formed per urea returns as oxaloacetate with one NADH (2.5 ATP per urea, 1.25 per nitrogen). Net for alanine: 15 − 2 + 1.25 ≈ 14.3 ATP, a little less than lactate. The cost of nitrogen disposal is modest; most of the energy of the skeleton is recovered.

4. A child with a deficiency of argininosuccinate lyase is treated with a low-protein diet supplemented with arginine. Explain why arginine is useful, and why it would be harmful in arginase deficiency.

   **Answer** With argininosuccinate lyase blocked, the cycle cannot regenerate ornithine through arginine; ornithine is depleted and carbamoyl phosphate can no longer be consumed, so ammonia accumulates. Arginine supplied in the diet is hydrolyzed by arginase to ornithine (and urea), which restores the supply of ornithine; each turn now fixes one $\ce{NH4+}$ (via carbamoyl phosphate) and one aspartate nitrogen into argininosuccinate, which cannot be cleaved but is excreted in the urine, carrying two nitrogen atoms out. In arginase deficiency arginine itself accumulates (argininemia, with spasticity and intellectual disability); giving more would worsen this, so arginine is restricted.

5. Use the model of Example 22.3.1 to estimate the residual activity $f$ above which a patient on a normal diet keeps plasma phenylalanine below $360\,\mu\mathrm{M}$.

   **Answer** At steady state $I = fV_{\max}F/(K_{\mathrm{M}} + F) + kF$. With $F = 360\,\mu\mathrm{M}$: $kF = 0.0067 \times 360 = 2.4$, so the hydroxylase must remove $10 - 2.4 = 7.6\,\mu\mathrm{M\,h^{-1}}$, and $f = 7.6/(57.6 \times 360/660) = 7.6/31.4 = 0.24$. With about a quarter of normal activity the patient needs no diet (mild hyperphenylalaninemia); below that, plasma phenylalanine rises steeply. This agrees with the clinical observation that heterozygotes (50%) are normal and that patients with several percent of residual activity still need treatment.

6. (Open question) Plants almost never oxidize amino acids for energy, and many animals excrete uric acid rather than urea. Discuss the costs and benefits of the three ways of disposing of nitrogen (ammonia, urea, uric acid) in terms of energy, water and toxicity, and explain why a tadpole excretes ammonia and the adult frog urea.

   **Answer** Outline: ammonia costs no energy and carries one N per molecule, but is toxic at micromolar levels and needs much water to dilute; it suits animals surrounded by water (fishes, tadpoles). Urea costs four high-energy phosphates per two N (partly recovered), is nontoxic and very soluble but must be dissolved in urine, which costs water; it suits terrestrial animals with access to water and allows nitrogen to be stored (sharks retain urea as an osmolyte). Uric acid (four N per molecule) is made by the costlier purine pathway (7.1.28) but is nearly insoluble, so it can be excreted as a paste with little water and stored in the egg without poisoning the embryo; it suits birds, reptiles and insects. At metamorphosis the frog moves onto land and induces the enzymes of the urea cycle (a classic experiment showing hormonal induction of CPS I and the other enzymes by thyroid hormone). Plants avoid the problem by keeping their amino acids for biosynthesis and recycling nitrogen, which is usually the limiting nutrient for their growth.
