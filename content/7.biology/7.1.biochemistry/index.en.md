Biochemistry describes life in the language of chemistry. It asks two questions of the molecules that all organisms share. The first is structural: how do a few classes of compounds in water, amino acids and **proteins**, carbohydrates, nucleotides and lipids, acquire shapes that let them bind, catalyze, transport and signal? The second is metabolic: how does a cell run thousands of reactions at once, extracting energy from fuels and spending it on **biosynthesis**, while it stays far from equilibrium and keeps every pathway under control? The course follows the first two parts of Lehninger, structure and catalysis, and bioenergetics and **metabolism**; the third part, the storage and transmission of genetic information, is the subject of [molecular biology](/7.2).

Each half has one thread that organizes its detail. In the structural half the thread runs from chemical properties to structure to function. It starts with water and the **weak interactions** that build every macromolecular structure, goes through the side chains of the amino acids to the purification, folding and three-dimensional structure of proteins, and arrives at what proteins do: binding, as in myoglobin, hemoglobin and antibodies, and catalysis by **enzymes**, with their mechanisms, kinetics and regulation. Binding and catalysis are treated in one quantitative language of saturable, more or less steep response curves, and the same language then carries the other classes of biomolecules, membranes and transport, and finally **signal transduction**, which turns out to be built from binding and catalysis. The metabolic half is organized by energy balance and feedback control. It begins with **bioenergetics** and the chemical logic of metabolism: a small number of reaction classes, the carriers ATP and NAD(P)H, and a few steps far from equilibrium at which pathways are committed and regulated. The pathways then follow Lehninger's order, with the breakdown and the synthesis of the same class of compounds close together: glycolysis beside gluconeogenesis and glycogen metabolism, the **citric acid cycle**, the oxidation of fatty acids and amino acids, **oxidative phosphorylation** and **photosynthesis** as the two energy-transducing membranes, the biosynthesis of lipids, amino acids and nucleotides, and lastly the hormonal integration of mammalian metabolism. Every pathway is summarized in the same form (location, net reaction, energy account, regulated steps and their effectors), and the course summary joins them into one map with three hubs and two energy currencies.

The course rests on the chemistry courses: acid–base and coordination chemistry from [inorganic chemistry](/6.1), the thermodynamics and kinetics of [physical chemistry](/6.3), where enzyme kinetics and diffusion are first derived, the reaction mechanisms of [organic chemistry](/6.5), and the separation and spectroscopic methods of [analytical chemistry](/6.4) used to work with proteins. On the biological side it is the base of [molecular biology](/7.2), which starts from its chapter on nucleotides and nucleic acids, and of [cell biology](/7.3), which places membranes, transport and signaling in their cellular context. The feedback loops, ultrasensitive switches and oscillations met in enzymes, signaling and metabolic regulation are analyzed quantitatively in [systems biology](/7.9), and the physics of macromolecules in [biophysics](/7.6).

Biochemistry was my first course in the biology major, and as it happens it was also the hardest of them. Every student of biology, in olympiad training and again at university, goes through the ordeal of memorizing biochemistry. With molecular biology left to the next course, what remains falls into structural and metabolic biochemistry, and both have a logic: structure is best sorted out from the chemical properties of the molecules, metabolism from energy balance and feedback regulation. The logic does not remove the need to remember. The detailed knowledge, above all the amino acids and the many pathways of metabolism, still has to be learned largely by heart, but it is much easier to keep once one sees, for instance, why a committed step far from equilibrium is the one that carries the allosteric control. Biochemistry comes first because, together with molecular biology, it is the foundation of biology at the molecular level; after these two courses an undergraduate knows enough to start taking part in research.

The course used the latest edition of Lehninger, the most authoritative textbook of the subject, and the lecture slides largely follow it, which is why these pages keep its order. Of the Chinese books I used in olympiad training, the *Concise Course in Biochemistry* is truly concise and suits a first encounter with the subject; Rongwu Yang's *Principles of Biochemistry* is systematic and full of stories and extensions, probably the most readable of the comprehensive books, though it follows Lehninger closely; and the two-volume textbook Biochemistry by Shenggeng Zhu and Changfa Xu, which we called "big biochemistry", is the most complete, in places more so than Lehninger, but it is a reference to consult rather than a book to learn from.
