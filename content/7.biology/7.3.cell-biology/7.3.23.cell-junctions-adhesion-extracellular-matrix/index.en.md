A multicellular organism is not a bag of cells. Its cells are held together, in a definite arrangement, by direct attachments to one another and by the material they secrete around themselves, and these attachments carry mechanical loads, seal compartments, pass signals and tell each cell where it is. This chapter begins the last group of the course, the cell in its society. It describes the extracellular matrix of animals, the junctions that join cells to each other and to the matrix, the junctions that join their cytoplasms, and the plant cell wall. The molecular parts were met before: the chemistry of **glycosaminoglycans** and **proteoglycans** in [7.1.10](/7.1.10), the **collagen** triple helix in [7.1.5](/7.1.5), **integrins** as bidirectional receptors in [7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.3.4), and **actin**, **intermediate filaments** and **cell migration** in [7.3.13](/7.3.13)–[7.3.15](/7.3.15). Here the question is how they are combined into tissues that are strong, selective and able to remodel, and how much of this can be captured by simple physical models: the Donnan swelling of a gel, the entropic spring, the sorting of cells by adhesion energies, the force dependence of single bonds, the spread of current through coupled cells and the yielding of a pressurized wall. The signaling pathways that read cell contacts and mechanics (Hippo–**YAP**, Wnt/**β-catenin**, Notch) were described in [7.3.19](/7.3.19); the next chapter uses cell contacts and niches to explain differentiation and **stem cells** ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)), and the breakdown of adhesion and of the basal lamina is one of the steps to invasive **cancer** ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

## 23.1. How Cells Build Tissues

Animal tissues use two strategies to withstand the forces that would pull them apart. In connective tissues such as tendon, dermis, cartilage and bone, cells are sparse and the space between them is filled with a matrix that bears most of the mechanical stress; the cells attach to the matrix, remodel it and sense its changes. In epithelia such as the lining of the gut or the epidermis, cells are packed side by side in sheets with little matrix between them, and stress is carried from cell to cell by cytoskeletal filaments anchored at cell–cell junctions; the sheet rests on a thin mat of specialized matrix, the basal lamina.

**Definition 23.1.1 (Extracellular Matrix)** The **extracellular matrix** (ECM) is the network of secreted macromolecules that fills the space between the cells of a tissue: fibrous proteins (collagens, elastin), multidomain adhesive glycoproteins (fibronectin, laminins and others) and proteoglycans with hyaluronan. It is assembled in close association with the cells that make it; it bears tension and compression, binds water, ions and signal molecules, and provides attachment sites and information that control the shape, survival, proliferation, migration and differentiation of cells.

**Definition 23.1.2 (Cell Junction)** A **cell junction** is a specialized site of contact between a cell and a neighbouring cell or the extracellular matrix, built from transmembrane adhesion or channel proteins and their intracellular partners. By function, junctions fall into four classes: **anchoring junctions** link the cytoskeleton of a cell to that of its neighbour or to the matrix (adherens junctions, desmosomes, actin-linked cell–matrix junctions such as focal adhesions, and hemidesmosomes); **occluding junctions** seal the gap between epithelial cells (tight junctions of vertebrates, septate junctions of invertebrates); **channel-forming junctions** connect the cytoplasms of adjacent cells (gap junctions of animals, plasmodesmata of plants); and **signal-relaying junctions** pass signals across a contact (chemical synapses, immunological synapses).

Many textbooks, including the Chinese ones, use three classes: occluding, anchoring and communicating junctions, the last including gap junctions, plasmodesmata and chemical synapses. A chemical synapse joins a neuron to a neuron, muscle or gland cell across a cleft of about $20\,\mathrm{nm}$: the presynaptic terminal (bouton) contains synaptic vesicles filled with neurotransmitter, which are released by $\ce{Ca^2+}$-triggered **exocytosis** ([7.3.8](/7.3.8)) and act on receptors in the postsynaptic membrane; the two membranes are held in register by adhesion proteins (neurexins and neuroligins, cadherins, Ig-superfamily proteins). Its physiology belongs to neurobiology ([7.14](/7.14)).

The anchoring junctions follow one design: a transmembrane adhesion protein binds a partner outside the cell and, through intracellular **adaptor proteins**, a cytoskeletal filament inside. Two superfamilies supply the transmembrane proteins, the cadherins at cell–cell junctions and the integrins at cell–matrix junctions, and each family has members linked to actin and members linked to intermediate filaments:

Tight junction, adherens junction and desmosome form, in that order from the apex, the junctional complex that electron microscopists described in the 1960s (Farquhar and Palade, 1963). The mechanical result is that the **cytoskeletons** of all the cells of an epithelium are linked into one network, so that forces applied to one cell are spread over the sheet and the sheet behaves as a material.

## 23.2. The Extracellular Matrix

The matrix of connective tissue is made locally, mainly by fibroblasts; chondroblasts make cartilage and osteoblasts make bone. Its **macromolecules** belong to three classes with complementary roles. Glycosaminoglycans and proteoglycans form a hydrated gel that fills space and resists compression; **fibrous proteins** resist tension (collagens) or provide elastic recoil (elastin); and adhesive **glycoproteins** (fibronectin, laminins) organize the matrix and attach cells to it. Tissues differ in the proportions: the vitreous humour of the eye is almost pure hyaluronan gel, tendon almost pure collagen, cartilage a collagen network inflated by proteoglycans, and bone collagen mineralized with calcium phosphate. Their stiffness spans many orders of magnitude, from about $0.1$–$1\,\mathrm{kPa}$ (Young's modulus) for brain tissue and $10\,\mathrm{kPa}$ for muscle to about $1\,\mathrm{GPa}$ for tendon and $10$–$20\,\mathrm{GPa}$ for cortical bone, and cells respond to this difference (23.4.4).

### 23.2.1. Glycosaminoglycans and Proteoglycans

The chemistry of the **glycosaminoglycans** (GAGs: hyaluronan, chondroitin sulfate, dermatan sulfate, keratan sulfate, heparan sulfate) and the structure of **proteoglycans** were described in [7.1.10](/7.1.10#sec-10.3.6). Hyaluronan stands apart from the others.

**Definition 23.2.1 (Hyaluronan)** **Hyaluronan** (hyaluronic acid) is the simplest glycosaminoglycan: an unsulfated, unbranched chain of up to about 25 000 repeating disaccharide units of D-glucuronic acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, not attached to any core protein, synthesized at the plasma membrane by hyaluronan synthases that extrude the growing chain directly into the extracellular space.

The other GAGs are added to core proteins in the **Golgi apparatus** ([7.3.9](/7.3.9)) and secreted with them by **exocytosis**. Hyaluronan, made at the cell surface, can be produced quickly and in large amounts, and its enormous hydrated volume makes it a space filler: in embryos, cells secrete hyaluronan to open cell-free spaces into which other cells migrate (in the formation of the heart valves and in neural crest migration), and hyaluronidase removes it when migration is over. Cells bind hyaluronan through the receptor CD44. In cartilage, about 100 molecules of the proteoglycan aggrecan (each with about 100 chondroitin sulfate and 30 keratan sulfate chains) bind one hyaluronan chain through link proteins, giving aggregates of more than $10^8\,\mathrm{Da}$. Smaller proteoglycans regulate other matrix molecules (decorin, a fibroblast proteoglycan with one GAG chain, binds collagen fibrils and controls their diameter: decorin-deficient mice have fragile skin), are part of the basal lamina (perlecan), or sit in the plasma membrane (syndecans, glypicans) and act as co-receptors that concentrate and present **growth factors** such as FGF.

Why does a proteoglycan gel resist compression? Each disaccharide carries one or two fixed negative charges (carboxylate and sulfate), and the gel retains a matching cloud of mobile cations. Treat the tissue as a compartment with fixed charge concentration $c_{\mathrm{F}}$ in contact with interstitial fluid of NaCl concentration $c_0$. At **Donnan equilibrium** ([6.3.21](/6.3.21)) the products of the mobile ion concentrations are equal inside and outside, $c_{+}c_{-} = c_0^2$, and electroneutrality inside requires $c_{+} = c_{-} + c_{\mathrm{F}}$. The excess of mobile ions inside creates an osmotic swelling pressure

$$
\Pi = RT\left(c_{+} + c_{-} - 2c_0\right) = RT\left(\sqrt{c_{\mathrm{F}}^2 + 4c_0^2} - 2c_0\right).
\tag{23.1}
$$

**Example 23.2.1** Articular cartilage has a fixed charge density of about $0.1$–$0.3\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. With $c_{\mathrm{F}} = 0.2\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, $c_0 = 0.15\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and $RT = 2.58\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ at $310\,\mathrm{K}$, Eq. (23.1) gives $\sqrt{0.04 + 0.09} - 0.30 = 0.061\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}} = 61\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$, and $\Pi \approx 2580 \times 61\,\mathrm{Pa} \approx 0.16\,\mathrm{MPa}$. The gel would swell until this pressure is balanced; in cartilage it is held by the tension of the type II collagen network. Under load, water is squeezed out of the gel only slowly through its fine pores, so cartilage behaves as a poroelastic cushion; as water leaves, $c_{\mathrm{F}}$ rises and the swelling pressure increases steeply (Eq. (23.1) grows almost linearly in $c_{\mathrm{F}}$ once $c_{\mathrm{F}} \gg c_0$). In osteoarthritis, the loss of aggrecan lowers $c_{\mathrm{F}}$ and the tissue loses its ability to carry load.

### 23.2.2. Collagens

The **collagens** are the main proteins of the matrix and about a quarter of all protein in mammals. Their defining feature, the triple helix of three α chains with the repeat Gly–X–Y, and the chemistry of hydroxyproline and of scurvy were described in [7.1.5](/7.1.5). Vertebrates have about 28 types, encoded by more than 40 genes, which differ in the length of their triple helix, the domains that interrupt or flank it, and the structures they form:

| Class | Types (examples) | Structure | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fibrillar | I, II, III, V, XI | long uninterrupted helix (300 nm); quarter-staggered fibrils | I: skin, tendon, bone, ligaments, cornea; II: cartilage, vitreous; III: skin, blood vessels, internal organs |
| Fibril-associated | IX, XII | interrupted helices on the fibril surface | bind fibrils (IX on II, XII on I) and other matrix molecules |
| Network-forming | IV | flexible, interrupted helix; head-to-head and tail-to-tail joins | basal laminae |
| Anchoring fibrils | VII | long dimers | link the basal lamina of the epidermis to the dermis |
| Transmembrane | XVII (BP180) | membrane-spanning | hemidesmosomes |

The synthesis of a fibrillar collagen shows how many compartments of the cell cooperate in making one extracellular structure.

**Definition 23.2.2 (Procollagen)** **Procollagen** is the soluble precursor of a fibrillar collagen: three pro-α chains joined in a triple helix and flanked by globular N-terminal and C-terminal propeptides, which keep it soluble inside the cell; extracellular procollagen proteinases remove the propeptides, leaving the collagen molecule (tropocollagen) that assembles into fibrils.

1. Pro-α chains are made on **ribosomes** of the rough **endoplasmic reticulum** and enter its lumen ([7.3.7](/7.3.7)).
2. In the ER, selected prolines and lysines are hydroxylated by prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases, which need ascorbate, and some hydroxylysines are glycosylated with galactose and glucose.
3. The C-propeptides select the right chains (two α1(I) and one α2(I) for type I) and align them; interchain disulfide bonds form and the triple helix zips up from the C terminus towards the N terminus, assisted by the collagen-specific **chaperone** Hsp47.
4. Procollagen, a stiff rod of $300\,\mathrm{nm}$, is too long for the 60–90 nm vesicles of **COPII** ([7.3.8](/7.3.8)). At ER exit sites, the transmembrane protein TANGO1 binds procollagen on the lumenal side and **COPII** components on the cytosolic side and organizes larger carriers or tunnels that allow its export (Malhotra and colleagues); defects in this step cause skeletal disorders.
5. Procollagen passes through the **Golgi apparatus** and is secreted.
6. Outside the cell, procollagen N-proteinase (ADAMTS2) and C-proteinase (BMP-1) remove the propeptides.
7. The collagen molecules self-assemble into fibrils, staggered by a quarter of their length.
8. Lysyl oxidase, a copper enzyme, deaminates lysines and hydroxylysines near the ends to aldehydes, which form covalent cross-links within and between molecules; cross-linking gives fibrils their tensile strength and increases with age.

The stagger explains the banding of collagen fibrils in the electron microscope. A molecule is about $4.46\,D$ long, where $D = 67\,\mathrm{nm}$; molecules in a row are separated by a gap of $0.54\,D$, and each row is shifted by $D$ relative to its neighbour. Every $D$ period therefore contains a gap zone, where one molecule in five is missing, and an overlap zone; negative stain fills the gaps, and fibrils show dark and light bands with period $D$. Fibrils are $10$–$300\,\mathrm{nm}$ thick and are bundled into fibers visible in the light microscope.

Why make the propeptides at all? A protein that self-assembles into insoluble fibrils is dangerous inside the cell: the propeptides prevent fibril formation until the molecule is outside, and the extracellular location of the cleavage puts fibril assembly under the control of the cell surface. Fibroblasts assemble fibrils in deep infoldings of their plasma membrane and then orient them by pulling. In a classic experiment, two small pieces of embryonic tissue placed on a collagen gel pull the collagen between them into a dense, aligned strap, as tendons form between muscles and bones (Stopak and Harris, 1982); fibroblasts in a floating collagen gel contract it to a fraction of its volume, an assay of their traction. Each tissue has its arrangement: parallel bundles in tendon, a plywood of orthogonal layers in bone and cornea, a felt in skin.

The genetics of collagen confirms each step. Glycine substitutions in type I collagen cause osteogenesis imperfecta (brittle bones); mutations in types III and V, or in ADAMTS2 and lysyl hydroxylase, cause forms of Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (fragile skin and vessels, hypermobile joints); inhibitors of lysyl oxidase in the seeds of sweet peas (β-aminopropionitrile) cause lathyrism, with weak tissues and aortic aneurysms. Excessive collagen deposition by activated fibroblasts (myofibroblasts), driven largely by TGF-β ([7.3.19](/7.3.19#sec-19.4.1)), is the basis of fibrosis in the liver, lungs, kidney and skin.

### 23.2.3. Elastin and Elastic Fibers

Skin, lungs and large arteries must stretch and recoil. Their elasticity comes from elastic fibers.

**Definition 23.2.3 (Elastin and Elastic Fiber)** **Elastin** is a hydrophobic, highly cross-linked extracellular protein, rich in glycine, proline, valine and alanine and not glycosylated, secreted as soluble tropoelastin (about $70\,\mathrm{kDa}$), whose molecules are joined by lysine-derived cross-links (desmosine and isodesmosine, made after oxidation of lysines by lysyl oxidase) into a rubberlike network. An **elastic fiber** is a core of elastin surrounded by a sheath of microfibrils of the glycoprotein fibrillin, which are deposited first and guide elastin assembly.

Tropoelastin alternates hydrophobic segments, which form loose random coils, with lysine-rich α-helical segments that carry the cross-links. Stretching the network straightens the coils and reduces their conformational entropy; the restoring force is mainly entropic, as in rubber. For an ideal network the nominal stress at extension ratio $\lambda$ (stretched length over relaxed length) under uniaxial stretch is

$$
\sigma = \frac{E}{3}\left(\lambda - \frac{1}{\lambda^2}\right), \qquad E = 3 n k_{\mathrm{B}} T,
\tag{23.2}
$$

where $n$ is the number of network chains per volume. A modulus $E \approx 1\,\mathrm{MPa}$, typical of elastin, corresponds to $n = E/(3k_{\mathrm{B}}T) \approx 10^6/(3 \times 4.28 \times 10^{-21})\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} \approx 8 \times 10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, a chain between cross-links every $(1/n)^{1/3} \approx 2\,\mathrm{nm}$. Elastin is about a thousand times more compliant than collagen fibrils (**Young's modulus** about $1\,\mathrm{GPa}$), and the two act together: in the aortic wall, elastin carries load at small strains and stores energy during systole, and collagen fibers, crimped at rest, take over at large strains and prevent rupture. Elastin is made mainly before adulthood and is not renewed: its half-life is decades, and its slow fragmentation contributes to the stiffening of arteries and the wrinkling of skin with age. Mutations in fibrillin-1 cause Marfan syndrome (tall stature, lens dislocation, aortic aneurysm), partly because fibrillin also binds and stores latent TGF-β; loss of one copy of the elastin gene, alone or as part of the deletion in Williams syndrome, narrows the aorta (supravalvular aortic stenosis).

### 23.2.4. Fibronectin and Other Multidomain Glycoproteins

The matrix also contains large glycoproteins made of many independently folded modules, each with its **binding sites**, which tie the other components together and to cells.

**Definition 23.2.4 (Fibronectin)** **Fibronectin** is a large extracellular glycoprotein, a dimer of two similar subunits of about $250\,\mathrm{kDa}$ joined by a pair of disulfide bonds near their C termini, each subunit a string of type I, II and III modules with binding sites for collagen, heparan sulfate, fibrin and other fibronectin molecules and, in the tenth type III module, the tripeptide Arg–Gly–Asp (RGD) recognized by integrins.

Short synthetic **peptides** containing RGD compete with fibronectin for cell attachment (Pierschbacher and Ruoslahti, 1984), which identified the minimal recognition signal; the neighbouring ninth type III module contains a synergy site that strengthens binding to integrin α5β1. RGD is also found in vitronectin, fibrinogen, osteopontin and the disintegrins of snake venoms, and RGD-mimetic drugs that block the platelet integrin αIIbβ3 are used to prevent thrombosis. Fibronectin is encoded by a single gene, but **alternative splicing** of three segments (the extra type III modules EIIIA and EIIIB and the variable region V) gives about 20 isoforms in humans. It exists in two forms: soluble plasma fibronectin, a compact dimer made by hepatocytes and lacking EIIIA and EIIIB, and insoluble cellular fibronectin, made by fibroblasts and other cells and assembled into disulfide-linked fibrils of the matrix and the cell surface; isoforms with EIIIA and EIIIB are abundant in embryos, wounds and **tumors**. Transformed fibroblasts lose most of the fibronectin from their surface (it was discovered as the large external transformation-sensitive protein, Hynes 1973), and adding fibronectin back makes them flatten and align as normal cells do. Fibril assembly requires cells: integrins bind fibronectin dimers and the actomyosin cytoskeleton pulls on them, stretching the type III modules and exposing cryptic sites for fibronectin–fibronectin binding. The matrix is thus built under tension, and the cytoskeleton of the cell that builds it determines its orientation. Mice lacking fibronectin die early in embryogenesis with defects in mesoderm, blood vessels and the heart. Other multidomain glycoproteins include tenascins, which modulate adhesion during development and wound healing, and thrombospondins.

### 23.2.5. The Basal Lamina

**Definition 23.2.5 (Basal Lamina)** The **basal lamina** is the thin (about $40$–$120\,\mathrm{nm}$), flexible sheet of specialized extracellular matrix that underlies epithelia and endothelia and surrounds individual muscle cells, fat cells and Schwann cells; its main components are laminins, type IV collagen, the bridging protein nidogen and the heparan sulfate proteoglycan perlecan. With the layer of collagen fibrils anchored beneath it, it forms the basement membrane seen in the light microscope (the two terms are often used interchangeably).

**Definition 23.2.6 (Laminin)** A **laminin** is a large cross-shaped glycoprotein, a heterotrimer of one α, one β and one γ chain (in mammals 5 α, 4 β and 3 γ chains combine into at least 16 isoforms, named by their chains, such as laminin-111 and laminin-332), whose three short arms polymerize into a sheet and whose long arm, a three-stranded coiled coil, ends in five laminin G-like (LG) domains of the α chain that bind cell-surface receptors (integrins, dystroglycan) and sulfated glycolipids.

Laminin is the first matrix protein made in the embryo, and basal laminae assemble from two networks: laminin polymerizes on the cell surface, anchored by its receptors, and type IV collagen molecules form a second, chicken-wire network by joining at their C-terminal globular (NC1) domains and their N-terminal (7S) domains; nidogen and perlecan link the two. The basal lamina has many jobs:

- Mechanical: it attaches epithelia to the connective tissue below and supports them; laminin-332 mutations detach the epidermis (junctional epidermolysis bullosa), and mutations in laminin-211 or in dystroglycan **glycosylation** cause muscular dystrophies.
- Filtration: in the kidney glomerulus the thick basement membrane, with the podocytes, is a size- and charge-selective filter for plasma; mutations in the α3, α4 or α5 chains of type IV collagen cause Alport syndrome (renal failure, often with deafness), and autoantibodies against the NC1 domain of α3(IV) cause Goodpasture syndrome.
- Polarity and differentiation: it defines the basal side of epithelial cells ([7.3.4](/7.3.4#sec-4.6.3)), and in many epithelia only cells in contact with it keep dividing.
- Barrier: most cells cannot cross it; **carcinoma** cells must breach it to invade ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).
- Scaffold and memory: after muscle injury, regenerating muscle fibers and nerve terminals form new synapses exactly at the sites of the old ones, guided by molecules left in the synaptic basal lamina, such as agrin, which clusters acetylcholine receptors (McMahan and colleagues).

A mixture of basal lamina proteins extracted from a mouse tumor (Matrigel) is the standard substrate for growing **organoids** ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)).

### 23.2.6. Remodeling the Matrix

The matrix is turned over continuously, and its local degradation is needed whenever cells migrate, tissues grow or wounds heal.

**Definition 23.2.7 (Matrix Metalloproteinase)** A **matrix metalloproteinase** (MMP) is a member of a family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases (23 in humans) that degrade matrix proteins; most are secreted as inactive proenzymes, kept inactive by a cysteine of the propeptide that coordinates the catalytic zinc (the cysteine switch) and activated by proteolysis, and some (the membrane-type MMPs, such as MT1-MMP) are anchored in the plasma membrane.

Other matrix-degrading enzymes are the ADAM and ADAMTS metalloproteases and **serine proteases** such as plasmin, generated from plasminogen by urokinase bound to its receptor on the cell surface. Their action is confined in three ways: local activation, binding of the enzymes to the cell surface (so that only the pericellular matrix is cut) and secreted inhibitors (tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases, TIMPs, and serpins for serine proteases). Invading cells concentrate MT1-MMP at protrusions called invadopodia. Proteolysis does more than clear a path. The matrix stores signal molecules: latent TGF-β is bound to fibrillin and released by proteases or by integrins that pull on it, and FGF, VEGF, Wnt and Hedgehog proteins bind heparan sulfate proteoglycans, which shape their gradients in tissues ([7.5](/7.5)). Cleavage releases them and also creates fragments with new activities, such as endostatin from type XVIII collagen, an inhibitor of blood vessel growth.

The matrix in turn controls cells. Most normal cells divide and survive only when attached to it (anchorage dependence, 23.4.2); cells follow aligned fibers (contact guidance); and mesenchymal stem cells cultured on gels of different stiffness become neuron-like on soft gels ($0.1$–$1\,\mathrm{kPa}$), muscle-like on gels of intermediate stiffness (about $10\,\mathrm{kPa}$) and bone-like on stiff gels (about $30\,\mathrm{kPa}$), matching the tissues they resemble (Engler and colleagues, 2006). The composition of the matrix matters as much as its stiffness: mammary epithelial cells cultured on plastic or on type I collagen lose their differentiated state, whereas on a basal lamina gel they form hollow, polarized acini and make milk proteins in response to prolactin, a response that requires laminin and β1 integrins (Bissell and colleagues). How cells sense stiffness is the subject of 23.4.4.

## 23.3. Cell–Cell Junctions

### 23.3.1. Cadherins and Adherens Junctions

**Definition 23.3.1 (Cadherin)** A **cadherin** is a member of a superfamily of single-pass transmembrane adhesion proteins whose extracellular region consists of repeated cadherin (EC) domains of about 110 residues with $\ce{Ca^2+}$-binding sites in the linkers between them. **Classical cadherins** (E-cadherin of epithelia, N-cadherin of neurons, muscle and lens, P-cadherin of placenta and epidermis, VE-cadherin of endothelia) have five EC domains and a conserved cytoplasmic tail that binds catenins, and they bind mainly homophilically, to the same or a closely related cadherin on the adjacent cell. The cadherin superfamily (more than 180 members in humans) also includes the desmosomal cadherins, the protocadherins and the giant cadherins Fat and Dachsous.

Cadherins are found in all animals and in their closest unicellular relatives, the choanoflagellates, but not in plants, fungi or bacteria; they are part of what makes an animal. The name comes from their $\ce{Ca^2+}$ dependence. Three $\ce{Ca^2+}$ ions bound at each linker rigidify the ectodomain into a curved rod; when $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is removed with EGTA, the domains flop, adhesion fails and epithelia fall apart, which is how tissues are dissociated in the laboratory (Takeichi discovered cadherins in 1977 as the adhesion that resists trypsin only when $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is present). Two cadherins from opposite cells bind by exchanging their N-terminal **β strands**: a conserved tryptophan (Trp2) of each EC1 domain docks into a pocket of the partner (the strand-swapped dimer), after passing through an intermediate X-shaped dimer. A single trans dimer is weak (**dissociation constants** of tens to hundreds of micromolar), but cadherins on the same cell also bind side by side (cis), and hundreds to thousands of them pack into a lattice: adhesion is strong because many weak bonds act together, and it is reversible because each bond can be broken and remade.

**Definition 23.3.2 (Catenin)** The **catenins** are the cytoplasmic adaptor proteins that bind the tails of classical cadherins: p120-catenin, which binds the membrane-proximal region and controls the endocytosis and stability of cadherins; β-catenin (Definition 19.3.2) or its relative plakoglobin (γ-catenin), which binds the distal region; and α-catenin, which binds β-catenin and actin filaments, directly and through vinculin.

**Definition 23.3.3 (Adherens Junction)** An **adherens junction** is a cell–cell anchoring junction in which clusters of classical cadherins, through catenins and vinculin, link the **actin filaments** of adjacent cells. In epithelia it forms the adhesion belt (zonula adherens), a continuous band around the apex of each cell just below the tight junction, attached to a contractile bundle of actin and **myosin II** that runs parallel to the membrane.

Building a large junction requires a change in the cytoskeleton. A free cell is rounded by the tension of its actomyosin **cell cortex**, much as a droplet is rounded by **surface tension**, and two cells first touch at a point. Engaged cadherins signal locally to activate **Rac** and inhibit **Rho**, which relaxes the cortex at the contact, lets the contact spread and allows more cadherins to join; later, Rho is reactivated and organizes the contractile belt that holds the junction under tension. The junction then works as a tension sensor. Pulling on the cadherin complex unfolds α-catenin and exposes a cryptic binding site for vinculin, which recruits more actin, and the α-catenin–actin bond itself strengthens under force (a catch bond, 23.4.4): the more a junction is pulled, the stronger it becomes, and the contractility of the two cells is balanced across it. Coordinated contraction of adhesion belts drives epithelial morphogenesis; apical constriction of a band of cells, for example, rolls a sheet into a tube (the neural tube, [7.5](/7.5)).

Adherens junctions also take part in signaling. **β-Catenin** is shared between junctions and the **Wnt signaling pathway** ([7.3.19](/7.3.19#sec-19.3.1)); junctions activate the **Hippo pathway** and contribute to **contact inhibition** of proliferation (Definition 22.5.1); and the loss of E-cadherin is a central step in the **epithelial–mesenchymal transition**, in which transcription factors of the Snail, Zeb and Twist families repress the *CDH1* gene and epithelial cells detach and become migratory. The transition is normal in gastrulation and in the emigration of neural crest cells, and it is used by carcinoma cells when they invade; germline loss-of-function mutations of *CDH1* cause hereditary diffuse gastric cancer ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

### 23.3.2. Cell Sorting and the Differential Adhesion Hypothesis

When the cells of an early amphibian embryo are dissociated, mixed and allowed to reaggregate, they sort out into an arrangement resembling the embryo: epidermis outside, neural tissue inside, mesoderm between (Townes and Holtfreter, 1955). Fibroblast L cells, a **cell line** that has no cadherins and does not stick to itself, become adhesive when transfected with a cadherin gene; cells expressing different cadherins sort out from each other (Nose, Nagafuchi and Takeichi, 1988), and so do cells expressing different amounts of the same cadherin, the high expressers gathering inside (Steinberg and Takeichi, 1994). Malcolm Steinberg explained sorting by an analogy with immiscible liquids.

**Definition 23.3.4 (Differential Adhesion Hypothesis)** The **differential adhesion hypothesis** (Steinberg, 1963) states that cells in an aggregate are motile enough to exchange neighbours, like the molecules of a liquid, and that the aggregate therefore approaches the configuration of lowest interfacial free energy: cells sort out into separate tissues, the tissue whose cells adhere to one another most strongly (the one with the highest tissue surface tension) ends up inside, and it is enveloped by the more weakly adhering tissue.

Tissue surface tensions can be measured by compressing spherical aggregates between plates: embryonic chick tissues range from about $1.6\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$ (neural retina) to $20\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$ (limb bud mesoderm), and their order predicts which tissue envelops which (Foty and colleagues, 1996). The prediction can be tested in a computer model in which cells have shape.

**Algorithm 23.3.1 (Cellular Potts Model)** (Graner and Glazier, 1992) Each cell $\sigma$ occupies a connected set of sites of a lattice; site $i$ carries the index $\sigma_i$ of its cell, and each cell has a type $\tau(\sigma)$ (or is the medium).

1. Define the energy
   $$
   H = \sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} J\bigl(\tau(\sigma_i), \tau(\sigma_j)\bigr)\bigl(1 - \delta_{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\bigr) + \lambda \sum_{\sigma}\bigl(a_\sigma - A_\sigma\bigr)^2,
   \tag{23.3}
   $$
   where the first sum runs over neighbouring sites and charges an energy $J$ per unit length of contact between different cells (a small $J$ means strong adhesion), and the second term holds the area $a_\sigma$ of each cell near its target $A_\sigma$ with stiffness $\lambda$.
2. Choose a site $i$ at random and a neighbour $j$ at random; if $\sigma_i \ne \sigma_j$, propose to copy $\sigma_j$ into site $i$ (the boundary of cell $\sigma_j$ advances).
3. Compute the energy change $\Delta H$ and accept the copy with probability $\min\{1, \mathrm{e}^{-\Delta H/T}\}$, where $T$ represents the random fluctuations of the cell membrane.
4. Repeat; one Monte Carlo step is as many attempts as there are sites.

The algorithm is the **Metropolis algorithm** of statistical physics ([3.5.9](/3.5.9)), a **Monte Carlo method** that samples configurations with probability proportional to $\mathrm{e}^{-H/T}$, so the aggregate relaxes towards low energies. The interfacial tension between two cell types $a$ and $b$ is $\gamma_{ab} = J_{ab} - (J_{aa} + J_{bb})/2$, and between type $a$ and the medium $\gamma_{a\mathrm{M}} = J_{a\mathrm{M}} - J_{aa}/2$. Sorting occurs if $\gamma_{ab} > 0$, and type $b$ engulfs type $a$ if $\gamma_{a\mathrm{M}} > \gamma_{b\mathrm{M}} + \gamma_{ab}$.

**Example 23.3.1** In the simulation of the figure, dark cells adhere strongly to each other ($J_{\mathrm{dd}} = 2$), light cells weakly ($J_{\mathrm{ll}} = 14$), with $J_{\mathrm{dl}} = 11$ and $J_{\mathrm{dM}} = J_{\mathrm{lM}} = 16$. Then $\gamma_{\mathrm{dl}} = 11 - 8 = 3 > 0$, so the cells sort, and $\gamma_{\mathrm{dM}} = 15 > \gamma_{\mathrm{lM}} + \gamma_{\mathrm{dl}} = 9 + 3 = 12$, so the light cells envelop the dark ones, as observed.

![Cell sorting by differential adhesion in the cellular Potts model (Algorithm 23.3.1; Graner and Glazier 1992). A: an aggregate of 4 × 4-site cells of two types in random mixture on a 44 × 44 lattice, after 0, 100 and 3000 Monte Carlo steps (MCS), with the contact energies of Example 23.3.1, target area 16 sites, λ = 4 and T = 8: clusters form and coarsen, and the strongly adhering dark cells end up inside, enveloped by the light cells. Later work refined the picture. Adhesion molecules do two things: they bind cells together, and they signal to the cortex to relax at the contact (23.3.1). The effective interfacial tension is the cortical tension of the contact minus the adhesion energy, and measurements in zebrafish germ-layer **progenitors** showed that differences in cortical tension, regulated by adhesion, contribute more to sorting than differences in adhesion energy alone (the differential interfacial tension hypothesis; Krieg and colleagues, 2008; Maître and colleagues, 2012). In the cellular Potts model both contributions are lumped into $J$. In embryos, sorting by cadherin type is seen when the neural tube separates from the ectoderm: neural cells switch from E-cadherin to N-cadherin, and neural crest cells switch off N-cadherin before migrating away and switch it on again when they aggregate into ganglia; forced expression of N-cadherin keeps crest cells in the neural tube.

### 23.3.3. Desmosomes

**Definition 23.3.5 (Desmosome)** A **desmosome** is a button-like cell–cell anchoring junction, about $0.2$–$0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ across, in which the desmosomal cadherins desmoglein and desmocollin join adjacent cells; their cytoplasmic tails bind plakoglobin and plakophilins in an outer dense plaque, which bind desmoplakin, a protein of the plakin family that anchors **intermediate filaments** (**keratins** in epithelia, desmin in heart muscle).

Desmosomes are most abundant in tissues under mechanical stress, the epidermis and the heart. Through them the keratin network of every cell of the epidermis is connected to that of its neighbours, and through hemidesmosomes to the basal lamina (23.4.3); desmoplakin is related to **plectin** (Definition 15.1.4). Mature desmosomes become independent of $\ce{Ca^2+}$, a hyperadhesive state that is reversed in wound healing. The importance of desmosomes is shown by disease. In pemphigus vulgaris, autoantibodies against desmoglein 3 (and often desmoglein 1) separate the keratinocytes of the epidermis and oral mucosa, causing blisters that can be fatal; in pemphigus foliaceus, the antibodies target desmoglein 1, and the exfoliative toxins of *Staphylococcus aureus* cleave the same protein in staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome. Mutations in plakophilin-2, desmoplakin or plakoglobin cause arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, in which heart muscle cells detach and are replaced by fat and fibrous tissue.

### 23.3.4. Tight Junctions

Epithelia separate compartments of different composition: the lumen of the gut from the blood, urine from plasma. For transcellular transport to be useful ([7.3.5](/7.3.5#sec-5.5)), the paracellular route between the cells must be sealed.

**Definition 23.3.6 (Tight Junction and Claudin)** A **tight junction** is the occluding junction of vertebrate epithelia and endothelia: a belt around the apex of each cell in which branching strands of transmembrane proteins in the two plasma membranes bind each other and bring the outer leaflets into contact, sealing the intercellular space. The main strand-forming proteins are the **claudins**, a family of small tetraspan membrane proteins (about two dozen in mammals) whose extracellular loops determine the tightness and the ion selectivity of the seal; occludin and tricellulin (four-span proteins) and the immunoglobulin-like JAMs are also present.

Tight junctions have two functions. As a barrier they restrict paracellular diffusion: electron-dense tracers such as lanthanum hydroxide, applied from either side, stop at the tight junction (Farquhar and Palade, 1963). As a fence they keep proteins and outer-leaflet lipids of the apical membrane domain from mixing with those of the basolateral domain, maintaining the **membrane domains** of epithelial cells ([7.3.4](/7.3.4#sec-4.6.3)). In **freeze-fracture electron microscopy** they appear as an anastomosing network of strands, and the number of strands correlates with the transepithelial electrical resistance (TER), which rises steeply (roughly exponentially) with strand number (Claude, 1978). Epithelia range from leaky to tight: TER is about $5$–$10\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ in the proximal tubule of the kidney and $50$–$100\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ in the small intestine, where much salt and water cross between the cells, and more than $10^4\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ in the urinary bladder.

A seal is not simply closed. Claudins form paracellular pores of a few ångströms radius that are selective for charge and size: claudin-2 forms cation- and water-permeable pores in the proximal tubule, claudin-16 and claudin-19 are needed for the paracellular reabsorption of $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ in the thick ascending limb (their mutations cause familial hypomagnesemia with hypercalciuria), and claudin-1-deficient mice die within a day of birth from water loss through the skin. Larger molecules cross slowly by a separate leak pathway thought to arise from transient breaks in strands. The scaffold proteins ZO-1, ZO-2 and ZO-3 bind claudins through PDZ domains and link them to actin; ZO proteins assemble by phase separation into **biomolecular condensates** at the junction (Beutel and colleagues, 2019). Tight junctions are regulated: **myosin** light-chain kinase contracts the perijunctional actin ring and increases paracellular permeability after glucose absorption begins in the intestine, and inflammatory cytokines open the barrier in inflammatory bowel disease. The endothelial tight junctions of the brain capillaries, rich in claudin-5, form the blood–brain barrier, which keeps most drugs out of the brain; tight junctions between the Sertoli cells of the seminiferous tubules (with claudin-11) form the blood–testis barrier, which isolates meiotic and postmeiotic germ cells from blood-borne molecules and from the immune system.

**Definition 23.3.7 (Septate Junction)** A **septate junction** is the occluding junction of invertebrate epithelia, in which the plasma membranes of adjacent cells run parallel about $15$–$20\,\mathrm{nm}$ apart and are bridged by regularly spaced septa, giving a ladderlike appearance in section; its proteins include neurexin IV, claudin-like proteins and the scaffold Coracle. The paranodal junctions between axons and myelinating glia in vertebrates are related.

The septate junction is also an organizer of epithelial polarity and growth. In *Drosophila* the scaffold protein Discs large (Dlg), with Scribble and Lethal giant larvae (Lgl), lies at the septate junction and defines the basolateral domain in opposition to the apical **Par complex** ([7.3.15](/7.3.15)); mutant imaginal discs lose polarity and septate junctions, overgrow without differentiating and form invasive tumors, so these genes were among the first tumor suppressors identified in flies (neoplastic tumor suppressors).

### 23.3.5. Transient Adhesion: Selectins and the Immunoglobulin Superfamily

Cadherin junctions are built to last. Cells in the bloodstream need adhesions that form and break within seconds.

**Definition 23.3.8 (Selectin)** A **selectin** is a $\ce{Ca^2+}$-dependent cell-surface **lectin** of the C-type family (L-selectin on leukocytes, E-selectin on activated endothelial cells, P-selectin stored in the Weibel–Palade bodies of endothelial cells and the α granules of platelets and moved to the surface within minutes of activation) that binds sialylated and fucosylated oligosaccharides, such as sialyl Lewis$^{\mathrm{x}}$ on the mucin PSGL-1, and mediates transient adhesion between cells in flowing blood.

**Definition 23.3.9 (Cell Adhesion Molecule)** A **cell adhesion molecule** is a cell-surface protein that mediates the binding of a cell to another cell or to the matrix; the term is used especially for the $\ce{Ca^2+}$-independent adhesion proteins of the immunoglobulin superfamily, which have one or more immunoglobulin-like domains and bind homophilically (NCAM, L1, nectins) or heterophilically (ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 of endothelial cells, which bind leukocyte integrins).

The two families cooperate when white blood cells leave the blood at a site of inflammation, in a cascade of steps:

1. Tethering and rolling: inflammatory signals induce P-selectin and E-selectin on the endothelium; selectin–ligand bonds form and break as the leukocyte rolls along the vessel wall at a few micrometres per second. The bonds are catch bonds (23.4.4), and leukocytes roll only above a threshold of shear stress.
2. Activation: chemokines displayed on the endothelium activate **G protein–coupled receptors** on the rolling leukocyte, which activate leukocyte integrins (αLβ2, called LFA-1, and α4β1) from the inside within seconds (23.4.2).
3. Arrest: the activated integrins bind ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, and the cell stops.
4. Crawling and transmigration: the leukocyte crawls to a junction and squeezes between endothelial cells, or sometimes through one, using PECAM-1, JAMs and the transient opening of VE-cadherin junctions.

Each step has a genetic disease: leukocyte adhesion deficiency type I (mutations in the β2 integrin), type II (a defect in fucosylation, so that selectin ligands lack sialyl Lewis$^{\mathrm{x}}$) and type III (mutations in kindlin-3, so that integrins cannot be activated) all cause recurrent bacterial infections with high blood leukocyte counts ([7.15](/7.15)). The antibody natalizumab, which blocks α4 integrins, keeps lymphocytes out of the brain and is used to treat multiple sclerosis. Among the homophilic immunoglobulin-superfamily molecules, NCAM carries long chains of polysialic acid that reduce adhesion where neurons migrate, L1 guides growing axons (its mutations cause hydrocephalus and intellectual disability), and nectins, with their adaptor afadin, initiate cell–cell contacts on which adherens junctions are then built.

The adhesion molecules met in this chapter belong to five families. All are glycoproteins built in the same way: an N-terminal extracellular region that recognizes the partner, usually a single transmembrane segment, and a C-terminal cytoplasmic region that binds the cytoskeleton (through adaptors) or signaling proteins. They bind in three modes: homophilically (to the same molecule on the other cell), heterophilically (to a different molecule) or through a multivalent extracellular linker that bridges receptors on two cells (fibrinogen joining αIIbβ3 integrins of two platelets).

| Family | $\ce{Ca^2+}$ or $\ce{Mg^2+}$ dependence | Binding | Examples | Junctions or functions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cadherins | $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | mainly homophilic | E-, N-, P-, VE-cadherin; desmogleins, desmocollins | adherens junctions, desmosomes; sorting |
| Ig superfamily | independent | homophilic or heterophilic | NCAM, L1, nectins, PECAM-1; ICAM-1, VCAM-1 | transient contacts, axon guidance, leukocyte arrest |
| Selectins | $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | heterophilic (carbohydrates) | L-, E-, P-selectin | leukocyte rolling, lymphocyte homing |
| Integrins | divalent cations | heterophilic | β1 integrins (matrix), β2 (leukocytes), β3 (platelets, αIIbβ3), α6β4 | focal adhesions, hemidesmosomes, leukocyte and platelet adhesion |
| Hyaladherins | independent | heterophilic (hyaluronan) | CD44, RHAMM, link proteins of cartilage | cell–matrix adhesion, migration |

Proteins that bind hyaluronan are collectively called *hyaladherins*; they share a hyaluronan-binding module, the link module, or a cluster of basic residues. The main cell-surface hyaladherin, CD44, a single-pass glycoprotein expressed in many variant isoforms by alternative splicing, binds hyaluronan of the pericellular matrix and links it to actin through ERM proteins; it mediates the uptake and degradation of hyaluronan, takes part in the homing of lymphocytes to lymph nodes and in the migration of activated T cells, and is highly expressed in many tumors, where its levels correlate with invasion and metastasis.

## 23.4. Cell–Matrix Junctions

### 23.4.1. Integrins

The **integrins**, introduced as receptors in [7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.3.4), are heterodimers of an α and a β subunit, both single-pass transmembrane glycoproteins. Humans have 18 α and 8 β subunits, which form 24 integrins with distinct specificities. The large extracellular head binds the ligand at the interface of the two subunits, where a divalent cation ($\ce{Mg^2+}$ at the metal-ion-dependent adhesion site of the β subunit) coordinates an acidic residue of the ligand, the aspartate of RGD in fibronectin; this is why integrin binding requires divalent cations and why $\ce{Mn^2+}$ activates integrins in the test tube. The cytoplasmic tails are short (about 20–70 residues), except that of β4, which has more than 1000 residues and binds plectin in hemidesmosomes.

|---|---|---|---|
| α5β1 | fibronectin (RGD + synergy site) | fibroblasts, many others | knockout embryos die with mesoderm defects |
| α1β1, α2β1 | collagens (GFOGER motif, bound by an extra αI domain) | many | |
| α3β1, α6β1, α7β1 | laminins | epithelia, muscle | α7: a congenital myopathy |
| α6β4 | laminin-332 | basal epithelial cells (hemidesmosomes) | junctional epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia |
| αvβ3 | vitronectin, fibronectin, osteopontin (RGD) | endothelial cells, osteoclasts | sealing zone of bone-resorbing osteoclasts |
| αvβ6, αvβ8 | latent TGF-β (RGD of the latency peptide) | epithelia, immune cells | activate TGF-β by pulling |
| αIIbβ3 | fibrinogen, von Willebrand factor | platelets | Glanzmann thrombasthenia (bleeding); antiplatelet drugs |
| αLβ2 (LFA-1) | ICAM-1, ICAM-2 | leukocytes | leukocyte adhesion deficiency type I |
| α4β1 (VLA-4) | VCAM-1, fibronectin | lymphocytes, monocytes | target of natalizumab |

Individual integrin–ligand bonds are weak (dissociation constants around $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-8}\,\mathrm{M}$), but each cell carries $10^4$–$10^5$ integrins, and adhesion strength is set by how many are engaged and clustered. Weak, numerous bonds let a cell attach firmly and still move, by breaking bonds at its rear while making new ones at its front (**cell migration**, [7.3.15](/7.3.15#sec-15.5)).

### 23.4.2. Inside-Out and Outside-In Signaling

Integrins switch between conformations of different affinity. In the bent conformation the head faces the membrane and the α and β legs and cytoplasmic tails are held together, in part by a salt bridge between the membrane-proximal regions of the tails; this state has low affinity. Extension of the legs raises the head away from the membrane, and opening of the headpiece, a swing-out of the hybrid domain of the β subunit, increases the affinity for ligand by orders of magnitude.

In inside-out signaling, a signal inside the cell activates the integrin. Receptors for chemokines, thrombin or antigen activate the small GTPase Rap1, whose effectors (RIAM, and in some cells talin itself) recruit talin to the membrane; the FERM-domain head of talin binds a membrane-proximal NPxY motif of the β tail and the negatively charged membrane, separates the tails, and, with kindlin, which binds a second, distal NxxY motif, switches the extracellular domain to the high-affinity state. This happens in less than a second when platelets are activated, and it is why platelets and leukocytes circulate without sticking until they receive a signal. In outside-in signaling, ligand binding and clustering of integrins assemble a cytoplasmic signaling complex. Focal adhesion kinase (FAK) autophosphorylates on Tyr397, which recruits Src; FAK and Src phosphorylate paxillin and p130Cas, which recruit adaptors that activate **Rac** (through the GEF DOCK180), the Ras–MAP kinase pathway and **phosphoinositide 3-kinase** ([7.3.19](/7.3.19#sec-19.2.3)). These signals are why most normal cells need to be attached to proliferate (anchorage dependence of growth) and to survive: epithelial cells that lose contact with their matrix die by **apoptosis**, a response called anoikis (Frisch and Francis, 1994), which prevents detached cells from growing in the wrong place; cancer cells escape it ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

### 23.4.3. Focal Adhesions and Hemidesmosomes

**Definition 23.4.1 (Focal Adhesion)** A **focal adhesion** is an integrin-based, actin-linked cell–matrix anchoring junction: an elongated plaque, about $1$–$5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long in cells on a stiff substrate, in which clustered integrins bound to the matrix are linked through talin, vinculin and many other adaptor and signaling proteins to the end of a **stress fiber**, so that the junction transmits contractile force to the matrix and assembles signaling complexes (FAK, Src, paxillin).

Focal adhesions mature under force. Nascent adhesions of a few integrins form within seconds under the protruding lamellipodium; most disassemble within a minute, and those that experience tension from actomyosin grow into focal complexes and then into focal adhesions; some develop into fibrillar adhesions that move towards the cell centre and pull fibronectin into fibrils (23.2.4). Superresolution microscopy shows a layered architecture: integrin tails, FAK and paxillin within about $20\,\mathrm{nm}$ of the membrane, a force-transducing layer of talin and vinculin, and an actin-regulatory layer (zyxin, VASP, α-actinin) at $50$–$60\,\mathrm{nm}$ (Kanchanawong and colleagues, 2010). Talin spans these layers: its rod of 13 helix bundles is stretched between the integrin and actin, and bundles unfold at forces of about $5\,\mathrm{pN}$, exposing binding sites for vinculin, which links more actin (del Rio and colleagues, 2009, measured with magnetic tweezers). The more force an adhesion carries, the more it grows; this is the reinforcement that the molecular clutch of migration requires ([7.3.15](/7.3.15#sec-15.5.3)). Traction stresses under focal adhesions are of the order of $1\,\mathrm{nN\,\mu m^{-2}}$ ($1\,\mathrm{kPa}$). In soft three-dimensional matrices, adhesions are much smaller.

**Definition 23.4.2 (Hemidesmosome)** A **hemidesmosome** is a cell–matrix anchoring junction that links the keratin filaments of basal epithelial cells to laminin-332 of the basal lamina, through the integrin α6β4 and type XVII collagen (BP180); the cytoplasmic tail of β4 and BP180 bind the plakins plectin and BP230, which anchor the keratin filaments.

Hemidesmosomes look like half desmosomes in the electron microscope, but their proteins are different. Below the basal lamina, anchoring fibrils of type VII collagen loop into the dermis and hold the lamina to it. The level at which the skin splits in the inherited blistering diseases called epidermolysis bullosa identifies the broken link: inside the basal keratinocytes when keratin 5 or 14 is defective (**epidermolysis bullosa simplex**, Definition 15.1.3), within the basal lamina when laminin-332, type XVII collagen or α6β4 is defective (junctional), and below the lamina when type VII collagen is defective (dystrophic). In bullous pemphigoid, a disease of the elderly, autoantibodies against BP180 and BP230 cause blisters at the same level as in junctional epidermolysis bullosa. In 2015 a child who had lost most of his epidermis to junctional epidermolysis bullosa with a laminin-332 mutation was given new skin grown from his own epidermal stem cells corrected with a retroviral vector, and it was still intact years later (Hirsch and colleagues, 2017; [7.3.24](/7.3.24)).

### 23.4.4. Mechanotransduction and Catch Bonds

**Definition 23.4.3 (Mechanotransduction)** **Mechanotransduction** is the conversion of mechanical forces or mechanical properties of the environment (stretch, shear stress, compression, confinement, substrate stiffness) into biochemical signals and changes in cell behaviour.

Cells use several mechanisms, often together:

1. Force-gated ion channels, such as Piezo1 and Piezo2, open when membrane tension rises (**mechanosensitive channels**, [7.3.5](/7.3.5#sec-5.7)).
2. Force unfolds proteins and exposes cryptic sites: talin and α-catenin bind vinculin when stretched, fibronectin type III modules expose self-association sites, and stretching of the substrate domain of p130Cas exposes tyrosines for phosphorylation by Src.
3. Force changes the lifetime of bonds (catch bonds, below).
4. Force reaches the nucleus through the cytoskeleton and the **LINC complex** ([7.3.16](/7.3.16)), deforming chromatin and stretching nuclear pores.
5. The tension of the actomyosin cytoskeleton, controlled by Rho and ROCK, regulates the transcription coactivators **YAP** and TAZ and the MRTF–SRF pathway ([7.3.19](/7.3.19#sec-19.4.2)).

To sense stiffness, a cell must pull: on a stiff substrate the same contractile force produces a small displacement and builds tension in the adhesion quickly, so talin unfolds and adhesions reinforce before the bonds fail; on a soft substrate force builds slowly and bonds break first. Stiffness sensing is therefore a property of the clutch as a whole, and it feeds into YAP, which is nuclear on stiff matrices and cytoplasmic on soft ones, and into the fate decisions of stem cells (23.2.6).

The mechanics of a single bond is quantitative. A noncovalent bond dissociates when thermal fluctuations carry it over an energy barrier; a pulling force $F$ tilts the energy landscape and lowers the barrier by about $F x_\beta$, where $x_\beta$ is the distance from the bound state to the barrier along the pulling direction. By the **Arrhenius equation** the off-rate then rises exponentially (Bell, 1978):

$$
k(F) = k_0\, \mathrm{e}^{F x_\beta / k_{\mathrm{B}}T}, \qquad \tau(F) = \frac{1}{k(F)} = \tau_0\, \mathrm{e}^{-F x_\beta / k_{\mathrm{B}}T}.
\tag{23.4}
$$

With $x_\beta = 0.5\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $k_{\mathrm{B}}T = 4.28\,\mathrm{pN\,nm}$ at $310\,\mathrm{K}$, every $8.6\,\mathrm{pN}$ of force shortens the lifetime $\mathrm{e}$-fold. Forces of this size are applied by single motor proteins and measured with **optical tweezers** or atomic force microscopes.

**Definition 23.4.4 (Slip Bond and Catch Bond)** A **slip bond** is a noncovalent bond whose lifetime decreases monotonically as the tensile force on it increases. A **catch bond** is one whose lifetime increases with force over a range of forces; most catch bonds become slip bonds at higher forces (catch–slip bonds).

A simple description of a catch–slip bond is the two-pathway model: the bond can dissociate along a pathway that force inhibits and along one that force promotes,

$$
k(F) = k_{\mathrm{c}}\, \mathrm{e}^{-F x_{\mathrm{c}}/k_{\mathrm{B}}T} + k_{\mathrm{s}}\, \mathrm{e}^{F x_{\mathrm{s}}/k_{\mathrm{B}}T},
\tag{23.5}
$$

so that the lifetime $1/k(F)$ is maximal where $\mathrm{d}k/\mathrm{d}F = 0$:

$$
F^* = \frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{x_{\mathrm{c}} + x_{\mathrm{s}}} \ln\frac{k_{\mathrm{c}} x_{\mathrm{c}}}{k_{\mathrm{s}} x_{\mathrm{s}}}.
\tag{23.6}
$$

**Example 23.4.1** With $k_{\mathrm{c}} = 10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $x_{\mathrm{c}} = 1.2\,\mathrm{nm}$, $k_{\mathrm{s}} = 0.05\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $x_{\mathrm{s}} = 0.8\,\mathrm{nm}$, Eq. (23.6) gives $F^* = (4.28/2.0)\ln 300 \approx 12\,\mathrm{pN}$. At $F = 0$ the lifetime is $1/10.05 \approx 0.1\,\mathrm{s}$; at $F^*$, $k = 10\,\mathrm{e}^{-3.42} + 0.05\,\mathrm{e}^{2.28} \approx 0.33 + 0.49 = 0.82\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and the lifetime is $1.2\,\mathrm{s}$, twelve times longer.

Catch bonds were first demonstrated for P-selectin and its ligand PSGL-1, whose lifetimes rise as force increases to about $10\,\mathrm{pN}$ and then fall (Marshall and colleagues, 2003). They explain why leukocytes need a minimum shear stress to roll: at low flow, bonds break too quickly; at moderate flow, the force on each bond prolongs it. The adhesin FimH of uropathogenic *Escherichia coli* binds mannose more tightly under flow, so bacteria stick better when urine is flowing; the integrin α5β1–fibronectin bond lives longest at about $30\,\mathrm{pN}$ (Kong and colleagues, 2009), and the bonds of vinculin and α-catenin with actin are catch bonds that are strongest when pulled in one direction along the filament. The functional consequence is collective. In a cluster of slip bonds sharing a load, the failure of one bond shifts its load to the others and accelerates their failure, a cascade; with catch bonds the load sharing is stabilizing, and adhesions strengthen as they are loaded.

## 23.5. Communicating Junctions

### 23.5.1. Gap Junctions

**Definition 23.5.1 (Gap Junction, Connexin and Connexon)** A **gap junction** is a channel-forming junction in which the plasma membranes of two adjacent cells come within about $2$–$4\,\mathrm{nm}$ of each other and are bridged by plaques of intercellular channels that connect the two cytoplasms. Each channel is made of two **connexons** (hemichannels), one contributed by each cell, docked end to end across the gap; in vertebrates each connexon is a hexamer of **connexins**, four-pass transmembrane proteins (21 in humans). The aqueous pore, about $1.4\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide, passes ions and water-soluble molecules up to about $1\,\mathrm{kDa}$, but not proteins or nucleic acids.

Invertebrates make gap junctions from an unrelated family, the innexins; their vertebrate homologues, the pannexins, form single membrane channels that release ATP rather than gap junctions. Coupling was discovered physiologically. Current injected into one cell changes the membrane potential of its neighbour (Furshpan and Potter, 1959, at an electrical synapse of the crayfish), and fluorescent dyes injected into one cell spread to coupled neighbours if their mass is below about $1\,\mathrm{kDa}$ (Loewenstein and colleagues); in freeze-fracture images, gap junctions are clusters of particles, each a connexon. Gap junctions serve three purposes:

- Electrical coupling: action potentials pass from cell to cell without the delay of a chemical synapse. Electrical synapses synchronize groups of neurons; in the heart, connexin 43 (ventricles) and connexin 40 (atria, conduction system) spread excitation and synchronize contraction, and in the uterus connexin 43 increases at the end of pregnancy so that labour contractions are coordinated.
- Metabolic coupling: nutrients, ions and second messengers (**cyclic AMP**, inositol trisphosphate, $\ce{Ca^2+}$) are shared, so that a tissue responds as a unit and fluctuations in single cells are averaged out. The fibre cells of the eye lens, which have no organelles, are fed by their neighbours through connexin 46 and 50 (mutations cause cataracts); granulosa cells nourish the oocyte through connexin 37.
- Development and disease: many cells of early embryos are coupled; mutations in connexin 26 (*GJB2*) are the most common cause of inherited nonsyndromic deafness, those in connexin 32 cause an X-linked form of Charcot–Marie–Tooth neuropathy, and many tumor cells have reduced coupling.

Gap junction channels are gated: they close when the cytoplasmic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ concentration rises or the pH falls, which isolates a damaged cell from its neighbours, and they are regulated by transjunctional voltage and by phosphorylation. Connexins turn over fast, with half-lives of a few hours: new connexons are added at the edge of a plaque and old ones are removed from its centre, when one cell endocytoses the double membrane of a piece of the plaque.

The spread of a signal through coupled cells can be calculated. Consider a row of identical cells, each with membrane (leak) conductance $g_{\mathrm{m}}$, coupled to its neighbours by junctional conductance $g_{\mathrm{j}}$, with a current injected into cell 0. At steady state, the current entering cell $n$ from its neighbours leaves through its membrane:

$$
g_{\mathrm{j}}\left(V_{n-1} - 2V_n + V_{n+1}\right) = g_{\mathrm{m}} V_n .
\tag{23.7}
$$

Trying $V_n = V_0 r^n$ gives $r + 1/r = 2 + g_{\mathrm{m}}/g_{\mathrm{j}}$, so that the voltage decays geometrically with a coupling length of $\lambda = -1/\ln r$ cells; for $g_{\mathrm{j}} \gg g_{\mathrm{m}}$, $\lambda \approx \sqrt{g_{\mathrm{j}}/g_{\mathrm{m}}}$, the discrete form of the cable equation. A small molecule spreads in the same way but in time: if $c_n$ is its concentration in cell $n$,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}c_n}{\mathrm{d}t} = k\left(c_{n-1} - 2c_n + c_{n+1}\right), \qquad k = \frac{P a}{v},
\tag{23.8}
$$

with $P$ the permeability of the junctional membrane, $a$ the junctional area and $v$ the cell volume. Over many cells this is diffusion with an effective coefficient $D_{\mathrm{eff}} = k\ell^2$ for cells of length $\ell$.

**Example 23.5.1** A cell with an input resistance of $100\,\mathrm{M\Omega}$ ($g_{\mathrm{m}} = 10\,\mathrm{nS}$) is coupled to each neighbour by about 1000 open channels of about $100\,\mathrm{pS}$, so $g_{\mathrm{j}} = 100\,\mathrm{nS}$ and $g_{\mathrm{m}}/g_{\mathrm{j}} = 0.1$. Then $r = 1.05 - \sqrt{1.05^2 - 1} \approx 0.730$ and $\lambda = 1/\ln(1/0.730) \approx 3.2$ cells, close to $\sqrt{10}$. For dye coupling, $k = 0.05\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\ell = 20\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ give $D_{\mathrm{eff}} = 20\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, an order of magnitude below the diffusion coefficient of a small molecule in cytoplasm: the junctions, not the cytoplasm, limit the spread.

### 23.5.2. Plasmodesmata

Plant cells are separated by walls at least $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ thick, too thick for connexons. They are connected instead by membrane-lined channels through the wall.

**Definition 23.5.2 (Plasmodesma)** A **plasmodesma** (plural plasmodesmata) is a cytoplasmic channel, about $20$–$40\,\mathrm{nm}$ in diameter, through the cell wall between adjacent plant cells, lined by plasma membrane that is continuous between the two cells, with a central desmotubule of tightly appressed endoplasmic reticulum that joins the ER of the two cells; small molecules move through the cytoplasmic sleeve between the plasma membrane and the desmotubule.

Primary plasmodesmata form during cytokinesis, where strands of ER are trapped in the growing **cell plate** (Definition 21.5.3); secondary plasmodesmata are inserted later through existing walls. With few exceptions all the cells of a plant are connected, and the cytoplasms joined by plasmodesmata form a continuous compartment (the symplast), as distinct from the walls and intercellular spaces (the apoplast). Tracer injections show a size exclusion limit of about $800$–$1000\,\mathrm{Da}$, similar to that of gap junctions, but unlike gap junctions plasmodesmata can be dilated to pass specific macromolecules. Viral movement proteins (such as the $30\,\mathrm{kDa}$ protein of tobacco mosaic virus) enlarge them and let viral genomes spread; transcription factors move between cells as signals that specify fate (the KNOTTED1 protein of maize in the shoot meristem, SHORT-ROOT from the vascular cylinder to the neighbouring cell layer of the root, where it specifies the endodermis); and RNAs that mediate gene silencing spread through them. Communication is regulated by callose, a (β1→3)-glucan deposited in the wall around the necks of plasmodesmata by callose synthases and removed by β-1,3-glucanases: callose narrows the channel and isolates cells or domains, for example the guard cells of stomata, which are symplastically isolated when mature, and cells attacked by pathogens.

## 23.6. The Plant Cell Wall

**Definition 23.6.1 (Cell Wall, Primary and Secondary)** A **cell wall** is a rigid extracellular layer outside the plasma membrane of the cells of plants, algae, fungi and most bacteria and archaea, which resists the internal turgor pressure and determines the shape of the cell. The plant cell wall is a fibre composite of cellulose microfibrils embedded in a matrix of cross-linking glycans (hemicelluloses), pectins and proteins. The **primary cell wall** is the thin (about $0.1$–$1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), extensible wall laid down while the cell grows; the **secondary cell wall** is the thicker, rigid wall deposited inside the primary wall after growth has stopped, richer in cellulose and in many cell types impregnated with lignin.

**Definition 23.6.2 (Cellulose Microfibril)** A **cellulose microfibril** is a crystalline, ribbonlike bundle of about 18 parallel chains of **cellulose** ([Definition 10.3.3](/7.1.10#def-10.3.3)), hydrogen-bonded to each other, a few nanometres wide and many micrometres long, spun into the wall by a cellulose synthase complex in the plasma membrane.

The plant cell wall is the plant's extracellular matrix, built on the same principle as the animal matrix and as reinforced concrete: fibres with high tensile strength embedded in a matrix that resists compression. The chemistry differs: plant walls contain almost no nitrogen, and the cheap carbon polymers cellulose and lignin make up most of the biomass of a tree.

| Component | Primary wall (eudicot, % of dry mass, roughly) | Secondary wall | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cellulose microfibrils | 20–30 | 40–50 | tensile strength; orientation sets the direction of growth |
| Cross-linking glycans (xyloglucan in eudicots; arabinoxylans and mixed-linkage glucans in grasses) | 20–25 | xylans, 20–30 | coat and tether microfibrils |
| Pectins (homogalacturonan, rhamnogalacturonans I and II) | 30–35 | little | hydrated gel; $\ce{Ca^2+}$ cross-links; middle lamella |
| Proteins (extensins, arabinogalactan proteins, expansins, enzymes) | 1–10 | little | strength, signaling, loosening |
| Lignin | 0 | 20–30 | rigidity, waterproofing (xylem, fibres) |

The polymers are made in two places. The **cellulose synthase** complexes, rosettes of six lobes each containing three CesA proteins, assemble in the Golgi apparatus and are delivered to the plasma membrane; there each CesA extrudes a glucan chain from UDP-glucose through the membrane, and the 18 chains of a complex crystallize into one microfibril. The polymerization pushes the complex through the membrane at about $200$–$400\,\mathrm{nm\,min^{-1}}$. The matrix polysaccharides are made in the Golgi apparatus and secreted ([7.3.9](/7.3.9)); homogalacturonan is secreted in a methylesterified form and de-esterified in the wall by pectin methylesterases, after which its carboxylates are cross-linked by $\ce{Ca^2+}$ into a gel (the egg-box structure). The middle lamella between adjacent cells is rich in pectin and cements them together; its regulated dissolution softens ripening fruit and lets leaves fall. Lignin is a phenolic polymer formed outside the cell by oxidative coupling of monolignols, catalysed by peroxidases and laccases in the secondary wall.

![The plant cell wall and its growth. A: the primary wall in face view: two lamellae of cellulose microfibrils at right angles, tethered by cross-linking glycans and embedded in a pectin gel. B: walls in section between two cells: the pectin-rich middle lamella cements the primary walls of the two cells; the lower cell has stopped growing and laid down a secondary wall in three layers (S1, S2, S3) with different microfibril orientations; cellulose synthase complexes in the plasma membrane move along cortical microtubules. D: the Lockhart model, Eq. (23.9): the relative growth rate is zero until turgor exceeds the yield threshold Y and then rises with slope φ; wall loosening raises φ and lowers Y (φ = 0.1 to 0.3 h⁻¹ MPa⁻¹, Y = 0.3 to 0.2 MPa; illustrative values)](./plots/plant-cell-wall.py)

A plant cell grows by yielding its wall to **turgor pressure** (Definition 5.3.1), which is typically $0.3$–$1\,\mathrm{MPa}$. Lockhart (1965) described growth as the viscoplastic flow of the wall above a yield threshold:

$$
\frac{1}{V}\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = \begin{cases} \varphi\,(P - Y), & P > Y, \\ 0, & P \le Y, \end{cases}
\tag{23.9}
$$

where $P$ is the turgor pressure, $Y$ the yield threshold of the wall and $\varphi$ its extensibility. Because growth enlarges the cell, water must enter to keep $P$ up; when water uptake is limited by the hydraulic conductance $L$ of the cell, the growth rate becomes $\varphi L/(\varphi + L)$ times the excess of the osmotic driving force over $Y$, and either the wall or water supply can limit growth.

**Example 23.6.1** With $\varphi = 0.1\,\mathrm{h^{-1}\,MPa^{-1}}$, $P = 0.6\,\mathrm{MPa}$ and $Y = 0.3\,\mathrm{MPa}$, the relative growth rate is $0.03\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$ and the volume doubles in $\ln 2/0.03 \approx 23\,\mathrm{h}$. If the wall is loosened so that $\varphi = 0.3\,\mathrm{h^{-1}\,MPa^{-1}}$ and $Y = 0.2\,\mathrm{MPa}$, the rate rises to $0.12\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$ and the doubling time falls to about $6\,\mathrm{h}$, without any change in turgor.

Cells control $\varphi$ and $Y$. In the acid growth response, auxin activates the plasma membrane $\ce{H+}$-ATPase; the wall acidifies to a pH of about 4.5–5, and wall proteins called expansins, which are active at low pH, loosen the wall without breaking covalent bonds, apparently by disrupting the binding between cellulose and its tethers; xyloglucan endotransglucosylases cut and rejoin xyloglucan chains. The direction of growth is set by the orientation of the microfibrils. In an elongating cell they are laid down mostly transversely, like hoops around a barrel, so the cell expands perpendicular to them, along its long axis. The orientation is set by cortical **microtubules** beneath the plasma membrane, along which the cellulose synthase complexes move (Paredez and colleagues, 2006; [7.3.14](/7.3.14)): drugs that depolymerize microtubules make newly made microfibrils disordered and cells swell isotropically. In the shoot apex, cortical microtubules align with the direction of maximal mechanical stress in the tissue, so that walls are reinforced where they are most stressed (Hamant and colleagues, 2008), a mechanical feedback between the wall and the cytoskeleton that shapes organs ([7.12](/7.12), [7.13](/7.13)). Other walled cells use other polymers: **peptidoglycan** in bacteria ([7.11](/7.11)), **chitin** and glucans in fungi.

## Questions

1. Cartilage is compressed so that its water content falls and its fixed charge density rises from $0.15$ to $0.30\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. By what factor does the Donnan swelling pressure increase ($c_0 = 0.15\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, $T = 310\,\mathrm{K}$)? What does this imply for the mechanics of cartilage?

   **Answer** By Eq. (23.1), $\Pi \propto \sqrt{c_{\mathrm{F}}^2 + 4c_0^2} - 2c_0$. At $c_{\mathrm{F}} = 0.15$: $\sqrt{0.0225 + 0.09} - 0.30 = 0.0354\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, $\Pi \approx 2580 \times 35.4\,\mathrm{Pa} \approx 91\,\mathrm{kPa}$. At $c_{\mathrm{F}} = 0.30$: $\sqrt{0.09 + 0.09} - 0.30 = 0.124\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, $\Pi \approx 320\,\mathrm{kPa}$. Doubling the charge density raises the pressure about 3.5-fold. The resistance of the gel to further compression therefore stiffens steeply as it is compressed, so cartilage becomes stiffer under load and springs back when the load is removed, as water is drawn back in.

2. A mutation replaces the C-propeptide of the pro-α1(I) chain with an unrelated sequence. Predict where in the cell the defect will appear and what happens to the protein.

   **Answer** The C-propeptides select and align the three chains and nucleate the triple helix in the ER, from the C terminus towards the N terminus. Without a functional C-propeptide, mutant chains cannot associate correctly; they remain unfolded in the ER, where they are retained by chaperones (Hsp47, BiP, the calnexin system) and degraded by ER-associated degradation or autophagy, and their accumulation can trigger the unfolded protein response ([7.3.7](/7.3.7)). Little type I collagen is secreted, and the tissue phenotype resembles a severe osteogenesis imperfecta. Correctly folded molecules that escape would lack the propeptide whose later cleavage controls fibril assembly.

3. Using the interfacial tensions of the cellular Potts model, predict the outcome of mixing two cell types with $J_{aa} = 4$, $J_{bb} = 10$, $J_{ab} = 7$ and $J_{a\mathrm{M}} = J_{b\mathrm{M}} = 12$. Would they sort? Which type would be inside? What changes if $J_{ab} = 6$?

   **Answer** $\gamma_{ab} = 7 - (4 + 10)/2 = 0$: the two types have no preference for like neighbours over unlike ones, and they remain mixed (as in a random mixture of two miscible liquids). With $J_{ab} = 6$, $\gamma_{ab} = -1 < 0$: the cells prefer heterotypic contacts and form an alternating, checkerboard-like mixture rather than sorting. Sorting needs $\gamma_{ab} > 0$, that is, $J_{ab} > (J_{aa} + J_{bb})/2$. In either case the more cohesive type $a$ ($\gamma_{a\mathrm{M}} = 10 > \gamma_{b\mathrm{M}} = 7$) tends to stay away from the medium, so if sorting were induced (by raising $J_{ab}$ above 7), $a$ would be inside.

4. A blister forms in the skin of a patient. Electron microscopy shows that the split lies within the lamina lucida of the basal lamina, and immunostaining shows that laminin-332 is present at normal levels. Name two candidate defects, one inherited and one acquired, and explain how to distinguish them.

   **Answer** A split within the basal lamina, below the basal cells, points to the hemidesmosome–basal lamina link. With laminin-332 present, candidates are an inherited junctional epidermolysis bullosa due to mutations in type XVII collagen (BP180) or in integrin α6β4 (with pyloric atresia in the latter), or the acquired autoimmune disease bullous pemphigoid, with autoantibodies against BP180 and BP230. They are distinguished by age and history (pemphigoid is mostly in the elderly; inherited forms present from birth), by direct immunofluorescence (linear deposits of IgG and complement along the basement membrane in pemphigoid), by circulating autoantibodies, and by staining for BP180 and β4 (absent or reduced in the inherited forms) and sequencing of *COL17A1*, *ITGA6* and *ITGB4*.

5. A bond follows the two-pathway model of Example 23.4.1. Compute its mean lifetime at $5$, $12$ and $25\,\mathrm{pN}$. A leukocyte rolls on P-selectin in flow; why would doubling the shear stress first increase and then decrease the number of bonds that hold the cell at any moment?

   **Answer** With $k_{\mathrm{B}}T = 4.28\,\mathrm{pN\,nm}$: at $5\,\mathrm{pN}$, $k = 10\,\mathrm{e}^{-1.40} + 0.05\,\mathrm{e}^{0.93} = 2.46 + 0.13 = 2.59\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $\tau = 0.39\,\mathrm{s}$; at $12\,\mathrm{pN}$, $k \approx 0.34 + 0.47 = 0.81\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $\tau \approx 1.2\,\mathrm{s}$; at $25\,\mathrm{pN}$, $k = 10\,\mathrm{e}^{-7.01} + 0.05\,\mathrm{e}^{4.67} = 0.009 + 5.35 = 5.36\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $\tau = 0.19\,\mathrm{s}$. The force on each bond is proportional to the shear stress divided by the number of bonds sharing the load. At low shear, the force per bond lies on the catch branch: increasing the shear prolongs bond lifetimes, so bonds accumulate faster than they break and the cell is held by more bonds (and rolls more slowly and regularly). Once the force per bond passes $F^*$, further increases shorten lifetimes, bonds break faster than they form, and the cell detaches or rolls faster. This produces the shear threshold and the optimum for rolling.

6. Two cells of a chain coupled by gap junctions have $g_{\mathrm{m}} = 5\,\mathrm{nS}$ each. How large must $g_{\mathrm{j}}$ be for the steady voltage to fall to half between neighbouring cells, and to $10\,\%$ at the tenth cell? How many connexon channels of $100\,\mathrm{pS}$ is that?

   **Answer** Half per cell means $r = 0.5$: $r + 1/r = 2.5 = 2 + g_{\mathrm{m}}/g_{\mathrm{j}}$, so $g_{\mathrm{m}}/g_{\mathrm{j}} = 0.5$ and $g_{\mathrm{j}} = 10\,\mathrm{nS}$, about 100 open channels. A voltage of $10\,\%$ at cell 10 means $r^{10} = 0.1$, $r = 0.794$; then $r + 1/r = 2.053$, $g_{\mathrm{m}}/g_{\mathrm{j}} = 0.053$ and $g_{\mathrm{j}} \approx 94\,\mathrm{nS}$, about 940 channels. Junctional plaques of thousands of channels are common, so coupling over several cells is easily achieved; closing most channels, as happens when $\ce{Ca^2+}$ rises, uncouples the cells.

7. Auxin added to a stem segment increases its growth rate fourfold within 15 minutes, without changing turgor. Using the Lockhart equation, what must have changed? Design two experiments that show the change is in the wall and that it is caused by acidification.

   **Answer** With $P$ constant, the rate $\varphi(P - Y)$ can rise only through a larger extensibility $\varphi$, a lower yield threshold $Y$, or both: auxin loosens the wall. Experiments: (a) measure wall properties directly by killing the tissue (freezing and thawing, which abolishes turgor and metabolism) and stretching it in an extensometer under constant load: walls from auxin-treated tissue, or walls incubated in acidic buffer, creep faster than walls at neutral pH; adding purified expansins to walls at low pH restores creep in walls whose own proteins were inactivated by heat. (b) Show that acidification is necessary and sufficient: acidic buffers (pH 4.5–5) induce rapid growth of living segments without auxin; neutral buffers, or inhibitors of the plasma membrane $\ce{H+}$-ATPase such as vanadate, block auxin-induced growth; fusicoccin, which activates the $\ce{H+}$-ATPase, mimics auxin.

8. (Open question) Tight junctions, adherens junctions and gap junctions are all disassembled at the start of the epithelial–mesenchymal transition. Explain why each would hinder a cell that is leaving an epithelium, and propose a measurement for each that would track the transition in a cultured epithelium.

   **Answer** Tight junctions keep apical and basolateral domains separate and attach the cell to its neighbours at the apex; a migrating cell must lose apicobasal polarity and its seals. Adherens junctions link its actin to that of neighbours and transmit tension; while they persist, the cell is held in the sheet, and E-cadherin signalling (Hippo, contact inhibition) restrains migration and proliferation. Gap junctions couple the cell electrically and metabolically, keeping its state similar to that of its neighbours and allowing the sheet to coordinate. Measurements: transepithelial electrical resistance (falls as tight junctions open) and immunostaining of ZO-1 and claudins; E-cadherin protein and mRNA (Western blot, qPCR) and the switch to N-cadherin and vimentin, with time-lapse imaging of cell scattering; dye transfer after scrape loading or microinjection of Lucifer yellow (coupling falls) and connexin 43 staining. Performing them in time series after inducing the transition with TGF-β would order the events.
