The previous chapter treated the nucleus as a compartment with a boundary and gates. This chapter looks at what the boundary encloses. Most of the volume of the nucleus is filled by its 46 chromosomes in a human cell, about two meters of DNA packed into a sphere a few micrometers across, and most of the rest by the nucleolus, the factory that makes **ribosomes**. The molecular structure of **chromatin**, from the **nucleosome** to loops and domains, is the subject of [7.2.4](/7.2.4), and the chemistry of its marks and their inheritance of [7.2.15](/7.2.15); here we take the view of the cell biologist and the cytologist, who see chromatin under the microscope, count and band chromosomes, and map where each part of the genome lies in the nucleus. We begin with chromatin as it appears in the light microscope and with the inactive X chromosome, the most visible case of chromatin states inherited by cells (17.1). We then describe the elements every chromosome needs (17.2), the proteins it carries (17.3), the karyotype by which chromosomes are recognized (17.4), two kinds of giant chromosome that made gene activity visible (17.5) and, briefly, the packing of the mitotic chromosome (17.6). Sections 17.7 and 17.8 map the **interphase** nucleus: **chromosome territories**, the nuclear periphery, nuclear bodies, and the nucleolus with its assembly line for ribosomes. Section 17.9 describes a recently recognized feature of **cancer** genomes, extrachromosomal DNA, whose random segregation is easily modeled. The genetics of chromosomes (dosage compensation, **aneuploidy**, rearrangements) is developed in [7.4](/7.4); the condensation and segregation of mitotic chromosomes in [7.3.21](/7.3.21).

## 17.1. Chromatin and Chromosomes in the Light Microscope

### 17.1.1. Chromatin and Chromosomes

The terms are a century older than their molecular meaning. Walther Flemming named **chromatin** (1879) after the material of the nucleus that stained strongly with basic dyes, and Wilhelm Waldeyer named the rod-shaped bodies that appear at cell division chromosomes (1888).

**Definition 17.1.1 (Chromosome)** A **chromosome** is a single DNA molecule (linear in the nuclei of eukaryotes) together with the proteins that package it, carrying a part of the genome and the elements needed for its replication and segregation. In interphase chromosomes are decondensed into chromatin; in mitosis and meiosis each condenses into a compact rod visible in the light microscope (after replication, two sister chromatids joined at the centromere).

Chromatin and chromosomes are therefore not different materials but the same DNA–protein complex in different states of compaction, which interconvert in every **cell cycle**. The haploid set of chromosomes carries the **genome**; a human diploid nucleus contains $2 \times 3.1 \times 10^9$ bp, about $2\,\mathrm{m}$ of DNA (at $0.34\,\mathrm{nm}$ per **base pair**), so the average chromosome, about $4.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ of DNA, is compacted about $10^4$-fold into a **metaphase** chromatid a few micrometers long ([7.2.4](/7.2.4#ex-4.6.1)).

### 17.1.2. Euchromatin and Heterochromatin

Under the light microscope, interphase chromatin is not uniform. Emil Heitz (1928), staining moss nuclei, distinguished **euchromatin**, which decondenses after **mitosis** and stains lightly, from **heterochromatin**, which stays condensed through interphase and stains darkly (both defined in [7.2.4](/7.2.4)). The cytological differences have molecular correlates: **euchromatin** is transcribed (it is labeled by brief pulses of $^3$H-uridine, [7.3.3](/7.3.3)) and replicates early in **S phase**; **heterochromatin** is largely silent, replicates late, lies at the nuclear periphery and around nucleoli, and silences genes moved into it (**position effect**, [7.2.15](/7.2.15)). Euchromatin is necessary but not sufficient for transcription: most euchromatic genes are silent in any one cell type.

**Definition 17.1.2 (Constitutive and Facultative Heterochromatin)** **Constitutive heterochromatin** is chromatin that is condensed in all cells and at all stages: in mammals mainly the pericentromeric regions, made of tandem satellite DNA, the telomeres, the secondary constrictions of some chromosomes and most of the long arm of the Y chromosome. It is gene-poor, marked by H3K9 trimethylation bound by HP1 and by DNA methylation, and replicates late; in mouse nuclei the pericentromeric regions of several chromosomes cluster into **chromocenters**. **Facultative heterochromatin** is chromatin that is heterochromatic only in some cells or at some stages and euchromatic in others, such as the inactive X chromosome of female mammals and developmentally silenced gene clusters, marked mainly by Polycomb-dependent H3K27 trimethylation.

**Constitutive heterochromatin**, built largely on **satellite DNA** ([7.2.2](/7.2.2#sec-2.2.3)), protects the genome: it suppresses recombination between repeats and **transposon** activity, and the pericentromeric heterochromatin recruits **cohesin** and helps **centromere** function. **Facultative heterochromatin** is a mechanism of gene regulation, and its amount increases with differentiation: **embryonic stem cells** have decondensed, mobile chromatin, whereas terminally differentiated cells such as neutrophils and the erythroblasts that will expel their nucleus have highly condensed nuclei.

The rod photoreceptors of nocturnal mammals give a striking demonstration that the position of heterochromatin is controlled. In mouse rods, heterochromatin is not at the periphery but in a single central mass, surrounded by a shell of euchromatin. This "inverted" nucleus acts as a microlens that focuses light along the stacked photoreceptors and reduces scattering in the retina (Solovei and colleagues, 2009). Inversion occurs because rods lack both of the two tethers that normally attach heterochromatin to the lamina, the **lamin** B receptor and lamin A/C ([7.3.16](/7.3.16)); without tethers, heterochromatin coalesces in the center (Solovei and colleagues, 2013).

### 17.1.3. X-Chromosome Inactivation

**Definition 17.1.3 (X-Chromosome Inactivation)** **X-chromosome inactivation** (XCI) is the transcriptional silencing, early in the development of female mammals, of all but one X chromosome in each somatic cell by its conversion into facultative heterochromatin. It equalizes the dose of X-linked gene products between XX females and XY males (dosage compensation). The inactive X (Xi), condensed against the nuclear envelope or the nucleolus in interphase, is visible as the **Barr body** (Barr and Bertram, 1949, in neurons of female cats).

Mary Lyon proposed in 1961 that one X chromosome is inactivated at random in each cell of the early embryo and that the choice is inherited by the cell's descendants. The mechanism has three features:

1. Counting and choice. A cell counts its X chromosomes relative to its sets of autosomes and inactivates all but one; the number of Barr bodies is the number of X chromosomes minus one (none in XO females with Turner syndrome, one in XXY males with Klinefelter syndrome, two in XXX females). In the cells of the epiblast of the mouse (at about embryonic day 5.5) the choice between the maternal and the paternal X is random; in the extraembryonic tissues of mice, an earlier imprinted form inactivates the paternal X, and marsupials inactivate the paternal X everywhere.
2. Initiation in cis by a **noncoding RNA**. The X-inactivation center on the X chromosome contains the gene *XIST* (*Xist* in mouse), which is transcribed only from the future inactive X. The **long noncoding RNA** Xist, about $17\,\mathrm{kb}$, coats the chromosome that makes it, spreading in three dimensions through its territory, and recruits silencing factors: SPEN, which brings **histone deacetylase** activity to **promoters**; Polycomb complexes PRC1 and PRC2, which add H2AK119 **ubiquitination** and H3K27 trimethylation; and, later, the variant histone macroH2A and **DNA methylation** of the **CpG islands** of X-linked promoters.
3. Clonal maintenance. Once established, the inactive state is maintained through all later divisions, even if *XIST* is deleted, by redundant marks; it is reversed only in the germline of females, so that every oocyte carries an active X.

About $15\%$ of human X-linked genes escape inactivation, among them the genes in the pseudoautosomal regions shared with the Y chromosome, and the expression of these escape genes in two doses partly explains the phenotypes of X aneuploidies. Other animals solve dosage compensation differently: male *Drosophila* double the transcription of their single X (by the MSL complex and H4K16 acetylation), and *Caenorhabditis elegans* hermaphrodites halve the expression of both X chromosomes with a condensin-like dosage-compensation complex; birds, with ZW females, compensate only partially ([7.4](/7.4)).

Because the choice is random and clonally inherited, every female mammal is a mosaic of patches of cells expressing either the maternal or the paternal X. The patches are visible when the two X chromosomes carry different alleles of a gene with a visible phenotype. In calico and tortoiseshell cats, which are almost always female, patches of orange and black fur correspond to clones that express the orange or the nonorange allele of the X-linked *Orange* locus (the white areas are due to an independent autosomal spotting gene); in female mice carrying a fluorescent-protein reporter on one X, the photoreceptors of the retina form radial stripes of labeled and unlabeled cells, reflecting the migration of clones during development (Wu and colleagues, 2014). In women heterozygous for the X-linked disease anhidrotic ectodermal dysplasia, patches of skin lack sweat glands.

Random inactivation does not contradict X-linked inheritance: the alleles a woman transmits follow Mendel's rules, because the germline reactivates the X, whereas XCI determines only how the alleles are expressed in her somatic tissues. A heterozygous carrier of an X-linked recessive mutation is usually healthy, because roughly half of her cells express the normal allele. Her phenotype depends, however, on the proportion of cells that silenced the normal allele, which varies by chance.

**Proposition 17.1.1 (Skewing of X Inactivation by Chance)** Suppose that a tissue descends from $n$ precursor cells present when inactivation occurs, each independently inactivating its paternal X with probability $1/2$, and that the descendants of all precursors contribute equally. Then the fraction $F$ of cells with an active maternal X satisfies $nF \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, 1/2)$, with mean $1/2$ and variance $1/(4n)$, and the probability of a skew at least as extreme as $f$ : $(1 - f)$ (with $f > 1/2$) is

$$
\mathbb{P}(\lvert F - 1/2\rvert \ge f - 1/2) = 2\sum_{k \ge nf} \binom{n}{k} 2^{-n}.
\tag{17.1}
$$

**Proof** Each precursor contributes an independent Bernoulli variable with $p = 1/2$; the number of precursors with an active maternal X is therefore binomial, and since every precursor contributes the same share of cells, $F$ is this number divided by $n$. The distribution is symmetric about $n/2$, so the probability of a deviation in either direction is twice the upper tail. $\square$

For $n = 8$ the probability of a skew of $80{:}20$ or more is $2(1 + 8)/256 \approx 7\%$; for $n = 16$ it falls to about $2\%$. Estimates of the number of precursors from the variance of inactivation ratios in blood are of this order (roughly 10–20 hematopoietic precursors at the time of inactivation), so a few percent of women are expected to show strong skewing by chance alone, and such women can express an X-linked disease ("manifesting heterozygotes", for example carriers of Duchenne muscular dystrophy with muscle weakness). Real tissues skew further, because clones expressing a deleterious allele can be selected against, and because blood becomes clonal with age. The model also explains why the size of the patches tells the time of inactivation and the pattern of growth: coherent growth of clones gives large patches, mixing of cells gives fine-grained mosaics.

## 17.2. The Functional Elements of a Chromosome

### 17.2.1. Origins, Centromeres and Telomeres

To be replicated, kept intact and passed on at each division, a eukaryotic chromosome needs three kinds of DNA element, and the demonstration that these are sufficient was the construction of artificial chromosomes. In budding yeast, a linear DNA molecule became a stable chromosome when it contained an autonomously replicating sequence (ARS, an **origin of replication**), a centromere (CEN) and two **telomeres** (TEL), the **yeast artificial chromosome** (Murray and Szostak, 1983), which was later used to clone segments of hundreds of kilobases ([7.2.5](/7.2.5)).

| Element | Copies per chromosome | Function | Structure (examples) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Replication origin** | many (human genome: some $30\,000$–$50\,000$ used per S phase) | initiation of replication after **origin licensing** in G1 ([7.2.7](/7.2.7#sec-7.6.1)) | yeast ARS: $\sim 100$–$200\,\mathrm{bp}$ with an 11-bp consensus; metazoan origins: no consensus, defined by chromatin |
| Centromere | one | assembly of the **kinetochore**, attachment to spindle **microtubules**, cohesion of sister chromatids until **anaphase** ([7.3.21](/7.3.21)) | point centromere of *S. cerevisiae*, $125\,\mathrm{bp}$ binding one microtubule; regional centromeres of *S. pombe* ($40$–$110\,\mathrm{kb}$) and humans ($0.3$–$5\,\mathrm{Mb}$ of α-satellite, 171-bp repeats organized into higher-order repeats); holocentric chromosomes (*C. elegans*) with microtubule attachment along their length |
| Telomere | two | protection of the ends from degradation, fusion and the DNA-damage response; solution of the end-replication problem | human: $5$–$15\,\mathrm{kb}$ of TTAGGG repeats ending in a $50$–$300$-nt 3′ overhang, folded into a T-loop and bound by shelterin |

The **centromere** of most eukaryotes ([7.2.2](/7.2.2#sec-2.3.1)) is specified epigenetically rather than by sequence: it is marked by nucleosomes containing the histone H3 variant CENP-A, which are copied to the same site in each cell cycle. Human neocentromeres, which arise occasionally at new sites lacking α-satellite DNA and are stably inherited, show that the satellite is neither necessary nor sufficient; conversely, dicentric chromosomes are stabilized by inactivation of one centromere, as on human chromosome 2, which arose by fusion of two ancestral ape chromosomes and still carries the remnants of a second centromere and of telomere repeats at the fusion point (2q13). In the light microscope, the centromere is the primary constriction of the metaphase chromosome.

Centromere and **kinetochore** are not synonyms. The centromere is the chromosomal region, DNA and chromatin; the kinetochore is the protein machine assembled on it that binds spindle microtubules ([7.3.21](/7.3.21)). Classical electron microscopy and cytology distinguished three domains in the centromeric region of a mitotic chromosome:

| Domain (classical) | Position and appearance | Modern counterpart |
|---|---|---|
| Kinetochore domain | on the outer surface: a trilaminar plate (inner plate, electron-lucent middle zone, outer plate) and, around unattached kinetochores, a fibrous corona | inner kinetochore (the CCAN proteins on CENP-A chromatin) and outer kinetochore (the KMN network that binds microtubules); the corona of **dynein**, CENP-E and checkpoint proteins |
| Central domain | below it, the bulk of the centromere | CENP-A chromatin interspersed with H3 chromatin on α-satellite arrays |
| Pairing domain | innermost, between the sister chromatids | pericentromeric heterochromatin where centromeric cohesin holds the sisters until anaphase |

The first centromere proteins were found with autoantibodies from patients with the CREST form of scleroderma (anticentromere antibodies), which stain the centromeres of all chromosomes; they identified CENP-A, CENP-B and CENP-C (Earnshaw and Rothfield, 1985), further sera revealed CENP-E and CENP-F, which bind kinetochores only in mitosis, and the list now runs to CENP-X, most of the later ones members of the constitutive centromere-associated network (CCAN). CENP-B, the only one that binds a specific DNA sequence (the 17-bp CENP-B box of α-satellite), is dispensable for centromere function.

The **telomere** ([7.2.2](/7.2.2#sec-2.3.2)) solves two problems. Because lagging-strand synthesis cannot copy the very end of a linear molecule, telomeric DNA would shorten by $50$–$200\,\mathrm{bp}$ at each replication if it were not extended by **telomerase**, the **reverse transcriptase** that adds TTAGGG repeats using its own RNA template ([7.2.7](/7.2.7#sec-7.6.3)). And a natural chromosome end must not be recognized as a double-strand break. The six-subunit shelterin complex (TRF1, TRF2, POT1, TIN2, TPP1 and RAP1) binds the repeats: TRF2 folds the end into a T-loop (Griffith and colleagues, 1999), in which the 3′ overhang invades the duplex upstream and is hidden, and suppresses the kinase ATM; POT1 binds the single-stranded overhang and suppresses ATR. Most somatic human cells have little **telomerase**, so their telomeres shorten with every division until critically short telomeres trigger a permanent arrest, **replicative senescence** ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)). Germ cells and some stem cells express telomerase, and so do about $85$–$90\%$ of cancers; most of the others maintain their telomeres by recombination (alternative lengthening of telomeres, ALT). Expression of telomerase together with the inactivation of **tumor** suppressors is one route to immortalizing cultured cells ([7.3.3](/7.3.3)).

### 17.2.2. What Else Is in a Chromosome

The other $98\%$ of the human genome, which is the subject of [7.2.2](/7.2.2), can be summarized in one table:

| Component | Share of the human genome | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| Protein-coding **exons** | about $1.5\%$ | about $20\,000$ genes, most of them single copy; gene families arose by duplication |
| **Introns** and untranslated regions | about $25\%$ | |
| LINEs (mostly L1) | about $17$–$21\%$ | full-length L1 $6\,\mathrm{kb}$; about $500\,000$ copies, of which about $100$ are still active |
| **SINEs** (mostly Alu) | about $11$–$13\%$ | Alu elements of about $300\,\mathrm{bp}$, more than one million copies |
| LTR **retrotransposons** and **DNA transposons** | about $8\%$ and $3\%$ | almost all inactive |
| Satellites and other tandem repeats | about $3$–$6\%$ | α-satellite at centromeres, satellites II and III, **microsatellites** |
| Multicopy genes | small | rRNA genes (about $400$ copies of the 45S unit, 17.8), 5S rRNA genes, histone genes, tRNA genes (several hundred) |

(The cytological division of repetitive DNA into unique, moderately repetitive and highly repetitive sequences comes from reassociation kinetics, [7.2.2](/7.2.2#sec-2.2.2).) Microsatellites, also called short tandem repeats, are tandem repeats of 2–6-bp units at tens of thousands of loci; the number of repeat units at a locus is highly polymorphic, so a set of 20 such loci, amplified by PCR, identifies an individual with near certainty and establishes parentage. This is the basis of forensic DNA profiling. Before PCR, the longer and equally polymorphic minisatellites were used in the first "DNA fingerprints" (Jeffreys, 1985).

## 17.3. Chromosomal Proteins

Isolated interphase chromatin contains DNA, histones, nonhistone proteins and RNA in mass ratios of roughly $1 : 1 : (0.5\text{–}1.5) : 0.05$ (classical analyses of rat liver chromatin): the histones match the DNA in mass, the nonhistone proteins vary with cell type and activity, and the RNA is mostly nascent transcripts. DNA and histones are the constant components. The proteins are of two kinds.

**Histones** ([7.2.4](/7.2.4#sec-4.1.1)) are small, strongly basic proteins rich in lysine and arginine that bind DNA without sequence specificity; the four core histones (H2A, H2B, H3, H4) form the nucleosome core, and the linker histone H1 binds at its entry and exit. They are among the most conserved of all proteins (H4 of peas and cows differ at two residues), and they are separated into their five classes by gel electrophoresis. The linker histone H1 is the exception: it is less conserved, has several subtypes that differ between species, tissues and developmental stages (H1⁰ in nondividing cells, H5 in the nucleated erythrocytes of birds, testis-specific forms), and it is less tightly bound, exchanging within minutes in living cells. From the cell's point of view, two classes must be distinguished:

| | Canonical (replication-dependent) histones | Histone variants |
|---|---|---|
| Synthesis | in S phase, coupled to DNA replication | throughout the cell cycle and in nondividing cells |
| Genes | clustered, multicopy, without introns; mRNAs without poly(A) tail, ending in a stem-loop bound by SLBP | single or few copies, with introns, polyadenylated mRNAs |
| Deposition | behind the replication fork by CAF-1 | replication-independent, at particular loci by dedicated chaperones |
| Examples | H2A, H2B, H3.1/H3.2, H4 | H3.3, CENP-A, H2A.Z, H2A.X, macroH2A ([7.2.4](/7.2.4#sec-4.5)) |

**Definition 17.3.1 (Histone Variant)** A **histone variant** is a nonallelic form of a core or linker histone, encoded by a separate gene and differing from the canonical histone in a few to many residues, that is incorporated into chromatin independently of replication at particular sites and gives the nucleosomes containing it distinct properties.

The variants mark functional regions of chromosomes: CENP-A the centromere, H3.3 transcribed genes and regulatory elements, H2A.Z promoters, macroH2A the inactive X; phosphorylated H2A.X ($\gamma$H2A.X) marks megabase domains around double-strand breaks. In the most extreme reorganization of chromatin, during spermiogenesis, histones are largely replaced by protamines, small arginine-rich proteins that pack sperm DNA about tenfold more tightly than in somatic nuclei.

Nonhistone proteins make up the rest, a heterogeneous set that differs between cell types and, unlike canonical histones, is made throughout the cycle. As a class they are acidic (rich in Asp and Glu) and often phosphorylated; those that bind DNA specifically read its sequence through hydrogen bonds and ionic contacts with the bases in the major groove, and the sequences they recognize are often conserved in evolution. They include abundant structural proteins (the HMG proteins, which bend DNA; topoisomerase II and the SMC complexes condensin and cohesin, [7.2.4](/7.2.4#sec-4.3.2); HP1 of heterochromatin; MeCP2, which in neurons is nearly as abundant as the histone octamer), the sequence-specific transcription factors with their DNA-binding motifs (**helix–turn–helix**, **zinc finger**, **leucine zipper**, **basic helix–loop–helix**, [7.2.14](/7.2.14)), and the enzymes that replicate, repair, transcribe and modify chromatin.

## 17.4. Karyotype and Chromosome Banding

### 17.4.1. The Karyotype

**Definition 17.4.1 (Karyotype)** The **karyotype** is the chromosome complement of a cell or species as seen at mitotic metaphase: the number of chromosomes and the size, centromere position and banding pattern of each. A karyogram is an image of the chromosomes of one cell arranged in homologous pairs in a standard order, and an ideogram is a diagram of the banding pattern of each chromosome.

Chromosome numbers vary widely and have little relation to the complexity or genome size of an organism:

| Species | Chromosome number |
|---|---|
| *Homo sapiens* | $2n = 46$ |
| *Pan troglodytes* (chimpanzee) | $2n = 48$ |
| *Mus musculus* | $2n = 40$, all acrocentric |
| *Drosophila melanogaster* | $2n = 8$ |
| *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* | $n = 16$ |
| *Arabidopsis thaliana* | $2n = 10$ |
| *Oryza sativa* (rice) | $2n = 24$ |
| *Triticum aestivum* (bread wheat) | $2n = 6x = 42$ (hexaploid, basic number $x = 7$) |
| Indian muntjac (*Muntiacus muntjak*) | $2n = 6$ (female), $7$ (male) |
| *Myrmecia pilosula* (an ant) | $n = 1$ in males |
| *Ophioglossum reticulatum* (a fern) | $2n \approx 1260$ |

Here $n$ is the number of chromosomes in a haploid set and $x$ the basic number of a polyploid series. Closely related species often differ by fusions and fissions that do not change gene content, as human chromosome 2 shows (17.2.1). Chromosomes are classified by the position of the centromere, which divides them into a short arm p (*petit*) and a long arm q: metacentric (arms about equal), submetacentric, acrocentric (subtelocentric in some nomenclatures; p arm very short; in humans chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22, whose short arms carry the rRNA genes, 17.8) and telocentric (centromere at the end; none in humans). Human chromosomes are numbered roughly by decreasing size and arranged in groups A–G, with the sex chromosomes X and Y. Besides the primary constriction, some chromosomes have a secondary constriction, a narrow, lightly staining segment whose number, position and length are characteristic of the chromosome. Most secondary constrictions are nucleolar organizer regions (17.8), but not all: the large heterochromatic secondary constrictions near the centromeres of human chromosomes 1, 9 and 16 are blocks of satellite DNA. The short segment beyond a secondary constriction at the end of a chromosome is called a satellite (or trabant), a cytological term unrelated to satellite DNA; in humans the five acrocentric chromosomes carry satellites on their short arms, beyond the NORs.

### 17.4.2. Banding

**Definition 17.4.2 (Chromosome Banding)** **Chromosome banding** is the staining of metaphase or prometaphase chromosomes so that each shows a reproducible pattern of transverse light and dark bands, allowing each chromosome and each region of it to be identified. The main methods are G-banding (mild trypsin digestion, then Giemsa stain), Q-banding (quinacrine fluorescence, the same pattern as G), R-banding (heat denaturation before staining, the reverse pattern), C-banding (alkali and salt treatment, staining only constitutive heterochromatin) and NOR staining (silver nitrate, staining the active rRNA gene clusters). G, Q and R bands cover the whole length of every chromosome, whereas C, NOR, T (telomeric) and Cd (centromeric dot) staining mark only particular regions.

The bands reflect the large-scale composition of the genome. Dark G bands are AT-rich, gene-poor, late-replicating and broadly correspond to the B compartment and the lamina-associated domains of interphase (17.7); light G bands are GC-rich, gene-dense and early-replicating. A metaphase karyotype shows about 400 bands per haploid set, a prometaphase one about 850, so that one band contains on average several megabases of DNA. Bands are named by the International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN), from the centromere outward by arm, region, band and subband: 1p36.3 is subband 3 of band 6 in region 3 of the short arm of chromosome 1. A karyotype is written as the number of chromosomes, the sex chromosomes and any abnormality: 46,XX; 47,XY,+21 for a boy with Down syndrome; 46,XY,t(9;22)(q34;q11) for the reciprocal translocation that creates the Philadelphia chromosome and the *BCR–ABL* fusion gene of chronic myeloid leukemia ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

Karyotype analysis is used to diagnose chromosome disorders, to study the relationships of species, and to follow the evolution of tumors, whose karyotypes are often grossly abnormal. Its resolution is limited to rearrangements of a few megabases. **Fluorescence in situ hybridization** with probes for whole chromosomes (chromosome painting, with 24 colors in multiplex FISH and spectral karyotyping) or for single loci, comparative genomic hybridization on microarrays, and genome sequencing now detect smaller changes; the genetics of chromosome abnormalities is treated in [7.4](/7.4).

## 17.5. Special Chromosomes

Two kinds of giant chromosome, in which interphase chromosomes are visible and gene activity can be watched, gave the first direct evidence that genes are transcribed at particular sites and times.

### 17.5.1. Polytene Chromosomes

**Definition 17.5.1 (Polytene Chromosome)** A **polytene chromosome** is a giant interphase chromosome made of many identical chromatids lying side by side in register, produced by repeated rounds of DNA replication without cell division (endoreplication). In the larval salivary glands of *Drosophila*, about ten rounds of endoreplication produce about a thousand chromatids per chromosome, and the homologues are paired along their length (somatic pairing), giving cables visible in the light microscope with a reproducible pattern of dark bands and light interbands.

**Definition 17.5.2 (Endoreplication)** **Endoreplication** (an endocycle) is a cell cycle that alternates S and G phases without mitosis or cytokinesis, so that the DNA content of the cell doubles with each cycle; it produces polytene chromosomes when the sister chromatids remain aligned, and polyploid nuclei when they separate.

Polytene chromosomes were first seen by Édouard-Gérard Balbiani (1881) in the salivary glands of the midge *Chironomus* and are typical of the larval tissues of flies (Diptera); they also occur in other larval tissues of flies (Malpighian tubules, gut, fat body, trachea), in plant suspensors and other cells of the ovule, and in the developing macronuclei of ciliates. (The older term endomitosis, sometimes used for their formation, refers to a different process, in which chromosomes condense within an intact nuclear envelope without separating.) In *Drosophila* the four chromosomes are joined at their pericentromeric heterochromatin, which is underreplicated, into a single chromocenter, and their arms show about 5000 bands, each containing on average some tens of kilobases of DNA. Because the banding pattern is fixed, deletions, duplications and inversions appear as missing bands, extra bands and loops in paired homologues; the band maps of Calvin Bridges (1935) were the first physical maps of a genome and were used for decades to map genes cytologically, including by in situ hybridization.

At certain sites and times, bands decondense into swellings called puffs, and very large puffs of *Chironomus* are called Balbiani rings.

**Definition 17.5.3 (Chromosome Puff)** A **chromosome puff** is a locally decondensed region of a polytene chromosome, where the chromatin of a band has unfolded and is being actively transcribed; puffs are labeled by $^3$H-uridine and contain RNA polymerase II and nascent RNA.

The sequence of puffs that follows a pulse of the steroid hormone ecdysone at the end of larval life was the first gene regulatory cascade to be seen (Ashburner, 1970s): a few early puffs appear within minutes, even when protein synthesis is blocked with cycloheximide; dozens of late puffs follow hours later and require protein synthesis, and the early puffs regress. Ashburner inferred that the ecdysone receptor directly activates a few early genes, whose protein products activate the late genes and repress the early ones; the early genes were later found to encode transcription factors (E74, E75, Broad), exactly as predicted. The giant Balbiani-ring transcripts of *Chironomus* also allowed the export of an mRNA particle to be followed from the gene to the nuclear pore by electron microscopy ([7.3.16](/7.3.16#sec-16.5.4)).

### 17.5.2. Lampbrush Chromosomes

**Definition 17.5.4 (Lampbrush Chromosome)** A **lampbrush chromosome** is a meiotic bivalent of the diplotene stage of growing oocytes, found in amphibians, birds, reptiles, fish and many invertebrates, whose chromatin forms a central axis of condensed chromomeres from which pairs of lateral loops of extended chromatin project, one loop from each sister chromatid, giving the bivalent the appearance of a nineteenth-century lamp brush. Each loop is a region of intense transcription by RNA polymerase II, covered by nascent ribonucleoprotein.

Oocytes of amphibians remain in diplotene for months while they grow to more than a millimeter in diameter and store the RNAs and proteins that the embryo will use before its own genome is activated ([7.5](/7.5)). Their lampbrush chromosomes are correspondingly enormous: an amphibian lampbrush bivalent can be up to $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ long and its loops tens of micrometers. A loop contains one or a few transcription units, and the nascent RNP fibrils grow longer from one end of the loop to the other, so that each loop is thin at its start and thick at its end, showing the direction of transcription; electron micrographs of spread loops reveal polymerases packed nearly as densely as on rRNA genes. In the light microscope, loops can be labeled by immunostaining for RNA polymerase II or splicing factors, and lampbrush chromosomes of the chicken are used to map sequences cytologically at high resolution.

### 17.5.3. B Chromosomes

A third kind of special chromosome is defined not by its structure but by its inheritance. Lewis Randolph (1928) called the normal chromosomes of maize A chromosomes and the extra, dispensable ones found in some plants B chromosomes.

**Definition 17.5.5 (B Chromosome)** A **B chromosome** (supernumerary chromosome) is a chromosome present in some individuals of a species but not others, in addition to the standard set (the A chromosomes), with which it does not pair or recombine; it is not needed for normal development and usually carries few functional genes.

B chromosomes are known in thousands of species of plants, animals and fungi, among them maize, rye, grasshoppers and some fish and rodents. They are often largely heterochromatic, and their number varies between cells and individuals. Since they bring no benefit and often reduce fertility when numerous, they persist by drive: they are transmitted to more than half of the gametes, for example by directed nondisjunction at a pollen mitosis of rye and maize, which places both chromatids in the nucleus that will form the sperm, so they are classic selfish genetic elements ([7.4](/7.4)). In the human karyotype, small supernumerary marker chromosomes found in a few per thousand people are derived from A chromosomes and are not B chromosomes in this sense.

## 17.6. From DNA to the Mitotic Chromosome

The molecular levels of chromatin packing are treated in [7.2.4](/7.2.4#sec-4.3), and only the overall picture is summarized here:

| Level | Diameter or size | Compaction relative to DNA | Organized by |
|---|---|---|---|
| DNA double helix | $2\,\mathrm{nm}$ | 1 | |
| Nucleosome chain ("10 nm fiber") | $11\,\mathrm{nm}$ | 6–7 | histone octamers, 147 bp per core |
| Irregular, locally folded nucleosome chain | $5$–$24\,\mathrm{nm}$ domains | about 40 | nucleosome–nucleosome contacts, H1, cations |
| Loops and domains (interphase) | $0.1$–$1\,\mathrm{Mb}$ loops | variable | cohesin and CTCF (**loop extrusion**) |
| Mitotic chromatid | about $700\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide | about $10^4$ | condensin I and II, topoisomerase IIα |

Older textbooks, and the course slides, describe the chromosome as a hierarchy of regular helices: a 30-nm solenoid of nucleosomes coiled into a $400\,\mathrm{nm}$ "supersolenoid", which coils again into the chromatid, with compaction factors multiplying to $7 \times 6 \times 40 \times 5 = 8400$. This model is no longer accepted. Regular 30-nm fibers form in vitro but are not found in most nuclei ([7.2.4](/7.2.4#sec-4.3.1)), and mitotic chromosomes are made by condensin: condensin II first forms large loops of about $400\,\mathrm{kb}$ around a helical axis, and condensin I nests smaller loops within them (Gibcus and colleagues, 2018). The "chromosome scaffold" seen after histone extraction (Paulson and Laemmli, 1977) is the line of condensin complexes at the bases of the loops ([7.3.16](/7.3.16#sec-16.3.4)). Two further proteins shape mitotic chromosomes as physical objects: topoisomerase IIα resolves the intertwined sister chromatids, and Ki-67, coating the surface of chromosomes, acts as a surfactant that keeps them individualized and prevents them from coalescing after the nuclear envelope breaks down (Cuylen and colleagues, 2016). The mechanics of condensation and segregation are treated with mitosis in [7.3.21](/7.3.21).

## 17.7. The Three-Dimensional Organization of the Interphase Nucleus

### 17.7.1. Chromosome Territories

**Definition 17.7.1 (Chromosome Territory)** A **chromosome territory** is the compact, roughly spherical region of the interphase nucleus occupied by one chromosome. Territories are separated by an interchromatin space of lower chromatin density and intermingle only at their borders.

Theodor Boveri inferred territories in 1909 from the observation that chromosomes reappear in the same arrangement after each division. Direct evidence came in the 1980s: when a small spot of a living nucleus was damaged by a laser microbeam and the cells were allowed to proceed to mitosis, only a few chromosomes carried damage, as expected if each occupies a small region (Cremer and colleagues, 1982). **Fluorescence in situ hybridization** with probes for whole chromosomes (chromosome painting) then showed the territories directly.

Territories are not randomly placed. In the spherical nuclei of human lymphocytes, gene-rich chromosomes (such as 19) are in the nuclear interior and gene-poor chromosomes of similar size (such as 18) at the periphery (Croft and colleagues, 1999); in flat fibroblast nuclei, position correlates more with size. Within a territory, active genes tend to lie at its surface, and very active genes or gene clusters can loop out of their territory into the interchromatin space: the thyroglobulin gene, $270\,\mathrm{kb}$ long and transcribed at a very high rate in thyroid cells, decondenses and extends out of its territory there but not in other cells; the *Hoxb* cluster moves out of its territory when it is activated during differentiation. The proximity of territories also influences which rearrangements occur: translocations form most often between chromosomes that are neighbors in the nucleus of the cell type concerned, such as the translocation between *MYC* on chromosome 8 and the immunoglobulin heavy-chain locus on chromosome 14 in B cells.

### 17.7.2. The Nuclear Periphery and Compartments

**Definition 17.7.2 (Lamina-Associated Domain)** A **lamina-associated domain** (LAD) is a region of the genome, typically $0.1$–$10\,\mathrm{Mb}$ long, that contacts the nuclear lamina. LADs were mapped by DamID, in which the DNA adenine methyltransferase of *E. coli* is fused to lamin B1, so that it methylates the DNA that comes near the lamina, which is then identified by sequencing (van Steensel and colleagues).

In human cells there are about 1000–1500 LADs, covering about a third of the genome. They are gene-poor, transcriptionally silent, late-replicating and enriched in H3K9 dimethylation and trimethylation; many coincide with the dark G bands. Some LADs are constitutive, at the lamina in all cell types; others are facultative, released from the lamina when their genes are activated during differentiation. The association is dynamic and stochastic: in any single cell only about $30\%$ of LADs are at the periphery, the rest are near the nucleolus (nucleolus-associated domains overlap LADs extensively) or in the interior, and after each mitosis LADs are partly reshuffled. Tethering of a gene to the lamina represses some genes but not all, so the periphery is a repressive environment rather than a switch.

The same organization appears in the contact maps of **chromosome conformation capture** and Hi-C ([7.2.4](/7.2.4#sec-4.3.2)), which measure which loci are close in space. At the largest scale, contacts are most frequent within each chromosome, which is the territory seen in sequencing data. At the scale of megabases the genome is divided into an A compartment (active, gene-rich, interior) and a B compartment (inactive, gene-poor, peripheral and perinucleolar), corresponding to LADs and the dark bands; at the scale of $0.1$–$1\,\mathrm{Mb}$ into **topologically associating domains**, formed by loop extrusion; and at the smallest scale into loops between convergent CTCF sites. Imaging of chromatin in single cells (multiplexed DNA FISH that traces the path of a chromosome segment) shows that domain-like structures exist in each cell but with boundaries that vary from cell to cell; the TADs of Hi-C maps are population averages, and single-cell domains persist even when cohesin is removed (Bintu and colleagues, 2018). Conversely, acute removal of cohesin erases TADs from Hi-C maps but leaves compartments intact (Rao and colleagues, 2017). Compartments reflect a phase separation of chromatin types, while TADs reflect loop extrusion, two mechanisms that compete.

| Scale | Structure | Typical size | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole chromosome | territory | $50$–$250\,\mathrm{Mb}$ | polymer confinement, slow mixing |
| Megabases | A/B compartments, LADs | $1$–$10\,\mathrm{Mb}$ | association of like chromatin, tethering to lamina and nucleolus |
| Sub-megabase | TADs | $0.1$–$1\,\mathrm{Mb}$ | loop extrusion by cohesin, stopped by CTCF |
| Tens to hundreds of kb | loops, enhancer–promoter contacts | $10$–$500\,\mathrm{kb}$ | CTCF–cohesin loops, transcription factors |

### 17.7.3. Nuclear Bodies

**Definition 17.7.3 (Nuclear Body)** A **nuclear body** is a membraneless structure of the nucleoplasm, visible by light or electron microscopy, in which particular proteins and RNAs are concentrated; most are biomolecular condensates that exchange their components with the nucleoplasm within seconds to minutes ([7.2.21](/7.2.21)).

Most of them are **biomolecular condensates**:

| Nuclear body | Number per nucleus | Markers | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nucleolus | 1–5 | rDNA, Pol I, fibrillarin, nucleophosmin | ribosome biogenesis (17.8) |
| Cajal body | 1–5 | coilin, SMN, scaRNAs | maturation and modification of snRNPs and snoRNPs; assembly of telomerase RNP |
| Nuclear speckles | 20–50 | SRSF2 (SC35), SON, SRRM2, *MALAT1* RNA | storage and supply of splicing factors; highly expressed genes cluster around them |
| Paraspeckles | 5–20 | *NEAT1* RNA, NONO, SFPQ | retention of edited RNAs ([7.2.21](/7.2.21#sec-21.5.1)) |
| PML bodies | 10–30 | PML protein, SUMO, Sp100, DAXX | stress responses, senescence, antiviral defense; protein modification by SUMO |
| Histone locus body | 2–4 | NPAT, FLASH | processing of replication-dependent histone mRNAs at the histone gene clusters |
| Polycomb bodies | variable | PRC1 | clusters of Polycomb-repressed genes |

**Definition 17.7.4 (Cajal Body, Nuclear Speckle and PML Body)** A **Cajal body** (coiled body; described by Santiago Ramón y Cajal in 1903) is a nuclear body marked by coilin in which snRNPs and snoRNPs are assembled and modified by small Cajal body–specific RNAs. **Nuclear speckles** (interchromatin granule clusters) are irregular nuclear bodies in the interchromatin space that store pre-mRNA splicing factors. A **PML body** is a nuclear body formed by the promyelocytic leukemia protein, a SUMO-modified scaffold that recruits many partner proteins.

PML bodies take their name from acute promyelocytic leukemia, in which the translocation t(15;17) fuses PML to the retinoic acid receptor α. The fusion protein disperses PML bodies and blocks differentiation of myeloid cells; arsenic trioxide, which binds PML and triggers its SUMOylation and degradation, together with retinoic acid, cures most patients, one of the rare cases in which a drug acts through a nuclear body.

## 17.8. The Nucleolus and Ribosome Biogenesis

### 17.8.1. Structure

**Definition 17.8.1 (Nucleolus)** The **nucleolus** is the largest nuclear body, the site of rRNA synthesis, processing and the assembly of ribosomal subunits. It forms around the clusters of tandemly repeated genes for the large rRNA precursor, the **nucleolar organizer regions** (NORs), and in amniotes it has three components visible by electron microscopy: **fibrillar centers** (FC), pale regions containing rDNA, RNA polymerase I and its factor UBF; the **dense fibrillar component** (DFC), surrounding the FCs, containing nascent pre-rRNA and the modification machinery (fibrillarin); and the **granular component** (GC), the surrounding mass of preribosomal particles in later stages of assembly (nucleophosmin).

The NORs were named by Barbara McClintock (1934), who saw that a nucleolus forms at a particular site of a maize chromosome. In humans they lie on the short arms of the five acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22), each carrying a tandem array of the $43\,\mathrm{kb}$ rDNA repeat ($13.3\,\mathrm{kb}$ transcribed, the rest intergenic spacer), about 400 copies in all (the number varies between individuals, from roughly 200 to 600 per diploid genome, and older textbooks give about 200), of which typically only about half are active; the silent copies are heterochromatic. In the light microscope the NORs are the secondary constrictions (stalks) of the acrocentric chromosomes. Several NORs usually cluster into one or a few nucleoli, which may fuse during interphase, so a human nucleus has one to five nucleoli. The size of the nucleolus reflects the activity of ribosome synthesis: it is large in growing and secretory cells and in cancer cells, small or absent in quiescent lymphocytes, sperm and muscle.

The three layers are the consequence of the condensate nature of the nucleolus ([7.2.21](/7.2.21#sec-21.5.1)), which behaves as a multiphase liquid: the DFC and GC are immiscible phases scaffolded by fibrillarin and nucleophosmin, nested according to their interfacial tensions, and pre-rRNA moves outward through them as it matures, like a product on an assembly line. Tripartite nucleoli are found in amniotes; most other eukaryotes, including fish, amphibians and yeast, have bipartite nucleoli without distinct fibrillar centers, so the third compartment arose during the evolution of the vertebrates, as rDNA spacers became longer (Thiry and Lafontaine, 2005). Like other condensates, the nucleolus scales with the cell: in early *C. elegans* embryos, nucleolar size falls with cell size because the concentration of its components, not their amount per cell, determines whether and how large they condense (Weber and Brangwynne, 2015), a cell-level example of the **limiting-pool model** of organelle size ([7.3.6](/7.3.6#sec-6.2)).

Spreads of nucleolar chromatin made by Oscar Miller and Barbara Beatty (1969) show the rDNA directly. Each active gene is covered by about 100 polymerases, from each of which a nascent RNP fibril extends; the fibrils lengthen along the gene, giving the image of a "Christmas tree" with its trunk on the DNA and the shortest branches at the promoter, and the trees are separated by stretches of nontranscribed spacer.

### 17.8.2. Ribosome Biogenesis

**Definition 17.8.2 (Ribosome Biogenesis)** **Ribosome biogenesis** is the synthesis and assembly of ribosomal subunits: transcription of the rRNAs, their processing and modification, the import and assembly of the ribosomal proteins, export of the subunits and their final maturation in the cytoplasm. In eukaryotes it involves the three nuclear RNA polymerases, about 80 ribosomal proteins, more than 200 assembly factors and about 75 snoRNAs.

The steps follow the layers of the nucleolus outward:

1. Transcription. RNA polymerase I transcribes the rDNA at the border between the fibrillar centers and the dense fibrillar component into a single precursor, the 47S pre-rRNA in humans (often called 45S in older texts, after the first processing intermediate), which contains the 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs separated by spacers ([7.2.12](/7.2.12)); the mature rRNAs make up only about half of its $13.3\,\mathrm{kb}$, and the spacers removed are the GC-rich, unmodified parts. Making the three rRNAs from one transcript guarantees their equal amounts.
2. Modification and early processing in the DFC. While still nascent, the pre-rRNA is bound by the small-subunit processome, a giant particle containing the U3 snoRNA, and modified at about 100 sites of 2′-O-methylation and 100 of pseudouridylation, each chosen by a guide **small nucleolar RNA** ([7.2.17](/7.2.17#sec-17.5.1)).
3. Cleavage and assembly in the GC. Cleavages in the spacers separate the precursors of the small (40S) and large (60S) subunits; ribosomal proteins, made in the cytoplasm and imported by importins (importin 7, transportin and others), assemble onto the rRNAs. The 5S rRNA, transcribed by RNA polymerase III outside the nucleolus from a separate gene cluster (on human chromosome 1), joins the large subunit as a preformed particle with the ribosomal proteins uL18 and uL5 (formerly L5 and L11).
4. Export. Preribosomal subunits are exported through the pores by CRM1 and other receptors ([7.3.16](/7.3.16#sec-16.5.4)).
5. Cytoplasmic maturation. The final steps, including the last cleavage of the 18S precursor and the release of assembly factors, occur in the cytoplasm, where a quality control tests the subunits before they join the pool of active ribosomes. A small subunit joins a large one only on an mRNA, at initiation of translation ([7.2.19](/7.2.19)).

The finished **ribosomes** ([7.2.19](/7.2.19)) are, seen from the cell, its most numerous large particles: in a mammalian cell several million, about $60\,\%$ rRNA and $40\,\%$ protein by mass, with the rRNA forming the core and the catalytic **peptidyl transferase center** and the proteins mostly on the surface. Their cellular distribution is summarized in a table:

| Ribosomes | Size | Where | What they make |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free cytosolic | 80S (40S + 60S) | cytosol, many on the cytoskeleton | cytosolic proteins and proteins imported into the nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts and peroxisomes ([7.3.6](/7.3.6)) |
| Membrane-bound | 80S, the same population | rough ER and outer nuclear membrane, attached through the nascent chain and the translocon | secretory, lysosomal and membrane proteins of the endomembrane system ([7.3.7](/7.3.7)) |
| Mitochondrial | 55S in mammals (70S-like in other eukaryotes) | matrix, bound to the inner membrane | the few mtDNA-encoded proteins ([7.3.12](/7.3.12)) |
| Chloroplast | 70S | stroma and thylakoid membranes | plastid-encoded proteins |
| Bacterial and archaeal | 70S (30S + 50S) | cytoplasm | all proteins |

Whether an 80S ribosome is free or bound depends only on the protein it is translating, and an mRNA is usually translated by a **polysome** of many ribosomes at once. A few highly specialized cells lose their ribosomes: reticulocytes still synthesize hemoglobin, but mature mammalian erythrocytes have degraded their ribosomes along with their other organelles, and sperm keep almost no cytoplasm.

**Example 17.8.1 (The ribosome budget of a dividing cell)** A HeLa cell contains about $5 \times 10^6$ ribosomes and divides every $24\,\mathrm{h}$, so it must make at least $5 \times 10^6/1440 \approx 3500$ ribosomes per minute, more if ribosomes are degraded. With about 200 active rRNA genes, each must produce about 17 precursors per minute. A gene of $13.3\,\mathrm{kb}$ transcribed by polymerases moving at about $40\,\mathrm{nt\,s^{-1}}$ and spaced about $130\,\mathrm{nt}$ apart (100 polymerases per gene) releases one transcript per $130/40 \approx 3\,\mathrm{s}$, about 18 per minute: the observed density of polymerases in Miller spreads is just what the budget requires. Each ribosome is exported as two subunits, about $7000$ particles per minute through some $3500$ pores, two per pore per minute, close to the value of about three per pore per minute estimated from the kinetics of export. In rapidly growing yeast, which makes about 2000 ribosomes per minute in a much smaller cell, rRNA accounts for about $60\%$ of all transcription and the mRNAs of ribosomal proteins for about half of all Pol II initiation events: ribosome synthesis is the largest single investment of a growing cell ([7.3.22](/7.3.22)).

Because the components are made by three polymerases in two compartments, their production must be coordinated. Growth signals coordinate them at the source: mTORC1 and MYC activate transcription by all three polymerases ([7.3.22](/7.3.22)), so that rRNAs and ribosomal-protein mRNAs rise and fall together. Errors are absorbed by degradation: ribosomal proteins made in excess are quickly ubiquitinated and degraded by the proteasome. And imbalance is signaled: when rRNA synthesis or processing fails, free particles of 5S rRNA with uL18 and uL5 accumulate and bind MDM2, the ubiquitin ligase of p53, stabilizing p53 and arresting the cell cycle.

**Definition 17.8.3 (Nucleolar Stress)** **Nucleolar stress** (ribosomal stress) is the response to a disruption of ribosome biogenesis, by damage, nutrient deprivation, inhibitors of Pol I or mutations in ribosome components, in which the nucleolus reorganizes and free ribosomal proteins and the 5S RNP inhibit MDM2, leading to activation of p53 ([7.3.22](/7.3.22)).

Nucleolar stress explains the paradoxical features of the ribosomopathies, inherited diseases of ribosome biogenesis: mutations in ribosomal protein genes (such as *RPS19* in Diamond–Blackfan anemia, a failure of red-cell production) or in nucleolar factors (*TCOF1* in Treacher Collins syndrome, a craniofacial disorder) affect particular tissues through p53 activation in their rapidly dividing progenitors, and later predispose to cancer. The nucleolus also serves as a protein quality-control compartment: under heat stress, misfolded nuclear proteins enter the liquid granular component, where they are kept from aggregating until the chaperone Hsp70 refolds them or they are degraded (Frottin and colleagues, 2019).

### 17.8.3. The Nucleolus in the Cell Cycle

At the onset of mitosis Cdk1 phosphorylates components of the Pol I machinery, transcription stops, and the nucleolus disperses; its proteins coat the condensed chromosomes (the perichromosomal layer) or disperse in the cytoplasm. UBF and the Pol I machinery remain bound to the active NORs, which therefore stain with silver throughout mitosis, whereas silent NORs do not. At telophase, when Cdk1 activity falls, Pol I transcription resumes at the NORs, prenucleolar bodies assemble from the processing machinery carried on the chromosomes, and they fuse at the NORs into new nucleoli. The nucleolus is thus rebuilt in every cell cycle around its genes, which nucleate the condensate by producing its RNA.

## 17.9. Extrachromosomal DNA

Cancer cells often amplify oncogenes to tens or hundreds of copies. In metaphase spreads the amplified DNA appears either as homogeneously staining regions within chromosomes or as pairs of small, acentric chromatin bodies called double minutes (first described in 1965).

**Definition 17.9.1 (Extrachromosomal DNA)** **Extrachromosomal DNA** (ecDNA) is circular DNA, typically $0.1$–$5\,\mathrm{Mb}$ long, that exists apart from the chromosomes in the nucleus of cancer cells, carries oncogenes and their enhancers at high copy number, lacks a centromere, and is packaged in chromatin that is less compacted and more accessible than that of chromosomes; the double minutes of cytogenetics are ecDNA.

Genome sequencing of thousands of tumors detected ecDNA in about $17\%$ of them, most often in glioblastomas, sarcomas and cancers of the esophagus and stomach, and rarely in normal tissue; it carries oncogenes such as *MYC*, *EGFR*, *MYCN* and *CDK4*, and patients whose tumors contain ecDNA have shorter survival (Kim and colleagues, 2020; Bailey and colleagues, 2024). Its chromatin is highly accessible, so its oncogenes are transcribed at much higher levels per copy than chromosomal copies, and ecDNAs cluster into hubs in the nucleus, where enhancers on one molecule can activate genes on another.

The distinctive property of ecDNA is its inheritance. Lacking a centromere, ecDNA molecules do not attach to the spindle; they are replicated once per cycle but segregate by hitchhiking on the chromosomes, so that each copy goes to either daughter cell with roughly equal probability.

**Proposition 17.9.1 (Random Segregation of ecDNA)** Let a cell with $X$ copies of an ecDNA replicate them to $2X$ and let each copy go independently to either daughter with probability $1/2$, so that a daughter receives $X' \mid X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(2X, 1/2)$ copies. Then $\mathbb{E}[X' \mid X] = X$, and in a lineage followed for $g$ generations without selection, starting from $X_0 = k$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[X_g] = k, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_g) = \frac{gk}{2}.
\tag{17.2}
$$
A daughter loses the ecDNA altogether with probability $2^{-2X}$ per division.

**Proof** The binomial gives $\mathbb{E}[X' \mid X] = X$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X' \mid X) = 2X \cdot \tfrac14 = X/2$. By the law of total expectation $\mathbb{E}[X_{g+1}] = \mathbb{E}[X_g] = k$. By the law of total variance $\operatorname{Var}(X_{g+1}) = \mathbb{E}[X_g/2] + \operatorname{Var}(X_g) = k/2 + \operatorname{Var}(X_g)$, and with $\operatorname{Var}(X_0) = 0$ induction gives (17.2). The probability that all $2X$ copies go to the other daughter is $2^{-2X}$. $\square$

Chromosomal amplicons, in contrast, are inherited exactly: each daughter receives the same copy number. Random segregation therefore makes ecDNA copy number a fast-evolving, heritable variable in a tumor population: the distribution of copy numbers broadens by drift in each generation (the copy number in a lineage is a martingale, like the frequency of a neutral allele in the Wright–Fisher model of [7.10](/7.10)), and selection acting on this variation shifts the mean within a few generations rather than waiting for new mutations. When glioblastoma cells carrying the oncogenic receptor EGFRvIII on ecDNA were treated with the EGFR inhibitor erlotinib, they lost the ecDNA and became resistant, and the ecDNA reappeared when the drug was withdrawn (Nathanson and colleagues, 2014). This speed of adaptation helps explain why ecDNA-positive tumors resist treatment. Normal cells also contain small circular DNAs, and in aging yeast mother cells extrachromosomal circles excised from the rDNA accumulate, because they replicate but are retained in the mother at division, and contribute to the limited replicative life span of the mother (Sinclair and Guarente, 1997), another case in which the behavior of a DNA molecule at division follows from its lack of a centromere.

## Questions

1. A woman is heterozygous for an X-linked mutation that causes a recessive enzyme deficiency. Her blood cells show a ratio of 90 : 10 in favor of the X carrying the mutation. Give two explanations and say how you would distinguish them.

   **Answer** (a) Skewing by chance: if few precursor cells (say $n = 8$) gave rise to the blood at the time of inactivation, a ratio of $90{:}10$ or more has a probability of about $2 \times 9/256 \approx 0.07$ in either direction (Proposition 17.1.1 with $nF \ge 7$), and half of that, about $3.5\%$, in favor of the mutant X; age-related clonal hematopoiesis raises the chance further. (b) Selection: the inactivation pattern may have been skewed by selection, for example against cells expressing the other X because it carries a second deleterious variant or a structural abnormality (an X–autosome translocation often leads to inactivation of the normal X). To distinguish them, measure the inactivation ratio in other tissues (buccal cells, skin fibroblasts): chance skewing in blood is usually tissue-specific, whereas a constitutional cause skews all tissues; examine the other X by karyotyping and sequencing; and follow the ratio over time. Clinically she may manifest the deficiency if the enzyme acts cell-autonomously in the skewed tissue.

2. Explain how each of the following observations supports the existence of chromosome territories or of nonrandom nuclear organization: (a) laser microirradiation of a small spot in the nucleus damages only a few chromosomes; (b) translocations between chromosomes 9 and 22 are common in myeloid cells; (c) in Hi-C maps the contact frequency between loci on the same chromosome falls with genomic distance but stays much higher than between loci on different chromosomes.

   **Answer** (a) If chromosomes were spread through the whole nucleus as intermingled threads, any spot would hit almost all of them; damage restricted to a few chromosomes means that each occupies a compact region. (b) A translocation requires the two broken ends to meet; its frequency between particular chromosomes reflects how often they are neighbors, so recurrent translocations indicate cell-type-specific proximity of the loci (*ABL1* and *BCR* are often close in hematopoietic nuclei), together with selection for the fusion product. (c) Loci on one chromosome are confined to the same territory and meet each other often, even when separated by many megabases; loci on different chromosomes meet only at territory borders. The decline with genomic distance reflects the polymer folding within a territory.

3. How many bands can a G-banded karyotype resolve, and why can't it detect a deletion of $500\,\mathrm{kb}$? Name two methods that can.

   **Answer** At about 400 bands per haploid set (850 in prometaphase), the $3.1 \times 10^9$ bp of the haploid genome gives an average of about $8\,\mathrm{Mb}$ per band (about $4\,\mathrm{Mb}$ at 850 bands); the smallest visible change is a loss or gain of roughly a band, several megabases. A $500\,\mathrm{kb}$ deletion is an order of magnitude smaller. It can be detected by FISH with a locus-specific probe (one signal instead of two), by comparative genomic hybridization on microarrays or SNP arrays (reduced signal), or by genome sequencing (reduced read depth and junction reads).

4. Why does a polytene chromosome look banded, and why does a puff look swollen? What did the ecdysone experiments with cycloheximide show?

   **Answer** About a thousand chromatids lie in register, so the same chromatin segment in each chromatid is at the same position; segments that are condensed (bands) contain more DNA per unit length and stain darkly, the decondensed segments between them (interbands) stain lightly. A puff is a band whose chromatin has unfolded in all chromatids together, because it is being transcribed; the extended DNA, polymerases and nascent RNP occupy a larger volume. In the presence of cycloheximide, ecdysone still induced the early puffs, so they are direct targets of the hormone–receptor complex, but the late puffs did not appear and the early puffs failed to regress: both the activation of late genes and the repression of early genes require newly made proteins, the products of the early genes. This revealed a two-step transcriptional cascade with negative feedback.

5. Using Example 17.8.1, estimate how the rate of ribosome production would have to change if a cell doubled its growth rate (doubling time 12 h instead of 24 h) with the same number of ribosomes per cell. How could the cell achieve this, given that the number of rDNA copies is fixed?

   **Answer** The required rate doubles, to about $7000$ ribosomes per minute. With a fixed number of genes, the cell can activate more of its rDNA copies (typically only about half are active, so this alone can nearly double output), load more polymerases per gene (initiation rate, set by UBF and SL1 activity under control of mTORC1 and MYC), and speed elongation and processing. Polymerase density is already high (about one per $130\,\mathrm{nt}$), so activating silent copies is the larger reserve. It must also increase the production of ribosomal proteins, 5S rRNA and assembly factors in proportion, and export, which at two to three subunits per pore per minute may require more nuclear pores.

6. A tumor cell population carries an oncogene on ecDNA at a mean of 20 copies. (a) What is the standard deviation of copy number among the descendants of one cell after 50 generations without selection? (b) What is the chance that a daughter of a cell with 2 copies receives none? (c) Why does random segregation favor the evolution of drug resistance compared with chromosomal amplification?

   **Answer** (a) By (17.2), $\operatorname{Var}(X_{50}) = 50 \times 20/2 = 500$, so the standard deviation is about $22$ copies, larger than the mean; the distribution becomes broad and skewed, with many cells having lost the ecDNA (the formula assumes copies are not limited and ignores the absorbing state at 0, which becomes important at this spread). (b) $2^{-2 \times 2} = 1/16$. (c) Each division generates daughters with widely different copy numbers without any mutation, so a population constantly offers cells with higher or lower oncogene dosage; selection by a drug (or by its removal) can enrich the fittest within a few generations, and the change is reversible. A chromosomal amplicon gives both daughters the same copy number, so changes in dosage require rare rearrangements.
