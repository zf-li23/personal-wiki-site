The endocytic pathway of [7.3.10](/7.3.10) ends in lysosomes, and so do three other streams of material: phagocytosed particles, macropinocytosed fluid and, by autophagy, parts of the cell's own cytoplasm. The lysosome is therefore the cell's main organelle of degradation, but it is not a dead end. The amino acids, sugars, lipids and nucleosides it releases are reused; its surface is where the cell reads its nutrient status and decides between growth and recycling; and in specialized cells its contents are secreted to resorb bone, kill targets or repair a torn plasma membrane. This chapter first describes the lysosome (11.1), how its enzymes are delivered (11.2), what it does (11.3) and what happens when one of its enzymes is missing (11.4); then the plant and fungal vacuole, its larger and more versatile relative (11.5); and finally autophagy, the route by which the cell delivers its own cytoplasm to the lysosome, in bulk (11.6) or selectively (11.7). The **proteasome** and the **ubiquitin–proteasome system** degrade individual proteins of the cytosol and nucleus ([7.2.20](/7.2.20)); lysosomes and autophagy take care of everything else, including large complexes, aggregates and whole organelles. Several steps lend themselves to simple models: the balance of pumps and leaks that sets the lysosomal pH, the kinetics by which autophagic flux is measured, and the switch by which PINK1 and Parkin mark damaged mitochondria.

## 11.1. Lysosomes

### 11.1.1. Discovery and Defining Features

Lysosomes were discovered by biochemistry before they were seen. In 1955 Christian de Duve, fractionating rat liver by centrifugation, noticed that the activity of acid phosphatase in a fraction of particles was low in fresh preparations and rose after freezing and thawing or treatment with detergent: the enzyme was "latent", enclosed by a membrane that kept it from its substrate. Several acid hydrolases behaved the same way and sedimented together, and de Duve named the particle the lysosome; electron microscopy with a histochemical stain for acid phosphatase later showed it in cells (Nobel Prize 1974, with Albert Claude and George Palade).

**Definition 11.1.1 (Lysosome)** A **lysosome** is a membrane-enclosed organelle, 0.1–1 µm in diameter and present at tens to hundreds per animal cell, whose lumen is kept at pH 4.5–5.0 by V-type ATPases and contains soluble acid hydrolases that can degrade all classes of biological macromolecules, and whose membrane carries heavily glycosylated proteins that protect it from digestion and transporters that export the products of digestion to the cytosol.

**Definition 11.1.2 (Acid Hydrolase)** An **acid hydrolase** is a hydrolytic enzyme with an acidic pH optimum, usually 4.5–5.5; the about 60 soluble acid hydrolases of lysosomes include proteases (cathepsins), nucleases, glycosidases, lipases, phospholipases, phosphatases and sulfatases. Many are made as inactive precursors (**zymogens**) that are activated by proteolysis in the acidic lumen.

The arrangement gives the cytosol double protection. The membrane keeps the hydrolases out of the cytosol, and if some leak, they are nearly inactive at the cytosolic pH of 7.2. The lumenal face of the membrane is coated by the glycans of the lysosome-associated membrane proteins LAMP1 and LAMP2 and of LIMP-2, which form a glycocalyx-like layer 5–8 nm thick on the inside, and the lipid bis(monoacylglycero)phosphate of intralumenal vesicles resists the lysosomal phospholipases. The membrane also contains the machinery of a working organelle:

### 11.1.2. Heterogeneity and the Lysosome Cycle

Unlike mitochondria or the Golgi apparatus, lysosomes vary widely in size, shape and density, because their content varies. The classical classification distinguishes primary lysosomes, small vesicles carrying newly made hydrolases that have not yet met a substrate; secondary lysosomes, which have fused with material to be digested, called heterophagic when the material came from outside (by endocytosis or phagocytosis) and autophagic when it came from the cell's own cytoplasm; and residual bodies, which contain indigestible remnants. In long-lived cells such as neurons and cardiac muscle, residual bodies accumulate as lipofuscin, a fluorescent "age pigment" of oxidized lipid and protein.

**Remark 11.1.1** Live imaging has replaced the idea of a primary lysosome budding from the Golgi with a cycle. Hydrolases are delivered by transport vesicles to early and late endosomes, not to lysosomes directly (11.2.1); late endosomes fuse with preexisting lysosomes into endolysosomes, where most digestion happens; when digestion is complete the endolysosome condenses into a dense "storage" lysosome, which can fuse again with a late endosome, a phagosome or an autophagosome. Endolysosomes and lysosomes are therefore stages of one maturation cycle rather than distinct organelles, and the terms of the classical scheme name states in this cycle.

Lysosomes also move. A perinuclear pool, held by dynein, is more acidic and fuses readily with autophagosomes; a peripheral pool, moved outward by kinesins through the BORC–Arl8 complex, takes part in exocytosis and in nutrient signaling at the cell surface. Starvation moves lysosomes inward and refeeding moves them outward, which changes how often they meet autophagosomes and where mTORC1 is active (11.3.3).

Lysosomes and peroxisomes look alike in the electron microscope, small single-membrane organelles of similar size, and the older literature groups the peroxisome with them as a "microbody". They differ in almost everything else ([7.3.6](/7.3.6) treats peroxisomes):

| Property | Lysosome | Peroxisome |
|---|---|---|
| Size and appearance | 0.1–1 µm, heterogeneous content, no crystalline core | 0.1–1 µm, fine granular matrix, often a crystalline core of urate oxidase (rat liver) |
| Lumenal pH | 4.5–5.0 | near neutral |
| Enzymes | acid hydrolases; no $\ce{O2}$ needed | flavin oxidases making $\ce{H2O2}$, catalase; $\ce{O2}$ consumed |
| Marker enzyme | acid phosphatase | catalase |
| Origin of enzymes | made on the rough ER, sorted in the Golgi by mannose 6-phosphate (11.2) | made on free ribosomes, imported posttranslationally by PTS1 or PTS2 |
| Biogenesis | maturation of endosomes and fusion with existing lysosomes; regeneration from autolysosomes (11.3.4) | growth and division of existing peroxisomes, and formation from ER-derived precursors |
| Function | digestion and recycling, nutrient signaling | oxidation of very-long-chain fatty acids, $\ce{H2O2}$ metabolism, ether lipids, photorespiration and the glyoxylate cycle in plants |

### 11.1.3. Maintaining an Acid Lumen

The V-type ATPase ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.5.3)) acidifies lysosomes, endosomes, the trans-Golgi network and secretory vesicles. It is a rotary pump of the same architecture as **ATP synthase** but always runs in the hydrolytic direction, moving two to four protons per ATP. Pumping alone, however, cannot acidify an organelle, and a short calculation shows why.

**Example 11.1.1 (The charge problem)** A spherical lysosome of radius $0.25\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ has a volume of $6.5 \times 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{L}$ and, with a specific capacitance of $1\,\mu\mathrm{F\,cm^{-2}}$, a membrane capacitance of $C = 7.9 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{F}$. Its lumen is buffered by proteins and other weak acids with a capacity of about $40\,\mathrm{mM}$ per pH unit, so lowering the pH from 7.2 to 4.7 requires about $2.5 \times 40\,\mathrm{mM} \times 6.5 \times 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{L} \times N_{\mathrm{A}} \approx 4 \times 10^6$ protons. If these protons were pumped without any compensating ion movement, the membrane potential would rise by $Q/C = 4 \times 10^6 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}/7.9 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{F} \approx 80\,\mathrm{V}$. In fact the pump stalls after about $10^4$ protons, at a potential of about 250 mV, having changed the pH by less than 0.01 unit. Acidification is possible only if a counter-ion moves with the protons: an anion ($\ce{Cl-}$) entering the lumen or a cation ($\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Na+}$) leaving it.

**Formula 11.1.1 (Pump–Leak Model of Organelle pH)** Let an organelle of volume $V$, membrane area $A$, capacitance $C$ and buffer capacity $\beta$ contain $N$ V-type ATPases; let $\Delta\psi$ be the lumenal potential relative to the cytosol and $\mathrm{pH_L}$, $\mathrm{pH_C}$ the lumenal and cytosolic pH. With the proton flux into the lumen by the pumps $J_{\mathrm{V}}$, the passive proton leak out $J_{\mathrm{L}}$ and the counter-ion flux $J_{\mathrm{X}}$ (anions in, or cations out, in moles of charge per unit time),
$$
\beta V \frac{\mathrm{d}\,\mathrm{pH_L}}{\mathrm{d}t} = -(J_{\mathrm{V}} - J_{\mathrm{L}}), \qquad C\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta\psi}{\mathrm{d}t} = F\,(J_{\mathrm{V}} - J_{\mathrm{L}} - J_{\mathrm{X}}). \tag{11.1}
$$
The pump slows as the proton electrochemical gradient it works against, $\Delta\tilde\mu_{\mathrm{H^+}} = F\Delta\psi + 2.303RT(\mathrm{pH_C} - \mathrm{pH_L})$, approaches its stall value $\Delta G_{\mathrm{ATP}}/n$ for $n$ protons per ATP; the leak follows the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz flux equation with proton permeability $P_{\mathrm{H}}$ (7.3.5); and the counter-ion flux is proportional to a conductance $G_{\mathrm{X}}$ and to the distance of $\Delta\psi$ from the counter-ion's equilibrium potential.

The model makes three points, shown in the figure. First, the potential equilibrates within milliseconds (the time constant is $C/G_{\mathrm{X}}$) and the pH within minutes, so the potential stays small (about 10–20 mV, lumen positive) while the pH falls; without a counter-ion conductance the potential jumps to the stall value and the pH does not change. Second, the steady-state pH is set by the ratio of pump to leak: neither the number of pumps nor the permeability alone determines it, and a threefold change in either shifts the pH by about half a unit. Third, inhibiting the pump with bafilomycin A1 or concanamycin A lets the pH rise back over tens of minutes at a rate set by the leak. Measurements broadly agree with these predictions (Grabe and Oster, 2001), with one twist: mice lacking ClC-7, the main $\ce{Cl-}$ transporter of lysosomes, have osteopetrosis and lysosomal storage but nearly normal lysosomal pH, which shows that cation movement (through TMEM175 and other channels) can supply the counter-charge; the identity and balance of counter-ions differ between organelles and cell types.

The pH gradient also concentrates drugs. Weak bases cross membranes in their neutral form and are trapped in acidic compartments after protonation; such "lysosomotropic" compounds include chloroquine, ammonium chloride, many antidepressants and antipsychotics, and the antiarrhythmic amiodarone.

**Example 11.1.2 (Ion trapping of weak bases)** If only the neutral base B crosses the membrane, its concentration is the same on both sides at equilibrium, and the total concentration in a compartment is $[\mathrm{B}](1 + 10^{\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} - \mathrm{pH}})$. The ratio of total concentrations between lysosome and cytosol is therefore
$$
\frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}}{c_{\mathrm{C}}} = \frac{1 + 10^{\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} - \mathrm{pH_L}}}{1 + 10^{\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} - \mathrm{pH_C}}} \approx 10^{\mathrm{pH_C} - \mathrm{pH_L}} \quad (\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} \gg \mathrm{pH_C}).
$$
For a monoprotic base with $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} = 9.2$ (ammonia) and pH 4.7 against 7.2, the ratio is about $10^{2.5} \approx 300$. Chloroquine has two basic groups ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ 8.1 and 10.2), and the ratio approaches $(10^{2.5})^2 = 10^5$; in practice the accumulated base consumes protons and raises the lysosomal pH, which limits uptake and is the reason why such drugs block lysosomal digestion, autophagy and the release of ligands from receptors in endosomes.

## 11.2. Biogenesis of Lysosomes

### 11.2.1. The Mannose 6-Phosphate Pathway

Lysosomal hydrolases are made like secretory proteins: they enter the ER cotranslationally, acquire N-linked glycans and are folded there ([7.3.7](/7.3.7)). In the Golgi apparatus they receive a tag that distinguishes them from the thousands of proteins destined for secretion.

**Definition 11.2.1 (Mannose 6-Phosphate and Its Receptors)** **Mannose 6-phosphate** (M6P) is the phosphorylated mannose residue on the **N-linked glycans** of soluble lysosomal hydrolases that serves as their sorting signal. It is made in two steps: in the cis Golgi network, GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase recognizes a three-dimensional signal patch of lysine residues on the folded hydrolase and transfers N-acetylglucosamine 1-phosphate from UDP-GlcNAc to the 6-hydroxyl of mannose residues; in the trans-Golgi network, the uncovering enzyme (a phosphodiester glycosidase) removes the GlcNAc and exposes the phosphate. The **mannose 6-phosphate receptors**, the cation-independent (CI-MPR, 300 kDa, which also binds IGF-II) and the cation-dependent receptor (CD-MPR, 46 kDa), are transmembrane proteins that bind M6P at the pH of the trans-Golgi network (about 6.5–7) and release it at pH below 6.

The receptors with their bound hydrolases are collected into clathrin-coated vesicles by the adaptors AP-1 and GGA, which read acidic-cluster–dileucine signals in the receptors' cytosolic tails, and are delivered to early and late endosomes. There the acidic pH releases the hydrolases, a lysosomal acid phosphatase removes the phosphate so that they cannot rebind, and the empty receptors return to the trans-Golgi network, mainly in tubules coated by retromer ([7.3.10](/7.3.10#sec-10.3.3)). Each receptor makes many round trips, while transport of the hydrolases is one way. The signal patch is the critical recognition event: it is formed by residues far apart in sequence and brought together only in the folded protein, so the phosphotransferase tags only correctly folded lysosomal enzymes and only their mannoses.

About 5–20% of the newly made hydrolases escape this sorting and are secreted. CI-MPR at the plasma membrane retrieves them by receptor-mediated endocytosis (the cation-dependent receptor does not bind ligand at the cell surface), and this salvage pathway makes enzyme replacement therapy possible: recombinant enzymes carrying M6P, infused into the blood, are taken up by cells through CI-MPR and reach their lysosomes.

### 11.2.2. I-Cell Disease

**Definition 11.2.2 (I-Cell Disease)** **I-cell disease** (inclusion-cell disease, mucolipidosis II) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by loss of GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase (mutations in *GNPTAB*), in which lysosomal hydrolases are not tagged with mannose 6-phosphate and are secreted instead of being delivered to lysosomes, so that many hydrolases are missing from the lysosomes of fibroblasts and other cells, which fill with undigested material (inclusions), while the enzymes are abundant in plasma and urine.

The disease was the key to the pathway. Elizabeth Neufeld had shown in 1968 that fibroblasts from patients with two different storage diseases (the Hurler and Hunter syndromes) corrected each other when grown together: each secreted the enzyme the other lacked, and the other took it up. Enzymes secreted by I-cell fibroblasts, however, could not be taken up by any cells, although they were catalytically active, and Hickman and Neufeld proposed in 1972 that I-cell hydrolases lacked a recognition marker for uptake and for delivery to lysosomes. The marker turned out to be mannose 6-phosphate. Children with I-cell disease have coarse features, skeletal abnormalities and developmental delay and die in the first decade; a milder form with residual enzyme activity is mucolipidosis III. Notably, hepatocytes, Kupffer cells and leukocytes of patients have nearly normal lysosomal enzyme levels: they must use other routes.

### 11.2.3. Routes Independent of Mannose 6-Phosphate

Some soluble hydrolases do not need M6P. β-Glucocerebrosidase, the enzyme missing in Gaucher disease, binds the lysosomal membrane protein LIMP-2 in the ER and travels with it; sortilin carries prosaposin (the precursor of the saposins, activators of sphingolipid hydrolases) and acid sphingomyelinase. Lysosomal membrane proteins such as the LAMPs reach lysosomes by tyrosine and dileucine signals in their tails, either directly from the trans-Golgi network to endosomes (with the adaptor AP-3) or through the plasma membrane and endocytosis. The number of lysosomes and the amount of their enzymes are also regulated as a whole, by transcription (11.3.3).

## 11.3. Functions of Lysosomes

### 11.3.1. Digestion and Recycling

Four routes converge on lysosomes ([7.3.10](/7.3.10)): endocytosis of receptors, ligands and fluid; macropinocytosis; phagocytosis of particles; and autophagy of cytoplasm (11.6). The products of digestion leave through transporters for amino acids, sugars, nucleosides and ions, or by vesicular routes; cholesterol from LDL is handed from NPC2 in the lumen to NPC1 in the membrane, which inserts it into the membrane for transfer to the ER, partly at membrane contact sites. Lysosomal digestion thus supplies building blocks, especially in starvation, when autophagy recycles the cell's own proteins into amino acids for essential synthesis and energy. Cystinosis shows how specific these exits are: without the cystine transporter cystinosin, cystine, the oxidized dimer of cysteine, accumulates and crystallizes in lysosomes and destroys the kidney; the drug cysteamine enters lysosomes and reacts with cystine to form cysteine and a mixed disulfide that leaves through a different transporter (PQLC2), a chemical bypass of a missing transporter.

### 11.3.2. Secretory Lysosomes, Bone Resorption and Membrane Repair

Lysosomes can fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents. In most cells this is a minor route, used to eliminate indigestible residues or to repair the membrane; in some cells it is the main function.

- Osteoclasts, multinucleated cells of the monocyte–macrophage lineage, attach to bone through a ring-shaped sealing zone and fuse lysosomes with the enclosed ruffled border. V-type ATPases (with the a3 subunit) in the ruffled border acidify the resorption lacuna to about pH 4.5, dissolving the hydroxyapatite mineral, while the secreted protease cathepsin K degrades the collagen. Mutations in the a3 subunit, in ClC-7 or in cathepsin K prevent resorption and cause osteopetrosis (dense, brittle bones) or pycnodysostosis.
- Lysosome-related organelles combine lysosomal features with a specialized cargo: the acrosome of the sperm head, whose hydrolases are released by exocytosis when the sperm binds the egg coat (the acrosome reaction, 7.5), melanosomes of melanocytes, the lytic granules of cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells (perforin and granzymes), the dense granules of platelets and the Weibel–Palade bodies of endothelial cells. Defects in their biogenesis or secretion cause albinism with bleeding (Hermansky–Pudlak syndrome, mutations in AP-3 and BLOC complexes) or albinism with immunodeficiency (Chédiak–Higashi syndrome, mutations in *LYST*).
- Hormone production and secretion: thyroid follicular cells endocytose thyroglobulin, iodinated in the follicle lumen, and lysosomal proteases cut it to release the hormones thyroxine and triiodothyronine, which leave the cell; lysosomes thus sit inside a secretory pathway. In the opposite direction, endocrine cells that have made more secretory granules than they release fuse the excess with lysosomes (*crinophagy*), as in pituitary lactotrophs when prolactin secretion is suppressed.
- Membrane repair: a tear in the plasma membrane lets $\ce{Ca^2+}$ in, which triggers fusion of nearby lysosomes through the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ sensor synaptotagmin VII and patches the hole with lysosomal membrane within seconds (Andrews and colleagues, 2001). The secreted acid sphingomyelinase converts sphingomyelin to ceramide at the wound, which promotes endocytosis of the damaged membrane, and ESCRT-III removes small pores by budding ([7.3.10](/7.3.10#sec-10.4.2)). Damaged lysosomes are repaired by the same ESCRT machinery or, if the damage is extensive, marked by galectins that bind the exposed lumenal glycans and removed by lysophagy (11.7.1).

### 11.3.3. A Signaling Hub: mTORC1 and TFEB

The lysosome is the place where cells measure their amino acids. **mTORC1** ([7.1.29](/7.1.29#sec-29.4.5)) is active only when it is recruited to the cytosolic surface of lysosomes, where two sets of small GTPases meet: the Rag GTPases (heterodimers of RagA or RagB with RagC or RagD), anchored to the lysosome by the Ragulator complex, bind raptor when amino acids are sufficient; and Rheb, on the same membrane, activates the kinase when growth factor signaling through **protein kinase B** has inactivated the TSC complex, the **GTPase-activating protein** of Rheb. The state of the Rags is set by amino acid sensors in the cytosol (Sestrin2 for leucine, CASTOR1 for arginine, SAMTOR for S-adenosylmethionine, which act through the GAP complex GATOR1) and in the lysosome (SLC38A9 for lumenal arginine, which also transmits cholesterol through NPC1), and by the V-type ATPase. The location makes sense: the amino acids most directly indicating whether the cell is recycling or growing are those released by lysosomal digestion.

**Definition 11.3.1 (TFEB)** **TFEB** (transcription factor EB) is a basic helix–loop–helix–leucine zipper transcription factor of the MiT/TFE family that binds the CLEAR element (coordinated lysosomal expression and regulation, consensus GTCACGTGAC) in the promoters of genes for lysosomal hydrolases, membrane proteins, V-ATPase subunits and autophagy proteins; when it is phosphorylated by mTORC1 on the lysosome it is bound by 14-3-3 proteins and kept in the cytosol, and when mTORC1 is inactive and calcineurin dephosphorylates it, it enters the nucleus and induces lysosome biogenesis and autophagy.

TFEB closes a loop. When nutrients are plentiful, mTORC1 drives growth and keeps TFEB and autophagy off. When amino acids fall, mTORC1 leaves the lysosome, ULK1 is released (11.6.4), and TFEB enters the nucleus; at the same time $\ce{Ca^2+}$ released from lysosomes through TRPML1 activates calcineurin, which dephosphorylates TFEB. The cell then makes more lysosomes and autophagy proteins, digests more of its cytoplasm, and the amino acids released reactivate mTORC1, a negative feedback that adjusts the degradative capacity to need (Sardiello and colleagues, 2009; Settembre and colleagues, 2011). Overexpression of TFEB clears storage material in models of lysosomal storage and neurodegenerative diseases, and chromosomal translocations that overactivate TFEB or its relative TFE3 cause a type of kidney cancer. **AMP-activated protein kinase** (AMPK, [7.1.29](/7.1.29#sec-29.4.4)) acts on the same system, inhibiting mTORC1 and activating ULK1 when energy is low.

### 11.3.4. Regeneration of Lysosomes

Autophagy consumes lysosomes: each fusion with an autophagosome turns a lysosome into an autolysosome, and prolonged starvation would exhaust the lysosome pool if lysosomes were not regenerated. Li Yu and colleagues (2010) found that after several hours of starvation, when the amino acids released by autophagy reactivate mTORC1, autolysosomes extend tubules positive for LAMP1 but not for LC3, which bud off protolysosomes that mature into new, functional lysosomes; this autophagic lysosome reformation needs mTORC1, PI(4,5)P₂ on the autolysosome, clathrin, the kinesin KIF5B and dynamin 2. It ends autophagy and restores the lysosome population, another coupling of nutrient sensing to organelle biogenesis. Lysosomes in general can tubulate, branch and divide, and their number also rises by new synthesis under TFEB control; in macrophages, stimulation with bacterial lipopolysaccharide converts the vesicular lysosomes into a tubular network that favors antigen presentation.

## 11.4. Lysosomal Storage Diseases

**Definition 11.4.1 (Lysosomal Storage Disease)** A **lysosomal storage disease** is an inherited disorder in which a defect in a lysosomal hydrolase, an activator protein, a membrane transporter or the sorting machinery causes undigested substrates to accumulate in lysosomes, enlarging them and impairing the cells in which the substrate is made or taken up.

More than 70 storage diseases are known; each is rare, but together they affect about 1 in 5000 live births. Most are autosomal recessive (a few are X-linked), and most affect the brain, because neurons do not divide and cannot dilute stored material and because the brain makes complex glycosphingolipids.

| Disease | Missing protein | Stored material | Main features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tay–Sachs disease | β-hexosaminidase A (α subunit, *HEXA*) | **ganglioside** GM2 | neurodegeneration from infancy, cherry-red spot in the retina; carrier frequency 1 in 30 among Ashkenazi Jews |
| Gaucher disease | β-glucocerebrosidase (*GBA1*) | glucosylceramide in macrophages | enlarged liver and spleen, bone disease; heterozygous *GBA1* variants are the commonest genetic risk factor for Parkinson's disease |
| Pompe disease | acid α-glucosidase | **glycogen** in lysosomes | cardiomyopathy and myopathy; a **glycogen storage disease** (type II) |
| Fabry disease (X-linked) | α-galactosidase A | globotriaosylceramide | pain, kidney and heart disease |
| Niemann–Pick disease A/B; type C | acid sphingomyelinase; NPC1 or NPC2 | sphingomyelin; cholesterol and glycolipids | neurodegeneration, enlarged organs |
| Hurler syndrome (MPS I) | α-L-iduronidase | **glycosaminoglycans** (dermatan and heparan sulfate) | skeletal deformities, cardiac and intellectual impairment |
| Cystinosis | cystinosin (transporter) | cystine crystals | kidney failure |
| I-cell disease (Definition 11.2.2) | GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase | many substrates | all M6P-dependent hydrolases mislocalized |

Treatments act at different steps of the same pathway: enzyme replacement therapy (infused enzymes taken up through CI-MPR, or through the mannose receptor of macrophages for glucocerebrosidase with exposed mannoses), substrate reduction (eliglustat inhibits glucosylceramide synthesis), pharmacological chaperones that stabilize a misfolded mutant enzyme in the ER so that it escapes ER-associated degradation and reaches lysosomes (migalastat for some Fabry mutations), hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, whose descendants secrete enzyme that neighboring cells take up (cross-correction), and gene therapy. Infused enzymes do not cross the blood–brain barrier, which is why the neurological forms are the hardest to treat.

Lysosomal damage, rather than enzyme deficiency, explains silicosis. Silica particles inhaled into the lung are phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages and, once in phagolysosomes, rupture their membranes (the silanol groups of the particle surface interact with membrane phospholipids and proteins); cathepsins released into the cytosol activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, the macrophages die and release interleukin-1β and other signals, and fibroblasts lay down collagen, producing fibrosis of the lung. The particles are not digested: released from dead macrophages, they are taken up by new ones, so the cycle of rupture and inflammation continues long after exposure ends. Asbestos fibers act in a similar way.

## 11.5. Plant and Fungal Vacuoles

**Definition 11.5.1 (Vacuole)** A **vacuole** of plant and fungal cells is a large lysosome-related organelle bounded by a single membrane, the tonoplast, which in mature plant cells occupies 30–90% of the cell volume and serves for degradation with acid hydrolases, storage of nutrients, ions, pigments and defensive compounds, control of turgor pressure and cell volume, and pH and ion homeostasis of the cytosol.

The tonoplast has two kinds of proton pump: V-type ATPases and the vacuolar $\ce{H+}$-pyrophosphatase, which uses the energy of pyrophosphate released in biosynthesis and is found in plants, some protists and bacteria but not in animals. The resulting proton gradient (the vacuole has pH 5–6 in most cells and as low as 2–3 in citrus fruits) and the positive lumenal potential drive the uptake of sugars, amino acids, ions and metabolites by **antiporters**, and the tonoplast contains abundant **aquaporins** (tonoplast intrinsic proteins).

The vacuole lets plants do things animals cannot:

- Growth at low cost. Because the vacuole is filled mainly with water and inorganic ions, a plant cell can increase its volume twentyfold during elongation while its cytoplasm grows little; turgor of 0.3–1 MPa pushes against the cell wall, and controlled loosening of the wall turns this pressure into oriented growth ([7.3.5](/7.3.5), [7.3.23](/7.3.23)).
- Storage. Seeds store proteins in protein storage vacuoles for years and mobilize them at germination; vacuoles hold sucrose (sugar beet), organic acids (the malate of CAM plants at night, [7.1.25](/7.1.25)), anthocyanin pigments that color flowers and fruits, and toxic alkaloids, cyanogenic glycosides and other defensive compounds, which are released when a cell is damaged. Many such products, from nicotine to the pigments of grapes, are harvested from vacuoles.
- Homeostasis and death. When the cytosol is acidified or loaded with ions, the tonoplast pumps them away; and in programmed cell death of plant cells, including the differentiation of xylem vessels and the hypersensitive response to pathogens, the tonoplast ruptures and the vacuole's hydrolases (including the caspase-like vacuolar processing enzyme) digest the cell from inside.

Plant cells have no separate population of small lysosomes; their lytic functions are carried out by the vacuole (in many cells a lytic vacuole and protein storage vacuoles coexist). The "spherosomes" of older plant histology, once considered plant lysosomes, are oil bodies (oleosomes), droplets of triacylglycerol surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer and the protein oleosin, which store lipid in seeds.

The yeast vacuole is the lysosome of fungi and the organelle in which most of the autophagy machinery was discovered (11.6.2); it stores amino acids (especially arginine), polyphosphate and metal ions.

## 11.6. Autophagy

### 11.6.1. Self-Eating: Concepts and Types

Christian de Duve coined the term autophagy in 1963, after electron micrographs showed lysosomes containing mitochondria and other parts of the cell. It is a normal process of every eukaryotic cell, not merely a response to death.

**Definition 11.6.1 (Autophagy)** **Autophagy** is the delivery of the cell's own cytoplasmic material (proteins, aggregates, organelles, intracellular microbes) to lysosomes or vacuoles for degradation. Its three main forms are **macroautophagy**, in which a double membrane forms around the cargo to make an autophagosome that then fuses with a lysosome; **microautophagy**, in which the lysosomal or vacuolar membrane itself invaginates or protrudes to engulf cytoplasm; and **chaperone-mediated autophagy**, in which individual cytosolic proteins carrying a KFERQ-like motif are recognized by the chaperone Hsc70 and unfolded and translocated across the lysosomal membrane through multimers of LAMP2A.

| Type | Membrane event | Selectivity | Machinery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Macroautophagy | new double membrane (phagophore → autophagosome) | bulk or selective (receptors) | ATG proteins, LC3 conjugation |
| Microautophagy | lysosome or vacuole membrane engulfs cytoplasm | bulk or selective | ESCRT-III, in yeast some ATG proteins |
| Chaperone-mediated autophagy (mammals) | protein translocation, no vesicle | KFERQ-like motif, one protein at a time | Hsc70, LAMP2A, lysosomal Hsc70 |

"Autophagy" without qualification usually means macroautophagy, the form treated in the rest of this section. It has two main roles: recycling of nutrients in starvation, which is largely nonselective, and quality control, the removal of excessive or damaged components and invaders, which is mostly selective (11.7).

### 11.6.2. Ohsumi's Screen

Yoshinori Ohsumi approached autophagy through the yeast vacuole. Reasoning that autophagic structures delivered to the vacuole would be destroyed too quickly to be seen, he starved yeast mutants lacking vacuolar proteases (*pep4*, *prb1*) of nitrogen: within an hour their vacuoles filled with spherical "autophagic bodies", single-membrane vesicles containing ribosomes, mitochondria and cytosol, which accumulated over three hours and were visible even by light microscopy (Takeshige and colleagues, 1992). The autophagic bodies were the inner membranes of autophagosomes whose outer membranes had fused with the vacuole. The phenotype gave a screen: in the protease-deficient background, Tsukada and Ohsumi (1993) isolated mutants that failed to accumulate autophagic bodies and died during starvation, defining the first set of *APG* genes (now *ATG*). Cloning them revealed two ubiquitin-like conjugation systems and a protein kinase, and homologs were soon found in mammals and plants (Nobel Prize 2016). More than 40 *ATG* genes are now known in yeast; about 20 form the core machinery common to all autophagy.

### 11.6.3. Building an Autophagosome

**Definition 11.6.2 (Phagophore, Autophagosome and Autolysosome)** A **phagophore** (isolation membrane) is the cup-shaped, flattened double-membrane cisterna that nucleates at a specialized site, usually a subdomain of the ER, and expands around cytoplasmic cargo. When its rim closes, it becomes an **autophagosome**, a vesicle of 0.5–1.5 µm (mammals) enclosed by two membranes; the fusion of its outer membrane with a lysosome gives an **autolysosome**, in which the inner membrane and the cargo are degraded.

**Definition 11.6.3 (ATG Proteins and LC3)** **ATG proteins** (autophagy-related proteins) are the products of the *ATG* genes that build the autophagosome. **LC3** (microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3) and the related GABARAP proteins are the mammalian members of the ATG8 family, ubiquitin-like proteins that are covalently attached through their C-terminal glycine to phosphatidylethanolamine in the phagophore membrane; the soluble form is called LC3-I and the lipid-conjugated form LC3-II.

The steps of macroautophagy in mammalian cells are well defined, although how the membrane grows is still being worked out:

1. Initiation. The ULK1 complex (ULK1 or ULK2 kinase, ATG13, FIP200 and ATG101; Atg1 complex in yeast), released from inhibition by mTORC1, assembles at sites on the ER and phosphorylates downstream components.
2. Nucleation. The class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase complex I (the kinase VPS34 with VPS15, Beclin 1 and ATG14) produces PI(3)P on an ER subdomain shaped like the Greek letter Ω, the omegasome, marked by the PI(3)P-binding protein DFCP1. PI(3)P recruits the WIPI proteins (Atg18 in yeast).
3. Expansion. The phagophore grows mainly by direct transfer of lipids from the ER through ATG2, a rod-shaped protein about 20 nm long with a hydrophobic groove that bridges the ER and the phagophore, while the scramblase ATG9, delivered in small vesicles from the Golgi and endosomes, equilibrates the transferred lipids between the two leaflets. Other membranes contribute, including the ER–Golgi intermediate compartment, which Liang Ge and colleagues identified as a site of LC3 lipidation, ER–mitochondria contacts, the plasma membrane and recycling endosomes.
4. LC3 lipidation. Two ubiquitin-like conjugation reactions decorate the growing membrane (figure, panel B). The protease ATG4 cleaves the precursor of LC3 after a glycine (LC3-I); the E1-like ATG7 activates LC3 and passes it to the E2-like ATG3. In parallel, ATG7 and the E2-like ATG10 join ATG12 covalently to ATG5, and the ATG12–ATG5 conjugate binds ATG16L1; this complex, recruited by WIPI2, acts as an E3-like enzyme that directs ATG3 to transfer LC3 to phosphatidylethanolamine (LC3-II). LC3-II on the outer surface helps recruit fusion machinery and adaptors; on the inner surface it binds cargo receptors (11.7.1).
5. Closure and fusion. The rim of the phagophore is sealed by ESCRT-III (another scission of a neck from its cytosolic side). The autophagosome moves on microtubules toward lysosomes and fuses with them through the autophagosomal SNARE syntaxin 17 with SNAP29 and the lysosomal VAMP8, aided by the HOPS tethering complex; after fusion ATG4 can remove LC3 from the outer membrane.
6. Degradation and export. Lysosomal lipases and proteases digest the inner membrane and the cargo, and the products leave through lysosomal transporters.

### 11.6.4. Regulation by Nutrients and Energy

Autophagy integrates several signals at the ULK1 and VPS34 complexes:

- mTORC1, active when amino acids and growth factors are abundant, phosphorylates ULK1 (at Ser757 in mouse) and ATG13 and keeps the initiation complex inactive; it also phosphorylates TFEB (11.3.3). Inhibiting mTORC1 with rapamycin or by starvation induces autophagy within minutes.
- AMPK, activated by a rise of AMP and ADP ([7.1.29](/7.1.29#sec-29.4.4)), phosphorylates ULK1 at activating sites and Beclin 1, and inhibits mTORC1 through TSC2 and raptor.
- Beclin 1 is held inactive by the antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2, which links the control of autophagy to that of **apoptosis** ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)); stress kinases that phosphorylate Bcl-2 release Beclin 1.

As with lysosome biogenesis, the loop is closed by the products: autophagic degradation releases amino acids, which reactivate mTORC1 and stop autophagy. In mammals, the hours after birth show the physiological importance of this system. The placental supply of nutrients stops abruptly, and autophagy rises in heart, lung and other tissues; mice lacking *Atg5* or *Atg7* are born normally but die within a day, with low plasma and tissue amino acid levels, unless they are force-fed (Kuma and colleagues, 2004).

### 11.6.5. Measuring Autophagic Flux

The number of autophagosomes in a cell is a poor measure of autophagy: it rises both when more are made and when fewer are cleared, for example when fusion with lysosomes fails.

**Definition 11.6.4 (Autophagic Flux)** **Autophagic flux** is the rate at which material passes through the whole autophagic pathway, from autophagosome formation through fusion with lysosomes to degradation; it is measured by the rate of degradation of autophagic substrates or by the rate at which autophagosomes or LC3-II accumulate when their lysosomal clearance is blocked.

**Formula 11.6.1 (Two-Compartment Model of Autophagic Flux)** Let autophagosomes $A$ form at rate $k_{\mathrm{f}}$ and fuse with lysosomes with rate constant $k_{\mathrm{fus}}$, and let autolysosomes $L$ be degraded with rate constant $k_{\mathrm{d}}$:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_{\mathrm{f}} - k_{\mathrm{fus}} A, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_{\mathrm{fus}} A - k_{\mathrm{d}} L, \tag{11.2}
$$
with steady state $A^* = k_{\mathrm{f}}/k_{\mathrm{fus}}$ and $L^* = k_{\mathrm{f}}/k_{\mathrm{d}}$. The flux is $k_{\mathrm{f}}$. If fusion and degradation are blocked at $t = 0$, $A(t) = A^* + k_{\mathrm{f}} t$: the slope of the accumulation equals the flux, whatever the steady state was.

The model shows why the standard assays work. A rise of $A^*$ can mean a higher $k_{\mathrm{f}}$ (induction) or a lower $k_{\mathrm{fus}}$ (blocked fusion), and $A^*$ alone cannot tell them apart. The increment of LC3-II measured by **Western blot** after a few hours with bafilomycin A1 (which inhibits the V-ATPase and also impairs fusion) or with lysosomal protease inhibitors is proportional to $k_{\mathrm{f}}$, and distinguishes the two (Mizushima, Yoshimori and Levine, 2010). The tandem reporter mRFP–GFP–LC3 gives a readout in single cells: GFP fluorescence is quenched at acidic pH and its protein degraded, mRFP is not, so autophagosomes appear yellow (both colors) and autolysosomes red only; induction raises both yellow and red puncta, whereas a fusion defect raises only the yellow ones. The steady-state level of autophagy substrates such as the receptor p62 gives a third, slower readout: it falls when flux rises.

## 11.7. Selective Autophagy

### 11.7.1. Receptors and LIR Motifs

**Definition 11.7.1 (Selective Autophagy and Autophagy Receptor)** **Selective autophagy** is macroautophagy that removes a specific cargo, such as damaged organelles, protein aggregates or intracellular bacteria, with little bulk cytoplasm. It depends on **autophagy receptors**, proteins that bind the cargo, directly or through a degradation mark (usually ubiquitin chains), and bind ATG8-family proteins on the phagophore through an LC3-interacting region (LIR), a short motif of the form [W/F/Y]xx[L/I/V].

The receptor does more than glue cargo to the membrane. Cargo-bound receptors oligomerize and recruit the initiation machinery (FIP200 and ULK1) and ATG9 vesicles to the cargo itself, so that the phagophore nucleates on the cargo and wraps it closely; autophagosomes of selective autophagy therefore have the shape of their contents.

| Cargo | Process | Main receptors and marks |
|---|---|---|
| Protein aggregates | aggrephagy | p62/SQSTM1, NBR1, TAX1BP1 (ubiquitin); CCT2 for solid aggregates |
| Mitochondria | mitophagy | ubiquitin-dependent (PINK1–Parkin: OPTN, NDP52); receptor-mediated (NIX, BNIP3, FUNDC1) |
| Cytosolic bacteria | xenophagy | ubiquitin and galectin-8: NDP52, OPTN, p62 |
| ER | ER-phagy | FAM134B, SEC62, RTN3L, CCPG1, TEX264 (intrinsic membrane receptors) |
| Damaged lysosomes | lysophagy | galectin-3, ubiquitin, TRIM16 |
| Peroxisomes, ribosomes, ferritin | pexophagy, ribophagy, ferritinophagy | NBR1; NUFIP1; NCOA4 |

### 11.7.2. Mitophagy

**Definition 11.7.2 (Mitophagy)** **Mitophagy** is the selective autophagy of mitochondria, which removes damaged or superfluous mitochondria and, in some cell types, all of them, as in the maturation of reticulocytes into red blood cells.

**Definition 11.7.3 (PINK1 and Parkin)** **PINK1** (PTEN-induced kinase 1) is a mitochondrially targeted Ser/Thr kinase that is continuously imported into healthy mitochondria, cleaved and degraded, but accumulates on the outer membrane of mitochondria that have lost their membrane potential, where it phosphorylates ubiquitin and the ubiquitin-like domain of **Parkin**, a cytosolic E3 ubiquitin ligase of the RBR family, at Ser65; activated Parkin ubiquitylates outer membrane proteins and so marks the damaged mitochondrion for mitophagy.

The logic of the PINK1–Parkin pathway uses protein import as a sensor ([7.3.6](/7.3.6)). In a polarized mitochondrion, PINK1 is pulled by its presequence through the TOM complex into the TIM23 complex, which needs the membrane potential; there the processing peptidase MPP and the intramembrane protease PARL cleave it, and the cleaved form, which now begins with a destabilizing phenylalanine, returns to the cytosol and is degraded by the N-degron pathway and the proteasome. PINK1 is therefore nearly undetectable in healthy cells. When the inner membrane depolarizes (by an uncoupler such as CCCP in experiments, or by damage to the **respiratory chain** in life), import stops, and full-length PINK1 stays in the TOM complex, dimerizes and autophosphorylates. It phosphorylates ubiquitin already present on the outer membrane; phospho-ubiquitin recruits Parkin and, with phosphorylation of Parkin itself, releases it from autoinhibition; Parkin adds more ubiquitin chains, which PINK1 phosphorylates in turn, a feed-forward loop that coats the mitochondrion with phospho-ubiquitin within tens of minutes (Narendra, Youle and colleagues, 2008). The receptors optineurin and NDP52, activated by the kinase TBK1, then recruit the autophagy machinery; Parkin-mediated degradation of mitofusins prevents the damaged mitochondrion from fusing back into the network, and degradation of Miro stops its movement.

**Formula 11.7.1 (A Switch Model of PINK1–Parkin Signaling)** Let $P$ be PINK1 on the outer membrane of one mitochondrion and $U$ its phospho-ubiquitin. PINK1 is made at rate $\sigma$ and removed by import, with a rate constant $k_{\mathrm{imp}}(\Delta\psi)$ that rises steeply with the membrane potential, and by slow turnover $\delta$; phospho-ubiquitin is made by PINK1 at a basal rate $a_0$ and with Parkin amplification $a_1 U^2/(K^2 + U^2)$, and removed by deubiquitylases with rate constant $\gamma$:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = \sigma - \bigl(k_{\mathrm{imp}}(\Delta\psi) + \delta\bigr) P, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t} = P\left(a_0 + \frac{a_1 U^2}{K^2 + U^2}\right) - \gamma U. \tag{11.3}
$$

The first equation converts a continuous variable, the membrane potential, into the amount of a protein: at steady state $P^* = \sigma/(k_{\mathrm{imp}} + \delta)$, which is low as long as import is much faster than turnover and rises toward $\sigma/\delta$ once import fails. The second equation, with the cooperative positive feedback of Parkin, turns this graded signal into an all-or-none decision: below a threshold of $P$ the phospho-ubiquitin stays low, above it the mitochondrion is rapidly coated (the figure). The feedback explains three observations: Parkin recruitment is switch-like and occurs on individual mitochondria in a cell whose other mitochondria are untouched; it follows depolarization with a delay; and deubiquitylases such as USP30, which raise the threshold, can be inhibited to promote mitophagy. The parameters are illustrative; measured kinetics depend strongly on cell type and on the expression of Parkin, which is low in many cultured cells.

Mutations in *PINK1* and *PRKN* (encoding Parkin) cause autosomal recessive, early-onset Parkinson's disease. Why the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra are particularly vulnerable is unclear; these neurons have long, highly branched axons and a high energetic and oxidative load. Reporter mice that measure mitophagy *in vivo* show much basal mitophagy that does not need PINK1, so the pathway may be mainly a response to acute damage. Other routes do not use ubiquitin: the outer membrane proteins NIX (BNIP3L), BNIP3 and FUNDC1 contain LIR motifs and act as receptors themselves. NIX removes all mitochondria from maturing reticulocytes (Sandoval and colleagues, 2008), and FUNDC1, identified by Quan Chen's group, mediates mitophagy in hypoxia, when its dephosphorylation increases its binding to LC3. Mitochondrial quality control as a whole, of which mitophagy is the last line, is taken up in [7.3.12](/7.3.12).

### 11.7.3. Aggrephagy and Condensates

**Definition 11.7.4 (Aggrephagy)** **Aggrephagy** is the selective autophagy of protein aggregates, mainly of misfolded and ubiquitylated proteins that are too large or too compact to be unfolded and degraded by the proteasome.

The receptor p62 (sequestosome 1) has an N-terminal PB1 domain that polymerizes into filaments, a LIR, and a C-terminal ubiquitin-associated domain. When it binds ubiquitin chains on misfolded proteins it forms liquid-like droplets (p62 bodies), a **biomolecular condensate** formed by **liquid–liquid phase separation** ([7.2.21](/7.2.21)) that concentrates the cargo and nucleates an autophagosome on its surface (Hong Zhang's and Sascha Martens's groups, 2018). In the *C. elegans* embryo, the receptor SEPA-1 delivers P granules of the soma to autophagy, a system in which Hong Zhang's group showed that the physical state of a condensate controls its degradation. Aggregates can mature from liquid to gel and solid states as their proteins form **amyloid fibrils**; solid aggregates are no longer handled by p62, and the chaperonin subunit CCT2, acting as a monomer, was identified by Liang Ge's group (2022) as a receptor that binds solid aggregates and LC3 independently of ubiquitin. The material state of the cargo is thus read by different receptors.

### 11.7.4. Autophagy in Disease and Aging

The consequences of losing autophagy depend on the tissue. Mice with neuron-specific deletion of *Atg5* or *Atg7* develop progressive neurodegeneration, with ubiquitin- and p62-positive inclusions in neurons even though no disease-causing protein is expressed (Hara and colleagues; Komatsu and colleagues, 2006): basal autophagy continuously clears proteins in long-lived cells. In liver, loss of autophagy causes hepatomegaly and accumulation of p62, which activates the transcription factor NRF2 and leads to tumors.

- Neurodegeneration: aggregates of huntingtin, α-synuclein and tau are autophagy substrates; mutations in autophagy genes or receptors cause rare neurodegenerative disorders (*WDR45* in a form of brain iron accumulation, *EPG5* in Vici syndrome) and contribute to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (*OPTN*, *TBK1*, *SQSTM1*).
- Infection and inflammation: a common variant of *ATG16L1* (T300A) increases the risk of Crohn's disease by impairing the clearance of intracellular bacteria and the function of Paneth cells, and many pathogens have evolved proteins that block autophagy.
- Cancer: autophagy suppresses tumor initiation by removing damaged mitochondria and p62 (*BECN1* is often monoallelically deleted in breast and ovarian cancers), but established tumors, especially those driven by Ras, depend on autophagy for nutrients under stress, and its inhibition is being tested in therapy ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).
- Aging: autophagic activity declines with age, and several interventions that extend lifespan in model organisms (dietary restriction, reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling and inhibition of TOR in *C. elegans*, rapamycin in mice, spermidine) require autophagy genes.

Whether autophagy itself can kill cells has been debated. In most dying cells autophagosomes accumulate because the cell is trying to survive. In a few cases, such as the destruction of the salivary glands during *Drosophila* metamorphosis and the $\ce{Na+}/\ce{K+}$-ATPase-dependent "autosis" of some stressed neurons, cell death requires the autophagy machinery and is classified as autophagy-dependent cell death ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)).

## Questions

1. Lysosomes isolated from rat liver release little acid phosphatase activity when assayed in isotonic sucrose, but much more in hypotonic medium or with detergent. Explain the observation and what it shows about the organelle. Why does a small leak of hydrolases into the cytosol do little harm?

   **Answer** The substrate (glycerophosphate) cannot reach the enzyme inside intact lysosomes; disrupting the membrane osmotically or with detergent releases the enzyme and exposes it to substrate. This "latency" showed that the enzyme is enclosed in a membrane-bounded particle, and the co-sedimentation of several latent acid hydrolases defined the lysosome (de Duve). Leaked hydrolases are much less active at the cytosolic pH of about 7.2 than at their pH optimum of 4.5–5.5, and cytosolic inhibitors (such as the cystatins for cathepsins) bind the remaining activity; only massive lysosomal rupture, as in silicosis, damages the cell.

2. Using Example 11.1.1, estimate how many chloride ions must enter a lysosome while it acidifies from pH 7.2 to 4.7. What is the final lumenal chloride concentration if the lumen started with 40 mM? Comment on the osmotic consequence.

   **Answer** Nearly every proton pumped must be accompanied by a counter-charge, because the capacitance absorbs only about $10^4$ charges per 250 mV: so about $4 \times 10^6$ chloride ions, minus the small number stored in the membrane potential. In a volume of $6.5 \times 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{L}$ this is $4 \times 10^6/(6.0 \times 10^{23} \times 6.5 \times 10^{-17}) \approx 0.10\,\mathrm{M}$, so lumenal chloride would rise from 40 to about 140 mM. The protons are almost all bound to buffers and do not add free particles, but each chloride adds one osmotically active particle, about 100 mOsm in total, which would draw water in and swell the lysosome unless cations leave in exchange (which is why cation channels such as TMEM175 also contribute counter-charge) or the buffers' counter-ions change. Measured lysosomal chloride is in fact high, about 80–110 mM.

3. Fibroblasts from a patient lack lysosomal β-hexosaminidase activity, but the enzyme protein and its activity are found at high levels in the culture medium; the patient's hepatocytes have normal lysosomal levels. (a) Which step is defective? (b) Predict the result of adding medium from the patient's fibroblasts to normal fibroblasts, and of adding medium from normal fibroblasts to the patient's fibroblasts.

   **Answer** (a) Tagging with mannose 6-phosphate: the hydrolase is made and active but not sorted, and is secreted, as in I-cell disease (GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase deficiency; a defect of the uncovering enzyme would give a milder version). Hepatocytes use M6P-independent routes. (b) Enzyme from the patient's medium lacks M6P and is not taken up by normal fibroblasts through CI-MPR. Enzyme secreted by normal fibroblasts (the fraction that escapes sorting) carries M6P, and the patient's fibroblasts, whose M6P receptors are normal, take it up and deliver it to lysosomes; this cross-correction is how the recognition marker was discovered.

4. In a cell line, the number of LC3 puncta per cell rises threefold after treatment with a drug X. Describe experiments, based on Formula 11.6.1, to decide whether X induces autophagy or blocks the clearance of autophagosomes, and predict the results in both cases.

   **Answer** Measure LC3-II by Western blot (or puncta) with and without bafilomycin A1 (or lysosomal protease inhibitors) for 2–4 hours, in control and X-treated cells. If X induces autophagy ($k_{\mathrm{f}}$ up), the bafilomycin-induced increment of LC3-II is larger with X than in controls (slope of accumulation higher). If X blocks fusion or lysosomal degradation ($k_{\mathrm{fus}}$ or $k_{\mathrm{d}}$ down), LC3-II is already high with X, and bafilomycin adds little or nothing beyond it, the increment being equal to or smaller than in controls. With the mRFP–GFP–LC3 reporter, induction increases both yellow and red-only puncta, a fusion block only yellow ones, and a block of acidification makes all puncta yellow. Degradation of p62 (or of a long-lived protein labeled with radioactive amino acids) is faster with an inducer and slower with a blocker.

5. Why does PINK1 accumulate only on depolarized mitochondria, and why does the cell need Parkin's feed-forward loop rather than simply letting PINK1 ubiquitylate the outer membrane? Use Formula 11.7.1.

   **Answer** PINK1 import into the inner membrane through TIM23 requires the membrane potential; in polarized mitochondria import, cleavage by MPP and PARL and degradation keep it at a very low steady state, $P^* = \sigma/(k_{\mathrm{imp}} + \delta)$ with $k_{\mathrm{imp}} \gg \delta$. When the potential falls, $k_{\mathrm{imp}}$ drops and $P^*$ rises toward $\sigma/\delta$, on the depolarized mitochondrion only, because each mitochondrion has its own potential. PINK1 is a kinase, not a ligase: it phosphorylates existing ubiquitin and Parkin, and the amount of ubiquitin it can mark is small. Parkin makes new ubiquitin chains that PINK1 phosphorylates, which recruits more Parkin; this cooperative positive feedback converts the graded PINK1 signal into a switch (a sharp threshold), so that mildly or transiently depolarized mitochondria are not destroyed, while those above the threshold are fully coated and efficiently engulfed. Deubiquitylases set the threshold.

6. (Open question) An organism lives in an environment with long periods of starvation. Discuss how the coupling between mTORC1, autophagy, TFEB and lysosome reformation allows its cells to survive starvation for hours to days without consuming themselves, and what would go wrong if each link were broken.

   **Answer** Outline: starvation inactivates mTORC1, which releases ULK1 (autophagy initiation) and TFEB (lysosome and autophagy gene expression); autophagy degrades cytoplasm and returns amino acids, which partly reactivate mTORC1. This negative feedback limits autophagy to what is needed and, through autophagic lysosome reformation, regenerates the lysosomes consumed by fusion with autophagosomes. Breaking the links: without autophagy (*Atg5*⁻/⁻), amino acids fall and cells die, as in neonatal mice; without mTORC1 reactivation, autophagy would continue unchecked, lysosomes would be exhausted (no reformation) and essential organelles would be degraded; without TFEB, the degradative capacity could not expand during prolonged starvation, and autophagosomes would accumulate; with constitutive mTORC1 (loss of TSC), autophagy could not be induced, and cells would depend on external nutrients and accumulate damaged components. Selective autophagy receptors would also be needed to protect essential organelles and to target excess or damaged ones first. A quantitative answer would add the time scales: autophagy induction within minutes, TFEB-driven transcription within hours, and reformation after several hours.
