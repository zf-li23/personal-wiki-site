The principles of [7.3.18](/7.3.18), switches, amplification, feedback, adaptation, localization and integration, explain what signaling pathways do in a cell; this chapter follows the signals to their targets. Three kinds of decision are taken. Within minutes, a cell changes its shape and movement, and the main link from receptors to the actin cytoskeleton is the Rho family of small GTPases, whose effectors were met in the cytoskeleton chapters ([7.3.13](/7.3.13)–[7.3.15](/7.3.15)). Within hours, a cell decides whether to divide: mitogens act through Ras, the MAP kinase cascade and the PI 3-kinase branch on the cyclin-dependent kinases that drive the cycle; the cycle itself and its checkpoints follow in [7.3.20](/7.3.20)–[7.3.22](/7.3.22). Over days, a cell adopts a fate, and a small set of pathways not covered in biochemistry, Wnt, Hedgehog, Notch, TGF-β, NF-κB and Hippo, controls most fate decisions in animal development and tissue renewal. These pathways look bewilderingly different, but most of them share one logic: they are kept off by an inhibitor and switched on by removing it. Their mutations are among the most common causes of cancer ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)), and their roles in pattern formation are the subject of developmental biology ([7.5](/7.5)).

## 19.1. Signals and the Cytoskeleton

### 19.1.1. The Rho Family

**Definition 19.1.1 (Rho Family GTPase)** A **Rho family GTPase** is a member of the Rho subfamily of Ras-related **small G proteins** (about 20 in humans, the best known being Rho, Rac and Cdc42) that, in its GTP-bound form at a membrane, binds effector proteins controlling actin polymerization, myosin contractility, microtubules, adhesion and gene expression; it is activated by GEFs of the Dbl (DH–PH) and DOCK families, inactivated by Rho GAPs, and held inactive in the cytosol by guanine nucleotide dissociation inhibitors (RhoGDIs), which bind its C-terminal geranylgeranyl group.

The numbers show where the regulation lies: humans have about 80 Dbl-family and 11 DOCK-family GEFs and more than 70 Rho GAPs, but only three GDIs and a few dozen GTPases. Each GEF and GAP is tied by its other domains to particular receptors, lipids, adhesions or microtubules, so the GTPases are switched on at defined places. Unlike Ras, which stays in the membrane when inactive, Rho GTPases cycle between the membrane and the cytosol: GDIs extract them from membranes, and their release at a membrane is a step of activation.

The division of labor among the three best-studied members was discovered by microinjecting constitutively active forms into quiescent fibroblasts (Ridley and Hall, 1992; Nobes and Hall, 1995): Rho produced **stress fibers** and focal adhesions, Rac **lamellipodia** and ruffles, Cdc42 **filopodia**. Their effectors (**formins**, the WASP and WAVE activators of the **Arp2/3 complex**, PAK and ROCK) and their places in a migrating cell, **Cdc42** and **Rac** at the front, Rho at the rear, are described in [7.3.15](/7.3.15#sec-15.5.2). Here the question is how receptors reach them.

### 19.1.2. From Receptors to GEFs

Each receptor class has its own route to the Rho GTPases:

- Receptors coupled to $\mathrm{G_{12/13}}$ (for thrombin, lysophosphatidic acid and sphingosine 1-phosphate) activate the RGS-domain RhoGEFs p115-RhoGEF, PDZ-RhoGEF and LARG, which are at the same time GAPs for the Gα subunit ([7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.2.5)); platelets and endothelial cells contract within seconds.
- **Receptor tyrosine kinases** and, through Gβγ, chemoattractant GPCRs activate **phosphoinositide 3-kinase**; PIP₃ recruits GEFs with PH domains (Vav, Tiam1, P-Rex) and activates Rac, so that a gradient of PDGF or of a chemoattractant becomes a gradient of protrusion (**chemotaxis**, [7.3.15](/7.3.15#sec-15.5.5)).
- **Integrins** that engage the matrix activate FAK and Src, which phosphorylate scaffolds (p130Cas, paxillin) that recruit Crk and the Rac GEF DOCK180–ELMO; Src also activates p190RhoGAP, which lowers Rho activity during spreading, before Rho rises again as focal adhesions mature. Adhesion and growth factor signals thus converge on the same GTPases.
- Eph receptors bound to ephrins on a neighboring cell phosphorylate the GEF ephexin, which activates RhoA in the growth cone of an axon; **myosin II** contraction collapses the growth cone and repels the axon from the ephrin-bearing territory (contact-dependent signaling, [7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.2.1)). Other guidance cues, semaphorins, netrins and Slits, act through GEFs and GAPs in the same way.

The GTPases also signal to the nucleus. Rho-driven polymerization of **actin filaments** consumes G-actin, which otherwise binds the transcriptional coactivator MRTF (myocardin-related transcription factor) and keeps it in the cytoplasm; freed MRTF enters the nucleus and, with the serum response factor SRF, activates genes for actin, myosin and adhesion proteins (Miralles and colleagues, 2003). The state of the cytoskeleton is thus read as a signal, a theme that returns with YAP (19.4.2).

### 19.1.3. Feedback and Spatial Patterns

The Rho network contains both positive and negative feedback, which turn small differences across a cell into a stable front and rear, the **cell polarity** of a migrating cell. Rac is activated by PIP₃ and in turn activates PI3K, a positive loop that sharpens the front; Rac and Rho inhibit each other (Rac through PAK, Rho through ROCK and the GAPs it activates). A minimal model of the mutual inhibition shows why this makes front and rear exclusive.

**Formula 19.1.1 (Mutual Inhibition of Rac and Rho)** Let the activities of Rac and Rho (in units of their removal rates) be produced at a maximal rate $a$, each inhibited cooperatively by the other with threshold $K$ and steepness $n$, and let a local input $s$ favor Rac:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\,\mathrm{Rac}}{\mathrm{d}t} = s + \frac{a}{1 + (\mathrm{Rho}/K)^n} - \mathrm{Rac}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\,\mathrm{Rho}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{a}{1 + (\mathrm{Rac}/K)^n} - \mathrm{Rho}. \tag{19.1}
$$

For $n > 1$ and sufficiently large $a$ the two nullclines cross three times (panel B above): the system is a toggle switch with a front state, a rear state and an unstable threshold between them (Definition 18.4.2 of [7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.4.3)). A modest bias $s$ at one end of a cell, from a chemoattractant or an adhesion, decides which part becomes the front, and the decision persists after the bias is removed. Real cells combine such local switches with global inhibition and with diffusion of the GTPases in the membrane, which can produce traveling waves: in the cortex of frog and starfish oocytes, Rho activity and F-actin form excitable waves, Rho activating its own GEF (Ect2) and F-actin recruiting a Rho GAP, the dynamics of an activator with delayed inhibitor (Bement and colleagues, 2015). The same Rho zone, focused by the spindle, positions the contractile ring in cytokinesis ([7.3.21](/7.3.21)).

## 19.2. Signals and the Cell Cycle

### 19.2.1. Mitogens Act Through Cyclin D

A mitogen is an extracellular signal that stimulates cell division, usually by overcoming the brakes that hold cells in G1 or G0 (the distinction between mitogens, growth factors and survival factors is drawn in [7.3.22](/7.3.22)). Most mitogens, PDGF, EGF, FGF, IGF and others, act through receptor tyrosine kinases on Ras and the **MAP kinase cascade** ([7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.1.3)). Their effect on the cycle unfolds in two waves of gene expression. Within 15–30 min ERK and its substrates activate the immediate early genes, whose transcription needs no new protein synthesis: *FOS*, *JUN* and *EGR1* (transcription factors, Fos and Jun forming AP-1) and *MYC*. Over the following hours these products induce the delayed early genes, among them *CCND1*, encoding cyclin D1. Cyclin D binds Cdk4 or Cdk6 and starts the phosphorylation of the retinoblastoma protein Rb, which releases the transcription factor E2F; E2F induces cyclin E, and cyclin E–Cdk2 completes the phosphorylation of Rb, a positive feedback loop that commits the cell to S phase and makes it independent of further mitogen. The point of no return, the restriction point of Arthur Pardee (1974), and the molecular details of the Rb–E2F switch belong to [7.3.22](/7.3.22); in the terms of 7.3.18, mitogen signaling converts a graded input into an irreversible switch by positive feedback.

Myc complements the transcriptional route. It is a transcription factor that, with its partner Max, raises the expression of thousands of genes for ribosome biogenesis, nucleotide and amino acid metabolism and cyclin–Cdk components, preparing the cell to grow and divide. Both cyclin D1 and Myc are short-lived (half-lives of about 30 min), so their levels follow the mitogen: when the signal stops they disappear within an hour and the cell returns to quiescence, unless it has passed the restriction point.

Antimitogenic signals such as TGF-β and cell contact induce Cdk inhibitors (p15, p21, p27)](./plots/mitogen-to-cell-cycle.py)

### 19.2.2. Growth and Survival

Division must be matched by growth, or cells would become smaller with each cycle, and the PI3K–**protein kinase B** branch provides the match. Through TSC and Rheb it activates **mTORC1**, which drives protein, lipid and nucleotide synthesis ([7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.6.2)); it inhibits **glycogen synthase kinase 3**, which otherwise phosphorylates cyclin D1 (Thr286) and Myc (Thr58, after priming by ERK at Ser62) and so targets them for nuclear export and degradation; it excludes FOXO transcription factors from the nucleus, lowering the Cdk inhibitor p27; and it phosphorylates Bad and other proteins that would trigger **apoptosis** ([7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.6.3)). The two branches of RTK signaling therefore cooperate, ERK supplying the transcriptional trigger and PKB the conditions for growth and survival. Cells deprived of survival factors (neurons without NGF, hematopoietic progenitors without IL-3) activate the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)).

More signaling is not always more growth. Excessive mitogenic signaling, as from an oncogenic Ras in a normal cell, induces the tumor suppressor ARF, which inhibits Mdm2 and stabilizes p53, and triggers DNA damage from overreplication; the cell arrests permanently in oncogene-induced senescence or dies ([7.3.22](/7.3.22), [7.3.25](/7.3.25)). Only cells that have also lost these safeguards proliferate unchecked, one reason why cancers need several mutations ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

### 19.2.3. Mitogens and Adhesion Cooperate

Most normal cells divide only when they are attached to a substrate, a property called anchorage dependence. Adhesion signals through **integrins** and focal adhesion kinase are needed for sustained ERK activity, for the expression of cyclin D1 and for the degradation of p27 in G1; a suspended fibroblast stimulated with growth factors activates ERK only transiently and does not enter S phase. Cell spreading and tension, read by Rho, ROCK and YAP (19.4.2), add a further requirement: cells confined to small islands of matrix stop dividing and may die, even with ample mitogen (Chen and colleagues, 1997). The loss of anchorage dependence, assayed by colony growth in soft agar, is a hallmark of transformed cells ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

## 19.3. Pathways Switched by Degradation and Proteolysis

The pathways in this section control fate rather than metabolism. Their effectors are latent transcription factors, kept inactive in the cytoplasm or bound as repressors to their target genes, and the signal acts by destroying an inhibitor or cutting a receptor. Because degradation and proteolysis are irreversible, none of these pathways can be reset by a phosphatase; they are switched off by making new inhibitor, often under the control of the pathway itself.

### 19.3.1. Wnt/β-Catenin

The Wnt proteins (19 in humans) are secreted lipid-modified glycoproteins: the acyltransferase Porcupine attaches a palmitoleoyl group that is essential for receptor binding, and the transporter Wntless carries them to the cell surface. The name joins *wingless*, a segment-polarity gene of *Drosophila*, with *Int1*, a mouse gene activated by insertion of mouse mammary tumor virus in breast tumors (Nusse and Varmus, 1982), so the pathway was linked to development and to cancer from the start.

**Definition 19.3.1 (Wnt Signaling Pathway)** The **Wnt signaling pathway** (canonical or Wnt/β-catenin pathway) is the signaling pathway in which a Wnt protein binds a receptor of the Frizzled family (a seven-transmembrane receptor) together with the co-receptor LRP5 or LRP6, inactivating the destruction complex so that β-catenin accumulates and activates transcription of target genes with TCF/LEF transcription factors.

**Definition 19.3.2 (β-Catenin and the Destruction Complex)** **β-Catenin** is an armadillo-repeat protein with two functions: at adherens junctions it links the cytoplasmic tail of cadherins to α-catenin and the actin cytoskeleton, and in the nucleus it is the transcriptional coactivator of TCF/LEF. The **destruction complex** is the cytoplasmic complex of the scaffolds Axin and APC (adenomatous polyposis coli) with the kinases CK1α and GSK3 that phosphorylates free β-catenin at its N-terminus (CK1α at Ser45, then GSK3 at Thr41, Ser37 and Ser33), creating the degron recognized by the ubiquitin ligase SCF^β-TrCP, which targets β-catenin for degradation by the **proteasome**.

Without Wnt, β-catenin is made continuously and destroyed almost as fast as it is made; the free cytosolic pool is small, and TCF/LEF proteins sit on their target genes bound to corepressors of the Groucho/TLE family. Wnt binding brings Frizzled and LRP6 together; Dishevelled binds the cytoplasmic tail of Frizzled and polymerizes through its DIX domain, LRP6 is phosphorylated on its PPPSPxS motifs by CK1γ and GSK3, and the phosphorylated tail binds Axin and inhibits GSK3. In these signalosomes the destruction complex is captured at the membrane and can no longer pass β-catenin to β-TrCP. Newly made β-catenin then accumulates, enters the nucleus without **importins** ([7.3.16](/7.3.16#sec-16.5.5)), displaces Groucho from TCF and recruits coactivators (BCL9, Pygopus, CBP/p300). Its targets include *MYC* and *CCND1*, which drive proliferation (course slides), the stem cell marker *LGR5*, and *AXIN2* and the E3 ligases *RNF43* and *ZNRF3*, which degrade Frizzled: negative feedbacks that limit the response. The R-spondins, which bind LGR5 and remove RNF43/ZNRF3, amplify Wnt signals in the stem cell niche of the intestinal crypt ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)).

Wnt does not stabilize β-catenin pools in junctions, which are protected by cadherin binding; the cadherin-bound and signaling pools are largely separate, although cadherins can buffer the free pool. Wnt proteins also act through β-catenin-independent pathways: in planar cell polarity Frizzled and Dishevelled act through Rho, ROCK and JNK to orient cells within an epithelial sheet (the hairs of the fly wing, the stereocilia of the inner ear), and some Wnts raise cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$.

A quantitative model explains several features of the pathway. Lee and colleagues (2003), measuring the components in *Xenopus* egg extracts, found that Axin is present at only about 20 pM, thousands of times less than β-catenin, APC and GSK3 (tens of nanomolar): Axin is the limiting scaffold, which makes the complex a sensitive point of control and explains why its concentration is kept low (a further increase, as from *AXIN2* induction, strongly inhibits signaling).

**Formula 19.3.1 (β-Catenin Turnover)** Let β-catenin $B$ be made at rate $\nu$ and degraded by the destruction complex, with rate constant $k_{\mathrm{dc}}D$ proportional to the complex activity $D$, and by slower independent turnover $k_{\mathrm{b}}$:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t} = \nu - (k_{\mathrm{b}} + k_{\mathrm{dc}}D)B, \qquad B^* = \frac{\nu}{k_{\mathrm{b}} + k_{\mathrm{dc}}D}, \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k_{\mathrm{b}} + k_{\mathrm{dc}}D}. \tag{19.2}
$$

When $k_{\mathrm{dc}}D \gg k_{\mathrm{b}}$, as in a resting cell, the steady level is low and inversely proportional to the activity of the complex, and β-catenin turns over fast (half-life about 30 min in many cells). Wnt lowers $D$; the level rises, but slowly, because the **response time** is now set by the slow residual degradation, and it falls quickly when Wnt is removed and the complex recovers: the pathway turns on in hours and off in tens of minutes. Losing half of the APC protein, as in the colon of a person heterozygous for an *APC* mutation, roughly doubles the basal level; losing both copies removes $k_{\mathrm{dc}}D$ and gives the Wnt-on level whatever Wnt is present.

Mutations that disable the destruction complex are the initiating event in most colorectal cancers. Inherited *APC* mutations cause familial adenomatous polyposis, in which hundreds to thousands of polyps develop in the colon, one of which almost inevitably progresses to cancer unless the colon is removed; about 80 % of sporadic colorectal cancers carry *APC* mutations, and many of the rest carry mutations of β-catenin's own phosphorylation sites (Ser33, Ser37, Thr41, Ser45) or of *RNF43*. The tumor cells are trapped in the proliferative program of crypt stem cells ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

### 19.3.2. Hedgehog

Hedgehog was found, like Wnt, in the screen of Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus for genes that pattern the *Drosophila* larva (mutant larvae are covered with denticles, like a hedgehog). Vertebrates have three Hedgehog proteins: Sonic hedgehog (Shh), which patterns the neural tube and the limbs, Indian hedgehog (bone and cartilage) and Desert hedgehog (gonads). The ligand is unusual: the precursor cleaves itself, and the cleavage attaches a cholesterol molecule to the C-terminus of the signaling domain, which is also palmitoylated at its N-terminus; the doubly lipidated protein is released from the producing cell by the transporter Dispatched.

**Definition 19.3.3 (Hedgehog Signaling Pathway and Smoothened)** In the **Hedgehog signaling pathway**, Hedgehog binds and inhibits the twelve-transmembrane receptor Patched, thereby relieving its inhibition of **Smoothened**, a seven-transmembrane, GPCR-like transducer that is activated by sterols; active Smoothened prevents the conversion of the Gli (in *Drosophila*, Cubitus interruptus) transcription factors into their repressor forms and releases them from the inhibitor SuFu, so that full-length Gli activates the target genes.

In vertebrates the pathway works in the **primary cilium** ([7.3.14](/7.3.14#sec-14.6.4)). Without Hedgehog, Patched sits in the ciliary membrane and keeps Smoothened out; the orphan GPCR GPR161 in the cilium raises cAMP, **protein kinase A** phosphorylates the Gli proteins, and GSK3 and CK1 add further phosphates that create a β-TrCP degron. The proteasome then degrades Gli3 (and Gli2) only partly, stopping at a tightly folded domain and leaving an N-terminal fragment that acts as a repressor of the target genes; SuFu holds the full-length proteins in the cytoplasm. When Hedgehog binds Patched, Patched leaves the cilium, Smoothened accumulates in it, GPR161 leaves, and PKA activity in the cilium falls; Gli–SuFu complexes move to the ciliary tip, dissociate, and full-length Gli enters the nucleus as an activator. The targets include *PTCH1* and *HHIP*, which bind Hedgehog and restrict its spread (negative feedback), and *GLI1*, an activator that reinforces the response (positive feedback). Mice whose cilia cannot form lose Hedgehog signaling, and many ciliopathies have Hedgehog-like defects such as polydactyly.

How Patched inhibits Smoothened was a puzzle, since the two do not bind each other stoichiometrically: a single Patched molecule can inhibit many Smoothened molecules. Patched resembles bacterial transporters of the RND family, and the current view is that it lowers the accessible cholesterol in the ciliary membrane, while Smoothened is activated by binding cholesterol in a groove of its extracellular and transmembrane domains: Hedgehog acts by stopping a lipid pump. This catalytic inhibition explains why the pathway responds steeply to Hedgehog gradients in the neural tube ([7.5](/7.5)).

Too little Hedgehog signaling causes developmental defects: mutations in *SHH* cause holoprosencephaly (failure of the forebrain to divide), and the plant alkaloid cyclopamine from corn lily, which binds Smoothened, causes cyclopic lambs when eaten by pregnant ewes. Too much causes cancer: people heterozygous for *PTCH1* mutations (Gorlin syndrome) develop many basal cell carcinomas of the skin and medulloblastomas, and almost all sporadic basal cell carcinomas have *PTCH1* loss or activating *SMO* mutations. The Smoothened antagonist vismodegib (2012) treats advanced basal cell carcinoma.

### 19.3.3. NF-κB

**Definition 19.3.4 (NF-κB and IκB)** **NF-κB** is a family of dimeric transcription factors (RelA, also called p65; RelB; c-Rel; p50 and p52, made by partial proteasomal processing of the precursors p105 and p100) that share a Rel homology domain for DNA binding and dimerization and regulate genes of inflammation, immunity and cell survival. **IκB** proteins (IκBα, IκBβ, IκBε, and the ankyrin-repeat regions of p105 and p100) bind NF-κB dimers, mask their nuclear localization signal and keep them in the cytoplasm until signal-induced phosphorylation by IKK marks them for ubiquitylation by SCF^β-TrCP and degradation.

NF-κB is activated by inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α through TNFR1, IL-1), by microbial products through Toll-like receptors (lipopolysaccharide through TLR4), by antigen receptors of lymphocytes and by DNA damage. The receptors assemble complexes of adaptors (TRADD, TRAF2, RIPK1 for TNFR1; MyD88 and IRAK kinases for IL-1R and TLRs) that are decorated with nondegradative ubiquitin chains linked through Lys63 and through the N-terminal Met (linear chains made by the LUBAC complex). These chains recruit the kinase TAK1 and the IκB kinase complex (IKK: the kinases IKKα and IKKβ with the regulatory subunit NEMO, which binds linear ubiquitin), and TAK1 activates IKKβ. IKKβ phosphorylates IκBα on Ser32 and Ser36, and IκBα is degraded within minutes; the freed RelA–p50 dimer enters the nucleus. In this canonical pathway ubiquitin works in two ways, as a scaffold that assembles the kinase and as a tag that destroys the inhibitor. A slower noncanonical pathway, used by a few receptors of lymphoid organogenesis, activates IKKα through the kinase NIK and processes p100 to p52.

Among the first NF-κB target genes are those for IκBα and for A20, a ubiquitin-editing enzyme that removes the Lys63 chains and inhibits IKK: two negative feedback loops with the delay of transcription and translation. Hoffmann and colleagues (2002) showed that in fibroblasts the nuclear activity of NF-κB after TNF stimulation oscillates and that the oscillation disappears in cells lacking IκBα, while cells lacking IκBβ and IκBε oscillate more strongly; Nelson and colleagues (2004) followed fluorescent RelA in single cells and saw it shuttle in and out of the nucleus with a period of about 100 min.

**Formula 19.3.2 (A Minimal NF-κB–IκBα Oscillator)** Krishna, Jensen and Sneppen (2006) reduced the system to nuclear NF-κB $N_{\mathrm{n}}$, IκBα mRNA $I_{\mathrm{m}}$ and cytoplasmic IκBα $I$ (total NF-κB $N_{\mathrm{tot}}$):
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}N_{\mathrm{n}}}{\mathrm{d}t} &= k_{\mathrm{Nin}}(N_{\mathrm{tot}} - N_{\mathrm{n}})\frac{K_{\mathrm{I}}}{K_{\mathrm{I}} + I} - k_{\mathrm{Iin}}I\frac{N_{\mathrm{n}}}{K_{\mathrm{N}} + N_{\mathrm{n}}}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}I_{\mathrm{m}}}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_{\mathrm{t}}N_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \gamma_{\mathrm{m}}I_{\mathrm{m}},\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t} &= k_{\mathrm{tl}}I_{\mathrm{m}} - \alpha\,[\mathrm{IKK}](N_{\mathrm{tot}} - N_{\mathrm{n}})\frac{I}{K_{\mathrm{I}} + I},
\end{aligned} \tag{19.3}
$$
where the first equation describes import of free NF-κB and its removal from the nucleus by newly made IκBα, the second the induction of IκBα transcription, and the third the synthesis of IκBα and its IKK-dependent degradation in complexes with cytoplasmic NF-κB.

With the published parameters and constant IKK, nuclear NF-κB rises within minutes, is pulled out of the nucleus by the new IκBα and returns in a train of pulses one to two hours apart; a brief pulse of TNF gives a single wave; and without the feedback, NF-κB stays in the nucleus. Which genes respond depends on the dynamics: genes for inflammatory mediators with unstable mRNAs follow each pulse, while others need prolonged activity, so that the number and timing of pulses carry information beyond their mean (Tay and colleagues, 2010, found that single fibroblasts respond to TNF digitally, with a fraction of cells oscillating that rises with the dose). NF-κB is central to innate and adaptive immunity ([7.15](/7.15)); its targets also include antiapoptotic genes (cIAPs, Bcl-xL, the caspase-8 inhibitor FLIP), which is why TNF usually kills cells only when NF-κB is blocked ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)), and constitutive activity drives some lymphomas.

### 19.3.4. Notch

Notch signaling is the archetype of contact-dependent signaling ([7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.2.1)). Both receptor and ligands are single-pass transmembrane proteins: mammals have four Notch receptors and five ligands of the DSL family (Delta-like 1, 3 and 4; Jagged 1 and 2).

**Definition 19.3.5 (Notch Signaling Pathway)** In the **Notch signaling pathway**, a transmembrane ligand of the Delta/Serrate family on one cell binds the Notch receptor on an adjacent cell and triggers two successive cleavages of Notch, by an ADAM metalloprotease outside the membrane and by γ-secretase within it; the released Notch intracellular domain (NICD) moves to the nucleus, binds the DNA-binding protein CSL (CBF1/RBPJ in mammals, Suppressor of Hairless in flies) and the coactivator Mastermind, and activates transcription of target genes such as those of the Hes and Hey repressors.

**Definition 19.3.6 (Regulated Intramembrane Proteolysis)** **Regulated intramembrane proteolysis** is the signal-controlled cleavage of a membrane protein within its transmembrane segment by an intramembrane protease, usually after a first cleavage has removed most of its extracellular or lumenal domain, which releases a soluble fragment that acts elsewhere in the cell (often as a transcriptional regulator).

**Definition 19.3.7 (γ-Secretase)** **γ-Secretase** is an intramembrane aspartyl protease complex of four subunits, presenilin (the catalytic subunit), nicastrin, APH-1 and PEN-2, that cleaves the transmembrane segments of many type I membrane proteins after their ectodomains have been shed, among them Notch and the amyloid precursor protein.

Each step is a point of control. Notch is first cleaved in the **Golgi apparatus** by furin (S1), so that it reaches the surface as a heterodimer; its EGF repeats are O-fucosylated, and the Fringe glycosyltransferases extend these sugars and make Notch more responsive to Delta and less to Jagged, which sharpens boundaries between tissues. In the absence of ligand a negative regulatory region covers the S2 site. Binding of a ligand that is ubiquitylated by the E3 ligase Mind bomb and endocytosed by the signaling cell pulls on Notch with a force of a few piconewtons, unfolds this region and exposes S2 to ADAM10; the remaining membrane-anchored fragment is cleaved by γ-secretase at S3, and NICD is released. In the nucleus NICD converts CSL from a repressor, bound to corepressors, into an activator. Ligands in the same cell as Notch inhibit it (cis-inhibition), which helps neighboring cells adopt opposite states.

Notch signaling has no amplification: one receptor gives one NICD, which acts once and is then phosphorylated and degraded (by the E3 ligase Fbw7). The level of signal is therefore set by the number of receptors activated, and the system responds with little delay to changes in contact. This suits its main use, comparing neighboring cells: in lateral inhibition, cells that express more Delta activate Notch in their neighbors, which then make less Delta, so that a uniform sheet resolves into a salt-and-pepper pattern of different fates (neural precursors among epidermal cells, hair cells among supporting cells; [7.3.24](/7.3.24)). Activating *NOTCH1* mutations are found in more than half of T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemias (Weng and colleagues, 2004), and *JAG1* or *NOTCH2* mutations cause Alagille syndrome (liver, heart and skeletal defects).

Regulated intramembrane proteolysis is used by other pathways too. γ-Secretase cleaves the amyloid precursor protein after β-secretase has shed its ectodomain, producing the amyloid-β peptides that accumulate in the brain in Alzheimer's disease; mutations in presenilin that shift the cleavage towards the longer, more aggregation-prone Aβ42 cause early-onset familial Alzheimer's disease. In the Golgi apparatus the site-1 protease and the intramembrane metalloprotease site-2 protease release the transcription factors SREBP, when sterols are low ([7.1.26](/7.1.26)), and ATF6, when unfolded proteins accumulate in the ER, in the **unfolded protein response** ([7.3.7](/7.3.7)).

## 19.4. Pathways Switched by Receptor Kinases and Nuclear Shuttling

### 19.4.1. TGF-β and Smad

The TGF-β superfamily comprises more than 30 secreted dimeric proteins: the TGF-βs, activins, nodal, the bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), growth and differentiation factors (GDFs) and anti-Müllerian hormone. They control cell proliferation, differentiation, extracellular matrix production and immune responses, and form morphogen gradients in embryos (Dpp, the fly BMP, of [7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.2.2)). TGF-β itself is secreted in a latent complex bound to the matrix and is released by proteases or by integrin-mediated pulling, so the matrix stores and gates the signal.

**Definition 19.4.1 (TGF-β Signaling Pathway)** In the **TGF-β signaling pathway**, a dimeric ligand of the TGF-β superfamily binds a complex of two type II and two type I receptor serine/threonine kinases; the constitutively active type II kinase phosphorylates the glycine–serine-rich (GS) region of the type I receptor, which activates its kinase, and the type I receptor phosphorylates receptor-regulated Smad proteins, which carry the signal to the nucleus.

**Definition 19.4.2 (Smad)** A **Smad** is a member of a family of transcriptional regulators named after the *Drosophila* Mad and *C. elegans* Sma proteins. Receptor-regulated Smads (R-Smads: Smad2 and Smad3 for TGF-β, activin and nodal; Smad1, Smad5 and Smad8 for BMPs) are phosphorylated at two C-terminal serines by type I receptors and then form complexes with the common mediator Smad4 (Co-Smad); the inhibitory Smads Smad6 and Smad7 antagonize the receptors.

Humans have seven type I receptors (ALK1–7) and five type II receptors, which combine with the ligands to select the R-Smad. In the absence of ligand the immunophilin FKBP12 binds the GS region of the type I receptor and keeps it inactive. The adaptor SARA, with a FYVE domain that binds PI(3)P, presents Smad2 and Smad3 to the receptors on early endosomes, another case of signaling organized by endocytosis ([7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.5.3)). Phosphorylated R-Smads form trimers with Smad4 and accumulate in the nucleus, where they bind DNA weakly (at GTCT or GC-rich motifs) and depend on cell-type-specific partners (FoxH1, Runx, AP-1 and many others) for their targets; the same ligand therefore activates different genes in different cells. Negative feedback comes from Smad7, which binds the activated type I receptor and recruits the ubiquitin ligases Smurf1 and Smurf2 (with WW domains that bind a PY motif of Smad7), and from nuclear phosphatases that dephosphorylate R-Smads. Like other receptors, TGF-β receptors also activate MAP kinases and Rho, the non-Smad branches.

Smads are not imported once and kept in the nucleus: they shuttle continuously between nucleus and cytoplasm, whether phosphorylated or not. Their nuclear accumulation during signaling results from slower export of phosphorylated Smad–Smad4 complexes, while dephosphorylation in the nucleus returns them to the shuttling pool (Schmierer and Hill, 2005). As a result the amount of Smad in the nucleus reports the current activity of the receptors: Inman, Nicolás and Hill (2002) showed that adding a type I receptor kinase inhibitor after an hour of TGF-β stimulation returned Smad2 to the cytoplasm within about half an hour.

TGF-β is a paradoxical signal in cancer. In epithelial cells it is cytostatic: Smad complexes induce the Cdk inhibitors p15 and p21 and repress *MYC* ([7.3.22](/7.3.22)), and inactivating mutations of *TGFBR2* (in colorectal cancers with mismatch-repair defects) and of *SMAD4* (in half of pancreatic cancers) remove this brake. In advanced tumors, however, cells that have lost the cytostatic arm but kept the rest respond to TGF-β with an epithelial–mesenchymal transition, invasion and immunosuppression. The course slides list other diseases of the superfamily: overproduction of TGF-β drives fibrosis of liver, lung and kidney by inducing matrix deposition; mutations of the BMP antagonist Noggin and of GDF5 cause fusions and malformations of joints and bones; mutations of the type I receptor ALK1 or the co-receptor endoglin cause hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia; and mutations of anti-Müllerian hormone or its receptor cause persistent Müllerian duct syndrome. Excess TGF-β signaling also underlies the vascular disease of Marfan syndrome (mutant fibrillin-1 fails to sequester latent TGF-β) and Loeys–Dietz syndrome.

### 19.4.2. Hippo–YAP/TAZ

How does an organ know when to stop growing? Genetic screens in *Drosophila* for mutations that cause tissue overgrowth identified a kinase cascade named after the overgrown, folded heads of mutant flies.

**Definition 19.4.3 (Hippo Pathway)** The **Hippo pathway** is a kinase cascade in which the kinase Hippo (MST1/2 in mammals), with the scaffold Salvador (SAV1), phosphorylates and activates the kinase Warts (LATS1/2) and its partner Mats (MOB1); LATS phosphorylates the transcriptional coactivators Yorkie (YAP and TAZ in mammals), which are then retained in the cytoplasm or degraded, so that the pathway restrains cell proliferation and organ growth.

**Definition 19.4.4 (YAP)** **YAP** (Yes-associated protein) and its paralog TAZ are transcriptional coactivators that lack DNA-binding domains, bind the TEAD transcription factors through their N-terminal regions and activate genes for proliferation, survival and matrix interactions; phosphorylation by LATS (at Ser127 of YAP) creates a binding site for 14-3-3 proteins that keeps them in the cytoplasm, and further phosphorylation creates a β-TrCP degron.

The logic is again double negative: active Hippo signaling turns YAP off, and growth requires that the cascade be inhibited. Its inputs are not a single ligand but the state of the cell in its tissue: contact with neighbors and apicobasal polarity (through Merlin/NF2, the protocadherin Fat and Crumbs, which activate the kinases), and the mechanical environment. Overexpression of YAP in the mouse liver increases the organ's size about fourfold within weeks, and the liver returns to normal size when YAP is switched off (Dong and colleagues; Camargo and colleagues, 2007); at high density, cultured cells inactivate YAP, which contributes to contact inhibition of proliferation (Zhao and colleagues, 2007; [7.3.22](/7.3.22)).

YAP and TAZ are also the main readers of mechanical signals. Cells on a stiff matrix, cells spread over a large area and cells under tension have nuclear YAP; on soft substrates or small islands YAP is cytoplasmic. This mechanical regulation requires Rho, ROCK and actomyosin tension but is partly independent of LATS (Dupont and colleagues, 2011), and part of it may come from the stretching of nuclear pores by force transmitted through the cytoskeleton and the **LINC complex** ([7.3.16](/7.3.16#sec-16.5.5)). GPCRs feed in through the same route: lysophosphatidic acid and sphingosine 1-phosphate, acting through $\mathrm{G_{12/13}}$ and Rho, inhibit LATS and activate YAP, whereas glucagon and epinephrine, acting through $\mathrm{G_s}$ and PKA, inhibit it (Yu and colleagues, 2012). Mechanical and hormonal signals and cell density thus converge on one coactivator, and the details of the mechanical inputs are taken up with cell junctions and the matrix ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)). Hippo pathway defects are found in mesothelioma (loss of *NF2*), uveal melanoma (activating mutations of $\mathrm{G_q}$ that activate YAP) and in rare sarcomas with *TAZ* or *YAP* fusion genes.

### 19.4.3. JAK–STAT and Nuclear Receptors Revisited

Two further routes to the nucleus were described in biochemistry. In the **JAK–STAT pathway** ([7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.2)), cytokine receptors activate associated Janus kinases, which phosphorylate STATs; phosphotyrosine–SH2 interactions dimerize the STATs, which enter the nucleus, and SOCS proteins provide negative feedback. **Nuclear receptors** ([7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.4)) are themselves the transcription factors: the hormone releases steroid receptors from Hsp90 in the cytoplasm, or exchanges corepressors for coactivators on receptors that already sit on DNA. Both pathways are short, with the transducer and the transcription factor in one protein or one step; they contrast with the long chains of inhibitors of the developmental pathways.

## 19.5. The Common Logic of Fate Pathways

### 19.5.1. Derepression and the Transcriptional Switch

Comparing the pathways reveals a shared design (table). Almost every one is kept off by an inhibitor and switched on by inactivating it, like the plant hormone pathways of [7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.5.2); and in most, the DNA-binding protein that mediates the response sits on its targets as a repressor in the absence of signal and becomes an activator when the signal brings a coactivator.

| Pathway | Default (off) state | Signal removes | Effector | Repressor → activator switch | Feedback from targets |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wnt/β-catenin | destruction complex degrades β-catenin | activity of the destruction complex | β-catenin | TCF/LEF–Groucho → TCF/LEF–β-catenin | Axin2, RNF43/ZNRF3 (negative); LGR5 |
| Hedgehog | Patched inhibits Smoothened; Gli processed to repressor | Patched activity | full-length Gli | Gli repressor → Gli activator | Ptch1, Hhip (negative); Gli1 (positive) |
| NF-κB | IκB retains NF-κB in the cytoplasm | IκB (degraded) | NF-κB dimers | (p50 homodimers repress some genes) | IκBα, A20 (negative) |
| Notch | CSL with corepressors on targets | (proteolysis of Notch) | NICD | CSL–corepressor → CSL–NICD–Mastermind | Hes repressors, Fringe |
| TGF-β/Smad | FKBP12 inhibits type I receptor; Smads shuttle | inhibition of the receptor | phospho-R-Smad–Smad4 | Smad partners with cell-specific cofactors | Smad7, Ski/SnoN (negative) |
| Hippo | (off when Hippo is active) LATS phosphorylates YAP | LATS activity | YAP/TAZ | TEAD–VGLL4 → TEAD–YAP | LATS2, NF2 (negative) |

Barolo and Posakony (2002) called three features of this design the "habits of highly effective signaling pathways". Default repression: target genes are actively repressed by the pathway's own DNA-binding protein in cells that do not receive the signal, which prevents expression by other activators. Activator insufficiency: the coactivator released by signaling is not sufficient on its own, and target genes need other, cell-type-specific activators as well, so the same signal activates different genes in different cells. Cooperative activation: the coactivator and these local factors act together on enhancers, so that the response is restricted to the right cells and the right time. The design turns a single pathway into a switch whose output is set by the cell's history: combinatorial control in the sense of [7.3.24](/7.3.24).

Derepression has two further advantages. A pathway kept off by an excess of active inhibitor is protected against noise: random fluctuations in the transducer have no effect until the inhibitor is removed. And degradation-based switches can be fast to switch on, because the effector is already made (NF-κB, preformed in the cytoplasm, enters the nucleus within minutes), or deliberately slow, because the effector must be newly made (β-catenin); the choice depends on how quickly the cell must respond.

### 19.5.2. Feedback, Crosstalk and Combinatorial Control

Every pathway induces some of its own inhibitors, and the delays of transcription and translation make many of them pulse (18.4.5): NF-κB with IκBα, Notch with Hes1 (the segmentation clock, [7.3.24](/7.3.24)), Wnt with Axin2 in the same clock, and p53 with Mdm2. Such feedback limits the duration of the response, buffers it against variation in the amounts of the components and, in some cases, makes the response to a ligand gradient adapt to fold changes (Goentoro and Kirschner, 2009, for β-catenin).

The pathways also share components. GSK3 acts in the Wnt destruction complex, phosphorylates Gli, and is inhibited by PKB in insulin signaling ([7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.1.5)); CK1 acts in Wnt and Hedgehog signaling; β-TrCP targets β-catenin, IκBα and Gli; the Smurf ubiquitin ligases and the WW domains of YAP link the TGF-β and Hippo pathways; YAP binds β-catenin and Smads and is required for some of their transcriptional responses. Insulation is maintained by scaffolds, by compartmentalization (a GSK3 molecule bound to Axin is not available to other substrates) and by the cell-specific cofactors of each pathway. In development the pathways are used in sequence and in combination: Wnt and BMP gradients set up the axes of the embryo, Hedgehog and BMPs pattern the neural tube in opposite directions, and Notch refines the boundaries; the same few pathways generate the diversity of animal form ([7.5](/7.5)).

### 19.5.3. Pathways in Disease

Because the fate pathways control proliferation and differentiation, their components are frequently mutated in cancer, and the type of tumor reflects the tissue in which each pathway drives stem or progenitor cells.

|---|---|---|---|---|

Notch is both an oncogene and a tumor suppressor depending on the tissue: it maintains progenitors in the T-cell lineage, but promotes differentiation in skin, where its loss lets keratinocyte progenitors proliferate. The context dependence of [7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.3.3) thus extends to disease. Colorectal cancer, in which the Wnt, Ras, PI3K, TGF-β and p53 pathways are hit in sequence, shows how a tumor accumulates defects in several of these pathways ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

## Questions

1. Constitutively active Rho, Rac and Cdc42 injected into fibroblasts produce stress fibers, lamellipodia and filopodia. Predict the effect of (a) a ROCK inhibitor on a migrating fibroblast, (b) loss of p190RhoGAP on cell spreading, (c) a dominant-negative ephexin on axon guidance by ephrins.

   **Answer** (a) ROCK raises myosin light-chain phosphorylation; its inhibitor lowers contractility, so stress fibers and mature focal adhesions disappear, the rear fails to retract and the cell becomes long with trailing tails, while Rac-driven protrusion at the front continues or even increases (less Rho-mediated inhibition of Rac). (b) p190RhoGAP lowers Rho during early spreading; without it Rho stays high, contractility opposes protrusion, and cells spread poorly and form premature stress fibers. (c) Without ephexin activation of RhoA, ephrin no longer collapses growth cones of Eph-expressing axons; axons fail to be repelled and grow into inappropriate territories.

2. Using Formula 19.1.1, explain why a transient gradient of chemoattractant can leave a cell with a persistent front, and what would happen to front–rear polarity if the inhibition of Rho by Rac were lost.

   **Answer** With mutual inhibition of sufficient strength the system has two stable states, front (Rac high, Rho low) and rear (Rho high, Rac low). A transient bias $s$ at one end pushes that region past the threshold into the front state, which then persists after the bias is gone (hysteresis, as in 18.4.3). If Rac no longer inhibits Rho, the toggle loses its bistability: Rho is everywhere at the level set by its own production, the front and rear are no longer mutually exclusive, and Rac activity becomes graded and depends continuously on the current input; cells polarize weakly, change direction often and form protrusions on several sides.

3. Cells are stimulated with serum, and cycloheximide (an inhibitor of protein synthesis) is added at the same time. Which mRNAs still accumulate, and which do not? Why does a cell stop progressing through G1 if serum is removed early, but not after a certain point?

   **Answer** Immediate early genes (*FOS*, *JUN*, *EGR1*, *MYC*) are induced by activation of preexisting transcription factors (such as ternary complex factors phosphorylated by ERK) and accumulate even more with cycloheximide (which also blocks synthesis of their unstable repressors). Delayed early genes such as *CCND1* need the newly made immediate early proteins and are not induced. Cyclin D and Myc are unstable, so early serum withdrawal lets them disappear and the cell returns to G0. Once cyclin D–Cdk4/6 and cyclin E–Cdk2 have phosphorylated enough Rb, E2F induces cyclin E and the positive feedback keeps the cycle going without mitogen: the restriction point.

4. Explain, with Eq. (19.2), why β-catenin accumulates slowly after Wnt addition but disappears quickly after Wnt removal. What would be the effect on the time course of doubling the synthesis rate $\nu$?

   **Answer** The approach to a new steady state is governed by the total removal rate constant after the change, $k_{\mathrm{b}} + k_{\mathrm{dc}}D$. With Wnt the destruction complex is inhibited, $D$ is small and the removal rate is low, so the rise has a long time constant (hours). After Wnt removal the complex is active again and the removal rate is high, so the decline takes tens of minutes. Doubling $\nu$ doubles both steady levels but does not change either time constant (the response time is independent of production, 7.2.22).

5. In basal cell nevus (Gorlin) syndrome, patients heterozygous for a *PTCH1* mutation develop many basal cell carcinomas. Explain why loss of Patched activates the pathway, why the tumors respond to vismodegib, and why an activating *SMO* mutation can make a tumor resistant to it.

   **Answer** Patched inhibits Smoothened; without functional Patched (after loss of the second allele in a skin cell), Smoothened is active even without Hedgehog, Gli is not processed into its repressor and activates growth-promoting targets. Vismodegib binds Smoothened and inhibits it; since the defect lies upstream of Smoothened, blocking Smoothened switches the pathway off. Mutations in *SMO* that prevent drug binding or activate it constitutively, or defects downstream (loss of *SUFU*, amplification of *GLI2*), keep Gli active in the presence of the drug.

6. Compare how NF-κB and Notch are switched off. Why can the NF-κB pathway oscillate while Notch signaling transmits a signal stoichiometrically?

   **Answer** NF-κB is switched off by newly made IκBα, which enters the nucleus, binds NF-κB and exports it, and by A20, which inactivates IKK; both are target genes, so the inhibitor arrives after the delay of transcription and translation, and with continued IKK activity the cycle of degradation and resynthesis repeats: delayed negative feedback produces oscillations. Notch signaling is switched off by the degradation of NICD in the nucleus (after phosphorylation and ubiquitylation by Fbw7) and by the removal of receptors; each NICD comes from one cleaved receptor and is not regenerated, so the signal is proportional to the number of activated receptors and has no amplifying or oscillating loop in the core pathway (oscillations in the segmentation clock arise downstream, from Hes1 repressing its own gene).

7. A cell line expresses a mutant Smad2 that cannot be exported from the nucleus. Predict the distribution of Smad2 before and after TGF-β, and the response to a type I receptor inhibitor added after an hour of TGF-β. What does this tell us about how cells read the duration of a TGF-β signal?

   **Answer** Without export Smad2 is nuclear even before stimulation, but unphosphorylated and inactive; TGF-β still phosphorylates it only when it is in the cytoplasm, so phosphorylation is reduced (newly made molecules are phosphorylated before entering, but nuclear molecules dephosphorylated by nuclear phosphatases cannot be rephosphorylated). After receptor inhibition, phospho-Smad2 is still dephosphorylated in the nucleus and the transcriptional response ends, but the nuclear localization no longer changes, so localization is no longer a readout. In normal cells, continuous shuttling and dephosphorylation make the nuclear level of active Smad2 track receptor activity with a delay of minutes, so target genes respond to how long the receptors are active rather than to a single initial event.

8. (Open question) YAP is nuclear in sparse cells on a stiff substrate and cytoplasmic in dense cells or on soft substrates. Design experiments to decide whether the effect of stiffness is mediated by LATS, and discuss how this mechanism could help an organ stop growing at the right size.

   **Answer** Culture cells on soft and stiff hydrogels of the same chemistry and measure YAP localization and target gene expression (CTGF, CYR61): in wild-type cells, in cells lacking LATS1 and LATS2 (CRISPR knockout), and in cells expressing a YAP mutant whose LATS sites are replaced by alanines (YAP 5SA). If stiffness still controls the localization of the nonphosphorylatable YAP, or of YAP in LATS-null cells, the effect is at least partly LATS independent (as found by Dupont and colleagues); treat cells with ROCK or myosin II inhibitors, or with latrunculin, to test the role of tension and F-actin, and measure phosphorylated YAP to see whether LATS activity changes. For organ size: as tissue grows, cell density increases, junctions and polarity complexes activate the Hippo kinases, and compression and lower tension at the tissue center reduce YAP activity, whereas stretched cells at the edge keep proliferating; the mechanical state thus integrates the size of the tissue and feeds back on proliferation, so growth slows as the organ approaches its target size. An open problem is how the set point is encoded, since mechanical feedback alone gives uniform growth only with additional morphogen gradients.
