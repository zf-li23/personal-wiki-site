Cell biology studies the cell as the smallest system that lives: one that takes in matter and energy, keeps itself far from equilibrium and makes copies of itself. Biochemistry and molecular biology have already described its molecules, its reactions and the flow of its genetic information; cell biology asks how these parts are arranged in space and coordinated in time so that the whole behaves as one unit. Its core questions are concrete. How do membranes divide a eukaryotic cell into compartments with different chemistries, and how does each of some $10^4$ proteins find the compartment where it works? How do the filaments of the **cytoskeleton** give the cell its shape, its forces and its internal transport? How does a cell receive and integrate signals, and how does it decide to grow, divide, differentiate or die? And how do cells hold together and cooperate in a tissue? Throughout, the course treats the cell as an integrated system and writes its properties, such as the size of an organelle, the length of a filament, the timing of the cycle and the sharpness of a decision, as simple quantitative models wherever the data allow.

The chapters follow the way a cell is built and run. They begin with the cell theory, the scale of cells and the numbers inside them, and the methods by which cells are seen, taken apart and perturbed: light and electron microscopy, culture, fractionation and genetic perturbation. Then comes the boundary and its subdivision: the membrane as it exists in a living cell, the pumps and channels that together set the cell's ion composition, volume and **membrane potential**, and the logic of **protein sorting** into a dozen compartments. The **endomembrane system** is followed along the flow of membrane, from the endoplasmic reticulum through the machinery of vesicle traffic and the Golgi apparatus to the plasma membrane, and back inward through endocytosis to lysosomes and autophagy. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are treated as **organelles** that grow, divide, fuse, import their proteins and keep a genome of their own, while their energy conversion belongs to biochemistry. Three chapters on actin, microtubules and intermediate filaments end with polarity and migration as problems of self-organization; two on the nucleus treat it first as a gated compartment and then as the space of chromosomes and the nucleolus. Signaling is taken from the view of the cell, as networks that make switches, pulses and memories and that coordinate the cytoskeleton, the cycle and the fate of a cell. The **cell cycle** is followed from its CDK control system through the mechanics of mitosis, cytokinesis and meiosis to the control of proliferation, growth and cell size. The last group places the cell in its society: junctions and the extracellular matrix, differentiation and stem cells, death and senescence, and cancer as the breakdown of that society. The course summary puts the cell back together, as a set of coupled steady states and decisions in which the same few designs, balances of inflow and outflow, switches and clocks, recur at every level.

The molecular foundations come from [biochemistry](/7.1), which covers membranes and transport, the molecular mechanisms of signal transduction and the chemiosmotic coupling of the energy organelles, and from [molecular biology](/7.2), which covers chromatin, gene regulation and translation. The thermodynamics of transport and of macromolecular solutions comes from [physical chemistry](/6.3), the fluorescence on which most imaging relies from [analytical chemistry](/6.4), the diffraction limit of the microscope from [optics](/5.4), and the language of the quantitative models from [ordinary differential equations](/2.4). The cell in turn is the setting for the chromosomes of [genetics](/7.4), the fate decisions and gametes of [developmental biology](/7.5), the tissues of [animal physiology](/7.14) and the immune cells of [immunology](/7.15); the mechanics of membranes, filaments and motors continues in [biophysics](/7.6), and the networks behind switches, oscillators and size control in [systems biology](/7.9).

Compared with biochemistry, cell biology is a more concrete subject and an easier one to learn. Its objects can be seen, and many of its concepts say what they mean in their names: one can guess what a contractile ring, a coated vesicle or a nuclear pore does before reading about it. That concreteness has a cost, because the subject easily becomes a catalogue of structures. I think the cell, as the most basic unit of life, is the place where a systems view should begin, with the question of how local parts coordinate to form a whole: how a compartment keeps its identity while membrane flows through it, how a cell that starts symmetric chooses a front, how growth is matched to division. This is why these pages add quantitative models wherever they can. I have heard that even a course explicitly named quantitative cell biology, in Peking University's integrated science program, is not really quantitative, and I can see why: an end-to-end model that predicts the behavior of a eukaryotic cell from its parts is still out of reach. What can be done is to model the pieces, the pump–leak balance of volume, the treadmilling and **dynamic instability** of filaments, the CDK oscillator, the scaling of organelles and cells with size, and to show that they connect.

In China the benchmark textbook of the subject has long been the textbook Cell Biology edited by Zhonghe Zhai. Its fifth edition is now edited by Mingxiao Ding and colleagues, and its quality has not declined: it is a book that is enough on its own, readable and complete at once, with excellent color figures. The olympiad training I received was built on it and covered even more ground than the university course. Among the English books, Alberts' *Molecular Biology of the Cell* is the classic and, besides the Chinese book, the main reference of the university course; its companion *Essential Cell Biology*, also available in translation, is an important source, and *Lewin's Cells* is comprehensive as well. Jinfa Wang's Cell Biology is worth opening only for unusual concepts or interpretations; the questions that call for it are usually dated, and for current ones the Ding book is entirely sufficient. The university course itself (30450514) was not very systematic. It mixed Chinese and English, with the second half taught mostly in Chinese while the exams were mostly in English; its content was up to date but not complete, and it spent much class time on questions already covered in general biology. The first half was organized systematically, but the second half carried too little information to support review, and on the whole I found studying the Ding book on my own more rewarding.
