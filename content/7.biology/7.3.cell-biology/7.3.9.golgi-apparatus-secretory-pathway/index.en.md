Proteins that have folded and passed quality control in the ER ([7.3.7](/7.3.7)) leave it in **COPII** vesicles ([7.3.8](/7.3.8)) and arrive at the Golgi apparatus, a stack of flattened cisternae through which every secreted protein, every plasma-membrane protein and every lysosomal enzyme passes. The Golgi does two things. It modifies its cargo: it trims and rebuilds **N-linked glycans**, adds **O-linked glycans**, assembles the **glycosaminoglycan** chains of **proteoglycans**, sulfates and cleaves proteins, and makes **sphingolipids**. And it sorts: at its exit face, the trans Golgi network, cargo is separated into carriers bound for the plasma membrane, for secretory granules that wait for a signal, and for **endosomes** and **lysosomes**. Because these operations happen in sequence across the stack, the Golgi is the clearest example of how a cell organizes a chemical assembly line in space. This chapter describes the structure of the stack (9.1), the processing of glycans along it (9.2), the long debate about how cargo moves through it (9.3), sorting and secretion at its exit (9.4), the special sorting problem of polarized epithelial cells (9.5), and the experiments by which the pathway and its machinery were discovered (9.6). The chemistry of glycans is in [7.1.10](/7.1.10); the coats, **Rabs** and **SNAREs** that carry out each transport step in [7.3.8](/7.3.8); traffic to lysosomes in [7.3.11](/7.3.11); and the endocytic routes that return membrane from the surface in [7.3.10](/7.3.10).

## 9.1. Structure of the Golgi Apparatus

### 9.1.1. A Polarized Stack

**Definition 9.1.1 (Golgi Apparatus)** The **Golgi apparatus** (Golgi complex) is an organelle formed by one or more stacks of flattened, membrane-enclosed cisternae, typically four to eight per stack, each about 0.5–1 µm across. A stack is polarized: its cis face receives material from the ER, its trans face sends material to the plasma membrane, secretory granules and endosomes, and the cisternae between them (cis, medial and trans) contain different sets of enzymes.

**Definition 9.1.2** The **cis Golgi network** (CGN) is the network of fused tubules and vesicular-tubular clusters on the cis face of the Golgi stack, where cargo arriving from the ER is received and escaped ER proteins are sorted for return. The **trans Golgi network** (TGN) is the network of tubules and vesicles on the trans face, where cargo leaving the Golgi is sorted into carriers for different destinations. Between the ER and the CGN of animal cells lies the ER–Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC), clusters of vesicles and tubules formed by the fusion of COPII vesicles, from which COPI vesicles return material to the ER.

Camillo Golgi described the **organelle** in 1898 as an "internal reticular apparatus" in nerve cells of the barn owl, stained by his silver impregnation method (the "black reaction"). Because it could not be seen in living cells and stained inconsistently, its reality was disputed for half a century, until electron microscopy in the 1950s showed the stacks in all eukaryotic cells. In sections of many cells the stack is curved, with a convex cis face towards the ER and nucleus and a concave trans face towards the plasma membrane, but the curvature varies and is not a reliable sign of polarity; the enzyme distribution is (9.1.2).

In animal cells the stacks are linked laterally into a single ribbon next to the **centrosome**, held by golgin tethers and GRASP proteins; the ribbon is unlinked and the stacks vesiculate during **mitosis**, so that each daughter cell receives part of the Golgi. Plant cells and many algae and fungi have hundreds of separate stacks (dictyosomes) that move along **actin filaments** with the ER exit sites they are attached to; budding yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* has isolated cisternae that are not stacked at all, which made it possible to watch single cisternae change their identity (9.3.2). The Golgi is particularly large in cells that secrete much, such as plasma cells, goblet cells and pancreatic acinar cells. In plants it also makes the hemicelluloses and pectins of the **cell wall**, so a growing plant cell exports more carbohydrate than protein through its Golgi. **Cellulose** itself is not made in the Golgi: it is spun at the plasma membrane by rosette-shaped **cellulose synthase** complexes, which are assembled in the Golgi and delivered to the surface in vesicles. During plant **cytokinesis**, Golgi-derived vesicles carrying wall polysaccharides fuse at the equator to build the **cell plate** ([7.3.21](/7.3.21)).

### 9.1.2. Biochemical Compartments

Cytochemical staining in the electron microscope first showed that the cisternae differ. Reduced osmium tetroxide reacts with the cis cisternae; the enzymes mannosidase II and NADPase are found in the medial cisternae; nucleoside diphosphatase and thiamine pyrophosphatase in one or two trans cisternae; and an acid phosphatase (CMPase) in the trans-most tubules of the TGN, the region once called GERL. Immunoelectron microscopy later localized the **glycosylation** enzymes in the same order in which they act (9.2.1). The distribution is a gradient rather than sharp boundaries, and it differs between cell types, but the order is conserved: enzymes that act early in a pathway are found earlier in the stack. In **cell fractionation** the Golgi as a whole is followed by the activity of glycosyltransferases, classically galactosyltransferase, a marker of the trans cisternae and TGN.

The membranes change gradually as well. In thickness and composition the Golgi membranes are intermediate between those of the ER and the plasma membrane: membrane made in the ER is converted on its way through the stack ("membrane differentiation"), which is how it can be added to the plasma membrane by exocytosis. **Cholesterol** and sphingolipids increase from cis to trans, so that the bilayer becomes thicker and more ordered towards the plasma membrane; the lumen becomes more acidic, from about pH 6.7 in the cis Golgi to about 6.0 in the TGN, through the action of V-type ATPases; and the phosphoinositide PI(4)P is concentrated in the trans Golgi and TGN ([7.3.8](/7.3.8)). These gradients are used for sorting: the pH gradient drives the **KDEL receptor** cycle and the binding of **mannose 6-phosphate receptors**, and the thickness gradient helps to keep resident membrane proteins in place (9.2.4).

## 9.2. Processing of Glycans

### 9.2.1. The Order of N-Glycan Processing

**N-linked glycans** arrive in the Golgi in the high-mannose form, mostly $\mathrm{Man_8GlcNAc_2}$, after trimming in the ER ([7.3.7](/7.3.7)). In the Golgi they are trimmed further and rebuilt into complex glycans in a strict order, because each enzyme requires the product of the previous one as its substrate (figure):

1. cis Golgi: Golgi mannosidase I removes the remaining α1-2-linked mannoses to give $\mathrm{Man_5GlcNAc_2}$. Lysosomal hydrolases are diverted here: GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase recognizes a patch of lysines on their surface and begins the mannose 6-phosphate tag that sorts them to lysosomes ([7.3.11](/7.3.11)).
2. medial Golgi: GlcNAc transferase I (GnT-I) adds a GlcNAc to the α1-3 arm; only then can mannosidase II remove the two outer mannoses of the α1-6 arm; GnT-II then adds a GlcNAc to the α1-6 arm. Fucosyltransferase 8 adds core fucose.
3. trans Golgi and TGN: galactosyltransferases add Gal, sialyltransferases add **sialic acid**, and other enzymes add fucose, further GlcNAc branches (which make tri- and tetra-antennary glycans) or sulfate.

Glycans that are buried in the folded protein, and therefore not reached by mannosidase I and GnT-I, remain high-mannose; glycans in which only one arm has been processed are hybrid. A single **glycoprotein** therefore carries a mixture of glycan classes, set by the accessibility of each site.

Endoglycosidase H cleaves the glycan until mannosidase II has acted (red bar); resistance to Endo H (green bar) therefore marks arrival in the medial Golgi.](./plots/n-glycan-processing.py)

The order of processing gives a simple biochemical clock for the progress of a protein along the pathway. Endoglycosidase H (Endo H) cleaves between the two core GlcNAcs of high-mannose and hybrid glycans but not of glycans that mannosidase II has trimmed. A protein labeled in a pulse and analyzed by **Western blot** or immunoprecipitation after Endo H digestion shifts to a faster-migrating form while it is in the ER and cis Golgi and becomes resistant once it has reached the medial Golgi; the half-time of acquisition of Endo H resistance is the standard measure of ER-to-Golgi transport, and its failure the standard sign that a mutant protein is retained in the ER. Peptide-N-glycosidase F (PNGase F), which removes all N-glycans, serves as the control.

Mutant **cell lines** in which a single step is missing show that the steps are not essential for the survival of cells in culture: Chinese hamster ovary cells lacking GnT-I (the Lec1 mutant) make only high-mannose glycans and grow normally, a fact Rothman used to build his cell-free transport assay (9.6.3). In animals the same steps are essential: mice lacking GnT-I die in mid-gestation, and human congenital disorders of glycosylation affecting Golgi processing cause multisystem disease.

### 9.2.2. O-Glycosylation and Proteoglycans

Mucin-type **O-linked glycans** begin in the cis Golgi, where a family of about 20 **polypeptide** GalNAc transferases adds GalNAc to serine and threonine; the cores are then extended by galactose, GlcNAc, fucose and **sialic acid** in later cisternae ([7.1.10](/7.1.10) shows the core structures). There is no sequon: O-glycosylation favors Ser- and Thr-rich, Pro-containing regions that are exposed in the folded protein, and mucins carry hundreds of O-glycans along extended stalks. Other kinds of O-glycosylation start in the ER, among them O-mannose on α-dystroglycan (whose defects cause muscular dystrophies), O-fucose and O-glucose on the EGF repeats of Notch ([7.3.19](/7.3.19)), and the Gal–Glc disaccharides on the hydroxylysines of **collagen**. The statement that O-glycosylation "takes place in the Golgi" is therefore true of mucin-type glycans but not of all O-linked sugars.

| | **N-linked glycans** | Mucin-type **O-linked glycans** |
|---|---|---|
| Where | transferred in the ER, cotranslationally; trimmed in the ER and rebuilt in the Golgi | built entirely in the Golgi (other O-glycan types start in the ER) |
| How | a preassembled $\mathrm{Glc_3Man_9GlcNAc_2}$ is transferred en bloc from **dolichol** ([7.3.7](/7.3.7)), then trimmed and extended | one sugar at a time from nucleotide sugars, without a lipid carrier |
| Acceptor | amide N of Asn in Asn–X–Ser/Thr (X ≠ Pro) | OH of Ser or Thr; no sequon (in other O-glycans also hydroxylysine of collagen and hydroxyproline of plant extensins) |
| First sugar | GlcNAc | GalNAc (Xyl in proteoglycans, Man, Fuc or Glc in other types) |
| Size | at least the pentasaccharide core $\mathrm{Man_3GlcNAc_2}$, typically 10–20 sugars | often 1–4 sugars on eight core types; longer in mucins and blood-group **antigens**, and the glycosaminoglycan chains of proteoglycans have hundreds |
| Detected by | Endo H, PNGase F (9.2.1) | O-glycanases, **lectins**; no general enzyme for complete removal |

The ABO blood-group antigens are terminal structures that can sit on N-glycans, O-glycans and **glycolipids** alike: the A and B transferases of the Golgi add GalNAc or Gal to the same H antigen. The O-GlcNAc that the enzyme O-GlcNAc transferase attaches to many cytosolic and nuclear proteins is a different modification: a single, reversible sugar added outside the secretory pathway, more like phosphorylation than like glycan synthesis ([7.1.10](/7.1.10)).

**Proteoglycans** are assembled in the Golgi as well. A xylose is attached to a serine of the core protein, followed by Gal–Gal–GlcA, the common tetrasaccharide linker; glycosyltransferases then polymerize the repeating disaccharides of the **glycosaminoglycan** chain (chondroitin, dermatan or heparan sulfate), and sulfotransferases add sulfate from the activated donor PAPS (3′-phosphoadenosine 5′-phosphosulfate), which is imported from the cytosol. Sulfation patterns, rather than chain length, determine which **growth factors** a heparan sulfate binds. Tyrosine sulfation of secreted and membrane proteins, for example of chemokine receptors and of the platelet receptor for von Willebrand factor, also takes place in the TGN.

### 9.2.3. What Glycans Are For

The functions of **glycosylation** follow from the physical and informational properties of glycans ([7.1.10](/7.1.10)):

- Folding and quality control: the glucose and mannose residues of N-glycans are read by the lectin **chaperones** and the **ERAD** machinery of the ER ([7.3.7](/7.3.7)); lectin cargo receptors such as ERGIC-53 help glycoproteins out of the ER.
- Protection and physical properties: glycans shield the polypeptide from proteases and from antibodies (the dense glycan shield of the HIV envelope protein hides most of its surface), keep proteins soluble, and give mucins their water-binding, gel-forming properties.
- Targeting: mannose 6-phosphate sorts lysosomal enzymes; glycans and **GPI anchors** contribute to apical sorting in epithelia (9.5).
- Signaling and recognition: the Fringe glycosyltransferase changes the O-fucose glycans of Notch in the Golgi and thereby which **ligand** Notch responds to; the **selectins** of leukocyte adhesion bind sialyl Lewis x; terminal sialic acids keep plasma glycoproteins in the circulation.

Unlike the synthesis of DNA, RNA and protein, glycan synthesis has no template. The structure of a glycan is the outcome of a set of enzymes acting in sequence on a substrate in a compartment, in competition with each other, and depends on the expression and location of the enzymes, the supply of nucleotide sugars and the time the cargo spends in each cisterna. The Golgi is the assembly line that makes this outcome reproducible.

### 9.2.4. Keeping the Enzymes in Place

The glycosyltransferases of the Golgi are type II membrane proteins with a short cytosolic tail, a single transmembrane domain and a lumenal stem and catalytic domain. Their nucleotide-sugar donors are made in the cytosol (or the nucleus, for CMP-sialic acid) and imported by **antiporters** that exchange them for the nucleoside monophosphate released by the reaction. How do the enzymes stay in their cisternae while cargo moves through? Several mechanisms contribute:

- Transmembrane domain length: Golgi enzymes have shorter transmembrane domains (about 15 residues) than plasma-membrane proteins (about 20), which fit the thinner, cholesterol-poor membranes of the early Golgi and partition out of the thicker membranes of the late Golgi and transport carriers. Lengthening the transmembrane domain of a Golgi enzyme sends it to the plasma membrane.
- Oligomerization: enzymes that act in sequence form complexes with each other ("kin recognition"), which may be too large to enter transport carriers.
- Retrieval: cytosolic tails of many glycosyltransferases bind the COPI adaptor GOLPH3 or coatomer directly, so the enzymes are continuously returned to earlier cisternae, the mechanism required by cisternal maturation (9.3).

## 9.3. How Cargo Moves Through the Stack

### 9.3.1. Two Models

**Definition 9.3.1 (Vesicular Transport Model)** The **vesicular transport model** holds that Golgi cisternae are stable compartments, each with a fixed set of resident enzymes, and that cargo moves forward from one cisterna to the next in transport vesicles that bud from one and fuse with the next.

**Definition 9.3.2 (Cisternal Maturation Model)** The **cisternal maturation model** holds that new cisternae form at the cis face from ER-derived carriers and progressively mature into medial and trans cisternae and finally the TGN, carrying their cargo with them, while resident Golgi enzymes are retrieved backwards from older to younger cisternae by COPI vesicles.

The two models make the same predictions about the order of glycan processing but different predictions about everything else. In the vesicular model, anterograde COPI vesicles carry cargo and the enzymes stay put; in the maturation model, COPI vesicles carry enzymes backwards and cargo never leaves its cisterna. Large cargo distinguishes them: procollagen I forms rigid aggregates about 300 nm long, far larger than a 70 nm COPI vesicle, yet it traverses the Golgi of fibroblasts and remains inside the cisternae throughout (Bonfanti and colleagues, 1998), and the scales of some algae, up to several micrometers across, are assembled inside Golgi cisternae and carried to the cell surface by them. COPI vesicles isolated from cells are enriched in Golgi enzymes and depleted of anterograde cargo, as maturation requires.

### 9.3.2. Watching Cisternae Mature

The direct test came from budding yeast, whose cisternae are dispersed and can be followed individually by fluorescence microscopy. Two groups (Losev, Glick and colleagues, and Matsuura-Tokita, Nakano and colleagues, both 2006) labeled an early Golgi protein and a late Golgi protein with different fluorescent proteins and saw single cisternae lose the early marker and acquire the late one over one to two minutes: the cisternae themselves change identity, as maturation predicts. In this view the Golgi is a series of Rab conversions ([7.3.8](/7.3.8)) applied to a whole compartment, with Rab1 (Ypt1) at the cis face giving way to Rab6 and to the TGN Rabs.

In mammalian cells the picture is less clear-cut. Patterson, Lippincott-Schwartz and colleagues (2008) measured the export of a fluorescent cargo from the Golgi after a synchronous pulse and found that it leaves with first-order, exponential kinetics, without the lag expected if cargo had to wait for its cisterna to mature, and that cargo of different sizes leaves at different rates. They proposed a rapid-partitioning model in which cargo moves quickly among cisternae through tubular connections and leaves from any of them, with export domains defined by lipid phases. Current views combine elements: cisternal progression carries large cargo, vesicular and tubular connections allow small cargo to bypass it, and the relative weights differ between organisms and cell types.

### 9.3.3. A Kinetic Comparison

The kinetic argument can be made quantitative. Suppose a pulse of cargo enters the cis face at $t = 0$ and the mean transit time is $\bar T$. The distribution of exit times depends on how many independent random steps the transit consists of:

- If cargo must pass $n$ sequential stages, each a first-order (memoryless) event with rate $n/\bar T$, the exit time follows a gamma (Erlang) distribution with density
  $$
  f_n(t) = \frac{(n/\bar T)^n\, t^{n-1}\,\mathrm{e}^{-nt/\bar T}}{(n-1)!},
  \tag{9.1}
  $$
  with mean $\bar T$ and coefficient of variation $1/\sqrt{n}$; it starts at zero and has a lag. Stepwise vesicular transport through four stable cisternae corresponds to $n = 4$.
- Cisternal maturation is close to deterministic, because a cisterna advances through many small changes of composition: a large $n$, a sharp peak at $\bar T$ and a long lag.
- Rapid partitioning, in which cargo mixes among all cisternae and leaves from any of them with a constant probability per unit time, gives $n = 1$, the **exponential distribution**: no lag, with the largest rate of exit at $t = 0$.

The fraction of the pulse remaining in the Golgi is the survival function $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \frac{(nt/\bar T)^k}{k!}\mathrm{e}^{-nt/\bar T}$, which on a logarithmic scale is a straight line only for $n = 1$. A measured export curve that is a straight line from the start therefore rules out a transit made of a few well-defined sequential steps for that cargo, which is the argument of Patterson and colleagues, although it does not exclude maturation for other cargoes; conversely, a lag before export argues for sequential steps.

## 9.4. Sorting at the Trans Golgi Network and Secretion

### 9.4.1. Three Exits

At the TGN cargo is separated into at least three streams. Lysosomal hydrolases bound to mannose 6-phosphate receptors are packaged with clathrin, AP1 and GGA adaptors into carriers for endosomes ([7.3.11](/7.3.11)). Proteins destined for secretory granules aggregate and bud in immature granules. Everything else, unless it carries a retention or sorting signal, is carried to the plasma membrane by default.

**Definition 9.4.1** **Constitutive secretion** is the continuous, unregulated delivery of newly made proteins and lipids from the TGN to the plasma membrane by carriers that fuse as soon as they arrive; it occurs in all cells and supplies the plasma membrane with membrane proteins and lipids and the extracellular matrix with its proteins. **Regulated secretion** is the storage of products in secretory granules and their release by exocytosis only in response to a signal, usually a rise in cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$; it occurs in specialized secretory cells such as endocrine, exocrine and nerve cells.

**Definition 9.4.2 (Secretory Granule)** A **secretory granule** (secretory vesicle of the regulated pathway; dense-core vesicle) is a membrane-enclosed organelle that buds from the TGN, concentrates secretory proteins (often to a dense, condensed core), matures by acidification and proteolytic processing of its content, and is stored in the cytoplasm until a signal triggers its fusion with the plasma membrane.

Constitutive secretion is the "default" route in the sense that a soluble protein with a signal sequence and no other signal is secreted constitutively: proteins such as albumin, antibodies from plasma cells and collagen from fibroblasts leave as they are made. In this case the secretion rate equals the synthesis rate, and a cell can change it only by changing gene expression or translation, with a delay of tens of minutes to hours. The regulated pathway makes a stored pool of product available within seconds to minutes, which is why it is used for hormones, digestive enzymes and neurotransmitters.

### 9.4.2. Making a Secretory Granule

Granule cargo is sorted largely by aggregation. In the TGN, the mildly acidic pH (about 6.0–6.3) and high $\ce{Ca^2+}$ cause regulated secretory proteins such as the granins (chromogranins and secretogranins) and prohormones to coaggregate; the aggregates exclude other proteins and become enclosed in budding membrane ("sorting by entry"). The immature granules then mature: V-type ATPases acidify them to pH 5.0–5.5, the content condenses into a dense core in which proteins can be concentrated up to 200-fold over their concentration in the TGN, and clathrin-coated buds remove membrane and missorted proteins such as lysosomal enzymes ("sorting by exit"), while immature granules may fuse with each other. Several mechanisms hold the content together: charge interactions of acidic granins with $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and with basic prohormones, intermolecular disulfide bonds, and in β cells the crystallization of insulin with $\ce{Zn^2+}$ as hexamers.

### 9.4.3. Proteolytic Processing

**Definition 9.4.3 (Proprotein Convertase)** A **proprotein convertase** is a member of a family of nine subtilisin-like serine proteases of the secretory pathway that activate precursor proteins by cleaving them after single or paired basic residues (Lys-Arg, Arg-Arg, or Arg-X-Lys/Arg-Arg). Furin, active in the TGN and endosomes, processes constitutively secreted and membrane proteins; PC1/3 and PC2, active in the acidic lumen of secretory granules, process prohormones and neuropeptides.

Many secreted proteins are made as inactive precursors and cut only late in the pathway: furin activates the receptor of insulin, many growth factors, collagen-modifying enzymes and, harmfully, viral envelope proteins (the furin site of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein favors its entry into lung cells) and bacterial toxins. Late cleavage keeps potentially active products inactive while they pass through compartments where they could act prematurely, and it allows small peptides, which would be too short to be targeted and translocated efficiently on their own, to be made as part of a larger precursor. Pancreatic acinar cells go one step further: their digestive enzymes are stored and secreted as inactive **zymogens** and activated only in the duodenum, starting with the cleavage of trypsinogen by enteropeptidase, so that the cell never contains active proteases in its granules.

**Example 9.4.1 (Proinsulin)** Preproinsulin (110 residues in humans) has a 24-residue signal peptide, removed in the ER, followed by the B chain (30 residues), a connecting segment (the C-peptide, 31 residues, flanked by Arg-Arg and Lys-Arg), and the A chain (21 residues). In the ER, proinsulin folds with three disulfide bonds, two between the B and A chains (B7–A7, B19–A20) and one within the A chain (A6–A11); the connecting peptide makes this folding efficient, because it holds the two chains in the right register (separated A and B chains reassociate poorly). In immature granules PC1/3 cuts at the B–C junction and PC2 at the C–A junction, and carboxypeptidase E removes the exposed basic residues. The mature granule contains equimolar **insulin** and C-peptide; since C-peptide is secreted with insulin but is not taken up by the liver, its level in blood is used clinically to measure a patient's own insulin secretion.

The same precursor can give different products in different cells. Pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) is cut by PC1/3 alone in the corticotropes of the anterior pituitary to give ACTH and β-lipotropin, and by PC1/3 together with PC2 in the intermediate lobe and hypothalamic neurons to give α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone and β-endorphin: one gene, several hormones, chosen by the set of convertases expressed. Proglucagon is a second example: PC2 in the α cells of the pancreatic islets releases glucagon, PC1/3 in the L cells of the intestine releases GLP-1 and GLP-2 instead. Other precursors are polyproteins with repeated copies of one peptide, so that a single translation event yields several active molecules: proenkephalin contains four copies of Met-enkephalin, two C-terminally extended forms of it and one Leu-enkephalin, and the yeast mating pheromone α-factor is cut from a precursor with four copies by Kex2, the yeast protease whose discovery led to the mammalian proprotein convertases. Constitutively secreted proteins are processed in the same way, for example proalbumin by furin in the TGN of hepatocytes.

### 9.4.4. Exocytosis

**Definition 9.4.4 (Exocytosis)** **Exocytosis** is the fusion of a transport vesicle or secretory granule with the plasma membrane, releasing its content into the extracellular space and adding its membrane to the plasma membrane. In constitutive exocytosis carriers fuse on arrival; in regulated exocytosis docked and primed granules fuse when a signal, usually a rise in cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$, triggers it.

Regulated granules move to the cell cortex on microtubules and actin, are docked by tethers and SNAREs, and are primed by Munc13 and Munc18 into a state in which partially zippered SNARE complexes are held by complexin and the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ sensor synaptotagmin ([7.3.8](/7.3.8)). A local rise in $\ce{Ca^2+}$, often to tens of micromolar near open channels, lets synaptotagmin bind the membrane and complete zippering within milliseconds in neurons, and within tens of milliseconds to seconds in endocrine cells. In the pancreatic β cell the chain from glucose to secretion runs through metabolism: glucose is taken up and metabolized, the ATP/ADP ratio rises, **ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels** close, the membrane depolarizes, voltage-gated $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels open, and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ triggers fusion (the triggering pathway); metabolic signals also increase the priming of new granules (the amplifying pathway).

The two pathways explain why insulin secretion after a step increase in glucose is biphasic: a first phase lasting several minutes and a slowly rising second phase. In a two-pool model, a readily releasable pool $R$ (at most $R_{\max}$ granules) is refilled from a large reserve and released at a rate that depends on $\ce{Ca^2+}$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_{\mathrm{r}}(R_{\max} - R) - k_{\mathrm{e}} R, \qquad \text{secretion rate} = k_{\mathrm{e}} R.
\tag{9.2}
$$

When glucose raises $k_{\mathrm{e}}$ suddenly, the pool that accumulated at rest is released in a burst that decays with rate constant $k_{\mathrm{e}} + k_{\mathrm{r}}$ (the first phase), and secretion then settles at $k_{\mathrm{e}}k_{\mathrm{r}}R_{\max}/(k_{\mathrm{e}} + k_{\mathrm{r}})$, which is close to $k_{\mathrm{r}}R_{\max}$ when release is fast: the second phase is limited by priming, and the amplifying pathway raises it (figure). In type 2 diabetes the first phase is lost early, an observation that points to the docking and priming steps. The β cell holds about 10 000 granules, of which only a small percentage are readily releasable.

Exocytosis adds membrane to the cell surface, and the addition can be measured. The plasma membrane has a specific capacitance of about $1\,\mathrm{\mu F\,cm^{-2}}$, so the fusion of a granule of diameter 300 nm (area $\pi d^2 \approx 0.28\,\mathrm{\mu m^2}$) increases the cell's **capacitance** by about $2.8\,\mathrm{fF}$; Neher and Marty (1982) resolved such single steps in mast cells with the patch clamp. Membrane added by exocytosis is retrieved by compensatory endocytosis ([7.3.10](/7.3.10)); in some cases the fusion pore opens only briefly and closes again ("kiss-and-run"), releasing small molecules but keeping the granule membrane intact.

## 9.5. Sorting in Polarized Epithelia

Epithelial cells have two plasma-membrane domains with different compositions: the apical surface, facing the lumen of an organ, and the basolateral surface, facing neighboring cells and the basal lamina. The tight junctions that seal the epithelium also prevent membrane proteins and outer-leaflet lipids from diffusing between the two domains ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)). Each domain must therefore receive its own proteins. In Madin–Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells infected with viruses, Rodriguez-Boulan and Sabatini (1978) saw influenza virus bud from the apical surface and vesicular stomatitis virus from the basolateral surface, because their envelope glycoproteins are sorted to different domains; these viral proteins became the standard markers for the two pathways.

| Domain | Sorting signals | Machinery |
|---|---|---|
| Basolateral | cytosolic tyrosine (YXXΦ), dileucine and other motifs | clathrin with the epithelial adaptor AP1B (with μ1B) in the TGN and recycling endosomes |
| Apical | GPI anchors; N- and O-glycans in the lumenal domain; transmembrane domains that partition into sphingolipid–cholesterol domains (**lipid rafts**) | lectins (galectins), raft clustering, Rab8 and Rab11 carriers |

Cells use these signals in two ways. MDCK cells sort most proteins directly in the TGN and recycling endosomes. Hepatocytes send almost all plasma-membrane proteins first to the basolateral (sinusoidal) surface and then move apical proteins to the bile canalicular surface by endocytosis and transcytosis ([7.3.10](/7.3.10)). The same signals are reused in neurons, where axonal and dendritic membranes differ in a similar way, and loss of epithelial polarity is one of the early changes of carcinomas ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

## 9.6. Classical Experiments

### 9.6.1. Following a Pulse: Palade

The route of secretory proteins was mapped by George Palade and colleagues in the pancreatic acinar cell, which secretes so much that most of its protein synthesis goes into the secretory pathway. Jamieson and Palade (1967) incubated slices of guinea-pig pancreas for 3 minutes with radioactive leucine (the pulse) and then with an excess of unlabeled leucine (the chase), fixed them at intervals, and located the label by electron-microscopic autoradiography ([7.3.3](/7.3.3)): at the end of the pulse it was over the rough ER, after about 7 minutes of chase over the Golgi, after about 37 minutes over condensing vacuoles at the trans face, and after about 2 hours over zymogen granules, from which it was released when secretion was stimulated. Combined with cell fractionation of the same tissue, these experiments established the sequence rough ER → Golgi → secretory granules → exocytosis, and they showed that secretory proteins never enter the cytosol on the way. Palade shared the 1974 Nobel Prize with Albert Claude and Christian de Duve.

### 9.6.2. Genetics: Schekman's sec Mutants

**Definition 9.6.1 (sec Mutant)** A **sec mutant** is a mutant of the yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, usually temperature sensitive, that is defective in protein secretion: at the restrictive temperature it stops secreting enzymes such as invertase and acid phosphatase and accumulates secretory proteins in an intracellular compartment determined by the step that is blocked.

Novick and Schekman (1979) reasoned that a yeast that continues to make proteins and lipids but cannot secrete them will become denser, and isolated such mutants by density-gradient centrifugation; Novick, Field and Schekman (1980) defined 23 complementation groups. At 37 °C each mutant accumulated one kind of membrane: expanded ER, Golgi-like stacks ("Berkeley bodies") or masses of 80–100 nm vesicles. Novick, Ferro and Schekman (1981) then combined mutations: a double mutant always accumulated the structure characteristic of the earlier block. This epistasis analysis ordered the genes along a pathway, ER → Golgi → vesicles → plasma membrane, the same order Palade had found by following labeled proteins in animal cells, which showed that the pathway is conserved across eukaryotes. Later screens added the genes for translocation into the ER (*sec61*, *sec62*, *sec63*).

Top: at the restrictive temperature each temperature-sensitive mutant stops secreting invertase and accumulates the compartment before its block: the ER for mutants blocked in ER-to-Golgi transport, Golgi-like Berkeley bodies for mutants blocked in Golgi exit, and 80–100 nm secretory vesicles for mutants blocked in fusion with the plasma membrane. Gene names are given with the molecular functions identified later: sec12 (the Sar1 GEF), sec13 and sec23 (COPII), sec16 (exit-site scaffold), sec17 (α-SNAP), sec18 (NSF), sec22 (a SNARE); sec7 (an Arf GEF) and sec14 (a phosphatidylinositol/phosphatidylcholine transfer protein); sec4 (a Rab), sec2 (its GEF), sec1 (an SM protein), sec6, sec8 and sec15 (exocyst subunits), sec9 (a SNARE). ### 9.6.3. Biochemistry: Rothman's Cell-Free System

Genetics identified the genes; biochemistry showed what their products do. Balch, Dunphy, Braell and Rothman (1984) mixed Golgi membranes from virus-infected mutant CHO cells lacking GnT-I (whose viral glycoprotein VSV-G could not receive GlcNAc) with Golgi membranes from uninfected wild-type cells, which had the enzyme, together with cytosol, ATP and radioactive UDP-GlcNAc. VSV-G became labeled only if it had been transported from the mutant to the wild-type membranes, so the incorporation of label measured transport. The assay needed cytosolic proteins and ATP and was abolished by N-ethylmaleimide; the factor whose re-addition restored it was purified as NSF, and then α-SNAP and the SNAP receptors, the SNAREs ([7.3.8](/7.3.8)). NSF turned out to be the product of *SEC18* and α-SNAP that of *SEC17*, so that the yeast genes and the mammalian proteins described the same machine. Rothman also isolated COPI-coated vesicles from the cell-free system with GTPγS, and Schekman reconstituted COPII budding from purified proteins. Thomas Südhof identified the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ sensor synaptotagmin and the roles of Munc18 and Munc13 at synapses, explaining how fusion can be both precise and triggered within a millisecond. Rothman, Schekman and Südhof shared the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discoveries of machinery regulating vesicle traffic".

## Summary

- The Golgi apparatus (Definition 9.1.1) is a polarized stack with a receiving cis Golgi network and a sorting trans Golgi network (Definition 9.1.2); its cisternae differ in enzymes (cytochemical markers: osmium cis; mannosidase II, NADPase medial; NDPase, TPPase trans; CMPase TGN; galactosyltransferase in fractionation), in lipid composition and thickness (intermediate between ER and plasma membrane), in pH (6.7 → 6.0) and in phosphoinositides.
- N-glycan processing is ordered because each enzyme needs the previous product: Man I (cis) → GnT-I → Man II → GnT-II, Fuc-T (medial) → Gal-T, Sia-T (trans); Endo H resistance marks the medial Golgi. Mucin-type O-glycans and proteoglycans are built in the Golgi, one sugar at a time on Ser/Thr starting with GalNAc, whereas N-glycans are transferred en bloc in the ER starting with GlcNAc on Asn; some O-glycans start in the ER. Glycosyltransferases are kept in place by short transmembrane domains, oligomerization and COPI retrieval.
- Transport through the stack: the vesicular transport model (Definition 9.3.1) and the cisternal maturation model (Definition 9.3.2); procollagen, algal scales and yeast imaging support maturation; exponential export of small cargo in mammalian cells supports rapid partitioning. The shape of the exit-time distribution, a gamma distribution with $n$ stages (Eq. (9.1)), distinguishes the models: lag for $n > 1$, none for $n = 1$.
- At the TGN cargo is sorted to endosomes (mannose 6-phosphate), to secretory granules (Definition 9.4.2) by aggregation at low pH and high $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and maturation, or to the plasma membrane by default (constitutive versus regulated secretion, Definition 9.4.1).
- Proprotein convertases (Definition 9.4.3) activate precursors late: furin in the TGN; PC1/3 and PC2 in granules (proinsulin, Example 9.4.1; POMC and proglucagon give different hormones in different cells; polyproteins such as proenkephalin give several copies of one peptide); zymogens are activated outside the cell.
- Exocytosis (Definition 9.4.4) is triggered by $\ce{Ca^2+}$ via synaptotagmin and SNAREs; the two-pool model (Eq. (9.2)) explains biphasic insulin secretion (release of the primed pool, then refilling-limited secretion); each granule adds about 3 fF of membrane capacitance.
- Polarized epithelia sort basolateral proteins by cytosolic signals and AP1B, apical proteins by GPI anchors, glycans and raft partitioning, directly or by transcytosis.
- Classical experiments: Palade's pulse–chase autoradiography (ER → Golgi → granules), Schekman's sec mutants (Definition 9.6.1) ordered by epistasis, Rothman's cell-free assay leading to NSF, SNAPs and SNAREs (2013 Nobel Prize, with Südhof).

## Questions

1. A pulse-labeled glycoprotein is immunoprecipitated at different chase times and digested with Endo H or PNGase F. At 0 min both enzymes shift it to the same faster band; at 30 min Endo H no longer shifts it, but PNGase F still does. What has happened, and how would the pattern look for a mutant retained in the ER?

   **Answer** At 0 min the protein carries high-mannose glycans in the ER, which both enzymes remove. By 30 min it has reached the medial Golgi, where mannosidase II has trimmed the glycans and they have been converted into complex glycans that Endo H cannot cleave; PNGase F removes all N-glycans and so still shifts the band. A mutant retained in the ER never acquires Endo H resistance; its Endo H-sensitive form persists and usually disappears over time as it is degraded by ERAD.

2. Why must GlcNAc transferase I act before mannosidase II, and what would the N-glycans of cells lacking GnT-I look like? How did Rothman use such cells?

   **Answer** Mannosidase II requires the GlcNAc added by GnT-I to the α1-3 arm before it can remove the mannoses of the α1-6 arm (its substrate is $\mathrm{GlcNAcMan_5GlcNAc_2}$). Without GnT-I, glycans stop at $\mathrm{Man_5GlcNAc_2}$: high-mannose, Endo H-sensitive, with no complex glycans. Rothman used Golgi membranes from such mutant cells infected with VSV as donors and wild-type Golgi as acceptors; VSV-G received radioactive GlcNAc only if it was transported to the acceptor Golgi, which measured transport in a cell-free system.

3. In the kinetic comparison of 9.3.3, cargo has a mean Golgi transit time of 20 min. For $n = 1$ and $n = 4$, what fraction of a pulse has left after 5 min?

   **Answer** For $n = 1$: $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-5/20} = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-0.25} \approx 0.22$. For $n = 4$ the rate per stage is $4/20 = 0.2\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, so $\lambda t = 1$ and the fraction remaining is $\mathrm{e}^{-1}(1 + 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{6}) \approx 0.981$; only about 2 % has left. Early export is the most sensitive discriminator between the models.

4. A mouse pituitary cell line expresses POMC and PC1/3 but not PC2. Which peptides does it secrete? What would happen to proinsulin processing in β cells of a mouse lacking PC2, and how would that change blood measurements?

   **Answer** With PC1/3 alone, POMC is cleaved into ACTH and β-lipotropin (as in corticotropes), but not into α-MSH and β-endorphin, which require PC2. In β cells lacking PC2, PC1/3 still cleaves the B–C junction, but the C–A junction is cut poorly, so the granules contain proinsulin and partially processed intermediates; blood proinsulin rises, mature insulin and C-peptide fall, and the mice have mild hyperglycemia (as observed in PC2 knockout mice, which also lack glucagon processing).

5. Use the two-pool model, Eq. (9.2), with $k_{\mathrm{r}} = 0.06\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, $k_{\mathrm{e}} = 0.3\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$ and $R_{\max} = 100$ after stimulation, and an initial pool of 91 granules. What are the peak secretion rate, the time constant of the first phase, and the steady second-phase rate? Which parameter would you change to model a loss of first-phase secretion?

   **Answer** Peak $= k_{\mathrm{e}}R(0) = 0.3 \times 91 \approx 27$ granules per minute. The pool relaxes with rate constant $k_{\mathrm{e}} + k_{\mathrm{r}} = 0.36\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, a time constant of about 2.8 min. The steady state is $R^* = k_{\mathrm{r}}R_{\max}/(k_{\mathrm{e}} + k_{\mathrm{r}}) \approx 16.7$, secretion $k_{\mathrm{e}}R^* = 5$ granules per minute. Loss of the first phase corresponds to a small initial pool, that is a low priming rate at rest (or a lower $R_{\max}$ of docking sites), so that little is stored before stimulation; impaired triggering (smaller $k_{\mathrm{e}}$) would instead flatten and prolong the response.

6. In an epistasis experiment, a double mutant of *sec18* (blocked between ER and Golgi) and *sec1* (blocked in vesicle fusion with the plasma membrane) accumulates ER, not vesicles. Explain the logic. What would you conclude if the double mutant accumulated both?

   **Answer** If the steps are sequential, cargo is stopped at the first block it encounters, so the double mutant shows the phenotype of the upstream block: ER accumulation places the *sec18* step before the *sec1* step. If both compartments accumulated, the two genes would act in parallel pathways (or the *sec18* block would be leaky, letting some cargo reach the later block), and the order could not be inferred. In fact NSF (Sec18) acts at every fusion step, but the earliest one, ER to Golgi, is limiting at the restrictive temperature.

7. Why is it advantageous for secretory cells to activate peptide hormones and digestive enzymes by proteolysis late in the pathway or outside the cell?

   **Answer** Late activation keeps active products away from compartments where they could do harm: active proteases would digest the cell's own proteins in the ER or Golgi (acute pancreatitis is in part premature activation of trypsinogen within the pancreas), and active hormones could bind receptors in the secretory pathway. Precursors can also carry folding information (the C-peptide of proinsulin) or encode several peptides in one gene (POMC), allowing small peptides to be targeted and translocated as part of a larger protein and different cell types to make different products from the same precursor.
