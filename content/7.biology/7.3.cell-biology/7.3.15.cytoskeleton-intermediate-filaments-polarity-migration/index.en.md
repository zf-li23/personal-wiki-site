The last of the three cytoskeleton chapters does two things. It completes the inventory of filaments with the one family that does not hydrolyze nucleotides and has no motors, the intermediate filaments, adds the septins and the cytoskeletons of bacteria, and asks what makes each filament mechanically suited to its job. Then it shows how the parts treated in [7.3.13](/7.3.13) and [7.3.14](/7.3.14) cooperate in two whole-cell behaviors, polarization and migration. Both are problems of self-organization: a cell that starts symmetric must decide on a front and a back, and the decision has to be made by a network of GTPases, lipids and filaments whose parts know only their local surroundings. The answers recur throughout cell biology: positive feedback with a limited pool, mutual antagonism, and the separation of fast global signals from slow local ones. The signaling networks behind them are taken up from the receptor side in [7.3.18](/7.3.18) and [7.3.19](/7.3.19), and cell junctions and the extracellular matrix, on which cells pull when they migrate, in [7.3.23](/7.3.23).

## 15.1. Intermediate Filaments

### 15.1.1. A Family of Ropes

**Definition 15.1.1 (Intermediate Filament)** An **intermediate filament** is a ropelike, nonpolar protein filament about $10\,\mathrm{nm}$ in diameter (in the smooth and developing muscle cells where they were first seen, intermediate between the thin actin and the thick myosin filaments, hence the name; it is also intermediate between actin filaments and microtubules) assembled from elongated proteins that share a central α-helical rod domain of about 310 residues with heptad repeats, flanked by variable nonhelical head and tail domains. Intermediate filaments bind no nucleotide and carry no motors; they give cells and tissues tensile strength.

Ten-nanometer filaments were first described in developing muscle (Ishikawa, Bischoff and Holtzer, 1968), and their biochemistry was opened up by their insolubility: when cells are extracted with nonionic detergent and high salt, actin filaments and microtubules dissolve and an intact network of intermediate filaments remains, from which the proteins can be purified. Intermediate filaments are found in animals; plants and fungi lack them, and among the animals the cytoplasmic forms are absent from insects, which have only lamins. Humans have about 70 genes in six types:

| Type | Proteins | Cells | Disease of mutation |
|---|---|---|---|
| I, II | acidic (type I) and basic (type II) keratins, obligatory heterodimers (for example K5/K14 in basal epidermis, K8/K18 in simple epithelia) | epithelia, hair, nails | epidermolysis bullosa simplex (K5, K14), other skin and hair disorders |
| III | vimentin (mesenchymal cells, fibroblasts, leukocytes), desmin (muscle), glial fibrillary acidic protein (astrocytes), peripherin | | desmin myopathies and cardiomyopathy; Alexander disease (GFAP) |
| IV | neurofilament proteins NF-L, NF-M, NF-H, α-internexin | neurons | some Charcot–Marie–Tooth neuropathies; aggregates in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis |
| V | nuclear lamins A, B, C | all nucleated animal cells | laminopathies, Hutchinson–Gilford progeria ([7.3.16](/7.3.16)) |
| VI | nestin, others | neural stem cells | |

**Definition 15.1.2 (Keratin, Vimentin and Neurofilament)** **Keratins** are the intermediate-filament proteins of epithelial cells (types I and II), which assemble only as heterodimers of one type I and one type II keratin and are anchored at desmosomes and hemidesmosomes. **Vimentin** is the type III intermediate-filament protein of mesenchymal cells and the first to be expressed in many cells in development and in culture. **Neurofilaments** are the intermediate filaments of neurons, made of the type IV proteins NF-L, NF-M and NF-H, whose long phosphorylated tails project from the filament and space neighboring filaments in axons.

Most cells express one or two types, and some of the proteins have older names: desmin, also called skeletin, links neighboring myofibrils at their Z discs and attaches them to the sarcolemma and the nucleus; glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) is made by astrocytes and by nonmyelinating Schwann cells; the three neurofilament proteins, of about 68, 160 and 200 kDa (apparent sizes in gels), are the "neurofilament triplet". The so-called β-keratins of reptile scales and bird feathers are not intermediate-filament proteins but unrelated β-sheet proteins (corneous β-proteins); hair, nails and wool are made of the α-keratins of Definition 15.1.2.

The family is used as a cell-type label: tumors that have lost most features of their tissue of origin often keep its intermediate-filament type, and antibodies against keratins, vimentin, desmin or GFAP help pathologists classify carcinomas, sarcomas, myosarcomas and gliomas. The density of neurofilaments sets the caliber of an axon, and with it the conduction velocity ([7.16](/7.16)).

### 15.1.2. Assembly Without Polarity

The rod domain is the key to assembly. It is divided by short nonhelical linkers into coil 1 (segments 1A and 1B) and coil 2 (segments 2A and 2B), and its two ends carry the most conserved sequences of the family, the helix initiation and termination motifs, which overlap in the end-to-end contacts of assembly. The heads and tails, by contrast, vary in length and sequence and give each type its specific interactions. The heptad repeats, with hydrophobic residues at the first and fourth positions, make two rods wind into a parallel **coiled coil**, the same structure that forms α-keratin and the tail of myosin II ([7.1.5](/7.1.5)). The steps, best known for vimentin, are:

1. two monomers form a parallel coiled-coil dimer about $45\,\mathrm{nm}$ long;
2. two dimers associate side by side, antiparallel and staggered, into a tetramer; because the two dimers point in opposite directions, both ends of the tetramer are the same;
3. eight tetramers associate laterally within seconds into a unit-length filament (ULF), about $60\,\mathrm{nm}$ long and $16\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide;
4. ULFs anneal end to end into long filaments, which compact radially within minutes to the mature $10\,\mathrm{nm}$ diameter, with about 32 polypeptides in cross-section.

Purified intermediate-filament proteins, kept soluble in urea or at low ionic strength, assemble spontaneously when salt is restored, without nucleotide, accessory proteins or a pronounced dependence on temperature, and almost all of the protein in a cell is polymerized. Since the tetramer, the soluble building block, is symmetric, the filament has no polarity, and nothing like treadmilling or dynamic instability is possible; no motor can walk on it in a definite direction. Intermediate filaments are nevertheless dynamic: subunits exchange along the length of the filament (seen by **fluorescence recovery after photobleaching**), filaments are transported along microtubules by kinesin and dynein, and phosphorylation of the head domains dissolves them. Nuclear lamins are disassembled by Cdk1 phosphorylation at the start of mitosis, vimentin is phosphorylated during cell division, and keratin networks are remodeled when epithelial cells migrate to close a wound.

### 15.1.3. Mechanical Function

Intermediate filaments are the least stiff of the three filaments at small deformations but the hardest to break. Their staggered coiled coils slide past one another and unfold to β-sheet structures under tension, so a single vimentin filament can be stretched to more than three times its length before it breaks, while actin filaments and microtubules break at strains of a few percent. Networks of intermediate filaments stiffen as they are deformed (15.3.3) and absorb mechanical energy that would otherwise tear the cell.

The clearest evidence is genetic. Epidermolysis bullosa simplex is a skin disease in which the basal cells of the epidermis rupture and the skin blisters after minor mechanical trauma.

**Definition 15.1.3 (Epidermolysis Bullosa Simplex)** **Epidermolysis bullosa simplex** is an inherited blistering disease caused by mutations, usually dominant, in keratin 5 or keratin 14, the keratin pair of the basal layer of the epidermis, which make the keratin network of the basal cells fragile, so that the cells break under shear.

The link was established by Fuchs and colleagues (1991): transgenic mice expressing a truncated K14, which acts as a dominant negative and disrupts the filament network, developed blistering skin like that of patients, and patients turned out to carry point mutations in K14 or K5, often in the short conserved sequences at the ends of the rod domain that mediate end-to-end assembly. Mutations in the keratins of the suprabasal epidermis, the cornea, hair and nails cause analogous diseases in those tissues, and desmin mutations cause muscle fiber fragility: intermediate filaments are the tissue's tension-bearing system.

### 15.1.4. Plakins and Links to the Nucleus

**Definition 15.1.4 (Plectin)** **Plectin** is a very large (about $500\,\mathrm{kDa}$) cytoskeletal linker protein of the plakin family that cross-links intermediate filaments to each other, to microtubules and actin filaments, and to hemidesmosomes and desmosomes.

Plectin belongs to the *intermediate filament–associated proteins* (IFAPs), the proteins that bundle intermediate filaments, cross-link them into networks and attach them to membranes and to the other filaments; most are specific for a cell type or a filament type. Filaggrin, for example, aggregates keratin filaments into the dense bundles of the cornified layer of the epidermis, and its loss-of-function mutations cause ichthyosis vulgaris and predispose to atopic dermatitis. Plakins connect the three filament systems into one mechanical network. Mutations in plectin cause a combination of epidermolysis bullosa with muscular dystrophy; desmoplakin, another plakin, attaches keratin filaments to desmosomes ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)). The cytoskeleton is also coupled to the nucleus. The LINC complex (linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton), formed by nesprins with KASH domains in the outer nuclear membrane and SUN proteins in the inner nuclear membrane, connects actin, microtubule motors and, through plectin, intermediate filaments in the cytoplasm to the nuclear lamina inside; it moves and anchors the nucleus and transmits mechanical force to chromatin ([7.3.16](/7.3.16)).

### 15.1.5. Septins

**Definition 15.1.5 (Septin)** A **septin** is a member of a family of GTP-binding proteins that form nonpolar hetero-oligomeric rods (hexamers and octamers in a fixed subunit order, for example Cdc11–Cdc12–Cdc3–Cdc10–Cdc10–Cdc3–Cdc12–Cdc11 in budding yeast), which polymerize end to end into filaments and rings on membranes, preferably on curved membranes.

Septins were found in yeast mutants that fail to complete cytokinesis (Hartwell, 1971), named for their role in septum formation. In budding yeast they form an hourglass and then a double ring at the bud neck that serves as a scaffold for the proteins of cytokinesis and as a diffusion barrier separating the membranes of mother and bud. In animal cells septins associate with actin bundles, the cleavage furrow, the base of cilia and the annulus of the sperm flagellum; they can sense micrometer-scale membrane curvature, which is larger than any single protein, by the cooperative binding of filaments. Their GTP is not used to drive dynamics as in tubulin; it seems to help assembly of the rods.

## 15.2. The Prokaryotic Cytoskeleton and Its Evolution

Bacteria were long thought to have no cytoskeleton, until FtsZ, the essential cell-division protein of *Escherichia coli*, was found to be a GTPase related to tubulin (1992), and MreB, required for the rod shape, to be related to actin (2001). Bacteria and archaea have relatives of all three filament types, and some filament systems with no eukaryotic counterpart:

| Protein | Eukaryotic relative | Function |
|---|---|---|
| FtsZ | tubulin | Z ring at the division site; treadmills around the ring and guides septal wall synthesis; recruits the divisome |
| MreB | actin | short membrane-bound filaments that move circumferentially with the wall-synthesis machinery; maintain rod shape |
| ParM | actin | plasmid segregation: antiparallel filaments with dynamic instability push plasmids to the poles |
| Crescentin | intermediate filaments (coiled-coil protein) | curved shape of *Caulobacter crescentus* |
| ParA, MinD | none (Walker ATPases) | chromosome and plasmid partition; Min oscillation that finds the cell middle |
| Asgard archaeal actins and profilins, tubulins in some archaea | actin, profilin, tubulin | possibly a cytoskeleton in the archaeal ancestors of eukaryotes |

**Definition 15.2.1 (FtsZ and MreB)** **FtsZ** is a bacterial tubulin homolog that polymerizes with GTP into protofilaments forming the Z ring at the future division site, the scaffold of the bacterial cell-division machinery. **MreB** is a bacterial actin homolog that forms short filaments on the inner face of the plasma membrane of rod-shaped bacteria, oriented around the cell circumference, which guide the insertion of new peptidoglycan and thereby maintain a rod shape.

The structural homology is clear but the sequence similarity is low, below $20\,\%$, which points to an ancient origin. The filaments are used differently: FtsZ forms single protofilaments, not tubes, and the bacterial "actins" form diverse filament architectures; ParM uses dynamic instability, an invention one might have thought unique to microtubules, for the same purpose, finding and pushing apart two objects. The polymerizing ATPase and GTPase folds thus predate eukaryotes, and the eukaryotic innovations were the elaboration of filament structure (the tube of 13 protofilaments, the two-stranded actin helix), the many accessory proteins, and above all the motors, for which no prokaryotic relatives exist. The Asgard archaea, the closest relatives of the archaeal host of the first eukaryotes ([7.3.1](/7.3.1)), encode actins and profilins that interact with eukaryotic actin, consistent with a dynamic actin cytoskeleton already in that host.

## 15.3. The Mechanics of the Cytoskeleton

### 15.3.1. Persistence Length

The three filaments differ most obviously in stiffness, and stiffness depends on length: a filament that is straight on the scale of a protein is floppy on the scale of a cell. A single number captures this.

**Definition 15.3.1 (Persistence Length)** The **persistence length** $\ell_{\mathrm{p}}$ of a polymer is the contour length over which the orientation of its axis becomes uncorrelated by thermal fluctuations; for a filament of flexural rigidity $\kappa$ (bending modulus times the second moment of area of the cross-section, $\kappa = EI$) at temperature $T$, $\ell_{\mathrm{p}} = \kappa/k_{\mathrm{B}}T$, and the tangent vectors $\boldsymbol{t}(s)$ at contour positions $0$ and $s$ satisfy $\langle \boldsymbol{t}(0)\cdot\boldsymbol{t}(s)\rangle = \mathrm{e}^{-s/\ell_{\mathrm{p}}}$ in three dimensions.

**Formula 15.3.1 (Worm-Like Chain)** In the worm-like chain model, a filament of contour length $L$ and persistence length $\ell_{\mathrm{p}}$ has a mean squared end-to-end distance in three dimensions

$$
\langle R^2\rangle = 2\ell_{\mathrm{p}}L\left[1 - \frac{\ell_{\mathrm{p}}}{L}\left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-L/\ell_{\mathrm{p}}}\right)\right],
\tag{15.1}
$$
which tends to $L^2$ (a rigid rod) for $L \ll \ell_{\mathrm{p}}$ and to $2\ell_{\mathrm{p}}L$ (a random coil) for $L \gg \ell_{\mathrm{p}}$. For a filament confined to a plane, $\ell_{\mathrm{p}}$ is replaced by $2\ell_{\mathrm{p}}$.

The persistence lengths were measured from the thermal shapes of fluorescent filaments (Gittes and colleagues, 1993): about $17\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ for actin filaments and about $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ for microtubules (in later measurements $1$–$8\,\mathrm{mm}$, depending on growth rate and length); for intermediate filaments it is about $0.5$–$1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and for double-stranded DNA $50\,\mathrm{nm}$ ([7.2.4](/7.2.4)). The ratio of microtubule to actin stiffness, about 300, follows mostly from geometry: for a tube, $I$ grows as the cube of the radius for a given wall thickness, and the microtubule is $3.5$ times wider. On the scale of a cell ($10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), microtubules are rigid rods, actin filaments are semiflexible, and intermediate filaments are flexible coils, which is why the first can bear compression and the last cannot.

B: tangent correlation along the contour from 500 simulated chains (points) against $\mathrm{e}^{-s/2\ell_{\mathrm{p}}}$, the two-dimensional form (lines). C: the mean squared end-to-end distance relative to $L^2$ against $L/\ell_{\mathrm{p}}$ (Formula 15.3.1 in two dimensions): rodlike below and coil-like above $L \approx \ell_{\mathrm{p}}$; the dots mark the three 10 µm filaments](./plots/filament-mechanics.py)

### 15.3.2. Compression and Buckling

A thin rod under compression along its axis buckles at the Euler force $F_{\mathrm{c}} = \pi^2\kappa/L^2$ (for free hinged ends). A $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ microtubule ($\kappa \approx 2\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2}$) buckles at about $2\,\mathrm{pN}$, the force of a single motor or of a growing end: microtubules pushing against the cortex are often seen to bend. In cells, however, microtubules can bear compressive forces a hundred times larger without buckling at long wavelengths, because the surrounding elastic cytoskeleton supports them laterally and confines buckling to short wavelengths (Brangwynne and colleagues, 2006). For actin the limit is severe (a free filament longer than about $0.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ buckles below its polymerization stall force; [7.3.13](/7.3.13#sec-13.4.2)), and the cell builds pushing structures from short branched filaments or from bundles.

### 15.3.3. Networks

The mechanics of the cell is that of networks rather than single filaments. Networks of semiflexible filaments, cross-linked by filamin or α-actinin, have unusual properties that matter physiologically:

- they are viscoelastic: elastic on short time scales and fluid on long ones, as cross-links unbind and filaments turn over; the cortex behaves as an active gel whose tension is generated by myosin;
- they stiffen under strain: their shear modulus rises steeply as they are deformed, because thermal undulations of the filaments are pulled out (Storm and colleagues, 2005), so that a cell is soft for small deformations and resists large ones; intermediate-filament networks stiffen most and tolerate the largest strains;
- motors make them active: myosin II contracts actin networks and prestresses them, which stiffens them in turn, and cells adjust this tension to the stiffness of their surroundings, which they sense through adhesions ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)).

Mechanical properties change with cell state. A cell rounding up in mitosis increases its cortical tension severalfold; a migrating cell softens at the front and contracts at the back. The rest of this chapter turns to how such spatial differences arise.

## 15.4. Cell Polarity

**Definition 15.4.1 (Cell Polarity)** **Cell polarity** is the asymmetric organization of a cell along an axis, with different molecular compositions, cytoskeletal structures and functions at the two ends or in different membrane domains: apical and basolateral in an epithelial cell, axon and dendrites in a neuron, front and rear in a migrating cell, anterior and posterior in an egg, the bud site in budding yeast.

Polarity is established by an external cue (a chemical gradient, a contact, sperm entry, a landmark left by the previous division) or spontaneously, and it is maintained by feedback. In nearly all eukaryotes it involves the small GTPase Cdc42.

### 15.4.1. Cdc42 and the Budding Yeast

**Definition 15.4.2 (Cdc42, Rac and Rho)** **Cdc42**, **Rac** and Rho are the best-known members of the Rho family of **small G proteins**, GTPases that switch between an active GTP-bound form at membranes and an inactive GDP-bound form, activated by GEFs, inactivated by GAPs and extracted from membranes into the cytosol by guanine nucleotide dissociation inhibitors (RhoGDIs). Cdc42 governs cell polarity and filopodia, Rac lamellipodia, and Rho contractile actomyosin structures; each acts through its own effectors on the nucleators and motors of [7.3.13](/7.3.13).

The cycle is that of every small G protein (**guanine nucleotide-exchange factor** and **GTPase-activating protein**, [7.1.14](/7.1.14)); the Rho-family network as a signaling system is taken up in [7.3.19](/7.3.19). Here we need its spatial logic.

A budding yeast cell grows only at one site, where the bud will form. In haploids the site is normally chosen next to the previous bud scar by landmark proteins and the GTPase Rsr1, but cells lacking the landmarks still polarize, at a random site: the system can break symmetry by itself. Cdc42-GTP at the site recruits the scaffold Bem1, which binds both the Cdc42 GEF Cdc24 and the PAK kinase Cla4 (an effector of Cdc42). The GEF activates neighboring Cdc42, which recruits more Bem1–GEF complexes: positive feedback. Once a cluster forms, it depletes the cytosolic pool of GEF and Cdc42 and wins over any competing cluster, so only one bud forms (competition between clusters has been visualized; Howell and colleagues, 2012). The cap activates the formin Bni1, which nucleates actin cables with their plus ends at the site, and myosin V carries secretory vesicles, mRNAs and organelles along the cables into the growing bud; septins assemble a ring at the bud neck.

Such systems are described by **reaction-diffusion equations**: the active GTPase is bound to the membrane and diffuses slowly, while the inactive form, extracted by RhoGDI, diffuses quickly through the cytosol.

**Formula 15.4.1 (Wave-Pinning Model)** Let $a(x,t)$ be the concentration of active, membrane-bound GTPase and $b(x,t)$ that of inactive, cytosolic GTPase, with diffusion coefficients $D_a \ll D_b$, in a cell with no-flux boundaries. With activation by positive feedback and first-order inactivation,

$$
\frac{\partial a}{\partial t} = D_a\frac{\partial^2 a}{\partial x^2} + f(a,b), \qquad \frac{\partial b}{\partial t} = D_b\frac{\partial^2 b}{\partial x^2} - f(a,b), \qquad
f(a,b) = b\left(k_0 + \frac{\gamma a^2}{K^2 + a^2}\right) - \delta a,
\tag{15.2}
$$
the total amount $\int (a + b)\,\mathrm{d}x$ is conserved. A sufficiently strong local stimulus launches a front of activation; as it spreads, the cytosolic pool $b$ is depleted until the front stops (is pinned), leaving one stable domain of high $a$ (Mori, Jilkine and Edelstein-Keshet, 2008).

Three features of the model are general. Polarization needs a threshold: small perturbations decay and the uniform state is stable, so that the cell does not polarize spontaneously in response to noise of small amplitude but does respond to a real cue (a landmark, a pheromone gradient). Mass conservation with a fast-diffusing inactive form makes the domains compete, which is why one site wins. And the polarized state is maintained as long as the feedback works, after the cue has disappeared: polarity has memory. Related models with Turing instabilities (**Turing pattern**) polarize from arbitrarily small noise; which class describes a given cell depends on its parameters, and yeast seems to use both, together with actin-based delivery of Cdc42 on vesicles.

### 15.4.2. PAR Proteins

**Definition 15.4.3 (PAR Proteins and the Par Complex)** The **PAR proteins** (from *partitioning defective*) are conserved polarity proteins first identified in genetic screens for mutants of *Caenorhabditis elegans* with a symmetric first division. The **Par complex** of PAR-3, PAR-6 and atypical protein kinase C (aPKC), with Cdc42-GTP, marks one cortical domain (anterior in the worm zygote, apical in epithelia); the kinase PAR-1 and its partners mark the complementary domain; the two domains exclude each other by mutual phosphorylation.

In the one-cell *C. elegans* embryo, the centrosome brought by the sperm marks the posterior. It locally inhibits Rho and actomyosin contractility in the nearby cortex; the contractile cortex then flows towards the anterior, carrying the anterior PAR proteins with it, and the posterior PAR proteins (PAR-1, PAR-2) bind the cleared posterior cortex. Once established, the two domains maintain each other's boundaries: aPKC phosphorylates PAR-1 and PAR-2 and displaces them from the cortex, PAR-1 phosphorylates PAR-3, and the boundary settles at a position set by the amounts of the proteins. The polarity then positions the spindle and segregates cytoplasmic determinants, so that the first division is unequal ([7.3.21](/7.3.21), [7.3.24](/7.3.24)). The same proteins polarize the neuroblasts of *Drosophila*, the axons of neurons and, as below, epithelial cells.

### 15.4.3. Epithelial Polarity

An epithelial cell has an apical domain facing the lumen or the outside and a basolateral domain facing neighboring cells and the basal lamina, separated by the tight junction. Genetic screens in *Drosophila* identified three modules: the Par complex and the Crumbs complex (Crumbs, Pals1/Stardust, PATJ), which promote apical identity and reinforce each other, and the Scribble module (Scribble, Discs large, Lethal giant larvae), which promotes basolateral identity; the apical and basolateral modules antagonize each other, as the anterior and posterior PAR proteins do in the worm. Cell junctions ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)) and the sorting of membrane traffic to the two domains ([7.3.9](/7.3.9), [7.3.10](/7.3.10)) follow from, and stabilize, this pattern; microtubules run from apical to basal with their minus ends at the apical cortex ([7.3.14](/7.3.14#sec-14.3.3)). Loss of polarity is linked to growth control: *scribble* and *lethal giant larvae* mutant tissue in flies overgrows into invasive tumors, partly through the Hippo pathway, and loss of epithelial polarity is a step in the progression of carcinomas ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

## 15.5. Cell Migration

### 15.5.1. The Migration Cycle

**Definition 15.5.1 (Cell Migration)** **Cell migration** is the active movement of a whole cell over or through a substratum or tissue. Crawling cells move by a cycle of four overlapping steps: protrusion of the leading edge by actin polymerization, adhesion of the protrusion to the substratum, contraction by actomyosin that moves the cell body forward, and release of adhesions with retraction at the rear.

Migrating cells include fibroblasts and keratinocytes closing a wound, neutrophils and macrophages chasing bacteria, lymphocytes and dendritic cells patrolling tissues, neural crest cells and the growth cones of axons in the embryo, and metastatic cancer cells. Their speeds span three orders of magnitude, from less than $1\,\mu\mathrm{m\,min^{-1}}$ for fibroblasts to $10$–$20\,\mu\mathrm{m\,min^{-1}}$ for neutrophils and fish keratocytes.

The four steps divide the cell into zones. At the front, a lamellipodium a few micrometers deep advances by the treadmilling of its branched actin network ([7.3.13](/7.3.13#sec-13.4.2)), and filopodia probe the environment. Under the lamellipodium, **integrins**, the transmembrane receptors that bind the extracellular matrix ([7.1.15](/7.1.15)), engage matrix proteins in small nascent adhesions; some of these grow under tension into larger focal adhesions, plaques of integrins, talin, vinculin, paxillin and focal adhesion kinase that anchor stress fibers (their structure is treated with cell–matrix junctions in [7.3.23](/7.3.23)). Behind the lamellipodium, in the lamella, myosin II contracts the network and pulls the cell body forward; at the rear, adhesions are disassembled, by calpain proteolysis, by microtubule-delivered factors and by tension itself, and the tail retracts.

### 15.5.2. Rac at the Front, Rho at the Rear

When constitutively active forms of the three GTPases were injected into quiescent fibroblasts, each produced a different actin structure (Ridley and Hall, 1992; Ridley and colleagues, 1992; Nobes and Hall, 1995): Rho produced stress fibers and focal adhesions, Rac a large lamellipodium and membrane ruffles around the whole cell, and Cdc42 filopodia. Their effectors explain this:

| GTPase | Main effectors | Effect on the cytoskeleton | Site in a migrating cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cdc42 | WASP/N-WASP (→ Arp2/3), formins (mDia2), PAK, Par6–aPKC | filopodia, polarity, orientation of the centrosome | front |
| Rac | WAVE complex (→ Arp2/3), PAK | lamellipodia, ruffles | front |
| Rho | mDia1 (formin), ROCK (Rho kinase) | stress fibers, focal adhesion maturation, contraction | rear and lamella |

ROCK raises myosin II activity in two ways, by phosphorylating the regulatory light chain and by inhibiting myosin light-chain phosphatase, and it activates LIM kinase, which inactivates cofilin and so stabilizes filaments ([7.3.13](/7.3.13#sec-13.5.4)). Rac and Rho inhibit each other: Rac through PAK, which inhibits myosin light-chain kinase, and Rho through ROCK, which activates GAPs for Rac; their GEFs and GAPs are localized by adhesions, lipids and microtubules. Mutual antagonism, as for the PAR proteins, makes the front and rear exclusive: a cell has Rac at the front and Rho at the rear.

### 15.5.3. Adhesion as a Clutch

Polymerization at the leading edge pushes in two directions: the membrane forward and the actin network backward (retrograde flow). Whether the edge advances depends on how firmly the network is held. In the molecular clutch model (Mitchison and Kirschner, 1988; Chan and Odde, 2008), adhesion complexes link the flowing network to integrins bound to the matrix; when the clutch is engaged, retrograde flow slows and polymerization advances the edge, and the force transmitted through the clutch pulls on the matrix. The relation between substrate stiffness, adhesion and speed is therefore biphasic: on very soft substrates the clutch slips, on very stiff ones adhesions load fast and fail; there is an optimal stiffness for traction and migration, which also explains why many cells migrate towards stiffer regions (durotaxis). Traction forces measured from the displacement of beads in deformable substrates are of the order of nanonewtons per square micrometer under focal adhesions.

### 15.5.4. Modes of Migration

| Mode | Cells | Protrusion | Adhesion | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mesenchymal | fibroblasts, many cancer cells | lamellipodia, filopodia (Rac) | strong, integrin-based focal adhesions; matrix proteolysis | $< 1\,\mu\mathrm{m\,min^{-1}}$ |
| Amoeboid | leukocytes, *Dictyostelium*, some tumor cells | thick pseudopodia, actin-rich | weak, nonspecific friction or transient adhesion | $10$–$20\,\mu\mathrm{m\,min^{-1}}$ |
| Blebbing | embryonic germ cells, cells in confinement | blebs: membrane detaches from the cortex and is pushed out by hydrostatic pressure from cortical contraction (Rho, myosin II) | low | fast |

Cells can switch between modes. In three-dimensional tissue, many cells move by amoeboid or blebbing modes that need little adhesion, squeezing through gaps; the nucleus, the largest and stiffest organelle, becomes the limiting factor, and dendritic cells choose the widest pores, sensing their size with the nucleus itself (Renkawitz and colleagues, 2019). Leukocytes in three-dimensional matrices migrate even when all their integrins are deleted (Lämmermann and colleagues, 2008), by pushing against the walls of the confining space. Invasive cancer cells switch from mesenchymal to amoeboid movement when matrix proteolysis is blocked, one reason why protease inhibitors failed in cancer therapy.

### 15.5.5. Chemotaxis

**Definition 15.5.2 (Chemotaxis)** **Chemotaxis** is the directed movement of a cell along a gradient of a soluble chemical, towards it (attractant) or away from it (repellent); a gradient of a bound, immobilized chemical guides cells by haptotaxis.

Neutrophils follow gradients of bacterial N-formylated peptides (fMLP) and of the chemokines and complement fragments released at sites of infection, and *Dictyostelium* amoebae follow cyclic AMP pulses from their neighbors to aggregate when starved. Both detect concentration differences of a few percent across their own length, about $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, over a wide range of mean concentrations, and both use **G protein–coupled receptors** distributed uniformly over the cell surface. The difference in receptor occupancy between front and back is small and noisy; the cell has to amplify it and ignore the mean. The pathway, in outline: receptors activate $G_{\mathrm{i}}$, whose βγ subunits activate Ras and **phosphoinositide 3-kinase** at the front; the product $\mathrm{PI(3,4,5)P_3}$ recruits Rac GEFs and drives protrusion, while the phosphatase PTEN, which removes it, is excluded from the front and concentrated at the sides and rear; the same receptors activate Rho through $G_{12/13}$ at the rear. Cells treated with latrunculin, which cannot move, still form a crescent of $\mathrm{PI(3,4,5)P_3}$ facing the source: gradient sensing is separable from motility.

The simplest explanation of how a uniform receptor array senses a relative gradient is local excitation, global inhibition.

**Formula 15.5.1 (Local Excitation, Global Inhibition)** At each point $\varphi$ of the cell perimeter, receptor occupancy $S(\varphi)$ drives a local activator $E$ and an inhibitor $I$ that diffuses so fast that it responds to the mean occupancy $\langle S\rangle$; together they set the activity $R$ of a membrane effector:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_E S - k_{-E}E, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_I\langle S\rangle - k_{-I}I, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_R E(1 - R) - k_{-R}IR .
\tag{15.3}
$$
At steady state $E \propto S$ and $I \propto \langle S\rangle$, so $R$ depends only on $S(\varphi)/\langle S\rangle$: a uniform change of concentration produces only a transient response (perfect adaptation), and a gradient produces a persistent front-to-back difference that depends on the relative, not the absolute, gradient (Levchenko and Iglesias, 2002).

The model makes the right qualitative predictions: cells adapt to uniform stimuli and respond to a sudden uniform increase only transiently, and their response to gradients is the same over a wide range of concentrations. It is the spatial version of the incoherent feed-forward motif that produces adaptation in time ([7.3.18](/7.3.18)). The response of LEGI is no steeper than the stimulus; amplification comes from the positive feedback downstream (PI3K, Rac and actin reinforce one another at the front, as Cdc42 does in yeast) and from the mutual inhibition of front and rear. *Dictyostelium* shows that the components of polarity, LEGI-type sensing, excitable waves of Ras and PI3K activity at the cortex, and Rac–Rho antagonism, can be combined in one cell; a polarized cell becomes more sensitive at its front and turns towards a gradient rather than repolarizing from scratch.

B: steady-state response around the cell perimeter in a 10 % gradient at three mean concentrations differing a hundredfold: the curves coincide, because the response depends only on the relative gradient; the response gradient is shallow, and downstream positive feedback amplifies it in cells](./plots/legi-chemotaxis.py)

### 15.5.6. The Whole Cell

Migration uses all three filament systems. Microtubules from the centrosome, which faces the front in most migrating cells (in front of the nucleus, positioned by Cdc42, Par6 and dynein), deliver membrane and Rac GEFs to the front, transport adhesion components and trigger the disassembly of focal adhesions when their plus ends reach them; cells treated with nocodazole still crawl, but erratically and without persistence. Vimentin filaments resist large deformations, support the nucleus as it is pushed through narrow spaces, and interact with focal adhesions; vimentin-deficient fibroblasts migrate and contract poorly. The nucleus itself is moved forward by actomyosin and dynein through the LINC complex. What looks like a single behavior is a cooperation of independent modules: a protrusion engine (actin and its nucleators), a clutch (adhesions), a contractile motor (myosin II), a compass (polarity proteins and the centrosome), and a mechanical chassis (intermediate filaments and the nucleus), coordinated by the spatial separation of Rac and Rho.

## Questions

1. A drug that blocks the phosphorylation of lamins is added to dividing cells. (a) What happens to the nuclear envelope at mitosis? (b) Why does no comparable drug against the nucleotide state of intermediate filaments exist? (c) How would a dominant mutation in the end of the rod domain of keratin 14 affect basal epidermal cells?

   **Answer** (a) Lamins are disassembled by Cdk1 phosphorylation at the onset of mitosis; without it the nuclear lamina persists and nuclear envelope breakdown is delayed or incomplete, which impairs spindle assembly and chromosome segregation. (b) Intermediate filaments bind no nucleotide: their assembly is controlled by phosphorylation of head and tail domains, not by NTP hydrolysis, so there is no nucleotide state to target. (c) The ends of the rod domain mediate end-to-end and lateral assembly; a mutant K14 still pairs with K5 and enters filaments but poisons further assembly (dominant negative), producing short, clumped filaments; basal cells lose their tensile network, rupture under shear, and the skin blisters: epidermolysis bullosa simplex.

2. Estimate the persistence lengths of actin filaments and microtubules from their structure. Treat both as homogeneous rods with Young's modulus $E \approx 2\,\mathrm{GPa}$: the actin filament as a solid cylinder of radius $3\,\mathrm{nm}$ ($I = \pi r^4/4$), the microtubule as a tube of outer radius $12.5\,\mathrm{nm}$ and inner radius $8.5\,\mathrm{nm}$ ($I = \pi(r_{\mathrm{o}}^4 - r_{\mathrm{i}}^4)/4$). Compare with the measured values.

   **Answer** Actin: $I = \pi(3\times10^{-9})^4/4 \approx 6.4\times10^{-35}\,\mathrm{m^4}$, $\kappa = EI \approx 1.3\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2}$, $\ell_{\mathrm{p}} = \kappa/k_{\mathrm{B}}T \approx 1.3\times10^{-25}/4.1\times10^{-21}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 30\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, close to the measured $17\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. Microtubule: $I = \pi(2.44\times10^{-32} - 5.2\times10^{-33})/4 \approx 1.5\times10^{-32}\,\mathrm{m^4}$, $\kappa \approx 3\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2}$, $\ell_{\mathrm{p}} \approx 7\,\mathrm{mm}$, close to the measured millimeters. The ratio, about 240, comes almost entirely from geometry; a protein material of similar modulus arranged as a wide tube is far stiffer, which is why microtubules can serve as struts and tracks across the cell.

3. In the wave-pinning model (Formula 15.4.1), explain (a) why the cytosolic form must diffuse much faster than the membrane form, (b) why the conservation of total GTPase limits the cell to one polar domain, (c) what would happen to polarization if the cell overexpressed the GTPase tenfold.

   **Answer** (a) The membrane form, diffusing slowly, keeps the active domain local; the fast cytosolic form spreads the depletion caused by the domain over the whole cell, so the domain draws inactive GTPase from everywhere. If both diffused fast, no domain would form; if both diffused slowly, depletion would be local and fronts would not stop. (b) Every domain consumes inactive GTPase from the common cytosolic pool; as domains grow the pool falls until fronts stop. Two domains compete for the same pool, and the larger one, which is more stable, absorbs the material of the smaller (slowly in this model, faster with additional feedback), so one domain wins. (c) With much more total GTPase, the pool is not depleted enough to pin the front: the active domain spreads over the whole cell (or the uniform active state becomes stable), and polarization is lost. Polarization works in a window of total amounts, which is why expression levels of polarity proteins are often tightly controlled.

4. A neutrophil is placed in a uniform concentration of fMLP that is suddenly raised tenfold, and later in a gradient of $5\,\%$ across the cell at two mean concentrations differing tenfold. Using Formula 15.5.1, predict the response of $\mathrm{PI(3,4,5)P_3}$ at the front in each case. What experiment would distinguish LEGI from a model in which the cell compares the concentration at its front with a memory of the concentration a moment ago (temporal sensing, as in bacteria)?

   **Answer** Uniform step: a transient rise everywhere followed by a return to baseline, since $E$ rises fast and $I$ catches up more slowly. Gradients: a persistent front-to-back difference, the same at both mean concentrations because $R$ depends on $S/\langle S\rangle$. To distinguish spatial from temporal sensing, immobilize the cell (latrunculin, so it cannot move up the gradient and experience temporal changes) and apply a static gradient: LEGI predicts a persistent crescent of $\mathrm{PI(3,4,5)P_3}$ facing the source, temporal sensing predicts none; this is what is observed in *Dictyostelium* and neutrophils. Bacteria, which are too small to measure a spatial gradient against noise, use temporal comparison instead ([7.11](/7.11)).

5. Explain why both too little and too much adhesion slow mesenchymal cell migration, using the molecular clutch. Why can leukocytes migrate in three-dimensional tissue without integrins but not on a flat, two-dimensional surface?

   **Answer** With too little adhesion the clutch slips: the actin network flows backward as fast as it polymerizes, the edge does not advance and no traction is transmitted. With too much, adhesions at the rear cannot be released and the cell body is held back; adhesions also mature into large, stable plaques that resist turnover. Speed is maximal at intermediate adhesion (as found by Palecek and colleagues, 1997). In three dimensions, a cell confined between fibers or cells can transmit force by friction and by pushing against the walls (intercalating protrusions behind obstacles); actin flow coupled to the surroundings by nonspecific friction suffices. On a flat surface, there is nothing to push against laterally and only specific adhesion can transmit force, so integrins are required.

6. (Open) Compare the logic of polarization in budding yeast, the C. elegans zygote and a chemotaxing neutrophil: what breaks the symmetry, what amplifies it, what maintains it, and what prevents a second front?

   **Answer** Symmetry breaking: in yeast a landmark (bud scar, Rsr1) or noise above threshold; in the zygote the sperm centrosome, which locally lowers Rho activity; in the neutrophil the external gradient, read by LEGI. Amplification: positive feedback (Cdc42–Bem1–GEF; cortical flow that advects anterior PAR proteins; PI3K–Rac–actin loops at the neutrophil front). Maintenance: feedback that persists after the cue (wave pinning), mutual antagonism of two complementary domains (anterior vs posterior PAR; Rac vs Rho), and cytoskeletal delivery (actin cables carrying Cdc42 in yeast; microtubules carrying Rac GEFs in leukocytes). Preventing a second front: competition for a limited, fast-diffusing pool (Cdc42, GEFs; global inhibitor in LEGI) and long-range inhibition by the rear (Rho contractility suppresses protrusion elsewhere). The common design is local positive feedback combined with global negative feedback or resource limitation, the same principle that makes Turing patterns and that recurs in cell-fate decisions ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)).
