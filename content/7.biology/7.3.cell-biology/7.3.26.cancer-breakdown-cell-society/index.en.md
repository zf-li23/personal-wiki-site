The cells of an animal form a society. Each cell divides, differentiates, stays put and dies according to signals from its neighbors, and each gives up its own reproductive future for the good of the germ line. The preceding chapters described the controls that maintain this society: **mitogens** and the **restriction point**, **cell-cycle checkpoints** and **p53** ([7.3.22](/7.3.22)), adhesion and junctions ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)), stem cells and differentiation ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)), apoptosis and senescence ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)). Cancer is what happens when a cell escapes them: a lineage of cells that proliferates in defiance of these controls and invades territories reserved for other cells. Because the escape is heritable and the escaped cells compete with one another, cancer is a process of evolution by mutation and natural selection played out among the cells of one body. This chapter follows that view: what cancers are (26.1), how they evolve, with models of their age dependence and clonal dynamics (26.2), what causes them (26.3), which genes are involved and how they fit into the control pathways (26.4), the properties of cancer cells (26.5) and how they are treated (26.6). The molecular definitions of **oncogenes** and **tumor suppressor genes** are in [7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.6.2), DNA repair and its defects in [7.2.8](/7.2.8), the signaling pathways in [7.3.18](/7.3.18) and [7.3.19](/7.3.19); tumor immunology belongs to immunology ([7.15](/7.15)), and the statistics of survival to biostatistics ([7.7](/7.7)).

## 26.1. Basic Concepts

**Definition 26.1.1 (Tumor, Benign and Malignant)** A **tumor** (neoplasm, "new growth") is an abnormal mass of cells that proliferate inappropriately. A **benign tumor** remains localized and does not invade the surrounding tissue; a **malignant tumor**, or **cancer**, consists of cells that invade surrounding tissue and can spread to other sites. **Metastasis** is the spread of cancer cells from the primary tumor through blood or lymphatic vessels (or across body cavities) to form secondary tumors, also called metastases, at distant sites.

Invasiveness is the defining property of cancer cells, and metastases, not the primary tumor, cause about 90% of cancer deaths, by destroying the function of vital organs. Benign tumors can usually be cured by removing them, although some are dangerous because of where they grow (in the brain) or what they secrete (hormones from pituitary adenomas). Tumors are classified by the tissue and cell type of origin.

**Definition 26.1.2 (Carcinoma, Sarcoma, Leukemia and Lymphoma)** A **carcinoma** is a cancer arising from epithelial cells; a **sarcoma** arises from connective tissue or muscle cells; **leukemias** and **lymphomas** arise from hematopoietic cells, white blood cells and their precursors, leukemias circulating in the blood and bone marrow and lymphomas forming solid masses in lymphoid tissues.

Carcinomas account for about 85% of human cancers, for two reasons: most of the cell turnover of an adult occurs in epithelia (intestine, skin, airways, glands), and epithelia are the tissues most exposed to physical and chemical damage. Cancers derived from cells of the nervous system and sarcomas are much rarer in adults, although relatively more common in children. Parallel names distinguish benign and malignant forms (an adenoma is a benign epithelial tumor with glandular organization, the corresponding cancer an adenocarcinoma; a lipoma is a benign fat tumor, a liposarcoma a malignant one). Cancers retain features of their cell of origin: a basal cell carcinoma of the skin keeps making keratin, rarely metastasizes and is cured by surgery, whereas a melanoma, from pigment cells, often keeps making melanin and metastasizes early. Cancers of different cell types are therefore different diseases, and, increasingly, cancers of the same tissue are subdivided by the mutations they carry (26.4).

## 26.2. Cancer as a Microevolutionary Process

### 26.2.1. Clonal Origin

Most cancers descend from a single cell. Two classical lines of evidence:

- In women, one X chromosome is inactivated at random in each cell of the early embryo and the choice is inherited by its descendants (**X-chromosome inactivation**, [7.3.17](/7.3.17#sec-17.1.3)), so normal tissues are mosaics of patches. In women heterozygous for two electrophoretic variants of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, a normal tissue contains both isozymes, but the cells of a tumor (leiomyomas of the uterus, and most other tumors studied by Fialkow and colleagues from the 1960s) express only one: they descend from one cell.
- In chronic myeloid leukemia almost every leukemic cell carries the Philadelphia chromosome, the product of a reciprocal t(9;22) translocation; the exact breakpoint differs by hundreds of base pairs between patients but is identical in all leukemic cells of one patient, as expected if the cancer arose from a single accident in one cell.

Clonal origin does not mean that a tumor is homogeneous: by the time it is detected, typically at about $10^9$ cells (a mass of about 1 cm³ and 1 g), it has diversified into many subclones and contains a large population of noncancerous stromal cells (fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune cells) recruited by the cancer cells. Nor does it exclude that the founding clone expanded within a larger field of altered tissue: in the "field cancerization" of the mouth and airways of smokers and in the normal skin and esophagus of older people, sequencing has found many clones with cancer driver mutations that never become cancers (Martincorena et al. 2015, 2018).

### 26.2.2. One Mutation Is Not Enough

Cancer cells carry somatic **mutations** (mutations of body cells, not inherited through the germ line), and agents that cause mutations cause cancer: carcinogenesis is linked to mutagenesis (26.3.2). But a single mutation is not enough. A human body makes about $10^{16}$ cell divisions in a lifetime, and with a spontaneous rate of about $10^{-6}$ mutations per gene per division every gene would be mutated about $10^{10}$ times in a lifetime; if one mutation sufficed, cancer would be universal. Several lines of evidence show that several rare events are needed.

- Age. The incidence of most carcinomas rises steeply with age, roughly as a power of age of 4–6; colon cancer is about a thousand times more frequent at 80 than at 20.
- Delay. Cancers appear years or decades after exposure to a carcinogen: lung cancer incidence rose about 20 years after cigarette smoking increased in a population, and leukemias among the survivors of Hiroshima and Nagasaki peaked 5–10 years after the bombs, solid tumors later still.
- Pathology. Many cancers pass through recognizable precursor stages (hyperplasia, dysplasia, carcinoma in situ, invasive carcinoma), and premalignant lesions such as colonic polyps carry some, but not all, of the mutations of the carcinoma.
- Experiments. Expression of a single oncogene rarely transforms normal cells, but combinations do (26.4.3).

**Definition 26.2.1 (Two-Hit Hypothesis)** The **two-hit hypothesis** (Knudson 1971) states that a tumor caused by the loss of a tumor suppressor gene requires the inactivation of both alleles of the gene in the same cell; in hereditary cases one mutant allele is inherited and one somatic hit suffices, whereas in sporadic cases two somatic hits are needed.

Knudson derived it from the epidemiology of retinoblastoma, a childhood tumor of the retina that arises from retinoblasts in the first few years of life, before they stop dividing ([7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.6.2)). Children with the hereditary form often develop several tumors in both eyes and early; children with the sporadic form develop a single tumor in one eye and later. If $N$ retinoblasts are at risk and each allele is hit at rate $\mu$, the expected number of tumors by age $t$ is

$$
m_1(t) = N\mu t \quad\text{(hereditary)}, \qquad m_2(t) \approx N(\mu t)^2 \quad\text{(sporadic)},
\tag{26.1}
$$
and the probability of disease is $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-m}$ (tumors arise as a Poisson process, [3.1](/3.1)). With $N \approx 1.5 \times 10^5$ and $\mu \approx 4 \times 10^{-6}$ per year, a hereditary carrier develops on average three tumors by age five (nearly complete penetrance, often bilateral), whereas only about one child in 17 000 develops sporadic retinoblastoma. On a log–log plot of cumulative incidence against age the two forms have slopes 1 and 2, which is what Knudson found. The gene, *RB1*, was cloned in 1986 from the region of chromosome 13 deleted in some patients; its product, the **retinoblastoma protein** (Rb), is the gatekeeper of the restriction point ([7.3.22](/7.3.22#sec-22.2.2)).

**Formula 26.2.1 (Armitage–Doll Multistage Model)** If a cell must undergo $k$ rare, independent, rate-limiting changes with rates $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k$, and $N$ cells are at risk, the cancer incidence (the **hazard function**) at age $t$ is approximately

$$
h(t) \approx \frac{N\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_k}{(k - 1)!}\,t^{k - 1},
\tag{26.2}
$$
so that $\log h$ rises linearly with $\log t$ with slope $k - 1$.

Armitage and Doll (1954) observed slopes of about 4–6 for many carcinomas and concluded that about 5–7 steps are needed. The estimate is only indicative. Steps that increase the number of cells at risk change the curve: in the two-stage clonal expansion model (Moolgavkar and Knudson 1981), intermediate cells produced by a first mutation at rate $\nu$ per normal cell form clones that grow at net rate $g$, and each intermediate cell becomes malignant at rate $\mu$; then $h(t) \approx N\nu\mu(\mathrm{e}^{gt} - 1)/g$, which looks like a high-order power law over the ages of interest although only two mutations are involved. The steep age dependence of cancer thus reflects both the number of mutations and the clonal expansions between them, and the incidence of some cancers flattens or falls at the oldest ages, as both models predict once the hazard is no longer small or the population at risk is depleted.

### 26.2.3. Tumor Progression and Clonal Evolution

**Definition 26.2.2 (Tumor Progression and Clonal Evolution)** **Tumor progression** is the process by which a tumor becomes more aggressive over time, from a mild disorder of cell behavior to an invasive, metastatic cancer. **Clonal evolution** (Nowell 1976) is its mechanism: repeated rounds of heritable change (mutation or epigenetic alteration) in individual cells, followed by the selective expansion of the variants that proliferate or survive better in their environment.

The selective environment inside a tumor is harsh, with little oxygen and nutrients, an acidic interstitium, immune attack and, later, therapy, and it changes as the tumor grows. Each advantageous change produces a clone that expands, partly replacing its predecessors (a selective sweep), and the descendants of the winners become the substrate of the next round. The mutations that confer an advantage are called *driver mutations*; the many others, which arose in the same cells and hitchhiked with the drivers, are passenger mutations. Typical solid tumors of adults carry 2–8 driver mutations among thousands of passengers (Vogelstein et al. 2013).

![Clonal evolution of a tumor (simulation). A tumor grows from one cell to a carrying capacity of 10⁹ cells; each new cell acquires a further driver mutation with probability 2 × 10⁻⁹, and each driver multiplies fitness by 1.1. (A) Muller plot: the fraction of the tumor occupied by each clone over time, each subclone drawn inside its parent and colored by its number of drivers. Drivers arise only once the population is large; each new clone expands, coexists with others for a time and is overtaken by its own descendants, so the tumor is always a mixture of subclones. (B) Tumor size (solid, log scale) and the mean number of drivers per cell (dashed): growth stops at the carrying capacity, but evolution continues by successive sweeps, each taking roughly ln(N)/s ≈ 200 cell generations](./plots/clonal-evolution.py)

The simulation shows features that are seen in real tumors. Most of the evolution happens when the population is large, because the supply of mutations is proportional to the number of cells; the time for a clone with advantage $s$ to take over a population of $N$ cells is about $\ln N/s$ generations, so a sweep of a tumor of $10^9$ cells with $s = 0.1$ takes about 200 generations, years in a slowly dividing tissue. Subclones coexist, so that biopsies from different regions of one tumor (multiregion sequencing; Gerlinger et al. 2012) differ in their mutations: the shared mutations on the trunk of the phylogenetic tree are early events, those on the branches late. Branches can also separate in space, giving metastases that evolve independently. This intratumor heterogeneity is one of the main reasons why cancers are hard to cure (26.6.5).

Evolution need not be gradual. Genome sequencing has found catastrophic events that change many genes at once: chromothripsis, the shattering and haphazard rejoining of one or a few chromosomes (in about 2–3% of cancers, and more often in bone cancers), whole-genome doubling (in about a third of cancers), and the amplification of oncogenes on **extrachromosomal DNA** ([7.3.17](/7.3.17)), which segregates unequally at mitosis and lets copy number change quickly.

### 26.2.4. Genetic Instability

**Definition 26.2.3 (Genetic Instability)** **Genetic instability** is an increased rate of genetic change in a cell lineage: of point mutations and small insertions and deletions (as in microsatellite instability caused by defective **mismatch repair**), or of chromosome number and structure (chromosomal instability, caused by defects in chromosome segregation, telomere maintenance or the repair of double-strand breaks).

A normal mutation rate may not be enough to accumulate several driver mutations in one lineage within a lifetime, and most cancers have a mutator phenotype (Loeb). Chromosomal instability is the commoner form: most solid tumors show **aneuploidy**, with gains and losses of whole chromosomes and arms and with structural rearrangements, and loss of *APC* in colon adenomas, for example, increases spindle defects. Defects in checkpoints and in apoptosis make instability tolerable: a normal cell with a damaged genome arrests or dies through p53 ([7.3.22](/7.3.22#sec-22.3.2)), whereas a cell without p53 continues to divide and accumulates more changes. Instability is a double-edged sword for the tumor: too much is lethal, and the vulnerability it creates can be exploited by therapy (26.6.3).

### 26.2.5. Cancer Stem Cells and Plasticity

Many tissues are renewed by stem cells ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)), which are long-lived and so accumulate mutations, and the cells of origin of many cancers are stem or progenitor cells (the Lgr5⁺ crypt stem cells for intestinal adenomas in mice, Barker et al. 2009). In some cancers only a subset of cells can regenerate the tumor when transplanted: in human acute myeloid leukemia, only the rare CD34⁺CD38⁻ cells initiated leukemia in immunodeficient mice (Bonnet and Dick 1997). These cancer stem cells divide asymmetrically to produce more differentiated, less tumorigenic progeny, so that a therapy that kills most of the tumor but spares them would be followed by relapse.

How general this hierarchy is has been debated. In human melanoma, about a quarter of the cells could form tumors in more severely immunodeficient mice (Quintana et al. 2008), and in many carcinomas differentiated cancer cells can revert to stem-like states, so that "stemness" is a reversible state rather than a fixed cell type. This phenotypic plasticity, which includes the transitions between epithelial and mesenchymal states (26.5.4), was added to the hallmarks of cancer in 2022 (26.5.2).

## 26.3. Causes of Cancer

### 26.3.1. Hereditary Cancer Syndromes

Inherited mutations contribute to about 5–10% of cancers. Most cancer-predisposition genes are tumor suppressors, inherited as dominant traits at the level of the family although recessive at the level of the cell: the second hit occurs somatically (Definition 26.2.1). Tumors from carriers usually show loss of the remaining normal allele, often by mitotic recombination, deletion or chromosome loss, which makes the tumor cells homozygous for the region (*loss of heterozygosity*), a property used to map tumor suppressor genes.

| Syndrome | Gene | Function | Main cancers (lifetime risk) |
|---|---|---|---|
| hereditary retinoblastoma | *RB1* | restriction point | retinoblastoma (≈ 90%), osteosarcoma |
| Li–Fraumeni | *TP53* | DNA damage response, apoptosis | sarcoma, breast, brain, adrenal, leukemia |
| familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) | *APC* | Wnt destruction complex | hundreds to thousands of colonic polyps; colorectal cancer ≈ 100% by age 40–50 without surgery |
| Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer) | *MLH1*, *MSH2*, *MSH6*, *PMS2* | mismatch repair | colorectal (≈ 50%), endometrial |
| hereditary breast and ovarian cancer | *BRCA1*, *BRCA2* | homologous recombination | *BRCA1*: breast ≈ 70%, ovary ≈ 45%; *BRCA2*: breast ≈ 70%, ovary ≈ 17% (by age 80) |
| xeroderma pigmentosum | *XPA*–*XPG* and others | nucleotide-excision repair | skin cancers (≈ 1000-fold risk) |
| neurofibromatosis type 1 | *NF1* | Ras GAP | neurofibromas, gliomas |
| von Hippel–Lindau | *VHL* | ubiquitin ligase for HIF-α | kidney, hemangioblastoma |

The distinction between the genes is instructive. *RB1*, *APC* and *TP53* are gatekeepers, which directly limit proliferation or survival; *BRCA1/2*, the mismatch-repair genes and the *XP* genes are caretakers, whose loss raises the mutation rate of all genes (genetic instability) ([7.2.8](/7.2.8#sec-8.7.2)). That loss of a gene expressed in every cell causes cancer only in particular tissues (breast and ovary for *BRCA1*, colon for *APC*) is not fully understood; tissue-specific hormones, the cell of origin and the tolerance of the tissue for the loss all contribute.

### 26.3.2. Chemical Carcinogens and Radiation

**Definition 26.3.1 (Carcinogen, Tumor Initiator and Tumor Promoter)** A **carcinogen** is an agent that causes cancer. A **tumor initiator** is a carcinogen that causes a heritable, usually mutational, change in the cells it acts on; a **tumor promoter** is an agent that is not itself mutagenic but promotes the proliferation of initiated cells, so that it causes tumors only after initiation, and only if applied repeatedly.

The distinction comes from the mouse-skin experiments of Berenblum (1940s): a single low dose of a polycyclic hydrocarbon (now DMBA) produced no tumors, nor did repeated applications of croton oil (whose active component is the phorbol ester TPA, an activator of protein kinase C); but the hydrocarbon followed by repeated TPA, even a year later, gave many papillomas, whereas TPA before the hydrocarbon did not. Initiation is fast and irreversible (DMBA causes a specific *Hras* mutation), promotion slow and initially reversible, a clonal expansion of initiated cells.

Most chemical carcinogens are mutagens, either directly (alkylating agents) or after metabolic activation, mainly by **cytochrome P-450** enzymes of the liver and other tissues, which convert inert hydrocarbons into reactive electrophiles: benzo[a]pyrene from tobacco smoke and burnt food is oxidized to a diol epoxide that binds guanine, and aflatoxin B1 from the mold *Aspergillus flavus* on stored grain and peanuts is converted to an epoxide that causes a characteristic G→T mutation at codon 249 of *TP53* in liver cancers in regions where it is common. Because most carcinogens are mutagens, the Ames test ([7.2.8](/7.2.8#sec-8.7.1)), which measures reversion of histidine-requiring *Salmonella* in the presence of liver extract, detects most of them quickly. Radiation causes cancer by damaging DNA: ionizing radiation causes double-strand breaks, translocations and deletions; ultraviolet light causes pyrimidine dimers and C→T transitions at dipyrimidine sites, a signature found in skin cancers.

Epidemiology shows that environment and lifestyle account for most of the differences in cancer incidence between populations. The incidence of a given cancer differs by up to a hundredfold between countries, and migrants acquire the rates of their new country within one or two generations. Tobacco is the largest single preventable cause (about a fifth to a quarter of cancer deaths in high-income countries); others are infections (26.3.3), alcohol, obesity and physical inactivity, diet, ultraviolet light and occupational carcinogens (asbestos, benzene, aromatic amines). About 40% of cancers in high-income countries are attributable to such modifiable factors.

### 26.3.3. Tumor Viruses

**Definition 26.3.2 (Tumor Virus)** A **tumor virus** is a **virus** that can cause cancer in its natural host or transform cells in culture, either by carrying an oncogene or by altering the expression of host genes.

Rous (1911) transmitted a chicken sarcoma with a cell-free filtrate; the agent, Rous sarcoma virus, is a retrovirus whose genome carries the oncogene *v-src*, needed for transformation but not for replication. Bishop and Varmus (1976) showed that *v-src* was derived from a normal cellular gene, *c-src*, present in all vertebrates (Nobel Prize 1989): oncogenes are captured and mutated forms of normal genes, **proto-oncogenes**. Dozens of other acutely transforming retroviruses carry other captured oncogenes (*v-ras*, *v-myc*, *v-erbB*, *v-abl*), and slowly transforming retroviruses cause cancer by integrating next to a proto-oncogene (insertional mutagenesis, 26.4.1). DNA tumor viruses act differently: their oncoproteins are viral, not cellular, and they inactivate the host's tumor suppressors to force the cell into S phase, where the virus can replicate.

About 12–15% of human cancers worldwide are attributable to infections:

| Agent | Type | Cancer | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| human papillomavirus (HPV 16, 18 and others) | DNA virus | cervical, anogenital, oropharyngeal | E7 binds Rb; E6 with E6AP ubiquitinates p53 |
| hepatitis B and C viruses | DNA, RNA virus | liver | chronic inflammation and regeneration; HBV integration |
| Epstein–Barr virus | herpesvirus | Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Hodgkin lymphoma | latent proteins (LMP1 mimics CD40) |
| Kaposi's sarcoma herpesvirus (HHV-8) | herpesvirus | Kaposi's sarcoma (mostly in AIDS) | viral cyclin, GPCR |
| HTLV-1 | retrovirus | adult T-cell leukemia | Tax activates NF-κB and CREB |
| Merkel cell polyomavirus | DNA virus | Merkel cell carcinoma | large T antigen binds Rb |
| *Helicobacter pylori* | bacterium | gastric adenocarcinoma, MALT lymphoma | chronic inflammation, CagA |

HPV shows how viral oncoproteins attack both arms of the cell's defenses ([7.3.22](/7.3.22#sec-22.5.4)). The high-risk HPV genome is usually integrated in cervical cancer cells in a way that disrupts the viral repressor E2, so that E6 and E7 are overexpressed. E7 binds hypophosphorylated Rb and releases E2F, driving S-phase entry; this would normally activate p53 through ARF, but E6 recruits the ubiquitin ligase E6AP to p53 and targets it for degradation. Because HPV is necessary for nearly all cervical cancers, the disease is preventable by vaccination against the viral capsid protein L1 (virus-like particles; zur Hausen, Nobel Prize 2008): in Scotland, no cases of invasive cervical cancer have been found in women fully vaccinated at age 12–13, and population-wide reductions of about 90% have been reported in Sweden and England.

### 26.3.4. The "Bad Luck" Debate

Why are some tissues far more prone to cancer than others? Tomasetti and Vogelstein (2015) plotted the lifetime risk of cancer in 31 tissues against the estimated total number of divisions of the stem cells that maintain each tissue and found a strong correlation on log–log axes (correlation coefficient 0.81). They argued that about two-thirds of the variation in risk among tissues is explained by the number of stem cell divisions, that is, by random mutations arising during normal DNA replication ("bad luck"), and later estimated that about two-thirds of the mutations in cancers arise from replication errors (Tomasetti, Li and Vogelstein 2017).

The claim was widely criticized and widely misread. The correlation concerns differences between tissues, not the fraction of cancer cases that could be prevented: a tissue with many stem cell divisions can still have most of its cancers caused by environmental exposures, because extrinsic factors multiply the risk of every tissue. Wu et al. (2016) argued, from the variation of incidence between populations, from mutational signatures and from modeling, that extrinsic factors contribute to 70–90% of cancers. The two views are compatible with a simple multiplicative picture: the risk of a tissue is roughly proportional to the number of stem cell divisions times the mutations acquired per division, which environmental mutagens and inherited defects increase. Replication errors set an unavoidable baseline, which is why early detection matters as much as prevention, but they do not make cancer unpreventable.

## 26.4. Cancer-Critical Genes

### 26.4.1. Oncogenes and Tumor Suppressor Genes

The genes mutated in cancer fall into two classes ([7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.6.2)): **proto-oncogenes**, whose gain of function by mutation or overexpression (the **oncogene**) drives cancer and acts dominantly in the cell, and **tumor suppressor genes**, whose loss of function drives cancer and is recessive in the cell, requiring both copies to be lost (although for some, such as *PTEN* and *TP53*, partial loss or a dominant-negative mutant protein already helps the tumor; haploinsufficiency). A proto-oncogene becomes an oncogene in four main ways.

![Four ways to turn a proto-oncogene into an oncogene. (A) Point mutation changes the protein: a substitution at codon 12 of KRAS (Gly to Val, Asp or Cys) abolishes GTP hydrolysis and locks Ras in the active, GTP-bound state. (B) Gene amplification produces many copies of a normal gene, in tandem arrays within a chromosome or on circular extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA), and overexpression of the normal protein, such as the receptor tyrosine kinase HER2 encoded by ERBB2. (C) Chromosomal translocation joins two genes: the Philadelphia chromosome of chronic myeloid leukemia fuses the 5′ part of BCR on chromosome 22 to ABL1 from chromosome 9, encoding the constitutively active BCR–ABL1 tyrosine kinase; other translocations place an intact gene next to a strong enhancer. (D) Insertional mutagenesis: a retrovirus integrated next to MYC drives its overexpression from the promoter or enhancer in its long terminal repeat (LTR)](./plots/oncogene-activation.py)

Tumor suppressor genes are inactivated by point mutations (often nonsense or frameshift mutations, and missense mutations in the DNA-binding domain of *TP53*), deletions, loss of heterozygosity, and epigenetic silencing: hypermethylation of the **CpG island** of the promoter silences *CDKN2A* (p16), *MLH1* and *BRCA1* in many sporadic cancers ([7.2.15](/7.2.15)), in which genome-wide hypomethylation and altered chromatin regulators are also common. Mutations in chromatin regulators themselves (*ARID1A*, *KMT2D*, *EZH2*) and in metabolic enzymes that produce an **oncometabolite** (*IDH1* and *IDH2*, whose mutant enzymes make 2-hydroxyglutarate, which inhibits DNA and histone demethylases; [7.1.20](/7.1.20)) show that epigenetic changes can themselves drive cancer.

### 26.4.2. How the Genes Were Found

Different methods were needed for the two classes. Oncogenes act dominantly and can be found by gain-of-function assays: Weinberg's and Wigler's groups (1981–1982) transfected DNA from human bladder carcinoma cells into mouse NIH 3T3 fibroblasts, isolated the transformed foci and cloned the transforming gene, *HRAS*, which differed from its normal counterpart by a single substitution, Gly12→Val. It was the human version of the oncogene of the Harvey sarcoma virus: the viral and the human routes had converged on the same genes. Tumor suppressor genes act recessively and had to be found by loss: by mapping the regions deleted or lost (loss of heterozygosity) in tumors and in families with hereditary cancer, as for *RB1*, *APC* and *BRCA1*.

Since the 2000s, systematic sequencing of tumor genomes (The Cancer Genome Atlas, the International Cancer Genome Consortium, and the Pan-Cancer Analysis of Whole Genomes of 2020) has catalogued the somatic changes of tens of thousands of tumors. Key findings:

- About 300–700 genes are recurrently mutated drivers across cancers, in about a dozen pathways (26.4.3); a typical tumor has a few drivers.
- The number of mutations varies a thousandfold, from about 0.1 per megabase in pediatric cancers and leukemias to more than 10 per megabase in melanomas and lung cancers of smokers, reflecting exposure (ultraviolet light, tobacco) and repair defects.
- Mutations carry signatures of their causes: C→T at dipyrimidines from ultraviolet light, C→A from tobacco smoke, characteristic spectra from mismatch-repair deficiency, homologous-recombination deficiency, APOBEC deaminases and aging (Alexandrov et al. 2013, 2020).
- Many genes are mutated at intermediate frequencies in many cancer types, so that cancers are increasingly classified and treated by their drivers rather than by their tissue alone.

### 26.4.3. The Core Pathways

Driver mutations cluster in a limited number of pathways that control the decisions described in the preceding chapters. In most cancers at least one component of each of three central pathways is altered.

Growth factors act through receptor tyrosine kinases on Ras, which drives the Raf–MEK–ERK cascade (inducing Myc and cyclin D) and, with the receptor, PI3K, which activates Akt and mTORC1 for cell growth and survival; NF1 inactivates Ras and PTEN opposes PI3K. Cyclin D–Cdk4/6, inhibited by p16INK4a, inactivates Rb and releases E2F for S-phase entry. Excess Myc and E2F induce ARF, and DNA damage activates ATM; both stabilize p53 by inhibiting Mdm2, which p53 induces (negative feedback). p53 induces p21, which inhibits Cdks, and the pro-apoptotic Puma and Bax; Akt opposes apoptosis (dashed). The HPV oncoproteins E7 and E6 inactivate Rb and p53](./plots/cancer-pathways.py)

1. Growth-factor signaling. Receptor tyrosine kinases (amplification of *ERBB2*, mutation of *EGFR*), **Ras** (*KRAS* mutated in about 90% of pancreatic and 40% of colorectal cancers), *BRAF* (about half of melanomas), *PIK3CA* (the catalytic subunit of **phosphoinositide 3-kinase**, mutated in about a third of breast cancers) and loss of *NF1* and *PTEN*. These mutations make the cell behave as though it were constantly stimulated by mitogens and survival factors, and they activate **mTORC1**, which drives growth and anabolic metabolism. Ras and PI3K signaling also make cells independent of anchorage (26.5.1).
2. The Rb pathway, which controls entry into the cycle at the restriction point: loss of *RB1*, deletion or silencing of *CDKN2A* (p16INK4a), amplification of *CCND1* (cyclin D1) or *CDK4*. Mutations in this pathway are nearly mutually exclusive in a tumor, as expected if any one of them suffices to disable the same control.
3. The p53 pathway, which responds to DNA damage, oncogenic signaling and other stresses with arrest, senescence or apoptosis ([7.3.22](/7.3.22#sec-22.3.2), [7.3.25](/7.3.25)): *TP53* is mutated in about half of all human cancers, and many of the rest overexpress **Mdm2** by gene amplification or delete *CDKN2A*, which encodes ARF as well as p16. Mice lacking p53 develop largely normally, but most develop cancer, mostly lymphomas and sarcomas, by six months of age (Donehower et al. 1992).

The *CDKN2A* locus, which encodes both p16INK4a (Rb pathway) and ARF (p53 pathway) in overlapping reading frames, is deleted in many cancers, which disables both arms with one lesion. Other pathways are altered in particular tissues: Wnt/β-catenin (*APC* in colon cancer), Hedgehog (basal cell carcinoma), Notch, TGF-β (*SMAD4* in pancreatic cancer), Hippo and the chromatin regulators ([7.3.19](/7.3.19#sec-19.5.3)). Transgenic mice show that oncogenes cooperate: mice expressing *Myc* or *Hras* in the mammary gland develop tumors after a long delay and only in a few cells, but mice expressing both develop tumors much earlier and in larger numbers (Sinn et al. 1987), and normal human cells were transformed in culture only by the combination of telomerase (hTERT), SV40 large T antigen (which inactivates Rb and p53), small t antigen (which inhibits the phosphatase PP2A) and oncogenic *HRAS* (Hahn et al. 1999).

### 26.4.4. Colorectal Cancer: A Paradigm

Colorectal cancer is the best-studied example of tumor progression, because the stages can be seen and removed by colonoscopy. The colonic epithelium is renewed every few days from stem cells at the base of the crypts, under the control of the **Wnt signaling pathway**. Fearon and Vogelstein (1990) correlated the stages with mutations:

| Stage | Typical change | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| aberrant crypt focus, small adenoma | loss of *APC* (or activating *CTNNB1*) | constitutive Wnt/β-catenin signaling; crypt cells fail to differentiate and migrate |
| intermediate adenoma | activating *KRAS* or *BRAF* | proliferation, larger adenoma |
| late adenoma | loss of *SMAD4* and other TGF-β pathway genes (18q loss); *PIK3CA* | escape from growth inhibition |
| carcinoma | loss of *TP53* (17p loss) | survival despite genetic damage and stress; invasion |
| metastasis | further, mostly non-recurrent changes | — |

About 15% of colorectal cancers follow a different route: they have defective mismatch repair (Lynch syndrome, or silencing of *MLH1* by methylation in sporadic cases), microsatellite instability, a near-diploid karyotype and a hundredfold more mutations, including frameshifts in microsatellites within genes such as *TGFBR2* and *BAX*; the many mutant proteins make them immunogenic and responsive to immune checkpoint blockade (26.6.4). The others have chromosomal instability. Sequencing and modeling suggest that it takes about 17 years from the first mutation to an advanced carcinoma and a few more years to metastasis (Jones et al. 2008), a long window for screening.

## 26.5. The Properties of Cancer Cells

### 26.5.1. Transformed Cells in Culture

**Definition 26.5.1 (Malignant Transformation and Anchorage Independence)** **Malignant transformation** is the conversion of a normal cell into one with the properties of a cancer cell, in an organism or in culture. **Anchorage independence** is the ability of transformed cells to proliferate without attachment to a solid substrate, assayed as colony formation in soft agar.

Transformed cells in culture, whether derived from tumors or produced by oncogenes, differ from normal cells in characteristic ways:

| Property | Normal cells | Transformed cells |
|---|---|---|
| density | stop at confluence (**contact inhibition**, [7.3.22](/7.3.22#sec-22.5.1)), form a monolayer | grow over one another, form multilayered foci |
| anchorage | need attachment and spreading to divide; die by anoikis when detached ([7.3.25](/7.3.25#sec-25.4.5)) | grow in suspension and in soft agar |
| growth factors | need serum or specific mitogens | low requirement, often autocrine |
| lifespan | Hayflick limit ([7.3.25](/7.3.25#sec-25.7.1)) | immortal (telomerase or ALT) |
| morphology and cytoskeleton | spread, stress fibers, ordered | rounded or spindle-shaped, fewer stress fibers |
| karyotype | diploid | often aneuploid and unstable |
| tumorigenicity | no tumors in immunodeficient mice | tumors in nude or NSG mice |

Each property reflects a broken control: anchorage independence, for example, results from oncogenic Ras and PI3K signaling that substitutes for integrin signals, and focus formation in cultures of NIH 3T3 cells was the original assay for oncogenes (26.4.2). Immortalization and transformation are distinct: immortal **cell lines** such as NIH 3T3 have bypassed senescence but still show contact inhibition and do not form tumors until an oncogene is added.

### 26.5.2. The Hallmarks of Cancer

**Definition 26.5.2 (Hallmarks of Cancer)** The **hallmarks of cancer** (Hanahan and Weinberg 2000, 2011; Hanahan 2022) are the functional capabilities that cells acquire as they progress from normality to malignancy, together with the enabling characteristics that accelerate their acquisition; they provide a framework for organizing the diversity of cancer-causing changes into a few principles.

![The hallmarks of cancer. Outer ring: acquired capabilities, colored by the year in which they were proposed (green: the six of 2000; orange: the two emerging hallmarks of 2011; blue: the two of 2022), with examples of the changes behind each. Inner disc: enabling characteristics, genome instability and mutation and tumor-promoting inflammation (2011), nonmutational epigenetic reprogramming and polymorphic microbiomes (2022)](./plots/hallmarks.py)

| Hallmark | Normal control it defeats | Typical changes | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| sustaining proliferative signaling | dependence on mitogens | RTK, Ras, BRAF, PI3K, Myc; autocrine PDGF and TGF-α | [7.3.22](/7.3.22), [7.1.15](/7.1.15) |
| evading growth suppressors | restriction point, contact inhibition, TGF-β | *RB1*, *CDKN2A*, *SMAD4*, *NF2* | [7.3.22](/7.3.22), [7.3.19](/7.3.19) |
| resisting cell death | apoptosis, the default fate without survival signals | *TP53*, Bcl-2, PI3K–Akt, loss of Fas signaling | [7.3.25](/7.3.25) |
| enabling replicative immortality | telomere-based senescence and crisis | telomerase (*TERT* promoter mutations), ALT | [7.3.25](/7.3.25), [7.2.7](/7.2.7) |
| inducing or accessing vasculature | diffusion limit of oxygen (100–200 µm) | VEGF, angiogenic switch | 26.5.4 |
| activating invasion and metastasis | adhesion, tissue boundaries | E-cadherin loss, EMT, proteases | 26.5.4, [7.3.23](/7.3.23) |
| reprogramming cellular metabolism | metabolism tuned for maintenance | aerobic glycolysis, glutamine use, *IDH1/2* | 26.5.3 |
| avoiding immune destruction | immune surveillance | PD-L1, loss of MHC class I | 26.5.5 |
| unlocking phenotypic plasticity | terminal differentiation | dedifferentiation, blocked differentiation, transdifferentiation | 26.2.5, [7.3.24](/7.3.24) |
| senescent cells | senescence as permanent arrest | SASP of senescent stromal and cancer cells | [7.3.25](/7.3.25#sec-25.7.5) |

Two features of the list deserve emphasis. First, the hallmarks are capabilities, not genes: one mutation can confer several (Ras sustains proliferation, blocks apoptosis and changes metabolism), and one capability can be acquired by many mutations. Second, several hallmarks are not properties of cancer cells alone but of their interaction with the rest of the body: the vasculature, the immune system, the stroma. A tumor is an organ-like ecosystem, not a bag of mutant cells.

### 26.5.3. Altered Metabolism

Otto Warburg observed in the 1920s that tumors take up glucose and convert it to lactate even when oxygen is plentiful (aerobic glycolysis, the **Warburg effect**, [7.1.18](/7.1.18#sec-18.4.4)), and he thought that cancer was caused by damaged respiration. Most cancer cells in fact have functional mitochondria. The current view is that rapid glycolysis serves growth: it supplies ATP quickly and, through branch pathways such as the **pentose phosphate pathway**, serine synthesis and the citric acid cycle fed by glutamine, the carbon skeletons and NADPH needed for nucleotides, lipids and amino acids; a cell dividing every day must double all its macromolecules. The switch is driven by the same pathways that drive proliferation: PI3K–Akt raises the glucose transporter GLUT1 and hexokinase, Myc induces glycolytic and glutaminolytic enzymes, and hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1α, stabilized by hypoxia or by loss of the VHL ubiquitin ligase, induces glycolysis and lactate export. High glucose uptake is exploited clinically in positron emission tomography with the glucose analog ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose, which is trapped as its 6-phosphate in glucose-avid tumors. Mutations in enzymes of the citric acid cycle (succinate dehydrogenase, fumarate hydratase) and in *IDH1/2* produce oncometabolites that inhibit dioxygenases and alter the epigenome.

### 26.5.4. Invasion, Metastasis and the Tumor Microenvironment

Metastasis is a cascade of steps, each of which most cancer cells fail: local invasion through the basement membrane, intravasation into blood or lymphatic vessels, survival in the circulation (against shear forces, anoikis and natural killer cells), arrest in the capillaries of a distant organ, extravasation, survival as micrometastases, and colonization, the outgrowth into a macroscopic tumor. Large numbers of cells enter the circulation (millions per gram of tumor per day in experimental models), but fewer than one in 10 000 forms a metastasis, and colonization is the most limiting step; dormant disseminated cells can survive for years before growing. The organs colonized depend partly on blood flow (the capillaries of liver and lung trap the first cells released from intestine and other organs) and partly on the compatibility of tumor cells and tissue, the "seed and soil" of Paget (1889): prostate cancer prefers bone, uveal melanoma the liver.

**Definition 26.5.3 (Epithelial–Mesenchymal Transition)** **Epithelial–mesenchymal transition** (EMT) is a program in which epithelial cells lose apical–basal polarity and cell–cell junctions (down-regulating E-cadherin) and acquire a migratory, invasive mesenchymal phenotype (expressing N-cadherin and vimentin), driven by transcription factors such as Snail, Slug, Twist and ZEB1/2; it occurs in normal development (gastrulation, the neural crest) and wound healing and is reversible (mesenchymal–epithelial transition).

In carcinomas, partial and reversible EMT at the invasive front, induced by TGF-β and other signals from the stroma, helps cells invade and confers stem-like properties and drug resistance; the loss of E-cadherin, a suppressor of invasion, is directly caused by mutations of *CDH1* in lobular breast cancer and hereditary diffuse gastric cancer. Invasion requires proteases (matrix metalloproteinases, often made by stromal cells), integrin-based adhesion and the migratory machinery of [7.3.15](/7.3.15).

The tumor depends on its stroma. Beyond 100–200 µm from a capillary, the limit of oxygen diffusion, cells become hypoxic, and tumors remain small until they acquire their own blood supply by an angiogenic switch, the secretion of VEGF and other factors that attract new vessels from the surroundings (Folkman). The new vessels are leaky and disorganized, which raises interstitial pressure and impairs drug delivery. Cancer-associated fibroblasts deposit a stiff extracellular matrix and secrete growth factors; tumor-associated macrophages, recruited by the tumor, often switch to a wound-healing state that promotes angiogenesis, invasion and immune suppression. Tumors have been called wounds that do not heal (Dvorak).

### 26.5.5. Escaping the Immune System

The immune system recognizes and eliminates many incipient tumors: mice and humans with immunodeficiency have more cancers, especially those caused by viruses, and cancers carry mutant proteins (neoantigens) that T cells can recognize. Tumors that grow have therefore been edited by the immune system and escaped it, by losing MHC class I molecules or antigen-processing proteins, by expressing ligands of inhibitory receptors on T cells (PD-L1), by recruiting regulatory T cells and suppressive myeloid cells, and by creating a microenvironment (hypoxia, adenosine, lactate, TGF-β) that suppresses T cells. The immunology of cancer is treated in [7.15](/7.15); its therapeutic consequence is 26.6.4.

## 26.6. Treatment

### 26.6.1. Traditional Therapies

Surgery and radiotherapy cure many localized cancers; chemotherapy is needed for disseminated disease. Classical chemotherapeutic drugs damage DNA (alkylating agents, cisplatin, topoisomerase poisons) or interfere with DNA synthesis (antimetabolites such as 5-fluorouracil and methotrexate) or with **mitosis** (taxanes, which stabilize **microtubules**, and vinca alkaloids, which depolymerize them). They exploit two peculiarities of cancer cells: many of them divide, and many have defective checkpoints and repair, so that they continue into S phase or mitosis with damage that a normal cell would stop to repair, and die (many by p53-independent mechanisms). Their toxicity to normal dividing cells (bone marrow, intestine, hair follicles) limits the dose. Some cancers are curable by chemotherapy alone, notably testicular cancer, childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia and Hodgkin lymphoma.

### 26.6.2. Targeted Therapy

**Definition 26.6.1 (Targeted Therapy)** **Targeted therapy** is treatment with a drug directed at a molecule specifically required by cancer cells, usually the product of a driver mutation, as opposed to cytotoxic drugs that act on all dividing cells.

The paradigm is imatinib for chronic myeloid leukemia. The disease is driven by a single initiating event, the BCR–ABL1 fusion kinase, and imatinib, which binds the ATP site of inactive ABL, induces remission in nearly all patients in the chronic phase and has turned the disease into a chronic condition with a near-normal life expectancy (Druker et al. 2001). Its success rests on *oncogene addiction*: a cancer cell that has evolved around an activated oncogene often depends on it more than a normal cell does on the corresponding proto-oncogene, so that inhibiting it kills the cancer cell selectively. Other examples are inhibitors of mutant EGFR (osimertinib) and ALK in lung cancer, BRAF and MEK inhibitors in melanoma, antibodies against HER2 (trastuzumab) in *ERBB2*-amplified breast cancer, covalent inhibitors of KRAS G12C (sotorasib, 2021) ([7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.6.2)), hormone therapies that block the estrogen and androgen receptors, and the BH3 mimetic venetoclax ([7.3.25](/7.3.25#sec-25.4.3)). Choosing the drug requires knowing the driver, so that tumors are now routinely sequenced.

### 26.6.3. Synthetic Lethality

**Definition 26.6.2 (Synthetic Lethality)** Two genes are **synthetically lethal** when the loss of either one alone is compatible with viability but the loss of both is lethal. **Synthetic lethality** in cancer therapy means killing cancer cells that have lost one gene, a tumor suppressor that cannot itself be targeted, with a drug that inhibits its synthetic-lethal partner, sparing normal cells, which retain the first gene.

The clinical example is PARP inhibitors (olaparib) in cancers with defects of homologous recombination, mostly those with *BRCA1* or *BRCA2* mutations (Bryant et al. and Farmer et al. 2005). PARP inhibitors trap PARP1 on single-strand breaks, which become double-strand breaks at replication forks; normal cells, including the cells of a carrier, which retain one functional *BRCA* allele, repair them by **homologous recombination**, whereas the tumor cells, which have lost the second allele, cannot ([7.2.8](/7.2.8#sec-8.7.2)). The drugs are approved for ovarian, breast, prostate and pancreatic cancers with these defects. Resistance arises, among other ways, by secondary mutations that restore the reading frame of *BRCA1/2*. Systematic genetic screens with **CRISPR–Cas9** ([7.2.24](/7.2.24)) in hundreds of cancer cell lines now search for other synthetic-lethal vulnerabilities, such as the dependence of microsatellite-unstable cancers on the WRN helicase.

### 26.6.4. Immune Checkpoint Blockade

**Definition 26.6.3 (Immune Checkpoint Blockade)** **Immune checkpoint blockade** is cancer therapy with **monoclonal antibodies** that block inhibitory receptors of T cells (CTLA-4, PD-1) or their ligands (PD-L1), releasing T cells, especially those that recognize tumor antigens, from inhibition.

PD-1 (programmed cell death protein 1), a receptor of the immunoglobulin superfamily on activated and exhausted T cells, binds PD-L1 and PD-L2; it recruits the phosphatase SHP-2, which dephosphorylates signaling molecules of the T-cell receptor and of the costimulatory receptor CD28 and so dampens T-cell activation, a normal brake that limits autoimmunity and tissue damage during chronic inflammation. Many tumors express PD-L1, often induced by interferon-γ from attacking T cells. Antibodies against CTLA-4 (ipilimumab, from Allison's work) and PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, from Honjo's discovery of PD-1; Nobel Prize 2018) produce durable remissions in a fraction of patients with melanoma, lung, kidney, bladder and mismatch-repair-deficient cancers, best in tumors with many neoantigens. The side effects are autoimmune. Other immunotherapies, such as T cells engineered to express chimeric antigen receptors (CAR-T cells) against B-cell antigens, and their mechanisms are described in [7.15](/7.15).

### 26.6.5. Drug Resistance and Combination Therapy

**Definition 26.6.4 (Drug Resistance)** **Drug resistance** in cancer is the ability of cancer cells to survive and proliferate in the presence of a therapy; it is primary when present before treatment and acquired when it emerges during treatment, by selection of genetic or non-genetic variants.

Resistance is the evolutionary consequence of treating a large, diverse population of cells with a strong selective agent, and its mechanisms are as varied as the cells: mutations of the target that prevent drug binding (the gatekeeper mutation T315I of BCR–ABL1 against imatinib, T790M of EGFR against first-generation inhibitors), amplification of the target, activation of a bypass pathway (amplification of *MET* in EGFR-inhibited lung cancer), changes of cell state (transformation of lung adenocarcinoma to small-cell carcinoma, EMT), drug efflux by **ABC transporters** such as P-glycoprotein ([7.1.13](/7.1.13)), and non-genetic, reversible drug-tolerant states in a few cells, from which genetically resistant cells later emerge. The variable delays of the apoptotic switch ([7.3.25](/7.3.25#sec-25.4.6)) are one source of such non-genetic tolerance.

**Example 26.6.1 (Pre-Existing Resistance and Combination Therapy)** Goldie and Coldman (1979) applied the reasoning of the Luria–Delbrück fluctuation test to tumors. A tumor of $N$ cells has gone through about $N$ divisions; if resistance to a drug arises by mutation at rate $\mu$ per division, the expected number of resistance mutations before treatment is about $\mu N$ and the probability that at least one resistant cell exists is

$$
P_{\mathrm{res}} = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\mu N}.
\tag{26.3}
$$
With $\mu = 10^{-7}$, a tumor of $10^9$ cells, the size at which it is usually detected, contains on average about 100 resistant cells ($P_{\mathrm{res}} \approx 1$). If resistance to two drugs with independent mechanisms requires two independent mutations, the rate is $\mu^2 = 10^{-14}$, and $P_{\mathrm{res}} \approx 10^{-5}$ for the same tumor: simultaneous combination therapy can cure where sequential monotherapy, which selects resistant cells to each drug in turn, cannot. The model also predicts that treatment is more effective the earlier it starts, because $P_{\mathrm{res}}$ rises with $N$.

![Pre-existing resistance and combination therapy, Eq. (26.3). (A) Probability that a tumor of N cells already contains at least one cell resistant to one drug (red, mutation rate 10⁻⁷ per division) or to two drugs with independent mechanisms (green, 10⁻¹⁴); the dashed line marks the size at which tumors are usually detected. (B) Treatment of a detected tumor of 10⁹ cells: with one drug (red), the sensitive cells die at net rate 0.1 per day and the tumor shrinks below the detection limit (remission), but about 100 pre-existing resistant cells (dotted) grow at 0.03 per day and cause relapse after about a year and a half; with two drugs (green), cells resistant to either drug alone are killed by the other, no double-resistant cell exists, and the tumor is eradicated](./plots/therapy-resistance.py)

The model explains why combination chemotherapy cured childhood leukemia and Hodgkin lymphoma when single drugs did not, why the treatment of HIV requires several drugs at once (the virus population is large and mutates rapidly; a similar calculation), and why combinations of targeted drugs, or of targeted therapy with immunotherapy, are being developed. Its assumptions also show its limits: resistance can be non-genetic, mutations that confer resistance to both drugs exist (efflux pumps, cell-state changes), and toxicities often prevent the full dosing of two drugs together. A different strategy, adaptive therapy (Gatenby), uses evolutionary competition instead of trying to eradicate the tumor: when resistance carries a fitness cost, keeping a population of sensitive cells by intermittent dosing can suppress the resistant ones for longer than continuous maximal treatment.

## Questions

1. Using Eq. (26.1), explain why hereditary retinoblastoma is usually bilateral and multifocal whereas sporadic retinoblastoma is unilateral and unifocal. If a carrier has on average three tumors, what fraction of carriers would be expected to remain free of the disease? Why are survivors of hereditary retinoblastoma at high risk of osteosarcoma later in life?

   **Answer** In carriers every retinoblast already has one mutant allele, so a single somatic hit in any of the roughly $10^5$ cells of each retina suffices; with an expected $m_1 = 3$ tumors, tumors are usually several and in both eyes. In noncarriers two hits are needed in the same cell, $m_2 \approx 6 \times 10^{-5}$, so a sporadic tumor is nearly always single. The number of tumors is Poisson distributed, so the fraction of carriers without a tumor is $\mathrm{e}^{-3} \approx 5\%$, consistent with the observed incomplete penetrance of about 90–95%. Carriers have one mutant *RB1* allele in every cell of the body, so a second hit in any other tissue where Rb loss is tolerated and promotes growth, notably bone (osteosarcoma) and soft tissue, can start a tumor; radiotherapy of the eye increases the risk further by causing second hits.

2. The incidence of a cancer rises as $t^5$ with age between 30 and 70. (a) How many rate-limiting steps does the Armitage–Doll model suggest? (b) In a population in which smoking multiplies the rate of one of these steps by 10, by what factor would the incidence change? (c) Why might the true number of mutations be smaller than the estimate in (a)?

   **Answer** (a) The slope is $k - 1 = 5$, so $k = 6$ steps. (b) Incidence is proportional to the product $\mu_1\cdots\mu_k$, so it rises tenfold at every age (the age curve is shifted up, keeping its slope); if smoking stops, the effect depends on which step is affected: an effect on an early step persists, one on the last step disappears quickly. (c) If some steps cause clonal expansion of the intermediate cells, the number of cells at risk for the next step grows with age, which steepens the age curve (two-stage clonal expansion model, panel C of the figure in 26.2.2); some rate-limiting steps may be epigenetic or environmental rather than mutational; and the power-law approximation fails at old ages.

3. Explain why the tumor suppressor genes *TP53* and *RB1* behave as recessive in the cell but cause cancer predisposition that is inherited as a dominant trait. Some *TP53* missense mutants are nonetheless partly dominant in the cell: suggest why, given that p53 acts as a tetramer.

   **Answer** One normal copy is sufficient for the function of the cell, so a cell with one mutant allele behaves normally (recessive at the cellular level); but in a carrier every cell has only one functional copy, and among the very many cells of a tissue at least one loses it by a somatic event during life, so cancer predisposition segregates in families as a dominant trait with high penetrance. p53 binds DNA as a tetramer; a missense mutant protein that still oligomerizes but cannot bind DNA poisons tetramers containing normal subunits. With equal amounts of both, only $(1/2)^4 = 1/16$ of tetramers are fully wild type (a dominant-negative effect), so a heterozygous cell has much less p53 activity; this is one reason why missense mutations, rather than deletions, are the common form of *TP53* loss in tumors, and why some of them additionally gain new activities.

4. A tumor contains $10^9$ cells and has a mutation rate of $10^{-9}$ per base pair per division. Estimate how many cells in the tumor carry any given point mutation that arose during its growth, if the mutation arose (a) at the first division, (b) in the last generation. What does this imply for the detection of mutations by sequencing a biopsy, and for therapy?

   **Answer** (a) A mutation at the first division is carried by about half of the tumor (a clonal or trunk mutation, if it is not lost), about $5 \times 10^8$ cells, and appears in sequencing at an allele frequency of about 25% (half the cells, one of two alleles), more if the region is lost. (b) In the last generation, about $10^9 \times 10^{-9} = 1$ new mutation arises per base pair across the tumor, so every possible point mutation of the genome is present in about one cell, and every mutation present in many copies arose early or was selected. Sequencing a biopsy detects clonal and large subclonal mutations but not rare ones, and different regions show different subclonal mutations (heterogeneity). For therapy it means that any single point mutation conferring resistance already exists in a detectable tumor (compare Eq. (26.3)), so a single targeted drug selects resistance unless the resistant variants are also killed.

5. Cells from a colon adenoma have lost both copies of *APC* and carry a *KRAS* G12D mutation. Predict the effect of (a) restoring *APC* function, (b) an inhibitor of KRAS G12D, and (c) a drug that inhibits PARP. Which of these is an example of oncogene addiction, and which might work through synthetic lethality, if any?

   **Answer** (a) Restoring APC rebuilds the destruction complex, lowers β-catenin and the Wnt target genes and lets the adenoma cells differentiate; in mouse models of *Apc* loss with oncogenic *Kras* and *Trp53* deletion, restoring *Apc* caused tumor regression and re-establishment of normal crypts (Dow et al. 2015), showing that the tumors remain dependent on the initiating lesion. This is oncogene (or "non-oncogene") addiction to the Wnt pathway. (b) A KRAS G12D inhibitor removes a proliferation and survival signal on which the cells may depend (addiction), slowing or shrinking the tumor; in practice colorectal cells often bypass KRAS inhibition through EGFR feedback, so combinations with EGFR antibodies are needed. (c) A PARP inhibitor has no selective effect unless the cells are deficient in homologous recombination; these cells are not (no *BRCA* lesion), so there is no synthetic lethality and little benefit.

6. Explain, in terms of Eq. (26.3), why (a) imatinib almost always works in chronic-phase CML but often fails in the blast crisis, and (b) HIV infection is treated with three drugs at once. What assumption of the model fails for resistance by drug efflux through ABC transporters?

   **Answer** (a) In chronic phase the leukemic population is large but genetically stable and dependent on BCR–ABL1; the probability of pre-existing kinase-domain mutations conferring resistance is moderate, and the disease responds; in blast crisis the population is larger, genetically unstable (higher $\mu$) and has acquired additional drivers that reduce dependence on BCR–ABL1, so $\mu N$ is large and resistant cells (such as T315I) are almost certainly present, causing rapid relapse. Second-generation inhibitors and ponatinib, active against T315I, reduce the problem. (b) An infected person has about $10^{10}$ virions produced per day and a mutation rate of about $3 \times 10^{-5}$ per base per replication, so every single resistance mutation arises many times every day: $\mu N \gg 1$ for one drug. For three drugs that need independent mutations, $\mu^3 N \ll 1$, and resistance cannot emerge while the virus is suppressed. The model assumes that resistance to the two drugs requires independent events; an efflux pump such as P-glycoprotein (ABCB1) exports many structurally unrelated drugs, so one change gives resistance to both (multidrug resistance), and the probability reverts to that of a single drug.

7. In a mouse model, a carcinogen applied once to the skin, followed by repeated applications of a phorbol ester for 20 weeks, produces papillomas; stopping the phorbol ester early causes many papillomas to regress. Explain the roles of the two agents and the regression. Which agent would the Ames test detect, and why?

   **Answer** The carcinogen is the tumor initiator: a single dose causes irreversible mutations (for DMBA, an *Hras* codon-61 mutation) in some keratinocyte stem cells. The phorbol ester is the tumor promoter: it activates protein kinase C and causes sustained proliferation and inflammation, which selectively expand the initiated cells into papillomas. The expansion depends on continued promotion at first, so papillomas regress when it stops; only those that acquire further mutations become autonomous and progress to carcinoma. The Ames test measures mutagenesis, so it detects the initiator (after metabolic activation by the liver extract it contains) but not the promoter, which is not mutagenic; this is a known limitation of mutagenicity tests for carcinogens that act as promoters.
