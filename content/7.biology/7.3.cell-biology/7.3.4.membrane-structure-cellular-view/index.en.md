The chemistry of membranes was the subject of [7.1.12](/7.1.12) and [7.1.13](/7.1.13): the amphipathic lipids that assemble into a **lipid bilayer**, the ways proteins span or attach to it, and the thermodynamics of transport across it. This chapter looks at the same structure from the side of the cell, asking three questions that chemistry alone does not answer. How do we know what a membrane looks like? The answer is a century of experiments, each of which overturned the model before it (4.1), and the tools for taking membrane proteins out of the bilayer and putting them back (4.3). What does the membrane of a living cell look like, as opposed to a pure bilayer? It is coated with sugars on the outside (4.4), crowded, partitioned by the cortical cytoskeleton beneath it, and divided into domains of different composition, so that its molecules diffuse ten to a hundred times more slowly than in a liposome (4.5, 4.6). And how does a cell shape its membranes? Bending a bilayer costs elastic energy that can be computed (4.7), which explains why cells need coats and scaffolds to make vesicles. Membrane transport at the level of the whole cell (cell volume, membrane potential, epithelia) follows in [7.3.5](/7.3.5), and the use of membranes to build compartments and the sorting of proteins into them in [7.3.6](/7.3.6). The theme of the course appears here for the first time: the properties that matter to the cell (a red cell that survives 120 days in the circulation, a sperm whose head and tail carry different receptors) arise from the cooperation of lipids, proteins and the cytoskeleton, not from any one of them.

## 4.1. Models of Membrane Structure: An Experimental History

### 4.1.1. Lipids Form a Bilayer

In the 1890s Charles Overton measured how fast hundreds of substances enter plant and animal cells and found that the rate rises with the solubility of the substance in olive oil relative to water: the cell is bounded by a layer that behaves like a lipid. Irving Langmuir (1917) showed that amphipathic lipids spread on water form a **monolayer** with their polar heads in the water, whose area can be measured in a trough. In 1925 Evert Gorter and François Grendel extracted the lipids from erythrocytes of six mammals, spread them in a Langmuir trough and compared the area of the monolayer with the surface area of the cells: the ratio was close to $2$. Because an erythrocyte has no internal membranes, they concluded that the plasma membrane is a lipid layer two molecules thick.

The conclusion was right, although the measurement contained two errors of about the same size in opposite directions. Acetone does not extract all the lipid, and the cell area was estimated from dried smears, which shrink; the first error lowers the monolayer area, the second lowers the cell area. Modern measurements also show that proteins occupy a large part of the membrane area (4.1.3), so the "lipid area" of the plasma membrane is less than its geometric area.

### 4.1.2. Protein Sandwiches and the Unit Membrane

A lipid bilayer alone has a high interfacial tension, but the surfaces of cells such as sea urchin eggs had a much lower one. Hugh Davson and James Danielli (1935) therefore proposed that the bilayer is coated on both faces by a layer of protein, later modified to include protein-lined pores to explain the permeability to ions. The model seemed confirmed when transmission electron microscopy of thin sections stained with osmium tetroxide or permanganate showed every cellular membrane as two dark lines about $2\,\mathrm{nm}$ thick separated by a light band of about $3.5\,\mathrm{nm}$, a total of $7$ to $8\,\mathrm{nm}$.

**Definition 4.1.1 (Unit Membrane)** The **unit membrane** model (J. David Robertson, 1959) holds that all biological membranes share one trilaminar structure, a lipid bilayer whose two dark-staining head-group layers are each covered by a sheet of protein in an extended conformation, seen in electron micrographs as a "railroad track" about $7.5\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide.

The trilaminar image is real (the dark lines are the stained head groups), but the interpretation failed on four grounds. Membranes differ in thickness ($5$–$10\,\mathrm{nm}$) and in their protein-to-lipid mass ratio, from about $0.25$ in myelin to about $3$ in the inner mitochondrial membrane ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.1.2)), which a uniform protein sheet cannot accommodate. Optical rotation and infrared spectra showed that membrane proteins are largely α-helical and globular, not extended sheets. Most membrane proteins can be released only by detergents (4.3), which means they are held by hydrophobic interactions with the lipid core, not by electrostatic attraction to the head groups. And freeze-fracture electron microscopy (Daniel Branton, 1966), which splits a frozen membrane between its two leaflets, revealed particles $5$–$10\,\mathrm{nm}$ across in the fracture faces, proteins embedded in the bilayer interior. Labeling experiments then showed that some proteins, glycophorin of erythrocytes among them, span the whole bilayer (Mark Bretscher, 1971).

### 4.1.3. The Fluid Mosaic Model and Its Revisions

**Definition 4.1.2 (Fluid Mosaic Model)** In the **fluid mosaic model** (S. Jonathan Singer and Garth Nicolson, 1972), a membrane is a two-dimensional fluid of lipids, arranged as a bilayer, in which globular proteins are embedded to various depths as a mosaic: integral membrane proteins with their hydrophobic surfaces in the hydrocarbon core and their polar parts in the water, and peripheral membrane proteins bound to the surfaces. Both lipids and proteins are free to diffuse laterally but not to cross from one leaflet to the other.

The model rested on thermodynamics (it puts every polar group in water and every nonpolar group in the core, which the previous models did not) and on the experiment of Frye and Edidin (4.5.1), and it is still the frame in which membranes are described. Fifty years of further work have revised three of its features.

| Feature of the 1972 model | Revision | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Proteins float in a sea of lipid | The membrane is crowded: proteins and their bound lipids occupy $20\,\%$ to more than $50\,\%$ of the area, and proteins are separated by only a few lipid diameters | Freeze-fracture particle counts; cryo-electron tomography; mass ratios |
| Lipids are randomly mixed | Lipids and proteins form domains, from nanoscale **lipid rafts** ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.2.4)) to micrometer-sized regions of polarized cells | Phase separation in model membranes; super-resolution and single-molecule imaging (4.6) |
| Free lateral diffusion | Diffusion is hindered and compartmentalized by the cortical cytoskeleton and its anchored proteins | Single-particle tracking at high frame rates (4.5.3) |
| The membrane is flat | Membranes are curved and shaped by proteins, and curvature itself sorts lipids and proteins | Membrane-bending proteins; mechanics of vesicles (4.7) |

B: the Davson–Danielli sandwich (1935), a bilayer covered on both faces by protein layers, inferred from the low surface tension of cells. ## 4.2. Composition at a Glance

The molecules of membranes are described in [7.1.12](/7.1.12) (lipids) and [7.1.13](/7.1.13) (proteins). The table collects what a cell biologist needs from those chapters.

| Component | Share and location | What it contributes at the cellular level |
|---|---|---|
| **Glycerophospholipids** (PC, PE, PS, PI) | about half of membrane lipid in animal cells; PC and sphingomyelin mainly in the outer leaflet, PE, PS and PI in the inner one | the bilayer itself; PS and the phosphoinositides give the cytoplasmic face a negative charge that binds peripheral proteins |
| **Sphingolipids** (sphingomyelin, **glycolipids** such as **gangliosides**) | up to $20\,\%$ of plasma membrane lipid; outer leaflet; scarce in the ER | thicker, more ordered regions; glycan headgroups for recognition |
| **Cholesterol** | up to about $40\,\%$ of the lipid molecules of the plasma membrane, a few percent in the ER | orders fluid chains, lowers permeability to small polar molecules, enables liquid-ordered domains |
| Phosphoinositides | under $1\,\%$ of lipid; restricted to particular membranes (PI(4,5)P₂ in the plasma membrane, PI(4)P in the Golgi, PI(3)P in early endosomes) | markers of membrane identity and sources of second messengers ([7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.3.1)); their distribution is discussed with vesicle traffic in [7.3.8](/7.3.8) |
| Integral and peripheral proteins, lipid-anchored proteins | $20\,\%$ to $30\,\%$ of genes encode integral membrane proteins; protein mass from $25\,\%$ (myelin) to $75\,\%$ (inner mitochondrial membrane) | transport, signaling, adhesion, energy conversion; anchoring of the cytoskeleton |
| Carbohydrate | $2\,\%$ to $10\,\%$ of plasma membrane mass, all on the noncytosolic face, as **N-linked glycans** and O-linked glycans of **glycoproteins**, glycolipids and **proteoglycans** | the glycocalyx (4.4) |

Phospholipases, which cut phospholipids at defined bonds, are both laboratory tools for probing which lipids face which side and signaling enzymes in the cell.

| Enzyme | Bond cleaved | Products | Cellular role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phospholipase A₁ / A₂ | acyl ester at C-1 / C-2 of glycerol | free fatty acid + lysophospholipid | PLA₂ releases arachidonate, precursor of eicosanoids ([7.1.12](/7.1.12#sec-12.5.2)); snake and bee venoms |
| Phospholipase B | both acyl esters | two fatty acids + glycerophosphoryl head group | lysophospholipase activity |
| **Phospholipase C** | glycerol–phosphate bond | diacylglycerol + phosphorylated head group (IP₃ from PI(4,5)P₂) | second messengers ([7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.3.1)) |
| Phospholipase D | phosphate–head group bond | phosphatidic acid + alcohol (choline) | membrane traffic and signaling |

What membranes do for the cell follows from this composition: they define the boundaries of the cell and of its organelles, which makes compartments with different contents possible ([7.3.6](/7.3.6)); they control the traffic of solutes and maintain gradients of ions ([7.3.5](/7.3.5)); they receive signals through **receptors** ([7.1.14](/7.1.14)); they mediate recognition and adhesion between cells ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)); they serve as scaffolds that bring enzymes together; and they convert energy, storing it as ion gradients and using it to make ATP ([7.1.23](/7.1.23)).

## 4.3. Studying Membrane Proteins: Detergents and Reconstitution

Integral membrane proteins are insoluble in water, so to purify one, determine its structure or measure its activity in isolation, it must be taken out of the bilayer without destroying it and, ideally, put back into a defined membrane.

**Definition 4.3.1 (Detergent)** A **detergent** is a small amphipathic molecule with a single hydrocarbon chain or a rigid hydrophobic ring system and a large polar head, more soluble in water than in lipid; its conical shape makes it form micelles rather than bilayers above its critical micelle concentration ([6.3.19](/6.3.19)).

Added to a membrane, detergent molecules first partition into the bilayer; at higher concentration the bilayer breaks up into mixed micelles of lipid and detergent; finally each **integral membrane protein** ends up in a micelle-like belt of detergent that covers its hydrophobic surface, a protein–detergent complex soluble in water. Above the **critical micelle concentration** (CMC) the concentration of free detergent monomers stays constant, so detergents with a high CMC can be removed quickly by dialysis or dilution, while those with a low CMC are removed by adsorption to hydrophobic beads.

| Detergent | Class | CMC in water | Effect on proteins |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) | anionic | about $8\,\mathrm{mM}$ | denatures: binds about one molecule per two residues and unfolds the chain; used in SDS-PAGE ([7.1.4](/7.1.4)) |
| Triton X-100 | nonionic | about $0.2\,\mathrm{mM}$ | mild; solubilizes most membranes, leaves detergent-resistant raft fractions in the cold |
| β-Octylglucoside | nonionic | about $20\,\mathrm{mM}$ | mild; easily removed by dialysis, used for reconstitution |
| Dodecylmaltoside (DDM) | nonionic | about $0.2\,\mathrm{mM}$ | mild; the standard detergent for crystallizing membrane proteins |
| CHAPS (a bile-salt derivative) | zwitterionic | about $8\,\mathrm{mM}$ | mild; keeps protein complexes together |

Mild detergents preserve the folded structure and often the activity of the protein. Removing the detergent in the presence of phospholipids makes the protein insert into the bilayers that re-form: the result is a **liposome** containing the protein (a proteoliposome), in which its function can be tested in a membrane of known composition and with known solutions on the two sides. Reconstitution answered several central questions. The purified Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, reconstituted into liposomes, pumps $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{K+}$ in the 3:2 ratio of the intact cell; and Efraim Racker and Walther Stoeckenius (1974) showed that liposomes containing bacteriorhodopsin, a light-driven proton pump, and mitochondrial **ATP synthase** make ATP in the light, a direct test of the **chemiosmotic theory** ([7.1.23](/7.1.23)).

A proteoliposome has the disadvantage that the protein is inserted in both orientations and that only one side of it is accessible after the vesicle has closed.

**Definition 4.3.2 (Nanodisc)** A **nanodisc** is a disc-shaped patch of lipid bilayer, about $10\,\mathrm{nm}$ in diameter, whose hydrophobic edge is covered by a belt of two amphipathic helical scaffold proteins (engineered from apolipoprotein A-I of high-density lipoprotein) or by an amphipathic polymer; a membrane protein incorporated into it sits in a native-like bilayer with both faces exposed to the solution.

Nanodiscs keep membrane proteins soluble without detergent, allow ligands to reach both faces, and are small and uniform enough for single-particle **cryo-electron microscopy**; many structures of receptors, channels and transporters are now of proteins in nanodiscs, often with the surrounding lipids resolved. Styrene–maleic acid copolymers go one step further: they cut discs directly out of cell membranes, so that a protein is purified together with its native lipid annulus, never having seen a detergent.

**Example 4.3.1** Which side of the membrane does a protein face? Erythrocytes lysed in hypotonic buffer leave empty membrane sacs, ghosts, that can be resealed either right-side-out or, at low ionic strength and with mechanical shearing, as inside-out vesicles. A reagent that cannot cross the bilayer (a protease, lactoperoxidase with radioactive iodide, a membrane-impermeant biotinylation reagent) labels only the parts of proteins exposed on the outer surface of the vesicles. Glycophorin is labeled from both preparations, but in its N-terminal part on right-side-out vesicles and in its C-terminal part on inside-out ones: it spans the membrane once, with its N-terminus and glycans outside. Spectrin (4.6.4) is labeled only on inside-out vesicles: it is a peripheral protein of the cytoplasmic face. The same logic, with impermeant reagents and protease protection, is used to establish the topology of organelle membrane proteins ([7.3.6](/7.3.6)).

## 4.4. The Glycocalyx

Electron micrographs of cells stained with ruthenium red or with labeled **lectins** show a fuzzy, carbohydrate-rich layer on the outer surface of the plasma membrane.

**Definition 4.4.1 (Glycocalyx)** The **glycocalyx** (cell coat) is the carbohydrate-rich zone on the noncytosolic surface of the plasma membrane, formed by the oligosaccharides of glycolipids and glycoproteins, the glycosaminoglycan chains of membrane proteoglycans, and secreted glycoproteins and proteoglycans adsorbed on the surface.

Its thickness ranges from a few tens of nanometers on most cells to several hundred nanometers on the luminal surface of blood vessels and on the microvilli of the intestinal brush border. The glycans are bound only to the noncytosolic face of membrane molecules, because glycosylation takes place in the lumen of the ER and Golgi apparatus, which is topologically equivalent to the outside of the cell ([7.3.6](/7.3.6)). Membrane proteoglycans include syndecans, which span the membrane, and glypicans, which are GPI-anchored; their heparan sulfate chains bind growth factors and present them to receptors. Because the boundary between glycocalyx and extracellular matrix is not sharp, the two are often treated together ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)).

The glycocalyx has four kinds of function.

- Protection and lubrication: a hydrated layer of negatively charged sugars protects against mechanical and chemical damage; mucins of epithelial surfaces, with hundreds of O-linked glycans, form a barrier against pathogens.
- Charge and spacing: **Sialic acids** give the surface a negative charge. Glycophorin A, close to a million copies per erythrocyte, carries most of the red cell's sialic acid; the resulting repulsion keeps erythrocytes from adhering to each other and to the vessel wall. Bulky glycoproteins also keep other cells at a distance and prevent unwanted contacts.
- Recognition: glycans are read by lectins; selectins on endothelial cells bind sialyl Lewis x on leukocytes and slow them for entry into inflamed tissue ([7.1.10](/7.1.10)); viruses such as influenza bind sialic acid.
- Identity: the ABO blood group antigens are terminal sugars of glycolipids and glycoproteins on erythrocytes and many other cells: the H antigen (group O) is extended by N-acetylgalactosamine in group A and by galactose in group B, by glycosyltransferases encoded by two alleles of one gene ([7.1.10](/7.1.10#sec-10.4.3)).

## 4.5. Membrane Fluidity in Living Cells

### 4.5.1. Mouse–Human Heterokaryons: The Experiment of Frye and Edidin

The first direct evidence that proteins move in the plane of a cell membrane came from an experiment with cell fusion (Larry Frye and Michael Edidin, 1970). Mouse and human cells in culture were fused with inactivated Sendai virus, which produces hybrid cells (heterokaryons) with one continuous plasma membrane. Surface proteins of the two species were labeled with different fluorescent antibodies: fluorescein-labeled antibodies against mouse H-2 antigens (green) and rhodamine-labeled antibodies against human surface antigens (red). Immediately after fusion, each color occupied its own half of the heterokaryon; after $40\,\mathrm{min}$ at $37\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the two colors were intermixed over the whole surface in more than $90\,\%$ of the cells. Three controls identified the process as diffusion. Mixing was not prevented by inhibitors of protein synthesis, so it did not result from insertion of new protein; it was not prevented by inhibitors of ATP production, so it did not require energy; and it slowed steeply with temperature and stopped below about $15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, as expected for a process that depends on the fluidity of the lipid ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.2.1)).

The time scale gives an estimate of the diffusion coefficient. Surface proteins diffusing on a sphere of radius $R$ with coefficient $D$, starting from one hemisphere, mix by the decay of the angular modes of the **diffusion equation** on the sphere, the Legendre polynomials $P_l(\cos\theta)$, each relaxing with the time constant $R^2/[l(l+1)D]$; the slowest mode, $l = 1$, has

$$
\tau_1 = \frac{R^2}{2D}.
$$
For a cell of radius $7.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, mixing in some $10$ to $40\,\mathrm{min}$ implies $D$ of order $10^{-2}\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, a hundred times less than that of a lipid ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.2.3)). Later measurements of the same kind of proteins by FRAP gave similar values.

### 4.5.2. FRAP, FLIP and Single-Particle Tracking

The modern measurements of lateral mobility use the imaging methods of [7.3.2](/7.3.2). In **fluorescence recovery after photobleaching** (FRAP; [7.1.13](/7.1.13#def-13.2.2), with the recovery curve of [7.1.13](/7.1.13#fml-13.2.1)) a spot is bleached and its recovery gives the diffusion coefficient and the mobile fraction. In fluorescence loss in photobleaching (FLIP) a spot is bleached repeatedly and the loss of fluorescence elsewhere is recorded: a region that does not lose fluorescence is not connected to the bleached spot, which tests whether a membrane is continuous (for example, whether the lumen of ER tubules is one compartment) and whether a molecule is confined to a domain. In single-particle tracking a single molecule, labeled with a gold particle, a quantum dot or a bright dye, is followed at video rate or much faster, and its trajectory gives the mean squared displacement (MSD) as a function of time. Free diffusion in two dimensions gives a straight line, $\langle r^2\rangle = 4Dt$; confinement, hopping and directed motion bend it in characteristic ways (4.5.3).

| Molecule and membrane | $D / (\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}})$ | Mobile fraction |
|---|---|---|
| Phospholipid in a liposome or a giant vesicle | $5$–$10$ | $1$ |
| Phospholipid in the plasma membrane (FRAP, $\mu\mathrm{m}$ scale) | $0.2$–$1$ | about $1$ |
| Transmembrane protein reconstituted in a pure bilayer | $1$–$5$ | $1$ |
| Transmembrane protein in the plasma membrane | $0.005$–$0.2$ | often $0.3$–$0.8$ |
| Band 3 in the erythrocyte membrane | about $0.004$ (mobile part) | partly immobile (bound to ankyrin) |

### 4.5.3. Why Proteins Move Slowly: The Picket-Fence Model

The Saffman–Delbrück relation of [7.1.13](/7.1.13#fml-13.2.2) predicts a coefficient of a few $\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ for any transmembrane protein, almost independent of its size. Proteins in a pure bilayer behave this way; proteins in the plasma membrane do not. The difference is not in the bilayer but in what surrounds it: **macromolecular crowding** by other proteins, binding to the cytoskeleton, to the extracellular matrix or to other cells, and the partitioning of the membrane by the cortex. Single-particle tracking at up to $40\,000$ frames per second (Akihiro Kusumi and colleagues, from 2002) revealed the last of these.

**Definition 4.5.1 (Picket-Fence Model)** In the **picket-fence model**, the actin-based membrane skeleton immediately beneath the plasma membrane (the "fence") and the transmembrane proteins anchored to it and lined up along it (the "pickets") divide the membrane into compartments some $40$–$250\,\mathrm{nm}$ across, depending on the cell type. Lipids and proteins diffuse freely within a compartment and pass to an adjacent one only occasionally, when a gap opens by thermal fluctuation of the skeleton; this is **hop diffusion**.

Within a compartment a phospholipid diffuses at $5$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, as fast as in a liposome, and it hops to the next one every $1$ to $20\,\mathrm{ms}$. Measured over micrometers, it therefore appears to diffuse at only $0.2$–$1\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, the value seen by FRAP. Removing actin filaments with latrunculin, or detaching the membrane from the cortex, enlarges the compartments and increases the long-range diffusion coefficient. Lipids in the outer leaflet, which have no contact with the cortex, are also corralled: the pickets, not the fence itself, confine them.

The two scales are related by a simple formula, which treats the fences as barriers of finite permeability.

**Formula 4.5.1 (Hop Diffusion)** Let molecules diffuse with coefficient $D_{\mu}$ inside square compartments of side $L$ whose walls they cross with permeability $\kappa$ (the flux across a wall per unit length divided by the concentration difference, a velocity). The long-range diffusion coefficient is

$$
\frac{1}{D_{\mathrm{M}}} = \frac{1}{D_{\mu}} + \frac{1}{\kappa L},
$$
and the mean residence time in a compartment is of order $\tau \approx L^2/(4D_{\mathrm{M}})$ when $\kappa L \ll D_{\mu}$.

The formula is that of resistors in series: a molecule traveling a distance $L$ must cross the interior of a compartment (resistance $L/D_{\mu}$) and one wall (resistance $1/\kappa$). Because the walls of a square grid are parallel to the axes, the motions in $x$ and $y$ are independent, and the one-dimensional result applies to each. For a walker on a lattice of spacing $a$ that crosses a wall with probability $p$ per attempt, $\kappa = pD_{\mu}/a$, so that $D_{\mathrm{M}}/D_{\mu} = pL/(pL + a)$.

**Example 4.5.1** A lipid diffuses at $D_\mu = 5\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ in compartments of $L = 100\,\mathrm{nm}$, and its long-range coefficient is $D_{\mathrm{M}} = 0.28\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. Then $1/(\kappa L) = 1/0.28 - 1/5 = 3.37\,\mathrm{s\,\mu m^{-2}}$, so $\kappa L = 0.30\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $\kappa = 3\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$: a wall lets through about $6\,\%$ of the flux that free diffusion would carry across a strip of width $L$. The residence time is $\tau \approx (0.1\,\mu\mathrm{m})^2/(4 \times 0.28\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}) \approx 9\,\mathrm{ms}$, in the measured range. A molecule observed at 30 frames per second (33 ms per frame) has already crossed several compartments between frames and seems to diffuse slowly and freely; only much faster imaging reveals the two regimes.

Compartmentalization has consequences for signaling. A receptor that binds its ligand and assembles a large cytoplasmic complex, or that oligomerizes, crosses the fences much less often than a monomer (oligomerization-induced trapping), so activated receptors and their partners stay together in a small area, which increases the rate of their encounters ([7.3.18](/7.3.18)).

## 4.6. Asymmetry and Lateral Heterogeneity

### 4.6.1. The Two Leaflets Differ

**Definition 4.6.1 (Membrane Asymmetry)** **Membrane asymmetry** is the difference in composition and function between the two leaflets of a membrane and the fixed orientation of its proteins: every copy of a given transmembrane protein faces the same way, glycans are found only on the noncytosolic face, and the leaflets contain different lipids.

The lipid asymmetry of the plasma membrane is described in [7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.1.3): in human erythrocytes about three-quarters of the PC and most sphingomyelin are in the outer leaflet, about four-fifths of the PE and virtually all the PS and phosphoinositides in the inner one. Freeze-fracture shows protein asymmetry directly: the fracture face of the cytoplasmic leaflet (the P face, protoplasmic) carries several times more particles than that of the outer leaflet (the E face, exoplasmic), because most transmembrane proteins remain attached to the cytoplasmic half.

The asymmetry has functions on both sides. On the cytoplasmic side, **protein kinase C** requires PS and diacylglycerol to bind the membrane, phosphoinositides are phosphorylated by **phosphoinositide 3-kinases** to recruit proteins with PH domains, and PI(4,5)P₂ is cleaved into second messengers ([7.1.14](/7.1.14)). On the outside, the absence of PS is a signal of health: its exposure, when **scramblases** are activated and **flippases** inactivated, marks an apoptotic cell for engulfment ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)) and provides the surface for coagulation on activated platelets. The asymmetry is made in the ER and kept through the secretory pathway, because a vesicle that buds from one membrane and fuses with another preserves the orientation of its components: the luminal leaflet of the ER becomes the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane ([7.3.6](/7.3.6)).

### 4.6.2. Phase Separation and Rafts

In giant unilamellar vesicles made of a single lipid or of PC and sphingomyelin, fluorescent probes are spread evenly (or separate into a gel and a fluid phase); in vesicles of PC, sphingomyelin and cholesterol in a $1{:}1{:}1$ ratio, they separate below a miscibility temperature into round domains of a liquid-ordered phase, rich in sphingomyelin and cholesterol, in a liquid-disordered phase rich in unsaturated PC. The domains are round because their boundary has a line tension, the two-dimensional analog of **surface tension**, and they coalesce like droplets. This **liquid–liquid phase separation** in two dimensions is the physical basis of the **lipid raft** of [7.1.13](/7.1.13#def-13.2.3).

Plasma membranes behave differently. Giant vesicles blebbed from the plasma membranes of living cells separate into two liquid phases only below about $15$–$25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and at physiological temperature their composition lies just above a critical point of miscibility. Near such a point a mixture is homogeneous on average but contains fluctuating, short-lived domains whose size grows as the critical point is approached. A membrane with this composition can be tipped into forming larger, stable domains by small changes: cross-linking of raft proteins by antibodies or multivalent ligands, binding to the cortex, or changes in cholesterol. This picture reconciles the old evidence that rafts exist (detergent-resistant membranes, cholesterol-dependent clustering of GPI-anchored proteins, enrichment of sphingolipids and cholesterol in the Golgi-derived carriers that deliver them to the apical surface of epithelial cells) with the failure to see stable micrometer-sized rafts in resting cells. Caveolae ([7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.2.4)) are a stable, protein-defined form of raft membrane.

### 4.6.3. Membrane Domains of Polarized Cells

Many cells keep different parts of a continuous plasma membrane different for their whole lives.

**Definition 4.6.2 (Membrane Domain)** A **membrane domain** is a region of a continuous membrane with a characteristic composition of lipids and proteins that differs from the composition of neighboring regions and is maintained by restricting the lateral diffusion of its components, by continual targeted delivery and removal, or both.

The mammalian sperm is a striking example. Monoclonal antibodies against surface molecules of guinea pig sperm label the anterior head, the posterior head or the tail, but not two of these; the head domains face the egg and the acrosomal reaction, the tail domain carries the machinery of motility. The domains are separated by ring-shaped diffusion barriers at their boundaries. In epithelial cells, tight junctions separate an apical domain, facing the lumen, from a basolateral domain facing the tissue, and prevent the diffusion of proteins and of the lipids of the outer leaflet between them ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)); transport across epithelia depends on this separation ([7.3.5](/7.3.5#sec-5.5)). Neurons keep axonal and somatodendritic proteins apart by a barrier at the axon initial segment, a dense array of ankyrin-G, βIV-spectrin and anchored channels, and the primary cilium is separated from the rest of the plasma membrane by a transition zone.

Cells restrict the movement of membrane molecules in four ways: by self-assembly of proteins into large aggregates (bacteriorhodopsin forms two-dimensional crystals, the purple membrane), by tethering to assemblies inside the cell (the cortex) or outside it (the extracellular matrix), by binding to proteins on the surface of another cell, and by diffusion barriers such as tight junctions.

### 4.6.4. The Erythrocyte Membrane Skeleton

The erythrocyte membrane, the only membrane of a mammalian red cell, was the first whose cytoskeleton was taken apart molecule by molecule, because ghosts are easy to obtain in large amounts. A red cell, $8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ across, must squeeze through capillaries and splenic slits of $2$–$3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ about half a million times during its 120-day life. A bilayer alone stretches by only $2$–$4\,\%$ before it ruptures and has no resistance to shear; the cell's deformability and its recovery of shape come from a protein network on the cytoplasmic face.

**Definition 4.6.3 (Spectrin)** **Spectrin** is a long, flexible cytoskeletal protein built of α and β chains, each a series of three-helix-bundle spectrin repeats, which wind side by side into an antiparallel heterodimer about $100\,\mathrm{nm}$ long; two heterodimers join head to head into a tetramer about $200\,\mathrm{nm}$ long. In erythrocytes, spectrin tetramers linked at their ends by short actin filaments form the **membrane skeleton**, a network on the cytoplasmic face of the plasma membrane that is attached to the bilayer through transmembrane proteins.

The erythrocyte skeleton is a nearly hexagonal network whose nodes are junctional complexes, each a short actin filament of $13$–$15$ subunits (about $37\,\mathrm{nm}$, capped by tropomodulin and adducin and stabilized by tropomyosin) bound by the tail ends of about six spectrin tetramers and by protein 4.1. In the intact cell the tetramers are not stretched: their ends are only $60$–$80\,\mathrm{nm}$ apart, a third of their contour length, so the network behaves like a two-dimensional net of entropic springs that can be extended reversibly when the cell is deformed. Two kinds of attachment hold the network to the bilayer, the "vertical" linkages: near the middle of each tetramer, ankyrin binds β-spectrin and the cytoplasmic domain of band 3, the anion exchanger of [7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.4.2), with protein 4.2; at the junctional complexes, protein 4.1 binds glycophorin C. The "horizontal" linkages are those within the network: spectrin dimer–dimer association and spectrin–actin–4.1 binding. The shear modulus of the composite membrane, about $6\,\mu\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, is very small, a red cell is easily deformed; but its area expansion modulus, set by the bilayer, is about $0.4\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, so its area is nearly fixed. A red cell can change shape but not surface area, which is why its biconcave shape, with about $40\,\%$ more surface than a sphere of the same volume, is essential.

**Example 4.6.1** Hereditary spherocytosis, the most common inherited hemolytic anemia in people of northern European descent (about 1 in 2000), is caused by mutations in ankyrin (about half of the cases), band 3, β-spectrin, α-spectrin or protein 4.2. Each weakens the vertical attachment of the skeleton to the bilayer. Patches of bilayer not held by the skeleton are released as small vesicles, so the cell gradually loses surface area while keeping its volume; it becomes a sphere, the shape with the least area for its volume, and can no longer deform. Spherocytes are osmotically fragile (a sphere cannot swell without stretching its membrane, so they lyse in mildly hypotonic saline, the basis of the osmotic fragility test, [7.3.5](/7.3.5#sec-5.3)) and are trapped and destroyed in the spleen, causing anemia, jaundice and gallstones; removing the spleen relieves the anemia, although the cells remain spherical. In hereditary elliptocytosis the horizontal links are defective, the network fails to recover its shape after shear, and the cells become permanently elongated.

Nucleated cells have spectrin, ankyrin and protein 4.1 homologs in a much denser and dynamic cortex built mainly of actin filaments and myosin ([7.3.13](/7.3.13)). The cortex supports the plasma membrane, anchors membrane proteins in specialized domains (ankyrin-G at the axon initial segment and at nodes of Ranvier), forms the fences of hop diffusion, and, through its tension, governs the shape of the cell.

## 4.7. Membrane Shape and Mechanics

### 4.7.1. The Energy of Bending

A fluid bilayer resists stretching strongly and shear not at all, so the deformation that cells most often impose on membranes is bending. Its energy depends only on the local curvature of the surface. At each point the surface has two principal curvatures $c_1$ and $c_2$ (reciprocal radii); their mean $H = (c_1 + c_2)/2$ is the mean curvature and their product $K = c_1c_2$ the Gaussian curvature.

**Definition 4.7.1 (Bending Rigidity and Spontaneous Curvature)** The **bending rigidity** $\kappa$ (bending modulus) of a membrane is the elastic constant of its bending energy, and its **spontaneous curvature** $c_0$ is the curvature at which the bending energy is zero; $c_0 \neq 0$ when the two leaflets differ in composition or when molecules that prefer one curvature are bound to one face.

**Formula 4.7.1 (Helfrich Energy)** The bending energy of a closed membrane surface $S$ is

$$
E = \frac{\kappa}{2}\oint_S (2H - c_0)^2\,\mathrm{d}A + \bar\kappa\oint_S K\,\mathrm{d}A,
$$
where $\bar\kappa$ is the Gaussian (saddle-splay) modulus (Wolfgang Helfrich, 1973). By the Gauss–Bonnet theorem $\oint_S K\,\mathrm{d}A = 4\pi(1 - g)$ for a surface of genus $g$ ($4\pi$ for a sphere), so the second term changes only when the topology changes, at fission or fusion.

For phospholipid bilayers $\kappa \approx 10$–$25\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ ($0.4$–$1 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$); cholesterol and saturated chains stiffen a bilayer, and the plasma membrane with its cortex is effectively stiffer. These numbers have two direct consequences.

**Proposition 4.7.1** The bending energy (first term of Formula 4.7.1) of a spherical vesicle with $c_0 = 0$ is $8\pi\kappa$, independent of its radius. With a spontaneous curvature $c_0$, a sphere of radius $R$ has energy $2\pi\kappa(2 - c_0R)^2$, which is minimal (zero) at $R = 2/c_0$.

**Proof** On a sphere of radius $R$, $c_1 = c_2 = 1/R$, so $2H = 2/R$, and the area is $4\pi R^2$. The integrand is constant, so $E = \frac{\kappa}{2}(2/R - c_0)^2 \cdot 4\pi R^2 = 2\pi\kappa(2 - c_0R)^2$; with $c_0 = 0$ this is $8\pi\kappa$. $\square$

With $\kappa = 20\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$, making a vesicle out of a flat membrane costs about $500\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$, the free energy of hydrolysis of some 25 molecules of ATP, whatever the vesicle's size. A cell cannot rely on thermal fluctuations to make vesicles; the energy comes from the binding of coat proteins, each contributing a few $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ and together imposing a spontaneous curvature that selects the vesicle radius ([7.3.8](/7.3.8)). A cylindrical tube of radius $R$ and length $\ell$ (with $c_1 = 1/R$, $c_2 = 0$) costs $\pi\kappa\ell/R$; held against a membrane tension $\sigma$, which adds $2\pi\sigma R\ell$, a tether pulled from a cell by an optical trap has the equilibrium radius and force

$$
R_{\mathrm{t}} = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2\sigma}}, \qquad f = 2\pi\sqrt{2\kappa\sigma}.
$$
Tether forces of $5$–$30\,\mathrm{pN}$ measured on many cell types give effective tensions of $10^{-5}$–$10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, a hundred times less than the lytic tension of a bilayer; cells regulate this tension by adding membrane through exocytosis, by flattening caveolae and by remodeling the cortex, and use it to coordinate protrusion at the front with retraction at the back during cell migration ([7.3.15](/7.3.15)).

### 4.7.2. How Cells Bend Membranes

Cells produce curvature by five mechanisms, which often act together (the molecular examples are in [7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.2.5)).

| Mechanism | How it creates curvature | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Lipid composition | cone-shaped lipids (PE, cardiolipin, diacylglycerol) favor negative curvature, inverted cones (lysophospholipids, PI(4,5)P₂ with its large head group) positive curvature of the leaflet that contains them; flippases create curvature by moving lipid from one leaflet to the other | tubules of the Golgi; budding in yeast |
| Wedging | amphipathic helices insert into one leaflet and expand it | epsin (ENTH domain), Arf1 and Sar1 in coat formation, N-BAR proteins |
| Scaffolding | rigid, curved protein domains bind the surface and impose their shape | BAR domains (concave), I-BAR domains (negative curvature in filopodia), dynamin helices |
| Coats and crowding | polymerizing coats form curved lattices; dense protein crowding on one face generates lateral pressure | clathrin, COPI, COPII ([7.3.8](/7.3.8)); ESCRT-III spirals bend membranes away from the cytoplasm ([7.3.10](/7.3.10)) |
| Cytoskeletal forces | polymerizing filaments push, motors pull membrane tubes | lamellipodia and filopodia ([7.3.13](/7.3.13)), kinesin-pulled ER tubules |

Curvature is also read. Proteins with amphipathic helices whose hydrophobic face is poorly packed (ALPS motifs) bind only to strongly curved membranes, where packing defects between lipid head groups are frequent; BAR domains bind preferentially to membranes whose curvature matches their own. Curvature therefore sorts proteins and lipids between a vesicle and its parent membrane, one of the physical bases of protein sorting ([7.3.6](/7.3.6)).

## Questions

1. Gorter and Grendel's two errors (incomplete extraction, shrunken cell area) roughly cancelled. Suppose acetone extracted $70\,\%$ of the lipid and the dried cells had $70\,\%$ of the true surface area. What ratio would they have measured if the membrane were a bilayer whose lipid covered the whole surface? Modern data say that proteins occupy about $25\,\%$ of the erythrocyte membrane area. What ratio would a complete extraction and a correct cell area give?

   **Answer** With complete extraction and correct area the ratio would be $2$ if lipid covered the whole surface. With $70\,\%$ extraction the monolayer area is $0.7 \times 2 = 1.4$ times the true area, divided by a measured cell area of $0.7$: ratio $1.4/0.7 = 2.0$, the two errors cancel. If proteins occupy $25\,\%$ of the area, the lipid covers only $75\,\%$ of each leaflet, and a correct measurement would give $2 \times 0.75 = 1.5$; the bilayer conclusion still holds, because the ratio is clearly above 1, but the number 2 was partly luck.

2. In a Frye–Edidin experiment at $37\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, half of the heterokaryons (radius $7.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) are mixed after $15\,\mathrm{min}$, with "mixed" meaning that one hemisphere holds less than $60\,\%$ of the label. Estimate $D$. How would the time change for a cell of twice the radius, and what would you expect at $15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$?

   **Answer** The fraction in the original hemisphere decays mainly through the $l = 1$ mode: $f(t) - \tfrac12 \approx \tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac12 \mathrm{e}^{-2Dt/R^2}$, since the coefficient of $P_1$ for a hemispherical start is $a_1 = \tfrac34$ and $\int_0^1 P_1\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12$; so $f(t) \approx \tfrac12 + \tfrac38\mathrm{e}^{-2Dt/R^2}$ (higher odd modes add a little at early times). Setting $f = 0.6$ gives $\mathrm{e}^{-2Dt/R^2} = 0.27$, $2Dt/R^2 = 1.3$, $D = 1.3 \times 56\,\mu\mathrm{m^2}/(2 \times 900\,\mathrm{s}) \approx 0.04\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. The time scales as $R^2$, so a cell of twice the radius needs four times as long (about an hour). At $15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the membrane approaches its ordered state and mixing essentially stops, as observed: the temperature dependence is much steeper than the factor $T/\eta$ of the Stokes–Einstein or Saffman–Delbrück relations would predict for a normal liquid.

3. Single-particle tracking of a transmembrane protein at $25\,\mathrm{kHz}$ gives $D_\mu = 2\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ over the first $0.1\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $D_{\mathrm{M}} = 0.05\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ over seconds, with a mean residence of $50\,\mathrm{ms}$. Estimate the compartment size and the permeability of the walls. After treatment with latrunculin, $D_{\mathrm{M}}$ rises to $0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. Interpret.

   **Answer** From $\tau \approx L^2/(4D_{\mathrm{M}})$, $L \approx \sqrt{4 \times 0.05 \times 0.05}\,\mu\mathrm{m} = 0.1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. From Formula 4.5.1, $1/(\kappa L) = 1/0.05 - 1/2 = 19.5\,\mathrm{s\,\mu m^{-2}}$, so $\kappa L \approx 0.051\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $\kappa \approx 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Depolymerizing actin removes or loosens the fences, increasing the compartment size or the wall permeability: with $D_{\mathrm{M}} = 0.5$, $\kappa L = 1/(2 - 0.5) = 0.67\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, a thirteenfold increase, for example larger compartments with more permeable walls. The remaining factor of four relative to $D_\mu$ reflects residual barriers, crowding and binding.

4. Explain why patients with hereditary spherocytosis have red cells that lyse at a higher NaCl concentration than normal cells in the osmotic fragility test, although their membranes are not intrinsically weaker. (Use the fact that the area of a red cell can increase by only about $3\,\%$ before lysis.)

   **Answer** In hypotonic saline the cells take up water and swell at nearly constant surface area until they become spheres; beyond that point further swelling stretches the bilayer, which ruptures at about $3\,\%$ area strain. A normal biconcave cell has about $40\,\%$ more area than a sphere of its volume, so it can swell considerably (to about $1.7$ times its volume) before it becomes a sphere, which requires a large drop in external osmolarity. A spherocyte has lost membrane by vesiculation and is already nearly spherical, so a small swelling, at a smaller dilution (higher NaCl concentration), brings it to the lytic limit. The defect lies in the ratio of area to volume, not in the strength of the bilayer.

5. A coat protein lattice imposes a spontaneous curvature $c_0 = 1/30\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}$ on the membrane it covers ($\kappa = 20\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$). (a) What vesicle radius minimizes the bending energy? (b) How much energy does the coat save, compared with an uncoated membrane, for a vesicle of that radius? (c) Why does the Gaussian term of Formula 4.7.1 not enter this comparison but matter for the final fission step?

   **Answer** (a) $R = 2/c_0 = 60\,\mathrm{nm}$. (b) The uncoated vesicle costs $8\pi\kappa \approx 500\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$; the coated one costs $2\pi\kappa(2 - c_0R)^2 = 0$, so the coat provides about $500\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$, which must come from the binding energy of the coat subunits (some hundreds of molecules, a few $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ each) and from the energy of GTP or ATP used to assemble and remove them. (c) The Gaussian term $\bar\kappa\oint K\,\mathrm{d}A = 4\pi\bar\kappa(1 - g)$ is the same for any closed shape of a given topology, so it does not depend on the radius or shape of a bud still connected to the parent membrane; it changes when the neck breaks and the bud becomes a separate closed surface, so it contributes to the energy of fission (and fusion), which is why fission needs dedicated machinery such as dynamin ([7.3.8](/7.3.8)).
