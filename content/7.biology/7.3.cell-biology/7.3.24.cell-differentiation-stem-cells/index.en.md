A human body contains about $3.7 \times 10^{13}$ cells of several hundred types, almost all with the same genome, all descended from one fertilized egg. How do cells with the same DNA become different, and how do they remain different through many divisions? This chapter answers these questions at the level of the cell, using the tools of the previous chapters: gene regulation ([7.2.12](/7.2.12)–[7.2.15](/7.2.15)), the signaling pathways that carry information between cells ([7.3.18](/7.3.18)–[7.3.19](/7.3.19)), asymmetric cell division ([7.3.21](/7.3.21)), and cell junctions and niches ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)). It also introduces the cells that keep adult tissues supplied with new differentiated cells, the stem cells, and the methods by which differentiated cells can be returned to a pluripotent state. Much of the logic is quantitative: a cell's identity is a stable state of a regulatory network, transitions between states are switches, and timing comes from delayed negative feedback, so the chapter uses the language of bistability and oscillators from [7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.4). How these processes produce the body plan of an embryo, its axes, germ layers and segments, is the subject of developmental biology ([7.5](/7.5)); the immune system's own differentiation programs belong to [7.15](/7.15).

## 24.1. Cell Differentiation

**Definition 24.1.1 (Cell Differentiation)** **Cell differentiation** is the process by which a cell acquires a specialized and stable pattern of gene expression, and with it the structure and function of a particular cell type, different from that of the cell from which it arose.

Differentiation is the basis of multicellular life, but not its monopoly: the bacterium *Bacillus subtilis* forms spores when starved, *Caulobacter crescentus* divides into a stalked and a swimming cell, and the budding yeast forms spores after meiosis. In animals and plants it has five general features:

- It is a change in gene expression. Different cell types contain largely the same DNA but different sets of RNAs and proteins: a typical cell expresses about 10 000–12 000 of the 20 000 protein-coding genes, of which a few thousand housekeeping genes are expressed everywhere and the others, including those for the specialized proteins (hemoglobin in erythroblasts, crystallins in the lens, keratins in the epidermis), in particular cell types. Single-cell RNA sequencing now classifies cells by their transcriptomes and finds many more types and states than the roughly 200 named by histology.
- Changes in protein composition change structure and function, and altering the composition alters the cell type.
- It is progressive. A cell is first specified (it will follow a fate if left alone in a neutral environment) and then determined or committed (it follows the fate even when moved to a different environment) before differences in its proteins become visible; transplantation experiments distinguish the stages ([7.5](/7.5)).
- It is stable and heritable. Terminally differentiated cells often stop dividing (neurons, skeletal muscle fibers, keratinocytes of the outer epidermis), but differentiated cells that do divide (hepatocytes, fibroblasts, pancreatic β cells) pass their state to their daughters.
- It is initiated by extracellular signals and by the asymmetric inheritance of cytoplasmic components, and maintained by intracellular mechanisms (24.3–24.4).

Gene expression can be controlled at six levels: transcription ([7.2.12](/7.2.12)–[7.2.15](/7.2.15)), RNA processing ([7.2.16](/7.2.16)–[7.2.17](/7.2.17)), RNA transport and localization, translation ([7.2.20](/7.2.20)), mRNA degradation ([7.2.18](/7.2.18)) and protein activity. All are used in differentiation, but most cell-type differences start with transcription.

## 24.2. Genomic Equivalence

The principle of **genomic equivalence** ([7.3.1](/7.3.1)) states that differentiated cells keep the complete genome of the zygote. Three kinds of experiment established it.

1. Plant regeneration. Single cells from the phloem of a carrot root, cultured in suspension, formed embryo-like structures and complete fertile plants (Steward, 1958). In tissue culture the ratio of two hormones decides what a mass of undifferentiated cells (a callus) makes: much auxin and little cytokinin gives roots, the reverse gives shoots (Skoog and Miller, 1957). Many differentiated plant cells are therefore totipotent, which is used to propagate and engineer crops.
2. Nuclear transfer in amphibians. Nuclei from blastula cells of the frog *Rana pipiens*, transplanted into enucleated eggs, supported normal development (Briggs and King, 1952), but nuclei from later stages did so less and less often. John Gurdon (1962) transplanted nuclei from the intestinal epithelium of feeding tadpoles of *Xenopus laevis* into eggs whose own nuclei had been destroyed by ultraviolet light: about 1–2 % developed into swimming tadpoles, and some of these into fertile adult frogs. Nuclei of keratinized skin cells of adult frogs supported development to tadpoles but not to adults (Gurdon, Laskey and Reeves, 1975).
3. Nuclear transfer in mammals.

**Definition 24.2.1 (Somatic Cell Nuclear Transfer)** **Somatic cell nuclear transfer** (SCNT) is the transfer of the nucleus of a somatic cell into an oocyte from which the chromosomes have been removed, followed by activation of the reconstructed egg, so that it develops into an embryo whose nuclear genome is that of the donor cell; transfer of such an embryo to a surrogate mother can produce a clone of the donor animal.

The sheep Dolly, born on 5 July 1996 at the Roslin Institute, was the first mammal cloned from an adult cell (Wilmut and colleagues, 1997). The donor was a mammary epithelial cell of a six-year-old Finn Dorset ewe, cultured and starved of serum so that it entered **G0 phase**; it was fused by an electric pulse with an enucleated oocyte of a Scottish Blackface ewe, and of 277 reconstructed eggs, 29 developed to embryos that could be transferred and one became a lamb. Mice, cattle, cats, dogs and other mammals followed; the first primates cloned by SCNT, two crab-eating macaques, were born in 2018 at the Institute of Neuroscience in Shanghai (Liu and colleagues, 2018). Efficiency remains low, a few percent at best, and many clones have abnormal placentas and other defects, because the egg cytoplasm does not completely reset the chromatin of the donor nucleus; regions of the genome marked by H3K9 trimethylation are particularly resistant, and removing this mark with the demethylase Kdm4d improves cloning.

![Three experiments showing that differentiated cells keep a complete genome. A: nuclear transfer in *Xenopus* (Gurdon 1962): the nucleus of a tadpole intestinal cell, injected into an egg whose nucleus has been destroyed by ultraviolet light, can direct the development of a tadpole and, in some cases, of a fertile frog. The experiments support two conclusions: differentiation does not, as a rule, involve irreversible changes in the DNA; and the cytoplasm of the egg contains factors that can reset the activity of a nucleus. The rule has exceptions that are instructive:

| Change in the genome | Cells | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Programmed rearrangement | B and T lymphocytes (**V(D)J recombination**, [7.2.9](/7.2.9#sec-9.7)) | mice cloned from mature lymphocytes carry the same rearranged antigen-receptor genes in all their cells (Hochedlinger and Jaenisch, 2002) |
| Loss of the nucleus | mammalian red blood cells, lens fiber cells, keratinocytes of the outer epidermis, platelets (cell fragments) | terminal, nonreversible |
| Polyploidy and endoreplication | megakaryocytes, many hepatocytes, plant cells, *Drosophila* salivary glands (polytene chromosomes, [7.3.17](/7.3.17)) | more copies, same sequences |

| Chromatin diminution | somatic cells of *Ascaris* and some copepods | parts of the genome are discarded |
| Somatic mutation | all dividing cells (tens of mutations per cell per year) | mosaicism; the basis of cancer ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)) |

## 24.3. Initiating Differentiation

Sister cells can be made different in two ways: they can inherit different components from their mother (intrinsic asymmetry), or they can be born alike and then be exposed to different environments (extrinsic asymmetry). Four mechanisms are common in development: asymmetric segregation of cytoplasmic determinants, induction by signals from other cells, lateral inhibition between equivalent cells, and the sorting of cells into separate tissues.

### 24.3.1. Cytoplasmic Determinants and Asymmetric Division

**Definition 24.3.1 (Cytoplasmic Determinant and Asymmetric Segregation)** A **cytoplasmic determinant** is a molecule, usually an mRNA or a protein, localized in a region of an egg or a cell, that is inherited by the daughter cells formed from that region and influences their fate. **Asymmetric segregation** is the unequal distribution of such determinants between the two daughters of a division.

An **asymmetric cell division** (Definition 21.6.1) needs a polarized mother cell. In the zygote of *Caenorhabditis elegans*, the centrosome brought in by the sperm marks the posterior and triggers a cortical flow that carries the anterior **PAR proteins** (PAR-3, PAR-6 and atypical protein kinase C) away from it, while PAR-1 and PAR-2 occupy the posterior cortex ([7.3.15](/7.3.15#sec-15.4.2)); downstream, the RNA-binding protein MEX-5 forms an anterior-to-posterior gradient, and the posterior P granules, **biomolecular condensates** of RNA and protein that specify the germ line, dissolve in the anterior and condense in the posterior, so that they are inherited by the posterior daughter at each of four successive divisions ([7.2.21](/7.2.21)). In the *Xenopus* egg, the mRNA of the T-box transcription factor VegT is localized in the vegetal hemisphere; VegT activates genes for Nodal-related TGF-β family signals that induce endoderm and mesoderm, whereas the ubiquitin ligase ectodermin, enriched in the animal hemisphere, degrades Smad4 and keeps the ectoderm from responding (Dupont and colleagues, 2005). In *Drosophila* neuroblasts and sensory organ precursors, the protein Numb is segregated to one daughter, where it promotes the endocytosis of Notch and blocks Notch signaling, so that the two daughters adopt different fates.

Initial asymmetries are often tiny and amplified by positive feedback, as in the polarization of budding yeast by Cdc42 (wave pinning, [7.3.15](/7.3.15#sec-15.4.1)): **cell polarity** and asymmetric division use the same symmetry-breaking circuits as cell migration.

### 24.3.2. Induction and the Reading of a Morphogen

**Definition 24.3.2 (Induction)** **Induction** is the process by which a signal from one group of cells (the inducing tissue) changes the developmental fate of another group (the responding tissue), which must be competent to respond.

Inducing signals belong to a few families: the **Wnt signaling pathway**, the TGF-β superfamily, Hedgehog, fibroblast **growth factors** and other receptor tyrosine kinase ligands, and Notch ligands, which act only between cells in contact ([7.3.19](/7.3.19)). When a signal spreads from a source and different concentrations induce different fates, it is called a morphogen, and the cells use the concentration as positional information (Wolpert's French flag model, [7.5](/7.5)). Here the cell-level question is how a cell converts a graded concentration into a discrete fate.

1. Thresholds: target genes with low-affinity enhancers respond only near the source, those with high-affinity enhancers also far away; cooperative binding of several transcription factor molecules makes each response steep (a high **Hill coefficient**, [7.2.22](/7.2.22#sec-22.4)).
2. Counting receptors: in *Xenopus* animal-cap cells exposed to the TGF-β family member activin, about 100 occupied receptors per cell induce the mesodermal gene *Xbra* and about 300 the dorsal mesoderm gene *goosecoid*: a threefold difference in signal selects between fates (Dyson and Gurdon, 1998).
3. Memory: cells respond to the highest concentration they have experienced and keep that response when the concentration later falls (a ratchet), because positive feedback in the response locks it in.
4. Precision: if the morphogen decays from its source exponentially, $c(x) = c_0\mathrm{e}^{-x/\lambda}$, a fate boundary at threshold $\theta$ lies at $x_\theta = \lambda\ln(c_0/\theta)$, and a relative error $\delta c/c$ in reading the concentration shifts the boundary by $\delta x = \lambda\,\delta c/c$. For the Bicoid gradient of the *Drosophila* embryo, $\lambda \approx 100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, so 10 % noise in reading would place a boundary within about $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, two nuclear spacings; real boundaries are sharper, which requires averaging over time and neighbouring nuclei.

Sequential induction builds complex patterns: a first signal divides a tissue into two regions, the boundary between them becomes a new signaling centre, and so on, so that cells acquire their identities step by step.

### 24.3.3. Lateral Inhibition

**Definition 24.3.3 (Lateral Inhibition)** **Lateral inhibition** is a mechanism in which a cell that begins to adopt a particular fate inhibits its immediate neighbours from adopting the same fate, so that a group of initially equivalent cells resolves into a spaced pattern of cells of two fates.

The **Notch signaling pathway** (Definition 19.3.5) implements lateral inhibition. Delta on one cell activates Notch on its neighbours; Notch signaling, through the Hes repressors, inhibits the expression of Delta and of the proneural genes in the receiving cell. Each cell thus reduces the Delta production of its neighbours, a double-negative loop between neighbours that amplifies any difference: the cell that happens to make a little more Delta inhibits its neighbours more, receives less inhibition in return, and wins. In *Drosophila*, neural precursors are chosen this way from proneural clusters of the ectoderm, and the sensory bristles of the thorax are spaced; in vertebrates, lateral inhibition chooses neurons among neural progenitors, hair cells among supporting cells in the inner ear and secretory cells (high Atoh1) among absorptive cells (high Hes1) in the intestine. In the sensory organ lineage of the fly, lateral inhibition picks the precursor and asymmetric segregation of Numb then makes its daughters different: the two mechanisms are combined.

Collier, Monk, Maini and Lewis (1996) gave a minimal model. For cell $i$ with Notch activity $N_i$ and Delta level $D_i$,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = f\bigl(\langle D\rangle_i\bigr) - N_i, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}D_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = v\bigl[g(N_i) - D_i\bigr], \qquad f(x) = \frac{x^2}{a + x^2}, \quad g(x) = \frac{1}{1 + b x^2},
\tag{24.1}
$$

where $\langle D\rangle_i$ is the mean Delta level of the neighbours, $f$ is increasing (Delta activates Notch in the neighbour) and $g$ decreasing (Notch represses Delta in its own cell), and time is measured in lifetimes of Notch activity. All cells start at the uniform steady state $(N^*, D^*)$. A small perturbation in which neighbours deviate in opposite directions grows if the loop gain is large enough: linearizing, a pattern whose neighbour average is $\kappa$ times the cell's own deviation is unstable when $f'(D^*)\,g'(N^*)\,\kappa > 1$. On a sheet of hexagonal cells the most negative possible value of $\kappa$ is $-1/2$ (one cell in three high, the others low), so the uniform state breaks when

$$
f'(D^*)\,\bigl\lvert g'(N^*)\bigr\rvert > 2 .
\tag{24.2}
$$

**Example 24.3.1** With $a = 0.01$ and $b = 100$, the uniform steady state is $D^* \approx 0.074$, $N^* \approx 0.354$, and $f'(D^*) \approx 6.2$, $g'(N^*) \approx -0.39$, so $f'\lvert g'\rvert \approx 2.4 > 2$: the uniform state is unstable, and noise grows into a salt-and-pepper pattern. The product exceeds the threshold only slightly, which is why the pattern takes several tens of time units to emerge; weaker feedback (smaller $b$) would leave the cells uniform.

(24.1) on a sheet of 14 × 12 hexagonal cells with periodic boundaries (a = 0.01, b = 100, v = 1), starting from the uniform state with 1 % noise: after 120 time units the sheet has resolved into isolated high-Delta cells (dark; future neural precursors or hair cells) surrounded by low-Delta cells. Cells that have become different must also stay together with their own kind. Cells sort out by their adhesion molecules and cortical tension, as described for cadherins and the differential adhesion hypothesis in [7.3.23](/7.3.23#sec-23.3.2); boundaries between compartments are sharpened by sorting and maintained by signaling at the boundary.

## 24.4. Maintaining the Differentiated State

A transient signal can have permanent consequences: the cell remembers. This section describes how.

### 24.4.1. Master Regulators

**Definition 24.4.1 (Master Regulator)** A **master regulator** is a transcription factor whose expression is sufficient to initiate a program of differentiation: it activates, directly or through other regulators, many of the genes characteristic of a cell type, and its ectopic expression can convert other cells, or cause ectopic structures, of that type.

The muscle regulator MyoD was found in a search for the genes switched on when the DNA demethylating drug 5-azacytidine converts mouse C3H10T1/2 fibroblasts into muscle, fat and cartilage cells; a single MyoD cDNA, expressed in fibroblasts, turned them into myoblasts that fused into contractile myotubes (Davis, Weintraub and Lassar, 1987). MyoD and its relatives Myf5, myogenin and MRF4 are **basic helix–loop–helix** proteins that bind E-box sequences with E proteins; mice lacking both MyoD and Myf5 make no skeletal muscle. In *Drosophila*, expression of the transcription factor Eyeless (the homologue of vertebrate Pax6) in the precursors of legs, wings or antennae produced ectopic eyes on these appendages, and mouse Pax6 did the same (Halder, Callaerts and Gehring, 1995). Eyeless acts at the top of a cascade: it activates the genes *eyes absent*, *sine oculis* and *dachshund*, which activate each other and Eyeless in a network of positive feedback loops, and the network then activates the thousands of genes needed to build an eye. Other master regulators include GATA1 (erythroid cells), PU.1 (myeloid cells), C/EBPα (macrophages), Pdx1 (pancreas) and Sox9 (cartilage).

### 24.4.2. Self-Sustaining Circuits and Cell Memory

**Definition 24.4.2 (Cell Memory)** **Cell memory** is the persistence of a cell's state of gene expression or of determination after the signal that induced it has disappeared, including its transmission to the daughter cells through cell divisions.

The simplest memory device is a transcription factor that activates its own gene. Let $x$ be its concentration:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = s + \alpha\frac{x^n}{K^n + x^n} - \gamma x ,
\tag{24.3}
$$

where $s$ is the production induced by an external signal, $\alpha$ the maximal rate of self-activation with cooperativity $n$ and threshold $K$, and $\gamma$ the rate of degradation and dilution. With $n \ge 2$ and enough self-activation the system shows **bistability** (Definition 18.4.2): for $s = 0$ there are two stable states, off and on, separated by a threshold, and a pulse of $s$ that carries $x$ beyond the threshold switches the cell on for good, with **hysteresis** ([7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.4.3)). MyoD activates its own gene and that of myogenin, which activates MyoD; Eyeless and its targets form loops; and two master regulators that inhibit each other and activate themselves, like GATA1 and PU.1 in blood progenitors (24.7.4), make a switch between two fates. A factor present in a cell is divided between the daughters at mitosis and resynthesized, so the state is inherited as long as each daughter receives enough of it.

### 24.4.3. Chromatin States and DNA Methylation

Memory is also stored in chromatin ([7.2.15](/7.2.15)). Polycomb complexes keep developmental genes silent in cells where they should not be expressed: PRC2 trimethylates histone H3 at Lys27, and one of its subunits binds the mark it makes, so that the mark is copied onto the new nucleosomes after replication (a read–write feedback; a model of this memory is in [7.2.15](/7.2.15#sec-15.6.4)); Trithorax complexes maintain the active state with H3K4 methylation. **DNA methylation** at CpG sites is copied semiconservatively: after replication, the hemimethylated CpGs are recognized by UHRF1, which recruits the maintenance methyltransferase DNMT1 to methylate the new strand ([7.2.15](/7.2.15#sec-15.7.2)), and methylated promoters (outside **CpG islands**, and at imprinted genes) stay silent. The conversion of fibroblasts by 5-azacytidine (24.4.1) shows that demethylation can unlock silent programs. In female mammals, **X-chromosome inactivation** ([7.3.17](/7.3.17)) is a chromosome-wide example: the choice of which X to silence is made once in the early embryo and then inherited by all descendants of each cell, making females mosaics. These mechanisms are part of **epigenetics**: heritable states of gene activity that do not depend on changes in DNA sequence.

### 24.4.4. Combinatorial Control

**Definition 24.4.3 (Combinatorial Control)** **Combinatorial control** is the specification of gene expression, and thereby of cell type, by the combination of regulatory proteins present in a cell rather than by any single one: a gene is expressed only when the right combination of activators is present and repressors are absent, so that $n$ regulators that are each on or off can in principle distinguish $2^n$ states.

Combinatorial control makes differentiation progressive: each step of development adds a regulator or a signal to those already present, so the combination present in a cell records its history (where it was, which signals it received), and the same signal can activate different genes in cells with different histories (the "habits" of signaling pathways, [7.3.19](/7.3.19#sec-19.5.1)). An embryo can be seen as a mass of cells subdivided step by step into regions defined by combinations of regulators ([7.5](/7.5)).

## 24.5. Oscillations and Timing

Positive feedback makes memories; delayed negative feedback makes clocks ([7.3.18](/7.3.18#sec-18.4.5)). Some differentiation programs run on them.

### 24.5.1. The Hes1 Oscillator

The transcriptional repressor Hes1, a Notch target, represses its own gene. In many types of cultured cells, a change of medium starts oscillations of *Hes1* mRNA and protein with a period of about two hours, and the half-lives of both are short, about 24 minutes for the mRNA and 22 minutes for the protein (Hirata and colleagues, 2002). A negative feedback loop with only two short-lived components would settle at a steady state; it oscillates because of the delay between the protein and the effect of its repression on protein synthesis, the time taken to transcribe, splice and export the mRNA and to translate it (Monk, 2003; Lewis, 2003):

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\alpha_m}{1 + \bigl(p(t - \tau)/p_0\bigr)^{n}} - \mu_m m, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}t} = \alpha_p m - \mu_p p,
\tag{24.4}
$$

where $m$ and $p$ are the numbers of mRNA and protein molecules per cell, $\mu_m = \ln 2/24\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$ and $\mu_p = \ln 2/22.3\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$ the degradation rates, $\alpha_m$ and $\alpha_p$ the rates of transcription and translation, $p_0$ the protein level that halves transcription, $n$ the steepness of repression and $\tau$ the delay. For short delays the steady state is stable and perturbations die away in damped oscillations; above a critical delay it loses stability in a **Hopf bifurcation** and the system settles on a **limit cycle** ([2.4](/2.4)). With $p_0 = 100$, $n = 5$ and $\tau = 18.7\,\mathrm{min}$ the period is about $2\,\mathrm{h}$, as measured, and the protein peaks about 20 minutes after the mRNA.

(24.4) with half-lives of 24 min (mRNA) and 22 min (protein), α_m = α_p = 1 per min, p₀ = 100, n = 5 and τ = 18.7 min: mRNA and protein oscillate with a period of about 2 h, the protein lagging. C: period (solid) and protein amplitude (dashed) for delays from 0 to 40 min: below a critical delay of about 16 min the oscillation dies out; above it the period grows with the delay](./plots/hes1-oscillator.py)

The oscillation carries information. In neural progenitors of the mouse, Hes1 and the proneural factors Ascl1 and Neurogenin2 oscillate out of phase, and the progenitors keep proliferating; when Hes1 falls and the proneural factors are expressed steadily, the cells differentiate into neurons. Optogenetic control of Ascl1 showed that oscillating expression maintains proliferation, whereas sustained expression of the same factor drives differentiation (Imayoshi and colleagues, 2013): the same protein gives different instructions depending on its dynamics. Delayed negative feedback also lies behind the pulses of p53 ([7.3.22](/7.3.22)) and of NF-κB ([7.3.19](/7.3.19)).

### 24.5.2. The Segmentation Clock

**Definition 24.5.1 (Segmentation Clock)** The **segmentation clock** is a molecular oscillator in the cells of the presomitic mesoderm of vertebrate embryos, based on delayed negative feedback of Hes/Her transcription factors and coupled to oscillations in the Notch, Wnt and FGF pathways, whose cycles are converted into the periodic pattern of somites: one somite forms at the anterior end of the presomitic mesoderm at each cycle.

The oscillation was discovered as waves of expression of the gene *c-hairy1* sweeping through the presomitic mesoderm of the chick once every 90 minutes, the time taken to form one somite (Palmeirim and colleagues, 1997). The period is about 30 minutes in zebrafish, 90 minutes in chick, 2–3 hours in mouse and about 5 hours in human cells differentiated in culture. The clock is read by a wavefront: a front of maturation, set by opposing gradients of FGF and Wnt (high posteriorly) and retinoic acid (high anteriorly), moves posteriorly as the axis elongates, and cells passing it at a given phase of the clock form a segment boundary (the clock-and-wavefront model, Cooke and Zeeman, 1976). The size of a somite is the distance the front moves in one period, and the number of somites is set by the ratio of the clock period to the duration of axis elongation: corn snakes, with about 315 vertebrae, have a clock about four times faster relative to their growth than mice (Gomez and colleagues, 2008). Notch signaling between neighbouring cells keeps their clocks synchronized; in zebrafish mutants in the Notch pathway, the cells oscillate but drift out of phase, and boundaries become irregular. Somitogenesis itself is a topic of [7.5](/7.5).

### 24.5.3. Circadian Clocks in Cells

Most cells of animals, plants and many microbes contain a circadian clock, a cell-autonomous oscillator with a period close to 24 hours. In mammals it is a transcription–translation feedback loop: the transcription factors CLOCK and BMAL1 activate the *Period* (*Per*) and *Cryptochrome* (*Cry*) genes; PER and CRY proteins accumulate in the cytoplasm, are phosphorylated by casein kinase 1δ/ε, enter the nucleus and repress CLOCK–BMAL1, and are then degraded by ubiquitin ligases (β-TrCP for PER, FBXL3 for CRY), which allows a new cycle. The long period comes from the delays of these modifications and of nuclear entry; mutations in CK1δ or PER2 that change phosphorylation shorten the period and cause familial advanced sleep phase syndrome. The clocks of individual cells are synchronized by a master pacemaker, the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, which is entrained by light detected by melanopsin-containing retinal ganglion cells and controls the release of melatonin from the pineal gland and the timing of body temperature, feeding and hormone secretion ([7.14](/7.14)). The clock gates other cell processes: in regenerating mouse liver, CLOCK–BMAL1 controls the expression of Wee1 and thereby the time of day of **mitosis** (Matsuo and colleagues, 2003). The genetic dissection of the clock in *Drosophila* was recognized by the 2017 Nobel Prize (Hall, Rosbash and Young).

## 24.6. Stem Cells

### 24.6.1. Potency and Self-Renewal

**Definition 24.6.1 (Stem Cell and Self-Renewal)** A **stem cell** is an undifferentiated or partly differentiated cell that can divide, over long periods, both to produce more stem cells like itself and to produce cells that differentiate. **Self-renewal** is the production, at division, of daughter cells that keep the identity and developmental potential of the stem cell, so that the stem-cell population is maintained.

The definition does not require that a stem cell can make many cell types: some stem cells (such as the spermatogonial stem cells of the testis) make only one. What a cell can make is its potency.

**Definition 24.6.2 (Totipotency, Pluripotency and Multipotency)** A cell is **totipotent** if it can give rise to all the cells of an organism, including the extraembryonic tissues of mammals (trophoblast of the placenta); in mammals only the zygote and the blastomeres of the earliest cleavage stages are totipotent, whereas many plant cells are. A cell is **pluripotent** if it can give rise to all the cells of the embryo proper, of all three germ layers and the germ line, but not the trophoblast, like the cells of the inner cell mass and embryonic stem cells. A cell is **multipotent** if it can give rise to several cell types of one lineage or tissue, like hematopoietic stem cells; cells that make only one type are unipotent.

Development can be seen as a progressive loss of potency: zygote (totipotent), epiblast and embryonic stem cells (pluripotent), tissue stem cells (multipotent), progenitors with few fates, and terminally differentiated cells.

### 24.6.2. Niches, Asymmetry and Amplification

A stem cell population can be maintained by two strategies. In the first, each stem cell divides asymmetrically, giving one stem cell and one cell that differentiates (Definition 21.6.1). In the second, divisions are often symmetric and each daughter chooses independently whether to remain a stem cell, so that the population, not each division, is balanced (population asymmetry). The second strategy is flexible: the probabilities can change, to expand the stem cells during growth or repair and to favour differentiation when differentiated cells are lost. In both, the choice depends largely on the cell's surroundings.

**Definition 24.6.3 (Stem-Cell Niche)** A **stem-cell niche** is the specialized microenvironment, made of supporting cells, extracellular matrix and the signals they provide, that maintains stem cells in their undifferentiated, self-renewing state; daughter cells that leave the niche lose these signals and differentiate.

The niche was proposed for hematopoietic stem cells (Schofield, 1978) and first identified at cellular resolution in the *Drosophila* gonads. In the testis, germline stem cells are attached to a cluster of somatic hub cells by E-cadherin; the hub secretes the cytokine Unpaired, which activates the **JAK–STAT pathway** in the stem cells, and the stem cells orient their spindle perpendicular to the hub, so that one daughter remains attached and the other is displaced and differentiates; the mother **centrosome** stays at the hub and is inherited by the stem cell (Yamashita and colleagues, 2007). In the ovary, cap cells secrete the BMP Dpp, which represses the differentiation gene *bag of marbles* in the adjacent stem cells only. In mammals, the stem cells of the intestinal crypt depend on Wnt from neighbouring Paneth cells and from the underlying mesenchyme, which also provides R-spondin and inhibitors of BMP; hematopoietic stem cells depend on stem cell factor and the chemokine CXCL12 made by perivascular stromal cells and endothelial cells of the bone marrow.

**Definition 24.6.4 (Progenitor Cell and Transit-Amplifying Cell)** A **progenitor cell** is a descendant of a stem cell that is committed to one or a few fates and has limited or no capacity for self-renewal. A **transit-amplifying cell** is a progenitor that divides rapidly a limited number of times, amplifying the number of cells produced from each stem-cell division before they differentiate terminally.

Amplification has a quantitative logic. If each daughter that leaves the stem cell compartment divides $k$ more times before differentiating, every stem-cell division that produces one committed daughter yields $2^k$ differentiated cells. In the mouse small intestine, transit-amplifying cells divide about every 12 hours for four or five rounds, so each committed daughter yields 16–32 cells, and the stem cells themselves need to divide only about once a day. Keeping the number of divisions in the long-lived stem cells low limits the accumulation of mutations in the cells that persist for the life of the animal.

### 24.6.3. Hematopoietic Stem Cells

Blood contains many types of cells, all short-lived and all made in the bone marrow of adults:

| Cell | Function | Concentration in human blood (per L) | Life span |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red blood cells (erythrocytes) | transport $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ | $5 \times 10^{12}$ | about 120 days |
| Neutrophils | phagocytose and kill bacteria | $5 \times 10^{9}$ | hours in blood, days in tissues |
| Eosinophils | attack parasites, allergic reactions | $2 \times 10^{8}$ | days |
| Basophils | release histamine | $4 \times 10^{7}$ | days |
| Monocytes | become tissue macrophages and some dendritic cells | $4 \times 10^{8}$ | days in blood, months as macrophages |
| B cells | make antibodies | $3 \times 10^{8}$ | weeks to years |
| T cells | kill infected cells, regulate immune responses | $2 \times 10^{9}$ | weeks to years |
| Natural killer cells | kill infected and tumor cells | $1 \times 10^{8}$ | weeks |
| Platelets (fragments of megakaryocytes) | blood clotting | $3 \times 10^{11}$ | about 8–10 days |

The numbers imply a large production. With about $5\,\mathrm{L}$ of blood, a person has $2.5 \times 10^{13}$ red blood cells; with a life span of 120 days, about $2 \times 10^{11}$ must be made every day, more than two million per second, and about $10^{11}$ neutrophils and $10^{11}$ platelets are made daily as well.

**Definition 24.6.5 (Hematopoietic Stem Cell)** A **hematopoietic stem cell** (HSC) is a multipotent, self-renewing stem cell of the bone marrow (in the fetus, of the liver) that gives rise to all types of blood cells, through progenitors of progressively restricted potential, and can reconstitute the entire blood system of an irradiated host.

Hematopoietic stem cells were identified by transplantation. Mice given a lethal dose of X-rays die within days from the loss of blood production, unless they receive bone marrow cells from a healthy donor. Till and McCulloch (1961) found that the injected cells formed colonies in the spleen, each containing several types of blood cells and derived from a single cell; cells of some colonies could repopulate secondary hosts. Sorting of marrow cells by their surface markers with the **fluorescence-activated cell sorter** ([7.3.3](/7.3.3)) showed that long-term repopulating cells are about 1 in $10^4$–$10^5$ marrow cells, and a single such cell can reconstitute the whole blood system of a mouse (Osawa and colleagues, 1996). Below the HSCs, multipotent progenitors split into a common myeloid progenitor and a common lymphoid progenitor, and the myeloid branch into megakaryocyte–erythroid and granulocyte–macrophage progenitors. Each stage depends on specific signal proteins: erythropoietin, made in the kidney in response to low oxygen, for red cells; thrombopoietin for megakaryocytes; granulocyte and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factors for neutrophils and macrophages; interleukin-7 for lymphocytes. Bone marrow (or mobilized blood and cord blood) transplantation is used to treat leukemias and inherited blood diseases, and the HSCs of the donor reconstitute the patient's blood for life ([7.3.26](/7.3.26), [7.15](/7.15)).

### 24.6.4. The Intestinal Crypt and Neutral Drift

The epithelium of the small intestine is the fastest-renewing tissue of mammals: its cells are made in the crypts, move up the villi and are shed at their tips within 4–5 days. Each crypt of the mouse contains about 14–16 stem cells at its base, slender cells that express the Wnt target gene *Lgr5* and lie between the Paneth cells (Barker and colleagues, 2007). The receptor LGR5 binds R-spondins and removes the ubiquitin ligases RNF43 and ZNRF3, which would otherwise degrade the Wnt receptors, so the stem cells respond to Wnt more strongly than their neighbours ([7.3.19](/7.3.19#sec-19.3.1)). Their descendants become transit-amplifying cells and then absorptive enterocytes or secretory cells (goblet, Paneth and enteroendocrine cells), a choice made by lateral inhibition (24.3.3).

Lineage tracing reveals how the stem cells behave. When a single stem cell is genetically labelled, using a Cre recombinase that is active only in Lgr5 cells and a reporter that can recombine to one of several fluorescent colours (the Confetti reporter; Snippert and colleagues, 2010; [7.3.3](/7.3.3)), its clone either disappears from the crypt or spreads until it fills the crypt, which then becomes monoclonal within a few months. This is what is expected if stem cells are equivalent and compete neutrally for a limited space.

**Definition 24.6.6 (Neutral Drift)** **Neutral drift** is the random fluctuation in the sizes of clones of equivalent stem cells in a niche of limited size, in which the loss of a stem cell (by differentiation or death) is compensated by the division of a neighbour, so that clones are lost or expand by chance until one clone fills the niche (monoclonal conversion).

A simple model treats the $N$ stem cells as a ring at the crypt base. Each is lost at rate $\lambda$ and replaced by the division of one of its two neighbours, chosen at random. A labelled clone then stays contiguous, and its size $n$ changes only at its two boundaries: it grows by one when an unlabelled cell next to it is replaced by a labelled neighbour, and shrinks by one when a labelled boundary cell is replaced by an unlabelled neighbour, each at rate $\lambda$. The clone size is a symmetric random walk, a **Markov chain** absorbed at $n = 0$ (clone lost) and $n = N$ (crypt monoclonal), and the classical results for this walk give

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{monoclonal} \mid n_0 = 1) = \frac{1}{N}, \qquad \mathbb{E}\bigl[T_{\text{fix}} \bigm| \text{fixation}\bigr] = \frac{N^2 - 1}{6\lambda}.
\tag{24.5}
$$

The fixation probability $1/N$ is that of any neutral variant in a population of size $N$, as in population genetics ([7.10](/7.10)). Surviving clones grow, and their sizes spread, in a way that fits the measured clone-size distributions (Lopez-Garcia and colleagues, 2010; Snippert and colleagues, 2010).

**Example 24.6.1** For $N = 16$ and $\lambda = 1\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$, one labelled stem cell takes over its crypt with probability $1/16 \approx 6\,\%$, and the clones that do so need on average $(256 - 1)/6 \approx 42$ days. A mutation that gives a stem cell an advantage changes the walk into a biased one and raises its probability of fixation; mutations in *APC* or *KRAS*, the first steps of many colorectal cancers, make clones that take over crypts more often than neutral ones, and the stem-cell niche thus acts as a filter in which most mutant clones are lost by chance ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)).

### 24.6.5. Other Tissues and Regeneration

Tissues differ in how they are renewed. In the epidermis, basal-layer progenitors divide to produce cells that move up, stop dividing and keratinize; in the tail skin of mice a single population of progenitors with stochastic fates maintains the tissue (Clayton and colleagues, 2007), and the hair follicle bulge contains stem cells that regenerate the follicle and repair the epidermis after wounding. Cultured human epidermal stem cells can regenerate skin: in the child with junctional epidermolysis bullosa mentioned in [7.3.23](/7.3.23#sec-23.4.3), transgenic grafts covered about 80 % of the body, and the new epidermis was maintained by a few long-lived stem-cell clones (Hirsch and colleagues, 2017). Some tissues have no dedicated stem cells and are renewed by division of differentiated cells: hepatocytes divide to restore the liver after two thirds of it are removed, and new pancreatic β cells arise mainly from existing β cells (Dor and colleagues, 2004). Others are hardly renewed: the radiocarbon released by nuclear bomb tests and incorporated into DNA showed that human heart muscle cells are replaced at about 1 % per year at age 25, falling with age (Bergmann and colleagues, 2009). Some animals regenerate whole bodies or organs: planarian flatworms from pluripotent stem cells called neoblasts, a single one of which can restore regeneration in an irradiated worm (Wagner, Wang and Reddien, 2011), and salamanders their limbs from a blastema of dedifferentiated, lineage-restricted cells (Kragl and colleagues, 2009).

### 24.6.6. Organoids

**Definition 24.6.7 (Organoid)** An **organoid** is a three-dimensional structure grown in culture from stem cells (adult tissue stem cells or pluripotent stem cells) that self-organizes into several cell types of an organ, arranged as in the organ and performing some of its functions.

A single Lgr5 stem cell embedded in a gel of basal lamina proteins (Matrigel, [7.3.23](/7.3.23#sec-23.2.5)) and given EGF, the BMP inhibitor Noggin and R-spondin grows into a structure with crypt-like buds and a villus-like lumen containing all the cell types of the intestinal epithelium (Sato and colleagues, 2009). Organoids have since been grown from the stomach, liver, pancreas, kidney, lung, prostate and many tumors, and cerebral organoids from pluripotent stem cells reproduce stages of brain development (Lancaster and colleagues, 2013). They are used to model diseases (cerebral organoids from patients with microcephaly; Zika virus infection of neural progenitors), to test drugs on a patient's own cells (organoids from patients with cystic fibrosis swell in response to forskolin only if their CFTR channel works, which predicts their response to CFTR modulators), and to study tissue biology that cannot be followed in a living animal.

## 24.7. Pluripotent Stem Cells and Reprogramming

### 24.7.1. Embryonic Stem Cells

**Definition 24.7.1 (Embryonic Stem Cell)** An **embryonic stem cell** (ES cell) is a pluripotent cell derived from the inner cell mass of an early blastocyst that can self-renew indefinitely in culture while keeping the ability to differentiate into cells of all three germ layers and the germ line.

Mouse ES cells were derived in 1981 (Evans and Kaufman; Martin) and human ES cells in 1998 (Thomson and colleagues). Mouse ES cells self-renew in the presence of the cytokine LIF, which activates STAT3, or of two inhibitors of the kinases MEK and GSK3 (the 2i condition, Ying and colleagues, 2008); human ES cells, which resemble the later, primed epiblast, need FGF2 and activin. Pluripotency is tested by injecting cells into blastocysts, where they contribute to all tissues of the chimeric mouse, including the germ line, and by their formation of teratomas, benign tumors containing tissues of all three germ layers, in immunodeficient mice. Pluripotency is maintained by a core network of the transcription factors Oct4, Sox2 and Nanog, which bind together at many enhancers, activate their own genes and each other's, and repress developmental regulators (Boyer and colleagues, 2005). Mouse ES cells carrying engineered mutations, injected into blastocysts, made the **knockout mouse** possible (Capecchi, Evans and Smithies, Nobel Prize 2007).

### 24.7.2. Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells

**Definition 24.7.2 (Reprogramming and Induced Pluripotent Stem Cell)** **Reprogramming** is the conversion of a differentiated cell into a cell of another developmental state, in particular into a pluripotent cell, by changing its regulatory state without changing its DNA sequence. An **induced pluripotent stem cell** (iPS cell) is a pluripotent cell obtained by reprogramming somatic cells through the expression of defined transcription factors.

Kazutoshi Takahashi and Shinya Yamanaka (2006) selected 24 genes expressed in ES cells and introduced them with retroviral vectors into mouse fibroblasts carrying a reporter activated in pluripotent cells; colonies resembling ES cells appeared, and by removing genes one at a time they found that four suffice: *Oct3/4* (*Pou5f1*), *Sox2*, *Klf4* and *c-Myc*. Human fibroblasts were reprogrammed with the same four factors a year later (Takahashi and colleagues, 2007), and with Oct4, Sox2, Nanog and Lin28 (Yu and colleagues, 2007). Gurdon and Yamanaka shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for the discovery that mature cells can be reprogrammed to become pluripotent".

Reprogramming is slow and inefficient: colonies appear after two to three weeks from about 0.01–0.1 % of the transduced cells. Yet it is not restricted to a rare special cell: followed for long enough, almost every clonal descendant of a transduced cell eventually gives rise to iPS cells, at a rate that increases with the rate of cell division, as if each division gave a small chance of crossing a barrier (Hanna and colleagues, 2009). The process has stages. Early, fibroblasts proliferate, lose their mesenchymal genes and undergo a mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition in which they express E-cadherin and form junctions ([7.3.23](/7.3.23#sec-23.3.1)); late, the endogenous *Oct4* and *Nanog* genes are activated as their promoters lose DNA methylation, and the cell no longer needs the exogenous factors. Heterochromatin, especially H3K9-methylated regions, is the main barrier. Because *c-Myc* is an **oncogene** and retroviruses integrate into the genome, current methods use non-integrating vectors (Sendai virus, mRNA, episomal plasmids) and fewer factors; mouse and, since 2022, human somatic cells have been reprogrammed with small molecules alone (Hou and colleagues, 2013; Guan and colleagues, 2022).

iPS cells can be made from any patient. Differentiated into the affected cell type (motor neurons in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, cardiomyocytes in inherited arrhythmias), they model the disease in a dish and allow drugs to be screened; corrected and differentiated, they may replace lost cells. The first transplant of iPS-derived cells, a sheet of retinal pigment epithelium for macular degeneration, was made in 2014 (Mandai and colleagues, 2017), and trials with dopaminergic neurons for Parkinson's disease and pancreatic islet cells for type 1 diabetes have followed. The risks are tumors from residual undifferentiated cells and from mutations acquired in culture, and immune rejection when cells are not the patient's own.

### 24.7.3. Direct Conversion Between Differentiated States

**Definition 24.7.3 (Transdifferentiation)** **Transdifferentiation** (direct reprogramming) is the conversion of one differentiated cell type into another without passing through a pluripotent or progenitor state.

The first example was MyoD itself (24.4.1). Expression of the macrophage regulator C/EBPα converts B cells into macrophages within days (Xie and colleagues, 2004); three neural transcription factors (Ascl1, Brn2 and Myt1l) convert mouse fibroblasts into functional neurons (Vierbuchen and colleagues, 2010); Gata4, Mef2c and Tbx5 convert cardiac fibroblasts into cardiomyocyte-like cells (Ieda and colleagues, 2010); and Ngn3, Pdx1 and MafA convert pancreatic exocrine cells into insulin-producing β-like cells in living mice (Zhou and colleagues, 2008). Transdifferentiation also occurs naturally: when the lens of a newt is removed, pigmented cells of the dorsal iris dedifferentiate and form a new lens.

### 24.7.4. The Waddington Landscape

**Definition 24.7.4 (Waddington Landscape)** The **Waddington landscape** is the image, introduced by Conrad Waddington (1957), of development as a ball rolling down a sloping surface of branching valleys, in which the valleys are cell fates, the ridges between them are barriers to changes of fate and the branch points are decisions. In the language of dynamical systems, the valleys are stable states (attractors) of the cell's gene regulatory network, the branch points are **bifurcations**, and the landscape can be quantified as the quasi-potential $U = -\ln P_{\mathrm{ss}}$, where $P_{\mathrm{ss}}$ is the steady-state probability density of gene expression states in the presence of noise.

The decision of a blood progenitor between the erythroid–megakaryocyte and the myeloid fates is a good example. GATA1 and PU.1 activate their own genes and inhibit each other (Huang and colleagues, 2007): with $x$ the level of GATA1 and $y$ that of PU.1,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = a\frac{x^4}{\theta^4 + x^4} + b\frac{\theta^4}{\theta^4 + y^4} - kx, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = a\frac{y^4}{\theta^4 + y^4} + b\frac{\theta^4}{\theta^4 + x^4} - ky .
\tag{24.6}
$$

With strong self-activation the system has three stable states: GATA1 high and PU.1 low, the reverse, and a state in which both are expressed at intermediate levels, which corresponds to the multipotent progenitor (progenitors do co-express lineage regulators at low levels, a phenomenon called lineage priming). When self-activation weakens, the central state loses stability in a bifurcation and the progenitor must fall into one of the two remaining valleys; which valley is chosen depends on the signals (erythropoietin, macrophage colony-stimulating factor) that tilt the landscape and on noise. Reprogramming in this picture means pushing a cell uphill and over a ridge, or reshaping the landscape so that the valley it sits in disappears; the low efficiency of reprogramming reflects the height of the barriers, which are made partly of chromatin (24.4.3). Because regulatory networks are not gradient systems, $U$ is not a true potential, and there are probability currents that circulate around the valleys, but its minima and saddles still mark the attractors and the transition states.

The steady-state probability density P of the Fokker–Planck equation was computed on a grid, and U = −ln P is plotted (clipped at 8). ## Questions

1. In Gurdon's experiments, the proportion of transplanted nuclei that supported development to a feeding tadpole fell as donor cells came from later stages. Give two explanations for this decline that are consistent with genomic equivalence, and an experiment that distinguishes them.

   **Answer** (a) The nuclei of differentiated cells are epigenetically stabilized (heterochromatin, H3K9 methylation, DNA methylation) and the egg cytoplasm resets them only incompletely in the short time before the first cleavage. (b) Nuclei of slowly dividing differentiated cells cannot replicate their DNA in the rapid cleavage cycles of the egg (about 30 minutes), so chromosomes are broken or lost, which kills the embryo regardless of gene activity. To distinguish them: serial transplantation (nuclei of a blastula cloned from a differentiated cell, transplanted again, give normal development much more often, which supports problem (b) or slow resetting in the first transfer); karyotyping and DNA damage markers in the early clones; and treatment of donor nuclei with chromatin-relaxing agents or expression of a demethylase (as Kdm4d in mammals), which improves development only if (a) is limiting.

2. Explain why lateral inhibition on a hexagonal sheet produces one high-Delta cell in three, while on a square lattice with four neighbours it can produce a checkerboard. Using Eq. (24.2), what is the instability condition for the square lattice?

   **Answer** The pattern that grows first is the one most strongly amplified, the mode whose neighbour average is most opposite to the cell's own deviation, that is, the most negative eigenvalue $\kappa$ of the neighbour-averaging operator. On a square lattice with four neighbours, the checkerboard has every neighbour of a high cell low, so $\kappa = -1$; on the hexagonal sheet a cell cannot be surrounded by six cells that are all of the opposite state while they are also of opposite state to each other (three mutually adjacent cells cannot alternate), and the best possible is $\kappa = -1/2$, with one high cell in three. The instability condition $f' g' \kappa > 1$ becomes $f'(D^*)\lvert g'(N^*)\rvert > 1$ on the square lattice, a weaker requirement than the value 2 for the hexagonal sheet.

3. For the self-activation model of Eq. (24.3) with $s = 0$, $n = 2$, $K = 1$ and $\gamma = 1$, find the values of $\alpha$ for which the "on" state exists. If a cell divides and each daughter receives half the protein, under what condition is the state inherited?

   **Answer** Steady states satisfy $\alpha x^2/(1 + x^2) = x$. Besides $x = 0$, they solve $\alpha x = 1 + x^2$, that is, $x^2 - \alpha x + 1 = 0$, with real positive roots if $\alpha \ge 2$: $x_\pm = (\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - 4})/2$. The larger root is the stable "on" state and the smaller root $x_-$ the threshold. At division each daughter receives about half the molecules and half the volume, so the concentration is unchanged and the "on" state is inherited. It is lost only if the concentration in a daughter falls below $x_-$, for example through unequal partition of the protein or dilution by growth without resynthesis; for $\alpha = 3$, $x_+ = 2.62$ and $x_- = 0.38$, so the state survives even a sevenfold reduction.

4. Using the parameters of Example 24.6.1, what fraction of crypts are monoclonal for a labelled clone after a long time, and how many labelled stem-cell lineages must be marked per crypt, on average, for half of the crypts to become monoclonal for some marked colour after a long time? How would a mutation that halves the loss rate of the mutant cell change the fixation probability, qualitatively?

   **Answer** Each stem cell's lineage eventually takes over the crypt with probability $1/N = 1/16$; over long times every crypt becomes monoclonal for the descendants of one of its 16 original stem cells. If each stem cell is marked independently with probability $q$, the crypt becomes monoclonal for a marked lineage with probability $q$ (the winner is a random one of the 16), so half of the crypts require $q = 1/2$, that is, 8 marked lineages per crypt on average. A mutant that is lost at half the rate but replaces neighbours at the normal rate does a biased random walk, growing twice as often as it shrinks at each boundary; its fixation probability rises from $1/16$ towards the value for a biased walk, $(1 - r)/(1 - r^{N})$ with $r = 1/2$, that is, about $1/2$: a large advantage, which is why stem-cell niches select for mutations that slow the loss of stem cells, an early step in colorectal cancer.

5. The Hes1 oscillator of Eq. (24.4) has a period of about 2 h. Explain qualitatively why the period cannot be much shorter than about $2(\tau + 1/\mu_m + 1/\mu_p)$, and estimate this value. How would doubling the protein half-life change the oscillation?

   **Answer** In one cycle, the protein level must rise and then fall. After transcription is released, protein increases only after the delay $\tau$ and with the lag of the mRNA and protein lifetimes ($1/\mu_m$ and $1/\mu_p$, about 35 and 32 minutes); after repression begins, the protein falls with the same lags. The loop must therefore traverse its delays twice per cycle, giving $T \gtrsim 2(\tau + 1/\mu_m + 1/\mu_p) \approx 2(19 + 35 + 32)\,\mathrm{min} \approx 170\,\mathrm{min}$; the measured and simulated period (120 min) is somewhat shorter because the effective lag of an exponential decay is less than its full lifetime, so the estimate is an upper scale. Doubling the protein half-life slows the fall of the protein, lengthens the period, and also filters the oscillation more strongly; beyond some value it damps out, because a long-lived component averages the delayed feedback (Hirata and colleagues showed that stabilizing Hes1 abolishes the oscillation).

6. Calculate how many red blood cells a human produces per second, and how many hematopoietic stem cell divisions this requires per day if each committed daughter of an HSC passes through 20 amplifying divisions before becoming a red blood cell, with no cell death. What does the answer suggest about HSC division rates in vivo?

   **Answer** $2.5 \times 10^{13}$ cells / 120 days $= 2.1 \times 10^{11}$ per day $\approx 2.4 \times 10^6$ per second. Each committed daughter yields $2^{20} \approx 10^6$ cells, so about $2 \times 10^5$ committed daughters are needed per day, and as many HSC divisions if each HSC division produces one committed daughter. The human marrow contains roughly $10^4$–$10^5$ long-term HSCs, so even this requires each to divide several times a day if they were the only source, which is impossible: in reality most daily production comes from long-lived, self-renewing progenitors downstream of the HSCs, and the HSCs themselves divide rarely (in humans perhaps once every few months to a year). The arithmetic shows why hierarchies with long-lived progenitors and many amplifying divisions are needed.

7. (Open question) Describe two experiments, one on iPS cell reprogramming and one on blood progenitors, that would test whether the Waddington landscape of Eq. (24.6) is a useful description. What measurements would show the "valleys", and what would show a bifurcation?

   **Answer** Valleys correspond to preferred, reproducible gene-expression states with fluctuations around them. In blood progenitors, measure GATA1 and PU.1 in single cells over time (fluorescent reporters, time-lapse imaging or single-cell RNA sequencing of sorted populations): a tristable landscape predicts three clusters in the $(x, y)$ plane, with the central (co-expressing) cluster shrinking as cells commit; perturbation by a transient pulse of one factor should switch cells permanently only if it pushes them past the saddle (hysteresis). A bifurcation is shown when a slowly changing parameter (a cytokine or the level of self-activation, controlled by an inducible construct) makes the central cluster disappear, with signs of critical slowing down before it: fluctuations become larger and slower and recovery from perturbation slows as the bifurcation approaches. In reprogramming, track single cells after factor expression: a landscape view predicts long waiting times followed by fast transitions (barrier crossing), cell-to-cell variability in the timing, rates that increase with factor dose (tilting) and with chromatin-relaxing treatments (lowering barriers), and intermediate states that are revisited by many cells, which can be identified by clustering time-series transcriptomes.
