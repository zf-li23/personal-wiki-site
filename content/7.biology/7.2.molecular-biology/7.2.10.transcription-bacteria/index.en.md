**Transcription** is the step of the **central dogma** at which most decisions about gene expression are taken, and bacteria are where its mechanism was first worked out. A single RNA polymerase makes every RNA of an *E. coli* cell; one detachable subunit, σ, tells it where to start; the start sites, promoters, are short sequences whose resemblance to a consensus sets how often a gene is transcribed; and the polymerase stops at terminators whose signals lie in the RNA it has just made. This chapter follows the transcription cycle in the order in which it was dissected, and, as in Weaver's account, almost every statement rests on one experiment that can be retold: the polymerase and its **subunits** (10.1), promoter recognition by σ (10.2) and by the α subunit (10.3), the steps of initiation and promoter escape (10.4), the fate of σ after initiation (10.5), the elongation complex (10.6) and termination (10.7). The quantitative thread is kinetic: initiation is a two-step binding reaction whose constants differ by orders of magnitude between promoters, and termination is a kinetic competition between elongation and release. The methods used (filter binding, footprinting, run-off transcription, FRET) are those of [7.2.6](/7.2.6); the regulation of initiation by repressors and activators, which uses all of these mechanisms, follows in [7.2.11](/7.2.11), and the eukaryotic polymerases, which share the catalytic core but not the σ factor, in [7.2.12](/7.2.12)–[7.2.13](/7.2.13).

## 10.1. RNA Polymerase

### 10.1.1. The Reaction

**Definition 10.1.1 (RNA Polymerase)** An **RNA polymerase** is an enzyme that synthesizes RNA from ribonucleoside triphosphates on a DNA template, adding ribonucleotides one at a time to the $3'$-OH end of the growing chain in the order specified by base pairing with the template; unlike a DNA polymerase, it starts chains de novo, without a primer.

The chemistry is that of DNA synthesis ([7.2.7](/7.2.7)): the $3'$-OH of the chain attacks the α-phosphate of the incoming NTP, and pyrophosphate leaves,
$$
(\mathrm{NMP})_n + \mathrm{NTP} \longrightarrow (\mathrm{NMP})_{n+1} + \mathrm{PP_i}, \tag{10.1}
$$
catalyzed by two $\ce{Mg^2+}$ ions held by three aspartates; hydrolysis of $\mathrm{PP_i}$ by pyrophosphatase makes the reaction irreversible in the cell. The first nucleotide keeps its $5'$-triphosphate, a fact that will serve as a label for initiation (Example 10.4.2). Only one of the two DNA strands is copied in any one gene.

**Definition 10.1.2** The DNA strand that is base-paired with the RNA during transcription, and is therefore read $3' \to 5'$, is the **template strand**; its complement, which has the same sequence as the RNA (with T for U), is the **coding strand** (nontemplate strand). Sequences of genes and promoters are written as the coding strand, $5' \to 3'$, and positions are numbered from the first transcribed nucleotide, $+1$, positive downstream and negative upstream (there is no position $0$).

Which strand is the template differs from gene to gene: in *E. coli* about half of the genes are transcribed in each direction around the chromosome. RNA polymerase is fast and moderately accurate: elongation proceeds at $40$–$90\,\mathrm{nt\,s^{-1}}$ in vivo (about $50$ in most genes, up to $90$ in rRNA genes), the error rate is about one in $10^4$–$10^5$ nucleotides, and an *E. coli* cell contains a few thousand polymerases, of which during fast growth most are engaged in transcription and half of those in rRNA genes. Errors in RNA are tolerable because each gene gives many short-lived copies, so the costly **proofreading** machinery of DNA replication is not needed; the polymerase nevertheless removes some misincorporated nucleotides by backtracking and cleavage (10.6.3).

### 10.1.2. Core and Holoenzyme

*E. coli* RNA polymerase was purified in 1960 by Hurwitz and by Weiss; by 1969 Burgess, Travers and colleagues had found that a loosely bound polypeptide separated from the enzyme during phosphocellulose chromatography, and that the remaining enzyme still made RNA but no longer transcribed the right genes.

**Definition 10.1.3** The bacterial **core polymerase** is the complex $\alpha_2\beta\beta'\omega$ (about $390\,\mathrm{kDa}$), which can elongate RNA but cannot initiate at promoters. A **σ factor** is a dissociable subunit that binds the core polymerase and directs it to initiate at promoters; core polymerase plus σ is the **RNA polymerase holoenzyme** (about $450\,\mathrm{kDa}$ with the housekeeping $\sigma^{70}$).

| Subunit (gene) | Mass / kDa | Function |
|---|---|---|
| α (*rpoA*), two copies | 37 | N-terminal **domain** (NTD): dimerization and assembly scaffold for β and β′; C-terminal domain (CTD) on a flexible linker: binds UP elements and many activators (10.3, [7.2.11](/7.2.11)) |
| β (*rpoB*) | 151 | One pincer of the "crab claw"; with β′ forms the **active site**; binds rifampicin and NTPs |
| β′ (*rpoC*) | 155 | Other pincer; carries the $\mathrm{NADFDGD}$ **motif** whose aspartates hold the catalytic $\ce{Mg^2+}$, the bridge helix and trigger loop that drive the nucleotide addition cycle; binds DNA; a **coiled coil** (**residues** 262–309) helps σ bind the −10 element |
| ω (*rpoZ*) | 10 | **Chaperone** for folding and assembly of β′; with β′ forms a **binding site** for the alarmone ppGpp |
| $\sigma^{70}$ (*rpoD*) | 70 | Promoter recognition (−10 and −35 elements), promoter melting, abortive initiation and escape (10.2–10.5) |

**Example 10.1.1 (Core polymerase transcribes nonspecifically)** Bautz and colleagues (1969) compared **holoenzyme** and core on phage T4 DNA. The holoenzyme transcribed T4 DNA asymmetrically, producing the RNAs of the early genes, as in an infected cell; the core transcribed far less, and what it made came from both strands. Core activity was high on calf thymus DNA, which is full of nicks and gaps, and on T4 DNA only after nicking. Interpretation: nicks and gaps are nonspecific entry points at which even core polymerase can start; intact DNA has few of them, and there the core cannot find authentic start sites. σ (the letter for "specificity") restores asymmetric, gene-specific transcription.

**Example 10.1.2 (Separation and reconstitution)** Heil and Zillig (1970) separated the subunits of polymerase from rifampicin-sensitive and rifampicin-resistant strains, mixed them in all combinations and reconstituted active enzymes. Resistance went with the β subunit. The same strategy assigned resistance to streptolydigin, which blocks elongation, to β as well. Why does one subunit govern both initiation and elongation? The two phases share one chemical step, phosphodiester bond formation, and affinity labeling (Grachev and colleagues, 1987) showed where it occurs: a reactive NTP analog bound in the active site and then joined by polymerization to a radioactive nucleotide labeled mainly β (with β′ contributing to the site). Rifampicin, it was later found, binds in the RNA exit channel of β about $1\,\mathrm{nm}$ from the active site and blocks the RNA once it is $2$–$3$ nt long: it prevents initiation, but a polymerase that has already escaped is resistant, because its RNA occupies the channel.

## 10.2. Promoter Recognition by σ

### 10.2.1. Promoters

**Definition 10.2.1 (Promoter)** A **promoter** is the DNA sequence at which RNA polymerase holoenzyme binds and initiates transcription, together with the start site; in bacteria it extends from about $-60$ to $+20$ and is defined by short elements whose positions relative to the start site are fixed.

Comparison of hundreds of $\sigma^{70}$ promoters (Pribnow, 1975; Hawley and McClure, 1983) showed two hexamers, each matching a **consensus sequence** only partially in any single promoter. The −10 element (Pribnow box), centered near $-10$, has the consensus $\mathrm{TATAAT}$; the −35 element has $\mathrm{TTGACA}$. In the percentages compiled by Lewin, the consensus base occurs at the six positions with frequency
$$
-35\colon\ \mathrm{T_{82}\,T_{84}\,G_{78}\,A_{65}\,C_{54}\,A_{45}}, \qquad -10\colon\ \mathrm{T_{80}\,A_{95}\,T_{45}\,A_{60}\,A_{50}\,T_{96}}.
$$
The spacer between them is $17 \pm 1\,\mathrm{bp}$ in about $90\,\%$ of promoters; its sequence matters little, but its length is critical, because $17\,\mathrm{bp}$ places the two elements on the same face of the helix at the spacing of the two σ domains that read them. Further elements contribute in some promoters: an *extended −10* $\mathrm{TGn}$ immediately upstream of the −10 hexamer, which can substitute for a poor −35; a *discriminator* of $6$–$8\,\mathrm{bp}$ between the −10 element and the start site, whose GC content tunes open-complex stability and the response to ppGpp; the UP element (10.3); and the start site itself, a purine (usually A) in over $90\,\%$ of promoters, often the central base of $\mathrm{CAT}$.

Two kinds of evidence show that the consensus is functional rather than a statistical accident. Mutations that make a promoter weaker (*down mutations*) almost always move it away from the consensus, and mutations that make it stronger (*up mutations*) move it toward the consensus; the *lacUV5* promoter, for instance, differs from the wild-type *lac* promoter by two **base pairs** that convert its −10 element $\mathrm{TATGTT}$ into $\mathrm{TATAAT}$, and it is strong enough to be independent of the activator CAP ([7.2.11](/7.2.11)). Yet natural promoters are rarely perfect: a promoter with consensus elements and spacer binds so tightly that the polymerase escapes slowly (10.4.3), and most promoters are tuned to intermediate strength and left to activators and repressors for the rest of their regulation.

### 10.2.2. σ Confers Tight and Specific Binding

**Example 10.2.1 (σ stimulates tight binding to promoters)** Hinkle and Chamberlin (1972) bound core or holoenzyme to ${}^3\mathrm{H}$-labeled phage T7 DNA, added a large excess of unlabeled T7 DNA, and measured the labeled DNA retained on nitrocellulose filters over time ([7.2.6](/7.2.6)): any polymerase that dissociated would rebind unlabeled DNA, so the retained label decayed with the dissociation rate. Complexes with core decayed with a half-time below one minute; complexes with holoenzyme decayed with a half-time of $30$–$60\,\mathrm{h}$. Titration showed about eight tight sites per T7 genome, close to the number of early promoters, and about $1300$ loose sites, bound by core and holoenzyme alike. The tight complexes started transcription immediately when nucleotides were added, without the lag that a search would require. And tight binding was strongly temperature dependent: complexes formed at $37\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ were much more stable than at $25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and those at $15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ dissociated quickly.

Two conclusions follow. First, σ does not simply add affinity: holoenzyme binds nonspecific DNA weakly, far more weakly than core does, and binds promoters very tightly, so that σ raises the ratio of promoter to nonspecific affinity by many orders of magnitude; this is what lets a few thousand polymerases find about $2000$ promoters among $4.6\times10^6$ base pairs of nonspecific DNA. Second, the temperature dependence suggested that tight binding requires local melting of the DNA. Hsieh and Wang (1978) confirmed this by the hyperchromic shift of promoter DNA bound by polymerase, which corresponded to about $10\,\mathrm{bp}$ opened per polymerase, and chemical probes (dimethyl sulfate, later permanganate, which attacks only unpaired thymines) located the melted region between about $-11$ and $+2$ (10.4.1). The search itself is accelerated by nonspecific binding and one-dimensional sliding, a problem treated quantitatively in [7.2.22](/7.2.22).

### 10.2.3. The Domains of σ and Their Contacts

Sequence comparison of σ factors from many bacteria divides $\sigma^{70}$ into four conserved regions. Region 1.1 is an acidic domain that in free σ masks the DNA-binding regions and in the holoenzyme occupies the DNA channel, from which incoming promoter DNA displaces it: free σ does not bind promoters, and only binding to core unmasks its DNA-binding domains. Region 2 binds the β′ coiled coil, and its subregions 2.3–2.4 recognize the −10 element and melt it. Region 3 contacts the extended −10 element, and its linker (region 3.2, the "σ finger") reaches into the RNA exit channel toward the active site. Region 4 contains a *helix–turn–helix* motif, two α helices joined by a tight turn, the second of which (the recognition helix) lies in the **major groove** and reads the −35 element; the same motif is used by the lac repressor, CAP and the λ repressors ([7.2.11](/7.2.11)). In the holoenzyme the domains σ2, σ3 and σ4 lie on the outer surface of the core, spaced to span the $\sim 25\,\mathrm{bp}$ between the two elements.

**Example 10.2.2 (Region 4 binds the −35 element)** Dombroski, Gross and colleagues (1992) expressed fragments of σ fused to glutathione-*S*-transferase. A fragment containing only the C-terminal $108$ amino acids (region 4) bound promoter DNA by itself, as shown by competition in filter binding: ${}^3\mathrm{H}$-labeled DNA with the *tac* promoter was mixed with the fragment and an excess of unlabeled competitor DNA. Unlabeled *tac* DNA competed far better than DNA lacking the promoter; a competitor with a deletion in the −10 element competed as well as intact *tac* DNA, but one with a deletion in the −35 element competed no better than DNA with no promoter at all. Region 4 therefore binds the −35 element and not the −10 element. A fragment containing region 2 bound the −10 element in the same way. Both fragments bound DNA even though intact σ does not, which confirmed that region 1.1 autoinhibits σ.

**Example 10.2.3 (Compensating mutations identify contacts)** If an amino acid contacts a particular base pair, a mutation of the amino acid may restore activity to a promoter with a mutation at that base pair while failing to help the wild-type promoter or promoters with other mutations; such *allele-specific suppression* is strong evidence of direct contact. Gardella, Moyle and Susskind (1989) found that a σ mutation in region 4.2 (Arg588 to His) suppressed mutations at position $-35$ specifically, and Siegele and colleagues (1989) found that mutations in region 2.4 (Gln437, Thr440) suppressed mutations at position $-12$ of the −10 element. Region 2.4 thus reads the −10 element and region 4.2 the −35 element, as the fragment experiments suggested.

**Example 10.2.4 (β′ helps σ bind the −10 element)** Arthur and Burgess (1998) had found that a fragment of β′ (residues 1–550) stimulates σ binding to the −10 element; Young, Gross and colleagues (2001) narrowed this to residues $262$–$309$ of β′. They mixed σ with β′ fragments and a short DNA carrying the −10 element, cross-linked with ultraviolet light and analyzed the products by SDS-PAGE. With the β′ fragment, σ cross-linked strongly to the nontemplate strand of the −10 region, the strand that is single-stranded in the open complex. In structures this segment of β′ is the coiled coil against which σ region 2 packs: binding to core reorganizes σ for recognition of single-stranded −10 DNA.

Crystal structures of *Thermus* holoenzyme with a fork-junction DNA, a duplex −35 region joined to a single-stranded −10 region as in an open promoter (Murakami and Darst, 2002), and later of complete open complexes, confirmed the genetic and biochemical picture in atomic detail. The promoter DNA is held mainly by σ; region 2.4 contacts the −10 element and region 4.2 the −35 element through the helix–turn–helix; three invariant aromatic residues of region 2.3 stack on the bases of the −10 element where melting starts; and invariant basic residues bind the phosphates. In the open complex the highly conserved bases $\mathrm{A_{-11}}$ and $\mathrm{T_{-7}}$ of the nontemplate strand are flipped out of the helix into pockets of σ2 (Feklistov and Darst, 2011): recognition and melting are the same event, because the base that σ recognizes can only reach its pocket once it has left its partner. This is why the most conserved positions of the logo are those two.

### 10.2.4. Alternative σ Factors

*E. coli* has seven σ factors, each recognizing promoters with a different consensus and so defining a set of genes, the σ's *regulon*, that can be switched on together.

| σ factor (gene) | Promoter consensus | Genes |
|---|---|---|
| $\sigma^{70}$ (*rpoD*) | $\mathrm{TTGACA}$–$17$–$\mathrm{TATAAT}$ | Housekeeping; about $1000$ promoters |
| $\sigma^{S}$, $\sigma^{38}$ (*rpoS*) | Like $\sigma^{70}$, extended −10 favored | Stationary phase and general stress |
| $\sigma^{H}$, $\sigma^{32}$ (*rpoH*) | $\mathrm{CCCTTGAA}$–$14$–$\mathrm{CCCGATNT}$ | Heat shock: chaperones and proteases |
| $\sigma^{E}$, $\sigma^{24}$ (*rpoE*) | $\mathrm{GAACTT}$–$16$–$\mathrm{TCAAA}$ | Envelope stress (misfolded outer-membrane proteins) |
| $\sigma^{F}$, $\sigma^{28}$ (*fliA*) | $\mathrm{TAAA}$–$15$–$\mathrm{GCCGATAA}$ | Flagella and chemotaxis |
| $\sigma^{FecI}$, $\sigma^{19}$ (*fecI*) | — | Ferric citrate uptake |
| $\sigma^{N}$, $\sigma^{54}$ (*rpoN*) | $\mathrm{TGGCACG}$ at $-24$, $\mathrm{TTGCA}$ at $-12$ | Nitrogen assimilation and others |

The first six belong to one structural family. $\sigma^{54}$ is unrelated: its holoenzyme binds the promoter in a closed complex that cannot open by itself, and melting requires an activator bound to an enhancer-like site $100$–$200\,\mathrm{bp}$ upstream that contacts the polymerase through a DNA loop and remodels it by ATP hydrolysis (NtrC for nitrogen genes). This is the bacterial mechanism closest to eukaryotic activation ([7.2.14](/7.2.14)).

Because all σ factors compete for a limited pool of core polymerase, their activities are set by their amounts, their affinities for core, and dedicated *anti-σ factors* that sequester them until a signal releases them: FlgM holds $\sigma^{28}$ until the flagellar basal body is complete and exports FlgM, and the membrane protein RseA holds $\sigma^{E}$ until misfolded envelope proteins trigger its proteolysis. Heat shock raises the amount and activity of $\sigma^{32}$ within minutes. The same principle drives developmental programs: sporulation of *Bacillus subtilis* runs through a cascade of σ factors ($\sigma^{H} \to \sigma^{F},\ \sigma^{E} \to \sigma^{G},\ \sigma^{K}$) in the forespore and mother cell, each made under the control of the previous one, and the *B. subtilis* phage SPO1 switches the host polymerase from early to middle and late genes with its own σ-like proteins (gp28, then gp33 and gp34). Phage T7 goes further and makes a single-subunit polymerase of its own, which recognizes a $23\,\mathrm{bp}$ promoter absent from the host genome; that is why T7 polymerase and promoter are used in **expression vectors** ([7.2.5](/7.2.5)).

## 10.3. The UP Element and the α Subunit

The seven rRNA operons of *E. coli* account for most transcription in rapidly growing cells, and their P1 promoters are among the strongest known. Their strength comes partly from an AT-rich sequence upstream of the −35 element.

**Definition 10.3.1 (UP Element)** An **UP element** is an AT-rich sequence between about $-60$ and $-40$ of some strong bacterial promoters that stimulates transcription by binding the C-terminal domains of the α subunits of RNA polymerase.

**Example 10.3.1 (The UP element is recognized by the α-CTD)** Ross, Gourse and colleagues (1993) transcribed the *rrnB* P1 promoter with its natural upstream sequence ($-88$ to $-41$), with this region replaced by an unrelated sequence (SUB), or without it, using polymerases reconstituted with wild-type α, with α lacking its C-terminal domain (α-235, truncated after residue 235), or with the point mutant R265C. With wild-type α the UP element stimulated transcription about 30-fold; with α-235 or with R265C it gave no stimulation, although both polymerases transcribed the core promoter normally. **DNase I footprinting** with purified α alone showed protection of the UP element, about $-40$ to $-60$; the holoenzyme protected a larger region including the core promoter. Interpretation: the α-CTD, tethered to the NTD by a flexible linker, binds the UP element in the **minor groove**, with Arg265 essential for the contact; this extra **binding energy** makes the promoter complex much more stable and increases initiation.

The α-CTD has two subsites in a full UP element (a proximal subsite at $-46$ to $-38$ and a distal one at $-57$ to $-47$), one for each α. Its flexibility makes it the polymerase's most versatile handle for regulation: many activators, including CAP at the *lac* promoter, work by touching the α-CTD from a site upstream ([7.2.11](/7.2.11)). The rRNA promoters combine the UP element with binding sites for the DNA-bending protein Fis and an unusually unstable open complex, which makes them exquisitely sensitive to the concentration of the initiating NTP and to ppGpp and DksA, the effectors of the *stringent response* that shuts down rRNA synthesis when amino acids run short.

## 10.4. Initiation

### 10.4.1. From Closed to Open Complex

**Definition 10.4.1** The complex of RNA polymerase holoenzyme with a promoter in which the DNA is still double-stranded is the **closed complex** ($\mathrm{RP_c}$). The complex in which about $13\,\mathrm{bp}$ of the promoter, from about $-11$ to $+2$, are melted and the template strand lies in the active site is the **open complex** ($\mathrm{RP_o}$).

The closed complex protects roughly $-55$ to $-5$ in a footprint; the open complex protects $-55$ to $+20$, its melted thymines react with permanganate, and it resists heparin, a polyanion that traps free and loosely bound polymerase. Opening needs no ATP: the energy comes from the binding of the flipped bases into σ and of the melted template strand in the active-site cleft, and from negative **supercoiling** of the template ([7.2.3](/7.2.3)), which favors strand separation. This is the main difference from RNA polymerase II, whose promoter is opened by an ATP-dependent translocase ([7.2.13](/7.2.13)).

The two-step character of initiation was turned into a measurement by McClure (1980) with the abortive initiation assay: when only the first two nucleotides of the transcript are supplied, each open complex makes short dinucleotide or trinucleotide products continuously, so their accumulation after mixing polymerase with promoter reports the moment at which each promoter becomes an open complex.

**Formula 10.4.1 (Two-step initiation)** If polymerase $\mathrm{R}$ is in excess over promoter $\mathrm{P}$, the closed complex equilibrates rapidly with **dissociation constant** $K_{\mathrm{c}}$, and isomerization to the open complex occurs with rate constant $k_2$,
$$
\mathrm{R} + \mathrm{P} \overset{K_{\mathrm{c}}}{\rightleftharpoons} \mathrm{RP_c} \xrightarrow{\ k_2\ } \mathrm{RP_o},
$$
then open complexes form with a single relaxation time $\tau$ given by
$$
\tau = \frac{1}{k_2} + \frac{K_{\mathrm{c}}}{k_2}\,\frac{1}{[\mathrm{R}]}. \tag{10.2}
$$

**Proof** With rapid equilibrium, the fraction of promoter-containing species that are closed complexes is $[\mathrm{R}]/(K_{\mathrm{c}} + [\mathrm{R}])$, and open complexes form at the rate $k_2$ times this fraction. The unopened promoters therefore decay exponentially with rate constant $k_{\mathrm{obs}} = k_2[\mathrm{R}]/(K_{\mathrm{c}} + [\mathrm{R}])$, and $\tau = 1/k_{\mathrm{obs}}$ gives Eq. (10.2). $\square$

A plot of $\tau$ against $1/[\mathrm{R}]$ (the "τ plot") is a straight line with intercept $1/k_2$ and slope $K_{\mathrm{c}}/k_2$, so the two steps are measured separately. Promoters differ in both by orders of magnitude, which is the kinetic meaning of "promoter strength", and regulators can act on either: at the *lac* promoter, CAP mainly strengthens closed-complex binding (lowers $K_{\mathrm{c}}$), whereas the λ repressor at the $P_{\mathrm{RM}}$ promoter mainly accelerates isomerization (raises $k_2$) ([7.2.11](/7.2.11)). Later work resolved the isomerization into several intermediates (polymerase first bends the DNA, then nucleates melting at $-11$, then loads the template strand), but the two-step description remains the standard way of classifying promoters and activators.

### 10.4.2. Abortive Initiation

**Example 10.4.1 (Abortive transcripts)** Carpousis and Gralla (1980) incubated polymerase with DNA carrying the *lacUV5* promoter, added heparin so that no polymerase could rebind DNA after leaving it, and then added $[\alpha\text{-}{}^{32}\mathrm{P}]\mathrm{ATP}$ with increasing amounts of the other three nucleotides. Gel electrophoresis showed, besides the full-length product, a set of very short oligonucleotides, from dinucleotides to hexamers, corresponding to the start of the *lac* transcript; and many more of them were made than there were polymerase molecules. Because heparin prevented reassociation, each polymerase must have made many short transcripts without leaving the promoter.

**Definition 10.4.2 (Abortive Initiation)** **Abortive initiation** is the repeated synthesis and release of short RNAs, typically $2$–$10$ nt long, by RNA polymerase that remains bound to the promoter, before it escapes into productive elongation.

The ratio of abortive to productive transcripts ranges from a few to more than a hundred among promoters; it is high where the polymerase binds the promoter tightly. An RNA longer than about $5\,\mathrm{nt}$ begins to collide with the σ finger (region 3.2) that occupies the RNA exit path, and longer RNAs must displace it; abortive release is the alternative outcome of this collision.

**Example 10.4.2 (Labeling initiation and elongation separately)** Because the first nucleotide keeps its triphosphate, a nucleotide labeled in the γ-phosphate, $[\gamma\text{-}{}^{32}\mathrm{P}]\mathrm{ATP}$ or $[\gamma\text{-}{}^{32}\mathrm{P}]\mathrm{GTP}$, enters RNA only at the $5'$ end, and only when transcripts start with that purine (most do); its incorporation counts initiation events. A nucleotide labeled in the base, $[{}^{14}\mathrm{C}]\mathrm{ATP}$, enters RNA throughout and measures total synthesis, initiation and elongation together. Travers and Burgess (1969) titrated core with σ and found that σ stimulated both. Because initiation is the slow step of transcription, σ could appear to stimulate elongation simply by providing more initiated chains. A direct test settled it: transcription was started with and without σ, further initiation was blocked with rifampicin (which does not affect chains already elongating), and the lengths of the RNAs made in a fixed time were compared by sedimentation. They were the same: σ stimulates initiation, not the rate of elongation.

### 10.4.3. Scrunching and Promoter Escape

In the initially transcribing complex the active site advances along the template while the polymerase still holds the −35 and −10 elements. Three models were proposed to reconcile these facts: transient excursions of the whole polymerase downstream and back; an "inchworm" in which a flexible enzyme stretches its front end downstream; and scrunching, in which the polymerase stays put and pulls the downstream DNA into itself.

**Definition 10.4.3 (Scrunching)** **Scrunching** is the mechanism of initial transcription in which RNA polymerase, keeping its contacts with the promoter, unwinds downstream DNA and pulls it into the enzyme, so that the transcription bubble grows by one base pair per nucleotide added; the unwound single strands are extruded as bulges within the enzyme.

**Example 10.4.4 (Scrunching seen in single molecules)** Kapanidis, Ebright and colleagues (2006) attached a fluorescence donor and acceptor to two points and measured **Förster resonance energy transfer** in single complexes as they synthesized short RNAs. Distances between the leading edge of the polymerase and the trailing edge, and between the trailing edge and upstream DNA, did not change during initial transcription, ruling out transient excursions and inchworming; the distance between the leading edge and downstream DNA shortened, as predicted by scrunching. In the same year Revyakin, Ebright and Strick held single **supercoiled** promoter DNAs in magnetic tweezers: each base pair unwound changes the **linking number** and hence the extension of the twisted molecule ([7.2.3](/7.2.3)), and the extension showed the expected extra unwinding, about one base pair per nucleotide, followed by rewinding when the polymerase escaped (or when it released an abortive transcript).

Scrunching explains abortive initiation and escape as two outcomes of one stressed state. Each base pair unwound stores free energy; when the RNA has grown to $9$–$11\,\mathrm{nt}$, the stored stress is released either by unscrunching, which ejects the short RNA (abortive initiation), or by breaking the contacts with the promoter, so that the polymerase moves downstream, the bubble shrinks to its elongation size and region 3.2 and then region 4 of σ are displaced from the exit channel (promoter escape). The probability of escape on each attempt decreases with the binding energy holding the polymerase to the promoter, which is why a perfect-consensus promoter is not the strongest one: it holds the polymerase too well.

## 10.5. The σ Cycle

**Example 10.5.1 (σ is reused)** Travers and Burgess (1969) ran transcription at low ionic strength, where core polymerase cannot leave the template at the end of a gene, so that after the first round no new initiation occurred, as shown by $[\gamma\text{-}{}^{32}\mathrm{P}]\mathrm{GTP}$ incorporation. When they added fresh core polymerase, initiation resumed, although no σ had been added. The σ released from the first holoenzymes had joined the new core, and the new holoenzymes could initiate on fresh DNA. σ thus cycles: it leaves the core at some point after initiation and is reused.

The classical *σ cycle* held that σ is released obligatorily at promoter escape and replaced by the elongation factor NusA. FRET between σ and the DNA ends tested this (Mukhopadhyay, Ebright and colleagues, 2001). With the acceptor at the upstream end of the DNA (*trailing-edge FRET*), FRET decreased after escape whether or not σ stayed on the core, because the core moves away from the upstream end in both cases. With the acceptor at the downstream end (*leading-edge FRET*), the two hypotheses predict opposite results: released σ would be farther away on average, but σ carried along by the core would come closer. FRET increased, so σ remained with the polymerase after escape in essentially all complexes in these experiments. Bar-Nahum and Nudler (2001) purified elongation complexes halted at $+32$ by capturing their RNA on beads and measured σ by immunoblotting: complexes contained $33\,\%$ of the stoichiometric σ when prepared from stationary-phase extracts and $6\,\%$ from exponential-phase extracts, less than the FRET experiments implied.

The two results were reconciled by the *stochastic release model*: σ is released from the core during elongation, not at one obligatory point but at random, with a rate that depends on the sequence. The template used in the FRET work, *lacUV5*, contains a second −10-like sequence just downstream of the start site, which σ region 2 binds in the elongation complex, causing a σ-dependent pause and helping to retain σ; when this sequence was mutated, σ was released several times faster, and single-molecule FRET showed that about half of the halted complexes with longer RNAs had lost σ. σ-dependent promoter-proximal pausing is used for regulation: at the late promoter of phage λ, the antiterminator Q acts on exactly such a σ-paused complex ([7.2.11](/7.2.11)).

## 10.6. Elongation

### 10.6.1. The Elongation Complex

**Definition 10.6.1 (Transcription Bubble)** The **transcription bubble** is the region of locally unwound DNA within the transcribing RNA polymerase, about $12$–$14\,\mathrm{bp}$ in the elongation complex, in which the template strand is paired with the $3'$ end of the RNA; it moves with the polymerase, the DNA being unwound ahead and rewound behind.

Unwinding ahead and rewinding behind a polymerase that cannot rotate around the DNA generate positive supercoils ahead and negative supercoils behind (the twin-supercoiled-domain model of [7.2.3](/7.2.3)), which **gyrase** and **topoisomerase** I remove. Inside the bubble the RNA forms a short RNA–DNA hybrid; upstream it separates from the template and leaves through an exit channel, so that the RNA is not wound around the template.

**Example 10.6.1 (The RNA–DNA hybrid is 8–9 bp)** Nudler, Goldfarb and colleagues (1997) measured the hybrid length by "walking" the polymerase. They halted elongation complexes on immobilized DNA, incorporated a single reactive UMP analog ($\mathrm{U}^\bullet$) at a known position ($+21$ or $+45$), and then advanced the complex step by step by adding limited sets of nucleotides, so that $\mathrm{U}^\bullet$ moved away from the $3'$ end one position at a time. At each position the analog was cross-linked (with $\mathrm{NaBH_4}$) to whatever it was near, and the labeled DNA and protein were separated by electrophoresis. $\mathrm{U}^\bullet$ cross-linked strongly to the template DNA when it was within about $8$ nucleotides of the $3'$ end, and the cross-linking dropped sharply beyond; it cross-linked to the polymerase (mainly β and β′) all along. The RNA is therefore paired with the template for $8$–$9$ nucleotides behind its $3'$ end, not for the $12$ that earlier models had assumed. Crystal structures show a $9$–$10\,\mathrm{bp}$ hybrid in post-translocation and pre-translocation states.

![The RNA–DNA hybrid length from cross-linking (schematic model of the experiment of Example 10.6.1). The reactive nucleotide U• is cross-linked to the template DNA only while it is in the hybrid; modeling the end of the hybrid as a step at 8–9 nt from the 3′ end, smoothed by the breathing of the upstream base pair, gives a DNA signal that is high up to about 8 nt and falls to background beyond 10 nt, while cross-linking to protein (β and β′) continues, declining slowly, as the RNA runs through the exit channel](./plots/hybrid-crosslinking.py)

### 10.6.2. Structure of the Polymerase

The first structure of a bacterial RNA polymerase, the core of *Thermus aquaticus* (Zhang, Darst and colleagues, 1999), looked like a crab claw: β and β′ form two pincers around a central channel about $2.7\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide that holds the downstream DNA; the catalytic $\ce{Mg^2+}$ sits at the floor of the channel, held by three aspartates of β′; the two α subunits and ω lie at the back, away from the DNA; and the rifampicin-binding pocket in β lies at the entrance of the RNA exit channel, explaining how the drug blocks RNA longer than a few nucleotides. In the elongation complex the β′ "clamp" closes over the DNA, which is why elongation complexes are so stable (they survive high salt and remain bound for hours) and so processive (a polymerase can transcribe tens of kilobases without dissociating): the DNA is topologically enclosed. Each cycle of nucleotide addition involves the closing of the β′ *trigger loop* over the incoming NTP, catalysis, and translocation by one base pair, coupled to a movement of the *bridge helix* that spans the cleft; streptolydigin and the antibiotic microcin J25 act on these elements. The same architecture, with the same two-metal-ion chemistry, is found in the eukaryotic polymerases, whose two largest subunits are homologous to β′ and β ([7.2.12](/7.2.12)).

### 10.6.3. Pausing and Backtracking

Elongation is not uniform. A polymerase pauses every hundred base pairs or so for a second or more, at sequences that make translocation slow (an *elemental pause*); a **hairpin** forming in the exit channel can extend the pause, and so can backward sliding, called backtracking, in which the RNA–DNA hybrid shifts upstream and the $3'$ end of the RNA is extruded beyond the active site. A backtracked polymerase is arrested until the extruded RNA is cleaved, which the polymerase does slowly by itself and rapidly when stimulated by the factors GreA and GreB (analogs of eukaryotic TFIIS, [7.2.13](/7.2.13)); because misincorporated nucleotides favor backtracking, this cleavage also serves as a form of proofreading. Pauses matter for regulation: they synchronize transcription with translation by the leading ribosome (in bacteria the two are coupled, [7.2.11](/7.2.11), [7.2.19](/7.2.19)), give time for RNA folding, and are the first step of termination. NusG, the only transcription factor conserved in all domains of life (its eukaryotic **homolog** is Spt5), binds the polymerase clamp, suppresses backtracking and links the polymerase to the ribosome; NusA does the opposite, stabilizing hairpin pauses.

## 10.7. Termination

### 10.7.1. Intrinsic Terminators

**Definition 10.7.1 (Intrinsic Terminator)** An **intrinsic terminator** is a DNA sequence at which bacterial RNA polymerase terminates without additional factors; it encodes an RNA with a GC-rich hairpin (an inverted repeat, typically $7$–$20\,\mathrm{bp}$ in the stem) followed by a run of about eight uridines, the end of the RNA lying within the U tract, $7$–$9\,\mathrm{nt}$ after the base of the hairpin.

![Intrinsic and ρ-dependent termination. A: an intrinsic terminator: the RNA forms a GC-rich stem-loop just upstream of the RNA–DNA hybrid, which at the termination point consists of weak rU–dA pairs; the hairpin invades the exit channel and destabilizes the paused complex. B: ρ-dependent termination: the hexameric ρ helicase binds a C-rich, unstructured rut site on the nascent RNA (often while already bound to the polymerase) and translocates 5′→3′ along the RNA with ATP hydrolysis, pulling the RNA out of a paused polymerase. C: termination as kinetic competition (Eq. (10.3)): the termination efficiency at a pause site against the elongation rate constant, for a strong terminator (release rate constant 2 s⁻¹) and a weak one (0.2 s⁻¹); slowing elongation (low NTP, a stalled polymerase) increases termination, and a stronger hairpin or weaker hybrid shifts the curve up](./plots/termination.py)

**Example 10.7.1 (Both parts of an intrinsic terminator are required)** Farnham and Platt (1980) studied the attenuator of the *E. coli trp* operon, which acts as an intrinsic terminator ([7.2.11](/7.2.11)). When the run of eight T's of the coding strand ($\mathrm{TTTTTTTT}$) was mutated to $\mathrm{TTTTGCAA}$, termination failed and the polymerase read through, making a long transcript. Nucleotide analogs gave the same answer from the RNA side: transcription with 5-bromo-UTP or allylamine-UTP, which pair more strongly with A than U does, reduced termination at the wild-type terminator, whereas transcription with inosine triphosphate instead of GTP, which weakens the G–C pairs of the hairpin, also abolished termination. Strong rU–dA pairs cause readthrough; a weak hairpin causes readthrough.

Two further observations constrain the mechanism. Hairpins destabilize elongation complexes stalled at sites that are not U-rich; and terminators missing half of the inverted repeat still pause at the U tract, though no hairpin can form. Hence the general model: the rU–dA pairs, the weakest of all base pairs, cause the polymerase to pause; during the pause the hairpin forms and destabilizes the already weak hybrid, and the RNA dissociates from the template. Gusarov and Nudler (1999) tested this with a terminator lacking the downstream half of its hairpin: the polymerase paused at the U tract but did not release the RNA from immobilized templates, and adding an oligonucleotide complementary to the remaining half, which formed a hybrid "hairpin" with the RNA, restored release. Conversely, the U tract was not needed if the polymerase was slowed to a crawl by withholding a nucleotide. The essence of an intrinsic terminator is twofold: something that makes the polymerase pause (the U tract), and something that pairs with the transcript next to the hybrid and pulls it apart (the hairpin). How the hairpin does this, by pulling the RNA out of the hybrid, pushing the polymerase forward without nucleotide addition, or opening the clamp from the exit channel, has been debated; recent **cryo-electron microscopy** structures of terminating complexes favor hairpin invasion of the exit channel followed by clamp opening and collapse of the bubble.

The competition between continuing and stopping can be written down.

**Formula 10.7.1 (Kinetic competition)** At a terminator where the polymerase either adds the next nucleotide with rate constant $k_{\mathrm{e}}$ or releases the RNA with rate constant $k_{\mathrm{t}}$, the termination efficiency is
$$
T = \frac{k_{\mathrm{t}}}{k_{\mathrm{t}} + k_{\mathrm{e}}}. \tag{10.3}
$$
For a terminator spread over several positions $i$ of the U tract, the readthrough fraction is the product $\prod_i (1 - T_i)$.

Equation (10.3) gathers the observations above: a stronger hairpin or weaker hybrid raises $k_{\mathrm{t}}$; a lower NTP concentration or a pause-inducing sequence lowers $k_{\mathrm{e}}$; both increase termination. Natural terminators span the whole range from nearly complete to about $10\,\%$ efficiency, so terminators, like promoters, are tuned rather than all-or-none, and an incompletely efficient terminator can set the ratio of expression of the genes on either side of it.

### 10.7.2. ρ-Dependent Termination

**Definition 10.7.2 (ρ Factor)** The **ρ factor** (Rho) is a hexameric, RNA-dependent ATPase and $5' \to 3'$ RNA translocase of bacteria that terminates transcription by binding a C-rich, G-poor, unstructured segment of the nascent RNA (the *rut* site, about $70$–$80$ nt) and dissociating the RNA–DNA hybrid of a paused polymerase downstream.

Roberts (1969) discovered ρ as a protein that depressed the transcription of phage λ DNA in vitro, and showed that the depression was termination.

**Example 10.7.2 (ρ terminates transcription)** Three experiments established that ρ is a termination factor. (1) In transcription of λ DNA with increasing ρ, initiation measured with $[\gamma\text{-}{}^{32}\mathrm{P}]\mathrm{GTP}$ was unaffected (if anything it increased), whereas total RNA synthesis measured with $[{}^3\mathrm{H}]\mathrm{UTP}$ fell: ρ acts after initiation, and because each termination forces a slow reinitiation, net synthesis falls. (2) RNA made in the presence of ρ was shorter, by sedimentation, than RNA made without it; RNA made without ρ and then incubated with ρ kept its size, so ρ does not degrade RNA but stops its synthesis. (3) In sedimentation with the DNA, transcripts made without ρ cosedimented with the template, still held in elongation complexes, whereas transcripts made with ρ sedimented separately: ρ releases RNA from the template.

ρ forms an open ring that closes around the RNA after loading on the *rut* site; with ATP hydrolysis it translocates $5' \to 3'$ along the RNA toward the polymerase and, upon reaching a paused complex, unwinds the hybrid and releases the RNA. In the classical "catch-up" model ρ loads on the free RNA and chases the polymerase, which explains why ρ-dependent termination occurs at pause sites and a short distance downstream of *rut*. Structural and biochemical work since 2010 (Epshtein, Nudler and colleagues; cryo-electron microscopy of ρ–polymerase complexes) supports a model in which ρ binds the polymerase near the RNA exit channel from the start of elongation, captures the emerging *rut* RNA and, by pulling on it, traps the polymerase in an inactive state that releases the RNA. Either way, ρ needs free, untranslated RNA: when ribosomes follow the polymerase closely, as on a translated mRNA, *rut* sites are covered and ρ cannot act.

This dependence explains *polarity*: a nonsense mutation early in the first gene of an operon reduces the expression of the downstream genes too, because ribosomes fall off at the premature stop codon and expose *rut* sites in the untranslated remainder of the mRNA, where ρ then terminates transcription. ρ is essential in *E. coli*; it terminates a large fraction of transcription units, including the ends of many genes, and genome-wide experiments with the ρ inhibitor bicyclomycin showed that it also suppresses widespread antisense and spurious transcription, especially of horizontally acquired DNA. The older statement that ρ-dependent terminators are rare in *E. coli* is therefore incorrect.

### 10.7.3. Antitermination

**Definition 10.7.3 (Antitermination)** **Antitermination** is the modification of an RNA polymerase, by proteins or RNA bound to it, so that it reads through terminators that would otherwise stop it.

Antitermination regulates the expression of genes that lie downstream of terminators. Phage λ uses it to control the order of its gene expression, with the proteins N and Q ([7.2.11](/7.2.11)); the rRNA operons of *E. coli* carry *boxA* sequences that recruit a complex of NusA, NusB, NusE (ribosomal protein S10) and NusG, making the polymerase resistant to ρ along the untranslated rRNA; and the NusG **paralog** RfaH, recruited by a short sequence (*ops*) in the RNA, protects long operons for surface polysaccharides and toxins. Antitermination complexes work by suppressing pausing and preventing hairpin formation in the exit channel, the two steps on which termination depends.

## Questions

1. Core polymerase binds DNA nonspecifically more tightly than holoenzyme does, yet holoenzyme binds promoters about $10^7$ times more tightly than nonspecific DNA. Explain why both properties are needed for a polymerase that must find promoters quickly.

   **Answer** A polymerase searching for a promoter must not get stuck on the $\sim 5\times10^6$ nonspecific sites: if its nonspecific affinity were high, nearly all holoenzyme would be bound at nonspecific sites with long dwell times, and the search would be slow. Weak nonspecific binding (fast dissociation) lets holoenzyme sample many sites quickly by binding, sliding a short distance and dissociating ([7.2.22](/7.2.22)). Once σ has found a promoter, very tight binding (an open complex with a half-life of hours) commits it to initiation. After initiation the opposite is needed: the core must hold the DNA very stably during elongation, which it does through the closed clamp, independent of sequence. Releasing σ, which lowers nonspecific binding of the core-plus-σ complex, would make no sense during elongation; the σ cycle switches the enzyme between these two modes.

2. A promoter has the −10 element $\mathrm{TATGTT}$ and the −35 element $\mathrm{TTTACA}$ with a $17\,\mathrm{bp}$ spacer (the wild-type *lac* promoter). Predict the effects of (a) changing the −10 element to $\mathrm{TATAAT}$; (b) changing the spacer to $15\,\mathrm{bp}$; (c) changing the −35 element to $\mathrm{TTGACA}$ together with (a).

   **Answer** (a) This is the *lacUV5* mutation: the −10 element now matches the consensus, the promoter forms open complexes more readily (mainly a faster isomerization), and transcription becomes independent of CAP–**cAMP**. (b) Shortening the spacer by $2\,\mathrm{bp}$ rotates the two elements by about $70^\circ$ relative to each other and brings them $0.7\,\mathrm{nm}$ closer, so σ regions 2.4 and 4.2 cannot contact both simultaneously; the promoter becomes much weaker (a down mutation) even though neither hexamer changed. (c) A full consensus promoter binds polymerase very tightly; transcription in vivo may be lower than for (a), because escape becomes slow and abortive cycling frequent. Strength is not maximized by maximal affinity.

3. In the experiment of Example 10.2.2, why was intact σ not used for the competition, and what does the failure of intact σ to bind DNA imply about region 1.1?

   **Answer** Intact σ does not bind promoter DNA on its own, so it would show no binding to compete with; the C-terminal fragment does bind. This shows that some part of σ that is missing from the fragment inhibits DNA binding in free σ: region 1.1, whose deletion allows σ to bind promoters. In the holoenzyme, binding to core moves region 1.1 into the DNA channel and frees the DNA-binding domains; promoter DNA later displaces region 1.1 from the channel. Autoinhibition keeps free σ from occupying promoters without the core.

4. The abortive initiation assay gives the following times for open complex formation at a promoter: $\tau = 60\,\mathrm{s}$ at $[\mathrm{R}] = 100\,\mathrm{nM}$ and $\tau = 150\,\mathrm{s}$ at $[\mathrm{R}] = 25\,\mathrm{nM}$. Find $k_2$ and $K_{\mathrm{c}}$. How long would open complex formation take at $10\,\mathrm{nM}$ polymerase?

   **Answer** From Eq. (10.2), $\tau = a + b/[\mathrm{R}]$ with $a = 1/k_2$ and $b = K_{\mathrm{c}}/k_2$. Then $60 = a + b/100$ and $150 = a + b/25$ ($[\mathrm{R}]$ in nM). Subtracting, $90 = b(1/25 - 1/100) = 0.03\,b$, so $b = 3000\,\mathrm{nM\,s}$ and $a = 60 - 30 = 30\,\mathrm{s}$. Hence $k_2 = 1/30 \approx 0.033\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $K_{\mathrm{c}} = b/a = 100\,\mathrm{nM}$. At $10\,\mathrm{nM}$, $\tau = 30 + 300 = 330\,\mathrm{s}$, dominated by the binding step: below $K_{\mathrm{c}}$ the promoter is binding limited, and an activator that lowers $K_{\mathrm{c}}$ would help most.

5. Why does rifampicin inhibit initiation but not elongation, while streptolydigin inhibits both? What would you expect from adding rifampicin to cells, measured as the rate of RNA synthesis over the next minutes?

   **Answer** Rifampicin binds in the RNA exit channel of β about $1\,\mathrm{nm}$ from the active site and clashes with RNA longer than $2$–$3\,\mathrm{nt}$; a polymerase that has already escaped has its RNA in the channel and the drug cannot bind, so elongating polymerases finish their genes. Streptolydigin binds near the bridge helix and trigger loop and blocks the nucleotide addition cycle itself, which every polymerase uses at every step. After adding rifampicin, RNA synthesis continues for the time needed to finish the transcripts already started (for a $5\,\mathrm{kb}$ operon at $50\,\mathrm{nt\,s^{-1}}$, about $100\,\mathrm{s}$) and then stops; the decline of labeling with time measures elongation rates and was used to measure the in vivo transcription speed.

6. In the walking experiment (Example 10.6.1), what result would you expect if the hybrid were $12\,\mathrm{bp}$? Why is the cross-linking of $\mathrm{U}^\bullet$ to protein not informative about the hybrid length?

   **Answer** With a $12\,\mathrm{bp}$ hybrid, $\mathrm{U}^\bullet$ would cross-link to the template DNA until it was about $12\,\mathrm{nt}$ from the $3'$ end, and the drop would come four positions later than observed. Cross-linking to protein occurs wherever the RNA is in contact with the enzyme: in the hybrid and also in the exit channel, where the single-stranded RNA lies against β and β′ for another $5$–$10$ nucleotides. It therefore does not change at the end of the hybrid and serves only as a control that the label is present.

7. A terminator stops $90\,\%$ of polymerases at $100\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ NTPs, where elongation through the U tract occurs with $k_{\mathrm{e}} = 10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. What termination efficiency is expected at $20\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ NTPs if $k_{\mathrm{e}}$ follows **Michaelis–Menten equation** kinetics with $K_{\mathrm{m}} = 50\,\mu\mathrm{M}$, and what does this imply for termination in starved cells?

   **Answer** From Eq. (10.3), $0.9 = k_{\mathrm{t}}/(k_{\mathrm{t}} + 10)$, so $k_{\mathrm{t}} = 90\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. With $k_{\mathrm{e}} = V[\mathrm{S}]/(K_{\mathrm{m}} + [\mathrm{S}])$, $k_{\mathrm{e}}(100) = 10$ gives $V = 15\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and $k_{\mathrm{e}}(20) = 15 \times 20/70 \approx 4.3\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Then $T = 90/94.3 \approx 0.95$. Lower NTP levels thus increase termination (and, for terminators of moderate efficiency, the effect is larger); in starved cells termination at attenuators and incompletely efficient terminators rises, reducing the expression of downstream genes, a coupling of gene expression to metabolic state that some operons exploit.

8. Explain the polarity of nonsense mutations. Would you expect a nonsense mutation near the end of the first gene of an operon to be as polar as one near its beginning?

   **Answer** Ribosomes released at a premature stop codon leave the downstream mRNA untranslated; if it contains a *rut* site (C-rich, unstructured), ρ binds there, translocates to the polymerase and terminates transcription before it reaches the downstream genes, so their expression falls. A nonsense mutation near the end of the gene exposes only a short untranslated stretch before ribosomes can reinitiate or the next gene begins, which is less likely to contain a functional *rut* site of $70$–$80$ nt; such mutations are much less polar. Polarity is relieved in ρ mutants and by bicyclomycin, which shows that it is a transcriptional effect.

9. (Open question) Bacterial promoters have been engineered and selected in vitro for maximal transcription. Using the ideas of this chapter, predict the features of the selected promoters and explain why natural promoters rarely look like them.

   **Answer** Selections for high output usually recover promoters with near-consensus −10 elements, an extended −10, an AT-rich UP element and a discriminator that does not hold the polymerase too tightly, together with a start-site region that minimizes abortive cycling; strong −35 elements are favored only up to the point where escape becomes limiting. Natural promoters are rarely like this because high constitutive output is seldom optimal: most genes must be regulated, which requires a promoter whose basal activity is low enough that a repressor can silence it and whose weak binding or isomerization leaves room for an activator to help. Promoter strength is a regulated set point, not a maximum, and the moderate match to the consensus is itself a target of selection.
