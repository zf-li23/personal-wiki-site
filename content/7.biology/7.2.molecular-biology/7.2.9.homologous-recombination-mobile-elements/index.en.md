Recombination rearranges DNA sequences: it joins segments that were apart and separates segments that were joined. The word covers three different kinds of chemistry. Homologous recombination exchanges sequences between DNA molecules that share a long region of nearly identical sequence and copies information from one to the other; site-specific recombination exchanges DNA at two short, defined sites recognized by a dedicated enzyme; and transposition moves a mobile element to a new, usually unrelated, site. For a long time recombination was studied as a source of genetic variation, the exchange that shuffles alleles in meiosis. The molecular view that emerged in the last forty years is different: in all cells, homologous recombination is first of all a repair process, the accurate way of mending double-strand breaks and of restarting broken **replication forks**, and its use in meiosis is a later specialization. This chapter follows the bacterial pathway from the processing of a broken end (9.1–9.2) through homology search and strand exchange (9.3) to the four-way junction and its resolution (9.4), then the eukaryotic pathways (9.5), site-specific recombination (9.6), the programmed rearrangement of **antibody** genes (9.7) and transposable elements (9.8). Double-strand breaks and the alternative of end joining were introduced in [7.2.8](/7.2.8); the genetic consequences of crossing over, gene mapping and meiosis belong to genetics ([7.4](/7.4)); the use of recombination for genome engineering returns in [7.2.24](/7.2.24).

## 9.1. Recombination as Repair

**Definition 9.1.1** **Homologous recombination** (HR) is the exchange or copying of genetic information between two DNA molecules, or two regions of one molecule, that share an extended region of nearly identical sequence; the reactions depend on base pairing between the interacting molecules, but not on any particular sequence. **Site-specific recombination** is the exchange of DNA between two short, defined sequences by a recombinase that recognizes them. **Transposition** is the movement of a discrete DNA segment, a transposable element, to a new site that need not be related in sequence.

Bacteria are haploid and have no meiosis, yet *recA* mutants of *E. coli*, which cannot recombine, are extremely sensitive to ultraviolet light and to agents that break DNA, and mutants lacking both of its loading pathways grow poorly even without damage: a substantial fraction of replication cycles in normal growth needs recombinational repair. In bacteria the main role of homologous recombination is the repair of replication forks: when a fork meets a nick in the template it collapses into a one-ended double-strand break, which cannot be joined to anything, and when it meets an unrepaired lesion it leaves a single-stranded gap behind. In both cases the missing information exists only in the sister chromosome, and recombination recovers it. Afterwards the **replisome** is reloaded at the repaired fork, independently of *oriC*, by the PriA-dependent restart primosome. In **eukaryotes** the same reactions repair breaks in S and G2 phase from the sister chromatid ([7.2.8](/7.2.8#sec-8.5)), and meiosis adds programmed breaks and a preference for the homologous chromosome over the sister.

All homologous recombination follows the same sequence of steps, which the following sections take up in order:

1. Processing of a break into a $3'$ single-stranded tail (presynapsis): RecBCD in *E. coli*, MRN–CtIP with EXO1 or DNA2 in eukaryotes.
2. Assembly of a recombinase filament on the tail: RecA, eukaryotic RAD51 and the meiotic DMC1.
3. Homology search and strand invasion (synapsis): the filament pairs the tail with a homologous duplex and forms a displacement loop.
4. DNA synthesis from the invading $3'$ end, using the homologous duplex as template.
5. Processing of the joint molecule (postsynapsis): branch migration, resolution or dissolution of Holliday junctions, or displacement of the invading strand.

## 9.2. Processing the Break: RecBCD and Chi

RecBCD is a heterotrimeric **helicase**–nuclease that binds a blunt or nearly blunt double-stranded end and unwinds DNA at up to $1000$–$2000\,\mathrm{bp\,s^{-1}}$ for tens of kilobases. It has two motors of opposite polarity: RecB translocates $3' \to 5'$ on one strand and RecD $5' \to 3'$ on the other, so both move away from the end. A nuclease **domain** on RecB degrades the unwound strands, the strand ending $3'$ at the entry point vigorously and the $5'$-ended strand less. This would destroy the DNA, and in this mode RecBCD also defends the cell against phage DNA, which enters as linear molecules. What converts the destroyer into a recombination enzyme is a short sequence.

**Definition 9.2.1 (Chi Site)** A **Chi site** (crossover hotspot instigator) is the octamer $5'$-GCTGGTGG-$3'$ in *E. coli* DNA. When RecBCD, unwinding from a double-stranded end, encounters Chi in the correct orientation (approaching from its $3'$ side), the RecC subunit binds it and the enzyme's activity changes: degradation of the $3'$-ended strand stops at Chi, the $5'$-ended strand continues to be degraded, and RecBCD loads RecA onto the $3'$-ended single strand that it now produces.

The recognition of Chi was first seen as Chi-specific nicking. In the experiment summarized in the course slides, a linear DNA labeled at one end and containing a Chi site $80$ nucleotides from that end was incubated with RecBCD and ATP; an $80$-nucleotide labeled single strand was released, ending at Chi. It appeared without heat **denaturation**, so RecBCD not only cut the strand at Chi but had also unwound the DNA from the end up to that point. Later single-molecule experiments showed the switch directly: RecBCD pauses at Chi, then continues at about half its previous speed, now led by the slower RecD motor while a loop of $3'$-ended single strand grows ahead of RecB and is coated with RecA.

**Example 9.2.1 (Chi is overrepresented)** A particular octamer occurs by chance once every $4^8 = 65{,}536$ positions. The *E. coli* chromosome has $2\times4.64\times10^6$ positions (both strands), so about $140$ Chi sequences would be expected, whereas $1009$ are found, a sevenfold excess; Chi occurs on average every $4.6\,\mathrm{kb}$ (in either orientation). Chi sites are also strongly biased in orientation: most lie on the leading-strand template in the orientation that RecBCD recognizes when it enters at a broken fork and moves back toward *oriC*. The two biases are the signature of selection for a sequence that marks the bacterium's own chromosome, which RecBCD repairs, and distinguishes it from foreign DNA, which lacks Chi at this frequency and is degraded. Recognition is probabilistic: RecBCD recognizes a Chi site with a probability of about $0.3$ at each encounter and continues through the others.

Without RecBCD, *E. coli* uses the RecF pathway, in which the RecQ helicase and the RecJ **exonuclease** resect breaks and RecF, RecO and RecR load RecA onto single-stranded gaps coated with **SSB**. This pathway is the main route for repairing the gaps left behind forks at lesions ([7.2.8](/7.2.8#sec-8.6)).

## 9.3. RecA: Filament, Homology Search and Strand Invasion

### 9.3.1. The Presynaptic Filament

RecA is a $38\,\mathrm{kDa}$ ATPase that polymerizes cooperatively on single-stranded DNA in the presence of ATP into a right-handed helical filament with about six monomers per turn. Each monomer binds three nucleotides, and the DNA in the filament is stretched to about $1.5$ times the length of **B-DNA** and underwound to about $18.6$ nucleotides per turn. The crystal structure of the filament (Chen, Yang and Pavletich, 2008) showed that the stretching is not uniform: groups of three nucleotides keep almost B-form geometry, and the extension is concentrated between the triplets. The filament grows mainly in the $5' \to 3'$ direction. SSB, which binds single-stranded DNA much faster, blocks nucleation, which is why RecA needs loading proteins (RecBCD, RecFOR).

The eukaryotic recombinase RAD51 forms a nearly identical filament. Its loading onto RPA-coated DNA is assisted by mediators: BRCA2, which binds RAD51 through eight BRC repeats and delivers it to single-stranded DNA, with PALB2 and BRCA1, and the RAD51 **paralogs**. Loss of BRCA2 abolishes this step, which explains the dependence of BRCA2-deficient cells on other repair pathways ([7.2.8](/7.2.8#sec-8.7)).

### 9.3.2. Synapsis and Strand Exchange

The filament searches the genome for a duplex with a matching sequence. Electron microscopy of reactions between RecA-coated single strands and duplex DNA showed the aligned molecules side by side before any Watson–Crick helix between them had formed: the first contact is a paranemic joint, in which the strands are aligned but not intertwined. When the homologous region is at an end of the duplex, or the duplex is **supercoiled**, the paired region can be converted into a plectonemic (intertwined) heteroduplex, and the strand of the duplex that is identical to the invading strand is displaced.

**Definition 9.3.1 (Displacement Loop)** A **displacement loop** (D loop) is the structure formed when a single-stranded DNA invades a homologous duplex and pairs with its complementary strand, displacing the other strand of the duplex as a single-stranded loop.

Homology search and pairing do not require ATP hydrolysis; filaments with the nonhydrolyzable analog ATPγS still pair and exchange strands over short distances. ATP hydrolysis is needed to disassemble RecA from the product and to drive extensive, unidirectional strand exchange, and it gives the reaction a direction. The invading strand always enters with its $3'$ end, which can then prime DNA synthesis on the homologous template, extending the D loop and copying the information lost at the break.

### 9.3.3. The Homology-Search Problem

The filament must find one matching sequence among millions without a guide. The search proceeds by random collisions of the filament with duplex DNA. Each contact tests a short stretch: the duplex is pulled into the filament and its bases are sampled triplet by triplet for complementarity with the bound strand, and only when about eight consecutive nucleotides match is the interaction stable long enough to test the next triplets; a mismatch causes rapid release. A stable product requires about $15$ or more matching nucleotides, and efficient exchange in vivo needs tens to hundreds.

**Example 9.3.1** How long must a matching sequence be to be unique? If a genome of $G$ **base pairs** were a random sequence, the expected number of sites (on both strands) matching a given $l$-mer is $2G/4^l$, which falls below $1$ when $l > \log_4(2G)$. For *E. coli*, $\log_4(9.3\times10^6) \approx 11.6$; for the human genome, $\log_4(6.2\times10^9) \approx 16.3$. The roughly $8$-nucleotide test that opens each contact therefore finds many false partners (about $140$ in *E. coli* for every octamer), which must be rejected cheaply, whereas the $15$–$20$-nucleotide stability threshold is just above the length at which a match becomes unique. Testing in small units, each passed only if the previous one matched, discriminates by repeated checking, in the spirit of **kinetic proofreading** ([6.3.18](/6.3.18)). Live-cell imaging shows that RecA filaments formed at a break extend across much of the cell, which reduces the search from three dimensions toward two or one and allows the **homolog** to be found within minutes.

## 9.4. The Holliday Junction

### 9.4.1. Structure and Branch Migration

In 1964 Robin Holliday proposed, to explain gene conversion in fungi, that recombination proceeds through an intermediate in which two duplexes exchange one strand each, so that the four strands form a four-way junction. Such junctions were later seen by electron microscopy in recombining **plasmids** and phage, and their structure was determined by crystallography.

**Definition 9.4.1 (Holliday Junction)** A **Holliday junction** is a four-way DNA junction formed by two homologous duplexes that have exchanged one strand each. Moving the junction along the duplexes, by unpairing and repairing base pairs so that one heteroduplex region grows at the expense of the parental duplexes, is called **branch migration**.

In the presence of $\ce{Mg^2+}$ an isolated junction folds into the stacked-X form, two pairs of coaxially stacked arms crossing at about $60^\circ$; bound to RuvA, it is held in the open, square-planar form with fourfold symmetry. Because every base pair broken during branch migration is replaced by an identical one in homologous DNA, branch migration has almost no free-energy change; it occurs spontaneously, but slowly and in both directions, and in cells it is driven by motors. In *E. coli*, RuvA tetramers bind the junction and two hexameric rings of the ATPase RuvB encircle opposite arms and pump the DNA through the junction, extending heteroduplex for kilobases. RecG, a monomeric helicase, catalyzes branch migration as well, and also converts stalled replication forks into junctions ("fork reversal").

### 9.4.2. Resolution

**Definition 9.4.2 (Resolvase)** A **Holliday junction resolvase** is a structure-specific endonuclease that cuts a Holliday junction by introducing two symmetric nicks in strands of the same polarity across the junction, so that the products are two nicked duplexes that can be sealed by DNA ligase.

The *E. coli* resolvase RuvC acts as a dimer bound with RuvAB at the junction; it cuts preferentially at the sequence $5'$-(A/T)TT↓(G/C)-$3'$, which branch migration must bring to the junction. Because the open junction has two pairs of equivalent strands, it can be cut in two ways, and the two ways give different products.

![The Holliday junction and its two resolutions. A: the open, square-planar junction; its four arms are colored by the parental duplex they derive from (blue: flanks A and B of one duplex; pink: flanks a and b of the homolog). Two strands (continuous) run between arms of the same parent, two (exchanged) pass between a blue and a pink arm. Heteroduplex DNA in the products may contain mismatches where the two parents differed. **Mismatch repair** ([7.2.8](/7.2.8#sec-8.4)) then corrects one strand to match the other; if it corrects the information of one parent to that of the other, the result is gene conversion, the nonreciprocal transfer of sequence that Holliday's model was proposed to explain.

## 9.5. The Eukaryotic Pathways

### 9.5.1. Double-Strand-Break Repair and Synthesis-Dependent Strand Annealing

The modern models of homologous recombination start from a double-strand break, as proposed by Szostak, Orr-Weaver, Rothstein and Stahl (1983) from experiments with gapped plasmids in yeast, which repaired the gap by copying the homologous chromosome. Both ends are resected to long $3'$ tails, one end invades the homolog, and its extension enlarges the D loop. Two routes then lead to different outcomes.

**Definition 9.5.1** In **synthesis-dependent strand annealing** (SDSA), the invading strand, after being extended on the homologous template, is displaced from the D loop and anneals to the complementary $3'$ tail of the other end of the break; filling of the remaining gaps and ligation complete the repair. In the **double-strand-break repair** (DSBR) model, the displaced strand of the D loop anneals to the second end of the break ("second-end capture"), both ends are extended, and ligation produces a joint molecule with two Holliday junctions (a **double Holliday junction**).

![Two pathways of double-strand-break repair by homologous recombination, drawn with the broken duplex in blue and the intact homolog in pink; arrowheads are 3′ ends, dashed segments new DNA copied from the homolog (pink dashes) or from the blue duplex (blue dashes). 1–3: a double-strand break is resected to 3′ single-stranded tails, and one tail invades the homolog, forming a D loop, and is extended. 4–5 (SDSA): the extended strand is displaced and anneals with the other 3′ tail; gap filling and ligation give a noncrossover product carrying a short tract copied from the homolog. 6–8 (DSBR): the displaced strand of the D loop captures the second end; ligation makes a double Holliday junction, which can be dissolved by convergent branch migration and decatenation (BLM–TOP3A–RMI1/2), giving only noncrossovers, or resolved by nucleases (MUS81–EME1, GEN1, SLX1–SLX4), giving crossovers or noncrossovers depending on which strands are cut at each junction.](./plots/dsb-repair-models.py)

In mitotic cells most homologous recombination follows SDSA and yields noncrossovers. This is a protective choice: a crossover between sister chromatids is harmless, but one between homologs causes loss of heterozygosity in the region distal to it, and one between repeated sequences at different positions causes deletions, inversions or translocations. Double Holliday junctions that do form are preferentially dissolved by the "dissolvasome" BLM–TOP3A–RMI1–RMI2 (BTR): the BLM helicase migrates the two junctions toward each other, and **topoisomerase** IIIα removes the final linkage between the two molecules, a reaction that by its mechanism can only produce noncrossovers. In Bloom syndrome, caused by loss of BLM, crossovers between sister chromatids are about ten times more frequent and genome instability leads to cancers of many types. Junctions that escape dissolution are cut by resolvases late in the cell cycle, MUS81–EME1 and SLX1–SLX4 in G2 and GEN1 in mitosis.

When only one end of a break finds homology, as at a collapsed fork or an eroded telomere, the invading end can be extended by a migrating D loop for hundreds of kilobases, to the end of the chromosome: break-induced replication (BIR). BIR is conservative (both new strands end up in the same molecule) and highly mutagenic, and it is used by telomerase-negative cancer cells to maintain their **telomeres** by copying them from other telomeres (alternative lengthening of telomeres; [7.2.7](/7.2.7#sec-7.6)).

### 9.5.2. Meiotic Recombination

In meiosis, recombination is initiated on purpose. The topoisomerase-like protein SPO11 makes hundreds of double-strand breaks per cell (about $150$–$250$ in yeast and mouse), remaining covalently attached to the $5'$ ends until it is released with a short oligonucleotide by MRX/MRN–Sae2/CtIP. In mammals the breaks are placed at hotspots specified by the **histone** methyltransferase PRDM9, whose zinc-finger array binds a particular DNA motif. The meiosis-specific recombinase DMC1 works together with RAD51 and prefers the homolog over the sister chromatid. Most breaks are repaired as noncrossovers, but at least one per chromosome pair becomes a crossover: a set of proteins (the ZMM group) protects selected joint molecules from dissolution, and the MutLγ complex (MLH1–MLH3) resolves the resulting double Holliday junctions into crossovers. The crossovers, visible as chiasmata, hold homologs together until the first meiotic division and ensure their segregation; their genetic consequences, recombination of linked alleles and genetic maps, are the subject of [7.4](/7.4).

## 9.6. Site-Specific Recombination

**Definition 9.6.1** A **site-specific recombinase** cuts and rejoins DNA strands at two short recognition sites, forming a covalent protein–DNA intermediate through an active-site tyrosine or serine, so that the exchange is precise, reciprocal and requires no high-energy cofactor. The outcome depends on the arrangement of the sites: two sites in the same orientation on one molecule lead to deletion of the DNA between them (or, in reverse, integration of a circle), two sites in opposite orientation to inversion, and sites on two molecules to integration or exchange.

The two families use the same transesterification chemistry as topoisomerases ([7.2.3](/7.2.3#sec-3.3)): the hydroxyl of the active-site **residue** attacks a phosphodiester bond, the protein becomes covalently linked to one DNA end, and a free hydroxyl from the partner DNA attacks the protein–DNA bond, restoring a phosphodiester bond with a new partner. Tyrosine recombinases (λ integrase, Cre, Flp, XerC/XerD) exchange one pair of strands, forming a Holliday junction that isomerizes, and then the second pair. Serine recombinases (the γδ and Tn3 resolvases, the Hin and Gin invertases, the integrases of phages φC31 and Bxb1) cut all four strands at once, rotate one half of the synaptic complex by $180^\circ$ relative to the other and rejoin.

The λ system shows how directionality is controlled. Integration needs Int, which binds both the core and "arm" sites flanking the core in the long *attP*, and the host protein IHF, which bends *attP* sharply into an intasome that captures *attB*. The products, *attL* and *attR*, are different sites; recombining them back (excision) requires in addition the phage protein Xis (and FIS), which bends the DNA into a different architecture. The phage thus decides the direction of the reaction by making or not making Xis. In the laboratory the loxP sites of Cre, in the same orientation around an **exon**, allow a gene to be deleted only in cells that express Cre from a tissue-specific promoter: a conditional knockout.

## 9.7. V(D)J Recombination

### 9.7.1. Assembling Antigen-Receptor Genes

The immune system makes about $10^{11}$ different antibodies and T-cell **receptors** from a genome of about $20{,}000$ genes. In 1976 Hozumi and Tonegawa found that the DNA encoding the variable region of an antibody light chain is arranged differently in an antibody-producing myeloma cell and in an embryo: in the embryo the variable segment lies far from the constant region, and in the B cell the two are close. Antibody genes are assembled during lymphocyte development by recombination of gene segments.

**Definition 9.7.1 (V(D)J Recombination)** **V(D)J recombination** is the site-specific rearrangement in developing B and T lymphocytes that assembles the exon encoding the variable region of an antibody or T-cell-receptor chain by joining one variable (V), in heavy chains and some T-cell-receptor chains one diversity (D), and one joining (J) gene segment, chosen from clusters of such segments, with deletion or inversion of the DNA between them.

The human immunoglobulin heavy-chain locus (*IGH*) on chromosome 14 contains about $40$ functional V segments, $23$ D segments and $6$ J segments upstream of the constant-region exons; the κ light-chain locus has about $40$ V and $5$ J segments, and the λ locus about $30$ V and $4$–$5$ J–C pairs. A developing B cell first joins a D to a J segment on the heavy-chain locus, then a V segment to the DJ unit; after it has produced a functional heavy chain it rearranges a light-chain locus.

**Definition 9.7.2 (Recombination Signal Sequence)** A **recombination signal sequence** (RSS) is the signal that flanks each V, D and J segment on the side to be joined: a conserved heptamer (consensus CACAGTG) adjacent to the coding segment, a spacer of $12$ or $23\,\mathrm{bp}$ of variable sequence, and a conserved nonamer (consensus ACAAAAACC).

**Property 9.7.1 (The 12/23 Rule)** Efficient V(D)J recombination occurs only between a segment flanked by an RSS with a $12\,\mathrm{bp}$ spacer and a segment flanked by an RSS with a $23\,\mathrm{bp}$ spacer.

The spacers correspond to about one and two turns of the helix, so the heptamer and nonamer lie on the same face of the DNA in both. In *IGH*, V segments carry 23-RSSs, D segments are flanked by 12-RSSs on both sides, and J segments carry 23-RSSs. The 12/23 rule therefore permits D–J and V–D joining but forbids the direct joining of V to J, which would skip the D segment, and the joining of two V or two J segments.

### 9.7.2. The Recombination Signals Are Necessary and Sufficient

The role of the RSSs was established with an artificial **substrate**. A plasmid carried, in order, a *lac* promoter, an RSS with a $12\,\mathrm{bp}$ spacer, a prokaryotic transcription terminator, an RSS with a $23\,\mathrm{bp}$ spacer, and the chloramphenicol acetyltransferase gene *cat*. In *E. coli* the terminator blocks transcription of *cat*. The plasmid was introduced into a pre-B cell line, which expresses the recombination machinery, then recovered and introduced into *E. coli*: plasmids on which the RSSs had recombined had lost (or inverted) the terminator and conferred chloramphenicol resistance. The frequency of resistant colonies measured the efficiency of recombination. Mutations in the heptamer or the nonamer reduced it to background, and so did insertions or deletions in the spacers that changed their length, whereas changes of spacer sequence that kept the length had little effect. Two RSSs with $12\,\mathrm{bp}$ spacers or two with $23\,\mathrm{bp}$ did not recombine.

### 9.7.3. The RAG Mechanism

The genes for the recombinase, *RAG1* and *RAG2* (recombination activating genes), were identified by Schatz, Oettinger and Baltimore (1989–1990) by transfecting fibroblasts with genomic DNA and selecting cells that could rearrange a recombination substrate; the two genes together conferred V(D)J recombination on non-lymphoid cells, and they are expressed only in developing lymphocytes.

RAG1–RAG2 binds a 12-RSS and a 23-RSS together and cuts at the border between each heptamer and its coding segment in two steps. First it nicks one strand, leaving a $3'$-OH on the coding segment; then that $3'$-OH attacks the phosphodiester bond on the opposite strand, a direct transesterification that closes the coding end into a **hairpin** and leaves a blunt signal end. The signal ends are joined precisely to each other, forming a circle that is lost from the cell (or an inversion when the segments are in opposite orientations). The coding ends are joined by the nonhomologous end-joining machinery ([7.2.8](/7.2.8#sec-8.5)): Artemis, activated by DNA-PKcs, opens each hairpin, often off-center, which leaves a few self-complementary nucleotides on one strand (P nucleotides, for palindromic); **terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase** (TdT, [7.2.5](/7.2.5)) adds random N nucleotides to the $3'$ ends; exonucleases remove some nucleotides; and XRCC4–ligase IV joins the ends. Loss of RAG function, of Artemis or of DNA-PKcs abolishes mature lymphocytes and causes severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID).

The chemistry of RAG is that of a transposase: a nick, then attack of the liberated $3'$-OH on DNA, with hairpin formation as in the excision of the bacterial transposons Tn5 and Tn10 (9.8). In vitro, RAG1–RAG2 can insert the excised signal-end fragment into an unrelated DNA, a transposition reaction. The evolutionary origin was found in 2016: the lancelet *Branchiostoma*, an invertebrate chordate, carries an active transposon, ProtoRAG, whose transposase genes resemble *RAG1* and *RAG2* and whose terminal inverted repeats resemble RSSs. The adaptive immune system of jawed vertebrates began with a domesticated transposon.

### 9.7.4. Counting Diversity

Three mechanisms generate the diversity of antibodies: combinatorial joining of segments, imprecise joining, and, after **antigen** stimulation, somatic hypermutation of the rearranged genes by the cytidine deaminase AID.

**Example 9.7.1** With the segment numbers of 9.7.1, combinatorial joining gives $40\times23\times6 = 5520$ heavy-chain variable regions and $40\times5 + 30\times4 = 320$ light-chain variable regions. Since any heavy chain can pair with any light chain, there are $5520\times320 \approx 1.8\times10^6$ combinations. Junctional diversity is larger. At each of the two junctions of a heavy chain, trimming of $0$–$10$ nucleotides from each end and addition of $0$–$10$ N nucleotides, each of four kinds, give a number of possible junction sequences that is dominated by the N regions: already two N regions of up to five nucleotides allow $(\sum_{k=0}^{5}4^k)^2 \approx 1.9\times10^6$ sequences. Multiplying the combinatorial and junctional factors gives potential repertoires conventionally quoted at $10^{11}$ to more than $10^{13}$, although frameshifts at the junctions make about two thirds of the joins nonproductive. Only a small part of this potential is realized: a human has about $10^{11}$ B cells, and sequencing estimates the number of distinct heavy chains in one person at $10^8$–$10^{10}$.

The same mechanism assembles T-cell-receptor genes, and the same rule applies; class switching and somatic hypermutation, which modify the rearranged genes later, are described with the immunological context in [7.15](/7.15).

## 9.8. Transposable Elements

### 9.8.1. Discovery and Classes

Barbara McClintock, studying maize in the 1940s, found genetic elements that moved from one chromosomal position to another and controlled the expression of the genes near which they landed: the *Ds* (dissociation) element broke chromosomes and could move only in the presence of *Ac* (activator). The idea that genomes contain mobile elements was confirmed in the late 1960s, when insertion mutations in the galactose operon of *E. coli* were found to be caused by insertion of discrete DNA segments; McClintock received the Nobel Prize in 1983.

**Definition 9.8.1** A **transposon** (transposable element) is a DNA segment that can move from one site in a genome to another, usually unrelated, site. **DNA transposons** move through a DNA intermediate, catalyzed by a transposase that they encode; **retrotransposons** move through an RNA intermediate, which is reverse-transcribed into a DNA copy that is inserted at the new site.

**Definition 9.8.2** A **target-site duplication** is the short direct repeat ($2$–$14\,\mathrm{bp}$, characteristic of each element) of host DNA that flanks an inserted transposable element; it arises because the transposase cuts the two strands of the target at staggered positions and the single-stranded gaps left at both sides after insertion are filled in by repair synthesis.

### 9.8.2. Mechanisms

Most DNA transposases and retroviral integrases belong to one structural family, named for the conserved D, D, E (aspartate, aspartate, glutamate) residues that bind two $\ce{Mg^2+}$ ions, and they use the same two reactions as RAG. The transposase binds both ends of the element and brings them together in a synaptic complex; it cleaves the element's $3'$ ends, exposing $3'$-OH groups; and in the strand-transfer step these $3'$-OH groups attack two phosphodiester bonds of the target, a few base pairs apart on opposite strands, by direct transesterification. The element is joined to the target, the staggered single-stranded gaps are filled in by host enzymes, and the target-site duplication results. The donor site is left with a double-strand break, repaired by end joining or by recombination with the sister chromatid (which can restore the element, so that cut-and-paste transposition can increase the copy number). In replicative transposition (Tn3, Mu), only the $3'$ ends are cut before strand transfer, so the element remains joined to the donor; replication through the element produces two copies and a cointegrate of donor and target, which a resolvase separates.

Non-LTR retrotransposons insert by target-primed reverse transcription: the LINE-1 ORF2 protein nicks the target DNA (often at $5'$-TTTT/A-$3'$), and the reverse transcriptase uses the $3'$-OH at the nick as the primer to copy the L1 RNA directly into the target site, after which the second strand is made and the element is joined. Most new copies are truncated at their $5'$ ends because reverse transcription stops early. Of more than $500{,}000$ LINE-1 copies in the human genome, only about $80$–$100$ are complete and active; they supply the enzymes for Alu elements, which compete for them, and for processed pseudogenes ([7.2.2](/7.2.2)). New Alu insertions occur in about one of every $20$–$40$ human births, LINE-1 insertions less often, and they occasionally cause disease by inserting into genes.

### 9.8.3. Hosts and Mobile Elements

Because insertions are mostly harmful, hosts restrain transposons. Bacterial transposases are made in small amounts and act preferentially in *cis* on the element that encodes them; transposition is coupled to replication or limited to newly replicated DNA by methylation. Eukaryotes silence transposons by DNA methylation and repressive chromatin, guided in the germline by PIWI-interacting RNAs ([7.2.18](/7.2.18)) and in mammals by hundreds of KRAB zinc-finger proteins that evolved to recognize particular element families. The P element shows what happens without such restraint: when males carrying P elements are crossed with females from strains that lack them, P elements transpose at high rates in the germline of the offspring, causing sterility and mutations (hybrid dysgenesis), whereas females carrying P elements transmit piRNAs that repress them.

Transposons are also a source of new functions. RAG (9.7.3), telomerase ([7.2.7](/7.2.7#sec-7.6)), the centromere protein CENP-B, the syncytin proteins of the placenta (derived from retroviral envelope genes), and many enhancers and promoters derived from transposon sequences are examples of domesticated elements. The genetic effects of transposition, its role in genome evolution, and the use of transposons for mutagenesis in genetics are treated in [7.4](/7.4); transposon-based tools such as *Sleeping Beauty* and *piggyBac* vectors, and transposons guided by CRISPR RNAs, appear in [7.2.24](/7.2.24).

## Questions

1. A linear DNA of $20\,\mathrm{kb}$ carries a Chi site $5\,\mathrm{kb}$ from its left end in the orientation recognized by RecBCD entering from that end. Describe the single-stranded products made by RecBCD entering from the left end, and from the right end, assuming recognition at every encounter.

   **Answer** From the left end, RecBCD unwinds and degrades both strands (the $3'$-ended strand vigorously) up to Chi. At Chi it stops degrading the $3'$-ended strand and continues to degrade the $5'$-ended strand, so beyond Chi it produces a $3'$-ended single strand that begins at Chi and extends toward the right as far as RecBCD travels, coated with RecA; this strand is the substrate for strand invasion. From the right end, RecBCD meets the same Chi in the wrong orientation (approaching from its $5'$ side) and ignores it: both strands are degraded as far as the enzyme goes, and no recombinogenic end is produced. In reality only about $30\,\%$ of correctly oriented encounters are recognized.

2. Why must the invading strand in homologous recombination carry a $3'$ end, and why are double-strand breaks resected to $3'$ rather than $5'$ tails?

   **Answer** After pairing with the template strand of the homologous duplex, the invading end must serve as a primer for DNA synthesis that copies the information lost at the break; DNA polymerases extend only $3'$-OH ends ([7.2.7](/7.2.7)). A $5'$ end could pair but not be extended. Resection by $5' \to 3'$ degradation, by RecBCD after Chi, by EXO1 or by DNA2 with BLM, therefore produces exactly the end that can prime repair synthesis.

3. A Holliday junction forms between two homologous chromosomes at a point between genes $A$ and $B$; one chromosome carries $A$ and $B$, the other $a$ and $b$. Which cuts generate chromosomes carrying (a) $A$ and $b$, (b) $a$ and $b$? Which outcome would a dissolution of a double Holliday junction give?

   **Answer** (a) Cutting the two continuous strands (those that do not pass between the two parental duplexes) joins the left flank of one parent to the right flank of the other, a crossover: products $Ab$ and $aB$. (b) Cutting the two exchanged strands restores the parental arrangement of flanks: products $AB$ and $ab$, with heteroduplex between the two genes (a noncrossover). Dissolution by BLM–TOP3A–RMI1/2 converges the two junctions and unlinks them without cutting, so it always yields the parental arrangement, $AB$ and $ab$.

4. Two *loxP* sites flank a gene. Predict the result of Cre action if the sites are (a) in the same orientation, (b) in opposite orientation. Why is the deletion reaction effectively irreversible in cells, although Cre catalyzes both directions?

   **Answer** (a) Recombination between directly repeated sites deletes the gene as a circle carrying one *loxP*, leaving one *loxP* in the chromosome. (b) Inverted sites lead to inversion of the gene, which can flip back and forth, giving a mixture of both orientations. The reverse of deletion requires the excised circle to find the remaining chromosomal *loxP*; the circle cannot replicate and is diluted and degraded, and the intramolecular deletion is much faster than the intermolecular integration, so the deletion accumulates.

5. In a pre-B cell, the plasmid of 9.7.2 is modified so that both RSSs have $12\,\mathrm{bp}$ spacers. What is the expected frequency of chloramphenicol-resistant colonies? What happens if a $10\,\mathrm{bp}$ insertion is made in the $23\,\mathrm{bp}$ spacer, and why does a $10\,\mathrm{bp}$ insertion matter more than a change of sequence?

   **Answer** With two 12-RSSs, the 12/23 rule forbids the reaction, and resistant colonies appear only at the background frequency. A $10\,\mathrm{bp}$ insertion converts the $23$-spacer into a $33\,\mathrm{bp}$ spacer, about one helical turn longer; the RAG complex requires one RSS with a $12\,\mathrm{bp}$ and one with a $23\,\mathrm{bp}$ spacer, because the heptamer and the nonamer bind different parts of the complex at fixed distances and orientations. The length of the spacer sets the distance and the angle between them; its sequence is not read, so sequence changes that keep the length have little effect.

6. Estimate the number of distinct heavy–light chain pairs that combinatorial joining can produce in the T-cell receptor αβ, given about $45$ functional Vα and $50$ Jα segments, and $50$ Vβ, $2$ Dβ and $13$ Jβ segments. Compare with the antibody estimate of Example 9.7.1.

   **Answer** α chains: $45\times50 = 2250$; β chains: $50\times2\times13 = 1300$; pairs: $2250\times1300 \approx 2.9\times10^6$, similar to the $1.8\times10^6$ for antibodies. As for antibodies, junctional diversity contributes far more, especially in the β chain with its two junctions; T-cell receptors do not undergo somatic hypermutation, so the junctions are their main source of diversity.

7. (Open question) Why might a genome tolerate transposable elements that occupy nearly half of it, as in humans? Discuss costs and benefits, and why most elements are inactive.

   **Answer** Points to include: transposons are selfish elements that spread by replicating within the genome faster than the host genome replicates, so they can increase in number even if they lower host fitness slightly, especially in species with small effective population sizes where weak selection is inefficient (a population-genetic argument, [7.10](/7.10)). Each active copy causes insertional mutations, so hosts evolved silencing (methylation, piRNAs, KRAB zinc fingers), and most copies decay by mutation into inert sequence; only about $100$ LINE-1s remain active in humans. Benefits are occasional: elements supply regulatory sequences, coding sequences that are domesticated (RAG, syncytins, telomerase reverse transcriptase is related to retrotransposon reverse transcriptases), and they promote genome rearrangement. Their abundance is therefore the result of a balance among insertion, silencing, decay and occasional positive selection rather than of a function for the whole mass.
