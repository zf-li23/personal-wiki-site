The machines of the previous chapters, **RNA polymerase**, the **spliceosome** and the **ribosome**, are assemblies of fixed composition and structure, a few to a few tens of nanometers across, whose atomic models come from crystallography and **cryo-electron microscopy**. A cell also contains compartments of a different kind: micrometer-sized bodies such as the nucleolus, nuclear speckles, **P-bodies** and **stress granules**, which have no membrane, contain hundreds of different proteins and RNAs, exchange their components with the surroundings within seconds, and fuse and round up like droplets. Since 2009 they have been understood as the products of liquid–liquid phase separation, the same demixing that separates oil from vinegar, driven by many **weak interactions** among multivalent proteins and nucleic acids. This chapter describes what condensates are and how they are recognized (21.1), the molecular features that make proteins and RNAs phase-separate (21.2), the physics of the threshold, which follows from the polymer thermodynamics of [6.3.9](/6.3.9) and [6.3.21](/6.3.21) (21.3), and three places where condensates bear on the subject of this course: transcription at super-enhancers (21.4), the nucleolus and **heterochromatin** (21.5), and the transition from liquids to pathological solids (21.6). The chapter ends with what is still uncertain (21.7), which is a good deal. The physics of demixing kinetics and of soft matter in cells is treated in [7.6](/7.6); the stress granules and P-bodies of **mRNA** **metabolism** were introduced in [7.2.18](/7.2.18).

## 21.1. From Machines to Condensates

### 21.1.1. Three Kinds of Assembly

Eukaryotic cells concentrate enzymes and **substrates** in three ways. Membrane-enclosed organelles (nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, lysosome) separate their contents from the cytosol with a **lipid bilayer** and **transporters** ([7.1.13](/7.1.13)). Macromolecular machines bind a fixed set of **subunits** at defined positions; every copy has the same parts and the same three-dimensional structure. Condensates are a third kind.

**Definition 21.1.1 (Liquid–Liquid Phase Separation)** **Liquid–liquid phase separation** (LLPS) is the spontaneous separation of a solution into two coexisting liquid phases of different composition, a dense phase enriched in some components and a dilute phase depleted of them, which occurs when this lowers the free energy of the system.

**Definition 21.1.2 (Biomolecular Condensate)** A **biomolecular condensate** (a membraneless organelle) is a two- or three-dimensional compartment, typically $0.1$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ across and without a surrounding membrane, that concentrates a set of proteins and usually RNA held together by many weak, constantly exchanging interactions, so that its components move within it and exchange with the surroundings.

The definition is deliberately descriptive: "condensate" names the structure, while phase separation is one mechanism, the best understood, that can produce it (21.7).

Condensates serve two broad purposes. Some are factories: concentrating enzymes and substrates speeds a reaction (the nucleolus makes ribosomes) or couples successive steps. Others are depots: sequestering molecules stops their activity until the condensate dissolves (stress granules hold mRNAs and **initiation factors** during stress). Assembly and disassembly are reversible and regulated: the nucleolus dissolves in mitosis and reforms in early interphase by the fusion of droplets that appear on each nucleolus organizer region; replication, repair and transcription "factories" appear only where and when these processes take place.

### 21.1.2. How Liquid Behavior Is Recognized

The liquid nature of a condensate was first shown for the P granules of *Caenorhabditis elegans* embryos (Brangwynne, Hyman and colleagues, 2009). In the one-cell embryo the granules first fill the cytoplasm and then become restricted to the posterior, the future germ line. Time-lapse imaging showed that they do not move as solid particles: they flow, drip when sheared, fuse into larger round drops, and the posterior localization arises because granules dissolve in the anterior and condense in the posterior, following a gradient in the concentration threshold for their assembly (set by the RNA-binding protein MEX-5). Since then the following observations have been used as evidence for a liquid:

1. *Round shape*, which minimizes the interfacial area at a given volume, the mark of a **surface tension** ([6.3.19](/6.3.19)).
2. *Fusion and relaxation*: two droplets that touch coalesce and relax to a sphere.
3. *Internal mixing and exchange*: after photobleaching part or all of a droplet, fluorescence recovers by diffusion inside it and by exchange with the dilute phase. This is measured by **FRAP** ([7.1.13](/7.1.13)); recovery half-times of seconds to minutes are typical.
4. *A concentration threshold* above which droplets appear and below which they dissolve (21.3), and sensitivity of this threshold to temperature, salt and specific mutations.

**Example 21.1.1 (The time scale of fusion)** When two droplets of radius $R$ fuse, the driving force is the surface tension $\gamma$ and the resistance is the viscosity $\eta$; dimensional analysis gives a relaxation time
$$
\tau \approx \frac{\eta R}{\gamma}.
$$
For P granules, viscosities of the order of $1\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ (about a thousand times that of water) and surface tensions of the order of $1\,\mu\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ (about $10^5$ times smaller than that of water against air, $72\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$) have been estimated, so a $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ droplet should relax in about $1\times10^{-6}/10^{-6} \approx 1\,\mathrm{s}$, as observed. Plotting the measured relaxation time of many fusion events against droplet size gives a straight line whose slope is the inverse capillary velocity $\eta/\gamma$, one of the few material constants that can be measured in a living cell. Very large values of $\eta/\gamma$, or relaxation that stops before the droplets are round, indicate a gel or a solid (21.6).

**Remark 21.1.1** None of these criteria proves phase separation. Round foci with rapid **FRAP** recovery are also formed by transient binding of molecules to a fixed scaffold (for example repeated **binding sites** on DNA), and many foci in cells are too small to be resolved, let alone to be watched fusing. The aliphatic alcohol 1,6-hexanediol, widely used to dissolve condensates, also disturbs other weak interactions and cell physiology at the concentrations used. The strongest evidence combines several criteria with a quantitative test of the threshold behavior predicted by thermodynamics (21.3.2).

## 21.2. Multivalency and Intrinsically Disordered Regions

### 21.2.1. Scaffolds, Clients and Valency

What kind of molecule makes a condensate? Each condensate has at least one scaffold, a protein or RNA that can make several independent interactions at once, with itself or with other molecules.

**Definition 21.2.1 (Multivalency)** The valence of a molecule is the number of interaction sites through which it can bind partners simultaneously; a molecule with two or more such sites is multivalent, and **multivalency** of the components is the property that allows interacting molecules to form extended networks rather than closed complexes of fixed size.

The reasoning is that of polymer gelation. Each individual contact is weak and breaks often, but while one contact is broken the others keep the molecule in the network; by the time those break, new contacts have formed elsewhere. All molecules in the condensate keep interacting even though the set of interactions changes from moment to moment. Molecules with only one site, called clients, can partition into the network by binding a scaffold, but they cap the network rather than extend it and cannot drive its formation. A monovalent **ligand** added in excess therefore dissolves a condensate by occupying sites, a useful experimental test.

**Example 21.2.1 (Valence controls the threshold)** Li, Rosen and colleagues (2012) used two engineered proteins: one with $n$ repeats of an SH3 domain, the other with $n$ repeats of a proline-rich motif that the SH3 domain binds with a dissociation constant of a few hundred micromolar. Mixed in vitro, the pairs with $n = 4$ or $5$ separated into liquid droplets at micromolar concentrations, far below the dissociation constant of a single contact, while lower valences required much higher concentrations or did not separate in the range tested; the boundary of the two-phase region moved steeply to lower concentrations as $n$ increased. The same behavior was found for natural proteins of the nephrin–Nck–N-WASP pathway in actin assembly: the phosphorylated tails of the membrane protein nephrin (three phosphotyrosines), the adaptor Nck (one SH2 and three SH3 domains) and N-WASP (several proline-rich motifs) formed droplets in which N-WASP stimulated Arp2/3 complex–driven actin polymerization more strongly than in solution. Condensation was thus not a side effect: it changed the biochemical activity of the components.

### 21.2.2. Intrinsically Disordered Regions and Low-Complexity Domains

The modular domains of Example 21.2.1 are one source of multivalency; the other, more common in natural condensates, is disordered polypeptide. **Intrinsically disordered proteins** were introduced in [7.1.5](/7.1.5); here the relevant unit is often a region within an otherwise folded protein.

**Definition 21.2.2 (Intrinsically Disordered Region)** An **intrinsically disordered region** (IDR) is a segment of a protein that has no stable folded structure under physiological conditions and exists as an ensemble of rapidly interconverting conformations. An IDR whose sequence is dominated by a few amino acids (for example glycine, serine, tyrosine and glutamine, or arginine and glycine) is called a low-complexity domain (LCD); those resembling the domains of yeast prions are called prion-like domains.

By sequence prediction, about a third of eukaryotic proteins contain disordered segments longer than $30$ residues, and many such segments are conserved over long evolutionary times, which means they have functions. Disorder is not one function: the elastin of the extracellular matrix is a cross-linked disordered network that stretches and recoils, and the activation domains of transcription factors bind their targets in disordered "fuzzy" complexes ([7.2.14](/7.2.14)). In condensates, IDRs supply many weak contacts.

The *sticker–spacer* picture (Pappu and colleagues) describes an IDR as a chain of stickers, residues or short motifs that make the attractive contacts, separated by spacers that determine the chain's flexibility and its interaction with water. In the low-complexity domain of FUS and related RNA-binding proteins, tyrosines are the main stickers and the arginines of the adjacent RGG repeats their partners (cation–π and π–π contacts); the concentration threshold for phase separation fell systematically as tyrosines and arginines were added and rose as they were removed, roughly in proportion to their number (Wang, Hyman and colleagues, 2018). Other IDRs use blocks of opposite charge (the DEAD-box helicase DDX4), hydrophobic contacts or hydrogen bonds. The contacts are **noncovalent interactions** of the kinds treated in [7.1.2](/7.1.2), each worth no more than a few $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$.

RNA is itself a multivalent scaffold: a long RNA offers many binding sites for RNA-binding proteins and can base-pair with other RNAs. At low concentrations RNA promotes the condensation of RNA-binding proteins by bridging them; at high concentrations it dissolves them, because each protein then finds its own RNA (re-entrant behavior). This is one way transcription can regulate condensates: the nucleolus and paraspeckles assemble on their nascent RNAs (rRNA, the long noncoding RNA *NEAT1*; **long noncoding RNA**, [7.2.18](/7.2.18)).

Because the contacts are weak, small changes in them move the threshold. Phosphorylation adds negative charge to IDRs and usually dissolves condensates (Pol II CTD, 21.4.3; FUS by DNA-PK); arginine methylation weakens cation–π contacts; temperature, salt and pH act on all of them. This sensitivity is what makes condensates regulatable, and is the physical content of the threshold, to which we now turn.

## 21.3. The Physics of Phase Separation

### 21.3.1. The Flory–Huggins Free Energy

Why should weak attractions demix a solution at all? Mixing increases entropy; separating into two phases is favorable only if the energy gained by bringing like molecules together exceeds the entropy lost. The balance was worked out for small molecules in the regular solution model ([6.3.9](/6.3.9)) and for polymers by Flory and Huggins (**Flory–Huggins theory**, [6.3.21](/6.3.21)). Place polymer chains of $N$ segments and solvent molecules on a lattice whose sites are the size of one segment, and let $\phi$ be the volume fraction of polymer.

**Formula 21.3.1 (Flory–Huggins Free Energy of Mixing)** The free energy of mixing per lattice site is
$$
\frac{f(\phi)}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T} = \frac{\phi}{N}\ln\phi + (1 - \phi)\ln(1 - \phi) + \chi\,\phi(1 - \phi), \tag{21.1}
$$
where the dimensionless interaction parameter $\chi$ measures the net free energy, in units of $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$, of replacing segment–solvent contacts by segment–segment and solvent–solvent contacts; $\chi > 0$ means that segments prefer each other.

The first two terms are the entropy of mixing, the third the interaction. The essential point is the factor $1/N$ in the first term: a chain of $N$ segments contributes only one unit of translational entropy, not $N$, because its segments are already tied together. For $N = 1$, Eq. (21.1) is the regular solution of [6.3.9](/6.3.9) with $\chi = W/RT$. For proteins and RNAs, which are polymers of hundreds of residues, the entropic cost of demixing is therefore much smaller than for an equal mass of small molecules, and a modest attraction per segment suffices.

**Proposition 21.3.1 (Spinodal and Critical Point)** For the free energy (21.1), a homogeneous solution of composition $\phi$ is locally unstable when $f''(\phi) < 0$; the boundary, the **spinodal**, is
$$
\frac{1}{N\phi} + \frac{1}{1 - \phi} = 2\chi, \tag{21.2}
$$
and the critical point, the lowest $\chi$ at which phase separation is possible, is
$$
\phi_{\mathrm{c}} = \frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{N}}, \qquad \chi_{\mathrm{c}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)^{2}. \tag{21.3}
$$

**Proof** Differentiating (21.1) twice, $f''/k_{\mathrm{B}}T = 1/(N\phi) + 1/(1 - \phi) - 2\chi$, which vanishes on the curve (21.2). The critical point is the minimum of $\chi$ along this curve, where also $f''' = 0$: $-1/(N\phi^2) + 1/(1 - \phi)^2 = 0$, so $(1 - \phi)/\phi = \sqrt{N}$ and $\phi_{\mathrm{c}} = 1/(1 + \sqrt{N})$. Substituting in (21.2), $2\chi_{\mathrm{c}} = (1 + \sqrt{N})/N + (1 + \sqrt{N})/\sqrt{N} = (1 + \sqrt{N})^2/N$. $\square$

For small molecules ($N = 1$) the critical point is at $\phi_{\mathrm{c}} = 1/2$ and $\chi_{\mathrm{c}} = 2$. For long chains, $\phi_{\mathrm{c}} \approx N^{-1/2} \to 0$ and $\chi_{\mathrm{c}} \to 1/2$, the θ condition at which the second **virial coefficient** of the polymer vanishes ([6.3.21](/6.3.21)): a long polymer phase-separates as soon as the solvent becomes marginally poor, and at low concentration. Outside the spinodal but inside the coexistence curve, the binodal, the homogeneous solution is metastable; the compositions of the two coexisting phases are given by the common tangent of $f$, that is, equal **chemical potentials** of each component in the two phases, exactly as in [6.3.9](/6.3.9).

Real condensates are made of heteropolymers with specific stickers, usually of several components, in a crowded solution far from ideal, and Eq. (21.1) is at best a qualitative guide. Its qualitative lessons are robust: the threshold falls steeply with chain length and with the strength and number of contacts; the dense phase is a concentrated but still liquid polymer solution (a few hundred milligrams of protein per milliliter); and the dilute phase can be very dilute.

### 21.3.2. The Saturation Concentration and the Lever Rule

**Definition 21.3.1 (Saturation Concentration)** At given temperature, salt and composition of the other components, the **saturation concentration** $C_{\mathrm{sat}}$ of a phase-separating macromolecule is the concentration above which the homogeneous solution separates into a dilute phase of concentration $C_{\mathrm{sat}}$ and a dense phase of concentration $C_{\mathrm{den}}$; for a single component these are the two branches of the binodal.

For a single component, conservation of mass gives the volumes of the two phases (**lever rule**, [6.3.9](/6.3.9)).

**Formula 21.3.2 (Lever Rule for a Condensate)** At total concentration $c$ with $C_{\mathrm{sat}} < c < C_{\mathrm{den}}$, the fraction of the volume occupied by the dense phase is
$$
\frac{V_{\mathrm{den}}}{V} = \frac{c - C_{\mathrm{sat}}}{C_{\mathrm{den}} - C_{\mathrm{sat}}}, \tag{21.4}
$$
and the concentration in the dilute phase stays equal to $C_{\mathrm{sat}}$ however much material is added.

This is the most useful quantitative prediction of phase separation, because it can be tested in cells: if foci form by phase separation of one scaffold, then increasing its expression should enlarge the condensates, not raise the concentration outside them. A corollary is that phase separation buffers the concentration of the scaffold in the dilute phase against cell-to-cell variation in expression, which is substantial for every protein (22.5): as long as a cell is above $C_{\mathrm{sat}}$, its nucleoplasm or cytoplasm contains the scaffold at $C_{\mathrm{sat}}$ (Klosin, Hyman and colleagues, 2020, showed such buffering for an engineered system and proposed it as a function).

**Example 21.3.1 (A test that real condensates fail)** Riback, Brangwynne and colleagues (2020) varied the expression of nucleolar proteins in human cells and measured their concentrations inside and outside nucleoli. For nucleophosmin (NPM1), the main scaffold of the granular component, the dilute-phase concentration was not constant: it rose with the total concentration, in contrast to Eq. (21.4). The explanation is that the nucleolus contains many components with heterotypic interactions (NPM1 binds rRNA and arginine-rich proteins). For several components the phase boundary is a surface in a multidimensional concentration space, and the tie lines that connect coexisting compositions need not be parallel to the axis of the component being varied; adding NPM1 changes the composition of both phases. The lever rule in its one-component form is therefore a test of whether a single scaffold dominates, not a general law, and buffering is weaker for multicomponent condensates. The same work found that the free energy of transferring a protein into the nucleolus could be computed from its partition coefficient, $\Delta G = -k_{\mathrm{B}}T\ln(C_{\mathrm{den}}/C_{\mathrm{dil}})$, and that it depends on the composition of the condensate.

### 21.3.3. Nucleation, Spinodal Decomposition and Coarsening

Phase separation can begin in two ways, depending on where the solution lies in the phase diagram. Between the binodal and the spinodal, the homogeneous solution is metastable: small fluctuations decay, and a dense droplet must reach a critical size before it grows, as in the condensation of a vapor (**homogeneous nucleation**, [6.3.19](/6.3.19)). The barrier is lowered at surfaces or on binding sites that locally raise the concentration (heterogeneous nucleation), which is how cells can determine where a condensate forms: the nucleolus nucleates on the rDNA, a transcriptional condensate on clustered binding sites at an enhancer (21.4).

**Definition 21.3.2 (Spinodal Decomposition)** Inside the spinodal, where $f''(\phi) < 0$, a homogeneous solution is unstable to infinitesimal composition fluctuations, and fluctuations of a characteristic wavelength grow everywhere at once without a nucleation barrier; this mode of demixing is called **spinodal decomposition**.

The kinetics are described by the Cahn–Hilliard equation, in which the flux of polymer is driven by the gradient of the local exchange chemical potential, including a gradient-energy term that penalizes sharp interfaces:
$$
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} = \nabla\cdot\left[M\,\nabla\left(f'(\phi) - \kappa\nabla^2\phi\right)\right],
$$
with mobility $M$ and gradient coefficient $\kappa$; its derivation and analysis belong to [7.6](/7.6). After the dense domains have formed, they coarsen: small droplets dissolve and large ones grow (**Ostwald ripening**, [6.3.19](/6.3.19)), and touching droplets fuse, which in a closed system ends with a single drop. In cells this end state is usually not reached: many nucleoli or speckles coexist for hours. Proposed reasons include the slowness of coarsening for few large drops, the elasticity of the chromatin that surrounds nuclear condensates, and active chemical reactions (modification and demodification, RNA synthesis and degradation) that keep condensates out of equilibrium and can select a size.

## 21.4. Transcriptional Condensates

### 21.4.1. Super-Enhancers

**Enhancers** recruit activators, coactivators and the Mediator complex to promoters ([7.2.14](/7.2.14)). Genome-wide maps of coactivator binding showed that a small number of enhancers are exceptional.

**Definition 21.4.1 (Super-Enhancer)** A **super-enhancer** is a cluster of enhancers, operationally defined by chromatin immunoprecipitation and sequencing as the top-ranked regions in the summed signal of a coactivator (Mediator subunit MED1, BRD4) or of the active histone mark H3K27ac, after merging enhancers less than $12.5\,\mathrm{kb}$ apart; super-enhancers span up to tens of kilobases and are bound by roughly an order of magnitude more coactivator than typical enhancers.

In mouse embryonic stem cells, Whyte, Young and colleagues (2013) identified 231 such regions, occupied by the master transcription factors Oct4, Sox2 and Nanog and associated with the genes that define the cell's identity (including those of the master factors themselves); in tumors, super-enhancers drive oncogenes such as *MYC*. Genes controlled by super-enhancers are unusually sensitive to perturbation of the shared machinery: low doses of JQ1, which displaces BRD4 from acetylated histones (a **bromodomain** inhibitor), or of inhibitors of the kinase CDK7 of TFIIH ([7.2.13](/7.2.13)) selectively reduce their expression (Lovén and colleagues, 2013; Kwiatkowski and colleagues, 2014). Why a cluster of enhancers should respond so steeply to the concentration of shared factors is what the phase-separation model tries to explain. The definition is operational, and whether super-enhancers are more than the sum of their parts has been debated: for the α-globin super-enhancer, deletion of its five constituent elements one at a time showed largely additive contributions (Hay, Higgs and colleagues, 2016).

### 21.4.2. The Phase-Separation Model of Transcriptional Control

Hnisz, Shrinivas, Young, Chakraborty and Sharp (2017) proposed that super-enhancers form phase-separated condensates of transcription factors, coactivators and Pol II, and made a minimal model to show what this would imply. The components (transcription factors, Mediator, BRD4, the Pol II CTD, nascent RNA) are represented as chains, each with $f$ residues that can form noncovalent cross-links with residues on other chains, but only when modified (for example phosphorylated or acetylated); modification and demodification are catalyzed by "modifiers" and "demodifiers" (kinases and phosphatases, acetyltransferases and deacetylases). The model has three parameters: the number $N$ of chains in the volume of the enhancer region (larger for a super-enhancer: $N = 50$ against $10$ for a typical enhancer, reflecting the tenfold difference in coactivator density), the valency $f$, and the equilibrium constant $K_{\mathrm{eq}} = k_{\mathrm{on}}/k_{\mathrm{off}}$ of a cross-link. Transcriptional activity is taken to be the size of the largest connected cluster divided by $N$.

The equilibrium theory of such associating polymers gives the threshold. It is the gel point of the classical Flory–Stockmayer theory of branching.

**Proposition 21.4.1 (Threshold for Network Formation)** Let molecules with $f$ equivalent sites at concentration $c$ form reversible bonds between sites on different molecules with equilibrium constant $K$, and neglect loops. A molecule-spanning network forms above the critical concentration
$$
c^* = \frac{f - 1}{K f (f - 2)^2} \approx \frac{1}{K f^2} \quad (f \gg 1). \tag{21.5}
$$

**Proof** Let $p$ be the fraction of sites that are bonded. The concentration of free sites is $(1 - p)fc$ and of bonds $pfc/2$, so mass action for the association of two free sites gives $pfc/2 = K'[(1 - p)fc]^2$, that is $p/(1 - p)^2 = Kfc$ with $K = 2K'$. On a tree, following a bond to a neighbor leads to $f - 1$ further sites, each bonded with probability $p$; the cluster becomes infinite when the expected number of continuations exceeds one, $p(f - 1) > 1$, so $p_{\mathrm{c}} = 1/(f - 1)$. Substituting, $Kfc^* = p_{\mathrm{c}}/(1 - p_{\mathrm{c}})^2 = (f - 1)/(f - 2)^2$. $\square$

This is the result quoted by Hnisz and colleagues, $C^* \propto 1/(K_{\mathrm{eq}}f^2)$: the threshold depends on the square of the valency and only linearly on the affinity. Doubling the number of modified sites lowers the threshold fourfold, while a fourfold increase in affinity would require a substantial change in each binding interface. A signal that phosphorylates or acetylates many sites at once, or a factor such as BRD4 that binds many partners, therefore moves an enhancer across the threshold far more effectively than a change in any single binding constant. In stochastic simulations of the model with the **Gillespie algorithm** ([4.4.7](/4.4.7#sec-7.2)), the super-enhancer reached 90 % of its maximal activity at twice the reference valency, the typical enhancer only at five times; activity rose abruptly for the super-enhancer and gradually for the typical enhancer, and the **Hill coefficient** of the response to valency grew with the number of components, roughly as $N^{0.4}$. Phase separation is a **cooperativity** effect of the whole assembly, in the same sense as cooperative binding to a protein with several sites ([7.1.7](/7.1.7)), but with the number of participating molecules, not of sites, setting the steepness.

The model made several predictions that could be tested:

1. *The components of super-enhancers form condensates in cells.* BRD4 and MED1 form nuclear puncta at super-enhancers that have liquid properties (fusion, FRAP, sensitivity to 1,6-hexanediol), and their IDRs form droplets in vitro (Sabari, Young and colleagues, 2018). Live-cell single-molecule and super-resolution imaging showed clusters of Mediator and Pol II hundreds of nanometers across, some stable for many seconds, at sites of active transcription (Cho, Cissé and colleagues, 2018). The activation domains of transcription factors (OCT4, GCN4, the estrogen receptor) partition into Mediator condensates, and mutations that abolish activation also abolish this partitioning (Boija and colleagues, 2018).
2. *Super-enhancer genes are hypersensitive to perturbations that reduce valency.* This is the observed sensitivity to BRD4 and CDK7 inhibition.
3. *Strong enhancers produce distinctive transcriptional bursts* ([7.2.22](/7.2.22)), and one condensate can drive several genes at once.
4. *A heavily cross-linked assembly can lock into a metastable state* that does not dissolve when the signal falls, a possible mechanism for pathological, signal-independent expression programs.

### 21.4.3. The CTD as a Switch Between Condensates

The **carboxyl-terminal domain** of Pol II ([7.2.12](/7.2.12)) is the clearest case of a disordered region whose modification moves a protein from one condensate to another. Its $52$ heptad repeats (in mammals) are disordered and multivalent. Unphosphorylated CTD binds the hydrogel-forming low-complexity domains of FUS and related proteins, and phosphorylation releases it (Kwon, McKnight and colleagues, 2013). Guo, Young and colleagues (2019) found that the hypophosphorylated CTD partitions into Mediator condensates, the state of polymerase recruited to a promoter, whereas phosphorylation by CDK7 (TFIIH) and CDK9 (P-TEFb) excludes it from Mediator condensates and allows it to enter condensates of splicing factors such as SRSF2. The phosphorylation that marks the transition from initiation to elongation ([7.2.13](/7.2.13)) would then also move polymerase from an initiation compartment to a processing compartment, coupling transcription and co-transcriptional processing ([7.2.17](/7.2.17)). This picture is not settled: structured-illumination imaging of fixed cells showed only sparse co-localization of Pol II with $3'$-end processing factors (Yao, Chen and colleagues, 2019), and splicing occurs co-transcriptionally at thousands of sites, so "processing factories", if they exist, must be small and dynamic.

## 21.5. Nuclear Bodies and Heterochromatin

### 21.5.1. The Nucleolus: A Multiphase Condensate

The nucleolus is the largest condensate of the nucleus and the one most clearly produced by phase separation. In the giant nuclei of *Xenopus laevis* oocytes, which contain hundreds of free nucleoli, Brangwynne, Mitchison and Hyman (2011) showed that nucleoli fuse and round up with an ATP-dependent viscosity. The nucleolus is layered (fibrillar centers within a dense fibrillar component within a granular component; Figure in 21.1), and Feric, Brangwynne and colleagues (2016) explained the layering as the coexistence of immiscible liquid phases. The two principal scaffolds, fibrillarin of the dense fibrillar component and nucleophosmin of the granular component, each form droplets with RNA in vitro, and the two kinds of droplet do not mix: fibrillarin droplets become engulfed by nucleophosmin droplets, reproducing the nucleolar arrangement. Which phase engulfs which is determined by the interfacial tensions: the configuration with the lowest total interfacial free energy places the phase with the higher surface tension against the nucleoplasm inside. The layers correspond to the steps of ribosome biogenesis ([7.2.17](/7.2.17)): Pol I transcribes the rDNA at the border of the fibrillar centers ([7.2.12](/7.2.12)), the nascent pre-rRNA is modified and cleaved in the dense fibrillar component (fibrillarin is the methyltransferase of the C/D box snoRNPs), and preribosomal particles are assembled in the granular component before export. The multiphase structure thus arranges a sequence of reactions in space, a function that a single liquid could not perform.

Other nuclear bodies use RNA as scaffold. Paraspeckles assemble on the long noncoding RNA *NEAT1*, which recruits NONO, SFPQ and FUS; deleting the middle domain of *NEAT1*, which binds NONO and SFPQ, prevents their assembly, an architectural role of an lncRNA in the strict sense. Nuclear speckles, which concentrate splicing factors near highly transcribed genes, depend on the large proteins SON and SRRM2.

### 21.5.2. Heterochromatin

The protein HP1, which binds H3K9-methylated nucleosomes through its **chromodomain** and spreads silencing ([7.2.15](/7.2.15)), forms liquid droplets in vitro under some conditions: human HP1α phase-separates when its N-terminal extension is phosphorylated or when it binds DNA (Larson, Narlikar and colleagues, 2017), and in early *Drosophila* embryos HP1a foci nucleate, fuse and show liquid behavior (Strom, Karpen and colleagues, 2017). A phase-separated heterochromatin would explain how silenced chromatin excludes some proteins (the transcription machinery) and admits others, and why heterochromatin clusters into large compartments. The mechanism has been questioned: in mouse cells, HP1 binds chromocenters dynamically but its concentration and dynamics did not show the behavior expected of a liquid HP1 droplet, and the chromocenters behaved as collapsed chromatin globules held by bridging proteins (Erdel, Rippe and colleagues, 2020). Chromatin itself is a polymer: nucleosome arrays phase-separate in vitro in the presence of linker histone and divalent ions, and acetylation dissolves them (Gibson, Rosen and colleagues, 2019). Whether the compartments of the nucleus (A and B compartments, [7.2.4](/7.2.4)) are best described as liquid condensates, polymer–polymer phase separation or bridging-induced clustering is an active question.

## 21.6. Aging, Solidification and Disease

A liquid condensate is not necessarily the most stable state of its components. Many low-complexity domains that form liquid droplets also form cross-β fibrils, ordered polymers resembling **amyloid fibrils** ([7.1.6](/7.1.6)), which are thermodynamically more stable. In a droplet, where the proteins are concentrated a hundredfold, the nucleation of fibrils is favored. Droplets of FUS made in vitro gradually lose their liquidity: they stop fusing, recover more slowly after photobleaching, and eventually contain fibers (Patel, Alberti, Hyman and colleagues, 2015). Mutations in FUS that cause amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), such as G156E, accelerate this aging. The same pattern holds for hnRNPA1 (mutations in its prion-like domain cause multisystem proteinopathy) and TDP-43, whose cytoplasmic inclusions are found in most cases of ALS and frontotemporal dementia; dipeptide repeats produced from the expanded *C9orf72* repeat (poly-PR, poly-GR) bind to low-complexity domains and alter many condensates at once. Stress granules are thought to be one place where such aggregates begin: they concentrate FUS, TDP-43 and hnRNPA1, and their repeated or prolonged assembly may raise the probability of a fibril. The **prion** domains of yeast proteins ([7.1.6](/7.1.6)) show that self-templating aggregates of this kind can also be functional and heritable.

Cells keep condensates liquid with energy-consuming machinery: ATP-dependent RNA helicases and chaperones (Hsp70 and its cofactors) dissolve stress granules and disaggregate their proteins, nuclear import receptors (transportin-1 for FUS) bind the nuclear localization signals of RNA-binding proteins and suppress their phase separation in the cytoplasm, and the cell's ATP may itself act as a hydrotrope at millimolar concentrations. The aging of droplets in vitro is thus a model of what happens when this maintenance fails. A consequence of interest for pharmacology is that condensates are chemically distinct solvents: small molecules partition into them selectively (for example, the anticancer drug cisplatin concentrates in MED1 condensates; Klein, Young and colleagues, 2020), which could be used to target drugs to a compartment.

## 21.7. Open Questions

The concept of condensates has reorganized cell biology in little more than a decade, and much of the evidence is still being re-examined. The main unresolved questions are:

| Question | State of the evidence |
|---|---|
| Are cellular foci formed by phase separation? | Clear for large bodies (nucleolus, P granules, stress granules); for small foci (transcriptional clusters, signaling clusters) alternatives fit many data: clusters of molecules bound to arrays of sites, or "hubs" formed by multivalent interactions below the saturation concentration. Clusters of defined size also exist in subsaturated solutions, as precursors of phase separation (Kar, Pappu and colleagues, 2022) |
| Is condensation needed for function? | Multivalent interactions of activation domains enhance transcription, but in some systems equally well when droplet formation is prevented (Trojanowski and colleagues, 2022): the interactions may matter more than the phase |
| What sets composition and specificity? | Hundreds of condensates with different compositions coexist; sticker grammar and multicomponent thermodynamics (tie lines, Example 21.3.1) are only beginning to be predictive |
| How do cells control size and number? | Active reactions, chromatin mechanics and nucleation sites are candidates; equilibrium theory predicts coarsening to one drop, which is not observed |
| What is the material state in vivo? | Liquid, gel and glassy states coexist; viscoelastic measurements in cells are difficult, and the relationship between material state and disease is correlative in most cases |

Experimental tools are improving: optogenetic constructs that oligomerize IDRs with light (optoDroplets; Shin, Brangwynne and colleagues, 2017) allow the threshold to be crossed at will at chosen places and times, quantitative imaging measures concentrations in both phases, and engineered condensates test which properties are sufficient for a function. The lever rule (Formula 21.3.2), the dependence of the threshold on valency (Proposition 21.4.1), and the material constants of Example 21.1.1 are the quantitative statements against which such experiments are judged.

## Questions

1. Compute the critical point of the Flory–Huggins model for $N = 1$, $100$ and $400$, and explain why a protein with a long low-complexity domain can phase-separate at micromolar concentrations while a solution of free amino acids with the same interactions does not.

   **Answer** From Eq. (21.3): $N = 1$: $\phi_{\mathrm{c}} = 0.5$, $\chi_{\mathrm{c}} = 2$; $N = 100$: $\phi_{\mathrm{c}} = 1/11 \approx 0.091$, $\chi_{\mathrm{c}} = \tfrac12(1.1)^2 = 0.605$; $N = 400$: $\phi_{\mathrm{c}} = 1/21 \approx 0.048$, $\chi_{\mathrm{c}} = \tfrac12(1.05)^2 \approx 0.551$. Linking residues into a chain reduces the translational entropy lost on demixing by a factor $N$, so a much weaker attraction per residue ($\chi$ just above $1/2$ instead of $2$) suffices, and the critical concentration and the dilute branch of the binodal move to low volume fractions; the dilute-phase concentration falls roughly exponentially with $N(\chi - \chi_{\mathrm{c}})$ (Figure in 21.3.1). Free amino acids with the same per-residue attraction stay mixed because their entropy of mixing dominates.

2. A scaffold has $C_{\mathrm{sat}} = 2\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ and $C_{\mathrm{den}} = 400\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ in a nucleus of $500\,\mu\mathrm{m}^3$. (a) What total concentration gives condensates occupying $1\,\%$ of the nuclear volume? (b) The cell doubles its expression of the scaffold. What happens to the condensate volume and to the dilute-phase concentration if the system behaves as a single component? (c) What would you conclude if the dilute-phase concentration rose by $50\,\%$?

   **Answer** (a) From Eq. (21.4), $0.01 = (c - 2)/(400 - 2)$, so $c = 2 + 3.98 \approx 6\,\mu\mathrm{M}$, a condensed volume of $5\,\mu\mathrm{m}^3$ (for example five droplets of radius about $0.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$). (b) At $c = 12\,\mu\mathrm{M}$, $V_{\mathrm{den}}/V = 10/398 = 2.5\,\%$, two and a half times the volume, while the dilute phase stays at $2\,\mu\mathrm{M}$. (c) The one-component lever rule fails: the condensate contains other components with heterotypic interactions, so the tie line through the new composition is not parallel to the scaffold axis (Example 21.3.1); the scaffold's dilute concentration is then only partly buffered.

3. Using Eq. (21.5), by how much does the threshold for network formation change when (a) the number of phosphorylated sites per molecule rises from $4$ to $8$, (b) the affinity of each contact rises fourfold? Relate the answer to the sensitivity of super-enhancer genes to kinase inhibitors.

   **Answer** (a) $c^* \propto (f - 1)/[f(f - 2)^2]$: for $f = 4$, $3/(4 \times 4) = 0.1875$; for $f = 8$, $7/(8 \times 36) = 0.0243$; the threshold falls about eightfold (the large-$f$ estimate $1/f^2$ gives fourfold; the exact tree result is steeper at small $f$). (b) $c^* \propto 1/K$ falls fourfold. A kinase that modifies many residues at once changes $f$ for all components together, which changes the threshold more than any plausible change in a single affinity. Inhibiting CDK7 (which phosphorylates the CTD and other components) or displacing BRD4 (which bridges many acetylated partners) reduces the effective valency and can move a super-enhancer, which sits just above its threshold with a steep response, below it, while typical enhancers with a graded response lose activity only in proportion.

4. Two nuclear droplets of radius $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ fuse and relax to a sphere in about $20\,\mathrm{s}$. Estimate their inverse capillary velocity $\eta/\gamma$ and, assuming $\gamma = 1\,\mu\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, their viscosity. What would you expect for droplets of radius $0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$?

   **Answer** From $\tau \approx (\eta/\gamma)R$, $\eta/\gamma \approx 20\,\mathrm{s}/2\,\mu\mathrm{m} = 10\,\mathrm{s\,\mu m^{-1}} = 10^7\,\mathrm{s\,m^{-1}}$. With $\gamma = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, $\eta \approx 10\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, ten thousand times the viscosity of water, typical of a concentrated protein liquid. Droplets of radius $0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ should relax in about $5\,\mathrm{s}$; a plot of $\tau$ against $R$ for many events is the standard way to obtain $\eta/\gamma$ and to check that the droplets are liquid (a straight line through the origin).

5. Propose a set of experiments to decide whether foci of a transcription factor at a super-enhancer form by phase separation or by binding of the factor to a cluster of DNA sites.

   **Answer** Key distinctions: (i) Concentration dependence: with phase separation, foci appear above a threshold and grow in size, not in internal concentration, as expression rises, and the nucleoplasmic concentration is clamped (Formula 21.3.2); with site binding, focus intensity saturates at the number of sites and the free concentration rises proportionally. Titrate expression (for example with an inducible promoter) and measure concentrations inside and outside foci by calibrated imaging. (ii) Dependence on DNA sites: deleting the binding-site cluster should abolish site-bound foci entirely, whereas a condensate may still form elsewhere above the threshold, possibly nucleated on other sites. (iii) Dependence on the IDR: replace the factor's IDR with IDRs that have the same activation function but different phase-separation propensity (or use optogenetic oligomerization to raise valency) and test whether foci and transcription track phase separation or activation. (iv) Material properties: fusion of foci and size-dependent relaxation, FRAP of the interior versus the boundary. (v) Function: measure transcription (bursting, [7.2.22](/7.2.22)) when foci are prevented while the binding interactions are kept. No single test suffices (Remark 21.1.1).

6. Explain how a multiphase nucleolus can arrange the steps of ribosome biogenesis in space, and why surface tensions determine the nesting of the phases.

   **Answer** rDNA and Pol I sit in the fibrillar centers; nascent pre-rRNA enters the surrounding dense fibrillar component, where fibrillarin-containing snoRNPs modify and cleave it; as it matures and binds ribosomal proteins, its affinity for the granular component (nucleophosmin) increases, and it moves outward, where preribosomal particles are assembled and released to the nucleoplasm. Each phase concentrates the factors of one stage, so the gradient of composition from inside to outside corresponds to the sequence of reactions. For three immiscible liquids, the arrangement minimizes the total interfacial free energy $\sum\gamma_{ij}A_{ij}$: a phase whose interface with the nucleoplasm is costly (high $\gamma$) is wrapped by a phase with lower surface tension against the nucleoplasm, provided the tension between the two inner phases is low enough; complete engulfment occurs when $\gamma_{\mathrm{DFC,np}} > \gamma_{\mathrm{GC,np}} + \gamma_{\mathrm{DFC,GC}}$. Changing the relative tensions (for example by mutating nucleophosmin) changes the nesting, as observed in vitro.
