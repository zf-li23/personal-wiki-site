The twenty-three chapters between the introduction and this one each answered a question about one part of the information system. This chapter puts the parts back together. It follows a single piece of information through its whole life, from a stretch of DNA in closed **chromatin** to a protein that is finally degraded (25.1), and then reads the same path four more times across its own dimensions: as five layers of regulation, each acting on its own time scale (25.2); as a sequence of copying and recognition steps with measurable accuracy, speed and cost (25.3); as a genealogy of methods, in which every conclusion of the course came from an experiment and most of those experiments became tools (25.4); and finally as a subject with a present state and open problems (25.5). Nothing new is introduced here: every number and every statement is one that an earlier chapter established, and each is cited so that the chapter can be used as an index to the course. The main line that the chapters follow — carrier, tools, maintenance, read-out, control, rewriting — is the subject of the first figure.

## 25.1. The Life of One Piece of Information

Take one protein-coding gene in a human cell and follow what happens to the information in it. Every step below was the subject of a chapter, and the list is the course read in the order in which a cell performs it.

![The life of one piece of information in a eukaryotic cell, drawn in SBGN process notation. In the nucleus (upper row): a gene in closed chromatin is opened by remodelers and acetyltransferases; general transcription factors, Mediator and activators assemble a preinitiation complex on the core promoter; TFIIH opens the DNA and, after promoter-proximal pausing is released by P-TEFb, Pol II elongates while the nascent RNA is capped, spliced, cleaved and polyadenylated; the product is a mature mRNP, exported through nuclear pores. In the cytoplasm (lower row): the mRNA is translated on polysomes, the protein is folded, modified and targeted, and both mRNA and protein are eventually degraded (the crossed circles are sinks). Above each step are the machines that carry it out, below it the regulators that act there; numbered badges mark the five regulatory layers of 25.2. The self-loop on the gene stands for its maintenance by replication, repair and recombination; the dashed arrow closes the loop, since some of the proteins made are the transcription factors acting on other genes.](./plots/life-of-information.py)

1. The carrier. The gene is a segment of DNA (Definition 1.2.1) in a genome of $3.1\,\mathrm{Gb}$ of which only $1.2\,\%$ is coding and about $46\,\%$ descends from mobile elements ([7.2.2](/7.2.2)). Its two strands are wound about each other once per $10.5\,\mathrm{bp}$, so the gene lies in a molecule with a fixed **linking number** (Definition 3.1.1) whose strands cannot be separated anywhere without consequences elsewhere, and it is wrapped, with $147\,\mathrm{bp}$ per **nucleosome** (Definition 4.1.3), into a fiber compacted some $10^4$-fold at mitosis.
2. Access. Before anything can read it, the **promoter** must be made accessible: **remodelers** slide or evict **nucleosomes**, acetyltransferases mark the **histones**, and the result is a nucleosome-depleted region detectable as a **DNase I hypersensitive site** (Definition 4.4.2, [7.2.15](/7.2.15)). In the opposite state, methylated DNA and H3K9me3–HP1 or Polycomb domains keep the gene silent and, through read–write feedback, keep it silent in the daughter cells (Example 15.6.1).
3. Recognition. **Activators** bind **enhancers** and proximal elements through the DNA-binding domains surveyed in 14.2, with affinity and specificity squared by dimerization (Eq. (14.1)), and reach the promoter by looping, which is worth an effective concentration of about $17\,\mathrm{nM}$ at $3\,\mathrm{kb}$ (Eq. (14.3)). Whether they find their sites at all is a search problem: a **repressor** spends about $90\,\%$ of its time sliding on nonspecific DNA and still needs minutes to find one **operator** in a bacterium (Example 22.2.2).
4. Initiation. The **general transcription factors** assemble on the **core promoter** (Definition 12.4.1) in the order TFIID–A → B → Pol II–F → E → H (Example 13.1.1), with **TBP** bending the DNA by $80°$ and TFIIB fixing the start site $25$–$30\,\mathrm{bp}$ downstream. **Mediator** and the **coactivators** relay the activators' signals (Definition 14.8.2). Equilibrium occupancy of the promoter is what all of this amounts to, and it can be written down: in bacteria, the **thermodynamic model** (Definition 11.6.1) gives the fold change from one **binding energy** and one copy number (Eq. (11.2)).
5. Elongation and processing. TFIIH opens $-9$ to $+2$ and phosphorylates Ser5 of the **carboxyl-terminal domain** (Definition 12.2.1); the polymerase clears the promoter at about $10\,\mathrm{nt}$, pauses $20$–$60\,\mathrm{nt}$ downstream until P-TEFb releases it, and then elongates at $1$–$4\,\mathrm{kb\,min^{-1}}$. The **CTD** carries the processing machinery along: the cap is added within the first $30\,\mathrm{nt}$ ([7.2.17](/7.2.17)), each **intron** is removed by two transesterifications in a **spliceosome** assembled on it (Definition 16.3.1, Definition 16.4.2), and cleavage at the poly(A) site both makes the $3'$ end and triggers termination through the torpedo mechanism (Definition 17.4.1).
6. Export, translation and the protein. The mature mRNP is exported, and in the cytoplasm the $43\mathrm{S}$ complex binds the cap and scans to the first AUG in good context (Definition 19.6.3). Elongation proceeds at $3$–$8\,\mathrm{aa\,s^{-1}}$ in **eukaryotes**, each cycle costing at least four phosphoanhydride bonds (Formula 19.8.1) and each **codon** read with an error of about $10^{-4}$. The nascent chain is processed, folded and, if it carries a **signal sequence** (Definition 19.9.1), taken to the translocon by the **signal recognition particle**.
7. Removal. The **mRNA** is deadenylated, decapped and degraded (Definition 18.2.1), with a median half-life in mammals of about $9\,\mathrm{h}$, or destroyed faster if it carries an **AU-rich element** or a **seed sequence** match to an abundant **microRNA**; the protein is ubiquitylated and degraded by the **proteasome** (Definition 20.7.1) with a median half-life of about two days. Both removal rates, not the production rates, set how fast the level of the product can change (Definition 22.3.1).
8. The loop closes. Many of the proteins thus made are themselves the regulators of step 3 for other genes, which is why the system is circular, and why that circularity raises the question of how it began ([7.2.23](/7.2.23)).

Two things are worth noting about this path. The first is that it is not a conveyor belt of independent stations: capping, **splicing** and $3'$-end formation happen on the polymerase and are coordinated by its CTD (Definition 17.3.1); splicing deposits the **exon junction complex** that later decides whether a **stop codon** counts as premature (Definition 18.2.3); the poly(A) tail and the cap act together in initiation. Coupling is the rule, not the exception, and in bacteria it is tighter still: translation starts on an RNA that is still being made, which is what makes **attenuation** possible (Definition 11.4.1) and what gives ρ its polarity.

The second is that each of the eight steps is a place where the cell intervenes.

## 25.2. The Five Layers of Regulation and Their Time Scales

The regulators encountered in the course sort into five layers. They differ in what they act on, in how much they can change the output, in how reversible they are, and — most usefully — in how fast they act, since a layer cannot control a process faster than its own relaxation time.

| Layer | Acts on | Mechanisms (chapters) | Typical dynamic range | Speed and reversibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Chromatin | accessibility of the DNA | remodelers, HATs and HDACs, methyl marks and their readers, **DNA methylation**, heterochromatin spreading ([7.2.4](/7.2.4), [7.2.15](/7.2.15)) | from fully silent to fully accessible; core histones repress basal transcription by about $75\,\%$, H1 much more (Example 15.1.1) | acetylation turns over in minutes (Eq. (15.1)); silenced states are inherited over cell generations, so this is the only layer with memory |
| 2. Initiation | occupancy of the promoter | repressors and activators, operators and enhancers, coactivators, insulators ([7.2.11](/7.2.11), [7.2.14](/7.2.14)) | $18$-fold from *lac* $O_1$ alone, $1300$-fold with all three operators (Example 11.2.2); $700$-fold for *trp* with attenuation | binding equilibrates in seconds (Example 22.1.1); fully reversible; the layer at which stopping wastes least |
| 3. Elongation and processing | what the polymerase produces | promoter-proximal pausing, antitermination, attenuation, alternative splicing and alternative polyadenylation, editing ([7.2.13](/7.2.13), [7.2.16](/7.2.16), [7.2.17](/7.2.17)) | qualitative rather than quantitative: a different product, not more of it ($95\,\%$ of human multi-exon genes are alternatively spliced) | seconds to minutes; decided co-transcriptionally, so it is coupled to elongation rate (Eq. (16.2)) |
| 4. Translation | use of existing mRNA | ribosome-binding-site structure, riboswitches and thermometers, eIF2α phosphorylation, 4E-BPs, uORFs, IREs, miRNAs ([7.2.18](/7.2.18), [7.2.20](/7.2.20)) | about $20$-fold for the ferritin and transferrin-receptor IREs (Example 18.4.1); global shut-off within minutes by the integrated stress response | minutes, no new synthesis needed, fully reversible; acts on all mRNAs at once when it works through initiation factors |
| 5. Decay | lifetime of mRNA and protein | deadenylation and decapping, the exosome, surveillance pathways, the ubiquitin–proteasome system ([7.2.18](/7.2.18), [7.2.20](/7.2.20)) | sets the steady state as $k_{\mathrm{syn}}/k_{\mathrm{deg}}$ (Formula 18.1.1) and therefore has unlimited range in principle | sets the response time of everything above it (Definition 22.3.1): minutes in bacteria, hours to days in mammalian cells |

The layers are not alternatives; a single gene is usually controlled at several of them, and the choice of layer follows the time scale of the problem. A bacterium responding to lactose uses layer 2, where the decision is cheapest and where its short-lived mRNAs make the response fast anyway. A cell responding to amino-acid starvation uses layer 4, because it must act within minutes on messages it already has and must be able to reverse the decision. A cell that has differentiated uses layer 1, because only there does a state survive division.

![Time scales of the five layers, as order-of-magnitude ranges on one logarithmic axis of seconds; dots mark median values. All values are those stated in the chapters: histone acetylation relaxes in minutes and a single nucleosome is remodeled in vitro over minutes to an hour, while marks at the IFN-β promoter persist for many hours and silenced states for many cell cycles (layer 1); a nonspecific DNA contact lasts milliseconds, residence on a specific site seconds to hours, the search for one operator minutes, and an active rRNA gene initiates every 1.5 s (layer 2); capping takes about a second, transcribing one kilobase tens of seconds, a median human gene minutes, and dystrophin about 16 h (layer 3); translating a 300-residue protein takes a quarter of a minute to two minutes and mRNA half-lives span minutes in *E. coli* to days in mammals (layer 4); and protein half-lives span tens of minutes for actively degraded regulators to days for the median mammalian protein (layer 5). The vertical dotted lines are the reference units.](./plots/layers-timescales.py)

The arithmetic that connects the layers is the same two-stage model everywhere (22.3.2): the mRNA follows the promoter with its own relaxation time $1/(k_{\mathrm{deg},m}+\mu)$ and the protein follows the mRNA with $1/(k_{\mathrm{deg},p}+\mu)$, where $\mu$ is dilution by growth. Its consequence is the most useful single fact about gene regulation: making more of something does not make it appear sooner. A bacterium can therefore change a stable protein's level only on the time scale of its cell cycle, and a mammalian cell whose median protein lives two days must actively degrade anything it wants to change within an hour.

![Switching a gene on, from the two-stage model of 22.3.2 with a delay τ between promoter activation and the first translatable mRNA; curves are normalized to their steady states, so the synthesis rates drop out. A: an *E. coli* gene (τ = 0.5 min, mRNA half-life 3 min, a stable protein removed only by dilution at a doubling time of 30 min). The mRNA reaches half of its steady state within about 3 min, the protein only after about 35 min, one cell cycle. B: a human gene (τ = 30 min for transcription, processing and export; mRNA half-life 9 h, protein half-life 2 d, doubling time 24 h, solid curves): the protein needs more than a day. Dashed: a short-lived regulator with an mRNA and a protein of one hour half-life each, which reaches half of its new level within a few hours. Dots mark the half-times.](./plots/expression-response.py)

Every factor of the two-stage model is set by one or a few chapters, and the steady-state protein number is their product,

$$
p^{*} = g\cdot p_{\mathrm{on}}\cdot r\cdot\Psi\cdot\frac{1}{k'_{\mathrm{deg}}}\cdot k_{\mathrm{tl}}\cdot\frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{p}}+\mu}:
$$
the gene dosage $g$ (Proposition 7.7.1 of [7.2.7](/7.2.7)), the promoter occupancy $p_{\mathrm{on}}$ ([7.2.11](/7.2.11), [7.2.14](/7.2.14), [7.2.15](/7.2.15)), the initiation and termination of transcription $r$ ([7.2.10](/7.2.10), [7.2.13](/7.2.13)), the fraction $\Psi$ processed into the functional transcript ([7.2.16](/7.2.16), [7.2.17](/7.2.17)), the mRNA lifetime ([7.2.18](/7.2.18)), translation ([7.2.20](/7.2.20)) and protein removal ([7.2.22](/7.2.22)). A regulator changes one factor, so on a logarithmic scale the effects of regulators at different steps add. Genome-wide measurements in mammalian cells show how far each factor actually varies: transcription rates and mRNA half-lives span about two orders of magnitude, and mRNA and protein half-lives are uncorrelated (Schwanhäusser et al., 2011); how much of the variation in protein levels translation explains is disputed, because translation rates estimated from that model span 219-fold for 95 % of genes but those measured by ribosome profiling only 5.8-fold (Li, Bickel and Biggin, 2014).

## 25.3. Fidelity, Speed, Energy and Specificity

The second reading of the path is quantitative. Four numbers characterize every step: how fast it goes, how often it is wrong, what it costs, and how it tells the right substrate or the right site from the wrong one. The first three were collected in 22.7; the comparison below adds the recognition steps, which obey the same logic as the copying steps.

| Step | Rate | Error per unit | Energy per unit | How accuracy is bought |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Replication | $1000\,\mathrm{nt\,s^{-1}}$ (*E. coli*), $15$–$50$ (human) | $10^{-10}$–$10^{-9}$ | $2$ phosphoanhydride bonds per nucleotide | three multiplied stages: base selection $10^{-4}$–$10^{-5}$, exonucleolytic **proofreading** $10^{-2}$, **mismatch repair** $10^{-2}$–$10^{-3}$ (Eq. (7.5)) |
| Transcription | $20$–$80\,\mathrm{nt\,s^{-1}}$ | $10^{-6}$–$10^{-5}$ | $2$ per nucleotide | base selection plus backtracking and transcript cleavage, $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0 k_{\mathrm{ext}}/(k_{\mathrm{c}}+k_{\mathrm{ext}})$ (Eq. (13.2)) |
| Aminoacylation | — | $10^{-5}$–$10^{-4}$ | $2$ per amino acid | the **double-sieve mechanism**: a synthetic site that excludes larger amino acids and an editing site that hydrolyses smaller ones (Definition 19.4.2) |
| Decoding | $3$–$20\,\mathrm{aa\,s^{-1}}$ | $10^{-4}$–$10^{-3}$ | $2$ more per peptide bond | initial selection, then **kinetic proofreading** after GTP hydrolysis, which squares the discrimination (Formula 19.8.2) |
| Splice-site choice | one intron in seconds to minutes | wrong by one nucleotide would shift the frame | ATP for eight helicases per cycle | an RNA–RNA network checked step by step and proofread by the helicases, plus exon definition and SR proteins ([7.2.16](/7.2.16)) |
| Finding a binding site | $k_{\mathrm{on}}$ near the diffusion limit, $\sim10^8\,\mathrm{M^{-1}s^{-1}}$ | hundreds of chance matches for a short site | sliding is free; cells pay in copy number | specificity lies in $k_{\mathrm{off}}$ (tenfold per $5.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$) and in site length; facilitated diffusion raises $k_{\mathrm{on}}$ above the three-dimensional limit (Example 22.2.1) |
| Homology search | homolog found within minutes | an octamer matches about $140$ times in *E. coli* | ATP for the RecA/RAD51 filament | matching is tested in triplets and extended only on success, stable above $15\,\mathrm{nt}$, just beyond the unique length $\log_4 2G$ (Example 9.3.1) |
| Programmed cleavage (Cas9) | hours to search a human genome | off-target cleavage at PAM-distal mismatches | ATP-independent; GTP for nothing | $20\,\mathrm{nt}$ of pairing checked directionally from the PAM, so the PAM-proximal seed is intolerant of mismatches ([7.2.24](/7.2.24)) |

Three regularities, already noted in 22.7, are worth restating as the quantitative summary of the course. First, a single equilibrium binding step cannot discriminate better than $\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta\Delta G/k_{\mathrm{B}}T}$, about $10^{-2}$ for the free-energy difference between a correct and a mismatched base pair; every accuracy beyond that is bought with energy, by repeating the test in a driven cycle ([6.3.18](/6.3.18)). Second, accuracy matches the cost of an error: the replication error that is inherited by every descendant is $10^6$ times rarer than the translation error that spoils one short-lived molecule. Third, accuracy costs speed, which is why hyperaccurate ribosomes grow slowly.

The same arithmetic answers the recognition question. A site of $l$ fully specified base pairs occurs by chance $2G/4^l$ times in a random genome of $G$ base pairs (Formula 5.1.1), which is below one only for $l > \log_4 2G$: about $11.6$ for *E. coli* and $16.3$ for the human genome. Every recognition system in the course is built around this bound. Short signals are not unique and are therefore used in combination or in a context — the *lac* operator is read by a tetramer with two auxiliary sites, a $17\,\mathrm{bp}$ λ operator subsite only in a set of three with a cooperativity hierarchy, a splice site only together with a branch point and a defined exon, a miRNA seed of $7\,\mathrm{nt}$ only with enough targets to make partial repression useful. Long signals are unique and can be used alone, which is exactly why the engineered nucleases are built long: a four-finger ZFN pair specifies $24\,\mathrm{bp}$, a TALEN pair $36$.

## 25.4. The Genealogy of the Methods

The third reading of the course is methodological, and it is the one that Weaver's organization of the subject makes visible: behind every conclusion stands an experiment that can be retold, and the enzymes used in those experiments were themselves discoveries of the same subject (1.3.2). The table reads in that direction, from a natural mechanism to the tool made from it and to the questions it then answered.

| Natural mechanism (chapter) | Tool it became | What the tool then established |

Two features of this table are the real lesson. The first is the regularity of the step from mechanism to tool: in every case, the part that recognizes a sequence was separated from the part that performs chemistry, and the two were recombined (Remark 24.1.1). The second is that the direction of travel is not only from nature to the laboratory. Each tool was then turned back on the system and settled questions that had been inaccessible: footprinting turned the operator from a genetic abstraction into $26\,\mathrm{bp}$ of DNA, sequencing turned the genome from an estimate of complexity into an inventory, and editing turned statements of the form "this element is necessary" from correlations into experiments.

## 25.5. The State of the Subject

The course began with a subject that could be suspected of being finished: its central mechanisms were established decades ago, its textbooks are long, and much of what remains looks like application. Four developments treated in the last chapters say otherwise, and they have a common shape — each turns a qualitative description into something that can be measured, modeled or built.

### 25.5.1. Condensates

The picture of the cell as a set of stoichiometric machines was incomplete. Micrometer-sized compartments without membranes concentrate hundreds of components, exchange them in seconds, and form above a **saturation concentration** that falls steeply with valence ([7.2.21](/7.2.21)). This reorganized the description of the nucleolus, of stress granules and of transcriptional clusters, and it brought with it a physics that makes predictions: the Flory–Huggins free energy and its critical point (Proposition 21.3.1), the lever rule and the buffering of the dilute phase it predicts (Formula 21.3.2), the network threshold $c^* \approx 1/(Kf^2)$ (Proposition 21.4.1). It also brought the field's clearest current controversy, since the predictions fail in cells as often as they hold (Example 21.3.1), and small foci can be explained equally well by binding to arrays of sites (21.7). This is a healthy state for a young idea: it is falsifiable and is being falsified in parts.

### 25.5.2. Noncoding and Regulatory RNA

The fraction of a genome that is transcribed but not translated turned out to be both large and functional in specific, mechanistically intelligible ways: microRNAs with a $7$-nucleotide seed that target more than $60\,\%$ of human genes with modest effects on each, piRNAs that silence transposons in the germ line, riboswitches that sense metabolites without protein, and a small number of long noncoding RNAs, *Xist* above all, whose mechanism is known in detail ([7.2.18](/7.2.18)). The open problem here is one of discipline rather than discovery: for most annotated long noncoding RNAs the function is not established, and the field's own standard — necessity and sufficiency in the natural context, which editing now makes testable — is the right one to apply.

### 25.5.3. Single Molecules, Structures and Measurement

Cryo-electron microscopy produced structures of the spliceosome at successive stages and of the ribosome and polymerase in the act; single-molecule fluorescence showed scrunching directly (Example 10.4.4) and counted proteins as they were made, about four per burst (Example 22.5.1); sequencing turned occupancy, translation and decay into genome-wide measurements. The consequence is that the quantities in 25.2 and 25.3 are no longer estimates from in vitro reconstitution but measurements in cells, and that the models of [7.2.22](/7.2.22) can be tested rather than merely written down.

### 25.5.4. Editing and Synthesis

The ability to make a designed change at a chosen position converts the subject from a descriptive into an interventional one ([7.2.24](/7.2.24)). Its boundaries are now mostly elsewhere: delivery is chemistry, guide design and screen analysis are computation (24.9). What remains inside molecular biology is a set of questions the tools themselves raised — why repair outcomes at a site are reproducible, what in chromatin decides whether a site can be edited at all, what else in nature is an RNA-guided enzyme.

### 25.5.5. Where a Basic Advance Could Come From

Where might a basic, rather than a technical, breakthrough come from? The chapters suggest three places. The first is a predictive account of gene regulation: given a promoter, its enhancers, the chromatin state and the concentrations of the factors, predict the output and its distribution across cells. The thermodynamic model does this for a bacterial promoter with two parameters (Example 11.6.1) and nothing comparable exists for a eukaryotic one, where the relevant interactions may not even be at equilibrium. The second is the material state of the nucleus: whether condensation, chromatin mechanics and loop extrusion constitute a mechanism of regulation or an epiphenomenon of the interactions that do the regulating. The third is the origin of the system itself, where the question is sharp and the criterion is stated: a ribozyme that copies a molecule of its own length above the error threshold of Theorem 23.6.1, inside a protocell that divides under selection ([7.2.23](/7.2.23)).

It is also worth being clear about what a basic breakthrough would not be. The subject's central claims — the dogma's restriction on the flow of sequence information (Definition 1.1.1), semiconservative replication, the triplet code, the chemistry of splicing and of peptide-bond formation — are not candidates for revision; they are as well established as anything in biology. What is open is one level up: not what the parts are, but how they combine into a system whose behavior can be computed, and how such a system can be made in the first place.

## Summary

The course followed one piece of information from a packaged, topologically constrained DNA sequence through access, recognition, initiation, elongation with coupled processing, export, translation and degradation (25.1), and then read that path three more ways: as five regulatory layers whose speeds span milliseconds to cell generations and whose response times are set by decay, not synthesis (25.2); as a chain of copying and recognition steps whose accuracy beyond $\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta\Delta G/k_{\mathrm{B}}T}$ is bought with energy and speed, and whose signals must be longer than $\log_4 2G$ to be unique (25.3); and as a genealogy in which each natural mechanism became an instrument that then answered the questions of later chapters (25.4). The subject is not finished: condensates, regulatory RNA, single-molecule and structural methods, and editing have each converted a description into something measurable or buildable, and the open problems — predictive gene regulation, the material state of the nucleus, the origin of the information system — are stated precisely enough to be attacked (25.5).

## Questions

1. A mammalian transcription factor is induced by a signal. Its gene is transcribed from a $25\,\mathrm{kb}$ locus, its mRNA has a half-life of $1\,\mathrm{h}$ and its protein of $30\,\mathrm{min}$; the cells double every $24\,\mathrm{h}$. Estimate the time from signal to half-maximal protein, and state which term dominates.

   **Answer** Transcription of $25\,\mathrm{kb}$ at $2.4\,\mathrm{kb\,min^{-1}}$ takes about $10\,\mathrm{min}$, and processing and export add tens of minutes, so the delay $\tau$ is of the order of half an hour. After that the mRNA relaxes with $\alpha_m = \ln 2/1\,\mathrm{h} + \ln 2/24\,\mathrm{h} \approx 0.72\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$ and the protein with $\alpha_p = \ln 2/0.5\,\mathrm{h} + \ln 2/24\,\mathrm{h} \approx 1.42\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$, so the protein reaches half of its new steady state roughly $2\,\mathrm{h}$ after the signal (Eq. (22.5) applied in two stages, 22.3.2). The dominant terms are the two decay constants, not the transcription delay and not the synthesis rates: this is why regulators are short-lived (18.1) and why raising their production does not make them appear sooner.

2. Why can chromatin store a decision while promoter occupancy cannot, although both are reversible?

   **Answer** Occupancy relaxes to the equilibrium set by the current factor concentration within seconds (Example 22.1.1), so it reports the present state and nothing else; the same is true of acetylation, which relaxes in minutes (Eq. (15.1)). What makes a chromatin state heritable is not its chemistry but read–write feedback: an enzyme that is recruited by the mark it places (Suv39h–HP1 at H3K9me3, PRC2 through EED, DNMT1–UHRF1 on hemimethylated CpG) restores the mark on the nucleosomes and the strand that replication left unmarked. Above a threshold ratio of recruited to random conversion, such a system is bistable and both states are inherited (Example 15.6.1). The ingredient is cooperative positive feedback, which is also why positive autoregulation of a transcription factor can store a decision without chromatin at all (Formula 22.6.1).

3. Replication achieves $10^{-10}$ per base pair and translation only $10^{-4}$ per codon. Show that both are nevertheless near the cost-matched optimum, and state what sets the floor on each.

   **Answer** The relevant quantity is the expected number of errors per product and the cost of one. An *E. coli* chromosome of $4.6\times10^6\,\mathrm{bp}$ at $10^{-10}$ carries about $10^{-3}$ errors per replication, so most daughter cells are mutation-free, which is what the inheritance of the error requires; a $400$-residue protein at $10^{-4}$ carries about $4\times10^{-2}$ errors, so about $96\,\%$ of molecules are correct by $(1-\varepsilon)^L$ (Formula 19.8.3) — adequate for a molecule that will be degraded. The floor in both cases is the same: a single binding step discriminates at best by $\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta\Delta G/k_{\mathrm{B}}T}$, roughly $10^{-2}$. Replication buys three further decades by multiplying three driven stages (Eq. (7.5)); translation buys one by kinetic proofreading, at the price of GTP hydrolysed on correct substrates that are rejected and of a slower cycle (Formula 19.8.2). Going further would cost growth rate, which is the regularity of 22.7: accuracy is purchased with energy and speed, and is bought only as far as the cost of an error justifies.

4. A bacterial operator is $26\,\mathrm{bp}$ and a Cas9 target is effectively $22\,\mathrm{bp}$, yet the repressor finds its site in minutes and Cas9 needs hours in a human cell. Explain, and say what the comparison implies for designing a guide.

   **Answer** Site length sets uniqueness, not speed. The repressor searches by facilitated diffusion, alternating three-dimensional hops with one-dimensional sliding over an antenna of about $45\,\mathrm{bp}$ in vivo, which raises its association rate above the three-dimensional diffusion limit — the resolution of Riggs's paradox (Example 22.2.1, Definition 22.2.1) — and it searches a genome of $4.6\,\mathrm{Mb}$. Cas9 does not slide: it collides with DNA in three dimensions and tests for a PAM, so its search is a succession of short interrogations of the $\sim10^8$ NGG sites of a $3.1\,\mathrm{Gb}$ genome, which takes hours (22.2.3). For design the implication is that specificity is decided not by the total $20\,\mathrm{nt}$ but by where mismatches fall: interrogation proceeds directionally from the PAM, so PAM-proximal seed mismatches abolish cleavage and PAM-distal ones are tolerated (24.4.2). A guide should therefore be chosen so that its genomic near-neighbors differ in the seed, and off-target cleavage should be assayed, not predicted from mismatch counts alone (Definition 24.8.1).

5. Suppose a cell were engineered to make one protein with no regulation at all beyond a constitutive promoter. Using the layers of 25.2, list what it would lose, and name one experiment from the course that demonstrates each loss.

   **Answer** It would lose: memory of a decision across division, since only chromatin feedback carries a state through replication (telomere position effect and the SIR proteins, 15.6; the Dodd model, Example 15.6.1); combinatorial and signal-dependent control, since that is what activators on enhancers provide (the chimeric activator of Example 14.3.1 and the *Endo16* modules of 14.6.1 show that the logic is built from separable parts); product choice, since one promoter with no processing control makes one isoform (the *Sxl* → *tra* → *dsx* cascade, Example 16.7.2); fast and reversible adjustment without new transcription (heme control of globin synthesis, Example 20.3.1, and the ferritin IRE, Example 20.5.1); and the ability to change its level at all on a time scale shorter than dilution, since the response time is set by removal (Definition 22.3.1; negative autoregulation accelerates it about fivefold, 22.6). The gene would still be expressed — but at one level, in one form, forever, which is a fair description of what regulation is for.
