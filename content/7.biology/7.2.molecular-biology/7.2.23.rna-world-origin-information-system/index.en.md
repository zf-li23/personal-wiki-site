Every chapter so far has described one part of a system in which nucleic acids are copied and read by proteins whose sequences are themselves written in nucleic acids. Taken as a whole, that system is circular: replication, transcription and translation each need protein enzymes, and every protein enzyme needs the system in order to exist. This chapter asks how such a system could have started, and the answer that organizes the field is that it did not start as a pair of polymers but as one. The **RNA world hypothesis** ([7.1.1](/7.1.1)) holds that RNA once carried the information and performed the catalysis, and that both roles were later handed over — catalysis to proteins, storage to DNA — leaving RNA in charge of the junctions between them. The chapter is deliberately an argument rather than a catalogue: Section 23.1 states the paradox and the hypothesis, 23.2 assembles the catalytic evidence that the preceding chapters produced piece by piece (self-splicing introns, RNase P, the spliceosome, the ribosome), 23.3 shows that a ribozyme satisfies the formal definition of an enzyme, 23.4 follows the handover to DNA, 23.5 asks where the genetic code came from and how it is now being rewritten, and 23.6 states what the hypothesis cannot yet explain and which experiments would decide it. The chemistry of nucleotides is in [6.5.33](/6.5.33) and [7.1.11](/7.1.11), the catalytic strategies of enzymes in [7.1.8](/7.1.8).

## 23.1. The Chicken-and-Egg Paradox

### 23.1.1. The Circularity of the Modern System

Maintaining the living state requires fast, selective catalysis and a reliable store of the instructions for building the catalysts. In every cell now alive these two requirements are met by different polymers: DNA stores, protein catalyses, and the copying of DNA and its translation into protein are performed by proteins. The origin of such a system cannot be described one component at a time, because each component presupposes the others. Four interlocking dependencies make the point concrete.

| Function now performed by | Requires | Which requires |
|---|---|---|
| DNA polymerase (protein) | an mRNA, a ribosome, charged tRNAs | a DNA gene to be replicated and transcribed |
| Ribosome | rRNA genes, ribosomal proteins, assembly factors | replication and translation |
| Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases | translation of their own mRNAs | a working code and ribosome |
| Deoxyribonucleotides | ribonucleotide reductase, thymidylate synthase ([7.1.28](/7.1.28)) | protein synthesis |

A system whose parts are mutually necessary can still have an evolutionary origin, but only if an earlier system existed in which one molecule did two jobs at once, so that the circle was a loop with a single element in it. The hypothesis under discussion is that this molecule was RNA.

### 23.1.2. Statement of the Hypothesis

Carl Woese, Francis Crick and Leslie Orgel independently suggested in the 1960s — before any catalytic RNA was known, and on the grounds that RNA is the only one of the three polymers that is both a template and a folded three-dimensional structure — that RNA might have been both the genetic material and the catalyst of early life. Walter Gilbert named the resulting picture the RNA world in 1986, after ribozymes had been found.

**Definition 23.1.1** In the **RNA world hypothesis**, contemporary life, in which DNA stores information and proteins catalyse, descends from a system in which RNA molecules both carried heritable information and catalysed the reactions needed to copy that information, including their own replication. The hypothesis makes no claim that RNA was the first replicating polymer, only that an RNA-based stage preceded the DNA–protein stage.

**Remark 23.1.1** Three distinct claims are often run together and should be separated, because they are supported by different evidence and are falsified by different findings: (i) RNA can catalyse the reactions required of it (a chemical claim, tested in the laboratory); (ii) an RNA-based system actually existed on the early Earth (a historical claim, tested only by inference from relics and from prebiotic chemistry); (iii) RNA was the first informational polymer (a stronger historical claim that most workers now doubt, since the prebiotic synthesis of ribonucleotides is difficult and simpler backbones such as threose nucleic acid and peptide nucleic acid can also pair — hence the usual talk of a "pre-RNA world"). This chapter keeps them apart.

A timeline sets the window. The Earth formed about $4.5$ billion years ago ($\mathrm{Ga}$); zircon isotopes indicate oceans by $4.4\,\mathrm{Ga}$ and a stable hydrosphere by $4.2\,\mathrm{Ga}$; stromatolites and isotopic signatures of biological carbon and sulfur cycling show that life existed by $3.5\,\mathrm{Ga}$. Whatever happened, happened in a few hundred million years, and no direct record of it survives — which is why the argument rests on relics inside present-day cells and on what can be made to happen in a test tube.

## 23.2. The Evidence That RNA Catalyses

### 23.2.1. Five Relics

The chapters on RNA processing and translation each ended at a catalytic RNA, without emphasizing that this is a pattern. Collected, the reactions that RNA still catalyses in cells are exactly those at the heart of the information system — splicing, tRNA maturation, peptide bond formation — and not the reactions of metabolism, which proteins took over.

| Catalytic RNA | Reaction | Catalytic strategy | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group I intron (*Tetrahymena* pre-rRNA) | two transesterifications, initiated by an exogenous guanosine | two metal ions; substrate alignment by the internal guide sequence; a bound G in a specific pocket | [7.2.16](/7.2.16) |
| Group II intron | two transesterifications, initiated by the $2'$-OH of the branch-point adenosine | two metal ions; domain V as the catalytic core | [7.2.16](/7.2.16) |
| Spliceosome (U2/U5/U6 snRNA) | the same two transesterifications on nuclear pre-mRNA | a two-metal centre formed by U6 and the intron, structurally homologous to domain V | [7.2.16](/7.2.16) |
| RNase P (M1 RNA) | hydrolysis of the phosphodiester $5'$ of the mature tRNA | one or more $\ce{Mg^2+}$ activating a water nucleophile; recognition of the substrate's shape rather than its sequence | [7.2.17](/7.2.17) |
| Peptidyl transferase centre (23S rRNA) | aminoacyl transfer: attack of an $\alpha$-amino group on an ester carbonyl | substrate positioning and entropic catalysis; proton shuttling through the $2'$-OH of the P-site tRNA A76 | [7.2.19](/7.2.19) |

Two features of the list carry the argument. First, the splicing reactions, the spliceosome and the group II introns share a chemistry and a fold, so the spliceosome is best read as a group II intron that was broken into pieces supplied in *trans* — a ribozyme surrounded by, but not replaced by, proteins. Second, the peptidyl transferase centre is the only ribozyme that acts on a carbon centre rather than a phosphorus centre, and the nearest protein side chain lies far from the site of chemistry. The most fundamental reaction of the protein world is therefore catalysed by RNA; the protein world has not managed to take this one over.

**Remark 23.2.1** A **ribozyme** ([7.1.8](/7.1.8)) is slow by protein standards. The nine or so structural classes of small self-cleaving ribozymes (hammerhead, hairpin, hepatitis delta virus, *Varkud* satellite, *glmS*, twister, twister sister, pistol, hatchet) all catalyse the same reaction — in-line attack of a $2'$-OH on the adjacent phosphodiester, giving a $2',3'$-cyclic phosphate — by general acid–base catalysis using nucleobases with shifted $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ values and by positioning the nucleophile in line with the leaving group. With only four chemically similar side chains and no hydrophobic core, RNA cannot do what a protein active site does; its rate accelerations are typically $10^5$–$10^{11}$ against $10^{10}$–$10^{17}$ for protein enzymes. That is the reason proteins displaced RNA, and it is also a reason to doubt that the displacement was ever complete where positioning rather than chemistry is what matters.

### 23.2.2. Nucleotide Cofactors as Fossils

A second class of relic is chemical rather than catalytic. Coenzyme A, FAD, NAD(P), S-adenosylmethionine and ATP itself all contain an adenosine that plays no part in the chemistry they perform: the reactive group is the thiol, the flavin, the nicotinamide, the sulfonium. The simplest explanation of a useless handle that is universal is that it was once a binding handle — the part by which a ribozyme held its cofactor — and that it was retained when protein enzymes inherited the cofactors. Similarly, the universal use of ribonucleoside triphosphates as the cell's energy currency, and the biosynthesis of every deoxyribonucleotide by reduction of the corresponding ribonucleotide ([7.1.28](/7.1.28)), place RNA metabolically upstream of DNA.

The argument from relics is circumstantial, and it is worth stating its weakness: a molecular relic shows only that RNA did the job before the present arrangement, not that RNA did it at the origin of life. What the relics establish is a direction of change.

### 23.2.3. In Vitro Selection: Expanding the Repertoire

Natural ribozymes catalyse a narrow set of reactions, all of them phosphoryl or aminoacyl transfer. If the hypothesis is right, ribozymes must once have catalysed the synthesis of their own precursors as well, so the relevant question is what RNA *can* do, not what it is still found doing. That question is answered experimentally by selecting from random sequence.

**Definition 23.2.1** **In vitro selection** (systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment, SELEX) is the isolation of rare functional molecules from a large pool of random sequences by repeated cycles of selection and amplification: a synthetic DNA pool with a randomized central region flanked by constant primer-binding regions is transcribed into RNA, the RNA is passed through a selection step that retains molecules with the desired property, the retained molecules are reverse-transcribed and amplified by PCR ([7.2.5](/7.2.5)), and the amplified pool enters the next cycle. An **aptamer** is an oligonucleotide selected in this way to bind a chosen molecular target with high affinity and specificity.

Selection for binding is easy — affinity chromatography on an immobilized target — and gives aptamers: an RNA aptamer for ATP of $36$ nucleotides binds adenosine nucleotides with $K_D < 50\,\mathrm{\mu M}$ using a conserved binding pocket, and aptamers now exist against dyes, amino acids, cofactors, proteins and whole cells. Selecting for catalysis, rather than for binding, requires designing a step in which the reaction changes the molecule that performed it, so that the catalyst can be amplified: a ribozyme that ligates a defined oligonucleotide tag to its own end becomes amplifiable with a primer against the tag. If the pool contains no sequence of sufficient activity, the amplification step is made mutagenic, so that each round explores the neighbourhood of the best molecules found — directed evolution rather than mere screening. Ribozymes selected in this way catalyse ester and amide bond formation, $\mathrm{S_N2}$ reactions, aldol condensations, Diels–Alder cycloadditions, porphyrin metalation and carbon–carbon bond formation, as well as RNA ligation and polymerization (Section 23.6).

The size of the search sets the limits of the method, and it is a calculation worth doing once.

**Example 23.2.1 (How much of sequence space is searched)** A pool of $10^{15}$ molecules, the practical limit set by the mass of RNA one can transcribe and handle ($10^{15}$ molecules of a $100$-mer is about $30\,\mathrm{\mu g}$), completely covers the sequence space of a randomized region of $n$ nucleotides only while $4^n \le 10^{15}$, that is $n \le 24$ ($4^{25} = 1.1\times 10^{15}$). A ribozyme of $100$ nucleotides lives in a space of $4^{100} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{60}$ sequences, so a pool samples a fraction $\sim 10^{-45}$ of it. Selection nevertheless works, which tells us something about the structure of the space: functional motifs are small, modular and redundant, so that active sequences are not isolated points but large neighbourhoods reachable from random starts. The same reasoning applies to natural selection on the early Earth, where pools were larger but the fraction of sequence space explored was, in absolute terms, just as negligible.

**Example 23.2.2 (How many rounds of selection are needed)** Let a round retain a fraction $f_+$ of the molecules that have the desired property and a fraction $f_-$ of those that do not, and let amplification be unbiased. The enrichment per round is $\varepsilon = f_+/f_-$, and after $r$ rounds the odds in favour of the functional molecules are multiplied by $\varepsilon^{\,r}$. Starting from one functional molecule in $10^{10}$ with a realistic $\varepsilon = 10^2$ (a column that retains most binders and $1\,\%$ of the rest), five rounds suffice to make the functional class the majority. Two practical consequences follow: enrichment, not retention, is what must be optimized — a leaky column with a high ratio beats a perfect one with a poor one — and more than about ten rounds usually means that the pool is being enriched for molecules that bind the matrix or amplify well rather than for the intended function, which is why a counter-selection against the matrix is standard.

## 23.3. L-19 IVS: A Ribozyme That Is an Enzyme

A catalyst, strictly, is not consumed. The self-splicing introns are, at first sight, exceptions to this: the group I intron of the *Tetrahymena* pre-rRNA is chemically altered by the reaction it performs on itself, which is better called intramolecular than catalytic. Cech's laboratory removed the objection with a derivative.

After excision, the $414$-nucleotide intron undergoes a series of intramolecular cyclizations and cleavages that remove $19$ nucleotides from its $5'$ end. The remaining linear $395$-nucleotide RNA, called L-19 IVS (for the intervening sequence lacking $19$ nucleotides), retains both the guanosine-binding site and part of the internal guide sequence, and therefore retains the machinery for binding an oligonucleotide by base pairing and transferring a nucleotidyl group — but it no longer contains the splice sites on which it would act.

**Theorem 23.3.1 (A ribozyme satisfies the definition of an enzyme)** L-19 IVS catalyses nucleotidyl transfer between external oligonucleotide substrates — lengthening some at the expense of others, for example converting $\mathrm{(C)_5}$ into $\mathrm{(C)_4}$ and $\mathrm{(C)_6}$ — with every property used to define enzymatic catalysis: it is unchanged at the end of each cycle and turns over about $100$ substrate molecules per hour; it follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics with a specificity constant $k_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_{\mathrm{m}} \approx 10^{3}\,\mathrm{M^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$; it accelerates the reaction by a factor of about $10^{10}$ over the uncatalysed rate; it is specific for RNA oligonucleotides that can pair with its guide sequence; it is competitively inhibited by substrate analogues; and it is inactivated by denaturation.

The mechanism is a cycle of transesterifications of the same kind as self-splicing. The $3'$-OH of the terminal G of L-19 IVS attacks a phosphodiester within a bound substrate, giving a covalent ribozyme–nucleotide intermediate and releasing the shortened substrate; a second substrate then binds and its $3'$-OH attacks the covalent intermediate, taking the nucleotide and regenerating the free ribozyme. Covalent catalysis, substrate positioning by base pairing and metal-ion catalysis — the three strategies used here — are all strategies of protein enzymes ([7.1.8](/7.1.8)).

Two lessons follow. The first is definitional: the discovery of catalytic RNA, for which Cech and Altman shared the 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, forced the definition of an enzyme to be detached from the chemical identity of the polymer. The second is quantitative. A specificity constant of $10^3\,\mathrm{M^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$ is five to six orders of magnitude below a good protein enzyme and eight below the diffusion limit ([6.3.17](/6.3.17)); a ribozyme is a real but mediocre enzyme. An RNA-based metabolism would therefore have been slow, which matters for the hypothesis: it is consistent with a world of small, slowly dividing compartments, and inconsistent with anything resembling the flux densities of a modern cell.

B: the catalytic cycle, a double transesterification in which a (C)5 substrate is shortened and the nucleotide is passed to a second oligonucleotide through a covalent intermediate on the terminal G. ## 23.4. The Handover to DNA

### 23.4.1. RNA to DNA Is a Reaction That Still Happens

If the hypothesis is right, information once stored in RNA was at some point copied into DNA, and the enzymes that do such copying should still exist. They do.

**Definition 23.4.1** A **reverse transcriptase** is an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase: an enzyme that synthesizes DNA in the $5' \to 3'$ direction from an RNA template. Retroviral reverse transcriptases carry three activities in one polypeptide — RNA-dependent DNA polymerase, RNase H (which degrades the RNA of the RNA–DNA hybrid) and DNA-dependent DNA polymerase — and so convert a single-stranded RNA genome into double-stranded DNA; they use a host tRNA annealed to the genome as the primer, and have no $3' \to 5'$ proofreading exonuclease.

Howard Temin inferred the existence of the enzyme from the behaviour of Rous sarcoma virus in the early 1960s — the provirus hypothesis, which held that a DNA copy of the viral RNA integrates into the host chromosome — and Temin and Mizutani and, independently, David Baltimore demonstrated the activity in virions in 1970 (Nobel Prize 1975, with Dulbecco). The enzyme broke the then-current statement of the central dogma as a one-way street and simultaneously handed molecular biology its most useful tool, cDNA synthesis ([7.2.5](/7.2.5), [7.2.6](/7.2.6)). The absence of proofreading sets the mutation rate of retroviruses at about $10^{-4}$–$10^{-5}$ per nucleotide per replication cycle, which is why antiretroviral therapy must be combined. The same chemistry, with an internal RNA template, maintains the ends of eukaryotic chromosomes: telomerase is a reverse transcriptase ([7.2.7](/7.2.7)). Retrotransposons and retroviruses ([7.2.9](/7.2.9)) run the reaction on a large scale, and they contribute a substantial fraction of the human genome ([7.2.2](/7.2.2)).

### 23.4.2. Why DNA Won the Storage Role

Two chemical differences separate DNA from RNA, and both of them favour storage.

The $2'$-hydroxyl makes RNA labile: it attacks the adjacent phosphodiester in line, giving a $2',3'$-cyclic phosphate — the very reaction the small self-cleaving ribozymes catalyse and the reason alkali hydrolyses RNA but not DNA. Estimates of the uncatalysed rates differ by an order of magnitude, but the ratio is robust: an RNA phosphodiester is cleaved some $10^{5}$–$10^{6}$ times faster than a DNA phosphodiester at neutral pH. A genome of $10^6$ nucleotides is therefore far safer written in DNA.

The second difference is subtler and is the better argument. Cytosine deaminates spontaneously to uracil, in DNA at a rate of order $10^{-13}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ per cytosine, which gives of order $10^2$ events per human cell per day ([7.2.8](/7.2.8)). If uracil were a normal constituent of DNA, such an event would be indistinguishable from a legitimate base and would become a $\mathrm{C{\cdot}G} \to \mathrm{T{\cdot}A}$ transition at the next replication. Because DNA uses thymine ($5$-methyluracil) instead, any uracil in DNA is by definition damage, and uracil-DNA glycosylase excises it. The methyl group at C-5 is thus not a chemical improvement but a marker, and the cost of that marker is an entire biosynthetic branch: thymidylate synthase makes dTMP from dUMP ([7.1.28](/7.1.28)), the target of 5-fluorouracil and methotrexate.

**Remark 23.4.1** The biosynthetic logic runs in the direction the hypothesis predicts. Every deoxyribonucleotide is made by reduction of the corresponding ribonucleotide by ribonucleotide reductase ([7.1.28](/7.1.28)), and thymine is made by methylating a uracil nucleotide. No pathway makes a ribonucleotide from a deoxyribonucleotide, and none makes uracil from thymine. A cell that had always used DNA would have no reason to build its DNA precursors out of RNA precursors.

## 23.5. The Origin and the Rewriting of the Code

### 23.5.1. Why This Code

The genetic code ([7.2.19](/7.2.19)) is nearly universal, which means it was fixed before the last universal common ancestor and has been almost frozen since: any change in the meaning of a codon alters every protein at once, so the code is hard to change once a proteome depends on it, as Crick's frozen-accident argument states. That explains the universality but not the assignment. Three hypotheses, not mutually exclusive, address the assignment itself.

| Hypothesis | Claim | Evidence for | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stereochemical | codons or anticodons have a physical affinity for their amino acids | aptamers selected to bind amino acids are statistically enriched in the cognate codon or anticodon triplets for most of the eight amino acids for which enough aptamers have been selected | the affinities are weak, the set of amino acids tested is small, and the statistics depend on how the null pool is modelled |
| Coevolution | the code grew as biosynthetic pathways grew, amino acids derived from one another inheriting related codons | related amino acids (Glu/Gln/Pro/Arg from $\alpha$-ketoglutarate; Asp/Asn/Thr/Met/Lys from oxaloacetate) occupy related codon blocks | the pattern is incomplete and partly explained by the next hypothesis |
| Error minimization | the assignment was selected to make mistranslation and mutation as harmless as possible | the standard code scores better than all but about one in $10^6$ random codes on the cost of amino-acid substitutions weighted by polar requirement; third positions are nearly degenerate and first-position changes mostly conserve hydrophobicity | selection on the code requires a population already translating, so this acts on refinement rather than origin |

The structure of the code described in [7.2.19](/7.2.19) is the data these hypotheses must explain: the third position carries almost no information, the second position correlates with hydrophobicity, and the wobble rules that make the third position degenerate are a property of the adaptor, not of the message.

### 23.5.2. Expanding the Code in the Laboratory

The modern counterpart of asking where the code came from is asking whether it can be changed. Three approaches are worth distinguishing, because they fail at different points, and their chemistry belongs to chemical biology ([6.6](/6.6)) rather than here.

1. *Reassigning a codon.* An orthogonal pair — an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase and a tRNA that interact with each other but with no host tRNA or synthetase — charges an amino acid that the cell cannot make onto a tRNA reading a codon that has been freed, usually the amber stop codon UAG. Pyrrolysyl-tRNA synthetase and its tRNA, from methanogenic archaea, tolerate a wide range of lysine derivatives and are the workhorse pair. The limit is competition with release factor 1 at UAG, and the standard solution is genome recoding: an *E. coli* strain in which all $321$ amber codons were replaced can have release factor 1 deleted, and a later synthetic strain uses only $61$ codons, freeing two sense codons and a stop codon for reassignment.
2. *Adding a base pair.* An unnatural base pair that a polymerase accepts — the hydrophobic pair dNaM–dTPT3 is the best developed — increases the alphabet from four letters to six, and hence the number of possible codons from $64$ to $216$. Semisynthetic bacteria propagate such a pair in a plasmid and transcribe and translate codons containing it, provided the unnatural nucleoside triphosphates are imported.
3. *Changing the ribosome.* Orthogonal ribosomes with altered anti-Shine–Dalgarno sequences translate only orthogonal messages, and tethered or evolved ribosomes have been made to read quadruplet codons, so that an orthogonal pair reading a four-nucleotide codon can be used without touching the triplet code.

**Remark 23.5.1** These experiments bear on the origin question in a limited but real way. They show that the code is a convention and not a chemical necessity: nothing in the chemistry of translation forbids $21$ or $22$ amino acids, a four-letter codon or a six-letter alphabet. What they also show is the cost of changing it, which is the frozen-accident argument made quantitative — every expansion so far pays with slower growth, lower fidelity and dependence on supplied precursors.

## 23.6. What the Hypothesis Cannot Yet Explain

### 23.6.1. The Four Open Problems

Where did the nucleotides come from? Condensing phosphate, ribose and a base does not work prebiotically; the bases, the sugar and the linkage are all problematic, and ribose in particular is unstable and is produced as one of many sugars by the formose reaction. The route that changed the outlook assembles the pyrimidine nucleotides without ever making free ribose or free base: glycolaldehyde and cyanamide give 2-aminooxazole, glyceraldehyde completes the pentose, cyanoacetylene completes the pyrimidine, and phosphate acts as buffer and catalyst throughout, giving cytidine and uridine $2',3'$-cyclic monophosphates; ultraviolet light partly converts cytosine to uracil and destroys the mismatched stereoisomers. Purine nucleotides have since been made along related lines. Homochirality — why $\mathrm{D}$-ribose and $\mathrm{L}$-amino acids — remains unexplained, although carbonaceous chondrites such as the Murchison meteorite contain amino acids with a small excess of the $\mathrm{L}$ form.

How were they joined? Polymerization of activated nucleotides is slow and unselective in solution, and the $3',5'$ and $2',5'$ linkages compete. Mineral surfaces (montmorillonite clays), freezing in eutectic phases and wet–dry cycling on mineral surfaces all concentrate nucleotides and accelerate the reaction, and a mixed $2',5'/3',5'$ backbone still forms duplexes and can still be copied, so early RNA need not have been regiochemically pure.

Is a self-replicating ribozyme possible? This is the decisive experimental question, and the answer so far is "nearly". Selection in two stages — first for a ligase that joins the $3'$-OH of one RNA to the $5'$-triphosphate of another on a template, then for the ability to add nucleotides one at a time to its own $3'$ end — produced an RNA polymerase ribozyme of about $200$ nucleotides; subsequent selections gave the variant tC19Z, which extends a primer on a separate template by up to $95$ nucleotides, enough to copy a hammerhead ribozyme into a catalytically active product. No ribozyme yet copies a molecule as long and as structured as itself, which is what self-replication requires: the polymerase must also be able to work through the folded structure of its own sequence. In the meantime, self-sustained replication has been achieved at the level of ligation rather than polymerization: two complementary ligase ribozymes $\mathrm{E}$ and $\mathrm{E}'$, each assembling the other from two oligonucleotide halves, amplify exponentially from a common pool of four substrates with a doubling time of about an hour, and populations of such replicators compete for substrates and are subject to selection.

Could it have evolved? Even granting a replicase, Darwinian evolution needs more, for two separate reasons.

**Theorem 23.6.1 (Eigen's error threshold)** Let a replicator of length $L$ copy with per-nucleotide fidelity $q = 1 - \mu$, and let the best sequence have a selective superiority $\sigma > 1$ over the average of its mutants. The fraction of error-free copies is $q^L$, and the sequence is maintained in the population only while $\sigma q^L > 1$, that is

$$
L < \frac{\ln\sigma}{-\ln q} \approx \frac{\ln\sigma}{\mu}. \tag{23.1}
$$

**Proof** Of the copies made from a master sequence a fraction $q^L$ carry no error; the rest are mutants of lower fitness. The master sequence therefore grows at a rate proportional to $\sigma q^L$ relative to the mutant cloud, and is lost from the population when this ratio falls below $1$. Expanding $-\ln q = -\ln(1-\mu) \approx \mu$ for small $\mu$ gives the stated bound. $\square$

With $\sigma$ of order a few, the maximum length is roughly $1/\mu$. Non-enzymatic template copying has $\mu \approx 10^{-2}$, which caps the genome at about $100$ nucleotides — shorter than the $200$-nucleotide polymerase ribozyme that would be needed to improve the fidelity. This circularity, that a longer genome needs higher fidelity and higher fidelity needs a longer genome, is Eigen's paradox; the proposed escapes are compartments containing several short, cooperating ribozymes rather than one long one, recombination between short molecules, and the fact that the real relation between length and fidelity is not fixed but is itself selectable.

The second reason is more elementary and is sometimes overlooked. In an uncompartmented soup, an improved replicase has no selective advantage: by construction it copies *other* molecules, so its improvement benefits its neighbours, and its descendants diffuse away from each other. Selection on replicases requires that a replicase and its relatives be kept together, which is what a compartment does.

**Definition 23.6.1** A **protocell** is a membrane-bounded compartment, made of simple amphiphiles such as fatty acids rather than phospholipids, that encapsulates replicating molecules and can itself grow and divide without protein machinery. Fatty-acid vesicles form spontaneously by the hydrophobic effect ([7.1.2](/7.1.2)), grow by accretion of further fatty acids, become unstable as they grow and divide under mild shear, and — because fatty acids pack less tightly than phospholipids — are permeable to nucleotides, so that an internal replicase can be fed by diffusion from outside.

Encapsulation also couples replication to growth: polymerizing diffusible nucleotides into a non-diffusible polymer raises the internal osmolarity, water enters, the membrane comes under tension, and the vesicle takes up lipid from its surroundings and eventually divides. A protocell containing a better replicase therefore grows and divides faster, and replication becomes a heritable trait of a compartment. The cost is that division randomly partitions a small number of molecules, so compartments regularly lose their replicases altogether.

### 23.6.2. What Would Settle It

The hypothesis is not a theory, and its status should be stated plainly: the chemical claim is established, the historical claim is not and may never be decidable. What experiments can still do is to remove or to sharpen the objections. A ribozyme that copies a molecule of its own length and complexity with fidelity above the error threshold would show that the central step is chemically possible; a protocell in which such a ribozyme is fed by diffusion and divides over many generations under selection would show that the step is sufficient for Darwinian evolution. Conversely, a demonstration that no RNA polymerase ribozyme can exceed the fidelity required by Equation 23.1 would be strong evidence that the RNA world, if it existed, must have been a cooperative, compartmented system from the beginning rather than a single self-copying molecule. Alternative scenarios remain live, in particular metabolism-first proposals in which networks of small-molecule reactions on mineral surfaces preceded any genetic polymer; they are not symmetrical competitors, because they must still explain how such a network acquired heredity, which is the harder half of the problem.

What is not in doubt is the asymmetry the chapter started from. Of the three polymers, RNA is the only one that both stores information and acts. Whether or not that dual ability is a fossil of the origin of life, it is the reason RNA still sits at every junction of the information system — as the message, the adaptor, the catalyst of peptide bond formation, the guide of splicing and modification ([7.2.17](/7.2.17)), the regulator of translation and stability ([7.2.18](/7.2.18)) and, in the next chapter, the programmable component that makes genome editing possible.

## Summary

- The paradox and the hypothesis. A system in which nucleic acids are copied and read by proteins encoded in nucleic acids cannot have arisen part by part; the RNA world hypothesis (Definition 23.1.1) proposes a prior stage in which RNA was both template and catalyst. Three claims must be kept apart (Remark 23.1.1): that RNA *can* do both, that it *did*, and that it was *first*. The window is $4.4$–$3.5\,\mathrm{Ga}$.
- Catalytic evidence. RNA still catalyses exactly the reactions of the information system: group I and group II intron splicing, the two transesterifications of the spliceosome, tRNA $5'$ maturation by RNase P and peptide bond formation by 23S rRNA, which is the only ribozyme acting on a carbon centre. Every one of them works by binding through base pairing or shape and positioning a nucleophile (Section 23.2.1), with rate accelerations far below those of protein enzymes (Remark 23.2.1). The universal adenosine handle of the nucleotide cofactors and the derivation of every dNTP from an NTP are chemical relics of the same stage.
- A ribozyme is an enzyme. L-19 IVS, the $395$-nucleotide derivative of the *Tetrahymena* intron, turns over external substrates about $100$ times an hour with Michaelis–Menten kinetics, $k_{\mathrm{cat}}/K_{\mathrm{m}} \approx 10^{3}\,\mathrm{M^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$ and a rate acceleration of $10^{10}$, and is competitively inhibited (Theorem 23.3.1); it is a genuine but mediocre catalyst.
- In vitro selection. SELEX (Definition 23.2.1) extracts aptamers and new ribozymes from pools of $10^{15}$ random sequences, which exhausts sequence space only up to $25$ nucleotides (Example 23.2.1); enrichment per round, not retention, determines how many rounds are needed (Example 23.2.2). Selected ribozymes catalyse reactions no natural ribozyme performs, including RNA polymerization.
- The handover. Reverse transcriptase (Definition 23.4.1), discovered in 1970 and now the basis of cDNA methods, shows that the RNA $\to$ DNA step is an ordinary reaction; telomerase is a specialized instance. DNA won storage because the $2'$-OH makes RNA $10^{5}$–$10^{6}$ times more labile and because thymine labels any uracil in DNA as the product of cytosine deamination, at the price of a biosynthetic branch (Remark 23.4.1).
- The code. Universality reflects the frozen accident; the assignment is addressed by stereochemical, coevolutionary and error-minimizing hypotheses, of which error minimization has the strongest quantitative support. Orthogonal synthetase–tRNA pairs, genome recoding, unnatural base pairs and orthogonal ribosomes show that the code is a convention, and measure what changing it costs (Remark 23.5.1).
- The open problems. Prebiotic nucleotide synthesis (solved in outline for pyrimidines by the 2-aminooxazole route), polymerization on surfaces, a true replicase ribozyme (tC19Z copies $95$ nucleotides; the cross-catalytic ligase pair replicates exponentially), and evolvability, which requires both that Equation 23.1 be satisfied — Eigen's paradox, that a longer genome needs a fidelity only a longer genome can provide — and that replicators be compartmented in protocells (Definition 23.6.1), since an improved replicase in open solution confers no advantage on itself.

## Questions

1. The circularity in Section 23.1.1 is sometimes answered by saying that proteins came first, since amino acids form readily in prebiotic experiments. State the strongest objection to a protein-first origin, and the strongest objection to a DNA-first origin.

   **Answer** Protein-first fails on heredity: a polypeptide cannot be copied. There is no base-pairing rule for amino-acid side chains — this is precisely why translation needs adaptors (Crick's adaptor hypothesis, [7.2.19](/7.2.19)) — so a catalytically superior peptide cannot transmit its sequence to descendants, and without transmission there is no cumulative selection. DNA-first fails on catalysis and on biosynthesis: double-stranded B-form DNA has little structural variety and no $2'$-OH, so it is a poor catalyst (DNAzymes exist but are rarer and were found only by selection), and every deoxyribonucleotide is made from a ribonucleotide, so a DNA-first world would have had to invent ribonucleotides afterwards in order to make its own precursors. RNA is the only candidate that is both copyable and catalytic.

2. Why is the peptidyl transferase centre a better argument for the RNA world than the *Tetrahymena* intron is?

   **Answer** The intron is a dispensable, lineage-specific element: a self-splicing intron in one ciliate's rRNA gene could in principle have arisen at any time, and introns are in fact mobile elements. The peptidyl transferase centre is universal, is the catalytic heart of the one machine every organism must have, and performs the central reaction of protein synthesis — so a protein-based alternative has had the whole history of life in which to displace it and has not. In addition it catalyses aminoacyl transfer at a carbon centre, not the phosphoryl chemistry that RNA does most naturally, so it shows that RNA catalysis was not confined to reactions on its own backbone. Its weakness as evidence is shared with all relics: it shows that RNA preceded protein in this function, not that RNA preceded protein at the origin of life.

3. A selection starts from $10^{14}$ molecules of which $10^{3}$ have the desired activity. Each round retains $50\,\%$ of active molecules and $0.1\,\%$ of inactive ones, and amplification is unbiased. After how many rounds are active molecules the majority? What limits the answer in practice?

   **Answer** The enrichment per round is $\varepsilon = 0.5/0.001 = 500$. The initial odds are $10^{3}/10^{14} = 10^{-11}$, and the majority is reached when $10^{-11}\,\varepsilon^{\,r} > 1$, that is $r > 11\ln 10/\ln 500 \approx 4.1$, so five rounds. In practice three things intervene: the active molecules are also being lost ($50\,\%$ per round, so after five rounds only $10^{3} \times 0.5^{5} \approx 31$ copies of each original molecule survive selection, and amplification must restore the pool without bias); PCR and transcription are not unbiased, so short and easily amplified sequences enrich on activity-independent grounds; and molecules that bind the matrix, the beads or the primers enrich just as well as those that bind the target, which is why counter-selection against the matrix alone is included in each round.

4. Estimate how long a genome an RNA world could have maintained if its replicase copied with the fidelity of the best polymerase ribozyme, about one error in $10^{2}$ nucleotides, taking the selective superiority as $\sigma = 10$. Compare with the length of that ribozyme and state the consequence.

   **Answer** By Equation 23.1, $L < \ln\sigma/\mu = \ln 10 / 10^{-2} \approx 230$ nucleotides. The polymerase ribozyme is itself about $200$ nucleotides, so the genome encoding it is just at the threshold — and it would have no capacity left for anything else, since a functioning system needs at least a replicase and whatever ribozymes make its substrates. The consequence is that a single-molecule RNA genome is not viable at this fidelity: the information has to be split among several short molecules that are kept together and copied separately, which requires compartments (Definition 23.6.1), or fidelity has to improve first, which requires a longer molecule. Resolving this circularity is the central problem of the field.

5. Design an experiment that would distinguish the stereochemical hypothesis for the origin of the code from the error-minimization hypothesis, and state what each predicts.

   **Answer** Select RNA aptamers from random pools against each of the $20$ amino acids immobilized through the same chemistry, sequence the binding sites of the winners, and ask whether the cognate codon or anticodon triplets are over-represented in the binding sites relative to the pool's base composition and relative to triplets of amino acids other than the target. The stereochemical hypothesis predicts enrichment specifically of the cognate triplets, by a factor that should be reproducible across independent selections and across immobilization chemistries; error minimization predicts no such enrichment, since on that account the assignment reflects selection on the consequences of mistranslation, not on affinity, and should be insensitive to the chemistry of the triplet–side-chain interaction. The controls are what make the experiment hard: the pool's composition, the linker used for immobilization and the bias of the amplification all create spurious triplet enrichments, and the test must be made against a correctly randomized null. Published selections give enrichment for most of the small set of amino acids tested so far, which is why the hypotheses are usually treated as partly complementary — affinity may have fixed an early, small set of assignments that error minimization then refined.

6. (Open) The chapter claims that a self-replicating polymerase ribozyme would settle the chemical claim but not the historical one. Design the strongest experimental programme you can for the historical claim, and say what it could and could not establish.

   **Answer** The programme has three parts. (i) Prebiotic plausibility: show that every step — nucleotide synthesis, activation, polymerization, and the assembly of a vesicle around the product — runs under one self-consistent set of geochemical conditions (one pH, one temperature range, one mineral assemblage, one wet–dry or freeze–thaw cycle), rather than each step under its own optimized laboratory conditions, which is the standard criticism of the field. (ii) Continuity: show that a protocell containing a replicase ribozyme can be propagated for hundreds of generations under selection, with the replicase fed only by diffusion of nucleotides supplied at prebiotically plausible concentrations, and that it responds to selection by improving. (iii) Relics: test the predictions the hypothesis makes about present-day cells — that the oldest parts of the ribosome are RNA-only, that cofactor-binding sites inherited from ribozymes still show nucleotide-handle geometry, that the earliest-diverging functions are RNA-catalysed — against phylogenetic and structural data. Even complete success would establish only that the scenario is sufficient and consistent with the relics, not that it is what happened: the historical claim concerns a unique event of which no physical record survives, and laboratory demonstration can at best remove the objection that it was impossible. This limit is a feature of all origin-of-life research rather than a weakness of the RNA world specifically.
