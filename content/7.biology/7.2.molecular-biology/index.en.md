Molecular biology studies how genetic information is stored, copied, read out and regulated, and it explains each of these processes by the structures and interactions of molecules. It continues where [biochemistry](/7.1) stops, at the information pathways. Its core questions follow the **central dogma**: what carries the information and how it is packaged in the cell; how it is maintained, by **replication**, repair and recombination, with an error rate low enough for a **genome** to survive many generations; how it is read out by **transcription** and **translation**; and how every step of that read-out is controlled. To these the course adds questions that have become answerable only recently: how fast, how accurately, at what energetic cost and with what variability from cell to cell each step takes place. The chapters combine the three classic textbooks, Weaver, Lewin and Watson, and take their quantitative view from the course Quantitative Molecular Biology.

The course has five parts in reading order. The first describes the carrier: what a genome contains, the topology and supercoiling of DNA, and its packaging into **chromatin** and nucleosomes. The second collects the tools with which the information is cloned, manipulated, measured and sequenced; nearly every experiment in the later chapters is built from them. The third treats the maintenance of the information: DNA replication, DNA damage and repair, homologous recombination and mobile elements. The fourth and longest part follows the read-out and its control, first on the side of transcription, from the bacterial polymerase, **operons** and phage through eukaryotic polymerases, general transcription factors, activators and enhancers to chromatin regulation and **epigenetics**, and then on the side of RNA and protein, from **RNA splicing**, capping and polyadenylation through mRNA stability and regulatory RNA to translation, the genetic code and translational control. The last part consists of integrative chapters, each combining several earlier ones: **biomolecular condensates** formed by **liquid–liquid phase separation**; a quantitative view of gene expression, in which binding, the search for a site, production and removal, the input–output relation of a promoter and the noise of expression all follow from mass action and small numbers of molecules; the **RNA world hypothesis** for the origin of the information system; and **genome editing**, which turns the subject from a descriptive into an interventional one. The course summary follows one piece of information through its whole life and reads the course again as layers of regulation, as a budget of fidelity, speed and energy, and as a genealogy of methods.

The chemistry of nucleotides, the double helix, enzymes and nucleotide metabolism comes from [biochemistry](/7.1), and the kinetics and thermodynamics of binding from [physical chemistry](/6.3); the statistics of small numbers of molecules use the distributions of [probability theory](/3.1), and the chemistry of modified nucleotides and editing enzymes belongs to [chemical biology](/6.6). Molecular biology in turn supplies the mechanisms behind the transmission of genes in [genetics](/7.4) and the compartments and cell cycle of [cell biology](/7.3). The mechanics of DNA, single-molecule methods and imaging are treated in [biophysics](/7.6), the analysis of sequences and omics data in [bioinformatics](/7.8), and regulatory networks, noise and synthetic circuits in [systems biology](/7.9).

Molecular biology has never been my strong subject, already in olympiad training. Learning the function of every molecule at every step of the central dogma did not interest me; classmates told me that it is a very logical course, and I am sorry that I never felt this myself. What I wanted instead was to understand it from a quantitative point of view, by asking how many molecules there are, how fast they act and how accurately, a view that has since grown into something close to bioinformatics. This is why I added Quantitative Molecular Biology to the sources of these pages. Besides the fragmentation of the knowledge, I once also suspected that molecular biology had grown old: its central mechanisms were settled decades ago, and perhaps it was best treated as a set of techniques. Could there still be basic breakthroughs? After looking more closely at research in the field, I now think the chances are good, and the last few years look like the beginning of a revival. Condensates, quantitative models of gene expression that single-molecule and sequencing measurements now make testable, and genome editing have reopened basic questions, such as predicting the output of a eukaryotic promoter from its sequence and its factors, or how such an information system could have arisen at all.

The course I took was the honors version, taught largely along Weaver. Of its two instructors, one taught flexibly, the other in great detail but more as a list of facts with less of a connecting thread, and for building the framework and the basic knowledge the honors course is not necessarily better than the regular Molecular Biology (30450453). I recommend the regular course to students without an olympiad background. The content of molecular biology is not much larger than that of biochemistry, but its materials are far more abundant, so these pages sort and combine them rather than follow one book. For reading in Chinese, a beginner can start from the molecular part of the *Concise Course in Biochemistry* or of Rongwu Yang's *Principles of Biochemistry*, or from Yang's *Molecular Biology*; only readers with a particular interest need Yuxian Zhu's *Modern Molecular Biology*; and Weaver, Lewin and Watson, also available in translation, are best used as references.
