The chapters before this one explain how to read the genome and how to measure what it does. This one is about writing it. The logic of the subject is stated best by the course material itself: sequencing is reading, synthesis is writing, and editing is the ability to change one chosen position in a genome inside a living cell. Every tool used for editing was taken from nature and repurposed — a transcription factor's DNA-binding module, a plant pathogen's effector, a bacterial immune system, an antibody-diversifying deaminase — and in each case the step from natural function to laboratory tool was the same: separate the part that recognizes a sequence from the part that performs chemistry, then recombine them at will. The chapter follows that logic: where the parts came from (24.1), what a targeted break does to the repair pathways of [7.2.8](/7.2.8) and [7.2.9](/7.2.9) (24.2), protein-guided nucleases (24.3), RNA-guided nucleases and the catalytically dead derivatives that regulate rather than cut (24.4), editing that avoids a double-strand break altogether (24.5), editing of RNA and of the mitochondrial genome (24.6), the use of editing at genome scale as a screen (24.7), the two things that limit its use in patients (24.8), and where the subject's boundaries now run (24.9). The chemistry of the deaminases and of the modified nucleotides belongs to chemical biology ([6.6](/6.6)) and the analysis of screen data to bioinformatics ([7.8](/7.8)); both are referred to and not developed here.

## 24.1. Tools from Nature

Four lineages of tools, four natural systems, and a single design principle.

| Natural system | Its biological job | What was taken | Used in |
|---|---|---|---|
| TFIIIA of *Xenopus*, which binds the 5S rRNA gene and its own transcript ([7.2.12](/7.2.12)) | transcription of 5S rRNA | the Cys$_2$His$_2$ **zinc finger**, a $\sim 30$-residue module that reads $3\,\mathrm{bp}$ | **zinc finger** nucleases; engineered transcription factors |
| TAL effectors of *Xanthomonas*, injected into plant cells to activate host genes | bacterial virulence | a repeat array in which one repeat reads one **base pair** | TALENs; TALE-fused deaminases and nickases |
| CRISPR–Cas adaptive immunity in bacteria and archaea ([7.11](/7.11)) | destruction of phage and **plasmid** DNA remembered as spacers | an RNA-guided nuclease: specificity supplied by a base-pairing guide | Cas9 and its derivatives; essentially all current editing |
| AID and the APOBEC family, and the ADAR family | **antibody** diversification ([7.2.9](/7.2.9)); innate immunity; **RNA editing** ([7.2.17](/7.2.17)) | single-strand-specific nucleobase deaminases | cytosine base editors; RNA editing with guide RNAs |

**Remark 24.1.1** The principle common to all four is modularity, and it is the same principle that made the transcription factors of [7.2.14](/7.2.14) intelligible: a DNA-binding **domain** and an effector domain are separable and recombinable, so that an **activator** becomes a nuclease by exchanging one half. What distinguishes the CRISPR tools from the rest is where the specificity lives. In a zinc finger or a TALE, specificity is written in protein sequence and must be rebuilt by protein engineering for every new target; in Cas9, specificity is written in an RNA that pairs with the target, so a new target costs one pair of synthetic oligonucleotides. That difference is why protein-guided nucleases were superseded within two years for most purposes, and why they are still used where RNA cannot go (Section 24.6).

## 24.2. Making a Break on Purpose

### 24.2.1. Gene Targeting Before Nucleases

**Homologous recombination** ([7.2.9](/7.2.9)) can in principle replace any genomic sequence with a designed one: a donor molecule carrying the desired change between two arms homologous to the target recombines with the chromosome and leaves the change behind. Gene targeting in mouse embryonic stem cells, developed by Capecchi, Smithies and Evans (Nobel Prize 2007), made this routine enough to build knockout mice, with positive–negative selection to enrich the rare correct events: a selectable marker inside the donor survives only if the donor integrates, and a thymidine kinase gene outside the arms is lost in homologous events but retained in random ones, so that the two drugs together select for homologous integration.

The problem was the frequency. Spontaneous homologous integration occurs in about $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-3}$ of treated cells, with random integration more frequent still, so targeting required strong selection and large numbers of clones, and was practical in mouse ES cells and in a few other systems — not in a patient's blood stem cells.

### 24.2.2. Editing Is the Deliberate Raising of a Local Break Rate

The experiment that changed this introduced a double-strand break at the target site with the homing **endonuclease** I-*Sce*I, whose $18\,\mathrm{bp}$ recognition site had been placed in the genome beforehand. A break at the target raised the frequency of homology-directed repair at that site by two to three orders of magnitude. Every editing technology since is a way of making such a break — or a nick, or a chemical change — at a site chosen by the experimenter rather than by the enzyme's own specificity.

**Definition 24.2.1** **Genome editing** is the introduction of a designed change at a chosen position in the genome of a living cell by a programmable enzyme that recognizes that position. In its commonest form the enzyme creates a double-strand break, and the change is produced by the cell's own repair of that break.

This definition makes the outcome a property of the cell, not of the tool. A double-strand break is repaired by the pathways of [7.2.9](/7.2.9), and which one acts decides what the experimenter gets.

| Outcome | Pathway | When | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small insertions and deletions at the break | **nonhomologous end joining**, and microhomology-mediated end joining | all phases of the cell cycle | frameshifts: gene knockout, the commonest outcome by far |
| The designed sequence from a donor | **homologous recombination** (homology-directed repair, HDR) | S and G2 only, when a sister chromatid is normally the template | precise substitutions, insertions, tags |
| Deletion of the intervening segment | two breaks, joined by end joining | all phases | deletion of an **exon**, a regulatory element or a whole gene |
| Translocations and large deletions | joining of breaks on different chromosomes, or resection past the break | all phases | unwanted, and a safety problem (Section 24.8) |

**Remark 24.2.1** Two consequences follow, and both are practical. First, knocking a gene out is easy and correcting it is hard: end joining works in every cell, homology-directed repair only in dividing cells in S or G2, which is why editing postmitotic tissue with a donor template is close to hopeless and why the editors of Section 24.5, which need no break and no donor, were developed. Second, the indel spectrum at a given site is not random: the microhomologies around the cut determine which deletions dominate, so the outcome is reproducible between experiments and predictable from the sequence — a prediction problem now solved well enough by machine learning on large outcome data sets that the dominant product of a cut can be chosen deliberately.

![A break on purpose, and what the cell does with it. A: the two repair routes from a targeted double-strand break — end joining, which is available throughout the cell cycle and gives small insertions and deletions and hence knockouts, and homology-directed repair, which needs a donor and a cell in S or G2 and gives the designed sequence. B: why a break is made at all. The frequency of homology-directed repair at a locus against the rate at which the locus is cut, from the kinetic model of Example 24.2.1: spontaneous recombination sits at 10^-6–10^-3, and a nuclease that cuts most cells raises it by two to three orders of magnitude. C: the time course from the same model — the fraction of cells with an unmodified site falls exponentially while indel and HDR products accumulate, their ratio set by the competition between the two pathways and by the fraction of cells in S/G2. D: the indel spectrum at one site is reproducible, dominated by deletions whose endpoints lie at short microhomologies flanking the cut, which is why the outcome can be predicted from the sequence.](./plots/break-to-outcome.py)

**Example 24.2.1 (The kinetics of editing a population of cells)** Treat cutting as a first-order process with rate $k_{\mathrm{cut}}$ per cell and let each break be repaired either by end joining, with probability $p_{\mathrm{NHEJ}}$, or by homology-directed repair, with probability $p_{\mathrm{HDR}} = 1 - p_{\mathrm{NHEJ}}$, where $p_{\mathrm{HDR}}$ is proportional to the fraction of cells in S/G2 and to the donor concentration. Indel products are not recut (the site is destroyed), but a correctly repaired or HDR-repaired site that retains the target sequence can be cut again. With $u$ the fraction of cells whose site is still unmodified,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t} = -k_{\mathrm{cut}}\,u, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{\mathrm{indel}}}{\mathrm{d}t} = p_{\mathrm{NHEJ}}k_{\mathrm{cut}}\,u, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{\mathrm{HDR}}}{\mathrm{d}t} = p_{\mathrm{HDR}}k_{\mathrm{cut}}\,u, \tag{24.1}
$$

so the final ratio of the two products is $p_{\mathrm{HDR}}/p_{\mathrm{NHEJ}}$, independent of $k_{\mathrm{cut}}$, while the total editing efficiency is $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-k_{\mathrm{cut}}t}$. The model explains the two things one actually observes: raising the dose of nuclease increases the total edited fraction but does not improve the precision, and precision is improved only by changing the repair competition — synchronizing cells in S phase, inhibiting end joining, or tethering the donor to the nuclease.

## 24.3. Protein-Guided Nucleases: ZFNs and TALENs

### 24.3.1. Zinc Finger Nucleases

**Definition 24.3.1** A **zinc finger nuclease** (ZFN) is a fusion of an engineered array of Cys$_2$His$_2$ zinc fingers, each recognizing about $3\,\mathrm{bp}$ of DNA, to the nuclease domain of the type IIS restriction enzyme FokI ([7.2.5](/7.2.5)). Because the FokI domain cleaves only as a dimer, two ZFNs must bind opposite strands on either side of the target with their nuclease domains facing a spacer of about $5$–$7\,\mathrm{bp}$, in which the double-strand break is made.

The design requirement of dimerization is also the source of the specificity: with arrays of four fingers on each side, the two half-sites together specify about $24\,\mathrm{bp}$, which is unique in a mammalian genome. Obligate-heterodimer FokI variants, which cannot form homodimers, remove the cleavage that would otherwise occur where one of the two ZFNs binds twice.

The practical difficulty of ZFNs is that fingers are not independent. The specificity of a finger depends on its neighbours, so arrays assembled from a library of individually characterized fingers frequently fail, and selection-based assembly methods were developed to deal with the context dependence. Designing a ZFN pair for a new target was therefore a project, not a step.

### 24.3.2. TAL Effectors and Their Cipher

*Xanthomonas* species inject TAL effectors into plant cells, where they act as transcription activators of host genes. The protein's central region consists of $33$–$35$ amino-acid repeats that differ from one another at only two positions, $12$ and $13$, the repeat-variable diresidue (RVD). In 2009 two groups, comparing effectors with the **promoters** they activate, cracked the correspondence.

**Theorem 24.3.1 (The TALE cipher)** One repeat recognizes one base pair, consecutive repeats recognize consecutive base pairs, and the identity of the base is specified by the RVD alone:

| RVD | Base read | Note |
|---|---|---|
| NI | A | |
| HD | C | |
| NG | T | the standard T-reader; also used for $5$-methylcytosine |
| NN | G | binds A as well, so less specific |
| NH | G | more specific for G than NN |
| N* (deletion at 13) | C, T | degenerate |

In addition the target must be preceded by a thymine, which is read by a cryptic repeat in the amino-terminal region, so a TALE target is written $\mathrm{T}\,N_{15-20}$.

A cipher of this kind — one module, one base, context-independent — is what zinc fingers are not, and it made design trivial while making construction laborious: a TALE array for $18\,\mathrm{bp}$ is $18$ nearly identical repeats, about $2$ kilobases of highly **repetitive DNA** that cannot be made by ordinary **PCR** and must be assembled hierarchically from repeat modules.

**Definition 24.3.2** A **TALEN** (transcription activator-like effector nuclease) is a fusion of a TALE repeat array to the FokI nuclease domain; as with ZFNs, two TALENs bind opposite strands across a spacer in which the dimerized nuclease cuts.

TALENs are specific, work in most organisms, and remain the method of choice where the targeting module must be a protein rather than an RNA — notably in mitochondria (Section 24.6.3).

## 24.4. CRISPR–Cas9

### 24.4.1. From Bacterial Immunity to a Programmable Nuclease

CRISPR arrays — clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats — are the memory of a prokaryotic adaptive immune system ([7.11](/7.11)): short spacers acquired from phage and plasmid DNA are transcribed and processed into CRISPR RNAs that guide Cas nucleases to destroy sequences matching them. In the type II systems of *Streptococcus pyogenes* the effector is a single protein, Cas9, guided by a duplex of the crRNA and a *trans*-activating crRNA (tracrRNA). In 2012 Jinek, Charpentier, Doudna and colleagues showed that Cas9 cleaves DNA in vitro under the direction of this duplex and that the two RNAs can be fused into one chimeric guide; in early 2013 several groups showed that the **two-component system** works in human cells.

**Definition 24.4.1** **CRISPR–Cas9** editing uses the RNA-guided endonuclease Cas9 together with a **single guide RNA** (sgRNA), a chimera of crRNA and tracrRNA whose first $20$ nucleotides, the spacer, pair with one strand of the target. The matching genomic sequence is the **protospacer**, and cleavage requires that it be immediately followed by a **protospacer adjacent motif** (PAM), $5'$-NGG for *S. pyogenes* Cas9, which is recognized by the protein and not by the guide. The HNH domain cleaves the strand paired with the guide and the RuvC domain the displaced strand, giving a blunt break $3\,\mathrm{bp}$ from the PAM.

The PAM requirement is what keeps the system from destroying the bacterium's own CRISPR array, where the spacer sequence is present but the PAM is not; in the laboratory it is the one constraint on where a target may be placed, loosened by Cas9 orthologues and engineered variants with different or relaxed PAMs.

### 24.4.2. Interrogation, the R Loop and the Seed

Cas9 does not search for a $20$-nucleotide match directly; that would be hopeless in a genome of $3 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{bp}$, by the target-search arguments of [7.2.22](/7.2.22). It searches for PAMs, of which there are some $10^{8}$, by three-dimensional diffusion and brief non-specific binding, and only on finding one does it attempt to unwind the adjacent duplex and pair the guide with it, starting from the PAM-proximal end. If pairing propagates, the RNA–DNA hybrid and the displaced strand form an *R loop*, the HNH domain docks, and the DNA is cut; if pairing stalls, the complex dissociates within milliseconds. Interrogation is therefore directional, and the consequences are quantitative.

1. The PAM-proximal $10$–$12$ nucleotides are a *seed*: a single mismatch there usually abolishes cleavage, because pairing never propagates far enough to commit the enzyme.
2. PAM-distal mismatches are tolerated, and are the main source of off-target cleavage (Section 24.8).
3. A guide can be made to bind without cutting by truncating it to $14$–$15$ nucleotides, which is enough for an R loop to form but not for the nuclease to be activated — the principle used in the earliest guide-bound imaging experiments and an independent check that binding and cleavage are separable steps.

Cloning a guide is correspondingly trivial: two annealed oligonucleotides are ligated into a vector cut with a type IIS enzyme ([7.2.5](/7.2.5)) such that the overhangs place the $20$-nucleotide spacer precisely at the start of the scaffold. This is the detail that makes libraries of $10^{5}$ guides a routine synthesis.

C: the free-energy picture — each matched base pair lowers the energy, each mismatch raises it, so a seed mismatch stalls the R loop before the enzyme commits while a PAM-distal mismatch is paid for after commitment, which is why cleavage depends on mismatch position and not merely on mismatch number. D: cleavage against the position of a single mismatch, the characteristic signature of directional interrogation. ### 24.4.3. Dead Cas9: Regulation Instead of Cutting

Inactivating both nuclease domains (D10A in RuvC and H840A in HNH) gives catalytically dead Cas9, dCas9, which still binds wherever the guide directs it. It is a programmable DNA-binding protein, and therefore a programmable anything, by the modularity of Section 24.1.

**Definition 24.4.2** **CRISPR interference** (CRISPRi) is the repression of transcription by dCas9 bound at or near a promoter, which blocks initiation or elongation sterically; fused to a repressor domain such as KRAB it silences the target gene over a window of a few hundred base pairs. **CRISPR activation** (CRISPRa) is the converse: dCas9 fused to, or recruiting, activation domains ([7.2.14](/7.2.14)) at a promoter raises transcription of the downstream gene.

Both are reversible and dose-dependent, neither changes the genome, and both inherit the targeting rules above. The same chassis carries fluorescent proteins for imaging a locus, chromatin-modifying enzymes for testing whether a mark is sufficient rather than merely correlated ([7.2.15](/7.2.15)), and, in the next section, deaminases and a **reverse transcriptase**.

## 24.5. Editing Without a Double-Strand Break

A double-strand break is a crude instrument: it gives indels where a substitution was wanted, it depends on the cell cycle, and it occasionally gives translocations and large deletions. Both classes of editor in this section avoid it by exploiting the one thing the R loop provides besides position — a short stretch of single-stranded DNA.

### 24.5.1. Base Editors

**Definition 24.5.1** A **base editor** is a fusion of a catalytically impaired Cas9 to a nucleobase deaminase that acts on the single-stranded DNA exposed in the R loop. A **cytosine base editor** (CBE) carries a cytidine deaminase and converts $\mathrm{C{\cdot}G}$ to $\mathrm{T{\cdot}A}$; an **adenine base editor** (ABE) carries an evolved adenine deaminase and converts $\mathrm{A{\cdot}T}$ to $\mathrm{G{\cdot}C}$. Neither requires a double-strand break or a donor template.

The chemistry is hydrolytic **deamination**, the same reaction that damages DNA spontaneously ([7.2.8](/7.2.8)) and that the ADAR enzymes perform on RNA ([7.2.17](/7.2.17)): a water molecule held by an active-site zinc ion loses a proton, and the resulting hydroxide attacks the ring carbon bearing the exocyclic amine, which leaves as ammonia. Cytosine gives uracil, which pairs as thymine; adenine gives hypoxanthine (as the nucleoside, inosine), which pairs as guanine.

![The chemistry of both base editors, drawn for the cytosine case. The zinc ion of the deaminase holds a water molecule and lowers its pKa; the resulting hydroxide attacks C-4 of the protonated cytosine ring; collapse of the tetrahedral intermediate expels ammonia and gives uracil, which pairs with adenine and is therefore read and replicated as thymine. The reaction at C-6 of adenine is the same addition–elimination and gives hypoxanthine, read as guanine. Both deaminases act only on single-stranded nucleic acid, which is what confines an editor's action to the window of the R loop.](./plots/deamination-chemistry.py)

Three features make the fusion work as an editor rather than as a mutagen.

1. *Confinement.* The deaminases used are specific for single-stranded nucleic acid, so they act only where the R loop exposes the non-target strand, within a window of roughly positions $4$ to $8$ of the $20$-nucleotide protospacer, counting from the PAM-distal end.
2. *Defeating repair.* A $\mathrm{U{\cdot}G}$ mismatch is excised by uracil-DNA glycosylase, restoring the original base pair; cytosine base editors therefore carry one or two copies of a uracil glycosylase inhibitor from a bacteriophage, which blocks the first step of base excision repair long enough for replication to fix the change. Adenine editors need no such protection, because the cell has no efficient glycosylase for inosine in DNA.
3. *Directing repair.* Cas9 is used as a nickase (D10A), which nicks the strand opposite the edited base. The cell repairs the nicked strand using the deaminated strand as template, so the edit is written into both strands.

**Remark 24.5.1** The adenine editor had to be invented rather than found: no natural enzyme deaminates adenine in DNA. It was obtained by directed evolution in *E. coli* from TadA, a **tRNA** adenosine deaminase, selected over many rounds for activity on DNA in the context of the fusion — a laboratory instance of the modularity principle and of the **in vitro selection** of [7.2.23](/7.2.23). Later rounds of evolution produced variants fast enough to edit in a single cell cycle.

A base editor's window is its chief limitation as well as its mechanism. If more than one cytosine lies in the window, all of them may be deaminated, which is called bystander editing; if the desired change is not a **transition** of one of the two supported kinds, a base editor cannot make it. **Transversions**, insertions and deletions require the next tool.

### 24.5.2. Prime Editing

**Definition 24.5.2** **Prime editing** writes a designed sequence into the genome without a double-strand break or a donor, using a fusion of a Cas9 nickase (H840A, which nicks the non-target strand) to an engineered reverse transcriptase, directed by a **prime editing guide RNA** (pegRNA) that extends beyond the scaffold into (i) a primer binding site complementary to the nicked $3'$ end and (ii) a reverse-transcriptase template carrying the edit. The nicked $3'$ end anneals to the primer binding site and is extended by the reverse transcriptase along the template, so that the new sequence is synthesized as a $3'$ flap and then resolved into the genome.

The steps are worth naming, because each is a place where the method fails or is improved: nicking; annealing of the $3'$ end to the **primer** **binding site**, which competes with re-annealing to the genome; **reverse transcription** of the edit; equilibration between the edited $3'$ flap and the unedited $5'$ flap, with excision of one of them by structure-specific nucleases; ligation; and finally the resolution of the resulting heteroduplex. **Mismatch repair** ([7.2.8](/7.2.8)) recognizes that heteroduplex and tends to restore the original strand, so transient inhibition of mismatch repair raises efficiency several-fold; nicking the non-edited strand as well biases repair towards the edit, at the cost of occasional double-strand breaks.

Prime editing can make all twelve single-base substitutions and insertions and deletions of tens of base pairs, and in its later forms larger insertions when combined with a recombinase. It is the most general of the editors and the most demanding to optimize, since the pegRNA has four designable parts.

## 24.6. Editing RNA and the Mitochondrial Genome

### 24.6.1. Why Edit RNA

Editing DNA is permanent, which is an advantage for a heritable correction and a liability for everything else: an off-target change in the genome cannot be withdrawn, and a therapy with a wide safety margin would be better made reversible and dose-dependent. Editing the **mRNA** instead gives exactly that, since the transcript is replaced continuously ([7.2.18](/7.2.18)). The enzyme for it already exists in every human cell: ADAR deaminates adenosine to inosine in double-stranded RNA, and inosine is read as guanosine by the **ribosome** ([7.2.17](/7.2.17)).

**Definition 24.6.1** In **guide-directed RNA editing**, a synthetic or expressed antisense RNA pairs with the target transcript and forms the double-stranded substrate that endogenous ADAR requires; a cytidine placed opposite the target adenosine creates the A–C mismatch that ADAR prefers, so that the chosen adenosine is deaminated to inosine and read as guanosine. No exogenous protein is delivered.

The approach was demonstrated in two forms in 2019, one using chemically modified antisense oligonucleotides and one using a genetically encodable guide of $100$–$150$ nucleotides. In the latter, the efficiency depends on the guide's length, rising from a few per cent at $51$ nucleotides to tens of per cent at $111$–$151$ nucleotides; the editing is lost in ADAR-deficient cells and restored by supplying either ADAR1 isoform, which shows that the endogenous enzyme is what acts. Reporter assays use a UAG **stop codon** placed in frame before a fluorescent protein: editing the A of UAG gives UIG, read as UGG (tryptophan), so that fluorescence counts edited transcripts. The same guides edit endogenous transcripts, and in fibroblasts from a patient with Hurler syndrome, whose *IDUA* gene carries a TGG$\to$TAG nonsense mutation, guide-directed editing of the transcript restored a substantial fraction of $\alpha$-L-iduronidase activity.

A second generation addressed the two weaknesses — efficiency and duration — by circularizing the guide, either in vitro with RNA ligase or in cells with a permuted **group I intron** ([7.2.16](/7.2.16)) that splices its ends together. A **circular RNA** has no free ends for **exonucleases** ([7.2.18](/7.2.18)), so it persists longer and gives higher editing at lower dose, with less bystander editing of neighbouring adenosines.

### 24.6.2. What RNA Editing Cannot Do

The chemistry restricts guide-directed RNA editing to A$\to$I, that is to the correction of $\mathrm{G}\to\mathrm{A}$ mutations, which is a large but limited class; bystander adenosines in the duplex are edited too; and an edited transcript is diluted by continued transcription of the mutant gene, so the effect requires continued presence of the guide. These are not objections to the approach but statements of where it belongs: reversible, titratable intervention on a transcript, as opposed to permanent correction of a gene.

### 24.6.3. Mitochondrial Editing, and Why It Needs Protein Targeting

The mitochondrial genome causes a substantial class of inherited disease, inherited maternally and usually present as a mixture of mutant and wild-type molecules in each cell, whose proportion — the **heteroplasmy** level — sets the severity; mitochondrial disorders affect about $1$ in $2000$ children and $1$ in $4300$ adults, and most of them involve the nervous system. The genome is inside two membranes, and this is where the RNA-guided tools stop: a guide RNA cannot be imported into the mitochondrial matrix. Cas9 can be targeted to mitochondria and remains useless there, because its specificity module cannot follow it.

Editing mitochondrial DNA therefore returned to protein-guided targeting, with TALE arrays, and required a deaminase that acts on double-stranded DNA, since there is no R loop to expose a single strand. Such an enzyme was found in a bacterial interbacterial toxin, DddA, a cytidine deaminase specific for double-stranded DNA; split into two inactive halves and fused to a pair of TALE arrays binding on either side of the target, it reassembles only at the target and deaminates cytosines in the intervening window, giving $\mathrm{C{\cdot}G}\to\mathrm{T{\cdot}A}$ editing in organelles. Its limitation is that it edits cytosines on both strands within that window, with no strand selectivity.

Strand-selective editors followed, combining a TALE-fused nickase with a TALE-fused deaminase — an evolved TadA variant for A$\to$G or a single-strand cytidine deaminase with a uracil glycosylase inhibitor for C$\to$T — so that the nick directs which strand is edited and repaired. They create disease models by inactivating a mitochondrial start codon and, in cells from a patient with Leber hereditary optic neuropathy carrying the m.11778G$>$A mutation in *MT-ND4*, correct the mutation in about a fifth of molecules, which restores ATP content and the cellular oxygen consumption rate ([7.1.23](/7.1.23)) to normal. Since the correction is to the heteroplasmy level rather than to every molecule, a partial edit can be enough: mitochondrial disease has a threshold, and lowering the mutant fraction below it restores function.

## 24.7. Editing at Genome Scale: Pooled Screens

### 24.7.1. The Design

The cheapness of guide RNA synthesis converts editing from a way of testing a hypothesis about one gene into a way of asking which genes matter, without a hypothesis at all.

**Definition 24.7.1** In a **pooled screen**, a library of guide RNAs covering many genes is delivered to a population of cells at low multiplicity of infection, so that most cells receive one guide and are thereby mutated in one gene; the population is then subjected to a selection, and the abundance of each guide before and after is measured by PCR amplification of the guide cassette from genomic DNA followed by sequencing ([7.2.6](/7.2.6)). Guides whose genes are required under the selection are depleted; guides whose genes oppose survival are enriched.

The arithmetic of coverage is the part that decides whether a screen works. A library of $10^{5}$ guides maintained at $500$-fold representation requires $5\times 10^{7}$ cells carrying guides at every step, and at a multiplicity of infection of $0.3$ — chosen so that the Poisson probability of two guides in one cell is small ([3.1.4](/3.1.4)) — some $1.7\times 10^{8}$ cells must be infected. In vivo, or with primary cells, that number is the binding constraint.

**Remark 24.7.1** Two refinements address it, and both are instructive about what a screen measures. The first is to put several internal barcodes into the scaffold of each guide, so that the same guide is represented by a handful of distinguishable molecules. The barcodes behave as built-in replicates: the variance between barcodes of one guide estimates the noise directly, so the multiplicity of infection can be raised to $3$ or even $10$ — where most cells carry several guides — without the resulting mixture of phenotypes destroying the analysis, and the cell numbers needed fall by one to two orders of magnitude. The second is to replace the cut with a base editor, choosing guides that destroy a splice site, a start codon or a codon that can be converted into a stop. Such a screen knocks genes out without making a double-strand break, which removes the artefact that cutting itself impairs growth — strongest in amplified regions, where many cuts are made in one cell — and brings screens within reach of cells sensitive to DNA damage.

### 24.7.2. What Screens Have Delivered, and Where the Resolution Now Lies

Pooled screens identify host factors for infection — the receptors used by viruses, found as the guides that survive a lethal challenge — the genes whose loss confers drug resistance or sensitivity, and the genes required for proliferation in a given cell type. Extended to non-coding targets, deletion or splice-site screens identify functional long non-coding RNAs ([7.2.18](/7.2.18)) and regulatory elements, where tiling guides across a locus map the element at the resolution of the cut.

Base editors raise the resolution from the gene to the residue. A library in which every guide converts one codon changes the question from "which genes" to "which positions in which proteins": a screen converting lysine codons with an adenine editor covers hundreds of thousands of lysine positions in one experiment, and the positions whose mutation is enriched or depleted mark residues whose modification or charge matters — among them lysines whose mutation is selected for in tumours. Protein-level screening of this kind is the point at which editing stops being a way of making mutants and becomes a method of structural and functional biology.

## 24.8. Specificity and Immunogenicity

### 24.8.1. Off-Target Editing

**Definition 24.8.1** An **off-target effect** of editing is any change made at a site other than the intended one. For a guided nuclease the main class is cleavage at sites that resemble the protospacer, with mismatches concentrated in the PAM-distal region (Section 24.4.2); for base editors there are two further classes, guide-independent deamination of single-stranded DNA exposed during replication or transcription, and deamination of RNA by the same enzyme.

The measurement of off-target editing has a methodological hierarchy that is worth keeping straight, because claims about specificity depend entirely on which method was used.

| Approach | What it detects | Blind to |
|---|---|---|
| Sequencing of computationally predicted sites | cleavage at sites similar in sequence to the target | sites that are dissimilar but accessible; anything the prediction misses |
| Unbiased capture of breaks in cells (tag integration at breaks, or detection of repair-factor binding) | breaks actually made in living cells, in their chromatin context | rare events below sequencing depth |
| In vitro cleavage of genomic DNA, then sequencing | all sites a nuclease can cut, free of chromatin | cannot say which are cut in cells; over-reports |
| Whole-genome sequencing of clones | everything, including translocations and large deletions | expensive; needs many clones to reach useful sensitivity |

For base editors the guide-independent classes require different assays again: genome-wide deamination is detected by supplying an orthogonal R-loop-forming nuclease and asking whether the editor deaminates the exposed strand, and transcriptome-wide editing by RNA sequencing, which revealed that both cytosine and adenine editors edit thousands of transcript positions. Engineered deaminases with reduced affinity for single-stranded substrates, and the variant adenine deaminases selected to be fast on DNA and slow on RNA, reduce these classes substantially; the point of principle is that an editor's specificity is three separate numbers, not one.

Structural variants are the class most often missed. A targeted break can give rise to large deletions, inversions and translocations, and two guides used together give the translocation between their sites at measurable frequency, so a screen for small indels at the two sites will declare an experiment clean that whole-genome sequencing would not.

### 24.8.2. Immunogenicity and Delivery

Cas9 is a bacterial protein from organisms that colonize most people, and a majority of healthy adults carry antibodies and T cells against the *S. pyogenes* and *S. aureus* enzymes. For ex vivo editing, in which the protein is present in cultured cells for hours and the edited cells are washed and returned, pre-existing immunity matters little; for in vivo editing, where the protein is expressed in the patient's tissue, it is a real constraint, and it is one reason the field prefers transient delivery as ribonucleoprotein or mRNA over expression from a viral vector.

Delivery, indeed, rather than editing chemistry, is now what limits the applications. The first regulatory approvals illustrate the point: exagamglogene autotemcel, approved in the United Kingdom in November 2023 and in the United States in December 2023 for sickle cell disease, edits a cell type that can be removed from the patient, edited in a dish and given back — the erythroid enhancer of *BCL11A* is disrupted in autologous haematopoietic stem cells, which de-represses fetal haemoglobin and compensates for the defective adult $\beta$-globin ([7.1.7](/7.1.7)). Editing tissues that cannot be taken out and put back requires getting a large ribonucleoprotein or its mRNA into a chosen cell type in vivo, which is a problem of formulation and targeting rather than of molecular biology.

## 24.9. Where the Boundaries Run

Editing is a technology, and the question this chapter has to answer honestly is whether it is only that. Three boundaries are worth drawing.

*To chemistry ([6.6](/6.6)).* The deaminases, the engineered reverse transcriptase, the unnatural base pairs and modified nucleotides of [7.2.23](/7.2.23), and above all the delivery vehicles are chemical problems. What limits editing in patients today is the chemistry of getting a large, charged ribonucleoprotein into a specified cell type, not the molecular biology of what it does when it arrives.

*To computation ([7.8](/7.8)).* Guide design, off-target prediction, the prediction of repair outcomes from sequence, and the statistics of pooled screens are data problems, and the last of them is where most screens actually fail. This chapter has given the experimental design and the counting; the models that turn counts into gene-level statements belong there.

*To the rest of molecular biology.* The interesting residue — what is left when the engineering is subtracted — is a set of questions this chapter has raised and not answered. Why is the repair outcome at a site reproducible, and what in the chromatin ([7.2.15](/7.2.15)) decides whether a site can be edited at all? Why does a nick on one strand determine which strand is repaired? What else in nature is an RNA-guided enzyme, given that the one system we domesticated was found by sequencing bacterial genomes rather than by looking for it? These are not questions about tools, and they are the reason the subject does not reduce to a methods manual.

It is fair to say that molecular biology looked, for a while, like a finished subject whose remaining work was application. The tools in this chapter are part of why that impression was wrong, and in two ways. They make previously untestable statements testable: every claim of the preceding chapters that an element, a modification or a residue is *necessary* can now be tested by removing it in its own context rather than by correlation or by overexpression. And they turn each new natural mechanism into a candidate instrument, so that the discovery of an unusual nuclease, recombinase or deaminase is no longer a curiosity in a bacterium but a possible way of writing something that cannot currently be written. The subject's revival, if that is what the last decade has been, consists in the conversion of a descriptive science into an interventional one — which is also why its boundaries with chemistry and computation have become the places where most of the difficulty now lies.

## Summary

- The parts come from nature (Section 24.1): the zinc finger from TFIIIA, the TALE repeat from a *Xanthomonas* effector, Cas9 from prokaryotic adaptive immunity, the deaminases from antibody diversification and RNA editing. All are used by separating recognition from chemistry and recombining them (Remark 24.1.1); the decisive difference is that CRISPR carries its specificity in an RNA, so a new target costs an oligonucleotide pair rather than a protein engineering project.
- Editing raises a local break rate (Definition 24.2.1). A targeted break raises homology-directed repair at a site by two to three orders of magnitude over the $10^{-6}$–$10^{-3}$ of spontaneous gene targeting; the outcome then belongs to the cell: end joining in every phase of the cycle gives indels and knockouts, homology-directed repair only in S/G2 gives the designed sequence (Remark 24.2.1). Equation 24.1 shows that the dose of nuclease sets the total efficiency and the repair competition sets the precision.
- Protein-guided nucleases. A ZFN (Definition 24.3.1) reads $3\,\mathrm{bp}$ per finger and must dimerize to cut, which makes the pair specific but the array context-dependent; a TALEN (Definition 24.3.2) reads one base pair per repeat by the RVD cipher of Theorem 24.3.1 (NI = A, HD = C, NG = T, NN/NH = G, preceded by T), which makes design trivial and construction laborious.
- Cas9 (Definition 24.4.1) is one protein with a $20$-nucleotide guide, an NGG PAM, and a blunt cut $3\,\mathrm{bp}$ from the PAM. It searches PAMs rather than protospacers, then interrogates directionally from the PAM, so the PAM-proximal seed is intolerant of mismatches and PAM-distal mismatches are tolerated (Section 24.4.2) — one mechanism explaining both specificity and off-target cleavage. Catalytically dead Cas9 gives CRISPRi and CRISPRa (Definition 24.4.2) and a general platform for anything that can be fused to a DNA-binding protein.
- Breaks can be avoided. Base editors (Definition 24.5.1) deaminate C to U or A to I in the single-stranded window of the R loop (positions $\approx 4$–$8$), with a glycosylase inhibitor to prevent excision and a nick to direct repair; they make only the two transitions and suffer bystander editing. Prime editing (Definition 24.5.2) writes any small change from a reverse-transcriptase template in the pegRNA, through a $3'$ flap whose resolution is opposed by mismatch repair.
- RNA and mitochondria. Guide-directed RNA editing (Definition 24.6.1) recruits endogenous ADAR with an A–C mismatch, delivering a reversible, dose-dependent A$\to$I correction whose efficiency rises with guide length and with circularization. Mitochondrial editing cannot use guide RNAs at all, since they are not imported, and therefore uses TALE arrays with a double-strand-specific deaminase or a strand-selective nickase–deaminase pair; because disease depends on heteroplasmy, a partial correction suffices.
- Screens (Definition 24.7.1) turn editing into an unbiased search. Coverage is the binding constraint ($10^{5}$ guides at $500$-fold representation and MOI $0.3$ needs $\sim 10^{8}$ infected cells); internal barcodes supply built-in replicates and allow high multiplicity, and base-editor screens remove the growth artefact of cutting (Remark 24.7.1). Resolution now extends from genes to single residues.
- Specificity is three numbers (Definition 24.8.1): guide-dependent cleavage, guide-independent DNA deamination and RNA deamination, each needing its own assay, with structural variants the class most often missed. Pre-existing immunity to Cas9 constrains in vivo but not ex vivo editing; delivery, not editing chemistry, limits the applications, as the ex vivo *BCL11A* enhancer editing approved in late 2023 for sickle cell disease illustrates.

## Questions

1. A screen requires that most cells carry exactly one guide. At what multiplicity of infection are $10\,\%$ of infected cells carrying more than one guide, and what fraction of the culture is then infected at all?

   **Answer** With Poisson-distributed integrations of mean $m$ ([3.1.4](/3.1.4)), the fraction of cells infected is $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-m}$ and the fraction of infected cells with more than one guide is $\big[1 - \mathrm{e}^{-m} - m\mathrm{e}^{-m}\big]/\big[1 - \mathrm{e}^{-m}\big]$. Setting this to $0.1$ gives $m \approx 0.21$, at which $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-0.21} \approx 19\,\%$ of the culture is infected. This is the cost of a pooled screen: four cells in five are discarded by the selection marker, so the number of cells that must be infected is about five times the number required for coverage.

2. A cytosine base editor is used to correct a $\mathrm{T{\cdot}A}$ to $\mathrm{C{\cdot}G}$ mutation. What is wrong with this plan?

   **Answer** A cytosine base editor converts $\mathrm{C{\cdot}G}$ to $\mathrm{T{\cdot}A}$, not the reverse; to restore a C at a position that is now T, one needs the $\mathrm{A{\cdot}T} \to \mathrm{G{\cdot}C}$ chemistry of an adenine editor acting on the A of the complementary strand, which requires a PAM positioned so that this A falls in the window on the strand displaced by the R loop. The general rule follows from Definition 24.5.1: a base editor's chemistry is fixed and only the strand is a matter of choice, so the question is never "which base do I want" but "is there a PAM that places the base I must deaminate inside the window on the right strand". If there is not, prime editing, which is indifferent to the direction of the change, is the tool.

3. Cas9 cleaves a site with two mismatches at protospacer positions $1$ and $2$ (PAM-distal) but not a site with one mismatch at position $19$ (PAM-proximal). Explain, and say what this implies for off-target prediction.

   **Answer** Interrogation is directional (Section 24.4.2): the R loop propagates from the PAM-proximal end, and the enzyme commits to cleavage — the HNH domain docks — only once the hybrid is nearly complete. A mismatch in the PAM-proximal seed stalls propagation while the complex is still able to dissociate, so no cleavage occurs; mismatches at the PAM-distal end are encountered after most of the hybrid, and hence most of the binding energy, is already in place, so the complex stays long enough to cut. The implication is that an off-target score cannot be a count of mismatches: it must weight them by position, and because the weighting depends on the energetics of R-loop propagation it also depends on the sequence and on chromatin, which is why computational prediction has to be checked by an unbiased, in-cell assay.

4. Why does a cytosine base editor need a uracil glycosylase inhibitor while an adenine editor does not, and what would you predict about the purity of the two editors' products?

   **Answer** Cytosine deamination gives uracil, which is exactly the lesion that uracil-DNA glycosylase evolved to remove ([7.2.8](/7.2.8)); without inhibition, base excision repair restores the $\mathrm{C{\cdot}G}$ pair, and the abasic intermediate can also be resolved as a transversion or a small indel, since translesion synthesis may insert any base opposite it. Adenine deamination gives inosine, for which mammalian cells have no efficient DNA glycosylase, so the lesion persists until replication reads it as G. The prediction is therefore that adenine editors should give cleaner products — predominantly the intended $\mathrm{A{\cdot}T} \to \mathrm{G{\cdot}C}$ transition — while cytosine editors should show a tail of $\mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{G}$ and $\mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{A}$ transversions and indels at the target, whose frequency depends on how well the glycosylase is inhibited. This is what is observed, and it is the reason the product purity of a cytosine editor is always reported alongside its efficiency.

5. Mitochondrial editing was achieved with TALE arrays and a double-strand-specific deaminase rather than with Cas9. State the obstacle to Cas9 and explain why it cannot be solved by better protein targeting.

   **Answer** Cas9's specificity resides in a guide RNA, and the mitochondrial inner membrane has no general import pathway for RNA of that size; the protein itself can be delivered with a mitochondrial targeting presequence, but it arrives without the molecule that tells it where to cut. Better protein targeting cannot help, because the missing component is not the protein. The alternative is to move specificity back into protein — a TALE array, which is imported with the rest of the polypeptide — and then to supply a chemistry that works on duplex DNA, since there is no R loop to expose a single strand. That is precisely what a split double-strand-specific deaminase provides, with the split enforcing that chemistry happens only where two arrays bind.

6. (Open) Design a screen to find the chromatin features that make a locus editable, and state what the controls must be.

   **Answer** The difficulty is that editing efficiency varies between sites for at least three reasons that are confounded in any ordinary comparison: the guide's own activity, the local sequence's effect on repair outcome, and chromatin. A design that separates them tiles one or a few hundred guides across many loci and, crucially, places the *same* target sequence at many genomic positions — a library of landing pads integrated at mapped sites, so that sequence is held constant while chromatin varies — and then measures editing at each position by amplicon sequencing, with the integration site's accessibility, nucleosome occupancy and marks measured in the same cells by ATAC-seq, MNase and ChIP ([7.2.6](/7.2.6), [7.2.15](/7.2.15)). The controls are: the same guides delivered as dCas9 to measure binding independently of cutting, so that an accessibility effect on binding can be distinguished from an effect on repair; a guide-free arm to measure the background indel rate at each position; nuclease dose titration, since an accessibility difference should show as a difference in the rate constant $k_{\mathrm{cut}}$ of Equation 24.1 rather than in the final plateau; and cell-cycle sorting, since HDR-to-indel ratios differ between phases and chromatin differs with replication timing. The expected result, and the one worth testing, is that accessibility affects the rate of cutting but that the repair outcome spectrum is set by local sequence — so that chromatin determines whether a site is edited and sequence determines how.
