This chapter treats the orders of the nitrogen-fixing clade of the fabids other than Fabales: Rosales, Cucurbitales and Fagales. In Cronquist's system these three orders fall in three subclasses: Rosaceae, Rhamnaceae and Elaeagnaceae in Rosidae; Urticales (Ulmaceae, Moraceae, Cannabaceae, Urticaceae), together with the "amentiferous" Juglandales, Fagales, Myricales and Casuarinales, in Hamamelidae; and Cucurbitaceae and Begoniaceae in Violales of the Dilleniidae. Molecular evidence unites them with Fabales in a monophyletic nitrogen-fixing clade, the first case in which DNA sequences revealed a character of clear biological significance, the ability to form root nodules with nitrogen-fixing bacteria, to be concentrated phylogenetically (§43.1). The focal families of the chapter are Rosaceae, Moraceae, Cucurbitaceae and Fagaceae: the hypanthium and fruit types of Rosaceae are the best material for understanding the morphology of flowers and fruits, the syconium of Moraceae and its fig wasps are a classic example of coevolution, and Fagaceae are the most important tree family of Northern Hemisphere forests. Fabales are treated in [7.12.42](/7.12.42). For how floral formulas are written, see [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.6).

## 43.1. The Nitrogen-Fixing Clade and Root-Nodule Symbiosis

**Definition 43.1.1 (Nitrogen-fixing clade)** The monophyletic group of the fabids composed of Fabales, Rosales, Cucurbitales and Fagales is called the **nitrogen-fixing clade**. All angiosperms that form root nodules with nitrogen-fixing bacteria belong to this clade.

The plants that can form nitrogen-fixing nodules belong to two symbiotic systems (**root nodules**, [7.12.20](/7.12.20#sec-20.6.1); for signaling and physiology see [7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.2.5)):

| Symbiont | Hosts (family) | Order |
|---|---|---|
| **Rhizobia** (α- and β-proteobacteria) | Fabaceae (most Papilionoideae and mimosoids); *Parasponia* of Cannabaceae, related to *Trema* | Fabales; Rosales |
| *Frankia* (**actinomycetes**) | Betulaceae (*Alnus*), Casuarinaceae, Myricaceae | Fagales |
| | Elaeagnaceae (*Elaeagnus*, sea buckthorn), Rhamnaceae (*Ceanothus* and others), Rosaceae (4 genera of Dryadoideae including *Dryas*) | Rosales |
| | Coriariaceae (*Coriaria*), Datiscaceae (*Datisca*) | Cucurbitales |

In morphological classification these 10 families are scattered among different subclasses. In 1995, the angiosperm tree constructed by Soltis and colleagues from *rbcL* sequences showed that all 10 families fall within one monophyletic group, which became the nitrogen-fixing clade (Fig. 43.1.1). But the great majority of families in the clade (such as most of Rosaceae, Moraceae, Cucurbitaceae and Fagaceae) do not form nodules, and the nodulating groups are scattered at the tips of the orders. Soltis and colleagues therefore proposed that the common ancestor of the clade acquired some genetic prerequisite that made root-nodule symbiosis "easy to arise" (a predisposition), after which nodulation arose independently many times. Ancestral-state reconstruction by Werner and colleagues (2014) from the nodulation status of more than 3400 species supports a pattern of one origin of the predisposition, followed by about 8 independent gains of nodulation and repeated losses of the predisposition itself; Griesmann and colleagues (2018), comparing the genomes of nodulating and non-nodulating plants, found that in non-nodulating groups the genes *NIN* (encoding a transcription factor) and *RPG*, essential for nodulation, had repeatedly been lost or inactivated independently, and proposed another explanation: root-nodule symbiosis may have arisen only once in the ancestor of the nitrogen-fixing clade and been lost in most lineages thereafter. Both explanations recognize the special status of the clade in symbiotic ability, and differ in the number of gains and losses.

Note that plants outside the nitrogen-fixing clade also live in symbiosis with nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria: *Gunnera* (*Nostoc* in stem glands, [7.12.40](/7.12.40#sec-40.4.3)), the coralloid roots of cycads, and the leaf cavities of *Azolla*; these are independent of root nodules.

![Phylogeny of the nitrogen-fixing clade and the distribution of root-nodule symbiosis (after Soltis et al. 1995 and Werner et al. 2014, simplified to family level). Fabales branch off first, and Rosales are sister to Cucurbitales + Fagales. Dots mark the families that contain nodulating species: solid dots, rhizobial symbiosis (Fabaceae and *Parasponia* of Cannabaceae); open dots, *Frankia* symbiosis (actinorhizal plants); families that are not marked (such as most of Rosaceae, Moraceae, Cucurbitaceae, Fagaceae) do not nodulate](./plots/nitrogen-fixing-clade.py)

Relationships within the nitrogen-fixing clade: Fabales branch off first, and Rosales are sister to (Cucurbitales + Fagales). Another trend recurs within the clade: wind pollination. The families of the Urticales, the families of Fagales, and Tetramelaceae and Datiscaceae among Cucurbitales are all groups with small, unisexual flowers lacking petals and pollinated by wind, whose ancestors were insect-pollinated; the "amentiferous" groups are not a primitive assemblage but wind-pollinated adaptations that evolved independently several times within the nitrogen-fixing clade ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.2.2)).

## 43.2. Rosales

### 43.2.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

In APG IV, Rosales have 9 families: Rosaceae, Barbeyaceae, Dirachmaceae, Elaeagnaceae, Rhamnaceae, Ulmaceae, Cannabaceae, Moraceae and Urticaceae, with about 8000 species. Rosaceae are sister to all the others; the remaining families fall into two branches: one of Rhamnaceae, Elaeagnaceae, Barbeyaceae and Dirachmaceae (the relationships among these four differ between analyses and remain unresolved) and the "urticalean rosids" (Ulmaceae + (Cannabaceae + (Moraceae + Urticaceae))). Possible synapomorphies are reduced or absent endosperm, a hypanthium (reduced in wind-pollinated members) and valvate sepals; the urticalean rosids commonly have cystoliths in the leaves.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Urticales (Ulmaceae, Cannabaceae, Moraceae, Urticaceae, Cecropiaceae, Barbeyaceae) were placed in Hamamelidae and regarded as a branch that evolved from Hamamelidales in the direction of wind pollination; molecular evidence puts them as a whole inside Rosales, the urticalean rosids being a wind-pollinated group derived from insect-pollinated ancestors of Rosales. The boundaries of families within Urticales were also adjusted: the Celtidoideae of Ulmaceae are included in Cannabaceae, Cecropiaceae are included in Urticaceae, and the boundary between Cannabaceae and Moraceae was redrawn (§43.2.8). Rhamnaceae were in Rhamnales and Elaeagnaceae in Proteales (Cronquist).

### 43.2.2. Rosaceae

Rosaceae have about 90 genera and 3000 species, distributed worldwide and mainly in the north temperate zone; China has about 55 genera and 950 species (Chinese textbooks count 100 genera and more than 3000 species worldwide, with 51 genera and more than 1000 species in China).

Diagnostic characters:

- Herbs, shrubs or trees, often with prickles or thorns, often with conspicuous lenticels.
- Leaves alternate, rarely opposite, simple or compound; stipules often present, often paired and adnate to the base of the petiole (*Rosa*); leaf teeth often glandular, and in *Prunus* a pair of nectaries (extrafloral nectaries) often at the top of the petiole or the base of the blade.
- Flowers bisexual, rarely unisexual, actinomorphic; 5-merous; the receptacle convex or concave, and the bases of the calyx, corolla and stamens are fused into a saucer-, cup-, urn- or jar-shaped hypanthium (floral cup, [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.2.7)), the calyx, corolla and stamens all appearing to arise from the rim of the hypanthium; the flower is therefore usually a **perigynous flower**.
- Sepals 5, petals 5, free, imbricate, rarely no petals; sometimes with an epicalyx (*Potentilla*, *Fragaria*).
- Stamens often numerous (mostly 20, in several whorls, often multiples of 5), filaments free; pollen tricolporate.
- Carpels 1 to many, free or fused; ovary superior, half-inferior or inferior; 1 to several anatropous ovules per carpel; styles free.
- Fruit types diverse: follicle, achene, drupe, pome, rarely capsule; often forming aggregate fruits or accessory fruits.
- Seeds without endosperm. The seeds and leaves often contain cyanogenic glycosides (amygdalin), present in small amounts in bitter almond, peach kernel and apple seeds. The chromosome base number is mainly $x = 7$ (Rosoideae), $x = 9$ (Dryadoideae), $x = 8$ (*Prunus*) and $x = 17$ (Maleae, formed by an ancient polyploidization).

Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{5\text{–}\infty} G_{1\text{–}\infty}$ (the position of the ovary varies within the family).

Hypanthium and fruit. The fruit type of Rosaceae is determined by the shape of the hypanthium, the number of carpels and whether they are free or fused, and the relation of the ovary to the hypanthium; it is the best material for understanding fruit morphology ([7.12.31](/7.12.31), Fig. 43.2.1):

| Receptacle (hypanthium) and carpels | Ovary | Fruit | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypanthium shallow, cup-shaped; carpels 5 (2–12), free or fused at the base | Superior | Aggregate of follicles (rarely capsules) | *Spiraea*, *Sorbaria*; *Exochorda* has capsules |
| Receptacle raised, conical or spherical; carpels numerous, free, each developing into an achene | Superior | Aggregate of achenes; an accessory fruit when the receptacle becomes fleshy | *Potentilla*, mock strawberry, strawberry (the edible part is the enlarged receptacle, and the "seeds" on its surface are achenes) |
| Receptacle raised; carpels numerous, each developing into a small drupe | Superior | Aggregate of drupelets | *Rubus* (the raspberry separates from the receptacle when ripe, becoming a hollow "cap"; the blackberry falls with the receptacle) |
| Hypanthium urn- or jar-shaped; carpels numerous, free, inserted on the inner wall of the hypanthium | Superior (perigynous) | Hip: numerous achenes enclosed in a fleshy hypanthium | *Rosa* (rose, Chinese rose, Cherokee rose) |
| Hypanthium cup-shaped; carpel 1, not fused with the hypanthium | Superior (perigynous) | Drupe: exocarp thin, mesocarp fleshy, endocarp stony (the pit) | *Prunus* (peach, plum, apricot, mei, cherry) |
| Hypanthium jar-shaped; carpels 2–5, fused and adnate to the inner wall of the hypanthium | Inferior or half-inferior | Pome: the fleshy part mostly derived from the hypanthium, the carpels forming the central core (papery or stony) | Apple, pear, hawthorn, loquat, quince |

Subfamilies: from four to three. The systems of Chinese textbooks, Engler and Cronquist all divide Rosaceae by fruit type into four subfamilies: Spiraeoideae (follicles), Rosoideae (aggregates of achenes and of drupelets), Maloideae (Pyroideae, pomes) and Prunoideae (Amygdaloideae, drupes). Molecular phylogenetics (Potter et al. 2007; the phylogenomic analysis of Xiang et al. 2017) shows that subfamilies delimited in this way are mostly not monophyletic, and current classification divides Rosaceae into three subfamilies (Fig. 43.2.2):

1. Rosoideae: reduced in scope and including only the groups with numerous free carpels, usually 1 ovule per carpel, and achenes or drupelets as fruits, chromosome base number $x = 7$ (a few 8). The main tribes are Roseae (*Rosa*), Rubeae (*Rubus*), Potentilleae (*Potentilla*, *Fragaria*, *Duchesnea*, *Alchemilla*), Sanguisorbeae (*Sanguisorba*, *Agrimonia*), Colurieae (*Geum*), Ulmarieae (*Filipendula*) and others.
2. Dryadoideae: only four genera, *Dryas*, *Cercocarpus*, *Purshia* and *Chamaebatia*, in the Arctic, alpine regions and western North America, all able to form actinorhizal nodules with *Frankia*; the achenes often have persistent, elongated, feathery styles, $x = 9$. *Dryas octopetala* var. *asiatica* occurs on the alpine tundra of northeastern China and Xinjiang and is a relict of the ice age; the "Younger Dryas" in Europe (a sudden cooling about 12,900 years ago) is named from the sudden reappearance of abundant pollen and leaves of *Dryas* in the sediments.
3. Amygdaloideae (called the enlarged Spiraeoideae in some literature): includes the former Spiraeoideae, Maloideae and Prunoideae, together with *Kerria*, *Rhodotypos* and others formerly placed in Rosoideae; the plants contain sorbitol (the sugar alcohol transported as a photosynthate), and the fruit is a follicle, capsule, drupe or pome. The main tribes are Amygdaleae (*Prunus*), Maleae (*Malus*, *Pyrus*, *Crataegus*, *Eriobotrya*, *Sorbus*, *Photinia*, *Chaenomeles*, *Cotoneaster*, *Pyracantha* and others), Spiraeeae (*Spiraea*), Sorbarieae, Exochordeae, Kerrieae (*Kerria*, *Rhodotypos*), Neillieae (*Neillia*, *Stephanandra*), Osmaronieae and others.

Among the three subfamilies, Rosoideae branch off first, and Dryadoideae and Amygdaloideae are sister groups. The pome of Maleae originated only once: the earliest-diverging genera of Maleae, such as *Vauquelinia* and a few other American genera, bear capsules or follicles, which shows that the juicy pome evolved from a dry, dehiscent fruit; the chromosome base number $x = 17$ of Maleae is the result of an ancient whole-genome duplication.

Characters and representative plants of the subfamilies (and the traditional four subfamilies):

(1) The spiraeoids (traditional Spiraeoideae, now in Amygdaloideae): shrubs, often without stipules; hypanthium shallow, cup-shaped; carpels usually 5, free or fused at the base; follicles, rarely capsules. Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{5:1:2\text{–}\infty}$.

- *Spiraea*: deciduous shrubs with simple leaves and no stipules; umbels, corymbs or panicles; stamens 15–60; carpels 5, free; follicles. About 100 species, about 70 in China, occurring north and south, and many species are cultivated as ornamentals. Japanese spiraea (*S. japonica*) has ovate leaves and compound corymbs of pink flowers, and *S. japonica* var. *fortunei* is its glabrous variety, occurring in the Yangtze basin; *S. blumei* has rhombic-ovate leaves, often shallowly 3-lobed, and umbels of white flowers, widespread; *S. trilobata*, *S. pubescens* and *S. chinensis* are common shrubs of mountains in northern China.
- *Sorbaria*: shrubs with odd-pinnate leaves and stipules; large terminal panicles of small white flowers; carpels 5, fused at the base; follicles. *S. kirilowii* has glabrous inflorescences and 20 stamens, occurs in northern China and is often cultivated; false spiraea (*S. sorbifolia*) has 40–50 stamens and occurs in northeastern China.
- Pearlbush (*Exochorda racemosa*): a shrub with large white flowers; carpels 5, fused; capsule with 5 ridges. It occurs in Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Jiangxi and is cultivated as an ornamental.
- *Stephanandra chinensis*: leaves doubly serrate, panicles, occurring in the Yangtze basin.

(2) Rosoideae: woody or herbaceous; leaves alternate, often compound, stipules well developed; perigynous flowers; carpels numerous, free, inserted on a concave or convex receptacle, ovary superior, 1–2 ovules per carpel; aggregate of achenes or of drupelets. Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_\infty \underline{G}_{\infty:1:1\text{–}2}$.

- *Rosa*: shrubs, often prickly; leaves odd-pinnate, stipules adnate to the petiole; hypanthium jar-shaped; flowers large, sepals 5, petals 5 (double in cultivars); stamens numerous; carpels numerous, free, inside the hypanthium; hip. About 200 species, about 95 in China. Rugosa rose (*R. rugosa*) has stems densely covered with prickles and bristles, 5–9 leaflets with wrinkled blade, fragrant purple-red flowers, and is native to the coasts of northern and northeastern China; rose oil is extracted from the flowers, which are also used for rose tea; China rose (*R. chinensis*) has sparse, large prickles, 3–5 smooth leaflets, glandular-ciliate stipules and flowers throughout the year, native to China; multiflora rose (*R. multiflora*) has 5–9 leaflets, corymbs of white flowers, is widespread and is the rootstock of many rose cultivars; Cherokee rose (*R. laevigata*) has ternate leaves, solitary white flowers and a pear-shaped, spiny hip, occurs from eastern to southern China, and the fruit is used medicinally; *R. xanthina* has yellow flowers and is often cultivated in northern China. Modern roses were bred from the late 18th to the early 19th century from China roses, tea-scented roses (*R. odorata*) and others introduced from China to Europe and crossed with European roses; the China rose brought the character of continuous flowering (repeat flowering), which is caused by a mutation (inactivation) of a gene that represses flowering. The English word rose covers the whole genus *Rosa*, whereas the Chinese names *meigui*, *yueji* and *qiangwei* refer to different species or groups within it: *meigui* has small, dense prickles, wrinkled leaves and one flowering season, *yueji* has large, sparse prickles, smooth leaves and flowers all year, and *qiangwei* (multiflora rose and its cultivars) is mostly climbing, with many leaflets and small flowers in clusters.
- *Rubus*: shrubs, mostly prickly; leaves simple or compound; calyx persistent, petals 5; stamens and carpels numerous; aggregate of drupelets (small drupes clustered on a raised receptacle). About 700 species, about 200 in China. *R. chingii* has leaves palmately 5-lobed to the base and a red aggregate fruit, occurs in eastern China, and the fruit is the drug *fupenzi*; *R. parvifolius* has ternate leaves densely white-hairy beneath and purple-red flowers, occurs through nearly all of China, and the fruit is edible; red raspberry (*R. idaeus*) occurs in northeastern China and Xinjiang and is an important berry crop; the fruit of *R. coreanus* is also used as *fupenzi*.
- *Fragaria*: perennial herbs with stolons; leaves ternate; an epicalyx present; flowers white; the receptacle becomes fleshy in fruit with the achenes on its surface. The cultivated strawberry (*F.* × *ananassa*) is an octoploid produced by chance in 18th-century France from the hybridization of the Virginia strawberry (*F. virginiana*) of North America and the Chilean strawberry (*F. chiloensis*) of South America, not directly "native to South America"; woodland strawberry (*F. vesca*) is diploid and a model plant for the study of Rosaceae.
- Mock strawberry (*Duchesnea indica*, which molecular evidence places in *Potentilla* as *Potentilla indica*): a creeping herb with ternate leaves, yellow flowers, and epicalyx lobes larger than the sepals and 3-lobed at the tip; the receptacle enlarges in fruit, bright red, spongy and tasteless; widespread throughout China, and the whole plant is used medicinally. Mock strawberry differs from strawberry in having yellow flowers (strawberry flowers are white) and in the receptacle being not juicy in fruit.
- *Potentilla*: herbs, rarely shrubs (shrubby cinquefoil *Dasiphora fruticosa*, now mostly placed in a separate genus); leaves pinnate or palmate; an epicalyx present, flowers mostly yellow; the receptacle dry in fruit. *P. chinensis* and *P. supina* are common weeds of northern China.
- Great burnet (*Sanguisorba officinalis*): a perennial herb with odd-pinnate leaves; flowers small, tetramerous, without petals, with purple-red sepals in short spike-like heads; the root is used medicinally as an astringent and hemostatic.
- Others: *Agrimonia pilosa* (leaflets of the pinnate leaf alternately large and small, hooked spines at the top of the hypanthium), *Geum aleppicum* (achenes with persistent hooked styles, dispersed by animals), *Filipendula*, and *Alchemilla* (often apomictic).

(3) The pomaceous group (traditional Maloideae, Pyroideae, now the tribe Maleae of Amygdaloideae): trees or shrubs; leaves simple or compound, with stipules; hypanthium jar-shaped; carpels 2–5, fused with the inner wall of the hypanthium, ovary inferior or half-inferior, 2 ovules per locule (rarely 1 or many); pome. Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_\infty \overline{G}_{(2\text{–}5:2\text{–}5:2)}$.

- *Pyrus*: leaves ovate; umbellate racemes; styles 2–5, free; the flesh contains stone cells (**sclereids**). *P. pyrifolia* (Asian sand pear) occurs in the Yangtze and Pearl River basins and has many cultivars; *P. bretschneideri* (Chinese white pear) is the main cultivated pear of northern China; European pear (*P. communis*) has a gourd-shaped fruit whose flesh softens on ripening; *P. betulifolia* is a commonly used rootstock.
- *Malus*: leaves elliptic; styles fused at the base; the flesh has no stone cells and the fruit is depressed at both ends. The apple (*M. domestica*, often given as *M. pumila* in Chinese textbooks) originated from the Xinjiang wild apple (*M. sieversii*) of the Tian Shan of Central Asia, and in spreading westward along the Silk Road hybridized extensively with the European crab apple (*M. sylvestris*); *M. asiatica* (flowering apple) has smaller fruit and occurs from northern to southwestern China; *M.* × *micromalus*, *M. halliana* (with slender, pendulous flower stalks) and *M. spectabilis* are common ornamental trees; *M. baccata* (Siberian crab apple) is a commonly used cold-hardy rootstock.
- *Crataegus*: often with thorns; leaves shallowly pinnately lobed; carpels 1–5, endocarp stony, forming 1–5 pits. *C. pinnatifida* occurs in northern China, the fruit red, nearly globose, 1–1.5 cm in diameter, with white lenticels, used as fruit and medicinally (*C. pinnatifida* var. *major* has large fruit and is the main cultivated type).
- Loquat (*Eriobotrya japonica*): an evergreen small tree with large, leathery leaves densely rusty-tomentose beneath; it flowers in autumn and winter and the fruit ripens in early summer, a globose, yellow pome with large seeds; native to the Yangtze basin of China, the fruit is eaten and the leaf used medicinally (against cough).
- *Chaenomeles*: shrubs or small trees, sometimes with thorns; flowers opening before or with the leaves. *C. sinensis* (placed by some authors in *Pseudocydonia*) is a small tree with bark flaking in plates and an oblong, dark yellow, woody, fragrant fruit, occurring from eastern to southern China, the fruit used medicinally; Japanese quince (*C. speciosa*) is a spiny shrub with scarlet flowers clustered on old branches, almost sessile, and its fruit is the main source of the drug *mugua*. Note that papaya (*Carica papaya*, Caricaceae, [7.12.44](/7.12.44)) is also often called *mugua* in Chinese, but belongs to a different order.
- Others: *Photinia serratifolia*, firethorn (*Pyracantha fortuneana*), *Cotoneaster*, *Sorbus*, and serviceberry (*Amelanchier*).

(4) The plum group (traditional Prunoideae, Amygdaloideae, now the tribe Amygdaleae of Amygdaloideae): trees or shrubs; leaves simple, with stipules, glands often at the top of the petiole or the base of the blade; hypanthium cup-shaped; carpel 1, ovary superior, 2 pendulous ovules; drupe with 1 seed. Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_\infty \underline{G}_{1:1:2}$.

"*Amygdalus* or *Prunus*?" Molecular phylogenetics shows that the genera traditionally separated from *Prunus* in the broad sense (*Prunus* s.l.), namely *Amygdalus*, *Armeniaca*, *Cerasus*, *Padus*, *Laurocerasus* and others, are all nested within *Prunus*, and treating them as separate genera would make *Prunus* paraphyletic; internationally (for example Plants of the World Online) *Prunus* in the broad sense (about 340 species) is widely adopted. Flora of China (2003) and Chinese textbooks, on the basis of the number of lateral buds, whether the ovary and fruit are hairy, whether flowers and leaves appear together, and the vernation of the young leaves, divide them into several genera:

|---|---|---|---|
| *Amygdalus* | *Prunus* subg. *Amygdalus* | Axillary buds often 3 together (the lateral ones flower buds); terminal bud present; ovary and fruit hairy; stone with grooves or pits | Peach (*P. persica*), flat peach (*P. persica* var. *compressa*), flowering almond (*P. triloba*), almond (*P. dulcis*) |
| *Armeniaca* | *Prunus* subg. *Prunus* sect. *Armeniaca* | Axillary buds solitary; no terminal bud; flowers opening before the leaves; ovary and fruit often shortly hairy; stone smooth or with honeycomb pits | Apricot (*P. armeniaca*), mei (*P. mume*) |
| *Prunus* s.s. | *Prunus* subg. *Prunus* sect. *Prunus* | Axillary buds solitary; no terminal bud; flowers and leaves appearing together; ovary and fruit glabrous, with a waxy bloom; stone rugose | Japanese plum (*P. salicina*), purple-leaf plum (*P. cerasifera* f. *atropurpurea*), European plum (*P. domestica*) |
| *Cerasus* | *Prunus* subg. *Cerasus* | Young leaves conduplicate; flowers often several in corymbs or short racemes; fruit without a longitudinal groove | Chinese cherry (*P. pseudocerasus*), *P. serrulata*, Yoshino cherry (*P.* × *yedoensis*), Japanese late cherry, *P. japonica* |

Uses of representative plants: the peach is native to China and has been cultivated for more than three thousand years, and is already recorded in the *Book of Songs*; it is one of the main temperate fruit trees of the world, the kernel and the flowers are used medicinally, and the double-flowered and weeping peaches are ornamental cultivars; mei is native to China south of the Yangtze, the fruit is sour and is used to make smoked *wumei* and preserved plums, the flower is a traditional famous flower of China, and cultivars are extremely numerous; the apricot is native to northern China, and apricot kernels are of bitter and sweet kinds, the bitter ones containing more amygdalin; the Chinese cherry has small red fruit and is native to China, whereas the European sweet cherry (*P. avium*) has large fruit and is the "cherry" (*cheliezi*) of the present market; flowering cherry is the symbol of Japan, but the wild cherry species are most numerous in southwestern China.

Key characters of the family: leaves alternate, often with stipules. Flowers bisexual, regular; receptacle convex to concave, often with a hypanthium, flowers mostly perigynous; floral parts 5-merous, whorled; stamens often numerous; ovary superior, rarely inferior. Seeds without endosperm.

Economic importance: Rosaceae are the family richest in temperate fruit trees: apple, pear, peach, plum, apricot, mei, cherry, hawthorn, loquat, strawberry and raspberry all belong to it, and many, native to China, were already cultivated more than two thousand years ago; ornamentals include roses, Chinese rose, multiflora rose, mei, flowering cherry, crab apple, spiraea, *Sorbaria*, firethorn and *Photinia*; rose oil, almond, peach kernel, hawthorn, quince, great burnet, *Agrimonia* and raspberry are used medicinally or as food.

### 43.2.3. Rhamnaceae

Rhamnaceae have about 50 genera and 900 species, widely distributed; China has about 14 genera and 130 species. Trees or shrubs, erect or climbing, rarely herbs, often with thorns (stipular spines or branch spines); leaves simple, often alternate, with conspicuous venation (often triplinerved, or pinnately veined with parallel, arching lateral veins), often with stipules; flowers small, bisexual or unisexual, actinomorphic, often in cymes; sepals 4–5, valvate, often with a keel-like midrib on the inner surface; petals 4–5, small, often hooded and enclosing the opposite stamens, or absent; stamens 4–5, opposite the petals; disc fleshy; ovary superior or half-inferior, partly sunk in the disc, 2–4-locular, with 1 basal ovule per locule; drupe, capsule or samara. Floral formula: $\ast K_{4\text{–}5} C_{0,\,4\text{–}5} A_{4\text{–}5} \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}4:2\text{–}4:1)}$.

Representative plants: jujube (*Ziziphus jujuba*) is a small deciduous tree with spines derived from stipules (one long and one short, the long one straight and the short one recurved) and triplinerved leaves; the twigs are of two kinds: long shoots zigzag, and the deciduous fruiting shoots (*zaodiao*) bear the leaves and flowers; the drupe is large, deep red when ripe, with a stone pointed at both ends. Native to China and cultivated for more than three thousand years, the fruit is used as a dried fruit and medicinally. Wild jujube (*Z. jujuba* var. *spinosa*) is a much-branched spiny shrub with small, sour drupes and a stone blunt at both ends, whose seed is the drug *suanzaoren* (to calm the spirit), and it is one of the commonest shrubs of the mountains of northern China. Japanese raisin tree (*Hovenia dulcis*) is a deciduous tree whose fruiting inflorescence axis is thick, twisted, fleshy and sweet, eaten fresh and used for making wine, the true fruits being the small drupes at its tip. *Rhamnus utilis* has twig tips ending in spines, and a green dye ("Chinese green") can be extracted from the fruit and bark. *Paliurus ramosissimus* has a drupe surrounded by a disc-like wing and is often used for hedges; *Sageretia thea* is often used for bonsai; *Berchemia floribunda* is a climbing shrub. *Ceanothus* of North America forms actinorhizal nodules.

Chinese textbooks, on the basis of stamens opposite the petals and the presence of a disc, place Rhamnaceae and Vitaceae in Rhamnales, and hold that Rhamnales and Celastrales may have come from a common ancestor with two whorls of stamens (the outer opposite the sepals): disappearance of the outer whorl would give the Rhamnales with stamens opposite the petals, and disappearance of the inner whorl the Celastrales. Molecular evidence places Vitaceae in Vitales ([7.12.41](/7.12.41#sec-41.3)), Celastraceae in the COM clade ([7.12.42](/7.12.42#sec-42.3)) and Rhamnaceae in Rosales, and the floral similarities among the three are convergent.

### 43.2.4. Elaeagnaceae

Elaeagnaceae have 3 genera and about 90 species, in the north temperate to subtropical zone. Shrubs or trees, often spiny; the whole plant is densely covered with silvery-white or brown peltate scales (peltate hairs), giving the leaf undersides and young twigs a silvery or rusty color; leaves simple, entire, without stipules; flowers bisexual or unisexual, with a single perianth whorl, the perianth tube long with 4 lobes at the top (2 in *Hippophae*), no petals; stamens 4 (or 8), inserted on the perianth tube; ovary superior, 1 carpel, 1-locular, 1 ovule; the lower part of the perianth tube persists in fruit and becomes fleshy, enclosing the achene or nut and forming a drupe-like accessory fruit. The roots have actinorhizal nodules, enabling the plants to grow on poor sandy and saline soils. Floral formula: $\ast P_{(4)} A_4 \underline{G}_{1:1:1}$.

Representative plants: *Elaeagnus pungens* is an evergreen spiny shrub with leaves silvery-white and brown-scaly beneath, flowering in autumn and winter and with fruit ripening in spring, occurring south of the Yangtze and often cultivated for hedges; Russian olive (*E. angustifolia*) is a deciduous tree with lanceolate, silvery-gray leaves, tolerant of salinity and drought and an important shelterbelt tree in the northwestern deserts; *E. umbellata* has red fruit with silvery spots, edible. Sea buckthorn (*Hippophae rhamnoides*) is a deciduous shrub or small tree with spiny branches and linear leaves silvery-white beneath; dioecious, wind-pollinated, with a 2-lobed perianth; the fruit is a globose, orange-yellow accessory fruit rich in vitamin C, carotenoids and oils, used for juice and health products; with a well-developed root system, actinorhizal nodules and strong suckering, it is a pioneer tree for soil conservation and soil improvement on the Loess Plateau and in northwestern China.

### 43.2.5. Ulmaceae and Cannabaceae

Ulmaceae in the strict sense have about 7 genera and 60 species, mainly in the north temperate zone; China has 3 genera and about 25 species. Deciduous trees or shrubs; leaves simple, alternate, distichous, the margin often doubly serrate, the base often oblique, pinnately veined with lateral veins running straight to the margin (craspedodromous); stipules early-falling; flowers small, bisexual or unisexual, solitary, clustered or in cymes, often opening before the leaves; perianth 4–8, campanulate, persistent; stamens as many as the perianth lobes and opposite them, the filaments erect in the bud; ovary superior, 2 carpels, 1-locular, 1 pendulous ovule, styles 2; samara (*Ulmus*, the nutlet surrounded by a membranous wing) or small nut (*Zelkova*). Wind-pollinated. Floral formula: $\ast P_{4\text{–}8} A_{4\text{–}8} \underline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$.

Representative plants: Siberian elm (*Ulmus pumila*) has bark longitudinally fissured and rough, leaves mostly singly serrate, flowers opening before the leaves, and nearly round samaras 1–2 cm long ("elm money," edible); it occurs from northeastern and northern China to the northwest and is an important timber, greening, shelterbelt and mildly saline afforestation tree of the north; Chinese elm (*U. parvifolia*) has bark flaking in irregular thin plates, flowers in autumn and smaller samaras, and occurs in the Yangtze basin; *Zelkova serrata* and *Z. schneideriana* have unbranched lateral veins running straight to the margin and oblique small nuts as fruit, with hard wood of beautiful grain, and are excellent timber and autumn-color trees; *Hemiptelea davidii* has spiny branches. Dutch elm disease (caused by the fungus *Ophiostoma* spread by bark beetles) killed great numbers of elms in Europe and North America in the 20th century.

Cannabaceae in APG IV include the former Celtidoideae of Ulmaceae, with about 10 genera and 110 species. They differ from Ulmaceae in leaves often triplinerved (three main veins from the base), fruit a drupe or small nut, no wing (except *Pteroceltis*), filaments incurved or erect in the bud, and cystoliths in many members. The original Cannabaceae contained only *Cannabis* and *Humulus*, both herbs or herbaceous climbers without latex, with palmately lobed leaves and dioecious. Floral formula (*Cannabis*): ♂ $\ast P_5 A_5$; ♀ $P_{(1)} \underline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$ (the perianth of the female flower is reduced to a membranous cup closely enclosing the ovary).

Representative plants:

- Hemp (*Cannabis sativa*): an annual erect herb with leaves palmately parted to the base into 3–11 serrate segments; dioecious, the male flowers in panicles with 5 tepals and 5 stamens, the female flowers clustered in the leaf axils, each enclosed in a bract; achene (*huomaren*). It is native to Central Asia and is one of the oldest fiber crops: in fiber hemp the phloem fibers of the stem bark are long and tough and are used for weaving and papermaking; the seeds contain oil and are edible and used medicinally. The glandular hairs on the inflorescences of female plants secrete a resin containing tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD); THC is hallucinogenic and is the constituent of the drug "marijuana"; fiber hemp and drug hemp are different cultivated types of the same species, the latter with a high THC content, and its cultivation is strictly controlled in China.
- *Humulus*: herbaceous climbers with recurved prickles on stems and petioles; leaves opposite, palmately 3–7-lobed; dioecious, the female inflorescence becoming cone-like in fruit and composed of imbricate, persistent bracts. In hop (*H. lupulus*) the glands at the base of the bracts of the female inflorescence (lupulin glands) secrete a resin containing bitter acids (humulone) and volatile oils, which is added in brewing to give beer its bitterness and aroma and to preserve it; it occurs in Xinjiang and is cultivated everywhere; *H. scandens* (also *H. japonicus*) is a very common twining herb of roadsides and waste ground, whose pollen is an important allergenic pollen in autumn.
- *Celtis*: trees with leaves oblique at the base, triplinerved, the lateral veins arching upward and not reaching the margin; drupe nearly globose. *C. sinensis* occurs south of the Yellow River; *C. bungeana* has a fruit stalk more than twice as long as the petiole, occurs from southern northeastern China to the southwest, and has good wood.
- *Pteroceltis tatarinowii*: a monotypic genus endemic to China, a tree with dark gray bark flaking in thin plates to expose gray-green inner bark; leaves triplinerved at the base; nut winged on both sides and notched at the tip. It is common on limestone low mountains and in river valleys; the bark fibers are the main raw material of the *xuan* paper produced around Jingxian in Anhui.
- Others: *Trema* (fast-growing pioneer trees), *Aphananthe aspera*; *Parasponia* (from Southeast Asia to the Pacific islands) is the only plant other than the legumes that can form nodules with rhizobia.

### 43.2.6. Moraceae

Moraceae have about 40 genera and 1100 species, mainly in the tropics and subtropics; China has about 9 genera and 140 species.

Diagnostic characters:

- Trees, shrubs or climbers, rarely herbs; the plants have laticifers, and white latex flows from broken parts; the leaf epidermis often has **cystoliths** ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)).
- Leaves simple, alternate, rarely opposite, entire, serrate or lobed; stipules conspicuous, early-falling, sometimes large and enclosing the terminal bud, leaving an annular stipular scar at the node after falling (*Ficus*).
- Flowers small, unisexual, monoecious or dioecious; in catkins, spikes, heads or panicles, or inserted on the inner wall of a hollow, fleshy receptacle (syconium).
- Perianth 4 (2–6), single whorl, often persistent; the stamens of male flowers as many as the perianth lobes and opposite them, the filaments incurved in the bud (*Morus*) or erect; ovary of female flowers superior or inferior, 2 carpels, 1-locular, 1 pendulous ovule, styles 2 or 1.
- Fruit an achene or small drupe, often forming, together with the persistent, fleshy perianth or the enlarged inflorescence axis, a **multiple fruit**.

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_4 A_4$; ♀ $\ast P_4 \underline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$.

Representative plants:

1. *Morus*: trees or shrubs; leaves ovate, serrate, sometimes lobed, palmately three-veined from the base; flowers unisexual, dioecious, in catkin-like spikes; the filaments of male flowers incurved in the bud and straightening rapidly at anthesis, flinging out the pollen (wind pollination); the perianth of female flowers has 4 members and becomes fleshy and juicy in fruit, enclosing a small achene, many such fruits clustered on the inflorescence axis to form a multiple fruit (the mulberry). White mulberry (*M. alba*) is native to China and cultivated everywhere; the leaves are used to feed silkworms, the fruit is edible and used for wine, the root bark (*sangbaipi*), leaves and twigs are used medicinally, and the bark fibers can make mulberry-bark paper; *M. australis* often has deeply lobed leaves. China was the first country in the world to plant mulberry and raise silkworms, and the Silk Road is named from this.
2. *Broussonetia*: deciduous woody plants with latex; male inflorescences catkin-like, female inflorescences in heads; multiple fruit globose, the fleshy part of each small drupe orange-red when ripe. Paper mulberry (*B. papyrifera*) has leaves covered with coarse hairs and variable in form (unlobed or 3–5-lobed), is dioecious, and is widespread on wasteland and by villages throughout China; the bark fibers are a traditional papermaking material, and on Pacific islands the bark is beaten into bark cloth (tapa); *B. kazinoki* is a shrub and monoecious.
3. *Ficus*: about 850 species, one of the largest woody genera of the tropics; trees, shrubs or climbers, often with aerial roots; stipules large and clasping, leaving an annular scar at the node after falling; the flowers are borne on the inner wall of a syconium (the receptacle, also called the fig or hypanthodium). See below.
4. *Artocarpus*: evergreen trees with latex; multiple fruit large. Jackfruit (*A. heterophyllus*) has inflorescences borne on the trunk or large branches (cauliflory) and an ellipsoid to globose multiple fruit 30–100 cm long and weighing up to more than 20 kg, one of the largest tree-borne fruits in the world, with a hexagonal-tuberculate rind; the edible part is the fleshy perianth and the seeds are rich in starch; it is native to India and cultivated in southern China. Breadfruit (*A. altilis*) is native to the Pacific islands and is one of the staple foods there. Note that pineapple (Bromeliaceae) is also a multiple fruit but belongs to the monocots ([7.12.39](/7.12.39)) and has nothing to do with jackfruit.
5. Others: *Maclura tricuspidata* (formerly *Cudrania tricuspidata*) has hard spines, a red multiple fruit and leaves that can feed silkworms; upas tree (*Antiaris toxicaria*) has latex containing cardiac glycosides, highly toxic and formerly used for poison arrows, occurring in southern Yunnan and Hainan; *A. parvifolius* and *A. styracifolius* occur in southern China.

The syconium and fig wasps. The syconium of *Ficus* ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.7.2)) is a hollow, nearly globose, fleshy receptacle with many sessile unisexual small flowers on its inner wall and a single small opening (the ostiole) at the top closed by many bracts. There are three kinds of small flowers: the male flowers are mostly near the ostiole; the female flowers are of two kinds, long-styled flowers that can set seed, and short-styled flowers (gall flowers) whose ovaries serve for oviposition and larval development of the pollinating fig wasps. *Ficus* and the pollinating wasps of Agaonidae form an obligate pollination **mutualism** ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.7)):

1. A female wasp carrying pollen enters the syconium through the gaps between the bracts of the ostiole, its wings and antennae often falling off as it enters;
2. The female wasp crawls inside the syconium, inserting her ovipositor through the style into the ovary of a short-styled flower to lay an egg, and actively wipes the pollen from the pollen baskets on her thorax onto the stigmas (in some species pollination is passive); the styles of long-styled flowers are too long for the ovipositor to reach the ovary, so these flowers are only pollinated and set seed;
3. After laying, the female wasp dies in the syconium; the larva develops in the gall flower, each gall flower raising one wasp;
4. The wingless males emerge first, bite open the gall flowers and mate with the females still inside, and then chew a passage through the wall of the syconium and die;
5. When the females emerge, the male flowers in the syconium are just ripe; the female wasps collect pollen and fly out through the passage or the ostiole to find another fig tree of the same species in the female phase.

Most species of *Ficus* have their own specific pollinating wasp, and the phylogenetic trees of the two are broadly parallel, which shows that they have undergone long-term **coevolution** for about 75 million years; but there are also cases in which one fig species corresponds to several wasps and cases of wasps switching hosts. Fig trees bear fruit all year round in tropical rainforests and are a "keystone resource" for many birds, bats and primates. The common fig (*F. carica*) is native to the Mediterranean to western Asia and is functionally dioecious: the wild male tree (the caprifig) has male flowers and gall flowers in its syconia and raises the pollinating wasp *Blastophaga psenes*; the syconia of the female tree have only long-styled female flowers, so a wasp that enters can only pollinate and cannot lay eggs. Cultivated common fig cultivars can set fruit parthenocarpically without pollination, and the edible part is the fleshy receptacle with the small fruits in it ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.2.5)), whereas some cultivars (such as the Smyrna type) must be pollinated by the wasps to set fruit, and growers hang syconia of the wild type in the trees to help pollination ("caprification").

Other representatives of *Ficus*: rubber fig (*F. elastica*) is an evergreen tree whose bud is enclosed by a red stipule and which has large, thick, leathery leaves, native to India and often used as an indoor foliage plant; sacred fig (*F. religiosa*) has round-cordate leaves with a very long drip tip, native to India, and according to tradition the Buddha attained enlightenment under it, so it is the sacred tree of Buddhism; Chinese banyan (*F. microcarpa*) is a large evergreen tree with many pendulous aerial roots that thicken into prop roots on reaching the ground, so that a single tree makes a "one-tree forest"; it is widespread in southern China, the southwest and Southeast Asia and is the commonest street tree and village old tree of the south; creeping fig (*F. pumila*) is a climbing or creeping shrub with two kinds of leaves (the leaves of sterile shoots small and thin, those of fruiting shoots large and leathery) and a large, pear-shaped syconium, and the pectin washed from the achenes of its variety *awkeotsang* is made into a jelly (*aiyu* jelly). Many tropical species of *Ficus* are stranglers: the seed germinates in the fork of another tree, and the aerial roots grow down the trunk into the soil, gradually surrounding and strangling the host.

Key characters of the family: woody, often with latex. Leaves simple, alternate, stipules early-falling (annular scars in *Ficus*). Flowers small, unisexual, gathered in various inflorescences, with a single perianth whorl, 4-merous; stamens opposite the perianth lobes; ovary 1-locular, 1 ovule. Achenes or drupes aggregated in various multiple fruits.

### 43.2.7. Urticaceae

Urticaceae in APG IV include Cecropiaceae, with about 54 genera and 2600 species, mainly in the tropics; China has about 25 genera and 340 species.

Diagnostic characters: herbs or subshrubs, rarely trees (*Cecropia*); no latex (the sap is clear); the stem bark has long, tough phloem fibers; the epidermal cells often have cystoliths, which show as dots or streaks on dried leaves; some species have stinging hairs: a stinging hair is a single-celled long hair swollen at the base and embedded in a multicellular pedestal, the cell wall at the top silicified and brittle, so that on contact the tip snaps off into a needle point, pierces the skin and injects a fluid containing histamine, acetylcholine, serotonin and oxalic acid, causing burning pain and swelling; leaves simple, alternate or opposite, often asymmetric at the sides, triplinerved from the base, with stipules; flowers small, unisexual, in cymes, often gathered in heads or spikes; with a single perianth whorl, 4–5-merous; the stamens of male flowers opposite the perianth lobes, the filaments incurved in the bud and suddenly straightening at anthesis to fling out the pollen (wind pollination); the female flower has 1 carpel (pseudomonomerous), ovary superior, 1-locular, 1 orthotropous basal ovule; achene or drupe, often enclosed in the persistent perianth. Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{4\text{–}5} A_{4\text{–}5}$; ♀ $\ast P_{4\text{–}5} \underline{G}_{1:1:1}$.

Representative plants:

- Ramie (*Boehmeria nivea*): a subshrub with alternate, broadly ovate, coarsely toothed leaves densely white-woolly beneath; ramie has been used in China for more than three thousand years, and the phloem fibers of the stem are long and slender, white, glossy and strong, a single fiber cell being among the longest in plants, used for weaving summer cloth, fine textiles and quality paper, called ramie or China grass in English; China ranks first in the world in ramie production.
- *Urtica*: herbs with stinging hairs; leaves opposite. Stinging nettle (*U. dioica*) is widespread in the north temperate zone, the young leaves can be eaten when cooked, and the bark fibers can be woven; *U. laetevirens* occurs in mountains of northern China.
- *Girardinia*: herbs with stems and leaves densely covered with stinging hairs that sting very painfully; *G. diversifolia* occurs in the southwest, and its bark fibers can be made into rope. *Laportea* and *Dendrocnide* (trees whose stinging hairs are highly toxic) also have stinging hairs.
- *Pilea*: succulent herbs without stinging hairs, with opposite leaves; the male flowers of artillery plant (*P. microphylla*) fling out pollen in clouds like smoke when disturbed; Chinese money plant (*P. peperomioides*) has peltate, round leaves, is native to Yunnan and Sichuan, and is a common indoor foliage plant.
- *Cecropia*: pioneer trees of tropical America with hollow stems; at the base of the petiole they produce food bodies rich in glycogen (Müller bodies) that feed the Azteca ants living in the stem, and the ants drive away herbivorous insects and climbing plants (an example of myrmecophytes, [7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.6)).

Key characters of the family: herbs, no latex, phloem fibers of the stem bark well developed, often with cystoliths and stinging hairs. Flowers unisexual, in cymes, with a single perianth whorl; stamens incurved in the bud; ovary 1-locular, 1 erect ovule. Achene or drupe.

### 43.2.8. The Fate of Cronquist's Urticales

Urticales are a branch that evolved along the road to wind pollination: flowers small, unisexual, with a single perianth whorl or none, often gathered in various inflorescences, with the stamens opposite the perianth lobes. The Urticales of Chinese textbooks include 6 families, Ulmaceae, Moraceae, Cannabaceae, Urticaceae and others; molecular evidence confirms that they form a monophyletic group (the urticalean rosids) but adjusts the limits of the families:

| Family in Chinese textbooks (Cronquist) | Scope | APG IV |
|---|---|---|
| Ulmaceae | Ulmoideae: *Ulmus*, *Zelkova*, *Hemiptelea* | Ulmaceae (strict sense) |
| | Celtidoideae: *Celtis*, *Pteroceltis*, *Trema*, *Aphananthe* | Included in Cannabaceae |
| Cannabaceae | *Cannabis*, *Humulus* | Cannabaceae (enlarged) |
| Moraceae | *Morus*, *Broussonetia*, *Ficus*, *Artocarpus*, *Maclura* | Moraceae |
| Urticaceae | *Urtica*, *Boehmeria*, *Girardinia*, *Pilea* | Urticaceae (enlarged) |
| Cecropiaceae | *Cecropia*, *Poikilospermum* | Included in Urticaceae |
| Barbeyaceae | *Barbeya* | Barbeyaceae (close to Rhamnaceae) |

The urticalean rosids as a whole are also unrelated to Eucommiaceae (Cronquist placed them in a separate order Eucommiales, and Chinese textbooks hold that they share an origin with Ulmaceae because the samara of *Eucommia* resembles the fruit of elms): Eucommiaceae belong to Garryales in the asterids ([7.12.47](/7.12.47)).

## 43.3. Cucurbitales

### 43.3.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

In APG IV, Cucurbitales have 8 families: Apodanthaceae (holoparasites), Anisophylleaceae, Corynocarpaceae, Coriariaceae, Cucurbitaceae, Tetramelaceae, Datiscaceae and Begoniaceae, with about 3000 species, of which Cucurbitaceae and Begoniaceae account for the great majority. The common characters are unisexual flowers, an inferior ovary and parietal placentation in many groups (Cucurbitaceae, Begoniaceae, Tetramelaceae, Datiscaceae); the close relationship of these four families had long been recognized by morphologists, whereas Coriariaceae, Corynocarpaceae and Anisophylleaceae were added on molecular evidence. Cronquist placed Cucurbitaceae, Begoniaceae, Tetramelaceae and Datiscaceae in Violales of the Dilleniidae (the parietal-placentation group), and Coriariaceae in Ranunculales of the Magnoliidae.

Chinese textbooks have discussed the position of Cucurbitaceae: some authors, on the basis of epigynous flowers, 5-merous flowers, a gamopetalous bell-shaped corolla, stamens tending to fuse (syngenesious) and sepals with leafy tips, considered them close to Campanulaceae and placed them in Campanulales of the sympetalous group; others, on the basis of an inferior ovary, parietal placentation, free petals in some genera and ovules with a large persistent nucellus and two integuments, considered them close to Passifloraceae and placed them in Violales, and held that the most appropriate treatment was to set up a separate order Cucurbitales near Violales. Molecular evidence supports a separate Cucurbitales, but it is closely related neither to Passifloraceae (Malpighiales, [7.12.42](/7.12.42#sec-42.5.6)) nor to Campanulaceae (Asterales); it belongs to the nitrogen-fixing clade, sister to Fagales.

### 43.3.2. Cucurbitaceae

Cucurbitaceae have about 95 genera and 1000 species, mainly in the tropics and subtropics; China has about 35 genera and 150 species, in addition to many cultivated species.

Diagnostic characters:

- Annual or perennial herbaceous climbers, climbing or trailing, rarely shrubs or trees; stems often 5-angled and scabrous; vascular bundles bicollateral ([7.12.5](/7.12.5#sec-5.3)); cystoliths often present.
- Tendrils lateral, at one side of the petiole base, simple or forked; the origin of the tendrils is still disputed (modified branch, leaf or bract).
- Leaves simple, alternate, often shallowly to deeply palmately lobed, rarely pedately compound (*Gynostemma*), without stipules.
- Flowers unisexual, monoecious or dioecious, solitary, clustered or in racemes or panicles; calyx fused, 5-lobed; corolla 5-lobed, gamopetalous (rarely with free petals, as in *Luffa* and *Lagenaria*), yellow or white.
- Male flowers: stamens 5, often fused in pairs with 1 free, appearing as "3" (one anther 1-locular, two anthers 2-locular), the anthers often bent into an S shape or folded; in some genera all 5 stamens are fused into a column (in *Cucurbita* the anthers are fused into a head).
- Female flowers: ovary inferior, 3 carpels fused, 1-locular, 3 parietal placentas, the placentas often extending toward the center of the ovary and thickened, ovules numerous; stigmas 3, often 2-lobed.
- Fruit a **pepo** ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.2.2)): a fleshy berry-like fruit developed from the inferior ovary together with the receptacle, the exocarp hard, the mesocarp and endocarp fleshy and the placentas well developed; there are also dry capsules (the luffa dries when ripe, and the vascular network that remains is the "loofah") or dehiscent fruits (squirting cucumber).
- Seeds numerous, often flat, without endosperm.

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_{1+(2)+(2)}$; ♀ $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} \overline{G}_{(3:1:\infty)}$.

Representative plants (most of the "gourds" we eat every day belong to this family, with the exception of papaya):

- *Cucurbita*: native to the Americas and one of the earliest crops domesticated there (about ten thousand years ago). The Chinese pumpkin (*C. moschata*, "butternut" type) has a five-angled fruit stalk that is flared where it joins the fruit; the winter squash (*C. maxima*) has a cylindrical, unflared fruit stalk; the zucchini or summer squash (*C. pepo*) has an angled, unflared stalk, and many ornamental gourds and spaghetti squash are also its cultivars.
- *Cucumis*: cucumber (*C. sativus*) is native to the southern foot of the Himalaya (India) and was introduced to China in the Han dynasty ("barbarian gourd"); the fruit is eaten young and has a surface with spiny tubercles; melon (*C. melo*) is native to Asia (probably India) and has very many cultivars, such as the Hami melon, white orchid melon and snake melon.
- Watermelon (*Citrullus lanatus*): native to northeastern Africa (wild watermelons around Sudan are regarded as close relatives of the domesticated watermelon), already cultivated in ancient Egypt and introduced to China through the Western Regions in the Five Dynasties; the seedless watermelon is a triploid produced by crossing a tetraploid obtained by colchicine treatment with a diploid, and it is sterile because the homologous chromosomes cannot pair normally in meiosis (**polyploidy**, [7.4.16](/7.4.16)).
- Wax gourd (*Benincasa hispida*): native to tropical Asia, with a large fruit whose surface bears a waxy white bloom.
- Sponge gourd (*Luffa aegyptiaca*, formerly *L. cylindrica*): petals free, stamens 5 (or 3); the young fruit is stir-fried, and the vascular network of the ripe fruit (loofah) is used medicinally and for washing.
- Bitter melon (*Momordica charantia*): fruit with many tuberculate projections and a bitter taste, the seeds with a red aril; the seeds of *M. cochinchinensis* (*mubiezi*) are poisonous and can serve as a pesticide.
- Bottle gourd (*Lagenaria siceraria*): the lower part of the fruit is larger than the upper and the middle is constricted, and the rind lignifies on ripening and can be made into various containers; it is one of the first plants domesticated, native to Africa, and spread very early to Asia and the Americas (possibly by drifting on ocean currents); *L. siceraria* var. *hispida* has a long, club-shaped fruit and is a vegetable.
- Medicinal plants: *Gynostemma pentaphyllum* is a climber with pedate leaves and contains gypenosides similar to ginsenosides; the tuberous root products of *Trichosanthes kirilowii* are *tianhuafen*, the pericarp *gualoupi* and the seeds *gualouren*, and it occurs north and south; monk fruit (*Siraitia grosvenorii*, formerly *Momordica grosvenorii*) has a globose fruit, occurs mainly in Guangxi, and contains the sweet mogrosides, a good antitussive and natural sweetener.
- Others: chayote (*Sechium edule*, with only one seed in the fruit, which germinates inside the fruit); *Hodgsonia macrocarpa* (a large woody climber whose seeds yield oil); squirting cucumber (*Ecballium elaterium*, without tendrils; the ripe fruit detaches from its stalk on touch and squirts mucilage and seeds from the break to a distance of several meters, the mucilage being poisonous).

Key characters of the family: trailing herbs with tendrils, 5-angled stems and bicollateral vascular bundles. Leaves alternate, palmately lobed. Flowers unisexual, 5-merous, corolla gamopetalous; stamens often fused in pairs with 1 free, anthers bent; gynoecium of 3 carpels with parietal placentation, ovary inferior. Pepo.

### 43.3.3. Begoniaceae

Begoniaceae have only 2 genera: *Begonia* has about 2000 species and is one of the largest genera of angiosperms, widely distributed in the tropics and subtropics; *Hillebrandia*, endemic to Hawaii with 1 species, is sister to *Begonia*. China has about 200 species of *Begonia*, occurring mainly on limestone in the southwest and south, and many species are found in only one or two caves or gorges.

Diagnostic characters: perennial succulent herbs, rarely subshrubs, often with rhizomes or tubers; nodes swollen; leaves simple, alternate, the base often oblique (asymmetric), often with colored markings; stipules large, early-falling; flowers unisexual, monoecious, in cymes; male flowers with 4 tepals (2 large and 2 small, decussate) or 2, stamens numerous, filaments free or fused; female flowers with 2–5 (–10) tepals, ovary inferior, often with 3 wings or ridges, 3-locular (rarely 2 or 4), axile placentation (the placentas often 2-lobed), stigmas often twisted and spiral; capsule, often with 3 unequal wings; seeds extremely small and numerous. Floral formula: ♂ $P_{2+2} A_\infty$; ♀ $P_{2\text{–}5} \overline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$. In *Begonia*, leaves, petioles and even a small piece of leaf blade can be propagated by cuttings into new plants, a common horticultural method; wax begonia (*B. cucullata*), angel-wing begonia (*B. maculata*) and rex begonia (*B. rex*) are common pot and foliage plants, and *B. grandis* subsp. *sinensis* occurs from northern to southwestern China and produces bulbils.

### 43.3.4. Coriariaceae and Other Families

- Coriariaceae: only the genus *Coriaria*, with about 15 species, disjunct in the Mediterranean, East Asia, New Zealand and the Americas. Shrubs with arching branches; leaves opposite, entire, triplinerved from the base and distichous, often making a twig look like a pinnate leaf; flowers small, 5-merous, the petals enlarging and becoming fleshy in fruit and enclosing the 5 free achenes to form a berry-like accessory fruit; roots with actinorhizal nodules. *C. nepalensis* is widespread on barren slopes in the southwest and is a pioneer plant; the whole plant is poisonous and contains coriamyrtin, and eating the fruit or honey made from *Coriaria* nectar causes poisoning. In Cronquist's system Coriariaceae belong to Ranunculales (carpels free), and Chinese textbooks treat them accordingly ([7.12.40](/7.12.40#sec-40.2.1)).
- Tetramelaceae: *Tetrameles nudiflora* is a deciduous tree of tropical rainforests with huge buttresses, dioecious, with tetramerous, wind-pollinated flowers, occurring in southern Yunnan.
- Datiscaceae: *Datisca*, 2 species, with actinorhizal nodules.
- Apodanthaceae: small-flowered holoparasites, parasitic on the stems of legumes and other plants, formerly placed in Rafflesiaceae ([7.12.42](/7.12.42#sec-42.5.10)).

## 43.4. Fagales

### 43.4.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

In APG IV, Fagales have 7 families: Nothofagaceae (*Nothofagus* of the Southern Hemisphere), Fagaceae, Myricaceae, Juglandaceae (including Rhoipteleaceae), Casuarinaceae, Ticodendraceae (1 species in Central America) and Betulaceae, with about 1200 species. The common characters of Fagales are: woody; leaves simple or pinnately compound, alternate; flowers unisexual, monoecious (rarely dioecious), wind-pollinated, the perianth small or absent; male flowers often in catkins; ovary mostly inferior (superior in Myricaceae and Casuarinaceae), 2–3 (–6) carpels, 1–2 ovules per locule of which usually only 1 develops; fruit a nut (often with a cupule formed from bracts, a fruit bract or wings), seeds without endosperm, cotyledons thick. The pollen of Fagales is mostly triporate or polyporate and appears abundantly in strata of the Late Cretaceous (*Normapolles* pollen), when they were among the dominant plants of the Northern Hemisphere.

The phylogeny of Fagales is as follows: Nothofagaceae branch off first, followed by Fagaceae, and the rest divide into two branches, (Myricaceae + Juglandaceae) and (Casuarinaceae + (Ticodendraceae + Betulaceae)). Within Fagales, Betulaceae (*Alnus*), Casuarinaceae and Myricaceae have actinorhizal nodules.

Changes in circumscription: Chinese textbooks (Cronquist) set up in the Hamamelidae the separate orders Fagales (Fagaceae, Betulaceae), Juglandales (Juglandaceae, Rhoipteleaceae), Myricales and Casuarinales, and held that all were derived from Hamamelidales in the direction of wind pollination (catkins, unisexual flowers, a single perianth whorl or none, inferior ovary, 2–6 locules, 1–2 ovules, no endosperm). Molecular evidence confirms that these families form a monophyletic Fagales, but it belongs to the nitrogen-fixing clade of the fabids and is not closely related to Hamamelidaceae (Saxifragales). *Nothofagus* was formerly placed in Fagaceae and is now a separate family, Nothofagaceae.

### 43.4.2. Fagaceae

Fagaceae (also called the beech family) have about 8–10 genera and 900–1000 species, mainly in mountains from the temperate zone to the tropics of the Northern Hemisphere, and are the main foundation trees of the temperate deciduous broadleaf forests and subtropical evergreen broadleaf forests of the Northern Hemisphere; China has 7 genera and about 300 species (Chinese textbooks count 7 genera and 294 species).

Diagnostic characters:

- Evergreen or deciduous trees, rarely shrubs.
- Leaves simple, alternate, leathery or papery, pinnately veined, the lateral veins often running straight to the margin and forming awn-like bristle tips at the tips of the teeth (*Quercus*); stipules early-falling.
- Flowers unisexual, monoecious; no petals.
- Male flowers: in catkins (pendulous or erect), rarely in heads (*Fagus*); 1 to several flowers in the axil of each bract; perianth 4–8-lobed; stamens 4–20, filaments slender, anthers 2-locular, dehiscing longitudinally.
- Female flowers: solitary or 2–3 in a small dichasium, borne inside an involucre; perianth 4–8-lobed and fused with the ovary; ovary inferior, 3 (–6)-locular, with 2 ovules per locule, but only 1 ovule of the whole ovary develops into a seed; styles as many as the locules, persistent.
- Fruit a nut, solitary or 2–3 in a cupule; the cupule cup-, bowl- or bag-shaped, half or fully enclosing the nut, with scales or spines on the outside, at maturity indehiscent, valvate or irregularly splitting.
- Seeds without endosperm, cotyledons thick and rich in starch ("acorns").

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{(4\text{–}8)} A_{4\text{–}20}$; ♀ $\ast P_{(4\text{–}8)} \overline{G}_{(3\text{–}6:3\text{–}6:2)}$.

**Definition 43.4.1 (Cupule)** The cup-, bowl- or bag-shaped structure of Fagaceae that surrounds or subtends the nut, with scales, spines or concentric rings on the outside and developed from the axis and bracts of the female inflorescence (a modified inflorescence branching system), is called the **cupule**.

Chinese textbooks describe the cupule as formed by the fusion of numerous bracts (an involucre); studies of developmental morphology and fossils show that the cupule is formed by modification of the dichasial inflorescence branches below the female flowers and the bracts on them, so that one cupule can contain 1–3 (*Castanea*) or more flowers; the scales and spines on the outside of the cupule are the bracts on these branches. The cupule originated only once, in the ancestor of Fagaceae, and is a synapomorphy of the family; the scale-like cupule enclosing a single nut evolved several times within the family. The cupule and nut of each genus (Fig. 43.4.1):

Molecular analyses show that the ancestor of Fagaceae was insect-pollinated and that wind pollination arose several times independently within the family: the "amentiferous" groups are not primitive.

Representative plants:

1. *Fagus* (beech): deciduous trees with smooth, gray-white bark; about 10 species in the north temperate zone, about 5 in China. *F. longipetiolata* has ovate leaves with long petioles and a cupule covered with brown tomentum and curved soft spines, occurring in mountains south of the Yangtze; European beech (*F. sylvatica*) is the most important forest tree of central Europe.
2. *Castanea* (chestnut): deciduous trees, the twigs without a terminal bud and extending through lateral buds; leaf margin with awn-like teeth; male inflorescences erect; 1–3 female flowers in an involucre, ovary 6-locular; the cupule fully closed, densely covered with needle-like spines and containing 1–3 nuts. About 8 species. Chinese chestnut (*C. mollissima*) is native to China and cultivated everywhere; the nuts are rich in starch and it is a famous woody grain crop, cultivated for more than two thousand years; *C. henryi* has only 1 nut per cupule, the nut conical, and occurs from eastern to central China; *C. seguinii* is a shrub or small tree with scale-like glands on the leaf underside, often with 3 nuts per cupule. The American chestnut (*C. dentata*) was once the dominant tree of the forests of eastern North America and was almost completely destroyed in the early 20th century by the chestnut blight fungus (*Cryphonectria parasitica*) introduced from Asia, and the Chinese chestnut, which is strongly resistant to the disease, became a parent in resistance breeding.
3. *Castanopsis*: evergreen trees; leaves entire or serrate, often distichous; male inflorescences erect; female flowers solitary or in threes in a dichasium, ovary 3-locular; cupule fully or half enclosing, with spines. About 120 species, about 60 in China, and a main component of the evergreen broadleaf forests of the south. *C. sclerophylla* has leaves serrate above the middle and shiny beneath, a flattened-globose cupule fully enclosing the nut, with triangular, needle-tipped bracts arranged in 4–6 concentric rings, occurs south of the Yangtze, and the nuts can be made into "*kuzhu* tofu"; *C. hystrix* has leaves reddish brown beneath and reddish-brown wood, an excellent timber of southern China; *C. eyrei* has thick, leathery leaves oblique at the base with a blunt tail.
4. *Lithocarpus* (stone oak, tanoak): evergreen trees; leaves leathery, often entire; male inflorescences erect, sometimes with male and female flowers in the same inflorescence; female flowers solitary, ovary 3-locular; the cupule bowl- or saucer-shaped, the scales imbricate, spiral or in whorls, containing only 1 nut, indehiscent. About 300 species, mainly in Southeast Asia, about 120 in China. *L. henryi* has oblong leaves 12–14 cm long, entire, thick, leathery and smooth, with cupules gathered in spikes, occurring in the Yangtze basin and southern China; *L. glaber* has lanceolate leaves and occurs in mountains south of the Yangtze; the young leaves of *L. litseifolius* are sweet and used as a sweet tea.
5. *Cyclobalanopsis* (ring-cupped oak): evergreen trees; male inflorescences pendulous; female flowers solitary, ovary 3-locular; the nut enclosed by the cupule only at the base, the cupule scales fused into concentric rings. About 70 species in China. *C. glauca* has leaves serrate above the middle, grayish white and shortly hairy beneath, 8–10 pairs of lateral veins, a shallow cup-shaped cupule, and occurs widely south of the Yangtze except in Yunnan; *C. myrsinifolia* has leaves with blunt teeth from base to tip, more than 13 pairs of lateral veins and a grayish-blue-white underside. Internationally *Cyclobalanopsis* is mostly treated as *Quercus* subg. *Cerris* sect. *Cyclobalanopsis* (with names such as *Quercus glauca*), while Flora of China still keeps two genera.
6. *Quercus* (oak): deciduous or evergreen trees, rarely shrubs; male inflorescences pendulous; female flowers 1–2 clustered, ovary 3–5-locular; cupule cup-shaped, the scales imbricate or broad and spine-like, half enclosing the nut; the nut is called an acorn. About 450 species, one of the most important tree genera of the Northern Hemisphere. *Q. acutissima* has lateral veins running to the teeth and projecting as long awns, and the cupule scales are broad, spine-like and recurved; widespread in China; *Q. variabilis* (Chinese cork oak) has leaves densely white stellate-tomentose beneath, dark brown bark with a well-developed cork layer up to 10 cm thick, occurs mainly in the east and north, and the cork is the main source of cork in China, used for bottle stoppers, sound insulation, life buoys and so on; *Q. mongolica* has obovate leaves with wavy coarse teeth and is the dominant tree of the mountains of northeastern and northern China; *Q. dentata* (Daimyo oak) has large, obovate leaves; the Chinese tussah silkworm (*Antheraea pernyi*) feeds on the leaves of oaks such as *Q. mongolica*, *Q. acutissima* and *Q. dentata*, and northeastern China and Shandong are the main producing regions of tussah silk in China. The Mediterranean cork oak (*Q. suber*) is the main source of cork in the world; the English oak (*Q. robur*) is the emblematic tree of Britain and central Europe; the galls formed on young twigs of *Quercus* by gall wasps (oak galls) contain much tannin and are used in tanning, dyes and ink.

Economic importance: Fagaceae are the main foundation family of the subtropical evergreen broadleaf forests and temperate deciduous broadleaf forests of China, with many species, wide distribution and many uses; the wood is tough and is the main timber for building, railway sleepers, vehicles, ships and furniture, and is also used as logs for growing shiitake and wood ear; the seeds (acorns) are rich in starch and can be eaten, made into liquor or used as fodder, and chestnuts are an important dried fruit; the bark and cupules contain tannin and can be made into tanning extract; *Quercus variabilis* and *Q. suber* provide cork.

Key characters of the family: woody. Leaves simple, alternate, pinnately veined, the lateral veins running to the margin. Monoecious, no petals; male flowers in catkins; 1–3 female flowers in an involucre; ovary inferior, 3–6-locular, 2 ovules per locule, only 1 developing. Nut, with a cupule.

### 43.4.3. Betulaceae

Betulaceae have 6 genera and about 160 species, in the north temperate zone and the South American Andes; China has 6 genera and about 90 species.

Diagnostic characters: deciduous trees or shrubs; leaves simple, alternate, pinnately veined, the margin doubly serrate, stipules early-falling; flowers unisexual, monoecious, wind-pollinated, often opening before the leaves; the male inflorescence a pendulous catkin with 1–3 male flowers in the axil of each bract, the perianth membranous and 4-lobed or absent, stamens 2–20 (the filaments often bifid); the female inflorescence a cylindrical, cone-like or head-like spike with 2–3 female flowers in each bract, with no perianth (Betuloideae) or with the perianth fused to the ovary and irregularly divided at the top (Coryloideae); ovary inferior, 2 carpels, 2-locular, 1–2 ovules per locule; nut, winged or wingless, often enclosed in a fruit bract (a leafy, bag-shaped or tubular involucre formed by the fusion of bract and bracteoles), or many nuts together with their woody fruit bracts forming a cone-like infructescence. Seeds solitary, without endosperm. Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{4\text{–}0} A_{2\text{–}20}$; ♀ $P_0 \overline{G}_{(2:2:1\text{–}2)}$.

The family is divided into two subfamilies: Betuloideae (*Betula*, *Alnus*), in which 2–3 male flowers are borne in the axil of each bract, with a perianth, and the nut is winged and forms with the bracts a cone-like infructescence; and Coryloideae (*Corylus*, *Carpinus*, *Ostrya*, *Ostryopsis*), in which the male flowers are solitary in the axil of the bract, without a perianth, and the nut is more or less enclosed by a leafy or bag-shaped fruit bract.

Representative plants:

1. *Betula* (birch): bark often flaking in paper-like sheets, with transverse lenticels; female inflorescence solitary, the fruit bracts membranous and 3-lobed, stamens 2; the nutlet has a broad membranous wing and, together with the fruit bracts, forms a cylindrical infructescence, the fruit bracts and nutlets falling together at maturity. About 60 species, pioneer trees of the northern coniferous forests and mountains. *B. platyphylla* (Asian white birch) has white bark and ovate-triangular leaves, occurs from northeastern and northern China to the southwest, birch oil can be extracted from the bark, and the sap can be drunk (birch sap); *B. luminifera* has smooth bark and occurs south of the Yangtze.
2. *Alnus* (alder): female inflorescences arranged in racemes, the fruit bracts woody and persistent, so that the infructescence looks like a small pine cone; the nutlet has a narrow wing; the roots have actinorhizal nodules, so that alders can grow on poor riverbanks and wetlands and improve the soil. *A. cremastogyne* occurs in Sichuan and Guizhou and is an important fast-growing afforestation tree of the upper Yangtze; *A. trabeculosa* occurs from eastern to central China; *A. japonica* (Japanese alder) occurs in northeastern China.
3. *Carpinus* (hornbeam): leaf margin doubly serrate, 7–24 pairs of lateral veins running to the tips of the teeth; each female flower has 1 bract and 2 bracteoles, which after fruiting fuse into a leafy fruit bract, the nut borne at the base of the fruit bract and crowned by the persistent perianth. *C. turczaninowii* occurs mainly in northern China and also in the southwest and east and is often used for bonsai; *C. cordata* occurs from northeastern and northern China to the northwest.
4. *Corylus* (hazel): deciduous shrubs or small trees; leaves often broadly ovate, doubly serrate; flowering before the leaves, the male inflorescence cylindrical and pendulous, the male flowers without a perianth and with 4–8 stamens with bifid filaments inside the bract; the female inflorescence short, head-like, with only the red stigmas exposed; nut large, enclosed by a leafy, bell-shaped or tubular fruit bract (formed by the fusion of 1 bract and 2 bracteoles). *C. heterophylla* has leaves truncate and notched at the tip and a bell-shaped fruit bract, occurs from northeastern to southwestern China, and the nut (hazelnut) is edible; European hazel (*C. avellana*) is the main source of commercial hazelnuts in the world.

Key characters of the family: deciduous woody plants. Leaves simple, alternate, pinnately veined, doubly serrate. Monoecious, with compound pendulous catkins, each bract containing a cluster of a short cyme; perianth often absent; ovary inferior, 2-locular. Nut, winged or enclosed in a fruit bract.

### 43.4.4. Juglandaceae

Juglandaceae in APG IV include Rhoipteleaceae, with about 10 genera and 60 species, in the north temperate to subtropical zone; China has 8 genera and about 20 species.

Diagnostic characters: deciduous trees with aromatic resin and glands; leaves pinnately compound, alternate, without stipules; the pith of the twig often lamellate (*Juglans*, *Pterocarya*, seen in longitudinal section as layer upon layer of transverse diaphragms); flowers unisexual, monoecious, wind-pollinated; the male flowers in pendulous catkins, the perianth fused with the bract and irregularly 3–6-lobed, stamens 3 to many; the female flowers solitary, clustered or in spikes, the perianth fused with the ovary and shallowly 4-lobed; ovary inferior, 2 carpels, 1-locular or incompletely 2–4-locular at the base, with 1 erect basal ovule; styles 2, often feathery; fruit a drupe-like nut (enclosed in a fleshy "exocarp" developed from the bract, bracteoles and perianth) or winged; seeds without endosperm, the cotyledons often wrinkled and rich in oil. Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{3\text{–}6} A_{3\text{–}\infty}$; ♀ $\ast P_4 \overline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$.

The "fruit" of the walnut is not a true drupe: the fleshy outer "exocarp" (the green husk) develops from the bract, bracteoles and perianth outside the female flower, and the hard shell (the walnut shell) is the true pericarp developed from the ovary wall, so it is a nut enclosed by accessory structures (a drupe-like nut).

Representative plants:

1. *Juglans* (walnut): pith of the twig lamellate; leaves odd-pinnate; nut irregularly wrinkled, 2–4-locular at the base, enclosed in a fleshy "exocarp" that becomes fibrous on drying and splits at germination. About 20 species. Persian walnut (*J. regia*) has 5–9 entire, glabrous leaflets and 1–3 female flowers in a spike, is native from northwestern China to Central Asia, and has been cultivated for more than two thousand years (according to tradition introduced by Zhang Qian of the Han dynasty from the Western Regions); the seed (walnut kernel) contains 60%–70% oil and is an important woody oil crop and dried fruit, and the wood is hard and can be made into gun stocks and fine furniture; Manchurian walnut (*J. mandshurica*) has 9–17 serrate leaflets, occurs in northeastern and northern China, and is one of the "three hard broadleaf trees of the northeast."
2. *Carya* (hickory): pith of the twig solid; exocarp woody, splitting regularly into 4 valves at maturity, the shell smooth with longitudinal ridges. *C. cathayensis* has yellow glands on the underside of the leaflets and occurs mainly in the Tianmu mountain area on the border of Zhejiang and Anhui (Lin'an, Zhejiang); the nut is small and commonly called "little walnut"; pecan (*C. illinoinensis*) is native to North America, has an oblong nut and is an important dried fruit of North America.
3. *Pterocarya* (wingnut): pith of the twig lamellate; infructescence long and pendulous; the nut has wings on both sides developed from the bracteoles. *P. stenoptera* (Chinese wingnut) has a winged leaf rachis and oblong wings on both sides of the nut extending obliquely upward like a "yuanbao" ingot; it occurs in all provinces north and south, often by streams, and is cultivated as a street tree.
4. *Cyclocarya paliurus* (wheel wingnut): the fruit wing forms a disc 2.5–6 cm in diameter like a string of copper coins; widespread south of the Yangtze, and the leaves are used as a health tea.
5. *Platycarya strobilacea*: the infructescence is an erect, cone-like structure composed of woody bracts, the nutlets narrowly winged; the infructescence contains tannin and can be used as a dye; occurs from the east to the southwest.
6. *Engelhardia roxburghiana*: an evergreen tree with a nut bearing a 3-lobed, leafy fruit bract, from southern China to the southwest.
7. *Rhoiptelea chiliantha*: odd-pinnate leaves and a large pendulous panicle like a horse's tail; flowers bisexual or unisexual, sepals 4, stamens 6, ovary incompletely 2-locular with 1 locule aborted; fruit a winged nutlet. It occurs in Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi and northern Vietnam and is a relict plant under second-class national protection in China. It was formerly separated as Rhoipteleaceae (placed in Juglandales in Chinese textbooks), and molecular evidence shows that it is sister to the remaining genera of Juglandaceae, so APG IV includes it in Juglandaceae.

Key characters of the family: deciduous trees with aromatic resin. Leaves pinnately compound, without stipules. Flowers unisexual, male inflorescence a catkin; ovary inferior, 1-locular or incompletely 2–4-locular, with 1 erect ovule. Drupe-like nut or winged.

### 43.4.5. Myricaceae and Casuarinaceae

Myricaceae have 3 genera and about 50 species. Evergreen or deciduous trees or shrubs with aromatic glands; leaves simple, alternate; flowers unisexual, often dioecious, without a perianth, in catkins; ovary 1-locular, 1 basal ovule; drupe, the exocarp with numerous papillate projections; roots with actinorhizal nodules. Chinese bayberry (*Myrica rubra*, placed by some authors in *Morella rubra*) is an evergreen tree with oblanceolate leaves, dioecious, and a globose drupe that is deep red, purple-red or white when ripe, the granules on the surface being papillate projections of the exocarp (the fleshy part); it occurs south of the Yangtze, is a specialty fruit of southern China cultivated for more than two thousand years, and is most famous in Yuyao and Xianju in Zhejiang. The wax myrtle of North America (*M. cerifera*) has a waxy layer on the fruit, formerly used to make candles.

Casuarinaceae have 4 genera and about 95 species, in Australia, Southeast Asia and the Pacific islands. Evergreen trees or shrubs; the twigs are green, slender and jointed, resembling *Equisetum* or *Ephedra*, with a whorl of minute scale-like leaves at each node, the leaf bases fused into a sheath; photosynthesis is carried out mainly by the green twigs; flowers unisexual, without a perianth; the male flowers in spikes, each with only 1 stamen; the female flowers in heads, each with 2 woody bracteoles; the infructescence cone-like, composed of woody bracteoles, the nutlets (samaras) winged; roots with actinorhizal nodules. Beefwood (*Casuarina equisetifolia*) is native from Australia to the Pacific islands, tolerant of salinity, drought, sand burial and wind, and is the main shelterbelt tree of the sandy coasts of southeastern China. The green twigs and scale-like leaves, the perianthless flowers with a single stamen, and the entry of the pollen tube into the ovule through the chalaza (chalazogamy, first reported by Treub in 1891) led some authors (such as Engler and Wettstein) to regard *Casuarina* as the most primitive living angiosperm, a group linking the gymnosperms (Ephedra) and the angiosperms. It is now known that these are derived characters adapted to drought and wind pollination; Casuarinaceae belong to Fagales and are close to Betulaceae.

## Questions

1. To which type do the fruits of strawberry, apple, peach, raspberry and rose belong? From which part of the flower does the edible part of each develop?

   **Answer** The strawberry is an aggregate of achenes; the receptacle is raised and becomes fleshy in fruit, the edible part is mainly the receptacle, and the "seeds" on the surface are achenes developed from the free carpels (an accessory fruit). The apple is a pome with an inferior ovary, the carpels fused with the jar-shaped hypanthium; most of the edible flesh is the hypanthium (the fused basal parts of receptacle, perianth and stamens), and only the central core develops from the carpels (an accessory fruit). The peach is a drupe developed from one superior ovary, with a thin exocarp, a fleshy mesocarp (the edible part) and a stony endocarp that forms the pit (a true fruit). The raspberry is an aggregate of drupelets, the small drupes developed from the numerous free carpels of one flower and clustered on a raised receptacle, separating from the receptacle when ripe, the edible part being the mesocarp of the drupelets. The fruit of the rose is a hip, numerous achenes enclosed in a fleshy, jar-shaped hypanthium, the fleshy part being the hypanthium.

2. Why does current classification no longer recognize the Maloideae and Prunoideae of Rosaceae? How has molecular evidence rewritten the subfamilies of Rosaceae?

   **Answer** The traditional four subfamilies were delimited by fruit type, and molecular phylogenetics shows that subfamilies delimited this way are not all monophyletic: the Spiraeoideae are paraphyletic, with their members mixed in the same clade as the pomaceous and plum groups; the pomaceous and plum groups are each a tribe within this large clade; genera such as *Kerria* formerly placed in Rosoideae also belong to this clade; and the four genera, including *Dryas*, that form actinorhizal nodules form a clade of their own. Current classification therefore divides Rosaceae into Rosoideae (numerous free carpels, achenes or drupelets, $x = 7$), Dryadoideae, and Amygdaloideae (containing sorbitol; including the former Spiraeoideae, Maloideae and Prunoideae), the pomaceous and plum groups being reduced to the tribes Maleae and Amygdaleae within Amygdaloideae.

3. Describe the coevolutionary relationship between *Ficus* and fig wasps. Does the fact that the common fig can set fruit without pollination contradict this symbiosis?

   **Answer** The flowers of *Ficus* are borne on the inner wall of a syconium (a hollow receptacle), with male flowers, long-styled female flowers and short-styled gall flowers. A pollen-bearing female wasp enters through the ostiole, pollinates the female flowers and lays eggs in the ovaries of the gall flowers, and then dies; the larvae develop in the gall flowers, and after emerging the wingless males mate with the females and chew a passage, and the females collect pollen from the ripe male flowers and fly out to another fig tree of the same species in the female phase. The fig depends on the wasp for pollination and the wasp depends on the fig's gall flowers for reproduction; the two are an obligate mutualism, mostly one-to-one, and have coevolved over a long time. Cultivated common fig cultivars that set fruit parthenocarpically are types selected by people that no longer need pollination; the wild fig and cultivars that require pollination (such as the Smyrna type) still depend on pollinating wasps, so there is no contradiction.

4. How do we distinguish *Fagus*, *Castanea*, *Castanopsis*, *Lithocarpus*, *Cyclobalanopsis* and *Quercus* of Fagaceae?

   **Answer** *Fagus*: deciduous, male inflorescence a pendulous head, cupule 4-valved with 2 three-angled nuts inside. *Castanea*: deciduous, male inflorescence erect, cupule fully enclosing and densely covered with needle-like spines, with 1–3 nuts inside, ovary 6-locular. *Castanopsis*: evergreen, male inflorescence erect, cupule spiny, fully or half enclosing, nuts 1–3. *Lithocarpus*: evergreen, male inflorescence erect, cupule bowl-shaped with imbricate scales, indehiscent, 1 nut. *Cyclobalanopsis*: evergreen, male inflorescence pendulous, cupule cup-shaped with scales fused into concentric rings, 1 nut. *Quercus*: mostly deciduous, male inflorescence pendulous, cupule cup-shaped with imbricate or broad, spine-like scales, half enclosing 1 nut (the acorn).

5. The "amentiferous" groups were regarded as primitive angiosperms in Engler's system. How does modern evidence view these groups?

   **Answer** Engler placed Salicaceae, Juglandaceae, Betulaceae, Fagaceae, Myricaceae, Casuarinaceae and others, with unisexual, apetalous or single-perianth flowers in catkins and wind pollination, at the front of the dicots, regarding them as primitive. Molecular phylogenetics shows that these families all belong to the fabid branch of the rosids among the core eudicots, far from the base of the angiosperm tree; nor do they form a monophyletic group: Salicaceae are in Malpighiales, Moraceae and Urticaceae in Rosales, and Fagaceae, Betulaceae, Juglandaceae, Myricaceae and Casuarinaceae in Fagales, the ancestors of each having been insect-pollinated with a perianth; even within Fagaceae the ancestor was insect-pollinated, and wind pollination arose several times independently within it. Catkins and unisexual, apetalous flowers are therefore derived characters adapted to wind pollination, which evolved convergently many times in the nitrogen-fixing clade and elsewhere, and they reflect simplification and not primitiveness.
