Angiosperms appear suddenly in the fossil record in the Early Cretaceous, and by the end of the Cretaceous they dominated many kinds of vegetation. Where they came from and what their ancestors looked like are among the oldest questions in botany. The previous chapter treated the characters, taxonomic evidence and classification systems of angiosperms ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)). This chapter deals with their origin and early evolution. We first ask when they arose, using fossils and molecular clocks (§34.1); then where they arose, what their ancestors may have been, and theories of the origin of the flower (§34.2); then how molecular systematics found the earliest-diverging angiosperms and what the reconstructed ancestral flower looked like (§34.3); and finally the three orders of the **ANA grade** (§34.4) and the taxonomically unusual Ceratophyllales (§34.5). The morphology of early seed plants (seed ferns, Caytoniales, Bennettitales) is covered in [7.12.25](/7.12.25#sec-25.3), and that of gnetophytes in [7.12.26](/7.12.26#sec-26.4); the principle of molecular clocks is in [7.10.24](/7.10.24). The next chapter treats the magnoliids and Chloranthales ([7.12.35](/7.12.35)).

## 34.1. The Timing of the Origin

### 34.1.1. The "Abominable Mystery"

In a letter to J. D. Hooker dated 22 July 1879, Darwin called the rapid development of the higher plants in recent geological time "an abominable mystery". In his view, the sudden appearance of angiosperms in Cretaceous strata and their rapid diversification were incompatible with the gradual, continuous evolution expected under natural selection and could only be blamed on the incompleteness of the fossil record. Over the following century and more, the "mystery" has come to comprise several distinct questions: When did angiosperms originate? What are their ancestors (sister group)? Why did they diversify so quickly after their origin? Today the first question has a fairly good answer, the third has several complementary explanations (§34.3.1), and the second remains unresolved.

### 34.1.2. The Fossil Record

Fossil evidence for angiosperms falls into three tiers according to reliability:

| Tier | Fossil | Age | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accepted | Monosulcate pollen with a tectate–columellate wall (Israel, England, Italy, etc.) | Early Cretaceous, Valanginian–Hauterivian (about 136–130 Ma) | Earliest reliable angiosperm fossils; the tectate–columellate exine is characteristic of angiosperm pollen |
| Accepted | Tricolpate pollen | Late Barremian–early Aptian (about 125 Ma) | Marks the appearance of eudicots |
| Accepted | Charcoalified flowers, fruits and seeds (mesofossils) from Portugal and eastern North America | Barremian–Albian (about 130–100 Ma) | Extant groups such as Nymphaeales, Austrobaileyales, Chloranthaceae and Laurales can already be recognized |
| Accepted | *Archaefructus*, Yixian Formation, Liaoning | About 125 Ma (Aptian) | Aquatic herb; systematic position disputed |
| Accepted | *Montsechia vidalii*, Spain | About 130 Ma (Barremian) | Submerged aquatic, possibly related to *Ceratophyllum* |
| Disputed | *Nanjinganthus dendrostyla*, *Schmeissneria*, etc. | Early Jurassic | Angiosperm nature not generally accepted |
| Rejected | *Glossopteris*, *Sanmiguelia*, etc. ("Paleozoic origin" and Triassic evidence) | Permian, Late Triassic | Not related to angiosperms |

Angiosperm pollen has an exine with a tectum, columellae and a foot layer, whereas gymnosperm pollen exine is mostly granular or alveolar, so dispersed pollen is the first thing to reveal the presence of angiosperms in the strata. Whether Valanginian pollen older than the Hauterivian (about 132 Ma) belongs to angiosperms is still doubtful. The diversity of Early Cretaceous angiosperm pollen increases rapidly from the Barremian to the Albian; tricolpate pollen appears in the Aptian and spreads from low to high latitudes.

Beginning in the 1980s, Friis, Crane, Pedersen and colleagues used sieving to recover large numbers of three-dimensionally preserved, charcoalified small flowers from Early Cretaceous deposits in Portugal and eastern North America, and studied their internal structure with synchrotron X-ray tomography. These flowers are all small (mostly a few millimeters across), with few floral parts, often undifferentiated perianths, stamens with poorly differentiated filaments and anthers, both unisexual and bisexual flowers, and mostly ascidiate carpels. They refuted the old view that the earliest angiosperms had large magnolia-like flowers.

*Archaefructus* is an Early Cretaceous angiosperm from the Yixian Formation of western Liaoning, China. G. Sun and colleagues described *A. liaoningensis* in 1998 and *A. sinensis* in 2002, followed by *A. eoflora* (2004). It was an aquatic herb with highly dissected leaves; its reproductive axes bore spirally arranged pairs of stamens without perianth and follicle-like carpels. It was first reported as Late Jurassic (about 144 Ma), but radiometric dating later revised the age to about 125 Ma. The analysis of Sun et al. (2002) placed it as sister to all extant angiosperms, and they erected the family Archaefructaceae for it; Friis et al. (2003) reinterpreted its reproductive axis as an inflorescence of many unisexual flowers and associated it with Nymphaeales. Most current analyses place *Archaefructus* within or near Nymphaeales: it represents a reduced aquatic type among Early Cretaceous angiosperms rather than the "first flower".

*Montsechia* is a submerged aquatic from lacustrine deposits of Barremian age (about 130 Ma) in the Spanish Pyrenees. It was originally described as other kinds of plant; Gomez et al. (2015) restudied it and concluded that it is an angiosperm: the leaves are small and opposite, and the female flower has a single carpel containing one orthotropous ovule, as in *Ceratophyllum*. It shows that fully submerged, underwater-pollinated angiosperms already existed in the Early Cretaceous (§34.5).

The rapid Cretaceous diversification of angiosperms is clearly recorded in the fossils. In the Albian Potomac Group of North America, angiosperm leaves progress from simple forms with irregular venation to diverse forms with regular venation. By the Cenomanian–Turonian of the Late Cretaceous (about 100–90 Ma), flowers assignable to extant families are present (for example, Turonian fossils from New Jersey of Lauraceae, Nymphaeaceae, Brassicales and Triuridaceae), and angiosperms made up the majority of floras at low and middle latitudes.

There have been many recent reports of Jurassic or even older "angiosperms", for example *Schmeissneria* from the Early Jurassic (X. Wang et al., 2007), *Euanthus panii* from the Late Jurassic (2016), and *Nanjinganthus*, described by Fu et al. (2018) from more than two hundred specimens in Early Jurassic strata near Nanjing (older than about 174 Ma). They share a common problem: most are compression fossils, the interpretation of key structures such as the "ovary" and "ovules" depends on preservation, and there is no direct evidence for angiosperm characters such as pollen, carpel closure or two integuments. Other authors consider them more likely to be gymnosperm cones or reproductive organs. Until they are generally accepted, they can serve only as disputed evidence. The "Paleozoic origin" hypothesis often cited in Chinese textbooks (in which *Glossopteris* was treated as the ancestor of angiosperms, and Ramshaw et al. inferred from cytochrome c that angiosperms originated 400–500 Ma) and the Late Triassic *Sanmiguelia* have both been rejected.

### 34.1.3. The Gap Between Molecular Clocks and Fossils

Fossils give only the minimum age of a group, whereas a **molecular clock** can infer divergence times from differences in DNA sequences. Molecular-clock estimates of the age of the angiosperm crown group (the most recent common ancestor of extant angiosperms) are mostly much older than the earliest fossils:

| Study | Data and method | Age of crown angiosperms |
|---|---|---|
| Ramshaw et al. 1972; Martin et al. 1989 | A few proteins or genes, strict clock | 420–300 Ma |
| Sanderson and Doyle 2001 | *rbcL* + 18S, several rate models | Most likely 190–140 Ma |
| Wikström et al. 2001 | 560 species, 3 genes, nonparametric rate smoothing | 179–158 Ma |
| Bell et al. 2005, 2010 | Bayesian relaxed clock (BEAST), 36 fossil calibrations | About 200–140 Ma; Mesangiospermae 140–128 Ma |
| Smith et al. 2010 | Age of eudicots not constrained by tricolpate pollen | 228–217 Ma (Late Triassic) |
| Magallón et al. 2015 | Nearly 800 taxa, 137 fossil calibrations | About 140 Ma (Early Cretaceous) |
| Barba-Montoya et al. 2018 | Phylogenomic data, Bayesian methods | 256–149 Ma (end-Permian to Late Jurassic) |

These estimates differ greatly. Most fall between the Triassic and the Jurassic, tens of millions of years older than the earliest reliable fossils (about 136–130 Ma) (Fig. 34.1.1). The discrepancy can be understood from two sides:

1. The molecular-clock side: rates of evolution differ greatly among angiosperm lineages (slow in woody lineages, fast in herbaceous ones), and the stem branch leading to the crown group is long, so rate models cannot extrapolate accurately; the choice of fossil calibrations and of prior distributions also strongly affects the result.
2. The fossil side: early angiosperms may have been small, rare plants of disturbed understory or waterside habitats that were unlikely to fossilize; fossils give only minimum ages; and the origin of the crown group may have preceded the time at which angiosperms became ecologically common (when abundant pollen appears).

The present consensus is that the angiosperm crown group originated no later than the Early Cretaceous and very probably existed in the Jurassic, while the **stem group** (the time of separation from extant gymnosperms) is older still and can be traced to the divergence of extant seed plants in the Carboniferous or Permian. Over the long interval from the stem group to the crown group, the ancestors of angiosperms left no identifiable fossils, which is the core of the "abominable mystery" today.

## 34.2. Place of Origin and Possible Ancestors

### 34.2.1. Place of Origin

Two opposing views on the birthplace of angiosperms are presented in Chinese textbooks.

High-latitude origin: Heer, on the basis of fossil floras of the Arctic, concluded that angiosperms first appeared on a northern high-latitude "Arctic continent" and spread southward. Later work showed that Early Cretaceous Arctic floras contain no angiosperms, and the hypothesis has been abandoned.

Low- and middle-latitude origin: Abundant fossils show that angiosperms appeared earlier at low and middle latitudes than at high latitudes: angiosperms already made up about 20% of the late Early Cretaceous flora of North America at a time when they were only just appearing in Canada, and tricolpate pollen also appears first at low and then at high latitudes. More than half of the families of extant angiosperms are concentrated in the tropics and subtropics, and many early-diverging groups (*Amborella* in New Caledonia, *Austrobaileya* in northeastern Australia, Winteraceae and Degeneriaceae in the southwestern Pacific) occur in this region; Bailey, Smith and Takhtajan therefore regarded the southwestern Pacific and Southeast Asia as the "cradle" of early angiosperm diversification, while Zhengyi Wu, starting from the Chinese flora, proposed that southern and southwestern China and the Indochinese peninsula between 20° and 40° N are richest in endemic ancient families and genera. Others, using plate tectonics, argued that angiosperms originated in the tropics of western Gondwana (today's Africa and South America).

Two things must be distinguished: where ancient extant groups are concentrated, and where angiosperms originated. Regions rich in ancient groups may simply be refugia in which relicts survived later climate change, and not necessarily the place of origin. Most authors now think that angiosperms first appeared in the Early Cretaceous in the tropical–subtropical zone of low and middle latitudes (the equatorial belt between northern Gondwana and southern Laurasia) and spread toward the poles in the middle Cretaceous; a more precise place of origin cannot be determined.

### 34.2.2. Monophyletic, Dual and Polyphyletic Origins

| Theory | Content | Proponents | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polyphyletic origin | Angiosperms came from many unrelated groups and developed in parallel | Wieland (1929; derived from Bennettitales, Cordaitales, ginkgos, conifers and cycads); H. H. Hu (1950); Meeuse (at least 4 ancestors) | Inconsistent with the synapomorphies of angiosperms and with molecular evidence |
| Dual (diphyletic) origin | Angiosperms came from two different ancestral groups that developed in parallel | Lam (axial-spored forms from gnetophytes, foliar-spored forms from cycads); Engler (amentiferous forms and polycarpic forms developing in parallel) | Rejected |
| Monophyletic origin | Extant angiosperms descend from a single common ancestor (the "proangiosperm") | Hutchinson, Takhtajan, Cronquist | Confirmed by molecular systematics |

The monophyletic view rests on a series of highly specialized characters shared by angiosperms: sieve tubes and companion cells in the phloem; a fixed relative position of stamens and carpels on the floral axis (stamens outside, carpels inside); stamens that all have four pollen sacs and a distinctive endothecium; the presence of carpels and stigmas; entry of the pollen tube into the embryo sac through a degenerating synergid; and double fertilization with triploid endosperm. It is extremely unlikely that all these characters arose independently two or more times. Molecular systematics consistently supports the monophyly of extant angiosperms ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.1.2)).

### 34.2.3. Close Relatives of Angiosperms: Rise and Fall of the Anthophyte Hypothesis

If angiosperms are monophyletic, what is their sister group? Candidates proposed traditionally include algae, ferns, conifers, gnetophytes, Bennettitales, seed ferns (Caytoniales, Glossopteridales) and *Pentoxylon*, of which the most discussed are Bennettitales, Caytoniales and gnetophytes.

- Bennettitales: their bisexual strobili resemble the flowers of *Magnolia* and *Liriodendron*, and the seeds have almost no endosperm and two fleshy cotyledons; they were once called "proangiosperms". Takhtajan pointed out that the microsporophylls of Bennettitales are whorled and fused at the base, the megasporophyll is reduced to a small axis bearing one terminal orthotropous ovule, and interseminal scales lie among the ovules; it is hard to imagine how this could become a carpel. Their ovules receive pollen through a micropylar tube, not a stigma.
- Caytoniales (*Caytonia*): the ovules are enclosed in recurved cupules with a lip-like opening, and the cupule was once interpreted as an angiosperm "fruit" (Thomas 1925). But it is unisexual, the pollen sacs are fused, the pollen is bisaccate, and the leaf venation differs from that of angiosperms ([7.12.25](/7.12.25#sec-25.3.2)).
- Gnetophytes: they have vessels, net-veined leaves (*Gnetum*), flower-like reproductive structures and a kind of "double fertilization"; on this basis Wettstein (1907) regarded them as the ancestors of angiosperms (the pseudanthial theory).

Cladistic morphological analyses of the 1980s combined these clues into the **anthophyte hypothesis**: Crane (1985) and Doyle and Donoghue (1986) found that Bennettitales, *Pentoxylon*, gnetophytes and angiosperms form a monophyletic group, with flower-like reproductive structures as the synapomorphy; gnetophytes were the closest living relatives of angiosperms (Fig. 34.2.1, left). The hypothesis was for a time widely accepted and stimulated research on the origin of the carpel and of double fertilization.

Around 1999, multigene molecular phylogenetic analyses consistently rejected it: extant gymnosperms (cycads, *Ginkgo*, conifers and gnetophytes) form a monophyletic group, gnetophytes lie within or near the conifers (most nuclear gene data support them as sister to Pinaceae, [7.12.25](/7.12.25#sec-25.3.5)), and angiosperms are sister to all extant gymnosperms (Fig. 34.2.1, right). Vessels of gnetophytes and angiosperms have different origins (those of gnetophytes evolved from tracheids with circular bordered pits, those of angiosperms from scalariform tracheids), and "double fertilization" is not homologous either (the second fertilization in gnetophytes forms no endosperm); the similarities are the result of convergence.

With the anthophyte hypothesis rejected, the sister group of angiosperms must be sought among extinct seed plants. Analyses that include fossils often place Caytoniales, Glossopteridales, Bennettitales and *Pentoxylon* near the angiosperm stem, but with low support. One influential hypothesis holds that the cupule of Caytoniales and Glossopteridales corresponds to the outer integument of the angiosperm ovule: if only one ovule remains in a cupule, the result is an anatropous ovule with two integuments, and the carpel is formed by closure of the leaf-like structure that bears the cupule (Doyle 2006, 2012). Another family of hypotheses starts from developmental genetics: gymnosperms have two *LEAFY* homologs, expressed in male and female strobili respectively, whereas angiosperms retain only the "male" copy; Frohlich and Parker (2000) therefore proposed the "mostly male theory", in which the flower derives mainly from the male strobilus and the ovules arose ectopically on it. None of these hypotheses has yet received decisive fossil support.

Left: the anthophyte hypothesis supported by cladistic morphological analyses of the 1980s (simplified from Doyle and Donoghue 1986): Bennettitales, *Pentoxylon*, gnetophytes and angiosperms form one clade, with flower-like reproductive structures as the synapomorphy (✝ marks extinct groups). Right: the result of molecular phylogenetic analyses: extant gymnosperms are monophyletic, gnetophytes are close to Pinaceae, and angiosperms are sister to extant gymnosperms; the positions of possible extinct relatives (Caytoniales, etc.) on the angiosperm stem are undetermined and drawn with dashed lines](./plots/seed-plant-hypotheses.py)

### 34.2.4. Modern Assessment of the Euanthial and Pseudanthial Theories

The **euanthial theory** and the **pseudanthial theory** ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.4.4)) were the two great schools of twentieth-century angiosperm systematics. In the light of present evidence:

1. The core claim of the pseudanthial theory has been refuted. Gnetophytes are not close relatives of angiosperms; the amentiferous groups (Salicaceae, Fagaceae, Juglandaceae, Betulaceae, Casuarinaceae, etc.) are derived, wind-pollinated groups within the rosids, and their unisexual, achlamydeous flowers, single integument and chalazogamy are all secondary; and a monophyletic origin of angiosperms contradicts the dual origin of the pseudanthial theory.
2. The specific inferences of the euanthial theory are also not fully valid. Bennettitales are not close relatives of angiosperms, and the similarity of their bisexual strobili to flowers is convergent; Magnoliales are not the earliest-diverging angiosperms, and the flowers of the earliest-diverging extant lineages (*Amborella*, Nymphaeales, Austrobaileyales) are small to medium in size with variable numbers of parts, so the "large magnolia-type flower" is secondary.
3. The basic concept of the euanthial theory, that the flower is a bisexual, determinate short shoot bearing sterile perianth parts and fertile sporophylls, agrees with modern morphology and developmental genetics. Floral organ identity is determined by combinations of MADS-box genes (the **ABC model of flower development**), and homologs of these genes are already expressed in male and female organs of gymnosperm strobili ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.1)), so the flower can be understood as a modified strobilus. In the earliest-diverging angiosperms, tepals grade into stamens and the expression boundaries of B- and C-class genes are gradually attenuated (the fading borders model), which indicates that the typical four-whorled flower was "sharpened" during evolution.

On the other hand, "false flowers" (pseudanthia) do exist as a morphological phenomenon, but all arose secondarily within angiosperms: the capitulum of Asteraceae, the cyathium of *Euphorbia*, the spadix of Araceae and the "flower" of Hydatellaceae are all inflorescences of many reduced flowers that function as a single flower. Takhtajan's theory of paedomorphosis to explain the origin of the flower, in which the flower results from strong shortening of the reproductive short shoot of a seed fern with juvenile characters retained to maturity, is likewise a stimulating speculation but also lacks direct evidence.

### 34.2.5. Origin of the Monocots

The traditional view is that dicots are more primitive than monocots, and that monocots are a monophyletic group derived from extinct, most primitive herbaceous dicots (Hutchinson, Takhtajan, Cronquist, Tamura). Two main views have been offered on which dicots these were:

| Theory | Evidence | Proponents |
|---|---|---|
| Aquatic Nymphaeales-like origin (the Cabombaceae–Alismatales origin) | Most monocots have monosulcate pollen, like Magnoliales and Nymphaeales and unlike the tricolpate, diffuse-porous Ranunculales; monocot vessels may have originated independently, so ancestors should be sought among vessel-less groups with monosulcate pollen; the scattered vascular bundles and lack of cambium in monocots can be explained as adaptations to an aquatic habit | Takhtajan (via Cabombaceae to Alismatales); Cronquist (Alismatidae as a side branch near the base of Liliopsida) |
| Terrestrial Ranunculales-like origin (the Ranunculales–Liliales origin) | Polycarpy is a primitive angiosperm character; the most primitive monocots (Butomaceae, Alismataceae, Potamogetonaceae) have free carpels; Butomaceae and Alismataceae have advanced vessels in the root metaxylem, which does not support an aquatic origin; Ranunculaceae and Liliales are similar in alkaloids, steroids, triterpenes, cyanogenic glycosides and fatty acids | Hutchinson (monocots from Ranunculales); Tamura (1974); chemical evidence of C. R. Yang and J. Zhou (1978) |

Molecular systematics has given the problem a new formulation. Monocots are a strongly supported monophyletic group, but they do not come from any extant "dicot" group: in the APG IV backbone, monocots are one of five branches of Mesangiospermae, diverging at nearly the same time as the magnoliids, Chloranthales, Ceratophyllales and eudicots (chloroplast data mostly support them as sister to Ceratophyllales + eudicots). Hence neither "monocots arose from Nymphaeales" nor "monocots arose from Ranunculales" holds: Nymphaeales belong to the earlier-diverging ANA grade, and Ranunculales are the basal group of the eudicots, so both are distant relatives of monocots. The earliest-diverging monocots are Acorales, followed by Alismatales ([7.12.36](/7.12.36)); the many aquatic groups in Alismatales and Early Cretaceous fossils of Araceae suggest that an aquatic or wetland habit was common in the early evolution of monocots, which agrees with some inferences of the aquatic origin theory. The earliest reliable monocot fossils are Early Cretaceous pollen and Araceae; the Late Triassic *Sanmiguelia* was once thought to be a monocot (its leaves resemble those of *Veratrum*) but is no longer accepted.

## 34.3. The Early Radiation of Angiosperms and the "First Flower"

### 34.3.1. Discovery of the ANA Grade

Before molecular systematics there were many candidates for the "most primitive" angiosperms: Cronquist and Takhtajan thought it was Magnoliales (or Winteraceae), Taylor and Hickey (1992) thought Chloranthaceae, the morphological analysis of Donoghue and Doyle (1989) found Magnoliales, and others proposed a group of "paleoherbs" (Piperales, Aristolochiaceae, Nymphaeales and monocots). The *rbcL* tree of Chase et al. (1993) placed *Ceratophyllum* at the base of all angiosperms, which was later shown to be an artifact of **long-branch attraction**.

In 1999, several research groups, using different genes and methods (combinations of chloroplast, mitochondrial and nuclear genes, and mutual rooting of phytochrome gene duplicates), independently obtained the same result: *Amborella* is sister to all other angiosperms, followed by Nymphaeales, and then Austrobaileyales (Mathews and Donoghue 1999; Parkinson et al. 1999; Qiu et al. 1999; Soltis et al. 1999). Qiu et al. called these groups ANITA, from the initials of Amborellaceae, Nymphaeales, Illiciaceae, Trimeniaceae and Austrobaileyaceae; after Illiciaceae, Trimeniaceae and Austrobaileyaceae were merged into Austrobaileyales, the name became ANA (Soltis et al. 2005; APG).

**Definition 34.3.1 (ANA Grade)** The paraphyletic group formed by the three lineages that diverged first, in succession, among angiosperms, namely Amborellales (A), Nymphaeales (N) and Austrobaileyales (A), is called the **ANA grade**, also known as the basal angiosperms. The remaining angiosperms form the monophyletic **Mesangiospermae**.

A "grade" is a set of lineages that branch off successively from the main stem and do not themselves form a clade. The three orders of the ANA grade together comprise only about 200 species, less than one thousandth of the angiosperms, whereas Mesangiospermae has about 300,000 species. Two topologies exist for ANA: *Amborella* alone is sister to all other angiosperms (most analyses, including most nuclear data), or *Amborella* + Nymphaeales together form the sister group (some chloroplast and mitochondrial data). This does not change the composition of ANA.

The poverty of species in the ANA lineages, and the divergence of all major branches of Mesangiospermae within a very short time, suggest that the "explosive" diversification of angiosperms occurred after their origin, at the base of Mesangiospermae. Factors proposed to explain this radiation include coevolution with pollinating insects; a rapid life cycle and reproduction (fast pollen tube growth, small ovules, and endosperm formed only after fertilization, which saves investment); higher photosynthetic and transpiration capacity from increased leaf vein density; and repeated **whole-genome duplications**. These factors probably acted together, rather than any single "key innovation" being responsible.

### 34.3.2. The *Amborella* Genome

In 2013 the Amborella Genome Project published the genome of *Amborella*. Because *Amborella* is sister to all other angiosperms, its genome acts like an "outgroup" and can be used to infer the genome of the common ancestor of all extant angiosperms:

- The genome is about 870 Mbp and confirms an ancient whole-genome duplication that predates all extant angiosperms (already inferred from transcriptomes by Jiao et al. 2011); the *Amborella* lineage has not undergone polyploidization since, so its gene order is conserved and suitable as a reference;
- Comparison with it confirmed the ancient hexaploidization shared by core eudicots (two successive duplications) and allowed reconstruction of the gene order of the core eudicot ancestor;
- The common ancestor of angiosperms is inferred to have had at least about 14,000 genes, of which more than a thousand gene lineages are new in angiosperms, including many genes related to flowering;
- The mitochondrial genome is exceptionally large (about 3.9 Mbp) and has acquired large segments of mitochondrial DNA from green algae, mosses and other angiosperms by horizontal gene transfer.

Resequencing of 12 individuals showed that wild *Amborella* populations fall into four genetic clusters with clear geographic structure, with genetic diversity comparable to that of poplars.

### 34.3.3. Reconstruction of the Ancestral Flower

Ancestral-state reconstruction of floral characters on a phylogenetic tree allows inference of the flower of the common ancestor of extant angiosperms. The synthesis of Soltis et al. (2018) is: perianth undifferentiated (tepals, no sepals and petals), with arrangement (spiral or whorled) uncertain; stamens numerous, with poorly differentiated filaments and anthers (laminar or nearly so); carpels free and ascidiate (closed from a cup-shaped primordium), sealed by secretion rather than tissue fusion, each with one ovule; pollen monosulcate; flowers small to medium in size. The reconstruction of Sauquet et al. (2017), based on 792 extant angiosperms and 27 floral characters, is more specific: the ancestral flower was bisexual and radially symmetric; tepals 10 or more, undifferentiated, in 3 or more whorls of 3; stamens 10 or more, also whorled in threes; carpels 5 or more, free and spirally arranged (Fig. 34.3.1). The conclusion of "whorled" arrangement was unexpected, since the traditional view and *Amborella* and *Austrobaileya* both suggest that spiral arrangement is primitive; it also implies that the trimerous, two-whorled perianth of monocots may be inherited directly from the ancestor, and that the pentamerous flower of eudicots evolved later.

![Reconstruction of the ancestral flower of extant angiosperms (redrawn as a floral diagram after Sauquet et al. 2017): bisexual, radially symmetric, with undifferentiated tepals and stamens each in several whorls of three, enclosing five or more free carpels arranged in a spiral. On the right, for comparison, the male and female flowers of *Amborella*: tepals spiral and undifferentiated; the male flower has a dozen or more laminar stamens, the female flower has 4–6 free carpels and 1–3 staminodes](./plots/ancestral-flower.py)

There are also two hypotheses on the habit and habitat of early angiosperms. The "dark and disturbed" hypothesis (Feild et al. 2004), based on the shade-tolerant physiology of *Amborella*, *Austrobaileya*, Chloranthaceae and others and on their vessel-less or primitive-vessel wood, holds that the earliest angiosperms were small shrubs of the moist, shaded, frequently disturbed understory. The "wet and wild" hypothesis, based on the early divergence of Nymphaeales and on aquatic fossils such as *Archaefructus* and *Montsechia*, holds that early angiosperms were mostly aquatic or wetland herbs. The two are not completely contradictory: early angiosperms may already have diversified in both understory and aquatic habitats.

## 34.4. The ANA Grade

The three orders of the ANA grade differ greatly in form (trees, shrubs, lianas and aquatic herbs), but retain some ancestral angiosperm characters: xylem without vessels or with only primitive vessels (with remnants of pit membranes in the end walls and scalariform perforation plates); free, ascidiate carpels sealed by secretion; monosulcate pollen or pollen derived from it; and tepals often spirally arranged and grading into bracts or stamens. In Nymphaeales and Austrobaileyales the embryo sac has only 4 cells and 4 nuclei (1 egg, 2 synergids and a mononucleate central cell), and double fertilization yields diploid endosperm, unlike the 7-celled, 8-nucleate *Polygonum*-type embryo sac and triploid endosperm of the great majority of angiosperms ([7.12.28](/7.12.28#sec-28.6.3), [7.12.30](/7.12.30#sec-30.2.6)); *Amborella* has an 8-celled, 9-nucleate embryo sac and triploid endosperm. Which type of embryo sac and endosperm represents the ancestral state in ANA cannot yet be determined.

### 34.4.1. Amborellales: Amborellaceae

Amborellales comprises a single family, genus and species: *Amborella trichopoda* (Amborellaceae). The Chinese name, "oil-free camphor", is relative to Lauraceae (camphor family): its parenchyma contains no oil cells, which is one of the differences from Laurales and the magnoliids. Cronquist placed it in Laurales, and Engler's system in Magnoliales.

*Amborella* is an evergreen shrub or small tree endemic to New Caledonia, growing in the understory of moist montane rainforest; fewer than 20 wild populations are known. Diagnostic characters:

- Xylem has only tracheids and no vessels (the frequently repeated statement that the xylem "has no vascular bundles" is wrong; it should read "has no vessels");
- Leaves simple, alternate, pinnately veined, with chloranthoid teeth on the margin (the tooth tip has a gland, possibly a hydathode that releases root-pressure water);
- Dioecious; flowers small, 3–5 mm across, in axillary small panicles; tepals spiral and not differentiated into sepals and petals, subtended by spirally arranged bracts into which they grade;
- The male flower has 9–11 tepals and 12–21 stamens; the stamens are laminar, with filament and anther hard to distinguish;
- The female flower has 7–8 tepals, 1–3 staminodes and 4–6 free, ascidiate carpels, each with one ovule; the staminodes in the female flower indicate that the ancestor had bisexual flowers;
- Each carpel develops into a small red drupe, and together they form a drupaceous aggregate fruit.

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{9\text{–}11} A_{12\text{–}21}$; ♀ $\ast P_{7\text{–}8} A_{0\text{–}3} \underline{G}_{4\text{–}6}$ (the $A$ of the female flower are staminodes).

The flower of *Amborella* rewards visitors only with pollen, and is both wind- and insect-pollinated (small beetles, flies, etc.). Its pollen tube grows more slowly than in any other angiosperm, but still far faster than in gymnosperms. The value of *Amborella* lies not in being "primitive" (it has its own derived features, such as dioecy) but in being the only lineage opposed to all other angiosperms, and thus a reference for comparative study of ancestral angiosperm characters.

### 34.4.2. Nymphaeales

Nymphaeales are aquatic herbs with rhizomes or tubers, scattered vascular bundles in the stem (as in monocots), no vessels in the stem, floating leaves, and well-developed aerenchyma. The embryo sac is 4-celled and 4-nucleate, the endosperm is diploid and scanty, and the seed reserves are stored mainly in the perisperm. Nymphaeales in APG IV include 3 families and about 90 species: Hydatellaceae, Cabombaceae and Nymphaeaceae. Changes in circumscription: traditional Nymphaeales also included Nelumbonaceae and Ceratophyllaceae (Cronquist system), whereas Engler's system placed Nymphaeaceae (including *Nelumbo*) and Ceratophyllaceae in Ranunculales; APG moves *Nelumbo* to Proteales among the eudicots ([7.12.40](/7.12.40)), makes *Ceratophyllum* a separate order, Ceratophyllales (§34.5), and moves Hydatellaceae from the monocots into Nymphaeales.

### 34.4.3. Nymphaeaceae

Nymphaeaceae are aquatic herbs with rhizomes. The leaves have long petioles and are cordate, hastate to peltate, floating or emergent. The flowers are large and solitary, often floating or emergent; sepals 4–6 (rarely 14), sometimes petaloid; petals 8 to many, often grading inward into stamens (rarely absent); stamens numerous; pollen monosulcate or with an annular aperture; the gynoecium consists of 3 to many carpels fused into a multilocular ovary, superior to inferior, with a discoid stigma and numerous ovules per locule borne on the septa (laminar placentation). The fruit is berry-like, spongy and irregularly dehiscent; seeds often have an aril, with both perisperm and endosperm. Chromosome number $x = 12$–$29$.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{4\text{–}6} C_{8\text{–}\infty} A_{\infty} G_{(3\text{–}\infty)}$ (ovary position varies within the family, so no bar is added).

The family has 5 genera and about 70 species, widespread in fresh water from the tropics to the temperate zone; China has about 3 genera and 8 species, found throughout the country.

- *Nymphaea*: leaf base deeply cordate, leaf surface smooth; ovary half-inferior. *N. tetragona* has white flowers and occurs throughout China; cultivated water lilies come in white, yellow, red and blue-violet, and tropical species include night-bloomers such as *N. nouchali* (blue water lily).
- *Euryale*: only *E. ferox* (gorgon plant, fox nut). Leaves large, peltate and round, spiny on the surface and veins; ovary inferior; the fruit is berry-like and spongy, enclosed in the spiny persistent calyx and shaped like a chicken's head; the seeds (qianshi, "euryale seeds") have starchy endosperm and are used as food and medicine. The flowers are often cleistogamous underwater.
- *Nuphar*: *N. pumila* has long-ovate leaves with a deeply cordate base; sepals 5, yellow and petaloid, petals small and oblong; ovary superior. It occurs in northern to eastern China.
- *Victoria*: leaves round, up to 2–3 m in diameter, with upturned margins and stout, spiny veins beneath that can bear considerable weight; flowers large, opening at night. Native to the Amazon basin of South America and cultivated in China; the common species are the Amazon water lily (*V. amazonica*, formerly *V. regia*) and *V. cruziana*.
- *Barclaya*: from Southeast Asia, with connate perianth, traditionally a separate family Barclayaceae; the former *Ondinea* has been merged into *Nymphaea*.

Molecular phylogenies show that *Victoria* and *Euryale* are sister groups, both nested within a broadly defined *Nymphaea*, which is thus paraphyletic. The flower of *Victoria* is a classic example of heat production used to attract beetles: it opens white on the first evening in the female phase (protogynous, the pistil maturing first), and the temperature in the floral chamber is clearly above ambient and releases a fragrance that attracts beetles; the beetles are trapped overnight in the closed flower and feed on floral appendages; on the second evening the flower turns pink or purple-red and enters the male phase, the anthers shed pollen, and the beetles carry pollen to another newly opened white flower. The very large numbers of floral parts in *Nymphaea* and *Victoria* are secondary increases associated with beetle pollination, while the reduction of floral parts in *Euryale* is associated with cleistogamy.

In Nymphaeaceae there is a continuous transition among tepals, petals and stamens: green sepals outside, then petals, then petaloid staminodes bearing partial anthers, and finally normal stamens (Fig. 34.4.1). This is a classic example of the fading borders model: B- and C-class genes are expressed in overlapping, gradually attenuated domains in adjacent organs, producing intermediate organs with characters of both kinds ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.1.2)).

### 34.4.4. Cabombaceae and Hydatellaceae

Cabombaceae are aquatic herbs. Floating leaves are peltate; submerged leaves (*Cabomba*) are palmately finely divided. Flowers small and solitary, with a trimerous perianth in two whorls ($P_{3+3}$), 3–36 stamens, 2–18 free carpels, ovary superior with 1–3 ovules per carpel; the fruit is leathery, follicle-like or nut-like. Floral formula: $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3\text{–}36} \underline{G}_{2\text{–}18}$. Two genera, 6 species.

- *Brasenia*: *B. schreberi* (watershield) has elliptic peltate leaves and a gelatinous sheath on young stems and the undersides of leaves; it is a famous vegetable of the Jiangnan region (for example West Lake chun cai). Flowers dark purple, stamens numerous, wind-pollinated.
- *Cabomba*: *C. caroliniana* (fanwort) is native to the Americas, with palmately finely divided submerged leaves; it is a common aquarium plant and has become an invasive species in the lower Yangtze region of China. Flowers white or yellow, insect-pollinated.

Cabombaceae are often treated as a subfamily of Nymphaeaceae. Takhtajan once held that monocots originated through an ancestor of the Cabombaceae type (§34.2.5).

Hydatellaceae consist only of *Trithuria* (including the former *Hydatella*), about 12 species from Australia, New Zealand and India: tiny aquatic or wetland annual herbs with linear, tufted leaves that look very much like grasses or Eriocaulaceae. Their "flower" is a pseudanthium formed by a cluster of naked unisexual flowers (a male flower has a single stamen, a female flower a single carpel) enclosed by bracts. Traditionally they were placed among the monocots (Cronquist's Hydatellales in Commelinidae, later near Centrolepidaceae); the DNA analysis of Saarela et al. (2007) found that they belong in Nymphaeales, as sister to Nymphaeaceae + Cabombaceae. The structure of their embryo sac, seed and perisperm was then confirmed to agree with Nymphaeales.

### 34.4.5. Removal of *Nelumbo*

*Nelumbo nucifera* (sacred lotus) and water lilies are both aquatic plants with rhizomes, peltate or cordate leaves, and large flowers with many parts, and were traditionally placed together in Nymphaeales. But the lotus has tricolpate pollen, free carpels embedded in a spongy receptacle, seeds without endosperm, and latex, all very different from Nymphaeaceae; molecular evidence places it in Proteales among the eudicots, close to Platanaceae and Proteaceae ([7.12.40](/7.12.40)). The similarity between lotus and water lilies is the result of convergent evolution in the aquatic environment ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.6.3)).

### 34.4.6. Austrobaileyales

Austrobaileyales are woody plants (trees, shrubs or woody lianas) with oil cells containing volatile oils; vessels are primitive, with many oblique scalariform perforation plates; tepals numerous and spirally arranged, often grading from bracts; stamens numerous, often laminar or poorly differentiated; carpels free and ascidiate; embryo sac 4-celled and 4-nucleate, endosperm diploid. APG IV includes 3 families:

- Austrobaileyaceae: only *Austrobaileya scandens*, from tropical rainforest in northeastern Queensland, Australia, a large woody liana with opposite leaves; flowers large, solitary, with a foul smell, fly-pollinated; stamens laminar and petaloid, the inner ones staminodes; carpels many, fruit berry-like. Cronquist placed it in Magnoliales.
- Trimeniaceae: *Trimenia*, about 5 species, from Southeast Asia to Australia and Pacific islands; shrubs or lianas; flowers small, bisexual or unisexual, tepals spiral, grading into bracts and early deciduous; stamens numerous, a single carpel, drupe. Cronquist placed it in Laurales.
- Schisandraceae (in the broad sense, including Illiciaceae): treated in detail below.

### 34.4.7. Schisandraceae

Schisandraceae are woody plants or woody lianas, mostly evergreen, with the whole plant fragrant when crushed (volatile oil cells). Leaves simple, alternate, often with translucent glandular dots, without stipules. Flowers solitary in the leaf axils, bisexual or unisexual; tepals 5 to many, spirally arranged in several whorls, often grading from bract-like outside to petal-like inside; stamens numerous, spiral or in one whorl, filaments and anthers poorly differentiated, sometimes fused; carpels numerous, free, spirally arranged (*Schisandra*, *Kadsura*) or in one whorl (*Illicium*), ovary superior, 1–5 ovules per carpel. The fruit is an aggregate of follicles (*Illicium*) or an aggregate of berries (*Schisandra*, *Kadsura*). Pollen tricolpate or derived from tricolpate (convergent with the tricolpate pollen of eudicots).

Floral formula: *Illicium* $\ast P_{\infty} A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{5\text{–}20}$ (carpels in one whorl); *Schisandra* ♂ $\ast P_{5\text{–}12} A_{4\text{–}60}$, ♀ $\ast P_{5\text{–}12} \underline{G}_{\infty}$.

The family has 3 genera and about 80 species, mainly in East and Southeast Asia, with some in southeastern North America and Mexico; China has 3 genera and about 55 species.

- *Illicium*: trees or shrubs; flowers bisexual, carpels in one whorl, fruit a star-shaped aggregate of follicles, each follicle with one seed. Star anise (*I. verum*) occurs in South and Southwest China; its fruit is a common spice and the main source of shikimic acid (the raw material for the anti-influenza drug oseltamivir). Related species such as *I. lanceolatum* and wild star anises have highly toxic fruits (containing anisatin and related toxins) that resemble star anise: the follicles of star anise usually number 8, with blunt or slightly pointed, straight tips, whereas toxic species mostly have 10–14 follicles with long, curved tips and a different aroma. Use of toxic species in place of star anise causes poisoning.
- *Schisandra*: woody lianas with unisexual flowers, monoecious or dioecious; in the female flower the receptacle elongates in fruit and the aggregate of berries forms a long spike. *S. chinensis* (northern schisandra) occurs in Northeast and North China and *S. sphenanthera* in the Yangtze valley; the fruits of both are used medicinally, and their taste combines sour, sweet, bitter, pungent and salty, hence the Chinese name "five-flavor fruit".
- *Kadsura*: woody lianas with unisexual flowers; the receptacle does not elongate in fruit and the aggregate of berries is nearly globose. *K. longipedunculata* occurs south of the Yangtze; *K. coccinea* has a large edible aggregate fruit.

Changes in circumscription: Engler's system placed Illiciaceae and Schisandraceae in Magnoliales, and Bentham and Hooker included them in Magnoliaceae; Cronquist made them a separate order Illiciales in Magnoliidae; APG merges them into a broadly defined Schisandraceae in Austrobaileyales (ANA grade). Their similarity to Magnoliaceae (woody habit, oil cells, numerous free, spirally arranged floral parts) consists of ancestral characters and does not indicate close relationship. The commonly stated "Schisandraceae split off from Magnoliaceae" refers to the early treatments of Bentham and Hooker and others.

## 34.5. Ceratophyllales

Ceratophyllales comprise only the genus *Ceratophyllum* (hornwort) in the family Ceratophyllaceae, with about 6 species (older counts reached 30), worldwide in distribution. Common species in China include *C. demersum* and *C. platyacanthum* subsp. *oryzetorum*.

*Ceratophyllum* is a perennial submerged herb that is rootless throughout life (the embryo does not even produce a radicle) and is anchored in bottom mud by the base of branched stems or specialized colorless branches. Leaves are whorled and one to four times dichotomously divided into linear segments with fine teeth on the margin; the stem has only a single reduced central vascular bundle and no vessels. Flowers are unisexual, the plants monoecious, the flowers solitary in the leaf axils and minute; the perianth consists of 8–12 linear, toothed, bract-like segments (often interpreted as an involucre or a perianth); the male flower has 10–20 stamens with very short filaments and a connective extended into a terminal appendage; the female flower has a single carpel, with a superior, one-locular ovary containing one orthotropous, pendulous ovule. Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{8\text{–}12} A_{10\text{–}20}$; ♀ $\ast P_{8\text{–}12} \underline{G}_{1:1:1}$.

*Ceratophyllum* is pollinated underwater: mature stamens detach from the plant and float to the surface, the anthers dehisce, and the pollen sinks slowly through the water onto the elongated stigmas of female flowers (hydrophily, [7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.3)). The fruit is a nut, often with 2 to several spiny projections (hence "spiny" in epithets such as *platyacanthum*; the generic name, "horned leaf", refers to the leaf segments); the seed has no endosperm, and the embryo has a pair of cotyledons and a well-developed plumule. *Ceratophyllum* is a common aquarium plant, fodder plant and water-purifying plant, and also a material for studying aquatic adaptation ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)). The earliest fruit fossils of *Ceratophyllum* that are reasonably secure come from the Late Cretaceous (Campanian) of North America (northern Mexico, with further records from southern Alberta); Early Cretaceous spiny fruit fossils and *Montsechia* may both be related to it.

The systematic position of *Ceratophyllum* is among the most difficult problems in angiosperm phylogeny. Its highly specialized morphology (submerged, rootless, flowers extremely reduced) provides almost no characters comparable with other groups, and in molecular data its long branch readily induces long-branch attraction. The conclusions of the various authors are as follows:

| System | Position of *Ceratophyllum* |
|---|---|
| Engler (1964) | Ranunculales |
| Cronquist (1981) | Nymphaeales, regarded as a close ancestral relative of monocots (flowers solitary, trimerous, vascular bundles scattered) |
| Chase et al. 1993 (*rbcL*) | Sister to all angiosperms (later shown to be long-branch attraction) |
| Multigene analyses of the late 1990s to early 2000s | Sister to monocots (moderate support) |
| Most chloroplast genome and multigene analyses; APG III, APG IV | Sister to eudicots |
| Some nuclear gene analyses | Sister to Chloranthales, or sister to monocots |

A genome analysis of *Ceratophyllum* published in 2020 supports it as sister to eudicots, but conflicts remain among genes, possibly related to the rapid early divergence of Mesangiospermae and incomplete lineage sorting. This course follows APG IV in treating Ceratophyllales as sister to eudicots; it is given its own order, and placed in this chapter, because it shares almost no morphological characters with eudicots.

## Questions

1. Which problems of the "abominable mystery" remain unsolved today? Why are molecular-clock estimates of the age of angiosperms often tens of millions of years older than the earliest fossils?

   **Answer** The time of origin of angiosperms is now broadly fixed as no later than the Early Cretaceous (the crown group very probably existed in the Jurassic), and several explanations exist for their rapid Cretaceous diversification; still unresolved are the sister group of angiosperms and the extinct relatives on the stem, and the evolutionary history over the long interval from stem group to crown group. The gap between molecular clocks and fossils has two sources: fossils give only a minimum age, and early angiosperms may have been small and rare and have lived in habitats unfavorable to fossilization; molecular clocks are affected by rate differences among lineages (fast in herbs, slow in woody plants), long stem branches and the choice of calibrations, and often overestimate ages.

2. What was the basis of the anthophyte hypothesis? How did molecular evidence refute it? What does its failure imply for the euanthial theory?

   **Answer** The basis was cladistic morphological analysis: Bennettitales, *Pentoxylon*, gnetophytes and angiosperms share flower-like reproductive structures, a thin megaspore wall and other characters, and gnetophytes also have vessels and "double fertilization", so they were grouped as a monophyletic group. Multigene molecular analyses consistently show that extant gymnosperms are monophyletic, gnetophytes are close to the conifers (Pinaceae), and angiosperms are sister to extant gymnosperms; the vessels and double fertilization of gnetophytes and angiosperms are not homologous. Flower-like structures therefore arose several times independently within seed plants. The specific inference of the euanthial theory that the flower derives from the bisexual strobilus of Bennettitales thus loses its basis, but the basic concept that the flower is a bisexual modified strobilus (short shoot) remains valid.

3. In Cronquist's system the lotus and water lilies belong to the same order, Nymphaeales, but in APG IV they are far apart. List the morphological and anatomical evidence that distinguishes them.

   **Answer** Lotus (*Nelumbo*): pollen tricolpate (a eudicot character), carpels free and each embedded in the obconical spongy receptacle (the seed head), fruit a nut, seeds without endosperm, latex present, leaves peltate and emergent. Nymphaeaceae: pollen monosulcate or with an annular aperture, carpels fused into a multilocular ovary with laminar placentation, fruit spongy and berry-like, seeds with perisperm, embryo sac 4-celled and 4-nucleate with diploid endosperm, perianth grading into stamens. Molecular evidence places the lotus in Proteales, and the similarity of their aquatic forms is convergent.

4. Why is the ANA grade a "grade" and not a group? What is the value of *Amborella* in the study of angiosperm evolution?

   **Answer** Amborellales, Nymphaeales and Austrobaileyales branch off in succession from the main stem of angiosperms; their common ancestor is also the ancestor of all angiosperms, so the three together do not include all descendants of this ancestor (Mesangiospermae is excluded) and form a paraphyletic group, which can only be called a "grade". *Amborella* is the only lineage opposed to all other angiosperms, so comparing it with the others allows inference of the characters of the common ancestor of extant angiosperms: for example its vessel-less xylem, undifferentiated spiral perianth, laminar stamens and ascidiate carpels; its genome has not undergone recent polyploidization and can be used to infer the genome of the angiosperm ancestor and later whole-genome duplication events.

5. How can star anise be distinguished from toxic *Illicium lanceolatum*? Why is *Illicium* placed in Schisandraceae and Austrobaileyales in APG IV, and not in Magnoliales?

   **Answer** The aggregate fruit of star anise usually consists of 8 follicles with blunt or slightly pointed, straight tips and a sweet aroma; toxic species such as *I. lanceolatum* mostly have 10–14 follicles that are smaller, with long, curved tips and a different smell. In molecular phylogenies *Illicium*, *Schisandra* and *Kadsura* form a clade that shares oil cells, spiral tepals grading into bracts, a 4-celled, 4-nucleate embryo sac with diploid endosperm and tricolpate-derived pollen, and lies in the ANA grade together with Austrobaileyaceae and Trimeniaceae; the woody habit and numerous free floral parts that it shares with Magnoliaceae are ancestral characters and do not show close relationship.
