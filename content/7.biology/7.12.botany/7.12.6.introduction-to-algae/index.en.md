In traditional botany the "lower plants" comprise the algae, fungi and lichens, and among them the **algae** are the largest and most diverse group. Photosynthetic algae live everywhere, from the marine prochlorophyte *Prochlorococcus*, less than 1 µm across, to giant kelp tens of meters long, and from hot springs and snowfields to the sea floor below 100 m. There are two reasons for studying algae in botany. First, algae are the main primary producers of aquatic ecosystems and are also food, industrial raw materials and indicators of water quality, so their form, life history and classification are the basis for understanding and using them. Second, the algae preserve the successive stages by which the plant body evolved from single cells to multicellular organisms, from undifferentiated to differentiated tissues, from isogamy to oogamy, and from no alternation of generations to alternation of generations. They are the frame of reference for understanding why the **embryophytes** are the way they are.

This chapter first treats the questions common to all algae: general features and thallus types (Section 6.1), the cellular characters on which the divisions are based and their meaning in the framework of endosymbiosis (Section 6.2), the traditional divisions compared with the current classification (Section 6.3), and the modes of reproduction and the life-cycle types of algae (Section 6.4, used in all the following chapters). Finally we treat the prokaryotic cyanobacteria (Section 6.5). The eukaryotic algae belong to several supergroups according to phylogeny and are treated in turn in [7.12.7](/7.12.7) (red algae and glaucophytes), [7.12.8](/7.12.8) (heterokont algae), [7.12.9](/7.12.9) (dinoflagellates, cryptophytes, haptophytes and euglenids, together with the ecology and economic importance of algae), [7.12.13](/7.12.13) (green algae) and [7.12.14](/7.12.14) (streptophyte algae and the origin of land plants). The cytological and molecular details of each group were introduced in [7.11.20](/7.11.20) in microbiology, and the cell structure, photosynthesis and nitrogen fixation of cyanobacteria are in [7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.4) and [7.11.6](/7.11.6), to which we only link here. This chapter and the next three take the botanical viewpoint: morphology, representative genera, complete life histories, the comparison of old and new classification systems, and economic importance.

## 6.1. General Features of the Algae

### 6.1.1. The Thallus

Algae have no differentiation into root, stem and leaf, and their plant bodies are collectively called thalli.

**Definition 6.1.1 (Thallus)** A plant body without differentiation into root, stem and leaf is called a **thallus**. The vegetative bodies of algae, fungi and lichens, and the gametophytes of bryophytes and ferns, are all thalli. Algae are autotrophic, photosynthetic thallophytes:

- The plant body is a thallus, from a few micrometers to tens of meters in size and from a single cell to tissue differentiation in structure, but without true roots, stems or leaves.
- The reproductive organs are mostly unicellular. In a few advanced algae they are multicellular, but every cell directly forms spores or gametes, and there is no protective layer of sterile cells outside.
- The zygote does not develop into a multicellular embryo within the parent.

The last two features distinguish algae from embryophytes: the archegonia and antheridia of bryophytes have a layer of sterile jacket cells, and the zygote develops into an embryo inside the archegonium ([7.12.15](/7.12.15)). A few algae are heterotrophic or temporarily heterotrophic (colorless euglenids, dinoflagellates and chrysophytes), but they can be distinguished from fungi by cell structure and storage products. Many algae are mixotrophic: they practice **mixotrophy**, photosynthesizing and also ingesting particles or absorbing dissolved organic matter ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.1.2)).

"Algae" is the name of a group defined by way of life and not a taxon. The prokaryotic cyanobacteria belong to the Bacteria, and the eukaryotic algae belong to at least five supergroups of the eukaryotic tree, most of whose ancestors acquired plastids by engulfing other algae (Section 6.2.6). Algae are therefore a **polyphyletic group**, and the word "plant" in the traditional term "algal plants" is only a convention. In the current classification only the glaucophytes, red algae and green algae belong to the same clade as land plants, the **Archaeplastida** ([7.12.1](/7.12.1)).

Algae are very widely distributed. Most live in fresh water (freshwater algae), sea water (seaweeds) or brackish water, as plankton, attached or epiphytic forms. On moist soil there are terrestrial algae, and on moist bark and walls there are subaerial algae. A few are parasitic in plants, such as *Cephaleuros virescens*, which forms algal spots in the leaves and twigs of camellia and lychee, and *Chlorella* lives symbiotically inside cells of *Paramecium bursaria*. Some cyanobacteria grow in hot springs above 70 °C, snow algae live on polar and alpine snowfields, and red and brown algae can grow on the sea floor deeper than 100 m. Algae form lichens with fungi, and live symbiotically with corals, sponges, ferns (*Azolla*) and cycads. Algae have modest nutritional requirements and are often the first colonizers of fresh substrates formed after volcanic eruptions and floods. By the estimate of Guiry (2012), there are more than 70,000 species of algae, of which more than 40,000 have been described.

### 6.1.2. Morphological Types of the Algal Body

Algal body forms have evolved independently in the same several types in different divisions (parallel evolution), which in itself shows that morphology cannot be the main basis for delimiting divisions (Section 6.2).

| Type | Structure | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Unicellular, motile | Flagellate, swimming throughout life | *Chlamydomonas*, *Euglena*, *Peridinium*, *Cryptomonas* |
| Unicellular, non-motile | No flagella, planktonic or attached | *Chlorella*, *Cyclotella*, *Chroococcus* |
| Coenobium (definite colony) | Fixed number and arrangement of cells, often with a common gelatinous envelope | *Gonium*, *Volvox*, *Pediastrum*, *Merismopedia* |
| Indefinite colony | Cells embedded in mucilage, of variable number and shape | *Microcystis*, palmelloid stages of *Euglena* and *Chlamydomonas* |
| Filament | Cells joined in single or multiple rows, unbranched or branched; those with creeping and erect branches are called heterotrichous | *Oscillatoria*, *Ulothrix*, *Cladophora*, *Ectocarpus* |
| Siphonous (coenocytic) tube | A single undivided multinucleate cell, often growing into a macroscopic branched tube | *Vaucheria*, *Botrydium*, *Codium* |
| Membranous (foliose) thallus | A thin sheet one or two cells thick | laver (*Porphyra*), sea lettuce (*Ulva*) |
| Pseudoparenchymatous thallus | Many filaments tightly interwoven and glued into a massive or branched body that looks like tissue | Most red algae; cord algae among the brown algae |
| Parenchymatous thallus | Cells divide in three directions to form true tissue, with differentiation of epidermis, cortex and medulla | kelp (*Laminaria*), *Fucus*, *Sargassum* |

Pseudoparenchyma differs from parenchyma in mode of development. The former consists of filaments that each grow from their own apex, so the filaments are not products of division of the same cell and merely adhere to each other (in red algae adjacent filaments are also joined by secondary pit connections, [7.12.7](/7.12.7)). The latter arises from meristematic cells dividing in all directions, as in the tissues of land plants. True parenchyma occurs among algae mainly in brown algae, and otherwise only in a few green algae (*Ulva*, the nodes of stoneworts) and a few red algae (such as the Bangiales, which include nori). In the medulla of the kelp *Laminaria*, trumpet hyphae that transport mannitol also differentiate, with a function similar to that of sieve tubes and an independent origin ([7.12.8](/7.12.8)).

Algal bodies also grow in several ways: apical growth (most red algae, stoneworts, *Dictyota*), intercalary growth (the meristem of kelp lies between blade and stipe), trichothallic growth (division at the bases of hairs on the thallus surface, as in young *Scytosiphon*) and diffuse growth (any cell of laver or *Ulva* can divide).

## 6.2. Criteria for Delimiting the Divisions

The divisions of algae are delimited chiefly by cellular characters: the kinds of photosynthetic pigments, storage products, cell wall composition, the number, type and insertion of flagella, the nucleus, and the arrangement of chromatophore membranes and thylakoids under the electron microscope. These characters are rather stable within a division and are thought to reflect relationships better than the form of the algal body. Modern molecular phylogeny has largely confirmed this view, but has also shown that some of these characters (pigments, chromatophore membranes) reflect the origin of the plastid and not that of the host cell (Section 6.2.6).

### 6.2.1. Photosynthetic Pigments and Storage Products

All oxygenic photosynthetic organisms have **chlorophyll** *a*, the pigment of the photosystem reaction centers. The divisions differ in the accessory pigments of the light-harvesting antennae.

- Chlorophyll *b*: green algae, euglenids, chlorarachniophytes and land plants, and the prochlorophyte cyanobacteria.
- Chlorophyll *c* (*c*₁, *c*₂, *c*₃, porphyrins lacking the phytol tail): heterokont algae (chrysophytes, xanthophytes, diatoms, brown algae), dinoflagellates, cryptophytes and haptophytes.
- Phycobiliproteins (phycoerythrin, phycocyanin, allophycocyanin): cyanobacteria, glaucophytes and red algae, attached to the thylakoid surface in the form of **phycobilisomes** ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#def-6.1.4)). Cryptophytes also have phycobiliproteins, but these lie inside the thylakoid lumen and do not form phycobilisomes.
- Carotenoids: β-carotene is universal. The xanthophylls differ among divisions and are important diagnostic markers: lutein in the green lineage, fucoxanthin in brown algae, diatoms, chrysophytes and haptophytes (which gives the algal body its brown or golden color), peridinin in dinoflagellates, alloxanthin in cryptophytes, and diadinoxanthin in xanthophytes and euglenids.

Accessory pigments widen the absorbed spectrum. Water absorbs long wavelengths strongly, red light is absorbed within a few meters of sea water, and only blue-green light reaches the depths. Phycoerythrin and fucoxanthin absorb green and blue-green light, so red and brown algae can live at greater depths. The vertical distribution of algae, however, does not strictly follow color: many red algae live in the intertidal zone and many green algae in deep water, and light intensity, nutrients and grazing are equally important.

Storage products of photosynthesis are also diagnostic of divisions, and their chemical nature and location matter as much:

| Storage product | Chemical nature | Location | Group |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starch | α-1,4-glucan, containing amylose and amylopectin, turns blue with iodine | Inside the chromatophore (often around the pyrenoid) | Green algae, land plants |
| Starch | Same | In the cytoplasm, outside the chromatophore | Glaucophytes, cryptophytes (between the chromatophore and its outer membrane), dinoflagellates |
| Floridean starch | α-glucan resembling amylopectin, turns yellow-brown and then wine-red to purple with iodine | In the cytoplasm | Red algae |
| Cyanophycean starch | Glycogen-type α-glucan | Between thylakoids | Cyanobacteria |
| Cyanophycin granules | Arginine–aspartate polypeptide (cyanophycin), stores nitrogen | In the cytoplasm | Cyanobacteria |
| Chrysolaminarin, laminarin | β-1,3-glucan, soluble | In vacuoles | Heterokont algae, haptophytes |
| Paramylon | β-1,3-glucan, insoluble, granules of definite shape, does not turn blue with iodine | In the cytoplasm | Euglenids |
| Mannitol, floridoside | Sugar alcohol, galactosyl glycerol | In the cytoplasm (dissolved) | Brown algae; red algae |
| Oil (lipid) | Triglyceride | In the cytoplasm | Xanthophytes, diatoms, chrysophytes, dinoflagellates and others |

### 6.2.2. Cell Walls and Cell Coverings

| Covering | Composition | Group |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall (prokaryotic) | Mainly peptidoglycan, with an outer membrane and a gelatinous sheath outside | Cyanobacteria |
| Cell wall (two layers) | Inner layer cellulose, outer layer pectic, often mucilaginous | Green algae |
| Cell wall (two layers) | Cellulose microfibrils embedded in a matrix of sulfated galactans (agar, carrageenan); many species deposit calcium carbonate | Red algae |

### 6.2.3. Flagella

Apart from cyanobacteria and red algae, most algae have flagella on their vegetative or reproductive cells (zoospores, gametes). Eukaryotic flagella and cilia have the same structure, with a 9 + 2 axoneme of **microtubules** inside the sheath and a **basal body** at the base ([7.3.14](/7.3.14)). Phycologists divide flagella into two kinds according to whether the surface bears hairs.

**Definition 6.2.1 (Tinsel and Whiplash Flagella)** A flagellum whose surface bears two rows or one row of hairs (mastigonemes) is called a **tinsel flagellum**; a flagellum with a smooth surface and no hairs is called a **whiplash flagellum**.

The hairs are not merely decorative. The hairs on the anterior flagella of heterokont algae and cryptophytes are rigid tubular structures (tripartite in heterokonts: base, tubular shaft and terminal filament), synthesized in Golgi-derived vesicles and transported to the flagellar surface. When a wave passes from the base to the tip of a smooth flagellum, it pushes the cell backward, whereas a flagellum with two rows of rigid hairs reverses the direction of thrust and pulls the cell forward ([7.12.8](/7.12.8)). The mastigonemes of euglenids are fine filaments arranged on one side and are not tubular, so "tinsel" is only a morphological term, and the mastigonemes of different groups are not homologous.

Under the electron microscope a flagellum has two parts, the shaft projecting outside the cell and the basal body embedded in it, with a transition zone between them. In cross section the shaft has a central pair of single microtubules surrounded by nine doublets, written 9(2) + 2; the basal body has no central microtubules and is surrounded by nine triplets, written 9(3) + 0. Flagellar movement results from the sliding of adjacent doublets past each other ([7.3.14](/7.3.14)). In a cell with two flagella the two basal bodies are connected by distal and proximal connecting fibers, and microtubular roots (flagellar roots) extend from the basal bodies into the cell. The number and arrangement of the roots differ among groups and are also important systematic characters (the cruciate microtubular root system of green algae, [7.12.13](/7.12.13)).

The number and length (equal or unequal) of flagella and their insertion (apical, subapical, lateral, ventral) are also diagnostic of divisions:

| Group | Number and length | Insertion | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyanobacteria, red algae | None | — | — |
| Glaucophytes | 2, unequal | Subapical | — |
| Green algae | 2 or 4, equal; many in a ring in *Oedogonium* | Apical | All whiplash (may bear scales or fine hairs) |
| Euglenids | 2, one emerging from the cell and the other very short, remaining inside the reservoir | Apical (emerging from the reservoir) | The emergent one has a single row of fine hairs and a paraflagellar body (photoreceptor) at its base |
| Cryptophytes | 2, slightly unequal | Subapical (in a depression on the ventral side at the anterior end) | Both tinsel (two rows of tubular hairs on the longer, one row on the shorter) |
| Haptophytes | 2, equal or unequal, plus one haptonema | Apical | Whiplash |
| Dinoflagellates | 2, one transverse flagellum in the transverse groove and one longitudinal flagellum running backward along the longitudinal groove | Ventral (apical in desmokont dinoflagellates) | Transverse flagellum ribbon-like with fine hairs, longitudinal flagellum whiplash |
| Chrysophytes, xanthophytes, brown algae | 2, unequal: the longer directed forward, the shorter backward | Apical or subapical (chrysophytes, xanthophytes) or lateral (brown algae) | Anterior tinsel, posterior whiplash |
| Diatoms | One, only in the sperm of centric diatoms | Apical | Tinsel, with a 9 + 0 axoneme (no central microtubules) |

### 6.2.4. The Nucleus

Algae have three kinds of nucleus. Cyanobacteria are prokaryotic: the DNA lies in the central region (centroplasm), with no nuclear envelope or nucleolus, and does not form chromosomes (Section 6.5.1). The nucleus of the great majority of eukaryotic algae is a typical eukaryotic nucleus. Chinese textbooks also call the nucleus of dinoflagellates and euglenids a mesokaryon.

**Definition 6.2.2 (Mesokaryon)** A eukaryotic nucleus in which the nuclear envelope and nucleolus do not disappear during mitosis and the chromosomes remain condensed in interphase is called a **mesokaryon**. It is found in dinoflagellates and euglenids.

The name comes from the old view of an "intermediate between prokaryotic and eukaryotic" nucleus. The chromosomes of dinoflagellates contain almost no histones and exist as liquid-crystalline fibrils, and during division the spindle microtubules lie in cytoplasmic channels through the nucleus and do not enter it ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.4.2), [7.12.9](/7.12.9)). Modern research shows that these features are derived and not primitive. The chromosomes of the dinoflagellate nucleus are packaged by proteins of viral origin, and the close relatives in the Alveolata (ciliates, apicomplexans) have normal eukaryotic nuclei. The euglenid nucleus has histones and nucleosomes, and its closed mitosis is quite common among protists. "Mesokaryon" is therefore only a morphological description and does not represent a stage in the transition from prokaryote to eukaryote.

### 6.2.5. Chromatophore Membranes and Thylakoids

In phycology the plastid of algae is often called the chromatophore, to stress its morphological diversity: cup-shaped (*Chlamydomonas*), spiral ribbon (*Spirogyra*), stellate (laver, *Porphyridium*), reticulate (*Oedogonium*), discoid (diatoms, brown algae) and so on.

**Definition 6.2.3 (Chromatophore and Pyrenoid)** The plastid containing photosynthetic pigments in an algal cell is called a **chromatophore**. In many algae the chromatophore contains one or several **pyrenoids**, each consisting of a protein core mainly of Rubisco and an outer sheath of starch (green algae) or other polysaccharide, the site of the $\ce{CO2}$-concentrating mechanism.

The pyrenoid concentrates Rubisco in one place and, together with carbonic anhydrase, raises the $\ce{CO2}$ concentration around it, functioning like the **carboxysome** of cyanobacteria. The presence and form of the pyrenoid (embedded in the chromatophore or projecting to one side, the "stalked type" of brown algae) are also taxonomic characters.

Under the electron microscope the number of envelope layers of the chromatophore and the arrangement of thylakoids differ among divisions:

| Group | Chromatophore envelope | Arrangement of thylakoids |
|---|---|---|
| Cyanobacteria | No chromatophore; thylakoids in the peripheral cytoplasm | Single, with phycobilisomes on the surface |
| Glaucophytes | 2 layers, with peptidoglycan between them | Single, with phycobilisomes |
| Red algae | 2 layers | Single, with phycobilisomes |
| Green algae, land plants | 2 layers | In stacks of 2–6, or forming grana |
| Euglenids | 3 layers | In stacks of 3 |
| Dinoflagellates (peridinin-containing) | 3 layers | In stacks of 3 |
| Cryptophytes | 4 layers, the outermost continuous with the nuclear envelope; nucleomorph between the second and third layers | In stacks of 2, phycobiliproteins in the thylakoid lumen |
| Haptophytes | 4 layers, the outermost continuous with the nuclear envelope | In stacks of 3 |
| Chrysophytes, xanthophytes, diatoms, brown algae | 4 layers, the outermost continuous with the nuclear envelope | In stacks of 3, with a girdle lamella around the periphery |

The outermost of the 4 envelope layers bears ribosomes and is continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum. Chinese textbooks call it the chloroplast endoplasmic reticulum. In cryptophytes, haptophytes and heterokont algae it is continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope, so the chromatophore lies inside the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum.

### 6.2.6. From Three Pigment Lines to Endosymbiosis

Pigments and chromatophore structure seemed to arrange the algae into three lines, which for a long time was the framework Chinese botany textbooks used to describe algal evolution.

- The red line: chlorophyll *a* and phycobilins, single thylakoids, no flagella. From the prokaryotic cyanobacteria to the eukaryotic red algae.
- The yellow line: chlorophylls *a* and *c*, thylakoids in stacks of 3 (2 in cryptophytes), the primitive types containing phycobilins. It includes cryptophytes, dinoflagellates, haptophytes, chrysophytes, xanthophytes, diatoms and brown algae, the brown algae being the most advanced.
- The green line: chlorophylls *a* and *b*, thylakoids in stacks of 2–6. From the prokaryotic prochlorophytes to the eukaryotic euglenids, green algae and stoneworts, and then to land plants.

The old interpretation held that each line evolved eukaryotic cells independently from one kind of prokaryotic alga: cyanobacteria gave rise to red algae, prochlorophytes to green algae, and a hypothetical prokaryotic alga containing chlorophyll *c* to the yellow line. Molecular phylogeny and evidence for endosymbiosis ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.2)) give a quite different picture: the three lines are lineages of plastids, not lineages of host cells.

1. **Primary endosymbiosis**: the plastid of the Archaeplastida originated only once. (A much later, separate primary endosymbiosis in the amoeba *Paulinella* is unrelated to the major groups of algae.) A heterotrophic eukaryotic cell engulfed a cyanobacterium, which became the plastid. The Archaeplastida so formed fall into three branches: glaucophytes, red algae and green plants. Primary plastids have two envelope membranes, the inner and outer membranes of the cyanobacterium; glaucophytes still retain a peptidoglycan wall between the two membranes.
2. **Secondary endosymbiosis**: another eukaryote engulfed a eukaryotic alga that already had a primary plastid, and the latter was reduced to a plastid. Euglenids and chlorarachniophytes engulfed a green alga (two independent events), while cryptophytes, haptophytes, heterokont algae and dinoflagellates engulfed a red alga (perhaps through one or several successive transfers, still unsettled). Secondary plastids have 3 or 4 membranes: the two original plastid membranes, the plasma membrane of the engulfed alga (lost in euglenids and dinoflagellates), and the membrane of the host's food vacuole (the chloroplast endoplasmic reticulum mentioned above). The nucleus of the engulfed alga has disappeared in most lineages and persists, as a **nucleomorph** between the second and third membranes, only in cryptophytes (red algal origin) and chlorarachniophytes (green algal origin) ([7.12.9](/7.12.9)).
3. Tertiary endosymbiosis and kleptoplasty: dinoflagellates have repeatedly replaced their plastids. The plastid of *Karenia* came from a haptophyte, the "diatom dinoflagellates" (*Kryptoperidinium*, *Durinskia*) retain almost a complete diatom cell, and *Lepidodinium* has a plastid of green algal origin. *Dinophysis* steals cryptophyte plastids from the ciliate *Mesodinium* on which it feeds and uses them temporarily; such plastids are called kleptoplasts ([7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.8.4)).

In this framework the characters of the "three lines" receive a new explanation. Chlorophyll *c* is a mark of secondary plastids of red algal origin and not of a prokaryotic ancestor. Cryptophytes have both phycobiliproteins and chlorophyll *c* because their plastid comes from a red alga and kept the phycobiliproteins of the red alga (though losing the phycobilisomes), and not because they are a "primitive type of the yellow line". Euglenids have the same pigments as green algae because their plastid comes from a green alga, whereas the host cell belongs with the trypanosomes in the Discoba and has nothing to do with green algae. The prochlorophytes are cyanobacteria that acquired chlorophyll *b* several times independently and have nothing to do with the origin of the plastid of green plants (Section 6.5.4). In one sentence: pigments tell us where the plastid came from, and the structure of flagella, cell coverings and nucleus tells us who the host is.

## 6.3. Classification of the Algae

### 6.3.1. The Traditional Algal Divisions

Opinions on how many divisions the algae form have long differed. Chinese botany textbooks used for a long time 8–9 divisions (cyanobacteria, euglenids, dinoflagellates, chrysophytes, xanthophytes, diatoms, green algae, red algae, brown algae). In 1978 Chinese phycologists proposed 11 divisions, adding the cryptophytes and stoneworts, and the monograph *Freshwater Algae of China* is arranged accordingly. Later the prochlorophytes, glaucophytes and haptophytes were split off in turn, reaching at most 13 divisions: cyanobacteria, prochlorophytes, glaucophytes, red algae, cryptophytes, dinoflagellates, haptophytes, chrysophytes, xanthophytes, diatoms, brown algae, euglenids and green algae (the stoneworts being separate or included in the green algae). These divisions were delimited by the cellular characters of Section 6.2, and most are still natural groups in modern classification, only their relationships and rank have changed completely.

### 6.3.2. The Current Higher Classification

The current classification of eukaryotes (Adl et al. 2019; Burki et al. 2020) no longer recognizes "algae" outside the plant kingdom, and assigns the divisions to several supergroups ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.4), [7.11.20](/7.11.20)). The table below shows where each traditional division has gone. The Latin names in parentheses are group names in common use in the current literature, and the ranks (division, class) are not uniform among systems.

| Traditional division | Current placement (supergroup: group) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cyanobacteria | Bacteria: Cyanobacteriota | Prokaryotes; the *International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants* and the prokaryote code are both used (Section 6.5.5) |
| Prochlorophytes | Bacteria: several genera within Cyanobacteriota | Not a natural group; the genera are scattered among cyanobacteria |
| Glaucophytes | Archaeplastida: *Glaucophyta* | Primary plastid, peptidoglycan retained |

| Euglenids | Discoba: Euglenida in the Euglenozoa | Secondary plastid (green algal origin); Euglenozoa also include the kinetoplastids (trypanosomes) |

Several points deserve notice. First, the groups traditionally placed on the "yellow line" belong to three supergroups (TSAR, Cryptista, Haptista), and their relationships to one another remain unresolved. Second, the closest relatives of brown algae and diatoms are not other algae but the heterotrophic oomycetes ([7.12.10](/7.12.10)), so the boundary between "algae" and "fungus-like organisms" is not a phylogenetic boundary either. Third, "Chrysophyta" and "Xanthophyta" are no longer used as divisions, and chrysophytes (Chrysophyceae), xanthophytes (Xanthophyceae), diatoms (Bacillariophyta), brown algae (Phaeophyceae) and others are classes within Ochrophyta. The chapters of this course are organized by the current groups, and the text also gives the division names of Chinese textbooks so that older literature can be consulted.

## 6.4. Reproduction and Life Histories of the Algae

Algae propagate in three ways: vegetative reproduction, asexual reproduction and sexual reproduction. The first two do not involve the union of gametes, and the last is accompanied by meiosis and fertilization, so that the life history contains two nuclear phases, haploid and diploid.

### 6.4.1. Vegetative and Asexual Reproduction

In vegetative reproduction a part of the plant body separates from the parent and grows directly into a new individual: cell division of unicellular algae, fragmentation of colonies and filaments (the hormogonia of cyanobacteria, Section 6.5.2), the propagules of brown algae (*Sphacelaria*), and longitudinal splitting of large algal bodies.

Asexual reproduction produces special reproductive cells, the spores, each of which grows into a new individual without fusion. Spores form in sporangia, and some are motile and some are not.

**Definition 6.4.1 (Zoospores, Aplanospores and Autospores)** A spore that has flagella, is motile and usually lacks a cell wall is called a **zoospore**. A spore that has no flagella, is non-motile and has a cell wall is called an **aplanospore**. An aplanospore with the same form as the parent cell is called an **autospore**.

Zoospores are the main spore type in many green, brown and xanthophyte algae. In the zoosporangium of *Vaucheria* the whole protoplast forms a single compound multinucleate zoospore with paired flagella over its entire surface ([7.12.8](/7.12.8)). Terrestrial and aerial species mostly produce aplanospores, and the aplanospore wall of chrysophytes is silicified and is called a statospore. Chlorococcalean green algae such as *Chlorella* and *Scenedesmus* reproduce by autospores ([7.12.13](/7.12.13)). Under unfavorable conditions the vegetative cell enlarges, accumulates storage products and thickens its wall, forming a resting stage that can remain dormant for a long time, the **akinete** (cyanobacteria, green algae, xanthophytes). All spores of red algae are non-motile: a sporangium producing one spore gives a monospore, and one producing four gives tetraspores ([7.12.7](/7.12.7)).

### 6.4.2. Sexual Reproduction

Sexual reproduction is the union of two gametes into a zygote. By the form and behavior of the gametes, sexual reproduction in algae can be arranged in a series.

**Definition 6.4.2 (Isogamy and Oogamy)** Sexual reproduction in which the two gametes are identical in shape, structure, size and motility is called **isogamy**. Sexual reproduction in which the two gametes differ markedly in size and behavior, the large gamete (egg) being non-flagellate and non-motile and the small gamete (sperm) flagellate and motile, is called **oogamy**.

Intermediate between the two is **anisogamy**: the two gametes have the same shape and structure and are both motile, but differ in size and motility, the female gamete being larger and more sluggish and the male gamete smaller and more active. In addition, the Zygnematophyceae (*Spirogyra*, desmids) have no flagellate gametes, and two amoeboid protoplasts fuse through a conjugation tube, which is called conjugation ([7.12.14](/7.12.14)). This series occurs in parallel in the green algae (isogamy in *Chlamydomonas*, anisogamy in *Pandorina*, oogamy in *Volvox*), the brown algae (*Ectocarpus*, *Dictyota*, kelp) and the xanthophytes (*Tribonema*, *Vaucheria*); oogamy is the most advanced type. The non-motile sperm and carpogonium of red algae are another line.

Whether a gamete can fuse with a gamete from the same individual (the same clone) also differs in two ways. Where gametes from the same individual can fuse the condition is called homothallism, and where only gametes from individuals of different mating types can fuse it is called heterothallism. For example, freshwater *Vaucheria* species are mostly homothallic and marine ones mostly heterothallic, and *Chlamydomonas* is mostly heterothallic, with two mating types, $mt^+$ and $mt^-$.

### 6.4.3. Alternation of Nuclear Phases and Life-Cycle Types

Sexual reproduction makes haploid ($n$) and diploid ($2n$) nuclear phases alternate in the life history: fertilization doubles the chromosome number and meiosis halves it, and this alternation is called alternation of nuclear phases. Algae that reproduce only vegetatively and asexually (cyanobacteria, many unicellular algae) have no alternation of nuclear phases. Meiosis can occur at three times, namely **zygotic meiosis**, **gametic meiosis** and **sporic meiosis** (defined in [7.4.1](/7.4.1#def-1.4.2)), and this determines how many kinds of plant bodies there are in the life history and what the nuclear phase of each is.

| Life-cycle type | Meiosis | Plant bodies | Examples among algae |
|---|---|---|---|
| Haplontic (monogenetic, haploid) | At germination of the zygote | Only haploid plant bodies; the zygote is the only diploid cell | *Chlamydomonas*, *Volvox*, *Ulothrix*, *Spirogyra*, *Chara*, *Vaucheria*, most dinoflagellates |
| Diplontic (monogenetic, diploid) | In forming gametes | Only diploid plant bodies; the gametes are the only haploid cells | Diatoms, *Fucus*, *Silvetia*, *Sargassum*, *Codium* |
| Diplohaplontic (digenetic) | In forming spores | Alternation of a haploid gametophyte and a diploid sporophyte (**alternation of generations**) | *Ulva*, *Ectocarpus*, *Dictyota* (isomorphic); kelp, *Scytosiphon*, *Monostroma* (heteromorphic) |
| Trigenetic | In forming tetraspores | Three generations: gametophyte, carposporophyte and tetrasporophyte | *Polysiphonia*, *Gelidium* and most florideophyte red algae ([7.12.7](/7.12.7)) |

"Genetic" (Chinese *yuan*, "element") refers to how many kinds of plant bodies there are in the life history, and "phasic" (Chinese *xiang*, "phase") to the nuclear phase of the plant body (haplophase is $n$, diplophase is $2n$). In a life history with alternation of generations, the diploid plant that produces spores, from the zygote up to meiosis, is the sporophyte (asexual generation), and the haploid plant from the spore up to the formation of gametes is the gametophyte (sexual generation). There are two kinds of alternation of generations.

**Definition 6.4.3 (Isomorphic and Heteromorphic Alternation of Generations)** An alternation of generations in which gametophyte and sporophyte have the same form and structure is called **isomorphic alternation of generations**. One in which the two differ in form and structure is called **heteromorphic alternation of generations**, in which either the sporophyte dominates (kelp) or the gametophyte dominates (*Scytosiphon*, *Monostroma*).

In isomorphic alternation the two generations can be distinguished only by chromosome number or reproductive organs: in *Ulva* the gametophyte produces gametes and the sporophyte produces zoospores. In heteromorphic alternation the two stages of the life history are often adapted to different seasons or environments: the microscopic gametophyte of kelp can survive the season unfavorable to the large sporophyte, and the filamentous conchocelis phase of laver spends the summer in shells ([7.12.7](/7.12.7)). All land plants have sporic meiosis and heteromorphic alternation of generations, with gametophyte dominance in bryophytes and sporophyte dominance in vascular plants ([7.12.18](/7.12.18)).

The three positions of meiosis in algae are not ranked as primitive or advanced: they arose independently in many divisions, and several often coexist in the same class (brown algae have alternation of generations, but the Fucales have gametic meiosis). Another feature of algal life histories is plasticity: in many species the generations can repeat themselves without sexual reproduction (the gametes of *Ectocarpus* can grow parthenogenetically into new gametophytes, and the monospores of the laver thallus grow directly into new thalli), and the course of the life history is often controlled by temperature and photoperiod.

## 6.5. Cyanobacteria

The **cyanobacteria** ([Definition 18.4.1](/7.11.18#def-18.4.1)) are traditionally called blue-green algae in botany and are listed at the head of the algae as the division Cyanophyta. They are the only prokaryotes that carry out oxygenic photosynthesis, and also the ancestors of all plastids. This section treats the morphology, reproduction and representative genera of cyanobacteria. The structure of the envelope, photosynthesis, nitrogen fixation and the development of heterocysts are in [7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.4), [7.11.6](/7.11.6) and [7.11.12](/7.11.12#sec-12.8) respectively.

### 6.5.1. Cell Structure

Under the electron microscope the cell wall of cyanobacteria has four layers, LI–LIV from inside out, with LI next to the plasma membrane. LII is the **peptidoglycan** layer, much thicker than in most Gram-negative bacteria, and the outermost LIV corresponds to the outer membrane, so the wall of cyanobacteria can be dissolved by lysozyme. Outside the wall is a gelatinous sheath of pectic acid and mucopolysaccharides. In some species the sheath hydrates easily and is transparent, in others it is firm and layered, and it often contains red, purple or yellow-brown pigments not involved in photosynthesis, such as the UV-absorbing scytonemin, which makes cyanobacteria living in strong light blackish brown.

The protoplast has two parts, distinguishable even under the light microscope:

- The central region (centroplasm, central body) lies in the center of the cell and contains the DNA, with no nuclear envelope or nucleolus, and the DNA is not bound with histones into chromosomes. The centroplasm also contains polyhedral carboxysomes (formerly polyhedral bodies) containing Rubisco and carbonic anhydrase.
- The peripheral region (chromatoplasm) surrounds the centroplasm and contains parallel single thylakoids (photosynthetic lamellae) with phycobilisomes attached to their surface. Storage products are scattered in it: cyanophycean starch, cyanophycin granules (stores of nitrogen) and polyphosphate granules.

Planktonic species have **gas vesicles** in the peripheral region. Many gas vesicles aggregate into the black or red-brown pseudovacuoles (gas bubbles) seen under the light microscope, which make the cell float. *Microcystis*, *Anabaena*, *Aphanizomenon* and others move up and down in the water column by adjusting the amounts of gas vesicles and storage products, rising by day to the well-lit surface layer, which is one reason they can form blooms in still water ([7.12.9](/7.12.9)).

Cyanobacteria have no flagella. Filamentous species such as *Oscillatoria* glide, the filaments moving back and forth and swinging from side to side. This movement is driven by type IV pili on the cell surface and secreted mucilage.

There are two specialized cells in filamentous species. One is the **heterocyst**: when combined nitrogen is lacking, vegetative cells differentiate at roughly regular intervals into heterocysts. A heterocyst is larger than a vegetative cell and has a thick wall; the wall at the junction with the neighboring cells appears under the light microscope as a refractive "polar nodule", and the contents are homogeneous and transparent (storage granules dissolved, photosynthetic lamellae reorganized). Heterocysts lack photosystem II, do not evolve oxygen and are the site of nitrogen fixation. The other is the **akinete**: a resting cell formed by enlargement of a vegetative cell, accumulation of storage products and thickening of the wall, which survives drought and cold and germinates into a new filament when conditions are suitable.

### 6.5.2. Reproduction

Cyanobacteria have no sexual reproduction and no gametes or meiosis (but have **horizontal gene transfer** and natural transformation). The modes of reproduction are:

- Cell division: in unicellular species the daughter cells separate immediately after division. In colonial species the daughter cells do not separate and form large colonies, which then break into small colonies. The cells of *Microcystis* divide in three directions, and those of *Merismopedia* in two directions, so they form a plate.
- Hormogonia: the main mode of reproduction in filamentous species.

**Definition 6.5.1 (Hormogonium)** A short segment of a few cells into which the filament of a filamentous cyanobacterium breaks is called a **hormogonium**, and each hormogonium can grow into a new filament.

Hormogonia form when some cells of the filament die (necridia), when biconcave separation disks form between two vegetative cells, when the filament breaks at a heterocyst, or by mechanical action. In the Nostocales the hormogonium is also a developmental stage: it consists of smaller cells without heterocysts that can glide, sometimes with gas vesicles, and is the stage of dispersal and of invasion of symbiotic hosts (*Azolla*, cycads, *Gunnera*, hornworts).

- Spores: filamentous species outside the Oscillatoriaceae produce akinetes. The Chamaesiphonales produce exospores: the cell divides transversely into two unequal parts, the smaller upper one becoming a spore and the larger basal one continuing to divide and produce spores, and after the spores are released the wall of the mother cell remains, called a pseudosheath. *Dermocarpa* and others produce endospores: the mother cell enlarges, the protoplast divides repeatedly to form many thin-walled small cells, and all are released when the wall of the mother cell ruptures. These endospores (also called baeocytes) have nothing to do with the endospores of *Bacillus*, and only share the name.

### 6.5.3. Representative Genera

Traditional cyanobacterial classification divided them into orders by body form and mode of reproduction, with a single class Cyanophyceae. The number of orders differs among textbooks. Some recognize 3 orders (Chroococcales, Chamaesiphonales and Oscillatoriales, with *Nostoc* and *Stigonema* both placed in the Oscillatoriales), and *Freshwater Algae of China* (Hu Hongjun and Wei Yinxin) recognizes 4 (Chroococcales, Oscillatoriales, Nostocales and Stigonematales, with Chamaesiphonales merged into the Chroococcales). The class has about 150 genera and 1500 species worldwide (Smith's count). Here we list the 5 orders in the finest division and note their current placement in the table (Komárek et al. 2014). After each genus the approximate number of species known in China is given in parentheses (from the counts of Hu Hongjun, Wei Yinxin and others).

Chroococcales: unicellular or colonial, with thin cell walls whose outer layer is gelatinized, reproducing by cell division.

- *Chroococcus* (about 26 species in China): unicells or small colonies of cells of two or more generations, each cell with a transparent colorless individual gelatinous sheath and a colonial sheath around the colony. The cells are hemispherical or tetrad-shaped, with flat contact surfaces, which is the diagnostic feature of the genus. It lives in the plankton of lakes and ponds and is also found on damp ground, tree trunks and dripping rocks.
- *Microcystis* (about 20 species in China): planktonic colonies, spherical, irregular or reticulate with many perforations, with many cells evenly distributed in a structureless gelatinous matrix; the cells are spherical and mostly have pseudovacuoles. The cells divide in three directions, and colonies reproduce by breaking up. *M. aeruginosa* is the main species of summer blooms in eutrophic lakes, many strains produce microcystins ([7.12.9](/7.12.9)), and they also secrete substances that inhibit other algae.
- *Merismopedia* (about 11 species in China): a plate-like colony one cell thick, with the cells often in pairs, two pairs in a group and four groups in a small colony, and many small colonies arranged in a regular square; the gelatinous sheath is indistinct.

Other common members of the order include *Gloeocapsa* (about 34 species in China, mostly subaerial, with the colonial envelope often distinctly layered, on moist soil and rocks), *Gloeothece* and *Coelosphaerium*.

Chamaesiphonales (which can also be merged into the Chroococcales): unicellular or colonial, reproducing by exospores or endospores.

- *Chamaesiphon*: unicellular, long rod-shaped and polar, attached by the base to aquatic angiosperms, mosses or other algae, reproducing by exospores.
- *Dermocarpa*: unicellular, spherical or pear-shaped, attached, reproducing by endospores.

Oscillatoriales: filamentous, without heterocysts, the Oscillatoriaceae without akinetes, the filaments of many species motile, reproducing by hormogonia.

- *Oscillatoria* (about 10 species in China): an unbranched filament of a single row of cells, often clustered into masses. The cells are short cylinders, wider than long, and the terminal cell is variable in form, often thickened or with a calyptra. The sheath is absent or indistinct. The filaments move back and forth, swing from side to side or rotate, hence the name. It lives on damp ground and in shallow water, and is especially common in still water polluted with organic matter, where mass development forms a dark blue-green layer.
- *Phormidium* (about 50 species in China): easily confused with *Oscillatoria*, but the filaments have a distinct gelatinous sheath, and many filaments adhere to each other into mats, gelatinous masses or crusts; it lives on moist soil, walls, tree trunks and in all kinds of fresh water.
- *Lyngbya* (about 20 species in China): filaments with a firm, sometimes layered sheath, adhering to each other into leathery layers; the cells are disk-shaped, and hormogonia form from necridia and separation disks. *L. majuscula* (now *Moorea producens* and others) is a common species on tropical coasts and produces skin-irritating toxins.
- *Spirulina*-type genera: the filament is coiled into a regular helix, without a sheath, and rotates. The commercial "spirulina" is *Arthrospira platensis* and *A. maxima*, which are not in the same genus as true *Spirulina*: the filaments of *Arthrospira* are thicker with distinct cross walls, whereas those of *Spirulina* are thin with indistinct cross walls. *Arthrospira* lives in alkaline tropical lakes, with about 60% of its dry weight being protein, and is cultivated on a large scale.

Nostocales (which can also be merged into the Oscillatoriales): filamentous, unbranched or with false branching, not tapering at the tip, with a sheath, heterocysts and akinetes.

- *Nostoc* (about 50 species in China): an unbranched filament of a row of round cells, like a string of beads. Many filaments are gathered irregularly in a common gelatinous sheath, forming macroscopic or microscopic spherical bodies, sheets or irregular masses. The heterocysts have thick walls and fix nitrogen. It reproduces by hormogonia, sometimes with akinetes. The genus includes three famous edible cyanobacteria: *N. commune* (ground-ear, gelatinous sheets that appear on grassland and hillsides after rain), *N. sphaeroides* (a spherical gelatinous colony, found in rice fields in Hubei and elsewhere) and *N. flagelliforme* (fat choy, hair-like thalli on the soil surface of dry grasslands in northwest China). Harvesting fat choy has seriously damaged grassland vegetation, and since 2000 China has banned its collection and sale; it is listed as a first-class nationally protected wild plant.
- *Anabaena* (about 16 species in China): very similar to *Nostoc*, with round cells joined into straight or curved filaments, solitary or aggregated into masses, but without a common gelatinous sheath. Planktonic species often form blooms together with *Microcystis*; in current classification they have been moved to *Dolichospermum* (Wacklin et al. 2009), for example *D. flos-aquae* (formerly *Anabaena flos-aquae*). *Anabaena* and *Nostoc* fix free nitrogen, and stocking them in rice fields increases rice yield. The symbiotic cyanobacterium in the leaf cavities of *Azolla* (water fern) was traditionally called "*Anabaena azollae*" and is now mostly placed in *Nostoc* ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.2.5)).
- *Tolypothrix* (about 8 species in China) and *Scytonema* (about 60 species in China): filaments with a firm sheath and intercalary heterocysts, with false branching, that is, one or two hormogonia break out of the side of the sheath and grow into branches. *Tolypothrix* often produces one false branch near a heterocyst, and *Scytonema* produces paired false branches where a vegetative cell between two heterocysts has died. Both live mostly on moist rocks, soil and walls.
- *Rivularia* and *Gloeotrichia*: the filaments are arranged radially in spherical or hemispherical gelatinous colonies, with a basal heterocyst and the tip tapering to a hair. In *Gloeotrichia* long cylindrical akinetes lie next to the heterocyst. They are often attached to water plants or floating, and can appear in large numbers in lakes in summer.

Stigonematales (which can also be merged into the Oscillatoriales): filamentous, with true branching and heterocysts, akinetes rare.

- *Stigonema* (about 20 species in China): irregularly branched filaments of one or several rows of cells, many filaments clustered into a blackish-brown felt, with a thick, firm, transparent or yellow-brown sheath. True branches form by cell division parallel to the long axis of the filament, unlike false branches, and adjacent cells are joined by plasmodesmata-like connections. It lives mostly on moist rocks. It reproduces by hormogonia or by apical branches.

### 6.5.4. The Prochlorophytes

In 1975 a green symbiotic prokaryote was found on the body surface and in the cloacal cavity of tropical ascidians (Chinese phycologists, including C. K. Tseng, also collected it in the waters of the Xisha Islands). It contains chlorophylls *a* and *b* and has no phycobilisomes, and was named *Prochloron didemni*; a division Prochlorophyta was erected for it. Later the filamentous *Prochlorothrix hollandica* (1986) from Dutch lakes and the minute marine *Prochlorococcus marinus* (1988) were found. The cell structure of the prochlorophytes is similar to that of cyanobacteria: prokaryotic, with thylakoids in pairs or stacks at the cell periphery, a Gram-negative-type cell wall containing diaminopimelic acid, no pseudovacuoles, and reproduction only by cell division.

The discovery of the prochlorophytes was for a time regarded as a major event in algal evolutionary history: they seemed to be exactly the ancestors of the chloroplasts of green plants, that is, the prokaryotic starting point of the "green line". Molecular phylogeny has refuted this. The three genera of prochlorophytes are scattered over different branches of the cyanobacterial tree, are distant relatives of one another, and are instead closely related to their own ordinary cyanobacterial relatives. Their chlorophyll *b*-binding light-harvesting proteins (Pcb) belong to the IsiA family of cyanobacteria and are not homologous to the light-harvesting complexes (LHC) of green plants, and chloroplasts originated from a cyanobacterial lineage close to *Gloeomargarita* ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.2.2)). The Prochlorophyta are therefore not a natural group, and all three genera are cyanobacteria that acquired chlorophyll *b* independently and should be placed in the cyanobacteria. Of them *Prochlorococcus* is the most abundant photosynthetic organism on Earth ([7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.4)).

### 6.5.5. Current Classification of Cyanobacteria

Cyanobacteria are governed by two nomenclatural codes. As "algae", their names are validly published under the **International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants**, on the basis of specimens and morphological descriptions. As bacteria, their names are governed by the International Code of Nomenclature of Prokaryotes, with cultures as types. The two sets of names are still used side by side and are often inconsistent.

Traditional classification was based only on morphology, and many genera are form genera. Komárek et al. (2014) proposed a polyphasic approach combining 16S rRNA and genomic phylogeny, ultrastructure (arrangement of thylakoids), morphology, ecology and physiology, and rebuilt the orders and families. For example, morphologically simple unicellular genera such as *Synechococcus* proved to be scattered all over the tree and had to be split, the heterocyst-bearing Nostocales and Stigonematales together form a monophyletic group (Nostocales), and the Oscillatoriales-type and Chroococcales-type groups lacking heterocysts are not natural groups. The phylum Cyanobacteriota of the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) also includes two non-photosynthetic sister classes (Vampirovibrionia, also called Melainabacteria, and Sericytochromatia): oxygenic photosynthesis arose within this phylum, in the ancestor of the photosynthetic class Cyanobacteriia.

### 6.5.6. The Place of Cyanobacteria in the Evolution of the Plant Kingdom

The role of cyanobacteria in Earth history is irreplaceable. **Stromatolites**, the layered structures formed when microbial mats trap sediment, first appear in rocks about 3.5 billion years old and were most abundant in the Proterozoic; modern stromatolites are built mainly by cyanobacteria. Whether the oldest stromatolites and microfossils were formed by cyanobacteria is still debated, however: the "cyanobacterial fossils" about 3.1 billion years old from South Africa cited in Chinese textbooks are no longer considered cyanobacteria. Reliable cyanobacterial fossils (such as *Eoentophysalis*, about 1.9 billion years old, from the Belcher Group of Canada, resembling modern chroococcalean forms) appear about 2.0 billion years ago. The accumulation of free oxygen in the atmosphere, the **Great Oxidation Event** about 2.4–2.3 billion years ago, shows that oxygenic photosynthesis had appeared at the latest by then. The oxygenic photosynthesis of cyanobacteria changed the chemistry of the atmosphere and oceans and made aerobic respiration and the evolution of eukaryotes possible, and their nitrogen fixation supplied ecosystems with large amounts of combined nitrogen.

The relation of cyanobacteria to the plant kingdom is through the plastid. The photosynthesis of all eukaryotic algae and land plants comes from cyanobacteria: primary endosymbiosis made a cyanobacterium the plastid of the Archaeplastida, and secondary and tertiary endosymbiosis passed this plastid on to other algae (Section 6.2.6). The old view held that red algae evolved directly from cyanobacteria because both have phycobilins, no flagella and single thylakoids. We now know that these shared characters are cyanobacterial characters inherited through the plastid, and that the host cell of red algae is eukaryotic, with no direct relationship to cyanobacteria.

The effects of cyanobacteria on humans are both beneficial and harmful. Nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria are a natural nitrogen fertilizer of rice fields, and *Arthrospira*, *Nostoc sphaeroides* and *N. commune* are edible. But in eutrophic waters the blooms formed by *Microcystis*, *Dolichospermum*, *Aphanizomenon* and others deplete dissolved oxygen and produce toxins, and are one of the main threats to drinking water safety ([7.12.9](/7.12.9)).

## Summary

- Algae are autotrophic thallophytes without differentiation into root, stem and leaf, whose reproductive organs have no sterile jacket and whose zygote does not develop into an embryo (Definition 6.1.1). The term is a polyphyletic life-form name, covering several supergroups of the Bacteria and the eukaryotes. Algal body types include unicells, colonies, filaments, siphonous coenocytic tubes, membranous thalli, pseudoparenchymatous and parenchymatous thalli, which appear in parallel in different divisions.
- Divisions are delimited by cellular characters: pigments, storage products, cell walls, flagella (Definition 6.2.1), the nucleus (Definition 6.2.2), and the envelope and thylakoids of the chromatophore (Definition 6.2.3). Pigments and chromatophore membranes reflect the origin of the plastid: primary plastids have 2 membranes, secondary plastids 3–4, and cryptophytes and chlorarachniophytes retain a nucleomorph. The "three pigment lines" are lineages of plastids, not of hosts.
- Modes of reproduction: vegetative reproduction; asexual reproduction producing zoospores, aplanospores, autospores and akinetes (Definition 6.4.1); sexual reproduction from isogamy and anisogamy to oogamy (Definition 6.4.2), plus conjugation. The position of meiosis gives four life-cycle types: haplontic, diplontic, diplohaplontic (isomorphic or heteromorphic alternation of generations, Definition 6.4.3) and trigenetic.
- Cyanobacteria are prokaryotic oxygenic photosynthesizers: four-layered cell wall, gelatinous sheath, centroplasm and peripheral region, single thylakoids with phycobilisomes, pseudovacuoles, heterocysts and akinetes. They reproduce by cell division, hormogonia (Definition 6.5.1) and spores and have no sexual reproduction. The prochlorophytes are cyanobacteria that acquired chlorophyll *b* several times independently and are not the ancestors of chloroplasts.

## Questions

1. Why is "algae" said to be the name of a life form and not a taxon? Give two examples showing that algal bodies of the same form appear independently in different divisions.

   **Answer** Algae are defined as photosynthetic, without differentiation into root, stem and leaf, with reproductive organs lacking a sterile jacket and a zygote that does not develop into an embryo. They include the prokaryotic cyanobacteria and the eukaryotic algae of the Archaeplastida, Discoba, TSAR, Cryptista and Haptista, which do not form a monophyletic group (polyphyletic). Siphonous coenocytic tubes occur in xanthophytes (*Vaucheria*, *Botrydium*) and green algae (*Codium*); the tissue-differentiated body with holdfast, stipe and blade occurs in brown algae (kelp), red algae (the erect axes and rhizoids of *Gelidium*) and green algae; filaments and coenobia occur in cyanobacteria, green algae, xanthophytes and chrysophytes. These similarities result from parallel evolution.

2. Cryptophytes contain chlorophylls *a* and *c* and phycobiliproteins. Where should they be placed among the "three pigment lines"? How should they be explained from the viewpoint of endosymbiosis?

   **Answer** By pigments they resemble both the red line (phycobilins) and the yellow line (chlorophyll *c*); the old view treated them as a flagellate branch of the red line, or as a primitive type of the yellow line. From the viewpoint of endosymbiosis, the plastid of cryptophytes comes from an engulfed red alga: the 4 envelope layers, the remnant nucleus of the red alga (nucleomorph) between the second and third membranes, and the phycobiliproteins inherited from the red alga (though they no longer form phycobilisomes and lie in the thylakoid lumen); chlorophyll *c* is a feature shared by secondary plastids of red algal origin. Pigments reflect the origin of the plastid, and the host of cryptophytes belongs to the Cryptista, with no direct host relationship to red algae or dinoflagellates.

3. Compare isomorphic alternation of generations, heteromorphic alternation of generations and the trigenetic life history, giving one example of each, and explain the difference between "alternation of nuclear phases" and "alternation of generations".

   **Answer** In isomorphic alternation the gametophyte and sporophyte have the same form (*Ulva*, *Ectocarpus*); in heteromorphic alternation they differ, with the sporophyte dominant (kelp) or the gametophyte dominant (*Scytosiphon*); the trigenetic life history has three generations: the gametophyte ($n$), the carposporophyte ($2n$, parasitic on the female gametophyte) and the free-living tetrasporophyte ($2n$) (*Polysiphonia*). Alternation of nuclear phases is the alternation of haploid and diploid phases in the life history, which occurs wherever there is sexual reproduction; alternation of generations is the alternation of two (or three) kinds of multicellular plant bodies and occurs only in life histories with sporic meiosis. *Chlamydomonas* has alternation of nuclear phases (the zygote is diploid) but no alternation of generations.

4. How can *Oscillatoria*, *Nostoc*, *Anabaena* and *Stigonema* be distinguished?

   **Answer** *Oscillatoria* is an unbranched filament of a single row of cells, without heterocysts and akinetes, with an indistinct sheath and filaments that oscillate; the beaded filaments of *Nostoc* are embedded in a common sheath, with heterocysts and akinetes, forming macroscopic gelatinous colonies; *Anabaena* resembles *Nostoc* but has no common sheath, with filaments solitary or loosely aggregated and mostly planktonic; *Stigonema* has true branches produced by longitudinal cell division, often multiseriate, with a thick, firm sheath (the branches of *Tolypothrix* and *Scytonema* are false branches formed by hormogonia breaking out of the sheath).

5. The discovery of the prochlorophytes was once thought to support the idea that "green algae arose from prokaryotic algae containing chlorophyll *b*". Why is this inference no longer accepted?

   **Answer** The three prochlorophyte genera are scattered over different branches of the cyanobacterial tree, each most closely related to ordinary cyanobacteria, which shows that chlorophyll *b* appeared several times independently in cyanobacteria. Their chlorophyll *b*-binding proteins belong to the IsiA/Pcb family of cyanobacteria and are not homologous to the LHC of green plants. The chloroplasts of green plants are homologous with the plastids of red algae and glaucophytes (a single primary endosymbiosis) and originated from a cyanobacterial lineage close to *Gloeomargarita*, and the chlorophyll *b* of green algae appeared in the green lineage after the plastid was acquired. The prochlorophytes are therefore unrelated to the origin of chloroplasts, and the Prochlorophyta should be merged into the cyanobacteria.
