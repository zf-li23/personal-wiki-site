A lichen is a stable symbiosis of a fungus with a green alga or a cyanobacterium: the hyphae of the fungus surround the photosynthetic cells, and together they form a thallus that is relatively constant in form, structure, physiology and genetics, and different from either partner growing alone. This association allows lichens to grow on cliffs, bare rock, bark, deserts and polar tundra, where they form extensive communities in places where other plants cannot survive; with the lichen acids they secrete they slowly weather rock and so create soil for later plants. Traditional botany treated lichens as a separate division (Lichenes), together with the algae and fungi among the lower plants ([7.12.1](/7.12.1#sec-1.1)); today lichens are classified by their fungus, and "lichen" is a way of life, not a taxon.

This chapter treats the composition of lichens (Section 12.1), their form and structure (Section 12.2), reproduction (Section 12.3), classification (Section 12.4), and their role in nature and economic importance (Section 12.5). The definition of a lichen and the physiology of the symbiosis (nutrient exchange, drought tolerance) are in [7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.6.2) and [7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.3.2); the ascomycetes and basidiomycetes that make up lichens are in [7.12.11](/7.12.11), the cyanobacteria among the photobionts in [7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.5), and the green algae in [7.12.13](/7.12.13). This chapter concentrates on the botanical side: thallus form and structure, reproductive structures, representative genera and classification.

## 12.1. Composition of Lichens

### 12.1.1. Mycobiont and Photobiont

A **lichen** ([Definition 21.6.2](/7.11.21#def-21.6.2)) consists of two kinds of partners.

**Definition 12.1.1 (Mycobiont and Photobiont)** The fungal component of a lichen is called the *mycobiont*; the photosynthetic component, a green alga or a cyanobacterium, is called the **photobiont**. The form of a lichen is determined almost entirely by the mycobiont, and the scientific name of a lichen is the name of its mycobiont.

- Mycobiont: about 98% are ascomycetes, most of them species that form apothecia (the Discomycetes of Chinese textbooks), followed by species that form perithecia (Pyrenomycetes); a few are basidiomycetes (several genera of the Agaricales and the "Aphyllophorales" of Chinese textbooks); and some have never been seen to have a sexual stage (Deuteromycetes). There are about 20,000 lichen-forming fungal species, almost 40% of known ascomycetes (about 19,000 according to Lücking et al. 2017).
- Photobiont: about 40 genera are known; among them the green algae *Trebouxia* and *Trentepohlia* and the cyanobacterium *Nostoc* account for about 90% of all lichen photobionts. *Trebouxia* is the most common and occurs in most temperate lichens such as *Parmelia* and *Usnea*; the photobiont of *Cladonia*, formerly also placed in *Trebouxia*, is now separated as *Asterochloris*. *Trentepohlia* is a filamentous, aerial green alga that is orange because of its large amount of carotenoids, found in the tropics and in crustose lichens on bark (*Graphis*); *Nostoc* fixes nitrogen and is the photobiont of *Peltigera*, *Leptogium*, *Collema* and others. One photobiont per lichen is the most common case, but about 4% of lichens contain both a green alga and a cyanobacterium: the green alga lies in the algal layer, and the cyanobacterium is concentrated on the surface of the thallus or in special small warts inside it, the cephalodia, as in *Peltigera aphthosa* and *Lobaria pulmonaria*.

The relationship between the two partners is unusual. The hyphae twine tightly around the algal cells, and some press against, or even penetrate, the algal cell wall with short haustoria or appressoria. The algal cells are isolated from the environment and cannot take up water, mineral salts and carbon dioxide directly from outside, but depend on the hyphae for them; most of the organic matter made by algal photosynthesis is taken by the fungus, as sugar alcohols (ribitol, erythritol, sorbitol) from green algae and as glucose from cyanobacteria ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.3.2)). When the alga and fungus of a lichen are cultured separately, the alga grows and multiplies vigorously, while the fungus grows poorly for lack of nutrients: most photobionts can live independently, while the mycobiont depends almost entirely on the photobiont. Some therefore regarded the lichen as a kind of "controlled parasitism" of the fungus on the alga; today the symbiotic unit is generally judged by its adaptation as a whole and regarded as **mutualism**, in which the photobiont gains habitats it could not occupy alone.

### 12.1.2. A Third Partner?

The classical view of "one fungus plus one alga" has been challenged in recent years. Spribille et al. (2016) found in many lichens of the Parmeliaceae yeasts of the basidiomycete order Cyphobasidiales embedded in the cortex, with abundances that correlate with the chemistry of the thallus (for example the production of vulpinic acid); basidiomycete yeasts have since been detected in other lichens as well, and lichen thalli also generally contain specific bacterial communities (mainly alphaproteobacteria) and other parasitic or symbiotic fungi.

The level of evidence for these findings must be kept apart, however. The presence of the yeasts rests mainly on DNA sequencing and fluorescence in situ hybridization; they are of very low abundance in some lichens and undetectable in others, and it has not been shown experimentally that they are required for lichen formation, nor what role they play in the thallus. The present view is therefore that a lichen is a small ecosystem centered on one mycobiont and one (or two) photobionts and inhabited by many other microbes (Hawksworth and Grube 2020); "tripartite symbiosis" as a general rule has not been confirmed. These additional partners may partly explain why it is so difficult to resynthesize lichens with normal morphology in the laboratory from fungus and alga alone.

### 12.1.3. Physiology of Lichens

Most lichens love light and require fresh air. They generally grow slowly: crustose lichens less than 1 mm per year, foliose lichens a few millimeters, fruticose lichens up to several centimeters. Lichens tolerate long drought: when dry their water content is only 2–10% of dry weight, metabolism almost stops and they enter dormancy, and the cortex thickens and darkens, shielding them from strong light; after rain or in humid air they take up water within minutes and resume photosynthesis. Lichens are also very cold-tolerant, growing and reproducing in alpine zones, tundra and the polar regions where other plants cannot survive, and often forming boundless lichen communities: about 400 lichen species have been recorded in Antarctica, against only 2 flowering plants.

## 12.2. Form and Structure of Lichens

### 12.2.1. Growth Forms

The form of lichens can be divided basically into three types (Figure 1):

**Definition 12.2.1 (Crustose, Foliose and Fruticose Lichens)** A lichen whose thallus is crust-like, with hyphae tightly attached to the substrate (some even penetrating into it), and which is very difficult to detach, is called a *crustose lichen*; a lichen whose thallus is leaf-like with lobes around the margin, attached to the substrate by rhizines or an umbilicus on the lower surface, and easily detached, is called a *foliose lichen*; and a lichen whose thallus is erect or pendent and tree-like, attached to the substrate only at its base, is called a *fruticose lichen*.

| Growth form | Attachment | Internal structure | Representatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crustose | The whole lower surface adheres to or is embedded in the substrate (rock, bark); no lower cortex | Mostly homoiomerous or without distinct layering, the medulla directly in contact with the substrate | *Lecanora* (on rock), *Graphis* (on bark), *Rhizocarpon* |
| Squamulose | One edge of the small scales is attached; intermediate between crustose and foliose | Heteromerous, with an upper cortex, the lower cortex often lacking | *Squamarina*, the primary thallus of *Cladonia* |
| Foliose | By rhizines or a central umbilicus; easily detached | Heteromerous, dorsiventral, with upper and lower cortex | *Parmelia*, *Peltigera*, *Umbilicaria* (attached by an umbilicus) |
| Fruticose | Attached only at the base, erect or pendent | Heteromerous, radially symmetrical: a cortex surrounds the algal layer and medulla, with a central axis or hollow center | *Cladonia*, *Usnea*, *Ramalina* |

Crustose lichens account for about 80% of all lichens. The three types are not sharply distinct, and many transitional or intermediate types exist: *Buellia* ranges from crustose to squamulose; some lichens of the Caliciaceae spread laterally and the crustose structure gradually disappears, leaving a powdery form (leprose lichens; in *Lepraria* the whole thallus consists of soredia-like granules); the thalli of *Collema* and *Leptogium* become jelly-like when wet (gelatinous lichens); and there are filamentous lichens whose thallus consists of filamentous algae surrounded by hyphae. *Cladonia* has two kinds of thallus: the primary thallus of squamules lying on the ground, and the erect, cup-shaped or branched podetia that grow from it, which develop from the stalks of the ascocarps.

### 12.2.2. Structure of Lichens

A cross section of a foliose lichen can be divided from top to bottom into upper cortex, algal layer, medulla and lower cortex (Figure 2):

- Upper and lower cortex: made of tightly interwoven hyphae, often forming a pseudoparenchyma ([7.12.10](/7.12.10#sec-10.4.2)), with hyphal walls often gelatinized; the upper cortex often contains large amounts of pigments and lichen substances, which make lichens yellow, orange or brown and protect the algal cells from strong light and ultraviolet radiation.
- Algal layer: below the upper cortex, a layer formed by an aggregation of algal cells, interspersed with hyphae.
- Medulla: between the algal layer and the lower cortex, made of loose, thick-walled hyphae and a few algal cells, with many spaces between the hyphae; it is the place for water storage and gas exchange and makes up about two thirds of the thickness of the thallus.
- Attachment organs: the lower cortex bears rhizines, bundles of hyphae that fix the thallus to the substrate; *Umbilicaria* attaches to rock by a stout central umbilicus on the lower surface. *Peltigera* and others lack a lower cortex, and the lower surface has a network of hyphal veins.

**Definition 12.2.2 (Heteromerous and Homoiomerous Lichens)** A lichen whose algal cells are concentrated beneath the upper cortex in an algal layer, so that the thallus is clearly layered, is called a *heteromerous lichen*; a lichen whose algal cells are evenly distributed through the medulla, with no algal layer, is called a *homoiomerous lichen*.

Heteromerous lichens include *Physcia* and *Parmelia*; homoiomerous lichens include *Leptogium* and *Collema*, whose photobiont is *Nostoc*, with the algal filaments dispersed in a gelatinous matrix, so that the whole thallus swells into a jelly when wet. Foliose lichens are generally heteromerous; crustose lichens are mostly homoiomerous, but some are heteromerous, mostly without a lower cortex, and the medulla is in direct contact with the substrate, with some hyphae penetrating crevices in the rock (endolithic lichens) or into bark (endophloeodal lichens). All fruticose lichens are heteromerous, with a radial internal structure: upper and lower cortex merge into one dense cortex, the algal layer is thin and surrounds the central medulla; in *Usnea* the middle of the medulla has a cartilaginous central axis of longitudinally arranged hyphae, which can still be stretched after the outer layers are pulled apart; the podetia of *Cladonia* are hollow.

![Cross section of a lichen thallus (cell-level schematic). A: heteromerous lichen (for example *Parmelia*): the upper cortex is tightly interwoven hyphae (pseudoparenchyma); below it is the algal layer, in which green algal cells (*Trebouxia*) are surrounded by hyphae; the medulla consists of loose hyphae with many spaces; the lower cortex is dense and gives rise below to rhizines made of hyphal bundles. B: homoiomerous lichen (for example *Collema*): there is no algal layer; the beaded filaments of *Nostoc* are dispersed throughout the gelatinous matrix of the thallus, with hyphae running between them.](./plots/lichen-sections.py)

## 12.3. Reproduction of Lichens

### 12.3.1. Vegetative Reproduction

Vegetative reproduction is the commonest mode of reproduction in lichens. Its advantage is that mycobiont and photobiont are dispersed together and need not combine anew in every generation. The main modes are the following (Figure 3):

- Fragmentation: a thallus breaks into several lobes, each of which can develop into a new individual; in fruticose lichens that are brittle when dry (such as the reindeer lichens), trampling by animals and scattering by wind are the main means of spread.
- Soredia and isidia:

**Definition 12.3.1 (Soredia and Isidia)** The minute powdery granules that consist of a few algal cells wrapped in hyphae, have no cortex, and are released in masses from breaks in the surface of the thallus are called **soredia**; the small rod-like, wart-like or coralloid outgrowths formed by local protrusion of the upper surface of the thallus, which contain algal cells and hyphae and are covered by a cortex, are called **isidia**, and become propagules when they break off at the base.

Soredia are often produced in particular places on the thallus surface or lobe margins called soralia, which look like a powdery frost; soredia are minute (25–100 µm in diameter) and can be carried far by wind, water and insects. Isidia are protected by a cortex and are firm and smooth, cylindrical, wart-like or coralloid, single or branched; the coralloid isidia of Chinese textbooks are coralloid branched isidia. Isidia are larger and heavier than soredia and mostly break off and settle nearby, and they also increase the photosynthetic area of the thallus. There are also other propagules, such as lobules. The presence or absence, form and position of soredia and isidia are important characters in lichen classification and identification.

### 12.3.2. Sexual Reproduction

Sexual reproduction is carried out by the mycobiont of the lichen and produces ascospores or basidiospores; the former are ascolichens, which make up the great majority of lichen species, and the latter basidiolichens, which are few.

- Apothecia: the mycobiont of most lichens forms apothecia, in which the hymenium contains asci and ascospores, with paraphyses between the asci (Figure 3). Apothecia project from the surface of the lichen. By the structure of the margin they fall into two types: lecanorine apothecia have around the margin a ring (thalline margin) that contains algal cells and is like the thallus, as in *Lecanora*, *Parmelia* and *Usnea*; lecideine apothecia have a margin made of fungal hyphae only, with no algal cells, and are often black, as in *Lecidea* and *Rhizocarpon*. The apothecia of *Graphis* are narrow and curved like writing and are called lirellae. Lichen apothecia can live for many years, producing spores slowly and continuously.
- Perithecia: when the mycobiont is an ascomycete that forms perithecia, the perithecia are embedded in the lichen body or slightly exposed, with only the pore opening at the surface, as in *Verrucaria* (common on wet rock and on the seashore).
- Pycnidia: many lichens form embedded pycnidia on the thallus that produce minute conidia, which can act as spermatia or germinate into hyphae.

After the ascospores are released, they must land on suitable algal cells, so that the hyphae that germinate can surround the algal cells; hyphae and algal cells then divide repeatedly and form a new lichen body. If the spores land on a substrate without suitable algal cells, the hyphae can germinate, but soon starve. In lichens that reproduce by sexual spores the symbiosis therefore has to be established anew in every generation. The mycobiont of some lichens obtains a photobiont by "borrowing" from other lichens or from free-living algae; in a few (such as *Endocarpon*) the hymenium contains algal cells that are discharged together with the ascospores.

B: isidia: a local protrusion of the upper surface forms a small cylindrical outgrowth with a cortex, containing algal cells and hyphae, which becomes a propagule when it breaks off at the base. C: longitudinal section of a lecanorine apothecium: the hymenium consists of asci (with ascospores) and paraphyses, below it is the subhymenium of hyphae, and around it is the thalline margin containing algal cells.](./plots/lichen-propagules.py)

## 12.4. Classification of Lichens

### 12.4.1. Traditional Classification

Lichens were long treated as a separate group alongside fungi and algae. Chinese textbooks follow the tradition from Zahlbruckner (1926) and divide the Lichenes into three classes, which are then divided into orders by the group of the mycobiont and the type of ascocarp:

- Ascolichenes: the mycobiont is an ascomycete; 99% of all lichens. By ascocarp they are divided into those with apothecia (discolichens) and those with perithecia (pyrenolichens).
- Basidiolichenes: the mycobiont is mostly a fungus of the Corticiaceae (Aphyllophorales), followed by fungi of the Tricholomataceae (Agaricales; *Omphalina*, whose lichenized species are now placed in *Lichenomphalia*) and of the Clavariaceae; the photobiont is a cyanobacterium. They are distributed mainly in the tropics, as in *Cora*, which grows on soil or trees, looks like a corticioid fungus and attaches to the substrate by lateral rhizines; in vertical section it has three layers, the uppermost a felty layer of loose hyphae, the middle an algal layer and the lowest a denser felty layer; the lower surface of the thallus has arc-shaped projections in concentric rings, the hymenophore, whose surface is the hymenium, with four terminal basidiospores on each basidium. Molecular studies show that what used to be treated as one species, *Cora pavonia*, in fact contains over a hundred species.
- Deuterolichenes (also called Lichenes imperfecti): lichens that by thallus structure and chemical reactions belong to the ascomycetes but in which asci and ascospores have never been seen, that is, asexual lichens; some species have pycnidia. An example is *Thamnolia*: the thallus is filamentous, branched, white to grayish white and grows on the ground; it turns pale yellow or lemon yellow with potassium hydroxide solution; in Chinese medicine it is called snow tea.

### 12.4.2. The Present Classification: Multiple Origins of Lichenization

The 1950 Stockholm International Botanical Congress ruled that the name of a lichen applies to its mycobiont; since then lichens have been incorporated into the classification of fungi and placed in classes, orders and families according to the relationships of the mycobiont, while the photobionts have their own names. Molecular phylogeny shows that lichen-forming fungi are scattered across many classes of the Ascomycota and in the Agaricomycetes of the Basidiomycota (Figure 4); that is, the way of life of forming lichens with algae arose independently many times in the fungi (at least five times, Gargas et al. 1995) and was lost again in some lineages. Lutzoni et al. (2001) even argued that the ancestors of many nonlichenized ascomycetes, such as *Penicillium* and *Aspergillus*, were lichenized.

According to the tally of Lücking et al. (2017), lichen-forming fungi belong to about eight classes of the Ascomycota and to the Agaricomycetes of the Basidiomycota, as follows:

| Group | Extent of lichenization | Representatives |
|---|---|---|
| Lecanoromycetes | Almost entirely lichenized, about three quarters of known lichens | Lecanorales: Parmeliaceae (*Parmelia*, *Usnea*, *Ramalina*, *Evernia*, *Cetraria*), Cladoniaceae (*Cladonia*), Lecanoraceae; Peltigerales (*Peltigera*, *Lobaria*, *Collema*, *Leptogium*); Umbilicariales (*Umbilicaria*); Ostropales (*Graphis*); Teloschistales (*Xanthoria*); *Rhizocarpon* |
| Arthoniomycetes | Mostly lichenized | *Roccella* (source of litmus) |
| Eurotiomycetes | Verrucariales lichenized; other orders (*Penicillium*, *Aspergillus*) not | *Verrucaria*, *Endocarpon* |
| Lichinomycetes | Entirely lichenized, with cyanobacterial photobionts | *Lichina*, *Peltula* |
| Dothideomycetes and others | A few lineages lichenized | Some tropical foliicolous lichens |
| Agaricomycetes (Basidiomycota) | A few lineages | *Lichenomphalia*, *Cora*, *Dictyonema* |

![Distribution of lichenization in the Ascomycota and Basidiomycota (cladogram; branch lengths are arbitrary; relationships among classes follow the phylogeny of Schoch et al. 2009, lichenized groups follow Lücking et al. 2017). Asterisk: classes in which all or almost all species are lichenized; dot: classes in which only a few lineages are lichenized; no mark: no lichens. Lichenized classes are scattered across the tree, showing that lichenization is a way of life acquired independently many times (and lost many times). The Lecanoromycetes, Arthoniomycetes and Lichinomycetes do not form a monophyletic group.](./plots/lichenization-tree.py)

The classification into Ascolichenes, Basidiolichenes and Deuterolichenes is therefore no longer used: the Ascolichenes are polyphyletic, the Basidiolichenes also contain several independent lineages, and the lichens of the Deuterolichenes are placed by DNA sequence in the appropriate ascomycete families (for example, *Thamnolia* in the Icmadophilaceae in the Lecanoromycetes). The classification of lichens still depends heavily on chemical characters: color reactions with potassium hydroxide (K), sodium hypochlorite (C) and *p*-phenylenediamine (P), and detection of lichen substances by thin-layer chromatography, are the basic methods of identifying lichens.

### 12.4.3. Representative Genera

- *Usnea*: fruticose, erect, semi-erect to pendent; the branches are cylindrical to angular, with a cartilaginous central axis; the apothecia are lecanorine, often with small cilia-like spines on the margin; the asci contain eight colorless, one-celled, elliptical spores; the thallus contains usnic acid and is yellow-green. *Dolichousnea longissima* (*Usnea longissima* of Chinese textbooks, now a separate genus) hangs from the branches of spruce and fir, up to several meters long, and is widespread; in Chinese medicine it is called songluo or "Laojun's beard."
- *Cladonia*: the primary thallus is squamulose, and the secondary podetia are single or tree-like with many branches, hollow, columnar, cup-shaped or branched, with the tips expanded or not; the hyphae in the podetial cortex are arranged perpendicular to the podetium; the apothecia are waxy-disc-shaped, brown or red. *C. rangiferina* (the "reindeer moss") forms extensive communities on arctic and alpine tundra and is the main winter food of reindeer.
- *Parmelia*: foliose, rather thin, with no expanded cavities between the upper and lower cortex; the upper surface is gray, gray-green, yellow-green to brown, with or without soredia or isidia; the lower surface is pale, brown to black, with rhizines; the apothecia are scattered on the upper surface, with eight colorless, one-celled spores in each ascus. The Parmeliaceae is one of the largest families of lichens.
- *Peltigera*: foliose, large and soft, without a lower cortex, the lower surface with a network of hyphal veins and rhizines; the photobiont is *Nostoc* (or a green alga plus *Nostoc* in cephalodia) and fixes nitrogen; the apothecia are borne at the tips of the lobes and are hemiangiocarpic.
- *Graphis*: crustose, on bark, superficial or immersed, without a cortex or with a weak one; the apothecia are linear or curved like writing, sunk into the substrate surface or projecting; the photobiont is *Trentepohlia*; the spores are colorless, darkening with maturity, long elliptical or sausage-shaped, two- to many-celled.
- *Rhizocarpon*: crustose, on siliceous rock; the thallus of the map lichen (*R. geographicum*) is yellow-green, divided into many small patches by the black prothallus, with adjacent individuals bounded by black lines like national borders on a map; it grows extremely slowly and steadily and is used to date newly exposed rock surfaces such as moraines and rockfalls (lichenometry).
- *Umbilicaria*: foliose, round, leathery, attached to rock by an umbilicus at the center of the lower surface; rock tripe (*U. esculenta*) grows on alpine rocks and is edible, a specialty of Huangshan in Anhui and other places.
- *Lecanora*: crustose, on rock or bark, with lecanorine apothecia, from which this type is named.

## 12.5. Role of Lichens in Nature and Their Economic Importance

### 12.5.1. Role in Nature

- Pioneer organisms: lichens growing on cliffs and rock secrete lichen acids (mostly phenolic depsides, depsidones and oxalic acid) that chelate metal ions in the rock; together with the mechanical action of wetting and drying after hyphae enter crevices, this causes the rock surface to crack and break up gradually. Lichens also trap dust and organic matter and gradually turn the rock surface into soil, creating conditions for the later establishment of mosses and higher plants, so lichens are regarded as the vanguard of plant distribution ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)). The secondary metabolites secreted by lichens (lichen substances) sometimes exceed 40% of the dry weight of the thallus; more than 1000 are known, and most occur only in lichens.
- Nitrogen fixation: lichens containing cyanobacteria supply fixed nitrogen to the soil and are an important source of nitrogen in the old-growth forests of the Pacific coast of North America, some tropical forests, deserts and tundra; biological soil crusts of deserts and arid regions (made of lichens, mosses and cyanobacteria) stabilize the soil and reduce erosion.
- Indicator organisms: lichens have no mechanism for excreting what they absorb, so when they take up nutrients from air and rain they also take up pollutants; sulfur dioxide destroys the chlorophyll of the photobiont, so many lichens are very sensitive to it. Lichens are absent near industrial cities (the "lichen desert"), and the species composition of the lichen community can be used to estimate the concentration of sulfur dioxide in the air (the scale of Hawksworth and Rose 1970). With falling sulfur dioxide emissions, lichens in European cities have been recovering in recent decades, and lichens are now used more to monitor nitrogen deposition (nitrophilous *Xanthoria* increases while nitrogen-sensitive species decline) and heavy metals. Lichens also accumulate radioactive material: after atmospheric nuclear tests and the Chernobyl accident, radioactive cesium accumulated in the lichens of the northern European tundra and passed through reindeer into humans.

### 12.5.2. Economic Importance

- Food and fodder: lichen bodies contain lichenin and other carbohydrates, and many species are edible, such as *Umbilicaria esculenta*, Iceland moss (*Cetraria islandica*) and some *Cladonia*; the extensive lichen communities of the arctic and alpine tundra are the main fodder of reindeer and other animals; some northern European countries once extracted starch, glucose and alcohol from lichens.
- Medicine: China has long used lichens as medicinal materials, and *Usnea*, *Cladonia*, *Umbilicaria* and *Thamnolia* (snow tea) are long-established Chinese medicines. Usnic acid is antibacterial and has been made into antibacterial preparations for external use; *Lobaria pulmonaria* was once used to treat lung disease; some lichen polysaccharides and lichen substances are being studied as antitumor agents.
- Perfumes and dyes: extracts of oakmoss (*Evernia prunastri*) and of *Ramalina* are fixatives for perfumes; natural dyes were formerly extracted from many lichens (Scottish Harris tweed was originally dyed with lichens) and have rarely been used since the appearance of coal-tar dyes. Litmus paper, often used in chemistry, was formerly made with the litmus pigment extracted from *Roccella tinctoria* and has largely been replaced by synthetic substitutes.
- Harm: the crowns of spruce and fir in forests are often hung with *Usnea* and other lichens, which affect the light and gas exchange of the trees and provide shelter for pests; some lichens that grow on tea and citrus trees also impede the growth of the trees.

## Summary

- A lichen is a stable symbiosis of a mycobiont and a photobiont (Definition 12.1.1); its form is determined by the mycobiont, and its scientific name is that of the mycobiont. About 98% of mycobionts are ascomycetes, and the photobionts are mainly *Trebouxia*, *Trentepohlia* and *Nostoc*. Lichen thalli also contain basidiomycete yeasts and bacteria, but there is no evidence that they are required partners.
- The growth forms are crustose, foliose and fruticose (Definition 12.2.1), with squamulose, powdery and gelatinous intermediate types; the structure is heteromerous or homoiomerous (Definition 12.2.2).
- Vegetative reproduction is by fragmentation, soredia and isidia (Definition 12.3.1), dispersing the partners together; sexual reproduction is carried out by the mycobiont alone, producing apothecia, perithecia or basidiocarps, and the photobiont has to be acquired anew in every generation.
- Lichenization arose independently many times in the fungi; lichens are classified by their mycobiont, mainly in the Lecanoromycetes; the old classes Ascolichenes, Basidiolichenes and Deuterolichenes are no longer used.
- Lichens are pioneer organisms on rock, taking part in rock weathering and soil formation, and cyanobacterial lichens fix nitrogen; they are sensitive to sulfur dioxide and are indicators of air quality.

## Questions

1. Why is a "lichen" not a taxonomic group? Why is the scientific name of a lichen the name of its mycobiont?

   **Answer** A lichen is a symbiosis of a fungus with an alga or cyanobacterium, not a group of organisms that evolved from a common ancestor: the fungi that form lichens are scattered across many classes of the Ascomycota and in the Basidiomycota, and the lichen way of life arose independently many times in the fungi and was lost again in some lineages, so "lichen" denotes a way of life and not a monophyletic group. The form, structure, reproductive structures and most lichen substances of the thallus are determined by the mycobiont, sexual reproduction is carried out by the mycobiont alone, and the same photobiont can form lichens of entirely different form with many fungi; since 1950 the code has therefore ruled that the name of a lichen applies to its mycobiont, and lichens are classified as fungi.

2. Compare the structure of heteromerous and homoiomerous lichens, and give an example of each.

   **Answer** A heteromerous lichen is clearly layered: an upper cortex (tightly interwoven hyphae), an algal layer (algal cells concentrated beneath the upper cortex), a medulla (loose hyphae with many spaces) and a lower cortex bearing rhizines, as in *Parmelia* and *Physcia*; foliose and fruticose lichens are mostly heteromerous, and in fruticose lichens the layering is radial. A homoiomerous lichen has no algal layer, and the algal cells (often the filaments of *Nostoc*) are evenly distributed through the whole thallus, often embedded in a gelatinous matrix, as in *Leptogium* and *Collema*; crustose lichens are mostly homoiomerous or indistinctly layered.

3. What is the difference between soredia and isidia? Why is vegetative reproduction especially important for lichens?

   **Answer** Soredia are minute granules of a few algal cells wrapped in hyphae, without a cortex, released in masses from breaks in the upper cortex (soralia), light and easily carried far by wind; isidia are outgrowths of the upper surface of the thallus, with a cortex and containing algal cells and hyphae, larger, and mostly settling nearby after breaking off at the base, and they also increase the photosynthetic area. Vegetative reproduction (including fragmentation) disperses mycobiont and photobiont together, so a new individual need not reestablish the symbiosis; ascospores or basidiospores contain only the fungus and after germination must happen to meet a suitable algal cell to form a lichen, or they starve, so the chance of success is small.

4. How should the discovery of a "third partner in lichens" be assessed?

   **Answer** Spribille et al. (2016) found basidiomycete yeasts in the cortex of many lichens of the Parmeliaceae, with abundances correlated with the content of lichen substances, and basidiomycete yeasts and specific bacterial communities have since been detected in other lichens. This shows that a lichen is a small ecosystem containing many microbes, which may partly explain why normal lichens are so hard to synthesize in the laboratory from fungus and alga alone. But the evidence is mainly correlative, from DNA sequencing and in situ hybridization: the yeasts are of very low abundance or undetectable in many lichens, it has not been shown experimentally that they are required for lichen formation, and their function is unknown. One can therefore say that lichen thalli generally harbor other fungi and bacteria, but "tripartite symbiosis" as a general rule has not been confirmed.

5. Why can lichens be pioneer organisms on rock, and why can they be used to monitor air pollution?

   **Answer** Lichens tolerate drought, cold and strong light: when dry their metabolism stops without killing them, and after wetting they resume photosynthesis within minutes; the photobiont supplies organic matter and the mycobiont absorbs and retains water and minerals, and cyanobacterial lichens also fix nitrogen, so they can grow on bare rock without soil. The lichen acids they secrete chelate and dissolve minerals of the rock, wetting and drying after hyphae enter crevices break up the rock, and lichens trap dust and organic matter, so that soil gradually forms and conditions are created for mosses and higher plants. On the other hand, lichens absorb nutrients from air and rain and have no mechanism for excreting what they absorb; sulfur dioxide destroys the chlorophyll of the photobiont, so many lichens are sensitive to it, and the species composition of the lichen community reflects the level of sulfur dioxide and nitrogen deposition in the air.
