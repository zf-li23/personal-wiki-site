*Botany* is the science of plant form and structure, classification and phylogeny, physiology, development, and the relationships between plants and their environment. The word "plant" itself needs a definition first. In the two-kingdom system the plant kingdom includes nearly every organism that does not move, from cyanobacteria and mushrooms to pines; present-day systematics recognizes only monophyletic groups, and "plant" has at least three circumscriptions of different breadth. This chapter begins with these circumscriptions and with the place of the algae, fungi and lichens that botany has traditionally studied on the **tree of life**. It then introduces plant growth forms and diversity, the roles of plants in nature and in human life, the branches and a short history of botany, and finally the organization of the course. The next chapter covers the tools of taxonomy (ranks, nomenclature, identification and phylogenetic classification); later chapters treat plant cells and tissues, spore-bearing plants, the organs of seed plants, reproduction, and classification. The general concepts of the tree of life and phylogeny were covered in evolutionary biology ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)), and the cell and molecular biology of protists and fungi in microbiology ([7.11.20](/7.11.20), [7.11.21](/7.11.21)); here we take only the botanical viewpoint.

## 1.1. What Is a Plant

### 1.1.1. The Plant Kingdom in the Two-Kingdom System

In the eighteenth century Linnaeus divided living things into two kingdoms: animals, which move and ingest food, and plants, which are fixed in place and do not ingest food. By this criterion bacteria, fungi, all kinds of algae and lichens belong to the plant kingdom, so the scope of botany was very broad. Chinese textbooks long used the two-kingdom system and divided the plant kingdom into 16 phyla. Eight of them (euglenoids, green algae, charophytes, golden algae, dinoflagellates, brown algae, red algae and blue-green algae) are together called **algae**; bacteria, slime molds and fungi make up three phyla of "fungi" in the broad sense; there is also a phylum of lichens; and four phyla are bryophytes, ferns, gymnosperms and angiosperms. Gymnosperms and angiosperms are often combined as the seed plants, and ferns, gymnosperms and angiosperms as the vascular plants.

Later multi-kingdom systems removed one non-plant group after another (the grounds of each system are given in [7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.7.3)), and the plant kingdom shrank accordingly:

| System | Plant kingdom includes | Groups removed from the plant kingdom |
|---|---|---|
| Two kingdoms (Linnaeus, 18th century) | Algae, fungi, lichens, bryophytes, ferns, seed plants | none |
| Three kingdoms (Haeckel, 1866) | Multicellular plants (the exact scope was revised several times) | Unicellular organisms (kingdom Protista) |
| Four kingdoms (Copeland, 1938, 1956) | Mainly green algae and land plants | Bacteria and cyanobacteria (Monera), most other algae and fungi (Protista) |
| Five kingdoms (Whittaker, 1969) | Red algae, brown algae, green algae, bryophytes, vascular plants | Fungi and slime molds (Fungi), unicellular algae (Protista), bacteria and cyanobacteria (Monera) |
| Five kingdoms (Margulis, 1974) | Bryophytes and vascular plants | All algae placed in Protista |
| Six kingdoms (Chinese scholars, 1970s) | As in the five-kingdom systems | A separate kingdom of acellular organisms for viruses and viroids |

Each multi-kingdom system gives its own "plant kingdom", but most of these kingdoms are grades of evolution: groups placed in one kingdom need not share a most recent common ancestor, and the boundaries between kingdoms were repeatedly redrawn. Phylogenetic studies finally showed that the traditional "plants" are scattered over many branches of the **tree of life** (Fig. 1.1.1).

### 1.1.2. Three Modern Circumscriptions of "Plant"

Present-day systematics names only monophyletic groups, so the word "plant" corresponds to three nested clades, and which one is meant in the literature depends on the author (Fig. 1.1.2).

**Definition 1.1.1 (Archaeplastida, Viridiplantae, Streptophyta and Embryophyta)** **Archaeplastida** (also called Plantae in the broad sense) are the eukaryotes whose plastids arose directly from a **primary endosymbiosis**, the engulfment of a cyanobacterium; they include the glaucophytes, the red algae and the green plants, and their plastids are surrounded by two membranes. **Viridiplantae** are the clade of Archaeplastida that contain chlorophylls *a* and *b* and store starch inside the plastid; they comprise the *Chlorophyta* (green algae) and the Streptophyta. **Streptophyta** are the clade of Viridiplantae composed of the charophyte and other freshwater algae together with the land plants. **Embryophyta** are the land plants, the clade of Streptophyta in which the zygote develops within the mother plant into a multicellular embryo nourished by the mother; they include the bryophytes, lycophytes, ferns and seed plants.

Each of the three circumscriptions has its uses. **Archaeplastida** stresses the origin of the plastid: red algae and glaucophytes share primary plastids with the green plants, whereas the plastids of other plastid-bearing eukaryotic algae (brown algae, diatoms, dinoflagellates and others) arose by **secondary endosymbiosis**, the engulfment of a red or green alga ([7.10.28](/7.10.28)). The monophyly of this group is broadly supported by molecular phylogenetics, although the position of cryptophytes and some other groups is still disputed. **Viridiplantae** are the "plants" of systematic works such as Judd et al., *Plant Systematics*; besides chlorophylls *a* and *b* and plastid starch, their synapomorphies include a star-shaped structure in the flagellar transition zone. **Embryophytes** are the "plants" of Raven, *Biology of Plants*, and of most botany textbooks, that is, plants in the narrow sense: they have maternal tissue that protects the embryo, multicellular gametangia (antheridia and **archegonia**) and sporangia enclosed by a layer of sterile cells, spores whose walls contain sporopollenin, and a **cuticle** that prevents water loss, and their life cycle alternates between multicellular haploid and diploid generations (**alternation of generations**). The oldest fossil spores of land plants date from the Middle Ordovician, about 470 million years ago.

### 1.1.3. The Subject Matter of Botany

This course follows the botanical tradition: besides the embryophytes it also covers algae, fungi and lichens. The reason is more than habit. The form, life cycles and classification of these organisms were long studied by botanists, the large herbaria hold them, and their names are governed by the same International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants ([7.12.2](/7.12.2)); moreover, the diversity of algae is the necessary background for understanding the origin of land plants, plastid evolution and life-cycle types. Their systematic positions are as follows ([7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.7.3), [7.11.20](/7.11.20)):

| Traditional group | Modern placement (supergroup) | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| Blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) | Domain Bacteria | **Prokaryotes**, ancestors of chloroplasts ([7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.4)) |
| Red algae, glaucophytes | Archaeplastida | Primary plastids |
| Green algae, charophytes | Archaeplastida: Viridiplantae | Belong to the green plants together with land plants |
| Brown algae, diatoms, golden algae, yellow-green algae | SAR: stramenopiles | Secondary plastids, derived from a red alga |
| Dinoflagellates | SAR: alveolates | Plastids repeatedly gained and lost |
| Euglenoids | Discoba | Secondary plastids, derived from a green alga |
| Cryptophytes, haptophytes | Cryptista, Haptista | Secondary plastids, derived from a red alga |
| Oomycetes (water molds) | SAR: stramenopiles | Allied with brown algae and diatoms; not fungi |
| Slime molds | Amorphea: amoebozoans | Together with animals and fungi in Amorphea |
| Fungi | Amorphea: opisthokonts | Sister group of animals |
| Lichens | none | Symbioses of a **fungus** with a green alga or cyanobacterium, classified by the fungus |

Thus "algae" and "fungi" are not natural groups. When this course treats them, morphology, representative genera, life cycles and classification are given at the level of detail customary in botany, while organelles and molecular mechanisms are linked to the corresponding chapters of microbiology and cell biology.

### 1.1.4. Traditional Groupings and Monophyletic Groups

Textbooks often divide plants into two large classes. These names are still in use, but one must know how they relate to clades.

**Definition 1.1.2 (Algae)** **Algae** is the collective name for autotrophic organisms that contain chlorophyll *a*, carry out oxygenic photosynthesis, have a body without differentiation into root, stem and leaf, and have reproductive organs that are mostly unicellular rather than enclosed by a layer of sterile cells; most are aquatic. Cyanobacteria are prokaryotes and the rest are eukaryotes.

**Definition 1.1.3 (Vascular Plants, Seed Plants and Cryptogams)** **Vascular plants** (tracheophytes) are embryophytes that have vascular tissue composed of lignified tracheids and other cells and a well-developed, free-living sporophyte; they include lycophytes, ferns and seed plants. **Seed plants** (spermatophytes, also called flowering plants in the broad traditional sense) are vascular plants that reproduce by seeds; they comprise the gymnosperms and the angiosperms. **Cryptogams** (spore-bearing plants) is the collective name for plants that produce no seeds and reproduce by spores; they include algae, fungi, lichens, bryophytes, lycophytes and ferns.

"Reproducing by spores" cannot serve as the definition of cryptogams, because seed plants also produce spores (microspores in pollen grains, megaspores in ovules); the spores simply do not leave the mother plant. The difference lies in whether a seed is formed.

**Definition 1.1.4 (Lower Plants, Higher Plants and Archegoniates)** **Lower plants** (non-embryophytes) are plants without an embryo in their development, namely algae, fungi and lichens; **higher plants** are plants with an embryo in their development, that is, the embryophytes. **Archegoniates** are plants whose female reproductive organ is an archegonium: the bryophytes, ferns and gymnosperms (the archegonium of gymnosperms is more or less reduced).

Other common groupings and their phylogenetic status are:

| Grouping | Includes | Criterion | Relation to clades |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower plants / higher plants | Algae, fungi, lichens / bryophytes, ferns, gymnosperms, angiosperms | Absence or presence of an embryo | Higher plants are the embryophytes, monophyletic; lower plants are polyphyletic |
| Cryptogams / seed plants | See Definition 1.1.3 | Whether seeds are formed | Seed plants are monophyletic; cryptogams are polyphyletic |
| Archegoniates | Bryophytes, ferns, gymnosperms | Female reproductive organ is an archegonium (more or less reduced in gymnosperms) | Paraphyletic: angiosperms are also descendants but lack archegonia |
| Vascular plants | Lycophytes, ferns, seed plants | Presence of vascular tissue | Monophyletic |
| Thallophytes / cormophytes | Algae, fungi, lichens, bryophytes, gametophytes of ferns / sporophytes of vascular plants | Whether the body has true roots, stems and leaves | Thallophytes are not a group but a body plan; the "stems" and "leaves" of mosses are not homologous with those of vascular plants |
| Algae / fungi | See the previous section | Autotrophic / heterotrophic | Both polyphyletic |

The general differences between lower and higher plants can be summarized as follows:

| | Lower plants | Higher plants |
|---|---|---|
| Embryo | Absent | Present |
| Root, stem, leaf | Absent | Mostly present (except bryophytes) |
| Tissue differentiation | Mostly absent | Present |
| Reproductive organs | Mostly unicellular | Multicellular, enclosed by a layer of sterile cells |
| Habitat | Mostly aquatic | Mostly terrestrial |
| Alternation of generations | Mostly absent, or isomorphic or heteromorphic | Always present, heteromorphic |

These names describe combinations of characters acquired step by step in evolution; "lower" and "higher" indicate only the order of appearance and the complexity of structure, not that any living group is better than another: every living group has evolved for the same length of time ([7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.2.2)). This course uses these names when needed, but classification always follows clades.

## 1.2. Plant Types and Distribution

### 1.2.1. Growth Forms

Seed plants are divided into several growth forms according to the texture and lifespan of the stem (Fig. 1.2.1). A growth form reflects the outward shape and way of life of a plant, not its relationships: one family, even one genus, can contain both trees and herbs (for example Fabaceae and Polygonaceae).

**Definition 1.2.1 (Woody Plants and Herbs)** **Woody plants** are perennial plants whose stems have well-developed xylem and much lignified tissue and are hard in texture; by the form of the stem they are divided into **trees**, with a conspicuous, erect trunk that branches at some height above the ground to form a crown, such as magnolia (*Magnolia*), paulownia (*Paulownia*), poplar (*Populus*), elm (*Ulmus*), pine (*Pinus*), dawn redwood (*Metasequoia glyptostroboides*) and eucalyptus (*Eucalyptus*); **shrubs**, with no conspicuous trunk and several stems arising together near the ground, such as Japanese spindle (*Euonymus japonicus*), winter jasmine (*Jasminum nudiflorum*), Chinese redbud (*Cercis chinensis*), rose of Sharon (*Hibiscus syriacus*), heavenly bamboo (*Nandina domestica*) and tea (*Camellia sinensis*); and **subshrubs**, which resemble shrubs but are woody only at the base of the stem, the aboveground parts mostly dying back in winter, such as St. John's wort (*Hypericum*) and some species of *Artemisia*. **Herbs** are plants whose stems have poorly developed xylem, little lignified tissue and a soft texture; by lifespan they are divided into **annual plants**, which complete the whole life cycle from seed germination to flowering, fruiting and death within one growing season, such as rice, maize, sorghum, soybean, cucumber and sunflower; **biennial plants**, which form only vegetative organs in the first year and flower, fruit and die in the second year after overwintering, such as Chinese cabbage, carrot, spinach, onion and beet; and **perennial plants**, whose aboveground parts die at the end of each growing season while the underground parts (roots or underground stems) live for many years, such as mint, chrysanthemum, iris and lily.

**Definition 1.2.2 (Vines)** **Vines** are plants with slender stems that cannot stand erect and grow along the ground or climb on other objects; they are herbaceous (morning glory, cypress vine) or woody (grape, wisteria).

The difference between trees and shrubs lies in growth form, not in internal structure; the same species can grow as a tree or a shrub in different habitats (for example the dwarfed trees of high mountains). There are also transitions between biennials and annuals: winter wheat, sown in autumn and harvested in the following summer, spans two calendar years but passes through only one growing season, and is generally regarded as a winter annual. The ways in which vines climb (twining stems, tendrils, adhesive discs, hooks and thorns, aerial roots) are treated in the chapter on modified vegetative organs ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)); the Raunkiær life forms, which are classified by the position of the dormant buds, belong to plant ecology ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)).

### 1.2.2. Species Numbers and Distribution

According to the count of Christenhusz and Byng (2016), about 374,000 plant species have been described and accepted, of which about 308,000 are vascular plants and about 295,000 are angiosperms, the great majority (Fig. 1.2.2); about 2,000 new species are described each year. The count excludes fungi: about 120,000 fungal species have been described, and the estimated actual number exceeds 2 million (Hawksworth and Lücking, 2017); lichens number about 20,000.

![Numbers of accepted species in the major plant groups (logarithmic scale; data: Christenhusz and Byng 2016). "Algae" is the broad count of that paper, including green algae, red algae and other photosynthetic eukaryotic algae. Angiosperms, about 295,000 species, outnumber all other groups together by an order of magnitude; the three groups of bryophytes together have about 22,000 species, ferns and lycophytes about 12,000, and gymnosperms only about 1,000](./plots/species-numbers.py)

Plants live in almost every part of the Earth's surface: from the equator to the poles, from sea level to above the snow line, from deserts to deep water. Some cyanobacteria still grow in hot springs up to about 73 °C, snow algae multiply on the surface of snow and stain it red, and red algae live at the greatest depths on the sea floor that receive light. Seed plants are the group with the most species, the widest distribution, the most complex structure and the closest relation to humans; crops, forest trees, horticultural plants and the overwhelming majority of economic plants are seed plants, which is why plant morphology and anatomy deal mainly with them. China is one of the countries richest in plant diversity: *Flora Reipublicae Popularis Sinicae* records 301 families, 3,408 genera and 31,142 species of vascular plants.

## 1.3. The Roles of Plants in Nature

### 1.3.1. Photosynthesis and Mineralization

Green plants use **photosynthesis** to synthesize organic matter from carbon dioxide and water, converting light energy to chemical energy and releasing oxygen. The net primary production of photosynthetic organisms is about 105 billion tonnes of carbon per year, about half on land and half from marine phytoplankton (Field et al., 1998). These three effects, the conversion of inorganic matter into organic matter, the storage of solar energy and the replenishment of atmospheric oxygen, are the basis of the existence of almost all other organisms: animals, fungi and most bacteria feed directly or indirectly on the organic matter plants synthesize; the roughly 21% of oxygen in the atmosphere comes from the long accumulation by photosynthetic organisms; coal was formed from the remains of ancient plants (especially of the Carboniferous) buried and transformed underground, and algae and other organisms also contributed to the formation of petroleum and natural gas. The mechanism of photosynthesis is treated in plant physiology.

With synthesis alone and no breakdown, the elements would be locked up in organisms. Organic matter is broken down partly by the respiration of organisms and partly by bacteria and fungi decomposing the remains of animals and plants; this process is called mineralization: complex organic matter is broken down into simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide, water and ammonium salts, which plants can use again. Photosynthesis and mineralization, synthesis and breakdown, keep the cycling of matter in nature going.

### 1.3.2. Plants in the Carbon and Nitrogen Cycles

In the **carbon cycle**, plants are the main route by which atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$ is fixed into organic carbon; respiration, decomposition, combustion and volcanic activity return carbon to the atmosphere. Since the Industrial Revolution the burning of fossil fuels has raised the atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$ concentration from about 280 ppm to more than 420 ppm in the mid-2020s, so the carbon-sequestering capacity of forests and soils has become an important part of the climate problem.

In the **nitrogen cycle**, atmospheric $\ce{N2}$ (about 78%) cannot be used directly by plants; only certain bacteria and cyanobacteria can fix it into nitrogen compounds. Plants take up ammonium and nitrate from the soil and synthesize proteins and nucleic acids, and animals obtain nitrogen from plants; decomposition of remains (ammonification) releases ammonium, nitrifying bacteria oxidize it to nitrate, and denitrifying bacteria reduce nitrate to $\ce{N2}$, which returns to the atmosphere. Legumes and some other plants form **root nodules** with nitrogen-fixing bacteria and obtain fixed nitrogen directly. The processes of the various cycles and the roles of microorganisms are given in [7.11.30](/7.11.30), and nodule symbiosis in [7.11.31](/7.11.31).

### 1.3.3. Environmental Protection and Soil Conservation

Plants absorb harmful gases through their leaves, foliage intercepts and adsorbs dust, and lawns reduce dust raised from the ground; some plants can absorb toxic substances from water and soil and transform them in their bodies. Plants with an exceptional capacity to accumulate heavy metals and similar substances can be used to remediate contaminated soil; for example, arsenic can accumulate in the fronds of Chinese brake fern (*Pteris vittata*) to more than 2% of dry weight (Ma et al., 2001), but accumulated toxins pass along the food chain, so harvested plants must be disposed of carefully. Plants especially sensitive to a toxic gas develop lesions at low concentrations and can serve as monitoring plants, such as gladiolus for hydrogen fluoride and alfalfa for sulfur dioxide.

Vegetation, forests in particular, intercepts precipitation, slows surface runoff and holds the soil: the canopy and the litter layer let rainwater seep slowly into the ground, reducing erosion of the topsoil, and thus conserve water sources, reduce flood peaks and reduce silting of rivers and reservoirs; transpiration returns water vapor to the atmosphere and moderates local climate. Soil erosion caused by the destruction of vegetation is one of the most serious ecological problems in regions such as China's Loess Plateau.

### 1.3.4. Plants and Humans

Most of human clothing, food, shelter, transport, medicine and industrial raw materials come directly or indirectly from plants. About 30 crops supply the great majority of the food energy of humankind, and three cereals, rice, wheat and maize, alone supply more than half; cotton and the bast fibers (hemp, flax and others) are the main textile fibers, wood and bamboo are used for building and paper, and many drugs come from plants or have plant products as leads, such as quinine (*Cinchona*), artemisinin (sweet wormwood, *Artemisia annua*) and paclitaxel (*Taxus*). The origin and **domestication** of crops are illustrated in the chapters on the grass family ([7.12.39](/7.12.39)) and on Rosaceae and others ([7.12.43](/7.12.43)). Recognizing plants and naming them correctly is the prerequisite for using and protecting plant resources ([7.12.2](/7.12.2)).

## 1.4. Branches and History of Botany

### 1.4.1. Branches

Botany is divided by subject matter into several branches:

| Branch | Subject | Place in this course or the site |
|---|---|---|
| Plant morphology | Internal and external form and structure of the plant body, the formation and development of organs, and their changes in ontogeny and phylogeny; includes plant cytology (cell structure), plant anatomy (microscopic and ultrastructure of tissues and organs) and plant embryology (structure, origin and differentiation of the embryo) | 7.12.3–7.12.5, 7.12.19–7.12.31 |
| Plant taxonomy and systematics | Description, identification, naming, classification and relationships of plant groups, and the building of classifications that reflect evolution | 7.12.2, 7.12.6–7.12.18, 7.12.25–7.12.26, 7.12.33–7.12.49 |
| Plant physiology | Photosynthesis, respiration, water and mineral metabolism, growth and development and their regulation, responses to the environment | 7.13 |
| Plant ecology and geobotany | Relationships of individual plants (ecology) or plant communities (geobotany, the science of vegetation) with the environment | 7.12.32, 7.19 |

By the group studied there are also phycology, mycology, lichenology, bryology, pteridology, seed-plant taxonomy and so on; by application, economic botany, medicinal botany, plant pathology, plant geography, paleobotany and others. Since the late twentieth century molecular biology and genomics have entered every branch of botany: in 2000 thale cress (*Arabidopsis thaliana*) became the first plant with a fully sequenced genome and is the most important **model organism** of plant genetics and developmental biology; DNA sequences moved systematics from the comparison of morphological characters into the era of molecular phylogenetics ([7.12.2](/7.12.2), [7.12.33](/7.12.33)).

### 1.4.2. Plant Knowledge in Ancient China

China was one of the first countries to record plants systematically. The *Shijing* (Book of Songs) already mentions many plants; the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, compiled in the Eastern Han dynasty, lists 365 drugs, most of them from plants. Every later dynasty produced "bencao" works devoted to medicinal plants: Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu* (Compendium of Materia Medica) of the Ming dynasty, completed in 1578, has 52 volumes and lists 1,892 drugs in 16 divisions and 60 classes; its plants fall into the five divisions of herbs, grains, vegetables, fruits and woody plants, further divided into thirty classes by habitat and use, such as mountain herbs and fragrant herbs, and it remains an important reference today. Wu Qijun's *Zhiwu Mingshi Tukao* (Illustrated Investigation of Plant Names and Identities) of the Qing dynasty (published 1848) records 1,714 plants, with pictures and text, in twelve classes (grains, vegetables, mountain herbs, marsh herbs, rock herbs, aquatic herbs, climbing herbs, fragrant herbs, poisonous herbs, ornamentals, fruits and woody plants), and is concerned with verifying plant names and the plants they refer to. In agronomy and horticulture, Jia Sixie's *Qimin Yaoshu* of the Northern Wei dynasty describes crop cultivation, grafting and the rotation of legumes and cereals; Zhu Su's *Jiuhuang Bencao* (1406) of the Ming dynasty records and illustrates wild plants that can be eaten in famine years; and there are many other monographs, such as the *Zhupu* (on bamboo), *Chajing* (on tea), *Jupu* (on chrysanthemum), *Lizhipu* (on lychee) and *Qunfangpu* (on ornamentals).

These works classify plants by use, habit or habitat without regard to evolutionary affinity, and so are artificial classifications. They are valuable empirical knowledge, but the names in the bencao literature do not always correspond to botanical names: for example, the authentic source plant of the drug "qinghao" is, botanically, sweet wormwood (*Artemisia annua*), from which artemisinin is extracted ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.3.1)).

### 1.4.3. The Development of Western Botany

Theophrastus of ancient Greece (about 371–287 BCE) wrote *Historia Plantarum* and *De Causis Plantarum*, recording about 500 plants; he divided plants into trees, shrubs, subshrubs and herbs and distinguished inflorescence types, the position of the ovary, and free versus fused petals, and is called the father of botany. Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* of the first century was the foundation of European herbal medicine for more than a thousand years.

Modern botany took shape in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: Cesalpino's *De Plantis* (1583) used fruits and seeds as the main basis of classification and separated botany from practical herbal medicine; C. Bauhin's *Pinax* (1623) arranged the names of about 6,000 plants, already distinguished genera and species, and commonly named a plant by the genus name plus one word; Hooke observed cork under the microscope in 1665 and introduced the word "cell"; Ray gave a definition of the species and distinguished monocots from dicots. In the eighteenth century Linnaeus established the 24-class "sexual system" based chiefly on the number and arrangement of stamens, and in *Species Plantarum* (1753) used **binomial nomenclature** systematically, laying the foundation of modern taxonomy and nomenclature.

In the nineteenth century A. L. de Jussieu (1789), A. P. de Candolle (who proposed the word taxonomy in 1813), and Bentham and J. D. Hooker (*Genera Plantarum*, 1862–1883, about 200 families and more than 7,500 genera) built natural classifications on the overall similarity of many characters; Schleiden (1838) and Schwann (1839) proposed the **cell theory**; Hofmeister (1851) found that bryophytes, ferns and seed plants share a life cycle with alternating gametophyte and sporophyte, revealing the homology among the groups of land plants; Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) made classification aim at relationships that reflect evolution; and Mendel discovered the laws of heredity from pea crosses (1866). Engler, Hutchinson, Takhtajan, Cronquist and others then proposed phylogenetic classifications of the angiosperms, and from 1998 the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG) published a series of classifications based on DNA sequences. The history of classification systems is treated systematically in [7.12.33](/7.12.33).

### 1.4.4. Modern Botany in China

In 1858 Li Shanlan and A. Williamson, working from Lindley's *Elements of Botany*, compiled the eight-volume *Zhiwuxue* (Botany), the first modern botanical translation in China. The Chinese word for "botany" and many terms such as cell, carpel, ovary, placenta, embryo and endosperm were fixed by this book and are still used; the word for "botany" was later also adopted in Japan.

From the late nineteenth century to the early twentieth, European and American missionaries and "plant hunters" (such as A. David, E. H. Wilson and G. Forrest) collected specimens and introduced plants from China on a large scale, so the type specimens of many Chinese plants are kept in herbaria abroad. Modern botanical research by Chinese scholars began in the early twentieth century: Zhong Guangguang was the first Chinese scholar to collect plant specimens on a large scale by modern methods; in 1922 Hu Xiansu, Bing Zhi and others founded the Biological Laboratory of the Science Society of China in Nanjing, and in 1928 the Fan Memorial Institute of Biology in Beiping, beginning the systematic survey of the native flora.

After 1949 research institutions and the teaching system of botany were established rapidly. *Flora Reipublicae Popularis Sinicae* was compiled from 1959 and fully published by 2004, in 80 volumes and 126 parts, recording 301 families, 3,408 genera and 31,142 species of vascular plants; it is the largest flora in the world and won the First Prize of the State Natural Science Award in 2009. Its English revision, *Flora of China*, a joint Chinese-American project, appeared in 1994–2013. Wu Zhengyi's studies of the seed-plant flora of China, the provincial floras, and *Flora Sporophytarum Sinicorum* make up the basic reference material for knowing Chinese plants (for reference works and online resources see [7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.5.3)).

## 1.5. Structure of the Course

The course interleaves morphology and anatomy with systematics, so that the reading order is roughly the order of evolution. After the general introduction, we first treat plant cells and tissues and then the spore-bearing plants, moving from aquatic to terrestrial and from simple to complex: first the cyanobacteria, red algae and the algae with secondary plastids, which are not green plants, then the fungi and lichens, and then up the lineage of green plants through the green algae and streptophyte algae to the bryophytes, lycophytes and ferns, ending with the life cycles and the colonization of land by spore-bearing plants. In this way concepts such as vascular tissue, stele, microphylls and megaphylls acquire an evolutionary background in the ferns before they enter the seed, root, stem and leaf of seed plants. The reproduction part begins with the ovule and seed of gymnosperms, followed by the flower, gametophyte development, double fertilization, seed and fruit of angiosperms, and the ecological adaptations of plants; finally the orders and key families of angiosperms are treated in the phylogenetic order of APG IV. The physiological mechanisms of plants are left to plant physiology (7.13), and communities and ecosystems to ecology (7.19).

- Three circumscriptions of "plant" (Definition 1.1.1): Archaeplastida (primary plastids) ⊃ Viridiplantae (chlorophylls *a* and *b*, starch stored in the plastid) ⊃ embryophytes (embryo, multicellular gametangia and sporangia, cuticle); the vascular plants and seed plants are successively nested clades (Definition 1.1.3).

## Questions

1. Why is "algae" not a taxon? Name two organisms that are only distantly related but were both called algae.

   **Answer** Algae is a set of organisms grouped by way of life (photosynthetic autotrophy, no root, stem or leaf, mostly aquatic) and is spread over several branches of the tree of life; no common ancestor includes only them, so the group is polyphyletic. For example, blue-green algae are bacteria and belong to the domain Bacteria; brown algae belong to the stramenopiles among eukaryotes, and their plastids come from secondary endosymbiosis, the engulfment of a red alga. The last common ancestor of the two is the common ancestor of all cellular organisms.

2. The textbook of Raven et al. says "plants" are embryophytes, while the systematics text of Judd et al. says "plants" are Viridiplantae. What is the rationale for each usage?

   **Answer** Taking embryophytes as plants stresses the whole set of features adapted to life on land (embryo, multicellular gametangia and sporangia, cuticle, alternation of generations), which is just the main subject of plant morphology and physiology. Taking Viridiplantae as plants stresses phylogeny: land plants evolved from the streptophytes among the green algae, so if only land plants are called plants, the remaining green algae form a paraphyletic group; combining the Chlorophyta, the streptophyte algae and the land plants into one clade makes the naming consistent with phylogeny. Both usages correspond to monophyletic groups and differ only in breadth.

3. Why are archegoniates a paraphyletic group?

   **Answer** Archegoniates comprise bryophytes, ferns and gymnosperms; their common ancestor is also the ancestor of the angiosperms, but the female gametophyte of angiosperms is highly reduced and has no archegonium, so angiosperms are not in the group. A group that contains a common ancestor and some but not all of its descendants is paraphyletic; the archegonium is an ancestral character of embryophytes, and groups defined by ancestral characters are usually paraphyletic.

4. Winter wheat is sown in autumn and harvested the next summer, spanning two calendar years. Is it a biennial?

   **Answer** Generally not. A biennial forms only vegetative organs in the first growing season and flowers and fruits only in the second after overwintering (such as carrot and Chinese cabbage). Winter wheat emerges and tillers after autumn sowing but does not enter a second complete vegetative growing season while overwintering; it elongates and heads in spring, and passes through only one growing season from germination to fruiting, so it is usually called a winter annual. It needs a cold period (vernalization) in order to flower (the mechanism is treated in plant physiology), which is why it must be sown in autumn.

5. (Open question) Choose a plant you know, and say where it would be placed in a Bencao Gangmu-style artificial classification, in Linnaeus's sexual system and in the APG system, and compare the bases of the three classifications.

   **Answer** Take the apple as an example. In the *Bencao Gangmu* it belongs to the division of fruits (by use); in Linnaeus's sexual system, with many stamens (about 20) inserted on the calyx, it falls in the class Icosandria (by number and insertion of stamens); in the APG system it belongs to angiosperms, eudicots, rosids, order Rosales, family Rosaceae, genus *Malus* (by clades inferred from DNA sequences). The first two classifications rest on one or two characters or on use and do not reflect relationships; the APG system rests on a phylogeny inferred from a large number of characters, every group is monophyletic, and it therefore predicts unstudied characters (such as the similar chemical constituents of plants in one family).
