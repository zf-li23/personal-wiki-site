The stem is the axial organ that connects roots and leaves. It carries water and minerals absorbed by the roots up to the leaves, carries the organic compounds made in the leaves down to the roots and other parts, and holds the leaves in positions in space where they can intercept light. Two features set the stem apart from the root: the stem bears nodes, the nodes bear leaves, and buds are borne in the leaf axils and at the stem tip; and the primary vascular tissues of the stem are arranged in vascular bundles in which xylem and phloem lie side by side, one inside the other. This chapter first describes the external form of the stem (nodes and buds, growth habit and branching), then shows how the shoot apex produces leaves, buds and nodes, and by what rules the leaves are arranged on the stem (phyllotaxis), and finally treats the primary structure of the stem and the vascular connections at the node. Secondary thickening, wood and bark follow in the next chapter ([7.12.22](/7.12.22)); modified stems such as underground stems and the vascular connections among the vegetative organs are treated in [7.12.24](/7.12.24); leaf morphology and structure are in [7.12.23](/7.12.23). The mechanisms of water and assimilate transport, and the hormonal control of apical dominance and phyllotaxis, belong to plant physiology.

## 21.1. Functions and Form of the Stem

### 21.1.1. Functions of the Stem

In seed plants the stem arises from the plumule of the embryo, sometimes together with part of the hypocotyl, and lateral branches arise from buds in the leaf axils. Apart from a few underground stems, the stem is the aboveground vegetative organ of the plant body; in most plants the stem tip can grow upward indefinitely, and together with its leaves it forms a large branching system.

- Conduction: the xylem of the stem (vessels and tracheids; most gymnosperms have only tracheids) carries water and inorganic salts absorbed by the roots to all parts of the plant body, and the sieve tubes of the phloem (sieve cells in gymnosperms) carry photosynthates from the leaves to the rest of the plant.
- Support: the mechanical tissues of the stem (fibers, sclereids, collenchyma) and the vessels and tracheids of the xylem act like reinforced concrete in a building. They hold up a large crown and heavy loads of flowers and fruits against wind, rain and snow, and they arrange branches, leaves and flowers in space in a way that favors photosynthesis, pollination, and the ripening and dispersal of fruits and seeds.
- Storage and propagation: the parenchyma of the stem often stores large amounts of reserves, especially in modified stems (lotus rhizome, arrowhead corm, potato tuber); many stems form adventitious roots and buds, which is why cuttings and layering are used to propagate seedlings in agriculture, forestry and horticulture.

Stems are economically very important. Sugarcane, potato, taro, lettuce (stem lettuce), water bamboo, lotus rhizome, arrowhead and ginger are common foods, and cinnamon bark is a spice; eucommia bark (*Eucommia ulmoides*), silk-tree bark (*Albizia julibrissin*), cinnamon twig, pinellia, gastrodia and Solomon's seal are well-known medicinal materials, and quinine, the antimalarial drug, comes from the bark of cinchona. Fibers, rubber, lacquer, cork, timber, bamboo, and the chemical feedstocks obtained by distilling wood all come from stems. Seed plants without a stem are extremely rare: in *Rafflesia* the vegetative body consists only of filaments of cells inside the host tissue, and only the flowers emerge. *Phacellaria* (Santalaceae), the example often given in older Chinese textbooks, is a hyperparasite on mistletoes (Loranthaceae) with leaves reduced to scales, but what emerges from the host tissue are slender stems bearing scale leaves, so it is not stemless. Dandelion and plantain have very short internodes and leaves arranged in a rosette; they are called rosette plants and are not stemless.

### 21.1.2. Nodes, Internodes and Shoots

Stems are mostly cylindrical, but some are triangular (sedges), square (faba bean, mint, motherwort) or flattened (orchid cactus, prickly pear). The distribution of mechanical and vascular tissues within the stem matches its outline and gives the stem its resistance to bending and compression.

**Definition 21.1.1 (Node, Internode, and Shoot)** The part of the stem where leaves are borne is called a *node*; the part between two successive nodes is called an *internode*. A stem together with the leaves and buds it bears is called a *shoot*.

The main external differences between stem and root are that the stem has nodes and internodes, with leaves at the nodes and buds in the leaf axils and at the tip, whereas the root has no nodes and bears no leaves (lateral roots arise internally, and their positions depend on the root's archetype; [7.12.20](/7.12.20)). The stem is therefore the axis that remains when the leaves and buds are removed from a shoot. In grasses (sugarcane, bamboo, rice, maize) and in the Polygonaceae the nodes are swollen and conspicuous; in the rhizome of lotus the internodes are swollen and the nodes constricted; in most plants the nodes are only slightly enlarged.

The length of internodes varies with how strongly a shoot elongates. In woody plants a shoot with markedly elongated internodes is called a long shoot, and a shoot with shortened internodes, whose nodes are crowded together or even indistinguishable, is called a short shoot; the leaves on a short shoot are therefore clustered. In ginkgo (*Ginkgo biloba*) many short shoots arise on the long shoots and the leaves are clustered on the short shoots; in Masson pine (*Pinus massoniana*) the short shoots are even shorter, bear many scales at the base and carry a tuft of two needles at the tip, and the short shoot falls together with its needles; in pear and apple most flowers are borne on short shoots, which are thus fruiting spurs and often form clusters of spurs. Long shoots extend the crown, and short shoots carry out photosynthesis and bear fruit: this division of labor is found in many trees.

On the winter twigs of perennial deciduous trees and shrubs, besides nodes, internodes and buds, one can see the following marks (Figure 1):

- Leaf scar: the mark left on the stem by the petiole after the leaf falls; its arrangement reflects the phyllotaxis (alternate in elm, opposite in lilac), and its shape and color vary among plants.
- Bundle scar: dots or lines raised within the leaf scar, marking where vascular bundles between petiole and stem were severed; their number and arrangement reflect the number of leaf traces ([21.6](#sec-21.6)), commonly three in eudicots.
- Bud scale scar: the ring of marks left when the outer bud scales of a terminal (scaly) bud fall as the bud opens. The terminal bud opens once each spring, so the segment between two successive rings of bud scale scars is one year's growth, from which the age of a twig can be judged; this is often used when choosing material for cuttings and grafts.
- Lenticel: the channel for gas exchange on woody stems ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)).

The leaf scars, bundle scars, bud scale scars, lenticels and buds of winter twigs are important features for identifying trees in the leafless season.

### 21.1.3. Buds

**Definition 21.1.2 (Bud)** A shoot, flower or inflorescence in the young, unexpanded state, that is, the rudiment of a shoot, flower or inflorescence before it has developed, is called a **bud**.

A longitudinal section of a vegetative bud shows the following (Figure 1). At the top is the apical meristem. Below it are protrusions, the leaf primordia, which are the earliest stage of leaf development; the lower a primordium, the larger it is, and the lowest have developed into young leaves. The protrusion in the axil of a young leaf is the axillary bud primordium (also called the lateral shoot primordium), which will become an axillary bud, in effect a smaller vegetative bud. The axis that bears the leaf primordia and young leaves is the bud axis, in fact a shortened stem whose internodes have not yet elongated. A vegetative bud is thus a compressed segment of a shoot, and when the internodes elongate the bud opens into a shoot.

Studying bud structure has practical value. The number and activity of vegetative buds determine the relation between trunk and lateral branches, that is, the vigor and habit of the plant; the crown shape of a tall tree is the result of successive years' opening of buds of various orders. The structure and time of differentiation of flower buds determine how early a plant flowers and how much it fruits.

Buds can be classified from several points of view:

1. By position on the shoot: normal buds and adventitious buds. Normal buds comprise terminal buds, at the tip of the main stem or a lateral branch, and axillary buds (also called lateral buds), in the leaf axils. After leaf fall the axillary buds on the upper part of a twig are often conspicuous, and those near the base are smaller. A leaf axil usually has a single axillary bud, but in honeysuckle, peach, osmanthus, mulberry and cotton some or all axils bear more than one; the later-formed ones are called accessory buds. In plane tree, fatsia and black locust the axillary bud lies low, covered by the swollen petiole base, and is exposed only after the leaf falls; such buds are called subpetiolar buds. A bud that is not borne at a shoot tip or in a leaf axil is an adventitious bud, as on the roots of sweet potato, dandelion, elm and black locust, on the leaves of *Kalanchoe* and begonia, and on old stems or wounds of mulberry and willow; adventitious buds are very useful in vegetative propagation.
2. By the presence of bud scales: the overwintering buds of most perennial woody plants are covered by scales (bud scales) and are called scaly buds. Bud scales are modified leaves with a thick cuticle, sometimes hairy or secreting resin or mucilage, which reduce transpiration and protect against drought and frost; they are very important for the overwintering of temperate woody plants (plane tree, poplar, mulberry, magnolia, loquat). The buds of all annual plants, most biennials and a few perennial woody plants have no bud scales and are enclosed only by young leaves; they are called naked buds, as in cucumber, cotton, castor bean, rapeseed and wingnut.
3. By the organ they will form: vegetative buds (formerly, incorrectly, called leaf buds), floral buds and mixed buds. A vegetative bud consists of the apical meristem surrounded by leaf primordia, axillary bud primordia and young leaves. A floral bud is the rudiment of a flower or inflorescence, composed of floral organ primordia or flower primordia, with no leaf or axillary bud primordia; its apical meristem stops growing once the floral parts have formed and cannot grow indefinitely. A bud containing the rudiments of both shoot and flower, which can develop into both, is called a mixed bud, as in pear, apple, photinia, white lilac, crabapple and buckwheat. Vegetative buds are usually slender and small, while floral and mixed buds are plump and larger; in plants that flower before the leaves emerge, such as magnolia and redbud, the flower buds open first and the vegetative buds become active only after flowering, so the two are easy to tell apart.
4. By physiological state: a bud that forms a new shoot, flower or inflorescence during the growing season is an active bud, and a bud that does not grow in the growing season and remains dormant is a dormant bud (also called a latent bud). Most buds of annual herbs are active; on many branches of temperate perennial woody plants only the terminal bud and some axillary buds near the tip are active, and most axillary buds are dormant. Dormant buds give the plant a large reserve; they resume activity and form new shoots only when the terminal bud or upper branches are damaged, attacked by insects or pruned. The "water sprouts" that arise on old trunks come from buds that have lain dormant for years.

**Definition 21.1.3 (Axillary Bud and Mixed Bud)** A bud borne in a leaf axil and developed from an axillary bud primordium is called an **axillary bud**; a bud that contains primordia of both shoot and flower, and on opening forms both, is called a **mixed bud**.

## 21.2. Growth Habit and Branching of the Stem

### 21.2.1. Growth Habit

Over the course of evolution the stems of different plants have acquired their own growth habits, which distribute the leaves suitably in space so that they intercept sunlight fully (Figure 2, left):

1. Erect stem: grows upward against gravity, as in castor bean, sunflower and poplar. Most plants have erect stems.
2. Twining stem: soft when young and unable to stand upright, it rises by twining around another support. The direction of twining is a stable characteristic of the species. Viewed from above, morning glory, cypress vine, Dutchman's pipe and common bean twine counterclockwise, which Chinese textbooks call "left-handed" twining; honeysuckle and hop twine clockwise, which Chinese textbooks call "right-handed"; the stem of fleeceflower (*Fallopia multiflora*) can twine in either direction and is called a neutral twiner. English-language literature usually names the direction by the handedness of the helix that results and calls twiners such as morning glory right-handed, the same direction as the Chinese "left-handed", so one must check the definition when reading. About nine tenths of twining plants worldwide are of the morning-glory type (Edwards et al. 2007). The stem of hop has recurved prickles and also climbs by hooking onto other objects, so it is sometimes classified with climbing stems.
3. Climbing stem: soft when young, it climbs another object by special structures. By the climbing structure there are five kinds: tendrils, as in loofah, cucumber and pumpkin (stem tendrils), grape and *Cayratia* (stem tendrils borne at the nodes opposite the leaves), and pea (leaf tendrils); aerial roots, as in ivy, star jasmine and creeping fig; petioles, as in nasturtium and *Clematis*; hooked prickles, as in rattan and cleavers; and adhesive discs, as in Boston ivy (the discs are modified tendril tips). The origin of tendrils and discs (stem, leaf or stipule) is discussed in [7.12.24](/7.12.24).
4. Creeping stem: slender and weak, it spreads along the ground; its internodes are generally long, adventitious roots form at the nodes, and the buds grow into new plants, as in strawberry, sweet potato and strawberry saxifrage. After the new plant of strawberry becomes independent, the internodes of the slender connecting stem die, so this type is sometimes set apart as the runner.

Plants with twining and climbing stems are collectively called **vines** ([7.12.1](/7.12.1)); there are herbaceous vines (common bean, pumpkin, nasturtium) and woody ones (grape, wisteria, honeysuckle). Vines shift the investment in support to their hosts and can therefore reach the canopy quickly with slender stems; they flourish especially in tropical and humid subtropical forests, where they form a characteristic landscape. When grape, beans and cucurbits are grown, trellises must be set up in time so that the shoots are properly spread.

### 21.2.2. Types of Branching

Branching is a universal phenomenon in plant growth. Elongation of the main stem and formation of lateral branches result from the separate development of terminal and axillary buds; lateral branches also have terminal and axillary buds and can branch further, forming a branch system. Because buds differ in nature and activity, branching patterns differ, but they follow certain rules (Figure 2).

**Definition 21.2.1 (Monopodial, Sympodial and False Dichotomous Branching)** A branching pattern in which the main stem (main axis) is formed by the terminal bud extending continuously upward, and the lateral branches are subordinate to it, is called *monopodial branching* (also racemose branching). A pattern in which the terminal bud of the main stem grows slowly during the season, dies or turns into a flower bud, and the axillary bud just below it grows out to replace it, with this alternation repeated each year so that the main stem consists of many linked segments of lateral branches, is called *sympodial branching*. A pattern in plants with opposite leaves, in which the terminal bud stops growing or flowers and the two axillary buds just below it on opposite sides develop into two equivalent branches, is called *false dichotomous branching*.

1. Monopodial branching: elongation and thickening of the main stem are much stronger than those of the lateral branches, and the main stem is very conspicuous. Some angiosperms such as poplar and beech, and most gymnosperms, such as pine, Chinese fir (*Cunninghamia*), larch, dawn redwood and juniper, branch monopodially. Monopodially branched trees have tall, straight trunks, and their wood is suited to construction and shipbuilding. Among ferns and gymnosperms monopodial branching predominates.
2. Sympodial branching: here the main stem is a "composite" axis; it is conspicuously zigzag when young but, owing to thickening growth, hard to distinguish on older branches. Most angiosperms branch sympodially, for example potato, tomato, fig, Chinese parasol tree, mulberry, bodhi tree, peach and apple. A sympodial crown is spreading and leafy, and well ventilated and lit, which increases the area for photosynthesis; the successive death of terminal buds releases the lower axillary buds from inhibition (apical dominance), promoting the development of many axillary buds, and sympodial branches often bear flower buds, so this is a relatively advanced and productive branching pattern.
3. False dichotomous branching: in fact a variant of sympodial branching, found in plants with opposite leaves such as lilac, jasmine, elder, pink (*Dianthus*) and chickweed. It differs from true dichotomous branching, in which the apical meristem itself divides into two. Dichotomous branching, in which the apical meristem splits equally in two, is common in algae (*Dictyota*), liverworts and lycophytes such as clubmosses and spikemosses; it is a rather primitive branching pattern (for dichotomous branching in early land plants and the telome theory see [7.12.16](/7.12.16)) and was replaced by other patterns in evolution.

Some plants have two branching patterns on one individual. In cotton the vegetative branches on the lower part of the plant branch monopodially and do not flower or fruit directly, while the fruiting branches above are sympodial. In cultivation the axillary buds on the lower part of cotton are therefore removed early so that nutrients are concentrated in the fruiting branches. Within a genus, monopodially branching species often fruit little and mature early. Topping and training in agriculture and horticulture use the rules of branching: in tomato and cucurbits, topping makes the axillary buds develop into lateral branches, and training then controls their number and distribution; pruning of fruit trees alters the tree form, promotes early fruiting, and adjusts alternate bearing.

The overall form of a tree is determined jointly by the branching pattern, the growth direction (upright or horizontal) of branches of each order, and the effect of flowering on the apex; tropical tree biology has accordingly summarized tree forms in more than twenty "architectural models" (Hallé & Oldeman). The recursive generation of tree form is simulated with L-systems in [7.12.22](/7.12.22#sec-22.7).

### 21.2.3. Tillering in Grasses

Branching in grasses such as rice and wheat differs from the patterns above.

**Definition 21.2.2 (Tillering)** The mode of branching in grasses in which axillary buds on the tillering node (a cluster of crowded nodes) below or near the ground surface form branches with adventitious roots is called *tillering*; such a branch is called a *tiller*.

Take wheat as an example. In the seedling stage the internodes at the base of the stem are very short, the nodes and internodes are crowded together, and many adventitious roots and axillary buds arise at the nodes. At jointing, the upper 4–7 internodes of the main stem and of some tillers elongate conspicuously one after another while the basal nodes remain crowded; this crowded node region, which bears the tillers, is called the tillering node. The tillering node contains abundant organic reserves, is swollen, and bears many adventitious root primordia on its sides (called secondary root primordia in agronomy). Tillers can form further tillers, giving primary tillers, secondary tillers and so on.

The node on which a tiller arises is its tiller position: a tiller emerging from the axil of the third leaf has tiller position three. After the first tiller appears, the tiller positions of later tillers shift upward in order; this is the common rule of tiller formation in wheat and rice. The lower the tiller position, the earlier the tiller appears, the longer its growing period, the more photosynthate it accumulates and the greater its chance of heading and setting grain. A tiller that can head and set grain is called a productive tiller, and one that cannot is an unproductive tiller. In agriculture, measures such as suitable planting density, control of water and fertilizer, and adjustment of sowing date promote productive tillers and suppress unproductive ones. Tillering capacity differs among crops: rice and wheat tiller strongly, whereas maize and sorghum tiller weakly and generally produce no tillers. Seen from the viewpoint of the apical meristem, each tiller is an axillary bud arising from a crowded node, so tillering is branching at the base with unelongated internodes, and this is how the tufted habit of grasses arises.

## 21.3. Structure and Development of the Shoot Apex

### 21.3.1. The Shoot Apical Meristem

The apical meristem of the stem resembles that of the root but is more complex: the formation of leaf primordia and the origin of lateral branches both trace back to the shoot apex, and the nodes and internodes, leaves, axillary buds and the reproductive structures into which they later change are all products of the activity of the shoot apical meristem. The foremost part of the shoot apex, comprising the initials and the derivative cells they have just produced, is the least differentiated promeristem; below it protoderm, ground meristem and procambium gradually differentiate, collectively called the primary meristems, and their activity and differentiation produce mature tissues that make up the primary plant body. The shoot apex has no protective structure like the root cap; it is protected in the bud by being enclosed in layers of young leaves.

A few terms need clarification. The apical meristem of the stem is sometimes called the growing point, but the term is inexact, because the most vigorous division and most conspicuous enlargement often do not occur at the very tip; the growing tip of agronomy generally means the part of the shoot tip above the last leaf primordium. Stem apex (shoot apex) and root apex are synonyms for the apical meristems of the stem and root respectively; stem tip and **root tip** usually mean the part from the apical meristem to where tissue differentiation is nearly mature, and the root tip also includes the root cap.

The organization of the shoot apex is described by the histogen theory (Hanstein 1868, more applicable to roots), the tunica–corpus theory (Schmidt 1924, applicable to angiosperm shoot apices) and the cytohistological zonation concept (Foster 1938, first described for ginkgo, applicable to gymnosperms and also widely used for angiosperms), together with the WUS–CLV3 feedback that maintains stem cells; these were treated in detail in [7.12.4](/7.12.4) (see Figure 2 there). Here we stress only two points relevant to organogenesis. In angiosperms the outermost one or two layers of the shoot apex (the tunica) divide only anticlinally, so the epidermis always arises from the outermost layer. Leaf primordia arise in the peripheral zone (on the flank) of the shoot apex, while the stem cells in the central zone remain undifferentiated and supply cells for indefinite growth of the apex.

### 21.3.2. Origin of Leaves and Buds

A leaf develops step by step from a leaf primordium. In gymnosperms and eudicots the cell divisions that initiate a leaf primordium generally begin in the second or third layer below the surface of the shoot apex: periclinal divisions add cells and produce a protrusion on the flank; the surface of the protrusion then divides anticlinally, and later anticlinal and periclinal divisions proceed simultaneously in deeper layers. In monocots leaf primordia often begin with periclinal divisions in the surface layer. When the tunica is thick, the whole leaf primordium can arise from derivatives of the tunica; otherwise it arises from tunica and corpus together. The flank protrusion just beginning to form is called the leaf buttress; it is the beginning of the whole leaf and not a part of the leaf. The relative positions at which leaf primordia appear around the shoot apex agree with the phyllotaxis on the shoot (Section 21.4).

Terminal buds arise at the shoot apex (the apical meristems of main and lateral shoots), and axillary buds arise from axillary bud primordia. In most angiosperms the axillary bud primordium arises in the axil of a leaf primordium, later than the leaf primordium that encloses it; its formation closely resembles that of a leaf, with periclinal and anticlinal divisions of some cells in the axil forming a protrusion whose cells are arranged like those of the shoot apex; it may itself begin to form leaf primordia, but generally forms few until it is some distance from the shoot apex. The leaves and buds of the stem arise from the first layer, or the second and third layers, of cells at the surface of the meristem; this mode of origin is called exogenous origin, in contrast to the endogenous origin of lateral roots ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)).

The formation of adventitious buds is not directly related to apical meristems. They can arise in callus near a cutting or wound, in the vascular cambium or the periphery of the vascular cylinder, or even in the epidermis, and on roots, stems, hypocotyls and leaves. By position of origin, adventitious buds are exogenous (arising near the surface) or endogenous (arising deep in internal tissue). An apical meristem first forms by cell divisions, and only when the first leaf forms is the adventitious bud connected to the existing structure through newly differentiated vascular tissue.

## 21.4. Phyllotaxis

**Definition 21.4.1 (Phyllotaxis)** The arrangement of leaves on the stem is called **phyllotaxis**.

The basic types of phyllotaxis are alternate (one leaf per node), opposite (two leaves facing each other at a node) and whorled (three or more leaves per node). Alternate phyllotaxis is further divided into distichous, in which successive leaves are 180° apart (grasses, iris), and spiral, in which successive leaves are separated by an angle less than 180° so that the leaves ascend the stem in a spiral. Among opposite arrangements, that in which successive pairs of leaves are at right angles is called decussate (Lamiaceae, Caryophyllaceae). The morphological terminology of phyllotaxis and leaf mosaics is treated in [7.12.23](/7.12.23); here we treat how phyllotaxis is generated at the shoot apex and the mathematical rules of spiral phyllotaxis.

### 21.4.1. The Divergence Angle and the Golden Angle

In spiral phyllotaxis, the angle between the attachment points of two successive leaves (or two successive leaf primordia), seen in a cross section of the stem, is called the divergence angle. Traditionally it is written as a fraction: if, going up the stem from one leaf, one makes $p$ turns around the stem and passes $q$ leaves before reaching another leaf directly above the first, the phyllotaxis is written $p/q$ and the divergence angle is $\frac{p}{q}\times 360^\circ$. The common phyllotactic fractions are

$$
\frac{1}{2},\ \frac{1}{3},\ \frac{2}{5},\ \frac{3}{8},\ \frac{5}{13},\ \frac{8}{21},\ \dots
$$

($1/2$ in grasses, $1/3$ in sedges, $2/5$ in peach and oak, $3/8$ in sunflower leaves, and so on; the Schimper–Braun series). The numerators and denominators of this series are Fibonacci numbers ($F_1 = F_2 = 1$, $F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1}$; for the closed form see the example in [2.1.6](/2.1.6)), and the $n$th term is $F_n/F_{n+2}$. As $n$ increases,

$$
\frac{F_n}{F_{n+2}} \to \frac{1}{\varphi^2} = 2 - \varphi \approx 0.381\,97, \qquad \varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},
$$

and the corresponding divergence angle tends to

$$
360^\circ \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{\varphi}\right) = \frac{360^\circ}{\varphi^2} \approx 137.508^\circ,
\tag{21.1}
$$

called the golden angle. In fact the divergence angle at which leaf primordia arise at the shoot apex of most spiral-leaved plants is close to $137.5^\circ$; rational numbers such as "$2/5$" and "$3/8$" only approximately describe the arrangement of leaves on a mature shoot, because internode elongation and twisting of the stem allow us to see only roughly aligned rows of leaves (orthostichies).

What is special about the golden angle is that it is the "most irrational" angle: the continued fraction expansion $[0; 1, 1, 1, \dots]$ of $1/\varphi$ has all terms equal to 1, and rational approximations converge to it most slowly. Primordia placed successively at the golden angle therefore never line up in straight rows; each later primordium falls into the largest gap among the existing ones, and on the limited area of the shoot apex they are packed most evenly and most tightly (Figure 3). For leaves this means that upper and lower leaves shade one another least, and for the achenes on a sunflower head or the seed scales of a pine cone it means the most elements packed into a given area.

### 21.4.2. Parastichies

When primordia are packed densely (sunflower head, pine cone, pineapple, succulent rosettes), the eye sees not a single spiral but two families of spiral lines running in opposite directions, called parastichies. Number the primordia in order of initiation as $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and use Vogel's (1979) model: the $k$th primordium lies at

$$
\theta_k = k\,\alpha, \qquad r_k = c\sqrt{k},
\tag{21.2}
$$

where $\alpha$ is the divergence angle and $r_k \propto \sqrt{k}$ gives each primordium roughly equal area. Joining primordia whose numbers differ by $m$ gives one family of parastichies, and joining those that differ by $n$ gives another. For the golden angle, the numbers of lines $(m, n)$ in the two most conspicuous families are always two consecutive Fibonacci numbers, such as $(5, 8)$ and $(8, 13)$ in pine cones, and $(34, 55)$ and $(55, 89)$ in sunflower heads. The reason is that for two primordia whose numbers differ by $F_j$, the angle difference $F_j\alpha$ is closest to an integer multiple of $360^\circ$ (this is exactly the result that Fibonacci numbers give the best rational approximations to $1/\varphi^2$), so they are almost radially aligned and spatially nearest neighbors. Going outward from the center, as the relative size of the primordia decreases, the visible pair of parastichy families changes from $(3, 5)$ successively to $(5, 8)$ and $(8, 13)$, which is the same divergence angle expressed at different scales.

### 21.4.3. How Phyllotaxis Is Generated

Phyllotaxis is generated at the shoot apex. New leaf primordia always arise in the peripheral zone of the apex at the gap farthest from the existing primordia, an empirical rule proposed by Hofmeister (1868). This suggests that existing primordia exert some inhibition on their surroundings and that a new primordium appears only where the inhibition is weak enough. Following this "inhibitory field" idea, as long as primordia arise successively on a continuously growing shoot apex, each new one appearing farthest from the earlier ones, the system spontaneously selects the golden angle. Douady and Couder (1992) dropped magnetized fluid droplets periodically onto the center of a dish; the droplets repel one another and drift outward, and at a suitable dropping period successive droplets spontaneously arrange themselves at an angle of about $137.5^\circ$ and Fibonacci parastichies appear. This shows that phyllotaxis is a **self-organization** pattern ([7.5.12](/7.5.12)): the divergence angle need not be "encoded" by genes but arises from interactions among primordia and the growth dynamics of the shoot apex.

It is now known that the material basis of this interaction is auxin. The auxin efflux carrier (PIN1) in the epidermal cells of the shoot apex concentrates auxin where a primordium will form, producing an auxin maximum that induces primordium initiation; the new primordium then acts as a sink that draws auxin from its surroundings, so that no new primordium can form nearby for a time (Reinhardt et al. 2003). The ratio of the spacing of primordia, and of the size of the shoot apex to the size of the primordia, determines the type of phyllotaxis: when this ratio changes, phyllotaxis can switch among opposite, decussate and spiral. The molecular mechanism belongs to plant developmental biology.

## 21.5. Primary Structure of the Stem

Tissues formed by division, growth and differentiation of cells derived from the primary meristems of the shoot apical meristem are called primary tissues, and they make up the primary structure of the stem (for definitions of primary growth and primary structure see [7.12.20](/7.12.20)). Internodes make up most of the stem, and the structure at the nodes is more complex (Section 21.6), so primary structure is generally represented by the internode.

### 21.5.1. Primary Structure of the Eudicot Stem

The primary structure of a eudicot stem has three parts: epidermis, cortex and vascular cylinder (Figure 4A).

1. Epidermis: usually a single layer of living cells developed from the protoderm, generally without chloroplasts, and the primary protective tissue of the stem. In cross section epidermal cells are rectangular or square, and in longitudinal section rectangular with the long axis parallel to the stem axis; the cells have a well-developed vacuole, with the protoplast pressed against the cell wall. The outer tangential wall exposed to air is thick and cutinized and bears a cuticle; the stems of castor bean and sugarcane also have a wax coating. These features, which regulate transpiration and increase the toughness of the epidermis, are common in the epidermis of aerial stems; in xerophytes the cuticle of the stem is markedly thickened, and in submerged plants it is very thin or absent. The epidermis bears stomata, the passages for water vapor and gases, and various trichomes: dense wool reflects strong light and reduces transpiration, stiff hairs deter feeding by animals, and hooked hairs allow the stem to climb. In some plants (castor bean, sugarcane) the epidermal cells contain anthocyanin, which makes the stem red or purple.
2. Cortex: lies between the epidermis and the vascular cylinder, differentiates from the ground meristem, is several cells thick and consists mainly of parenchyma. The parenchyma cells are living, thin-walled, with intercellular spaces, and generally isodiametric in cross section; in young stems the parenchyma cells near the epidermis contain chloroplasts and carry out photosynthesis, and they usually also store nutrients; in the stems of aquatic plants the cortical parenchyma has well-developed intercellular spaces that form aerenchyma. One to several layers of cortical cells just inside the epidermis often differentiate into **collenchyma**, distributed as a continuous layer or in strands; in square (mint, faba bean) or angled (celery) stems the collenchyma is concentrated at the corners or ridges, the distribution that best resists bending. In the cortex of some stems there are also fibers or sclereids, as in pumpkin, whose cortex contains both fibers and collenchyma. The innermost cortical layer is sometimes an endodermis, but in most stems it is indistinct or absent, and an endodermis is common only in the stems of aquatic plants and in the underground stems of some plants. In nasturtium, pumpkin, faba bean and others the innermost cortical layer (corresponding to the endodermis) is rich in starch grains and is called the starch sheath; sedimentation of amyloplasts in the starch sheath is related to gravitropism of the stem and corresponds to the statoliths of the columella cells of the root cap.
3. Vascular cylinder: the part inside the cortex; in most eudicot stems it comprises vascular bundles, pith and pith rays. Unlike in most roots, there is no distinct endodermis and no pericycle in the stem, so the boundary between the cortex and the vascular cylinder is hard to draw (for the history and usage of the term "stele" see [7.12.16](/7.12.16)).

A vascular bundle is a strand-like structure composed jointly of primary xylem and primary phloem and differentiated from the procambium ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)). In the internodes of most plants the vascular bundles are arranged in one ring, separated from one another by interfascicular parenchyma; in some plants the bundles appear to merge into a ring, but on close observation one can still see some separation among them. In eudicots there is cambium between the primary xylem and primary phloem of the bundle, which can go on producing new xylem and phloem, so the bundle is open; most are collateral, whereas in Cucurbitaceae, Convolvulaceae, Solanaceae and Apocynaceae the bundles are bicollateral (there is no cambium, or only very weak cambium, between the internal phloem and the xylem).

- Primary xylem: composed of vessels, tracheids, xylem parenchyma and xylem fibers. The primary xylem of the stem develops in **endarch** order ([7.12.16](/7.12.16)): protoxylem lies to the inside (near the pith) and consists of annular or spiral vessels of small diameter; metaxylem lies to the outside and consists of scalariform, reticulate or pitted vessels of larger diameter, of which pitted vessels are the most common. This is exactly the reverse of the exarch order in the root. Xylem parenchyma is more abundant in the protoxylem and has a storage role; xylem fibers occur mostly in the metaxylem.
- Primary phloem: composed of sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma and phloem fibers, and it mainly transports organic compounds. Its order of development is the same as in the root, also exarch (centripetal): protophloem is on the outside and metaphloem on the inside. Phloem parenchyma cells are scattered throughout the primary phloem, larger than the companion cells, and often contain crystals, tannins and starch; phloem fibers are often arranged in strands at the outermost side of the primary phloem, differentiate from the protophloem, and were formerly mistaken for pericycle fibers.
- Fascicular cambium: a layer of tissue with latent meristematic ability between primary phloem and primary xylem, retained from the procambium during differentiation of the primary vascular tissue, which later plays the main part in the thickening of woody stems ([7.12.22](/7.12.22)).

**Definition 21.5.1 (Fascicular Cambium)** The layer of cells, in the vascular bundles of eudicot and gymnosperm stems, lying between the primary xylem and primary phloem and retained from the procambium so that it keeps its ability to divide, is called the *fascicular cambium*.

The pith is the central part of the primary structure of the stem, made of parenchyma, produced by the ground meristem and often storing nutrients. In some plants (camphor) the pith contains sclereids; in others (linden) there are small thick-walled cells at the outer edge of the pith surrounding large inner cells, with a sharp boundary between them, and the outer zone is called the perimedullary zone; in the Apiaceae and Cucurbitaceae the pith matures early and is torn apart as the stem goes on elongating, forming a pith cavity; the stems of walnut and wingnut retain some plate-like pith tissue in the internodes (diaphragmed pith), a diagnostic feature of these two genera.

**Definition 21.5.2 (Pith Ray)** The radially arranged parenchyma in the primary structure of the stem that lies between the vascular bundles, connects the pith inward with the cortex outward, and appears as radial bands in cross section, is called a *pith ray* (also a primary ray).

Pith rays are produced by the ground meristem and function in lateral transport and storage. They differ in origin from the vascular rays of the secondary structure produced by the vascular cambium ([7.12.22](/7.12.22)).

### 21.5.2. Primary Structure of the Gymnosperm Stem

The primary structure of a gymnosperm stem is broadly similar to that of a eudicot stem and also comprises epidermis, cortex and vascular cylinder. Take pine as an example. The epidermis is a layer of tightly packed isodiametric cells. The cortex is several layers of parenchyma cells, generally round, highly vacuolated and containing chloroplasts, with intercellular spaces, and schizogenous resin canals ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)) lie in the cortex; there is no distinct boundary between cortex and vascular cylinder. The vascular cylinder consists of vascular bundles, pith and pith rays, and the bundles have fascicular cambium between xylem and phloem, which later produces the secondary structure that thickens the stem.

The main differences lie in the composition of the vascular tissue. The xylem of most gymnosperms consists only of tracheids (except the gnetophytes, [7.12.5](/7.12.5)); the protoxylem has annular or single-spiral tracheids and the metaxylem has multiple-spiral or scalariform tracheids. The phloem consists of sieve cells rather than sieve tubes, and has no companion cells (it has albuminous cells). All gymnosperms are woody and have no herbaceous stems; after a brief primary stage they all enter secondary growth, whereas eudicots include herbaceous stems that stay in the primary state for life.

### 21.5.3. Primary Structure of the Monocot Stem

Most monocot stems have only primary structure and are fairly simple; the few that have secondary structure form it in a way different from eudicots ([7.12.22](/7.12.22)). The vascular bundles of the great majority of monocots consist of xylem and phloem, have no fascicular cambium, and are closed collateral bundles. The bundles are clearly separate from one another and are generally arranged in one of two ways. In one they are scattered irregularly throughout the ground tissue, more numerous and smaller toward the outside and fewer and larger toward the center, so that cortex and pith are hard to distinguish, as in maize, sorghum and sugarcane (this arrangement of vascular tissue is called an **atactostele**, [7.12.16](/7.12.16)). In the other they are arranged more regularly, generally in two rings with the pith at the center, which breaks down as the stem grows and forms a pith cavity, as in rice and wheat.

Take maize as an example (Figure 4B). A cross section through an internode of the mature stem shows three parts: epidermis, ground tissue and vascular bundles.

1. Epidermis: in cross section the cells are fairly regularly arranged. A peeled piece of epidermis shows that it consists of alternating long cells and short cells. The long cells are cutinized epidermal cells and make up most of the epidermis; the short cells lie between two long cells and are of two kinds, suberized cork cells and silica cells containing silicon dioxide; in addition there are a few sparsely arranged stomatal complexes ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)).
2. Ground tissue: except for the part adjoining the epidermis, it is all parenchyma, with cells larger toward the center and vascular bundles scattered in it, so that cortex and pith cannot be separated and the ground tissue combines the functions of both. The ground tissue near the epidermis consists of thick-walled cells that strengthen the stem and are important in resisting lodging; in young stems the ground-tissue cells near the surface contain chloroplasts and are green. In old stems the epidermis and hypodermis become lignified, making the stem still stronger.
3. Vascular bundles: each mature bundle is nearly oval in cross section and surrounded on the outside by sclerenchyma (fibers) that forms a sheath-like *bundle sheath*. Within the bundle, going from outside inward, one meets first the phloem and then the xylem; there is no cambium. The cells of the metaphloem are neatly arranged, and in cross section one sees nearly hexagonal or octagonal sieve tubes and rectangular companion cells in alternating arrangement; on the outside of the phloem, where it meets the bundle sheath, there is a band of crushed cells of indistinct shape, the protophloem, which differentiated first and was later destroyed. Next to the metaphloem lie the two large pitted vessels of the metaxylem, with a narrow band of small thick-walled tracheids between them; further inward is the protoxylem, consisting of two or three smaller annular or spiral vessels in a row. The two pitted vessels and the row of annular and spiral vessels form a "V" in cross section, a striking feature of grass stems. The innermost vessel of the protoxylem is often torn as the stem elongates, and the surrounding parenchyma cells separate from one another to form a cavity, called the protoxylem lacuna. Thus in the vascular bundles of monocot stems the phloem differentiates from outside inward (exarch) and the xylem from inside outward (endarch); this is a feature of the stem and has no exception in grasses. The vascular bundles are more numerous at the periphery of a maize stem cross section because many bundles run obliquely outward from within the stem into the leaf sheaths at the leaf bases.

Bamboos are also grasses, and their stem is generally called a culm. The nodes of the bamboo culm are particularly conspicuous: a node bears two rings, the upper called the culm ring and the lower the sheath ring, the ring where the culm sheath (the leaf sheath enclosing a bamboo shoot, the shoot husk) is attached; the segment between the two rings is called the intranode, and the three together make up the bamboo node, at which a transverse diaphragm closes off the hollow internode. The part of the culm of moso bamboo (*Phyllostachys edulis*) from the epidermis to the pith cavity is called the culm wall and, from outside inward, consists of the outer green layer (epidermis and the chlorophyll-containing ground tissue near the epidermis, green), the middle layer (the ground tissue in between) and the inner yellow layer (the wall of the pith cavity). The structure of the moso bamboo culm lies between maize and wheat: the vascular bundles are scattered, as in maize, and the internodes are hollow, as in wheat. It is distinctive in three respects:

1. Mechanical tissue is particularly well developed: beneath the epidermis is a hypodermis of sclerenchyma, near the pith cavity are several layers of sclereids, and each vascular bundle is surrounded by a sheath of fibers; the closer to the periphery, the better developed and more numerous the bundle fibers, with correspondingly fewer xylem and phloem cells, and the outermost bundles may consist of fibers alone; these fibers are thick-walled and lignified.
2. After the protoxylem lacuna has formed, it is filled in again by the surrounding parenchyma cells.
3. The cell walls of the ground tissue are much thicker than in maize and wheat, and all later become lignified.

Although the bamboo culm has only primary tissues, it is therefore solid and has excellent mechanical properties, and people regard it as a woody stem; bamboo timber rivals wood. The hollow tube with a dense periphery gives the culm the greatest bending stiffness for a given amount of material (buckling and beam mechanics in [7.6.26](/7.6.26#sec-26.2.1)), and its specific strength is close to that of steel.

The vascular bundles of most monocot stems have no cambium and cannot thicken indefinitely, but the stems of maize, sugarcane and palms nonetheless thicken noticeably. There are two reasons: first, thousands upon thousands of cells in the primary tissue enlarge; second, a median longitudinal section of the shoot apex shows, beneath the leaf primordia and young leaves, several layers of flattened cells forming the primary thickening meristem, parallel to the stem surface and dividing periclinally to add cells, so that the young stem thickens continuously. The palm stem first thickens by means of the primary thickening meristem near the ground in youth to the thickness of the mature stem ("establishment growth") and only then begins to elongate upward, so that the palm trunk is nearly equally thick from bottom to top ([7.12.22](/7.12.22#sec-22.5)).

## 21.6. Anatomy of the Node

The node is where leaves are attached. Vascular bundles in the leaf pass through the node into the stem and connect with the stem's bundles, and the vascular bundles of the axillary bud in the leaf axil also join the stem here, so the arrangement of vascular tissue at the node is much more complex than in the internode.

**Definition 21.6.1 (Leaf Trace and Branch Trace)** The segment of a vascular bundle of the stem that, from the point where it departs from the stem's vascular system, curves outward through the cortex into the base of the petiole is called the **leaf trace**; the segment of vascular bundle on the vascular cylinder of the stem that leads to a lateral branch, passing through the cortex into the branch, is called the *branch trace*.

Some leaf traces enter the petiole directly at the node after departing from a bundle of the stem; others first merge with other leaf traces and then ascend through the cortex past one or more nodes before entering the base of the petiole. Where a leaf trace departs from the vascular cylinder of the stem and curves outward, a region filled with parenchyma appears in the vascular cylinder above it, the **leaf gap** ([7.12.16](/7.12.16)); the similar region above a branch trace is called a branch gap. In eudicots and gymnosperms the branch traces leading to an axillary bud are generally two, though one or several also occur. In the eustele of seed plants the vascular bundles are separate to begin with, and the parenchyma at the departure of the leaf trace merges with the interfascicular region, so the "leaf gap" here is not homologous with the leaf gap in the tubular siphonostele of ferns, and some authors call it a leaf trace gap.

The number of leaf traces and of leaf gaps per leaf at a node is rather stable within a family or genus, and nodes can be divided into several types accordingly (Figure 5):

| Node type | Gaps per leaf | Traces per leaf | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unilacunar | 1 | 1, or 2 (from the same gap) | Ericaceae, Lamiaceae, many gymnosperms (mostly 1 trace); ginkgo has 1 gap with 2 traces, which Bailey regarded as the ancestral nodal condition of angiosperms |
| Trilacunar | 3 | 3 | Rosaceae, Salicaceae, Moraceae, many Fabaceae: one central trace and one lateral trace on each side |
| Multilacunar | many | many | Apiaceae, Polygonaceae, Magnoliaceae, many monocots (leaf sheath clasping the stem, with up to dozens of leaf traces) |

The number of leaf traces is related to the width of the leaf base: leaves with a broad base, stipules or a sheath clasping the stem have many leaf traces. The bundle scars in the leaf scars of winter twigs are cross sections of the leaf traces, so the number of bundle scars reflects the node type (the statement that "eudicots generally have three bundle scars" is a manifestation of the trilacunar node). The vascular system of the stem is in fact built from a series of linked leaf traces: in many plants every bundle in the internode, traced upward, eventually leaves the vascular cylinder as the leaf trace of some leaf, so the vascular system of the stem can be viewed as a "sympodium" of leaf traces. The one-to-one correspondence between the vascular system and the phyllotaxis shows once more that stem and leaf develop as a whole, the shoot. The connection of leaf traces with the vascular bundles of the leaf, and of the vascular tissue of stem and root in the transition region, is discussed further in [7.12.24](/7.12.24).

## 21.7. Comparison of the Primary Structure of Root and Stem

Root and stem both consist of three tissue systems, epidermis, cortex and vascular cylinder, and both arise from the protoderm, ground meristem and procambium of the apical meristem, but they are adapted to different functions and environments and differ markedly in the structure of each part:

| Feature | Root | Stem (eudicot) |
|---|---|---|
| Apex | Root cap protects the apical meristem | No root cap; protected in the bud by enclosing young leaves |
| Lateral organs | Lateral roots, endogenous (pericycle), no nodes | Leaves and axillary buds, exogenous (surface of the shoot apex), with nodes and internodes |
| Epidermis | Thin-walled, thin cuticle, no stomata, with root hairs (absorptive tissue) | Cutinized outer wall, with cuticle, stomata and trichomes (protective tissue) |
| Cortex | Wide, mainly parenchyma, with exodermis | Narrower, often with collenchyma at the periphery, contains chloroplasts |
| Endodermis | Distinct, with Casparian strip | Mostly indistinct, sometimes a starch sheath |
| Pericycle | Distinct, gives rise to lateral roots, vascular cambium and cork cambium | Mostly indistinct |
| Arrangement of vascular tissue | Primary xylem and primary phloem alternate (radial bundles) | Primary xylem and primary phloem side by side in bundles (collateral, bicollateral) |
| Development of primary xylem | Exarch | Endarch |
| Development of primary phloem | Exarch | Exarch |
| Pith | Mostly absent (present in monocots and some eudicots) | Present |
| Pith rays | Absent | Present |
| Origin of vascular cambium | Parenchyma inside the phloem plus the pericycle opposite the xylem ridges, wavy at first and then becoming a ring | Fascicular plus interfascicular cambium, a ring from the start |
| First cork cambium | Pericycle | Mostly cortical cells just below the epidermis |

Monocot roots and stems also differ in a similar way: the root has a polyarch radial vascular cylinder with endodermis and pericycle, and the stem has scattered closed collateral bundles.

- A bud (Definition 21.1.2) is an unexpanded shoot, flower or inflorescence: apical meristem, leaf primordia, axillary bud primordia, young leaves, bud axis. Four classifications: normal buds (terminal and axillary buds, Definition 21.1.3; accessory and subpetiolar buds) and adventitious buds; scaly and naked buds; vegetative, floral and mixed buds (Definition 21.1.3); active and dormant buds.

- Branching (Definition 21.2.1): monopodial (gymnosperms, poplar), sympodial (most angiosperms; advanced and productive), false dichotomous (opposite leaves, lilac), dichotomous (apical meristem splits equally; lycophytes, liverworts); tillering (Definition 21.2.2), tiller position and productive tillers.
- Shoot apex: promeristem → protoderm, ground meristem, procambium; leaf and axillary bud primordia of exogenous origin, leaf buttress; adventitious buds.
- Phyllotaxis (Definition 21.4.1): divergence angle, Schimper–Braun fractions $F_n/F_{n+2} \to 1/\varphi^2$, golden angle $137.5^\circ$ (Eq. (21.1)); Vogel's model (Eq. (21.2)) and parastichies in consecutive Fibonacci numbers; Hofmeister's rule, auxin maxima and self-organization.
- Primary structure of the eudicot stem: epidermis (cuticle, stomata), cortex (collenchyma, starch sheath), vascular cylinder (open collateral bundles, endarch primary xylem, exarch primary phloem, fascicular cambium Definition 21.5.1, pith, pith rays Definition 21.5.2). Gymnosperms: tracheids, sieve cells, resin canals, no herbaceous stems. Monocot (maize): epidermis of long and short cells, ground tissue, scattered closed bundles (bundle sheath, "V"-shaped xylem, protoxylem lacuna); bamboo: culm ring and sheath ring, well-developed mechanical tissue, lacuna filled in, lignified ground tissue; primary thickening meristem.
- Node: leaf traces and branch traces (Definition 21.6.1), leaf gaps and branch gaps; unilacunar, trilacunar and multilacunar nodes; the vascular system of the stem is built of leaf traces.

## Questions

1. How can an underground stem be told from a root by external form? And how can a herbaceous stem be told from a young root in cross section?

   **Answer** In external form, the stem has nodes and internodes; the nodes bear leaves (mostly reduced scale leaves on underground stems) or leaf scars, buds lie in the axils and at the tip, and lateral branches arise at the nodes. The root has no nodes or leaves, lateral roots arise internally and are often arranged in longitudinal rows according to the number of xylem poles, and the root tip bears a root cap. In cross section, a young root has an epidermis with root hairs and no stomata, a distinct endodermis (Casparian strip) and pericycle, primary xylem and primary phloem in alternating arrangement with exarch xylem, and often no pith. A herbaceous stem has an epidermis with cuticle and stomata, collenchyma outside the cortex, bundles in which xylem and phloem lie side by side with endarch xylem, and a pith with pith rays.

2. A spiral-leaved plant has a divergence angle of exactly $144^\circ$ (that is, $2/5$ phyllotaxis). Compared with a plant with a divergence angle of $137.5^\circ$, how will its leaves be arranged when viewed from directly above the stem? Which shades the lower leaves less?

   **Answer** $144^\circ = \frac{2}{5}\times 360^\circ$ is a rational fraction: after every 5 leaves the sequence has gone twice around the stem, and the 6th leaf lies exactly above the 1st, so all the leaves fall in 5 straight rows (orthostichies) and upper leaves directly shade the lower leaves in the same row. The golden angle is irrational, so no leaf lies exactly above another; the leaves fall one after another into the gaps among the existing leaves, and in plan view they are most evenly distributed and thus shade the lower leaves less. In real plants, elongation of internodes and twisting of petioles further adjust leaf position (leaf mosaic).

3. Use Eq. (21.2) to explain why the numbers of visible parastichies on a sunflower head are always consecutive Fibonacci numbers rather than arbitrary pairs of integers.

   **Answer** Joining primordia whose numbers differ by $m$ gives a family of parastichies. This family is conspicuous when these primordia are spatially closest to one another, that is, when the angle difference $m\alpha$ is closest to an integer multiple of $360^\circ$ while the radius difference is not large. For $\alpha = 360^\circ/\varphi^2$, the difference between $m\alpha/360^\circ = m/\varphi^2$ and the nearest integer is smallest when $m$ is a Fibonacci number (ratios of Fibonacci numbers are the best rational approximations to $1/\varphi^2$), so only primordia whose number difference is a Fibonacci number are almost radially aligned. At a given radius the two nearest neighbors differ in number by two consecutive Fibonacci numbers $F_j$ and $F_{j+1}$, so the numbers of lines in the two visible families are $(F_j, F_{j+1})$; as the radius increases, the neighbor relation changes in turn to $(F_{j+1}, F_{j+2})$.

4. A maize stem has no cambium; why can it still grow much thicker than the seedling? Why does a bamboo shoot grow only taller and not thicker after emerging from the ground?

   **Answer** The thickening of the maize stem comes from primary growth: the primary thickening meristem beneath the leaf primordia at the shoot tip divides periclinally and thickens the young stem, and at the same time the cells of the primary tissue enlarge greatly; thickening is completed near the shoot tip and does not continue afterward. In bamboo the primary thickening meristem likewise acts only in the shoot (bamboo shoot): the shoot has already reached the final thickness of the culm in its underground bud before emerging, and after emergence it grows tall rapidly only by division and cell elongation of the intercalary meristems at the bases of the internodes. Bamboo has no vascular cambium, so the mature culm cannot thicken and can only lignify and harden year by year.

5. In winter you examine a twig of Chinese parasol tree or poplar. How can you tell how many years of growth it has? How can you tell its phyllotaxis and node type?

   **Answer** Going down from the terminal bud, each dense ring of bud scale scars marks the end of a year's growth, and the segment between two rings is one year's growth; counting the rings of bud scale scars gives the age of the twig (the age of a lateral branch is judged in the same way). The arrangement of leaf scars on the stem reflects the phyllotaxis: one leaf scar per node arranged in a spiral means spiral alternate phyllotaxis, and two opposite scars per node mean opposite phyllotaxis. The number of bundle scars within a leaf scar reflects the number of leaf traces: three bundle scars usually mean a trilacunar node, one bundle scar a unilacunar node, and many bundle scars in an arc a multilacunar node.
