The lamiids are the largest branch of the **asterids**, with about 45,000 species, and include coffee, potato, tomato, eggplant, pepper, tobacco, sweet potato, olive and sesame, as well as mint, *Salvia* and most other common culinary and medicinal herbs (honeysuckle, by contrast, is a campanulid). Cronquist's subclass Asteridae corresponds roughly to the APG asterids, but its orders have been recombined: Gentianales has absorbed the formerly independent Rubiales, Eucommia has moved in from the Hamamelidae, Boraginaceae has been removed from Lamiales to form its own order Boraginales, and Solanales has lost Menyanthaceae and Polemoniaceae ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.5.2)). Most lamiids have opposite, entire leaves, a superior ovary and a sympetalous corolla that is already a ring early in development. This chapter treats the circumscription of the lamiids and the small basal orders (§47.1), then Gentianales (§47.2), Boraginales (§47.3) and Solanales (§47.4); Lamiales, the largest order of the lamiids, is left to [7.12.48](/7.12.48). Cornales and Ericales, also asterids, are in [7.12.46](/7.12.46), and the campanulids are in [7.12.49](/7.12.49). For the notation of floral formulas see [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.6).

## 47.1. Circumscription and Synapomorphies of the Lamiids

### 47.1.1. Circumscription of the Lamiids

**Definition 47.1.1 (Lamiids)** The monophyletic group formed in the APG system by Icacinales, Metteniusales, Garryales, Gentianales, Boraginales, Vahliales, Solanales and Lamiales is called the **lamiids**, also known as the euasterids I and, in phylogenetic nomenclature, as Lamiidae (or Garryidae). It is the sister group of the campanulids ([7.12.49](/7.12.49)); together they form the euasterids.

In euasterids the corolla is sympetalous, the stamens equal the corolla lobes in number (or are fewer) and are borne on the corolla tube, and the gynoecium usually consists of 2 fused carpels. The general morphological differences between lamiids and campanulids are as follows (Bremer et al. 2001):

| Character | Lamiids | Campanulids |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves | Often opposite, entire | Often alternate, toothed |
| Ovary | Superior (except Rubiaceae) | Inferior (except Aquifoliales and Pittosporaceae) |
| Corolla development | Early sympetaly: the corolla primordium is a ring from the start | Late sympetaly: free petal primordia appear first and fuse later |
| Stamens | Filaments fused to the corolla tube | Filaments often free |
| Fruit | Often a capsule | Often indehiscent |
| Chemistry | Iridoids common | Polyacetylenes common, iridoids rare |

These are only general tendencies; each has exceptions and parallel origins, and none can be used alone for identification. The monophyly of the lamiids is supported mainly by molecular data: with only 3 genes the support is low, and with 6 or more genes it approaches 100%.

In APG IV the lamiids first split off Icacinales, Metteniusales and Garryales in succession, and the remaining five orders form the "core lamiids": Gentianales, Boraginales, Vahliales, Solanales and Lamiales. The relationships within the core lamiids are not fully resolved; a fairly consistent result is that Boraginales and Lamiales are sister groups, both having a terminal endosperm haustorium (Figure 47.1.1).

### 47.1.2. The Small Basal Orders

- Icacinales: the traditional Icacinaceae is polyphyletic, and molecular data place its members in three places: Icacinaceae in the narrow sense remain in Icacinales among the lamiids, a second group forms Metteniusaceae (Metteniusales), and a third has moved to Stemonuraceae and Cardiopteridaceae in Aquifoliales among the campanulids ([7.12.49](/7.12.49)). Icacinaceae in the narrow sense are tropical trees, shrubs and woody climbers; China has *Iodes*, *Mappianthus*, *Nothapodytes* and a few others. *Nothapodytes* (*N. pittosporoides* and others) contains the precursor of the anticancer drug camptothecin and is a second plant source of camptothecin besides *Camptotheca acuminata* (Nyssaceae, [7.12.46](/7.12.46)). Oncothecaceae consists only of the genus *Oncotheca* of New Caledonia.
- Metteniusales: only Metteniusaceae, with about 11 genera and 50 species in the tropics, including *Apodytes* and *Platea* (the latter occurring in southern China), formerly in Icacinaceae.
- Vahliales: only Vahliaceae with the genus *Vahlia* (5 species), herbs of Africa to northwestern India. Its petals are free and the ovary is inferior, and it was traditionally placed in Saxifragaceae; iridoids and tenuinucellate ovules support its place among the asterids, but its exact position within the core lamiids is not yet settled.

### 47.1.3. Garryales and Eucommiaceae

Garryales (also called Eucommiales) comprise Eucommiaceae and Garryaceae. Garryaceae include *Garrya* of western North America and *Aucuba* of eastern Asia; *Aucuba chinensis* and *A. japonica* (spotted laurel) are evergreen shrubs with opposite leaves and unisexual flowers on separate plants, 4-merous, with an inferior ovary, formerly placed in Cornaceae. The synapomorphies of Garryales are: woody plants, dioecious, with an inferior ovary bearing a single short style and apical placentation, and valvate tepals; all three genera contain iridoids such as aucubin. The affinity of *Eucommia* with Garryaceae was one of the unexpected results of molecular systematics.

Eucommiaceae comprise only *Eucommia ulmoides* (hardy rubber tree), a single genus with a single species, a relict endemic to China, native from central to southwestern China and cultivated widely. Diagnostic features:

- Deciduous tree; all parts of the plant have **laticifers** containing white rubber threads, visible as silvery threads connecting the pieces when bark is broken or a leaf is torn.
- Leaves simple, alternate, serrate, pinnately veined, without stipules.
- Dioecious; flowers without a perianth, borne from scaly buds before or with the leaves, in the axils of bracts. Male flowers clustered and short-stalked, with 4–10 stamens, very short filaments and 4-locular anthers dehiscing longitudinally; pollen tricolpate. Female flowers solitary, with an ovary of 2 fused carpels of which only 1 develops, unilocular, with 2 anatropous, pendulous ovules and a 2-branched stigma.
- Fruit a flat, oblong **samara** winged all around, with 1 seed; seed with endosperm. Chromosome base number $x = 17$.

Floral formula: ♂ $P_0 A_{4\text{–}10}$; ♀ $P_0 \underline{G}_{(2:1:2)}$.

The rubber threads of *Eucommia* are trans-1,4-polyisoprene (eucommia gum, identical to gutta-percha), an isomer of the cis-1,4-polyisoprene of the rubber tree (*Hevea*, Euphorbiaceae): eucommia gum is hard and inelastic at room temperature, insulating and resistant to acids and bases, and was once used for submarine cables. The bark is used in medicine ("duzhong"), and the leaves also contain eucommia gum and iridoids (aucubin, geniposidic acid). Fossil fruits and leaves of *Eucommia* were widespread in North America, Europe and East Asia in the Paleogene, and the single living species survives only in China.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist placed Eucommiaceae in Eucommiales of the Hamamelidae, thinking that it had evolved from Hamamelidales (Eupteleaceae) and, because of its samara resembling that of elms, linking it with Ulmaceae of Urticales; these similarities are superficial, and molecular evidence places it in Garryales among the lamiids.

## 47.2. Gentianales

### 47.2.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Gentianales comprise about 1100 genera and 20,000 species in 5 families: Rubiaceae, Gentianaceae, Loganiaceae, Gelsemiaceae and Apocynaceae (including Asclepiadaceae). It is one of the best supported orders of the lamiids, with the synapomorphies:

- leaves opposite or whorled, entire, with stipules or a stipular line connecting the petiole bases;
- mucilage-secreting glandular hairs (colleters) in the leaf axils or on the inner face of the stipules;
- corolla lobes often contorted in bud;
- vestured pits in the wood;
- frequent occurrence of monoterpenoid indole alkaloids (for example quinine, strychnine, vinblastine, reserpine).

Rubiaceae are the sister group of the other four families; the latter share intraxylary phloem (bicollateral bundles, [7.12.21](/7.12.21)), and the relationships among them are not yet certain. Cronquist's Gentianales included Loganiaceae, Gentianaceae, Apocynaceae, Asclepiadaceae and others, and placed Rubiaceae in a separate order Rubiales, thought to be closest to Loganiaceae (opposite leaves, well-developed stipules, regular flowers); APG confirmed this relationship and merged the two orders.

### 47.2.2. Rubiaceae

Rubiaceae (madder family) are the fourth largest family of angiosperms, with about 600 genera and 13,000 species (the Chinese textbook counts 660 genera and 11,150 species, with 97 genera and 701 species in China). They are distributed worldwide, most diverse in the tropics and especially rich in the understory shrub layer of tropical rain forests.

**Definition 47.2.1 (Interpetiolar Stipule)** A stipule situated between the two petioles of opposite leaves, on either side of the stem, is called an **interpetiolar stipule**. It usually arises from the fusion of one stipule of each of the two adjacent leaves, so that there are two at each node.

Diagnostic features:

- Trees, shrubs or herbs, sometimes climbing.
- Leaves simple, opposite or whorled, often entire; with interpetiolar stipules (rarely intrapetiolar), free or fused, often persistent; colleters on the inner face of the stipule base.
- Flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, 4- or 5-merous, solitary or in various cymes, sometimes crowded into heads (*Uncaria*, *Adina*).
- Calyx tube adnate to the ovary; sometimes one calyx lobe enlarged into a colored, leaf-like blade (*Emmenopterys*, *Mussaenda*).
- Corolla sympetalous, tubular, funnelform, salverform or rotate, with lobes valvate, contorted or imbricate; stamens as many as the corolla lobes and alternate with them, borne on the corolla tube.
- Ovary inferior, usually 2-locular, with 1 to many ovules per locule; style filiform, stigma capitate or branched.
- Capsule, berry or drupe; seeds with endosperm.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(4\text{–}5)} C_{(4\text{–}5)} A_{4\text{–}5} \overline{G}_{(2:2:1\text{–}\infty)}$.

Key features of the family: leaves opposite or whorled, entire, with interpetiolar stipules. Flowers 4–5-merous, regular, sympetalous, with the stamens inserted on the corolla tube. Ovary inferior, usually 2-locular. In a plant that is not flowering, "opposite entire leaves plus interpetiolar stipules" almost certainly means Rubiaceae.

**Example 47.2.1 (The "Whorled Leaves" of *Rubia*)** In madder (*Rubia cordifolia*) and in cleavers such as *Galium spurium*, 4–8 leaves are whorled, but in each whorl only 2 leaves have axillary buds and the others do not. This shows that only 2 of the "leaves" in a whorl are true leaves, and the rest are stipules of the same shape and size as the leaves: the whorled leaves of the tribe Rubieae result from the leaf-like development of interpetiolar stipules.

Rubiaceae are usually divided into 3 subfamilies: Rubioideae (more herbs, often with raphides and anthraquinones, for example madder, *Hedyotis*, cleavers), Cinchonoideae (for example cinchona, *Uncaria*) and Ixoroideae (for example gardenia, coffee, *Ixora*). Heterostyly is very common in Rubioideae (for example *Ophiorrhiza*, *Psychotria*, *Mussaenda*) and is classical material for the study of **heterostyly** ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3)).

Representative plants:

1. *Gardenia*: shrubs with stipules fused into a sheath within the petiole; corolla salverform with contorted lobes; fruit a berry. Cape jasmine (*G. jasminoides*) has opposite or 3-whorled leaves, white, strongly scented flowers and yellow fruits with 5–9 winged ribs; it is native to southern and central China and widely cultivated. The fruit is used in medicine and contains geniposide (an iridoid) and crocin-type pigments, and is a traditional yellow dye.
2. *Uncaria*: woody climbers; flowers 5-merous, crowded into globose heads; capsule with seeds winged at both ends. *U. rhynchophylla* has square young shoots, and the leaf axils bear hooks formed from undeveloped peduncles, by which it climbs; it occurs in eastern to southern China, and the hook-bearing stems are used in medicine and contain rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline.
3. *Emmenopterys*: *E. henryi* is a large deciduous tree with large terminal panicles; in some flowers one calyx lobe is enlarged into a white leaf-like blade that persists in fruit; capsule 3–5 cm long, red when ripe. A species endemic to China, found in the southwest and the Yangtze basin, it is a nationally protected species (Class II) and a fine ornamental and timber tree.
4. *Rubia* (madder): climbing herbs with 4-angled stems bearing recurved prickles on the angles; leaves 4–8 in whorls (Example 47.2.1); flowers 5-merous, rotate; berry small, globose, black. The root of madder contains alizarin (an anthraquinone) and was one of the most important red dyes of antiquity (in Europe from *R. tinctorum*) until alizarin was synthesized in 1869.
5. *Hedyotis*: herbs or subshrubs with small 4-merous flowers; capsule. *H. diffusa* (recently often treated as *Scleromitrion diffusum*) is a small annual herb with linear leaves, stipules bearing bristles at the tip, and solitary white flowers in the leaf axils; it occurs in southeastern to southwestern China, and the whole plant is used in medicine.
6. *Coffea* (coffee): shrubs or small trees; flowers clustered in the leaf axils, white, fragrant, salverform with contorted lobes; fruit a drupe with 2 seeds (the "coffee beans" are the seeds). About 130 species, mainly in tropical Africa and Madagascar. Arabica coffee (*C. arabica*) is an **allopolyploid** ($2n = 4x = 44$) that arose by hybridization between *C. canephora* (robusta) and *C. eugenioides*; it originated in Ethiopia, makes up most of the world's coffee production, and is grown in Yunnan and Hainan. The seeds contain caffeine.
7. *Cinchona*: evergreen trees with early-falling stipules; flowers 5-merous in terminal panicles, with long tubular corollas whose lobes are valvate and hairy at the margin; capsule septicidal, seeds small and winged. About 25 species, native to the Andes of South America. The bark contains quinine, which reached Europe in the 17th century and was the first effective remedy for malaria; *C. officinalis*, red cinchona (*C. pubescens*, called *C. succirubra* in Chinese textbooks) and yellow cinchona (*C. calisaya*, to which the quinine-rich cultivated lineage *C. ledgeriana* belongs) are all cultivated and have been introduced to Yunnan, Hainan and Taiwan.
8. Others: the root of *Morinda officinalis* is used in medicine; *Paederia foetida* (skunkvine) has a foul smell when crushed; *Serissa japonica* and *Ixora chinensis* are ornamentals; *Adina* and *Mussaenda* (in which one sepal is enlarged into a white leaf-like blade that attracts pollinators) are common in southern China.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Rubiales included Rubiaceae and Theligonaceae; APG places Rubiaceae in Gentianales and merges *Theligonum* (a wind-pollinated herb with opposite leaves and reduced flowers) into Rubiaceae. *Carlemannia* and *Silvianthus*, formerly in Caprifoliaceae, form the family Carlemanniaceae, placed in Lamiales ([7.12.48](/7.12.48)).

### 47.2.3. Gentianaceae

Gentianaceae (gentian family) comprise about 100 genera and 1700 species (APweb), distributed worldwide and found mainly in temperate regions and tropical mountains; China has about 20 genera and over 400 species, mainly in the high mountains of the southwest, where they are important components of the alpine meadows of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and the Hengduan Mountains.

Diagnostic features:

- Herbs, occasionally shrubs; often containing bitter secoiridoid glycosides (gentiopicroside).
- Leaves simple, opposite, entire, without stipules (leaf bases often fused into a short sheath).
- Flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, 4–5-merous, solitary or in cymes; calyx tubular or divided, 4–5-lobed.
- Corolla sympetalous, funnelform, campanulate or rotate, with lobes often contorted; in *Gentiana* there are plicae between the corolla lobes, in *Swertia* the base of each corolla lobe has 1–2 nectar pits, in *Halenia* the base of the corolla lobes extends into a spur, and in *Gentianopsis* the corolla lobe margins are finely fringed.
- Stamens as many as the corolla lobes and alternate with them, borne on the corolla tube.
- Ovary superior, of 2 fused carpels, unilocular with parietal placentation and many ovules; style single, stigma 2-lobed.
- Capsule dehiscing by 2 valves; seeds small, numerous, with endosperm.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(4\text{–}5)} C_{(4\text{–}5)} A_{4\text{–}5} \underline{G}_{(2:1:\infty)}$.

Representative plants: *Gentiana scabra* is a perennial herb with leaf margins and veins on the lower surface rough, and blue-purple flowers clustered at the shoot tip; the root and rhizome are used in medicine ("longdan"), as are *G. manshurica*, *G. triflora* and *G. rigescens*. *G. macrophylla* has a base covered with fibers of dead leaves and flowers clustered in heads, and its root is used in medicine. *Gentiana* has about 400 species, about 250 of them in China, and many alpine species with large blue flowers are famous alpine garden flowers. *Swertia bimaculata* has a rotate, yellowish green corolla, each lobe bearing small purple spots in the upper part and 2 large yellowish green nectar patches in the middle; *S. mileensis* and others are used in medicine.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Gentianaceae did not include Menyanthaceae (which he placed in Solanales); APG confirmed that Menyanthaceae belong to Asterales among the campanulids ([7.12.49](/7.12.49)), with stems lacking bicollateral bundles, valvate corolla lobes and alternate leaves. On the other hand, the tribe Potalieae (*Fagraea* and relatives), formerly in Loganiaceae, has been moved into Gentianaceae.

### 47.2.4. Loganiaceae and Gelsemiaceae

Cronquist's Loganiaceae was a heterogeneous polyphyletic group. APG broke it up: *Buddleja* moved to Scrophulariaceae of Lamiales ([7.12.48](/7.12.48)), the tribe Potalieae to Gentianaceae, *Gelsemium* and relatives became Gelsemiaceae, and *Plocosperma* became Plocospermataceae of Lamiales; what remains, Loganiaceae in the narrow sense, has about 13 genera and 400 species, with *Strychnos*, *Gardneria*, *Mitreola*, *Geniostoma* and *Mitrasacme* in China.

Loganiaceae are woody or herbaceous with opposite leaves and stipules often reduced to a line connecting the two petioles; flowers 4–5-merous, corolla sympetalous, valvate or imbricate; ovary superior, 2-locular, with axile placentation; intraxylary phloem present. Floral formula: $\ast K_{(4\text{–}5)} C_{(4\text{–}5)} A_{4\text{–}5} \underline{G}_{(2:2:\infty)}$.

*Strychnos* has about 200 species, in the tropics. The strychnine tree (*S. nux-vomica*) is a tree with leaves having 3–5 basal veins, a globose berry, and discoid seeds densely covered with silky hairs; the seeds contain strychnine and brucine and are highly poisonous. Strychnine is an antagonist of the glycine receptor in the spinal cord and causes tetanic convulsions; in small doses it was used as a central stimulant. Some South American species of *Strychnos* (for example *S. toxifera*) are among the sources of curare.

Gelsemiaceae have only 2 genera and about 13 species. *Gelsemium elegans* (heartbreak grass) is an evergreen twining vine with opposite leaves and yellow funnelform corollas, native to southern and southwestern China; the whole plant contains indole alkaloids such as gelsemine and is highly poisonous, and accidental ingestion (often through confusion with honeysuckle) can cause death by respiratory paralysis. The evergreen *G. sempervirens* of North America is a garden ornamental.

### 47.2.5. Apocynaceae

In APG IV, Apocynaceae (dogbane family) include the former Asclepiadaceae and comprise about 366 genera and 5100 species (Endress et al. 2014; the Chinese textbook counts 405 genera and 4000 species, with 88 genera and 415 species in China), mainly in the tropics and subtropics, with a few in temperate regions.

Diagnostic features:

- Trees, shrubs, vines or herbs; the plant body has **laticifers**, usually with white latex (watery sap in *Rauvolfia* and some others).
- Leaves simple, opposite or whorled, rarely alternate, entire; stipules absent or reduced to a line, with glands (colleters) often in the leaf axils or at the petiole base.
- Flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, 5-merous, solitary or in cymes; calyx 5-lobed, often with glands on the inner face of the base.
- Corolla sympetalous, salverform, funnelform, campanulate or rotate, with contorted lobes; scales, hairs or a corona often present in the throat.
- Stamens 5, borne on the corolla tube; anthers often sagittate, either converging and adhering to the stigma head, or fused with the stigma to form a gynostegium.
- Pistil of 2 carpels, ovary superior, the two ovaries often free, the styles united at the tip and expanded into a mucilage-secreting style head, the true receptive surface being on the underside of the style head.
- Fruit a pair of follicles (paired follicles), rarely a berry, drupe or samara; seeds often with a tuft of long hairs (coma), dispersed by wind.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_5 \underline{G}_{2:1:\infty}$ (the two ovaries are free and the styles united at the tip; Chinese textbooks write $G_{2:2}$).

**Definition 47.2.2 (Corona)** A whorl of petal-like, scale-like or filamentous appendages borne between the corolla and the stamens, or on the corolla tube or the backs of the stamens, is called a **corona**.

In Apocynaceae the corona arises from tissue of the corolla or the stamens (oleander has a much-divided linear corona in the throat; the corona of *Asclepias* consists of 5 hooded structures, each with an exserted horn). A corona also occurs in daffodil (Amaryllidaceae) and passionflower (Passifloraceae), and is a structure that has arisen independently many times.

The five subfamilies of Apocynaceae show an evolutionary series of progressive fusion of androecium and gynoecium and progressive aggregation of pollen (Judd et al. 1994; Endress et al. 2014):

| Subfamily | Anthers and style head | Pollen | Pollen transfer | Representatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rauvolfioideae | Anthers free, not adherent to the style head | Single grains, loose | The insect's mouthparts pick up mucilage from the style head and then carry the pollen away | *Rauvolfia*, *Catharanthus*, frangipani, *Allamanda*, yellow oleander |
| Apocynoideae | Sagittate anthers converge into a cone and adhere to the style head | Single grains | The insect's mouthparts pass in and out between the anthers and pick up mucilage and pollen | Oleander, *Apocynum*, *Trachelospermum* |
| Periplocoideae | Anthers adherent to the style head | Tetrads, carried on spoon-shaped translators | The sticky disc at the base of the translator attaches to the insect | *Periploca* |
| Secamonoideae | Gynostegium | 2 pollinia per anther sac, 4 in all | The corpusculum clamps onto the insect | *Secamone* |
| Asclepiadoideae | Gynostegium | 1 pollinium per anther sac; the 2 pollinia of adjacent anther sacs form a pollinarium | The corpusculum of the pollinarium clamps onto the insect's leg | *Asclepias*, *Metaplexis*, *Cynanchum*, *Tylophora*, *Hoya* |

Traditionally the first two subfamilies formed Apocynaceae in the narrow sense and the last three formed Asclepiadaceae; but molecular phylogenies show that Asclepiadaceae are nested within Apocynaceae (Asclepiadoideae and Secamonoideae are sister groups, and Periplocoideae branch off earlier), so that Apocynaceae in the narrow sense are paraphyletic, and APG therefore merges the two families.

**Definition 47.2.3 (Gynostegium and Pollinarium)** In Asclepiadoideae and others, the filaments of the 5 stamens fuse into a tube surrounding the pistil, and the anthers adhere closely to the style head, forming a central columnar structure called the **gynostegium**. The pollen of each anther sac is glued into a waxy pollinium; the two pollinia of two adjacent anthers are each connected by a caudicle (an arm of the translator) to a hard, dark corpusculum lying between the two anthers, and the three parts together are called the **pollinarium**.

Pollination in *Asclepias* proceeds as follows (Figure 47.2.1). On each of the 5 sides of the gynostegium, the margins of two adjacent anthers project outward as wings, and between the wings a narrow slit (guide rail) is formed, with the corpusculum at its upper end. When an insect feeds on the nectar in the corona, its leg slips into the slit and, as it is pulled up, is clamped by the corpusculum and the whole pollinarium is carried away; as the caudicles dry they twist, turning the pollinia into a suitable orientation; when the insect visits the next flower, the pollinia are inserted into another slit and germinate on the stigmatic surface inside it. The pollinarium is a mechanism for transferring pollen "in one batch" and is convergent with the pollinia of Orchidaceae ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.6)).

Representative plants:

1. Madagascar periwinkle (*Catharanthus roseus*): a perennial herb or subshrub with opposite leaves and 1–2 flowers in the leaf axils; corolla salverform, red or white; follicles slender. Native to Madagascar and cultivated throughout the tropics. The whole plant contains over 100 monoterpenoid indole alkaloids, among them vinblastine and vincristine, which are dimeric alkaloids that bind tubulin and prevent spindle formation and are used to treat leukemias and lymphomas, but their content in the plant is extremely low (about one part in a hundred thousand).
2. *Rauvolfia*: shrubs with opposite or whorled leaves, cymose inflorescences, salverform corollas and drupes. *R. verticillata* occurs in southern and southwestern China; the root of Indian snakeroot (*R. serpentina*) contains reserpine, which depletes the norepinephrine stored in nerve endings and was one of the first antihypertensive drugs and the beginning of modern psychopharmacology (the depression caused by reserpine contributed to the monoamine hypothesis).
3. Oleander (*Nerium oleander*): an evergreen shrub with watery sap and leaves in whorls of 3, leathery and narrowly lanceolate; corolla funnelform, with a much-divided linear corona in the throat, anthers sagittate with the connective prolonged into a filament; native from the Mediterranean to Asia and widely cultivated. The whole plant contains cardiac glycosides (cardenolides) such as oleandrin and is highly poisonous.
4. Dogbane (*Apocynum venetum*): a subshrub with latex and opposite, finely toothed leaves; corolla cylindrical-campanulate, with a corona at the base inside the corolla tube; follicles paired and pendulous. It occurs on saline soils and sands from northwestern and northern to northeastern China; the bark fibers are long, fine and pliant and are an excellent textile raw material, and the leaves are used to make "luobuma tea".
5. Star jasmine (*Trachelospermum jasminoides*): an evergreen woody vine with aerial roots, and white, fragrant flowers whose corolla lobes twist to the right like a pinwheel; common south of the Yangtze and grown for vertical greening.
6. Chinese silk vine (*Periploca sepium*): a deciduous sprawling shrub whose corolla lobes are thickened in the middle and reflexed; the pollen tetrads are carried on spoon-shaped translators. It occurs from northern to northwestern China; the root bark is the Chinese drug "xiangjiapi" (northern wujiapi), which contains periplocin (a cardiac glycoside) and is poisonous and must not be confused with the wujiapi (southern wujiapi) of Araliaceae.
7. *Cynanchum*: herbs or vines; corona cup-shaped or lobed; one pollinium per locule, with 2 adjacent pollinia attached to a single corpusculum. *C. paniculatum*, *C. stauntonii*, *C. auriculatum* and others are used in medicine. *Metaplexis japonica* is a perennial twining herb whose seeds bear a tuft of white silky hairs. *Tylophora ovata* has a corona of 5 ovate fleshy lobes, and its root is used in medicine.
8. Milkweed (*Asclepias curassavica*): a perennial herb with reflexed red corolla lobes and a yellow corona; native to tropical America and cultivated or escaped throughout the world. The latex of *Asclepias* contains cardiac glycosides; the larvae of the North American monarch butterfly (*Danaus plexippus*) feed only on *Asclepias*, storing the glycosides in their bodies so that the adults are toxic to birds, and advertise this with conspicuous warning colors. This is a classic example of **coevolution** between plant chemical defense and herbivorous insects.
9. Others: frangipani (*Plumeria rubra*), *Allamanda schottii* and yellow oleander (*Thevetia peruviana*) are tropical ornamentals, the last with highly poisonous cardiac glycosides in its seeds; *Hoya* and *Stapelia* (whose flowers smell of rotting meat and are fly-pollinated) are common succulent cultivated plants; the latex of tropical woody vines such as *Chonemorpha* and *Ecdysanthera* yields rubber.

Key features of the family: latex present. Leaves simple, opposite or whorled, entire. Flowers 5-merous, corolla lobes contorted, a corona often present; anthers often sagittate and adherent to the style head, or fused with the stigma into a gynostegium, and pollen often united into tetrads or pollinia. Two carpels free, styles united at the tip. Fruit a pair of follicles, seeds often with a coma.

## 47.3. Boraginales

Boraginales have a single family: Boraginaceae in the broad sense of APG IV, with about 150 genera and 2700 species, distributed worldwide and richest in the dry regions from the Mediterranean to Central Asia. In Cronquist's system Boraginaceae belonged to Lamiales because, like Lamiaceae, they have a four-lobed ovary and a gynobasic style; molecular evidence shows that this similarity is convergent, and Boraginaceae form their own order, the sister group of Lamiales.

Diagnostic features:

- Mostly herbs, with shrubs and trees in the tropics (*Ehretia*, *Cordia*); the plant is often densely covered with stiff hairs (bristles) whose bases often contain calcareous cystoliths, so that the leaf surface is rough.
- Leaves simple, alternate, entire, without stipules.
- Inflorescence a scorpioid cyme (a monochasium whose axis is coiled before flowering and gradually unrolls and straightens from the base upward during flowering), or arranged in panicles; flowers bisexual, actinomorphic (zygomorphic in *Echium* and others).
- Calyx 5-lobed, persistent; corolla sympetalous, rotate, funnelform or salverform, 5-lobed and imbricate, with 5 scale-like or cushion-like appendages in the throat (throat appendages); the corolla color often changes from pink to blue during flowering (anthocyanins change color as the vacuolar pH rises).
- Stamens 5, borne on the corolla tube, included.
- Ovary superior, of 2 fused carpels, each with 2 ovules, divided by a false septum into 4 locules with 1 ovule each; in Boraginoideae the ovary is deeply 4-lobed and the style arises from the base between the 4 lobes (gynobasic style); in *Ehretia* and others the ovary is entire and the style terminal.
- Fruit of 4 nutlets (Boraginoideae), or a drupe (*Ehretia*, *Cordia*).

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_5 \underline{G}_{(2:4:1)}$.

B: flower of *Trigonotis peduncularis*: corolla rotate, 5-lobed, with 5 appendages in the throat. C: pistil with a deeply 4-lobed ovary and a gynobasic style (schematic longitudinal section). Representative plants:

1. *Bothriospermum*: herbs with scales in the corolla throat; nutlets kidney-shaped, with a transverse pit on the ventral face and a reticulate or tuberculate dorsal face. *B. chinense* has crisped leaf margins and occurs in northern China and elsewhere; the whole plant is used in medicine.
2. *Trigonotis peduncularis*: a small annual herb with terminal scorpioid cymes and pale blue corollas with 5 yellow appendages in the throat; the 4 nutlets are obliquely trigonal-pyramidal. It is widespread in China and one of the commonest early spring weeds of fields and roadsides.
3. *Ehretia*: shrubs or trees; corolla without appendages in the throat, ovary entire, style terminal and 2-lobed; fruit a drupe. *E. acuminata* (called *E. thyrsiflora* in Chinese textbooks) is a deciduous tree of eastern, central and southwestern China, with wood used in construction and decoration.
4. *Lithospermum*: herbs with white, yellow or blue-purple flowers; nutlets ovate, smooth, attached at the base. *L. erythrorhizon* has a thick taproot that is purple when dry and is widespread in China; the root contains shikonin (a naphthoquinone pigment) and acetylshikonin and is used in medicine and as a purple dye. *Arnebia euchroma* of Xinjiang is also used as a source of the drug. Cell culture of *Lithospermum* was an early success in the industrial production of plant secondary metabolites (shikonin was produced by cell culture in Japan in 1983).
5. *Myosotis*: herbs with scorpioid cymes lacking bracts, a short corolla tube and scales in the throat; nutlets ovate, smooth. *M. alpestris* (called *M. sylvatica* in Chinese textbooks), forget-me-not, has pale blue corollas with a yellow throat; native to Europe and grown as an ornamental.
6. Others: *Lappula myosotis* has nutlets with anchor-shaped prickles on the margin, dispersed by animals; borage (*Borago officinalis*) is a culinary herb; comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*) was introduced in large amounts as fodder, but it contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids, and long-term consumption causes hepatic veno-occlusive disease, so it is no longer recommended as fodder or "health tea"; *Heliotropium*, *Cordia* and the North American *Phacelia* (formerly Hydrophyllaceae) also belong to this order. Many genera of Boraginaceae contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are one of the chemical characters of the family.

Key features of the family: herbs, often with bristly hairs. Leaves alternate, without stipules. Scorpioid cymes; flowers regular, corolla sympetalous, often with appendages in the throat; stamens 5, included. Ovary superior, 2 carpels with 4 locules, often deeply 4-lobed, with a gynobasic style. Four nutlets.

Changes in circumscription: Boraginaceae in APG IV are broadly defined and include the former Hydrophyllaceae (except *Hydrolea*, which has moved to Solanales), Heliotropiaceae, Cordiaceae, Ehretiaceae and the parasitic Lennoaceae. The Boraginales Working Group (2016) advocated dividing the order into 11 families: Boraginaceae in the narrow sense, Heliotropiaceae, Cordiaceae, Ehretiaceae, Hydrophyllaceae, Lennoaceae, Namaceae, Codonaceae, Coldeniaceae, Hoplestigmataceae and Wellstediaceae; Chinese textbooks mostly use Boraginaceae in the narrow sense (excluding Heliotropiaceae, Cordiaceae and Ehretiaceae), and under that treatment *Ehretia* belongs to Ehretiaceae. The two treatments recognize the same phylogenetic relationships and differ only in the extent of families; this course follows APG IV in using the broad Boraginaceae and notes the narrow treatment.

## 47.4. Solanales

### 47.4.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Solanales in APG IV comprise 5 families and about 165 genera and 4100 species: Solanaceae, Convolvulaceae, Montiniaceae, Sphenocleaceae and Hydroleaceae, in two branches: Solanaceae + Convolvulaceae, and Montiniaceae + (Sphenocleaceae + Hydroleaceae). The common characters of Solanales are alternate leaves and (mostly) actinomorphic flowers, with a sympetalous corolla that is often plicate (folded) or contorted, and stamens as many as the corolla lobes; Solanaceae and Convolvulaceae both have intraxylary phloem (bicollateral bundles), and both have tropane alkaloids in place of the iridoids common in the other lamiids.

Tropane alkaloids are a class of alkaloids with an 8-azabicyclo[3.2.1]octane skeleton, synthesized from ornithine via putrescine and found mainly in Solanaceae (hyoscyamine, atropine, scopolamine), Convolvulaceae (calystegines) and Erythroxylaceae (cocaine). Hyoscyamine and scopolamine of Solanaceae are antagonists of the muscarinic acetylcholine receptor and are used as antispasmodics, mydriatics, preanesthetic medication and against motion sickness.

Sphenocleaceae have only *Sphenoclea zeylanica*, a weed of tropical paddy fields occurring in southern China, formerly placed in Campanulaceae; of Hydroleaceae, *Hydrolea zeylanica* also occurs in southern China and was formerly placed in Hydrophyllaceae; Montiniaceae (3 genera, in Africa and Madagascar) have unisexual flowers and were formerly placed in the broadly defined Saxifragaceae.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Solanales also included Polemoniaceae, Menyanthaceae and Hydrophyllaceae, which APG moves to Ericales ([7.12.46](/7.12.46)), Asterales ([7.12.49](/7.12.49)) and Boraginales (§47.3) respectively; Cuscutaceae are merged into Convolvulaceae.

### 47.4.2. Solanaceae

Solanaceae (nightshade family) comprise about 100 genera and 2500 species (the Chinese textbook counts 95 genera and 2300 species, with 20 genera and 101 species in China), distributed from the temperate zone to the tropics and most diverse in South America. They include the most important group of crops in the world: potato, tomato, eggplant, pepper and tobacco, as well as many medicinal and poisonous plants.

Diagnostic features:

- Herbs and shrubs, rarely trees; with bicollateral bundles (intraxylary phloem); often with stellate hairs, glandular hairs or prickles.
- Leaves simple, alternate, entire, lobed or pinnately compound, without stipules; on flowering shoots often two unequal leaves occur together (the lateral branch arising from the axillary bud of one leaf is fused with the main stem, so that its leaf is carried up to the next node).
- Flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, rarely zygomorphic (*Petunia*, *Schizanthus*), solitary or in cymes; because the inflorescence axis is fused with the stem, the inflorescence is often extra-axillary or opposite a leaf.
- Calyx 5-lobed, persistent and often enlarged after flowering (in *Physalis* the calyx inflates into a lantern enclosing the berry).
- Corolla sympetalous, rotate, campanulate, funnelform or salverform, 5-lobed, with lobes valvate or plicate (longitudinally folded in the bud).
- Stamens 5, alternating with the corolla lobes and borne on the corolla tube; anthers 2-locular, dehiscing longitudinally or by apical pores (*Solanum*: the anthers converge into a cone and open by apical pores, pollinated by the buzzing of bees, [7.12.29](/7.12.29)).
- Ovary superior, of 2 fused carpels and 2-locular, with an oblique septum so that the ovary is displaced relative to the median plane of the flower; axile placentation, the placentae often swollen, with many ovules; a disc often present below the ovary. Cultivars of tomato and others have more carpels and are multilocular.
- Berry or capsule (*Datura*, *Nicotiana*, *Petunia*); seeds numerous, embryo curved or annular, endosperm abundant and fleshy.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_5 \underline{G}_{(2:2:\infty)}$.

The chief chemical constituents of Solanaceae are of three kinds: tropane alkaloids (belladonna, *Datura*, henbane, *Anisodus*), the pyridine alkaloid nicotine (*Nicotiana*), and steroidal alkaloid glycosides (solanine and tomatine of *Solanum*). When potato tubers turn green in light or sprout, the solanine content of the skin and around the eyes increases greatly, and eating them causes poisoning.

Like Plantaginaceae and Rosaceae, Solanaceae have gametophytic **self-incompatibility** with S-RNase as the pistil determinant ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3)): when a pollen tube grows in the style, if the S allele of the pollen is the same as either of the two in the pistil, the S-RNase secreted by the pistil degrades the RNA of the pollen tube and stops its growth. The pollen tubes of *Petunia* and *Nicotiana* are the main material for the study of this mechanism; cultivated tomato, eggplant and pepper are self-compatible, and self-compatibility has evolved repeatedly and independently in Solanaceae from self-incompatible ancestors.

Solanaceae are divided into about 7 subfamilies; the largest, Solanoideae, mostly have berries and curved embryos and a chromosome base number $x = 12$, and include *Solanum*, *Capsicum*, *Lycium*, *Physalis*, *Datura*, *Atropa*, *Hyoscyamus* and others; *Nicotiana* belongs to Nicotianoideae and *Petunia* to Petunioideae.

A: floral diagram of *Solanum*: sepals 5, corolla 5-lobed (plicate), stamens 5 alternating with the corolla lobes and converging, 2 carpels fused, 2-locular, axile placentation, ovules numerous, septum oblique to the median plane of the flower (oblique ovary). Representative plants:

1. *Solanum*: herbs, shrubs or small trees; leaves simple, occasionally compound; corolla often rotate; anthers converging at the sides and dehiscing by apical pores; berry. About 1500 species, one of the largest genera of angiosperms, with about 40 species in China. Molecular phylogenies show that tomato (the former genus *Lycopersicon*) is nested within *Solanum* and is the sister group of the clade containing potato, so tomato is now called *S. lycopersicum* (Spooner et al. 1993).
   - Potato (*S. tuberosum*): an herb with odd-pinnate leaves whose leaflets alternate large and small; corymbose cymes; the tip of the underground stem swells into a **tuber**, and the "eyes" on the tuber are axillary buds at the nodes. Native to the Andes of South America and domesticated about 8000 years ago, it became an important staple crop after its introduction to Europe in the 16th century; the late blight caused by the oomycete *Phytophthora infestans* produced the Irish famine of 1845–1849. Cultivated potatoes are mostly autotetraploid and are generally propagated vegetatively by tubers.
   - Tomato (*S. lycopersicum*): the whole plant covered with glandular hairs and with a characteristic odor; leaves pinnate; inflorescences extra-axillary; anthers narrowed at the tip into a long beak, converging into a cone, dehiscing longitudinally; berry. Native to western South America and domesticated in Mexico, it is now one of the most important vegetables in the world and a model plant for the study of fruit development and ripening (ethylene).
   - Eggplant (*S. melongena*): the whole plant covered with stellate hairs and often with prickles; flowers solitary, purple; berry large, with great variation in shape and color. It was domesticated in Asia (from India to southern China) and belongs to a different clade of *Solanum* from tomato and potato, which are native to the Americas.
   - Black nightshade (*S. nigrum*): an annual herb with short, extra-axillary scorpioid cymes, white flowers and black berries; widespread throughout the world, and the unripe berries contain solanine. *S. lyratum* is an herbaceous vine, and the whole plant is used in medicine.
2. *Capsicum*: in pepper (*C. annuum*) the flowers are solitary, the calyx cup-shaped, the fruit stalk stout, and the berry hollow with a fleshy pericarp. Native to tropical America and introduced to China in the Ming dynasty, it is now an important vegetable and condiment; the pungency comes from capsaicin, secreted mainly by epidermal cells of the placenta (the "ribs" of the pepper), and the seeds themselves are not pungent. Bell pepper, chili pepper and others are cultivar groups of *C. annuum*.
3. *Nicotiana*: tobacco (*N. tabacum*) is a tall herb covered with glandular hairs, with terminal panicled cymes, funnelform corollas and capsules. Native to South America, it is an allotetraploid that arose by hybridization between *N. sylvestris* and *N. tomentosiformis*. The leaves contain nicotine, which is synthesized in the roots and transported to the leaves for storage; it is highly poisonous and was once used as an insecticide. Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) was the first virus to be discovered ([7.11](/7.11)), and *N. benthamiana* is a common material for transient expression in plant molecular biology.
4. *Datura*: stout, poisonous plants; flowers solitary and large, calyx long-tubular, corolla funnelform with a plicate limb; capsule often prickly, dehiscing by 4 valves. In *D. stramonium* the calyx tube has 5 ridges and the capsule is erect, dehiscing regularly by 4 valves; in *D. metel* the calyx tube has no ridges and the capsule is ascending or horizontal, dehiscing irregularly by 4 valves. The leaves and flowers contain hyoscyamine and scopolamine, and the flower ("yangjinhua") is a Chinese anesthetic drug, said to have been an ingredient of Hua Tuo's "mafeisan". The woody *Datura* (called *Datura arborea* in Chinese textbooks) now belongs to *Brugmansia*, with pendulous flowers and a berry-like, indehiscent fruit.
5. *Lycium*: spiny shrubs with leaves often clustered on short shoots; flowers solitary or in clusters of a few in the leaf axils; berry red. In *L. barbarum* (goji, wolfberry) the corolla tube is clearly longer than the lobes and the fruit is sweet, and it occurs in northwestern and northern China; in *L. chinense* the corolla tube is shorter than or equal to the lobes and the fruit is sweet with a slightly bitter aftertaste. The fruit ("gouqizi") and the root bark ("digupi") are used in medicine.
6. Other medicinal and ornamental plants: belladonna (*Atropa belladonna*) contains atropine and is native to Europe, the name "belladonna" (beautiful lady) coming from the use of its juice by Renaissance women to dilate the pupils; henbane (*Hyoscyamus niger*) and *Anisodus tanguticus* contain hyoscyamine-type alkaloids, and anisodamine (654-2), discovered in China from *Anisodus tanguticus*, is used to treat septic shock; the persistent calyx of Chinese lantern (*Physalis alkekengi* var. *franchetii*) swells into an orange-red lantern; petunia (*Petunia* × *atkinsiana*, that is, *P. hybrida*) and night jessamine (*Cestrum nocturnum*) are ornamentals.

Key features of the family: often herbs with bicollateral bundles. Leaves simple, alternate, with inflorescences often extra-axillary. Flowers bisexual, regular, 5-merous, calyx persistent, corolla rotate or plicate; anthers often opening by pores. Ovary superior, 2 carpels and 2 locules, oblique, ovules numerous. Berry or capsule.

Distinction from Scrophulariaceae: the flowers of Solanaceae are often regular, with as many stamens as corolla lobes, and have intraxylary phloem; the few Solanaceae with zygomorphic corollas can still be distinguished from Scrophulariaceae by the oblique ovary.

### 47.4.3. Convolvulaceae

Convolvulaceae (morning glory family) comprise about 60 genera and 1650–1900 species (the Chinese textbook counts 58 genera and 1650 species, with 20 genera and 129 species in China), distributed worldwide and found mainly in the tropics and subtropics of the Americas and Asia.

Diagnostic features:

- Mostly twining or prostrate herbs, rarely shrubs or trees; the plant often contains latex (resin canals); the stem has bicollateral bundles; storage roots often present.
- Leaves simple, alternate, entire or lobed, occasionally compound; without stipules.
- Flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, 5-merous, solitary in the leaf axils or in cymes, with bracts.
- Sepals 5, free or fused only at the base, imbricate, persistent.
- Corolla sympetalous, funnelform, campanulate or salverform, with the limb nearly entire or shallowly 5-lobed; in bud it is contorted-plicate (the 5 "midpetaline bands" are exposed in bud, and the thin parts between them fold inward and twist).
- Stamens 5, alternating with the corolla lobes, borne at the base or in the lower middle of the corolla tube, often unequal in length and included.
- Ovary superior, with an annular disc below; 2 (rarely 3–5) carpels fused, 2-locular with axile placentation and 2 erect basal ovules per locule, sometimes divided by a false septum into 4 locules with 1 ovule each.
- Capsule, rarely a berry; seeds 4 (or fewer), endosperm scanty, cotyledons folded.

Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_{(5)} A_5 \underline{G}_{(2:2:2)}$.

Representative plants:

1. *Ipomoea* (sweet potato genus): herbs or shrubs with often twining stems; corolla funnelform or campanulate; stamens and style included; pollen spherical and spiny. About 600 species, the largest genus of the family; Flora of China includes *Pharbitis*, *Quamoclit* and others in this genus.
   - Sweet potato (*I. batatas*): an annual herb with storage roots and prostrate stems; native to tropical America and introduced to China in the Ming dynasty, it is a high-yielding, drought-tolerant staple crop. Cultivated sweet potato is hexaploid, and its genome naturally contains T-DNA fragments of *Agrobacterium*, making it a "naturally transgenic crop" (Kyndt et al. 2015).
   - Water spinach (*I. aquatica*): an aquatic or wet-ground herb with hollow stems, whose young stems and leaves are eaten as a vegetable; native to China.
   - Japanese morning glory (*I. nil*, called *Pharbitis nil* in Chinese textbooks): an annual twining herb with 3-lobed leaves; corolla funnelform and blue-purple, opening in the early morning and withering before noon; the seeds ("qianniuzi") contain pharbitin, are purgative and poisonous. *I. purpurea* has heart-shaped leaves, is native to the Americas and has become naturalized. *I. nil* is a traditional material of Japanese plant breeding and genetics, and the mutations of flower color and form (many caused by transposon insertions) are well studied. Cypress vine (*I. quamoclit*) has deeply pinnately divided leaves and red, salverform flowers, a hummingbird-pollinated type.
2. *Calystegia*: twining or prostrate herbs with funnelform corollas and 2 large bracts below the flower enclosing the calyx. *C. hederacea* is a common field weed with pale pink flowers. *Convolvulus* lacks the large bracts and has linear stigmas; *C. arvensis* (field bindweed) has well-developed rhizomes and is a farmland weed that is hard to eradicate.
3. *Cuscuta* (dodder): parasitic twining herbs with yellow or red stems and leaves absent or reduced to small scales, inserting **haustoria** into the vascular tissue of the host to take up water and nutrients ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.1)). The flowers are small, in clustered heads or spikes; the inner face of the corolla tube has 5 fringed scales at the base below the stamens; capsule circumscissile. About 200 species. *C. chinensis* has hair-thin stems and often parasitizes herbs such as legumes and composites, and is a harmful weed of crops such as soybean; the seeds ("tusizi") are used in medicine. *C. japonica* has thicker stems and often parasitizes woody plants. Dodder is a holo-**parasitic plant** that has almost lost photosynthesis (some species retain a little chlorophyll); its plastid genome has lost many genes, and through the haustoria it can also exchange mRNA and genes with the host (horizontal gene transfer).
4. Others: *Dichondra micrantha* is a small creeping herb with round-reniform leaves, used as a lawn ground cover; moonflower (*Ipomoea alba*) opens at night and is moth-pollinated.

Key features of the family: often twining herbs, often with latex and bicollateral bundles. Corolla funnelform, contorted-plicate. Ovary superior, 2-locular, 2 erect ovules per locule. Capsule, seeds 4, cotyledons folded.

Distinction from Solanaceae: in Convolvulaceae the ovules are few and definite in number (2 per locule) and basal and erect, and seeds are few; in Solanaceae the ovules are numerous, borne on a swollen axile placenta.

Changes in circumscription: Chinese textbooks and Cronquist's system treat *Cuscuta* as a separate family Cuscutaceae; molecular phylogenies show that *Cuscuta* is nested within Convolvulaceae (close to the tribe Ipomoeeae and relatives, its exact position still disputed), and APG treats it as a genus of Convolvulaceae. *Cuscuta* and *Cassytha* of Lauraceae ([7.12.35](/7.12.35#sec-35.3.2)) look extremely similar, an example of **convergent evolution** of different groups under the parasitic habit.

## Questions

1. How can a plant of Rubiaceae be recognized when it is not flowering? What is the "whorled leaves" of madder?

   **Answer** The leaves of Rubiaceae are opposite (or whorled) and entire, with interpetiolar stipules between the two petioles and colleters on the inner face of the stipules; "opposite entire leaves plus interpetiolar stipules" is an almost unique combination of Rubiaceae (Loganiaceae have only a stipular line, and Gentianaceae have no stipules). Of the 4–8 "leaves" in a whorl of madder or cleavers, only 2 have axillary buds, which shows that only these are true leaves and the others are leaf-like interpetiolar stipules.

2. Why was Asclepiadaceae merged into Apocynaceae? What changes occur in the flower from Rauvolfioideae to Asclepiadoideae?

   **Answer** Molecular phylogenies show that the three groups of the traditional Asclepiadaceae (Periplocoideae, Secamonoideae and Asclepiadoideae) are nested within Apocynaceae, so that Apocynaceae in the narrow sense are paraphyletic; the two families also share latex, opposite leaves, contorted corollas, free ovaries with styles united at the tip, a mucilage-secreting style head, paired follicles and a coma, and so they are merged. From Rauvolfioideae to Asclepiadoideae the anthers change from free to adherent to the style head and then to fused with the stigma into a gynostegium; the pollen changes from single grains to tetrads (carried on translators) and then to a pollinarium made of pollinia; and pollen transfer changes from being picked up with mucilage to being clamped on in one batch by a corpusculum, making pollination more specific and more efficient.

3. What features of Solanaceae are easy to observe? Why is tomato said to be "a species of *Solanum*"?

   **Answer** Solanaceae: herbs or shrubs with simple alternate leaves, inflorescences often extra-axillary, and often two leaves together on flowering shoots; flowers 5-merous, with a persistent calyx that often enlarges after flowering, a rotate or plicate corolla, 5 stamens that often converge and, in *Solanum*, open by apical pores; ovary 2-locular with an oblique septum, swollen placentae and numerous ovules; berry or capsule. Molecular phylogenies show that the former genus *Lycopersicon* is nested within *Solanum* and is the sister group of the clade containing potato; if *Lycopersicon* were kept, *Solanum* would be paraphyletic, so tomato is merged into *Solanum* and its name changed to *Solanum lycopersicum*. The anthers of tomato are extended at the tip into a beak and dehisce longitudinally, which was the main reason for placing it in a separate genus, but this is only an apomorphy within a genus.

4. Lamiaceae and Boraginaceae both have "a four-lobed ovary, a gynobasic style and 4 nutlets"; why are they no longer placed in the same order?

   **Answer** The four nutlets of the two families are convergent: molecular evidence places Boraginaceae in the separate order Boraginales and Lamiaceae in Lamiales; although the orders are sister groups, the basal groups of Lamiales (Oleaceae, Plantaginaceae and others) all have capsules or drupes, and four nutlets evolved only within Lamiales, in Lamiaceae (and part of Verbenaceae). The two families also differ in other respects: Boraginaceae have alternate leaves, regular flowers, scorpioid cymes and bristly hairs; Lamiaceae have opposite leaves, square stems, zygomorphic flowers, verticillasters and aromatic oils.

5. Why are belladonna, *Datura* and tobacco, all in Solanales, poisonous? What functions might these toxic constituents have in the plant?

   **Answer** Belladonna and *Datura* contain tropane alkaloids (hyoscyamine, scopolamine, atropine), which are antagonists of the muscarinic acetylcholine receptor; tobacco contains nicotine, an agonist of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor. Both kinds of alkaloids act on acetylcholine receptors in the nervous system of animals and are toxic to herbivorous insects and mammals; they are chemical defenses of the plant: nicotine is synthesized in the roots, is increased by mechanical damage and induced by jasmonate, and is then transported to the leaves for storage. The shared tropane alkaloids of Solanaceae and Convolvulaceae are part of the chemical evidence that they belong to the same order, Solanales.
