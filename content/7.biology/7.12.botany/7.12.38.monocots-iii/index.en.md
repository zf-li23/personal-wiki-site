The latest-diverging branch of the monocots is called the commelinids; it comprises Arecales, Commelinales, Zingiberales and Poales, about 30,000 species. They differ enormously in outward appearance: there are tall palms and the giant herbs of banana, but also delicate dayflowers, the aquatic water hyacinth and the most numerous of all, the grasses. What links them is not the structure of the flower but the chemistry of the cell wall. This chapter treats the characters of the commelinids (§38.1) and three of their orders: Arecales (§38.2), Commelinales (§38.3) and Zingiberales (§38.4); Poales is left to [7.12.39](/7.12.39). In Cronquist's system these plants belonged to three subclasses: Arecaceae to Arecidae (together with Araceae and Pandanaceae), Commelinaceae to Commelinidae, Pontederiaceae to Liliidae, and Zingiberales and Bromeliales formed the Zingiberidae ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.5.2)). For the overview of the monocots and the phylogenetic tree see [7.12.36](/7.12.36).

## 38.1. The Commelinids

**Definition 38.1.1 (Commelinids)** The monophyletic group of monocots formed in the APG system by Arecales, Poales, Commelinales and Zingiberales is called the **commelinids**. It is the **sister group** of Asparagales.

The **synapomorphies** of the commelinids are mainly chemical and ultrastructural characters:

- The cell walls contain ferulic acid and *p*-coumaric acid ester-linked to hemicellulose (arabinoxylan). Ferulic acid emits blue-green fluorescence under ultraviolet light, so that sections of commelinid tissue glow throughout under a fluorescence microscope, whereas the unlignified cell walls of other monocots do not fluoresce (Harris and Hartley 1980). This is a simple way to tell whether a monocot belongs to the commelinids.
- The leaf epidermis or bundle sheaths often contain silica bodies of silicon dioxide (conspicuous in Poaceae, Cyperaceae, Arecaceae and Zingiberales).
- The epicuticular wax of the leaf is often of the *Strelitzia* type (clusters of rod-like wax crystals).
- The endosperm is rich in starch (in some groups the starch grains are compound).

Within the commelinids, Commelinales and Zingiberales are sister groups, with good support; the positions of Arecales (including Dasypogonaceae) and Poales are less well supported, and most analyses show Arecales as the sister group of the other three orders (Givnish et al. 2018).

The commelinids went unrecognized for a long time because their members converge in the outward form of flowers and inflorescences with other monocots: the spadix and spathe of Arecaceae resemble those of Araceae and Pandanaceae, and the petaloid perianths of Pontederiaceae and Haemodoraceae resemble those of Liliaceae, so that Cronquist placed them in three subclasses. Molecular phylogenetics and the evidence of cell-wall chemistry confirm one another and unite them in a single clade.

## 38.2. Arecales

### 38.2.1. Circumscription of the Order

APG IV Arecales comprise Arecaceae and Dasypogonaceae. Dasypogonaceae have 4 genera and 16 species, only in southwestern Australia; they are shrubby or tree-like herbs resembling the Australian grass trees ([7.12.37](/7.12.37#sec-37.4)) and were once placed near Asparagales; they show the ferulic acid fluorescence of the commelinids, and molecular evidence shows that they are the sister group of Arecaceae.

### 38.2.2. Diagnostic Characters of Arecaceae

Diagnostic characters of Arecaceae (Palmae, the palm family):

- Trees, shrubs or climbers; the stem is usually unbranched and a single erect trunk (clustered forms also occur), with one large apical meristem at the top; persistent leaf bases or ring-like leaf scars are often present on the stem.
- Leaves large, evergreen, alternate, mostly crowded at the top of the stem to form the "palm-type" crown (scattered in climbing species); leaves palmately divided (fan leaves) or pinnately divided to the base (feather leaves), rarely bipinnate (*Caryota*); the petiole base is often expanded into a fibrous sheath.
- Flowers small, often yellow-green, bisexual or unisexual, monoecious or dioecious, basically trimerous; borne in branched or unbranched spadices (large, panicle-like inflorescences) subtended by one to several large spathe-like bracts, arising among the leaves or below the bundle of leaf sheaths.
- Tepals 6 in two whorls, free or fused, the outer often smaller (sepal-like); stamens 6 in two whorls, rarely 3 or numerous, the filaments free or fused at the base into a ring; pollen often monosulcate.
- Carpels 3, free or fused to varying degrees, ovary superior, 1–3-locular, 1 ovule per locule; style short, stigmas 3.
- Fruit a drupe or berry, the exocarp fleshy, fibrous or covered with imbricate scales (Calamoideae); seed 1, with abundant endosperm, homogeneous or ruminate, often hard (storing hemicellulosic mannans).

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3}$; ♀ $\ast P_{3+3} \underline{G}_{3,\,(3)}$ (carpels free or fused); bisexual flower $\ast K_3 C_3 A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:1)}$.

### 38.2.3. Growth of Palms: Apical Meristem and Primary Thickening

The structure of a palm trunk is completely different from that of a dicot tree. It has no **vascular cambium** and cannot undergo **secondary growth**, and the thickness of the trunk is essentially fixed in the juvenile stage:

1. Establishment growth: during the first several to a dozen years after germination the stem hardly elongates but thickens continuously near the ground. Below the leaf primordia of the shoot apex lies a broad primary thickening meristem ([7.12.21](/7.12.21#sec-21.5.3)), whose periclinal divisions make the shoot apex gradually wider and the stem base obconical, until the thickness characteristic of the adult of the species is reached.
2. Elongation growth: thereafter the width of the shoot apex no longer increases, and the stem begins to elongate upward to form a cylindrical trunk of uniform thickness. Further thickening of an old trunk comes only from continued enlargement of ground tissue cells and lignification of cell walls ("diffuse primary growth"), so palms have no annual rings, and the rings on the trunk are only leaf scars.
3. A single growing point: a single-stemmed palm has only one apical meristem, protected in the center of the crown by successive leaf sheaths and forming the tender "palm heart". If the terminal bud is destroyed or eaten, the whole tree dies; harvesting palm hearts (as from cabbage palm and peach palm) is therefore destructive.

The development of the palm leaf is also special: the blade of the leaf primordium is first a single piece and later separates along fold lines (by local tissue death or separation) to form the palmate or pinnate segments. The orientation of the folds has two types: the cross section of the segments is V-shaped (induplicate) or inverted-V-shaped (reduplicate), one of the diagnostic characters of the subfamilies.

### 38.2.4. Classification and Representative Plants

Arecaceae have about 180 genera and 2600 species, distributed in the tropics and subtropics with centers in tropical America and tropical Asia, a few reaching the temperate zone (in China the windmill palm reaches south of the Qinling); China has about 18 genera and 77 species (about 22 genera including cultivated ones), mainly in the south to southeast. The fossil record of Arecaceae begins in the Late Cretaceous (leaf and stem fossils about 90 million years old) and is a calibration point commonly used in molecular-clock studies of monocots. Molecular phylogenetics divides the family into 5 subfamilies (Baker et al. 2009): Calamoideae (fruits with imbricate scales), Nypoideae, Coryphoideae (mostly palmate leaves), Ceroxyloideae and Arecoideae (mostly pinnate leaves, flowers often in triads of two male flowers flanking one female flower). Representative plants:

1. *Trachycarpus*: evergreen trees with palmate leaves divided deeply, with many segments each shallowly 2-lobed at the tip; flowers often unisexual, dioecious, the much-branched spadix with a conspicuous spathe; fruit reniform or globose. The windmill palm (*T. fortunei*) occurs south of the Yangtze, is one of the most cold-hardy palms and is cultivated everywhere; the fibers of the leaf sheath can be made into rope, raincoats, mattresses and brushes, the young leaves into fans and hats, and the fruits and leaf-sheath fibers are used in medicine.
2. *Livistona*: trees with long, spiny-margined petioles, the blade palmate, divided to the middle, the segments 2-lobed at the tip and drooping; flowers bisexual, stamens 6 with filaments fused into a ring; drupe. The Chinese fan palm (*L. chinensis*) occurs in southern China, and the young leaves are made into palm-leaf fans, conical hats and raincoats; the seeds are used in medicine.
3. *Cocos*: only the coconut (*C. nucifera*). Evergreen trees with leaves pinnately divided to the base; flowers unisexual and monoecious, the branched spadix with male flowers above and female flowers at the base; the ovary of the female flower is 3-locular with 1 ovule per locule, but only 1 develops. The fruit is a large drupe: the exocarp is leathery, the mesocarp thick and fibrous (coir), the endocarp bony and hard (the shell), with 3 germination pores ("eyes") near the base, only one of which lies over the embryo and through which the embryo emerges at germination; seed 1, the endosperm liquid in early development (coconut water, the free-nuclear stage of nuclear endosperm) and later deposited on the inner wall as white solid endosperm (coconut meat), leaving a large central cavity ([7.12.30](/7.12.30#sec-30.2.2)). The thick, light fibrous mesocarp lets the fruit float at sea, and the coconut is therefore widespread on tropical coasts ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.3.3)). The coconut has very many uses: the wood is used for building, the leaves for mats and thatch, the sap flowing from the cut inflorescence for sugar and wine, the water as a drink, the meat eaten raw, pressed for oil (coconut oil) or made into desiccated coconut, and the coir for rope and mats.
4. *Elaeis*: the oil palm (*E. guineensis*), native to West Africa, has pinnate leaves; the mesocarp of the fruit is rich in oil (palm oil), and the kernel contains palm kernel oil. The oil palm has the highest oil yield per unit area of any oil crop, and palm oil is the world's largest vegetable oil by production; large-scale planting of oil palm in Southeast Asia has caused extensive deforestation of tropical rain forest, and sustainable production is now an important issue. It has been introduced in Hainan and southern Yunnan in China.
5. *Areca*: the areca palm (*A. catechu*) is single-stemmed with pinnate leaves, native to Southeast Asia and cultivated in Hainan, southern Yunnan and Taiwan; the seeds contain alkaloids such as arecoline and are used in Chinese medicine as an anthelmintic. Chewing betel nut (often together with betel leaf and lime, [7.12.35](/7.12.35#sec-35.4.2)) is a definite cause of oral cancer, and the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies areca nut as a Group 1 carcinogen.
6. *Phoenix*: the date palm (*P. dactylifera*), native to western Asia and North Africa, has leaves pinnately divided to the base with the basal segments turned into spines; dioecious. It is one of the oldest cultivated fruit trees, and reliefs from Mesopotamia already show artificial pollination: farmers hang male inflorescences on female trees, so that a few male plants ensure fruit set. The fruit (dates) is an important food in the Middle East. The Canary Island date palm (*P. canariensis*) is often used as a street tree.
7. *Calamus* (rattan): climbing vines in which a whip-like extension with backward-pointing hooked spines grows from the leaf rachis tip or the leaf sheath and attaches the plant to other trees, the stem reaching over a hundred meters in length; the fruit surface bears imbricate scales. About 500 species (including the merged *Daemonorops* and others), the largest genus of Arecaceae, from tropical Asia to Oceania. The stems of rattans (*C. tetradactylus* and others) are the "rattan" of which rattan chairs and mats are woven; the red resin secreted by the fruits of dragon's blood palm (*C. draco*, formerly in *Daemonorops*, now merged into *Calamus*) is one source of "dragon's blood".
8. Others: *Caryota maxima* (fishtail palm, often given as *C. ochlandra* in Chinese textbooks) has bipinnate leaves whose ultimate segments resemble fish tails and is the only genus of Arecaceae with bipinnate leaves; it is monocarpic, producing inflorescences in the leaf axils successively from the crown downward, and the plant dies after the lowest inflorescence has fruited; the pith of the stem contains starch. The royal palm (*Roystonea regia*), with a swollen mid-trunk, is native to Cuba and often used as a street tree; the Alexandra palm (*Archontophoenix alexandrae*), native to Australia, is common in gardens. The pith of the sago palm (*Metroxylon sagu*) contains large amounts of starch and is made into sago. The talipot palm (*Corypha umbraculifera*) has one of the largest inflorescences in the plant kingdom and is also monocarpic; its leaves were used to inscribe Buddhist scriptures (palm-leaf manuscripts), and Dai temples in Xishuangbanna, Yunnan, still preserve palm-leaf manuscripts. The nipa palm (*Nypa fruticans*) grows in tropical mangroves, with a stem creeping in the mud and leaves growing directly from the ground; it occurs in Hainan, China. The lady palm (*Rhapis excelsa*) and parlor palm (*Chamaedorea elegans*) are common potted foliage plants. The double coconut (*Lodoicea maldivica*) occurs in the Seychelles, with seeds weighing up to 15–30 kg, the largest seeds in the plant kingdom; the leaves of *Raphia* can reach 25 m in length, the longest leaves in the plant kingdom.

Key characters of the family: woody, the stem often unbranched with only one apical meristem and no secondary growth; large leaves crowded at the top of the stem, palmately or pinnately divided, with fibrous leaf sheaths; spadices much branched, with a spathe; flowers trimerous; ovary superior with 1 ovule per locule; drupe or berry, endosperm abundant.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Arecidae also included Arales, Pandanales and Cyclanthales; APG IV moves Araceae into Alismatales and Pandanaceae and Cyclanthaceae into Pandanales ([7.12.36](/7.12.36)). The family name of palms has two legitimate forms: Palmae (a conserved descriptive name) and Arecaceae (formed from the type genus *Areca*), and the Chinese textbooks also use the name "areca family".

## 38.3. Commelinales

### 38.3.1. Circumscription of the Order

APG IV Commelinales comprise 5 families: Commelinaceae, Hanguanaceae, Haemodoraceae, Philydraceae and Pontederiaceae, about 70 genera and over 800 species. Molecular evidence strongly supports their monophyly, but there are few morphological characters in common: abundant endosperm, tannin-containing cells in the perianth, and phenylphenalenone compounds (the roots of Haemodoraceae are orange-red for this reason). Commelinaceae and Hanguanaceae (*Hanguana*, Southeast Asia) form one clade, and Philydraceae, Haemodoraceae and Pontederiaceae form another.

Changes in circumscription: of Cronquist's Commelinales (Commelinaceae, Xyridaceae, Mayacaceae, Rapateaceae) only Commelinaceae remain in APG IV Commelinales, and the other families are moved to Poales; Pontederiaceae, Haemodoraceae and Philydraceae belonged in Cronquist's system to Liliales of the subclass Liliidae because they have large petaloid perianths.

### 38.3.2. Commelinaceae

Diagnostic characters of Commelinaceae (the spiderwort family):

- Annual or perennial herbs, the stem with conspicuous nodes and internodes, often fleshy, the lower part creeping and rooting at the nodes.
- Leaves alternate, with a conspicuous closed sheath; blade entire, parallel-veined.
- Inflorescence a scorpioid cyme, often enclosed in a spathe-like bract, or a panicle.
- Flowers bisexual, radially or bilaterally symmetric; sepals 3, green; petals 3, free or fused in the middle into a tube, often blue, purple or white; the flower lasts only one day, and the petals turn to mucilage as they wither ("open in the morning, wither at noon").
- Stamens 6, all fertile, or only 2–3 fertile and the rest staminodes; filaments often with long, beaded hairs (staminal hairs); pollen monosulcate.
- Ovary superior, 3-locular (or reduced to 2-locular), 1 to several orthotropous ovules per locule; style 1.
- Capsule loculicidal; seeds ridged, the hilum linear, with a disc-like embryotega (pushed off by the radicle at germination), the endosperm abundant.

Floral formula: $\ast,\, \uparrow K_3 C_3 A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3)}$ (fertile stamens often reduced to 2–3).

The family has about 40 genera and 650 species, mainly in the tropics and a few in the subtropics and temperate zones; China has about 15 genera and 60 species, most in the south and southwest. Representative plants:

- *Commelina communis* (Asiatic dayflower): an annual herb whose lower stem creeps and roots at the nodes; the cyme is enclosed in a folded, cordate spathe; flowers bilaterally symmetric, the 2 upper petals large, deep blue and clawed, the lower 1 small and white; fertile stamens 3, staminodes 3 with butterfly-shaped tips (yellow and conspicuous, attracting pollinating bees, though they produce almost no pollen); capsule with 4 seeds. It occurs throughout China and is a common field weed, and the whole plant is used in medicine; the blue of the petals comes from a metal-complex pigment of anthocyanin, flavone and magnesium ions (commelinin), which was once used as a dye in Japan.
- *Tradescantia*: flowers radially symmetric, all 6 stamens fertile, the filaments with dense, beaded long hairs. *T. ohiensis* is native to North America and often cultivated. Its staminal hairs consist of a row of large single cells in series; the cells are transparent with purple anthocyanin in the vacuoles, and vigorous cytoplasmic streaming can be seen directly under the microscope, making them classic material for botany laboratory classes. The cells of the staminal hairs are sensitive to ionizing radiation and chemical mutagens: after irradiation, heterozygous *Tradescantia* clones show mutant cells in the staminal hairs that change from blue to pink, which has been used as a bioassay for monitoring low-dose radiation and environmental mutagens. Purple heart (*T. pallida*, often given as *Setcreasea purpurea* in Chinese textbooks) is purple throughout, and wandering jew (*T. zebrina*) has leaves with silvery stripes; both are common ground covers and potted plants.
- *Murdannia*: *M. triquetra* is an aquatic or marsh herb with 3 fertile stamens and staminodes with hastate tips; it occurs in rice fields and by water in eastern, central and southwestern China.
- Others: *Streptolirion volubile* is a twining herb; the fruits of *Pollia* are indehiscent, blue and metallic, the color arising from the layered cellulose structure of the cell walls of the outer pericarp (structural color), not from a pigment (Vignolini et al. 2012).

Key characters of the family: herbs with conspicuous nodes; leaves with a closed sheath; perianth differentiated into sepals and petals, the petals often blue-purple and lasting only one day; stamens 6 or partly reduced, the filaments often with beaded hairs; ovary superior; capsule; seeds ridged and with an embryotega, the endosperm abundant.

### 38.3.3. Pontederiaceae

Pontederiaceae (the water hyacinth family) are aquatic or marsh herbs with rhizomes or stolons; leaves basal or alternate, with a sheath and a conspicuous petiole, the blade broad with arcuate veins (submerged leaves ribbon-like); inflorescence a raceme or panicle, borne within a spathe-like leaf sheath; flowers bisexual, radially or bilaterally symmetric, the perianth 6, petaloid, often blue-purple, fused at the base into a tube; stamens 6 (or 3), often adnate to the perianth tube; ovary superior, 3-locular; capsule. Floral formula: $\ast,\, \uparrow P_{(3+3)} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$. About 6–9 genera and 35 species in the tropics and subtropics. Recent molecular studies merge *Eichhornia*, *Monochoria* and others into *Pontederia*, leaving the family with only 2 genera (Pellegrini et al. 2018); this course still uses the traditional generic names.

- Water hyacinth (*Eichhornia crassipes*, now *Pontederia crassipes*): a floating herb whose petiole is swollen in the middle into a gourd-shaped air bladder (aerenchyma) that keeps the plant afloat; fibrous roots well developed, hanging in the water; spike with pale purple flowers, the upper tepal with a yellow eye-spot. Native to the Amazon basin of South America, it was introduced as an ornamental to all parts of the world, and in the 1950s and 1960s it was promoted in southern China as pig fodder; it later multiplied massively in rivers and lakes, blocking waterways, shading out light and lowering dissolved oxygen, and has been listed among the "100 of the world's worst invasive alien species". It reproduces mainly vegetatively by stolons, and one plant can produce tens of thousands of offspring in a growing season. In its native range water hyacinth is tristylous (individuals in a population have long, mid or short styles, as in purple loosestrife, [7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3)), whereas in invaded regions it is mostly of the mid-styled morph only, with few seeds and almost complete dependence on vegetative reproduction. This phenomenon has been used to study changes in the mating system during colonization (Barrett 1979).
- *Monochoria*: *M. korsakowii* has cordate leaves and blue flowers and grows in ponds and rice fields in the north; *M. vaginalis* is a common weed in rice fields in the south, and the young leaves are edible.
- Pickerel weed (*Pontederia cordata*): native to the Americas, with a blue-purple spike, often planted at water's edge.

### 38.3.4. Other Families of Commelinales

- Haemodoraceae: mainly in Australia, South Africa and the Americas, with roots and rhizomes containing red pigments; the kangaroo paws (*Anigozanthos*) of Australia have a densely hairy perianth tube and flowers shaped like a kangaroo's paw, are bird-pollinated and are the state flower of Western Australia.
- Philydraceae: *Philydrum lanuginosum* grows in paddy fields and marshes of southern China and Southeast Asia and is covered throughout with white woolly hairs; flowers bilaterally symmetric, with only 1 stamen.

## 38.4. Zingiberales

### 38.4.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Zingiberales are a morphologically very clearly defined order of 8 families and about 2500 species: Musaceae, Heliconiaceae, Strelitziaceae, Lowiaceae, Cannaceae, Marantaceae, Zingiberaceae and Costaceae, all in the tropics and subtropics. Their synapomorphies are:

- Large herbs with rhizomes; the aerial "stem" is often a pseudostem formed by successive wrapping leaf sheaths; leaves distichous (spirally arranged in a single rank in Costaceae).
- The leaf is differentiated into sheath, petiole and blade, the blade rolled into a cylinder in the bud (convolute); the blade has a stout midrib and many parallel lateral veins (pinnate-parallel venation), and the mesophyll between the lateral veins is easily torn by wind (banana); the petiole has large air canals; the vascular bundle sheaths contain silica cells.
- Flowers bilaterally symmetric or asymmetric, trimerous; perianth differentiated into sepals and petals (or composite tepals); ovary inferior, 3-locular; septal nectaries present.
- The stamens, from a basis of 6, undergo reduction and petaloidy to varying degrees: 5, 1 or "half" a stamen is fertile, and the rest are staminodes, which are often large and petaloid and are the most conspicuous part of the flower.
- Seeds often with an aril, with endosperm and perisperm.

The order falls into two parts: Musaceae, Heliconiaceae, Strelitziaceae and Lowiaceae (the "banana group", a paraphyletic group) have 5 fertile stamens; Cannaceae, Marantaceae, Zingiberaceae and Costaceae form the monophyletic "ginger group" with only 1 fertile stamen (only half a stamen in Cannaceae and Marantaceae, that is, only one anther locule is fertile), the other stamens becoming petaloid staminodes. The phylogenetic relationships are: Musaceae are the sister group of all the other families; Strelitziaceae and Lowiaceae are sister groups; within the ginger group, Cannaceae + Marantaceae and Zingiberaceae + Costaceae form two pairs of sister groups (Kress et al. 2001).

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Zingiberidae comprised Zingiberales and Bromeliales, and APG IV moves Bromeliaceae into Poales ([7.12.39](/7.12.39)). The Musaceae of the Chinese textbooks include *Strelitzia*, *Ravenala* and *Heliconia*, which are now separate as Strelitziaceae and Heliconiaceae, leaving Musaceae with only 3 genera, *Musa*, *Ensete* and *Musella*; the Zingiberaceae of the Chinese textbooks include *Costus*, which is now a separate family, Costaceae.

### 38.4.2. Reduction of Fertile Stamens and Petaloidy of Staminodes

The flowers of the families of Zingiberales can be arranged in a series that shows how the androecium is reduced step by step and "turns into petals" (Fig. 38.4.1). The basis is the androecium of a trimerous pentacyclic flower: 3 stamens in the outer whorl (1 abaxial median and 2 adaxial-lateral) and 3 in the inner whorl (1 adaxial median and 2 abaxial-lateral).

| Family | Fertile stamens | Staminodes | Conspicuous part of the flower |
|---|---|---|---|
| Musaceae, Heliconiaceae | 5 | 1 (the adaxial member of the inner whorl, small or absent) | Large colored bracts of the inflorescence |
| Strelitziaceae, Lowiaceae | 5 | 1 or none | Sepals and petals (bird of paradise: orange sepals and arrow-shaped blue petals) |
| Cannaceae | 1, with only half of the anther fertile and the other half flattened into a petal-like structure | 3–4, large and petaloid (the most brilliant "petals" of the flower) | Staminodes; the style is also flattened and petaloid |
| Marantaceae | Half, as in Cannaceae | 3–4, one hooded and one callose, part of the explosive pollination mechanism | Staminodes |
| Zingiberaceae | 1 (the central adaxial member of the inner whorl) | The other 2 of the inner whorl fused into the labellum; the 2 lateral members of the outer whorl are lateral staminodes (petaloid, tooth-like or absent), the abaxial member of the outer whorl is absent | Labellum |
| Costaceae | 1 | 5 fused into one large labellum | Labellum |

The reduction of fertile stamens goes together with the staminodes taking over the function of attracting pollinators, while the true petals become inconspicuous: the most brilliant structures of *Canna* and ginger lily are both derived from stamens. The labellum of Zingiberaceae has the same name as the labellum of Orchidaceae but a completely different origin: the orchid labellum is a petal (of the inner perianth whorl), whereas the ginger labellum is formed by the fusion of two staminodes. Developmental genetic studies show that the petaloidy of staminodes in Zingiberales is associated with changes in the expression patterns of class B and class C MADS-box genes in the stamen whorl.

### 38.4.3. Musaceae

Musaceae (the banana family) are large perennial herbs with rhizomes or corms; the leaf sheaths overlap to form a thick pseudostem, the leaves large, oblong, pinnately parallel-veined, often torn by wind along the lateral veins; inflorescence terminal, emerging from the center of the pseudostem and pendent or erect, with large, often colored spathe-like bracts, each bract enclosing 1–2 rows of flowers; flowers unisexual, those at the base of the inflorescence female and those above male; flowers bilaterally symmetric, tepals 6, of which 5 (3 outer and 2 inner) are fused into one composite tepal and the adaxial inner one is free; fertile stamens 5, the sixth reduced or absent; ovary inferior, 3-locular, axile placentation; berry, fleshy.

Floral formula: $\uparrow P_{(3+2)+1} A_{5} \overline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$.

The family has 3 genera and about 80 species in the Old World tropics. Of *Musa*, the Japanese banana (*M. basjoo*) is generally thought to be native to China and is often cultivated for ornament south of the Yangtze; in *Ensete* the base of the pseudostem is swollen like an elephant's leg; *Musella lasiocarpa* is a small banana endemic to Yunnan and Sichuan, with an erect inflorescence and golden bracts shaped like a lotus rosette, and is one of the "five trees and six flowers" of Buddhist temples.

Banana. Cultivated bananas derive from two wild species of Southeast Asia: *Musa acuminata* (genome denoted A) and *M. balbisiana* (genome denoted B). The fruits of wild bananas are full of hard seeds; cultivated bananas are diploid or triploid hybrids of the two and their descendants, the most important being triploids: AAA (such as the Cavendish group of international trade), AAB (plantains and some of the "apple" bananas) and ABB (such as the cooking bananas). In a triploid the homologous chromosomes cannot pair normally in meiosis and almost no functional gametes are formed, while the female flower of the banana can develop into a fruit without fertilization (spontaneous parthenocarpy, [7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.1.3)), so bananas have no seeds, only the degenerated ovules left in the pulp (the small black dots). Bananas are propagated by **vegetative propagation** through suckers arising from the rhizome, and all plants of one cultivar are genetically a single clone ([7.4.16](/7.4.16)).

This genetic uniformity makes bananas extremely vulnerable to disease. Fusarium wilt (Panama disease) is caused by the soil fungus *Fusarium oxysporum* f. sp. *cubense*, which enters from the roots and blocks the vascular bundles, causing the plant to wilt and die. In the first half of the 20th century race 1 of the wilt destroyed 'Gros Michel', then the main cultivar of international trade, and the industry was forced to switch to the resistant Cavendish; tropical race 4 (TR4), which appeared in the 1990s, can infect Cavendish and has spread from Southeast Asia to South Asia, the Middle East, Africa and South America. Breeding new cultivars from the resistance genes of wild species and diploid cultivars, and conserving the genetic diversity of bananas, are the main ways of meeting this crisis. The banana is one of the world's largest fruit crops; the leaf-sheath fibers of Manila hemp (abacá, *M. textilis*) are used for rope and for banknote paper.

### 38.4.4. Strelitziaceae, Heliconiaceae and Lowiaceae

- Strelitziaceae: 3 genera and 7 species in South Africa, Madagascar and South America. In the bird of paradise (*Strelitzia reginae*) the inflorescence emerges from a boat-shaped bract, with 3 orange-yellow sepals and 3 blue petals, 2 of them fused into an arrow shape that encloses the stamens and style; when a sunbird perches on the arrow-shaped petals to take nectar, its weight pushes the petals apart and pollen sticks to the bird's feet and belly (bird pollination, [7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.3)). The traveler's palm (*Ravenala madagascariensis*) occurs in Madagascar, with distichous leaves arranged in an enormous fan and rainwater collecting in the leaf sheaths, said to be drinkable by travelers; its flowers are pollinated by lemurs, and the seeds have a blue aril.
- Heliconiaceae: only *Heliconia*, about 200 species, mainly in tropical America; the bracts of the inflorescence are distichous, boat-shaped, bright red or orange-yellow, like a scorpion's tail; the flowers are pollinated by hummingbirds.
- Lowiaceae: only *Orchidantha*, about 20 species, from Southeast Asia to southern China, with flowers opening close to the ground and the median petal specialized into a lip, resembling an orchid; some species have a foul smell and are pollinated by beetles or flies. *O. chinensis* occurs in Guangdong and Guangxi.

### 38.4.5. Cannaceae and Marantaceae

Cannaceae have only *Canna*, about 10–20 species, native to tropical America. Perennial herbs with tuberous rhizomes; leaves large, pinnately parallel-veined; flowers bisexual, asymmetric; sepals 3, green; petals 3, small, fused at the base; the most brilliant "petals" of the flower are 3–4 petaloid staminodes, one of which is recurved downward and called the labellum; the single fertile stamen has a petal-like flat filament with one fertile anther locule on one side; the style is flat and also petaloid; ovary inferior, 3-locular, with small warty protuberances on the surface; the capsule is warty. *C. indica* is cultivated everywhere for ornament; Queensland arrowroot (*C. indica* 'Edulis', formerly *C. edulis*) has rhizomes rich in starch that can be made into noodles. The pollen of *Canna* is shed onto the flat style already in the bud (secondary pollen presentation) and is picked up from the style by visiting insects or birds.

Marantaceae have about 30 genera and 550 species in the tropics, most in the Americas; at the top of the petiole (where it joins the blade) there is a swollen pulvinus that senses light and moves, so that the blade folds up at night (nyctinastic movement); flowers often borne in pairs, asymmetric; the fertile stamen has only one anther locule; the style is held under "tension" in the bud, clamped by a hooded staminode, and when an insect touches the "trigger" on another staminode the style springs up, first scraping off the pollen the insect has brought and then smearing the flower's own pollen on the insect, the whole process taking less than a second and happening only once. Arrowroot (*Maranta arundinacea*) has rhizomes from which starch (arrowroot) is made; *Calathea* (now mostly merged into *Goeppertia*) has leaves with beautiful patterns and is a common indoor foliage plant; powdery alligator-flag (*Thalia dealbata*) is often planted at water's edge.

### 38.4.6. Zingiberaceae

Diagnostic characters of Zingiberaceae (the ginger family):

- Perennial herbs, usually aromatic (oil cells in the parenchyma); with creeping or tuberous rhizomes, sometimes with the root ends swollen into tuberous roots (as in *Curcuma aromatica*).
- Aerial stem often very short, or a pseudostem formed by the overlapping of many leaf sheaths.
- Leaves distichous, with an open or closed sheath at the base and a conspicuous ligule at the top of the sheath (between sheath and blade); the blade has one midrib and many pinnately parallel veins.
- Flowers bisexual, bilaterally symmetric, solitary or in spikes, heads, racemes or panicles, often with conspicuous bracts; the inflorescence is borne either at the top of a leafy stem (*Alpinia*) or on a leafless scape arising from the rhizome (*Zingiber*).
- Sepals 3, green or pale green, fused below into a tube; petals 3, fused below into a tube, the adaxial (posterior) lobe often larger.
- Stamens originally 6 in two whorls in development: the adaxial member of the inner whorl is the fertile stamen, with a grooved filament and a 2-locular anther; the other 2 of the inner whorl are fused into the petaloid labellum; the 2 lateral members of the outer whorl are lateral staminodes, petaloid, tooth-like or absent, and the abaxial median member of the outer whorl is absent.
- Ovary inferior, 3 carpels, 3-locular (axile placentation) or 1-locular (parietal placentation), ovules numerous; style 1, filiform, passing through the groove of the fertile stamen's filament and emerging between the two anther locules, stigma capitate.
- Capsule loculicidal into 3 valves, or fleshy, indehiscent and berry-like; seeds with an aril, with abundant endosperm and perisperm.

Floral formula: $\uparrow K_{(3)} C_{(3)} A_{1} \overline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$.

The family has about 50 genera and 1300–1600 species, mainly in tropical Asia and a few in Africa and the Americas; China has about 20 genera and 220 species, mainly in the southwest to southeast. Molecular phylogenetics divides the family into 4 subfamilies: Siphonochiloideae, Tamijioideae, Zingiberoideae and Alpinioideae. Representative plants:

1. *Zingiber*: rhizome fleshy with short, finger-like branches; the inflorescence is borne on a leafless scape arising from the rhizome, the bracts imbricate; the connective appendage is elongated into a beak that encloses the style. Ginger (*Z. officinale*) has a flattened rhizome, a yellow-green corolla and a labellum with purple and yellowish white spots; native to tropical Asia, cultivated ginger rarely flowers. The pungent principles of the rhizome are mainly gingerols, which are partly converted to the more pungent shogaols on heating or drying; it is used as a vegetable, a condiment and a drug (fresh ginger, dried ginger). *Z. mioga* has cylindrical rhizomes and inflorescences that emerge from the ground, and the young inflorescences and shoots are eaten as vegetables.
2. *Alpinia*: inflorescence terminal on a leafy stem; the lateral staminodes small or absent, the labellum large and conspicuous. About 250 species, one of the largest genera of Zingiberaceae. *A. japonica* occurs south of the Yangtze, and the fruits and rhizomes are used in medicine; shell ginger (*A. zerumbet*) has a pendent inflorescence and a yellow labellum with red stripes and is often cultivated for ornament; the rhizomes of galangal (*A. officinarum*) and the fruits of *A. oxyphylla* and *A. hainanensis* are commonly used drugs and spices. *Alpinia* shows a special "flexistyly": there are two kinds of individuals in a population, one in which the style curls upward in the morning and extends downward in front of the anthers in the afternoon, and the other doing the opposite; this behavioral herkogamy ensures cross-pollination (Li et al. 2001; [7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.1)).
3. *Amomum* and related genera: the inflorescence is borne on a scape arising from the rhizome, the labellum large; capsule spiny or winged. The seed mass of *A. villosum* (recently moved to *Wurfbainia* in some treatments), the "sharen", is an aromatic stomachic and comes from Guangdong, Guangxi, Yunnan and Fujian; the fruit of *A. tsao-ko* is used as a condiment and a drug; the fruit of *A. kravanh* is the Chinese drug "doukou". Cardamom (*Elettaria cardamomum*), native to southern India, has seeds that are one of the most expensive spices in Western and South Asian cooking (after saffron and vanilla).
4. *Curcuma*: rhizome stout, the root ends often swollen into tuberous roots; the upper bracts of the spike are often colored and flowerless. Turmeric (*C. longa*) has a yellow rhizome containing curcumin and is the principal spice of curry and a natural yellow pigment; the tuberous roots of *C. aromatica* are the Chinese drug "yujin"; the rhizomes of *C. phaeocaulis* and others are also used in medicine.
5. Others: white ginger lily (*Hedychium coronarium*) has white, extremely fragrant flowers that open at night, is pollinated by hawkmoths and is often used as a cut flower; the rhizome of kencur (*Kaempferia galanga*) is a condiment; torch ginger (*Etlingera elatior*) has a large red inflorescence; *Globba* has pendent inflorescences with slender filaments.

Key characters of the family: perennial herbs, often aromatic; leaves distichous, with a conspicuous ligule at the top of the sheath; flowers bilaterally symmetric, the sepals and petals clearly different, with 1 fertile stamen and a petaloid labellum (2 fused staminodes); ovary inferior; seeds with an aril.

### 38.4.7. Costaceae

Costaceae have about 7 genera and 120 species in the tropics. They resemble Zingiberaceae, but the plant has no aromatic oils; the stem is often branched; the leaves are arranged spirally in a single rank along the stem (monostichous spiral phyllotaxis), and the leaf sheaths are closed into a tube ("closed sheath"); the labellum is formed by the fusion of 5 staminodes and is large and conspicuous; the single fertile stamen is petaloid. *Hellenia speciosa* (formerly *Costus speciosus*, crepe ginger) occurs in southern and southwestern China, with a terminal inflorescence, red bracts and a white labellum, and the rhizome is used in medicine. Costaceae were treated as the subfamily Costoideae of Zingiberaceae and are now a separate family.

## Questions

1. Palms, pines and poplars are all tall trees. Compare their trunks in mode of growth and structure, and explain why cutting off the crown of a palm kills the whole tree.

   **Answer** Pines and poplars have a vascular cambium and a cork cambium, so the trunk undergoes secondary growth every year, producing secondary xylem (annual rings) and periderm, and can thicken continuously; the branches bear many terminal and axillary buds. A palm has no cambium; the thickness of the trunk is reached in the juvenile establishment growth by the primary thickening meristem, and afterward it thickens slightly only by cell enlargement and lignification; the vascular bundles are scattered and there are no annual rings. Most single-stemmed palms have only one apical meristem, enclosed within the leaf sheaths in the center of the crown, with no lateral bud that could replace it. Cutting off the crown destroys this only growing point, the plant can produce no new leaves, and it therefore dies.

2. How does the labellum of Zingiberaceae differ from that of Orchidaceae? What organs are the most brilliant "petals" of the *Canna* flower?

   **Answer** The orchid labellum is one petal of the inner perianth whorl (lying below after resupination); the ginger labellum is formed by the fusion of two staminodes of the inner whorl and is not homologous with a petal, only similar in function (analogous organs). The true petals of the *Canna* flower are small and inconspicuous, and the most brilliant "petals" are 3–4 petaloid staminodes; its single fertile stamen has an anther locule on only one side, the other half being flat like a petal, and the style is also petaloid.

3. Why do cultivated bananas have no seeds? What problem does this mode of reproduction bring?

   **Answer** Cultivated bananas are mostly triploid (AAA, AAB, ABB); the homologous chromosomes cannot pair normally in meiosis and almost no functional gametes are formed; at the same time the female flower of the banana can develop into a fruit without fertilization (spontaneous parthenocarpy), so the fruit contains only degenerated ovules and no seeds. Bananas can be propagated only vegetatively by suckers, and the plants of one cultivar are a genetically uniform clone lacking genetic variation for disease resistance, so a new pathogen can destroy the whole cultivar: race 1 of Fusarium wilt destroyed 'Gros Michel', and tropical race 4 now threatens the present Cavendish.

4. Water hyacinth is tristylous in its native South America but has almost only one style morph in invaded regions. What does this show? Why can it become a noxious weed?

   **Answer** Tristyly is a mechanism promoting outcrossing, and normal seed set requires individuals of all three morphs. Invading populations are usually founded by a few individuals (even a single clone) that brought only one morph, so sexual reproduction is blocked and the population is maintained almost entirely by vegetative reproduction; this shows that a bottleneck during colonization can change the mating system of a plant. Water hyacinth becomes a noxious weed because its vegetative reproduction is extremely fast (stolons produce new plants, and one plant can give tens of thousands in a growing season), the swollen air bladders of the petioles let it float and spread, and in eutrophic water it lacks enemies and competitors; once it covers large areas of water surface it shades out light, lowers dissolved oxygen, and blocks rivers and irrigation channels.

5. The commelinids include palms, dayflowers, bananas, gingers and grasses, which differ enormously in outward appearance. On what basis are they regarded as a monophyletic group? Why did Cronquist not place them together?

   **Answer** On molecular phylogeny and on synapomorphies such as ester-linked ferulic acid in the cell walls (fluorescing under ultraviolet light), silica bodies in the epidermis and *Strelitzia*-type epidermal wax. Cronquist relied mainly on the morphology of flowers and inflorescences: Arecaceae, with a spadix and spathe resembling Araceae and Pandanaceae, were placed in Arecidae; Pontederiaceae, with a petaloid perianth, were placed in Liliidae; Zingiberales and Bromeliales were combined as Zingiberidae; and Commelinaceae, Poaceae and Cyperaceae were in Commelinidae. These similarities of flowers and inflorescences are mostly convergences or plesiomorphies and masked the relationships reflected by cell-wall chemistry.
