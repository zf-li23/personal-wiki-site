Traditional botany grouped the nonphotosynthetic, heterotrophic lower plants as the "fungal plants" (Chinese textbooks' term), comprising three phyla: bacteria, slime molds and fungi. Like the algae they lack roots, stems and leaves, reproduce by spores and have mostly unicellular reproductive organs, so the two-kingdom system placed them in the plant kingdom, together with the algae and lichens, as the "lower plants" ([7.12.1](/7.12.1#sec-1.1)). Molecular phylogenies show that this "fungal" assemblage comes from at least three supergroups of the eukaryotic tree: the fungi belong with the animals in the opisthokonts, the slime molds are amoebozoans, and the oomycetes belong with the brown algae and diatoms among the **stramenopiles**. Nevertheless, the morphology, life histories and classification of these organisms have long been part of botany, their names are governed by the *International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants* ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.4)), and they are closely tied to plants in plant diseases, mycorrhizae, lichens and the origin of land plants.

This chapter first describes the traditional range of the "fungal plants" and their modern placement (§10.1), then deals with two groups once treated as fungi, the slime molds (§10.2) and the oomycetes (§10.3); it then turns to the vegetative body of fungi (§10.4), their reproduction (§10.5) and their classification (§10.6), and ends with the "lower fungi": chytrids, Blastocladiomycota, Mucoromycota and Glomeromycotina (§10.7). The Ascomycota and Basidiomycota are treated in [7.12.11](/7.12.11) and the lichens in [7.12.12](/7.12.12). The cell biology of fungi (chitinous cell walls, apical growth and the Spitzenkörper, septal pores, yeast life cycles and mating types) was covered in [7.11.21](/7.11.21), and the organelles of protists in [7.11.20](/7.11.20); here we only link to them and concentrate on botanical content: morphology and structure, representative genera, complete life histories, a comparison of old and new classification systems, and ecological and economic significance. Bacteria and actinomycetes are prokaryotes (see [7.11.17](/7.11.17) and [7.11.18](/7.11.18)); cyanobacteria, as "algae", are treated in [7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.5).

## 10.1. Scope and Placement of the Fungal Organisms

### 10.1.1. The Traditional Fungal Plants

In Chinese textbooks the "fungal plants" are "a group of lower heterotrophic plants without photosynthetic pigments", traditionally divided into three phyla:

| Phylum | Vegetative body | Cell wall | Reproduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bacteria (Bacteriophyta) | unicellular, prokaryotic | peptidoglycan | cell division, no sexual reproduction |
| Slime molds (Myxomycota) | naked, multinucleate plasmodium that moves and feeds like an amoeba | none in the vegetative phase; spores have cellulose walls | spores; sexual reproduction |
| Fungi (Eumycota) | unicellular or mycelial | mostly chitin, in a few cellulose | various asexual and sexual spores |

Bacteria feed in basically the same ways as fungi, and the two-kingdom system therefore placed them in the same chapter. Actinomycetes are bacteria that form branched hyphae and conidia; they were once regarded as a transitional group between bacteria and fungi but are in fact typical prokaryotes (the phylum **Actinomycetota**, [7.11.18](/7.11.18)), resembling fungi only through **convergent evolution**.

In 1969 Whittaker's five-kingdom system divided the multicellular eukaryotes by mode of nutrition into three kingdoms: the photosynthetic autotrophic plants, the ingestive animals and the absorptive fungi. Ainsworth's system (1973) divided the kingdom Fungi into two phyla, Myxomycota (slime molds) and Eumycota (true fungi), the latter further divided into five subdivisions (§10.6.1). From then on the phylum endings were changed from the plant ending -phyta to -mycota (the slime molds were once written Myxomycophyta or Myxothallophyta, and the true fungi Eumycophyta). Chinese textbooks long followed this system.

### 10.1.2. Modern Placement

Molecular phylogenies scatter the "fungal plants" among several supergroups of the eukaryotic tree (Figure 1; see also the tree of life in [7.12.1](/7.12.1#sec-1.1.3)):

| Traditional group | Current placement | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Bacteria (including actinomycetes) | domain Bacteria | prokaryotic cells |
| Myxomycetes (plasmodial slime molds), dictyostelids | eukaryotes, Amorphea: Amoebozoa | lobose pseudopodia, tubular mitochondrial cristae, rRNA and multigene phylogenies |
| Acrasids (*Acrasis*) | eukaryotes, Discoba: Heterolobosea | discoid mitochondrial cristae, close to *Naegleria* |
| Plasmodiophorids | eukaryotes, SAR: Rhizaria | multigene phylogenies |
| Oomycetes, hyphochytrids, labyrinthulids | eukaryotes, SAR: stramenopiles | heterokont flagella (hairy and smooth), cellulosic walls |
| Fungi (chytrids to basidiomycetes) | eukaryotes, Amorphea: Opisthokonta | a single posterior flagellum (chytrids), chitinous wall, glycogen storage, sister group of the animals |

The *opisthokonts* are named because their motile cells (animal sperm, chytrid zoospores) have a single flagellum at the posterior end. The Amoebozoa and the opisthokonts together form the Amorphea, so the slime molds are in fact more closely related to the fungi than the oomycetes are. Oomycetes resemble fungi in feeding by hyphae and absorption, but this is convergent evolution between two lineages: their cell walls, flagella, ploidy and lysine biosynthetic pathway all differ from those of fungi (§10.3.1).

The discipline that studies these organisms is still called mycology, and they are often discussed together in plant pathology and ecology. The 8th edition of Ainsworth and Bisby's *Dictionary of the Fungi* (Hawksworth et al. 1995) assigned the organisms studied by mycologists to three kingdoms: Fungi, Chromista (including the oomycetes) and Protozoa (including the slime molds).

**Definition 10.1.1 (Fungi Sensu Lato)** **Fungi sensu lato**, also called fungal organisms, is the collective term for all the organisms studied by mycology: the fungi of the kingdom Fungi, together with groups traditionally treated as fungi that are phylogenetically unrelated to them but feed by absorption or phagocytosis through hyphae or plasmodia and reproduce by spores, such as the slime molds and oomycetes. The range is drawn by research tradition, not by relationship.

The Chinese term for fungal organisms was advocated by Weifan Qiu and colleagues in the 1990s; the Mycological Society of China adopted its present name in 1993, and the Chinese title of the Code likewise uses it. In this course, therefore, "fungi" means only the kingdom Fungi (**fungi**, [Definition 21.1.1](/7.11.21#def-21.1.1)), whereas "fungi sensu lato" and "fungal organisms" include the slime molds and oomycetes.

## 10.2. Slime Molds

In the vegetative phase slime molds resemble animals: they have no cell wall, move by amoeboid motion and ingest bacteria, yeasts and organic particles. In the reproductive phase they resemble plants or fungi: they form fruiting bodies and produce spores with cellulose walls. About 1000 species of plasmodial slime molds have been described worldwide; most live on dark, damp rotting wood, fallen leaves and other moist organic matter in forests. Almost all are saprotrophic (phagotrophic) and have no direct economic importance. The cell biology and definition of slime molds are given in [7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.7) (**slime molds**), and the aggregation and pattern formation of cellular slime molds in [7.5.13](/7.5.13).

### 10.2.1. The Vegetative Body of Plasmodial Slime Molds

**Definition 10.2.1 (Plasmodial Body)** A **plasmodium** is the vegetative body of the plasmodial slime molds (class Myxogastria): a mass of protoplasm without a cell wall, containing many synchronously dividing diploid nuclei, capable of amoeboid movement and taking up nutrients by phagocytosis and absorption; Chinese textbooks also call it the amoeboid body.

A plasmodium is usually fan-shaped or net-like, several centimeters across; *Physarum polycephalum* can grow to tens of centimeters or even more than 1 m. The veins of the network consist of a more gelled outer layer and a more solated inner layer of protoplasm; the inner protoplasm streams back and forth about once every minute or two, driven by rhythmic contraction of actomyosin, and the fan-shaped front advances toward food ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.7)). The nuclei divide but the cell does not, so the plasmodium is a single giant multinucleate cell; it can pass through silk cloth or filter paper without damage.

Under unfavorable conditions the plasmodium has two kinds of resting structures: on drying it becomes a hard **sclerotium** (the same name as the fungal sclerotium, but here only a hardening of the plasmodium, see §10.4.2); when food is lacking the vegetative cells (myxamoebae) round up and secrete a thin wall to become microcysts, which can survive for more than a year.

### 10.2.2. Sporangia and Life History

When food is exhausted and the plasmodium has migrated to a dry, bright place, it enters the reproductive phase. In most species the plasmodium divides into many small masses of similar size, each developing into a stalked sporangium; in some species the whole plasmodium keeps its shape and becomes a plasmodiocarp (e.g., *Hemitrichia serpula*), or collects into a large cushion-shaped aethalium (e.g., *Lycogala*, *Fuligo septica*).

Take *Stemonitis* as an example. The plasmodium forms many hair-like projections on rotting wood, and each develops into a stalked sporangium. The sporangium is elongate-cylindrical, purple-brown, enclosed by a peridium; the part of the stalk that extends into the sporangium is the columella, and the capillitium threads in the sporangium interweave into a capillitial network. The protoplasm of the plasmodium cleaves into many uninucleate pieces, each secreting a wall and becoming a spore, lodged in the meshes of the capillitium; when ripe, the peridium breaks and the elasticity of the capillitium scatters the spores. The presence and form of capillitium, the structure of the peridium and columella and the color of the spores are the main bases for dividing the slime molds into genera: in *Trichia* the capillitial threads have spiral thickenings, in *Arcyria* the capillitium expands into a cottony mass after the peridium breaks, and in *Cribraria* (formerly *Dictydium*) only a lattice remains of the peridium.

The **life history** (Figure 2) is of a type other than zygotic meiosis: the vegetative body (the plasmodium) is diploid, and meiosis takes place during spore formation (in most species after the spore is cleaved off, 3 of the 4 nuclei produced by meiosis in each spore degenerating), so the spores are haploid. A spore germinates to release one myxamoeba or a flagellated swarm cell with two unequal whiplash flagella; the two forms can interconvert, feed on bacteria and multiply by mitosis. Two swarm cells or myxamoebae of compatible mating types fuse as gametes (karyogamy follows plasmogamy immediately), the zygote undergoes no resting period, its nucleus divides repeatedly by mitosis without cytokinesis, and it grows into a new diploid plasmodium. In a few strains the plasmodium grows directly from a single myxamoeba and is haploid (apomixis).

When food is exhausted, the diploid plasmodium forms stalked sporangia (with columella and capillitium); meiosis takes place during spore formation, and the spores germinate into myxamoebae or swarm cells; two compatible gametes fuse into a zygote, whose nucleus divides repeatedly without cell division and grows into a new plasmodium. On drying the plasmodium becomes a sclerotium, which resumes activity when conditions improve.](./plots/myxomycete-life-cycle.py)

### 10.2.3. Dictyostelids, Acrasids and Plasmodiophorids

Chinese textbooks divide the slime mold phylum into three classes: Myxomycetes, Acrasiomycetes and Plasmodiophoromycetes. We now know that they belong to three different supergroups:

- Dictyostelids (cellular slime molds, Dictyostelia): the vegetative phase consists of dispersed uninucleate amoebae; when food is exhausted they respond to waves of **cyclic AMP** and aggregate into a slug-like pseudoplasmodium, which then differentiates into a fruiting body made of a stalk and a spore mass; the stalk cells die and become encased in cellulose, and the cells of the spore mass become spores. Dictyostelids belong with the plasmodial slime molds in the Amoebozoa. The life history of the model organism *Dictyostelium discoideum*, proportion regulation and mathematical models of aggregation are treated in [7.5.13](/7.5.13). The old Acrasiomycetes included the dictyostelids and *Acrasis*, whose fruiting body is also formed by aggregated amoebae, but molecular and ultrastructural evidence (discoid mitochondrial cristae) places it in the Heterolobosea of the Discoba, so its similarity to the dictyostelids is a result of convergence.
- Plasmodiophorids (Plasmodiophorida): parasites inside the cells of higher plants, algae or oomycetes; the vegetative body is a plasmodium that forms no fruiting body, and resting spores form directly inside the host cell. *Plasmodiophora brassicae* attacks the roots of crucifers and makes them swell into galls (clubroot, popularly called "big head disease" in China), an important disease of rape, Chinese cabbage and cabbage; the resting spores can survive in the soil for more than ten years, germinate to release zoospores that enter through root hairs, lose their flagella and develop into multinucleate plasmodia in the host cells, which finally form resting spores again. Molecular phylogenies place the plasmodiophorids in the Rhizaria, unrelated to either fungi or slime molds. *Spongospora subterranea*, the cause of powdery scab of potato, also belongs here.
- Labyrinthulomycetes: the "slime nets" of the sea, whose cells glide within an ectoplasmic network that they secrete themselves; traditionally also treated as slime molds, they now belong to the stramenopiles. *Labyrinthula zosterae* causes the wasting disease of eelgrass.

### 10.2.4. The Position of Slime Molds in the Living World

The vegetative body of slime molds resembles an amoeba, yet reproduction yields spores with walls, so for a long time they wavered between animals and fungi. De Bary called them Mycetozoa in 1859 and regarded them as animals; Schröter (1889) separated slime molds from bacteria and fungi as an independent phylum of the plant kingdom; Martin (1949) held that slime molds arose from protozoan-like ancestors but still placed them among the fungi; Ainsworth (1973) placed the Myxomycota in the kingdom Fungi. Molecular phylogenies finally support de Bary's view: the plasmodial slime molds, dictyostelids and protosteloids (single amoebae forming tiny fruiting bodies with only one or a few spores) form the Mycetozoa branch of the Amoebozoa, close relatives of the lobose amoebae; their sexual reproduction shows that sex arose very early in eukaryotes.

## 10.3. Oomycetes

### 10.3.1. Characteristics of Oomycetes

The **oomycetes** (phylum Oomycota; [Definition 20.5.2](/7.11.20#def-20.5.2)) comprise more than a thousand species. The vegetative body is a branched, aseptate, multinucleate mycelium that forms cross walls only at the bases of reproductive organs. Asexual reproduction yields biflagellate zoospores; sexual reproduction is oogamous and yields thick-walled oospores, from which the phylum name is derived (Greek *oon*, egg). In Ainsworth's system they belong to the class Oomycetes of the Mastigomycotina; today they belong, with the brown algae and diatoms, to the **stramenopiles**: their genomes retain genes derived from a plastid, which shows that the ancestors of oomycetes were probably photosynthetic organisms that lost their plastids.

| Feature | Oomycetes | Fungi |
|---|---|---|
| Systematic position | stramenopiles | opisthokonts |
| Cell wall | cellulose and β-1,3-glucan, very little chitin | chitin and β-glucan |
| Ploidy of the vegetative body | diploid; meiosis occurs in the oogonium and antheridium (**gametic meiosis**) | mostly haploid or dikaryotic; meiosis follows karyogamy immediately (**zygotic meiosis**) |
| Flagella | 2: anterior hairy and posterior whiplash (in the primary zoospores of *Saprolegnia* both are apical) | chytrids etc. have 1 posterior whiplash flagellum; most groups have none |
| Mitochondrial cristae | tubular | flat |
| Lysine biosynthesis | diaminopimelate pathway (as in plants and bacteria) | α-aminoadipate pathway |
| Sterols | *Phytophthora* and *Pythium* cannot synthesize sterols and must obtain them from the host | ergosterol |
| Sexual reproduction | oogamous: oogonium and antheridium, fertilization tube | no oogamy |

These differences have practical significance: many fungicides that act on chitin or ergosterol synthesis are ineffective against oomycetes, and oomycete diseases must be controlled with special agents (such as metalaxyl and dimethomorph).

The sexual organs of oomycetes are the *oogonium* (called the egg sac in Chinese textbooks) and the antheridium (called the sperm sac in Chinese textbooks). The oogonium is spherical and contains one to several egg cells (oospheres); the antheridium is smaller, applied to the oogonium, and puts out a fertilization tube that penetrates the oogonial wall and delivers the male nucleus into the oosphere.

**Definition 10.3.1 (Oospore)** The thick-walled diploid zygote formed in the oogonium of an oomycete after fertilization of the oosphere is called the **oospore**; after a resting period it germinates into a germ tube or a sporangium, and is the structure by which oomycetes overwinter and survive adverse conditions.

### 10.3.2. Water Molds (*Saprolegnia*)

*Saprolegnia* belongs to the order Saprolegniales and is mostly a saprotroph in fresh water, living on the bodies of dead fishes, tadpoles, insects and other animals; there are also parasitic species, such as *S. parasitica*, which grows on the gill covers, lateral lines and other injured skin of freshwater fish and on fish eggs and is a great scourge of fish (Figure 3).

The mycelium is white and downy, develops from a single cell, and is aseptate and multinucleate. There are two kinds of hyphae: short rhizoidal hyphae that penetrate the host tissue to absorb nutrients, and long, slender, branched hyphae that grow outward from the surface of the substrate and form a small, richly branched mycelium.

In asexual reproduction the tip of a hypha swells slightly, many nuclei flow into it, and a cross wall forms at the base, producing an elongate-cylindrical zoosporangium (about 300–650 µm × 30–65 µm). When the sporangium is ripe, a round pore opens at its tip and the zoospores swim out one after another. Then a second sporangium grows up from the base of the old one, extending into the empty shell of the old sporangium; sporangia can be produced three or four times in this way, one inside another, which is called internal proliferation of sporangia and is one of the main characters of *Saprolegnia* (in the related genus *Achlya*, the new sporangium arises at the side of the old one).

The zoospores that emerge are pear-shaped with two apical flagella and are called primary zoospores. After swimming for a short time, a zoospore withdraws its flagella and forms a spherical resting spore (aplanospore); soon the resting spore germinates into a kidney-shaped secondary zoospore with two lateral flagella (one hairy and one smooth), which swims for a time and again becomes a resting spore, and finally germinates into a germ tube that enters a new substrate or host and grows into a mycelium.

**Definition 10.3.2 (Diplanetism)** The phenomenon in which zoospores swim for a time, come to rest and then germinate into zoospores of another form, so that the same individual produces two kinds of zoospores in succession, is called **diplanetism**. In *Saprolegnia* the primary zoospore is pear-shaped with two apical flagella, and the secondary zoospore is kidney-shaped with two lateral flagella.

The two swimming phases and two resting phases are interpreted as increasing the dispersal distance and the chances of finding a substrate; in many oomycetes the primary swimming phase has been lost: in *Achlya* the primary zoospores come to rest immediately outside the sporangium mouth and aggregate into a mass, and in the Peronosporales only the kidney-shaped secondary type of zoospore occurs.

Sexual reproduction takes place under unfavorable conditions such as nutrient shortage: oogonia and antheridia form at the tips of hyphae. The oogonium is spherical, with a cross wall at the base, and contains 1–20 oospheres; the antheridium is smaller, elongate and multinucleate, and usually arises on the same hypha as the oogonium (homothallism; *Achlya* includes heterothallic species). Meiosis takes place in the oogonium and antheridium, so the oospheres and male nuclei are haploid. The antheridium puts out one to several fertilization tubes that penetrate the oogonial wall and release male nuclei, which fuse with the oospheres to form diploid oospores. After a resting period, the oospores are released from the broken oogonium and germinate into germ tubes, which grow into a diploid mycelium. Chinese textbooks once held that meiosis occurs when the oospore germinates and that the vegetative body is haploid; cytological and genetic studies show that the vegetative body of oomycetes is diploid and the life history is of the diploid type ([7.4.1](/7.4.1); **gametic meiosis**), the same as in the brown alga *Pelvetia*, in diatoms and in animals.

*Achlya ambisexualis* is the classical material for studying the sex hormones of eukaryotes: in the 1930s–1950s J. R. Raper found that a steroid hormone secreted by the female strain (later called antheridiol) induces the male strain to form antheridia, and the antheridia in turn secrete oogoniol, which induces the female strain to form oogonial initials; this is one of the earliest pheromone systems elucidated in a fungus-like organism.

Water mold disease (saprolegniasis) is most serious in fry and in eggs being incubated, and mortality is high in densely stocked fish ponds. The water mold enters through wounds in the fish, so abrasion of the fish must be avoided during stocking; infected fish are commonly bathed in salt water or in a mixture of sodium bicarbonate and salt, and ponds are cleared with lime to kill the spores. Malachite green, once widely used, has been banned for food fish in most countries because it is carcinogenic, and control of saprolegniasis has therefore become a difficulty in aquaculture.

### 10.3.3. Peronosporales: Downy Mildews, *Plasmopara* and *Phytophthora*

The Peronosporales are terrestrial oomycetes, mostly obligate or facultative parasites of higher plants, and the causes of "downy mildew" and "blight" diseases. Their sporangia emerge from the host's stomata on sporangiophores, are shed and are spread by wind; in the presence of water a sporangium releases kidney-shaped zoospores or germinates directly into a germ tube that enters the host, in which case the sporangium acts as a conidium, so Chinese textbooks often call it a "conidium". The mycelium grows between host cells and sends haustoria (§10.4.3) into the cells to absorb nutrients; late in growth, oogonia and antheridia form in the host tissue and oospores are produced to overwinter.

- *Peronospora* and related genera: *P. parasitica*, the crucifer downy mildew of Chinese textbooks, is now placed in the genus *Hyaloperonospora* as *Hyaloperonospora parasitica* (in the strict sense *H. parasitica* is the species on shepherd's purse, and the one on *Brassica* vegetables is now *H. brassicae*), the main pathogen of Chinese cabbage, rape, cabbage, radish and other crucifer vegetables; the related species *H. arabidopsidis* on *Arabidopsis* is a model pathogen for studying plant disease resistance. The disease occurs mainly on leaves, with pale yellow-green lesions and a white downy mold layer (the sporangiophores and sporangia) on the underside; later the lesions turn orange-yellow and the leaves die. The sporangiophores are single or in clusters, dichotomously branched 4–7 times in the upper part, with pointed tips bearing nearly spherical, colorless sporangia. Control relies mainly on seed disinfection, crop rotation, removal of diseased residues and resistant cultivars.
- *Plasmopara*: the sporangiophores branch monopodially, the branches at right angles to the main axis. *P. viticola* causes downy mildew of grape; it is native to North America and was brought to France in the late 1870s with American rootstocks resistant to phylloxera, and within a few years threatened the whole French wine industry. Pierre-Marie-Alexis Millardet of the University of Bordeaux noticed in 1882 that vines along roadsides in the Médoc that had been sprayed with a mixture of copper sulfate and lime (to deter passers-by from picking the grapes) were not diseased, and after experiments published the formula of this mixture in 1885: Bordeaux mixture (a suspension of $\ce{CuSO4}$ and $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$). It was the first widely used chemical against plant disease and is still in use today.
- *Phytophthora* ("plant destroyer"): about 200 species, many important plant pathogens. *P. infestans* causes late blight of potato and tomato: water-soaked brown spots appear on the leaves, white mold forms on the underside in wet weather, and the tubers rot. It was brought from the Americas to Europe in 1845 and destroyed the potatoes on which the Irish poor depended; the Great Famine of 1845–1849 killed about a million people and forced as many to emigrate ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.5.3)), and it is still the most serious disease of potato today. *P. infestans* has two mating types, A1 and A2; when both are present they reproduce sexually, and the oospores can overwinter in the soil. Its genome is about 240 Mb and rich in transposons, and the rapidly evolving effector genes lie in repeat-rich regions of the genome, so that the resistance of resistant cultivars often breaks down within a few years. In addition, *P. cinnamomi* is a soil inhabitant that has destroyed large areas of eucalypt forest and many avocado orchards in Australia; *P. ramorum*, the cause of sudden oak death, was identified in California in 2000 and attacks many oaks, rhododendrons and redwoods; and *P. sojae* causes root rot of soybean.
- *Albugo* (white rusts, now placed in their own order Albuginales): obligate parasites of crucifers that form white blister-like sporangial pustules on the underside of the leaf and on the stem, often occurring together with downy mildew.

### 10.3.4. *Pythium*

*Pythium* is a facultative parasite living in the soil, distributed all over the world, and is the main cause of damping-off: it attacks seeds and makes them rot, or attacks the stem base of seedlings, so that they topple and die before emergence (pre-emergence damping-off) or after emergence (post-emergence damping-off); it does most harm in densely sown seedbeds and greenhouses and is also an important disease of lawns. The sporangium produces a vesicle, in which the zoospores form and from which they are released. *P. oligandrum* parasitizes other oomycetes and fungi and has been developed as a biocontrol agent.

## 10.4. The Vegetative Body of Fungi

Fungal cells have no chlorophyll or plastids and are typical heterotrophs, absorbing organic matter from living and dead animals and plants, excreta, leaf litter and soil humus. The stored food is mainly **glycogen**, with a little protein, lipid and trace vitamins, rather than starch; this distinguishes fungi from parasitic colorless green algae. With few exceptions, fungi have a distinct cell wall, are usually immobile and reproduce by spores.

### 10.4.1. Hyphae and Mycelium

Apart from unicellular fungi such as yeasts, the great majority of fungi are made of **hyphae**, and all the hyphae of one individual together are called the **mycelium** ([Definition 21.2.1](/7.11.21#def-21.2.1)). Hyphae are tubes 2–10 µm in diameter that grow only at the tip (the vesicle supply center at the tip is the **Spitzenkörper**, [7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.2.1)), and are of two kinds:

- Aseptate (coenocytic) hyphae: a single long, tubular, multinucleate cell, branched or unbranched, forming cross walls only at the bases of reproductive organs or at wounds; the hyphae of chytrids and Mucoromycota (and of oomycetes) are of this kind.
- Septate hyphae: cross walls divide the hypha into many cells, each containing 1, 2 or several nuclei. The septum has a central pore through which cytoplasm and even nuclei can pass; the septal pore of ascomycetes is simple, with a Woronin body beside it, and in the Agaricomycotina there is a dolipore septum with membranous pore caps on both sides of the pore ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.2.1)). The hyphae of the Ascomycota and Basidiomycota are septate.

The main components of the fungal cell wall are **chitin** and β-glucan (the lower fungi and oomycetes are often lumped together as having "cellulose walls", but the cellulosic wall is in fact characteristic of oomycetes, while the walls of all fungi contain chitin). The composition of the wall changes with age and environment, and in some fungi the wall contains melanin and other substances and is black, brown or another color. The hyphal cells contain protoplasm, nuclei, vacuoles and stored protein, oil droplets and glycogen; the nuclei are very small in vegetative cells and large and conspicuous in reproductive cells.

Hyphae are also the organs of nutrient absorption. Fungi have a very large surface-to-volume ratio, and any part of the mycelium is only a few micrometers from the outside world. The hyphae secrete extracellular hydrolytic enzymes (cellulases, pectinases, proteases, lignin-degrading enzymes and others) into the substrate, break macromolecules down into soluble small molecules and absorb them by virtue of a high osmotic pressure; the osmotic pressure of parasitic fungi is generally 2–5 times that of the host, and that of saprotrophic fungi is higher still.

### 10.4.2. Hyphal Tissues

In some fungi, under adverse conditions or at the time of reproduction, the hyphae interweave tightly and the mycelium is metamorphosed into structures of definite shape called hyphal tissues. Two kinds of tissue make them up: prosenchyma, in which the hyphae are loosely arranged and elongate hyphae can still be recognized, and pseudoparenchyma, in which the hyphae are tightly interwoven, the cells are nearly isodiametric and the section looks like the parenchyma of higher plants. Fungal "tissues" are only interwoven hyphae and not formed by cell division from meristematic cells, and they are not homologous with plant tissues ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)).

**Definition 10.4.1 (Sclerotium, Stroma and Rhizomorph)** A dormant body formed of tightly interwoven hyphae, with a dark, hard outer layer and rich food reserves inside, able to resist adverse conditions, is called a **sclerotium**; a cushion- or club-shaped structure made of hyphal tissue, on or in which fruiting bodies form, is called a **stroma**; and a cord-like structure of the higher fungi, made of tightly packed parallel hyphae, resembling a root in form and with a growing point at the tip, is called a *rhizomorph*.

- **Sclerotium**: ranging in size from a mouse dropping to larger than a human head; in some the outer layer is pseudoparenchyma and the interior prosenchyma, and in others there is no tissue differentiation. Sclerotia are strongly resistant to drought and to high and low temperatures, and germinate into mycelium or fruiting bodies when conditions are suitable. Examples: the ergot of *Claviceps* ([7.12.11](/7.12.11)), the black sclerotia of *Sclerotinia sclerotiorum* (sclerotinia stem rot of rape), and the medicinal poria (*Wolfiporia cocos*) and polyporus (*Polyporus umbellatus*).
- **Stroma**: a transitional form between the vegetative and reproductive phases, made of pseudoparenchyma and prosenchyma. The sclerotium of ergot germinates into stalked spherical stromata with perithecia embedded in the surface layer; the club-shaped part that grows from the head of the host larva in the caterpillar fungus is also a stroma ([7.12.11](/7.12.11)).
- **Rhizomorph**: the outer layer is darker and is a cortex made of pseudoparenchyma; the inner layer, made of prosenchyma, is the core (medulla). It can extend from one food source to another, up to several meters, stops growing when conditions are harsh and resumes when they are suitable. It is common in wood-decay fungi, the best known being *Armillaria* (honey fungus): its rhizomorphs spread under the bark and in the soil and cause root rot of many trees; one individual of *A. ostoyae* in Oregon, USA, covers about 9.6 km² and is estimated to be several thousand years old, one of the largest known individual organisms. The orchid *Gastrodia elata* has no chlorophyll and obtains nutrients by digesting the *Armillaria* rhizomorphs that invade its tuber, so in artificial cultivation of *Gastrodia* one must first prepare *Armillaria* culture logs ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)).

The hyphal tissue formed by higher fungi at reproduction, which produces sexual spores, is called the **fruiting body**: that of the ascomycetes is called an ascocarp and that of the basidiomycetes a basidiocarp ([7.12.11](/7.12.11)).

### 10.4.3. Specialized Hyphae

Hyphae also differentiate structures for anchorage, penetration and capture ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.2.2)):

**Definition 10.4.2 (Haustorium and Appressorium)** A specialized branch that a parasitic fungus (or oomycete) sends from an intercellular hypha into a living host cell, where it is surrounded by the invaginated host plasma membrane and absorbs nutrients, is called a *haustorium*; a swelling at the tip of a germ tube or hypha, pressed against the host surface and producing a penetration peg that pierces the epidermis, is called an *appressorium*.

Haustoria may be spherical, finger-like, branched and so on, and are characteristic of biotrophic pathogens such as rusts, powdery mildews and downy mildews: they pierce only the host cell wall and not the plasma membrane, so that the host cell remains alive and continually supplies nutrients to the pathogen. The appressorium concentrates turgor on a single fine penetration peg (the turgor of the appressorium of the rice blast fungus can reach about 8 MPa) and can pierce the cuticle directly. In addition, saprotrophic fungi often have rhizoids that extend into the substrate (as in *Rhizopus*), and some fungi form sticky nets, sticky knobs or constricting rings that capture nematodes (*Arthrobotrys*).

### 10.4.4. Modes of Nutrition

Fungi are heterotrophic in three ways:

- Saprotrophy: taking nutrients from the bodies and excreta of dead animals and plants and from nonliving organic matter. Fungi and bacteria are the main decomposers of the biosphere, and fungi are especially the mainstay of the decomposition of lignin and cellulose (white rot and brown rot, [7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.6.1)).
- Parasitism: taking nutrients from living animals and plants. Those that can only parasitize are called obligate parasites (rusts, powdery mildews, downy mildews), those that can only live saprotrophically are obligate saprotrophs; those that are mainly parasitic but can also live saprotrophically are facultative saprotrophs, and those that are mainly saprotrophic but can also parasitize are facultative parasites. There is no strict boundary between them: many fungi first parasitize living hosts and continue to live saprotrophically on them after the host dies. Plant pathology more often uses another division based on the way of feeding: biotrophs (feeding from living cells by means of haustoria), necrotrophs (killing the tissue with toxins and enzymes and then feeding on it) and hemibiotrophs (first biotrophic and then necrotrophic, such as the rice blast fungus and *Phytophthora*).
- Symbiosis: forming **mycorrhizae** with plant roots ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.6.2); for the anatomy of roots see [7.12.20](/7.12.20)), forming **lichens** with algae or cyanobacteria ([7.12.12](/7.12.12)), living as endophytes in the tissues of healthy plants, or being cultivated by leaf-cutting ants, termites and bark beetles.

## 10.5. Reproduction of Fungi

Fungal reproduction is usually of three kinds: vegetative reproduction, asexual reproduction and sexual reproduction. The form of the spores and of the structures bearing them is the main basis of fungal identification and classification (for diagrams of the various spores see Figure 3 of [7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.4.1)).

### 10.5.1. Vegetative Reproduction and Asexual Spores

A few unicellular fungi (fission yeasts, *Schizosaccharomyces*) produce daughter cells by cell division, and budding yeasts reproduce by budding; fragments of the hyphae of filamentous fungi can also grow into new individuals. Chinese textbooks place the following three kinds of spores, produced directly by vegetative hyphae, under vegetative reproduction:

- Blastospores (budding spores): formed by budding from a cell and growing into a new individual after separating from the parent (*Candida*).
- Chlamydospores: resting spores formed when individual cells in the middle or at the tip of a hypha swell, the protoplasm condenses and the wall thickens; after surviving adverse conditions they germinate into mycelium (*Mucor racemosus*). Chinese textbooks also write "thick-walled spore", which is not the same structure as the akinete of cyanobacteria in [7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.5); in this course the term chlamydospore is used.
- Arthrospores: formed by the breaking of a hypha at the septa (*Geotrichum candidum*).

Asexual reproduction produces special spores:

- **Zoospores**: wall-less, flagellate, motile spores formed in zoosporangia by aquatic fungi (chytrids, Blastocladiomycota) and spread by water; the zoospores of fungi have a single posterior whiplash flagellum.
- **Sporangiospores**: nonmotile spores formed in a sporangium by cleavage of the protoplasm and dispersed by air currents (*Mucor*, *Rhizopus*).
- **Conidia**: nonmotile spores produced outside by spore-forming cells at the tip or side of a conidiophore by budding or fragmentation and dispersed by air currents, rain or animals (*Penicillium*, *Aspergillus*); they are the most important asexual spores of the Ascomycota and extremely diverse in form, unicellular or multicellular.
- Ballistospores: spores formed on sterigmata and shot off at maturity by the sudden coalescence of surface drops of water (Buller's drop), as in *Sporobolomyces*. The basidiospores of basidiomycetes are discharged in the same way.

For the definitions of **sporangiospores** and **conidia** see [7.11.21](/7.11.21#def-21.4.1). Sporangiospores are "endogenous" (formed inside the sporangium), whereas conidia, blastospores, arthrospores and ballistospores are "exogenous"; the same distinction applies to sexual spores: ascospores are endogenous and basidiospores exogenous.

### 10.5.2. The Three Stages of Sexual Reproduction

Fungal sexual reproduction consists of three steps that may be separated in time:

**Definition 10.5.1 (Plasmogamy and Karyogamy)** The fusion of the protoplasm of two cells (gametes, gametangia or hyphal cells), bringing two haploid nuclei into the same cell, is called **plasmogamy**; the fusion of the two nuclei in the same cell into one diploid nucleus is called **karyogamy**. The diploid nucleus (zygote nucleus) produced by karyogamy undergoes meiosis immediately or after a resting period, restoring the haploid state.

In the lower fungi karyogamy follows plasmogamy immediately and the dikaryotic phase is very short; in the ascomycetes and basidiomycetes, a **dikaryon** phase in which two nuclei coexist without fusing ($n + n$, [Definition 21.4.2](/7.11.21#def-21.4.2)) lies between plasmogamy and karyogamy. In most fungi the diploid is only a zygote nucleus and not a vegetative body, so the life histories of fungi have only an alternation of nuclear phases and no alternation of generations (Blastocladiomycota are an exception, §10.7.2). The general course of the life history is: a spore germinates to form a germ tube and grows into a new mycelium, and in one growing season several generations of asexual spores and mycelia may be produced (the asexual stage); late in the growing season the sexual stage begins, and through plasmogamy, karyogamy and meiosis sexual spores are produced.

### 10.5.3. Modes of Plasmogamy

The structures that take part in plasmogamy are very diverse in fungi; according to Alexopoulos et al. (1996) there are five modes (Figure 5):

| Mode | Process | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Planogametic copulation | two motile gametes unite; according to their size, isogamy, anisogamy or oogamy | chytrids, Blastocladiomycota (anisogamy) |
| Gametangial contact | two gametangia touch, and the male nucleus passes through a fertilization tube or a small pore at the point of contact into the female gametangium; the male gametangium itself does not enter | antheridium and oogonium of oomycetes; antheridium and ascogonium of *Pyronema* ([7.12.11](/7.12.11)) |
| Gametangial copulation | the walls at the point of contact of two gametangia dissolve and all the protoplasm fuses into one cell | conjugation of *Rhizopus* and *Mucor*; fusion of two vegetative cells of yeast |
| Spermatization | small, nonmotile, uninucleate male cells (spermatia, microconidia) are carried by insects, wind or water to the receptive hyphae of the female organ and fuse with them | spermatia and receptive hyphae of rusts; microconidia of *Neurospora* |
| Somatogamy | no sexual organs are formed, and cells of two vegetative hyphae fuse directly | most basidiomycetes (primary hyphae fuse into dikaryotic secondary hyphae), many ascomycetes |

From planogametic copulation to somatogamy, the sexual organs become simpler until they disappear altogether; the general evolutionary trend is for sexual reproduction to become independent of water and for the dikaryotic phase to lengthen (§10.5.5).

B: gametangial contact (oomycetes: the antheridium delivers the male nucleus through a fertilization tube to the oosphere in the oogonium). C: gametangial copulation (*Rhizopus*: the walls at the contact of two gametangia dissolve and the protoplasm fuses to form the zygosporangium). ### 10.5.4. Homothallism and Heterothallism

As in the algae ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.4.2)), if two structures of the same mycelium (one grown from a single spore) can mate, the species is homothallic, and if only mycelia of different mating types can mate, it is heterothallic. *Rhizopus stolonifer* is heterothallic; the two mating types cannot be told apart morphologically and are written (+) and (−) strains. Ascomycetes mostly have a bipolar system with two mating types; mushroom-forming basidiomycetes often have two unlinked mating-type loci (tetrapolar), each with many alleles, so that almost any two individuals are compatible, whereas only one quarter of the basidiospores from the same fruiting body can mate with one another ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.4.2)). Homothallism can be further divided into primary homothallism (a single haploid nucleus can complete sexual reproduction by itself) and secondary homothallism (each spore receives two nuclei of different mating types, as in the button mushroom *Agaricus bisporus*, [7.12.11](/7.12.11)). Heterothallism ensures outcrossing and is similar to the **self-incompatibility** of plants.

### 10.5.5. Sexual Spores and the Evolution of the Dikaryotic Phase

Sexual reproduction results in sexual spores, which are the marks of the major groups of fungi:

- **Oospores**: oomycetes (§10.3).
- **Zygospores**: thick-walled resting spores formed by gametangial copulation ([Definition 21.4.3](/7.11.21#def-21.4.3)); Mucoromycota and Zoopagomycota.
- **Ascospores**: formed in the ascus after karyogamy and meiosis, usually 8, endogenous; Ascomycota ([7.12.11](/7.12.11)).
- **Basidiospores**: formed on the basidium after karyogamy and meiosis, usually 4, exogenous; Basidiomycota ([7.12.11](/7.12.11)).

In the evolution of fungi the dikaryotic phase between plasmogamy and karyogamy has lengthened step by step: in chytrids and Mucoromycota karyogamy follows plasmogamy immediately (or soon afterward in the resting spore); in ascomycetes the dikaryotic phase is confined to the ascogenous hyphae, and the vegetative hyphae are haploid; in basidiomycetes the dikaryotic mycelium is the main body of the life history and can survive for many years and produce basidiocarps. The dikaryotic phase amounts to "delayed karyogamy": a dikaryotic mycelium keeps the genetic diversity of both parents (complementing recessive mutations, like a diploid) without losing the independence of the haploid nuclei, and finally the nuclei fuse simultaneously in large numbers of basidia or asci, so that one plasmogamy yields thousands of meioses. The lengthening of the dikaryotic phase has also been accompanied by the simplification of the sexual organs (from gametangial contact to somatogamy) and by adaptation to life on land (motile gametes are no longer needed).

## 10.6. Classification Systems of the Fungi

### 10.6.1. Ainsworth's Five Subdivisions

Chinese textbooks use the Ainsworth (1973) system: the Eumycota are divided into 5 subdivisions. It is based on the type of sexual spore and the presence or absence of zoospores, and can be written as a key:

1. Zoospores present; spores of the sexual stage typically oospores …… Mastigomycotina
1. Zoospores absent
   2. Sexual stage present
      3. Sexual spores zygospores …… Zygomycotina
      3. Sexual spores ascospores or basidiospores
         4. Ascospores …… Ascomycotina
         4. Basidiospores …… Basidiomycotina
   2. Sexual stage lacking (unknown or absent) …… Deuteromycotina

| Subdivision | Vegetative body | Asexual reproduction | Sexual reproduction | Representatives in Chinese textbooks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mastigomycotina (about 1100 species, 4 classes) | unicellular or aseptate multinucleate hyphae | zoospores (uniflagellate or biflagellate) | oospores or resting spores; isogamy or anisogamy in the lower forms | *Saprolegnia*, downy mildews |
| Zygomycotina (about 610 species, 2 classes) | well-developed aseptate multinucleate hyphae | sporangiospores | gametangial copulation, zygospores | *Rhizopus*, *Mucor* |
| Ascomycotina | unicellular or septate hyphae | budding, conidia | asci and ascospores; most form ascocarps | yeasts, *Penicillium*, ergot |
| Basidiomycotina | septate hyphae; primary, secondary and tertiary mycelia | rare (conidia, budding) | basidia and basidiospores | rusts, smuts, mushrooms |
| Deuteromycotina | mostly septate hyphae | conidia | unknown | rice blast fungus, *Colletotrichum* |

The five subdivisions of this system are all defined by single characters: flagellate or not, which kind of sexual spore, whether the sexual stage is known. It is convenient in practice, but only the ascomycetes and basidiomycetes are natural groups:

- The Mastigomycotina is a **polyphyletic group**: oomycetes and hyphochytrids belong to the stramenopiles, plasmodiophorids to the Rhizaria, and chytrids and Blastocladiomycota are among the earliest branches of the fungi; "flagellate" is only a **plesiomorphy** retained from a common ancestor.
- The Zygomycotina is a **paraphyletic group**: it includes several branches, such as Mucorales, Entomophthorales and Zoopagales, that do not form a monophyletic group together, and the zygospore is a plesiomorphy shared by these lineages.
- The Deuteromycotina is not a taxon but a collection of fungi whose "sexual stage is unknown"; the great majority are the asexual stages of ascomycetes, and a few of basidiomycetes ([7.12.11](/7.12.11)).

### 10.6.2. The Current Phylum-Level System

The main differences between the fungal system reconstructed by molecular phylogenetics (rRNA, multigene and phylogenomic analyses) and the old system are that the Mastigomycota and Zygomycota are broken up, several parasitic groups have been added to the kingdom Fungi, and the ascomycetes and basidiomycetes form the subkingdom Dikarya. This course adopts the phylum-level framework of Spatafora et al. (2016) (Figure 1):

| Phylum (subphylum) | Main characters | Representatives | Position in the old system |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cryptomycota (also called Rozellomycota) | parasites inside the cells of chytrids, oomycetes and algae; zoospores flagellate, vegetative phase wall-less | *Rozella* | Mastigomycotina (Chytridiomycetes) |
| **Microsporidia** | obligate intracellular parasites of animals, entering host cells by a polar tube | *Nosema* (pébrine of the silkworm) | Protozoa |
| **Chytridiomycota** | unicellular or rhizoidal; zoospores with a single posterior whiplash flagellum | *Synchytrium*, *Batrachochytrium* | Mastigomycotina (Chytridiomycetes) |
| Neocallimastigomycota | anaerobic chytrids in the digestive tracts of herbivores, with hydrogenosomes | *Neocallimastix* | newly discovered |
| Blastocladiomycota | zoospores uniflagellate; some with isomorphic alternation of generations | *Allomyces*, *Physoderma*, *Coelomomyces* | Mastigomycotina (Chytridiomycetes) |
| Zoopagomycota: Entomophthoromycotina, Kickxellomycotina, Zoopagomycotina | aseptate hyphae or hyphae with regular septa; mostly parasites of animals and fungi | *Entomophthora* | Zygomycotina |
| Mucoromycota: Mucoromycotina, Mortierellomycotina, Glomeromycotina | aseptate multinucleate hyphae; sporangiospores, zygospores; Glomeromycotina form arbuscular mycorrhizae | *Rhizopus*, *Mucor*, *Mortierella*, *Rhizophagus* | Zygomycotina; Glomeromycotina formerly in the Endogonaceae |
| **Ascomycota** | septate hyphae or yeasts; ascospores | yeasts, *Penicillium*, morels | Ascomycotina; most Deuteromycotina |
| **Basidiomycota** | septate hyphae, dikaryotic mycelium, often with clamp connections; basidiospores | rusts, smuts, mushrooms | Basidiomycotina; a few Deuteromycotina |

The internal system of the fungi is still changing. Tedersoo et al. (2018) and the *Outline of Fungi* of Wijayawardene et al. (successive editions from 2020) raise many of the subphyla in the table to phyla (such as Glomeromycota, Mortierellomycota and Entomophthoromycota) and recognize about 20 phyla; the two treatments express basically the same phylogenetic relationships and differ only in rank. Whether Cryptomycota and Microsporidia should be placed in the kingdom Fungi is also disputed: their cell walls appear only in the resting spores and they have no typical hyphae; if they are excluded, the kingdom Fungi begins with the chytrids. About 150 000 species of fungi have been described, and environmental DNA surveys indicate that the real number of species may exceed 2 million, the great majority being ascomycetes and basidiomycetes (together more than 95 % of the described species, [7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.5.1)).

## 10.7. The Lower Fungi

### 10.7.1. Chytrids

The **chytrids** (phylum Chytridiomycota, [Definition 21.5.1](/7.11.21#def-21.5.1)) are fungi of water and soil whose vegetative body is a single cell (holocarpic, the whole cell becoming a sporangium at maturity) or a cell with branched rhizoids (eucarpic); they form no true hyphae. The zoospores and gametes have a single posterior whiplash flagellum, a character that fungi retain from their flagellate ancestors. Chytrids decompose pollen, chitin and plant remains in water and soil and parasitize algae, protists, other fungi and plants.

- *Synchytrium endobioticum* causes wart disease (black wart) of potato: cauliflower-like galls grow on the tubers, and the resting sporangia can survive in the soil for decades; it is a quarantine disease in many countries.
- *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis* (Bd) infects the keratinized skin of amphibians, disrupts their electrolyte balance and causes cardiac arrest; together with the related *B. salamandrivorans* it has spread around the world with the amphibian trade from East Asia. Scheele et al. (2019) estimated that at least 501 amphibian species have declined because of chytridiomycosis, about 90 of which may be extinct (the precision of this estimate is disputed); this is the greatest loss of biodiversity caused by a single pathogen on record ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.5.1)).
- *Olpidium brassicae* (now placed in its own phylum Olpidiomycota) parasitizes plant root cells, and its zoospores can transmit plant viruses.

### 10.7.2. Blastocladiomycota

*Allomyces* of the Blastocladiomycota lives in tropical soil and water and is one of the few groups of fungi with **alternation of generations**: in species such as *A. arbusculus* and *A. macrogynus* the haploid gametophyte and the diploid sporophyte look alike and cannot be told apart before the reproductive organs form, so that this is an **isomorphic alternation of generations**.

- The gametophyte ($n$) bears colorless female gametangia and orange male gametangia (the orange coming from γ-carotene) in pairs at the tips of the hyphae and releases uniflagellate gametes of unequal size (**anisogamy**): the male gamete is about half the size of the female and is attracted by the pheromone sirenin secreted by the female gamete.
- The female and male gametes unite into a zygote, which loses its flagellum and germinates into a diploid sporophyte.
- The sporophyte ($2n$) forms two kinds of sporangia: thin-walled, colorless mitosporangia, which release diploid zoospores that regenerate sporophytes, and thick-walled, red-brown resistant sporangia, in which meiosis takes place after a resting period and which release haploid zoospores that germinate into gametophytes.

The life cycle of *Allomyces* thus has both **sporic meiosis** and two morphologically identical multicellular generations, similar to that of the alga *Ulva* ([7.12.13](/7.12.13)), which shows that alternation of generations arose independently many times in different lineages. The Blastocladiomycota also include *Physoderma maydis*, which parasitizes maize leaves (brown spot of maize), and *Coelomomyces*, which uses mosquito larvae and copepods as its two hosts and has been studied for the biological control of mosquitoes.

### 10.7.3. Mucoromycota: *Rhizopus* and *Mucor*

The Mucoromycota (the order Mucorales of the Mucoromycotina is the main body of what Chinese textbooks call Zygomycotina) have well-developed aseptate multinucleate hyphae in which rapid cytoplasmic streaming is often visible; asexual reproduction produces sporangiospores in sporangia, and sexual reproduction is by the copulation of two gametangia, forming **zygospores**. Most are saprotrophs, a few parasitize plants, insects or small animals, and occasionally they cause serious infections of humans and livestock (mucormycosis). From the Mastigomycotina to the Zygomycotina the fungi evolve clearly from aquatic to terrestrial life: zoospores become nonmotile spores spread by air currents, and the union of motile gametes becomes the copulation of gametangia.

The commonest species of *Rhizopus* is *R. stolonifer* (also called black bread mold), which lives on bread, steamed buns and other starch-rich foods and causes them to rot, and is an important pathogen of stored fruits, vegetables and sweet potatoes (soft rot of sweet potato: hyphae enter through wounds and secrete pectinase that breaks down the cell walls, the flesh turns to mush, and white hairs and black sporangia cover the diseased part). Its life history is as follows (Figure 6):

1. A spore lands on the substrate and germinates with a germ tube, growing into a cottony mycelium. Most of the hyphae grow in the substrate and absorb nutrients; arched aerial hyphae, the stolons, spread over the surface of the substrate, and where they touch the substrate (at the nodes) they produce rhizoids that penetrate it.
2. Above the rhizoids one or several erect sporangiophores arise, each swelling at the tip into a sporangium. Much cytoplasm and many nuclei flow into the young sporangium and spread around it, leaving an empty central part separated from the periphery by a wall, which becomes the columella; the protoplasm around the columella cleaves into many multinucleate sporangiospores. The sporangial wall turns black at maturity, which makes this mold black; after the wall ruptures the spores are released and germinate into new mycelium. After the sporangium bursts the columella collapses at the top of the sporangiophore, shaped like an umbrella (the apophysis).
3. Sexual reproduction is less common and heterothallic: when (+) and (−) mycelia meet, under the induction of pheromones (trisporic acid and its precursors, each mating type synthesizing part of the precursors and exchanging them), each grows a side branch and the branches touch one another (progametangia); the tips swell and gametangia are cut off by cross walls, the part below being the suspensor.
4. The walls at the point of contact of the two gametangia dissolve and the protoplasm mixes (plasmogamy); the (+) and (−) nuclei fuse in pairs (karyogamy), forming a cell with many diploid nuclei, the zygospore (strictly, the thick-walled structure is the zygosporangium, and the zygospore within it contains many diploid nuclei). The zygospore is black, thick-walled, with warty projections, and rests for several months.
5. On germination meiosis takes place, the zygospore puts out a sporangiophore, and a sporangium (germ sporangium) forms at its tip, in which the haploid spores are of the two mating types (+) and (−); the spores are released and germinate into new mycelium.

The vegetative body of *Rhizopus* is therefore haploid, the diploid being only the nuclei in the zygospore, and the life history is of the haploid type (**zygotic meiosis**).

*Mucor* differs from *Rhizopus* mainly in having no stolons or rhizoids; the sporangiophores arise singly from the hyphae, branched or unbranched. *M. racemosus* also forms chlamydospores in the hyphae and can grow yeast-like in the absence of oxygen and at high $\ce{CO2}$ (an example of dimorphism, [7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.3.4)). *Rhizopus* and *Mucor* contain large amounts of amylase and can break starch down into glucose: this saccharification is the first step of Chinese wine-making, in which *R. oryzae* and others in the wine starter (xiaoqu) saccharify first and yeasts then ferment the sugar into alcohol; fermented bean curd (furu) is made with the proteases secreted by the hyphae of Mucor-type fungi such as *Actinomucor elegans*, and tempeh by fermenting soybeans with *R. microsporus* var. *oligosporus*. *Mortierella alpina* of the Mortierellomycotina is used in the industrial production of arachidonic acid.

*Pilobolus* is a mucoralean fungus living on herbivore dung that has a most ingenious way of dispersing its spores. The sporangiophore is 5–10 mm tall and grows toward light; the swollen part below the sporangium (the subsporangial vesicle) acts like a lens, focusing light on a photosensitive region at its base, and the sporangiophore bends toward the light source when illumination is uneven. The vacuole of the subsporangial vesicle has a very high solute concentration, so that turgor keeps increasing as it takes up water and finally the vesicle bursts, shooting the whole black sporangium in the direction of the light: with an initial velocity of about 10–20 m s⁻¹ and an acceleration of about 20 000 times that of gravity (high-speed photography of Yafetto et al. 2008), and a range of more than 2 m. The sporangium is thus shot away from the dung pile (animals do not feed beside the dung), lands on grass, is swallowed by a herbivore and passes out with fresh dung, beginning a new cycle.

### 10.7.4. Zoopagomycota: Entomophthoromycotina

The Entomophthoromycotina are pathogens of insects: *Entomophthora muscae* invades the body cavity of houseflies and other flies and, before the host dies, drives it to climb to a high place and spread its wings; after death the hyphae emerge from between the segments, form conidiophores and forcibly shoot conidia onto flies below ("summit disease"). Entomophthoromycosis of aphids and locusts often causes epidemic collapse of host populations in nature and has been studied for biological control. The Zoopagomycotina parasitize amoebae, nematodes and other fungi; the Kickxellomycotina include many species living in the guts of arthropods (part of the old Trichomycetes).

### 10.7.5. Glomeromycotina

The Glomeromycotina (treated as the phylum Glomeromycota in some systems) comprise about 300 described species, all of which form **arbuscular mycorrhiza** with plant roots: the hyphae extend between the cortical cells of the root and form finely branched arbuscules and storage vesicles in the cells, and extraradical hyphae extend into the soil to absorb phosphate and water (for the types and physiology of mycorrhizae see [7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.3.1), and for their anatomy in roots [7.12.20](/7.12.20)). About 70–80 % of terrestrial plant species form arbuscular mycorrhizae. The Glomeromycotina are obligate symbionts and cannot be cultured away from the host; they reproduce only by large multinucleate spores (40–800 µm in diameter, containing hundreds to thousands of nuclei) formed at the ends of hyphae, and sexual reproduction has not been seen. The representative species *Rhizophagus irregularis* (formerly *Glomus intraradices*) is a model for the study of arbuscular mycorrhizae and is also used as a biofertilizer.

Structures identical to modern arbuscules are already present in the cortical cells of the early land plant *Aglaophyton* in the Rhynie chert of about 407 million years ago (Remy et al. 1994), showing that arbuscular mycorrhizae are almost as old as land plants and very probably helped plants to colonize the land ([7.12.14](/7.12.14)).

## Summary

- The traditional "fungal plants" (the three phyla of bacteria, slime molds and fungi) are a polyphyletic group defined by heterotrophy and the absence of roots, stems and leaves: bacteria are prokaryotes, slime molds are Amoebozoa, oomycetes are stramenopiles and plasmodiophorids are Rhizaria; only the fungi (opisthokonts) form a monophyletic kingdom, and "fungi sensu lato" (Definition 10.1.1) means all these organisms studied by mycology.
- The vegetative body of plasmodial slime molds is a diploid plasmodium (Definition 10.2.1), and meiosis takes place at spore formation; oomycetes have a diploid-type life history and produce zoospores and oospores (Definition 10.3.1), and *Saprolegnia* shows diplanetism (Definition 10.3.2) and internal proliferation of sporangia.
- Fungi feed by absorption through hyphae and store glycogen; the hyphal tissues (sclerotia, stromata and rhizomorphs, Definition 10.4.1) are made of pseudoparenchyma and prosenchyma; parasitic fungi invade the host with haustoria and appressoria (Definition 10.4.2).
- Sexual reproduction has three steps, plasmogamy, karyogamy (Definition 10.5.1) and meiosis; plasmogamy takes five forms; the dikaryotic phase is confined to the ascogenous hyphae in ascomycetes and becomes the main body of the life history in basidiomycetes.
- In the current system (Spatafora et al. 2016) the Mastigomycotina (polyphyletic) and the Zygomycotina (paraphyletic) are broken up, and the Deuteromycotina is placed in the Ascomycota and Basidiomycota.

## Questions

1. Why is the resemblance of oomycetes to fungi said to be only the result of convergent evolution? List at least four features that distinguish oomycetes from fungi.

   **Answer** Oomycetes and fungi both grow by branched hyphae, feed by absorption and reproduce by spores; these are adaptations of two lineages to the same way of life (absorbing nutrients in a substrate). But oomycetes belong to the stramenopiles, close relatives of brown algae and diatoms: their cell wall is mainly cellulose and β-glucan rather than chitin; their vegetative body is diploid, with meiosis in the oogonium and antheridium (gametic meiosis), whereas that of fungi is haploid or dikaryotic; their zoospores have one hairy and one smooth flagellum, whereas fungal zoospores have a single posterior whiplash flagellum; their sexual reproduction is oogamous; their mitochondrial cristae are tubular; lysine is synthesized by the diaminopimelate pathway; and many species cannot synthesize sterols.

2. Compare the life histories of plasmodial slime molds, *Rhizopus* and *Saprolegnia*: what is the ploidy of the vegetative body in each, and when does meiosis occur?

   **Answer** The vegetative body of plasmodial slime molds (the plasmodium) is diploid, meiosis takes place at spore formation, and the haploid phase consists only of the spores and the myxamoebae or swarm cells that germinate from them. The vegetative body of *Rhizopus* is haploid, the diploid being only the nuclei in the zygospore, and meiosis occurs when the zygospore germinates (zygotic meiosis). The vegetative body of *Saprolegnia* is diploid, meiosis takes place in the oogonium and antheridium, and the haploid phase consists only of the oospheres and male nuclei (gametic meiosis). In terms of the time of meiosis they thus represent, respectively, a diploid-type life history with meiosis at spore formation, the haploid type with zygotic meiosis and the diploid type with gametic meiosis.

3. What is diplanetism? Why are the sporangia of *Saprolegnia* said to form by internal proliferation?

   **Answer** The zoosporangium of *Saprolegnia* releases pear-shaped primary zoospores with two apical flagella; after swimming, a zoospore comes to rest as a resting spore and then germinates into a kidney-shaped secondary zoospore with two lateral flagella, which comes to rest again and germinates into a hypha. That the same individual produces two kinds of zoospores in succession is called diplanetism. After the sporangium has emptied, the cross wall at its base grows upward and forms a new sporangium inside the empty shell of the old one; repeated three or four times, this gives sporangia one inside another, which is called internal proliferation.

4. From the modes of sexual reproduction, explain the lengthening of the dikaryotic phase in fungal evolution and its significance.

   **Answer** In chytrids and Mucoromycota karyogamy follows plasmogamy immediately (the nuclei in the zygospore fuse in pairs) and the dikaryotic phase is extremely short; in ascomycetes a dikaryotic ascogenous hypha forms after plasmogamy, the dikaryotic state being kept only in the ascogenous hyphae while the vegetative hyphae remain haploid; in basidiomycetes the somatogamy of two uninucleate hyphae forms a dikaryotic mycelium, which is the main body of the life history, can grow for many years and finally undergoes karyogamy in large numbers of basidia. The significance of the longer dikaryotic phase: after one plasmogamy the dikaryotic mycelium can grow for a long time and produce thousands of basidia, each undergoing one karyogamy and meiosis, which greatly increases the products of recombination; the two different nuclei complement each other's recessive mutations while keeping the independence of haploid nuclei; and at the same time the sexual organs are simplified to somatogamy, so that sexual reproduction no longer depends on water and motile gametes, an adaptation to life on land.

5. Of the five subdivisions of Ainsworth's system, which are monophyletic? Where have the others gone in the current system?

   **Answer** Only the Ascomycotina (Ascomycota) and Basidiomycotina (Basidiomycota) are monophyletic, and together they form the Dikarya. The Mastigomycotina is polyphyletic: oomycetes and hyphochytrids belong to the stramenopiles and plasmodiophorids to the Rhizaria, while the true fungi among them are divided among the Chytridiomycota and Blastocladiomycota (and the Cryptomycota, Neocallimastigomycota, Olpidiomycota and others). The Zygomycotina is paraphyletic and is divided into the Mucoromycota (Mucorales, Mortierella, Glomeromycotina) and the Zoopagomycota (Entomophthorales and others). The Deuteromycotina is a collection of fungi whose sexual stage is unknown and is assigned on DNA sequences to the Ascomycota (most) or the Basidiomycota; since 2013 a fungus has only one name, and no separate names are given to asexual stages.
