The previous chapter dealt with the individual cell; this chapter and the next describe how cells are organized into tissues. The body of a seed plant is built from only a few kinds of tissue: meristems, which retain the capacity to divide and continually produce new cells, and mature tissues, which differentiate from the derivative cells of meristems. This chapter treats the meristems and three of the simpler mature tissues: the dermal tissue that covers the surface (epidermis and periderm), and the parenchyma and mechanical tissues (collenchyma, sclerenchyma) that make up the "matrix" of the plant body. Conducting tissues, secretory structures and tissue systems follow in the next chapter. Almost all of the criteria for identifying these tissues are features of the cell wall: its thickness and the pattern of thickening, whether it is lignified or suberized, the type of pits, and whether the protoplast dies at maturity ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)). The chapters on root, stem and leaf ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)–[7.12.23](/7.12.23)) combine these tissues into organs; the molecular regulation of meristems and the mechanism of stomatal movement belong to plant physiology, and the general principles of stem cells and cell differentiation are in [7.3.24](/7.3.24).

## 4.1. Tissues and Their Classification

**Definition 4.1.1 (Tissue)** A group of cells of the same type or of different types that have the same origin in ontogeny (growing and differentiating from one meristematic cell or one group of meristematic cells) and are related in structure and function is called a **tissue**. A tissue composed of only one type of cell is a **simple tissue**, such as parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma; a tissue composed of several types of cells is a **complex tissue**, such as epidermis, periderm, xylem and phloem.

Each kind of organ contains certain tissues, each with a characteristic distribution and one main function, and the tissues depend on and cooperate with one another. For example, the main function of the leaf is photosynthesis: a large amount of assimilatory tissue photosynthesizes, dermal tissue covers it to prevent water loss and mechanical injury, and conducting tissue runs through it, supplying water and carrying away assimilates.

By ability to divide, plant tissues fall into two broad classes:

- Meristems: tissues that retain the ability to divide throughout life (Section 4.2).
- Mature tissues (also called permanent tissues): tissues formed when most of the cells derived from the meristems gradually lose the ability to divide and grow and differentiate further. Chinese textbooks classify them by function into protective tissue, parenchyma (ground tissue), mechanical tissue, conducting tissue and secretory structures.

"Mature" or "permanent" is relative. The cells of some mature tissues differ little from those of meristems, have general metabolic activity and can divide; others change greatly in form, are specialized in function and have lost the ability to divide entirely. A tissue that is not strongly differentiated can sometimes specialize further into another kind of tissue as the plant develops, or under certain conditions undergo **dedifferentiation** and become a meristem; for example, the cork cambium and the interfascicular cambium both arise from mature parenchyma cells regaining the ability to divide, and parenchyma cells at wounds can form callus and adventitious roots.

English-language plant anatomy textbooks usually organize this material not by function but by "simple tissues / complex tissues" and "tissue systems": parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma are the three simple tissues, xylem, phloem, epidermis and periderm are complex tissues, and these are further assigned to the dermal system, the vascular system and the ground system ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)). The two classifications correspond as follows: protective tissue = dermal system; conducting tissue = the main part of the vascular system; parenchyma and mechanical tissue = the main part of the ground system; secretory structures are scattered through all systems.

## 4.2. Meristems

### 4.2.1. The Concept of Meristem

**Definition 4.2.1 (Meristem)** In seed plants, cells capable of division are confined to certain regions of the plant body. The cells in these regions retain a strong, continuing ability to divide throughout the life of the plant: they keep adding new cells to the plant body and at the same time "perpetuate" themselves. Such a group of cells with a persistent capacity to divide is called a **meristem**.

The self-perpetuating cells of a meristem are called *initials*: of the two daughter cells produced by division of an initial, one stays in place and remains an initial, while the other becomes a derivative, which after several more divisions differentiates into mature tissue. Initials correspond to animal **stem cells**; in the shoot apex the region that contains them is called the stem-cell niche (Section 4.2.5). Unlike animals, plant meristems produce new organs (leaves, lateral branches, flowers, lateral roots) throughout life, so the form of the plant is built up continually through the whole life cycle; this "open growth" is the basic way in which sessile plants adapt to their environment.

The general features of meristematic cells are: small, isodiametric cells with thin walls, closely packed without intercellular spaces; a large nucleus in the center, dense cytoplasm and deep staining; small, scattered vacuoles (provacuoles), plastids at the proplastid stage, and usually no ergastic substances. Lateral meristems such as the cambium are exceptions, as described below.

### 4.2.2. Classification by Position

**Definition 4.2.2 (Apical, Lateral and Intercalary Meristems)** By position in the plant body, meristems fall into three classes (see the figure below): **apical meristems**, located at the tips of the main axes and lateral branches of shoot and root; **lateral meristems**, located around the sides of roots and stems and arranged parallel to the surface of the organ, namely the vascular cambium and the cork cambium; and **intercalary meristems**, sandwiched between tissues that are already more or less differentiated.

The division of the apical meristems lengthens roots and stems, forms leaves and lateral branches on the stem and increases the vegetative surface; the shoot apical meristem finally also changes into a flower or inflorescence and produces the reproductive organs.

The lateral meristems comprise the **vascular cambium** and the **cork cambium**. The activity of the vascular cambium thickens roots and stems, while the cork cambium forms new protective tissue (periderm) on the surface of thickening roots and stems or at wounds. Lateral meristems occur mainly in gymnosperms and woody dicots; in herbaceous dicots they are only weakly active or absent, and in monocots they are generally absent, so that roots and stems of these plants show no marked thickening growth (the special mode of thickening in monocots is covered in [7.12.22](/7.12.22)). The cells of lateral meristems differ markedly from those of apical meristems: most cambial cells are long and spindle-shaped, the protoplast is highly vacuolated, the cytoplasm is not dense, and division often shows a pronounced periodicity with the seasons (which forms the annual rings).

Intercalary meristems are parts of apical meristems that are retained locally in certain organs. Typical intercalary meristems occur in the stems and leaves of many monocots: cereal crops such as rice and wheat retain intercalary meristems at the base of the internodes, and after the apex has differentiated into a young panicle, activity of these meristems still produces jointing and heading, so that the stem grows rapidly taller; leaves of onion, garlic and Chinese chive continue to elongate after the upper part has been cut off, as a result of activity of the intercalary meristem at the leaf base; and a lodged grass stem can right itself through asymmetric growth of the intercalary meristem at the node. The intercalary meristem at the base of the gynophore of peanut elongates after flowering and pushes the ovary into the soil, where it develops. Compared with apical and lateral meristems, the cells of intercalary meristems remain active for a shorter time and after a period of division all turn into mature tissue; there are no initials in them.

### 4.2.3. Classification by Origin

By the nature of their origin, meristems fall into three classes:

- *Promeristem*: retained directly from the cells of the embryo, generally with a persistent and strong ability to divide, located at the very front of the root and shoot tips. The cells are small, nearly isodiametric polyhedra with thin walls, dense cytoplasm, a large nucleus, many very small vacuoles, close packing and deep staining.
- *Primary meristem*: composed of cells newly derived from the promeristem; the first differentiation has appeared in form, but the cells still divide strongly. It is a tissue that divides and differentiates at the same time and can also be regarded as the tissue transitional between meristem and mature tissue. Its activity produces the primary body.
- *Secondary meristem*: a meristem formed when cells of mature tissue undergo physiological and morphological changes, leave their former mature state (dedifferentiate) and are converted again; it generally refers to the vascular cambium (its interfascicular part) and the cork cambium. Its activity produces the secondary body.

**Definition 4.2.3 (Protoderm, Ground Meristem and Procambium)** The primary meristem of shoot and root tips differentiates into three kinds: the outermost layer is called the **protoderm**, whose cells are brick-shaped and mostly divide anticlinally and which develops into the epidermis; the part inside the protoderm occupying the largest area in cross section is the **ground meristem**, whose cells are polyhedral, with vacuoles gradually enlarging, stain more lightly and divide in all directions, and which develops into ground tissues such as cortex and pith; the bundle-like or column-like groups of long, slender, more deeply staining cells distributed in the ground meristem are the **procambium**, which divides only longitudinally and develops into the primary vascular tissue (in the stem, a ring of separate procambial strands; in the root, a solid central cylinder).

Plant anatomy divides the structure of the plant body into primary and secondary body. The result of activity of the primary meristems produced by the apical meristems is primary growth and the primary body; the result of activity of the lateral meristems (secondary meristems) is secondary growth and the secondary body. Herbaceous plants have only primary growth or only a little secondary growth throughout life, and the whole body of an annual herb or the aerial part of a perennial herb dies in winter; woody plants have secondary growth after primary growth is complete, produce secondary tissues year after year, and form large, hard plant bodies ([7.12.22](/7.12.22)).

The two classifications correspond as follows:

| By position \ By origin | Promeristem | Primary meristem | Secondary meristem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apical meristem (broad sense) | Initials at the very front of shoot and root apex and their first derivatives (apical meristem in the narrow sense) | Protoderm, ground meristem, procambium | — |
| Lateral meristem | — | Fascicular cambium (see below) | Interfascicular cambium, cork cambium (typical) |
| Intercalary meristem | — | Base of internodes, leaf base (parts of the apical meristem retained locally) | — |

Several meristems are hard to assign to any one of these classes, and are a favorite topic for questions:

- Fascicular cambium: it arises directly from the layer of cells that remains from the procambium when the primary vascular tissue differentiates, so by origin it belongs to the primary meristems, yet it produces the secondary body, and it is difficult to place in either the primary or the secondary class; the interfascicular cambium, by contrast, arises by dedifferentiation of parenchyma cells between the vascular bundles and is a typical secondary meristem.
- Primordia of lateral roots, adventitious roots and adventitious buds: lateral roots originate from the pericycle of the root ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)), and adventitious roots and buds originate from mature tissue, so by origin they should be secondary meristems; but they have the structure of an apical meristem and perform the function of producing the primary body.
- Thus the meanings of "primary" and "secondary" do not always agree at the two levels of origin and product, and the classification by position is more commonly used in practice.

### 4.2.4. Theories of Apical Organization

How the initials of an apical meristem are arranged, and what tissues the derivative cells of the different layers produce, were explained by four theories in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries (see the figure below):

1. Apical cell theory (Nägeli and others, mid-nineteenth century): at the very top of the apical meristem there is a single initial that retains the ability to divide, the apical cell; its activity produces the whole apical meristem and controls all growth of the plant. The apical cell is often tetrahedral (an inverted pyramid) and cuts off derivative cells in turn from its three sides. This theory applies to many spore plants, such as some large algae (the brown alga *Fucus*), bryophytes and most ferns (horsetails, most leptosporangiate ferns), but the root and shoot tips of seed plants have no single apical cell.
2. Histogen theory (Hanstein, 1868): the angiosperm shoot apex is composed of three histogens, the precursors of three tissue regions, called dermatogen, periblem and plerome, each with its own initials and producing epidermis, cortex and stele respectively (roughly corresponding to protoderm, ground meristem and procambium). This theory fits the root better: the initials of many root tips are indeed arranged in layers, and sometimes there is a separate calyptrogen that produces the root cap. In the shoot apex, however, the three histogens usually cannot be clearly distinguished, and even where they can, tissue origin does not fully fit the assumption: the plerome does not necessarily develop only into stele but may also produce pith, and the periblem may also produce part of the stele. The theory is therefore no longer applicable to the shoot apex.
3. The **tunica–corpus theory** (see Definition 4.2.4).
4. Cytohistological zonation (Foster, 1938): in the shoot apex of *Ginkgo*, the apical meristem can be divided by cell size, frequency of division and staining intensity into several zones: the surface group of apical initials; beneath it the central mother cell group (central zone), with larger, more vacuolated, more lightly staining cells that divide less and replenish the tissues below and around; beneath the central mother cells a transition zone, which at peak activity divides like a cambium; further below the pith meristem, which by transverse divisions forms longitudinal files of cells (rib meristem) and produces the pith; and the peripheral zone, deeply staining and actively dividing, in which local intensification of division forms leaf primordia and which thickens the stem by periclinal divisions and lengthens it by anticlinal divisions. The zones also differ in their DNA, RNA and protein composition.

**Definition 4.2.4 (Tunica–Corpus Theory)** The theory that the apical meristem of the angiosperm stem is composed of two parts, an outer **tunica** and an inner **corpus**, is called the **tunica–corpus theory** (Schmidt, 1924): the tunica consists of one to several layers of cells that divide only anticlinally, so that each layer remains one cell thick and the surface area increases; the corpus is the mass of cells enclosed by the tunica, which divides in all directions and increases volume. Tunica and corpus each have their own initials; the initials of the tunica lie in the center of the axis, and the initials of the corpus lie beneath those of the tunica.

The greatest difference between the tunica–corpus theory and the histogen theory is that it does not assume predetermined tissue regions in the apical meristem (the promeristem part). Apart from the outermost layer of the tunica, which always produces epidermis, what tissue the derivatives of the other layers will differentiate into is not determined in advance, which is also consistent with experimental results with periclinal chimeras (plants whose layers carry different genetic markers): in model plants the layers are often denoted L1 (producing the epidermis), L2 and L3. The theory applies chiefly to the stems of angiosperms: the tunica of dicots is mostly two layers (sometimes four or five), and that of monocots generally one or two. The shoot apex of most gymnosperms has no surface layer that divides steadily anticlinally, that is, no tunica; a tunica-like structure can be distinguished only in a few groups such as *Araucaria*, *Ephedra* and *Gnetum*, so the gymnosperm shoot apex is better described by cytohistological zonation.

Cytohistological zonation shows that the apical meristem should include not only the most distal promeristem but also the primary meristem derived from it, which is the "apical meristem in the broad sense". Modern literature on the angiosperm shoot apex usually combines the two points of view: the concept of layers (L1, L2, L3; tunica and corpus) describes the lineage of the cells, and the concept of zones (central zone, peripheral zone, rib zone) describes their functional state (panel A of the figure below). The organization of the root tip (closed and open types, the quiescent center) is discussed in [7.12.20](/7.12.20).

### 4.2.5. Maintenance of Stem Cells: WUS–CLV3 Feedback

How do the stem cells in the central zone of the shoot apex keep their number constant while continually giving off derivatives? Genetics in *Arabidopsis* gave an answer in the form of negative feedback (Mayer et al., 1998; Schoof et al., 2000; Brand et al., 2000). A small group of cells in L3 beneath the central zone, called the *organizing center*, expresses the transcription factor WUSCHEL (WUS); the WUS protein moves through plasmodesmata into the stem cells above, maintains their stem-cell state and activates the gene for the secreted small peptide CLAVATA3 (CLV3); CLV3 is perceived by receptor kinases such as CLV1 and represses expression of *WUS* in the organizing center. When there are too many stem cells, CLV3 increases and WUS decreases, and the stem cells decrease accordingly; the reverse also holds. Mutants confirm this circuit: the shoot apical meristem of *wus* mutants is exhausted very soon and growth terminates (German *wuschelig* means "tousled", describing the malformed appearance); the meristems of *clv* mutants keep enlarging, the number of floral organs increases and the fruits are club-shaped (Latin *clava* means "club").

In the simplest model, let $W$ be the WUS level in the organizing center and $C$ the level of CLV3 signal,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{a}{1 + (C/K)^n} - \delta_W W, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}t} = b\,W - \delta_C C,
\tag{4.1}
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are synthesis rates, $\delta_W$ and $\delta_C$ are degradation rates, and $K$ and $n$ describe the strength and steepness of repression of *WUS* by CLV3. The steady state of CLV3, $C^* = bW^*/\delta_C$, increases monotonically with $W$, while the synthesis of WUS decreases monotonically with $C$, so the two nullclines have only one intersection, the system has a unique stable steady state, and the size of the stem-cell population is "clamped" within a certain range; weakening the feedback (the *clv* mutant, corresponding to $b \to 0$) raises $W$ to $a/\delta_W$ and the meristem enlarges. This is a typical example of negative feedback maintaining homeostasis in development (the general idea of feedback is in [7.5.3](/7.5.3), network motifs in [7.9](/7.9)); the modulation of this circuit by hormones (cytokinin, auxin) and the positioning of leaf primordia belong to plant physiology.

## 4.3. Dermal Tissue

Dermal tissue is the tissue that covers the surface of the plant body and protects it; it reduces transpiration of water from the body, controls gas exchange between the plant and its environment, and guards against attack by pests and pathogens and against mechanical injury. It comprises the primary epidermis and the secondary periderm.

### 4.3.1. Epidermis and Cuticle

**Definition 4.3.1 (Epidermis)** The layer of cells on the surface of young roots and stems, leaves, flowers, fruits and so on is called the **epidermis**; it differentiates from the protoderm. It is the layer in direct contact between the plant body and the external environment, is generally only one cell thick, and is composed of several kinds of cells with different characters and functions: epidermal cells proper are the most basic component, and the guard cells and subsidiary cells of stomatal complexes and trichomes are scattered among them.

Epidermal cells are plate-like in various shapes; the margins of leaf epidermal cells are often wavy and interlock, and the cells are very tightly packed, with no intercellular spaces except at stomata. Epidermal cells are living cells that generally contain no chloroplasts (except the epidermal cells of aquatic and shade plants, and guard cells), but often contain leucoplasts and chromoplasts and store metabolic products such as starch grains, pigments, tannins and crystals: the inner epidermis of an onion scale is colorless and transparent, while the outer epidermis is purple because of anthocyanin in the vacuoles. The epidermis of a grass leaf consists of alternating long cells and pairs of short cells (silica cells and cork cells), and the upper epidermis of the leaf also has large bulliform cells ([7.12.23](/7.12.23)). The leaves of oleander and Indian rubber tree have a multilayered epidermis formed by periclinal divisions of the protoderm, called a multiple epidermis, in which the outer layer protects and the inner layers store water.

The outer tangential wall of epidermal cells of aerial parts such as stems and leaves is often thickened and cutinized, and a distinct cuticle is deposited on the surface of the wall.

**Definition 4.3.2 (Cuticle)** The lipid film consisting mainly of cutin and wax that covers the outer wall of epidermal cells of the aerial parts of plants is called the **cuticle**. The cuticle in the broad sense (also called cuticular membrane) consists of two layers: the outer layer, composed of cutin and wax, is the cuticle proper (the cuticle in the narrow sense); the inner layer, composed of cutin and cellulose, is the cuticular layer; between the cuticular layer and the primary wall there is a distinct pectin layer.

The word cuticle therefore has two usages: in the broad sense it means the whole cuticular membrane, commonly used in identifying plants and fossil plants and in studying the penetration of applied substances into the leaf; in the narrow sense it means only the outer layer. The cuticle makes the epidermis highly impermeable to water and effectively reduces transpiration; a hard cuticle can also prevent entry of pathogens and add mechanical support. The cuticle is thick in xerophytes, and the submerged leaves of aquatic plants have almost none (which is why the *Hydrilla* leaves used for observing cytoplasmic streaming can be mounted directly and show no cuticle). Some plants have a waxy "bloom", epicuticular wax, outside the cuticle, as in the stem of sugarcane, the fruits of grape and plum and the leaves of cabbage; it makes the surface hard to wet and prevents germination of pathogen spores on the surface. The wax microcrystals and papillae on the surface of the lotus leaf form a two-tier rough structure on which water droplets can hardly spread and which carries away dust as they roll off, called the "lotus effect" (Barthlott and Neinhuis, 1997). The structure of the epidermis and the sculpture of the cuticle are a basis for plant classification and also a factor that must be considered when breeding disease-resistant varieties and using pesticides and herbicides.

When some secretory structures secrete, they first discharge the secretion between the cuticle and the cell wall, so that the cuticle separates from the wall and bulges, and then release the secretion through rupture of the cuticle or through a pore (glandular hairs, [7.12.5](/7.12.5)).

How long the epidermis persists on the plant body depends on whether the organ undergoes thickening growth: the roots and stems of gymnosperms and most dicots thicken markedly, so the epidermis is destroyed and shed as the organ thickens and is replaced by a periderm produced internally; on organs with little or no secondary growth (leaves, fruits, the roots and stems of most monocots) the epidermis can persist for a long time.

### 4.3.2. The Stomatal Complex

**Definition 4.3.3 (Stomata, Guard Cells and Subsidiary Cells)** The structure in the aerial epidermis composed of two specialized **guard cells** and the pore between them is called a **stoma**, the gateway for gases entering and leaving the plant (sometimes only the pore is called the stoma). In many plants, one side or the surroundings of the guard cells also carry two to several cells that differ from ordinary epidermal cells in size, shape or arrangement, called **subsidiary cells**; the subsidiary cells together with the stoma they surround are called the **stomatal complex**.

Guard cells are kidney-shaped in dicots and dumbbell-shaped in the Poaceae and Cyperaceae. They contain chloroplasts, have dense cytoplasm and abundant organelles such as endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria; their walls are unevenly thickened: in kidney-shaped guard cells the wall on the side next to the pore is thicker and that on the side away from the pore thinner, and the cellulose microfibrils in the wall are arranged radially around the pore. When guard cells take up water and swell, the thin outer wall stretches more, and since the radial microfibrils restrict lateral widening, the two guard cells bend outward and the pore opens; when they lose water the pore closes. The ion transport and signal regulation that drive uptake and loss of water by guard cells belong to plant physiology. Below the stoma there is a larger intercellular space in the mesophyll, called the substomatal chamber, which connects with the system of intercellular spaces in the leaf. Between mature guard cells and the surrounding cells there are no plasmodesmata ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)), so substances can enter and leave only across membranes.

The distribution of stomata varies among plants. In most dicots stomata are mainly on the lower epidermis of the leaf; floating leaves (water lily) have them only on the upper epidermis; many monocots (grasses) have them on both surfaces; submerged leaves usually have none. There are tens to hundreds per square millimeter of leaf surface.

Stomatal complexes of dicots are generally divided into four basic types according to the number and arrangement of subsidiary cells (see the figure below):

| Type | Alternative name | Features | Representatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anomocytic (ranunculaceous type) | Irregular-celled | No subsidiary cells; the guard cells are irregularly surrounded by several cells of the same shape and size as ordinary epidermal cells | Ranunculaceae (*Ranunculus*), Cucurbitaceae (*Citrullus*), Malvaceae |
| Anisocytic (cruciferous type) | Unequal-celled | Three subsidiary cells of unequal size surround the guard cells, one of them markedly smaller | Brassicaceae (*Brassica*), Crassulaceae (*Sedum*), Solanaceae |
| Paracytic (rubiaceous type) | Parallel-celled | Each guard cell is accompanied laterally by one or several subsidiary cells whose long axis is parallel to the pore | Rubiaceae (*Rubia*), Fabaceae (*Phaseolus*), Magnoliaceae |
| Diacytic (caryophyllaceous type) | Cross-celled | Each stoma is surrounded by two subsidiary cells whose common wall is at right angles to the long axis of the guard cells | Caryophyllaceae (*Dianthus*), Acanthaceae (*Justicia*), Lamiaceae |

There are also the tetracytic type with four subsidiary cells (found in monocots such as the Commelinaceae and Arecaceae) and types in which the subsidiary cells are arranged radially or in a ring. The stomata of monocots and gymnosperms mostly do not fit these four types: the stomatal complex of grasses consists of two dumbbell-shaped guard cells and two rhomboid or dome-shaped subsidiary cells, arranged in longitudinal rows in the leaf epidermis; the guard cells are swollen at both ends and have thick walls in the middle, and when the swollen ends take up water they push the middle apart and the pore opens; the stomata of pine needles are sunken, the guard cells have partly lignified walls and lie beneath the subsidiary cells, and the vestibule above them is plugged with wax (leaf structure in [7.12.23](/7.12.23)).

A: anomocytic (ranunculaceous) type, with no subsidiary cells, the stoma surrounded by ordinary epidermal cells; B: anisocytic (cruciferous) type, three subsidiary cells, one markedly smaller; C: paracytic (rubiaceous) type, one subsidiary cell on each side with long axis parallel to the pore; D: diacytic (caryophyllaceous) type, two subsidiary cells whose common wall is at right angles to the guard cells; E: grass stoma: dumbbell-shaped guard cells with one subsidiary cell on each side, in a file of long and short cells; F: cross section of the sunken stoma of a pine needle: guard cells with partly lignified walls lie beneath thick-walled subsidiary cells, the vestibule above is plugged with wax, and the substomatal chamber below opens into the mesophyll](./plots/stomatal-types.py)

When a stoma forms, the stomatal initial in the protoderm first divides unequally, producing a large cell and a small cell: the large cell develops into an epidermal cell, while the small cell, with dense cytoplasm and a large nucleus, develops into the guard mother cell, and some small cells divide a few more times before becoming guard mother cells. The guard mother cell divides once along its long axis to produce two guard cells; as the guard cells mature, the middle lamella between them dissolves locally, the cells separate locally and the pore forms. According to the relation of the subsidiary cells to the guard cells in origin, development of stomatal complexes is divided into three types (Pant, 1965):

| Developmental type | Origin of the subsidiary cells (or adjacent epidermal cells) |
|---|---|
| Mesogenous | Derived from the same stomatal initial as the guard cells, that is, of common origin with them |
| Perigenous | Derived from other protodermal cells around the guard mother cell, not of common origin with the guard cells |
| Mesoperigenous | At least one of common origin with the guard cells and the rest not (dual origin) |

Stomatal complexes of the same form but different origin cannot be regarded as homologous, so development must be considered when stomatal type is used to infer relationships. Research in *Arabidopsis* has revealed the genetic program of the stomatal lineage: the transcription factor SPEECHLESS initiates the unequal division, MUTE converts the meristemoid into a guard mother cell, and FAMA controls the last symmetric division and differentiation of the guard cells; secreted peptides such as EPF1 and EPF2 act through receptors to inhibit the formation of stomata in neighboring cells, so that two stomata are separated by at least one cell (the "one-cell-spacing rule"), which is lateral inhibition in the same sense as discussed in [7.3.24](/7.3.24).

Are the stomata of bryophytes and those of vascular plants homologous? Among bryophytes, the sporophytes of mosses and hornworts have stomata, while liverworts have none. It was long thought that bryophyte stomata are not homologous to those of vascular plants and result from convergent evolution. Recent evidence favors homology: in the moss *Physcomitrium patens* the SMF genes controlling stomatal development are homologous to SPEECHLESS, MUTE and FAMA, and the EPF–ERECTA signaling system is shared as well (Chater et al., 2016); phylogenomics supports the monophyly of bryophytes, and stomata should already have been present in the common ancestor of embryophytes and later lost in liverworts and some mosses (Harris et al., 2020). This question is taken up again in the chapter on bryophytes ([7.12.15](/7.12.15)).

### 4.3.3. Trichomes

**Definition 4.3.4 (Trichomes)** Unicellular or multicellular appendages formed by outgrowth of epidermal cells are called **trichomes** (also called hairs); those with a secretory function are glandular hairs and those without are nonglandular hairs.

The form of trichomes is extremely diverse (unicellular hairs, multicellular uniseriate hairs, branched hairs, stellate hairs, peltate scales, vesicular hairs and so on) and is a character often used in classification. Nonglandular hairs protect and reduce water loss: white trichomes whose lumen is filled with air after death reflect strong light and lower leaf temperature; dense hairs slow air movement over the leaf surface and reduce transpiration; some hairs can pierce or entangle insect larvae and hinder their feeding and egg laying. The stinging hairs of *Urtica* are unicellular hairs with a silicified tip that readily breaks into a slanted needle, and the swollen basal cell injects irritants such as histamine and acetylcholine into the skin. The "fibers" of cotton are trichomes of the seed coat (the floss of kapok consists of trichomes of the inner fruit wall): cotton fiber is a unicellular hair formed by elongation of an epidermal cell of the outer integument of the ovule after fertilization, several centimeters long, with a secondary wall of almost pure cellulose, and is not a fiber in the anatomical sense. Glandular hairs and other secretory structures are covered in the next chapter.

The epidermis of the maturation zone of the root tip is mainly concerned with absorption of water and mineral salts and is therefore also an absorbing tissue; its cell walls are thin and the cuticle is thin, and the outer wall of many cells extends outward into a long tubular protrusion, the **root hair**. A root hair is only part of an epidermal cell and does not form a separate cell, so it is generally not counted as a trichome. Outgrowths in which tissues beneath the epidermis also take part are not trichomes but emergences, such as the prickles on rose stems ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)).

### 4.3.4. Periderm

**Definition 4.3.5 (Periderm and Cork Cambium)** The secondary protective tissue that replaces the epidermis is called the **periderm**, found on the surface of roots and stems that undergo thickening growth. It is formed by a lateral meristem, the **cork cambium** (phellogen): the cork cambium divides periclinally to form radial files of cells, which differentiate outward into the **phellem** (cork) and inward into the **phelloderm**; phellem, cork cambium and phelloderm together constitute the periderm.

The cork cambium is a typical secondary meristem, formed by dedifferentiation of mature living cells, and usually begins at the same time as activity of the vascular cambium. In the stem it mostly arises from the layer of cortical cells immediately beneath the epidermis, in *Salix*, oleander, apple and others from the epidermis itself, and in *Vitis* and others from deeper cortex or even the primary phloem; in the root the first cork cambium arises from the pericycle ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)).

The phellem has many cell layers; in cross section the cells are rectangular and tightly packed into regular radial files without intercellular spaces; the walls are rather thick and strongly suberized, and at maturity the protoplast dies and disintegrates and the lumen is usually filled with air (see the figure of periderm and lenticel in Section 4.3.5). These features make cork highly impermeable to water and gases, and give it resistance to compression, thermal and electrical insulation, light weight, elasticity and resistance to organic solvents and many chemicals; it protects the plant body effectively and has considerable commercial importance, being used for bottle stoppers, lightweight insulating material and life-saving equipment. Commercial cork comes mainly from the cork oak (*Quercus suber*) of Europe; in China it comes mainly from Chinese cork oak (*Q. variabilis*) and Amur cork tree (*Phellodendron amurense*). The "cells" that Hooke saw in 1665 were the cork cells of bottle cork ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)). Cork cells may be thin-walled or thick-walled, and in some plants (such as white birch) the two kinds alternate in concentric layers. The color of cork cell walls (brown, yellow or colorless) has nothing to do with suberization itself but is due to tannin infiltrating the wall or to colored resin or tannin filling the lumen.

The phelloderm consists of thin-walled living cells, often only one cell layer thick, generally produced by the periclinal division that forms the cork cambium; it lies in the same regular radial file as the cork cells outside it, and generally can be distinguished from cortical parenchyma only on this basis. The phelloderm often stores starch, and some contains chloroplasts and can photosynthesize; some plants have no phelloderm, while in some gymnosperms and tropical trees the phelloderm has many layers.

When the periderm is wounded, a wound periderm forms at the wound and repairs it: first the walls of the parenchyma cells beneath the dead cells at the wound become lignified and suberized, temporarily preventing evaporation of water from the wound and microbial infection; then the cells beneath regain the ability to divide and form a wound cork cambium, which produces normal phellem and phelloderm outward and inward and joins the existing periderm, so that the periderm becomes a complete protective layer again. The "wound cork" formed on the cut surface of potato tuber pieces is also a wound periderm, and is called curing in agriculture.

The cork cambium of woody plants is generally short-lived, and the first periderm cannot accommodate the thickening of the interior of the stem and is finally ruptured, so longitudinal cracks appear in the stem; cortical cells beneath the cracks regain the ability to divide and produce a new cork cambium and new periderm, which joins the old periderm on both sides of the crack. This process is repeated, the cork cambium arises ever deeper, and finally it can arise in the parenchyma of the secondary phloem. The tissues outside the new periderm die because they are cut off from water and nutrients and, together with the many layers of cork, constitute the rhytidome; the **bark** of botany is the collective term for all tissues outside the vascular cambium (see [7.12.22](/7.12.22)).

In a few monocots (the Arecaceae, Zingiberaceae, Bromeliaceae and groups with secondary growth such as *Dracaena* and *Cordyline*) the stem does not form a typical periderm; instead, cortical cells divide periclinally in succession, producing radial files of suberized cells that die at maturity and become cork cells, called storied cork, which also serves as secondary protection.

### 4.3.5. Lenticels

The phellem of the periderm is impermeable to gases, and the living cells inside depend on aerating structures in the periderm for gas exchange with the outside.

**Definition 4.3.6 (Lenticel)** At certain restricted places in the periderm the cork cambium is more active than elsewhere and produces outward a tissue of loosely arranged cells with well-developed intercellular spaces, called *complementary tissue*; as the complementary tissue increases it ruptures the epidermis and phellem and forms an aerating structure in the periderm, the **lenticel**.

The cork cambium at a lenticel (lenticel phellogen) often lies deeper and curves inward (see the figure below). The cells of the complementary tissue are spherical and thin-walled, often suberized or not; in some plants (such as cherry and birch) the lenticel phellogen alternately produces several layers of loosely arranged complementary cells with unsuberized walls and one to several layers of closely packed, suberized closing layer, and the cells of the closing layer still have intercellular spaces between them, so that the lenticel allows aeration yet prevents excessive water loss. Lenticels appear on twigs as small protuberances of various shapes, and their color, shape and arrangement are characters for identifying trees in winter: the transversely elongated linear lenticels on the trunk of white birch and the transverse lenticels of peach and cherry are conspicuous. In old bark the lenticels lie at the bottom of the bark fissures and are hard to observe. The small spots on the surface of apple and pear fruits are also called lenticels, but they are formed from stomata on the epidermis of the young fruit and the suberized cells beneath them, and differ in origin from the lenticels of twigs produced by the periderm.

## 4.4. Ground Tissue

### 4.4.1. Parenchyma

**Definition 4.4.1 (Parenchyma)** The tissue composed of cells with thin walls (primary wall only), rather large vacuoles, intercellular spaces and a living protoplast, which is the most widely distributed tissue in the plant body and carries out various metabolic activities, is called **parenchyma**, also called ground tissue.

Parenchyma is distributed in the cortex and pith of roots and stems, the mesophyll, the parts of the flower, and the fruits and seeds, and other tissues are often embedded in it; it is the main component of the ground tissue system. Its cells are often isodiametric polyhedra (the typical tetrakaidecahedron, [7.12.3](/7.12.3)) and can photosynthesize, respire, store and synthesize many metabolites. Parenchyma is not strongly differentiated, is relatively close to meristem, has latent ability to divide and is highly plastic: in wound healing (callus), in the formation of adventitious roots, adventitious buds and callus in cuttings and grafts, in the origin of secondary meristems (interfascicular cambium, cork cambium) and in the expression of totipotency in vitro, it is parenchyma cells that act; it also often develops further into more highly specialized tissue.

By main physiological function, parenchyma is divided into the following kinds:

| Type | Features | Distribution and examples |
|---|---|---|
| Assimilatory tissue (chlorenchyma) | Cells with many chloroplasts that photosynthesize | All green parts: mesophyll, cortex of young stems, developing fruits and seeds |
| Storage tissue | Stores starch, protein, lipids, sugars and so on | Tuberous roots, tubers, corms, bulbs, fruits, endosperm and cotyledons of seeds, cortex and pith of roots and stems; large starch grains and a few protein bodies in the cells of potato tuber, oil droplets, starch grains and protein bodies in peanut cotyledon, oil droplets in coconut endosperm |
| Water-storage tissue | Large cells whose vacuoles contain much viscous sap and can store water | Photosynthetic organs of xerophytic succulents, such as the stems of cacti, the leaves of *Aloe* and *Agave*, the leaves of *Salsola*, the hypodermis of begonia leaves |
| Aerating tissue (*aerenchyma*) | Many intercellular spaces forming air cavities or air channels | Roots, stems and leaves of aquatic and wetland plants (see below) |
| Absorbing tissue | Thin walls, large surface area | Epidermis and root hairs of the maturation zone of the root tip |
| *Transfer cells* | Wall ingrowths enlarging the plasma membrane area (see below) | Sites of intensive short-distance transport of solutes |

The **aerenchyma** of aquatic and wetland plants runs through root, stem and leaf, so that oxygen produced by photosynthesis in the leaves or obtained from the air can diffuse to roots immersed in oxygen-poor mud and water; it also increases buoyancy and provides support with little material. Its air cavities can form schizogenously (cells separate from each other) or lysigenously (some cells undergo programmed death, as in the roots of rice and flooded maize), see [7.12.3](/7.12.3); the large channels in the rhizome (lotus root) and petiole of the lotus are the most familiar examples.

**Transfer cells** (named by Gunning and Pate, 1969) are a kind of specialized parenchyma cells whose walls grow many finger-like or reticulate ingrowths, with the plasma membrane lining them enlarged accordingly to more than ten times the area of an ordinary cell, which greatly enhances short-distance transmembrane transport of solutes; the wall is still a primary wall, plasmodesmata are well developed, the nucleus varies in shape, and organelles such as mitochondria, Golgi apparatus and rough endoplasmic reticulum are abundant. Transfer cells occur where solutes require intensive short-distance transport: the phloem and xylem parenchyma in the minor veins of leaves, the vascular tissue at the nodes of stems or inflorescence axes, secretory structures (nectaries, hydathodes), cotyledons, the outer layer of the endosperm, the suspensor and the synergids, and some companion cells also develop into transfer cells.

### 4.4.2. Collenchyma

Mechanical tissue is the tissue that mainly supports the plant body; it resists compression, tension and bending, and its common feature is that the cell wall is thickened locally or throughout and sometimes lignified. Mechanical tissue falls into two kinds, collenchyma and sclerenchyma.

**Definition 4.4.2 (Collenchyma)** The supporting tissue composed of living cells with unevenly thickened walls of primary nature, the thickening mostly at the corners where several cells meet, is called **collenchyma**.

Collenchyma cells are elongated in longitudinal section, with ends that are truncate, oblique or pointed. Besides cellulose, the wall contains much pectin and hemicellulose and no lignin; pectin is strongly hydrophilic and the wall holds much water, so fresh material shows a characteristic pearly luster under the light microscope, which disappears when the material is dehydrated in making permanent slides. By the location of thickening, collenchyma falls into four types (top row of the figure of mechanical tissues below):

| Type | Location of thickening | Appearance in cross section | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Angular collenchyma | Corners where cells meet | The thickened parts of adjacent cells merge into triangles or polygons; a thick band in longitudinal section | Stems and petioles of herbaceous dicots (most common) |
| Lacunar collenchyma | Walls around intercellular spaces | Polygons with thick margins surrounding an intercellular space | Stems and petioles of some Asteraceae and Malvaceae |
| Lamellar (plate) collenchyma | Inner and outer tangential walls | Thickened into parallel plates | Stems of *Sambucus*, *Rhamnus* and *Rheum* |
| Annular collenchyma | The whole wall thickened uniformly | Cell lumen nearly circular | Stems of some Lamiaceae and Apiaceae, petioles of some Magnoliaceae and Araliaceae |

Collenchyma resembles parenchyma in many respects: the walls are primary, the cells are living, often contain chloroplasts and have the potential to divide, and in some plants take part in forming the cork cambium; collenchyma can be regarded as a specialized parenchyma. Its wall is plastic and tough and extends as the organ grows, so it both supports and does not hinder elongation of young organs, and it is the main supporting tissue of growing stems, petioles, leaf veins and flower stalks. Collenchyma is mostly located at the periphery of the organ, forming a cylinder or separate strands beneath the epidermis; in ridged stems and petioles it is especially well developed in the ridges (the ribs of celery petioles, the four corners of the square stems of motherwort and other Lamiaceae); roots generally have no collenchyma, and it is rare in monocots. Once sclerenchyma has developed in an organ, the supporting role of collenchyma becomes secondary; where there is no sclerenchyma it is the main supporting tissue, and may even develop lignified secondary walls and change into sclerenchyma.

### 4.4.3. Sclerenchyma

**Definition 4.4.3 (Sclerenchyma, Sclereids and Fibers)** The mechanical tissue composed of cells with uniformly thickened, often lignified secondary walls, whose protoplasts usually die at maturity, is called **sclerenchyma**. Those of its cells that are mostly isodiametric or slightly elongated and of various shapes are called **sclereids**, and those that are long and slender with sharp ends are called **fibers**.

Sclereids have very thick walls whose secondary wall is strongly lignified and bears many round simple pits; because the wall is so thick, the pits become tubular pit canals, which often merge into branched pit canals as the wall thickens; the lumen is very small, and the cell is usually dead at maturity (bottom row of the figure below). Sclereids are widely distributed, often scattered singly or embedded in clusters in parenchyma, and can also form layers, increasing the hardness and supporting capacity of the organ. By form they are commonly divided into:

- Brachysclereids (stone cells): nearly isodiametric; the hard granules visible to the naked eye in the flesh of pear are clusters of sclereids, and single sclereids are visible under the microscope after crushing; the endocarp ("stone") of walnut and peach consists of sclereids.
- Macrosclereids: columnar, arranged in a palisade layer, as in the seed coat of legumes such as bean.
- Osteosclereids: bone-shaped, with swollen ends, as in the hypodermal layer of the pea seed coat and the mesophyll of some plants.
- Astrosclereids: branched and star-shaped, as in the petiole and leaf of water lily and the petiole of camellia.
- Trichosclereids: long and slender, sometimes branched, as in the leaf of olive.

Fiber cells are tapered at both ends and spindle-shaped; the wall is distinctly thickened by secondary wall, to a varying degree of lignification, and pits are few and often slit-like; they are generally dead at maturity, but a few retain the protoplast and also function in storage (such as the septate fibers of grape and others). Fibers are often arranged overlapping with their tips interlocking and aggregate in bundles, making the organ hard yet elastic and able to resist compression and bending; they take no part in longitudinal transport. Fibers are widely distributed:

- Xylary fibers: in the xylem, generally with walls thicker and more strongly lignified than the tracheids of the same plant, hard and rather brittle. By pits they are divided into libriform fibers (simple pits) and fiber-tracheids (with smaller bordered pits), both derived from tracheids ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)).
- Extraxylary fibers: mainly phloem fibers (also called bast fibers), but also cortical fibers, pericyclic fibers and the fibers of the bundle sheath in monocots. The walls of phloem fibers are less lignified or not lignified, and the tissue is tough and elastic with strong resistance to bending.

The fibers of many plants are long, rich in cellulose and soft, and are important textile raw materials:

| Class | Source | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Soft fibers | Phloem fibers of dicot stems | Ramie, flax (long and not lignified; used for clothing and tents); hemp; jute, kenaf, velvetleaf (shorter, somewhat lignified; used for sacks and ropes) |
| Hard fibers | Fibers around the vascular bundles in monocot leaves | Sisal (*Agave sisalana*), abaca (Manila hemp, *Musa textilis*), used for cables |
| Seed and fruit trichomes | Trichomes of the seed coat or inner fruit wall (not fibers) | Cotton (seed coat), red silk-cotton tree and kapok (inner fruit wall) |

## Summary

Entries:

- Tissue (Definition 4.1.1): simple tissues (parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma) and complex tissues (epidermis, periderm, xylem, phloem); meristems and mature tissues; "mature" is relative, and dedifferentiation is possible.
- Meristem (Definition 4.2.1): initials perpetuate themselves; by position apical, lateral and intercalary (Definition 4.2.2), by origin promeristem, primary (protoderm, ground meristem and procambium, Definition 4.2.3) and secondary; the fascicular cambium and the primordia of lateral and adventitious roots are hard to classify.
- Intercalary meristem: short-lived, turns entirely into mature tissue, has no initials; jointing and heading of cereals, regrowth of onion and chive after cutting, the peanut gynophore entering the soil.
- Four theories of apical organization: apical cell theory (spore plants), histogen theory (fits the root better), tunica–corpus theory (Definition 4.2.4; angiosperm stem; dicot tunica mostly two layers, monocots one or two, gymnosperms mostly no tunica), cytohistological zonation (*Ginkgo*; central, peripheral and rib zones); WUS–CLV3 negative feedback maintains the number of stem cells, Eq. (4.1).
- Epidermis (Definition 4.3.1): one layer of living cells, no intercellular spaces, generally no chloroplasts; the two senses of cuticle (Definition 4.3.2); epicuticular wax; multiple epidermis.
- Stomata (Definition 4.3.3): kidney-shaped or dumbbell-shaped guard cells, unevenly thickened walls, radial microfibrils; the four dicot types (anomocytic, anisocytic, paracytic, diacytic); dumbbell-shaped in grasses, sunken in *Pinus*; the three developmental types mesogenous, perigenous and mesoperigenous; recent evidence favors homology of bryophyte and vascular-plant stomata.
- Trichomes (Definition 4.3.4): glandular and nonglandular hairs; cotton fiber is a seed-coat trichome; root hairs are not counted as trichomes; prickles are emergences.
- Periderm (Definition 4.3.5) = phellem + cork cambium + phelloderm; wound periderm; the cork cambium arises progressively deeper, forming the rhytidome; storied cork in monocots; lenticels (Definition 4.3.6) with complementary tissue and closing layers.
- Parenchyma (Definition 4.4.1), six kinds (assimilatory, storage, water-storage, aerating, absorbing, transfer cells); collenchyma (Definition 4.4.2), four types; sclerenchyma (Definition 4.4.3): five types of sclereid, xylary fibers (libriform fibers, fiber-tracheids) and phloem fibers.

## Questions

1. How can a twig with an epidermis be distinguished from one with a periderm by appearance and in sections? Why must a twig with a periderm have lenticels?

   **Answer** A twig with an epidermis is generally green (the epidermis is transparent and the cortex beneath contains chloroplasts), with a smooth surface or trichomes, and has stomata but no lenticels; in sections the outermost layer is one layer of tightly packed living cells with a cuticle outside. A twig with a periderm is brown or gray, with point-like or line-like protuberant lenticels on the surface; in sections the outermost part is several layers of flat dead cells in radial files (cork), inside which lie a layer of thin-walled cork cambium and the phelloderm. Cork cells are suberized and impermeable to gases, while the cortex, phloem, cambium and sapwood inside the periderm are all living cells that need oxygen, so the periderm must have lenticels, formed by loosely arranged complementary tissue with well-developed intercellular spaces, for gas exchange.

2. After the upper part of the leaves of onion or chive is cut off, the leaves can continue to elongate; cereal crops can still undergo jointing after panicle differentiation. Which meristem is involved? How does it differ from apical and lateral meristems?

   **Answer** The intercalary meristem: the leaf bases of onion and chive and the bases of grass internodes retain intercalary meristems, and after the apex has stopped growing (cut off or changed into the panicle), division and cell elongation at the base can still lengthen the leaf or internode. The intercalary meristem is a part of the apical meristem retained locally in the organ and by origin belongs to the primary meristems; compared with the apical meristem it has no self-perpetuating initials and is active for a shorter time, and after a period of division all its cells turn into mature tissue; compared with the lateral meristems, its activity lengthens rather than thickens the organ and produces no secondary body.

3. Collenchyma and sclerenchyma are both mechanical tissues. Why do growing stems and petioles depend mainly on collenchyma for support, and parts that have stopped growing mainly on sclerenchyma? Explain in terms of their walls.

   **Answer** Collenchyma cells are alive and the wall is a primary wall, rich in pectin and hemicellulose, not lignified, plastic and tough, and extends as the organ elongates, so it supports without hindering growth. Sclerenchyma has uniformly thickened, often lignified secondary walls that are hard and almost inextensible, and the protoplasts are mostly dead and can no longer follow the elongation of the organ; it can develop only after the organ has stopped elongating, and then provides stronger support. Thus in young stems collenchyma comes first, and after maturity sclerenchyma (fibers, sclereids) becomes the main supporting tissue; even collenchyma itself may form lignified secondary walls and change into sclerenchyma.

4. Use Eq. (4.1) to explain how the shoot apical meristem changes if the CLV3 signal fails, and if WUS fails. Is this consistent with the phenotypes of the mutants?

   **Answer** Failure of CLV3 corresponds to $b \to 0$ (or failure of the CLV1 receptor, corresponding to $K \to \infty$), so WUS synthesis is no longer repressed and $W$ rises to its upper limit $a/\delta_W$; WUS maintains stem cells, so the stem-cell population enlarges and the meristem keeps growing, consistent with the *clv* mutant's enlarged meristem, increased number of floral organs and club-shaped fruits. When WUS fails, $W = 0$, the stem cells cannot be maintained and CLV3 is no longer expressed ($C \to 0$); the meristem is exhausted after producing a few organs, consistent with the premature termination of the shoot apex in *wus* mutants. The significance of negative feedback is that whether a perturbation increases or decreases the number of stem cells, the circuit pulls it back to the steady state, so that the meristem keeps roughly the same size over decades or even thousands of years (in long-lived trees).

5. The stomatal complex of a dicot has two subsidiary cells whose common wall is perpendicular to the long axis of the guard cells; the stomatal complex of another plant also has two subsidiary cells, but they lie outside the two guard cells with their long axes parallel to the pore. Which types are they? Can the relationship of the two plants be judged from this alone?

   **Answer** The former is diacytic (caryophyllaceous type) and the latter paracytic (rubiaceous type). Relationship cannot be judged from the form of the mature stomatal complex alone: complexes of the same form can have different modes of development (mesogenous, perigenous, mesoperigenous), and complexes of different origin cannot be regarded as homologous; moreover, several types of stomatal complex are often found within one family or even one plant. Stomatal type is only one line of taxonomic evidence and has to be judged together with development, other morphological characters and molecular phylogeny ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)).
