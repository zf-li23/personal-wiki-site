The magnoliids are among the earliest-diverging lineages of Mesangiospermae. They comprise four orders, Magnoliales, Laurales, Piperales and Canellales, with about 10,000 species, including many important timber trees (camphor tree, nanmu), spices (pepper, cinnamon, nutmeg, bay laurel), fruit trees (custard apple, avocado) and ornamentals (Magnolia, Michelia, tulip tree, wintersweet). In Cronquist's system Magnoliales were regarded as the most primitive order of angiosperms, and the subclass Magnoliidae was the "basal complex of flowering plants" ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.4.4)); APG divides this complex into three parts: the earliest-diverging ANA grade ([7.12.34](/7.12.34)), the magnoliids of this chapter, and Ranunculales and Nelumbonaceae among the basal eudicots ([7.12.40](/7.12.40)). The magnoliids retain many ancestral characters (woody habit, numerous free floral parts, monosulcate pollen, small embryo with abundant endosperm), but they also have derived characters of their own. This chapter begins with the circumscription and synapomorphies of the magnoliids (§35.1), then treats Magnoliales (§35.2), Laurales (§35.3), Piperales (§35.4) and Canellales (§35.5), and finally Chloranthales, whose position is unresolved (§35.6). For the notation of floral formulas see [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.6).

## 35.1. Circumscription and Synapomorphies of the Magnoliids

**Definition 35.1.1 (Magnoliids)** The monophyletic group formed in the APG system by Canellales, Piperales, Magnoliales and Laurales is called the **magnoliids**, called Magnoliidae in phylogenetic nomenclature. Together with Chloranthales, the monocots, Ceratophyllales and the eudicots, it belongs to Mesangiospermae.

Note that the magnoliids differ from Cronquist's subclass Magnoliidae, which also included the families of the ANA grade, Ranunculales, Papaverales and Nelumbonaceae and was paraphyletic; the magnoliids are a monophyletic group circumscribed by molecular evidence.

The synapomorphies of the magnoliids are mainly anatomical and chemical:

- Parenchyma generally contains oil cells with volatile oils, giving the plant a characteristic aroma (this character also occurs in Chloranthales and may be a shared ancestral character; in the ANA grade only Austrobaileyales have oil cells);
- They often contain benzylisoquinoline alkaloids, aporphine alkaloids and special lignans, neolignans and phenylpropanoids (such as asarone);
- In most families the sieve-element plastids are of the P type ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.9));
- Except in Piperales, the phloem is stratified and the phloem rays are wedge-shaped.

The magnoliids fall into two pairs of sister orders: Magnoliales + Laurales, and Canellales + Piperales (Fig. 35.1.1). This result was unexpected on morphological grounds: Winteraceae were traditionally considered close to Magnoliaceae (Cronquist placed them in Magnoliales), and Piperales, together with Aristolochiales and Chloranthaceae, were regarded as "paleoherbs". The fossil record of magnoliids begins in the Early Cretaceous: *Archaeanthus* from the late Albian of North America has a Magnoliaceae-like elongated receptacle with numerous free carpels, and flower fossils of Lauraceae (such as *Perseanthus* from the Turonian of New Jersey) and Annonaceae are known from the Late Cretaceous.

## 35.2. Magnoliales

### 35.2.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Magnoliales are woody plants, often with oil cells; leaves alternate, often distichous (Annonaceae, Myristicaceae) or spiral (Magnoliaceae); phloem stratified; flowers bisexual or unisexual, the floral parts often trimerous; seeds with abundant endosperm, ruminate in Annonaceae, Myristicaceae and others (the inner layer of the seed coat irregularly infolded into the endosperm). Magnoliales in APG IV include 6 families: Myristicaceae (sister to all the others), Magnoliaceae, Degeneriaceae, Himantandraceae, Eupomatiaceae and Annonaceae.

Changes in circumscription: Magnoliales in the Chinese textbook "include Magnoliaceae, Annonaceae, Myristicaceae and 10 families in all", that is, Cronquist's Magnoliales, of which Winteraceae and Canellaceae belong to Canellales (§35.5), Austrobaileyaceae to the ANA grade ([7.12.34](/7.12.34#sec-34.4.6)), and Lactoridaceae are merged into Aristolochiaceae (§35.4.4). Engler's Magnoliales were larger still, also including Lauraceae, Calycanthaceae, Illiciaceae and Schisandraceae.

### 35.2.2. Magnoliaceae

Diagnostic characters of Magnoliaceae:

- Woody; bark, leaves and flowers aromatic (oil cells).
- Leaves simple, alternate, entire, rarely shallowly lobed (*Liriodendron*); stipules large, enclosing the young bud and early deciduous, leaving annular stipular scars at the nodes. This is the most reliable character for recognizing Magnoliaceae when not in flower.
- Flowers large and showy, solitary at the end of a branch or in a leaf axil, bisexual (rarely unisexual), radially symmetric; receptacle elongated into a column.
- Perianth not differentiated into sepals and petals, tepals 6–15 (or more), in whorls of three arranged in several whorls, often petaloid; sometimes the outer whorl is smaller and sepal-like (as in *M. liliiflora* and *Liriodendron*).
- Stamens numerous, free, spirally arranged on the lower part of the columnar receptacle; filaments short, anthers long, 2-locular, longitudinally dehiscent; pollen monosulcate, large and bilaterally symmetric, with a thin exine.
- Carpels numerous, free, spirally arranged on the upper part of the receptacle, ovary superior, 2 ovules per carpel (4 or more in *Manglietia*); the gynoecium is sometimes stalked (gynophore, *Michelia*).
- Fruit an aggregate of follicles, the follicles dehiscing along the dorsal suture and clustered into a cone-like head; *Liriodendron* has an aggregate of samaras (indehiscent).
- Seeds large, the outer seed coat red and fleshy (often wrongly called an aril), hanging from the opened follicle on a fine thread, which is the unrolled secondary wall of the spiral vessels in the funiculus; embryo small, endosperm abundant and oily.
- Chromosome number $x = 19$.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{6\text{–}15} A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{\infty:1:2}$. By species: *M. denudata* and *M. grandiflora* $\ast P_{3+3+3} A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{\infty:1:2}$; *M. liliiflora* $\ast K_3 C_{3+3} A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{\infty:1:2}$; *Liriodendron chinense* $\ast K_3 C_{3+3} A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{\infty:1:2}$.

Magnoliaceae are distributed mainly in the tropics and subtropics of southeastern Asia, with a few in southeastern North America to South America; southern and southwestern China is a center of distribution. The number of genera in the family varies greatly with taxonomic treatment. Molecular phylogenies show that Magnoliaceae fall into two branches, Liriodendroideae (only *Liriodendron*) and Magnolioideae, and that the traditional genera *Michelia*, *Manglietia*, *Parakmeria* and others are nested within a broadly defined *Magnolia*, so that *Magnolia* in the narrow sense is paraphyletic. Two treatments therefore exist: one merges all of Magnolioideae into a single broadly defined *Magnolia* (*Magnolia* s.l., divided into several subgenera and sections), giving only 2 genera and about 300 species for the family; the other splits it into a dozen or so small genera, as in the English edition of Flora of China (2008), which recognizes 13 genera and 112 species for China. Chinese textbooks write "2 (17) genera, 300 species; China 2 (13) genera, 112 species", giving both treatments at once. The question "Magnolia or Yulania?" arises from the same source: *M. denudata*, the yulan, is *Yulania denudata* in the narrow treatment and *Magnolia denudata* in the broad treatment, two names for the same species ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.4.5)). This course follows the broadly defined *Magnolia* in the text and gives the Flora of China genus names in parentheses.

Magnoliaceae flowers are mostly beetle-pollinated and protogynous: when the flower opens on the first day the stigmas are receptive but the anthers have not dehisced, the tepals close at night and trap beetles in the flower, and on the second day the anthers shed pollen and beetles carrying pollen fly to newly opened flowers. The large flower, thick tepals and carpels enclosed in a compact gynoecium are all related to the feeding and activity of beetles in the flower.

Classification and representative plants:

1. *Magnolia* (in the narrow sense): flowers terminal, no gynophore, 2 ovules per carpel, follicles dehiscing along the dorsal suture. Southern magnolia (*M. grandiflora*) is an evergreen tree with thick leathery leaves rusty-tomentose beneath and white flowers 15–20 cm across with 9 tepals; native to southeastern North America and widely cultivated south of the Yangtze in China. Houpu magnolia (*M. officinalis*, *Houpoea officinalis* in Flora of China) is a deciduous tree with large leaves clustered at the branch tips, endemic to China in the Yangtze valley and South China; the bark (houpu), flowers and fruits are used medicinally and contain magnolol and honokiol; subsp. *biloba* has a notch at the leaf apex dividing it into two lobes.
2. *Magnolia* subg. *Yulania* (*Yulania*): deciduous, flowers terminal and opening before the leaves. Yulan (*M. denudata*, *Y. denudata*) has white, fragrant flowers with 9 tepals of nearly equal size; it is native to central China, cultivated everywhere, and is the city flower of Shanghai. Lily magnolia (*M. liliiflora*, *Y. liliiflora*, "xinyi") is a deciduous shrub with 3 outer tepals sepal-like and lanceolate and 6 inner tepals purple-red outside, native to Hubei and cultivated everywhere. Saucer magnolia (*M.* × *soulangeana*) is the hybrid of yulan and lily magnolia, with tepals pale purple-red outside and white inside. *M. biondii* (*Y. biondii*) occurs in central China and the south of North China, and its flower buds are one of the main sources of the traditional medicine "xinyi".
3. *Michelia* (section *Michelia* of the broadly defined *Magnolia*): flowers axillary, the tepals not fully spreading when open; gynoecium stalked, the stalk elongating in fruit; 2 or more ovules per carpel. Banana shrub (*M. figo*) is an evergreen shrub with brown hairs on young twigs, buds and petioles and creamy-yellow flowers with a banana-like scent, native to South China. White champaca (*M.* × *alba*) is a tree with lanceolate leaves and white flowers with long narrow tepals, extremely fragrant, native to Indonesia and cultivated in South China; the flowers and leaves yield aromatic oil. Champaca (*M. champaca*) has orange-yellow flowers.
4. *Manglietia* (section *Manglietia* of the broadly defined *Magnolia*): evergreen, flowers terminal, 4 or more ovules per carpel. *M. fordiana* has long elliptic-lanceolate leaves and red-brown petioles and occurs from Zhejiang and Anhui southward. China also has some critically endangered Magnoliaceae, such as *Pachylarnax sinica* (formerly *Manglietiastrum sinicum*) of southeastern Yunnan, with only a few dozen wild individuals.
5. *Liriodendron*: leaves long-petioled, with 1–2 lobes on each side and a truncate apex, shaped like a Chinese mandarin jacket; flowers solitary at the branch tip, cup-shaped, sepals 3, petals 6; anthers extrorse; fruit an aggregate of samaras, each indehiscent with 1–2 seeds. Chinese tulip tree (*L. chinense*) has gray twigs, a broad lobe on each side of the middle of the leaf, and tepals yellow-green inside; it occurs south of the Yangtze, is often planted as a street tree, and the bark is medicinal. American tulip tree (*L. tulipifera*) has brown twigs, leaves often with 2 lobes on each side, and an orange spot at the base of the tepals; it occurs in eastern North America and is an important timber tree there. Their hybrid is fast-growing and adaptable and widely planted in China.

*Liriodendron* is a classic example of the East Asian–North American disjunction (Fig. 35.2.2). From the Late Cretaceous to the Tertiary, leaf and fruit fossils of *Liriodendron* occurred throughout the Northern Hemisphere, in North America, Europe, Greenland and East Asia; with global cooling in the late Tertiary and repeated Quaternary glaciations, European species went extinct because east–west mountain ranges such as the Alps blocked southward migration, while the species of East Asia and eastern North America survived in north–south mountain ranges, producing today's **disjunct distribution** with one species on each continent ([7.10.30](/7.10.30#sec-30.2.2)). Molecular-clock estimates place the divergence of the two species in the Miocene, consistent with this history. Many groups, such as Magnoliaceae, *Illicium*, *Sassafras* and *Nelumbo*, show similar East Asian–North American disjunctions.

Key characters of the family: woody, with stipular scars. Leaves simple, alternate. Flowers solitary, perianth trimerous and often undifferentiated, bisexual, radially symmetric; stamens and carpels numerous, free, spirally arranged on an elongated columnar receptacle, ovary superior. Fruit an aggregate of follicles. Seeds with abundant endosperm.

Economic importance: yulan, lily magnolia, southern magnolia, banana shrub, white champaca and the tulip trees are important ornamental trees; houpu and xinyi (flower buds of *M. biondii* and others) are medicinal; white champaca and champaca yield perfume materials; *Manglietia*, *Liriodendron* and others are good timber trees.

Changes in circumscription: Bentham and Hooker's Magnoliaceae also included *Illicium* and *Schisandra* and others, which were later separated as Illiciaceae and Schisandraceae (Engler, Cronquist); APG merges them into a broadly defined Schisandraceae in Austrobaileyales ([7.12.34](/7.12.34#sec-34.4.7)). So the Chinese textbook's phrase "excluding Schisandraceae in the narrow sense and Illiciaceae" refers to the earlier broadly defined Magnoliaceae.

### 35.2.3. Annonaceae

Annonaceae (custard apple family) are the largest family of Magnoliales, with about 110 genera and 2400 species, widespread in the tropics and most numerous in tropical Asia and South America; China has more than 20 genera and over 100 species, in the southwest to southeast.

Diagnostic characters: trees, shrubs or woody lianas with oil cells; leaves simple, alternate, distichous, entire, without stipules. Flowers bisexual, rarely unisexual, radially symmetric, solitary or clustered; sepals 3; petals 6 in two whorls, often thick and fleshy; stamens numerous, spirally arranged and densely packed into a ball, the connective expanded at the tip into a truncate or peltate shape; carpels numerous (rarely few), free, spirally arranged on a raised receptacle, with 1 to many ovules per carpel. The fruit is an aggregate of berries, the monocarps often stalked (*Polyalthia*, *Artabotrys*); in *Annona* the carpels fuse with the receptacle into a single fleshy aggregate fruit. Seeds with ruminate endosperm.

Floral formula: $\ast K_3 C_{3+3} A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{\infty}$.

Annonaceae are now divided into 4 subfamilies (Chatrou et al. 2012). Representative plants: sugar apple (*Annona squamosa*), native to tropical America, has an aggregate fruit with scale-like carpel scars on the surface and sweet white flesh, and is cultivated in South China; the related soursop (*A. muricata*) and cherimoya (*A. cherimola*) are also tropical fruits. Climbing ylang-ylang (*Artabotrys hexapetalus*) is a climbing shrub whose flower stalks are bent into hooks for climbing, with extremely fragrant flowers from which perfume is extracted. The flowers of ylang-ylang (*Cananga odorata*) yield ylang-ylang oil by distillation, a valuable perfume material.

### 35.2.4. Myristicaceae

Myristicaceae (nutmeg family) are sister to all the other families of Magnoliales, with about 20 genera and 500 species in the tropics; China has 3 genera and a dozen or so species in Yunnan, Guangxi and Hainan.

Diagnostic characters: evergreen trees with red sap flowing from wounded bark; leaves simple, alternate, distichous, entire, with translucent glandular dots. Flowers unisexual, dioecious, small, in inflorescences; perianth 3-lobed ($P_{(3)}$), no petals; stamens 2–40 with filaments fused into a column and anthers longitudinally dehiscent; the female flower has only 1 carpel, ovary superior, 1-locular, with 1 basal ovule. Fruit fleshy, dehiscing by 2 valves at maturity; seed with a red, lacerate aril and ruminate endosperm.

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{(3)} A_{(2\text{–}40)}$; ♀ $\ast P_{(3)} \underline{G}_{1:1:1}$.

Nutmeg (*Myristica fragrans*) is native to the Maluku Islands of Indonesia; the seed (nutmeg) and aril (mace) are important spices and in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries were among the most expensive commodities in the spice trade. Nutmeg contains myristicin, and eating it in quantity causes poisoning.

### 35.2.5. Other Families of Magnoliales

- Degeneriaceae: only two species of *Degeneria*, from Fiji. Each flower has a single carpel, which does not close completely along the ventral suture at flowering, so that the stigmatic surfaces on the two sides spread outward; the stamens are broad, flat and laminar, with no distinction between filament and anther. Bailey and Smith (1942) took it as a model of the "primitive carpel" and "foliar stamen", holding that the carpel is a single folded megasporophyll. It is now known that the carpel of the ancestor of extant angiosperms was ascidiate ([7.12.34](/7.12.34#sec-34.3.3)), and the folded carpel and laminar stamens of *Degeneria* are derived within Magnoliales.
- Eupomatiaceae: *Eupomatia* occurs in eastern Australia and New Guinea; the flower has no perianth, and the flower bud has a cap-like cover formed by extension of the receptacle apex, which falls at anthesis; the inner stamens become staminodes whose secretions are food for weevils, a classic case of beetle pollination.
- Himantandraceae: only *Galbulimima*, from New Guinea to northeastern Australia, with laminar, petaloid stamens and bark containing several alkaloids.

## 35.3. Laurales

### 35.3.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

The best synapomorphy of Laurales is the perigynous flower: the receptacle is hollowed into a cup or urn (floral cup) and the tepals and stamens are inserted on the rim of the floral cup ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.2.7)). Other characters are unilacunar nodes; leaves mostly opposite (alternate in Lauraceae); staminodes often present within the stamens; anthers mostly valvate (opening by 2 or 4 flaps lifting upward), a pair of glands often at the base of the filaments; pollen without apertures, with a thin exine and often small spines; carpels often one, with 1 ovule per carpel. Calycanthaceae lack the latter characters (anthers longitudinally dehiscent, carpels many, 2 ovules per carpel); they are sister to all the other families of Laurales.

Laurales in APG IV include 7 families: Calycanthaceae, Siparunaceae, Atherospermataceae, Gomortegaceae, Hernandiaceae, Monimiaceae and Lauraceae, the last three forming one clade. Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Laurales also included Amborellaceae and Trimeniaceae (now in the ANA grade, [7.12.34](/7.12.34)); he considered Laurales a more advanced group derived from Magnoliales, as shown by definite numbers of floral parts, whorled arrangement, trimery, distinct filaments, fused carpels, few ovules and pollen with no or two pores; but the unilacunar nodes of Laurales may be more primitive than the multilacunar nodes of Magnoliales. The traditional Monimiaceae are polyphyletic, and Atherospermataceae and Siparunaceae have been separated from them. Engler's system had no Laurales; Lauraceae and Calycanthaceae were both in Magnoliales.

### 35.3.2. Lauraceae

Diagnostic characters of Lauraceae:

- Evergreen or deciduous trees or shrubs; only *Cassytha* is a leafless parasitic twining herb; all parts of the plant have oil cells containing volatile oils and are fragrant when crushed.
- Leaves simple, alternate, rarely opposite or whorled, leathery, entire, rarely 3-lobed (*Sassafras*); venation triplinerved, three-veined or pinnate, the underside often glaucous (grayish white); no stipules.
- Flowers small, bisexual or unisexual (dioecious), radially symmetric, trimerous, in panicles, racemes, umbels or heads, the umbels often with an involucre (*Litsea*, *Lindera*).
- Tepals 6 in two whorls, alike, fused at the base into a short floral tube that enlarges and persists in fruit, or falls.
- Stamens 3–12, usually 12 in 4 whorls of 3, the 4th (innermost) whorl reduced to staminodes; anthers of whorls 1 and 2 introrse, of whorl 3 extrorse, with a pair of glands at the base of the filament; anthers 4-locular or 2-locular, valvate (each locule opening by a flap lifting upward).
- Pollen subglobose, without apertures, the exine thin and the surface often with small spines.
- Gynoecium of 1 carpel (Chinese textbooks often write 3 fused carpels on the basis of the 3-lobed stigma), ovary superior, 1-locular, with 1 pendulous anatropous ovule; style 1, stigma discoid or 2–3-lobed.
- Fruit a drupe or berry-like, often seated on a cup- or disc-shaped cupule developed from the floral tube (or receptacle); seeds without endosperm, cotyledons thick.
- Chromosome number $x = 7, 12$.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3+3+3} \underline{G}_{1:1:1}$ (the 4th whorl of $A$ is staminodes; Chinese textbooks write $\underline{G}_{(3:1:1)}$).

Lauraceae comprise about 50 genera and 2500–3000 species, mainly in the tropics and subtropics, most numerous in Southeast Asia and South America; China has more than 20 genera and over 400 species (the Chinese textbook gives 52 genera and over 2850 species worldwide, with about 20 genera and 423 species in China), mostly south of the Yangtze, where they are dominant trees of the evergreen broad-leaved forest of southern China, and many are excellent timber, oil and medicinal plants. Classification and representative plants:

1. *Cinnamomum*: evergreen; leaves alternate or subopposite, often triplinerved or three-veined, with domatia in the vein axils; inflorescence a panicle; fertile stamens in 3 whorls (9), anthers 4-locular, those of whorls 1 and 2 introrse, whorl 3 extrorse with glands at the base, whorl 4 staminodes; in fruit the tepals fall and the floral tube becomes the cupule. Camphor tree (*C. camphora*) has triplinerved leaves with raised domatia in the vein axils and occurs south of the Yangtze, a common street tree and ancient tree; camphor is extracted from the wood and roots and camphor oil from twigs, leaves and fruits for industry, medicine and ore flotation; the dense, fragrant, insect-repellent wood is used for chests and carving. Chinese cassia (*C. cassia*) has large, subopposite leaves with three strong basal veins and occurs in South China; the bark is "cassia bark", and the bark and cassia oil are used in medicine and as seasoning; the bark of Ceylon cinnamon (*C. verum*) is the cinnamon used in the West. In recent years some authors have separated the camphor tree and its relatives on molecular grounds as a distinct genus *Camphora*, with the cassia group remaining in *Cinnamomum*; this course follows the broad *Cinnamomum* of Flora of China.
2. *Lindera*: flowers unisexual, dioecious, umbels with 4 involucral bracts; tepals 6–9; stamens 9, anthers 2-locular, introrse, the stamens of whorl 3 with glands; leaves pinnately veined, three-veined or triplinerved. *L. glauca* has elliptic leaves grayish green beneath that wither but do not fall in winter, occurs mainly south of the Yangtze, the wood is usable and the fruits and leaves yield aromatic oil. *L. aggregata* (wuyao) is an evergreen shrub with ovate, acuminate leaves with three strong basal veins, grayish beneath, occurring south of the Yangtze, with medicinal roots.
3. *Litsea*: flowers unisexual, dioecious, in umbels or cymose umbels with an involucre; tepals 6-lobed; anthers 4-locular, introrse; leaves mostly pinnately veined. May chang (*L. cubeba*) has membranous leaves grayish beneath and black when dry and occurs south of the Yangtze; the flowers, leaves and pericarp are the raw material for citral (litsea oil), used in perfumery, medicine and industry.
4. *Machilus*: flower structure as in *Cinnamomum*, but the leaves are pinnately veined; tepals persistent, the lobes recurved outward in fruit. *M. pauhoi* has oblanceolate leaves pale pinkish beneath and occurs in the southeast; shavings of the wood soaked in water give a mucilage that was formerly used as hair gel and paper paste. *M. thunbergii* has obovate leaves with red veins, grayish beneath, and occurs south of the Yangtze to Japan; the wood is used in construction and the bark is medicinal.
5. *Phoebe*: flowers and leaves similar to *Machilus*, but the tepal lobes persist in fruit, erect and appressed to the base of the fruit. *P. zhennan* has a tall, straight trunk and dense, durable, lustrous wood ("golden-thread nanmu"), a precious timber tree of China, occurring in Sichuan, Guizhou and elsewhere. *P. sheareri* has obovate leaves with dense reticulate veins and hairs beneath and occurs south of the Yangtze and in the southwest; the wood is good and used for furniture.
6. *Sassafras*: deciduous, flowers unisexual; leaves often shallowly 3-lobed (entire and 2–3-lobed leaves often on the same plant). *S. tzumu* occurs in mountains south of the Yangtze and is a fast-growing afforestation tree, with wood used for shipbuilding, construction and furniture. In North America, the root bark of white sassafras (*S. albidum*) contains safrole and was formerly used to flavor beverages but is now banned. *Sassafras* is also a genus with an East Asian–North American disjunction.
7. *Cassytha*: *C. filiformis* is a parasitic twining herb with thread-like green or yellow stems attached to the host by disc-like haustoria; leaves reduced to scales; flowers in spikes, stamens 9; the fruit is enclosed in the fleshy floral tube. In outward appearance it is very similar to dodder (*Cuscuta*) of Convolvulaceae, an example of convergent evolution of different groups under the parasitic habit ([7.12.47](/7.12.47)). It occurs in southern China and the whole plant is medicinal.
8. Others: avocado (*Persea americana*), native to Central America, has oil-rich fruit and is an important tropical fruit; bay laurel (*Laurus nobilis*), native to the Mediterranean, has leaves used as a Western seasoning, and branches were woven into laurel wreaths in ancient Greece.

Key characters of the family: woody, with oil cells. Leaves simple, alternate, leathery. Flowers bisexual, regular, whorled, trimerous; perianth in 2 whorls; stamens in 4 whorls, the innermost reduced, anthers valvate, whorl 3 extrorse with glands; ovary superior, 1-locular, 1 ovule. Fruit a drupe, often with a cupule. Seeds without endosperm.

### 35.3.3. Calycanthaceae

Calycanthaceae (sweetshrub family) are deciduous or evergreen shrubs with oil cells; leaves simple, opposite, entire, without stipules. Flowers solitary, bisexual, radially symmetric, fragrant; tepals numerous, spiral, grading from bract-like outside to petal-like inside, with no distinction of sepals and petals; stamens 5–30, the outer fertile and the inner staminodes, anthers 2-locular, longitudinally dehiscent; carpels numerous, free, borne on the inner wall of an urn-shaped floral cup, 2 ovules per carpel (1 develops). The fruit is a number of achenes enclosed in the fleshy or woody floral cup, forming a pseudocarp.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{\infty} A_{5\text{–}30} \underline{G}_{\infty}$ (perigynous flower).

The family has 3 genera and about 10 species in East Asia, North America and Australia. *Chimonanthus* is a genus endemic to China; wintersweet (*C. praecox*) flowers from winter to early spring before the leaves, with waxy, yellow, highly fragrant tepals, and is a traditional winter ornamental of China whose flowers yield aromatic oil. *Calycanthus chinensis* (formerly *Sinocalycanthus chinensis*) occurs in Zhejiang and bears white flowers with pink-edged tepals in summer; it belongs to the same genus as the North American Carolina allspice (*C. floridus*), another example of East Asian–North American disjunction. The seedlings of *Idiospermum australiense* of Australia often have 3–4 cotyledons, and it was once separated as the family Idiospermaceae.

### 35.3.4. Other Families of Laurales

- Hernandiaceae: trees, shrubs or woody lianas with valvate anthers and an inferior ovary; the fruit is winged or enclosed in an enlarged involucre. China has *Illigera* (woody lianas, fruit with 2–4 wings) and *Hernandia* (Hainan).
- Monimiaceae: mainly in the tropics of the Southern Hemisphere, often dioecious, with flowers having numerous carpels in a floral cup; not found in China.
- Atherospermataceae, Siparunaceae and Gomortegaceae: from the temperate Southern Hemisphere, tropical America and Chile respectively; in *Siparuna* the floral cup is almost closed and is pollinated by gall midges that lay eggs in it.

## 35.4. Piperales

### 35.4.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Piperales are mostly herbs, lianas or shrubs, often with oil cells and a pungent or special smell; the vascular bundles of the stem are often in two rings or scattered, and the nodes are often swollen; leaves simple, alternate, often distichous, with a cordate base and palmate or arcuate venation; sympodial branching; flowers small, with the perianth reduced or in a single whorl. Piperales in APG IV include 3 families: Saururaceae and Piperaceae are sister groups, and Aristolochiaceae (including the former Lactoridaceae and Hydnoraceae) are sister to them.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Piperales included Chloranthaceae, Saururaceae and Piperaceae, with Aristolochiaceae in a separate order Aristolochiales; Engler's Aristolochiales also included Rafflesiaceae. Molecular evidence moves Chloranthaceae out into the separate order Chloranthales (§35.6), moves Rafflesiaceae to Malpighiales ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)), and places Aristolochiaceae in the same order as Piperaceae and Saururaceae. Lactoridaceae (*Lactoris fernandeziana*, found only on the Juan Fernández Islands of Chile) were formerly placed in Magnoliales, and Hydnoraceae (*Hydnora*, *Prosopanche*, leafless plants wholly parasitic on the roots of other plants) near Rafflesiales; APG IV merges both into Aristolochiaceae.

### 35.4.2. Piperaceae

Diagnostic characters of Piperaceae:

- Herbs, shrubs or woody lianas; in climbing species the nodes are often swollen and bear adventitious roots; vascular bundles scattered, vessels small.
- Leaves alternate, rarely opposite or whorled, pungent, the base often asymmetric, often triplinerved or palmately veined; stipules often fused to the petiole, or absent.
- Flowers very small, bisexual or unisexual (dioecious), without perianth, borne in the axils of peltate bracts and crowded into spikes or spadices, the inflorescence opposite a leaf.
- Stamens 1–10 (often 2–6); pollen subglobose, without apertures or monosulcate.
- Gynoecium of 1–4 fused carpels, ovary superior, 1-locular, with 1 orthotropous basal ovule; stigmas 1–5.
- Fruit a small drupe (berry-like); seeds small, embryo very small, endosperm scanty, perisperm abundant.
- Chromosome numbers $x = 8, 11, 12, 13$, etc.

Floral formula: $P_0 A_{1\text{–}10} \underline{G}_{(1\text{–}4:1:1)}$.

The family has about 5 genera and 3600 species, mainly *Piper* (about 2000 species) and *Peperomia* (about 1600 species), in the tropics and subtropics; China has 3 genera and over 70 species, in the southwest to southeast. Representative plants:

- Black pepper (*Piper nigrum*): a woody liana, flowers unisexual, dioecious or polygamous-monoecious, the bract fused at its base with the rachis into a shallow cup. Native to southern India, cultivated in South China, Yunnan and Taiwan. Immature fruits dried with the pericarp shriveled and blackened give black pepper; mature fruits soaked to remove the skin and dried, white in color, give white pepper. The pungent principle is mainly piperine.
- Betel (*P. betle*): native to Southeast Asia, the leaves contain aromatic oil and are pungent. In Southeast Asia and parts of southern China there is a custom of chewing betel leaf wrapping areca nut (the seed of the palm *Areca catechu*) and lime; betel quid chewing is an established cause of oral cancer, and the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies areca nut as a Group 1 carcinogen.
- Others: the fruit spikes of long pepper (*P. longum*) are used in medicine and seasoning; leaves of *P. sarmentosum* are used as a condiment in Yunnan and Guangxi; kava (*P. methysticum*) is the source of the traditional Pacific island drink kava. *Peperomia pellucida* is an annual fleshy small herb native to tropical America, growing in shady damp places and naturalized in southern China; *Peperomia* species such as *P. tetraphylla* are common indoor foliage plants.

Key characters of the family: leaves often pungent, triplinerved. Flowers small, without perianth, crowded into spikes; ovary superior, 1-locular, with 1 orthotropous ovule. Drupe. Seeds with abundant perisperm.

### 35.4.3. Saururaceae

Saururaceae (lizard's-tail family) are perennial herbs of wet places with rhizomes and a special smell; leaves simple, alternate, stipules fused with the petiole; flowers small, bisexual, without perianth, crowded into spikes or racemes, and in some species the base of the inflorescence has white petaloid involucral bracts; stamens 3–8; carpels 3–4, free or fused, ovary superior or inferior; fruit a capsule or schizocarp. Floral formula: $P_0 A_{3\text{–}8} G_{3\text{–}4}$ (carpels free or fused, ovary position varying within the family).

The family has 4 genera and 6 species, in East Asia and North America. Chameleon plant (*Houttuynia cordata*) has cordate leaves with a fishy smell when crushed, and the base of the spike has 4 white petaloid involucral bracts so that the whole inflorescence resembles a flower (pseudanthium); the 3 carpels are fused with parietal placentation; it occurs south of the Yangtze, the whole plant is medicinal, and the young rhizome (zheergen) is eaten as a vegetable in southwestern China. *Saururus chinensis* gets its Chinese name ("three white") from 2–3 leaves below the inflorescence turning white at flowering; the inflorescence is a raceme; it occurs from eastern to southern China, and the whole plant is medicinal. *Gymnotheca chinensis* has an inferior ovary and occurs in South and Southwest China; American lizard's tail (*Saururus cernuus*) of North America and *S. chinensis* are sister species with an East Asian–North American disjunction.

### 35.4.4. Aristolochiaceae

Aristolochiaceae (birthwort family) are herbs or lianas, rarely shrubs; leaves simple, alternate, often cordate, palmately veined, entire, without stipules. Flowers bisexual, the perianth usually of a single whorl (often interpreted as the calyx), fused below into a tube, 3-lobed at the apex or expanded into a tongue, radially symmetric (*Asarum*) or bilaterally symmetric (*Aristolochia*), often dark purple or mottled; *Saruma* has both sepals and petals, and some species of *Asarum* have vestiges of petals, showing that the single-whorled perianth arose by loss of petals. Stamens 6–12, often fused with the style into a gynostemium (*Aristolochia*); ovary inferior or half-inferior, 4–6 carpels fused, 4–6-locular, axile placentation, ovules numerous. Capsule; seeds numerous, with endosperm.

Floral formula: *Aristolochia* $\uparrow P_{(3)} A_{6} \overline{G}_{(6:6:\infty)}$; *Asarum* $\ast P_{(3)} A_{12} \overline{G}_{(6:6:\infty)}$ (ovary half-inferior to inferior).

The family (in the broad sense) has about 8 genera and over 700 species in the tropics and temperate zone; China has 4 genera and about 90 species. It is divided into 4 subfamilies: Asaroideae (*Asarum*, *Saruma*), Aristolochioideae (*Aristolochia*, *Thottea*), Lactoridoideae and Hydnoroideae.

- *Aristolochia*: herbaceous or woody lianas; the perianth tube is bent into an S or pipe shape and swollen at the base into a bladder with downward-pointing hairs on the inner wall; the flower has a foul smell that attracts flies, which are temporarily trapped in the bladder until the anthers shed pollen and the hairs wither, and the flies leave carrying pollen, a kind of "trap flower" ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)). The capsule often dehisces from the base and the fruit stalk also splits into 6 strips, like an inverted hanging basket. The fruits (maduoling), roots (qingmuxiang) and stems (tianxianteng) of *A. debilis* and *A. contorta* were formerly used in medicine; *A. manshuriensis* (guan mutong) and *A. fangchi* (guang fangji) were also widely used medicinally.
- *Asarum*: perennial herbs with rhizomes, 1–2 cordate leaves, flowers solitary in the leaf axils close to the ground, perianth bell- or urn-shaped and 3-lobed, pollinated by ants or fungus gnats. The roots and rhizomes of *A. heterotropoides* var. *mandshuricum* and *A. sieboldii* are the Chinese medicine "xixin", pungent in taste, of which the old saying "do not exceed one qian of xixin" warns, because the volatile oils (methyleugenol, safrole, etc.) and aristolochic acid they contain are toxic.
- *Saruma*: only *S. henryi*, endemic to China, with a flower having two whorls of sepals and petals, a type in Asaroideae that retains petals.

Aristolochic acid and medicinal safety. *Aristolochia*, *Asarum* and other plants contain *aristolochic acid* and its derivatives (aristolactams), which are biosynthetically related to benzylisoquinoline alkaloids ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.7)). After metabolism in the body, aristolochic acid binds to DNA and causes characteristic A:T → T:A transversion mutations, leading to renal interstitial fibrosis and urothelial carcinoma of the upper urinary tract, known as aristolochic acid nephropathy. In the early 1990s a Belgian slimming clinic mistakenly substituted *A. fangchi* for *Stephania tetrandra* (Menispermaceae) in its prescriptions, causing progressive renal failure in over a hundred patients, many of whom later developed urothelial carcinoma, an event that drew wide attention to the toxicity of aristolochic acid; Balkan endemic nephropathy was traced to seeds of *A. clematitis* mixed into wheat. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies aristolochic-acid-containing plants as Group 1 carcinogens. China withdrew the medicinal standards for guan mutong, guang fangji and qingmuxiang in 2003–2004. This example shows that the "name" of a Chinese medicine must correspond to a botanical "species": drug names such as fangji and mutong refer to plants of different families in different regions, and only accurate identification of the source plant can avoid misuse ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.3.1)).

## 35.5. Canellales

Canellales comprise Winteraceae and Canellaceae, which receive extremely strong support in multigene analyses. Synapomorphies include branched sclereids, clearly differentiated sepals and petals, a highly differentiated transmitting tissue of the pollen tube channel, an outer integument only 2–4 cells thick, and a palisade layer in the outer seed coat. The order was called Winterales in early literature.

- Winteraceae: evergreen trees or shrubs with oil cells; xylem without vessels, only tracheids; leaves often glaucous beneath, the stomata plugged with wax; calyx fused and early deciduous, petals numerous, stamens numerous, carpels numerous and free (sometimes fused), the carpels of some genera not completely closed at flowering; pollen in tetrads. About 5 genera and 90 species, in the temperate Southern Hemisphere and tropical America, extending north through Southeast Asia to the Philippines (not in China). The bark of winter's bark (*Drimys winteri*) was used by Captain Winter of Drake's fleet to treat scurvy (hence the family name); the fruits and leaves of *Tasmannia* are used as spices in Australia. *Takhtajania perrieri* of Madagascar was rediscovered in 1994 after being thought extinct in the wild, and DNA showed it to be sister to the rest of Winteraceae. The vessel-less wood of Winteraceae was long taken as evidence of the primitiveness of angiosperms (Cronquist and Takhtajan both regarded Winteraceae as among the most primitive extant families), but phylogenetic analyses show that their ancestor had vessels and that the absence of vessels is a secondary loss, possibly related to growth in cold, wet environments in which tracheids are safer against freeze–thaw embolism. Pollen fossils of Winteraceae can be traced to the late Barremian–early Aptian of the Early Cretaceous.
- Canellaceae (wild cinnamon family): trees with aromatic bark; calyx 3, petals 4–12, stamens fused into a tube, ovary superior, parietal placentation. About 5 genera and 20 species, in the American tropics and Africa. The bark of white cinnamon (*Canella winterana*) is used as a spice and medicine.

## 35.6. Chloranthales

Chloranthales comprise only Chloranthaceae, with 4 genera and about 75 species: *Hedyosmum* (about 45 species, tropical America and Southeast Asia), *Chloranthus* (about 13 species, East Asia), *Ascarina* (Pacific islands and Madagascar) and *Sarcandra* (East to Southeast Asia). China has about 15 species in 3 genera, including *Chloranthus* and *Sarcandra*.

Diagnostic characters: herbs, shrubs or small trees with oil cells and fragrance; nodes markedly swollen; leaves simple, opposite, the petiole bases fused into a sheath, margin with chloranthoid glandular teeth; stipules small. Flowers minute, bisexual or unisexual, without perianth (female flowers of *Hedyosmum* have 3 small tepals), arranged in spikes, heads or panicles; stamen 1 (*Sarcandra*) or 3 fused into one three-lobed stamen (*Chloranthus*), attached to one side of the ovary; gynoecium of 1 carpel, ovary 1-locular, with 1 orthotropous, pendulous ovule; fruit a drupe. The xylem of *Sarcandra* has no vessels (secondarily lost).

Floral formula: *Chloranthus* $P_0 A_{(3)} G_{1:1:1}$; *Sarcandra* $P_0 A_1 G_{1:1:1}$.

Representative plants: *Chloranthus spicatus* (zhulan, "bead orchid") is a subshrub with spikes forming panicles and yellow-green, highly fragrant flowers used to scent tea, occurring from South to Southwest China and cultivated everywhere. *C. serratus* and *C. japonicus* are perennial herbs with medicinal, toxic roots. *Sarcandra glabra* (zhongjiefeng) is an evergreen subshrub with swollen nodes and red drupes, occurring south of the Yangtze, the whole plant medicinal, also used as a fruit-ornamental. The three-lobed stamen of *Chloranthus* has two interpretations: three stamens fused at the base (fossil evidence), or one stamen divided into three lobes (organogenesis evidence, Kong et al. 2002).

Chloranthaceae are among the angiosperm groups with the earliest fossil record: some dispersed pollen of the Early Cretaceous (such as *Clavatipollenites* and *Asteropollis* types), together with small flowers and stamen fossils, resemble *Hedyosmum* and *Ascarina*, showing that plants of the Chloranthaceae type were once quite common in the early radiation of angiosperms, whereas most extant species diverged more recently.

The systematic position of Chloranthales is still undetermined. Cronquist placed Chloranthaceae in Piperales; Takhtajan set up a separate order Chloranthales, considering it close to Trimeniaceae and Piperales; Taylor and Hickey (1992) and others once thought it to be sister to all angiosperms. In molecular evidence, chloroplast data mostly support it as sister to the magnoliids, while some nuclear gene data support it as forming a clade with Ceratophyllales that is sister to the eudicots; a recently published genome analysis of *Chloranthus* has not given a consistent conclusion either. APG IV therefore treats Chloranthales as an independent lineage of unresolved position within Mesangiospermae. It shares ancestral characters with the ANA grade, such as ascidiate carpels, nodal anatomy and chloranthoid leaf teeth.

## Questions

1. How can one recognize plants of Magnoliaceae, Lauraceae and Annonaceae when not in flower?

   **Answer** All three are woody, aromatic (oil cells), with simple, alternate, entire leaves. Magnoliaceae: annular stipular scars on the twigs, and the bud is enclosed by stipules. Lauraceae: no stipules and no stipular scars, leaves mostly leathery, often triplinerved or three-veined, often glaucous beneath, with a camphor- or lemon-like scent when crushed, and *Cinnamomum* often has domatia in the vein axils. Annonaceae: no stipules, leaves arranged in two neat ranks on the twig.

2. What is the relationship between *Magnolia* and *Yulania*, *Michelia*, *Manglietia*? Why does the same plant have two scientific names, *Magnolia denudata* and *Yulania denudata*?

   **Answer** Molecular phylogenies show that the traditional genera of Magnolioideae, *Michelia*, *Manglietia*, *Yulania* and others, are nested within a broadly defined *Magnolia*, so that *Magnolia* in the narrow sense is paraphyletic. To make genera monophyletic, one may merge Magnolioideae into a single broadly defined *Magnolia* (with *Michelia*, *Manglietia*, *Yulania* and others reduced to subgenera or sections), or continue to split the narrow *Magnolia* into many small genera. Both treatments recognize the same phylogenetic relationships and differ only in the range of genera; in the broad treatment the correct name of the yulan is *Magnolia denudata*, in the narrow treatment *Yulania denudata*, and the two names are synonyms.

3. What is special about the stamens of Lauraceae? How do they distinguish *Cinnamomum*, *Lindera* and *Litsea*?

   **Answer** The stamens of Lauraceae are in 4 whorls of 3, the innermost reduced to staminodes; the anthers are valvate, those of whorls 1 and 2 introrse and those of whorl 3 extrorse with a pair of glands at the base of the filament. *Cinnamomum*: flowers bisexual, panicles, anthers 4-locular, leaves often triplinerved; *Lindera*: flowers unisexual and dioecious, umbels with an involucre, anthers 2-locular; *Litsea*: flowers unisexual and dioecious, umbels with an involucre, anthers 4-locular, leaves mostly pinnately veined.

4. How was the harm of aristolochic acid discovered? From the viewpoint of plant taxonomy, how can similar drug harm be avoided?

   **Answer** In the early 1990s a Belgian slimming clinic mistakenly used *A. fangchi*, which contains aristolochic acid, in place of *Stephania tetrandra*, and many patients developed progressive renal failure and urothelial carcinoma; Balkan endemic nephropathy was also shown to be related to *Aristolochia* seeds mixed into flour. Aristolochic acid binds to DNA and causes characteristic mutations and is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen, and China subsequently withdrew the medicinal standards for guan mutong, guang fangji and qingmuxiang. The key to avoiding similar harm is that a drug name must correspond to an accurately identified source plant (scientific name); homonyms in different regions (fangji and mutong each refer to several plants of different families) must be distinguished by morphology, microscopy and DNA barcoding; and one should remain vigilant about groups known to contain toxic constituents (Aristolochiaceae).

5. Winteraceae and *Sarcandra* both lack vessels, and Cronquist regarded them as primitive on this basis. How does modern evidence view this?

   **Answer** Phylogenetic analyses show that the relatives of Winteraceae (Canellales) and of *Sarcandra* (Chloranthaceae) have vessels and that their vessel-less condition is a secondary loss: Winteraceae grow in cold, wet environments in which tracheids are less prone to freeze–thaw embolism, so the loss of vessels may be an adaptation; the truly primitive vessel-less condition is found only in the earliest-diverging *Amborella*. This shows that the same character state (no vessels) can be either an ancestral character or a derived character formed by reversal, and can be judged only on a phylogenetic tree ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.2.2)).
