Ferns are the largest group of vascular plants outside the seed plants, with about 10,000 living species. Most are herbs, a few are tree ferns up to 10 m or more tall, and they occupy habitats from tropical rain-forest trunks to temperate grasslands, paddy fields and desert rock crevices. China has more than 2000 species of lycophytes and ferns, especially rich in the southwest. In ferns both the sporophyte and the gametophyte live independently: the sporophyte has roots, stems, leaves and vascular tissue like a seed plant, whereas the gametophyte is a green prothallus a few millimeters across that needs water for fertilization, like a bryophyte. Ferns are therefore the key group for understanding how the sporophyte came to dominate on land while the gametophyte was reduced.

This chapter first sets out the scope of the ferns in the current classification (§17.1), then the morphology of the sporophyte and gametophyte (§17.2), and the life cycle, including the mechanics of spore ejection from leptosporangia (§17.3). The four subclasses of PPG I follow: horsetails (§17.4), whisk ferns and moonworts (§17.5), marattioid ferns (§17.6), and the leptosporangiate ferns that make up the great majority of ferns (§17.7). Finally we discuss the origin and evolution of ferns (§17.8) and their economic value (§17.9). The general features of vascular plants, steles and the origin of organs were treated in [7.12.16](/7.12.16); heterospory and the origin of seeds continue in [7.12.25](/7.12.25).

## 17.1. Scope and Classification of the Ferns

### 17.1.1. Euphyllophytes and Ferns

Living vascular plants fall into two lineages, the **lycophytes** and the euphyllophytes ([7.12.16](/7.12.16#sec-16.1.2)).

**Definition 17.1.1 (Euphyllophytes)** The clade of vascular plants that excludes the lycophytes, comprising the ferns and the seed plants, is called the **euphyllophytes** (Euphyllophyta). Its synapomorphies include a branching pattern with differentiated main axis and lateral branches (formed by overtopping), **megaphylls** derived from lateral branch systems, lateral roots arising endogenously from the pericycle, multiflagellate sperm (flagella have been lost in most seed plants), and an inversion of about 30 kb in the chloroplast genome.

The euphyllophytes in turn divide into two lineages, the seed plants and the ferns.

**Definition 17.1.2 (Ferns)** The sister group of the seed plants within the euphyllophytes, that is, the clade formed by the living horsetails, whisk ferns, moonworts, marattioid ferns and leptosporangiate ferns, is called the **ferns** (monilophytes); in PPG I (2016) it is the class Polypodiopsida. Ferns reproduce by spores, the sporophyte is dominant, the gametophyte lives independently, and the great majority are homosporous.

Morphological evidence for the monophyly of ferns is scarce: mainly protoxylem restricted to the projecting lobes of the stele ("beaded" protoxylem, whence the name monilophyte) and a distinctive sperm structure. Monophyly was established chiefly by phylogenies based on chloroplast and nuclear genes (Pryer et al. 2001, 2004). On this circumscription the traditional division Pteridophyta, which included the lycophytes and the ferns, is a **paraphyletic group**; "ferns" in this chapter means only Polypodiopsida. Horsetails and whisk ferns differ greatly in appearance from the true ferns, and Chinese textbooks used to group them with the lycophytes as "fern allies"; molecular evidence shows that both lie within the ferns: the whisk ferns are the sister group of the moonworts, and the horsetails are sister to the remaining ferns or lie at the base of the ferns.

### 17.1.3. Comparison with the Ching System

Ren-Chang Ching (1978) divided the ferns of China into 5 subdivisions and 63 families, later revised to 52 families; Chinese textbooks follow this system, dividing the subdivision Filicophytina by sporangial development into Eusporangiatae, Primitive Leptosporangiatae and Leptosporangiatae, the last containing Polypodiales (Filicales), Marsileales and Salviniales. The correspondence between the two systems is as follows (for the lycophyte part see [7.12.16](/7.12.16#sec-16.5.1)):

## 17.2. Morphology of the Sporophyte and Gametophyte

### 17.2.1. Roots, Stems and Appendages

The fern sporophyte is mostly a perennial herb, a few species are annual (for example *Ceratopteris*), and a very few are tree-like (tree ferns such as *Alsophila*). Except in the whisk ferns (*Psilotum*), which have only rhizoids, all ferns have **adventitious roots** arising from the stem; the root usually has a diarch protostele.

The stem is mostly a **rhizome**, creeping, ascending or erect, and in tree ferns an erect, trunk-like aerial stem; primitive forms have both a rhizome and an aerial stem (*Psilotum*, horsetails). The stele of the stem is more varied in ferns than in any other group: protostele (*Lygodium*), ectophloic siphonostele (*Osmunda*), amphiphloic siphonostele (*Dennstaedtia*, *Marsilea*), dictyostele (most Polypodiales) and polycyclic stele (bracken, *Alsophila*); all except the protostele have leaf gaps. The xylem consists chiefly of **tracheids**; a few species have **vessels** (in the rhizome and roots of bracken, where vessels arose independently), and the phloem has **sieve cells** but no sieve tubes. Apart from a very few forms such as the moonworts, living ferns have no vascular cambium; the trunk of a tree fern is thickened and supported by dense leaf bases and a mantle of adventitious roots around it.

The epidermis of stems, petioles and leaves often bears protective appendages: hairs (unicellular, jointed or stellate) and scales (flat appendages one cell layer thick, some with transparent thickened walls, called clathrate scales). The presence and form of hairs and scales are important for classifying families and genera: Dryopteridaceae mostly have scales, Thelypteridaceae mostly needle-like hairs, and the rhizome of *Cibotium barometz* is densely covered with long golden hairs.

### 17.2.2. Leaves

The leaves of true ferns, simple or compound, are all megaphylls, often called fronds, with a petiole and a blade that is once to many times pinnately divided or pinnately compound.

**Definition 17.2.1 (Fiddlehead)** The young frond, whose blade and each order of pinnae are coiled adaxially from the apex toward the base like a watch spring and then unroll gradually from base to apex as they grow (circinate vernation), is called a **fiddlehead** (crozier).

Coiling protects the tender leaf tip and apical meristem on the inside of the leaf, and the blade can keep growing for a very long time (the rachis of *Lygodium* grows almost indefinitely and can climb several meters). Fiddleheads are characteristic of the true ferns (marattioid and leptosporangiate ferns); the young leaf of *Ophioglossum* is not coiled but erect and folded. Fiddleheads are the edible part of wild vegetables such as bracken and royal fern.

The parts of a pinnately compound leaf are named as follows. The central axis of the blade is the rachis; the leaflets first divided from the rachis are pinnae, and their axes are rachises of the pinnae (the pinna rachis); the leaflets divided from a pinna are pinnules, with their own axes; the midrib of the last-order pinnule or lobe is the costa. Venation falls into three types that represent a direction of evolution: free (veins simple or dichotomously branched, veinlets free; primitive), intermediate (lateral veins near the midrib joined into areoles, marginal veinlets free) and reticulate (all joined into a network, sometimes with included veinlets in the areoles; most advanced).

**Definition 17.2.2 (Trophophylls and Sporophylls; Monomorphic and Dimorphic Leaves)** In ferns a leaf that only photosynthesizes is called a trophophyll (sterile leaf); a leaf that mainly produces sporangia and spores is called a **sporophyll** (fertile leaf). If trophophylls and sporophylls are not distinct and have the same shape the leaves are monomorphic; if they differ clearly in shape the leaves are dimorphic.

Monomorphic leaves are common, as in bracken, *Cyrtomium* and *Dryopteris*; dimorphic leaves occur in *Matteuccia* (the pinnae of the sporophyll roll under the back into a pod that encloses the sori), *Onoclea* (the pinnules of the sporophyll shrink into little balls), *Osmunda*, *Blechnum* and related genera other than *Woodwardia*, and *Plagiogyria* (the sporophyll is slender, with the pinnae narrowed into lines); in some the sterile and fertile pinnae differ on one and the same leaf (pinna dimorphism, as in *Claytosmunda* and *Lygodium*). The usual view is that evolution proceeded from monomorphic to dimorphic leaves: a narrowed sporophyll that has lost chlorophyll reduces transpiration from the fertile part and lifts the sporangia to a position favorable for dispersal.

### 17.2.3. Sporangia

Sporangia are borne on the margin or underside of the sporophyll, or on specialized sporophylls. By sporangial development and structure the ferns fall into two groups.

**Definition 17.2.3 (Eusporangiate and Leptosporangiate Ferns)** Ferns whose sporangia arise from several initial cells of the leaf surface together, have a wall of several cell layers, are large, contain many spores (hundreds to thousands) and have no typical annulus are called **eusporangiate ferns**, such as the horsetails, whisk ferns, moonworts and marattioid ferns; ferns whose sporangia arise from a single initial cell, have a wall of one cell layer, a slender stalk, a small and fixed number of spores (usually 64) and mostly an annulus are called **leptosporangiate ferns**, that is, the Polypodiidae.

The eusporangium is the primitive state in vascular plants (including the lycophytes and the pollen sacs and nucellus of seed plants); the leptosporangium is a synapomorphy of the leptosporangiate ferns. The sporangium of *Osmunda* arises from a single initial cell, but its stalk is formed by many cells, its wall has one layer, and only one side bears a group of thickened cells, so it is intermediate; Chinese textbooks therefore placed the Osmundaceae in a separate class, the "primitive leptosporangiate ferns". In PPG I it is the earliest-diverging lineage of the leptosporangiate ferns. The eusporangiate ferns form a **paraphyletic group**.

**Definition 17.2.4 (Annulus)** The row or group of cells with locally thickened walls on the wall of a leptosporangium is called the **annulus**; when the spores mature the annulus cells lose water and shrink, so that the sporangium splits at the thin-walled lip cells (stomium cells) and the spores are ejected.

The position of the annulus differs among groups, and the general direction of evolution is from apical and transverse (a transverse band in the middle) through oblique to vertical:

| Annulus | Position | Representatives |
|---|---|---|
| Peltate, lateral | A group of thickened cells on one side of the wall (subapical) | Osmundales |
| Apical | A ring at the top of the sporangium | Schizaeales (*Lygodium*) |
| Transverse | A ring across the middle of the sporangium | Gleicheniales (*Dicranopteris*) |
| Oblique | An uninterrupted oblique ring | Hymenophyllales, Cyatheales |
| Vertical | From one side of the stalk over the top of the sporangium to the other, interrupted near the stalk, leaving the lip cells | Polypodiales |
| Absent | Does not dehisce; spores are released by gelatinization or decay | Salviniales |

Sporangia are often aggregated into groups on the leaf:

**Definition 17.2.5 (Sori and Indusia)** The group formed by many sporangia of a leptosporangiate fern aggregated together is called a **sorus**; the membranous protective structure that covers the sorus is called an **indusium**. One that grows from the epidermis of the blade is a true indusium; a membranous structure formed by the rolled-back leaf margin is a false indusium.

The shape of the sori (round, elongate, linear, reniform), their position (leaf margin, underside, vein ends, on the veins, on both sides of the midrib, covering the whole underside) and the presence and shape of the indusium (round-reniform, peltate, elongate, linear, cup-shaped, clam-shaped, false) are the main bases for classifying families and genera:

| Sorus | Indusium | Representatives |
|---|---|---|
| Round, on the veins of the underside | Absent | Polypodiaceae (*Lepisorus*, *Pyrrosia*, *Polypodiodes*), some genera of Thelypteridaceae |
| Round, on the underside | Round-reniform, attached at the sinus | *Dryopteris*, *Nephrolepis* |
| Round, on the underside | Round-peltate, attached at the center | *Cyrtomium*, *Polystichum* |
| Elongate or linear, along the veinlets | Elongate or linear, attached on one side | *Asplenium*, *Athyrium*, *Diplazium* |
| Linear, continuous on both sides of the midrib | Linear, opening toward the midrib | *Blechnum*, *Woodwardia* (elongate, in rows) |
| Linear, continuous along the leaf margin | False indusium of the rolled leaf margin | *Pteris*, bracken (inner and outer false indusia), *Onychium* |
| Marginal, at the tips of veinlets | False indusium of the rolled leaf margin | *Adiantum* |
| At vein ends | Cup-shaped | *Dennstaedtia*, *Odontosoria* |
| At the ends of lobes | Bivalved, clam-shaped | *Cibotium* |
| Covering the whole underside | Absent | *Acrostichum* ("acrostichoid") |

The sori of the aquatic *Marsilea* and *Salvinia* are enclosed in a specialized structure:

**Definition 17.2.6 (Sporocarp)** The hard or membranous specialized structure that encloses the sori in the Marsileaceae and Salviniaceae is called a **sporocarp**; the sporocarp of *Marsilea* is formed from a modified pinna, and that of *Salvinia* and *Azolla* from a modified indusium.

### 17.2.4. Spores

Fern spores are produced by meiosis of spore mother cells and are haploid. The great majority of ferns are homosporous; only Marsileaceae and Salviniaceae are heterosporous. By their arrangement in the tetrad and the germination aperture, spores are of two kinds: bilateral spores, reniform, with a single (monolete) scar and bilateral symmetry (as in *Matteuccia* and most Polypodiales); and tetrahedral spores, rounded or bluntly triangular, with a triple (trilete) scar and radial symmetry (as in *Osmunda*, *Lygodium*, bracken and *Alsophila*). In most ferns the spore has, outside the exine, a perispore, often ornamented with tubercles, spines, a network or folds, which is used in palynological identification. The spores of horsetails, *Osmunda*, filmy ferns and others contain chloroplasts and live only a few days to a few weeks, so they must germinate quickly; the spores of most other ferns have no chloroplasts and can survive for years.

### 17.2.5. The Gametophyte

**Definition 17.2.7 (Prothallus)** The gametophyte formed by the germination of a fern spore, especially its mature stage, a flat, usually heart-shaped, green, dorsiventral thallus lying against the ground, is called the **prothallus**; on its ventral side it bears rhizoids, antheridia and archegonia.

Strictly speaking, the gametophyte is the whole course of development from spore germination, through a filament (a row of a few cells) and a plate (a spatulate plate formed by oblique divisions of the apical cell), to the production of antheridia and archegonia; the prothallus is only the adult at its mature stage. The gametophytes of ferns come in several forms:

| Form | Nutrition | Representatives |
|---|---|---|
| Tuberous or cylindrical, subterranean | Symbiotic with endophytic fungi (**mycorrhiza**), no chlorophyll | *Psilotum* (cylindrical, forked), *Ophioglossum*, *Botrychium*, *Helminthostachys* |
| Filamentous | Green | *Schizaea*, some genera of Hymenophyllaceae |
| Plate-like with irregular lobes | Green | *Equisetum* |
| Heart-shaped | Green, lives a few weeks to a few months | Most leptosporangiate ferns (bracken, *Adiantum*, *Dryopteris*) |
| Ribbon- or tongue-shaped, long-lived | Green, can reproduce vegetatively | *Angiopteris* (over 2 cm long, with endophytic fungi), some species of Polypodiaceae and *Vittaria* |
| Developing inside the spore wall | Depends on nutrients stored in the spore | *Marsilea*, *Salvinia*, *Azolla* |

The heart-shaped prothallus is about 0.5–1 cm wide, one cell layer thick at the margin and several layers thick in the middle (the midrib); unicellular rhizoids arise on the ventral side near the base. The **antheridia** are mostly near the rhizoids, spherical and protruding from the surface of the prothallus; in leptosporangiate ferns the wall of the antheridium consists of only 3 cells (a basal ring cell, an upper ring cell and a cap cell) and encloses 30–60 sperm; the sperm are spiral with dozens of flagella at the anterior end. The **archegonia** arise near the apical notch with the venter embedded in the prothallus tissue; the neck is short, 4–5 cell layers high, and inclined toward the posterior end of the prothallus, and contains one neck canal cell (often binucleate), one ventral canal cell and one egg. When the egg is mature the neck canal cell and ventral canal cell disintegrate into mucilage, the neck opens, and a substance that attracts the sperm chemotactically is released.

The gametophytes of homosporous ferns are bisexual, but they often form antheridia first and archegonia later, or become unisexual, which promotes outcrossing: the prothalli that grow larger first secrete a substance called antheridiogen (mostly gibberellin-like compounds) that makes the smaller, later-germinating gametophytes around them form only antheridia. Outcrossing keeps the sporophytes of ferns mostly heterozygous; a few species, such as pioneers of open ground, are mainly selfing. The physiological regulation of sex expression belongs to plant physiology.

## 17.3. Life Cycles

### 17.3.1. The Life Cycle of Bracken

Bracken (*Pteridium aquilinum* var. *latiusculum*, Dennstaedtiaceae) is a common large fern, and we use it to illustrate the life cycle of ferns (after the culture observations of Wenmei Bao and colleagues, and Chinese textbooks).

1. **Sporophyte**: up to more than 1 m tall; the rhizome creeps underground, branches, bears adventitious roots and is covered with brown tomentum. Each year leaves arise from the rhizome, with a long, stout petiole and a large triangular blade, 2–4 times pinnately compound, the young leaf coiled. In cross section the rhizome has an outer epidermis (which ruptures as the rhizome grows), a cortex of sclerenchyma and parenchyma, and separate vascular bundles arranged in an inner and an outer ring (polycyclic stele) with a band of sclerenchyma between the rings; each bundle is surrounded by an endodermis (bundle sheath), and the xylem is mesarch.
2. Sporangia and spores: the sori are linear, continuous along the leaf margin on a connecting veinlet of the underside, covered externally by a false indusium formed by the rolled-back leaf margin and internally by an inconspicuous inner indusium. Each sporangium has a long stalk and a vertical annulus; the inner and lateral walls of the annulus cells are lignified and thickened, and two unthickened lip cells lie near the stalk. The sporangium contains 16 spore mother cells, which produce 64 tetrahedral spores (n) by meiosis.
3. Spore ejection: when the spores mature the annulus loses water and curls outward, so that the sporangium splits transversely at the lip cells, the wall breaks into two valves and the spores are exposed; the annulus continues to curl to its limit and then snaps back suddenly, throwing the spores far away (§17.3.2).
4. **Gametophyte**: spores germinate in 3–4 days at 25–27 °C, first putting out a colorless first rhizoid, with green cells arising on the other side and dividing into a filament of 4–6 cells; the apical cell divides obliquely and alternately, forming a plate with an apical growing point; later the lateral divisions speed up, giving a heart-shaped prothallus in which the growing point is replaced by a meristem of several cells. In culture, antheridia appear about 20 days after sowing and archegonia about 30 days on the larger heart-shaped prothalli; prothalli that form antheridia early often form no archegonia and become unisexual. Each antheridium produces about 60 sperm, which are band-shaped, coiled in 3–4 spiral turns, with 30–50 flagella at the anterior end.
5. Fertilization and the embryo: sperm swim into the archegonium through the film of water formed by rain or dew and fuse with the egg to form the zygote (2n). The eggs of several archegonia on one prothallus may be fertilized, but only one develops into an embryo. The zygote begins to divide about a day after fertilization, and at the four-cell stage the cotyledon, radicle, foot and stem apex are already differentiated; the foot is embedded in the prothallus and absorbs nutrients, and about a month later the first cotyledon is visible to the naked eye. After the young sporophyte has grown true leaves and a rhizome it becomes independent, and the prothallus dies.

### 17.3.2. Spore Ejection from Leptosporangia

The sporangia of Polypodiales are only 0.2–0.3 mm in diameter, yet they throw spores a few millimeters to about 1 cm, by a catapult-like mechanism of the annulus (Noblin et al. 2012).

- Energy storage: the annulus consists of about 12–14 cells whose inner and lateral walls are thickened and outer walls thin. After the sporangium opens, water continually evaporates from the annulus cells; the cohesion of water produces a large negative pressure in the cells, the thin outer walls are drawn inward, the lateral walls of adjacent cells move together, and the whole annulus therefore curls outward and straightens, storing elastic energy like a drawn bow, with the spores left in the curled "spoon".
- Release: when the negative pressure reaches its limit the water in the cells cavitates, bubbles appear, and the tension vanishes instantly; the annulus snaps back in about 10 µs, and the spores are thrown out at about 10 m/s with an acceleration of about $10^5$ times that of gravity.
- Braking: the annulus does not return to its original position in one movement but stops abruptly at about half way and then returns slowly, on a time scale of milliseconds. The fast step is determined by inertia and elasticity, the slow step by the flow of water through the porous structure of the cell wall (poroelasticity); the mid-way "braking" is like the cross-bar of a medieval catapult, so that the spores in the spoon gain enough speed to fly out instead of returning to the original position with the annulus.

A simple model illustrates the two time scales. Treat the annulus as an elastic arm with moment of inertia $J$ and torsional stiffness $\kappa$, opened through an angle $\theta$, with equilibrium at $\theta = 0$. The fast recoil after cavitation is a nearly undamped spring–mass system:

$$
J\ddot{\theta} + \kappa\theta = 0, \qquad \tau_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\frac{J}{\kappa}}
$$
where $\tau_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the time needed to snap back to equilibrium, only of the order of microseconds. If the annulus is "caught" at the angle $\theta_{\mathrm{s}}$ by water in the cells that has not yet flowed, the remaining return is controlled by poroelastic relaxation, approximately an exponential decay $\theta(t) = \theta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{p}}}$ with $\tau_{\mathrm{p}} \sim \eta L^2/(k E)$, proportional to the viscosity $\eta$ of water and to the square of the cell size $L$, and inversely proportional to the permeability $k$ and elastic modulus $E$ of the wall, of the order of milliseconds. The speed at which a spore leaves the end of the annulus is about $v \approx \omega R$, where $R \approx 0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ is the radius of the annulus; if it turns through about 1 rad in $\tau_{\mathrm{in}} \approx 10\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, then $\omega \approx 10^5\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ and $v \approx 20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, of the same order as the measured value of about 10 m/s, and the acceleration $v/\tau_{\mathrm{in}} \sim 10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, that is, about $10^5 g$. A spore is very small and air drag decelerates it quickly (its terminal velocity is very low), so the range is only millimeters to centimeters, but this suffices to carry the spore out of the still air layer under the leaf and hand it to air currents for dispersal. The mechanics of rotation and oscillation is treated in [5.1](/5.1).

### 17.3.3. Apogamy and Apospory

The alternation of generations in ferns sometimes proceeds without fertilization or meiosis.

**Definition 17.3.1 (Apogamy and Apospory)** The development of a sporophyte directly from vegetative cells of the gametophyte (often near the archegonium), without fusion of gametes, is called **apogamy**; the direct growth of a gametophyte from vegetative cells of the sporophyte (such as leaf cells), without meiotic production of spores, is called **apospory**.

Apogamy is fairly common in ferns: about 10% of species have it as their normal mode of reproduction, in species of dry habitats (for example some species of *Aleuritopteris*, *Cyrtomium* and *Dryopteris*), where fertilization needs very little water and the gametophyte grows an embryo directly. The gametophyte of the *Cyrtogonellum* ferns, endemic to China (formerly the genus *Cyrtogonellum*, merged into *Polystichum* in PPG I), grows in dry rock crevices; although it forms normal antheridia and archegonia, in dry conditions the sperm cannot swim, the archegonia shrivel, and cells at the apex of the prothallus divide and differentiate directly into a cotyledon and radicle (observations of Wenmei Bao). Such ferns usually double their chromosomes by a mitosis before spore formation (the Döpp–Manton mechanism), so that the spores are unreduced and sporophyte and gametophyte have the same chromosome number.

The gametophyte produced by apospory is diploid, and the diploid gametes it produces fuse to form a tetraploid sporophyte; a diploid gamete fusing with a normal haploid gamete gives a triploid sporophyte. These two phenomena make polyploids easy to form in ferns and are one reason for their exceptionally high chromosome numbers (§17.8.3). The development of an egg directly into a sporophyte without fertilization is called parthenogenesis.

In a few ferns the gametophyte is dominant and reproduces vegetatively by gemmae, with almost no sporophyte: *Vittaria appalachiana* of the Appalachian Mountains of North America exists only as a ribbon-like gametophyte and no sporophyte has ever been found (Farrar et al. 1991); *Crepidomanes intricatum* (formerly *Trichomanes intricatum*) of the Hymenophyllaceae has only a filamentous, *Cladophora*-like gametophyte. The "*Vittaria lineata*" mentioned in Chinese textbooks is in fact this independent gametophyte.

## 17.4. Equisetidae

The Equisetidae contain only a single genus, *Equisetum*, in the Equisetaceae of the Equisetales, with about 15 species distributed worldwide except Australia and New Zealand; about 10 species occur in China, growing along rivers, in forests, grasslands, marshes and fields. Horsetails flourished in the Carboniferous, with trees up to 20 m tall (*Calamites*); the living genus is the only relict, which is why it is often called a "living fossil".

Sporophyte: a perennial herb with a rhizome and aerial stems, both with distinct nodes and internodes, the internodes hollow. The rhizome is brown and spreads deep underground, bearing adventitious roots at the nodes and sometimes tubers; its vegetative reproduction is so vigorous that it is a weed hard to eradicate from fields (field horsetail). The aerial stems are mostly annual, erect, with longitudinal ridges and furrows on the surface; the outer walls of the epidermal cells are heavily impregnated with silica, making the stem rough and hard. A whorl of small scale-like leaves arises at each node, fused at the base into a sheath whose upper margin bears teeth (sheath teeth); the leaves are small and lack chlorophyll or have very little, and photosynthesis is carried out mainly by the green stem; these leaves are reduced megaphylls, which Chinese textbooks call microphylls on the basis of morphology. The branches arise in whorls at the nodes and emerge between the leaf sheaths.

- Field horsetail (*E. arvense*): the aerial stems are dimorphic; in spring unbranched, pale reddish-brown fertile shoots (spring shoots) appear first and bear a strobilus at the apex, and wither after the spores are shed; in summer green vegetative shoots (summer shoots) appear with many branches in whorls at the nodes.
- Branched horsetail (*E. ramosissimum*) and scouring rush (*E. hyemale*): the aerial stems are not divided into vegetative and fertile shoots, are green, and bear strobili at the branch tips; the scouring rush has a coarse, hard stem, unbranched or sparsely branched, with especially abundant silica. Chinese textbooks put these evergreen species in the genus *Hippochaete* and species with annual, dimorphic stems in the genus *Equisetum*; PPG I recognizes a single genus, with the two groups as subgenus *Hippochaete* and subgenus *Equisetum*.

Stem structure: in cross section through an internode the outermost layer is the epidermis, with stomata sunk in the furrows; inside it lies a cortex of several cell layers, with well-developed sclerenchyma beneath the ridges and green assimilatory tissue beneath the furrows; in the cortex beneath each furrow is a large cavity, the vallecular canal. Inside the cortex is the endodermis (in some species each bundle has its own). The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring facing the ridges; the xylem is poorly developed and endarch, the protoxylem breaks down during development to form the carinal canal, with the metaxylem on both sides of it; the center of the stem is a large pith cavity (central canal). The cavities of adjacent internodes are separated at the node by a diaphragm and do not communicate; the vascular tissue at the node is continuous in a ring. Chinese textbooks call this stele an arthrostele, or jointed stele ([7.12.16](/7.12.16#sec-16.2.5)).

Strobilus: borne at the tip of a branch, resembling a brush tip, and made up of many sporangiophores. The sporangiophore is a specialized sporophyll, peltate, with a hexagonal shield at the top and a stalk in the middle underneath, attached transversely to the axis of the strobilus; 5–10 sporangia hang around the inner side of the shield, pointing toward the axis, and dehisce longitudinally at maturity. The base of the strobilus often bears a ring of sterile projections (a ring-like collar). According to the telome theory, the sporangiophore arose by the bending and fusion of a fertile syntelome ([7.12.16](/7.12.16#sec-16.4.4)).

Spores and elaters: the spores are homosporous and green, containing chloroplasts; the outermost wall layer (perispore) splits into four ribbon-like **elaters**, slightly widened at both ends, which coil around the spore. The elaters move with humidity, uncoiling when dry and coiling when moist, which helps the sporangium open and the spores be shed, and makes the spores hook together and fall in clumps, so that later male and female gametophytes grow side by side and fertilization is completed. Spores can germinate within 10–12 h under suitable conditions and die within days under poor ones. Chinese textbooks say the elaters are formed by splitting of the "outer spore wall"; in current spore-wall terminology this is the perispore.

Gametophyte: the spore first divides unequally into two cells, the smaller elongating into the first, colorless rhizoid and the larger being a green cell; after a filamentous, spherical or club-shaped stage it forms a green plate-like prothallus with many irregular lobes: the base is a cushion of several cell layers bearing rhizoids below, and the upper lobes are only one cell layer thick. Although the spores are of one kind, the prothalli are often unisexual: in *E. arvense* about half the spores grow into male and half into female gametophytes; with good nutrition they are mostly female and with poor nutrition mostly male; female gametophytes can later also produce antheridia at the margin and become bisexual. Male prothalli are small, with few blunt lobes and antheridia embedded at the lobe tips; in water the cap cells at the tip split open all around and release several hundred sperm, which are spiral with several dozen flagella at the anterior end. The number and shape of the cap cells of the antheridium differ among species (4 in *E. arvense*, 2 in *E. hyemale*) and can serve as an identification character. Female prothalli are large with many lobes, and the archegonia arise at the base of the lobes, with a short neck (3–4 cell layers) projecting from the prothallus. All the archegonia of *E. hyemale* can be fertilized and all the zygotes develop into embryos; the stem apex of the embryo emerges and first produces 3 leaves in a whorl, the radicle enters the substrate, and the prothallus gradually degenerates.

Fossil horsetails: horsetails arose in the Devonian and flourished most in the Carboniferous, when they were among the main plants of the swamp forests and of coal formation.

- *Calamites*: tree-like, up to more than 20 m tall, with a creeping rhizome and erect aerial stems, hollow internodes (pith cavity), whorled branches and narrow leaves at the nodes, and a vascular cambium with secondary xylem; the strobilus consists of alternating whorls of sporangiophores and bracts, each sporangiophore bearing 4 hanging sporangia, and the spores are homosporous or heterosporous. The pith casts formed when the pith cavity of *Calamites* filled with sediment are among the commonest Carboniferous fossils.
- *Sphenophyllum*: a climbing or creeping herb with whorls of 6 (a multiple of 3) wedge-shaped leaves at the nodes and dichotomous venation; the stem has a triarch protostele and secondary xylem; Carboniferous to Permian.
- *Hyenia* and *Calamophyton* of the Devonian, mentioned in Chinese textbooks, were once regarded as ancestors of the horsetails, but are now placed in the Cladoxylopsida, another lineage of early ferns (§17.8.1).

## 17.5. Ophioglossidae

The Ophioglossidae comprise the Psilotales and the Ophioglossales. They differ greatly in appearance but share eusporangiate sporangia, subterranean tuberous or cylindrical gametophytes (nourished through mycorrhiza) and reduced roots (*Psilotum* is rootless, and the roots of *Ophioglossum* are thick and lack root hairs); molecular phylogenies strongly support them as sister groups.

### 17.5.1. Psilotales

The Psilotales contain only the Psilotaceae, with the genera *Psilotum* (2 species) and *Tmesipteris* (about a dozen species, in Australia, New Zealand and the South Pacific islands, mostly epiphytic on tree-fern trunks). Only one species occurs in China, *Psilotum nudum*, in the tropics and subtropics, growing in rock crevices and humus or epiphytic on trees, often cultivated as an ornamental.

Sporophyte: divided into a creeping rhizome and erect aerial shoots. The rhizome is brownish, without roots and with only hair-like rhizoids, and contains symbiotic fungi. The aerial shoots branch dichotomously several times, are reddish-brown at the base and green above, and photosynthesize; the main shoot has 3–5 longitudinal ridges and the twigs are flat or triangular; the epidermis has a cuticle and stomata; the outer cortex is green tissue and the inner cortex sclerenchyma and parenchyma; the stele is a protostele (actinostele) or an exarch siphonostele. The shoots bear scale-like appendages in a spiral, without veins or stomata; on fertile shoots the appendages are forked and bear sporangia in their axils.

Sporangia: yellow, three sporangia fused into a 3-chambered synangium (2-chambered in *Tmesipteris*), with a short stalk, borne in the axil of a forked appendage, thick-walled and dehiscing longitudinally at maturity; the spores are homosporous and reniform.

Gametophyte: develops in humus or rock crevices, cylindrical, irregularly forked and much like the rhizome of the sporophyte, brown, without chlorophyll, densely filled with symbiotic fungal hyphae and bearing unicellular rhizoids on the surface; in some polyploid gametophytes there is a discontinuous stele of tracheids with annular or scalariform thickenings. Antheridia and archegonia protrude from the surface of the gametophyte and the sperm are multiflagellate; the neck of the archegonium is about 6 cell layers high and the upper layers break apart at maturity; embryo development also requires the symbiotic hyphae.

Systematic position: *Psilotum* is rootless, dichotomously branched and bears its sporangia near the shoot tips, and so resembles the Devonian *Rhynia* closely; for more than a hundred years it was regarded as a relict of the rhyniophytes and the most primitive living vascular plant. This view faces two difficulties: *Psilotum* has no fossil record, leaving a gap of 400 million years between it and the Devonian rhyniophytes; and its sporangia are fused into synangia and the origin of its appendages is unclear, unlike the rhyniophytes. Chloroplast and nuclear phylogenies agree that *Psilotum* is the sister group of the moonworts, deep inside the ferns; it has no roots or leaves because it is secondarily simplified from an ancestor with roots and leaves. The reduced roots and subterranean gametophytes of the moonworts are also similar to it. The statement in Chinese textbooks that the absence of roots in *Psilotum* is a primitive character and not a reduction no longer stands in the face of molecular evidence.

### 17.5.2. Ophioglossales

The Ophioglossales contain only the Ophioglossaceae, with the subfamilies Ophioglossoideae, Botrychioideae, Helminthostachyoideae and Mankyuoideae, about 10 genera and more than 110 species; Chinese textbooks divide them into the Ophioglossaceae, Botrychiaceae and Helminthostachyaceae.

The sporophyte is a small herb with a short stem deeply buried in the soil and fleshy roots without root hairs, with mycorrhiza. Usually only one leaf is produced each year, consisting of a vegetative part (trophophore) and a fertile part (sporophore) arising from its base or adaxial surface; the young leaf is not coiled. The sporangia are large, thick-walled and transversely dehiscent, with tetrahedral spores, which are numerous (thousands per sporangium). The gametophyte is tuberous, subterranean and perennial, without chlorophyll and in symbiosis with fungi, living in the soil for many years before appearing above ground; it is monoecious, the antheridia and archegonia are mostly embedded in the gametophyte tissue, and the sperm are multiflagellate. The Ophioglossaceae (*Botrychium*) are among the few living ferns that have secondary growth (a vascular cambium).

- *Ophioglossum*: a single simple trophophore, ovate or lanceolate, entire, with reticulate venation; a single sporophore with two rows of sporangia sunk in the rachis of the spike. About 6 species occur in China, the common ones being adder's-tongue (*O. vulgatum*) and *O. thermale*. *O. reticulatum* has a somatic chromosome number of about 1440 (recorded by Khandelwal 1990, $2n \approx 1440$), among the highest known in any organism (§17.8.3).
- *Botrychium* s.l.: the trophophore is once to many times pinnately divided, the sporophore is paniculate and the sporangia are not sunk. The common Chinese species is *Sceptridium ternatum*, a medicinal plant; moonwort (*B. lunaria*) grows in high mountains.
- *Helminthostachys zeylanica*: the trophophore is palmately trifoliate and the sporangia on the sporophore are gathered into spikelets; it occurs in southern China and is a Class II nationally protected wild plant.

## 17.6. Marattiidae

The Marattiidae contain only the Marattiaceae of the Marattiales, with 6 genera and about 110 species, including *Angiopteris*, *Marattia* s.l., *Christensenia*, *Danaea* and *Ptisana*, in the tropics and subtropics; Chinese textbooks call the order Angiopteridales and divide it into the Angiopteridaceae, Marattiaceae and Christenseniaceae.

*Angiopteris* is the representative genus. The rhizome is fleshy, tuberous, half buried in the soil and erect; the leaves are large, once to twice pinnately compound and up to several meters long (the leaf of *A. evecta* is 5–7 m long with a petiole about 2 m long, making it one of the largest ferns); the petiole base bears a pair of fleshy stipules, and after the leaves fall the stipules and petiole bases persist around the rhizome in the shape of a lotus base, whence the Chinese name "Guanyin lotus seat"; the stipules are rich in starch and can grow into new plants. The sporangia form no sporophore but are packed in two rows on either side of the veins near the margin on the underside of the leaf, forming linear sori without indusia; the sporangial wall is several layers thick, with a tapetum and sporogenous tissue inside (eusporangiate). In *Marattia* and *Christensenia* the sporangia are fused into synangia. The gametophyte is green and large, ribbon- or heart-shaped, more than 2 cm long, with a multilayered midrib, long-lived, and contains endophytic fungi; the antheridia and archegonia are sunk into the prothallus tissue and the sperm are multiflagellate.

*A. fokiensis* is common in China, growing under forests in southern mountains, and its rhizome is edible and also ornamental. The tree fern *Psaronius* of the late Carboniferous, up to 8 m tall, belongs to the marattioid ferns; its trunk was supported by a mantle of thickening adventitious roots, and it was one of the dominant plants of the swamp forests of the time (§17.8.1).

## 17.7. Polypodiidae (Leptosporangiate Ferns)

The Polypodiidae are the main body of living ferns, with about 10,000 species; their synapomorphy is the leptosporangium (Definition 17.2.3).

### 17.7.1. Osmundales

The Osmundales contain only the Osmundaceae, of which PPG I recognizes 6 genera and about 18 species. In *Osmunda* the sporophyte has a short, stout, erect or ascending rhizome covered with persistent petiole bases; the leaves are clustered at the stem apex, the young leaves are coiled and covered with brown tomentum that falls off at maturity, and the leaves are once to twice pinnately compound. In some species the trophophylls and sporophylls are separate (Japanese royal fern *O. japonica*, whose sporophyll has its pinnules reduced to lines densely covered with sporangia, reddish-brown and without chlorophyll, and withers before the trophophylls); in others the sterile and fertile pinnae differ on one leaf (interrupted fern *Claytosmunda claytoniana*, with fertile pinnae in the middle of the leaf). The sporangia are fairly large, form no sori, have no indusium and are borne on the margin of the fertile pinnae; a sporangium arises from a single initial cell and its stalk is formed by many cells, the wall has one layer, and one side bears a group of thickened cells forming a subapical peltate annulus, from which the sporangium splits longitudinally toward the side of the stalk. The spores are tetrahedral, green and contain chloroplasts, and germinate soon after being shed (a life of about a month at room temperature). The stele of the rhizome is an ectophloic siphonostele with mesarch xylem.

The gametophyte of *Osmunda* is a green heart-shaped prothallus with no filamentous stage and a thick midrib (6–7 cell layers); the antheridia are large, the wall consists of 7–9 cells and the cap cell is divided into two, and each antheridium has only about 50 sperm; the archegonia are large, the neck is 6 cell layers high and perpendicular to the prothallus, and at maturity the cells at the front of the neck all spread apart. These differences from the Polypodiales (antheridial wall of 3 cells, neck of 4–5 layers inclined backward) reflect the primitiveness of *Osmunda* (observations of Wenmei Bao and colleagues).

The Osmundaceae have a long fossil record: in Early Jurassic osmundaceous rhizomes from Sweden, nuclei and chromosomes are preserved, and their nuclear size is the same as in the living *Osmundastrum cinnamomeum*, showing that the genome size of this group has hardly changed in 180 million years (Bomfleur et al. 2014).

About 8 species occur in China, the common ones being *O. japonica*, *Plenasium vachellii* and *Osmundastrum cinnamomeum* (formerly *O. cinnamomea*, cinnamon fern). The young leaves of *O. japonica* are a wild vegetable; the rhizome and petiole remains are the Chinese drug "Ziqi Guanzhong".

### 17.7.2. Hymenophyllales, Gleicheniales and Schizaeales

Hymenophyllales: only the Hymenophyllaceae (filmy ferns), about 430 species, mostly on the damp trunks and rocks of tropical rain forests. The blade is as thin as a membrane, only one cell layer thick apart from the veins, without stomata or cuticle, and absorbs water directly from humid air, so the plants can live only in permanently saturated humidity, although some species tolerate repeated drying. The sori are at the vein ends on the leaf margin, enclosed in a tubular or two-lipped involucre, the receptacle elongates and the sporangia mature in succession; the annulus is oblique. Common in China are *Hymenophyllum* and *Crepidomanes minutum* (formerly *Gonocormus minutus*, with a cup-shaped involucre).

Gleicheniales: the Gleicheniaceae, Dipteridaceae and Matoniaceae, mostly originating in the Mesozoic. In the Gleicheniaceae there is a dormant bud at the top of the petiole and the rachis branches pseudodichotomously (a dormant bud at each fork), allowing unlimited growth into large thickets; the sori are round, without indusium, composed of a few large sporangia, and the annulus is transverse. *Dicranopteris pedata* (called *D. dichotoma* in Chinese textbooks) often grows in extensive stands on acid soils in southern China and is an indicator of acid soil, and its petioles can be woven; *Diplopterygium glaucum* is similar. *Dipteris conjugata* has a blade deeply divided in two into a fan shape and is a Jurassic relict.

Schizaeales: the Lygodiaceae, Schizaeaceae and Anemiaceae, with an apical annulus. The "vine" of *Lygodium japonicum* is in fact a single leaf: the rachis grows without limit and twines and climbs to more than 4 m, the pinnae on the rachis are divided into vegetative and fertile pinnae, the pinnules of the fertile pinnae bear fringe-like spikes of sporangia on their margins, and each sporangium is covered by an indusium-like scale; the spores of *L. japonicum* are the Chinese drug "Haijinsha", used against urinary stones. The gametophyte of *Schizaea* is filamentous.

### 17.7.3. Salviniales

The Salviniales are aquatic or marsh heterosporous ferns whose sporangia have no annulus and are borne in sporocarps; they are the only heterosporous group among the ferns, and heterospory arose here independently of the lycophytes and the seed plants. PPG I includes two families, Marsileaceae and Salviniaceae.

*Marsilea quadrifolia* (water clover, Marsileaceae) is a herb of shallow water or marshes, growing in paddy fields, streams, ditches and ponds. The rhizome is slender and creeping, dichotomously branched, able to grow indefinitely, and bears adventitious roots on its ventral side; the stem has an amphiphloic siphonostele and air cavities in the cortex. The leaves have a long petiole and are coiled when young; the blade consists of 4 obtriangular leaflets arranged in a cross, shaped like the Chinese character for "field", with veins forking dichotomously outward from the base of the leaflet and joined into a network; in plants growing in water the petiole is long, slender and soft and lengthens as the water rises, so that the blades float on the surface, while in plants of shallow water or wet ground the petiole is short and stout. The sporocarps are borne at the base of the petiole, oblong-reniform, green and densely hairy when young, brown-black and hard when mature; a sporocarp is formed from a modified pinna, and its wall encloses many sori, each sorus containing both megasporangia and microsporangia, a megasporangium containing a single megaspore and a microsporangium many microspores. The sporocarp does not open until the second or third year: when it opens, the gelatinous ring inside the sporocarp swells with water and extends out, bringing out all the sori attached to it; after the sporangial walls gelatinize, the megaspores and microspores fall into the water and develop into female and male gametophytes inside the spore wall. The male gametophyte has two antheridia and multiflagellate sperm (unlike the biflagellate sperm of *Selaginella*); the female gametophyte forms a single archegonium at its apex, and after fertilization the embryo develops in place within the megaspore wall. The sporocarps of *Marsilea* tolerate drought and can germinate after storage dry for decades. According to Bao, the *Marsilea* south of the Yangtze River is mostly *M. crenata*.

*Salvinia natans* (floating fern, Salviniaceae) is a small floating plant of ponds, lakes, paddy fields and still water. The stem is prostrate, hairy and rootless. At each node there are three leaves in a whorl: the two upper leaves are oblong and float on the water, their surface densely covered with papillae whose terminal hairs make the leaf hydrophobic and hard to wet; the lower leaf is finely divided into a root-like structure hanging in the water, called the submerged leaf, which takes over the function of roots. The sporocarps are borne in clusters on short stalks at the base of the submerged leaf and are of two kinds: the megasporocarps are smaller and contain 8–10 megasporangia, each with a single megaspore; the microsporocarps are larger and contain many long-stalked microsporangia, each with 64 microspores. The wall of the sporocarp is formed from a modified indusium. The male gametophyte germinates inside the microsporangium, elongates and breaks through the sporangial wall, and forms two antheridia at its tip, each producing 4 sperm; the female gametophyte develops within the megaspore wall and forms several archegonia at its apex, of which only one egg develops into an embryo.

*Azolla pinnata* subsp. *asiatica* (mosquito fern, Salviniaceae; also called red or green azolla) is a small floating plant whose body is triangular, rhomboid or round and floats on the water surface. The rhizome is slender and pinnately branched, with roots hanging in the water. The leaves are small, sessile and imbricate, and each is deeply divided into an upper and a lower lobe: the upper lobe floats on the water, contains chloroplasts and photosynthesizes; the lower lobe sinks obliquely into the water, is colorless and has an absorptive function. A cavity at the base of the upper lobe contains mucilage in which lives the nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium *Anabaena azollae* (now often called *Nostoc azollae*). This symbiosis is obligate and inherited from generation to generation: the cyanobacterium is passed to the next generation with the sporocarp and has lost the ability to live independently. The leaves of *Azolla* contain anthocyanin, green when young and turning red in autumn and winter, which gives a surface of red water and hence the name. The sporocarps are borne in pairs on the lobe of the first submerged leaf of a lateral branch; the microsporocarp is large and spherical and contains many microsporangia, in which the 64 microspores are embedded in several massulae with anchor-shaped glochidia on their surface; the megasporocarp is small and long-ovoid, with only one megasporangium containing one megaspore, with several massulae at its apex. The glochidia of the microspore massulae hook onto the megaspore, so that the sperm of the male gametophyte can enter the archegonium of the female gametophyte close by. The dry weight of *Azolla* contains up to about 4.6% nitrogen, and together with *Anabaena* it is a traditional green manure of paddy fields in southern China and a feed for livestock, ducks and fish. *A. filiculoides* is native to the Americas and has escaped from cultivation after introduction to China.

The nitrogen fixation of *Azolla* may have had global effects in geological history: in the Eocene (about 49 million years ago) the surface of the Arctic Ocean was briefly freshened and *Azolla* multiplied massively for about 800,000 years, its remains sinking to the anoxic sea floor; it has been suggested that this "*Azolla* event" buried large amounts of carbon and contributed to the later fall in atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$ and the cooling of the climate (Brinkhuis et al. 2006). The physiology of nitrogen fixation and symbiosis is treated in [7.11.31](/7.11.31).

### 17.7.4. Cyatheales

The Cyatheales comprise 8 families and about 700 species, most of them tree ferns: the stem is erect and stout, up to ten meters or more tall, bearing at its apex a cluster of large twice- to thrice-pinnate leaves, and the trunk is wrapped in dense adventitious roots and persistent petiole bases. The sori are on the underside of the leaf and the annulus is oblique and uninterrupted.

- Cyatheaceae: sori round, on the veinlets, with or without indusium; the stem has a polycyclic dictyostele. *Alsophila spinulosa*, up to 6–8 m tall, has prickly petioles and rachises and occurs in southern China; it is a living representative of a group present since the Jurassic, and most species of Cyatheaceae are Class II nationally protected wild plants.
- Cibotiaceae: the rhizome of *Cibotium barometz* is large and covered with golden-yellow long soft hairs, resembling a little dog; the sori are at the vein ends on the margin of the lobes, and the indusium is two-valved, clam-shaped. The rhizome is the Chinese drug "Gouji" and the hairs stop bleeding; it is a Class II nationally protected wild plant.
- Dicksoniaceae: indusium clam-shaped, as in the Australian soft tree fern (*Dicksonia antarctica*), often cultivated.
- Plagiogyriaceae: leaves dimorphic, the pinnae of the sporophyll narrowed into lines, as in *Plagiogyria euphlebia*.

### 17.7.5. Polypodiales

The Polypodiales are the largest order of ferns, with about 8700 species, the great majority terrestrial or epiphytic and homosporous; the sporangium has a slender stalk (one to three rows of cells), the vertical annulus is interrupted near the stalk and bears lip cells, and sorus morphology is extremely diverse. PPG I divides the order into 6 suborders and 26 families, among which two large clades, the Aspleniineae (eupolypods II) and the Polypodiineae (eupolypods I) together comprise about two thirds of fern species. ## 17.8. Origin and Evolution of the Ferns

### 17.8.1. Paleozoic and Mesozoic Ferns

Chinese textbooks held that the rhyniophytes evolved in the Devonian along three lines, lycophytes, horsetails and true ferns: the lycophytes through *Baragwanathia*, the horsetails through *Hyenia* and *Calamophyton*, and the true ferns through *Protopteridium* and *Archaeopteris*. Current understanding differs in three respects. First, the lycophytes arose from the zosterophylls and separated from the other two lines as early as the Silurian ([7.12.16](/7.12.16#sec-16.3.3)); the horsetails and true ferns both lie within the euphyllophytes and arose from trimerophyte-like ancestors. Second, *Archaeopteris* is a progymnosperm with secondary xylem and a eustele, a close relative of the seed plants and not a fern ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)); *Protopteridium* from Yunnan in the Chinese textbooks also belongs to the aneurophytalean progymnosperms, and its type species has been transferred to *Rellimia*. Third, *Hyenia* and *Calamophyton* belong to the cladoxylopsid ferns and are not direct ancestors of the horsetails.

Several lineages of early ferns existed from the Middle to the Late Devonian:

- Cladoxylopsida: the stem has several radially arranged xylem strands and the ends of the lateral branches are forked; they were among the earliest trees. The "fossil forest" at Gilboa, New York, consists of them, among which *Wattieza* was about 8 m tall with a crown made of branches rather than leaves (about 385 million years ago, Stein et al. 2007).
- Iridopteridales, Zygopteridales and *Rhacophyton*: the lateral branch systems begin to flatten, and some already have a planar, pinnate structure.

The Carboniferous was the "age of ferns": the tree fern *Psaronius* (a marattioid fern), the horsetails *Calamites* and *Sphenophyllum*, and arborescent lycophytes together formed the tropical swamp forests; many fossil "fern fronds" in fact belong to seed ferns ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)). The drought and mass extinction at the Permian–Triassic boundary eliminated most Paleozoic ferns; from the Late Triassic to the Jurassic the earlier-diverging leptosporangiate ferns, the Osmundaceae, Gleicheniaceae, Matoniaceae, Dipteridaceae, Schizaeales and others, diversified rapidly, and many families and genera persist to the present; the Cyatheales also appeared in the Jurassic.

### 17.8.2. In the Shadow of the Angiosperms

The angiosperms arose in the Cretaceous and replaced the gymnosperms and ferns as the dominant plants, and it was once thought that ferns have since been merely a declining relict. Molecular clock analyses reached the opposite conclusion: most families of the Polypodiales (about 80% of living ferns) radiated only after the Cretaceous, once angiosperm forests had formed (Schneider et al. 2004, "ferns diversified in the shadow of angiosperms"). Angiosperm forests created new habitats: the dim, humid forest floor beneath the closed canopy and abundant epiphytic sites on angiosperm trunks and branches; epiphytic Polypodiaceae, Aspleniaceae and vittarioid ferns diversified mainly in the Cenozoic (Schuettpelz and Pryer 2009). One molecular character associated with this radiation is a novel photoreceptor, neochrome (a chimeric red and blue light receptor), which may help ferns grow in the weak light under angiosperm forests; interestingly, this fern gene was acquired from hornworts by horizontal gene transfer (Li et al. 2014).

### 17.8.3. Chromosome Numbers and Polyploidy

The chromosome numbers of homosporous ferns are exceptionally high: the mean haploid chromosome number of homosporous ferns is about 57, against about 16 in angiosperms (Klekowski and Baker 1966); *Ophioglossum reticulatum* has $2n \approx 1440$. Heterosporous ferns have much lower numbers (*Marsilea* $n = 20$, *Salvinia* $n = 9$, *Azolla* $n = 22$). One explanation is that the gametophytes of homosporous ferns are bisexual and self-fertilization would make the sporophyte completely homozygous, and repeated polyploidization can preserve genetic variation through pairing between homologous chromosomes; another is that the frequency of polyploidization in ferns is no higher than in angiosperms but diploidization and loss of chromosomes rarely follow polyploidization. Apogamy and apospory (§17.3.3) provide routes to the formation of polyploids, and hybridization and polyploidization are important modes of speciation in ferns ([7.10.12](/7.10.12)).

## 17.9. Economic Value

Ferns are closely related to human life. Ancient ferns and lycophytes formed great quantities of coal, and living ferns have many uses.

The safety of eating bracken needs attention: bracken (*Pteridium*) contains ptaquiloside, a sesquiterpene glycoside that forms a strong alkylating agent under alkaline conditions, damages DNA and is carcinogenic in animal experiments; cattle that eat large amounts of bracken develop hematuria and bladder tumors, and epidemiological studies suggest that long-term heavy consumption of bracken may increase the risk of stomach and esophageal cancer in humans. Traditional soaking in rice-washing water or plant-ash water, or blanching, removes part of the ptaquiloside but not all of it, so bracken should not be eaten in large amounts over a long period.

## Summary

Ferns (Polypodiopsida in PPG I) are the sister group of the seed plants, and together they make up the euphyllophytes; the traditional division Pteridophyta includes the lycophytes and is paraphyletic. The fern sporophyte has a rhizome, adventitious roots and megaphylls, and the young leaves are mostly coiled; the sporangia are of two kinds, eusporangiate (horsetails, whisk ferns, moonworts, marattioid ferns) and leptosporangiate (Polypodiidae, with an annulus); the form of the sori and indusia and the position of the annulus are the main bases of classification; the gametophyte is an independent prothallus, and fertilization needs water.

## Questions

1. Why is the "division Pteridophyta" a paraphyletic group? How do the positions of *Psilotum* and horsetails in PPG I differ from those in the Chinese textbooks?

   **Answer** Molecular and morphological evidence shows that living vascular plants fall into the lycophytes and the euphyllophytes, and the euphyllophytes into the ferns and the seed plants; the traditional Pteridophyta includes the lycophytes and the ferns but excludes another descendant group of their common ancestor (the seed plants), so it is paraphyletic. Chinese textbooks regarded *Psilotum* as a relict of the rhyniophytes (subdivision Psilophytina) and placed the horsetails in their own subdivision, Sphenophytina; in PPG I *Psilotum* is the sister group of the moonworts (Ophioglossidae) and the horsetails are the earliest-diverging lineage of the ferns (Equisetidae), both lying within the ferns, and their simple morphology (no roots, scale-like leaves) is the result of secondary simplification.

2. Compare eusporangia and leptosporangia, and explain the structure of the annulus and the process of spore ejection. Why must the annulus "stop" on its way back?

   **Answer** A eusporangium arises from several surface cells together, has a wall of several layers, many spores and no annulus; a leptosporangium arises from a single cell, has a wall of one layer, a slender stalk, few spores (usually 64) and an annulus (Definitions 17.2.3, 17.2.4). In the Polypodiales the annulus runs vertically over the top of the sporangium, with thickened inner and lateral walls and thin outer walls; when the spores are ripe the annulus loses water, the negative pressure in the cells makes it curl outward, the sporangial wall splits transversely at the lip cells, and the annulus stores elastic energy; when the negative pressure reaches its limit the water cavitates, the annulus snaps back in about 10 µs, and at about half way it is held by water in the cell walls and then returns slowly on a millisecond time scale. The mid-way braking is like the cross-bar of a catapult, so that the spores in the "spoon" leave the annulus at maximum speed instead of returning to the original position with it.

3. Use bracken to describe the life cycle of ferns, and compare it with those of bryophytes and *Selaginella*.

   **Answer** The sporophyte (2n) of bracken is large, with linear sori along the leaf margin on the underside; spore mother cells undergo meiosis to produce homosporous spores (n); a spore germinates and grows through a filament and a plate into a heart-shaped prothallus bearing rhizoids, antheridia and archegonia on the ventral side; multiflagellate sperm swim in water into the archegonium and fertilize the egg, the zygote develops on the prothallus into an embryo with cotyledon, radicle, foot and stem apex, and the prothallus dies after the young sporophyte becomes independent. Compared with bryophytes, in bracken the sporophyte is dominant and independent, and the gametophyte is also independent but very small; compared with *Selaginella*, bracken is homosporous with a bisexual gametophyte living independently outside the spore wall, whereas *Selaginella* is heterosporous with unisexual gametophytes developing within the spore wall.

4. Why can *Azolla* be used as a green manure? How do the sporocarps of *Marsilea* and *Salvinia* differ?

   **Answer** An obligate symbiotic nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium, *Anabaena azollae*, lives in the cavity at the base of the upper lobe of the *Azolla* leaf and is passed on from generation to generation with the sporocarps; the plant multiplies quickly, its dry weight contains about 4.6% nitrogen, and when plowed into a paddy field it serves as nitrogen fertilizer. The sporocarp of *Marsilea* is formed from a modified pinna and borne at the base of the petiole, and one sporocarp contains many sori that each have both megasporangia and microsporangia, which are brought out by the gelatinous ring swollen with water when the sporocarp opens; the sporocarps of *Salvinia* (and *Azolla*) are formed from a modified indusium and are unisexual, the megasporocarp containing only a few (in *Azolla*, one) megasporangia and the microsporocarp many microsporangia.

5. (Open question) Is the statement "ferns declined after the rise of the angiosperms" correct? Discuss in light of the fossil and molecular evidence.

   **Answer** Not entirely. The Paleozoic ferns typified by tree ferns and *Calamites* did decline in the Permian–Triassic, and many Mesozoic ferns also declined with changes in gymnosperm forests; but molecular clock analyses show that most families of the Polypodiales, to which about 80% of living ferns belong, radiated after the Cretaceous, once angiosperm forests had formed (Schneider et al. 2004). Angiosperm forests provided a dim forest floor and abundant epiphytic habitats, and the epiphytic Polypodiaceae, Aspleniaceae and others diversified mainly in the Cenozoic; the novel photoreceptor neochrome may help them adapt to the weak light under the forest. Modern ferns therefore arose to a large extent "in the shadow of the angiosperms" and are not merely relicts.
