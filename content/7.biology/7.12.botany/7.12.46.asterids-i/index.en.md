The **asterids** are the largest clade of angiosperms, with about 100 families and nearly 100,000 species, about a third of all angiosperm species. Its core is the traditional "sympetalous" group: de Jussieu's (1789) Monopetalae, the Sympetalae of the Engler system and Cronquist's Asteridae, that is, plants whose petals are fused into a tube and whose stamens are borne on the corolla. Molecular systematics shows that the sympetalous plants form a largely natural group but that its circumscription must be enlarged: Theales, Ericales, Ebenales and Primulales, which Cronquist placed in the Dilleniidae, and Cornales, Apiales, Aquifoliaceae and Balsaminaceae, which he placed in the Rosidae, all belong to the asterids ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.5.2)). The asterids fall into four branches: Cornales, Ericales, the lamiids and the campanulids, the last two together forming the euasterids. This chapter treats the earliest-diverging Cornales and Ericales, many of whose members still have free petals but already have the ovule and chemical characters of the asterids; the lamiids are treated in [7.12.47](/7.12.47)–[7.12.48](/7.12.48) and the campanulids in [7.12.49](/7.12.49). The chapter first treats the circumscription and characters of the asterids (§46.1), then Cornales (§46.2) and Ericales (§46.3).

## 46.1. Circumscription and Synapomorphies of the Asterids

**Definition 46.1.1 (Asterids)** The monophyletic group formed in the APG system by Cornales, Ericales, the lamiids (Icacinales, Metteniusales, Garryales, Boraginales, Gentianales, Vahliales, Solanales, Lamiales) and the campanulids (Aquifoliales, Asterales, Escalloniales, Bruniales, Paracryphiales, Dipsacales, Apiales) is called the **asterids**; in phylogenetic nomenclature it is the Asteridae.

The monophyly of the asterids is strongly supported by almost all molecular analyses. Its morphological and chemical characters are a set of traits that often occur together but all have exceptions, and they do not draw a sharp boundary:

| Character | Situation in the asterids | Exceptions and comments |
|---|---|---|
| Sympetalous corolla | The euasterids are almost all sympetalous, the corolla developing either from a ring-shaped primordium (the "early sympetaly" of the lamiids) or by later fusion of separate petal primordia (the "late sympetaly" of the campanulids) | Many families of Cornales and Ericales have free petals; Plumbaginaceae, Caricaceae and Cucurbitaceae outside the asterids evolved a sympetalous corolla independently |
| Stamens borne on the corolla (epipetalous), as many as the corolla lobes and alternate with them | General in the euasterids | In Ericales the stamens are often twice the petals in number and free of the corolla; in Primulaceae the stamens are opposite the corolla lobes |
| Unitegmic, tenuinucellate ovule | The ancestor of the asterids probably had a unitegmic, tenuinucellate ovule ([7.12.28](/7.12.28#sec-28.5.2)) | In Ericales most revert to bitegmic ovules, while Ericaceae and Actinidiaceae are unitegmic again |
| Cellular endosperm, often with haustoria at both ends | Most | Primulaceae and Polemoniaceae have nuclear endosperm |
| Iridoids | Almost all angiosperms that produce iridoids are in the asterids ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.7)) | Most of Solanales and Ericales do not produce them; Solanales has tropane alkaloids instead |
| Pollen shed at the three-celled stage | Most | — |

The concept of the "sympetalous group" as a taxon is therefore right, but the sympetalous corolla itself arose independently at least several times; a more reliable mark of the asterids is the ovule and chemical characters. Cronquist already used iridoids to delimit the Asteridae, in rough agreement with the asterids of APG.

## 46.2. Cornales

### 46.2.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Cornales are mostly woody plants with simple, often opposite leaves; the flowers are often 4–5-merous, with small sepals or sepals reduced to teeth; the ovary is inferior or half-inferior, with an epigynous disc; the fruit is mostly a drupe whose stone has a germination pore or germination valve (from which it splits when the seed germinates); the basic chromosome number is often 11. Possible synapomorphies are the inferior ovary, the epigynous disc and the small sepals.

APG IV Cornales has 7 families: Cornaceae, Nyssaceae, Hydrangeaceae, Loasaceae (American herbs with stinging hairs), Hydrostachyaceae (submerged plants of Africa and Madagascar), Curtisiaceae and Grubbiaceae (two small families of South Africa). It falls into two clades: Cornaceae with the two small South African families form one, and Nyssaceae, Hydrangeaceae, Loasaceae and Hydrostachyaceae form the other.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Cornales included Cornaceae, Alangiaceae, Nyssaceae and Garryaceae (including Aucuba). In APG IV Alangiaceae is merged into Cornaceae; *Aucuba* and Garryaceae move to Garryales of the lamiids ([7.12.47](/7.12.47)), and *Helwingia* and *Torricellia* to Aquifoliales and Apiales of the campanulids ([7.12.49](/7.12.49)); Hydrangeaceae (formerly in a broadly circumscribed Saxifragaceae) and Loasaceae (formerly in Violales) move in. APG II and APG III merged Nyssaceae into Cornaceae, and APG IV restores it as a separate family.

### 46.2.2. Cornaceae

In APG IV Cornaceae has only 2 genera and about 85 species: *Cornus* and *Alangium*.

*Cornus* consists of trees or shrubs; leaves simple and opposite (rarely alternate), entire, with arcuate lateral veins that curve toward the leaf tip (when the blade is pulled apart, the spiral vessels of the lateral veins draw out as white threads, a good field character); the hairs are often T-shaped with arms of equal length; flowers small, 4-merous, in cymes, umbels or heads, and in some species the inflorescence is subtended by 4 large, white, petal-like bracts; sepals 4, small; petals 4, valvate; stamens 4, alternate with the petals; epigynous disc present; ovary inferior, 2-locular, with 1 pendent ovule per locule; fruit a drupe. Floral formula: $\ast K_4 C_4 A_4 \overline{G}_{(2:2:1)}$.

Chinese textbooks often split *Cornus* in the broad sense into several genera, which modern classifications mostly treat as subgenera of one broad genus:

1. Japanese cornel (*Cornus officinalis*): a deciduous small tree with umbels that open before the leaves, subtended by 4 small yellow-green bracts, and yellow flowers; the drupe is oblong and red, and the flesh is used medicinally; it occurs from East and Central China to the south of Northwest China.
2. Kousa dogwood (*C. kousa* subsp. *chinensis*, often given as *Dendrobenthamia japonica* var. *chinensis* in Chinese textbooks): the head is subtended by 4 large white bracts and looks like a single large flower; the drupes fuse into a spherical red, edible multiple fruit. Flowering dogwood (*C. florida*) of North America is a common ornamental tree in the eastern United States.
3. Giant dogwood (*C. controversa*, often given as *Bothrocaryum controversum* in Chinese textbooks): leaves alternate (rare in the genus), branches spreading in whorls, tier upon tier like a lamp stand; inflorescence a corymbose cyme without bracts.
4. Tatarian dogwood (*C. alba*) has blood-red twigs, ornamental in winter; the seeds of *C. macrophylla* and *C. walteri* contain oil, and they are woody oil plants.

*Alangium* has about 40 species in the tropics and subtropics of the Old World. The leaves are alternate, often oblique at the base and palmately veined; petals 4–10, narrow, recurved when the flower opens; stamens as many as the petals; drupe. *A. chinense* is a deciduous small tree with leaves of variable shape (entire or 3–7-lobed) and fragrant white flowers; it occurs south of the Yangtze, and the roots and rootlets are used medicinally (poisonous). *Alangium* and *Cornus* are similar in floral morphology and embryology and differ in chemistry (*Alangium* has isoquinoline alkaloids) and in some morphological characters; molecular evidence shows that they are sister groups.

### 46.2.3. Nyssaceae

In APG IV Nyssaceae has 5 genera and about 37 species: *Nyssa*, *Davidia*, *Camptotheca*, and *Diplopanax* and *Mastixia*, which were formerly treated as the separate family Mastixiaceae. Deciduous trees with simple, alternate leaves; flowers unisexual or polygamous, on dioecious or monoecious plants, often in heads; calyx small, petals 5 or absent; ovary inferior, 1-locular (or 6–10-locular), with 1 ovule per locule; drupe or samara, the stone with germination valves that open from the apex.

1. Dove tree (*Davidia involucrata*): a deciduous tree endemic to China, in the mountains of Sichuan, Hubei, Hunan, Guizhou and Yunnan. A head of many male flowers and 1 bisexual flower is subtended by 2 large, creamy white bracts (up to 15–20 cm long), and when the tree is in flower the bracts cover it like perched white doves, whence the name "dove tree". The bracts are green at the beginning of flowering and then turn white, and they can shelter the pollen from rain (Sun et al. 2008) and are also a visual signal that attracts pollinators (bees). The drupe is oblong and purple-green. The dove tree was discovered in 1869 at Baoxing, Sichuan, by the French missionary Armand David, for whom the genus is named; in the early twentieth century E. H. Wilson introduced seeds to Europe. It is a Tertiary relict (fossil fruits are known from the Paleocene of North America) and a first-class nationally protected wild plant; the separate family Davidiaceae sometimes used in Chinese textbooks is no longer adopted.
2. Happy tree (*Camptotheca acuminata*): a deciduous tree endemic to China south of the Yangtze; the heads are spherical and the fruit is a samara, many of them clustered into a spherical infructescence. The roots, bark and fruit contain camptothecin, an inhibitor of DNA topoisomerase I, isolated from the tree by Wall and Wani in 1966; irinotecan and topotecan, developed with camptothecin as the lead compound, are important anticancer drugs for colon and ovarian cancer and others.
3. *Nyssa*: an East Asian–North American disjunct genus; black tupelo (*N. sylvatica*) and water tupelo (*N. aquatica*) of North America grow in swamps; *N. sinensis* of China occurs south of the Yangtze and has brilliant red autumn leaves.

### 46.2.4. Hydrangeaceae

Hydrangeaceae has about 17 genera and 220 species, chiefly in the northern temperate zone and the subtropics; China has about 11 genera and 120 species. Cronquist (and Chinese textbooks) treated it as the subfamily Hydrangeoideae of a broad Saxifragaceae or as a separate family in Rosales; molecular evidence shows that it belongs to Cornales and is the sister group of the American Loasaceae, both containing special iridoids.

Diagnostic characters: shrubs, rarely small trees or climbers; leaves simple and opposite, without stipules; flowers bisexual, 4–5-merous, in corymbose or paniculate cymes; the margin of the inflorescence often bears sterile flowers whose sepals are large and petal-like; petals 4–5, valvate; stamens 8–10 to numerous; ovary inferior or half-inferior, 2–5 carpels fused, with free styles; capsule, opening at the apex. Floral formula: $\ast K_{4\text{–}5} C_{4\text{–}5} A_{8\text{–}10} \overline{G}_{(2\text{–}5)}$.

Representative plants:

1. *Hydrangea*: bigleaf hydrangea (*H. macrophylla*) is native to China and Japan, and in the cultivars the inflorescence consists almost entirely of large sterile flowers and is spherical; flower color changes with the acidity of the soil: in acidic soil aluminum ions dissolve, are taken up by the plant and combine with the anthocyanin (delphinidin) in the sepal cells to form a blue complex, so that the flowers turn blue, whereas in neutral or alkaline soil they are pink. In wild bigleaf hydrangea and panicled hydrangea (*H. paniculata*) the inflorescence has only a few sterile flowers at the margin and fertile small flowers in the middle: the large sterile flowers attract pollinating insects and the small fertile flowers set fruit, a division of labor in the inflorescence.
2. *Deutzia*: leaves opposite with stellate hairs; flowers white, 5-merous, stamens 10 with often winged filaments. *D. scabra* and *D. grandiflora* are widely cultivated as ornamentals.
3. *Philadelphus*: flowers white, 4-merous, with numerous stamens, fragrant. *P. pekinensis* is a traditional flowering shrub of the imperial gardens of Beijing; *P. incanus* occurs from the northwest to central China.
4. *Dichroa febrifuga*: a shrub whose roots contain febrifugine, a traditional Chinese antimalarial; a derivative of febrifugine, halofuginone, is now used in veterinary medicine against coccidiosis.

## 46.3. Ericales

### 46.3.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

APG IV Ericales has 22 families and about 12,000 species and is a surprisingly large order established by molecular systematics: it is made up of six orders of Cronquist's Dilleniidae (Theales, Lecythidales, Ericales, Diapensiales, Ebenales, Primulales) and Sarraceniaceae, plus Polemoniaceae of the Asteridae and Balsaminaceae of the Rosidae. Morphologically, Ericales are mostly woody plants; leaves simple and alternate, with a characteristic glandular tooth at the margin (a vein enters the tooth and ends in an opaque gland or a deciduous cap, the theoid tooth); corolla free or sympetalous; stamens often twice as many as petals or numerous, often free of the corolla (unlike the epipetalous stamens of the euasterids); placentation often axile with a prominent, thickened placenta; ellagic acid absent.

The relationships among the families of Ericales are mostly unresolved, and only a few clades are strongly supported:

| Clade | Families | Characters | Treated in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balsaminoid clade | Balsaminaceae, Marcgraviaceae, Tetrameristaceae | Sister group of the rest of Ericales; specialized nectaries on the leaves, sepals or petals | §46.3.2 |
| Lecythidaceae | Lecythidaceae | Stamens numerous, large tropical trees | §46.3.10 |
| Polemonioid clade | Polemoniaceae, Fouquieriaceae | Herbs or desert shrubs | §46.3.3 |
| Primuloid clade | Sapotaceae, Ebenaceae, Primulaceae | Stamens often opposite the corolla lobes | §46.3.4–46.3.5 |
| Pentaphylacaceae, Theaceae | Each separate | Two parts of the former Theaceae | §46.3.3, §46.3.6 |
| Styracoid clade | Symplocaceae, Styracaceae, Diapensiaceae | — | §46.3.7 |
| Core Ericales | Actinidiaceae, Sarraceniaceae, Roridulaceae, Cyrillaceae, Clethraceae, Ericaceae | Anthers inverted in development and poricidal, style hollow, endosperm haustoria at both ends, iridoids present | §46.3.8–46.3.10 |

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Theales had 18 families, including Theaceae, Actinidiaceae, Dipterocarpaceae and Clusiaceae, of which Dipterocarpaceae moves to Malvales ([7.12.44](/7.12.44)) and Clusiaceae to Malpighiales ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)), and Theaceae is split into Theaceae in the narrow sense and Pentaphylacaceae; the 5 families of Ebenales and the 3 families of Primulales are all merged into Ericales, and Myrsinaceae and Theophrastaceae are merged into Primulaceae; Pyrolaceae, Monotropaceae and Empetraceae of Ericales are merged into Ericaceae.

### 46.3.2. Balsaminaceae

Balsaminaceae has only 2 genera: *Impatiens* (about 1,000 species) and *Hydrocera* (1 species). *Impatiens* is one of the largest genera of angiosperms, occurring mainly in the mountains of tropical Asia and Africa; China has about 270 species, concentrated in the southwest, and many are narrowly endemic.

Diagnostic characters: succulent herbs with translucent stems and often swollen nodes; leaves simple, toothed, the teeth often tipped with a gland; flowers bilaterally symmetric and often resupinate; sepals 3 (rarely 5), the posterior one large and petal-like, prolonged at the base into a curved spur that secretes nectar; petals 5, the upper one (the standard) free and the 4 lateral ones fused in pairs (the wings); stamens 5, with short, flat filaments and anthers fused into a cap covering the ovary; ovary superior, 5-locular, with axile placentation; the capsule is fleshy and at maturity the 5 valves coil elastically inward from the base and shoot out the seeds (the genus name *Impatiens* means "impatient", that is, "not tolerating touch"). Floral formula: $\uparrow K_3 C_{1+(2)+(2)} A_{(5)} \underline{G}_{(5:5:\infty)}$.

Garden balsam (*I. balsamina*) is an annual herb native from India to southern China, cultivated widely; the crushed petals were used to stain fingernails and the seeds are used medicinally. Touch-me-not (*I. noli-tangere*) occurs in wet forest floors from Northeast to North China and has yellow flowers. The spur length of *Impatiens* is matched to the length of the mouthparts of the pollinators (bees, moths and birds), and floral form is extremely diverse within the genus.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist placed Balsaminaceae in Geraniales (Rosidae, [7.12.44](/7.12.44)) because it too has an explosively dehiscing capsule; molecular evidence shows that it belongs to Ericales, as one of the sister groups of the other families.

### 46.3.3. Polemoniaceae and Pentaphylacaceae

Polemoniaceae has about 26 genera and 400 species, chiefly in western North America. Mostly herbs; leaves alternate or opposite, often pinnately parted; flowers radially symmetric, 5-merous, the corolla sympetalous, salverform or campanulate, with contorted lobes; stamens 5, borne on the corolla tube; ovary superior, of 3 carpels, 3-locular, with axile placentation; capsule. *Polemonium caeruleum* (Jacob's ladder) occurs in the alpine meadows of Northeast and North China; garden phlox (*Phlox paniculata*) and annual phlox (*P. drummondii*) are common garden flowers. The relation of floral form to pollinators in Polemoniaceae has been studied in depth: within one genus there are bee-, butterfly-, moth-, hummingbird- and bat-pollinated species, and pollination syndromes have shifted many times within the genus. Cronquist placed the family in Solanales of the Asteridae; molecular evidence shows that it belongs to Ericales.

Pentaphylacaceae is enlarged in APG IV and includes the Ternstroemioideae of the former Theaceae (*Ternstroemia*, *Eurya*, *Adinandra*, *Cleyera* and others), *Pentaphylax*, *Sladenia* and a few other genera, about 12 genera and 340 species, chiefly in tropical to subtropical Asia. It differs from Theaceae in the narrow sense in having smaller flowers with fewer stamens whose anthers are basifixed; the embryo is curved (straight in Theaceae); and the fruit is a berry or indehiscent (mostly a capsule in Theaceae). Representative plants: *Eurya* consists of evergreen shrubs with alternate, finely toothed leaves and small, unisexual, dioecious flowers clustered in the leaf axils and small berries; it is dominant in the shrub layer of the evergreen broad-leaved forests of southern China, with *E. muricata* and *E. alata* (young twigs 4-angled) common; flowering is early, and it is an important nectar source in winter. *Ternstroemia gymnanthera* is a small evergreen tree with leaves clustered at the twig tips and fruit that opens when ripe, the seeds having a bright red outer seed coat; it is a common garden tree. *Adinandra millettii* occurs from East to South China; *Cleyera japonica* is the sakaki of Japan, used in Shinto rituals.

### 46.3.4. Sapotaceae and Ebenaceae

Sapotaceae has about 65 genera and 1,100 species, pantropical. The plants are woody with white latex; leaves alternate, often clustered at the twig tips, the underside often with rusty T-shaped hairs; flowers sympetalous, stamens opposite the corolla lobes, with staminodes often present; berry with a large, hard, glossy seed and a large hilum. Sapodilla (*Manilkara zapota*) is native to Central America, with an edible berry and a latex that was the traditional base of chewing gum (chicle); miracle fruit (*Synsepalum dulcificum*) has fruit containing miraculin, which makes sour things taste sweet; the seeds of shea (*Vitellaria paradoxa*) yield shea butter; the latex (gutta-percha) of *Palaquium gutta* was once the insulating material for submarine cables.

Diagnostic characters of Ebenaceae: trees or shrubs with often black, hard wood; leaves simple, alternate, entire, without stipules; flowers unisexual, on dioecious plants or polygamous; female flowers often solitary, male flowers in cymes; calyx 3–7-lobed, persistent and often enlarged in fruit; corolla sympetalous, campanulate or urceolate, with contorted lobes; stamens often 2–4 times as many as the corolla lobes, borne at the base of the corolla tube; female flowers with staminodes; ovary superior, often of 4 carpels, each divided by a false septum into 2 locules, with 1 pendent ovule per locule; berry with a large persistent calyx (the persimmon calyx); seeds often with ruminate endosperm. Floral formula: persimmon ♂ $\ast K_{(4)} C_{(4)} A_{16}$; ♀ $\ast K_{(4)} C_{(4)} \underline{G}_{(4:8:1)}$.

Ebenaceae has about 3–4 genera and 500–800 species, almost all in *Diospyros*; China has about 60 species, from the southwest to the southeast. Persimmon (*D. kaki*) is native to China and has been cultivated for more than 3,000 years, with many cultivars; the berry is rich in glucose and fructose, and when unripe it contains large amounts of soluble tannins (persimmon tannin) and tastes astringent, becoming edible only after deastringency treatment (which makes the tannins insoluble); the white bloom on dried persimmons is mainly mannitol and glucose; the calyx is used medicinally. Date-plum (*D. lotus*) has small, blue-black fruit, and its seedlings are the rootstock for grafting persimmon. *D. rhombifolia* has spiny twigs and orange-yellow fruit; *D. armata* is a bonsai tree. The heartwood of *Diospyros* is dark brown and is known as ebony, with *D. ebenum* of India the best known.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Ebenales included 5 families, among them Ebenaceae, Sapotaceae, Symplocaceae and Styracaceae, which are now all in Ericales; APG IV Ebenaceae also absorbs Lissocarpaceae of South America.

### 46.3.5. Primulaceae

APG IV Primulaceae (Primulaceae s.l.) merges Cronquist's Myrsinaceae, Theophrastaceae of tropical America and Maesaceae, with about 57 genera and 2,600 species. The reason for the merger is that Primulaceae in the narrow sense is paraphyletic, *Lysimachia*, *Cyclamen* and others being more closely related to Myrsinaceae than to *Primula*. The four former families are now four subfamilies: Maesoideae, Theophrastoideae, Primuloideae (*Primula*, *Androsace* and others) and Myrsinoideae (*Ardisia*, *Myrsine*, and *Lysimachia*, *Cyclamen*, *Anagallis* and others formerly in Primulaceae).

The characters shared by the whole family are a sympetalous corolla; stamens as many as the corolla lobes and opposite them (the outer whorl has been lost, [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.4)), the outer whorl sometimes becoming staminodes (Theophrastoideae); ovary 1-locular with free-central placentation; and plant bodies often with glandular dots (black or translucent dots formed by glands or secretory canals on the leaf).

Diagnostic characters (the herbs of Primuloideae and Myrsinoideae):

- Annual or perennial herbs, rarely subshrubs; often with glandular dots or a white farina.
- Leaves basal, alternate, opposite or whorled, simple, without stipules.
- Flowers bisexual, radially symmetric, 5-merous; calyx persistent; corolla sympetalous, rotate, campanulate, funnelform or salverform.
- Stamens 5, borne on the corolla tube and opposite the corolla lobes.
- Ovary superior (rarely half-inferior), 5 carpels fused, 1-locular, with free-central placentation and many ovules; style 1.
- Capsule, dehiscing by valves or a lid; seeds small and many, with abundant endosperm.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_5 \underline{G}_{(5:1:\infty)}$ (stamens epipetalous and antepetalous).

Classification and representative plants:

1. *Primula*: leaves all basal, in a rosette; umbels borne at the top of a scape; corolla salverform with the tube longer than the lobes; most species show **heterostyly** (distyly): in a population half the plants are long-styled and half short-styled, the heights of stigma and anthers are complementary, and only pollination between different morphs sets seed (intramorph incompatibility, [7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3)). Darwin (1862) first used primroses to clarify the significance of heterostyly. Floral morph is controlled by an S supergene; recent genomic studies show that the short-styled plant carries a hemizygous region of about 280 kb, in which the gene *CYP734A50* shortens the style by degrading brassinosteroids (Li et al. 2016; Huu et al. 2016). *Primula* has about 500 species, about 300 of them in China, chiefly in the Hengduan Mountains of the southwest, and is a famous ornamental; *P. malacoides*, *P. sinensis* and German primrose (*P. obconica*, with glandular hairs on the leaves that can cause dermatitis on contact) are native to China and cultivated in greenhouses everywhere.
2. *Androsace*: small herbs with a short corolla tube and constricted throat. *A. umbellata* is annual, widespread in China, and the whole plant is used medicinally; the cushion androsaces of the high mountains represent alpine cushion plants.
3. *Lysimachia* (now in Myrsinoideae): erect or creeping herbs, the leaves often with black or red glandular dots or streaks; corolla lobes contorted. Gooseneck loosestrife (*L. clethroides*) has terminal, nodding racemes of dense white flowers; *L. christinae* is a creeping herb with yellow flowers in axillary pairs, and the whole plant is used medicinally. In modern treatments *Anagallis* is also merged into *Lysimachia*.
4. *Cyclamen* (now in Myrsinoideae): perennial herbs with a flattened globose tuber; flowers solitary and nodding, with the corolla lobes reflexed upward. Cyclamen (*C. persicum*) is native to the eastern Mediterranean and is a pot plant of winter and spring.
5. *Ardisia*: shrubs or small trees with glandular-dotted leaves; corolla lobes contorted; drupe spherical and often red. *A. japonica* is a creeping dwarf shrub; coral ardisia (*A. crenata*) has wavy glandular teeth at the leaf margin, in which bacterial nodules formed by symbiotic bacteria occur, and red fruit that persist through winter, making it the New Year potted plant "fortune seeds"; *A. crispa* is also often cultivated.
6. *Maesa* (Maesoideae): shrubs whose ovary is half-inferior, clearly different from the other subfamilies. *M. japonica* occurs south of the Yangtze.

Key characters of the family: herbs (the former Myrsinaceae are woody), often with glandular dots or farina; corolla sympetalous, stamens as many as the corolla lobes and opposite them; ovary superior, 1-locular, with free-central placentation. Capsule (a drupe in the former Myrsinaceae).

### 46.3.6. Theaceae

In APG IV Theaceae is a family in the narrow sense that excludes the Ternstroemioideae (moved to Pentaphylacaceae), the Bonnetioideae (moved to Malpighiales) and *Pelliciera* (moved to Tetrameristaceae, also in Ericales); only about 9 genera and 300 species remain, divided into 3 tribes: Theeae (*Camellia*, *Polyspora*, *Pyrenaria* and others), Gordonieae (*Schima*, the American *Gordonia* and *Franklinia*) and Stewartieae (*Stewartia*). It occurs chiefly in East and Southeast Asia, with a few species in the Americas; China is the center of distribution of *Camellia* (the number of species varies greatly with the treatment of *Camellia*), and Theaceae is an important component of the evergreen broad-leaved forests south of the Yangtze.

Diagnostic characters:

- Evergreen trees or shrubs; leaves simple, alternate, leathery, often serrate (theoid teeth, with a deciduous gland at the tip); stipules absent.
- Flowers bisexual, radially symmetric, often solitary in the leaf axils, large and showy.
- There is often a gradual transition among bracts, sepals and petals (in *Camellia* bracts and sepals are often hard to distinguish); sepals 5 to many, imbricate, persistent or deciduous.
- Petals 5 (often double in cultivars), usually fused at the base and with the filaments of the outer stamens.
- Stamens numerous in several whorls, the filaments of the outer ones often fused into a short tube or in 5 bundles, borne at the base of the petals; anthers versatile, longitudinally dehiscent.
- Ovary superior, 3–5 carpels fused, 3–5-locular, with axile placentation and 2 to many ovules per locule.
- Fruit a loculicidal woody capsule with a persistent columella; seeds large, without endosperm, the cotyledons thick and oil-rich (*Camellia*), or seeds flat and winged (*Schima*, *Polyspora*).

Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{(3\text{–}5:3\text{–}5:2\text{–}\infty)}$ (Chinese textbooks often write $\ast K_{4\text{–}\infty} C_5 A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}8:2\text{–}8)}$, with the petal bases fused with the outer stamens).

![Floral diagram and formula of Theaceae: bracts and sepals grade into one another (small bracts on the two sides, 5 imbricate sepals), 5 imbricate petals; stamens numerous, the filaments of the outer ones fused at the base into bundles and with the base of the petals (arcs), the inner ones free; ovary superior, 3 carpels fused, 3-locular, with axile placentation and several ovules per locule](./plots/theaceae-floral-diagram.py)

Classification and representative plants:

1. *Camellia*: evergreen shrubs or small trees with leathery, serrate leaves; flowers large, with bracts and sepals often grading into one another; capsule, seeds large and wingless. There are about 120–280 species (the treatments differ greatly), with China the center of distribution and the great majority of species in southwestern and southern China.
   - Tea (*C. sinensis*): an evergreen shrub (pruned to a shrub in cultivation) with oblong leaves whose lateral veins join in a network near the margin; flowers white, stalked and nodding; sepals persistent. It is native to southwestern China, and drinking and growing tea in China has a history of at least 2,000 years (the "jia" of the Erya is tea), with Lu Yu writing the *Classic of Tea* in the Tang dynasty. Tea leaves contain caffeine (2%–5%), tea polyphenols (mainly catechins) and theanine. Green, oolong and black teas differ not in the species of the raw material but in the extent to which the tea polyphenols are oxidized during processing (popularly called "fermentation"): in green tea fixation prevents oxidation, oolong is partly oxidized, black tea is fully oxidized, and dark tea (ripe pu-erh and others) undergoes microbial post-fermentation. In the mid-nineteenth century the Englishman Robert Fortune took tea plants and the technique of tea making out of China and developed tea gardens in the Indian Himalaya (such as Darjeeling); the tea gardens of Assam relied mainly on the locally native Assam tea. Assam tea (*C. sinensis* var. *assamica*) is a tree with large leaves (8–20 cm long) occurring in Yunnan, Guizhou and South China, and Yunnan has wild and cultivated old tea trees several hundred to over a thousand years old. Genomic studies show that Chinese tea (small-leaved) and Assam tea (large-leaved) diverged before human domestication and may have been domesticated in different regions.
   - Oil-seed camellia (*C. oleifera*): flowers white and sessile, sepals deciduous; the valves fall off together with the columella; the seeds contain more than 30% oil, and "tea oil" is an important woody edible oil of the southern mountains of China, with a fatty acid composition similar to olive oil.
   - Japanese camellia (*C. japonica*): leaves ovate and glabrous beneath, flowers sessile and red, 7–10 cm in diameter, with deciduous sepals; native to eastern China and Japan, with thousands of cultivars, and a traditional famous flower of China. Yunnan camellia (*C. reticulata*) has a leaf venation network that is evident at least on the upper surface and a hairy ovary, and occurs in Yunnan, with numerous mostly double-flowered cultivars. Golden camellia (*C. petelotii*, often given as *C. chrysantha* in Chinese textbooks) has golden yellow flowers, occurs in Guangxi, is a second-class nationally protected plant, and is the source of the yellow gene in camellia breeding.
2. *Schima superba*: a large evergreen tree with white flowers, a woody capsule and flat winged seeds; the wood is hard, and the thick bark has a high water content and is fire-resistant, so that it is often planted as a firebreak and is a dominant tree of the evergreen broad-leaved forests of South and East China.
3. *Stewartia sinensis*: a deciduous tree with smooth, reddish brown bark that peels in flakes; flowers white, solitary, with 2 leaf-like bracts; it occurs in the mountains south of the Yangtze.
4. *Polyspora axillaris* (often given as *Gordonia axillaris* in Chinese textbooks): flowers large and white, occurring in South China. The Franklin tree (*Franklinia alatamaha*) of North America has not been found in the wild since 1803, and all living plants are descended from seeds collected by John and William Bartram in the 1770s.

Key characters of the family: evergreen woody plants with simple, alternate, leathery, serrate leaves; flowers bisexual, radially symmetric and 5-merous, with bracts, sepals and petals often grading into one another; stamens numerous, the filaments of the outer ones often fused in bundles and with the base of the petals; ovary superior with axile placentation. Woody capsule.

### 46.3.7. Symplocaceae and Styracaceae

Symplocaceae has only *Symplocos*, with about 300 species in the tropics and subtropics of Asia, Australia and the Americas; China has over 40 species (Flora of China), south of the Yangtze. Woody plants whose winter buds are often several in a superposed series; leaves simple, alternate, often with glandular serrations and usually turning yellow-green on drying (the plant accumulates aluminum); flowers radially symmetric, the calyx 5-lobed and persistent; corolla deeply divided into 5 lobes; stamens numerous, often in several whorls, with the filaments fused at the base into bundles and borne on the corolla; ovary inferior or half-inferior, 2–5-locular; drupe, crowned by the persistent calyx lobes. Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_{\infty} \overline{G}_{(2\text{–}5:2\text{–}5:2\text{–}4)}$. Sapphire berry (*S. paniculata*) is a deciduous shrub or small tree with blue-black drupes and an edible seed oil; *S. sumuntia* (often given as *S. caudata* in Chinese textbooks) is evergreen with fragrant white flowers; *S. stellaris* and *S. cochinchinensis* var. *laurina* have hard wood. The leaves of Symplocaceae can be used as a yellow dye (containing aluminum, they act as a mordant), whence the Chinese name "fan" (alum).

Styracaceae (snowbell family) has about 11 genera and 160 species, in East and Southeast Asia, the Mediterranean and the Americas. Woody plants, often with stellate hairs or scales; leaves simple and alternate; flowers bisexual, radially symmetric, often nodding and white; corolla sympetalous, deeply 4–5-lobed; stamens twice as many as the corolla lobes, the filaments fused below into a tube adnate to the corolla; ovary superior (*Styrax*) to inferior, 3–5-locular; drupe or capsule, often with a persistent calyx. *Styrax*: Japanese snowbell (*S. japonicus*) has white nodding flowers and is widely cultivated; the resin (benzoin) that exudes from cuts in the trunk of Siam benzoin (*S. tonkinensis*) and Sumatra benzoin (*S. benzoin*) is a perfume and drug. *Sinojackia xylocarpa*, endemic to China, has fruit shaped like a steelyard weight, occurs around Nanjing, Jiangsu, was one of the first endemic genera named by a Chinese botanist (Hu Xiansu, 1928), and is a second-class nationally protected plant; *Pterostyrax* and *Alniphyllum* occur south of the Yangtze.

### 46.3.8. Actinidiaceae

In APG IV Actinidiaceae has 3 genera and about 360 species: *Actinidia*, *Saurauia* and *Clematoclethra*, chiefly in tropical and subtropical Asia with a few in the Americas.

Diagnostic characters: woody climbers, shrubs or small trees; the pith of *Actinidia* is often lamellate (in longitudinal section the pith is divided into a series of thin plates) or solid; leaves simple and alternate, often toothed, covered with coarse or stellate hairs, without stipules; flowers bisexual or unisexual, *Actinidia* being dioecious (functionally: the flowers of male plants have a vestigial ovary and those of female plants have sterile anthers), often in cymes; sepals 5, persistent; petals 5; stamens numerous and free, the anthers versatile, often inverted, dehiscing by pores or longitudinally; ovary superior, many carpels fused, multilocular, with axile placentation and many ovules; styles numerous, radiating and persistent; berry, with many small seeds and abundant endosperm. Floral formula: ♂ $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{\infty}$; ♀ $\ast K_5 C_5 \underline{G}_{(\infty:\infty:\infty)}$.

*Actinidia* has about 55 species, almost all in China. Chinese kiwifruit (*A. chinensis*) is a deciduous climber with nearly round leaves densely covered with gray-white stellate tomentum on the underside and an oblong, hairy berry, occurring south of the Yangtze; its variety *A. chinensis* var. *deliciosa* has larger fruit covered with coarse bristles. In 1904 Mary Isabel Fraser, a headmistress in New Zealand, brought seeds of var. *deliciosa* from Yichang, Hubei, back to New Zealand, where horticulturists selected large-fruited cultivars (such as 'Hayward'); in the mid-twentieth century the fruit was exported around the world under the name "kiwifruit", making New Zealand one of the largest exporters of kiwifruit; since the 1970s China has also surveyed and cultivated kiwifruit resources and is now the world's largest producer. Kiwifruit are rich in vitamin C (about 50–400 mg per 100 g of flesh, depending on species and cultivar). Hardy kiwi (*A. arguta*) has small, glabrous fruit that can be eaten with the skin and occurs from Northeast to South China; *A. eriantha* and *A. kolomikta* (whose leaves become partly white or pink at flowering) are also common. *Actinidia* has abundant ploidy variation (diploid to octoploid), and var. *deliciosa* is mostly hexaploid.

### 46.3.9. Ericaceae

APG IV Ericaceae (Ericaceae s.l.) merges Cronquist's Pyrolaceae, Monotropaceae and Empetraceae, and also Epacridaceae and Vacciniaceae, which some systems recognize. Both molecular and morphological evidence show that these families are nested within Ericaceae and that separating them would make Ericaceae paraphyletic.

Diagnostic characters:

- Shrubs or small trees, rarely subshrubby herbs, climbers, or mycoheterotrophic herbs without chlorophyll (Monotropoideae); almost all have mycorrhizas (see below).
- Leaves simple and alternate, rarely opposite or whorled, often leathery and evergreen, the surface often with scales or glandular hairs (sometimes forming a "scurf"), without stipules.
- Flowers bisexual, radially symmetric or slightly bilaterally symmetric; sepals 4–5, persistent.
- Corolla sympetalous (rarely free, as in *Rhododendron tomentosum* and *Pyrola*), urceolate, campanulate, funnelform or salverform, 4–5-lobed.
- Stamens often twice as many as the corolla lobes (two whorls, the outer opposite the corolla lobes, that is, obdiplostemonous) or equal in number, often free of the corolla and arising from the base of the disc; anthers inverted in development (the tip of the anther points downward at maturity), dehiscing by apical pores, often with awn-like or spur-like appendages.
- Pollen often in tetrads (four pollen grains joined in a tetrahedral arrangement), sometimes with viscin threads.
- Ovary superior or inferior (*Vaccinium*), 4–5 carpels fused, 4–5-locular, with axile placentation and many ovules; style 1, the stigma capitate.
- Capsule, septicidal or loculicidal, or a berry or drupe; seeds small, with abundant endosperm.

Floral formula: *Rhododendron* $\uparrow K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_{5+5} \underline{G}_{(5:5:\infty)}$; *Vaccinium* $\ast K_{(4\text{–}5)} C_{(4\text{–}5)} A_{8\text{–}10} \overline{G}_{(4\text{–}5:4\text{–}5:\infty)}$.

![Flowers and anthers of Ericaceae. B: stamens of Rhododendron: the tip of the anther has 2 pores, and the pollen is in tetrads joined in chains by viscin threads. E: arrangement of a pollen tetrad](./plots/ericaceae-flower.py)

Ericaceae has about 125 genera and 4,000 species, worldwide, chiefly in the temperate and subarctic zones and also in tropical mountains; China has about 22 genera and 830 species, most in the mountains of the southwest. The family is divided into 8 subfamilies (Kron et al. 2002), of which those common in China are Enkianthoideae, Monotropoideae (including the pyroloids), Arbutoideae, Ericoideae (including *Rhododendron*, *Empetrum*, *Calluna* and *Erica*) and Vaccinioideae (including *Vaccinium*, *Gaultheria*, *Lyonia* and *Pieris*).

The roots of Ericaceae are very fine ("hair roots") and have no root hairs; the epidermal cells contain coils of hyphae of ascomycetes (such as *Rhizoscyphus ericae*), and this endomycorrhiza is called the ericoid mycorrhiza ([7.12.20](/7.12.20#sec-20.6.2)). The mycorrhizal fungi can break down organic nitrogen and phosphorus that are hard to use in the soil and reduce the toxicity of heavy metals, so that Ericaceae can dominate acidic, nutrient-poor soils rich in organic matter in which mineralization is slow (peatlands, heathlands, alpine scrub). This is one reason why rhododendrons and blueberries must be grown in acidic soil.

Classification and representative plants:

1. *Rhododendron*: woody; corolla sympetalous, funnelform to campanulate, 5-merous, often slightly bilaterally symmetric; stamens 5 or 10, the anthers without appendages, dehiscing by apical pores; capsule septicidal. There are about 1,000 species, about 570 of them in China, and the Hengduan Mountains and eastern Himalaya are the world's center of distribution; they dominate the alpine scrub and the understory of conifer forests. *R. simsii* (azalea) is a deciduous shrub with brown, flat, appressed bristles over the whole plant and red flowers, common in the hills south of the Yangtze; *R. molle* (Chinese azalea) is a deciduous shrub with yellow flowers and 5 stamens, whole plant containing grayanane toxins (rhodojaponin) and highly poisonous, usable as a pesticide; *R. dauricum* is semi-evergreen with leaves densely scaly beneath and purple-red flowers opening before the leaves, in Northeast China; *R. micranthum* has small white flowers, occurs in North China, and is poisonous. Labrador tea (*R. tomentosum*, often given as *Ledum palustre* in Chinese textbooks) is a small evergreen shrub with free petals, occurring in the peatlands of Northeast and northern China, the leaves containing Ledum oil; molecular evidence shows that *Ledum* is nested within *Rhododendron*, into which it has now been merged.
2. *Vaccinium*: corolla urceolate or campanulate; the back of the anther often has 2 spurs and the tip is prolonged into tubes opening by pores; ovary inferior; berry. *V. bracteatum* is an evergreen shrub whose leaves are pressed for juice to dye rice for "black rice", occurring south of the Yangtze; lingonberry (*V. vitis-idaea*) is a creeping small shrub with red berries, in Northeast China; blueberries are cultivars of North American highbush blueberry (*V. corymbosum*) and others, domesticated in the early twentieth century by Frederick Coville and others, rich in anthocyanins, and among the fruits with the shortest history of domestication; cranberry (*V. macrocarpon*) grows in the acid bogs of North America.
3. *Gaultheria*: stamens 10, anthers awned; the sepals enlarge and become fleshy in fruit and enclose the capsule, forming a berry-like pseudocarp. *G. leucocarpa* var. *yunnanensis* (often given as *G. yunnanensis* in Chinese textbooks) has twigs and leaves containing methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen), with a characteristic smell, used in medicine and perfumery.
4. *Lyonia*: corolla urceolate, the filaments with 2 awn-like appendages near the tip; capsule loculicidal, the sutures thickened. *L. ovalifolia* var. *elliptica* occurs south of the Yangtze.
5. *Enkianthus*: umbellate or racemose inflorescences nodding; corolla campanulate or urceolate; stamens 10, the anthers with 2 awns at the tip. *E. quinqueflorus* has a corolla swollen at the base into an urn, pink or red, flowering around the Spring Festival, and is the traditional New Year flower of the Guangzhou flower market. Enkianthoideae is the sister group of the rest of Ericaceae.
6. *Pieris japonica* (Japanese andromeda) is an evergreen shrub with nodding racemes of white urceolate flowers; it is poisonous (containing grayanotoxin) and livestock that eat it stagger, whence the Chinese name "horse-drunk tree".
7. Monotropoideae: wintergreen (*Pyrola calliantha*) is an evergreen herb with basal leaves, free petals and a long, curved style, common under forests, and the whole plant is used medicinally; ghost plant (*Monotropa uniflora*) and pinesap (*Hypopitys monotropa*) are white or pale yellow and fleshy throughout, with no chlorophyll and leaves reduced to scales, and obtain carbohydrates from surrounding trees through mycorrhizal fungi, typical **mycoheterotrophs** ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.2)).
8. Crowberry (*Empetrum nigrum*) is an evergreen creeping small shrub with dense linear leaves and small, unisexual, wind-pollinated flowers and purple-black berries, occurring in the high mountains and Arctic tundra of Northeast China; heather (*Calluna vulgaris*) of Europe and *Erica* (about 800 species, chiefly in South Africa) dominate the heathlands (the English "heath" comes from this).

The poricidal anthers of Ericaceae and the pollen, often joined into tetrads with viscin threads, are adapted to buzz pollination: bumble bees and other bees clasp the anthers and vibrate them at high frequency with the flight muscles (without beating the wings) and shake pollen out of the apical pores to land on the underside of the bee ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.2)). Honeybees do not collect pollen by buzzing, so blueberries and tomato (Solanaceae) in greenhouses often require bumble bees to be released for pollination.

Key characters of the family: often shrubs with simple, alternate, often leathery leaves; corolla sympetalous, urceolate, campanulate or funnelform; stamens often twice as many as the corolla lobes, free of the corolla and arising from the base of the disc; anthers often inverted and poricidal, often with awns or spurs; pollen often in tetrads; axile placentation with many ovules. Capsule or berry. Roots with ericoid mycorrhiza.

Economic importance: *Rhododendron* is a world-famous ornamental, and wild Chinese rhododendrons were introduced into Europe in large numbers from the end of the nineteenth century to the early twentieth, giving rise to tens of thousands of cultivars; *Vaccinium* provides berries such as blueberry, cranberry and lingonberry; *Gaultheria* yields oil of wintergreen. Many Ericaceae contain grayanane diterpene toxins, and honey made from their nectar (such as the "mad honey" of the Turkish Black Sea coast) can cause poisoning.

### 46.3.10. Other Families of Ericales

- Lecythidaceae: large tropical trees with leaves often clustered at the twig tips; flowers large, with extremely numerous stamens often inclined to one side to form a stamen "hood"; fruit a woody capsule (circumscissile) or indehiscent. The spherical woody fruit of Brazil nut (*Bertholletia excelsa*) holds 10–25 seeds rich in oil and selenium; cannonball tree (*Couroupita guianensis*) is cauliflorous with fruit like cannonballs. *Barringtonia racemosa* is a small tree of the coasts of South China and Taiwan with long, pendent racemes that open at night. Cronquist placed Lecythidaceae in its own order Lecythidales, and some placed it in Myrtales.
- Sarraceniaceae: carnivorous herbs of North America and northern South America whose leaves are modified into erect pitchers or trumpets with downward-pointing hairs on the inner wall, and insects that fall into the liquid at the bottom are digested. Together with Roridulaceae of southern Africa (leaves with sticky glandular hairs) it belongs to the core Ericales, another independent origin of carnivory in angiosperms ([7.12.45](/7.12.45#sec-45.3.9)).
- Clethraceae: *Clethra* consists of shrubs or small trees with racemes of fragrant white flowers with free petals, closely related to Ericaceae.
- Mitrastemonaceae: *Mitrastemon yamamotoi* is a holoparasitic herb parasitizing the roots of Fagaceae (*Castanopsis*, *Lithocarpus*), with only the flowers emerging above ground, the perianth fused into a cup, and the stamens fused into a cap that falls off the flower; it occurs in southern China and Japan. It was formerly placed in Rafflesiaceae, and molecular evidence shows that it belongs to Ericales.
- Diapensiaceae: low evergreen herbs or subshrubs of the northern temperate zone and high mountains; *Diapensia* forms cushion plants on the high mountains of the Himalaya and the Hengduan Mountains.

## Questions

1. What are the most reliable characters of the asterids? Why can the "sympetalous corolla" not by itself delimit the asterids?

   **Answer** The monophyly of the asterids rests on molecular evidence; in morphology and chemistry the most consistent characters are embryological ones: a unitegmic, tenuinucellate ovule, iridoids and cellular endosperm. Although a sympetalous corolla is general in the euasterids, it cannot delimit the asterids by itself: on the one hand, many families of the earliest-diverging Cornales and Ericales (Cornaceae, Hydrangeaceae, Theaceae, Actinidiaceae and others) have free petals; on the other hand, Plumbaginaceae, Caricaceae and Cucurbitaceae outside the asterids also have a sympetalous corolla, which evolved independently; and even within the asterids the sympetalous corolla may have arisen several times by different developmental routes (early and late sympetaly).

2. Compare the stamens of Theaceae, Ericaceae and Primulaceae and explain their significance in systematics.

   **Answer** In Theaceae the stamens are numerous, the filaments of the outer ones fused in bundles and with the base of the petals; in Ericaceae they are often twice the number of corolla lobes, free of the corolla and arising from the base of the disc, with inverted, poricidal anthers; in Primulaceae they are as many as the corolla lobes and opposite them, borne on the corolla tube (the outer whorl having been lost). In Cronquist's system the three belong to Theales, Ericales and Primulales respectively and represent a line of evolution of the stamens from numerous to definite in number and from free to epipetalous; APG IV places all three in Ericales, which shows that the staminal states in Ericales are diverse and differ from the state "stamens as many as the corolla lobes and alternate with them, epipetalous" of the euasterids.

3. How does the heterostyly of *Primula* promote outcrossing? What is its genetic basis?

   **Answer** The populations of *Primula* contain long-styled and short-styled plants whose stigma and anther heights are complementary: pollen carried at one height on an insect's mouthparts lands exactly on the stigma at the same height in the other morph; in addition, the pollen grain size and stigma papillae of the two morphs differ, and pollination within a morph is incompatible (intramorph incompatibility), so that only crosses between long- and short-styled plants set seed, which ensures outcrossing. Genetically, floral morph is controlled by an S supergene: short-styled plants carry a hemizygous region of about 280 kb present on only one chromosome (S/s), while long-styled plants are s/s; the gene *CYP734A50* in it shortens the style and other genes control anther position and pollen size, and these genes are tightly linked and do not recombine, so that the combination of characters of the two morphs is maintained.

4. Why can Ericaceae dominate acidic, nutrient-poor soils? What should be heeded in cultivating blueberries and rhododendrons?

   **Answer** The roots of Ericaceae have ericoid mycorrhiza: hyphae of ascomycetes form coils in the epidermal cells of the root, and the enzymes secreted by the mycorrhizal fungi can break down organic nitrogen and phosphorus in the soil and reduce the toxicity of metal ions such as aluminum and iron in acidic soil, helping the plants obtain nutrients in peatlands, heathlands and alpine scrub that are rich in organic matter and slow in mineralization. Blueberries and rhododendrons need an acidic (pH about 4.5–5.5), loose substrate rich in organic matter; in neutral or alkaline soil the availability of iron and other elements decreases, the plants readily suffer iron-deficiency chlorosis, and mycorrhizas form poorly. In addition, because the anthers of Ericaceae are poricidal, blueberries in greenhouses need bumble bees released for buzz pollination.

5. In the processing of tea, how do green, oolong and black teas differ? Why are there so many old trees and wild forms of tea in southwestern China?

   **Answer** All three come from one species (*Camellia sinensis*) and differ in the extent of oxidation of the tea polyphenols during processing: for green tea the leaves are fixed rapidly after picking, which inactivates polyphenol oxidase, so that the polyphenols are essentially not oxidized; oolong tea undergoes steps such as shaking, and the leaf margins are partly oxidized; for black tea rolling breaks the cells and the polyphenols are fully oxidized under enzyme action to produce theaflavins and thearubigins; dark tea also involves microbial post-fermentation. Southwestern China (Yunnan, Guizhou, Sichuan) is the center of distribution and diversity of *Camellia* and of section *Thea*, and one of the regions where tea was first used and domesticated; the warm, humid montane forest environment there suits arborescent tea trees, and together with a long tradition of tea growing by the ethnic minorities it has preserved many old wild and cultivated tea trees.
