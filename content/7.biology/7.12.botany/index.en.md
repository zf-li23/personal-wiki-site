**Botany** studies the form and structure, reproduction, classification and phylogeny of plants. It has traditionally had two main parts: morphology and anatomy describe the plant body and its development from cells and tissues to organs, while taxonomy describes, names and identifies plants and places them in a system that reflects their relationships. Even the scope of "plant" has to be settled first. The algae, fungi and lichens that botany has always studied lie on widely separated branches of the **tree of life**, and modern systematics recognizes only monophyletic groups, so that plants in the strict sense are the **green plants**, or more narrowly the **embryophytes**. Three questions run through the course. How is the plant body built: how do cells differentiate into tissues, how do tissues make up roots, stems, leaves, flowers and fruits, and how are these structures adapted to aquatic, dry, saline or parasitic ways of life? What plants are there and how are they related: from the algae in the water to the seed plants on land, which characters were inherited from common ancestors and which evolved independently in different lineages? And how do form and classification explain each other: morphological terms are the language in which plants are described and identified, while **phylogeny** gives the diversity of form a historical explanation.

The course interleaves morphology and anatomy with systematics. The introduction delimits plants and botany, and the foundations of taxonomy cover ranks, the codes of nomenclature, keys and the methods of phylogenetic classification; then come the plant cell, meristems and the mature tissues, and vascular tissues and secretory structures. The part on spore plants begins with the groups outside the green plants, the cyanobacteria, the red algae and glaucophytes and the divisions of algae with secondary plastids, inserts the fungi and **lichens** among them, and then climbs the lineage of green plants: the green algae, the streptophyte algae and the colonization of land, the bryophytes, the origin of vascular plants and the lycophytes, and the ferns, closing with a summary of life cycles and terrestrialization. In this way vascular tissue, the **stele**, **microphylls** and **megaphylls** acquire an evolutionary background among the ferns before they reappear in the vegetative organs of seed plants: seeds and seedlings, the root, the stem with its primary structure and **secondary growth**, the leaf, and the modified vegetative organs. The part on reproduction starts from the ovule and the origin of the seed in **gymnosperms**, continues with the diversity of gymnosperms, and then turns to the angiosperm flower, the development of the anther, the ovule and the male and female gametophytes, flowering, pollination and **double fertilization**, the formation of embryo, endosperm and seed, the fruit, and the ecological adaptations of plants to the abiotic environment, to pollinators and to dispersers. The part on classification first discusses the principles, the evidence and the classification systems of angiosperms from the herbals to **APG IV**, then the origin and early evolution of angiosperms, and then follows the order of APG IV through the orders and principal families of the magnoliids and Chloranthales, the monocots, the early-diverging eudicots, Saxifragales and Vitales, the rosids, the superasterids and the asterids; each of about 160 families is described by its morphological characters and floral formula and compared with its position in the **Cronquist system**. The course summary lists six key innovations along the phylogenetic tree, traces the four turning points in the decline of the gametophyte in the **alternation of generations**, and compares what changed and what did not between the Cronquist system and APG IV.

Botany builds on several earlier courses. Monophyletic groups, homology and convergence, the inference of phylogenetic trees, and the origins of eukaryotes, of multicellularity and of land plants against the geological time scale come from [evolutionary biology](/7.10); the cell and molecular biology of cyanobacteria, protists and fungi, and the symbioses in root nodules, mycorrhizae and lichens, come from [microbiology](/7.11), and only the botanical point of view is added here; the biochemistry of photosynthesis comes from [biochemistry](/7.1), organelles and the cell wall from [cell biology](/7.3), meiosis and types of life cycle from [genetics](/7.4), and the genetic control of floral organ development connects with [developmental biology](/7.5). In turn, this course is the morphological and systematic background of later ones: [plant physiology](/7.13) treats photosynthesis, water transport, mineral nutrition, plant hormones and flowering on the leaves, roots, stomata and vascular tissues described here, and [ecology](/7.19) builds its populations, communities and ecosystems on the life forms, ecological adaptations and element cycles of plants.

Botany is probably where the dream begins for many students in the biology olympiad: after a general introduction to biology, it is often the first subject they study systematically, and in my own three years of high school my interest in biology was rekindled three times, each time by botany. High school biology has no courses on plants or animals, but looking from the animal side at a plant kingdom as magnificent as our own, yet organized in an entirely different way, gives a sense of familiarity in strangeness: plants do not move, they meet their environment with meristems that keep growing and organs that can be repeated without limit, and their bodies and modes of reproduction differ from those of animals at every turn. The arrangement of this course reflects a choice of mine. Morphology and anatomy concern almost only the seed plants, and the sequence from cells to tissues and then to vegetative and reproductive organs resembles the evolution of plants from the primitive to the advanced: cells become connected and divide their labor, form tissues and finally differentiate mechanical tissues and vascular bundles, just as the evolutionary path runs from algae through bryophytes to ferns. I therefore interleaved morphology and anatomy with systematics, so that each discipline would help the reader understand the other. The analogy holds only for the rough order, and it is not a recapitulation of phylogeny by ontogeny: the dominant generation of bryophytes is the gametophyte, whereas vascular tissue arose in the sporophyte, and the anatomy of seed plants describes only the endpoint of evolution. As an order of reading, however, it lets every morphological concept have a history before it has details, and the present course keeps this arrangement, except that the gymnosperms have been moved to the beginning of the part on reproduction and the fungi inserted after the heterokont algae.

The other consideration was the classification system. The most authoritative Chinese botany textbooks, the two volumes by Shiwan Lu, Guofang Wu and their coauthors, explain morphology and the Cronquist system very clearly, but their system is out of date. The course I took, Seed Plant Taxonomy, teaches classification and leaves morphology and anatomy to self-study, yet classification itself cannot do without form: because they are conservative, the reproductive organs are both the most fascinating part of morphology and the most important basis of classification. The classification of angiosperms here therefore follows APG IV while giving each family's position in the Cronquist system alongside, so that readers who learned from Chinese textbooks can see how the old system corresponds to the new one, and can also see that most families and their diagnostic characters have not changed with molecular systematics. Of the books, the textbook of Weiliang Ma, with its fine illustrations and accessible explanations and with molecular phylogenetics added in its third edition, is the best for beginners; the textbooks of Shiwan Lu, Guofang Wu and coauthors can serve as the main body; the books of Mu Liu, Shiyi Hu and Xuelin Huang are the clearest on morphology and anatomy, on reproduction and on development respectively; the book of Wenmei Bao and Jianguo Cao treats the flowerless groups in more detail the earlier they diverged; and the textbook of Judd and coauthors and the monograph of Soltis and coauthors are the basis for adopting the APG system, the latter of which I read in its Chinese translation.
