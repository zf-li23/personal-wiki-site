The golden algae, yellow-green algae, diatoms and brown algae of Chinese textbooks (each treated there as a separate phylum) belong to a single group in modern classification. Their motile cells all have a forward flagellum bearing stiff hairs and a smooth backward flagellum; their plastids all derive from an engulfed red alga, have four envelope membranes and contain chlorophylls *a* and *c*; and their storage product is in all cases a soluble β-1,3-glucan. The size range of this group is almost unmatched among living things, from unicellular golden algae and diatoms a few micrometers across to giant kelp tens of meters long with tissue differentiation. Diatoms account for about one fifth of photosynthesis on Earth, brown algae form the kelp forests of temperate coasts, and kelp (*Saccharina*), wakame (*Undaria*) and *Sargassum* are important economic seaweeds.

This chapter first treats the common features of the stramenopiles (§8.1), and then, from unicellular to multicellular, the golden algae (§8.2), yellow-green algae (§8.3), diatoms (§8.4) and brown algae (§8.5). The heterotrophic group most closely related to the brown algae and diatoms is the oomycetes, treated in [7.12.10](/7.12.10). The cytology of diatoms and the mathematics of size reduction (the MacDonald–Pfitzer rule) were covered in [7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.5.1) and are only linked here; general algal concepts are in [7.12.6](/7.12.6).

## 8.1. Common Features of the Stramenopiles

**Definition 8.1.1 (Stramenopiles)** The **stramenopiles** (heterokonts) are the group of eukaryotes whose motile cells bear two different flagella, a forward tinsel flagellum with two rows of tripartite tubular hairs and a backward smooth whiplash flagellum, and include photosynthetic groups such as golden algae, yellow-green algae, diatoms and brown algae and heterotrophic groups such as the oomycetes.

The stramenopiles were formerly known as the "yellow algae" or Heterokontae (the name used by Luther in 1899 when he separated the yellow-green algae from the green algae). Their photosynthetic members together form the Ochrophyta, which in the current classification comprises about 15 classes and belongs to the TSAR supergroup ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.3.2), [7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.5)). Shared features include:

- Flagella: the anterior flagellum is longer and bears two rows of stiff tripartite hairs (consisting of a base, a tubular shaft and terminal filaments) that reverse the direction of thrust, so that when a wave passes from base to tip the cell is pulled forward. The posterior flagellum is shorter and smooth, usually with a swelling (the flagellar swelling or paraflagellar body) at its base, which together with the eyespot located in the plastid directly below it forms a photoreceptive apparatus. In diatoms the sperm have only one anterior flagellum, in the Fucales the sperm have a short anterior and a long posterior flagellum, and in *Dictyota* the sperm have only a single tinsel flagellum.
- Plastids: four envelope membranes, of which the outermost (the chloroplast endoplasmic reticulum) is continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope, so that the plastid lies inside the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum; thylakoids in stacks of three, with a girdle lamella running around the whole plastid close to the envelope.
- Pigments: chlorophylls *a*, *c*₁ and *c*₂, β-carotene, and xanthophylls dominated by fucoxanthin (the yellow-green algae are an exception, with diadinoxanthin predominating), giving the thallus a golden, yellow-brown to brown color.
- Storage products: soluble β-1,3-glucan (chrysolaminarin, called laminarin in brown algae), stored in vacuoles and not in the plastid; in addition oil droplets, and mannitol in brown algae.

## 8.2. Golden Algae

### 8.2.1. General Features

The golden algae (chrysophytes) comprise about 200 genera and 1000 species worldwide, living mainly in fresh water. They prefer clear, cold, soft water that is poor in organic matter and slightly acidic (pH 4–6), and are common in springs, mountain streams and small valley pools, growing vigorously in winter, early spring and late autumn; they are sensitive to water quality and serve as indicator organisms for monitoring it.

- Thallus: mainly flagellate unicells or colonies, the cells of a colony arranged radially to form spherical or oval colonies, some with a transparent gelatinous envelope; next, nonmotile amorphous colonies; a few are amoeboid, and very few are branched or unbranched filaments.
- Cell covering: most motile species lack a wall and are naked and able to change shape; some are covered with many silica scales (*Synura*, *Mallomonas*), and some secrete a cellulosic lorica open at the front (*Dinobryon*); filamentous species have a cell wall consisting mainly of cellulose.
- Plastids: one or two, plate-like, parietal, with four envelope membranes; fucoxanthin predominates among the pigments, giving a golden or yellow-brown color. The storage product is chrysolaminarin (formerly called leucosin), in vesicles at the rear of the cell, together with oil droplets. At the front of the cell there are one or more contractile vacuoles; the eyespot is present or absent, located between the outermost thylakoid and the envelope of the plastid.
- Flagella: one or two, apical or subapical; when there are two, the longer one points forward and is of the tinsel type and the shorter one points backward and is of the whiplash type (in some species the short flagellum is reduced and only one is visible).
- Nutrition: many golden algae are **mixotrophs**, ingesting bacteria with pseudopodia or at the base of the flagella; some colorless species are wholly heterotrophic.

### 8.2.2. Reproduction

Unicellular species reproduce by longitudinal cell division; colonial species by fragmentation of the colony; in species with a lorica, after longitudinal division of the protoplast one daughter cell swims out of the lorica, attaches to the rim of the parental lorica and secretes a new lorica. Nonmotile species produce zoospores with one or two flagella.

The reproductive cell characteristic of the golden algae is the *statospore* (also called a resting spore or chrysophyte cyst; Chinese textbooks call it an endogenous spore): a motile cell comes to rest, loses its flagella and rounds up, and inside the protoplast forms a silica deposition vesicle that encloses the nucleus, plastids, storage products and so on; a two-valved silica wall is deposited inside the vesicle, with its apex projecting as a collared pore. The protoplasm outside the vesicle gradually disappears or flows through the pore into the wall, and the pore is closed with a plug of mucilage or silica. The statospore accumulates large amounts of chrysolaminarin and oil and can survive unfavorable conditions; its wall is smooth or bears punctate or spiny projections, with a form that varies with the species. On germination the wall dissolves at the pore, and the protoplast escapes like an amoeba and grows flagella. Statospores are preserved in lake sediments, and their species assemblages serve as indicators for reconstructing past lake acidity and temperature. Sexual reproduction is rare and isogamous.

### 8.2.3. Classification and Representative Genera

The classification of golden algae is very unsettled. Chinese textbooks divide them into five orders according to the evolutionary stages of thallus from unicell to filament: Chrysomonadales, Rhizochrysidales, Chrysocapsales, Chrysosphaerales and Phaeothamniales, the last two having cell walls. *Freshwater Algae of China* (in Chinese) divides the Chrysophyta into the Chrysophyceae (with six orders: Chromulinales, Chrysamoebales, Chrysocapsales, Hibberdiales, Hydrurales and Phaeothamniales) and the Synurophyceae. The Synurophyceae were separated as an independent class within the Ochrophyta on the basis of scale ultrastructure and pigment composition; recent molecular phylogenies show them to be very close to the Chrysophyceae, and many authors return them to that class as the order Synurales; *Phaeothamnion* is placed in another independent class (Phaeothamniophyceae).

- *Chromulina* (about 140 species worldwide, 7 in China): free-swimming unicells that can change shape, naked and wall-less, with a smooth or granular surface; one flagellum at the front and the other reduced, with two or more contractile vacuoles at the flagellar base; one or two plate-like golden plastids, in some species with a pyrenoid; chrysolaminarin at the rear of the cell. Mostly in fresh water, sometimes multiplying in large numbers to color the water or to form a golden film at the surface.
- *Dinobryon* (about 41 species worldwide, 8 in China): unicells or tree-like or tufted colonies, planktonic or attached; the cell is enclosed in a conical, bell-shaped or cylindrical cellulose lorica, transparent or yellow-brown; the cell is spindle-shaped, attached to the bottom of the lorica by a short cytoplasmic stalk at its end, with two unequal apical flagella, the longer extending out of the lorica; one or two plastids and one eyespot. After longitudinal division, one daughter cell swims to the inner rim of the parental lorica, secretes a new lorica and attaches by a short stalk, and repetition of this produces a tree-like colony; the colony is not very motile. Sexual reproduction is isogamous. It lives in clean, oligotrophic waters and is a common planktonic alga of ponds and lakes, disappearing when the organic content of the water is high.
- *Synura* (Synurophyceae; about 18 species worldwide, 7 in China): spherical or oval motile colonies with no colonial mucilage envelope, the rear of each cell elongated into a firm mucilaginous stalk, the stalks joined at the center of the colony and radiating outward; the cells are pear-shaped and wall-less, their surface covered with silica scales arranged in an imbricate spiral, the scales with ornamentation or stiff spines whose ultrastructure is the basis for species identification; two slightly unequal flagella at the front; two parietal yellow-brown plastids and no eyespot; 2–8 contractile vacuoles at the rear. The cells divide longitudinally, and the colony can also split into daughter colonies. It lives in puddles, rice paddies, ponds and lakes and can occur in large numbers in late autumn, early spring or winter, turning the water brown and giving it a fishy odor.
- *Mallomonas* (Synurophyceae; about 121 species worldwide, 37 in China): unicells without a wall, silica scales arranged in an imbricate or spiral pattern on the cell surface, with or without bristles on the scales; one flagellum at the front and the other reduced; two plastids on the two sides of the cell and no eyespot; three or more contractile vacuoles. Scales and statospores are well preserved in lake sediments and are used in paleolimnology.
- *Phaeothamnion*: a branched filament, with one basal cell specialized into a hemispherical holdfast and attached epiphytically to other algae; each cell has two plastids and stores granular chrysolaminarin; for reproduction 1, 2, 4 or 8 zoospores with two unequal flagella are produced inside the cell. The genus has only about 3 species and is a rare freshwater alga, found by Qinzhi Rao at Beibei in Sichuan; it represents the most advanced thallus type among the golden algae.

Until the twentieth century, the golden algae were placed in the protozoan class Flagellata, subclass Phytomastigina, with *Synura* called a "Synura animalcule" and *Dinobryon* a "bell animalcule". In the early twentieth century, chrysophytes with typical plant cell walls (the chrysosphaerid and phaeothamnioid types) were discovered whose protoplasts and reproductive cells are built like those of flagellate golden algae, and the golden algae were then moved into the plant kingdom. This history itself shows that no boundary between animals and plants exists among the unicellular stramenopiles.

## 8.3. Yellow-Green Algae

### 8.3.1. General Features

The yellow-green algae (*Xanthophyceae*, also called Tribophyceae) comprise more than 100 genera and 600 species worldwide (Quanxi Wang et al. 2007), living mainly in fresh water, preferring semipermanent or permanent soft-water ponds, and also on damp soil, tree bark and walls, growing vigorously in the cooler seasons; marine species are few. The plastids are yellow-green, and the algae look much like green algae, in which they were placed throughout the nineteenth century.

- Thallus: unicells, colonies, multinucleate tubular thalli and filaments; the simplest species have no cell wall.
- Cell wall: in unicellular and colonial species the wall mostly consists of two equal or unequal "U"-shaped pieces that fit together; in filamentous species the wall consists of many "H"-shaped pieces that overlap each other, the two halves of each "H" belonging to two neighboring cells, so that when the filament breaks it separates at the pieces and the end cell of the filament is "U"-shaped. The main component of the wall is pectic substance; some species deposit silica, and the walls of *Tribonema* and *Vaucheria* consist of cellulose.
- Plastids: one to many, disc-shaped, plate-like or band-shaped, parietal; four envelope membranes and thylakoids in stacks of three; some have pyrenoids. The pigments are chlorophyll *a*, a little chlorophyll *c* and β-carotene, with diadinoxanthin and vaucheriaxanthin the chief xanthophylls and no fucoxanthin, which is why the cells are yellow-green and not golden brown.
- Storage products: oil droplets and chrysolaminarin; no starch (no blue color with iodine, which distinguishes them from green algae).
- Eyespot: formed by a layer of oil droplets containing β-carotene, located inside the plastid envelope near the flagellar swelling of the short flagellum.
- Motile cells: two unequal subapical flagella, the longer pointing forward and of the tinsel type, the shorter pointing backward and of the whiplash type.

Reproduction is mostly by zoospores and aplanospores, and some species produce silica statospores similar to those of golden algae; sexual reproduction is rare, with reliable reports for only a few genera: isogamy in *Tribonema*, isogamy or anisogamy in *Botrydium*, and oogamy in *Vaucheria*.

### 8.3.2. Classification and Representative Genera

The Xanthophyta were formerly divided into six orders (Heterochloridales, Rhizochloridales, Heterocapsales, Heterosphaerales, Heterotrichales and Botrydiales); *Freshwater Algae of China* (in Chinese) divides them into two classes, the Xanthophyceae (four orders: Mischococcales, Tribonematales, Botrydiales and Vaucheriales) and the Raphidophyceae. In the current classification the Xanthophyceae and the Raphidophyceae are separate classes within the Ochrophyta.

- *Goniochloris* (Mischococcales; about 14 species worldwide): unicellular, floating; the cell is laterally flattened, triangular or quadrangular in apical view, with or without small spines at the corners; the wall consists of two "U"-shaped pieces that fit together, with fine hexagonal pores; 2–5 disc-shaped plastids; stores oil droplets and chrysolaminarin; reproduces by zoospores and autospores. Often found in acidic waters.
- *Tribonema* (Tribonematales; about 10 species worldwide, more than 10 in China): an unbranched filament of a single row of cells, attached at one end when young and later often aggregated into floccose masses; the cells are cylindrical or barrel-shaped, 2–5 times as long as wide, the wall consisting of two "H"-shaped halves and made of cellulose; the cells are uninucleate, with one to many parietal disc-shaped plastids and no pyrenoids; oil droplets and chrysolaminarin are stored. In asexual reproduction a vegetative cell becomes a zoosporangium and produces one or two zoospores with two unequal flagella, which escape through the gap between two wall pieces and on germination point downward and grow a holdfast, the lower cell no longer dividing and the upper cell dividing into a filament; sexual reproduction is isogamous. It lives in fresh water and is a common alga of early spring and late autumn.
- *Botrydium* (Botrydiales; about 7 species worldwide): a unicellular coenocyte living on damp soil, at the water's edge or on field banks. The upper part of the cell is a spherical, obovate or lobed vesicle above the soil surface, visible to the naked eye and yellow-green; the lower part consists of colorless branched rhizoids extending into the soil. The cell wall consists of cellulose and pectin, with a large central vacuole, and a thin peripheral layer of protoplasm containing numerous nuclei and many disc-shaped plastids. When water is present, the protoplast divides into many uninucleate zoospores, released through a pore at the top; under dry conditions or at the end of growth, all the protoplasm flows into the rhizoids and divides into many pieces, each containing several nuclei and secreting a thick wall, forming multinucleate thick-walled spores (cysts) that survive adverse conditions and, when conditions improve, germinate into many uninucleate zoospores; sexual reproduction is isogamous or anisogamous.
- *Vaucheria* (Vaucheriales; several dozen species worldwide, freshwater, terrestrial and marine): a sparsely branched tubular coenocyte attached to mud by colorless rhizoids, often making the soil surface a yellow-green felt; it grows in shallow water, on damp soil and in saline marshes on the seashore. There is a large central vacuole running through the whole thallus and a thin cell wall; the outer layer of the protoplasm contains many disc-shaped or oval plastids and the inner layer many small nuclei; the storage product is oil, with no starch; the thallus grows by elongation of the branch tips.
  - Asexual reproduction: the tip of a branch swells and a cross wall forms at its base, producing a zoosporangium; inside it the nuclei move to the surface, and at each nucleus a pair of flagella arises, so that the whole protoplast becomes one large, multinucleate compound zoospore bearing pairs of flagella over its whole surface; after release the flagella are retracted, a cell wall is secreted, and it germinates immediately, growing a tubular thallus and rhizoids from the two ends. Terrestrial species reproduce by aplanospores.
  - Sexual reproduction: oogamous; freshwater species are generally homothallic and marine species heterothallic. The antheridium is formed by a tubular outgrowth from the side or tip of a branch that curves into a hook, with a cross wall at the base and an opening at the tip, and contains many sperm with two unequal flagella; the oogonium appears slightly later, often near the antheridium, round or oval, with a cross wall at the base and a beak at the tip or side, and inside it a single nucleus develops into the egg while the rest degenerate. Sperm enter the oogonium through the beak and fuse with the egg; the zygote has a thick wall and after dormancy germinates, undergoing meiosis, into a new haploid thallus (zygotic meiosis). Specimens collected in the field often lack reproductive organs and are hard to identify to species; aplanospores can be taken back to the laboratory and cultured, and within 3 weeks they produce paired antheridia and oogonia in sequence. Common species in China include *V. geminata*, *V. sessilis*, *V. woroniniana*, *V. hamata* and *V. terrestris*.

The oogamous reproduction of *Vaucheria* led many phycologists to place it among the green algae; its pigments, plastid ultrastructure, storage products (oil rather than starch) and the unequal flagella of its motile cells, however, are those of the yellow-green algae. Molecular phylogeny confirms that *Vaucheria* belongs to the Xanthophyceae, and its resemblance to the tubular coenocytes of green algae (*Codium*, *Bryopsis*) is the result of convergent evolution.

The Raphidophyceae (also called Chloromonadineae) are wall-less unicells with two nearly apical flagella, one directed forward and of the tinsel type, the other directed backward and of the whiplash type, lying in a longitudinal groove on the ventral side; the plastids are numerous and disc-shaped, bright green to yellow-brown; oil droplets are stored; at the front of the cell there is a large reservoir connected to the gullet; in most species the cell periphery bears many radially arranged trichocysts that are discharged on stimulation. Freshwater species such as *Vacuolaria* and *Gonyostomum* live in ponds and marshes, and *Gonyostomum semen* forms blooms in humic lakes of northern Europe that make swimmers' skin itch; the marine *Chattonella* and *Heterosigma akashiwo* can form red tides that kill large numbers of farmed fish.

## 8.4. Diatoms

### 8.4.1. Structure of the Frustule

Diatoms are unicellular algae that may be joined into filamentous, fan-shaped, ribbon-like, radiating or tufted colonies, planktonic or attached; attached species often have mucilaginous stalks or are embedded in mucilaginous masses or tubes. The smallest cells are only 3–4 µm in diameter and the largest are several hundred micrometers long.

The **frustule** ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#def-20.5.1)) consists of two halves that fit together like a Petri dish: the larger one that fits over the outside is the epitheca and the smaller one inside is the hypotheca. Each half consists of a valve (the cover or the bottom plate) and several ring-shaped girdle bands attached to it, and the overlap of the girdle bands of the two halves is the girdle. A diatom cell thus has two views: that facing the valve is valve view, and that facing the girdle is girdle view. The wall consists of amorphous hydrated silica and organic matter and contains no cellulose; it bears striae made of rows of many small pores or chambers (areolae) or costae formed by thickening of the silica, and the shape, arrangement and density of this ornamentation are the main basis of diatom classification.

- Centric diatoms: the valve is mostly round or polygonal and radially symmetrical, with ornamentation radiating outward from a central point;
- Pennate diatoms: the valve is mostly boat-shaped, spindle-shaped, arched or club-shaped and bilaterally symmetrical, with striae or costae arranged pinnately on both sides of the axial area. In many pennate diatoms the center and the two ends of the valve have silica thickenings, the central nodule and polar nodules, and between them the valve wall bears a longitudinal slit, the raphe; in species without a true raphe the axial area is only an unornamented narrow band, called a pseudoraphe.

Pennate diatoms with a raphe can glide over the substratum, moving forward or backward along the long axis. Older textbooks attributed this to circulation of protoplasm at the raphe; we now know that the movement is driven by actin filaments and myosin beneath the raphe: the cell secretes mucilage strands from the raphe, one end of each strand is attached to the substratum, and the other end is pulled backward along the raphe by intracellular motors, so the cell glides forward.

Diatoms have one to many small disc-shaped or plate-like plastids with four envelope membranes, with or without pyrenoids; the pigments are chlorophylls *a* and *c*, β-carotene and a little α-carotene, with fucoxanthin the main xanthophyll followed by diatoxanthin and diadinoxanthin, giving the plastids an orange-yellow or yellow-brown color. The storage products are chrysolaminarin and oil. There is a central vacuole and the cell is uninucleate. Vegetative cells have no flagella; only the sperm of centric diatoms have a single tinsel flagellum, whose axoneme is 9 + 0, lacking the central pair of microtubules.

### 8.4.2. Reproduction and Auxospores

Cell division is the main mode of reproduction in diatoms. During division the protoplast swells and the two valves move slightly apart; the nucleus divides mitotically, the plastids and other organelles divide with it, and the protoplast is cleaved along a plane parallel to the valve, one daughter protoplast remaining in the epitheca of the mother cell and the other in the hypotheca. Each daughter cell takes the inherited half as its epitheca and secretes a new hypotheca inside it, so the daughter cell that inherits the hypotheca of the mother cell is smaller than the mother. As a result of successive divisions the mean size of the population gradually declines ([Eq. 20.5.1](/7.11.20#fml-20.5.1)). The decline is not unlimited: when cells have shrunk to a certain size, the original size is restored by means of an auxospore.

The **auxospore** is generally associated with sexual reproduction. The vegetative cells of diatoms are all diploid, and in sexual reproduction meiosis produces gametes (gametic meiosis, the diplontic life cycle, [7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.4.3)), and the zygote formed by fusion of gametes expands into an auxospore, enclosed in a wall of organic matter or lightly silicified material (the perizonium), within which a new cell of maximum size grows.

- Isogamy: for example *Cocconeis placentula* var. *klinoraphis*, in which two cells come together, each retains a single haploid nucleus after meiosis, and the two cells act as gametes and fuse, producing one auxospore;
- Anisogamy: for example *Cymbella lanceolata*, in which two cells come together, each undergoes meiosis and two of the four nuclei degenerate, the remaining two nuclei forming two unequal gametes that fuse with the gametes of the other cell, producing two auxospores;
- Oogamy: the sexual reproduction of centric diatoms is always oogamous, for example *Melosira varians*, in which one cell forms one egg after meiosis and another forms many flagellate sperm (called microspores in Chinese textbooks), which swim in through the gap between the epitheca and hypotheca of the egg cell and fuse with the egg, and the zygote becomes an auxospore.

Pennate diatoms have no flagellate gametes, and their gametes are amoeboid; centric diatoms have motile sperm, one piece of evidence that diatoms originated from flagellate ancestors. In some centric diatoms the auxospore is formed without sexual reproduction, by direct expansion of the protoplast of a vegetative cell. When conditions are unfavorable, many marine centric diatoms also form thick-walled resting spores that sink to the sea floor and germinate when conditions improve, and these are the seed source of red tides and algal blooms that recur year after year.

### 8.4.3. Classification and Representative Genera

Diatoms comprise more than 200 genera and about 16,000 species worldwide (Hustedt's count; current estimates put the number of living species at tens of thousands to 100,000). Chinese textbooks divide them into two classes:

- Centricae: mostly marine planktonic species, fewer in fresh water, divided into the Coscinodiscales, Rhizosoleniales and Biddulphiales.
- Pennatae: mostly freshwater species, divided according to the raphe into five orders: Araphidiales (only a pseudoraphe), Eunotiales (short raphe, only at the two ends of the valve), Monoraphidales (only one valve has a true raphe, the other a pseudoraphe), Biraphidales (both valves have a true raphe) and Aulonoraphidales (the raphe on a keel-like projection at the edge of the valve, tubular).

Molecular phylogeny shows that the centric diatoms are paraphyletic. The current classification (Medlin and Kaczmarska 2004; Adl et al. 2019) divides the diatoms into three classes: the radially symmetrical centric diatoms (Coscinodiscophyceae, *Coscinodiscus*, *Melosira*), the bi- or multipolar centric diatoms and the Thalassiosirales (Mediophyceae, *Cyclotella*, *Stephanodiscus*, *Biddulphia*, *Thalassiosira*), and all the pennate diatoms (Bacillariophyceae, the groups with and without a raphe). The raphid pennate diatoms are monophyletic, and the raphe arose only once.

Representative centric genera:

- *Melosira*: the cells are cylindrical and joined by the valves into chain-like colonies; the valve is round, flat or convex, and the girdle often has striae. *M. granulata* (now *Aulacoseira granulata*) is a common species in lake phytoplankton.
- *Cyclotella*: unicells, in some species joined by the valves into ribbon-like colonies; the cell is disc-shaped or drum-shaped, the valve round with radially arranged striae and areolae at the margin and a smooth or granular center; the girdle is smooth, without intercalary bands; many small disc-shaped plastids; each cell produces one auxospore. About 40 species, marine or freshwater plankton, occurring in large numbers in early spring.
- *Stephanodiscus*: the cell is disc-shaped, the valve round, with radiating bundles of puncta arranged in several rows at the margin and in a single row at the center; a common species of eutrophic lakes.
- *Coscinodiscus*: marine, the cell large, the valve round with a pattern of fine dots or meshes radiating from the center to the margin and a ring of small spines at the margin; one of the major groups of marine planktonic diatoms.

| Araphidiales | Only a pseudoraphe | *Tabellaria* (valve swollen in the middle, cells joined in zigzag colonies), *Diatoma*, *Meridion* (valve club-shaped, joined in fan-shaped colonies), *Fragilaria*, *Synedra* (valve long and rod-shaped) |

- *Pinnularia* (Biraphidales, Pinnulariaceae, placed in the Naviculaceae in Chinese textbooks; about 200 species): unicells or filamentous colonies; the valve is linear, elliptical to lanceolate with parallel sides, rarely swollen in the middle or symmetrically undulate; transverse parallel costae on both sides of the valve; the axial area is wide, sometimes exceeding one third of the valve width and broadened at the central and polar nodules; the girdle view is rectangular, without intercalary bands; two plate-like plastids on the two sides of the girdle, each with a pyrenoid. Found in both fresh and sea water.
- *Cymbella* (Cymbellaceae): the valve is crescent-shaped, linear, semi-elliptical or boat-shaped, with distinct dorsal and ventral sides, the dorsal side convex and the ventral side straight or slightly convex in the middle, and the striae slightly radiating; many species live attached by mucilaginous stalks.
- *Gomphonema* (Gomphonemataceae): the valve is club-shaped or lanceolate, the two ends asymmetrical, the upper end wider than the lower; the transverse striae consist of coarse or fine puncta and are slightly radiating; usually attached to the substratum by mucilaginous stalks, forming branched colonies.

The cells of *Rhopalodia* and *Epithemia* contain endosymbionts derived from nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria (spheroid bodies); they have lost photosynthesis and only fix nitrogen for the host, and are a nitrogen-fixing organelle in the making.

### 8.4.4. Significance of Diatoms

Diatoms are very widely distributed, from the poles to hot springs at temperatures up to 40 °C; they grow in fresh water, brackish water, sea water, damp soil, rock, bark and moss cushions, and reproduce throughout the year.

- Marine primary production: diatoms dominate the phytoplankton of cold, nutrient-rich waters and upwelling zones, accounting for about one fifth of photosynthesis on Earth; they are the main food of fish, shellfish and zooplankton, and mass occurrences of diatoms are directly related to fishery yields. Diatom frustules are heavy, and a large part of their production sinks into the deep ocean, contributing greatly to marine carbon export ([7.11.29](/7.11.29)).
- Diatomaceous earth: after diatoms die, the organic matter decomposes and the silica frustules accumulate on lake or sea floors, forming the loose, porous diatomaceous earth, which is used as a filter aid (for beer and sugar solutions), an adsorbent (Nobel used it to absorb nitroglycerin to make dynamite), thermal insulation and a polishing agent. Reliable diatom fossils first occur in the Early Cretaceous (about 120 million years ago; Early Jurassic records are disputed), and the Late Cretaceous and the Cenozoic were the heyday of diatoms.
- Indicators and paleoenvironments: diatom species are very sensitive to pH, nutrients and salinity, and the frustules are preserved for a long time, so diatom assemblages are widely used in biological assessment of water quality (diatom indices) and in reconstructing past environments; in forensic medicine diatoms in the lungs and bone marrow of drowning victims are used to determine the place of drowning.
- Harm: several species of *Pseudo-nitzschia* produce domoic acid, causing amnesic shellfish poisoning ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.5.1)); mass occurrences of *Melosira* can grow on the gills of fish and shellfish and cause death.

## 8.5. Brown Algae

### 8.5.1. Thallus Construction

The *brown algae* comprise about 240 genera and 1500–2000 species worldwide, almost all marine; there are only about 8 freshwater brown algae in the world, 2 of them in China, both collected by Qinzhi Rao from rocks in fast-flowing parts of the Jialing River in Sichuan (*Lithoderma zonatum* and *Sphacelaria fluviatilis*). Brown algae are cold-water algae, most abundant in polar and temperate seas, distributed vertically mainly in the low intertidal zone and down to about 30 m below the low-tide line, mostly attached to rock by holdfasts, a few floating at the sea surface (the floating *Sargassum* of the Sargasso Sea).

Brown algae are all multicellular, with no unicellular or colonial types. There are three basic thallus types:

1. A branched filament, in some species differentiated into a creeping part and an erect part, called a heterotrichous thallus (*Ectocarpus*);
2. A pseudoparenchyma formed by closely cemented branched filaments (many species of the Chordariales);
3. A parenchymatous body produced by cell division in all directions, with a holdfast, stipe and blade resembling root, stem and leaf in outward form, and an interior differentiated into epidermis, cortex and medulla (*Saccharina*, *Fucus*, *Sargassum*).

In brown algae with tissue differentiation, the epidermis (meristoderm) consists of one or two layers of small, tightly packed cells containing many plastids and covered by a mucilage layer, and is the site of photosynthesis and of secondary thickening; the cortex consists of large thin-walled cells and has a storage and mechanical role, with large mucilage canals in the cortex of kelp; the medulla lies in the center and consists of colorless long cells (medullary filaments) that serve in conduction and storage. In the medulla of the Laminariales there are trumpet hyphae: one end of a medullary cell is swollen like a trumpet and connected with the trumpet-shaped end of another cell, and the connecting end wall bears many small pores and is called a sieve plate. Tracer experiments with $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ showed that mannitol synthesized in the blades of giant kelp is transported to other parts of the thallus through this phloem-like structure at rates of tens of centimeters per hour. Trumpet hyphae are functionally similar to the sieve tubes of land plants, but the two arose independently, an example of **convergent evolution**.

The growth points of brown algae lie in particular places on the thallus: diffuse growth (cells all over the thallus can divide, some species of *Ectocarpus*), trichothallic growth (the growth point at the base of a hair, some species of *Ectocarpus* and the young stage of *Scytosiphon*), apical growth (division of an apical cell, *Dictyota*, *Sphacelaria*, *Fucus*) and intercalary growth (the growth point in the middle of the thallus, in kelp at the junction of the blade base and the stipe).

**Definition 8.5.1 (Holdfast)** The part at the base of a large seaweed that fixes the thallus to the substratum is called the **holdfast**; it is discoid, conical or branched and root-like, and serves only for attachment, not for absorbing water or nutrients.

### 8.5.2. Cell Structure

- Cell wall: the inner layer is cellulose and the outer layer consists of alginate (alginic acid and its calcium, magnesium and sodium salts, composed of mannuronic and guluronic acids) and fucoidan (a sulfated fucose polymer). Fucoidan and alginate make the thallus surface mucilaginous and keep the thallus from drying out when exposed to air at low tide.
- The cell is uninucleate, with a nuclear envelope and nucleolus, and the nuclear envelope often bears a conspicuous centrosome (brown algal cells have centrioles); there are one or several vacuoles in the center.
- Plastids: one to many, granular or small discs; four envelope membranes, the outermost chloroplast endoplasmic reticulum being continuous with the nuclear envelope; thylakoids in stacks of three. Many brown algae have pyrenoids, but these are not embedded in the plastid: they project from one side of the plastid, attached to it by a short stalk and covered with storage polysaccharide, and are called stalked pyrenoids. The Dictyotales, Sphacelariales, Laminariales and Fucales have no pyrenoids and are regarded as the more advanced groups. The pigments are chlorophylls *a* and *c*, β-carotene and several xanthophylls, among which fucoxanthin is the most abundant and masks the chlorophylls, giving the thallus its brown color.
- Storage products: laminarin and mannitol, both soluble, which can reach 5%–35% of dry weight.
- Physodes: brown algal cells contain characteristic small vesicles (called brown-algal small vacuoles in Chinese textbooks) containing phlorotannins, acidic in reaction, abundant in meristematic tissue, assimilatory tissue and reproductive cells, and serving in defense against herbivores, in absorbing ultraviolet light and in cell wall formation.
- Iodine: many brown algae accumulate iodine in large amounts; in kelps the iodine content can reach about 1% of dry weight, tens of thousands of times its concentration in sea water; iodine accumulates as iodide in the cell walls of the outer cells and is released under oxidative stress, serving as an antioxidant. Kelp ash was once the industrial raw material for extracting iodine, and iodine was discovered in 1811 in seaweed ash.
- Motile cells: sperm and zoospores are generally pear-shaped with two unequal lateral flagella, the longer pointing forward and of the tinsel type, the shorter pointing backward and of the whiplash type; in the Fucales the sperm are reversed, the backward whiplash flagellum being longer and the front bearing a beak-like projection; the sperm of *Dictyota* have only a single forward tinsel flagellum. The eyespot consists of 40–80 oil droplets and lies in the plastid.

### 8.5.3. Reproduction and Life Cycles

Vegetative reproduction: fragmentation of the thallus, in which an attached thallus splits longitudinally into two or more pieces, each growing into a new individual; fragments that detach from the parent and float grow into new individuals; *Sphacelaria* produces special propagules that drop off and attach to grow into new individuals. Floating *Sargassum* reproduces only by fragmentation.

Asexual reproduction: by zoospores and aplanospores. Brown algae have two kinds of sporangia:

**Definition 8.5.2 (Unilocular and Plurilocular Sporangia)** A sporangium formed by the enlargement of a single cell, whose nucleus undergoes meiosis to produce 4–128 haploid zoospores (or 4 aplanospores), is called a **unilocular sporangium**; a sporangium formed from a single cell by repeated transverse and longitudinal mitotic divisions into many small chambers, each producing one or two zoospores, is called a **plurilocular sporangium**.

Unilocular sporangia occur only on the diploid sporophyte and are the site of meiosis, their zoospores germinating into gametophytes; in *Dictyota* the unilocular sporangium produces only four aplanospores, called tetraspores. Plurilocular sporangia may occur on the sporophyte or on the gametophyte, and the zoospores they produce (neutral spores) do not undergo meiosis, have the same nuclear phase as the parent, and on germination repeat the parental generation. The plurilocular gametangia formed on the gametophyte are built like plurilocular sporangia but produce gametes.

Sexual reproduction: isogamy, anisogamy and oogamy all occur, and oogamy has arisen independently many times in the brown algae. The female gametes (or eggs) of many brown algae release volatile hydrocarbon pheromones (such as ectocarpene in *Ectocarpus* and fucoserratene in *Fucus*) that attract sperm of the same species and, in kelp and others, induce the release of sperm from the antheridia.

Life cycle: except in the Fucales, brown algae have alternation of generations. Isomorphic alternation occurs in *Ectocarpus*, *Sphacelaria* and *Dictyota*; in heteromorphic alternation the sporophyte is mostly large and the gametophyte small (*Saccharina*, *Undaria*, *Macrocystis*), and in a few the sporophyte is small and the gametophyte large (*Scytosiphon*); the Fucales have only a diploid sporophyte, meiosis produces gametes directly, and there is no alternation of generations (diplontic cycle).

### 8.5.4. Classification

Traditional classification divided the brown algae into three classes according to the presence and type of alternation of generations:

| Class | Life cycle | Orders |
|---|---|---|
| Isogeneratae | Isomorphic alternation of generations | Ectocarpales, Sphacelariales, Cutleriales, Tilopteridales, Dictyotales |
| Heterogeneratae | Heteromorphic alternation of generations | Chordariales, Sporochnales, Desmarestiales, Punctariales, Dictyosiphonales, Laminariales |
| Cyclosporae | No alternation of generations, no spores | Fucales |

Molecular phylogeny shows that none of these three classes is a natural group: the type of life cycle has changed independently many times in the brown algae and cannot serve as a basis for classes. The current classification (Silberfeld et al. 2014) divides the Phaeophyceae into four subclasses: the earliest-diverging Discosporangiophycidae and Ishigeophycidae are small groups of a few taxa; the Dictyotophycidae include the Dictyotales, Sphacelariales and others; and the Fucophycidae include the great majority of brown algae, among them the Ectocarpales (which has absorbed the former Chordariales, Punctariales, Dictyosiphonales and the family Scytosiphonaceae, to which *Scytosiphon* belongs), Laminariales, Fucales, Desmarestiales, Sporochnales, Tilopteridales (including the former Cutleriales) and others. Thus the Ectocarpales with isomorphic alternation, the Fucales with no alternation and the Laminariales with heteromorphic alternation are neighbors on the phylogenetic tree. Compared with the red and green algae, the Phaeophyceae are a very young group.

### 8.5.5. Representative Genera

*Ectocarpus* (Ectocarpales): widely distributed, on intertidal rocks or epiphytic on other algae. The thallus is a small, brown, tufted, uniseriate branched filament, differentiated into an irregular creeping attachment part and a tufted erect part whose branches taper toward the ends; the cells are uninucleate, with a few band-shaped or many disc-shaped plastids bearing pyrenoids; growth is mostly diffuse. The life cycle is an isomorphic alternation of generations:

1. On the sporophyte ($2n$), two kinds of sporangia form at the tips of lateral branchlets: unilocular sporangia produce, after meiosis, 32–64 haploid zoospores that germinate into haploid female and male gametophytes; plurilocular sporangia have hundreds of small chambers, each producing one diploid neutral spore that germinates into a new sporophyte, so that the sporophyte generation can perpetuate itself.
2. The gametophyte ($n$) has the same appearance as the sporophyte and forms plurilocular gametangia at the tips of lateral branchlets; the gametes have two unequal lateral flagella and are isogamous or anisogamous, gametes from different thalli fuse into a zygote, and the zygote germinates without dormancy into a sporophyte. Unfused gametes can undergo **parthenogenesis** and germinate into new haploid gametophytes.

The two generations can alternate or each continue for a long time on its own, and the life cycle is highly plastic. *Ectocarpus* is a model organism of the brown algae: its genome (about 200 Mb) was published in 2010, its sex is determined by U/V sex chromosomes in the haploid generation, and the switch between generations is controlled by TALE homeobox genes (*OUROBOROS* and *SAMSARA*).

*Sphacelaria* (Sphacelariales): mostly marine, epiphytic on large algae or on intertidal rocks. The thallus consists of erect and creeping branches and is a branched filament of several cell rows; the tip bears a large, dark-colored apical cell (hence the name), which divides transversely to form many segments, each of which divides longitudinally into 2–4 elongated cells and produces branches; the cells are uninucleate, with many disc-shaped yellow-brown plastids. Vegetative reproduction is by fragmentation of the thallus and by special propagules (three-pronged propagulate branches), which are clustered on the main axis or branches and on dropping off grow into new thalli. The life cycle is an isomorphic alternation of generations. *S. fluviatilis* of the Jialing River in China is a rare freshwater brown alga: under subaerial conditions it lies closely on rock, less than 1 cm tall and yellow-brown or dark brown, and in water it can reach 11 cm.

*Dictyota* (Dictyotales): grows on rocks of the low intertidal zone and in rock pools. The thallus is flat and membranous, dichotomously branched many times, attached to the substratum by branched rhizoids or a holdfast at the base. At the tip is a lens-shaped apical cell that divides longitudinally into two equal apical cells, each of which continues to divide, producing repeated dichotomous branching. The life cycle is an isomorphic alternation of generations: unilocular tetrasporangia form on the surface of the tetrasporophyte, in which meiosis forms four aplanospores that germinate into female and male gametophytes; sexual reproduction is oogamous, the male gametophyte forming sori of antheridia on its surface, each sorus consisting of 100–200 colorless antheridia surrounded by several rows of sterile filaments bearing plastids and forming a cup, with sperm having a single tinsel flagellum; the female gametophyte forms sori of oogonia on its surface, each sorus consisting of 20–25 dark brown oogonia, each producing one egg, which is fertilized outside the thallus after release, and the zygote germinates into a tetrasporophyte. The gametes of *Dictyota* are released synchronously under the control of the lunar phase.

*Saccharina* (Laminariales): the Laminariales grow mostly below the low-tide line in polar or subpolar regions, with large, structurally complex sporophytes and filamentous gametophytes, and a heteromorphic alternation of generations. The kelp cultivated in China was originally named *Laminaria japonica*; molecular phylogeny separated it and its relatives from the genus *Laminaria*, and its current name is *Saccharina japonica* (Lane et al. 2006). Kelp is native to Hokkaido in Japan, northern Korea and the Russian Far East coast, requires a mean summer water temperature not exceeding 20 °C, and the optimum temperature for sporophyte growth is 5–10 °C; it was introduced from Japan to Dalian in 1927, and in the 1950s Chinese phycologists (Chengkui Zeng and others) studied the relation between kelp growth and development and water temperature and created the methods of summer seedling culture (gametophytes and young sporophytes are raised indoors at low temperature and put out to sea in autumn) and raft culture, and by using fertilizer made it possible for kelp to grow in nutrient-poor waters, so that the cultivation area gradually extended to the southern coasts of Zhejiang and Fujian.

1. Sporophyte ($2n$): generally 3–4 m long and 20–30 cm wide, consisting of a branched root-like holdfast, a stout cylindrical stipe and a flat ribbon-like blade; the blade is undivided and without a midrib, and often bumpy when young. The growth point is at the junction of the blade base and the stipe (intercalary growth), so the blade grows from the base and is continually shed from the tip. Blade and stipe are both differentiated into epidermis, cortex and medulla.
2. When the sporophyte matures (September–October in the north), irregular dark brown sori (sporangial areas) form on both sides of the blade: many club-shaped unilocular zoosporangia are interspersed with elongated paraphyses, whose tips bear a transparent protective gelatinous cap. The sporangial mother cell undergoes meiosis and several mitoses to produce 32 haploid pear-shaped zoospores, each with two unequal lateral flagella; the zoospores are morphologically identical but already physiologically differentiated into female and male.
3. Within a few hours of release the zoospores attach and germinate, growing into tiny female and male gametophytes ($n$): the male gametophyte is a branched filament of a dozen to several dozen cells, each of which can form an antheridium producing one sperm with two lateral flagella; the female gametophyte consists of one to several larger cells, 2–3 times larger than the male gametophyte and with few branches, and a unicellular oogonium forms at the branch tip when it has 2–4 cells, each oogonium producing one egg.
4. After release the egg attaches to the mouth of the empty oogonium and is fertilized outside the parent to form a zygote; the zygote does not leave the parent and germinates after a few days into a young sporophyte. Kelp seedlings collected in autumn grow fastest in March–April of the following year and can reach 1.3–3 m in length by June; the natural life span is two years and in cultivation one year.

The large sporophyte ($2n$, with holdfast, stipe and blade) forms sori on the blade surface, and meiosis in the unilocular sporangia produces 32 zoospores ($n$); the zoospores germinate into male gametophytes visible only under the microscope (branched filaments, each cell producing one sperm) and female gametophytes (a few large cells producing oogonia); after release the egg stays on the mouth of the oogonium and is fertilized, and the zygote germinates on the female gametophyte into a young sporophyte. Blue sectors are diploid stages and orange sectors are haploid stages.](./plots/kelp-life-cycle.py)

Wakame (*Undaria pinnatifida*, Laminariales): a large annual brown alga of soft leathery texture, the blade lanceolate with a distinct raised midrib and thinner on both sides, often forming many pinnate lobes, densely covered with mucilage glands; the holdfast consists of forked branched rhizoids with adhesive discs at the ends; the stipe is short and nearly flattened-cylindrical, and at maturity two sides of the stipe bear overlapping, folded sporophylls, on which the sori form (popularly called the wakame root or "wakame cloud ear"). The life cycle is the same as that of kelp. Wakame is an important edible seaweed in East Asia and has spread around the world with ship ballast water and aquaculture, and is listed among the most harmful invasive species.

*Scytosiphon* (Ectocarpales, Scytosiphonaceae): heteromorphic alternation of generations, but with the gametophyte dominant. The gametophyte is the thallus usually seen, tubular or flattened-tubular with constrictions, tapering at the base and attached by a small discoid holdfast; growth is trichothallic when young and later intercalary; the medulla consists of large colorless thin-walled cells and the epidermal cells are small, each with a plate-like plastid and a pyrenoid; colorless hairs occur singly or in tufts; only plurilocular gametangia are produced. The sporophyte is a small crust-like body bearing only unilocular sporangia. The plate-like plastids, pyrenoids and plurilocular organs of *Scytosiphon* separated it from the Dictyosiphonales and gave it an independent order or family, the Scytosiphonales or Scytosiphonaceae.

*Macrocystis* (Laminariales): the largest alga, with sporophytes more than 45 m long that under optimum conditions can grow about 0.5 m per day. The holdfast is branched and the stipe branches many times, each branch bearing many blades with gas bladders, which float the blades near the surface and form a canopy. *Macrocystis* grows on the cold-temperate coasts of both hemispheres (the Pacific coast of North America, South America, South Africa, Australia and New Zealand), forming kelp forests, one of the most productive marine ecosystems, which provide habitat for fish, sea otters and sea urchins; the holdfasts and canopy make a complex three-dimensional habitat. *Macrocystis* is the main raw material for extracting alginate and can be harvested several times a year.

*Silvetia* (Fucales): the species originally named *Pelvetia siliquosa* is now *Silvetia siliquosa*, molecular phylogeny having separated the North Pacific species; the genus has about 4 species worldwide and 1 in China, distributed along the coasts of Liaoning and Shandong on wave-splashed rocks of the mid and upper intertidal zone, growing in spring and edible. The thallus is cartilaginous, yellow-olive when fresh and black when dry, 6–15 cm tall; the base is a conical, disc-like holdfast, the middle a short, flattened-cylindrical stipe, and the upper part dichotomously branched 2–8 times, with fewer branches in wave-exposed places and more in calm water; the interior is differentiated into epidermis, cortex and medulla, with sieve-tube-like structures in both cortex and medulla; there are no gas bladders on the branches. The plant is diploid, and for reproduction receptacles form at the branch tips: a mature receptacle is siliqua-like, thicker than an ordinary branch and with conspicuous nodular projections, each with a cavity opening to the outside, the conceptacle. *Silvetia* is monoecious with both sexes in one conceptacle: on the branches inside the conceptacle there are 2–3 unicellular antheridia among paraphyses, the first nuclear division of the antheridium being meiotic and the later ones mitotic, forming 64 sperm, which have two unequal lateral flagella, the backward one longer; the oogonia are also unicellular, and after meiosis only two eggs mature and the other nuclei degenerate. Eggs and sperm unite in the sea water, and the zygote develops into a new diploid plant.

*Fucus* (Fucales): the dominant brown alga of the intertidal zone on rocky shores of the North Atlantic and North Pacific. The thallus is flat and dichotomously branched, with a conspicuous midrib and a stipe and holdfast below; the tip has a tetrahedral apical cell, and the thalli of many species bear large raised gas bladders. The receptacles at the branch tips are swollen and contain many conceptacles, monoecious or dioecious; antheridia and oogonia are interspersed with paraphyses, and the mouth of the conceptacle has projecting ostiolar filaments. The antheridia are borne on antheridial filaments on the branches and after meiosis produce 64 sperm, which are elongate-ovoid, the backward whiplash flagellum longer and the forward tinsel flagellum shorter. The oogonium is ovoid and borne singly on a short stalk, producing 8 eggs after meiosis; the oogonial wall has three layers, which rupture one after another from the outside inward at maturity to release the eggs. After release the eggs emit a pheromone that attracts sperm, a large number of sperm surround the egg and make it rotate, and only one sperm enters the egg and fuses with the egg nucleus. The zygote of *Fucus* at first has no polarity, and the growth axis is established under the influence of light and other environmental gradients, a rhizoid growing from the end away from the light; this is classic material for studying the establishment of polarity in plant cells.

A: the thallus is dichotomously branched with gas bladders, the branch tips swollen into receptacles, the dots on them being the openings of the conceptacles. B: section of a conceptacle (monoecious case): ostiolar filaments at the opening; oogonia borne on the conceptacle wall, each containing 8 eggs after meiosis and one mitosis; antheridia borne on branched filaments, each producing 64 sperm; sterile paraphyses among them. *Sargassum* (Fucales; more than 300 species worldwide, more than 60 in China): grows on rocks of the intertidal or low intertidal zone and is the largest group of brown algae in warm seas. The thallus consists of three parts, holdfast, "stem" and "leaves": the holdfast is conical and disc-like, the stem cylindrical and branching radially in all directions, and the leaves flat or club-shaped, entire or toothed, with gas bladders and receptacles arising in the leaf axils, the bladders making the thallus float; the interior is differentiated into epidermis, cortex and medulla. The receptacles are spindle-shaped or cylindrical, monoecious or dioecious; the antheridia are produced from antheridial filaments, have two wall layers and form 64 sperm after meiosis, with flagella short in front and long behind; the oogonia are borne on the inner wall of the conceptacle and have 8 nuclei after meiosis, of which at maturity 7 degenerate, leaving a single egg; after the sperm escape they circle the egg with their anterior flagella and finally one sperm fuses with the egg. Common species in China include *S. pallidum*, *S. thunbergii* and *S. fusiforme* (formerly *Hizikia fusiformis*, edible). In the Sargasso Sea of the Atlantic live *Sargassum* species that reproduce only by fragmentation and float throughout life (*S. natans*, *S. fluitans*); in recent years floating *Sargassum* has bloomed on a large scale in the Atlantic and the Yellow Sea ("golden tides") and piled up on shores, causing damage.

### 8.5.6. Position and Significance of the Brown Algae

Brown algae are an ancient group of plants: Silurian and Devonian sediments contain kelp-like fossils, but the most reliable brown algal fossils are found from the Mesozoic on; molecular clocks place the origin and radiation of the Phaeophyceae much later than those of red and green algae. Brown algae have motile cells with two unequal lateral flagella, whose morphology, structure and pigments are similar to those of the motile cells of golden and yellow-green algae, and they may have evolved from a unicellular ancestor with two unequal flagella; within the Ochrophyta their sister group is a small group of filamentous algae with only a few species (Schizocladiophyceae and others), not the golden algae. Brown algae are the only stramenopile group to have evolved a complex multicellular body, tissue differentiation and conducting tissue, and together with red algae and green plants they represent the independent origins of complex multicellularity among eukaryotes ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5)).

The economic importance of brown algae is treated in [7.12.9](/7.12.9#sec-9.6): kelp, wakame, hijiki (*Sargassum fusiforme*) and *Silvetia* are eaten; alginate, mannitol and iodine are extracted from kelp, giant kelp and *Sargassum*; seaweeds are also used as fertilizer and feed.

## Summary

- The photosynthetic stramenopiles (Definition 8.1.1), the Ochrophyta, share anterior tinsel and posterior whiplash flagella, plastids with four membranes whose outer membrane is continuous with the nuclear envelope, chlorophylls *a* and *c*, fucoxanthin (except in the yellow-green algae) and chrysolaminarin in vacuoles; their plastids come from red algae, and their closest heterotrophic relatives are the oomycetes.
- Golden algae are golden-brown flagellates with silica scales or a lorica, surviving unfavorable conditions as silica statospores; yellow-green algae are yellow-green, lack fucoxanthin, and have cell walls of "U"-shaped or "H"-shaped pieces that fit together, with *Vaucheria* a tubular coenocyte with oogamous reproduction.
- Diatoms have a silica frustule and are divided into centric and pennate diatoms; division reduces the mean size and auxospores restore it; vegetative cells are diploid and meiosis is gametic.
- Brown algae are all multicellular, from heterotrichous thalli and pseudoparenchyma to parenchymatous bodies with holdfast (Definition 8.5.1), stipe and blade; meiosis occurs in unilocular sporangia, while plurilocular sporangia and gametangia divide mitotically (Definition 8.5.2); life cycles are isomorphic or heteromorphic alternation of generations, or the gametic meiosis of the Fucales; the traditional three classes are not natural groups.

## Questions

1. Why can a flagellum with stiff hairs "pull" the cell forward? What is the relation between the posterior flagellum and the eyespot in the stramenopiles?

   **Answer** A wave passing from base to tip along a smooth flagellum pushes water toward the tip, and the cell moves in the opposite direction (backward). The two rows of rigid tubular hairs on the anterior flagellum project at right angles to the flagellar axis, and when the wave passes, the flow produced by the sweeping hairs is opposite to that driven by the flagellum itself and predominates, so that the water flows from tip to base and the cell is pulled forward in the direction the flagellum points. The swelling at the base of the posterior flagellum (the photoreceptor) lies directly over the eyespot in the plastid, and the eyespot shades light coming from one side, so that the cell perceives the direction of light as it swims while rotating.

2. Compare the life cycles of *Saccharina* and *Fucus*. Where does meiosis occur in each? Which generation of *Saccharina* does the thallus of *Fucus* correspond to?

   **Answer** *Saccharina* has a heteromorphic alternation of generations: meiosis occurs in the unilocular sporangia on the sporophyte blade and produces zoospores that grow into tiny female and male gametophytes, which produce eggs and sperm. *Fucus* has no alternation of generations: meiosis occurs in the oogonia (producing 8 eggs) and antheridia (producing 64 sperm) in the conceptacles and directly produces gametes. The thallus of *Fucus* is diploid and corresponds to the sporophyte; its oogonia and antheridia can be seen as derived from unilocular sporangia, the gametophyte generation being reduced to the gametes themselves.

3. Why do diatoms become smaller and smaller as they divide? What is the relation between auxospores and sexual reproduction?

   **Answer** Each daughter cell takes the inherited half as its epitheca and builds a new hypotheca inside it, so the daughter cell that inherits the hypotheca of the mother cell is smaller than the mother, and the mean size of the population declines with the number of divisions. Only when cells have shrunk below a critical size can they reproduce sexually: diploid cells undergo meiosis to produce gametes, the gametes fuse into a zygote, and the zygote expands into an auxospore, in which a new cell of maximum size forms; in a few centric diatoms the auxospore forms without sexual reproduction. Size reduction thus acts as a "clock" controlling the sexual cycle.

4. The oogamous reproduction of *Vaucheria* once led to its being placed among the green algae. By which features can it be shown to belong to the yellow-green algae?

   **Answer** The plastids of *Vaucheria* have four membranes and thylakoids in stacks of three; among the pigments are chlorophyll *c* and vaucheriaxanthin and other xanthophylls characteristic of yellow-green algae, and there is no chlorophyll *b*; the storage product is oil, with no starch (no blue color with iodine); the flagella of the sperm and zoospores are paired and unequal (tinsel and whiplash). Molecular phylogeny confirms that it belongs to the Xanthophyceae; its resemblance to the tubular coenocytes of green algae (*Codium*, *Bryopsis*) is convergent.

5. Why are the traditional three classes of brown algae (Isogeneratae, Heterogeneratae, Cyclosporae) not natural groups?

   **Answer** The three classes were defined by life cycle type, but molecular phylogeny shows that the life cycle type has changed independently many times in the brown algae: the Ectocarpales with isomorphic alternation, the Laminariales with heteromorphic alternation and the Fucales with no alternation all belong to the subclass Fucophycidae, while the Dictyotales and Sphacelariales, which also have isomorphic alternation, belong to another subclass (Dictyotophycidae); the former Punctariales, Dictyosiphonales and Chordariales have all been merged into the Ectocarpales. The same life cycle type is distributed over different branches, so classes defined by life cycle are polyphyletic.
