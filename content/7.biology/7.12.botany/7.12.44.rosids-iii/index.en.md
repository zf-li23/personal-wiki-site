The malvids are the second major branch of the **rosids**, alongside the fabids ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)–[7.12.43](/7.12.43)). They comprise 8 orders, nearly 60 families and more than 30,000 species, and include many of the most important economic plants: the fiber plants cotton, jute and velvetleaf, the beverage plant cacao, the fruit trees citrus, mango, lychee, longan, pomegranate and guava, the vegetables and oil crops cabbage, kale, radish and rapeseed, the spices clove, Sichuan pepper and eucalyptus oil, the timber trees eucalyptus, maple, linden and the dipterocarps, and the most important **model organism** in plant biology, *Arabidopsis*. In Cronquist's system these families were scattered over two subclasses: Malvales, Capparales and Dipterocarpaceae lay in the Dilleniidae, and Myrtales, Sapindales and Geraniales in the Rosidae ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.5.2)). Molecular systematics unites them in a single monophyletic malvid clade and moves a few morphologically similar families elsewhere: Elaeocarpaceae and Oxalidaceae to Oxalidales, Rhizophoraceae to Malpighiales ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)), and Balsaminaceae to Ericales ([7.12.46](/7.12.46)). This chapter first treats the circumscription of the malvids (§44.1), then, in phylogenetic order, Geraniales (§44.2), Myrtales (§44.3), Sapindales (§44.4), Malvales (§44.5) and Brassicales (§44.6). The notation for floral formulas is explained in [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.6).

## 44.1. Circumscription of the Malvids

**Definition 44.1.1 (Malvids)** The monophyletic group formed in the APG system by Geraniales, Myrtales, Crossosomatales, Picramniales, Sapindales, Huerteales, Malvales and Brassicales is called the **malvids**; in phylogenetic nomenclature it is the Malvidae, and early APG papers called it the eurosids II.

The malvids originally comprised only Brassicales, Malvales, Sapindales and Huerteales, which a three-gene analysis already supported strongly. The positions of Geraniales, Myrtales and Crossosomatales wavered in early analyses (Myrtales once grouped with the fabids) and settled in the malvids only as the number of genes increased (Soltis et al. 2018). The current picture is that Myrtales + Geraniales are the sister group of all the other orders; among these, Crossosomatales and Picramniales diverge in succession, leaving Sapindales, Huerteales and (Brassicales + Malvales). The malvids have no clear morphological synapomorphy; they are a clade defined by DNA sequences.

The three small orders are only briefly introduced. The synapomorphy of Crossosomatales may be a fully lignified seed coat. China has *Staphylea bumalda* and *Euscaphis japonica* (follicles red, seeds black and glossy) of the Staphyleaceae, and *Stachyurus chinensis* (Stachyuraceae), whose spikes hang down and open in early spring before the leaves. Cronquist placed Staphyleaceae in Sapindales and Stachyuraceae in Violales. In Huerteales, *Tapiscia sinensis* is a deciduous tree endemic to China, with pinnately compound leaves and small, dioecious or polygamous flowers, formerly placed in Staphyleaceae; *Dipentodon sinicus* occurs in southwestern China and was once placed in Celastraceae or Santalales. Picramniales contains only 3 genera, native to the American tropics and formerly placed in Simaroubaceae.

## 44.2. Geraniales

### 44.2.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

APG IV Geraniales has two families: Geraniaceae (about 800 species, including *Hypseocharis* of the Andes) and Francoaceae in the broad sense (including the Melianthaceae and Greyiaceae of southern Africa and *Francoa* of Chile, about 40 species). Possible synapomorphies are carpels that are more or less separate in fruit and extrafloral nectar glands. This circumscription rests entirely on molecular evidence; the two families are not morphologically similar: *Francoa* and *Greyia* were formerly placed in a broadly circumscribed Saxifragaceae, and Melianthaceae in Sapindales.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Geraniales had 5 families: Oxalidaceae, Geraniaceae, Tropaeolaceae, Limnanthaceae and Balsaminaceae. In APG IV, Oxalidaceae belongs to Oxalidales of the fabids ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)), Tropaeolaceae and Limnanthaceae to Brassicales (§44.6.5), and Balsaminaceae to Ericales ([7.12.46](/7.12.46)); only Geraniaceae remains.

### 44.2.2. Geraniaceae

Diagnostic characters of Geraniaceae:

- Herbs or subshrubs, often with glandular hairs and a characteristic odor.
- Leaves simple, alternate or opposite, often palmately or pinnately lobed, or compound; stipules paired.
- Flowers bisexual, radially symmetric (*Erodium*, *Geranium*) or bilaterally symmetric (*Pelargonium*); solitary or in umbels or cymes.
- Sepals 5, persistent; in *Pelargonium* the adaxial sepal has a basal spur fused to the pedicel, so that the spur is not visible from outside.
- Petals 5, imbricate.
- Stamens 10 in two whorls, the outer whorl opposite the petals (obdiplostemonous); in *Erodium* only the inner 5 are fertile and the outer are staminodes; filaments often fused at the base; nectaries 5, alternating with the petals.
- Ovary superior, 5 carpels fused, 5-locular with 1–2 ovules per locule; the styles fuse above the ovary into a long column (carpophore) that splits into 5 lobes at the tip.
- Fruit a schizocarp: at maturity the five 1-seeded mericarps separate from the base of the central axis (carpophore) upward, each carrying a long beak developed from the style; seeds with no or little endosperm, embryo curved.

Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{5+5} \underline{G}_{(5:5:2)}$ (the outer stamens of *Erodium* are staminodes); for *Pelargonium*, $\uparrow K_5 C_5 A_{(7\text{–}10)} \underline{G}_{(5:5:2)}$.

The family and genus names, like the English names crane's-bill and stork's-bill, come from the fruit: the long beak makes the whole fruit resemble the long bill of a crane or stork. The movement of the beak is the key to dispersal in this family and differs in the three genera ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.3.5)):

1. *Geranium*: when the mericarps ripen, the outer tissue of the beak loses water and contracts, and the beak suddenly curls up from the base and catapults the seed out to a distance of several meters; in some species the mericarp is thrown together with the beak, in others only the seed is flung out.
2. *Erodium*: the beak coils helically when dry and straightens when moist, and bears backward-pointing hairs; after the seed lands, the repeated coiling and uncoiling with the daily cycle of humidity makes the mericarp drill into the soil like an auger. The coiling of the beak comes from the helical arrangement of cellulose microfibrils in the cell wall; it is a passive hygroscopic movement, and together with the mechanical structure for burrowing it is often used as an example in biomimetics.
3. *Pelargonium*: the beak bears long feathery hairs, and the mericarps are dispersed by wind.

Geraniaceae has about 5–7 genera and 800 species, mainly in temperate regions and southern Africa; China has 2 genera (*Geranium* and *Erodium*) with about 60 species, and *Pelargonium* is cultivated. Representative plants: *Erodium stephanianum*, with twice pinnately parted leaves and a beak 4 cm long, occurs north of the Yangtze and the whole plant is used medicinally. *Geranium wilfordii* and Nepal geranium (*G. nepalense*) have palmately parted leaves, and the whole plant is used in traditional Chinese medicine. Garden geranium (*Pelargonium* × *hortorum*) is native to South Africa and has bilaterally symmetric flowers, the three lower petals being larger; it is widely cultivated as an ornamental. Rose geranium (*P. graveolens*) yields geranium oil from its leaves, a rose-scented perfume material. The plastid genome of *Pelargonium* is among the largest known in angiosperms (about 218 kb) and has undergone extensive gene-order rearrangements, and the substitution rates of its mitochondrial and plastid genes are also unusually high.

Key characters of the family: herbs with stipules. Flowers 5-merous, stamens 10 (or 5 fertile), ovary superior with 5 carpels fused; the fruit is a schizocarp whose mericarps bear long beaks and separate from the base of the carpophore upward.

## 44.3. Myrtales

### 44.3.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Myrtales are mostly woody plants with simple, opposite, entire leaves and no stipules or very small ones; the stem has internal phloem (bicollateral bundles, [7.12.5](/7.12.5#sec-5.3)), and the vessels of the secondary xylem have vestured pits; the flowers are often 4-merous and have a well-developed hypanthium, with the calyx, petals and stamens borne on its rim and the inner surface often secreting nectar; the stamens are inflexed in bud; the carpels are fused with a single style; the ovary is superior to inferior with axile placentation. This combination of characters is rare outside Myrtales, so the order was already a largely natural group in traditional systems.

APG IV Myrtales comprises 9 families. Combretaceae is the sister group of all the others; Lythraceae and Onagraceae form one clade; Myrtaceae and the Neotropical Vochysiaceae form another; and Melastomataceae together with Crypteroniaceae, Alzateaceae and Penaeaceae form a third.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Myrtales had 12 families. In APG IV, Thymelaeaceae moves to Malvales (§44.5.3), Trapaceae, Punicaceae and Sonneratiaceae are merged into Lythraceae, and Memecylaceae into Melastomataceae; Vochysiaceae was formerly placed in Polygalales, but molecular evidence shows that it belongs to Myrtales and is closest to Myrtaceae. Rhizophoraceae formed its own order Rhizophorales in Cronquist's system, was sometimes placed in Myrtales, and now belongs to Malpighiales ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)).

### 44.3.2. Lythraceae

Lythraceae is a family enlarged in APG IV, with about 30 genera and 600 species. Diagnostic characters: herbs, shrubs or trees, the stem often 4-angled; leaves simple, opposite, entire; flowers bisexual, radially or bilaterally symmetric, 4-, 6- or 8-merous; hypanthium well developed, tubular or campanulate, with the sepals borne on its rim and often with appendages between the sepals; petals borne on the upper rim of the hypanthium, crumpled in bud and often wrinkled after opening (most conspicuous in crape myrtle); stamens usually twice as many as petals, borne on the inner wall of the hypanthium and of unequal length; ovary superior (inferior or half-inferior in pomegranate and water chestnut), 2–6-locular, with axile placentation; fruit a capsule.

Floral formula: purple loosestrife $\ast K_{(6)} C_6 A_{6+6} \underline{G}_{(2:2:\infty)}$.

Representative plants:

- Purple loosestrife (*Lythrum salicaria*) is a wetland herb with purple-red spikes and the classic example of tristyly (heterostyly): a population contains individuals with long, mid and short styles, and in each type the two whorls of stamens stand at the heights of the other two style types; Darwin (1877) carried out detailed pollination experiments with it ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3)). It is native to Eurasia and is a serious invasive species in North American wetlands.
- Crape myrtle (*Lagerstroemia indica*) is a deciduous shrub or small tree with smooth bark; the 6 petals are crinkled and long-clawed; the stamens are numerous and of two kinds: the 6 outer ones are especially long and supply the pollen used for pollination, while the many shorter inner ones, with bright yellow anthers, provide edible pollen for pollinators (heteranthery); it is cultivated widely.
- Pomegranate (*Punica granatum*) is native from Iran to the Himalayas and reached China in the Han dynasty. The hypanthium and sepals are red and fleshy and persist at the top of the fruit; the ovary is inferior, with the carpels arranged in two superposed tiers during development; the fruit is a special kind of berry (balausta), and the edible part is the juicy fleshy layer developed from the outer seed coat.
- Water chestnut (*Trapa natans*, including the forms cultivated in China) is a floating herb with rhombic leaves whose petioles are swollen in the middle into spongy air bladders that hold the leaves in a rosette on the water surface; the ovary is half-inferior, the fruit is a nut, and the persistent sepals become 2–4 sharp horns; each fruit has a single seed with unequal cotyledons, the larger of which stores starch and is eaten.
- *Sonneratia* is an important constituent of the mangroves of southern China and has peg-like pneumatophores projecting above the mud ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)).
- In *Cuphea* the hypanthium is swollen on one side at the base into a spur, and the plants are often cultivated as ornamentals.

### 44.3.3. Onagraceae

Onagraceae (evening primrose family) has about 22 genera and 650 species, cosmopolitan but mainly American. Diagnostic characters: herbs, rarely shrubs; leaves simple, alternate or opposite; flowers bisexual, radially symmetric, usually 4-merous; the hypanthium is often prolonged above the ovary into a tube, with the calyx, petals and stamens borne at its top; stamens 4 or 8 (in two whorls); ovary inferior, 4-locular, with axile placentation and many ovules; stigma 4-lobed or capitate; fruit a capsule, berry or nut; pollen grains often joined into chains by viscin threads. The embryo sac is the characteristic *Oenothera* type: it develops from a single megaspore at the micropylar end and has only 4 nuclei (the egg apparatus and 1 polar nucleus, with no antipodals), so the endosperm is diploid ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.6)).

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(4)} C_4 A_{4+4} \overline{G}_{(4:4:\infty)}$.

Representative plants:

- *Oenothera*: common evening primrose (*O. biennis*) is a biennial herb with yellow flowers that open in the evening and are pollinated by hawkmoths; its seed oil contains γ-linolenic acid. The genus is an important material in the history of genetics: de Vries (1901) took the variants appearing in *O. lamarckiana* as evidence for his mutation theory, and it was later found that most of them were changes in chromosome structure; in many evening primroses the chromosomes are joined by reciprocal translocations into a ring that segregates as a whole at meiosis, which keeps the individual permanently heterozygous ([7.4.15](/7.4.15)).
- Fuchsia (*Fuchsia hybrida*) has pendent flowers with a red calyx tube and sepals, purple petals, and exserted stamens and style; the original species are from Central and South America and are bird-pollinated, and hybrids are widely cultivated as ornamentals.
- Hairy willowherb (*Epilobium hirsutum*) is a wetland herb whose seeds bear a tuft of long white hairs (a coma) at the tip and are dispersed by wind; fireweed (*Chamerion angustifolium*) often grows in large stands on burned ground.
- *Ludwigia* is a wetland or aquatic genus in which the receptacle is not prolonged into a tube and the fruit is a cylindrical capsule.

### 44.3.4. Myrtaceae

Diagnostic characters of Myrtaceae:

- Evergreen trees or shrubs; the whole plant has oil cavities (secretory cavities, [7.12.5](/7.12.5#sec-5.4.2)), which appear in the leaf as translucent oil dots and give an aroma when crushed.
- Leaves simple, opposite or alternate, entire, leathery, often with an intramarginal vein (a lateral vein joining the others inside the margin and running parallel to it); stipules absent.
- Flowers bisexual, radially symmetric, solitary or in cymes, racemes or heads.
- Sepals 4–5, the calyx tube fused with the ovary; in *Eucalyptus* the sepals and petals are fused into a cap (calyptra, or operculum) that falls off as a whole at anthesis.
- Petals 4–5, borne on the margin of the disc, or absent.
- Stamens numerous, inflexed in bud, the filaments free or in bundles opposite the petals, the connective often tipped with a gland; the stamens are often the most conspicuous part of the flower (the "flowers" of paperbark, bottlebrush, eucalyptus and *Syzygium* are chiefly a mass of stamens).
- Ovary inferior or half-inferior, 2–5-locular, with axile placentation and a single style; fruit a berry, drupe or capsule; seeds without endosperm.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(4\text{–}5)} C_{4\text{–}5} A_{\infty} \overline{G}_{(2\text{–}5:2\text{–}5:\infty)}$.

The family has about 130 genera and 5,900 species in the tropics and subtropics, chiefly in Australia and the American tropics; about 10 genera and over 120 species are native to China, and many more genera have been introduced. It was traditionally divided by fruit type into two subfamilies: Myrtoideae (fruit mostly a berry, mainly in the American and Asian tropics) and Leptospermoideae (fruit mostly a capsule, mainly in Australia); molecular evidence shows that fleshy fruits arose several times within the family, and Wilson et al. (2005) instead recognized Psiloxyloideae and Myrtoideae, with almost all genera in the latter, divided into more than a dozen tribes. Representative plants:

1. *Eucalyptus*: trees whose juvenile and adult leaves often differ in form (heterophylly: in blue gum the juvenile leaves are opposite, sessile and bluish white, the adult leaves alternate, sickle-shaped and pendent); the sepals and petals form a cap, and the capsule is enclosed in a woody calyx tube. There are about 700 species, almost all native to Australia, where they dominate the forests. Mountain ash (*E. regnans*) reaches 100 m and is among the tallest angiosperms ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.1.1)). Eucalypts have been introduced into southern China for more than a century; the common species are *E. robusta*, *E. tereticornis*, blue gum (*E. globulus*), river red gum (*E. camaldulensis*) and *E. urophylla* and their hybrids, which are fast-growing trees for paper pulp and timber; distillation of the twigs and leaves yields eucalyptus oil (mainly 1,8-cineole), used in medicine and perfumery. Lemon-scented gum is now usually assigned to *Corymbia* (*C. citriodora*).
2. *Syzygium*: leaves opposite, fruit a drupe-like berry. Rose apple (*S. jambos*) has a rose-scented fruit and occurs in South China; wax apple (*S. samarangense*) is a tropical fruit; clove (*S. aromaticum*) is native to the Moluccas of Indonesia, and its dried flower buds are the spice clove, which contains eugenol. The spice clove is unrelated to lilac, *Syringa* of Oleaceae ([7.12.48](/7.12.48)); the two share the same name in Chinese.
3. Rose myrtle (*Rhodomyrtus tomentosa*) is a shrub with leaves three-veined above the base and gray-tomentose beneath, purple-red flowers and an edible berry; it is a common shrub of the red-soil hills of South China.
4. Guava (*Psidium guajava*) is native to the American tropics; the berry is rich in vitamin C, and the plant has become naturalized in South China.
5. *Melaleuca*: *M. cajuputi* subsp. *cumingiana* (often given as *M. leucadendra* in Chinese textbooks) has a gray-white, spongy bark that peels off in layers and is planted as a street tree in South China; the leaves of tea tree (*M. alternifolia*) are distilled to give tea tree oil.
6. *Callistemon* (now often merged into *Melaleuca*): the red spikes look like a bottlebrush, with long, numerous stamens.

Key characters of the family: evergreen woody plants with simple entire leaves bearing translucent oil dots and an intramarginal vein. Flowers 4–5-merous, stamens numerous and inflexed in bud; ovary inferior with axile placentation.

### 44.3.5. Melastomataceae and Combretaceae

Melastomataceae has about 170 genera and 5,000 species, pantropical; China has about 20 genera and over 100 species south of the Yangtze. The leaves are opposite with a distinctive venation: 3–9 main veins arise from the leaf base and converge at the apex, with many parallel cross-veins between them (acrodromous venation). The flowers are 4–5-merous, with stamens often twice the number of petals and poricidal anthers whose connective is often prolonged into a foot-like extension or bears appendages; in many species the stamens are of two kinds (heteranthery): one set is brightly colored and supplies edible pollen to bees, the other is darker and dabs pollen on the bee's back for pollination, and bees collect the pollen by buzzing ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.2)). Most Melastomataceae have no nectar, which is a synapomorphy of the family. Representative plants: *Melastoma malabathricum* (often given as *M. candidum* in Chinese textbooks) has large purple-red flowers; *M. dodecandrum* is a creeping subshrub with edible fruit.

Combretaceae has about 14 genera and 500 species, pantropical. The plants are woody, with simple leaves and often characteristic hairs (combretaceous hairs, unicellular and septate at the base); the hypanthium is prolonged above the ovary into a long tube; the ovary is inferior and 1-locular, with 2–6 pendent ovules of which only 1 develops; the fruit often has 2–5 longitudinal wings. Rangoon creeper (*Quisqualis indica*, now often merged into *Combretum* as *C. indicum*) is a climbing shrub with a slender calyx tube and flowers that change from white to red; its seeds are a traditional remedy for roundworms. Indian almond (*Terminalia catappa*) is a common coastal tree of South China, and the fruit of *T. chebula* is used medicinally. *Lumnitzera racemosa* of the mangroves also belongs to this family.

## 44.4. Sapindales

### 44.4.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Sapindales are mostly woody plants whose leaves are often pinnately compound without stipules (sometimes palmately compound, trifoliolate or simple); the flowers are small and 4–5-merous, with stamens often 8 or 10 in two whorls; the disc is conspicuous and lies inside the stamens (intrastaminal disc); the carpels are usually 2–5, fused or fused at the top; the ovary is superior, with 1–2 ovules per locule. Pinnately compound leaves and an intrastaminal disc may be synapomorphies of the order.

APG IV Sapindales comprises 9 families: Biebersteiniaceae and Nitrariaceae diverge in succession; the others fall into two clades, one of (Kirkiaceae + (Burseraceae + Anacardiaceae)), the other of Sapindaceae and (Rutaceae, Simaroubaceae, Meliaceae). Burseraceae and Anacardiaceae both have vertical resin canals (in the phloem); Rutaceae, Simaroubaceae and Meliaceae all contain limonoid and quassinoid triterpenes that are related in biosynthesis ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.7)), and lack resin canals.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Sapindales had 15 families, of which Staphyleaceae moves to Crossosomatales (§44.1) and Zygophyllaceae to Zygophyllales of the fabids ([7.12.41](/7.12.41)), while *Nitraria* and *Peganum*, split off from Zygophyllaceae, form the broadly circumscribed Nitrariaceae; Aceraceae and Hippocastanaceae are merged into Sapindaceae. Takhtajan and others set up a separate order Rutales for Rutaceae, Meliaceae, Simaroubaceae and their relatives, which APG does not adopt.

### 44.4.2. Anacardiaceae

Diagnostic characters of Anacardiaceae:

- Trees or shrubs; the phloem has vertical resin canals, and wounded bark exudes a resin or milky sap that is often poisonous and causes dermatitis on contact.
- Leaves alternate, odd-pinnate, trifoliolate or simple; stipules absent.
- Flowers small, radially symmetric, bisexual, unisexual or polygamous, in panicles.
- Sepals 5, fused at the base; petals 5, rarely absent (*Pistacia*); stamens 5–10, borne at the base outside the disc (the intrastaminal disc, that is, the disc lies inside the stamens); in *Mangifera* only 1 stamen is fertile.
- Carpels 1–5, ovary superior, often 1-locular, with 1 anatropous ovule per locule.
- Fruit a drupe, often slightly flattened; seeds with no or little endosperm.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_5 A_{5\text{–}10} \underline{G}_{(3:1:1)}$.

The family has about 80 genera and 700 species, mainly in the tropics and subtropics, with a few extending into the northern temperate zone; China has about 17 genera and 55 species, mainly south of the Yangtze. Classification and representative plants:

1. *Toxicodendron*: inflorescence an axillary panicle; the exocarp of the drupe is glabrous and the mesocarp waxy, white and longitudinally striate. Lacquer tree (*T. vernicifluum*) is a deciduous tree with odd-pinnate leaves and entire leaflets, native to China (cultivated in Japan and Korea) and cultivated for more than 7,000 years (lacquered bowls were excavated at Hemudu). The milky latex exuded from cuts in the bark (raw lacquer) consists mainly of urushiol, a catechol derivative, which is oxidatively polymerized in air under catalysis by laccase into a hard, acid-resistant, heat-resistant and rot-proof lacquer film; urushiol is a strong sensitizer and causes "lacquer dermatitis". North American poison ivy (*T. radicans*) is likewise known for its urushiol.
2. *Rhus*: inflorescence a terminal panicle; the drupe is covered with glandular hairs and red when ripe, the exocarp and mesocarp fused and the endocarp separate. Chinese sumac (*R. chinensis*) has coarsely serrate leaflets and a broadly winged rachis and is widespread in China; the aphid *Schlechtendalia chinensis* induces hollow galls on its compound leaves, the "Chinese gallnuts", which contain 50%–70% tannin (gallotannin) and are used for tanning, medicine and ink. The outer layer of the fruit bears a sour, salty white crystalline deposit that country people used as a substitute for salt.
3. *Mangifera*: mango (*M. indica*) is an evergreen tree with simple leaves clustered at the twig tips and polygamous flowers, of whose 5 stamens only 1 is fertile; the drupe is large with juicy fleshy mesocarp. It is native from India to Myanmar, has been cultivated for more than 4,000 years, is an important tropical fruit, and is grown in South China.
4. Cashew (*Anacardium occidentale*) is native to Brazil: the kidney-shaped drupe sits on a fleshy, pear-shaped "cashew apple" (an accessory fruit) formed from the swollen pedicel and receptacle; the seed (cashew nut) is eaten roasted, while the raw shell contains the irritant cardol.
5. Smoke tree (*Cotinus coggygria*) is a deciduous shrub or small tree with nearly round simple leaves that turn red in autumn, the main source of the red leaves of the Fragrant Hills in Beijing; the pedicels of the sterile flowers elongate and are covered with long hairs, so that the infructescence looks like a puff of smoke.
6. Chinese pistache (*Pistacia chinensis*) is a deciduous tree with even-pinnate leaves and dioecious, apetalous flowers; the wood is bright yellow and the seed oil can be used as a biodiesel feedstock. Pistachio (*P. vera*), of the same genus, is native to Central Asia and its seeds are eaten.
7. *Choerospondias axillaris* is a deciduous tree of the area south of the Yangtze; the stone of the drupe has 5 germination pores at the tip, and the fruit is edible.

Key characters of the family: woody plants with resin canals. Leaves pinnately compound or simple; flowers small with an intrastaminal disc; ovary often 1-locular with 1 ovule. Fruit a drupe.

### 44.4.3. Sapindaceae

APG IV Sapindaceae is a family in the broad sense that merges Cronquist's Aceraceae and Hippocastanaceae. Molecular phylogenies show that *Acer* and *Aesculus* are nested within Sapindaceae, so that separating them would make Sapindaceae a **paraphyletic group**. The characters shared by all three are: secretory cells that produce triterpene saponins; a conspicuous disc; stamens usually 8 with hairy filaments; and a radicle separated from the rest of the embryo by a deep fold of the seed coat.

Diagnostic characters: trees, shrubs or climbing herbs; leaves alternate (opposite in *Acer* and *Aesculus*), pinnately compound, rarely palmately compound or simple, without stipules. Flowers small, unisexual, polygamous or bisexual, radially or bilaterally symmetric, in panicles, racemes or cymes; sepals and petals 4–5 each, the petals often with hairs or scales at the inner base, sometimes absent; disc well developed, often outside the stamens; stamens 8–10; ovary superior, usually of 3 carpels and 3-locular (2-locular in *Acer*), with 1–2 ovules per locule. Fruit a capsule, drupe, berry, nut or samara; seeds often with an **aril** and no endosperm.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{4\text{–}5} C_{4\text{–}5} A_{8} \underline{G}_{(3:3:1\text{–}2)}$; *Acer* $\ast K_5 C_5 A_8 \underline{G}_{(2:2:2)}$.

Sapindaceae in the broad sense has about 140 genera and 1,900 species, widespread in the tropics and subtropics, with *Acer* and *Aesculus* representing its radiation in the northern temperate zone; China has about 25 genera and over 160 species (including *Acer*). APG papers divide it into 4 subfamilies:

| Subfamily | Characters | Representative genera |
|---|---|---|
| Xanthoceroideae | Sister group of the other subfamilies; petals large, disc with 5 horn-like appendages, 7–8 ovules per locule | *Xanthoceras* |
| Hippocastanoideae | Leaves opposite; northern temperate | *Acer*, *Aesculus*, *Dipteronia*, *Handeliodendron* |
| Dodonaeoideae | 2 or more ovules per locule, capsule often winged | *Dodonaea* |
| Sapindoideae | 1 ovule per locule, seeds often with an aril | *Sapindus*, *Koelreuteria*, *Litchi*, *Dimocarpus*, *Nephelium* |

Representative plants:

1. *Acer*: leaves opposite, simple and palmately lobed, or pinnately or trifoliolately compound; flowers small, with male and bisexual flowers on the same or different plants; the fruit is a double samara (a schizocarp that splits along the median line into two mericarps, each with one wing), whose wings make the mericarp spin as it falls, slowing its descent and dispersing it by wind ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.3.2)). There are about 160 species, about 100 of them in China, the center of diversity of the genus. Painted maple (*A. pictum* subsp. *mono*, often given as *A. mono* in Chinese textbooks) has 5-lobed leaves with entire lobes and wings spreading at an obtuse angle to nearly horizontally; Shantung maple (*A. truncatum*) has a truncate leaf base and is a tree of autumn color in North China; Japanese maple (*A. palmatum*) has leaves cut 5–9-lobed to the base and is widely cultivated as an ornamental; trident maple (*A. buergerianum*) has 3-lobed leaves; box elder (*A. negundo*) has odd-pinnate leaves and dioecious flowers without petals or disc and is native to North America. Sugar maple (*A. saccharum*) of North America yields sap in early spring that is boiled down to maple syrup, and its leaf is the emblem on the Canadian flag.
2. *Dipteronia*: endemic to China, with odd-pinnate leaves and a fruit surrounded by a round wing with the seed at the center, resembling an old Chinese coin. *D. sinensis* occurs from central and northwestern to southwestern China.
3. *Aesculus*: leaves palmately compound and opposite, with 5–9 leaflets; flowers bilaterally symmetric in large, erect panicles; capsule, the seeds large and chestnut brown with a large hilum, containing the poisonous saponin aescin. *A. chinensis* is a tree often planted at temples (commonly called the "sala tree", which is not the sal of the Buddhist scriptures); European horse chestnut (*A. hippocastanum*) is a street tree in Europe.
4. Soapberry (*Sapindus saponaria*, often given as *S. mukorossi* in Chinese textbooks) is a deciduous tree with even-pinnate leaves and a drupe-like fruit whose pericarp contains saponins and was formerly used as soap; the seeds are black and hard and were used as prayer beads.
5. *Koelreuteria*: *K. paniculata* has once- or twice-pinnate leaves and a large panicle of yellow, bilaterally symmetric flowers; the capsule is bladder-like, its three membranous valves resembling a lantern and turning reddish brown when ripe, with black seeds; it occurs from North China to the Yangtze valley and is a common street tree in the north. *K. bipinnata* has bipinnate leaves and a capsule that is pink to purplish red and occurs south of the Yangtze.
6. Lychee (*Litchi chinensis*) is an evergreen tree with even-pinnate leaves and no petals; the pericarp has wart-like protuberances and the seed is enveloped by a white, juicy aril; it is native to South China and has been cultivated in Lingnan for more than 2,000 years. Longan (*Dimocarpus longan*) has petals and a pericarp with warty bumps when young that becomes smooth later, and a white, translucent aril; it occurs in South and Southwest China. In both, the edible part is the aril developed from the funiculus (or from the funiculus and the outer integument). Rambutan (*Nephelium lappaceum*) has a pericarp with long soft spines.
7. *Xanthoceras sorbifolium* is a shrub or small tree peculiar to North China, with showy white flowers whose petals are purple-red at the base; the capsule is woody, and the seed oil is edible, making it a woody oil plant of the north.
8. *Dodonaea viscosa* is a shrub with simple leaves and a 2–3-winged capsule; it is a pioneer in the dry-hot valleys of southwestern China.

A: the double samara of Acer: two mericarps, each with one wing, joined along the median line, the wings with radiating veins; at maturity they separate and spin down. B: the fruit of Dipteronia, each mericarp surrounded by a round wing with the seed at the center. Key characters of the family: woody plants, mostly with pinnate leaves (in *Acer*, simple, opposite and palmately lobed); flowers small and polygamous with a well-developed disc and usually 8 stamens; ovary superior, usually of 3 carpels and 3-locular. The seeds often have an aril and no endosperm; *Acer* has a double samara.

### 44.4.4. Rutaceae

Diagnostic characters of Rutaceae:

- Trees, shrubs or woody climbers, rarely herbs; often armed with thorns (branch thorns or prickles).
- The whole plant has lysigenous oil cavities ([7.12.5](/7.12.5#sec-5.4.2)), which appear in the leaf as translucent oil glands and give a strong aroma when crushed, from volatile oils (limonene, linalool and others).
- Leaves alternate, rarely opposite, pinnately compound, trifoliolate, unifoliolate or simple; stipules absent.
- Flowers bisexual or unisexual, radially symmetric, rarely bilaterally symmetric (*Dictamnus*); sepals and petals 4–5 each, free.
- Stamens usually twice as many as petals, in two whorls with the outer whorl opposite the petals (obdiplostemonous), rarely numerous (15–60 in *Citrus*, the filaments fused into several bundles); disc well developed, inside the stamens.
- Carpels 4–5 (or 1 to many), free or fused, ovary superior, with 1–2 ovules per locule (many in *Citrus*).
- Fruit a follicle (*Zanthoxylum*), capsule, drupe (*Phellodendron*), samara or **hesperidium** (*Citrus*).

Floral formula: $\ast K_{4\text{–}5} C_{4\text{–}5} A_{8\text{–}10} \underline{G}_{(4\text{–}5)}$; *Citrus* $\ast K_{(5)} C_5 A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{(8\text{–}15:8\text{–}15:\infty)}$.

The family has about 155 genera and 2,000 species, from the tropics to the temperate zone and chiefly in southern Africa and Australia; China has about 22 genera and 126 species (Flora of China). APG IV Rutaceae also absorbs Cneoraceae of the Mediterranean and Ptaeroxylaceae of southern Africa. In the molecular phylogeny of Appelhans et al. (2021) Rutaceae is divided into 6 subfamilies, of which Aurantioideae (fruit berry-like, leaves often unifoliolate) and Zanthoxyloideae (fruit mostly a follicle or drupe) include most of the genera in China.

Classification and representative plants:

1. *Zanthoxylum*: trees or shrubs, often with prickles; leaves odd-pinnate with translucent oil dots on the leaflets; flowers small and unisexual; carpels free or fused at the base, the fruit consisting of 1–5 follicles that each dehisce and contain a single black, glossy seed. Sichuan pepper (*Z. bungeanum*) has a red pericarp with warty oil dots, occurs throughout China, and is a traditional spice; its numbing sensation comes from hydroxy-α-sanshool, which acts on tactile receptors of the skin and mouth to produce a vibration-like numbness. *Z. simulans* has a narrowly winged rachis; *Z. armatum* has a broadly winged rachis, and the "green Sichuan pepper" of Sichuan is a cultivated form of this species.
2. *Phellodendron*: deciduous trees with a thick cork layer in the bark; leaves odd-pinnate and opposite; flowers unisexual and dioecious; fruit a drupe. Amur cork tree (*P. amurense*) occurs in Northeast and North China, and the inner bark is yellow and contains berberine and is used medicinally; the cork layer can be made into stoppers. *P. chinense* occurs in southwestern China.
3. *Citrus*: evergreen, often with branch thorns; leaves unifoliolate, the petiole often winged; flowers bisexual, solitary or clustered, with 5 white, fragrant petals; stamens 15–60, the filaments often fused into several bundles; ovary 8–15-locular with many ovules per locule. The fruit is a hesperidium ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.2.2)): the exocarp is leathery with many oil cavities (oil glands); the mesocarp is white, loose and spongy (the vascular bundles in it form the albedo "strings"); the endocarp is membranous and divided into segments, from whose inner surface grow many juice-filled multicellular juice vesicles, which are the edible part. Flora of China (2008) merges *Fortunella* and *Poncirus* into *Citrus*: kumquat (*C. japonica*, formerly *Fortunella*) has a 3–6-locular ovary with 2 ovules per locule and a small fruit with a sweet rind; trifoliate orange (*C. trifoliata*, formerly *Poncirus*) is deciduous with trifoliolate leaves and hairy fruit that is sour, bitter and inedible, and is often used as a hedge and as a cold-hardy rootstock for grafting citrus.
4. *Murraya*: *M. exotica* and *M. paniculata* are evergreen shrubs with odd-pinnate leaves, very fragrant white flowers and red berries, planted as hedges in South China.
5. Others: *Tetradium ruticarpum* (often given as *Evodia rutaecarpa* in Chinese textbooks) has fruit used medicinally; gas plant (*Dictamnus dasycarpus*) is a herb with bilaterally symmetric flowers whose root bark is used medicinally; wampee (*Clausena lansium*) is a fruit tree of South China; rue (*Ruta graveolens*) is a strongly scented Mediterranean herb that gives the family its name.

The classification of cultivated citrus has long been confused, with the number of "species" recognized by different authors ranging from a dozen to more than a hundred, because most cultivated citrus are hybrids or hybrid derivatives among a few wild species and are often maintained by apomixis (nucellar embryony, [7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.6.2)) and grafting, so that a "species" is in fact a clone. Genome sequencing (Wu et al. 2014, 2018) has confirmed that cultivated citrus derives mainly from 4 ancestral species:

| Ancestral species | Scientific name | Fruit |
|---|---|---|
| Pomelo | *C. maxima* | Large, thick rind, monoembryonic |
| Mandarin | *C. reticulata* | Small, rind easily peeled |
| Citron | *C. medica* | Rind thick and rich in oil, little juice |
| Small-flowered papeda (a wild species of the Philippines) | *C. micrantha* | Small, sour |

The other cultivated citrus are hybrids among them to various degrees: sour orange (*C.* × *aurantium*) is a pomelo × mandarin hybrid (an F1 type); sweet orange (*C.* × *sinensis*) is also a pomelo × mandarin hybrid but has undergone complex backcrossing, so that its genome is mostly mandarin; grapefruit is a hybrid of pomelo and sweet orange; lemon is a hybrid of sour orange and citron; Mexican lime is a hybrid of *C. micrantha* and citron. Buddha's hand (*C. medica* 'Fingered') is a variety of citron whose carpels separate into finger-like parts. Many mandarins themselves carry introgressed pomelo genes. Molecular-clock estimates place the origin of *Citrus* in the late Miocene (about 8 million years ago) at the southeastern foot of the Himalayas, followed by dispersal to Southeast Asia and Australia. This is a model example of using genomes to solve a classification problem of cultivated plants: a classification can write cultivated citrus as combinations of ancestral species according to their hybrid origin (for example *C.* × *sinensis* = *C. maxima* × *C. reticulata*) without naming a species for each clone ([7.10.12](/7.10.12#sec-12.1.7)).

![Fruit and hybrid origin of Citrus (Rutaceae). A: schematic cross section of a hesperidium: the exocarp is leathery and covered with oil cavities (oil glands), the mesocarp is white and spongy, the endocarp is membranous and divided into segments filled with juice vesicles growing from its inner surface, and the seeds are attached to the central axis (axile placentation). B: hybrid relationships of cultivated citrus (after Wu et al. 2018): the four ancestral species (pomelo, mandarin, citron and small-flowered papeda) lie on the outer ring, and arrows point to their hybrids; sweet orange and grapefruit arose through several crosses and backcrosses](./plots/citrus-hybrids.py)

Economic importance: Rutaceae is one of the economically most important families. Citrus fruits are among the world's largest fruit crops, the flesh eaten fresh or made into juice and the rind yielding essential oils (orange and lemon oil) and pectin; dried tangerine peel and immature fruits are common drugs in traditional Chinese medicine; Sichuan pepper is an important condiment; *Phellodendron*, *Tetradium* and *Dictamnus* are medicinal.

Key characters of the family: well-developed oil cavities, with translucent oil dots in the leaves and aromatic oils; leaves mostly compound or unifoliolate; stamens usually twice the number of petals, the outer whorl opposite the petals, and a well-developed disc; ovary superior.

### 44.4.5. Meliaceae, Simaroubaceae and Burseraceae

Meliaceae has about 50 genera and 600 species, pantropical; China has about 17 genera and 40 species. The plants are woody with alternate, pinnately compound leaves without stipules; flowers bisexual and 4–5-merous; the best diagnostic character of the family is that the filaments are fused into a staminal tube with the anthers borne at its mouth or inside it; the disc is well developed; the ovary is superior and 2–5-locular; the fruit is a capsule, berry or drupe, and the seeds are often winged. Floral formula: $\ast K_{(4\text{–}5)} C_{4\text{–}5} A_{(8\text{–}10)} \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}5:2\text{–}5:1\text{–}2)}$. Representative plants: chinaberry (*Melia azedarach*) is a deciduous tree with twice- to thrice-pinnate leaves, pale purple flowers and yellow drupes; the fruit and bark contain toxic limonoids such as toosendanin and can be used as an insecticide. Chinese toon (*Toona sinensis*) is a deciduous tree with even-pinnate leaves whose young shoots have a distinctive aroma and are a traditional vegetable of North China; the capsule splits into 5 valves, and the seeds are winged at one end. *Aglaia odorata* is an evergreen shrub with small, yellow, very fragrant flowers, cultivated in South China; the seeds of neem (*Azadirachta indica*) contain azadirachtin, an important botanical insecticide; mahogany (*Swietenia mahagoni*) is a famous timber for fine furniture.

Simaroubaceae has about 20 genera and 110 species, pantropical. The plants are woody with alternate, pinnately compound leaves, and the bark and wood taste bitter (from quassinoids); flowers small, unisexual or polygamous, with a disc; carpels 2–5, free or fused at the base, and the fruit is often a samara or drupe. Tree of heaven (*Ailanthus altissima*) is a deciduous tree with odd-pinnate leaves bearing 1–2 glandular teeth on each side at the base of each leaflet, and the crushed leaf has an unpleasant smell; the 5 free carpels of each female flower develop into 1–5 samaras, each with one seed at the center of the wing. It is highly adaptable and a common native tree in North China, and has become a serious invasive species after introduction to North America and Europe; it is a host of the ailanthus silkmoth (*Samia cynthia*). *Picrasma quassioides* and *Brucea javanica* are used medicinally.

Burseraceae has about 19 genera and 750 species, pantropical. The plants are woody with resin canals, the bark often smooth and peeling in flakes and yielding aromatic resin; leaves odd-pinnate; fruit a drupe. Chinese white olive (*Canarium album*) occurs in South China, and its oblong drupe tastes bitter and astringent at first and then sweet; *C. pimela* has a purple-black fruit. The resins of frankincense (*Boswellia sacra*) and myrrh (*Commiphora myrrha*) were famous perfumes of ancient Arabia and East Africa.

### 44.4.6. Nitrariaceae

In APG IV, Nitrariaceae comprises *Nitraria*, *Peganum*, *Malacocarpus* and *Tetradiclis* (absorbing Peganaceae and Tetradiclidaceae), all formerly placed in Zygophyllaceae ([7.12.41](/7.12.41)). It is one of the earliest-diverging lineages in Sapindales and is adapted to arid and saline environments. *Nitraria* consists of much-branched shrubs whose twig tips often become thorns; the leaves are simple, fleshy and clustered; the flowers are small and 5-merous; the fruit is a drupe with a deep red juice. *N. sibirica* and *N. tangutorum* occur in the deserts and saline soils of northwestern and northern China, often forming nebkhas (coppice dunes) on sand dunes, and are important sand-fixing plants; the fruit is edible. Syrian rue (*Peganum harmala*) is a perennial herb with fleshy, deeply parted leaves; the seeds contain harmine, yield a red dye, and are poisonous.

## 44.5. Malvales

### 44.5.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Malvales are mostly woody plants with abundant bast fibers in the bark; they often have stellate hairs or peltate scales; mucilage cells or cavities are present; the secondary phloem is stratified (storied) and wedge-shaped; the leaves are alternate, simple and often palmately veined, with stipules; the sepals are valvate; the stamens are often numerous and develop centrifugally; the seed has a palisade layer in the inner integument, and the seed oil contains cyclopropene fatty acids. The last two are the most reliable anatomical and chemical synapomorphies of the order.

APG IV Malvales comprises 10 families: Neuradaceae (formerly placed near Rosaceae) and Thymelaeaceae diverge in succession, and the relationships of the others are not fully resolved, the main ones being Bixaceae (including Cochlospermaceae), Cistaceae, Dipterocarpaceae, Muntingiaceae, the parasitic Cytinaceae, and Malvaceae.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Malvales had only 5 families: Tiliaceae, Malvaceae, Elaeocarpaceae, Sterculiaceae and Bombacaceae. Of the families in the Dilleniidae, Dipterocarpaceae was in Theales, Bixaceae and Cistaceae in Violales, and Thymelaeaceae in Myrtales. In APG IV, Elaeocarpaceae moves to Oxalidales ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)), and the other 4 families are merged into the broadly circumscribed Malvaceae.

### 44.5.2. Malvaceae

APG IV Malvaceae (Malvaceae s.l.) merges Cronquist's Tiliaceae, Sterculiaceae, Bombacaceae and the narrowly circumscribed Malvaceae. The close relationship of these four families has been recognized since Linnaeus (the "Columniferae"), but the boundaries between them have always been hard to draw. A combined molecular and morphological analysis (Judd and Manchester 1997; Bayer et al. 1999; Alverson et al. 1999) showed that Sterculiaceae and Tiliaceae are both **polyphyletic groups** and Bombacaceae is paraphyletic, with the traditional genera interleaved among them: for example, Byttnerioideae, containing *Theobroma* (Sterculiaceae), and Grewioideae, containing *Grewia* (Tiliaceae), are sister groups, *Durio* (Bombacaceae) is closer to *Helicteres* of Sterculiaceae, and the narrowly circumscribed Malvaceae is nested within part of Bombacaceae. Only by merging them into a single family is a monophyletic Malvaceae obtained. The synapomorphies of Malvaceae in the broad sense are nectaries consisting of dense multicellular glandular hairs, usually on the inner side of the sepals; a special vertical "tile cell" in the wood rays; and inflorescences made up of a special "bicolor unit".

Diagnostic characters:

- Trees, shrubs or herbs; the stem bark is rich in bast fibers; mucilage canals (cavities) present; stellate hairs or peltate scales common.
- Leaves simple and alternate, often palmately veined and palmately lobed, or palmately compound (Bombacoideae); stipules caducous.
- Flowers bisexual, radially symmetric; calyx of 5 sepals, valvate, fused at the base; often with an *epicalyx* of bracts below the calyx.
- Petals 5, contorted (each petal overlaps its neighbor on one side, so that the whole is like a windmill) and often fused at the base with the staminal tube; sometimes absent (*Sterculia*).
- Stamens numerous (rarely 5), the filaments fused to various degrees: in Malvoideae they form a staminal tube around the style (monadelphous stamens, [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.2.4)), with 1-locular, kidney-shaped anthers (in fact "half-anthers" formed when the 2-locular anther splits lengthwise during development); in Tilioideae the stamens are united at the base into bundles; in Sterculioideae, cacao and others there is often a gynophore or androgynophore.
- Pollen grains often very large and spiny (Malvoideae); the pollen of Malvoideae is often more than 100 µm in diameter, among the largest in angiosperms.
- Carpels 2 to many, fused, ovary superior, with axile placentation; in *Malva* and relatives the many carpels are arranged in a ring on a flattened receptacle.
- Fruit a loculicidal capsule, schizocarp, drupe, berry or follicle; seeds sometimes hairy (cotton) or winged.

Floral formula: Malvoideae $\ast K_{(5)} C_5 A_{(\infty)} \underline{G}_{(3\text{–}\infty:3\text{–}\infty)}$ (with an epicalyx outside the calyx); *Tilia* $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{\infty} \underline{G}_{(5:5:2)}$.

![The merger of Malvaceae with four former families. Malvaceae in the broad sense has about 240 genera and 4,200 species, cosmopolitan but chiefly tropical; China has about 50 genera and 260 species. The nine subfamilies (Bayer et al. 1999) and their correspondence to the old families are as follows; Byttnerioideae and Grewioideae form one clade and the other seven subfamilies another (Malvadendrina), in which Bombacoideae and Malvoideae are sister groups:

| Subfamily | Former family | Characters | Representative plants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grewioideae | Tiliaceae | Petals often with a gland at the base, stamens free | Jute, *Grewia*, *Microcos*, *Corchoropsis* |
| Byttnerioideae | Sterculiaceae | Petals often hooded at the base with a long appendage, stamens alternating with staminodes | Cacao, *Byttneria* |
| Sterculioideae | Sterculiaceae | Flowers unisexual, no petals, calyx petaloid, with an androgynophore; follicles | Chinese parasol tree, *Sterculia*, kola nut, bottle tree |
| Dombeyoideae | Sterculiaceae | Stamens alternating with staminodes, petals persistent | *Dombeya*, *Pterospermum* |
| Tilioideae | Tiliaceae | Peduncle fused with a tongue-shaped bract | *Tilia* |
| Brownlowioideae | Tiliaceae | Carpels often free | *Excentrodendron* |
| Helicteroideae | Sterculiaceae, Bombacaceae | Androgynophore well developed | *Helicteres*, durian |
| Bombacoideae | Bombacaceae | Palmately compound leaves, large flowers, pollen smooth and flattened triangular | Red silk-cotton tree, kapok, baobab, Malabar chestnut |
| Malvoideae | Malvaceae (and part of Bombacaceae) | Monadelphous stamens, anthers 1-locular, pollen large and spiny, epicalyx well developed | Cotton, hibiscus, mallow, hollyhock, velvetleaf, okra |

Classification and representative plants (only the subfamilies common in China are listed):

Malvoideae corresponds roughly to the narrowly circumscribed Malvaceae; China has 19 genera and 81 species.

1. *Gossypium*: annual shrubby herbs (cultivated) or shrubs; leaves palmately lobed; epicalyx of 3 large, leaf-like, toothed bracts; corolla white or yellow, often with a purple center; capsule 3–5-valved and loculicidal; the epidermal cells of the seed grow outward into long fibers (cotton lint), beneath which lies a layer of short fuzz. There are 4 cultivated species, domesticated independently on two continents: the diploid tree cotton (*G. arboreum*) and Levant cotton (*G. herbaceum*), native to the Old World, have the A genome; the tetraploid upland cotton (*G. hirsutum*) and Sea Island cotton (*G. barbadense*), native to the Americas, have the AADD genome ($2n = 52$) and are **allopolyploids** formed 1–2 million years ago by hybridization of an Old World cotton with the A genome and an American wild cotton with the D genome, followed by chromosome doubling ([7.4.16](/7.4.16#sec-16.6.2)). Upland cotton has a high yield and accounts for more than 90% of the world's cotton production; Sea Island cotton has the longest and finest fibers (long-staple cotton) and is cultivated on a large scale in Xinjiang. All parts of the cotton plant bear dark pigment glands containing the toxic gossypol, which makes the abundant oil and protein of the cottonseed hard to use; glandless cotton, bred in the mid-twentieth century, made the seed usable as edible oil and feed.
2. *Hibiscus*: epicalyx of 5–15 entire segments; calyx campanulate and 5-toothed; corolla bell-shaped; staminal tube long and exserted; carpels 5, the style with 5 branches; capsule loculicidal with 5 valves. Rose of Sharon (*H. syriacus*) is a deciduous shrub with 3-lobed leaves and purple, pink or white flowers that open in the morning and fade in the evening; it is widely planted as a hedge, the white-flowered form can be eaten as a vegetable, and it is the national flower of Korea. Chinese hibiscus (*H. rosa-sinensis*) is an evergreen shrub with large, red, pendent flowers, native to southern China; *H. schizopetalus* has petals deeply cut into fringes and recurved; cotton rose (*H. mutabilis*) has 5–7-lobed leaves and white or pink flowers that change from white to red during a single day, whence the nickname "Hibiscus City" for Chengdu; kenaf (*H. cannabinus*) has bast fibers used for sacking; sea hibiscus (*H. tiliaceus*, *Talipariti tiliaceum* in Flora of China) is an evergreen tree of the coasts of South China.
3. *Malva*: epicalyx of 3 free segments; carpels numerous, arranged in a ring; the fruit is a schizocarp that breaks at maturity into many kidney-shaped mericarps. *M. cathayensis* (often given as *M. sinensis* in Chinese textbooks) and curled mallow (*M. verticillata*) have tender seedlings that can be eaten as vegetables; the "kui" that headed the "five vegetables" of antiquity is *M. verticillata*.
4. Hollyhock (*Alcea rosea*, often given as *Althaea rosea* in Chinese textbooks) is a tall biennial herb with large flowers of rich colors and an epicalyx of 6–7 segments, widely cultivated as an ornamental.
5. Velvetleaf (*Abutilon theophrasti*) is an annual herb without an epicalyx, with bast fibers used for sacking and rope.
6. *Abelmoschus*: okra (*A. esculentus*) has tender fruits eaten as a vegetable; *A. manihot* has large pale yellow flowers with a purple center and is ornamental and medicinal.

Tilioideae has only the genus *Tilia*, with about 23 species of deciduous trees of the northern temperate zone: leaves long-petioled, with a heart-shaped, oblique base and serrate margin; in the cymose inflorescence about half of the peduncle is fused with a large, membranous, tongue-shaped bract, which, when the fruit ripens, acts like a wing and carries the infructescence spinning down on the wind; the fruit is nut-like and indehiscent. Mongolian linden (*T. mongolica*) has irregularly coarsely serrate and often 3-lobed leaves and occurs in Northeast and North China; *T. tuan* has stellate hairs beneath the leaf and occurs from central to southwestern China; *T. japonica* (often given as *T. eurosinica* in Chinese textbooks) occurs in the mountains of East China. The bast fibers of linden can substitute for hemp, the flowers are an important nectar source (linden honey), and the fine-grained wood is used for carving and plywood. The small-leaved linden (*T. cordata*) and large-leaved linden (*T. platyphyllos*) of Europe are common street trees (called lime or linden in English).

Grewioideae: jute (*Corchorus capsularis*) is an annual herb with serrate leaves whose lowest pair of teeth are elongated into tails, and a spherical capsule; tossa jute (*C. olitorius*) has a cylindrical capsule, and its young leaves are eaten as a vegetable in Egypt and elsewhere. Jute yields the plant fiber produced in greatest quantity after cotton and is used for sacking and carpets. *Grewia biloba* is a shrub with a red drupe and usable bast fibers.

Sterculioideae: Chinese parasol tree (*Firmiana simplex*, often given as *F. platanifolia* in Chinese textbooks) is a deciduous tree with smooth, blue-green bark and large, palmately 3–5-lobed leaves; the flowers are unisexual, without petals, with 5 petaloid sepals and an androgynophore; the fruit is a follicle that splits along the ventral suture before ripening into leaf-like, membranous carpels bearing the seeds on their margins, like a small boat carrying a few beans and dispersed by the wind. This is the "wutong" on which the phoenix perches in classical literature (it differs from the "French wutong", the plane tree of Platanaceae, [7.12.40](/7.12.40)). *Sterculia monosperma* has seeds that can be boiled and eaten and occurs in South China. Kola nut (*Cola nitida*) has seeds containing caffeine and was the original ingredient of cola drinks.

Byttnerioideae: cacao (*Theobroma cacao*) is native to the Amazon basin of South America, an evergreen small tree whose small flowers arise directly on the old stems and trunk (cauliflory) and are pollinated by midges; the large, elongate-ovoid, indehiscent berry-like fruit holds seeds (cocoa beans) that are fermented and roasted to make cocoa powder and chocolate, containing theobromine and cocoa butter.

Bombacoideae: red silk-cotton tree (*Bombax ceiba*) is a large deciduous tree with conical prickles on the trunk and palmately compound leaves; it flowers in early spring before the leaves, with large red flowers, and the capsule contains woolly floss (silk cotton); it occurs in South and Southwest China and is the city flower of Guangzhou. Kapok (*Ceiba pentandra*) has hairs on the inner wall of the capsule (kapok fiber), used to stuff life jackets and pillows. *Adansonia* (baobab) occurs in Africa and Madagascar, with a massive water-storing trunk and flowers pollinated mainly by bats (some Madagascan species by hawkmoths or lemurs). Malabar chestnut (*Pachira aquatica*, sold as the "money tree" in pots). The wood of balsa (*Ochroma pyramidale*) is among the lightest commercial woods.

Helicteroideae: durian (*Durio zibethinus*) is native to Southeast Asia, with a large capsule armed with hard spines and a fleshy aril with a strong smell, pollinated by bats. *Helicteres angustifolia* is a small shrub common in South China and is used medicinally.

Brownlowioideae: *Excentrodendron tonkinense* occurs on the limestone hills of Guangxi and southeastern Yunnan; its wood is extremely heavy and hard, and it is a nationally protected plant.

Key characters of the family: well-developed bast fibers in the stem bark, with stellate hairs and mucilage; leaves simple, alternate and palmately veined, with stipules; flowers bisexual and 5-merous, sepals valvate and often with an epicalyx, petals contorted; stamens numerous with the filaments fused (monadelphous or in bundles); ovary superior with axile placentation. Capsule or schizocarp.

Economic importance: Malvaceae supplies the most important fibers (cotton, jute, kenaf, velvetleaf, and the fruit floss of kapok and red silk-cotton tree), beverages (cacao, kola nut), fruit (durian), vegetables (okra, curled mallow), timber (linden, balsa, *Excentrodendron*) and many ornamentals (hibiscus, Chinese hibiscus, cotton rose, hollyhock, Chinese parasol tree, red silk-cotton tree).

### 44.5.3. Thymelaeaceae

Thymelaeaceae has about 50 genera and 900 species, widespread from the temperate zone to the tropics; China has about 9 genera and over 110 species. Diagnostic characters: trees or shrubs, rarely herbs; the bast fibers of the bark are extremely well developed and tough (bark that cannot be torn apart is a good field character); internal phloem present; leaves simple, entire, alternate or opposite; flowers bisexual or unisexual, 4–5-merous, often in heads or umbels; the hypanthium (calyx tube) is well developed, tubular, petaloid and 4–5-lobed at the tip; petals absent or reduced to scales; stamens borne on the inner wall of the calyx tube, often twice the number of calyx lobes in two whorls; ovary superior, 1-locular, with a single pendent ovule (formed from 2 fused carpels of which only 1 is fertile, a pseudomonomerous gynoecium); fruit a drupe, berry or nut, often enclosed in the persistent calyx tube.

Floral formula: *Daphne* $\ast K_{(4)} C_0 A_{4+4} \underline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$.

Representative plants: winter daphne (*Daphne odora*) is a small evergreen shrub with terminal heads, the calyx tube purple-red outside and white inside, very fragrant, and widely cultivated; lilac daphne (*D. genkwa*) is a deciduous shrub that flowers before the leaves, is poisonous and is used medicinally. Paperbush (*Edgeworthia chrysantha*) is a deciduous shrub whose supple twigs can be tied in knots, with pendent yellow heads that open before the leaves; its bast fibers are the raw material for fine papers (such as Japanese "mitsumata" paper and banknote paper). Agarwood (*Aquilaria sinensis*) is an evergreen tree of South China; when the trunk is wounded or infected by fungi, the xylem accumulates resin, and the resin-impregnated wood is the valuable perfume and drug "agarwood". *Stellera chamaejasme* is a perennial herb with a thick, poisonous root, an indicator of degraded alpine steppe, and in Tibet the fibers of its root are used to make Tibetan paper. The bark of *Wikstroemia* is also used for paper.

### 44.5.4. Dipterocarpaceae

Dipterocarpaceae has about 16 genera and 680 species, chiefly in tropical Asia (the center of distribution is from Malaysia to Indonesia) with a few in Africa and South America; China has about 5 genera and 12 species, in Yunnan, Guangxi, Hainan and southeastern Tibet. Diagnostic characters: large evergreen trees with buttresses; the trunk has resin canals that exude fragrant resin; leaves simple, alternate, entire and pinnately veined, with large, early-falling stipules; flowers bisexual, radially symmetric and 5-merous, with 5 sepals, 5 contorted petals and numerous stamens whose connective is often prolonged into an appendage; ovary superior, 3-locular, with 2 ovules per locule; the fruit is a nut, often with a single seed, in which 2–5 of the persistent sepals enlarge into wings (2 in *Dipterocarpus*, whence the family name, "two-winged fruit"), making the fruit spin down like a shuttlecock.

Dipterocarpaceae is the most important group of canopy trees in the tropical rain forests of Southeast Asia, often making up more than half of the canopy trees, with many species 50–70 m tall, and an important source of the world's hardwoods (meranti). They are known for mast fruiting: many species in a region flower and fruit heavily in the same year every 2–10 years and scarcely fruit in the others, which is generally thought to be related to the drought and low temperatures caused by the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, and may also be a strategy to satiate seed predators. Representative plants: *Parashorea chinensis* is over 60 m tall and was discovered in 1975 in Xishuangbanna, Yunnan, where its discovery proved the existence of true tropical rain forest in southern Yunnan; *Hopea* and *Vatica* occur in Hainan. The resin of *Dipterocarpus* can be made into varnish; camphor tree of Borneo (*Dryobalanops aromatica*) occurs in Sumatra and Borneo, and the crystals that exude from cracks in the trunk are natural borneol, the traditional source of the drug "borneol" in traditional Chinese medicine, which gives the family its Chinese name.

### 44.5.5. Other Families of Malvales

- Cistaceae: shrubs or herbs with opposite leaves; sepals 5, of which 2 are smaller; petals 5, contorted and extremely fragile (the flower lasts only half a day, whence the Chinese name "half-day flower"); stamens numerous; placentation parietal; fruit a capsule. The family has about 8 genera and 180 species, chiefly in the Mediterranean region (*Cistus* is characteristic of Mediterranean sclerophyll scrub and secretes aromatic resin). China has only *Helianthemum songaricum*, in the deserts of Xinjiang and western Inner Mongolia, a relict of the ancient Mediterranean flora.
- Bixaceae: annatto (*Bixa orellana*) is native to the American tropics, and the red outer layer of its seed coat contains bixin (a carotenoid), a natural red pigment for foods and cosmetics.

## 44.6. Brassicales

### 44.6.1. Characters of the Order and Glucosinolates

Brassicales has about 17 families and 4,700 species and is extremely diverse in form: from the herbaceous Brassicaceae and Tropaeolaceae through the shrubby Capparaceae and the arborescent Moringaceae and Caricaceae to the fleshy seashore Bataceae. Cronquist placed these families in five or six orders in three subclasses, but they share one chemical character.

**Definition 44.6.1 (Glucosinolates)** Sulfur-containing glucosides derived from amino acids via aldoximes are called **glucosinolates**. They are stored in different cells from the enzyme that hydrolyzes them, myrosinase (a thioglucosidase); when tissue is chewed, the two meet and the glucosinolate is hydrolyzed, releasing pungent products that are toxic to animals and microorganisms, such as isothiocyanates (mustard oils) and nitriles.

This "mustard oil bomb" is a chemical defense: toxic substances are produced only when the tissue is damaged and are harmless to the plant itself otherwise. The pungency of mustard, horseradish and wasabi and the characteristic smell of radish and cabbage all come from the hydrolysis products of glucosinolates. Dahlgren (1975) first placed these families in one order on the basis of glucosinolates, and later abandoned the idea because their morphological differences were too great; molecular systematics has confirmed this "glucosinolate clade": nearly all families of Brassicales produce glucosinolates, and in angiosperms there is only one exception, *Drypetes* (Putranjivaceae) of Malpighiales ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)), which independently evolved the same system. The biosynthetic pathway of glucosinolates was most probably recruited from the widespread cyanogenic glucoside pathway: both use aldoximes as intermediates, and the formation of the aldoximes is catalyzed by enzymes of the same cytochrome P450 family.

Glucosinolates are also a classic example of plant–insect **coevolution** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). The larvae of Pierinae (cabbage whites and relatives) have an enzyme that directs the hydrolysis of glucosinolates to nontoxic products, so that they can feed on Brassicales and use glucosinolates as a cue for finding host plants; phylogenetic analysis shows that Pierinae switched to Brassicales hosts soon after the order appeared, after which Brassicales evolved new types of glucosinolates and the butterflies new detoxification mechanisms, the diversification of the two corresponding in time (Edger et al. 2015).

The relationships among the families are as follows: Tropaeolaceae + Akaniaceae (including *Bretschneidera*) diverge first, then Moringaceae + Caricaceae, followed in succession by Limnanthaceae and Setchellanthaceae; the other families form the "core Brassicales", with flowers often 4-merous, curved embryos and little or no endosperm, in which Capparaceae is the sister group of (Cleomaceae + Brassicaceae).

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Capparales had 5 families: Brassicaceae, Capparaceae (broad sense), Moringaceae, Resedaceae and others; Caricaceae was in Violales and Tropaeolaceae in Geraniales. APG II merged Cleomaceae and Brassicaceae into a more broadly circumscribed Brassicaceae, and APG III and APG IV again divide them into three families, Capparaceae, Cleomaceae and Brassicaceae (Hall et al. 2002).

### 44.6.2. Brassicaceae

Diagnostic characters of Brassicaceae (or by the conserved name Cruciferae):

- Annual to perennial herbs, rarely shrubs; often with a pungent smell (glucosinolates); hairs simple, branched or stellate, the form of the branched hairs being an important character for distinguishing genera.
- Leaves simple and alternate, often in a basal rosette, entire to deeply pinnately lobed; stipules absent.
- Inflorescence a raceme (often corymbose) without bracts; flowers bisexual, radially symmetric.
- Sepals 4 in two whorls of 2, the inner often saccate at the base.
- Petals 4, arranged in a cross (cruciform corolla), often clawed; nectaries on the receptacle, often opposite the sepals.
- Stamens 6, the outer 2 short and the inner 4 long, a tetradynamous androecium ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.2.4)); from the floral ground plan the outer whorl should have 4 stamens, of which 2 are missing ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.4)).
- Gynoecium of 2 fused carpels, ovary superior and 1-locular with 2 parietal placentas; between the two placentas a thin membrane (the false septum) grows inward from the ovary wall and divides the ovary into two false locules; the placentas form a thickened frame at the outer margin of the ovary (the replum); ovules many.
- The fruit is a **silique** ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.2.3)): a silique proper when its length is more than 3 times its width, otherwise a silicle; at maturity the two valves separate from the replum from the bottom upward, leaving the seeds on the replum on either side of the false septum; a few are indehiscent (the silique of radish is constricted between the seeds and breaks crosswise into segments at maturity).
- Seeds without endosperm, the embryo curved; there are three relative positions of the cotyledons and radicle: accumbent (the radicle lies against the edges of the two cotyledons), incumbent (the radicle lies against the back of one cotyledon) and conduplicate (the cotyledons are folded lengthwise, enclosing the radicle), which are important in distinguishing genera.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{2+2} C_4 A_{2+4} \underline{G}_{(2:2:\infty)}$ (the ovary is divided into 2 locules by the false septum; Chinese textbooks often write $\underline{G}_{(2:1)}$, counting it as 1-locular).

B: dehiscence of a silique (Brassica): the two valves fall away from the bottom upward, and the seeds remain on the replum and false septum, with a sterile beak at the tip. Brassicaceae has about 340 genera and 3,700 species, distributed worldwide and chiefly in the northern temperate zone, with the greatest richness in the dry and alpine regions of the Mediterranean, West Asia and Central Asia; China has about 102 genera and over 410 species. Molecular phylogenies divide the family into about 50 tribes in three or four major lineages, the earliest-diverging of which is the tribe Aethionemeae (*Aethionema*), centered in West Asia. Traditional tribes were based on fruit form, sepal arrangement, embryo folding and indumentum, but many of these characters are convergent, and molecular classification is gradually replacing the old tribes.

Classification and representative plants:

1. *Brassica*: annual or biennial herbs, the basal leaves often pinnately lobed; flowers yellow with long-clawed petals; silique cylindrical with a beak; seeds spherical with conduplicate cotyledons. The genus includes some of the most important everyday vegetables and oil crops (§44.6.3).
2. Radish (*Raphanus sativus*): the fleshy taproot is eaten and the cultivars are very numerous; the silique is indehiscent and constricted between the seeds; the seeds are used medicinally.
3. Shepherd's purse (*Capsella bursa-pastoris*): an annual or biennial herb with an inversely triangular-cordate silicle; a wild vegetable of early spring, and also a classic material for the study of flower and embryo development in Brassicaceae (see [7.12.30](/7.12.30) for embryo development in shepherd's purse). It is itself an **allopolyploid** ($2n = 32$), formed by hybridization and doubling of two diploid *Capsella* species.
4. *Arabidopsis thaliana* (thale cress): a small annual herb with a basal rosette, white flowers and slender siliques. Its small size, short life cycle (6–8 weeks), selfing, many seeds and small genome (about 135 Mb, 5 chromosome pairs) made it in 2000 the first plant with a completely sequenced genome, and it is the most important model organism in plant genetics, developmental biology and molecular biology ([7.5.3](/7.5.3)). The ABC model of flower development was established in *Arabidopsis* and snapdragon ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.1.2)).
5. *Orychophragmus violaceus* (Chinese violet cress): large pale purple flowers that open in early spring and siliques with long beaks; common in North and East China and often growing in large patches under trees in Beijing; the seeds contain oil.
6. Woad (*Isatis tinctoria*, including *I. indigotica*): the silicle is indehiscent, flat and winged; the root is used medicinally as "banlangen" and the leaf as "daqingye", and the leaf was also the traditional source of indigo.
7. The seeds of *Lepidium apetalum* and flixweed (*Descurainia sophia*) are used medicinally as "tinglizi"; field pennycress (*Thlaspi arvense*) has a round silicle with broad wings.
8. *Cardamine*: at maturity the valves of the silique coil up suddenly from the base and eject the seeds. Hairy bittercress (*C. hirsuta*) has become a new model plant for the study of compound-leaf development and explosive dehiscence.
9. Ornamental and condiment plants: stock (*Matthiola incana*), sweet alyssum (*Lobularia maritima*) and wallflower (*Erysimum cheiri*) are common garden flowers; the rhizomes of horseradish (*Armoracia rusticana*) and wasabi (*Eutrema japonicum*) are ground as condiments (the "wasabi" sold commercially is usually made with horseradish instead).

Key characters of the family: herbs, often with pungent sap; leaves simple, alternate, without stipules; inflorescence a raceme; sepals 4, corolla cruciform, stamens tetradynamous; 2 carpels fused, with parietal placentation and a false septum. Fruit a silique or silicle.

### 44.6.3. *Brassica* and the Triangle of U

*Brassica* has about 40 species, of which the relationships among the 6 cultivated species were clarified by the Korean-Japanese scientist Woo Jang-choon (U Nagaharu, 1935) and are known as the *triangle of U*: three diploid species carry the A, B and C genomes respectively, and hybridization between each pair of diploid species followed by chromosome doubling produced three **allopolyploid** species whose chromosome numbers are exactly the sums of those of their parents ([7.4.16](/7.4.16#sec-16.6.2)). U reached this conclusion from interspecific hybridization and chromosome pairing in the meiosis of the hybrids, and later genome sequencing has fully confirmed it. Each species has also been differentiated by artificial selection into many morphologically very different cultivated forms (varieties or subspecies), one of the best demonstrations of how artificial selection changes the morphology of plant organs ([7.10.12](/7.10.12#sec-12.1.6)):

Rapeseed (*B. napus*) is the world's third-largest source of vegetable oil. Traditional rapeseed oil contains a great deal of erucic acid and the meal is high in glucosinolates, so neither could be used for food or feed; in the 1970s Canadian breeders developed "double-low" cultivars low in both erucic acid and glucosinolates (called canola in Canada), which made rapeseed oil a high-quality edible oil and the meal a protein feed. Most *Brassica* species have sporophytic self-incompatibility ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3)), which is often used together with cytoplasmic male sterility to produce hybrid seed.

### 44.6.4. Cleomaceae and Capparaceae

Cleomaceae and Capparaceae were formerly united as a broadly circumscribed Capparaceae (Cronquist). Molecular phylogenies show that Cleomoideae is closest to Brassicaceae and Capparoideae is the sister group of the two, so APG IV recognizes three families (with only two families, the broad Capparaceae would be paraphyletic). All three share a long gynophore (or androgynophore) and a similar glucosinolate biosynthetic pathway.

Cleomaceae has about 17 genera and 270 species, mostly herbs, often with glandular hairs and a strong smell; leaves palmately compound; racemes with leaf-like bracts; flowers bilaterally symmetric, with 4 sepals, 4 petals, usually 6 stamens and a long gynophore (or androgynophore); placentation parietal; the capsule is slender, without a false septum. Floral formula: $\uparrow K_4 C_4 A_6 \underline{G}_{(2:1:\infty)}$. Spider flower (*Cleome gynandra*, *Gynandropsis gynandra* in Flora of China) has an androgynophore, and its young leaves are pickled and eaten; *Tarenaya hassleriana* (formerly *Cleome spinosa*) has pink or white petals and stamens and a gynophore so long that they project from the flower like a butterfly's antennae, and it is widely cultivated as an ornamental. Cleomaceae includes C4 plants (*C. gynandra*), which are material for the study of the evolution of C4 photosynthesis ([7.12.23](/7.12.23#sec-23.5.6)).

Capparaceae has about 17 genera and 450 species, mostly shrubs, trees or vines of the tropics, with simple leaves whose stipules are often modified into thorns; petals 4, stamens numerous, gynophore long; the fruit is often a berry. Caper bush (*Capparis spinosa*) occurs from the Mediterranean to Xinjiang, and its pickled flower buds are the capers used as a seasoning in Western cooking.

### 44.6.5. Other Families of Brassicales

- Caricaceae: papaya (*Carica papaya*) is native to the American tropics, a small evergreen soft-wooded tree with an unbranched stem, latex, and large palmate leaves clustered at the top; flowers unisexual, on dioecious plants or bisexual, with a sympetalous corolla (rare in Brassicales); the berry is large with parietal placentation and many seeds. Dioecy in papaya is determined by a pair of young sex chromosomes (XY) and is material for the study of sex chromosome evolution. Papaya is not the Chinese quince (*Chaenomeles sinensis*) of Rosaceae ([7.12.43](/7.12.43)) and does not belong to Cucurbitaceae.
- Tropaeolaceae: only *Tropaeolum*, with about 90 species, in Central and South America. Nasturtium (*T. majus*) has round, peltate leaves and bilaterally symmetric flowers with 5 sepals, of which one is prolonged into a long spur, and 5 orange-red or yellow petals; it is widely cultivated as an ornamental; it contains glucosinolates, and the leaves are pungent.
- Moringaceae: only *Moringa*, with 13 species, in tropical Africa and Asia. Drumstick tree (*M. oleifera*) is a deciduous tree with twice- to thrice-pinnate leaves, a long, 3-valved capsule and 3-winged seeds; the leaves are rich in protein and vitamins, and the seeds can be used to clarify turbid water; it is promoted in tropical countries as a nutritional and water-purifying plant.
- Resedaceae: mignonette (*Reseda odorata*) is native to North Africa, a herb with small, fragrant flowers and an ovary open at the apex (the carpels are not closed at the tip); dyer's rocket (*R. luteola*) was a yellow dye of ancient Europe.
- Akaniaceae: *Bretschneidera sinensis*, found from southern China to Vietnam, Laos and northern Thailand, is a deciduous tree with odd-pinnate leaves and large racemes of pink flowers; it was formerly placed in its own family Bretschneideraceae, sometimes in Sapindales; molecular evidence shows that it is the sister group of *Akania* of eastern Australia and belongs to Brassicales. *Bretschneidera* is a second-class nationally protected wild plant in China (in the 2021 list; first-class before).

## Questions

1. Why does the APG system merge Tiliaceae, Sterculiaceae and Bombacaceae into Malvaceae? After the merger, how can the former groups be recognized within the family?

   **Answer** A combined molecular and morphological analysis showed that Sterculiaceae and Tiliaceae are both polyphyletic and Bombacaceae is paraphyletic, with the traditional genera interleaved among the four families (the subfamilies containing *Theobroma* and *Grewia* are sister groups, and the narrowly circumscribed Malvaceae is nested within part of Bombacaceae); only by merging them into Malvaceae in the broad sense is a monophyletic group obtained. After the merger the old families correspond roughly to subfamilies: Malvoideae (monadelphous stamens, 1-locular anthers, spiny pollen, epicalyx), Tilioideae (peduncle fused with a tongue-shaped bract), Sterculioideae (no petals, androgynophore, follicles), Bombacoideae (palmately compound leaves, large flowers) and so on, but the old Tiliaceae and Sterculiaceae are each scattered among several subfamilies.

2. The leaves of both Rutaceae and Myrtaceae have translucent oil dots. How can the two be distinguished?

   **Answer** In both families the oil dots are secretory cavities (oil cavities) in the leaf. In Rutaceae the leaves are often pinnately compound or unifoliolate and alternate, without an intramarginal vein; the stamens are usually twice the number of petals with the outer whorl opposite the petals, there is an intrastaminal disc, and the ovary is superior. Myrtaceae has simple leaves, often opposite, entire and leathery, with an intramarginal vein; the stamens are numerous and inflexed in bud, and the ovary is inferior. In fruit, Rutaceae has hesperidia and follicles, while Myrtaceae has mostly berries or capsules (the capsule of *Eucalyptus* is enclosed in a woody calyx tube).

3. Why is the classification of cultivated citrus particularly difficult? What explanation has genome research given?

   **Answer** Most cultivated citrus are not natural species but hybrids among a few ancestral species (pomelo, mandarin, citron and small-flowered papeda) and their backcross derivatives, and they are often maintained as clones through apomixis by nucellar embryos and by grafting; their morphology also varies continuously, so that the number of "species" delimited on morphology ranges from a dozen to over a hundred. Genome sequencing writes each cultivated form as a combination of ancestral genomes: sour orange is a pomelo × mandarin hybrid, sweet orange is a backcross derivative of such a hybrid, grapefruit is a hybrid of pomelo and sweet orange, and lemon is a hybrid of sour orange and citron. A reasonable treatment is therefore to recognize a few ancestral species and to treat the cultivated forms as hybrids (×) or cultivar groups.

4. Why are glucosinolates called a "mustard oil bomb"? What is their significance in systematics?

   **Answer** Glucosinolates are nontoxic in themselves and are stored in different cells from the myrosinase that hydrolyzes them; when the tissue is chewed by a herbivore the two meet and rapidly generate toxic, pungent isothiocyanates, like a bomb detonated by tissue damage. In systematics, glucosinolates are a chemical synapomorphy of Brassicales (in angiosperms they arose twice, in Brassicales and in *Drypetes*): Dahlgren used them to place together the morphologically very different Brassicaceae, Cleomaceae, Moringaceae, Caricaceae, Tropaeolaceae and others, and molecular systematics later confirmed that they form a monophyletic group.

5. What does the triangle of U show? List several cultivated forms of cabbage and say which organ artificial selection has acted on in each.

   **Answer** The triangle of U shows that three cultivated species of *Brassica*, *B. juncea* (AABB), *B. napus* (AACC) and *B. carinata* (BBCC), are allotetraploids formed by hybridization and chromosome doubling between each pair of *B. rapa* (AA), *B. nigra* (BB) and *B. oleracea* (CC), with chromosome numbers equal to the sum of those of the two parents: a classic example of speciation by allopolyploidy. Cultivated forms of cabbage (*B. oleracea*): head cabbage was selected for the terminal bud (the head), Brussels sprouts for the axillary buds, cauliflower and broccoli for the inflorescence, kohlrabi for the stem, and kale and Chinese kale for the leaves. Artificial selection has enlarged different organs of the same species, producing vegetables that differ greatly.
