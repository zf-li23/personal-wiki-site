Asparagales are the largest order of monocots, with about 36,000 species, roughly half of all monocots, of which Orchidaceae alone account for about 28,000. The order includes some of the horticulturally most important plants (orchids, irises, gladiolus, daffodils, clivia, hyacinths, hostas, aloes, agaves), vegetables (onion, garlic, leek, asparagus, daylily buds), and many medicinal "lilioid" plants such as *Gastrodia*, *Dendrobium*, *Polygonatum*, *Ophiopogon* and *Anemarrhena*. In Cronquist's system these plants were divided between Liliales of the subclass Liliidae (broadly defined Liliaceae, into which Amaryllidaceae were also merged; Iridaceae, Agavaceae and others) and Orchidales (Orchidaceae); APG combines the groups of the broadly defined Liliaceae that have black seeds and mostly septal nectaries with Orchidaceae and Iridaceae in Asparagales ([7.12.36](/7.12.36#sec-36.6.1)). This chapter first treats the circumscription and characters of the order (§37.1), then Orchidaceae (§37.2), Iridaceae (§37.3), Asphodelaceae (§37.4), Amaryllidaceae (§37.5) and Asparagaceae (§37.6). For the pollination mechanisms of orchids see [7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.6); for bulbs, corms and cladodes and other underground stems and modified organs see [7.12.24](/7.12.24#sec-24.3).

## 37.1. Circumscription and Characters of Asparagales

APG IV Asparagales comprise 14 families: Orchidaceae, Boryaceae, Blandfordiaceae, Asteliaceae, Lanariaceae, Hypoxidaceae, Doryanthaceae, Ixioliriaceae, Tecophilaeaceae, Iridaceae, Xeronemataceae, Asphodelaceae, Amaryllidaceae and Asparagaceae. Those with many species and commonly met in China are Orchidaceae, Hypoxidaceae, Iridaceae, Asphodelaceae, Amaryllidaceae and Asparagaceae. Orchidaceae are the **sister group** of all the other families; Hypoxidaceae form a clade with Asteliaceae, Blandfordiaceae, Lanariaceae and Boryaceae; Iridaceae and several small families (Ixioliriaceae, Doryanthaceae and others) diverge in turn; the last to diverge, Asphodelaceae, Amaryllidaceae and Asparagaceae, form the "core Asparagales", in which Amaryllidaceae and Asparagaceae are sister groups.

Characters of Asparagales:

- Seeds: the outer layer of the seed coat carries a black, acellular, crust-like layer of *phytomelan* that makes the seed black; this is the best morphological synapomorphy of the order. Groups with fleshy fruits (*Asparagus*, *Convallaria*, *Dracaena*) and Orchidaceae, with their dust-like seeds, have lost this layer.
- Flowers: mostly septal nectaries (nectaries in the septa of the ovary), tepals seldom spotted; in the basal families (Orchidaceae, Hypoxidaceae, Iridaceae and others) the ovary is inferior and microsporogenesis is simultaneous, whereas the core Asparagales mostly have a superior ovary and successive microsporogenesis.
- Leaves: mostly linear or ribbon-like with parallel venation, often basal in a rosette, lacking the fine reticulate venation common in Liliales.
- Stem: only Asparagales (and *Dioscorea*) show true monocot secondary thickening: *Dracaena*, *Yucca*, *Agave*, *Aloe*, *Cordyline* and others form a secondary thickening meristem in the cortex that produces amphivasal bundles inward ([7.12.22](/7.12.22#sec-22.5)).
- Chromosomes: in most plants the chromosome ends carry the *Arabidopsis*-type telomere repeat (TTTAGGG)$_n$, but most groups of Asparagales have lost it and replaced it with the human-type (TTAGGG)$_n$ or other sequences, and *Allium* has a unique 12 bp repeat unit (CTCGGTTATGGG)$_n$ (Adams et al. 2001; Sýkorová et al. 2003; Fajkus et al. 2016). This is a rare example of an order defined by a molecular character.

The flowers of Asparagales are hard to tell from those of Liliales by their outward appearance; the differences are summarized in Table 37.1.1. Cronquist set apart Agavaceae (*Agave*, *Yucca*, *Sansevieria*, *Dracaena*), mainly on the basis of the woody habit and secondary thickening; APG places them in Asparagaceae and Asphodelaceae, which shows that the woody habit arose several times within Asparagales.

| Character | Liliales | Asparagales |
|---|---|---|
| Seed coat | No phytomelan; seeds pale, often flat | Mostly with a phytomelan layer; seeds black (except fleshy fruits and Orchidaceae) |
| Nectaries | At the tepal base (tepal nectaries) | Mostly septal nectaries |
| Tepals | Often spotted or striped | Seldom spotted |
| Anthers | Mostly extrorse | Mostly introrse |
| Leaves | Often with fine reticulate veins (*Smilax*, *Paris*) | Mostly linear, parallel-veined |
| Secondary thickening | Absent | *Dracaena*, *Yucca*, *Agave*, *Aloe* and others |
| Representatives | Lily, tulip, fritillary, *Veratrum*, *Smilax*, *Colchicum* | Orchid, iris, daylily, aloe, onion, *Lycoris*, asparagus, *Polygonatum*, hosta, agave |

## 37.2. Orchidaceae

### 37.2.1. Diagnostic Characters

Orchidaceae and Asteraceae are the two families with the most species among the angiosperms. The orchid flower derives developmentally from an ordinary trimerous, pentacyclic monocot flower, but through a series of specializations (reduction of stamens, fusion of stamens and pistil, resupination of the ovary) it has become the most unusual in structure among angiosperms.

**Definition 37.2.1 (Specialized structures of the orchid flower)** In the flower of Orchidaceae, the median member of the inner perianth whorl is specialized as a **labellum** (lip) that differs markedly in shape and color from the other tepals; the stamens and the style (with the stigma) are fused into a columnar body called the **gynostemium** (column); the pollen of each anther locule is cohered into a waxy or powdery mass called a **pollinium**; a projection formed by the specialization of one stigma lobe above the fertile stigma is called the **rostellum**, which secretes a sticky substance and forms the **viscidium** to which the pollinia adhere. The definition and function of these structures are also given in [7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.6).

Diagnostic characters of Orchidaceae (the orchid family):

- Perennial herbs, terrestrial, epiphytic or saprophytic (mycoheterotrophic), rarely climbing vines (*Vanilla*); terrestrial species have rhizomes or tubers, and epiphytic species have aerial roots covered by a velamen ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)), which consists of several layers of dead cells that absorb water rapidly and reduce transpiration; the stem is often swollen at the base or throughout into a pseudobulb of one to several internodes that stores water and nutrients.
- Leaves alternate, often distichous, rarely opposite or whorled, with a clasping sheath at the base; the leaves of epiphytes are often thick and fleshy, and many carry out **crassulacean acid metabolism**. The leaves of saprophytic species are reduced to scales.
- Scape terminal or lateral; flowers solitary or in racemes, spikes or panicles.
- Flowers bisexual (rarely unisexual), bilaterally symmetric. Tepals 6 in two whorls: the outer 3 are sepals, often petaloid, the median one called the dorsal sepal and the lateral ones the lateral sepals, which are sometimes adnate to the column foot to form a mentum, or fused into a synsepal (*Paphiopedilum*, *Cypripedium*); of the inner whorl the 2 lateral members are petals and the median one is the labellum. The labellum is often lobed, with appendages such as ridges, lamellae and calli, and often has a sac or spur at the base that contains nectar.
- In most species the pedicel and ovary twist through 180° during development, or the inflorescence is pendent, so that the labellum moves from the adaxial upper side to the abaxial lower side and becomes a landing platform for pollinators, a process called *resupination*. A few species do not resupinate (the labellum remains above), as in some *Malaxis*.
- Stamens and style are fused into the gynostemium, whose face is turned toward the labellum, whose base sometimes extends into a column foot, and whose apex often bears an anther bed. There is 1 fertile stamen (the median stamen of the outer whorl, borne on the back of the column apex), or 2 (the lateral stamens of the inner whorl, on the two sides of the column), rarely 3; the remaining stamens are reduced to staminodes or lost.
- Anthers 2-locular; pollen grains often cohere into pollinia (2–8 per flower), which often have a caudicle connected through the stipe of the rostellum to the viscidium; tetrads or monads also occur (Apostasioideae, Cypripedioideae).
- Gynoecium of 3 fused carpels, ovary inferior, 1-locular, with parietal placentation and a great many ovules (up to millions in one ovary); in species with a single stamen only 2 of the 3 stigmas are fertile and fuse into a concave stigmatic cavity, the third being specialized as the rostellum; in species with two stamens the 3 stigmas fuse into one and there is no rostellum.
- Capsule, often triangular, splitting at maturity lengthwise into 3–6 valves still joined at the apex; seeds extremely numerous, minute and dust-like (often only a few micrograms each), without endosperm, the embryo undifferentiated, the testa membranous or winged, dispersed by wind.

Floral formula: $\uparrow P_{3+3} A_{1\text{–}2} \overline{G}_{(3:1:\infty)}$.

### 37.2.2. Seeds and Mycorrhiza

The "dust seed" of orchids has only an undifferentiated embryo of several tens to several hundreds of cells, enclosed in a transparent seed coat, without endosperm and with almost no stored reserves. For seeds to germinate in nature they must be invaded by particular soil fungi (mostly basidiomycetes such as *Tulasnella* and *Ceratobasidium*): fungal hyphae form hyphal coils (pelotons) in the embryo cells and deliver to the embryo carbohydrates obtained from soil organic matter or other plants, and the embryo then digests the pelotons to obtain nutrients. The embryo first grows into a small spheroid without roots or leaves, called a *protocorm*, a character unique to Orchidaceae; the protocorm stage may last from several months to several years before leaves and roots appear. The **mycorrhiza** of Orchidaceae is of a distinctive type (orchid mycorrhiza), and all orchids are **mycoheterotrophs** at least in the protocorm stage ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.2)); most green adult orchids gradually become autotrophic, while a few (*Gastrodia*, *Galeola* and the like) depend on fungi all their lives. Only "asymbiotic sowing" or tissue culture on sugar-containing media in the laboratory has made large-scale propagation of orchids possible.

### 37.2.3. Five Subfamilies and the Reduction of Stamen Number

Orchidaceae have about 750 genera and 28,000 species, distributed worldwide and most numerous in tropical montane rain forests; China has about 190 genera and over 1500 species, mainly south of the Yangtze, with the southwest and Taiwan richest. Molecular phylogenetics divides the family into 5 subfamilies (Chase et al. 2003, 2015), which diverge in the following order (see the phylogeny of the orchid subfamilies below):

| Subfamily | Species | Fertile stamens | Pollen | Habit | Representative genera |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apostasioideae | about 16 | 2–3 (1 median outer + 2 lateral inner; 2 in *Apostasia*) | Powdery, monads | Terrestrial | *Neuwiedia*, *Apostasia* |
| Vanilloideae | about 250 | 1 (median outer) | Monads or tetrads, no pollinia | Terrestrial or climbing, some saprophytic | *Vanilla*, *Pogonia*, *Galeola* |
| Cypripedioideae | about 170 | 2 (lateral inner); the median outer one is a shield-like staminode | Sticky pollen mass, no caudicle | Terrestrial or lithophytic | *Cypripedium*, *Paphiopedilum* |
| Orchidoideae | about 3600 | 1, anther erect | Pollinia soft, with caudicle and viscidium | Terrestrial, often tuberous | *Orchis*, *Platanthera*, *Habenaria*, *Ophrys*, *Spiranthes*, *Goodyera* |
| Epidendroideae | about 21,000 | 1, anther bent forward in development (incumbent) | Pollinia waxy, hard | Mostly epiphytic, also terrestrial and saprophytic | *Cymbidium*, *Dendrobium*, *Bletilla*, *Gastrodia*, *Phalaenopsis*, *Cattleya*, *Bulbophyllum* |

This shows one main line in the evolution of Orchidaceae: from the 3 (or 2) stamens, powdery pollen and incomplete column of Apostasioideae, to the 2 stamens of Cypripedioideae, and then to the single stamen and highly organized pollinia of Orchidoideae and Epidendroideae. The phylogenetic tree shows that the "single stamen" arose at least twice independently (once in Vanilloideae and once in the ancestor of Orchidoideae and Epidendroideae), and that the two stamens of Cypripedioideae are the lateral inner stamens, the same as in Apostasioideae. Dahlgren (1985) divided the orchids into three families according to stamen number, Apostasiaceae, Cypripediaceae and Orchidaceae, and molecular evidence shows that this division is not monophyletic. The genome of *Apostasia shenzhenica*, published in 2017, shows that the common ancestor of the orchids underwent a whole-genome duplication, and that the development of the labellum and column is associated with the expansion and diversification of the MADS-box gene family (Zhang et al. 2017).

### 37.2.4. Classification and Representative Plants

1. *Neuwiedia*: terrestrial herbs with short rhizomes; flowers nearly radially symmetric, the labellum similar to the petals, 3 fertile stamens, the pollen powdery and not forming pollinia. About 8 species from Southeast Asia to New Guinea; China has one species, *N. singapureana* (often given as *N. veratrifolia* in Chinese textbooks), in Guangdong, Hainan and Yunnan. Together with *Apostasia* (2 stamens) it forms the earliest-diverging group of the orchids.
2. *Cypripedium* (lady's slipper): terrestrial herbs with cauline leaves that are convolute when young; the labellum is a deep sac (slipper-shaped), the fertile stamens are the 2 lateral inner ones, and the median outer stamen is a shield-like staminode; the pollen is granular and does not form pollinia. About 50 species in the north temperate zone, over 30 in China, most in the southwest and center. *C. japonicum* usually has 2 nearly opposite leaves with fan-shaped venation; *C. macranthos* has purplish red flowers and occurs in the northeast and north. The sac-like labellum of *Cypripedium* is a "trap": a bee that falls into the sac cannot climb out through the smooth opening and can only escape through a small side opening along the hairs at the base inside the sac, passing first the stigma and then brushing past the anther, thereby carrying away pollen. *Paphiopedilum* (slipper orchid) is the tropical Asian counterpart, with evergreen, leathery leaves; southwestern China has many rare species, such as *P. micranthum*.
3. *Orchis* and related genera: terrestrial herbs with tubers; the labellum has a spur at the base; the viscidia of the pollinia are enclosed in a bursicle. According to molecular phylogenetics the traditional *Orchis* is polyphyletic; *Orchis* in the strict sense occurs only from Europe to North Africa, and most Chinese species have been moved to genera such as *Hemipilia* (including the former *Ponerorchis*). *O. chusua* of the Chinese textbooks is now *Ponerorchis chusua* (or *Hemipilia chusua*). *Ophrys* occurs in the Mediterranean region and is the classic case of sexual deception ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.6)).
4. *Spiranthes* (ladies' tresses): *S. sinensis* is a small terrestrial orchid whose small flowers are arranged spirally in a raceme, pink, common in grassland and on field banks, one of the most widely distributed orchids in China. The leaves of *Goodyera* and of *Anoectochilus* have beautiful reticulate patterns, and *A. roxburghii* is used in medicine as a whole plant.
5. *Bletilla*: terrestrial herbs with flattened-globose pseudobulbs marked with rings like those of a water chestnut; leaves several, papery, pleated; raceme with fairly large flowers, labellum 3-lobed and spurless. *B. striata* has purplish red flowers and occurs from the Yangtze valley to the southwest; the pseudobulb contains bletilla gum and is used in Chinese medicine as an astringent and hemostatic and to reduce swelling and promote healing; it is also a common garden flower.
6. *Dendrobium*: epiphytic herbs with tufted, yellow-green stems with conspicuous nodes; flowers often large and showy, the lateral sepals fused with the column foot to form a mentum; pollinia 4. About 1500 species, one of the largest genera of the orchids, from tropical Asia to Oceania; about 80 in China. *D. nobile* has slightly flattened stems and white flowers tinged purplish red; *D. officinale* has slender stems and leaf sheaths with purple spots and is the raw material of the valuable Chinese drug "tiepi fengdou"; wild resources are scarce and it is now widely cultivated.
7. *Cymbidium*: epiphytic, terrestrial or saprophytic herbs; stem very short or swollen into a pseudobulb; leaves leathery, ribbon-like; raceme, perianth spreading, column long; pollinia 2, waxy. About 70 species from tropical and subtropical Asia to Oceania; China has about 50 species and many cultivars, south of the Yangtze. The "guolan" (national orchids) of Chinese tradition are mainly small-flowered terrestrial species, prized for the posture of the leaves and the fragrance of the flowers: *C. goeringii* has narrowly ribbon-shaped leaves and solitary flowers (rarely 2) opening in spring; *C. faberi* has many flowers on a scape and opens in late spring; *C. ensifolium* has softer leaves and flowers in summer and autumn; *C. sinense* has broad, dark green leaves and flowers in late winter and early spring; *C. kanran* flowers in winter. Epiphytic large-flowered species (such as *C. hookerianum*) and the "large-flowered cymbidiums" bred by crossing them with these are common New Year pot plants.
8. *Gastrodia*: chlorophyll-free saprophytic herbs; a thick, horizontal tuber underground with ringed nodes; stem erect, yellow-brown, with sheathing scales at the nodes; flowers fairly small, the sepals and petals fused into an oblique tube with 5 lobes at the apex; pollinia 2. *G. elata* occurs from the northeast to the southwest, and the tuber is the Chinese drug "tianma", containing gastrodin and others, used to calm wind and relieve spasm. *Gastrodia* depends on fungi all its life: seed germination relies on fungi of *Mycena*, and the grown tuber obtains nutrients from the honey fungus *Armillaria* ([7.12.11](/7.12.11)) that invades it; in cultivation of *Gastrodia* a bed of *Armillaria* culture wood must be prepared first.
9. *Phalaenopsis* (moth orchid): epiphytic, stem very short, leaves thick and fleshy and carrying out **crassulacean acid metabolism**, roots flattened, green and photosynthetic; inflorescence long, flowers large, shaped like a butterfly with spread wings. *P. aphrodite* occurs in Taiwan and the Philippines and is one of the world's largest pot-plant crops. *Cattleya* occurs in tropical America and is called the "queen of orchids".
10. *Vanilla*: climbing vines with aerial roots at the nodes; *V. planifolia*, native to Mexico, has capsules ("vanilla beans") that produce vanillin after fermentation and are one of the most important natural flavorings. Outside its native region pollinating bees are lacking, and in 1841 Edmond Albius, an enslaved boy on Réunion, invented the method of hand pollination in which the rostellum is lifted with a small bamboo stick and the pollinia are pressed onto the stigma, a method still in use.

Key characters of the family: herbs; flowers bilaterally symmetric, the median member of the inner perianth whorl specialized as a labellum; ovary inferior, often twisted through 180°; stamens and style fused into a gynostemium with 1 or 2 (rarely 3) fertile stamens, the pollen often cohering into pollinia; parietal placentation; seeds minute and without endosperm, germination dependent on fungi.

### 37.2.5. Pollination and Diversification

Orchid flowers are almost all animal-pollinated and often highly specialized. Pollinia allow a single visit to deliver a whole mass of pollen, enough to fertilize a large number of ovules in one ovary, which lowers the requirement for visits and allows specialized, uncommon pollinators to sustain a population; about one third of orchids offer no reward and deceive pollinators by color, scent or shape, including food deception and the sexual deception found mainly in Orchidaceae ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.6)). Studying the comet orchid of Madagascar (*Angraecum sesquipedale*), Darwin predicted from its spur, up to 30 cm long, the existence of a hawkmoth with an equally long proboscis; this moth (*Xanthopan praedicta*, originally the subspecies *X. morganii praedicta*) was described in 1903 and was observed pollinating the orchid in the wild only in the 1990s.

Why do orchids have so many species? Phylogenetic analyses by Givnish et al. (2015) and Freudenstein and Chase (2015) show that there is no single "key innovation": pollinia, the epiphytic habit (which arose several times in Epidendroideae and is associated with CAM, velamen and pseudobulbs), dispersal to the Neotropics, topographic isolation in tropical mountains, and the strong reproductive isolation caused by deceptive and specialized pollination together raised the rate of speciation. The long-distance dispersal ability of the dust-like orchid seeds also enables them to colonize new islands and mountains rapidly.

### 37.2.6. Conservation

Many orchids have narrow ranges and are collected in large numbers for ornament and medicine, which makes them one of the most severely threatened plant groups. The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) lists all Orchidaceae in Appendix II (international trade requires permits) and genera such as *Paphiopedilum* and *Phragmipedium* in Appendix I (commercial trade prohibited). China's List of National Key Protected Wild Plants (2021) also includes many orchids, and the wild populations of most species of *Paphiopedilum* and *Cypripedium*, and of *Anoectochilus*, *Dendrobium officinale*, *Gastrodia elata* and others, are protected. Artificial propagation (asymbiotic sowing, tissue culture) and cultivation are the main way to reduce collection from the wild.

## 37.3. Hypoxidaceae and Iridaceae

### 37.3.1. Hypoxidaceae

Hypoxidaceae are perennial herbs with tuberous rhizomes or corms; leaves basal, often pleated and hairy; flowers trimerous, tepals 6, often hairy on the outside, stamens 6, ovary inferior, 3-locular; capsule or berry-like, seeds black. About 7 genera and 200 species, mainly in the Southern Hemisphere. *Curculigo orchioides* has yellow flowers and the rhizome is used in medicine; *C. capitulata* has large, pleated leaves and is often cultivated as an ornamental; *Hypoxis aurea* is a small herb of grassland in southern China. Cronquist merged *Curculigo* into Liliaceae; its inferior ovary places it with Orchidaceae and Iridaceae among the basal "inferior-ovary" groups of Asparagales.

### 37.3.2. Diagnostic Characters of Iridaceae

Diagnostic characters of Iridaceae (the iris family):

- Perennial herbs, rarely annual or shrubby; with underground rhizomes, corms or bulbs.
- Leaves mostly basal, linear or sword-shaped, sheathing at the base, distichous and overlapping (equitant, as in *Acorus*), the blade identical on both faces (unifacial), often folded lengthwise.
- Inflorescence a special type of cyme (a rhipidium, or fan-shaped cyme), enclosed by one to several bracts; flowers bisexual, radially or bilaterally symmetric (*Gladiolus*), often large and brightly colored.
- Tepals 6 in two whorls, petaloid, often fused at the base into a tube; in *Iris* the outer tepals are large and drooping (falls) and often bear appendages (crests or beards), while the inner tepals are erect (standards).
- Stamens 3, the outer whorl (the inner whorl is entirely lost), opposite the outer tepals; anthers extrorse.
- Ovary inferior, 3 carpels fused, 3-locular, axile placentation, ovules numerous; style 1, branching in the upper part into 3 branches, which are cylindrical or flattened and petaloid (*Iris*), the stigma on the underside of the branches.
- Capsule loculicidal; seeds numerous.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3} \overline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$ ($\uparrow$ in *Gladiolus*).

The flower of *Iris* consists of three identical "pollination units": each unit comprises one outer tepal (a fall, serving as a landing platform), a petaloid style branch above it, and a stamen sandwiched between the two, forming a tunnel leading to the nectar. When a bee enters the tunnel its back first brushes the stigmatic lip on the underside of the style branch and then the anther; on backing out the stigmatic lip is pushed upward and does not pick up the bee's own pollen. A single iris flower is therefore functionally equivalent to three bilaterally symmetric flowers.

### 37.3.3. Classification and Representative Plants

Iridaceae have about 66 genera and 2000–2200 species, widespread in the tropics and temperate zones and most diverse in South Africa (the Cape region); China has about 3 genera and over 60 species. Molecular phylogenetics divides the family into several subfamilies: Isophysioideae (*Isophysis*, only in Tasmania, with a superior ovary, sister to all the other subfamilies), Iridoideae, Crocoideae and others. Representative plants:

- *Iris*: with rhizomes or bulbs; flowers large, the outer tepals drooping and the inner erect, the style branches petaloid. About 300 species in the north temperate zone; about 60 in China. *I. tectorum* has a crest-like appendage on the midvein of the outer tepals and blue-violet flowers, is cultivated everywhere, and the rhizome is used in medicine; *I. japonica* has pale blue flowers and grows in shady, moist places at forest margins; *I. lactea* has tough leaves and is common in saline-alkaline soils and steppe in the north, the leaves are used for weaving and the well-developed root system for soil binding; yellow flag (*I. pseudacorus*), native to Europe, has yellow flowers, grows in shallow water and is often planted at water's edge. The rhizomes of bearded iris (*I. germanica*) and *I. pallida* have a violet-like fragrance after long storage (orris root) and are used in perfumery.
- *I. domestica* (formerly *Belamcanda chinensis*, the blackberry lily): rhizome yellow, leaves distichous, equitant, arranged in a fan; flowers orange-red with dark red spots, tepals nearly equal and spreading, the style not petaloid; seeds black and glossy. It occurs throughout China and the rhizome is used in medicine to clear heat, remove toxins and soothe the throat. Molecular analyses show that it is nested within *Iris*, and it has therefore been merged into *Iris*.
- *Crocus*: saffron crocus (*C. sativus*) has a corm and flowers in autumn, the style has 3 deep red branches that protrude from the flower. The dried stigmas are the spice and drug saffron, which requires the stigmas of about 150,000 flowers per kilogram and is the most expensive spice. The saffron crocus is triploid and sterile and can be propagated only by corms; it probably originated from the wild *C. cartwrightianus* in Bronze Age Greece (Nemati et al. 2019). Its Chinese name, "Tibetan red flower", derives from the fact that it was once brought into the interior of China through Tibet; it does not grow in Tibet.
- *Gladiolus*: with corms; leaves sword-shaped; one-sided spike, flowers bilaterally symmetric, the lower part of the tepals fused into a curved tube. Garden gladiolus (cultivated groups such as *G.* × *gandavensis*) was bred by crossing species native to South Africa and is one of the world's most important cut flowers. *Freesia*, also from South Africa, has fragrant flowers and is often used as a cut flower and pot plant.

Key characters of the family: herbs with rhizomes, corms or bulbs; leaves distichous, equitant, sword-shaped; tepals 6, petaloid; stamens 3; ovary inferior, 3-locular, axile placentation; style with 3 branches, sometimes petaloid; capsule.

## 37.4. Asphodelaceae

Asphodelaceae are the family formed in APG IV by merging the asphodeloid and hemerocallid groups of the former Liliaceae with the formerly separate Aloaceae and Xanthorrhoeaceae (called Xanthorrhoeaceae in APG III, and renamed with the conserved name Asphodelaceae in APG IV). They are mostly herbs, succulents or woody plants, with leaves often basal, in a rosette, clasping, parallel-veined; inflorescences mostly erect racemes or panicles; tepals 6, often fused at the base into a tube; stamens 6; ovary superior, 3-locular; capsule or berry; seeds black, with a phytomelan layer. Floral formula: $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$. About 40 genera and 900 species, mainly in the Old World, especially in southern Africa and Australia. There are 3 subfamilies:

- Hemerocallidoideae: *Hemerocallis* (daylily) has fleshy tuberous roots and basal, ribbon-like leaves; spiral cymes form a panicle, the flowers are large, the lower part of the perianth fused into a funnel-shaped tube, and the stamens are inserted in the throat of the perianth tube; capsule. About 15–20 species in East Asia, about 11 in China. *H. fulva* has orange-red, unscented flowers, is cultivated everywhere, and is traditionally called the "forget-sorrow plant"; *H. citrina* (the source of the vegetable "golden needles") has large, pale yellow, fragrant flowers that open in the evening and wither before noon the next day, a night-flowering type pollinated by hawkmoths; the dried buds are the "daylily buds" of cooking, and fresh buds contain toxic compounds and must be thoroughly cooked or dried before they can be eaten. *H. minor* has smaller flowers and occurs in the north. *Dianella ensifolia* has blue-purple berries that are poisonous; the leaf fibers of New Zealand flax (*Phormium tenax*) are tough and were once an important fiber crop in New Zealand.
- Asphodeloideae: *Aloe*, about 500 species, mostly in southern Africa and Madagascar, has fleshy leaves with spiny margins in a rosette, and some species grow into tree-like plants with secondary thickening. The leaf gel of *A. vera* is used in skin-care products and foods, and the yellow sap beneath the leaf epidermis contains anthraquinones with a laxative effect (the Chinese drug "luhui"). *Haworthia* and *Gasteria* are common small succulents; *Kniphofia* (red-hot poker) has a dense raceme of orange-red flowers; *Eremurus* occurs from Central Asia to Xinjiang and Tibet in China. Cronquist set *Aloe* apart as Aloaceae; *Aloe* and its relatives share the same bimodal karyotype ($2n = 14$, 4 pairs of long and 3 pairs of short chromosomes).
- Xanthorrhoeoideae: *Xanthorrhoea* (grass tree) occurs in Australia, with a stout trunk wrapped in persistent leaf bases and a tall spike; it is fire-tolerant and flowers abundantly after fire.

Changes in circumscription: in the Chinese textbooks *Hemerocallis* is in Liliaceae and *Aloe* in Liliaceae or (Cronquist) Aloaceae.

## 37.5. Amaryllidaceae

### 37.5.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Family

APG IV Amaryllidaceae (the amaryllis family) is an enlarged family comprising 3 subfamilies: Agapanthoideae (the former Agapanthaceae), Allioideae (the former Alliaceae, including the tribe Allieae of the former Liliaceae) and Amaryllidoideae (Amaryllidaceae in the strict sense). The whole family has about 75 genera and 1600 species. Their shared characters are: herbs, often with bulbs; leaves linear and basal; inflorescence an umbel (in fact a pseudo-umbel composed of several helicoid cymes) borne at the top of a leafless scape, subtended by 2 (or several) membranous involucral bracts that enclose the whole inflorescence in the bud stage; tepals 6; stamens 6; ovary 3-locular; capsule or berry; seeds often black.

Changes in circumscription: the Chinese textbooks (following the Engler system) defined Amaryllidaceae by the inferior ovary (Cronquist merged the whole family into the broadly defined Liliaceae) and placed the superior-ovaried *Allium* in Liliaceae, while genera with an inferior ovary such as *Agave* and *Curculigo* were also placed in Amaryllidaceae. APG instead defines Amaryllidaceae by the structure of the umbel and its involucre and by molecular phylogeny: Hypoxidaceae, Ixioliriaceae, Doryanthaceae and the agavoids (now in Asparagaceae) are excluded, the tribes Agapantheae and Allieae are merged, but the brodiaeoids, which also have an umbel and were placed in Alliaceae (now the subfamily Brodiaeoideae of Asparagaceae), are not included. One family thus contains both the superior-ovaried onion and the inferior-ovaried daffodil, and the position of the ovary is no longer a family character here.

### 37.5.2. Allioideae

Diagnostic characters of Allioideae: perennial herbs with bulbs (covered by a membranous, fibrous or leathery tunic) or rhizomes, with a pungent onion or garlic smell throughout; leaves basal, linear, cylindrical (hollow) or flat; umbel at the top of the scape, at first enclosed by a membranous involucre, sometimes with bulbils in the inflorescence; tepals 6, free or fused at the base; stamens 6, the filaments often fused at the base and adnate to the tepals, those of the inner whorl sometimes toothed; ovary superior, 3-locular, 2 (to several) ovules per locule, septal nectaries; capsule loculicidal; seeds black.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:2)}$.

![Bulb and inflorescence of *Allium* (schematic). A: longitudinal section of an onion bulb: a flattened basal plate (the stem) bears adventitious roots below and successive concentric fleshy scale leaves (the leaf bases of the aerial leaves) above, with the terminal bud in the center and a dry, membranous tunic outside. B: transverse section of a garlic bulb: within a common membranous tunic are several cloves (small bulbs in the axils of scale leaves, each with its own bud) surrounding the remnant of the central scape. C: umbel at the top of a leafless scape, enclosed in a membranous involucre in the bud stage and splitting open at flowering; the garlic inflorescence often contains bulbils and sterile flowers](./plots/allium-bulb.py)

*Allium* has about 900–1000 species and is one of the largest genera among monocots, mainly in the north temperate zone with its center of distribution in Central Asia; China has about 140 species, found everywhere. The cells of *Allium* contain odorless sulfur-containing amino acids (such as alliin of garlic, S-allylcysteine sulfoxide), which are kept in a different compartment from the enzyme alliinase; when the tissue is chopped the two meet and alliin is broken down into strongly smelling thiosulfinates such as allicin. In onion a separate lachrymatory factor synthase converts an intermediate into volatile propanethial S-oxide, the substance that makes people weep when cutting onions (Imai et al. 2002). These compounds are chemical defenses against herbivores and pathogens. Representative plants:

- Onion (*A. cepa*): bulb large, flattened-globose, the scale leaves completely surrounding the basal plate; scape hollow and swollen in the middle; filaments of the inner whorl toothed at the base. Native to western Asia (Central Asia), one of the world's largest vegetable crops.
- Welsh onion (*A. fistulosum*): bulb club-shaped, only slightly thicker than the aerial part; leaves cylindrical and hollow; filaments of the inner whorl untoothed. Native to Asia and cultivated in China since ancient times.
- Garlic (*A. sativum*): the bulb consists of several to more than ten fleshy, clove-shaped small bulbs (cloves, which develop from axillary buds of the scale leaves), covered by a common membranous tunic; leaves flat, ribbon-like; the umbel at the top of the scape (the garlic scape) often contains many bulbils, and most flowers are sterile. Cultivated garlic almost never forms seeds and is propagated only by cloves and bulbils; it is native to Central Asia.
- Chinese chive (*A. tuberosum*): with rhizomes, bulb narrowly conical, the tunic fibrous; leaves linear, flat; flowers white. Both the leaves ("chives") and the scapes are eaten; blanched chives grown in the dark are called yellow chives.
- Others: the bulbs of rakkyo (*A. chinense*) are pickled and eaten; leek (*A. porrum*) has a thick pseudostem; wild *A. senescens*, *A. macrostemon* and *A. victorialis* are also often gathered for food.

### 37.5.3. Amaryllidoideae

Diagnostic characters of Amaryllidoideae: perennial herbs with bulbs (rarely rhizomes, as in *Clivia*); leaves linear or ribbon-like, basal; scape solid or hollow, bearing at the apex an umbel or a single flower, with an involucre; flowers bisexual, radially or slightly bilaterally symmetric; tepals 6, petaloid, often fused at the base into a tube, sometimes with a corona (a cup- or tube-like structure formed from appendages of the tepals or stamens); stamens 6, the filaments free or fused at the base; ovary inferior, 3-locular, axile placentation, ovules numerous; capsule or berry. The whole subfamily generally contains the alkaloids characteristic of Amaryllidaceae (such as lycorine and galantamine) and is poisonous.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{(3+3)} A_{3+3} \overline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$.

The subfamily has about 60 genera and 850 species, mainly in South America, southern Africa and the Mediterranean region; about 10 genera are wild or cultivated in China. Representative plants:

- *Lycoris* (spider lily): bulb with a brown tunic; flowers and leaves do not appear at the same time. *L. radiata* sends up the scape and flowers first in autumn, with bright red flowers whose tepals are crisped at the margin and strongly recurved, and stamens and style that project far out of the flower; leaves appear after the flowers fade, growing in winter and withering in summer. The cultivated and wild forms of this species in China are mostly triploid and do not set seed, propagating by bulbs; they are often seen in graveyards and on field banks (popularly called the equinox flower). *L. aurea* has bright yellow flowers; *L. sprengeri* has pale purplish red flowers. *Lycoris* has about 20 species, mainly in China and Japan.
- *Narcissus* (daffodil): bulb ovoid; leaves and scape emerge at the same time; flowers salver-shaped with a conspicuous corona, long and tubular like a trumpet or short and shallowly cup-like. About 50–60 species from the Mediterranean region to western Asia. The Chinese sacred lily (*N. tazetta* var. *chinensis*) has white flowers with a bright yellow, shallowly cup-shaped corona; it is derived from the Mediterranean paperwhite narcissus, which spread to China along the Silk Road and escaped along the coast of Fujian and Zhejiang; it is triploid and sets no seed, and the bulbs cultivated at Zhangzhou in Fujian are the most famous. The trumpet daffodil (*N. pseudonarcissus*) has a long, tubular corona.
- *Clivia*: with a rhizome and without a bulb, leaves distichous, broadly ribbon-like; umbel; berry red. *C. miniata*, native to South Africa, has a long tradition of cultivation in northeastern China (Changchun).
- Others: amaryllis (*Hippeastrum vittatum*, often given as *Amaryllis vittata* in Chinese textbooks; the true genus *Amaryllis* has only 2 species in South Africa), *Crinum asiaticum* var. *sinicum*, rain lily (*Zephyranthes candida*), rosepink zephyr lily (*Z. carinata*), spider lily (*Hymenocallis littoralis*), snowdrop (*Galanthus nivalis*) and snowflake (*Leucojum*) are all common ornamentals.

Medicinal use of the alkaloids of Amaryllidaceae. Galantamine was first isolated from *Galanthus* and later found also in *Lycoris* (*L. aurea*), *Narcissus* and *Leucojum*; it is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, formerly used to treat the sequelae of poliomyelitis and muscle weakness and now used mainly to treat Alzheimer's disease.

### 37.5.4. Agapanthoideae

Agapanthoideae contain only *Agapanthus*, with about 7–10 species in South Africa. They have rhizomes and ribbon-like leaves; the umbel is borne at the top of a long scape, with blue or white, funnel-shaped flowers; ovary superior. *A. africanus* is often cultivated as an ornamental. It was formerly placed in Liliaceae or treated as a separate family, Agapanthaceae.

Key characters of the family: herbs, often with bulbs; leaves linear, basal; umbel at the top of the scape, subtended by a membranous involucre; tepals 6, stamens 6, ovary 3-locular; in Allioideae the ovary is superior and there is an onion or garlic smell, in Amaryllidoideae the ovary is inferior, a corona is often present and the Amaryllidaceae alkaloids occur.

## 37.6. Asparagaceae and Other Families

### 37.6.1. Circumscription and Characters of Asparagaceae

APG IV Asparagaceae (the asparagus family) is a very broadly defined family, composed of a dozen or more small families formerly belonging to the broadly defined Liliaceae, Amaryllidaceae and Agavaceae, with about 115 genera and 2900 species, divided into 7 subfamilies: Agavoideae (including the former Agavaceae, Hostaceae, Anthericaceae and Anemarrhenaceae), Aphyllanthoideae, Asparagoideae (Asparagaceae in the strict sense), Brodiaeoideae (the former Themidaceae), Lomandroideae (the former Laxmanniaceae), Nolinoideae (including the former Convallariaceae, Dracaenaceae, Nolinaceae and Ruscaceae) and Scilloideae (the former Hyacinthaceae). The textbook by Judd et al. (2016) still treats several of these subfamilies as separate families (Agavaceae, Hyacinthaceae, Ruscaceae); APG IV adopts the merged treatment to avoid recognizing many families with one or a few genera, and both describe the same phylogenetic relationships.

Asparagaceae vary greatly in morphology, and it is hard to give a single set of diagnostic characters: herbs, shrubs or tree-like plants (*Dracaena*, *Yucca* and *Agave* have secondary thickening); leaves often long and narrow, ribbon-like, linear or sword-shaped, sometimes reduced to scales with cladodes in their place (*Asparagus*, *Ruscus*); inflorescence often a terminal raceme, spike or panicle; tepals 6, free or fused; stamens 6; ovary mostly superior (rarely half-inferior or inferior), 3-locular; capsule or berry. In groups with capsules the seeds are black and have phytomelan, while in groups with berries the phytomelan layer is often lost.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$ (varying greatly among genera).

### 37.6.2. Representative Plants of the Subfamilies

Asparagoideae: *Asparagus* has about 200 species, with rhizomes or clustered fleshy tuberous roots; stems erect or climbing, leaves reduced to dry, membranous scales, with green, needle-like or flattened cladodes arising in their axils and carrying out photosynthesis; flowers small, unisexual or bisexual; berry. *A. cochinchinensis* is a climber with fusiform tuberous roots used in medicine ("tiandong"); garden asparagus (*A. officinalis*) is erect and dioecious, and the young shoots that sprout in spring are the vegetable asparagus; asparagus fern (*A. setaceus*) has delicate cladodes and is often cultivated as an ornamental.

Nolinoideae:

- *Polygonatum* (Solomon's seal): rhizome stout, fleshy, jointed; stem unbranched; leaves alternate, opposite or whorled; flowers axillary, the tepals fused into a tubular bell; berry. About 70 species, mainly in East Asia, about 40 in China. *P. sibiricum* has whorled leaves whose tips are curled into hooks; *P. cyrtonema* has alternate leaves and filaments swollen at the apex. The rhizomes of both are the Chinese drug "huangjing". *P. odoratum* has alternate leaves, and its rhizome is the Chinese drug "yuzhu".
- *Convallaria*: lily of the valley (*C. majalis*) has 2 basal leaves and a one-sided raceme of white, bell-shaped, nodding, fragrant flowers; the whole plant contains cardiac glycosides (convallatoxin) and is poisonous, and was formerly used to treat heart failure.
- *Ophiopogon* (mondo grass) and *Liriope* (lilyturf): leaves basal, linear, the fibrous roots often swollen into fusiform small tubers; scape short, raceme. *Ophiopogon* has a half-inferior ovary, more or less drooping tepals and acute anthers; *Liriope* has a superior ovary, erect flowers and obtuse anthers. The tuberous roots of *O. japonicus* are the Chinese drug "maidong", and this species, *O. bodinieri* and *L. spicata* are widely used as ground covers. In both genera the seeds develop exposed after the pericarp ruptures early, so that the fruit looks like a berry ("seed berry") and is blue-black.
- *Reineckea carnea*, *Rohdea japonica* (to which *Campylandra* has been merged), cast-iron plant (*Aspidistra elatior*, with purple flowers that open at ground level), *Maianthemum* (including the former *Smilacina*, false Solomon's seal; flowers dimerous in *M. bifolium*) all occur in China and are commonly seen in the forest understory or cultivated as ornamentals.
- *Dracaena* (including the former *Sansevieria*): tree-like or herbaceous, leaves sword-shaped; tree-like species have secondary thickening. *D. cochinchinensis* occurs in Yunnan and Guangxi, and the red resin that flows from wounds is the Chinese drug "xuejie" ("dragon's blood"; another source is rattan palm, *Daemonorops*, of Arecaceae); *D. cinnabari* of the island of Socotra has an umbrella-shaped crown, and *D. draco* of the Canary Islands is also a source of "dragon's blood". Snake plant (*D. trifasciata*, formerly *Sansevieria trifasciata*) has fleshy, erect, cross-banded leaves, carries out crassulacean acid metabolism and is a common indoor plant; molecular evidence shows that *Sansevieria* is nested within *Dracaena*, and it has been merged into *Dracaena* since 2018. Lucky bamboo (*D. sanderiana*) is also a common foliage plant.
- *Ruscus*: butcher's broom (*R. aculeatus*) occurs in the Mediterranean region, its leaves are reduced to scales and the lateral branches flattened into ovate, spine-tipped cladodes, with the flowers borne on the midrib of the cladode ([7.12.24](/7.12.24#sec-24.3.1)). Ponytail palm (*Beaucarnea recurvata*) has a swollen stem base and occurs in Mexico.

Agavoideae:

- *Agave*: leaves large, fleshy, in a rosette, with spines on the margin and at the tip; mostly monocarpic (semelparous) plants that, after a dozen to several decades of growth, send up a tall panicle and die after flowering and fruiting ("century plants"). *A. americana* is cultivated everywhere as an ornamental; sisal (*A. sisalana*) has tough leaf fibers used for rope and sacks and is cultivated in southern China; the stem ("heart") of blue agave (*A. tequilana*) is cooked, fermented and distilled into tequila. *Agave* is mostly bat-pollinated and carries out crassulacean acid metabolism.
- *Yucca*: leaves sword-shaped and stiff, flowers white and nodding in a large panicle; the North American yuccas are pollinated only by yucca moths, a classic example of obligate pollination mutualism ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.7)). *Y. gloriosa* is often cultivated as an ornamental.
- *Hosta* (plantain lily): leaves basal, long-petiolate, the blade ovate with conspicuous arcuate veins; raceme, flowers funnel-shaped. *H. plantaginea* has white, fragrant flowers that open in the evening; *H. ventricosa* has purple flowers. Native to East Asia and often planted in the forest understory as a ground cover.
- *Anemarrhena*: *A. asphodeloides* has a stout, horizontal rhizome and basal, linear leaves; scape slender, raceme, flowers small, pale purplish red, stamens 3; it occurs from the northeast and north to the northwest, and the rhizome is a commonly used Chinese drug.
- Spider plant (*Chlorophytum comosum*): leaves linear, the scape bears plantlets with leaf tufts and aerial roots that serve for vegetative propagation; tuberose (*Polianthes tuberosa*) has white flowers with a strong fragrance at night and is used to extract perfume.

The bimodal karyotype of *Agave* and *Yucca* (5 pairs of large and 25 pairs of small chromosomes) was one of the early pieces of evidence for grouping them with *Hosta* and others ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.5)).

Scilloideae: mostly bulbous, with basal leaves; racemes. Hyacinth (*Hyacinthus orientalis*), native from the eastern Mediterranean to western Asia, has strongly scented flowers, and its bulbs were bred in large numbers in the Netherlands in the 18th century; *Barnardia japonica* (formerly *Scilla scilloides*) is a common wild species on hillsides in northern China, with pink flowers; *Ornithogalum* is also often cultivated.

Brodiaeoideae occur in western North America; the umbel resembles that of *Allium*, but the underground organ is a corm rather than a bulb and the plants lack the onion odor; molecular evidence places it in Asparagaceae and not in Amaryllidaceae, an example of the **convergent evolution** of the umbel within Asparagales.

Changes in circumscription: genera of the former broadly defined Liliaceae (*Asparagus*, *Polygonatum*, *Convallaria*, *Ophiopogon*, *Liriope*, *Hosta*, *Anemarrhena*, *Chlorophytum*, *Hyacinthus*, *Barnardia*, *Rohdea*, *Aspidistra*, *Reineckea*), of Agavaceae (*Agave*, *Yucca*, *Polianthes*, *Sansevieria*, *Dracaena*) and others are merged into this family.

## Questions

1. From what "typical" monocot flower is the orchid flower derived by specialization? Using floral diagrams, explain which part of the original flower the labellum, the gynostemium and the fertile stamens each correspond to.

   **Answer** The prototype is the pentacyclic flower $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3} G_{(3)}$. Of the inner perianth whorl, the median member (developmentally on the adaxial side, moved to the lower side by resupination) is specialized as the labellum, the other two are petals, and the outer 3 are sepals. Of the androecium, Orchidoideae and Epidendroideae retain only the median fertile stamen of the outer whorl, Cypripedioideae retain the 2 lateral stamens of the inner whorl (the median outer one becoming a shield-like staminode), and Apostasioideae retain 3 (or 2); the remaining stamens are reduced or lost. The fertile stamens are fused with the style and stigma into the gynostemium; of the 3 stigma lobes, 2 fuse into the fertile stigmatic cavity and the third becomes the rostellum. The ovary is of 3 fused carpels, inferior, 1-locular, with parietal placentation.

2. Why can orchid seeds be as small as dust? What effect does such a seed have on the life of orchids?

   **Answer** Orchid seeds have no endosperm, the embryo consists of only a few undifferentiated cells and stores almost no nutrients, and at germination an invading fungus supplies carbohydrates (orchid mycorrhiza), so the seed can be reduced to an extreme. The advantage is that one capsule can produce tens of thousands to millions of seeds, dispersed over long distances by wind and able to reach new islands and mountains rapidly; the cost is that each seed can germinate only if it meets a suitable fungus, and after germination it must pass through a slowly growing protocorm stage, so that most seeds die. In artificial propagation seeds must be sown aseptically on sugar-containing media, or the symbiotic fungus must be supplied. Saprophytic orchids such as *Gastrodia* that depend on fungi all their lives are the extreme case of this dependence.

3. Iridaceae, Amaryllidaceae and Liliaceae all have flowers with 6 petaloid tepals. How can the three families be distinguished?

   **Answer** Liliaceae: stamens 6, ovary superior, anthers extrorse, seeds flat and pale, mostly with bulbs. Amaryllidaceae: stamens 6, with an umbel and a membranous involucre, leaves basal; Amaryllidoideae have an inferior ovary and often a corona, Allioideae a superior ovary and an onion or garlic smell, and black seeds. Iridaceae: only 3 stamens (outer whorl), opposite the outer tepals; ovary inferior; leaves distichous, equitant and sword-shaped; style with 3 branches, often petaloid.

4. In the Chinese textbooks *Allium* belongs to Liliaceae, and in APG IV to Amaryllidaceae. Why is the position of the ovary no longer used to define Amaryllidaceae?

   **Answer** Traditional Amaryllidaceae were separated from Liliaceae (ovary superior) by the inferior ovary, with the result that *Agave* and *Curculigo*, which have an inferior ovary, were put into Amaryllidaceae, while *Allium*, which has an umbel and a membranous involucre, was put into Liliaceae. Molecular phylogenetics shows that *Allium*, *Agapanthus*, daffodils, *Lycoris* and others form a clade, whose shared character is an umbel at the top of the scape enclosed by two membranous involucral bracts (composed of helicoid cymes); *Agave* belongs to Asparagaceae and *Curculigo* to Hypoxidaceae. The position of the ovary changed several times independently within Asparagales, and so it cannot serve as a defining character of the family.

5. Which plants of Asparagaceae use modified stems or leaves in place of ordinary leaves for photosynthesis? What do they have in common with cacti?

   **Answer** In *Asparagus* and *Ruscus* the leaves are reduced to scales, and photosynthesis is carried out by cladodes (modified stems) arising in the leaf axils: in asparagus and asparagus fern the cladodes are needle-like or linear, and in *Ruscus* they are ovate and can bear flowers. The leaves of snake plant and agave are fleshy and carry out crassulacean acid metabolism. In cacti the leaves are reduced to spines and photosynthesis is carried out by the fleshy stem; like asparagus and *Ruscus* they are analogous organs in which the stem replaces the leaf, and like snake plant and agave they are adapted to drought and carry out crassulacean acid metabolism, all as a result of convergent evolution of different groups in similar environments.
