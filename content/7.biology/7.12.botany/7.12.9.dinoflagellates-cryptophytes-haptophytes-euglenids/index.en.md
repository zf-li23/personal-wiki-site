This chapter completes the survey of the algae with the groups that consist mainly of unicellular flagellates: the dinoflagellates, cryptophytes, haptophytes and euglenids, together with the chlorarachniophytes, which Chinese textbooks do not rank as a phylum. These organisms are small, mostly motile and nutritionally versatile, and all of their plastids arose by secondary (in dinoflagellates, sometimes tertiary) endosymbiosis. The plastids of dinoflagellates, cryptophytes and haptophytes come from red algae; those of euglenids and chlorarachniophytes come from green algae. For this reason these groups were the hardest to place in the old "pigment lineage" scheme, and they are the clearest illustrations of the endosymbiotic framework. The last two sections summarize the algae as a whole: evolutionary trends (§9.5) and ecological and economic importance (§9.6).

The cell biology of each group is covered in [7.11.20](/7.11.20) (dinoflagellates in §20.4.2, haptophytes and cryptophytes in §20.9, euglenids in §20.2.1). The endosymbiotic origin of plastids is treated in [7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.2.6) and [7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.2.3), and the green algae in [7.12.13](/7.12.13).

## 9.1. Dinoflagellates

### 9.1.1. General Characteristics

The **dinoflagellates** ([Definition 20.4.2](/7.11.20#def-20.4.2); Chinese textbooks call them the phylum Pyrrophyta or Dinophyta) are an important group of planktonic algae. More than 2000 extant species have been described (older counts gave 126 genera and a little over 1000 species). Most are marine, a few live in fresh and brackish water, and some are parasites of fishes, copepods and other invertebrates. Dinoflagellates and diatoms are the main food of many aquatic animals; when they multiply excessively they cause red tides (§9.6).

- Thallus: the great majority are unicellular, ranging from spherical to needle-like and dorsiventrally or laterally flattened. A very few are nonmotile coccoid forms or branched filaments (*Dinoclonium*, *Dinothrix*).
- Cell covering: beneath the plasma membrane lies a layer of flattened vesicles, the amphiesma. In many species the vesicles contain cellulosic plates (thecal plates); such species are called armored (thecate), whereas those whose vesicles contain no plates are unarmored (naked). In desmokont dinoflagellates (e.g., *Prorocentrum*) the wall consists of two lateral valves and there are neither transverse nor longitudinal grooves. In dinokont dinoflagellates the wall is divided into an upper part (epitheca, or epicone) and a lower part (hypotheca, or hypocone), separated by a slightly depressed transverse groove (cingulum) around the cell's waist; a longitudinal groove (sulcus) on the ventral face crosses the cingulum. The shape, number and arrangement of the plates differ among genera. Following Kofoid, the plates are numbered in series (apical, precingular, cingular, postcingular and antapical) and written as a plate formula, which is the main basis of taxonomy.
- Flagella: two, emerging from the flagellar pore where the cingulum and sulcus meet. The transverse flagellum is ribbon-shaped and lies in the cingulum, beating in a wave that rotates the cell; the longitudinal flagellum is thread-like, extends backward along the sulcus and beats like a whip, driving the cell forward. Both have a 9 + 2 axoneme and are covered with fine hairs. In desmokont dinoflagellates both flagella emerge from the anterior end.
- Nucleus: the dinokaryon (the mesokaryon of Chinese textbooks; [7.12.6](/7.12.6#def-6.2.2)) is large and conspicuous. Its chromosomes remain condensed throughout the cell cycle and look like strings of beads; histones are scarce and the DNA is packaged by nucleoproteins of viral origin. During mitosis the nuclear envelope and nucleolus persist; channels formed by invaginations of the envelope run through the nucleus, the spindle microtubules lie in the cytoplasm inside these channels, and the chromosomes attach to the envelope. In anaphase the nucleus elongates sideways and is pinched in two by the cingulum. Dinoflagellate genomes are among the largest known.
- Chromatophores (plastids): usually many, discoid, plate-like, rod-like or ribbon-like, mostly parietal; rod-shaped ones are often radially arranged. A typical peridinin-containing plastid has three envelope membranes, the outermost not continuous with the nuclear envelope, and thylakoids in stacks of three. The pigments are chlorophylls *a* and *c*₂, β-carotene and xanthophylls such as peridinin; the yellow pigments greatly outnumber chlorophyll, so the plastids are golden, yellow-green or brown. In some dinoflagellates the plastids come from haptophytes, diatoms or green algae ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.2.6)), and the pigment composition changes accordingly; about half of all dinoflagellates have no plastids and live heterotrophically.
- Storage products: starch (in the cytoplasm) and oil.
- Other organelles: many marine species have a pusule, a noncontractile sac that opens by a pore near the flagellar pore and may function in osmoregulation and uptake. Some species have an eyespot made of lipid droplets, and a few colorless species have an elaborate "eye" (ocelloid) with a lens-like and a retina-like structure, like a camera. Trichocysts, produced by the Golgi apparatus, discharge threads up to 200 µm long when stimulated and cannot be retracted.

Many dinoflagellates are mixotrophic or entirely heterotrophic: they prey on bacteria, other algae and even small animals with pseudopodia, a feeding tube (peduncle) or a feeding net, and some are parasitic. Bioluminescent species (*Noctiluca*, the gonyaulacoid dinoflagellates) flash when the water is disturbed, making the waves glow blue at night ("sea fire").

### 9.1.2. Reproduction and Life Cycle

Vegetative reproduction is mainly by cell division, often oblique and longitudinal. In some armored species each daughter cell inherits half of the parent's theca and regenerates the other half and the second flagellum (*Ceratium*); in others the whole theca is shed before division. Some species produce zoospores, aplanospores or thick-walled resting spores.

Sexual reproduction has been observed in only a few species and is generally isogamous. Two gametes (often smaller than vegetative cells) fuse to form a quadriflagellate motile zygote, which loses its flagella and becomes a thick-walled resting cyst (hypnozygote) filled with reserves and sinks to the bottom. On germination it undergoes meiosis (zygotic meiosis), so the vegetative cells of dinoflagellates are haploid (*Noctiluca* is an exception and is diploid). The wall of the resting cyst is made of decay-resistant organic material and is preserved in sediments as dinocyst fossils, first known from the Triassic (about 240 million years ago); they are important material for biostratigraphy and paleoceanography. Cysts in the seafloor sediments are also the seed stock from which harmful red tides erupt year after year.

### 9.1.3. Classification

Chinese textbooks divide the dinoflagellates into two classes (or one class with two subclasses):

- Desmophyceae (subclass Desmokontae): wall made of two lateral valves, no transverse or longitudinal groove, two apical flagella. Only the order Prorocentrales (sometimes divided into Desmomonadales and Prorocentrales) is included, and the group was considered primitive.
- Dinophyceae (subclass Dinokontae): wall of several plates or absent, usually with a cingulum and a sulcus, flagella ventral. It includes Gymnodiniales, Peridiniales, Dinophysiales, Phytodiniales, Dinococcales, Dinotrichales and others.

Molecular phylogenies show that the prorocentroids are nested within the dinokonts, so their apical flagella and two-valved shell are derived rather than primitive. Current classification places all dinoflagellates with a dinokaryon in the class Dinophyceae, divided into Gymnodiniales, Peridiniales, Gonyaulacales, Prorocentrales, Dinophysiales, Suessiales (including the family of symbiotic dinoflagellates) and others; *Noctiluca* is often assigned to its own class, Noctilucophyceae. Dinoflagellates belong to the alveolates and share with the ciliates and the apicomplexans (including the malaria parasite *Plasmodium*) the vesicles beneath the plasma membrane. The apicoplast of apicomplexans is homologous to the plastids of dinoflagellates: it is a red-algal plastid that has lost photosynthesis.

### 9.1.4. Representative Genera

*Exuviaella* (desmokont; now mostly merged into *Prorocentrum*): marine, unicellular, elliptical and laterally flattened. The wall consists of two valves with irregular pores; the two apical flagella emerge from a ring-shaped flagellar pore surrounded by small toothlike projections. There are two plate-like parietal chromatophores, with or without pyrenoids; starch and oil are stored, and the nucleus lies at the posterior end. The cell divides longitudinally, each daughter cell receiving one valve and secreting a new one.

*Prorocentrum* (Prorocentrales): cells ovate or slightly heart-shaped, laterally flattened. Two flagella emerge between the two valves at the anterior end, and a toothlike projection lies beside the flagellar pore. The valve surface is covered with pores except along the longitudinal suture; there are two chromatophores. *P. micans* and *P. donghaiense* are the main red-tide species of Chinese coastal waters, and *P. lima* produces okadaic acid, which causes diarrhetic shellfish poisoning.

*Gymnodinium* (Gymnodiniales): unicellular, round or elliptical, naked or with a thin wall, the epicone smaller or larger than the hypocone. The two grooves are distinct and the flagella emerge at their intersection. Chromatophores may be present or absent; when present they are numerous, discoid or rod-shaped, parietal or radially arranged, and golden-brown, green or blue-green. Many marine species lack chromatophores, and an eyespot may or may not be present. Reproduction is by cell division, thick-walled spores may form, and sexual reproduction is isogamous. Some species cause red tides. In current classification the former *G. breve* has been moved to *Karenia* (*Karenia brevis*, producer of brevetoxins) and the former *Gymnodinium sanguineum* to *Akashiwo* (*Akashiwo sanguinea*).

*Noctiluca* (Noctilucales): a single species, *N. scintillans* (synonym *N. miliaris*), the best-known luminescent organism of the world's oceans. It is a large unicellular organism up to 1–2 mm in diameter, spherical or kidney-shaped, with a motile tentacle at the apex and a vestigial flagellum beside it; the grooves are indistinct. The center is occupied by a large vacuole traversed by cytoplasmic strands; the cell has one nucleus and no chromatophores and is heterotrophic, catching plankton with its tentacle. The cytoplasm contains countless luminous granules (scintillons), which make the sea glow at night when the organism blooms, and the bloom forms a pink or orange-red tide. Green *Noctiluca* of tropical seas photosynthesizes by means of endosymbiotic green algae (*Pedinomonas*). Vegetative cells multiply by binary fission; in sexual reproduction one cell produces hundreds of small, gymnodinioid gametes with two flagella and no tentacle.

*Peridinium* (Peridiniales; about 200 species): a main planktonic genus of fresh and marine waters, most species being freshwater. Cells are unicellular, spherical, elliptical or ovate (rarely polygonal) and dorsiventrally flattened, with a slightly convex dorsal and a flat or concave ventral face. The anterior end is often dome-shaped or drawn out into a horn, the posterior end blunt or bifurcate; the sulcus and cingulum are distinct. The theca consists of plates that have definite names and are arranged in a definite plate formula, which is the basis for distinguishing species. Chromatophores may be present or absent, usually numerous and granular, yellow-green, yellow-brown or brownish red; pyrenoids and an eyespot may be present; starch and oil are stored; the large nucleus lies in the middle of the cell. Reproduction is by oblique longitudinal division, and zoospores and thick-walled spores also occur; a few species reproduce sexually.

*Ceratium* (Gonyaulacales; about 80 species): unicellular and asymmetrical, with a long apical horn formed by plate extension and open at the tip, and two or three short antapical horns. The cell surface is covered by plates; the cingulum lies in the middle and a ventral slit gives passage to the flagella. There is a nucleus and one eyespot; the chromatophores are numerous, small, granular and orange-yellow. Cells divide obliquely, each daughter cell receiving half of the old wall and one flagellum and then regenerating the other half and a second flagellum; thick-walled resting spores form at the end of the growing season. The freshwater *C. hirundinella* often dominates lake phytoplankton in summer; the marine species have been moved to *Tripos* (Gómez 2013).

*Dinoclonium* (Dinotrichales): a single species, *D. conradii*, a rare freshwater dinoflagellate that grows epiphytically on other algae. The thallus is a branched filament differentiated into a prostrate and an erect part, the cells at the tips of the erect branches gradually tapering, like the heterotrichous filaments of green algae. The cells are barrel-shaped with patterned walls and a central vacuole; chromatophores are numerous, with pyrenoids, oil droplets are stored, and some cells have a conspicuous eyespot. At reproduction each vegetative cell produces one or two zoospores, with an eyespot, a sulcus, a cingulum and two flagella, looking just like a *Gymnodinium*.

*Dinothrix* (Dinotrichales): a single species, *D. paradoxa*, also a rare freshwater dinoflagellate, growing on filaments of green algae. The thallus is a short, stout branched filament of short barrel-shaped cells whose protoplast has the typical dinoflagellate organization, with a conspicuous eyespot and a sulcus and cingulum; the cells divide obliquely and each daughter cell develops an eyespot, a sulcus and a cingulum. *Dinoclonium* and *Dinothrix* represent the most complex thallus types among dinoflagellates, but their zoospores are still typical dinoflagellates, which shows that the filaments arose secondarily within the group.

*Alexandrium* (Gonyaulacales): armored unicellular organisms, in some species forming short chains, and the main producers of the paralytic shellfish toxin saxitoxin ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.4.2)). Resting cysts overwinter in the sediment and are the reason red tides recur at the same place.

Symbiotic dinoflagellates: the family Symbiodiniaceae (formerly the single genus *Symbiodinium*, now divided into several genera) lives inside the cells of reef-building corals, sea anemones, giant clams and foraminifera in the form of **zooxanthellae**. They lose their flagella and theca and exist as spherical nonmotile cells, passing most of their photosynthate to the host; outside the host they can revert to flagellated motile cells. The coral–dinoflagellate symbiosis and its breakdown under heat stress (**coral bleaching**) are treated in [7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.5.4).

## 9.2. Cryptophytes

**Definition 9.2.1 (Cryptophytes and Nucleomorph)** **Cryptophytes** are unicellular flagellates with a dorsiventrally flattened cell, a longitudinal groove or gullet on the anterior ventral side, two slightly unequal flagella bearing tubular hairs that emerge from the anterior ventral side, and plastids of red algal origin that retain a **nucleomorph** between their envelope membranes. The nucleomorph is the remnant nucleus of the engulfed alga and lies between the second and third membranes of the secondary plastid.

There are about 20 genera and over 200 species worldwide (older counts gave 5 families and 9 genera), and more than 34 species in 4 genera are known from China. Cryptophytes live in fresh and marine water, some preferring waters rich in organic matter and nitrogen, and a few occur in soil. They were early treated as a class of the dinoflagellates; because the structure of their cells, plastids and flagella differs from that of dinoflagellates they were later given a separate phylum.

- Thallus: most are unicellular with two flagella, a few form amorphous colonies (palmelloid stage). Cells are elongate-elliptical or ovate, with the anterior end broad and blunt or obliquely truncate, markedly flattened, the dorsal side slightly convex and the ventral side flat or slightly concave. A longitudinal groove extends backward from one side of the anterior ventral end, and some species also have a gullet.
- Cell surface: there is no cellulose wall; beneath (or above) the plasma membrane lies a periplast made of proteinaceous plates, whose shape, visible under the scanning electron microscope, is a taxonomic character.
- Ejectosomes: structures unique to cryptophytes, each formed by two coiled ribbons and structurally unlike the trichocysts of dinoflagellates. Large ejectosomes line the groove and gullet, small ones are scattered around the cell periphery; when stimulated they eject a long thread that makes the cell dart backward.
- Chromatophores: one or two, large and lobed, with four envelope membranes, the outermost continuous with the nuclear envelope, and thylakoids in stacks of two. The pigments are chlorophylls *a* and *c*₂, α-carotene, alloxanthin and one phycobiliprotein (phycoerythrin or phycocyanin, each species having only one). Unlike those of cyanobacteria and red algae, the phycobiliproteins of cryptophytes do not form phycobilisomes on the outer surface of the thylakoids but are dissolved in the thylakoid lumen. Because of differences in pigment composition and proportions, cells are yellow-green, yellow-brown, blue-green, green or red; some species lack plastids. There are one to several pyrenoids.
- Nucleomorph: located in the periplastidial space between the plastid envelope and the outer chloroplast endoplasmic reticulum, enclosed by two membranes and containing DNA. The nucleomorph genome of *Guillardia theta* has only about 550 000 base pairs on three chromosomes, one of the smallest known eukaryotic nuclear genomes.
- Storage products: starch, located in the periplastidial space (between the plastid envelope and the outer endoplasmic reticulum), and oil.
- Flagella: two, slightly unequal, emerging from the anterior ventral side (at the gullet); the longer carries two rows of lateral tubular hairs and the shorter one row of short hairs. There are one or two contractile vacuoles at the anterior end.

Cryptophytes usually reproduce by longitudinal cell division: the motile cell rounds up and comes to rest, and as the protoplast divides a gelatinous sheath is secreted around the daughter cells, which gradually return to the motile unicellular state; amorphous colonies may form. A few species produce zoospores, and sexual reproduction is very rare (*Proteomonas* has morphologically different haploid and diploid phases).

- *Cryptomonas* (more than 30 species in China): unicellular, elliptical, bean-shaped, ovate or spindle-shaped, with the anterior end blunt or obliquely truncate, indented in the middle and with a blunt posterior end. The groove and gullet are distinct and ventral, and the two flagella emerge from the gullet; ejectosomes may be present or absent. There are one or two yellow-green or yellow-brown (sometimes red) chromatophores and one to four pyrenoids, or none; the nucleus lies at the posterior end. *Cryptomonas* lives in lakes, ponds and fish ponds and is an excellent food for zooplankton.
- *Chroomonas*: two unequal flagella; the nucleus lies at the posterior end and a contractile vacuole at the anterior end. There are one or two parietal chromatophores containing phycocyanin, blue to blue-green; the starch grains are large and arranged in rows, and there is a single pyrenoid in the center or at the posterior end of the cell.

Cryptophytes often dominate under ice and in cold waters, and are used as food for shellfish hatcheries and zooplankton culture (*Rhodomonas*). The ciliate *Mesodinium* retains the plastids, and even the nuclei, of the cryptophytes it ingests and keeps them working, and the dinoflagellate *Dinophysis* in turn steals these plastids from *Mesodinium* ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.2.6)); this is a living example of plastid transfer between lineages.

Cryptophytes and dinoflagellates differ as follows:

| Feature | Cryptophytes | Dinoflagellates |
|---|---|---|
| Cell covering | proteinaceous periplast, no cellulose | vesicles beneath the plasma membrane, often with cellulosic thecal plates |
| Grooves | longitudinal groove and gullet on the anterior ventral side | transverse groove around the cell and ventral longitudinal groove |
| Flagella | two, emerging from the anterior ventral side, both with tubular hairs | transverse and longitudinal flagella, emerging at the intersection of the grooves |
| Nucleus | ordinary eukaryotic | dinokaryon (mesokaryon) |
| Plastid envelope | 4 membranes, with nucleomorph | 3 membranes (peridinin type) |
| Thylakoids | in stacks of 2, phycobiliproteins in the lumen | in stacks of 3, no phycobiliproteins |
| Pigments | chlorophylls *a*, *c*, alloxanthin, phycobiliproteins | chlorophylls *a*, *c*, peridinin |
| Discharge organelles | ejectosomes made of two coiled ribbons | rod-shaped trichocysts produced by the Golgi apparatus |

In the old "pigment lineage" scheme, cryptophytes contain chlorophyll *c* (the yellow lineage) and also phycobilins (the red lineage), yet have thylakoids in stacks of two; they were treated as "another flagellated branch on the chlorophyll *a* and phycobilin line". The endosymbiotic explanation is much simpler: the cryptophyte host engulfed a red alga, whose organelles were mostly digested, leaving only the plastid and a residual nucleus (the nucleomorph). The phycobiliproteins are a legacy of the red alga, and chlorophyll *c* is common to secondary plastids of this kind.

## 9.3. Haptophytes

**Definition 9.3.1 (Haptophytes and Haptonema)** Unicellular algae that bear, between the two flagella, a slender thread-like appendage, the **haptonema**, are called **haptophytes**. The haptonema is not a flagellum: it consists of 6–7 microtubules surrounded by a sheath of endoplasmic reticulum and can coil and extend rapidly, serving in attachment and prey capture.

There are about 300 species of haptophytes (also called prymnesiophytes) worldwide, most of them marine and a few in fresh or brackish water. The great majority are unicellular flagellates, and some have an amoeboid, palmelloid or filamentous stage in the life cycle. The cell surface is covered with many thin scales of organic material (mainly cellulose-like polysaccharides) or calcareous scales. There are contractile vacuoles, and an eyespot may or may not be present. The 2–4 chromatophores are parietal, plate-like and golden or yellow-brown, with four envelope membranes (the outermost continuous with the nuclear envelope) and thylakoids in stacks of three; there is often a pyrenoid, but no girdle lamella as in the heterokont algae. The pigments are chlorophylls *a* and *c*, β-carotene and fucoxanthin (and its derivative 19′-hexanoyloxyfucoxanthin). The storage product is chrysolaminarin, held in a vesicle at the posterior end of the cell. The two flagella are smooth (whiplash type), equal or unequal in length; in some species the haptonema is reduced or absent. Vegetative reproduction is by longitudinal cell division, and aplanospores are also formed.

Prey capture by the haptonema has been best studied in *Chrysochromulina*: the haptonema picks up small edible particles from the environment, the particles move along it toward the base and collect into a larger mass in its lower-middle part, and the mass then moves along the haptonema to the tip; the haptonema bends toward the cell and delivers the food mass to the posterior end of the cell, where it is engulfed. Many haptophytes are therefore mixotrophic.

Chinese textbooks divide the haptophytes into four orders: Isochrysidales, Prymnesiales, Coccosphaerales and Pavlovales, of which only a few species of the Prymnesiales live in fresh water. Current classification divides the haptophytes into two classes, Pavlovophyceae (two unequal flagella, short haptonema) and Prymnesiophyceae (with the orders Phaeocystales, Prymnesiales, Isochrysidales, Coccolithales and others). They belong to the Haptista and are not as closely related to the heterokonts as the older view held.

- *Chrysochromulina* (Prymnesiales): unicellular, spherical, naked and slightly deformable. The anterior end bears three appendages: two equal or nearly equal flagella at the sides and, between them, a long haptonema that is often coiled into a spiral. The cell surface is covered with many small, thin scales of organic material that can be seen only with the electron microscope; there are 2–4 parietal golden-brown chromatophores, and chrysolaminarin lies at the posterior end of the cell.
- *Prymnesium*: *P. parvum* forms blooms in brackish water, and its ichthyotoxins (prymnesins) cause mass mortality of cultured fish.
- *Phaeocystis*: the life cycle includes a unicellular motile stage and a spherical colonial stage with thousands of cells embedded in a gel. Colonial blooms produce masses of foam at the sea surface and are an important source of marine dimethyl sulfide.
- *Isochrysis*: two equal flagella, haptonema reduced; rich in unsaturated fatty acids and an important food for shellfish hatcheries.

A *coccolithophore* is a haptophyte whose surface is covered with calcareous scales, the coccoliths. Coccoliths form as calcite crystals inside coccolith vesicles derived from the Golgi apparatus and are then secreted to the cell surface, where they are arranged in a layer (the coccosphere). The exquisite and varied shapes of coccoliths are used to identify species. *Emiliania huxleyi* (transferred to *Gephyrocapsa* in 2023) is the most abundant coccolithophore in the oceans. Its diploid phase is nonmotile and covered with coccoliths, whereas the haploid phase is flagellated and has only organic scales, and both phases can reproduce for long periods by mitosis (a haplodiplontic-like life cycle with two independently proliferating phases). Its blooms can cover several hundred thousand square kilometers and look milky white in satellite images.

Coccolithophores affect the global carbon cycle in two opposing ways. Photosynthesis fixes $\ce{CO2}$ as organic carbon, which on sinking carries carbon into the deep ocean (the organic carbon pump). Calcification, $\ce{Ca^2+ + 2HCO3- -> CaCO3 + CO2 + H2O}$, releases $\ce{CO2}$ as calcium carbonate forms (the carbonate pump) and so slightly raises the $\ce{CO2}$ of surface water in the short term; but the calcium carbonate coccoliths make aggregates of organic matter heavier and speed their sinking (the ballast effect), and in the long term bury carbon in sediments. The large coccolith deposits of the Cretaceous became chalk (the Cretaceous is named for it), and the white cliffs of Dover in England are an accumulation of coccoliths. Ocean acidification reduces the calcification ability of coccolithophores, which is a focus of current research. Coccolithophores and *Phaeocystis* also produce large amounts of dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP); its breakdown product dimethyl sulfide enters the atmosphere, forms sulfate aerosols and acts as cloud condensation nuclei.

## 9.4. Euglenids and Chlorarachniophytes

### 9.4.1. General Characteristics of Euglenids

**Definition 9.4.1 (Euglenids and Pellicle)** **Euglenids** are unicellular flagellates without a cellulose cell wall, whose cell surface is a **pellicle** made of interlocking proteinaceous strips beneath the plasma membrane, whose plastids have three membranes and are of green algal origin, and whose storage product is paramylon.

There are about 40 genera and 800–1000 species worldwide; following *Freshwater Algae of China* there are 1 class, 1 order, 5 families and 21 genera in China. Euglenids live mainly in fresh water, especially in small standing waters rich in organic matter; they thrive as the temperature rises, and when they multiply massively the water turns deep green and blooms may form at the surface. A few live in brackish water, very few in the sea, a few are snow algae, and some are parasites in the digestive tracts of aquatic animals (amphibians). Many species are colorless and feed osmotrophically or by ingesting solid food. Euglenids were once regarded as the common ancestors of animals and plants; in zoology they are called euglenoid flagellates and placed in the class Mastigophora of the Protozoa.

- Pellicle: euglenids have no cell wall; the pellicle consists of many spirally arranged, tightly joined proteinaceous strips, each with a ridge protruding on one side and a groove on the other, and adjacent strips interlock like joints. Microtubules and mucus-secreting bodies (mucocysts) lie beneath the strips. In species with a flexible pellicle the strips can slide past one another, so that the cell can contract and change shape (euglenoid movement); species with a rigid pellicle have a fixed shape (*Phacus*). Some species have a lorica outside the cell, open at the anterior end, through which the flagella emerge.
- Anterior structures: the cell has an invaginated cytostome (canal) at its anterior end, leading to a narrow cytopharynx (canal) and a pouch-shaped reservoir; in pigmented species this is not a feeding passage but an outlet for wastes. Near the reservoir lies a large contractile vacuole surrounded by a ring of small ones, which collect water and wastes, empty them into the reservoir and discharge them to the outside through the canal.
- Flagella: two, arising from the base of the reservoir. One extends outside through the canal; this is the locomotory flagellum, with one or two rows of fine mastigonemes (not tubular) and a paraxonemal rod inside the flagellum, and with a swelling at its base, the paraflagellar body. The other is very short and stays inside the reservoir.
- Eyespot: lies in the cytoplasm at the side of the reservoir, dorsal to the canal (not inside a plastid), and consists of 20–50 orange-red oil droplets containing β-carotene and its derivatives. The true photoreceptor is the paraflagellar body, in which a photoactivated adenylyl cyclase senses light; the eyespot is a shading device. As the cell swims in a spiral, the eyespot periodically shades the paraflagellar body from light coming from one side, so that the cell perceives the direction of light and adjusts its movement to swim toward suitable illumination.
- Nucleus: usually large and conspicuous, in the middle or posterior part of the cell. The chromosomes remain condensed in interphase, and in mitosis the nuclear envelope and nucleolus persist and the spindle forms inside the nucleus (closed mitosis); Chinese textbooks call this nucleus the mesokaryon ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#def-6.2.2)).
- Chromatophores: many, discoid, lobed, ribbon-like or star-shaped, parietal or radially arranged, green; three envelope membranes (the outer one is an endoplasmic-reticulum-like membrane not continuous with the nuclear envelope) and thylakoids in stacks of three. The pigments are chlorophylls *a* and *b*, β-carotene and xanthophylls such as diadinoxanthin, similar to but not identical with those of green algae. In some species the pyrenoid consists of two halves protruding on both sides of the plastid and is capped with paramylon.
- Storage products: paramylon (euglenin) and oil. Paramylon is a β-1,3-glucan, insoluble in water and not stained blue by iodine, found only in the cytoplasm and never in the plastids. Its granules have definite shapes (discs, rods, cups or coils) and are attached to the pyrenoid or scattered in the cytoplasm; they are used in identification.

### 9.4.2. Reproduction

Euglenids reproduce by longitudinal cell division, which can take place in the motile or in the palmelloid state. At the start of division the flagellar end of the cell invaginates, the nucleus divides mitotically, the flagellar apparatus and the eyespot also divide, and the cell then constricts longitudinally from front to back, each daughter cell retaining half of the plastids and paramylon grains; one daughter retains the original flagellum and the other grows a new one. In the palmelloid state the cells first lose their flagella and secrete a thick gelatinous sheath, within which they divide repeatedly to form multicellular palmelloid colonies; when conditions are favorable each cell develops into a new individual. Under adverse conditions the cell stops moving and secretes a thick wall to form a cyst, from which the protoplast emerges and germinates when conditions improve. Euglenids have no asexual spores, and sexual reproduction has not been confirmed.

### 9.4.3. Representative Genera

- *Euglena* (about 155 species worldwide, about 60 in China): cells spindle-shaped, long-spindle-shaped or cylindrical, with a broad, blunt anterior end and a pointed posterior end that may extend into a tail or spine; no lorica. The pellicle is flexible or semirigid, with spirally arranged striations or granules, and the shape is variable. There is one locomotory flagellum, the other being reduced and remaining in the reservoir; the eyespot is conspicuous and the nucleus large and round. There are one to many chromatophores, mostly parietal discs, in a few species axial and star-shaped (only 1–2). A few species are colorless, or red because they contain euglenarhodone (astaxanthin); blooms of *E. sanguinea* turn ponds red. Most species live in fresh water, preferring warm small standing waters rich in organic matter; some tolerate pollution, and only one (*E. marina*) is marine. *E. gracilis* is a model organism for physiology and biotechnology; in the dark, or when treated with streptomycin, it loses its chromatophores and becomes heterotrophic ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.2.1)), and its paramylon is produced industrially. Molecular phylogenies show that the traditional genus *Euglena* is polyphyletic, and part of its species have been moved to *Euglenaria*, *Lepocinclis* and other genera.
- *Phacus*: unicellular, with a rigid pellicle and a fixed shape; the thallus is laterally flattened, leaf-like and often twisted. The pellicle has longitudinal or spiral striations; there are two flagella, the locomotory one extending outside and the short one hidden in the reservoir. There are many chromatophores and one or several paramylon bodies, often large and ring- or disc-shaped; the eyespot is conspicuous and the nucleus lies at the posterior end.
- *Trachelomonas* (about 191 species worldwide, about 100 in China): unicellular, enclosed in a lorica, the protoplast within being naked. The lorica consists of gelatinous material and precipitated iron or manganese compounds, and is colorless, yellow, orange or brown depending on the kind and amount of metal, transparent or opaque; the surface is smooth or has dots, pores, a network, granules or spines, and the apex has a round flagellar pore with or without a collar. When the cell divides longitudinally, in some species both daughter cells leave the parent's lorica, whereas in others only one escapes and the other remains in the old lorica and grows a flagellum; the daughter cell secretes a gelatinous material to form a new lorica. It is widespread in fish ponds, puddles, ponds, lakes, marshes and reservoirs, and in large numbers it turns the water yellow-brown.
- *Colacium* (Colaciales; about 11 species worldwide, about 6 in China): the cells are enclosed in a gelatinous envelope and have no flagella; they attach to rotifers, cladocerans and other zooplankton by a gelatinous stalk or pad secreted at the anterior end, so that the eyespot and reservoir lie at the lower end. When the cell divides, each daughter cell secretes its own gelatinous stalk and does not leave the parent but remains on the parent's stalk, forming a tree-like colony. Cells can leave the colony and develop into uniflagellate motile cells, which swim for a short time, lose the flagellum and attach to a new animal with a gelatinous stalk. *Colacium* shows the euglenid trend from free-swimming to attached and from unicellular to colonial.
- *Peranema* (about 15 species in China): a colorless phagotrophic euglenid with a variable shape. There are two flagella, a thick, long, forward-directed locomotory one and a shorter one that adheres to the cell surface and trails behind; near the reservoir lies a rod organ made of microtubules, used to capture and ingest solid food.

### 9.4.4. The Position of Euglenids

The older view took the flagellates as the common ancestors of animals and plants because euglenids combine animal and plant features, and, because their pigments are the same as those of green algae, considered euglenids closely related to the green algae. Molecular phylogenies show that euglenids belong to the Euglenozoa within the Discoba and are close relatives of the kinetoplastids, such as the trypanosomes, and the diplonemids; they share subpellicular microtubules, paraxonemal rods in the flagella and discoid mitochondrial cristae. The photosynthetic euglenids form a monophyletic group nested within the phagotrophic euglenids: their ancestor engulfed a green alga (related to the Pyramimonadales), which was reduced to a plastid with three membranes. The resemblance between euglenids and green algae therefore lies only in the plastid; the host cell has nothing to do with the green algae, and no alga evolved from euglenids.

### 9.4.5. Chlorarachniophytes

The chlorarachniophytes (Chlorarachniophyta) are a small group of marine amoeboid flagellates belonging to the Cercozoa within the Rhizaria. Their vegetative cells are amoeboid and joined to one another by slender filose pseudopodia into a network, with which they prey on bacteria; there is also a flagellated motile stage (with a single flagellum). The plastid comes from a green alga and contains chlorophylls *a* and *b*; it has four envelope membranes and retains the green algal nucleomorph between the second and third membranes (the nucleomorph genome of *Bigelowiella natans* has about 370 000 base pairs). Together with the cryptophytes, the chlorarachniophytes are "living fossils" of secondary endosymbiosis: in one cell, the host's nucleus and mitochondria coexist with the residual nucleus and plastid of the engulfed alga ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.2.3)).

## 9.5. Evolutionary Trends in the Algae

The algae have evolved from unicellular to multicellular, from simple to complex, and from "lower" to "higher" forms. Traditional botany summarizes these trends under four headings: cells, thalli, reproduction and life cycles. Modern phylogenies keep these trends in outline but change their interpretation: many of the "trends" did not occur one after another in a single lineage, but in parallel in several independent lineages.

### 9.5.1. Evolution of the Cell

The older interpretation, using the structure of photosynthetic pigments and the photosynthetic apparatus as its guide, recognized three evolutionary lineages of algae, all containing chlorophyll *a* and photosystem II (which is what fundamentally distinguishes them from photosynthetic bacteria):

1. The chlorophyll *a* and phycobilin lineage: phycobilins are the main light-harvesting pigments of photosystem II, and phycobilisomes are attached to the thylakoid surface. It runs from the prokaryotic cyanobacteria to the eukaryotic red algae. Cyanobacteria have single thylakoids scattered in the cytoplasm; red algae have differentiated chromatophores, but their thylakoids are still single. Neither has flagella.
2. The chlorophyll *a* and *c* lineage: chlorophyll *c* is the main light-harvesting pigment, and the primitive types also contain phycobilins. It includes the cryptophytes, dinoflagellates, golden algae, yellow-green algae, diatoms and brown algae. Of these, cryptophytes (thylakoids in stacks of two, with phycobilins) and dinoflagellates (with a mesokaryon, or with both mesokaryon and eukaryotic nucleus; a blue dinoflagellate containing phycobilins was found in a freshwater lake by Hu Hongjun of the Chinese Academy of Sciences) are considered more primitive, and the brown algae the most advanced.
3. The chlorophyll *a* and *b* lineage: chlorophyll *b* is the main light-harvesting pigment. It includes the prochlorophytes, euglenids, green algae and charophytes. The prochlorophytes are the prokaryotic starting point; the thylakoids of green algae and charophytes are in stacks of 2–6, the mitosis of charophytes resembles that of land plants, and the green algae occupy the main trunk of the evolution of the plant kingdom.

On this interpretation the transition from prokaryotic to eukaryotic algae occurred independently in the three lineages, at different times: the earliest eukaryotic algae were the red algae, followed by the algae containing chlorophylls *a* and *c*, and finally those containing chlorophylls *a* and *b*.

The endosymbiotic tree rearranges these facts (see the figure of plastid evolution in [7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.2.6)). The eukaryotic cell arose only once, and plastids entered eukaryotes through a single primary endosymbiosis (the Archaeplastida). The three "lineages" are actually the three branches of the Archaeplastida (glaucophytes, red algae, green plants) and the other groups that obtained plastids from red and green algae through secondary endosymbiosis.

- The chlorophyll *a* and phycobilin lineage corresponds to the glaucophytes and red algae of the Archaeplastida, which retain phycobilisomes; cyanobacteria are their common plastid source, not the host ancestor of the red algae.
- The chlorophyll *a* and *c* lineage is the range of distribution of secondary plastids of red algal origin. Its groups (cryptophytes, haptophytes, heterokonts, dinoflagellates) belong to three supergroups, and the host relationships among them are still unresolved. The phycobiliproteins and the nucleomorph of cryptophytes are direct evidence of this origin and do not mean that they are "primitive".
- In the chlorophyll *a* and *b* lineage, only the green algae and charophytes (and the land plants) form a branch of the Archaeplastida; the plastids of euglenids and chlorarachniophytes come from two independent secondary endosymbioses, and the prochlorophytes are cyanobacteria that independently acquired chlorophyll *b* several times.

The number of plastid membranes (2, 3 or 4) and the presence or absence of a nucleomorph are precisely a record of the number of endosymbioses and the degree of reduction. The change of thylakoids from single to stacks of two or three and up to grana likewise does not form a single evolutionary series but reflects specialization within each plastid lineage. On the other hand, cell differentiation is a real parallel trend: in unicellular and some colonial algae (*Chlamydomonas*) one cell serves both vegetative and reproductive functions; in multicellular *Volvox*, charophytes and most red and brown algae, vegetative and reproductive cells are clearly differentiated; and the structurally complex red and brown algae (kelp) also differentiate tissues such as epidermis, cortex and medulla.

### 9.5.2. Evolution of the Thallus

The evolution of thallus structure likewise follows a course from unicellular to colonial and multicellular, from simple to complex, and from free-swimming to nonmotile and attached. The simplest and most primitive type is the unicellular organism that has flagella and swims freely during the vegetative phase; it occurs in euglenids, green algae, dinoflagellates and golden algae. From it evolution proceeded in several directions:

1. The flagella are retained, giving rise to motile colonies and multicellular bodies, such as the series from *Chlamydomonas* to *Volvox* in the green algae ([7.12.13](/7.12.13));
2. The flagella are lost and the cells do not divide during the vegetative phase (the chlorococcalean algae); those whose nuclei can divide to form coenocytes develop into tubular coenocytes (*Vaucheria*, *Codium*);
3. The flagella are lost and the cells keep dividing during the vegetative phase, forming unbranched filaments, branched filaments and sheets (*Ulothrix*, *Cladophora*, *Ulva*), most of them attached; they further differentiate into heterotrichous thalli with prostrate and erect branches, or into branched bodies resembling root, stem and leaf, and the interior of the thallus also advances from undifferentiated to having various tissues (kelp).

Rhizopodial, flagellate, palmelloid, coccoid and filamentous thalli occur in the green algae, golden algae, yellow-green algae and dinoflagellates alike, and "stem and leaf"-like tissue bodies occur in the green, red and brown algae; this is parallel evolution. The green, red and brown algae reached a relatively high level in thallus structure, reproduction and life-cycle type, and are traditionally called the higher algae, the other phyla being the lower algae. The modern view agrees with this picture of parallel evolution and stresses that complex multicellularity arose independently at least several times among eukaryotes (brown algae, red algae, green plants, animals, fungi), three times among the algae ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5)).

### 9.5.3. Evolution of Reproduction and Life Cycles

Algae have evolved ways of perpetuating themselves along the course from vegetative reproduction through asexual reproduction to sexual reproduction. Only vegetative reproduction occurs in some cyanobacteria and some unicellular algae; some cyanobacteria reproduce asexually by endospores and exospores. These life cycles contain no sexual reproduction and therefore no meiosis or change of nuclear phase. Most eukaryotic algae reproduce sexually, and sexual reproduction evolved from isogamy through anisogamy to oogamy; oogamy arose independently in the green, brown, yellow-green and red algae and the diatoms.

The appearance of sexual reproduction introduced meiosis and the alternation of nuclear phases into the life cycle. According to the timing of meiosis, there are three basic life cycle types: zygotic meiosis, gametic meiosis and sporic meiosis ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.4.3)).

- Zygotic meiosis (only haploid plants, the zygote being the sole diploid stage): *Chlamydomonas*, *Volvox*, *Ulothrix*, *Spirogyra*, charophytes, *Vaucheria*, dinoflagellates.
- Gametic meiosis (only diploid plants, the gametes being the sole haploid stage): *Codium*, diatoms, *Silvetia*, *Fucus*, *Sargassum*.
- Sporic meiosis and alternation of generations (two or three kinds of plants): isomorphic alternation (*Ulva*, *Ectocarpus*, *Dictyota*, *Polysiphonia*, *Gelidium*) is considered more primitive, and from it heteromorphic alternation evolved; in heteromorphic alternation the sporophyte dominates in one group (*Saccharina*) and the gametophyte in another (*Scytosiphon*, *Monostroma*, *Pyropia*). The sporophyte-dominant forms are generally regarded as more advanced and as the main direction of evolution: among land plants, ferns, gymnosperms and angiosperms are all sporophyte-dominant, the bryophytes being the exception.

The older view arranged these three types in a series "from zygotic meiosis through alternation of generations to sporophyte dominance". Comparative phylogenies show that they arose several times independently among the algae, and several often coexist within a single class or even order (brown algae), so no one of them can be called primitive. The trend toward diploid dominance deserves attention: a diploid can mask harmful recessive mutations, and alternation of generations can multiply the product of one fertilization into large numbers of spores. The ancestors of the embryophytes (the charophytes) were haploid-dominant with zygotic meiosis, and the sporophyte of land plants arose by the "intercalation" of a diploid mitotic stage into a zygotic-meiosis life cycle (the intercalation theory, [7.12.14](/7.12.14)), not by direct descent from a green alga with isomorphic alternation, such as *Ulva*.

## 9.6. Ecology and Economic Importance of Algae

### 9.6.1. Primary Production and Ecological Roles

The oceans account for about half of the net primary production of the Earth, the great majority of it by phytoplankton, namely microscopic cyanobacteria, diatoms, dinoflagellates, haptophytes and small green algae. In fresh water, phytoplankton and attached algae are likewise the base of the food chain. The standing biomass of phytoplankton is less than 1 % of that of land plants, but it turns over within a few days ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.10.1), [7.11.29](/7.11.29)). Algae are the main food of aquatic animals: the yield of the oil sardine on the Indian coast is directly related to the occurrence of the diatom *Fragilaria oceanica*, and fertilizing ponds to promote algal growth increases fish yield. Chemical analysis shows that the protein, fat and ash of planktonic algae are comparable to those of the best forage grasses, and that they also contain vitamins essential to animal growth. Large seaweeds along the coast are both food for fishes and the places where they spawn and rear their young; together with sea grasses and coral reefs, the kelp and giant kelp forests are the most productive and biodiverse ecosystems of the ocean.

The relationship between algae and coral reefs is particularly close. The zooxanthellae of reef-building corals provide most of the energy corals need (§9.1.4), crustose coralline algae cement coral debris into solid reef frameworks ([7.12.7](/7.12.7)), and the overgrowth of algal communities (algal turf) on reefs is in turn a sign of reef degradation.

Algae are also pioneer organisms on new substrates. On bare ground after volcanic eruptions and floods, cyanobacteria and the algae of lichens are the first to settle and fix carbon and nitrogen; in arid regions, biological soil crusts of cyanobacteria, green algae, lichens and mosses on the soil surface reduce wind and water erosion. In Earth history, the oxygenic photosynthesis of cyanobacteria created the oxygen-containing atmosphere, and the charophytes among the green algae gave rise to the land plants ([7.12.14](/7.12.14)).

### 9.6.2. Algal Blooms and Red Tides

**Definition 9.6.1 (Algal Bloom and Red Tide)** The massive multiplication of planktonic algae in a body of water within a short time, markedly changing the color of the water or forming a layer of algae on its surface, is called an **algal bloom**; a bloom in the sea that often turns the water red or brown is called a **red tide**.

Blooms are natural phenomena: the spring diatom blooms and summer cyanobacterial blooms of temperate lakes and the diatom and dinoflagellate blooms of marine upwelling regions all result from the combined action of nutrients, light and temperature. **Eutrophication** (the heavy input of nitrogen and phosphorus) caused by human activities has greatly increased the frequency, intensity and duration of blooms ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.3.3)). Blooms are harmful in three ways:

1. Oxygen depletion: after the massive death of algae, their decomposition by bacteria consumes dissolved oxygen and suffocates fish, shrimp and shellfish; at night the respiration of the algae also makes dissolved oxygen fall sharply.
2. Toxins: freshwater cyanobacterial blooms (*Microcystis*, *Dolichospermum*, *Aphanizomenon*, *Planktothrix*) produce microcystins (liver-damaging cyclic heptapeptides that inhibit protein phosphatases), anatoxin-a (a neurotoxin) and cylindrospermopsin, endangering drinking water safety and the health of livestock and wildlife; in 2007 a cyanobacterial bloom in Lake Taihu cut off the tap water supply of the city of Wuxi. In the sea, toxins produced by poisonous dinoflagellates, diatoms and haptophytes accumulate in filter-feeding shellfish and cause, when eaten by people, paralytic (saxitoxin, *Alexandrium*), diarrhetic (okadaic acid, *Dinophysis* and *Prorocentrum*), neurotoxic (brevetoxin, *Karenia*) and amnesic (domoic acid, *Pseudo-nitzschia*) shellfish poisoning, and ciguatera fish poisoning (*Gambierdiscus*).
3. Mechanical and sensory damage: masses of algae clog fish gills, *Oscillatoria* and *Phormidium* give fish flesh an earthy taste, and blooms spoil the scenery and tourism of lakes.

Only a few dozen phytoplankton species are toxic, yet the frequency of harmful algal blooms is still rising worldwide, related both to declining water quality and to climate warming. The fundamental remedy is to control nutrient input (sewage interception, ecological restoration) rather than to kill the algae after the fact.

The dynamics of blooms can be illustrated with a simple nutrient–algae–toxin model (for the methods of ordinary differential equations see [2.4](/2.4)). Let $N$ be the concentration of available nutrient in the lake water, $A$ the algal biomass and $T$ the concentration of dissolved algal toxin:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} &= D\,(N_{\mathrm{in}}(t) - N) - \frac{1}{Y}\,\mu(N, t)\,A,\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \mu(N, t)\,A - (m + D)\,A,\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} &= q\,A - (k + D)\,T,
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\mu(N, t) = \mu_{\max}\,f(t)\,\frac{N}{K_N + N}.
\tag{9.1}
$$

Here $D$ is the flushing (water exchange) rate of the water body, $N_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the nutrient concentration of the inflow (eutrophication means a rise in $N_{\mathrm{in}}$, and storm runoff raises it in pulses), $Y$ is the yield of algal biomass per unit of nutrient, $\mu_{\max}$ is the maximum specific growth rate, $K_N$ is the half-saturation constant for the nutrient (Monod kinetics), $f(t)$ is a modulating factor between 0 and 1 for the seasonal changes of temperature and light, $m$ is the rate of algal death and grazing, $q$ is the toxin production rate per unit of biomass and $k$ is the toxin degradation rate. The model shows that when the nutrient input is low, the algae form only a small peak in summer; when the input rises, the summer algal biomass increases manyfold, and the toxin concentration lags behind the algal peak and remains high as the bloom declines (toxins are released when algal cells die and burst, represented in the model by a very small degradation rate).

In B–D the dashed lines are an oligotrophic lake ($N_{\mathrm{in}} = 0.05\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$) and the solid lines a eutrophic lake ($N_{\mathrm{in}} = 0.5\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$, with a pulse of nutrients from storm runoff on day 180). ### 9.6.3. Economic Importance

Food. Algae are of high nutritional value, containing large amounts of sugars, protein, minerals, many vitamins and organic iodine, as well as trace elements such as boron, cobalt, copper, manganese and zinc.

- Cyanobacteria: *Nostoc sphaeroides* (ge xian mi), *N. commune*, *Brachytrichia quoyi*; the protein of *Arthrospira* (spirulina) can reach 50 %–70 % of dry weight, and it is rich in B vitamins, unsaturated fatty acids and β-carotene. The people living along Lake Chad in Africa have collected and dried it for food since ancient times, and China has several large spirulina factories. Collecting hair-like *Nostoc flagelliforme* (fa cai) is now prohibited ([7.12.6](/7.12.6)).
- Green algae: *Ulva* (sea lettuce), *Enteromorpha* (*Ulva* in the broad sense), *Monostroma*, *Prasiola*, *Cladophora*, *Spirogyra*; the unicellular *Chlorella* and *Scenedesmus* contain about 50 % protein, 20 % fat and 20 % carbohydrate and are rich in amino acids and vitamins, and are cultured on a large scale as nutritional supplements and feed. The diatom *Phaeodactylum tricornutum* can be cultured to extract polyunsaturated fatty acids as food supplements.
- Red algae: nori (*Pyropia* and *Porphyra*; dried nori contains 25 %–35 % crude protein and about 50 % sugars, and about half as much vitamin C as citrus fruit), *Gloiopeltis*, *Gelidium*, *Gracilaria*, *Eucheuma*, *Meristotheca*, *Nemalion*; the protein content of *Eucheuma* and *Gracilaria* can reach 20 %–40 %.
- Brown algae: kelp (*Saccharina*), wakame (*Undaria*), hijiki (*Sargassum fusiforme*), *Silvetia*, *Endarachne*.

China is the largest seaweed-farming country in the world. The world production of cultured aquatic algae is about 35 million tonnes (wet weight; FAO data for 2020), over half of it from China, mainly kelp, *Gracilaria* (*Gracilaria lemaneiformis*), wakame and nori. The cultivation of kelp and nori is built on research into their life cycles ([7.12.7](/7.12.7), [7.12.8](/7.12.8)).

Fisheries and agriculture. Planktonic algae are food for fish, shrimp and shellfish: the silver carp feeds directly on planktonic algae, and the bighead carp eats both planktonic algae and the zooplankton that feed on them, so raising fish in ponds requires promoting algal growth; unicellular algae (*Chlorella*, *Isochrysis*, diatoms, cryptophytes) are essential food in aquaculture hatcheries. Algae can be used as fertilizer: organic mud of lakes and ponds, coastal brown algae (rich in potassium) and the charophytes of lake districts (rich in calcium carbonate). Nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria are a biological nitrogen fertilizer for rice fields; species with heterocysts can often fix nitrogen, about 120 nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria are known, the nitrogen fixed in rice fields can reach 1.7–6.7 kg of pure nitrogen per mu (1 mu is about 667 m²), and inoculating paddies with nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria has increased rice yield by an average of 15 %–30 %. Nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria also secrete substances that promote crop growth. The *Azolla*–*Anabaena* symbiosis is a traditional green manure ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.2.5)). Farmers observe whether algae in the paddies thrive in autumn and winter to estimate the next year's rice harvest. On the other hand, blooms and red tides do great damage to fisheries, attached chlorococcalean algae and *Melosira* can make fish and shellfish sick and kill them, and masses of filamentous algae in fish ponds can entangle fry.

Energy. Algae grow fast, and their biomass yield per unit area can reach 5–10 times that of land crops, while culture does not require arable land, so they are regarded as a source of biofuels. Diatoms and some green algae (*Botryococcus braunii*, a large part of whose dry weight is long-chain hydrocarbons) accumulate large amounts of oil, which can be extracted and converted into biodiesel; the cellulose- and polysaccharide-rich biomass of brown and green algae can be fermented into ethanol or digested anaerobically to produce methane. The cost of algal biofuels is still far above that of fossil fuels, and contamination in culture and the energy consumed in harvesting and extraction are the main obstacles. In geological history, ancient algae were the source of part of the petroleum and of "boghead coal": dinocyst fossils are the main fossils of many oil-source strata and are often used as a guide in petroleum exploration, and the fossils of large numbers of *Pediastrum* in a Chinese oilfield provided evidence for the theory of continental oil generation.

Indication, monitoring and wastewater treatment. Algae respond very sensitively to the environment: the composition and abundance of algal communities in a water body are determined by its organic matter and nutrients, so algae are widely used to assess, monitor and forecast water quality. According to the degree of organic pollution, water bodies can be divided into polysaprobic, mesosaprobic and oligosaprobic zones, each inhabited by different algae: *Oscillatoria chlorina*, *Euglena viridis* and *Anisonema ovale* are indicator organisms of the polysaprobic zone, and diatoms such as *Tabellaria fenestrata* indicate the oligosaprobic zone (the saprobic system); the species assemblage of diatoms and the dominance of cyanobacteria are commonly used indices. Algae release oxygen in photosynthesis and promote the bacterial decomposition of organic matter in wastewater, and the $\ce{CO2}$ from bacterial decomposition is in turn used by the algae; this is the principle of oxidation ponds and algal–bacterial systems for treating sewage. Some algae absorb and accumulate harmful elements (*Scenedesmus quadricauda* can accumulate cerium and yttrium to 20 000 times the level of the environment), and are used for bioremediation of heavy metals. An algal turf scrubber uses attached algae to take up nutrients from eutrophic water, and the harvested algae can also be used as fuel feedstock.

Carbon cycle and climate. Marine phytoplankton absorb a large part of the $\ce{CO2}$ emitted by humans: photosynthesis fixes carbon as organic matter, part of which sinks into the deep sea with dead algae and fecal pellets (the biological pump), and the calcification of coccolithophores and coralline algae buries carbon in sediments as calcium carbonate (§9.3). To what extent these processes can offset anthropogenic emissions is still debated in climate research. Seaweed farming is also discussed as one route to "blue carbon".

Medicine and other uses. Iodine extracted from brown algae is used to treat and prevent goiter; calcium alginate fiber is used as a hemostatic dressing; the red alga *Digenea simplex* contains kainic acid, an anthelmintic against roundworms, and *Caloglossa* is also anthelmintic; agar has a laxative effect; natural products of algae (such as the antitumor peptides of cyanobacteria) are objects of drug research. Fossil algae serve as indicators in prospecting for minerals.

## Summary

- Dinoflagellates (§9.1): thecal vesicles and plates, cingulum and sulcus, transverse and longitudinal flagella; the dinokaryon; peridinin plastids (three membranes), often replaced; starch and oil; haploid, with zygotic meiosis and resting cysts; *Prorocentrum*, *Peridinium*, *Ceratium*, *Noctiluca*, *Dinoclonium*, *Dinothrix*; symbiotic dinoflagellates and red tides.
- Cryptophytes (Definition 9.2.1): periplast, longitudinal groove and gullet, ejectosomes; a nucleomorph retained in the four-membrane plastid of red algal origin; phycobiliproteins in the thylakoid lumen.
- Haptophytes (Definition 9.3.1): haptonema, organic or calcareous scales; calcification and chalk in coccolithophores, the carbonate pump and the organic carbon pump.
- Euglenids (Definition 9.4.1): proteinaceous pellicle, reservoir, contractile vacuoles, eyespot and paraflagellar body, paramylon, three-membrane plastid of green algal origin, Euglenozoa; the green algal plastid of chlorarachniophytes retains a nucleomorph.
- Evolutionary trends: cell differentiation, thallus complexity, sexual reproduction from isogamy to oogamy and the trend toward diploid and sporophyte dominance all arose in parallel in several independent lineages; the "three pigment lineages" are plastid lineages.
- Algal blooms and red tides (Definition 9.6.1) are intensified by eutrophication, and their harm lies in oxygen depletion, toxins and sensory damage.

## Questions

1. How can cryptophytes, dinoflagellates and euglenids, three kinds of unicellular flagellates, be distinguished under the microscope?

   **Answer** Dinoflagellates have a transverse groove around the cell and a ventral longitudinal groove, the two flagella emerging at the intersection of the grooves, one lying in the transverse groove and beating in a wave, and many species have a theca of plates. Cryptophytes have a dorsiventrally flattened cell with a longitudinal groove or gullet on the anterior ventral side, two slightly unequal flagella emerging from the anterior ventral side, and often yellow-brown, red or blue-green plastids; they have no transverse groove. Euglenids are green (or colorless), with a reservoir and a red eyespot at the anterior end, only one flagellum emerging from the cell, a flexible pellicle that allows contraction and deformation in some species, and paramylon grains of definite shape in the cytoplasm that are not stained blue by iodine.

2. The plastids of dinoflagellates have three membranes, those of cryptophytes four membranes and a nucleomorph, and those of euglenids also three membranes. What is the origin of each, and what does the difference between three and four membranes show?

   **Answer** The plastids of dinoflagellates and cryptophytes come from an engulfed red alga, and that of euglenids from an engulfed green alga; all are secondary plastids. The four membranes are, from the inside out, the two envelope membranes of the original plastid, the plasma membrane of the engulfed alga and the membrane of the host's food vacuole; the three-membrane plastids (dinoflagellates, euglenids) have lost the plasma membrane of the engulfed alga. Cryptophytes also retain the residual nucleus of the engulfed alga, the nucleomorph. The number of membranes and the presence or absence of a nucleomorph record the kind of endosymbiosis and the degree of reduction of the engulfed alga, not the relationships among the hosts.

3. The calcification of coccolithophores releases $\ce{CO2}$ in the short term; why are they nevertheless considered to help remove carbon from the atmosphere?

   **Answer** Calcification, $\ce{Ca^2+ + 2HCO3- -> CaCO3 + CO2 + H2O}$, releases $\ce{CO2}$ in surface water, but coccolithophores also photosynthesize and fix organic carbon. The dense coccoliths make dead cells, fecal pellets and organic aggregates sink faster (the ballast effect), which raises the efficiency with which the organic carbon pump exports carbon to the deep sea, and the calcium carbonate itself is buried in sediments for a long time (chalk). The net effect depends on the ratio of photosynthetic fixation to calcification.

4. Use the model of Eq. (9.1) to explain why controlling nutrient input is a more fundamental cure for blooms than killing the algae afterward.

   **Answer** In the model the upper limit of algal biomass is set by the nutrient supply: at steady state $\mu(N^*) = m + D$, and the corresponding nutrient concentration $N^*$ is very low; from $\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}t = 0$ the algal biomass is $A^* = \frac{Y D}{m + D}(N_{\mathrm{in}} - N^*)$, which increases linearly with the nutrient concentration of the input. Killing the algae lowers only $A$; the nutrient remains, and as $N$ recovers the algae regrow at a rate close to $\mu_{\max}$ and quickly restore the bloom; moreover, killing the algae bursts the cells and releases a large amount of toxin at once. Lowering $N_{\mathrm{in}}$ directly lowers the maximum biomass that can be reached.

5. What are the fundamental differences between the traditional "three evolutionary lineages" and the modern endosymbiotic tree in explaining the evolution of algal cells?

   **Answer** The traditional account held that the three lineages each evolved eukaryotic cells independently from a kind of prokaryotic alga (cyanobacteria → red algae, prokaryotic algae with chlorophyll *c* → the yellow algae, prochlorophytes → green algae), the pigments and the arrangement of the thylakoids marking evolutionary stages. The modern view holds that the eukaryotic cell and the primary plastid each arose only once (the Archaeplastida), and that pigments and plastid structure reflect plastid lineages: the plastids of red and green algae were passed to other hosts by secondary and tertiary endosymbiosis, so the hosts of groups in the same "lineage" (such as euglenids and green algae, or cryptophytes and brown algae) can be entirely unrelated; and the prochlorophytes are not the ancestors of the green algae. The trends in thallus, reproduction and life cycles are retained in this framework but understood as parallel evolution.
