In bryophytes the gametophyte is the main plant body and the sporophyte remains attached to it for life, so plant height is limited by the gametophyte's dependence on water ([7.12.15](/7.12.15)). Vascular plants took a different path: the sporophyte became an independent, repeatedly branching plant with lignified conducting tissue that can lift water from the soil to a height of tens of meters, while the gametophyte shrank until it was fed entirely by the sporophyte. This chapter describes how that path began. We first treat the general features of vascular plants (§16.1) and the stele, which describes how vascular tissue is arranged in the stem and is the anatomical character most often used to compare groups of vascular plants (§16.2). We then turn to the early vascular plants of the Silurian and Devonian (§16.3) and the theories of the origin of root, stem, and leaf based on them (§16.4). Finally we treat the earliest-diverging lineage of living vascular plants, the lycophytes (§16.5), and the arborescent lycophytes whose remains formed Carboniferous coal (§16.6). Ferns (including horsetails and whisk ferns) are treated in [7.12.17](/7.12.17) and the origin of the seed in [7.12.25](/7.12.25); the life cycles of all spore-bearing plants and their adaptation to land are summarized in [7.12.18](/7.12.18). The cells of vascular tissue (tracheids, vessel elements, sieve cells, and so on) are described in [7.12.5](/7.12.5), and the geological time scale and the colonization of land in general in [7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.5.2).

## 16.1. Characteristics of Vascular Plants

### 16.1.1. Dominance of the Sporophyte

The **vascular plants** ([7.12.1](/7.12.1)) comprise the lycophytes, ferns, and seed plants. What fundamentally distinguishes them from algae, fungi, lichens, and bryophytes is the vascular system: the **xylem** contains water-conducting **tracheids** or **vessel elements**, and the **phloem** contains **sieve cells** or sieve tubes that transport assimilates. Most vascular plants are terrestrial. Lycophytes and ferns still need water for fertilization, whereas seed plants deliver sperm to the egg by a pollen tube.

All vascular plants have a **heteromorphic alternation of generations** dominated by the sporophyte:

- The **sporophyte** is well developed, independent, and long-lived; it depends on the gametophyte (or, in seed plants, on the maternal sporophyte) for nutrition only at the embryo stage.
- The **gametophyte** is small and simple and short-lived; in lycophytes and ferns it is free-living, whereas in seed plants it lives entirely within the sporophyte.
- The sporophyte varies greatly in form and structure; the earliest vascular plants were merely dichotomously branched axes, and root, stem, and leaf differentiated only later.

Compared with bryophytes, the vascular plant sporophyte differs in three fundamental ways. It branches repeatedly, and the tip of each branch can produce a sporangium, so that one fertilization yields far more spores. It grows indeterminately from apical meristems, whereas the bryophyte sporophyte has a single sporangium and stops growing when mature. And it has lignified water-conducting elements and an epidermis with a **cuticle** and **stomata**, so that it can stand upright away from the ground. Lignin stiffens the walls of tracheids, enabling them both to withstand the negative pressures of water transport and to support the plant body, which is the precondition for vascular plants to grow tall and eventually become trees.

### 16.1.2. The Two Major Lineages

Living vascular plants fall into two clades (see the phylogenetic tree in [7.12.1](/7.12.1)).

| | Lycophytes: Lycopodiopsida | Euphyllophytes |
|---|---|---|
| Includes | Lycopodiales, Selaginellales, Isoëtales | Ferns (including horsetails and whisk ferns) and seed plants |
| Leaves | Microphylls, with a single unbranched vein, no leaf gap | Megaphylls (euphylls), with branching veins |
| Sporangia | Lateral, borne singly on the adaxial surface of a sporophyll or in a leaf axil, mostly reniform and transversely dehiscent | Primitively terminal on branch tips, later borne on leaves |
| Branching | Mainly dichotomous | Differentiated into main axis and lateral branches (pseudomonopodial branching) |
| Roots | Dichotomously branched at the tip | Lateral roots arise endogenously from the pericycle |
| Sperm | Biflagellate (multiflagellate in *Isoëtes*) | Multiflagellate (mostly without flagella in seed plants) |
| Plastid genome | None | A roughly 30 kb inversion (Raubeson and Jansen 1992) |

The two lineages diverged in the Early Devonian or earlier, more than 400 million years ago; leaves, roots, and **secondary growth** each originated independently in the two lineages. The traditional "Pteridophyta" combines lycophytes and ferns and is a **paraphyletic group** (compared with the seed plants, the lycophytes branched off earlier), and "fern allies" (club mosses, spike mosses, quillworts, whisk ferns, horsetails) is not a natural group either. This course follows PPG I (2016) in treating lycophytes (this chapter) and ferns ([7.12.17](/7.12.17)) separately.

The system of Ching (1978) divided the Pteridophyta into five subphyla: Lycophytina, Isoetophytina, Psilotophytina, Sphenophytina, and Filicophytina, and Chinese textbooks follow this system; its correspondence with PPG I is given in the summary of [7.12.17](/7.12.17). Bold, Alexopoulos, Delevoryas, and others also divided the vascular plants into many phyla (Psilophyta, Lycophyta, Sphenophyta, Pterophyta, Cycadophyta, Ginkgophyta, Coniferophyta, Gnetophyta, Anthophyta, and so on), on the grounds that several morphologically very different groups of vascular plants appeared simultaneously in the Devonian. Molecular phylogenies show that the vascular plants are a **monophyletic group**, which supports the recognition of a single phylum Tracheophyta; phylum-level divisions within it must be redefined on the basis of clades.

## 16.2. The Stele

### 16.2.1. The Stele Concept and Modes of Xylem Development

The arrangement of vascular tissue in the stem is one of the most important anatomical characters for comparing groups of vascular plants, and it is described by the stele.

**Definition 16.2.1 (Stele)** In the stem or root of a vascular plant, the central axis inside the cortex, consisting of primary xylem, primary phloem, and the parenchyma between them (and sometimes pith and **pericycle**), is the **stele**. According to the arrangement of primary xylem and primary phloem, steles are classified into protosteles, siphonosteles, dictyosteles, eusteles, atactosteles, and other types.

Outside the stele there is often an **endodermis**, the innermost layer of the cortex; many ferns have an endodermis on both the outer and the inner side of the stele. Stele types refer only to the primary structure; secondary growth makes the original form of the stele hard to recognize.

The first xylem to mature is the protoxylem and the later xylem is the metaxylem, and their relative positions are another important character.

**Definition 16.2.2 (Exarch, Mesarch, and Endarch)** Primary xylem that matures from the outside inward, with the protoxylem on the outside, is **exarch**; primary xylem that matures from the inside outward, with the protoxylem on the inside (toward the center of the stem), is **endarch**; and primary xylem with the protoxylem in the middle of the metaxylem, maturing from the middle toward both the inside and the outside, is mesarch.

Exarch xylem occurs in the stems of lycophytes and in the roots of all vascular plants; mesarch xylem occurs in many ferns (*Osmunda*, bracken) and in early euphyllophytes; endarch xylem occurs in horsetails, adder's-tongue ferns, and the stems of seed plants. The exarch condition is generally considered the most primitive and the endarch the most advanced; but roots are always exarch, which is related to the need for the protoxylem of the root to connect first with the root-hair zone, and so cannot simply be explained as "primitive".

### 16.2.2. The Protostele

**Definition 16.2.3 (Protostele)** A stele with a solid central core of xylem surrounded by phloem and without pith is a **protostele**. According to the shape of the xylem, protosteles are classified as follows. In a **haplostele** the xylem is circular in cross section; in an **actinostele** the xylem projects outward in radiating ridges; and in a **plectostele** the xylem is divided into many parallel or interwoven plates with phloem between them.

The protostele is the simplest and earliest stele. It occurs in the Devonian *Rhynia* and *Asteroxylon* (§16.3), the living whisk ferns (actinostele), *Lycopodium* (actinostele or plectostele), the rhizomes of *Selaginella* and *Lygodium*, the young stems of many ferns, and the roots of almost all vascular plants. The sporangia of early vascular plants with protosteles, such as *Horneophyton*, contain a central axis resembling the columella of **hornworts**, which was once cited as evidence of their primitiveness.

### 16.2.3. The Siphonostele and Leaf Gaps

**Definition 16.2.4 (Siphonostele)** A stele with a central pith, in which the xylem forms a cylinder around the pith, is a **siphonostele**. An **ectophloic siphonostele** has phloem only outside the xylem, as in *Osmunda*; an **amphiphloic siphonostele** has phloem on both the outer and the inner side of the xylem, as in *Dennstaedtia* and *Marsilea*, and is often called a **solenostele**; it commonly has a layer of endodermis and of pericycle on each side.

The part of the vascular bundle of a leaf that departs from the stele and enters the leaf is the **leaf trace**. In the siphonostele of ferns, the place where a leaf trace leaves the stele is marked by a gap filled with parenchyma:

**Definition 16.2.5 (Leaf Gap and Branch Gap)** In a siphonostele, a gap filled with parenchyma above the point where a leaf trace departs is a **leaf gap**; a similar gap left where the vascular tissue of a branch departs is a **branch gap**.

Leaf gaps are associated with megaphylls: in microphylls the leaf trace leaves the stele without leaving a gap (§16.4). The presence or absence of leaf gaps was therefore once used to distinguish megaphylls from microphylls and the "lycophyte" from the "fern" lineage (Jeffrey 1902). The siphonostele of *Selaginella* and its relatives has no leaf gaps, whereas that of ferns has. In the eustele of seed plants, the parenchymatous region where a leaf trace departs looks like a leaf gap but is not homologous to the leaf gap of ferns in origin; Raven et al. call it a leaf trace gap.

### 16.2.4. The Dictyostele and the Polycyclic Stele

When the internodes of the stem are very short and the leaves very densely arranged, the leaf gaps of the siphonostele overlap, and the stele is divided into a longitudinal network.

**Definition 16.2.6 (Dictyostele and Polycyclic Stele)** A siphonostele that is dissected by crowded, overlapping leaf gaps into many bundles arranged in a ring in cross section is a **dictyostele**. Each bundle (a meristele) has xylem in the center, phloem around it, and an endodermis outermost, and corresponds to a miniature protostele. A stele in which the vascular bundles are arranged in two or more rings is a **polycyclic stele**, as in the two-ringed dictyostele of the rhizome of *Pteridium*.

The dictyostele is the most common stele of leptosporangiate ferns (Polypodiales and others); polycyclic steles occur in the thick rhizomes or stems of *Pteridium*, tree ferns, and others. The stem of *Selaginella* often contains two to many protosteles, which is another kind of dissection (polystely).

### 16.2.5. The Eustele, Atactostele, and Arthrostele

**Definition 16.2.7 (Eustele and Atactostele)** A stele in which discrete vascular bundles are arranged in a ring around a pith, with parenchyma (pith rays) between the bundles, and in which each bundle has phloem outside and xylem inside, is a **eustele**; it occurs in the stems of progymnosperms, gymnosperms, and most eudicots. A stele in which the vascular bundles are scattered in the ground tissue, with no clear boundary between pith and cortex, is an **atactostele**; it occurs in the stems of most monocots.

The stem of *Equisetum* has a ring of vascular bundles facing the longitudinal ridges of the stem surface; in each bundle the protoxylem breaks down into a carinal canal, and the center of the stem is a hollow pith cavity. This is called an arthrostele (jointed stele): the stele is a continuous tube at the node and a ring of discrete bundles in the internode. Its xylem is endarch, and many English textbooks regard it as a kind of eustele ([7.12.17](/7.12.17)).

### 16.2.6. Evolution of the Stele and Stelar Theory

Stelar theory was proposed by Van Tieghem and Douliot (1886): stem and root both consist of epidermis, cortex, and stele; the stele is an independent morphological unit, and its type reflects phylogenetic relationships. With additions by Jeffrey, Brebner, and others, an evolutionary sequence was established:

- The protostele is the primitive type, found in the earliest vascular plants and in young stems and roots of all groups;
- When the central xylem of a protostele is replaced by parenchyma, a siphonostele results (medullation); evidence for this is the tracheids left in the pith of some siphonosteles;
- As leaf gaps in the siphonostele increase and overlap, they give rise to the dictyostele and the polycyclic stele;
- With still more leaf gaps and shorter internodes, the eustele of seed plants arises, and from the eustele the atactostele of monocots.

![The main types of stele (schematic cross sections of stems; xylem blue, phloem orange, parenchyma and pith light, endodermis a thin dark line). Top row: three kinds of protostele (haplostele, actinostele, plectostele), the ectophloic siphonostele and the amphiphloic siphonostele (solenostele). Bottom row: dictyostele (each meristele surrounded by an endodermis), a two-ringed polycyclic stele, the arthrostele of *Equisetum* (bundles facing the ridges, carinal canals on the inner side, central pith cavity), eustele, and atactostele. The arrows show the evolutionary sequence of stelar theory; the dashed arrow shows the conclusion of modern comparative anatomy: the eustele evolved from the protostele directly, through the progymnosperms, without passing through the siphonostele of ferns.](./plots/steles.py)

Ontogeny often "recapitulates" this sequence: in many ferns the lower part of a young stem is a protostele, which changes upward into a siphonostele and a dictyostele (recapitulation is discussed in [7.12.18](/7.12.18)). Stelar theory provided a common language for comparing vascular plants, but it has three limitations:

1. The eustele does not come from the siphonostele. Fossils of progymnosperms and early seed plants show that the eustele formed directly from a protostele (actinostele), by differentiation of pith in the center and splitting of the surrounding xylem into bundles; the siphonostele and dictyostele of ferns are a separate, independent evolutionary line. The leaf trace gaps of seed plants are therefore not homologous to the leaf gaps of ferns, and the single-line sequence "siphonostele → dictyostele → eustele" does not hold.
2. Stele types are highly homoplastic. Siphonosteles and dictyosteles each appeared independently in lycophytes (*Selaginella*), ferns, and seed plants, and the stele of *Equisetum* resembles the eustele of seed plants but is unrelated to it. Inferring relationships from stele type alone often mistakes convergence for homology (**homoplasy**).
3. The form of the stele is controlled by developmental conditions. The thickness of the stem and the size and density of the leaves directly determine the arrangement of leaf traces and leaf gaps: in the same plant a thin stem has a protostele and a thick stem a dictyostele. Wetmore and Rier (1963) grafted buds onto callus or applied auxin and sucrose, and induced nodules of vascular tissue: at a sucrose concentration of about 1.5–2.0% only xylem differentiated; at 2.5–3.5% nodules with xylem in the center and phloem around it formed, with a cambium sometimes appearing between them; and at higher concentrations phloem predominated. Hormonal regulation of vascular differentiation belongs to plant physiology.

## 16.3. Early Vascular Plants

### 16.3.1. The Fossil Record

The earliest fossils of land plants are the cryptospores of the Middle Ordovician (about 470 million years ago), which may come from plants at the liverwort grade ([7.12.14](/7.12.14), [7.12.15](/7.12.15)). The earliest plants with a branched sporophyte appear in the middle Silurian, namely *Cooksonia*; plants diversified rapidly in the Early Devonian (about 419–393 million years ago); by the Middle Devonian roots, leaves, secondary xylem, and trees had appeared, and by the Late Devonian forests and seeds.

Chinese textbooks group these early vascular plants as psilophytes, placed under the "Psilophytina" subphylum, with *Rhynia*, *Psilophyton*, and *Asteroxylon* as representative genera. The name goes back to *Psilophyton*, described by Dawson (1859). It is now clear that the psilophytes are a heterogeneous assemblage united by the character "simple"; Banks (1968) divided them into three groups, each related to a different clade of living plants:

| Group | Representatives | Sporangia | Branching | Xylem | Phylogenetic position |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rhyniophytes (Rhyniophyta) | *Rhynia*, *Cooksonia* | Terminal on branch tips, dehiscent by a longitudinal slit or not | Equal dichotomous | Small protostele, mesarch | Stem group of vascular plants; some genera may not be vascular plants |
| Zosterophylls (Zosterophyllophyta) | *Zosterophyllum*, *Sawdonia* | Lateral, reniform, dehiscent transversely along the outer margin | Dichotomous and K- or H-shaped branching, often spiny | Exarch, often elliptical or band-shaped in cross section | Stem group of lycophytes |
| Trimerophytes (Trimerophytophyta) | *Psilophyton*, *Pertica* | Paired, spindle-shaped, terminal on the tips of lateral branches, longitudinally dehiscent | Main axis and lateral branches differentiated (pseudomonopodial) | Mesarch protostele | Stem group of euphyllophytes (ferns and seed plants) |

The living whisk ferns were once regarded as relics of the psilophytes, but they have no fossil record, and molecular evidence shows that they are close relatives of the adder's-tongue ferns, their simple form being the result of secondary reduction ([7.12.17](/7.12.17)).

### 16.3.2. The Rhynie Chert and the Rhyniophytes

The Rhynie chert lies near the village of Rhynie in Aberdeenshire, Scotland. It was discovered by W. Mackie in 1912 and described by Kidston and Lang in 1917–1921. It is a siliceous sediment of an Early Devonian hot-spring area (about 407 million years ago): silica-rich hot-spring water flooded a wetland plant community and rapidly petrified whole plants together with fungi, lichens, algae, and arthropods, preserving details of cells and even of gametophytes and reproductive organs. The Rhynie chert remains the most complete window on early terrestrial ecosystems.

- *Rhynia gwynne-vaughanii*: about 20 cm tall, with a prostrate rhizome and erect aerial stems, with neither leaves nor roots, and unicellular **rhizoids** on the rhizome. The aerial stems branch in equal dichotomies and often bear adventitious branches and hemispherical projections. The epidermis has a **cuticle** and **stomata**; the cortex is wide, contains chloroplasts, and carries out photosynthesis; the center is a small protostele whose xylem consists of tracheids with helical thickenings (S-type tracheids) surrounded by elongated phloem cells. The sporangia are cylindrical, borne terminally on the branch tips, thick-walled, and contain spores of one kind.
- *Aglaophyton major*: formerly *Rhynia major*, about 50 cm tall. Edwards (1986) found that its water-conducting cells lack lignified secondary wall thickenings and resemble the hydroids of mosses, and moved it into a new genus. Its sporophyte is branched and independent but has no true tracheids, and it has been called a "protracheophyte", which suggests that a branched sporophyte may have preceded tracheids. Arbuscule-like fungal structures were found in its cortical cells (Remy et al. 1994), one of the earliest fossil records of **arbuscular mycorrhiza**.
- *Horneophyton lignieri*: the sporangia are forked and contain a sterile central axis like the columella of hornworts.
- The Rhynie chert also preserves the gametophytes of these plants (such as *Lyonophyton* and *Remyophyton*), which are erect axes with a cup-shaped tip bearing archegonia or antheridia, with vascular tissue and stomata, and much more complex than the gametophytes of living vascular plants. This suggests that the two generations of early land plants may have been rather similar, only later evolving separately into the gametophyte dominance of bryophytes and the sporophyte dominance of vascular plants.

*Cooksonia* was named by Lang (1937) for Isabel Cookson. It is the earliest fossil plant with a branched sporophyte, found from the middle Silurian (Wenlock, about 430 million years ago) to the Early Devonian in Britain, Ireland, the Czech Republic, and elsewhere. The plants are only a few centimeters tall, with naked axes branching in equal dichotomies and a trumpet-shaped or reniform sporangium at the tip of each branch. *C. pertoni* of the Early Devonian is confirmed to have tracheids and stomata; earlier specimens preserve only the outline and are not necessarily all vascular plants, so "*Cooksonia*" is often treated as a collective name for a group of early polysporangiophytes of similar form.

### 16.3.3. Zosterophylls and *Asteroxylon*

*Zosterophyllum*, named for its resemblance to the seagrass *Zostera*, is among the most widespread plants of the Early Devonian. The stem is leafless and dichotomously branched, often with H- or K-shaped branching at the base; the sporangia are reniform, short-stalked, and lateral, borne spirally or in two rows on the upper part of the stem, and at maturity dehisce along the outer margin into two valves; the xylem is exarch. Lateral, reniform, transversely dehiscent sporangia and exarch xylem are all lycophyte characters, so the zosterophylls are regarded as the stem group of the lycophytes. *Zosterophyllum yunnanicum* was found in the Early Devonian Xujiachong Formation at Qujing, Yunnan.

*Asteroxylon mackiei* is also from the Rhynie chert. It is about 50 cm tall; from a prostrate rhizome arise branched rhizomatous axes (which replace rhizoids), and the erect aerial stem has a main axis and dichotomous lateral branches, densely covered with spirally arranged scale-like projections a few millimeters long. These projections photosynthesize but have no internal vein: the leaf trace extends only to their base. The stem has a protostele whose xylem is star-shaped in cross section (an actinostele), which gives the genus its name; the cortex has an inner and an outer zone and contains leaf traces. The sporangia are reniform, short-stalked, and borne on the stem among the projections. Chinese textbooks place *Asteroxylon* among the psilophytes; on the basis of its lateral reniform sporangia it is now placed in the lycophyte stem group (Kerp et al. 2013). Its projections stand between "veinless enations" and "microphylls with one vein" (§16.4.3).

*Baragwanathia longifolia* (Lang and Cookson 1935) comes from the Late Silurian (Ludlow, about 420 million years ago; some place it in the Early Devonian) of Victoria, Australia. The stem is 1–2 cm thick and dichotomously branched, with an actinostele, and densely bears spirally arranged, slender microphylls up to 4 cm long, each with one vein; the sporangia are reniform and borne near the leaf axils rather than at branch tips. It is almost a complete lycophyte, earlier than the zosterophylls and *Asteroxylon*, which shows that lycophytes had already formed in the Silurian. Chinese textbooks regard it as a transitional type between psilophytes and typical lycophytes, but on its age it looks more like an early crown-group member of the lycophytes.

### 16.3.4. Trimerophytes and *Psilophyton*

*Psilophyton* is the first Devonian plant described, by Dawson (1859) from fossils of the Gaspé Peninsula, Canada; it belongs to the trimerophytes of the Early and Middle Devonian. The plant is up to 1 m tall with a distinct main axis; the lateral branches branch dichotomously, with tips often curved inward; in some species the stem bears spine-like projections (*P. princeps*); the sporangia are spindle-shaped, borne in pairs and clusters at the ends of lateral branches, pendent, and longitudinally dehiscent; the protostele is mesarch. Differentiation of main axis and lateral branches arises by overtopping (§16.4.1), and flattening of the weaker lateral branch systems gives the precursor of the megaphyll. The trimerophytes are therefore regarded as the group containing the common ancestor of ferns, horsetails, and seed plants (the euphyllophytes).

### 16.3.5. The Early Devonian Floras of Yunnan

Yunnan, China, preserves several important Early Devonian floras (Pragian, about 410 million years ago), represented by the Posongchong Formation at Wenshan and the Xujiachong Formation at Qujing, where Hao Shougang and colleagues have described dozens of early vascular plants.

- Several zosterophylls, including *Zosterophyllum yunnanicum* and other species of *Zosterophyllum*, and spiny zosterophylls;
- *Eophyllophyton bellum* (Hao and Beck 1993), from the Posongchong Formation, a small plant with fan-shaped flat leaves only a few millimeters long on the lateral branches, with dichotomous veins; it is one of the earliest known euphyllophytes with a leaf blade and shows that the rudiments of the megaphyll already existed in the Early Devonian;
- Some plants with mesarch xylem and differentiated lateral branches, related to the trimerophytes and early euphyllophytes.

These floras are of about the same age as the Rhynie chert and show that the lycophyte and euphyllophyte lines had already separated in the Early Devonian and diversified independently on the South China block.

## 16.4. The Origin of Organs

Before early vascular plants were discovered there was much speculation about the origin of root, stem, and leaf: some thought that the leaf appeared first and the stem and root later; others thought that all the organs of angiosperms appeared simultaneously. Devonian fossils show that the earliest vascular plants had only a single dichotomously branched, independent axis: the stem axis appeared first and carried out photosynthesis, leaves arose on the axis later, and roots appeared last.

### 16.4.1. The Telome Theory

**Definition 16.4.1 (Telome Theory)** The theory that the body of the primitive vascular plant consisted of dichotomously branched axes, that the terminal parts of the axes are **telomes**, which may or may not bear sporangia (fertile and sterile telomes), that the parts between telomes are **mesomes**, that a group of telomes forms a **syntelome**, and that the leaves, sporophylls, and other organs of vascular plants were derived from telomes by a few basic processes, is the **telome theory**.

The telome theory was proposed by the German botanist W. Zimmermann (1930) and has been revised many times. Its archetype resembles *Rhynia*: a dichotomously branched axis with rhizoids at the base and sporangia on the fertile tips. Zimmermann identified five basic processes:

| Process | Content | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Overtopping | The dichotomous branches become unequal; one grows faster and becomes the main axis, and the other is pushed to the side as a lateral branch | Differentiation of main axis and lateral branches (pseudomonopodial branching) |
| Planation | The telomes of a lateral branch system become arranged in one plane | A planar lateral branch system, the rudiment of a leaf |
| Syngenesis (fusion) | Parenchyma grows between the telomes in one plane and joins them into a sheet (webbing), and their vascular tissue also merges | A leaf blade with branched veins; fused steles |
| Reduction | The telome system shortens and simplifies until only one telome is left | Microphylls (in the telome-theory interpretation); the simple form of whisk ferns |
| Recurvation | The tips of the telomes bend inward and curl back, turning the sporangia toward the axis | The peltate sporangiophores of horsetails; the anatropous ovules of seed plants |

### 16.4.2. Megaphylls and Microphylls

**Definition 16.4.2 (Microphyll and Megaphyll)** A leaf that is usually small, has a single unbranched vein, leaves no leaf gap when its trace departs from the stele, and has no petiole is a **microphyll** (also called a lycophyll), as in the leaves of *Lycopodium*, *Selaginella*, and *Isoëtes*. A leaf that has a petiole, branched veins, and leaves a leaf gap in the siphonostele of ferns is a **megaphyll** (also called a euphyll), as in the leaves of ferns and seed plants.

"Micro" and "mega" refer to origin, not size: the leaves of *Isoëtes* are tens of centimeters long, and those of the Carboniferous *Lepidodendron* and *Sigillaria* were up to 1 m long, and they are still microphylls; the scale-like leaves of horsetails and the scale-like appendages of whisk ferns, though tiny, belong evolutionarily to the euphyllophytes, the former being reduced megaphylls and the nature of the latter still disputed. Chinese textbooks call the leaves of club mosses, horsetails, and whisk ferns all "microphylls", which is a morphological and not a phylogenetic usage.

According to the telome theory, the **megaphyll** arose from a lateral branch system by overtopping, planation, and syngenesis: a weaker lateral branch system is pushed aside and arranged in one plane, mesophyll grows between the telomes, and the vascular tissue of the original dichotomous branches becomes the veins. This interpretation is strongly supported by fossils: trimerophytes have a main axis and lateral branch systems, the Early Devonian *Eophyllophyton* has tiny fan-shaped leaves, and the Late Devonian progymnosperm *Archaeopteris* has planar lateral branch systems and flat leaf blades ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)). Modern studies further show that megaphylls with a leaf blade originated independently at least three times, in ferns, horsetails, and seed plants (Boyce and Knoll 2002; Tomescu 2009), each time by a marginal meristem of the leaf primordium forming the blade. That leaves resemble shoots in development (for example, expression of *KNOX* genes in compound leaf primordia) also supports the hypothesis that leaves derive from lateral branch systems; the molecular regulation of leaf development belongs to plant physiology and developmental biology.

The veins of megaphylls also show an evolutionary trend: open dichotomous venation (many ferns, *Ginkgo*) is primitive, and reticulate venation appeared independently several times, in ferns, gnetophytes, and angiosperms.

### 16.4.3. The Origin of Microphylls

There are three hypotheses on the origin of microphylls.

**Definition 16.4.3 (Enation Theory)** The theory that the microphylls of lycophytes originated from enations, outgrowths of the surface of the stem axis that at first had no vascular tissue, with leaf traces gradually arising, first reaching the base of the enation and finally entering it to become a vein, is the **enation theory**.

The enation theory was proposed by Bower (1935), and the fossil record contains a sequence consistent with it: the veinless spiny projections on the stem of *Psilophyton princeps*, the projections of *Asteroxylon* (the leaf trace reaching only the base), and the one-veined microphylls of *Baragwanathia*.

The other two hypotheses are:

- The reduction hypothesis (the telome-theory interpretation): the microphyll was formed by reduction of a telome system to a single telome, followed by planation.
- The sterilization theory (Kenrick and Crane 1997): zosterophylls bear many lateral sporangia on the stem, and the microphyll arose by sterilization and flattening of some of these lateral sporangia; it can explain why lycophyte sporangia always correspond one to one with leaves, borne singly in the leaf axil or on the adaxial surface of the leaf.

The three hypotheses may not be mutually exclusive: the separate attachment of the enations and sporangia of *Asteroxylon* on the stem supports the enation theory, and the close relationship of lycophyte sporophylls and sporangia supports the sterilization theory. In any case, microphylls and megaphylls originated independently and are not homologous; "leaf" in vascular plants is a functional concept that includes at least several organs of different origins.

B: a megaphyll, with the leaf trace departing from a siphonostele and a leaf gap filled with parenchyma above it, and branched veins. ### 16.4.4. The Origin of Sporophylls and Strobili

**Definition 16.4.4 (Sporophyll and Strobilus)** A leaf that bears sporangia is a **sporophyll** or fertile leaf; a leaf that only photosynthesizes is a vegetative or sterile leaf. A globose or spike-like structure formed by sporophylls crowded together at the end of a shoot is a **strobilus** or sporophyll spike.

The origin of the sporophyll is tied to the origin of the leaf:

- Sporophylls of ferns: on the telome theory, once a fertile syntelome has become flattened and fused, the sporangia that were originally borne at the branch tips come to lie at the margin of the leaf blade; the sporangia later move onto the underside of the leaf and aggregate into sori ([7.12.17](/7.12.17)).
- Sporophylls of lycophytes: the telome theory holds that the sterile part of the sporophyll and the sporangium come from the same syntelome, with the sporangium-bearing telome shortened and placed in the axil; the enation theory holds that the sporangium came first and an enation later grew on the outer side of the sporangium, the two together forming the sporophyll; and the sterilization theory holds that the sporophyll itself is a sterilized sporangium.
- The sporangiophore of horsetails: it is generally regarded as a specialized sporophyll; the telome-theory explanation is that it formed by recurvation and fusion of the tips of a fertile syntelome, so that the sporangia hang inverted on the inner side of the shield ([7.12.17](/7.12.17)).

The aggregation of sporophylls into strobili at the end of the shoot arose independently in lycophytes and horsetails, and the later cones and flowers evolved on this basis ([7.12.25](/7.12.25), [7.12.27](/7.12.27)). The evolutionary trend of sporophylls runs from no distinction between vegetative leaves and sporophylls (isophylly) to their differentiation (anisophylly), and from scattered to aggregated into strobili.

### 16.4.5. The Origin of the Root

The root is the last organ to appear, and the fossil evidence is meager. Chinese textbooks list three views: the root evolved from a rhizome branch of a psilophyte growing downward (such as the rhizomatous axes of *Asteroxylon*); it was transformed from the rhizoids of the underground part of a psilophyte; or it was a newly arisen structure.

The conclusion of modern research is that roots originated at least twice in vascular plants. The root of lycophytes branches dichotomously at the tip, has no lateral roots, and has a single protoxylem pole (monarch), unlike the root of euphyllophytes. Hetherington and Dolan (2018) found that the rhizomatous axes of *Asteroxylon* in the Rhynie chert still had no root cap and no root hairs, which shows that lycophyte roots acquired a root cap only after *Asteroxylon* and that they were assembled stepwise on a separate line from the roots of euphyllophytes. The functional similarity of the roots of the two lineages is the result of convergence. The structure of the root with a root cap, endogenously arising lateral roots, and root hairs became fixed in euphyllophytes ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)). The "roots" of *Isoëtes* and of the fossil *Lepidodendron* (small roots on the rhizomorph) have a special structure and are thought to be modified leaves or shoots.

## 16.5. The Lycophytes

### 16.5.1. Characteristics and Classification

**Definition 16.5.1 (Lycophytes)** Vascular plants whose sporophyte bears microphylls, whose sporangia are borne singly at the base of the adaxial surface of the sporophyll or in the leaf axil and are mostly reniform and transversely dehiscent, whose primary xylem in the stem is exarch, and whose roots branch dichotomously at the tip are **lycophytes**; in PPG I (2016) they are the class Lycopodiopsida. They are the earliest-diverging clade of living vascular plants and the **sister group** of all other vascular plants (the euphyllophytes).

Lycophytes arose in the Silurian, at the same time as or earlier than the zosterophylls. In the Carboniferous they included both herbs and trees tens of meters tall (§16.6); after the Permian the arborescent forms went extinct one after another, and the living lycophytes are all herbaceous. The sporophyte is differentiated into root, stem, and leaf; the stem is mostly dichotomously branched and shows all intermediate forms from protostele to polycyclic siphonostele; the spores are homosporous or heterosporous; the sperm are mostly biflagellate.

PPG I divides the living lycophytes into three orders and three families, with 18 genera and about 1,338 species:

| Order | Family | Genera and species | Ligule | Spores | Gametophyte |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lycopodiales | Lycopodiaceae | 3 subfamilies, 16 genera, about 388 species | Absent | Homosporous | Bisexual, subterranean and mycoheterotrophic or green and surface-dwelling, developing outside the spore wall |
| Isoëtales | Isoëtaceae | 1 genus, *Isoëtes*, about 250 species | Present | Heterosporous | Unisexual, developing inside the spore wall |
| Selaginellales | Selaginellaceae | 1 genus, *Selaginella*, about 700 species | Present | Heterosporous | Unisexual, developing inside the spore wall |

The ligulate Selaginellales and Isoëtales together form a clade (Isoëtopsida) that is the sister group of the Lycopodiales; heterospory originated only once in this clade.

**Definition 16.5.2 (Ligule and Rhizophore)** A small tongue-shaped projection at the base of the adaxial surface of the leaf (including the sporophyll) of *Selaginella*, *Isoëtes*, and relatives is the **ligule**; it secretes mucilage early in leaf development and may keep the young leaf moist, but its function is not fully understood. The leafless, downward-growing, columnar organ on the stem of *Selaginella* whose tip produces adventitious roots is the **rhizophore**.

The nature of the rhizophore has long been disputed: it arises exogenously and resembles a leafless shoot, but it bears roots at its tip and so resembles a root; it is now generally regarded as an organ peculiar to *Selaginella*. The ligule at the junction of leaf blade and sheath in grasses ([7.12.23](/7.12.23)) shares only a name with the ligule of lycophytes and is not homologous to it.

Chinese textbooks (the system of Ching) divide the group into the subphylum Lycophytina (Lycopodiales: Lycopodiaceae and Huperziaceae; Selaginellales: Selaginellaceae) and the subphylum Isoetophytina (Isoëtales), placing the quillworts in a subphylum of their own because their sperm are multiflagellate and their appearance differs greatly from other lycophytes; molecular phylogenies show that *Isoëtes* and *Selaginella* are sister groups and both belong to the lycophytes. In PPG I the Huperziaceae are included in the Lycopodiaceae as the subfamily Huperzioideae.

### 16.5.2. Lycopodiales: Lycopodiaceae

Lycopodiaceae are mostly perennial herbs, with prostrate, erect, or pendent (in epiphytic species) stems that often branch dichotomously and bear adventitious roots; the leaves are scale-like or needle-like microphylls, densely spiral, with a single midvein and no ligule. Sporophylls and vegetative leaves are alike or different; the sporangia are large, reniform, short-stalked, borne singly in the axil on the adaxial side of the sporophyll, with a wall of several cell layers (eusporangiate development) and transverse dehiscence; the spores are of one kind, tetrahedral, trilete, and yellow.

PPG I divides the Lycopodiaceae into three subfamilies:

| Subfamily | Main genera | Features | Common species in China |
|---|---|---|---|
| Huperzioideae | *Huperzia*, *Phlegmariurus*, *Phylloglossum* | Stems erect or pendent, equally dichotomous; sporophylls similar to or smaller than vegetative leaves, without distinct strobili (*Phlegmariurus* forms linear sporangial spikes at the branch tips); gemmae common; spore wall with honeycomb ornamentation; gametophytes club-shaped or ribbon-shaped | *H. serrata* (qiancengta), *H. selago* (northern firmoss), *Phlegmariurus* |
| Lycopodioideae | *Lycopodium*, *Diphasiastrum*, *Lycopodiastrum*, and others | Main stem long, creeping or climbing, with erect lateral branches; sporophylls and vegetative leaves different, aggregated into distinct strobili; spore wall reticulate; gametophytes massive, subterranean, mycoheterotrophic | *L. japonicum*, *L. clavatum* (stag's-horn clubmoss), *D. complanatum* (northern running-pine), *Lycopodiastrum* |
| Lycopodielloideae | *Palhinhaea*, *Lycopodiella* | Strobili often pendent or erect on short branches; gametophytes partly above ground, with chloroplasts | *P. cernua* (nodding clubmoss) |

Sporophyte and stem structure of *Lycopodium*: the stem is divided into epidermis, cortex, and stele. The epidermis is one layer; the cortex is wide, with parenchyma and sclerenchyma, the sclerenchyma near the stele or beneath the epidermis, and contains leaf traces; the stele has no pith and no cambium and is an actinostele or plectostele, with exarch xylem.

Life cycle: in *Lycopodium* the spores must lie dormant underground for many years before germinating, and after germination need a specific symbiotic fungus to develop. The spores of *L. clavatum* germinate after about 6–7 years, and the gametophyte takes a further 12–15 years or so to mature (observations of Bao Wenmei and colleagues); gametophytes are known for less than one tenth of lycophyte species. Gametophyte form varies with subfamily:

- A massive body entirely buried in the soil, without chloroplasts, and nourished by symbiotic fungi (**mycorrhiza**), as in *L. japonicum* and *L. clavatum*;
- A body partly buried in the soil, with chloroplasts in the part above ground, as in *D. complanatum*;
- A body lying on the soil surface, with chloroplasts and capable of photosynthesis, as in *P. cernua*.

The gametophyte lives a long time and is bisexual, with **antheridia** and **archegonia** embedded in its upper surface. The antheridium is elliptical with a wall one cell layer thick and produces biflagellate sperm; the neck of the archegonium projects above the gametophyte, and the archegonium contains neck canal cells, a ventral canal cell, and the egg. At fertilization the neck canal cells and ventral canal cell break down and the sperm swim in; fertilization still depends on water. The zygote develops into an embryo with a suspensor and a foot, which begins to differentiate root, stem, and leaf; when the young sporophyte can live independently, the gametophyte dies. In culture, adding glucose or sucrose lets the gametophyte grow without fungi and shortens the growing period to 2–3 years, showing that the main contribution of the fungus is carbon.

Uses: the spores of *Lycopodium* (lycopodium powder) are rich in oil and highly flammable, and were once used in flash powder and for illumination; they are also used as a mold-release agent in metal casting and as a pill coating. *Huperzia serrata* contains huperzine A, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor isolated by Chinese researchers from qiancengta (1986) and used in the treatment of Alzheimer's disease; wild resources have become scarce through overcollection, and *Huperzia* and *Phlegmariurus* were listed as National Class II Key Protected Wild Plants in 2021. The whole plant of *L. japonicum* (shenjincao) is used in medicine. Lycophytes prefer acidic soil and can serve as indicators of acidic soils.

### 16.5.3. Selaginellales: Selaginellaceae

The family Selaginellaceae has only one genus, *Selaginella*, with about 700 species; it is the largest genus of lycophytes, mostly distributed in the tropics and subtropics, with about 70 species in China, growing in moist forest understory, grassland, or on rock, and including drought-tolerant species.

Sporophyte: *Selaginella* falls into two kinds:

- Species with erect stems and leaves all alike and spirally arranged (isophyllous species, such as the main stem of *S. sibirica* and *S. moellendorffii*);
- Species with creeping or ascending, dorsiventral stems and leaves of two kinds (anisophyllous species, most species): the leaves are scale-like in four rows, the two lateral rows being larger lateral leaves and the two median rows smaller median leaves, with lateral and median leaves arranged oppositely, as in *S. sinensis* and *S. uncinata*.

The stems of creeping species often bear rhizophores whose tips produce many adventitious roots, as in *S. uncinata*; erect species root at the base of the stem. Each leaf (including sporophylls) has a ligule on the adaxial surface near the axil.

The stem is divided into epidermis, cortex, and stele. The epidermis has no stomata; between cortex and stele there is a large space, and the stele is suspended in a network of radially arranged elongated cells (trabeculae), which are endodermal cells with Casparian strips. The stele ranges from a simple protostele to a polycyclic siphonostele with many intermediate forms, and in some species the stem has two or more protosteles (for example *S. kraussiana* has two); the xylem is exarch, and many species have vessels, which arose independently in *Selaginella* (**convergent evolution**, [7.12.5](/7.12.5)).

Strobili and spores: the sporophylls of *Selaginella* are crowded at the branch tips and form a four-angled strobilus. Each sporophyll bears one sporangium in its axil, and the sporangia are of two kinds:

**Definition 16.5.3 (Homospory, Heterospory, Megaspore, and Microspore)** The production by a plant of only one kind of spore, of uniform size and form, is **homospory**, as in *Lycopodium* and the great majority of ferns. The production of two kinds of spores that differ in size and developmental fate is **heterospory**, as in *Selaginella*, *Isoëtes*, *Marsilea* and *Salvinia*, and all seed plants. Of heterosporous spores, the large, few spores that develop into female gametophytes are **megaspores**, borne in megasporangia; the small, numerous spores that develop into male gametophytes are **microspores**, borne in microsporangia.

The gametophyte of homosporous plants is usually bisexual and develops outside the spore wall (exosporic); that of heterosporous plants is unisexual and develops inside the spore wall (endosporic). The difference between megaspores and microspores lies first in function (developing into a female or male gametophyte) and only second in size.

The megasporangium of *Selaginella* is yellow and usually only one megaspore mother cell undergoes meiosis, producing 4 (1–4) megaspores; the microsporangium is orange-red and produces several hundred microspores. In *S. tamariscina* (resurrection plant) the megaspores are about 450–500 µm in diameter and the microspores about 50–63 µm, a volume difference of several hundredfold to a thousandfold. The arrangement of megasporophylls and microsporophylls varies with species: microsporophylls in the upper part of the strobilus and megasporophylls at the base, or the two alternating in vertical rows, or no fixed pattern. The spore wall is ornamented with tubercles, rods, spines, and so on, which are used to distinguish species.

Gametophyte: the gametophyte of *Selaginella* is extremely reduced and develops inside the spore wall:

- Male gametophyte: the microspore begins to divide before the microsporangium opens, dividing unequally into a small prothallial cell (a vegetative cell that does not divide again) and a large cell; the large cell divides to form one antheridium, whose outer layer is a wall of 8 cells and whose interior is spermatogenous tissue, finally producing about 256 biflagellate sperm that escape through the trilete mark of the microspore. The entire male gametophyte is a prothallial cell plus an antheridium.
- Female gametophyte: the nucleus of the megaspore first divides many times to form many free nuclei, and cell walls then form inward from the top of the spore (below the trilete mark), forming nutritive tissue; the trilete mark opens, the apex of the gametophyte is exposed through the crack and produces rhizoids and archegonia, the archegonia having only four cover cells exposed; the uncellularized interior of the gametophyte stores nutrients that supply the development of the embryo.

The megaspores of *Selaginella* often begin to develop into female gametophytes before the megasporangium opens, and fertilization sometimes takes place on the strobilus; the megaspores of *S. tamariscina* need a cold period (overwintering) to germinate, and fertilization may be of megaspores left on the plant from the previous year by microspores of the current year. Several archegonia on one female gametophyte may be fertilized, but only one develops into an embryo. The embryo first extends the stem, two cotyledon-like leaves form on both sides of the stem apex, and only then does the radicle appear; the megaspore wall remains around the female gametophyte, and the nutrients in the female gametophyte supply the young sporophyte, like the seed coat and endosperm in germinating seeds.

Development of the female gametophyte inside the megaspore wall with food storage, fertilization of the megaspore while it is still on the parent plant, and development of the embryo within the female gametophyte are precursors of the "seed habit". But the megaspore of *Selaginella* is ultimately released, the megasporangium is not enclosed by an integument, and after fertilization it is not dispersed as a unit, so it is not a seed (the definition of the seed is given in [7.12.25](/7.12.25)).

Representative plants and uses: *S. tamariscina* (resurrection plant) grows on sunny rocks; in drought its small branches curl inward into a ball and the plant seems dead, and it unfolds again after rain, making it a resurrection plant; the whole plant is used medicinally. *S. moellendorffii*, *S. uncinata*, and *S. doederleinii* are common in southern forest understories, and the leaves of *S. uncinata* have a blue-green iridescence and are often cultivated as ornamentals. *Selaginella* is a model group for studying the evolution of vascular plants: *S. moellendorffii* was the first non-seed vascular plant to have its whole genome sequenced (Banks et al. 2011), with a genome of only about 100 Mb, one of the smallest plant genomes known.

### 16.5.4. Isoëtales: Isoëtaceae

The family Isoëtaceae has only one genus, *Isoëtes*, with about 250 species, mostly submerged, amphibious, or marsh plants and a few in seasonal wetlands, with about five species in China. Quillworts look like young leeks or chives and were once mistaken for seed plants (a question in Chinese textbooks asks which characters justify placing a plant that resembles a seed plant among the ferns).

Sporophyte: the stem is short and stout, massive (**corm**-like), buried in the substrate, often with two or three longitudinal grooves from which many fibrous roots arise; some regard the root-bearing lower part of the stem as a rhizophore, homologous to the rhizomorph of the fossil *Lepidodendron*. The stem has a protostele, with helical and reticulate tracheids; the stem has a special cambium that produces vascular tissue inward (the "prismatic layer") and parenchyma outward, so the stem thickens slowly. This is the only secondary growth among living lycophytes and is evidence of their relationship to the arborescent lycophytes. The leaves are slender, clustered, and tightly spiral, with four longitudinal air canals in the leaf and a ligule at the base of the adaxial surface.

Sporangia: every leaf may be a sporophyll; megasporophylls are mostly at the periphery of the stem, microsporophylls mostly nearer the center, and the innermost leaves are immature. The sporangia are large and borne at the base of the adaxial leaf surface in a depression (fovea) below the ligule, partly or wholly covered by a thin membranous velum; inside the sporangium sterile cell plates (trabeculae) partly divide it. A megasporangium contains about 100–300 megaspores, a microsporangium up to more than 300,000 microspores. The sporangia have no opening mechanism, and spores are released when the sporangium wall or the leaf decays.

Gametophyte: as in *Selaginella*, it is extremely reduced and develops inside the spore wall. The male gametophyte consists of a prothallial cell and an antheridium, which has only 4 wall cells and a single spermatogenous cell and produces only 4 sperm, the smallest number outside the seed plants; the sperm are multiflagellate, however, unlike those of *Selaginella*. The female gametophyte first undergoes free nuclear divisions, then forms cell walls inward from the top of the megaspore, emerges at the trilete mark, and produces archegonia.

Special features of photosynthesis: many submerged quillworts carry out **crassulacean acid metabolism**: where $\ce{CO2}$ diffuses slowly in water and is depleted by other aquatic plants during the day, they fix $\ce{CO2}$ at night as malate and release it for photosynthesis during the day (Keeley 1981); some species (such as the Andean *I. andicola*) have no stomata on the leaves and take up carbon mainly through the roots from $\ce{CO2}$-rich sediment. The biochemistry of CAM belongs to plant physiology.

Conservation: *I. sinensis* is found in shallow marshes of the lower Yangtze, *I. yunguiensis* in Yunnan and Guizhou, and *I. taiwanensis* in Taiwan; their habitats have been lost to reclamation and water pollution, and all species of *Isoëtes* were listed as National Class I Key Protected Wild Plants in 2021. The "*I. japonica*" of southwestern China in Chinese textbooks should be *I. yunguiensis* and its relatives.

## 16.6. Fossil Lycophytes

Lycophytes became trees in the Late Devonian and in the Carboniferous were the dominant plants of tropical swamp forests, forming the great coalfields of Europe, North America, and North China of the Carboniferous. When the climate became drier after the Permian, the arborescent lycophytes declined in different regions one after another and were essentially extinct after the Triassic, with only the related *Isoëtes* surviving to the present.

*Lepidodendron* reached 40 m in height with a trunk about 2 m in diameter, its top dichotomously branched to form a crown. The branches bore densely spiral, needle-like microphylls with ligules; when the leaves fell, they left rhomboid leaf cushions arranged in spirals on the bark, like fish scales, which gave the genus its name. The stem had a vascular cambium but not much secondary xylem, and the trunk was supported mainly by a very thick periderm (bark). At the base of the trunk was a dichotomously branched rhizomorph (*Stigmaria*) densely covered with spiral small roots, which are thought to be modified leaves. The strobili (*Lepidostrobus*) were borne at the tips of small branches and heterosporous, with 8–16 megaspores in a megasporangium and many microspores in a microsporangium. *Lepidodendron* may have been monocarpic: the trunk grew to a certain height, then branched rapidly, produced strobili, and died.

*Sigillaria* was also a tree, up to 30 m tall, with an unbranched trunk or only a few branches at the top, and a crown of linear leaves up to 1 m long; the leaf bases were mostly hexagonal and arranged in vertical rows, like rows of seals, which gave the genus its name; it also had a cambium, and its strobili were 15–30 cm long.

*Lepidocarpon* was the megasporangiate cone of a Carboniferous kind of *Lepidodendron*. In each megasporangium only one megaspore was functional and the other three aborted; the megasporangium was enclosed by two lateral wings extending upward from the sporophyll, leaving only a slit at the top; the female gametophyte developed inside the megaspore, and at maturity the megaspore, megasporangium, and sporophyll were shed together and dispersed. This structure is functionally similar to a seed (a single megaspore, enclosed, dispersed as a whole) and has been called "seed-like", but what encloses it is the sporophyll and not an **integument**, and it is not homologous to the **ovule** of seed plants; it is an example of convergent evolution of the seed habit in heterosporous plants.

The decline of the arborescent lycophytes is linked to climate change: in the Late Carboniferous (about 305 million years ago) the tropical rain forests of Europe and North America collapsed as the climate dried (Sahney et al. 2010), and the wet-adapted lycophyte forests were replaced by more drought-tolerant ferns and seed plants. The Triassic *Pleuromeia* was an unbranched lycophyte about 2 m tall with a rhizomorph-like stem base, regarded as transitional from the arborescent lycophytes to *Isoëtes*.

## Summary

Vascular plants have a dominant, branching sporophyte, and lignified vascular tissue allows them to stand upright and grow tall, while the gametophyte is progressively reduced. Living vascular plants fall into two lineages, the lycophytes and the euphyllophytes; leaves, roots, and secondary growth originated independently in the two lineages.

## Questions

1. Why do we say that vascular plants and bryophytes "took two different paths"? Explain in terms of branching of the sporophyte, its mode of growth, and spore output.

   **Answer** The bryophyte sporophyte is unbranched, has a single sporangium, has determinate growth, and depends on the gametophyte for life; spore output is increased by letting one protonema give rise to many gametophytes, each producing one sporophyte. The vascular plant sporophyte branches and grows indeterminately from its apex, and the tip of every branch can produce a sporangium, so one fertilization yields a large number of spores; it has lignified conducting tissue, a cuticle, and stomata, and can live independently and grow tall. The sporophyte thus becomes the main body of the life cycle and the gametophyte is progressively reduced.

2. Compare the protostele, siphonostele, dictyostele, and eustele, and explain why the sequence "siphonostele → dictyostele → eustele" is now thought not to be a single line of evolution.

   **Answer** The protostele has no pith and a solid central core of xylem; the siphonostele has a central pith with the xylem in a cylinder, ectophloic or amphiphloic; the dictyostele is a siphonostele with overlapping leaf gaps, dissected into a ring of meristeles each with its own endodermis; the eustele is a ring of discrete collateral bundles around a pith. Fossils show that the eustele arose in progymnosperms directly from a protostele by differentiation of pith and bundles, a separate evolutionary line from the siphonostele and dictyostele of ferns; the leaf trace gaps of seed plants are likewise not homologous to the leaf gaps of ferns.

3. What is the telome theory? How does it explain the origin of the megaphyll and of the sporangiophore of horsetails? What other hypotheses are there for the origin of the microphyll?

   **Answer** The telome theory holds that the primitive vascular plant consisted of dichotomously branched axes whose terminal parts (telomes) did or did not bear sporangia, and that all organs were derived from telomes by five processes: overtopping, planation, syngenesis, reduction, and recurvation (Definition 16.4.1). The megaphyll arose from a lateral branch system by overtopping, planation, and syngenesis (webbing); the peltate sporangiophore of horsetails arose when the tips of a fertile syntelome curled back and fused, so that the sporangia hang inverted on the inner side of the shield. For the microphyll there are also the enation theory (Definition 16.4.3: an enation on the stem surface later acquired a vein, as shown by the spines of *Psilophyton*, the projections of *Asteroxylon*, and the leaves of *Baragwanathia*) and the sterilization theory (lateral sporangia became sterile).

4. What "seed habit" characters does *Selaginella* have? Why is it still not a seed plant?

   **Answer** *Selaginella* is heterosporous and both female and male gametophytes develop inside the spore wall; the megaspore often stays in the megasporangium and may even be fertilized on the strobilus; the female gametophyte stores food, the embryo develops on the female gametophyte inside the megaspore wall, and on germination the megaspore wall and female gametophyte act like the seed coat and endosperm. But the megasporangium of *Selaginella* is not enclosed by an integument, the megaspore is ultimately released from the sporangium, and after fertilization no unit enclosed by an integument and dispersed as a whole is formed, so it is not a seed. *Lepidocarpon* of the Carboniferous is functionally closer to a seed, but it is enclosed by the sporophyll and so is not homologous to an ovule.

5. In Chinese textbooks *Lycopodium*, *Selaginella*, and *Isoëtes* were placed in two subphyla. Why does PPG I place them in a single class? With which fossil plants is *Isoëtes* related?

   **Answer** All three have microphylls, sporangia borne singly at the base of the adaxial surface of the sporophyll, transversely dehiscent sporangia, exarch xylem, and dichotomously branched roots, and molecular phylogenies show that they form a monophyletic group that is the sister group of the euphyllophytes. The reasons for placing *Isoëtes* in a separate subphylum (multiflagellate sperm, a short stout stem) are only derived characters within this lineage; it and *Selaginella* both have a ligule, heterospory, and endosporic gametophytes, and the two are sister groups. The cambium and secondary growth of the *Isoëtes* stem and its rhizophore-like root-bearing lower stem resemble those of the Carboniferous *Lepidodendron* and *Sigillaria* (rhizomorphs) and the Triassic *Pleuromeia*, and *Isoëtes* is regarded as the only living descendant of the arborescent lycophytes.
