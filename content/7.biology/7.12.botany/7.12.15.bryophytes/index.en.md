Bryophytes are the earliest-diverging lineages of land plants, and the only living land plants in which the gametophyte is the dominant generation. The patch of liverwort or tuft of moss we see is a haploid gametophyte; the diploid sporophyte is only a small capsule at its tip, nourished by the gametophyte throughout its life. Bryophytes have no lignified vascular tissue, remain small, and need water for fertilization, so most live in shaded, moist places. Yet they also grow on rock, tree bark, tundra, and desert crusts, and many species tolerate almost complete desiccation, resuming photosynthesis within minutes of rewetting. There are about 20,000 species in three phyla (liverworts, mosses, and hornworts), which together form a monophyletic group that is the sister group of the vascular plants ([7.12.14](/7.12.14#sec-14.6)).

This chapter first describes the general features of bryophytes (§15.1), then liverworts (§15.2), mosses (§15.3), and hornworts (§15.4), and then discusses the origin and evolution of bryophytes (§15.5) and their ecological and economic importance (§15.6). The streptophyte algae and the colonization of land are treated in the previous chapter. Whether bryophyte stomata are homologous to those of vascular plants was raised in [7.12.4](/7.12.4#sec-4.3.2); the phylogenetic side of the question is discussed here.

## 15.1. General Features of Bryophytes

**Definition 15.1.1 (Bryophytes)** **Bryophytes** are embryophytes in which the gametophyte is dominant and the sporophyte is unbranched, remains attached to the gametophyte throughout its life, and lacks lignified vascular tissue. They comprise the liverworts, mosses, and hornworts.

### 15.1.1. Gametophyte and Sporophyte

All embryophytes have a life cycle with sporic meiosis and alternation of generations ([7.12.14](/7.12.14#sec-14.5.2)); the two generations are called the gametophyte and the sporophyte.

**Definition 15.1.2 (Gametophyte and Sporophyte)** In a life cycle with alternation of generations, the haploid plant that develops from a spore and produces gametes is the **gametophyte** (the sexual generation); the diploid plant that develops from a zygote and produces spores by meiosis is the **sporophyte** (the asexual generation). The spore is the first cell of the gametophyte generation, and the zygote is the first cell of the sporophyte generation.

The bryophyte gametophyte is an independent green plant. It is long-lived, grows for many years, and produces gametes repeatedly. The sporophyte usually lives only a few weeks to a few months and dies after releasing its spores once.

Gametophyte development begins with spore germination, which first produces a filamentous or flat stage from which the mature gametophyte then grows.

**Definition 15.1.3 (Protonema)** The filamentous or flat structure that forms when a bryophyte spore germinates, and on which the mature gametophyte arises, is the **protonema**.

The protonema of mosses is well developed and consists of branched filaments; each protonema produces many buds and so gives rise to many plants. The protonemata of liverworts and hornworts are poorly developed, consisting of only a few cells, and usually give rise to a single plant. In *Sphagnum* and *Andreaea* the protonema is filamentous at first and later flat. The protonema resembles a filamentous green alga and is a developmental stage unique to bryophytes.

The mature gametophyte has one of two forms:

**Definition 15.1.4 (Thalloid and Leafy Gametophytes)** A flat, bilaterally symmetrical, dorsiventral gametophyte without differentiation into stem and leaves is a **thalloid gametophyte** (thallus); a gametophyte differentiated into a stem-like axis and leaf-like appendages is a **leafy gametophyte**. The "stems" and "leaves" of bryophytes have no vascular tissue and are not homologous to the stems and leaves of vascular plants; they are sometimes called caulidia and phyllidia.

Hornworts and most marchantialean liverworts are thalloid; leafy liverworts and all mosses are leafy. The leafy gametophytes of mosses are radially symmetrical with spirally arranged leaves; those of leafy liverworts are dorsiventrally flattened with leaves in two or three rows. Bryophyte leaves are mostly one cell layer thick. In many mosses the leaf has a midrib (costa) of elongated thick-walled cells, and the stem has a central strand of elongated cells; both give support, and in some species they also conduct (§15.3.4).

The gametophyte is anchored to the substrate, and absorbs water, by rhizoids.

**Definition 15.1.5 (Rhizoid)** A filamentous structure, either a single cell or a single row of cells, that extends from epidermal cells of a bryophyte gametophyte or a fern prothallus is a **rhizoid**. It anchors and absorbs but is not an organ and has no vascular tissue. Rhizoids of liverworts and hornworts are unicellular; those of mosses are multicellular, uniseriate, often branched, and have oblique cross walls.

### 15.1.2. Archegonia and Antheridia

The reproductive organs of bryophytes are multicellular, are surrounded by a wall of sterile cells, and are borne on the gametophyte.

**Definition 15.1.6 (Archegonium and Antheridium)** The female sex organ of embryophytes is the **archegonium**. It is flask-shaped: the slender neck is made of a single layer of cells around a central canal that contains a row of neck canal cells; the swollen venter contains an egg cell with a ventral canal cell above it. The male sex organ is the **antheridium**. It is club-shaped, ovoid, or spherical, stalked, with a wall one cell thick, and produces many sperm.

Bryophyte sperm are slender and coiled, with two flagella at the anterior end. When the egg matures, the neck canal cells and ventral canal cell break down, forming a mucilage-filled channel, and a substance that attracts sperm is released. Sperm swim to the archegonium in raindrops, dew, or a water film, enter through the neck canal, and fuse with the egg. Fertilization in bryophytes therefore depends on water, and sperm usually travel only a few centimeters; in some species splashing raindrops or small arthropods carry sperm farther.

Bryophytes may be monoicous (one gametophyte bears both antheridia and archegonia, as in *Funaria*) or dioicous (as in *Marchantia* and *Porella*). Dioicous bryophytes often have sex chromosomes. Because the gametophyte is haploid, female gametophytes carry a U chromosome, male gametophytes a V chromosome, and the sporophyte is UV. (Plant sex chromosomes were first discovered in the liverwort *Sphaerocarpos*; Allen, 1917.) The foot cells of the *Marchantia* sporophyte therefore contain both sex chromosomes, whereas gametophytes, gemmae, and spores contain only one.

### 15.1.3. Embryo and Sporophyte

The zygote divides within the archegonium without a resting period, forming a multicellular embryo. Between the embryo and the gametophyte lies a placenta of **transfer cells** with wall ingrowths, through which nutrients pass from the gametophyte to the embryo; this is the sense of the name "embryophyte". The embryo develops into the sporophyte, which usually has three parts.

**Definition 15.1.7 (Capsule, Seta, Foot, and Elaters)** The spore-producing sporangium at the tip of a bryophyte sporophyte is the **moss capsule** (in liverworts and hornworts also simply called the capsule); the stalk below it is the **seta**; the lowest part of the sporophyte, embedded in gametophyte tissue and absorbing nutrients from it, is the foot. In liverwort capsules, long unicellular sterile cells with helical wall thickenings, intermingled with the spores, are the **elaters**.

In the capsule, the sporogenous tissue forms spore mother cells, each of which undergoes meiosis to form four spores (a spore tetrad). The spore wall contains sporopollenin and is ornamented with various patterns, which suits wind dispersal. Liverwort and hornwort capsules also contain sterile cells: in liverworts, unicellular elaters with helical thickenings; in hornworts, multicellular pseudoelaters. They twist with changes in humidity and help to release the spores. The capsules of mosses and hornworts have a central column of sterile tissue, the **columella**, which is absent from most liverworts.

After fertilization the venter of the archegonium grows with the embryo and forms the calyptra that encloses the young sporophyte. In mosses the elongating seta tears the calyptra loose and carries it up on top of the capsule, so the moss calyptra sits on the capsule and is gametophyte tissue.

### 15.1.4. Comparison of the Three Phyla

Bryophytes were traditionally treated as one phylum divided into two classes (Hepaticae and Musci) or, in Chinese textbooks, three classes (adding Anthocerotae). Current classification treats the three groups as separate phyla: liverworts (Marchantiophyta, about 7,500 species; Söderström et al., 2016), mosses (Bryophyta, about 12,800 species; Goffinet and Buck), and hornworts (Anthocerotophyta, about 220 species); "bryophytes" is used as a collective name for the three.

| Feature | Liverworts | Mosses | Hornworts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gametophyte | Thalloid, or dorsiventrally flattened leafy | Radially symmetrical leafy | Thalloid, simple in structure |
| Leaves | No midrib, often lobed, in 2–3 rows | Usually with a midrib, spirally arranged | None |
| Rhizoids | Unicellular | Multicellular, uniseriate, branched | Unicellular |
| Protonema | Reduced, gives rise to 1 plant | Well developed, gives rise to many plants | Reduced |
| Chloroplasts per cell | Many, small, no pyrenoid | Many, small, no pyrenoid | 1 (or a few), large, usually with a pyrenoid |
| Oil bodies | Present (membrane-bound terpenoid oil bodies unique to liverworts) | Absent | Absent |
| Sex organs | Superficial, or on gametangiophores or branch tips | Superficial, in perigonial and perichaetial clusters at branch tips | Embedded in the thallus |
| Seta | Delicate, elongates rapidly only after the capsule matures | Firm, elongates before the capsule matures | Absent |
| Capsule | No columella; splits into 4 valves or irregularly | Columella present; usually opening by an operculum, often with a peristome | Horn-shaped, with a columella; splits into 2 valves |
| Stomata on the capsule | Absent | Usually present (on the apophysis) | Present |
| Sterile cells | Elaters (unicellular) | None | Pseudoelaters (multicellular) |
| Sporophyte growth | Determinate | Apical, determinate | Basal meristem, continuous |

## 15.2. Liverworts

**Definition 15.2.1 (Liverworts)** **Liverworts** (Marchantiophyta) are bryophytes whose gametophyte is thalloid or a dorsiventrally flattened leafy shoot, whose cells often contain oil bodies, whose rhizoids are unicellular, whose sporophyte lacks stomata, whose seta elongates only after the capsule has matured, and whose capsule has no columella, usually opens by four valves, and produces elaters.

Liverworts grow mostly on shaded, moist soil, rock, and tree trunks; some grow on leaves (epiphyllous liverworts, common in tropical rain forests), and a few float on or are submerged in water. The **oil body** is an organelle unique to liverworts: a small body bounded by a single membrane and containing terpenoids and other secondary metabolites. Oil bodies give many liverworts a characteristic odor, may deter herbivores and protect against desiccation, and their shape and number are used in classification.

### 15.2.1. *Marchantia*

*Marchantia polymorpha* (Marchantiales, Marchantiaceae) is cosmopolitan and common on shaded, moist ground under woods, beside wells, along walls, and in nurseries.

**Gametophyte** The gametophyte is a green, dichotomously branched thallus lying flat on the ground and up to 10 cm long. The apical cell, at the notch of the forked tip, is the growing point. The upper surface shows a network of rhombic (oblique hexagonal) areas, each with a white dot at its center. In cross section the thallus is several cell layers thick and differentiated into several layers (see the figure below):

- Upper epidermis: one cell layer, with a cuticle.
- Air chamber layer: beneath the upper epidermis is a layer of air chambers separated by walls one cell thick; these walls produce the network on the surface. The floor of each chamber bears many loosely arranged, short, chloroplast-rich columnar cells that form the assimilatory tissue. A pore, the white dot on the surface, lies in the center of the roof of each chamber; it is surrounded by a barrel-shaped structure of four tiers of four cells each, has no guard cells, and cannot open or close.
- Storage tissue: several layers of large parenchyma cells containing starch or oil droplets; some are oil cells containing oil bodies, and mucilage canals may be seen.
- Lower epidermis: it bears purple-brown multicellular scales and two kinds of unicellular rhizoids (smooth rhizoids and tuberculate rhizoids with peg-like wall projections). Together they anchor the plant and take up and retain water, and capillary action along the bundles of rhizoids on the ventral side of the thallus conducts water forward.

Vegetative reproduction is mainly by gemmae.

**Definition 15.2.2 (Gemma)** A multicellular vegetative propagule produced on a bryophyte gametophyte that, once shed, grows directly into a new gametophyte is a **gemma**. The gemma of *Marchantia* is a flat, round, multicellular body with an indented growing point on each side; it is borne in a green cup-shaped structure, the gemma cup, on the dorsal midrib of the thallus.

When gemmae mature, the mucilaginous stalk at the base swells with water and the gemma detaches; raindrops splash it out of the cup, and it germinates into a new thallus of the same sex as the parent. In addition, when the older parts of the thallus die and decay, the young parts at the forks each become new plants.

*Marchantia* is dioicous. The male plant bears a long-stalked antheridiophore, whose disc-shaped receptacle usually has eight shallow lobes along its margin. The receptacle contains many antheridial chambers, each holding one antheridium and opening to the outside through a pore. The antheridium is ovoid with a short stalk and a wall one cell thick. When mature it takes up water, the cap cell at the tip opens, and the sperm pour out of the chamber opening; the sperm are slender with two long flagella and swim to the female plant in splashed raindrops or a water film.

The female plant bears an archegoniophore, whose umbrella-shaped receptacle has 8–10 pendent finger-like rays at the margin. Rows of archegonia hang inverted beneath the receptacle between the rays, and on both sides of each row is a thin, deeply fringed membrane, the involucre; together they protect the row of archegonia. The usual account is that the archegonia stand upright on the upper surface of the young receptacle, and after fertilization the center of the receptacle rises and the margin bends downward, so the archegonia come to hang beneath it. (Observations of living material by Bao Wenmei and colleagues did not reveal an upright stage, and they consider this process to need further study.)

**Sporophyte** After fertilization the zygote develops into an embryo within the archegonium, and the embryo differentiates into a foot, a seta, and a capsule. The young sporophyte is then protected by three layers of gametophyte tissue: the calyptra developed from the venter of the archegonium, a cup-shaped membrane outside the calyptra that grows from the base of the archegonium (the pseudoperianth), and the involucre around each row of archegonia. In the capsule, part of the sporogenous tissue undergoes meiosis to produce spores, and the rest does not divide but elongates into long cells with two helical thickenings, the elaters. When the capsule matures it turns from green to orange-yellow, and the seta elongates rapidly within a short time, pushing the capsule out beyond the pseudoperianth and involucre. Horseshoe-shaped thickenings of the wall cells at the top of the capsule make it split irregularly into 7–8 tooth-like valves, and the spores are scattered by the hygroscopic movements of the elaters. There are usually 10–20 sporophytes beneath one female receptacle.

The spores are of one kind but of two sexes, growing into male or female gametophytes. A spore germinates into a filamentous or globular protonema of a few cells. One cell becomes an apical cell, which divides obliquely on both sides to form a flat plate, which grows into a heart-shaped young thallus and finally differentiates air chambers and pores to become the mature thallus. The complete life cycle of *Marchantia* is: spore ($n$) → protonema → female and male gametophytes → antheridia and archegonia → sperm and egg → zygote ($2n$) → embryo → sporophyte (foot, seta, capsule) → meiosis of spore mother cells → spores.

*Marchantia* has become a **model organism** for studies of the development and evolution of land plants: it has a short life cycle, propagates easily by gemmae on culture medium, has a haploid gametophyte that is convenient for genetic analysis, and has a small genome with little redundancy (Bowman et al., 2017). Many regulatory factors that exist as multigene families in angiosperms (for example in auxin signaling and phytochrome) have only one or two copies in *Marchantia*, which makes it easier to study their ancestral function.

### 15.2.2. Other Thalloid Liverworts

- Other genera of Marchantiales: *Asterella* (Aytoniaceae); the snakeskin liverwort (*Conocephalum conicum*, Conocephalaceae; a large thallus with a snakeskin-like network of areolae, aromatic when crushed); and, in Ricciaceae, *Riccia* (a small thallus without gametangiophores, whose sporophyte is entirely embedded in the thallus, with no seta or foot, and whose spores are released only when the capsule wall disintegrates) and the floating *Ricciocarpos natans*. The sporophyte of *Riccia* is reduced to a capsule, the simplest sporophyte among liverworts, generally considered the result of secondary reduction.
- Metzgeriidae and Pelliidae: thalli thin, without internal air-chamber differentiation, often with a central midrib of elongated cells, as in *Metzgeria*, *Aneura*, and *Pellia*. The sporophyte arises on the dorsal side of the thallus (*Pellia*) or on short ventral or lateral branches (*Metzgeria*, *Aneura*), with a rather long seta and a capsule that splits into four valves.

### 15.2.3. Leafy Liverworts: *Porella*

Leafy liverworts (Jungermanniidae) are the largest group of liverworts and include the great majority of species. The gametophyte is a dorsiventrally flattened leafy shoot, growing prostrate, with two rows of lateral leaves and often a row of smaller ventral leaves (underleaves) on the underside. The leaves are one cell layer thick, without a midrib, and often deeply divided into two or more lobes. Even within the Jungermanniidae there are forms intermediate between thalloid and leafy (*Schiffneria*), while in the Metzgeriidae of the same class *Riccardia* and others are thalloid, showing that thalloid and leafy forms can grade into each other within liverworts.

*Porella* (Porellales, Porellaceae) grows in tufts on shaded, moist rock surfaces and tree trunks. The gametophyte is robust, with stems 5–8 cm long that are two to three times pinnately branched; the stem is round in cross section and without tissue differentiation. The leaves are in three rows. The two lateral rows are larger, and each lateral leaf is deeply divided into two lobes: the larger upper dorsal lobe is flat and spoon-shaped, and the smaller lower ventral lobe is tongue-shaped and parallel to the stem. The ventral row consists of underleaves resembling the ventral lobes, so that from below the leaves appear to be in "false five rows". The rhizoids are unicellular and arise at the bases of the underleaves.

*Porella* is dioicous. On male plants the leaves at the tips of short lateral branches form imbricate perigonial bracts, with long-stalked, round antheridia in their axils. On female plants the perichaetial bracts at the tips of short lateral branches enclose a cluster of archegonia, and the inner side of the bracts forms a tubular perianth. The sporophyte consists of a foot, a seta, and a capsule; the capsule is spherical with a wall of 2–6 cell layers. At maturity the seta elongates and pushes the capsule out beyond the calyptra and perianth, the capsule splits lengthwise into four valves, and the spores are scattered by the elaters.

### 15.2.4. Current Classification of Liverworts

The current classification of liverworts follows Söderström et al. (2016) and recognizes three classes:

| Class | Features | Main groups and representative genera |
|---|---|---|
| Haplomitriopsida | The earliest-diverging lineage; erect leafy shoots or thalli; *Haplomitrium* has no rhizoids and produces mucilage | *Haplomitrium*, *Treubia* |
| Marchantiopsida | Mostly thalli with air-chamber differentiation, often with gametangiophores | Marchantiales (*Marchantia*, *Asterella*, *Conocephalum*, *Riccia*, *Ricciocarpos*), Sphaerocarpales (*Sphaerocarpos*), Lunulariales (*Lunularia*), *Blasia* |
| Jungermanniopsida | Leafy shoots, or thalli without air chambers | Pelliidae (*Pellia*), Metzgeriidae (*Metzgeria*, *Aneura*), Jungermanniidae (Porellales: *Porella*, *Frullania*, *Radula*, *Lejeunea*; Jungermanniales: *Lepidozia*, *Scapania*, *Plagiochila*) |

Chinese textbooks (Hu Renliang, 1987) divide the liverworts into two subclasses, Marchantiidae and Jungermanniidae, with seven orders, and place the Takakiales among the liverworts. The gametophyte of *Takakia* looks like an alga, with leaves deeply divided into cylindrical segments, and it was first thought to be the most primitive liverwort. When its sporophyte was discovered in the 1990s, the capsule was found to have a columella and to open along a single spiral slit, as in mosses, and molecular phylogenies also place it at the base of the mosses (§15.3.6). Two species of *Takakia* occur in Xizang (Tibet), China.

## 15.3. Mosses

**Definition 15.3.1 (Mosses)** **Mosses** (Bryophyta) are bryophytes whose gametophyte is a radially symmetrical leafy shoot, with leaves usually having a midrib, multicellular uniseriate rhizoids, and a well-developed protonema, and whose sporophyte has a firm seta that elongates before the capsule matures and a capsule with a columella, usually opening by an operculum, and lacking elaters.

Mosses tolerate cold and drought better than liverworts and often form extensive communities in cold and temperate regions, on mountains, in tundra, and in bogs.

### 15.3.1. *Sphagnum* (Peat Moss)

*Sphagnum* (Sphagnopsida, Sphagnaceae) is the only large genus of its class, with about 300 species, about 50 of them in China, mainly in the mountains of the northeast and southwest. Peat mosses grow in acidic bogs and wet ground with a pH of 3.5–5.5 and often form extensive hummocks.

**Gametophyte** The gametophyte is gray-white, gray-yellow, or purple-red and has no rhizoids. The stem is erect, and the branches at the top are crowded into a head. The lateral branches occur in fascicles, each with spreading branches that turn upward and weaker pendent branches that lie against the stem and form a capillary system carrying water upward. The stem is divided into a cortex and a central cylinder; the cortex consists of large, transparent, dead cells that store water. The leaf is one cell layer thick, has no midrib, and consists of two kinds of cells (see the figure below):

- Green cells: narrow, elongated living cells containing chloroplasts, joined into a network, that carry out photosynthesis.
- Hyaline cells: large, colorless dead cells in the meshes of the network. Their walls have helical thickenings (which keep the cells from collapsing) and small pores through which they take up and store water rapidly.

This dimorphism of the leaf cells arises at the base of the young leaf: of several green cells, the central one loses its chloroplasts and dies, helical thickenings and pores appear in its wall, and the surrounding green cells elongate around it. The hyaline cells allow *Sphagnum* to absorb water equal to about 20 times its dry weight.

![The two cell types of the *Sphagnum* leaf (schematic). A: surface view of the leaf: large hyaline cells (dead, with helical thickenings and pores in the wall) form the meshes, and narrow green cells (living, with chloroplasts) form a network that encloses them. B: cross section of the leaf: the green cells are wedged between the swollen hyaline cells and are triangular or trapezoidal in section; the hyaline cells store water for the plant when they are filled.](./plots/sphagnum-leaf.py)

*Sphagnum* may be monoicous or dioicous. The antheridia are spherical and long-stalked, borne in the axils of perigonial leaves on male branches; one to four archegonia are borne at the tips of female branches. The sporophyte has a large foot and a very short true seta. As the sporophyte matures, the tip of the female branch that bears it elongates into a long leafless stalk that lifts the capsule above the perichaetial leaves; this stalk is gametophytic and is called the pseudopodium. The capsule is spherical and deep red-purple, with a round operculum at the top and no peristome. At its center is a hemispherical columella, over which the sporogenous tissue is draped like a cap (as in hornworts). As the capsule dries it contracts and compresses the air inside, until the operculum is blown off and the spores are shot out as a vortex ring to a height of more than 10 cm, into the air currents above (Whitaker and Edwards, 2010). Spores germinate first into a filamentous protonema and then a flat one, and buds arise at the margin of the flat protonema and grow into leafy gametophytes.

The upper part of a *Sphagnum* plant keeps growing while the lower part gradually dies. In a bog that is water-saturated, anoxic, acidic, and poor in nutrients, the dead parts are hard for bacteria to decompose and accumulate over a long time as peat. *Sphagnum* exchanges hydrogen ions for cations through galacturonic acid and other groups in its cell walls, further acidifying the surrounding water and suppressing other plants and microbes, and so "engineers" its own habitat. Peatlands cover only about 3% of the land surface but store about one third of soil organic carbon and are an important carbon reservoir; draining and mining peatlands releases large amounts of $\ce{CO2}$.

### 15.3.2. *Andreaea*

*Andreaea* (Andreaeopsida), the granite mosses, has about 100 species, about 6 of them in China, and grows on acidic rocks such as granite in mountains, cold regions, and the polar regions. The gametophyte is a small leafy shoot, brown or black. The stem consists entirely of thick-walled cells and has no central strand. The leaf cells have thick walls, are often papillose, and contain oil droplets; the midrib is 1–2-ribbed or reduced. The seta of the sporophyte is very short, and the foot is inserted in a pseudopodium formed by an extension of the gametophyte. The capsule is elongate-ovoid, with a calyptra, no operculum, and a central columella. At maturity the capsule wall splits along four (rarely 6–8) longitudinal slits that remain joined at the top and base, like a small lantern that opens when dry and closes when moist. This mode of dehiscence is unique among mosses.

### 15.3.3. *Funaria*

*Funaria hygrometrica* (Bryopsida, Funariaceae) is a small, terrestrial, nitrophilous moss common in fields, along roads, and on burned ground in woods. The plants are about 1–3 cm tall; the gourd-shaped capsule gives the plant its Chinese name, and the epithet *hygrometrica* refers to the twisting of the seta in response to humidity.

**Gametophyte** The gametophyte is an erect leafy shoot with a short stem and uniseriate rhizoids at its base. The stem is differentiated into epidermis, cortex, and a central strand, whose cells are elongated lengthwise but do not form true conducting tissue. At the stem tip is an inverted-pyramid-shaped apical cell that cuts off new cells on three sides to form leaves and stem. The leaves are clustered on the upper part of the stem, ovate or tongue-shaped, and, apart from the midrib, one cell layer thick.

*Funaria* is monoicous, but antheridia and archegonia are borne on different branches: antheridia at the tip of the main branch, and archegonia at the tips of lateral branches. Formation of the sex organs requires a period of low temperature (below about 10 °C). The leaves at the tip of the male branch are larger and spread outward like a small green flower; this is the perigonium, containing about 50 club-shaped antheridia and many paraphyses. Paraphyses are uniseriate filaments whose terminal cell is swollen and spherical and contains chloroplasts; they retain water and protect the antheridia. The leaves at the tip of the female branch are more tightly folded, like a bud; this is the perichaetium, containing several stalked archegonia whose long neck makes up about two thirds of the archegonium. Several archegonia in a perichaetium may be fertilized, but only one develops into a sporophyte.

**Sporophyte** The zygote develops into an embryo within the archegonium, and the embryo differentiates into a foot, a seta, and a capsule. The seta elongates rapidly and tears the archegonium at its base; the upper part rises with the capsule and covers its top as the calyptra. The calyptra is hood-shaped, with a long beak, and falls off by itself after the capsule matures. The mature capsule is bent and pendent and has three parts (see the figure below):

- Operculum: the lid at the top of the capsule, made of a single layer of thick-walled cells.
- Urn: the main body of the capsule. The outermost layer is the epidermis; within it is a capsule wall several cell layers thick containing air spaces traversed by filaments of assimilatory cells. At the center is the columella, and between the columella and the wall lies the sporogenous tissue (archesporium), which produces the spore mother cells that form spores by meiosis.
- Apophysis: the base of the capsule, whose epidermis bears many stomata and which contains chloroplast-bearing parenchyma capable of photosynthesis.

**Definition 15.3.2 (Operculum, Peristome, and Columella)** The lid-like part at the top of a moss capsule that falls off at maturity and exposes the mouth of the capsule is the **operculum**; the tooth-like structures inside the mouth, made of cell wall remnants or of whole cells, arranged in one or two rings and able to open and close with changes in humidity, are the **peristome**; and the central column of sterile cells in the capsule is the **columella**. The ring of thickened epidermal cells at the junction of the operculum and the urn is the annulus.

At maturity the thin-walled cells of the annulus shrink on drying, the operculum falls off, and the peristome is exposed. The *Funaria* peristome has two rings (outer and inner), of 16 teeth each, and the tips of the outer teeth are joined into a disc. When dry, 16 slits open between the teeth and the spores are released through them; when moist, the teeth press against each other and cover the mouth of the capsule. Spores are therefore released gradually in dry weather, which suits wind dispersal. *Funaria* spores remain viable for many years under dry conditions.

**Protonema** Under suitable conditions spores germinate in a day or two, and the protruding part divides into a uniseriate branching filament. The protonema has two kinds of filaments: chloronemata, which grow along the substrate surface, contain many ellipsoidal chloroplasts, and have cross walls perpendicular to the long axis; and caulonemata, which grow faster, have fewer, spindle-shaped chloroplasts, and have oblique cross walls. Colorless branches that grow into the substrate are rhizoids. Buds arise from the caulonemata, each with an apical cell, and grow into small leafy gametophytes. A protonema grown from one spore can produce more than 100 leafy shoots within three months, which is why mosses often grow in extensive patches.

### 15.3.4. *Polytrichum*: A Moss "Vascular Tissue"

*Polytrichum commune* (Polytrichopsida), the common haircap moss, is a large moss 10–30 cm tall that grows under forest and at bog margins; *Dawsonia superba* of New Zealand, the tallest moss, reaches more than 50 cm. Both gametophyte and sporophyte have well-differentiated conducting tissues:

- Hydroids: elongated dead cells in the central strand of the stem, with thin walls, no lignin, and oblique, partly dissolved end walls; they transport water and resemble tracheids in function.
- Leptoids: elongated living cells surrounding the hydroids, with oblique end walls with plasmodesmata and degenerate nuclei; they transport organic compounds and resemble sieve elements in function.

In addition, the upper surface of the *Polytrichum* leaf bears many longitudinal green lamellae that increase the photosynthetic area and are covered by the inrolled leaf when dry. The capsule is four-angled, and the calyptra is densely covered with long hairs (hence the name haircap moss). The peristome consists of whole cells (nematodontous) in 64 teeth joined at the tips by a membrane (the epiphragm), and the spores escape through small pores between the teeth.

Hydroids and leptoids lack lignified secondary walls and differ from the tracheids and sieve elements of vascular plants in development and structure. On the monophyletic tree of bryophytes they are conducting tissues that evolved independently within mosses and are not homologous to the vascular tissue of vascular plants; older views regarded them as precursors of vascular tissue.

### 15.3.5. *Physcomitrium patens*: A Model Organism

*Physcomitrium patens* (formerly *Physcomitrella patens*, Funariaceae) is the **model organism** of moss research. Its life cycle takes about three months in the laboratory, and the haploid protonema can be propagated in large quantities on culture medium. Its outstanding feature is a very high efficiency of **homologous recombination**: when DNA fragments homologous to the genome are introduced, they replace the target gene precisely, as in yeast (Schaefer and Zrÿd, 1997). It was therefore the first plant in which **gene targeting** could be done routinely, and its genome was published in 2008 (Rensing et al., 2008). *Physcomitrium patens* is used to study apical cells and stem cells, the transition from protonema to bud, the actions of auxin and cytokinin, tolerance of drought and osmotic stress, and the origin of the sporophyte developmental program ([7.12.14](/7.12.14#sec-14.5.3)).

### 15.3.6. Current Classification of Mosses

The current classification of mosses follows the system of Goffinet and Buck (Goffinet et al., 2009, and later revisions) and recognizes eight classes, of which Bryopsida contains more than 95% of the species:

| Class | Features | Representative genera |
|---|---|---|
| Takakiopsida | Leaves deeply divided into cylindrical segments; capsule opening along a spiral slit; sister to all other mosses | *Takakia* |
| Sphagnopsida | Leaf cells dimorphic, pseudopodium, operculum bursting off | *Sphagnum* |
| Andreaeopsida | Saxicolous, pseudopodium, capsule splitting into 4 longitudinal valves | *Andreaea* |
| Andreaeobryopsida | Capsule splitting lengthwise, with a true seta | *Andreaeobryum* |
| Oedipodiopsida | No peristome | *Oedipodium* |
| Polytrichopsida | Hydroids and leptoids, lamellae on the leaf, nematodontous peristome | *Polytrichum*, *Pogonatum*, *Atrichum*, *Dawsonia* |
| Tetraphidopsida | Four nematodontous peristome teeth | *Tetraphis* |
| Bryopsida | Arthrodontous peristome (made of cell wall remnants), single or double | *Funaria*, *Physcomitrium*, *Bryum*, *Mnium*, *Dicranum*, *Hypnum* |

The peristome is the main basis of moss classification. A nematodontous peristome consists of whole dead cells (Polytrichopsida, Tetraphidopsida); an arthrodontous peristome consists only of the wall remnants of adjacent cells, with transverse articulations on the teeth, and is either haplolepidous, with a single ring of teeth (Dicranales), or diplolepidous, with an outer and inner ring (the bryalean mosses, to which *Funaria* belongs). Chinese textbooks divide the Bryopsida into the subclasses Sphagnidae, Andreaeidae, and Bryidae, and the Bryidae into the bryalean mosses, the *Buxbaumia* group (cigar-stalk mosses), and the polytrichalean mosses (Chen Bangjie and colleagues). In this system the Sphagnidae and Andreaeidae correspond to the current classes, the polytrichalean mosses to the Polytrichopsida, and the bryalean mosses roughly to the current Bryopsida.

## 15.4. Hornworts

**Definition 15.4.1 (Hornworts)** **Hornworts** (Anthocerotophyta) are bryophytes whose gametophyte is a simple thallus, whose cells usually contain a single large chloroplast (usually with a pyrenoid), whose sex organs are embedded in the thallus, and whose sporophyte has no seta, is horn-shaped, and grows continuously from a basal meristem, with a columella, pseudoelaters, and stomata.

There are about 220 species of hornworts in five families and about 12 genera. Common genera are *Anthoceros*, *Phaeoceros*, *Notothylas*, and *Megaceros*; they grow mostly on shaded, moist, clayey soil and are common in ditches, rice fields, and cultivated land.

**Gametophyte** The gametophyte is a bright green, dorsiventral thallus a few millimeters to a few centimeters across, with notched margins and unicellular rhizoids on the lower surface and no scales. The thallus is 5–10 cell layers thick and has no internal tissue differentiation; growth is from an apical cell. The most distinctive cellular feature of hornworts is that each cell usually has a single large chloroplast containing a pyrenoid, as in most green algae; among land plants only hornworts have this. (Pyrenoids have been lost and regained several times in hornworts, and some groups, such as *Megaceros*, have none.) On the lower surface of the thallus are mucilage-filled cavities inhabited by nitrogen-fixing *Nostoc*, which supplies nitrogen to the hornwort.

Most hornworts are monoicous. The antheridia occur in groups of 2–4 in antheridial chambers within the thallus, arise from subepidermal cells (endogenous), and are stalked. The archegonia are embedded in the thallus with only the tip of the neck exposed, and the neck canal has about five neck canal cells.

**Sporophyte** The sporophyte is long and needle-like, like a small erect horn. A well-developed foot is embedded at the base in the thallus, and the capsule rises directly from the foot with no seta; the base of the sporophyte is surrounded by a sheath of gametophyte tissue. An intercalary meristem at the base of the capsule keeps producing new tissue, so the sporophyte can elongate for months. The capsule wall is several cell layers thick, and its epidermis has stomata, each surrounded by two guard cells. The wall cells contain chloroplasts and photosynthesize, so the hornwort sporophyte is only partly parasitic and can live independently for a time after the gametophyte dies. At the center of the capsule is a columella of sterile cells, and the sporogenous tissue surrounds the columella like a long tube, producing spores and pseudoelaters made of 3–5 cells joined together, with irregularly thickened walls. The spores mature from the top downward; the capsule splits lengthwise into two valves starting at the tip, the slit gradually extending downward, and the spores are scattered by the twisting of the pseudoelaters.

The hornwort gametophyte resembles an alga in structure, while its sporophyte is the most "vascular-plant-like" of the bryophytes (continuous growth, stomata, photosynthesis); this duality once led to its being regarded as a transitional group between bryophytes and vascular plants. The hornwort genome (Li et al., 2020) shows that the genes of its pyrenoid-associated $\ce{CO2}$-concentrating mechanism are homologous to those of green algae, and it has special genes associated with cyanobacterial symbiosis. Phylogenetically, hornworts lie outside the clade of liverworts and mosses and not as the sister group of vascular plants.

## 15.5. Origin and Evolution of Bryophytes

### 15.5.1. The Two Older Hypotheses

Chinese textbooks record two hypotheses on the origin of bryophytes:

- Green algal origin: the bryophyte chloroplast resembles that of green algae, with the same chlorophylls and xanthophylls and starch as the storage product; hornworts have pyrenoids; the protonema closely resembles a filamentous green alga; the sperm have two equal flagella; the zygote develops within the gametophyte, as in *Coleochaete*; and the oogonia and antheridia of charophytes can be compared with archegonia and antheridia. *Fritschiella* (heterotrichous, with subterranean rhizoids and aerial erect branches) and the alga-like *Takakia* were taken as intermediates.
- Reduction from psilophytes: the gametophyte-dominant bryophytes were derived by reduction from early vascular plants with a dominant sporophyte ("psilophytes"). The arguments were that *Rhynia* and *Horneophyton* have no true roots or leaves, only rhizoids on a creeping stem; that the sporangia of *Horneophyton* and *Sporogonites* contain a central axis like a columella; that, on the telome theory, branched sporangia would be transformed into a concentrated sporangium, with the occasional malformed forked capsule in *Bryum* cited in support; and that psilophyte fossils were then believed to appear in the Silurian, earlier than bryophytes.

The two hypotheses lead to different inferences about which of the liverworts, mosses, and hornworts is most primitive. If bryophytes arose from a disc-like green alga of the *Coleochaete* type, the dorsiventral thallus should have come first (liverworts first); if from an erect charophyte-like alga, the radially symmetrical leafy shoot should have come first (mosses first); if from reduction of psilophytes, hornworts, with the most developed sporophyte and the simplest gametophyte, would be the most primitive.

### 15.5.2. Phylogeny Rewrites the Story

Modern phylogenies and fossil evidence give a different picture:

- Land plants arose from streptophyte algae, whose closest living relatives are the Zygnematophyceae. *Fritschiella* in the "green algal origin" hypothesis belongs to the green algae (Chlorophyta) and has nothing to do with land plants, and *Takakia* is a moss, not a liverwort.
- The earliest fossil evidence for land plants is the cryptospores of the Middle Ordovician, similar in form to liverwort spores and older than any fossil of a vascular plant ([7.12.14](/7.12.14#sec-14.5.4)); the chronology on which the "reduction from psilophytes" hypothesis rested does not hold. A "Chinese *Parafunaria*" (*Parafunaria sinensis*; Yang Ruidong et al., 2004), resembling *Funaria*, has been reported from the Cambrian of Guizhou, and on that basis bryophytes were said to have appeared more than 100 million years before vascular plants, but neither the plant nature nor the identification of these specimens has been generally accepted, and they should not be used as evidence.
- Liverworts, mosses, and hornworts form a monophyletic group: liverworts and mosses are sister groups, and hornworts are sister to both (Puttick et al., 2018; One Thousand Plant Transcriptomes Initiative, 2019). The question of which of the three is the most primitive must therefore itself be reformulated: the three are equally old living lineages, none is the ancestor of the other two, and each combines ancestral and derived characters.

Within this framework one can reconstruct the characters of the common ancestor of embryophytes. A gametophyte-dominant life cycle with an unbranched sporophyte is shared by all three bryophyte phyla, but whether it is an ancestral character of embryophytes or a derived character of bryophytes depends on what the earliest ancestor of vascular plants looked like. The gametophytes of early polysporangiophytes in the Rhynie chert have branched erect axes, and their sporophytes are branched and independent, which shows that the earliest land plants were not necessarily like modern bryophytes; the bryophyte sporophyte may have undergone simplification (Harris et al., 2020).

The problem of stomata is rewritten as well ([7.12.4](/7.12.4#sec-4.3.2)). The sporophytes of mosses and hornworts have stomata and those of liverworts do not. If bryophytes are monophyletic, the most parsimonious explanation is that the common ancestor of embryophytes already had stomata and that liverworts (and some mosses) lost them during evolution, rather than mosses, hornworts, and vascular plants each having evolved stomata independently. Moss genes that control stomatal development are homologous to *SPEECHLESS*, *MUTE*, and *FAMA* of angiosperms, which also supports homology of stomata. By contrast, the hydroids and leptoids of mosses (§15.3.4) evolved independently within mosses and are not precursors of vascular tissue.

Although bryophytes have differentiated stems and leaves, these are simple in construction, with no lignified vascular tissue and no true roots, and fertilization depends on water. A life cycle centered on the gametophyte limits their body size: the gametophyte must stay close to water and the ground to ensure fertilization, so bryophytes have never become tall plants. Older textbooks therefore called bryophytes a "blind branch" of plant evolution. It is more accurate to say that bryophytes represent a different way of life on land, evolving in parallel with vascular plants: small, desiccation-tolerant gametophytes occupy microhabitats where vascular plants have difficulty living (rock faces, bark, tundra, peat bogs), and in these habitats they remain dominant today.

## 15.6. Ecological and Economic Importance

In nature, bryophytes have the following roles:

- Pioneer plants: together with lichens, bryophytes are the first to grow on bare rock, sand, burned ground, and newly exposed rock strata. They secrete acids that dissolve rock, and their remains accumulate as the first soil, creating conditions for other plants to become established.
- Water retention and soil conservation: dense tufts of bryophytes can absorb 15–20 times their dry weight in water while evaporating far less than an open water surface, conserving water and preventing soil erosion; moss layers on the forest floor and in tundra regulate soil temperature and moisture.
- Succession of lakes, bogs, and forests: *Sphagnum*, *Calliergon*, *Drepanocladus*, and others grow in extensive stands at lake margins and in bogs. Their tops extend year by year while the lower parts die and accumulate, so lakes and bogs gradually become land and give way to grassland and forest; where the air is very humid, the water storage of *Sphagnum* can in turn waterlog the ground, killing trees and forming raised bogs.
- Carbon reservoir: the organic carbon stored in peatlands is comparable to the carbon in the global atmosphere (§15.3.1); the moss layers of boreal forest and tundra are also important carbon sinks.
- Symbiosis: many bryophytes are symbiotic with nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria (hornworts with *Nostoc*; cyanobacteria growing on moss leaves in boreal forests are an important source of nitrogen input to these forests), and liverworts and hornworts form associations with fungi similar to **arbuscular mycorrhiza**.
- Indicator plants: particular bryophyte species often occur in particular habitats and serve as general indicators. For example, in northern China *Sphagnum* is common in larch and fir forests and *Polytrichum* in Korean pine and spruce forests; after a forest has been destroyed, the original forest type can be inferred from the remaining bryophytes. Bryophyte leaves are one cell layer thick and usually have no cuticle, so pollutants enter the cells directly and bryophytes are very sensitive to air pollution such as sulfur dioxide; epiphytic bryophytes on tree trunks can be used to monitor air pollution.

The main economic uses of bryophytes are:

- Peat: used as fuel, fertilizer, and horticultural substrate (seedling mix and potting media), and an important energy source in some countries; but peat forms over thousands of years, and large-scale extraction is not sustainable.
- *Sphagnum*: it absorbs water quickly and in large amounts, and replaced cotton as a surgical dressing in World War I; in horticulture it is used to pack and ship seedlings, to cover seedbeds, and to grow orchids.
- Medicinal use: in Chinese folk medicine *Polytrichum* (tumazong), *Rhodobryum giganteum*, and others are used, and extracts of some mosses have antibacterial activity.
- Chinese gallnut: the sumac gall aphid forms galls (Chinese gallnuts) on sumac (*Rhus chinensis*), but its overwintering hosts are mosses such as *Mnium*, so gallnut production requires protecting and cultivating these mosses as well.
- Ornamental use: in Japan and elsewhere, gardens are designed with moss (moss gardens), and moss is often used to cover the soil surface in bonsai.

## Summary

- Bryophytes (Definition 15.1.1) are embryophytes in which the gametophyte (Definition 15.1.2) is dominant, the sporophyte is attached to the gametophyte, and there is no vascular tissue. The gametophyte develops from a protonema (Definition 15.1.3), is thalloid or leafy (Definition 15.1.4), and is anchored by rhizoids (Definition 15.1.5); the sex organs are multicellular archegonia and antheridia (Definition 15.1.6), and fertilization requires water. The sporophyte consists of foot, seta, and capsule (Definition 15.1.7), and the embryo is nourished by the placenta of the gametophyte.
- Liverworts (Definition 15.2.1): thalloid or dorsiventrally flattened leafy gametophytes, oil bodies, a sporophyte without stomata or columella and with elaters; *Marchantia* reproduces vegetatively by gemmae (Definition 15.2.2) and sexually by female and male gametangiophores; *Porella* has dorsal lobes, ventral lobes, and underleaves.
- Mosses (Definition 15.3.1): radially symmetrical leafy shoots, a well-developed protonema, a firm seta, and a capsule with operculum, peristome, and columella (Definition 15.3.2); *Sphagnum* has dimorphic leaf cells and a pseudopodium, *Andreaea* a capsule splitting into four valves, *Funaria* a diplolepidous peristome, and *Polytrichum* hydroids and leptoids; *Physcomitrium patens* is a model organism for gene targeting.
- Hornworts (Definition 15.4.1): thallus cells with a single large chloroplast, usually with a pyrenoid, symbiosis with *Nostoc*, a sporophyte that grows continuously from a basal meristem and has a columella, pseudoelaters, and stomata.
- Bryophytes are monophyletic, with liverworts and mosses as sister groups and hornworts sister to both; the "green algal origin" and "reduction from psilophytes" hypotheses have both been rewritten by phylogeny; stomata may be a character of the common ancestor of embryophytes, whereas moss conducting tissue evolved independently.

## Questions

1. Which of the following structures of *Funaria* belong to the gametophyte and which to the sporophyte: calyptra, operculum, peristome, seta, rhizoids, paraphyses, protonema, spores? What about the gemmae, pseudoperianth, and elaters of *Marchantia*?

   **Answer** *Funaria*: the calyptra is formed from the upper part of the torn archegonium and is gametophytic; the operculum, peristome, and seta are parts of the sporophyte; rhizoids, paraphyses, and protonema are gametophytic; spores are products of meiosis and are the first cell of the gametophyte generation (haploid). *Marchantia*: gemmae are gametophytic; the pseudoperianth grows from the base of the archegonium and is gametophytic; elaters differentiate from sporogenous cells in the capsule that do not undergo meiosis and are sporophytic (diploid).

2. Why can bryophytes not grow as tall as vascular plants? Using *Polytrichum* as an example, explain what mosses have achieved in this respect and its limits.

   **Answer** The body of a bryophyte is the gametophyte, which has no lignified vascular tissue and no true roots; water moves mainly through capillary spaces on the surface or by diffusion between cells, and support depends only on cell wall turgor and a few thick-walled cells; sperm must swim through water to the archegonium, so the gametophyte must stay close to the ground and to water. The stem of *Polytrichum* has hydroids that transport water and leptoids that transport organic compounds, and the leaves bear lamellae, so it can reach several tens of centimeters. But hydroids lack lignified secondary walls and cannot withstand the negative pressures and weight of a tall plant, the sporophyte is only an unbranched capsule, and fertilization still needs water, so *Polytrichum* remains a plant at most a few tens of centimeters tall.

3. Compare the elaters of liverworts with the pseudoelaters of hornworts, and the pseudopodium of *Sphagnum* with the seta of *Funaria*.

   **Answer** Elaters are unicellular, elongated dead cells with helical wall thickenings, formed from sporogenous cells that do not undergo meiosis, and are sporophytic; pseudoelaters consist of 3–5 cells joined together with irregularly thickened walls, are also sterile cells of the sporophyte, but are multicellular. Both twist with changes in humidity and help to scatter spores. The seta of *Funaria* is part of the sporophyte and elongates before the capsule matures; in *Sphagnum* the true seta is very short, and it is the pseudopodium, formed by elongation of the tip of the female branch, that lifts the capsule, and it is gametophytic.

4. Why does phylogenetic research hold that the question of which of the liverworts, mosses, and hornworts is the most primitive needs to be reformulated? How does this affect our understanding of the evolution of stomata?

   **Answer** If bryophytes were paraphyletic, the group that diverged first (for example liverworts) would retain the most ancestral characters, and one could ask "which is most primitive". The current phylogeny shows that bryophytes are monophyletic and that liverworts, mosses, and hornworts are equally old lineages in the crown group of embryophytes, each combining ancestral and derived characters, none being the ancestor of the other two. For stomata, the absence of stomata in liverworts was formerly taken as the primitive condition, with stomata appearing only in the common ancestor of mosses, hornworts, and vascular plants; on the monophyletic bryophyte tree, mosses, hornworts, and vascular plants all have stomata, and the most parsimonious explanation is that the common ancestor of embryophytes already had them and that they were lost in liverworts, which is also supported by homology of the stomatal development genes.

5. Peatlands cover only about 3% of the land surface. Why are they so important in the carbon cycle? How does *Sphagnum* maintain an environment favorable to peat accumulation?

   **Answer** In peatlands the decomposition of plant remains has long been slower than production, so organic carbon accumulates year after year as peat; over thousands of years about one third of soil organic carbon has been stored there. When a peatland is drained or mined, the peat is oxidized and becomes a source of atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$. The hyaline cells of *Sphagnum* store large amounts of water, keeping the hummock water-saturated and anoxic; polysaccharides in the cell walls exchange hydrogen ions and acidify the water; the remains of *Sphagnum* are themselves hard to decompose, and the habitat is nutrient-poor. Water saturation, anoxia, acidity, and low nutrients together inhibit decomposition by bacteria and fungi and also suppress competition from other plants.
