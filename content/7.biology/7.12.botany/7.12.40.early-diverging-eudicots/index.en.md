The eudicots make up about three quarters of all angiosperm species. From buttercups and poppies to roses, legumes and daisies, nearly all of the familiar "dicots" belong to this one clade. Its clearest synapomorphy is tricolpate pollen. Phylogenetically, a few small lineages branch off first, in succession: Ranunculales, Proteales, Trochodendrales and Buxales, together called the early-diverging eudicots. The remainder form the core eudicots, from which Gunnerales and then Dilleniales branch off first. In Cronquist's system these groups are scattered among the subclasses Magnoliidae (Ranunculales, Papaverales, and Nelumbonaceae in Nymphaeales), Hamamelidae (Trochodendrales, Platanaceae), Rosidae (Proteaceae, Gunneraceae, Buxaceae) and Dilleniidae (Dilleniaceae) ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.5.2)). The resemblance of Ranunculales to Magnoliales, with numerous free, spirally arranged floral parts, was once taken as evidence that both belong to the "apocarpous" lineage. Phylogenetic analyses show that Ranunculales lie far from the magnoliids ([7.12.35](/7.12.35)); the similarity consists of shared ancestral characters. In this chapter we first treat the circumscription of the eudicots (§40.1), then Ranunculales (§40.2), Proteales (§40.3), Trochodendrales, Buxales and Gunnerales (§40.4), and finally Dilleniales, whose position is unresolved (§40.5). The two great branches of the core eudicots, the superrosids and the superasterids, begin with [7.12.41](/7.12.41). For how floral formulas are written, see [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.6).

## 40.1. Circumscription of the Eudicots

### 40.1.1. Tricolpate Pollen

**Definition 40.1.1 (Eudicots)** The monophyletic group of angiosperms whose pollen has three germination furrows (tricolpate, tricolporate, or types derived from these) is called the **eudicots**, also known as the tricolpates. Together with the monocots, magnoliids, Chloranthales and Ceratophyllales, it belongs to the Mesangiospermae.

The pollen of the ancestral angiosperm was monosulcate, with a single germination **aperture** on the distal face ([7.12.28](/7.12.28#sec-28.3.3)), and the ANA grade, magnoliids and monocots retain this condition. The three furrows of tricolpate pollen are equally spaced around the equator and bear a fixed relation to the contact points between neighboring grains in the tetrad. A pollen tube can emerge from any of the furrows, so the grain can germinate whichever face lands on the stigma; this may be one reason for the success of the eudicots. In later evolution, the three furrows gave rise to tricolporate (Rosaceae, Fabaceae), triporate (Urticaceae), polyporate and pantoporate (Caryophyllaceae, Amaranthaceae) and inaperturate types. "Eudicots" therefore denotes the group whose ancestor had tricolpate pollen, and not every member has such pollen.

Tricolpate pollen is also the most easily recognized marker of an angiosperm group in the fossil record. It first appears at about the Barremian–Aptian boundary of the Early Cretaceous (about 125 Ma), slightly later than monosulcate angiosperm pollen, and becomes abundant during the Albian. *Leefructus mirus* (about 125 Ma) from the Yixian Formation of Liaoning is a herb preserved with leaves and fruits; its leaves are deeply lobed and the receptacle carries five free carpels. It is regarded as a stem-group member of Ranunculaceae and shows that the eudicot crown group had already diversified in the Early Cretaceous.

### 40.1.2. The Phylogenetic Backbone

Apart from tricolpate pollen, the eudicots have few morphological synapomorphies. Their circumscription rests mainly on molecular data, from early *rbcL* sequences to plastid genomes and nuclear genomes, with consistent results and very high support. In APG IV the eudicots divide into the following lineages, in order of divergence (Fig. 40.1.1):

**Definition 40.1.2 (Core eudicots and Pentapetalae)** The monophyletic group of eudicots that remains after removing Ranunculales, Proteales, Trochodendrales and Buxales is called the **core eudicots**, with the phylogenetic name Gunneridae. The monophyletic group of core eudicots that remains after removing Gunnerales is called the **Pentapetalae**; it comprises Dilleniales, the superrosids and the superasterids.

The core eudicots are characterized mainly in three respects:

- Floral parts are definite in number, often pentamerous, with calyx and corolla clearly differentiated and each forming a whorl (a synapomorphy of the Pentapetalae, whose name means "five petals"; tetramerous flowers were derived from them several times). The flowers of the early-diverging eudicots are mostly dimerous or trimerous, with the perianth often undifferentiated or absent.
- Chemically, ellagic acid and gallic acid are widespread (they are essentially absent from the early-diverging eudicots).
- Genomically, they went through an ancient whole-genome triplication (the gamma event). The grape genome (2007) first showed that the extant genome is built from three sets of ancestral chromosomes, and this event is shared by all core eudicots; the lotus genome lacks it, so it occurred after the divergence of Proteales ([7.10.18](/7.10.18)). It is one of the best-known examples of **whole-genome duplication** in angiosperms.

Ranunculales, Proteales, Trochodendrales and Buxales branch off in succession and form the early-diverging eudicots; the rest are the core eudicots, from which Gunnerales branch off first, followed by the Pentapetalae (Dilleniales, superrosids and superasterids; the position of Dilleniales is unresolved). The flower structure of the early-diverging eudicots is rather unstable: the floral merism is 2, 3, 4, 5 or numerous, the perianth is sometimes differentiated into sepals and petals and sometimes has a single whorl or none, and both spiral and whorled arrangements occur. In this respect they resemble the ANA grade and the magnoliids, which suggests that the combination of definite number, whorled arrangement and pentamery became fixed only in the core eudicots ([7.12.34](/7.12.34#sec-34.3.3)).

## 40.2. Ranunculales

### 40.2.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Ranunculales are the sister group of all other eudicots, with about 200 genera and 4500 species, mostly herbs of the north temperate zone. In APG IV the order includes seven families: Eupteleaceae, Papaveraceae, Circaeasteraceae, Lardizabalaceae, Menispermaceae, Berberidaceae and Ranunculaceae. Eupteleaceae are sister to all the others, followed by Papaveraceae; the remaining five families form a clade in which Lardizabalaceae + Circaeasteraceae and Menispermaceae + (Berberidaceae + Ranunculaceae) are sister groups.

Common characters of Ranunculales:

- Mostly herbs or woody climbers; woody trees are few.
- They contain benzylisoquinoline **alkaloids**, such as berberine (yellow, which makes the roots and wood of *Coptis* and *Berberis* yellow) and the morphinan alkaloids.
- Sieve-element plastids are of the S type; the leaf surface bears tubular wax crystals.
- Flowers are often trimerous (or dimerous), with free parts, numerous stamens and often numerous free carpels.
- Except in Eupteleaceae and Papaveraceae, every family has, at least in some species, nectar-secreting petals derived from stamens (honey leaves, see §40.2.3); this may be a synapomorphy of the clade of five families.
- Seeds have a small embryo and abundant endosperm.

Changes in circumscription: Engler's Ranunculales (Ranales) was a very large "apocarpous" assemblage that included Magnoliaceae, Lauraceae, Nymphaeaceae and others; Cronquist divided it into Magnoliales, Laurales, Ranunculales, Papaverales, Nymphaeales and others within the subclass Magnoliidae. Cronquist's Ranunculales (Ranunculaceae, Berberidaceae, Sargentodoxaceae, Lardizabalaceae, Menispermaceae, Sabiaceae, Coriariaceae, Circaeasteraceae) changed in three ways in APG IV: Sargentodoxaceae are included in Lardizabalaceae; Sabiaceae move to Proteales (§40.3.5); and Coriariaceae move to Cucurbitales ([7.12.43](/7.12.43)). Papaverales (Papaveraceae and Fumariaceae) are merged into Papaveraceae in the broad sense, also placed in Ranunculales; Eupteleaceae come from Hamamelidales; and *Paeonia* is removed from Ranunculaceae and forms Paeoniaceae in Saxifragales ([7.12.41](/7.12.41)).

### 40.2.2. Papaveraceae

In APG IV, Papaveraceae are circumscribed broadly to include the former Papaveraceae and Fumariaceae, with about 44 genera and more than 760 species, mainly in the north temperate zone. The family has three subfamilies: Pteridophylloideae (only *Pteridophyllum* from East Asia), Papaveroideae, and Fumarioideae (including *Hypecoum*). The flowers of the two large subfamilies look very different (Fig. 40.2.1), but they share the following: 2 early-falling sepals; 4 petals (2 + 2), often crumpled in the bud; a gynoecium of 2 to many fused carpels, ovary superior, 1-locular, with parietal placentation; small seeds with abundant endosperm and a small embryo; and isoquinoline alkaloids in the plant body.

Diagnostic characters of Papaveroideae:

- Herbs, rarely shrubs or small trees; laticifers present, and white, yellow or orange-red latex flows from broken parts.
- Leaves alternate, often divided, without stipules.
- Flowers large, solitary, actinomorphic, bisexual; sepals 2 (rarely 3), early-falling; petals 4–6 (rarely 8–12, or absent), in two whorls, crumpled in the bud; stamens numerous, free.
- Gynoecium of 2 to many fused carpels, ovary superior, 1-locular, the parietal placentas sometimes projecting deeply into the center as incomplete partitions; stigmas arranged radially in a stigmatic disc at the top of the ovary (*Papaver*) or 2-lobed.
- Capsule, porose (*Papaver*: a ring of small pores below the stigmatic disc releases the seeds, like a pepper shaker) or valvate; the capsule of *Chelidonium* is long and slender, opening from the base upward by two valves, resembling the silique of Brassicaceae.

Floral formula: $\ast K_2 C_{2+2} A_\infty \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}\infty:1:\infty)}$.

Papaveroideae have about 23 genera and 230 species, of which 12 genera and 67 species occur in China. Representative plants:

- Opium poppy (*Papaver somniferum*): an annual herb, glaucous throughout, with clasping leaves and large red, purple or white flowers. It is native to the eastern Mediterranean and western Asia. The latex that flows from cuts in the unripe capsule dries to opium, which contains more than 30 alkaloids, including morphine, codeine, thebaine, papaverine and noscapine; morphine and codeine are important analgesics and antitussives, and heroin is the diacetyl derivative of morphine. Cultivation of the opium poppy is strictly controlled in China. The seeds (which contain no alkaloids) yield oil or are used as food.
- Corn poppy (*P. rhoeas*): an annual herb with bristly hairs and 4 red petals that often have a black spot at the base; native to Europe and grown as an ornamental. Arctic poppy (*P. nudicaule*) has yellow or orange flowers and all leaves basal, and occurs from northeastern through northern to northwestern China; oriental poppy (*P. orientale*) is a perennial, large-flowered ornamental species.
- Greater celandine (*Chelidonium majus*): a perennial herb with orange-yellow latex; leaves pinnately parted to the base; flowers yellow in umbels, with 4 petals; capsule slender and silique-like. It occurs from northeastern and northern China to Xinjiang and Sichuan; the whole plant is poisonous and used medicinally.
- Plume poppy (*Macleaya cordata*): a large perennial herb with yellow latex; leaves broadly ovate, shallowly palmately lobed, glaucous beneath; large panicles, sepals 2, no petals, stamens numerous; capsule flattened. It occurs in the Yangtze basin and is poisonous throughout; it is used as an insecticide and veterinary drug (the sanguinarine in feed additives is extracted from it).
- Blue poppies (*Meconopsis*): alpine perennial herbs with large blue, purple or yellow flowers, famous wildflowers of the Himalaya–Hengduan region, such as *M. horridula*. Molecular evidence shows that the traditional genus is polyphyletic: the European type species *M. cambrica* is nested within *Papaver* and has been transferred to it, while the Asian species form a separate clade.
- Others: California poppy (*Eschscholzia californica*), native to western North America, is the state flower of California; Mexican prickly poppy (*Argemone mexicana*), native to the Americas, has spiny leaves and has become naturalized in southern China.

Diagnostic characters of Fumarioideae:

- Herbs, often with tubers or rhizomes; no laticifers (the sap is clear); isoquinoline alkaloids are common.
- Leaves often several times pinnate or ternately divided, with small segments.
- Flowers in racemes; zygomorphic (*Corydalis*) or disymmetric (*Lamprocapnos*, with two planes of symmetry); sepals 2, small, scale-like, early-falling; petals 4 in two whorls, of the 2 outer petals 1 (*Corydalis*) or 2 (*Lamprocapnos*) with a basal spur or pouch, the 2 inner petals narrower and usually joined at the tip, enclosing the stamens and stigma.
- Stamens 6, united into 2 bundles, in each bundle the central anther 2-locular and the two lateral anthers each 1-locular (usually interpreted as two stamens that each split lengthwise: the central one complete, the lateral ones each a half-stamen); the spur contains nectar produced by an extension of the stamen base. *Hypecoum* has 4 free stamens.
- Ovary superior, 2 carpels, 1-locular, with 2 parietal placentas; capsule, sometimes a nutlet.

Floral formula: *Corydalis* $\uparrow K_2 C_{2+2} A_{(3)+(3)} \underline{G}_{(2:1:\infty)}$; *Lamprocapnos* has disymmetric flowers with the same formula, except that both outer petals are spurred.

Fumarioideae have about 20 genera and 570 species; China has 7 genera and about 370 species, of which *Corydalis* has about 500 species worldwide and about 360 in China, with the greatest diversity in the mountains of the southwest. Representative plants: *C. yanhusuo*, whose tuber is the Chinese drug *yanhusuo* and contains tetrahydropalmatine with analgesic action, from Zhejiang and elsewhere; *C. edulis*, *C. bungeana* (whose whole plant is the drug *kudiding*) and *C. speciosa* are common wild species. Bleeding heart (*Lamprocapnos spectabilis*, formerly *Dicentra spectabilis*) has pink, heart-shaped flowers that hang from an arching inflorescence axis; it occurs in northeastern and northern China and is grown as an ornamental. Common fumitory (*Fumaria officinalis*) is a common weed in Europe, and the family name Fumariaceae derived from *Fumaria*.

Changes in circumscription: in Engler's system Papaveraceae and Fumariaceae were two families of Rhoeadales, an order that also included Capparaceae, Brassicaceae and Resedaceae. Cronquist pointed out that the capparoids lack isoquinoline alkaloids but have mustard oils (myrosin cells) and centrifugal stamen development, unlike the papaverids, and divided them into Papaverales and Capparales. Molecular evidence confirmed this division: Capparaceae and Brassicaceae belong to Brassicales in the malvids ([7.12.44](/7.12.44)), far from Papaveraceae; and the fumitory group is nested in Papaverales as sister to the poppy group, so APG merged the two into one family, reducing the former Fumariaceae to the subfamily Fumarioideae.

### 40.2.3. Ranunculaceae

Ranunculaceae are the largest family of Ranunculales, with about 60 genera and 2500 species, distributed worldwide and most numerous in the north temperate and cold zones; China has 38 genera and about 920 species, with the greatest richness in the mountains of the southwest.

Diagnostic characters:

- Perennial or annual herbs, a few shrubs or woody climbers (*Clematis*); in *Cimicifuga*, *Actaea* and relatives the vascular bundles of the stem are scattered.
- Leaves alternate or basal, rarely opposite (*Clematis*), often palmately or pinnately divided, or once to several times ternately compound; the petiole base is often expanded into a sheath; stipules absent (*Thalictrum* and others have small stipule-like appendages).
- Flowers bisexual, rarely unisexual; actinomorphic or zygomorphic (*Aconitum*, *Delphinium*); solitary or in racemes, panicles or cymes.
- Perianth: sepals 3 to many, often petaloid and brightly colored, and in genera without petals taking over the function of the corolla; petals present or absent, when present often specialized as honey leaves (see below), with a nectar pit at the base or extended into a spur.
- Stamens numerous, free, spirally arranged; anthers 2-locular, longitudinally dehiscent; the innermost whorl is sometimes staminodial. Pollen tricolpate or pantoporate or pantocolpate.
- Carpels many to few (rarely 1), free, spirally arranged on a raised receptacle (carpels fused in *Nigella*); ovary superior, with 1 to many ovules per carpel.
- Fruit an aggregate of achenes (*Ranunculus*, *Clematis*, *Pulsatilla*) or an aggregate of follicles (*Aconitum*, *Coptis*, *Aquilegia*), rarely a berry (*Actaea*); seeds with endosperm and a small embryo.
- Chromosomes fall into two types: a large R type ($x = 8$, most genera) and a small T type (the *Thalictrum* type; $x = 7$ in *Thalictrum*, *Aquilegia* and others, $x = 9$ in *Coptis*); this difference corresponds roughly to the phylogeny within the family.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{3\text{–}\infty} C_{0,\,3\text{–}\infty} A_\infty \underline{G}_{\infty\text{–}1}$; *Ranunculus* $\ast K_5 C_5 A_\infty \underline{G}_{\infty:1:1}$; *Aconitum* $\uparrow K_5 C_{2\text{–}8} A_\infty \underline{G}_{3\text{–}5:1:\infty}$.

**Definition 40.2.1 (Honey leaf)** In Ranunculaceae and other Ranunculales, a specialized petal that lies between the sepals and the stamens, has a nectary at its base or in its spur, and secretes nectar is called a **honey leaf** (nectar leaf). Developmental and gene-expression evidence shows that honey leaves are derived from stamens (they share with the stamens the expression of a paralog of the class B gene *AP3*, [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.1.2)) and arose independently of the petals of the core eudicots.

Honey leaves vary greatly in form within the family and are the main basis for distinguishing genera (Fig. 40.2.2). In *Ranunculus* the petals (honey leaves) are flat, yellow and glossy, with a nectar pit covered by a small scale at the base; in *Trollius* the petals are narrowly linear with a nectar groove at the base; in *Aquilegia* all 5 petals extend backward into long spurs with nectar stored at the tip; in *Delphinium* the upper sepal is spurred and the spurs of the 2 upper petals extend into the sepal spur; in *Aconitum* the upper sepal forms a helmet (galea), and 2 petals become long-clawed, spurred honey leaves hidden inside the galea while the remaining petals are reduced or lost. Spur length matches the length of the pollinator's mouthparts: in *Aquilegia*, bee-, moth- and hummingbird-pollinated species differ greatly in spur length, and the spur is considered a "key innovation" that promoted rapid speciation in the genus (Hodges and Arnold 1995), which has therefore become a model group for the study of floral evolutionary development.

Molecular phylogenetics divides Ranunculaceae into five subfamilies: Glaucidioideae and Hydrastidoideae (one genus each; successive sisters of the remaining genera), Coptidoideae, Thalictroideae and Ranunculoideae. Ranunculoideae are the largest and divide into the tribes Delphinieae (*Aconitum*, *Delphinium*, *Consolida*), Anemoneae (*Anemone*, *Pulsatilla*, *Clematis*), Ranunculeae, Adonideae, Cimicifugeae and others. The characters and representative plants of the genera:

1. *Ranunculus*: herbs; leaves simple or ternately compound; sepals 5, green; petals 5, yellow, glossy, with a nectar pit at the base; stamens and carpels numerous, spirally arranged on a raised receptacle; fruit an aggregate of achenes, head-like. About 600 species, worldwide. Japanese buttercup (*R. japonicus*) is hairy throughout, with leaves palmately 3-parted, and occurs on ditch and field margins throughout China; the whole plant is poisonous, is used externally as a vesicant, and also serves as a pesticide. Celery-leaved buttercup (*R. sceleratus*) is glabrous, with a long-cylindrical fruiting head, and grows by water. *Ranunculus* contains ranunculin, which breaks down on injury to the irritant protoanemonin; livestock therefore generally avoid buttercups, and the fresh sap blisters skin.
2. *Coptis*: small perennial herbs with a yellow, extremely bitter rhizome; leaves basal, ternately parted or ternately compound; sepals 5, petaloid; petals linear, shorter than the sepals; carpels 8–12, stalked; fruit an aggregate of follicles. Chinese goldthread (*C. chinensis*) has leaves ternately parted with a long-stalked central segment and occurs in central, southern and southwestern China; the rhizome (the "chicken-foot *huanglian*") contains berberine and is a famous heat-clearing, detoxifying drug. *C. deltoidea* and *C. teeta* are also medicinal. In Ranunculaceae, *Coptis* belongs to Coptidoideae, with small T-type chromosomes.
3. *Clematis*: woody or herbaceous climbers climbing by twining petioles, rarely erect herbs; leaves opposite, pinnately or ternately compound; sepals 4–5, petaloid, valvate; no petals; stamens and carpels numerous; the achenes have a persistent, elongate, feathery style and are gathered in a head, dispersed by wind. About 300 species, about 150 in China. *C. chinensis* (*weilingxian*) is a vine whose leaves turn black on drying, with 5 leaflets, and its root is used medicinally against rheumatism and pain; the horticultural cultivars of *C. brevicaudata*, *C. patens* and others are famous climbing flowers.
4. *Aconitum*: perennial herbs with tuberous roots; leaves palmately divided; flowers in racemes, zygomorphic, sepals 5, petaloid, blue-purple or yellow, the upper sepal helmet-shaped or cylindrical; petals 2, modified into long-clawed honey leaves with a spur at the tip, hidden in the galea, the other 3 reduced or absent; stamens numerous; carpels 3–5, free; fruit an aggregate of follicles. About 400 species, about 200 in China, mainly in the southwest. The mother root of *A. carmichaelii* is the drug *chuanwu* and the lateral daughter roots are processed into *fuzi*; the tuberous root of *A. kusnezoffii* is *caowu*; *A. coreanum* has pale yellow flowers. *Aconitum* contains diterpenoid alkaloids such as aconitine and is highly toxic: a few milligrams of the raw drug can be fatal, but processing (prolonged boiling, which hydrolyzes the alkaloids) greatly reduces the toxicity. *A. barbatum* var. *puberulum* has yellow flowers and occurs in northern China. Molecular evidence suggests that the flower of *Aconitum* may have evolved from an ancestral type like that of *Delphinium*.
5. *Adonis*: herbs with solitary yellow or red flowers at the branch tips; sepals 5–8, petals 5–16, stamens and carpels numerous, spirally arranged; fruit an aggregate of achenes. The number of floral parts is not fixed, and the genus is often regarded as a relatively primitive group in Ranunculaceae. *A. amurensis* blooms yellow in early spring and occurs in northeastern China; the whole plant contains cardiac glycosides.
6. *Delphinium*: herbs; leaves palmately divided; flowers in racemes; sepals 5, petaloid, the upper one extended into a spur; petals 2, spurred, extending into the sepal spur; staminodes 2, clawed (the lower pair of "petals"); stamens numerous; carpels 3–7; follicles. *D. anthriscifolium* has leaves 2–3 times pinnately parted and blue-purple flowers, is widespread in China, and the whole plant is used medicinally; *D. grandiflorum* occurs in northern China and is grown as an ornamental. *Consolida* (now mostly included in *Delphinium*) has only 1 petal and 1 carpel.
7. *Pulsatilla* (molecular evidence supports including it in *Anemone* in the broad sense): perennial herbs covered with long white hairs; leaves basal; flowers solitary at the top of a scape, sepals 6, blue-purple, petaloid, no petals; achenes with a persistent, elongated, feathery style. *P. chinensis* occurs from northeastern to eastern China and its root is used medicinally; its Chinese name, "white-headed old man," refers to the white feathery styles that cover the head at fruiting.
8. *Anemone*: perennial herbs with basal leaves; the scape bears 3 leaf-like involucral bracts; sepals petaloid, no petals; fruit an aggregate of achenes. *A. hupehensis* has ternate leaves, pink sepals and woolly achenes, occurs from the southwest to central China, and its roots are poisonous and used as a pesticide; the European poppy anemone (*A. coronaria*) is a common cut flower.
9. Others: *Aquilegia* (*A. yabeana*, and ornamental cultivars such as *A.* × *hybrida*), with 5 long-spurred petals; *Trollius* (*T. chinensis*, with yellow sepals, in mountain meadows of northern and northeastern China); *Thalictrum* (*T. petaloideum*, with small flowers lacking petals and conspicuous flattened filaments, mostly wind-pollinated or also insect-pollinated, some species dioecious); *Cimicifuga* (mostly merged into *Actaea*; *A. cimicifuga*, whose rhizome is used medicinally); *Semiaquilegia adoxoides* (tuberous roots used medicinally); *Caltha* (no petals, in wet places); *Nigella damascena* (carpels fused, rare in the family).

Key characters of the family: herbs. Leaves divided or compound, without stipules. Flowers bisexual, actinomorphic or zygomorphic, often pentamerous; sepals often petaloid; petals often specialized as honey leaves or absent; stamens and carpels numerous, free, spirally arranged on a raised receptacle. Fruit an aggregate of achenes or follicles. Seeds with endosperm.

Economic importance: Ranunculaceae are one of the families richest in poisonous and medicinal plants. Aconite, *fuzi*, goldthread, *Cimicifuga*, *Pulsatilla* and *Clematis chinensis* are all commonly used drugs; the cardiac glycosides of *Adonis* and of lily of the valley, the diterpenoid alkaloids of aconites, the berberine of goldthread and the protoanemonin of buttercups reflect the rich secondary metabolism of the family. Ornamentals include clematis, larkspur, columbine, anemone, Persian buttercup (*Ranunculus asiaticus*), globeflower and Christmas rose (*Helleborus*).

Changes in circumscription: neither Engler's nor Cronquist's Ranunculaceae include *Paeonia*, but earlier systems (and many Chinese sources) placed *Paeonia* in Ranunculaceae as a subfamily or tribe. *Paeonia* has a persistent calyx, centrifugal stamens, a fleshy disc, thick leathery carpels and a chromosome base number of $x = 5$, differing greatly from Ranunculaceae; since the 1950s it has mostly been treated as a separate family Paeoniaceae, which molecular evidence places in Saxifragales ([7.12.41](/7.12.41)). The Ranunculaceae of APG IV exclude Paeoniaceae and Circaeasteraceae; the latter (*Circaeaster* and *Kingdonia*) were placed in Ranunculaceae by some authors.

### 40.2.4. Berberidaceae

Berberidaceae have about 17 genera and 650 species, mainly in the north temperate zone and most numerous in East Asia; China has 11 genera and about 300 species.

Diagnostic characters:

- Shrubs or perennial herbs; the wood and roots are often yellow (containing berberine).
- Leaves alternate, simple or once to three times pinnately compound, rarely palmately compound; stipules mostly absent. On the long shoots of *Berberis*, the leaves are transformed into branched spines (**leaf spines**, [7.12.24](/7.12.24#sec-24.4.4)), and normal leaves are clustered on short shoots in the axils of the spines.
- Flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, solitary or in racemes, panicles or cymes; the perianth is often trimerous in several whorls: outer sepals (often with a transition through bracteoles), inner petals or nectar-secreting petals; the differentiation of the perianth is not distinct.
- Stamens 6 (or 4), opposite the petals; anthers dehiscing by valves: two flaps form in the anther wall and lift from the bottom upward, with the pollen adhering to the flaps (similar to Lauraceae but convergent); in *Berberis* the stamens, when touched, bend rapidly inward and press pollen onto the insect.
- Gynoecium of 1 carpel, ovary superior, 1-locular, ovules 1 to many, basal or along the ventral suture; style short, stigma often persistent.
- Berry (*Berberis*, *Mahonia*, *Nandina*) or follicle or capsule (*Epimedium*).

Floral formula: *Berberis* $\ast K_{3+3} C_{3+3} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{1:1:1\text{–}\infty}$.

Representative plants:

1. *Berberis*: deciduous or evergreen shrubs with branched leaf spines on the twigs; flowers yellow, with a pair of nectaries at the base of each petal; berries red or purple-black. About 500 species, about 250 in China. Japanese barberry (*B. thunbergii*) and its purple-leaved cultivars are common hedge plants; berberine can be extracted from the roots and stems of *B. julianae*, *B. poiretii* and others. European barberry (*B. vulgaris*) and relatives are the alternate hosts of the wheat stem rust fungus (*Puccinia graminis*): the rust produces urediniospores and teliospores on wheat and completes its sexual reproduction on barberry, producing spermatia and aeciospores (**heteroecism**, [7.12.11](/7.12.11#sec-11.2.3)); the alternate host of the stripe rust fungus was shown to be barberry only in 2010.
2. *Mahonia*: evergreen shrubs with odd-pinnate leaves whose leaflets have spiny teeth; no leaf spines; flowers yellow in clustered racemes; berries blue-black, glaucous. *M. fortunei* and *M. bealei* occur south of the Yangtze, with stems and roots used medicinally, and are common garden plants. Molecular evidence shows that *Mahonia* is nested within *Berberis*, and some authors merge them into a broadly circumscribed *Berberis* (Yu and Chung 2017), whereas Flora of China still keeps them separate.
3. Heavenly bamboo (*Nandina domestica*): an evergreen shrub with leaves two to three times pinnate, often turning red in winter; flowers white in panicles; berries globose, red. It occurs south of the Yangtze to Japan and is grown as an ornamental, with the fruit used medicinally. *Nandina* is a relatively isolated lineage of Berberidaceae (Nandinoideae).
4. *Epimedium*: perennial herbs with twice-ternate leaves whose leaflets are cordate at the base and oblique; flowers tetramerous, the inner sepals petaloid, the petals spurred; follicles. The aerial parts of *E. brevicornu*, *E. sagittatum* and others are used medicinally.
5. Others: *Dysosma* (*D. versipellis*, with peltate leaves and dark red flowers hanging beneath the leaves) and *Sinopodophyllum hexandrum* (with leaves palmately deeply lobed and red berries, from the alpine southwest) contain podophyllotoxin, the raw material of the semisynthetic anticancer drug etoposide; American mayapple (*Podophyllum peltatum*) is also a source of podophyllotoxin.

Key characters of the family: shrubs or herbs. Flowers trimerous, with the perianth in several whorls; stamens opposite the petals, anthers dehiscing by valves; a single carpel. Berry or follicle.

### 40.2.5. Lardizabalaceae and Menispermaceae

Lardizabalaceae have about 7 genera and 40 species, mainly in East Asia, with a few in Chile (an East Asia–South America disjunction). They are woody climbers, rarely erect shrubs (*Decaisnea*); leaves alternate, palmately or ternately compound, rarely pinnately compound, without stipules; flowers unisexual, monoecious or dioecious, rarely polygamous, trimerous, sepals 3 or 6, petaloid, petals 6 or absent, often nectary-like; stamens 6, filaments free or fused; carpels 3 to many, free, with many ovules per carpel; fruit a fleshy follicle or berry, some dehiscing along the ventral suture at maturity, with white, sweet, edible pulp. Floral formulas: ♂ $\ast K_{3+3} C_{0,\,6} A_6$; ♀ $\ast K_{3+3} C_{0,\,6} \underline{G}_{3\text{–}\infty}$. Chocolate vine (*Akebia quinata*) has palmate leaves with 5 leaflets and dark purple flowers, and its fruit splits along the ventral suture when ripe (hence the Chinese name "August burst"); the vine stems are used medicinally (one of the genuine sources of the drug *mutong*). Three-leaf akebia (*A. trifoliata*) has 3 leaflets. *Decaisnea insignis* is an erect shrub with pinnate leaves and blue cylindrical fruits, occurring from the southwest to central China. *Sargentodoxa cuneata* is a deciduous woody climber with ternate leaves and unisexual flowers, stamens 6, and numerous carpels spirally arranged on an enlarged receptacle, so that the fruit is a cluster of berry-like small fruits on a fleshy receptacle; its stem is used medicinally (*hongteng*). It was formerly separated as Sargentodoxaceae and is now the most basal lineage of Lardizabalaceae (Sargentodoxoideae).

Menispermaceae have about 70 genera and 440 species, mainly in the tropics and subtropics; China has about 20 genera and more than 70 species. They are twining climbers, rarely erect shrubs; leaves alternate, simple, often palmately veined and sometimes peltate (*Stephania*), without stipules; flowers small, unisexual, dioecious, trimerous, with 2 whorls each of sepals and petals, stamens 3–6 or numerous, often fused into a column; carpels 3–6, free; fruit a drupe with a bony endocarp, often curved into a horseshoe or kidney shape and sculptured on the surface, a diagnostic character of the genera. Floral formulas: ♂ $\ast K_{3+3} C_{3+3} A_{3\text{–}6}$; ♀ $\ast K_{3+3} C_{3+3} \underline{G}_{3\text{–}6}$. Representative plants: Asian moonseed (*Menispermum dauricum*, with peltate leaves and a rhizome that is the drug *beidougen*), *Stephania japonica*, *S. tetrandra* (whose root is the drug *fangji* and contains tetrandrine), *Cocculus orbiculatus*, and *Tinospora sagittata* (tuberous root, *jinguolan*). The South American *Chondrodendron tomentosum* is one of the main sources of curare, whose tubocurarine blocks the acetylcholine receptors of the neuromuscular junction and was used as a muscle relaxant in surgery. Note that the drug names *fangji* and *mutong* refer to plants of different families in different regions: *S. tetrandra* (Menispermaceae) versus *Aristolochia fangchi* (Aristolochiaceae), and *Akebia* (Lardizabalaceae) versus *Aristolochia manshuriensis* (Aristolochiaceae); the latter contain aristolochic acid, which causes kidney disease and cancer ([7.12.35](/7.12.35#sec-35.4.4)).

### 40.2.6. Eupteleaceae and Circaeasteraceae

Eupteleaceae contain only the genus *Euptelea*, with two species in East Asia. They are deciduous trees or shrubs; the flowers open before the leaves, bisexual, without a perianth, with numerous stamens with red anthers and numerous free, stalked carpels; fruit a samara; wind-pollinated. *E. pleiosperma* occurs from central to southwestern China and is a Tertiary relict. Because *Euptelea* is wind-pollinated and lacks a perianth, it was traditionally placed in Hamamelidae (Cronquist's Hamamelidales), close to Cercidiphyllaceae and Trochodendraceae; molecular evidence, however, places it in Ranunculales as sister to all the other families of the order.

Circaeasteraceae contain only two small herbs: *Circaeaster agrestis* (annual, with leaves clustered at the top of the stem in a star shape) and *Kingdonia uniflora* (perennial, with a single palmately parted leaf and a single flower per plant), both from the high mountains of southwestern to northwestern China; *Kingdonia* is endemic to China and a first-class nationally protected wild plant. In both, the venation is open and dichotomous, with no closed meshes, which is extremely rare in angiosperms (as in *Ginkgo*) and was once taken as a primitive character, but their position in the phylogeny shows that it is secondary.

## 40.3. Proteales

### 40.3.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

In APG IV, Proteales include four families: Nelumbonaceae, Platanaceae, Proteaceae and Sabiaceae. The first three form a clade in which Platanaceae and Proteaceae are sister groups; Sabiaceae are sister to them (Sabiaceae were included in Proteales only in APG IV). The four families differ enormously in form, from the aquatic herb lotus through the wind-pollinated plane tree and the Southern Hemisphere shrubs of Proteaceae to the East Asian climbers of Sabiaceae, and no morphological system has ever placed them together; their grouping into one order is among the most unexpected results of molecular systematics. Possible morphological synapomorphies are 1–2 ovules per carpel, little or no endosperm in the seed, and tubular or rod-shaped epicuticular wax.

Changes in circumscription: in the systems of Engler and Cronquist, Nelumbonaceae belong to Nymphaeales ([7.12.34](/7.12.34#sec-34.4.5)); Platanaceae belong to Hamamelidales; Proteaceae form their own order Proteales in Cronquist's system, placed in Rosidae near Elaeagnaceae; Sabiaceae belong to Ranunculales in Cronquist's system and to Sapindales in other systems.

### 40.3.2. Nelumbonaceae

Nelumbonaceae contain only the genus *Nelumbo*, with 2 species: sacred lotus (*N. nucifera*), from Asia to northern Australia, and American lotus (*N. lutea*), from eastern North America to Central America.

Diagnostic characters:

- Perennial aquatic herbs with latex; the rhizome (lotus root) creeps, with thick internodes containing longitudinal air canals and adventitious roots at the nodes.
- Leaves basal, peltate, nearly circular, with a prickly petiole, often held above the water; young leaves are rolled inward. The leaf surface has dense papillae covered with nanoscale wax tubules, so that water forms nearly spherical droplets that roll off and carry away dust (the "lotus effect," a superhydrophobic self-cleaning surface and a classic example for biomimetic materials).
- Flowers large, solitary at the top of a scape held above the water; tepals 22–30, spirally arranged, the outer 2–5 small, green and sepal-like, grading inward to larger, petal-like ones; stamens numerous (200–400), spirally arranged, with the connective extended into a club-shaped appendage; pollen tricolpate.
- Carpels numerous (12–40), free, each embedded in a pit in the large, flat-topped, spongy, obconical receptacle with only the stigma exposed; each carpel has 1 pendulous anatropous ovule.
- Fruit a nut (lotus seed) with a hard, leathery pericarp; seed without endosperm, with 2 thick cotyledons and a green plumule (lotus plumule).
- In the first days of anthesis the flower is thermogenic, maintaining a temperature of 30–36 °C, more than 10 °C above the air, which may attract beetle pollinators.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{\infty} A_\infty \underline{G}_{\infty:1:1}$ (the outer 2–5 tepals sepal-like).

The lotus is one of the aquatic plants longest in cultivation in China and is divided by use into rhizome lotus (grown for the rhizome), seed lotus (grown for the seeds) and flower lotus (ornamental); the leaf, node of the rhizome, receptacle, seed and plumule are all used medicinally. Lotus seeds are extremely long-lived: ancient lotus seeds unearthed from a peat layer at Pulandian in Liaoning were dated by radiocarbon directly on the seed itself at about 1300 years, and still germinated and flowered (Shen-Miller et al. 1995); these are the "thousand-year-old lotus seeds." The longevity of lotus seeds is attributed to the hard, impermeable pericarp (with only a small pore, normally plugged), antioxidants in the pericarp, and enzymes in the embryo that repair protein damage. The claim in Chinese sources that seeds can remain viable after more than 3000 years in the soil has no reliable dating basis.

Convergence of lotus and water lily. Lotus and water lily are both aquatic plants with rhizomes, large leaves, large flowers and numerous floral parts, and were traditionally placed in the same order, Nymphaeales, with the lotus as a subfamily of Nymphaeaceae. But the differences are fundamental (Fig. 40.3.1): lotus pollen is tricolpate, whereas that of Nymphaeaceae is monosulcate or has an annular aperture; the carpels of lotus are free and embedded in the receptacle, whereas those of Nymphaeaceae are fused into a multilocular ovary; lotus leaves are peltate, without a notch, and held above the water, whereas water lily leaves float and have a deep basal notch; lotus has latex and a seed without endosperm, whereas water lily has no latex and a seed with perisperm. Palynology first suggested that the two are not closely related ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.3)), and molecular evidence placed lotus in Proteales among the eudicots, as sister to the clade of Platanaceae and Proteaceae. The similar rhizomes, aerenchyma and large leaves are **convergent evolution** to the aquatic environment; the gradual transition from tepals to stamens, resulting from a "fading borders" pattern of class B gene expression in their flowers, also arose independently. Fossils of *Nelumbo* go back to the Early Cretaceous (the extinct *Nelumbites*), and the divergence of Nelumbonaceae and Platanaceae is estimated at about 110 Ma.

### 40.3.3. Platanaceae

Platanaceae contain only the genus *Platanus*, with 8–10 species in North America, southeastern Europe, western Asia and Indochina.

Diagnostic characters: large deciduous trees; bark pale, flaking in large thin plates to expose the pale greenish-white inner bark, so that the trunk looks mottled; young twigs and leaves with stellate hairs; leaves simple, alternate, large, long-petioled, palmately 3–7-lobed, palmately veined; the petiole base is swollen and caps the axillary bud (a subpetiolar bud); stipules annular, clasping the stem. Flowers unisexual, monoecious, each type crowded in a hanging globose head; flowers small, 3–8-merous, with an inconspicuous perianth; male flowers with 3–8 stamens; female flowers with 3–8 free carpels, each with 1–2 orthotropous ovules; wind-pollinated. Fruit an aggregate of nutlets (achene-like), with long basal hairs; many nutlets form a globose fruiting head that hangs on the tree through winter and breaks apart the following spring, the nutlets being dispersed by wind on their hairs.

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{3\text{–}8} A_{3\text{–}8}$; ♀ $\ast P_{3\text{–}8} \underline{G}_{3\text{–}8}$.

Three plane trees are commonly cultivated in China, distinguished by the number of globose fruiting heads usually borne on each fruiting stalk:

| Common name | Scientific name | Heads per stalk | Leaf | Origin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| American sycamore (one-ball plane tree) | *P. occidentalis* | 1, rarely 2 | 3–5 shallow lobes, middle lobe wider than long | Eastern North America |
| London plane (two-ball plane tree) | *P.* × *acerifolia* (*P.* × *hispanica*) | usually 2, sometimes 1 or 3 | 3–5 medium lobes, middle lobe about as long as wide | Cultivated hybrid |
| Oriental plane (three-ball plane tree) | *P. orientalis* | 3–6 | 5–7 deep lobes, middle lobe longer than wide | Southeastern Europe to western Asia |

The London plane is a hybrid between the oriental plane and the American sycamore that arose in Europe (possibly in Spain or England) in the 17th century; it grows fast, tolerates pruning and pollution, and is one of the most widely used street trees in the world. What is called the "French parasol tree" in Chinese usage is mostly in fact the London plane, a name that arose from the large-scale planting in the French Concession in Shanghai and elsewhere in modern times; it has nothing to do with the Chinese parasol tree (*Firmiana*, Malvaceae, [7.12.44](/7.12.44)). The fruit hairs and pollen of plane trees drift in spring and cause respiratory allergies, so cultivars with few fruits are often chosen for cities.

Platanaceae are among the most common angiosperm fossils of the mid-Cretaceous, with greater leaf diversity than in the living species and also compound-leaved types; the Cretaceous flowers had a more distinct perianth and were 4- or 5-merous. The leaves of *Platanus* resemble those of *Acer* (Sapindaceae), but *Acer* has opposite leaves and double-winged fruits, whereas *Platanus* has alternate leaves and a subpetiolar bud, so the two are easy to tell apart.

### 40.3.4. Proteaceae

Proteaceae have about 80 genera and 1600 species, mainly in the Southern Hemisphere, concentrated in Australia and South Africa, a typical Gondwanan group; China has 4 genera and about 25 species, in the southwest and south. They are trees or shrubs; leaves alternate, leathery, often deeply lobed and very variable in form; flowers bisexual, often zygomorphic, in racemes, spikes or dense heads (often with showy bracts); tepals 4, petaloid, valvate, often reflexed outward at anthesis; stamens 4, opposite the tepals, with the filaments adnate to them; gynoecium of 1 carpel, ovary superior, often stalked, style long, the stigma region receiving pollen from the same flower before anthesis and then presenting it to pollinators ("pollen presentation"); fruit a follicle, nut or drupe. Many species have proteoid roots (dense clusters of short, determinate rootlets on the lateral roots), which secrete organic acids that dissolve phosphorus in the soil, an adaptation to the extremely phosphorus-poor ancient soils of the Southern Hemisphere.

Floral formula: $\uparrow P_4 A_4 \underline{G}_{1:1:1\text{–}2}$ (rarely actinomorphic).

Representative plants: *Helicia* (about 20 species in China); silky oak (*Grevillea robusta*), native to eastern Australia, with leaves twice-pinnately deeply parted and silvery beneath and orange-yellow flowers, grown as a street tree in southern China; macadamia (*Macadamia integrifolia* and *M. tetraphylla*), native to Australia, whose seeds are a famous nut; king protea (*Protea cynaroides*), the national flower of South Africa, with large, showy flower heads; *Banksia* is emblematic of Australia, and in many species the cones open to release the seeds only after a forest fire.

### 40.3.5. Sabiaceae

Sabiaceae have about 3 genera and 100 species in the tropics to subtropics of Asia and the Americas; China has *Sabia* (climbing woody vines with small flowers) and *Meliosma* (trees or shrubs with large panicles and zygomorphic flowers in which the 2 inner petals are minute). The stamens are opposite the petals, with a disc; ovary superior, 2 carpels. *S. japonica* and *M. myriantha* are common species.

## 40.4. Trochodendrales, Buxales and Gunnerales

### 40.4.1. Trochodendrales

Trochodendrales contain only Trochodendraceae, with the genera *Trochodendron* and *Tetracentron*, one species each, in East Asia; both are Tertiary relicts. They were formerly separated as Trochodendraceae and Tetracentraceae (Cronquist's Trochodendrales), and APG merged them into one family.

- *Trochodendron aralioides*: an evergreen tree; leaves nearly whorled at the branch tips, long-petioled, with blunt serrations; flowers in racemes, bisexual, without a perianth; stamens numerous (40–70), grading from spiral to whorled arrangement; carpels 6–11 in one whorl, somewhat fused laterally; fruit an aggregate of follicles. It occurs in Taiwan, Japan and southern Korea.
- *Tetracentron sinense*: a deciduous tree; each short shoot bears only one leaf; leaves cordate, palmately veined, with glandular teeth; spikes hanging; flowers small, bisexual, tepals 4, stamens 4, carpels 4, fused at the ventral suture; capsule, the styles being pushed to the base of the fruit at maturity. It occurs in Shaanxi, Hubei and the southwest of China to Nepal and Myanmar. It is a second-class nationally protected wild plant in China.

*Trochodendron* and *Tetracentron* were long thought to have xylem with "tracheids only and no vessels" and, like Winteraceae, were treated as "primitive vesselless angiosperms," from which it was concluded that they came from the primitive Magnoliales or were a bridge from the magnoliids to the hamamelids. Recent anatomical studies have found vessel elements with scalariform perforation plates in the xylem of both genera (Ren et al. 2007; Li et al. 2011); the vessel elements are merely very long and the perforations small, resembling tracheids. Their phylogenetic position (early-diverging eudicots, far from the ANA grade and magnoliids) also shows that this tracheid-like xylem is a secondary specialization and not the most primitive state of the angiosperms. The fossils of *Tetracentron* are known from the Late Cretaceous, and fossils of Trochodendraceae are widespread in the Tertiary of the Northern Hemisphere (such as the extinct *Nordenskioldia*).

### 40.4.2. Buxales

Buxales contain only Buxaceae, which in APG IV includes Didymelaceae (*Didymeles*, Madagascar) and *Haptanthus* (Honduras), with about 6 genera and 100 species. Buxales are the sister group of the core eudicots.

Buxaceae: evergreen shrubs or small trees, rarely herb-like subshrubs; leaves simple, opposite (*Buxus*) or alternate, leathery, entire, without stipules; flowers small, unisexual, monoecious, with an inconspicuous perianth (4–6 members), clustered or in spikes, often with one female flower at the top of the inflorescence surrounded by several male flowers; male flowers with 4 (or 6) stamens opposite the tepals; female flowers with a superior ovary of 3 fused carpels, 3-locular, 2 ovules per locule, and 3 persistent styles; capsule with three horn-like persistent styles at the top, loculicidal at maturity, seeds black and shiny. They contain steroidal alkaloids. Floral formulas: ♂ $\ast P_4 A_4$; ♀ $\ast P_{4\text{–}6} \underline{G}_{(3:3:2)}$.

Representative plants: Chinese boxwood (*Buxus sinica*), an evergreen shrub with opposite, obovate leaves notched at the tip, occurring from the Yangtze basin to southern North China; its wood is extremely dense and yellow, a superior material for carving and for making combs and seals, and it grows very slowly and is often used for bonsai and hedges; *B. bodinieri* has narrow leaves. *Pachysandra* (*P. terminalis*, Japanese spurge, is a common woodland ground cover) and *Sarcococca* are subshrubs or shrubs with alternate leaves. Note that Japanese spindle (*Euonymus japonicus*), often called "large-leaved boxwood" in gardens, belongs to Celastraceae ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)), with opposite, serrate leaves, tetramerous flowers and a capsule with an orange-red aril; it is unrelated to *Buxus*.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist placed Buxaceae in Euphorbiales (the same order as Euphorbiaceae), because both have unisexual flowers, a 3-locular ovary and pendulous ovules in each locule; Takhtajan placed them near the hamamelids. Molecular evidence places Buxaceae among the early-diverging eudicots.

### 40.4.3. Gunnerales

Gunnerales include Gunneraceae (only *Gunnera*, about 60 species) and Myrothamnaceae (*Myrothamnus*, 2 species); they form the earliest-diverging branch of the core eudicots, and their relationship is supported only by molecular evidence, since they differ greatly in morphology.

- *Gunnera*: perennial herbs of the mountains of the Southern Hemisphere and tropical America, absent from China. Some species have enormous leaves; the blade of *G. manicata* from Brazil can reach 2–3 m in diameter, with a prickly petiole, and it is often grown in European gardens; there are also small species only a few centimeters tall. Flowers small, unisexual or bisexual, dimerous. *Gunnera* is the only angiosperm genus that forms an intracellular symbiosis with cyanobacteria: glands beneath the petiole at the base of the stem secrete mucilage that attracts *Nostoc* into the gland cells, where it forms numerous **heterocysts** and fixes nitrogen, supplying the plant with nitrogen. This is a form of nitrogen-fixing symbiosis independent of the legumes and the actinorhizal nitrogen-fixing clade ([7.12.43](/7.12.43)), and also independent of the cyanobacterial symbioses in the coralloid roots of cycads ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)) and in the leaf cavities of *Azolla* ([7.12.17](/7.12.17)).
- *Myrothamnus*: small shrubs of southern Africa and Madagascar; leaves opposite, fan-shaped, pleated; in the dry season they lose water until the water content is extremely low, and the leaves fold and turn brown, but within a few hours of rain they turn green again and unfold; it is one of the few "resurrection plants" among the angiosperms.

Cronquist placed Gunneraceae in Haloragales of the Rosidae and Myrothamnaceae in Hamamelidales of the Hamamelidae.

## 40.5. Dilleniales

Dilleniales contain only Dilleniaceae, with about 11 genera and 300–500 species, mainly in the tropics and most numerous in Australia; China has 2 genera, *Dillenia* and *Tetracera*, with about 5 species, in Yunnan, Guangxi, Guangdong and Hainan.

Diagnostic characters: trees, shrubs or woody climbers; leaves simple, alternate, pinnately veined, with many parallel lateral veins running straight to the marginal teeth, the surface often rough (with silicified hairs). Flowers bisexual, rarely unisexual, actinomorphic; sepals 5, imbricate, persistent; petals 5, crumpled in the bud, early-falling; stamens numerous, centrifugal, free or in several bundles, anthers dehiscing longitudinally or by apical pores; carpels 1–20, free or fused at the base, with 1 to many ovules per carpel; fruit a follicle or berry-like; seeds often with an aril. Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_\infty \underline{G}_{1\text{–}20}$.

Representative plants: elephant apple (*Dillenia indica*), an evergreen tree with large leaves with 30–50 pairs of lateral veins and white flowers up to 20 cm across, the fruit enclosed by the enlarged, fleshy sepals and edible, occurring from southern Yunnan to South Asia; *D. turbinata*, with 5–20 fused carpels, from southern China; *Tetracera*, woody climbers with extremely rough leaves formerly used to polish tinware, with 1–5 free carpels and follicles. *Hibbertia* is one of the largest genera in Australia.

The systematic position of Dilleniaceae is one of the unresolved problems in angiosperm phylogeny. Cronquist placed Dilleniaceae together with Paeoniaceae at the base of the Dilleniidae, regarding them, with their numerous floral parts, free carpels and numerous stamens, as the "bridge" linking Magnoliidae and Dilleniidae (Theales, Malvales, Violales and others, 78 families), and held that the syncarpous groups of the Dilleniidae were all derived from such an ancestor. Molecular evidence refuted this idea: Paeoniaceae belong to Saxifragales, and the remaining families of the Dilleniidae are scattered among Malpighiales, Malvales, Brassicales, Ericales and others, while Dilleniaceae themselves lie at the base of the Pentapetalae, being in different analyses sister to the superasterids or to the superrosids, or forming a polytomy with the two; APG IV treats them only as a separate order within the Pentapetalae and leaves their position uncertain.

## Questions

1. Ranunculaceae and Magnoliaceae are both groups with numerous free, spirally arranged floral parts and were traditionally considered closely related. How does modern evidence view this similarity? What are the differences between the two?

   **Answer** Phylogenetics shows that Magnoliaceae belong to the magnoliids and Ranunculaceae to Ranunculales among the eudicots, which lie on widely separated branches of the Mesangiospermae; numerous, free, spirally arranged floral parts are ancestral characters retained by early angiosperm groups and do not show close relationship. As for the differences: Magnoliaceae are woody plants with oil cells, monosulcate pollen, annular stipular scars and a perianth that is often undifferentiated; Ranunculaceae are mostly herbs without oil cells but with isoquinoline or diterpenoid alkaloids, with tricolpate pollen (or types derived from it), often with honey leaves derived from stamens, with leaves often divided, sheathing at the base and without stipules.

2. How do we distinguish *Ranunculus*, *Aconitum*, *Delphinium*, *Aquilegia* and *Clematis*?

   **Answer** *Ranunculus*: herbs, sepals 5 and green, petals 5 yellow with a nectar pit at the base, actinomorphic, fruit an aggregate of achenes. *Aconitum*: zygomorphic, the upper sepal helmet-shaped, 2 long-clawed honey leaves hidden in the galea, fruit an aggregate of follicles, the tuberous root highly toxic. *Delphinium*: zygomorphic, the upper sepal extended into a spur into which the spurs of the 2 upper petals extend, follicles. *Aquilegia*: actinomorphic, all 5 petals with long spurs, 5 follicles. *Clematis*: mostly climbers with opposite leaves, sepals 4–5, petaloid and valvate, no petals, achenes with a feathery persistent style.

3. Why were the former Fumariaceae and Papaveraceae merged, while Capparaceae and Brassicaceae of the former Papaverales (Rhoeadales) were moved away?

   **Answer** Molecular phylogenetics shows that the fumitory group and the poppy group are sister groups nested together in Ranunculales; they share 2 early-falling sepals, 4 petals in two whorls, a fused ovary with parietal placentation, and isoquinoline alkaloids, differing only in the presence of latex and in floral symmetry, so APG merged them into Papaveraceae in the broad sense and divided it into subfamilies. Capparaceae and Brassicaceae also have 2 carpels, parietal placentation and tetramerous flowers, but they contain glucosinolates (myrosinase) and no isoquinoline alkaloids and have centrifugal stamens; molecular evidence shows that they belong to Brassicales in the malvids, and their resemblance to Papaveraceae is convergent.

4. Why are lotuses no longer placed in Nymphaeales? List the differences between lotus and water lily in pollen, carpels, leaves and seeds.

   **Answer** Lotus pollen is tricolpate, the pollen of the eudicots; the pollen of Nymphaeaceae is monosulcate or has an annular aperture. The carpels of lotus are free and each is embedded in the spongy obconical receptacle, whereas those of Nymphaeaceae are fused into a multilocular ovary. Lotus leaves are peltate, without a notch, and held above the water; water lily leaves float and have a deep basal notch. Lotus has latex, seeds without endosperm and a green plumule; Nymphaeaceae have no latex and seeds with perisperm. Molecular evidence places lotus in Proteales, close to Platanaceae and Proteaceae, and its resemblance to water lily is convergent evolution to the aquatic environment.

5. What plant is the "French parasol tree" of urban street plantings? How do we distinguish the one-, two- and three-ball plane trees?

   **Answer** It is usually the London plane (*Platanus* × *acerifolia*), a hybrid between the oriental plane and the American sycamore that arose in 17th-century Europe; it belongs to Platanaceae in Proteales and is unrelated to the Chinese parasol tree of Malvaceae. The three are distinguished by the number of globose fruiting heads usually borne on each fruiting stalk: the American sycamore (one-ball) mostly has 1 and shallowly lobed leaves; the London plane (two-ball) usually has 2 and medium-lobed leaves; the oriental plane (three-ball) has 3–6 and deeply lobed leaves with the middle lobe longer than wide.
