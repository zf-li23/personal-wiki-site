Primary growth of the stem, treated in the previous chapter, makes the stem elongate. Perennial gymnosperms and woody eudicots also thicken continually, to support an ever larger crown and to conduct more water; this is secondary growth. It is carried out by two lateral meristems: the vascular cambium produces secondary xylem (wood) toward the inside and secondary phloem toward the outside, and the cork cambium produces the periderm, which replaces the epidermis. This chapter first treats the origin, composition and activity of the vascular cambium, then its main product, wood (growth rings, heartwood and sapwood, identification on the three planes, dendrochronology), then the cork cambium and bark, and finally secondary growth in gymnosperms and monocots, the various anomalous secondary structures, woody versus herbaceous stems, and tree longevity and form. Secondary growth of the root was treated in [7.12.20](/7.12.20); its principle is the same as in the stem, but the cambium arises differently. The structure of the various xylem and phloem cells is in [7.12.5](/7.12.5), and the histology of periderm and lenticels in [7.12.4](/7.12.4). The hormonal control of cambial activity and the molecular mechanisms of wood formation belong to plant physiology.

## 22.1. The Vascular Cambium

Activity of the shoot apical meristem makes the stem elongate, which is primary growth ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)); primary growth also includes some thickening, but generally very little. Later, division, growth and differentiation of lateral meristems thicken the stem, a process called **secondary growth**, and the secondary tissues it forms make up the secondary structure (defined in [7.12.20](/7.12.20)). Perennial woody plants grow ever taller and thicker and need more water and nutrients and greater mechanical support; the formation and development of secondary structure meets these needs. A few monocot stems also have secondary structure, but of a different nature and with limited thickening (Section 22.5).

### 22.1.1. Origin of the Vascular Cambium

**Definition 22.1.1 (Vascular Cambium)** The lateral meristem that lies between the secondary xylem and the secondary phloem, is composed of fusiform initials and ray initials, and produces secondary xylem toward the inside and secondary phloem toward the outside, is called the **vascular cambium**, or simply the cambium.

In the stems of eudicots and gymnosperms the vascular cambium is formed by joining two parts (Figure 1):

1. Fascicular cambium: when the procambium of the primary meristem formed mature tissues, it did not all differentiate into vascular tissue; between the primary xylem and primary phloem of each bundle it left a layer of tissue with latent meristematic ability, the fascicular cambium ([7.12.21](/7.12.21)).
2. Interfascicular cambium: in the pith rays between the vascular bundles of the primary structure, some parenchyma cells at the same position as the fascicular cambium resume meristematic ability; these are called the *interfascicular cambium*.

Once the interfascicular cambium has formed, it joins the fascicular cambium, and in cross section the cambium becomes a complete ring. In origin the fascicular cambium arises by transformation of procambium and the interfascicular cambium by renewed division of already differentiated parenchyma cells, which are entirely different; but later they are highly coordinated in their division activity and in the nature and quantity of the cells they produce, and together they make up one secondary meristem (for the classification of the fascicular cambium see [7.12.4](/7.12.4)).

The procambium and the fascicular cambium differ as follows. The procambium lies beneath the apical meristem; its cells are small with dense cytoplasm, it is a fairly uniform tissue, and it later transforms entirely into primary vascular tissue, with no initials remaining. The fascicular cambium lies between primary xylem and primary phloem; its cells vary in size and length and are already vacuolated, it is a nonuniform tissue, and of the two daughter cells of each division one differentiates into vascular tissue or ray and the other keeps its meristematic ability and goes on dividing, that is, it has self-renewing initials.

In herbaceous stems in which the vascular bundles lie far apart, the interfascicular cambium may not form (some Cucurbitaceae) or may be very weakly active; in the stem of buttercup even the fascicular cambium is poorly developed, and there is almost no secondary structure. Conversely, in young woody stems with closely arranged bundles that almost form a cylinder, the cambium is nearly a complete ring from the start. In roots the vascular cambium is formed jointly by the parenchyma inside the primary phloem and the pericycle opposite the xylem ridges, and is wavy at first ([7.12.20](/7.12.20#sec-20.5)).

### 22.1.2. Cellular Composition and Division of the Cambium

Cambial cells are of two types (Figure 2):

**Definition 22.1.2 (Fusiform Initial and Ray Initial)** The initials of the vascular cambium that are sharply pointed at both ends, several to many times longer than wide, wider on the tangential than on the radial face, and with the long axis parallel to the stem axis are called *fusiform initials*; the initials that are slightly elongated to nearly isodiametric and lie in groups between the fusiform initials are called *ray initials*.

The derivatives produced by division of the fusiform initials differentiate into the various cells of the axial system: vessel elements, tracheids, xylem fibers and xylem parenchyma, and sieve tube elements, companion cells, phloem fibers and phloem parenchyma; their long axes agree with those of the fusiform initials and are all parallel to the stem axis. Derivatives of the ray initials differentiate into radially arranged ray cells, which make up the radial system perpendicular to the stem axis. Both kinds of initials are themselves continually renewed and always keep their ability to divide. The length of the fusiform initials varies with the plant, from about 0.1–0.5 mm in angiosperms to over 3–5 mm in conifers. In the cambium they are in some plants staggered (nonstoried cambium) and in others arranged in neat horizontal tiers (storied cambium, as in black locust and padauk); the latter produces wood in which regular horizontal striations (ripple marks) can be seen on the tangential face.

How the cambium produces secondary vascular tissue and rays depends on the mode of division:

- Periclinal (tangential) division: cambial cells produce secondary vascular tissue by periclinal divisions. The result is an increase in the number of cell layers: the inward derivatives (xylem mother cells) differentiate into secondary xylem, added outside the primary xylem, and the outward derivatives (phloem mother cells) differentiate into secondary phloem, added inside the primary phloem (Figure 2B). In theory the cambial initials form a single layer, but during active division new derivatives have already formed while older derivatives are still dividing, and derivatives usually divide once or several times more before differentiating, so initials and derivatives are hard to distinguish. The layer consisting of the initials and the undifferentiated derivatives still dividing periclinally is therefore generally called the cambial zone, which is what is commonly meant by the "cambium". Because all derivatives are produced by repeated periclinal divisions of the same initial, the cells of the secondary xylem and secondary phloem are arranged in neat radial files in cross section, an important mark for recognizing secondary tissue.
- Multiplicative division: as the stem thickens, the circumference of the cambium must increase and its position shift outward to enclose the growing secondary xylem, which requires an increase in the number of cambial initials themselves. The divisions by which the cambium increases its own initials are called *multiplicative divisions*. Those of fusiform initials take three forms: radial anticlinal division, in which a fusiform initial divides vertically or nearly so into two, and tangential growth of the daughter cells widens the tangential face; lateral anticlinal division, in which a new cell is cut off on one side; and pseudotransverse division, an oblique anticlinal division almost like a transverse division, in which the two daughter cells slide past each other at their tips and invade the space between neighboring cells along the middle lamella (intrusive growth), so that the two daughter cells end up side by side and both the length and the tangential width of the initial can increase. In woody angiosperms pseudotransverse division predominates.
- Increase of ray initials: as the stem thickens, new rays are continually added to the secondary xylem and secondary phloem. Ray initials arise from fusiform initials, by cutting off a portion from the middle of the side of a fusiform initial or a piece near the apex transversely; by dividing half or all of a fusiform initial into uniseriate ray initials; or by shrinkage and shortening of a fusiform initial. A ray initial can divide further into a file; uniseriate ray initials become biseriate or multiseriate through anticlinal divisions; and biseriate and multiseriate rays can also widen by merging. The multiplication and enlargement of ray initials also contribute somewhat to the increase in cambial circumference.

The cambium produces far more secondary xylem cells inward than secondary phloem cells outward. In woody stems two or three years old, most of the stem is secondary xylem, and in woody stems of more than ten years almost all of it is secondary xylem; the primary xylem and pith are compressed so that they are hard to recognize. Secondary xylem is therefore the source of wood, and is sometimes itself called wood.

### 22.1.3. Secondary Xylem, Secondary Phloem and Vascular Rays

The secondary xylem of the eudicot stem is similar in composition to the primary xylem and comprises vessels, tracheids, xylem parenchyma and xylem fibers, all lignified to varying degrees. Pitted vessels are the most common; scalariform and reticulate vessels are few. The size, number and distribution of vessels vary greatly among plants. Xylem parenchyma in the secondary xylem occurs in strands or tiers, in considerable amounts, and is distributed around or along the vessels in several patterns (paratracheal and apotracheal), one of the bases for identifying wood. Xylem fibers are more abundant in the secondary than in the primary xylem, especially in latewood; they are the main structure that provides mechanical support in woody stems and, apart from vessels, the main constituent. What distinguishes secondary from primary xylem in composition is that it has xylem rays.

The secondary phloem is similar in composition to the metaphloem of the primary structure and comprises sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma and phloem fibers, sometimes also sclereids, though the amount, shape and distribution of the components vary with the plant. When the secondary phloem forms, the primary phloem is pushed outward; since its cells are mostly thin-walled and easily crushed, as the stem thickens the primary phloem, apart from its fibers, often leaves only fragmentary wall remnants. In plants with bicollateral bundles the cambium lies only between the external phloem and the xylem, with no cambium or only very weak cambium between the internal phloem and the xylem, so secondary structure forms only on the outside.

**Definition 22.1.3 (Vascular Ray)** The radially arranged groups of parenchyma cells in the secondary xylem and secondary phloem produced by the ray initials are called *xylem rays* and *phloem rays* respectively; the two are connected through the ray initials of the cambium and together are called the *vascular ray*, also the secondary ray.

Xylem ray cells are parenchyma, but their walls are often lignified; phloem ray cells have unlignified walls and are not as regular in shape as xylem ray cells. Water in vessels or tracheids can be conducted laterally through the vascular rays to the cambium and secondary phloem, and organic compounds in the sieve tubes can likewise be conducted through them to the cambium and secondary xylem; ray cells also store starch and other substances, and in winter wood the rays are the main storage site. Vascular rays and pith rays are similar in direction and function, but entirely different in origin, position and number. Vascular rays are produced by ray initials, lie within the secondary xylem and secondary phloem, and are not fixed in number but increase as new vascular tissue forms and the stem thickens. Pith rays are produced by the ground meristem, lie between the primary vascular bundles, connect the pith inward with the cortex outward, and continue to lengthen during secondary growth, but their number stays fixed. In the outer part of the secondary phloem the rays often dilate as the circumference enlarges, their cells dividing more, to form wedge-shaped dilated rays (as in the phloem of linden).

In cross section the secondary phloem is far narrower and thinner than the secondary xylem, for two reasons: the cambium divides outward less often than inward, and the secondary phloem is functional for a shorter time; the conducting function of sieve tubes generally lasts no more than one or two years, and once the cork cambium arises in the secondary phloem, the secondary phloem outside the cork is destroyed and dies, becoming part of the rhytidome (Section 22.3). Many plants have secretory structures in the secondary phloem that produce special saps, which are important industrial raw materials: latex from the laticifers of the rubber tree is processed into natural rubber, and lacquer from the resin canals of the lacquer tree is made into raw lacquer; jute, paper mulberry and others have well-developed phloem fibers that can be used for textiles, rope and paper. When tapping rubber the knife may cut only into the secondary phloem and must not injure the cambium, or the bark cannot regenerate.

## 22.2. Wood

### 22.2.1. Growth Rings

Cambial activity is strongly influenced by the seasons. In temperate and subtropical regions with marked warm and cold seasons, or in the tropics with wet and dry seasons, cambial activity waxes and wanes rhythmically with the season, producing cells that are more or fewer, larger or smaller, thicker- or thinner-walled, so that the structure of the secondary xylem shows marked differences.

**Definition 22.2.1 (Earlywood, Latewood and Growth Ring)** The secondary xylem formed when the cambium is vigorous early in the growing season (spring in the temperate zone, the wet season in the tropics), with cells of large diameter and thin walls, loose texture and pale color, is called *earlywood* (also spring wood); the secondary xylem formed when cambial activity weakens late in the growing season (late summer and early autumn in the temperate zone, the dry season in the tropics), with cells of small diameter and thick walls, dense texture and darker color, is called *latewood* (also summer wood or autumn wood). The concentric layer made up of the earlywood and latewood of one growing season is called a **growth ring**, and represents the secondary xylem formed in one year.

From earlywood to latewood the change is gradual with the season; although color and texture differ, there is no sharp boundary. Between the latewood of the previous year and the earlywood of the current year, however, the shape, size and wall thickness of the cells differ greatly, and the boundary is very distinct (Figure 4). In temperate regions winters are cold and dry, the cambium rests temporarily and becomes active again in spring; the greater the climatic change, the more marked the difference between earlywood and latewood. The latewood of conifers consists mainly of tracheids that are narrow radially and very thick-walled, and the density and strength of the wood depend mainly on the proportion of latewood.

In regions with marked seasonal climate the secondary xylem of many plants normally forms one ring each year, so the rings are customarily called annual rings. But many plants form more than one ring in a normal year; for example the stem of *Citrus* can form three rings in a year, so that three rings represent one year's growth, and these are called false rings. Abnormal climate, insect attack, repeated alternation of warm and cold spells or of leaf fall and flushing can make cambial activity alternately blocked and revived and form false rings. Conversely, in a year of severe drought or insect damage the cambium may be completely inactive, producing a "missing ring". In tropical regions without a dry–wet seasonal change trees generally form no distinct rings. The term "annual ring" is therefore not strictly correct, but it is widely used.

The age of a tree can be determined from the number of rings at the base of the trunk; higher up the trunk there are fewer rings, because the stem at each height begins secondary growth only after the apex has grown up to that point, and once a stem segment has begun secondary growth it no longer elongates. The rings also record the tree's growth in past years, management practices and changes of climate.

### 22.2.2. Heartwood and Sapwood

The cambium produces secondary xylem every year, and the secondary xylem accumulates in large amounts year by year; in old perennial stems the properties of the inner and outer secondary xylem change, giving heartwood and sapwood.

**Definition 22.2.2 (Heartwood and Sapwood)** The part of the secondary xylem near the center of the stem, formed earlier, whose cells are all dead, which has lost its conducting and storage functions and is often darker in color, is called **heartwood**; the part near the bark, formed more recently, containing living parenchyma cells, still able to conduct and store, paler and often wetter, is called **sapwood** (also called alburnum).

The vessels and tracheids of heartwood lose their conducting function, partly because they lie deep in the stem, where nutrients and oxygen penetrate with difficulty and the tissue ages and dies, and partly because neighboring parenchyma cells bulge through the pits into the lumen of the vessel or tracheid and form partial or complete blockages, the **tyloses** ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)); the bordered pits of conifers become closed when the torus is displaced to one side (pit aspiration). During heartwood formation, ray and xylem parenchyma cells synthesize and accumulate resins, tannins, oils, pigments and other secondary metabolites before they die, and these permeate the cell walls and lumina; heartwood formation is now regarded as a way in which the plant removes secondary metabolites that might harm living cells from the growing region. These substances make many heartwoods hard, wear-resistant and decay-resistant and give them characteristic colors: the heartwood of mahogany is red, of walnut brown, of ebony (*Diospyros*) black, and that of padauk and Dalbergia is the prized rosewood, which gives heartwood great value in craftsmanship.

The secondary xylem produced each year forms new sapwood, while the inner sapwood gradually becomes heartwood as it loses its conducting function and its cells die, so that heartwood increases year by year while the thickness of the sapwood stays fairly constant. The proportion of heartwood to sapwood, and the color and distinctness of the heartwood, vary with the plant: maple, birch and ash have thick sapwood, black locust, catalpa and yew have thin sapwood, and poplar, willow and fir show no obvious difference between heartwood and sapwood. Although solid heartwood has lost its conducting function, it forms a hard central axis that increases the load-bearing and supporting capacity of tall trees. Some woody plants form no heartwood, or soft heartwood that is easily attacked by fungi and rots into a hollow; as long as the sapwood is intact, the tree can still live, but it is liable to be broken by wind and rain. Such old trees need the rotten hollow filled with reinforcing material.

### 22.2.3. Reaction Wood

Leaning trunks and branches form a special kind of wood that restores or maintains their original orientation, called reaction wood. In conifers reaction wood forms on the lower side of the leaning part and is called compression wood: cambial activity is stronger on the lower side, the rings are eccentric and especially wide on the lower side, the tracheids are round in cross section with very thick walls and have much lignin and little cellulose, and their tendency to elongate "pushes" the branch or trunk upright. In angiosperms reaction wood forms on the upper side of the leaning part and is called tension wood: cambial activity is stronger on the upper side, and the innermost layer of the secondary wall of the wood fibers is a gelatinous layer (gelatinous fiber) that is almost unlignified and very rich in cellulose; its contraction "pulls" the branch or trunk upright. When reaction wood dries, its longitudinal shrinkage can be more than ten times that of normal wood, so boards warp and crack; apart from use as fuel it is almost useless and is a defect to be avoided in wood processing.

### 22.2.4. The Three Planes of Wood

To understand the three-dimensional structure of the secondary xylem fully, one must compare observations on three planes (Figure 3).

- Transverse section: the plane perpendicular to the long axis of the stem. Vessels, tracheids, xylem parenchyma and wood fibers are all seen in transverse view, showing cell diameters and cross-sectional shapes; the growth rings appear as concentric rings; the rays appear as radial bands, which are longitudinal sections of rays, showing their length and width.
- Radial section: a longitudinal section through the center of the stem, that is, along a diameter. Vessels, fibers and rays are all seen in longitudinal view, and the cells are fairly regularly arranged; the ray cells are perpendicular to the long axis, and rectangular cells in several rows resemble a brick wall, showing the height and length of the ray; the growth rings appear as parallel longitudinal stripes.
- Tangential section: a longitudinal section perpendicular to a radius that does not pass through the center of the stem. Vessels, tracheids and so on are seen in longitudinal view, showing length, width and the shape of the ends; the rays are seen in cross section, with a spindle-shaped outline, showing ray height, width, the number of cell rows and the shape of the end cells; the growth rings appear as a "V" or parabolic pattern.

Of the three planes, the shape of the rays is the most conspicuous and serves as the indicator for telling the plane:

| Plane | Appearance of the rays | Shows the ray's | Vessels, fibers | Growth rings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Transverse | Radial thin bands | Length and width | Transverse view (pores) | Concentric rings |
| Radial | Horizontal "brick wall" | Length and height | Longitudinal view | Parallel longitudinal stripes |
| Tangential | Spindle-shaped, uniseriate or multiseriate | Height and width (number of rows) | Longitudinal view | "V"-shaped pattern |

In wood processing, boards sawn along the radius (quartersawn boards) shrink little and warp little, and the surface shows the bright figure formed by the rays (the "silver grain" of oak); flatsawn (tangential) boards show the beautiful parabolic growth-ring figure. The science that studies the anatomy of the secondary xylem is wood anatomy (xylotomy): only by understanding the anatomical structure of wood thoroughly can one judge and compare the properties, quality and uses of woods, and wood structure also provides evidence for the phylogeny and relationships of plants.

### 22.2.5. Ring-Porous Wood, Diffuse-Porous Wood and Conifer Wood

The secondary xylem of broad-leaved trees (woody eudicots) has vessels, which appear as many small pores in cross section, so hardwood is also called porous wood (hardwood in the timber trade); the wood of conifers (gymnosperms) has no vessels but only tracheids and is called nonporous wood (softwood; here "softwood" is distinct from cork, the bark tissue). According to the distribution of vessels in the growth ring, hardwoods fall into three types (Figure 4):

| Type | Distribution of vessels | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Ring-porous wood | Earlywood vessels markedly larger than those of latewood, forming a distinct ring along the growth ring | *Quercus*, *Fraxinus*, black locust, elm, mulberry, chestnut |
| Diffuse-porous wood | Vessels of earlywood and latewood of roughly equal diameter, evenly dispersed in the ring | Poplar, willow, birch, maple, linden, plane tree, camphor, boxwood, tulip tree |
| Semi-ring-porous wood (semi-diffuse-porous) | Earlywood vessels larger and slightly ring-forming, gradually smaller toward the latewood, with an indistinct boundary | Walnut, wingnut, Chinese tallow tree, cherry |

The large vessels of ring-porous wood form only at the start of the growth ring, and water conduction takes place almost entirely in the outermost ring, at a rate about ten times that in diffuse-porous wood; but large vessels easily lose function through freeze-induced embolism in winter, so ring-porous trees must form new earlywood vessels each spring before they can leaf out, and they leaf out late. Diffuse-porous wood has narrower vessels that better resist freezing embolism, and rings of many years can take part in water conduction. This is a trade-off between conducting efficiency and safety, consistent with the Hagen–Poiseuille relation for vessel diameter ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)).

The structure of conifer wood is simple and uniform: the axial system is almost entirely tracheids (more than 90 % of the volume), which communicate through bordered pits with a torus in the center of the pit membrane; earlywood tracheids have thin walls and large lumina, and latewood tracheids are narrow radially with thick walls; the rays are generally uniseriate and made of ray parenchyma cells, and in the Pinaceae and others the rays also contain ray tracheids; the woods of *Pinus*, *Picea*, *Larix* and *Pseudotsuga* have axial and radial resin canals (Section 22.4). Because there is no differentiation into vessels and wood fibers, conifer wood has a straight, even grain and is the main material for construction and paper.

### 22.2.6. Dendrochronology

**Definition 22.2.3 (Dendrochronology)** The discipline that uses year-to-year variation in the width, density and isotopes of tree rings to determine the year in which each ring formed and, on that basis, to study past climate, environment and events is called *dendrochronology*.

Ring width is influenced by that year's light, temperature, precipitation, soil moisture and the length of the growing season: rings are wide in favorable years and narrow in unfavorable ones. In semiarid regions the main limiting factor is precipitation, and trees become sensitive "rain gauges"; at alpine treelines and in high latitudes growing-season temperature is the main limiting factor. Since trees in the same region are influenced by the same climate, their ring-width series share the same pattern of wide and narrow rings, like a bar code. Aligning the series of living trees by pattern with those of dead trees, timbers of old buildings and archaeological wood (crossdating) can extend the chronology far into the past: the oldest living bristlecone pine (*Pinus longaeva*) in the Great Basin of the western United States is about 4800 years old, and continuous ring sequences linked to the wood of dead trees extend over 8000 years; in Europe continuous ring sequences more than ten thousand years long have been built with oak and pine and used to calibrate radiocarbon dating.

Reconstructing climate from rings first requires removing growth trends unrelated to climate. As a tree ages its rings tend to narrow (the same amount of wood is spread over an ever larger circumference), so the raw series is first fitted with a curve that decreases monotonically with age (such as a negative exponential curve), and the measured width is then divided by the fitted value to give a dimensionless ring index; this step is called standardization. After the indices of many trees are averaged, a regression relation with instrumental meteorological records is established, and the ring index can be used to infer the climate of years without instrumental records (Figure 5). Rings also record fires (fire scars), insect outbreaks, frosts, cold years after volcanic eruptions, and earthquakes. This kind of analysis is an application of time series and regression methods ([3.3](/3.3)).

## 22.3. Cork Cambium and Periderm

### 22.3.1. Origin and Activity of the Cork Cambium

Cambial activity steadily increases the secondary vascular tissue, especially the secondary xylem, and the diameter of the stem keeps growing. Epidermal cells generally cannot divide and so cannot grow correspondingly; before long they are ruptured by the pressure from internal growth and lose their protective function. In the early stage of secondary growth, cells in some part near the outside of the stem resume meristematic ability and form another secondary meristem, the **cork cambium**; the cork it produces replaces the protective role of the epidermis ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)). The cork cambium is simpler in structure than the vascular cambium, containing only one kind of initial, a narrow rectangle in cross section and a fairly regular polygon in tangential section. It divides mainly periclinally, producing **phellem** (cork layer) toward the outside and **phelloderm** toward the inside; the three together make up the **periderm**.

The first cork cambium to form has different origins in different plants (Figure 6):

| Origin of the first cork cambium | Examples |
|---|---|
| Epidermis | Mediterranean cork oak (*Quercus suber*), willow, pear, apple |
| The cortical layer immediately below the epidermis (most common) | Poplar, walnut, elm |
| Second and third layers of the cortex | Black locust, Dutchman's pipe |
| Parenchyma near the phloem (primary phloem) | Grape, pomegranate, tea |

The period of activity of the cork cambium varies with the plant, and in most plants it is active for only a few months. In some plants, however, the first cork cambium is active longer: in pear and apple it persists for 6–8 years, in pomegranate and a few species of *Populus* and *Prunus* for two or three decades, and in Mediterranean cork oak and beech it can be active for life without a new cork cambium ever forming, so that the bark of beech and (when young) white birch stays smooth.

In some woody plants the stem epidermis matures late and its cells can still divide as the stem thickens, persisting for many years and keeping pace with the growth of internal tissue, as in camphor; the epidermis of Chinese parasol tree has a long life and can delay periderm formation, so the branches and trunk of this tree stay green for many years.

### 22.3.2. Repeated Formation of Periderm and Bark

When the first cork cambium ceases activity, a new cork cambium arises on its inner side and forms a new periderm; thereafter, old is steadily replaced by new, each successive cork cambium arising farther inward and deeper, in old trunks often as deep as the secondary phloem (Figure 6). Each time a new periderm forms, all living tissues outside it (cortex, primary and secondary phloem) die one after another because their supply of water and nutrients is cut off, and the result is a harder and gradually thickening layer on the outside of the stem.

**Definition 22.3.1 (Bark and Rhytidome)** In plant anatomy, all tissues outside the vascular cambium are called **bark**. On older woody stems the bark comprises a dead outer bark and a living inner bark: the innermost periderm together with all the dead tissue outside it (repeatedly formed periderms and the dead phloem and cortex enclosed between them) is called the *rhytidome*, that is, the outer bark (hard bark); the innermost cork cambium, the phelloderm (if present) and the functional phloem inside it are called the inner bark (soft bark).

The bark in the secondary state thus includes the secondary phloem and any primary tissue remaining outside it, the periderm, and all dead tissue outside the periderm; the so-called bark of a stem in the primary state includes only the primary phloem, cortex and epidermis. The word "bark" has different meanings in everyday life and in forestry: people often call the rough dead outer layer of the stem bark, whereas bark stripped from a felled tree includes the living inner bark (it separates along the cambium, whose thin-walled cells are easiest to part in the growing season). "Bark" is not a strictly technical term, but given the anatomical definition above it becomes a very useful one.

The appearance of the rhytidome depends on the shape and arrangement of the periderms. When new periderms are in the form of plates that overlap one another, the outer bark flakes off in scales (scaly bark, as in the mottled bark of pine and plane tree); when a new periderm forms a complete cylinder, the outer bark peels off in rings (ring bark, as in grape, the papery peeling of birch, and cherry); when the rhytidome does not flake off but accumulates year after year, its surface cracks into deep furrows (as on the old trunks of oak, black locust and poplar). The cork layers in the rhytidome are impermeable to water, insulating, and rich in defensive substances such as tannins, protecting the trunk against drought, fire and pests: the bark of giant sequoia, Douglas fir and many savanna trees is very thick and can withstand forest fires. Lenticels often occur at the bottoms of cracks in the rhytidome ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)).

The bark of eucommia, silk tree, Amur cork tree, magnolia bark (*Magnolia officinalis*), cinnamon and cinchona is of great economic value. In the past such bark was often obtained by felling trees, seriously damaging the resource. Extensive girdling of bark was long thought to cut off the transport of organic compounds and kill the whole plant ("a tree fears having its bark stripped"), yet farmers often girdle pear, apple, apricot and other trees moderately to increase yield. Anatomical studies of bark regeneration after stripping in eucommia (by Li Zhengli, Cui Keming and colleagues) found that stripping at a suitable time and by a suitable method allows new bark to regenerate almost always. After stripping, most immature xylem elements near the surface soon change into suberized cells that form a protective layer, and a cork cambium then gradually arises inside the protective layer and forms a periderm; deeper in the xylem, some immature xylem elements gradually convert into a new vascular cambium, which joins from discontinuous small patches into a complete ring and differentiates new xylem and phloem inward and outward. The new bark is therefore not regenerated from remnant cambium, as long believed.

### 22.3.3. Cork Oak and Cork

The first cork cambium of the Mediterranean cork oak (*Q. suber*), if not stripped, is active throughout life and produces a very thick cork layer, which is commercial cork; the Chinese cork oak (*Q. variabilis*) also has a well-developed cork layer and is the source of cork produced in China. Cork consists of tightly packed suberized dead cells filled with air, and is therefore light, elastic, impermeable to water and gas, insulating, sound-absorbing and decay-resistant; it is used for bottle stoppers, insulation and sound-proofing material, flooring and life-saving equipment. In harvesting, the cork layer is stripped off along the cork cambium without injuring the phloem and vascular cambium inside it (in *Q. suber* the first harvest is at about 25 years of age, and thereafter about once every nine years); the original cork cambium dies and a new cork cambium forms on the inside, and the regenerated cork it produces is of better quality. The "cells" that Hooke saw under the microscope in 1665 were the cork cells of cork ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)).

## 22.4. Secondary Structure of Gymnosperm Stems

All gymnosperms are woody, and the structure of the stem is basically the same as the woody stem of eudicots: it consists of epidermis, cortex and vascular cylinder, has a long-lived vascular cambium that produces secondary structure, thickens the stem year by year, and shows distinct growth rings. The difference lies in the composition of the vascular tissue:

1. Secondary xylem: the secondary xylem of most gymnosperms consists mainly of tracheids, xylem parenchyma and rays; there are no vessels (only gnetophytes have vessels) and no typical wood fibers. Tracheids both conduct water and provide support; compared with the secondary xylem of eudicots it is simpler and more primitive, and in cross section it is evenly and neatly arranged (Section 22.2.5, Figure 4C). The secondary xylem of gymnosperms also shows earlywood, latewood, heartwood and sapwood. The rays of pine are generally uniseriate, several to a dozen cells high, composed of ray parenchyma cells and ray tracheids at the upper and lower edges of the ray; rays that cross a resin canal become fusiform multiseriate rays.
2. Secondary phloem: also simple in structure, consisting of sieve cells, phloem parenchyma (including albuminous cells) and rays, generally without companion cells or phloem fibers; the secondary phloem of some conifers (such as the Cupressaceae) has tangential bands of phloem fibers and sclereids.
3. Resin canals: in the cortex and vascular cylinder (phloem, xylem, pith and even rays) of the stem of many conifers there are tubular secretory structures, the resin canals. Resin canals are schizogenous, lined by a layer of epithelial cells that secrete resin ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)). The woods of pine, spruce, larch and Douglas fir have normal axial and radial resin canals; fir, hemlock, cedar and others normally have none and form traumatic resin canals only after wounding. Pine resin is produced by the resin canals of pines and distilled into rosin and turpentine; resin flowing from a wound hardens and seals it, protecting against insects and pathogens, and amber is fossil resin from ancient times. The woody stems of eudicots have no resin canals (some families have similar secretory canals, such as Dipterocarpaceae).

## 22.5. Secondary Growth in Monocots

Most monocots have no vascular cambium and hence no secondary growth; their stems thicken through cell enlargement and periclinal division of the primary thickening meristem ([7.12.21](/7.12.21#sec-21.5.3)). The thickening of palm stems is entirely primary: the young palm first forms a broad shoot apex near the ground by means of the primary thickening meristem, and only when the stem has reached its mature thickness does it begin to elongate upward, a phase called establishment growth; thereafter the stem thickness remains essentially constant, with only continued slow enlargement of ground-tissue cells and lignification of walls thickening the stem slightly, so the palm trunk is nearly equally thick from bottom to top and has no growth rings.

A few tropical or subtropical monocots have secondary growth and secondary structure in addition to the usual primary structure, such as *Dracaena*, *Cordyline*, *Yucca*, *Aloe* and *Sansevieria* (mostly in the Asparagaceae). The formation and activity of their cambium differ from those of eudicots: generally a cambium arises in the cortex outside the primary vascular tissue and forms new vascular tissue (secondary vascular bundles), arranged in a pattern that varies with the plant. Take dragon tree (*Dracaena draco*) as an example. Parenchyma cells outside the vascular bundles turn into a cambium that divides tangentially, producing a small number of parenchyma cells outward and a ring of ground tissue inward; some cells in this ring are smaller in diameter and longer and appear in strands that later differentiate into secondary vascular bundles (Figure 7). The secondary bundles are also scattered, denser than the primary ones, and differ from them in structure: they contain less phloem and the xylem, composed of tracheids, surrounds the phloem, forming amphivasal bundles, whereas the primary bundles are collateral with xylem composed of vessels. Because this cambium can be active for many years, *Dracaena* can grow into a stout, branching tree, and the trunks of old dragon trees in the Canary Islands can reach several meters in diameter. In monocots, therefore, "having secondary growth" does not mean producing secondary xylem and secondary phloem but producing entire secondary vascular bundles.

## 22.6. Anomalous Secondary Structure

The secondary growth described so far, in which a single vascular cambium continually produces secondary xylem inward and secondary phloem outward, is called normal secondary growth. In many plants the position, number or mode of activity of the vascular cambium differs from this and produces anomalous secondary structure (anomalous secondary growth, also called cambial variants in recent literature); the common kinds are the following (Figure 7):

1. Successive cambia: after the normal cambium has been active for a time it ceases, and a new cambium arises in the parenchyma of the phloem or cortex outside it; this repeats and forms several concentric rings of vascular tissue, each with xylem and phloem, with parenchyma between the rings. The concentric rings of the beet storage root (formed by repeated activity of extra cambia derived from the pericycle, [7.12.24](/7.12.24)) are a typical example; the stems of many plants of the Amaranthaceae (including the former Chenopodiaceae), Nyctaginaceae and Phytolaccaceae, and of some lianas such as *Gnetum*, Menispermaceae and some Fabaceae, have this structure. Structure produced by extra cambia outside the normal secondary structure is called "tertiary structure" in Chinese textbooks.
2. Extra cambia arising within the xylem (with included phloem): parts of the cambium produce some phloem inward, so that strands of phloem are embedded in the secondary xylem (included phloem, or interxylary phloem), as in some species of *Strychnos* and *Combretum*; the extra cambium in the radish storage root formed by renewed division of xylem parenchyma cells ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)) also belongs here.
3. Uneven activity of cambial segments (furrowed xylem): in some sectors the cambium produces less xylem inward and more phloem outward, so that the xylem forms four or more deep grooves in cross section filled with phloem, as in many lianas of the Bignoniaceae (trumpet creeper and others, the "cross-shaped" xylem); or the cambium splits into several segments, each acting around a mass of xylem, so that the stem becomes a twisted cable of several strands (some lianas of *Bauhinia* and some Sapindaceae).
4. Anomalous vascular bundles in storage roots: in the storage root of fleeceflower, many single or compound anomalous bundles form in the cortex around the normal vascular cylinder, each with its own cambium, giving a "brocade pattern" in cross section, a diagnostic feature for identifying the drug; in the storage root of sweet potato, parenchyma cells around the vessels produce many extra cambia ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)).

Anomalous secondary structure is commonest in lianas, and this is no accident: the slender stem of a liana must bend and twist around its support, and its xylem is divided by parenchyma or phloem into several strands, so that the stem can conduct large amounts of water (liana vessels are often extremely wide and long) and is flexible and hard to break, and after injury the living parenchyma in each strand can repair it.

## 22.7. Woody Stems, Herbaceous Stems and Tree Form

### 22.7.1. Woody and Herbaceous Stems

In summary, stems are either woody or herbaceous: gymnosperms have only woody stems, eudicots have both woody and herbaceous stems, and most monocots have herbaceous stems.

1. Woody stem: in the course of plant evolution woody stems appeared earlier, and the fact that gymnosperms have only woody stems is evidence of this (secondary xylem first appears in the Middle Devonian in progymnosperms and some lycophytes and sphenophytes, [7.12.16](/7.12.16), [7.12.25](/7.12.25)). The secondary structure of woody stems is well developed, lignified tissue makes up over 70 %, the texture is hard, and the stems are thick, in many cases more than 50 cm in diameter. Plants with woody stems are called **woody plants** ([7.12.1](/7.12.1)); their lifespan is generally decades to over a hundred years, even over a thousand years. The older stems of most woody plants have a rough surface covered by bark composed of cork and other tissues, with lenticels on the bark; the buds are mostly covered by bud scales over winter. While the epidermis of a young woody stem is still present, the tissue just inside it contains chlorophyll and can photosynthesize; in most woody plants the epidermis is found only in the primary part, and once periderm replaces the epidermis the stem loses its ability to photosynthesize. The epidermis of woody stems generally matures early, and new shoots form cork during their first growing season. Most woody stems around a year old consist mainly of primary structure and resemble herbaceous stems in appearance and structure; as the stem ages the woody features gradually develop.
2. Herbaceous stem: herbaceous stems are derived from woody types (in angiosperms herbs evolved from woody plants many times independently, and some evolved back to woody, such as some woody Asteraceae on islands). Herbaceous stems are generally soft and green with no, or very little, lignified tissue (no more than 40 %), cannot grow very thick, and generally remain at the primary stage. Plants with herbaceous stems are called **herbs** ([7.12.1](/7.12.1)); their lifespan is often short, generally annual or biennial; some herbs have annual aerial stems and perennial roots or underground stems and live many years (perennial herbs), and their roots can be woody, as in hollyhock, larkspur and columbine. In herbs such as sunflower and cotton the stem forms some lignified tissue, but in small amounts, and it remains herbaceous. In most herbs the epidermis of the stem persists for a long time and has stomata; the tissue inside the epidermis has chlorophyll, is green and can photosynthesize; support of the stem depends on collenchyma, sclerenchyma and the turgor of parenchyma cells. Vascular tissue makes up a small proportion of the herbaceous stem, a result of the poor development and reduced activity of the vascular cambium: some Cucurbitaceae have only fascicular cambium and no interfascicular cambium, and buttercup does not even develop fascicular cambium well, so that secondary structure is slight or absent.

The type of stem is not fixed: tomato and castor bean are annual herbaceous stems in cooler temperate regions but become perennial woody stems in the tropics.

### 22.7.2. Tree Longevity and Tree Form

Trees are the longest-lived and largest organisms on Earth. The oldest known living individual is a bristlecone pine on White Mountain in California, about 4800 years old by ring count; ancient trees such as the "Xuanyuan cypress" (*Platycladus orientalis*) at Huangling in Shaanxi and the Taiwan red cypress (*Chamaecyparis formosensis*) on Alishan in Taiwan are also estimated to be several thousand years old. The largest single tree by volume is the giant sequoia "General Sherman" in California (about $1500\,\mathrm{m^3}$), and the tallest is the coast redwood (about 116 m). Tree height is limited by the physics of water transport: the water column in the xylem rises by transpirational pull, and the taller the tree, the lower the water potential in the leaves, which limits leaf growth and photosynthesis; the maximum height of trees is estimated at about 120–130 m (Koch et al. 2004). The thickness of the trunk also follows mechanical laws: a tall trunk is like an elastic column, and if it is not to buckle under its own weight (Euler buckling, [7.6.26](/7.6.26#sec-26.2.1)), the critical height $H_{\mathrm{c}}$ and the diameter $D$ satisfy

$$
H_{\mathrm{c}} \propto \left(\frac{E}{\rho g}\right)^{1/3} D^{2/3},
\tag{22.1}
$$

where $E$ is the elastic modulus of the wood and $\rho$ its density. The taller the tree, the faster the trunk must thicken, as $D \propto H^{3/2}$, so large trees always have relatively stouter trunks than young ones (the elastic similarity model of McMahon 1973); most real trees stand at about a quarter of the critical height, leaving a large safety margin.

The overall form of a tree is determined by the branching pattern ([7.12.21](/7.12.21#sec-21.2.2)), the growth direction and length proportions of branches of each order, and the effect of flowering on the branch tip. The development of a shoot can be viewed as repeated production of the same "module" (internode, leaf, axillary bud), so tree form can be described by recursive rules. The L-system (Lindenmayer system, Lindenmayer 1968) is such a formal grammar: starting from an initial string, at each step all symbols are replaced simultaneously according to production rules (unlike grammars in formal language theory, which rewrite one symbol at a time, the rewriting of an L-system is parallel; for formal grammars see [4.8.2](/4.8.2)), and the resulting string is interpreted as drawing commands (grow forward a segment, turn left, turn right, begin a branch, end the branch). For example, the production $A \to F[+A][-A]$ turns every terminal $A$ at each step into a stem segment $F$ and two lateral branches, and a few iterations give a binary tree form; adding decreasing length, branching angle and bending toward gravity to the lateral branches allows simulation of the pagoda-shaped crown of monopodial branching (spruce, fir), the umbrella-shaped crown of sympodial branching (frangipani), weeping forms (weeping willow), unbranched palms, and the dichotomous branching of early land plants (Figure 8). This correspondence between tree form and branching pattern shows once more that secondary growth determines only "thickness", while "shape" is determined by the apical meristems and the buds they produce.

## Summary

Items:

- Secondary growth is carried out by lateral meristems: the vascular cambium (Definition 22.1.1) = fascicular cambium (retained procambium) + interfascicular cambium (pith ray cells resuming division).
- Cambial cells: fusiform initials (axial system) and ray initials (radial system) (Definition 22.1.2); periclinal divisions produce secondary vascular tissue (cells in radial files, the cambial zone), and multiplicative divisions (radial anticlinal, lateral anticlinal, pseudotransverse division and intrusive growth) enlarge the circumference; ray initials arise from fusiform initials. Far more cells are produced inward than outward.
- Secondary xylem (vessels mostly pitted, wood fibers, xylem parenchyma, xylem rays); secondary phloem (sieve tubes active for only a year or two; laticifers, lacquer canals, phloem fibers); vascular rays (Definition 22.1.3) versus pith rays.
- Growth ring (Definition 22.2.1): earlywood and latewood, false rings and missing rings; heartwood and sapwood (Definition 22.2.2), tyloses and pit aspiration, heartwood substances; reaction wood: compression wood in conifers (lower side), tension wood in angiosperms (upper side, gelatinous fibers).
- Three planes: transverse (rays radial, rings concentric), radial (rays like a brick wall), tangential (rays spindle-shaped); ring-porous (oak, ash), diffuse-porous (poplar, maple), semi-ring-porous (walnut); conifer wood (tracheids, bordered pits, uniseriate rays, resin canals). Dendrochronology (Definition 22.2.3): crossdating, standardization, climate reconstruction.
- Cork cambium: origin (epidermis, outer cortex, deeper cortex, primary phloem), period of activity, repeated formation and inward shift; bark (Definition 22.3.1) = all tissue outside the vascular cambium = rhytidome (outer bark) + inner bark; scaly and ring bark; regeneration after stripping; cork oak and cork.
- Gymnosperms: tracheids, sieve cells, no vessels and wood fibers (gnetophytes have vessels), ray tracheids, resin canals.
- Monocots: palms rely on primary thickening and establishment growth; *Dracaena* has a cambium in the cortex that produces amphivasal secondary bundles.
- Anomalous secondary structure: successive cambia ("tertiary structure"; beet, Amaranthaceae), included phloem, furrowed xylem (bignoniaceous lianas), anomalous bundles (fleeceflower).
- Woody and herbaceous stems; tree longevity and height limit, elastic similarity and Eq. (22.1); L-systems and tree form.

## Questions

1. A nail is driven into a tree trunk 1 m above the ground. How high above the ground will the nail be after ten years? Will it be enclosed by wood?

   **Answer** Still 1 m above the ground. Elongation of the stem takes place only at the tip (and at the bases of internodes), and once a trunk segment has begun secondary growth it no longer elongates, so the height of the nail does not change. Secondary growth thickens the trunk: each year the cambium adds a new growth ring outside the xylem, and if the head of the nail is driven only into the bark and outer wood, it is gradually enclosed by new wood and bark, and after ten years it is buried about ten growth rings below the surface.

2. Why does girdling a ring of bark gradually kill a tree, whereas stripping a longitudinal strip of bark does not? Why can eucommia regenerate after bark is stripped at the proper time?

   **Answer** The functional phloem lies in the bark; girdling cuts all of it, so organic compounds made by the leaves cannot be transported to the roots, and the roots starve once their reserves are exhausted and the tree dies (the xylem remains, so the aboveground parts at first do not wilt). Stripping one longitudinal strip cuts only part of the phloem, and organic compounds can bypass it on both sides. When eucommia is stripped in the vigorous growing season, the immature xylem cells remaining on the exposed surface are still alive and, in humid conditions, can dedifferentiate; the outer ones form a suberized protective layer and a new cork cambium, and deeper ones form a new vascular cambium, so new bark grows; if the surface dries out after stripping, or the tree is stripped in the dormant season, these cells die and there is no regeneration.

3. A wood sample viewed in cross section shows a ring of very large pores at the start of each growth ring and very small pores elsewhere; another wood sample shows no pores at all. From what kinds of trees might each come? What is characteristic of the first kind of tree in early spring?

   **Answer** The first is ring-porous wood, from broad-leaved trees such as oak, ash, elm and black locust; the second is nonporous wood, from conifers such as pine, fir and cypress (tracheids only). The large vessels of ring-porous wood form only at the start of each growing season, and winter freezing causes embolism that disables most of the previous year's large vessels, so in spring these trees must first grow new earlywood vessels after the cambium resumes activity before they can leaf out; they leaf out later than diffuse-porous trees (poplar, willow, maple).

4. The ring width of a tree narrows steadily from the center outward. Does this mean that its growing environment is getting worse? How is this handled when rings are used to reconstruct climate?

   **Answer** Not necessarily. Even if the volume of wood produced each year stays the same, the circumference of the trunk increases with radius, so the same volume spread over a larger circumference makes the ring narrower; changes in crown and leaf area as the tree ages also affect growth. This age trend, unrelated to climate, must be removed before reconstruction: fit the raw widths of each tree with a curve that changes monotonically with age (such as a negative exponential or spline), divide the measured values by the fitted values to obtain ring indices, then average the indices of many trees so that interannual fluctuation mainly reflects the shared climate signal, and regress against instrumental meteorological data.

5. Palms and *Dracaena* are both monocot trees. How do their modes of trunk thickening differ? Why do neither have true growth rings?

   **Answer** Palms have no secondary cambium at all; the thickness of the trunk is reached mainly during juvenile establishment growth by the primary thickening meristem and thereafter stays about the same (only cell enlargement and lignification), so the trunk is equally thick from bottom to top. *Dracaena* has a cambium in the cortex that can be active for many years and produces ground tissue and amphivasal secondary bundles inward, so it thickens year by year and grows into a stout tree. Neither has a layer of secondary xylem produced inward by a single cambium and varying with the season; the secondary bundles are scattered, so there are no growth rings; the tissue produced by the *Dracaena* cambium varies in density but cannot reliably reflect years.
