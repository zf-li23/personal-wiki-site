The monocots comprise some 65,000 to 70,000 species, more than a fifth of all angiosperms, and include the most important food plants of humankind (rice, wheat, maize), sugar plants (sugarcane), oil and fruit plants (oil palm, coconut, banana, pineapple), spices (ginger, vanilla, saffron) and by far the largest number of ornamentals (orchids, lilies, tulips, irises). They are one of the few large angiosperm groups recognized as a natural group in both traditional classification and molecular phylogenetics: from de Candolle to Cronquist the monocots formed a class, and in the APG system they are a clade within the Mesangiospermae, alongside the magnoliids, Chloranthales, Ceratophyllales and the eudicots ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.5.1)). What has changed most is the internal classification: Cronquist's five subclasses have been regrouped into 11 orders, and his Liliaceae has been split into a dozen or more families placed in two orders. The monocots are treated in four chapters. This chapter gives an overview of the monocots and treats the earliest-diverging orders: Acorales, Alismatales, Petrosaviales, Dioscoreales, Pandanales and Liliales. [7.12.37](/7.12.37) treats Asparagales (Orchidaceae, Iridaceae, Amaryllidaceae, Asparagaceae and others), and [7.12.38](/7.12.38) and [7.12.39](/7.12.39) treat the commelinid orders Arecales, Commelinales, Zingiberales and Poales. For the origin of the monocots see [7.12.34](/7.12.34#sec-34.2.5); for the anatomy of roots, stems and leaves see [7.12.20](/7.12.20)–[7.12.23](/7.12.23); for the notation of floral formulas see [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.6).

## 36.1. Characters and Synapomorphies of the Monocots

### 36.1.1. The Concept of Monocots

**Definition 36.1.1 (Monocots)** The clade of angiosperms whose embryo has a single cotyledon is called the **monocots** (monocotyledons), known in traditional classification as the class Monocotyledoneae or Liliopsida. In APG IV it comprises the orders Acorales, Alismatales, Petrosaviales, Dioscoreales, Pandanales, Liliales and Asparagales, together with the commelinid orders Arecales, Poales, Commelinales and Zingiberales: 11 orders and about 77 families in all.

The status of the monocots as a **monophyletic group** is supported by nearly all molecular data (Chase et al. 2006; Soltis et al. 2018), and *Acorus* is the **sister group** of all other monocots. By contrast, the traditional "dicots" form a **paraphyletic group** ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.1.3)): the monocots are not a separate branch arising from outside the "dicots" but a lineage nested among the early-diverging angiosperms.

### 36.1.2. Synapomorphies

The following characters are widespread in monocots. Some of them are **synapomorphies** of the monocots; others are **plesiomorphies** of angiosperms that look like "monocot characters" only because the eudicots have changed:

Two explanations have been offered for the origin of the monocot leaf. One holds that the parallel-veined blade corresponds to the petiole and sheath of a dicot leaf (the blade being reduced and the petiole flattened, the "petiole hypothesis"); the other holds that the monocot blade is the expanded basal part of the whole leaf (the lower leaf zone). Developmental studies support the latter: the monocot leaf arises almost entirely from the basal meristematic zone of the leaf primordium, whereas the blade of a dicot leaf comes mainly from the upper part of the primordium (Judd et al. 2016). That the leaves of *Acorus* and of some Alismatales also have a part derived from the upper part of the primordium does not contradict this interpretation.

### 36.1.3. Exceptions

All of these characters have exceptions, and the exceptions are concentrated in a few groups, which makes them taxonomically informative:

- Reticulate venation: Araceae (taro, *Monstera*), Dioscoreaceae, Smilacaceae, Stemonaceae, and the genera *Paris* and *Trillium* of Melanthiaceae have broad, petiolate leaves with reticulate veins. Most of them grow in the forest understory, and reticulate venation is regarded as a **convergent evolution** that arose several times independently in shaded environments, not as a primitive character (Givnish et al. 2005).
- Secondary thickening: the trunk of palms thickens by a primary thickening meristem; *Dracaena*, *Cordyline*, *Yucca*, *Aloe* and *Sansevieria* form a secondary thickening meristem in the cortex that produces amphivasal bundles inward, the "anomalous secondary structure" of monocots ([7.12.22](/7.12.22#sec-22.5)).
- Number of floral parts: Potamogetonaceae and *Paris* (4 to many) have flowers with 4 or more parts per whorl; the flower of *Maianthemum* is dimerous (4 tepals, 4 stamens); Stemonaceae are dimerous.
- Cotyledons: the embryo of Orchidaceae and of some mycoheterotrophs is completely undifferentiated and has no cotyledon; conversely, a few dicots such as Nymphaeaceae and Ranunculaceae also include species with a single cotyledon ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.1.3)).

### 36.1.4. Phylogeny of the Monocots

The 11 orders of APG IV (Fig. 36.1.2) diverge in the following sequence: Acorales are sister to all other monocots; next come Alismatales; Petrosaviales are sister to all the remaining orders; Dioscoreales and Pandanales form a clade; further up come Liliales, Asparagales and the commelinids in turn. The commelinids comprise Arecales, Poales and the sister orders Commelinales and Zingiberales ([7.12.38](/7.12.38)).

This sequence differs from traditional views in two respects. First, Cronquist considered the subclass Alismatidae (aquatic, with free carpels and numerous floral parts) closest to the ancestor of the monocots, and Takhtajan derived the monocots from the aquatic Nymphaeales; in molecular phylogenies Alismatales are indeed an early-diverging lineage, but the first to branch off is the wetland genus *Acorus*, and the free carpels and numerous stamens of Alismatales are mostly secondarily increased. Second, the reticulate-veined groups traditionally regarded as "primitive" (Dioscoreaceae, Smilacaceae, *Trillium*) are scattered among several different orders, and the characters they share are the result of convergence.

The fossil record of monocots begins early: monosulcate, reticulate pollen of the *Liliacidites* type is present in the Early Cretaceous (Barremian to Aptian), and the pollen of *Mayoa portugallica* from the Early Cretaceous of Portugal resembles that of extant groups of Araceae (Friis et al. 2004), showing that Alismatales had already diverged during the early radiation of the angiosperms.

## 36.2. Acorales

Acorales comprise a single family, Acoraceae, with one genus, *Acorus*, in which two species are now generally recognized: sweet flag (*A. calamus*) and grassy-leaved sweet flag (*A. gramineus*), both native to China; the plant source of the Chinese drug "shichangpu" is often given in Chinese literature as *A. tatarinowii*, which most taxonomic treatments merge into *A. gramineus*.

Diagnostic characters: perennial wetland or marsh herbs with creeping rhizomes and a characteristic aroma throughout; leaves distichous, sword-shaped, overlapping at the base (equitant), identical on both faces (unifacial, that is, the blade develops from the abaxial side of the leaf); the scape flattened and leaf-like, its tip extended into a leaf-like "spathe" that makes the cylindrical spadix appear to arise on the side of a leaf-like stem; flowers small, bisexual, tepals 6, stamens 6, ovary superior, 2–3-locular; fruit a berry.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}3)}$.

*Acorus* was long placed in Araceae (in the Chinese textbooks and in the Engler and Cronquist systems alike) because it too has a spadix. But it differs from Araceae in many important respects: it has oil cells containing volatile oil (Araceae lack them and instead have bundles of calcium oxalate raphides and laticifers); its leaves are unifacial and sword-shaped (those of Araceae are mostly broad and petiolate); its flowers are bisexual and have a perianth; anther wall development is of the same type as in dicots; and its "spathe" is a continuation of the scape and does not enclose the inflorescence. On this basis Grayum (1987) argued for separating it as Acoraceae; later molecular analyses showed it to be the sister group of all other monocots, and the order Acorales was established. Acoraceae have thus become a key group for studying the ancestral state of the monocots: they suggest that the last common ancestor of the monocots may have been a wetland herb with oil cells.

Sweet flag grows in marshes, ponds and along streams and is often cultivated; in China it is customary to hang sweet flag and mugwort at the Dragon Boat Festival. The rhizome contains volatile oils (asarone and others) and is used in Chinese medicine to "open the orifices and resolve phlegm". European and North American sweet flag has several cytotypes, diploid, triploid and tetraploid; the European triploid sets no seed and propagates vegetatively by rhizomes, and was introduced from Asia in the 16th century. β-Asarone is carcinogenic, and the United States has prohibited the use of sweet flag as a food additive.

## 36.3. Alismatales

### 36.3.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

APG IV Alismatales comprise 14 families and about 4500 species: Araceae, Tofieldiaceae, Alismataceae, Butomaceae, Hydrocharitaceae, Scheuchzeriaceae, Aponogetonaceae, Juncaginaceae, Maundiaceae, Zosteraceae, Potamogetonaceae, Posidoniaceae, Ruppiaceae and Cymodoceaceae. Possible synapomorphies are small scales or glandular hairs inside the leaf sheath at the nodes (intravaginal squamules), extrorse anthers, large cotyledons and a green embryo. Araceae are sister to all the other families; the remaining families, apart from Tofieldiaceae (*Tofieldia* and relatives, lily-like and growing in wet places), form the "aquatic clade", with air spaces in the stem, seeds without **endosperm** (the endosperm is **helobial endosperm** early in development and is absorbed by maturity), and all are **hydrophytes** or wetland plants. The aquatic clade splits into two branches:

- The petaloid branch (Alismataceae, Butomaceae, Hydrocharitaceae): the perianth is differentiated into sepals and petals, there are more than 6 stamens or more than 3 carpels (secondarily increased), and the ovules are scattered over the inner carpel wall (laminar placentation).
- The tepaloid branch (Potamogetonaceae, Zosteraceae, Cymodoceaceae and others): the perianth is inconspicuous or absent, the pollen is often inaperturate with a reduced exine, and many are water-pollinated, including all seagrasses.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Alismatidae contained Alismatales (Alismataceae, Butomaceae and others), Hydrocharitales, Najadales (Potamogetonaceae, Zosteraceae, Najadaceae and others) and Triuridales; Araceae and Lemnaceae formed the order Arales, placed in the subclass Arecidae, close to Arecaceae. APG IV moves Araceae (including Lemnaceae) into Alismatales, merges Najadaceae into Hydrocharitaceae and the yellow-flowered Limnocharitaceae into Alismataceae, and moves Triuridaceae into Pandanales (§36.5). The floral diversity of Alismatales is extremely high: within one small lineage we find everything from the complete trimerous flower to "flowers" with only a single stamen or a single carpel, and some authors consider certain of these "flowers" to be highly reduced inflorescences.

### 36.3.2. Araceae

Diagnostic characters of Araceae (the arum family):

- Perennial herbs with rhizomes or tubers, but also climbing vines (*Monstera*, pothos), epiphytes and floating plants; laticifers or mucilage canals are often present, the parenchyma contains bundles of calcium oxalate raphides that irritate the mouth and throat when the plant is eaten raw, and many species are poisonous.
- Leaves basal or cauline, often long-petiolate, with a sheath at the petiole base; blade shape varies, entire, divided or compound, often with reticulate venation (rare in monocots).
- Flowers small, bisexual or unisexual, sessile, crowded on a fleshy axis to form a spadix; the inflorescence is subtended by a large bract, the spathe ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.7.2)), which is often colored, with a lower tubular part that encloses the inflorescence and an upper expanded blade. Such an inflorescence is called a spathe-and-spadix inflorescence.
- In monoecious species with unisexual flowers the female flowers occupy the lower part of the inflorescence and the male flowers the upper part, with sterile neuter flowers often between them; the tip of the axis is often extended into a flowerless appendage that emits odor, and many species produce heat while flowering.
- Bisexual flowers have 4–6 tepals, unisexual flowers usually no perianth; stamens 1–6 (–12), free or fused; carpels 2–3 (rarely up to 15), syncarpous, ovary superior, 1 to many locules, ovules 1 to many.
- Fruit a berry, crowded on the axis; a utricle in duckweeds.

Floral formula: bisexual flower $\ast P_{4\text{–}6} A_{4\text{–}6} \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}3)}$; unisexual flowers ♂ $P_0 A_{1\text{–}6}$; ♀ $P_0 \underline{G}_{(1\text{–}3)}$ (Chinese textbooks write $\ast P_{0,\,4\text{–}6} A_{6\text{–}\infty} \underline{G}_{(2,\,3)}$).

Araceae have about 115 genera and 3500–3700 species, mainly tropical, richest in the American and Asian tropics; China has about 30 genera and 190 species, mostly in the south. Molecular phylogenetics divides the family into 8 subfamilies (Cusimano et al. 2011). The largest, Aroideae, has unisexual, monoecious flowers and includes most of the familiar genera such as taro, *Arisaema*, *Pinellia*, *Amorphophallus* and calla lily; the climbers *Monstera* and *Epipremnum* belong to Monsteroideae; *Anthurium* (flamingo flower) belongs to Pothoideae; the duckweeds form Lemnoideae. Classification and representative plants:

1. *Pinellia*: perennial herbs with tubers; bulbils often on the petiole base or leaf blade base; the tip of the spathe is closed, with a transverse constriction between tube and blade; the female zone is adnate to the spathe, and the spadix ends in a slender appendage. Crow-dipper (*P. ternata*) has small, globose tubers; seedling leaves are simple and ovate-cordate, while leaves of plants 2–3 years old are trifoliolate; the spathe is green, often purplish above. It is distributed throughout China and is common in fields. The tuber is poisonous and, after processing, is used in Chinese medicine to dry dampness, resolve phlegm and relieve vomiting. Its Chinese name refers to the fact that it can be dug up at midsummer.
2. *Arisaema*: tuberous; 1–2 leaves, 3-lobed or pedately or radiately divided to the base; the tube of the spathe is rolled into a cylinder and the blade is arched or hooded, often long-acuminate; about 180 species, about 80 in China, mainly in the southwest. *A. heterophyllum* has pedately divided leaves with 13–19 leaflets and a tail-like appendage; *A. erubescens* has radiately divided leaves with 5–20 leaflets and a club-shaped appendage. The tubers of both are used as the Chinese drug "tiannanxing" and are poisonous. *Arisaema* has a special sexual system: in the same individual, male flowers are produced in years when the tuber is small and nutrition is poor, and female flowers once the tuber has grown larger (sex change), so that smaller individuals are male and larger ones female.
3. *Amorphophallus*: with a large, flattened-globose tuber; a single leaf per year, with a stout, mottled petiole and a blade that is first divided into three and then pinnately divided, like an umbrella; the inflorescence appears before the leaf, the spathe is large and the appendage thick, and at flowering it gives off a smell of rotting meat that attracts carrion-feeding beetles and flies. Konjac (*A. konjac*; often given as *A. rivieri* in Chinese textbooks) is native to southern and southwestern China, and its tuber, rich in glucomannan, is processed into konjac tofu and konjac flour. The titan arum (*A. titanum*), native to Sumatra, has an inflorescence up to 3 m tall, the largest unbranched inflorescence; at flowering the appendage is about 10 °C warmer than the surroundings, and the heat helps volatilize the odor.
4. *Colocasia*: taro (*C. esculenta*) has peltate leaves with the base 2-lobed; the tuber (corm) is rich in starch and is eaten as a staple and vegetable, and the young petioles are also edible; native to tropical Asia and cultivated since ancient times, it rarely flowers in temperate regions. In *Alocasia*, giant taro (*A. odora*) is a large herb common in southern China, poisonous throughout.
5. Ornamentals: calla lily (*Zantedeschia aethiopica*), native to South Africa, has a white, funnel-shaped spathe and a yellow spadix. Swiss cheese plant (*Monstera deliciosa*), native to Central America, is a climbing vine with long aerial roots; adult leaves have many holes and deep pinnate lobes, and the ripe berries are edible; Chinese textbooks sometimes treat it and *Epipremnum pinnatum* as one species, but they belong to two genera. Pothos (*E. aureum*), flamingo flower (*Anthurium andraeanum*, with a red, waxy spathe), peace lily (*Spathiphyllum*), Chinese evergreen (*Aglaonema modestum*), *Philodendron* and dumb cane (*Dieffenbachia*) are all common indoor foliage plants.
6. Water lettuce (*Pistia stratiotes*): a floating herb with leaves in a rosette and stolons; it spreads rapidly by vegetative propagation, probably originated in South America, is now widespread in the tropics, and has become a serious invasive aquatic weed in southern China. Molecular evidence shows that it belongs to Aroideae and adapted to a floating life independently of the duckweeds.
7. Duckweeds (formerly Lemnaceae): tiny floating or submerged herbs whose body is reduced to a thallus (no differentiation of stem and leaf), with or without roots; new thalli are produced vegetatively in pouches at the base of the thallus, flowering is occasional, and the flowers are minute, borne in small pouches at the margin or on the surface of the thallus. Greater duckweed (*Spirodela polyrhiza*) has a thallus purple on the underside and several roots; common duckweed (*Lemna minor*) has a single root per thallus; spotless watermeal (*Wolffia globosa*) has no root, and its ovoid thallus is less than 1 mm long, making it the smallest known angiosperm, its flower having a single stamen and a single pistil. Duckweeds can double their numbers in a day or two, the fastest growth among angiosperms, and are used as fodder, as material for water purification and as a model system in biology.

The inflorescences of Araceae often pollinate by "trapping". In the European lords-and-ladies (*Arum maculatum*), on the evening of the first day of flowering the appendage produces heat and a dung-like smell that attracts small flies such as moth flies; insects that slide into the tube of the spathe are blocked by downward-pointing hairs and trapped in the female zone, where the female flowers are receptive first and pollen carried by the insects fertilizes them; on the second day the male flowers shed pollen onto the insects, the hairs then wither, and the insects crawl out with pollen and fly to another, newly opened inflorescence. This **dichogamy** (protogyny) combined with temporary imprisonment ensures **cross-pollination** ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3)). Heat production in Araceae, as in water lilies and custard apples, depends on the alternative oxidase of mitochondria, and the inflorescence of skunk cabbage (*Symplocarpus*) can even melt the surrounding snow in early spring.

Key characters of the family: herbs, often with raphides and latex; leaves often long-petiolate with reticulate venation; flowers small, crowded in a spadix subtended by one spathe; in monoecious species female flowers below and male flowers above; fruit a berry.

Changes in circumscription: *Acorus* is excluded (Acoraceae, §36.2) and Lemnaceae are merged.

### 36.3.3. Alismataceae

Diagnostic characters of Alismataceae (the water-plantain family):

- Aquatic or marsh annual or perennial herbs with rhizomes, stolons or corms; laticifers present, and a broken plant exudes latex.
- Leaves often basal, long-petiolate, with an open sheath at the base; emergent blades elliptic, cordate or sagittate, with arcuate main veins and cross veins between them; submerged leaves often ribbon-like.
- Flowers bisexual or unisexual, radially symmetric; the flowers are borne in whorls on a scape, forming whorled racemes or panicles.
- Perianth in 2 whorls: the outer 3 green, sepal-like and persistent; the inner 3 white or pale purple, petal-like and caducous.
- Stamens 6 to many (rarely 3); pollen often with several scattered pores and a spiny exine.
- Carpels 6 to many, free, arranged spirally on a raised receptacle (*Sagittaria*) or in a whorl on a flat receptacle (*Alisma*); ovary superior, 1-locular, ovules 1 or several.
- Fruit an aggregate of **achenes**, rarely **follicles**; seeds without endosperm, the embryo horseshoe-shaped.

Floral formula: $\ast K_3 C_3 A_{6\text{–}\infty} \underline{G}_{6\text{–}\infty}$.

The family (including Limnocharitaceae) has about 17 genera and over 100 species, widespread in freshwater wetlands around the world; China has about 6 genera and 18 species. Representative plants:

- *Sagittaria* (arrowhead): leaves mostly sagittate; flowers unisexual, female in the lower part and male in the upper part of the raceme; stamens and carpels both numerous, carpels spiral. Chinese arrowhead (*S. trifolia* subsp. *leucopetala*, often given as *S. sagittifolia* in Chinese textbooks) is a cultivated vegetable whose stolon tips swell into corms (the edible "cigu") that can also be made into starch; wapato (*S. trifolia*) is a weed of rice fields.
- *Alisma* (water plantain): leaves ovate or elliptic with a cuneate or cordate base; panicle, flowers bisexual, stamens 6, carpels many, in a whorl. *A. orientale* and *A. plantago-aquatica* are distributed throughout China, and the tuberous rhizome (corm) is used in Chinese medicine to promote diuresis and drain dampness.
- Yellow velvetleaf (*Limnocharis flava*) and water poppy (*Hydrocleys nymphoides*): formerly Limnocharitaceae (Butomaceae in the broad sense), with follicles and yellow petals, often grown in ponds; *Limnocharis flava* has escaped from cultivation in southern China.

The flowers of Alismataceae, with free carpels, numerous stamens and a perianth differentiated into sepals and petals, resemble those of Ranunculaceae and Magnoliaceae, and this was once the main basis for the views that "monocots originated from the ranunculoids" and that "Alismatales are primitive". We now know that the numerous stamens and carpels in the flowers of Alismataceae arose secondarily from smaller numbers: in development they appear in pairs or bundles and arise centrifugally, unlike the centripetal development in Ranunculaceae.

Key characters of the family: aquatic or marsh herbs with latex; flowers in whorls on a scape; outer perianth sepal-like, inner petal-like; carpels numerous and free; aggregate of achenes.

### 36.3.4. Butomaceae

Butomaceae contain only the flowering rush (*Butomus umbellatus*), distributed in temperate Eurasia and occurring in northern China. It is a perennial aquatic herb with basal, distichous, triangular-linear leaves; the umbel is borne at the top of the scape; flowers bisexual, tepals 6, pink, stamens 9, carpels 6, free, ovules numerous and scattered over the inner carpel wall; fruit a follicle. Floral formula $\ast P_{3+3} A_{9} \underline{G}_{6}$. It is the sister group of Alismataceae; Cronquist's Butomaceae also included the Limnocharitaceae, which are now merged into Alismataceae.

### 36.3.5. Hydrocharitaceae

Diagnostic characters of Hydrocharitaceae (the frog's-bit family):

- Floating or submerged herbs of fresh or sea water.
- Leaves basal in a rosette, or on the stem distichous, alternate, opposite or whorled; blades linear, ribbon-like or round and petiolate.
- Flowers solitary or in inflorescences, enclosed in 2 fused or opposite bracts (a spathe-like involucre); flowers often unisexual, rarely bisexual, radially symmetric.
- Perianth in 1–2 whorls of 3, the outer sepal-like and the inner petal-like; stamens 3 to many (rarely 2).
- Ovary inferior, carpels 3–6 (rarely 2–20) fused, 1-locular, with parietal (or laminar) placentation and numerous ovules; fruit fleshy, berry-like, opening irregularly.

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast K_3 C_3 A_{3\text{–}\infty}$; ♀ $\ast K_3 C_3 \overline{G}_{(3\text{–}6:1:\infty)}$.

The family (including the former Najadaceae) has about 16 genera and over 130 species, widespread from the tropics to the temperate zone; China has about 9 genera and over 20 species. Representative plants:

- Hydrilla (*Hydrilla verticillata*): a submerged herb with elongated, branched stems; leaves 4–8 in whorls, linear, with a minutely serrate margin; dioecious, the male flowers detach on maturity and open at the water surface. The leaf is only two cell layers thick, and it is a classic experimental material for observing **cytoplasmic streaming** and chloroplast movement. Hydrilla is listed as one of the most serious invasive aquatic weeds in North America.
- *Vallisneria* (tape grass): submerged, stemless herbs with long ribbon-like leaves; dioecious, the male flowers minute, numerous in one spathe and detaching on maturity to float to the surface; the female flowers are solitary at the tips of extremely long scapes and float at the surface, where pollination takes place; after fertilization the scape coils into a spiral and pulls the fruit back to the bottom ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.3)). Tape grass (*V. natans*, often given as *V. spiralis* in Chinese textbooks) is a common submerged plant of ponds and lakes throughout China, is fodder for fish and is also used in water remediation.
- Frogbit (*Hydrocharis dubia*): a floating herb with stolons and round or reniform leaves with a spongy aerenchyma in the middle of the underside that keeps the blade afloat; dioecious, male flowers with 6–9 stamens and staminodes.
- *Ottelia acuminata*: endemic to southwestern China, submerged, with white flowers floating at the surface; it grows only in clear water and is an indicator of water quality, and in Yunnan the scapes are eaten as a vegetable; *O. alismoides* is widespread in rice fields and ponds.
- *Elodea* (waterweed): native to the Americas; Canadian waterweed (*E. canadensis*) spread rapidly after its introduction to Europe in the 19th century, mostly as female plants reproducing only vegetatively; it is often used to observe cells and cytoplasmic streaming.
- *Najas* (water nymph): submerged, with unisexual and extremely simplified flowers, the male flower with a single stamen and the female with a single carpel, pollinated under water; formerly a separate family, Najadaceae.
- Marine genera: *Halophila*, *Enhalus* and *Thalassia* grow in shallow seas along tropical coasts and are constituents of seagrass beds.

Key characters of the family: floating or submerged plants; flowers often unisexual, borne within spathe-like bracts; ovary inferior, carpels fused, parietal placentation.

### 36.3.6. Potamogetonaceae and Seagrasses

Potamogetonaceae (the pondweed family) are submerged or floating herbs of fresh water with rhizomes; leaves alternate, submerged leaves linear or ribbon-like, floating leaves broad and petiolate, the leaf base with a stipule-like sheath; spikes held above the water; flowers bisexual, tetramerous, the connective of each stamen extended outward into a tepal-like appendage (often called the "perianth"), carpels 4, free; fruit a small drupelet. Wind- or surface-pollinated. Floral formula $\ast P_4 A_4 \underline{G}_4$ ($P$ is the appendage of the connective). About 4 genera and over 100 species, mainly *Potamogeton*. *P. distinctus* is a common floating-leaved weed of rice fields; curly pondweed (*P. crispus*) has a wavy leaf margin, grows in winter and spring, and often multiplies massively in eutrophic lakes. In Cronquist's system Potamogetonaceae, Najadaceae and others formed the order Najadales; in APG IV they all belong to the tepaloid branch of Alismatales.

**Definition 36.3.1 (Seagrasses)** The angiosperms that can complete their entire life cycle in sea water, including flowering, pollination and fruiting under water, are collectively called **seagrasses**.

All seagrasses belong to Alismatales, with about 60–70 species in 4 families: Zosteraceae, Cymodoceaceae, Posidoniaceae and three marine genera of Hydrocharitaceae; molecular phylogenetics shows that the marine habit originated at least three times independently within Alismatales (Les et al. 1997). All are clonal plants with rhizomes, with ribbon-like leaves and extremely simplified flowers, and most are pollinated under water: the pollen is often filiform, without an exine, with a density close to that of sea water, drifts with the current and becomes entangled on the elongated stigmas ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.3.3)).

- Zosteraceae: eelgrass (*Zostera marina*) occurs in the subtidal zone of temperate coasts of the Northern Hemisphere, with ribbon-like leaves up to 1 m long and a flattened inflorescence enclosed in a leaf-sheath-like spathe, male and female flowers alternating along the axis; the pollen is filiform. It is widely distributed along the Yellow Sea and Bohai Sea coasts of China, where the traditional "eelgrass houses" of the northern coast use its dried leaves as roofing.
- Posidoniaceae: in *Posidonia*, a single clone of *P. australis* in Shark Bay, Western Australia, extends about 180 km (Edgeloe et al. 2022), the largest known plant individual by area.

Seagrass beds are among the most productive ecosystems of shallow seas: they provide food and habitat for fish, dugongs and sea turtles, stabilize sediments, and bury organic carbon in the sediment for long periods ("blue carbon").

## 36.4. Petrosaviales and Dioscoreales

Petrosaviales comprise only Petrosaviaceae, with 2 genera and 4 species; molecular analyses show that they are the sister group of the large clade formed by Dioscoreales, Pandanales, Liliales, Asparagales and the commelinids, and they are therefore placed in a separate order. *Petrosavia* is a chlorophyll-free **mycoheterotroph**, and *P. sakuraii* occurs in southern China; the Japanese *Japonolirion osense* is green.

### 36.4.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

APG IV Dioscoreales comprise Dioscoreaceae, Burmanniaceae and Nartheciaceae. Characters shared by Dioscoreaceae and Burmanniaceae are an inferior ovary, often winged, stamens often bent inward, and simultaneous microsporogenesis (uncommon in monocots). Burmanniaceae are mostly slender mycoheterotrophic herbs with reduced or small leaves and blue or white flowers, the ovary inferior and 3-winged; *Burmannia* and *Thismia* occur in the forest understory of southern China. Nartheciaceae are wetland herbs, and *Aletris* is common in China.

Changes in circumscription: Dahlgren's (1985) broadly defined Dioscoreales included almost all the reticulate-veined "lilioid" monocots (Dioscoreaceae, Smilacaceae, Stemonaceae, Trilliaceae and others); molecular evidence shows that they belong to three orders. Cronquist and the Chinese textbooks place Dioscoreaceae in Liliales.

### 36.4.2. Dioscoreaceae

Diagnostic characters of Dioscoreaceae (the yam family):

- Perennial twining herbaceous vines, rarely erect herbs; tubers or rhizomes underground; stems twining to the left or the right, the direction being fixed within a species and a useful diagnostic character; some species have bulbils (aerial tubers) in the leaf axils.
- Leaves simple and alternate, often opposite above the middle, or palmately compound; petioles long, blades often cordate, with 3–9 basal arcuate main veins and a fine reticulum between them.
- Flowers small, unisexual, dioecious (rarely monoecious), radially symmetric, in spikes, racemes or panicles.
- Tepals 6 in 2 whorls, fused at the base; male flowers with 6 stamens, or 3 fertile and 3 reduced; female flowers often with 3–6 staminodes.
- Ovary inferior, 3 carpels fused, 3-locular, 2 ovules per locule, 3 styles; fruit a 3-winged **capsule**, rarely a berry or samara; seeds often winged.

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{(3+3)} A_{3+3}$; ♀ $\ast P_{(3+3)} \overline{G}_{(3:3:2)}$.

The family (including Taccaceae and Trichopodaceae) has about 4 genera and 650 species, widespread in the tropics and subtropics, mainly *Dioscorea* (about 630 species); China has about 50 species of *Dioscorea* as well as *Tacca* and others. Representative plants:

- Chinese yam (*Dioscorea polystachya*, often given as *D. opposita* in Chinese textbooks): the tuber is long-cylindrical and there are bulbils in the leaf axils; cultivated throughout China, and the material from the Jiaozuo region of Henan is known as "Huai shan yao"; the tuber is eaten as a vegetable and is also used in medicine as a nourishing tonic. Water yam (*D. alata*) has a 4-winged stem and tubers of varied shape and is an important food crop in the tropics. West Africa is the world's main yam-producing region, and yams are one of the staples there.
- Air potato (*D. bulbifera*): bulbils spherical in the leaf axils, tuber solitary; the tuber and bulbils are poisonous and used in medicine.
- *D. cirrhosa*: the tuber is rich in tannins and, when cut, exudes a reddish-brown juice; in Guangdong it is used together with river mud to dye silk (the fabrics "Xiangyunsha" and "Heijiaochou") and also to dye fishing nets.
- *D. nipponica* and *D. zingiberensis*: the rhizomes contain steroidal saponins whose aglycone diosgenin is an important raw material for the synthesis of steroid hormones (cortisone, sex hormones, oral contraceptives). In the 1940s R. Marker obtained progesterone from the diosgenin of wild Mexican yams by a few steps of chemical degradation, which made large-scale production of steroid hormones possible.
- Elephant's foot (*D. elephantipes*): native to South Africa, the tuber is exposed above ground and its surface cork layer cracks into a tortoise-shell pattern; it is cultivated by succulent enthusiasts.
- *Tacca* (bat flower): erect herbs whose umbel is subtended by several large involucral bracts and bears many pendulous thread-like bracteoles ("tiger whiskers"), the flowers purplish black; *T. chantrieri* occurs in southern and southwestern China and is often cultivated as an ornamental. It was formerly a separate family, Taccaceae, which APG merges into Dioscoreaceae.

Key characters of the family: twining herbs with tubers or rhizomes; leaves with basal arcuate main veins and a fine reticulum; flowers unisexual, trimerous, ovary inferior; capsule 3-winged, seeds winged.

## 36.5. Pandanales

In molecular analyses Pandanales are the sister group of Dioscoreales and comprise 5 families: Pandanaceae, Cyclanthaceae, Stemonaceae, Triuridaceae and Velloziaceae. This combination has no obvious morphological shared character; all have a very small embryo and a nucellar cap at the apex of the nucellus. Pandanaceae and Cyclanthaceae form a clade sharing incomplete flowers, indehiscent fruits with fused carpels and compound vascular bundles in the stem.

- Pandanaceae (screw pines): trees, shrubs or climbing vines, often with stout prop roots on the stem; leaves spirally arranged in 3 ranks at the branch tips (hence "screw pine"), linear, with spines on the margin and the abaxial midrib; dioecious, flowers without perianth, crowded in spadix-like inflorescences subtended by spathe-like bracts; the female flower has numerous carpels aggregated into a large multiple fruit. About 800 species, mainly *Pandanus* and *Freycinetia*, in the Old World tropics to the Pacific islands. Screw pine (*Pandanus tectorius*) grows on the coasts of southern China and Taiwan, the ripe fruit is orange-red and shaped like a pineapple; the leaves of pandan (*P. amaryllifolius*) are fragrant (they contain 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline, the same aroma compound as in fragrant rice) and are used in Southeast Asia as a flavoring and green colorant for pastries.
- Cyclanthaceae (Panama hat plant family): tropical America; leaves fan-shaped and 2-lobed, inflorescence a spadix. The straw hats woven from fibers of the young leaves of *Carludovica palmata* are called "Panama hats" although they are in fact produced mainly in Ecuador.
- Stemonaceae: perennial herbs or subshrubs, often climbing, with clustered, fusiform tuberous roots; leaves with basal arcuate main veins and dense cross veins; flowers bisexual, dimerous: 4 tepals, 4 stamens with the connective often extended into an appendage; ovary superior or half-inferior, 1-locular; capsule 2-valved. About 4 genera and over 30 species, from East Asia to Australia; *Croomia* occurs in southeastern China, Japan and southeastern North America, another example of an East Asian–North American disjunction. The tuberous roots of *Stemona sessilifolia*, *S. japonica* and *S. tuberosa* are the Chinese drug "baibu", containing many **alkaloids** such as stemonine, which relieve cough by moistening the lung, and are also used as insecticide. Cronquist placed Stemonaceae in Liliales near Dioscoreaceae, and molecular evidence moves them to Pandanales. Floral formula $\ast P_{2+2} A_{2+2} \underline{G}_{(2:1:\infty)}$.
- Triuridaceae: small, chlorophyll-free mycoheterotrophic herbs with unisexual or bisexual flowers. In the Mexican *Lacandonia schismatica* the stamens are central and the carpels surround them, the reverse of the arrangement in almost all angiosperms. The position of Triuridaceae rests mainly on a few data such as 18S rDNA, and Cronquist placed them in Alismatidae.
- Velloziaceae: mainly in rocky mountains of South America and Africa; woody stems wrapped in persistent leaf sheaths and adventitious roots, drought- and fire-tolerant. *Acanthochlamys bracteata* of western Sichuan and southeastern Tibet is the only species of the family in East Asia and is sometimes treated as a separate family, Acanthochlamydaceae.

## 36.6. Liliales

### 36.6.1. Characters of the Order and the Splitting of the "Broadly Defined Liliaceae"

APG IV Liliales comprise 10 families and about 1500 species: Alstroemeriaceae, Campynemataceae, Colchicaceae, Corsiaceae, Liliaceae, Melanthiaceae, Petermanniaceae, Philesiaceae, Ripogonaceae and Smilacaceae. Liliales are mostly geophytes with rhizomes, bulbs or corms; leaves often elliptic with arcuate main veins and a fine reticulum; flowers often large and showy, tepals 6, petaloid, often spotted or striped; nectaries at the base of the tepals (tepal nectaries), not in the septa of the ovary (in Asparagales mostly septal nectaries); anthers mostly extrorse; no phytomelan in the seed coat (the seeds of many Asparagales groups have a black, crust-like phytomelan layer), and seeds often flat and pale.

**Definition 36.6.1 (Broadly and narrowly defined Liliaceae)** The Liliaceae of Cronquist's system and of the Chinese textbooks, which include almost all monocot herbs with a petaloid perianth, 6 stamens and a superior or inferior ovary, are called **Liliaceae s.l.** (*sensu lato*, broadly defined Liliaceae); the Liliaceae of APG IV include only about 15 genera most closely related to *Lilium* and are called **Liliaceae s.s.** (*sensu stricto*, narrowly defined Liliaceae).

Cronquist's broadly defined Liliaceae had about 280 genera and 4000 species, which research following Dahlgren (1985) and APG has distributed among a dozen or more families in two orders (Table 36.6.1, Fig. 36.1.2). The differences among these groups are subtle in morphology, which is why Cronquist preferred to combine them in one large family; but molecular evidence shows that their shared characters (6 tepals, 6 stamens, 3 fused carpels) are **plesiomorphies** of the monocots, so that Liliaceae s.l. is a **paraphyletic group** in which morphologically specialized families such as Orchidaceae and Iridaceae are nested. The result of the split is that the groups with spotted tepals, pale seeds and nectaries at the base of the tepals remain in Liliales, while the groups with often black seeds and mostly septal nectaries move to Asparagales.

### 36.6.2. Liliaceae

Diagnostic characters of Liliaceae s.s. (the lily family):

- Perennial herbs, most with **bulbs** (scales fleshy; the bulb of *Lilium* has no membranous tunic), a few with rhizomes (*Tricyrtis*, *Clintonia*).
- Leaves basal or cauline, alternate, rarely whorled, linear to ovate, parallel- or arcuate-veined.
- Flowers solitary or in racemes, often large and beautiful, bisexual, radially or slightly bilaterally symmetric.
- Tepals 6 in 2 whorls, free, petaloid, often spotted or striped, usually with a nectary at the base (nectar furrow or pit).
- Stamens 6 in 2 whorls; anthers 2-locular, dehiscing longitudinally, extrorse or latrorse, often versatile; pollen monosulcate, with a reticulate pattern.
- Ovary superior, 3 carpels fused, 3-locular with axile placentation and numerous ovules per locule; style 1, stigma 3-lobed or capitate.
- Fruit a loculicidal capsule, rarely a berry; seeds numerous, flat, often winged, the seed coat pale.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$.

The family has about 15 genera and over 700 species, mainly in the north temperate zone; China has about 13 genera and over 150 species, most in the mountains of the southwest. Molecular phylogenetics divides it into 3 subfamilies: Calochortoideae (*Calochortus* of North America and *Tricyrtis* of East Asia), Streptopoideae (*Streptopus* and relatives) and Lilioideae (the tribe Lilieae with *Lilium*, *Cardiocrinum*, *Nomocharis*, *Fritillaria*, *Tulipa*, *Amana*, *Erythronium* and *Gagea*, as well as the tribe Medeoleae with *Clintonia*). Classification and representative plants:

1. *Lilium*: the bulb consists of many fleshy scales without a membranous tunic; stem erect and leafy; flowers solitary or in racemes, large and beautiful, funnel-shaped, trumpet-shaped or with recurved tepals; anthers versatile; stigma capitate. About 110 species, mainly in the north temperate zone, about 55 in China, where the mountains of the southwest are one center of distribution. *L. brownii* var. *viridulum* has creamy white, trumpet-shaped flowers tinged purplish outside, and the bulb is eaten and used in medicine (to moisten the lung and relieve cough, to clear the heart and calm the mind). Tiger lily (*L. lancifolium*) often bears bulbils in the leaf axils and has orange-red flowers with purplish black spots and recurved tepals; cultivated tiger lilies are mostly triploid and do not set seed normally, propagating by bulbils and bulbs, and the bulbs are eaten (Lanzhou lily is a cultivar of *L. davidii*). *L. pumilum* has linear leaves and bright red flowers and occurs in the northeast, north and northwest; *L. concolor* has erect, star-shaped flowers. Regal lily (*L. regale*) occurs in the Min River valley of Sichuan and was introduced to Europe and America by E. H. Wilson in the early 20th century, becoming a parent of many garden lilies; Easter lily (*L. longiflorum*), native to the Ryukyu Islands and Taiwan, has white, trumpet-shaped flowers and is an important cut flower.
2. *Cardiocrinum*: leaves basal and cauline, long-petiolate, the blade cordate with reticulate veins; the bulb dies after flowering and fruiting (monocarpic). Giant lily (*C. giganteum*) has a stem up to 3 m tall and occurs from southwestern China to the Himalayas.
3. *Fritillaria*: the bulb consists of few (2–4) thick scales; leaves opposite, whorled or scattered, the tip often curled into a tendril; flowers bell-shaped and nodding, with a nectar pit at the base of the tepals and often a checkered pattern; capsule ribbed or winged. About 130 species, about 25 in China, most of which are used in medicine. *F. cirrhosa* occurs in the high mountains of Sichuan, Yunnan and Tibet, with yellow-green to yellow flowers marked with purple checks; *F. thunbergii* occurs in Zhejiang and Jiangsu and has larger bulbs; *F. ussuriensis* occurs in the northeast. *Fritillaria* has some of the largest genomes among angiosperms, and in many species the 1C value exceeds 50 pg (about 3.3 pg in humans).
4. *Tulipa*: the bulb has a membranous or leathery tunic; leaves basal or on the lower part of the stem; flowers solitary at the top of the stem, erect, cup-shaped, tepals 6, nectaries absent. The garden tulip (*T. gesneriana*) is a cultivated group selected over a long time from species native to Central Asia and Turkey; in the 1630s the Netherlands experienced the famous "tulip mania", in which the most expensive "broken" cultivars (with flame-like stripes on the petals) were in fact the result of infection with tulip breaking virus (a potyvirus). *T. iliensis* of Xinjiang and others are wild species. *Amana edulis* (formerly *Tulipa edulis*) occurs in eastern China, and its bulb is used in medicine ("guangcigu").
5. *Erythronium* (trout lily): the bulb is slender and shaped like a pig's tooth; leaves 2, often mottled; flowers solitary and nodding, the tepals strongly recurved. *E. japonicum* occurs in the northeast and flowers in early spring as a spring ephemeral.
6. *Gagea* (including the former *Lloydia*): small bulbous herbs with yellow or white flowers that open in early spring, in the north and in high mountains; *Clintonia* has rhizomes, basal leaves and blue-black berries, with an East Asian–North American disjunct distribution; *Tricyrtis* (toad lily) has clasping leaves, tepals with purple spots and a deeply 3-lobed stigma, and occurs south of the Yangtze in China.

Key characters of the family: herbs, mostly with bulbs; flowers solitary or racemose, large and beautiful, tepals 6, petaloid, often spotted, with nectaries at the base; stamens 6, anthers extrorse; ovary superior, 3-locular, axile placentation; capsule; seeds flat and pale.

Changes in circumscription: only the calochortoids, tricyrtoids, streptopoids and the Lilieae and related groups are retained, and all other groups are excluded (Table 36.6.1).

### 36.6.3. Melanthiaceae

Melanthiaceae (the bunchflower family) are perennial herbs with rhizomes and an inconspicuous or absent bulb; the tepals are often persistent, anthers extrorse, the carpels often free in the upper part, and the 3 styles free. The family (including Trilliaceae) has about 16 genera and 170 species in the north temperate zone; China has *Veratrum*, *Paris*, *Trillium*, *Ypsilandra*, *Heloniopsis*, *Chionographis* and others. It is divided into 5 tribes. Representative plants:

- *Veratrum* (false hellebore): the persistent leaf sheaths at the base tear into fibers; leaves broad with conspicuous longitudinal pleats; a large panicle with numerous flowers, tepals persistent, anthers reniform; fruit a capsule. *V. nigrum* occurs in the northeast, north and northwest, and the root is poisonous and was formerly used as an emetic and insecticide. *Veratrum* contains many steroidal **alkaloids**: cyclopamine from the California false hellebore (*V. californicum*) of North America caused ewes that ate it to bear one-eyed lambs, and cyclopamine was later found to inhibit the Hedgehog signaling pathway and became a tool for studying this pathway and the related tumors.
- *Paris*: rhizomes stout; a whorl of 4 to many leaves at the top of the stem, with reticulate veins; a single flower above the whorl of leaves, the outer tepals (sepals) green and leaf-like, the inner tepals (petals) linear; stamens twice the number of tepals; ovary 4–10-locular. The rhizomes of *P. polyphylla* var. *chinensis* and *P. polyphylla* are the Chinese drug "chonglou" and a raw material of many patent medicines, and wild resources have become scarce through over-collection. The Japanese *P. japonica* has a 1C value of about 152 pg (about 149 billion base pairs) and was long the largest known plant genome (Pellicer et al. 2010).
- *Trillium*: close to *Paris*, but with 3 leaves, 3 sepals and 3 petals and a 3-locular ovary; disjunct between East Asia and North America. *T. tschonoskii* occurs in central and southwestern China.
- Others: *Ypsilandra* and *Heloniopsis* are small herbs of the forest understory in southern China.

Floral formula: *Veratrum* $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$ (carpels free in the upper part); *Paris* $\ast K_n C_n A_{n+n} \underline{G}_{(n:n:\infty)}$, $n = 4\text{–}10$.

### 36.6.4. Smilacaceae

Diagnostic characters of Smilacaceae (the greenbrier family):

- Climbing shrubs, rarely herbs or erect shrubs; rhizomes thick, tuberous or creeping and bamboo-whip-like; stems often prickly.
- Leaves alternate, with 3–7 basal arcuate main veins and a fine reticulum between them; the petiole often has wing-like sheaths on both sides, usually with a pair of tendrils at the tip of the sheath (of debated origin, interpreted either as stipules or as extensions of the leaf sheath), and often a joint on the upper part of the petiole.
- Flowers small, unisexual, dioecious, in axillary umbels.
- Tepals 6, free or fused; male flowers with 6 stamens; female flowers often with staminodes, ovary superior, 3-locular, 1–2 ovules per locule.
- Fruit a berry, often red or blue-black.

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3}$; ♀ $\ast P_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:1\text{–}2)}$.

Smilacaceae in APG IV contain only *Smilax* (including the former *Heterosmilax*), about 250–300 species, widespread from the tropics to the temperate zone; China has about 80 species. *S. china* has a sparsely prickly stem, nearly round leaves and berries red when ripe, and the rhizome is rich in starch and tannins and can be used for brewing and extracting tannin and in medicine. *S. glabra* has an unarmed stem, and its rhizome is the Chinese drug "tufuling". *S. riparia* is a herbaceous vine whose young shoots are eaten as a wild vegetable. The roots of several Central American *Smilax* species are the source of sarsaparilla, formerly used to flavor beverages.

In Cronquist's system Smilacaceae were a separate family in Liliales; the Chinese textbooks mostly place *Smilax* in Liliaceae. Its reticulate veins, tendrils and climbing habit resemble those of Dioscoreaceae, and the two were once thought to be related; molecular evidence shows that it is closer to Liliaceae.

### 36.6.5. Colchicaceae

Colchicaceae (the autumn crocus family) are perennial herbs with corms or rhizomes; leaves alternate, often clasping; tepals 6, free or fused at the base into a tube; stamens 6; ovary superior, 3-locular, 3 styles; fruit a capsule, rarely a berry. All species contain colchicine and its analogues. About 15 genera and 250 species occur in the Old World, and *Uvularia* in North America.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$.

- *Colchicum*: autumn crocus (*C. autumnale*), native to Europe, has an underground corm; it flowers in autumn without leaves, the lower part of the tepals fused into a long tube, the ovary remaining underground, and leaves and fruit appear only the next spring. Colchicine binds to tubulin and prevents microtubule assembly, arresting mitosis at metaphase, and is therefore used to induce **polyploidy** ([7.4.16](/7.4.16)) and to prepare chromosome spreads, and in medicine to treat gout; it is highly toxic, and ingestion can be fatal.
- *Gloriosa*: flame lily (*G. superba*) is a climbing herb with leaf tips extended into tendrils and red-and-yellow, strongly recurved, wavy tepals, native to tropical Africa and Asia (it occurs in southern Yunnan), and the tuber is extremely poisonous.
- *Disporum*: with rhizomes and a branched upper stem that resembles bamboo; flowers nodding, fruit a berry. *D. cantoniense* and *D. uniflorum* occur in forests throughout China.

Changes in circumscription: the Chinese textbooks and Cronquist place *Colchicum* and its relatives in Liliaceae; Dahlgren (1985) separated them as Colchicaceae, which APG adopted, and *Disporum* and others, formerly in "Uvulariaceae", were also merged into this family.

### 36.6.6. Other Families of Liliales

- Alstroemeriaceae: Central and South America; the petiole or leaf base is twisted through 180°, so that the underside of the leaf faces up (resupinate leaves); flowers bilaterally symmetric, ovary inferior. *Alstroemeria* (Peruvian lily) is an important cut flower.
- Philesiaceae and Ripogonaceae: climbers of southern South America, Australia and New Zealand; the copihue (*Lapageria rosea*), the national flower of Chile, belongs to Philesiaceae.
- Campynemataceae and Corsiaceae: the former occurs in Australia and New Caledonia; the latter are mycoheterotrophs, and *Corsiopsis* occurs in Guangdong, China.

Clues for recognizing monocots and the lilioid groups:

- Synapomorphies of the monocots: one cotyledon, fibrous root system, scattered determinate vascular bundles, parallel venation and leaf sheaths, PII-type sieve-element plastids, trimerous flowers; monosulcate pollen is a plesiomorphy. Reticulate venation (Araceae, Dioscoreaceae, Smilacaceae, *Paris*), secondary thickening (*Dracaena*, primary thickening of palms) and dimerous or tetramerous flowers (Stemonaceae, Potamogetonaceae, *Paris*) are exceptions.
- Alismatales: intravaginal squamules at the nodes; all except Araceae are aquatic or wetland plants with seeds lacking endosperm; from the complete trimerous flower (Alismataceae) to the extremely simplified water-pollinated flowers (*Najas*, Zosteraceae). All seagrasses are in Alismatales, and the marine habit arose independently at least three times.
- Difference between Liliales and Asparagales (the basis for splitting Liliaceae s.l.): in Liliales the tepals are often spotted, the nectaries are at the base of the tepals, the anthers are extrorse and the seeds are pale; Asparagales mostly have septal nectaries, and the seeds often have a black phytomelan layer ([7.12.37](/7.12.37)).

## Questions

1. Why are the monocots a monophyletic group while the dicots are not? Can monosulcate pollen serve as a synapomorphy of the monocots?

   **Answer** In molecular phylogenies all monocots form a single clade and share synapomorphies such as one cotyledon, scattered vascular bundles and PII-type sieve-element plastids; the "dicots", by contrast, include the ANA grade, the magnoliids, Chloranthales, Ceratophyllales and the eudicots, with the monocots nested among them, so the dicots are a paraphyletic group. Monosulcate pollen cannot serve as a synapomorphy of the monocots: it also occurs in the ANA grade and the magnoliids and is a plesiomorphy of angiosperms; only the tricolpate pollen of the eudicots is a derived character. Monosulcate pollen is diagnostic only in comparison with the eudicots.

2. Why was *Acorus* separated from Araceae and placed in an order of its own?

   **Answer** *Acorus* and Araceae both have a spadix, but they differ in many important respects: *Acorus* has oil cells and no raphides, whereas Araceae have bundles of raphides and laticifers and no oil cells; the leaf of *Acorus* is a sword-shaped unifacial leaf, distichous and equitant, whereas the leaves of Araceae are mostly broad and petiolate; the flowers of *Acorus* are bisexual with 6 tepals, and anther wall development is of the dicot type; the leaf-like "spathe" of *Acorus* is a continuation of the scape and does not enclose the inflorescence. Molecular analyses further show that *Acorus* is the sister group of all other monocots and is far removed from Araceae (Alismatales), so it is placed in its own family and order, Acoraceae and Acorales.

3. The flowers of Alismataceae have free carpels, numerous stamens and a differentiated calyx and corolla, like Ranunculaceae. Can we conclude from this that Alismatales are the most primitive monocots, or that the monocots originated from the ranunculoids?

   **Answer** No. First, the earliest-diverging order in the phylogeny is Acorales, Alismatales are the second branch, and Araceae, the earliest-diverging family of Alismatales, have fused carpels; free carpels and numerous stamens occur only in the petaloid branch of Alismatales and were secondarily increased there: the stamens and carpels of Alismataceae arise in pairs or bundles centrifugally, unlike the centripetal development in Ranunculaceae. Second, the seeds of Alismatales lack endosperm and the pollen is trinucleate, both derived characters. Third, Ranunculaceae belong to the eudicots and have tricolpate pollen; they diverged later than the monocots and cannot be their ancestors. The resemblance between Alismataceae and Ranunculaceae results from convergence or from the retention of plesiomorphies.

4. Into which families has Cronquist's Liliaceae been split in APG IV? What is the basis of the split? Give a representative genus for each family.

   **Answer** Remaining in Liliales are Liliaceae (s.s.; *Lilium*, *Fritillaria*, *Tulipa*, *Erythronium*), Melanthiaceae (*Veratrum*, *Paris*, *Trillium*), Smilacaceae (*Smilax*) and Colchicaceae (*Colchicum*, *Gloriosa*, *Disporum*); moved to Asparagales are Asphodelaceae (*Hemerocallis*, *Aloe*), Amaryllidaceae (*Allium*, *Agapanthus*), Asparagaceae (*Asparagus*, *Polygonatum*, *Hosta*, *Convallaria*, *Ophiopogon*, *Hyacinthus*, and *Agave* and *Yucca* of the former Agavaceae) and Hypoxidaceae. The basis is molecular phylogeny: the shared characters of Liliaceae s.l. (6 tepals, 6 stamens, 3 fused carpels) are plesiomorphies of the monocots, the group is not monophyletic, and Orchidaceae, Iridaceae and others are nested within it; in morphology, Liliales often have spotted tepals, nectaries at the base of the tepals and pale seeds, whereas Asparagales mostly have septal nectaries and seeds with a black phytomelan layer.

5. Why can colchicine induce polyploids? Why might its widespread occurrence in Colchicaceae be advantageous to the plants?

   **Answer** Colchicine binds to tubulin and prevents microtubule polymerization, so that the spindle cannot form; the chromosomes in the cell have already replicated but cannot move to the two poles, and the cell arrests at metaphase; when it returns to interphase it becomes a cell with a doubled chromosome number, and the tissue or plant developing from it is polyploid. For the Colchicaceae themselves, colchicine is a defensive secondary metabolite: it is highly toxic to animals and deters herbivores from feeding. The plants' own tubulin has a low affinity for colchicine, so they tolerate high concentrations in their tissues.
