In ferns and lycophytes the gametophyte lives independently and fertilization requires water ([7.12.16](/7.12.16), [7.12.17](/7.12.17)). Seed plants hand both problems over to the sporophyte. The megaspore stays inside its sporangium on the parent plant, where the female gametophyte develops under protection and supply from the parent. The microspore develops into the male gametophyte, the *pollen grain*, which wind or animals carry to the ovule, and a pollen tube then delivers the sperm to the egg. After fertilization the whole ovule becomes a seed, which replaces the spore as the unit of dispersal. This chapter treats the origin of this mode of reproduction and its expression in gymnosperms. We first describe the main features of gymnosperms (§25.1) and the origin of the seed (§25.2), then the early fossil groups of seed plants (§25.3). We then use pine as the model for a detailed account of the gymnosperm life cycle (§25.4), treat the special features of reproduction in cycads and ginkgo (§25.5) and in gnetophytes (§25.6), and finally compare gymnosperms with angiosperms (§25.7) as preparation for the chapters on the flower, the ovule and double fertilization ([7.12.27](/7.12.27)–[7.12.30](/7.12.30)). The classification of living gymnosperms and their families is the subject of [7.12.26](/7.12.26). The structure of gymnosperm stems, wood and needles was treated as a contrast in [7.12.21](/7.12.21)–[7.12.23](/7.12.23), and the general structure of the seed in [7.12.19](/7.12.19).

## 25.1. Main Features of Gymnosperms

### 25.1.1. Definition and Position

**Definition 25.1.1 (Gymnosperm)** A seed plant whose ovules are naked, not enclosed by carpels, and which therefore forms no fruit, is called a **gymnosperm**. The living gymnosperms comprise four groups, the cycads, ginkgo, conifers and gnetophytes, with a little over 1000 species in all.

Gymnosperms stand between ferns and angiosperms. Like ferns, most of them retain archegonia and have a multicellular gametophyte. Like angiosperms, they produce pollen and seeds, and fertilization no longer needs external water. Traditionally the bryophytes, ferns and gymnosperms together are called the **archegoniates**, and the gymnosperms and angiosperms together the **seed plants** (spermatophytes, "flowering plants" in the broad old sense).

**Definition 25.1.2 (Ovule)** In seed plants, the structure that consists of the **nucellus** (the megasporangium) and one or two surrounding **integuments**, contains one female gametophyte, and develops into a seed after fertilization is called an **ovule**. The small opening that the integuments leave at the apex is the **micropyle**, through which pollen or the pollen tube enters the nucellus; the region at the base where the integuments and nucellus are joined is the chalaza. Gymnosperm ovules usually have one integument (two in gnetophytes), angiosperm ovules usually two.

Ovule and seed correspond as follows: the integument develops into the **seed coat**; the nucellus is mostly resorbed, leaving a thin layer; the female gametophyte (in gymnosperms) or the endosperm (in angiosperms) becomes the storage tissue; and the zygote develops into the **embryo**.

### 25.1.2. Two Sets of Terms

The reproductive organs of seed plants were first described with one set of morphological terms (flower, stamen, carpel, pollen, ovule). Only later was it recognized that they correspond one to one with the strobili, sporophylls, sporangia and spores of ferns. In 1851 Hofmeister compared the embryology of bryophytes, ferns and conifers and showed that all of them have an **alternation of generations** between gametophyte and sporophyte, after which the two sets of terms could be used side by side:

| Fern term | Gymnosperms | Angiosperms |
|---|---|---|
| Strobilus | Cone (pollen cone, seed cone) | Flower |
| Microsporophyll | Stamen (microsporophyll) | Stamen |
| Megasporophyll | Ovuliferous scale, collar, aril-bearing receptacle, epimatium, or pinnate megasporophyll | Carpel |
| Microsporangium | Pollen sac | Pollen sac (anther locule) |
| Megasporangium | Nucellus | Nucellus |
| Microspore | Uninucleate pollen grain | Uninucleate pollen grain |
| Megaspore | Uninucleate female gametophyte (megaspore) | Uninucleate embryo sac |
| Male gametophyte | Multicellular pollen grain and pollen tube | Two- or three-celled pollen grain and pollen tube |
| Female gametophyte | Female gametophyte (called "endosperm" in the seed, $n$) | Mature embryo sac (the endosperm arises separately by fertilization) |

This chapter uses both sets: "pollen cone", "seed cone" and "ovule" when we discuss morphology, and "microsporophyll", "megaspore" and "male gametophyte" when we discuss the life cycle.

**Definition 25.1.3 (Male and Female Gametophytes)** In a heterosporous plant, the gametophyte that develops from a microspore and produces only sperm is the **male gametophyte** (microgametophyte); the gametophyte that develops from a megaspore and produces only eggs is the **female gametophyte** (megagametophyte). The male gametophyte of a seed plant is the pollen grain together with the pollen tube formed on germination; the female gametophyte develops within the nucellus of the ovule. Both depend on the sporophyte for nutrition.

### 25.1.3. Five Main Features

Chinese textbooks summarize the characters of gymnosperms in five points, each of which is best understood by comparison with ferns and angiosperms.

1. The sporophyte is well developed. All gymnosperms are perennial woody plants, most of them tall trees with monopodial branching, and the branches are often differentiated into long shoots and short shoots ([7.12.21](/7.12.21)). They have a vascular cambium and secondary growth, and the stem has a **eustele** with collateral bundles. The xylem consists almost entirely of **tracheids** (gnetophytes have **vessels**), and the phloem has only **sieve cells**, with no sieve tubes or **companion cells**. Leaves are mostly needle-, linear- or scale-shaped, with a few broad flat leaves, and the leaf surface often bears pale stomatal bands made of several rows of stomata. The root system is a well-developed taproot system.
2. Ovules are naked. Sporophylls are mostly aggregated into unisexual strobili (cones), monoecious or dioecious. Microsporophylls are aggregated into pollen cones, each bearing pollen sacs on its lower side. Megasporophylls do not form closed carpels; the ovules sit on their surface or margin. In the different groups the megasporophyll is modified into the ovuliferous scale of conifers, the collar of ginkgo, the aril-bearing receptacle of yews, the epimatium of podocarps, the pinnate megasporophyll of cycads and the envelope (perianth-like covering) of gnetophytes.
3. Archegonia are present. Except in *Gnetum* and *Welwitschia*, the female gametophyte forms archegonia at the end near the micropyle. Gymnosperm archegonia are more reduced than those of ferns: the neck is very short, with only 2–4 neck cells (two tiers in pine) and no neck canal cells; the venter has an egg cell and a ventral canal cell that soon disappears; and the whole archegonium is embedded in the female gametophyte.
4. Pollen goes directly to the ovule at pollination. Pollen is mostly wind-borne and lands in the pollination drop secreted at the micropyle; as the drop retracts it draws the pollen into the micropyle, and the pollen germinates at the top of the nucellus. In angiosperms, by contrast, pollen lands on the stigma and the pollen tube must grow through the style to reach the ovule. In many gymnosperms the top of the nucellus bears a hollow, the pollen chamber, in which pollen is held. The interval from pollination to fertilization is long, about a year and more in pine.
5. Polyembryony is common. Several embryos often begin to develop in one ovule, but usually only one matures (Definition 25.1.5).

In addition, gymnosperm pollen is monosulcate or inaperturate, and the pollen of many Pinaceae and Podocarpaceae has two air sacs; it lacks the tricolpate, tricolporate and polyporate types common in angiosperms.

**Definition 25.1.4 (Pollination Drop)** The droplet of liquid that the ovule of a gymnosperm secretes from the micropyle during the receptive period is called the **pollination drop**. It captures pollen that lands nearby and is then resorbed, carrying the pollen into the micropyle and onto the nucellus.

The pollination drop contains sugars, amino acids, organic acids and many proteins, the last thought to take part in defense against pathogens and in regulating pollen germination. In *Ephedra*, *Gnetum* and *Welwitschia* the drop is rich in sugar and attracts insects that feed on it, so pollination is partly by insects. The stigmatic secretion of angiosperms can be regarded as a transfer of this function.

**Definition 25.1.5 (Polyembryony)** The formation of more than one embryo in an ovule is called **polyembryony**. When the eggs of several archegonia in one female gametophyte are fertilized and each develops into an embryo, the phenomenon is **simple polyembryony**; when a single zygote divides during development into several embryos, it is **cleavage polyembryony**.

The female gametophytes of most gymnosperms have two or more archegonia, so simple polyembryony is widespread; cleavage polyembryony occurs in a few genera such as *Pinus* and *Cupressus* (§25.4.6). Through "embryo selection" usually only one embryo matures, and about 3%–4% of pine seeds contain two or more embryos.

### 25.1.4. Are Gymnosperms a Monophyletic Group?

By definition, "gymnosperms" are a group delimited by shared absences (no carpels, no fruit), and such groups are often **paraphyletic groups**. This is indeed the case when fossils are included: angiosperms arose from one lineage of ancient seed plants, and the early seed ferns, cordaites and others form the seed plants together with angiosperms. "All gymnosperms" would exclude angiosperms and is therefore paraphyletic (§25.3).

For living plants, however, molecular phylogenetic analyses agree that the cycads, ginkgo, conifers and gnetophytes form a **monophyletic group** that is the **sister group** of the angiosperms. The living seed plants therefore divide into two main branches, the living gymnosperms and the angiosperms (see the figure in §25.3.5). The gnetophytes, which look morphologically "intermediate between gymnosperms and angiosperms", actually lie within the gymnosperms, and their similarities to angiosperms (vessels, a perianth-like envelope, loss of archegonia) result from **convergent evolution**.

## 25.2. The Origin of the Seed

### 25.2.1. From Heterospory to the Ovule

A seed is an ovule that has been fertilized, contains an embryo and food reserves, and is protected by a seed coat. From spore plants to seed plants the megasporangium underwent a series of changes (usually summarized from the fossils as six or seven steps; here they are combined into five, see the figure below):

1. **Heterospory**: one plant produces two kinds of spores, the megaspores developing into female gametophytes and the microspores into male gametophytes. Heterospory arose independently several times, in lycophytes (*Selaginella* and the arborescent lycophytes), in ferns (Salviniales) and in progymnosperms ([7.12.16](/7.12.16#sec-16.5.3), [7.12.18](/7.12.18#sec-18.3.2)).
2. The number of megaspores is reduced to one: each megasporangium contains a single megaspore mother cell, and of the four megaspores produced by meiosis only one is functional while the other three degenerate.
3. The female gametophyte develops inside the megaspore wall and inside the megasporangium: the functional megaspore is no longer shed, the endosporic female gametophyte is nourished by the parent sporophyte, and the embryo also begins to develop within it.
4. Integuments surround the megasporangium: several sterile lobes (integumentary lobes) grow from the base of the megasporangium and surround it, and the lobes gradually fuse, leaving only the micropyle at the apex.
5. The apex of the megasporangium becomes a pollen-receiving structure: a pollen chamber forms at the top of the nucellus and a mechanism for secreting the pollination drop appears, so that pollen falls directly on the megasporangium and germinates there.

These changes were accompanied by a change in the dispersal unit: spore plants disperse by spores, whereas seed plants disperse by the megasporangium that encloses the embryo (the seed).

### 25.2.2. The Earliest Ovules

The fossil record of seed origin is concentrated from the Late Devonian to the Early Carboniferous, and roughly follows the order above (ages are approximate):

| Fossil | Age | Features |
|---|---|---|
| *Runcaria heinzelinii* | Middle Devonian, about 385 Ma, Belgium | A megasporangium surrounded by a ring of sterile appendages and bearing an apical projection that may have received pollen, but with no complete integument; called a "proto-ovule" (Gerrienne et al. 2004); about 20 million years older than the earliest seeds |
| *Elkinsia polymorpha* | Late Devonian, about 365 Ma, West Virginia, USA | One of the earliest known seeds; the nucellus has four or five nearly free, inward-curving integumentary lobes, and the ovules are borne in pairs in a sterile cupule with dichotomous branches |
| *Archaeosperma arnoldii* | Late Devonian, Pennsylvania, USA | The ovule is surrounded by a cupule with 8–11 finger-like projections; the megasporangium preserves one functional megaspore and three aborted ones |
| *Genomosperma* → *Eurystoma* → *Stamnostoma* | Early Carboniferous, Scotland | The integumentary lobes range from completely free through partly fused to fully fused with only the micropyle left, a series showing the stepwise formation of the integument |
| *Lagenostoma* (the ovule of *Lyginopteris*) | Carboniferous | The integument is complete, the top of the nucellus forms a beak-like projection surrounded by a pollen chamber, and the cupule bears glands; pollen grains are often found in the pollen chamber, but pollen tubes have not been seen |

Three points about these early ovules deserve notice. They have no flowers or fruits, so seeds appeared more than 200 million years before flowers. Well-developed embryos have almost never been found in the seeds, so the seeds may have been fertilized, or the embryo developed, only after they were shed. Only pollen grains, and no pollen tubes, are found in the pollen chamber, so the sperm probably swam to the archegonia, as in cycads and ginkgo.

Two explanations have been given for the origin of the integument. One holds that the integumentary lobes arose by fusion of sterile terminal branches (telomes; see the telome theory in [7.12.16](/7.12.16#sec-16.4.1)) that surrounded the sporangium. The other holds that the integument came from several sterile sporangia. The first explanation agrees better with the morphology of fossils such as *Elkinsia* and *Genomosperma*. Another view in Chinese textbooks, that the integument arose by union of several units each representing a sterile megasporophyll, so that the whole ovule is a synangium, can be regarded as a variant of the second explanation.

### 25.2.3. The Significance of the Seed

The seed gave seed plants a great advantage over spore plants:

- Protection: the seed coat and the several tissue layers of the ovule protect the embryo against desiccation, mechanical injury and pathogens.
- Nutrition: at the critical stage of germination and seedling establishment the embryo has stored food at its disposal and need not start from a single cell as a spore does.
- Dormancy and dispersal: most seeds can become dormant to survive unfavorable seasons, and wind, water and animals disperse them in space and time.
- Freedom from water: the male gametophyte travels as pollen and the megaspore never leaves the parent, so fertilization no longer depends on external water, and seed plants could occupy dry uplands and continental interiors.

When the climate became drier in the Permian, seed plants replaced the arborescent lycophytes and tree ferns of Carboniferous swamps as the main component of land vegetation ([7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.5.3)).

## 25.3. Progymnosperms and Early Groups of Seed Plants

### 25.3.1. Progymnosperms

**Definition 25.3.1 (Progymnosperm)** One of a group of woody plants from the Middle Devonian to the Early Carboniferous that had secondary xylem (composed of tracheids with bordered pits) and secondary phloem like those of conifers but still reproduced by dispersed spores, without ovules or seeds, is called a **progymnosperm**. Progymnosperms are thought to be the group closest to the ancestors of seed plants.

Progymnosperms were the first vascular plants with a bifacial vascular cambium, producing secondary xylem inward and secondary phloem outward, a character of seed plants. Their branching systems were also more complex and better differentiated than those of the trimerophytes. There are two main types:

- Aneurophytales (for example *Aneurophyton*): Middle Devonian trees whose ultimate branchlets forked in three dimensions rather than lying flat, with no leaf veins. The sporangia were ovate and borne on the ultimate branchlets, and the spores were of one kind. The "primitive fern" *Protopteridium* from the Devonian of Yunnan, as Chinese textbooks call it, also belongs to this group ([7.12.17](/7.12.17#sec-17.8)).
- Archaeopteridales (for example *Archaeopteris*): they appeared in the Late Devonian about 370 Ma and lasted into the Early Carboniferous about 340 Ma, and were the main components of the earliest forests on Earth. The trees reached more than 20 m, and their fossil trunks, called *Callixylon*, could exceed 1 m in diameter. The lateral branch systems were flat and arranged in one plane, bearing flat, veined leaves, so that the whole branch system resembled a fern frond and was long taken for a fern. The stem had a pith and a **eustele**, and the sporangia were borne on fertile branchlets; some species of *Archaeopteris* were already **heterosporous**.

*Archaeopteris* thus combined the wood, eustele and heterospory of seed plants with reproduction by spores, showing that wood and heterospory evolved before the seed. It was once regarded as "a transitional type between true ferns and gymnosperms", and was assigned to the progymnosperms only after Beck showed in the 1960s that the leafy branches of *Archaeopteris* and the *Callixylon* wood belong to the same plant. It is now generally held that seed plants evolved from progymnosperm-like ancestors, and progymnosperms together with seed plants form the lignophytes; the progymnosperms themselves may be a **paraphyletic group**.

### 25.3.2. Seed Ferns

**Definition 25.3.2 (Seed Fern)** One of a number of seed plants from the Late Devonian to the Mesozoic that had large fern-like pinnate leaves but reproduced by seeds is called a **seed fern** (pteridosperm). It is not a natural group but an artificial assemblage of early seed plants.

The leaves of seed ferns closely resemble fern leaves, and many Carboniferous "fern" leaf fossils were later found to bear seeds. In 1903–1904 Oliver and Scott showed that the leaves of *Lyginopteris oldhamia* and the ovule *Lagenostoma* belong to the same plant, and the concept of seed ferns was established. The important groups are:

- Lyginopteridales: sprawling or climbing Carboniferous plants with slender stems, a large pith and secondary xylem; the ovules were small and surrounded by a glandular cupule (§25.2.2).
- Medullosales (*Medullosa*): Late Carboniferous to Early Permian upright plants about 5 m tall with a tuft of huge pinnate leaves at the top, a stem with several vascular cylinders (polystele) and very large ovules up to several centimeters long. They were common in the drier parts of tropical swamps and were once thought to be related to cycads.
- Glossopteridales (*Glossopteris*): the dominant plants of Permian Gondwana, with tongue-shaped simple leaves with a midrib and reticulate lateral veins; the ovules were borne on reproductive organs attached to the leaves. The *Glossopteris* flora has been found in South America, Africa, India, Australia and Antarctica, and is among the important evidence Wegener used for continental drift.
- *Gigantopteris*: a representative of the Permian Cathaysian flora, with large leaves and a complex reticulate venation resembling that of angiosperms; its systematic position is unclear, and it is mostly placed with the seed ferns.

The land floras of the Carboniferous and Permian can be divided into four phytogeographic provinces: the Euramerican and Cathaysian floras (*Gigantopteris*) grew in the humid tropical belt, the Angara flora in the seasonal northern regions, and the Gondwana flora (*Glossopteris*) on the southern continents.

Several Mesozoic groups are often placed with the seed ferns. Among them the Caytoniales (*Caytonia*) bore ovules enclosed in a nearly closed, recurved cupule with a lip-like opening that received pollen, and in 1925 Thomas took it for an angiosperm on this basis; its relation to the angiosperm carpel is discussed in [7.12.34](/7.12.34).

### 25.3.3. Cordaites

The cordaites (Cordaitales) were tall Carboniferous to Permian trees with strap-shaped simple leaves up to 1 m long and parallel, repeatedly forked veins. The wood was dense and conifer-like, and the pith was often divided transversely into plates. The seed and pollen cones formed loose compound cone axes, with the ovules borne on short shoots with sterile bracts, and pollen grains are often found in the pollen chamber. The reproductive short shoots of cordaites closely resemble the cones of early conifers such as *Lebachia*, so Chinese textbooks held that conifers are descendants of cordaites. The present view is that cordaites, conifers and ginkgo belong to one closely related "coniferophyte" lineage of seed plants, but which was ancestral to which cannot yet be determined.

### 25.3.4. Bennettitaleans

The Bennettitales (also called cycadeoids) were seed plants of the Triassic to the end of the Cretaceous that looked very much like cycads, with a short, thick or columnar stem covered with leaf bases and a crown of pinnate leaves. They differ from cycads in the following ways:

- The stomata differ in structure: in bennettitaleans the subsidiary cells and guard cells arise from the same mother cell (syndetocheilic), whereas in cycads they do not (haplocheilic).
- The strobili are often bisexual. In the Jurassic *Williamsoniella*, for example, the center is a receptacle bearing many orthotropous ovules, with club-shaped interseminal scales between the ovules, surrounded by a whorl of microsporophylls and outermost by bracts, so that the whole looks like a flower. The bisexual strobili of *Cycadeoidea* probably never opened and were mainly self-fertilized.
- The seed has almost no female gametophyte tissue and is filled by a large embryo with two cotyledons; Chinese textbooks therefore call these seeds "endosperm-free".

The bisexual "flowers" of bennettitaleans led to their being regarded as ancestors or close relatives of angiosperms (the anthophyte hypothesis, see §25.3.5). Combined molecular and fossil analyses do not support this hypothesis, and the similarities between bennettitaleans and angiosperms are mostly the result of independent evolution.

### 25.3.5. The Five Living Lineages of Seed Plants

The living seed plants fall into five lineages: cycads, ginkgo, conifers, gnetophytes and angiosperms, the first four together being the living gymnosperms (§25.1.4). Several hypotheses have been proposed for their relationships:

| Hypothesis | Content | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Anthophyte hypothesis | Gnetophytes, bennettitaleans and angiosperms form one branch, with flower-like reproductive structures as the shared derived character | Once supported by morphological analyses, rejected by molecular data |
| Gnetifer hypothesis | Gnetophytes are the sister group of all conifers | Supported by some molecular analyses |
| Gnepine hypothesis | Gnetophytes are nested within the conifers as the sister group of Pinaceae | Supported by most phylogenomic analyses (for example Ran et al. 2018), but genes and methods still conflict |

Under any of them, cycads and ginkgo are sister groups and form the basal branch of the living gymnosperms (see [7.12.26](/7.12.26#sec-26.1)).

## 25.4. The Life Cycle of Pine

The genus *Pinus* is the most thoroughly studied and most familiar gymnosperm, and we use it to represent the gymnosperm life cycle. From the appearance of the seed cone to the ripening of the seed, the reproductive cycle of pine spans two growing seasons, about two years.

### 25.4.1. The Sporophyte

The pine sporophyte is a tall evergreen tree with monopodial branching, an upright trunk and whorled lateral branches. There are two kinds of branches. Long shoots grow indefinitely and bear spirally arranged scale leaves; in the axils of the scale leaves arise very short dwarf shoots, each bearing a single fascicle of needles (usually 2, 3 or 5 needles per fascicle, depending on the species), with a sheath at the base made of 8–12 bud scales. A needle fascicle is in fact a short shoot of limited growth, and in special cases such as injury to the terminal bud its apical meristem can become active again and grow into a new shoot. More than 90% of the stem is tracheids, resin canals make up about 1% and rays about 6% (wood structure in [7.12.22](/7.12.22)); for the xeromorphic structure of the needle see [7.12.23](/7.12.23).

Pollen and seed cones are borne on the same tree. Pollen cones are mostly at the base of new shoots, often in the lower crown; seed cones are near the tip of new shoots, often in the upper crown. Pollen is generally not blown directly upward, and this arrangement favors cross-pollination between trees.

### 25.4.2. The Pollen Cone and Microspores

The pollen cone (microsporangiate strobilus) is 1–2 cm long and consists of many spirally arranged microsporophylls, each bearing on its lower (abaxial) surface a pair of elongate microsporangia (pollen sacs). In early spring the microspore mother cells in the microsporangia undergo meiosis and each forms four microspores (uninucleate pollen grains). The microspore has two wall layers, and the outer wall bulges outward on two sides to form two air-filled sacs (wings or bladders) that let the pollen grain float in the air; after the grain falls into the pollination drop they also keep it floating, with the germinal aperture oriented toward the nucellus.

### 25.4.3. The Male Gametophyte

The microspore is the first cell of the male gametophyte and begins to germinate while still in the microsporangium, undergoing several unequal divisions (figure below):

1. The microspore divides into a small first prothallial cell and a large embryonic cell.
2. The embryonic cell divides again into a second prothallial cell and an antheridial initial (central cell).
3. The antheridial initial divides into a tube cell and a generative cell.

The two prothallial cells are remnants of the vegetative part of the male gametophyte and soon degenerate. The pollen is shed at the four-celled stage (two degenerate prothallial cells, one generative cell and one tube cell), which is an immature male gametophyte. After pollination the generative cell divides into a stalk cell (sterile cell) and a body cell (spermatogenous cell), and the tube cell elongates into the pollen tube. About a year later, before the pollen tube reaches the archegonia, the body cell divides into two nonflagellate sperm, and the male gametophyte is mature. The male gametophyte of seed plants no longer forms an antheridium.

After pollination the generative cell divides into a stalk cell and a body cell, and about a year later the body cell divides into two nonmotile sperm.](./plots/pine-male-gametophyte.py)

### 25.4.4. The Seed Cone and Ovule

One or several seed cones (megasporangiate strobili) arise near the tip of the current year's shoot. They are red or purple when young, turn green later and are brown when ripe. A cone is made up of many "megasporophylls" spirally arranged on a central axis, but each unit consists of two parts: below is a smaller thin scale, the bract scale, and above it a larger part thickened at the top, the ovuliferous scale, whose adaxial surface bears two inverted ovules at the base, with the micropyles directed toward the axis.

**Definition 25.4.1 (Cone)** The female strobilus of conifers, made of many bract–ovuliferous scale complexes arranged spirally, decussately or in whorls on a central axis, which becomes woody (rarely fleshy) after the ovules develop into seeds, is called a **cone**. In each unit, the leaf-like structure on the outside is the **bract scale**, the ovule-bearing structure in its axil is the **ovuliferous scale**, and after fertilization the ovuliferous scale enlarges and becomes woody as the **seed scale** (also cone scale).

Chinese textbooks commonly say that "the ovuliferous scale is a megasporophyll and the bract scale a megasporophyll that has lost its fertility". Florin (1951) compared conifer fossils from the Carboniferous to the Mesozoic and showed that the ovuliferous scale is a flattened structure formed by fusion of the sterile and fertile scales of an axillary bud (a dwarf shoot), and that the bract scale is the leaf in whose axil it stands. The seed cone is therefore a compound strobilus (equivalent to an inflorescence), whereas the pollen cone is a simple strobilus (microsporangia borne directly on microsporophylls). The degree of separation or fusion of bract and ovuliferous scales is an important character for classifying conifer families: they are free in Pinaceae and semi-fused to completely fused in Cupressaceae ([7.12.26](/7.12.26#sec-26.7)).

The ovule consists of a very thick integument and the nucellus. The nucellus is the megasporangium; one of its cells develops into the megaspore mother cell, and meiosis forms four megaspores in a row, a linear tetrad. The three nearest the micropyle soon degenerate, and only the one at the chalazal end develops into the female gametophyte.

### 25.4.5. Pollination, Development of the Female Gametophyte and Fertilization

Pine is pollinated in spring (April–May in northern China). The axis of the seed cone elongates slightly and the bract and ovuliferous scales open somewhat. The pollen sacs split lengthwise on their back, and large amounts of pollen are scattered by the wind, land on the ovuliferous scales, and many stick to the pollination drop exuded at the micropyle; as the drop retracts the pollen is drawn into the micropyle and rests in the shallow pollen chamber at the top of the nucellus. After pollination the ovuliferous scales close again to protect the developing ovules. Soon after the pollen lands on the nucellus it germinates and grows a pollen tube. Most ovules that receive no pollen abort.

At pollination meiosis has not yet taken place in the nucellus. About a month after pollination the four megaspores form, and the functional megaspore begins free nuclear divisions to form 16–32 free nuclei; the female gametophyte at this point is a thin layer of cytoplasm around a large central vacuole. With the onset of winter the female gametophyte and the pollen tube resting in the nucellus enter dormancy. In the second spring the free nuclei continue to divide, increasing to about 2000, and the gametophyte enlarges; cell walls then begin to form around the free nuclei, and the female gametophyte becomes cellular tissue. About 15 months after pollination, archegonia differentiate at the micropylar end of the female gametophyte, usually 2–3 in *Pinus* (2–7 in Chinese textbooks, depending on the species).

The pollen tube advances slowly through the nucellus, digesting its way, and on reaching an archegonium destroys the neck cells and bursts at its tip, so that the two sperm, the tube nucleus and the stalk cell nucleus flow into the egg cytoplasm; one sperm fuses with the egg nucleus to form the zygote and the other degenerates. Chinese textbooks give this interval as "13 months after pollination" (pollination in spring of the first year, fertilization in summer of the second), whereas English textbooks usually give about 15 months; the difference is the span from spring to early summer, which varies with species and region. In any case, pine takes more than a year from pollination to fertilization, whereas most other conifers need only a few days to three or four weeks and the cones ripen in the same year.

### 25.4.6. Embryo Development

Embryo development in pine is complex and clearly staged, usually divided into four successive stages:

1. Proembryo stage: the zygote undergoes three successive free nuclear divisions to form 8 free nuclei, which move to the base of the archegonium and arrange themselves in two tiers of 4, and cell walls begin to form. Above the upper 4 cells no wall forms, so their cytoplasm is continuous with that of the egg cytoplasm (the open tier), and the lower tier is the primary embryonic tier. Each tier divides once more to give 16 cells in four tiers, from top to bottom the upper tier, the rosette tier, the suspensor tier (primary suspensor tier) and the embryonic tier, which together form the *proembryo*.
2. Embryo selection stage: the upper tier is at first absorptive and soon disintegrates, and the rosette tier divides a few times and disappears. The 4 cells of the suspensor tier stop dividing and elongate strongly, forming the primary suspensors that push the embryonic cells out of the archegonium and deep into the female gametophyte tissue. The cells of the embryonic tier divide and the cells immediately behind them also elongate, forming secondary suspensors, which together with the primary suspensors make up a much coiled suspensor system. The 4 cells of the embryonic tier separate from each other lengthwise and each develops into an embryo, which is cleavage polyembryony. Together with the simple polyembryony caused by the separate fertilization of several archegonia, 16 or more embryos can begin to develop in one ovule, but through competition (embryo selection) usually only one differentiates normally and matures.
3. Organ and tissue differentiation stage: the embryo elongates into a cylinder; the cells at the end nearer the suspensor (the proximal region) divide mainly transversely and are neatly arranged, developing into the root end and root cap, while the cells at the distal end divide in varying directions and differentiate into the hypocotyl, plumule and cotyledons.
4. Maturation stage: the mature embryo has a radicle, an axis, a plumule and cotyledons (usually 7–10 in pine, depending on the species). The surrounding female gametophyte continues to grow and the nucellus is finally reduced to a thin layer. The integument develops into the seed coat, which has three layers: a fleshy outer layer (poorly developed), a stony middle layer and a papery inner layer.

### 25.4.7. The Seed and the Cone

The pine seed consists of the products of three generations:

| Part | Origin | Ploidy |
|---|---|---|
| Embryo | Zygote | New sporophyte generation, $2n$ |
| "Endosperm" | Female gametophyte | Gametophyte generation, $n$ |
| Seed coat (and remnant of nucellus) | Integument (nucellus) | Parental sporophyte, $2n$ |

The gymnosperm "endosperm" is a female gametophyte already formed before fertilization, and differs in origin from the triploid **endosperm** of angiosperms formed by fertilization ([7.12.30](/7.12.30)); strictly speaking it should be called female gametophyte tissue.

After fertilization the seed cone continues to grow and the ovuliferous scales become woody seed scales. The exposed, thickened tip of the seed scale is the apophysis, and the central raised or depressed part of the apophysis is the umbo; whether the umbo is terminal or dorsal is one of the characters used to distinguish subgenera ([7.12.26](/7.12.26#sec-26.5)). Part of the epidermal tissue of the seed scale separates from the seed and forms the wing at the top of the seed. In autumn of the second year the cone ripens, the seed scales open, and the winged seeds are scattered by the wind. The cones of some pines open only after being heated by wildfire (for example lodgepole pine, *Pinus contorta*, of North America), and some pines (for example Korean pine, Chinese white pine and the North American pinyon pines) have large wingless seeds that birds such as nutcrackers and rodents cache and disperse. At germination the radicle emerges first through the micropyle; the cotyledons at first remain in the seed and absorb food from the female gametophyte, and later are carried out of the ground with the seed coat as the hypocotyl elongates.

## 25.5. Reproduction in Cycads and Ginkgo

The cycads and ginkgo are the only living seed plants with motile sperm. In 1896 Sakugoro Hirase of Tokyo Imperial University discovered motile sperm in the ovule of ginkgo, and in the same year Seiichiro Ikeno found them in the cycad *Cycas revoluta*. This was the first demonstration that seed plants still retain the fern type of fertilization, and it strongly supported the view that seed plants evolved from fern-like ancestors.

### 25.5.1. Cycads

Cycads are dioecious. The pollen cone is borne at the top of the stem and consists of many closely spirally arranged wedge-shaped microsporophylls, each bearing on its lower side many sori of 3–5 microsporangia. The microsporangia are eusporangiate in development, with a wall of several cell layers that splits lengthwise through uneven thickening of the epidermal cell walls, the only gymnosperm example of a sporangium opening mechanism like that of ferns. Female plants of *Cycas* form no seed cone: the megasporophylls form a tuft at the top of the stem, densely covered with yellow-brown hairs, pinnately divided in the upper part and narrowly stalked below, with 2–10 ovules on both sides of the stalk (see the figure in [7.12.26](/7.12.26#sec-26.2)). These are the megasporophylls most similar to vegetative leaves, and the Chinese saying "the sago palm flowers" refers to these megasporophylls unfolding at the top of the stem. In Zamiaceae the megasporophylls are aggregated into cone-like seed cones.

The ovule is large and orthotropous with one thick integument. The top of the nucellus has a beak and a pollen chamber communicating with the micropyle. The female gametophyte passes through a free nuclear stage and then becomes cellular, and 2–5 archegonia lie at the micropylar end, each with a neck of only two cells; a few days before fertilization the central cell nucleus divides into the egg nucleus and a ventral canal cell nucleus that soon disappears.

The microspore germinates to form a three-celled male gametophyte with one prothallial cell (which does not divide again), one tube cell and one generative cell, shed at the three-celled stage. Pollen is carried by wind (and in many cycads chiefly by beetles and other insects) to the micropyle and drawn into the pollen chamber by the pollination drop. The pollen tube grown by the tube cell is not a channel for delivering sperm; like a haustorium it branches through the nucellar tissue and absorbs nutrients, for several months. The generative cell divides into a stalk cell and a body cell, and the body cell divides again to form two sperm. The mature sperm is top-shaped with a spiral band bearing thousands to tens of thousands of flagella, and reaches 0.3–0.4 mm in diameter, visible to the naked eye, the largest sperm in the plant kingdom (Chinese textbooks say "in the living world", which is inaccurate, since the sperm of some animals such as *Drosophila* are much longer). When the sperm mature the base of the pollen tube ruptures and the sperm are released together with the fluid in the tube into the archegonial chamber above the archegonia, swim into an archegonium, and one of them fuses with the egg.

The zygote first undergoes a long period of free nuclear division to form an undifferentiated proembryo; the cells at the base elongate into the suspensor and those at the tip develop into the embryo. The mature seed has an embryo with two cotyledons embedded in the female gametophyte. The integument develops into a seed coat of three layers: a thick fleshy outer layer, a hard middle layer of stone cells and a thin papery inner layer. Cycad seeds have no dormant period, and at germination the radicle emerges through the micropyle while the cotyledons remain in the seed to absorb food.

### 25.5.2. Ginkgo

Ginkgo is dioecious. The pollen cone is catkin-like and borne in the axils of scales at the tips of short shoots, each microsporophyll having a short stalk usually bearing two pendent pollen sacs at its end. The seed cone is very simple: a long stalk arises on a short shoot and forks at the end, each fork bearing a disc-like collar with one orthotropous ovule on it, usually only one of which develops into a seed (occasionally several ovules occur and are regarded as atavistic).

The male gametophyte of ginkgo differs from that of cycads in having two prothallial cells: it is shed at the four-celled stage (two prothallial cells, generative cell and tube cell). The ginkgo pollen tube is likewise haustorial, growing between the nucellar cells and branching much; later the base of the pollen tube swells into a sac containing two multiflagellate sperm, and after the sac ruptures the sperm swim to the archegonia. Development of the female gametophyte is similar to that of cycads, but it contains chlorophyll and so is pale green, the ventral canal cell is distinct, and there are usually 2–3 archegonia. Pollination is in spring, and fertilization is delayed until late summer or early autumn, 4–7 months later; sometimes the embryo is still very small when the seed falls and continues to develop on the ground.

The seed is nearly spherical and yellow when ripe, covered with a white bloom, and is often wrongly called a "fruit"; in fact ginkgo has no carpels and forms no fruit (the "white nut" is the seed with the outer seed coat removed). The seed coat has three layers: the outer layer is thick and fleshy and contains oils and foul-smelling substances such as butyric acid that can cause dermatitis on contact with skin; the middle layer is white and bony with 2–3 longitudinal ridges; the inner layer is reddish brown and membranous. The endosperm (female gametophyte) is fleshy, there are two cotyledons, and germination is hypogeal.

### 25.5.3. The Significance of Haustorial Pollen Tubes

The pollen tubes of cycads and ginkgo do not enter the archegonium but absorb nutrients from the nucellus for the growth of the male gametophyte and the production of sperm; only in conifers, gnetophytes and angiosperms does the pollen tube deliver nonmotile sperm directly to the egg. This shows that the pollen tube originated as a haustorium and that delivery of sperm is a later change of function (an example of functional shift). The sperm of cycads and ginkgo still have to swim in a small drop of liquid above the archegonium, but this liquid is secreted by the ovule itself, so their fertilization is already independent of external water.

## 25.6. Reproductive Features of Gnetophytes

The gnetophytes comprise only three genera, *Ephedra*, *Gnetum* and *Welwitschia*, which differ greatly in form but share several characters: vessels in the secondary xylem; an envelope (pseudoperianth) around the pollen and seed cones that resembles a perianth; an integument (the inner integument in *Gnetum* and *Welwitschia*) extended at the tip into a long, narrow *micropylar tube* from whose mouth the pollination drop is secreted; nonflagellate sperm; and archegonia that are extremely reduced (*Ephedra*) or absent (*Gnetum*, *Welwitschia*).

### 25.6.1. *Ephedra*

*Ephedra* is dioecious, occasionally monoecious, and even bears female and male flowers together on one cone, which is regarded as a trace of derivation of unisexual flowers from bisexual ancestors. The pollen cone is made of several pairs of opposite bracts, each bearing in its axil a male flower: two membranous envelope scales at the base and a stalk in the middle formed by fused filaments, bearing 2–8 pollen sacs at its tip. The seed cone is made of several pairs of decussate bracts or whorls of three, and only the uppermost 1–3 bracts each bear an ovule, which is surrounded by a thick sac-like envelope; the upper part of the integument is extended into the micropylar tube. The male gametophyte is like that of pine, with prothallial cell, tube cell and generative cell at shedding. The female gametophyte at maturity is cellular tissue, usually with two archegonia whose necks are made of more than 32 cells, longer than in other gymnosperms. When the seed ripens the envelope develops into a leathery or fleshy aril-like covering, and in some species the bracts become red and fleshy and enclose the seed, so that the whole seed cone is berry-like and edible.

In 1990 Friedman discovered **double fertilization** in *Ephedra nevadensis*: the central cell nucleus of the archegonium divides into an egg nucleus and a ventral canal nucleus, both remaining in the same egg cytoplasm. Of the two sperm released by the pollen tube into the egg cell, the first fuses with the egg nucleus to form the zygote and the second with the ventral canal nucleus to form a second diploid zygote. The second zygote also begins to develop into an embryo (a kind of additional polyembryony) but soon degenerates, and no endosperm is formed; the nutritive tissue of the *Ephedra* seed remains the female gametophyte.

### 25.6.2. *Gnetum*

The vegetative body of *Gnetum* closely resembles that of a dicotyledon: mostly large woody lianas with simple opposite leaves with pinnate lateral veins and reticulate veinlets, and the vessels of the secondary xylem are much wider than in *Ephedra* and *Welwitschia*. The pollen and seed cones form slender spikes made of several whorls of shallow cup-shaped involucres (formed by fused bracts), each whorl bearing several male or female flowers. The male flower has a tubular envelope with a central stalk bearing two pollen sacs, and sometimes sterile ovules occur among the male flowers. The ovule has two envelopes, the outer thick and formed by fusion of two envelope scales, the inner being the outer integument; the inner integument is extended at the tip into the micropylar tube.

The female gametophyte of *Gnetum* remains in the free nuclear state until fertilization: the megasporangium often has 2–3 megaspore mother cells, each forming four megaspore nuclei (tetrasporic development), several of which begin to develop and only one finally continues. At fertilization some of the free nuclei become surrounded by cytoplasm and act as eggs; no archegonia form, so *Gnetum* has been called "an archegoniate without archegonia". The male gametophyte has only one prothallial cell, or none. Pollen appears to be carried by insects to the pollination drop secreted from the micropylar tube, and the pollen tube delivers two sperm into the female gametophyte, both of which can fuse with free egg nuclei (this double fertilization was documented in *Gnetum gnemon* in the 1990s by Carmichael and Friedman), again without forming endosperm. Embryo development has no free nuclear stage, and there are a well-developed foot, a long axis and two cotyledons; only after fertilization does the female gametophyte become cellular and serve as storage tissue. The seed is drupe-like and enclosed in a red or orange-red fleshy aril.

### 25.6.3. *Welwitschia*

*Welwitschia* has only one pair of strap-shaped leaves throughout its life. It is dioecious, with bright red cones. The pollen cone is made of decussate bracts, each bearing in its axil a male flower with 6 stamens fused at the base and an incompletely developed ovule in the center, which likewise indicates descent from bisexual ancestors. The ovule has two integuments; the inner integument extends into the micropylar tube, and the outer integument spreads to both sides and later becomes the wing of the seed. The female gametophyte forms no archegonia; some cells at its top grow tubular projections upward into the nucellus, meeting the downward-growing pollen tube, and sperm and egg fuse in the tube. *Welwitschia* is pollinated mainly by insects.

### 25.6.4. Similarities to and Differences from Angiosperms

The vessels, envelopes, traces of bisexual flowers, archegonia-free female gametophyte and double fertilization of gnetophytes are often listed as "the most advanced characters among gymnosperms" and were taken as evidence of homology with angiosperms (the anthophyte hypothesis of §25.3.5). But the details of these characters differ from those of angiosperms: the vessels of gnetophytes form by dissolution of the bordered pits in the end walls of tracheids (circular perforation plates), whereas angiosperm vessels arise from the scalariform pitting of the tracheid end walls; and the "double fertilization" of *Ephedra* and *Gnetum* yields a second embryo, not endosperm. Molecular phylogenies show that gnetophytes lie within the living gymnosperms, and these similarities are all **homoplasy**.

## Summary

Gymnosperms (Definition 25.1.1) are seed plants with naked ovules (Definition 25.1.2); the living cycads, ginkgo, conifers and gnetophytes form a monophyletic group, but together with the fossil groups they are paraphyletic. The seed originated through five steps: heterospory, a single megaspore, an endosporic female gametophyte, the integument and a pollen-receiving nucellar apex (§25.2). The earliest ovules date from about 385 to 365 Ma (*Runcaria*, *Elkinsia*); progymnosperms (Definition 25.3.1) had wood and heterospory first, and seed ferns (Definition 25.3.2) had seeds first.

## Questions

1. Why is it said that "seeds appeared before flowers"? List the fossil evidence supporting this conclusion.

   **Answer** *Elkinsia* and *Archaeosperma* of the Late Devonian (about 365 Ma) already had ovules (seeds) with integuments and cupules, and *Runcaria* of the Middle Devonian already had a proto-ovule; reliable angiosperm (flower) fossils appear only in the Early Cretaceous about 130 Ma. The Carboniferous seed ferns (*Lyginopteris*, *Medullosa*) and the Permian *Glossopteris* all reproduced by seeds, yet had no flowers or fruits.

2. The female gametophyte of pine and the endosperm of angiosperms are both storage tissues in the seed. How do they differ, and what is the significance for the plant?

   **Answer** The pine female gametophyte forms from the megaspore by free nuclear division and cellularization, belongs to the haploid gametophyte generation, and is fully grown and stocked with food before fertilization. Angiosperm endosperm forms by fusion of one sperm with the central cell, is mostly triploid, belongs to the new sporophyte generation and develops only after fertilization. The former invests nutrients whether or not the ovule is fertilized; the latter invests only after fertilization succeeds, saving resources and also making seed development faster.

3. Why does pine need two years from pollination to seed ripening? Describe the development of the male and female sides in chronological order.

   **Answer** Pollination takes place in spring of the first year, when meiosis has not yet occurred in the ovule. After germination the pollen tube stops growing in the nucellus and overwinters, and in the first year the female gametophyte undergoes only free nuclear division and then goes dormant. In the second spring the female gametophyte resumes division and cellularizes, archegonia differentiate about 15 months after pollination, the pollen tube resumes growth and the body cell divides into two sperm, and fertilization occurs in the second summer (13–15 months after pollination). The embryo then develops and the seed ripens and is shed in autumn of the second year.

4. Is the motile sperm of cycads and ginkgo in conflict with their status as seed plants? What does the haustorial pollen tube show?

   **Answer** No. In cycads and ginkgo the megaspore stays in the ovule and the male gametophyte reaches the ovule as pollen carried by wind or insects. The sperm need to swim only in a small drop of liquid secreted by the ovule above the archegonium, so fertilization does not depend on external water, and they are typical seed plants. Their pollen tube does not deliver sperm but absorbs nutrients from the nucellus like a haustorium, which shows that the pollen tube originated as a vegetative organ and became a channel for delivering nonmotile sperm only in conifers, gnetophytes and angiosperms.

5. Gnetophytes have vessels, envelopes and "double fertilization", and were once thought to be close relatives of angiosperms. Why are these now considered convergent?

   **Answer** Molecular phylogenies place gnetophytes within the living gymnosperms (often as the sister group of Pinaceae), and the living gymnosperms as a whole are the sister group of angiosperms, so the characters shared by gnetophytes and angiosperms cannot come from a common ancestor. The details also differ: gnetophyte vessels form by dissolution of bordered pits in the end walls, angiosperm vessels from scalariform pitting; the "double fertilization" of *Ephedra* and *Gnetum* forms a second embryo, not endosperm; and the envelope is a bract-derived structure not homologous with the angiosperm perianth. They are therefore expressions of homoplasy.
