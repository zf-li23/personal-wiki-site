The living gymnosperms number only a little over a thousand species, less than one percent of the angiosperms, yet they form extensive coniferous forests in cold-temperate regions and on high mountains and supply most of the world's timber and pulp. They include the tallest (coast redwood), the largest (giant sequoia) and the longest-lived (bristlecone pine) organisms on Earth. China is one of the richest countries in gymnosperms and the home of relict plants such as ginkgo, dawn redwood, Cathaya and golden larch. The previous chapter used pine to describe gymnosperm reproduction ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)); this chapter treats their diversity. We first follow the changes in classification up to the current system of Christenhusz et al. (2011) (§26.1), then follow that system through the cycads (§26.2), ginkgo (§26.3), the gnetophytes (§26.4) and the three orders of conifers (§26.5–§26.7), and finally treat the gymnosperm resources of China (§26.8). For each family we give diagnostic characters, representative genera and species, economic importance and the correspondence with the older system (changes of family and genus). The vegetative organs of gymnosperms (tracheid wood, resin canals, needle structure) were treated in [7.12.21](/7.12.21)–[7.12.23](/7.12.23).

## 26.1. Classification Systems

### 26.1.1. The Traditional Class and Order System

Chinese gymnosperm classification long followed the system of Zheng Wanjun in volume 7 of *Flora Reipublicae Popularis Sinicae* (1978), which treats the **gymnosperms** as one division with 4 classes, 9 orders, 12 families, 71 genera and nearly 800 species:

| Class | Order | Family |
|---|---|---|
| Cycadopsida | Cycadales | Cycadaceae |
| Ginkgopsida | Ginkgoales | Ginkgoaceae |
| Coniferopsida | Coniferales | Pinaceae, Taxodiaceae, Cupressaceae, Araucariaceae |
| | Podocarpales | Podocarpaceae |
| | Cephalotaxales | Cephalotaxaceae |
| | Taxales | Taxaceae |
| Gnetopsida | Ephedrales, Gnetales, Welwitschiales | Ephedraceae, Gnetaceae, Welwitschiaceae |

In the second volume of the textbook *Botany* by Wu Guofang and colleagues, Podocarpaceae, Cephalotaxaceae and Taxaceae are separated from the conifers and placed in a separate class, Taxopsida, on the grounds that their megasporophylls are specialized into a receptacle or epimatium, they form no cones, and their seeds have a fleshy aril or outer seed coat; this gives 5 classes. The gnetophytes are also called Chlamydospermopsida. These systems rest mainly on morphology, and the division into classes gives great weight to whether cones are present.

### 26.1.2. The System of Christenhusz et al. (2011)

Molecular phylogenetic studies changed this picture:

- Taxodiaceae is not a **monophyletic group**: Cupressaceae in the narrow sense is nested within Taxodiaceae, and the two are merged into Cupressaceae in the broad sense (Cupressaceae s.l.). *Sciadopitys* of Japan, formerly in Taxodiaceae, is only distantly related to the other genera and forms its own family, Sciadopityaceae.
- Podocarpaceae, Cephalotaxaceae and Taxaceae do not form one lineage: Cephalotaxaceae and Taxaceae are **sister groups** and are merged into Taxaceae in the broad sense, which belongs with Cupressaceae; Podocarpaceae is the sister group of Araucariaceae. "Taxopsida" is therefore invalid, and the presence of cones changed several times within the conifers.
- The conifers fall into two branches: Pinaceae, and the "cupressophytes" (Cupressophyta, also called conifer II) comprising the remaining families.

On this basis Christenhusz et al. (2011, *Phytotaxa* 19: 55–70) gave the living gymnosperms a linear classification into 4 subclasses, 8 orders, 12 families and 83 genera, treating them as one group:

| Subclass | Order | Family | Genera/species (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cycadidae | Cycadales | Cycadaceae | 1/120 |
| | | Zamiaceae | 9/250 |
| Ginkgoidae | Ginkgoales | Ginkgoaceae | 1/1 |
| Gnetidae | Welwitschiales | Welwitschiaceae | 1/1 |
| | Gnetales | Gnetaceae | 1/40 |
| | Ephedrales | Ephedraceae | 1/70 |
| Pinidae | Pinales | Pinaceae | 11/230 |

Species numbers are current approximations (cycads after The World List of Cycads, the rest after POWO and the counts of Christenhusz et al.) and differ considerably between sources. The sections of this chapter follow this order; a simplified teaching version with four orders, Cycadales, Ginkgoales, Coniferales and Gnetales, is also used.

### 26.1.3. The Position of the Gnetophytes

Only the gnetophytes remain uncertain in the relationships among the subclasses. All analyses agree that the cycads and ginkgo form one lineage at the base of the living gymnosperms. The gnetophytes may be the **sister group** of all conifers (the gnetifer hypothesis), or nested within the conifers as the sister group of Pinaceae (the gnepine hypothesis), or even the sister group of the cupressophytes. Ran et al. (2018) used a phylogenomic analysis of many nuclear genes to support the gnepine hypothesis and concluded that the similarities between gnetophytes and angiosperms (vessels, a perianth-like envelope, loss of archegonia) are partly convergent or homoplastic; but genes and methods conflict clearly with one another, and the problem is not fully resolved. Christenhusz et al. kept the gnetophytes as a separate subclass, a cautious treatment that does not depend on any particular hypothesis. If the gnepine hypothesis is correct, the "conifers" are a **paraphyletic group**.

## 26.2. Cycads

### 26.2.1. Characters of Cycadales

The cycads are the most primitive group of living seed plants. They arose in the Permian and were at their height in the Jurassic (together with the similar-looking bennettitaleans, which is why the Mesozoic is often called the "Age of Cycads"), and declined afterwards; about 370 species survive in the tropics and subtropics.

- Evergreen woody plants with stout stems that are often unbranched ("pachycaulous"), a very thick pith and cortex and only a narrow ring of xylem; the cambium is active only briefly, and some species undergo anomalous secondary growth through successive cambial rings arising in the cortex; leaf bases persist, so the stem surface is covered with leaf scars.
- Leaves are spirally arranged and of two kinds: small scale leaves densely covered with brown felt, and large once-pinnate (rarely bipinnate) foliage leaves, borne in alternating whorls; the foliage leaves are crowded at the top of the stem, stiff and leathery; the young leaves and leaflets of *Cycas* are circinate, as in ferns.
- Lateral roots include upward-growing, repeatedly dichotomous **coralloid roots**, whose cortex contains a ring of symbiotic **cyanobacteria** (mainly *Nostoc*, and *Anabaena* has also been reported) that fix nitrogen. (Chinese textbooks call these "mycorrhizae", which is inaccurate: cycads also have arbuscular **mycorrhizae**, whereas coralloid roots are a symbiosis with cyanobacteria; see [7.11.31](/7.11.31).)
- The plants are **dioecious**; the pollen cone is large and made of many microsporophylls with many sori of microsporangia on the lower side; the sperm are multiflagellate ([7.12.25](/7.12.25#sec-25.5.1)).
- Many cycads are pollinated by beetles (weevils and others) or thrips, with the pollen cones heating up and giving off odors that attract the insects, an ancient example of insect pollination among seed plants.
- The seeds are large, with a fleshy, often red or orange outer seed coat and a hard middle layer; seeds and stem pith are rich in starch but contain toxins such as cycasin and must be soaked and fermented for a long time before they can be eaten.

### 26.2.2. Cycadaceae and Zamiaceae

| | Cycadaceae | Zamiaceae |
|---|---|---|
| Genera | Only *Cycas* | 9 genera: *Zamia*, *Encephalartos*, *Dioon*, *Macrozamia*, *Ceratozamia*, *Stangeria* and others |
| Distribution | East Asia, Southeast Asia, Australia, Madagascar and East Africa | Americas, Africa, Australia |
| Leaflets | With a midrib, no lateral veins (except *Stangeria*) | Mostly parallel-veined, no midrib |
| Megasporophylls | Leaf-like, pinnately divided above, with 2–8 ovules on both sides below, tufted at the top of the stem, forming no seed cone | Peltate, two ovules each, aggregated into seed cones |

*Cycas* has about 120 species and is the largest genus of cycads and the only one in China (Flora of China lists 16 species, and several new species have been described since), occurring from the southwest to the southeast; it is found in Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Yunnan, Sichuan, Guizhou and Taiwan. The familiar sago palm (*C. revoluta*) is native to Fujian, Taiwan and southern Japan, is widely cultivated, and is grown as a pot plant in the north. The Panzhihua cycad (*C. panzhihuaensis*) was described only in 1981; it occurs in the hot dry valleys of the Jinsha River at Panzhihua in Sichuan and northern Yunnan, is one of the northernmost cycads, and forms extensive natural stands; the Panzhihua Cycad National Nature Reserve was established to protect it.

## 26.3. Ginkgo

### 26.3.1. Ginkgoaceae

Ginkgoaceae has a single genus with a single species, ginkgo (*Ginkgo biloba*, also called maidenhair tree).

- A deciduous tree up to 40 m tall; the branches are differentiated into long shoots and short shoots. Long shoots (vegetative) grow fast, about 0.5 m a year, with spirally scattered leaves; short shoots (reproductive) grow extremely slowly, only a few centimeters in several years, with leaves clustered at the tip, and the cones are also borne on short shoots. The anatomy of the two kinds of shoots differs: long shoots have a small pith, thin cortex and thick xylem, short shoots the opposite.
- The leaf is fan-shaped with a long petiole and repeatedly dichotomous venation with no midrib; leaves on long shoots are often divided into two lobes in the middle (the epithet *biloba* means "two-lobed"), those on short shoots mostly wavy-margined. The autumn color is golden yellow.
- All organs have secretory cavities; the xylem consists of tracheids and shows clear annual rings.
- The plants are **dioecious**. The pollen cone is catkin-like; the seed cone has a long stalk with two collars at the end, each bearing one orthotropous ovule ([7.12.25](/7.12.25#sec-25.5.2)).
- The seed is drupe-like, with a fleshy, foul-smelling outer seed coat, a bony middle seed coat and a membranous inner seed coat; the endosperm (female gametophyte) is abundant, there are two cotyledons, and germination is hypogeal.

Chinese textbooks list differences between the vegetative bodies of female and male trees (male trees with a narrower crown, long branches ascending, leaves deeply lobed, and no resin cavities in a cross section of the petiole; female trees with a broad crown and spreading or drooping long branches, and so on). These differences are only statistical, and the sexes can in practice be told apart only at flowering; in recent years seedling sex identification based on sex-linked DNA markers has been developed. Male trees are mostly chosen as street trees, so that the fleshy outer seed coats do not fall and smell on the ground.

### 26.3.2. Living Fossils and Wild Populations

**Definition 26.3.1 (Living Fossil)** An organism that appeared very early in the fossil record, has changed little in form over a long time, and now survives as only a few species with a narrow distribution is called a **living fossil**. In botany the term is often used interchangeably with **relict plant**, a plant that was once abundant and widespread in geological history, but later mostly became extinct and survives only in restricted regions.

Ginkgo is the best-known living fossil. Ginkgoales appeared as early as the Early Permian (for example *Trichopitys*), were diverse in the Mesozoic and spread over the Northern Hemisphere, and the Jurassic *Ginkgo yimaensis* (Yima, Henan, about 170 Ma) already has ovules close to those of modern ginkgo. The group gradually declined in the Cenozoic, disappeared in Europe and North America after the Late Miocene, and survived after the Quaternary glaciations only in China. Fossil ginkgos had 5–6 pollen sacs per microsporophyll and more ovules, so the reproductive structures of modern ginkgo are the result of "simplification". "Living fossil" is a description and does not mean that form is completely static: the ginkgo genome has undergone much change during its long evolution ([7.10.32](/7.10.32)).

Whether truly wild populations of ginkgo still exist has long been disputed. Chinese textbooks state that "only West Tianmu Mountain in Zhejiang has trees in the wild state", but the ginkgos of Tianmu Mountain mostly grow near temples and show low genetic diversity, and are probably semi-wild or escaped from ancient cultivation. Zhao et al. (2019) resequenced the whole genomes of 545 ginkgo trees and concluded that southwestern China (Jinfo Mountain in Chongqing and the Wuchuan area of Guizhou), southern China (the Nanling) and eastern China (Tianmu Mountain) may each have held a glacial refugium, with the southwestern population the most genetically diverse; but the effect of human cultivation is hard to exclude completely. Wild ginkgo populations are listed as first-class nationally protected wild plants.

Ginkgo wood is of high quality; the kernel (white nut) is eaten and used in medicine, but contains the toxin ginkgotoxin (4′-O-methylpyridoxine), and eating it raw or in excess easily causes poisoning, especially in children; extracts of the leaves contain ginkgolides and flavonoids and have been developed into drugs and health products. Ginkgo is also a common ornamental tree throughout the world, highly resistant to pollution and pests.

## 26.4. Gnetophytes

The gnetophytes (subclass Gnetidae) have only three families and three genera, which differ greatly in form, but all have **vessels** in the secondary xylem, opposite or whorled leaves and jointed stems, cones with envelopes, integuments extended into a micropylar tube, and nonflagellate sperm ([7.12.25](/7.12.25#sec-25.6)). They have no resin canals.

### 26.4.1. Ephedraceae

Ephedraceae has a single genus, *Ephedra*, with about 70 species in the arid regions and deserts of Asia, southeastern Europe, North Africa and the Americas; China has 14 species (Flora of China), mostly in the northwest, Inner Mongolia and the southwestern plateau.

- Shrubs, subshrubs or herb-like plants, much branched, with opposite or whorled green jointed twigs with fine longitudinal grooves in the internodes, and the pith in cross section often reddish brown; they look much like horsetails but are woody and have vessels in the secondary xylem.
- The leaves are reduced to membranous scales, 2–3 opposite or whorled and fused at the base into a sheath, and photosynthesis is carried out mainly by the green young stems.
- The plants are dioecious, rarely monoecious; at maturity the bracts of the seed cone become red and fleshy and enclose the seed, giving a berry-like structure.

The stems of *Ephedra* contain **alkaloids** such as ephedrine and pseudoephedrine, and ephedrine was isolated from *Ephedra* by Nagayoshi Nagai of Japan in 1885. Chinese ephedra (*E. sinica*), *E. intermedia* and *E. equisetina* are the sources of ephedra (ma huang) listed in the Chinese pharmacopoeia, used against asthma and to induce sweating; ephedrine is also a precursor in the synthesis of methamphetamine, so the production and circulation of ephedra herb and ephedrine are strictly controlled. *Ephedra* grows in deserts and sandy land and helps fix sand.

### 26.4.2. Gnetaceae

Gnetaceae has a single genus, *Gnetum*, with about 40 species in the tropical rain forests of Asia, Africa and South America, mostly large evergreen woody lianas, with a few shrubs or small trees (such as the melinjo, *G. gnemon*, cultivated in Southeast Asia). The stem nodes are swollen, the vessels of the secondary xylem are wide and the rays broad; the leaves are simple, opposite and elliptical, with pinnate lateral veins and reticulate veinlets, very like those of a dicotyledon. The plants are dioecious, the cones form slender spikes with several cup-shaped involucres, and the seeds are drupe-like and enclosed in a red fleshy **aril**. China has 7–9 species, in Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, Yunnan and elsewhere, the common ones being *G. montanum* and *G. parvifolium*; the bark fibers are used to make sacks and fishing nets, and the seeds can be roasted for eating or pressed for oil.

### 26.4.3. Welwitschiaceae

Welwitschiaceae has a single species, *Welwitschia mirabilis* (*W. bainesii*, the name used in Chinese textbooks, is a synonym), found near the coast of the Namib Desert in southwestern Africa (Namibia and Angola). The stem is short, thick and tuberous, buried in the ground, with a very deep taproot; after the cotyledons fall the plant has only one pair of strap-shaped leaves throughout its life, up to several meters long, growing continuously from a meristem at the leaf base while the tips split and fray in the wind and sand, so that it looks like many leaves. Large individuals are more than a thousand years old by radiocarbon dating (the Chinese textbook statement that it "can live for more than a hundred years" is far too conservative). *Welwitschia* obtains water from coastal fog and is a typical xerophyte; the cones are bright red, the plants dioecious, and pollination is mainly by insects.

## 26.5. Pinaceae

### 26.5.1. Characters of Pinaceae

Pinaceae is the largest and economically most important family of conifers, with about 11 genera and 230 species, almost all in the Northern Hemisphere, from subtropical mountains to the arctic tree line and from sea level to 4800 m; the boreal coniferous forests consist mainly of larch, spruce, fir and pine of Pinaceae. China has 10 genera and about 110 species (Flora of China; whether *Nothotsuga* is recognized accounts for the difference between 10 and 11 genera), distributed throughout the country, mostly forest and timber trees, with many endemic genera and species and relict species.

- Trees, rarely shrubs, mostly evergreen (*Larix* and *Pseudolarix* are deciduous); branches whorled, often with long and short shoots; resin canals in wood and leaves, with the bark and leaves often aromatic.
- Leaves linear or needle-shaped: linear leaves are flat, rarely four-angled, spirally scattered on long shoots and clustered on short shoots; needle leaves are in fascicles of 2–5 borne at the tips of extremely reduced short shoots, with a basal sheath (*Pinus*).
- The plants are monoecious; the microsporophylls of the pollen cone are spirally arranged, each with 2 pollen sacs, and the pollen mostly has two air sacs (without or with reduced air sacs in *Larix*, *Pseudolarix*, *Pseudotsuga*, *Tsuga* and others).
- The seed cone is made of many spirally arranged bract–ovuliferous scale complexes, with the bract and ovuliferous scales free (joined only at the base) and two inverted ovules at the base of the adaxial surface of each ovuliferous scale; after fertilization the ovuliferous scale develops into a woody or leathery seed scale, and the **cone** is erect or pendent and ripens in the same year or the second year.
- The seed usually has a long wing at the top formed from the epidermal tissue of the seed scale; there are 2–18 cotyledons. The chromosome base number is mostly $x = 12$.

The diagnostic features of Pinaceae are therefore needle-shaped or linear leaves, leaves and seed scales spirally arranged, bract and seed scales free, and two winged seeds per seed scale. Inverted ovules, the long wing at the top of the seed, a particular mode of proembryo development and proteinaceous plastids in the sieve elements are synapomorphies of Pinaceae, and molecular phylogenies place Pinaceae as the sister group of the remaining conifers (the cupressophytes), or in one branch with the gnetophytes (§26.1.3).

### 26.5.2. The Two Subfamilies and the Genera

Morphological and molecular evidence divides Pinaceae into two subfamilies: Abietoideae (*Cedrus*, *Abies*, *Keteleeria*, *Pseudolarix*, *Tsuga*, *Nothotsuga*) and Pinoideae (*Pseudotsuga*, *Larix*, *Cathaya*, *Picea*, *Pinus*). Pinoideae has no resin canals in the seed coat and two resin canals in the vascular cylinder of the young root; *Cedrus* lies at the base of Abietoideae.

There is also the endemic *Nothotsuga longibracteata*, with projecting bract scales and erect cones, intermediate between *Tsuga* and *Keteleeria*. *Cathaya* was discovered in 1955 by Zhong Jixin and colleagues at Huaping, Longsheng, Guangxi, and described in 1958 by Chen Huanyong and Kuang Keren; because it resembles a Tertiary European fossil genus it is called a living fossil.

### 26.5.3. *Pinus*

*Pinus* has about 110 species, is among the largest genera of gymnosperms, and is widespread in the Northern Hemisphere; China has more than 20 species, which are the main forest trees. They are evergreen trees, rarely shrubs, with conspicuous winter buds. The leaves are of two kinds: the scale leaves (primary leaves) are spirally arranged, flat and linear in seedlings and later reduced to membranous bract-like scales; the needles (secondary leaves) are in fascicles of 2, 3 or 5 (in a few species 1 or 6–8) with a basal sheath. The pollen cones are numerous and clustered at the base of the new shoot; the seed cones are single or 2–4 near the tip of the new shoot; and the cones ripen in autumn of the second year.

*Pinus* is divided into two subgenera, distinguished first by the number of vascular bundles in the needle:

| | Subgenus *Strobus* (soft or white pines) | Subgenus *Pinus* (hard or yellow pines) |
|---|---|---|
| Vascular bundles in the needle | 1 | 2 |
| Sheath | Deciduous | Persistent |
| Scale leaves at the needle base | Not decurrent | Decurrent |
| Wood | Softer, pale, less resinous | Harder, darker, more resinous |
| Umbo | Mostly terminal, unarmed (dorsal and spined in *P. bungeana*) | Dorsal, often spined |
| Representative species | Korean pine *P. koraiensis* (5 needles, northeast), Chinese white pine *P. armandii* (5 needles), lacebark pine *P. bungeana* (3 needles, endemic, the bark of old trees flaking off in white patches), Himalayan blue pine *P. wallichiana* (5 needles, Himalaya) | Chinese pine *P. tabuliformis* (2 needles, endemic, north China), Masson's pine *P. massoniana* (2, rarely 3 needles, south of the Yangtze), Taiwan pine *P. taiwanensis* (2 needles), Mongolian Scots pine *P. sylvestris* var. *mongholica* (2 needles, northeast and the sandy lands of Inner Mongolia), Japanese black pine *P. thunbergii* (2 needles, native to Japan and Korea), Yunnan pine *P. yunnanensis* (3, rarely 2 needles), loblolly pine *P. taeda* and slash pine *P. elliottii* (introduced from North America) |

The number of needles per fascicle varies within both subgenera (three-needled species include both *P. bungeana* and *P. yunnanensis* and *P. taeda*), so identification must consider vascular bundles, sheath and umbo together. The shape of the needle in cross section and the number and position of the resin canals (marginal, medial or internal) are also important characters for separating species: the needle of a two-needled fascicle is semicircular in cross section, of a three-needled one a fan-shaped triangle, of a five-needled one triangular.

A fascicle taken together forms a cylinder, so each needle of a two-needled fascicle is semicircular in cross section, of a three-needled one a 120° sector, and of a five-needled one a 72° sector. Inside the hypodermis is the mesophyll with resin canals, and inside the endodermis are the transfusion tissue and 1 (subgenus *Strobus*) or 2 (subgenus *Pinus*) vascular bundles. *P. tabuliformis* and *P. massoniana* have two needles, two bundles and marginal resin canals; *P. bungeana* has three needles and one bundle; *P. koraiensis* has five needles, one bundle and medial resin canals.](./plots/pine-needles.py)

Common Pinaceae in the Beijing area are Chinese pine (the main conifer and ornamental tree of the Beijing mountains), lacebark pine (a famous tree of classical gardens, such as the "White-Robed General" at Tuancheng in Beihai Park), Chinese white pine, Himalayan blue pine (cultivated), *Picea meyeri* and *P. wilsonii*, deodar, and *Larix gmelinii* var. *principis-rupprechtii* (on higher mountains).

*Pinus* has the greatest economic value: the wood is used for construction, railway sleepers, mine props and papermaking; resin is tapped from the trunk and distilled into turpentine, the residue being rosin, and China is the world's largest producer of rosin, mainly from Masson's pine and slash pine; the large seeds (pine nuts) of Korean pine, Chinese white pine and others are edible; and pine needles, pollen and knots are used in medicine.

## 26.6. Araucariales

### 26.6.1. Araucariaceae

Araucariaceae has 3 genera and about 40 species, mainly in the Southern Hemisphere (South America, Australia, New Zealand, New Caledonia and Southeast Asia), and was widespread in both hemispheres in the Mesozoic. They are large evergreen trees with branches often whorled; the leaves are spirally arranged, awl-shaped, scale-like or broadly lanceolate; the plants are dioecious or monoecious; bract and ovuliferous scales are fused (in *Agathis* almost only the bract scale remains), and each seed scale bears only 1 seed; the cones are large and the seed scales fall from the axis at maturity; the pollen has no air sacs. The structure of the wood (multiseriate bordered pits on the tracheids, crowded against each other into polygons) resembles that of the Paleozoic cordaites.

- *Araucaria* (about 20 species): hoop pine (*A. cunninghamii*, native to eastern Australia) and Norfolk Island pine (*A. heterophylla*, native to Norfolk Island) are often cultivated as garden trees in southern China and as pot plants in the north; the seeds of the monkey puzzle (*A. araucana*) of Chile are edible.
- *Agathis* (about 20 species): the leaves are broad with many parallel veins; the kauri of New Zealand (*A. australis*) is a huge timber tree whose resin (kauri gum) was once mined in large quantities.
- *Wollemia*: only the Wollemi pine (*W. nobilis*), discovered in 1994 in a gorge in Wollemi National Park west of Sydney, Australia; fewer than 100 wild adult individuals exist, and until then only fossils of its relatives were known, making it another "living fossil".

### 26.6.2. Podocarpaceae

Podocarpaceae has about 19 genera and 190 species, mainly in the Southern Hemisphere and tropical mountains, and is the largest conifer family of the Southern Hemisphere. They are evergreen trees or shrubs; the leaves are linear, lanceolate or scale-like, rarely flat and broad (*Nageia*) or reduced to phylloclades; the plants are mostly dioecious. The seed cone is reduced, with only one or a few fertile ovuliferous scales, each enclosing one inverted ovule; this modified ovuliferous scale is called the *epimatium*, and at seed maturity it becomes the fleshy or leathery outer layer. In many genera the bracts fuse with the axis and swell into a fleshy, brightly colored receptacle that attracts birds to eat and disperse the seeds. The microsporophylls have two pollen sacs and the pollen usually has air sacs.

China has 4 genera and 12 species (Flora of China), south of the Yangtze:

- *Podocarpus* (about 100 species): *P. macrophyllus* (Buddhist pine) has linear-lanceolate leaves with a conspicuously raised midrib; the seed is ovoid and purple when ripe, and the fleshy receptacle below it purplish red, resembling a luohan (arhat) in a robe, whence the name; it is a common garden and bonsai tree. *P. neriifolius* also occurs.
- *Nageia*: *N. nagi*, with opposite, ovate leaves with many fine parallel veins and no midrib, like bamboo leaves; the wood is of good quality and the seeds can be pressed for oil. Chinese textbooks place it in *Podocarpus* (*Podocarpus nagi*).
- *Dacrydium*: *D. pectinatum*, from Hainan, in which the epimatium is free of the integument, the seed is nut-like and surrounded only at the base by a cup-shaped aril, and the bracts do not develop into a fleshy receptacle. The name *D. pierrei* used in Chinese textbooks is a synonym.
- *Dacrycarpus*: *D. imbricatus*, with two kinds of leaves, awl-like linear leaves in two ranks and scale-like leaves overlapping like tiles, in Hainan, Guangxi and Yunnan. Chinese textbooks place it in *Podocarpus* (*Podocarpus imbricatus*).

## 26.7. Cupressales

### 26.7.1. Sciadopityaceae

Sciadopityaceae has a single species, the Japanese umbrella pine (*Sciadopitys verticillata*), endemic to Japan, with fossils from the Mesozoic onward throughout the Northern Hemisphere. It is an evergreen tree; the long shoots bear membranous scale leaves, in whose axils the short shoots each bear a long, linear, doubly grooved "double needle", and many double needles are whorled at the shoot tip like the ribs of an umbrella; the double needle is thought to be formed by fusion of two needles, or to be a flattened short shoot (phylloclade). In the cone the bract and seed scales are largely fused, and each seed scale has 5–9 winged seeds. It was traditionally placed in Taxodiaceae, and molecular evidence shows it to be the sister group of Cupressaceae and Taxaceae together. The Japanese umbrella pine, cedar, *Araucaria*, golden larch and giant sequoia (or coast redwood) are often called the "five great garden trees of the world".

### 26.7.2. Cupressaceae

Cupressaceae in the broad sense (Cupressaceae s.l.) has about 30 genera and 160 species, distributed in both hemispheres, and includes the traditional Taxodiaceae and Cupressaceae in the narrow sense.

- Trees or shrubs; the leaves are scale-like, needle-like, linear or awl-shaped, spirally arranged (most genera of the former Taxodiaceae) or opposite or in whorls of three (former Cupressaceae), with two kinds of leaves often on one plant; leaf bases are mostly decurrent, and old leaves often fall together with the twigs.
- The plants are monoecious or dioecious; the microsporophylls have 2–9 pollen sacs (mostly 3–6 in Cupressaceae in the narrow sense) and the pollen has no air sacs.
- Bract and ovuliferous scales are semi-fused to completely fused, each seed scale having 1–9 (in some up to 20) erect or inverted seeds; the cones are woody or leathery and open when ripe, whereas in *Juniperus* the seed scales are fleshy and fused, giving a berry-like cone.
- The seeds have narrow wings on both sides or all around, or none. The chromosome base number is $x = 11$.

The degree of fusion of bract and seed scales forms a series in Cupressaceae (figure below), which is one of the morphological grounds for merging Taxodiaceae and Cupressaceae: in *Cunninghamia* the bract scale is large and the seed scale small and joined to the bract only at the base; in *Cryptomeria* only the toothed upper part of the seed scale is free; in *Metasequoia*, *Sequoia* and *Cupressus* the seed scale is peltate and completely fused with the bract, leaving only a small point in the middle; and in *Juniperus* they fuse into a fleshy "berry".

![The bract–seed scale complex of conifer cones (seen from one side of the axis, schematic). The bract scale (green) is a leaf, and the seed scale in its axil (brown) is a modified short shoot. In Pinaceae the two are free, each seed scale bearing two seeds winged at the tip; in Araucariaceae they are fused and there is only one seed. In Cupressaceae s.l. the degree of fusion increases from *Cunninghamia* (bract large, seed scale small and three-lobed, three seeds) and *Cryptomeria* (only the teeth at the tip of the seed scale free, 2–5 seeds) to *Taxodium*, *Sequoia* and *Cupressus* (peltate, completely fused); in *Platycladus* the seed scale is flat with a recurved hooked tip and the seeds are wingless; in *Juniperus* the seed scales are fleshy and fused into a berry-like cone.](./plots/bract-scale-fusion.py)

Molecular phylogenies divide Cupressaceae into 7 subfamilies (Gadek et al. 2000), in the following order:

| Subfamily | Main genera | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Cunninghamioideae | *Cunninghamia* | Leaves linear-lanceolate, toothed; bract large, seed scale small and three-lobed at the tip, 3 seeds per seed scale |
| Taiwanioideae | *Taiwania* | Leaves awl-shaped; cones small; *T. cryptomerioides* in Taiwan and the southwest |
| Athrotaxidoideae | *Athrotaxis* (Tasmania) | |
| Sequoioideae | *Sequoia*, *Sequoiadendron*, *Metasequoia* | Seed scales peltate; the coast redwood is the tallest tree in the world (over 115 m), the giant sequoia the largest by volume, living more than 3000 years |
| Taxodioideae | *Cryptomeria*, *Glyptostrobus*, *Taxodium* | Mostly in wetlands; *Taxodium* has knee-shaped pneumatophores |
| Callitroideae | *Callitris*, *Austrocedrus* and others | Southern Hemisphere |
| Cupressoideae | *Platycladus*, *Cupressus*, *Juniperus*, *Fokienia*, *Chamaecyparis*, *Thuja* and others | Leaves opposite or whorled, scale-like or needle-like, Northern Hemisphere |

The first five subfamilies are the traditional Taxodiaceae; they branch off successively outside Cupressoideae and Callitroideae, so Taxodiaceae is a **paraphyletic group**.

China has 5 genera and 7 species of the former Taxodiaceae, with another 4 genera and 7 species introduced:

- Chinese fir (*Cunninghamia lanceolata*): an evergreen tree with linear-lanceolate leaves with finely toothed margins, twisted at the base into two ranks on lateral branches, with stomatal lines on both sides; the cone is nearly spherical, the bract scale large with finely toothed margin, the seed scale small and three-lobed at the tip with three seeds on the adaxial side, and the seeds narrowly winged on both sides. It is endemic to China, widely cultivated south of the Qinling, and is the main planted timber tree of the south by area, fast-growing, with light, soft, decay-resistant and easily worked wood. *C. konishii* of Chinese textbooks is now mostly treated as a variety or synonym of Chinese fir.
- Japanese cedar (*Cryptomeria japonica* var. *sinensis*, *C. fortunei* in Chinese textbooks): the leaves are awl-shaped, spirally arranged and roughly in five ranks, curved inward at the tip; the cone is nearly spherical, the upper part of the seed scale with 4–5 teeth and 2 seeds per scale, in Tianmu Mountain in Zhejiang, Fujian and Jiangxi; the typical Japanese cedar has 2–5 seeds per scale.
- Chinese swamp cypress (*Glyptostrobus pensilis*): the leaves are of three kinds (scale leaves persistent, linear and linear-awl-shaped leaves falling together with the lateral short shoots); the cone is erect, the seed scales woody with 6–10 pointed teeth on the upper margin, and the seeds have a long downward wing. It was widespread in the Northern Hemisphere in the Cretaceous and Cenozoic and survived after the Quaternary glaciations only in southern China and northern Vietnam, with very few wild individuals; it is a first-class nationally protected wild plant. It grows by water, has swollen roots and light wood, and was used for life preservers and bottle corks.
- Bald cypress (*Taxodium distichum*) and pond cypress (*T. distichum* var. *imbricarium*, *T. ascendens* in Chinese textbooks): native to the southeastern United States, deciduous, the lateral twigs falling in winter together with the leaves; widely introduced in the water-network regions of the Yangtze basin.
- Dawn redwood (*Metasequoia glyptostroboides*): a deciduous tree whose twigs, leaves and seed scales are all opposite (which is again like Cupressoideae); the linear leaves are twisted at the base into two ranks and fall in winter together with the lateral twigs; the seed scales of the cone are peltate with 5–9 seeds per scale.
- Coast redwood (*Sequoia sempervirens*) and giant sequoia (*Sequoiadendron giganteum*): native to California, both monotypic genera, introduced in China.

The discovery of the dawn redwood was a major event in 20th-century botany. In 1941 the Japanese paleobotanist Shigeru Miki established the genus *Metasequoia* on fossils from Japan and elsewhere, and at that time regarded it as long extinct. In the early 1940s Chinese foresters found a huge deciduous taxodiaceous tree at Modaoxi, Lichuan, Hubei (then part of Wanxian, Sichuan); complete specimens were collected in 1946, and in 1948 Hu Xiansu and Zheng Wanjun named it *Metasequoia glyptostroboides*, showing that Miki's fossil genus still had a living species. The dawn redwood was widespread in North America and Eurasia in the Cretaceous and Cenozoic, and its wild distribution is now confined to the border of Hubei, Hunan and Chongqing, where the wild population is a first-class nationally protected wild plant; it has been introduced into more than fifty countries and is the famous "living fossil".

Cupressoideae has 8 genera and more than 40 species in China:

- Chinese arborvitae (*Platycladus orientalis*): the twigs bearing scale leaves are flat and held in one plane, spreading; the scale leaves are opposite; the cone ripens in the first year and opens when ripe, the seed scales woody and flat with a recurved hooked point near the top of the back, the seeds wingless. It is a monotypic genus endemic to China, found nearly throughout the country except Xinjiang and Qinghai, tolerates drought and poor soil, and is the main species for afforestation of barren northern hills and for classical gardens and tombs (the ancient cypresses of Beijing are mostly *Platycladus*).
- *Cupressus*: the twigs bearing scale leaves are four-angled or cylindrical and not arranged in one plane (except in *C. funebris*); the cone ripens in the second year, the seed scales are peltate in 4–8 pairs, and the seeds are angled and narrowly winged on both sides. *C. funebris* (Chinese weeping cypress, with drooping twigs, endemic to China), *C. duclouxiana*, *C. gigantea* (in the Yarlung Tsangpo valley, Tibet).
- *Juniperus*: the cone is fleshy, the seed scales fused and not opening when ripe, the seeds wingless. Chinese textbooks divide it into two genera: *Sabina* (leaves needle-like or scale-like or of both kinds) and *Juniperus* in the narrow sense (leaves all needle-like, in whorls of three, jointed at the base); molecular evidence shows that *Juniperus* in the narrow sense and *Sabina* are sister groups that together form a monophyletic group, and the current system merges them into *Juniperus* in the broad sense, with *Sabina* as a section within it. *J. chinensis* (Chinese juniper; young trees mostly with needle leaves, old trees with scale leaves and middle-aged trees with both), *J. formosana*.
- Fujian cypress (*Fokienia hodginsii*), Sawara cypress (*Chamaecyparis pisifera*) and hinoki cypress (*C. obtusa*, introduced), and *Thuja sutchuenensis* (Chengkou, Chongqing, once believed extinct and rediscovered in 1999).

The diagnostic features of Cupressoideae are leaves opposite or in whorls of three, scale-like or needle-like and often of two kinds; bract and seed scales completely fused; seed scales opposite or in whorls of 3–4; and ovules erect.

### 26.7.3. Taxaceae

Taxaceae in the broad sense (Taxaceae s.l.) has 6 genera and about 30 species, mainly in the Northern Hemisphere, and includes the traditional Taxaceae and Cephalotaxaceae.

- Evergreen trees or shrubs; the inner walls of the tracheids have spiral thickenings; the leaves are linear or lanceolate, spirally arranged or opposite, with a stomatal band on each side of the midrib on the lower surface.
- The plants are mostly dioecious; the microsporophylls have 3–9 pollen sacs (peltate) and the pollen has no air sacs.
- No cones are formed: the seed cone has only one ovule (a pair in *Cephalotaxus*), borne at the tip of a short shoot with a disc-like or funnel-shaped receptacle at its base.
- The seed is drupe-like or nut-like and partly or wholly enclosed in a fleshy, brightly colored **aril** (formed from the receptacle at the base of the ovule) and dispersed by birds.

The genera:

| Genus | Features | Representatives |
|---|---|---|
| *Taxus* (yew; about 11 species) | Leaves spirally arranged, with two pale yellow or pale gray-green stomatal bands on the lower surface and no resin canals; the seed nut-like, borne in a red cup-shaped fleshy aril with the upper part exposed | *T. wallichiana* var. *chinensis* (Chinese yew, *T. chinensis* in Chinese textbooks), *T. wallichiana* var. *mairei*, *T. cuspidata* (Japanese yew) |
| *Pseudotaxus* | Twigs nearly opposite or whorled, with two white stomatal bands on the lower leaf surface; aril white | *P. chienii*, a monotypic genus endemic to China |
| *Amentotaxus* | Leaves opposite, with resin canals; pollen cones many, forming spikes; seed almost completely enclosed in a bright red sac-like aril | *A. argotaenia* |
| *Torreya* | Leaves opposite, with a spiny tip and resin canals; seed ripening in the second year and wholly enclosed in the aril | *T. grandis* (Chinese torreya), whose cultivar "Xiangfei" is a famous dried nut (around Zhuji and Shaoxing, Zhejiang) |
| *Cephalotaxus* (plum yew; formerly Cephalotaxaceae) | Leaves in two ranks on lateral branches, with two white stomatal bands below; pollen cones clustered in heads; ovules paired in the axils of bracts, the seed ripening in the second year and wholly enclosed in a fleshy outer layer (traditionally called an aril, but now mostly regarded as a fleshy outer seed coat) | *C. fortunei*, *C. sinensis*, both endemic to China |
| *Austrotaxus* | Endemic to New Caledonia | |

The bark, twigs and leaves of *Taxus* contain paclitaxel (Taxol). Paclitaxel was isolated and its structure determined in 1971 by Wani, Wall and colleagues from the bark of the Pacific yew (*T. brevifolia*); it stabilizes microtubules and blocks mitosis, was approved in 1992 for ovarian cancer and has since been used for breast and lung cancer and others (mechanism in [7.3.14](/7.3.14)). Early extraction from the bark badly damaged wild resources, and it is now produced mainly by semisynthesis from precursors in the twigs and leaves of cultivated yews or by cell culture; all species of *Taxus* in China are first-class nationally protected wild plants. Plants of *Cephalotaxus* contain **alkaloids** such as harringtonine and homoharringtonine, which were used in China in the 1970s to treat leukemia, and homoharringtonine (omacetaxine) was approved in the United States in 2012 for chronic myeloid leukemia.

## 26.8. Gymnosperms of China

China is one of the richest countries in gymnosperms in the world: *Flora Reipublicae Popularis Sinicae* (1978) records 11 families, 41 genera and 236 species (including 1 family, 7 genera and 51 species introduced and cultivated), and many new species have been described since. In the current system, 8 families are native to China (Cycadaceae, Ginkgoaceae, Ephedraceae, Gnetaceae, Pinaceae, Podocarpaceae, Cupressaceae and Taxaceae), and Araucariaceae, Sciadopityaceae, Zamiaceae and others are introduced and cultivated; every family except Welwitschiaceae can be seen in China.

During the Quaternary glaciations many gymnosperms became extinct in Europe and North America, whereas the mountains of southwestern, central and southern China were little affected by the ice and became their refugia. China therefore has many endemic or nearly endemic relict genera and species: ginkgo, dawn redwood, Chinese swamp cypress, Cathaya, golden larch, *Pseudotaxus*, *Cunninghamia*, *Platycladus* and *Taiwania*, as well as rare species such as the Panzhihua cycad, *Abies beshanzuensis*, *Thuja sutchuenensis* and *Cupressus gigantea*. The List of Wild Plants under Special State Protection issued in 2021 places all species of *Cycas*, ginkgo (wild populations), *Cathaya*, *Abies beshanzuensis*, *Glyptostrobus*, dawn redwood (wild populations) and all species of *Taxus* in the first class of protection.

The main uses of Chinese gymnosperms are shown in the following table:

| Use | Main plants |
|---|---|
| Timber | Chinese fir, Masson's pine, Chinese pine, Yunnan pine, larch, Korean pine, spruce, fir, *Cupressus funebris* (construction, furniture, papermaking); gymnosperm wood is often called "softwood" |
| Resin | Masson's pine, slash pine and others are tapped, distilled to turpentine and rosin, used in paints, inks, adhesives and paper sizing |
| Food | Pine nuts of Korean pine and Chinese white pine, torreya nuts, ginkgo kernels (white nuts), the "ephedra fruit" of *Ephedra*, *Gnetum* seeds |
| Medicine | Paclitaxel (*Taxus*), harringtonines (*Cephalotaxus*), ephedrine (*Ephedra*), ginkgo leaf extracts; pine pollen |
| Ornamental | Deodar, golden larch, Japanese umbrella pine, *Araucaria*, Chinese juniper, Chinese arborvitae, lacebark pine, Chinese pine, Buddhist pine, sago palm, ginkgo, dawn redwood |
| Ecology | Dominants of boreal and subalpine coniferous forests, water conservation; Mongolian Scots pine, Chinese arborvitae and *Ephedra* for sand fixation and afforestation of barren hills |

A rule of thumb for the pine, "taxodiaceous" and cypress groups (the former three families): in Pinaceae the leaves are spiral, the seed scales are free from the bract scales, and there are 2 seeds per scale; in the former Taxodiaceae the leaves are mostly spiral (opposite in *Metasequoia*), the scales are semi-fused, and there are 2–9 seeds per scale; in Cupressoideae the leaves are opposite or whorled, the scales completely fused, and there are 1 to many seeds per scale.

## Questions

1. Why does the current system merge Taxodiaceae into Cupressaceae and Cephalotaxaceae into Taxaceae, and abolish "Taxopsida"?

   **Answer** Molecular phylogenies show that Cupressaceae in the narrow sense is nested among the genera of the traditional Taxodiaceae (Cunninghamioideae, Taiwanioideae and the others branch off successively), so Taxodiaceae is paraphyletic and becomes monophyletic only when merged with Cupressaceae; *Cephalotaxus* is the sister group of the remaining genera of Taxaceae, and the merged Taxaceae is monophyletic. Podocarpaceae is most closely related to Araucariaceae and does not form one lineage with Taxaceae, so Taxopsida, which was based on "no cones and a fleshy outer seed layer", is polyphyletic and cannot stand. The continuous series of fusion of bract and seed scales (*Cunninghamia*, *Cryptomeria*, *Metasequoia*, *Cupressus*) also shows that there is no sharp line between the taxodiaceous and cypress groups.

2. How are the common genera of Pinaceae distinguished? Draft a concise key based on cones and leaves.

   **Answer** A possible key: 1. Needles in fascicles of 2–5 with a basal sheath, cone ripening in the second year, seed scale with apophysis and umbo ... *Pinus*; 1. Leaves single or clustered. 2. Leaves clustered on short shoots: 3. Evergreen, leaves stiff ... *Cedrus*; 3. Deciduous: 4. Seed scales of the cone persistent ... *Larix*; 4. Seed scales falling at maturity, leaves spreading radially into a disc ... *Pseudolarix*. 2. Leaves single on long shoots: 5. Cone erect: 6. Seed scales falling, leaf scar round ... *Abies*; 6. Seed scales persistent ... *Keteleeria*. 5. Cone pendent: 7. Bract scale three-lobed and projecting ... *Pseudotsuga*; 7. Bract scale not projecting: 8. Leaf four-angled, on a raised leaf cushion ... *Picea*; 8. Leaf flat with a short petiole ... *Tsuga* (with leaves crowded at the ends of internodes and axillary cones, *Cathaya*).

3. How do the two subgenera of *Pinus* differ? Why can the number of needles per fascicle not be used alone?

   **Answer** Subgenus *Strobus* has 1 vascular bundle in the needle, a deciduous sheath, non-decurrent scale leaves, softer wood and a mostly terminal umbo; subgenus *Pinus* has 2 vascular bundles, a persistent sheath, decurrent scale leaves, harder wood and a dorsal umbo. Needle number overlaps between the subgenera: all five-needled pines belong to subgenus *Strobus* and most two-needled ones to subgenus *Pinus*, but three-needled species include both *P. bungeana* (one bundle) and *P. yunnanensis* and *P. taeda* (two bundles), so the number of bundles and the sheath must be considered together.

4. Ginkgo and the dawn redwood are both called "living fossils". What is the biological significance of their discovery and study?

   **Answer** Both were widespread in the Northern Hemisphere in the Mesozoic or Cenozoic and survived after the Quaternary glaciations only in China. Ginkgo retains multiflagellate motile sperm (Hirase 1896), which proved that seed plants came from fern-like ancestors, and it is living material for studying the early reproduction of seed plants. The dawn redwood was first established as a genus from fossils by Miki (1941) and the living tree was found in Lichuan, Hubei, only in the 1940s, so that the morphology, ecology and anatomy of a fossil plant could be compared directly with a living one; it also shows that the mountains of southwestern and central China were an important refugium for Tertiary plants. Both remind us to protect the wild populations and genetic diversity of relict plants.

5. Which important drugs are provided by yews, plum yews and *Ephedra*? What should be borne in mind in using these resources?

   **Answer** *Taxus* contains paclitaxel, used against ovarian and breast cancer and others; *Cephalotaxus* contains harringtonine and homoharringtonine, used against leukemia; *Ephedra* contains ephedrine and pseudoephedrine, used against asthma and to induce sweating. Yews grow slowly and early stripping of bark for extraction badly damaged wild resources, so cultivated twigs and leaves should now be the raw material, with semisynthesis and cell culture developed, and wild *Taxus* is in the first class of national protection; ephedrine is a precursor of illicit drugs, so the harvest and circulation of *Ephedra* are strictly controlled; *Cephalotaxus* is mostly endemic species, and cultivation should likewise replace collecting from the wild.
