The Pentapetalae of the core eudicots divide into two great branches, the superrosids and the superasterids ([7.12.40](/7.12.40#sec-40.1.2)). The superrosids comprise Saxifragales and the rosids, and the rosids divide in turn into Vitales, the fabids and the malvids. This chapter treats the two orders that branch off first within the superrosids: Saxifragales and Vitales. Saxifragales are among the most heterogeneous orders of angiosperms. They range from tall sweetgums and witch hazels to alpine saxifrages, succulent stonecrops, submerged watermilfoils and the parasitic *Cynomorium*; in traditional systems their members were scattered among a dozen orders of the subclasses Hamamelidae, Rosidae and Dilleniidae, and only molecular evidence ties them together. The order is also the main destination of the breakup of the broadly defined Saxifragaceae. Vitales contain only Vitaceae, characterized by tendrils opposite the leaves, whose origin is a classic problem of homology in morphology. The orders of the fabids are treated in [7.12.42](/7.12.42) and [7.12.43](/7.12.43), and the malvids in [7.12.44](/7.12.44). For how floral formulas are written, see [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.6).

## 41.1. Circumscription of the Superrosids

**Definition 41.1.1 (Superrosids)** The monophyletic group formed by Saxifragales and the rosids (Vitales, fabids and malvids) is called the **superrosids**, with the phylogenetic name Superrosidae. Together with the superasterids and Dilleniales it belongs to the Pentapetalae.

The superrosids comprise about 18 orders and more than a quarter of angiosperm species. Like that of the core eudicots, their circumscription rests mainly on large-scale molecular data, and morphological synapomorphies are not obvious. The relationships among the three lineages within the superrosids, Saxifragales, Vitales and the eurosids (fabids + malvids), are still disputed: analyses of 17 genes support Saxifragales as sister to (Vitales + eurosids), the result adopted in APG IV, which therefore places Vitales within the rosids; some plastid-genome analyses instead find Vitales and Saxifragales as sister groups. Molecular clocks estimate that the superrosids and their main branches diversified rapidly within a window of about ten million years in the mid-Cretaceous, about 100 Ma ago, the period in which the angiosperms became dominant in terrestrial vegetation.

## 41.2. Saxifragales

### 41.2.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

In APG IV, Saxifragales have 15 families and about 2500 species: Peridiscaceae, Paeoniaceae, Altingiaceae, Hamamelidaceae, Cercidiphyllaceae, Daphniphyllaceae, Iteaceae, Grossulariaceae, Saxifragaceae, Crassulaceae, Aphanopetalaceae, Tetracarpaeaceae, Penthoraceae, Haloragaceae and Cynomoriaceae.

The order has few morphological synapomorphies; those often mentioned are flowers often 4–5-merous, carpels often 2 and fused at the base but free above, styles free and persistent, capsules or follicles often with a "beak" formed by the persistent styles, and seeds with abundant endosperm. The order falls roughly into two branches: a woody "hamamelid" branch (Paeoniaceae, Altingiaceae, Hamamelidaceae, Cercidiphyllaceae, Daphniphyllaceae) and a mostly herbaceous "saxifrage" branch (Saxifragaceae, Grossulariaceae, Iteaceae, Crassulaceae, Haloragaceae, Penthoraceae and others); the position of Paeoniaceae is unstable.

Changes in circumscription: in Cronquist's system, Hamamelidaceae and Cercidiphyllaceae belong to Hamamelidales of the Hamamelidae, and Daphniphyllaceae form their own order Daphniphyllales; Saxifragaceae, Grossulariaceae and Crassulaceae belong to Rosales of the Rosidae; Haloragaceae belong to Haloragales of the Rosidae; Paeoniaceae belong to Dilleniales of the Dilleniidae; Cynomoriaceae were placed near Balanophoraceae (Santalales) in Cronquist's system and their position was long uncertain, until analyses of mitochondrial genes placed them in Saxifragales, a treatment formally accepted in APG IV.

### 41.2.2. Paeoniaceae

Paeoniaceae contain only the genus *Paeonia*, with about 33 species in the north temperate zone; apart from 2 species in western North America all occur in Eurasia, and China has the most (about 15 species).

Diagnostic characters:

- Perennial herbs (section *Paeonia* and the North American section) or deciduous shrubs (the tree peonies, section *Moutan*, "woody peonies"); roots often thickened.
- Leaves alternate, twice-ternate or pinnately compound, without stipules.
- Flowers large and showy, solitary at the branch tips, bisexual, actinomorphic; sepals 5, persistent, often leaf-like, often subtended by leaf-like bracts; petals 5–10 (double in cultivars), red, purple, pink, yellow or white.
- Stamens numerous, centrifugal in development (the earliest to develop are innermost); disc fleshy, in the tree peonies forming a leathery sheath enclosing the carpels.
- Carpels 2–5 (rarely 8), free, thick and leathery, with broad, thick stigmas; ovules numerous in each carpel.
- Fruit a follicle; seeds large, black or red. Chromosome base number $x = 5$.

Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_{5\text{–}10} A_\infty \underline{G}_{2\text{–}5:1:\infty}$.

Representative plants:

- Tree peony (*P. suffruticosa*): a deciduous shrub with twice-ternate leaves and large flowers, with very many cultivars; it is a traditional famous flower endemic to China, with Luoyang and Heze as famous centers of cultivation; the root bark is used medicinally (*danpi*). The wild species of the section *Moutan* are all endemic to China, such as *P. rockii*, *P. ostii* and *P. ludlowii* (endemic to Tibet), and most are endangered. The seeds of *P. ostii* have a high oil content with a high proportion of α-linolenic acid, making it an emerging woody oil crop.
- Chinese peony (*P. lactiflora*): a perennial herb with stout roots; flowers white, pink or purple-red; it occurs from northeastern and northern China to the northwest and has a long history of cultivation; the root is used medicinally, peeled as *baishao* and unpeeled as *chishao*. The flowers of tree peony and herbaceous peony are similar and are distinguished by habit: the tree peony is woody and its aerial parts persist through winter; the herbaceous peony is herbaceous and its aerial parts die back in winter.

Changes in circumscription: *Paeonia* was long placed in Ranunculaceae (Bentham and Hooker, early editions of Engler, and many Chinese sources), but its persistent, leathery sepals, centrifugal stamens, disc, thick leathery carpels, broad stigmas and chromosome base number $x = 5$ distinguish it from Ranunculaceae in anatomy, cytology, palynology and serology, and since the 1950s it has mostly been separated as Paeoniaceae; Cronquist placed it with Dilleniaceae in the same order because of its centrifugal stamens. Molecular evidence shows that Paeoniaceae belong to Saxifragales, far from both Ranunculaceae and Dilleniaceae ([7.12.40](/7.12.40#sec-40.2.3)).

### 41.2.3. Altingiaceae and Hamamelidaceae

Altingiaceae have about 15 species; molecular evidence supports placing them all in one broadly defined *Liquidambar* (merging *Altingia* and *Semiliquidambar*; Ickert-Bond and Wen 2013), with an East Asian–North American disjunction, and more than ten species in China. They are deciduous or evergreen trees with aromatic resin; leaves alternate, palmately 3–5-lobed (sweetgum) or unlobed (*Altingia*), with stipules; flowers unisexual, monoecious, without a perianth; male flowers in heads or spikes with numerous stamens; female flowers in globose heads, ovary half-inferior, 2 carpels; the fruiting head is globose and composed of many woody capsules, each with persistent, spiny styles. Chinese sweetgum (*L. formosana*) is one of the commonest deciduous broadleaf trees south of the Yangtze, with palmately 3-lobed leaves that turn red in autumn; it is often confused with maples in the common name "maple," but maples have opposite leaves and double-winged fruits, whereas sweetgum has alternate leaves and a spiny, ball-like fruiting head; the resin (*baijiaoxiang*) is used medicinally, and the fruiting head is the drug *lulutong*. American sweetgum (*L. styraciflua*) is an important timber tree of eastern North America, and its resin (storax) was used in perfumery and chewing gum. Altingiaceae were formerly the subfamily Altingioideae of Hamamelidaceae; the APG system makes them a separate family, so in treating Hamamelidaceae one must say "excluding Altingiaceae."

Hamamelidaceae in the strict sense have about 27 genera and more than 100 species, mainly in the subtropics of East Asia and North America; China has more than half of the species and is the center of distribution of the family.

Diagnostic characters:

- Trees or shrubs; stellate hairs common.
- Leaves simple, alternate, rarely opposite, pinnately veined, entire or serrate, with stipules.
- Flowers bisexual or unisexual, in heads, spikes or racemes; the hypanthium more or less adnate to the ovary, sepals 4–5; petals 4–5, often ribbon-like and narrow, or absent; stamens 4–5 or numerous, anthers 2–4-locular, dehiscing longitudinally or by valves; staminodes often present.
- Ovary half-inferior or inferior, rarely superior, of 2 fused carpels, 2-locular, free in the upper part, styles 2, persistent; ovules 1 to many in each locule, pendulous.
- Capsule woody, loculicidal or septicidal, with two pointed beaks (persistent styles) at the top; exocarp and endocarp separate, and on drying and contracting they eject the seeds. Seeds with endosperm.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(4\text{–}5)} C_{4\text{–}5} A_{4\text{–}5} \overline{\underline{G}}_{(2:2:1\text{–}\infty)}$ (petals sometimes absent).

![Floral diagrams and formulas of Saxifragales and Vitales. A: *Paeonia*, sepals 5, petals 5–10, stamens numerous (centrifugal), a disc (dashed circle) surrounding 2–5 free carpels; B: *Hamamelis*, tetramerous, sepals fused and adnate to the ovary, 4 ribbon-like petals, 4 fertile stamens alternating with 4 scale-like staminodes, ovary half-inferior, 2 carpels, 1 ovule per locule; C: *Saxifraga*, perigynous flower (dashed line: floral cup), sepals 5, petals 5, stamens 10, 2 carpels fused in the lower part; D: *Sedum*, pentamerous, stamens 10 in two whorls, 5 free carpels; E: *Vitis*, sepals 5 (fused, minute), petals 5, stamens 5 opposite the petals, disc inside the stamens, ovary 2-locular with 2 ovules per locule](./plots/saxifragales-floral-diagrams.py)

Representative plants:

1. Chinese witch hazel (*Hamamelis mollis*): a deciduous shrub or small tree with obovate leaves densely stellate-tomentose beneath; it flowers in late winter to early spring before the leaves, with 4 yellow, ribbon-like petals about 1.5 cm long that curl like threads (hence the name); it occurs in the Yangtze basin and is grown as an ornamental. The American witch hazel (*H. virginiana*) of North America flowers in autumn, and an extract of its bark and leaves (witch hazel water) is used as an astringent.
2. Chinese fringe flower (*Loropetalum chinense*): an evergreen shrub or small tree with brown stellate hairs on the twigs and the underside of the leaves; leaves small, ovate, oblique at the base; flowers in heads with 4 white, ribbon-like petals; it occurs south of the middle and lower Yangtze, and the root, leaf and flower are used medicinally. The red-flowered fringe flower (*L. chinense* var. *rubrum*) has dark purple-red leaves and purple-red flowers and is one of the commonest ornamental shrubs and hedges in southern China.
3. *Distylium racemosum*: a small evergreen tree with thick, leathery, elliptic leaves; flowers in racemes, without petals, with red stamens; the leaves often bear galls formed by aphids, from which the insects emerge through a hole (hence the Chinese name "mosquito-mother tree"); it tolerates smoke and dust and is often used in southern China for hedges and street trees.
4. *Corylopsis*: deciduous shrubs with leaves like those of hazel; racemes pendulous, opening before the leaves, petals 5, yellow, spoon-shaped; *C. sinensis* occurs south of the Yangtze, with the root bark used medicinally, and is also grown as an ornamental.
5. Others: *Sinowilsonia henryi* (a monotypic genus endemic to China), *Disanthus cercidifolius* subsp. *longipes* (leaves like those of redbud), *Exbucklandia populnea*, *Rhodoleia championii* (the inflorescence resembles a single flower and is bird-pollinated), and *Parrotia subaequalis* (formerly *Shaniodendron subaequale*, endemic to China and critically endangered).

Key characters of the family: woody plants with stellate hairs. Leaves simple, alternate, with stipules. Hypanthium adnate to the ovary, ovary half-inferior to inferior, 2-locular, styles 2, persistent. Capsule woody, with 2 beaks.

Hamamelidaceae are the core of the Hamamelidae in Cronquist's system: the flowers of the Hamamelidae are mostly unisexual, with a single perianth whorl or none, wind-pollinated and in catkins, and Cronquist held that Hamamelidales evolved from Magnoliales and Trochodendrales in the direction of wind pollination, with Urticales, Juglandales, Fagales and other "amentiferous" orders derived in turn from Hamamelidales. Molecular evidence overturned this evolutionary sequence: Hamamelidaceae are in Saxifragales, Trochodendrales are among the early-diverging eudicots ([7.12.40](/7.12.40#sec-40.4.1)), and the "amentiferous" orders are scattered among Fagales and Rosales of the rosids ([7.12.43](/7.12.43)), each having adapted independently to wind pollination.

### 41.2.4. Cercidiphyllaceae and Daphniphyllaceae

Cercidiphyllaceae contain only the genus *Cercidiphyllum*, with 2 species in China and Japan. They are large deciduous trees with long shoots and short shoots; leaves opposite on long shoots and solitary on short shoots, cordate, palmately veined, turning yellow and red in autumn, and the fallen leaves have a caramel-like sweet fragrance (from maltol); flowers unisexual, dioecious, without a perianth, opening before the leaves; male flowers with numerous stamens, female flowers with 1 carpel (several female flowers are clustered and are often mistaken for a single flower with many carpels); fruit a follicle, seeds winged. Katsura tree (*C. japonicum*) occurs from central and western China to Japan and is a Tertiary relict and a second-class nationally protected wild plant in China.

Daphniphyllaceae contain only the genus *Daphniphyllum*, with about 30 species from East Asia to Southeast Asia. They are evergreen trees or shrubs; leaves alternate, often clustered at the branch tips, entire, with long, often reddish petioles; flowers unisexual, dioecious, without petals; ovary superior, 2-locular; fruit a drupe. In *D. macropodum* the old leaves fall only after the new leaves have grown out, so that old and new leaves "give way" to each other (hence the Chinese name "yielding tree"); *D. oldhamii* occurs south of the Yangtze. The whole genus contains structurally complex *Daphniphyllum* alkaloids. Cronquist placed Daphniphyllaceae in their own order Daphniphyllales of the Hamamelidae.

### 41.2.5. Grossulariaceae and Iteaceae

Grossulariaceae contain only the genus *Ribes*, with about 150 species in the north temperate zone and the South American Andes. They are shrubs, often spiny; leaves simple, alternate, palmately lobed; flowers bisexual or unisexual, in racemes; a hypanthium (floral cup) is present, sepals 4–5, often petaloid, petals 4–5, small; stamens 4–5, alternating with the petals; ovary inferior, 2 carpels, 1-locular, with parietal placentation; fruit a berry with persistent sepals at the top. Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_5 A_5 \overline{G}_{(2:1:\infty)}$. The berries of blackcurrant (*R. nigrum*), redcurrant (*R. rubrum*) and gooseberry (*R. uva-crispa*) are rich in vitamin C and are eaten fresh or made into juice and jam; *R. mandshuricum* occurs in northeastern and northern China. *Ribes* is the alternate host of white pine blister rust (*Cronartium ribicola*), and in North America wild currants were uprooted on a large scale to protect the eastern white pine. Grossulariaceae were formerly the subfamily Ribesioideae of the broadly defined Saxifragaceae.

Iteaceae include *Itea* and *Pterostemon* (Mexico). *Itea* are shrubs or small trees with alternate leaves with glandular teeth, long racemes of small white flowers, and capsules; *I. chinensis* occurs from southern to southwestern China, and Virginia sweetspire (*I. virginica*) is an ornamental shrub with bright red autumn leaves. *Itea* was formerly the subfamily Iteoideae of the broadly defined Saxifragaceae.

### 41.2.6. Saxifragaceae

Saxifragaceae in the strict sense have about 35 genera and 600 species, mainly in the north temperate zone and Arctic–alpine regions; China has about 27 genera and more than 400 species, with the Hengduan Mountains one of the centers of distribution.

Diagnostic characters:

- Perennial herbs, rarely annual or subshrubs; rhizomes or stolons often present.
- Leaves alternate or basal, simple, often shallowly palmately lobed, rarely pinnately or ternately compound (*Astilbe*); stipules absent, the petiole base often expanded into a sheath.
- Flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, rarely zygomorphic; often in cymes, panicles or racemes; sepals 4–5, petals 4–5, often clawed, rarely absent (*Chrysosplenium*); the floral cup (hypanthium) is distinct and the flowers are mostly perigynous.
- Stamens 5 or 10, equal to or twice the number of petals, inserted on the floral cup; an arrangement with the outer stamens opposite the petals (obdiplostemony) is common.
- Carpels usually 2, fused in the lower part and free above, styles 2, free; ovary superior, half-inferior or inferior; placentation axile or parietal, ovules numerous; a disc often present.
- Capsule with 2 beaks at the top (persistent styles), rarely a follicle; seeds small, numerous, with abundant endosperm.

Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{5+5} \overline{\underline{G}}_{(2:2:\infty)}$ (the position of the ovary varies within the family).

Representative plants:

1. *Saxifraga*: perennial herbs; about 450 species, about 220 in China, many of them cushion plants of alpine and Arctic regions; the genus name means "rock breaker," referring to their growth in rock crevices. Strawberry begonia (*S. stolonifera*) has slender red-purple stolons; leaves reniform, fleshy, often purple-red beneath with white veins; flowers zygomorphic, with 5 petals, the upper 3 small and spotted and the lower 2 long, pendulous and white; ovary half-inferior, 2-locular, styles 2; it occurs from eastern to southwestern China, is often grown as a ground cover and pot plant, and the whole plant is used medicinally.
2. *Astilbe chinensis*: a perennial herb with twice- to thrice-ternate leaves; panicles large with small purple-red or pink flowers; carpels 2, nearly free; it occurs from northeastern China to the Yangtze basin, the rhizome is used medicinally, and its horticultural cultivars are common border plants.
3. *Bergenia*: perennial evergreen herbs with stout rhizomes; leaves large, basal, leathery, obovate; flowers in cymes, pink to purple. *B. purpurascens* occurs in the alpine southwest, the whole plant is used medicinally, and it contains bergenin; *B. crassifolia* (heart-leaf bergenia) occurs from the Altai Mountains of Xinjiang to Siberia.
4. Others: *Chrysosplenium* (no petals, sepals yellow-green, in shady, moist places), *Rodgersia* (large palmately compound leaves), *Tiarella polyphylla*, *Heuchera* (North America, with many foliage cultivars), *Mitella* (petals pinnately dissected).

Key characters of the family: mostly herbs. Leaves often alternate, without stipules. Flowers 4–5-merous, perigynous, petals often clawed; stamens once or twice the number of petals; carpels 2, fused in the lower part, styles free. Capsule with 2 beaks.

Breakup of the broadly defined Saxifragaceae. In Engler's system Saxifragaceae were circumscribed very broadly, with about 80 genera and 1200 species and a dozen or so subfamilies, including the herbaceous saxifrages and also woody plants such as hydrangea, mock orange, *Deutzia*, currant and *Itea*; Takhtajan (1980) split the family into 10 families, and Cronquist kept the herbaceous genera in Saxifragaceae (about 40 genera and 700 species) and set up the woody genera as Grossulariaceae, Hydrangeaceae and others, but still within Rosales. Molecular evidence shows that even Cronquist's Saxifragaceae in the strict sense are not monophyletic; the members of the broadly defined Saxifragaceae are scattered among nearly 10 orders of the superrosids and the superasterids (see the figure below):

### 41.2.7. Crassulaceae

Crassulaceae have about 35 genera and 1400 species, distributed worldwide and most numerous in South Africa, Mexico and the Mediterranean–Himalayan region; China has about 13 genera and 230 species.

Diagnostic characters:

- Succulent herbs or subshrubs, rarely shrubs; plants often rosette-forming; often glaucous; vegetative propagation is strong, and many species form new plants from leaves, stem segments or bulbils.
- Leaves alternate, opposite or whorled, simple, succulent, entire or shallowly toothed, without stipules.
- Flowers bisexual, rarely unisexual, actinomorphic, 4–5-merous (*Kalanchoe* tetramerous, *Sempervivum* up to 20-merous), often in cymes.
- Sepals and petals 4–5 each, free or fused at the base; stamens twice the number of the petals, in two whorls, the outer opposite the petals (10 stamens when there are 5 petals), a few genera with the same number as the petals.
- Carpels as many as the petals, free or fused at the base, ovary superior; each carpel with a scale-like nectary on the outside at the base; ovules numerous.
- Follicles, leathery or membranous; seeds small, with little endosperm.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{4\text{–}5} C_{4\text{–}5} A_{4\text{–}5+4\text{–}5} \underline{G}_{4\text{–}5:1:\infty}$.

Crassulaceae are typical succulent **xerophytes** ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.2)): the parenchyma cells of the leaves are large with well-developed vacuoles, storing much water and organic acids; the epidermis has a thick cuticle and wax bloom, and the stomata are few and often sunken; in many species the stomata open at night to take up CO₂, which is stored in the vacuoles as malic acid, and close during the day, when the CO₂ is released for photosynthesis (see the figure below). This mode of photosynthesis was first studied in detail in Crassulaceae and is therefore called **crassulacean acid metabolism** (CAM); it arose independently in many unrelated families such as Cactaceae, Bromeliaceae and Orchidaceae, another example of **convergent evolution**. The biochemistry and regulation of CAM belong to plant physiology. Some species of Crassulaceae are obligate CAM plants, and others switch between C₃ and CAM with water conditions.

Representative plants:

1. *Sedum* (broad sense): about 400 species, mostly small succulent herbs with often yellow, pentamerous flowers. *S. sarmentosum* has creeping stems, leaves in whorls of 3, oblanceolate, and yellow flowers; it occurs throughout China, the whole plant is used medicinally, and it is a common plant for green roofs and ground cover; *S. lineare* has linear leaves in whorls of 3 and the same uses. Molecular evidence shows that *Sedum* in the broad sense is highly polyphyletic, and many species have been moved to other genera.
2. *Phedimus*: *P. aizoon* (formerly *Sedum aizoon*) has alternate, broad, toothed leaves and yellow cymes, occurs from northeastern China to the Yangtze basin, and the whole plant is a hemostatic; *P. kamtschaticus* occurs in northeastern and northern China.
3. *Hylotelephium*: *H. erythrostictum* and *H. spectabile* (showy stonecrop) have opposite or whorled, flat leaves, large corymbose cymes of pink or white flowers, and stamens longer than the petals, and are often grown as autumn border plants.
4. *Orostachys*: biennial or perennial; in the first year a rosette of leaves, and the whole plant dies after flowering (monocarpic); *O. fimbriata* has rosette leaves with a white cartilaginous spine at the tip and grows on tile crevices of roofs and on rocks throughout China.
5. *Kalanchoe*: flowers tetramerous, corolla fused into a tube. The mother of thousands or air plant (*K. pinnata*) forms rooted adventitious buds at the notches of the leaf margin that grow into new plants on landing; it is native to Madagascar in Africa and widely naturalized in the tropics; *K. daigremontiana* and flaming Katy (*K. blossfeldiana*) are common pot plants.
6. *Rhodiola*: perennial with stout rhizomes, often dioecious, in the alpine regions and Arctic of the Northern Hemisphere; the rhizomes of roseroot (*R. rosea*) and *R. crenulata* are used medicinally, contain salidroside, and have been developed as anti-fatigue health products, and wild resources have declined from overharvesting.
7. Others: *Crassula* (jade plant *C. ovata* and others, native to South Africa), *Echeveria* (native to Mexico, rosette-forming, one of the mainstays of what is called "succulents" in horticulture), *Sempervivum* (mountains of Europe).

Key characters of the family: succulent herbs with succulent leaves. Flowers regular, bisexual, 4–5-merous; floral parts free, stamens twice the number of petals; carpels free, with a scale-like nectary at the base. Follicles.

The free carpels and follicles of Crassulaceae resemble those of Ranunculaceae, and Cronquist and Chinese textbooks therefore held that they retain relatively primitive characters within Rosales, whereas the whorled, definite arrangement of floral parts is an advanced character. Phylogenetically they belong, together with Haloragaceae, Penthoraceae and others, to the mostly herbaceous branch of Saxifragales.

### 41.2.8. Haloragaceae, Penthoraceae and Cynomoriaceae

- Haloragaceae: about 8 genera and 150 species, herbs, terrestrial or aquatic; flowers small, unisexual or bisexual, wind-pollinated, 2–4-merous, ovary inferior; fruit nut-like or drupe-like. *Gonocarpus micranthus* (formerly *Haloragis micrantha*) is a slender small herb of barren hills and grassland; *Myriophyllum* are submerged herbs with whorled, pinnately dissected leaves and spikes held above the water, and Eurasian watermilfoil (*M. spicatum*) and *M. verticillatum* are common water plants of ponds and lakes, also used in water purification and aquarium decoration. Cronquist's Haloragales also included Gunneraceae (now in Gunnerales, [7.12.40](/7.12.40#sec-40.4.3)).
- Penthoraceae: only the genus *Penthorum*, with 2 species, one each in East Asia and North America. Perennial herbs of wet places; flowers small, pentamerous, carpels 5, fused in the lower part; capsule 5-valved, the valve tips beaked and arranged in a five-pointed star. *P. chinense* occurs from northeastern to southwestern China, and the whole plant is used medicinally.
- Cynomoriaceae: only the genus *Cynomorium*, with 1–2 species, in the Mediterranean region, Central Asia and the deserts of northwestern China. Perennial fleshy parasitic herbs without chlorophyll, parasitic on the roots of *Nitraria* (Nitrariaceae, [7.12.44](/7.12.44)) and other plants; the aerial part is a dark purple-red club-shaped inflorescence axis densely covered with minute unisexual flowers and scales. The fleshy stem of *C. songaricum* is used medicinally and is one of the medicinal plants most harvested in the northwestern deserts. *Cynomorium* is one example of an independent origin of parasitism in angiosperms ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.1)); its plastid genome is severely reduced, and its systematic position could be determined only from mitochondrial and nuclear genes.

## 41.3. Vitales

### 41.3.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Vitales contain only Vitaceae, which in APG IV include Leeaceae. In APG IV, Vitales are the sister group of the rest of the rosids (fabids + malvids) and therefore belong to the rosids. Cronquist placed Vitaceae with Rhamnaceae and Leeaceae in Rhamnales of the Rosidae, on the grounds that in both the stamens are opposite the petals and a disc is present; molecular evidence places Rhamnaceae in Rosales ([7.12.43](/7.12.43)), and the stamens opposite the petals are convergent.

The genome of Vitaceae holds a special place in comparative genomics: the grape (*Vitis vinifera*) was the first woody fruit crop to be sequenced (2007), and its genome retains the structure that followed the ancient whole-genome triplication shared by the core eudicots (the gamma event) without any further doubling, so it is often used as a "reference" for the genomic evolution of other eudicots.

### 41.3.2. Vitaceae

Vitaceae have about 16 genera and 950 species, mainly in the tropics to the temperate zone; China has 8 genera and about 150 species.

Diagnostic characters:

- Woody or herbaceous climbers, rarely erect shrubs or small trees (*Leea*); stems sympodial, the nodes often swollen; climbing by branched tendrils opposite the leaves.
- Leaves alternate, simple (often palmately lobed), palmately compound, pedately compound or pinnately compound, with stipules; the mesophyll often contains idioblasts with raphide bundles.
- Flowers small, bisexual or unisexual, actinomorphic, in cymes or panicles, the inflorescences also opposite the leaves (in the position of the tendrils).
- Calyx small, cup-shaped, with 4–5 shallow lobes or nearly entire; petals 4–5, valvate, free, or joined at the tip and falling as a single cap at anthesis (*Vitis*).
- Stamens 4–5, opposite the petals; a ring-shaped or lobed disc inside the stamens.
- Ovary superior, usually of 2 fused carpels, 2-locular, with 2 ovules per locule (*Leea* 3–6-locular with 1 ovule per locule).
- Berry; seeds 1–4, with a depression or ridge on the back formed by the chalaza and two grooves on the ventral side, endosperm abundant, often ruminate.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(4\text{–}5)} C_{4\text{–}5} A_{4\text{–}5} \underline{G}_{(2:2:2)}$.

Homology of tendril and inflorescence. Why are the tendrils of Vitaceae opposite the leaves? The stem of the grape is sympodial: at each internode the terminal bud develops into a tendril or inflorescence and is pushed to the side, while the bud in the leaf axil continues to grow upward as the next segment of the stem, so that tendrils and inflorescences both appear opposite the leaves. Tendrils and inflorescences are homologous organs: tendrils often fork midway, in the same branching pattern as the inflorescence; transitional types with a few flowers, half inflorescence and half tendril, are often found on one grape plant; in developmental genetics, the *LEAFY* homolog (*VFL*) that controls floral meristem identity is also expressed in the tendril primordium, and gibberellin treatment can convert tendrils into inflorescences. The tendrils of Vitaceae are therefore modified, sterile inflorescences (a kind of stem tendril) and are not homologous to the leaf tendrils of pea ([7.12.24](/7.12.24#sec-24.4.2)) or the tendrils of Cucurbitaceae ([7.12.43](/7.12.43)) (see the figure below). In *Parthenocissus*, the branch tips of the tendrils swell into adhesive discs after contact with a wall or bark and secrete a sticky substance that attaches them firmly, which is how the plants climb smooth walls.

Representative plants:

1. *Vitis*: woody climbers with bark shredding in long strips; pith brown, with a diaphragm at the nodes; tendrils forked, intermittently opposite the leaves (two of every three nodes bear tendrils); leaves simple, palmately 3–5-lobed; flowers in panicles; the petals joined at the tip and falling as a cap at anthesis; berry. About 70 species, about 37 in China. The grape (*V. vinifera*) is native to western Asia and southern Europe and was domesticated from the wild *V. vinifera* subsp. *sylvestris* about 10,000 years ago in the Caucasus and western Asia (Dong et al. 2023); it is one of the most widely grown fruit crops in the world, used fresh, dried and for wine, and was introduced to China through the Western Regions in the Han dynasty; in the course of domestication, the dioecy of wild grapes changed to the bisexual flowers of cultivars. The wild Amur grape (*V. amurensis*, from northeastern China, cold-hardy), *V. davidii* (with prickly stems), *V. flexuosa* and others are also used in China. American species such as riverbank grape (*V. riparia*) and sand grape (*V. rupestris*) resist phylloxera, and after the vineyards of Europe were devastated by phylloxera in the late 19th century, European grapes were restored by grafting them onto American rootstocks.
2. *Ampelopsis*: woody climbers with non-shedding bark and white pith; petals free and spreading; flowers in cymes. *A. glandulosa* has cordate, shallowly 3-lobed leaves and blue-purple, spotted berries; *A. aconitifolia* has palmate leaves with 5 pinnately deeply cut leaflets and occurs from northern to southwestern China.
3. *Parthenocissus*: tendrils short and much branched, the tips swollen into adhesive discs. Boston ivy (*P. tricuspidata*) has shallowly 3-lobed leaves, often ternate on short shoots, turning red in autumn; it is native to East Asia and is the most widely used plant for vertical greening; Virginia creeper (*P. quinquefolia*) has palmate leaves with 5 leaflets and is native to eastern North America.
4. *Causonis japonica* (formerly *Cayratia japonica*): a herbaceous climber with pedate leaves of 5 leaflets; inflorescences axillary, flowers tetramerous, disc orange-red; berry black; widespread on roadsides and waste ground throughout China.
5. *Cissus*: woody or herbaceous climbers with tetramerous flowers and stems often glaucous; *C. repens* occurs in southern China. Veldt grape (*C. quadrangularis*) has succulent, four-angled stems.
6. *Tetrastigma*: flowers tetramerous, stigma 4-lobed; the tuberous root of *T. hemsleyanum* is used medicinally (*jinxian diaohulu*); *T. planicaule* has flattened stems and occurs from southern to southwestern China. Species of *Tetrastigma* in Southeast Asia are the only hosts of *Rafflesia* ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)).
7. *Leea*: erect shrubs or small trees without tendrils, with pinnate leaves and a 3–6-locular ovary, in tropical Asia and Africa; formerly Leeaceae, now the most basal lineage of Vitaceae.

Key characters of the family: climbers; tendrils forked, opposite the leaves and homologous with the inflorescence; inflorescences also opposite the leaves. Flowers small, 4–5-merous, petals valvate; stamens opposite the petals, a disc present; ovary superior, 2-locular with 2 ovules per locule. Berry.

## Questions

1. Saxifragales put trees, alpine herbs, succulents, water plants and parasites into one order. How was this order established, and what limitation of morphological classification does it show?

   **Answer** Saxifragales first appeared as a consistently supported clade in analyses of gene sequences such as *rbcL*, and later large data sets from plastid, mitochondrial and nuclear genes repeated this result; a parasite with a degraded plastid genome such as *Cynomorium* was placed by mitochondrial and nuclear genes. Its members have almost no morphological synapomorphies, only some characters that occur widely among the core eudicots (flowers 4–5-merous, 2 carpels with free styles, beaked capsule). This shows that habit, the morphology of vegetative organs and "generalized" floral characters easily converge or retain ancestral conditions, so that groups circumscribed by them (the Hamamelidae, the broad Saxifragaceae) may not be monophyletic; delimiting higher taxa requires evidence independent of morphology.

2. What distinguishes tree peony from herbaceous peony? Why was *Paeonia* separated from Ranunculaceae?

   **Answer** The tree peony is a deciduous shrub (woody) whose aerial parts persist through winter; the herbaceous peony is a perennial herb whose aerial parts die back in winter; the flowers are similar, and the root bark or root of both is used medicinally (*danpi*; *baishao* and *chishao*). *Paeonia* differs from Ranunculaceae in having persistent, leathery sepals; centrifugal stamens; a fleshy or leathery disc; thick, leathery carpels with broad stigmas; a chromosome base number of $x = 5$; and different anatomical, palynological and serological characters. Molecular evidence further shows that it belongs to Saxifragales, far from Ranunculales.

3. Why are the tendrils of Vitaceae opposite the leaves? Are they homologous with the tendrils of pea and squash?

   **Answer** The grape stem is sympodial: at each segment the terminal meristem develops into a tendril or inflorescence and is pushed to the side, while the bud in the leaf axil continues to grow as the next segment of stem, so tendrils and inflorescences both appear opposite the leaves. The tendrils of grape are homologous with the inflorescence (same branching pattern, transitional types bearing flowers, expression of a *LEAFY* homolog in the tendril) and are modified sterile inflorescences. The tendrils of pea are leaf tendrils derived from the terminal leaflets of the compound leaf, and the tendrils of squash (Cucurbitaceae) arise at one side of the petiole base, their origin being disputed; all three serve climbing but are not homologous, being analogous organs.

4. How do we distinguish sweetgum from maple? To which orders do they belong?

   **Answer** Sweetgum (Altingiaceae, Saxifragales) has alternate, palmately 3-lobed leaves with stipules; the flowers are unisexual without a perianth, and the fruiting head is a spiny ball composed of many woody capsules. Maple (Sapindaceae, Sapindales) has opposite, palmately lobed leaves; the flowers have a perianth; the fruit consists of two winged mericarps (a double samara). Both turn red in autumn and are often lumped together in Chinese as "maple."

5. What structural features of Crassulaceae adapt them to drought? Why is crassulacean acid metabolism an example of convergent evolution?

   **Answer** Structurally, leaves and stems are succulent, the parenchyma cells are large with well-developed vacuoles that store water and organic acids; the cuticle is thick, often with a wax bloom, and the stomata are few and sunken; the plant is often rosette-forming, reducing the exposed surface; and vegetative propagation is strong. Physiologically, many species open their stomata at night, fix CO₂ as malic acid stored in the vacuoles, and close the stomata during the day while releasing the CO₂ for photosynthesis (crassulacean acid metabolism), reducing transpirational water loss. CAM arose independently in many unrelated families, such as Crassulaceae, Cactaceae, Bromeliaceae, Orchidaceae and Euphorbiaceae, which belong to widely separated branches of the monocots and the eudicots; this shows that similar environmental pressures led different groups to evolve the same physiological adaptation independently.
