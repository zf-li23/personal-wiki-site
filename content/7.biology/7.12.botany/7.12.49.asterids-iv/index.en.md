The campanulids comprise about 35,000 species, two thirds of which belong to Asteraceae. They include familiar food, medicinal and ornamental plants such as chrysanthemum, sunflower, lettuce, carrot, celery, ginseng, notoginseng, honeysuckle, balloon flower and holly, as well as noxious invaders such as ragweed and Crofton weed. The campanulids are a group established by molecular systematics and agree with none of the earlier morphological systems: they unite the Campanulales, Asterales and Dipsacales of Cronquist's Asteridae with the Apiales and the Aquifoliaceae of Celastrales from the Rosidae. This chapter treats first the circumscription and synapomorphies of the campanulids (§49.1), then Aquifoliales (§49.2), Asterales (§49.3), Dipsacales (§49.4) and Apiales (§49.5); for the sister group, the lamiids, see [7.12.47](/7.12.47) and [7.12.48](/7.12.48). For the notation of floral formulas see [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.6).

## 49.1. Circumscription and Synapomorphies of the Campanulids

**Definition 49.1.1 (Campanulids)** The monophyletic group formed in the APG system by Aquifoliales, Asterales, Escalloniales, Bruniales, Paracryphiales, Dipsacales and Apiales is called the **campanulids**, also known as the euasterids II and, in phylogenetic nomenclature, as Campanulidae. It is the sister group of the lamiids.

Compared with the lamiids, campanulids often have alternate, toothed leaves (Dipsacales are an exception, with opposite leaves), an often inferior ovary (superior in Aquifoliales and Pittosporaceae), and a corolla that is often "late sympetalous" (free petal primordia appear first and are joined at the base into a corolla tube only later; the petals of Apiaceae and Araliaceae remain free throughout), the filaments often free from the corolla tube, and fruits often indehiscent ([7.12.47](/7.12.47#sec-47.1.1)). Chemically, campanulids often contain polyacetylenes, whereas the iridoids common in the lamiids are absent from most campanulids; the biosynthesis of the two competes for the same precursors, and their distribution among the asterids is roughly complementary. Asterales also store inulin (a fructan) as a storage polysaccharide and often have the chromosome base number $x = 9$.

Aquifoliales are the sister group of the remaining campanulids; the other six orders form a clade in which Apiales is closer to (Paracryphiales + Dipsacales), while the positions of Asterales, Escalloniales and Bruniales are still unstable (Figure 49.1.1). Escalloniales (Escalloniaceae, shrubs of South America and Australia), Bruniales (Bruniaceae of South Africa and Columelliaceae of the Andes) and Paracryphiales (Paracryphiaceae, Australia to New Caledonia) are small orders that do not occur in China and were formerly scattered among Saxifragaceae, Hamamelidales and other groups.

Changes in circumscription: in Cronquist's system the orders of the campanulids belonged to two subclasses: Campanulales (Campanulaceae, Goodeniaceae, Stylidiaceae and others), Asterales (Asteraceae) and Dipsacales (Caprifoliaceae, Adoxaceae, Valerianaceae, Dipsacaceae) were in the Asteridae; Apiales (Araliaceae, Apiaceae), Aquifoliaceae and Cardiopteridaceae of Celastrales, and Pittosporaceae and Escalloniaceae of Rosales were in the Rosidae; *Helwingia* was in Cornaceae. The statement in Chinese textbooks that "Apiales may be related to Sapindales" has not been supported by molecular evidence.

## 49.2. Aquifoliales

The synapomorphies of Aquifoliales (the holly order) are: woody plants with stipules (rare in the asterids) and small, unisexual flowers (often dioecious), perianth parts 4–5, ovary usually superior with 1–2 ovules per locule pendulous from the apex, and fruit a drupe. Aquifoliales fall into two branches: Cardiopteridaceae + Stemonuraceae (formerly in Icacinaceae), and Aquifoliaceae + (Helwingiaceae + Phyllonomaceae).

Aquifoliaceae (holly family) comprise only the genus *Ilex*, with about 500–600 species (about 200 in China), distributed from the tropics to the temperate zone and most diverse in South America and East Asia, often small trees in evergreen broad-leaved forest or shrubs of the understory. Diagnostic features:

- Trees or shrubs, mostly evergreen; leaves alternate, simple, leathery, entire or with teeth or spiny teeth; stipules minute and early-falling.
- Flowers small, unisexual, dioecious (or polygamous), actinomorphic, 4–5-merous (mostly 4), clustered or in cymes in the leaf axils; no disc.
- Sepals 4–6, persistent; petals 4–5, free or united at the base; stamens as many as the petals and alternate with them; female flowers with staminodes, male flowers with a pistillode.
- Ovary superior, 3 to many locules (often 4), each with 1–2 pendulous ovules; stigma sessile, disc-shaped.
- Fruit a berry-like drupe, often red, with 3 to many pyrenes, each with 1 seed; seed with abundant endosperm and a small embryo.

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast K_{4\text{–}6} C_{4\text{–}5} A_{4\text{–}5}$; ♀ $\ast K_{4\text{–}6} C_{4\text{–}5} \underline{G}_{(4\text{–}8:4\text{–}8:1\text{–}2)}$.

Representative plants: *Ilex chinensis* (called *I. purpurea* in Chinese textbooks) is an evergreen tree with shallowly crenate leaves, pale purple-red flowers and red fruit, occurring south of the Yangtze; Chinese holly (*I. cornuta*) is an evergreen shrub with oblong leaves bearing 3 stiff spines at the apex and red fruit, occurring in the middle and lower Yangtze, a common hedge and fruit-display plant, while the holly used for Christmas decoration in the West is the European holly (*I. aquifolium*) of the same genus; *I. latifolia* has large, thick leathery leaves, occurs south of the Yangtze, and its leaves are processed into "kuding tea" (another kuding tea comes from *I. kaushue*; some species of *Ligustrum* in Oleaceae are also called kuding tea); *I. rotunda* and *I. pubescens* are used in medicine. *I. paraguariensis* of South America has leaves containing caffeine, from which yerba mate, a traditional drink of Argentina, Paraguay and elsewhere, is made. The tricolpate pollen of *Ilex* has a distinctive clavate ornamentation, is easy to recognize, and is common in Paleogene strata throughout the world.

Helwingiaceae comprise only the genus *Helwingia*, found in East Asia. *H. japonica* is a deciduous shrub with small, unisexual, dioecious flowers; the female flowers, 1–3 in number, are borne on the middle of the midrib on the upper surface of the leaf, and the fruit is black when ripe, looking like a bean resting on the leaf ("pearl on the leaf"). This results from the fusion of the peduncle with the midrib of the leaf (epiphylly); Phyllonomaceae (Mexico to the Andes of South America) share this rare character, supporting a sister-group relationship between the two. *Helwingia* was traditionally placed in Cornaceae.

## 49.3. Asterales

### 49.3.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Asterales comprise about 1700 genera and 27,000 species in 11 families: Asteraceae, Calyceraceae, Goodeniaceae, Menyanthaceae, Stylidiaceae, Alseuosmiaceae, Argophyllaceae, Phellinaceae, Rousseaceae, Campanulaceae and Pentaphragmataceae. Their synapomorphies are: storage of inulin; corolla lobes valvate; ovary inferior; and secondary pollen presentation. Asteraceae and the South American Calyceraceae are sister groups and form a clade with Goodeniaceae and Menyanthaceae.

**Definition 49.3.1 (Secondary Pollen Presentation)** The phenomenon in which the pollen of the anthers is transferred, before or during anthesis, to another part of the flower (usually the hairs around the style or stigma, or a cup-like structure) and presented to pollinators from there is called **secondary pollen presentation**.

Secondary pollen presentation is often combined with protandry: the flower is first in a "male phase", in which the style presents the pollen; later the stigma lobes open and the flower enters the "female phase", which promotes **cross-pollination**. The modes of secondary pollen presentation in Asterales differ: in *Platycodon* and *Campanula* the anthers dehisce in the bud and the pollen sticks to the hairs of the upper part of the style, which gradually retract after the flower opens so that the pollen is carried away by insects, and then the 3–5 stigma lobes unfold; in *Lobelia* and Asteraceae the anthers are fused into a tube and dehisce inward, and the style elongates in the anther tube and pushes out the pollen like a piston (the "pump" type); in Goodeniaceae the tip of the style has a cup-shaped indusium into which the pollen is received in the bud. Molecular phylogenies show that these modes evolved independently several times in Asterales.

### 49.3.2. Campanulaceae

In APG IV, Campanulaceae (bellflower family) include the subfamily Lobelioideae and comprise about 85 genera and 2400 species (the Chinese textbook counts 86 genera and 2300 species, with 16 genera and 159 species in China), distributed worldwide and most diverse in temperate regions and tropical mountains.

Diagnostic features:

- Annual or perennial herbs, rarely subshrubs or trees (the Hawaiian lobelioids include woody forms); often with white latex (articulated **laticifers**); roots often thick and fleshy.
- Leaves alternate, rarely opposite or whorled, simple, without stipules.
- Flowers bisexual, actinomorphic (Campanuloideae) or zygomorphic (Lobelioideae), solitary or in cymes, the inflorescence appearing racemose, spicate or paniculate.
- Calyx 5-lobed (rarely 3–10), persistent, adnate to the ovary; corolla sympetalous, campanulate or tubular, 5-lobed, valvate; in Lobelioideae the corolla is split longitudinally to the base on the adaxial side, the lobes being turned to one side (the "half-lobelia").
- Stamens 5, alternating with the corolla lobes, borne at the base of the corolla or on the disc, the filament bases often expanded; anthers free but converging around the style (Campanuloideae) or fused into a tube (Lobelioideae).
- Ovary inferior or half-inferior, usually 3-locular (2–5), with axile placentation and many ovules; stigma 2–5-lobed; protandrous, with secondary pollen presentation.
- Capsule dehiscing by valves at the apex, by lateral pores or irregularly, rarely a berry, often crowned by persistent calyx lobes; seeds small, numerous, with abundant endosperm.

Floral formula: Campanuloideae $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_5 \overline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$; Lobelioideae $\uparrow K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_{(5)} \overline{G}_{(2:2:\infty)}$.

Representative plants:

1. *Codonopsis*: perennial twining herbs with latex and thick roots; flowers solitary, corolla campanulate or broadly tubular; stigma 3–5-lobed; capsule dehiscing by valves at the apex. *C. pilosula* (dangshen) has cylindrical roots and pale yellowish green corollas, occurs mainly in northeastern, northern and northwestern China, and its root is a commonly used tonic drug; *C. lanceolata* has obovate-fusiform roots, is also called "shanhailuo", and is widespread.
2. *Platycodon grandiflorus* (balloon flower): a perennial herb with thick, fleshy roots; flowers large, campanulate, blue-purple; ovary inferior, 5-locular, capsule dehiscing by 5 valves at the apex; a single species, widespread in East Asia. The root contains platycodin saponins, is used in medicine, and is also a traditional Korean dish.
3. *Adenophora* (ladybells): perennial herbs; flowers nodding, campanulate; the lower part of the filaments expanded and hairy; disc tubular, surrounding the base of the style; capsule dehiscing irregularly from the base. *A. tetraphylla* often has 4 cauline leaves in a whorl, and the roots of *A. stricta* and others are used in medicine as "southern shashen" (the root of *Glehnia littoralis* of Apiaceae is "northern shashen").
4. *Lobelia*: flowers zygomorphic, corolla split longitudinally on the adaxial side; anthers fused around the style; ovary inferior, 2-locular. *L. chinensis* is a perennial, small, creeping herb with solitary flowers whose corolla lobes are all turned to one side, found from the Yangtze basin to southern China, and the whole plant is used in medicine; *L. davidii* is an erect herb. The giant lobelias of the East African mountains (for example *L. telekii* of Mount Kenya) are giant rosette herbs, and the Hawaiian lobelioids (about 125 species, evolved from a single colonization) are a famous example of **adaptive radiation** on islands.
5. Others: *Campanula* (bellflower) has about 400 species and is a common temperate ornamental; *Wahlenbergia marginata* is a small herb common in southern China.

Key features of the family: often herbs with latex. Leaves simple, alternate. Corolla campanulate, valvate; stamens as many as corolla lobes, anthers free but converging or fused; ovary inferior, usually 3-locular, with many ovules; capsule.

Changes in circumscription: the Hutchinson system separated *Lobelia* and relatives, with zygomorphic flowers and fused anthers, as Lobeliaceae; Cronquist and APG both keep them in Campanulaceae. *Sphenoclea*, formerly in Campanulaceae, has moved to Solanales ([7.12.47](/7.12.47#sec-47.4.1)), and *Pentaphragma* has become Pentaphragmataceae (Asterales).

### 49.3.3. Menyanthaceae and Goodeniaceae

Menyanthaceae (buckbean family) comprise about 5 genera and 70 species of aquatic or marsh herbs with alternate leaves, valvate corolla lobes often with a fringe or wing at the margin, and the ovary superior to half-inferior. *Nymphoides peltata* (yellow floating heart) is a floating herb with round floating leaves and yellow flowers whose corolla lobes have a fringed margin, widespread in the lakes and ponds of China; *N. indica* has white flowers; *Menyanthes trifoliata* (buckbean) is a marsh herb with trifoliolate leaves, found in northeastern and southwestern China. In Cronquist's system Menyanthaceae belonged to Solanales (formerly the subfamily Menyanthoideae of Gentianaceae); their stems lack bicollateral bundles, and molecular evidence places them in Asterales, forming a clade with Asteraceae, Calyceraceae and Goodeniaceae.

Goodeniaceae are found mainly in Australia, with zygomorphic flowers whose corolla is split on the adaxial side and a cup-shaped indusium at the tip of the style. *Scaevola taccada* is an evergreen shrub of tropical coasts, occurring along the southern coast of China and on the South China Sea islands, with all corolla lobes turned to one side like half a flower.

### 49.3.4. Asteraceae

Asteraceae (daisy family; Compositae) comprise about 1600–1700 genera and 24,000–25,000 species (248 genera and 2336 species in China). Together with Orchidaceae they are the largest family of angiosperms, accounting for about 8%–10% of angiosperm species; they are distributed worldwide and especially rich in temperate to tropical mountains, dry grasslands and Mediterranean-type shrublands.

Diagnostic features:

- Herbs, subshrubs or shrubs, rarely trees; some have laticifers (tribe Cichorieae), some have resin canals or oil ducts; sesquiterpene lactones, polyacetylenes and others are often present.
- Leaves alternate, rarely opposite or whorled, without stipules.
- Many sessile small flowers are crowded on an enlarged receptacle, forming a capitulum ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.7.2)); the outside of the inflorescence has an involucre of 1 to many whorls of bracts; the receptacle is flat, convex or conical, and in some has paleae (bracts in the axil of each floret, as in sunflower). The capitulum is solitary or forms a compound inflorescence in racemes, corymbs, panicles and so on.
- Calyx reduced to a pappus (hairy, plumose, scaly or awned) or absent.
- Corolla sympetalous, 5-lobed, of diverse forms (Definition 49.3.2).
- Stamens 5, borne on the corolla tube, filaments free, anthers fused into a tube (syngenesious), dehiscing inward, the connective often with an apical appendage and sometimes a tail at the base.
- Ovary inferior, of 2 fused carpels, 1-locular, with 1 basal anatropous ovule; style 1, 2-lobed at the tip, and the form of the style branches (hairs, appendages) is an important character for the division into tribes.
- Fruit a **cypsela** (an achene with an inferior ovary and calyx tissue), often crowned by a persistent pappus; seed without endosperm.

**Definition 49.3.2 (Floret Types of Asteraceae)** The florets of the capitulum of Asteraceae fall into five types according to the form of the corolla: the **tubular floret** is actinomorphic and bisexual, with a 5-lobed corolla and equal lobes; the **ligulate floret** is zygomorphic and bisexual, the 5 corolla lobes being united on one side into a ligule with 5 teeth at the apex, as in dandelion; the **bilabiate floret** is zygomorphic and bisexual, with the upper lip 2-lobed and the lower lip 3-lobed, as in the tribe Mutisieae; the **ray floret** is zygomorphic and female or neutral, with only 3 teeth at the apex of the ligule (formed by the union of 3 lobes, the other 2 being reduced), as the marginal florets of sunflower; the **funnel floret** is sexless, with a funnelform corolla of 5–7 unequal lobes, as the marginal florets of cornflower.

A capitulum with only tubular florets is called discoid, as in thistles and *Atractylodes*; with only ligulate florets, liguliflorous, as in dandelion and lettuce; with ray florets at the margin and tubular florets in the center, radiate, as in sunflower, chrysanthemum and aster. A radiate capitulum functions like a single flower: the involucre corresponds to the calyx, the marginal florets to the petals (attracting pollinators), and the disc florets to the stamens and pistil. The ray form of the marginal florets of sunflower is determined by the expression of the CYC-like gene *HaCYC2c*, and its mutation turns the marginal florets tubular (Chapman et al. 2012), which is the convergent use of the same class of genes as in the floral symmetry of snapdragon ([7.12.48](/7.12.48#ex-48.4.1)).

Floral formula: tubular floret $\ast K_{0\text{–}\infty} C_{(5)} A_{(5)} \overline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$; ligulate floret $\uparrow K_{0\text{–}\infty} C_{(5)} A_{(5)} \overline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$; ray floret ♀ $\uparrow K_{0\text{–}\infty} C_{(3)} \overline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$ ($K_{0\text{–}\infty}$ means that the sepals are absent or become numerous pappus bristles, and $A_{(5)}$ means syngenesious stamens).

Key features of the family: usually herbs. Leaves alternate. Capitulum with an involucre; corolla sympetalous; syngenesious stamens; ovary inferior, 1-locular, with 1 ovule; cypsela, often with a pappus.

Pollination of Asteraceae (taking cornflower as an example): the great majority of Asteraceae are insect-pollinated and protandrous. The anthers are fused into a tube and dehisce inward, scattering the pollen in the anther tube; when an insect visits and touches the filaments, the filaments contract and pull the anther tube down, or the style elongates and the sweeping hairs on the outside of the style branches push the pollen out from the top of the anther tube to be carried away by the insect; after the pollen is exhausted the style emerges from the anther tube and its two branches spread, exposing the stigmatic surfaces on the inner side to receive pollen brought from another capitulum. In some species, when no cross-pollen is received, the style branches curl downward until they touch the pollen remaining on their own style, completing **self-pollination**. A few groups (*Artemisia*, *Xanthium*, *Ambrosia*) have turned to wind pollination: the anthers are free or nearly free, the pollen is dry, and the capitula are small and nodding, often unisexual.

**Example 49.3.1 (The Golden Angle in the Sunflower Disc)** In the disc of sunflower, the florets (and later the achenes) arise in sequence from the margin toward the center, and the divergence angle between two adjacent florets is about $137.5^\circ$, the golden angle ([7.12.21](/7.12.21#sec-21.4.1)); to the eye the pattern appears as two sets of oppositely directed parastichies, whose numbers are two adjacent Fibonacci numbers, often 34 and 55, 55 and 89, or 89 and 144 in large discs. Vogel's (1979) model places the $k$th floret at the polar coordinates $\left(c\sqrt{k},\ k \times 137.5^\circ\right)$ and reproduces this arrangement well; statistics on hundreds of discs show that about 80% conform to Fibonacci parastichies (Swinton et al. 2016) (Figure 49.3.2).

Subfamilies of Asteraceae. Chinese textbooks divide Asteraceae by corolla type and the presence of latex into the Tubuliflorae (capitula entirely of tubular florets, or with ray or funnel florets at the margin, without latex) and the Liguliflorae (all florets ligulate, with latex, comprising only the tribe Cichorieae). Molecular phylogenies show that the Liguliflorae (Cichorieae) are monophyletic but nested within a larger clade (now the subfamily Cichorioideae, which also includes the tribes Vernonieae, Arctotideae and others), and that the Tubuliflorae are paraphyletic. The current system (Funk et al. 2009; Panero and Crozier 2016; the phylogenomic study of Mandel et al. 2019) divides Asteraceae into 13 subfamilies and about 45 tribes. The earliest branch is the South American Barnadesioideae (about 90 species), which lacks the roughly 22 kb inversion of the chloroplast genome shared by the rest of the family (Jansen and Palmer 1987), a molecular synapomorphy of the rest of Asteraceae; then several small South American subfamilies branch off in succession (Mutisioideae and others, with mostly two-lipped corollas); and the last three large subfamilies are:

Representative plants (the current subfamily and tribe in parentheses):

1. *Artemisia* (Asteroideae, tribe Anthemideae): herbs or semishrubs, bitter or aromatic; capitula small, nodding, entirely of tubular florets, wind-pollinated; cypselae without a pappus. About 400 species, about 190 in China, important components of the dry steppes and deserts of the Northern Hemisphere. *A. argyi* (mugwort) has leaves densely covered beneath with gray-white tomentum, and the leaves are made into moxa floss for moxibustion; the seedlings of *A. capillaris* are used in medicine; *A. ordosica* is a sand-fixing plant of the Ordos region. *A. annua* (sweet wormwood) contains artemisinin (a sesquiterpene lactone with an endoperoxide bridge): inspired by the sentence "take a handful of qinghao, soak it in two sheng of water, and wring out the juice" in Ge Hong's *Zhouhou Beiji Fang* of the Eastern Jin dynasty, Tu Youyou switched to low-temperature ether extraction and obtained an extract effective against malaria parasites in 1971, isolated artemisinin in 1972, and was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2015. Notably, the source plant of the Chinese drug "qinghao" is *A. annua*, whereas *A. caruifolia*, which bears the botanical name "qinghao", does not contain artemisinin: the drug name and the plant name do not agree, and the scientific name must be relied upon ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.3.1)).
2. *Atractylodes* (Carduoideae, tribe Cardueae): perennial herbs with stout rhizomes; the capitulum is subtended by a whorl of pinnately deeply divided bracts with spiny lobes. *A. macrocephala* has a fist-like rhizome, comes from Zhejiang and elsewhere, and tonifies the spleen and qi; the rhizomes of *A. lancea* and *A. chinensis* are used in medicine as "cangzhu".
3. Safflower (*Carthamus tinctorius*, tribe Cardueae): an annual herb with spiny-toothed leaf margins; capitula entirely of orange-red tubular florets; native to the Middle East, the flowers are used in medicine to invigorate blood and are also a red dye, and the seeds are pressed for oil.
4. Cornflower (*Centaurea cyanus*, tribe Cardueae): an annual herb with involucral bracts pectinate at the margin; marginal florets funnelform, sexless, blue, the disc florets tubular; native to Europe and cultivated as an ornamental.
5. *Chrysanthemum* (Anthemideae; Flora of China uses this name, and Chinese textbooks often write *Dendranthema*): perennial herbs; capitula radiate, involucral bracts scarious at the margin; cypselae without a pappus. Florist's chrysanthemum (*C. morifolium*) is an ornamental of hybrid origin that took shape through more than a thousand years of cultivation in China, with thousands of cultivars, and the flowers are also used in medicine (Hangbaiju, Bozhou ju); *C. indicum* is widespread.
6. *Helianthus* (tribe Heliantheae): sunflower (*H. annuus*) is a tall annual herb with lower leaves opposite and upper leaves alternate; capitula large, with ray florets sterile and neutral and disc florets tubular and bisexual, with paleae; cypselae with 2 scale-like, readily falling awns at the apex. Native to North America and domesticated in eastern North America about 4000 years ago, with seeds 40%–50% oil, it is one of the major oil crops of the world (Example 49.3.1). The tubers of Jerusalem artichoke (*H. tuberosus*) are rich in inulin.
7. *Saussurea* (tribe Cardueae): about 400 species, about 300 in China, many growing on alpine scree of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and the Hengduan Mountains. *S. involucrata* (snow lotus of the Tianshan) has large, translucent, pale yellow bracts outside the capitulum, which act like a "greenhouse" and keep the temperature inside the capitulum above that outside; *S. medusa* is densely covered with white wool. These are typical forms of alpine plants adapted to low temperature and strong radiation.
8. *Senecio* (tribe Senecioneae): about 1200 species, one of the largest genera of Asteraceae; involucral bracts in 1 whorl with several outer bracts at the base; style branches truncate at the tip with brush-like hairs. *S. scandens* is a climbing herb, and the whole plant is used in medicine; many species contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids and are hepatotoxic.
9. Cocklebur (*Xanthium strumarium*, called *X. sibiricum* in Chinese textbooks; tribe Heliantheae): an annual herb with unisexual capitula on the same plant; male capitula globose, the involucre of the female capitula united into a sac bearing hooked spines and enclosing 2 female florets, wind-pollinated; the hooked involucres are dispersed by animals. The whole plant is poisonous, the seedlings and fruits most so.
10. Tribe Cichorieae (Cichorioideae): all florets ligulate, with latex. Lettuce (*Lactuca sativa*) is an important vegetable, of which celtuce (stem lettuce) and leaf lettuce are varieties; dandelion (*Taraxacum mongolicum*) has basal leaves and solitary capitula at the end of a hollow scape, the cypsela prolonged into a long beak whose pappus spreads out as a "parachute"; chicory (*Cichorium intybus*) has roots from which chicory coffee and inulin are made, and the leaves are a vegetable; sow thistle (*Sonchus oleraceus*) and *Ixeris* are common weeds. The Russian dandelion (*T. kok-saghyz*), native to Kazakhstan, has latex in its roots that contains rubber, was planted over large areas in the Soviet Union during the Second World War, and is now regaining attention as a source of temperate-zone rubber besides *Hevea*.
11. Others: *Aster tataricus* has roots used in medicine; *Cirsium japonicum* and *C. arvense* var. *integrifolium* (the whole plants) stop bleeding; the involucral bracts of burdock (*Arctium lappa*) are hooked at the tip and cling to clothing (the invention of Velcro was inspired by them), and the fleshy root is a vegetable; beggarticks (*Bidens pilosa*) has cypselae bearing 2–4 barbed awns at the apex; pyrethrum (*Tanacetum cinerariifolium*) has capitula containing pyrethrins, a natural insecticide and the prototype of the pyrethroid pesticides; stevia (*Stevia rebaudiana*) has leaves containing steviol glycosides; dahlia, zinnia, cosmos, marigold, calendula, gerbera, daisy and China aster are common ornamentals.

Several seriously harmful invasive plants occur in Asteraceae: common ragweed (*Ambrosia artemisiifolia*) and giant ragweed (*A. trifida*) are native to North America, produce large quantities of pollen by wind pollination, are the main allergens of autumn pollinosis (hay fever), and are also noxious weeds of farmland; Crofton weed (*Ageratina adenophora*, tribe Eupatorieae), native to Mexico, spread from Myanmar to Yunnan in the 1940s and has now spread through the provinces of the southwest, rapidly forming monodominant stands by allelopathy and apomixis, displacing native plants and poisonous to livestock; mile-a-minute weed (*Mikania micrantha*) is a "plant killer" of southern China, twining over and covering the forest canopy; Canada goldenrod (*Solidago canadensis*) is spreading in the middle and lower Yangtze.

The success of Asteraceae can be summarized as follows: (1) the capitulum, as a "pseudanthium", lets many florets share a single display that attracts pollinators, so that one visit can pollinate many florets, and the division of labor between marginal and disc florets increases efficiency; (2) syngenesious stamens and secondary pollen presentation combined with protandry ensure outcrossing while retaining selfing as a backup; (3) the cypsela and pappus favor long-distance dispersal, and the pappus of dandelion forms a stable detached vortex ring below it that makes the seed descend very slowly (Cummins et al. 2018); (4) chemical defenses (sesquiterpene lactones, polyacetylenes, latex); (5) a diversity of life forms, from annual herbs to giant rosette plants and trees, with frequent polyploidization and hybridization. The statement in Chinese textbooks that the "fossils of Asteraceae appear only in the Oligocene of the Tertiary" is out of date: pollen of Asteraceae has been found in Late Cretaceous strata of Antarctica (about 76–66 million years ago), and Eocene Patagonia (about 47.5 million years ago) has well-preserved fossil capitula (Barreda et al. 2010, 2015), so the family must have originated in the Late Cretaceous, with its large-scale radiation taking place in the Cenozoic.

### 49.3.5. Other Families of Asterales

Stylidiaceae (trigger plant family) are found mainly in Australia; the 2 stamens are fused with the style into a springy "trigger" (the trigger column), which flips over rapidly when touched by an insect and strikes pollen onto it; *Stylidium uliginosum* occurs in southern China. Calyceraceae occur in South America and also have capitula; they are the sister group of Asteraceae.

## 49.4. Dipsacales

Dipsacales comprise about 1100 species, found mainly in the Northern Temperate Zone, and include Adoxaceae and Caprifoliaceae in the broad sense. Their synapomorphies are: opposite leaves (rare among the campanulids); flowers 4–5-merous, ovary inferior, often with some of the carpels sterile (only 1 locule per flower developing and containing 1 ovule), and the calyx often reduced; iridoids and caffeoylquinic acids are often present. Dipsacales are one of the few orders supported by both morphological classification and molecular phylogenies (the morphological analysis of Bremer et al. 2001 also recovered it).

### 49.4.1. Adoxaceae

Adoxaceae (moschatel family) comprise about 5 genera and 200 species, including *Viburnum* (about 165 species), *Sambucus*, *Adoxa*, *Sinadoxa* (endemic to Qinghai) and *Tetradoxa* (endemic to Sichuan). They traditionally belonged to Caprifoliaceae (*Viburnum*, *Sambucus*) and to a separate Adoxaceae, and molecular evidence shows that they form a monophyletic group, the sister group of Caprifoliaceae. Shared characters: flowers small, actinomorphic, with a rotate corolla, a very short style and a 3–5-lobed stigma; ovary half-inferior to inferior; glandular appendages often present; fruit a drupe.

Change of family name: APG IV uses the name Adoxaceae, but under the principle of **priority**, Viburnaceae Raf. (1820) antedates Adoxaceae E. Mey. (1839); a proposal to conserve Adoxaceae as a **conserved name** was not passed by the nomenclature committee, so APweb and later works use Viburnaceae (the viburnum family) as the correct name of this family, whose circumscription is unchanged ([7.12.2](/7.12.2)). This course follows APG IV in writing Adoxaceae, and the two names refer to the same family.

Representative plants:

1. *Viburnum*: shrubs with simple opposite leaves; cymes often arranged in umbels or panicles, in some species with large, sterile, radiate flowers at the margin of the inflorescence (attracting pollinators, as in Chinese snowball); drupe with 1 seed. *V. dilatatum* has broadly obovate toothed leaves and compound umbel-like inflorescences, widespread south of the Yangtze; *V. odoratissimum* is an evergreen shrub often used as a hedge in the south; *V. farreri* is cultivated in the north; in *V. macrocephalum* (Chinese snowball) the inflorescence consists entirely of large sterile flowers, a result of long cultivation and selection, whereas the wild form (*V. macrocephalum* f. *keteleeri*) has sterile flowers only at the margin. Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_5 \overline{G}_{(3:1:1)}$ (3 carpels, only 1 locule developing).
2. *Sambucus* (elder): shrubs or perennial herbs with odd-pinnate leaves, with stipules or stipule-like glands; panicles or corymbs; berry-like drupe. *S. williamsii* is a deciduous shrub with yellow-brown pith in the branches and leaflets that smell unpleasant when crushed, occurring in both north and south, and the stems and leaves are used in medicine; *S. javanica* (called *S. chinensis* in Chinese textbooks) is an herb or subshrub whose inflorescence bears yellow cup-shaped nectaries derived from sterile flowers.
3. Moschatel (*Adoxa moschatellina*): a small perennial herb with a musk-like smell; 5 yellowish green small flowers form a cube-shaped capitate cyme, the terminal flower 4-merous and the 4 lateral flowers 5-merous, facing in four directions, hence the English common name "town hall clock".

### 49.4.2. Caprifoliaceae

Caprifoliaceae (honeysuckle family) in the broad sense comprise about 36 genera and 810 species, found mainly in the Northern Temperate Zone. Diagnostic features:

- Shrubs or vines, rarely herbs (Dipsacoideae and Valerianoideae are mostly herbs); leaves opposite, simple, rarely pinnately compound, often without stipules.
- Flowers bisexual, zygomorphic to nearly actinomorphic, 4–5-merous, in cymes or clusters, often borne in pairs; calyx adnate to the ovary, with 4–5 lobes, or reduced.
- Corolla sympetalous, tubular or funnelform, often two-lipped (*Lonicera*) or 5-lobed, imbricate, often with nectar-secreting hairs or a sac at the base.
- Stamens 4–5 (1–4 in Valerianoideae), borne on the corolla tube.
- Ovary inferior, 2–5-locular, often with some locules sterile; 1 to many ovules per locule.
- Berry, drupe, capsule or achene; seeds with endosperm.

Floral formula: *Lonicera* $\uparrow K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_5 \overline{G}_{(2\text{–}3:2\text{–}3:\infty)}$.

Caprifoliaceae in the broad sense are divided into 6 subfamilies: Diervilloideae, Caprifolioideae, Linnaeoideae, Morinoideae, Dipsacoideae and Valerianoideae (the last three formerly separate families). Flora of China keeps Diervillaceae, Linnaeaceae, Morinaceae, Dipsacaceae and Valerianaceae separate, and APG IV merges all of them into Caprifoliaceae. Representative plants:

1. *Lonicera* (honeysuckle): erect or twining shrubs; flowers often in pairs in the leaf axils, the ovaries of the two flowers sometimes fused; corolla two-lipped or nearly equally 5-lobed; berry. About 180 species, many of them in China. Japanese honeysuckle (*L. japonica*) is a semi-evergreen twining vine with white corollas that turn yellow before they fall, so that yellow and white flowers occur together on one plant (hence the Chinese name "jinyinhua", "gold-and-silver flower"); the change of color is a signal to pollinators, the yellow older flowers no longer having nectar, and hawkmoths and bees visit mainly the white new flowers. The flower buds are used in medicine and contain chlorogenic acid and luteolin glycoside; since 2005 the Chinese Pharmacopoeia has called the flower buds of *L. japonica* "jinyinhua" and listed the flower buds of *L. macranthoides*, *L. confusa* and others separately as "shanyinhua". *L. maackii* is an erect shrub with red fruit, often cultivated.
2. *Weigela florida*: a deciduous shrub with funnelform-campanulate rose-red corollas; capsule; often cultivated as an ornamental.
3. Linnaeoideae: *Linnaea borealis* (twinflower) is a creeping evergreen subshrub with paired nodding flowers, found under conifers in the cold-temperate zone of the Northern Hemisphere; the genus was named for Linnaeus by Gronovius at his request, and Linnaeus, who was fond of it, was often portrayed holding it; *Abelia* (some species now in *Zabelia*) and *A. chinensis*, *Kolkwitzia amabilis* (beautybush, a monotypic genus endemic to China) and *Dipelta* also belong to this subfamily, the fruit often bearing persistent wing-like sepals or bracts.
4. Dipsacoideae: herbs with flowers crowded in heads with an involucre, each flower enclosed by an epicalyx formed by the fusion of bracteoles; ovary inferior, 1-locular, 1 ovule; achene enclosed in the epicalyx. The root of *Dipsacus asper* is used in medicine, "xuduan" meaning "to join broken bones"; the bracts of the capitulum of the European fuller's teasel (*D. sativus*) are stiff and hooked at the tip and were once used to raise the nap of woolen cloth; *Scabiosa* (pincushion flower) is an ornamental. The capitulum of Dipsacoideae closely resembles that of Asteraceae, an example of **convergent evolution**: the two differ in having free or fused stamens and in the origin of the single ovule in the ovary.
5. Valerianoideae: herbs with opposite or basal leaves, often pinnately divided; cymes arranged in corymbs or panicles; stamens 1–4; ovary 3-locular with only 1 locule developing; achene, sometimes with winged bracts or pappus-like sepals. *Patrinia scabiosifolia* has roots with the smell of spoiled soy paste ("baijiang") and the whole plant is used in medicine; valerian (*Valeriana officinalis*) has roots containing valerenic acid and others, with a sedative effect; *Nardostachys jatamansi* has an aromatic rhizome and occurs on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau.
6. Morinoideae: *Acanthocalyx* and *Morina*, with spiny leaf margins, found from the Himalayas to the Hengduan Mountains.

Key features of the family: often woody. Leaves opposite, often without stipules. Flowers often in pairs, 5-merous, actinomorphic or zygomorphic, the corolla often imbricate; ovary inferior, usually 3-locular. Distinction from Rubiaceae: Caprifoliaceae have no stipules (or only poorly developed ones), flowers often zygomorphic and the corolla imbricate; Rubiaceae have interpetiolar stipules, regular flowers, and the corolla often valvate or contorted ([7.12.47](/7.12.47#sec-47.2.2)). The similarity of the two families is convergent: the Cronquist system and Chinese textbooks held that Dipsacales originated from Rubiales, whereas molecular evidence places them in two different branches of the asterids.

## 49.5. Apiales

### 49.5.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

APG IV Apiales comprise 7 families: Griseliniaceae, Torricelliaceae, Pennantiaceae, Pittosporaceae, Araliaceae, Myodocarpaceae and Apiaceae, with about 5500 species. The synapomorphies are: umbels (or compound inflorescences made up of them), flowers small and 5-merous, petals valvate and often free, ovary inferior with an epigynous disc (stylopodium), secretory canals (resin canals, oil ducts) in the plant, and frequent polyacetylenes (such as panaxynol and falcarinol) and coumarins. Pittosporaceae, with a superior ovary, are an exception.

Pittosporaceae comprise about 9 genera and 200 species of the tropics and subtropics of the Old World. *Pittosporum tobira* is an evergreen shrub with obovate, leathery leaves crowded at the ends of the branches and white, fragrant flowers; capsule dehiscing by 3 valves, with the seeds embedded in a red, sticky, resin-like substance and dispersed by birds. It occurs along the coast from eastern to southern China and is a common hedge plant. Cronquist placed Pittosporaceae in Rosales; their resin canals and polyacetylenes support their position in Apiales.

Torricelliaceae contain *Torricellia*, a small deciduous tree of southwestern China whose petiole base is expanded into a sheath, formerly placed in Cornaceae.

### 49.5.2. Araliaceae

Araliaceae (ginseng family) comprise about 43 genera and 1450 species, distributed from the tropics to the temperate zone in both hemispheres and most diverse in Southeast Asia and tropical America; China has about 23 genera and 180 species, most of them in the southwest.

Diagnostic features:

- Trees, shrubs or woody climbers, rarely perennial herbs; stems often prickly, with a well-developed pith; secretory canals present.
- Leaves alternate, rarely whorled, simple, palmately compound or pinnately compound; stipules often fused with the petiole base into a sheath.
- Flowers small, regular, bisexual or polygamous, in umbels, heads or spikes, often forming compound panicles.
- Calyx tube adnate to the ovary, calyx teeth 5, small; petals 5 (occasionally 10), valvate, often free; stamens as many as the petals and alternate with them; an epigynous disc covers the top of the ovary.
- Ovary inferior, 2–5 (or more) carpels fused, with as many locules as carpels, each with 1 pendulous anatropous ovule; styles free or united.
- Berry or drupe; seed with abundant endosperm and a small embryo.

Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_5 \overline{G}_{(2\text{–}5:2\text{–}5:1)}$.

Key features of the family: mostly woody and often prickly; leaves often palmately or pinnately compound; umbels, 5-merous, ovary inferior with 1 ovule per locule; berry or drupe.

Representative plants:

1. *Panax*: perennial herbs with a short rhizome (ginseng) or a creeping, fleshy rhizome (bamboo-joint ginseng), and a spindle-shaped fleshy root below; palmate leaves 3–6 in a whorl at the top of the stem; umbel solitary at the top of the stem; drupe-like berry, red when ripe. Ginseng (*P. ginseng*) occurs in the Changbai Mountains of northeastern China, the Korean peninsula and the Russian Far East; its root contains various ginsenosides (dammarane-type triterpenoid saponins) and is a famous tonic drug. Its rhizome grows only one node each year (the stem scars on the "lutou" can be used to estimate age), and wild ginseng ("shanshen") is close to extinction and is a second-class nationally protected wild plant (2021 list), the ginseng on the market being mostly cultivated "garden ginseng". Notoginseng (*P. notoginseng*) occurs in Wenshan in Yunnan and in Guangxi; its root (tuberous root) stops bleeding and disperses blood stasis and is the main ingredient of "Yunnan baiyao", and it exists almost only in cultivation. American ginseng (*P. quinquefolius*) is native to eastern North America and is another example of the East Asia–North America **disjunct distribution** ([7.12.35](/7.12.35#sec-35.2.2)).
2. *Eleutherococcus* (called *Acanthopanax* in Chinese textbooks): shrubs, stems often prickly, leaves palmately compound, leaflets often 5. The root bark of *E. nodiflorus* is the Chinese drug "wujiapi" (southern wujiapi); *E. senticosus* (Siberian ginseng) occurs in northeastern and northern China, and the root and stem are used in medicine, with an "adaptogenic" effect similar to ginseng.
3. *Aralia*: *A. elata* (Japanese angelica tree) is a prickly shrub or small tree with 2–3-pinnate leaves and large panicles made up of umbels; the young buds ("cilaoya", "cinenya") are a wild vegetable of northeastern China. *A. cordata* (udo) and others are herbs.
4. Ivy (*Hedera nepalensis* var. *sinensis*): an evergreen climbing vine clinging by aerial roots, with 3–5-lobed leaves on the vegetative branches and entire leaves on the reproductive branches (heterophylly), occurring from central to southwestern China and grown for vertical greening; English ivy (*H. helix*) of Europe is a common indoor foliage plant.
5. Rice-paper plant (*Tetrapanax papyrifer*): a shrub with large leaves palmately 5–11-lobed, with a large white pith in the stem, which is the Chinese drug "tongcao"; sliced thin it is called "rice paper" and was formerly used to make rice-paper paintings and artificial flowers.
6. Others: *Schefflera heptaphylla* is a common tree of southern China, and *S. arboricola* and the Australian umbrella tree (*S. actinophylla*) are foliage plants; *Kalopanax septemlobus* is a tall deciduous tree with stout spines on the trunk; *Fatsia japonica* is a shade-tolerant foliage shrub.

### 49.5.3. Apiaceae

Apiaceae (carrot family; Umbelliferae) comprise about 434 genera and 3780 species (100 genera and 614 species in China), found mainly in the Northern Temperate Zone and in the tropics only in the mountains.

Diagnostic features:

- Annual to perennial aromatic herbs, rarely subshrubs; stems often hollow and longitudinally ridged; oil ducts and resin canals present, with volatile oils.
- Leaves alternate, often 1- to many-pinnately divided or ternate-pinnate, rarely simple (*Bupleurum*); petiole base expanded into a sheath clasping the stem.
- Inflorescence a compound umbel ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.7.2)), rarely a simple umbel or a head (*Eryngium*); the base of the compound umbel often bears involucral bracts and the base of each umbellet involucel bracts.
- Flowers small, bisexual, regular, 5-merous; the marginal flowers of the inflorescence are often zygomorphic with larger outer petals (carrot, coriander), so that the whole inflorescence looks like a single large flower.
- Calyx teeth 5 or indistinct; petals 5, free, often inflexed at the apex; stamens 5, alternate with the petals, inflexed in the bud.
- Ovary inferior, of 2 fused carpels, 2-locular, with 1 pendulous ovule per locule; styles 2, expanded at the base into an epigynous disc (stylopodium) that secretes nectar, which is exposed and suitable for short-tongued flies, beetles and others.
- The fruit is a **cremocarp**: at maturity it separates along the commissure of the two carpels into 2 mericarps, hanging from a forked central carpophore ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.2.4)). Each mericarp has 5 primary ridges (1 dorsal, 2 intermediate, 2 lateral), and some have 4 secondary ridges between the primary ones; between the ridges are the furrows (vallecula), under which and in the commissure there are often longitudinal oil ducts; the mericarps are compressed dorsally or laterally.
- Seed with abundant endosperm and a small embryo.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)\text{–}0} C_5 A_5 \overline{G}_{(2:2:1)}$.

Key features of the family: aromatic herbs, stem often hollow, petiole base sheathing; compound umbels; flowers 5-merous, ovary inferior, 2-locular, with a stylopodium; cremocarp. Apiaceae are easy to recognize as a family, but genera and species are difficult to identify, mainly using the fruit: the presence of bristles and spines, whether the mericarps are compressed dorsally or laterally, the form of the primary and secondary ridges (winged or not), and the number and position of the oil ducts in the furrows and commissure; secondarily, whether the inflorescence is a compound or a simple umbel, the presence of involucral and involucel bracts, flower color and the calyx teeth.

Apiaceae are divided into 4 subfamilies: Apioideae (the great majority of genera), Saniculoideae (*Eryngium*, *Sanicula*), Azorelloideae and Mackinlayoideae (*Centella*). Representative plants:

1. Carrot (*Daucus carota* var. *sativus*): a biennial herb with a fleshy conical taproot containing carotene; leaves 2–3-pinnately deeply divided; the compound umbel has pinnately divided involucral bracts, and the center of the umbel of wild carrot often bears a single sterile, deep purple flower; the secondary ridges of the cremocarp are winged and bear bristles. Native to Central Asia, the orange color of cultivated carrot is the result of breeding in the Netherlands in the 17th century.
2. Celery (*Apium graveolens*): the thick petioles are used as a vegetable; water dropwort (*Oenanthe javanica*) grows in wet places, and the young stems and leaves are used as a vegetable.
3. Fennel (*Foeniculum vulgare*): leaves finely divided many times into threads, flowers yellow, without an involucre; the fruit ("xiaohuixiang") contains anethole and is used as a seasoning. Note that "dahuixiang" (star anise) is *Illicium verum* of Schisandraceae ([7.12.34](/7.12.34)).
4. Coriander (*Coriandrum sativum*): an annual herb with markedly larger outer petals in the marginal flowers; cremocarp globose; the whole plant has a distinctive odor (aldehydes), and dislike of this odor is associated with variation in the olfactory receptor gene *OR6A2*.
5. *Angelica*: large herbs with often hollow stems and ternate-pinnate leaves; cremocarp compressed dorsally with broadly winged lateral ridges. *A. sinensis* (dong quai) comes mainly from Minxian in Gansu, and its root is an important gynecological drug; *A. dahurica* has roots used in medicine.
6. *Bupleurum* (thorow-wax): herbs with simple, entire leaves with parallel or arcuate venation, which is highly unusual in Apiaceae; involucral bracts leaf-like; cremocarp compressed laterally. The root of *B. chinense* is an important antipyretic.
7. Chuanxiong (*Ligusticum sinense* 'Chuanxiong', often written *L. chuanxiong*): a cultivated plant that hardly sets seed and is propagated vegetatively by stem nodes ("lingzi"), occurring mainly in Sichuan, with the rhizome used in medicine.
8. Other medicinal plants: *Saposhnikovia divaricata*, *Peucedanum praeruptorum*, *Cnidium monnieri* and *Notopterygium incisum*; the root of *Glehnia littoralis* is "northern shashen"; *Centella asiatica* is a creeping herb with round-reniform leaves, used in medicine.

**Example 49.5.1 (Identifying Poisonous Apiaceae)** Several highly poisonous plants occur in Apiaceae that resemble edible species, and deaths from accidental ingestion occur repeatedly. Water hemlock (*Cicuta virosa*) grows by water and has a stout, short rhizome whose longitudinal section shows many cavities divided by transverse septa (the most reliable diagnostic character); it contains cicutoxin (a polyacetylene compound), which acts on the central nervous system and causes convulsions. Poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*) has purple spots on the stem, smells of mouse urine when crushed, and contains coniine; Socrates is recorded as having been sentenced to die by drinking it. They are easily confused with water dropwort, wild carrot or celery. The sap of *Heracleum* such as giant hogweed (*H. mantegazzianum*) contains furanocoumarins, which cause severe phototoxic dermatitis when the skin touched by it is exposed to sunlight. These examples show that the identification of Apiaceae must rely on the fruit and the underground parts and cannot depend on leaf shape alone.

Changes in circumscription: Apiaceae and Araliaceae are sister groups. The traditional subfamily Hydrocotyloideae of Apiaceae is polyphyletic: *Hydrocotyle* and some other genera have moved to Araliaceae (Hydrocotyloideae), *Centella* and others remain in Apiaceae (Mackinlayoideae), and *Mackinlaya* of the former Araliaceae has been merged into Mackinlayoideae of Apiaceae. The Hutchinson system placed the woody Araliaceae and the herbaceous Apiaceae in two evolutionary lines ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.4.4)), a typical error resulting from classification by habit; Cronquist placed Apiales in the Rosidae, and molecular evidence moves it to the campanulids among the asterids.

## Questions

1. What are the general morphological differences between campanulids and lamiids? Why can these differences not be used alone for identification?

   **Answer** Lamiids often have opposite, entire leaves, a superior ovary, early sympetaly, filaments fused to the corolla tube, often dehiscent fruits, and often contain iridoids; campanulids often have alternate, toothed leaves, an inferior ovary, late sympetaly, often free filaments, often indehiscent fruits, and often contain polyacetylenes. But each of these has exceptions: Rubiaceae (lamiids) have an inferior ovary, Dipsacales (campanulids) have opposite leaves, and Aquifoliales and Pittosporaceae (campanulids) have a superior ovary. These characters evolved in parallel or reverted several times among the asterids, and the monophyly of the two branches is established mainly by molecular data, morphology giving only general tendencies.

2. Why is the capitulum of Asteraceae called a "pseudanthium"? What roles do the marginal and disc florets of sunflower play?

   **Answer** The capitulum consists of many florets crowded on a receptacle and surrounded by an involucre; structurally it is an inflorescence, but functionally it resembles a single flower: the involucre protects the inflorescence and corresponds to the calyx; the marginal ray florets (female or neutral) have large, showy ligules that correspond to petals and attract pollinators; the central tubular florets are bisexual and produce pollen and seeds. The marginal florets of sunflower are neutral and sterile and serve only to attract; the disc florets, hundreds to over a thousand in number and arranged by the golden angle, are the seed-producing part. A pollinator can pollinate many disc florets in one visit.

3. How should we understand the status of the "Tubuliflorae" and "Liguliflorae" of Chinese textbooks in the current system?

   **Answer** The Liguliflorae (only the tribe Cichorieae, with all florets ligulate and with latex) are monophyletic, but in the current system they are only one tribe within Cichorioideae; the Tubuliflorae include all of Asteraceae except the Cichorieae and are paraphyletic. The current system, based on molecular phylogeny, recognizes 13 subfamilies: the earliest to branch off is the South American Barnadesioideae (which lack the 22 kb chloroplast inversion shared by the rest of the family), followed by several small South American subfamilies, and finally the three large subfamilies Carduoideae, Cichorioideae and Asteroideae, of which Asteroideae account for about two thirds of the species of the family.

4. What is the relationship between Apiaceae and Araliaceae? How can edible and poisonous species of Apiaceae be distinguished?

   **Answer** The two families are sister groups in Apiales and share umbels, small 5-merous flowers, an inferior ovary, an epigynous disc and secretory canals. Araliaceae are mostly woody and often prickly, with berries or drupes, 2–5 carpels and 1 ovule per locule; Apiaceae are mostly aromatic herbs with hollow stems, sheathing petioles, compound umbels and a cremocarp of 2 carpels. Traditionally the boundary between them was not entirely clear, and *Hydrocotyle* and others have moved from Apiaceae to Araliaceae. Identification of Apiaceae relies on the fruit (ridges, wings, bristles, oil ducts) and the underground parts: the rhizome of water hemlock has cavities divided by transverse septa in longitudinal section, and poison hemlock has purple spots on the stem and a smell of mouse urine; using leaf shape alone it is easy to confuse them with water dropwort, wild carrot and celery.

5. What do the capitulum of Dipsacus and the capitulum of Asteraceae have in common, and how do they differ?

   **Answer** Common: both consist of many sessile florets crowded together with an involucre outside, the ovary is inferior, 1-locular with 1 ovule, and the fruit is achene-like, often with a pappus-like or spiny calyx. Different: *Dipsacus* (Caprifoliaceae, Dipsacoideae) has opposite leaves, an epicalyx formed by fused bracteoles outside each floret, 4 free stamens, and a 1-locular ovary resulting from the sterility of some carpels; Asteraceae mostly have alternate leaves, no epicalyx, 5 stamens whose anthers are fused into a syngenesious tube, protandry and secondary pollen presentation. The two belong to Dipsacales and Asterales respectively, and the capitulum is a result of convergent evolution.
