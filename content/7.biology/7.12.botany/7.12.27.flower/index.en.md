The flower is the most distinctive feature of the angiosperms and the central tool of the second half of this course. Angiosperm classification ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)–[7.12.49](/7.12.49)) rests almost entirely on floral structure: the diagnostic characters of every family are written as a floral formula, and many families are illustrated with a floral diagram. The two preceding chapters dealt with the origin of seeds and the reproduction of gymnosperms ([7.12.25](/7.12.25), [7.12.26](/7.12.26)): the **ovules** of a gymnosperm lie exposed on the megasporophylls (ovuliferous scales), and the megasporophylls and microsporophylls are grouped into unisexual **strobili**. In angiosperms the microsporophylls (stamens) and megasporophylls (carpels) are brought together on a single short axis of determinate growth and surrounded by a sterile perianth; the carpels close and enclose the ovules in an ovary. This structure is the flower. This chapter begins with the concept of the flower and the genetic model of floral organ identity, then treats the parts of the flower in turn, flower sex and symmetry, evolutionary trends in floral parts, the specialized flowers of grasses, the floral formula and floral diagram, and finally the arrangement of flowers on the plant, the inflorescence. How gametophytes arise inside the anther and the ovule is the subject of the next chapter ([7.12.28](/7.12.28)); anthesis, pollination and fertilization follow in [7.12.29](/7.12.29), and the fruit in [7.12.31](/7.12.31). The physiology of flowering (photoperiod, vernalization and the regulatory network of flowering genes) belongs to plant physiology; here we cover only morphology and the models needed for it.

## 27.1. The Concept and Origin of the Flower

### 27.1.1. The Flower as a Modified Short Shoot

A seed plant germinates and first grows vegetatively. Once conditions such as light and temperature are met and hormonal signals have acted, it switches to reproductive growth. Part or all of the shoot apical meristems then stop producing leaf primordia and axillary bud primordia and instead produce floral primordia or inflorescence primordia. At the start of the transition, cell division accelerates in one or several surface layers of the apical dome, while the cells in the center divide more slowly, enlarge and develop large vacuoles; the surface cells accumulate no starch but are rich in protein and RNA. The surface cells then form several whorls of protrusions, from the outside inward, which develop into the primordia of the floral parts. The usual sequence is centripetal: sepal primordia first, then petal and stamen primordia, and last the pistil primordium (as in rapeseed, *Brassica napus*). There are exceptions; in cotton (*Gossypium*), petal and stamen primordia appear almost simultaneously, and the outermost primordia to appear are those of the epicalyx. A floral primordium cannot grow indefinitely: once all parts have formed, the apical meristem ceases activity, so the flower is a structure of determinate growth.

The correspondence between flower and shoot was noticed early. In *The Metamorphosis of Plants* (1790), the German poet and naturalist J. W. von Goethe proposed that all the aerial organs of a plant are modifications of one fundamental organ, the leaf, and that the flower is a shoot modified for reproduction: the pedicel and receptacle correspond to the stem, and sepals, petals, stamens and carpels to leaves borne at nodes. The floral parts do retain leaf-like properties in form and structure: sepals are often green and bear stomata; the stamens of the most primitive angiosperms are flat, leaf-like blades (Section 27.2.4); and the carpel is a leaf folded inward (Section 27.2.5). Phylogenetically, the floral parts that bear megaspores and microspores derive from sporophylls and the receptacle corresponds to the axis of a strobilus, so the flower is regarded as a specialized **strobilus**. This is the strobilus theory, which is linked to the euanthial theory of angiosperm origin ([7.12.34](/7.12.34)).

**Definition 27.1.1 (Flower)** In angiosperms, the reproductive structure consisting of a short axis of determinate growth (the receptacle) and the perianth, stamens and carpels borne on it in a definite order is called a **flower**. The flower is an unbranched modified short shoot: stamens correspond to microsporophylls, carpels to megasporophylls, and the perianth consists of sterile appendages; within the flower, megaspores and microspores and the female and male gametophytes are formed, and after pollination and fertilization it develops into the fruit and seed.

The precise meaning of "flower" is not uniform in the literature. Bateman, Hilton and Rudall (2006) compared several definitions. The broadest calls any determinate short axis bearing sporophylls a flower, in which case the cones of cycads and pines are also "flowers". A narrower definition requires a sterile perianth surrounding the sporophylls, which could make the reproductive structures of the gnetophytes flowers as well. The narrowest definition, the one adopted in this course, applies only to angiosperms and requires closed carpels on the same axis, usually together with stamens and a perianth. The flower so defined is one of the synapomorphies of the angiosperms and, together with double fertilization, sieve tube–companion cell complexes and endosperm, distinguishes them from the other seed plants.

Flowers have many economic uses. Ornamentals beautify the environment; the flowers of jasmine (*Jasminum sambac*), michelia (*Michelia alba*) and bitter orange (*Citrus aurantium*) scent teas, and roses and osmanthus (*Osmanthus fragrans*) yield aromatic oils. Safflower (*Carthamus tinctorius*), clove (the flower bud of *Syzygium aromaticum*), honeysuckle (*Lonicera japonica*) and chrysanthemum are used in medicine. Daylily buds (*Hemerocallis citrina*), cauliflower (an inflorescence) and osmanthus are eaten, and the garden balsam (*Impatiens balsamina*) provides a dye.

### 27.1.2. Floral Organ Identity: The ABC Model

Why are the parts of the flower always arranged from the outside in as sepals, petals, stamens and carpels? Since the 1980s, mutants of a class of **homeotic genes** have been found in *Arabidopsis thaliana* and snapdragon (*Antirrhinum majus*) in which one whorl of floral organs is replaced by another kind of organ, for example petals converted to stamens or stamens to petals. Such changes of organ identity are the genetic expression of the "metamorphosis" of which Goethe spoke. On this basis Coen and Meyerowitz (1991) proposed a model that explains the identity of the four whorls of floral organs.

**Definition 27.1.2 (ABC Model of Flower Development)** The four whorls of organs of a typical eudicot flower are, from the outside in, sepals, petals, stamens and carpels, and their identities are determined by the combination of three classes of function: A function acts in whorls 1 and 2, B function in whorls 2 and 3, and C function in whorls 3 and 4. A alone specifies sepals, A + B specifies petals, B + C specifies stamens, and C alone specifies carpels; moreover, A and C are mutually antagonistic, so that when one is absent the other extends into all four whorls. This model is called the **ABC model of flower development**.

In *Arabidopsis*, A function is provided by *APETALA1* (*AP1*) and *APETALA2* (*AP2*), B function by *APETALA3* (*AP3*) and *PISTILLATA* (*PI*), and C function by *AGAMOUS* (*AG*). Except for *AP2*, these are all MADS-box genes encoding MADS-domain transcription factors. The strength of the model is that it predicts the phenotypes of single and multiple mutants (Figure 1):

| Mutant | Function lost | Whorl 1 | Whorl 2 | Whorl 3 | Whorl 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wild type | none | sepal (A) | petal (A+B) | stamen (B+C) | carpel (C) |
| *ap2* | A (C extends into the outer two whorls) | carpel | stamen | stamen | carpel |
| *ap3*, *pi* | B | sepal | sepal | carpel | carpel |
| *ag* | C (A extends into the inner two whorls) | sepal | petal | petal | sepal; the center of the flower repeatedly produces "sepal–petal–petal" and the flower becomes indeterminate |
| A–B double mutants such as *ap2 pi* | A, B | carpel | carpel | carpel | carpel |
| B–C double mutants such as *ap3 ag* | B, C | sepal | sepal | sepal | sepal (repeated) |
| A–B–C triple mutant | all | leaf-like organ | leaf-like organ | leaf-like organ | leaf-like organ |

Besides altering organ identity, *ag* mutants lose the determinacy of the flower: C function is also responsible for terminating floral meristem activity. Most double flowers in cultivation (double peonies, roses and camellias) result from the conversion of stamens, or even carpels, into petals, a phenotype resembling reduced C function. In the triple mutant all floral organs become leaf-like, which is the most direct evidence that floral organs are modified leaves.

![The ABC model of floral organ identity. A: expression domains of the A, B and C functions in the four whorls of an *Arabidopsis* flower (whorls 1–4) and the organs they specify. In *ap2* (A lost), C extends into the outer two whorls and the flower becomes carpel–stamen–stamen–carpel; in *ap3* and *pi* (B lost) it is sepal–sepal–carpel–carpel; in *ag* (C lost), A extends into the inner two whorls, giving sepal–petal–petal–sepal, and the center of the flower repeatedly produces new floral organs. B: the ABCDE model (E function, SEP, is required in all organs; D function specifies ovules) and the MADS-domain protein tetramers corresponding to each organ in the floral quartet model](./plots/abc-model.py)

The domain of B-class gene expression differs among groups, which explains many perianth forms. In tulips (*Tulipa*) and lilies (*Lilium*), both perianth whorls are showy, petal-like tepals because B-class genes are also expressed in whorl 1 (the "sliding boundary" or modified ABC model; van Tunen et al. 1993). In early-diverging angiosperms such as the Nymphaeales and *Amborella*, the perianth members grade gradually from the outside inward into stamen-like organs, and the expression boundaries of B- and C-class genes are likewise gradual and overlapping; this is the fading borders model (Soltis et al. 2007). This suggests that the sharply delimited four whorls of typical eudicots were "sharpened" during evolution from an ancestral state with blurred boundaries ([7.12.34](/7.12.34)).

### 27.1.3. The ABCDE Model and the Floral Quartet Model

Later research added two more classes of function to ABC, giving the ABCDE model (Figure 1B):

- D function specifies ovule identity. When the petunia genes *FBP7* and *FBP11* are cosuppressed, ovules are converted into carpel-like structures (Colombo et al. 1995); the corresponding *Arabidopsis* genes are *SEEDSTICK* (*STK*) and *SHATTERPROOF1/2* (*SHP1/2*).
- E function is a cofactor required for the action of all the A, B, C and D functions, and is provided by *SEPALLATA1–4* (*SEP1–4*). In *sep1 sep2 sep3* triple mutants, petals, stamens and carpels all become sepal-like (Pelaz et al. 2000), and in the quadruple mutant all floral organs become leaf-like (Ditta et al. 2004).

Thus A + E specifies sepals, A + B + E petals, B + C + E stamens, C + E carpels, and C + D + E ovules.

Why are E-class genes indispensable? Theißen (2001) proposed the floral quartet model: MADS-domain proteins bind DNA in groups of four molecules, and different tetramer combinations activate the developmental programs of different organs. According to this model, sepals are specified by an AP1–AP1–SEP–SEP tetramer, petals by AP1–SEP–AP3–PI, stamens by AG–SEP–AP3–PI, carpels by AG–AG–SEP–SEP, and ovules by tetramers containing STK/SHP and SEP. SEP proteins are the "bridge" in nearly all tetramers, so without E function no organ identity can be realized. The floral quartet model puts the formal rule that identity is determined by combination on a molecular footing; it has been shown in vitro and in vivo that these proteins can form tetramers.

The ABC model specifies only "what organ each whorl is", not "where flowers form, how many whorls form and how many members each has". The initiation of a floral meristem also requires floral meristem identity genes such as *LEAFY* (*LFY*): in *lfy* mutants, shoots develop where flowers should be and no normal flower forms; whether an inflorescence is determinate is related to *TERMINAL FLOWER1* (*TFL1*) (Section 27.7.4). The regulatory network of these genes and the environmental signals for flowering belong to plant physiology; the general methods of developmental genetics are treated in [7.5.3](/7.5.3).

## 27.2. The Parts of the Flower

A typical flower consists of five parts: pedicel, receptacle, perianth, androecium and gynoecium (Figure 2). The pedicel and receptacle are modifications of the stem; the perianth, stamens and pistil are modifications of leaves.

![The parts of the flower. Left: longitudinal section of a complete flower; the tip of the pedicel is swollen into the receptacle, which bears from the outside inward the sepals (together the calyx), petals (together the corolla), stamens (filament and anther) and pistil (stigma, style and the ovary containing ovules). Right: the external form of a stamen and a cross section of the anther: the anther consists of two thecae joined by the connective, each theca containing two pollen sacs (microsporangia), and the connective contains a vascular bundle](./plots/flower-structure.py)

### 27.2.1. Pedicel and Receptacle

**Definition 27.2.1 (Pedicel and Receptacle)** The small stalk bearing a flower is called the **pedicel**; the slightly enlarged tip of the pedicel, which bears the parts of the flower, is called the **receptacle**. A flower with all four parts (calyx, corolla, androecium and gynoecium) is called a **complete flower**; a flower lacking one or several of them is called an **incomplete flower**.

The pedicel is the channel connecting the flower to the stem and displays the flower at a suitable position on the shoot; its structure is the same as that of a stem, and after the fruit forms it becomes the fruit stalk. Its length varies among species: the weeping Chinese crabapple (*Malus halliana*) has a very long pedicel and the Japanese quince (*Chaenomeles speciosa*) a very short one, and some plants have sessile flowers. The shape of the receptacle varies greatly and determines how the floral parts are arranged on it, in particular the position of the ovary (Section 27.2.7):

| Shape of receptacle | Examples |
|---|---|
| Elongated, cylindrical | magnolia (*Magnolia*), Yulan magnolia (*M. denudata*) |
| Raised, conical or inverted bowl-shaped | strawberry (*Fragaria*) |
| Concave in the center, cup- or urn-shaped | peach (*Prunus persica*), rose (*Rosa*), apple (*Malus*) |
| Enlarged, inverted-conical (the "lotus pod") | sacred lotus (*Nelumbo nucifera*) |

Some receptacles show special development. The receptacle of citrus (*Citrus*) is enlarged at the base of the pistil into a nectar-secreting disc. In the peanut (*Arachis hypogaea*), the receptacle elongates at the base of the pistil into a gynophore (also called the ovary stalk) that grows rapidly after fertilization and pushes the ovary into the soil, where the pod matures. In passionflower (*Passiflora*) and *Sterculia*, the part of the receptacle inside the corolla elongates into an androgynophore. In catchfly (*Lychnis* s.l.) and some other Caryophyllaceae, the part of the receptacle inside the calyx elongates into an anthophore.

### 27.2.2. The Perianth: Calyx and Corolla

**Definition 27.2.2 (Perianth, Calyx and Corolla)** The sterile part of the flower consisting of flat blades and borne around the outside of the receptacle is called the **perianth**. When the perianth is divided into an outer and an inner part, the outer whorl is called the **calyx** and consists of several **sepals**; the inner whorl is called the **corolla** and consists of several **petals**. When the perianth has two whorls that do not differ in form and color, each member is called a **tepal**.

According to the presence and differentiation of the perianth, flowers fall into four types:

- Dichlamydeous (heterochlamydeous) flowers have both calyx and corolla, as in rapeseed, pea (*Pisum sativum*), peach and tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*).
- Homochlamydeous flowers have two perianth whorls without a distinction between calyx and corolla, all members being tepals, as in lily, yucca (*Yucca*) and tulip.
- Monochlamydeous flowers have only one perianth whorl, usually regarded as a calyx whatever its color, as in hemp (*Cannabis sativa*), buckwheat (*Fagopyrum esculentum*), mulberry (*Morus*) and clematis (*Clematis*).
- Achlamydeous (naked) flowers have a completely reduced perianth, as in the male flowers of poplar (*Populus*), willow (*Salix*) and walnut (*Juglans*).

Achlamydeous and monochlamydeous flowers are morphologically "simple", but most of them arose by secondary simplification from dichlamydeous flowers and are often associated with wind pollination ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)); they should not be taken to be primitive (Section 27.4).

The **calyx** is usually green and leaf-like, with abundant chlorenchyma but no differentiation into palisade and spongy tissue; it protects the interior of the flower in the bud and can also photosynthesize. In some plants the calyx is large and colored and petal-like, which helps to attract pollinators, as in larkspur (*Delphinium*), clematis and monkshood (*Aconitum*). Terms commonly used for the calyx are as follows:

| Term | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Polysepalous / gamosepalous calyx | sepals free from one another / fused into a tube (the upper part often divided into calyx teeth) | rapeseed, peach / cherry (*Prunus*), pink (*Dianthus*), eggplant (*Solanum melongena*) |
| Regular / irregular calyx | sepals equal in size and shape / unequal | |
| Caducous calyx | sepals fall at or before anthesis | opium poppy (*Papaver somniferum*) |
| Deciduous calyx | falls together with the corolla | rapeseed, peach |
| Persistent calyx | does not fall after flowering but develops with the fruit | eggplant, persimmon (*Diospyros kaki*), tomato, chili pepper (*Capsicum*) |
| Epicalyx | a whorl of sepal-like bracts outside the calyx | cotton, hollyhock (*Alcea rosea*), strawberry |
| Spur | a tubular backward extension at the base of a sepal (or petal), often holding nectar | garden balsam, nasturtium (*Tropaeolum majus*), larkspur |
| Pappus | sepals modified into hairs, scales or bristles that aid fruit dispersal | Asteraceae: dandelion (*Taraxacum*; simple hairs), field thistle (*Cirsium setosum*; plumose), sunflower (*Helianthus annuus*; scales), beggarticks (*Bidens*; barbed awns) |

The **corolla** lies inside or above the calyx; petals are thinner than sepals and are mostly brightly colored. The epidermal cells of petals are often papillate and refract light, giving the petals a velvety sheen; the base of the petal often bears a **nectary**, and the epidermal cells of many petals also secrete volatile oils that produce fragrance. Besides protecting the stamens and pistil, the color, scent and nectar of the corolla are signals and rewards that attract pollinators ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)).

Flower color is determined by a few classes of pigments. Yellow and orange come mostly from carotenoids in chromoplasts; red, purple and blue come mostly from anthocyanins (a class of flavonoids) in the vacuole, whose color changes with the pH of the cell sap, red when acidic and blue when alkaline, and is also affected by metal ions and copigments. Flavonols, another class of flavonoids, are pale yellow or creamy white and absorb ultraviolet light strongly, forming ultraviolet patterns that are invisible to humans but visible to bees. The Caryophyllales (except for the Caryophyllaceae and a few other families) lack anthocyanins, and their red and yellow colors come from betalains ([7.12.45](/7.12.45)). When neither kind of pigment is present, air in the intercellular spaces of the petal scatters light and the flower appears white.

According to whether the petals are free or fused and to symmetry, corollas are divided into choripetalous and sympetalous, and regular (actinomorphic) and irregular (zygomorphic) types (the first three in the table below are choripetalous, the rest sympetalous):

| Corolla type | Characteristics | Symmetry | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rosaceous | petals 5 (or more), free, spreading radially | radial | peach, plum (*Prunus mume*), rose |
| Cruciform | petals 4, free, arranged in a cross | radial | rapeseed, Chinese cabbage (*Brassica rapa*) (Brassicaceae) |
| Papilionaceous | petals 5, free: the uppermost and largest is the standard, the two lateral ones are wings, and the two lowest are fused at the tip to form the keel | bilateral | pea, broad bean (*Vicia faba*), black locust (*Robinia pseudoacacia*) (Fabaceae, subfamily Faboideae) |
| Rotate (wheel-shaped) | corolla tube very short, lobes spreading flat from the base | radial | eggplant, tomato, goji (*Lycium*) |
| Salverform (hypocrateriform) | corolla tube long and slender, the upper part suddenly flattened into a disc | radial | primrose (*Primula*), Madagascar periwinkle (*Catharanthus roseus*), winter jasmine (*Jasminum nudiflorum*) |
| Funnelform | corolla tube gradually widening upward from the base | radial | morning glory (*Ipomoea*), bindweed (*Calystegia*), jimsonweed (*Datura*) |
| Campanulate | corolla tube broad and short, the upper part widened into a bell | radial | bellflower (*Campanula*), balloon flower (*Platycodon*), codonopsis (*Codonopsis*) |
| Tubular | most of the corolla fused into a tube | radial | disc florets (tubular florets) of sunflower, birthwort (*Aristolochia*) |
| Urceolate | corolla tube swollen and ovoid, constricted into a short neck above | radial | persimmon, blueberry relatives (*Vaccinium*), *Pieris* (Ericaceae) |
| Bilabiate (two-lipped) | upper part of the corolla tube divided into an upper and a lower lip (often 2/3 lobed) | bilateral | motherwort (*Leonurus*), dead-nettle (*Lamium*), scarlet sage (*Salvia splendens*) (Lamiaceae) |
| Ligulate | corolla tube short, the upper part extended to one side into a flat strap | bilateral | the flower of dandelion, ray florets of sunflower |
| Spurred | base of the petals (or corolla) extended into a spur | bilateral or radial | pansy (*Viola*), columbine (*Aquilegia*), toadflax (*Linaria*) |

According to time of abscission, corollas are also caducous (grape, *Vitis*), deciduous (peach) or persistent (cucumber, *Cucumis sativus*, in which the corolla remains at the top of the fruit).

### 27.2.3. Aestivation

The way in which sepals or petals (tepals) are arranged relative to one another in the flower bud is called aestivation. It is fixed before the flower opens, is an important diagnostic character of families and genera, and can be shown directly in floral diagrams (Section 27.6.2):

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Valvate | margins of the members meet without overlapping | grape corolla, calyx of Malvaceae, balloon flower |
| Contorted (twisted) | one edge of each member overlaps its neighbor and the other edge is overlapped by another, in sequence around the flower; Chinese textbooks also call it "spiral" | Apocynaceae, corolla of Malvaceae, morning glory, gentian (*Gentiana*) |
| Imbricate | similar to contorted, but at least one member is wholly outside and one wholly inside | calyx of Chinese cabbage, camellia (*Camellia*) |
| Quincuncial | a kind of imbricate aestivation: of 5 members, 2 are wholly outside, 2 wholly inside, and 1 is partly outside and partly inside | calyx of rose and peach |

Imbricate arrangement provides a well-known diagnostic character in the Fabaceae. In the subfamily Faboideae the corolla is "descending imbricate": the uppermost standard petal is outermost and covers the lateral wings, which in turn cover the lowest keel petals. In the traditional Caesalpinioideae the corolla is "ascending imbricate", the uppermost petal being innermost ([7.12.42](/7.12.42)).

### 27.2.4. Androecium and Stamens

**Definition 27.2.3 (Stamen and Androecium)** The pollen-producing organ of the flower is called the **stamen**. It corresponds to a microsporophyll and typically consists of a slender **filament** and an enlarged terminal **anther**; the anther is usually composed of four pollen sacs (microsporangia) divided into two halves (two thecae) joined in the middle by the **connective**. The stamens of a flower collectively form the **androecium**.

The androecium lies inside or above the perianth, with its members arranged spirally or in whorls on the receptacle. Stamens are usually inserted directly on the receptacle; some are inserted on the corolla (epipetalous stamens, as in Solanaceae, Lamiaceae and Primulaceae), and some are fused with the pistil into a gynostemium (Orchidaceae and the milkweed group). In a few primitive angiosperms the stamen is not differentiated into filament and anther but is a flat, leaf-like blade with the pollen sacs embedded in its adaxial or abaxial surface, as in the Australian genera *Degeneria*, *Austrobaileya* and *Galbulimima* (*Himantandra*); the stamens of *Magnolia* are also broad and flat with an extended connective. Such stamens support the view that the stamen is a modified leaf. The filament also varies: in the lotus it is flat and ribbon-like, in gardenia (*Gardenia*) it almost disappears, and in canna (*Canna*) most of the stamens are petaloid, with only one stamen retaining half an anther on one side. A stamen that has lost its anther and cannot produce pollen is called a staminode; it often becomes nectary-like or petal-like, as in *Scrophularia* and canna.

The number, relative length and fusion of stamens are important taxonomic characters (Figure 3A):

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Free (distinct) stamens | stamens separate from one another | buttercup (*Ranunculus*), rice (*Oryza sativa*), wheat (*Triticum aestivum*), peach |
| Monadelphous | filaments fused into one bundle (often a tube surrounding the style), anthers free | cotton, rose of Sharon (*Hibiscus syriacus*) (Malvaceae), camellia |
| Diadelphous | filaments fused into two bundles, often 9 of 10 stamens fused and 1 free | pea, broad bean (Faboideae); in *Corydalis*, 6 stamens in two bundles |
| Triadelphous | filaments fused into three bundles | *Hypericum* (a few species) |
| Polyadelphous | filaments fused into four or more bundles | St John's wort (*Hypericum*), castor bean (*Ricinus communis*), linden (*Tilia*) |
| Syngenesious | filaments free, anthers fused into a tube | Asteraceae, *Lobelia* (Campanulaceae) |
| Didynamous | 4 stamens, 2 long and 2 short | Lamiaceae, Scrophulariaceae, Verbenaceae |
| Tetradynamous | 6 stamens, the inner whorl 4 long, the outer whorl 2 short | Brassicaceae |

The stamens of the Cucurbitaceae are often described as "syngenesious", but the situation is more complex: in most genera, of 5 stamens, 4 are fused in pairs and 1 is free, appearing as "3 stamens"; the thecae are often sinuous, and in some genera (such as *Cucurbita*) the anthers are fused entirely into a head ([7.12.43](/7.12.43)).

Anthers are attached to the filament in three ways (Figure 3B). When the tip of the filament joins the base of the anther, the anther is basifixed (innate), as in barberry (*Berberis*) and sedges (Cyperaceae); when the back of the anther is attached along the filament, it is dorsifixed (adnate), as in *Magnolia*; when the tip of the filament is joined to a single point at the middle of the back of the anther so that the anther can swing, it is versatile, as in lily and grasses, whose anthers shake easily in the wind or when touched by insects and so shed their pollen. An anther that opens toward the pistil is introrse, and one that opens toward the corolla is extrorse.

When the anther is mature it dehisces in a characteristic way and releases the pollen (Figure 3C):

| Mode of dehiscence | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Longitudinal | splits lengthwise along the junction of the two pollen sacs; the commonest type | rapeseed, morning glory, lily |
| Transverse | splits crosswise across the middle of the anther | rose of Sharon, hollyhock, cotton |
| Poricidal | a small pore opens at the tip of the anther and the pollen escapes through it | eggplant, tomato, rhododendron (*Rhododendron*) |
| Valvular | the side wall of a theca splits into several flaps that lift upward, and the pollen escapes through the openings beneath | camphor (*Cinnamomum camphora*), barberry |

Poricidal anthers usually need bees to "shake out" the pollen by vibrating their thoracic muscles, which is called buzz pollination ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)). How the anther wall develops and why it dehisces are described in [7.12.28](/7.12.28).

### 27.2.5. Gynoecium and Pistil

**Definition 27.2.4 (Carpel, Pistil and Gynoecium)** The basic unit of which the pistil is built is called the **carpel**. It is a modified leaf with a reproductive function, corresponds to a megasporophyll, and has its margins rolled inward and fused so as to enclose the ovules. A structure formed from one or several carpels and differentiated into three parts, **stigma**, **style** and **ovary**, is called a **pistil**. The pistils of a flower collectively form the **gynoecium**.

The suture formed where the carpel margins fuse is called the ventral suture, and the ovules are borne along it; the opposite suture, corresponding to the midrib of a leaf, is called the dorsal suture. Both sutures contain vascular bundles: a stouter dorsal bundle runs along the dorsal suture, and a ventral bundle lies on each side of the ventral suture; they supply water and nutrients to the carpel and branch into the ovules. The same two kinds of suture can be seen in a pistil of fused carpels: the ventral sutures lie at the junctions of adjacent carpels, so that a pistil of 3 carpels has 3 ventral and 3 dorsal sutures.

According to the number and fusion of carpels, the gynoecium is of three kinds:

- A simple pistil: a flower has a single pistil formed of one carpel, as in pea, soybean (*Glycine max*) and peach.
- An apocarpous gynoecium: a flower has several carpels, separate from one another, each forming its own pistil, as in *Magnolia*, buttercup, lotus and strawberry. Each pistil is still a simple pistil.
- A syncarpous pistil, also called a compound pistil: a flower has a single pistil formed by the fusion of two or more carpels, as in cotton, tomato and lily. The degree of fusion varies: in some, ovary, style and stigma are all fused (tomato); in some the ovary and style are fused and the stigmas are free (sunflower, pumpkin); and in some only the ovary is fused while styles and stigmas are free (pink, pear).

To determine the number of carpels in a syncarpous pistil, one examines the number of divisions of the style and stigma and the ribs or grooves on the outside of the ovary, then cuts the ovary transversely and counts the locules, placentae and dorsal bundles; the several results usually corroborate one another.

The mode of carpel closure in early angiosperms differs from that in most angiosperms. In most angiosperms the carpel folds inward from its margins like a folded leaf and the margins fuse on the ventral side; this is the plicate carpel. In the ANA grade (*Amborella*, Nymphaeales, Austrobaileyales) the carpels are mostly ascidiate (urn-shaped), and the opening of the carpel is sealed not by tissue fusion but by secretion (Endress 2005). The "angiospermy" of angiosperms (enclosure of ovules by carpels) may originally have been just such a secretory seal ([7.12.34](/7.12.34)).

The stigma lies at the apex of the pistil and is the site where pollen is received; it is often enlarged or expanded into a head, disc, feather or branched shape, and its epidermal cells are often extended into papillae or hairs. The surface of the stigma at pollination falls into two types (Heslop-Harrison and Shivanna 1977):

- Wet stigma: the epidermal cells secrete water, sugars, lipids, phenolics, hormones, enzymes and so on, which hold the pollen and provide a substrate for its germination, as in tobacco (*Nicotiana*), lily, apple and legumes.
- Dry stigma: no secretion is produced, but the surface carries a hydrophilic protein film (the pellicle), and pollen takes up water through gaps in the cuticle beneath it. This type is common in angiosperms, for example in Brassicaceae, Caryophyllaceae, castor bean, rose (*Rosa chinensis*) and pineapple (*Ananas comosus*), and in grasses such as rice, wheat and maize.

The style is the part between stigma and ovary and is the channel through which the pollen tube enters the ovary. It is usually long and slender; the style of maize (together with its stigma, the "corn silk") is especially long, whereas that of rice and wheat is very short. Styles are of two kinds. In most eudicots (cotton, tobacco, tomato, shepherd's purse *Capsella bursa-pastoris*) the style is solid, its center filled with slender, thin-walled transmitting tissue rich in protoplasm and starch, and the pollen tube grows through its intercellular spaces. In lilies, fritillaries (*Fritillaria*) and many other monocots, and in some Papaveraceae, Aristolochiaceae and Fabaceae, the style is hollow, with a stylar canal at the center whose surface is lined with epidermis or several layers of secretory cells, and the pollen tube grows along that surface. In either case the style supplies nutrients and guidance cues to the pollen tube ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)).

The **ovary** is the enlarged basal part of the pistil and consists of the ovary wall, locules, placentae and ovules; after fertilization the ovary wall develops into the pericarp and the ovules into seeds. The hollow space in the ovary is called a locule. The ovary of a simple pistil has a single locule. The ovary of a syncarpous pistil may be unilocular (cucumber, sweet violet *Viola philippica*), or multilocular where the carpel margins grow inward and fuse in the center (tobacco 2 locules, lily 3, evening primrose *Oenothera* 4, garden balsam 5). In some, the ovary is originally unilocular and later divided into two locules by a false septum formed from tissue other than the carpels, as in rapeseed and other Brassicaceae and in milkvetch (*Astragalus*); in others an originally multilocular ovary loses its septa and becomes unilocular, as in pink and primrose. The inner and outer surfaces of the ovary wall bear epidermis, stomata and hairs.

### 27.2.6. Placentation

**Definition 27.2.5 (Placenta)** The site in the ovary where the ovules are borne is called the placenta and often forms a fleshy protuberance; the way in which the ovules are attached in the ovary is called the type of **placentation**.

The type of placentation is determined by the number of carpels, the way they are fused and the number of locules (Figure 4B):

| Placentation type | Pistil | Locules | Position of ovules | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marginal | one carpel | 1 | in a row along the ventral suture of the carpel | pea, broad bean (Fabaceae) |
| Parietal | fused carpels | 1 | in several longitudinal rows on the ovary wall along the ventral sutures of adjacent carpels | opium poppy, sweet violet, cucumber, passionflower |
| Axile | fused carpels | several | carpel margins fused in the center of the ovary into a central axis, the ovules borne on the axis (at the inner angle of each locule) | narcissus (*Narcissus*), lily, iris (*Iris*), cotton, citrus, tomato |
| *Free central* | fused carpels | 1 (septa lost) | around a central axis (or short column formed from the receptacle) that is not joined to the ovary wall | pink, purslane (*Portulaca*), primrose |
| Basal | one carpel or fused carpels | 1 | at the base of the ovary | sunflower (Asteraceae), buckwheat |
| Apical (pendulous) | one carpel or fused carpels | 1 | at the top of the ovary, hanging in the locule | mulberry, elm (*Ulmus*), daphne (*Daphne*) |
| Laminar (superficial) | free or fused carpels | 1 or several | over the inner surface of the carpels or over all faces of the septa | Gorgon plant (*Euryale ferox*), water lily (*Nymphaea*), water plantain relatives (*Butomus*) |

The evolutionary trend in placentation is roughly: laminar or marginal placentation in free carpels → axile placentation in fused carpels; loss of the septa of axile placentation gives free central placentation, carpels joined only at the margins give parietal placentation, and reduction of the ovules to one gives basal or apical placentation. Laminar placentation occurs in early-diverging groups such as the Nymphaeales but has also arisen secondarily in several groups.

B: types of placentation in cross sections of the ovary: marginal (one carpel, ovules on the ventral suture), parietal (fused carpels, one locule, ovules on the ovary wall at the junctions of adjacent carpels), axile (fused carpels, several locules, ovules on the central axis), free central (one locule, ovules on a free central column) and basal (one locule, a single ovule at the base of the ovary)](./plots/ovary-placentation.py)

### 27.2.7. Ovary Position

Changes in the shape of the receptacle alter the relative positions of the floral parts on it. The most important of these is the height of the ovary relative to the insertion of the calyx, corolla and stamens (Figure 4A).

**Definition 27.2.6 (Ovary Position)** When the ovary is attached to the receptacle only by its base, and the calyx, corolla and stamens are inserted on the receptacle below the ovary, the ovary is said to be superior. When the ovary is sunk in a concave receptacle (or receptacle tube), the ovary wall is completely fused with it, and the calyx, corolla and stamens are inserted above the ovary, the ovary is inferior. When the lower half of the ovary is fused with the receptacle tube and the upper half is free, the ovary is half-inferior. Correspondingly, according to the insertion of the perianth and stamens, a flower with a superior ovary in which the floral parts are inserted below the ovary is a **hypogynous flower**; a flower whose parts are inserted on the rim of a cup-shaped receptacle (hypanthium) that surrounds the ovary but is not fused with it is a **perigynous flower**; and a flower whose parts are inserted above the ovary is an **epigynous flower**.

| Ovary position | Flower type | Receptacle | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Superior | hypogynous | cylindrical, domed or flat-topped | buttercup, rapeseed, peony (*Paeonia*), broad bean, *Magnolia* |
| Superior | perigynous | cup- or bowl-shaped, free from the ovary | peach, cherry, rose |
| Half-inferior | perigynous | cup-shaped, the lower half fused with the ovary | saxifrage (*Saxifraga*), balloon flower, purslane |
| Inferior | epigynous | deep cup, completely fused with the ovary wall | pear (*Pyrus*), apple, cucumber, sunflower, Orchidaceae |

"Superior" and "inferior" describe two different objects here and are easily confused: a flower with a superior ovary is hypogynous, and a flower with an inferior ovary is epigynous. Chinese textbooks also call a flower with a half-inferior ovary perigynous, so the ovary of a perigynous flower may be superior or half-inferior.

The cup-like structure around the ovary is called the floral cup or hypanthium. By vascular anatomy it has two origins: in most groups it is formed by the fusion of the bases of the calyx, corolla and stamens (appendicular origin), as in the Rosaceae, whereas in a few groups it is formed of true receptacular tissue (axial origin), as in the Cactaceae and some Santalaceae. An inferior ovary likewise has two origins; the two are hard to tell apart externally, and one must look at whether the vascular bundles "turn back" in the ovary wall.

The general evolutionary trend of the receptacle is toward shortening, widening and central concavity, so that ovary position evolves from superior to half-inferior and inferior (Section 27.4). An inferior ovary gives the ovules double protection and, together with a tubular corolla and the position of the nectary, is an adaptation to pollinators that probe deep into the flower; it has arisen independently many times in angiosperms (Maloideae of the Rosaceae, Cucurbitaceae, Asteraceae, Orchidaceae, Apiaceae and others).

## 27.3. Flower Sex and Symmetry

### 27.3.1. Flower Sex

According to whether a flower has fertile stamens and pistils, flowers fall into the following kinds:

**Definition 27.3.1 (Bisexual, Unisexual and Neutral Flowers)** A flower that has both fertile stamens and a fertile pistil is called a **bisexual flower** (also a perfect flower); a flower that has only fertile stamens or only a fertile pistil is called a **unisexual flower**, the former being a male (staminate) flower and the latter a female (pistillate) flower; a flower in which both stamens and pistil are reduced or poorly developed is called a **neutral flower** (also an asexual flower), as in the ray florets at the edge of a sunflower head and the sterile flowers of hydrangea (*Hydrangea*).

Unisexual flowers are distributed on plants in several ways. When female and male flowers are borne on the same plant, the species is **monoecious**, as in maize (*Zea mays*), castor bean, pumpkin (*Cucurbita*) and oak (*Quercus*); when they are borne on different plants, the species is **dioecious**, as in poplar, willow, mulberry, ginkgo (*Ginkgo biloba*, a gymnosperm), spinach (*Spinacia oleracea*) and hemp. A plant bearing both bisexual and unisexual flowers is polygamous, as in maple (*Acer*) and persimmon; bisexual and male flowers on the same plant is andromonoecy (horse chestnut, *Aesculus*, and many Apiaceae), and bisexual and female flowers on the same plant is gynomonoecy (many Asteraceae). At the level of the population there are also gynodioecy (some plants bear only female flowers and others bisexual flowers, as in thyme, *Thymus*, and some species of *Fragaria*) and androdioecy. Dioecy accounts for only 5%–6% of angiosperm species (Renner 2014), yet it is scattered through about half of all families and has evolved from bisexual ancestors hundreds to a thousand times independently; its sex determination and sex chromosomes are treated in [7.4.6](/7.4.6).

In floral formulae, ♂ denotes a male flower and ♀ a female flower; a bisexual flower is left unmarked or written ⚥ (Section 27.6.1).

### 27.3.2. Floral Symmetry

**Definition 27.3.2 (Floral Symmetry)** A flower through whose center more than two planes of symmetry can be drawn is called an **actinomorphic flower** (also a regular or polysymmetric flower); a flower through whose center only one plane of symmetry can be drawn is called a **zygomorphic flower** (also an irregular or monosymmetric flower); a flower without any plane of symmetry is called an asymmetric flower.

Actinomorphic flowers include peach, plum, lily and rapeseed (the parts of each whorl alternate on the receptacle according to the floral ground plan). Zygomorphic flowers include the papilionaceous corolla of the pea, the bilabiate corolla of the Lamiaceae and the flowers of the Orchidaceae; the plane of symmetry is usually the median plane passing through the axis of the inflorescence (or the parent axis) and the bract. Some flowers allow only two mutually perpendicular planes of symmetry and are called disymmetric flowers, as in bleeding heart (*Lamprocapnos*, formerly *Dicentra*) and close relatives of *Corydalis*, in which the flower is symmetrical top to bottom and left to right separately. Asymmetric flowers are exemplified by canna: the perianth is nearly actinomorphic, but the partial petaloidy of the stamens, with only one retaining half an anther, leaves the whole flower without a plane of symmetry; the flowers of valerian (*Valeriana*) and of some legumes are also asymmetric.

Evolutionarily, radial symmetry is the ancestral state and bilateral symmetry is derived; bilateral symmetry has arisen independently many times in angiosperms and is mostly associated with oriented visits by pollinators such as bees: a zygomorphic flower gives the pollinator a fixed landing orientation so that pollen is always placed on the same part of its body, which increases the precision of pollination. In snapdragon, bilateral symmetry is determined by the expression of genes such as *CYCLOIDEA* (*CYC*) in the adaxial region of the flower, and *cyc* mutants have radially symmetrical flowers (Luo et al. 1996). Linnaeus described a radially symmetrical variant of toadflax (*Linaria vulgaris*), called peloria; more than 200 years later it was found to be an epigenetic mutant of the gene *Lcyc*, in which the DNA sequence is unchanged but the gene is silenced by methylation (Cubas et al. 1999).

## 27.4. Evolutionary Trends in Floral Parts

Floral form is fairly constant within a species and is related to evolution, so angiosperm classification relies to a large extent on floral morphology. Traditionally (the "guiding principles" of Bessey 1915 and the systems of Takhtajan and Cronquist), the following trends have been used to judge which character states are primitive and which advanced ([7.12.33](/7.12.33) systematizes them):

| Aspect | Primitive state | Advanced state | Notes and examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number | floral parts many and indefinite in number | few and definite, usually 3, 4, 5 or multiples | stamens and pistils many in *Magnolia*, lotus and buttercup; the relatively fixed number of floral parts is called the floral merism: usually 3 in monocots and 4 or 5 in eudicots; the number of carpels is often smaller than the merism and the number of stamens often twice as large |
| Arrangement | spiral, receptacle elongated | whorled, receptacle flat-topped or concave | Magnoliaceae and Ranunculaceae still retain spiral arrangement; in whorled arrangements the members of adjacent whorls alternate |
| Symmetry | radial | bilateral, asymmetric | papilionaceous and bilabiate flowers, orchid flowers |
| Ovary position | superior (hypogynous flower) | half-inferior, inferior (perigynous, epigynous flowers) | apple, pear, Asteraceae, Orchidaceae |
| Fusion | parts free (choripetalous, free stamens, free carpels) | fused (sympetalous, monadelphous or syngenesious stamens, syncarpous) | sympetalous corollas in the asterids and others |
| Perianth | perianth differentiated into calyx and corolla | secondarily simplified to monochlamydeous or achlamydeous | wind-pollinated flowers of poplar and willow, walnut and birch |
| Sex | bisexual | unisexual | monoecy and dioecy are secondarily derived |

Alternation of the members of adjacent whorls (sepals alternate with petals, petals with stamens) is a general rule. Apparent exceptions are often the result of the loss of one whorl: iris originally had two whorls of stamens, and after the inner whorl is lost the outer stamens stand opposite the carpels; in primrose the outer whorl of stamens is lost and the inner stamens stand opposite the petals. The floral merism also often fails to "fit" because of partial reduction: the merism of the Brassicaceae is 4 and two whorls of stamens should give 8, but the 2 stamens of the outer whorl are lost, leaving 6 (tetradynamous); in lilac (*Syringa*) the merism is 4 but only 2 stamens remain. Transformations between floral parts are common in cultivated plants: in the double flowers of peony and rose the stamens decrease and the petals increase; in the flower of water lily a series of intermediate forms from petals to stamens can be seen, in which the filaments gradually flatten while the tips retain anthers.

Three cautions apply when using these trends. First, the parts of a flower do not evolve in step, and a flower is often a mosaic of primitive and advanced characters: in apple and pear, the free calyx and corolla and the many stamens are primitive, while the inferior ovary is advanced. Second, the trends are not irreversible: there is molecular phylogenetic evidence for reversal from bilateral to radial symmetry, from unisexual to bisexual flowers and from sympetalous to choripetalous corollas, and "simple" flowers (achlamydeous, unisexual) are mostly the result of secondary simplification, so the Cronquist system's placement of the catkin-bearing groups in a primitive position has been abandoned. Third, these rules are only corollaries of the hypothesis that "the primitive angiosperm resembled a magnolia" (the euanthial theory) and must be tested against phylogeny. Sauquet et al. (2017) reconstructed, within a molecular phylogenetic framework, the flower of the common ancestor of all extant angiosperms: bisexual and actinomorphic, with a perianth of several whorls of tepals not differentiated into calyx and corolla, each whorl of 3 members, with stamens in several whorls of 3 each and 5 or more free carpels arranged spirally. This "ancestral flower" agrees with the table above in most respects, but its perianth and stamens are whorled rather than spiral ([7.12.34](/7.12.34)).

## 27.5. The Flower of Grasses

The flower of the grass family Poaceae ([7.12.39](/7.12.39)) is highly specialized and quite different from the general form described above. Wheat, rice, maize and many weeds are grasses; we take wheat as the example below (Figure 5D shows it as a floral diagram).

**Definition 27.5.1 (Spikelet and Floret)** The basic unit of the grass inflorescence is called the **spikelet**: at the base of the spikelet axis are two firm bracts called **glumes**, the lower being the outer (first) glume and the upper the inner (second) glume; above the glumes are borne one to several **florets**. Each floret is enclosed by two scale-like bracts: the outer is called the **lemma** and the inner the **palea**; inside the palea are 2 (–3) small, sac-like **lodicules**, which absorb water and swell at anthesis, forcing the lemma and palea apart.

The wheat ear (a compound spike, Section 27.7) has a main axis bearing many alternate spikelets. Each spikelet has 2 glumes and, inside them, 3–9 florets, of which the basal 2–3 set seed normally, while the upper ones are often sterile florets with only a lemma and palea and no stamens or pistil. In some cultivars the midrib of the lemma extends into an awn. A fertile floret has 3 stamens with long, slender filaments and large, versatile anthers that hang outside the lemma and palea at anthesis and release large quantities of pollen in the wind; the single pistil has a unilocular ovary with 1 ovule, an inconspicuous style and 2 feathery stigmas on top, suited to catching wind-borne pollen.

The rice spikelet has only 1 fertile floret: the glumes are reduced to two crescent-shaped protuberances at the tip of the spikelet stalk, above which are 2 lanceolate sterile lemmas (the "rudimentary glumes" or sterile lemmas), and the fertile floret has a lemma, a palea, 2 lodicules, 6 stamens and a pistil with 2 plumose stigmas. The maize flower is unisexual and the plant monoecious: male spikelets are borne in pairs on a terminal panicle (the tassel), and female spikelets in rows on the stout axis of an ear in a leaf axil (the ear, enclosed by husks); each female flower has a very long style that extends beyond the husks, the "corn silk".

The homologies of the parts of the grass floret have long been debated. The lemma is the bract of the floret, which is not in doubt. Chinese textbooks generally hold that the palea and lodicules are both reduced perianth members: the palea corresponds to the outer perianth whorl (or to two fused members) and the lodicules to the inner whorl. English-language literature mostly regards the palea as a prophyll (bracteole). Molecular evidence supports the homology of lodicules with petals: in rice and maize, B-class MADS-box genes are expressed in the lodicules, and in the maize B-class mutant *silky1* the lodicules become lemma-like and the stamens become carpels, as the ABC model predicts (Ambrose et al. 2000); the nature of the palea remains unsettled.

## 27.6. Floral Formulae and Floral Diagrams

To describe the structure of a flower concisely, the composition, number, arrangement, fusion and ovary position of its parts can be written as a formula or drawn as a diagram, called a floral formula and a floral diagram respectively. They are the standard forms in which the characters of families and genera are described in angiosperm classification.

### 27.6.1. The Floral Formula

**Definition 27.6.1 (Floral Formula)** A formula that uses letters, numbers and symbols to express the composition, number, fusion, number of whorls, symmetry and ovary position of the parts of a flower is called a **floral formula**.

The notation of floral formulae differs among textbooks. This course uses throughout the notation of Chinese textbooks (that of the *Botany* of Shiwan Lu et al., Vol. 1, and of Chinese textbooks of seed plant taxonomy), and the family characters in the following chapters are all written in it:

**Definition 27.6.2 (The Floral Formula Notation of This Course)** A floral formula is written from left to right as: sex (may be omitted), symmetry, calyx, corolla (or perianth), androecium, gynoecium, with a space between items.

1. **Letters**: $K$ denotes the calyx (German *Kelch*), $C$ the corolla, $P$ the perianth (used in place of $K$ and $C$ when calyx and corolla are not distinguished), $A$ the androecium and $G$ the gynoecium.
2. **Numbers**: written as a right subscript to the letter, giving the number of members of that part; $\infty$ denotes many (generally more than 10) or an indefinite number, and $0$ denotes absence or reduction; when the number varies it is written $K_{4\text{–}5}$.
3. **Fusion**: a number enclosed in parentheses indicates that the part is fused, so $C_{(5)}$ means a sympetalous corolla with 5 lobes.
4. **Whorls**: when a part has several whorls or groups, the numbers for the whorls (groups) are joined by $+$, so $P_{3+3}$ means two perianth whorls of 3 members each, and $A_{(9)+1}$ means diadelphous stamens.
5. **Pistil**: the subscript of $G$ gives, in order, the number of carpels, the number of locules and the number of ovules per locule, separated by colons (the last two may be omitted); when the carpels are fused the whole is enclosed in parentheses, as in $G_{(5:5:2)}$, and for a single carpel or free carpels there are no parentheses, as in $G_{1:1:\infty}$ and $G_{\infty}$.
6. **Ovary position**: a superior ovary is shown by a bar under $G$, $\underline{G}$; an inferior ovary by a bar over $G$, $\overline{G}$; a half-inferior ovary by bars both over and under $G$, $\overline{\underline{G}}$. The notation follows the position of the ovary, so a perigynous flower, whose ovary is mostly superior, is still written $\underline{G}$.
7. **Symmetry**: radial symmetry is written $\ast$ and bilateral symmetry $\uparrow$, placed before the calyx.
8. **Sex**: a male flower is written ♂ and a female flower ♀; a bisexual flower is left unmarked (or written ⚥); for unisexual flowers the female and male flowers are written separately, a monoecious species is written "♂ …; ♀ …", and dioecy is noted in the accompanying text.

Examples (Figure 5):

| Plant | Floral formula | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Lily | $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$ | radially symmetric; perianth of 6 free members in two whorls of 3; 6 stamens in two whorls; pistil of 3 fused carpels, ovary superior, 3 locules, many ovules per locule |
| Peach | $\ast K_5 C_5 A_\infty \underline{G}_{1:1:2}$ | radially symmetric; 5 sepals, 5 petals, all free; many stamens; simple pistil, ovary superior (perigynous flower), 1 locule, 2 ovules |
| Apple | $\ast K_5 C_5 A_\infty \overline{G}_{(5:5:2)}$ | radially symmetric; 5 sepals, 5 petals; many stamens; 5 fused carpels, ovary inferior, 5 locules, 2 ovules per locule |
| Broad bean | $\uparrow K_{(5)} C_{1+2+(2)} A_{(9)+1} \underline{G}_{1:1:\infty}$ | bilaterally symmetric; 5 fused sepals; corolla of 5 members: 1 standard and 2 free wings, 2 keel petals fused; diadelphous stamens; single carpel, ovary superior, 1 locule, many ovules |
| Rapeseed | $\ast K_{2+2} C_4 A_{2+4} \underline{G}_{(2:2:\infty)}$ | radially symmetric; 4 sepals in two whorls; cruciform corolla; tetradynamous stamens (outer whorl 2, inner whorl 4); 2 fused carpels, ovary superior, divided into 2 locules by a false septum |
| Mulberry | ♂ $\ast P_4 A_4$; ♀ $\ast P_4 \underline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$ | unisexual flowers; male flower with 4 tepals and 4 stamens; female flower with 4 tepals and 2 fused carpels, ovary superior, 1 locule, 1 ovule |
| Sunflower (disc floret) | $\ast K_0 C_{(5)} A_{(5)} \overline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$ | radially symmetric; sepals modified into a pappus (scale-like, often recorded as 0 or noted separately); sympetalous corolla; 5 syngenesious stamens; 2 fused carpels, ovary inferior, 1 locule, 1 ovule |

Syngenesious stamens (anthers fused) are written $A_{(5)}$ in this notation, like monadelphous stamens, with a note in the text where needed; epipetalous stamens (inserted on the corolla) are likewise indicated only in the text. In describing a family, numbers are often written as ranges or with $n$, as in the Rosaceae, $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{5\text{–}\infty} G_{1\text{–}\infty}$ (no bar is drawn when the ovary position varies within the family).

Other textbooks write it differently. English-language textbooks often write "CA 5 CO 5 A ∞ G 1" (Judd et al.) or use Ca and Co for calyx and corolla, with the bar for ovary position drawn beneath or above the carpel number; some Chinese sources write ⚥ at the very beginning for bisexual flowers. When consulting such sources one need only rewrite them according to the conventions above.

### 27.6.2. The Floral Diagram

**Definition 27.6.3 (Floral Diagram)** A schematic cross section of a flower that shows the number, fusion, arrangement and relative positions of its parts is called a **floral diagram**. A floral diagram is the projection of the parts of the flower onto a plane perpendicular to the floral axis.

Conventions for drawing a floral diagram (Figure 5):

1. The floral axis (the inflorescence axis or the parent axis bearing the flower) is represented by a small circle at the top of the diagram; the bract is drawn at the bottom as a crescent-shaped arc with a central protrusion (the midrib), and bracteoles are drawn at the left and right. A terminal flower is drawn without axis or bract.
2. The calyx is represented by crescent-shaped arcs with a midrib (or a short line), and the corolla by solid crescent-shaped arcs; free members are drawn separately, and fused ones are linked by a line joining adjacent arcs; a spur is shown by an extension of the arc; the aestivation (valvate, imbricate, contorted) can also be drawn according to the overlap of the members.
3. Stamens are represented by cross sections of the anthers, showing the number of whorls, fusion and whether they are opposite or alternate with the petals; epipetalous stamens are joined to the petals by a line, and staminodes are shown by small dots or dashed circles.
4. The pistil is represented by a cross section of the ovary, showing the number of carpels, fusion, number of locules, type of placentation and attachment of the ovules.
5. The plane of symmetry of a zygomorphic flower passes through the floral axis and the bract and can be marked with a dashed line.

A floral diagram contains more information than a floral formula: it shows the relative positions of the members of the whorls (opposite or alternate), the aestivation of the perianth and the orientation of the stamens, which makes it easy to compare flowers of different groups and to infer which whorl has been lost. For example, lily and iris have similar floral formulae, but the floral diagrams show that iris has only the outer whorl of 3 stamens, opposite the outer tepals; the floral diagram of a papilionaceous flower shows the odd sepal on the abaxial (lower) side and the standard on the adaxial (upper) side.

## 27.7. Inflorescences

### 27.7.1. Structure of the Inflorescence

Among angiosperms, the flowers of some species are borne singly at the tip of a shoot or in a leaf axil, and are called solitary flowers, as in *Magnolia*, tree peony, peony, lotus and peach; in most plants the flowers are arranged in a definite pattern on a common stalk.

**Definition 27.7.1 (Inflorescence)** The way in which flowers are arranged on the floral axis is called the **inflorescence**. The common stalk or main axis of the inflorescence is called the rachis; the part below the rachis that bears no flowers is called the peduncle, and the stalk of each flower is called a pedicel; the modified leaf beneath each flower or each branch of the inflorescence is called a bract, and bracts clustered at the base of the inflorescence form an involucre.

Bracts and involucres protect the flower buds or fruits. Some of the leaves below an inflorescence are reduced, and others are very large and colored, serving as "petals" that attract pollinators: the large bract outside the inflorescence of the Araceae is called the spathe, as in calla lily (*Zantedeschia*) and anthurium (*Anthurium*); the purple-red "petals" of bougainvillea (*Bougainvillea*) are three bracts; and the red "flower" at the top of poinsettia is made of bracts. The involucral bracts (phyllaries) outside the capitulum of the Asteraceae are also bracts. The glumes at the base of the grass spikelet are likewise specialized bracts.

There are many types of inflorescence, differing in the length of the axis, branching or not, the presence or absence of pedicels on the flowers, the sequence of flowering and so on. According to whether the tip of the inflorescence axis continues to grow and the order in which flowers open, inflorescences fall into two broad classes, indeterminate and determinate (Figure 6).

### 27.7.2. Indeterminate Inflorescences

**Definition 27.7.2 (Indeterminate Inflorescence)** An inflorescence whose axis continues to grow during flowering and keeps producing new bracts and flower buds, with flowers opening in sequence from the base of the axis toward the apex or, if the axis is shortened, from the margin toward the center, is called an **indeterminate inflorescence** (also racemose or monopodial inflorescence).

The growth of an indeterminate inflorescence resembles monopodial branching. Inflorescences whose axis is unbranched are called simple inflorescences:

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Raceme | axis long and unbranched, bearing from bottom to top many flowers with pedicels of about equal length | wisteria (*Wisteria sinensis*), shepherd's purse, rapeseed, Chinese cabbage |
| Corymb | similar to a raceme, but the lower pedicels are longer and the upper progressively shorter, so that the flowers lie in nearly one plane | pear, apple, hawthorn (*Crataegus*), cherry; the inflorescence of rapeseed is corymb-like at first, and later the axis elongates and it becomes a raceme |
| Umbel | axis extremely shortened, many flowers with pedicels of about equal length borne at its tip, forming an umbrella; flowering proceeds from the outside inward | ginseng (*Panax ginseng*), *Eleutherococcus*, ivy (*Hedera*) |
| Spike | axis long, bearing many sessile or nearly sessile bisexual flowers | plantain (*Plantago*), vervain (*Verbena officinalis*) |
| Catkin (ament) | axis soft (mostly pendulous, sometimes erect), bearing many sessile or short-stalked unisexual flowers with the perianth often reduced; the whole inflorescence falls after flowering (male inflorescences) or after the fruits ripen | poplar, willow, oak, wingnut (*Pterocarya*), birch (*Betula*), male inflorescences of hazel (*Corylus*) |
| Spadix | similar to a spike, but the axis is thick, short, fleshy and bears many sessile unisexual florets; one with a spathe outside is called a spathe-spadix inflorescence | *Arisaema*, *Pinellia ternata*, taro (*Colocasia esculenta*), calla lily; female inflorescences of maize and cattail (*Typha*) |
| Capitulum (head) | axis extremely shortened and enlarged into a flat, convex or spherical receptacle densely covered with many sessile florets, with an involucre outside; flowering proceeds from the outside inward | chrysanthemum (*Chrysanthemum*), dandelion, sunflower (Asteraceae); clover (*Trifolium*), happy tree (*Camptotheca acuminata*) |
| Hypanthodium | axis enlarged, concave in the center into a sac, with many sessile florets on the inner wall, almost completely hidden, leaving only a small opening at the apex to the outside, the passage for pollinating fig wasps | common fig (*Ficus carica*), creeping fig (*Ficus pumila*), *Ficus* |

All the florets of a hypanthodium are unisexual: the male flowers are mostly on the upper part of the inner wall (near the opening) and the female flowers in the lower part; the female flowers are in turn of two kinds, long-styled flowers that can set seed and short-styled gall flowers in which the fig wasps lay their eggs ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)).

An inflorescence whose axis branches, each branch being itself a simple inflorescence of some kind, is called a compound inflorescence:

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Panicle | also called a compound raceme: the axis branches, each branch being a raceme | heavenly bamboo (*Nandina domestica*), rice, oat (*Avena sativa*), yucca |
| Compound spike | axis branching once or twice, each branch being a spike (that is, a spikelet) | wheat, barley (*Hordeum vulgare*), crabgrass (*Digitaria*) |
| Compound umbel | many rays of about equal length crowded at the tip of the axis, each ray bearing an umbel (umbellet); an involucre often at the base and an involucel at the base of each umbellet | carrot (*Daucus carota*), *Peucedanum*, fennel (*Foeniculum vulgare*) (Apiaceae) |
| Compound corymb | branches arranged in a corymb, each branch again a corymb | mountain ash (*Sorbus*), *Photinia* |
| Compound capitulum | each unit of the capitulum is itself a small capitulum | *Syncalathium*, globe thistle (*Echinops*) |

### 27.7.3. Determinate Inflorescences

**Definition 27.7.3 (Determinate Inflorescence)** An inflorescence in which the terminal flower of the axis opens first, thereby limiting further growth of the axis, and later flowers are produced successively by lateral branches below the terminal flower, so that flowering proceeds from top to bottom or from the inside outward, is called a **determinate inflorescence** (also cymose inflorescence); one in which each branch below the terminal flower ends in a flower and then branches again in turn is called a **cyme**.

Determinate inflorescences grow sympodially: each order of branch ends in a flower, and growth is continued by a lateral branch below. According to the number of lateral branches produced each time below the terminal flower, they are divided into:

- Monochasium: only one lateral branch below the terminal flower; after this branch bears a flower at its tip, only one lateral branch again arises below it, and so on, so that the whole inflorescence is a sympodium. If successive branches arise alternately to the left and right and the branches and flowers do not lie in one plane, the inflorescence is a scorpioid cyme (cincinnus), as in gladiolus (*Gladiolus*), cinquefoil (*Potentilla*) and iris; if successive branches all arise on the same side and the inflorescence is coiled, it is a bostryx (helicoid cyme), as in forget-me-not (*Myosotis*) and *Trigonotis peduncularis* (Boraginaceae).
- Dichasium: below the terminal flower the main axis gives off one branch on each side, each branch bears a flower at its tip and branches again on both sides, and so on, as in mouse-ear chickweed (*Cerastium*), chickweed (*Stellaria*), catchfly (*Silene conoidea*) (Caryophyllaceae) and Japanese spindle (*Euonymus japonicus*).
- Pleiochasium: below the terminal flower the main axis gives off three or more branches, each of which again forms a small cyme, as in sun spurge (*Euphorbia helioscopia*; where the flower stalks at the tips of the branches are very short and the flowers crowded, the inflorescence is called a dense cyme).

There are also several special determinate inflorescences:

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Verticillaster | cymes of many sessile or nearly sessile flowers borne in the axils of opposite leaves and arranged in a whorl at the node | motherwort, mint (*Mentha*), *Lagopsis supina* (Lamiaceae) |
| Cymose umbel | looks like an umbel but the central flowers open first; in fact a shortened cyme | green onion (*Allium fistulosum*), Chinese chive (*Allium tuberosum*) (Amaryllidaceae, *Allium*) |
| Cyathium | a cup-shaped involucre outside, with glands on its margin; inside it several male flowers with a single stamen and no perianth, and in the center one long-stalked female flower with a single pistil; the whole closely resembles a bisexual flower | *Euphorbia* (sun spurge, poinsettia, *E. lunulata*) |

Note that the distinction between "scorpioid cyme" (cincinnus) and "helicoid cyme" (bostryx) is used differently in different sources. The definitions above are those of Chinese textbooks (by direction of branching); English-language literature often calls the gradually unrolling coiled inflorescence of the Boraginaceae simply a scorpioid cyme. When reading foreign literature, rely on the description of the branching pattern.

### 27.7.4. Mixed Inflorescences and the Development of Inflorescences

The inflorescences of many plants combine features of both classes. One with an indeterminate main axis and cymose lateral branches is called a thyrse, as in lilac, horse chestnut and grape; the capitula of the Asteraceae arranged again in a corymb or panicle, and the compound umbels of the Apiaceae, are also compound. A solitary flower is sometimes really a reduced inflorescence reduced to a single flower. In describing an inflorescence one must distinguish the "unit" (capitulum, spikelet, cyathium) from the way the units are arranged.

The sequence of flowering reflects the development of the inflorescence meristem. The tip of an indeterminate inflorescence always remains an inflorescence meristem and produces flower primordia successively on its flanks, so the flowers at the base (or periphery) are the oldest and open first; in a determinate inflorescence the apical meristem converts to a floral meristem very early and growth is continued by lateral branches, so the flowers at the tip (or center) are the oldest and open first (in Figure 6 the size of the flowers shows the order of opening). In *Arabidopsis*, *TFL1* maintains the indeterminacy of the inflorescence apex, and in *tfl1* mutants a terminal flower forms at the tip of the raceme and the inflorescence becomes determinate; *LFY* and *AP1*, in turn, convert the meristem into a flower. Differences in the timing and place of expression of such genes are the developmental basis of inflorescence diversity, and the mechanisms are left to plant physiology.

The type of inflorescence has ecological significance for pollination and reproduction: racemes and panicles flower for a long period and continuously attract pollinators; capitula, umbels and corymbs assemble many small flowers into a conspicuous "pseudanthium", so that a pollinator landing once can visit several flowers; catkins and spikes produce large amounts of pollen and suit wind pollination. The capitulum is one of the important reasons for the great diversity of the Asteraceae ([7.12.49](/7.12.49)).

## Questions

1. How can the ABC model explain "double flowers"? If the flower of a mutant has, from the outside in, sepals, sepals, carpels, carpels, which class of function is lost?

   **Answer** Double flowers are mostly the result of the conversion of stamens (sometimes together with carpels) into petals: whorl 3 changes from B + C to A + B, which is equivalent to weakened C function in whorl 3 with A function extending in. If C function is lost entirely (as in *ag*), whorl 4 becomes sepals and the floral meristem is no longer terminated, so that the center of the flower repeatedly produces petals, giving an extremely double flower. A flower of sepals, sepals, carpels, carpels lacks B function (*ap3* or *pi*): whorl 2 retains only A and whorl 3 only C.

2. Write the floral formula for the following description: bilaterally symmetric; 5 fused sepals; corolla sympetalous, 5-lobed, two-lipped; 4 stamens, didynamous, inserted on the corolla; 2 fused carpels, ovary superior, deeply 4-lobed into 4 locules, 1 ovule per locule.

   **Answer** $\uparrow K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_{2+2} \underline{G}_{(2:4:1)}$. This is the typical floral formula of the Lamiaceae (didynamous stamens are written $A_{2+2}$ or $A_4$ with a note that they are didynamous; epipetalous stamens are indicated only in the text).

3. Why is a flower with a superior ovary called a hypogynous flower? Peach and apple both have a cup-shaped hypanthium, so why is one written $\underline{G}$ and the other $\overline{G}$ in the floral formula?

   **Answer** "Superior" and "inferior" describe different objects: ovary position describes the height of the ovary relative to the insertion of the other floral parts, whereas the flower type describes the insertion of the perianth and stamens relative to the ovary. When the ovary is superior the floral parts are inserted below it, so the flower is hypogynous. In peach the hypanthium is free from the ovary, which is attached only by its base at the bottom of the cup, and so is still superior (a perigynous flower); in apple the hypanthium is completely fused with the ovary wall, and the perianth and stamens are inserted above the ovary, so the ovary is inferior (an epigynous flower). The floral formula is written according to ovary position only.

4. Someone says: "Poplar and willow flowers have no perianth and are structurally simple, so the Salicaceae are primitive angiosperms." What is wrong with this reasoning?

   **Answer** "Simplicity" of floral parts may be primitive or may be the result of secondary simplification. The achlamydeous unisexual flowers of the Salicaceae are associated with wind pollination and arose by reduction from bisexual flowers with a perianth; molecular phylogenies place the Salicaceae among the rosids, in the Malpighiales, far from the base of the angiosperms. Judging whether a character is primitive or advanced requires comparison on a phylogenetic tree; the evolutionary trends of floral parts are only hypotheses and can be reversed.

5. How can one tell whether an inflorescence is indeterminate or determinate when observing it? Use the "umbel" of green onion as an example.

   **Answer** Look at the order of flowering and at the tip of the axis: an indeterminate inflorescence opens from the bottom upward or from the outside inward, and at flowering its tip still carries buds and can continue to grow; in a determinate inflorescence the flowers at the tip or center open first and later flowers are produced by lateral branches below. The inflorescence of green onion looks like an umbel, but the central flowers open first and the outer flowers later, and the flowers are actually arranged in several shortened cymes, so it is a cymose umbel and belongs to the determinate inflorescences.
