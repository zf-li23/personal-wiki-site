Plant anatomists identify cells, tissues and organs almost entirely by features visible under the light microscope: the thickness and layering of the cell wall, the shape of pits, the color of plastids, the size of vacuoles, and the form of starch grains and crystals. This chapter describes the plant cell from that point of view. The molecular mechanisms of the plasma membrane, endomembrane system, cytoskeleton and division machinery have been covered in cell biology and are only linked here (plant and animal cells are compared in [7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.4.3)); we concentrate on their morphology in the plant body and their anatomical significance. The cell wall, pits and division planes underlie the next two chapters (meristems, dermal tissue, ground tissue and vascular tissue); plastids and ergastic substances recur in the chapters on seeds, roots, stems, leaves and fruits; and the mode of cytokinesis in meiosis is used again in the development of the anther.

A plant cell consists of a **protoplast** and a **cell wall**. The term protoplast emphasizes structure: it denotes all the living material inside the wall, that is, the plasma membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus. The older term protoplasm emphasizes the substance of which the protoplast is made. The cell wall, plastids and the large central vacuole are the three features that most clearly distinguish plant cells from animal cells, and we take them up in turn.

## 3.1. Discovery, Shape and Size of Cells

### 3.1.1. The Discovery of the Cell

In 1665 Robert Hooke described in his *Micrographia* thin slices of cork (the phellem of oak bark) examined with a microscope of his own making: many small honeycomb-like chambers, which he called cells. What he saw were actually the walls left behind by dead cells. Marcello Malpighi (1675) and Nehemiah Grew (1682) then described plant tissues systematically and founded plant anatomy. In 1838 Matthias Schleiden proposed that all plants are composed of cells, and in the following year Theodor Schwann extended the idea to animals; together their proposals make up the **cell theory** ([7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.1)). Botany is thus one of the birthplaces of the cell theory.

### 3.1.2. The Shape of Cells

The shape of a plant cell reflects its function and the mutual pressure of its neighbors. Free cells or loosely arranged ones (storage cells of fruit flesh, cells in suspension culture) are nearly spherical; tightly packed parenchyma cells press against one another into polyhedra; water-conducting cells are elongated into tubes; supporting fibers are long and slender with pointed ends; epidermal cells are flat plates, often with wavy margins that interlock; and spongy mesophyll cells may be star-shaped.

For equal cells under uniform pressure that fill space, the most economical polyhedron is the orthic tetrakaidecahedron (truncated octahedron), with 8 hexagonal and 6 quadrilateral faces. This is Kelvin's (1887) partition of space with minimal surface area, the same as in soap foam. The measured mean number of faces of cells in apical meristems and in the pith parenchyma of some stems is indeed close to 14; differentiated cells deviate from this shape because of uneven growth.

### 3.1.3. The Size of Cells

Most higher-plant cells are $10$–$100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter and invisible to the naked eye. There are two reasons why cells are small:

- A single nucleus can control only a limited volume of cytoplasm.
- Exchange of materials takes place across the cell surface. Volume $V$ grows with the cube of the linear dimension but surface area $S$ only with the square: for a spherical cell of radius $r$, $S/V = 3/r$, and for a cube of edge $a$, $S/V = 6/a$. Increasing the diameter from $20\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ to $200\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ reduces the relative surface area to one tenth (a quantitative discussion of the limits on cell size is in [7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.3.2)).

In general, metabolically active cells are small (meristematic cells) and metabolically inactive cells are large (storage cells of fruit flesh and pith). Plant cells enlarge "cheaply" by means of the central vacuole: the vacuole is filled with water and inorganic ions, so a cell can increase its volume many tens of times with little increase in cytoplasm ([7.3.11](/7.3.11#sec-11.5)). Some cells are extremely long and very thin: the epidermal hairs of cotton seeds (cotton fibers) reach several centimeters, the phloem fibers of ramie (*Boehmeria nivea*) up to about $55\,\mathrm{cm}$, and the non-articulated laticifers of *Euphorbia* and of the Apocynaceae extend and branch as the plant grows, reaching several meters; they are often considered the longest cells in the plant body. The juice sacs visible to the naked eye in citrus fruit are not single cells but multicellular hair-like outgrowths of the endocarp epidermis, whose juice-filled parenchyma cells are also very large.

Cell size is also affected by water and nutrient supply, light and temperature; for example, in dense stands plants are slender and the internode cells are especially elongated. The physiological mechanism of such growth responses belongs to plant physiology.

## 3.2. The Protoplast

### 3.2.1. The Nucleus

A plant cell usually has a single nucleus. The position and shape of the nucleus change as the cell grows: in a young cell the nucleus lies in the center, is nearly spherical and occupies a large fraction of the cell volume; as the cell grows and the central vacuole forms, the nucleus and cytoplasm are pushed against the wall, the nucleus becomes hemispherical or flattened and occupies only a small fraction of the volume; sometimes the nucleus is suspended in the middle of the cell by several cytoplasmic strands crossing the vacuole (the cytoplasmic strand in Figure 1). The size of the **nucleolus** varies with the physiological state: it is large in metabolically active nuclei such as those of the meristem and small in quiescent ones.

A few cells have two or more nuclei:

| Situation | Examples |
|---|---|
| Normal coenocytes (nuclear division without cytokinesis) | Non-articulated laticifers; the free-nuclear phase of nuclear endosperm; the early female gametophyte of most gymnosperms and many ferns, and the free-nuclear stage of the gymnosperm proembryo; multinucleate algae such as *Vaucheria* and *Cladophora*, and the hyphae of many fungi |
| Binucleate or multinucleate cells | Tapetal cells of the anther are often binucleate or multinucleate |
| Nucleus degenerates at maturity | Sieve-tube elements ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)); dead cells such as vessel elements, tracheids and fibers |

### 3.2.2. Plastids

**Plastids** are double-membrane organelles unique to plant cells; they contain their own DNA and ribosomes and all develop from **proplastids** in meristematic cells (definition, ultrastructure and division mechanism are in [7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.7)). Botanists divide plastids into three classes according to pigments and function:

- **Chloroplasts** contain chlorophylls a and b and carotenoids (carotenes and xanthophylls), carry out photosynthesis, and are found in the cells of all green parts of the plant; a higher-plant mesophyll cell generally contains several tens to over a hundred.
- **Chromoplasts** contain only carotenoids and are yellow, orange or orange-red depending on the ratio of carotenes to xanthophylls; they color many petals, fruits and some roots and attract animals for pollination and seed dispersal. The way the pigment accumulates determines the shape of the chromoplast: most commonly it accumulates in plastoglobules, as in many petals; in the chromoplasts of red pepper fruit the pigment is deposited in fibrils; in carrot root ($\beta$-carotene) and ripe tomato (lycopene) the pigment forms crystals and the chromoplast takes the needle-like, plate-like or irregular shape of the crystal; the chromoplasts of nasturtium petals are needle-like.
- Leucoplasts (see Definition 3.2.1).

**Definition 3.2.1 (Leucoplasts)** Plastids that contain no visible pigment are collectively called **leucoplasts**; they are widespread in storage cells, in tissues not exposed to light (pith, underground organs) and in the epidermal cells of the stems and leaves of many plants. According to the substance stored, leucoplasts are divided into starch-storing **amyloplasts**, lipid-accumulating elaioplasts and protein-accumulating proteinoplasts.

Amyloplasts are abundant in tuberous roots, tubers and the endosperm of seeds; the amyloplasts of the central cells of the root cap also act as statoliths that sense gravity (the mechanism is left to plant physiology). Elaioplasts occur, for example, in the tapetum of the anther; "proteinoplast" is now rarely used, since storage proteins mostly accumulate in vacuoles or the endoplasmic reticulum (Section 3.4.2). The leucoplasts in some epidermal cells are small and cluster around the nucleus.

A proplastid is a small double-membrane vesicle with few internal membranes that is able to divide. In the light, the inner membrane folds inward and develops thylakoids and grana, and the proplastid becomes a chloroplast; in the dark, the inner membrane forms a paracrystalline lattice of interconnected tubules (the prolamellar body), and the proplastid becomes an **etioplast**, which is why plants become etiolated; within a few hours of exposure to light, the etioplasts of an etiolated seedling turn into chloroplasts. Most plastid types can be converted into one another (Figure 2):

- Leucoplasts can turn into chloroplasts in the light: potato tubers turn green on exposure to light; in the spider plant (*Chlorophytum comosum*) the plantlets on the stolons are often suspended in the air and exposed to light, and their thickened aerial roots turn green as the surface cells develop chloroplasts.
- Chromoplasts mostly develop from chloroplasts or leucoplasts, rarely directly from proplastids: during tomato fruit development the plastids change from leucoplasts to chloroplasts, and on ripening chlorophyll and thylakoids break down and lycopene accumulates, giving chromoplasts; the chromoplasts of carrot roots and many petals arise from leucoplasts.
- Chromoplasts can also revert to chloroplasts: the part of a carrot root that is exposed above ground turns green in the light; citrus fruit can "regreen" if exposed to high temperature before harvest.
- In senescing leaves, chloroplasts dismantle their thylakoids and export nitrogen, becoming gerontoplasts; in callus culture, plastids can dedifferentiate to a proplastid-like state.

The color of a plant organ is determined jointly by plastids and vacuoles. When chlorophyll predominates, the leaf is green; in autumn, under poor nutrition or in senescence, chlorophyll breaks down faster than the carotenoids and the leaf turns yellow; the red autumn leaves of many trees are due to newly synthesized anthocyanins in the vacuole. In seed plants, plastids that have developed from proplastids can still increase in number by division (FtsZ ring and plastid-dividing ring, [7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.7.2)).

### 3.2.3. The Vacuole

A large central vacuole is a hallmark of the mature living plant cell. Meristematic cells have only many small, scattered vacuoles (provacuoles), visible only under the electron microscope; as the cell grows, the small vacuoles take up water, swell and fuse into a large central vacuole occupying 30%–90% of the cell volume, while the cytoplasm and nucleus are squeezed into a thin layer against the wall (Figure 1); sometimes several larger vacuoles remain, and the nucleus is suspended among them on cytoplasmic strands. By pressing the cytoplasm into a thin layer, the vacuole enlarges the surface through which the cytoplasm exchanges gases and nutrients with its surroundings.

The **vacuole** is bounded by a single membrane, the tonoplast, and contains *cell sap*, whose composition varies greatly with species, organ and developmental stage (the cell-biological definition, proton pumps and transporters are in [7.3.11](/7.3.11#sec-11.5)):

| Component | Examples |
|---|---|
| Sugars and organic acids | Sucrose in sugar beet root and sugarcane stem; citric acid in lemon juice (the pH of the sap can be as low as 2–3); malic acid accumulated at night by CAM plants |
| Storage proteins | Protein storage vacuoles in the cotyledons of legumes (Section 3.4.2) |
| Pigments | Flavonoids such as anthocyanins, giving red, purple and blue colors to petals, fruits, autumn leaves and the outer epidermis of purple onion scales |
| **Alkaloids** and other defense substances | Morphine in the latex of opium poppy (*Papaver somniferum*), nicotine in tobacco, caffeine in tea and coffee; cyanogenic glycosides |
| Tannins, mineral salts, crystals | Calcium oxalate crystals (Section 3.4.5) |

There is no strict boundary between storage substances and "excretory products": tannins, alkaloids and crystals are often regarded as metabolic wastes, but they play a clear role in defense against herbivores.

Anthocyanins dissolve in the cell sap, and their color changes with pH: red when acidic (flavylium cation), purple near neutral, and bluish when weakly alkaline (quinoidal base). The flower buds of *Ipomoea tricolor* are purplish red; when the flower opens, the vacuolar pH of the petal epidermal cells rises from about 6.6 to about 7.7 and the flower turns sky blue (Yoshida et al., 1995). Most blue flowers, however, do not rely on an alkaline vacuole: their anthocyanins form complexes with flavonoid co-pigments and metal ions. The blue pigment of dayflower (*Commelina communis*) is a supramolecular complex containing $\ce{Mg^2+}$, and bigleaf hydrangea (*Hydrangea macrophylla*) takes up aluminum ions in acidic soil and bears blue flowers.

The functions of the vacuole can be summarized as storage; maintenance of osmotic pressure and **turgor pressure**, which keeps the cell firm; lowering the freezing point and raising the water-holding capacity through solutes in the cell sap, contributing to cold and drought tolerance; accumulation of defense substances; and degradation (see the next section).

### 3.2.4. Oil Bodies, Lysosomal Functions and Microbodies

The *oil body*, called a spherosome in the older plant histology, is a small droplet storing triacylglycerol, $0.5$–$2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter, and is abundant in the cotyledons or endosperm of oilseeds (peanut, soybean, rape, sunflower). In the electron microscope its boundary shows only one dark band because it is surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer rather than a bilayer: triacylglycerol is synthesized and accumulates between the two phospholipid leaflets of the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, forcing them apart, and finally an oil droplet bounded by a half-membrane buds off from the ER. Embedded in the monolayer are tack-shaped *oleosin* proteins, whose hydrophobic middle segment is inserted into the oil droplet while the hydrophilic ends remain at the surface; they prevent neighboring oil bodies from fusing and maintain their size. Oil bodies contain lipase, which hydrolyzes the fat into glycerol and fatty acids at germination.

Plant cells do not have a separate population of small lysosomes as animal cells do. Degradation is carried out mainly by the vacuole: it contains various acid hydrolases that, under certain conditions, break down its own stored proteins (such as the aleurone grains at germination), and the tonoplast can engulf senescent mitochondria, plastids and fragments of endoplasmic reticulum (plant autophagy); in **programmed cell death**, as in the maturation of vessel elements, the tonoplast ruptures and the hydrolases digest the whole protoplast ([7.3.11](/7.3.11#sec-11.5)).

The microbodies of botany textbooks are the **peroxisomes**, small single-membrane-bound bodies containing oxidases and catalase and sometimes a crystalline core (Figure 1). In plants they have two main functional specializations:

- Leaf peroxisomes occur in mesophyll cells, in close contact with chloroplasts and mitochondria; the three cooperate in the metabolism of glycolate in **photorespiration**, recovering the two-carbon compounds produced by photorespiration as glycerate and returning it to the chloroplast.
- Glyoxysomes occur mainly during the germination of oilseeds and, together with oil bodies and mitochondria, convert stored fat into sugar through $\beta$-oxidation of fatty acids and the **glyoxylate cycle** (biochemistry in [7.1](/7.1)). After the cotyledons emerge and turn green in the light, glyoxysomes gradually change into leaf peroxisomes.

The matrix proteins of peroxisomes are synthesized on free ribosomes and then imported; new peroxisomes can arise either by growth and division of existing ones or by budding from the endoplasmic reticulum ([7.3.6](/7.3.6)). The specific metabolic pathways and their physiological significance belong to plant physiology.

### 3.2.5. Plant-Specific Features of the ER, Golgi Body and Cytoskeleton

The general mechanisms of these three are covered in [7.3.7](/7.3.7)–[7.3.9](/7.3.9), [7.3.13](/7.3.13) and [7.3.14](/7.3.14). In plant cells the following are noteworthy:

- **Endoplasmic reticulum**: a cortical ER forms a network along the plasma membrane; it passes through plasmodesmata and connects with the ER of neighboring cells (as the desmotubule), so that the ER of the whole plant forms one continuous system of channels ([7.3.23](/7.3.23#sec-23.5.2)); in seeds it directly forms the protein bodies that store storage proteins (zein of maize, prolamin of rice) and the oil bodies.
- **Golgi apparatus**: unlike in animal cells, it is not concentrated near the nucleus but is dispersed as several hundred small Golgi stacks that move through the cell with cytoplasmic streaming. It synthesizes and secretes hemicelluloses and pectins (cellulose is made by cellulose synthase in the plasma membrane, Section 3.3.3) and supplies the vesicles that form the cell plate at cytokinesis; it also secretes mucilage, such as the mucilage secreted by the outer root-cap cells, which eases the passage of the root tip through the soil, and that of the glandular hairs of carnivorous plants. The resin secreted by the epithelial cells of conifer resin canals consists of terpenoids, which are synthesized mainly in plastids and the endoplasmic reticulum.
- **Cytoskeleton**: cortical **microtubules** beneath the plasma membrane determine the direction in which cellulose microfibrils are deposited and thus indirectly the direction of cell expansion and the cell's shape (it is the cell wall that directly supports the cell and determines its shape); microtubules also form the spindle, the preprophase band and the phragmoplast (Section 3.5.1). Microfilaments are involved in cytoplasmic movement.
- Cytoplasmic movement: in living plant cells the cytosol continually flows and carries organelles along. In a cell with a single central vacuole, the cytoplasm circulates in one direction along the wall around the vacuole, called *cytoplasmic streaming* (cyclosis); in cells with several vacuoles, different cytoplasmic strands may flow in different directions. Cytoplasmic streaming is driven by myosin XI moving along microfilaments and reaches speeds of several tens of micrometers per second in the internodal cells of *Chara* ([7.3.13](/7.3.13)); in the leaves of aquatic plants such as *Hydrilla verticillata* and *Vallisneria*, it can be observed under the microscope using chloroplasts as markers.
- **Centrosomes**: there are no centrioles in the cells of flowering plants and conifers; the spindle nucleates on the surface of the nuclear envelope and assembles around the chromosomes (for the concept of the **centrosome** see [7.3.14](/7.3.14)). Centrioles occur only in the spermatogenous cells of plants that produce motile sperm, where they form de novo as the basal bodies of the flagella (blepharoplasts): this is the case in bryophytes, lycophytes, ferns, and, among gymnosperms, cycads and *Ginkgo*. The motile sperm of ginkgo (Sakugoro Hirase, 1896) and cycads (Seiichiro Ikeno, 1896) are the only ones among seed plants. The sperm of conifers, gnetophytes and angiosperms are nonmotile, and centrioles are lost entirely.

## 3.3. The Cell Wall

The **cell wall** is the tough shell around the protoplast; it protects the protoplast, resists turgor pressure and determines the shape of the cell (the cell-biological definition and molecular composition are in [7.3.23](/7.3.23#sec-23.6)). Although it is a nonliving component, it is closely tied to the protoplast: in young cells the wall and protoplast are inseparable, and the wall contains many proteins and enzymes involved in growth, uptake, recognition, defense and wall degradation. After the protoplast of a mature cell dies, the wall remains and performs its supporting and conducting functions, so in plant anatomy cell types are identified mainly by their walls.

### 3.3.1. Layers of the Cell Wall

By time of formation and chemical composition, the cell wall is divided into three layers:

**Definition 3.3.1 (Middle Lamella)** The layer shared between the walls of two adjacent cells, composed mainly of pectin and cementing the adjacent cells together, is called the **middle lamella**. It is derived from the **cell plate** formed at cytokinesis and is the earliest layer of the cell wall to form (some authors do not count it as part of the wall).

- **Primary wall**: the wall layer secreted by the protoplast on both sides of the middle lamella before the cell stops growing; it consists mainly of cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin and a small amount of structural protein, is generally thin ($1$–$3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) and soft, and is plastic, extending as the cell grows. In many cells no further wall layers accumulate after the primary wall has formed, and the primary wall is their permanent wall, for example in most parenchyma cells, meristematic cells and collenchyma cells.
- **Secondary wall**: the wall layer that continues to accumulate on the inner side of the primary wall after the cell has stopped growing; its main component is cellulose, with less hemicellulose, very little pectin, often lignin, and generally no structural protein. The secondary wall is thick and hard and increases the mechanical strength of the wall. Only some cells form secondary walls, mainly fibers, sclereids, tracheids and vessel elements; most of them lose their protoplasts at maturity, and the walls that remain support and protect the plant body.

Under the light microscope, the secondary wall of many cells shows three layers of different refractivity: an outer (S1), a middle (S2) and an inner layer (S3). The direction of the cellulose microfibrils differs among the three (Section 3.3.3): in S1 and S3 it is nearly transverse, in S2 nearly longitudinal, and S2 is the thickest (Figure 3A).

### 3.3.2. Chemical Composition of the Cell Wall

The structural substance of the plant cell wall is **cellulose**; in addition, pectin and hemicellulose are generally present, and these two classes of polysaccharides are together called noncellulosic polysaccharides. All wall materials are hydrophilic, so the cell wall is completely permeable to water and water-soluble substances; selective permeability resides in the plasma membrane.

| Component | Chemical nature | Role in the wall |
|---|---|---|
| Cellulose | Long chains of $\beta$-1,4-glucan; about 18 chains aligned in parallel and bound by hydrogen bonds into a crystalline **cellulose microfibril** | Framework of the wall, with high tensile strength and chemical stability; the most abundant organic substance on Earth; 20%–30% of the dry weight of the primary wall and more in the secondary wall |
| Hemicellulose (cross-linking glycans) | Heteropolysaccharides of xylose, glucose, mannose, arabinose, galactose and so on, such as the xyloglucan of the primary wall of dicots and the xylan of secondary walls and of grasses; not an intermediate of cellulose degradation | Binds to the surface of microfibrils and tethers them together |
| Pectin | Acidic polysaccharides rich in galacturonic acid, highly hydrated, cross-linked by $\ce{Ca^2+}$ into a gel | Fills the spaces between microfibrils; forms the middle lamella and cements adjacent cells |
| Structural proteins | Hydroxyproline-rich glycoproteins such as extensin, expansins and various enzymes | Reinforcement, wall loosening during growth, signaling and defense |
| Lignin | Phenolic polymer formed by oxidative polymerization of three monolignols ($p$-coumaryl, coniferyl and sinapyl alcohols) | Mainly in the secondary wall, making the wall hard and impermeable to water |

### 3.3.3. Microfibrils and Cellulose Synthase Complexes

Under the electron microscope, the structural unit of the wall is the microfibril; microfibrils interweave into a network that forms the framework of the wall (the cellulose fibril system), while pectin, hemicellulose and other substances fill the spaces of the network. Several microfibrils further aggregate into macrofibrils visible under the light microscope.

Cellulose is synthesized by **cellulose synthase** complexes in the plasma membrane. In freeze-fracture electron micrographs the complex is a "rosette" of six subunits, each containing three CesA proteins; each protein extrudes one glucan chain from UDP-glucose, and 18 chains crystallize outside the membrane into one microfibril. The complex moves in the membrane along the cortical microtubules, so the direction of microfibril deposition coincides with the orientation of the microtubules beneath the plasma membrane (molecular mechanism in [7.3.23](/7.3.23#sec-23.6)). Hemicellulose and pectin are synthesized in the Golgi apparatus and secreted into the wall in vesicles. In *Arabidopsis*, the complex that synthesizes primary-wall cellulose is composed of CESA1, CESA3 and CESA6, and that for secondary-wall cellulose of CESA4, CESA7 and CESA8; the cellulose made by the latter has higher crystallinity and degree of polymerization.

The orientation of microfibrils determines the direction of cell growth and the mechanical properties of the wall:

- In the primary wall the microfibrils are interwoven into a network; in elongating cells most are oriented transversely and, like barrel hoops, restrict lateral expansion, so the cell elongates along its long axis.
- In the secondary wall, the microfibrils within each layer are roughly parallel but differ in direction between layers: in S1 and S3 they make a large angle with the long axis of the cell (nearly transverse), in S2 a small angle (generally $5^\circ$–$30^\circ$ in wood fibers, the microfibril angle). This alternating arrangement strengthens the wall like plywood; the smaller the microfibril angle in S2, the greater the bending stiffness of fiber and wood (reaction wood in the compression zone has a large microfibril angle, and the wood is soft and brittle).

### 3.3.4. Modifications of the Cell Wall

Different cells deposit or infiltrate other substances into the wall; the four most common are:

| Modification | Substance | Location | Result and function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lignification | Lignin | May infiltrate the middle lamella, primary wall and secondary wall; begins when secondary wall formation begins and ends when it is complete | Greater hardness, less elasticity, impermeability to water; lignified walls of vessels and tracheids prevent lateral water loss and favor longitudinal conduction; support by fibers and sclereids. Phloroglucinol–HCl stains lignified walls red |
| Cutinization | Cutin (a polyester of hydroxy fatty acids) and wax | Outer wall of epidermal cells, forming a cuticle outside the wall ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)) | Reduces transpiration and prevents pathogen entry |
| Suberization | Suberin (a composite polymer of aliphatic polyesters and phenolics) and wax | Deposited as a suberin lamella on the inner side of the primary wall, all around the cell | Impermeable to water and gases; cork cells die soon after suberization. Endodermal cells of the root remain alive after forming the suberin lamella (the main component of the **Casparian strip** itself is lignin, [7.12.20](/7.12.20)) |
| Mineralization | Silica (silicon dioxide), calcium carbonate and so on | In the wall or in the cell lumen | Hardness, increased mechanical strength, deterrence of herbivores; the silica cells in grass leaf epidermis make leaf margins able to cut a finger, and the rough stems of horsetails (*Equisetum*) were used for scouring; the siliceous wall of diatoms belongs to the algae ([7.12.8](/7.12.8)) |

In grasses and other plants, silica deposited in cells forms silica bodies of fixed shapes, which persist for a long time in soil and sediments after the plant dies and are called phytoliths; they are important evidence in archaeology and paleoecology for identifying ancient crops (such as the domestication of rice).

### 3.3.5. Intercellular Spaces

Intercellular spaces are formed in three ways:

- Schizogenous: the pectin of the middle lamella dissolves and adjacent cells separate at the corners. This is the most common way; in parenchyma the intercellular spaces communicate with one another and form an aeration system through the entire plant body that finally opens to the outside through stomata or lenticels, ensuring gas exchange for every living cell.
- Lysigenous: cavities left after some cells disintegrate and die, such as the aerenchyma formed in rice and maize roots under oxygen deficiency.
- Schizolysigenous: a combination of the two.

Secretory cavities and canals are likewise formed in these three ways ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)). Pectin is readily dissolved by acids or pectinases: when fruits such as tomato, apple and watermelon ripen, the middle lamellae of the flesh cells dissolve, the cells separate from one another and the fruit softens; some pathogenic fungi enter the plant body by secreting pectinase; and retting, in which the stems of fiber plants are soaked in water and microbes break down the middle lamellae so that the fibers separate, uses the same process.

### 3.3.6. Primary Pit Fields, Plasmodesmata and Pits

The thickening of the cell wall is not uniform; thinner areas are left that allow exchange of materials between adjacent cells.

The distinct depressed areas of the primary wall are called *primary pit fields*, on which **plasmodesmata** are concentrated; a few plasmodesmata may also be scattered over other parts of the wall (structure and permeability in [7.3.23](/7.3.23#sec-23.5.2)). Plasmodesmata join the protoplasts of adjacent cells into a whole (the symplast) and allow materials to move between cells without crossing a membrane; they regulate transport only by changing the aperture of the channel. Plasmodesmata are common where exchange is frequent, as between mesophyll cells and between sieve tubes and companion cells; there are none between dead cells that have no protoplast on either side; and between mature guard cells and the surrounding epidermal cells the plasmodesmata are closed, so the guard cells are symplastically isolated and substances can enter and leave only across membranes, under control.

**Definition 3.3.2 (Pits and Pit Pairs)** The areas that are left uncovered by secondary wall when the secondary wall thickens, either at an existing primary pit field or elsewhere, are called **pits**. A pit consists of a pit cavity and a pit membrane: the **pit cavity** is the cavity enclosed by the secondary wall, and its opening toward the cell lumen is the **pit aperture**; the primary wall and middle lamella at the bottom of the cavity form the **pit membrane**. Pits in the walls of adjacent cells usually lie opposite one another as a **pit pair**, whose pit membrane consists of two primary walls and one middle lamella; a pit with no counterpart on the other side is a blind pit.

Pits are divided into two classes according to whether the secondary wall arches over the margin of the pit cavity:

- Simple pits: the secondary wall ends at the edge of the pit cavity without arching, the pit cavity has about the same diameter from outside to inside, and in face view the pit is a circle. Simple pits occur in the walls of parenchyma cells, sclereids and so on; in sclereids the wall is especially thick, so the pits become long, narrow pit canals, and several canals may merge as the wall thickens into ramiform pits. In phloem fibers and libriform fibers the canal of a simple pit narrows inward and appears in face view as an oblique slit. Simple pits are found mainly in the walls of living cells, but also in the walls of dead cells such as fibers.

**Definition 3.3.3 (Bordered Pits and the Torus)** A pit in which the secondary wall arches over the pit cavity toward the cell lumen at its margin, forming an arched border (the pit border) that markedly narrows the pit aperture, is called a **bordered pit**; in face view it appears as two concentric circles, the outer one the outline of the border and the inner one the aperture. In the bordered pits of tracheids of gymnosperms such as conifers, the middle of the pit membrane carries a disc-shaped thickening of primary-wall nature, the **torus**, whose diameter is greater than that of the aperture; the part of the membrane around the torus is porous and permeable to water and is called the **margo**.

Bordered pits occur in tracheids, vessel elements and fiber-tracheids, which are dead water-conducting or supporting cells, and represent a compromise for conducting cells that must "let water pass yet withstand negative pressure": the arched border gives a pit membrane of large area but a small aperture, with little loss of wall strength. Where a tracheid abuts a parenchyma cell, the tracheid side has a bordered pit and the parenchyma side a simple pit, together called a half-bordered pit pair. A bordered pit with a torus appears in face view as three concentric circles (border, torus and aperture). When the pit membrane is centered, water flows mainly through the pores of the margo to the adjacent tracheid; when the pressures on the two sides differ (for example when one tracheid has lost tension through entry of an air bubble), the membrane is pushed toward the side of lower pressure and the torus blocks the pit aperture on that side, preventing the flow of water and the spread of the bubble; this is called pit aspiration. Bordered pits with a torus thus control the flow of water; after wood is dried most pits are aspirated, which makes the heartwood of conifers difficult to penetrate with preservatives.

Primary pit fields, pits and plasmodesmata all facilitate exchange of materials between cells and between cells and the environment, so that the multicellular plant body is a unified whole in structure and function.

## 3.4. Ergastic Substances

**Definition 3.4.1 (Ergastic Substances)** Nonprotoplasmic substances produced by the protoplast in the course of metabolism are called **ergastic substances**; they include storage substances (starch, protein, lipids) and intermediate products and wastes of metabolism (tannins, crystals and so on). They occur as granules, droplets or crystals in the cytoplasm, plastids or vacuoles, and can also infiltrate the cell wall.

The morphology and chemical reactions of ergastic substances are an important basis for identifying plant tissues, medicinal herbs and food materials. Common microchemical reactions are as follows:

| Substance | Reagent | Reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Starch | Iodine–potassium iodide solution | Blue to blue-violet |
| Protein | Iodine–potassium iodide solution | Yellow to yellow-brown |
| Fats and oils | Sudan III / Sudan IV in ethanol; osmic acid | Orange-yellow / orange-red to red; black |
| Lignified walls | Phloroglucinol–HCl | Red |
| Tannins | Ferric chloride | Blue-black or dark green |

### 3.4.1. Starch

**Starch** is the most common storage carbohydrate of plants and is always synthesized in plastids. During the day chloroplasts temporarily convert part of the photosynthetic products into assimilatory (transitory) starch, which is broken down to sugar at night and exported; sugar transported to storage organs is synthesized into storage starch in amyloplasts, forming starch grains. In the amyloplast, starch is deposited layer after layer outward from a center called the hilum; the concentric layers around the hilum are called lamellae and arise from the alternation of growth layers of different crystallinity (semicrystalline layers formed by amylopectin alternating with more amorphous layers), which makes adjacent layers differ in refractive index. Chinese textbooks often attribute the lamellae to alternate deposition of amylose and amylopectin; this explanation has been superseded by more precise structural studies.

The form of starch grains varies among plants and is a basis for identifying the source of starch (Figure 4A):

- By position of the hilum: grains with a central hilum and concentric lamellae are concentric (legumes, wheat); grains with the hilum toward one end and eccentric lamellae are eccentric (potato).
- By number of hila: a simple grain has only one hilum; a compound grain has two or more hila, each surrounded only by its own lamellae, that is, many small grains formed in one amyloplast (rice, oat); a semicompound grain has two or more hila, each with its own lamellae and all enclosed by common lamellae (seen in potato).

Starch consists of **amylose** (generally 20%–30%) and **amylopectin**. Iodine molecules enter the interior of the amylose helix and give the color, which depends on chain length: chains shorter than a dozen or so glucose residues give almost no color, twenty to thirty give red, and forty or more give blue; the branches of amylopectin are short, and it gives a red-violet color with iodine. The color of starch with iodine, from blue to red-violet, therefore depends on the ratio of the two starches: the starch of waxy rice and waxy maize is almost entirely amylopectin and gives a reddish brown with iodine.

### 3.4.2. Storage Proteins

The protein stored in cells is physiologically inactive and relatively stable, unlike the proteins that make up the protoplasm. It may be crystalline or amorphous: crystalline protein, which has the properties of both crystals and colloids, is called a crystalloid and is often cubic or polyhedral; amorphous protein is often enclosed by a membrane into spherical granules.

**Definition 3.4.2 (Aleurone Grains)** Membrane-bound granules that store protein in seeds are called **aleurone grains** (also protein bodies); they contain amorphous protein, and some also contain one to several protein crystalloids and phosphorus-containing globoids; the globoids consist mainly of calcium, magnesium and potassium salts of inositol hexaphosphate (phytic acid), that is, phytin, which is the form in which seeds store phosphorus.

Aleurone grains are abundant in the endosperm or cotyledons of seeds: they are common in the parenchyma cells of legume cotyledons; those in the endosperm of castor bean (*Ricinus communis*) are large, each containing one crystalloid and one globoid (Figure 4B). The outermost one or several layers of cells of the endosperm of cereal grains (one layer in wheat, several in barley) are filled with aleurone grains and are called the *aleurone layer*; at germination it secretes hydrolytic enzymes that digest the starch of the endosperm ([7.12.19](/7.12.19)).

Aleurone grains form in two ways:

1. Vacuolar pathway: storage proteins are synthesized on the rough endoplasmic reticulum, transported through the Golgi apparatus (or directly from the ER) into the vacuole and accumulate there; one large vacuole is divided into many small vacuoles, each of which forms an aleurone grain, and the tonoplast becomes the membrane of the grain. These protein storage vacuoles are most typical in legume cotyledons. At germination the grains are digested and the small vacuoles fuse again into one large vacuole.
2. ER pathway: storage proteins synthesized on the ER accumulate directly in the ER lumen, forming protruding spherical vesicles that constrict off; when the seed dehydrates at maturity, the protein precipitates in an amorphous state and each small vesicle becomes a membrane-bound protein body. The prolamins of cereals such as maize and sorghum are stored this way. After the storage cell dies, the membrane of the protein body disappears.

Both pathways coexist in wheat endosperm.

### 3.4.3. Lipids

Lipids are the nutrient with the highest energy content and the most economical form of storage, and often occur as oil droplets in the cytoplasm; in oilseeds they are stored in oil bodies (Section 3.2.4), and elaioplasts can also accumulate lipids. Those that are solid at room temperature and rich in saturated fatty acids are called fats, and those that are liquid and rich in unsaturated fatty acids are called oils. Lipids give an orange-yellow color with Sudan III, orange-red with Sudan IV and black with osmic acid; in a section of peanut cotyledon stained with Sudan III, the oil droplets are orange-yellow.

### 3.4.4. Tannins

Tannins are polymers of phenolic compounds, usually located in the cell sap of vacuoles, but also present as droplets in the cytoplasm or infiltrating the cell wall: the walls of the cork cells of bark are often infiltrated with tannin, giving the bark a brown color. Tannins taste astringent and can bind and precipitate proteins: unripe persimmon (*Diospyros kaki*) is astringent because of the soluble tannin in its cells, and "deastringency treatment" polymerizes it into an insoluble form. The rapid browning of cut fruit, of tea leaves and of persimmon wounds is mainly the result of polyphenol oxidase oxidizing phenolics to quinones, which then polymerize. In permanent slides tannins often appear as yellow, red or brown granules and are easy to recognize. Tannins make plants less susceptible to microbial infection and to herbivory; tannin-rich barks (*Quercus*, *Larix*) are traditional raw materials for tanning leather.

### 3.4.5. Crystals

The most common crystals in plant cells are calcium oxalate crystals, formed in the vacuole, often with each crystal enclosed in a membrane-bound crystal chamber. Oxalic acid is a product of certain kinds of metabolism, is water soluble and is toxic to the cell; calcium is abundant in soil and readily absorbed. The two combine into insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, which is generally thought to detoxify oxalic acid and excess $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and may also be a form of calcium storage; needle-shaped crystals and others can also deter feeding by animals. Crystal-containing cells are often large and of special shape, scattered among other cells, and are a kind of *idioblast*. The forms of calcium oxalate crystals are (Figure 4B):

- Solitary crystals (prismatic crystals): single prismatic or pyramidal crystals, as in citrus leaves; in legumes the crystal cells beside fiber bundles are arranged in rows.
- Raphides: needle-shaped crystals with sharp ends, often aggregated in bundles, as in taro (*Colocasia esculenta*) and *Monstera* of the Araceae and in the Vitaceae; the stinging and numbness of the mouth and tongue on eating raw taro or dumb cane (*Dieffenbachia*) are related to raphides piercing the mucosa and carrying in irritant substances.
- Druses: spherical compound structures formed by the aggregation of many single crystals, the tip of each crystal projecting from the surface of the sphere, as in *Begonia* and *Opuntia* and in the leaves and bark of many plants.
- In addition there are styloids, crystal sand (in the leaves of Solanaceae) and others.

**Definition 3.4.3 (Cystoliths)** In a markedly enlarged cell of the leaf epidermis that sinks into the mesophyll (an idioblast other than the sclereid, called a lithocyst), the outer wall grows a cellulose stalk into the cell lumen, and calcium carbonate is deposited at the tip of the stalk and combines with wall material to form a stalked, spherical or grape-cluster-like structure hanging in the lumen, called a **cystolith**.

Cystoliths occur in only a few families, such as the Moraceae (the leaf of Indian rubber tree, *Ficus elastica*), Urticaceae, Acanthaceae, Cannabaceae and Cucurbitaceae, and are a taxonomic character.

### 3.4.6. Secondary Metabolites

Many of the tannins, alkaloids, anthocyanins and crystals mentioned above are **secondary metabolites**: they are not required for the growth and reproduction of all cells, their kinds vary among plant groups, and they act mainly in defense against herbivores and pathogens, in attracting pollinators and seed dispersers, and in protection against ultraviolet light. Plants produce more than 200,000 secondary metabolites, which by biosynthetic pathway fall into three major classes:

| Class | Biosynthetic origin | Examples | Location in the cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Terpenoids (**isoprenoids**) | Isoprene units (mevalonate pathway and the plastidial MEP pathway, [7.1.26](/7.1.26)) | Menthol, turpentine and resins (monoterpenes, diterpenes), artemisinin (sesquiterpene), paclitaxel (diterpene), rubber (polyterpene), carotenoids (tetraterpenes) | Plastids, endoplasmic reticulum; secreted into glandular hairs, secretory cavities, resin canals and laticifers |
| Phenolics | Shikimate pathway and phenylpropanoid metabolism | Lignin, tannins, flavonoids such as anthocyanins, coumarins | Vacuole, cell wall |
| Nitrogen-containing compounds | Amino acids | **Alkaloids** (morphine, nicotine, caffeine, quinine, vinblastine), cyanogenic glycosides, glucosinolates (Brassicaceae) | Mostly in the vacuole |

Many secondary metabolites have high economic value (drugs, flavors, dyes, rubber), and some classes are restricted to particular groups, for example glucosinolates occur almost only in the Brassicales and betalains only in the Caryophyllales (where anthocyanins are absent), so they are a basis for chemotaxonomy ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)). Their biosynthetic pathways and regulation belong to plant physiology and biochemistry.

## 3.5. Cell Division, Growth and Differentiation

Cell division in plants is usually divided into three modes: mitosis, amitosis and meiosis. The molecular machinery of division is covered in [7.3.21](/7.3.21); this section deals with their morphological features and anatomical significance in the plant body.

### 3.5.1. Special Features of Plant Mitosis

The process of **mitosis** (also called indirect division) in plant cells is the same as in animal cells, but with two outstanding features:

1. There is no centrosome. The spindle is assembled from microtubules nucleated on the surface of the nuclear envelope around the chromosomes, and its poles are broad, giving a barrel shape.
2. Cytokinesis does not form a cleavage furrow but proceeds, guided by the **phragmoplast**, with the **cell plate** forming from the inside of the cell outward.

Microtubules are arranged in four successive arrays in the cell cycle (the microtubule cycle):

| Stage | Microtubule array | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Interphase | Cortical microtubules: beneath the plasma membrane, encircling the long axis of the cell, evenly dispersed | Determine the direction of microfibril deposition |
| G2 to early prophase | **Preprophase band**: microtubules and microfilaments gather around the nucleus at the position of the equatorial plane, forming a ring | Marks beforehand where the cell plate will meet the mother-cell wall and so determines the orientation of the division plane; disassembles at the end of prophase |
| Prophase to anaphase | Spindle | Movement of chromosomes |
| Telophase | Phragmoplast: two sets of microtubules and microfilaments whose plus ends overlap at the equator | Guides Golgi-derived vesicles to the equatorial plane |

Cytokinesis usually begins at the end of anaphase as the chromosomes approach the poles: a barrel-shaped zone composed of many short microtubules, the phragmoplast, appears between the two daughter nuclei; small polysaccharide-containing vesicles derived from the Golgi apparatus are carried along the microtubules of the phragmoplast to the equatorial plane, fuse with one another and release polysaccharides that make up the cell plate, while the vesicle membranes fuse into the new plasma membranes on both sides of the plate. The phragmoplast gradually expands from the center to the periphery, and the cell plate extends outward with it and finally joins the lateral wall of the mother cell at the position marked by the preprophase band. Some endoplasmic reticulum and strands of cytoplasm are retained in the cell plate and become the primary plasmodesmata that traverse the two daughter cells. The cell plate initially contains mainly callose, which is later replaced by pectin, and the plate becomes the middle lamella; the protoplasts of the two daughter cells secrete cellulose microfibrils on both sides of the middle lamella to form their own primary walls, and a new primary wall also forms along the inner side of the old wall of the mother cell, so each daughter cell is surrounded by new wall layers. The old wall of the mother cell is stretched thin and burst as the daughter cells grow, and the old and new middle lamellae are connected through the breaks (mechanism in [7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.5.3)).

Plant cells cannot move, and the orientation of the division plane together with the direction of cell expansion determines the shape of the organ, so the role of the preprophase band in "predetermining the division plane" is especially important in plants. Nuclear division is not necessarily followed at once by cytokinesis: in nuclear endosperm and laticifers the nuclei divide repeatedly without forming walls, producing coenocytes in which walls form later or never.

### 3.5.2. Amitosis

Amitosis, also called direct division, is division in which the nucleus constricts directly into two daughter nuclei without chromosomes or a spindle appearing; the commonest form is transverse constriction, and there are also fragmentation and budding. Amitosis is fast and uses little energy, but does not ensure equal distribution of genetic material. In plants there are reports of amitosis in endosperm, callus and some parenchyma, but most of the evidence is inadequate and can often be explained as fragmentation of polyploid nuclei or misinterpretation of certain stages of mitosis (see the note on amitosis at the beginning of [7.3.21](/7.3.21)); it cannot yet be regarded as the way cells proliferate in normal tissue. Even the root cap, which is constantly renewed and seems not to require genetic stability, replenishes its cells by mitosis.

### 3.5.3. Mode of Cytokinesis in Meiosis

In plants **meiosis** produces spores (megaspores or microspores), which develop into haploid gametophytes (alternation of generations is covered in [7.12.18](/7.12.18)). The pollen mother cells of seed plants undergo cytokinesis in meiosis in one of two ways, which is a taxonomic character:

| Mode | Process | Arrangement of the tetrad | Distribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simultaneous | No cytokinesis after meiosis I (and almost no interphase), proceeding directly to meiosis II; after a transient four-nucleate stage, a single cytokinesis cuts off 4 daughter cells at once | Mostly tetrahedral | Most eudicots |
| Successive | Cytokinesis after meiosis I forms a dyad; another cytokinesis after meiosis II, two in all | Mostly bilateral (in one plane), also T-shaped and linear | Most monocots |

Both modes have exceptions in other groups. Meiosis and tetrads in the anther are covered in [7.12.28](/7.12.28).

### 3.5.4. Planes of Division

In an organ, the orientation of division is named with reference to the surface and axis of the organ (Figure 5).

**Definition 3.5.1 (Periclinal and Anticlinal Division)** A division in which the newly formed cell wall is parallel to the surface of the organ (or to the nearest tangent of the circumference) is called a **periclinal division**, also called a tangential division; it increases the number of layers, the daughter cells lie one inside the other, and the new wall is a tangential wall. A division in which the new wall is perpendicular to the surface is called an **anticlinal division**; of these, the one in which the new wall runs radially and parallel to the long axis is a **radial division**, which widens the circumference and places the daughter cells side by side; the one in which the new wall is perpendicular to the long axis is a **transverse division**, which increases the number of cells in a longitudinal file and so lengthens the organ, the daughter cells lying one above the other.

In a narrow sense, "anticlinal division" is sometimes used only for radial division. "Longitudinal division", as opposed to transverse division, means a division in which the new wall is parallel to the long axis of the cell, including both tangential and radial ones; it is commonly applied to the formation of procambium, stomatal mother cells and companion cells and in algae. These terms are used repeatedly in the next chapter, on meristems (the tunica divides only anticlinally and the corpus in all directions), the cambium and the cork cambium (periclinal divisions produce radial files of cells).

### 3.5.5. Unequal Division and Polarity

**Cell polarity** refers to the difference in structure and physiology between one end of a cell (or of an organ or a whole plant) and the other, shown by different concentrations of cytoplasm and numbers of organelles at the two ends, a nucleus toward one end, and so on. Polarity is the first condition of cell differentiation: when a polarized cell divides, the new wall lies to one side and the two daughter cells differ in size and fate; this is **asymmetric cell division** (unequal division). There are many examples in plants:

- The first division of the zygote produces a small, densely cytoplasmic apical cell and a large, vacuolated basal cell; the former develops into the embryo proper and the latter mainly forms the suspensor ([7.12.30](/7.12.30)).
- In stomatal development, the unequal division of a protodermal cell produces a smaller stomatal initial ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)).
- The first mitosis of the microspore produces a large vegetative cell and a small generative cell ([7.12.28](/7.12.28)).
- The mother cell of the sieve-tube element undergoes a single unequal longitudinal division, producing a sieve-tube element and a companion cell ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)).

### 3.5.6. Cell Growth and Differentiation

Growth of the multicellular plant depends not only on an increase in the number of cells but even more on increases in cell volume and length. The outstanding feature of cell growth is vacuolation: the small vacuoles of meristematic cells take up water, swell and fuse into a central vacuole, the cell volume can increase many tens of times with little increase in cytoplasm, and at the same time the wall loosens and extends and new wall material is synthesized (wall loosening and the Lockhart model of turgor-driven growth are in [7.3.23](/7.3.23#sec-23.6)). In most cells the wall expands over its whole surface (diffuse growth); root hairs and pollen tubes expand only at the tip (tip growth).

Cell differentiation is the process by which cells of common origin gradually become different from one another in structure and function, shown in internal physiological and biochemical changes and in external morphological changes (the general principles of **cell differentiation** are in [7.3.24](/7.3.24)). In plants, the results of differentiation are expressed anatomically above all in the cell wall: wall thickness, presence of a secondary wall and the pattern of its thickening, the type of pits, wall modifications, and whether the protoplast dies; the tissues of the next two chapters are classified by these features.

A notable feature of plant cell differentiation is that it is reversible: differentiated living cells can, under certain conditions, regain the ability to divide (**dedifferentiation**) and then differentiate again. Many plant somatic cells are **totipotent**, that is, every living cell contains all the genetic information and the potential to develop into a whole plant. Gottlieb Haberlandt (1902) was the first to propose demonstrating totipotency by culturing single isolated cells; F. C. Steward and colleagues (1958) cultured whole plants from carrot root phloem cells, and plants were later regenerated from single tobacco cells (1965) and protoplasts (1971). In plant tissue culture, isolated organs, tissues or cells (explants) are placed on a sterile medium and dedifferentiate to form **callus**, which then differentiates shoots and roots or forms somatic embryos under different hormone ratios; it is widely used in rapid propagation, production of virus-free plantlets, haploid breeding and genetic transformation. The mechanism of hormonal control is left to plant physiology; plant cells and animal stem cells are compared with respect to totipotency in [7.3.24](/7.3.24#sec-24.6).

## Summary

Entries:

- Plant cell = protoplast + cell wall; the three features that distinguish it from the animal cell are the cell wall, plastids and the large central vacuole. The typical parenchyma cell is nearly a tetrakaidecahedron (8 hexagonal + 6 quadrilateral faces); diameters are mostly $10$–$100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, limited by the nucleocytoplasmic ratio and $S/V$.
- The nucleus moves from the center to the periphery as the cell vacuolates; multinucleate cells: non-articulated laticifers, nuclear endosperm, early gymnosperm female gametophytes, tapetum (often binucleate), multinucleate algae and fungi.
- Plastids develop from proplastids and interconvert (light gives chloroplasts, dark gives etioplasts; chromoplasts mostly come from chloroplasts or leucoplasts); organ color = plastids (chlorophylls, carotenoids) + vacuole (anthocyanins).
- Vacuole: provacuoles fuse into the central vacuole; the sap contains sugars, organic acids, storage proteins, anthocyanins, alkaloids, tannins and crystals; anthocyanins are red when acidic and blue when alkaline, but most blue flowers depend on metal complexation and co-pigments.
- Oil bodies: phospholipid monolayer + oleosin; lysosomal functions of plants are carried out by the vacuole; microbodies = leaf peroxisomes (photorespiration) + glyoxysomes (fat to sugar at germination).
- Centrioles occur only in the spermatogenous cells of plants with motile sperm (bryophytes, lycophytes, ferns, cycads, ginkgo); cytoplasmic streaming is driven by myosin XI along microfilaments.
- Cell wall: middle lamella (Definition 3.3.1), primary wall, secondary wall S1, S2, S3; four modifications; schizogenous, lysigenous and schizolysigenous intercellular spaces; pits (Definition 3.3.2), bordered pits and the torus (Definition 3.3.3).
- Ergastic substances (Definition 3.4.1): starch grains (hilum, lamellae; simple, compound, semicompound), aleurone grains (Definition 3.4.2; vacuolar and ER pathways), lipids, tannins, calcium oxalate crystals (solitary, raphides, druses), cystoliths (Definition 3.4.3); the three classes of secondary metabolites.
- Division: special features of plant mitosis (no centrosome, preprophase band, phragmoplast and cell plate); the evidence for amitosis is inadequate; simultaneous and successive cytokinesis in pollen mother cell meiosis; periclinal and anticlinal division (Definition 3.5.1); unequal division and polarity; dedifferentiation and totipotency.

| Modification | Substance | Location | Function | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lignification | Lignin (phenolic polymer) | Middle lamella, primary wall, secondary wall | Hard, impermeable, favors longitudinal water conduction | Vessels, tracheids, fibers, sclereids |

## Questions

1. Temporary mounts are made from the outer epidermis of an onion bulb scale, red pepper fruit skin, a potato tuber and a *Hydrilla* leaf. Which cell structures or ergastic substances are most easily seen in each, and what method makes them clearer?

   **Answer** Outer epidermis of onion scale: anthocyanin in the vacuole colors the cells purplish red, and the nucleus, the cell wall and the primary pit fields in the wall can be seen (the bright bands in the walls of adjacent cells are the middle lamella and thinner wall areas); iodine staining makes the nucleus clearer; no chloroplasts are visible in these cells, only small leucoplasts. Red pepper skin: orange-red chromoplasts, and conspicuous simple pits in the wall (the skin cells have thickened walls). Potato tuber: large eccentric starch grains (hilum and lamellae; mostly simple grains, with some compound and semicompound ones), turned blue by iodine–potassium iodide solution. *Hydrilla* leaf: abundant chloroplasts, and cytoplasmic streaming can be observed using the chloroplasts as markers (warming or illumination speeds up the flow).

2. The tracheids of a certain wood have bordered pits with a torus. Explain from the structure how this reconciles the efficiency and the safety of water transport, and why such wood is difficult to penetrate with preservatives after drying.

   **Answer** The border of a bordered pit arches over the cavity, so that the pit membrane has a large area but the aperture is small: the large, porous margo gives low resistance to flow, while the small aperture and arched border cost the wall little strength through perforation, so it can withstand the negative pressure of transport. When one tracheid admits air (embolism), the pressure in it approaches atmospheric while the adjacent tracheid still conducting water is under negative pressure; the pressure difference pushes the membrane toward the conducting tracheid of lower pressure, the torus seals the aperture on that side, the bubble cannot pass, and the embolism is confined to a single tracheid. When wood dries, the surface tension of water likewise pulls the membrane to one side and most pits are aspirated, leaving few liquid pathways between tracheids, so preservatives cannot penetrate, especially in heartwood.

3. Someone says: "The lamellae of plant cells result from alternate deposition of amylose and amylopectin"; "microbodies take part in the glyoxylate cycle to convert the glycolate produced by photorespiration into hexose"; "centrosomes are lost only in higher plants". Point out what is inaccurate in each statement.

   **Answer** The lamellae of starch grains arise from the alternation of growth layers of different crystallinity (semicrystalline layers dominated by amylopectin alternating with more amorphous layers), not from the two starches forming separate layers; and to speak of "plant cell lamellae" is also wrong, since the lamellae belong to the starch grain. There are two kinds of microbodies: leaf peroxisomes take part in photorespiration (converting glycolate to glycine, finally recovered as glycerate) and neither take part in the glyoxylate cycle nor produce hexose; glyoxysomes convert fat to sugar when oilseeds germinate. The somatic cells of all land plants lack centrosomes; centrioles occur only in the spermatogenous cells of plants with motile sperm (bryophytes, lycophytes, ferns, cycads, ginkgo), where they form de novo as flagellar basal bodies; conifers, gnetophytes and angiosperms have lost centrioles entirely.

4. Sections of pumpkin stem, pear flesh and peanut cotyledon are observed. Which wall modifications or ergastic substances will be seen in each, and how can they be confirmed by microchemical methods?

   **Answer** Pumpkin stem: the walls of vessels and fibers are lignified and are stained red by phloroglucinol–HCl (the annular and spiral vessels of the vascular bundles of pumpkin stem are especially clear); the outer walls of the epidermis are cutinized, and Sudan III stains the cuticle orange-yellow. Pear flesh: clusters of sclereids with strongly lignified secondary walls and branched pit canals, stained red by phloroglucinol–HCl. Peanut cotyledon: abundant oil droplets (stained orange-yellow by Sudan III), also starch grains (blue with iodine) and aleurone grains (yellow with iodine).

5. A meristematic cell undergoes in succession one periclinal division and two anticlinal divisions (one radial, one transverse). How are its daughter cells arranged in the organ, and what morphological consequence do such combinations of divisions have in the plant body?

   **Answer** The periclinal division places the two daughter cells one inside the other (one at the surface, one inward); the radial division then splits one of them into two side by side, and the transverse division splits the other into two stacked one above the other. The form of an organ is determined by the division planes of the cells in each layer: a layer that divides only anticlinally (such as the tunica of the shoot apex) remains a single layer and only increases in area; a layer that divides periclinally adds layers and thickens the organ (as the cambium produces radial files of secondary xylem and secondary phloem cells); transverse divisions lengthen the organ. Because plant cells cannot move, the form of the organs is determined almost entirely by the orientation of the division planes and the direction of cell expansion.
