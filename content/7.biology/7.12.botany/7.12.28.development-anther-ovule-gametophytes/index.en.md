The previous chapter treated the stamen as a microsporophyll and the carpel as a megasporophyll ([7.12.27](/7.12.27)). This chapter looks inside them and explains how the two gametophytes of angiosperms arise. In the anther, pollen mother cells undergo meiosis to form microspores, and each microspore develops into a pollen grain, the male gametophyte; in the nucellus of the ovule, a megasporocyte undergoes meiosis to form megaspores, and a megaspore develops into the embryo sac, the female gametophyte. The **alternation of generations** reaches an extreme here: both gametophytes are reduced to a few cells and depend entirely on the sporophyte. Compared with gymnosperms ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)), the male gametophyte is reduced from the 4 cells of pine (plus the early-degenerating prothallial cells) to 2–3 cells, and the female gametophyte from a tissue of several thousand cells with archegonia to 7 cells without archegonia. The structures and embryological terms of this chapter are the basis for double fertilization in [7.12.29](/7.12.29) and for the development of embryo and endosperm in [7.12.30](/7.12.30); the morphology of pollen and embryo sacs is also important taxonomic evidence ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)). The cytology of meiosis is treated in [7.4.1](/7.4.1).

## 28.1. Development of the Anther

### 28.1.1. Structure of the Stamen

A stamen consists of a filament and an anther. The filament is not directly involved in reproduction; it holds the anther in a position favorable to pollination and conveys nutrients to it. Its structure is simple: the outermost layer is a cutinized epidermis, sometimes with hairs and stomata; inside the epidermis is parenchyma, with a single amphivasal (concentric) vascular bundle at the center, composed of sieve tubes and spiral tracheary elements, which runs up to the connective.

The anther is the main part of the stamen and corresponds to a group of microsporangia. In most angiosperms the anther has 4 pollen sacs (microsporangia) in two halves (two thecae) joined by the connective; in a few groups the anther has only 2 pollen sacs, as in the Malvaceae (anthers 1-locular) and some Lamiaceae. When the anther matures, the partition between the two pollen sacs of each side breaks down so that the two sacs are continuous and each side appears to have a single pollen sac, from the opening of which the pollen is shed ([7.12.27](/7.12.27), Figure 2).

### 28.1.2. Development of the Anther Wall

The apical part of the stamen primordium develops into the anther and the base elongates into the filament. A young anther is very simple in structure: a layer of protoderm outside and, inside, a mass of similar, actively dividing cells. Soon the four corners of the anther primordium divide faster, giving a four-ribbed cross section, and beneath the epidermis at each corner one or several large cells with dense cytoplasm and large nuclei differentiate, called archesporial cells. In cross section the archesporial cells of each corner are in some species single (wheat, cotton) and in most several; in longitudinal section they form one or several rows. The cells in the middle of the anther later differentiate into the parenchyma and vascular bundle of the connective.

An archesporial cell divides once periclinally into two layers, an outer and an inner. The outer layer is called the primary parietal cell (also primary parietal layer); it will form, together with the epidermis, the wall of the pollen sac. The inner layer is the primary sporogenous cell, which will develop into pollen mother cells. The primary parietal cells divide periclinally and anticlinally to form 3–5 layers, which differentiate from the outside inward into the endothecium, the middle layer and the tapetum (Figure 1).

**Definition 28.1.1 (Anther Wall)** The wall of the pollen sac consists, from the outside inward, of:

- the epidermis: one layer of cells, often flattened when the anther matures, in some cases leaving only traces;
- the **endothecium**: a layer of larger cells immediately beneath the epidermis. As the anther nears maturity the cells elongate radially and develop band-like cellulosic thickenings on the inner tangential and transverse walls (slightly lignified at maturity), while the outer tangential wall remains thin; the layer is then also called the **fibrous layer**;
- the middle layer: 1–3 layers of cells inside the endothecium, which at first store much starch and other reserves; during meiosis of the pollen mother cells the reserves decrease, the cells are flattened, and finally the layer disintegrates and disappears;
- the **tapetum**: the innermost layer of the wall of the pollen sac, with large cells with dense cytoplasm and the features of gland cells, which supplies nutrients and materials for pollen development and breaks down before the pollen matures.

How the primary parietal cells divide to give these layers varies among groups. Davis (1966) distinguished four types of anther wall development according to the division pattern of the secondary parietal layers (Figure 1B). The primary parietal cell first divides periclinally into an outer and an inner secondary parietal cell, and then:

| Type | Outer secondary parietal cell | Inner secondary parietal cell | Wall layers before maturity | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basic | divides into endothecium and outer middle layer | divides into inner middle layer and tapetum | epidermis, endothecium, two middle layers, tapetum | *Magnolia*, many basal angiosperms |
| Dicotyledonous | divides into endothecium and middle layer | becomes the tapetum directly | epidermis, endothecium, middle layer, tapetum | most eudicots |
| Monocotyledonous | becomes the endothecium directly | divides into middle layer and tapetum | epidermis, endothecium, middle layer, tapetum | most monocots, grasses |
| Reduced | becomes the endothecium directly | becomes the tapetum directly | epidermis, endothecium, tapetum, no middle layer | some aquatic plants |

The mature wall layers of the dicotyledonous and monocotyledonous types look the same; they differ in the origin of the middle layer, which in the former is homologous with the endothecium and in the latter with the tapetum. This origin can be traced only in serial developmental sections and is taxonomic evidence in embryology.

The band-like thickenings of the endothecium are the mechanical basis of anther dehiscence. When the anther matures the tissue loses water; the thin outer tangential walls of the fibrous-layer cells contract, whereas the thickened bands of the inner tangential and transverse walls cannot, so that the whole wall curls outward and pulls the pollen sacs open. At the junction of the two pollen sacs there are several thin-walled lip cells, where splitting begins; this slit is called the stomium, and it is where the pollen is shed. In some species of the Hydrocharitaceae, in cleistogamous plants and in plants with poricidal anthers (*Solanum*, Ericaceae), the endothecium does not develop band-like thickenings.

### 28.1.3. The Tapetum

The tapetum is at first uninucleate, with dense cytoplasm and small vacuoles; later the nucleus divides without forming new walls, making the cells binucleate or multinucleate, and in some the nuclei also undergo endoreduplication and become polyploid. The tapetum is rich in RNA, protein, lipids and carotenoids. Toward the end of meiosis of the pollen mother cells it begins to show signs of degeneration, and by the late stage of microspore development and the formation of the male gametes only remnants of the tapetum are left or it has disappeared entirely. This is a precisely regulated process of programmed cell death, and if it is too early or too late pollen abortion results (Section 28.4).

According to the way it breaks down, the tapetum is of two types:

| Type | Process | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Secretory (glandular, parietal) tapetum | the cells remain in place and continually secrete substances into the locule, disintegrating by autolysis in place only when the pollen is mature; the locule side often forms many small sporopollenin-coated granules (Ubisch bodies) | most angiosperms: cotton, lily, *Arabidopsis* |
| Amoeboid (periplasmodial) tapetum | the inner tangential and radial walls break down early, the protoplasts escape and fuse with one another to form a multinucleate periplasmodium that invades the locule, fills the space between the microspores, and is absorbed and used by them | Asteraceae, Alismataceae, Araceae, cattail |

The tapetum acts on pollen development in several ways:

1. Nutrition: it supplies sugars, amino acids, lipids and so on to the pollen sac for the growth of the microspores.
2. Secretion of callase: during meiosis the pollen mother cells and the tetrads are enclosed in a wall of **callose**, which callase secreted by the tapetum dissolves, separating the four microspores from one another; a disturbed timing of callase secretion causes abortion.
3. Supply of sporopollenin precursors: the sporopollenin of the pollen exine is largely synthesized from precursors made by the tapetum, which are then deposited on the microspore surface (Section 28.3.2).
4. Formation of the pollen coat: on disintegration the tapetum smears lipids, carotenoids, flavonoids and proteins onto the exine, forming the sticky pollenkitt on the surface of insect-pollinated pollen (called tryphine in the Brassicaceae), which makes the pollen clump and gives it color and scent.
5. Supply of recognition proteins: the exine proteins of the pollen wall come from the tapetum, and the recognition molecule of the pollen side in sporophytic self-incompatibility in the Brassicaceae (SCR/SP11) is produced by the tapetum and transferred to the pollen surface ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)).

## 28.2. Formation of Microspores

The primary sporogenous cells undergo several mitoses (in some cases none) to form a large number of cells with large size, large nuclei and dense cytoplasm; these no longer divide and are ready for meiosis.

**Definition 28.2.1 (Pollen Mother Cell and Microspore)** The diploid cell of the pollen sac that develops from the sporogenous cells and is about to undergo meiosis is called the **pollen mother cell** (microsporocyte). Each pollen mother cell yields four haploid microspores by meiosis; the four microspores are at first joined together and are called a tetrad.

Before the pollen mother cells begin meiosis, plasmodesmata connect the cells to one another and the pollen mother cells to the tapetum, and there are even rather wide cytoplasmic channels between pollen mother cells, which keep meiosis synchronous. In prophase I a callose wall forms outside the protoplast of the pollen mother cell, separating each cell from its surroundings; after meiosis is complete each microspore is likewise enclosed by callose.

After the two nuclear divisions of meiosis, cytokinesis proceeds in one of two ways (Figure 2A):

| Type of cytokinesis | Process | Arrangement of the tetrad | Common in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Successive | a cell plate forms immediately after the first division, giving a dyad; new walls form again after the second division | the two division planes are perpendicular and the four microspores lie in one plane (isobilateral type); decussate, T-shaped and linear types also occur | most monocots, such as grasses including rice and maize |
| Simultaneous | no wall forms after the first division; after the second division, new walls grow simultaneously from the periphery to the center between the four nuclei | the new walls are not in one plane and the four microspores are arranged tetrahedrally | most eudicots, such as cotton and sweet clover (*Melilotus*) |

The microspores of a tetrad usually separate later and each develops into a single pollen grain (monad). In some groups the microspores do not separate and the pollen is dispersed as tetrads (Ericaceae, cattail, Juncaceae) or as larger compound units (polyads, often of 8–32 grains, in *Acacia* of the Mimosoideae); the Orchidaceae and the Asclepiadoideae of the Apocynaceae cement the pollen of an entire pollen sac into a *pollinium*, which is carried off whole by the pollinator ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)).

## 28.3. Development and Morphology of the Pollen Grain

### 28.3.1. Development of the Male Gametophyte

A microspore released from the tetrad is a uninucleate cell with a very thin wall, dense cytoplasm and a central nucleus, called a uninucleate pollen grain. It takes up nutrients from the tapetum and grows rapidly, and a large vacuole appears that pushes the nucleus against one side of the cell wall (the vacuolate stage). The nucleus then undergoes an asymmetric mitosis (pollen mitosis I), and a curved cell plate divides the cell into two cells of very unequal size (Figure 2B).

**Definition 28.3.1 (Vegetative Cell and Generative Cell)** Of the two cells produced by the asymmetric division of the microspore, the larger is the vegetative cell, which later forms the pollen tube and contains large amounts of starch, lipids and other reserves; the smaller is the generative cell, which later divides once to produce two **sperm cells**, the male gametes.

Soon after the generative cell forms, the callose wall between it and the pollen wall dissolves, and the generative cell detaches from the pollen wall and enters the cytoplasm of the vegetative cell, becoming a "cell within a cell": the generative cell is surrounded only by two plasma membranes (its own and that of the vegetative cell), with no cellulosic wall between them. The generative cell is at first spherical and later elongates into a spindle shape. It replicates its DNA soon after formation but synthesizes little RNA; in many angiosperms it contains almost no plastids, which is one of the cytological bases of the predominantly maternal inheritance of plastids in angiosperms ([7.4.11](/7.4.11)).

At the time the pollen matures and is shed, the number of cells is of two kinds:

- Two-celled pollen: at maturity only a vegetative cell and a generative cell are present, and the generative cell divides into two sperm cells in the pollen tube after germination, as in cotton, peach, plum, tea (*Camellia sinensis*), poplar, citrus, lily and tomato. About 70% of angiosperms belong to this type, which is the ancestral state.
- Three-celled pollen: the generative cell divides in the anther before the pollen matures, so that at maturity the pollen contains one vegetative cell and two sperm cells, as in rice, wheat, barley, maize (Poaceae), rapeseed (Brassicaceae) and sunflower (Asteraceae). The three-celled type has arisen independently in several groups.

Three-celled pollen is metabolically very active and germinates very quickly (grass pollen germinates within minutes of landing on the stigma), but it generally has a short life and is hard to store and germinate in vitro; two-celled pollen lives longer. In mature pollen (or in the pollen tube after germination) the vegetative nucleus and the two sperm cells are physically connected and move together as a unit, the male germ unit. A mature pollen grain with its pollen tube is the male gametophyte of angiosperms; it undergoes only two mitoses and is the most reduced among seed plants.

### 28.3.2. The Pollen Wall

Development of the pollen wall begins soon after meiosis ends: the exine forms first, and the intine is then formed on the inside of the exine.

**Definition 28.3.2 (Pollen Wall)** The wall of a mature pollen grain has two layers. The **exine** is thick and hard and lacks elasticity; its main component is **sporopollenin**, and its surface often carries various kinds of ornamentation. The intine is thin, soft and elastic, composed of cellulose, pectin, hemicellulose and protein, and surrounds the protoplast of the pollen. The exine has thin areas or openings of definite shape, number and position through which the pollen tube emerges, called **apertures**.

The exine itself has a complex layering (Figure 2C). In Erdtman's terminology the exine is divided into an outer layer (sexine) and an inner layer (nexine): the sexine consists of the roof-like tectum and a row of rod-like columellae supporting it; the nexine consists of the continuous foot layer and the innermost endexine. In the terminology of Faegri and Iversen, the tectum, columellae and foot layer together are called the ektexine, as opposed to the endexine; both sets of terms are in common use. Perforations, spines and reticulate patterns on the tectum make up the ornamentation of the pollen; the cavities between the columellae store substances and exine proteins derived from the tapetum. The pattern of the exine is already fixed at the tetrad stage: inside the callose wall, the microspore first forms a layer of polysaccharide primexine outside the plasma membrane as a template, and sporopollenin is then deposited on the template.

Sporopollenin is one of the most chemically resistant biopolymers known, withstanding strong acids, strong bases and microbial degradation, and is preserved in sedimentary rocks for hundreds of millions of years. It is built of aliphatic polyketides and phenylpropanoid monomers cross-linked by ester and ether bonds and is universal in the spore and pollen walls of land plants, one of the key adaptations for the colonization of land ([7.12.14](/7.12.14)). For this reason pollen of ancient plants can be found in strata and peat everywhere, from which the vegetation and climate of the time can be inferred; the discipline that uses pollen morphology to identify plants and study evolutionary relationships and geographic distribution is **palynology** ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)).

The size, shape and ornamentation of pollen grains vary among plants. Most angiosperm pollen is 15–50 μm in diameter, some Boraginaceae have pollen of only about 5 μm and some Annonaceae of up to 350 μm; rice pollen is about 42 μm, maize 77–89 μm, cotton 125–138 μm, and pumpkin pollen is larger still. The shape may be spherical (rice, wheat, maize, cotton), elliptical (rapeseed, broad bean, mulberry), triangular (tea) and so on; the exine surface may be smooth (cucumber, rapeseed, maize), may bear various protuberances and patterns (beech, willow) or may carry many spines (pumpkin, hollyhock); pine (a gymnosperm) pollen has two air sacs. The features of the exine are usually adapted to the mode of pollination: wind-borne pollen is small and smooth, while insect-borne pollen is large with spines or a reticulate pattern and carries sticky pollenkitt ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)).

### 28.3.3. Apertures and Pollen Types

At an aperture the exine is absent or very thin, and the intine is often thickened. By shape, apertures are of two kinds: elongated ones are called colpi (singular colpus, a furrow) and round ones pori (singular porus, a pore); a furrow with a pore at its center is a colporus. According to the number and position of apertures, the common pollen types are (Figure 2D):

| Type | Apertures | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Monosulcate | one furrow on the distal face | basal angiosperms such as *Magnolia*, water lily and *Amborella*, most monocots; gymnosperms such as cycads and ginkgo |
| Monoporate | one pore | Poaceae, Cyperaceae |
| Tricolpate | three furrows evenly spaced along the equator | basal eudicots such as buttercup and plane tree (*Platanus*) |
| Tricolporate | three furrows, each with a pore | most core eudicots: camellia, Rosaceae, Fabaceae, Asteraceae |
| Triporate | three pores | Betulaceae, nettle (*Urtica*) |
| Pantoporate | many pores scattered over the whole surface | Caryophyllaceae, Amaranthaceae (*Chenopodium*), Malvaceae (cotton has 8–16 apertures) |
| Inaperturate | no distinct aperture in the exine | Lauraceae, some Araceae |

The type of aperture has great phylogenetic significance. Monosulcate pollen is a plesiomorphy of seed plants and in angiosperms is retained in the ANA grade, the magnoliids and most monocots; tricolpate pollen and the types derived from it (tricolporate, triporate, pantoporate and so on) occur only in eudicots and are a synapomorphy of this large clade, which is why the eudicots are also called the tricolpates. In the fossil record tricolpate pollen appears about 125 million years ago (from the end of the Barremian to the Aptian, Early Cretaceous), which gives the earliest minimum date for the origin of the eudicots ([7.12.34](/7.12.34)).

Mature pollen contains 7%–26% protein, 24%–48% carbohydrate, 0.9%–14.5% fat, 0.9%–5.4% minerals and 7%–16% water, together with many vitamins (the B group predominating) and enzymes. According to the reserve, pollen is divided into starchy and fatty (oily) pollen, the former being common in wind-pollinated plants and the latter in insect-pollinated plants. Pollen is a source of protein for many insects (especially bee larvae), and bee pollen is also used as a nutritional supplement.

Some proteins of pollen (mostly in the cavities of the exine, released within minutes of contact with water) are powerful allergens and cause hay fever (seasonal allergic rhinitis and conjunctivitis). The main culprits are wind-borne pollens: they are produced in huge numbers, are shed directly into the air, are small and light, and can drift far and enter the respiratory tract; the pollen of insect-pollinated plants is large and sticky and rarely causes hay fever. In the northern temperate zone the allergenic pollen of spring comes mainly from trees such as oak, elm, birch and poplar, that of summer mainly from grasses, and that of autumn mainly from ragweed (*Ambrosia*; North America) and from *Artemisia*, Japanese hop (*Humulus scandens*) and Chenopodiaceae (northern China). Some wind-borne pollens produced in huge amounts, such as those of maize and pine, are rarely allergenic, showing that allergenicity depends on the nature of the pollen proteins.

## 28.4. Pollen Abortion and Male Sterility

### 28.4.1. Pollen Abortion

A mature anther generally releases normally developed pollen. Owing to internal or external factors, pollen sometimes fails to develop normally and loses its fertility, a phenomenon called pollen abortion. Its causes are many:

1. The pollen mother cells cannot undergo meiosis normally, for example when pollen mother cells adhere to one another into a cytoplasmic mass;
2. multipolar spindles or nucleolar adhesion appear in meiosis and the chromosomes are distributed abnormally;
3. the four microspores produced are of unequal size and cannot develop into normal pollen;
4. after meiosis the pollen stops at the uninucleate or binucleate stage and cannot produce sperm cells;
5. poor nutrition, so that the pollen cannot develop fully;
6. malfunction of the tapetum, for example when it fails to disintegrate at the proper time and instead continues to divide and enlarge, or disintegrates too early, so that the microspores receive neither nutrients nor callase;
7. unfavorable environmental conditions, such as temperatures that are too low or too high or severe drought, which usually act through the steps above.

### 28.4.2. Male Sterility

**Definition 28.4.1 (Male Sterility)** The phenomenon in which, for genetic or physiological reasons, the anthers or pollen of a plant fail to develop normally and become malformed or entirely degenerate while the pistil develops normally is called **male sterility**.

Male sterility has three morphological expressions: the anthers degenerate and shrivel completely, leaving only the filaments; the anthers produce no pollen; or the pollen produced is aborted. According to the mode of inheritance, male sterility is divided into:

| Type | Genetic basis | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Genic male sterility** | mutation of a nuclear gene, mostly recessive | crossing a sterile plant with a fertile one gives progeny that segregate in Mendelian ratios, and no maintainer line that propagates sterility stably can be found; one class is regulated by day length or temperature (photoperiod- and thermo-sensitive genic male sterility), being sterile under one condition and fertile under another | many pollen development mutants of *Arabidopsis* and rice; the photoperiod- and thermo-sensitive genic male sterile rice 'Nongken 58S' (discovered in Hubei in 1973 by Mingsong Shi) |
| **Cytoplasmic male sterility** | rearrangement of the mitochondrial genome producing chimeric open reading frames whose products interfere with mitochondrial function in the anther | maternally inherited; sterility is passed from the mother to all progeny | maize T cytoplasm (*urf13* gene) |
| Nuclear–cytoplasmic interaction male sterility | a sterile cytoplasm (S) acts together with recessive fertility genes (rf) in the nucleus; a dominant restorer gene (Rf) in the nucleus suppresses the effect of the sterile cytoplasm | only S(rfrf) is sterile; N(rfrf) and S(Rf_) are both fertile | rice 'wild abortive' (WA) type, rapeseed, sorghum |

Male-sterile plants are very useful in heterosis breeding: used as the female parent they dispense with manual emasculation and allow large-scale production of hybrid seed. Nuclear–cytoplasmic interaction male sterility can be organized into a three-line system:

- the sterile line (A line): S(rfrf), male sterile, used as the female parent in seed production;
- the maintainer line (B line): N(rfrf), fertile; crossed with the sterile line it gives progeny that are still S(rfrf), and it is used to propagate the sterile line;
- the restorer line (R line): N(RfRf) or S(RfRf), fertile; crossed with the sterile line it gives S(Rfrf) hybrids with restored fertility, used for field production.

This is precisely the use of **cytoplasmic male sterility** under nuclear–cytoplasmic interaction; the genetic analysis is given in [7.4.11](/7.4.11). Longping Yuan began research on male sterility in rice in 1964; in 1970 Bihu Li and Keshan Feng found a wild rice with aborted pollen ("wild abortive", WA) at Sanya, Hainan, and with it as the source of sterile cytoplasm Yuan's team and a national cooperative group achieved the three-line system for indica hybrid rice in 1973, after which hybrid rice was adopted over large areas in China and raised yields markedly. The two-line method using photoperiod- and thermo-sensitive genic male sterile lines dispenses with the maintainer line: the sterile line is sterile under long days and high temperature and is used for seed production, and is fertile under short days and low temperature and is used for its own propagation.

Cytoplasmic male sterility also has a cost: in the United States in the 1960s almost all hybrid maize was produced with T sterile cytoplasm, and the URF13 protein of T cytoplasm happens to make mitochondria sensitive to the toxin of race T of the southern corn leaf blight fungus, so that the epidemic of 1970 caused a large loss of maize yield. This shows that narrowing of the genetic base entails great risk.

In agriculture, male sterility is also induced with chemicals, called chemical emasculation, using agents such as 2,4-D, naphthaleneacetic acid, colchicine, gibberellin and ethephon.

## 28.5. Development of the Ovule

### 28.5.1. Structure of the Ovule

The **ovule** develops from a protuberance formed on the inner surface of the carpel at the ventral suture (on the placenta) (its origin is compared with that of the gymnosperm ovule in [7.12.25](/7.12.25)). By nature, the carpel corresponds to a megasporophyll and the ovule to a megasporangium surrounded by integuments, just as the anther is made of microsporangia. A mature ovule consists of the nucellus, integuments, micropyle, funiculus and chalaza (Figure 3).

**Definition 28.5.1 (Funiculus and Chalaza)** The stalk-like structure joining the base of the ovule to the placenta is called the funiculus; the vascular bundle of the carpel branches into the ovule through the funiculus. The region at the base of the ovule where integuments, nucellus and funiculus are joined is called the chalaza; it lies opposite the micropyle, and the vascular bundle ends in the chalaza after entering the ovule. In an anatropous ovule, the outer integument next to the funiculus is fused with it, forming an outward-projecting longitudinal ridge called the raphe.

### 28.5.2. Development of the Ovule

Development of the ovule begins with periclinal divisions of some cells beneath the epidermis of the placenta, which produce a protuberance, the ovule primordium. The tip of the primordium becomes the **nucellus**, the most important part of the ovule, from which the embryo sac will later arise; the base differentiates into the funiculus. Next the epidermal cells at the base of the nucellus divide faster and produce a ring-shaped protuberance that gradually grows upward and encloses the nucellus, leaving only a small opening at the apex; this covering is the **integument** and the small opening at its apex is the **micropyle**.

The number of integument layers and the thickness of the nucellus are important embryological characters:

- Bitegmic and unitegmic ovules: a majority of angiosperms have two integuments, the inner one called the inner integument and the outer one the outer integument; the outer integument arises in the same way from the base of the ovule after the inner integument, as in wheat, rice, rapeseed, cotton and lily. The micropyle is then often formed by the openings of the two integuments, which may be offset in a zigzag. Some plants have only one integument, such as walnut, sunflower and *Anemone*. Two integuments are the ancestral state in angiosperms, and the single integument arose secondarily from two in several groups, particularly in the asterids, where it is widespread.
- Crassinucellate and tenuinucellate ovules: distinguished by the thickness of the nucellar tissue above the megasporocyte; see the definition below.

**Definition 28.5.2 (Crassinucellate and Tenuinucellate Ovules)** An ovule in which the archesporial cell first divides periclinally to cut off one or several parietal cells, so that the megasporocyte is deeply buried beneath many layers of nucellar cells, is called a **crassinucellate ovule**; an ovule in which the archesporial cell becomes the megasporocyte directly, with only a single layer of nucellar epidermis above it, is called a **tenuinucellate ovule**.

The crassinucellate ovule is the ancestral state and is found in most basal angiosperms, monocots and rosids; the tenuinucellate ovule is mostly found in the asterids and often occurs together with a single integument.

### 28.5.3. Ovule Types

As the ovule grows, the funiculus and the other parts grow at unequal rates, so that the orientation of the ovule on the funiculus differs (Figure 3):

**Definition 28.5.3 (Ovule Types)** According to the relative positions of micropyle, nucellus and funiculus, ovules are divided into:

- the **orthotropous ovule**: all parts of the ovule grow evenly, funiculus, nucellus and micropyle lie in a straight line, and the micropyle is at the end opposite the funiculus, as in rhubarb (*Rheum*), dock (*Rumex*), buckwheat (Polygonaceae), pepper (*Piper*) and nettle;
- the **anatropous ovule**: the funiculus is long and slender, the body of the ovule is inverted through 180°, the nucellus is not curved, the micropyle is turned to the side of the base of the funiculus, and a raphe is present; this is the commonest type in angiosperms;
- the **hemitropous ovule**: one side of the ovule grows faster, so that the body of the ovule lies at a right angle to the funiculus and the micropyle faces to one side; Chinese textbooks often gloss it as amphitropous;
- the **campylotropous ovule**: the lower half of the ovule remains upright and the upper half curves so that the micropyle faces the base, close to the funiculus, and nucellus and embryo sac are also slightly curved, as in rapeseed, amaranth (*Amaranthus*), pea, broad bean and grasses;
- the **circinotropous ovule**: the funiculus is particularly long and curves around and encloses the ovule, as in *Opuntia* of the Cactaceae.

The anatropous ovule is considered the ancestral state in angiosperms: anatropy brings the micropyle close to the placenta, so that the pollen tube can grow directly from the surface of the placenta to the micropyle, which may be why it is so overwhelmingly dominant in angiosperms. The orthotropous ovule has arisen secondarily in several groups (Polygonaceae, Piperaceae, Urticaceae, Juglandaceae). In English-language literature the term amphitropous is also applied specially to ovules in which the body of the ovule is curved and the nucellus and embryo sac are bent into a horseshoe shape; this differs from the meaning of the term amphitropous given as the gloss for the "hemitropous ovule" in Chinese textbooks, which readers should keep in mind.

In the orthotropous ovule funiculus, chalaza and micropyle lie in a straight line; the anatropous ovule is inverted through 180°, with the micropyle near the base of the funiculus and the funiculus fused with the outer integument to form the raphe; in the hemitropous ovule the body of the ovule is at a right angle to the funiculus; in the campylotropous ovule the body of the ovule itself is curved, with the micropyle turned toward the funiculus; in the circinotropous ovule the long funiculus coils around and encloses the ovule](./plots/ovule-types.py)

## 28.6. Megasporogenesis and Development of the Embryo Sac

### 28.6.1. Megasporogenesis

When the integuments begin to form, or slightly earlier, changes also take place in the nucellus. Beneath the epidermis of the nucellus at the micropylar end a large archesporial cell with dense cytoplasm and a large nucleus appears. In a tenuinucellate ovule the archesporial cell becomes the megasporocyte directly; in a crassinucellate ovule the archesporial cell first divides periclinally once to form an outer parietal cell and an inner sporogenous cell, the parietal cell may later not divide again or may divide periclinally and anticlinally to form several layers of nucellar cells, and the sporogenous cell generally becomes the megasporocyte directly without dividing. Sometimes there is more than one archesporial cell, but usually only one continues to develop.

**Definition 28.6.1 (Megasporocyte)** The diploid cell of the nucellus that develops from the archesporial cell (or sporogenous cell) and is about to undergo meiosis to produce megaspores is called the megasporocyte (also the embryo sac mother cell).

Callose also accumulates around the megasporocyte. In the commonest case, after meiosis, with new walls formed after each nuclear division, it produces four haploid **megaspores** in a row at the micropylar end (a linear tetrad), or occasionally in a T shape. Of these, the one at the chalazal end, farthest from the micropyle, enlarges and becomes the functional megaspore; the three at the micropylar end degenerate and disappear by programmed cell death (Figure 4). This process is called megasporogenesis, and the subsequent development of the megaspore into the embryo sac is called megagametogenesis.

### 28.6.2. Development of the Polygonum-Type Embryo Sac and Structure of the Mature Embryo Sac

In about 70% of angiosperms the embryo sac develops from a single megaspore by three mitoses; it is called the Polygonum-type embryo sac, after the genus *Polygonum* in which it was first described in detail, and is also called the normal type. The process is as follows (Figure 4):

1. The functional megaspore absorbs nutrients from its surroundings and gradually enlarges, develops a large vacuole (the uninucleate embryo sac) and finally comes to occupy most of the volume of the nucellus.
2. The nucleus of the uninucleate embryo sac undergoes the first mitosis, and the two daughter nuclei move to the two ends of the embryo sac, with the large vacuole in the center (the binucleate embryo sac).
3. Each nucleus divides twice more in succession, forming four nuclei at each end (the four-nucleate and eight-nucleate embryo sacs). None of these three divisions is accompanied by cytokinesis or the formation of new walls, so there is a free-nuclear stage.
4. One of the four nuclei at each end moves to the center of the embryo sac, and these are the polar nuclei; three nuclei remain at each end.
5. Cellularization: each of the three nuclei at the micropylar end is surrounded by a mass of cytoplasm and a thin wall and becomes a cell, and the three nuclei at the chalazal end likewise become three cells; the two central polar nuclei and the abundant cytoplasm around them form one large cell.

![Development of the Polygonum-type embryo sac (micropylar end up). The megasporocyte in the nucellus forms four megaspores in a row by meiosis, the three at the micropylar end degenerate and the one at the chalazal end enlarges into the functional megaspore; three free-nuclear mitoses form in turn the two-, four- and eight-nucleate embryo sacs (large central vacuole); one nucleus from each end moves to the center to become a polar nucleus; after cellularization the micropylar end has the egg cell and two synergids (with a filiform apparatus at the micropylar end), the chalazal end has three antipodal cells, and the center is the central cell containing the two polar nuclei](./plots/embryo-sac-development.py)

**Definition 28.6.2 (Embryo Sac)** The female gametophyte that develops from the megaspore in the ovule of angiosperms is called the **embryo sac**. A mature Polygonum-type embryo sac has 7 cells and 8 nuclei: at the micropylar end one **egg cell** and two **synergids** together form the **egg apparatus**; at the chalazal end are three **antipodal cells**; and in the center is a large **central cell** containing two **polar nuclei**.

Structure and function of the cells of the mature embryo sac:

- The egg cell is the female gamete and develops into the embryo after fertilization. It has marked polarity: the large vacuole lies at the micropylar end, and the large nucleus and most of the cytoplasm at the chalazal end. The wall of the egg cell differs among plants: the egg cell of maize and cotton has a wall only on the micropylar side and elsewhere only a plasma membrane; the egg cell of shepherd's purse is walled almost entirely, discontinuous only at the chalazal end. Plasmodesmata connect the egg cell with the synergids on either side.
- The **synergids** are the most complex in structure of the cells of the embryo sac and play the greatest role in fertilization. The two synergids lie close against the egg cell, and the three are arranged in a triangle at the micropylar end. The polarity of the synergids is the reverse of that of the egg cell: the nucleus is at the micropylar end and the large vacuole at the chalazal end; the wall is incomplete, thicker at the micropylar end and only a plasma membrane at the chalazal end. The wall at the micropylar end of the synergid grows into the cell as many irregular plate-like or finger-like projections, the filiform apparatus, which greatly increases the surface area of the plasma membrane so that the synergid, like a transfer cell, can absorb, store and transport substances from the nucellus. The synergids secrete signal molecules that attract the pollen tube and guide it into the embryo sac ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)); when the pollen tube arrives, one synergid degenerates and the pollen tube ruptures in it, releasing the sperm cells. Synergids are short-lived and generally disintegrate soon after fertilization.
- The central cell is the largest cell of the embryo sac, mostly occupied by one vacuole, with the two polar nuclei often close to the egg apparatus and surrounded by cytoplasm. In some plants the two polar nuclei fuse before fertilization into one diploid secondary nucleus (as in cotton), and in others they fuse only at fertilization together with the sperm nucleus. After fusion with the second sperm cell the central cell develops into the endosperm.
- The number, shape and number of nuclei of the antipodal cells vary greatly; even in the Polygonum-type embryo sac there are not always three antipodal cells, and they often continue to divide into a group of cells: in grasses the antipodal cells can proliferate to several tens, and in some species of the Bambusoideae to about 300. The nuclei of antipodal cells may be one, two or several. They are usually short-lived and often disappear, or leave only traces, when the embryo sac matures; but in grasses they persist longer and have the features of transfer cells, and are thought to transport nutrients from the mother plant into the embryo sac.

### 28.6.3. Types of Embryo Sac

According to the number of megaspore nuclei taking part in forming the embryo sac, embryo sac development falls into three broad classes (Figure 5):

- Monosporic embryo sac: in meiosis of the megasporocyte, walls form after both divisions, giving four megaspores, of which only one develops into the embryo sac;
- Bisporic embryo sac: a wall forms after the first meiotic division, giving a dyad; one of the dyad cells degenerates, and the other undergoes the second division without wall formation, so that two haploid nuclei jointly take part in forming the embryo sac;
- Tetrasporic embryo sac: no wall forms after either meiotic division, so that four haploid nuclei coexist in the megasporocyte and all take part in forming the embryo sac.

Each class contains several types, named after the genus in which they were first described:

| Embryo sac type | Megaspore nuclei involved | Number of nuclear divisions (after meiosis) | Mature embryo sac | Ploidy of the central cell (primary endosperm nucleus) | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Polygonum | 1 (chalazal) | 3 | 8 nuclei, 7 cells: egg apparatus 3, central cell (2 polar nuclei), antipodal cells 3 | 2n (3n) | about 70% of angiosperms |
| Oenothera | 1 (micropylar) | 2 | 4 nuclei, 4 cells: egg apparatus 3, central cell (1 polar nucleus), no antipodal cells | n (2n) | Onagraceae |
| Allium | 2 (the chalazal dyad cell) | 2 | 8 nuclei, 7 cells, same as the Polygonum type | 2n (3n) | onion, arrowhead (*Sagittaria*) |
| Fritillaria | 4 (1 at the micropylar end, 3 fused at the chalazal end) | 2, with a "second four-nucleate stage" in between | 8 nuclei, 7 cells, but the chalazal polar nucleus and the antipodal cells are 3n | 4n (5n) | lily, fritillary |
| Peperomia | 4 | 2 | 16 nuclei: egg cell, 1 synergid, 6 peripheral cells, central cell with 8 polar nuclei | 8n (9n) | *Peperomia* (Piperaceae) |

The lily embryo sac is a common material in botany teaching and laboratory work. Its mature embryo sac has 8 nuclei and 7 cells, as in the Polygonum type, but its development is of the tetrasporic Fritillaria type: after meiosis of the megasporocyte, the four haploid nuclei are arranged 1 + 3, one at the micropylar end and three at the chalazal end; the micropylar nucleus then divides normally into two haploid nuclei, while at the chalazal end the spindles of the three nuclei fuse into one when they divide, producing two triploid nuclei that are larger and have more nucleoli, and a second four-nucleate stage appears (two n, two 3n); each of the four nuclei then divides once, giving eight nuclei, four haploid at the micropylar end and four triploid at the chalazal end. One nucleus from each end moves to the center as a polar nucleus, the micropylar end forms the egg cell and two synergids, and the chalazal end forms three antipodal cells. The antipodal cells and one polar nucleus of lily are therefore triploid, and the primary endosperm nucleus after fertilization is pentaploid. Thus when lily sections are used to observe embryo sac development, what one sees is "megasporocyte → four-nucleate embryo sac → eight-nucleate embryo sac", not the "one-nucleate → two-nucleate → four-nucleate → eight-nucleate" sequence of the Polygonum type.

The earliest-diverging angiosperms do not have the Polygonum-type embryo sac. In the Nymphaeales and Austrobaileyales the mature embryo sac has only 4 cells and 4 nuclei (an egg apparatus and a central cell with only one polar nucleus), similar to the Oenothera type and often called the Nuphar/Schisandra type, with a diploid primary endosperm nucleus; the embryo sac of *Amborella* has 8 cells and 9 nuclei, with three synergids in the egg apparatus (Friedman 2006). On this basis Friedman and Williams (2003) proposed the module hypothesis: the angiosperm embryo sac is built of "four-nucleate modules", each produced by two divisions of one nucleus, one nucleus of which migrates into the central cell; the ancestral embryo sac had a single module (4 nuclei), and the Polygonum-type embryo sac results from the duplication of a second module at the chalazal end (8 nuclei), which gave the central cell a second polar nucleus and so changed the endosperm from diploid to triploid ([7.12.30](/7.12.30)).

## Summary

Development of the male and female gametophytes compared:

| | Male gametophyte (pollen grain) | Female gametophyte (embryo sac) |
|---|---|---|
| Site | pollen sacs (microsporangia) of the anther | nucellus (megasporangium) of the ovule |
| Position of the archesporial cells | beneath the epidermis at the four corners of the anther, often several | beneath the nucellar epidermis at the micropylar end, often one |
| First division of the archesporial cell | periclinal: primary parietal cell (→ endothecium, middle layer, tapetum) + primary sporogenous cell | crassinucellate: parietal cell (→ nucellar tissue) + sporogenous cell; tenuinucellate: develops directly |
| Mother cell | pollen mother cell (microsporocyte), numerous | megasporocyte (embryo sac mother cell), usually one |
| Products of meiosis | tetrad, all four microspores develop | linear tetrad, the one at the chalazal end functional and three degenerate (Polygonum type) |
| Cytokinesis | successive (monocots) or simultaneous (eudicots) | usually a wall after each division (monosporic) |
| Nuclear divisions after meiosis | 2 (asymmetric division, division of the generative cell) | 3, free-nuclear (Polygonum type) |
| Mature structure | vegetative cell + generative cell (two-celled) or vegetative cell + 2 sperm cells (three-celled) | 8 nuclei, 7 cells: egg cell, 2 synergids, central cell (2 polar nuclei), 3 antipodal cells |
| Gametes | 2 sperm cells | egg cell (and the central cell, which takes part in the second fertilization) |
| Nutrient source | tapetum | nucellus; transported by synergids and antipodal cells |
| Protective structures | pollen wall (sporopollenin exine, intine) | integuments, nucellus, ovary wall |

Terms for the structure of anther and ovule:

| Structure | Terms | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Anther wall | epidermis, endothecium (fibrous layer), middle layer, tapetum | the mature anther retains only the epidermis and fibrous layer; the band-like thickenings of the fibrous layer cause dehiscence |
| Types of anther wall development | basic, dicotyledonous, monocotyledonous, reduced | distinguished by whether the two secondary parietal layers divide further |
| Tapetum | secretory, amoeboid | nutrition, callase, sporopollenin precursors, pollenkitt, recognition proteins |
| Pollen wall | exine (tectum, columellae, foot layer, endexine), intine | exine contains sporopollenin; exine is absent at the apertures |
| Apertures | monosulcate, monoporate, tricolpate, tricolporate, triporate, pantoporate | monosulcate is a plesiomorphy; the tricolpate group is a synapomorphy of eudicots |
| Male sterility | genic, cytoplasmic, nuclear–cytoplasmic interaction | three lines: sterile line S(rfrf), maintainer line N(rfrf), restorer line (RfRf) |
| Ovule | nucellus, integuments, micropyle, funiculus, chalaza, raphe | bitegmic and unitegmic; crassinucellate and tenuinucellate |
| Ovule types | orthotropous, anatropous, hemitropous, campylotropous, circinotropous | anatropous is commonest and ancestral |

The types of embryo sac (table in Section 28.6.3) can be summarized as follows: the number of megaspore nuclei taking part (1, 2, 4) determines "monosporic, bisporic, tetrasporic"; the number of nuclear divisions after meiosis determines the number of nuclei in the mature embryo sac (3 divisions → 8 nuclei, 2 divisions → 4 or 8 nuclei); and the ploidy of the participating nuclei determines the ploidy of the central cell and the endosperm.

## Questions

1. In the development of the anther, into which structures do the descendants of an archesporial cell finally differentiate? What is the fate of each?

   **Answer** The archesporial cell divides periclinally into a primary parietal cell and a primary sporogenous cell. The primary parietal cell forms, with the epidermis, the wall of the pollen sac: the endothecium develops into the fibrous layer, which is retained at maturity and is responsible for anther dehiscence; the middle layer is flattened and absorbed and disappears during meiosis; the tapetum supplies nutrients and materials for pollen development and breaks down before the pollen matures. The primary sporogenous cell divides to form pollen mother cells, which produce microspores by meiosis, and these develop into pollen grains (the male gametophyte).

2. The pollen of a rice line stops developing at the uninucleate stage, and microscopy shows that the tapetum fails to break down long after meiosis of the pollen mother cells. Which kind of male sterility might this be? How can crosses be used to decide whether it is genic or cytoplasmic sterility?

   **Answer** This is pollen abortion caused by delayed programmed cell death of the tapetum, which can be caused by nuclear genes or by sterile cytoplasm. Use the sterile plant as the female parent in crosses with several fertile lines. If F₁ is fertile, sterile plants segregate in Mendelian ratios in F₂, and the reciprocal cross with it as the male parent produces no sterility, the sterility is genic. If crosses with some lines give F₁ that are all sterile (these lines are maintainers, with nuclei rfrf), the sterility is transmitted with the maternal cytoplasm, and crosses with other lines give F₁ with restored fertility (restorer lines, carrying Rf), the sterility is cytoplasmic male sterility with nuclear–cytoplasmic interaction.

3. Compare the ploidy of the primary endosperm nucleus in the Polygonum, Fritillaria and Oenothera types of embryo sac, and explain the reasons.

   **Answer** Polygonum type: two haploid polar nuclei + one haploid sperm nucleus, 3n. Fritillaria type: the chalazal polar nucleus is a descendant of the triploid nucleus formed by fusion of three megaspore nuclei and is 3n, the micropylar polar nucleus is n, and adding the sperm nucleus gives 5n. Oenothera type (and the four-celled embryo sacs of the Nymphaeales and Austrobaileyales): the central cell has only one haploid polar nucleus, and after fertilization it is 2n. The ploidy depends on the number and ploidy of the polar nuclei contained in the central cell.

4. Why is "tricolpate pollen" important in angiosperm systematics? If the pollen of a plant is monosulcate, can one conclude that it is a monocot?

   **Answer** Tricolpate pollen and the types derived from it occur only in eudicots and are a synapomorphy of this monophyletic group, so they distinguish eudicots from other angiosperms and, in fossils, give a date for the appearance of eudicots (about 125 million years ago). Monosulcate pollen is a plesiomorphy of seed plants and occurs not only in monocots but also in the ANA grade and the magnoliids (such as *Magnolia* and nutmeg, *Myristica*) and in cycads and ginkgo, so a monocot cannot be concluded from it. A plesiomorphy cannot be used to define a group.

5. What simplifications do the female and male gametophytes of angiosperms show compared with those of pine? What adaptive significance might these simplifications have?

   **Answer** Male gametophyte: pine pollen has prothallial cells, a tube cell and a generative cell (which divides again into a stalk cell and a body cell), whereas the angiosperm has only a vegetative cell and a generative cell (or two sperm cells) and undergoes only two mitoses. Female gametophyte: the pine female gametophyte has several thousand cells and archegonia and accumulates large amounts of nutrients before fertilization; the angiosperm embryo sac has only 7 cells and no archegonia, and the nutritive tissue (endosperm) forms only after double fertilization. Simplification of the gametophytes shortens the time from pollination to fertilization (usually a few hours to a few days in angiosperms, about a year in pine) and lets the mother plant invest nutrients only in ovules in which fertilization has succeeded, reducing waste; this fits the short life cycles and rapid reproduction of angiosperms.
