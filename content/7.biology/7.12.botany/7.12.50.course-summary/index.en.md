This course has two threads. The first is systematics: we begin with the cyanobacteria, which are not plants, pass through the red algae, the algae with secondary plastids (with the fungi and lichens inserted among them), and the green algae and charophytes that colonized land, and continue through bryophytes, lycophytes, ferns and gymnosperms to the orders and key families of angiosperms ([7.12.6](/7.12.6)–[7.12.18](/7.12.18), [7.12.25](/7.12.25)–[7.12.26](/7.12.26), [7.12.33](/7.12.33)–[7.12.49](/7.12.49)). The second is morphology: cells, tissues, vegetative organs and reproductive organs, and their adaptations to the environment ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)–[7.12.5](/7.12.5), [7.12.19](/7.12.19)–[7.12.24](/7.12.24), [7.12.27](/7.12.27)–[7.12.32](/7.12.32)). The two threads are interleaved in the course ([7.12.1](/7.12.1#sec-1.5)), and this chapter brings them together. We first list, along the phylogenetic tree, the most important evolutionary novelties of the plant kingdom and how they appear in morphology and anatomy (Section 50.1), then follow the evolution of the alternation of generations that runs through all land plants (Section 50.2), then discuss how morphology and classification explain each other (Section 50.3) and what changed and what stayed the same between the Cronquist system and APG IV (Section 50.4), and finally collect the links between this course and others (Section 50.5). The chapter introduces no new material; the details of each item are in the chapters cited. A summary of the spore-bearing plants is also given in [7.12.18](/7.12.18), and the principles and evidence of angiosperm classification are in [7.12.33](/7.12.33).

## 50.1. Six Key Innovations

### 50.1.1. The Main Line and the Nested Groups

From algae to angiosperms, the evolution of the plant kingdom can be summarized as six novelties added one after another: plastids, multicellularity, the embryo, vascular tissue (together with true roots and leaves), the seed, and the flower and fruit. Their positions on the phylogenetic tree define a series of nested groups: **Archaeplastida** ⊃ **Viridiplantae** ⊃ **Streptophyta** ⊃ **embryophytes** ⊃ **vascular plants** ⊃ **seed plants** ⊃ **angiosperms** (Definition 1.1.1, Definition 1.1.3, Definition 33.1.1). Each new character gave its bearers a new way of life: photosynthetic autotrophy, a large plant body, life on land, a tall sporophyte, independence of fertilization from water, and cooperation with animals in pollination and dispersal.

In evolutionary biology a **key innovation** in the strict sense ([7.10.31](/7.10.31#def-31.5.2)) is a new character that raises the rate of diversification of a lineage, which has to be tested by methods such as sister-group comparison. In this chapter we use the term more loosely, for a novelty that opened a new adaptive zone and was inherited by the whole group that followed.

Two points about this table deserve notice. First, plastids, the embryo, the seed and the flower each arose only once in the known groups, and they define clades; multicellularity, roots, megaphylls, heterospory, wood and vessels arose independently several times (Section 50.3.2). Second, each novelty is built on the previous one: without the embryo and the sporophyte there would be no vascular tissue developing on the sporophyte; without heterospory and a retained megaspore there would be no ovule; without the ovule there would be nothing for the carpel to enclose. Chinese textbooks often summarize this process as the plant kingdom advancing from aquatic to terrestrial, from simple to complex, and from "lower" to "higher". This needs a qualification: it describes only the line of land plants, whereas living algae, bryophytes and ferns have also undergone a long evolution and are not ancestors of the angiosperms ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.6.3)).

The rise and fall of the groups corresponds roughly to the appearance of these innovations. From the Silurian to the Early Devonian early vascular plants dominated, and most of them had neither leaves nor roots; from the Middle Devonian lycophytes, horsetails, ferns and **seed ferns** formed the swamp forests of the Carboniferous; from the Permian gymnosperms dominated, until angiosperms appeared in the Early Cretaceous and rose to dominance in many regions about 100 million years ago ([7.12.18](/7.12.18#sec-18.1)).

## 50.2. The Evolution of the Alternation of Generations: The Rise and Fall of the Gametophyte

The life cycle of all embryophytes is a heteromorphic alternation of generations with sporic meiosis (Definition 6.4.3, [7.12.18](/7.12.18#sec-18.3)). In the evolution of land plants, the relative standing of the two generations passed through four turning points:

1. Insertion of the sporophyte: charophytes all have zygotic meiosis and no multicellular diploid stage; in embryophytes the zygote stays in the archegonium, first divides mitotically to form the embryo and sporophyte, and only then undergoes meiosis to produce spores (the **antithetic theory**, Definition 14.5.1). In bryophytes the sporophyte remains parasitic on the gametophyte throughout its life.
2. The sporophyte becomes independent and dominant: in vascular plants the sporophyte has vascular tissue, can branch and lives independently; the gametophyte is reduced to a small **prothallus** (Definition 17.2.7) but is still free-living, and fertilization still needs water.
3. Endospory of the gametophyte: in heterosporous plants (*Selaginella*, *Isoëtes*, *Marsilea*, *Salvinia*) the unisexual gametophytes develop inside the spore wall and live on the food stored in the spore; in seed plants the megaspore never leaves the megasporangium, the female gametophyte develops on the maternal sporophyte, and the male gametophyte is dispersed as **pollen**, whose **pollen tube** carries the sperm to the egg (Definition 25.1.3).
4. Extreme reduction of the gametophyte: the **embryo sac** of angiosperms usually has 7 cells and 8 nuclei and no archegonium; the pollen grain has only 2–3 cells. Because of double fertilization, the endosperm develops only after fertilization, whereas the female gametophyte of gymnosperms, which serves as the nutritive tissue, is formed before fertilization, so that the timing of nutritional investment differs between the two ([7.12.25](/7.12.25#sec-25.7)).

There are three explanations, not mutually exclusive, for the dominance of the sporophyte ([7.12.18](/7.12.18#sec-18.3.2)): diploidy masks recessive deleterious mutations; the sporophyte is not limited by the need for water in fertilization and can grow tall to compete for light and lift the sporangia; and branching of the sporophyte lets one fertilization produce many sporangia. Seed plants went further and brought the gametophyte entirely under the protection and nourishment of the sporophyte, which is one of the reasons seed plants could enter dry habitats ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)).

## 50.3. How Morphology and Classification Explain Each Other

### 50.3.1. Morphological Terms Are the Language of Classification

Taxonomy begins with description: the circumscription of a family, genus or species must in the end be expressed in observable morphological characters and written into descriptions, **identification keys** (Definition 2.5.1) and **floral formulae** (Definition 27.6.2). Even though the backbone of the APG system comes mainly from DNA sequences, identifying plants in the field, in herbaria and among fossils still depends on the terminology of vegetative and reproductive organs. Morphological terms play a second role in classification: the **synapomorphies** of many clades are themselves morphological, anatomical or chemical characters, for example the tricolpate pollen of eudicots (Definition 40.1.1), the P-type sieve-element plastids of monocots (Definition 33.3.2), the **betalains** of the core Caryophyllales (Definition 45.4.1), the **glucosinolates** of the Brassicales (Definition 44.6.1), and the unitegmic, tenuinucellate ovules and sympetalous corollas of the asterids ([7.12.46](/7.12.46#sec-46.1)). Molecular phylogenetics established the tree, and morphological characters acquired polarity and meaning on it.

### 50.3.2. Homology, Homoplasy and Convergence

Conversely, phylogeny gives morphology a historical explanation. The similarity of two groups may come from a common ancestor (**homology**), or it may have been acquired independently under similar selection pressures (**convergent evolution**, parallel evolution and reversal, together called **homoplasy**, [7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.6.2)); only homologous derived characters indicate relationship, whereas shared ancestral characters (**symplesiomorphies**) do not. Many disputes in the traditional systems came down in the end to deciding whether a similar character was homologous or homoplastic. Examples from this course:

These examples illustrate the conclusion of [7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.6.3): the position of a group cannot be judged from a few "primitive" or "advanced" characters, and morphological similarity cannot be taken directly as evidence of relationship. This does not, however, lower the value of morphology. Whether two structures are homologous is decided in the end by comparing position, connections, vascular tissue and ontogeny ([7.12.24](/7.12.24#sec-24.1)), and the phylogenetic tree tells us which similarities need to be explained by adaptation: carnivory, parasitism, succulence and the aquatic habit recur in different families precisely because they are adaptations to particular environments (nutrient-poor bogs, hosts, drought and water) ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)).

## 50.4. From Cronquist to APG IV

### 50.4.1. What Changed

The fundamental difference between the **Cronquist system** (Definition 33.4.4) and the **APG system** (Definition 33.4.5) lies in principle: the former relies on overall morphological similarity and the criteria of "primitive and advanced characters" and allows paraphyletic groups; the latter recognizes only **clades** and uses no formal ranks above the order, expressing relationships by clades. The resulting changes fall on several levels (for the fate of the families see the overview table in [7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.5.2)):

| Level | Cronquist (1981) | APG IV (2016) | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basis | Overall morphological, anatomical and chemical characters, evolutionary criteria | Molecular phylogeny (chloroplast, mitochondrial and nuclear genes), only clades recognized | — |
| Largest divisions | Magnoliopsida (dicots) and Liliopsida (monocots) | The ANA grade, magnoliids, Chloranthales, monocots, Ceratophyllales, eudicots | The "dicots" are a **paraphyletic group**; the monocots are nested within the angiosperms |
| Subclasses | 6 subclasses of Magnoliopsida, 5 of Liliopsida | No subclasses, replaced by clades (superrosids, superasterids, etc.) | Magnoliidae, Hamamelidae, Dilleniidae and Rosidae are not clades |
| Orders | 83 orders | 64 orders | Hamamelidales, Urticales and the catkin-bearing orders broken up; Caryophyllales enlarged (including Polygonaceae, Plumbaginaceae, Nepenthaceae and others) |
| Merged families | The three families of Fabales; Malvaceae, Tiliaceae, Sterculiaceae and Bombacaceae; Chenopodiaceae; Asclepiadaceae; Aceraceae and Hippocastanaceae; Illiciaceae; Dipsacaceae and Valerianaceae | Fabaceae in the broad sense; Malvaceae in the broad sense; included in Amaranthaceae; included in Apocynaceae; included in Sapindaceae; included in Schisandraceae; included in Caprifoliaceae | The old boundaries cut across a clade |
| Split families | Liliaceae, Scrophulariaceae, Euphorbiaceae and Capparaceae in the broad sense | More than ten families in Liliales and Asparagales; Plantaginaceae, Orobanchaceae and others; Phyllanthaceae and others; Capparaceae and Cleomaceae | The old families were paraphyletic or polyphyletic |
| Problematic families | Rafflesiaceae, Nepenthaceae, Hydatellaceae, Ceratophyllaceae and others of uncertain or wrong placement | Malpighiales, Caryophyllales, Nymphaeales, Ceratophyllales | Highly specialized or reduced groups |
| Totals | 383 families | 416 families | — |

### 50.4.2. What Did Not Change

The changes concern mainly the arrangement of the higher ranks and the circumscription of a few large families. What did not change is just as important:

- The great majority of families and almost all genera and species are unchanged; the circumscriptions of key families such as Magnoliaceae, Lauraceae, Orchidaceae, Poaceae, Ranunculaceae, Rosaceae, Brassicaceae, Apiaceae and Asteraceae are the same as in the Cronquist system or differ only slightly.
- Many traditional groups proved to be monophyletic: the angiosperms, the **monocots**, Caryophyllidae (enlarged to the Caryophyllales), the core of Asteridae (roughly the lamiids and campanulids within the **asterids**), Cronquist's Fabales (Fabaceae in the broad sense), and others.
- Morphological diagnostic characters remain valid: the diagnostic features of families (Section 50.3.1) apply equally in APG IV; for families that were split or merged, new morphological boundaries can often be found too (for example the bulbs and extrorse anthers of Liliaceae in the narrow sense, the umbels and membranous spathes of Amaryllidaceae in the broad sense), although these boundaries have to be re-examined on the phylogenetic tree.
- Many inferences from morphological and chemical evidence were confirmed by molecular evidence: *Paeonia* does not belong to Ranunculaceae (Cronquist had already placed it in its own family, Paeoniaceae), palynology had long suggested that lotus and water lilies are not closely related, and the core Caryophyllales, circumscribed by betalains, form a clade.

Learning APG IV therefore does not mean abandoning earlier morphological knowledge but placing it on a new framework: morphological characters are the tools of identification and description, and the phylogenetic tree is the framework that explains them. Floras and many textbooks in China still follow the Engler or Cronquist system, and when reading them one has to convert with the comparison table in [7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.5.2).

Main conclusions:

- The six key innovations (Section 50.1.1) define nested clades: plastids (Archaeplastida), the embryo (embryophytes), vascular tissue (vascular plants), the seed (seed plants), and the flower and fruit (angiosperms); multicellularity, roots, megaphylls, heterospory, wood and vessels arose independently several times.
- The order of the morphological part, cells, tissues, vegetative organs and reproductive organs, roughly follows the order in which these structures appeared in evolution (Section 50.1.2).
- The four turning points of the alternation of generations: insertion of the sporophyte (embryophytes), a free-living and dominant sporophyte (vascular plants), endospory of the gametophyte (heterospory and seed plants), and extreme reduction of the gametophyte (angiosperms) (Section 50.2).
- Morphological terms are the language of classification, and phylogeny gives morphology a historical explanation: only homologous derived characters indicate relationship; lotus and water lily, cacti and spurges, carnivory, parasitism and catkins are typical cases of convergence (Section 50.3).
- APG IV changed the arrangement of the higher ranks and the circumscription of a few large families, whereas the great majority of families and their morphological diagnostic characters are unchanged (Section 50.4).

## Questions

1. Why are plastids, the embryo, the seed and the flower said to be characters that define clades, whereas multicellularity, roots and heterospory are not? Give one piece of evidence for each.

   **Answer** The first four arose only once in the known groups, and every living group that has them descends from a single ancestor: the plastids of Archaeplastida all have two membranes and come from the same primary endosymbiosis; all embryophytes have an embryo in the archegonium and an alternation of generations with sporic meiosis; the ovules of seed plants have a uniform structure; angiosperms all have carpels and double fertilization. Multicellularity arose independently in red algae, brown algae, green algae and streptophytes, and brown algae and green plants even belong to different eukaryotic supergroups; roots arose separately in lycophytes and euphyllophytes, and the two kinds of root develop differently; heterospory arose separately in *Selaginella*, *Isoëtes*, aquatic ferns and the ancestors of seed plants.

2. Summarize the change in the gametophyte from bryophytes to angiosperms under the heading "where the gametophyte lives", and explain how double fertilization makes the nutritional investment of angiosperms different from that of gymnosperms.

   **Answer** The gametophyte of bryophytes is free-living and is the main body of the plant; that of ferns is a small, free-living prothallus; in heterosporous plants such as *Selaginella* the gametophytes develop inside the wall of the shed spore; in seed plants the female gametophyte stays in the ovule and is nourished by the maternal sporophyte, while the male gametophyte is dispersed as pollen; in angiosperms the female gametophyte is further reduced to a 7-celled embryo sac enclosed in the ovary. The female gametophyte of gymnosperms, which serves as the nutritive tissue, is formed before fertilization, so this nutrition has been invested even if the ovule is not fertilized; the endosperm of angiosperms results from double fertilization and develops only after fertilization, so the investment depends on the success of fertilization.

3. Cronquist placed the lotus in Nymphaeales, Nepenthaceae in Dilleniidae, and the catkin-bearing groups in Hamamelidae. How does APG IV treat them? What do these changes mean for morphology?

   **Answer** Nelumbonaceae moved to Proteales among the eudicots, as sister to Platanaceae + Proteaceae, whereas Nymphaeaceae remain in Nymphaeales in the ANA grade; Nepenthaceae and Droseraceae moved to the carnivorous clade of Caryophyllales; the catkin-bearing groups are scattered between Malpighiales (Salicaceae, which Cronquist placed in the Dilleniidae) and Fagales (Fagaceae, Betulaceae, Juglandaceae, Myricaceae, Casuarinaceae). This shows that the aquatic form of lotus and water lily and the wind-pollination characters of catkins are convergent and cannot serve as evidence of relationship. Morphology has not lost its value, however: the tricolpate pollen and free carpels of the lotus had long suggested that it differs from water lilies, and on the new tree these characters become synapomorphies and help to explain which similarities have to be accounted for by adaptation.

4. What should one rely on to decide whether two similar structures are homologous? Illustrate with stem tendrils and leaf tendrils, and with the spines of cacti and of spurges.

   **Answer** One relies on the position of the structure on the plant body, its connections with other organs, its internal vascular tissue and its ontogeny, and where possible on a comparison of the genes controlling development, not on external form or function; between groups one also looks at their distribution on the phylogenetic tree. A stem tendril arises in a leaf axil (the position of a branch), while a leaf tendril is a leaf or part of a leaf modified, so the two are analogous organs. The spines of cacti arise on areoles, which are short shoots, and the spines are modified leaves; the spines of succulent spurges are mostly modified stipules or peduncles, and because Cactaceae and Euphorbiaceae belong to Caryophyllales and Malpighiales respectively, the fleshy spiny stems of the two are the result of convergence.

5. Someone says: "Since the APG system is based on DNA, there is no longer any need to learn morphological terms." What do you think?

   **Answer** This claim is wrong. First, identifying plants depends on morphology: identification in the field, in herbaria and in floras relies on keys and morphological descriptions, and DNA barcodes are only a supplement. Second, fossils preserve only morphology, so the origin and early evolution of plants (the origin of the seed and the flower) can be studied only through morphology and anatomy. Third, the synapomorphies of many clades are morphological, anatomical or chemical characters (tricolpate pollen, sieve-element plastids, betalains); once the molecular tree is established, the evolution, convergence and adaptation of morphological characters have to be interpreted on the tree. APG IV changed the arrangement of the higher ranks and the circumscription of a few families, and the morphological diagnostic characters of the great majority of families remain valid.
