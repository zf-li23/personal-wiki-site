Pollen is produced in the anther and the embryo sac in the ovule ([7.12.28](/7.12.28)), yet the two gametophytes may be far apart. The pollen must leave the anther and land on the stigma of the same flower or of another plant; the pollen tube must then grow through the stigma, style and ovary into the ovule and deliver the sperm cells to the egg cell and the central cell. This chapter treats the three stages of the process in turn: flowering, pollination and fertilization. Pollination is where angiosperms interact most closely with animals, and nearly everything about a flower, including its color, scent, shape and flowering time, can be understood in terms of pollination; the problem of how to avoid selfing explains many otherwise curious floral structures. The theory of the evolution of mating systems (inbreeding depression, the automatic advantage of selfing, and balancing selection on self-incompatibility) was covered in [7.10.14](/7.10.14), and the theory of coevolution and mutualism in [7.10.17](/7.10.17); here we cite only the conclusions and concentrate on plant morphology and mechanisms. The development of the embryo and endosperm after double fertilization is treated in [7.12.30](/7.12.30), the fruit in [7.12.31](/7.12.31), and seed and fruit dispersal, together with a summary of plant adaptations to pollination, in [7.12.32](/7.12.32).

## 29.1. Flowering

**Definition 29.1.1 (Anthesis)** The opening of a flower, which has until then been tightly enclosed by the perianth, so that the stamens and pistil are exposed when the pollen in the stamens and the embryo sac in the pistil (or one of the two) are mature, is called flowering or *anthesis*.

Flowering habits differ greatly among plants, mainly in the age at which flowering begins, the season of flowering and the duration of flowering. They are adaptations to the conditions of the plant's native habitat and are genetically determined:

- Age at first flowering. Annuals and biennials usually flower after a few months of growth, flower once in their lifetime, and die after flowering and fruiting. Perennials flower regularly every year once they reach flowering age and continue to do so for many years; the age at first flowering varies widely, from 3–5 years in peach (*Prunus persica*) and 10–12 years in birch (*Betula*) to 20–25 years in linden (*Tilia*).
- Monocarpy and polycarpy. A plant that flowers and fruits only once and dies afterwards is a *monocarpic plant*. Besides annuals and biennials, some perennials are monocarpic, such as many bamboos (*Phyllostachys*, *Fargesia* and others, whose populations flower simultaneously after decades of vegetative growth and then die over large areas), century plants (*Agave*, which send up a huge inflorescence after 10 to several decades of vegetative growth and die after fruiting) and the talipot palm (*Corypha umbraculifera*). Most perennials flower every year and are *polycarpic plants*.
- Season of flowering. Most plants flower in spring, but some flower in summer, autumn or winter (wintersweet, *Chimonanthus praecox*, flowers in winter). Most plants produce leaves before flowers, but some flower before leafing out, such as wintersweet, magnolia (*Yulania*), plum (*Prunus mume*) and winter jasmine (*Jasminum nudiflorum*); these are mostly woody plants that flower in early spring.
- Time of day. Most plants flower in the daytime, but some open in the evening or at night, such as tuberose (*Agave amica*), evening primrose (*Oenothera*), night-blooming cereus (*Epiphyllum oxypetalum*) and four o'clock (*Mirabilis jalapa*). The flowers of plants in one region open at fairly fixed times of day, and Linnaeus used this to design a "floral clock".
- Duration of flowering. A single flower or a whole plant may flower for a few days, as in peach, apricot and plum, or for a month or two or longer, as in wintersweet. Some plants shed all their flowers after one mass bloom, while others open them in succession, as cotton and tomato do. Certain tropical plants flower nearly all year, such as cacao, eucalyptus and lemon.

The switch from vegetative to reproductive growth and flowering at the right season is regulated by day length (photoperiod), low temperature (vernalization) and endogenous hormones; the mechanisms belong to plant physiology.

At anthesis the anthers dehisce in a particular way (longitudinally, transversely, by pores or by valves; [7.12.27](/7.12.27)) and release the pollen. Released pollen is alive and retains its capacity to germinate for a limited time under suitable temperature and humidity: the trinucleate pollen of grasses survives only from a few minutes to a few hours, whereas many binucleate pollens survive for days to weeks. High temperature, drought or excessive rain destroy pollen viability and also affect stigma secretion, so crops that meet heat, drought or continuous rain at flowering often suffer yield losses.

## 29.2. Modes of Pollination

### 29.2.1. Self-Pollination and Cross-Pollination

**Definition 29.2.1 (Pollination)** The transfer of pollen from the pollen sacs, by some agent, to the stigma of the pistil of the same flower or of another flower is called **pollination**.

Pollination is an indispensable step in sexual reproduction. The egg cell lies deep in the embryo sac of an ovule inside the ovary, so only if the pollen, which produces the sperm cells, is first brought to the stigma can it germinate and produce a pollen tube that carries the sperm cells into the embryo sac. The stigma also selects among the pollen that lands on it, and only compatible pollen can germinate (Section 29.4).

**Definition 29.2.2 (Self-Pollination and Cross-Pollination)** Pollination in which pollen falls on the stigma of the same flower is called **self-pollination**; pollination in which pollen is transferred from one flower to the stigma of another flower is called **cross-pollination**. Genetically, pollination between different flowers of the same plant (*geitonogamy*) is equivalent to self-pollination, since both result in selfing; only pollination between different plants (*xenogamy*) results in outcrossing. In agriculture, pollination between different plants of the same cultivar of a fruit tree (a single clone) is likewise equivalent to self-pollination.

Self-pollinating plants include barley, wheat, rice, pea, tomato and soybean. Their flowers generally meet three conditions: they are bisexual, with stamens arranged close around the pistil so that pollen easily falls on the stigma of the same flower; the pollen sacs and the embryo sac mature at the same time; and there is no physiological barrier to germination of the flower's own pollen on the stigma or to growth of its pollen tubes.

**Definition 29.2.3 (Cleistogamy)** The completion of pollination and fertilization in the flower bud, without the flower opening, is called **cleistogamy**; the opposite condition, in which pollination and fertilization take place after the flower has opened, is called *chasmogamy*.

Cleistogamy is an extreme form of self-pollination; in some plants the pollen germinates directly inside the pollen sac and the pollen tubes grow through the sac wall toward the stigma, so strictly speaking there is no "pollination" step. Peanut (*Arachis hypogaea*), pea and vetch (*Vicia*) complete fertilization before the flower opens. Many species of violet (*Viola*) produce two kinds of flowers: chasmogamous flowers with petals, which open in spring, and petalless cleistogamous flowers borne at the base of the stem in summer and autumn. Cleistogamy ensures reproduction under conditions unsuitable for flowering and pollination, and the pollen is not wetted by rain or eaten by insects.

### 29.2.2. The Biological Significance of Selfing and Outcrossing

In *The Effects of Cross and Self Fertilisation in the Vegetable Kingdom* (1876), Darwin compared the offspring of selfing and outcrossing in 57 species in experiments lasting more than a decade. He found that the progeny of outcrossing were generally superior in height, vigor and fertility to those of selfing, and concluded that "nature abhors perpetual self-fertilisation." The modern genetic explanation is that **selfing** makes heterozygous loci homozygous generation after generation, so that harmful, partly recessive alleles hidden in heterozygotes are expressed and the fitness of the offspring falls; this is **inbreeding depression**. **Outcrossing**, in contrast, maintains heterozygosity and recombines variation from different individuals. Outcrossing crops such as maize and rye become clearly dwarfed and lose yield after a few generations of artificial selfing; the heterosis of hybrids is the other side of the same phenomenon.

On the other hand, selfing is widespread in nature because it, too, has advantages:

1. Reproductive assurance. When pollinators are scarce, population density is very low, or the plant has just colonized a new site, selfing guarantees seed set. Baker (1955) noted that most island plants and weeds that can found a population from a single individual are self-compatible (Baker's law).
2. Automatic selection advantage. Ignoring inbreeding depression, an allele that causes ovules to be selfed while pollen is exported as usual is transmitted in 3/2 as many copies as an allele of an outcrosser; outcrossing is favored only when the inbreeding depression $\delta > 1/2$ ([Theorem 14.4.1](/7.10.14#thm-14.4.1)).
3. In populations that have selfed for a long time, harmful alleles are gradually purged and inbreeding depression becomes small, so habitually selfing crops such as wheat, rice and soybean show no marked inbreeding depression.

The mating system of a plant is therefore the result of a balance among these factors. Most angiosperms mainly outcross and have various mechanisms that prevent selfing (Section 29.2.3); about 10%–15% of species mainly self, many species have mixed mating, and some outcrossing plants use "delayed selfing" late in the flower's life as a backup (Figure 1). Chinese textbooks explain inbreeding depression by saying that "the two gametes are produced under the same environmental conditions and lack genetic differentiation"; this is a historical formulation, and the modern explanation is the one given above.

### 29.2.3. Mechanisms That Promote Outcrossing

Plants with bisexual flowers are the most prone to selfing. Angiosperms have evolved many structural and physiological mechanisms that reduce or prevent selfing (summarized in the tables of the Summary).

Unisexual flowers and dioecy. Unisexual flowers make selfing within a single flower impossible. **Monoecious** plants (maize, castor bean, squash) can still undergo geitonogamy, whereas **dioecious** plants (poplar, willow, ginkgo, spinach, kiwifruit) can only outcross.

**Definition 29.2.4 (Dichogamy)** The failure of the stamens and pistil of a bisexual flower to mature at the same time is called **dichogamy**. When the stamens mature first it is called *protandry*, and when the pistil matures first, *protogyny*.

Protandrous plants include sunflower and other Asteraceae, bellflowers (*Campanula*), sages and other Lamiaceae, lettuce and maize (the tassel sheds pollen before the ear is receptive); protogynous plants include magnolia, plantain (*Plantago*) and figwort (*Scrophularia*). In some protandrous flowers the stigma unfolds only after the anthers have shed their pollen, as in *Campanula* and the Asteraceae, where the elongating style pushes pollen out of the anther tube ("plunger" pollen presentation) and the stigma opens afterward. Avocado (*Persea americana*) shows an elegant population-level dichogamy: flowers of type A plants open as females in the morning and as males the next afternoon, whereas those of type B plants do the opposite, and good fruit set requires both types to be planted together. Dichogamy prevents selfing within a flower but not geitonogamy.

Herkogamy. In herkogamy the anthers and stigma are separated in space, so that pollinators are less likely to carry pollen of a flower to its own stigma. Examples are the rostellum of orchids, which separates the anther from the stigma, and the petal-like style branches of *Iris*, with the stigma on the back side beneath them.

**Heterostyly** In a heterostylous species, individuals have two or three floral morphs in which the heights of the style and stamens are complementary between morphs (Figure 2). Populations with *distyly* contain two kinds of plants, the long-styled morph (pin, with a long style and stamens inserted low in the corolla tube) and the short-styled morph (thrum, with a short style and stamens inserted high in the corolla tube), as in primrose (*Primula*), forsythia (*Forsythia*), buckwheat (*Fagopyrum*) and *Adina*. Populations with *tristyly* contain plants with long, mid and short styles, each with its stamens at the other two heights, as in purple loosestrife (*Lythrum salicaria*), some species of wood sorrel (*Oxalis*) and water hyacinth (*Eichhornia crassipes*). Pollen picked up at one height on the body of a pollinating insect is deposited on the stigma at the same height in the other morph. Heterostyly is usually accompanied by heteromorphic self-incompatibility: only pollination between different morphs (legitimate pollination) leads to fertilization, whereas pollination within a morph is incompatible, and the two morphs often also differ in pollen size and stigmatic papillae. Darwin first explained this system experimentally in *The Different Forms of Flowers on Plants of the Same Species* (1877). In *Primula* the S locus controlling distyly is a supergene present on only one chromosome of the short-styled morph (hemizygous), and its gene *CYP734A50* shortens the style by degrading brassinosteroids (Li et al. 2016; Huu et al. 2016).

Enantiostyly and flexistyly. In *enantiostyly* the style of the flower is deflected to the left or the right, so that a pollinator removes pollen on one side and receives pollen on the other. Plants that bear both left- and right-deflected flowers are monomorphic enantiostylous, as in some species of *Solanum* and in *Senna*; plants in which left- and right-deflected flowers are borne on different individuals are dimorphic enantiostylous, as in a few species of Pontederiaceae. *Flexistyly* occurs in *Alpinia* (Zingiberaceae). The population has two phenotypes in a ratio of about 1∶1: in the upward type the style is raised and the anthers shed pollen in the morning, and in the afternoon the style bends down so that the stigma receives pollen; the downward type is the reverse, with the stigma hanging down to receive pollen in the morning and rising in the afternoon (Li et al. 2001). Individuals of the two phenotypes play the female and male roles at different times, which avoids selfing and also reduces interference between the male and female functions.

**Self-incompatibility** Both the stamens and the pistil are normal, but pollen of the same flower or plant cannot germinate on the stigma, or germinates and its tube cannot grow normally or complete fertilization; this is **self-incompatibility**. It is the most widespread and most effective mechanism by which angiosperms prevent selfing, present in about 40% of species, including buckwheat, flax, many cultivars of apple, pear and peach, cabbage and cherry. By genetic basis and floral morphology, self-incompatibility is classified as follows (Figure 3):

| Type | What determines the pollen's recognition phenotype | Recognition molecules (pistil / pollen) | Where pollen is inhibited | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heteromorphic | Linked to the floral morph of heterostyly | Linked to the S supergene | Stigma or style | Primrose, buckwheat, purple loosestrife |
| Gametophytic (GSI) | The S allele of the (haploid) pollen itself, the gametophyte | S-RNase / F-box proteins (SLF, SFB) | Pollen tube in the style | Solanaceae, Rosaceae, Plantaginaceae, Campanulaceae |
| Gametophytic (poppy type) | Same | PrsS small peptide / PrpS transmembrane protein | Stigma surface | Papaveraceae |
| Sporophytic (SSI) | The two S alleles of the (diploid) parent plant that produced the pollen, the sporophyte, often with dominance relations | SRK receptor kinase / SCR (SP11) small peptide | Stigma surface; pollen cannot hydrate or germinate | Brassicaceae, Asteraceae, Convolvulaceae |

In gametophytic self-incompatibility, the pollen tube is inhibited whenever the S allele of the pollen matches either of the two S alleles of the pistil: an S₁S₂ pistil rejects S₁ and S₂ pollen and accepts S₃ pollen, so in an S₁S₂ × S₁S₃ cross half of the pollen can fertilize. In Solanaceae, Rosaceae and others, S-RNase secreted by the style enters all pollen tubes and degrades their RNA; a "non-self recognition" system of a dozen or more *SLF* genes in the pollen detoxifies all non-self S-RNases and fails to recognize only the self type, so that only self pollen tubes are killed (Kubo et al. 2010). The mechanism in poppy is entirely different: when the stigmatic PrsS matches the pollen PrpS, a signaling cascade of calcium influx is triggered in the pollen, ending in the programmed death of the self pollen tube.

In sporophytic self-incompatibility the phenotype of the pollen is determined by the parent: the SCR peptide is synthesized in the tapetum of the anther and coated onto the pollen surface ([7.12.28](/7.12.28)), so every pollen grain carries the products of both alleles of its parent. SCR binds the SRK receptor of the same haplotype on the surface of the stigmatic papilla cells and activates downstream signaling that blocks pollen hydration and germination; when the pollen cannot germinate, callose often accumulates in the papilla cell wall and at the pollen aperture.

Self-incompatibility can be overcome artificially by bud pollination (in Brassicaceae the stigma of the flower bud has not yet expressed SRK), mixed-pollen pollination, removal of the stigma or shortening of the style before pollination, intra-ovarian pollination, in vitro fertilization, and treatment with 3%–5% carbon dioxide (in cabbages). In breeding, self-incompatible lines can replace male-sterile lines in producing hybrids, as in cabbage and Chinese cabbage. In evolution, self-incompatibility is easily lost: both *Arabidopsis thaliana* and the red shepherd's purse *Capsella rubella* became selfing by loss of SRK or SCR function from self-incompatible ancestors. Balancing selection at the S locus and the diversity of its alleles are discussed in [7.10.14](/7.10.14).

## 29.3. Pollination Vectors and Pollination Syndromes

Cross-pollination requires an external agent to carry pollen to another flower. The vectors are wind, water and animals (insects, birds, bats and others), and the commonest are animals (mainly insects) and wind. About 90% of angiosperms are pollinated by animals, and the most important of these are bees. Flowers that depend on different vectors often have particular adaptive structures.

### 29.3.1. Wind Pollination

**Definition 29.3.1 (Anemophilous and Entomophilous Flowers)** Pollination by wind is called *anemophily*, and a flower pollinated by wind is an **anemophilous flower**; pollination by insects is called *entomophily*, and a flower pollinated by insects is an **entomophilous flower**.

About 10% of angiosperms are wind-pollinated, including most grasses and sedges and, among woody plants, oak, poplar, birch, hazel, walnut and plane tree; nearly all gymnosperms are wind-pollinated. The features of anemophilous flowers are as follows:

- The flowers are often crowded in spikes or catkins and are unisexual, with male and female flowers separate on one plant or on different plants; the perianth is small and inconspicuous or absent, and there is no color, scent or nectary.
- Large amounts of pollen are produced and released at the same time; the grains are small (mostly 20–40 μm), light, dry and smooth, and do not stick together, so that they are easily carried by wind; pine pollen has air sacs.
- The filaments are slender and the anthers large and often versatile, protruding from or hanging out of the flower and swaying in the wind to shed pollen (grasses).
- The stigmas are large, often enlarged into feathery or branched forms and protruding from the flower, which increases the area for catching pollen.
- Most wind-pollinated woody plants flower before leafing out (poplar, elm, hazel), so that the pollen released is not blocked by leaves; wind-pollinated plants are mostly distributed in temperate regions and often form large stands of a single species, which helps pollen find stigmas of the same species.

Not all of these features are necessary: the flowers of grasses are bisexual, and some wind-pollinated plants such as maples also have a perianth. Wind pollination is very inefficient, and the number of pollen grains per ovule is much higher than in insect-pollinated plants. Cruden (1977) compared pollen–ovule ratios (pollen grains per flower divided by ovules per flower) across mating systems: about 5 in cleistogamous plants, about 28 in obligate selfers, about 170 in facultative selfers, about 800 in facultative outcrossers and about 5900 in obligate outcrossers; the ratio is higher still in wind-pollinated plants. The pollen–ovule ratio reflects the probability that pollen reaches a stigma and is a simple index of the mating system.

### 29.3.2. Insect Pollination

Most angiosperms are pollinated by insects, and the common pollinating insects are bees, butterflies, moths, flies and beetles. Insects visit flowers to feed on pollen and nectar, or to lay eggs, rest or warm themselves, and in doing so they inevitably touch the anthers and stigmas and so carry pollen to other flowers. Entomophilous flowers generally have the following features:

- The flowers are large and conspicuous, or many small flowers are gathered into a showy inflorescence; they have bright colors: day-opening flowers are mostly red, yellow, blue or purple, and night-opening ones mostly white, so that only night-flying moths can recognize them.
- Most have a particular scent that attracts insects, which may be fragrant or foul.
- Most secrete nectar; the nectaries are either distributed over various parts of the flower or developed into special organs, and nectar accumulates at the base of the flower or in the spur. Flowers with exposed nectar are mostly visited by beetles, flies and short-tongued bees and moths, and flowers with nectar hidden deep in the corolla tube or spur by long-tongued butterflies and moths.
- The pollen grains are relatively large, with a rough exine, mostly spiny or reticulate, and a sticky pollenkitt; after the anthers dehisce the pollen adheres to the anther and is not blown away, but easily sticks to insects visiting the flower. The amount of pollen is much smaller than in anemophilous flowers. The stigma is also often sticky.
- The size, shape and structure of the flower and the position of the nectaries are often matched to the size, body form, mouthparts and behavior of the pollinating insect.

Two classic examples show the fine fit between floral structure and pollinating insect.

The flower of birthwort (*Aristolochia*) is a "trap flower": the perianth is fused into a long tube with a swollen base, the flower is protogynous, and the inner wall of the tube bears hairs pointing toward the base. When the pistil is mature, small flies attracted by the scent crawl down the hairs into the base of the tube, and the pollen they carry is smeared on the stigma; because the hairs block their way, the flies cannot get out until the anthers mature and shed pollen and the hairs gradually wither, whereupon they leave covered with pollen and visit another flower. Before pollination the flower of birthwort is erect and after pollination it hangs down.

The stamens of sage (*Salvia*, Lamiaceae) are levers. The corolla is two-lipped, with a helmet-shaped upper lip and a projecting lower lip that serves as a landing platform for bees; beneath the upper lip lie 2 stamens and 1 style. In each stamen the connective is greatly elongated into a lever: the upper arm is long and bears a fertile pollen sac at its tip, and the lower arm is short, with a sterile sac flattened into a thin plate; the plates of the two stamens block the entrance at the throat of the corolla tube. When a bee pushes deep into the corolla tube to take nectar, its head pushes the plates and the long arms of the levers swing down, so that the anthers strike the bee's back and deposit pollen there. At this time the stigma is still inside the upper lip; after the pollen has been shed the style elongates and bends down so that the stigma comes to lie exactly where the bee's back is, and pollination is completed when another bee carrying pollen enters. A sage flower is thus first male and then female, and does not self.

In addition, the anthers of eggplant, blueberry (*Vaccinium*) and others dehisce by pores (those of tomato open by slits but are joined into a cone, from which pollen likewise has to be shaken out), and the pollen can be "shaken" out of the pores only by bumblebees and other bees vibrating their thoracic flight muscles at high frequency; this is *buzz pollination*. Honeybees cannot buzz-collect pollen, which is why bumblebees are often introduced to pollinate greenhouse tomatoes.

By the group of pollinating insects, entomophilous flowers differ further in their features (see the table in Section 29.3.5):

- Bee flowers: blue, purple or yellow, often with ultraviolet patterns, scented, bilaterally symmetric, with a landing platform, with nectar and pollen both serving as rewards, as in the flowers of Lamiaceae, Papilionoideae (Fabaceae) and Scrophulariaceae.
- Butterfly flowers: brightly colored, mostly red or orange, open by day, with a slender corolla tube or spur and often a flat landing platform, as in pinks (*Dianthus*), lantana (*Lantana camara*) and butterfly bush (*Buddleja*).
- Moth flowers: white or pale, opening in the evening or at night and giving off a strong sweet fragrance, with a slender corolla tube or spur and no landing platform, so that hovering hawkmoths can take nectar with their long proboscis, as in tuberose, tobacco, honeysuckle and evening primrose. The spur of the comet orchid of Madagascar (*Angraecum sesquipedale*) is about 30 cm long, and from this Darwin (1862) predicted a hawkmoth with an equally long proboscis; this moth (*Xanthopan morganii praedicta*) was not described until 1903.
- Fly flowers: one kind is the pale, faintly scented, saucer-shaped flowers (Apiaceae), the other the dark purple-brown carrion-fly flowers with a smell of rotting meat, often with traps, as in birthwort, *Rafflesia*, titan arum (*Amorphophallus titanum*) and the carrion flowers (*Stapelia*) of the milkweed subfamily.
- Beetle flowers: large and bowl-shaped, white or green, strongly scented (fruity or fermented), with abundant pollen and ovules deeply buried and protected; beetles often feed, mate and even spend the night in the flower, as in magnolia, water lily and Annonaceae. Beetles are thought to have been among the earliest pollinators.

### 29.3.3. Bird, Bat and Water Pollination

Bird pollination. Flowers pollinated by birds (*ornithophily*) are mostly bright red or orange (birds are sensitive to red, which bees cannot see), with a tubular or pendent corolla, a thick, tough perianth, stamens and stigma protruding from the flower, abundant but dilute nectar, almost no scent (birds have a poor sense of smell), and open by day. New World hummingbirds hover to take nectar, whereas the sunbirds, honeyeaters and white-eyes of the Old World perch to feed, so that bird flowers in the Old World often have an inflorescence axis or bracts on which the birds can perch, as in coral tree (*Erythrina*), kapok (*Bombax ceiba*), bottlebrush (*Callistemon*) and bird of paradise (*Strelitzia*).

Bat pollination. Some tropical plants are pollinated by nectar-feeding bats (*chiropterophily*). Bat flowers open at night and are white or dull in color, large and sturdy, or many flowers form a stout inflorescence; they have a strong fermented, fruity or musty smell and produce very large amounts of nectar. The flowers often hang beyond the foliage or grow directly on the trunk (cauliflory), which makes them easy for bats to reach, as in baobab (*Adansonia*), durian (*Durio*), agave and the columnar cacti of North America. In addition, some Proteaceae in South Africa are pollinated by rodents, and Australia has honey possums that feed specifically on nectar.

Water pollination. Among aquatic plants, hornwort (*Ceratophyllum*), hydrilla (*Hydrilla*), eelgrass (*Vallisneria*) and seagrass (*Zostera*) are pollinated by water, which is called *hydrophily*. Eelgrass (*Vallisneria*) is dioecious and grows on the bottom: when the male flowers mature they detach from the pedicel, float to the surface and open there; the pedicel of the female flower elongates rapidly and carries the flower to the surface; as a male flower drifts near a female flower the anthers touch the stigma and pollination is completed; afterward the pedicel of the female flower coils into a spiral and draws the ovary back down to the bottom, where it develops into a fruit. This is surface pollination. Seagrasses such as hornwort and *Zostera* are pollinated underwater, and the thread-like pollen of *Zostera* is carried by the current and wraps around the stigma.

### 29.3.4. Floral Signals and Rewards

The relation between flower and pollinator is an exchange: the flower advertises with signals and pays with rewards in return for the pollinator's services.

Signals consist mainly of color, shape and scent, and sometimes temperature.

- Color. Flower color is determined mainly by anthocyanins (red, purple, blue), flavonols (pale yellow, cream) and carotenoids (yellow, orange) ([7.12.27](/7.12.27)). The vision of bees is sensitive to ultraviolet, blue and green light, and bees cannot see red. Many flowers that look uniformly yellow to the human eye absorb ultraviolet at the center, which appears dark, while the periphery reflects ultraviolet, forming a "bull's-eye" that indicates the position of the nectar, a *nectar guide*; the spots inside the lower lip of the foxglove corolla (*Digitalis*) are a nectar guide visible to the human eye. Some flowers change color after pollination, such as the patch at the base of the petals of horse chestnut (*Aesculus*), which turns from yellow to red, and the flowers of lantana, which turn from yellow to purple-red, signaling to pollinators that the flower no longer offers a reward.
- Scent. Floral scent consists of volatiles such as benzenoids, terpenoids and fatty acid derivatives, and different pollinators prefer different scents: bees prefer a fresh sweet fragrance, hawkmoths a strong sweet fragrance at night, and carrion flies the putrid smell of sulfur compounds and amines.
- Temperature. In some Araceae (skunk cabbage, *Philodendron*, titan arum) the inflorescence produces large amounts of heat at flowering through the alternative oxidase pathway of the mitochondria, which helps volatilize the scent and provides a warm place for beetles; the receptacle of the sacred lotus (*Nelumbo nucifera*) can keep its temperature at 30–36 °C during flowering, more than ten degrees above the air temperature (Seymour & Schultze-Motel 1996).

Rewards. The commonest are nectar (mainly sucrose, glucose and fructose, with a little amino acid) and pollen (the protein source for bee larvae). In addition there are floral oils (the flowers of Malpighiaceae and some Plantaginaceae secrete oil, which is collected by specialized oil-collecting bees), resins (*Dalechampia* in Euphorbiaceae), fragrances (male orchid bees collect scent compounds from orchids for courtship), and sites for egg-laying and rearing of young (fig and yucca, Section 29.3.7) and for shelter and warmth.

Pollination syndromes illustrate the power of convergent evolution: unrelated plants under selection by the same class of pollinators evolve similar flowers. But the syndrome is only a generalization and cannot be applied mechanically to infer the pollinator. Many plants have flowers visited by several kinds of animals (generalized pollination), and the "effective pollinator" of a flower is not necessarily its most frequent visitor; the test by Ollerton et al. (2009) found that in a considerable number of species the pollinator predicted by the syndrome was not the actual principal pollinator. The study of pollination requires field observation of the behavior of floral visitors and measurement of how efficiently they carry and deposit pollen.

When pollinators shift from one kind of flower to another, rapid evolution of floral form results, which is one of the important drivers of speciation in angiosperms. For example, in the American columbines (*Aquilegia*) the length of the spur increases successively as pollination shifts from bees to hummingbirds and then to hawkmoths (Whittall & Hodges 2007); the differences between two closely related monkeyflowers (the bee-pollinated *Mimulus lewisii* and the hummingbird-pollinated *M. cardinalis*) are determined mainly by a few genes controlling flower color (Bradshaw & Schemske 2003).

### 29.3.6. Pollination in Orchids

The orchid family, Orchidaceae ([7.12.37](/7.12.37)), is one of the largest families of angiosperms, with about 800 genera and 25,000–30,000 species (about 194 genera and 1400 species in China), and its flowers represent the extreme specialization of insect-pollinated flowers. Orchid flowers are bilaterally symmetric, with 6 tepals in two whorls; the median tepal of the inner whorl is specialized into a *labellum* of varied shape and color, which serves as a landing platform and guide for pollinators. In most orchids the pedicel or ovary twists through 180° in development (resupination), which brings the labellum from the upper to the lower side. The stamens and pistil are fused into a columnar structure, the *gynostemium*; usually only 1 stamen remains (Orchidoideae and Epidendroideae), and the pollen of each anther sac is glued into a waxy *pollinium*, which is attached by a stalk (caudicle or stipe) to a sticky *viscidium*; the viscidium is produced by the *rostellum*, a specialized part of the stigma, which at the same time separates the anther from the fertile stigma (herkogamy).

When an insect enters the flower its head or back touches the viscidium and the whole pollinium is glued to the insect and carried away. Within tens of seconds to a few minutes the stalk of the pollinium dries and bends, changing the orientation of the pollinium so that it strikes the stigma when the insect reaches the next flower; this delay also reduces geitonogamy. A single pollination delivers thousands of pollen grains to the stigma, enough to fertilize tens of thousands of ovules in one ovary. Darwin described these mechanisms in detail in *On the Various Contrivances by Which British and Foreign Orchids Are Fertilised by Insects* (1862).

About one third of orchids give pollinators no reward and rely on deceptive pollination:

- Food deception. The flower mimics the color, shape and scent of nectar-bearing flowers but produces no nectar, and attracts inexperienced insects.
- Sexual deception. This occurs almost only in the orchids (the daisy *Gorteria diffusa* is one of the few exceptions). The labellum of *Ophrys* mimics a female bee in shape, hairiness and scent and releases scent compounds almost identical to the sex pheromone of the female bee; the male bee "pseudocopulates" on the labellum and carries the pollinia away (pseudocopulatory pollination). The Australian *Drakaea* and *Chiloglottis* are pollinated by male thynnid wasps.

Deceptive pollination often lets each species attract only one or a few pollinators and creates strict reproductive isolation, which is thought to be one of the reasons for the very large number of orchid species.

### 29.3.7. Obligate Pollination Mutualisms

The most extreme specialization is found in one-to-one (or nearly one-to-one) mutualisms between plants and pollinating insects, in which the larvae of the pollinator feed on part of the seeds of the plant and the pollinator pollinates the flowers actively (compare **mutualism** in [7.10.17](/7.10.17)):

- Fig (*Ficus*) and fig wasps. The genus *Ficus* has about 750–800 species, and almost every one is pollinated by one (or a few) specialized fig wasp species (Agaonidae). The inflorescence of *Ficus* is a syconium ([7.12.27](/7.12.27)), with all the small flowers hidden inside a hollow receptacle. A female wasp carrying pollen enters the inflorescence through the narrow bract-lined opening at the apex, often losing her wings and antennae on the way, and does not come out again; she lays eggs in the ovaries of gall flowers, which have short styles, and at the same time places pollen on the stigmas of female flowers (in actively pollinating species she carries the pollen in thoracic pollen pockets and applies it deliberately). The larvae develop in the gall flowers; the wingless males emerge first, mate with the females inside the inflorescence and die; the females emerge when the male flowers open, pick up pollen and fly out to find another inflorescence in the female phase. Cultivated fig is dioecious, and the edible female trees need to be pollinated by fig wasps emerging from the wild "caprifigs", although some cultivars are parthenocarpic ([7.12.31](/7.12.31)).
- Yucca and yucca moths. North American *Yucca* is pollinated only by female yucca moths (*Tegeticula* and *Parategeticula*). The female moth uses specialized tentacle-like mouthparts to scrape pollen from the anthers into a ball, flies to another flower, lays eggs in the ovary, and then climbs to the top of the stigma and deliberately stuffs the pollen ball into the stigmatic cavity; the larvae eat only part of the seeds. The plant aborts flowers that have received too many eggs, which limits the moths' "cheating".

### 29.3.8. Artificial Supplementary Pollination

Cross-pollination is easily limited by environmental conditions: when wind-pollinated plants meet windless or continuously rainy weather, or insect-pollinated plants meet strong wind or low temperature that keeps pollinators from flying, the chances of pollination and fertilization fall and the yield of fruit and seed decreases. In agriculture this is compensated for by artificial supplementary pollination: pollen is collected from the stamens and dusted on the stigmas, or the collected pollen is stored at low temperature in dry conditions for later use. Artificial supplementary pollination greatly increases the amount of pollen on the stigma, which favors pollen germination and pollen tube growth and raises the fertilization rate. For example, maize is protandrous, and when the ear becomes receptive there is often not enough pollen, so that grains are missing at the tip of the ear; artificial supplementary pollination can raise yield by 8%–10%. Under natural pollination sunflower has many empty seeds, and artificial supplementary pollination raises seed set and oil content. Artificial pollination is also needed in cultivar rejuvenation and cross breeding.

In modern agriculture, pollination service has become an important input to production: colonies of bees are rented or bumblebees introduced in orchards and greenhouses, and vanilla (*Vanilla planifolia*), which lacks its specialized pollinating bee outside its native range, has always been pollinated by hand, flower by flower. Most of the world's major food crops depend on or benefit from animal pollination (Klein et al. 2007), and the decline of pollinating insects has therefore received wide attention.

## 29.4. Interaction of Pollen and Pistil

**Definition 29.4.1 (Fertilization)** The fusion of the female and male gametes, the egg cell and the sperm cell, is called **fertilization**. In angiosperms, after pollination the pollen germinates on the stigma into a pollen tube, which grows through the style into the ovary and ovule and delivers sperm cells into the embryo sac, where they fuse with the egg cell and the central cell respectively.

### 29.4.1. Pollen Germination on the Stigma

Pollen that lands on a stigma is first held by the stigma surface and then takes up water from it. After hydration the respiration of the pollen grain rises sharply, protein synthesis increases markedly and the grain swells; the Golgi apparatus produces many small vesicles carrying enzymes and wall-building materials, which move toward the germination aperture; vacuolation of the vegetative cell increases and so does its internal pressure, which makes the intine of the pollen protrude outward from one (or several) apertures to form the pollen tube (Figure 5). This process is the germination of the pollen.

**Definition 29.4.2 (Pollen Tube)** The tubular structure formed when the protoplast and intine of the vegetative cell protrude and elongate through the germination aperture during pollen germination is called the **pollen tube**. The pollen tube grows only at its tip and carries the vegetative nucleus and the generative cell (or the two sperm cells) toward the ovule; a germinated pollen grain together with its pollen tube is the mature male gametophyte.

Wet and dry stigmas ([7.12.27](/7.12.27)) treat pollen differently. The secretion of a wet stigma (water, sugars, lipids, phenolics, enzymes, vitamins and others) holds pollen of all kinds and supplies water and a substrate for germination, and recognition of pollen mostly takes place in the style. The surface of a dry stigma has only a thin hydrophilic protein film, and pollen must obtain water from the papilla cells, so that a dry stigma can recognize pollen strictly even before germination (as in sporophytic self-incompatibility of Brassicaceae). Changes in phenolic substances in the stigmatic secretion promote or inhibit pollen germination and act as a "selection"; boron reduces pollen bursting, raises the germination rate and promotes pollen tube growth, and calcium has an inducing effect on the pollen tube; all of these have been applied in media for pollen germination in vitro.

The enzymes and metabolites stored in the pollen itself are the internal conditions for germination: the various hydrolases in the pollen wall leach out of the wall on contact with water, and the cutinase in the pollen and pollen tube dissolves the cuticle of the stigma surface, opening a path for the pollen tube.

There are two classes of proteins in the pollen wall: exine proteins, secreted by the tapetum and stored in the cavities of the exine, which are of sporophytic origin; and intine proteins, produced by the pollen itself and deposited in the intine (more abundantly near the aperture), which are of gametophytic origin. When pollen lands on a stigma and becomes moist, the exine proteins are released within minutes and the intine proteins also leak out through the aperture, and they interact with proteins at the stigma surface; this is the material basis of pollen–stigma "recognition," and it corresponds exactly to the two origins of the recognition molecules in the two classes of self-incompatibility (Section 29.2.3). Incompatible pollen either fails to germinate, or germinates but its tube grows very slowly and cannot enter the stigma, or is inhibited in the style, and often forms callose plugs at the aperture or at the tip of the pollen tube. Incompatibility in wide crosses also often occurs at this stage.

The speed of pollen germination varies among plants: the pollen of maize and Russian dandelion (*Taraxacum kok-saghyz*) germinates almost immediately after landing on the stigma, whereas cotton, wheat and beet need several minutes or longer. Excessive air humidity or low temperature impairs germination, pollination right after rain or fog usually does not result in seed set, and rapid drying after the pollen has been wetted is fatal.

### 29.4.2. Growth of the Pollen Tube

The pollen exine is hard and leaves room for extension only at the germination aperture, so the protoplast and intine of the pollen usually protrude from one aperture to form a pollen tube; pollen with several apertures, as in Malvaceae and Cucurbitaceae, may produce several tubes at once, but only one continues to grow. The pollen tube shows typical tip growth: growth occurs only in the apical 3–5 μm, where there is an accumulation of secretory vesicles and a gradient of calcium ion concentration decreasing from the tip backward, and growth is often pulsatile. All the contents of the pollen grain move into the tube and are concentrated toward the tip: in trinucleate pollen the vegetative nucleus and the two sperm cells enter the tube; in binucleate pollen the generative cell enters the tube and divides there into two sperm cells. The farther a part is from the tip the older it is; in many angiosperms the side walls of the pollen tube contain **callose** and form a callose plug at intervals, which seals off the emptied rear part and keeps the tip turgid.

After passing through the stigma the pollen tube enters the style. In a solid style the tube secretes pectinases and other enzymes and grows in the extracellular matrix between the cells of the transmitting tissue, taking nutrients from it; in a hollow style it grows along the secretion on the surface of the stylar canal. Pollen tubes of angiosperms grow very fast, about 1000 times faster than those of most gymnosperms. The time from pollination to fertilization varies widely: it is only 1–2 hours in grass crops such as rice and wheat, 15–32 hours in cotton and about 5 days in apple; in the red oaks (*Quercus*) it is more than a year (the pollen tube overwinters in the style). This is mainly genetically determined and is also affected by pollen quality, temperature and humidity.

Many pollen grains usually germinate on one stigma at the same time, and the pollen tubes compete in the style, the fast-growing ones reaching the ovule first. This pollen competition (gametophytic selection) is thought to eliminate pollen carrying harmful alleles and is also part of the "selectivity of fertilization" (Section 29.5.4).

### 29.4.3. Guidance of the Pollen Tube

The pollen tube grows several centimeters in the style, or even tens of centimeters (maize), and in the end always finds the micropyle of the ovule accurately and enters the embryo sac, which shows that signals guiding the pollen tube are present in the pistil. Guidance can be divided into two stages: in the style and ovary the tube is guided by sporophytic tissue (sporophytic guidance), and near the ovule it is guided by the embryo sac (gametophytic guidance).

Sporophytic guidance relies on glycoproteins of the transmitting tissue (such as the TTS arabinogalactan protein in tobacco), concentration gradients of γ-aminobutyric acid and other cues, and guides the pollen tube through the style and along the placenta and funiculus.

The key evidence for gametophytic guidance came from wishbone flower (*Torenia fournieri*). In this plant part of the embryo sac protrudes beyond the micropyle and can be manipulated directly under the microscope. Higashiyama et al. (2001) killed the cells of the embryo sac one at a time with a laser and found that the pollen tube was no longer attracted to the ovule only when both synergids were killed, whereas killing the egg cell or central cell had no effect: the attractant comes from the synergids. Okuda et al. (2009) then identified two small cysteine-rich polypeptides from the synergids and named them LURE1 and LURE2; they belong to the defensin-like peptides, are specifically expressed in the synergids, can attract pollen tubes of the same species in vitro, and are species-specific. LURE peptides were later also found in *Arabidopsis*, together with PRK6, the receptor kinase at the tip of the pollen tube that receives the LURE signal (Takeuchi & Higashiyama 2016).

After the pollen tube reaches the ovule, it either grows directly toward the micropyle and enters the embryo sac (orthotropous ovule) or bends and turns into the micropyle (anatropous and campylotropous ovules); this is called *porogamy* and is the mode of the great majority of angiosperms. In a few plants the pollen tube enters the embryo sac through the chalaza at the base of the ovule, which is called *chalazogamy*, as in elm, walnut and *Casuarina*; in others it passes through the integument and enters the embryo sac from the side, which is called *mesogamy*, as in squash. In ovules with a thick nucellus the pollen tube must first pass through the nucellar tissue to reach the embryo sac.

How the pollen tube enters the embryo sac depends on the synergids: in some plants it enters between the egg cell and a synergid (buckwheat), in some it penetrates one synergid (cotton), in some it destroys one synergid as a passage (*Pelargonium*), and in others it enters through an already degenerated synergid (maize). The receptor kinase FERONIA on the synergid membrane takes part in "receiving" the pollen tube, and in *feronia* mutants the pollen tube does not burst after entering the embryo sac but continues to grow (Escobar-Restrepo et al. 2007). After the pollen tube enters the embryo sac its tip bursts and discharges the two sperm cells and other contents into the degenerating synergid. The cause of bursting was traditionally thought to be the low-oxygen environment in the embryo sac, an abrupt pressure change on contact with the synergid, or dissolution of the tube wall (tomato, sesame); it is now known to be controlled by signaling between the synergid and the pollen tube. Once an ovule has received one pollen tube it usually prevents other pollen tubes from entering.

## 29.5. Double Fertilization

### 29.5.1. The Discovery of Double Fertilization

In 1898 the Russian botanist S. G. Nawaschin observed in the lily *Lilium martagon* and the fritillary *Fritillaria tenella* that of the two sperms released by the pollen tube one fused with the egg cell and the other with the two polar nuclei; the next year the Frenchman L. Guignard independently reported the same phenomenon. This discovery explained the origin of the endosperm and also the "pollen effect" (*xenia*) then known: traits such as the color and texture of the endosperm of the maize grain show the influence of the male parent in the very generation of the cross, because the endosperm itself contains genetic material from the male parent.

### 29.5.2. The Course of Double Fertilization

After the pollen tube has entered one synergid and burst, the two sperm cells are released into the region between the degenerating synergid and the egg cell and central cell (Figure 5). As the synergid degenerates, a "corona" of actin appears near the sperm cells and extends toward the egg cell and central cell, perhaps taking part in the movement of the sperm cells. Then:

![From pollination to double fertilization. A: pollen that has landed on the stigma germinates, and the pollen tube grows down the style into the ovary and reaches the ovule. B: the ovule at fertilization: the pollen tube enters the embryo sac through the micropyle and releases the two sperm cells into one synergid; one sperm cell fuses with the egg cell (the zygote, which will develop into the embryo) and the other with the two polar nuclei of the central cell (the primary endosperm nucleus, which will develop into the triploid endosperm); the antipodal cells lie at the chalazal end](./plots/pollen-tube-fertilization.py)

1. One sperm cell fuses with the egg cell. The plasma membranes fuse first and the sperm nucleus enters the egg cell; the chromatin of the sperm nucleus attaches to the nuclear envelope of the egg nucleus, the envelopes fuse, the chromatin of the two nuclei mixes, and the nucleolus of the male nucleus also fuses with that of the egg nucleus, forming the diploid zygote (fertilized egg), which will develop into the embryo.
2. The other sperm cell fuses with the central cell: the sperm nucleus fuses with the two polar nuclei (or the already fused secondary nucleus) to form the triploid primary endosperm nucleus, which will develop into the endosperm.

Fusion of a sperm cell with the central cell is faster than with the egg cell, so the primary endosperm nucleus forms earlier; endosperm development also usually precedes the first division of the zygote ([7.12.30](/7.12.30)). Electron microscopy shows that at fertilization usually only the sperm nucleus enters the egg cell, whereas the cytoplasm of the sperm cell (including its plastids and mitochondria) remains in the degenerating synergid; this is why plastids and mitochondria are maternally inherited in most angiosperms. In a few plants (such as *Pelargonium* and alfalfa) plastids can be transmitted to the offspring through the sperm cell. Fusion of the plasma membranes of sperm and egg requires the HAP2 (GCS1) protein on the sperm surface and activation of the sperm cell by the EC1 peptides secreted by the egg cell (Sprunck et al. 2012).

**Definition 29.5.1 (Double Fertilization)** Of the two sperm cells released by the pollen tube, one fuses with the egg cell to form the zygote and the other fuses with the central cell (two polar nuclei) to form the primary endosperm nucleus; this simultaneous fusion of the egg cell and the central cell with two separate sperm cells is called **double fertilization**, a phenomenon unique to sexual reproduction in angiosperms.

Are the two sperm cells identical? Russell (1980–1985) studied the pollen of leadwort (*Plumbago zeylanica*) by electron microscopy and found that the two sperm cells differ in form: one is attached to the vegetative nucleus by a projection and contains more mitochondria, and the other is connected to the first through plasmodesmata and contains more plastids; moreover, the plastid-rich sperm cell fuses preferentially with the egg cell (preferential fertilization). In most plants the two sperm cells show no obvious difference, and which target cell each fuses with may be random.

After fertilization the other synergid also degenerates, and the antipodal cells either divide and increase in number, serving as nutrients in the early development of the embryo and endosperm, and finally all disappear.

Double fertilization in gnetophytes. In *Ephedra* and *Gnetum* the two sperm nuclei likewise fuse with the egg nucleus and with the ventral canal cell nucleus (*Ephedra*) or another female gametophyte nucleus (*Gnetum*), so by definition this is also "double fertilization"; but the product of the second fertilization is an additional embryo, which later degenerates, and not endosperm (Friedman 1990). This suggests that double fertilization may have originated from a primitive process that "produces two embryos", of which the angiosperms converted one into endosperm.

### 29.5.3. The Biological Significance of Double Fertilization

1. The zygote. Fusion of two haploid gametes restores the original chromosome number of the plant and keeps the heredity of the species relatively stable; when the egg and sperm come from different individuals (in outcrossing), the genetic material of both parents is combined and new genetic variation arises, which provides material for selection.
2. The endosperm. The primary endosperm nucleus likewise combines the genetic material of both parents, and the triploid endosperm that develops from it serves as nutrition for the embryo and is thought to make the offspring more vigorous and adaptable. More important in evolutionary terms is that the nutritive tissue of angiosperms forms only after fertilization, so the mother invests storage material only in ovules that have been successfully fertilized, avoiding the situation in gymnosperms in which a large amount of female gametophyte nutrient is accumulated before fertilization and is wasted entirely in unfertilized ovules.
3. The ratio of maternal to paternal genomes in the endosperm is 2∶1 (*Polygonum* type embryo sac), and this dosage is important for normal endosperm development: in many species, whenever the maternal and paternal genomes depart from 2∶1 (for example in crosses between parents of different ploidy), the endosperm develops abnormally and the seed aborts, which is an important reproductive barrier in interspecific and interploidy crosses (the "triploid block"). The endosperm also has many imprinted genes, of which only the maternal or only the paternal copy is expressed; the parental conflict hypothesis of Haig and Westoby (1989) holds that this reflects a conflict of interest between father and mother over the allocation of resources to the seed.

Double fertilization is the most advanced and most specialized form of sexual reproduction in the plant kingdom, and together with the flower, the closed carpel and the sieve tube–companion cell complex it forms one of the main shared derived characters of angiosperms.

### 29.5.4. Selectivity of Fertilization

The stigma's treatment of pollen germination, the style's treatment of pollen tube growth and the embryo sac's acceptance of sperm cells are all selective: only pollen that is physiologically and biochemically compatible with the pistil can germinate and complete fertilization. A stigma often carries pollen from different plants of the same species, or even from different species, but only compatible pollen produces an effect. Darwin pointed out that if fertilization were not selective it could avoid neither the harm of selfing and close inbreeding nor gain the benefit of outcrossing. In practice, mixed-pollen pollination often raises seed set and can overcome part of the incompatibility of self-incompatibility and of wide crosses. In angiosperms, occasionally two sperm cells enter one egg cell (dispermy) or many sperm cells enter an egg cell (polyspermy), and "heterofertilization" also occurs, in which sperm cells from two different pollen tubes fertilize the egg cell and the central cell separately (maize); all of these are rare.

## 29.6. Apomixis and Polyembryony

### 29.6.1. Apomixis

**Definition 29.6.1 (Apomixis)** The production of seeds containing embryos without fusion of sperm and egg is called **apomixis** (in the narrow sense, *agamospermy*). The offspring of apomixis are usually genotypically identical to the mother plant, and apomixis is thus a kind of asexual reproduction through seeds.

Apomixis is known in about 80 families and nearly 300 genera of angiosperms (Hojsgaard et al. 2014), and is commonest in Asteraceae, Poaceae and Rosaceae; apomictic plants are mostly polyploid and often retain some capacity for sexual reproduction (facultative apomixis). By the origin of the embryo it is divided into two large classes:

| Type | Embryo sac | Origin of the embryo | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gametophytic apomixis: *diplospory* | Formed directly from the megaspore mother cell without meiosis (or with failed meiosis), unreduced (2n) | The unreduced egg cell develops into an embryo without fertilization (parthenogenesis) | *Taraxacum*, *Antennaria*, *Ixeris* |
| Gametophytic apomixis: **apospory** | Develops directly from a somatic cell of the nucellus, unreduced (2n), often coexisting with a sexual embryo sac | Same | *Hieracium*, *Pennisetum*, *Panicum*, Kentucky bluegrass (*Poa pratensis*) |
| Sporophytic apomixis: *adventitious embryony* | Normal sexual embryo sac | Somatic cells of the nucellus or integument divide directly into an embryo, which grows into the embryo sac and often coexists with the zygotic embryo | Polyembryonic cultivars of *Citrus* and mango |

In gametophytic apomixis, the development of the egg cell of the unreduced embryo sac into an embryo without fertilization is **parthenogenesis**; endosperm formation differs among plants: in dandelion the endosperm develops autonomously from the central cell without fertilization, whereas in many apomictic grasses and rosaceous plants pollination is still required and a sperm cell fuses with the central cell to form the endosperm while the egg cell is not fertilized, which is called *pseudogamy*.

**Definition 29.6.2 (Adventitious Embryo)** An embryo that develops directly from somatic cells of the nucellus or integument is called an **adventitious embryo**; in *Citrus* it is also called a nucellar embryo.

By the table above, the statement common in Chinese textbooks that "apospory is the direct development of an embryo from cells of the nucellus or integument (*Citrus*)" in fact describes adventitious embryony; apospory means that a somatic cell of the nucellus first forms an embryo sac (that is, a gametophyte is formed without passing through a spore), and the embryo then develops from the egg cell of this embryo sac. This agrees with the meaning of **apospory** and **apogamy** in ferns ([7.12.17](/7.12.17)).

There is also haploid apomixis: in a normally reduced haploid embryo sac the egg cell develops into a haploid embryo without fertilization (haploid parthenogenesis), or a synergid or antipodal cell develops into an embryo (haploid apogamy), as observed in onion, *Iris* and *Mimosa*. The offspring so produced are haploid and usually sterile and cannot be perpetuated in nature, so this is not apomixis in the narrow sense; but haploids are very useful in breeding, since doubling yields homozygous diploids rapidly (haploid breeding).

Apomixis can fix the heterosis of a hybrid, so that farmers need not buy hybrid seed every year, and it is therefore called "asexual seed". In recent years genome editing has been used in rice to convert meiosis into mitosis (the *MiMe* mutation) and to make the egg cell express the embryogenesis gene *BBM1* and develop parthenogenetically, giving hybrid rice that can be propagated clonally through seed (Khanday et al. 2019).

### 29.6.2. Polyembryony

**Definition 29.6.3 (Polyembryony)** The presence of two or more embryos in one seed is called **polyembryony**.

Polyembryony has several origins: one zygote divides early into several parts, each of which develops into an embryo (cleavage polyembryony, common in gymnosperms, [7.12.25](/7.12.25)); one ovule contains several embryo sacs, each forming an embryo; synergids or antipodal cells develop into embryos; and, commonest of all, adventitious embryos. For example, besides one zygotic embryo the seed of *Citrus* often contains several to a dozen nucellar embryos, which grow more vigorously than the zygotic embryo. The embryoids (somatic embryos) formed from somatic cells in tissue culture resemble adventitious embryos, being embryos formed from somatic cells without fertilization. The genotype of a nucellar embryo is the same as that of the mother plant, so seedlings of *Citrus* retain the maternal traits and are used to propagate uniform rootstocks; but this also makes cross breeding of *Citrus* difficult, because most of the seedlings germinating from hybrid seed are nucellar seedlings of the mother.

|---|---|---|---|

|---|---|---|---|

| Gametophytic self-incompatibility | Pollen rejected if its own S allele matches the pistil | Yes | Solanaceae, Rosaceae, Papaveraceae |

## Questions

1. The flower of a plant is bisexual and protogynous. Can it avoid selfing completely? What else is needed to make it able to outcross only?

   **Answer** Protogyny prevents selfing only within a single flower. When different flowers on the plant are at different stages, a flower in the female phase can still receive pollen from a flower of the same plant in the male phase (geitonogamy), which is still genetically selfing. To make outcrossing the only possibility, one needs either that all the flowers of the plant be in the same sexual phase at the same time (as in the A and B types of avocado), or self-incompatibility, or dioecy.

2. In a plant with gametophytic self-incompatibility, an S₁S₂ plant is used as the mother and an S₂S₃ plant as the father. What are the genotypes and proportions of the offspring? If the self-incompatibility is sporophytic and S₂ is dominant over S₃, can this cross succeed?

   **Answer** Gametophytic: S₂ pollen is rejected, and only S₃ pollen can fertilize, so the offspring are S₁S₃ and S₂S₃ in equal proportions (1/2 each). Sporophytic: the phenotype of the pollen is determined by the paternal genotype S₂S₃, and when S₂ is dominant all the pollen shows the S₂ phenotype, which matches the S₂ of the mother, so all of it is rejected and the cross fails (if S₃ were dominant over S₂, all pollen would show S₃ and the cross would succeed).

3. A plant has white, tubular flowers about 10 cm long, which open in the evening, give off a rich sweet fragrance and hide the nectar at the base of the corolla tube. Infer its pollinator and state the limits of the inference.

   **Answer** By the pollination syndrome the pollinator is inferred to be a hawkmoth: it is active at night, has a keen sense of smell, can see white in the dark, and hovers to take nectar with a long proboscis. The limitation is that a pollination syndrome is only a tendency: the actual visitors may include other noctuid moths, bats and others, and the most frequent visitor is not necessarily the effective pollinator, so field observation and measurement of pollen carriage and deposition are necessary.

4. How would you design an experiment to distinguish the roles of the synergids and the egg cell in pollen tube guidance? Why is *Torenia* chosen?

   **Answer** Use a laser to kill one or both synergids, the egg cell, or the central cell, and then observe in in vitro culture whether pollen tubes are still attracted to these ovules and enter the micropyle. Attraction is lost only when both synergids are killed, which shows that the attractant comes from the synergids. *Torenia* is chosen because part of its embryo sac is exposed beyond the micropyle, so that the individual cells of the embryo sac can be identified and manipulated under the microscope without cutting the ovule open, and in vitro pollination is possible.

5. Why do seedlings of *Citrus* often retain the traits of the mother plant? What problem does this create for *Citrus* breeding?

   **Answer** *Citrus* is polyembryonic: besides the zygotic embryo the seed contains several adventitious embryos (nucellar embryos) that develop from nucellar cells, have the same genotype as the mother and grow more vigorously than the zygotic embryo, so most seedlings are clones of the mother. This is useful for propagating uniform rootstocks, but in cross breeding most seedlings that germinate from hybrid seed are nucellar seedlings of the mother, and true hybrids are hard to obtain; one needs embryo rescue or molecular markers to identify the zygotic seedlings, or must choose monoembryonic cultivars as the mother.
