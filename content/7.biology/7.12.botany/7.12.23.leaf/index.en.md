The leaf is the main organ of photosynthesis and transpiration in seed plants, and also the most variable organ in their morphology. Leaf form is fairly constant within a species but differs enormously between species, so the terminology of leaf morphology is the basic language for describing and identifying plants and will be used repeatedly in the chapters on angiosperm classification ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)–[7.12.49](/7.12.49)). This chapter first treats the functions and parts of the leaf, then gives the morphological terminology systematically, then describes how leaves arise at the shoot apex and differentiate, and the anatomy of three kinds of leaf (eudicot, grass and pine). It ends with the ecological types of leaves and leaf fall. The leaves of seed plants are **megaphylls**; their origin is discussed in [7.12.16](/7.12.16), the arrangement of leaves on the stem and its mathematics in the chapter on the stem ([7.12.21](/7.12.21)), and the modifications of leaves in the next chapter ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)). The tissues on which the leaf depends (epidermis, stomata, vascular bundles and so on) were covered in [7.12.4](/7.12.4) and [7.12.5](/7.12.5). The physiological mechanisms of photosynthesis, transpiration, stomatal movement and leaf abscission belong to plant physiology, so only morphology and anatomy are treated here.

## 23.1. Leaf Functions

**Definition 23.1.1 (Leaf)** A **leaf** is a lateral organ that arises from the flank of the shoot apical meristem, has limited growth, and is usually flat with distinct upper and lower surfaces. Leaves are borne at the nodes of the stem; the angle between leaf and stem is the leaf axil, which usually bears an axillary bud. This is what distinguishes a leaf from a leaf-like shoot (a cladode, [7.12.24](/7.12.24)).

The principal physiological functions of the leaf are **photosynthesis** and transpiration. The thin, broad blade, the chloroplast-rich mesophyll cells, the well-developed intercellular spaces connected to the stomata, and the veins that bring in water and carry out photosynthetic products are all adaptations to photosynthesis. Transpiration is the loss of water as vapor from the plant surface into the atmosphere; the leaf is the main transpiring organ, and most of the water absorbed by the roots is lost this way. Transpiration has three kinds of significance: it is the main driving force for water uptake by the roots and for the ascent of water in the plant body; the mineral salts absorbed by the roots are carried upward in the transpiration stream; and it lowers the leaf temperature so that leaves do not overheat in strong light. Many leaf structures (stomata concentrated on the lower epidermis, dense hairs on the epidermis, sunken stomata or stomata in stomatal crypts) reduce transpiration.

Leaves also function in absorption, storage and reproduction. The leaf surface can absorb sprayed fertilizers and pesticides (foliar feeding; organophosphorus insecticides enter the plant through the leaf). The bulb scales of onion and lily and the fleshy leaves of aloe and stonecrop (*Sedum*) store nutrients or water. In mother-of-thousands (*Bryophyllum pinnatum*), plantlets with adventitious roots arise in the notches of the leaf margin and, after dropping off, grow into new individuals. The physiological mechanisms of these functions belong to plant physiology; leaf modifications are treated in [7.12.24](/7.12.24).

Leaves have many economic uses. Chinese cabbage, cabbage, spinach, celery (the petiole is eaten) and Chinese chives are leaf vegetables; tea leaves make a beverage and tobacco leaves are made into cigarettes. The steviol glycosides in the leaves of stevia (*Stevia rebaudiana*) are about 200–300 times as sweet as sucrose; foxglove (*Digitalis purpurea*) leaves contain cardiac glycosides, deadly nightshade (*Atropa belladonna*) leaves contain hyoscyamine and scopolamine, and mint and mulberry leaves are used medicinally. Leaves of scented geranium and spearmint yield essential oils; the leaf fibers of sisal (*Agave sisalana* and others) are made into rope; the leaves of mulberry, castor bean and sawtooth oak feed silkworms; bamboo leaves of *Indocalamus* wrap zongzi and Chinese fan palm leaves are made into fans; the leaf-sheath fibers of the windmill palm (*Trachycarpus fortunei*) are made into ropes and mattresses.

## 23.2. Parts of the Leaf

### 23.2.1. Blade, Petiole and Stipules

**Definition 23.2.1 (Blade, Petiole and Stipules)** A typical leaf has three parts. The **leaf blade** (lamina) is the main part, usually a green flat body. The **petiole** is the slender stalk-like part between blade and stem; its upper (distal) end joins the blade and its lower (proximal) end joins the stem. The **stipules** are small appendages on both sides of the petiole base, usually paired. A leaf with all three parts is a complete leaf; one lacking one or two of them is an incomplete leaf.

The leaves of pear, peach, pea and rose are complete. The most common incomplete leaves lack stipules (tea, Chinese cabbage, lilac, camphor tree). In some plants the stipules fall early and only stipule scars can be seen (in *Magnolia* the stipule scars encircle the twig). A leaf lacking both petiole and stipules is a sessile leaf, as in the stem leaves of lettuce, sow thistle and shepherd's purse. Leaves lacking a blade are rare; in *Acacia confusa*, apart from the seedling stage, all the "leaves" of the plant are petioles flattened into blades (phyllodes, [7.12.24](/7.12.24)).

Petioles can twist as they grow and so adjust the orientation of the blade, so that blades do not shade one another (**leaf mosaic**, §23.3.6). In some plants the base of the petiole or petiolule is conspicuously swollen to form a pulvinus; changes in turgor of its parenchyma cells move the blade. In the sensitive plant (*Mimosa pudica*), for example, the main petiole, the pinnae and the leaflets all have pulvini, so that on touch the leaflets fold together and the petiole droops; the "sleep movements" of the leaflets of many legumes also depend on pulvini (the mechanism belongs to plant physiology).

The form of the **stipule** varies greatly and is an important taxonomic character:

| Type | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Free stipules | Separate from the petiole, often small and early deciduous | apple, pear, mulberry |
| Adnate stipules | Fused to the petiole base, forming wings on both sides of the petiole | rose |
| Foliaceous stipules | Large and green, photosynthetic | pea (stipules larger than leaflets) |
| Ocrea | Two stipules fused into a sheath surrounding the node | Polygonaceae (buckwheat, *Pleuropterus multiflorus*) |
| Tendril-like stipules | A tendril on each side of the petiole, traditionally interpreted as modified stipules | *Smilax* |
| Interpetiolar stipules | The adjacent stipules of opposite leaves fused between the two petioles, sometimes leaf-like | Rubiaceae (madder, gardenia) |
| Stipular spines | Stipules turned into spines | black locust, jujube |
| Scarious stipules | Thin and membranous, protecting young leaves | ramie |

How the blade is attached to the stem is also a commonly described character: petiolate and sessile; among sessile leaves, those whose base clasps the stem are amplexicaul (as in *Ixeris sonchifolia*); those whose bases are fused on the far side of the stem, so that the stem seems to pass through the blade, are perfoliate; those whose base runs down the stem as a wing are decurrent (as in *Carduus*); those whose petiole is attached to the lower surface of the blade rather than to its margin are peltate (lotus, castor bean, nasturtium), and such leaves are also called shield-shaped leaves; those whose base is expanded into a sheath enclosing the stem are sheathing (Poaceae, Cyperaceae, Apiaceae, Zingiberaceae). The thick "trunk" of banana and plantain is in fact a pseudostem made of many nested leaf sheaths.

### 23.2.2. Grass Leaves

The leaf of a grass is simple, without petiole or stipules, and consists of a blade and a sheath; at their junction there are several characteristic appendages (Fig. 1B).

**Definition 23.2.2 (Leaf Sheath, Ligule and Auricle)** The expanded base of a grass leaf that wraps around the culm is the **leaf sheath**. The membranous or hairy, upward-projecting structure on the adaxial side at the junction of blade and sheath is the **ligule**. The pair of flap-like, claw-like or hairy projections that extend from the blade margin at its base on either side of the ligule are the **auricles**. The paler band on the outer (abaxial) side of the junction is the collar.

The leaf sheath protects the young bud and the **intercalary meristem** at the base of the internode, and strengthens the culm. The ligule prevents rain water, insects and fungal spores from entering the sheath, and also makes the blade spread outward; the collar is elastic and extensible and adjusts the angle of the blade. The grass ligule has the same name as the ligule of lycophytes (Definition 16.5.2), but the two are not homologous: the latter is the small tongue-like outgrowth on the adaxial base of the microphylls of *Selaginella* and *Isoetes*, whereas the former is the appendage at the top of the leaf sheath in the grasses, an angiosperm family.

The presence, shape and size of ligule and auricles are important for identifying grasses, especially for telling cereal seedlings from weeds in the field. Rice has a ligule and auricles: the ligule tip is split into two narrow triangular lobes, and the auricles are narrow and long, with hairs. Barnyard grass (*Echinochloa crus-galli*) has neither ligule nor auricles, which is the usual way to distinguish it from rice seedlings in the seedbed. Sugarcane has an arched ligule and triangular or lanceolate auricles; barley has large auricles and wheat small ones. Sedges (Cyperaceae) also have closed leaf sheaths but usually no ligule (*Carex* has a "ligule" formed at the mouth of the sheath); the three-angled stem and three-ranked leaves distinguish them from grasses ([7.12.39](/7.12.39)).

## 23.3. Terminology of Leaf Morphology

Leaf blades differ greatly in size and shape among plants. The scale leaves of cypress are only a few millimeters long; the blade of Japanese banana (*Musa basjoo*) reaches 1–2 m; the floating leaf of the Amazon water lily (*Victoria amazonica*) can exceed 2 m in diameter and bear a load of several tens of kilograms; the pinnate compound leaves of the palm genus *Raphia* can exceed 20 m, the longest leaves in the plant kingdom. Within a single species, however, leaf form is fairly stable, which is why it serves in identification and classification. A leaf is generally described in the order: phyllotaxis, blade shape, apex, base, margin, lobing, venation, size, texture, surface indumentum, petiole and stipules. The terms in the following sections follow the usage of the *Flora of China*, with the corresponding English terms given in parentheses where the Chinese name differs; representative plants for each term are collected in the quick-reference table in the Summary.

### 23.3.1. Leaf Shape

Leaf shape generally refers to the outline of the whole blade and is determined mainly by two things: the ratio of length to width, and the position of the widest part (near the base, in the middle, or near the apex). A table arranged by these two criteria organizes the common leaf shapes into a system:

| Length : width | Widest near base | Widest at middle | Widest near apex |
|---|---|---|---|
| about 1:1 | broadly ovate | orbicular | broadly obovate |
| about 1.5–2:1 | ovate | broadly elliptic | obovate |
| about 3–4:1 | lanceolate | oblong, elliptic | oblanceolate |
| about 6:1 | — | ensiform (sword-shaped) | — |
| 10:1 or more | — | linear | — |

When describing shape, qualifiers such as "narrowly", "broadly" and "ob-" (inverted) are added to the basic shape: an elliptic leaf that is long is narrowly elliptic or oblong, an ovate leaf that is wide is broadly ovate, an ovate leaf inverted (broad at the apex, narrow at the base) is obovate, and likewise oblanceolate, obcordate and so on. The ratios in the table are approximate and individual plants may deviate; transitions between two shapes are expressed by compound terms such as "ovate-lanceolate" or "elliptic-lanceolate", in which the first word is the minor component.

Besides those in the table, common leaf shapes include the following (Fig. 2A).

- Acicular (needle-shaped): slender with a sharp tip, as in the leaves of pine and spruce.
- Linear: narrow and long, with nearly parallel margins, as in the leaves of rice, wheat, chive, daffodil and fir.
- Subulate (awl-shaped): short and stiff, tapering from base to apex, as in the leaves of Japanese cedar (*Cryptomeria japonica*).
- Squamiform (scale-like): small and scale-like, pressed against the twig, as in the leaves of Oriental arborvitae (*Platycladus orientalis*) and Chinese weeping cypress (*Cupressus funebris*).
- Lanceolate: as in the leaves of willow and peach; elliptic: as in camphor tree and lilac daphne (*Daphne genkwa*); ovate: as in sunflower and ramie.
- Cordate (heart-shaped): like ovate but with the base indented in a heart shape, as in the leaves of Chinese redbud (*Cercis chinensis*).
- Reniform (kidney-shaped): base with an obtuse indentation, apex rounded, broader than long, as in the leaves of gotu kola (*Centella asiatica*) and common mallow.
- Orbicular (circular): as in lotus leaves (peltate).
- Rhombic: approximately equilateral rhombus, as in the leaves of water chestnut (*Trapa*).
- Spatulate: broad and rounded at the apex, narrowing toward the base, as in the leaves of pot marigold.
- Flabellate (fan-shaped): broad and truncate or wavy at the apex, cuneate at the base, as in the leaves of ginkgo (*Ginkgo biloba*).
- Deltoid (triangular): as in the leaves of eastern cottonwood (*Populus deltoides*) and mile-a-minute weed (*Persicaria perfoliata*).
- Falcate (sickle-shaped): narrow, long and curved like a sickle, as in the adult leaves of blue gum (*Eucalyptus globulus*).
- Ensiform (sword-shaped): long, slightly broader, with a pointed apex, often thick and stiff, as in the leaves of iris.
- Fistular (tubular): a hollow cylinder, as in the leaves of onion.

### 23.3.3. Leaf Margin and Lobing

The edge of the blade is the margin. By the degree of indentation, the types of margin are as follows (Fig. 2D).

| Margin | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| entire | even, without teeth or notches | Chinese privet (*Ligustrum lucidum*), yulan magnolia, camphor tree, Chinese redbud, lilac |
| ciliate | entire but with a row of hairs along the edge | some grasses, *Rhododendron* |
| undulate | edge rising and falling in waves | oleaster (*Elaeagnus*), daimyo oak (*Quercus dentata*) |
| crispate | waves larger than in undulate margins, edge crinkled | ornamental kale |
| revolute | edge rolled toward the lower surface | rosemary, some *Rhododendron* species |
| serrate | teeth sharp and pointing toward the apex (forward) | rose, Ussurian pear (*Pyrus ussuriensis*) |
| serrulate | teeth fine | kiwifruit |
| doubly serrate | the teeth themselves bearing smaller teeth | false spiraea (*Sorbaria*), flowering almond (*Prunus triloba*) |
| dentate | teeth pointing straight outward, the two sides nearly equal | mulberry, *Najas* |
| denticulate | teeth fine | — |
| crenate | teeth rounded, not sharp | gotu kola, Japanese spindle (*Euonymus japonicus*) |
| spinose | teeth ending in stiff spines | Chinese holly, sow thistle |

Deeper indentations of the margin are lobes (the notches are sinuses), and the parts between the sinuses are lobes. By the arrangement of the lobes, lobing is pinnate or palmate: lobes arranged on either side of the midrib make a pinnately lobed leaf, as in dandelion, shepherd's purse and cypress vine (*Ipomoea quamoclit*); lobes radiating from the base make a palmately lobed leaf, as in sweetgum, Chinese parasol tree, plane tree, castor bean and cotton; a leaf with only three lobes is trilobed (trident maple, morning glory). By depth of incision there are three grades:

| Degree | Depth of incision (measured from margin to midrib or base) | Pinnate example | Palmate example |
|---|---|---|---|
| lobed | less than halfway (often about 1/3) | Mongolian oak | Chinese parasol tree, plane tree |
| parted | more than halfway | shepherd's purse, dandelion | castor bean |
| divided (dissected) | nearly to the midrib or base | cypress vine | — |

Divided leaves are easily confused with compound leaves. The difference is that the segments of a divided leaf have no petiolules at their bases, the veins of the segments are still connected to one another at the midrib or leaf base, and the blade falls as a whole; the leaflets of a compound leaf usually have petiolules and can fall singly. Division is a form intermediate between simple and compound leaves, and there is no sharp boundary between them.

### 23.3.4. Veins and Venation

**Definition 23.3.1 (Veins and Venation)** The vascular bundles running through the mesophyll, together with the mechanical tissue around them, are the **veins**. They are the conducting and supporting structures of the leaf and connect through the petiole with the vascular tissue of the stem. The pattern in which the veins are distributed in the blade is **venation**. The largest, central vein is the midrib; veins branching from it are lateral veins, finer ones are collectively veinlets, and the ends of the veinlets are vein endings.

There are three main kinds of venation: reticulate, parallel and dichotomous (Fig. 3A).

- Reticulate venation: there is a distinct main vein from which lateral veins branch, these branch again and again, and the veinlets join into a network. This is the venation of most dicotyledons. Where there is a single main vein with lateral veins arranged feather-like on both sides, the venation is pinnate (privet, peach, plum); where several main veins arise together from the leaf base and are arranged like a palm, it is palmate (castor bean, grape, cotton). Palmate venation with only three main veins is trinerved: if all three arise from the leaf base it is basal (jujube), and if the two lateral main veins leave the midrib some distance above the base it is suprabasal (camphor tree). In peltate leaves the main veins radiate from the point of petiole attachment (lotus, nasturtium). If the lateral veins run to the margin and end in the teeth, the venation is craspedodromous (elm, chestnut); if they curve within the margin and join one another in arcs, it is brochidodromous (privet).
- Parallel venation: the veins are nearly parallel, connected by fine cross veins, and common in monocotyledons. Where the veins run parallel from base to apex it is straight-parallel (rice, wheat, maize); where they leave the base parallel, gradually diverge in arcs and converge again at the apex it is arcuate (plantain, hosta); where they radiate from the base it is radiating (Chinese fan palm, windmill palm); where the midrib is prominent and the lateral veins leave it at right angles or obliquely and run parallel to the margin it is pinnate-parallel (banana, plantain, canna).
- Dichotomous venation: the veins fork repeatedly in twos and there is no midrib, as in ginkgo. This more primitive venation is common in ferns and agrees with the telome theory ([7.12.16](/7.12.16)), according to which megaphylls arose from dichotomously branching telomes.

The distinction of venation between monocotyledons and dicotyledons is only approximate: *Dioscorea*, *Smilax* and the Araceae among monocots have reticulate venation, while plantain is a eudicot (Plantaginaceae) but has arcuate veins.

### 23.3.5. Simple and Compound Leaves

**Definition 23.3.2 (Simple and Compound Leaves)** A leaf with one blade on a petiole is a **simple leaf**; a leaf with two or more blades on one petiole is a **compound leaf**. The petiole of a compound leaf is the common petiole; the axis bearing the leaflets above it is the rachis; the blades borne on the rachis are leaflets; and the stalk of a leaflet is a petiolule.

By the arrangement of the leaflets, compound leaves fall into the following types (Fig. 3B).

- Pinnately compound leaf: leaflets arranged feather-like on both sides of the rachis. If there is a terminal leaflet and the total number of leaflets is odd, the leaf is odd-pinnate (rose, black locust, Japanese pagoda tree, wisteria); if the tip bears a pair of leaflets and the total is even, it is even-pinnate (peanut, honey locust); the faba bean, with only a short point at the tip of the rachis, is also even-pinnate. If the rachis is unbranched the leaf is once-pinnate (black locust); if the rachis branches once and each branch (pinna) bears leaflets it is twice-pinnate (silk tree, *Albizia julibrissin*); branching twice gives a thrice-pinnate leaf (heavenly bamboo, *Nandina domestica*), and more times a multiply pinnate leaf (chinaberry, *Melia azedarach*). A leaf with an especially large terminal leaflet and smaller lower leaflets is lyrate; one with large and small leaflets alternating is interruptedly pinnate (potato).
- Palmately compound leaf: the leaflets are all borne at the tip of the common petiole and spread like a palm, as in horse chestnut, *Vitex negundo* and *Schefflera*. Where the lateral petiolules branch again to bear leaflets and the whole resembles a bird's foot, the leaf is pedate (*Cayratia japonica*, jiaogulan).
- Trifoliolate leaf: the rachis bears only three leaflets. If the petiolule of the terminal leaflet is clearly longer, the leaf is pinnately trifoliolate (alfalfa, soybean, common bean); if the three petiolules are equal and arise together from the tip of the common petiole, it is palmately trifoliolate (rubber tree, wood sorrel). A trifoliolate leaf whose leaflets are again trifoliolate is biternate.
- Unifoliolate compound leaf: externally like a simple leaf, but with a distinct joint between blade and petiole, and the petiole is usually expanded into wings on both sides, as in the citrus plants orange, pomelo and citron. It is generally believed to derive from a trifoliolate leaf by reduction of the two lateral leaflets, the joint being where the three leaflets were originally attached.

A compound leaf is sometimes confused with a twig bearing many simple leaves. The distinguishing points are: the rachis has no terminal bud, whereas a twig often does; there are no axillary buds in the axils of the leaflets, buds appearing only in the axil of the common petiole, whereas every leaf on a twig has an axillary bud; the leaflets of a compound leaf lie in one plane with the rachis, whereas leaves on a twig make an angle with the twig and point in different directions; and when the leaf falls, the leaflets of a compound leaf drop first and the rachis last (or the whole leaf drops together), whereas on a twig only the leaves drop and the twig itself does not.

### 23.3.6. Phyllotaxis and Leaf Mosaic

The way leaves are arranged on the stem is **phyllotaxis** ([7.12.21](/7.12.21)). The basic types are as follows.

- Alternate: one leaf per node, the leaves arranged in a spiral on the stem, as in camphor tree, poplar and plane tree. If successive leaves are 180° apart and form two ranks, the arrangement is distichous (rice, wheat, elm; iris and fan-shaped grass seedlings are also two-ranked).
- Opposite: two leaves per node, facing each other, as in lilac, mint, privet and pinks. If the leaves of successive nodes cross at right angles, the arrangement is decussate, the commonest form of opposite phyllotaxis.
- Whorled (verticillate): three or more leaves per node, arranged radially, as in oleander, lily, catalpa and whorled hydrilla.
- Fascicled: the internodes of a branch are extremely shortened and several leaves are clustered on a short shoot, as in ginkgo, goji (*Lycium*), larch and golden larch. The needles of *Pinus* are also clustered at the tip of a short shoot, with a sheath at the base and a fixed number, forming a needle fascicle (§23.5.7).
- Basal: the stem is very short and the leaves arise near the ground, often forming a rosette, as in the basal leaves of dandelion, plantain and shepherd's purse.

Alternate phyllotaxis can be summarized by a fraction. Starting from any leaf (the starting leaf), connect the insertion points of successive leaves with a thread, spiraling upward, until a leaf lies directly above the starting leaf on the same vertical line of the stem; this stretch is one cycle. Taking the number of turns around the stem in a cycle as numerator and the number of leaves as denominator gives the phyllotactic fraction, and the angle between successive leaves (the divergence) equals $360^\circ$ times the fraction. Common phyllotactic fractions are 1/2 (divergence $180^\circ$; rice, wheat, elm), 1/3 ($120^\circ$; mulberry, mallow, Cyperaceae), 2/5 ($144^\circ$; peach, plum, geranium), 3/8 ($135^\circ$; chrysanthemum, hemp, plantain), 5/13, 8/21 and so on. The numerators and denominators of these fractions are Fibonacci numbers, and the divergence approaches the golden angle $137.5^\circ$; the origin of phyllotaxis and its mathematics are discussed in [7.12.21](/7.12.21).

**Definition 23.3.3 (Leaf Mosaic)** Whatever the phyllotaxis, the leaves of successive nodes on a branch are always arranged so as not to overlap one another, forming a mosaic. This phenomenon is the **leaf mosaic**.

Leaf mosaics are produced mainly by differences in petiole length, twisting of the petiole and different angles of blade insertion. Seen from above the plant, the mosaic is particularly clear, especially in species with very short internodes and clustered leaves, such as tobacco, plantain, dandelion and tatsoi. The leaves of Boston ivy, English ivy and Lady Banks' rose spread evenly over a wall or fence as a result of leaf mosaic, which is why they are good materials for vertical greening. Leaf mosaic lets leaves avoid shading one another and receive full light, and also balances the load on the sides of the stem.

### 23.3.7. Heterophylly

**Definition 23.3.4 (Heterophylly)** The occurrence of leaves of different shapes on the same plant is **heterophylly**.

Heterophylly is of two kinds. In one, leaf shape differs with the age of the branch (the developmental stage of the plant), also called heteroblasty. On young shoots of blue gum (*Eucalyptus globulus*) the leaves are small, ovate, sessile and opposite; on old branches they are larger, lanceolate or falcate, petiolate, alternate and often drooping. Chinese juniper (*Juniperus chinensis*) and northern white cedar (*Thuja occidentalis*) have needle-shaped leaves on young branches and scale-like leaves on old ones. In Chinese cabbage and rape the basal leaves are large with a strap-shaped petiole, and the upper leaves are small, sessile and clasping. In *Acacia confusa* and many Australian species of *Acacia*, the seedling first produces normal pinnate compound leaves and later leaves consist only of flattened petioles ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)). The other kind is ecological heterophylly caused by the environment. In arrowhead (*Sagittaria trifolia*) the aerial leaves are sagittate, the floating leaves elliptic and the submerged leaves ribbon-like; in water crowfoots (aquatic species of *Ranunculus*) the aerial leaves are flat and broad, while the submerged leaves are finely divided into threads, which increases the surface in contact with water and resists tearing in currents.

## 23.4. Leaf Development

### 23.4.1. Leaf Primordia and Leaf Growth

All parts of the leaf are formed well before the bud opens and are folded in various ways within the bud; after the bud opens the young leaf gradually grows into the mature leaf. Leaves arise very early: a group of cells (founder cells) at a certain position in the peripheral zone ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)) of the **apical meristem** of the shoot tip change the direction of their divisions and expansion and form a lateral protrusion (leaf buttress), which grows on to become a leaf primordium. According to the **tunica–corpus theory**, the leaf primordium of angiosperms arises from periclinal divisions of cells of the second tunica layer (L2) and the corpus (L3), while the outermost tunica layer (L1) divides only anticlinally, covers the surface of the primordium and later becomes the leaf epidermis. The adaxial side of the primordium (facing the shoot tip, the future upper surface) is flatter and the abaxial side more convex, so there is dorsiventrality from the very beginning. The position of the primordium on the stem determines phyllotaxis ([7.12.21](/7.12.21)); cells in the leaf axil form the axillary bud primordium somewhat later.

After formation, the primordium first undergoes apical growth, which quickly elongates it into a cone; this stage is brief, and later growth of the leaf does not depend mainly on the apex. Then a band of densely packed dividing cells appears along each side of the primordium, the marginal meristem, whose activity expands the primordium laterally into a flat blade, while the central part of the primordium differentiates into the midrib (the rachis in compound leaves); in the petiole region the marginal meristem is inactive or stops very early. In an entire simple leaf the marginal meristem is active only for a short time; in leaves with lobes or teeth it is divided into faster- and slower-growing segments, the fast ones forming lobes or teeth and the slow ones forming sinuses; the leaflets of compound leaves also arise by local activity of the marginal meristem at the corresponding positions. After the form of the blade is established, its enlargement depends mainly on intercalary growth, that is, cell division and enlargement (chiefly enlargement) throughout the blade.

Unlike the indeterminate growth of roots and stems, leaf growth is determinate: the leaf stops growing on reaching a certain size. In dicotyledons, leaf maturation generally begins at the apex and proceeds toward the base (basipetal maturation), the apex stopping first and the base last. In monocotyledons, especially grasses, an **intercalary meristem** persists for a long time at the leaf base and the leaf keeps growing from the base, so that it can continue to grow after the upper blade is cut off (which is why lawns can be mowed repeatedly); in the grass leaf primordium the upper part develops into the blade and the lower part into the sheath, the boundary between them being marked by the appearance of the ligule.

### 23.4.2. Differentiation of Mesophyll and Veins

Cell division, enlargement and differentiation of the leaf primordium produce the **mesophyll**. The number of mesophyll layers is characteristic of a given kind of plant and is fixed early; once the layers are formed they divide only anticlinally, so the blade enlarges in area without increasing in thickness. Mesophyll differentiation begins with anticlinal elongation of the future palisade cells, accompanied by anticlinal divisions; the cells of the spongy mesophyll also divide anticlinally, but fewer times than palisade cells, and remain nearly isodiametric. Division lasts longest in the palisade tissue; while it is still dividing, the neighboring epidermal cells have already stopped dividing and expand (mainly parallel to the leaf surface), so that in mature leaves several palisade cells are often attached beneath one epidermal cell. After division ends, the cells separate from one another along the anticlinal walls, forming intercellular spaces; this happens earlier in the spongy than in the palisade tissue, and spongy cells grow locally as they separate, developing in many plants into branched or armed cells (as in pear leaves).

Vein development is described here for dicotyledons. While the leaf primordium is still a small peg-like protrusion, the **procambium** at the site of the future midrib begins to differentiate; it extends upward into the primordium and is continuous with the procambium of the **leaf trace** in the stem. The midrib and the larger lateral veins differentiate acropetally (appearing first at the leaf base, then extending upward toward the margin); the orders of lateral veins arise from derivatives of the marginal meristem, the larger lateral veins arising earlier than smaller ones and lying closer to the marginal meristem. Smaller veins develop between the larger ones, usually beginning near the apex and proceeding downward, and are formed continually throughout the period of intercalary growth: the ground tissue between earlier veins retains for a long time the ability to produce new procambial strands. The finest veins may arise from procambium of a single cell file. Phloem differentiates continuously along the procambium, whereas the first mature xylem appears in isolated segments and only later joins into a continuous system. In leaves with parallel venation, several veins of similar size develop acropetally at the same time, and the cross veins form last.

### 23.4.3. Dorsiventrality and the Development of Compound Leaves

From the beginning the leaf has distinct adaxial and abaxial sides. This dorsiventrality is determined by two mutually antagonistic sets of genes: the adaxial side expresses HD-ZIP III transcription factors (*PHABULOSA*, *PHAVOLUTA* and *REVOLUTA* of *Arabidopsis*), whose mRNAs are degraded on the abaxial side by miR165/166, while the abaxial side expresses genes of the *KANADI* and *YABBY* families. The blade expands along the margin only at the boundary between the two fates: the *phantastica* mutant of snapdragon loses adaxial identity and, in severe cases, produces needle-like, nearly radially symmetric leaves without a blade (Waites and Hudson, 1995), showing that formation of a flat blade requires a boundary between adaxial and abaxial cells. The development of compound leaves is related to KNOX class homeobox genes: KNOX genes (*SHOOT MERISTEMLESS* in *Arabidopsis*) maintain the meristem at the shoot tip and are switched off in leaf primordia, and the formation of a simple leaf requires this switching off; in many compound-leaved plants such as tomato, KNOX is expressed again in the leaf primordium, so that the primordium retains marginal meristematic activity for a long time and produces leaflets, and overexpression of KNOX genes in tomato turns the compound leaf into a multiply divided "super-compound" leaf (Hareven et al., 1996). Pea is an exception: its compound leaf is controlled by *UNIFOLIATA* (a homolog of *LEAFY*) rather than by KNOX. The distal part of the pea compound leaf is tendrils; the *afila* mutation converts all leaflets into tendrils and the *tendril-less* mutation converts tendrils into leaflets (*TL* encodes a class I HD-ZIP transcription factor, Hofer et al., 2009), and the double mutant produces a leaf branching repeatedly and ending in minute leaflets, showing that leaflets and tendrils are two fates of the same lateral structure. The molecular details of these developmental programs belong to plant physiology (plant development); here only the framework needed to explain morphology is given. Leaf tendrils are discussed further in [7.12.24](/7.12.24).

## 23.5. Leaf Structure

### 23.5.1. Dorsiventral and Isobilateral Leaves

However much leaf form varies, the blade is made up of three basic structures: the epidermis forms the outermost layer and protects; the mesophyll lies inside the epidermis, carries out photosynthesis and stores nutrients; and the veins are embedded in the mesophyll and conduct and support. Their shapes, arrangement and numbers vary with the plant and its habitat, but all three are always present. By differentiation of the mesophyll, leaf blades fall into two classes.

**Definition 23.5.1 (Dorsiventral and Isobilateral Leaves)** A blade that extends nearly horizontally, with unequal illumination of its two sides, whose mesophyll is differentiated into palisade tissue near the upper epidermis and spongy tissue near the lower epidermis, and which is dark green above (adaxial) and pale green below (abaxial), is a **dorsiventral leaf** (also called a bifacial leaf). A blade that stands nearly erect, with similar illumination on both sides, and whose mesophyll is not differentiated into palisade and spongy tissue or has palisade tissue on both sides, is an **isobilateral leaf**.

Most dicotyledons have dorsiventral leaves. The drooping falcate leaves of adult blue gum branches are lit about equally on both sides and have palisade tissue on both sides, so they are isobilateral; the mesophyll of grasses is undifferentiated, so grass leaves are also isobilateral. The ensiform leaves of iris and the tubular leaves of onion are more unusual: in development they arise only from the abaxial side of the leaf primordium, so both surfaces of the leaf are abaxial; such leaves are called unifacial.

### 23.5.2. Epidermis

The **epidermis** (Definition 4.3.1) covers the whole blade and is divided into an upper and a lower epidermis, usually consisting of one layer of living cells. In paradermal sections, parallel to the leaf surface, the epidermal cells are regular or irregular in shape; in many dicotyledons the anticlinal walls are wavy and interlock like jigsaw pieces, which joins the cells very firmly. In transverse sections the epidermal cells are roughly rectangular with a thicker outer wall covered by the **cuticle**, over which there is often wax. The thickness of the cuticle varies with species and habitat: it is thick in xerophytes and evergreens and almost absent in submerged leaves. Epidermal cells generally contain no chloroplasts, and the transparent epidermis lets light into the mesophyll. The leaves of oleander and Indian rubber tree (*Ficus elastica*) have a multiple epidermis of several cell layers, the outer protecting and the inner storing water; the upper epidermis of the rubber tree also contains large idioblasts holding **cystoliths**.

The leaf epidermis bears many **stomata** (Definition 4.3.3), which are the gateway for gas exchange, the passage for water-vapor loss in transpiration, and the route by which sprayed liquids enter the leaf. The types of stomatal complex (anomocytic, anisocytic, paracytic, diacytic) and their development are described in [7.12.4](/7.12.4); they are used in identification and classification. The number and distribution of stomata vary among plants:

| Plant | Upper epidermis ($\mathrm{mm^{-2}}$) | Lower epidermis ($\mathrm{mm^{-2}}$) |
|---|---|---|
| wheat | 33 | 14 |
| oat | 25 | 23 |

| sunflower | 85 | 156 |

(Data from Lu Shiwan, *Zhiwuxue* [Botany], vol. 1, 2nd ed.) In most dicotyledons the stomata are found mainly or only on the lower epidermis (hypostomatic, as in nasturtium and apple); the floating leaves of water lily and lotus have stomata only on the upper epidermis (epistomatic); grasses, sunflower and castor bean have them on both sides (amphistomatic), the numbers on the two sides being similar in grasses; in oleander the stomata occur only in sunken stomatal crypts of the lower epidermis; submerged leaves generally have no stomata (pondweed, *Potamogeton*). On one plant, upper leaves have more stomata than lower leaves, and the leaf tip and the area near the midrib have more than the base and margin; plants of one species growing in full sun have more stomata than those growing in shade. On the epidermis at the leaf tip or at the teeth of the margin there are also the water pores of **hydathodes** (Definition 5.4.3), modified stomata whose guard cells remain permanently open and cannot close. The presence and type of trichomes vary among species (§23.3.8).

### 23.5.3. Mesophyll

**Definition 23.5.2 (Mesophyll, Palisade Mesophyll and Spongy Mesophyll)** The green assimilatory tissue between the upper and lower epidermis of a leaf is collectively the **mesophyll**, composed of parenchyma cells rich in chloroplasts. In dorsiventral leaves, the mesophyll cells near the upper epidermis are long and columnar, with their long axes perpendicular to the leaf surface and arranged neatly like a palisade; this is the **palisade mesophyll**. The mesophyll cells between the palisade tissue and the lower epidermis are irregular in shape and loosely arranged with large intercellular spaces; this is the **spongy mesophyll**.

The palisade tissue has one to several cell layers, the number depending on species and light; its cells contain many chloroplasts, most of the chloroplasts of the leaf are in the palisade tissue, and the upper side of the leaf is therefore a deeper green. There are also intercellular spaces between the columnar palisade cells, so that light can be conducted downward between and within the cells and reach the chloroplasts of the lower layers; chloroplasts can also move within the cell with light intensity (in strong light they lie against the side walls parallel to the light). The spongy mesophyll cells contain fewer chloroplasts, so the lower side of the leaf is paler; their intercellular spaces are interconnected and communicate with the larger substomatal chambers beneath the stomata, forming the internal aeration system of the leaf through which carbon dioxide diffuses to every mesophyll cell. Photosynthesis takes place mainly in the mesophyll. The mesophyll sometimes also contains secretory cavities (the oil cavities of citrus), idioblasts containing crystals, and sclereids (as in the leaves of tea and osmanthus).

### 23.5.4. Veins and Petiole

The veins are the vascular bundles of the leaf, and their structure differs with the size of the vein. The thick midrib consists of a **vascular bundle** and accompanying mechanical tissue. The vascular bundles enter the leaf from the stem laterally, so in the leaf the xylem is above (adaxial) and the phloem below (abaxial).

**Definition 23.5.3 (Bundle Sheath)** The one or more layers of tightly packed cells surrounding a vascular bundle in the leaf form the **bundle sheath**; it consists mostly of parenchyma cells and sometimes of thick-walled cells. In some plants, one or several rows of cells of the bundle sheath extend toward the upper and lower epidermis and join it; these are bundle-sheath extensions.

In the midrib and larger veins there is often a cambium between xylem and phloem, but it is active only briefly and produces little secondary tissue. Above and below the bundle there is often a considerable amount of **collenchyma** or **sclerenchyma** in direct contact with the epidermis, better developed below, so the midrib often projects prominently on the lower surface of the leaf. As the veins divide into finer branches, their structure becomes simpler: first the cambium disappears, then the mechanical tissue gradually decreases and vanishes, and then the composition of xylem and phloem becomes simplified. Veins of intermediate size generally have only primary structure. The last-order veins end in the mesophyll as free vein endings, with xylem of only one or two spiral **tracheids** and phloem of only short, narrow sieve-tube elements and enlarged **companion cells**, sometimes with xylem only. These small veins are surrounded by mesophyll cells, which are generally only a few cells away from the nearest vein, and the exchange of water and photosynthetic products takes place mainly in the small veins (the total vein length per unit leaf area in angiosperms is much greater than in other plants, which is considered one reason for the high photosynthetic capacity of angiosperms).

The structure of the petiole is simpler than that of the blade and resembles the stem: the outermost layer is the epidermis; inside it is the ground tissue, whose outer part often has several layers of **collenchyma** and whose inner part is parenchyma; in the ground tissue there are vascular bundles of variable number and size, arranged in cross section in an arc (open side up), a ring or a row, with xylem above and phloem below, each bundle often surrounded by thick-walled cells. In the vascular bundles of dicot petioles there is often a briefly active cambium. The separation and merging of bundles in the petiole make their number and arrangement vary greatly, and the outline of the petiole in cross section (half-moon, round, triangular) and the arrangement of the bundles can be used in identification. Foliaceous stipules have a structure much like that of the blade.

### 23.5.5. The Grass Leaf Blade

The leaves of monocotyledons vary in shape: linear (rice, wheat), tubular (onion), ensiform (iris), ovate (hosta), lanceolate (dayflower, *Commelina*); the venation is mostly parallel, rarely reticulate (*Dioscorea*, *Smilax*). The grass blade likewise consists of epidermis, mesophyll and veins, but each part has its own characteristics (Fig. 4B).

The epidermal cells are regular in shape and arranged in longitudinal rows, of two kinds, long cells and short cells. Long cells are rectangular prisms, with their long axes parallel to the long axis of the leaf and nearly square in cross section; their walls are not only cutinized but also heavily silicified, which is characteristic of grass leaves. Short cells are of two kinds, silica cells and cork cells; the silica cells are often filled with a single silica body (phytolith), and the cork cell walls are suberized. On the epidermis, one long cell generally alternates with two short cells (one silica cell and one cork cell), and sometimes several short cells are clustered together. Grass leaves are stiff and their edges can cut a finger because of the silica they contain; the phytoliths formed by silica cells differ in shape, and phytoliths in soils and archaeological deposits can be used to identify ancient cereal crops. Both upper and lower epidermis have stomata, arranged in longitudinal rows parallel to the veins, consisting of two dumbbell-shaped guard cells and two subsidiary cells ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)). In the upper epidermis there are also several rows of large, thin-walled cells between two vascular bundles:

**Definition 23.5.4 (Bulliform Cells)** In the upper epidermis of the leaves of grasses and other monocotyledons, a group of large water-containing cells arranged in longitudinal rows between two veins are the **bulliform cells**, also called motor cells. Their vacuoles are large, they have no chloroplasts or only a few, their anticlinal walls are thin and the outer wall is thicker; in cross section several cells are arranged in a fan, the middle one being the largest.

It is generally thought that when water is scarce the bulliform cells lose water quickly and shrink, so that the blade rolls upward into a tube, reducing transpiration, and that when water is ample they swell and the blade unrolls; but experiments also show that rolling is caused mainly by shrinkage of mesophyll and epidermal tissue other than the bulliform cells, and the role of bulliform cells remains debated.

The mesophyll is fairly uniform and not differentiated into palisade and spongy tissue; grass leaves are isobilateral. The intercellular spaces of the mesophyll are small, with larger substomatal chambers only beneath the stomata. In wheat, rice and others the mesophyll cell walls are infolded, so that the cell forms a structure of "peaks, valleys, waists and rings", with chloroplasts arranged along the cell wall, which enlarges the photosynthetic surface.

The vascular bundles of the veins are collateral bundles with limited growth (no cambium), like those of the stem, and large and small bundles alternate in parallel rows; above and below the larger bundles, sheets of sclerenchyma join the epidermis, dividing the mesophyll and giving support. The midrib of rice projects toward the lower side of the leaf and consists of several bundles of alternating size and parenchyma, with large, partitioned air cavities in the center that communicate with the **aerenchyma** of stem and root, so that the oxygen released by photosynthesis in the leaf can be carried to the roots to supply respiration of the flooded roots. Around the bundles is the bundle sheath, whose type is related to the pathway of photosynthesis and is discussed in the next section.

### 23.5.6. Leaves of C3 and C4 Plants

By the first product of carbon dioxide fixation in photosynthesis, plants are divided into C3 and C4 plants ([7.1.25](/7.1.25)). The **C4 pathway** divides the labor in space: mesophyll cells first fix $\ce{HCO3-}$ into a four-carbon acid, which enters the bundle-sheath cells and is decarboxylated, raising the $\ce{CO2}$ concentration around Rubisco. This division of labor requires a special leaf anatomy (Fig. 4).

**Definition 23.5.5 (Kranz Anatomy)** In the leaves of C4 plants, the bundle sheath consists of one layer of large, neatly arranged parenchyma cells rich in chloroplasts, and closely adjacent to its outside is a ring of radially arranged mesophyll cells; in cross section the two form two concentric rings. This arrangement is **Kranz anatomy** (German *Kranz*, "wreath").

Kranz leaves have several mutually coordinated features: the veins are dense, generally only two to four mesophyll cells separate adjacent veins, and every mesophyll cell lies against a bundle-sheath cell; the bundle-sheath cells are large and thick-walled, with numerous plasmodesmata between them and the mesophyll cells, and their chloroplasts are large and numerous and often accumulate starch; in maize, sorghum and sugarcane (NADP-malic enzyme type) these chloroplasts have almost no grana; the chloroplasts of the mesophyll cells are smaller and have normal grana. The leaves of C3 plants have no Kranz anatomy, the veins are sparser, the bundle-sheath cells have few or no chloroplasts, and the whole process of photosynthesis takes place in the mesophyll cells.

The bundle sheath of grasses has two basic compositions, which can be used as a guide to subfamilies:

| | C3 grasses (Pooideae: wheat, barley, oat; Oryzoideae: rice) | C4 grasses (NADP-malic enzyme type of Panicoideae: maize, sorghum, sugarcane, etc.) | C4 grasses (NAD-malic enzyme and PEP carboxykinase types of Chloridoideae and Panicoideae, e.g., foxtail millet, Bermuda grass) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of sheath layers | two | one | two |
| Outer layer | larger parenchyma cells with few chloroplasts | — | large parenchyma cells rich in chloroplasts (the inner ring of the Kranz) |
| Inner layer | smaller thick-walled cells with almost no chloroplasts, walls often suberized (the mestome sheath) | only one layer of large, chloroplast-rich parenchyma sheath (at the position of the mestome sheath) | mestome sheath |
| Mesophyll | not arranged around the veins, veins spaced farther apart | radially surrounding the bundle sheath (the outer ring of the Kranz) | same as at left |

So the rule "two sheath layers means a C3 plant, one means a C4 plant" holds only for common crops such as wheat and rice versus maize and sorghum and cannot serve as a general criterion; the criterion is whether there is a Kranz arrangement of chloroplast-rich sheath cells and radially arranged mesophyll.

C4 plants are not confined to grasses: they also occur in Cyperaceae, Amaranthaceae (including the saltworts *Salsola* and orache *Atriplex* formerly placed in Chenopodiaceae), some *Euphorbia* species, and *Flaveria* in Asteraceae. The C4 pathway arose independently at least 60 times in angiosperms and is a classic example of **convergent evolution**; soybean, tobacco, rice and wheat are all C3 plants. Not all C4 plants have Kranz anatomy: in the Amaranthaceae *Bienertia* and *Suaeda aralocaspica*, the two steps of the C4 pathway take place at the two ends of a single mesophyll cell (Voznesenskaya et al., 2001), without Kranz anatomy. Plants with **crassulacean acid metabolism** (CAM: stonecrop, cacti, pineapple, many epiphytic orchids) divide the labor not in space but in time (fixing carbon dioxide at night); their leaves or stems are succulent, the mesophyll cells have large vacuoles, and there is no Kranz anatomy. The biochemistry and physiology of these pathways belong to plant physiology ([7.1.25](/7.1.25)).

### 23.5.7. Pine Needles

The leaves of the gymnosperm genus *Pinus* are needles, commonly called pine needles, borne at the tip of short shoots, solitary or in fascicles of two to five (rarely up to eight) with a sheath at the base formed by bud scales; a needle fascicle is in fact a short shoot of limited growth ([7.12.26](/7.12.26)). The number of needles per fascicle differs, and so does the shape of the cross section: Masson pine (*Pinus massoniana*), Chinese pine (*P. tabuliformis*) and Taiwan pine (*P. taiwanensis*) have two needles per fascicle and semicircular cross sections; Yunnan pine (*P. yunnanensis*, three needles) and Armand pine (*P. armandii*, five needles) have triangular (sector-shaped) cross sections. The small surface area of the needle reduces the transpiring surface, and needles often have a xeromorphic form. Taking Masson pine as an example, the structure of the needle from outside to inside is as follows (Fig. 5).

- Epidermis: the cell walls are very thick and lignified, the cell lumina very small, and there is a well-developed cuticle outside. The stomata are arranged in longitudinal rows on the epidermis, the guard cells sunken beneath the subsidiary cells, and the cavity above them is often plugged with wax ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)), so the needle surface bears white stomatal lines.
- Hypodermis: one to several layers of thick-walled, lignified fiber-like cells beneath the epidermis, thicker at the corners, giving support.
- Mesophyll: not differentiated into palisade and spongy tissue; it consists of thin-walled cells whose walls project inward and form many folds (plicate mesophyll), with chloroplasts arranged along the folds, which enlarges the photosynthetic surface. The mesophyll contains several resin canals whose epithelial cells secrete resin; a canal lying against the hypodermis is external (marginal), one within the mesophyll is medial, and one lying against the endodermis is internal. The number and position of resin canals are commonly used characters for separating pine species (Masson pine has about 4–8, external).
- Endodermis: inside the mesophyll lies a distinct layer of **endodermis**, whose cells are tightly packed and whose walls have Casparian strips; it separates the mesophyll from the central vascular tissue.
- Transfusion tissue: the group of cells inside the endodermis surrounding the vascular bundles, consisting of dead transfusion tracheids (with bordered pits in the walls) and living transfusion parenchyma cells; it transports water and nutrients between mesophyll and bundles and is characteristic of gymnosperm leaves.
- Vascular bundles: in the center of the needle, two in Masson pine, with xylem on the adaxial side and phloem on the abaxial side as in angiosperm leaves.

The number of vascular bundles divides *Pinus* into two subgenera: subgenus *Strobus* (soft pines, e.g., Armand pine, Korean pine, lacebark pine) has one bundle per needle and softer wood; subgenus *Pinus* (hard pines, e.g., Masson pine, Chinese pine, Scots pine) has two bundles per needle. The thick-walled epidermis and hypodermis, sunken stomata, folded mesophyll, resin canals and distinct endodermis make pine needles adapted to cold and dry environments; leaves of other gymnosperm groups are treated in [7.12.26](/7.12.26).

## 23.6. Ecological Types of Leaves

The leaf is the organ in direct contact with the environment and the one that changes most readily with it, so adaptation of plants to water and light shows first in leaf form and structure. By relation to water, plants are divided into terrestrial plants and **hydrophytes**, and terrestrial plants into **xerophytes**, mesophytes and hygrophytes; the structure of the angiosperm leaf described above refers in general to the leaf of a mesophyte. By relation to light, plants are divided into sun plants, shade plants and shade-tolerant plants, and even on a single plant sun leaves facing the light differ in structure from shade leaves facing away from it (as with the leaves on the south side of a sugar maple crown and those inside it). The main features of their leaves are compared below.

| | Xerophyte | Submerged leaf | Sun leaf | Shade leaf |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blade | small and thick, or densely hairy; succulents thick and juicy | small and thin, often finely divided into threads or ribbons | smaller and thicker | larger and thinner |
| Epidermis | thick walls, well-developed cuticle, often a multiple epidermis | thin walls, not or only slightly cutinized, with chloroplasts, serving for both absorption and assimilation | small cells, thick walls, thicker cuticle, sometimes hairy | thin cuticle, epidermal cells sometimes with chloroplasts |
| Stomata | sunken, or restricted to local regions such as stomatal crypts | absent | small and dense | fewer |
| Mesophyll | palisade of several layers, spongy tissue and intercellular spaces poorly developed; succulents with well-developed water-storage parenchyma | poorly developed, no differentiation into palisade and spongy tissue, intercellular spaces well developed, forming aerenchyma | palisade well developed, often two or three layers, sometimes on both sides; spongy tissue and intercellular spaces poorly developed | palisade poorly developed, intercellular spaces well developed; chloroplasts large and chlorophyll content high |
| Veins and mechanical tissue | veins dense, much mechanical tissue | vascular and mechanical tissue greatly reduced (no vessels) | veins fine and dense, mechanical tissue well developed | veins sparser |
| Significance | reduce transpiring area and rate of transpiration, store water | obtain gases and light in water | adapt to strong light and strong transpiration | absorb light fully in weak light |
| Examples | oleander, cycad; purslane, stonecrop, aloe, agave | curly pondweed (*Potamogeton crispus*), hydrilla, hornwort (*Ceratophyllum*) | field crops, grassland and desert plants | forest understory plants, ferns, ginseng |

Xerophytes are of two types: one has small, thick, hard leaves with a well-developed epidermis and sunken stomata, chiefly reducing water loss (sclerophylls, as oleander with stomata in hairy stomatal crypts); the other has thick, juicy leaves or stems storing a great deal of water (succulents), and in cacti the leaves are reduced and the stem is thick and juicy. Among hydrophytes, floating leaves (water lily) have stomata only on the upper epidermis and well-developed aerenchyma in the mesophyll; aerial leaves emerging above water resemble those of mesophytes. Sun plants tend toward xeromorphic form, but this does not make them xerophytes: rice is both a hydrophyte and a sun plant. These adaptations are discussed in detail in [7.12.32](/7.12.32); the ecology of communities and habitats is left to the ecology course.

## 23.7. Leaf Fall and the Abscission Layer

Leaves have a limited life span. The leaves of ordinary plants live only a few months; leaves of evergreens live longer: privet leaves live 1–3 years, pine needles 3–5, podocarp (*Podocarpus*) 2–8, fir 3–10 and yew 6–10, and the needles of the bristlecone pine of the western North American mountains (*Pinus longaeva*) can remain on the branch for thirty or forty years. In annual plants the leaves die with the plant; in herbs such as rice, faba bean and pea the leaves often remain on the plant after death. In most trees dead leaves fall soon afterward, which is leaf fall. By the manner of leaf fall, trees are of two kinds: those whose leaves all die and fall together when the cold or dry season arrives, leaving bare branches, are deciduous trees (plane tree, oak, peach, willow, dawn redwood); those in which old leaves fall only gradually after new leaves have appeared, so that the tree stays green all year, are evergreen trees (tea, boxwood, camphor tree, southern magnolia, loquat, pine). Evergreen trees thus also shed leaves, but not all within one period.

Leaf fall has internal and external causes. After a leaf has been physiologically active for some time, a large amount of metabolic products (especially some minerals) accumulates in its cells, its function gradually declines and it finally dies; this is the internal cause. Deciduous trees always shed their leaves in the unfavorable season, which is the external cause: in the temperate winter the soil is cold, so that roots take up water with difficulty while transpiration is not reduced, and in the tropical dry season water is lacking, so that leaf fall greatly reduces the transpiring area. Leaf fall in late autumn or the dry season is an adaptation by which plants avoid excessive water loss and, through long natural selection, has become a fixed developmental rhythm of deciduous trees. Before leaf fall, the chlorophyll in the leaf is broken down first, the carotenoids previously masked show their yellow, and in some plants anthocyanins are newly synthesized, giving red color (autumn foliage); at the same time, reusable elements such as nitrogen, phosphorus and magnesium, as well as amino acids and sugars, are recovered into the stem for storage.

Why do leaves fall, and why is the leaf scar left after they fall so smooth? Because before leaf fall, structural and biochemical changes occur in a region at or near the base of the petiole.

**Definition 23.7.1 (Abscission Zone, Abscission Layer and Protective Layer)** The region formed at the base of the petiole before leaf fall that is directly concerned with the shedding of the leaf is the **abscission zone**. It has two parts: on the outer side (the side toward the leaf), several cell layers whose walls become weakened and at which the leaf separates are the **abscission layer** (also called the separation layer); on the inner side (the side toward the stem), several cell layers in whose walls and intercellular spaces protective substances such as suberin and wound gum are deposited form the protective layer.

When a leaf is about to fall, the parenchyma cells in one region of the abscission zone often divide first, producing a group of small cells; later the middle lamellae of these cells (sometimes together with the primary walls) are broken down by enzymes and the cells separate from one another, so that this layer becomes very weak mechanically, and under gravity and wind the leaf breaks off at the abscission layer, finally held only by a few vascular bundles, which are then torn through. The protective layer beneath the abscission layer protects the wound from water loss and from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria after the leaf has fallen; before leaf fall, tyloses also often form in the vessels and seal the water-conducting pathway. In woody plants, the protective layer is later replaced by **periderm** developing beneath it and continuous with the periderm of the rest of the stem; the leaf scar left on the stem after leaf fall is the surface of the protective layer or periderm, and the small dots within the scar (bundle scars) are the broken vascular bundles. The formation of the abscission zone and the breakdown of cell walls are controlled by plant hormones: ethylene is generally thought to promote abscission, auxin produced by the blade inhibits it, and abscisic acid acts indirectly; the mechanisms belong to plant physiology. In production, defoliants (such as mixtures of thidiazuron and ethephon) make cotton drop its leaves together before harvest, facilitating mechanical picking.

## Summary

Key points:

- The leaf (Definition 23.1.1) is a lateral organ of the shoot tip, with limited growth, dorsiventrality and a bud in its axil; its functions are photosynthesis and transpiration (driving force for water uptake, transport of mineral salts, cooling), together with absorption, storage and reproduction.
- A complete leaf = blade + petiole + stipules (Definition 23.2.1); eight forms of stipule; the pulvinus and leaf movements; modes of attachment (petiolate, sessile, amplexicaul, perfoliate, decurrent, peltate, sheathing).
- The grass leaf = blade + sheath, with ligule, auricles and collar at the junction (Definition 23.2.2); rice has ligule and auricles, barnyard grass has neither; the grass ligule is not homologous with the lycophyte ligule (Definition 16.5.2).
- Leaf shape is determined by the length-to-width ratio and the position of the widest part ("narrowly", "broadly", "ob-"); apex, base, margin and lobing (pinnate and palmate × lobed, parted, divided) are described independently; divided leaves differ from compound leaves in petiolules and continuity of veins.
- Veins and venation (Definition 23.3.1): reticulate (pinnate, palmate, trinerved, radiating), parallel (straight, arcuate, radiating, pinnate-parallel), dichotomous (ginkgo, ferns); the distinction between monocots and dicots is only approximate.
- Simple and compound leaves (Definition 23.3.2): pinnate (odd, even; once to multiply), palmate, pedate, trifoliolate (pinnate, palmate), unifoliolate; four points distinguishing a compound leaf from a twig.
- Phyllotaxis: alternate, opposite (decussate), whorled, fascicled, basal; phyllotactic fractions 1/2, 1/3, 2/5, 3/8, ..., divergence = $360^\circ$ × fraction; leaf mosaic (Definition 23.3.3); heterophylly (Definition 23.3.4): developmental (blue gum, juniper) and ecological (arrowhead, water crowfoot).
- Development: leaf primordium → apical growth (brief) → marginal growth (marginal meristem, forming blade, lobes, leaflets) → intercalary growth (blade expansion); leaf growth is determinate with basipetal maturation; grasses keep an intercalary meristem at the base. Number of mesophyll layers is fixed early and cells divide only anticlinally; the midrib differentiates acropetally and connects with the leaf trace. Dorsiventrality: HD-ZIP III (adaxial) versus KANADI and YABBY (abaxial); re-expression of KNOX and compound leaves.
- Dorsiventral and isobilateral leaves (Definition 23.5.1), unifacial leaves (iris, onion); mesophyll, palisade and spongy tissue (Definition 23.5.2); bundle sheath and its extensions (Definition 23.5.3); the finer the vein, the simpler its structure, vein endings having only tracheids and sieve-tube elements.
- Grass leaf: long and short cells (silica cells, cork cells), dumbbell-shaped guard cells, bulliform cells (Definition 23.5.4), undifferentiated mesophyll, collateral bundles with limited growth.
- Kranz anatomy (Definition 23.5.5) = chloroplast-rich bundle sheath + radially arranged mesophyll; dense veins; the C4 pathway arose independently many times; single-cell C4 and CAM plants have no Kranz anatomy.
- Pine needle: thick-walled epidermis, hypodermis, sunken stomata, plicate mesophyll, resin canals, endodermis, transfusion tissue, one or two vascular bundles (subgenus *Strobus* and subgenus *Pinus*).
- Abscission zone = abscission layer + protective layer (Definition 23.7.1); nutrients are recovered before leaf fall; the protective layer is replaced by periderm, leaving a leaf scar with bundle scars.

## Questions

1. A branch of black locust has many leaflets arranged on one "axis". How can we tell whether this axis is the rachis of a compound leaf or a twig bearing many simple leaves?

   **Answer** Observe four points. The rachis has no terminal bud, whereas a twig often does. The leaflets have no axillary buds in their axils, buds occurring only in the axil of the common petiole, whereas every leaf on a twig has an axillary bud. The leaflets lie in one plane with the rachis, whereas leaves on a twig point in different directions. At leaf fall the leaflets drop first and the rachis then falls as a whole, whereas a twig stays on the tree. In black locust this axis has no bud at its tip, the axillary bud occurs only at the base of the common petiole (hidden beneath the leaf scar, a subpetiolar bud), and in autumn the leaflets and the rachis fall in succession, so it is the rachis of an odd-pinnate compound leaf; the pair of spines at its base are stipular spines.

2. Why do most dicotyledons have dorsiventral leaves, whereas grass leaves and the leaves on adult branches of blue gum are isobilateral? How is dorsiventrality determined in development?

   **Answer** The blade of most dicotyledons extends nearly horizontally, the upper side receiving light directly and the lower side little, so the mesophyll is differentiated into palisade tissue above, chloroplast-rich and compact, and spongy tissue below, with large intercellular spaces favoring gas exchange. Grass leaves and the drooping leaves of adult blue gum branches stand nearly erect and are lit about equally on both sides, so the mesophyll is undifferentiated (grasses) or has palisade tissue on both sides (blue gum). But dorsiventrality is not simply a direct result of light: from the moment the leaf primordium arises it has adaxial and abaxial sides, the adaxial expressing HD-ZIP III genes and the abaxial expressing KANADI and YABBY genes, which antagonize each other, and the blade expands along the boundary between the two fates; disrupting this partition (as in the *phantastica* mutant of snapdragon) produces nearly radially symmetric needle-like leaves. Isobilateral and unifacial leaves are variations on this basic program.

3. Looking at cross sections of maize and wheat leaves, how can we tell structurally which is the C4 plant? How is this structure related to the mode of photosynthesis? If a plant lacks Kranz anatomy, can we conclude that it is a C3 plant?

   **Answer** In maize the veins are dense, with only two to four mesophyll cells between adjacent veins; each bundle is surrounded by a layer of large, neatly arranged bundle-sheath cells with many large chloroplasts, and closely against it lies a ring of radially arranged mesophyll cells, forming Kranz anatomy, so maize is a C4 plant. In wheat the veins are sparser, the bundle sheath has two layers, the outer parenchyma cells have few chloroplasts and the inner thick-walled cells almost none, and the mesophyll cells are not arranged around the veins, so wheat is a C3 plant. In C4 plants, the mesophyll cells first fix $\ce{HCO3-}$ into a four-carbon acid, which enters the bundle-sheath cells through plasmodesmata and is decarboxylated; Rubisco works only in the sheath cells, where the $\ce{CO2}$ concentration around it is high. Kranz anatomy places every mesophyll cell against a sheath cell, and the thick-walled sheath reduces leakage of $\ce{CO2}$. The absence of Kranz anatomy does not prove a plant is C3: *Bienertia* and *Suaeda aralocaspica* of the Amaranthaceae carry out the C4 pathway within a single cell, and CAM plants separate the two steps in time; none of them has Kranz anatomy. The photosynthetic type is finally decided by biochemistry (first product, enzyme localization) or by the carbon isotope ratio.

4. Which structures of the pine needle are adaptations to cold and drought? How does it differ from the dorsiventral leaf of a dicotyledon in protection, assimilation and conduction?

   **Answer** Protection: the needle has a small surface area, the epidermal cell walls are very thick and lignified, the cuticle is well developed, a thick-walled hypodermis lies beneath the epidermis, and the stomata are sunken beneath the subsidiary cells with their cavity plugged by wax; all of this reduces water loss and also makes the needle frost-tolerant, whereas the epidermis of a dicot leaf is a single layer of thin-walled living cells and its stomata are not sunken. Assimilation: the mesophyll of the needle is not differentiated into palisade and spongy tissue, the mesophyll cell walls are folded inward and the chloroplasts lie along the folds, enlarging the photosynthetic surface within a small volume; the resin canals in the mesophyll defend against insects and fungi through their resin. Conduction: the central vascular tissue of the needle is surrounded by a distinct endodermis, and transfusion tissue lies between the bundles and the endodermis, so that water and nutrients are transported between bundles and mesophyll through it; in a dicot leaf the veins branch into a network with fine veins penetrating the mesophyll, and there is neither endodermis nor transfusion tissue.

5. Why is the leaf scar smooth after the Chinese parasol tree sheds its leaves in autumn? What is the significance of leaf fall for the tree?

   **Answer** Before leaf fall an abscission zone forms at the base of the petiole: in the outer abscission layer the middle lamellae are broken down by enzymes and the cells separate from one another, so the leaf falls cleanly along the layer and the surface is smooth; in the inner protective layer suberin and wound gum are deposited in cell walls and intercellular spaces and tyloses form in the vessels, sealing the wound, and the layer is later replaced by periderm, leaving several bundle scars on the leaf scar. The significance of leaf fall: in winter the soil is cold and roots take up water with difficulty while transpiration is not reduced, so leaf fall greatly reduces the transpiring area and keeps the plant from losing water; before leaf fall, nutrient elements such as nitrogen, phosphorus and magnesium, together with sugars and amino acids, are recovered into the stem for storage and use the next year; some metabolic products that accumulated in the aging leaf are removed with the leaf; and leaf fall also reduces the load of winter snow and wind on the crown.
