From this chapter on, the course turns from spore-bearing plants to the vegetative organs of seed plants. The seed is the reproductive structure unique to **seed plants**. After fertilization the **ovule** develops into a seed that already contains a small, morphologically differentiated plant, the embryo, together with stored food and a protective seed coat. The seed matures on the parent plant, is shed, remains dormant, and germinates under suitable conditions into a seedling, which grows into a plant with roots, stems and leaves. The seed is itself a reproductive organ, but its structure already prefigures the positions and relations of the three vegetative organs, and its germination is the starting point of individual development. We therefore treat seeds and seedlings first, and then the root ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)), the stem ([7.12.21](/7.12.21), [7.12.22](/7.12.22)) and the leaf ([7.12.23](/7.12.23)). How the seed forms in the ovule, that is, the development of the embryo and endosperm and the differentiation of the seed coat, belongs to the reproductive part ([7.12.30](/7.12.30)); the origin of the seed and the seeds of gymnosperms are covered in [7.12.25](/7.12.25). The physiology of dormancy and germination (the roles of hormones and photoreceptors, the mobilization of reserves) belongs to plant physiology; this chapter deals with morphology and structure, and with the external conditions directly related to form.

## 19.1. The Body of a Seed Plant

### 19.1.1. Organs

The mature body of a seed plant consists of several parts that are easy to distinguish in external form and have specific functions.

**Definition 19.1.1 (Organ, Vegetative Organ and Reproductive Organ)** A part of the plant body that is composed of several tissues, has a distinctive external form and a specific function, and is easily distinguished from other parts is called an **organ**. The root, stem and leaf, which carry out vegetative growth (absorption, synthesis, transport and storage), are called **vegetative organs**; the flower, fruit and seed, which are concerned with reproduction, are called **reproductive organs**.

During vegetative growth most seed plants have only the three organs root, stem and leaf. The root absorbs water and minerals underground and anchors the plant; the leaf carries out photosynthesis and transpiration in the air; the stem connects the two and holds the leaves out in space. A stem bearing leaves and buds is called a shoot, and all the shoots above ground make up the shoot system, whereas the roots below ground make up the **root system** ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)). At a certain stage the stem apex changes into a flower or inflorescence and forms reproductive organs; after pollination and fertilization, the ovary develops into a **fruit** and the ovules develop into seeds.

The division into organs reflects the division of labor that accompanied adaptation to life on land. The gametophyte of **bryophytes** has only "stem-like and leaf-like" parts and no true roots; only the sporophyte of **vascular plants** has roots, stems and leaves traversed by vascular tissue ([7.12.16](/7.12.16)). Seed plants add the flower (in angiosperms only), the fruit and the seed, which free sexual reproduction from dependence on water and allow the next generation to survive unfavorable seasons as seeds and to disperse over long distances.

### 19.1.2. Development Begins with the Seed

The life cycle of a seed plant can be viewed as a loop: the seed germinates into a seedling, the seedling grows into a plant with a root system and shoot system (vegetative growth), the plant flowers and sets fruit (reproductive growth), and new seeds are produced. When a seed leaves the parent, the new generation is already formed within it and has undergone a preliminary morphological differentiation: the embryo has a radicle, which will become the root, a plumule, which will become the stem and leaves, and the first leaves, the cotyledons. Organ formation in plant development can therefore be traced back to the embryo: the apical meristem of the radicle produces the primary root, the apical meristem of the plumule produces the stem and leaves, and the lateral organs on the roots and stems (lateral roots, leaves, axillary buds) are in turn produced step by step by their own meristems.

Unlike that of an animal, the plant embryo in the seed is only a rudimentary body, and most organs are produced continuously by apical meristems after germination. This is the open nature of postembryonic development in plants: the tens of thousands of leaves and thousands of branches of a large tree are all produced by **apical meristems** ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)) that remain active after germination.

## 19.2. Seed Structure

Seeds differ greatly in size, shape and color. Large ones, such as the coconut, may be 15–20 cm in diameter; small ones, such as tobacco seeds, resemble fine sand; orchid seeds are smaller still, dust-like, weighing only a few micrograms each. Seeds are kidney-shaped (soybean, common bean), spherical (rapeseed, pea), flattened (broad bean), ellipsoid (peanut) and so on, and their color may be uniform or patterned (castor bean). The external features of seeds have important diagnostic value in plant classification, seed testing and quarantine.

Despite these differences in external form, the basic structure of seeds is uniform. Seeds occur only in **gymnosperms** and **angiosperms**.

**Definition 19.2.1 (Seed)** A reproductive structure that develops from the ovule after fertilization and contains an embryo, a storage tissue and a seed coat is called a **seed**. The seed is the unit of reproduction and dispersal of seed plants (gymnosperms and angiosperms).

A seed generally consists of three parts, the embryo, the endosperm and the seed coat, and in a few taxa also a perisperm (see the figure of seed structure in Section 19.2.4).

### 19.2.1. The Embryo

**Definition 19.2.2 (Embryo)** The young sporophyte in the seed that develops from the fertilized egg (zygote) is called the **embryo**. The angiosperm embryo consists of four parts: plumule, embryonic axis, cotyledons and radicle; the plumule, embryonic axis and radicle together form the axis of the embryo.

**Definition 19.2.3 (Plumule, Radicle, Embryonic Axis and Cotyledon)** The upper end of the embryonic axis, which will develop into the stem and leaves, is called the **plumule**; it consists of the shoot apical meristem (growing point) and the young leaves around it. The lower end of the axis, which will develop into the primary root, is called the **radicle**; it consists of the root apical meristem and the root cap and is generally conical. The axis between plumule and radicle is the **embryonic axis**: the part between the point of attachment of the cotyledons and the first foliage leaf is the **epicotyl**, and the part between the point of attachment of the cotyledons and the radicle is the **hypocotyl**. The earliest leaves of the plant, borne on the embryonic axis, are called **cotyledons**.

Chinese textbooks often call the hypocotyl simply the "embryonic axis". The tips of the radicle and plumule consist of embryonic cells, which are small, thin-walled, densely cytoplasmic, with a relatively large nucleus and no or only small vacuoles. At germination these cells divide and enlarge rapidly, so that the radicle and plumule elongate, break through the seed coat, and grow into the primary root and the leafy shoot. The embryonic axis is generally very short and grows along with the germinating seedling, becoming part of the young root or stem; in the seedling the hypocotyl is the transition region between root and stem ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)).

The number of cotyledons gives the two large groups of angiosperms their traditional names. Plants with two cotyledons are called dicotyledons, such as legumes, cucurbits, cotton and rape; those with a single cotyledon are **monocots**, such as rice, wheat, maize and onion. In the APG system the "dicots" are not a **clade**: the monocots arose from within them, the great majority of dicots belong to the **eudicots**, and the remainder are the early-diverging lineages, the **ANA grade** and the **magnoliids** ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)). Two cotyledons remain a **plesiomorphy** of angiosperms, so "dicot" continues to be used in morphology as a descriptive name. The number of cotyledons in gymnosperm seeds varies: two in Chinese juniper (*Juniperus chinensis*) and ginkgo (*Ginkgo biloba*), several to a dozen or more in pine, spruce and fir. Abnormal cotyledon numbers (one more, one fewer, or two fused cotyledons) are occasionally seen in many plants.

The physiological role of the cotyledons differs among species:

- Storage of nutrients: the cotyledons of soybean, peanut and broad bean are thick and fleshy and hold large reserves used in germination and seedling growth.
- Photosynthesis: the cotyledons of cotton and rape emerge above ground after germination, expand and turn green, and photosynthesize briefly before the foliage leaves develop; the thick cotyledons of soybean also turn green when they emerge into the light.
- Digestion and absorption of the endosperm: the cotyledons of wheat, rice and castor bean are thin and sheet-like; at germination they secrete enzymes that digest the nutrients of the endosperm and then absorb them and transport them to the growing regions of the embryo.

### 19.2.2. Endosperm and Perisperm

**Definition 19.2.4 (Endosperm)** The tissue in the seed in which nutrients are stored for use in embryo development and germination is called the **endosperm**. In angiosperms the endosperm develops from the fertilized polar nuclei (primary endosperm nucleus) and is usually triploid; in gymnosperms the endosperm is the haploid female gametophyte, formed before fertilization.

The angiosperm endosperm originates from **double fertilization** ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)): one sperm fuses with the egg cell to form the zygote, and the other sperm fuses with the two polar nuclei of the central cell of the embryo sac to form the primary endosperm nucleus. Thus the endosperm and the embryo arise from the same fertilization event but follow different developmental courses: the former is a nutritive tissue that serves only the embryo and is eventually consumed. Gymnosperms have no double fertilization; their "endosperm" is female gametophyte tissue that develops within the megaspore wall and is filled with nutrients before fertilization ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)). The two kinds of "endosperm" bear the same name but differ in origin and in ploidy (see the table at the end of Section 19.2.5).

The endosperm is generally fleshy and occupies a certain volume of the seed. The amount present in the mature seed varies widely. In castor bean and rice the endosperm is thick and makes up most of the seed; in legumes such as *Sesbania* and honey locust (*Gleditsia*) it is only a thin layer around the embryo; in soybean, broad bean and peanut the endosperm is absorbed by the embryo during seed development, the nutrients are stored in the cotyledons, and the mature seed has no endosperm or only a dry thin remnant. The seeds of Orchidaceae, Podostemaceae and *Trapa* (placed in Lythraceae in APG IV) form no endosperm at all: in orchids the primary endosperm nucleus does not divide or degenerates very early, and the seed contains only a small undifferentiated embryo.

The nutrients stored in the endosperm and cotyledons are mainly carbohydrates, lipids and proteins, with small amounts of minerals and vitamins:

- Carbohydrates: **starch** is the most common, reaching about 70% of dry weight in wheat and rice seeds and less in legumes. Soluble sugars are mostly sucrose, which gives the sweetness of mature maize and chestnut seeds. A few plants store hemicellulose; their endosperm cell walls are very thick and consist of hemicelluloses (mannans, galactomannans) that are hydrolyzed at germination and absorbed by the embryo, as in date palm, *Allium*, coffee, *Asparagus*, persimmon, and the legume guar (*Cyamopsis tetragonoloba*, the source of guar gum).
- Lipids: stored in the endosperm (castor bean) or in the cotyledons (peanut, rape) in the form of oil bodies ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)).
- Proteins: soybean cotyledons are rich in protein; the outermost layer of the grass endosperm, the aleurone layer, is rich in protein and lipids stored as **aleurone grains** ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)).

The main nutrient contents of several common crop seeds (percentage of dry weight, order of magnitude only):

| Plant | Carbohydrate | Protein | Lipid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maize *Zea mays* | 75 | 12 | 9 |
| Wheat *Triticum aestivum* | 75 | 12 | 2 |
| Barley *Hordeum vulgare* | 76 | 12 | 3 |
| Pea *Pisum sativum* | 56 | 24 | 6 |
| Soybean *Glycine max* | 26 | 37 | 17 |
| Peanut *Arachis hypogaea* | 12 | 31 | 48 |
| Rape *Brassica napus* | 19 | 21 | 48 |

In cereals and pea the carbohydrate column is mainly starch; in soybean, rapeseed and other oilseeds it consists mostly of soluble sugars and cell-wall polysaccharides, with little starch. Cereal seeds are mainly starch, legume seeds mainly protein, and oilseed crops mainly lipid; this difference also determines the amount of water taken up in germination (Section 19.5.1).

**Definition 19.2.5 (Perisperm)** The part of the nucellus that is not completely absorbed by the embryo sac as the ovule develops into a seed, and that remains as a storage tissue, is called the **perisperm**. The perisperm arises from the maternal nucellus and is diploid.

A seed with perisperm may have little or no endosperm and use the perisperm as its main storage tissue, as in Amaranthaceae (beet, spinach, *Amaranthus*) and Caryophyllaceae (*Dianthus*), whose curved embryo surrounds a central starchy perisperm. Endosperm and perisperm may also occur together, as in black pepper, ginger and the water lily relative *Euryale ferox*. In addition, a few plants store nutrients mainly in the hypocotyl, such as the aquatic pondweed (*Potamogeton*) and arrowhead (*Sagittaria*).

### 19.2.3. The Seed Coat

**Definition 19.2.6 (Seed Coat)** The protective layer that develops from the integuments of the ovule and covers the outside of the seed is called the **seed coat** (testa). In an ovule with two integuments, the outer integument develops into the outer seed coat (testa) and the inner integument into the inner seed coat (tegmen); the two layers often cannot be sharply distinguished, or one of them disappears during development.

The seed coat comes from the **integuments** and often consists of several cell layers. It protects the seed from mechanical injury and from pests and pathogens, and regulates the passage of water and gases. Its character and thickness vary among plants and are related to the division of labor with the pericarp:

- Seeds that remain enclosed in a tough pericarp after maturity and are protected by it have a weak, membranous or papery seed coat, as in peach and peanut.
- Seeds exposed after the fruit dehisces generally have a thick, hard seed coat with well-developed mechanical tissue; it may be leathery (broad bean, soybean) or hard-shelled (tea).
- In the grains of grasses such as wheat and rice, the seed coat is tightly fused with the surrounding pericarp to form a common protective layer that is hard to distinguish; such a fruit is called a **caryopsis** ([7.12.31](/7.12.31)).

The cells of the epidermis of a thick seed coat often have lignified or cutinized walls. The seed coat of legumes is the most typical. The outer integument is differentiated into three layers. The outermost is a layer of long, columnar, thick-walled cells with parallel long axes and tight packing, called the palisade layer (Malpighian cells); a "light line" of different refractivity in their walls is the key region that makes the seed coat impermeable to water. Below it is a layer of I-shaped osteosclereids with distinct lumina, touching each other only at their ends. The innermost layer is several layers of thin-walled cells that store nutrients early in seed development and are later crushed (for these two kinds of **sclereids** see [7.12.4](/7.12.4)). The epidermal cells of the cotton seed coat extend outward into long hairs, which are cotton fibers; they are epidermal hairs of the seed coat, not fibrous tissue. The epidermal cells of the seed coat generally contain pigments, which give seeds their various colors.

The seed coat of a mature seed often shows marks left from the development of the ovule into the seed (see the figure of seed structure in Section 19.2.4):

**Definition 19.2.7 (Hilum, Micropyle, Raphe and Caruncle)** The scar left on the seed coat when the seed separates from its stalk (funiculus) is called the **hilum**. The small opening at one end of the hilum, left by the micropyle of the ovule, is the **micropyle**. In a seed formed from an anatropous ovule, the slightly raised longitudinal ridge along the ventral side of the seed from the hilum to the chalaza is the **raphe**, the region where the funiculus is fused with the outer integument and through which a vascular bundle passes. A spongy outgrowth of the outer seed coat near the micropyle is the **caruncle**.

On the broader end of a broad bean seed there is a black, eyebrow-shaped mark, the hilum; at one end of the hilum there is an inconspicuous small opening, the micropyle, from which a drop of water emerges when the soaked seed is gently squeezed at the hilum. At one end of a castor bean seed there is a spongy caruncle that covers the hilum and micropyle, which can be seen only after the caruncle is removed; along the middle of the ventral side runs a raphe almost as long as the seed. Only seeds formed from anatropous ovules have a raphe: in an anatropous ovule the funiculus is closely fused with part of the outer integument and the vascular bundle enters the ovule through the funiculus, so when the integuments develop into the seed coat the fused part leaves the raphe as a trace on the coat, and the remaining vascular bundle runs within it (for ovule types see [7.12.28](/7.12.28)). The chalaza, at the end of the raphe opposite the hilum, is where the integuments join the nucellus. The caruncle differs from the **aril**: the caruncle is only a small projection of the outer seed coat near the micropyle, whereas the aril develops from the funiculus or placenta and often envelops the whole seed (the "flesh" of longan and litchi, [7.12.30](/7.12.30)). The caruncle of castor bean contains lipids that attract ants to carry the seed away; such an appendage is called an *elaiosome* and is an adaptation for dispersal by ants ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)).

### 19.2.4. Structure of Dicot Seeds

Three common seeds illustrate the structure (see the figure below).

- Common bean and broad bean (without endosperm): the seed coat is tough; beneath it are two thick, flat, closely apposed white fleshy cotyledons that fill the entire seed. At the hilum end, where the cotyledons meet, a small conical structure connected to both cotyledons is the radicle. When the cotyledons are separated, another structure attached to the radicle and lying between the cotyledons, looking like several young leaves, is the plumule; the plumule of common bean has two already formed foliage leaves. The radicle and plumule are connected by a stout short embryonic axis, and the two cotyledons are borne directly on the axis. The seed coat of broad bean is green and hard when dry, and becomes soft and leathery when soaked; the hilum is a black eyebrow-shaped streak, and the raphe is short and inconspicuous.
- Castor bean (with endosperm): the seed is oval and slightly flattened, with a smooth hard coat bearing mottled markings; there is a caruncle at one end and a raphe in the middle of the ventral side. Beneath the seed coat is white endosperm rich in oil (castor oil). The embryo is flat and lies in the center of the endosperm: the two cotyledons are large and thin with conspicuous veins, and connect at the base with a short embryonic axis, with the radicle below the axis and the plumule between the cotyledons above. Median longitudinal sections perpendicular and parallel to the plane of the cotyledons show the thickness of the embryo and the outline of the cotyledons, respectively.

### 19.2.5. Structure of Monocot Seeds

- Wheat and maize (with endosperm): the outer protective layer of the grain is not simply a seed coat but a composite layer in which pericarp and seed coat are fused, so the grain is in fact a fruit (a caryopsis). In longitudinal section the endosperm occupies most of the grain, and the embryo lies on one side of the base and occupies only a small part. The endosperm consists of two kinds of cells: the outermost one layer (wheat) or several layers (two to three in barley) form the aleurone layer, which lies against the seed coat and contains protein, fat and other substances of high nutritional value (it is milled away from polished rice and refined flour); the rest are starch-storing endosperm cells.
- Arrowhead (without endosperm): the seed is very small and enclosed in a flattened, triangular achene, each fruit containing a single seed; the seed coat is extremely thin, only one cell layer. The embryo is curved, with the tip of the radicle close to the cotyledon end; the single cotyledon is long and columnar, borne on the axis and enclosing the plumule at its base; the plumule has a growing point and an already formed primary leaf; the radicle and hypocotyl form a short axis that stores nutrients. The seeds of *Alisma* and other Alismatales are of this type.

The embryo of grasses is one of the most complex among angiosperms (see the figure below):

**Definition 19.2.8 (Scutellum, Coleoptile and Coleorhiza)** In the embryo of grasses, the shield-shaped cotyledon borne on one side of the axis and pressed against the endosperm is called the **scutellum**; the sheath-like structure that encloses the plumule is called the **coleoptile**; and the sheath-like structure that encloses the radicle (growing point and root cap) is called the **coleorhiza**.

In the wheat embryo the plumule lies above the axis and consists of a terminal growing point and several young leaves surrounded by the coleoptile; the radicle lies below the axis and consists of the growing point, the root cap and the outer coleorhiza. One side of the axis is joined to the scutellum, whose other side lies against the endosperm, so the scutellum is sandwiched between the endosperm and the axis; in the scutellum one can see the initial cells of what will develop into vascular bundles (**procambium**). The face of the scutellum next to the endosperm has a layer of regularly arranged cells, the epithelial (columnar) cells; at germination they secrete enzymes into the endosperm that break down its stored substances, and then absorb the products and transport them to the growing regions of the embryo (the aleurone layer also secretes hydrolytic enzymes, and its hormonal regulation is a classic experimental system of plant physiology). On the side of the axis opposite the scutellum there is also a small thin, membranous projection, the epiblast: it was formerly regarded as an incompletely developed second cotyledon, but is now generally considered to be an upward extension of the coleorhiza or a lobe on the axis, and its homology remains debated. The epiblast is present in wheat, rice, barley and others, but is generally absent in the Panicoideae, such as maize and sorghum.

The grass embryo also has a special internode: the portion between the node bearing the scutellum (the scutellar node) and the node bearing the coleoptile is called the mesocotyl. The mesocotyl is extremely short in wheat but long in maize and sorghum, where it elongates markedly when the seed is sown deep and pushes the tip of the coleoptile toward the soil surface (Section 19.6.2). The homologies of the parts of the grass embryo have long been debated in morphology: whether the scutellum is the whole cotyledon, whether the coleoptile is part of the cotyledon or the first leaf, and whether the mesocotyl belongs to the axis or to the cotyledon. A common view is that the scutellum and coleoptile together correspond to one cotyledon (the scutellum is its absorptive part and the coleoptile its sheath), and that the mesocotyl is the elongated axis between the scutellar node and the coleoptile node; different textbooks do not agree entirely, so the definitions used should be noted when reading the literature.

The basic structure of the seed can be summarized as follows:

| Part | Origin (angiosperms) | Ploidy | Composition and features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seed coat | Integuments | Maternal, $2n$ | Protective layer; fused with the pericarp in grasses |
| Plumule | Zygote | $2n$ | Growing point and young leaves; a coleoptile in grasses |
| Embryonic axis | Zygote | $2n$ | Connects plumule and radicle and bears the cotyledons; divided into epicotyl and hypocotyl |
| Radicle | Zygote | $2n$ | Growing point and root cap; a coleorhiza in grasses |
| Cotyledons | Zygote | $2n$ | One, two or several; called the scutellum in grasses |
| Endosperm | Primary endosperm nucleus (double fertilization) | Usually $3n$ | Storage tissue; absorbed early by the embryo in exalbuminous seeds |
| Perisperm | Nucellus | Maternal, $2n$ | Present in a few plants |
| Gymnosperm "endosperm" | Female gametophyte (megaspore) | $n$ | Formed before fertilization |

The ploidy "usually $3n$" for the endosperm refers to the most common Polygonum-type embryo sac (two polar nuclei plus one sperm); in other embryo sac types the endosperm may be $2n$ (*Oenothera*) or $5n$ (*Fritillaria*) and so on ([7.12.28](/7.12.28)). The table shows that an angiosperm seed contains tissues of three "generations": the maternal seed coat (and perisperm), the embryo of the next generation, and the endosperm produced by double fertilization.

## 19.3. Types of Seeds

**Definition 19.3.1 (Albuminous and Exalbuminous Seeds)** A seed that has endosperm at maturity is called an **albuminous seed**; a seed in which the endosperm has been absorbed by the embryo, the nutrients stored in the cotyledons, and which consists only of seed coat and embryo, is called an **exalbuminous seed**.

According to the number of cotyledons and the presence of endosperm, seeds fall into four types, plus one in which perisperm predominates:

| Type | Composition | Representative plants | Representative families (APG IV) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dicot, albuminous | Seed coat, embryo, endosperm | Castor bean, tobacco, tomato, mulberry, *Sesbania* | Magnoliaceae, Annonaceae, Ranunculaceae, Papaveraceae, Euphorbiaceae, Moraceae, Solanaceae, Apiaceae, Oleaceae, Ebenaceae |
| Dicot, exalbuminous | Seed coat, embryo (thick cotyledons) | Soybean, peanut, broad bean, cotton, rape, cucurbits | Fabaceae (most), Brassicaceae, Cucurbitaceae, Rosaceae (most), Asteraceae, Fagaceae, Juglandaceae |
| Monocot, albuminous | Seed coat (often fused with pericarp), embryo, endosperm | Rice, wheat, maize, sorghum, onion | Poaceae, Amaryllidaceae (*Allium*), Arecaceae, Liliaceae, Commelinaceae |
| Monocot, exalbuminous | Seed coat, embryo | Arrowhead, *Alisma*, pondweed, orchids (embryo undifferentiated) | Alismataceae, Potamogetonaceae, Hydrocharitaceae, Orchidaceae |
| With perisperm | Seed coat, embryo, perisperm (endosperm scant or present) | Beet, spinach, *Amaranthus*, *Dianthus*; black pepper, ginger, *Euryale* (both present) | Amaranthaceae, Caryophyllaceae; Piperaceae, Zingiberaceae, Nymphaeaceae |

The distinction between albuminous and exalbuminous seeds has some systematic significance: early-diverging angiosperms such as the magnoliids and Ranunculales mostly have seeds with abundant endosperm and a small embryo, whereas many later-diverging groups (Fabales, Rosales, Cucurbitales, Asterales) have a large embryo and little or no endosperm, so a "small embryo and abundant endosperm" is generally regarded as a plesiomorphy of angiosperm seeds. But this character also varies within many families: in Fabaceae the seeds of *Sesbania*, honey locust and guar have a hemicellulose endosperm; in Malvaceae, *Tilia* has endosperm whereas cotton has very little; in Crassulaceae the endosperm is scant or absent. "Albuminous dicot" therefore cannot be applied to families across the board.

## 19.4. Seed Dormancy and Longevity

### 19.4.1. Seed Dormancy

A seed that has been formed is mature, but even under suitable environmental conditions it often cannot germinate at once; it must pass through a period of relative quiescence.

**Definition 19.4.1 (Seed Dormancy and After-Ripening)** The state in which a viable seed does not germinate even though water, temperature and oxygen are all suitable is called **seed dormancy**. The phenomenon in which the embryo is already morphologically mature but can germinate only after a period under certain conditions (usually cold and moist) is called **after-ripening**.

Dormancy differs from the mere "quiescence" of seeds that do not germinate because of lack of water or low temperature: a quiescent seed germinates as soon as it receives water and a suitable temperature, whereas a dormant seed must first be released from dormancy. The length of the dormant period varies greatly. The seeds of ginkgo, buttercup, *Paris verticillata* and pine need weeks, months or even years; the seeds of rice, wheat, pea, sesame and many alpine plants germinate quickly under suitable conditions after maturing and are quiescent only when conditions are unfavorable. When seeds without a dormant period meet continuous rain while ripening, they often germinate on the plant (on the ear), which is called vivipary or preharvest sprouting and causes great losses, so such crops must be harvested promptly. The seeds of mangrove plants (*Kandelia*, *Bruguiera*) germinate before the fruit falls, growing hypocotyls tens of centimeters long, a normal vivipary that is an adaptation to the intertidal zone. Dormancy is a biologically advantageous trait: it prevents seeds from germinating in unsuitable seasons, protects seedlings from damage, and allows seeds to persist in the soil for many years, forming the soil seed bank.

The causes of dormancy are varied, and dormancy can be broken or shortened only by measures adapted to the cause:

1. Restriction by the seed coat: the seed coat is thick and hard, containing cutin, a cuticle or phenolic compounds, and is impermeable to water and barely permeable to oxygen, as in certain species of Fabaceae and Malvaceae and in cocklebur (such seeds are called hard seeds). The mature seed coat of *Amaranthus* does not impede water uptake but is so hard that the living embryo cannot break through it. Treatments include mechanically scarifying the seed coat, brief treatment with concentrated sulfuric acid followed by rinsing with clean water, or soaking in cold water for about 12 hours and then placing in boiling water for 30–60 seconds; for seeds with especially hard coats, freezing or the action of soil microorganisms that soften the coat gradually can be used.
2. Causes in the embryo: first, the embryo is immature, not yet fully developed when the seed or fruit leaves the parent, and must wait until it has grown, as in ginkgo, buttercup and *Corydalis*; second, the embryo is mature but requires after-ripening, and the seeds of many temperate plants must spend weeks or even months in humid, cold conditions (generally $0$–$6\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) before they germinate. In production, seeds are layered with moist sand (or soil, peat) and left outdoors or in cold storage over winter, generally for 2–3 months at $0$–$6\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, which is called stratification; gibberellin can also be applied. Embryo dormancy is regulated by the balance between abscisic acid (which causes dormancy) and gibberellin (which promotes germination), a mechanism that belongs to plant physiology.
3. Presence of inhibitors: organic acids, alkaloids, certain plant hormones, and organic compounds that release ammonia or cyanide on decomposition can prevent germination. Some are in the embryo, some in the seed coat, and some in the pulp or juice of the fruit: seeds of tomato, citrus or cucurbits cannot germinate inside the fruit and germinate only after leaving it, for this reason. Inhibitors may also be in the soil, mostly brought in by the decay of fallen leaves, as with some desert plants, which germinate only after a few spring rains have washed the inhibitors out of the soil, so that seeds germinate only when rainfall is sufficient to sustain the seedlings.

Current seed ecology divides seed dormancy into five classes according to the site of dormancy and the way it is released (Baskin & Baskin 2004), which correspond roughly to the three causes above:

| Dormancy class | Cause | Release | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physiological dormancy (PD) | Physiological state of the embryo (hormone balance); the most common | Cold or warm stratification, after-ripening, gibberellin | Apple, many temperate trees and herbs |
| Morphological dormancy (MD) | Underdeveloped embryo (small, poorly differentiated) | Waiting under moist conditions until the embryo has grown | Celery, many Apiaceae and Ranunculaceae |
| Morphophysiological dormancy (MPD) | Underdeveloped embryo plus physiological dormancy | Embryo growth during stratification | Ginkgo, ginseng, *Fritillaria* |
| Physical dormancy (PY) | Seed coat or pericarp impermeable to water (hard seed) | Scarification, acid, hot water, natural high temperatures and wetting and drying cycles | Fabaceae, Malvaceae, Convolvulaceae |
| Combinational dormancy (PY + PD) | Water-impermeable coat plus physiological dormancy of the embryo | First break the coat, then stratify | Some Fabaceae, Anacardiaceae |

### 19.4.2. Seed Longevity

**Definition 19.4.2 (Seed Longevity)** The longest period for which a seed retains viability (the ability to germinate) under given conditions is called **seed longevity**.

Seed longevity differs enormously among plants, from over a hundred years to only a few weeks. The seeds of most cultivated plants remain viable for only one or two years, and some for 5–10 years: seeds of onion, lettuce and carrot lose the ability to germinate after two or three years of storage, especially in humid regions; the seeds of cereal crops generally remain viable for 5–10 years or even longer; only seeds of a few plants live longer than 50 years. The seeds of rubber tree and willow live only a few weeks. An especially long-lived example is the sacred lotus: ancient lotus seeds unearthed from a peat layer at Puland, Liaoning, radiocarbon dated at about 1300 years, still germinated into seedlings (Shen-Miller et al. 1995), and a date palm seed about 2000 years old from the Masada site in Israel was also germinated successfully (Sallon et al. 2008). The pericarp of the lotus (the hard shell of the lotus "nut") is extremely dense and impermeable to water, and the metabolism of the seed almost completely stops in anoxic, cold peat, which explains its longevity.

Seed longevity depends first of all on the genetic characteristics of the seed. According to tolerance of desiccation, seeds fall into two classes:

- Orthodox seeds: they dry naturally at maturity and tolerate desiccation to a water content of about 5%; in dry, cold conditions their longevity increases as water content and temperature fall. The seeds of the great majority of crops and temperate plants are of this type.
- Recalcitrant seeds: they have a high water content at maturity and do not tolerate drying; they die when the water content falls below a critical value (often above 20%–30%), and most also do not tolerate low temperature, so they cannot be stored for long by drying and cooling. Examples are rubber tree, cacao, mango, litchi, longan, oaks (*Quercus*) and chestnut, mostly tropical plants or plants with large seeds. There is also an intermediate class between the two (coffee, papaya).

Storage conditions have a marked effect on the longevity of orthodox seeds. The best conditions for storage are dry and cold, when respiration is weakest and the consumption of nutrients least; at high humidity and temperature the stored organic nutrients are consumed in quantity by respiration and longevity is shortened. But complete dryness is not good for storage either: during storage there is a safe range of water content (about 10% for rape, for example), and values above or below it are unsuitable. A store must be kept dry and ventilated so that the heat produced by seed respiration is dissipated promptly; seeds can also be sealed in plastic film filled with nitrogen to prevent increased respiration and deterioration. The seeds of aquatic plants, by contrast, lose viability when dry, but kept in water, especially at low temperature, they overwinter well and retain viability for a longer time. Longevity also depends on the health of the parent plant, the maturity of the seed, the integrity of the seed coat, and pests and diseases. The main causes of loss of viability are the destruction of enzymes, the consumption of stored nutrients, and the deterioration of embryo cells (membranes, DNA, proteins).

The decline of seed viability with storage time can be described quantitatively. In a batch of orthodox seeds, the times of death of individuals are approximately normally distributed, so the probit of percentage survival (the inverse of the standard normal distribution function; for the **probit model** see [7.7.14](/7.7.14)) declines linearly with storage time. On this basis Ellis and Roberts (1980) proposed the seed viability equation:

$$
v = K_i - \frac{p}{\sigma}, \qquad \log_{10}\sigma = K_E - C_W \log_{10} m - C_H T - C_Q T^2,
\tag{19.1}
$$

where $v$ is the probit of survival after $p$ days of storage, $K_i$ is the probit of the initial quality of the batch (independent of storage conditions), $\sigma$ is the number of days needed for the probit to fall by 1 under the given conditions (the standard deviation of the time to death), $m$ is the seed water content (percentage of fresh weight), $T$ is the storage temperature ($^\circ\mathrm{C}$), and $K_E$, $C_W$, $C_H$, $C_Q$ are species constants (the last two are similar in many species). The time needed for survival to fall to 50% ($v = 0$) is the "half-life" $p_{50} = K_i \sigma$. Equation (19.1) shows that the effects of water content and temperature are multiplicative: lowering either the water content or the temperature increases $\sigma$ exponentially, which is the origin of the rule of thumb that seed longevity roughly doubles for each 1% reduction in water content or each $5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ reduction in storage temperature (Harrington 1972) (see the figure below).

Modern seed banks store orthodox seeds on exactly this principle: seeds are first dried at low humidity and moderate temperature to a water content of about 3%–7%, then sealed and kept at about $-18\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for long-term storage, with expected longevity of decades to centuries, and viability must be tested periodically and the seeds regenerated. Examples are the Germplasm Bank of Wild Species in southwest China (Kunming, built in 2007), the Millennium Seed Bank at the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and the Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway (opened in 2008 as a backup for the seed banks of different countries). Recalcitrant seeds can be preserved only by cryopreservation (in liquid nitrogen) of isolated embryos or embryonic axes, or as living plants.

## 19.5. Seed Germination

**Definition 19.5.1 (Seed Germination)** The process in which a seed released from dormancy, under suitable external conditions, passes from the relatively quiescent state into active growth of the embryo, with the radicle breaking through the seed coat, is called **seed germination**.

In physiology, the emergence of the radicle (or another part of the embryo) through the seed coat is generally taken as the completion of germination; the further growth of the radicle and plumule and the establishment of the seedling are regarded as seedling growth.

### 19.5.1. External Conditions for Germination

The indispensable external conditions for germination are sufficient water, a suitable temperature and enough oxygen; some seeds also need light (or darkness) to germinate.

1. Sufficient water: the water content of a dry seed is generally only 5%–10% of its weight, at which many life processes cannot proceed, so uptake of sufficient water is the first condition of germination. Water acts in several ways. The seed coat absorbs water and softens, so that more oxygen passes through it into the seed, strengthening respiration and metabolism, while carbon dioxide is released. Stored organic nutrients cannot be used in the dry state; once the cells have absorbed water the enzymes become active and break the stored substances down into a soluble form for transport to the embryo. The embryo and endosperm increase in volume after absorbing water, and the soft seed coat ruptures easily under the pressure, which creates the conditions for the radicle and plumule to emerge. The amount of water absorbed in germination depends on the nature of the stored nutrients: generally more than about 30% of the dry weight of the seed, for example about 40% for rice grain, about 56% for wheat, about 52% for cotton, about 48% for rape and 40%–60% for peanut, but about 120% for soybean, about 150% for broad bean and about 186% for pea. Seeds rich in protein absorb much water because protein is strongly hydrophilic; seeds rich in fat absorb less because fat is hydrophobic; starchy seeds generally absorb moderate amounts. Seeds can also absorb water from the atmosphere: when the air humidity is very high, mature seeds germinate on the plant or in the air (vivipary).
2. Suitable temperature: the breakdown of stored substances and the synthesis of new protoplasm in germination are catalyzed by enzymes, whose activity requires a certain temperature; at too low a temperature enzyme action nearly stops, and at too high a temperature enzymes are inactivated. Germination therefore has three cardinal temperatures: minimum, optimum and maximum (table below). The requirements of different plants result from long adaptation to the regions of origin: temperate cereals and cabbage can germinate close to $0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, whereas cucumber, cotton and rice, of tropical origin, require higher temperatures. Knowing the optimum temperature of germination helps in choosing a suitable sowing season.
3. Enough oxygen: during germination the metabolism of cells speeds up sharply, and the breakdown and transport of stored substances and the building of new cells all require energy, which can be supplied only by respiration, especially vigorous in early germination. Loosening the soil before sowing is meant to supply oxygen; the seeds of dryland crops such as sorghum, peanut and cotton cannot grow if they are completely submerged or buried in compacted soil. Rice can germinate at low oxygen (the coleoptile elongates while the radicle is inhibited), but prolonged soaking likewise prevents normal growth, so soaking and forcing germination require control of the oxygen supply.
4. Light: germination of most seeds has little to do with light, but a few seeds germinate well only in light and are called light-requiring seeds, such as tobacco, lettuce, rhododendron, iris and *Arabidopsis*; others germinate only in darkness and are called dark-requiring seeds, such as amaranth and dodder. Light-requiring seeds are mostly very small with few reserves and can emerge only when they germinate close to the soil surface, and light is the signal by which the seed "senses" that it lies at the surface. The effect of light on germination is perceived by **phytochrome** (red light promotes, far-red light inhibits), a mechanism that belongs to plant physiology. Soil pH also has some influence on germination, which is generally good under neutral, slightly acid or slightly alkaline conditions.

| Maize | 5–10 | 35 | 44 |
| Rice | 10 | 30 | 43 |

### 19.5.2. The Process of Germination

The process by which a seed germinates into a seedling can be summarized in the following steps.

1. Imbibition: the seed absorbs water from the outside, the originally dry and hard seed coat gradually softens, water continues to penetrate into the endosperm and embryo, and the whole seed swells and finally bursts the seed coat. Water uptake by a dry seed usually occurs in three phases: at the start the colloidal substances of the seed (proteins, cell walls, starch) absorb water rapidly, and even dead seeds imbibe; then water uptake reaches a plateau, during which metabolism resumes and enzymes are activated or synthesized; finally the radicle emerges, growth begins, and water uptake rises rapidly again.
2. Mobilization of reserves: the insoluble macromolecules (starch, protein, fat) stored in the endosperm or cotyledons during seed formation are broken down by the enzymes activated after water uptake into soluble simple substances (sugars, amino acids), which are transported to the radicle, plumule and axis and taken up by the cells for use; this process is called digestion. The uptake and transport of the soluble substances proceeds mainly by symplastic transport between cells through plasmodesmata (for apoplast and symplast see [7.12.20](/7.12.20)).
3. Growth of the embryo: the embryonic cells assimilate these nutrients into new protoplasm, the cells enlarge and then divide to increase in number, so that the radicle, plumule and axis grow rapidly. The energy for this growth is supplied by the oxidation of part of the organic matter, so germinating seeds respire especially vigorously, releasing much carbon dioxide and heat (a pile of germinating seeds becomes warm).
4. Emergence of the radicle: generally the radicle is the first to break through the soft seed coat and grows downward to form the primary root. In taprooted plants the primary root becomes the main axis of the root system and gives rise to lateral roots of successive orders; in grasses such as wheat, rice and maize, soon after the radicle emerges several adventitious roots about as thick as the primary root grow from the base of the axis, forming a fibrous root system ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)). Forming the root first allows the young seedling to be anchored in the soil and to take up water and nutrients in good time.
5. Emergence of the plumule: soon after the radicle emerges, the cells of the embryonic axis grow and elongate accordingly, pushing the plumule, or the plumule together with the cotyledons, out of the soil. Emerged cotyledons expand and turn green and photosynthesize (cotton, rape), and after the young leaves of the plumule have expanded and begun photosynthesis the cotyledons soon wither and fall.
6. Establishment of the seedling: by now a small plant that can live independently has formed, the seedling. From germination to seedling formation the plant relies mainly on the organic nutrients ready-made in the seed to grow the embryo into an independent young plant; before the nutrients in the cotyledons or endosperm are exhausted, the seedling must establish the ability to feed itself.

The mode of germination is not exactly the same in all plants. Orchid seeds are as small as dust and have almost no stored nutrients, and the embryo is incompletely developed, so germination cannot proceed independently; the seed must be in symbiosis with a **mycorrhizal** fungus, which supplies carbon, before it can develop and survive ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.3.1)). The coconut seed is very large and holds large reserves (the coconut "meat" is solid endosperm and the coconut water is liquid free-nuclear endosperm), while the embryo is very small and the reserves can continue to be supplied for a long time after germination.

## 19.6. Types of Seedlings

When the seeds of different plants germinate, the parts of the embryo, especially the axis, grow at different rates, and the resulting seedlings also differ in form. The basic difference between epigeal and hypogeal germination is which part of the axis, the epicotyl or the hypocotyl, elongates during germination.

**Definition 19.6.1 (Epigeal Seedling and Hypogeal Seedling)** A seedling in which the hypocotyl elongates rapidly during germination and pushes the cotyledons and plumule together out of the soil is called an **epigeal seedling**; a seedling in which the hypocotyl does not elongate or elongates very little while the epicotyl elongates, pushing only the plumule out of the soil and leaving the cotyledons (or endosperm) in the soil, is called a **hypogeal seedling**.

### 19.6.1. Epigeal Seedlings

The exalbuminous seeds of dicots such as soybean, common bean, cotton, rape and the cucurbits, and albuminous seeds such as castor bean, all germinate into epigeal seedlings (row A of the figure of seedling types in Section 19.6.2). The radicle first emerges from the seed coat and enters the soil to form the primary root; then the hypocotyl elongates rapidly, bending into a hook within the soil, with the back of the hook pushing the soil aside and pulling the cotyledons and plumule out of the ground, and after emerging the hypocotyl straightens. The apical hook keeps the delicate plumule and cotyledons pointing downward while they pass through the soil, protecting them and reducing friction with soil particles (the formation and straightening of the hook is regulated by ethylene and light, see plant physiology). The thick cotyledons of soybean and others continue after emergence to transport their stored nutrients to the root, stem and leaves until they are used up, when the cotyledons shrivel and fall; the cotyledons of cotton are thinner and expand and turn green immediately after emerging to photosynthesize, and wither and fall only after the foliage leaves emerge. In castor bean germination the nutrients of the endosperm are broken down to supply the developing embryo, and with the elongation of the hypocotyl the remaining endosperm, attached to the cotyledons, is carried out of the soil and soon falls off and disappears.

The germination of onion, a monocot, differs from this: at the start of germination the lower and middle parts of the cotyledon elongate, pushing the root tip and axis out of the seed coat; afterward the cotyledon elongates rapidly and bends into an arch above the soil surface, while the cotyledon tip remains enclosed in the endosperm and absorbs nutrients; further growth gradually straightens the bent cotyledon and pulls its tip, together with the seed coat, out of the soil, and when the nutrients of the endosperm have been absorbed and used up the seed coat falls off the cotyledon tip; after emerging the cotyledon turns green and photosynthesizes, then the first foliage leaf emerges from a slit in the cotyledon sheath, and adventitious roots grow around the primary root. The onion seedling is therefore also epigeal, but it is the cotyledon itself that elongates, not the hypocotyl.

### 19.6.2. Hypogeal Seedlings

The exalbuminous seeds of dicots such as broad bean, pea, litchi and citrus, the albuminous seeds such as rubber tree, and the monocots wheat, maize and rice all form hypogeal seedlings (rows B and C of the figure below). In their germination the hypocotyl does not elongate; instead the epicotyl elongates, so the cotyledons or endosperm do not emerge with the plumule but stay in the soil until their nutrients are exhausted and they die. When broad bean germinates, the radicle first emerges through the seed coat and grows downward to become the main axis of the root system; the elongation of the epicotyl soon pushes the plumule out of the soil, and it is the epicotyl, not the hypocotyl, that forms the hook in the soil. The seedling type may differ between species of the same genus: common bean (*Phaseolus vulgaris*) is epigeal, whereas the runner bean (*P. coccineus*) of the same genus is hypogeal.

The seedling of grasses is a special type of hypogeal seedling: the scutellum remains in the grain and absorbs the endosperm, and what emerges is the coleoptile. The coleoptile is a sheath closed at the tip with only two vascular bundles, stiff and pointed so that it can pass through soil; after emerging into the light it stops elongating, and the first foliage leaf emerges from a slit at its tip. When maize and sorghum are sown deep, the mesocotyl elongates markedly and pushes the tip of the coleoptile to a position near the ground surface, so that seedlings can emerge from deeper soil layers; the mesocotyl of wheat hardly elongates, and when sown deep wheat emerges through the elongation of the coleoptile and the first internode, so its ability to emerge is weaker. The root system of a grass seedling consists of the primary root formed from the radicle (seminal root), several seminal adventitious roots arising from the scutellar node, and numerous nodal roots (crown roots) arising later from the nodes at the base of the stem, the last forming the fibrous root system of the adult plant ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)).

### 19.6.3. Agricultural Significance of Seedling Types

Knowing the seedling type guides agriculture, forestry and horticulture, because it is closely related to sowing depth. In general, seeds that form epigeal seedlings should be sown shallowly so that the hypocotyl can push the cotyledons and plumule out of the soil, while seeds that form hypogeal seedlings can be sown somewhat deeper. But the force with which seeds push up through the soil differs among crops, and seed size also affects it: a seed with strong emergence force may be sown slightly deeper even if epigeal, whereas one with weak force must be sown shallowly. Sowing depth must therefore be decided according to the particular seed. Hypogeal seedlings have a further advantage: only the plumule emerges, while the cotyledons and their axillary buds remain in the soil, so if the aerial part of the seedling is killed by frost or eaten by animals, the axillary buds at the cotyledonary node can still sprout and regenerate; if an epigeal seedling is destroyed above the cotyledonary node, it can hardly regenerate.

The form of the seedling (shape of the cotyledons, epigeal or hypogeal emergence, phyllotaxis of the first pair of foliage leaves) is fairly stable within families and genera, and in forest nurseries and weed control it is often used to identify plants at the seedling stage, when there are no flowers or fruit; for many tropical trees, seedling morphology is also used as a taxonomic character.

## Questions

1. How many "generations" of tissue does an angiosperm seed contain? Why is the gymnosperm "endosperm" said to have the same name as, but a different nature from, the angiosperm endosperm?

   **Answer** Three. The seed coat (and perisperm) belongs to the maternal sporophyte, $2n$; the embryo is the sporophyte of the next generation, $2n$; the endosperm is formed by the fusion of a sperm with the two polar nuclei in double fertilization, is usually $3n$, and is a nutritive tissue serving only the embryo. Gymnosperms have no double fertilization; their endosperm is the female gametophyte, $n$, formed from the megaspore within the megasporangium (nucellus), already formed and filled with nutrients before fertilization, whereas the angiosperm endosperm begins to develop only after fertilization, so the investment of nutrients depends on successful fertilization. The two have the same function but differ in origin and ploidy.

2. How do the wheat "grain", the maize "grain" and the broad bean seed differ in structure? After sowing, to what type does each seedling belong?

   **Answer** The grains of wheat and maize are caryopses: outside is a protective layer in which pericarp and seed coat are fused; inside are the well-developed endosperm (with an aleurone layer outside) and the embryo on one side, which has a scutellum, coleoptile and coleorhiza (wheat also has an epiblast, maize does not), and maize has a longer mesocotyl. Broad bean is a true seed without endosperm; inside the seed coat are two thick cotyledons with the plumule and radicle between them. All three seedlings are hypogeal: broad bean by elongation of the epicotyl, while in wheat and maize the scutellum stays in the grain and the coleoptile emerges (in maize the mesocotyl elongates to reach near the ground surface).

3. The seeds of a legume do not germinate for a long time under moist, warm conditions, but germinate quickly when the seed coat is scratched with sandpaper; the seeds of a temperate shrub still do not germinate after scarification, and germinate only after two months in moist sand at $4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Identify the type of dormancy in each case and explain the principle of breaking it.

   **Answer** The first is physical dormancy (hard seed): the palisade layer of the seed coat is impermeable to water, the embryo itself is not dormant, and scarifying the coat to admit water allows germination; in nature the dormancy is released when the seed coat is broken by high temperatures, wetting and drying cycles or soil microorganisms. The second is physiological dormancy of the embryo: the dormancy lies in the embryo (hormone balance) and is released by cold, moist stratification (after-ripening); scarification is ineffective. If seeds still need stratification after scarification before germinating, the dormancy is combinational, with both components.

4. Use Eq. (19.1) to estimate how long it takes for survival of a batch of barley seeds ($K_i = 2$) stored at 12% water content and $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to fall to 50%. How long if the water content is reduced to 8%? (For barley $K_E = 9.983$, $C_W = 5.896$, $C_H = 0.040$, $C_Q = 0.000428$.)

   **Answer** At 12%, $\log_{10}\sigma = 9.983 - 5.896 \times 1.0792 - 0.040 \times 20 - 0.000428 \times 400 \approx 2.649$, so $\sigma \approx 446$ days and $p_{50} = K_i\sigma \approx 890$ days, about 2.4 years. At 8%, $\log_{10} 8 = 0.9031$, $\log_{10}\sigma \approx 3.687$, $\sigma \approx 4860$ days and $p_{50} \approx 9700$ days, about 27 years. Reducing the water content from 12% to 8% lengthens longevity about 11-fold, roughly consistent with the rule of thumb that longevity doubles for each 1% reduction in water content ($2^4 = 16$, the same order of magnitude).

5. Orchid seeds have no endosperm and an undifferentiated embryo, yet one capsule may contain hundreds of thousands to millions of seeds; a coconut fruit has only one huge seed. Explain the two strategies in terms of the trade-off between seed size and number.

   **Answer** The resources a parent plant can devote to reproduction are limited, so the larger the seeds, the fewer there are. Orchids spread their resources over a great many tiny seeds, which are dispersed over long distances by wind like dust, increasing the chance of reaching a suitable habitat; the cost is that the seeds cannot feed themselves and germination depends on mycorrhizal fungi to supply carbon, so the vast majority of seeds die where no suitable fungus is present. The coconut concentrates its resources in a single seed; the large amount of endosperm and the fibrous pericarp allow it to drift across the sea and to support the seedling for a long time on a poor beach; the cost is very small numbers. These are the two extremes of the seed size-number trade-off, and most plants lie between them, taking intermediate values according to habitat (more large seeds under closed forest, more small seeds in open ground).
