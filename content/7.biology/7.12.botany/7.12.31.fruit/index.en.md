The carpel encloses the ovules within the ovary, which gives the angiosperms their name ([7.12.27](/7.12.27)). After fertilization the ovary develops into a fruit and the ovules into seeds ([7.12.30](/7.12.30)). The fruit is an organ unique to the flowering plants. It protects the developing seeds, often stores food, and, above all, helps disperse the seeds. Gymnosperm seeds are dispersed by their own wings or fleshy seed coats; angiosperms have handed the task to structures developed from the ovary wall and often from the receptacle, the perianth or even the whole inflorescence. The result is an enormous diversity, from the dry, splitting capsule to the juicy berry and from the winged samara to the hooked achene. This chapter first describes how fruits form (§31.1), then fruit types (§31.2), a vocabulary that is among the most useful for recognizing angiosperm families ([7.12.35](/7.12.35)–[7.12.49](/7.12.49)). It then turns to the adaptations of fruits and seeds to the various dispersal agents (§31.3) and closes with the evolution of fruits (§31.4). Ethylene and the hormonal control of ripening belong to plant physiology; the theory of coevolution between fruits and their dispersers is treated in evolutionary biology ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)).

## 31.1. Fruit Formation

### 31.1.1. Fruit and Pericarp

After fertilization the parts of the flower change markedly. The calyx (except in species with a persistent calyx) and the corolla generally wither and fall, and the stamens and the stigma and style of the pistil also shrivel. Only the ovary, or the ovary together with other parts attached to it, grows rapidly and develops into the fruit.

**Definition 31.1.1 (Fruit)** The seed-bearing organ that develops, after fertilization, from the ovary of an angiosperm (sometimes together with other parts such as the receptacle, perianth, bracts or inflorescence axis) is called a **fruit**.

**Definition 31.1.2 (Pericarp)** The wall of a fruit, formed from the mature ovary wall, is called the **pericarp**. It can usually be divided into three layers: the outermost **exocarp**, the middle **mesocarp** and the innermost **endocarp**.

The ovary is originally composed of thin-walled parenchyma cells; as it matures into a fruit these differentiate further into a variety of tissues, in ways that differ from plant to plant, and this is precisely the basis of fruit classification. The thickness and texture of the three layers vary from fruit to fruit. In a drupe the three layers are sharply delimited, whereas in many fruits they are indistinct or even merge, so that they cannot be told apart. Pericarp development is complex: the three layers often cannot be matched simply with the outer epidermis, middle layers and inner epidermis of the ovary wall, and each layer may differentiate further during development, so tracing their origins is difficult. Strictly speaking, the pericarp is only the mature ovary wall; when a fruit also contains the receptacle or other structures, the term can be extended to these additional parts.

Fruit development generally has three phases: a period of cell division in the ovary after fertilization (a few weeks), a period of rapid cell expansion, and ripening. During ripening, fleshy fruits undergo a series of physiological changes that give them color, aroma and flavor:

- Starch is converted to soluble sugars, organic acids decrease, and tannins are oxidized or become insoluble, so sweetness increases and sourness and astringency decrease.
- Chlorophyll in the plastids breaks down, and anthocyanins appear in the cell sap or carotenoids accumulate in chromoplasts, so the fruit changes from green to red, yellow, purple or black ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)).
- The flesh cells produce volatile compounds such as esters, which give the fruit its aroma.
- The middle lamella between flesh cells is dissolved by pectinase and other enzymes, and the flesh softens.

Apples, bananas, tomatoes and similar fruits show a sudden rise in respiration and release ethylene as they ripen. They are called climacteric fruits and can be picked and ripened artificially. Citrus, grape and strawberry show no such climacteric and must ripen on the plant. The regulation of ripening belongs to plant physiology.

### 31.1.2. True Fruits and Accessory Fruits

**Definition 31.1.3 (True Fruits and Accessory Fruits)** A fruit whose pericarp is formed solely from the ovary wall is called a **true fruit**. A fruit to whose formation parts other than the ovary contribute, such as the receptacle, hypanthium (floral cup), perianth, bracts or even the inflorescence axis, is called an **accessory fruit** (false fruit).

Most plants bear true fruits, for example peach, soybean, citrus and tomato. The parts that contribute to accessory fruits differ, which shows that "accessory fruit" is not a fruit type but a description of origin:

| Contributing part | Examples | Edible part |
|---|---|---|
| Receptacle | Strawberry | Enlarged fleshy receptacle; the "seeds" on its surface are achenes |
| Hypanthium (floral cup) fused with an inferior ovary | Apple, pear (pomes) | Mainly the hypanthium |
| Receptacle fused with an inferior ovary | Cucumber, squash, watermelon (pepos) | Pericarp or placenta |
| Perianth | Mulberry (the perianth of each pistillate flower becomes fleshy); sea grape (*Coccoloba*, fleshy perianth) | Perianth |
| Calyx | Persistent calyx forming wings in Dipterocarpaceae (*Dipterocarpus*); persistent calyx forming an inflated bladder in ground cherry (*Physalis*) | None |
| Involucre | Cupule of oak and chestnut | None |
| Inflorescence axis and bracts | Pineapple, fig | Inflorescence axis and bracts; the enclosed inflorescence axis (syconium) in the fig |

Fruits with an inferior ovary always involve the receptacle or hypanthium, so the position of the ovary ([7.12.27](/7.12.27)) often decides whether a fruit is true or accessory.

### 31.1.3. Parthenocarpy and Seedless Fruits

Fruit formation is generally closely tied to fertilization: the developing seeds produce auxin and gibberellin after fertilization, which promote ovary growth, so a flower normally sets fruit only after fertilization, and fruits with few seeds are often lopsided. Some plants, however, can develop fruits without fertilization, either under natural conditions or under human control.

**Definition 31.1.4 (Parthenocarpy)** The development of a fruit from an ovary without fertilization is called **parthenocarpy**. Parthenocarpic fruits contain no seeds and are called seedless fruits.

- Autonomous parthenocarpy occurs under natural conditions. The banana is the outstanding example: its inflorescence is a spike with male flowers in the upper part of the axis and female flowers in the lower part, and the female flowers form fruits without pollination or fertilization. Cultivated bananas are mostly triploid (AAA and others), with highly irregular meiosis, and produce almost no functional gametes ([7.4.16](/7.4.16)). Some grape cultivars, satsuma mandarin, persimmon and some cucurbits also show autonomous parthenocarpy; such seedless fruits of high quality are prized cultivars in horticulture.
- Induced parthenocarpy is brought about by a stimulus. Seedless fruits can be obtained by stimulating tomato stigmas with potato pollen or grape stigmas with Virginia creeper pollen. More commonly, growth regulators are smeared or sprayed on the flowers: auxins such as 2,4-D and indoleacetic acid induce parthenocarpy in tomato and cucurbits, and treating grape inflorescences with gibberellin yields seedless grapes.

Seedless fruits do not always arise from parthenocarpy. Some grape cultivars (such as 'Thompson Seedless') are normally fertilized, but the embryo aborts very early and only traces of the seed remain in the fruit. The triploid seedless watermelon is another case. Diploid watermelon is doubled to a tetraploid with colchicine, and a cross with the tetraploid as female parent and a diploid as male parent gives triploid seeds. In meiosis of the triploid plant the three homologous chromosomes cannot pair and segregate normally, so almost no fertile gametes are formed ([7.4.16](/7.4.16#sec-16.4)). A diploid cultivar is interplanted, and its pollen stimulates the ovary to swell, so that fruits without mature seeds are produced.

## 31.2. Fruit Types

### 31.2.1. Basis of Classification

The classification of fruits is an artificial, descriptive system. The variation in fruit structure is often continuous, fruits with similar functions have evolved independently from similar or different pistils in different lineages (convergence), and other parts of the flower often take part in the fruit, so no classification can place every fruit neatly. The usual scheme combines three criteria:

1. how many flowers and pistils form the fruit: simple, aggregate and multiple fruits;
2. the texture of the pericarp: fleshy fruits and dry fruits;
3. whether a dry fruit opens at maturity and how it opens, together with the number of carpels and seeds.

**Definition 31.2.1 (Simple, Aggregate and Multiple Fruits)** A flower with a single pistil (simple or compound) that forms a single fruit gives a **simple fruit**. A flower with many separate pistils, each of which forms a small fruit, the small fruits being clustered on the same receptacle, gives an **aggregate fruit**. A fruit developed from a whole inflorescence, with parts of the inflorescence also contributing to it, is called a **multiple fruit**, also known as an infructescence or compound fruit.

Simple fruits are divided by the nature of the pericarp into fleshy fruits and dry fruits: in the former the pericarp is thick and fleshy, in the latter it is dry and juiceless at maturity. The figure below summarizes the main fruit types.

### 31.2.2. Fleshy Fruits

**Definition 31.2.2 (Types of Fleshy Fruits)** Fleshy fruits are divided by the origin and nature of the pericarp into the following types.

- **Berry**: formed from one or several carpels; the exocarp is thin, the mesocarp and endocarp are both fleshy and juicy, and the fruit contains one to many seeds. Examples are grape, tomato, persimmon, eggplant and kiwifruit.
- **Hesperidium**: a berry developed from a superior ovary of many carpels with axile placentation; the exocarp is leathery with oil glands, the mesocarp is loose and spongy with vascular bundles, and the endocarp is membranous and divided into several locules, which are filled with juice sacs developed from epidermal hairs of the endocarp. Examples are orange, pomelo and lemon.
- **Pepo**: a berry-like accessory fruit developed jointly from an inferior ovary of three carpels with parietal placentation and the receptacle; the exocarp is hard, and the mesocarp, endocarp and placenta are fleshy. It is the fruit characteristic of the Cucurbitaceae, as in cucumber, squash and watermelon.
- **Drupe**: usually developed from a pistil of a single carpel and containing one seed; the exocarp is thin, the mesocarp fleshy or fibrous, and the endocarp, composed of stone cells, is hard and forms the stone. Examples are peach, plum, Japanese apricot, apricot, cherry and olive.
- **Pome**: an accessory fruit developed jointly from an inferior ovary and the hypanthium (floral cup); the thick fleshy outer part is mainly hypanthium and the true pericarp lies inside it, with no clear boundary between the exocarp, mesocarp and hypanthium, while the endocarp is made of lignified thick-walled cells and is leathery or cartilaginous. Examples are apple, pear, hawthorn and Chinese quince.

The edible part of the tomato is chiefly the well-developed fleshy placenta; the edible part of watermelon is also the placenta, whereas that of squash and winter melon is mainly the pericarp. The white strands on the inside of a dried citrus peel are the vascular bundles of the mesocarp. The juice sacs of citrus are a special kind of epidermal hair, which is unique among angiosperms.

Among drupes there are some types with a dry mesocarp. In the coconut the mesocarp is fibrous (coir) and the endocarp is the hard shell, inside which lies the seed (the endosperm, [7.12.30](/7.12.30)). In almond the seed is eaten, and the mesocarp splits open at maturity. The walnut is more complicated: its green husk is formed from the involucre and perianth fused with the ovary wall, and the hard shell is the endocarp; it is often called a drupe-like nut or pseudodrupe. The fruit of the raspberry and its relatives is an aggregate of many small drupes (§31.2.5).

### 31.2.3. Dry Fruits: Dehiscent Fruits

**Definition 31.2.3 (Types of Dehiscent Fruits)** Dry fruits whose pericarp opens by itself at maturity are called dehiscent fruits. The main types are the following.

- **Legume**: developed from a single carpel; at maturity it splits along both the dorsal and the ventral suture. It is the fruit characteristic of the Fabaceae.
- **Follicle**: developed from a single carpel; at maturity it splits along one suture only.
- **Capsule**: developed from a compound pistil of fused carpels; the ovary has one or several locules, each with many seeds, and the capsule opens in various ways at maturity.
- **Silique**: developed from a pistil of two carpels; the ovary has one locule, but a septum (the false septum, or replum) grows from the fused margins of the carpels toward the center and divides the ovary into two locules. At maturity the two valves split off from the base upward along the two ventral sutures and fall away, leaving only the replum, along whose margin the seeds are attached. It is the fruit characteristic of the Brassicaceae. A fruit more than twice as long as wide is a silique, and one about as long as wide is a silicle.

Not all legumes dehisce. The legumes of peanut, silk tree (*Albizia*) and honey locust do not open at maturity. The legumes of sensitive plant, *Senna* and tick trefoil (*Desmodium*) are constricted between the seeds and break into one-seeded segments at maturity; such a fruit is called a loment. The cylindrical legume of the Japanese pagoda tree (*Styphnolobium japonicum*) is constricted between the seeds into a string of beads and does not open. The legume of alfalfa (*Medicago*) is coiled in a spiral, and in some species is covered with spines.

Some follicles split along the ventral suture, as in peony, tree peony, star anise, *Firmiana* (Chinese parasol tree), larkspur and columbine; others split along the dorsal suture, as in *Magnolia*. When the several free carpels of a flower each develop into a follicle, the result is an aggregate of follicles (star anise, peony, magnolia). In the Apocynaceae (milkweed and its relatives) the ovaries of the two carpels are separate but the styles are united, and the resulting pair of follicles contains seeds with a tuft of long hairs at the tip.

The capsule is the most common dehiscent fruit, and its mode of dehiscence is a diagnostic character of many families (see the figure below):

| Mode of dehiscence | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Loculicidal | Splits along the dorsal suture (midrib) of each carpel; the slit opens into the locule | Iris, lily, cotton, wood sorrel (*Oxalis*), violet |
| Septicidal | Splits along the junctions (septa) of adjacent carpels; the carpels separate from one another | Autumn crocus (*Colchicum*), birthwort (*Aristolochia*), rhododendron |
| Septifragal | The valves split along the dorsal or septal lines but the septa remain attached to the central axis | Morning glory, cypress vine, thorn apple (*Datura*) |
| Poricidal | The carpels do not separate; a small pore opens above each locule and the seeds are scattered through the pores | Poppy, snapdragon, bellflower (*Platycodon*) |
| Circumscissile | The upper or middle part of the fruit splits transversely and falls off like a lid; also called a pyxis | Purslane, plantain, sea milkwort (*Glaux*) |
| Denticidal | The tip of the fruit splits into a number of teeth | Pink (*Dianthus*), mouse-ear chickweed (*Cerastium*), cowherb (*Vaccaria*) |

The silique is the hallmark of the Brassicaceae. Siliques occur in rapeseed, Chinese cabbage, cabbage and *Arabidopsis*; silicles occur in shepherd's purse, field pennycress (*Thlaspi arvense*) and peppergrass (*Lepidium*). The silique of radish (*Raphanus*) is an exception: it does not open, but constricts or breaks crosswise between the seeds at maturity.

### 31.2.4. Dry Fruits: Indehiscent Fruits

**Definition 31.2.4 (Types of Indehiscent Fruits)** Dry fruits whose pericarp does not open at maturity are called indehiscent fruits. They usually contain one seed. The main types are the following.

- **Achene**: a small indehiscent fruit of one to three carpels; the pericarp is tough, the fruit contains one seed, and the pericarp is attached to the seed coat at one point only, so they separate easily.
- **Caryopsis**: the pericarp is thin and leathery, the fruit contains one seed, and the pericarp is fused tightly to the seed coat and cannot be separated from it. It is the fruit characteristic of the Poaceae.
- **Samara**: essentially an achene or nut whose pericarp is extended into a wing, an adaptation to wind dispersal.
- **Nut**: the pericarp is hard and woody, the fruit contains one seed, and it is often enclosed or subtended by a cupule formed from the involucre of the original inflorescence.
- **Cremocarp**: developed from an inferior ovary of two carpels; at maturity the two carpels separate along the median line into two mericarps, which hang from the upper end of the forked central stalk (carpophore) while the seeds remain enclosed in the mericarps. It is the fruit characteristic of the Apiaceae.
- **Utricle**: formed from fused carpels, with one seed; the pericarp is thin and inflated, loosely surrounds the seed and separates from it very easily.

Examples of achenes are pasqueflower (*Pulsatilla*), buttercup (*Ranunculus*, one carpel, often forming an aggregate of achenes) and buckwheat (three carpels). The "achene" of the Asteraceae is formed from an inferior ovary of two carpels and is fused externally with the hypanthium; strictly it is called a cypsela, and it often bears at its tip a pappus derived from the calyx, as in sunflower and dandelion. The fruit of the Cyperaceae is also an achene-like small nut.

The caryopsis is very small and is generally mistaken for a seed. The "seeds" of rice, wheat and maize are in fact caryopses ([7.12.19](/7.12.19), [7.12.30](/7.12.30)). A caryopsis differs from an achene only in whether the pericarp is fused with the seed coat.

Among samaras, the elm fruit has a wing all around (a circumferential samara), ash has a wing at one end only (a single-winged samara), and the samara of the tree of heaven (*Ailanthus altissima*) is long and elliptical with the seed in the middle. The fruit of maple (*Acer*) develops from two carpels and at maturity splits into two nutlets, each with a long wing; this is called a double samara and, taxonomically, belongs to the schizocarps discussed below.

Examples of nuts are oak, chestnut and hazel. The involucre of chestnut is spiny and often encloses two or three nuts; the cupule of oak only subtends the base of the nut. Nuts and achenes are both one-seeded indehiscent fruits; the difference is that the nut is larger and has a hard, woody pericarp, and it often develops from an ovary of fused carpels in which only one ovule matures.

An indehiscent fruit formed from two or more carpels that separates at maturity into several one-seeded mericarps is called a schizocarp. The cremocarp of the Apiaceae is one kind of schizocarp. The fruit of the Malvaceae (velvetleaf, mallow) breaks up into a ring of mericarps, and the mericarps of the Geraniaceae bear long beaks that coil with changes in humidity. In the Lamiaceae and Boraginaceae the ovary is deeply four-lobed and becomes four nutlets at maturity, which is also a kind of schizocarp.

Utricles occur in the Amaranthaceae (goosefoot, orache, summer cypress and part of the genus *Amaranthus*), in duckweed (*Lemna*) and in sea lavender (*Limonium*).

### 31.2.5. Aggregate and Multiple Fruits

Aggregate fruits are named for the type of the individual small fruits:

| Aggregate fruit | Small fruits | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Aggregate of achenes | Achenes on an enlarged fleshy receptacle, or enclosed in a fleshy hypanthium | Strawberry (fleshy receptacle); buttercup, pasqueflower, clematis (receptacle not enlarged); rose hip of the genus *Rosa* (achenes enclosed in an urn-shaped fleshy hypanthium) |
| Aggregate of drupelets | Small drupes | *Rubus* (raspberry, blackberry) |
| Aggregate of follicles | Follicles | Star anise, peony, magnolia |
| Aggregate of nuts | Small nuts embedded in a spongy receptacle | Lotus (the seed head is the enlarged receptacle, and the lotus "seeds" are nuts) |
| Aggregate of berries | Berries | *Schisandra* (receptacle elongating in fruit), custard apple (small berries fused with the receptacle) |

Examples of **multiple fruits** are the following.

- Mulberry: each flower of the pistillate inflorescence develops into a small achene enclosed in the thickened, fleshy and juicy perianth, and many such small fruits are clustered on the inflorescence axis to form the mulberry fruit. The multiple fruit of paper mulberry (*Broussonetia*) is similar.
- Pineapple: the ovaries of many flowers develop into small berries, which fuse with the fleshy bracts and inflorescence axis into a single unit. The edible part is mainly the inflorescence axis and bracts, and the crown at the top is a tuft of leaves produced by continued growth of the inflorescence axis.
- Fig: in the enclosed inflorescence (syconium, [7.12.27](/7.12.27)) the inflorescence axis is enlarged into a fleshy, pouch-like structure with many small flowers on its inner wall. The true fruits are the many small achenes (drupe-like) inside the pouch, and what is eaten is mainly the fleshy inflorescence axis.
- Jackfruit: a large multiple fruit of the Moraceae that can weigh tens of kilograms; the fleshy perianth is eaten.
- Plane tree (*Platanus*): many small hairy nuts form a spherical multiple fruit.

## 31.3. Dispersal of Fruits and Seeds

### 31.3.1. Significance and Modes of Dispersal

Dispersal of fruits and seeds to different places enlarges the range of the offspring, reduces competition between parents and offspring and among siblings, and gives seeds a chance to reach new suitable habitats, all of which benefit the prosperity and the adaptive expansion of a population. Fruits and seeds are dispersed mainly by wind, by water, by being carried by animals and humans, and by mechanical forces generated by the fruit itself, and each agent has corresponding morphological adaptations. The modes of dispersal are listed in the table below. The types often overlap, and one plant may use several agents in succession (for example, first ballistic ejection, then transport by ants):

| Mode of dispersal | Agent | Typical morphology |
|---|---|---|
| Wind (anemochory) | Wind | Tiny, light seeds; hairs, pappus, wings, bladders; whole plant rolling in the wind |
| Water (hydrochory) | Currents, raindrops | Fruits that float (aerenchyma, fibers, air); waterproof shells |
| Animals (zoochory): internal | Birds, mammals, fish, reptiles | Fleshy fruits, arils, bright colors, hard seed coats |
| Animals: external | Mammal fur, bird feathers | Hooks, spines, barbs, mucilage |
| Animals: caching | Squirrels, nutcrackers, jays | Large, nutritious nuts and seeds |
| Ants (myrmecochory) | Ants | Elaiosomes on the seeds |
| Self-dispersal (autochory) | Tension or turgor of the pericarp | Explosively or ballistically dehiscent fruits |

### 31.3.2. Adaptations to Wind Dispersal

Fruits and seeds dispersed by wind are generally small and light, so that they stay suspended in the air and are blown far away. Alternatively they bear surface fluff, fruit wings or other structures that help them catch the wind:

- Minute seeds: the seeds of orchids are as small as dust, only a few micrograms each, and can be blown several kilometers.
- Hairs: in cotton the epidermal cells of the seed coat extend into long hairs (cotton fibers); the seeds of poplar and willow bear a tuft of long hairs at the base, growing from the funiculus and placenta (poplar and willow "cotton"); the seeds of milkweed and star jasmine (*Trachelospermum*) of the Apocynaceae bear a tuft of hairs at the tip (a coma); the cypselas of dandelion and other Asteraceae bear at the tip a pappus derived from the calyx, which opens like a parachute at maturity; the achenes of pasqueflower and clematis bear persistent feathery styles.
- Wings: the pericarps of maple, elm, ash and tree of heaven extend into wings. Pine, spruce and fir seeds also have wings, but these are not pericarp or seed coat; they are a layer of tissue peeled from the surface of the seed scale (gymnosperms have no fruit, [7.12.26](/7.12.26)). The fruits of the Dipterocarpaceae bear several wings formed from the enlarged persistent calyx.
- Bladders: in the genus *Physalis* the persistent calyx enlarges in fruit into an inflated bladder enclosing the berry, which can roll in the wind or float on water.
- Tumbleweeds: in species of the genera *Salsola* and *Gypsophila* of prairies and deserts, the spherical plant breaks off at the root crown when the seeds are ripe and rolls in the wind, scattering seeds along the way.

A winged or pappus-bearing fruit quickly reaches a constant speed as it falls, the terminal velocity at which drag balances gravity. For flow at high Reynolds number ([7.6.22](/7.6.22#sec-22.3)) the drag is $F_{\mathrm{D}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_{\mathrm{D}} S v^2$, where $\rho$ is the air density, $S$ is the frontal area (wing area) and $C_{\mathrm{D}}$ is the drag coefficient. Setting $F_{\mathrm{D}} = mg$ gives

$$
v_{\mathrm{t}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\rho C_{\mathrm{D}}}\cdot\frac{mg}{S}},
$$

so the terminal velocity is proportional to the square root of the wing loading $mg/S$. If a seed is released from the parent plant at height $H$ and falls at constant speed in a horizontal wind of speed $u$, the horizontal drift distance is approximately

$$
x \approx \frac{H u}{v_{\mathrm{t}}}.
$$

This simple ballistic model shows why the morphology of wind-dispersed fruits matters: reducing the wing loading to 1/4 (by increasing the wing area fourfold or reducing the mass to 1/4) halves the terminal velocity and doubles the dispersal distance (see the figure below). The double samara of maple rotates about itself as it falls and generates lift like a helicopter rotor, so at the same wing loading it falls more slowly than a non-rotating flat plate. The pappus of dandelion increases drag by means of a stable separated vortex ring that forms above it. Real dispersal distances also depend on turbulence and updrafts, and an occasional strong gust can carry seeds much farther than the average. This "long tail" of rare long-distance dispersal events is especially important for plant migration and for colonizing islands.

The slope is 1/2: each reduction of the wing loading to 1/4 halves the terminal velocity. ### 31.3.3. Adaptations to Water Dispersal

The fruits and seeds of aquatic and marsh plants are often dispersed by water. The fruit of the lotus (the lotus "seed") sits in an obconical receptacle (the seed head), whose tissue is loose and light, floats on the water, drifts with the current, and then scatters the nuts. Among land plants, the coconut is dispersed by ocean currents. Its mesocarp is loose and rich in fiber, so the fruit floats on water; its endocarp is thick and hard and keeps seawater out; and the large amount of coconut water in the fruit allows the embryo to germinate during the voyage and in a saline environment. Coconut groves are widespread on tropical coasts, and many newly formed Pacific coral islands soon acquire coconut palms, all because of this mode of dispersal. The viviparous seedlings of mangroves also drift with seawater (§32.2.4). For plants growing on hillsides, seeds are also often scattered by rain wash.

### 31.3.4. Adaptations to Dispersal by Animals and Humans

A number of fruits and seeds are carried by animals and humans, in several ways:

- External attachment: the fruit or seed bears bristles or barbed hooks, or secretes mucilage, and hangs or sticks to the fur and feathers of animals or to human clothing, to be carried far away. Examples are hedge parsley (*Torilis*), beggarticks (*Bidens*, whose pappus has become two to four barbed awns), cocklebur (*Xanthium*, whose dispersal unit is the hooked burr formed from the involucre and containing two achenes), puncturevine (*Tribulus*), cleavers (*Galium aparine*), and the mucilage glands on the persistent calyx of *Salvia*. The fruits of the African grapple plant (*Harpagophytum*) bear enormous hooks that catch on the legs of large mammals. The berries of mistletoe contain sticky mucilage; after a bird eats them it wipes its beak on a branch or passes them in its droppings, and the seeds stick to the twigs.
- Internal dispersal: most fleshy fruits are favorite foods of birds and mammals. When the fruit is swallowed, the pericarp is digested and absorbed, whereas the seeds, protected by a tough seed coat or endocarp, are not digested and are passed in the feces or regurgitated, landing elsewhere. Passage through the gut partly abrades the seed coat and sometimes promotes germination. Unripe fleshy fruits are mostly green, astringent from tannins or alkaloids, and change color and become sweet only when the seeds are ripe; this "signal" ensures that animals eat only seeds that are ready to be dispersed. Arils function like fleshy fruits ([7.12.30](/7.12.30)).
- Caching: nuts and large seeds are food for squirrels, nutcrackers, jays and others, which carry the fruits away and bury them singly in the ground; those that are forgotten or not eaten germinate where they lie. The regeneration of oak, chestnut, walnut and Korean pine depends largely on this mode of dispersal.
- Dispersal by ants: the seeds of many herbaceous plants carry an appendage rich in lipids, proteins and sugars, called an elaiosome, which may develop from the caruncle, aril, funiculus and other parts. Ants carry the seeds back to the nest, eat the elaiosome and discard the intact seeds in the nest or on a refuse pile beside it, where the seeds escape seed-eating animals and find richer soil. Violets (*Viola*), trilliums (*Trillium*), *Corydalis*, bloodroot and castor bean (caruncle, [7.12.19](/7.12.19)) are all dispersed in this way; in the herbaceous understory of the deciduous forests of eastern North America, ant-dispersed species may make up a third of the total.
- Dispersal by humans: humans discard seeds everywhere as they eat fruits, and through agriculture, horticulture and trade they have carried many plants beyond their native ranges, some of which have become invasive.

### 31.3.5. Dispersal by the Fruit's Own Mechanical Forces

In some plants the fruit generates an elastic or ejecting force as it opens abruptly, and the seeds are flung out. The pericarp of a dehiscent fruit becomes dry and hard at maturity, and because the thick-walled cells of its layers are oriented in different directions, shrinkage on water loss produces a twisting force that suddenly splits and twists the valves and ejects the seeds, as in soybean, broad bean and rapeseed. In the capsule of impatiens (touch-me-not) at maturity the five valves suddenly curl inward under tension created by turgor and fling the seeds out. The fruit of the squirting cucumber (*Ecballium elaterium*) is full of fluid under high pressure when ripe, and at the instant the fruit detaches from its stalk the fluid and seeds are squirted out together through the break. The capsule of witch hazel (*Hamamelis*) dries and the endocarp shrinks, flinging seeds up to 10 m away. The fruit of dwarf mistletoe (*Arceuthobium*) shoots out its sticky seeds under very high hydrostatic pressure, at an initial speed of about $100\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}$ and a range of up to 15 m.

Self-dispersal generally carries seeds only a few meters, but it is often combined with other modes in relay: many seeds that have been flung out are then carried by ants. For crops, spontaneous opening of the fruit at maturity is a disadvantage: soybean, rapeseed and the like must be harvested promptly after ripening, or the fruits dry out and dehisce and the seeds are lost in the field (§31.4).

## 31.4. The Evolution of Fruits

Fruits appeared together with carpels. Once the carpel closed, seeds were no longer directly exposed, and the task of dispersal passed to the ovary wall and its accessory structures. This gave the angiosperms a "dispersal organ" that could be remodeled again and again: the same function can be carried out by different tissues, and the same kind of organ can be shaped into entirely different forms.

- Early fruits: fossil angiosperms from the Early Cretaceous (such as *Archaefructus* from Liaoning, about 125 million years old, whose carpels were follicle-like at maturity, [7.12.34](/7.12.34)) show that the earliest angiosperms mostly bore small fruits formed from free carpels, with small seeds dispersed mainly by abiotic agents or by the plant itself. From the Late Cretaceous to the Paleogene, the mean seed size and the proportion of fleshy fruits both increased markedly, coinciding with the radiation of birds and mammals, and the rise of vertebrate frugivores is generally thought to have driven the evolution of fleshy fruits.
- Convergence: fleshy fruits have originated independently many times in the angiosperms, often repeatedly within a single family. Winged fruits, fruits with a pappus and hooked fruits have likewise each evolved many times independently. Structures with the same function can come from different parts: the fleshy part of the strawberry is the receptacle, of the apple the hypanthium, of the mulberry the perianth, of the pineapple the inflorescence axis and bracts, of the tomato and watermelon the placenta, of citrus the hairs of the endocarp, and of the lychee the aril ([7.12.30](/7.12.30)). This is exactly why fruit classification can only be artificial.
- Transitions within groups: molecular phylogenetics makes it possible to reconstruct transitions in fruit type on phylogenetic trees. The ancestor of the Rosaceae, for example, probably had a dry follicle or achene-like fruit, and various fleshy fruits later evolved in different branches: the drupe (*Prunus*), the aggregate of drupelets (*Rubus*), the aggregate of achenes with fleshy receptacle (*Fragaria*) and the pome (the apple tribe) ([7.12.43](/7.12.43)).
- Developmental basis of dehiscence: in *Arabidopsis* the dehiscence zone between the valves and the replum of the silique is defined by a group of transcription factors (such as SHATTERPROOF, INDEHISCENT and FRUITFULL), and mutations can make the silique indehiscent or turn the whole fruit into a dehiscence zone. Tomato ripening is also controlled by transcription factors such as the MADS-box genes. The action of these genes suggests that transitions between fruit types may require only a change in the expression domains of a few regulatory genes.
- Domestication: the commonest change in the fruits of cultivated crops compared with their wild ancestors is the loss of the ability to disperse the seeds by themselves. In wild rice and wild wheat the seeds fall off when ripe (shattering), whereas in cultivars the seeds stay on the ear awaiting harvest. This trait is controlled by a few genes (such as *sh4* in rice and the brittle-rachis genes in wheat) and was one of the first traits selected by humans. Other domestication traits are larger fruits, seedlessness, reduced bitter compounds and uniform ripening.

## Questions

1. Among peach, watermelon, apple and pineapple, which are true fruits? Among peach, strawberry, raspberry, star anise, mulberry and fig, which are multiple fruits? Give reasons.

   **Answer** Only the peach is a true fruit: its pericarp develops entirely from the ovary wall of a superior ovary (a drupe). The watermelon is a pepo, developed jointly from an inferior ovary and the receptacle; the apple is a pome, whose fleshy part is mainly hypanthium; the pineapple is a multiple fruit with the inflorescence axis and bracts contributing. All three are accessory fruits. In the second group there are two multiple fruits: the mulberry (developed from a whole pistillate inflorescence, with the perianth of each flower becoming fleshy) and the fig (the inflorescence axis of an enclosed inflorescence becomes fleshy). The peach is a simple fruit; the strawberry (aggregate of achenes on a fleshy receptacle), raspberry (aggregate of drupelets) and star anise (aggregate of follicles) are aggregate fruits formed from the free carpels of one flower.

2. Is the "grain" of rice, the "seed" of sunflower, the lotus "seed" and the peanut a fruit or a seed? Of what type is each?

   **Answer** Brown rice, that is, rice with the glumes removed, is a caryopsis (pericarp fused with the seed coat); the grain with its glumes is a caryopsis plus the bracts of the spikelet (lemma and palea). The sunflower "seed" is a cypsela (formed from an inferior ovary; inside the hard shell of the pericarp is one seed, and only the "kernel" is the seed). The lotus "seed" is a nut, borne in the seed head (enlarged receptacle), and the whole seed head is an aggregate of nuts. The peanut "shell" is an indehiscent legume, and the "peanut" inside is the seed.

3. How do you distinguish loculicidal, septicidal and septifragal dehiscence of a capsule? What would you see in an opened lily fruit?

   **Answer** Look at where the opening is and what happens to the septa. In loculicidal dehiscence the slit lies along the back (midrib) of each carpel, the opening leads straight into the locule, and the septum remains attached to the middle of the valve. In septicidal dehiscence the slit follows the junction (septum) of adjacent carpels, the carpels separate from one another, and each valve is one complete carpel. In septifragal dehiscence the valves, wherever they split, separate from the central axis, while the septa remain attached to the axis. The lily is a three-carpel capsule with axile placentation and loculicidal dehiscence: the fruit splits at the top along the three dorsal sutures into three valves, each carrying a septum in its middle, and the seeds are exposed in the three locules.

4. Use the terminal-velocity model to explain why most wind-dispersed seeds are very small or bear a pappus or wings. If the mass of a samara doubles while its wing area stays the same, how does its mean dispersal distance change in the same wind?

   **Answer** The terminal velocity $v_{\mathrm{t}} = \sqrt{2mg/(\rho C_{\mathrm{D}} S)}$ is proportional to the square root of the wing loading $mg/S$, and the mean dispersal distance $x \approx Hu/v_{\mathrm{t}}$ is inversely proportional to the terminal velocity. A small seed has a small mass, and a pappus or wing increases the frontal area and the drag coefficient, both reducing the wing loading or $v_{\mathrm{t}}$; the seed stays longer in the air and is carried farther by the wind. If the mass doubles with the wing area unchanged, the wing loading doubles, $v_{\mathrm{t}}$ increases by a factor $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$, and the dispersal distance shortens to about $1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.71$ of its former value. This shows a trade-off between seed size (which provides the seedling with more reserves) and dispersal distance.

5. Why is "accessory fruit" not a fruit type? Name three fruits whose edible part comes from structures other than the ovary, and say what this suggests about the evolution of fruits.

   **Answer** "Accessory fruit" says only that structures other than the ovary contribute to the fruit. The contributing structure may be the receptacle (strawberry), hypanthium (apple, pear), perianth (mulberry), inflorescence axis and bracts (pineapple, fig) or involucre (the cupule of chestnut), and these differ entirely in form and structure, so "accessory fruit" is a description of origin and not a morphological type. These examples show that fleshy structures that attract animals have evolved independently from different organs in different groups, as convergent adaptations to the same dispersal function. Fruit classification can therefore only be artificial and descriptive, and in judging relationships one must look at the origin of the structures and not merely at similar outward form.
