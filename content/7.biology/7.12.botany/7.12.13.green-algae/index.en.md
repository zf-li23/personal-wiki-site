The green algae are the largest and morphologically most diverse group of algae. They range from marine picoplankton less than 1 µm across to seaweeds tens of centimeters long that consist of a single giant multinucleate cell, and almost every type of algal body plan has arisen among them. They share with land plants the same photosynthetic pigments (chlorophylls *a* and *b*), the same storage product (starch inside the plastid) and similar flagella, and for a long time they were regarded as the "ancestors of the higher plants." Molecular phylogenetics has made this picture precise: the traditional "green algae" belong to two lineages of the **Viridiplantae**. The Chlorophyta, one of these lineages, diversified in the sea and in fresh water and never moved onto land as embryophytes; land plants arose in the other lineage, the **Streptophyta**, from among the streptophyte algae.

This chapter treats the Chlorophyta. We first define the Viridiplantae and the Chlorophyta (§13.1), then describe green algal cell structure and the mode of cell division that distinguishes the two lineages (§13.2) and reproduction (§13.3). We then survey representative genera under the traditional orders, with the current classes alongside (§13.4), and finally summarize the evolutionary trends of the Chlorophyta and their significance as model organisms (§13.5). The Zygnematophyceae and Charophyceae, traditionally included in the green algae, belong to the Streptophyta and are discussed in the next chapter together with the origin of land plants ([7.12.14](/7.12.14)). General algal concepts (thallus, pyrenoid, spores and life cycle types) are in [7.12.6](/7.12.6); a cytological overview of green algae is in [7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.8.2).

## 13.1. Green Plants and the Chlorophyta

### 13.1.1. The Two Lineages of Green Plants

The **Viridiplantae** are the lineage of the **Archaeplastida** whose plastids contain chlorophylls *a* and *b*, lack phycobilisomes, and store starch inside the plastid ([7.12.1](/7.12.1), [7.12.7](/7.12.7#sec-7.1)). Their plastids, like those of the red algae and glaucophytes, derive from a single **primary endosymbiosis**; they have only two envelope membranes and no chloroplast endoplasmic reticulum. Phylogenomics divides the Viridiplantae into two **clades**:

- the Chlorophyta: most marine and freshwater green algae, including *Chlamydomonas*, *Volvox*, *Chlorella*, *Ulva* (sea lettuce), *Cladophora*, *Codium*, *Acetabularia*, and the marine picoplanktonic green algae;
- the **Streptophyta**: the streptophyte algae (charophytes in the broad sense) and all land plants.

The two lineages diverged in the Neoproterozoic or earlier; most molecular-clock estimates lie around one billion years ago. *Proterocladus antiquus*, from the roughly one-billion-year-old Nanfen Formation of Liaoning, is the oldest accepted green algal fossil and resembles the modern Cladophorales (Tang et al., 2020). Earlier "green algal fossils" in older textbooks (for example multinucleate algae more than a billion years old) are mostly unreliable identifications. A 2020 analysis of the *Prasinoderma* genome suggested that the small group of marine unicellular algae it represents may be a third lineage of the green plants, the Prasinodermophyta, sister to the other two (Li et al., 2020); this treatment has not been generally adopted.

Traditional classification placed all green algae other than the stoneworts in a single division, the Chlorophyta, and treated the stoneworts as a class or division of their own. From the 1970s, electron microscopic and biochemical studies of cell division, the flagellar apparatus and photorespiratory enzymes first showed that green algae follow two distinct lines (§13.5.3), and molecular phylogenetics later confirmed this. "Green algae" therefore has two meanings today: in the narrow sense it means the Chlorophyta; in the broad sense it means all algae among the green plants, that is, all green plants except the embryophytes, which form a **paraphyletic group**.

**Definition 13.1.1 (Green Algae)** The **green algae** (in the narrow sense) are the Chlorophyta of the green plants: algae whose plastids contain chlorophylls *a* and *b* and store starch inside the plastid, whose motile cells usually bear two or four apical, equal, whiplash flagella, and whose flagellar bases carry four microtubular roots arranged in a cross. In the broad sense the green algae also include the streptophyte algae of the Streptophyta.

### 13.1.2. Classes of the Chlorophyta

In the current classification the Chlorophyta comprise several early-diverging groups of small marine flagellates and a "core chlorophyte" clade (Leliaert et al., 2012). The core chlorophytes are the Chlorodendrophyceae, Trebouxiophyceae, Ulvophyceae, Chlorophyceae and the small Pedinophyceae.

| Group | Main features | Representative genera |
|---|---|---|
| Palmophyllophyceae | Gelatinous colonies or single cells in deep, dim water; possibly the earliest-diverging lineage of the Chlorophyta | *Palmophyllum*, *Prasinococcus* |
| Prasinophytes: Mamiellophyceae, Pyramimonadophyceae, Nephroselmidophyceae, etc. | Unicellular, mostly flagellate, body usually covered with organic scales; important marine phytoplankton; not monophyletic | *Micromonas*, *Ostreococcus*, *Pyramimonas* |
| Core chlorophytes: Chlorodendrophyceae | Four flagella; scales on the cell surface fused into a theca | *Tetraselmis* |
| Core chlorophytes: Trebouxiophyceae | Mostly nonmotile single cells or simple colonies; terrestrial, aerial, or symbiotic with fungi or animals | *Trebouxia*, *Chlorella*, *Prasiola* |
| Core chlorophytes: Ulvophyceae | Mostly marine macroalgae; filaments, sheets or tubes, often multinucleate | *Ulva*, *Ulothrix*, *Cladophora*, *Codium*, *Acetabularia* |
| Core chlorophytes: Chlorophyceae | Mostly freshwater; with a phycoplast; flagellar basal bodies arranged clockwise or directly opposed | *Chlamydomonas*, *Volvox*, *Scenedesmus*, *Oedogonium* |

The relationships among the three large core classes are not fully resolved, and in some analyses the Ulvophyceae are not monophyletic. Chinese textbooks (Wu Guofang, vol. 2) divide the Chlorophyta into the Chlorophyceae and the Charophyceae, with 13 orders in the Chlorophyceae; *Freshwater Algae of China* (Hu Hongjun and Wei Yinxin) divides the green algae into the Chlorophyceae, Conjugatophyceae, Charophyceae and others. These older orders were defined mostly by body form; §13.4.1 gives their correspondence to the current classes.

## 13.2. General Features of the Chlorophyta

### 13.2.1. Body Form

Green algae display almost the whole range of algal body types ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.1.2)), and the same type often arose independently in different classes:

- Unicellular: motile with flagella (*Chlamydomonas*) or nonmotile (*Chlorella*, *Chlorococcum*).
- Colonial: many cells held together by mucilage. An indefinite colony, whose cell number and arrangement are not fixed and can keep increasing, is exemplified by the palmelloid colonies that *Chlamydomonas* forms under unfavorable conditions; a definite colony has its cell number fixed when the colony is formed.
- Filamentous: unbranched uniseriate filaments (*Ulothrix*, *Oedogonium*) or branched filaments (*Cladophora*, *Chaetophora*); a filament differentiated into a prostrate and an erect part is heterotrichous (*Stigeoclonium*, *Fritschiella*).
- Sheets and tubes: membranous thalli one or two cells thick, and hollow tubes (*Ulva*, *Enteromorpha*).
- Siphonous (coenocytic) thalli: the body is a single undivided giant cell containing thousands of nuclei, with cross walls only when reproductive organs form (*Codium*, *Caulerpa*, *Acetabularia*). The body of *Cladophora* is made of many multinucleate cells and is intermediate between a filament and a siphonous thallus.

**Definition 13.2.1 (Coenobium)** A colony composed of a definite number of cells, whose cell number and arrangement are fixed when the colony forms and which afterward only enlarges without adding cells, is a **coenobium**. A coenobium reproduces by having each cell form a daughter colony resembling the parent (an autocolony).

*Gonium*, *Pandorina*, *Volvox*, *Scenedesmus*, *Pediastrum* and *Hydrodictyon* all form coenobia. The cell number of a coenobium is usually a power of 2 (4, 8, 16, 32, …), because the daughter colony arises from a single cell that divides a fixed number of times and only then assembles into a colony.

By the shape of the chloroplast, green algal cells are often classified into two "cell types." The *Chlamydomonas* type has one cup-shaped chloroplast and two apical, equal flagella (all members of the Volvocales are built of *Chlamydomonas*-type cells). The *Ulothrix* type has a sheet- or band-shaped chloroplast with one to several pyrenoids, as in the filamentous and sheet-like forms such as *Ulothrix* and *Ulva*.

### 13.2.2. Cell Structure

- Cell wall: most species have a wall, but its chemical composition varies greatly. Chinese textbooks often describe it as "cellulose inside and pectin outside," which applies only to some filamentous and sheet-like species. The walls of *Chlamydomonas* and *Volvox* contain no cellulose but consist of a crystalline layer of hydroxyproline-rich glycoproteins; the wall of *Cladophora* is highly crystalline cellulose, with an outer layer of insoluble material (once thought to be chitin); the walls of siphonous algae such as *Codium* and *Caulerpa* have a framework of xylan or mannan; the wall of *Ulva* contains a sulfated polysaccharide (ulvan); prasinophytes have no wall and are covered with organic scales.
- Chloroplast: homologous with the chloroplast of land plants, with two envelope membranes and thylakoids stacked in bundles of 2–6 or more, forming grana-like stacks in places. The pigments are chlorophylls *a* and *b*, α- and β-carotene and xanthophylls; siphonous algae also contain siphonaxanthin and siphonein, which let them use the green light that penetrates to depth. Chloroplast shape is an important criterion for distinguishing genera: cup-shaped (*Chlamydomonas*), sheet-like (*Ulva*), girdle-shaped (*Ulothrix*), net-like (*Oedogonium*, *Cladophora*), spiral ribbons (*Spirogyra*), stellate (*Zygnema*), flat plates (*Mougeotia*), and many discs (*Codium*).
- Pyrenoid: the chloroplasts of most species contain one to several **pyrenoids**, whose core is protein consisting mainly of Rubisco, surrounded by a starch sheath (sheathed pyrenoids); pyrenoids without a starch sheath are naked. In *Chlamydomonas*, Rubisco is cross-linked by a linker protein (EPYC1) into a liquid condensate, which is the site of the $\ce{CO2}$-concentrating mechanism ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.2.5)). Among land plants only the hornworts retain pyrenoids ([7.12.15](/7.12.15)).
- Storage product: **starch**, composed of amylose and amylopectin as in land plants, formed inside the chloroplast and usually accumulated around the pyrenoid; it stains blue with iodine. A few species store oil droplets.
- Nucleus: usually one; the mature cells of siphonous algae, *Cladophora* and *Hydrodictyon* are multinucleate. Mitosis is described in §13.2.4.
- Eyespot: motile cells usually have an orange-red eyespot, made of one or more layers of carotenoid-containing lipid globules between the thylakoids inside the chloroplast, at one side of the anterior end (unlike the eyespot of euglenids, which lies in the cytoplasm). The eyespot itself is not photosensitive; it shades and reflects light so that the photoreceptor in the plasma membrane directly above it, **channelrhodopsin**, can detect the direction of the light.
- Contractile vacuoles: freshwater motile cells usually have two contractile vacuoles at the flagellar bases, which contract alternately and expel the water that enters the cell.
- Flagella: motile cells usually have two or four equal apical flagella (a ring of many in *Oedogonium*) with smooth surfaces and no mastigonemes; they are whiplash (acronematic) flagella ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.2.3)) with a 9 + 2 axoneme.

### 13.2.3. The Flagellar Apparatus

In the cell, the basal bodies of the flagella are connected to bundles of microtubules, the flagellar roots, which anchor the flagella and organize the cytoskeleton at the anterior end of the cell. In the Chlorophyta there are four roots, arranged in a cross in the "X–2–X–2" pattern (roots of X microtubules alternate with roots of 2), and the whole apparatus shows 180° rotational symmetry. The relative position of the two basal bodies differs among classes: clockwise (CW) in most Chlorophyceae (*Chlamydomonas*) or directly opposed (DO) in the Sphaeropleales (*Scenedesmus* and relatives), and counterclockwise (CCW) in the Ulvophyceae and Trebouxiophyceae. The motile cells of the Streptophyta (zoospores and sperm of the stoneworts and *Coleochaete*, and sperm of bryophytes and ferns) are entirely different: the flagella are lateral or subapical, there is a single broad microtubular root, a multilayered structure (MLS) lies at the base, and the whole apparatus is asymmetric. The flagellar apparatus of land plant sperm continues this asymmetric structure, and this was one of the first shared features of the Streptophyta to be recognized.

### 13.2.4. Cell Division: Phycoplast and Phragmoplast

The Chlorophyta and the Streptophyta differ in a related set of features of mitosis and cytokinesis, first revealed by electron microscopy (Pickett-Heaps, 1975).

**Definition 13.2.2 (Phycoplast)** In the Chlorophyceae, Trebouxiophyceae and some other green algae, the spindle breaks down at the end of mitosis, and a set of microtubules forms between the two closely spaced daughter nuclei, perpendicular to the spindle axis and parallel to the future division plane; this array is the **phycoplast**. The cleavage furrow or cell plate forms along the phycoplast.

The counterpart of the phycoplast is the **phragmoplast** of land plants ([7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.5.3)). The spindle does not break down at telophase; the remnants of the two sets of spindle fibers form a barrel-shaped microtubule system between two widely separated daughter nuclei, with the microtubules parallel to the spindle axis and perpendicular to the division plane. Golgi-derived vesicles travel along the microtubules to the middle and fuse into the **cell plate**, which expands from the center outward until it meets the parent cell wall; **plasmodesmata** are trapped in the plate as it forms.

| | Chlorophyceae, Trebouxiophyceae | Ulvophyceae | Early-diverging streptophyte algae | Charophyceae, Coleochaetophyceae, Zygnematophyceae, embryophytes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nuclear envelope | Persists (closed mitosis) or opens only at the poles | Persists | Breaks down (open mitosis) | Breaks down |
| Telophase spindle | Collapses; daughter nuclei close together | Persists; daughter nuclei far apart | Persists | Persists |
| Cytokinesis | Furrow (*Chlamydomonas*) or cell plate (*Scenedesmus*, *Chaetophora*) formed along the phycoplast | Furrow, no phycoplast | Furrow (*Klebsormidium*, *Mesostigma*) | Phragmoplast and cell plate (*Spirogyra* uses furrow and phragmoplast together) |
| **Preprophase band** | Absent | Absent | Absent | Only in embryophytes |

A cell plate formed along a phycoplast can also leave plasmodesmata (*Chaetophora*, *Fritschiella*), but the two kinds of plasmodesmata develop differently and are not homologous. The **preprophase band**, which appears at the future division plane before division in land plants, has not been found in any alga.

### 13.2.5. Distribution

About 90% of green algae live in fresh water and about 10% are marine, but the marine green algae are mostly large ulvophycean seaweeds and tiny prasinophytes, whereas in fresh water the unicellular, colonial and filamentous Chlorophyceae and Trebouxiophyceae predominate. Freshwater green algae occur in rivers, lakes, ponds, paddy fields and puddles. Many live on damp soil, rock, walls and tree trunks (aerial algae such as *Trentepohlia*), some on snow and ice (snow algae), some form **lichens** with fungi (*Trebouxia*), some live inside animals (zoochlorellae in *Paramecium* and *Hydra*), and a few are parasites in plant leaves (*Cephaleuros*). Marine species mostly attach to rocks in the intertidal and shallow subtidal zones, and their distribution is controlled by water temperature.

## 13.3. Reproduction

### 13.3.1. Vegetative Reproduction and Asexual Reproduction

Vegetative reproduction is mainly by cell division and fragmentation. Unicellular species multiply by cell division; filaments break because of water turbulence, grazing, or empty cells left after spore release, and each fragment grows into a new individual. Many unicellular green algae lose their flagella and gelatinize their walls under unfavorable conditions; the protoplasts divide repeatedly without separating and are embedded in mucilage as an indefinite palmelloid colony (the palmella stage), and when conditions improve each cell grows flagella and swims out.

Asexual reproduction produces spores ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.4.1)):

- **Zoospores**: wall-less cells with two or four flagella, built like a *Chlamydomonas* cell. The cell that forms zoospores often does not differ from a vegetative cell; its protoplast divides into a power of 2 of daughter protoplasts, usually at night, and they are released at dawn. After swimming for a time, a zoospore retracts or sheds its flagella, secretes a wall and grows into a new individual.
- **Aplanospores**: walled spores without flagella. An aplanospore that already has the shape of the parent cell is an **autospore** (*Chlorella*, *Scenedesmus*); it has the form of the adult within the parent cell wall.
- Akinetes and hypnospores: formed when a vegetative cell (or aplanospore) accumulates reserves and thickens its wall, enabling it to survive drought and cold (*Pithophora*, *Oedogonium*).

### 13.3.2. Sexual Reproduction

All three types of sexual reproduction occur in the Chlorophyta, and in the Volvocales, the *Ulothrix*–*Ulva* group and the siphonous algae each evolved in parallel from isogamy to oogamy:

- **Isogamy**: the two gametes are alike in shape, size and motility (most *Chlamydomonas*, *Ulothrix*, *Ulva*, *Cladophora*, *Gonium*).
- **Anisogamy**: gametes of the same shape but different size and motility (*Pandorina*, *Eudorina*, *Codium*, *Bryopsis*, some *Chlamydomonas*).
- **Oogamy**: a large nonmotile egg fuses with a small motile sperm (*Volvox*, *Oedogonium*, *Sphaeroplea*, *Dichotomosiphon*). Eggs are produced in an oogonium and sperm in an antheridium; in the Chlorophyta both are single cells with no covering of sterile cells.

After the gametes fuse, the zygote either germinates without a rest (*Ulva*, *Codium*) or secretes a thick wall and becomes a dormant zygote (*Chlamydomonas*, *Volvox*, *Oedogonium*). In most species the zygote wall bears spines or sculpturing, and the reserve changes from starch to red oil. Depending on whether gametes can fuse with those of the same individual, mating is homothallic or heterothallic; *Chlamydomonas*, *Ulothrix* and *Ulva* are mostly heterothallic, with gametes of two mating types ($mt^+$ and $mt^-$).

The position of meiosis determines the life cycle ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.4.3)). The Chlorophyceae and most freshwater green algae have **zygotic meiosis** and a haploid vegetative body only. The Ulvales, most Cladophorales and many other ulvophyceans have **sporic meiosis**, with **isomorphic alternation of generations** or **heteromorphic alternation of generations**. Siphonous algae such as *Codium*, *Caulerpa* and *Acetabularia* have **gametic meiosis** and a diploid vegetative body.

### 13.4.2. Volvocales: *Chlamydomonas*

The vegetative cells of the Volvocales (Chlorophyceae, Chlamydomonadales) are all of the *Chlamydomonas* type, with two apical equal flagella, and remain motile throughout life; the body is a single cell or a coenobium.

The genus *Chlamydomonas* has about 500 species (Hu and Wei). It lives in small bodies of water rich in organic matter and multiplies in large numbers in early spring and late autumn, turning the water green; a few species live on snow and color snowfields red with abundant carotenoids (the snow alga *Chlamydomonas nivalis*, some of which are now placed in *Sanguina*). The body is an ovoid or spherical single cell about 10 µm long (panel A of the figure below):

- At the anterior end are two equal flagella, often arising from a papilla; two contractile vacuoles lie at the flagellar bases.
- The cell wall is made of glycoproteins, with a mucilage layer outside.
- There is a single thick-bottomed, cup-shaped chloroplast occupying most of the cell, with a pyrenoid with a starch sheath in the base of the cup and an orange-red eyespot at one side of the anterior end.
- The nucleus lies in the hollow of the cup-shaped chloroplast, and the cytoplasm contains mitochondria, a Golgi apparatus and other organelles.

Asexual reproduction: the vegetative cell comes to rest, the flagella are retracted or shed, and the protoplast divides longitudinally one to four times within the parent wall to form 2–16 daughter protoplasts, each of which secretes a wall and grows flagella; they are released when the parent wall gelatinizes and ruptures, and act as zoospores. In damp soil and similar habitats, the daughter cells grow no flagella and form a palmelloid colony embedded in the gelatinized parent wall.

Sexual reproduction: most species are isogamous, a few anisogamous (*C. braunii*) or oogamous (for example *Oogamochlamys*, formerly in *Chlamydomonas*). In the model species *C. reinhardtii*, gametes differentiate under nitrogen starvation: vegetative cells become gametes directly or after one or two mitotic divisions, and the gametes resemble vegetative cells in form. Gametes of the two mating types recognize and adhere to each other by agglutinins at the flagellar tips; an adhesion signal in the flagella causes the cell walls to dissolve, a fertilization tube extended by one mating type fuses with the other, and the two gametes unite into a quadriflagellate zygote that swims for a few hours. The zygote then rounds up, loses its flagella and secretes a thick, spiny wall, becoming a dormant zygote (zygospore) that tolerates drought; when conditions are favorable it germinates, undergoing meiosis to produce four (sometimes eight, after a further mitosis) haploid motile cells that break out of the wall (panel B). The vegetative cells of *Chlamydomonas* are haploid, and the zygote is the only diploid cell in the life cycle, so meiosis is zygotic (haplontic cycle). The four cells from a single zygote can be separated and cultured individually, so *Chlamydomonas*, like yeast, permits tetrad analysis ([7.4.9](/7.4.9)).

*Chlamydomonas* is the best-studied green alga, and *C. reinhardtii* is a **model organism** for photosynthesis, flagella (cilia) and photoreception. Its photosynthetic mutants can grow heterotrophically on acetate, so mutants completely unable to photosynthesize can be maintained. **Intraflagellar transport** was first discovered in its flagella, and many genes of human ciliopathies have *Chlamydomonas* orthologs ([7.3.14](/7.3.14#sec-14.6.3)). Channelrhodopsin in the plasma membrane of the eyespot region is a light-gated cation channel and later became the main tool of **optogenetics**. The nuclear genome of *C. reinhardtii* is about 120 million base pairs and was sequenced in 2007.

Other unicellular members of the Volvocales are *Dunaliella* and *Haematococcus*. *Dunaliella* has no cell wall and lives in salt lakes and salterns, balancing the hyperosmotic environment with glycerol accumulated in the cell; *D. salina* accumulates large amounts of β-carotene and is an industrial source of natural carotenoids. Under strong light and nutrient deficiency, *Haematococcus* forms red, thick-walled spores that accumulate astaxanthin and is used to produce it. *Tetraspora* (formerly the Tetrasporales) is a gelatinous colony in which cells are often arranged in groups of two or four near the periphery of the mucilage, each with two nonmotile pseudocilia; it shows that nonmotile colonies can arise from *Chlamydomonas*-like ancestors by loss of motility.

### 13.4.3. Volvocales: The Volvocine Series

The coenobial members of the Volvocales form a series by cell number and degree of differentiation (table and figure below). In every genus each cell is of the *Chlamydomonas* type and has its own mucilage sheath, and the colony is enclosed in a common mucilage envelope.

| Genus | Cell number | Colony form | Cell differentiation | Sexual reproduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Gonium* | 4–32 (usually 16) | Square flat plate, cells in one layer, joined by mucilage into a net | None; every cell can form a daughter colony | Isogamy |
| *Pandorina* | 4–32 (usually 16) | Sphere; cells tightly packed with no gaps | None | Anisogamy (or isogamy) |
| *Eudorina* | 16–64 (usually 32) | Sphere or ellipsoid; cells separate, at the periphery of the envelope, with a large central cavity | In a few species the anterior cells are sterile | Anisogamy, close to oogamy |
| *Pleodorina* | 32–128 | As in *Eudorina* | Small anterior cells are vegetative; large posterior cells are reproductive | Anisogamy or oogamy |
| *Volvox* | 512 to tens of thousands | Hollow sphere 0.5–3 mm across, filled with mucilage and water | The great majority of cells are sterile somatic cells; a few are gonidia | Oogamy |

*Volvox* often appears in freshwater ponds and temporary pools in summer and disappears after two or three weeks (data in the table are from Wu Guofang and Bao Wenmei). The cells of the colony form a single layer, and the mucilage sheaths of neighboring cells press against one another, so that the surface appears polygonal; in many species neighboring cells are joined by cytoplasmic strands (cytoplasmic bridges like plasmodesmata). The flagella of all cells point outward and the colony swims forward rotating; the cells at the anterior (light-facing) end have larger eyespots.

Asexual reproduction: a few cells in the posterior half of the colony lose their flagella and enlarge to more than ten times the size of a vegetative cell; these are called gonidia. A gonidium divides repeatedly to form a curved, dish-shaped plate of cells (plakea) with the anterior ends of the cells facing inward, which continues to divide into a hollow sphere with an opening (the phialopore) at one end; the whole sphere then turns itself inside out through the phialopore (inversion), so that the flagellated ends of the cells now face the surface, and a daughter colony is formed. The daughter colonies swim in the cavity of the parent colony and are released when it ruptures; before release, they can in turn form grand-daughter colonies. In *V. carteri*, the asymmetric division of the gonidium early on separates large gonidial precursors from small somatic precursors; once differentiated, the somatic cells die and do not divide again. The study of *regA* and other genes controlling somatic cell fate is treated in [7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5.2).

Sexual reproduction is oogamous. A few gonidia become male or female gonidia. A male gonidium divides and inverts as in asexual reproduction, forming a plate-like or spherical sperm packet of 16–512 sperm, which swims as a unit to the vicinity of an egg before dissociating; a female gonidium does not divide but enlarges into a single nonmotile egg. The sperm penetrate the mucilage sheath of the egg and fuse with it, and the zygote secretes a thick wall (smooth or spiny) and can survive for years. Before germination the zygote undergoes meiosis, but only one haploid nucleus is retained, forming a single zoospore (or aplanospore), which then divides and inverts as in asexual reproduction and grows into a new colony. In *V. carteri*, sexual reproduction is triggered by a sex-inducing glycoprotein secreted by the colony, which converts vegetative colonies to sexual ones at concentrations as low as about $10^{-16}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$; heat stress can induce colonies to produce the inducer, so that the population shifts to sexual reproduction and forms stress-resistant zygotes before conditions deteriorate.

From left to right: *Chlamydomonas* (single cell), *Gonium* (16 cells in a plate), *Pandorina* (16 cells tightly packed in a sphere), *Eudorina* (32 cells at the periphery of the envelope, with a central cavity), *Pleodorina* (small vegetative cells anteriorly, large reproductive cells posteriorly), *Volvox* (thousands of somatic cells and a few gonidia, with daughter colonies in the cavity). From *Chlamydomonas* to *Volvox*, three parallel trends can be seen: the body goes from a single cell through a colony to a multicellular organism; the nutritive and reproductive functions go from being undivided to being divided between cell types; and sexual reproduction goes from isogamy through anisogamy to oogamy. Molecular phylogenetics shows that this series is not a linear evolutionary sequence: *Volvox* is polyphyletic, cell differentiation and oogamy arose independently at least two or three times in the Volvocaceae, and reversals occurred in individual lineages (Herron and Michod, 2008). The volvocine series is therefore a model for how **multicellularity** and germ cell differentiation evolved; the evolutionary models are treated in [7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5.2) and are not repeated here.

### 13.4.4. Chlorococcalean Algae

The vegetative cells of the Chlorococcales have no flagella and no vegetative cell division: the cell only grows, and then reproduces by zoospores or autospores. In the current classification this order is distributed among the Chlamydomonadales and Sphaeropleales of the Chlorophyceae and the Trebouxiophyceae (§13.4.1).

*Chlorococcum* (Chlorophyceae, Chlamydomonadales) is mostly aerial, living on damp soil, rock and tree trunks. It is a spherical single cell of very variable size; young cells are uninucleate, with one cup-shaped chloroplast and one pyrenoid, while old cells are multinucleate, with chloroplasts dispersed throughout the cell and the wall irregularly thickened and layered. It reproduces by producing 8 or 16 biflagellate zoospores; sexual reproduction is isogamous, with zygotic meiosis.

*Chlorella* (Trebouxiophyceae) is a unicellular planktonic alga with spherical cells 2–10 µm in diameter and a cup- or sheet-shaped chloroplast; most species have a pyrenoid. It is very widely distributed, occurring in fresh water, brackish water, damp soil and bark, and some forms (zoochlorellae) live inside the cells of animals such as *Paramecium* and *Hydra*. In asexual reproduction the protoplast divides into 2, 4, 8 or 16 autospores, released when the parent wall ruptures (four daughter cells crowded within the parent wall are often seen); no sexual reproduction has been found. *Chlorella* grows fast and about half of its dry weight is protein, so it is used as feed and health food. In the mid-20th century Calvin and colleagues traced $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ in suspension cultures of *Chlorella* and *Scenedesmus* to elucidate the pathway of photosynthetic carbon assimilation. "*Chlorella pyrenoidosa*" is a name often used in older literature but is in fact a composite of several different species.

*Scenedesmus* (Chlorophyceae, Sphaeropleales) is one of the most common planktonic algae of fresh water. Its coenobium usually consists of 4 or 8 cells arranged with their long axes parallel in one row or alternately in two rows; the cells are ellipsoidal, spindle-shaped or crescent-shaped, with a wall that is smooth or bears spines, teeth, ridges and granules, and the end cells often carry long spines. In asexual reproduction the protoplast of each cell divides transversely and then longitudinally to form an autocolony identical to the parent colony, which is released through a longitudinal slit in the parent wall. The colony form is plastic: in water containing chemical signals released by *Daphnia*, unicellular forms produce larger colonies that are harder to ingest, a classic example of inducible defense. In the current classification the spiny species are separated as *Desmodesmus*.

*Pediastrum* (Chlorophyceae, Sphaeropleales) forms a coenobium of 4–128 cells in a flat disc one cell thick; the cells of the outer ring often have 1–4 projections, and the colony may or may not be perforated. Mature cells are multinucleate, with a net-like chloroplast. In asexual reproduction one cell forms as many zoospores as the parent colony has cells; the zoospores are released through a rupture in the parent wall together with a thin vesicle, swim briefly in the vesicle, then come to rest and arrange themselves into a new colony. Sexual reproduction is isogamous, and the zygote undergoes meiosis without a rest to produce four zoospores, each of which secretes a thick wall and becomes a polygonal resting cell (polyeder); when a polyeder germinates it again produces as many zoospores as the parent colony had cells, which form a new colony.

The coenobium of *Hydrodictyon* (Chlorophyceae, Sphaeropleales) is a large saccate net up to several tens of centimeters long; the meshes are mostly pentagons or hexagons, each formed of 5–6 long cylindrical multinucleate cells joined end to end. In asexual reproduction thousands of zoospores form in a single cell; they do not leave the parent cell but swim in it for a time, then lose their flagella, arrange themselves into a net and secrete walls, growing into a small net inside the parent cell, which is released when the parent wall ruptures. The net then enlarges only by growth of its cells and does not divide again. Sexual reproduction is isogamous, and the zygote germinates by forming polyeders as well. *Hydrodictyon* multiplies rapidly in fish ponds and entangles fry.

### 13.4.5. Oedogoniales, Chaetophorales and Sphaeropleales

These three old orders all belong to the current Chlorophyceae; all are filamentous, and cell division involves a phycoplast.

The Oedogoniales comprise one family and three genera. *Oedogonium* is a common unbranched filament of fresh water, attached when young by a holdfast on the basal cell and later often floating. It has three distinctive features:

- Cell division: before division a ring-like thickening (the ring) forms on the inside of the lateral wall near the upper end of the cell; after nuclear division the lateral wall splits transversely at the ring, and the ring is stretched out to become the new lateral wall of the upper daughter cell, while the upper rim of the old wall remains at the top of the cell as a "cap." Each division leaves one cap, so the number of caps at the top of a cell records the number of times it has divided (apical cap cells).
- Flagella: zoospores, sperm and androspores bear a ring of many flagella at the anterior end (stephanokont).
- Oogamy: the oogonium is a swollen cell with one egg, which opens by a small pore in the lateral wall when mature; the antheridium is a series of flattened short cells, each producing two sperm. In some species the antheridia lie directly on filaments of normal size (macrandrous species); in others, androsporangia form first, and the androspores they produce swim to the oogonium or its vicinity, attach and germinate into dwarf males of only a few cells, which produce sperm at their tips (nannandrous species). After fertilization the zygote wall thickens and turns red, and after a rest the zygote undergoes meiosis to produce four zoospores.

*Bulbochaete* also belongs to the Oedogoniales; it is a unilaterally branched filament, most of whose cells bear a long hair with a bulbous base on one side (hence the name), and most species are nannandrous. Note that *Bulbochaete* and *Coleochaete* (a streptophyte) are completely different algae.

The Chaetophorales are branched heterotrichous filaments with an erect and a prostrate part, the branch tips often extended into colorless multicellular hairs, and most are embedded in mucilage. *Chaetophora* forms macroscopic mucilaginous balls attached to aquatic plants and stones; the erect part of *Stigeoclonium* is highly variable in form, so identification relies on the stable prostrate part; *Draparnaldia* has a stout main axis with tufts of short branchlets and lives in clean, cold water, where it is an indicator of good water quality. The zoospores have four flagella and, after settling, divide into an apical cell and a basal cell, the former growing into the erect filament and the latter into the prostrate filament and rhizoids. *Fritschiella tuberosa* lives in the surface layer of damp soil, with subterranean prostrate parts and colorless rhizoids, primary erect branches at the surface and secondary erect branches projecting above the soil; some cells divide longitudinally into multiseriate regions, the surface has a cuticle-like covering, and isomorphic alternation of generations has been reported. Its degree of adaptation to land is rare among green algae, and Bower and others considered that land plants arose from this kind of green alga (§13.5.3); it is now known to be a member of the Chlorophyceae, and these features are the result of convergent evolution.

*Sphaeroplea* is an unbranched, free-floating long filament without a holdfast; the cells are 3–20 times as long as wide and multinucleate, and the large central vacuole is divided by a series of transverse cytoplasmic partitions with ring-shaped chloroplasts arranged in them. Sexual reproduction is oogamous: the antheridia produce large numbers of biflagellate sperm, the oogonia form many eggs, and the sperm enter through small pores in the oogonial wall; the zygote forms a patterned, thick red wall within the oogonium and undergoes meiosis after a rest.

### 13.4.6. Ulotrichales, Ulvales and Cladophorales

These three old orders belong to the current Ulvophyceae and are mostly marine macroalgae.

*Ulothrix* lives in flowing fresh water, where it grows in tufts on rock in waterfalls and rapids as green, slimy, velvety growths; a few species are marine. The body is an unbranched uniseriate filament; the basal cell is differentiated as a holdfast and the others are short cylinders, each with a girdle-shaped chloroplast (the *Ulothrix*-type cell) containing several pyrenoids. All cells except the holdfast can produce zoospores: one cell forms 2–32 quadriflagellate zoospores, which are released through a small pore in the lateral wall and, after settling, divide transversely into the holdfast and the first cell of the filament. In sexual reproduction one cell produces 8–64 biflagellate gametes, and only gametes from different filaments fuse (heterothallic isogamy); after a rest the zygote undergoes meiosis and produces zoospores or aplanospores. Asexual and sexual reproduction can occur on the same filament. Older textbooks used *Ulothrix* as a typical example of the haplontic cycle, but it is now known that in some species the zygote first grows into a unicellular, stalked diploid stage (the *Codiolum* stage) before meiosis, which is in effect an extremely heteromorphic alternation of generations. *Monostroma*, a membranous thallus one cell thick, has the same unicellular diploid stage ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.4.3)).

*Ulva* (Ulvales) grows on intertidal rocks, is commonly called sea lettuce and is edible. The body is a large sheet two cells thick, elliptical, lanceolate or ribbon-shaped; cells in the lower part grow colorless tubular rhizoidal filaments toward the base, which grow downward between the two cell layers and interweave outside the thallus to form a perennial holdfast, from which new sheets grow each spring. The cells are tightly and irregularly packed, polygonal in surface view and rectangular in section; each cell is uninucleate, with the nucleus on the inner side and a sheet-like chloroplast with one pyrenoid on the outer side.

*Ulva* is a classic case of **isomorphic alternation of generations**:

- Sporophyte ($2n$): when mature, all cells except the base can form zoosporangia, and meiosis produces 8–16 haploid quadriflagellate zoospores, which settle on rock and germinate into gametophytes.
- Gametophyte ($n$): when mature the surface cells form gametangia, each producing 16–32 biflagellate gametes; only gametes from different thalli (the $+$ and $-$ mating types) can fuse, isogamously or slightly anisogamously, and the zygote germinates without a rest into a sporophyte.

The two kinds of plant are identical in appearance and can be distinguished only by the reproductive cells they produce (quadriflagellate zoospores or biflagellate gametes) or by chromosome number. The morphogenesis of *Ulva* depends on epiphytic bacteria: axenically cultured *Ulva* grows only as an irregular mass of cells and returns to the normal sheet only when particular marine bacteria (such as *Roseovarius* and *Maribacter*) or the morphogenetic substances they secrete are added (Spoerner et al., 2012), a model for algal–microbe interactions.

*Enteromorpha* in the old sense is a hollow tube one cell thick in the Ulvales, uniseriate when young and attached by a holdfast at the base, with the same life cycle as *Ulva*. Molecular evidence shows that the tubular and sheet-like forms have changed into one another several times within *Ulva*, so *Enteromorpha* has been merged into *Ulva* (Hayden et al., 2003); the scientific name of the "Enteromorpha" commonly eaten is *Ulva prolifera*, and the ribbon-like species is *U. clathrata*. *Ulva* can multiply explosively in eutrophic bays, and the huge quantities of floating thalli that pile up on the shore are called green tides. Since 2007 the Yellow Sea has experienced green tides, mainly of *U. prolifera*, almost every summer; in 2008 one affected the sailing venue of the Qingdao Olympics, and the floating thalli came mainly from *Ulva* epiphytic on nori culture rafts on the Jiangsu coast.

*Cladophora* (Cladophorales) occurs in both fresh and sea water and is a branched filament attached by its basal cell. The cells are long cylinders, multinucleate, with a large central vacuole and a net-like chloroplast pressed against the wall with many pyrenoids; the wall is thick, with an inner layer of highly crystalline cellulose and a tough outer layer that feels rough to the touch and often carries many epiphytic diatoms. Although every cell can divide, growth is mainly at the apical cells, and branches arise from the side of the cell apex, often looking dichotomous. Asexual reproduction is by quadriflagellate zoospores and sexual reproduction is isogamous; most marine species have isomorphic alternation of generations, while some freshwater species have none. *Cladophora* multiplies in large numbers in nutrient-rich lakes, clogging water intakes and rotting on shores. *Aegagropila linnaei*, also in the Cladophorales (called "marimo" in Japan), is rolled by currents on lake bottoms into green balls up to more than 20 cm in diameter. *Pithophora* forms dark, barrel-shaped thick-walled spores only at the tips or in the middle of branches and lives in still water.

*Trentepohlia* (Ulvophyceae, Trentepohliales) is an aerial branched filament that forms orange-red velvety patches on tree trunks, rocks and walls south of the Yangtze River; its cells accumulate large amounts of carotenoid-containing oil droplets (formerly called haematochrome) that mask the chlorophyll, and there are no pyrenoids. The sporangia fall off whole and are dispersed by wind, producing zoospores in damp conditions. *Trentepohlia* is the photosynthetic partner of many tropical lichens ([7.12.12](/7.12.12)); *Cephaleuros*, in the same order, is a parasite in the leaves of tea, citrus and other plants and causes algal leaf spot (red rust).

### 13.4.7. Siphonous Algae: Bryopsidales and Dasycladales

The siphonous algae of the old system (the Siphonales; now the Bryopsidales and Dasycladales of the Ulvophyceae) are overwhelmingly marine. The body is a coenocyte: a single undivided giant cell whose cytoplasm forms a thin layer against the wall, with many disc-shaped chloroplasts in the outer layer, many small nuclei in the inner layer and a large central vacuole, and cross walls form only when reproductive organs develop. Wounds in the coenocyte are sealed within minutes by gelation of the cytoplasm. Most species have a diploid vegetative body, with meiosis occurring when gametes form (gametic meiosis).

*Codium* (Bryopsidales) attaches to rocks in the low intertidal zone; the body is a spongy, dark green cylindrical branching mass of interwoven siphons, with antler-like dichotomous branching in appearance (dead man's fingers, *C. fragile*). The central siphons are slender, colorless and loosely arranged, forming the medulla; they give off lateral, swollen club-shaped short branches called utricles, which are tightly packed to form the cortex, and the chloroplasts lie mostly at the distal ends of the utricles; there are no pyrenoids. The walls of the medullary filaments often have inward-growing ring-shaped thickenings that give added support. In sexual reproduction, female and male gametangia arise on the sides of the utricles, separated from them by a cross wall; in a gametangium some nuclei degenerate while the others enlarge and undergo meiosis, each forming one biflagellate gamete. The female gametes are large and contain many chloroplasts, and the male gametes are small with only one or two chloroplasts (anisogamy). The zygote germinates immediately into a new diploid thallus. *C. magnum* from Mexico reaches more than 8 m in length; *C. fragile* has spread with shipping and shellfish culture and is an invasive species on temperate coasts.

*Caulerpa* (Bryopsidales) lives in shallow tropical and subtropical seas. The thallus has a creeping "stem," downward rhizoidal branches and erect "leaves," and it is named for its resemblance to a fern, but the whole thallus is a single cell, up to several meters long and one of the largest known single-celled organisms; numerous crisscrossing internal struts (trabeculae) support the cell cavity. In reproduction the reproductive part of the thallus grows many papilla-like projections (extrusion papillae) through which the gametes escape, with meiosis during gamete formation; the whole thallus dies after the gametes are released (holocarpy). *C. lentillifera* is eaten as "sea grapes." An aquarium strain of *C. taxifolia* escaped from the Monaco Oceanographic Museum into the Mediterranean in 1984 and has since spread rapidly by vegetative fragments, becoming a famous invasive seaweed.

In *Bryopsis* the erect branches are bipinnately branched and the creeping branches rhizoid-like; the base of the pinnules forms a cross wall to make a gametangium that produces biflagellate gametes of unequal size (anisogamy). The thallus of *Halimeda* is composed of many flat, calcified segments, and when the segments die they form the abundant white carbonate sand of tropical seas. *Dichotomosiphon* is one of the few freshwater siphonous algae, dichotomously branched and oogamous.

*Acetabularia* (Dasycladales) lives in shallow tropical and subtropical seas. The thallus is 3–10 cm high, divided into basal branching rhizoids, a slender stalk and an umbrella-like cap at the top, and the whole thallus is one giant uninucleate cell: the only primary nucleus (diploid) lies in the rhizoid. After the cap forms, the primary nucleus undergoes meiosis and repeated mitosis to produce thousands of secondary nuclei, which are carried by cytoplasmic streaming into the radial chambers of the cap, where many thick-walled cysts form; the cysts release biflagellate isogametes, and the zygote grows into a new thallus. From the 1930s, Joachim Hämmerling used this property of *Acetabularia* in classic cutting and grafting experiments. After the cap is cut off, the nucleated part can regenerate a cap but the enucleate part cannot regenerate for long; when the stalk of *A. acetabulum* (with an entire cap) is grafted onto the rhizoid of *A. crenulata* (with a cap divided into radial rays), the first regenerated cap is often intermediate in form, whereas a cap regenerated after a second cutting is entirely of the type of the species that provided the nucleus. He concluded that the nucleus determines cell form through a substance (later shown to be mRNA) that can be stored in the cytoplasm for some time. Calcified fossils of the Dasycladales first appear in the Cambrian and are index fossils in paleoenvironmental studies.

### 13.4.8. Trebouxiophyceae

Most Trebouxiophyceae are nonmotile single cells or simple colonies, and many live on land: on soil, bark and rock surfaces, or in symbiosis with fungi or animals. They have a phycoplast, but the flagellar apparatus of their motile cells is counterclockwise, unlike that of the Chlorophyceae.

- *Trebouxia* is the most common **photobiont** of lichens; it forms no zoospores within the lichen thallus and reproduces by autospores, and free-living populations away from lichens are rare ([7.12.12](/7.12.12)). Lichenization has arisen and been lost several times in the Trebouxiophyceae.
- *Chlorella*: see §13.4.4.
- *Prasiola* lives in cold streams, alpine torrents and on damp rocks and walls; it is filamentous when young and grows into a flat blade one cell thick, with cells often in groups of four and stellate chloroplasts; it reproduces by thick-walled spores and aplanospores. In some parts of China it has long been eaten under local names.
- *Botryococcus braunii* secretes long-chain hydrocarbons from its colonial cells, which can make up most of the dry weight; it contributed to the formation of some oil shales and is studied as a biofuel organism.
- *Prototheca* is a close relative of *Chlorella* that has lost photosynthesis and can infect humans and dairy cows (protothecosis).

### 13.4.9. Prasinophytes

The prasinophytes are several basal lineages of marine unicellular algae in the Chlorophyta; many have one to several flagella, and the cell and flagellar surfaces are covered with several layers of organic scales. They lie outside the core chlorophytes on the phylogenetic tree, form a **paraphyletic group**, and are thought to have the form closest to the common ancestor of the Chlorophyta.

- *Ostreococcus tauri* (Mamiellophyceae) is less than 1 µm in diameter, with no cell wall or flagella and only one chloroplast, one mitochondrion and one Golgi body; its nuclear genome is about 13 million base pairs (Derelle et al., 2006). It is the smallest known free-living eukaryote and is abundant among the picophytoplankton of the oceans. *Micromonas*, in the same class, is one of the most common eukaryotic phytoplankton from polar to tropical seas.
- *Tetraselmis* (Chlorodendrophyceae, now placed in the core chlorophytes though formerly counted among the prasinophytes) has four flagella and is used as feed in shellfish and shrimp-larva culture; one species is the symbiotic alga in the marine acoel *Symsagittifera roscoffensis*, supplying the host with all its organic matter.
- *Pyramimonas* has 4–16 scale-covered flagella. The green plastid of euglenids derives from a green alga related to the Pyramimonadales by **secondary endosymbiosis** ([7.12.9](/7.12.9)).

## 13.5. Evolutionary Trends in the Chlorophyta

### 13.5.1. Evolution of the Body

The body of the Chlorophyta became more complex along several parallel lines, starting from unicellular motile flagellates:

- The motile colonial line: *Chlamydomonas* → *Gonium*, *Pandorina* → *Eudorina*, *Pleodorina* → *Volvox*, with increasing cell number and the appearance of a division of labor between somatic and reproductive cells (§13.4.3).
- The nonmotile unicellular and colonial line: *Tetraspora* (a gelatinous colony that has lost motility), *Chlorococcum*, *Chlorella* (nonmotile single cells), *Scenedesmus*, *Pediastrum*, *Hydrodictyon* (nonmotile coenobia).
- The filamentous and sheet-like line: *Ulothrix* (unbranched filament) → *Cladophora* (branched filament) → *Chaetophora*, *Fritschiella* (heterotrichous filaments), and *Ulva*, *Enteromorpha* (sheets and tubes).
- The siphonous line: *Bryopsis*, *Codium*, *Caulerpa*, *Acetabularia*, in which morphological differentiation is achieved within a single giant multinucleate cell.

Molecular phylogenetics shows that these lines belong to different classes, and that filaments, sheets and siphonous thalli each arose several times independently in the Chlorophyta (both Ulvophyceae and Chlorophyceae have filaments, and both Trebouxiophyceae and Ulvophyceae have sheets). Multicellularity arose independently at least several times within the Chlorophyta, and is unrelated to the multicellularity of the Streptophyta, red algae and brown algae ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5.1)).

### 13.5.2. Sexual Reproduction and Life Cycles

Sexual reproduction evolved from isogamy and anisogamy to oogamy independently in the Volvocales (*Gonium* → *Pandorina* → *Eudorina* → *Volvox*), in the siphonous algae of the Ulvophyceae (anisogamy in *Bryopsis* and *Codium* → oogamy in *Dichotomosiphon*) and in filamentous Chlorophyceae (oogamy in *Oedogonium* and *Sphaeroplea*). Life cycles also diversified: the freshwater Chlorophyceae and Trebouxiophyceae kept zygotic meiosis, in which the zygote is a resting stage that resists drought and cold; the marine Ulvophyceae evolved sporic meiosis and alternation of generations (isomorphic in *Ulva*, heteromorphic in *Monostroma*), and gametic meiosis with a diploid vegetative body (*Codium*, *Caulerpa*, *Acetabularia*). The three life cycles arose in parallel in the divisions of algae, and none is a necessary stage toward another; but in the Ulvophyceae the diploid phase can be seen developing from a single cell (*Ulothrix*, the *Codiolum* stage of *Monostroma*) to a form identical to the gametophyte (*Ulva*).

### 13.5.3. Two Lines: From the "*Chlamydomonas* Line and the Bryophyte Line" to Two Divisions

The traditional Chlorophyta were regarded as containing the direct ancestors of land plants: green algae and land plants share the same photosynthetic pigments, storage product and flagellar type, and both have cellulose in their walls. People therefore sought the ancestors of land plants among the complex green algae. *Fritschiella*, discovered in the 1930s, has subterranean creeping branches and erect aerial branches, a cuticle-like covering and alternation of generations, and Bower considered that land plants might have arisen from green algae of this kind; others favored the stoneworts, but their zygote undergoes meiosis on germination and forms no diploid vegetative body, so they were thought unlikely to be ancestors.

From the 1970s, electron microscopic and biochemical studies divided the green algae into two lines (Pickett-Heaps, 1975; Mattox and Stewart, 1984):

| | *Chlamydomonas* line (Chlorophyta) | Bryophyte line (Streptophyta) |
|---|---|---|
| Cytokinesis | Spindle collapses; phycoplast | Spindle persists; phragmoplast (furrow in the more primitive forms) |
| Flagellar apparatus | Apical; four cruciate flagellar roots; symmetric | Lateral or subapical; one broad microtubular root and a multilayered structure; asymmetric |
| Enzyme oxidizing glycolate in photorespiration | Glycolate dehydrogenase in the mitochondria | Glycolate oxidase in **peroxisomes** (as in land plants) |
| Cellulose synthase complex | Linear | Rosette (Charophyceae, Coleochaetophyceae, Zygnematophyceae and land plants) |
| Representatives | *Chlamydomonas*, *Volvox*, *Chlorococcum*, *Ulva*, *Cladophora*, *Codium* | The classes of streptophyte algae (*Spirogyra*, *Coleochaete*, *Chara*), land plants |

Some Chinese textbooks place the siphonous algae on the bryophyte line, which also disagrees with molecular phylogenetics; the siphonous algae are members of the Ulvophyceae. Molecular phylogenetics (based on ribosomal RNA, chloroplast genomes and hundreds of nuclear genes) confirmed the division into two lines and defined them as the Chlorophyta and the Streptophyta (Lewis and McCourt, 2004; Leliaert et al., 2012). The "terrestrial features" of *Fritschiella* are convergent evolution within the Chlorophyceae; the ancestors of land plants lie among the streptophyte algae, and their closest living relatives are the structurally simple Zygnematophyceae ([7.12.14](/7.12.14)).

### 13.5.4. Green Algae as Model Organisms and Resources

Although the Chlorophyta did not give rise to land plants, they supplied many model systems for plant science: *Chlamydomonas* (genetics of photosynthesis, flagella and cilia, photoreception and optogenetics, tetrad analysis), *Volvox* (multicellularity and cell differentiation), *Acetabularia* (nucleocytoplasmic relations and morphogenesis), *Chlorella* and *Scenedesmus* (photosynthetic carbon assimilation), *Ostreococcus* (the smallest eukaryotic genome and the circadian clock), and *Ulva* (algal–bacterial interactions). Economically, green algae are a source of food (*Ulva*, *Enteromorpha*, sea grapes, *Prasiola*), feed (*Tetraselmis*, *Chlorella*), pigments (β-carotene from *Dunaliella*, astaxanthin from *Haematococcus*) and biofuels (*Botryococcus*, *Chlorella*); on the other hand, the overgrowth of filamentous green algae and *Enteromorpha* is a nuisance in waters and coastal seas affected by **eutrophication** (*Hydrodictyon*, *Cladophora*, green tides).

## Summary

- The green plants comprise two lineages, the Chlorophyta and the Streptophyta; green algae in the narrow sense are the Chlorophyta (Definition 13.1.1), and in the broad sense they are a paraphyletic group that includes the streptophyte algae. The Chlorophyta divide into the early-diverging prasinophytes (paraphyletic) and the core chlorophytes (Chlorodendrophyceae, Trebouxiophyceae, Ulvophyceae, Chlorophyceae and others).
- Green algal cells resemble those of land plants: chlorophylls *a* and *b*, plastid starch and pyrenoids with a starch sheath, an eyespot within the chloroplast, and whiplash flagella; but wall composition differs (glycoprotein in *Chlamydomonas*, crystalline cellulose in *Cladophora*, xylan or mannan in siphonous algae). The Chlorophyta have cruciate flagellar roots, and the Chlorophyceae and Trebouxiophyceae complete cytokinesis with a phycoplast (Definition 13.2.2), in contrast to the phragmoplast of the Streptophyta.
- Body forms include single cells, indefinite colonies, coenobia (Definition 13.2.1), filaments, heterotrichous filaments, sheets, tubes and siphonous coenocytes; reproduction includes vegetative reproduction, zoospores, aplanospores, autospores and akinetes, and isogamy, anisogamy and oogamy; life cycles are of three kinds: zygotic meiosis, sporic meiosis (isomorphic and heteromorphic alternation of generations) and gametic meiosis.
- Most of the old orders defined by body form are polyphyletic: the Volvocales and Tetrasporales belong to the Chlamydomonadales of the Chlorophyceae; the Chlorococcales are distributed among the Chlamydomonadales, Sphaeropleales and Trebouxiophyceae; *Ulothrix*, *Ulva*, *Cladophora*, *Trentepohlia* and the siphonous algae belong to the Ulvophyceae; the Zygnematales, Desmidiales, Charales and *Coleochaete* belong to the Streptophyta.
- The evolutionary trends within the Chlorophyta (single cell → colony → multicellular body, division of labor among cells, isogamy → oogamy) occurred in parallel along many lines; land plants did not arise from the Chlorophyta but from the Streptophyta ([7.12.14](/7.12.14)).

## Questions

1. Why can we say that "green algae" has two meanings today? Group *Ulva*, *Volvox*, *Spirogyra*, *Chara* and *Physcomitrium patens* according to their phylogenetic relationships, and state which combinations are monophyletic and which are not.

   **Answer** Green algae in the narrow sense means the Chlorophyta; in the broad sense it means all green plants other than the embryophytes. *Ulva* (Ulvophyceae) and *Volvox* (Chlorophyceae) belong to the Chlorophyta; *Spirogyra* (Zygnematophyceae) and *Chara* (Charophyceae) are streptophyte algae in the Streptophyta; *Physcomitrium patens* is an embryophyte and also belongs to the Streptophyta. {*Ulva*, *Volvox*} is a clade (members of the Chlorophyta), {*Spirogyra*, *Chara*, *Physcomitrium*} is a clade (members of the Streptophyta), and all five together form a clade (the Viridiplantae). But {*Ulva*, *Volvox*, *Spirogyra*, *Chara*}, the green algae in the broad sense, excludes the land plants that arose from within them and is therefore a paraphyletic group.

2. Compare the phycoplast with the phragmoplast. Why do the cells of *Chaetophora* also have plasmodesmata, and why can this not be taken as evidence that it is related to land plants?

   **Answer** The phycoplast is a set of microtubules formed between two closely spaced daughter nuclei after the spindle collapses at telophase, perpendicular to the spindle axis (parallel to the division plane); the phragmoplast consists of retained spindle microtubules parallel to the spindle axis (perpendicular to the division plane), along which Golgi vesicles travel to the center and fuse into the cell plate. In *Chaetophora* and *Fritschiella* the cell plate forms along a phycoplast, and the plasmodesmata arise independently in such a plate; *Chaetophora* has a cruciate flagellar apparatus and glycolate dehydrogenase, and molecular phylogenetics also places it in the Chlorophyceae. The two kinds of plasmodesmata develop differently, and their similarity is the result of homoplasy (convergence), not a shared derived character.

3. The volvocine series is often used to illustrate the evolution "single cell → colony → multicellular body." In what sense does the series hold, and in what sense does it need to be revised?

   **Answer** Morphologically, *Gonium*, *Pandorina*, *Eudorina*, *Pleodorina* and *Volvox* show, in turn, increasing cell number, colonies going from a flat plate through a solid sphere to a hollow sphere, vegetative and reproductive cells going from undivided to divided functions, and sexual reproduction going from isogamy through anisogamy to oogamy; this shows that such combinations of characters are possible in the Volvocales and provides comparative material for the evolution of multicellularity. But these genera are living parallel lineages, not ancestors of one another; molecular phylogenetics shows that *Volvox* itself is polyphyletic and that cell differentiation and oogamy arose independently more than once in the Volvocaceae. The series should therefore be taken as a series of "evolutionary grades," not a linear ancestor–descendant line.

4. *Ulva* and *Codium* are both large seaweeds. What are the nuclear phases of their vegetative bodies? If a large number of quadriflagellate motile cells are seen being released from a thallus of *Ulva*, can we tell whether it is a sporophyte or a gametophyte?

   **Answer** *Ulva* has isomorphic alternation of generations, so the vegetative body can be a diploid sporophyte or a haploid gametophyte; *Codium* has gametic meiosis, so the vegetative body is always diploid and the gametes are the only haploid cells. The sporophyte of *Ulva* produces quadriflagellate zoospores by meiosis, and the gametophyte produces biflagellate gametes, so the thallus releasing quadriflagellate cells is a sporophyte (note that unfertilized gametes can sometimes develop parthenogenetically, but they still have two flagella).

5. Why did Hämmerling use *Acetabularia* for his grafting experiments? Why is the first regenerated cap often intermediate in form?

   **Answer** *Acetabularia* is a single cell several centimeters high whose only nucleus lies in the basal rhizoid, so the nucleated and enucleate parts can be separated, or the stalk and rhizoid of two species grafted together, without destroying the integrity of the cell; the caps of the two species differ in shape, so it is easy to judge from which partner the cap character comes. After grafting, the stalk still contains substances (mRNA) from the original nucleus that determine cap form, and these act together with the substances produced by the new nucleus, so the first regenerated cap is often intermediate; after a second cutting the original substances are exhausted and the cap is determined entirely by the nucleus now present.
