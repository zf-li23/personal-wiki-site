The spore-bearing plants comprise the algae, fungi, lichens, bryophytes, lycophytes and ferns ([7.12.1](/7.12.1)). Systematically they belong to many branches of the eukaryote tree, and only the green plants emerged onto land and gave rise to the seed plants. The preceding twelve chapters ([7.12.6](/7.12.6)–[7.12.17](/7.12.17)) described the morphology, life cycles and classification of these groups one by one; this chapter reorganizes that material around several themes that run through it: the timeline of plant evolution (§18.1), the evolution of sexual reproduction (§18.2), the evolution of life-cycle types, especially the changing relation between gametophyte and sporophyte (§18.3), the adaptation of plants to the terrestrial environment (§18.4), and the relation between ontogeny and phylogeny (§18.5). It is also the bridge from the spore-bearing plants to the seed plants (from [7.12.19](/7.12.19) on). For the overall account of geological time and the history of life see [7.10.29](/7.10.29).

## 18.1. A Timeline of Plant Evolution

The dates in the table of "geological ages and the dominant plants of each period" common in Chinese textbooks mostly come from sources before the 1970s, and several of them differ greatly from current understanding: for example "bacteria and cyanobacteria appeared 4.0–3.5 billion years ago", "eukaryotic algae appeared 1.5–1.4 billion years ago", "multicellular algae appeared 0.9–0.7 billion years ago" and "psilophytes colonized land in the Late Silurian". Two further statements in the Chinese textbooks need updating. First, the first plants to reach land were not "algae living in the littoral or shallow-sea tidal zone": the sister group of land plants is the freshwater Zygnematophyceae, and the ancestors of land plants probably lived in fresh water or on moist land surfaces ([7.12.14](/7.12.14)). Second, the earliest land plants were not "psilophytes": bryophyte-grade plants (cryptospores) preceded the earliest vascular plants by about 40 million years, and the psilophytes were early members of a relatively late branch of land plants, the vascular plants.

## 18.2. The Evolution of Sexual Reproduction

### 18.2.1. Isogamy, Anisogamy and Oogamy

Prokaryotes (including cyanobacteria) have only vegetative and asexual reproduction (cell division, hormogonia, akinetes) and no meiosis or fusion of gametes; their genetic recombination proceeds by transformation, conjugation and similar processes ([7.11.13](/7.11.13)). Sexual reproduction is general in eukaryotic algae, and by the degree of differentiation of the gametes it falls into three types ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#def-6.4.2)):

| Type | Gametes | Representatives |
|---|---|---|
| **Isogamy** | The two gametes are the same in form, size and behavior | Most species of *Chlamydomonas*, *Ulothrix*, *Ulva*, *Ectocarpus* |
| **Anisogamy** | Both gametes can swim but differ in size, the larger being the female | *Pandorina*, *Eudorina* (the female gamete is larger and cannot swim away from the parent alone), some *Chlamydomonas* |
| **Oogamy** | The large gamete (egg) has no flagellum and cannot move, the small gamete (sperm) is flagellate | *Volvox*, *Coleochaete*, stoneworts, the brown algae *Saccharina* and *Fucus*, red algae (sperm without flagella), all embryophytes |

Within isogamy there is a further distinction between homothallism (gametes of one individual or clone can fuse with one another, as in some species of *Chlamydomonas*) and heterothallism (only gametes of different mating types can fuse, as in most species of *Chlamydomonas*, *Gonium* and *Ulothrix*); heterothallism requires differentiation between individuals and is considered more advanced than homothallism. In conjugating algae (Zygnematophyceae) the vegetative cells do not differentiate into flagellate gametes, and the protoplasts of two cells fuse directly, which is another special mode.

It is generally held that isogamy is the most primitive, anisogamy comes next and oogamy is the most advanced. This sequence has repeated independently in many groups: in the Volvocales, *Chlamydomonas* (isogamous) → *Pandorina* (isogamous or slightly anisogamous) → *Eudorina* (anisogamous) → *Volvox* (oogamous), with gamete differentiation increasing as the colony grows larger ([7.10.28](/7.10.28)); changes from isogamy to oogamy have also occurred separately in the filamentous Chlorophyceae (*Oedogonium*, *Sphaeroplea*), the siphonous green algae (Bryopsidales), brown algae and red algae. This shows that oogamy is the result of repeated convergence under selection: one kind of gamete specializes in storing nutrients and is large, the other specializes in finding its partner and is small and numerous, which is more effective than two gametes of intermediate size (for the theory of gamete competition and the evolution of anisogamy see [7.10.13](/7.10.13)).

### 18.2.2. The Evolution of Gametangia

Gametes are produced in gametangia, and the structure of gametangia also shows an evolutionary sequence:

1. Unicellular, without a sterile cell jacket: the great majority of algae, in which the gametangium (antheridium, oogonium) is a single cell and the whole content of the cell forms the gametes, as in *Chlamydomonas*, *Volvox*, *Coleochaete* and the carpogonium and spermatangium of red algae.
2. Multicellular, without a sterile cell jacket: the plurilocular gametangia of a few brown algae (*Ectocarpus*, *Sphacelaria*), made up of many small chambers each producing one gamete but with no sterile cell layer outside.
3. Multicellular, with a sterile cell jacket: the antheridia and archegonia of embryophytes, in which a layer of sterile wall cells protects the gamete-producing cells; this is a synapomorphy of the embryophytes ([7.12.15](/7.12.15)). The oogonium of stoneworts is wrapped by five spirally arranged tube cells and the antheridium is covered by eight shield cells, so both also have a sterile cell jacket, but their structure differs from the reproductive organs of bryophytes and they cannot be regarded as homologous ([7.12.14](/7.12.14)).

Among embryophytes the **archegonium** and **antheridium** are best developed in bryophytes; later, as the sporophyte became dominant and the gametophyte reduced, the reproductive organs became simpler and simpler: the antheridium of ferns has only 3 wall cells (Polypodiales), and the male gametophyte of *Selaginella* and *Isoetes* is reduced to one antheridium and one prothallial cell ([7.12.16](/7.12.16#sec-16.5.3)); gymnosperms have no antheridia and mostly still have archegonia, while the archegonia have disappeared in *Gnetum* and *Welwitschia*; angiosperms replace the archegonium with the embryo sac and deliver the nonmotile sperm to the egg through the pollen tube, thus freeing fertilization completely from dependence on water ([7.12.25](/7.12.25), [7.12.29](/7.12.29)). Cycads and *Ginkgo* still have sperm with about a thousand (*Ginkgo*) to tens of thousands (cycads) of flagella, which are released at the end of the pollen tube and swim a short distance, a witness to this transition.

### 18.2.3. No Absolute Boundary between Gametes and Spores

On the origin of sexual reproduction, Chinese textbooks cite several phenomena: in *Chlamydomonas* and *Ulothrix* the zoospores and gametes may be identical in shape, size and structure; normal gametes can, under suitable conditions, develop into new individuals alone without fusion (parthenogenesis); and normal zoospores can sometimes fuse as gametes. Conjugating algae even use vegetative cells directly as gametes. These facts show that there is no absolute boundary between gametes and spores, and that gametes may have differentiated from zoospores.

This conclusion holds at the cellular level: gametes and asexual zoospores are both flagellate reproductive cells, differing in that the former develop only after fusion. In evolutionary terms, however, it should be noted that the molecular machinery of meiosis and gamete fusion (homologous recombination, meiosis-specific proteins) is homologous in all eukaryotes, and phylogenetic analyses show that the last common ancestor of eukaryotes already had meiosis and sexual reproduction ([7.10.28](/7.10.28)). So "sexual reproduction arose from asexual reproduction" does not mean that each algal division invented sex from nothing, but that the morphological differentiation of gametes (isogamy, anisogamy, oogamy) began, in each group, from reproductive cells resembling zoospores and arose repeatedly and independently.

## 18.3. Life-Cycle Types and Their Evolution

### 18.3.1. Three Kinds of Meiosis

In plants with sexual reproduction the life cycle always has fusion of gametes (the nuclear phase changes from haploid to diploid) and meiosis (from diploid to haploid), that is, an alternation of nuclear phases. By the stage at which meiosis occurs there are three types (for the definitions see [7.4.1](/7.4.1#sec-1.4.2); for examples among algae see [7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.4.3)):

| Type | Stage of meiosis | Multicellular generation | Alternation of generations | Representatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Zygotic meiosis** (haplontic) | At germination of the zygote | Haploid only | None; the zygote is the only diploid cell | *Chlamydomonas*, *Volvox*, *Ulothrix*, *Spirogyra*, stoneworts; many fungi |
| **Gametic meiosis** (diplontic) | When gametes are produced | Diploid only | None; the gametes are the only haploid cells | The brown algae *Silvetia*, *Fucus*, *Sargassum*; diatoms; the green alga *Codium* of the Bryopsidales; animals |
| **Sporic meiosis** (haplodiplontic, intermediary meiosis) | When the diploid plant produces spores | Haploid gametophyte and diploid sporophyte | Present | *Ulva*, *Enteromorpha*, *Cladophora*; brown algae other than the fucoids; most red algae (three phases); all embryophytes |

The life cycle with sporic meiosis is the **alternation of generations**: the diploid **sporophyte** produces spores by meiosis, the spores germinate into haploid **gametophytes**, the gametophytes produce gametes, the gametes fuse into zygotes, and the zygotes develop into sporophytes. By the form of the two generations it is further divided into:

- **isomorphic alternation of generations**: gametophyte and sporophyte have the same form and structure and can be told apart only by chromosome number and reproductive organs, as in *Ulva*, *Enteromorpha*, *Ectocarpus*, and the gametophyte and tetrasporophyte of *Polysiphonia*;
- **heteromorphic alternation of generations**: the two generations differ in form, and are further divided into those with a dominant gametophyte (bryophytes, and *Scytosiphon* and *Monostroma*) and those with a dominant sporophyte (*Saccharina*, *Undaria* and all vascular plants).

*Polysiphonia*, *Porphyra* and other red algae also have a carposporophyte parasitic on the female gametophyte, forming three generations: gametophyte, carposporophyte and tetrasporophyte (the three-phase life cycle, [7.12.7](/7.12.7)).

### 18.3.2. From Gametophyte Dominance to Sporophyte Dominance

Alternation of generations arose independently many times in algae: green algae (*Ulva*, *Cladophora*), brown algae and red algae each evolved isomorphic and heteromorphic alternation. In land plants alternation of generations arose only once, from an ancestor (resembling the Zygnematophyceae and stoneworts) that was haplontic, with immediate meiosis of the zygote. The diploid generation of land plants was "intercalated" because the zygote first divided mitotically within the parent and developed into a multicellular embryo and sporophyte before undergoing meiosis (the antithetic theory, Čelakovský 1874, Bower 1908). This contrasts with the homologous theory, in which sporophytes gradually reduced or enlarged among algal ancestors with isomorphic alternation, and in which land plants came from green algae with isomorphic alternation; current phylogenetic evidence supports the former, since none of the closest algal relatives of land plants has a multicellular sporophyte.

Within land plants the relative status of the two generations changed markedly:

| Group | Gametophyte | Sporophyte | Dominant generation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bryophytes | Well developed, with a leafy shoot or thallus, independent, long-lived | Small, a single capsule, parasitic on the gametophyte, short-lived | Gametophyte |
| Lycophytes and ferns (homosporous) | Small, a prothallus, independent, lives from several weeks to many years | Well developed, with root, stem and leaf, independent | Sporophyte |
| *Selaginella*, *Isoetes*, aquatic ferns (heterosporous) | Minute, unisexual, developing within the spore wall | Well developed | Sporophyte |
| Gymnosperms | The female gametophyte is an endosperm of several thousand cells, the male gametophyte a pollen grain of 4–6 cells, both parasitic on the sporophyte | A tree or shrub | Sporophyte |
| Angiosperms | The embryo sac mostly has 7 cells and 8 nuclei, the pollen grain 2–3 cells | A herb or woody plant | Sporophyte |

![Relative sizes of gametophyte and sporophyte in the main groups (logarithmic axis; each pair of bars shows the typical length or height of the gametophyte and sporophyte of a representative plant; solid bars are generations able to live independently, hatched bars are generations attached to the other generation or developing within the spore wall). In bryophytes (*Marchantia*, *Polytrichum*) the gametophyte is larger than the sporophyte; from the lycophytes and ferns on, the sporophyte is two to three orders of magnitude larger than the gametophyte; in heterosporous plants and seed plants the gametophyte shrinks to a few hundred or a few tens of micrometers and is attached to the sporophyte or the spore wall. The values are schematic, to the order of magnitude.](./plots/generation-dominance.py)

Why has the sporophyte become dominant? There are three explanations that are not mutually exclusive. First, diploidy masks recessive deleterious mutations, which is more advantageous in large, long-lived plants with many cells. Second, the gametophyte needs water for fertilization and can live only close to the ground in damp places, whereas the sporophyte is not so limited and can grow tall to compete for light and raise its sporangia to aid dispersal. Third, branching of the sporophyte lets a single fertilization produce many sporangia, greatly increasing spore output; bryophytes increase spore output instead by growing many gametophytes from one protonema (the comparison of Judd et al.).

The reduction of the gametophyte in vascular plants proceeded in several steps: the gametophyte of homosporous plants lives independently outside the spore wall (exosporic) → the gametophyte of heterosporous plants develops within the spore wall, relying on nutrients stored in the spore (endosporic) → the megaspore is not released from the sporangium and the female gametophyte develops on the parent sporophyte (seed plants) → the male gametophyte is dispersed as a pollen grain, and the pollen tube replaces motile sperm. In this process the lycophytes (*Selaginella*, arborescent lycophytes), the ferns (*Marsilea*, *Salvinia*) and the ancestors of the seed plants each evolved heterospory independently, whereas the seed arose only once, in the progymnosperm–seed plant lineage ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)).

![Mapping of reproductive modes and life-cycle types on the phylogenetic tree. Left: a simplified phylogeny of the main groups of algae and land plants (the brown algae, which acquired plastids independently, are placed outermost for comparison). Marks show the origin of characters: oogamy arose several times in the Volvocales, stoneworts, brown algae, red algae and land plants; sporic meiosis and alternation of generations arose independently in *Ulva*, brown algae and red algae, and once in land plants through the insertion of a multicellular sporophyte; the embryo and multicellular gametangia with a sterile jacket (embryophytes); sporophyte dominance (vascular plants); the repeated origins of heterospory; the seed. Right: gamete type, stage of meiosis and dominant generation of each group.](./plots/life-cycle-tree.py)

In sum, the evolution of plant life cycles went from simple to complex: prokaryotes have no alternation of nuclear phases; eukaryotes acquired it with sexual reproduction; alternation of generations later appeared in multicellular algae; among alternating life cycles sporic meiosis is the commonest, and in land plants the sporophyte became more and more dominant and the gametophyte more and more reduced. It should be noted that these trends concern the land-plant lineage only, and do not mean that the haplontic *Chlamydomonas* or the diplontic *Fucus* is "lower": each is adapted to its own environment, and all three types arose several times independently among algae.

## 18.4. Adaptation of Plants to the Terrestrial Environment

In moving from water to land, plants faced the main problems of desiccation, support, transport of materials, gas exchange, intense ultraviolet radiation, and reproduction away from water. The table below summarizes the adaptations described in the earlier chapters (for details of bryophytes, lycophytes and ferns see [7.12.15](/7.12.15)–[7.12.17](/7.12.17), for seed plants the chapters that follow):

| Problem | Bryophytes | Lycophytes and ferns | Seed plants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water loss | Gametophyte thin, often tolerant of desiccation (resurrection); sporophyte with a cuticle | **Cuticle**, **stomata**; the gametophyte is still not drought-tolerant | Cuticle, stomata; the gametophyte is enclosed in the sporophyte |
| Protection of spores and gametes | Spore wall contains sporopollenin, airborne; gametangia with a sterile jacket | Same; sporangia with a thick wall or annulus, strobili | Pollen wall contains sporopollenin; ovule, seed, fruit |
| Protection and nutrition of the embryo | Embryo in the archegonium, nourished by the gametophyte | Embryo in the archegonium, the foot absorbs nutrients from the gametophyte | Embryo in the seed, with endosperm or cotyledons storing nutrients, able to remain dormant |
| Support | Cell turgor, plants small | Lignified **tracheids**, some with sclerenchyma; Carboniferous trees supported by periderm | Secondary xylem (wood), up to more than 100 m tall |
| Transport of water and nutrients | Rhizoids; a few have water-conducting and food-conducting cells | **Xylem**, **phloem**; true roots | Vascular tissue, with vessels and sieve tubes |
| Nutrient uptake | Rhizoids, often in association with fungi | Roots; **mycorrhiza** (arbuscular mycorrhiza-like structures already present in the Early Devonian *Aglaophyton*) | Roots, root hairs and mycorrhiza, root nodules |
| Gas exchange | Air chambers and pores of the thallus (*Marchantia*), stomata of the capsule | Stomata and intercellular spaces | Stomata, intercellular spaces, lenticels |
| Ultraviolet radiation | Phenolics such as flavonoids | Same; lignin also derives from phenylpropanoid metabolism | Same |
| Fertilization | Sperm swim in water to the archegonium | Same; fertilization still needs water | Pollen and pollen tube; fertilization does not need external water |
| Dispersal | Spores dispersed by air, the seta lifts the capsule | Spores; a tall sporophyte with sporangia in high positions; ejection by the annulus | Pollen, seeds and fruits, dispersed by wind and animals |

Chinese textbooks describe the disappearance of the psilophytes as "caused by further uplift of the land at the end of the Devonian and a drier climate", which is too simple: the decline of the early vascular plants was mainly due to their replacement by better-differentiated descendants (lycophytes, ferns, progymnosperms). On the other hand, the colonization of land by plants in turn changed the Earth profoundly: roots and mycorrhiza accelerated rock weathering and soil formation, and the spread of Devonian forests increased the burial of organic carbon, reducing atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$ and raising oxygen, which may have contributed to the Late Devonian glaciation and ocean anoxic events ([7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.3.1)).

## 18.5. Ontogeny and Phylogeny

### 18.5.1. Concepts

**Definition 18.5.1 (Ontogeny)** The whole process of an individual from the zygote (or spore, or vegetative propagule) through growth and differentiation to maturity and senescence is called **ontogeny**.

The counterpart is the history of the formation and development of a group, that is, **phylogeny**. Chinese textbooks often write the term as "xitong fayu"; in this course the term used throughout is "phylogeny" ([7.12.2](/7.12.2)). Large groups have their phylogeny, such as the whole plant kingdom, a division or a family; small groups also have their own phylogeny, and even the subspecies and varieties within a species have an evolutionary history.

The relation between the two is that phylogeny is composed of successive generations of ontogenies, every change in evolution being the result of a change in some stage of ontogeny; ontogeny in turn is constrained by the developmental program of the ancestors. Modern evolutionary developmental biology studies precisely this relation between them ([7.10.19](/7.10.19)).

### 18.5.2. Recapitulation in Plants

**Definition 18.5.2 (Recapitulation)** The phenomenon in which the early stages of ontogeny resemble in form certain stages of ancestral groups, as if repeating the course of phylogeny in a short time, is called **recapitulation**.

The example given in Chinese textbooks is that the ontogeny of higher plants starts from a fertilized egg (corresponding to a unicellular stage), divides transversely into a short filament (corresponding to a filamentous alga), then divides in several directions (corresponding to a plate-like or branched filamentous alga), and finally shows tissue differentiation and vascular tissue (corresponding to a vascular plant). Other commonly cited examples of recapitulation in plants are:

- the spore of a bryophyte germinates into a filamentous protonema, similar in form to a filamentous green alga ([7.12.15](/7.12.15));
- the spore of a fern germinates first into a filament, then a plate, and finally a heart-shaped prothallus ([7.12.17](/7.12.17#sec-17.3.1));
- the young stem of many ferns is a protostele and only later becomes a siphonostele and a dictyostele ([7.12.16](/7.12.16#sec-16.2.6));
- in species of *Acacia* in which the leaf blade is reduced to a phyllode, the seedling first produces bipinnate compound leaves; the seedling of pine first produces single primary leaves in a spiral, and only later the needles in fascicles.

In the nineteenth-century controversy between evolution and special creation, Haeckel's "biogenetic law" (ontogeny is a brief recapitulation of phylogeny) offered strong support for evolution, and botanists also used it to infer relationships among groups. But it has clear limitations:

1. What resembles the ancestor is not the adult but the early stages of development. As von Baer pointed out long ago, the embryos of related groups are more similar than the adults, because early development is less differentiated and costlier to change; the resemblance of the protonema of a bryophyte to a filamentous green alga does not show that the ancestor of bryophytes was the adult of a filamentous green alga.
2. Resemblance may also result from functional constraint. A very small mass of cells dividing in three-dimensional space forms a filament or plate first, determined by the geometry of the division planes and the need for surface area, which does not necessarily reflect the ancestral state; the protostele in the thin stems of ferns may likewise be due only to stems being too thin and having too few leaves ([7.12.16](/7.12.16#sec-16.2.6)).
3. The timing of development itself evolves. **Heterochrony** can make descendants retain juvenile features of the ancestor (paedomorphosis) or add new stages beyond the ancestral adult, so that the order of ontogeny does not necessarily agree with the order of phylogeny.

Recapitulation can therefore serve as a clue for proposing phylogenetic hypotheses but cannot replace phylogenetic analysis based on synapomorphies ([7.12.2](/7.12.2)). The modern synthesis of ontogeny and phylogeny relies on comparative developmental genetics: for example, the developmental program of the leaf differs in lycophytes and euphyllophytes, supporting the independent origin of their leaves; and bryophytes and vascular plants share many regulatory genes in sporophyte development, showing that the vascular-plant sporophyte was built by extension of the developmental program of a bryophyte-grade sporophyte.

Main conclusions:

- Timeline: reliable cyanobacterial fossils about 1.9 billion years ago (the Great Oxidation Event about 2.4–2.1 billion years ago), eukaryotes about 1.65 billion years ago, multicellular red and green algae about 1 billion years ago, land plants about 470 million years ago (Middle Ordovician), vascular plants about 430 million years ago (Silurian), ferns, forests and seeds in the Devonian, angiosperms in the Early Cretaceous.
- Sexual reproduction: isogamy → anisogamy → oogamy repeated independently in several groups; gametangia from unicellular to multicellular and from lacking to having a sterile jacket (embryophytes), then simplified in seed plants to the embryo sac and pollen tube; there is no absolute boundary between gametes and spores, but meiosis and sex arose only once in eukaryotes.
- Life cycles: the three kinds of meiosis (zygotic, gametic, sporic); isomorphic and heteromorphic alternation of generations; in land plants the shift from gametophyte dominance (bryophytes) to sporophyte dominance (vascular plants), with the gametophyte passing from exosporic through endosporic to parasitic on the sporophyte (seed plants).
- Adaptation to land: cuticle, stomata, sporopollenin, the embryo, lignified vascular tissue, roots and mycorrhiza, seeds and the pollen tube.
- Ontogeny (Definition 18.5.1) and phylogeny; recapitulation (Definition 18.5.2) is a clue and not a proof.

## Questions

1. Chinese textbooks say "bacteria and cyanobacteria appeared 4.0–3.5 billion years ago" and "psilophytes were the first to colonize land in the Late Silurian". What is wrong with each of these statements?

   **Answer** Stromatolites and carbon isotopes 3.5 billion years old show only that microbes existed, and whether they were oxygenic cyanobacteria is disputed; reliable cyanobacterial microfossils are about 1.9 billion years old, and free oxygen appeared in the atmosphere (the Great Oxidation Event) about 2.4–2.1 billion years ago. The earliest land-plant fossils are the cryptospores of the Middle Ordovician (about 470 million years ago), from bryophyte-grade plants, about 40 million years older than the Silurian *Cooksonia*; the psilophytes (early vascular plants) came later, and the ancestors of land plants were freshwater streptophyte algae, not littoral algae.

2. Use the Volvocales to illustrate the evolution of sexual reproduction, and explain why this evolution is said to have occurred many times independently in plants.

   **Answer** *Chlamydomonas* is isogamous (the two gametes are alike), *Pandorina* isogamous or slightly anisogamous, *Eudorina* is anisogamous (the female gamete is larger and cannot swim alone), and *Volvox* is oogamous (the egg has no flagella, the sperm are small and swimming); as the colony grows larger the gametes differentiate more and more. Changes from isogamy to oogamy also occur in the filamentous Chlorophyceae (*Oedogonium*, *Sphaeroplea*), the siphonous green algae (Bryopsidales), brown algae, red algae and land plants, whose ancestors are different (brown algae even belong to a different eukaryotic supergroup from green algae), which shows that oogamy is the result of convergent evolution under similar selection pressures.

3. Compare the life cycles of the three kinds of meiosis and give an example of each. Why is the alternation of generations in land plants said to be "intercalated"?

   **Answer** Zygotic meiosis: meiosis occurs at germination of the zygote and the multicellular form is haploid only, as in *Chlamydomonas*, *Spirogyra*, stoneworts; gametic meiosis: meiosis occurs when gametes are formed and the multicellular form is diploid only, as in *Fucus*, diatoms, animals; sporic meiosis: meiosis occurs when the diploid sporophyte produces spores, with an alternation of haploid gametophyte and diploid sporophyte, as in *Ulva*, kelp and all embryophytes. The closest algal relatives of land plants (Zygnematophyceae, stoneworts) all have zygotic meiosis and no multicellular diploid; the sporophyte of land plants is thought to have arisen when the zygote postponed meiosis within the parent and first divided mitotically, so that it was "intercalated" into a haplontic life cycle (the antithetic theory).

4. From bryophytes to angiosperms, how does the relative status of gametophyte and sporophyte change? Why can the sporophyte become dominant?

   **Answer** In bryophytes the gametophyte is well developed and independent, with the sporophyte parasitic on it; in lycophytes and ferns the sporophyte is well developed and the gametophyte small and independent; in heterosporous plants such as *Selaginella* and *Isoetes* the gametophyte develops within the spore wall; in seed plants the gametophyte is parasitic within the sporophyte, and the embryo sac of angiosperms has only 7 cells and the pollen grain 2–3 cells. Reasons for sporophyte dominance: diploidy masks deleterious recessive mutations; the sporophyte is not limited by the need for water in fertilization, so it can grow tall to compete for light and raise its sporangia; and branching of the sporophyte greatly increases the spores produced by each fertilization.

5. What is recapitulation? Give two examples from plants, and explain why recapitulation alone cannot be used to infer phylogeny.

   **Answer** It is the resemblance of early stages of ontogeny to certain stages of ancestral groups (Definition 18.5.2), for example the protonema of bryophytes resembling a filamentous green alga, the germination of fern spores through a filament and a plate into a prothallus, and the protostele of young fern stems later becoming a siphonostele or dictyostele. It cannot alone be used to infer phylogeny because what resembles the ancestor is often an early stage of development and not the ancestral adult (von Baer), because resemblance may result from functional constraints such as the geometry of cell division or organ size, and because the timing of development itself changes through heterochrony; phylogeny must be established by analysis of synapomorphies.
