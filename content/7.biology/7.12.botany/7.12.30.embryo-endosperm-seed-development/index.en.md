**Double fertilization** ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)) starts two developmental programs at once in the embryo sac: the zygote, formed by the fusion of a sperm cell with the egg cell, develops into the embryo, and the primary endosperm nucleus, formed by the fusion of the other sperm cell with the central cell, develops into the endosperm. At the same time the **integuments** surrounding the embryo sac develop into the seed coat, the whole **ovule** becomes the seed, and the ovary develops into the fruit ([7.12.31](/7.12.31)). The gametophytes of the previous chapter consist of only a few cells; this chapter follows how, within a few weeks, they become a seed containing a young plant, a storage tissue and a protective shell. The mature structure, types, dormancy and germination of seeds were treated in [7.12.19](/7.12.19); this chapter deals with their origin: how the embryo builds an apical–basal axis and the three primary meristems from a single cell (Section 30.1), in which three ways the endosperm develops and why it is triploid (Section 30.2), how the seed coat and aril form (Section 30.3), and how the seed matures and desiccates into a resting state (Section 30.4). Finally the flower, gametophytes, fertilization and seed are joined into the complete life cycle of angiosperms, which is compared with those of ferns and gymnosperms (Section 30.5). The hormonal and genetic control of embryonic pattern formation, and the physiology of storage accumulation and desiccation tolerance in seeds, belong to plant physiology; this chapter describes only morphology and developmental processes.

## 30.1. Development of the Embryo

### 30.1.1. The Zygote and the Proembryo

After the egg cell is fertilized, the zygote secretes a cellulose cell wall and passes through a period of dormancy of varying length: in rice the first division takes place 4–6 h after fertilization, in wheat after 16–18 h, in cotton after 2–3 d, and in tea and autumn crocus (*Colchicum*) it can be delayed for months. Development of the endosperm usually precedes that of the embryo: the primary endosperm nucleus begins to divide without dormancy, and by the time the zygote divides there are often already many endosperm nuclei in the embryo sac, which supply nutrients to the early embryo.

The zygote is a highly polarized cell. In shepherd's purse, cotton and other plants the egg cell itself is already polarized, with the nucleus and most of the cytoplasm at the chalazal end and a large vacuole at the micropylar end ([7.12.28](/7.12.28)); after fertilization the polarity becomes more pronounced. The first division of the zygote is usually an unequal transverse division, and this division establishes the apical–basal axis of the embryo.

**Definition 30.1.1 (Apical Cell and Basal Cell)** Of the two cells produced by the first division of the zygote, the smaller one with dense cytoplasm, lying away from the micropyle, is the **apical cell**; the larger, highly vacuolated one, lying near the micropyle, is the **basal cell**. The apical cell is the main source of the embryo proper, and the basal cell develops mainly into the suspensor.

The two cells are connected by plasmodesmata, but from then on their fates differ: the apical cell is embryonic, and the basal cell has essentially only a nutritive and supporting role.

**Definition 30.1.2 (Proembryo and Suspensor)** The young embryo from the first division of the zygote until the organs (cotyledons) begin to differentiate is called the **proembryo**. The row or mass of cells formed by division of the basal cell that connects the embryo to the micropylar end of the embryo sac is the **suspensor**; the part above the suspensor that will develop into the embryo is the *embryo proper*.

The suspensor is not a permanent structure: it is absorbed as the embryo proper grows, breaks down by programmed cell death at the torpedo stage, and at most leaves a remnant of the basal cell in the mature seed. It was formerly regarded only as a structure that pushes the embryo proper into the endosperm and places the embryo in a position favorable for absorbing nutrients, and this is indeed the main role of the suspensors of the lycophyte *Selaginella* and of pine ([7.12.16](/7.12.16), [7.12.25](/7.12.25)). The angiosperm suspensor, however, is metabolically very active: the suspensor cell walls of shepherd's purse have ingrowths and, like **transfer cells**, absorb nutrients from the surrounding tissue and pass them to the embryo proper; the suspensor also synthesizes growth regulators such as gibberellins for use by the early embryo proper. The size of the suspensor varies enormously: it is a single cell in the orchids, but in scarlet runner bean (*Phaseolus coccineus*) it is a large mass of polyploid cells containing giant polytene chromosomes.

Suspensor cells themselves have the potential to develop into an embryo, which is normally suppressed by the embryo proper. In *Arabidopsis* mutants such as *twin*, when development of the embryo proper is blocked, the suspensor cells proliferate and are converted into a second embryo, so that two or even three viable embryos appear in one seed. This shows that inhibitory signaling exists between the embryo proper and the suspensor.

### 30.1.2. Dicot Embryo Development: Shepherd's Purse

Development of the dicot embryo is classically studied in shepherd's purse (*Capsella bursa-pastoris*) (see the figure below). Its ovule is campylotropous, and the embryo bends with it during development. The process can be divided into the stages of proembryo, globular embryo, heart-shaped embryo, torpedo-shaped embryo and mature embryo:

1. Two-cell stage: the zygote divides unequally and transversely into the apical cell and the basal cell.
2. Formation of the suspensor: the basal cell, which is slightly larger, divides repeatedly in the transverse plane to form a suspensor of a row of 6–10 cells; the cell nearest the micropyle is especially large, is called the basal cell of the suspensor, is highly vacuolated, and absorbs nutrients from its surroundings.
3. Quadrant and octant: the apical cell first undergoes two mutually perpendicular longitudinal divisions to become 4 cells (quadrant stage); each cell then divides once transversely, giving a spheroid of 8 cells in two tiers, the *octant*. The 4 cells of the upper tier later form the cotyledons and shoot apex, and the 4 of the lower tier form the hypocotyl.
4. Globular embryo: each cell of the octant first undergoes one periclinal division, cutting off an outer **protoderm** from the inner cells; divisions in all directions afterward turn the embryo into a ball of tissue. The protoderm later develops into the epidermis.
5. Heart-shaped embryo: the cells on the two sides of the top of the globular embryo divide faster and form two protrusions, the cotyledon primordia, so that the embryo has a heart shape in longitudinal section; in the depression between the two cotyledons the shoot apical meristem, the plumule, differentiates. At the same time the cell at the tip of the suspensor next to the embryo proper, the *hypophysis*, divides together with the cells at the base of the globular embryo to form the root apex: the descendants of the hypophysis become the quiescent center of the root apex and the columella cells of the root cap. Inside the embryo, longitudinal divisions set apart the central **procambium** and the surrounding **ground meristem**.
6. Torpedo-shaped embryo: the cells of the cotyledons and embryonic axis divide transversely and elongate, the embryo elongates into a torpedo shape, and the three primary meristems run continuously along the axis; the suspensor begins to degenerate.
7. Mature embryo: the cotyledons and axis continue to elongate; constrained by the curved space of the ovule, the cotyledons bend toward one side of the axis and the whole embryo is bent into a horseshoe (walking-stick) shape. The suspensor disappears, most of the endosperm is absorbed by the embryo, and at seed maturity only a single layer of endosperm remains, the nutrients being stored in the cotyledons.

A: the first, unequal transverse division of the zygote forms the smaller apical cell (orange) and the larger, vacuolated basal cell (gray); B: the basal cell divides into a row forming the suspensor, and the apical cell undergoes two longitudinal divisions into a quadrant; C: octant, 4 cells in each of two tiers; D: globular embryo, with the protoderm outside and the hypophysis at the tip of the suspensor; E: heart-shaped embryo, with two cotyledon primordia and the shoot apical meristem between them, and the procambium differentiating inside; F: torpedo-shaped embryo, with the cotyledons and axis elongating; G: mature embryo bent into a horseshoe, with two cotyledons, plumule, hypocotyl and radicle all formed and the suspensor degenerated](./plots/capsella-embryogenesis.py)

The cell divisions in the embryos of shepherd's purse and of *Arabidopsis* (in the same family, Brassicaceae) are almost invariant, and the lineage of every cell can be traced, which is why *Arabidopsis* became the model system for studying pattern formation in the plant embryo. Embryogenesis must establish two basic patterns: the successive arrangement along the apical–basal axis of shoot apical meristem, cotyledons, hypocotyl, radicle and root apical meristem, and the arrangement in cross section from the outside inward of the three **tissue systems**, epidermis, ground tissue and vascular tissue ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)). The mutant phenotypes correspond directly to these two patterns: *gurke* lacks the shoot apex and cotyledons, *fackel* lacks the hypocotyl, *monopteros* lacks the radicle, and *gnom* lacks both apex and base, leaving only a stretch of axis with the three tissues. Establishment of the apical–basal axis depends on the polar transport and distribution of auxin within the embryo, the mechanism of which belongs to plant physiology.

By the direction of division of the zygote and of the apical and basal cells, and the contribution of the basal cell to the embryo proper, embryology divides angiosperm embryo development into several types, which are used as embryological characters ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)):

| Embryo type | First division of the apical cell | Contribution of the basal cell to the embryo proper | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onagrad (crucifer type) | Longitudinal | Very small or none (forms only the hypophysis or does not take part) | Shepherd's purse, *Arabidopsis*, evening primrose |
| Asterad | Longitudinal | Both basal and apical cells contribute to the embryo proper | Asteraceae, Lamiaceae |
| Solanad | Transverse | None; forms a suspensor of two or more cells | Solanaceae |
| Caryophyllad | Transverse | None; the basal cell does not divide and alone forms the suspensor | Caryophyllaceae |
| Chenopodiad | Transverse | Both basal and apical cells contribute | *Chenopodium* |
| Piperad | The first division of the zygote is itself longitudinal | — | Piperaceae |

### 30.1.3. Monocot Embryo Development: Wheat

The early embryo of monocots also passes through a globular proembryo stage; the difference comes after the cotyledon forms: only one cotyledon forms, located at the apex of the embryo, and the shoot apical meristem lies to one side of the base of the cotyledon, enclosed by a sheath extending from the cotyledon base. The embryo of arrowhead (*Sagittaria*) is of this kind: after the transverse division of the zygote the basal cell becomes a large suspensor cell, the descendants of the apical cell form a cylindrical embryo proper, the cotyledon is terminal, and the depression on one side is the shoot apex.

The grass embryo is the most highly differentiated embryo among angiosperms (for the mature structure see [7.12.19](/7.12.19)). Taking wheat as an example (see the figure below):

1. Proembryo: the first division of the zygote is oblique, and each of the two daughter cells divides obliquely once more to give a proembryo of 4 cells; divisions then occur in all directions, and by the 16- to 32-cell stage the embryo is club-shaped, the swollen upper part being the forerunner of the embryo proper and the slender lower part differentiating into the suspensor, with the whole embryo proper covered by a layer of protoderm.
2. Notch and scutellum: a small notch appears on one side of the club-shaped embryo proper, and at the notch the shoot apical meristem of the axis forms; the part above the notch develops into the cotyledon, which grows rapidly and projects above the apical meristem; later the cells near the top undergo intercalary growth, which extends the cotyledon upward into the **scutellum**, which encloses one side of the embryo and lies against the endosperm.
3. Coleoptile and epiblast: soon after the apical meristem differentiates, the primordium of the coleoptile appears as a layer of folded tissue covering the apical meristem and the primordium of the first foliage leaf; at the same time a new protrusion appears on the opposite side of the embryo from the cotyledon and grows into the epiblast.
4. Radicle and coleorhiza: when the coleoptile begins to differentiate, the primordia of the radicle and coleorhiza appear at the lower end of the embryo; the two grow at different rates, and a schizogenous cavity forms around the radicle and enlarges as the embryo grows.
5. Mature embryo: the single scutellum lies on the inner side of the embryo, against the endosperm; the apical meristem of the shoot and several young leaves form the plumule, enclosed by the coleoptile; the opposite end is the radicle, enclosed by the coleorhiza; and the epiblast lies on the side opposite the scutellum. The embryos of maize and other Panicoideae have no epiblast.

![Embryogenesis of wheat (*Triticum aestivum*) (longitudinal sections, schematic, not to scale; the micropylar end faces down and the endosperm is on the right). A: 4-cell proembryo formed by oblique divisions; B: club-shaped embryo, with the swollen embryo proper above covered by protoderm and the slender suspensor below; C: a notch appears on the side of the embryo proper facing away from the endosperm, the shoot apical meristem lies at the notch, and the tissue above the notch will develop into the cotyledon; D: the cotyledon extends upward as the scutellum, the primordium of the coleoptile appears outside the apical meristem and the first leaf primordium, a protrusion for the epiblast appears opposite the scutellum, and the radicle and coleorhiza appear below; E: mature embryo, with the scutellum against the endosperm, the plumule enclosed by the coleoptile, the radicle enclosed by the coleorhiza, and a schizogenous cavity between the two](./plots/wheat-embryogenesis.py)

The homologies of the parts of the grass embryo (whether the scutellum and coleoptile together correspond to one cotyledon, and whether the epiblast is a vestige of a second cotyledon or an extension of the coleorhiza) have long been disputed in morphology and were discussed in [7.12.19](/7.12.19). Developmentally, the scutellum and coleoptile both arise from the same mass of tissue above the notch, which supports the view that together they make up one cotyledon.

Embryo development of angiosperms differs from that of most gymnosperms: the pine zygote first undergoes three free-nuclear divisions ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)), whereas in angiosperms, as in ferns, the first nuclear division of the zygote is accompanied by cell wall formation.

### 30.1.5. Polyembryony

Ordinarily an ovule produces one embryo sac and one egg cell, and only one embryo develops after fertilization. But in the seeds of some plants there are two or more embryos, which is **polyembryony** ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)). In angiosperms it has several origins: the proembryo from one fertilized egg splits into several embryos (cleavage polyembryony); an ovule has two or more embryo sacs; synergids, antipodal cells and others develop into embryos; and, commonest of all, cells of the nucellus or integument develop directly into embryos, that is, adventitious embryos ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)). In *Citrus* and mango, nucellar cells enter the embryo sac after fertilization and develop at the same time as the zygotic embryo, so a seed may contain from a few to a dozen embryos, of which only one is the sexual zygotic embryo and the rest are nucellar embryos with the same genotype as the mother. In *Citrus* breeding, nucellar seedlings are therefore often used to obtain uniform, virus-free rootstocks; conversely, to obtain hybrids one must identify the zygotic embryo among the many.

## 30.2. Development of the Endosperm

### 30.2.1. The Primary Endosperm Nucleus

The **endosperm** arises from the second fusion of double fertilization: the nucleus of the sperm cell fuses with the two polar nuclei of the central cell (or with the secondary nucleus already formed by their fusion), a process called *triple fusion*, and the product is the primary endosperm nucleus. The primary endosperm nucleus usually begins to divide without dormancy, so the endosperm develops earlier than the embryo.

In the commonest, *Polygonum* type of embryo sac, the primary endosperm nucleus is triploid, with two sets of chromosomes from the mother and one from the father. The $2:1$ ratio of maternal to paternal genomes is important for normal endosperm development: when parents of different ploidy are crossed and this ratio is broken in the endosperm, endosperm failure and shriveled seeds often result, which is an important cause of incompatibility in interspecific and interploidy crosses. This is related to genomic imprinting in the endosperm (some genes are expressed only from the maternal or only from the paternal copy), a mechanism that belongs to epigenetics. The endosperm combines paternal and maternal heredity and is also the nutrient supply for the developing embryo.

Embryo sacs of other types have endosperm of different ploidy ([7.12.28](/7.12.28)):

| Embryo sac type | Polar nuclei of the central cell | Primary endosperm nucleus | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Polygonum*, *Allium* | 2 × $n$ | $3n$ | Most angiosperms; onion, arrowhead |
| *Oenothera*, *Nuphar*/*Schisandra* | 1 × $n$ | $2n$ | Onagraceae; Nymphaeales, Austrobaileyales |
| *Fritillaria* | 1 × $n$ + 1 × $3n$ | $5n$ | Lily, fritillary |
| *Peperomia* | 8 × $n$ | $9n$ | *Peperomia* |

### 30.2.2. Nuclear Endosperm

Endosperm of angiosperms develops in three ways (see the figure of endosperm development in Section 30.2.4).

**Definition 30.2.1 (Nuclear, Cellular and Helobial Endosperm)** By whether cell walls form immediately after division of the primary endosperm nucleus, endosperm development is divided into three types:

- **Nuclear endosperm**: the first division of the primary endosperm nucleus and the divisions for some time afterward are not accompanied by cell wall formation, many free nuclei lie in one mass of cytoplasm, and cell walls form between the nuclei only at a later stage;
- **Cellular endosperm**: the first division of the primary endosperm nucleus and every later division are accompanied by cell wall formation, and there is no free-nuclear stage;
- **Helobial endosperm**: the first division of the primary endosperm nucleus forms a transverse wall that divides the embryo sac into a large micropylar chamber and a small chalazal chamber; the divisions in both chambers are then free-nuclear, the micropylar chamber later forms cells, and the chalazal chamber often remains in the free-nuclear state.

Nuclear endosperm is the commonest, and monocots and most dicots (especially the polypetalous groups) belong to this type. The number of nuclei in the free-nuclear stage varies among plants: in walnut and apple it reaches hundreds to thousands before cell formation, whereas in coffee it is only 4–16. As the number of nuclei increases, a large central vacuole appears in the embryo sac and the nuclei and cytoplasm are pushed to the periphery, denser at the micropylar and chalazal ends and only a thin layer at the sides; nuclear division is mostly mitotic, although the nuclei at the chalazal end sometimes divide abnormally. Cell walls then form between the free nuclei, starting at the periphery of the embryo sac and advancing layer by layer toward the center; this process is called *cellularization*. The extent of cellularization also varies:

- All the free nuclei form cells, as in maize and wheat (in maize, about 26–34 h after pollination there are only the zygote and a few endosperm nuclei, at 3 d there are 128–256 free nuclei, and at 4 d endosperm cells have formed);
- Only one or two layers of cells form around the periphery of the embryo sac, with free nuclei and liquid remaining in the center;
- Cells form only at the micropylar end;
- In a few, cells never form.

Coconut is the extreme case of the second kind: the white flesh of the mature coconut is endosperm that has cellularized at the periphery, and the coconut water in the center is liquid endosperm that remains in the free-nuclear state, which is why coconut water can be used in tissue culture as a supplement that promotes cell division.

### 30.2.3. Cellular Endosperm

Cellular endosperm is multicellular from the start. Most sympetalous plants (the asterids, [7.12.46](/7.12.46)–[7.12.49](/7.12.49)) and the Nymphaeales of the **ANA grade** belong to this type. In cellular endosperm a haustorium often forms at the micropylar or chalazal end and extends into the nucellus or integument to absorb nutrients, as in many members of Orobanchaceae, Scrophulariaceae and Plantaginaceae.

### 30.2.4. Helobial Endosperm

The helobial type is intermediate between the nuclear and cellular types and is named for its first discovery in the Helobiae (roughly the present Alismatales, [7.12.36](/7.12.36)). In examples such as *Echinodorus*, arrowhead and *Eremurus*, the first division divides the embryo sac into a micropylar chamber and a chalazal chamber; the micropylar chamber is larger, and its nuclei divide many times in the free state and form cells later; the nuclei of the chalazal chamber divide fewer times, remain free throughout and often degenerate. The type is not confined to the Alismatales: *Eremurus* belongs to Asphodelaceae in the Asparagales, and the helobial type occurs in many orders of monocots and rarely in dicots.

### 30.2.5. The Fate of the Endosperm and the Perisperm

The nutrients of the endosperm in a seed are in some cases stored until germination, when the embryo uses them, and the mature seed has endosperm; in others they are absorbed by the embryo during its development and moved into the cotyledons, so that at seed maturity the endosperm is absent or only a thin layer remains, giving an exalbuminous seed ([7.12.19](/7.12.19)). This division of storage between endosperm and cotyledons is decided in the second half of seed development: in legumes and cucurbits the embryo grows until it fills the embryo sac and the endosperm is consumed; in grasses the embryo is only a small part of the grain, the endosperm cells are filled with starch, the outermost layer differentiates into the aleurone layer, and the scutellum is only responsible for absorption. The endosperm may also differ in texture: uniform, or divided by folds growing inward from the seed coat into a marbled, chewed-looking ruminate endosperm, as in the seeds of nutmeg and Annonaceae.

Some plants have no endosperm in the seed at all: in the orchids and water chestnut (*Trapa*, now placed in Lythraceae) the polar nuclei are fertilized, but the primary endosperm nucleus soon degenerates and disappears, and no endosperm develops; in Podostemaceae the central cell is not fertilized at all. Orchid seeds are dust-like, with only an undifferentiated embryo, and at germination they depend on symbiosis with fungi for nutrition ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.2)).

Embryo and endosperm absorb the nucellar tissue around the embryo sac as they develop, so the nucellus is generally destroyed and disappears. In a few plants the nucellus persists and develops into a storage tissue, the **perisperm** ([7.12.19](/7.12.19)); it has the same function as endosperm but a different origin, being diploid maternal tissue. Seeds with perisperm may lack endosperm, as in *Amaranthus*, *Dianthus* and beet, or may have endosperm as well, as in pepper and ginger.

### 30.2.6. The Origin of the Endosperm

Endosperm is a tissue unique to angiosperms. The storage tissue of other seed plants is the haploid female gametophyte, which forms before fertilization ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)): the plant invests nutrients before it knows whether the ovule can be fertilized. Angiosperms establish endosperm only in ovules that have been successfully fertilized, and this "supply on demand" is thought to be one of their advantages. Two main hypotheses account for where the endosperm came from:

- The endosperm corresponds to a modified "twin embryo": the second fertilization originally produced an additional embryo, which later became a nutritive tissue supporting its sibling embryo. *Ephedra* and *Gnetum* also have a second fertilization, which yields an extra diploid embryo and not endosperm ([7.12.25](/7.12.25)), and this is regarded as supporting evidence for the hypothesis; but molecular phylogenetics shows that the gnetophytes are not close relatives of the angiosperms, so the second fertilizations of the two groups must have evolved independently.
- The endosperm is delayed female gametophyte tissue: the part of the female gametophyte concerned with nutrition postpones its proliferation until after fertilization, with fertilization serving only as the trigger signal.

The earliest-diverging angiosperms offer a clue: in Nymphaeales and Austrobaileyales the embryo sac has only one polar nucleus, so that the endosperm is diploid (one set from each of father and mother), and it is cellular ([7.12.28](/7.12.28)). The earliest angiosperm endosperm may therefore have been diploid, and the extra polar nucleus of the *Polygonum*-type embryo sac made the endosperm triploid and increased the weight of the maternal genome. This agrees with the parental conflict hypothesis: the endosperm determines how much nutrition each embryo can obtain from the mother; paternal genes tend to make it demand more, whereas maternal genes tend to spread resources equally among all offspring, and a ploidy in which the maternal genome predominates favors control by the mother.

## 30.3. Formation of the Seed Coat

The seed coat develops from the integuments at the same time as the embryo and endosperm develop. The number of integuments and the changes they undergo in development determine the structure of the seed coat:

| Integuments | Seed coat | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 layer | Sunflower, walnut, and most sympetalous plants |
| 2, both develop | Testa (from the outer integument) and tegmen (from the inner integument) | Rapeseed, castor bean |
| 2, only the outer develops | The inner integument is absorbed by the embryo, and the seed coat develops from the outer integument | Soybean, broad bean |
| 2, only the inner persists briefly | The outer integument degenerates very early, the inner integument also largely disappears, and protection is provided by the pericarp | Rice, wheat |

Two typical developmental sequences:

- Broad bean (*Vicia faba*): the inner integument is absorbed and consumed by the embryo, and the seed coat develops from the outer integument. The outer integument differentiates into three layers: outermost a layer of long columnar thick-walled cells with their long axes parallel (the palisade layer), then a layer of bone-shaped sclereids swollen at both ends into an "I" shape, and innermost several layers of undifferentiated parenchyma cells, which are flattened as the seed grows (for the structure see [7.12.19](/7.12.19)). The early seed coat cells contain starch and are a site of nutrient storage, so the young seed coat of broad bean is soft and edible, and becomes a tough protective layer only when old.
- Wheat: the two integuments each begin with two cell layers. The outer integument begins to degenerate at the first division of the zygote, its protoplasm gradually disappears, and the cells are crushed, lose their shape and finally vanish; the inner integument persists temporarily and enlarges, and at the milk stage its outer cells begin to disappear while the inner cells persist only briefly. By the time the seed matures and dries, what remains of the seed coat can no longer protect, and the grain is protected mainly by the pericarp developed from the carpel, so that the grain is a caryopsis ([7.12.31](/7.12.31)).

The tissues of the seed coat come from different layers of the integuments in different plants, and in taxonomy seed coat types are distinguished by the layer that bears the mechanical function: those derived from the outer epidermis, middle layer or inner epidermis of the outer integument are called exotestal, mesotestal and endotestal respectively, and likewise for the inner integument (tegmic). The palisade layer of legumes comes from the outer epidermis of the outer integument and is of the exotestal type. The structure of the seed coat is therefore important taxonomic evidence.

The seed coat of angiosperms is mostly dry, but a few are fleshy, a *sarcotesta*: the outer seed coat of the pomegranate seed consists of juicy cell layers and is the edible part of the seed. A sarcotesta is common in gymnosperms, and the outer seed coats of cycads and ginkgo are both thick and fleshy ([7.12.26](/7.12.26)).

Appendages formed from structures other than the integuments may also be present outside the seed.

**Definition 30.3.1 (Aril)** A fleshy or membranous structure that develops after fertilization from the funiculus, the placenta or tissue near the micropyle and partly or wholly encloses the seed is called an **aril**.

The edible white "flesh" of longan and lychee is an aril: their pericarp is thin and brittle, and the seed is enclosed in a juicy aril; after the capsule of spindle tree (*Euonymus*) dehisces it reveals an orange-red aril; and the red, netlike aril around the seed of nutmeg (*Myristica fragrans*) is the spice mace. The red, cup-shaped fleshy structure around the mature seed of the gymnosperm yew (*Taxus*) is also called an aril. The aril and the caruncle ([7.12.19](/7.12.19)) are both appendages outside the seed coat: the caruncle is a small outgrowth of the testa near the micropyle, whereas the aril is larger, often encloses the seed, is brightly colored and rich in sugars or lipids, and is a structure that attracts birds and mammals to eat it and disperse the seed ([7.12.31](/7.12.31)).

## 30.4. Seed Formation and Maturation

Seed development can be divided into three successive stages:

1. Embryogenesis and tissue differentiation: the zygote divides, the organs and primary meristems of the embryo form, and the endosperm passes through free-nuclear divisions and cellularization. In this stage cell division is vigorous and the water content of the seed is very high.
2. Accumulation of storage reserves (seed filling): the embryo stops dividing, cells enlarge, assimilates from the mother plant are imported in large quantity, and starch, storage proteins and oils are synthesized in the endosperm, perisperm or cotyledons; the dry weight of the seed increases rapidly. In cereal crops this process is called, by the appearance of the grain, the milk stage, dough stage and full ripe stage in turn: at the milk stage the grain is green and a milky liquid flows out when it is squeezed; at the dough stage the endosperm is waxy and can be cut with a fingernail; at the full ripe stage the grain is hard and the dry weight reaches its maximum.
3. Maturation drying: the vascular connection between the seed and the mother plant is cut, the water content falls rapidly and more than 90% of the water is lost; the water content of a mature, dry seed is usually only a few to a dozen percent. The seed coat hardens at this time, enclosing the embryo and reserves in a hard shell, metabolism falls to an almost undetectable level, and the seed enters quiescence or dormancy.

The ability to withstand this desiccation and remain viable is a key adaptation of seed plants: a dry seed can survive cold and drought and be preserved in the soil for many years. Desiccation tolerance is acquired late in maturation, when protective proteins and soluble sugars accumulate in the embryo so that the cytoplasm forms a glassy state on water loss; the process is regulated by abscisic acid and belongs to plant physiology. The seeds of a few plants do not dry out, but remain high in water content at maturity and lose viability soon after leaving the mother plant, that is, recalcitrant seeds (such as lychee, longan, mango, chestnut and rubber tree, [7.12.19](/7.12.19)); these mostly live in the humid tropics, and the seeds germinate quickly after dispersal. More extreme is vivipary, in which the seed germinates in the fruit without dormancy; for the viviparous seedlings of mangroves see [7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.4). When cereal crops meet continuous rain at maturity, the grains germinate on the ear, which is called pre-harvest sprouting, a result of interruption of the desiccation and dormancy programs and very damaging to wheat production.

Seed formation is coordinated with fruit growth: the fertilized ovule sends signals to the ovary and stimulates its growth; in strawberry, if the achenes are removed from the receptacle, the receptacle does not enlarge. For fruit formation see [7.12.31](/7.12.31).

## 30.5. The Life Cycle of Angiosperms

### 30.5.1. From Seed to Seed

The whole course of developmental and reproductive stages through which a plant passes in its life, following one another and recurring in regular sequence, is called the *life cycle*. The life cycle of an angiosperm generally begins with a seed (see the figure below): the seed passes through dormancy and, under suitable conditions, germinates into a seedling and gradually grows into a plant with root, stem and leaf; after a period of vegetative growth, some terminal or axillary buds change into flower buds and form flowers; pollen grains form in the anthers of the stamens and embryo sacs in the ovules of the ovary of the pistil; the pollen grains and embryo sacs produce sperm cells and egg cells respectively; after pollination and double fertilization, one sperm cell fuses with the egg cell into the zygote, which later develops into the embryo, and the other fuses with the two polar nuclei of the central cell and develops into the endosperm; the ovary develops into the fruit and the ovules into seeds. The embryo in the seed is the rudiment of the next generation of the plant, and so the whole course "from seed to seed" is generally called the life cycle of an angiosperm.

The life cycle of angiosperms has two generations (**alternation of generations**, [7.4.1](/7.4.1)):

- The **sporophyte** generation ($2n$, asexual generation): from the zygote, through embryo and seedling, to the plant with root, stem, leaf and flower, up to just before meiosis of the pollen mother cells (microspore mother cells) in the anthers and the megaspore mother cell in the ovule. It occupies the greater part of the life cycle, and all cells of the plant body are diploid.
- The **gametophyte** generation ($n$, sexual generation): from the uninucleate pollen grain (microspore) and the uninucleate embryo sac (megaspore) produced by meiosis, to the formation of the pollen tube containing two sperm cells and the mature embryo sac containing the egg cell, and the completion of double fertilization. It occupies only a very short part of the life cycle and cannot live independently of the sporophyte.

**Meiosis** and fertilization are the turning points between the two generations and are the key to the whole life cycle: meiosis reduces the chromosome number from $2n$ to $n$ and carries the life cycle from the sporophyte into the gametophyte; fusion of sperm and egg restores the chromosome number to $2n$ and returns the life cycle to the sporophyte. Fertilization in angiosperms is double fertilization, which, besides producing a new sporophyte (the embryo), at the same time produces a tissue that belongs neither to the sporophyte nor to the gametophyte, the triploid endosperm.

![The life cycle of angiosperms (blue background: diploid sporophyte generation; orange background: haploid gametophyte generation). The adult plant flowers; pollen mother cells in the anther and the megaspore mother cell in the ovule undergo meiosis and produce microspores (a tetrad) and megaspores (in the linear tetrad of which the one at the chalazal end is functional); the microspore develops into a pollen grain with a vegetative cell and a generative cell, and the megaspore develops by three nuclear divisions into an embryo sac of 7 cells and 8 nuclei; after pollination the pollen tube delivers two sperm cells into the embryo sac and double fertilization is completed: sperm and egg fuse into the zygote ($2n$), and the other sperm fuses with the two polar nuclei into the primary endosperm nucleus ($3n$); the ovule develops into the seed (embryo $2n$, endosperm $3n$, seed coat maternal $2n$) and the ovary into the fruit; the seed germinates into a seedling and grows into a new plant](./plots/angiosperm-life-cycle.py)

### 30.5.2. Comparison with Ferns and Gymnosperms

From ferns through gymnosperms to angiosperms the life cycle changes in one direction: the sporophyte becomes ever more elaborate, and the gametophyte ever more reduced and ever more dependent on the sporophyte ([7.12.18](/7.12.18), [7.12.25](/7.12.25)).

| Aspect | Ferns (homosporous, e.g. bracken) | Gymnosperms (pine) | Angiosperms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sporophyte | With root, stem and leaf, independent, perennial herb | Tall woody plant with secondary growth | Woody or herbaceous, with the most complex structure |
| Spores | Homosporous, shed | Heterosporous; the megaspore stays in the nucellus | Heterosporous; the megaspore stays in the nucellus |
| Male gametophyte | Prothallus (bisexual), independent | Pollen grain, 4 cells when shed (including 2 prothallial cells) | Pollen grain, 2 or 3 cells |
| Female gametophyte | Archegonia on the prothallus | Thousands of cells, with archegonia, inside the ovule | Embryo sac, 7 cells and 8 nuclei, without archegonia |
| Nutrition of the gametophyte | Autotrophic | Dependent on the sporophyte | Dependent on the sporophyte |
| Male gamete | Multiflagellate sperm | Nonflagellate sperm (flagellate in cycads and ginkgo) | Nonflagellate sperm cell |
| Condition for fertilization | External water needed | Pollen tube, no external water needed | Pollen tube, no external water needed |
| Pollination to fertilization | No pollination | About 13–15 months | A few hours to a few days |
| Mode of fertilization | Single fertilization | Single fertilization | Double fertilization |
| Nutritive tissue of the embryo | Gametophyte | Female gametophyte ($n$), formed before fertilization | Endosperm (mostly $3n$), formed after fertilization |
| Dispersal unit | Spore | Seed, naked | Seed, enclosed in the fruit |
| Timing of meiosis | Spores form in the sporangium | In the pollen sac and nucellus | In the anther and nucellus |

In both gymnosperms and angiosperms the sporophyte is completely dominant and the gametophyte is parasitic on it, which is a common feature of the life cycle of seed plants; but in angiosperms the gametophyte is still more reduced and the sporophyte more complex, and the time from pollination to seed production is greatly shortened, which lets angiosperms complete the life cycle within one growing season, so that annual herbs are almost all angiosperms.

## Summary

Embryo development compared:

| Stage | Dicot (shepherd's purse) | Monocot (wheat) |
|---|---|---|
| Two-cell | The zygote divides transversely and unequally: apical cell (away from the micropyle), basal cell (near the micropyle, vacuolated) | The zygote divides obliquely |
| Proembryo | The apical cell divides twice longitudinally → quadrant → transverse division → octant; the basal cell divides transversely into a suspensor of 6–10 cells | Two oblique divisions → 4-cell proembryo → club-shaped embryo of 16–32 cells, with embryo proper above and suspensor below |
| Organ primordia | Globular embryo: periclinal divisions set apart the protoderm; protrusions on both sides of the apex form two cotyledons, the embryo is heart-shaped, and the plumule differentiates in the depression between the cotyledons | A notch appears on one side of the club-shaped embryo (shoot apical meristem), and the part above the notch develops into the cotyledon |
| Radicle | Formed jointly by the hypophysis at the tip of the suspensor and cells at the base of the globular embryo | Primordia of radicle and coleorhiza appear at the lower end of the embryo, with a schizogenous cavity between them |
| Later stages | Cotyledons and axis elongate by transverse divisions (torpedo shape) and, constrained by space, bend into a horseshoe | The cotyledon grows intercalarily into the scutellum; the coleoptile appears outside the apical meristem; the epiblast appears opposite the cotyledon |
| Suspensor | Transfer-cell-like, supplying nutrients and hormones; degenerates after the complete embryo forms | Degenerates |
| Mature embryo | 2 cotyledons, plumule, axis, radicle | Scutellum, plumule with coleoptile, radicle with coleorhiza, epiblast (absent in maize) |

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nuclear | × | × (free-nuclear stage) | All cellularized; only one or two peripheral layers; only the micropylar end; or none (coconut water) | Monocots and most dicots |

| Helobial | √ (transverse wall setting off micropylar and chalazal chambers) | × | The micropylar chamber cellularizes, the chalazal chamber stays free-nuclear | Alismatales and other monocots |
| No endosperm formed | — | — | The primary endosperm nucleus degenerates, or the central cell is not fertilized (Podostemaceae) | Orchidaceae, Podostemaceae, *Trapa* |

| Structure in the ovule | Structure in the seed | Ploidy |
|---|---|---|
| Zygote (egg cell + sperm cell) | Embryo (through the proembryo and suspensor stages) | $2n$ |

The life cycle of angiosperms: sporophyte ($2n$) → flower → meiosis in anthers and ovules → microspores and megaspores ($n$) → pollen grains and embryo sacs (gametophytes) → sperm cells and egg cell → double fertilization → zygote ($2n$) and primary endosperm nucleus ($3n$) → seed and fruit → germination → sporophyte. The two turning points are meiosis and double fertilization.

## Questions

1. In the embryogenesis of shepherd's purse, what do the "apical cell" and the "basal cell" each develop into? Which cells form the radicle?

   **Answer** The apical cell, after two longitudinal divisions and one transverse division, becomes the octant; the 4 cells of the upper tier form the cotyledons and the shoot apex (plumule), and the 4 cells of the lower tier form the hypocotyl (and most of the radicle); the outer layer set apart by periclinal divisions becomes the protoderm. The basal cell divides transversely into a row forming the suspensor, and the cell at the top of the suspensor next to the embryo proper is the hypophysis, whose descendants form the quiescent center of the root apex and the central cells of the root cap. The radicle is thus formed jointly by cells at the base of the globular embryo and the hypophysis at the tip of the suspensor, which is the only contribution of the basal cell to the embryo proper; the other suspensor cells degenerate at the torpedo stage.

2. In a maize grain and a broad bean seed, which tissues come from the mother and which from fertilization? State the ploidy of each.

   **Answer** The maize grain is a caryopsis: the pericarp (ovary wall) and the remaining seed coat (integuments) are maternal, $2n$; the endosperm comes from the primary endosperm nucleus, $3n$ (two maternal sets, one paternal); the embryo comes from the zygote, $2n$. In the broad bean seed the seed coat develops from the outer integument and is maternal, $2n$; the embryo (including the thick cotyledons that store nutrients) comes from the zygote, $2n$; the endosperm is absorbed by the embryo in development and is absent at maturity. The pod (pericarp) of broad bean is also maternal tissue.

3. Why is helobial endosperm called "intermediate between the nuclear and cellular types"? What observation would distinguish the three types?

   **Answer** In the helobial type the first division is accompanied by cell wall formation (as in the cellular type) and divides the embryo sac into a micropylar and a chalazal chamber; the later divisions in both chambers are free-nuclear (as in the nuclear type), and the micropylar chamber cellularizes later. Observe a longitudinal section of the embryo sac soon after fertilization: if after the first division the sac contains only two free nuclei with no wall, it is nuclear; if a transverse wall appears and every later division is accompanied by a wall, it is cellular; if a transverse wall sets off one large and one small chamber and free nuclei then appear in each chamber, it is helobial.

4. A seed of *Citrus* is sown and gives three seedlings, two of which closely resemble the mother plant and one is clearly different. How is this explained? What use does this phenomenon have in breeding?

   **Answer** *Citrus* has adventitious embryos: after fertilization nucellar cells invade the embryo sac and develop into nucellar embryos, which coexist with the zygotic embryo in one seed (polyembryony). Nucellar embryos are formed by mitosis from somatic cells of the mother, so their genotype is the same as that of the mother plant and the seedlings resemble it; the odd one comes from the zygotic embryo and is a product of sexual reproduction. Nucellar seedlings are uniform and generally free of the mother's viruses and can be used as rootstocks or to maintain the cultivar; but in cross breeding they interfere with the recovery of hybrids, and the zygotic seedlings must be identified among the polyembryonic seedlings by morphology or molecular markers.

5. How does the endosperm of angiosperms differ from the "endosperm" of pine? From the standpoint of the plant's "investment" of nutrients, what is the advantage of the angiosperm way?

   **Answer** The "endosperm" of pine is the female gametophyte, which develops from the megaspore by free-nuclear divisions, is haploid, and has already accumulated nutrients before fertilization (in the spring of the second year after pollination); the endosperm of angiosperms arises from the second fusion of double fertilization, is mostly triploid, contains paternal genetic material, and begins to develop only after fertilization. In pine a storage tissue is established in the ovule whether or not it is fertilized, and the nutrients in unfertilized ovules are wasted; angiosperms establish endosperm only in ovules that have been successfully fertilized, and unfertilized ovules abort very early, so the mother's investment is more efficient. This suits the short life cycle and the large number of ovules of angiosperms.
