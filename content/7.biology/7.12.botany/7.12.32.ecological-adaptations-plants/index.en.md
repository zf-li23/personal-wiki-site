The preceding chapters described the form and structure of roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds ([7.12.19](/7.12.19)–[7.12.31](/7.12.31)). The same organ differs enormously from plant to plant, and a large part of that difference is adaptation to the environment: in desert plants the leaves are reduced and the stems thick and juicy; in aquatic plants the leaves are finely divided and the body is full of air spaces; in understory plants the leaves are large and thin. This chapter gathers these phenomena, scattered through earlier chapters, by environmental factor. We first discuss life forms, which summarize how plants cope with unfavorable seasons (§32.1), then morphological and anatomical adaptations to water (§32.2) and to light and temperature (§32.3), then special ways of life such as parasitism, mycoheterotrophy, carnivory, epiphytism, climbing and living with ants (§32.4), and finally the relationships of plants with pollinators, dispersers, herbivores and microbes (§32.5). This chapter treats only the form and structure of individual plants; the physiological mechanisms of water relations, photosynthesis and stress resistance belong to plant physiology, questions at the level of populations, communities and ecosystems belong to ecology, and the theory of coevolution is treated in [7.10.17](/7.10.17).

## 32.1. Life Forms

### 32.1.1. Raunkiær Life Forms

[7.12.1](/7.12.1) classified plants by the texture and longevity of the stem into growth forms such as trees, shrubs, herbs and climbers. In 1905–1907 the Danish botanist Raunkiær proposed another classification that focuses on adaptation: how a plant survives the unfavorable season of the year (a cold winter or a dry season) depends on how much protection its renewal buds (dormant buds) receive, which is determined by their position.

**Definition 32.1.1 (Life Form)** The type of outward appearance that a plant shows as a result of long-term adaptation to its environment is called its **life form**. Raunkiær life forms are divided into five classes according to the position of the renewal buds (or seeds) during the unfavorable season:

1. Phanerophytes: the renewal buds are on shoots more than 25 cm above the ground. They include trees, shrubs and most woody climbers, and by height are divided into megaphanerophytes (over 30 m), mesophanerophytes (8–30 m), microphanerophytes (2–8 m) and nanophanerophytes (0.25–2 m).
2. Chamaephytes: the renewal buds are above the ground but below 25 cm, and are often protected in winter by snow or litter. Examples are cowberry, thyme, and many cushion plants and subshrubs.
3. Hemicryptophytes: the aerial parts die in the unfavorable season and the renewal buds lie at ground level, protected by dead leaves and the surface layer of the soil. Examples are rosette herbs such as dandelion and plantain, and many tufted grasses.
4. Cryptophytes, also called geophytes in the broad sense: the renewal buds are buried in the soil or under water. Those that overwinter by bulbs, tubers, corms or rhizomes are called geophytes (for example lily, potato and tulip); marsh plants are called helophytes, and aquatic plants hydrophytes.
5. Therophytes (annuals): the whole plant dies in the unfavorable season and survives only as seeds.

From the first class to the last, the renewal buds lie progressively closer to the ground and are progressively better protected (Figure 1).

### 32.1.2. The Life-Form Spectrum

The percentages of species in the flora of a region that belong to each life form are called the life-form spectrum. Raunkiær drew about 1000 species at random from around the world and obtained the "normal spectrum": phanerophytes 46%, chamaephytes 9%, hemicryptophytes 26%, cryptophytes 6% and therophytes 13%. Comparing the spectrum of a region with it reveals the character of the local climate:

| Climate | Dominant life forms | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tropical rainforest | Phanerophytes | Warm and humid all year, with no unfavorable season, so the buds need no protection |
| Temperate deciduous forest and grassland | Hemicryptophytes | Cold winters; buds at ground level are protected by litter and snow |
| Polar and alpine regions | Chamaephytes, hemicryptophytes | Short growing season; small plants overwinter under the snow |
| Mediterranean climate | Cryptophytes (geophytes), therophytes | Dry summers, survived as bulbs, corms or seeds |
| Desert | Therophytes | Long dry periods and unpredictable rainfall; survival as seeds, with the life cycle completed rapidly after rain |

Life forms reflect convergent adaptation and are unrelated to phylogeny: the succulents of the Cactaceae and the Euphorbiaceae, and the ephemerals of different families in deserts, have each independently arrived at similar life forms. The use of life-form spectra in the study of communities and vegetation belongs to ecology.

## 32.2. Adaptations to Water

### 32.2.1. Water Ecotypes of Plants

The water in a plant is consumed mainly by transpiration, and the leaf is the organ of transpiration, so the relation of a plant to water is reflected first of all in the form and structure of the leaf ([7.12.23](/7.12.23)). By their relation to water, plants are divided into terrestrial and aquatic plants, and terrestrial plants into xerophytes, mesophytes and hygrophytes.

**Definition 32.2.1 (Xerophytes and Hydrophytes)** A terrestrial plant that can endure long drought and grow normally, with very strong drought resistance, is called a **xerophyte**. A plant that lives wholly or partly in water is called a **hydrophyte**.

Mesophytes lie between the two and grow best where water is moderate; most crops and the broad-leaved trees and herbs of temperate regions belong here, and the leaf structure described in the preceding chapters is generally that of mesophytes. Hygrophytes grow in moist environments and have little drought resistance, such as the begonias of damp spots in forests and the rushes and lobelias at the edges of marshes. Figure 2 compares the leaves of the three groups.

B: mesophyte (dorsiventral leaf of a dicot): a single epidermis, a single layer of palisade parenchyma, spongy mesophyll with large intercellular spaces, stomata in the lower epidermis. ### 32.2.2. Xerophytes

Xerophytes use several different strategies to cope with drought:

- Drought escape: the life cycle is completed before the drought arrives, and the dry season is passed as seeds. Desert ephemerals germinate, flower and fruit within a few weeks after rain, as do many small crucifers and borages in the early spring of the Junggar Desert in Xinjiang, China. They are not xeromorphic in form; they are annuals or geophytes that pass the dry summer as bulbs.
- Water uptake: a well-developed root system takes up water from deep soil. The roots of mesquite (*Prosopis*) may reach tens of meters, and the root systems of camelthorn and saxaul are also far larger than the aerial parts. Many cacti do the opposite, with very shallow, widely spreading roots that quickly absorb surface water after light rain.
- Water conservation: reduced transpiring surface and slowed transpiration, as in sclerophyllous xerophytes.
- Water storage: large amounts of water are stored in the body, as in succulent xerophytes (succulents).
- Drought tolerance: the protoplasm can endure extreme water loss without dying. The spikemoss *Selaginella tamariscina* (the "resurrection plant") curls up into a ball in drought, its water content falling very low, and unrolls and turns green again after rain; such plants are called resurrection plants.

The morphological and structural features of sclerophyllous xerophytes are as follows.

- The plant is small with a well-developed root system. The leaves are small, thick and hard, or hairy, and in some the leaves are reduced to scales or spines and photosynthesis is carried out by green stems, as in *Ephedra*, saxaul, *Calligonum* and *Casuarina*.
- The epidermal cells are small with thick outer walls, and the **cuticle** is well developed, often with a wax coating. In some plants the epidermis is a multiple epidermis of several cell layers, or beneath the epidermis there are one to several layers of thick-walled hypodermis.
- The stomata are sunken or restricted to stomatal crypts on the lower leaf surface. The leaf of oleander (*Nerium oleander*) is typical: both upper and lower epidermis are multiple, and the lower epidermis is invaginated into many crypts bearing epidermal hairs, with the stomata in the epidermis of the crypt. Air movement in the crypt is slow and its humidity is high, which slows transpiration.
- The mesophyll has a multilayered palisade parenchyma, sometimes on both faces (isobilateral leaf), and the spongy mesophyll and intercellular spaces are poorly developed. The veins are dense and the mechanical tissue is abundant, so the leaf does not wilt or deform in drought.
- In some leaves rolling occurs in drought: in feather grass (*Stipa*), fescue (*Festuca*) and sand-dwelling grasses, the upper leaf surface has grooves and bulliform cells with the stomata concentrated in the grooves, and on water loss the blade rolls lengthwise toward the upper surface into a tube that encloses the stomata (for bulliform cells see [7.12.23](/7.12.23)).
- The pine needle also has a xeromorphic structure: thick-walled epidermis, well-developed hypodermis, sunken stomata and mesophyll cells with infolded walls ([7.12.23](/7.12.23)).

These structures, together with the low water content of the protoplasm and the high osmotic pressure of the cell sap, give xerophytes strong drought resistance.

In succulent xerophytes (succulents) the leaves or stems are thick and juicy, with well-developed thin-walled water-storage parenchyma that holds a large amount of water, and water loss is also small. Succulent leaves occur in purslane, stonecrop (*Sedum*), aloe, agave and living stones (*Lithops*); succulent stems occur in the Cactaceae, in *Euphorbia* of the Euphorbiaceae (the African "cactus-like" spurges) and in the carrion flower (*Stapelia*) of the Apocynaceae, and the leaves are often reduced to spines while the stems are ribbed to allow swelling and shrinking. Many succulents carry out **crassulacean acid metabolism**: the stomata open at night to take up $\ce{CO2}$ and close during the day, greatly reducing transpirational water loss (for the biochemistry see [7.1.25](/7.1.25)). Stonecrop often grows between roof tiles, which illustrates its drought resistance.

### 32.2.3. Hydrophytes

Hydrophytes do not lack water; the problem is how to obtain enough gas and light, since $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ diffuse about ten thousand times more slowly in water than in air, and light weakens rapidly with depth. By their relation to the water surface, hydrophytes are divided into several groups:

| Type | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Submerged plants | The whole plant is under water | Hornwort (*Ceratophyllum*), *Hydrilla*, *Vallisneria*, curly pondweed (*Potamogeton crispus*), water milfoil |
| Floating-leaved plants | Rooted at the bottom, leaves floating on the surface | Water lily, floating heart (*Nymphoides*), water chestnut (*Trapa*), floating leaves of pondweed |
| Free-floating plants | The plant floats on the surface with the roots hanging in the water | Duckweed, water hyacinth, water lettuce, *Azolla*, floating fern (*Salvinia*) |
| Emergent plants | Rooted at the bottom, with stems and leaves mostly above water | Lotus, reed, cattail, arrowhead (*Sagittaria*), rice |

The submerged leaf is utterly unlike the leaf of a xerophyte and represents the other extreme:

- The leaves are generally small and thin, and some are divided into threads (hornwort, water milfoil), which increases the surface in contact with water and for gas uptake.
- The epidermal cell walls are thin, not cutinized or only slightly so, and the cells contain chloroplasts and there are no stomata; water, dissolved gases and salts are absorbed directly, so the epidermis of a submerged leaf combines absorption and assimilation.
- The mesophyll is poorly developed, with no differentiation into palisade and spongy tissue, and few cell layers to let light through: in *Potamogeton* the leaf is mostly only three cells thick except near the midrib.
- Vascular and mechanical tissues are extremely reduced, the xylem often being left as a single cavity, because support and conduction matter little in water.
- The intercellular spaces are especially well developed and form **aerenchyma**, which stores the $\ce{O2}$ released by photosynthesis and the $\ce{CO2}$ released by respiration so that each can be used by the other process, and also lets the plant float and stand upright.

Floating leaves have stomata only in the upper epidermis and well-developed aerenchyma in the mesophyll; in the petiole and blade of the water lily star-shaped sclereids also give support. The surface of a lotus leaf bears micrometer-scale papillae and wax crystals, which keep water drops from spreading so that they roll off and carry dust away (the "lotus effect"). The petiole of water hyacinth swells into an air bladder full of aerenchyma, which keeps the plant afloat. The roots, rhizomes and stems of emergent and wetland plants also have well-developed aerenchyma; the channels in the rhizome of the lotus communicate with the air cavities of the petioles and blades, so that air entering through the leaves is carried all the way to the rhizome buried in oxygen-poor mud. The aerial leaves that stand above the water resemble the leaves of mesophytes in structure.

Many aquatic plants show heterophylly ([7.12.23](/7.12.23)): on the same plant the submerged leaves are finely divided or ribbon-like and the floating or aerial leaves are broad and entire, as in water buttercup, water chestnut and arrowhead.

### 32.2.4. Halophytes

When the salt content of the soil is high, plants have difficulty taking up water (the osmotic pressure of the soil solution is high) and are also poisoned by ions.

**Definition 32.2.2 (Halophyte)** A plant that grows normally and completes its life cycle in saline soil or sea water of very high salt content is called a **halophyte**.

By the way in which they deal with salt, halophytes fall into three groups:

- Salt accumulators (true halophytes): the salt taken up is accumulated in the vacuoles, and the high osmotic pressure lets the plant take up water from saline soil, while stems and leaves become succulent and store large amounts of water that dilutes the salt. Examples are annual seepweed (*Suaeda salsa*) and glasswort (*Salicornia europaea*).
- Salt secretors: after absorbing salt the plant excretes it through salt glands on the surface of stems and leaves, and white salt crystals are often visible on the leaf surface. Examples are tamarisk, sea lavender (*Limonium*), *Aeluropus*, and the mangrove *Avicennia marina*. In saltbush (*Atriplex*) the leaf surface bears bladder-like salt bladder hairs in which salt accumulates, and the bladders burst and fall off with the salt.
- Salt excluders: the roots are of low permeability to salt and take up essentially no salt from the soil, as in reed and in the roots of *Rhizophora*.

Mangroves are woody plant communities of the intertidal zone of tropical and subtropical coasts. The mangrove plants that compose them come from different families: Rhizophoraceae (*Rhizophora*, *Kandelia*, *Bruguiera*), the grey mangrove *Avicennia marina* of the Acanthaceae, *Sonneratia* of the Lythrales, and the river mangrove *Aegiceras corniculatum* of the Primulaceae. They have convergently adapted in form to salt, to oxygen-poor mud and to the scouring of tides:

- Prop roots and pneumatophores: *Rhizophora* produces arching prop roots from the trunk and branches; *Avicennia* and *Sonneratia* have peg-like pneumatophores, and *Bruguiera* has knee roots; the roots bear many lenticels and have well-developed aerenchyma ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)).
- *Vivipary*: in the Rhizophoraceae the fruit does not fall when ripe, and the seed germinates in the fruit without dormancy; the hypocotyl elongates, breaks through the pericarp and grows into a long club-shaped propagule (up to 20–40 cm in *Kandelia*), which continues to grow on the mother tree for some time and then falls, to stick in the mud or drift with seawater and take root elsewhere. In *Avicennia* and *Aegiceras* the embryo germinates inside the fruit without breaking through the pericarp, which is called cryptovivipary. Vivipary spares the seedling the difficulty of germinating in salt water and mud.
- Salt handling: *Avicennia* and *Aegiceras* excrete salt through salt glands, *Rhizophora* relies mainly on salt exclusion by the roots, and the leaves are mostly succulent with water-storage tissue.

## 32.3. Adaptations to Light and Temperature

### 32.3.1. Sun Plants, Shade Plants and Shade-Tolerant Plants

**Definition 32.3.1 (Sun Plants and Shade Plants)** A plant that grows well in full direct sunlight and poorly in shade is called a **sun plant** (heliophyte). A plant that grows well under weaker light (in a shaded environment) is called a **shade plant** (sciophyte).

Most crops, plants of grassland and desert, plants that flower before their leaves emerge, and trees of the upper layer of a community are sun plants, such as pine, poplar, willow and birch. Shade plants grow mostly in damp shady places or under dense forest, such as many ferns, ginseng, *Panax notoginseng*, wood sorrel and begonia. A shade plant does not require the weakest possible light: when the light is so weak that it does not reach the plant's light compensation point, it cannot grow normally either. Shade-tolerant plants lie between the two: they grow best in full sun but can also endure moderate shade, such as spruce, fir, yew, beech and walnut, and species differ greatly in the degree of shade tolerance. The division into sun plants and shade plants is significant in forestry (the combination of species for afforestation), in intercropping and in landscaping.

### 32.3.2. Sun Leaves and Shade Leaves

The leaves of a sun plant receive strong light and transpire strongly, so they tend toward a xeromorphic form, whereas the leaves of a shade plant are suited to absorbing and using light under weak illumination. The leaf structure of one species also changes when it grows under different light, and even on a single plant the sun leaves facing the light differ from the shade leaves facing away from it, as with leaves on the south side of the crown of a sugar maple and those inside the crown.

| | Sun leaf (sun plant) | Shade leaf (shade plant) |
|---|---|---|
| Blade | Smaller and thicker | Larger and thinner |
| Cuticle and epidermis | Thicker cuticle; epidermal cells small, thick-walled and closely packed, sometimes with hairs | Thin cuticle; epidermal cells sometimes with chloroplasts |
| Stomata | Small and dense | Fewer |
| Palisade parenchyma | Well developed, often 2–3 layers, sometimes on both faces | Poorly developed, often a single layer of shorter cells |
| Spongy mesophyll and intercellular spaces | Not very well developed, small spaces | Intercellular spaces well developed |
| Chloroplasts | Smaller; chlorophyll content per unit area higher | Larger, with more grana; chlorophyll content per unit mass higher, with a higher proportion of chlorophyll b |
| Veins and mechanical tissue | Veins fine, dense and long; mechanical tissue well developed | Veins sparser; little mechanical tissue |
| Significance | Adaptation to strong light and strong transpiration | Full capture of weak light, with less investment in building the leaf |

In some sun plants the epidermal hairs are dead cells whose lumina are filled with air and appear white, reflecting light and preventing the leaf from overheating in strong light. Sun plants tend toward xeromorphic form, but this does not make them xerophytes: many xerophytes are sun plants, but among sun plants there are also hygrophytes and even hydrophytes, and rice is both a hydrophyte and a sun plant.

These structural differences between sun and shade leaves appear in photosynthesis as different light-response curves (Figure 3). The net photosynthetic rate $A$ of a leaf as a function of light intensity $I$ is often described by a non-rectangular hyperbola:

$$
\theta A_{\mathrm{g}}^2 - (\phi I + A_{\max})A_{\mathrm{g}} + \phi I A_{\max} = 0,\qquad A = A_{\mathrm{g}} - R_{\mathrm{d}},
$$

where $A_{\mathrm{g}}$ is the gross photosynthetic rate, taken as the smaller root of the equation. Each of the four parameters has a biological meaning. $\phi$ is the initial slope of the curve under weak light, that is, the apparent quantum yield (the $\ce{CO2}$ fixed per photon absorbed), and is similar in sun and shade leaves. $A_{\max}$ is the maximum gross photosynthetic rate at light saturation, which depends on the photosynthetic machinery per unit leaf area (the thickness of the palisade parenchyma and the amounts of enzymes and electron-transport chains) and is much larger in sun leaves. $R_{\mathrm{d}}$ is the respiration rate of the leaf, which is also higher in sun leaves. $\theta$ (between 0 and 1) describes how sharply the curve turns from the linear to the saturated part. The intersection of the curve with the horizontal axis, $A = 0$, is the light compensation point, at which the $\ce{CO2}$ fixed by photosynthesis equals that released by respiration; the light intensity beyond which $A$ no longer increases appreciably is the light saturation point. Shade leaves have a low respiration rate and a low light compensation point and can keep a positive carbon balance in the weak light of the forest floor. Sun leaves have a high light saturation point and a high maximum photosynthetic rate and fix more carbon in strong light, but under weak light they do worse than shade leaves because of their large respiratory expense. The biochemistry of photosynthesis and models of leaf gas exchange belong to plant physiology.

### 32.3.3. Adaptations to Temperature

Temperature and water often act together; only the morphological adaptations are sketched here.

- Cold: temperate deciduous trees shed their leaves in winter ([7.12.23](/7.12.23)) and their buds are protected by bud scales. Alpine and polar plants are small and cushion-like or rosette-like, hugging the ground to take advantage of the higher temperature at the surface and the protection of snow, as in cushion rock jasmine (*Androsace*) and sandwort (*Arenaria*). In the "snow lotus" species of *Saussurea*, such as *S. laniceps*, the whole plant is densely covered with long white woolly hairs that form a layer of insulation, whereas the Tianshan snow lotus (*S. involucrata*) encloses its capitulum in large membranous bracts. The inflorescence of the Himalayan *Rheum nobile* is enclosed in large translucent bracts like a greenhouse, within which the temperature is clearly higher than outside and ultraviolet light is blocked.
- Heat: leaves are held upright or with the margin upward (many eucalypts have drooping leaves that present their edge to the midday sun), and the surface is densely covered with white hairs or a wax coating that reflects sunlight. Thick bark lets trees withstand forest fires (as in the giant sequoia of North America), and in species such as jack pine (*Pinus banksiana*) the cones are sealed by resin and open to release seeds only after a fire.

## 32.4. Special Ways of Life

### 32.4.1. Parasitic Plants

**Definition 32.4.1 (Parasitic Plant)** A plant that obtains all or part of its water, mineral salts and organic nutrients from another plant (the host) is called a **parasitic plant**. A parasitic plant that cannot photosynthesize at all and depends entirely on the host for nutrition is called a holoparasite; one that has chlorophyll and can make organic matter itself, taking only water and mineral salts from the host, is called a hemiparasite.

Parasitic plants insert specialized **haustoria** into host tissue, connecting with the host's vascular tissue ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)). By the site of parasitism they are divided into stem parasites and root parasites:

| | Stem parasites | Root parasites |
|---|---|---|
| Holoparasites | *Cuscuta* (Convolvulaceae) | *Orobanche* and *Aeginetia* (Orobanchaceae), *Rafflesia* (Rafflesiaceae), *Cynomorium*, *Cistanche* |
| Hemiparasites | Loranthaceae (*Loranthus*, *Taxillus*), mistletoe (*Viscum*, Santalaceae) | *Striga* and lousewort (*Pedicularis*) (Orobanchaceae), sandalwood |

Dodder (*Cuscuta chinensis*) is the best-studied stem parasite (Figure 4). After its seed germinates in the soil the seedling has no cotyledons and no true root; the thread-like stem makes circling movements in the air to find a host, twines around it once it touches one, and the original root dies. On the side of the stem in contact with the host a haustorium forms: first a mass of dividing cells forms a disc-shaped holdfast pressed against the host surface, and then cells in the center pass through the host epidermis and cortex. On reaching the vascular tissue, cells at the tip of the haustorium differentiate hypha-like "searching cells" that connect to the host's vessels and sieve tubes; some of these differentiate into vessel elements joined to the host xylem, and others join the host phloem, forming continuous conduction pathways. The leaves of dodder are reduced to scales, and the stem is yellow or orange and can hardly photosynthesize. Between host and dodder flow not only water and nutrients but also large amounts of mRNA, protein and even genes. A single dodder plant can parasitize several plants at once, linking them into a network and passing signals among them. Dodder is a harmful weed of crops such as soybean and is also the source of the traditional medicine "tusizi".

*Rafflesia arnoldii* is the most extreme holoparasite: it has no roots, stems or leaves, its vegetative body is reduced to fungus-like filaments of cells, and it lives entirely within the roots and stems of its host (*Tetrastigma* of the Vitaceae), with only the flower emerging. Its flower may reach 1 m in diameter, the largest single flower in the world, and gives off the smell of rotting meat to attract flies as pollinators. Molecular phylogenetics shows that the Rafflesiaceae belong to the Malpighiales and are close to the Euphorbiaceae; its mitochondrial genome contains many genes from the host.

The Loranthaceae and mistletoes are evergreen hemiparasitic shrubs that grow on tree branches; their leaves are green, and their haustoria connect only to the host xylem, from which they obtain water and mineral salts. Their berries have sticky mucilage and are dispersed by birds ([7.12.31](/7.12.31)). The seeds of *Striga* are tiny and germinate only on contact with strigolactone secreted by host roots; the seedling then forms a haustorium that invades the host root. It is a serious pest of maize, sorghum and millet in the semi-arid regions of Africa and Asia.

The parasitic habit has originated independently at least 12 times in the angiosperms and involves about 4500 species, concentrated in the Santalales, the Orobanchaceae (Lamiales), the Convolvulaceae (Solanales) and other groups ([7.12.45](/7.12.45), [7.12.47](/7.12.47), [7.12.48](/7.12.48)). From hemiparasite to holoparasite, plants progressively lose leaves, roots and chlorophyll, and the photosynthesis genes of the plastid genome are also gradually lost.

### 32.4.2. Mycoheterotrophs

**Definition 32.4.2 (Mycoheterotroph)** A plant that does not photosynthesize, or does not rely entirely on photosynthesis, and obtains organic carbon through mycorrhizal fungi is called a **mycoheterotroph**. Those whose carbon comes entirely from fungi are called full mycoheterotrophs, and those whose carbon comes partly from fungi partial mycoheterotrophs.

Mycoheterotrophs used to be wrongly called "saprophytes", in the belief that they decompose humus directly. In fact no vascular plant can decompose organic matter itself; they obtain carbon from the symbiotic **mycorrhizal** fungi ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)), whose carbon in turn comes from their ectomycorrhizal association with forest trees or from the decomposition of litter. Examples are the following.

- *Gastrodia elata* (Orchidaceae) has no green leaves, only an underground tuber and a flowering stem produced once a year. Seed germination depends on nutrition from fungi such as *Mycena*, and growth of the tuber depends on digesting the honey fungus (*Armillaria*) that invades it, while the honey fungus obtains its own nutrition from tree roots or rotting wood. The artificial cultivation of *Gastrodia* is based on this symbiotic relationship.
- Ghost pipe (*Monotropa uniflora*, Ericaceae) is white throughout and has no chlorophyll; through fungi of the Russulaceae it obtains carbohydrates from the ectomycorrhizas of neighboring trees, so that it is in effect indirectly parasitic on the trees.

Orchid seeds are minute and have no endosperm ([7.12.30](/7.12.30)); all orchids are mycoheterotrophic during germination and the protocorm stage, most turn green and autotrophic as adults, and a few remain mycoheterotrophic throughout life. Mycoheterotrophy has also arisen independently many times in the angiosperms and occurs in the Orchidaceae, Ericaceae, Burmanniaceae, Triuridaceae, Gentianaceae and others.

### 32.4.3. Carnivorous Plants

**Definition 32.4.3 (Carnivorous Plant)** A plant that lures, captures and digests small animals (mainly insects) and absorbs their nutrients is called a **carnivorous plant**.

The four types of trap leaves (pitcher, adhesive, snap and bladder) are described in [7.12.24](/7.12.24). Carnivorous plants mostly live in sunny, moist, nutrient-poor environments such as acidic peat bogs, wet sand or epiphytic sites, where the soil contains little nitrogen and phosphorus, and the animals captured supply these two elements. They generally have chloroplasts and can photosynthesize, and can survive without animal food, but grow better and set more seed when suitable animal food is available. The trapping structures consume photosynthate (glands, nectar, digestive enzymes) and reduce the photosynthetic area of the leaf, so carnivory pays only in habitats where light and water are abundant and nutrients are the main limitation. This cost–benefit explanation shows why carnivorous plants are concentrated almost entirely in sunny bogs. There are about 800 species of carnivorous plants worldwide; the habit has originated independently at least 10 times, in the Caryophyllales (Nepenthaceae, Droseraceae), the Lamiales (Lentibulariaceae), the Ericales (Sarraceniaceae) and others. Some carnivorous plants have more complex relationships with animals: the pitchers of several Bornean *Nepenthes* are shaped like toilet bowls, and tree shrews lick the nectar on the lid while defecating into the pitcher, from whose droppings the plant gets more nitrogen than from insects.

### 32.4.4. Epiphytes

**Definition 32.4.4 (Epiphyte)** A plant that grows on another plant (mainly on the trunk and branches of trees), using it for support without taking nutrients from it, is called an **epiphyte**.

Epiphytes are most common in the moist forests of the tropics and subtropics. There are about 30 000 species worldwide, about a tenth of all vascular plants, with the Orchidaceae, Bromeliaceae, Araceae, Piperaceae (*Peperomia*) and ferns the most numerous; mosses and lichens are even more commonly epiphytic. The epiphytic way of life lets plants reach positions in the canopy with better light, but their roots cannot reach the soil and the supply of water and nutrients is intermittent, so they often show xeromorphic features:

- Orchidaceae: the aerial roots are covered with a velamen of several layers of dead cells, which fills with air when dry and absorbs water quickly after rain ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)); the stem base swells into storage pseudobulbs; the leaves are thick and leathery, and many carry out crassulacean acid metabolism.
- Bromeliaceae: in many species the leaf bases overlap tightly into a "tank" that stores rainwater and organic debris that falls in, and the tank is home to many small animals such as frogs and insect larvae; the leaf surface is densely covered with shield-shaped absorptive scales that can take up water and nutrients directly. Spanish moss (*Tillandsia usneoides*) has almost no roots and absorbs water over its whole surface by its scales, hanging from trees like a lichen.
- Ferns: the leaves of the bird's-nest fern (*Asplenium nidus*) form a funnel that collects fallen leaves into a "humus basket"; basket ferns (*Drynaria*) and staghorn ferns (*Platycerium*) have two kinds of leaves, sterile leaves pressed against the trunk to collect humus and spreading fertile leaves.

There are also hemiepiphytes, whose life cycle is partly epiphytic and partly rooted in the ground: the seeds of many tropical figs (*Ficus*) are carried by birds to a branch and germinate there, the plant is at first epiphytic, and then aerial roots hang down into the soil and gradually enclose and strangle the host, making it a "strangler".

### 32.4.5. Climbers

**Vines** have slender, weak stems that cannot stand upright and rely on other objects to climb, placing their leaves in the light at the top with less investment in supporting tissue. By the manner of climbing they fall into the following groups:

- Twining stems: the stem itself twines around the support, as in morning glory, wisteria, fleeceflower (*Fallopia multiflora*) and Japanese hop (*Humulus scandens*). The direction of twining is generally fixed within a species: seen from above, morning glory and common bean twine counterclockwise, whereas hop and honeysuckle twine clockwise.
- Tendrils: derived from the stem (grape, squash), from the leaf or part of it (the stipules of pea and greenbrier) ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)); a tendril that touches a support curls rapidly and then twists into a helix, pulling the plant toward the support and acting as a spring.
- Adhesive disks and climbing roots: the tendril tips of Virginia creeper swell into disks that secrete sticky material and attach to walls; the stems of ivy, trumpet creeper and star jasmine produce adventitious roots that attach to the support ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)).
- Hooks and prickles: cleavers, bedstraw and rattan palms (*Calamus*, the source of rattan) hook onto other plants with hooks or prickles.

Woody vines are especially luxuriant in tropical rainforests. Their stems are slender yet carry large volumes of water, their vessels are exceptionally wide, and there is often anomalous secondary growth (multiple cambia, phloem wedges in the xylem and so on), so that the stem does not break when bent and twisted ([7.12.22](/7.12.22)).

### 32.4.6. Ant Plants

Some plants provide shelter and food for ants, and the ants protect the plants from herbivores and climbers; such plants are called ant plants (myrmecophytes). The bullhorn acacia of Central America (*Vachellia cornigera*, formerly *Acacia cornigera*) has swollen, hollow stipular spines in which ants nest, extrafloral nectaries on the petioles that secrete nectar, and small bodies at the tips of the leaflets (Belt's bodies) rich in protein and lipid that the ants eat; the ants (*Pseudomyrmex*) attack insects that feed on the leaves and bite off other plants that come close, and when the ants are removed the acacia is badly damaged. In the trumpet tree (*Cecropia*) of South America the stem internodes are hollow, and the petiole bases bear glycogen-rich Müller's bodies, in association with Azteca ants. *Myrmecodia* (Rubiaceae) of Southeast Asia is an epiphyte whose hypocotyl swells into a tuber with labyrinthine channels in which ants live, and the plant absorbs nutrients from the ants' waste. The evolution of such mutualisms is treated in [7.10.17](/7.10.17#sec-17.3).

## 32.5. Coevolutionary Relationships of Plants with Other Organisms

Plants live fixed in place and cannot actively seek mates, move their offspring or escape enemies, so they have formed all kinds of mutualistic or antagonistic relationships with animals and microbes, and many forms and structures can be understood only in the context of these relationships.

### 32.5.1. Plants and Pollinators

About 90% of angiosperms depend on animals for pollination. The color, scent, shape and time of opening of the flower and its rewards (nectar, pollen) have adapted to the sensory abilities, body size and behavior of the pollinators, forming pollination syndromes ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)): bee flowers are mostly blue or yellow with nectar guides and landing platforms; moth flowers are white, open at night and have a long corolla tube; bird flowers are red, unscented and rich in nectar; fly flowers are dark purple-brown with a putrid smell (as in *Rafflesia*); wind-pollinated flowers have a reduced perianth and abundant, light pollen. The closest relationships are obligate mutualisms: yucca is pollinated only by yucca moths, whose larvae eat some of the seeds after the moth lays eggs in the ovary; and nearly every species of fig (*Ficus*) has its own specific fig wasp, which lays eggs and pollinates within the syconium ([7.10.17](/7.10.17#sec-17.3)).

### 32.5.2. Plants and Dispersers

The relationships between fleshy fruits and arils and birds and mammals, between nuts and caching rodents and corvids, between elaiosomes and ants, and between hooks and the fur of mammals are described in [7.12.31](/7.12.31). Compared with pollination, dispersal relationships are generally looser: the fruit of one plant is often eaten by many animal species, and one animal eats many kinds of fruit, which is diffuse coevolution.

### 32.5.3. Plants and Herbivores: Defensive Structures

Plants cannot escape being eaten and can only defend themselves by structures and chemicals. The main structural defenses are the following.

- Thorns: branch thorns derived from stems (honey locust, hawthorn), leaf thorns derived from leaves or stipules (barberry, the stipular spines of black locust), and prickles formed from epidermis and cortex (rose, Sichuan pepper); for their origins see [7.12.24](/7.12.24). Thorns mainly deter large mammals: acacias that are frequently browsed by giraffes in the African savanna have longer and denser thorns.
- Epidermal hairs: dense hairs make it difficult for small insects to walk, feed and lay eggs; glandular hairs secrete mucilage or toxic substances that trap or poison insects (tobacco, wild tomatoes); the hooked hairs of a passionflower (*Passiflora adenopoda*) can pierce the body of a caterpillar; the stinging hair of nettle ends in a brittle, silicified bead that breaks off after piercing the skin and injects irritants such as histamine and acetylcholine.
- Latex: the latex in the laticifers ([7.12.5](/7.12.5)) of the Euphorbiaceae, Moraceae, Apocynaceae, Papaveraceae, Campanulaceae and the ligulate-flowered Asteraceae often contains toxic or sticky substances, and an insect that bites a laticifer has its mouthparts glued together; the larvae of some beetles and moths have evolved the behavior of first cutting the veins to drain the latex before feeding.
- Crystals and silica: cells of the Araceae (taro, konjac, giant taro), Balsaminaceae, Vitaceae and others contain bundles of calcium oxalate raphides ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)) that pierce the oral mucosa on chewing and cause intense pain; in the Poaceae large amounts of silica are deposited in the epidermal cells (phytoliths), wearing the teeth of herbivorous animals and the mandibles of insects and reducing the digestibility of the leaf.
- Toughness: a thick cuticle, sclerenchyma and a high content of cellulose and lignin make old leaves difficult to chew and of lower nutritional value, so many insects eat only young leaves.

Chemical defenses (secondary metabolites such as alkaloids, terpenoids, phenolics, glucosinolates and cardiac glycosides) and the adaptations of herbivores to them are classic subjects of coevolution research ([7.10.17](/7.10.17#sec-17.2.3)). Many defenses are induced: only after a leaf is wounded does it synthesize defensive substances such as protease inhibitors and release volatiles that attract natural enemies of the herbivorous insects (parasitoid wasps), which is indirect defense; the attraction of ants by extrafloral nectaries to protect the plant (§32.4.6) is also indirect defense. The signal transduction of induced defense belongs to plant physiology.

### 32.5.4. Plants and Microbes

- Nitrogen-fixing symbioses: legumes form **root nodules** with rhizobia ([7.11.31](/7.11.31); for the anatomy of nodules see [7.12.20](/7.12.20)); the roots of alder, bog myrtle (*Myrica*), sea buckthorn and *Casuarina* form actinorhizal nodules with the actinomycete *Frankia*; nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria (*Nostoc*, *Anabaena*) live in the coralloid roots of cycads, in the leaf cavities of the fern *Azolla* and in the stem glands of *Gunnera*. The symbiosis of *Azolla* with *Anabaena* has long made it a green manure in the rice fields of southern China.
- Mycorrhizas: about 70%–80% of land plants have arbuscular mycorrhizas, temperate trees such as the Pinaceae, Fagaceae and Betulaceae mostly have ectomycorrhizas, the Ericaceae have ericoid mycorrhizas, and the Orchidaceae have orchid mycorrhizas ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)). The mycorrhizal fungi take up phosphorus, nitrogen and water from the soil and supply them to the plant, and the plant repays them with photosynthate; mycoheterotrophs (§32.4.2) are the "cheaters" in this exchange.
- Endophytes: fungi and bacteria that live within plant tissues without causing disease. Endophytic fungi of the genus *Epichloë* in grasses (such as tall fescue and ryegrass) produce alkaloids that protect the plant from herbivorous insects and livestock and also increase drought tolerance.
- Pathogenic microbes: coevolution also occurs between plants and pathogenic fungi, bacteria and viruses; plant immunity and disease resistance belong to plant physiology and plant pathology.

## Questions

1. In one region the life-form spectrum has more than 80% phanerophytes, and in another more than 40% therophytes. What climates are these likely to be, and why?

   **Answer** The former is probably a tropical rainforest climate: it is warm and humid all year, with no cold or dry unfavorable season, so the plants' renewal buds need no protection and can be borne high up, and tall woody plants predominate. The latter is probably a desert or Mediterranean climate: the dry period is very long and the rainfall unpredictable, surviving the unfavorable season as seeds is the most reliable way, and many plants complete their life cycle rapidly in the brief rainy season, so the proportion of therophytes is high. The life-form spectrum reflects the demands of the climate for protection of the renewal buds, and can serve as an indicator of climate.

2. Compare the structure of an oleander leaf with that of a curly pondweed leaf, and explain to what environmental condition each difference is an adaptation.

   **Answer** The oleander leaf is thick, with a thick cuticle and a multiple epidermis, which reduce epidermal transpiration; its stomata lie in hairy crypts, which slow stomatal transpiration; and its multilayered palisade, small intercellular spaces and abundant mechanical tissue suit strong light and prevent wilting on water loss. All of these are adaptations to drought. The submerged leaf of curly pondweed is thin and only a few cell layers thick, so weak light can penetrate; its epidermis has no cuticle and no stomata and contains chloroplasts, so it can absorb gases and salts directly from the water and photosynthesize; the mesophyll is undifferentiated and the aerenchyma well developed, storing the $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ and letting them be used by each other; and the vascular and mechanical tissues are reduced because conduction and support matter little in water. The two represent the two extremes of leaf adaptation, to water shortage, and to shortage of gas and light.

3. Using the parameters of the light-response curve, explain why shade plants can survive on the forest floor while the seedlings of many sun plants die there.

   **Answer** The light intensity on the forest floor is often below $50\,\mathrm{\mu mol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$. A shade leaf has a low respiration rate $R_{\mathrm{d}}$ and a low light compensation point, so its net photosynthetic rate is still positive in such weak light, it can keep its carbon balance and grow slowly, and its small maximum photosynthetic rate $A_{\max}$ is of no consequence since it cannot be used there anyway. A sun leaf has high $A_{\max}$ and high $R_{\mathrm{d}}$ and so a high light compensation point; in the weak light of the forest floor the carbon fixed by photosynthesis does not offset the respiratory loss, the net photosynthetic rate is near zero or negative, and the seedling gradually exhausts its reserves and dies. The apparent quantum yields $\phi$ of the two are similar, and the difference arises mainly from $R_{\mathrm{d}}$ and $A_{\max}$, that is, from how much is invested in the photosynthetic machinery and in maintaining it.

4. Dodder and *Gastrodia* both lack green leaves. How does their way of obtaining nutrition differ fundamentally? How does a Loranthaceae mistletoe differ from dodder?

   **Answer** Dodder is a parasitic plant: its haustoria invade the vascular tissue of the host plant directly, and it obtains water, mineral salts and organic nutrients from the host xylem and phloem. *Gastrodia* is a mycoheterotroph: it does not contact another plant itself but obtains carbon from fungi (*Armillaria*, *Mycena*) through a mycorrhizal relationship, and the fungal carbon in turn comes from trees or rotting wood. A mistletoe of the Loranthaceae has green leaves and can photosynthesize; it is a hemiparasite, and its haustoria connect only to the host xylem, from which it takes water and mineral salts. Dodder can hardly photosynthesize and is a holoparasite, and its haustoria connect to both the xylem and the phloem of the host.

5. Mangroves and cacti live in utterly different environments; why may both have succulent leaves or stems? What other special adaptations do mangroves have?

   **Answer** Both face "physiological drought": the desert of the cactus lacks water, and the sea water of the mangrove has a high salt content and osmotic pressure, so in both cases the roots find it hard to take up water. Succulence stores a large amount of water in the body, which the cactus uses to survive the dry period and the mangrove uses to dilute the salt it absorbs, so the two converge in form. Other adaptations of mangroves are the following: prop roots support the plant in the mud and resist the scouring of tides; peg-like or knee-like pneumatophores rise above the mud surface and supply oxygen through lenticels and aerenchyma to roots in the oxygen-poor mud; salt glands excrete salt or the roots exclude it; and in vivipary the seed germinates on the mother tree into a long club-shaped propagule before falling, avoiding the difficulty of germinating in salt water and mud.
