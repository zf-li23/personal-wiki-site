After Gunnerales and Dilleniales diverge, the core eudicots split into two large branches: the superrosids ([7.12.41](/7.12.41)–[7.12.44](/7.12.44)) and the superasterids. The superasterids include three smaller orders, Berberidopsidales, Santalales and Caryophyllales, and the species-rich **asterids** ([7.12.46](/7.12.46)–[7.12.49](/7.12.49)). This chapter treats the first two of these distinctive orders. Santalales are almost entirely parasites, and include the mistletoes and the famous sandalwood. Caryophyllales concentrate the angiosperm lineages adapted to extreme environments: goosefoot, saxaul, seablite and the cacti of deserts and saline soils, the ice plants of the seashore, the sundews and pitcher plants of nutrient-poor bogs, and familiar plants such as pinks, chickweed, amaranth, spinach, beet, buckwheat and rhubarb. In Cronquist's system Caryophyllales, Polygonales and Plumbaginales formed the subclass Caryophyllidae, one of the few subclasses largely retained in the molecular system ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.5.2)); Santalales lay in the Rosidae, near Celastrales and Rhamnales. This chapter first treats the circumscription of the superasterids (§45.1), then Santalales (§45.2) and Caryophyllales (§45.3), and finally several problems in the evolution of characters in Caryophyllales (§45.4).

## 45.1. Circumscription of the Superasterids

**Definition 45.1.1 (Superasterids)** The monophyletic group formed in the APG system by Berberidopsidales, Santalales, Caryophyllales and the asterids is called the **superasterids**; in phylogenetic nomenclature it is the Superasteridae.

The superasterids were recognized only through multigene and plastid genome analyses (Moore et al. 2010; Soltis et al. 2011), and no morphological synapomorphy is known. The relationships among the orders are as follows: Santalales diverge first, and Berberidopsidales and Caryophyllales form a clade that is the sister group of the asterids (Soltis et al. 2018); some analyses place Berberidopsidales outside (Caryophyllales + asterids). Dilleniales sometimes appear at the base of the superasterids, and their position is not yet stable ([7.12.40](/7.12.40)).

Berberidopsidales has only two families and 4 species, Berberidopsidaceae (*Berberidopsis*, climbers of Chile and eastern Australia with spirally arranged tepals) and Aextoxicaceae (*Aextoxicon* of Chile); in morphological classification the two were never placed together, which was an unexpected result of molecular systematics.

## 45.2. Santalales

### 45.2.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Santalales are almost all **parasitic plants** ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.1)): some are root hemiparasites (sandalwood, bastard toadflax), some stem parasites growing on branches (the mistletoes), and some holoparasites that have lost chlorophyll altogether (*Balanophora*); only a few of the earliest-diverging lineages (part of Olacaceae) are not parasitic. Possible morphological synapomorphies are: no root hairs; the calyx reduced to a cup-shaped rim; valvate petals; stamens opposite the petals; ovules pendent and often undifferentiated (without integuments, or even not forming a definite ovule); fruit a drupe or berry; seeds with a small embryo and abundant endosperm.

The parasitic organ of Santalales is the **haustorium**, a specialized structure formed where the root or stem touches the host, which penetrates the host tissue and connects to the host xylem (in hemiparasites) or to both xylem and phloem (in holoparasites). Hemiparasites have green leaves, photosynthesize on their own, and obtain mainly water and mineral salts from the host; holoparasites have no chlorophyll and obtain all their nutrition from the host. Phylogenetic analysis shows that the stem-parasitic habit arose independently at least 5 times within Santalales (Vidal-Russell and Nickrent 2008), from a root-parasitic ancestor: root parasitism came first, followed by a shift to the host's branches, accompanied by dispersal of the seeds by birds.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Santalales included 10 families, among them Olacaceae, Santalaceae, Loranthaceae, Viscaceae and Balanophoraceae. APG II and APG III merged Viscaceae into Santalaceae to avoid a paraphyletic Santalaceae; APG IV Santalales broadly comprises Olacaceae (broad sense, paraphyletic), Opiliaceae, Santalaceae (broad sense, including Viscaceae), Misodendraceae, Schoepfiaceae, Loranthaceae and Balanophoraceae. Nickrent et al. (2010) subdivided Santalales into about 18 families (splitting Olacaceae into 8 and Santalaceae into 7), which APG IV did not adopt because support for the backbone branches is low.

### 45.2.2. Santalaceae

APG IV Santalaceae (Santalaceae s.l.) has about 44 genera and 1,000 species and merges Viscaceae and several other small families. They are mostly hemiparasitic herbs, shrubs or trees, parasitic on roots or stems; leaves simple, often opposite and entire, sometimes reduced to scales; flowers small, bisexual or unisexual, with a single perianth whorl of 3–6 lobes; stamens as many as the perianth lobes and opposite them; ovary inferior or half-inferior, 1-locular, with 1–3 ovules borne on a free-central placenta and without integuments; fruit a drupe or berry.

1. Sandalwood (*Santalum album*): a small evergreen tree and root hemiparasite whose seedlings need a host to grow normally (in cultivation it is planted mixed with host plants); the heartwood is yellow-brown with a rich and lasting fragrance, containing sandalwood oil (mainly santalol), and is a valuable perfume, carving and medicinal wood. It is native from southern India to Indonesia and has been introduced into South China.
2. *Thesium chinense*: a delicate perennial herb whose roots parasitize the roots of grasses and others, with linear leaves and small white flowers; the whole plant is used medicinally and it occurs throughout China.
3. Asian mistletoe (*Viscum coloratum*): an evergreen stem-parasitic hemiparasitic small shrub, dichotomously or trichotomously branched, with green branches and conspicuous nodes; leaves opposite, thickly leathery and sessile, with 3–5 main veins; flowers unisexual and dioecious, small, with a 4-lobed perianth, the anthers of the male flower attached directly to the perianth lobes (no filaments); the berry is spherical and pale yellow or orange-red, with a layer of sticky material in the mesocarp (viscin). It parasitizes elm, poplar, willow, birch, pear, oak and other trees, occurs from Northeast and North China to central China, and the leafy twigs are used medicinally. European mistletoe (*V. album*) is the traditional Christmas decoration of Europe. The fruits of *Viscum* are eaten by birds (such as waxwings); the sticky seeds are voided with the droppings or wiped off on branches by the bird, adhere to the branch and germinate, and the radicle forms a haustorium on contact with the bark and invades the host, a typical case in Santalales of the coevolution of stem parasitism and bird dispersal.

Floral formula: mistletoe ♂ $\ast P_4 A_4$; ♀ $\ast P_4 \overline{G}_{(3\text{–}4:1)}$ (ovules undifferentiated).

### 45.2.3. Loranthaceae

Loranthaceae has about 75 genera and 1,000 species, chiefly in the tropics and the Southern Hemisphere; China has about 8 genera and over 50 species, most in the south. Almost all are stem-parasitic hemiparasitic shrubs (only a few, such as the Australian Christmas tree *Nuytsia floribunda*, are root-parasitic trees); leaves opposite or alternate, leathery and entire; flowers bisexual, often large and showy, with a tubular red or yellow corolla adapted to pollination by birds (sunbirds, flowerpeckers); the receptacle is cup-shaped and fused with the ovary, with a ring at the top that is entire or toothed (the calyculus, in fact a reduced calyx); tepals 4–6, petaloid, valvate, free or fused into a tube; stamens as many as the tepals and opposite them, borne on the tepals; ovary inferior, 1-locular, forming no definite ovules but only sporogenous tissue; the fruit is a berry-like pseudocarp covered with a sticky layer, with a single seed and no seed coat.

Floral formula: *Taxillus* $\uparrow P_{(4)} A_4 \overline{G}_{(3\text{–}4:1)}$.

Representative plants: *Taxillus chinensis* is an evergreen parasitic small shrub on mulberry, longan, lychee, camellia, banyan and other plants, with a corolla tube about 2.5 cm long, occurring in South China; the leafy twigs are used medicinally. *Macrosolen cochinchinensis* has a tubular orange-yellow corolla and occurs in South and Southwest China; *Korthalsella japonica* (now in Santalaceae) parasitizes members of Fagaceae. Loranthaceae and mistletoes of Santalaceae are similar in appearance and were formerly united in one family; they differ in that Loranthaceae has bisexual, large, brightly colored, bird-pollinated flowers with a calyculus, whereas mistletoe flowers are unisexual, tiny and green, without a calyculus, and the two also differ in chromosomes and embryology. Molecular evidence shows that they belong to two different lineages of Santalales, and the stem-parasitic habit evolved in parallel.

### 45.2.4. Olacaceae and Balanophoraceae

Olacaceae is the assemblage of the earliest-diverging lineages of Santalales (a paraphyletic group), mostly nonparasitic or root-hemiparasitic trees and shrubs of the tropics. *Malania oleifera* is a tree endemic to Guangxi and Yunnan whose seed oil is rich in nervonic acid, and a second-class nationally protected plant; tallowwood (*Ximenia americana*) is a pantropical coastal shrub and a root parasite.

Balanophoraceae are fleshy holoparasitic herbs growing on the roots of hosts; the plant body has no chlorophyll, and the underground part is a tuber-like rhizome that forms a complex composite tissue with the host root; only the inflorescence emerges above ground, resembling a mushroom or a snake's head, with extremely small and extremely numerous flowers. *Balanophora* is common under forests in southern China. The plastid genome of Balanophoraceae is extremely reduced, with a very fast rate of substitution, and its systematic position was long uncertain; it is now placed in Santalales.

## 45.3. Caryophyllales

### 45.3.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Caryophyllales in APG IV have 38 families and about 12,500 species, an enlargement of the Caryophyllidae. Traditionally its core was the "Centrospermae", a group of families (Caryophyllaceae, Chenopodiaceae, Amaranthaceae, Phytolaccaceae, Nyctaginaceae, Aizoaceae, Portulacaceae, Cactaceae and others) in which the seeds lie on a central placenta in the fruit and the embryo is curved around the **perisperm**. The characters of Cronquist's Caryophyllales were: mostly herbs, often succulent or halophytic; perianth often a single whorl; stamens definite in number, opposite the tepals when in one whorl; ovary superior with campylotropous ovules and axile to free-central or basal placentation; embryo curved, enclosing the starchy perisperm; most contain betalains; sieve-element plastids of type PIII ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.9)).

Molecular systematics divides Caryophyllales into two large clades:

1. Noncore Caryophyllales (suborder Polygonineae): Polygonaceae, Plumbaginaceae, Tamaricaceae and Frankeniaceae, plus the carnivorous clade of Droseraceae, Nepenthaceae, Drosophyllaceae, Dioncophyllaceae and Ancistrocladaceae. These families lack betalains and mostly contain naphthoquinones, and the seeds often have endosperm rather than perisperm. Cronquist's Polygonales and Plumbaginales both belong here; Droseraceae and Nepenthaceae were formerly in Nepenthales of the Dilleniidae, and Tamaricaceae in Violales.
2. Core Caryophyllales (suborder Caryophyllineae): Caryophyllaceae, Amaranthaceae, Phytolaccaceae, Nyctaginaceae, Aizoaceae, Cactaceae, Portulacaceae, Basellaceae, Molluginaceae and others, roughly equivalent to the Centrospermae. The synapomorphies are: a continuous cambium; sieve-element plastids with a peripheral ring of protein filaments (type PIII); campylotropous ovules; an embryo that is curved and encloses the starchy perisperm; and betalains in the great majority (Caryophyllaceae and Molluginaceae are exceptions).

Caryophyllales fall into two clades, the noncore Caryophyllales (Polygonineae) and the core Caryophyllales; colored blocks mark betalains (probably originating only once in the core Caryophyllales and reverting to anthocyanins in Caryophyllaceae and Molluginaceae), C4 photosynthesis (arising independently many times in Amaranthaceae, Portulacaceae, Molluginaceae, Aizoaceae and others), CAM (Cactaceae, Aizoaceae, Portulacaceae, Didiereaceae and others) and carnivory (the carnivorous clade containing Droseraceae and Nepenthaceae arose only once)](./plots/caryophyllales-tree.py)

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Caryophyllidae comprised 3 orders and 14 families: Caryophyllales, Polygonales and Plumbaginales. In APG IV the three orders are merged into a single Caryophyllales, to which Nepenthaceae, Droseraceae, Tamaricaceae, Frankeniaceae, Ancistrocladaceae and others are added; Chenopodiaceae is merged into Amaranthaceae; Portulacaceae is split into Portulacaceae in the narrow sense, Talinaceae, Montiaceae, Anacampserotaceae and others; and Phytolaccaceae and Molluginaceae are also divided.

### 45.3.2. Caryophyllaceae

Diagnostic characters of Caryophyllaceae:

- Annual or perennial herbs, rarely subshrubs; nodes often swollen.
- Leaves simple and opposite, entire, often connate at the base; stipules present (membranous) or absent.
- Flowers bisexual, radially symmetric, 5-merous, solitary or in dichasia (the terminal flower opens first, and a branch arises on each side below it, repeating at each level).
- Sepals 4–5, free (Alsinoideae) or fused into a tube (Caryophylloideae), often with membranous margins, persistent.
- Petals 4–5, often distinctly clawed, the blade entire, 2-lobed or fringed; sometimes absent.
- Stamens usually 10 in two whorls (or 5 in one whorl), the outer whorl opposite the petals.
- Ovary superior, 2–5 carpels fused, 1-locular with free-central placentation (occasionally with an incomplete septum below, giving axile placentation), ovules many to 1; styles 2–5, free.
- Fruit usually a capsule, dehiscing by teeth or valves at the apex, the number of teeth or valves once or twice the number of styles; rarely a berry.
- Seed with a curved embryo enclosing the perisperm; contain anthocyanins and no betalains.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_5 A_{5+5} \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}5:1:\infty)}$; *Dianthus* $\ast K_{(5)} C_5 A_{5+5} \underline{G}_{(2:1:\infty)}$, *Stellaria* $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{5+5} \underline{G}_{(3:1:\infty)}$.

The family has about 80–100 genera and 2,600 species, worldwide, with most in the northern temperate zone and the Mediterranean region; China has about 30 genera and 390 species. Traditionally it is divided into three subfamilies: Paronychioideae (stipules membranous, flowers small, fruit mostly a utricle), Alsinoideae (no stipules, sepals free, petals without claws) and Caryophylloideae (no stipules, sepals fused into a tube, petals with long claws); molecular phylogenies show that the first two subfamilies are not monophyletic and the family is now divided into about 11 tribes, but the two large classes "sepals free" and "sepals fused" remain convenient for identification.

Classification and representative plants:

1. *Dianthus*: calyx fused into a tube with 5 teeth and 2–4 pairs of bracts below it; petals 5, with the blade at a right angle to the claw and the margin toothed or fringed; stamens 10; styles 2; capsule opening with 4 teeth at the apex. China pink (*D. chinensis*) is a perennial herb with linear-lanceolate leaves and petals with shallowly toothed margins, red, pink or white, native to China and cultivated as an ornamental; fringed pink (*D. superbus*) has petal tips cut deeply into thread-like segments and the whole plant is used medicinally; carnation (*D. caryophyllus*) is native to southern Europe, has fragrant flowers and is one of the most important cut flowers in the world; sweet William (*D. barbatus*) has dense cymes.
2. *Stellaria*: sepals free; petals 5, deeply 2-lobed so that they appear to be 10; styles 3. Common chickweed (*S. media*) is one of the commonest field weeds in the world, with a line of hairs along one side of the stem.
3. *Silene* (including the merged *Lychnis* and *Melandrium*): the calyx tube is inflated and often glandular-hairy, secreting a sticky substance that catches crawling insects (hence the name "catchfly"); styles 3–5; the base of the ovary often has a septum (incomplete axile placentation). White campion (*S. latifolia*) is dioecious with XY sex chromosomes and is a model for the study of the origin of plant sex chromosomes; *S. fulgens* has red flowers.
4. *Gypsophila*: loose paniculate cymes with many small flowers. Baby's breath (*G. paniculata*) is a filler for cut flowers; *G. oldhamiana* occurs in North China.
5. *Pseudostellaria heterophylla*: the tuberous root is long and spindle-shaped; there are two kinds of flowers (ordinary flowers at the top of the stem and cleistogamous flowers lower down), and the tuberous root is used medicinally.
6. Cowherb (*Vaccaria hispanica*, often given as *V. segetalis* in Chinese textbooks): the calyx tube has 5 winged ridges and the flowers are pink; the seeds are used medicinally.
7. Common mouse-ear (*Cerastium fontanum* subsp. *vulgare*, often given as *C. caespitosum* in Chinese textbooks): a roadside weed with 2-lobed petal tips and a cylindrical capsule opening with 10 teeth at the apex.

Key characters of the family: herbs with swollen nodes and simple opposite leaves. Inflorescence a dichasium; petals often clawed; stamens 10; ovary superior, 1-locular, with free-central placentation. Capsule dehiscing by teeth or valves. Embryo curved around the perisperm.

### 45.3.3. Amaranthaceae

APG IV Amaranthaceae (Amaranthaceae s.l.) merges Cronquist's Chenopodiaceae. Traditionally the two were distinguished as follows: in Amaranthaceae the perianth and bracts are scarious and often colored and the stamen filaments are fused at the base, whereas in Chenopodiaceae the perianth is herbaceous or fleshy and green and the stamens are free. But all these differences have exceptions, and molecular phylogenies show that Amaranthaceae in the narrow sense is nested within Chenopodiaceae (or that the boundary between the two does not correspond to monophyletic groups), so they are merged into one family. The merged Amaranthaceae has about 170 genera and 2,300 species, worldwide; China has about 57 genera and over 230 species (including the former Chenopodiaceae).

Diagnostic characters:

- Annual or perennial herbs, rarely shrubs or small trees; many species of the former Chenopodiaceae are halophytes or xerophytes with succulent leaves or stems.
- The former Chenopodiaceae often have bladder hairs (sac-like epidermal hairs), which burst and shrivel on drying and make the plant surface look dusted with meal or scurf (the "meal" on the underside of leaves of *Chenopodium album*).
- Leaves simple, alternate or opposite, without stipules.
- Flowers small, often densely crowded in spikes, panicles or heads; bisexual or unisexual; each flower usually has 1 bract and 2 bracteoles, those of Amaranthoideae scarious.
- Tepals 3–5 in a single whorl, scarious (Amaranthoideae) or herbaceous to fleshy (former Chenopodiaceae), often enlarged and persistent in fruit and sometimes becoming wings or spines.
- Stamens as many as the tepals and opposite them, the filaments free or fused at the base into a cup.
- Ovary superior, 2–3 carpels fused, 1-locular, with 1 campylotropous ovule borne at the base of the ovary (basal placentation).
- The fruit is a **utricle** ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.2.4)), often enclosed in the persistent perianth or bracts, indehiscent or opening by a circumscissile lid (*Amaranthus*).
- Seeds flattened, the embryo curved into a ring or horseshoe enclosing the perisperm, or spiral and with almost no perisperm (Salsoloideae and others).

Floral formula: $\ast P_{3\text{–}5} A_{3\text{–}5} \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}3:1:1)}$.

Classification and representative plants (subfamilies after Kadereit et al. 2003 and APweb):

1. Amaranthoideae and Gomphrenoideae: perianth and bracts scarious. Chinese spinach (*Amaranthus tricolor*) often has red, purple or yellow blotches on the leaves, and the young stems and leaves are eaten as a vegetable; redroot pigweed (*A. retroflexus*), *A. blitum* and others are common weeds, and many *Amaranthus* weeds have become resistant to herbicides. Grain amaranths (*A. hypochondriacus*, *A. caudatus*) were ancient food crops of the Americas, and the seeds are rich in lysine. Silver cockscomb (*Celosia argentea*) has silvery white spikes, and the seeds are used medicinally; cockscomb (*C. cristata*) has a flattened, crest-like inflorescence (a fasciated axis) and is cultivated as an ornamental; *Achyranthes bidentata* has medicinal roots, and the bracteoles of the fruit become spiny and adhere to animals for dispersal; globe amaranth (*Gomphrena globosa*) has spherical heads with purple-red scarious bracts that persist for a long time.
2. Chenopodioideae: lamb's quarters (*Chenopodium album*) has rhombic-ovate leaves with meal on the underside and is one of the commonest weeds, and the young shoots are edible; quinoa (*C. quinoa*) is native to the Andes, and the seeds are a highly nutritious grain; epazote (*Dysphania ambrosioides*) has a strong smell and contains the vermifuge oil of chenopodium; spinach (*Spinacia oleracea*) is dioecious and native to Iran; burning bush (*Bassia scoparia*, often given as *Kochia scoparia* in Chinese textbooks) has fruit used medicinally, and the old stems can be made into brooms.
3. Betoideae: beet (*Beta vulgaris*) is a biennial herb. In the mid-eighteenth century Marggraf found that beet root contains the same sucrose as sugarcane, and subsequent selection by Achard and others raised the sugar content from about 6% to 16%–20% in modern cultivars, making it the main sugar crop of the temperate zone; about one-fifth of the world's sugar comes from sugar beet. The same species also includes the leaf form, chard (var. *cicla*), and the root form, beetroot. The red of beetroot is a betalain (§45.4.1).
4. Salicornioideae, Suaedoideae and Salsoloideae: typical halophytes and xerophytes ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.4)). Glasswort (*Salicornia europaea*) has succulent, jointed stems and leaves reduced to scales, and grows in coastal and inland salt marshes; it is one of the most salt-tolerant land plants. *Suaeda salsa* has succulent, linear leaves that turn purple-red in autumn, and forms the landscape of the "Red Beach" at Panjin, Liaoning; saxaul (*Haloxylon ammodendron*) is a small tree of the desert whose leaves are reduced to scales and which photosynthesizes with green assimilating branches; it is the main tree for sand-fixing afforestation in the northwestern deserts of China, and its roots host the valuable medicinal plant *Cistanche* (Orobanchaceae, [7.12.48](/7.12.48)); *Kali collinum* (formerly *Salsola collina*) has cylindrical, spine-tipped leaves and the tepals develop wings on the back in fruit.
   Many of these halophytic and xerophytic groups are plants with the **C4 pathway**, with **Kranz anatomy** in the leaf or a special single-cell C4 structure ([7.12.23](/7.12.23#sec-23.5.6)); Amaranthaceae s.l. is the family with the most C4 species among the eudicots, and C4 photosynthesis arose in it independently more than ten times (§45.4.2).

Key characters of the family (former Chenopodiaceae): herbs, often with bladder hairs; leaves simple, succulent; flowers small with a single perianth whorl, the stamens opposite the tepals; ovary of 2–3 carpels fused, 1-locular, with 1 basal ovule. Fruit a utricle, embryo curved. Amaranthaceae in the former narrow sense is further characterized by the scarious perianth and bracts.

### 45.3.4. Polygonaceae

Diagnostic characters of Polygonaceae:

- Herbs, rarely shrubs or climbers; nodes often swollen.
- Leaves simple, alternate, entire; the stipules are often fused into a membranous sheath around the node, the ocrea (defined below).
- Flowers small, bisexual, rarely unisexual, radially symmetric, in spikes, racemes or panicles, with several flowers often clustered in the axil of a bract (the bract often also sheath-like).
- Tepals 3–6, petaloid, persistent, often in two whorls; in *Rumex* the inner tepals enlarge into wings in fruit, often with tubercles on the back.
- Stamens 6–9, often 8.
- Ovary superior, 2–3 carpels fused, 1-locular, with 1 orthotropous basal ovule; styles 2–3.
- Fruit a trigonous or lenticular **achene**, often partly or wholly enclosed in the persistent perianth; seeds with abundant endosperm (not perisperm) and the embryo curved or straight.

**Definition 45.3.1 (Ocrea)** The membranous or herbaceous sheath formed by the fusion of the two stipules and surrounding the stem node above the base of the petiole is called the **ocrea**; it is the most important diagnostic character of Polygonaceae.

Floral formula: $\ast P_{3\text{–}6} A_{6\text{–}9} \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}3:1:1)}$.

The family has about 50 genera and 1,100 species, mainly in the northern temperate zone; China has about 13 genera and 240 species. It is divided into Polygonoideae and Eriogonoideae, which occurs mainly in North America and has no ocrea. Molecular evidence shows that *Polygonum* s.l. in the traditional broad sense is not monophyletic, and modern classifications often split it into *Polygonum* s.s., *Persicaria*, *Bistorta*, *Reynoutria*, *Knorringia* and others; Flora of China (2003) still adopts a broader treatment, and the names used in Chinese textbooks are given in parentheses below.

Classification and representative plants:

1. *Polygonum* (broad sense): perianth often 5-lobed, colored, enclosing the achene in fruit. Fo-ti (*Reynoutria multiflora*, *Fallopia multiflora* in Flora of China, often given as *Polygonum multiflorum* in Chinese textbooks) is a twining herb with a large tuberous root, and the tuberous root and the vine are used medicinally; the raw root is hepatotoxic. Japanese knotweed (*Reynoutria japonica*) has hollow stems with red-purple spots and is dioecious; the root is used medicinally and contains resveratrol, and after introduction to Europe and North America it became a serious invasive species. Knotgrass (*Polygonum aviculare*) is a prostrate herb widespread in the Northern Hemisphere. Kiss-me-over-the-garden-gate (*Persicaria orientalis*) is tall with pendent red inflorescences and is ornamental; water pepper (*P. hydropiper*) has pungent leaves; Chinese indigo (*P. tinctoria*) leaves are the traditional source of indigo; mile-a-minute (*P. perfoliata*) has recurved prickles on the stem and a leaf-like ocrea through which the stem passes; bistort (*Bistorta officinalis*) has a medicinal rhizome. *Knorringia sibirica* (often given as *Polygonum sibiricum* in Chinese textbooks) grows on saline wasteland.
2. *Rheum*: tall perennial herbs with stout yellow rhizomes; basal leaves large, palmately shallowly or deeply lobed; tepals 6, not enlarging in fruit; stamens 9; achene with 3 broad wings. The rhizomes of *R. palmatum*, *R. tanguticum* and *R. officinale* are the famous purgative rhubarb (containing anthraquinones); the petioles of garden rhubarb (*R. rhabarbarum*) are eaten as a vegetable in Europe. Noble rhubarb (*R. nobile*) of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau has large, translucent bracts that act as a "greenhouse" protecting the inflorescence, a famous example of the adaptation of alpine plants to strong ultraviolet radiation and low temperature.
3. *Rumex*: tepals 6, the inner 3 enlarging into wings in fruit, often with tubercles on the back. Common sorrel (*R. acetosa*) has arrow-shaped leaf bases and is dioecious, and the young leaves are edible (containing oxalic acid, which gives the sour taste); *R. japonicus* has cordate leaf bases and a medicinal root.
4. *Fagopyrum*: common buckwheat (*F. esculentum*) is an annual herb with an often red stem, triangular leaves and white or pinkish flowers that are distylous ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3)), with a trigonous achene whose seed is ground into flour; Tartary buckwheat (*F. tataricum*) is self-pollinating with small green flowers and a grooved achene, rich in rutin. Buckwheat is a "pseudocereal": its "grain" is not the caryopsis of Poaceae but the starch-rich endosperm of an achene.
5. Others: coral vine (*Antigonon leptopus*) is a tropical ornamental climber; sea grape (*Coccoloba uvifera*) is a coastal tree of tropical America.

Key characters of the family: herbs with swollen nodes, simple alternate leaves and an ocrea. Tepals 3–6, petaloid and persistent; ovary superior, 1-locular, with 1 orthotropous ovule. Trigonous or lenticular achene enclosed in the persistent perianth.

### 45.3.5. Cactaceae

Cactaceae has about 130 genera and 1,500–1,800 species, almost all native to the Americas (only *Rhipsalis baccifera* is naturally distributed to Africa and Sri Lanka, probably dispersed by birds), from southern Canada to Patagonia and concentrated in the arid regions of Mexico and South America; China has no native species, and many species are cultivated and naturalized.

Diagnostic characters:

- Perennial succulent plants; the stem is succulent and juicy, green, columnar, globular, flattened and jointed or three-angled, often ribbed or tuberculate, with well-developed water-storage parenchyma, and carries out photosynthesis ([7.12.24](/7.12.24#sec-24.3.1)).
- Leaves generally reduced (only a few primitive genera such as *Pereskia* have normal flat leaves); spines are borne on areoles (see the definition below).
- Flowers often solitary on areoles, bisexual, radially symmetric, large and showy; tepals numerous, spirally arranged, grading from sepal-like on the outside to petal-like on the inside.
- Stamens numerous, spirally arranged; ovary inferior (surrounded by a receptacular tube formed from stem tissue, which often bears areoles and scales), 1-locular, with 3 to many parietal placentas and many ovules.
- Fruit a berry, often with spines or scales; seeds many.
- They carry out **crassulacean acid metabolism** (CAM): stomata open at night to take up CO₂, which is fixed as malic acid and stored in the vacuole, and by day the stomata are closed and CO₂ is released for the Calvin cycle, so that water loss in drought is greatly reduced ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.2)).
- They contain betalains, so despite their peculiar form they have been recognized since the 1960s as belonging to Caryophyllales (Centrospermae).

**Definition 45.3.2 (Areole)** A very much shortened short shoot on the stem of a cactus, appearing as a cushion- or pit-like structure bearing hairs and spines, is called an **areole**; it is the position of an axillary bud, the spines are leaves (or bud scales) of the short shoot that have been modified, and flowers and new branches also grow from the areole.

A: Pereskia: normal flat green leaves, with spines borne on the short shoot (areole) in the leaf axil, showing that an areole is a short shoot in the axil of a leaf. B: a flattened stem segment of Opuntia: areoles arranged in a spiral, bearing long spines and clusters of fine barbed spines (glochids); flowers and new stem segments arise from the areoles. C: enlargement of one areole: the areole is a greatly shortened short shoot, the surrounding spines derive from leaves of the short shoot, and the growing point lies in the center. Cactaceae is divided into 4 subfamilies: Pereskioideae (with flat leaves, regarded as closest to the ancestral state, but molecular analyses show that it is paraphyletic), Opuntioideae (with glochids, that is, clusters of fine barbed spines on the areoles, and seeds with a bony aril), Maihuenioideae (only 1 genus) and Cactoideae (the most species, stems columnar or globular, leaves completely reduced). Molecular-clock estimates place the origin of Cactaceae about 35 million years ago, with the main diversification 5–10 million years ago, consistent with the expansion of arid habitats in the Americas (Arakaki et al. 2011). The closest relative is Portulacaceae in the narrow sense (§45.3.7).

Representative plants: prickly pear (*Opuntia dillenii*) and Indian fig (*O. ficus-indica*) have flattened stem segments and edible berries and have become naturalized along the coast of South China and in the dry-hot valleys of the southwest; dragon fruit (*Selenicereus undatus*) has a three-angled, climbing stem and large night-blooming flowers, with a red berry, and is a tropical fruit; queen of the night (*Epiphyllum oxypetalum*) has flat leaf-like stems and large white flowers that open at night and wither within a few hours, pollinated by hawkmoths; Christmas cactus (*Schlumbergera truncata*) and orchid cacti (*Disocactus*) are epiphytic forest cacti often cultivated as ornamentals; saguaro (*Carnegiea gigantea*) reaches over ten meters and is the emblem of the Sonoran Desert of the southwestern United States; peyote (*Lophophora williamsii*) contains the hallucinogen mescaline.

The succulent stem, reduced leaves and CAM photosynthesis of Cactaceae appeared independently in *Euphorbia* (Euphorbiaceae) and in Asclepiadoideae of Apocynaceae (carrion flowers) in Africa; the three look extremely similar, a classic example of **convergent evolution** ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.1.2)); the difference is that *Euphorbia* has white latex and no areoles, and its spines are often paired (stipular spines).

### 45.3.6. Aizoaceae, Phytolaccaceae and Nyctaginaceae

Aizoaceae has about 130 genera and 1,900 species, chiefly in southern Africa (the succulent plateau of western South Africa is its center of diversity), mostly succulent herbs or subshrubs; leaves opposite and succulent, the epidermis often with large bladder cells that look like grains of ice (ice plant, *Mesembryanthemum crystallinum*); tepals 5, sepal-like; the numerous "petals" are in fact petaloid staminodes derived from the outer stamens; ovary inferior or half-inferior; the capsule opens in a star shape at the apex, hygroscopically, and relies on raindrops to splash out the seeds. New Zealand spinach (*Tetragonia tetragonioides*) is native to Australia and New Zealand and is eaten as a vegetable; the plant body of *Lithops* (living stones) consists of only a pair of extremely thick leaves half buried in gravel and looks like a pebble, an example of mimicry; *Lampranthus* and baby sun rose (*Mesembryanthemum cordifolium*) are often cultivated as ornamentals. Ice plant can switch from C3 to CAM under salt stress and is a model plant for the study of CAM induction.

Phytolaccaceae in the narrow sense has about 5 genera and over 30 species, mostly herbs or shrubs; leaves simple, alternate, entire; flowers bisexual, tepals 4–5, stamens numerous to as many as the tepals, carpels many, free or fused, in one whorl; berry oblate. Indian pokeweed (*Phytolacca acinosa*) is a tall perennial herb with a large, fleshy, poisonous root (containing phytolaccosides) used medicinally; American pokeweed (*P. americana*) has pendent racemes and purple-black berries, is native to North America and widely naturalized in China, and people often eat its root by mistake for ginseng and are poisoned. Because the carpels of Phytolaccaceae are free and arranged in a whorl, Chinese textbooks regard the family as reflecting a link between Caryophyllales and the polycarpic groups.

Nyctaginaceae has about 30 genera and 300 species, mainly in the American tropics. Leaves simple, often opposite, entire; flowers bisexual, often 3 to many in cymes surrounded by brightly colored involucres or bracts; the perianth is fused into a petaloid tube, persistent at the base around the ovary and hardening in fruit, and together with the pericarp forms a ribbed anthocarp; ovary superior, with 1 carpel and 1 ovule; fruit an achene. Floral formula: $\ast P_{(5)} A_5 \underline{G}_{1:1:1}$. Four-o'clock (*Mirabilis jalapa*) is a herb with opposite leaves, each flower having a 5-lobed, calyx-like involucre below it, and a funnel-shaped perianth that opens in the evening and is pollinated by hawkmoths; flowers of different colors often occur on one plant, and Correns (1909) used its variegated leaves to demonstrate cytoplasmic (plastid) inheritance ([7.4](/7.4)). Bougainvillea (*Bougainvillea spectabilis* and *B. glabra*) are woody climbers with branch thorns, each cluster of 3 small flowers being surrounded by 3 large, showy purple-red bracts; the ornamental "petals" are in fact bracts, and they are widely cultivated in South China.

### 45.3.7. Portulacaceae and Basellaceae

Portulacaceae in the narrow sense has in APG IV only *Portulaca*, with about 115 species. The formerly broad Portulacaceae proved to be a paraphyletic group (Cactaceae is nested within it) and was split into Portulacaceae, Talinaceae, Montiaceae, Anacampserotaceae and others; with Cactaceae they form a clade (suborder Portulacineae) whose shared characters are only 2 sepals (in fact bracteoles), succulence, and frequent CAM or facultative CAM. *Portulaca* has succulent leaves; sepals 2; petals 4–6; stamens numerous; ovary half-inferior, 1-locular, with free-central or basal placentation; the capsule is circumscissile (the upper half falls off like a lid). Floral formula: $\ast K_2 C_{4\text{–}6} A_{\infty} \overline{\underline{G}}_{(3\text{–}5:1:\infty)}$. Purslane (*P. oleracea*) is a worldwide field weed with a prostrate stem and obovate leaves; it is a C4 plant that in drought can also operate CAM, and is material for the study of the coexistence of two carbon-concentrating mechanisms in one leaf; the young shoots and leaves are edible and rich in ω-3 fatty acids. Rose moss (*P. grandiflora*) has large flowers of rich colors and is cultivated as an ornamental. *Talinum paniculatum* has been separated into Talinaceae; the root is fleshy and used medicinally.

Basellaceae are twining succulent herbs with fleshy leaves and a persistent perianth that becomes fleshy in fruit. Malabar spinach (*Basella alba*) has succulent, mucilaginous stems and leaves eaten as a vegetable, and the juice of the fruit contains purple-red betalains; Madeira vine (*Anredera cordifolia*) bears bulbils in the leaf axils and has become invasive in South China.

### 45.3.8. Plumbaginaceae and Tamaricaceae

These two families belong to the noncore Caryophyllales and are both adapted to dry and saline environments, with salt glands on the leaves: multicellular glands that secrete excess salt absorbed by the roots onto the leaf surface, where it dries to white salt crystals ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.4)).

Plumbaginaceae (leadwort family) has about 25 genera and 440 species, growing mostly in dry, saline and coastal environments, with the greatest numbers in the Mediterranean and Central Asia; China has about 7 genera and 46 species. Herbs or subshrubs with leaves often basal and bearing salt glands; flowers bisexual and 5-merous, the calyx fused and conspicuously ribbed, persistent, often scarious and colored; corolla sympetalous (rare in Caryophyllales, and arising independently of the sympetalous corolla of the asterids); stamens 5, opposite the corolla lobes; ovary superior, 1-locular, with 1 basal anatropous ovule and 5 styles; fruit enclosed in the persistent calyx. Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_5 \underline{G}_{(5:1:1)}$. *Limonium bicolor* is a perennial herb common on saline soils in North and Northwest China, with a funnel-shaped, scarious white or pale purple calyx and a yellow corolla; the calyx keeps its color when dried and it is an "everlasting flower" (the statice and similar dried flowers are mostly species of the same genus). Ceylon leadwort (*Plumbago zeylanica*) and Cape leadwort (*P. auriculata*) have stalked glandular hairs on the calyx that catch small insects.

Tamaricaceae has about 4 genera and 90 species in the deserts, saline soils and riverbanks of Eurasia and Africa; China has 3 genera and about 30 species, mainly in the northwest. Shrubs or small trees with small, scale-like, alternate leaves bearing salt glands; flowers small, often in racemes or panicles; petals 4–5; stamens 4–10; ovary superior, 1-locular, with parietal or basal placentation; capsule, the seeds with a tuft of long hairs at the tip, dispersed by wind. Chinese tamarisk (*Tamarix chinensis*) has slender pendent branches, scale-like leaves and pink racemes, and flowers two or three times a year; it tolerates salinity and is an important tree for afforestation of saline soils and the seashore in North and Northwest China; *T. ramosissima* is a dominant plant of desert riverbanks in the northwest, and its roots host *Cistanche tubulosa*. *Reaumuria soongarica* is a super-xerophytic small shrub of the northwestern deserts.

### 45.3.9. Droseraceae and Nepenthaceae

Droseraceae and Nepenthaceae belong to the carnivorous clade of Caryophyllales (together with Drosophyllaceae, Dioncophyllaceae and Ancistrocladaceae), in which carnivory arose only once and was lost in Ancistrocladaceae and some Dioncophyllaceae (Soltis et al. 2018). This is another unexpected result of molecular systematics: Cronquist placed Nepenthaceae and Droseraceae in Nepenthales of the Dilleniidae, and morphologists also generally regarded them as close to other **carnivorous plants** such as Sarraceniaceae (Ericales, [7.12.46](/7.12.46)) and Lentibulariaceae (Lamiales, [7.12.48](/7.12.48)); it is now known that carnivory arose independently at least 10 times in angiosperms ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.3)).

Droseraceae has 3 genera and about 200 species. In *Drosera* the leaves are densely covered with glandular hairs (tentacles) tipped with sticky mucilage; when an insect is caught by the mucilage, the tentacles and the leaf blade slowly bend to enclose it, the glands secrete digestive enzymes, and the digestion products are absorbed (a flypaper trap). Venus flytrap (*Dionaea muscipula*) occurs only in southeastern North America; the two halves of the leaf blade are like a clam shell, each bearing 3 sensory hairs on the inside, and when an insect touches the sensory hairs twice within a short time the blade closes within a fraction of a second (a snap trap); this movement depends on electrical signals and changes in turgor in the cells of the blade. Waterwheel plant (*Aldrovanda vesiculosa*) is a rootless submerged plant whose leaf blades close rapidly under water to catch small aquatic animals; it is distributed in northeastern China and Heilongjiang and is extremely rare. *Drosera peltata* and round-leaved sundew (*D. rotundifolia*) occur in China. In *Insectivorous Plants* (1875) Darwin described in detail his experiments on capture and digestion in the round-leaved sundew.

Nepenthaceae has only *Nepenthes*, with about 170 species in the tropics of Southeast Asia (most in Borneo and Sumatra), mostly climbing subshrubs; the midrib of the leaf extends into a tendril whose tip swells into a pitcher-shaped trap (a pitfall trap), with a slippery lip and nectar glands at the mouth of the pitcher that lure insects to slip into the digestive fluid at the bottom; dioecious, with a capsule. *N. mirabilis* occurs in Guangdong and Hainan and is the only native species in China. Some pitcher plants have formed special mutualisms with animals: the pitchers of *N. rajah* and others in Borneo are used as "toilets" by tree shrews, and the plant obtains nitrogen from their droppings.

Most carnivorous plants grow in poor, acidic, waterlogged bogs or on sandstone, where the soil lacks nitrogen and phosphorus, and trapping animals is an adaptation to nutrient poverty; they still have green leaves and can photosynthesize, and the animals they capture are only a supplementary source of mineral nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus.

## 45.4. Evolution of Characters in Caryophyllales

### 45.4.1. Betalains and Anthocyanins

**Definition 45.4.1 (Betalains)** The class of nitrogen-containing, water-soluble pigments synthesized from tyrosine via L-DOPA are called **betalains**; they include the red-violet betacyanins and the yellow betaxanthins.

The red, purple and blue colors of flowers, fruits and leaves of angiosperms come in the great majority from the anthocyanins of the flavonoids ([7.12.3](/7.12.3#sec-3.2.3)). Most families of the core Caryophyllales, however, do not synthesize anthocyanins and use betalains instead: the color of beetroot, red amaranth, cockscomb, the bracts of bougainvillea, the flowers of cacti and the flesh of dragon fruit, and the fruit of Malabar spinach are all betalain colors. Betalains and anthocyanins never occur together in one plant, and the two are mutually exclusive in biosynthesis, although the precursors of anthocyanins (proanthocyanidins and flavonols) are still present in these plants. Outside angiosperms, betalains occur only in a few basidiomycetes (the red of fly agaric).

Betalains are a classic example of chemotaxonomy ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.7)). In the 1960s Mabry and others found that betalains are universal in the families of the Centrospermae, and on this basis placed the morphologically peculiar Cactaceae in the Centrospermae (Caryophyllales) and put the anthocyanin-containing Caryophyllaceae in a separate suborder (or even excluded it), which gave rise to a long controversy at the time. Molecular phylogeny finally gave the explanation: Caryophyllaceae is nested within the betalain-containing group, and its anthocyanins are a secondary restoration. Transcriptome studies (Brockington et al. 2015) show that betalain synthesis depends on the duplication and neofunctionalization of two key enzyme genes (DOPA 4,5-dioxygenase, *DODA*, and cytochrome P450 *CYP76AD1*), an event that probably occurred only once in the ancestor of the core Caryophyllales; afterward, in at least three lineages including Caryophyllaceae and Molluginaceae, these genes lost their function and anthocyanin synthesis was switched back on. Chemical characters thus also revert, and have to be interpreted on the phylogenetic tree and cannot be used alone as a basis for delimiting groups ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.2.2)).

### 45.4.2. C4, CAM and Extreme Environments

Caryophyllales is the order with the highest concentration of angiosperm groups adapted to extreme environments: deserts, saline soils, seashores, high mountains and nutrient-poor bogs are all dominated by members of Caryophyllales. These adaptations have appeared many times independently on the phylogenetic tree:

| Adaptation | Structure and physiology | Distribution in Caryophyllales | Origin |
|---|---|---|---|
| C4 photosynthesis | Kranz anatomy or single-cell C4; high photosynthetic efficiency under high light, high temperature and drought | Amaranthaceae (Salsoloideae and Suaedoideae of the former Chenopodiaceae, and *Amaranthus*), Portulacaceae, Molluginaceae, Aizoaceae | Independently more than 20 times, more than ten times in Amaranthaceae alone |
| CAM | Succulent, fixing CO₂ at night and closing the stomata by day | Cactaceae, Aizoaceae, Portulacaceae, Anacampserotaceae, Didiereaceae | Many times, often facultative CAM |
| Halophytism | Succulence, salt glands, salt bladders (bladder hairs), ion compartmentation | Amaranthaceae (glasswort, seablite, saltbush), Plumbaginaceae, Tamaricaceae, Aizoaceae | Many times |
| Carnivory | Leaves modified into traps, glandular hairs secreting digestive enzymes | Droseraceae, Nepenthaceae, Drosophyllaceae, Dioncophyllaceae | Once |

C4 photosynthesis arose independently more than 60 times in angiosperms, about a third of them in Caryophyllales, which makes the order an ideal group for the study of the repeated evolution of a complex trait. It is generally thought that the common ancestor of these lineages already had certain "preadaptations", such as a fairly well-developed bundle sheath and denser leaf venation, which allowed the C4 pathway to appear again and again in hot, dry or saline environments. In leaf structure, the C4 species of the former Chenopodiaceae show many different kinds of Kranz anatomy, and there are even special types in which the C4 cycle is completed within a single cell (*Suaeda aralocaspica* and others), showing that the same physiological pathway can be realized by different anatomical structures ([7.12.23](/7.12.23#sec-23.5.6)). The physiological and biochemical mechanisms of the photosynthetic pathways are treated in the plant physiology course.

### 45.4.3. Evolution of Placentation and the Origin of "Petals"

Chinese textbooks often use Caryophyllales to illustrate the evolution of placentation types: in *Silene* the lower part of the ovary has a septum, giving axile placentation, and the septum disappears in the upper part; in most Caryophyllaceae the septum disappears completely, giving free-central placentation; with further simplification the ovules remain only at the base of the ovary (basal placentation), and finally, as in Amaranthaceae and Polygonaceae, only 1 ovule is left ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.2.6)). This direction of evolution from axile through free-central to basal placentation is largely consistent with the phylogeny of the core Caryophyllales.

The "petals" of Caryophyllales are equally noteworthy. The ancestral perianth of the core Caryophyllales consisted of a single whorl of sepal-like tepals; the structures that look like petals in many families formed independently in different lineages: the petals of Caryophyllaceae (and the "petals" of Aizoaceae) are petaloid staminodes derived from stamens, whereas the petal-like structures of Portulacaceae and Talinaceae come from sepals (Brockington et al. 2012; Ronse De Craene 2013). So the flower of Caryophyllaceae with "5 sepals, 5 petals, 10 stamens" resembles in outward form the pentamerous flower of typical core eudicots such as Rosaceae, but its petals are not homologous organs.

## Questions

1. How can Caryophyllaceae and Polygonaceae be distinguished? What is their relationship in the system?

   **Answer** Both are herbs with swollen nodes. Caryophyllaceae has simple opposite leaves and no ocrea; the flowers have sepals and petals, the petals are often clawed and the stamens usually 10; the ovary is 1-locular with free-central placentation and many ovules; the capsule opens by teeth at the apex; the embryo is curved around the perisperm. Polygonaceae has simple alternate leaves with a membranous ocrea; the tepals are 3–6 and petaloid, not differentiated into calyx and corolla; the ovary is 1-locular with 1 orthotropous ovule; the fruit is a trigonous or lenticular achene enclosed in the persistent perianth; the seed has endosperm. In APG IV both belong to Caryophyllales but to its two different large clades: Caryophyllaceae lies in the core Caryophyllales and Polygonaceae in the noncore Caryophyllales (together with Plumbaginaceae, Tamaricaceae and the carnivorous clade).

2. Of what organ are the spines of a cactus a modification? Why is Cactaceae placed in Caryophyllales?

   **Answer** The areole of a cactus is a greatly shortened short shoot in a leaf axil, and the spines are modified leaves (or bud scales) of this short shoot; *Pereskia* has normal leaves, with spines borne on the areole in the leaf axil, which clearly shows this homology. Flowers and new branches also grow from the areole, which shows that the areole is the position of a bud. The evidence that Cactaceae belongs in Caryophyllales is: betalains rather than anthocyanins (chemical evidence); sieve-element plastids of the PIII type characteristic of Caryophyllales (ultrastructural evidence); and molecular phylogeny shows that it is nested within the core Caryophyllales and closest to Portulacaceae in the narrow sense.

3. Why is the distribution of betalains a classic example of chemotaxonomy? How should the "exception" of anthocyanins in Caryophyllaceae be explained?

   **Answer** Betalains and anthocyanins are mutually exclusive in one plant, and betalains occur in angiosperms only in Caryophyllales, so they can be used to link the morphologically very different Cactaceae, Nyctaginaceae, Amaranthaceae, Aizoaceae and others, and to include the morphologically peculiar Cactaceae. Caryophyllaceae has anthocyanins and was once thought to be excluded from Caryophyllales or placed in a separate suborder; molecular phylogeny shows that Caryophyllaceae is nested within the betalain-containing group, and its anthocyanins are a secondary restoration: the betalain synthesis genes (*DODA*, *CYP76AD1*) acquired new functions in the ancestor of the core Caryophyllales and were later lost in lineages such as Caryophyllaceae, and anthocyanin synthesis was switched back on. This shows that chemical characters also revert and must be interpreted on the phylogenetic tree.

4. Loranthaceae and the mistletoes of Santalaceae look similar. Why do they belong to different families? How did the parasitic habit evolve in Santalales?

   **Answer** In Loranthaceae the flowers are bisexual, larger, with a tubular and brightly colored corolla adapted to bird pollination, and with a calyculus; in the mistletoes the flowers are unisexual, tiny and green, without a calyculus; embryology and chromosomes also differ. Molecular evidence shows that they belong to different lineages of Santalales (the mistletoes are now merged into Santalaceae s.l.), and the stem-parasitic habit arose independently at least 5 times in Santalales. The order of evolution is: the earliest-diverging Olacaceae include nonparasitic groups and root hemiparasitic groups, and root parasitism is the ancestral state; stem parasitism evolved from root parasitism, accompanied by the sticky berries and dispersal by birds (seeds stuck to branches germinate there); a few groups (Balanophoraceae) went further and lost chlorophyll, becoming holoparasites.

5. (Open question) C4 photosynthesis arose more than 20 times in Caryophyllales and not once in many other orders. What do you think are the possible reasons? How could your hypothesis be tested by the comparative method?

   **Answer** Outline: one explanation is "preadaptation": the ancestors of many Caryophyllales lineages already had a fairly well-developed bundle sheath, denser leaf venation and higher expression of genes related to photorespiration, so that the C4 pathway needed fewer steps to be established; another is ecological opportunity: Caryophyllales is concentrated in hot, dry, saline and open habitats, where the advantage of C4 is greatest. To test this, ancestral leaf anatomical characters (bundle sheath cell size, vein density) and habitats can be reconstructed on the phylogenetic tree of Caryophyllales, and phylogenetic comparative methods used to test the correlation of "C4 origin" with these ancestral characters or habitats (for example by independent contrasts or models of correlated evolution of discrete characters), with sister lineages that lack C4 as controls; one can also compare the anatomy and physiology of C3–C4 intermediates (found in *Portulaca* and in Molluginaceae) to see whether C4 appears repeatedly along the same intermediate stages.
