The preceding chapters described the typical form and structure of roots, stems and leaves. This chapter takes up two problems that link the three vegetative organs. The first is metamorphosis: the same kind of organ can perform entirely different functions in different plants, with a corresponding change in form and structure. The "root" of a radish, the "tuber" of a potato, the "head" of an onion, the tendril of a pea and the spine of a cactus can be understood only by going back to the organs from which they derive, and the method of judging that origin is the criterion of homology treated in [7.10.2](/7.10.2). The second is the connection between organs: how the vascular tissue runs from root through stem to leaf, and how root and shoot, terminal bud and axillary buds influence one another in growth. Finally we treat vegetative propagation, which relies mostly on modified vegetative organs and is the most widely used way of propagating plants in agriculture and horticulture; sexual reproduction begins with the flower and is taken up in the chapters from [7.12.27](/7.12.27) on. The structure of root, stem and leaf used in this chapter is in [7.12.20](/7.12.20)–[7.12.23](/7.12.23); the hormonal control of apical dominance, rooting and graft union belongs to plant physiology, and only morphology and phenomena are described here.

## 24.1. Metamorphosis, Homologous Organs and Analogous Organs

In most cases the form and structure of a given organ are much alike in different plants. In nature, however, the organs of some plants have adapted to special environments, changing their original function and, with it, form and structure, and after long natural selection these changes have become stable hereditary features of the species.

**Definition 24.1.1 (Metamorphosis of Vegetative Organs)** The marked changes in form and structure of the vegetative organs of a plant (root, stem, leaf) that result from a change of function and are stably inherited are the **metamorphosis** of vegetative organs. Metamorphosis is a normal, healthy hereditary trait, unlike pathological or accidental malformation.

The term "metamorphosis" comes from Goethe's *Die Metamorphose der Pflanzen* (1790): he held that the various aerial organs of a plant are different transformations of one basic organ (the leaf), and that the flower is a modified shoot adapted for reproduction ([7.12.27](/7.12.27)). From the standpoint of metamorphosis, the origin of a modified organ is judged by its position on the plant body, its connections with other organs, its internal structure (especially the vascular tissue) and its ontogeny, not by outward form or function; these are exactly the criteria of **homology** by which comparative morphology recognizes homologous organs ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)). Organs of the same origin that have diverged in form through adaptation to different functions are homologous; leaf spines, scale leaves, insect-trapping leaves and leaf tendrils, for example, are all modified leaves.

**Definition 24.1.2 (Analogous Organs)** Organs of different origin that resemble one another in form because they perform similar functions are **analogous organs**.

Stem tendrils and leaf tendrils, stem thorns and leaf spines, rhizomes and roots, cladodes and leaves are all pairs of analogous organs. The similarity of analogous organs is the result of **convergent evolution**. Whether some organs are homologous or analogous is not easy to decide (the tendrils of Cucurbitaceae, the tendrils of *Smilax*) and requires a combined study of form, structure and development and, where necessary, a comparison of the genes controlling their development (the tendrils and leaflets of the pea leaf in [7.12.23](/7.12.23)).

## 24.2. Modifications of the Root

Root modifications are chiefly of three kinds: storage roots, aerial roots and parasitic roots.

### 24.2.1. Storage Roots

Storage roots are thick and juicy, store nutrients, vary in shape, and are common in biennial or perennial herbaceous dicotyledons; the nutrients stored supply bolting, flowering and fruiting in the following year and are an adaptation for overwintering. By origin they are of two kinds, fleshy taproots and root tubers.

**Definition 24.2.1 (Fleshy Taproot and Root Tuber)** A storage root formed mainly by thickening of the taproot, one per plant, is a fleshy taproot. A storage root formed by thickening of lateral or adventitious roots, of which a plant may have several, is a **root tuber**.

The upper part of a fleshy taproot derives from the hypocotyl and has no lateral roots; it is the root neck. At the very top is the stem base with extremely short internodes, bearing many leaves, called the crown. The swollen taproot forms the main body of the fleshy taproot and has lateral roots or the scars they leave. The fleshy taproots of radish, carrot and beet are similar in outward form and all have diarch primary xylem, but they thicken in different ways and the storage tissue has different origins (Fig. 1).

- Carrot: the **vascular cambium** acts normally, and most of the secondary tissue it produces is secondary phloem, whose parenchyma is extremely well developed and stores large amounts of nutrients (carotene accumulates in its leucoplasts and chromoplasts, making the root orange); there is less secondary xylem, most of it xylem parenchyma with few vessels, and it forms the "core".
- Radish: the reverse; the secondary xylem is well developed, with few vessels and no fibers, parenchyma forms the main part and stores large amounts of nutrients, and there is little secondary phloem. Some cells of the xylem parenchyma can resume division and become new cambia producing xylem inward and phloem outward. These cambia arising outside the normal vascular cambium are called supernumerary cambia (also accessory cambia), and the vascular tissue they produce is tertiary xylem and tertiary phloem, together tertiary structure ([7.12.22](/7.12.22)).
- Beet: the initial cambial activity is the same as in the other two; while it is still active, a ring of supernumerary cambium arises in the pericycle (and parenchyma derived from it), forming new vascular tissue and abundant parenchyma, and later new supernumerary cambia arise in this parenchyma, so that in turn several rings can be produced, each lying outside the phloem of the preceding ring of bundles. The result is that rings of vascular tissue and rings of parenchyma alternate, giving conspicuous concentric rings in cross section. In good cultivars the fleshy root can have 8–12 or more rings, counting the initial cambium; sugar is stored in the parenchyma, especially in the xylem parenchyma within the vascular tissue, so the development of the xylem parenchyma can be used to judge the sugar yield of a beet cultivar.

Root tubers differ from fleshy taproots: they develop from lateral or adventitious roots, contain no hypocotyl or stem part, and are entirely root. The root tubers of sweet potato, cassava and dahlia are of this kind. In sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*), root tubers form from adventitious roots when the plant is propagated by cuttings and from lateral roots when it is grown from seed. The primary xylem of the early root tuber is triarch to hexarch; in the secondary structure the parenchyma is well developed, and the vessels of the xylem are separated by parenchyma into many vessel groups or single vessels scattered in the parenchyma; later many supernumerary cambia arise in the parenchyma around each vessel group or vessel, and sometimes in vessel-free parenchyma or outside the phloem. Unlike beet, they do not form concentric rings; instead many small cambia centered on vessel groups form on the inner side of the primary cambium, each producing tertiary xylem, tertiary phloem and abundant starch-storing parenchyma, so that the root tuber keeps enlarging. The phloem of the sweet potato tuber also contains laticifers, and white latex runs out when it is cut.

### 24.2.2. Aerial Roots

Roots growing in the air above the ground are aerial roots, of which there are three common kinds.

- Prop roots: adventitious roots arising from the stem nodes near the ground in maize, sorghum and sugarcane; as they lengthen their tips enter the soil and go on producing lateral roots, forming an auxiliary root system that strengthens support. The epidermis of maize prop roots is often cutinized and sclerenchyma is well developed. Prop roots are produced abundantly in fertile soil and humid air, and hilling up with soil also promotes them. Banyan (*Ficus*) produces many hanging aerial roots from its branches; after entering the soil they become woody prop roots by secondary growth, forming the "one tree makes a forest" landscape in the tropics and subtropics. The mangrove genus *Rhizophora* (Rhizophoraceae) produces arching prop roots from trunk and branches, supporting the plant in intertidal mud.
- Climbing roots: in ivy, star jasmine (*Trachelospermum*), trumpet creeper (*Campsis*) and creeping fig (*Ficus pumila*), the stem is slender and weak and cannot stand erect; adventitious roots arise on the stem and fix it to tree trunks, rocks or walls so that the plant climbs.
- Pneumatophores: in plants growing in coastal mud or swamps, some lateral roots grow upward and project above the mud surface, with lenticels outside and well-developed **aerenchyma** inside, adapted to the oxygen-poor soil. Grey mangrove (*Avicennia marina*) and *Sonneratia* have peg-like pneumatophores, and *Bruguiera gymnorhiza* has knee-like ones; among gymnosperms, Chinese swamp cypress (*Glyptostrobus*) and bald cypress (*Taxodium*) also form knee-like "knees" on wet ground.

The aerial roots of epiphytic orchids have a velamen on the surface, made of several layers of dead cells, which is filled with air and silvery-white when dry and quickly absorbs water in rain; the aerial roots of *Dendrobium*, spider plant (*Chlorophytum*) and others also contain chloroplasts and can photosynthesize.

### 24.2.3. Parasitic Roots

Parasitic plants send specialized roots into the tissues of the host, connecting with its vascular tissue and drawing water and nutrients from it; such a root is a parasitic root, also called a **haustorium**. Dodder (*Cuscuta chinensis*) twines with its stem around the host stem, its leaves are reduced to scales, it can hardly photosynthesize and depends wholly on the host for nutrition, and the haustoria produced on the stem enter the host stem, their vessels and sieve tubes connecting with the xylem and phloem of the host; when the host is weak, dodder often twines around itself and takes up nutrients from its own other branches in order to flower and fruit. Mistletoe (*Viscum coloratum*) and Loranthaceae also have haustoria but have green leaves of their own, make organic nutrients themselves and take only water and mineral salts from the host; they are hemiparasites, unlike holoparasites such as dodder. Broomrape (*Orobanche*) and *Aeginetia* parasitize the roots of hosts. Parasitic plants arose independently more than ten times in angiosperms; see [7.12.45](/7.12.45) (Santalales) and other chapters.

## 24.3. Modifications of the Stem

Stem modifications fall into aerial stems and underground stems. However it is modified, the stem keeps the basic features of a stem: it has nodes and internodes, the nodes bear leaves (often reduced to scale leaves) or leaf scars, and the leaf axils bear buds; a root has no nodes, internodes, leaves or buds, has a root cap at its tip, and its lateral roots arise endogenously. These are the main criteria for recognizing a modified stem.

### 24.3.1. Modifications of Aerial Stems

The aerial stem is closely related to the leaves and is also called the aerial shoot. Its main modifications are as follows.

- Stem thorn (branch thorn): the stem (branch) is turned into a thorn, borne in the leaf axil (the position of the axillary bud), connected to the stem by vascular tissue and sometimes branching or bearing leaves, as in the simple thorns of hawthorn, bitter orange and citrus and the branched thorns of honey locust (§24.4.4).
- Stem tendril (branch tendril): the branch of a climbing plant is turned into a tendril. The grape tendril is opposite the leaf, at a position corresponding to an inflorescence (grape tendrils occasionally bear flowers), and the tendrils of Boston ivy (*Parthenocissus tricuspidata*) swell at the tip into adhesive discs.
- Cladode (phylloclade): the stem is flat or finely divided and green, replacing the leaves in photosynthesis, while the leaves are reduced to scales. In butcher's broom (*Ruscus aculeatus*), the lateral branches become ovate cladodes, the leaves are reduced to scales, and small flowers are borne in the axil of the scale (the middle of the cladode), often mistaken for "flowers borne on leaves". The "leaves" of asparagus and asparagus fern are filiform or needle-like cladodes, the real leaves being the small scales at the base of the branch. The cladodes of *Homalocladium platycladum* are flat and jointed, with small or no leaves. The flat stem segments of orchid cactus (*Epiphyllum*) and Christmas cactus (*Schlumbergera*) are also cladodes. A true leaf bears a bud in its axil, whereas a cladode has no bud in its axil (it itself arises in the axil of a scale leaf), and this distinguishes the two.
- Fleshy (succulent) stem: the stem is thick and juicy, green, with well-developed water-storage tissue, capable of photosynthesis, and the leaves are often reduced; it is an adaptation to dry environments, as in the cacti (prickly pear, *Opuntia ficus-indica*) and succulent species of *Euphorbia*. Aerial fleshy stems also include the swollen globose stem of kohlrabi and the fleshy stem of stem lettuce.
- Bulblet: a small bulb borne on the aerial part of the plant. In garlic (*Allium sativum*) the inflorescence often bears many small globular bodies with thick small scale leaves, called bulbils; in tiger lily (*Lilium lancifolium*) small purplish-black bulblets are often borne in the leaf axils of the aerial stem. When they have grown they fall off and, under suitable conditions, develop into new plants.
- Tubercle: a small tuber borne on the aerial part, without scale leaves; in Chinese yam and begonia the axillary buds often grow into fleshy small balls (bulbils).
- Stolon (runner): a slender stem arising from the base of the plant and growing prostrate along the ground, with adventitious roots at the nodes and buds growing into new plants, as in strawberry, mock strawberry (*Potentilla indica*), Bermuda grass and strawberry saxifrage (§24.7.2).

### 24.3.2. Modifications of Underground Stems

Stems growing underground resemble roots in outward form but have nodes and internodes, with leaves generally reduced to scales that leave leaf scars when they fall and with axillary buds in the leaf axils, so they are easily distinguished from roots. There are four common kinds of underground stem (Fig. 2).

**Definition 24.3.1 (Rhizome)** A modified stem lying horizontally underground, root-like in form but with distinct nodes and internodes, scale leaves and axillary buds at the nodes, and bearing adventitious roots, is a **rhizome**.

Bamboo, lotus, reed and ginger, and many weeds (cogongrass, Bermuda grass, *Aster indicus*), have rhizomes. Rhizomes store abundant nutrients, and in spring the axillary buds develop into new aerial shoots. The lotus "root" is the thicker, terminal-bud-bearing segments at the end of the rhizome of the lotus, with reduced scale leaves at the nodes and flower stalks and petioles emerging from the leaf axils. The bamboo whip is the rhizome of bamboo, with distinct nodes and internodes; the bamboo shoot is a bud on a node of the whip that emerges above ground and later develops into the aerial culm. Bamboo, reed and many weeds spread in all directions by rhizomes to form clumps; when the rhizomes of weeds are cut by plowing, each piece can grow independently into a new plant, which is why cogongrass and field thistle are hard to eradicate.

**Definition 24.3.2 (Tuber)** A short, thick modified stem formed by swelling at the tip of an underground stem (mostly a stolon) as nutrients accumulate, with buds (eyes) arranged in a spiral on its surface, is a **tuber**.

The tuber of the potato (*Solanum tuberosum*) is the commonest. In plants grown from seed the tubers form at the tips of the underground stolons; when tuber pieces are planted, the tubers form at the ends of underground rhizomes. The many depressions on the tuber are the eyes, corresponding to nodes; when young they bear reduced scale leaves, which later fall, the depression being the axil of the scale leaf. The eyes are arranged in a spiral on the tuber, like the phyllotaxis of the aerial stem, each eye usually has three buds, only one of which usually sprouts, and the tip of the tuber (the end away from the stolon) bears a terminal bud. The internal structure of the tuber is the same as that of an aerial stem, differing only in the amounts of the tissues: from outside in, periderm (the "skin"), cortex, external phloem, cambium, xylem, internal phloem and pith (the vascular bundles of Solanaceae are bicollateral, [7.12.5](/7.12.5)), of which the internal phloem is well developed and forms the main part of the tuber; in phloem and xylem alike, the starch-storing parenchyma is the best developed. The tubers of Jerusalem artichoke (*Helianthus tuberosus*) store inulin and can be used for syrup or the extraction of fructose; the tubers of Chinese artichoke (*Stachys sieboldii*) are bead-like and are pickled as "screw vegetable".

**Definition 24.3.3 (Bulb)** An underground modified stem consisting of a flat or disc-shaped, extremely short stem (the basal plate) and many thick, fleshy scale leaves borne on it is a **bulb**.

Adventitious roots arise below the basal plate, there is a terminal bud in the center, and axillary buds lie between the scale leaves. In onion (*Allium cepa*) the scale leaves do not form conspicuous segments but each encloses the basal plate completely as a whole; each scale leaf is the base of an aerial leaf. The outer few become dry, membranous scale leaves when the aerial leaves die, enclose the bulb and protect it, and the inner fleshy scale leaves are rich in sugar and are the main edible part; a bulb with membranous scales outside is a tunicate bulb. In lily the scale leaves are separate and form segments arranged in an overlapping manner, with no membranous tunic, and the bulb is called scaly; the starch-rich scale leaves are the edible part. Garlic resembles onion: the young bulb, tender scale leaves and aerial leaves (green garlic) are all edible; when mature it bolts (garlic scapes) and flowers, the scale leaves dry up into membranes, and the swollen axillary buds between the scale leaves (cloves, each itself a small bulb) become the main edible part. Scallion, rakkyo, daffodil, red spider lily (*Lycoris*), hyacinth and tulip also have bulbs.

**Definition 24.3.4 (Corm)** A short, thick, globose or flattened-globose underground modified stem, with distinct nodes and internodes, membranous scale leaves at the nodes and a stout terminal bud at the tip, in which nutrients are stored in the stem itself, is a **corm**.

The corms of Chinese water chestnut (*Eleocharis dulcis*) and arrowhead (*Sagittaria trifolia*) form by swelling at the tip of an underground stolon (rhizome); in water chestnut there are many lateral buds clustered around the terminal bud. The main corm of taro (*Colocasia esculenta*) is formed by swelling of the stem base of the plant, with daughter corms arising around it; the underground parts of gladiolus, saffron crocus and konjac are also corms, a new corm forming above the old one each year. Corms and bulbs are similar in outward form; the difference is that the main body of a bulb is its thick scale leaves and the stem (basal plate) is very small, whereas the main body of a corm is the thick stem and the scale leaves are thin, small and membranous.

## 24.4. Modifications of the Leaf

### 24.4.1. Bracts, Scale Leaves and Phyllodes

A modified leaf borne below a flower or inflorescence is a bract; many bracts clustered around the outside of an inflorescence form an involucre. Bracts are generally small and green but may be large and variously colored. Bracts and involucres protect flower buds or fruits, and serve other functions as well. The involucre around the capitulum of Asteraceae, its shape and number of layers, is a basis for distinguishing genera and species. The four white involucral bracts at the base of the spike of chameleon plant (*Houttuynia cordata*) and the two large white bracts beneath the head of dove tree (*Davidia involucrata*) look like petals and attract pollinating insects. The showy "petals" of poinsettia and bougainvillea are also bracts. The involucre of cocklebur is fused into a sac enclosing the fruit and bears hooked spines on the outside, which attach easily to animals and favor dispersal of the fruit. The spathe of Araceae and the glumes of grass spikelets are also bracts.

A leaf specialized in function or reduced to a scale is a scale leaf. Scale leaves are of two kinds. One is the scale leaves outside the scaly buds of woody plants, often brown, with hairs or mucilage, protecting the bud; these are bud scales. The other is the scale leaves on underground stems, of fleshy and membranous kinds: fleshy scale leaves occur on bulbs, thick and juicy and storing abundant nutrients, as in onion and lily; membranous scale leaves occur on corms (water chestnut, arrowhead) and rhizomes (lotus, bamboo whip), brown and dry-membranous, and are reduced leaves. Onion has both fleshy scale leaves and, outside them, membranous ones.

**Definition 24.4.1 (Phyllode)** A modified leaf whose blade is poorly developed or entirely reduced, and whose petiole is expanded into a flat blade-like structure that takes over photosynthesis from the blade, is a **phyllode**.

*Acacia confusa* produces a few normal twice-pinnate compound leaves only as a seedling; the leaflets of later leaves are wholly reduced, leaving only sickle-shaped phyllodes with several parallel longitudinal veins. In many *Acacia* species of the arid regions of Australia, the first leaves of the seedling are normal pinnate compound leaves, but in later leaves the petiole gradually flattens and the leaflets gradually decrease, until only the phyllode remains (Fig. 3C). Like a blade, a phyllode can photosynthesize, but it stands on edge, is lit about equally on both sides, is often isobilateral, and transpires little, which is an adaptation to drought.

### 24.4.2. Leaf Tendrils and the Origin of Tendrils

**Definition 24.4.2 (Leaf Tendril)** A leaf of which all or part is turned into a tendril, serving for climbing, is a **leaf tendril**.

In pea (*Pisum sativum*) the several terminal pairs of leaflets of the pinnate compound leaf become tendrils (Fig. 3A); the pair of tendrils on both sides of the petiole in *Smilax* is traditionally interpreted as modified stipules; in *Flagellaria indica* and flame lily the leaf tip is extended into a tendril. Tendrils are typical analogous organs: tendrils of the same outward form can come from different organs, and in judging them the position must be examined. A leaf tendril is borne on the leaf (the tip of the rachis, the leaf tip or the base of the petiole) and has no bud in its axil; a stem tendril is borne in a leaf axil (pumpkin, cucumber) or opposite a leaf (grape) and itself occupies the position of a bud. The origins of the various tendrils are as follows.

| Origin of the tendril | Position and recognition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Terminal leaflets | at the tip of the rachis of a pinnate leaf, normal leaflets below | pea, sweet pea, *Vicia* |
| Leaf tip | the midrib of a simple leaf extended into a tendril | *Flagellaria indica*, flame lily |
| Stipules (base of petiole) | one on each side of the petiole | *Smilax* |
| Petiole | the petiole itself twines around a support (no tendril is formed) | *Clematis*, nasturtium |
| Stem (branch) | opposite a leaf, at the position of an inflorescence; often branched | grape, Boston ivy |
| Stem (axillary) | borne beside the leaf axil, often branched | pumpkin, cucumber, loofah (interpretation still disputed) |
| Inflorescence | the end of the inflorescence axis turned into a tendril | coral vine (*Antigonon leptopus*) |

The origin of the cucurbit tendril has long been disputed: it is borne to one side of the leaf axil and is traditionally regarded as a stem tendril, but some regard it as a modified first leaf (prophyll) of the axillary branch, or as a composite organ made of branch and leaf together; this also shows that judging homology sometimes requires developmental and molecular evidence ([7.12.23](/7.12.23), the *afila* and *tendril-less* mutations of pea).

### 24.4.3. Insect-Trapping Leaves

Some plants have modified leaves that can trap and eat small animals; these are insect-trapping leaves, and plants with them are **carnivorous plants**. Trapping leaves mostly occur in poor acid bogs, wet ground or epiphytic habitats, and the captured insects, once digested, supply mineral nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus. Carnivorous plants generally have chloroplasts and can photosynthesize; they survive without animal food and grow better and set more seed when given suitable animal food. Trapping leaves take several forms.

- Pitcher: in the pitcher plant *Nepenthes mirabilis*, the base of the leaf is flat like a blade and photosynthesizes, the midrib extends into a tendril, and the tip of the tendril swells into a trapping pitcher with a lid over the mouth (Fig. 3B). Nectar glands lie under the lid and on the lip of the pitcher; the ring-like lip (peristome) at the rim is extremely slippery when wet, and insects slip on it and fall into the pitcher (Bohn and Federle, 2004); the upper part of the inner wall is waxy and the lower part has digestive glands that secrete a digestive fluid containing proteases. In the North American pitcher plants (*Sarracenia*) the trapping pitcher is formed by rolling up the whole leaf.
- Disc (sticky trap): the leaf of sundew (*Drosera peltata*) is half-moon-shaped or disc-shaped, with many tentacles (glandular hairs) on the upper surface whose tips are swollen and secrete mucilage; after an insect is stuck, the tentacles bend inward and enclose it, and secrete digestive fluid to digest and absorb it.
- Snap trap: the two halves of the blade of Venus flytrap (*Dionaea muscipula*) can close together, and each has three sensory hairs on its inner surface; when an insect touches the sensory hairs twice within a short time, the blade closes within a fraction of a second.
- Bladder: bladderwort (*Utricularia vulgaris*) is a submerged perennial whose leaves are finely divided, some of the segments becoming trapping bladders. Each bladder has an opening closed by a trap door that opens only inward; absorbing hairs on the bladder wall pump water out, so the inside is under negative pressure; when a small animal touches the stiff hairs outside the door, the door opens and the animal is sucked in with the water, after which the door shuts at once; glands on the bladder wall secrete digestive fluid and the products of digestion are absorbed by the wall.

Carnivorous plants arose independently several times in angiosperms: Nepenthaceae and Droseraceae (including the Venus flytrap) belong to the Caryophyllales ([7.12.45](/7.12.45)), bladderwort to Lentibulariaceae in the Lamiales ([7.12.48](/7.12.48)), and *Sarracenia* to the Ericales ([7.12.46](/7.12.46)); trapping leaves of different origins are another example of convergent evolution.

### 24.4.4. Leaf Spines and the Origin of Spines

**Definition 24.4.3 (Leaf Spine)** A spine formed from a leaf or part of a leaf (such as a stipule) is a **leaf spine**; one formed from a stipule is a stipular spine.

In *Berberis*, the leaves on the long shoots are turned into three-pronged spines, with a bud in the axil of the spine that later develops into a short shoot bearing normal leaves; the spines of cacti are modified leaves borne on an areole (an extremely shortened short shoot). The stipules of black locust become a pair of spines at the stipule position on both sides of the petiole base, which are very easy to recognize; the stipular spines of jujube are one long and one short, the long one straight and the short one curved downward.

By origin, plant spines are of four kinds. They can look very similar outwardly, and recognition depends on position and on whether vascular tissue connects them with the stem (Fig. 4).

| Spine | Origin | Position | Vascular tissue connection with the stem | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thorn | branch (axillary bud) | leaf axil, often branched or bearing leaves | connected, not easily detached | hawthorn, honey locust, citrus, firethorn (*Pyracantha*), trifoliate orange |
| Leaf spine | leaf | position of a leaf, with a bud in the axil of the spine | connected | barberry, cacti |
| Stipular spine | stipule | on both sides of the petiole base, paired | connected | black locust, jujube, sour jujube |
| Prickle | epidermis and the cortex beneath it (an outgrowth) | no fixed position, scattered on stem, petiole and veins | not connected, easily detached | rose, rugosa rose, *Rubus*, Sichuan pepper |

In addition, a leaf tip or marginal tooth turned into a stiff spine (the leaf tip of *Yucca gloriosa*, the spinose teeth of Chinese holly and sow thistle) is a local change of the blade and is usually not listed as a modification. Spines reduce feeding by animals; in the bullhorn acacia (*Vachellia cornigera*) of Central America the swollen, hollow stipular spines are inhabited by ants that protect the plant (mutualism, [7.10.17](/7.10.17)). The "spines" on the stems of *Rosa* are prickles, not thorns, the most common misidentification.

## 24.5. Vascular Connections Between Vegetative Organs

The vascular tissue of root, stem and leaf is continuous and makes up a vascular system running through the whole plant body.

### 24.5.1. Stem and Leaf, Stem and Branch

The epidermis, cortex and vascular bundles of the petiole are continuous with the corresponding structures of the stem, the most important being the connection of the vascular tissue. The segment of a vascular bundle that bends outward from the node, crosses the cortex and enters the petiole base is the **leaf trace** ([7.12.21](/7.12.21)). Different plants differ in how the leaf traces enter the petiole: in some the trace leaves the stem bundle and enters the petiole base directly at the node; in others it merges with other leaf traces and ascends through the cortex for one or several nodes before entering the petiole. After entering the petiole the leaf trace connects with the vascular bundles of the leaf, runs through the petiole into the blade and branches extensively there to form the veins. After a leaf trace departs from the vascular cylinder of the stem and bends outward, it leaves above it in the vascular cylinder a region filled with parenchyma, the **leaf gap**; the corresponding region in the eustele of seed plants resembles the leaf gap of the fern siphonostele in form but is not the same in origin ([7.12.16](/7.12.16)).

The axillary bud in the leaf axil later develops into a branch, and the connection between stem and branch is like that between stem and leaf: the vascular bundles that depart from the vascular cylinder of the stem, cross the cortex and enter the lateral branch are the **branch trace**, and the region filled with parenchyma above the branch trace is the branch gap. In dicotyledons and gymnosperms there are generally two branch traces, in some plants one or several. Because of the production of leaf traces and branch traces, the divergence and merging of vascular tissue in the stem near the nodes are extremely complex, and the situation is more complex still in stems with short internodes, crowded leaves, many whorled leaves and leaf sheaths, where the leaf traces are more numerous. To understand the vascular system of the stem, the whole of the mutual continuity of the vascular systems of stem and leaf must be studied (the types of nodal anatomy, unilacunar, trilacunar and so on, are in [7.12.21](/7.12.21)).

### 24.5.2. The Root-Stem Transition Region

Stem and root together make up the main axis of the plant body, and in embryonic development they arise as one continuous structure. But the arrangement of primary vascular tissue differs greatly between them: in the root the primary xylem and primary phloem are independent of each other and alternate on different radii, the primary xylem being exarch; in the stem the primary xylem and primary phloem lie on the same radius, arranged inside and outside in vascular bundles, the primary xylem being endarch. From root to stem there must therefore be a process of reorganization and connection of the vascular tissue.

**Definition 24.5.1 (Transition Region)** The region in a seedling where root and stem meet and the vascular tissue changes from the arrangement of the root to that of the stem is the root-stem **transition region**.

The transition region is usually short, from less than 1 mm to 2–3 mm, seldom reaching a few centimeters; it generally lies at the base, middle or upper part of the hypocotyl above the radicle, and ends at the cotyledonary node. In seed plants other than most monocotyledons, the transition occurs in the vascular system connecting the root to the cotyledons. In the transition region the epidermis and cortex are directly continuous, and only the vascular tissue is reorganized. Taking the diarch root of *Cucurbita* and *Phaseolus* as an example (Fig. 5): from below upward the vascular cylinder first widens, and each primary xylem strand of the root seems to split lengthwise into two forks, the two branches turning toward the left and right as they rise and each seeming to rotate nearly 180° at the same time, and finally joining the split and displaced phloem inside and outside to form the endarch vascular bundles of the stem (upper hypocotyl and cotyledons), with a pith appearing in the center. The "splitting" and "rotation" are only a description: in fact, at different heights, division and differentiation of procambial cells produce different kinds and arrangements of tissue, so that xylem elements appear in succession at different positions and orientations; no strand of tissue is really twisted. The number of primary vascular bundles in the stem can therefore differ from the number of primary phloem strands in the root. The structure of the transition region can be seen clearly only in the primary structure and is concealed by secondary tissues after secondary growth.

## 24.6. Interactions Among Vegetative Organs

Vascular tissue connects root, stem and leaf, and the organs also promote or restrict one another through the supply of nutrients, hormonal regulation, and the distribution of water and mineral nutrients.

The underground and aerial parts depend on each other. When a seed germinates the root generally appears first, and only after the root has grown to a certain extent do the hypocotyl and plumule emerge from the soil to form the aerial shoot system; throughout the period of growth, the healthy development of the root system ensures the supply of water, mineral salts, amino acids and hormones to the aerial parts, hence "deep roots, luxuriant leaves; firm base, flourishing branches". Pulling up seedlings inevitably damages the root system, and "helping seedlings grow by pulling them up" cannot make the shoot system grow faster. Conversely, once the nutrients in the seed are exhausted, growth of the root system depends on the organic nutrients, vitamins and hormones made in the leaves and carried in through the stem, and transpiration of the leaves is also one of the driving forces for water uptake by the roots; in cuttings even a single leaf left on speeds the formation of adventitious roots compared with a leafless cutting. A wide, deep girdle cut in the trunk severs the phloem, the roots, receiving no photosynthetic products from the leaves, gradually starve, and most trees die as a result.

The ratio of root growth to aerial growth is commonly expressed by the root/shoot ratio (R/S), the ratio of dry (or fresh) weight of the underground part to that of the aerial part. The root/shoot ratio varies with species, developmental stage and environment: when the soil is dry or nitrogen or phosphorus is deficient, organic nutrients are distributed preferentially to the root and the ratio rises; when water is ample, nitrogen fertilizer is abundant and light is weak, the aerial part grows vigorously and the ratio falls. In agriculture the ratio is regulated by controlling water and fertilizer: crops harvested for their underground parts, such as sweet potato, radish and beet, need a higher ratio late in growth; in vegetable production "squatting seedlings" means appropriately withholding water at the seedling stage to encourage roots to go deeper.

Terminal bud and axillary buds also restrict one another. Not all the buds on a branch of a plant open; generally only the terminal bud and a few axillary buds near it open, and most axillary buds remain dormant. When the terminal bud develops well, the main stem grows fast while the axillary buds are inhibited and cannot develop into branches or develop slowly; only when the terminal bud is removed (topping) or injured do the axillary buds beneath it become active and grow fairly quickly into new branches.

**Definition 24.6.1 (Apical Dominance)** The phenomenon in which the growth of a plant's terminal bud predominates and inhibits the growth of the axillary buds below it is **apical dominance**.

The strength of apical dominance varies with species, developmental stage and nutritional conditions: in sunflower, jute, reed and moso bamboo it is very strong and the aerial stem is generally unbranched or weakly branched; in pines and firs and other conifers it makes the crown pagoda-shaped; in rice and wheat at the tillering stage it is weaker and tillering can occur many times at the underground tillering nodes. In production apical dominance is kept or removed as needed: in fiber crops such as jute, moderately dense planting keeps apical dominance and suppresses branching to raise the yield and quality of the fiber; fruit trees and cotton need reasonable pruning and timely topping to remove apical dominance and promote many strong branches with good ventilation and light and more flowers and fruits; timber stands protect the terminal bud to obtain a straight main trunk; hedges become dense after clipping because lateral branches sprout. Apical dominance was first explained by auxin produced by the terminal bud inhibiting axillary buds (axillary buds being more sensitive to auxin than terminal buds); it is now known that cytokinin, strigolactone and sugar signals are also involved, and the mechanism belongs to plant physiology.

## 24.7. Vegetative Propagation

### 24.7.1. Types of Reproduction

When a plant has grown and developed to a certain stage, it produces new individuals from itself in a certain way to continue its offspring; this is reproduction. Reproduction increases the number of individuals and widens the range of the plant, and sexual reproduction also enriches the genetic variation of the offspring. Plant reproduction is of three types.

**Definition 24.7.1 (Vegetative Propagation)** The mode of reproduction in which a part of the vegetative body of the plant (root, stem, leaf) separates from the mother plant (sometimes not at once) and directly forms a new, independent individual is **vegetative propagation**.

The other two are asexual reproduction (in the narrow sense), in which asexual reproductive cells called **spores** separate from the mother plant and develop directly into new individuals, as in ferns and mosses ([7.12.15](/7.12.15), [7.12.17](/7.12.17)); and sexual reproduction, in which two gametes fuse into a zygote and the zygote develops into a new individual, which in angiosperms takes place in the flower ([7.12.27](/7.12.27)–[7.12.30](/7.12.30)). Asexual reproduction in the broad sense includes vegetative propagation. Vegetative propagation is possible because detached vegetative organs have the capacity for **regeneration**, producing adventitious roots and buds at the cut surface or the nodes; its offspring arise from somatic cells of the mother plant by mitosis, are genetically identical with the mother plant, and form a clone. Vegetative propagation maintains the good characters of the mother plant and is faster than propagation by seed; but offspring of a clone are genetically uniform, have the same resistance to disease, and pathogens such as viruses accumulate in the propagating material from generation to generation.

### 24.7.2. Natural Vegetative Propagation

Angiosperms propagate vegetatively in nature, producing new plants without human help, mostly through modified organs.

| Propagating organ | Mode | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Bulb | bulblets in the axils of scale leaves separate and grow into new plants; bulbils in aerial leaf axils or inflorescences | lily, daffodil, garlic, hyacinth; bulbils of tiger lily and garlic |
| Tuber | sprouting of the terminal bud and of axillary buds in the eyes | potato, Jerusalem artichoke, *Caladium* |
| Corm | sprouting of terminal and lateral buds, or formation of daughter corms | arrowhead, water chestnut, taro, gladiolus |
| Rhizome | buds at the nodes emerge above ground as aerial shoots, adventitious roots at the nodes | bamboo, lotus, ginger, cogongrass, field thistle |
| Root tuber | adventitious buds near the stem end, adventitious roots at the tail end | sweet potato, dahlia |
| Root sucker | adventitious buds on ordinary roots grow into sucker shoots (root-sprouting plants) | black locust, poplar, jujube, aspen |
| Stolon | adventitious roots form where a node touches the ground, the bud grows into a new plant and sends out new stolons | strawberry, Bermuda grass, strawberry saxifrage |
| Leaf | plantlets with adventitious roots and buds grow at the leaf margin | *Bryophyllum pinnatum*, mother of thousands (*Kalanchoe daigremontiana*) |

In some plants that have long propagated vegetatively, the sexual reproductive organs are reduced and little or no seed is set, as in many cultivars of garlic, banana and sugarcane. Vegetative propagation can overcome the drawbacks of seeds that germinate poorly, are short-lived, or require a long juvenile period; but not all bulb-bearing plants propagate by bulbs, onion generally being grown from seed. Bamboos such as moso bamboo can spread for decades by rhizomes alone without flowering, and often die in whole stands after flowering.

### 24.7.3. Artificial Vegetative Propagation

Making use of the capacity of plants for vegetative propagation, people in agriculture, forestry, horticulture and fruit growing take various measures to speed up propagation and to preserve or improve cultivars. The common methods are division, cuttings, layering and grafting. For plants that cannot produce seed or whose seed is ineffective (many cultivars of banana and fig), plants with very short-lived seed (*Salix*), plants that rarely flower in a lifetime (bamboos), and fruit trees and flowers whose seed propagation does not maintain the good characters of the parents (apple, pear, strawberry, rose), artificial vegetative propagation is often the main method.

Division: new plants grown from rhizomes, root suckers, stolons and so on are cut off from the mother plant and transplanted separately. What is transplanted is already a small rooted plant, so survival is very high. Apple, cherry, ginkgo and Chinese fir are often propagated by root suckers, and Chinese fir stands are regenerated by new shoots from adventitious buds on stumps or old roots; banana, chrysanthemum, daylily and hosta are also often propagated by division.

**Definition 24.7.2 (Cutting)** The method of cutting from a plant a length of branch bearing one or two buds, a piece of root or a leaf, and inserting it in moist soil or another well-drained medium so that it roots and sprouts and grows into a new plant, is a **cutting**.

At the cut surface of a cutting, **callus** forms first, and later adventitious roots arise in the callus or inside the stem (often from cells near the vascular cambium, the pericycle or the rays), while the existing buds or newly formed adventitious buds develop into the aerial part. Stem cuttings are usual; rose, pear, apple, fig and silk tree can be propagated by root cuttings; begonia, mother-in-law's tongue (*Sansevieria*) and African violet by leaf cuttings. The key to survival is whether adventitious roots form in time: willow, poplar, hydrangea and jasmine root easily; tung tree, oil-seed camellia, apple, Japanese apricot and plum root with difficulty, and the base of the cutting can first be treated with auxin-type substances such as indolebutyric acid (IBA) and naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) to promote rooting (the mechanism belongs to plant physiology). The survival rate is higher when the medium is moist, loose, aerated and well drained and the temperature moderate (about 18–25 °C); a cutting must be inserted in the same polarity as it had on the mother plant, and a physiologically mature branch is preferable. Cuttings of monocotyledons form callus with difficulty, and adventitious roots often form only after a bud in a basal leaf axil has grown into a new plant.

Layering: a branch on the mother plant is made to form adventitious roots without being detached from the mother, and is then cut off and planted, so the survival rate is very high, and the method is often used for species that root slowly. Generally in early spring a branch near the ground is chosen, half or all of the way around the bark is ringed or a cut is made at the part to be buried, and the branch is bent down into the soil, fixed with a wooden fork and covered with moist soil, leaving only the bud-bearing tip exposed; organic nutrients and hormones brought by the leaves accumulate at the girdled part, adventitious roots soon form, and the branch can be cut from the mother. Grape, *Rubus*, forsythia and tea are often layered in this way. For tall plants with stiff branches that cannot easily be bent to the ground, air layering is used: a ring of bark is stripped from a branch or a cut is made, a bamboo tube split in half, a clay pot or a plastic bag is placed around the girdled part and filled with moist soil or sphagnum, kept moist, and after rooting the branch is cut off below the girdle and transplanted, as in lily magnolia, white champaka, osmanthus and lychee.

**Definition 24.7.3 (Grafting)** The method of transferring a branch or bud of one plant onto another rooted plant so that the two unite and grow together into a new plant is **grafting**. The plant that keeps its root system and receives the graft is the rootstock, and the branch or bud attached is the scion.

In grafting, the **vascular cambium** of the two cut surfaces must be aligned and pressed together; each multiplies cells, forming callus that interweaves, and new cambium differentiates in the callus, joining the cambia of rootstock and scion into a ring, which then produces new xylem and phloem that connect the vascular tissue of the two into one whole. Therefore only dicotyledons and gymnosperms, which have a cambium, are easily grafted; monocotyledons have no vascular cambium and are very hard to graft (successful grafts in grasses using the tillering node or the embryo have been reported).

Whether a graft survives depends on the compatibility of rootstock and scion: generally, the more closely related the two and the more similar their type of metabolism, the more readily they unite and the higher the survival rate; grafting between cultivars is easiest, between species harder, and between genera and families harder still. This is not absolute, however; some combinations of distant relatives sometimes survive, such as trifoliate orange and citrus, privet and osmanthus, quince and pear. Some combinations at first unite but only after several years show growth decline or breaking of the union, called delayed incompatibility. In addition, woody plants are easier to graft than herbaceous plants and vines.

The new grafted plant has the root system of the rootstock and the branches of the scion, and the two can influence each other as they grow. The rootstock is usually chosen from cold-hardy, drought-tolerant, disease-resistant, soil-tolerant or vigorous species, or a dwarfing rootstock is used to dwarf the fruit tree and bring it into bearing earlier (the M-series dwarfing rootstocks of apple); grafting can bring fruit trees into bearing earlier, increase yield and improve quality, preserve good cultivars that seed propagation cannot maintain (apple, pear, citrus, rose), allow seedless cultivars to be propagated, and put several cultivars on one tree. Cucurbit crops are often grafted onto pumpkin or bottle-gourd rootstocks to control Fusarium wilt. At the graft union a shoot containing cells of both genotypes sometimes arises from a mixed zone of rootstock and scion cells; this is a graft **chimera**.

There are three main grafting methods.

- Approach grafting: branches of two plants are brought together, a slice is cut from the facing side of each, the cut surfaces are pressed together, bound and waxed, and after the union has taken, the scion branch is cut from its original plant and the top of the rootstock is cut off; it is used for species that do not readily take.
- Scion grafting: a length of branch with buds serves as the scion. When scion and rootstock are equally thick, tongue grafting or saddle grafting can be used so that the joint interlocks; when the scion is thinner than the rootstock, cleft grafting or side grafting is used: the rootstock is cut off, a slit is cut or split lengthwise in the cut face, and a scion trimmed to a wedge is inserted so that the cambia of the two line up on one side, and bound tightly.
- Budding: a single bud serves as the scion. A T-shaped cut is made in the stem of the rootstock, deep to the xylem, and the bark is lifted; an axillary bud is cut from the scion branch with a small piece of bark and a thin layer of xylem and trimmed into a shield shape, the leaf blade below the bud is cut off leaving a short piece of petiole, and the shield-shaped bud is inserted under the bark of the rootstock so that the bud lies at the center of the cut, and tied tightly. Budding saves scion material, is quick, and leaves a small union; it is the commonest method for raising fruit trees and roses.

### 24.7.4. Tissue Culture and Micropropagation

The extreme form of vegetative propagation is the regeneration of a complete plant from a small piece of tissue or even a single cell. The **totipotency** of plant cells ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)) was first conceived by Haberlandt (1902); Skoog and Miller (1957) found that the ratio of auxin to cytokinin in the medium determines the direction of differentiation of callus: a high proportion of cytokinin gives shoots, a high proportion of auxin gives roots, and when the two are comparable the callus continues to proliferate; Steward and colleagues (1958) obtained somatic embryos and complete plants from cultured cells of carrot root phloem; and the MS medium of Murashige and Skoog (1962) is still the most widely used basal medium.

**Definition 24.7.4 (Plant Tissue Culture)** The technique of inoculating organs, tissues or cells of a plant (explants) on an artificial medium under sterile conditions so that they proliferate, differentiate and regenerate into complete plants is **plant tissue culture**; the use of tissue culture to produce clonal plantlets rapidly in large numbers is **micropropagation**.

The general course of micropropagation is as follows: explants such as shoot tips, axillary buds and leaves are sterilized and inoculated; the explant directly produces adventitious or clustered shoots, or first forms callus and then differentiates shoots; the shoots are divided and multiplied repeatedly on multiplication medium; finally they are transferred to rooting medium to root, hardened off and transplanted. Tens of thousands of plants can be propagated from one explant in a year, and orchids, banana, potato, strawberry, sugarcane, many ornamentals and forest trees (eucalyptus) are produced as seedlings in this way. Because the shoot apical meristem is often free of viruses (the meristem has no vascular tissue and its cells divide rapidly), culturing a very small shoot tip (0.1–0.5 mm) can give virus-free plantlets (Morel and Martin, 1952, dahlia), and virus-free seed stock of potato, strawberry and sweet potato is an important means of raising yield. Tissue culture is also the basis of plant genetic transformation, haploid breeding (anther culture) and germplasm preservation; its hormonal control and cellular mechanisms belong to plant physiology and plant development.

## Summary

Key points:

- Metamorphosis (Definition 24.1.1) is a stably inherited change in form and structure caused by a change of function; origin is judged by position, connections, vascular tissue and development, not by outward form. Homologous organs have the same origin and different functions; analogous organs (Definition 24.1.2) have different origins and similar function and form (convergence).
- Root modifications: fleshy taproots and root tubers (Definition 24.2.1); carrot is mainly secondary phloem, radish mainly secondary xylem with supernumerary cambia, beet has several rings of supernumerary cambium forming concentric rings, and sweet potato produces many supernumerary cambia around vessel groups; aerial roots (prop roots, climbing roots, pneumatophores, the velamen of orchids); parasitic roots, i.e., haustoria (holoparasitic dodder, hemiparasitic mistletoe).
- Stem modifications: aerial: thorns, stem tendrils, cladodes (no bud in the axil), fleshy stems, bulblets (bulbils), tubercles, stolons; underground: rhizome (Definition 24.3.1), tuber (Definition 24.3.2), bulb (Definition 24.3.3), corm (Definition 24.3.4); recognizing a stem: nodes, internodes, scale leaves, axillary buds, leaf scars.
- Leaf modifications: bracts and involucres, scale leaves (bud scales, fleshy and membranous scale leaves), phyllodes (Definition 24.4.1), leaf tendrils (Definition 24.4.2), insect-trapping leaves (pitcher, disc, snap trap, bladder), leaf spines and stipular spines (Definition 24.4.3).
- Vascular connections: leaf trace and leaf gap, branch trace and branch gap; the transition region (Definition 24.5.1) lies in the hypocotyl, where xylem exarch and alternating with phloem changes to endarch and arranged inside the phloem.
- Interactions: root/shoot ratio (rises under drought and nitrogen deficiency); apical dominance (Definition 24.6.1), topping and pruning.
- Vegetative propagation (Definition 24.7.1): natural (bulbs, tubers, corms, rhizomes, root tubers, root suckers, stolons, leaves) and artificial (division, cuttings (Definition 24.7.2), layering, grafting (Definition 24.7.3)); grafting depends on cambium alignment and compatibility; tissue culture and micropropagation (Definition 24.7.4), shoot-tip virus elimination.

## Questions

1. How can we show from morphology that the "tuber" of the potato is a stem whereas the "tuber" of the sweet potato is a root?

   **Answer** The potato tuber bears eyes arranged in a spiral; each eye is a node, with reduced scale leaves when young, the depression being the leaf axil, each eye contains buds, and the tip of the tuber bears a terminal bud; the tuber is borne at the tip of an underground stolon, and its internal structure is that of a stem (periderm, cortex, external phloem, cambium, xylem, internal phloem and pith; it has a pith and bicollateral bundles). The sweet potato root tuber has no nodes or eyes on its surface and bears no scale leaves; its buds are adventitious buds arising near the stem end without a fixed arrangement; it develops by swelling of an adventitious or lateral root, in cross section the center shows the radial primary xylem of a root (polyarch), there is no pith, and the enlargement is due to supernumerary cambia around vessel groups; fibrous roots are also found at the tail end of the tuber.

2. What organs are the "head" of onion, the garlic head and the water chestnut? Which part do we eat?

   **Answer** The onion "head" is a tunicate bulb, consisting of an extremely short basal plate and scale leaves enclosing it layer upon layer; the outer dry membranous scale leaves protect, and what is mainly eaten is the inner fleshy scale leaves, the leaf bases. The garlic head is also a bulb, but at maturity the original scale leaves dry up into membranes, and the "cloves" that are eaten are swollen axillary buds in the axils of the scale leaves, each clove itself being a small bulb (with one protective scale leaf outside and one thick storage scale leaf and the bud inside). The water chestnut is a corm, formed by swelling at the tip of an underground stolon, and what is eaten is the thick stem itself; the ring marks on the surface are nodes, the brown membranous structures on the nodes are scale leaves, and the "beak" at the top is the terminal and lateral buds.

3. Hawthorn, barberry, black locust and rose all have spines. How can we judge their origins? Why are three of them homologous organs yet analogous to the fourth?

   **Answer** Look at position and vascular tissue. The hawthorn spine is borne in a leaf axil (the position of an axillary bud), sometimes bears leaves, and is connected to the stem by vascular tissue; it is a thorn (a modified stem). The barberry spine is borne on a long shoot at the position of a leaf, with a bud in the axil of the spine that develops into a short shoot; it is a leaf spine. The black locust spines occur as a pair at the stipule position on both sides of the petiole base; they are stipular spines. The rose prickles have no fixed position and are scattered on the stem, their bases attached only to epidermis and cortex without vascular tissue, and they are easily detached; they are prickles. The barberry leaf spine and the black locust stipular spine both come from leaves (the stipule is part of the leaf), so they are homologous with the leaf of barberry, the pinnate leaf of black locust and other leaf modifications; the hawthorn thorn comes from a stem and the rose prickle from the epidermis, so they differ in origin from the leaf spines but resemble them in form and defensive function, and are analogous to each other and to them, a result of convergent evolution.

4. The primary xylem is exarch in the root and endarch in the stem. Where and how are the two connected? Why can this connection be observed only in seedlings?

   **Answer** They are connected in the transition region, which usually lies at the base, middle or upper part of the hypocotyl, from less than 1 mm to a few millimeters long, ending at the cotyledonary node. Taking a diarch root as an example, from below upward the vascular cylinder widens, each primary xylem strand seems to split lengthwise into two forks, the two halves turn toward the two sides and rotate nearly 180°, so that the protoxylem moves from the outside to the inside; the phloem also splits and shifts, and finally both are arranged inside and outside on the same radius, forming the endarch vascular bundles of the stem, with a pith appearing in the center. This "rotation" is in fact due to the different kinds and arrangement of tissues differentiated from the procambium at different heights. The transition region is a way of connecting primary structures; later secondary growth of the hypocotyl produces large amounts of secondary xylem and phloem that compress and conceal the primary structure, so it can be seen clearly only in the primary structure of seedlings.

5. Why must the cambia of rootstock and scion be aligned in grafting? Why is apple easy to graft and wheat very hard? Why can apple grown from seed not maintain the cultivar's characters, while grafting can?

   **Answer** Union in grafting depends on the cambia of the two cut surfaces and the cells near them proliferating callus, in which a new cambium differentiates, joining the cambia of rootstock and scion into a ring and then producing new xylem and phloem that connect the conducting systems of the two; if the cambia are not aligned, no continuous cambium and vascular tissue can form, the scion receives no water and the root receives no photosynthetic products from the scion. Apple is a woody dicotyledon with an active vascular cambium and readily forms continuous union tissue; wheat is a monocotyledon with scattered, closed vascular bundles and no vascular cambium, and can hardly produce new vascular tissue connecting the two parts. Apple is self-incompatible and cultivars are highly heterozygous; seeds result from cross-pollination, and genetic recombination makes the seedling offspring segregate and fail to retain the characters of the parent cultivar; the scion of a grafted plant consists of somatic cells of the cultivar's mother plant, so the crown that grows is genetically identical with the mother plant and the characters of the cultivar are kept, the rootstock influencing only tree vigor, resistance and the earliness of fruiting.
