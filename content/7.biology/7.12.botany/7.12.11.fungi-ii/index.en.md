The Ascomycota and Basidiomycota together account for more than 95% of described fungal species. They form the largest and evolutionarily youngest branch of the kingdom Fungi, the subkingdom Dikarya. Their hyphae have regular septa, and in sexual reproduction both groups pass through a **dikaryon** stage between plasmogamy and karyogamy; the sexual spores are formed inside asci and on basidia, respectively. Almost all large fungal fruiting bodies, from morels and truffles to mushrooms, bracket fungi (*Ganoderma*) and puffballs, belong to these two phyla. They also include the most important plant pathogens (rice blast, rusts, smuts, powdery mildews, Fusarium head blight), the main sources of mycotoxins and antibiotics, and the principal fungal partners in mycorrhizae and lichens.

## 11.1. Ascomycota

### 11.1.1. General Characteristics

The **Ascomycota** ([Definition 21.5.3](/7.11.21#def-21.5.3)) is the phylum with the most species, more than 60,000 described (counts range from 60,000 to 90,000), with complex structure and reproduction. Chinese textbooks summarize four main features:

1. Vegetative body: apart from the unicellular yeasts, most species have a mycelium of septate hyphae. Each cell usually contains one nucleus, sometimes several. The septal pores are simple and flanked by Woronin bodies ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.2.1)).
2. Asexual reproduction: unicellular species reproduce by budding; multicellular species mainly produce **conidia**. Conidia are formed externally by conidiogenous cells on conidiophores, which is the main difference from the Mucoromycota, whose spores form inside sporangia.
3. Sexual reproduction: an **ascus** is formed; the zygote undergoes meiosis inside it and produces a fixed number of **ascospores**, usually eight (sometimes 4, 16 or more).
4. Neither zoospores nor motile gametes are produced, so sexual reproduction is completely independent of water, a terrestrial trait. In unicellular species the asci are naked and no fruiting body is formed; in multicellular species the asci are enclosed in a fruiting body, called an **ascocarp**.

The life cycle of ascomycetes is dominated by a haploid mycelium (Figure 1). Ascospores germinate into haploid mycelia, which multiply by conidia for many generations during a growing season. In sexual reproduction an antheridium and an ascogonium (the female organ) are formed; after plasmogamy, dikaryotic ascogenous hyphae develop, asci form at their tips, and karyogamy and meiosis take place in the ascus. The diploid phase is only the zygote nucleus in the ascus, and the dikaryotic phase is confined to the ascogenous hyphae, so the life cycle is haplontic with a brief dikaryotic stage. Some ascomycetes are homothallic, others heterothallic (two mating types, *MAT1-1* and *MAT1-2*).

### 11.1.2. Formation of Asci and Ascospores

The formation of asci, ascospores and ascocarps is usually described using the fire fungus *Pyronema omphalodes* (Chinese textbooks use *P. confluens*). It is common on burned soil; its mycelium is white and cottony, and dense, stalkless apothecia are formed on top of it. They are small, brick-red to orange, 1–3 mm in diameter.

1. Sex organs: short, erect, forked hyphal branches arise on the mycelium. One multinucleate terminal cell develops into the antheridium (club-shaped, with more than 100 nuclei) and another into the ascogonium (spherical, whose nuclei also divide repeatedly to more than 100). The ascogonium bears at its tip a curved tube, the trichogyne, which has a basal septum and grows toward the antheridium.
2. Plasmogamy: when the trichogyne touches the antheridium, the wall at the contact point dissolves, as does the basal septum of the trichogyne. Most of the cytoplasm and nuclei of the antheridium flow through the trichogyne into the ascogonium (plasmogamy), but male and female nuclei do not fuse; they pair up.
3. Ascogenous hyphae: stimulated by plasmogamy, the upper part of the ascogonium produces many tubular ascogenous hyphae, into which the paired female and male nuclei move. The hyphae form septa and divide into cells, each containing one pair of nuclei.
4. Crozier formation and development of the ascus: the dikaryotic cell at the tip of an ascogenous hypha elongates and bends into a hook, the two nuclei divide simultaneously into four, and two septa divide the hook into three cells.

**Definition 11.1.1 (Crozier)** The process in which the dikaryotic terminal cell of an ascogenous hypha bends into a hook, both nuclei divide simultaneously, and septa then divide the hook into three cells is called crozier formation, and the hook is a **crozier** (or ascogenous hypha hook). The tip cell at the end of the hook and the stalk cell at its base each contain one nucleus; the middle, curved cell, the crozier head, contains two nuclei of different origin and is the ascus mother cell.

5. The two nuclei in the ascus mother cell undergo karyogamy to form a diploid zygote nucleus. The zygote nucleus divides by meiosis into four haploid nuclei, and one mitosis then gives eight. Each nucleus, together with surrounding cytoplasm, is enclosed in a membrane (free cell formation) and develops into one of eight ascospores. The ascus mother cell meanwhile elongates into a club-shaped ascus, in which the spores lie in a row.
6. At the same time the tip cell and the stalk cell fuse, the nucleus of the tip cell passes into the stalk cell, and a new dikaryotic cell results, which can again form a crozier head, tip cell and stalk cell. Repeating this many times, one ascogenous hypha can form many asci.

A single plasmogamy can therefore give rise to many asci: the crozier ensures that every ascus mother cell contains exactly one pair of nuclei from the two parents. It resembles the clamp connection of basidiomycetes (Section 11.2.1) and is thought to be homologous to it.

As the contents of the antheridium flow into the ascogonium, sterile hyphae beneath the ascogonium grow immediately and enclose the ascogonium and ascogenous hyphae, forming the wall of the ascocarp. Sterile hyphae also develop between the asci as paraphyses.

**Definition 11.1.2 (Hymenium)** The fertile layer of an ascocarp or basidiocarp, in which asci (or basidia) are arranged side by side with sterile cells such as paraphyses, is called the hymenium.

The structure and manner of dehiscence of the ascus is an important taxonomic character:

| Type | Structure and spore release | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Prototunicate | Ascus wall thin, dissolving at maturity; spores released passively | Asci in cleistothecia (*Penicillium*, *Aspergillus*) |
| Unitunicate, operculate | A lid at the ascus tip opens at maturity; spores are shot out by turgor | Pezizales (morels, cup fungi) |
| Unitunicate, inoperculate | The ascus tip bears a canal-like apical apparatus through which spores are discharged | Sordariomycetes (*Neurospora*, ergot), Leotiomycetes |
| Bitunicate | Ascus wall has two layers; after the outer layer ruptures the inner layer stretches and discharges the spores like a jack-in-the-box | Dothideomycetes (apple scab fungus) |

In many ascomycetes the ascus takes up water and swells at maturity, and the turgor pressure shoots the spores several millimeters to centimeters. When thousands of asci in an apothecium discharge at once, a puff of "smoke" can be seen and a faint hissing heard.

### 11.1.3. Ascocarps

**Definition 11.1.3 (Ascocarp)** The fruiting body of an ascomycete, a structure in which sterile hyphae enclose asci and paraphyses and in which ascospores are produced, is called an **ascocarp**. By form and manner of opening, ascocarps are divided into the completely closed, spherical *cleistothecium*; the flask-shaped *perithecium* with an apical pore; the disc- or cup-shaped *apothecium* whose hymenium is fully exposed; and the ascostroma, in which locules are dissolved out of stromatic tissue and asci grow inside them (also called a pseudothecium).

The form of the ascocarp is an important basis of ascomycete classification (Figure 2):

| Type | Form | Asci | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cleistothecium | Spherical, without a pore; spores are released when the wall breaks or decays | Scattered in the cavity, prototunicate | Eurotiales (sexual stages of *Penicillium* and *Aspergillus*); powdery mildews (asci arranged in one layer, with appendages; now called a chasmothecium) |
| Perithecium | Flask-shaped, with a neck and pore; periphyses in the neck | Unitunicate, arranged as a hymenium; spores shot out of the pore | Sordariomycetes (*Neurospora*, ergot, *Fusarium*, *Cordyceps*, rice blast fungus) |
| Apothecium | Disc-, cup- or bowl-shaped, hymenium exposed, stalked or sessile | Unitunicate, operculate or inoperculate | Pezizomycetes (*Pyronema*, cup fungi, morels), Leotiomycetes (*Sclerotinia*), Lecanoromycetes (most lichens) |
| Ascostroma (pseudothecium) | A stroma is formed first, and cavities dissolved in it contain asci that develop directly, with no separate ascocarp wall | Bitunicate, with pseudoparaphyses mixed in | Dothideomycetes (apple scab fungus *Venturia inaequalis*) |

Among perithecial and ascostromatic types, many ascocarps are borne on or embedded in a **stroma** ([7.12.10](/7.12.10#sec-10.4.2)). The ergot stroma is stalked with a spherical head, and the perithecia are embedded in the surface layer of the head; the stroma of *Cordyceps* is club-shaped; that of *Xylaria* is black and carbonaceous. Chinese textbooks recognize three types of ascocarp, the cleistothecium, perithecium and apothecium; the ascostroma is a fourth type distinguished by Luttrell and others on the basis of ascocarp development (ascolocular versus ascohymenial development). The Loculoascomycetes, characterized by it, correspond roughly to the present-day Dothideomycetes.

![The four types of ascocarp (schematic longitudinal sections). A: apothecium: disc-shaped, with a hymenium of asci and paraphyses exposed on the upper surface and the excipulum beneath. B: perithecium: flask-shaped, with a neck and pore; asci line the base of the fruiting body and spores are shot out of the pore. C: cleistothecium: spherical and closed, with asci scattered in the cavity and appendages outside (powdery mildew). D: ascostroma (pseudothecium): bitunicate asci grow in a cavity dissolved out of stromatic tissue, mixed with pseudoparaphyses.](./plots/ascocarp-types.py)

### 11.1.4. Classification of Ascomycetes

The Ascomycota is divided into three subphyla ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.5.2)):

- Taphrinomycotina: *Taphrina*, the fission yeast (*Schizosaccharomyces*), *Pneumocystis* and others. This is the earliest-diverging branch of the Ascomycota; no ascocarps are formed, or only very simple ones.
- Saccharomycotina: budding yeasts such as baker's yeast (*Saccharomyces*), *Candida* and *Komagataella*; the asci are naked.
- Pezizomycotina: filamentous ascomycetes, almost all forming ascocarps, comprising the great majority of ascomycete species. The main classes are Pezizomycetes, Leotiomycetes, Sordariomycetes, Dothideomycetes, Eurotiomycetes, Lecanoromycetes (most lichens, [7.12.12](/7.12.12)) and others.

Chinese textbooks follow Ainsworth (1973), who recognized six classes in the Ascomycotina. They correspond to the present system roughly as follows:

| Class of Ainsworth (1973) | Ascocarp | Position in the present system | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hemiascomycetes | None; asci naked | Saccharomycotina; Taphrinomycotina | Yeasts, *Taphrina* |
| Plectomycetes | Cleistothecium | Eurotiomycetes; Erysiphales moved to Leotiomycetes | *Penicillium*, *Aspergillus*, powdery mildews |
| Pyrenomycetes | Perithecium | Mainly Sordariomycetes | *Neurospora*, ergot, *Gibberella*, *Cordyceps* |
| Loculoascomycetes | Ascostroma | Mainly Dothideomycetes | Scab fungus |
| Discomycetes | Apothecium | Several classes: Pezizomycetes, Leotiomycetes, Lecanoromycetes and others | Cup fungi, morels, *Sclerotinia*, most lichens |
| Laboulbeniomycetes | Perithecium, on the surface of insects | Laboulbeniomycetes | *Laboulbenia* |

Classes defined by ascocarp type are thus broadly valid, but apothecia and cleistothecia evolved independently in different lineages, and neither the old Discomycetes nor the Plectomycetes is monophyletic.

### 11.1.5. Representative Genera

Ascomycetes are extremely numerous; the following are the genera most closely related to humans and most common (arranged by the present subphyla and classes).

*Taphrina* (Taphrinomycotina): parasites of plants that form no ascocarp; the asci form a hymenium under the host epidermis and break through the cuticle to become exposed. *T. deformans* causes peach leaf curl: in spring young leaves thicken, wrinkle and turn red, and a gray-white layer of asci appears on the surface. It has two phases: inside the host a dikaryotic mycelium (unusual among ascomycetes), outside a haploid, yeast-like phase that reproduces by budding.

*Saccharomyces* (Saccharomycotina): Chinese textbooks treat it as the most primitive genus of ascomycetes. It is unicellular and reproduces by budding; before buds detach they often form a temporary pseudohypha. In sexual reproduction two vegetative cells (or two ascospores) fuse, and the diploid cell becomes an ascus directly, producing four ascospores after meiosis. Baker's yeast (*S. cerevisiae*) breaks down monosaccharides to carbon dioxide and ethanol under anaerobic conditions (**alcoholic fermentation**) and is used in brewing, baking and industry. Its cell, life cycle, mating types and significance as a model organism are in [7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.3) and are not repeated here.

*Neurospora* (Sordariomycetes): *N. crassa*, the red bread mold, forms orange-red masses of conidia on bread and corncobs; it is heterothallic, with mating types A and a. The perithecia are black and pear-shaped; the asci are long and narrow, and the eight ascospores lie in a row in the order set by the spindle orientations of meiosis and the following mitosis, so the position of crossovers can be read directly from the arrangement of the spores (ordered tetrads, [7.4.9](/7.4.9)). Beadle and Tatum (1941) used its auxotrophic mutants to propose the one gene–one enzyme hypothesis.

*Penicillium* and *Aspergillus* (Eurotiomycetes, Eurotiales): the most familiar molds, extremely widespread; they often grow at wounds of fruit such as tomatoes and cause rot, and are also found on starchy foods, brewing materials, leather, clothing and textiles.

- *Penicillium*: the mycelium is pale green and reproduces mainly by conidia. The tip of the conidiophore branches several times into a brush-like penicillus; the terminal cells are phialides, from which chains of green conidia arise ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.4.1)). *P. citrinum* occurs mostly on citrus, pears and apples, and *P. expansum* causes blue mold of apples and produces patulin; *P. roqueforti* and *P. camemberti* are used for cheese. Penicillin was first discovered by Fleming (1928) in a contaminated *Penicillium* culture and is produced industrially with *P. chrysogenum*; Fleming's strain was later identified as *P. rubens*.
- *Aspergillus*: the conidiophore is erect and aseptate, and its tip swells into a spherical vesicle covered with phialides (in some species with a layer of metulae beneath), from which chains of conidia arise. *A. oryzae* is used for koji, soy sauce, soybean paste, Chinese rice wine and sake; *A. niger* is used to produce citric acid and enzyme preparations; *A. flavus* and *A. parasiticus* produce the strongly carcinogenic aflatoxins, and *A. fumigatus* causes human aspergillosis (**mycotoxins** and mycoses in [7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.7)).

Both form cleistothecia in their sexual stages, but many species rarely or never show sexual reproduction and were once placed among the Deuteromycetes; a sexual cycle has since been induced in *A. fumigatus* (O'Gorman et al. 2009) and *P. chrysogenum* (Böhm et al. 2013). The Eurotiomycetes also include *Monascus purpureus*, used to make red yeast rice, which produces monacolin K, that is, lovastatin.

Powdery mildews (Erysiphales, Leotiomycetes): almost all are obligate parasites of higher plants. The hyphae grow on the host surface and only haustoria enter the epidermal cells. In the asexual phase chains of conidia form on the leaf surface and make a white powdery layer, hence "powdery mildew." Late in the season black cleistothecia (chasmothecia) are formed, containing one to several asci with 2–8 ascospores each. The appendages on the outside (filamentous, hooked, forked and so on) are the basis for distinguishing genera: *Erysiphe* has filamentous appendages, *Uncinula* hooked tips, *Microsphaera* dichotomously branched ones. *Blumeria graminis* (Chinese textbooks use *Erysiphe graminis*) infects the leaves, sheaths, stems and ears of wheat and barley and is an important disease of cereals.

Ergot (*Claviceps purpureus*, Sordariomycetes, Hypocreales): it parasitizes the ovaries of rye, barley, wheat, oats and many grasses; the **sclerotia** it produces are what is called ergot in medicine.

1. In spring the ascospores (filiform, one-celled) are carried by wind to open host flowers and germinate with a germ tube that enters the ovary. A white, cottony mycelium develops, destroys the ovary tissue and spreads outside it, forming pairs of short conidiophores that bear ovoid conidia at their tips.
2. The mass of conidia produces a sweet secretion (the "honeydew stage") that attracts insects, which carry the spores to other ears and repeat the infection.
3. After spore production ends, the mycelium in the ovary turns into a hard, black-violet sclerotium, nearly cylindrical, with horn-shaped ends, 1–2 cm long and white inside, which replaces the grain in the ear.
4. The sclerotium falls to the ground and overwinters; the following spring it germinates into 10–20 stalked stromata whose heads are spherical, 1–2 mm in diameter and purplish red. The perithecia are embedded in the surface layer of the head with the pores protruding; each perithecium contains several long cylindrical asci with eight filiform ascospores each. The ascospores are discharged just as the grasses flower.

Ergot contains many **alkaloids**, collectively called ergot alkaloids (ergotamine, ergometrine and others, all derivatives of lysergic acid). Eating flour contaminated with ergot causes ergotism: vasoconstriction leading to gangrene of the limbs, or convulsions and hallucinations, called "St. Anthony's fire" in medieval Europe; livestock can also be poisoned and die. Ergot is also an important medicinal material: ergometrine contracts the uterus and is used against postpartum hemorrhage, ergotamine is used for migraine, and in 1938 Albert Hofmann synthesized LSD from lysergic acid.

*Fusarium* (Hypocreales): the wheat scab fungus *Gibberella zeae* of Chinese textbooks (also *G. saubinetii*) is now called by the name of its anamorph, *Fusarium graminearum*, under the one-fungus-one-name rule (Section 11.3). It attacks the ears of wheat, barley and other grasses and causes Fusarium head blight: water-soaked brown spots first appear at the base of the glumes and spread over the whole ear; late in the disease a pink mold layer (conidia) forms at the junctions of the glumes, and at ripening small blue-black grains (perithecia) appear. The macroconidia are sickle-shaped with 3–5 septa; the ascospores are spindle-shaped with three septa. Diseased grain contains mycotoxins such as deoxynivalenol (DON, vomitoxin), which poison humans and livestock; this is the greatest harm of the disease, so wheat from affected fields must never be eaten or used as feed. Control relies mainly on resistant cultivars, crop rotation and fungicide sprays at flowering. *Gibberella fujikuroi* (now *Fusarium fujikuroi*) causes bakanae ("foolish seedling") disease of rice, in which the plants grow abnormally tall; Eiichi Kurosawa (1926) found a growth-promoting substance in its culture fluid, later identified as the plant hormone gibberellin, which is named after this genus.

*Cordyceps* and relatives (Hypocreales): the stroma arises from the body of an insect and is fleshy, mostly club-shaped and erect. The Tibetan caterpillar fungus (*Ophiocordyceps sinensis*, *Cordyceps sinensis* of Chinese textbooks) parasitizes the larvae of ghost moths (Hepialidae, *Thitarodes* and others) in meadow soil at 3000–5000 m on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and surrounding areas. The hyphae fill the larval body, which dies stiff as a sclerotium-like "caterpillar"; the next summer a single club-shaped stroma, the "grass," grows from the head of the larva. Its upper part is swollen with embedded perithecia, and the ascospores are filiform and multiseptate. Molecular phylogeny (Sung et al. 2007) shows that *Cordyceps* in the old sense is polyphyletic; the Tibetan caterpillar fungus was moved to *Ophiocordyceps* (Ophiocordycipitaceae), which is in a different family from *Cordyceps militaris* (Cordycipitaceae), a parasite of lepidopteran pupae with an orange-yellow stroma, cultivable artificially and containing cordycepin. The Tibetan caterpillar fungus is a prized Chinese medicine, and overharvesting has sharply reduced its resources; in 2021 it was listed in China's National Key Protected Wild Plants list (Class II). These fungi also manipulate host behavior: ants infected with *O. unilateralis* climb to the underside of a leaf and bite the leaf vein before dying ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.6.3)).

*Morchella* and *Peziza* (Pezizomycetes, Pezizales): most Pezizales are saprotrophs with operculate asci, and the ascocarps are medium to large apothecia, most common in forests. The apothecium of *Peziza* is cup-shaped, sessile or nearly so, with cylindrical asci each containing eight elliptical, colorless ascospores in a row. The fruiting body of a morel consists of a cap and a stalk: the cap is nearly spherical or conical, its surface ridged in a network with the hymenium lining the pits, which makes it equivalent to a large, everted apothecium compounded of many small ones; the stalk is hollow. The common morel (*M. esculenta*), *M. importuna* and others are prized edible fungi, and since the 2010s China has cultivated them on a large scale.

*Tuber* (Pezizales): the ascocarp is formed underground and is a closed, modified apothecium with labyrinthine veins inside; the asci cannot discharge spores actively. At maturity it gives off a strong odor (containing volatile sulfur compounds and steroids) that attracts wild boars, squirrels and other animals, which dig it up and disperse the spores, which is why trained pigs or dogs are used to find it. Truffles form **ectomycorrhizae** with oak, hazel, pine and other trees. The Périgord black truffle (*T. melanosporum*) and the Italian white truffle (*T. magnatum*) are among the most expensive foods in the world; southwestern China has several species such as *T. indicum*.

In addition, among the Leotiomycetes *Sclerotinia sclerotiorum* forms black sclerotia in rapeseed stems (Sclerotinia stem rot of rape), and the sclerotia germinate into stalked apothecia; *Botrytis cinerea* causes gray mold of many fruits and flowers; and among the Dothideomycetes the apple scab fungus (*Venturia inaequalis*) overwinters as ascostromata in fallen leaves.

## 11.2. Basidiomycota

### 11.2.1. General Characteristics

The **Basidiomycota** ([Definition 21.5.4](/7.11.21#def-21.5.4)) is a diverse group of higher fungi, with about 30,000–50,000 species described, all terrestrial; most are obligate parasites or saprotrophs of plants, and many are edible, medicinal or poisonous, so they are closely related to human life. All are multicellular, with septate hyphae; the mushroom-forming species have dolipore septa. There are three kinds of mycelium:

- Primary mycelium: it arises from a uninucleate, haploid basidiospore. At first it is multinucleate, but septa soon form and divide it into many uninucleate cells. It is short-lived in the life cycle.
- Secondary mycelium: when two compatible primary hyphae meet they undergo somatic fusion, with plasmogamy but no karyogamy; the nuclei of one hypha migrate into the cells of the other, and each cell keeps two nuclei ($n + n$), forming a dikaryotic mycelium. It lives a long time in the life cycle, is the main vegetative body of basidiomycetes, and some live for decades or longer.
- Tertiary mycelium: under suitable conditions the secondary mycelium becomes organized and forms the basidiocarp; the hyphae that make up the basidiocarp are also dikaryotic. In the basidiocarps of polypores the tertiary hyphae further differentiate into generative hyphae (which can give rise to other hyphae), thick-walled unbranched skeletal hyphae and richly branched binding hyphae. Fruiting bodies with only generative hyphae are called monomitic, and those with two or three kinds dimitic or trimitic; this is an important character in polypore classification.

Cell division in dikaryotic hyphae is often accompanied by a **clamp connection** (Figure 3). A backward-curving, beak-like outgrowth forms between the two nuclei in the middle of the cell, and one of the two nuclei moves into it. Both nuclei divide simultaneously into four. After division one daughter nucleus stays in the outgrowth, one pair of daughter nuclei of different origin moves to the front of the cell, and the last stays at the back. Septa then divide the cell into an upper dikaryotic cell, a lower uninucleate cell and the uninucleate beak; the tip of the beak fuses with the lower cell and its nucleus flows into it, so the lower cell is again dikaryotic. In this way one dikaryotic cell divides into two dikaryotic cells, leaving a beak-like scar between them, the clamp connection. It resembles the crozier of ascomycetes, and in both the function is to ensure that each cell after division contains two nuclei of different origin. Clamp connections are characteristic of basidiomycetes (especially Agaricomycotina), but not all basidiomycetes have them.

**Definition 11.2.1 (Basidium and Basidiocarp)** The cell of a basidiomycete in which karyogamy and meiosis take place and which bears basidiospores on small stalks (sterigmata) on its outer surface is called a **basidium**. The fruiting body of a basidiomycete, a structure built of tertiary mycelium in which basidia and basidiospores are produced in a hymenium, is called a **basidiocarp**.

Development of the basidium: the terminal cell of a dikaryotic hypha swells into a club-shaped young basidium; the two nuclei fuse (karyogamy) to form a diploid nucleus, which immediately undergoes meiosis to give four haploid nuclei. Four sterigmata arise at the top of the basidium, their tips swell, and the four nuclei move into them, forming four uninucleate **basidiospores**, two of the (+) and two of the (−) type (in bipolar species). Basidiospores are formed externally, which is the fundamental difference from ascospores, formed inside the ascus. A mature basidiospore is shot off by the momentum of the sudden fusion of a Buller's drop, rapidly formed near the hilar appendix, with the water film on the spore surface; the range is about 0.1 mm, just enough to take the spore out of the hymenium without striking the opposite gill, after which it falls under gravity between the gills and is carried away by air currents.

Basidiomycetes generally carry out little or no asexual reproduction; some produce conidia, oidia, blastospores or chlamydospores. The aeciospores and urediniospores of rusts are dikaryotic asexual spores (Section 11.2.3).

### 11.2.2. Types of Basidia and Classification of Basidiomycetes

The structure of the basidium is an important basis of basidiomycete classification:

- Holobasidium: single-celled, club-shaped, usually with four sterigmata at the tip, as in agarics and polypores.
- Phragmobasidium (septate basidium): transversely septate (the basidium is divided by transverse septa into four cells in a row, each bearing one lateral sterigma), as in rusts, smuts and wood ear; or longitudinally septate (the basidium is spherical and divided by longitudinal septa into four cells, forming a cross in cross section), as in white jelly fungus (*Tremella*).
- Forked basidium: the basidium forks into two long arms, each bearing one basidiospore, as in the Dacrymycetes (*Dacrymyces*, *Dacryopinax*).

Traditional classification divides the basidiomycetes by whether the basidium is septate into the Phragmobasidiomycetidae and the Holobasidiomycetidae. Ainsworth (1973) divided them into three classes according to the presence or absence of a basidiocarp and whether it opens: Teliomycetes (no basidiocarp, basidia arising from teliospores), Hymenomycetes (well-developed basidiocarps with an exposed hymenium in which basidia are arranged) and Gasteromycetes (basidiocarps angiocarpic, the hymenium enclosed in a peridium at maturity, so spores are exposed only after they ripen). Molecular phylogeny divides the Basidiomycota into three main subphyla ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.5.3)):

| Subphylum | Main classes | Septal pore | Basidium | Examples | Relation to the old system |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pucciniomycotina | Pucciniomycetes, Microbotryomycetes and others | Simple pore | Mostly transversely septate | Rusts (about 8000 species); *Rhodotorula*, *Sporobolomyces*; anther smut (*Microbotryum*) | Pucciniales of the Teliomycetes, plus some "smuts" and yeasts |
| Ustilaginomycotina | Ustilaginomycetes, Exobasidiomycetes | Varied | Septate or aseptate | Smuts, bunt fungi, *Exobasidium* | Ustilaginales of the Teliomycetes |
| Agaricomycotina | Agaricomycetes, Tremellomycetes, Dacrymycetes | Dolipore | Aseptate, longitudinally septate or forked | Mushrooms, polypores, puffballs, stinkhorns, wood ear, white jelly fungus, *Cryptococcus* | Hymenomycetes and Gasteromycetes |

In addition, the Wallemiomycotina is a small group separated from the Agaricomycotina around 2017 (the xerophilic *Wallemia*). The old Gasteromycetes are a **polyphyletic group**: the closed, gasteromycete-type basidiocarp with passive spore release arose independently many times in many orders of the Agaricomycetes. Puffballs moved into the Agaricales, earthstars became the Geastrales, stinkhorns the Phallales, and the earthballs and false truffles moved into the Boletales. The wood ear and white jelly fungus of the Hymenomycetes have septate basidia and belong to the Auriculariales of the Agaricomycetes and to the Tremellomycetes, respectively.

### 11.2.3. Rusts

The rusts (Pucciniales, Uredinales in Chinese textbooks) are all obligate parasites of seed plants and ferns and form no basidiocarps; they are named for the rust-colored spore masses on diseased parts of the host, and number 7000–8000 species. Their life cycle is the most complex among fungi: the primary mycelium is uninucleate and produces spermatia; the secondary mycelium is dikaryotic and produces aeciospores, urediniospores and teliospores. Most rusts overwinter as teliospores, in which karyogamy occurs, and on germination of the teliospore meiosis produces basidiospores.

**Definition 11.2.2 (Rust Spores)** A rust life cycle has at most five kinds of spores, numbered 0–IV in order of appearance: the uninucleate *spermatia* (0), formed in spermogonia and acting as male gametes; the dikaryotic *aeciospores* (also called spring spores, I), formed in aecia; the dikaryotic *urediniospores* (II), formed in uredinia and able to reinfect the same host repeatedly; and the thick-walled **teliospores** (III), formed in telia, in which karyogamy takes place and which germinate to produce the promycelium, that is, the basidium; the basidium bears uninucleate, haploid basidiospores (IV).

Rusts with all five spore stages are called macrocyclic, and those lacking some stages (for example, no urediniospore stage) microcyclic. The life cycles of some rusts need two hosts:

**Definition 11.2.3 (Heteroecism)** The phenomenon in which a parasitic fungus (especially a rust) must alternate between two distantly related hosts to complete its life cycle is called **heteroecism**; if the life cycle can be completed on one host, the fungus is autoecious.

*Puccinia graminis* (wheat stem rust) is the most familiar macrocyclic, heteroecious rust (Figure 4). The primary host is wheat, barley, oats and other grasses, and the alternate host is certain species of *Berberis* (barberry) and *Mahonia*. Its life cycle is as follows:

1. Teliospores: when the wheat grain begins to ripen, teliospores begin to form in the uredinia, making black telia (the "black rust stage"). A teliospore has two thick-walled cells and a stalk; each cell at first has two nuclei, and over winter karyogamy forms the diploid zygote nucleus.
2. Basidiospores: in spring the teliospores germinate, each cell producing a promycelium; the zygote nucleus moves into it and undergoes meiosis to give four haploid nuclei. Septa divide the promycelium into a four-celled basidium, each cell on one side bears a sterigma, and a basidiospore forms on it. Of the four basidiospores, two are (+) and two (−).
3. Spermogonia: basidiospores blown onto young barberry leaves germinate with a germ tube, enter through the upper epidermis and form flask-shaped (+) or (−) spermogonia in the palisade tissue. The spermogonium opens at the upper epidermis; around the pore is a tuft of long, sterile hyphae (periphyses), and inside are many rod-shaped spermatiophores whose tips produce chains of round, uninucleate spermatia; flexuous hyphae (receptive hyphae) also project from the pore. The spermatia flow out of the pore in a fragrant nectar that attracts insects, which carry (+) spermatia to the flexuous hyphae of (−) spermogonia, or the reverse. *P. graminis* is heterothallic: the two fuse with plasmogamy only, forming dikaryotic hyphae. This is the fertilization described in [7.12.10](/7.12.10#sec-10.5.3).
4. Aecia: the dikaryotic hyphae spread into the spongy tissue of the leaf and form cup-shaped aecia in the lower epidermis, surrounded by a peridium. Inside, the hyphae are densely packed in bundles, and each produces at its tip a chain of dikaryotic aeciospores, with a small flattened intercalary cell between every two aeciospores, which disappears at maturity. The aecium breaks through the lower epidermis and releases yellow, spherical, spiny aeciospores.
5. Urediniospores: aeciospores blown onto wheat leaves, sheaths or stems germinate with a germ tube that enters through a stomatal opening and develops into a dikaryotic mycelium in the tissue, soon forming uredinia and dikaryotic urediniospores. Urediniospores are one-celled, long elliptical, orange-yellow and finely spiny; as they grow they break through the host epidermis and the diseased part shows rust-red pustules (the "red rust stage"). Urediniospores are very numerous and are carried by wind to the same or other plants, and one cycle takes only 7–12 days; they reinfect throughout the summer and are therefore the main cause of rust epidemics, and can also travel hundreds to thousands of kilometers on high-altitude air currents.
6. When the wheat grain ripens, teliospores again begin to form in the uredinia, completing the life cycle.

The life cycle shows that on wheat there are only dikaryotic stages (the mycelium after aeciospore infection, urediniospores and teliospores), while the haploid stages (the mycelium after basidiospore infection and the spermogonia) occur only on barberry. Where the fungus must overwinter as teliospores and complete sexual reproduction on barberry in spring, eradicating barberry therefore breaks the disease cycle: after 1918 the United States carried out a large-scale barberry eradication campaign. In regions with warm winters, however, where urediniospores can survive all year on grasses, *P. graminis* persists without barberry.

Rust is one of the most important diseases of wheat. Resistance breeding is the main control measure, but mutation and recombination in the pathogen often overcome the resistance of new cultivars within a few years. The race Ug99 (TTKSK) of *P. graminis*, discovered in Uganda in 1998 and named in 1999, overcame the then widely used resistance gene *Sr31* and has since spread to East Africa, Yemen and Iran. In China the most damaging wheat rust is stripe rust (*P. striiformis* f. sp. *tritici*), followed by leaf rust (*P. triticina*); the alternate host of the stripe rust fungus was long unknown until Jin et al. showed in 2010 that barberry is also its alternate host. Pear rust (*Gymnosporangium asiaticum*) is another heteroecious rust: the spermogonia and aecia are on pear leaves and the telia on twigs of Chinese juniper (*Juniperus chinensis*), with no urediniospore stage, so junipers should not be planted near orchards.

![Life cycle of *Puccinia graminis*, with the spore stages numbered 0–IV. A blue background marks the dikaryotic phase ($n + n$) and orange the haploid phase ($n$). On wheat: after aeciospore infection the dikaryotic mycelium produces uredinia, and urediniospores reinfect wheat every 7–12 days (red rust stage, II); later two-celled, thick-walled teliospores are produced (black rust stage, III), in which karyogamy gives a diploid nucleus during overwintering. ### 11.2.4. Smuts

The smuts (Ustilaginales and others) are all parasites of higher plants, most on grasses and sedges; among crops they damage wheat, maize, sorghum, millet and rice. They form no basidiocarps; large numbers of black teliospores (called thick-walled spores in Chinese textbooks) form in the inflorescence, grain or other parts of the host, turning the diseased part into a mass of black powder. Unlike rusts, smuts are autoecious and have a simpler life cycle.

Corn smut (*Ustilago maydis*) causes common smut of maize: all aerial parts of the plant can be infected, often at the junction of leaf and sheath, at axillary buds near the nodes, and on the ears and tassels. The infected parts form white galls (more than 10 cm across in large ones) in which teliospores are produced; at maturity the outer membrane ruptures and releases dark brown teliospores.

1. The teliospores are spherical with distinct fine spines and overwinter in soil, compost and diseased plant debris. The two nuclei in a teliospore have already fused during its formation, so mature teliospores are diploid.
2. The next year the teliospores germinate; after meiosis a promycelium grows from the break in the spore, divided into a four-celled basidium, each cell bearing basidiospores (sporidia) laterally. The basidiospores continue to multiply by budding as haploid, yeast-like cells.
3. Two basidiospores or budding cells of compatible mating type (differing at both the *a* and *b* loci) fuse to form a dikaryotic infection hypha, which enters the host through young tissue. Stimulated by the hyphae, host cells enlarge and divide vigorously and nutrients are diverted to the infected part, forming a gall; later almost all the tissue inside the gall is consumed by hyphae, the dikaryotic hyphae divide into many segments, and each segment forms a thick-walled teliospore, dispersed by wind. Smut is a local infection and can infect several times a year.

*U. maydis* is a model organism for studying the pathogenicity and mating types of plant-pathogenic fungi; its young galls are eaten in Mexico as huitlacoche.

Loose smut of wheat (*Ustilago tritici*) infects the flowering spike of wheat. Diseased plants head slightly early, all the spikelets of the diseased ear turn to black powder, covered at first by a thin gray membrane that soon ruptures, leaving finally only the bare rachis. When healthy plants flower, teliospores blown onto the stigma germinate and the hyphae enter the ovary and lie dormant in the embryo of the seed; infected grain looks no different from healthy grain. When diseased grain is sown, the hyphae rise with the growing point of the seedling and invade the inflorescence at heading, forming teliospores. Because the mycelium is hidden in the embryo, fungicides can hardly kill it, and traditional control uses hot-water seed treatment (soaking the seed in water at 44–46 °C for 3 hours) or dressing with systemic fungicides. The seeds infected with common bunt of wheat (*Tilletia caries*, Exobasidiomycetes) smell of fish (trimethylamine).

Water bamboo (*jiaobai*) is a special relationship between a smut and a plant: the smut *Ustilago esculenta* infects the wild rice *Zizania latifolia*, suppresses its heading, and causes the tip of the stem to swell into a tender, fleshy gall, the edible water-bamboo shoot. It is harvested when the mycelium is in the stem and teliospores have not yet formed in quantity; if there are too many teliospores it becomes the inedible "gray jiaobai."

### 11.2.5. The Agaric Fruiting Body

The hymenomycetes (the group of Agaricomycetes with open basidiocarps) generally have well-developed basidiocarps, which may be membranous, waxy, gelatinous, leathery, woody, corky or fleshy, in many shapes. The basidia are arranged in a hymenium, which lies on both sides of the trama; trama and hymenium make up the hymenophore. The hymenophore may be lamellate (gills; Agaricales, Russulales), tubular (tubes; Boletales, Polyporales), spiny or toothed (*Hericium*, hydnoid fungi), ridged (chanterelles) or smooth (corticioid fungi); all are ways of increasing the hymenial surface. Besides basidia, the hymenium has interspersed sterile basidioles (basidium-like sterile cells), cystidia (longer and thicker than the basidia), setae and gloeocystidia, which are characters used for identification.

The basidiocarps of agarics (Agaricales and others) are fleshy, a few nearly leathery, corky or membranous, with an umbrella- or cap-shaped pileus (cap) and a stipe (stalk), the stipe mostly central, sometimes lateral or eccentric. Under the cap the gills are arranged radially, and the hymenium covers both sides of the gills. In a cross section of a gill (Figure 5) three layers of tissue can be seen: on the surface the hymenium, made of club-shaped basidia alternating with basidioles; beneath it the subhymenium of isodiametric cells; and in the center the trama of long tubular hyphae whose long axes are parallel to the hymenium. The gills hang vertically, and the distance between adjacent gills ensures that the discharged basidiospores can fall out between them.

When the basidiocarp is young, a partial veil often joins the cap margin to the stipe and covers the gills; when the cap expands fully the partial veil ruptures and often remains on the stipe as a ring (annulus). Some species also have a universal veil surrounding the whole basidiocarp; as the stipe elongates the universal veil ruptures, and part of it remains at the base of the stipe as a volva, while the part over the cap breaks into scales or warts, or disappears. Among agarics some species have both ring and volva (*Amanita*), some only a ring (*Agaricus*) or only a volva (the straw mushroom), and some neither (shiitake, oyster mushroom); these are important bases for separating genera. The manner of attachment of gills to stipe (free, adnate, sinuate, decurrent) and the color of the spore print (the pattern of spores that fall when the cap is placed on paper) are also important identification characters.

By the manner of development of the hymenium, basidiocarps are divided into gymnocarpic (hymenium exposed from the start, as in polypores and chanterelles), hemiangiocarpic (hymenium covered by the partial veil when young and exposed at maturity, as in *Agaricus*) and angiocarpic (hymenium always enclosed in a peridium, spores released only when ripe, the gasteromycete type, as in puffballs).

Take the field mushroom (*Agaricus campestris*) as an example. In rich garden soil in summer one often finds a patch of white, downy mycelium (secondary mycelium), which soon forms rhizomorphs; small white balls 1 to several millimeters in diameter appear on the rhizomorphs, the basidiocarp primordia. A few days later pure-white buttons several centimeters in diameter, broadly ovoid to nearly spherical, are formed, and the buttons expand into umbrella-shaped basidiocarps. The cap is at first hemispherical and gradually flattens, up to 10–20 cm in diameter, with a white surface that sometimes has hairy scales; the stipe is nearly cylindrical, 5–12 cm long and easily separated from the cap; the ring is white, membranous and attached to the upper stipe; the flesh is white and consists of long tubular dikaryotic hyphae. The gills are free, white at first, then pink, and finally dark brown, which is the color of ripe spores; the spore print is dark purple-brown.

### 11.2.6. Representative Genera

Agaricales:

- *Agaricus*: the cap is fleshy and regular; the stipe is central and easily separated from the cap; there is a ring but no volva; the gills are free and the spore print is dark purple-brown. It grows on the ground, and many species are edible. The field mushroom (*A. campestris*) is a common wild edible; the most widely cultivated is the button mushroom (*A. bisporus*, the white mushroom), whose basidia have only two sterigmata. The four nuclei produced by meiosis enter the spores in pairs, so each basidiospore contains two nuclei of different mating type and germinates directly into a fertile mycelium (secondary homothallism, [7.12.10](/7.12.10#sec-10.5.4)).
- Shiitake (*Lentinula edodes*, *Lentinus edodes* of Chinese textbooks, Omphalotaceae): the basidiocarp grows on wood, semi-fleshy to leathery, tough, shrinking when dry and reviving when wet; the cap is 3–12 cm wide with pale brown scales; the gills are white and crowded; the spore print is white. It grows on fallen broadleaf trees and is a wood-rotting fungus. In Zhejiang and Fujian in China it has been grown on logs for several hundred years, and it is now produced in large quantities on substrate bags; the lentinan it contains has immunomodulatory activity.
- *Pleurotus* (Pleurotaceae): the stipe is lateral or absent. The oyster mushroom (*P. ostreatus*) is one of the easiest edible fungi to cultivate and grows on agricultural waste such as straw and cottonseed hulls.
- *Amanita* (called "poison umbrella" in Chinese textbooks): grows on the ground, fleshy, with an umbrella-shaped cap, a ring and a volva; the stipe separates easily from the cap, the gills are free and the spore print is white. Most of the genus is poisonous, and only a few species are edible; most form ectomycorrhizae with trees.
- In addition, the straw mushroom (*Volvariella volvacea*, with volva and no ring) and the enoki (*Flammulina filiformis*) are important cultivated species; the matsutake (*Tricholoma matsutake*) and *Termitomyces* (associated with termites) cannot be cultivated; puffballs and the bird's nest fungi (*Cyathus*, whose spores are enclosed in "eggs" and splashed out by raindrops) also belong to the Agaricales (Section 11.2.2).

Polyporales (the core of the Aphyllophorales or Polyporales of Chinese textbooks): the basidiocarps are annual to perennial, woody, corky, leathery or fleshy, hoof-shaped, fan-shaped, hemispherical and so on, and the hymenium lines the tubes; most grow on living trees, dead standing trees, fallen logs or wood and cause wood decay.

- *Ganoderma*: the basidiocarp is annual, woody or corky, with a lateral stipe or none, and a hard crust with a lacquer-like gloss; the tubes are small; the spores are ovoid, truncate, with a double wall (the outer wall colorless and smooth, the inner wall brown with fine projections), unlike the single-walled spores of other genera of the Polyporaceae. The cap of lingzhi is semicircular to kidney-shaped, red-brown, with a lacquer-like gloss and concentric ridges, and the stipe is lateral and of the same color as the cap; it grows at the base or on roots of oaks and other broadleaf trees. In Chinese medicine lingzhi is used for the brain, neurasthenia, chronic hepatitis and indigestion. The medicinal lingzhi of China was long named as the European *G. lucidum*, but Cao et al. (2012) showed that it is a different species and named it *G. lingzhi*.
- *Wolfiporia cocos* forms huge sclerotia on pine roots, the medicinal fuling (poria); the sclerotia of *Polyporus umbellatus* (zhuling) are also medicinal; polysaccharides of turkey tail (*Trametes versicolor*) are used as adjuvants in cancer therapy.

Russulales: the basidiocarp of lion's mane (*Hericium erinaceus*, Hericiaceae) is annual, fleshy, lumpy and pure white, attached laterally at the base to the tree trunk, 5–20 cm in diameter; spines (the hymenophore) cover the middle and lower surface, 2–6 cm long, turning yellow to yellow-brown on drying and resembling a monkey's head; it grows on standing or dead standing oak, walnut and other trees and is a commonly eaten edible fungus. *Russula* and *Lactarius* also belong to this order and are important ectomycorrhizal fungi.

Auriculariales: the fruiting body is gelatinous, ear-shaped, crust-like or cushion-like; the basidium is a phragmobasidium divided into four cells in a row, each with one long sterigma. The fruiting body of the wood ear (*Auricularia auricula* of Chinese textbooks) is ear-, leaf- or cup-shaped, thin, 3–10 cm wide, fixed to the substrate by a short lateral stalk or narrow attachment, often overlapping like roof tiles; at first soft, gelatinous and elastic, it shrinks strongly on drying into a brittle, horny mass; the underside is purple-brown to dark bluish gray, sparsely covered with short hairs, and the upper (inner) surface is smooth and bears the hymenium. It grows on dead standing oak, maple, elm and other trees and is now cultivated on a large scale on logs and substrate bags; Wu et al. (2014) showed that the wood ear cultivated in East Asia differs from the European *A. auricula-judae* and named it *A. heimuer*. The hairy wood ear (*A. cornea*, *A. polytricha* of Chinese textbooks) has much longer hairs, and its food value is second only to that of the wood ear.

Tremellales (Tremellomycetes): the fruiting body is gelatinous and gymnocarpic; the basidium is spherical with longitudinal septa dividing it into four cells, and the upper part of each cell elongates into a tube bearing a sterigma and one basidiospore at the tip. The fruiting body of the white jelly fungus (*Tremella fuciformis*) is pure white and consists of many wavy lobes; it grows on dead standing oak and other broadleaf trees and is a valued edible fungus and medicine. It is in fact a mycoparasite of other fungi: in wood it depends on an ascomycete (*Annulohypoxylon*) to break down the wood, so in cultivation the two fungi must be inoculated together. The golden jelly fungus (*T. mesenterica*) is bright orange-yellow to golden yellow and also edible.

Phallales and Geastrales and other gasteromycete-type groups: the basidiocarp is a young ovoid "egg" that opens at maturity. In the Phallales the receptaculum elongates and emerges, with the peridium remaining at its base as a volva; the spore-bearing tissue (gleba) is sticky and foul-smelling at maturity, attracting flies that feed on it and disperse the spores. The long-skirted stinkhorn (*Phallus indusiatus*; the short-skirted *Dictyophora duplicata* of Chinese textbooks is now *Phallus duplicatus*) has a white net-like indusium below the cap and is a prized edible fungus, now cultivated; the common stinkhorn (*P. impudicus*) has a basidiocarp shaped like a coarse brush. The basidiocarps of puffballs (*Lycoperdon*, *Calvatia*) are spherical or pear-shaped with a peridium of two to several layers; at maturity the inside turns entirely into brown spore powder mixed with capillitium, which escapes from the apical pore or a crack. The giant puffball (*C. gigantea*) can exceed 35 cm in diameter; it is edible when young, and the spore powder is used to stop bleeding. In earthstars (*Geastrum*) the outer peridium splits open in a star shape and curls back at maturity, lifting up the inner peridium; when raindrops strike the inner peridium the spores puff out of the apical pore.

### 11.2.7. Poisonous Mushrooms

Poisoning, even death, from eating poisonous mushrooms has been reported frequently, past and present, in every country. In China mushroom poisoning is one of the leading causes of death from food poisoning, with several hundred cases reported each year, mostly in the rainy summer and autumn in Yunnan, Guizhou, Hunan, Sichuan and elsewhere. By clinical presentation it can be divided into several types:

| Type of poisoning | Toxin | Main species |
|---|---|---|
| Acute liver injury (the main fatal type) | Amatoxins (α-amanitin inhibits RNA polymerase II, [7.2.12](/7.2.12)) | White or gray species of *Amanita* such as *A. exitialis* and *A. fuliginea*; some species of *Galerina* and *Lepiota* |
| Neuropsychiatric | Ibotenic acid and muscimol; psilocybin | *A. pantherina*, fly agaric (*A. muscaria*); *Psilocybe* |
| Gastroenteritis (the most frequent) | Various | *Chlorophyllum molybdites* and others |
| Rhabdomyolysis | Cycloprop-2-ene carboxylic acid | *Russula subnigricans* |
| Acute kidney injury | 2-Amino-4,5-hexadienoic acid and others | *A. pseudoporphyria* and others |

Amatoxin poisoning has a latent period of several to more than ten hours, followed by severe gastroenteritis; after an improvement of a day or two (the "false recovery" period), liver failure sets in, so treatment is easily delayed. The cap of *A. pantherina* is gray to yellow-brown with white patchy scales, the ring hangs down, and the volva is collar-like with 3–5 concentric rings; it mainly causes neuropsychiatric symptoms (nausea, vomiting, delirium, coma) and may kill in severe cases. Folk rules such as "brightly colored ones are poisonous," "those that blacken when boiled with silver or garlic are poisonous" and "those eaten by insects are harmless" are all unreliable, and many deadly species of *Amanita* are pure white; the only reliable prevention is not to eat wild mushrooms one cannot identify.

## 11.3. Deuteromycetes and "One Fungus, One Name"

### 11.3.1. Deuteromycetes

The Deuteromycetes (also called Fungi imperfecti) are fungi of which only the asexual stage is known and no sexual stage has been found. Chinese textbooks treat them as the subdivision Deuteromycotina; more than 1800 genera and over 26,000 species are known, of which about 300 genera contain pathogens of crops and forest trees, and some cause skin diseases of humans and animals. The great majority have septate hyphae and reproduce asexually only by conidia, sexual reproduction is rarely seen, and in some even conidia have not been found. For taxonomic convenience these fungi were grouped into a single subdivision, but they are in fact the asexual stages of ascomycetes or basidiomycetes whose sexual stage has not been found, occurs rarely or has been lost.

The Deuteromycetes were classified following Saccardo (1899) into four orders (form orders) by the way the conidia are borne under natural conditions:

| Form order | Bearing of conidia | Examples (present name and affiliation) |
|---|---|---|
| Moniliales (later Hyphomycetes) | Conidiophores scattered or clustered on the substrate surface | Rice blast fungus (*Piricularia oryzae* of Chinese textbooks, now *Pyricularia oryzae*, teleomorph *Magnaporthe*, Sordariomycetes); *Penicillium*, *Aspergillus* |
| Melanconiales | A disc-shaped acervulus is formed under the host epidermis and breaks through it | *Colletotrichum* (Sordariomycetes), for example *Colletotrichum* species causing anthracnose of cucurbits |
| Sphaeropsidales | Produced in a spherical or flask-shaped pycnidium with a pore | Eggplant fruit rot fungus (*Phomopsis vexans*, now *Diaporthe vexans*, Sordariomycetes) |
| Agonomycetales (Mycelia Sterilia) | No conidia; only mycelium and the sclerotia and rhizomorphs it forms | *Rhizoctonia solani* (teleomorph *Thanatephorus*, Cantharellales, Basidiomycota) |

Rice blast is the most serious disease of rice and can cause seedling blast, leaf blast, node blast, neck blast and grain blast. Lesions on leaves are spindle-shaped with yellow-brown margins and gray centers, with gray moldy growth (conidia) on the underside; neck blast turns the panicle neck black and breaks it, killing the whole panicle. The conidiophores arise in clusters of 2–5 from stomata; the conidia are pear-shaped with two septa. Conidia germinate in water and penetrate the host with an appressorium ([7.12.10](/7.12.10#sec-10.4.3)); disease can appear within 3 days, and new conidia form within a further 6–8 hours, so a local outbreak soon spreads over the whole field. Control relies mainly on resistant cultivars, together with proper fertilization and fungicides. *Rhizoctonia solani* attacks the seedlings of eggplant, beet, cotton, rice and soybean, rotting the young roots and causing the stem base to constrict and break (damping-off), and also causes sheath blight of rice; it is a basidiomycete, which shows that not all Deuteromycetes are ascomycetes.

### 11.3.2. One Fungus, One Name

**Definition 11.3.1 (Teleomorph and Anamorph)** The form of a fungus in its sexual stage is called its *teleomorph*, and its form in the asexual stage its *anamorph*; the form of the whole life cycle, including both, is called the holomorph.

Because the teleomorph and anamorph differ completely in form and often occur at different times, in different places and even on different hosts, they were often named separately by different people in the past; moreover, a natural classification based on the sexual stage cannot be applied to fungi of which only the asexual stage is known. The nomenclatural code therefore once permitted (Article 59) separate names for the teleomorph and anamorph of the same fungus, so one fungus could have two or more correct names, for example the teleomorph *Gibberella fujikuroi* and the anamorph *Fusarium fujikuroi*, or the teleomorph *Magnaporthe oryzae* and the anamorph *Pyricularia oryzae*.

DNA sequences make it possible to place fungi of which only the asexual stage is known directly in the phylogenetic tree, so dual nomenclature lost its necessity. The code adopted at the 2011 Melbourne International Botanical Congress deleted Article 59, and from 1 January 2013 a fungus has only one correct name ("one fungus, one name," [7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.4)). The teleomorph name no longer has automatic priority; mycologists choose a name to be conserved for each genus according to how widely it is used. For example, *Aspergillus* is kept (rather than the teleomorphic *Eurotium*, *Emericella* and others), as are *Penicillium*, *Fusarium* (rather than *Gibberella*), *Trichoderma* (rather than *Hypocrea*) and *Pyricularia* (rather than *Magnaporthe*). The Deuteromycetes as a taxon disappeared with this change: today "deuteromycete" is only an informal term for "an ascomycete or basidiomycete whose sexual stage is unknown."

## 11.4. Origin, Relationships and Economic Importance of Fungi

### 11.4.1. Origin and Fossils

Chinese textbooks hold that organisms evolved from a primitive ancestor along three nutritional lines: the line of photosynthetic autotrophy developed into the plant kingdom, the line of ingesting ready-made food into the animal kingdom, and the line of heterotrophy by absorption into the fungal kingdom. Molecular phylogeny has revised this picture: fungi and animals both belong to the opisthokonts, whose common ancestor was a unicellular organism with a single posterior flagellum. The sister group of the fungi is the nucleariids, multinucleate amoebae such as *Nuclearia* that feed on algae and bacteria, and the sister group of the animals is the choanoflagellates. Fungi are therefore not "plants that have lost chlorophyll" but a lineage that evolved absorptive nutrition and a chitinous cell wall from a phagotrophic unicellular ancestor; chytrids retain the ancestral flagellum, and other fungi lost the flagellum several times around the time of the move onto land.

Fungi are soft-bodied and leave few fossils. Molecular clocks put the divergence of fungi and animals at about 1 billion years ago or earlier (estimates range from 0.7 to 1.5 billion years). The reliable fossil evidence includes:

- *Ourasphaira*, about 0.9–1.0 billion years old, from Canada (Loron et al. 2019), is interpreted as the earliest fungus but remains controversial.
- Fossils of septate hyphae and spores about 440 million years old (Silurian; *Tortotubus* and others).
- The Rhynie chert of Scotland, about 407 million years old (Early Devonian), preserves chytrids, glomeromycete mycorrhizae identical to modern arbuscular ones ([7.12.10](/7.12.10#sec-10.7.5)) and ascomycetes with perithecia (*Paleopyrenomycites*); the giant columnar fossil *Prototaxites* of the same period (up to 8 m tall) was long thought to be a fungus, and more recent work suggests that it may belong to a separate extinct lineage.
- Carboniferous hyphae already have clamp connections, showing that basidiomycetes existed more than 300 million years ago; the earliest agaric fossils are preserved in Cretaceous amber (about 100 million years old).

The history of fungi is closely tied to that of land plants: arbuscular mycorrhizae probably helped early plants obtain phosphorus from rock and poor soil, and the ability of white-rot fungi to break down lignin appeared at the end of the Carboniferous ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.6.1)).

### 11.4.2. Relationships among the Phyla

On the basis of morphology and life cycles, Chinese textbooks inferred the evolutionary relationships among the fungal groups: flagellated fungi (with zoospores, aquatic) → zygomycetes (hyphae similar to those of the flagellated fungi but without zoospores, with zygosporic sexual reproduction, passing from water to land) → ascomycetes (no zoospores or motile gametes; in yeasts two cells unite and develop directly into an ascus, similar to the gametangial copulation of *Mucor*) → basidiomycetes (the clamp connection resembles the crozier of ascomycetes; the dikaryotic hypha and the ascogenous hypha are both dikaryons produced after sexual union; development of the basidium and ascus is basically the same, nuclei of both sexes fusing before spore production and followed immediately by meiosis).

Molecular phylogeny confirms this inference in its broad direction: the flagellated chytrids and Blastocladiomycota diverge earliest, the zygomycete-type Mucoromycota and Zoopagomycota lie in the middle, and the Ascomycota and Basidiomycota appear last and are sister groups, forming the Dikarya; croziers and clamp connections may be homologous, and the dikaryotic phase is a **synapomorphy** of the two. Two points in the inference have been revised, however: the oomycetes among the flagellated fungi are unrelated to fungi, and neither ascomycetes nor basidiomycetes evolved from any living "zygomycete" but share a common terrestrial ancestor with the Mucoromycota and Zoopagomycota. The general trend within the fungi, from simple to complex, from aquatic to terrestrial, with loss of zoospores and motile gametes, lengthening of the dikaryotic phase and the appearance and enlargement of fruiting bodies, still holds.

### 11.4.3. Economic Importance

- Food and medicine: many large fungi are delicious edible fungi, such as the mushroom, shiitake, oyster mushroom, enoki, straw mushroom, wood ear, white jelly fungus, lion's mane, morel, matsutake and truffle; more than 1000 species of fungi are edible in China, the largest producer of edible fungi, with an annual output of about 40 million tonnes (early 2020s). Medicinal fungi include the Tibetan caterpillar fungus, poria, *Polyporus umbellatus*, lingzhi, turkey tail and *Omphalia lapidescens*; many fungal polysaccharides have been studied as adjuvants in cancer therapy and for immunomodulation.
- Fermentation and industry: the brewing industry uses yeast, *Aspergillus*, *Mucor* and *Rhizopus* to make wine, sauces and vinegar; the food industry uses yeast to make bread and steamed buns; fungi are also used to produce citric acid, enzyme preparations (cellulase, amylase, pectinase) and vitamins ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.3.3)).
- Medicine: **antibiotics** such as penicillin (*Penicillium chrysogenum*) and cephalosporin (*Acremonium chrysogenum*), the immunosuppressant cyclosporine (*Tolypocladium inflatum*), the lipid-lowering statins (lovastatin from *Aspergillus terreus*) and the antifungal echinocandins all come from ascomycetes.
- Biological control: *Beauveria bassiana* and *Metarhizium* are used against corn borers, pine caterpillars, locusts and other pests, and *Trichoderma* against soil-borne diseases.
- Ecology: fungi living in rotten wood, dead twigs, fallen leaves and soil are the main decomposers of lignin, cellulose and other organic matter and, in raising soil fertility and cycling matter, play a larger role than bacteria; most land plants obtain nutrients through **mycorrhizae** ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.3)), and healthy plants generally contain endophytic fungi, some of which protect the host from herbivores.
- Harm: spoilage of food, decay of timber and buildings, diseases of forests and crops (fungi cause most infectious plant diseases, such as rice blast, rusts, smuts, powdery mildews, scab and gray mold; the pathogens of downy mildew and late blight are oomycetes, [7.12.10](/7.12.10#sec-10.3.3)), damage to humans and livestock by **mycotoxins** (aflatoxin, DON, ergot alkaloids, [7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.7)), mycoses of humans and livestock ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.8)) and mushroom poisoning.

## Summary

- Ascomycota: septate hyphae or yeasts; asexual reproduction mainly by conidia. In sexual reproduction the ascogonium and antheridium undergo plasmogamy and form dikaryotic ascogenous hyphae, from which asci arise through crozier formation (Definition 11.1.1); in the ascus karyogamy, meiosis and one mitosis produce eight endogenous ascospores. The hymenium (Definition 11.1.2) and the ascocarp (Definition 11.1.3: cleistothecium, perithecium, apothecium, ascostroma) are the basis of classification. The phylum is now divided into the three subphyla Taphrinomycotina, Saccharomycotina and Pezizomycotina.
- Basidiomycota: primary mycelium (uninucleate, short-lived), secondary mycelium (dikaryotic, long-lived, often with clamp connections) and tertiary mycelium (forming the basidiocarp); in the basidium (Definition 11.2.1) karyogamy and meiosis are followed by the exogenous formation of four basidiospores on sterigmata; basidia may be holobasidia, transversely septate, longitudinally septate or forked. The phylum is now divided into the three subphyla Pucciniomycotina, Ustilaginomycotina and Agaricomycotina, and the old Gasteromycetes are polyphyletic.
- Rusts have up to five kinds of spores (Definition 11.2.2), and *Puccinia graminis* is heteroecious between wheat and barberry (Definition 11.2.3); smuts are autoecious and overwinter as teliospores.
- Deuteromycetes are ascomycetes and basidiomycetes whose sexual stage is unknown; the dual naming of teleomorph and anamorph (Definition 11.3.1) has been abolished since 2013.
- Fungi and animals are opisthokonts; the Dikarya diverged last, and the dikaryotic phase is their synapomorphy.

## Questions

1. Using *Pyronema* as an example, describe the process from plasmogamy to the formation of eight ascospores. What is the significance of the crozier?

   **Answer** The contents of the antheridium flow through the trichogyne into the ascogonium (plasmogamy), and female and male nuclei pair up without fusing; the ascogonium grows dikaryotic ascogenous hyphae; the terminal cell of an ascogenous hypha bends into a hook, both nuclei divide simultaneously, and septa divide the hook into a uninucleate tip cell, a binucleate crozier head and a uninucleate stalk cell (crozier formation). The crozier head is the ascus mother cell, in which the two nuclei fuse to a diploid nucleus, which divides by meiosis into four nuclei and by one mitosis into eight, each acquiring cytoplasm and a membrane to become one of eight ascospores, while the ascus mother cell elongates into the ascus. After the tip cell fuses with the stalk cell the dikaryotic state is restored and a crozier can form again. The crozier ensures that every ascus mother cell contains exactly one pair of nuclei from different parents, and allows a single plasmogamy to produce many asci.

2. Compare the four types of ascocarp of ascomycetes and give an example of each.

   **Answer** The cleistothecium is spherical and without a pore, with asci scattered inside and spores released when the wall breaks, as in the sexual stages of *Penicillium* and *Aspergillus* and in powdery mildews. The perithecium is flask-shaped with a neck and pore, the asci form a hymenium, and spores are shot out of the pore, as in *Neurospora* and ergot. The apothecium is disc- or cup-shaped with the hymenium exposed, as in *Pyronema*, cup fungi, morels (a compound apothecium), *Sclerotinia* and most lichens. The ascostroma is a cavity dissolved out of a stroma, with bitunicate asci and no wall of its own, as in the apple scab fungus.

3. Which spores does *Puccinia graminis* produce on wheat and on barberry? What is the nuclear condition of each? Why can eradicating barberry control stem rust in some regions?

   **Answer** On wheat it produces urediniospores (dikaryotic, reinfecting wheat) and teliospores (dikaryotic, with karyogamy to the diploid during overwintering); on germination of the teliospore, meiosis takes place and haploid basidiospores are produced on the basidium. On barberry it produces spermatia in spermogonia (haploid, acting as male gametes) and flexuous hyphae; after plasmogamy, aeciospores (dikaryotic) form in aecia, and they infect wheat. In regions with cold winters, where urediniospores cannot overwinter, the pathogen can overwinter only as teliospores and in spring must complete sexual reproduction on barberry and produce aeciospores before it can infect wheat again; eradicating barberry therefore cuts off the source of primary infection and also reduces the sexual recombination that produces new races. In warm regions where urediniospores survive all year, the measure is ineffective.

4. How are the ring and the volva of agarics formed? Why is it impossible to tell whether a mushroom is poisonous from the presence of a ring or volva, or from its color?

   **Answer** The ring is the part of the partial veil, which in the young basidiocarp joins the cap margin to the stipe and covers the gills, that remains on the stipe when it ruptures as the cap expands; the volva is the part of the universal veil, which surrounds the whole young basidiocarp, that remains at the base of the stipe when it ruptures as the stipe elongates, and the remains of the universal veil on the cap become scales or warts. Having both ring and volva is characteristic of *Amanita*, which contains both deadly and edible species; deadly species such as *A. exitialis* are often pure white, and the volva is buried in the soil and easily lost in picking; many poisonous species have no ring or volva (for example *Russula subnigricans*, and *Chlorophyllum molybdites* resembles edible species in some features). Color, blackening of silver, insect damage and similar folk tests have nothing to do with the toxins, so one can rely only on reliable identification of the species and must not eat wild mushrooms one cannot identify.

5. What are the Deuteromycetes? Why have the anamorphs of fungi not been named separately since 2013?

   **Answer** The Deuteromycetes are fungi of which only the asexual stage (mostly reproduction by conidia) is known and the sexual stage has not been found or has been lost; following Saccardo they were divided into the form orders Moniliales, Melanconiales, Sphaeropsidales and Agonomycetales, and they are in fact the asexual stages of ascomycetes (most) or basidiomycetes (as *Rhizoctonia solani*). The code once allowed separate names for teleomorph and anamorph, so one fungus could have two correct names. DNA sequences make it possible to place the anamorph directly in the phylogenetic tree, so dual naming is both unnecessary and a source of confusion; the 2011 Melbourne code therefore abolished the provision, and since 1 January 2013 a fungus has only one name, with mycologists choosing the conserved name for each genus (for example, *Fusarium* is kept rather than *Gibberella*), and the Deuteromycetes as a taxon disappeared.
