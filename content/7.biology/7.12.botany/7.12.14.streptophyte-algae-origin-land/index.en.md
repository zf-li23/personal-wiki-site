Land plants did not step onto land in a single move from some "advanced green alga." They form the **Streptophyta** together with a group of freshwater algae, the streptophyte algae treated in this chapter (traditionally the "charophytes"). The streptophyte algae differ enormously in form: there are single swimming flagellate cells, short filaments on the soil surface, stoneworts up to half a meter tall on the bottom with differentiated nodes and internodes, and desmids that consist of a single cell yet are famous for their elaborate conjugation. It is within this group that many characters of land plants (the phragmoplast and cell plate, plasmodesmata, apical growth, oogamy, a zygote retained on the parent) appear one after another; and the most surprising conclusion of phylogenomics is that the living algae most closely related to land plants are the structurally simple, aflagellate Zygnematophyceae.

We first survey the six classes of streptophyte algae and the characters they share with land plants (§14.1), then treat the Zygnematophyceae (§14.2), Charophyceae (§14.3) and Coleochaetophyceae (§14.4). We then discuss the origin of land plants: the problems of life on land and the responses to them, the origin of alternation of generations, and fossils and molecular clocks (§14.5). The last section deals with phylogenetic relationships among the major groups of land plants (§14.6). The Chlorophyta are treated in the preceding chapter ([7.12.13](/7.12.13)), the bryophytes in the next ([7.12.15](/7.12.15)), and the place of the colonization of land in geological history in [7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.5.2).

## 14.1. Streptophyte Algae

### 14.1.1. Six Classes

Traditional textbooks treated the stoneworts as a class of the Chlorophyta (or a separate division of their own), placed *Spirogyra*, desmids and their relatives in the Zygnematales and Desmidiales of the Chlorophyceae or in a separate class Conjugatophyceae, and put *Coleochaete* in the Chaetophorales. From the 1970s, studies of cell division and the flagellar apparatus showed that these algae share a set of characters with land plants ([7.12.13](/7.12.13#sec-13.5.3)), and molecular phylogenetics later confirmed that they belong with land plants in the Streptophyta.

**Definition 14.1.1 (Streptophyte Algae)** The algae of the Streptophyta other than the embryophytes are the **streptophyte algae** (also called charophytes, in the broad sense). They form a **paraphyletic group** that comprises the Mesostigmatophyceae, Chlorokybophyceae, Klebsormidiophyceae, Charophyceae, Coleochaetophyceae and Zygnematophyceae.

| Class | Body | Cytokinesis | Reproduction | Representative genera | Habitat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mesostigmatophyceae | Unicellular flagellate with body scales and two lateral flagella | Furrow | Asexual | *Mesostigma* | Fresh water |
| Chlorokybophyceae | Mucilaginous clump of a few cells (sarcinoid) | Furrow | Zoospores | *Chlorokybus* | Aerial, soil |
| Klebsormidiophyceae | Short unbranched filaments | Furrow, spindle persists | Zoospores; no sexual reproduction observed | *Klebsormidium* | Soil crusts, rock surfaces, fresh water |
| Charophyceae | Large, with nodes, internodes and whorled branchlets | Phragmoplast | Oogamy | *Chara*, *Nitella* | Bottoms of fresh and brackish water |
| Coleochaetophyceae | Discoid or branched filaments with sheathed bristles | Phragmoplast | Oogamy, zygote retained on the parent | *Coleochaete*, *Chaetosphaeridium* | Attached to aquatic plants |
| Zygnematophyceae | Single cells, unbranched filaments or colonies | Phragmoplast (together with a furrow) | Conjugation | *Spirogyra*, *Zygnema*, *Mougeotia*, *Closterium*, *Cosmarium* | Fresh water, wetlands, snow and ice surfaces |

The Mesostigmatophyceae and Chlorokybophyceae form the earliest-diverging lineage of the Streptophyta, followed by the Klebsormidiophyceae; the Charophyceae, Coleochaetophyceae, Zygnematophyceae and embryophytes form a clade, the Phragmoplastophyta, named for their shared phragmoplast. *Klebsormidium* and *Chlorokybus* already live on land surfaces: members of the ancestral lineage of the Streptophyta were probably living on damp land long before embryophytes appeared.

### 14.1.2. Characters Shared with Land Plants

Streptophyte algae did not acquire all the characters of land plants at once. Moving up the tree from the base, these characters accumulate step by step (see the figure below):

- All Streptophyta: flagella of motile cells lateral or subapical, an asymmetric flagellar apparatus with a multilayered structure; glycolate oxidized in photorespiration by peroxisomal glycolate oxidase; the nuclear envelope breaks down in mitosis (open mitosis).
- Klebsormidiophyceae and above: the spindle persists at telophase, and the two daughter nuclei lie far apart.
- Phragmoplastophyta: the **phragmoplast** and **cell plate**; rosette-type cellulose synthase complexes; **plasmodesmata** in the stoneworts and *Coleochaete*.
- Charophyceae and Coleochaetophyceae: apical growth by an apical cell, a tissue-like body, oogamy with the egg fertilized in the oogonium on the parent; sporopollenin-like material in the zygote wall; flavonoid and cutin precursors.
- *Coleochaete*: the zygote remains on the parent after fertilization, is covered by cells of the parent, and obtains nutrients through transfer cells with wall ingrowths (§14.4).

These features show that many of the "adaptations to land" of embryophytes already existed in aquatic ancestors and only later came into play in the colonization of land, a phenomenon called **preadaptation**. Molecular data further show that many gene families associated with life on land were already present in streptophyte algae: abscisic acid receptors, components of auxin signaling, enzymes that synthesize cutin and sporopollenin, signaling genes involved in the symbiosis that forms **arbuscular mycorrhiza**, and others (de Vries and Archibald, 2018). The genomes of the zygnematophytes *Spirogloea* and *Mesotaenium* suggest that some of these gene families (such as GRAS transcription factors and PYR/PYL-type abscisic acid receptors) may have been acquired from soil bacteria by **horizontal gene transfer** (Cheng et al., 2019).

Marks on the branches indicate the origin of characters: 1, asymmetric flagellar apparatus with multilayered structure, glycolate oxidase; 2, spindle persisting at telophase; 3, phragmoplast, cell plate and rosette cellulose synthase; 4, apical growth, plasmodesmata, oogamy, egg retained on the parent; 5, embryophytes: embryo, multicellular sporophyte, multicellular gametangia, cuticle, sporopollenin spore wall; 6, vascular tissue. ## 14.2. Zygnematophyceae

### 14.2.1. General Features

The Zygnematophyceae (formerly the Conjugatophyceae) comprise about 55 genera and are the largest class of green algae in species number; the desmids alone number thousands of species (Hu Hongjun and Wei Yinxin record more than 6000 worldwide). All are freshwater or terrestrial, living in lakes, ponds, marshes and paddy fields; a few live on damp soil, rock surfaces and the surfaces of glaciers and snow (the glacier alga *Ancylonema* darkens the surface of the Greenland ice sheet and accelerates melting).

- Body: single cells, unbranched uniseriate filaments or simple colonies; cells uninucleate.
- Chloroplast: varied in form and often beautiful, and the main basis for distinguishing genera: parietal spiral ribbons (*Spirogyra*), axial stellate (*Zygnema*), axial flat plates (*Mougeotia*), axial chloroplasts with longitudinal ridges (*Closterium*, *Cosmarium*); chloroplasts contain one to several pyrenoids and store starch.
- Cell wall: made of a single piece (Zygnematales), or of the walls of two semicells joined in the middle (Desmidiales), often with pores, spines, warts and patterns, and covered with mucilage.
- No flagellated cell at any stage of the life cycle: vegetative cells, spores and gametes are all nonmotile, and there are no centrioles.
- Reproduction: vegetative reproduction by cell division or filament fragmentation; asexual reproduction by aplanospores, akinetes and parthenospores (thick-walled spores formed by gametes without fusion); sexual reproduction by conjugation.

### 14.2.2. Conjugation

**Definition 14.2.1 (Conjugation)** **Conjugation** is sexual reproduction by the fusion of two amoeboid gametes without flagella. In the Zygnematophyceae, two neighboring cells each become a gametangium, usually joined by projections into a conjugation tube; the protoplasts contract into gametes, and the gamete of one cell moves by amoeboid motion through the tube into the other, or the two gametes meet in the tube and fuse into a zygote.

The zygote secretes a three-layered thick wall (exospore, mesospore and endospore; the exospore is often patterned), accumulates reserves (starch at first, later fat) and sinks to the bottom with the parent to rest. On germination, meiosis produces four haploid nuclei, three of which degenerate, and the protoplast with the one remaining nucleus germinates into a new thallus (*Spirogyra*, *Zygnema*); in desmids the zygote forms two new individuals on germination, each containing one retained nucleus. The Zygnematophyceae therefore have zygotic meiosis, and the vegetative body is entirely haploid.

### 14.2.3. Zygnematales

The body of the Zygnematales is a single cell or an unbranched filament, without a constriction in the middle of the cell; the wall is a single piece, and no new semicell forms at division.

*Spirogyra* is the most common filamentous green alga, floating in large masses in streams, ponds, ditches and paddy fields; the outer layer of the cell wall is rich in pectin, so that it feels slimy to the touch (unlike the rough feel of *Cladophora*). The cells are cylindrical, with cellulose in the inner wall layer and pectin in the outer; 1–16 ribbon-shaped chloroplasts coil spirally along the wall, each with a row of pyrenoids; the center of the cell is a large vacuole traversed by cytoplasmic strands, and the nucleus is suspended in the center and connected to the surrounding cytoplasm by strands. There are two modes of conjugation (see the figure below):

- Scalariform conjugation: two filaments lie parallel and close together, opposing cells each produce a projection, the projections meet and the walls dissolve at the contact to form a conjugation tube; the gametes of the cells of one filament move through the tubes into the cells of the other filament and fuse with the gametes there. Afterward one filament is left with only empty walls (behaviorally male) and each cell of the other contains a zygote (female); with the conjugation tubes the two filaments look like a ladder. These *Spirogyra* are dioecious.
- Lateral conjugation: between two neighboring cells of the same filament, a projection grows on each side of the cross wall to form a conjugation tube, and the protoplast of one cell moves through it into the other; alternatively, a passage opens in the common cross wall (direct lateral conjugation). Afterward, empty cells and cells containing zygotes alternate along the same filament; these *Spirogyra* are monoecious.

When two gametes fuse, the cytoplasm fuses first and the nuclei fuse slightly later; the chloroplast from the "male" partner degenerates in the zygote, so chloroplasts are inherited uniparentally (maternally).

C: the zygote secretes a thick wall and rests; on germination meiosis produces four nuclei, three of which degenerate, and the remaining nucleus grows into a new filament.](./plots/spirogyra-conjugation.py)

The cells of *Zygnema* have two stellate axial chloroplasts, each with a large pyrenoid, and the nucleus lies between them; conjugation is mostly scalariform. The cell of *Mougeotia* has one flat axial chloroplast, almost as long and wide as the cell lumen, with several pyrenoids; the chloroplast can rotate with the light: in weak light its broad face turns toward the light, and in strong light its edge does (hence the name). This chloroplast movement is reversibly controlled by red and far-red light and is classic material for the action of **phytochrome** (the mechanism is left to plant physiology). *Mougeotia* conjugates mainly by the scalariform mode, the gametes often fuse in the conjugation tube, and the zygote lies in the tube.

### 14.2.4. Desmidiales

The great majority of Desmidiales are single cells divided into two symmetrical semicells joined at a central constriction (the isthmus); the nucleus lies in the isthmus, and each semicell has one to several chloroplasts. The cell wall consists of the walls of the two semicells and often bears pores, spines, warts and patterns; mucilage is secreted through the pores, enabling the cell to glide slowly. At cell division the two semicells separate at the isthmus and each grows a new semicell, so each daughter cell consists of one old and one new semicell.

- *Closterium*: crescent-shaped, slightly or markedly curved, with no constriction in the middle; each semicell has one chloroplast with longitudinal ridges and a row of pyrenoids; at each end of the cell is a vacuole containing crystals of barium sulfate (or strontium sulfate) that show Brownian motion. Each division leaves a suture between the walls of the old and new semicells, and the number of sutures records the number of divisions. In conjugation, opposing cells each produce a projection, the gametes fuse in the conjugation tube, and the original cells are left as empty shells.
- *Cosmarium*: the largest genus of the Desmidiales, with about 1200 species; the cell is deeply constricted in the middle into a sinus, the two semicells are mostly semicircular in front view, and the wall has punctae or granules. In conjugation the two cells come together and secrete mucilage, the cell splits open at the isthmus, and the protoplasts fuse outside the cells as gametes.
- *Micrasterias*: the semicells are deeply cut into symmetrical radiating lobes, and the genus is a model for the study of cell morphogenesis.

Desmids mostly live in slightly acidic, nutrient-poor soft water, and their species composition reflects the trophic state of the water, making them indicators of water quality.

### 14.2.5. The Sister Group of Land Plants

The traditional view held that the stoneworts or *Coleochaete* are closest to land plants, because they have a tissue-like body, apical growth and oogamy and are morphologically "most like" bryophytes. Phylogenomics (hundreds to thousands of nuclear genes), however, has repeatedly placed the Zygnematophyceae as the **sister group** of the embryophytes (Wodniok et al., 2011; Wickett et al., 2014; One Thousand Plant Transcriptomes Initiative, 2019). The zygnematophyte genomes sequenced since support this conclusion: those of *Spirogloea muscicola* and *Mesotaenium endlicherianum* (Cheng et al., 2019) and of *Zygnema* all contain many gene families that had been thought to be unique to land plants.

Why should the algae closest to land plants be structurally simple? The usual explanation is secondary simplification: the ancestors of the Zygnematophyceae may also have had a more complex body and oogamy, and later lost the flagella (and with them motile sperm and oogamy, replaced by conjugation), apical growth and tissue differentiation. In support, the complex characters shared by the Charophyceae and Coleochaetophyceae are widely distributed among phragmoplastophytes other than the Zygnematophyceae, and explaining them by one origin plus loss in the Zygnematophyceae is more parsimonious than by independent origins in the stoneworts, *Coleochaete* and embryophytes. On the other hand, the Zygnematophyceae live at the margins of fresh water and wetlands, and many species tolerate drought and strong light (forming thick-walled spores and accumulating phenolic pigments); these physiological traits may also have been characteristics of the ancestors of land plants. So "closest relative" does not mean "ancestral appearance": living zygnematophytes and embryophytes have each undergone more than half a billion years of evolution, and the ancestor of land plants was more likely a streptophyte alga living on damp land surfaces with a form more complex than that of living zygnematophytes.

## 14.3. Charophyceae

The only living members of the Charophyceae are the order Charales and the family Characeae, with about 6 genera and 400 or so species (from 300-odd to 700, depending on the author) and many fossil species. They grow in tufts on the bottom of fresh or brackish water and form dense stands in clear lakes, ponds, rice fields and marshes; some species favor hard, calcium-rich water, depositing calcium carbonate on the surface so that they feel rough, hence the English name "stonewort."

**Definition 14.3.1 (Stonewort)** The **stoneworts** are the large freshwater streptophyte algae whose thallus is differentiated into nodes and internodes, with whorls of branchlets of limited growth at the nodes, and which reproduce oogamously by oogonia and globules; they constitute the Charophyceae.

### 14.3.1. Thallus Structure

A stonewort is 10–60 cm tall and divided into an erect part and an underground part. The erect part has a main axis (the "stem") differentiated into nodes and internodes, with whorls of branchlets (short branches, formerly called "leaves") at the nodes, from which lateral branches also arise; in appearance it closely resembles the angiosperm *Ceratophyllum* (hornwort). The underground part consists of colorless, uniseriate, multicellular branched rhizoids anchored in the bottom mud.

- Apical growth: the main axis and lateral branches each end in a hemispherical apical cell, which divides transversely to give a cell below it that divides transversely once more; the upper cell is the node initial and the lower the internode initial. The internode initial no longer divides but elongates into a giant internodal cell (central cell) several centimeters long; the node initial divides longitudinally several times to form a node consisting of 2 central cells and 6–20 surrounding peripheral cells, and the peripheral cells give rise to branchlets and lateral branches.
- Branchlets have no apical cell and stop growing on reaching a certain length; they are also differentiated into nodes and internodes, the nodes bearing only whorls of unicellular spine-like projections. The main axis and lateral branches are capable of unlimited growth.
- Cortex: in *Chara* the central cell of the internode is surrounded by cortical cells derived upward and downward from the node cells; in *Nitella* the internode has no cortex and only a naked central cell.
- Cells: young cells are uninucleate, and mature internodal cells are multinucleate with a large central vacuole; many lens-shaped chloroplasts lie in spiral rows around the periphery of the cell, with no pyrenoids (unlike most green algae), and starch is stored. The internodal cells show rapid cytoplasmic streaming along a spiral band, and the giant internodal cells of *Nitella* and *Chara* are classic material for the study of **cytoplasmic streaming**, membrane potentials and action potentials.

Vegetative reproduction: fragments of the thallus sink to the bottom and grow rhizoids and branches from the nodes; the base of the thallus can also form starch-rich bulbils, similar to the tubers of seed plants, from which new plants grow.

### 14.3.2. Oogonia and Globules

Sexual reproduction in stoneworts is oogamous, and the reproductive organs are complex, each covered with a layer of sterile cells, which is very rare among algae.

**Definition 14.3.2 (Oogonium and Globule)** The female reproductive organ of a stonewort is the **oogonium** (also called the nucule), which consists of an egg cell and five tube cells spiraling around it, with the corona cells at the tips of the tube cells forming the corona. The male reproductive organ is the **globule** (also called the antheridium), a spherical body whose outer wall consists of 8 (rarely 4) shield cells and which contains many spermatogenous filaments.

Both oogonium and globule are attached to the node of a branchlet by a basal stalk cell. In *Chara* the oogonium is above the spine-like projection and the globule below it; in *Nitella* it is the reverse, and the globule is often above the oogonium.

- The oogonium is long-ovoid; the five tube cells around the egg cell spiral around it, and each bears at its tip one corona cell (*Chara*, five in all, in one tier) or two (*Nitella*, ten in all, in two tiers).
- The globule is spherical; its eight triangular shield cells contain many orange-red chromoplasts, so a mature globule is a small orange-red ball to the naked eye. At the center of the inner side of each shield cell is attached a long cylindrical manubrium, at whose end are one or two capitula (primary capitula) bearing several secondary capitula, from which arise several uniseriate spermatogenous filaments, each cell of a filament producing one sperm. The sperm are slender, with two equal flagella at the tip (the flagella are in fact subapical, and the base has a multilayered structure like that of the sperm of bryophytes and ferns).

When mature, the shield cells separate from one another, exposing the spermatogenous filaments and releasing the sperm into the water; a slit opens between the corona cells of the oogonium, and the sperm enter through it and fuse with the egg. After fertilization the corona cells fall off and the zygote secretes a thick wall; the inner walls of the tube cells thicken and are often calcified, and the zygote with its covering turns dark brown and falls to the bottom to rest. The calcified oogonial shell is called a gyrogonite; gyrogonites are abundant in sedimentary rocks from the Silurian onward and are index fossils for stratigraphy.

After a rest the zygote germinates; meiosis forms four nuclei, three of which degenerate, and the remaining haploid nucleus divides into two cells that grow into the primary protonema and the primary rhizoid, and a new plant with nodes and internodes arises on the protonema (see the figure below). Stoneworts therefore also have zygotic meiosis, the whole vegetative body is haploid, and the zygote is the only diploid cell.

The stonewort body is highly differentiated, the reproductive organs are covered with sterile cells, and the zygote produces a protonema on germination; these resemble features of bryophytes in many respects, so stoneworts were once considered the ancestors of land plants or placed in a division of their own. The objection is that the stonewort zygote undergoes meiosis on germination, producing no diploid vegetative body and no asexual generation that produces spores. We now know that the Charophyceae are a relatively early-diverging branch of the phragmoplastophytes, and that their complexity is partly homologous with that of embryophytes (apical growth, oogamy) and partly independently evolved (the sterile coverings of the oogonium and globule are not homologous with the archegonium and antheridium). The genome of the stonewort *Chara braunii* (Nishiyama et al., 2018) shows that it already has many of the same hormone-signaling and transcription-regulation gene families as land plants.

## 14.4. *Coleochaete*

*Coleochaete* (Coleochaetophyceae) has about 15 species, which grow on the surfaces of aquatic higher plants or large algae, a few living within the cuticle of stoneworts. The body is a dichotomously branched filament; in most species the prostrate filaments radiate from a center and the sides of neighboring filaments are completely fused, forming a discoid thallus one cell thick (pseudoparenchyma); growth occurs at the margin of the disc, by division of the marginal cells. Each cell has one sheet-like chloroplast and 1–2 pyrenoids, and many cells bear a long bristle with a sheathed base (hence the name; do not confuse it with *Bulbochaete* of the Oedogoniales in the Chlorophyta).

In asexual reproduction each cell produces one biflagellate zoospore. Sexual reproduction is oogamous, with monoecious or dioecious plants: the antheridia are small and arise at the ends of branches, each producing a single sperm; the oogonium is a swollen cell at the end of a branch whose tip is often extended into a trichogyne, and it contains a single egg. The sperm enter the oogonium through the trichogyne and fuse with the egg.

The changes after fertilization are the most noteworthy:

- the zygote does not leave the parent but remains in the oogonium;
- vegetative cells around the oogonium grow a layer of cells that envelops it, forming the spermocarp;
- the enveloping cells and the parent cells adjacent to the zygote form transfer cells with wall ingrowths, which move nutrients from the haploid parent to the zygote, similar to the placental transfer cells with which the gametophyte nourishes the embryo in embryophytes (Graham and Wilcox, 2000);
- the zygote enlarges and sporopollenin-like material is deposited in its wall; after overwintering the zygote undergoes meiosis and then mitosis to produce 8–32 zoospores, which are released when the spermocarp ruptures.

The life cycle of *Coleochaete* is still one with zygotic meiosis: the zygote is the only diploid cell and there is no multicellular sporophyte. But the protection and nourishment of the zygote by the parent, and the repeated divisions of the zygote after meiosis to yield more spores, are exactly the prerequisites for the "embryo" and "sporophyte" of embryophytes. The interpolation theory (§14.5.3) envisages that if the zygote undergoes several mitoses before meiosis, a multicellular diploid generation could arise from a *Coleochaete*-like life cycle.

## 14.5. The Origin of Land Plants

### 14.5.1. Problems of Life on Land and Their Solutions

Moving from water to land, plants faced a new set of problems. These were solved one by one, in different lineages and at different times; the table below lists the main solutions in embryophytes and their descendants.

| Problem | Situation in water | Solution on land | First seen in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water loss | Immersed in water, no risk of desiccation | Cuticle (cutin and wax); cells able to survive drying and revive (bryophytes) | Embryophytes |
| Gas exchange | $\ce{CO2}$ diffuses in from the water over the whole body | **Stomata** (regulable openings), aerated tissues (air chambers of *Marchantia*) | Embryophytes (lost in liverworts) |
| Support | Buoyancy of water | Thickened, lignified cell walls; the central strand of mosses | Vascular plants |
| Conduction | Every cell is in direct contact with water | Vascular tissue (xylem and phloem); hydroids and leptoids of mosses | Vascular plants (evolved independently in mosses) |
| Ultraviolet radiation | Water layer absorbs UV | Flavonoids and other phenolics, sporopollenin in the spore wall | Precursors already in streptophyte algae |
| Drying and dispersal of propagules | Swimming spores and gametes | Spores with a sporopollenin wall, dispersed by air; tetrads | Embryophytes |
| Fertilization away from water | Gametes swim in water | Multicellular gametangia (antheridia, archegonia) protect the gametes; later the pollen tube (seed plants) | Embryophytes |
| Protection of the young | Zygote develops independently in water | Embryo retained in the parent and nourished by it | Embryophytes |
| Mineral nutrition | Absorbed from the water | Symbiosis with fungi (**mycorrhiza**); later true roots | Embryophytes |

Sporopollenin is a chemically extremely stable polymer derived from fatty acids and phenolics that forms the outer wall of spores and pollen of land plants; it resists desiccation, ultraviolet radiation and microbial decay, and allows spores to be preserved in rock for hundreds of millions of years, making them the earliest fossils of land plants (§14.5.4).

### 14.5.2. Synapomorphies of Embryophytes

The **embryophytes** ([7.12.1](/7.12.1)) are a clade, and their **synapomorphies** include:

1. Alternation of generations: a multicellular haploid gametophyte alternates with a multicellular diploid sporophyte, and meiosis takes place at spore formation (sporic meiosis).
2. The embryo: the zygote develops on the maternal gametophyte into a multicellular embryo, which is nourished by the gametophyte through a placenta (an interface with transfer cells); this is the origin of the name "embryophyte."
3. Multicellular gametangia: the female archegonium (flask-shaped, with a neck and a venter that contains one egg) and the male antheridium, each with a wall of one layer of sterile cells.
4. Multicellular sporangia: spores form in the sporangium by meiosis of spore mother cells, in groups of four (tetrads); the spore wall contains sporopollenin.
5. Apical meristems (an apical cell in the gametophyte or sporophyte), and a surface cuticle.

In addition, the sporophyte or gametophyte of embryophytes bears stomata (except in liverworts), the preprophase band appears before cell division, and the great majority form symbioses with mycorrhizal fungi. Bryophytes have all these characters, but the sporophyte is unbranched and parasitic on the gametophyte; the sporophyte of vascular plants is independent, branched and has vascular tissue ([7.12.16](/7.12.16)).

### 14.5.3. Origin of Alternation of Generations: Interpolation and Homologous Theories

The streptophyte algae all have zygotic meiosis (a haploid vegetative body, with the zygote the only diploid cell), whereas embryophytes all have two multicellular generations. How did the diploid sporophyte arise? Since the late nineteenth century there have been two theories, named by Čelakovský (1874).

**Definition 14.5.1 (Interpolation and Homologous Theories)** The **interpolation theory** (also called the antithetic theory) holds that the sporophyte of land plants is a new generation: the ancestor was an alga with zygotic meiosis, in which several mitoses were interpolated before meiosis, forming a multicellular diploid phase that gradually expanded into the sporophyte, so that sporophyte and gametophyte differ in origin. The **homologous theory** (also called the transformation theory) holds that the ancestral alga already had two morphologically identical generations (isomorphic alternation of generations, as in *Ulva*), that sporophyte and gametophyte are two nuclear phases of the same kind of plant, and that on land they differentiated in different directions, so that the two are homologous.

The arguments for the two theories:

- Interpolation theory (Bower, 1908, *The Origin of a Land Flora*): the streptophyte algae closest to land plants all have a haploid vegetative body and none has isomorphic alternation of generations; in *Coleochaete* the zygote is retained on the parent, nourished by it, and produces many spores after meiosis, which is exactly the starting point required for "interpolation"; the sporophyte of bryophytes is small and simple and wholly dependent on the gametophyte, like a generation just interpolated and not yet independent.
- Homologous theory (Pringsheim, 1876, and others): in ferns and bryophytes apogamy (a sporophyte growing directly from the gametophyte without fertilization) and apospory (a gametophyte growing directly from the sporophyte without meiosis) can occur, showing that the developmental programs of the two generations can be converted into each other; the gametophytes of early polysporangiophytes in the Rhynie chert (such as the gametophytes of *Aglaophyton* and *Horneophyton*) have branched erect axes and even simple conducting tissue and resemble the sporophytes in form, looking like remnants of isomorphic alternation.

Modern evidence on the whole supports the interpolation theory. Phylogenetically, no class of streptophyte algae has alternation of generations, and *Ulva*-type isomorphic alternation occurs only in the distantly related Chlorophyta, so the homologous theory would require an ancestor with isomorphic alternation that has since disappeared entirely. In developmental genetics, the program that starts diploid development is the heterodimer of the TALE homeodomain proteins KNOX and BELL: in *Chlamydomonas*, the two mating types contribute a KNOX-type and a BELL-type protein, which combine in the zygote and initiate zygote development and meiosis; in *Physcomitrium patens*, knockout of the KNOX2 genes makes the embryo grow directly into gametophyte-like structures (Sakakibara et al., 2013). This indicates that the sporophyte developmental program was expanded from the "zygote program" of algal ancestors, consistent with the interpolation theory. The more complex gametophytes of the Rhynie chert can be explained as secondary complication of the gametophyte in early vascular plants.

### 14.5.4. Fossils and Molecular Clocks

The earliest direct evidence of land plants is not plant bodies but spores:

- Cryptospores: spores in pairs or groups of four (dyads, tetrads) without a trilete mark, often enclosed in a membrane, first seen in the Middle Ordovician (about 470 million years ago, Argentina; Rubinstein et al., 2010); their form resembles the spores of some living liverworts, so they are thought to come from plants of bryophyte grade.
- Trilete spores: single spores with a trilete mark (the Y-shaped scar left by contact among spores in a tetrad) appear in the Late Ordovician and become common in the Silurian; trilete spores are characteristic of living vascular plants and many bryophytes.
- Plant fragments: Ordovician and Silurian rocks contain cuticle-like sheets and tubular structures that may come from early land plants.
- Macrofossils: the earliest macrofossils of vascular plants, such as *Cooksonia*, appear in the middle Silurian (about 430 million years ago) and are only a few centimeters tall; the Early Devonian Rhynie chert (about 407 million years old) preserves early land plants together with fungi down to the cellular level, and is left for [7.12.16](/7.12.16). The earliest macrofossils of liverworts are from the Middle Devonian (such as *Metzgeriothallus*).

Estimates by **molecular clocks** are generally earlier than the fossils: with multigene data and fossil calibration, the **crown group** of embryophytes originated between the mid-Cambrian and the Early Ordovician (about 515 to 470 million years ago; Morris et al., 2018). The gap is generally explained by early land plants being small and lacking lignified tissue, so that they are hard to preserve as macrofossils; even the earliest spores represent only plants that already had a sporopollenin spore wall. Calcified stonewort fossils first appear in the Silurian. Various authors have proposed earlier fossils of land plants (such as the Cambrian "Parafunaria"), but none has been generally accepted ([7.12.15](/7.12.15)). The geological background of the colonization of land (changes in atmospheric oxygen, weathering and climate) is treated in [7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.5.2) and not repeated here.

## 14.6. Phylogeny of Land Plants

The embryophytes divide into two great groups, the bryophytes (liverworts, mosses, hornworts) and the vascular plants, and the vascular plants into the lycophytes and the euphyllophytes (ferns and seed plants). The relationships among bryophytes, and between bryophytes and vascular plants, were long disputed:

- Liverworts first: liverworts have no stomata and no columella in the capsule, and were regarded as the most primitive land plants, with mosses or hornworts the sister group of vascular plants (Mishler and Churchill, 1985; Qiu et al., 2006). This view makes the bryophytes a paraphyletic "grade" and suggests that stomata and the water-conducting cells of mosses are precursors of the structures of vascular plants.
- Hornworts first: the gametophyte of hornworts is simple and has pyrenoids, and some analyses placed them at the base.
- Bryophyte monophyly: analyses with large numbers of nuclear genes and better-fitting substitution models place liverworts, mosses and hornworts in a single clade, with liverworts and mosses sister to each other (together called Setaphyta) and the hornworts sister to both; the bryophytes as a whole are the sister group of the vascular plants (Puttick et al., 2018; One Thousand Plant Transcriptomes Initiative, 2019; Harris et al., 2020).

The current conclusion is the third. It rewrites our understanding of the early evolution of land plants: the bryophytes are not an "ancestral grade" of the vascular plants but a sister group that has evolved in parallel with them for more than 400 million years; the common ancestor of embryophytes may already have had stomata (lost in liverworts and some mosses) and a more complex sporophyte than those of living bryophytes, and the simplicity of the bryophyte sporophyte may partly result from secondary simplification. The characters and phylogeny of the bryophyte groups are treated in [7.12.15](/7.12.15).

## Summary

- The streptophyte algae (Definition 14.1.1) are the paraphyletic group of the Streptophyta other than the embryophytes, comprising the Mesostigmatophyceae, Chlorokybophyceae, Klebsormidiophyceae, Charophyceae, Coleochaetophyceae and Zygnematophyceae; the phragmoplastophytes (Charophyceae, Coleochaetophyceae, Zygnematophyceae and embryophytes) form a clade.
- Many characters of embryophytes appear stepwise in streptophyte algae: an asymmetric flagellar apparatus and glycolate oxidase (all Streptophyta), the phragmoplast and rosette cellulose synthase (phragmoplastophytes), apical growth and oogamy (stoneworts, *Coleochaete*), and a zygote retained on the parent and nourished by transfer cells (*Coleochaete*).
- The Zygnematophyceae have no flagellated cells and reproduce by conjugation (Definition 14.2.1): *Spirogyra* has scalariform conjugation (dioecious) and lateral conjugation (monoecious); desmids consist of two semicells. The Zygnematophyceae are the sister group of the embryophytes, and their simple structure is mostly interpreted as secondary simplification.
- Stoneworts (Definition 14.3.1) have nodes and internodes, whorled branchlets and growth from an apical cell, and reproduce oogamously by oogonia and globules with sterile coverings (Definition 14.3.2); in *Coleochaete* the zygote stays on the parent, a forerunner of the "embryo." Both have zygotic meiosis.
- The synapomorphies of embryophytes are alternation of generations, the embryo, multicellular gametangia and sporangia, a sporopollenin spore wall, a cuticle and apical meristems; the interpolation theory (Definition 14.5.1) of the origin of alternation of generations has gained more support. The earliest fossils are Middle Ordovician cryptospores, and molecular clocks give mid-Cambrian to Early Ordovician. The bryophytes are a clade and the sister group of the vascular plants.

## Questions

1. Why do the Zygnematophyceae, the living algae closest to land plants, have neither flagella nor oogamy? What does this suggest about the form of the ancestor of land plants?

   **Answer** Phylogenetically, among the phragmoplastophytes the Charophyceae, Coleochaetophyceae and embryophytes all have motile sperm, oogamy and apical growth, and the Zygnematophyceae are nested among them; the most parsimonious explanation is that these characters were already present in the common ancestor of the phragmoplastophytes and were secondarily lost in the Zygnematophyceae (after the loss of flagella and centrioles, sexual reproduction was replaced by conjugation of amoeboid gametes). So the "closest living relative" does not represent the ancestral form: the ancestor of land plants may have been a streptophyte alga with a more complex body, motile sperm and oogamy, while the Zygnematophyceae and the embryophytes both underwent long independent evolution afterward; in inferring the ancestor we must integrate the distribution of characters across the classes rather than take a single living group as the template.

2. What sexual features of *Spirogyra* do scalariform and lateral conjugation each illustrate? Why does only one chloroplast system survive in the zygote after conjugation?

   **Answer** Scalariform conjugation takes place between two filaments: all the gametes of one filament move out (male) and the other receives them and forms zygotes (female), so these *Spirogyra* are dioecious. Lateral conjugation takes place between neighboring cells of the same filament, which therefore has both cells that supply gametes and cells that receive them, and is monoecious. After fusion the chloroplast from the migrating partner degenerates in the zygote and chloroplasts are transmitted only by the receiving partner, so inheritance is uniparental (maternal), as in the maternal inheritance of plastids in many plants.

3. Compare in a table the oogonium of a stonewort with the archegonium of a bryophyte. Are they homologous structures?

   **Answer** Similarities: both enclose an egg, have a covering of sterile cells, and the zygote remains inside after fertilization. Differences: the sterile covering of the oogonium consists of five spiraling tube cells and five or ten corona cells, produced by the node cell below the egg cell and wrapping around it, and the sperm enter through the slit between the corona cells; the archegonium is a flask-shaped organ formed by division of a single initial cell, with a neck (neck wall and neck canal cells) and a venter (venter wall, ventral canal cell and egg), and the sperm enter through the disintegrated neck canal cells. The zygote rests in the oogonium and then undergoes meiosis, whereas in the archegonium it develops by mitosis into an embryo. The two develop differently and belong to different branches of the tree (the Coleochaetophyceae and Zygnematophyceae lie between the Charophyceae and the embryophytes), and they are generally regarded as similar structures that evolved independently, not homologous.

4. Use the interpolation theory to explain the origin of the sporophyte of bryophytes, and state what support *Coleochaete* provides.

   **Answer** The interpolation theory holds that the ancestor was a haploid alga with zygotic meiosis, in which several mitoses were interpolated before meiosis to form a multicellular diploid phase that remained attached to the parent, was nourished by it, and was responsible only for producing spores by meiosis; this is the sporophyte of bryophytes (capsule, seta and foot). In *Coleochaete* the zygote stays on the parent, is enveloped by parent cells and is supplied with nutrients by transfer cells with wall ingrowths, and after meiosis divides again to produce 8–32 zoospores: these are exactly the prerequisites for the "embryo" (protection and nourishment by the parent) and for "prolific spore production," lacking only the mitoses before meiosis. The KNOX–BELL proteins, which start development in the *Chlamydomonas* zygote and control the sporophyte program in *Physcomitrium patens*, likewise indicate that the sporophyte program was expanded from the algal zygote program.

5. The molecular-clock age of land plants differs from the earliest fossils by tens of millions of years. What are the possible reasons?

   **Answer** Early land plants were small and lacked lignified tissue, so macrofossils are hard to preserve; the earliest spore fossils represent only plants that already had a sporopollenin spore wall, and members at the time the crown group arose may not yet have produced recognizable spores; the spatial distribution of the fossil record is uneven, and there are few Cambrian continental deposits; and molecular clocks themselves depend on calibration fossils and rate models, and variation of rate among groups and through time can bias estimates toward older ages. The gap may therefore reflect the incompleteness of the fossil record, but may also arise partly from systematic error in the molecular clock.
