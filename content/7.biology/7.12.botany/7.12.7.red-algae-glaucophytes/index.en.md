The red algae are an ancient and highly specialized group. They share an origin with the green algae and land plants, since all three acquired their plastids by a single primary endosymbiosis, yet they took a completely different path. No cell in the entire life cycle has a flagellum; the nonmotile sperm reach the female organ on water currents, and the zygote remains on the parent and develops into a parasitic diploid generation, which gives rise to the triphasic life cycle found nowhere else among plants. The cell walls contain agar and carrageenan, important industrial raw materials, and nori (*Pyropia* and relatives) is among the most valuable cultivated seaweeds in the world.

This chapter first reviews the Archaeplastida and primary plastids (§7.1), then treats the glaucophytes, which retain a cyanobacterial peptidoglycan wall (§7.2), then red algal morphology, reproduction and life cycles (§7.3), classification and representative genera (§7.4), and finally the fossil record, phylogenetic position and economic importance of the group (§7.5). General algal concepts (thallus, plastid types, spores and life cycle types) are in [7.12.6](/7.12.6); a cytological overview of red algae and glaucophytes is in [7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.8).

## 7.1. Archaeplastida and Primary Endosymbiosis

Glaucophytes, red algae and green plants together form the **Archaeplastida** ([7.12.1](/7.12.1)). Their plastids all derive from a single **primary endosymbiosis**, in which a heterotrophic eukaryote engulfed a cyanobacterium that was then reduced to a plastid in the course of evolution ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.2.2), [7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.2.6)). The shared features of the Archaeplastida all relate to this event:

- the plastid has only two envelope membranes, corresponding to the inner and outer membranes of the cyanobacterium, and no chloroplast endoplasmic reticulum;
- the protein import machinery (the TOC and TIC complexes) is homologous, and the gene content and arrangement of the plastid genome also point to a single origin;
- the storage glucan is an α-1,4-glucan (starch or floridean starch), not the β-1,3-glucan of the stramenopiles.

The divergence of the three lineages is reflected in plastids and flagella. Glaucophytes retain the cyanobacterial peptidoglycan wall and phycobilisomes. Red algae lost the peptidoglycan, kept the phycobilisomes, and lost all flagella and centrioles. Green plants lost the phycobilisomes, acquired light-harvesting complexes based on chlorophyll *b*, and store starch inside the plastid. Phylogenomic analyses mostly support the monophyly of the Archaeplastida, but the branching order of the three lineages (whether glaucophytes or red algae diverged first) is unresolved, and a few groups such as cryptophytes fall inside the clade in individual analyses.

## 7.2. Glaucophytes

The **glaucophytes** are the smallest lineage of the Archaeplastida, comprising only a few genera (*Cyanophora*, *Glaucocystis*, *Gloeochaete* and others), all freshwater and not abundant. They are unicellular or colonial. *Cyanophora* is a swimming cell with two unequal flagella and no cell wall. *Glaucocystis* is nonmotile, with a thick, layered cellulose wall that is thickened on the inner side at both ends of the cell and only vestigial flagellar basal bodies; the 4–16 autospores produced by division usually remain inside the mother cell wall and form a colony. *Gloeochaete* lives attached, and its cells bear two long, nonmotile hairs. Glaucophytes reproduce by autospores or zoospores; sexual reproduction has not been observed.

The plastid of glaucophytes is blue-green and was formerly called the cyanelle; there are one to several per cell, disc-shaped or band-shaped and often arranged in a star. It has three cyanobacterial features: a peptidoglycan layer between the two envelope membranes (hence the alternative name muroplast), single thylakoids bearing phycobilisomes, and a central polyhedral body resembling a carboxysome. The pigments are chlorophyll *a*, phycocyanin and allophycocyanin, with no phycoerythrin; the storage product is starch in the cytoplasm.

Because the cyanelle looks like an endosymbiotic cyanobacterium that still has a cell wall, glaucophytes were long interpreted as an "early stage of endosymbiosis", an intermediate state in which the engulfed cyanobacterium had not yet been fully converted into a plastid. Modern research does not support this view. The cyanelle genome of *Cyanophora paradoxa* is only about 136 kb, similar in size to other plastid genomes, and nearly all cyanobacterial genes have been lost or transferred to the nucleus; the cyanelle imports nucleus-encoded proteins with the same TOC/TIC machinery as green plants; and molecular phylogeny places glaucophytes in a single monophyletic group with red algae and green plants. The cyanelle is therefore a true plastid, as ancient as those of red and green algae. The peptidoglycan wall is an ancestral trait retained from the common ancestor; mosses and some ferns among the green plants even retain the genes for peptidoglycan synthesis, which they use in plastid division. The older idea that glaucophytes are close to green algae because of their similar flagellar apparatus has likewise not been supported by molecular evidence.

**Definition 7.2.1 (Glaucophytes)** The **glaucophytes** are freshwater unicellular algae of the Archaeplastida that retain a peptidoglycan layer between the plastid envelope membranes, contain chlorophyll *a* and phycobiliproteins, and store starch in the cytoplasm.

## 7.3. General Features of the Red Algae

**Definition 7.3.1 (Red Algae)** The **red algae** are algae of the Archaeplastida that have no flagella or centrioles at any stage of the life cycle, whose plastids contain chlorophyll *a* and phycobilisomes, and which store floridean starch in the cytoplasm.

There are more than 7000 species of red algae, the great majority marine multicellular seaweeds attached to rock or to other algae, ranging from the intertidal zone to the lower limit of the photic zone. Fewer than 200 species live in fresh water, mostly in clear, fast-flowing streams, waterfalls and springs. Thalli are generally small, about 10 cm tall, and only a few exceed 1 m.

### 7.3.1. Thallus Construction

A few red algae are unicellular (*Porphyridium*, the hot-spring red algae), or uniseriate filaments (*Bangia*), or one- to two-layered blades (nori). In the great majority the thallus is built of branched filaments. A hemispherical apical cell cuts off axial cells at the tip, each axial cell gives rise to lateral branches, and many lateral branches press together and are cemented by mucilage into a pseudoparenchyma that looks like true tissue ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.1.2)). According to the number of axes, pseudoparenchymatous thalli fall into two construction types.

- Uniaxial: the center of the thallus has a single axial filament produced by the apical cell; each cell of the axial filament bears lateral branches in all directions, and the ends of these branches press together to form the "cortex". *Polysiphonia*, *Ceramium* and *Gelidium* are uniaxial.
- Multiaxial: the center of the thallus has many parallel axial filaments forming a medulla, the apical cells of the axial filaments grow simultaneously, and the medulla bears lateral branches in all directions that press together into a cortex. *Nemalion*, *Gloiopeltis* and *Rhodymenia* are multiaxial.

The uniaxial condition is most readily recognized at the young tip, where there is a single apical cell; in older parts it is often hard to distinguish because the cortex is well developed. Growth in red algae is mostly apical, rarely intercalary; in nori any vegetative cell can divide, so growth is diffuse.

### 7.3.2. Pit Connections

When a red algal cell divides, the new cross wall grows inward from the periphery but does not close completely, leaving a small pore in the center. Protein is then deposited in the pore to form a lens-shaped plug, usually covered on both sides by cap-shaped polysaccharide layers; this is the pit plug.

**Definition 7.3.2 (Pit Connection)** The structure between adjacent red algal cells consisting of a pore in the center of the cross wall and the protein plug (pit plug) filling it is called a **pit connection**. A pit connection formed directly by cell division is a primary pit connection; one formed between two cells that are not sisters (usually through a small connecting cell that fuses with the neighboring cell) is a secondary pit connection.

The pit plug separates the protoplasts of adjacent cells, but the plasma membranes on both sides pass around the plug and are continuous, so it is not a cytoplasmic channel like a plasmodesma; its main roles are mechanical connection and signaling between cells. Secondary pit connections join the different filaments of a multiaxial thallus into a whole, and in parasitic red algae they also join the cells of the parasite to those of the host. The structure of the pit plug (presence and number of cap layers, whether there is a membrane covering) is an important character in the higher classification of red algae. The blades of the Bangiophyceae have no pit connections, but their filamentous stage (the conchocelis of nori) does.

### 7.3.3. Cell Walls and Cell Structure

The red algal cell wall has two layers: an inner layer of cellulose microfibrils (xylan or mannan in the Bangiales) and an outer layer that is a gel matrix of sulfated galactans. The matrix polysaccharides differ among groups: *Gelidium* and *Gracilaria* contain agar (agarose and agaropectin), while *Chondrus*, *Eucheuma* and *Kappaphycus* contain carrageenan (κ, ι, λ and other types). These polysaccharides dissolve in hot water and form a gel on cooling, which is the chief economic value of red algae (§7.5). In the Corallinales, calcium carbonate in the form of calcite is deposited in the cell wall, making the thallus stony hard. The protoplasm of red algae is highly viscous and adheres firmly to the cell wall, and is sensitive to strong plasmolyzing agents.

Most red algal cells are uninucleate; a few are uninucleate when young and multinucleate when old, with a central vacuole. In primitive types the plastid is a single axial, stellate plastid with a central pyrenoid (*Porphyridium*, nori); in more advanced types there are many parietal disc-shaped or band-shaped plastids without pyrenoids. The plastid has two envelope membranes and single thylakoids arranged in parallel and bearing phycobilisomes on their surface; in the Florideophyceae there is also a peripheral thylakoid encircling the plastid. The pigments are chlorophyll *a*, β-carotene, zeaxanthin, lutein (in some species) and phycobiliproteins (phycoerythrin, phycocyanin and allophycocyanin). Phycoerythrin usually predominates, giving the thallus a red to purple-red color; but shallow-water species growing in strong light have less phycoerythrin and are often olive, brown or black-purple, so color alone is not sufficient to identify a red alga.

The storage product is floridean starch, an amylopectin-like α-glucan that occurs as granules in the cytoplasm, not in the plastid. With iodine it turns first yellow-brown, then grape-red and finally purple, never blue as starch does; in primitive red algae the floridean starch granules surround the pyrenoid in the cytoplasm. Many red algae also store floridoside (a galactosyl glycerol) in dissolved form, which is also an important osmoregulatory compound.

### 7.3.4. Reproduction

There are no flagellated cells at any point in the red algal life cycle; all spores and sperm are nonmotile and are dispersed by water currents.

Asexual reproduction is by various nonmotile spores.

- Monospores: a sporangium produces a single spore, which germinates into a thallus identical to the parent; found in the Bangiophyceae such as nori and in many Florideophyceae;
- Tetraspores: on the diploid tetrasporophyte, tetrasporocytes undergo meiosis and produce four haploid spores, each a *tetraspore*, which germinate into gametophytes. The arrangement of tetraspores in the sporangium may be cruciate, zonate or tetrahedral, and is a taxonomic character;
- Carpospores: spores produced by the carposporophyte (see below); in most Florideophyceae they are diploid and germinate into tetrasporophytes.

Sexual reproduction is always oogamous. Red algae are generally dioecious, rarely monoecious. The male organ is the *spermatangium*, each of which produces a single nonmotile *spermatium* without a cell wall or flagellum; spermatangia often occur in clusters on specialized branches of the male gametophyte. The female organ is the *carpogonium*, a uninucleate cell whose lower part is swollen and contains the egg nucleus and whose upper part is drawn out into a slender *trichogyne*; the trichogyne is short in primitive species and long in advanced ones. The carpogonium is usually borne at the tip of a specialized short branch of several cells (the carpogonial branch). A spermatium carried by the water sticks to the trichogyne, the wall dissolves at the point of contact, and the spermatial nucleus travels down the trichogyne to the base of the carpogonium and fuses with the egg nucleus.

Development after fertilization is unique to the red algae. The zygote does not leave the parent or germinate directly into a new individual; instead, a mass of diploid gonimoblast filaments grows on the female gametophyte, and carposporangia form at their ends. In some species the zygote nucleus is first transferred to another cell, the auxiliary cell, and the gonimoblast filaments are then produced from the auxiliary cell, so that the fertilization of one carpogonium can be "copied" in the auxiliary cell and give rise to a large number of carpospores.

**Definition 7.3.3 (Carposporophyte and Cystocarp)** In the Florideophyceae, the diploid generation that develops from the zygote on the female gametophyte and depends on the gametophyte for nutrition is called the **carposporophyte**; it produces carpospores. The carposporophyte together with the enclosing envelope (pericarp) formed from gametophyte tissue is called the **cystocarp**.

### 7.3.5. Life Cycle

Most Florideophyceae have three generations: the haploid gametophyte ($n$), the diploid carposporophyte ($2n$) living on the female gametophyte, and the free-living diploid tetrasporophyte ($2n$) that develops from the carpospores.

**Definition 7.3.4 (Tetrasporophyte and Triphasic Life Cycle)** The diploid red algal plant that develops from a carpospore and produces tetraspores by meiosis is called the **tetrasporophyte**. A life cycle in which the three generations, gametophyte, carposporophyte and tetrasporophyte, alternate in sequence is called a **triphasic life cycle**.

In the triphasic life cycle meiosis takes place in the tetrasporangium (sporic meiosis), which corresponds to the ternary type in [7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.4.3). The gametophyte and tetrasporophyte may be isomorphic (*Polysiphonia*, *Gelidium*, *Gracilaria*, in which the two can be distinguished only by their reproductive organs) or heteromorphic. For example, in *Nemalion* and *Asparagopsis* the tetrasporophyte is a tiny filament that for a long time was taken to be a different genus (the tetrasporophyte of *Asparagopsis* was named *Falkenbergia*).

The triphasic life cycle is generally regarded as an adaptation to the immotility of the sperm: the chance of fertilization is limited, and each successful fertilization is amplified by the carposporophyte into a large number of carpospores, which are then amplified again by the free-living tetrasporophyte.

Older textbooks divided red algal life cycles into "with sporophyte" and "without sporophyte" types, holding that in the latter (for example nori, *Nemalion* and *Batrachospermum*) the zygote undergoes meiosis at germination, producing haploid carpospores, so that the life cycle has only a gametophyte. Later research showed that most of these examples do not hold: the carpospores of nori are diploid and germinate into a diploid filamentous stage (§7.4.2); *Nemalion* has a tiny tetrasporophyte; and the carpospores of *Batrachospermum* germinate into a diploid "chantransia" filamentous stage, in which meiosis occurs in the apical cells before the gametophyte grows out. The "sporophyteless" type is thus in most red algae only a misreading of a heteromorphic life cycle. The Bangiophyceae (nori, *Bangia*) have only two generations, a haploid blade and a diploid filament, with no carposporophyte, a diphasic life cycle.

## 7.4. Classification and Representative Genera

### 7.4.1. Classification

Traditional classification divided the red algae (a phylum with a single class) into two subclasses: the Bangioideae, with simple thalli, diffuse growth, usually no pit connections between cells, and stellate plastids; and the Florideae, with complex thalli, apical growth, pit connections and a carposporophyte. In some textbooks the two are raised to the classes Bangiophyceae and Florideophyceae. Molecular phylogeny shows that the Florideophyceae form a monophyletic group, whereas the "Bangiophyceae" are a paraphyletic grade: the unicellular and simple groups included in it belong to several independent lineages. The current classification (Yoon et al. 2006; Muñoz-Gómez et al. 2017) divides the red algae into three subphyla and seven classes:

| Subphylum | Class | Characteristics | Representative genera |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyanidiophytina | Cyanidiophyceae (hot-spring red algae) | Unicellular, in acidic hot springs; no phycoerythrin | *Cyanidium*, *Galdieria*, *Cyanidioschyzon* |
| Proteorhodophytina | Porphyridiophyceae | Unicellular, with a mucilaginous sheath and a stellate plastid | *Porphyridium* |
| | Rhodellophyceae | Unicellular | *Rhodella* |
| | Stylonematophyceae | Unicellular or simple filaments | *Stylonema* |
| | Compsopogonophyceae | Filamentous or membranous, mostly marine, a few freshwater | *Compsopogon*, *Erythrotrichia* |
| Eurhodophytina | Bangiophyceae | Blades or filaments, with a conchocelis stage | Nori (*Porphyra*, *Pyropia* and others), *Bangia* |
| | Florideophyceae | Apical growth, pit connections, triphasic life cycle | *Gelidium*, *Gracilaria*, *Polysiphonia*, coralline algae, *Batrachospermum* |

The Florideophyceae include the great majority of red algal species and are divided into five subclasses: Hildenbrandiophycidae (crustose, with only a tetrasporophyte), Nemaliophycidae (including the freshwater Batrachospermales and the marine Nemaliales), Corallinophycidae (the calcified groups such as the Corallinales), and Ahnfeltiophycidae and Rhodymeniophycidae (the Gelidiales, Gracilariales, Gigartinales, Ceramiales and others, comprising most marine red algae).

### 7.4.2. Representative Genera

*Porphyridium* (Porphyridiophyceae): unicellular, round or oval, enclosed in a thick mucilaginous sheath; a single axial stellate plastid with a central pyrenoid and no starch sheath around the pyrenoid. It reproduces by cell division. It lives on damp ground, in corners of walls and on flowerpot surfaces, often forming blood-red mucilaginous films. It is easily grown in pure culture, is a material for studying red algal cells and phycobilisomes, and is also used to produce phycoerythrin and polysaccharides.

Nori genera (Bangiales, Bangiophyceae): the thallus is a blade, ovate, bamboo-leaf-shaped or irregularly rounded, with a more or less ruffled margin, 20–30 cm tall, attached to rock by a discoid holdfast at the base. The thallus consists of one (rarely two) layer of cells with an outer mucilage layer and is purple-red, purple or purple-blue; the cells are uninucleate, with a stellate plastid and a pyrenoid, and growth is diffuse. The traditional genus *Porphyra* has been split into several genera on the basis of molecular phylogeny (Sutherland et al. 2011; Yang et al. 2020). The species cultivated in China as tiaoban nori is now *Neopyropia yezoensis* (formerly *Porphyra yezoensis*), the one cultivated as tan nori is now *Neoporphyra haitanensis* (formerly *Porphyra haitanensis*), and the one called gan nori is *Pyropia tenera*.

The life cycle of nori (taking *N. yezoensis* and *P. tenera* as examples) has two morphologically very different stages:

1. The blade stage ($n$, the nori usually seen, growing in autumn and winter): monoecious or dioecious. Any vegetative cell can be converted into a spermatangium and divides to form 64 or 128 spermatia; the carpogonium is formed from a vegetative cell with slight modification, one end rising slightly into a short trichogyne. Spermatia reach the trichogyne on water currents, enter the carpogonium and unite with the egg.
2. The zygote divides mitotically to form several diploid carpospores (also called zygotospores), which are released and settle on the shells of mollusks such as clams and oysters, bore into the shell and germinate into uniseriate branched filaments; this is the conchocelis stage ($2n$). The filaments have pit connections and pass the high temperatures of summer in the shell.
3. In autumn, when the water temperature falls to about 15–20 °C, the filaments form conchosporangia and release conchospores. Meiosis takes place as the conchospore germinates: the four cells produced by the first two divisions each develop into a part of the blade, so that a blade is often genetically composed of four sectors.
4. The blade can also produce monospores, which under suitable conditions germinate directly into new blades, allowing nori to multiply rapidly within one growing season; in the hot season small blades ("small nori") continue to propagate by monospores.

The conchocelis was first thought to be an independent genus (*Conchocelis rosea*). In 1949 the British phycologist Kathleen Drew-Baker discovered that the carpospores of nori grow into this filament in shells; Chinese phycologists such as Chengkui Zeng then worked out the complete life cycle of nori and in the 1950s developed methods of raising the filaments on shells and artificially collecting conchospores to produce seedlings. This discovery is the basis of modern nori cultivation.

*Gelidium* (Gelidiales, Florideophyceae): the thallus is differentiated into an erect part and creeping, colorless rhizoids; the erect part branches pinnately on both sides, often two to three times. It is uniaxial, with a distinct apical cell at the tip and an interior differentiated into epidermis, cortex and medulla, with thick-walled rhizoidal filaments between medulla and cortex. The life cycle is triphasic with isomorphic gametophyte and tetrasporophyte: spermatangia are produced by epidermal cells at the tips of the male gametophyte, and carpogonia in the cortex of the female gametophyte, with long trichogynes; after fertilization a carposporophyte develops and forms a cystocarp bulging on both sides of a branchlet; on the tetrasporophyte, cruciately divided tetrasporangia form in the surface layer of the branchlets. The genus has more than a hundred species worldwide, several of them on the coasts of China, living on rocks below the low-tide line in clean, fast-flowing water. It is perennial, with new branches growing from the base each year; the erect part is 10–30 cm tall and purple-red, and may be pale yellow in strong light. *Gelidium amansii* is the main raw material for high-quality agar and is common on the coasts of the Yellow Sea and Bohai Sea in China.

*Gracilaria* (Gracilariales): the thallus is cylindrical or flattened with linear branching and a small discoid holdfast at the base; the life cycle is triphasic with isomorphic gametophyte and tetrasporophyte, and the cystocarp is hemispherical and protrudes from the thallus surface. *Gracilaria* is the main raw material for agar production (in quantity exceeding *Gelidium*). The species cultivated on a large scale in China under the name longxucai was originally *Gracilaria lemaneiformis* and is now placed in *Gracilariopsis* (*Gracilariopsis lemaneiformis*); it is used both to extract agar and as feed for cultured abalone.

*Polysiphonia* (Ceramiales, Rhodomelaceae): the genus has about 150 species, about 20 in China, and is the classic material for studying the red algal triphasic life cycle. The thallus is a branched filament of several cell rows; some species are differentiated into erect and creeping parts, attached to rocks on the shore by unicellular rhizoids at the base, 3–20 cm tall. It is uniaxial: the center of the filament is a row of thicker cells, the axial cells, surrounded by a ring of 4–24 thinner pericentral cells, which together form the polysiphonous structure; in many species there is also a layer of cortical cells outside the pericentral cells; the branch tips bear colorless trichoblasts. The gametophyte and tetrasporophyte are identical in appearance.

1. Spicate spermatangial branches form on the trichoblasts at the tips of the male gametophyte branches, with a two-celled stalk at the base: the axial cell of a spermatangial branch is surrounded by pericentral cells, which produce spermatangial mother cells, each producing 2–3 spermatangia, and each spermatangium releases one nonmotile spermatium; the mature spermatangial branch is grape-like.
2. The second cell from the base of a trichoblast at the tip of a female gametophyte branch gives rise to a special pericentral cell, the supporting cell, which bears a carpogonial branch of 4 cells; the terminal cell is the carpogonium with its trichogyne, which can be up to 3 times as long as the carpogonium. The supporting cell also bears sterile filaments laterally. Spermatia are carried by water to the trichogyne, pass through it into the carpogonium and fuse with the egg nucleus.
3. After fertilization the supporting cell cuts off an auxiliary cell between itself and the carpogonium, and the zygote nucleus enters the auxiliary cell. The supporting cell, the auxiliary cell and the sterile filaments fuse into a large placental cell (fusion cell), the gametophyte nuclei degenerate, and from it several gonimoblast filaments grow out; the diploid nuclei produced by division of the zygote nucleus enter the gonimoblast filaments, and carposporangia form at their ends, each containing one diploid carpospore. Meanwhile the pericentral cells below the trichoblast divide and develop into a flask-shaped pericarp with an opening at the top, which together with the carposporophyte forms the cystocarp.
4. Carpospores escape through the opening of the cystocarp and germinate into diploid tetrasporophytes. On the fertile branches of the tetrasporophyte, each pericentral cell first divides longitudinally in two; the outer cell is a sterile cover cell, and the inner cell divides transversely into a lower stalk cell and an upper sporangial mother cell. The sporangial mother cell undergoes meiosis and produces four haploid tetraspores in a tetrahedral arrangement. The tetrasporangia of the fertile segments are arranged in a straight line or a spiral. Tetraspores germinate into female and male gametophytes.

*Batrachospermum* (Batrachospermales): over 100 species worldwide, about 23 in China, in fresh water, attached in cool springs, streams, lakes and marshes. Taking *B. gelatinosum* as an example: the thallus is 2–5 cm tall, purple-brown, blue-green to olive, surrounded by mucilage, with a holdfast at the base. The thallus is uniaxial, the axis consisting of long cylindrical cells divided into nodes and internodes, with the internodes covered by cortical cells; each node bears many twice- to thrice-forked branchlets (primary branches) forming spherical whorls up to 400–900 µm wide, so that the thallus looks like a string of beads, hence the name; the branchlets end in terminal hairs. The cells contain disc-shaped or plate-shaped plastids without pyrenoids. The gametophyte is the large thallus: the terminal cells of the primary branches produce unicellular spermatangia, each containing one colorless, spherical, nonmotile spermatium; the carpogonial branches on the primary branches end in carpogonia with club-shaped or flask-shaped trichogynes. After fertilization the base of the carpogonium produces gonimoblast filaments and involucral filaments, forming a compact carposporophyte located between the whorls; carpospores germinate into a diploid, creeping, branched filamentous stage (the chantransia stage, which can reproduce by monospores), and meiosis takes place in the apical cells of its branches, from which new gametophytes grow. *Batrachospermum* is sensitive to water quality and is an indicator of clean water.

*Nemalion* (Nemaliales): grows on wave-splashed rocks of the mid or upper intertidal zone; the thallus is a cylinder of many branching filaments, multiaxial, with mucilage filling the spaces between the filaments, looking like purple noodles, hence the Chinese name "sea noodle". The cells are uninucleate, with a single axial, stellate plastid with a pyrenoid. Spermatangia are borne in whorls at the ends of lateral branches, and the carpogonium has a tubular trichogyne; after fertilization the carpogonium produces gonimoblast initials and forms the carposporophyte (cystocarp), and carpospores germinate into filaments. The older view held that the zygote of *Nemalion* undergoes meiosis at the first division, making it the typical "sporophyteless" type; culture studies showed that the filaments from the germinating carpospores are tiny diploid tetrasporophytes, so *Nemalion* too has a heteromorphic triphasic life cycle. It is edible; the Chinese species are *N. helminthoides* var. *vermiculare* and *N. pulvinatum*.

*Gloiopeltis* (Gigartinales, Endocladiaceae): grows on rocks of the mid and upper intertidal zone, tufted, attached by a discoid holdfast; the thallus is erect, cylindrical or slightly flattened, 6–15 cm tall, purple-red and turning yellow with age, forked or irregularly branched with frequent constrictions at the branch points; the medulla gradually disappears with growth so that the thallus becomes hollow; the cells are mostly multinucleate. The life cycle is triphasic and isomorphic, the tetrasporangia divide cruciately, and the spherical cystocarps protrude from the thallus surface. *G. furcata* and *G. tenax* are edible and medicinal, and the gloiopeltis gum is used in dyeing and textile sizing.

*Ceramium* (Ceramiales): annual, growing on rocks of the low intertidal and on other seaweeds of the intertidal zone; the thallus is red, 10–20 cm long, with alternate or forked branches whose tips curve like forceps, and with conspicuous nodes and internodes: division of the apical cell produces large axial cells, and the pericentral cells at the nodes divide to form the cortex. The tetrasporangia are arranged in a ring at the nodes and divide tetrahedrally; the supporting cell produces two carpogonial branches, and the cystocarp formed after fertilization is usually surrounded by several inward-curving branchlets.

Coralline algae (Corallinales): calcium carbonate is deposited in the cell wall and the thallus is hard. Articulated species (*Corallina*) consist of alternating calcified internodes and uncalcified flexible joints (genicula), which allow them to bend without breaking on wave-swept rocky shores; nonarticulated crustose species (*Lithothamnion* and others) form hard pink crusts on rock. In tropical reefs, crustose coralline algae cement coral debris together; they are important reef builders and resist wave erosion. Small amounts of lignin have been detected in the uncalcified joints of the articulated coralline alga *Calliarthron*, a finding suggesting that the metabolic pathway for lignin synthesis may be older than land plants.

Other common marine red algae include *Rhodomela*, *Plocamium*, *Chondrus*, *Eucheuma* and *Kappaphycus*.

## 7.5. Position, Fossils and Economic Importance of the Red Algae

### 7.5.1. Fossils and Phylogenetic Position

Red algae are among the oldest eukaryotic algae in the fossil record. *Bangiomorpha pubescens* from the Hunting Formation of the Canadian Arctic (Butterfield 2000) is a filament of uniseriate or multiseriate cells almost indistinguishable from modern *Bangia*, with differentiated spore-forming cells, and is regarded as one of the earliest known sexually reproducing multicellular eukaryotes; its age was first estimated at about 1.2 billion years and revised by isotopic dating to about 1.05 billion years (Gibson et al. 2018). *Rafatazmia* and *Ramathallus* from India, about 1.6 billion years old, may also be red algae, but this is disputed. The statement found in Chinese textbooks that unicellular red algal fossils occur in the Cambrian, "about 1.9 billion years ago", is wrong: the Cambrian began about 539 million years ago. After the Paleozoic, calcified coralline algae left an abundant fossil record.

Three older speculations about the phylogenetic position of red algae have all been rejected. First, red algae and cyanobacteria both have phycobilins, lack flagella and have single thylakoids, so red algae were thought to have evolved directly from cyanobacteria; we now know that these are cyanobacterial traits inherited with the plastid, and the red algal cell is a eukaryotic cell. Second, the green alga *Prasiola* and the red alga *Porphyra* both have stellate plastids, and it was proposed that red algae evolved from green algae of the *Prasiola* type; their pigments and storage products are entirely different, and the stellate plastid is convergent. Third, the carpogonium and trichogyne of red algae resemble the ascogonium and trichogyne of ascomycetes, and it was imagined that the ascomycetes originated from red algae that had lost their pigments; molecular phylogeny shows that fungi and animals belong together among the opisthokonts and are unrelated to red algae. Red algae are an independent lineage within the Archaeplastida, and their plastids became, through secondary endosymbiosis, the source of the plastids of cryptophytes, haptophytes, stramenopile algae and dinoflagellates ([7.12.6](/7.12.6#sec-6.2.6)).

### 7.5.2. Economic Importance

- Food: nori is the most important edible seaweed in East Asia and is cultivated on a large scale in China, Japan and Korea; in China tan nori is cultured in the south and tiaoban nori in the north. *Gloiopeltis*, *Eucheuma*, *Gracilaria*, *Nemalion* and *Gelidium* are also eaten. Dulse (*Palmaria palmata*) is used as a vegetable along the North Atlantic coasts.
- Agar: extracted from *Gelidium*, *Gracilaria*, *Pterocladiella* and others; it is used in microbiological culture media, as a food gelling agent, and in drug capsules and dental impression materials; purified agarose is the medium for gel electrophoresis.
- Carrageenan: extracted from *Chondrus crispus* (Irish moss), *Eucheuma* and *Kappaphycus*; it is used as a thickener and stabilizer in dairy products, jellies and cosmetics; *Kappaphycus* and *Eucheuma* are cultivated on a large scale in Southeast Asia.
- Other: *Porphyridium* produces phycoerythrin (a fluorescent label) and polysaccharides; *Digenea simplex* contains kainic acid, which expels roundworms; coralline algae play an important role in reef building; adding a small amount of *Asparagopsis* to feed can greatly reduce methane emissions from ruminants, and this is under study.

## Summary

- The plastids of the Archaeplastida (glaucophytes, red algae, green plants) all derive from a single primary endosymbiosis and have only two envelope membranes. Glaucophytes (Definition 7.2.1) retain a peptidoglycan wall, and the cyanelle is a true plastid, not an "early stage of endosymbiosis".
- Red algae (Definition 7.3.1) lack flagella and centrioles, have plastids with chlorophyll *a* and phycobilisomes, and store floridean starch; the cell wall consists of cellulose and sulfated galactans such as agar and carrageenan; the thallus is mostly a uniaxial or multiaxial pseudoparenchyma with pit connections between cells (Definition 7.3.2).
- Reproduction: monospores, tetraspores, carpospores; sexual reproduction is oogamous, with nonmotile spermatia reaching the trichogyne of the carpogonium; the zygote develops on the female gametophyte into the carposporophyte, which with the pericarp forms the cystocarp (Definition 7.3.3). Most Florideophyceae have a triphasic life cycle (Definition 7.3.4), and the Bangiophyceae a diphasic life cycle of blade and filament.
- Current classification: 3 subphyla and 7 classes, with the Florideophyceae monophyletic and the Bangiophyceae paraphyletic; *Bangiomorpha* (about 1.05 billion years) is one of the earliest fossil sexually reproducing multicellular eukaryotes.

## Questions

1. Compare the life cycles of *Polysiphonia* and nori: how many generations does each have, and where does meiosis occur?

   **Answer** *Polysiphonia* has a triphasic life cycle: haploid female and male gametophytes, the diploid carposporophyte living on the female gametophyte, and the diploid tetrasporophyte identical in appearance to the gametophytes; meiosis occurs in the tetrasporangia. Nori has only two stages, the haploid blade and the diploid conchocelis (filament); the zygote divides mitotically to produce diploid carpospores, there is no parasitic carposporophyte, and meiosis occurs when the conchospore germinates. Nori also propagates asexually by monospores.

2. Why is the triphasic life cycle of red algae regarded as an adaptation to the immotility of the sperm?

   **Answer** The nonmotile spermatia can reach the trichogyne only by chance on water currents, so fertilization is a rare event. The carposporophyte on the female gametophyte multiplies the zygote from one fertilization into many carpospores (in species with auxiliary cells the zygote nucleus is further amplified by being transferred into the auxiliary cell), and the tetrasporophyte in turn multiplies each carpospore into a large number of tetraspores, so that the product of a single fertilization is amplified twice.

3. The cyanelle of glaucophytes has a peptidoglycan wall. Why is it no longer regarded as an "early stage of endosymbiosis"?

   **Answer** The cyanelle genome (about 136 kb) is similar in size to other plastid genomes, and nearly all cyanobacterial genes have been lost or transferred to the nucleus; it imports nucleus-encoded proteins with the same TOC/TIC machinery as green plants; and phylogenetically glaucophytes, red algae and green plants belong together in the Archaeplastida, with plastids of a single origin. The peptidoglycan layer is an ancestral trait retained from the common ancestor (mosses still have genes for peptidoglycan synthesis that function in plastid division) and does not show that the endosymbiosis happened late.

4. How are uniaxial and multiaxial red algae distinguished? How do pit connections differ from plasmodesmata?

   **Answer** A uniaxial thallus has a single axial filament at its center produced by one apical cell (*Polysiphonia*, *Gelidium*), whereas a multiaxial thallus has several axial filaments forming a medulla (*Nemalion*); the two are most easily distinguished by counting the apical cells at the tip of the thallus. A pit connection is a pore in the center of a cross wall filled by a protein pit plug, with no cytoplasmic channel between adjacent protoplasts; a plasmodesma is a plasma-membrane-lined channel through the cell wall in which the cytoplasm and endoplasmic reticulum are continuous and molecules can be transported.

5. Which components of the red algal cell wall have economic value, and from which genera are they extracted?

   **Answer** Agar (agarose and agaropectin) is extracted mainly from *Gelidium*, *Pterocladiella* and *Gracilaria*; carrageenan from *Chondrus*, *Eucheuma* and *Kappaphycus*; both are sulfated galactans of the cell wall matrix. The calcified walls of coralline algae play a role in reef building.
