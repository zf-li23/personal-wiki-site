Lamiales comprise about 24,000 species and are the largest order of the lamiids ([7.12.47](/7.12.47#sec-47.1.1)) and one of the taxonomically most difficult groups of angiosperms. They include many important spices and medicinal plants (mint, perilla, *Salvia miltiorrhiza*, skullcap, winter jasmine, *Forsythia*, *Rehmannia*, foxglove), ornamentals (lilac, osmanthus, jasmine, snapdragon, trumpet creeper, African violet), timbers (teak, *Paulownia*, ash) and oil crops (olive, sesame). Cronquist divided these plants among three orders, Lamiales, Scrophulariales and Plantaginales; molecular systematics unites them in a single monophyletic Lamiales and at the same time completely reorganizes the traditional Scrophulariaceae and Verbenaceae. The breakup of Scrophulariaceae is the best-known example of molecular systematics correcting traditional classification: a large family defined by floral morphology that looked quite "natural" proved to be a polyphyletic group assembled from about ten lineages. This chapter treats first the circumscription and synapomorphies of Lamiales and the course of the breakup of Scrophulariaceae (§48.1), then Oleaceae (§48.2), Gesneriaceae (§48.3), the successors of the former Scrophulariaceae (§48.4), and Lamiaceae and its relatives (§48.5). For the notation of floral formulas see [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.6).

## 48.1. Circumscription and Synapomorphies of Lamiales

### 48.1.1. Circumscription and Synapomorphies

APG IV Lamiales comprise 24 families: Plocospermataceae, Carlemanniaceae, Oleaceae, Tetrachondraceae, Calceolariaceae, Gesneriaceae, Plantaginaceae, Scrophulariaceae, Stilbaceae, Byblidaceae, Linderniaceae, Pedaliaceae (sesame family), Martyniaceae, Acanthaceae, Bignoniaceae, Thomandersiaceae, Lentibulariaceae, Schlegeliaceae, Verbenaceae, Lamiaceae, Mazaceae, Phrymaceae, Paulowniaceae and Orobanchaceae.

Most synapomorphies of Lamiales are chemical and micromorphological characters that are extremely rare outside the order:

- presence of verbascoside (a phenylethanoid glycoside), C₁₁-decarboxylated iridoid glycosides (such as catalpol and aucubin) and 6-oxygenated flavones;
- glandular hairs consisting of a uniseriate stalk and a head of 2 to many cells formed by anticlinal divisions (peltate glands);
- frequent protein crystals in the nuclei of mesophyll cells;
- a "pollen sac placenta" in young anthers (a projection of sterile tissue into the anther locule, also found in Boraginales);
- fewer than 5 fertile stamens: the flowers are zygomorphic and the adaxial stamen is reduced to a staminode or lost, leaving 4, often didynamous; in some groups two more are lost, leaving only 2 (Oleaceae, *Veronica*, *Salvia*).

Relationships within the order (see the figure below): Plocospermataceae branch off first, followed by Oleaceae + Carlemanniaceae; then Tetrachondraceae, (Calceolariaceae + Gesneriaceae), Plantaginaceae and Scrophulariaceae branch off in succession; the remaining dozen or so families form the "higher core Lamiales", including Lamiaceae, Orobanchaceae, Acanthaceae, Bignoniaceae, Verbenaceae and others, whose relationships among themselves are still partly uncertain. The basal position of Oleaceae in the order agrees with its special features of actinomorphic, 4-merous flowers with 2 stamens: the didynamous stamens and zygomorphic flowers shared by Lamiales became fixed only after Oleaceae had branched off.

### 48.1.2. The Breakup of Scrophulariaceae

The traditional Scrophulariaceae (Scrophulariaceae s.l.) had about 275 genera and 5000 species and were defined by the following characters: herbs; flowers zygomorphic, the corolla often two-lipped; didynamous stamens; ovary superior, 2-locular, with axile placentation and many ovules; capsule. All these characters are plesiomorphic or common in Lamiales and do not show that these plants form a monophyletic group. Olmstead and Reeves (1995), using chloroplast *rbcL* and *ndhF* sequences, first found that the traditional Scrophulariaceae consisted of at least two distantly related lineages, and Olmstead et al. (2001), in "Disintegration of the Scrophulariaceae", divided it further into several families; later studies added a few small families. The result is as follows (see the figure below and the table in the Summary):

- Plantaginaceae (expanded): received *Veronica*, *Digitalis*, *Antirrhinum*, *Linaria*, *Veronicastrum*, *Limnophila* and others of the former Scrophulariaceae, together with the formerly independent Plantaginaceae, Callitrichaceae and Hippuridaceae; the name Veronicaceae was once used for this branch, but Plantaginaceae is a conserved name and has priority.
- Orobanchaceae (expanded): received all the hemiparasitic genera of the former Scrophulariaceae (*Pedicularis*, *Melampyrum*, *Siphonostegia*, *Phtheirospermum*, *Striga*, *Euphrasia* and others), and also the nonparasitic *Rehmannia*, *Triaenophora* and *Lindenbergia*; the holoparasitic *Orobanche* and *Cistanche* were already in Orobanchaceae.
- Scrophulariaceae (s.s.): only *Scrophularia*, *Verbascum*, *Pentacoelium* and a few others remain, and *Buddleja* of the former Loganiaceae was added.
- New small families: Linderniaceae (*Lindernia*, *Torenia*), Mazaceae (*Mazus*), Paulowniaceae (*Paulownia*), Phrymaceae (*Mimulus*, together with *Phryma* of the former Verbenaceae), Calceolariaceae (*Calceolaria*), Stilbaceae and others.

This reorganization shows that pollination-related characters such as the two-lipped corolla and didynamous stamens appeared repeatedly in Lamiales and are homoplasies that cannot be used to define families; what agrees with the molecular lineages is often inconspicuous characters such as the parasitic habit (Orobanchaceae), the 4-lobed corolla and 2 stamens (tribe Veroniceae), and the structure of seeds and pollen.

Genera of the Chinese textbook "Scrophulariaceae" are used as examples and labeled with the family they belong to today: the colored families all received genera of the former Scrophulariaceae, which are scattered over several branches of Lamiales; Oleaceae, Gesneriaceae, Lamiaceae, Verbenaceae and others were independent families already](./plots/scroph-split-tree.py)

## 48.2. Oleaceae

Oleaceae (olive family) comprise about 25 genera and 700 species (the Chinese textbook counts 28 genera and 400 species, with over 160 species in 10 genera in China), distributed from the temperate zone to the tropics.

Diagnostic features:

- Trees, shrubs or woody climbers.
- Leaves opposite, rarely alternate, simple, trifoliolate or pinnately compound; without stipules.
- Flowers bisexual, rarely unisexual or polygamous, actinomorphic, in panicles, cymes or clusters, rarely solitary.
- Calyx often 4-lobed, sometimes with many lobes or truncate; corolla sympetalous, 4-lobed (5–9-lobed in *Jasminum*), rarely polypetalous (some *Fraxinus*) or absent (most species of *Fraxinus*).
- Stamens 2, borne on the corolla tube and crossing the position of the carpels (placed at the left and right sides of the flower).
- Ovary superior, of 2 fused carpels, 2-locular, usually with 2 ovules per locule; style single, stigma 2-lobed.
- Fruit a samara (*Fraxinus*), capsule (*Forsythia*, *Syringa*), drupe (*Olea*, *Osmanthus*, *Ligustrum*) or berry (*Jasminum*).

Floral formula: $\ast K_{(4)} C_{(4)} A_2 \underline{G}_{(2:2:2)}$.

Key features of the family: woody, with opposite leaves. Flowers regular, calyx and corolla often 4-lobed; stamens 2; ovary superior, 2-locular, usually with 2 ovules per locule.

Oleaceae are divided into 5 tribes: Oleeae (*Fraxinus*, *Ligustrum*, *Osmanthus*, *Syringa*, *Olea*, *Chionanthus* and others), Forsythieae, Jasmineae (*Jasminum*), Fontanesieae and Myxopyreae. Representative plants:

1. *Fraxinus* (ash): deciduous trees; leaves odd-pinnate, opposite; flowers small, corolla 4-merous or absent, wind- or insect-pollinated; fruit a single-winged samara (with a long wing at one end). *F. chinensis* (Chinese ash) has 5–9 leaflets and flowers without a corolla and occurs throughout China, used as a street and embankment tree; scale insects ("white wax insects") are reared on its branches and leaves, and the white wax they secrete is used in industry and medicine. *F. mandshurica* (Manchurian ash) has 7–11 leaflets with yellow-brown tomentum along the veins beneath and occurs in northeastern and northern China; its wood is tough, elastic and handsomely grained, and is one of the three great hardwoods of northeastern China. The bark of *F. chinensis* subsp. *rhynchophylla* and others ("qinpi") is used in medicine.
2. *Ligustrum* (privet): leaves simple, opposite, entire; corolla 4-lobed with a short tube; drupe blue-black. *L. lucidum* (glossy privet) is an evergreen tree with leathery leaves, occurring south of the Yangtze and planted as a street tree, its fruit ("nüzhenzi") being used in medicine. *L. sinense* (Chinese privet) is a semi-evergreen shrub with densely pubescent branchlets, often used as a hedge.
3. *Forsythia*: deciduous shrubs with yellow flowers opening before the leaves, heterostylous; capsule with winged seeds. *F. suspensa* has hollow branches and simple or trifoliolate leaves and occurs in northern and central China; the fruit is used in medicine to clear heat and detoxify. *F. viridissima* has lamellate pith in the branches and simple leaves and is cultivated as an ornamental.
4. *Jasminum* (jasmine): shrubs or vines; leaves opposite or alternate, often trifoliolate or pinnately compound; corolla salverform, 5–9-lobed; berry. Arabian jasmine (*J. sambac*) is an evergreen shrub with simple leaves and very fragrant white flowers; native to India and cultivated in southern China, its flowers are used to scent tea and to extract perfume. Winter jasmine (*J. nudiflorum*) is a deciduous shrub with arching, 4-angled branches and trifoliolate leaves, bearing yellow flowers before the leaves in early spring; it occurs in northern and southwestern China.
5. *Olea*: olive (*O. europaea*) is a small evergreen tree with lanceolate leaves bearing silvery scales beneath; drupe with flesh 20%–30% oil. Native to the Mediterranean region and domesticated about 6000 years ago, it is one of the symbols of Mediterranean civilization and olive oil is an important edible oil; it has been introduced and cultivated in southern Gansu (Longnan), Sichuan and elsewhere in China.
6. *Syringa* (lilac): deciduous shrubs or small trees with simple opposite leaves; panicles, corolla salverform and fragrant; capsule. *S. oblata* has broadly ovate leaves and is the commonest ornamental shrub of spring in northern China; *S. reticulata* subsp. *amurensis* is a small tree with white flowers.
7. *Osmanthus*: sweet osmanthus (*O. fragrans*) is an evergreen shrub or small tree with flowers clustered in the leaf axils and very fragrant; drupe. Native to southwestern China, with cultivar groups such as gold, silver, orange-red and perpetual osmanthus, it is a traditional famous flower of China, and its flowers are used for osmanthus tea, pastries and perfume.
8. Others: *Chionanthus retusus* (fringe tree) has a corolla divided deeply into 4 linear lobes and is cultivated as an ornamental; *Fontanesia philliraeoides* var. *fortunei*.

Changes in circumscription: the Engler system placed Oleaceae near Loganiaceae in Gentianales, Takhtajan placed it in an independent Oleales, and Cronquist moved it to Scrophulariales of the Asteridae ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.4.4)); molecular evidence shows that it belongs at the base of Lamiales, as the sister group of Carlemanniaceae.

## 48.3. Gesneriaceae

Gesneriaceae (gesneriad family) comprise about 150 genera and 3400 species (the Chinese textbook counts 133 genera and 3000 species, with 56 genera and 442 species in China), mainly in the tropics and subtropics; the limestone mountains of southwestern and southern China are one of its centers of diversity.

Diagnostic features: mostly herbs, often with a rosette of leaves or stolons, rarely shrubs or epiphytes; leaves opposite or basal, often unequal (one larger and one smaller in a pair), often pubescent; flowers zygomorphic, corolla two-lipped with an oblique limb; fertile stamens 4 (didynamous) or 2, often with 1 staminode, the anthers often united in pairs or all together; ovary superior, half-inferior or inferior, of 2 fused carpels, unilocular with 2 parietal placentae (often projecting inward and divided) and many ovules; capsule often long and horn-like or spirally twisted, or a berry; seeds small, numerous. Floral formula: $\uparrow K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_{4} \underline{G}_{(2:1:\infty)}$ (the position of the ovary varies within the family).

Gesneriaceae are divided into 3 subfamilies: the Old World Didymocarpoideae, the New World Gesnerioideae and the South American Sanangoideae. Representative plants:

- *Conandron ramondioides*: a perennial herb with basal leaves and rotate purple corollas, occurring from eastern China to Taiwan and Japan.
- African violet (*Streptocarpus ionanthus*, formerly *Saintpaulia ionantha*): native to the mountain forests of Tanzania in East Africa, with basal, fleshy, densely hairy leaves and purple or white flowers, it is the commonest indoor potted flower. Adventitious buds and roots form at the cut petiole of a leaf, so that it can be propagated vegetatively by leaf cuttings; molecular phylogenies show that *Saintpaulia* is nested within *Streptocarpus* and it was merged into that genus in 2015. Some species of *Streptocarpus* have only one cotyledon that grows throughout life (unifoliate plants) and are material for the study of the modularity of plant morphology.
- *Primulina*: about 200 species in China, mostly narrowly distributed species endemic to limestone caves and cliffs of southern and southwestern China, which are subjects of the study of speciation in karst regions.
- *Dorcoceras hygrometricum* (formerly *Boea hygrometrica*): its leaves dehydrate completely and curl up in drought and recover within a few hours after rewatering, an example of a resurrection plant.
- Gloxinia (*Sinningia speciosa*), *Episcia*, lipstick plants and others are common ornamentals.

## 48.4. Successors of the Former Scrophulariaceae

### 48.4.1. Plantaginaceae

The expanded Plantaginaceae (plantain family) comprise about 90 genera and 1900 species, distributed worldwide. They are morphologically very diverse, from wind-pollinated plantain and aquatic water starwort to insect-pollinated foxglove and snapdragon, so that no uniform diagnostic features can be given; most are herbs with opposite or alternate leaves, zygomorphic flowers, a two-lipped or 4-lobed corolla, 4 or 2 stamens, a superior, 2-locular ovary with many ovules, and a capsule.

Representative plants:

1. *Plantago* (plantain): herbs with basal leaves and arcuate venation; spikes; flowers small, 4-merous, with a scarious corolla, wind-pollinated; stamens 4, with long exserted filaments; capsule circumscissile (a pyxis). *P. asiatica*, *P. depressa* and *P. major* are widespread; the seeds become mucilaginous when wet and are dispersed by animals and humans, and the whole plant and the seeds ("cheqianzi") are used in medicine. Floral formula: $\ast K_{4} C_{(4)} A_4 \underline{G}_{(2:2:\infty)}$.
2. *Veronica* (speedwell): herbs (the woody New Zealand species, formerly *Hebe*, have been merged); corolla tube short, rotate, 4-lobed (the upper lobe, formed by the fusion of two petals, is larger); stamens 2; capsule laterally compressed, often notched at the apex. *V. persica* is native to western Asia and is a common invasive weed of fields in early spring in China, with blue flowers; *V. anagallis-aquatica* is an aquatic or marsh herb. Floral formula: $\uparrow K_4 C_{(4)} A_2 \underline{G}_{(2:2:\infty)}$.
3. *Veronicastrum*: herbs or subshrubs with trailing stems; stamens 2, exserted from the flower. *V. axillare* occurs in eastern China, and the whole plant is used in medicine.
4. *Digitalis* (foxglove): *D. purpurea* is a biennial herb with a one-sided raceme and campanulate, purple-red corollas spotted inside, native to Europe. The English physician William Withering first recorded systematically in 1785 the effect of foxglove leaf in treating edema (heart failure); the leaves contain cardiac glycosides such as digoxin and digitoxin, which inhibit the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase of cardiac muscle cell membranes and strengthen cardiac contraction, the therapeutic and toxic doses being very close. *D. lanata* is the main raw material for extracting digoxin.
5. Snapdragon (*Antirrhinum majus*): the corolla is personate (a swelling of the lower lip closes the corolla throat, and only bumblebees heavy enough can force it open), native to the Mediterranean; it is a model plant for the study of flower development and floral symmetry (see below).
6. Toadflax (*Linaria vulgaris*): corolla personate, with a long spur at the base; its actinomorphic "peloric" mutant was first described by Linnaeus (1744).
7. Others: *Callitriche* and mare's tail (*Hippuris vulgaris*) are aquatic plants with extremely simplified flowers, each formerly in a family of its own; *Limnophila*, *Bacopa monnieri*, *Scoparia dulcis* and others are common in wetlands of southern China; *Trapella sinensis* was formerly placed in Pedaliaceae.

**Example 48.4.1 (Molecular Genetics of Floral Symmetry)** The snapdragon flower is zygomorphic: the 2 adaxial (upper) petals form the upper lip, the 3 abaxial petals form the lower lip, and the 5th, adaxial stamen is reduced to a small staminode. Luo et al. (1996, 1999) found that *CYCLOIDEA* (*CYC*) and its paralog *DICHOTOMA* (*DICH*) are expressed in the adaxial region of the floral meristem and encode TCP transcription factors, which confer "adaxial identity" on petals and stamens and suppress the development of the adaxial stamen; the flowers of the *cyc dich* double mutant are completely actinomorphic, all petals resemble the petals of the lower lip, and the 5th stamen also develops into a fertile stamen. Another MYB gene, *RADIALIS*, is activated by CYC and in the adaxial region suppresses the abaxial identity gene *DIVARICATA*. CYC-like genes have been used repeatedly to produce zygomorphy in angiosperms (in Fabaceae, in the ray flowers of Asteraceae, in *Iberis* of Brassicaceae), an example of the repeated use of the same developmental tool in **convergent evolution**.

In the peloria mutant of toadflax, the DNA sequence of *Lcyc* (the homolog of *CYCLOIDEA*) is unchanged, but the gene is heavily methylated and no longer expressed (Cubas et al. 1999); this methylation state is stably inherited and occasionally reverts, restoring the zygomorphic flower. This was the first naturally occurring **epiallele** to be confirmed ([7.4.18](/7.4.18#sec-18.6.3)).

### 48.4.2. Scrophulariaceae (s.s.)

Scrophulariaceae in the narrow sense (figwort family) comprise about 60 genera and 1800 species (the Chinese textbook counts 52 genera and 1680 species), most diverse in southern Africa and the Mediterranean region. Their circumscription is based on molecular evidence; morphological characters include a two-lipped or nearly rotate corolla, 4 or 5 stamens (*Verbascum* has 5 fertile stamens with hairy filaments), often a staminode, and a septicidal capsule. Floral formula: $\uparrow K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_4 \underline{G}_{(2:2:\infty)}$.

Representative plants: *Scrophularia ningpoensis* is a tall perennial herb with opposite leaves; corolla purple-brown, nearly globose, with the upper lip longer than the lower, 4 fertile stamens and 1 rounded staminode behind, wasp-pollinated; the tuberous roots are used in medicine. *S. buergeriana* has yellowish green corollas. Mullein (*Verbascum thapsus*) is a tall biennial herb densely covered with stellate wool, with yellow, nearly actinomorphic flowers, native to Europe and occurring wild or naturalized in Xinjiang, southwestern China and elsewhere. *Buddleja* are shrubs with 4-lobed corollas, formerly placed in Loganiaceae (or in a separate Buddlejaceae); *B. lindleyana* is poisonous in all parts and was formerly used to stupefy fish, and *B. davidii* (butterfly bush) is a common ornamental shrub that attracts butterflies. *Pentacoelium bontioides* (formerly Myoporaceae) is a semi-mangrove plant of the southern Chinese coast.

### 48.4.3. Linderniaceae, Mazaceae, Paulowniaceae, Phrymaceae and Calceolariaceae

- Linderniaceae: herbs with two-lipped corollas, the filaments of the two anterior stamens often bearing appendages. *Lindernia* are mostly small herbs of fields. Wishbone flower (*Torenia fournieri*) is an ornamental and also classical material for the study of pollen tube guidance: part of its embryo sac projects beyond the micropyle and is easy to observe under the microscope, which allowed Tetsuya Higashiyama and colleagues to discover the LURE peptides secreted by the synergids to attract pollen tubes ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.4.3)).
- Mazaceae: *Mazus pumilus* is a small annual herb with pale purple corollas whose lower lip has a yellow patch and two folds, and is one of the commonest weeds of fields and lawns.
- Paulowniaceae: *Paulownia* are deciduous trees with large, opposite, heart-shaped leaves; large panicles, corollas purple or white, funnelform-campanulate and indistinctly two-lipped; capsule woody, loculicidal, seeds small and winged. *P. fortunei* and *P. tomentosa* are fast-growing species endemic to China, with light wood that resists warping and moisture, suitable for musical instruments (the soundboards of the guqin and pipa), furniture and model aircraft. The systematic position of *Paulownia* was long uncertain (some placed it in Bignoniaceae), and molecular evidence supports its recognition as a separate family related to Orobanchaceae, Phrymaceae and others.
- Phrymaceae: *Phryma leptostachya* has small flowers in spikes that are pendulous in fruit, and the fruit is an achene enclosed in the persistent calyx, whose tip bears hooks; it was placed formerly in Verbenaceae or in a separate Phrymaceae. *Mimulus* (monkey flower; the American species are now mostly treated as *Erythranthe*) has been moved into this family from Scrophulariaceae; the North American *E. lewisii* (bumblebee-pollinated) and *E. cardinalis* (hummingbird-pollinated) are classical material for the study of pollinator-mediated speciation.
- Calceolariaceae: *Calceolaria* (slipper flower) has about 250 species in South America, with the lower lip of the corolla inflated into a pouch, and oil-secreting glandular hairs on the flower, pollinated by oil-collecting bees; its corolla is 4-merous, unlike that of Scrophulariaceae.

### 48.4.4. Orobanchaceae

The expanded Orobanchaceae (broomrape family) comprise about 100 genera and 2000 species, the great majority of them parasites; from nonparasitic through hemiparasitic to holoparasitic they form a complete series and are the best group for the study of the evolution of **parasitic plants** ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.1)):

| Mode of nutrition | Characters | Representatives |
|---|---|---|
| Nonparasitic | Autotrophic, no haustoria | *Rehmannia*, *Lindenbergia* |
| Facultative hemiparasite | Chlorophyll present, can live independently; when a host is present, attaches to the host root by haustoria on its own roots | *Phtheirospermum* |
| Obligate hemiparasite | Chlorophyll present, must parasitize to complete the life cycle | *Pedicularis*, *Melampyrum*, *Siphonostegia*, *Euphrasia*, *Striga* |
| Holoparasite | No chlorophyll, leaves reduced to scales | *Orobanche*, *Cistanche*, *Aeginetia*, *Boschniakia* |

Holoparasitism arose independently 3 times in Orobanchaceae (McNeal et al. 2013); with increasing degrees of parasitism, the photosynthesis genes of the plastid genome are lost in batches. The seeds of Orobanchaceae are extremely small and very numerous, and germinate only when stimulated by signals such as the strigolactones secreted by the roots of the host; strigolactones were later found to be a class of plant hormones and also the signal by which plants recruit arbuscular **mycorrhizal** fungi.

Representative plants:

1. *Rehmannia glutinosa*: a perennial herb with the whole plant covered with glandular hairs, basal, coarsely toothed leaves, tubular purple-red corollas and a fleshy yellow rhizome. Formerly placed in Scrophulariaceae and now in Orobanchaceae. The rhizome is an important Chinese drug: used fresh it is "fresh dihuang", dried it is "raw dihuang", and steamed with wine it is "cooked dihuang"; the Huai dihuang of Jiaozuo in Henan (ancient Huaiqing Prefecture) is a genuine regional drug.
2. *Pedicularis* (lousewort): obligate hemiparasitic herbs with about 600 species, about 350 of them in China, concentrated in the Hengduan Mountains and the Himalayas; the upper lip (galea) of the corolla is often elongated into a beak, and the forms are extremely diverse, related to fine adaptations to bumblebee pollination (different beak lengths deposit pollen on different parts of the bee's body and reduce interspecific hybridization).
3. *Orobanche* (broomrape): holoparasitic herbs without chlorophyll, with fleshy stems and scale-like leaves, parasitic on the roots of Asteraceae, Fabaceae and others; *O. cumana* is a serious parasitic weed of sunflower.
4. *Cistanche deserticola*: a holoparasite on the roots of the saxaul (*Haloxylon ammodendron*; Chenopodiaceae, now Amaranthaceae) in deserts, with a fleshy stem used in medicine and known as "desert ginseng"; wild resources have become scarce through overharvesting, and it is now cultivated by artificial inoculation.
5. Others: *Striga* (witchweed) is one of the most serious parasitic weeds of cereals such as sorghum and maize in Africa; *Siphonostegia chinensis* and *Melampyrum roseum* are hemiparasitic herbs; *Aeginetia indica* parasitizes the roots of Poaceae and Zingiberaceae.

## 48.5. Lamiaceae and Related Families

### 48.5.1. Lamiaceae

Lamiaceae (mint family; Labiatae) comprise about 236 genera and 7200 species (the Chinese textbook counts 252 genera and 6800 species), distributed worldwide and most diverse in the dry and semi-arid regions from the Mediterranean to Central Asia, where they are the main component of the shrubland vegetation; China has about 100 genera and over 900 species.

Diagnostic features:

- Herbs, rarely shrubs or trees; the plant often has glandular hairs and contains volatile aromatic oils.
- Stem often square ("square stem").
- Leaves simple, rarely compound, opposite or whorled; without stipules.
- Flowers bisexual, zygomorphic, rarely nearly actinomorphic; the cymes in the axils of opposite leaves are arranged in a whorl at the node, called a verticillaster ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.7.3)), and the verticillasters often form spikes or racemes.
- Calyx fused, 5-lobed or two-lipped (often 3 teeth in the upper lip and 2 in the lower), persistent.
- Corolla sympetalous, two-lipped (upper lip 2-lobed and lower lip 3-lobed, the 2/3 type), rarely one-lipped (*Teucrium*: the upper lip almost absent), pseudo-one-lipped (*Ajuga*: the upper lip very short), 4/1-type two-lipped (*Isodon*, *Ocimum*) or nearly actinomorphic (*Mentha*); the corolla tube often has a ring of hairs inside.
- Stamens 4, didynamous (the anterior pair longer), rarely 2 (*Salvia*, *Lycopus* and others); borne on the corolla tube; anthers 2-locular, with the locules parallel, divergent or separated by an elongated connective.
- Disc hypogynous, fleshy; ovary superior, of 2 fused carpels, each divided by a false septum into 2 locules, often deeply 4-lobed, with 1 erect anatropous ovule per locule; style often arising from the base between the 4 lobes (gynobasic style), stigma shallowly 2-lobed.
- Fruit of 4 nutlets; seeds with little endosperm or none.

Floral formula: $\uparrow K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_{4} \underline{G}_{(2:4:1)}$ ($A_2$ in *Salvia*).

Key features of the family: often herbs, aromatic, with square stems. Leaves simple and opposite. Verticillasters; labiate corolla; didynamous stamens or 2 stamens; ovary superior, deeply 4-lobed, with a gynobasic style; 4 nutlets.

Distinction from similar families: some Scrophulariaceae and Acanthaceae also have opposite leaves and square stems, but their ovary is not deeply lobed and the fruit is a capsule; Verbenaceae (s.s.) often have spikes, a terminal style and an ovary that is not deeply lobed.

Lamiaceae are now divided into 12 subfamilies (Li et al. 2016). Genera of the former Verbenaceae with cymose inflorescences, terminal styles and drupes are nested within the basal branches of Lamiaceae according to molecular evidence, so APG merges them into Lamiaceae: *Vitex*, *Clerodendrum*, *Callicarpa*, *Caryopteris*, *Tectona*, *Premna*, *Gmelina* and others. The deeply four-lobed ovary and gynobasic style shared by the traditional Lamiaceae were fully formed only within Lamiaceae (the core branches Nepetoideae, Lamioideae and others).

Representative plants (the first five genera were formerly in Verbenaceae):

1. *Vitex*: shrubs or trees with opposite palmate leaves of 3–5 leaflets; paniculate cymes; drupe. *V. negundo* and *V. negundo* var. *cannabifolia* are widespread from eastern to southern China and are common in low-mountain shrubland, the branches and leaves being used in medicine. *V. rotundifolia* is a creeping shrub of sea beaches.
2. *Clerodendrum*: trees or shrubs with simple opposite leaves, often with gland dots; corolla salverform, stamens 4, exserted; drupe fleshy, often 4-lobed. *C. trichotomum* (harlequin glorybower) has an unpleasant smell in all parts, a purple-red persistent calyx and blue fruit, occurs south of the Yellow River, and the leaves are used in medicine. *C. cyrtophyllum* has root and leaves used in medicine and is one of the sources of "daqingye".
3. *Callicarpa* (beautyberry): shrubs with leaves often bearing stellate hairs and gland dots; flowers 4-merous in dichasial cymes; berry-like drupe, purple. *C. formosana* occurs in southeastern China and the leaves are used in medicine to stop bleeding; *C. bodinieri* and others are cultivated for their fruit.
4. *Caryopteris*: *C. incana* is a small shrub with pale blue corollas, the median lobe of the lower lip fringed, and fragrant.
5. Teak (*Tectona grandis*): a large deciduous tree with large leaves and drupes enclosed in the inflated persistent calyx; native to India, Myanmar and Thailand, the wood is golden brown, oily, rot-resistant and termite-resistant, and is a world-famous timber for shipbuilding and furniture, introduced to southern China.
6. *Ajuga* (bugle): corolla pseudo-one-lipped, with a very short upper lip. *A. nipponensis* and *A. decumbens* are used as whole plants in medicine.
7. *Scutellaria* (skullcap): calyx two-lipped, the upper lip bearing on its back a shield-like appendage and closing in fruit; corolla tube geniculate at the base. *S. baicalensis* has thick roots that are yellow in section and contain baicalin (a flavonoid glycoside); it occurs in northern China and is a common Chinese drug.
8. *Agastache*: *A. rugosa* has heart-shaped ovate leaves and verticillasters forming a dense cylindrical spike; the whole plant contains volatile oil and is used in medicine. Patchouli (*Pogostemon cablin*) belongs to *Pogostemon*, is native to the Philippines, is the main ingredient of "huoxiang zhengqi" and the source of patchouli oil in perfumes.
9. *Prunella*: *P. vulgaris* (self-heal) has a two-lipped calyx and a corolla with a helmet-shaped upper lip; verticillasters form a compact terminal spike that withers and blackens in summer (hence the Chinese name "xiakucao", "summer-withering herb"); the whole plant is used in medicine.
10. *Leonurus* (motherwort): *L. japonicus* (called *L. artemisia* in Chinese textbooks) has two kinds of leaves, the basal ones nearly round and the cauline ones palmately deeply lobed, with pink corollas; the whole plant is used in medicine, and the nutlets are called "chongweizi".
11. *Salvia* (sage): about 1000 species, the largest genus of Lamiaceae; stamens 2, with a lever mechanism (see below). *S. miltiorrhiza* (danshen) has pinnate leaves and thick roots that are red outside and white inside, containing tanshinones (diterpenoid quinones) and salvianolic acids, and is used in medicine; scarlet sage (*S. splendens*) is native to Brazil, with bright red calyx and corolla, bird-pollinated, and is the commonest bedding plant; common sage (*S. officinalis*) is a culinary herb; rosemary was formerly the genus *Rosmarinus* (*R. officinalis*), but molecular evidence shows that it is nested within *Salvia*, and in 2017 it was renamed *S. rosmarinus*.
12. *Mentha* (mint): aromatic herbs with gland dots beneath the leaves; corolla 4-lobed, nearly actinomorphic; stamens 4, nearly equal. *M. canadensis* (called *M. haplocalyx* in Chinese textbooks) is a perennial herb with rhizomes, the whole plant containing mint oil (mainly menthol), and China is among the world's leading producers. Spearmint (*M. spicata*) contains carvone and is used in chewing gum and toothpaste.
13. *Ocimum*: basil (*O. basilicum*) is an annual herb with a 4/1-type two-lipped corolla whose lower lip points downward; the leaves are a seasoning in Western cooking and in Taiwanese cooking ("jiucengta").
14. *Perilla*: *P. frutescens* has purple or green leaves (white perilla); the leaves, stems and seeds are used in medicine, the seeds are pressed for oil, and the young leaves are a herb.
15. Others: *Lavandula*, *Thymus* and *Origanum* are spice and perfume plants; *Nepeta tenuifolia*, *Elsholtzia ciliata* and *Glechoma longituba* are used in medicine; *Lagopsis supina* flowers in early spring; *Coleus scutellarioides* is a foliage plant.

**Definition 48.5.1 (Staminal Lever)** In *Salvia* the filaments of the 2 stamens are very short, and the connective is greatly elongated and forms a movable joint where it meets the filament, becoming a lever: the upper arm is longer and bears at its tip a fertile anther locule hidden inside the upper lip of the corolla; the lower arm is shorter and bears at its tip a sterile locule, often enlarged into a plate that, together with the lower arm of the other stamen, blocks the mouth of the corolla tube. Such a stamen is called a **staminal lever**.

When a bee enters the corolla to take nectar, its head pushes the lower arm blocking the mouth of the tube, the lever turns around the joint, and the upper arm and the fertile anther locule swing down and dab pollen on the back of the bee (nototribic pollination); after the stamens have matured, the elongated style bends into the same position, so that the stigma touches the pollen on the back of the next visiting bee. The length of the lever and the position of the joint determine where the pollen is deposited on the bee's back and match the body size of the pollinator; molecular phylogenies show that the staminal lever arose independently at least 3 times in *Salvia* and its relatives (Walker and Sytsma 2007).

### 48.5.2. Verbenaceae, Acanthaceae and Bignoniaceae

Verbenaceae (verbena family, in the narrow sense) comprise about 32 genera and 800 species, mainly in the tropics and subtropics of the Americas. After the reorganization, only the group with racemose or spicate (indeterminate) inflorescences, a terminal style and an ovary that is not deeply lobed remains, with a drupe or a fruit splitting into 2–4 nutlets. Representative plants: vervain (*Verbena officinalis*) is a perennial herb with a square stem and long slender spikes that lengthen in fruit like a horse whip (hence the Chinese name "mabiancao"), and the whole plant is used in medicine; lantana (*Lantana camara*) is a prickly shrub with capitate inflorescences whose flower color changes from yellow to red, native to tropical America and a seriously harmful invasive plant in southern China and the tropics of the world, poisonous in all parts; *Duranta erecta*, *Phyla nodiflora* and lemon verbena (*Aloysia citriodora*) also belong to this family. Results of the reorganization of Verbenaceae: the subfamilies Viticoideae, Caryopteridoideae and Symphorematoideae of the former Verbenaceae moved to Lamiaceae, *Avicennia* moved to Acanthaceae, and *Phryma* to Phrymaceae; the Chinese textbook expresses this as "excluding Avicennioideae, Symphorematoideae, Viticoideae and Caryopteridoideae".

Acanthaceae (acanthus family) comprise about 220 genera and 4000 species (the Chinese textbook counts 36 genera and 306 species in China), mainly in the tropics. Diagnostic features: mostly herbs or shrubs with swollen nodes and opposite leaves, the leaf surface often with linear spots formed by cystoliths; conspicuous bracts and bracteoles outside the flower; corolla two-lipped or nearly actinomorphic, stamens 4 (didynamous) or 2; ovary superior, 2-locular; capsule club-shaped, loculicidal into 2 valves, the seeds borne on hook-like outgrowths of the funiculus (jaculators), which flick out the seeds when the fruit opens. Representative plants: *Justicia procumbens* is a small herb common at field margins; *Andrographis paniculata* is native to South Asia, the whole plant containing andrographolide, extremely bitter and used in medicine; the leaves of *Strobilanthes cusia* are processed into indigo (qingdai and lan dian) and the root is "southern banlangen", and many species of *Strobilanthes* flower synchronously after many years of growth and die after fruiting; *Acanthus ilicifolius* and *Avicennia marina* (formerly Verbenaceae) are mangrove plants, *Avicennia* having cryptovivipary and finger-like pneumatophores ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.4)). The decorative motif of the capital of the ancient Greek Corinthian order is the deeply lobed leaf of the Mediterranean *A. mollis*.

Bignoniaceae (trumpet creeper family) comprise about 80 genera and 800 species (the Chinese textbook counts 12 genera and 35 species in China), mainly in the tropics, mostly trees, shrubs or woody climbers. Diagnostic features: leaves opposite, often compound; flowers large, calyx campanulate, corolla campanulate or funnelform and often two-lipped; stamens 4 (didynamous), the 5th reduced to a short staminode; stigma 2-lobed and lamellate, closing when touched (sensitive stigma); ovary superior, 2-locular; capsule often long and horn-like or long and linear, seeds flat with a membranous wing. Representative plants: *Campsis grandiflora* (Chinese trumpet creeper) is a climbing vine that clings by aerial roots, with orange-red flowers, cultivated as an ornamental; *Catalpa ovata* and *C. bungei* are deciduous trees with large leaves and capsules as long and slender as bean pods, with fine wood; *Incarvillea* are herbs of the high mountains of southwestern and northwestern China; jacaranda (*Jacaranda mimosifolia*) and the African tulip tree (*Spathodea campanulata*) are tropical ornamental trees, the flowers of the sausage tree (*Kigelia africana*) open at night and are bat-pollinated, and the seeds of *Oroxylum indicum* are used in medicine.

### 48.5.3. Lentibulariaceae and Pedaliaceae

Lentibulariaceae (bladderwort family) are the carnivorous plants of Lamiales, with about 3 genera and 350 species: *Utricularia*, *Pinguicula* and *Genlisea* ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.3)). *Utricularia* are aquatic or wetland rootless herbs with leaves divided into filaments bearing many traps: the cells of the trap wall pump water out of the trap, creating a negative pressure inside, and the mouth of the trap has a valve controlled by trigger hairs; when a small animal touches the trigger hairs the valve opens inward in about 1 millisecond, water and prey are sucked in, and the valve closes at once, one of the fastest movements in plants. Terrestrial species such as *U. bifida* have traps in wet soil. The corolla is two-lipped with a spur at the base of the lower lip, and there are 2 stamens; capsule. The genome of *Utricularia* is very small (about 80 Mb in *U. gibba*) but has a similar number of genes to other plants, with very little noncoding DNA.

Pedaliaceae (sesame family) comprise about 13 genera and 70 species, from tropical Africa to Asia. Sesame (*Sesamum indicum*) is an annual herb with opposite leaves, solitary flowers in the leaf axils, and a tubular white or purple-tinged corolla that is indistinctly two-lipped; the capsule is long-cylindrical and dehisces when ripe (non-shattering mutants exist among cultivars). Sesame was domesticated on the Indian subcontinent and is one of the oldest oil crops; the seeds contain about 50% oil and lignans such as sesamin, which are antioxidants, so that sesame oil does not easily turn rancid.

## Questions

1. Why is the breakup of Scrophulariaceae said to be a typical example of "molecular systematics correcting traditional classification"? Which morphological characters misled the traditional classification?

   **Answer** The traditional Scrophulariaceae were defined by a two-lipped corolla, didynamous stamens, a superior 2-locular ovary, many ovules and a capsule, and looked very tidy; but chloroplast and nuclear gene sequences show that these plants belong to about ten lineages of Lamiales, some closer to Lamiaceae and Orobanchaceae and some closer to Plantaginaceae, so that the traditional Scrophulariaceae were a polyphyletic group. The characters that misled the classification were the floral characters related to pollination that appear repeatedly in Lamiales (two-lipped corolla, didynamous stamens) and the plesiomorphies shared by Lamiales (superior 2-locular ovary, capsule); they are homoplasies or shared ancestral characters and cannot demonstrate monophyly.

2. Lamiaceae, Scrophulariaceae (s.s.), Verbenaceae (s.s.) and Boraginaceae may all have "square stems and opposite leaves" or "four nutlets". How can they be told apart?

   **Answer** Lamiaceae: stem square, leaves opposite, aromatic oils present, verticillasters, corolla two-lipped, ovary deeply 4-lobed with a gynobasic style, 4 nutlets. Verbenaceae: leaves opposite, inflorescences mostly spikes or heads, style terminal, ovary not deeply lobed, drupe or nutlets. Scrophulariaceae: ovary not lobed, style terminal, capsule. Boraginaceae: leaves alternate, bristly, flowers regular, scorpioid cymes; although they have 4 nutlets and a gynobasic style, they belong to another order ([7.12.47](/7.12.47#sec-47.3)).

3. How does the staminal lever of *Salvia* work? What is its significance for pollination?

   **Answer** The connective of the stamen is elongated into a lever joined to the short filament by a joint; the long upper arm bears a fertile anther locule hidden in the upper lip, and the short lower arm bears a sterile locule that blocks the mouth of the corolla tube. When a bee enters the corolla it pushes the lower arm, and the upper arm swings down around the joint and dabs pollen on the bee's back; later the elongated style touches the pollen on the back of a visiting bee at the same position. In this way pollen is placed precisely at a particular position on the bee's back where it is not easily groomed off, cross-pollination is efficient and little pollen is wasted; the lever dimensions differ among species, so that pollen is deposited on different parts of the pollinator's body, which also reduces interspecific hybridization.

4. The *cyc dich* double mutant of snapdragon and the peloria mutant of toadflax both bear actinomorphic flowers; how do their genetic bases differ?

   **Answer** The double mutant of snapdragon carries mutations in the DNA sequences of the two genes *CYCLOIDEA* and *DICHOTOMA* (transposon insertions and so on), whose function is lost, so that the adaxial region of the flower loses its "adaxial identity". In the peloria of toadflax the sequence of *Lcyc* is unchanged, but it is heavily methylated and not expressed, an epimutation; this epiallele can be inherited stably and can also demethylate spontaneously in a few progeny, restoring the zygomorphic flower. Both cases show that expression of CYC-like genes in the adaxial region is necessary for the development of zygomorphic flowers.

5. Why is Orobanchaceae an ideal group for the study of the evolution of parasitic plants?

   **Answer** Within a single family, Orobanchaceae include all the transitional types from nonparasitic (*Rehmannia*) through facultative hemiparasitic (*Phtheirospermum*) and obligate hemiparasitic (*Pedicularis*, *Striga*) to holoparasitic (*Orobanche*, *Cistanche*), so that closely related species with different degrees of parasitism can be compared on the phylogenetic tree: the origin of the haustorium, the dependence on host signals (strigolactones), the loss of photosynthesis genes and of plastid genome content, and the multiple independent origins of holoparasitism (3 times) can all be traced within the same lineage.
