The rosids make up the largest branch of the superrosids and account for about a quarter of all angiosperm species; they include Vitales ([7.12.41](/7.12.41)) and the two great branches of fabids and malvids. This chapter and the next treat the fabids. This chapter covers Zygophyllales, which lies at the base of the fabids, the COM clade (Celastrales, Oxalidales, Malpighiales), whose position is disputed, and Fabales; [7.12.43](/7.12.43) covers Rosales, Cucurbitales and Fagales, which belong with Fabales to the nitrogen-fixing clade, and the malvids are in [7.12.44](/7.12.44). These orders are all products of molecular systematics: Malpighiales unites families that Cronquist's system scattered among Euphorbiales, Salicales, Violales, Rhizophorales, Linales, Theales and a dozen other orders, wind-pollinated and insect-pollinated, woody and herbaceous; Fabales puts Fabaceae and Polygalaceae together. Compared with Cronquist's system as used in Chinese textbooks, this chapter merges what were Euphorbiales, Salicales, part of Violales, Rhizophorales, Celastrales, Fabales and Zygophyllaceae of Sapindales. Fabaceae are the focus of the chapter; it is the third largest family of angiosperms and one of the economically most important. For how floral formulas are written, see [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.6).

## 42.1. Circumscription of the Rosids and Fabids

**Definition 42.1.1 (Rosids)** The monophyletic group of the superrosids that remains after removing Saxifragales, composed of Vitales, the fabids and the malvids, is called the **rosids**, with the phylogenetic name Rosidae (in the broad sense of the subclass Rosidae).

**Definition 42.1.2 (Fabids and the COM clade)** The clade of the rosids composed of Zygophyllales, Celastrales, Oxalidales, Malpighiales, Fabales, Rosales, Cucurbitales and Fagales is called the **fabids**, with the phylogenetic name Fabidae, formerly eurosids I. The clade within it composed of Celastrales, Oxalidales and Malpighiales is called the **COM clade** (named from the initial letters of the three orders).

The relationships within the fabids are as follows: Zygophyllales branch off first, and the remainder divide into the COM clade and the nitrogen-fixing clade (Fabales, Rosales, Cucurbitales, Fagales, [7.12.43](/7.12.43#sec-43.1)). The position of the COM clade is one of the best-known "nuclear–plastid conflicts" in angiosperm phylogeny at present: plastid genome data consistently place the COM clade in the fabids, as sister to the nitrogen-fixing clade, whereas analyses of many nuclear and mitochondrial genes place it in the malvids, and floral morphology (such as the mode of development of petals and stamens) also has characters supporting the latter relationship. One explanation is that the COM clade arose through ancient hybridization or plastid capture: its nuclear genome is more closely related to the ancestor of the malvids, but its plastids came from the ancestor of the fabids. APG IV follows the plastid result and places the COM clade in the fabids, noting that its position is uncertain.

## 42.2. Zygophyllales

Zygophyllales include Zygophyllaceae in the strict sense and Krameriaceae (*Krameria*, hemiparasitic shrubs of the arid regions of the Americas); the two differ greatly in form, and the conclusion that they form an order comes only from molecular evidence; it is the earliest-diverging branch of the fabids.

Zygophyllaceae have about 22 genera and 285 species, mainly in arid and saline regions from the tropics to the temperate zone; China has 3 genera and about 30 species, mainly in the northwest and north. Diagnostic characters: herbs, subshrubs or shrubs, often succulent; leaves opposite, rarely alternate, often even-pinnate or 2-foliolate, rarely simple; stipules paired, persistent, sometimes spinescent; flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, solitary or in cymes; sepals 4–5, petals 4–5, sometimes absent; stamens 1–3 times the number of petals, filaments often with scale-like appendages at the base; a disc present; ovary superior, usually of 5 fused carpels, 4–5-locular, with 1 to many ovules per locule and a single style; capsule or schizocarp, rarely a berry or drupe. Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{5+5} \underline{G}_{(5:5:1\text{–}\infty)}$.

Representative plants: puncture vine (*Tribulus terrestris*) is an annual prostrate herb with even-pinnate leaves and small yellow flowers; the fruit is a schizocarp of 5 mericarps, each with long and short spines that easily catch on animal fur and shoe soles for dispersal; it is distributed worldwide and the fruit is used medicinally. *Zygophyllum xanthoxylum* (placed by some authors in *Sarcozygium*) is a low desert shrub with 2-foliolate leaves with succulent, linear leaflets, occurring in Inner Mongolia, Gansu and Xinjiang, an important plant for wind-breaking and sand fixation; *Tetraena mongolica* is an ancient relict shrub endemic to China, occurring only in a small area of western Inner Mongolia. Creosote bush (*Larrea tridentata*) of the Americas is the dominant shrub of the deserts of southwestern North America, and ring-shaped clones formed by clonal propagation are estimated to have survived for over ten thousand years; the wood of *Guaiacum* (lignum vitae) is one of the heaviest and hardest commercial woods.

Changes in circumscription: in Chinese textbooks Zygophyllaceae are placed in Sapindales and include *Nitraria* and *Peganum*. Molecular evidence moves these two genera out: *Nitraria*, *Peganum* and others form Nitrariaceae, which belongs to Sapindales in the malvids ([7.12.44](/7.12.44)); Zygophyllaceae in the strict sense belong to Zygophyllales in the fabids. Siberian nitre bush (*Nitraria sibirica*, a drupe, a sand-fixing shrub of the deserts) and Syrian rue (*Peganum harmala*, a perennial herb whose seeds contain harmaline and can serve as a red dye) should both be treated under Nitrariaceae.

## 42.3. Celastrales

Celastrales include Celastraceae and Lepidobotryaceae (a small family of tropical Africa and America).

Celastraceae have about 100 genera and 1300 species, distributed from the tropics to the temperate zone; China has 14 genera and about 190 species. Diagnostic characters:

- Trees, shrubs or woody climbers, rarely herbs (*Parnassia*); twigs often green or four-angled, sometimes with corky wings.
- Leaves simple, opposite or alternate, often serrate; stipules small, early-falling or absent.
- Flowers small, bisexual or unisexual, actinomorphic, green, yellow-green or white, often in dichasial cymes; 4–5-merous; calyx and corolla 4–5 each.
- Stamens 4–5, alternating with the petals, inserted on or at the margin of a conspicuous, often fleshy disc.
- Ovary superior, often fused with the disc or sunk into it, of 2–5 fused carpels, 2–5-locular, with 2 ovules per locule (rarely 1 or many).
- Capsule, loculicidal, or a samara, drupe or berry; seeds often with a bright (red or orange) fleshy aril that attracts birds for dispersal.

Floral formula: $\ast K_{4\text{–}5} C_{4\text{–}5} A_{4\text{–}5} \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}5:2\text{–}5:2)}$.

Representative plants:

1. *Euonymus*: leaves opposite; flowers 4–5-merous, with a flat, fleshy disc; capsule 3–5-valved, seeds with an orange-red aril. About 130 species, about 90 in China. Winged spindle (*E. alatus*) has twigs with 2–4 broad corky wings, a capsule deeply 4-lobed and red autumn leaves, and the winged twigs are used medicinally; *E. maackii* is a small deciduous tree with ovate leaves and pink capsules, common in northern China; Japanese spindle (*E. japonicus*) is an evergreen shrub with leathery, bluntly serrate leaves that tolerates pruning and is one of the commonest hedge plants, called "large-leaved boxwood" in gardens, but it belongs to a different family from the boxwood of Buxaceae (opposite, entire leaves, [7.12.40](/7.12.40#sec-40.4.2)); winter creeper (*E. fortunei*) is an evergreen climbing shrub that climbs by aerial roots.
2. Oriental bittersweet (*Celastrus orbiculatus*): a deciduous vine-like shrub with alternate leaves; flowers 5-merous; capsule yellow, 3-valved, exposing the red aril; it occurs from northeastern China to the Yangtze basin and is a seriously damaging invasive plant in eastern North America.
3. Thunder god vine (*Tripterygium wilfordii*): a climbing shrub with 4–6-angled twigs densely covered with wart-like lenticels and rusty short hairs; flowers polygamous, 5-merous; samara with three membranous wings. It occurs south of the Yangtze; the root, stem and leaf are highly poisonous and were formerly used as an insecticide; the triptolide it contains has strong immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory effects and is used to treat rheumatoid arthritis and other diseases, but its toxicity is high and the dose must be strictly controlled.
4. *Maytenus* (the Chinese species are now mostly placed in *Gymnosporia*): spiny or unarmed shrubs; *G. hookeri* occurs in Yunnan; maytansine, isolated from African species of *Maytenus*, has strong antitumor activity, and its derivatives are cytotoxic components widely used in antibody–drug conjugates.
5. *Parnassia*: perennial herbs with long-petioled basal leaves and a single sessile leaf on the scape; flowers solitary, white, 5-merous, in front of each petal a staminode whose margin is divided into filaments tipped with glands (false nectaries, attracting flies), stamens 5; capsule. *P. palustris* and *P. foliosa* grow in wet meadows and alpine regions of the north temperate zone. In Cronquist's system *Parnassia* belongs to Saxifragaceae (it was also separated as Parnassiaceae), and molecular evidence places it in Celastraceae ([7.12.41](/7.12.41#sec-41.2.6)).
6. Others: the leaves of khat (*Catha edulis*) contain cathinone and are chewed as a stimulant in East Africa and the Arabian Peninsula; *Loeseneriella* was formerly the family Hippocrateaceae and was included in Celastraceae in APG IV.

Key characters of the family: mostly woody, with simple leaves. Flowers small, green or white, often in cymes; a conspicuous disc, with the stamens inserted on it; ovary often fused with the disc. Capsule, seeds often with a red aril.

Changes in circumscription: Cronquist's Celastrales also included Aquifoliaceae and Icacinaceae; Aquifoliaceae now belong to Aquifoliales in the asterids ([7.12.49](/7.12.49)) and Icacinaceae to Icacinales; Aquifoliaceae (*Ilex*, holly) placed under Celastrales in Chinese textbooks should be treated under Aquifoliales.

## 42.4. Oxalidales

Oxalidales have 7 families: Oxalidaceae, Connaraceae, Cunoniaceae, Elaeocarpaceae, Cephalotaceae, Brunelliaceae and Huaceae, with about 2000 species, mainly in the Southern Hemisphere and the tropics. The order has no obvious morphological synapomorphy; commonly found characters are leaves with teeth (each tooth bearing a gland), 5-merous flowers and 10 stamens. *Cephalotus follicularis* is a carnivorous plant endemic to southwestern Australia whose leaves are modified into lidded pitcher traps, convergent with the pitchers of Nepenthaceae and Sarraceniaceae ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.3)).

Oxalidaceae have about 6 genera and 800 species, of which *Oxalis* has about 700; China has 3 genera and about 10 species. Herbs, rarely shrubs or trees (*Averrhoa*); the plants contain oxalic acid (the English name oxalic acid derives from *Oxalis*); leaves alternate or basal, palmately ternate (*Oxalis*) or pinnate, the leaflets drooping and closing at night or when touched; flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, 5-merous; sepals 5, petals 5, contorted in the bud; stamens 10 in two whorls, the filaments fused at the base; ovary superior, of 5 fused carpels, 5-locular, with 5 free styles; capsule, the seeds with an elastic aril that ejects the seed when it turns inside out. Many species show **heterostyly**; in *Oxalis* there is "tristyly," with stamens and styles each in three lengths, classic material studied by Darwin ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3)). Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_{(5+5)} \underline{G}_{(5:5:\infty)}$.

Representative plants: creeping woodsorrel (*Oxalis corniculata*) is a small creeping herb with yellow flowers and a cylindrical capsule, distributed worldwide and one of the commonest weeds of gardens and roadsides; pink woodsorrel (*O. debilis* subsp. *corymbosa*) has bulbs and rose-red flowers, is native to South America and commonly escapes in southern China; purple shamrock (*O. triangularis*) has purple leaves and is grown as ground cover. Carambola (*Averrhoa carambola*) is a tree with odd-pinnate leaves and a berry with 5 sharp ridges, star-shaped in cross-section; it is native to Southeast Asia and cultivated in southern China.

Elaeocarpaceae have about 12 genera and 600 species, mainly in the tropics. Trees; leaves simple, often serrate; petals often lacerated into a fringe at the tip; stamens numerous, anthers opening by apical pores; drupe or capsule. In *Elaeocarpus*, the old leaves turn bright red one by one before falling, so there are always a few red leaves in the evergreen crown; *E. decipiens* and *E. sylvestris* are used as street trees in the south; the capsule of *Sloanea sinensis* is densely spiny and, on opening, shows red valves and black seeds. In Cronquist's system Elaeocarpaceae belong to Malvales (close to Tiliaceae); molecular evidence places them in Oxalidales, while *Muntingia* of the former Tiliaceae is moved to Malvales.

Cunoniaceae are mainly Southern Hemisphere (New Zealand, Australia, South America) and an important component of the temperate rainforests of the Southern Hemisphere; *Bauera* of the former broad Saxifragaceae is included in this family.

## 42.5. Malpighiales

### 42.5.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Malpighiales have 36 families and about 16,000 species, about 8% of the eudicots, one of the largest orders in APG IV and a main component of the understory and lower canopy of tropical rainforests. Its members differ enormously in form: wind-pollinated willows and poplars without a perianth, insect-pollinated, spurred violets, the genus *Euphorbia* with inflorescences that closely resemble a flower, climbing passion flowers, viviparous mangroves, riverweeds (Podostemaceae) that grow on rocks in rushing streams, and *Rafflesia*, with flowers up to 1 m in diameter and a body without leaves or roots. Malpighiales have almost no morphological synapomorphies (possible ones are a gland or an opaque seta at the tip of each marginal tooth of the leaf, the "violoid tooth" or "salicoid tooth," and a thick inner integument); the order is a group defined entirely by molecular evidence, and the relationships among its families were largely resolved only after large-scale plastid-genome and nuclear-gene analyses, with the position of many families still unstable.

### 42.5.2. Euphorbiaceae

Euphorbiaceae in the strict sense have about 220 genera and 6000 species, mainly in the tropics; China has about 50 genera and 250 species. *Euphorbia* has about 2000 species and is one of the largest genera of angiosperms.

Diagnostic characters:

- Trees, shrubs or herbs, or succulent, leafless plants that resemble cacti (*Euphorbia*); the plants often have white latex (**laticifers**), and some lack latex and have red or colorless sap.
- Leaves alternate, rarely opposite, simple, rarely compound (trifoliolate in *Hevea*) or reduced; stipules present; a pair of glands often at the tip of the petiole or at the leaf base.
- Flowers unisexual, monoecious or dioecious; in racemes, spikes, cymes or cyathia; perianth in two whorls, one whorl or absent, a disc or glands often present.
- Male flowers: stamens 1 to many, filaments free or fused (in *Ricinus* the filaments are repeatedly branched).
- Female flowers: ovary superior, usually of 3 fused carpels, 3-locular, with 1 pendulous anatropous ovule per locule (an obturator above the ovule guides the pollen tube); styles 3, often bifid again.
- Fruit a capsule, at maturity the 3 carpels (cocci) split away elastically from the central axis, leaving a persistent columella; a few are drupes or berries. Seeds often with a caruncle, endosperm abundant, often oily.

Floral formula: ♂ $\ast K_{0\text{–}5} C_{0\text{–}5} A_{1\text{–}\infty}$; ♀ $\ast K_{0\text{–}5} C_{0\text{–}5} \underline{G}_{(3:3:1)}$.

The cyathium. The "flower" of *Euphorbia* is in fact a highly reduced inflorescence ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.7.3)) called a **cyathium**: on the outside is a cup-shaped involucre formed by the fusion of 5 bracts, with 4–5 thick glands at its margin (some glands bear petal-like appendages), alternating with the involucral lobes; inside the involucre are several male flowers, each with a single stamen and no perianth, with a joint between the filament and the pedicel, which shows that it is a flower and not a stamen; in the center of the involucre is a single female flower, also without a perianth (or with an extremely reduced one), consisting of a long-stalked 3-carpellate pistil that often extends out of the involucre and hangs down at anthesis (see the figure below). The whole cyathium looks and functions like a bisexual flower: the glands secrete nectar and attract insects, and the "stamens" and the "pistil" are inside the involucre; the bright red "petals" of poinsettia are bracts beneath the inflorescence. The cyathium is a famous example for the **pseudanthial theory** ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)), but it is a pseudanthium formed secondarily within *Euphorbia* and does not show that the angiosperm flower originated from an inflorescence.

Representative plants:

1. *Euphorbia*: herbs, shrubs or succulents with white latex; cyathia; capsule. About 80 species in China. Sun spurge (*E. helioscopia*) is an annual herb with 5 whorled leaf-like bracts at the top of the stem above which the cyathia are arranged in a many-branched cyme; it is widespread in fields and roadsides throughout China, and the whole plant is poisonous and used medicinally; *E. pekinensis* has a root used medicinally; leafy spurge (*E. esula*), *E. humifusa* (prostrate, with opposite leaves, common in cracks in paving) and spotted spurge (*E. maculata*, with purple spots on the leaf, native to North America) are common weeds; poinsettia (*E. pulcherrima*) is native to Mexico, with vermilion bracts below the inflorescence, a winter ornamental; crown of thorns (*E. milii*) has spiny stems and is native to Madagascar. Many succulent spurges of the deserts of Africa and Madagascar (such as pencil tree *E. tirucalli*), as well as *E. royleana* from the Himalaya to southwestern China, have succulent, ridged, spiny stems and reduced leaves and strongly resemble the cacti of the Americas, a classic example of **convergent evolution**; the two can be distinguished by latex: spurges have white latex and Cactaceae do not.
2. *Vernicia*: deciduous trees with latex; leaves entire or 3–5-lobed, with 2 glands at the tip of the petiole; flowers unisexual, monoecious, petals 5, white with red stripes; drupe large. Tung tree (*V. fordii*) has a smooth fruit wall and occurs south of the Yangtze; the kernel contains 50%–70% oil, from which tung oil is pressed, an excellent drying oil used in paints, coatings and waterproofing, a traditional specialty product of China; *V. montana* has fruits with 3 longitudinal ridges and net-like wrinkles and occurs mainly in the Pearl River basin.
3. Castor bean (*Ricinus communis*): a stout annual herb (a small tree in the tropics) with peltate leaves palmately 5–11-lobed; flowers unisexual, monoecious, without petals, the filaments of the male flowers repeatedly branched; capsule with soft spines; seeds with a conspicuous caruncle and a smooth, mottled seed coat. It is native to East Africa and cultivated everywhere; the seeds contain about 50% oil, and castor oil is an important industrial lubricant and laxative; the seeds contain the highly toxic ricin, which inactivates ribosomes, and a few seeds can be fatal.
4. Chinese tallow tree (*Triadica sebifera*, formerly *Sapium sebiferum*): a deciduous tree with latex; leaves rhombic with 2 glands at the tip of the petiole, brilliant red in autumn; flowers unisexual, monoecious, without petals, in spikes; capsule, the seeds coated with a white wax layer. It occurs south of the Qinling–Huai River line and has been cultivated for more than a thousand years; the wax layer of the seeds (*piyou*) was formerly used to make candles and soap, and the kernel oil is an industrial oil; in the southeastern United States it is a seriously damaging invasive tree.
5. Rubber tree (*Hevea brasiliensis*): a tall tree with abundant white latex; leaves trifoliolate with glands at the tip of the petiole; flowers small, unisexual, monoecious; capsule large, 3-locular. It is native to the Amazon basin; in 1876 Wickham brought tens of thousands of seeds to Kew, and the seedlings raised there were sent to Sri Lanka and the Malay Peninsula, after which Southeast Asia became the main region of natural rubber production; it is cultivated in Hainan, Xishuangbanna in Yunnan, and Taiwan. When the bark is cut, latex flows from the laticifers and coagulates to give raw rubber.
6. Cassava (*Manihot esculenta*): a shrub with palmately 3–7-lobed leaves; the tuberous roots are thick and rich in starch, the most important starch crop in the tropics after rice and maize. It is native to South America; the roots contain cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin) and must be peeled, soaked or heated to remove the toxin before eating.
7. Others: physic nut (*Jatropha curcas*, native to the Americas) has oily seeds that can serve as a biodiesel feedstock; croton (*Croton tiglium*) has seeds that yield croton oil, highly toxic and a strong purgative; *Mallotus apelta* has leaves white beneath and oily seeds; *Acalypha australis* is a common field weed whose female flowers are borne inside a shell-like bract; croton plant (*Codiaeum variegatum*) has variable leaf shape and color and is a common foliage plant; *Excoecaria agallocha* is a common associate tree in mangrove forests.

Key characters of the family: often with latex. Glands often at the tip of the petiole or at the leaf base. Flowers unisexual; ovary superior, often 3-locular with 1 pendulous ovule per locule and 3 styles. Capsule, splitting elastically into 3 cocci; seeds often with a caruncle.

### 42.5.3. Phyllanthaceae and Other Families Separated from Euphorbiaceae

Traditional Euphorbiaceae (in the broad sense) were divided into 5 subfamilies, and molecular evidence shows that they are polyphyletic: the two subfamilies with 2 ovules per locule and the three subfamilies with 1 ovule per locule belong to different branches of Malpighiales. APG separated the groups with 2 ovules per locule as Phyllanthaceae, Putranjivaceae and Picrodendraceae, and also separated Peraceae and Pandaceae, leaving in Euphorbiaceae in the strict sense only the groups with 1 ovule per locule.

Phyllanthaceae have about 60 genera and 2000 species, mainly in the tropics; China has about 15 genera and 130 species. The main differences from Euphorbiaceae are: no latex; 2 ovules per locule; capsule, drupe or berry; leaves mostly simple, distichous, entire. In many species of *Phyllanthus* the branches are of two kinds: on the main branches the leaves are reduced to scales, and the lateral twigs bear two rows of small leaves, so that the whole twig looks like a pinnate leaf and falls off entire in winter ("phyllanthoid branching"), the flowers borne in the axils of the leaves on the underside of the twig. *P. urinaria* is an annual herb whose small, round capsules are arranged beneath the twig like a string of beads (hence the Chinese name "pearl under the leaf"), and the whole plant is used medicinally; amla (*P. emblica*) is a tree with spherical fruits that taste sour and astringent at first and sweet afterward, occurring from southern to southwestern China, and the fruit is eaten and used medicinally (called amla in Ayurvedic medicine). *Bischofia polycarpa* is a deciduous tree with trifoliolate leaves and a berry, occurring south of the Qinling–Huai River line and often used as a street tree; *B. javanica* is evergreen and occurs in southern China. *Glochidion* (capsule oblate, with longitudinal grooves like abacus beads; molecular evidence supports including it in *Phyllanthus*), *Sauropus androgynus* (young leaves eaten as a vegetable, called "sweet leaf"), and *Breynia* also belong to this family.

*Drypetes* and *Putranjiva* of Putranjivaceae contain glucosinolates, which are extremely rare outside Brassicales of the malvids.

### 42.5.4. Salicaceae

Salicaceae in APG IV are circumscribed broadly: besides Salicaceae in the strict sense (*Populus* and *Salix*), they include most genera of the former Flacourtiaceae, with about 55 genera and 1200 species, distributed worldwide. A common feature of Salicaceae in the broad sense is the "salicoid tooth" on the leaf margin (each tooth ending in an opaque, deciduous, seta-like gland), and the plants contain phenolic glycosides such as salicin. China has 13 genera and about 350 species, of which *Populus* and *Salix* make up about 330.

Diagnostic characters of Salicaceae in the strict sense (*Populus* and *Salix*):

- Deciduous trees or shrubs; winter buds with bud scales.
- Leaves simple, alternate, with stipules; leaf margin serrate, often with glands at the tip of the petiole (*Populus*).
- Flowers unisexual, dioecious, rarely monoecious; in catkins, often opening before the leaves; each flower in the axil of a membranous bract; no perianth, but with a cup-shaped disc derived from a reduced perianth (*Populus*) or 1–2 glands (*Salix*).
- Male flowers: stamens 2 to many.
- Female flowers: ovary superior, of 2 fused carpels, 1-locular, with 2–4 parietal placentas and numerous ovules; stigmas 2, often bifid.
- Capsule, 2–4-valved; seeds small, with numerous long white silky hairs growing from the funicle at the base, no endosperm.

Floral formula: *Populus* ♂ $K_0 C_0 A_{4\text{–}\infty}$; ♀ $K_0 C_0 \underline{G}_{(2:1:\infty)}$; *Salix* ♂ $K_0 C_0 A_2$; ♀ $K_0 C_0 \underline{G}_{(2:1:\infty)}$.

Representative plants:

1. *Populus*: about 100 species, about 70 in China, the main afforestation and shelterbelt trees of the north. *P. tomentosa* has grayish-white bark with rhombic lenticels, triangular-ovate leaves densely felted beneath, is endemic to China, and is the commonest street and timber tree of the North China Plain; white poplar (*P. alba*) has leaves palmately 3–5-shallowly lobed and densely silvery-felted beneath, occurs in Xinjiang and is cultivated everywhere; *P. simonii* has rhombic-ovate, smaller leaves, is widespread from northeastern to southwestern China and is an important afforestation tree; Euphrates poplar (*P. euphratica*) is the foundation species of the riparian forests of deserts, with leaves on young branches linear like those of willow and leaves on old branches broadly ovate and toothed on the same tree (**heterophylly**, [7.12.23](/7.12.23)), tolerant of salinity and drought, with roots that extend along the groundwater, and the Tarim River basin has the largest Euphrates poplar forests in the world; Canadian poplar (*P.* × *canadensis*) and various hybrid poplars are fast-growing plantation trees. Black cottonwood (*P. trichocarpa*) was the first tree to have its genome sequenced (2006) and is a model plant for the study of wood formation.
2. *Salix*: about 450 species, from trees to prostrate dwarf shrubs only a few centimeters tall in the Arctic and the mountains, about 260 in China. Weeping willow (*S. babylonica*) has long, slender, drooping branches and narrow lanceolate leaves and female flowers with 1 gland, the commonest ornamental tree by water; *S. matsudana* has erect branches and female flowers with 2 glands and occurs from northeastern China to the Yangtze basin, and the corkscrew willow (*S. matsudana* f. *tortuosa*) is a form with twisted branches; *S. sinopurpurea* has young branches purple-red, leaves nearly opposite, and 2 stamens with fused filaments; *S. integra* and *S. linearistipularis* have pliable branches used for weaving baskets and also for windbreak and sand fixation. Salicin in willow bark is converted in the body to salicylic acid and was the starting point for the development of aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid).
3. Genera of the former Flacourtiaceae: *Idesia polycarpa* (a deciduous tree with large, cordate leaves, pendulous panicles and clusters of red berries, with oily seeds), *Xylosma congesta* (an evergreen shrub or small tree with spiny branches and hard wood), *Poliothyrsis sinensis*, *Carrierea calycina*, *Flacourtia indica*, *Homalium cochinchinense* and *Casearia balansae*. Most have a perianth (sepals and petals), bisexual or unisexual flowers and parietal placentation and look very different from willows and poplars, but they share the salicoid leaf teeth and the phenolic glycosides.

Economic importance: Salicaceae are the most important fast-growing timber, paper-making, shelterbelt and greening trees of the north temperate zone; *Populus* and *Salix* are easily propagated by cuttings. The "poplar fluff" and "willow fluff" of spring are the long hairs on the seeds of female trees and are not pollen, so male trees are mostly chosen for urban greening, or inhibitors are injected.

Key characters of the family (strict sense): woody, with simple, alternate leaves with stipules. Flowers unisexual, dioecious, in catkins; no perianth, a disc or glands present; ovary 1-locular with parietal placentation. Capsule; seeds small with numerous long silky hairs at the base.

Changes in circumscription: in Engler's system Salicaceae were placed among the "amentiferous" groups, wind pollination, absence of a perianth and unisexual flowers being regarded as primitive; Cronquist and Chinese textbooks, on the basis of parietal placentation and numerous ovules, placed Salicales in the Dilleniidae, close to Violales and Flacourtiaceae, and held that Salicaceae differentiated from the primitive "apocarpous" groups, became dioecious after taking the path to wind pollination, and *Salix* then secondarily turned to insect pollination, with the perianth transformed into nectaries. Molecular evidence supports the part of the latter view that holds willows and poplars to be related to Flacourtiaceae, and further includes most of Flacourtiaceae in Salicaceae; but Salicaceae are not closely related to Fagales, Juglandaceae and the other "amentiferous" groups, whose catkins evolved independently. *Hydnocarpus* (chaulmoogra, whose oil was long the main drug against leprosy) and *Gynocardia* of the former Flacourtiaceae, which contain chaulmoogric acid, are placed in another family, Achariaceae.

### 42.5.5. Violaceae

Violaceae have about 25 genera and 1000 species, of which *Viola* has about 600, distributed from the temperate zone to the tropics; China has 3 genera and about 110 species.

Diagnostic characters:

- Perennial herbs, rarely shrubs or trees (tropical species).
- Leaves simple, alternate or basal, with stipules, sometimes large and leaf-like.
- Flowers bisexual, zygomorphic, solitary or in inflorescences.
- Sepals 5, persistent, often with appendages at the base.
- Petals 5, the lower (abaxial) one larger and extended at the base into a spur.
- Stamens 5, filaments short, the anthers connivent or adhering in a ring around the ovary, the connective with a membranous appendage at the tip; the two lower stamens each have a slender nectary at the base of the connective that extends into the spur of the petal, and the nectar is stored in the spur.
- Ovary superior, of 3 fused carpels, 1-locular, with 3 parietal placentas and numerous anatropous ovules; style single, the stigma variable in shape.
- Capsule, 3-valved, the valves contracting inward on drying and ejecting the seeds; seeds often with an elaiosome (attracting ants for dispersal) and fleshy endosperm.

Floral formula: $\uparrow K_5 C_5 A_5 \underline{G}_{(3:1:\infty)}$.

Many species of *Viola* have two kinds of flowers: the flowers that open in spring are large and have petals and are cross-pollinated by insects; those produced in summer and autumn are small, with reduced petals, and do not open, self-fertilizing in the bud and setting abundant seed, a process called **cleistogamy** ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3)). This "double insurance" mode of reproduction ensures seed production when conditions are unfavorable. The adaptation of the pansy flower to pollination is highly refined: the stripes on the petals point to the entrance of the spur (nectar guides), the spur has papillate hairs, the stigma is capitate with a flap-covered stigmatic opening beneath it; when an insect puts its proboscis into the spur to take nectar, pollen on the proboscis is scraped by the flap into the stigmatic opening, and when the proboscis is withdrawn the flap turns over and keeps the flower's own pollen out, thereby avoiding self-pollination.

Representative plants: *Viola philippica* is a small perennial herb with no aerial stem, basal narrowly ovate leaves and purple flowers, occurring throughout China, and the whole plant is used medicinally to clear heat and detoxify; wild pansy (*V. tricolor*) is native to Europe and has flowers of blue, yellow and white, and the large-flowered garden pansy (*V.* × *wittrockiana*) is a common early-spring flower; sweet violet (*V. odorata*) has fragrant purple flowers from which perfume can be extracted; *V. diffusa* has stolons and the whole plant is used medicinally.

Key characters of the family: herbs. Leaves simple, with stipules. Flowers zygomorphic, 5-merous, the lower petal spurred; stamens 5, anthers connivent; ovary superior, 1-locular, with 3 parietal placentas. Capsule 3-valved.

### 42.5.6. Passifloraceae

Passifloraceae in APG IV include the former Turneraceae and Malesherbiaceae, with about 27 genera and 1000 species, mainly in tropical America and Africa. *Passiflora* has about 550 species and consists of herbaceous or woody climbers climbing by axillary tendrils (the tendrils are part of a modified inflorescence axis); leaves alternate, often glandular; flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, with 5 sepals and 5 petals, and between the perianth and the stamens one to several whorls of filamentous or scale-like coronal appendages, brightly colored; the 5 stamens and the ovary borne on an elongated androgynophore; ovary superior, 3 carpels, 1-locular, with parietal placentation; berry. Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_5 \underline{G}_{(3:1:\infty)}$ (with corona and androgynophore). Passion fruit (*P. edulis*), native to Brazil, has a juicy, fragrant aril and is widely cultivated in southern China; blue passion flower (*P. caerulea*) is cultivated as an ornamental; stinking passion flower (*P. foetida*) has pinnately divided, glandular-hairy bracts, is native to the Americas and has escaped in southern China. The leaves of *Passiflora* contain cyanogenic glycosides and are the exclusive food plants of the larvae of *Heliconius* butterflies; the two are a classic system for the study of **coevolution** of plants and herbivorous insects: nectaries on the leaves of passion flowers attract ants, and in some species there are protuberances on the petiole that resemble butterfly eggs, so that a female butterfly mistakenly thinks eggs are already present and does not lay on the plant. *Turnera ulmifolia* has yellow flowers that open in the early morning and close at noon and is often grown in tropical gardens.

### 42.5.7. Hypericaceae and Clusiaceae

Hypericaceae have about 9 genera and 600 species, of which *Hypericum* has about 500, distributed throughout the temperate zone. Herbs or shrubs; leaves opposite, entire, often with translucent or black glandular dots (containing hypericin and others); flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, 5-merous, yellow; stamens numerous, the filaments fused at the base into 3–5 bundles (polyadelphous); ovary superior, 3–5 carpels; capsule. *H. monogynum* is an evergreen subshrub with golden flowers whose numerous long stamens look like golden threads and is often cultivated in the south; St. John's wort (*H. perforatum*) has leaves with translucent glands, and its extract is used to treat mild depression, but the hypericin it contains makes people and livestock photosensitive; *H. sampsonii* has opposite leaves joined at the base so that the stem passes through them. Hypericaceae were formerly the subfamily Hypericoideae of Clusiaceae.

Clusiaceae (Guttiferae) in the strict sense have about 14 genera and 800 species, mainly in the tropics. Trees or shrubs with yellow or white resin; leaves opposite, entire, often leathery; flowers often unisexual; stamens numerous; berry, drupe or capsule. Mangosteen (*Garcinia mangostana*) is a famous tropical fruit of Southeast Asia, whose edible part is the white aril, the purple shell being the pericarp; the resin of *G. hanburyi* is the traditional yellow pigment gamboge; *G. oblongifolia* occurs in southern China. *Calophyllum inophyllum* (a coastal windbreak tree) and *Mesua ferrea* have been separated from Clusiaceae as Calophyllaceae.

### 42.5.8. Rhizophoraceae

Rhizophoraceae have about 16 genera and 150 species, distributed in the Old World and New World tropics; China has 6 genera and about 13 species, occurring from southern to southwestern China, of which *Rhizophora*, *Bruguiera*, *Kandelia* and *Ceriops* grow in the intertidal zone of the coast and are the main components of mangrove forests, while the rest grow in inland mountain forests.

Diagnostic characters: evergreen trees or shrubs; leaves simple, opposite, leathery, entire, with interpetiolar stipules (early-falling, leaving a ring scar at the node); flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, solitary or in cymes; sepals 4–5 (rarely 3–16), fused at the base into a tube, persistent; petals as many as the sepals, often divided or with appendages; stamens twice the number of petals or numerous, often enclosed in pairs by the petals; ovary inferior or half-inferior, rarely superior, 2–6-locular, with often 2 ovules per locule; fruit leathery or fleshy, often indehiscent. Floral formula (*Rhizophora*): $\ast K_4 C_4 A_{8\text{–}12} \overline{\underline{G}}_{(2:2:2)}$.

Mangrove Rhizophoraceae have a series of structures adapted to the intertidal zone: downward-curving prop roots growing from trunk and branches into the mud, supporting the plant on soft silt (*Rhizophora*); knee-shaped or peg-shaped pneumatophores growing from horizontal roots above the mud surface, with many lenticels, connected to well-developed aerenchyma inside the root (*Bruguiera*); and leathery leaves with the ability to excrete or accumulate salt ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.4)). The most distinctive is viviparity ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)): after the fruit matures it remains on the mother tree, and the seed germinates in the fruit without dormancy, the hypocotyl elongating, breaking through the pericarp, and growing into a club-shaped or spindle-shaped seedling (propagule) up to several tens of centimeters long; when mature, the propagule drops from the mother tree and plunges by its own weight into the mud, growing lateral roots that anchor it within a few hours, or it floats in the sea water and strands and settles on a suitable beach (see the figure below). Viviparity means that the seedling already has considerable size and stored reserves when it lands and can root quickly despite the scouring of the tides.

Representative plants: *Rhizophora apiculata* occurs in Hainan, with well-developed prop roots, 4 sepals and 4 petals; *R. stylosa* occurs along the coast of southern China; oriental mangrove (*Bruguiera gymnorhiza*) has knee-shaped pneumatophores and 10–14 red sepals; *Kandelia obovata* (formerly confused with *K. candel*) is the most cold-tolerant mangrove plant, with a natural distribution reaching north to Fuding in Fujian (about 27°N), and it has been introduced to Zhejiang; *Ceriops tagal*. Mangrove forests are wave-breaking and embankment-protecting forests of tropical and subtropical coasts and also important habitats of fish, shrimps, crabs and water birds; the mangrove forests of China are mainly at Dongzhaigang in Hainan, Shankou in Guangxi, Zhanjiang in Guangdong and Futian in Shenzhen. Mangrove forests also contain mangrove plants of other families, such as gray mangrove (*Avicennia marina*, Acanthaceae), *Sonneratia* (Lythraceae), river mangrove (*Aegiceras corniculatum*, Primulaceae) and *Excoecaria* (Euphorbiaceae); they belong to different orders, and their similarity is convergence to the intertidal environment.

Changes in circumscription: Rhizophoraceae were separated as Rhizophorales in Cronquist's system as used in Chinese textbooks, and Engler placed them in Myrtales. Myrtales almost all have internal phloem, poorly developed stipules and little endosperm in the seed, whereas Rhizophoraceae lack internal phloem, have large interpetiolar stipules and abundant endosperm, so Cronquist set them apart; molecular evidence places them in Malpighiales, sister to Erythroxylaceae (*Erythroxylum*; the leaves of coca, *E. coca*, contain cocaine).

### 42.5.9. Linaceae

Linaceae have about 14 genera and 250 species, widely distributed. Herbs or shrubs; leaves simple, alternate or opposite, entire; flowers bisexual, actinomorphic, 5-merous, the petals early-falling, stamens 5 (alternating with staminodes), fused at the base; ovary superior, 3–5 carpels, each locule divided by a false septum into 2 chambers with 1 ovule per chamber; capsule. Floral formula: $\ast K_5 C_5 A_5 \underline{G}_{(5:10:1)}$. Flax (*Linum usitatissimum*) is one of the oldest cultivated plants, with a history of cultivation of about nine thousand years in the Near East: fiber flax has tall plants with few branches and long, tough phloem **fibers** in the stem ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)), woven into linen, already used in ancient Egypt to wrap mummies; oil flax (linseed) has short plants with many branches and seeds containing about 40% oil, and linseed oil is rich in α-linolenic acid and used as an edible oil in northwestern China.

### 42.5.10. Rafflesiaceae

Rafflesiaceae have 3 genera and about 50 species: *Rafflesia*, *Sapria* and *Rhizanthes*, in Southeast Asia. They are completely parasitic plants: there are no roots, stems or leaves and no chloroplasts, and the vegetative body is only cell filaments, resembling fungal hyphae, living inside the roots and stems of *Tetrastigma* (Vitaceae); only the flower grows out through the bark of the host before opening. The flower of *Rafflesia arnoldii* can be up to 1 m in diameter, the largest single flower among the angiosperms; the perianth is thick and fleshy, reddish brown with white spots, and gives off the smell of rotting flesh, and it is pollinated by blowflies. *Sapria himalayana* occurs in southeastern Tibet and southern Yunnan. The systematic position of Rafflesiaceae was long hard to determine because they have no chloroplast genes to compare; analyses of mitochondrial and nuclear genes place them in Malpighiales, close to Euphorbiaceae (Davis et al. 2007), whose flowers are usually very small, which shows that the gigantic flowers of *Rafflesia* evolved from an ancestor with small flowers, the flower diameter having increased about 80-fold during evolution. The mitochondrial genome of *Rafflesia* also contains many genes "stolen" from its host *Tetrastigma*, an example of horizontal gene transfer between plants. Cronquist placed them with Mitrastemonaceae, Hydnoraceae and other parasitic plants in Rafflesiales (Balanophoraceae were in Santalales); Mitrastemonaceae, also completely parasitic, now belong to Ericales, and Apodanthaceae to Cucurbitales ([7.12.43](/7.12.43)), so parasitism arose independently in these groups.

### 42.5.11. Other Families of Malpighiales

- Malpighiaceae: about 70 genera and 1300 species, mainly in tropical America, mostly woody climbers, with paired oil glands on the sepals and clawed petals, pollinated by oil-collecting bees; the fruit of acerola (*Malpighia emarginata*, West Indian cherry) has an extremely high vitamin C content; *Banisteriopsis caapi* is one of the sources of a hallucinogenic drink of the Amazon.
- Chrysobalanaceae: tropical trees, often placed in Rosaceae as a subfamily (having a floral cup and numerous stamens), but the ovary is borne on one side of the floral cup with a basal style, and they lack the cyanogenic glycosides of Rosaceae.
- Achariaceae: includes *Hydnocarpus* and *Gynocardia* (§42.5.4).
- Ochnaceae: in *Ochna* the receptacle enlarges and turns red in fruit and bears several black drupes.
- Podostemaceae: aquatic herbs on the surface of rocks in tropical torrents, the plant body flat like a moss or lichen and extremely specialized in form; molecular evidence places them in Malpighiales, close to Hypericaceae.
- Small tropical families such as Ctenolophonaceae, Humiriaceae and Ixonanthaceae.

## 42.6. Fabales

### 42.6.1. Characters and Circumscription of the Order

Fabales include four families, Fabaceae, Polygalaceae, Quillajaceae and Surianaceae, with about 20,000 species. These four families have never been put together in morphological classification: Polygalaceae, because of their zygomorphic flowers, were often placed in Polygalales, *Quillaja* in Rosaceae, and Surianaceae in Sapindales or Rosales. The relationships among the four families are still unstable in molecular analyses. A possible morphological synapomorphy of Fabales is clawed petals and rather free carpels; in the nitrogen-fixing clade, the order is sister to Rosales + (Cucurbitales + Fagales) ([7.12.43](/7.12.43#sec-43.1)).

### 42.6.2. Fabaceae

Fabaceae (Leguminosae) have about 770 genera and 19,500 species, the third largest family of angiosperms after Asteraceae and Orchidaceae and the second most important family economically after Poaceae. They are distributed worldwide, mostly woody in the tropics and mostly herbaceous in the temperate zone; China has about 170 genera and 1600 species.

Diagnostic characters:

- Trees, shrubs, climbers or herbs; roots often with **root nodules** ([7.12.20](/7.12.20#sec-20.6.1)).
- Leaves alternate, often once or twice pinnately compound or ternate, rarely simple or reduced (phyllodes); stipules present, the leaflets of pinnate leaves often with stipels; the bases of the petiole and petiolules are often swollen into pulvini, in which changes in the turgor of motor cells allow the leaflets to open by day and fold at night (nyctinasty) or to close on touch (the seismonastic movement of *Mimosa*), a mechanism that belongs to plant physiology.
- Flowers bisexual, actinomorphic or zygomorphic, 5-merous; calyx 5, often fused; petals 5, free or fused in the lower part.
- Stamens usually 10, free or fused as monadelphous or diadelphous ($A_{(9)+1}$), or numerous (mimosoids).
- Gynoecium of 1 carpel, ovary superior, 1-locular, with marginal placentation, ovules 1 to many in two rows along the ventral suture.
- Fruit a **legume**: developed from a single carpel, at maturity usually splitting along both the dorsal and ventral sutures ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.2.3)); also indehiscent (peanut, pagoda tree) or a loment that breaks between the seeds (*Desmodium*).
- Seeds without or with little endosperm, the cotyledons thick, rich in protein and oil (soybean, peanut) or starch (pea, broad bean); the seed coat hard, often with a conspicuous hilum.

Key characters of the family: mostly compound leaves with stipules and pulvini. Flowers 5-merous, single carpel, ovary superior. Legume. Seeds without endosperm.

Nitrogen-fixing symbiosis of legumes. Most legumes live in symbiosis with **rhizobia** (*Rhizobium*, *Bradyrhizobium* and others, α- and β-proteobacteria), forming root nodules on the roots in which atmospheric N₂ is reduced to ammonia for the plant's use (**nitrogen fixation**; for signaling and the infection process see [7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.2)), so that legumes can grow on poor soils and raise the nitrogen content of the soil through leaf fall and roots. The rotation and intercropping of legume crops with cereals, and the use of Chinese milk vetch, vetch, sesbania and alfalfa as green manure, are traditional agricultural practices that exploit this property. Not all legumes can form nodules: among the species examined, the great majority of the papilionoids and mimosoids can nodulate, whereas Cercidoideae and Detarioideae largely cannot, and only a few non-mimosoid caesalpinioids (such as *Chamaecrista*) can, which indicates that nodulation appeared after the major branches of the family had diverged and may have originated independently several times.

### 42.6.3. Subfamilies of Fabaceae: From Three Families to Six Subfamilies

In the course of evolution the flowers of Fabaceae formed a series from actinomorphic to zygomorphic, and traditional classification is based on this. Cronquist and Chinese textbooks divide the legumes into three families, whereas Engler and others treat them as three subfamilies of Fabaceae:

| Traditional family (subfamily) | Floral symmetry | Aestivation of the corolla | Stamens | Leaf |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mimosaceae | Actinomorphic | Valvate, petals often fused | Numerous (rarely as many as petals), often long and conspicuous | Bipinnate |
| Caesalpiniaceae | Zygomorphic (pseudopapilionaceous corolla) | Ascending imbricate: the uppermost petal smallest and innermost | 10 or fewer, free | Once or twice pinnate, rarely simple |
| Fabaceae (Papilionaceae) | Zygomorphic (papilionaceous corolla) | Descending imbricate: the uppermost petal, the standard, largest and outermost | 10, often diadelphous or monadelphous | Once pinnate or ternate, with stipels |

**Definition 42.6.1 (Papilionaceous corolla)** The zygomorphic corolla composed of a large **standard** (vexillum) at the adaxial (upper) side that encloses the others in the bud, two **wings** at the sides, and two **keel** petals at the abaxial (lower) side whose margins are fused into a boat shape enclosing the stamens and gynoecium, is called the **papilionaceous corolla**.

The petals of the papilionaceous corolla are arranged in descending imbricate aestivation (the standard outermost); the "pseudopapilionaceous corolla" of the caesalpinioids is similar in appearance, but the uppermost petal is innermost (ascending imbricate) and the keel petals are not fused, as in the flowers of *Cercis* (see the figure below). Chinese textbooks describe from this an evolutionary trend of the order: the androecium from numerous to definite in number and from free to fused, and the corolla from actinomorphic to zygomorphic. The mimosoid flower is actinomorphic with numerous stamens; the caesalpinioids, under selection by insect pollination, began to differentiate their petals and formed the pseudopapilionaceous corolla; the papilionoids became further specialized, with a large and conspicuous standard projecting above to attract insects and the keel fused to enclose the stamens and gynoecium inside, so that when a bee lands on the wings and keel and presses the petals down, the stamens and pistil are exposed to touch the bee's belly, and after the insect leaves the petals return to their position by their elasticity; in the diadelphous androecium the free stamen leaves a gap through which the bee's mouthparts can reach the base of the staminal tube to take nectar.

### 42.6.4. Cercidoideae and Detarioideae

Cercidoideae: *Cercis* has about 10 species with a disjunct East Asian–North American–Mediterranean distribution; leaves simple, cordate or nearly round, entire; flowers purple-red or pink, clustered on old branches or on the trunk (cauliflory), opening before the leaves, with a pseudopapilionaceous corolla and 10 free stamens; legume flat. Chinese redbud (*C. chinensis*) is native to China and often cultivated, and its bark and pedicels are used medicinally; eastern redbud (*C. canadensis*) is native to North America. *Bauhinia* (in the broad sense) has about 300 species with leaves bilobed at the tip, resembling a sheep's hoof (thought to be formed by the fusion of two leaflets): *B. purpurea* and *B. variegata* occur in southern China; Hong Kong orchid tree (*B.* × *blakeana*) has large purple-red flowers, is probably a hybrid of the two, sets almost no seed and is propagated by grafting, and it is the district flower of Hong Kong (the "bauhinia" on the emblem and flag of the district is this species) and belongs to a different genus from the *Cercis* redbud.

Detarioideae: mostly tropical trees. Tamarind (*Tamarindus indica*), native to Africa, has a sour-sweet pulp in the legume used as a seasoning and for making drinks; the flowers of *Saraca indica* and *S. dives* are orange-red, without petals, with bright bracts and calyx tube, and the genus is an important plant in Buddhism; *Afzelia xylocarpa* has hard seeds with a red aril.

### 42.6.5. Caesalpinioideae

Caesalpinioideae (the caesalpinioids in the strict sense and the mimosoids) are mostly trees, shrubs or woody climbers, with leaves often bipinnate.

Representative non-mimosoid plants:

- *Biancaea decapetala* (formerly *Caesalpinia decapetala*): a spiny climbing shrub with bipinnate leaves, racemes of yellow flowers and a legume, occurring south of the Yangtze, with the root and fruit used medicinally. Sappanwood (*B. sappan*) has red heartwood from which a red dye (brazilin) can be extracted and which is used medicinally. The traditional genus *Caesalpinia* was polyphyletic and was split into a dozen or so genera in 2016.
- Chinese honey locust (*Gleditsia sinensis*): a deciduous tree with stout, branched, conical thorns on branches and trunk; leaves once even-pinnate, often bipinnate on sprouts; flowers polygamous, in racemes; legume strap-shaped, rich in saponins and formerly boiled in water for washing, hence the Chinese name "soap pod"; the thorns (*zaojiaoci*) and the fruit are used medicinally. It occurs in most parts of China.
- *Senna*: the traditional broadly defined *Cassia* was split into *Cassia* s.s., *Senna* and *Chamaecrista*. *S. tora* is an annual subshrub-like herb with even-pinnate leaves of 6 leaflets, yellow flowers, a four-angled legume and nearly rhombic, shiny seeds, the drug *juemingzi*; golden shower tree (*Cassia fistula*) has long, pendulous yellow inflorescences and a cylindrical legume up to 60 cm long and is native to South Asia.
- Flamboyant (*Delonix regia*): a deciduous tree with bipinnate leaves up to 60 cm long and large, bright red flowers, native to Madagascar and widely grown in the tropics as a street tree.
- Others: *Gymnocladus chinensis* and *Erythrophleum fordii* (a valuable timber tree under second-class national protection).

The mimosoids: flowers small, actinomorphic, often in heads or spikes; calyx and corolla often fused, petals valvate; stamens numerous (rarely 4–10), the filaments long and conspicuous, extending beyond the corolla and forming the main attractant to pollinators; pollen often in polyads (tetrads or aggregated in 8, 16 or 32 grains); legume. Floral formula: $\ast K_{(5)} C_{(5)} A_\infty \underline{G}_{1:1:\infty}$. Representative plants:

- Sensitive plant (*Mimosa pudica*): a subshrub-like herb with prickles; leaves bipinnate with 2–4 pinnae arranged palmately at the top of the petiole, and on touch the leaflets close in succession and the petiole droops; flowers in heads, pale red, petals 4, stamens 4; native to the Americas and widely naturalized in the tropics.
- Silk tree (*Albizia julibrissin*): a deciduous tree with twice even-pinnate leaves whose sickle-shaped leaflets have the midrib off center and close at night; flowers in heads with slender, pale red filaments like a powder puff; it occurs south of the Yellow River, is often used as a street tree, and the bark and flowers are used medicinally.
- Acacias: *Acacia* in the broad sense has about 1300 species, and molecular evidence shows that it is polyphyletic; after it was split into several genera, the name *Acacia* was conserved by a decision of the 2005 Vienna International Botanical Congress for the Australian group of about 1000 species (many with reduced leaves and flattened petioles forming phyllodes, [7.12.24](/7.12.24#sec-24.4.1)), while the African species that had served as the type were transferred to the new name *Vachellia*: sweet acacia (*V. farnesiana*, formerly *Acacia farnesiana*) has stipules transformed into spines and yellow, strongly fragrant flowers. *Acacia confusa* has phyllodes and abundant root nodules and is a pioneer tree for afforestation of barren hills and soil conservation in southern China; the acacias (*Vachellia*) of the African savanna have hollow stipular spines and live in mutualism with the ants that inhabit them ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.6)).
- Others: *Leucaena leucocephala* (native to Central America, used for fodder and fuelwood, and also an invasive plant in the tropics), *Adenanthera microsperma* (the seeds are bright red and often wrongly called "love beans"), *Entada phaseoloides* (a woody climber with a legume up to 1 m long), and *Calliandra* (with red powder-puff inflorescences).

### 42.6.6. Papilionoideae

Papilionoideae have about 500 genera and 14,000 species, from herbs to trees, and are mostly herbaceous in the temperate zone. Diagnostic characters: leaves once odd- or even-pinnate or ternate, rarely simple, with stipules and stipels, the terminal leaflet sometimes transformed into a tendril (pea, *Vicia*); flowers zygomorphic, with a papilionaceous corolla in descending imbricate aestivation; stamens 10, often diadelphous (9 fused and 1 free), or all 10 fused into a monadelphous androecium (peanut, lupine), or a few free (pagoda tree, *Ormosia*); legume.

Floral formula: $\uparrow K_{(5)} C_{1+2+(2)} A_{(9)+1} \underline{G}_{1:1:\infty}$.

Representative plants (by use):

1. Edible pulses and oil crops:
   - Soybean (*Glycine max*): an annual herb with trifoliolate leaves and small white or purple flowers, and a hairy legume. Soybean originated in China, domesticated from the wild soybean (*G. soja*, a twining herb with small black seeds and a second-class nationally protected wild plant) in the Yellow River basin and elsewhere, and has been cultivated for thousands of years; the seeds contain about 40% protein and about 20% oil and are the most important source of plant protein and edible oil, and tofu, soy milk, soy sauce and bean sprouts are all made from it. The root nodules of soybean are formed by slow-growing rhizobia (*Bradyrhizobium*).
   - Peanut (*Arachis hypogaea*): an annual herb with even-pinnate leaves of 4 leaflets; flowers yellow, with a monadelphous androecium. The peculiarity of the peanut is that it flowers above ground and fruits underground: after fertilization the **intercalary meristem** at the base of the ovary elongates rapidly, forming a downward-growing gynophore (commonly called the "peg") that pushes the ovary into the soil, where the ovary swells and develops into the legume; the legume is indehiscent and the pericarp is net-veined. It is native to South America and is an **allopolyploid** (allotetraploid) formed by the hybridization of two wild diploid species, and the seeds contain about 50% oil.
   - Pea (*Pisum sativum*): an annual climbing herb with even-pinnate leaves whose terminal leaflets are transformed into branched tendrils (**leaf tendrils**), the stipules larger than the leaflets; flowers white or purple. It is native to the Mediterranean and the Near East and was the material with which Mendel (1865) discovered the laws of inheritance; the 7 pairs of contrasting traits he chose (seed round or wrinkled, cotyledons yellow or green, flower color, pod shape and color, flower position, plant height) are all controlled by single genes ([7.4.2](/7.4.2)); in the papilionaceous flower of the pea the keel tightly encloses the stamens and pistil, and self-pollination is completed before the flower opens, so that cultivars remain pure lines and artificial emasculation and crossing are easily controlled, the morphological reason for its suitability for genetic experiments.
   - Broad bean (*Vicia faba*): an annual erect herb with a four-angled stem and even-pinnate leaves whose terminal leaflet is reduced to a short point; flowers white, the wings with a black spot; seeds large, flat. It is native to western Asia and North Africa; the seeds contain vicine, and people with G6PD deficiency may suffer acute hemolysis (favism) after eating them.
   - Others: common bean (*Phaseolus vulgaris*, native to the Americas), mung bean (*Vigna radiata*), adzuki bean (*V. angularis*), cowpea (*V. unguiculata*), hyacinth bean (*Lablab purpureus*), sword bean (*Canavalia gladiata*), pigeon pea (*Cajanus cajan*), chickpea (*Cicer arietinum*), lentil (*Lens culinaris*). Chinese textbooks place mung bean and adzuki bean in *Phaseolus*; both are now in *Vigna*.
2. Forage and green manure: alfalfa (*Medicago sativa*) is a perennial herb with trifoliolate leaves, purple flowers and a spirally coiled legume, the most important legume forage, introduced when Zhang Qian opened the Western Regions in the Han dynasty; barrel medic (*M. truncatula*) is a model plant for the study of nodule symbiosis; *Trifolium* (clover; white clover *T. repens* is a common lawn and forage plant); *Melilotus* (sweet clover); Chinese milk vetch (*Astragalus sinicus*, a winter green manure of rice fields in the south); sesbania (*Sesbania cannabina*, a salt-tolerant green manure whose seed endosperm galactomannan gum is used in hydraulic fracturing fluids); *Vicia* (vetch).
3. Medicinal: Chinese licorice (*Glycyrrhiza uralensis*) is a perennial herb with stout roots and rhizomes tasting sweet and containing glycyrrhizic acid (about 50 times as sweet as sucrose), one of the most used drugs in Chinese medicine, "harmonizing the other drugs," and wild licorice has been protected because of overharvesting; the root of *Astragalus mongholicus* (including *A. membranaceus*) is used medicinally to tonify qi; *Sophora flavescens*, kudzu (*Pueraria montana* var. *lobata*, whose tuberous root is *gegen*, rich in starch and isoflavones), *Spatholobus suberectus* and relatives, and *Cullen corylifolium*. Kudzu is an extremely fast-growing vine introduced to the United States in 1876 as an ornamental and soil-conservation plant, and in the southeast it has become a famous invasive plant that covers forests and buildings, called "the vine that ate the South."
4. Timber and ornamentals: black locust (*Robinia pseudoacacia*) is a deciduous tree with stipules transformed into spines, odd-pinnate leaves and pendulous racemes of white, fragrant flowers; it is native to eastern North America and widely cultivated in China as a street tree, shelterbelt tree and honey plant; Chinese scholar tree (*Styphnolobium japonicum*, formerly *Sophora japonica*) is a deciduous tree without spines, with green twigs, yellowish-white flowers, 10 free stamens, and a fleshy, indehiscent legume constricted between the seeds like a string of beads; it is native to China and is the city tree of Beijing, and the weeping and five-leaflet (leaflets clustered, leaf form variable) forms are its cultivars. Note that *Styphnolobium* and *Robinia* are different genera: *Robinia* has stipular spines, diadelphous stamens and a flat, dehiscent legume, whereas *Styphnolobium* has no spines, free stamens and a moniliform, indehiscent legume. Chinese wisteria (*Wisteria sinensis*) is a large deciduous vine with pendulous racemes of purple flowers opening before the leaves in spring, a traditional plant for arbors in China; *Caragana*, sweet pea (*Lathyrus odoratus*), *Lupinus* and *Erythrina* are also often cultivated as ornamentals. Valuable timbers include *Pterocarpus* (padauk, the "rosewood" of Chinese furniture), *Dalbergia* (*D. odorifera*, the "Hainan huanghuali," from Hainan and endangered; *D. hupeana* is a host on which lac insects are raised), and *Ormosia* (seeds bright red, one of the "red beans").
5. Others: *Indigofera tinctoria* is one source of indigo dye; the roots of *Derris* contain rotenone and are used as an insecticide; rosary pea (*Abrus precatorius*) has seeds half red and half black, containing the highly toxic abrin, and is one of the plants meant by "red beans grow in the southern land"; *Crotalaria* contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids.

### 42.6.7. Economic Importance and Evolution of Fabaceae

Fabaceae are the most important source of plant protein in the human diet; in the main centers of origin of agriculture, pulses and cereals were domesticated as pairs: wheat and barley with pea, lentil and chickpea in the Near East, rice and millet with soybean in East Asia, maize with common bean in the Americas, and sorghum with cowpea in West Africa. The lysine that is deficient in cereal proteins is abundant in pulses, and the sulfur-containing amino acids that are deficient in pulses are abundant in cereals, so the two complement each other nutritionally. Fabaceae also supply forage, green manure, timber, dyes, gums (gum arabic comes from *Senegalia senegal* and guar gum from the guar, *Cyamopsis tetragonoloba*), drugs and ornamental plants.

In the fossil record the legumes and leaflets of Fabaceae first appear in the Paleocene (about 60 Ma) and are widespread in the Eocene; molecular clocks estimate that the crown group of Fabaceae arose between the end of the Cretaceous and the beginning of the Paleocene. In all systems Fabaceae have been thought to be related to Rosaceae: Chinese textbooks hold that Fabales originated from the Prunoideae of Rosaceae or share a common ancestor with them, the single carpel having evolved into the legume while the fused floral cup, well-developed stipules and 5-merous, whorled flowers were retained. Molecular evidence shows that the two do indeed belong to the same nitrogen-fixing clade, but Fabales and Rosales are two independent branches within it (Fabales branching off first), and Fabaceae do not originate from within Rosaceae; the single carpel evolved independently in the two.

### 42.6.8. Polygalaceae

Polygalaceae have about 20 genera and 1000 species, of which *Polygala* has about 500, widely distributed. Herbs to trees; leaves simple, alternate; flowers zygomorphic, resembling a papilionaceous flower in appearance but entirely different in structure: sepals 5, the inner 2 large and petal-like (called "wings," not homologous with the wings of Fabaceae); petals 3 (or 5), the lower one keel-shaped and often with a crest-like fringed appendage at the tip; stamens 8, the filaments fused into a sheath and adnate to the petals, anthers opening by apical pores; ovary superior, 2 carpels, 2-locular, 1 ovule per locule; capsule flat, seeds often hairy and with a caruncle. Floral formula: $\uparrow K_{3+2} C_3 A_{(8)} \underline{G}_{(2:2:1)}$. *Polygala tenuifolia* is a perennial herb with linear leaves and pale blue-purple flowers, occurring from northeastern to northwestern China, and the root is used medicinally (to calm the spirit); *P. japonica* and *P. fallax* occur in the south. The resemblance of the flower of Polygalaceae to the papilionaceous flower is convergent: its "wings" are sepals and its "keel" is one petal. Cronquist placed Polygalaceae in Polygalales of the Rosidae.

## Questions

1. How do we recognize the cyathium of *Euphorbia*? Why is it an inflorescence and not a single flower?

   **Answer** The outside of a cyathium is a cup-shaped involucre formed by fused bracts, with thick glands at the margin; inside the involucre are several male flowers, each containing a single stamen and no perianth, and in the center a single female flower consisting of a long-stalked 3-carpellate pistil. The evidence that it is an inflorescence is as follows: between the filament of each "stamen" and the stalk below it there is a joint, and below the joint is the pedicel, showing that each "stamen" is a reduced male flower; the central "pistil" has its own stalk and in related genera a reduced perianth is still present; the involucre corresponds to bracts, and the flowers within it develop in cymose order. The whole structure functions as a bisexual flower and is a pseudanthium formed secondarily within *Euphorbia*.

2. Compare *Populus* and *Salix* in a table. Why does the "fluff" in cities come from female trees?

   **Answer** *Populus* has monopodial branching with a terminal bud, many bud scales, broad leaves with long petioles, pendulous catkins, bracts divided at the apex, a cup-shaped disc, numerous stamens, wind pollination, and a 2–4-valved capsule; *Salix* has sympodial branching with the terminal bud abortive, a single bud scale, mostly lanceolate leaves with short petioles, often erect catkins, entire bracts, no disc but 1–2 glands, usually 2 stamens, mainly insect pollination, and a 2-valved capsule. Salicaceae are dioecious, and the "poplar fluff" and "willow fluff" are the long silky hairs growing from the funicle at the base of the seeds, released when the capsule opens and helping the seeds to be dispersed by wind; only female trees bear fruit, so the fluff comes from female trees, and male trees are mostly chosen for greening.

3. Fabaceae were traditionally divided into Mimosaceae, Caesalpiniaceae and Papilionaceae. Explain the differences among the flowers of the three, and why LPWG 2017 divided the family into six subfamilies.

   **Answer** Mimosoid flowers are actinomorphic, with valvate, often fused petals and numerous stamens with conspicuous filaments; caesalpinioid flowers are zygomorphic with a pseudopapilionaceous corolla, the uppermost petal innermost (ascending imbricate) and 10 free stamens; papilionoid flowers have a papilionaceous corolla with the standard outermost (descending imbricate), the keel fused, and stamens usually diadelphous. Molecular phylogenetics shows that the papilionoids and mimosoids are each monophyletic but the caesalpinioids are paraphyletic: the mimosoids are nested within a clade composed of *Caesalpinia* s.l., *Gleditsia* and others, whereas *Cercis*, *Bauhinia*, tamarind, *Saraca*, *Dialium* and *Duparquetia* belong to several earlier-diverging lineages. To make all the subfamilies monophyletic, LPWG divided the caesalpinioids into five subfamilies, Cercidoideae, Detarioideae, Duparquetioideae, Dialioideae and Caesalpinioideae, merged the mimosoids into Caesalpinioideae, and retained Papilionoideae.

4. Why is the "viviparity" of Rhizophoraceae an adaptation to the intertidal zone? Do all plants in mangrove forests belong to Rhizophoraceae?

   **Answer** The mud of the intertidal zone is soft, anoxic and saline and is constantly scoured by the tides, so a seed that lands on the mud surface has difficulty germinating and rooting. A viviparous seed germinates while still on the mother tree, the hypocotyl elongating into a stout propagule that, when it falls, already has considerable size, stored reserves and a developed radicle; it can plunge into the mud by its weight and put out lateral roots that anchor it within a few hours, or float away and settle at a suitable place. Not all plants in mangrove forests belong to Rhizophoraceae: *Avicennia* (Acanthaceae), *Sonneratia* (Lythraceae), *Aegiceras* (Primulaceae), *Excoecaria* (Euphorbiaceae) and others are also mangrove plants; they belong to different orders, and the similar prop roots, pneumatophores, salt excretion and viviparity (or cryptoviviparity) are convergent evolution to the intertidal environment.

5. Why does the peanut "flower above ground and fruit underground"? Which morphological features of the pea make it suitable as Mendel's genetic material?

   **Answer** After fertilization of the peanut, the intercalary meristem at the base of the ovary divides and elongates rapidly, forming a downward gynophore (the peg) that pushes the ovary into the soil; only in the dark soil does the ovary swell and develop into the legume. In the papilionaceous corolla of the pea the keel tightly encloses the stamens and pistil, and self-pollination is completed before the flower opens, so that the cultivars are pure lines and seldom cross naturally; when a cross is needed, opening the keel at the bud stage to emasculate and then pollinating by hand is also easy; the pea also has many stable, easily distinguished contrasting traits (seed shape and cotyledon color, flower color, pod shape and color, flower position, plant height), a short growth period and many seeds, which is convenient for counting.
