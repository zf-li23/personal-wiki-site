Poales are the order of monocots with the second-largest number of species after Asparagales, about 23,000 species; its Poaceae and Cyperaceae make up the bulk of the world's grasslands, meadows and wetlands, and Poaceae alone provide more than half of the calories consumed by humans: rice, wheat and maize together account for about half of world grain production, and with barley, sorghum, millet, oat, sugarcane and bamboo the economic importance of Poaceae exceeds that of any other plant family. Most plants of Poales have small flowers without a showy perianth and are wind-pollinated; Cronquist placed such families (Cyperaceae, Poaceae, Juncaceae, Typhaceae) in the subclass Commelinidae, and put the showy, animal-pollinated Bromeliaceae in the Zingiberidae; molecular phylogenetics shows that Bromeliaceae are one of the earliest-diverging families of Poales, and that wind pollination and floral simplification arose several times independently within Poales. This chapter first treats the circumscription of the order and several small families (§39.1), then Cyperaceae (§39.2) and Poaceae (§39.3). For the synapomorphies of the commelinids see [7.12.38](/7.12.38#sec-38.1); for the terminology of the grass flower and spikelet see [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.5); for leaf structure and the Kranz anatomy of C4 plants see [7.12.23](/7.12.23#sec-23.5.5); for the caryopsis and the structure of the grass seed see [7.12.19](/7.12.19) and [7.12.31](/7.12.31).

## 39.1. Circumscription of Poales and the Small Families

### 39.1.1. Circumscription and Characters of the Order

APG IV Poales comprise 14 families: Typhaceae, Bromeliaceae, Rapateaceae, Xyridaceae, Eriocaulaceae, Mayacaceae, Thurniaceae, Juncaceae, Cyperaceae, Restionaceae, Flagellariaceae, Joinvilleaceae, Ecdeiocoleaceae and Poaceae. Phylogenetically they fall into several branches: Typhaceae and Bromeliaceae (with Rapateaceae) diverge first in turn; Xyridaceae and Eriocaulaceae form one branch; Juncaceae, Cyperaceae and Thurniaceae form the "cyperids"; and Restionaceae, Flagellariaceae, Joinvilleaceae, Ecdeiocoleaceae and Poaceae form the "graminids", with Ecdeiocoleaceae the **sister group** of Poaceae.

Characters shared by Poales: silica bodies in the epidermal cells; absence of calcium oxalate raphides (unlike most other monocots); pollen often monoporate (graminids); perianth often reduced to dry membranous scales or absent; wind pollination arose separately in the cyperids and the graminids. The systems of Cronquist and Dahlgren divided these families among many small orders (Bromeliales, Typhales, Juncales, Cyperales, Poales and others); APG combines them in one large order, and Judd et al. (1999) proposed dividing it into Juncales and Poales, which was not adopted.

Changes in circumscription: the Cyperales of the Chinese textbooks comprise only Cyperaceae and Poaceae, and the argument that they are "properly united in one order" is that the floral parts are highly simplified and the ovary has 2–3 carpels, 1 locule and 1 ovule; molecular evidence shows that they belong to the cyperids and the graminids respectively, and their similarity is **convergent evolution**: the closest relatives of Cyperaceae are Juncaceae, and the closest relatives of Poaceae are Ecdeiocoleaceae, Joinvilleaceae and Flagellariaceae.

### 39.1.2. Typhaceae

Typhaceae (the cattail family) are perennial marsh or aquatic herbs with creeping rhizomes; leaves distichous, linear, sheathing at the base; flowers unisexual, monoecious, minute, crowded in spikes or heads. APG merges the former Sparganiaceae into this family, giving 2 genera and about 50 species:

- *Typha* (cattail): the spike is cylindrical, the upper part a male inflorescence and the lower part a female inflorescence (the "cattail"); male flowers have only 2–3 stamens and a perianth of hairs; the ovary of the female flower is long-stalked and the stalk bears filamentous hairs, by which the ripe fruit is dispersed. In *T. orientalis* the male and female inflorescences are contiguous; in *T. angustifolia* there is a gap between them. The pollen ("puhuang") is used in medicine; the young pseudostem ("pucai") is edible, and the leaves are used for weaving mats, bags and fans. Cattails are important emergent plants of wetlands and are often used in constructed wetlands to purify sewage.
- *Sparganium* (bur-reed): the inflorescence consists of globose heads arranged on a scape, with male heads above and female heads below. The tuber of *S. stoloniferum* is used in medicine ("sanleng").

Floral formula: ♂ $P_0 A_{2\text{–}3}$; ♀ $P_0 \underline{G}_{1:1:1}$ (*Typha*; the perianth is hairs or scales).

### 39.1.3. Bromeliaceae

Bromeliaceae (the bromeliad family) have about 70 genera and 3500 species, almost all in the tropics and subtropics of the Americas, with only one species (*Pitcairnia feliciana*) in West Africa, thought to be the result of long-distance dispersal. Mostly epiphytic or terrestrial herbs; leaves often basal in a rosette, the blade linear, often with spiny teeth and covered with peltate absorptive scales (trichomes); inflorescence often with brightly colored bracts; flowers bisexual, sepals 3, petals 3, stamens 6, ovary superior to inferior, 3-locular; berry or capsule. Many epiphytic species carry out **crassulacean acid metabolism**. Floral formula: $\ast K_3 C_3 A_{3+3} G_{(3:3:\infty)}$ (the position of the ovary varies within the family).

- Pineapple (*Ananas comosus*): terrestrial, native to South America; each flower of the spike develops into a berry, and these, together with the bracts and the fleshy axis, fuse into one fleshy **multiple fruit**, topped by a tuft of leaves (the crown) that can be used for vegetative propagation ([7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.2.5)). Cultivated pineapple sets fruit without fertilization, and the fruit contains no seeds. Pineapple carries out crassulacean acid metabolism and is drought-tolerant; the stem and fruit contain bromelain, which breaks down proteins, so eating too much irritates the mouth.
- *Tillandsia*: "air plants", epiphytic on branches, wires and even rocks, with reduced roots that serve only for anchorage; they take up water and minerals from rain and fog through the peltate scales densely covering the leaf surface. Spanish moss (*T. usneoides*) hangs like a lichen and is very common on oaks in the southeastern United States.
- Tank bromeliads: in many epiphytic bromeliads (such as *Guzmania*, *Vriesea* and *Aechmea*) the leaf bases clasp one another to form a "tank" that collects rainwater and fallen organic matter, from which the absorptive scales of the leaf bases take up nutrients; these small pools are inhabited by tadpoles, insect larvae and many other animals and form a miniature ecosystem.
- *Puya raimondii* of the Andes is the largest bromeliad, with an inflorescence up to 10 m tall; the plant grows for several decades before flowering once and dying.

### 39.1.4. Juncaceae, Eriocaulaceae and Other Small Families

- Juncaceae (the rush family): mostly wetland herbs, the stem (culm) cylindrical, solid, with a pith; leaves basal, the blade linear or cylindrical, the sheath open; flowers small, trimerous, tepals 6, glume-like, stamens 6 or 3, ovary superior, 3-locular or 1-locular; capsule. Floral formula: $\ast P_{3+3} A_{3+3} \underline{G}_{(3:3:\infty)}$. About 8 genera and 450 species, mainly in temperate and cold regions. The white, spongy pith of the stem of soft rush (*Juncus effusus*) was formerly used as the wick of oil lamps and is also used in medicine; *Luzula* (woodrush) has long hairs on the leaf margin. The chromosomes of both Juncaceae and Cyperaceae are holocentric (§39.2.2), and the two families are closely related; the flower of Juncaceae has a complete perianth of 6 members and can be seen as the form of the Cyperaceae flower "before simplification".
- Eriocaulaceae (the pipewort family): wetland herbs with basal rosette leaves; a head terminates the scape, subtended by involucral bracts; flowers unisexual and minute. The head of *Eriocaulon buergerianum* is used in medicine.
- Xyridaceae (the yellow-eyed grass family): in *Xyris* the head is cone-like and the flowers yellow; in wetlands of southern China.
- Flagellariaceae: *Flagellaria indica* is a climber whose leaf tips are extended into tendrils, in Hainan and Taiwan, China.
- Restionaceae (the restio family): mainly in the Southern Hemisphere (the Cape region of South Africa and Australia), with green stems and leaves reduced to sheaths; a main component of the South African fynbos and known as the "grasses of the Southern Hemisphere".

## 39.2. Cyperaceae

### 39.2.1. Diagnostic Characters

Diagnostic characters of Cyperaceae (the sedge family):

- Perennial, less often annual herbs, often with rhizomes, which in some species swell at the tip into tubers or corms (purple nutsedge, water chestnut).
- The stem is called a culm, often triangular in section, solid (the culm of water chestnut has transverse septa), and without nodes except at the base.
- Leaves basal or on the culm, usually in 3 ranks, the blade linear, with a closed sheath at the base (the sheath forms a tube that does not split), generally without a ligule; sometimes the blade is reduced and only the sheath is present.
- Flowers small, bisexual or unisexual, each solitary in the axil of a scale (called a glume in the Chinese textbooks); several to many flowers with their scales form a spikelet, and the spikelets are arranged in spikes, racemes, panicles, heads or cymes; the inflorescence is usually subtended by 1 to many leaf-like, bristle-like or scale-like involucral bracts.
- Perianth absent, or reduced to hypogynous bristles or hypogynous scales.
- Stamens 3, rarely 2 or 1; pollen in pseudomonads (of the four nuclei after meiosis of the microspore mother cell only one develops, and the tetrad as a whole becomes a single pollen grain), often pear-shaped.
- Gynoecium of 2–3 fused carpels, ovary superior, 1-locular, with 1 basal ovule; style 1, stigmas 2–3.
- Fruit a nutlet (achene-like), trigonous, biconvex or plano-convex; in *Carex* the female flower and the fruit are enclosed in a sac-like structure formed from a bracteole (prophyll), called the perigynium.

Floral formula: bisexual flower $P_0 A_3 \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}3:1:1)}$; unisexual flowers ♂ $P_0 A_3$; ♀ $P_0 \underline{G}_{(2\text{–}3:1:1)}$.

Cyperaceae have about 100 genera and 5500 species (the Chinese textbooks count 106 genera and 5400 species, with 33 genera and 865 species in China), widespread around the world and most numerous in wetlands of cold and temperate regions; *Carex* and *Cyperus* are its two large genera. Many sedges are dominants of wetlands, tundra and alpine meadows, and some are noxious weeds.

### 39.2.2. Holocentric Chromosomes

The chromosomes of Cyperaceae and Juncaceae are holocentric: the spindle fibers attach along the whole chromosome rather than concentrating at a single centromere. After a chromosome breaks, each fragment can still segregate normally, so that chromosome number changes frequently in these two families and can change by fission and fusion of chromosomes (rather than by polyploidization). The chromosome number in *Carex* varies almost continuously from $2n = 12$ to $2n = 124$, and different chromosome numbers often occur even within one species; this lability of the karyotype is thought to be related to the species diversity of *Carex* ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.5)).

### 39.2.3. Classification and Representative Plants

1. *Scirpus* and related genera: culm triangular, rarely cylindrical; cymes simple or compound, or contracted into heads, the bract below the inflorescence often looking like a continuation of the culm; spikelets with few to many flowers, scales spirally arranged, each scale enclosing 1 bisexual flower; hypogynous bristles 2–9 or absent. The traditional *Scirpus* (in the broad sense) is now split into several genera: *Schoenoplectus triqueter* (often given as *Scirpus triqueter* in Chinese textbooks) has a stout, triangular culm and a pseudolateral inflorescence, and the culm can be woven into mats and made into paper; *Bolboschoenus yagara* has a tall culm and a tuber used in medicine; *Schoenoplectus tabernaemontani* (softstem bulrush) has a cylindrical culm and is often planted at water's edge.
2. *Cyperus* (flatsedge): culms scattered or tufted, often triangular; leaves basal; cymes simple or compound, with several leaf-like bracts at the base, umbrella-like; spikelets compressed, the scales in 2 ranks; no hypogynous bristles; stigmas 3 or 2. About 950 species, one of the genera with the most species in Cyperaceae, many of them C4 plants. Representative plants:
   - Purple nutsedge (*C. rotundus*): rhizomes slender and creeping, bearing many oblong, dark brown tubers; 2–3 leaf-like bracts and several rays of unequal length at the top of the culm. It is called "the world's worst weed", spreading rapidly in fields by tubers, with chains of tubers reaching tens of centimeters underground. The dried tubers ("xiangfu") contain volatile oils such as cyperone and are used in medicine to regulate qi, relieve depression, regulate menstruation and relieve pain.
   - *C. malaccensis* subsp. *monophyllus*: occurs in saline and brackish wetlands of the southeastern coast, the culm can be woven into mats, and it is a plant for improving saline-alkaline soil.
   - Tiger nut (*C. esculentus*): the tubers contain over 20% oil and can be eaten or pressed for oil; native from the Mediterranean to Africa and cultivated in ancient Egypt; in many places it is also a weed of fields.
   - Papyrus (*C. papyrus*): a tall wetland herb with a large tuft of slender rays at the top of the culm, growing in the swamps of Africa; the ancient Egyptians cut the pith of the culm into thin strips and laid them crosswise to make the writing material papyrus, the source of the English word paper. Umbrella plant (*C. involucratus*) is often grown at water's edge.
3. *Carex* (sedge): flowers unisexual, without perianth; male flowers with 3 stamens; the ovary of the female flower is enclosed in a perigynium, with the style protruding from its mouth and 2–3 stigmas; male and female spikelets separate or combined in one spikelet. About 2000 species, one of the genera with the most species among angiosperms, widespread around the world and most numerous in the north temperate and cold zones; China has about 500 species in every province, mainly in the north and in high mountains. (Chinese sources often write the genus name with a character meaning "moss", which is incorrect; the standard name in the Flora of China uses the character for the flowering stalk, as in garlic scape and chive scape.) *C. dispalata* and *C. ligulata* (with a conspicuous rusty ligule at the mouth of the leaf sheath, a rare example of a ligule in Cyperaceae) are fairly widespread; *C. meyeriana* occurs in the northeast, and in the past it was packed into boots (called "wula") for warmth, forming with ginseng and sable fur the "three treasures of the northeast". *Carex* is one of the constructive genera of the alpine meadows of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and of northern wetlands and is also an important forage for many herbivores.
4. *Eleocharis* (spikerush): culms tufted or solitary, with only leaf sheaths and no blades, and no bracts; spikelet 1, terminal, with many bisexual flowers; the style base is swollen and persistent at the tip of the nutlet. In Chinese water chestnut (*E. dulcis*) the tip of the creeping rhizome swells into a corm, the edible water chestnut; the culm is cylindrical, hollow, with many transverse septa; cultivated everywhere, and the corm is also used in medicine.
5. Others: *Fimbristylis* is widespread in north and south; the whole plant of *Kyllinga brevifolia* is used in medicine; the culm of *Lepironia articulata* is used for weaving mats and bags.

Key characters of the family: culm triangular, solid, nodeless; leaves in 3 ranks, sheaths closed; flowers in the axils of scales, the perianth reduced to bristles or absent; nutlet.

### 39.2.4. How Cyperaceae Differ from Poaceae

Cyperaceae and Poaceae are both wind-pollinated herbs with highly simplified flowers, and in the field they are often lumped together as "grass". They can be distinguished by the following table (Fig. 39.2.1):

| Character | Cyperaceae | Poaceae |
|---|---|---|
| Culm | Often triangular, solid, without nodes except at the base | Cylindrical, with conspicuous nodes, the internodes often hollow |
| Phyllotaxis | 3-ranked | 2-ranked |
| Leaf sheath | Closed (tubular) | Usually split on one side |
| Ligule and auricle | Generally absent | Ligule usually present, auricle often present |
| Bracts of the flower | One scale per flower | Two bracts (lemma and palea) per floret, 2 glumes at the base of the spikelet |
| Perianth | Hypogynous bristles, scales or absent | Lodicules 2–3 |
| Stigma | 2–3, not feathery | Usually 2, feathery |
| Pollen | Pseudomonads | Monoporate pollen, trinucleate |
| Fruit | Nutlet, the pericarp separate from the seed coat | Caryopsis, the pericarp fused with the seed coat |
| Position of the embryo | At the base of the seed, surrounded by endosperm | At one side of the base of the caryopsis, with a scutellum |
| Chromosomes | Holocentric | Monocentric |
| Relationship | Juncaceae (cyperids) | Ecdeiocoleaceae, Joinvilleaceae (graminids) |

## 39.3. Poaceae

### 39.3.1. Vegetative Organs

Vegetative organs of Poaceae (Gramineae, the grass family):

- Herbs or woody plants (bamboos); with or without underground stems; the aerial stem is called a culm, with conspicuous nodes and internodes, the nodes solid and the internodes mostly hollow, in a few species solid (maize, sorghum, sugarcane). The base of each internode has an **intercalary meristem**, and elongation of the culm depends mainly on simultaneous growth at the base of all the internodes, so that bamboo shoots can grow into culms within a few weeks ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)); branches and adventitious roots arise at the lower nodes of the culm, a process called **tillering** ([7.12.21](/7.12.21#sec-21.2.3)).
- Leaves simple, alternate, 2-ranked, each leaf divided into a **leaf sheath**, a blade and a **ligule**: the sheath clasps the culm and is usually split on one side (closed in a few, such as *Bromus* and *Melica*); the blade is ribbon-like, linear to lanceolate, parallel-veined; the ligule arises on the inner side at the junction of blade and sheath, membranous, a ring of hairs or completely reduced; the two sides of the top of the sheath often bear **auricles**. The shapes of the ligule and auricles are important characters for identifying grasses, particularly for distinguishing crops from weeds (such as rice and barnyard grass) before flowering ([7.12.23](/7.12.23#sec-23.2.2)).
- The upper epidermis of the leaf has rows of large **bulliform cells**, which shrink when water is lost and make the leaf roll; the epidermis has long cells and short cells (silica cells and cork cells) alternating, and the guard cells of the stomata are dumbbell-shaped, flanked by subsidiary cells ([7.12.23](/7.12.23#sec-23.5.5)).
- The culm leaves of bamboos differ from ordinary leaves: the leaves on the culm (culm sheaths, the bamboo-shoot husks) consist of a sheath blade and a small blade without a conspicuous midrib; at the junction of sheath and blade there is a ligule on the inner side and auricles on the two sides; ordinary leaves (foliage leaves) have a short petiole with a joint where it meets the sheath, and fall readily from the sheath.

### 39.3.2. Spikelet and Flower

The inflorescence of grasses is built on the **spikelet** as the basic unit (Definition 27.5.1). The spikelet has a very short rachilla, with 2 **glumes** often at its base: the lower (outer) is called the first glume and the upper (inner) the second glume. The rachilla bears 1 to many **florets**, each enclosed by 2 bracts: the **lemma** is thicker and harder, often with an awn at the apex or on the back, and sometimes thickened and hardened at the base to form a callus; the **palea** often has 2 raised veins like keels and is usually enclosed by the lemma. Between lemma and palea, at the base of the ovary, are 2 (rarely 3) transparent, fleshy small scales called **lodicules**, which swell by taking up water at flowering and push the lemma and palea apart so that the stigmas and stamens protrude from the flower ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.5)).

The other parts of the floret: stamens usually 3, rarely 1, 2, 4 or 6 (6 in rice and bamboos); filaments slender, anthers versatile and able to sway in the wind; pollen nearly spherical, trinucleate, with a single pore surrounded by an annular thickening; pistil 1, of 2–3 carpels, ovary superior, 1-locular, 1 ovule; styles 2 (rarely 1 or 3), stigmas often feathery or brush-like, suited to catching wind-borne pollen. The spikelets are further arranged on the axis into compound inflorescences that are spike-like (wheat), digitate (crabgrass, bermudagrass), racemose or paniculate (rice, oat).

Floral formula: $\uparrow P_2 A_3 \underline{G}_{(2:1:1)}$ (rice and bamboos $P_{2\text{–}3} A_{3+3}$; $P$ denotes the lodicules, and the lemma and palea, as bracts, are not written in the floral formula).

Homologies of the parts of the spikelet: glumes and lemma are bracts; the palea is generally regarded as a bracteole (prophyll), and the Chinese textbooks often interpret it as the outer perianth whorl; the lodicules correspond to the inner perianth whorl, and molecular evidence supports the homology of lodicules with petals ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.5)). A grass floret is thus really "a flower plus its two bracts", and the spikelet is a highly compressed spike.

Variation of the spikelet is the main basis of grass classification: whether the spikelet is laterally or dorsiventrally compressed; whether it contains one or several florets and whether the fertile florets are in the lower or upper part; whether at maturity the spikelet disarticulates above the glumes (glumes persistent, as in Pooideae) or falls as a whole together with the glumes (as in Panicoideae); and whether the lemma has an awn and whether the awn is geniculate or twisted.

The fruit is a **caryopsis**: the pericarp is thin and fused with the seed coat and cannot easily be separated, and the whole caryopsis is customarily called the "seed" (grain); rarely a utricle or a berry (*Melocanna*). The seed has abundant starchy **endosperm**, with an **aleurone layer** at its periphery; the embryo lies at one side of the base of the caryopsis, with a cotyledon specialized as an absorptive organ (the **scutellum**), a **coleoptile** and a **coleorhiza** ([7.12.19](/7.12.19)).

### 39.3.3. Subfamilies and Phylogeny

Poaceae have about 770 genera and 12,000 species (Soreng et al. 2017 count 768 genera and 11,506 species; the Chinese textbooks count 700 genera and 11,000 species, with about 226 genera and 1795 species in China), distributed throughout the world, with grasses wherever seed plants can grow; on the grasslands of the temperate and tropical zones grasses are the constructive species of the community. The Chinese textbooks usually divide Poaceae into 2 subfamilies, Bambusoideae and Pooideae, or into 3, 5 or 7 subfamilies. The current classification follows the Grass Phylogeny Working Group (GPWG 2001, GPWG II 2012) and the system of Soreng et al. (2017), with 12 subfamilies:

1. The 3 earliest-diverging small subfamilies: Anomochlooideae (only in South America, without typical spikelets), Pharoideae (the "broad-leaved forest grasses") and Puelioideae (in tropical Africa), with only about 30 species, mostly broad-leaved grasses of the tropical forest understory. The ancestor of the grasses was probably such a forest-understory plant, and grasses of open habitats diverged later.
2. The BOP clade: Bambusoideae, Oryzoideae (formerly Ehrhartoideae) and Pooideae, whose initials form BOP. All are C3 plants; Oryzoideae are mainly in tropical wetlands, Bambusoideae in tropical and subtropical forests, and Pooideae are the grasses of the temperate and cold zones, including the temperate cereals wheat, barley, oat and rye.
3. The PACMAD clade: Panicoideae, Aristidoideae, Chloridoideae, Micrairoideae, Arundinoideae and Danthonioideae, whose initials form PACMAD. They occur mainly in open, dry or hot habitats of the tropics and subtropics; all the C4 plants of Poaceae are in this clade.

### 39.3.4. Repeated Origins of C4 Photosynthesis in Grasses

C4 photosynthesis (the **C4 pathway**) lets plants fix carbon efficiently at high temperature, strong light and low CO₂ concentration: PEP carboxylase in the mesophyll cells first fixes CO₂ into a four-carbon acid, which is then transported to the bundle sheath cells and releases CO₂ there, where Rubisco enters it into the Calvin cycle, avoiding the loss through photorespiration (for the biochemical mechanism see [7.1.25](/7.1.25), left to plant physiology for details). Anatomically, the leaf of C4 plants has **Kranz anatomy**: the veins are closely spaced, the bundle sheath cells are large and rich in chloroplasts, and the mesophyll cells are arranged radially around the bundle sheath ([7.12.23](/7.12.23#sec-23.5.6)).

There are about 8000 C4 species among all angiosperms, of which about 4500 are grasses and about 1500 are sedges. The analysis of GPWG II (2012) shows that C4 photosynthesis arose independently at least 22–24 times in Poaceae, all in the PACMAD clade; there are no C4 plants in the BOP clade. The ancestor of the PACMAD clade already had some "preadaptations" favorable to the origin of C4, a small distance between veins and fairly large bundle sheath cells, which allowed C4 photosynthesis to appear repeatedly in this clade. Molecular-clock estimates place the earliest origin of C4 in grasses in the Oligocene (about 30 million years ago), coinciding with the marked decline of atmospheric CO₂ concentration at that time; C4 grasses became dominant in tropical savannas in the late Miocene (about 8–3 million years ago), which is related to aridification and the frequent occurrence of fire. The C4 grasses of different origins also differ anatomically: the bundle sheath can be single (maize and sugarcane of Panicoideae) or double (Chloridoideae), and the different types of C4 enzyme systems (NADP-ME, NAD-ME and PCK types) also correspond to different lineages.

Among C4 plants are the crops with the highest yields in the world, maize, sugarcane and sorghum, and also the most difficult weeds to remove (crabgrass, goosegrass, purple nutsedge). Introducing C4 photosynthesis into rice (the "C4 rice" project) is a long-term goal of crop improvement.

### 39.3.5. Bambusoideae

Diagnostic characters of Bambusoideae (the bamboos): culms generally woody, mostly shrubby or tree-like, the internodes often hollow; culm leaves (culm sheaths) clearly different from ordinary leaves, the blade reduced and without a conspicuous midrib; ordinary leaves with a short petiole, jointed where it meets the sheath, falling readily from the sheath; spikelets often pseudospikelets (formed by clusters of bracts bearing buds), lodicules often 3, stamens often 6. Bambusoideae have about 125 genera and 1700 species, mainly in the monsoon regions of the tropics and subtropics, with centers in East and Southeast Asia and in South America; China has about 40 genera and over 500 species, mainly south of the Yangtze, and is the country with the richest bamboo resources in the world. The subfamily is divided into 3 tribes: the temperate woody bamboos (Arundinarieae), the tropical woody bamboos (Bambuseae) and the herbaceous bamboos (Olyreae). The basic chromosome number of woody bamboos is $x = 12$, and most are tetraploid or hexaploid ([7.12.33](/7.12.33#sec-33.3.5)).

The underground stem of bamboos is an important taxonomic character and has three types:

- Monopodial, running type (running bamboos): the underground stem (rhizome) is slender and runs horizontally underground, lateral buds at the nodes come up as culms, and the culms are scattered on the ground, as in *Phyllostachys*;
- Sympodial, clumping type (clumping bamboos): there is no horizontal rhizome, buds at the base of the culm grow directly into new culms, and the culms are densely clustered, as in *Bambusa* and *Dendrocalamus*;
- Amphipodial, mixed type (mixed bamboos): both are present, and the culms are both scattered and clustered, as in *Indocalamus*, *Pleioblastus* and *Chimonobambusa*.

Flowering of bamboos: most bamboos have a very long vegetative phase and flower only once in several decades, and the same species (the same stand derived by vegetative propagation) often flowers simultaneously over a wide area and dies in large patches after flowering and fruiting. The flowering cycle of *Phyllostachys reticulata* is recorded as about 120 years; the bamboo *Melocanna baccifera* of South Asia flowers synchronously about every 48 years, and the large quantity of fruit makes rodent populations erupt, after which the rodents turn to crops and cause famine in northeastern India. Around 1983 the arrow bamboo *Bashania faberi* flowered and died over large areas at Wolong in Sichuan and other places, threatening the giant panda that feeds mainly on it. The mechanism of synchronous flowering in bamboos is not clear; one explanation is predator satiation: producing at one time far more seeds than seed predators can eat ensures that enough seeds survive.

Representative plants:

1. *Indocalamus*: shrubby or small shrubby bamboos with a mixed rhizome; 1 branch per node, nearly as thick as the main culm; blades large. *I. latifolius* occurs in eastern China and southern Shaanxi, the culm is suitable for pen holders and chopsticks, and the broad leaves are used to wrap zongzi and to make bamboo hats and boat awnings.
2. *Bambusa*: rhizome sympodial, culms clumped, the internodes cylindrical, with many branches at each node, and in some species the twigs hardening into thorns; culm-sheath blades erect, as wide at the base as the top of the culm sheath, auricles conspicuous. *B. multiplex* (hedge bamboo) has culms 2–7 m tall and many branches clustered at one node, occurs in southern and southwestern China and is cultivated for ornament; *B. multiplex* 'Fernleaf' has dwarf culms, with a dozen or so leaves on a twig arranged in two ranks like a pinnately compound leaf; *B. ventricosa* (Buddha's belly bamboo) has deformed culms with internodes swollen into a bottle shape, occurs in Guangdong and is often potted for ornament.
3. *Dendrocalamus*: tree-like bamboos, sympodial and clumped, the culm erect with the tip often drooping, many branches at each node, the culm-sheath blades often reflexed; spikelets without a joint, lodicules absent, stamens 6. *D. latiflorus* (ma bamboo) has a culm 20–25 m tall and occurs from southern to southwestern China; the shoots are sweet and eaten as a vegetable, the culm is used in building and the leaves for bamboo hats. *D. giganteus* has a culm up to 30 m tall and 20–30 cm thick, one of the largest bamboos.
4. *Phyllostachys*: culms scattered, cylindrical, flattened or grooved on the side of the branch, with 2 branches at each node. About 50 species, mostly in East Asia and centered in China. Moso bamboo (*P. edulis*, often given as *P. pubescens* in Chinese textbooks) is a tall, tree-like bamboo with culms up to 20 m, hairy and white-powdery when new and glabrous when old; the culm ring is flat and the sheath ring is raised, so there is only 1 ring at each node; the culm sheath is thick and leathery, densely covered on the back with brownish purple small bristles and brownish black blotches. Moso bamboo occurs in the Yangtze valley and the provinces to the south and is the bamboo species with the largest cultivated area and the highest economic value in China: the shoots are eaten (winter shoots, spring shoots), the culm is used for building, bamboo plywood and rafts and for splitting into strips for weaving, and the fibers are a raw material for paper. At the peak of growth the shoots of moso bamboo can grow about 1 m in a day and a night, one of the fastest-growing organs among plants; the culm reaches its final height about 2 months after emerging as a shoot and thereafter only lignifies and fills in, without further growth in height or thickness. The color of the culm changes with age, pale green and densely hairy in the first year, then gradually turning yellow-green to yellow, and culms four or five years old are generally cut for use. *P. reticulata* (often given as *P. bambusoides* in Chinese textbooks) has a raised culm ring and 2 conspicuous rings at each node, with yellow-brown culm sheaths with black-purple spots and a tough culm, an important timber bamboo. Black bamboo (*P. nigra*) has purplish black culms and is often cultivated for ornament.
5. Others: *Pseudosasa amabilis* has a straight, hard culm and is used to make ski poles, fishing rods and sports equipment; *Chimonobambusa quadrangularis* (square bamboo) has a nearly square culm; *Bashania faberi* and others are the main food of the giant panda.

### 39.3.6. Oryzoideae and Pooideae

*Oryza*: panicle terminal; spikelets laterally compressed, with 3 florets of which only the terminal one is fertile, the lower 2 reduced to tiny lemmas (sterile lemmas); glumes reduced to two crescent-shaped projections attached at the tip of the pedicel; the lemma of the fertile floret hard-papery, awned or awnless, 5-veined, the palea 3-veined; stamens 6. About 25 species, in the tropics of Asia, Africa, the Americas and Oceania; China has 3 wild species, *O. rufipogon*, *O. officinalis* and *O. meyeriana* subsp. *granulata*. Rice (*O. sativa*) is an annual crop with conspicuous auricles when young. Cultivated rice was domesticated from *O. rufipogon* and is divided into two subspecies (or groups), indica and japonica; the rice remains of the Shangshan site in Zhejiang (about 10,000 years ago) and the Hemudu site (about 7000 years ago) show that the middle and lower Yangtze is one of the centers of the origin of rice cultivation. During domestication, mutations of genes controlling shattering, such as *sh4* and *qSH1*, made the grains no longer drop at maturity, and a mutation of *PROG1*, which controls plant architecture, changed the prostrate habit of wild rice to an erect one (Huang et al. 2012 and others). Rice is the staple of half of the world's population, and China ranks first in the world in rice production. Another cultivated rice, African rice (*O. glaberrima*), was domesticated independently in Africa.

Other plants of Oryzoideae: *Zizania latifolia* (often given as *Z. caduciflora* in Chinese textbooks) grows in shallow water; when the culm base is infected by the smut fungus *Ustilago esculenta*, hormones secreted by the hyphae stimulate the stem to swell and become tender, the vegetable "jiaobai" (water bamboo); infected plants do not flower. The caryopses of the North American wild rice (*Z. palustris*) are a traditional food of Native Americans.

Pooideae are the main body of temperate grasses, including the cereals (tribe Triticeae), and the tribes Aveneae, Poeae, Stipeae and Bromeae. Kentucky bluegrass (*Poa pratensis*) and sheep fescue (*Festuca ovina*) of the Poeae and perennial ryegrass (*Lolium perenne*) are the main species of temperate lawns and pastures; orchard grass (*Dactylis glomerata*), Japanese brome (*Bromus japonicus*), *Elymus dahuricus* and crested wheatgrass (*Agropyron cristatum*) are all good forage grasses. Representative plants:

1. *Triticum* (wheat): annual or biennial herbs; the spike (compound spike) erect, terminal; spikelets sessile, solitary and alternate at the nodes of the rachis, laterally compressed, with 3–9 florets; glumes subcoriaceous, ovate, with 3 to several veins, the midvein raised into a keel; lemma thick-papery, often awned at the apex; caryopsis separate from the bracts and easily threshed. About 20 species (including several ploidy levels), in Europe, the Mediterranean and western Asia. Bread wheat (*T. aestivum*) has a culm up to 1 m or more tall with 6–7 nodes; the spike consists of about 10–20 spikelets; the caryopsis is elliptic with a deep longitudinal groove on the ventral side. Wheat is the crop with the largest planting area in the world and the main staple of northern China; bran is fodder, and the straw can be woven and made into paper.
2. *Hordeum* (barley): each node of the rachis bears 3 spikelets (a triplet), each spikelet with only 1 floret; glumes linear-lanceolate, extended at the tip into an awn-like point. Cultivated barley (*H. vulgare*) was domesticated in the Fertile Crescent from the wild *H. vulgare* subsp. *spontaneum*; those with all 3 spikelets fertile at each node are six-rowed barley, and those with only the middle one fertile are two-rowed barley, the difference being determined mainly by one gene (*Vrs1*). The caryopsis usually adheres to the palea and lemma at maturity (hulled barley) and is the raw material for beer and malt sugar and is also used as feed and in flour foods; in naked barley (hulless barley, *H. vulgare* var. *coeleste*, often given as var. *nudum* in Chinese textbooks, called "qingke") the caryopsis separates readily from the bracts at maturity, and it is the main staple of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, used to make tsampa and highland barley wine.
3. *Avena* (oat): panicle; spikelets pendent, with 2 to several florets; glumes herbaceous, longer than the lower florets, 7–11-veined; lemma often with a geniculate awn on the back. Oat (*A. sativa*) is hexaploid and was originally a weed of wheat fields, domesticated as a secondary crop as wheat and barley spread north; its caryopsis is rich in β-glucan. Naked oat (*A. nuda*) is cultivated in northern and northwestern China. Wild oat (*A. fatua*) has a rachilla densely covered with stiff hairs and brittle nodes that break easily and is a serious weed of wheat fields.
4. *Stipa* (feather grass, needle grass): perennial herbs; spikelets with only 1 floret; lemma slender, cylindrical, tightly rolled around the palea, with a long awn at the apex, jointed where the awn joins the lemma, the awn often twice geniculate and twisted. About 300 species, in the temperate zones around the world and one of the constructive species of the Eurasian steppe; China has about 30 species, mainly in the north and west. *S. bungeana* has leaves rolled lengthwise into needles and can be used as fodder before heading; at fruiting the fruit (with its sharp callus and long awn) easily pierces the skin and mouth of livestock and causes injury. The awn of feather grass is sensitive to humidity, straightening when it takes up moisture and twisting when dry, and together with the sharp callus it drills the caryopsis into the soil.
5. Others: rye (*Secale cereale*) is cold-hardy and used for rye bread; triticale (× *Triticosecale*) is an artificial allopolyploid of wheat and rye.

### 39.3.7. The Allohexaploid Origin of Wheat

Bread wheat is an allohexaploid ($2n = 6x = 42$, genome AABBDD) formed from three diploid ancestors ($2n = 2x = 14$) by two natural hybridizations and chromosome doublings (Fig. 39.3.3; [7.4.16](/7.4.16), [7.10.12](/7.10.12#sec-12.1.6)):

1. The A genome comes from *Triticum urartu* (AA); the B genome comes from a goatgrass such as *Aegilops speltoides* (SS, the ancestor of BB). The two hybridized naturally some hundreds of thousands of years ago, and the chromosomes of the hybrid doubled, forming the allotetraploid wild emmer wheat (*T. turgidum* subsp. *dicoccoides*, AABB, $2n = 28$).
2. About 10,000 years ago people in the Fertile Crescent (now southeastern Turkey, Syria and northern Iraq) domesticated wild emmer, giving cultivated emmer (AABB); its descendants include the durum wheat used today for pasta (*T. turgidum* subsp. *durum*).
3. Cultivated emmer spread east to the southern shore of the Caspian Sea and hybridized naturally with the local wild goatgrass (*Aegilops tauschii*, DD), and the chromosomes of the hybrid doubled, forming hexaploid bread wheat (AABBDD). This hybridization took place in human fields about 8000–9000 years ago.

The addition of the D genome adapted bread wheat to a wider range of climates (continental cold and drought), and the composition of its gluten proteins makes the flour suitable for leavened bread. The wheat genome is about 16 billion base pairs, about 5 times the human genome, and the three partially homoeologous subgenomes keep their own independent pairing (the *Ph1* gene prevents pairing between homoeologous chromosomes, so that meiosis of the hexaploid is as regular as in a diploid). The most important changes in domestication include a rachis that no longer breaks at maturity (mutation of the *Br* genes) and glumes that become soft and easily threshed (the *Q* and *Tg* genes).

### 39.3.8. Panicoideae and Other Subfamilies

In Panicoideae the spikelets are dorsiventrally compressed with 2 florets, the lower one sterile or male and the upper one fertile, and at maturity the whole spikelet falls together with the glumes; most are C4 plants, mainly in the tropics and subtropics. The tribe Paniceae (proso millet, foxtail millet, barnyard grass, crabgrass, fountain grass) and the tribe Andropogoneae (maize, sorghum, sugarcane, *Miscanthus*, cogongrass, Job's tears) are the two largest tribes. Representative plants:

1. *Setaria*: panicle compact, cylindrical; spikelets solitary or clustered, each subtended by 1 to several bristle-like sterile branchlets; the spikelet disarticulates on the cup-like pedicel and separates from the persistent bristles. Foxtail millet (*S. italica*) was domesticated from green foxtail (*S. viridis*) in northern China; the millet remains at the Cishan site in Hebei are eight to ten thousand years old, and foxtail millet and proso millet (*Panicum miliaceum*) were the earliest cereals of the Neolithic in the Yellow River valley. Green foxtail is a common weed of fields and is also a model plant for the study of C4 photosynthesis.
2. *Saccharum* (sugarcane): perennial herbs with stout, solid culms rich in sugar juice; the panicle large, the spikelets in pairs at each node of the axis, one sessile and one pedicelled, with long silky hairs at the callus; the axis disarticulates readily at each node. About 35 species (in the broad sense), mainly in the tropics and subtropics of Asia. The tropical species (*S. officinarum*, the "noble cane") is native to New Guinea, and the Chinese cane (*S. sinense*) is an ancient hybrid of it with a wild species; modern sugarcane cultivars are interspecific hybrids of the tropical species with *S. spontaneum*, with chromosome numbers of about 100–130, highly polyploid and aneuploid. The culm is used to make sugar and fuel ethanol, and the bagasse is used for paper and electricity generation.
3. *Sorghum*: panicle; spikelets in pairs at each node of the axis, the sessile spikelet bisexual and the pedicelled spikelet male or neuter. Sorghum (*S. bicolor*, often given as *S. vulgare* in Chinese textbooks) has a solid culm with prop roots at the base and is native to Africa (from Sudan to Ethiopia); it is widely cultivated in northeastern China, and the grain is eaten, made into malt syrup and brewed into sorghum liquor, and the culm of sweet sorghum contains sugar; it is drought- and heat-tolerant and is an important staple in the drylands of Africa. Sudan grass (*S. sudanense*) is a good forage grass.
4. *Zea*: maize (*Z. mays*) is an annual crop with an erect, solid culm, often with prop roots (aerial roots) at the lower nodes; monoecious: the top of the culm bears a spreading, paniculate male inflorescence (tassel) with male spikelets in pairs; a cylindrical female inflorescence (ear) arises in a leaf axil, enclosed in many sheathing husk leaves, with female spikelets in rows on a thick axis, and each female flower has an extremely long style protruding beyond the husks, the "corn silk". The embryo of maize is high in oil and can be pressed for oil; the cob can be used to make furfural; the styles are used in medicine; the stalks and leaves are fodder. Maize is now the cereal with the highest production in the world.
5. Others: in Job's tears (*Coix lacryma-jobi*) the female spikelet is enclosed in a hard, bead-like involucre formed from a leaf sheath, with male spikelets above it in a raceme; the caryopsis is eaten and used in medicine. Barnyard grass (*Echinochloa crus-galli*) is the commonest weed of rice fields and resembles rice in the seedling stage but has no ligule or auricles. *Miscanthus sinensis* and *M. sacchariflorus* are common tall grasses, and the hybrid *M.* × *giganteus* is used as a biomass energy plant; cogongrass (*Imperata cylindrica*) is a cosmopolitan noxious weed, and the rhizome is used in medicine; lemongrass (*Cymbopogon citratus*) is used to extract aromatic oil.

The origin of maize. The female ear of maize is unlike that of any wild grass, and its ancestor was long a mystery. G. Beadle and others showed between the 1930s and 1970s that maize and the annual Mexican weed teosinte can hybridize freely and are the same species. Molecular marker studies established that maize originated only once, from the small-glumed teosinte (*Zea mays* subsp. *parviglumis*) of the Balsas River valley in southern Mexico, and that domestication took place about 9000 years ago (Matsuoka et al. 2002). The female "ear" of teosinte has only 5–12 seeds in two ranks, each enclosed in a hard fruitcase formed by the rachis internode and the glume, and the rachis disarticulates at each node at maturity; the ear of maize has hundreds of naked kernels arranged in 8–20 rows and does not disarticulate at maturity. These great differences are determined mainly by a few genes: variation in the regulatory region of *tb1* (*teosinte branched1*) gives maize a single strong main culm with shortened lateral branches, the ear borne at the tip of a shortened lateral branch (Doebley et al. 1997); one amino acid substitution in *tga1* (*teosinte glume architecture1*) causes the hard fruitcase to degenerate and the kernels to be naked (Wang et al. 2005). The example of maize shows that large morphological changes in crop domestication can arise from mutations in a few regulatory genes.

Other subfamilies: Chloridoideae are C4 grasses of dry regions and include teff (*Eragrostis tef*, the staple of Ethiopia), finger millet (*Eleusine coracana*), goosegrass (*E. indica*, a common weed) and the lawn grasses bermudagrass (*Cynodon dactylon*) and zoysia (*Zoysia japonica*); *Spartina* (cordgrass) is a pioneer of coastal salt marshes, and *S. anglica* and *S. alterniflora* were introduced to the coast of China to protect beaches and dikes, then spread over large areas of coastal mudflats, crowding out native coastal plants and mangroves, and are now listed as invasive species (the Chinese textbooks mostly introduce it as an "excellent coastal pioneer plant"). In Arundinoideae, the common reed (*Phragmites australis*, often given as *P. communis* in Chinese textbooks) is one of the most widely distributed plants in the world, perennial with stout rhizomes, growing in wetlands of lakes, riverbanks and seashores, with a large panicle and spikelets of 4–7 florets with long silky hairs on the callus; the reed is an excellent plant for binding dikes, the culm is used for paper, building and weaving, and the rhizome ("lugen") is used in medicine; giant reed (*Arundo donax*) is taller still, and its culm can be made into reeds for musical instruments.

### 39.3.9. Origin and Domestication of Cereal Crops

Several cereals of Poaceae were domesticated independently in several different centers of agricultural origin in the world (**domestication**): wheat, barley, rye and oat in the Fertile Crescent of western Asia (about 11,000–10,000 years ago), rice and foxtail and proso millet in China (the Yangtze and Yellow River valleys, about 10,000–8000 years ago), maize in Mexico (about 9000 years ago), sorghum, pearl millet and African rice in the Sahel of Africa, and teff in Ethiopia. Different cereals underwent a similar set of changes in characters during domestication, called the *domestication syndrome*:

1. Non-shattering at maturity: in wild grasses the rachis or spikelet breaks at maturity and the seeds scatter; in cultivated forms the rachis is tough and waits for human harvest (*Br* in wheat and barley, *sh4* in rice).
2. Free threshing: the glumes become soft or separate from the caryopsis (*Q* in wheat, *tga1* in maize).
3. Larger grains, more of them, with uniform germination (loss of dormancy).
4. Compact plant form: branching or tillering reduced and erect (*tb1* in maize, *PROG1* in rice).
5. Uniform maturity and a changed response to day length, allowing the crop to spread to high latitudes.

These same changes are caused in different cereals by different genes or by homologous genes, and are **convergent evolution** under artificial selection. The "Green Revolution" of the 1960s then produced dwarf varieties of wheat (*Rht* genes) and rice (the *sd1* gene): these are all mutants in gibberellin synthesis or signaling, with short culms resistant to lodging, able to withstand heavy fertilization without lodging, which multiplied grain yield per unit area.

Key characters of the family: culm cylindrical, with nodes, the internodes hollow; leaves 2-ranked, sheaths split, usually with a ligule and auricles; the inflorescence built on spikelets, the floret with lemma, palea and 2 lodicules, stamens 3 (or 6), stigmas feathery; caryopsis.

Key points:

- The similarities of Cyperaceae and Poaceae (simplified perianth, 1 locule with 1 ovule, wind pollination) are convergent evolution; they belong to the cyperids (related to Juncaceae) and the graminids (related to Ecdeiocoleaceae) respectively.

## Questions

1. How can one tell whether a non-flowering herb belongs to Poaceae or Cyperaceae?

   **Answer** Look at the culm, leaves and leaf sheaths. In Cyperaceae the culm is often triangular and solid, without nodes except at the base; the leaves are in 3 ranks; the sheath is closed into a tube, and there is generally no ligule or auricle. In Poaceae the culm is cylindrical with conspicuous nodes and often hollow internodes; the leaves are in 2 ranks; the sheath is split on one side, there is often a ligule between blade and sheath, and the top of the sheath often bears auricles. In flower one can also look at the flower: in Cyperaceae each flower has only 1 scale, the stigmas are 2–3 and not feathery, and the fruit is a nutlet; in Poaceae the spikelet has 2 glumes at its base, each floret has a lemma, a palea and 2 lodicules, the stigmas are feathery, and the fruit is a caryopsis.

2. In the grass spikelet, what parts of an ordinary flower do the glumes, lemma, palea and lodicules correspond to? Why is a floret said to be "a flower plus its bracts"?

   **Answer** The glumes are bracts at the base of the spikelet (a compressed spike); the lemma is the bract of each floret; the palea is generally regarded as a bracteole (prophyll), although the Chinese textbooks also interpret it as the outer perianth whorl; the lodicules correspond to the inner perianth whorl, and molecular evidence (class B genes are expressed in the lodicules, and in mutants the lodicules are transformed into bract-like organs) supports their homology with petals. Only the lodicules, stamens and pistil belong to the "flower" itself, and the lemma and palea are the bract and bracteole that enclose it, so a floret is a simplified flower plus its bracts.

3. How did bread wheat arise? Why is it called a "species formed in human fields"?

   **Answer** *Triticum urartu* (AA) hybridized naturally in the wild with a goatgrass (BB), and the chromosomes of the hybrid doubled to form wild emmer (AABB); about 10,000 years ago it was domesticated in the Fertile Crescent as cultivated emmer. Cultivated emmer spread with agriculture to the southern shore of the Caspian Sea and hybridized in the field with wild *Aegilops tauschii* (DD), and the chromosomes of the hybrid doubled to form bread wheat (AABBDD). The second hybridization could occur only in fields where cultivated emmer planted by people met the local wild grass, and bread wheat has no known wild form, so it is a species formed after the appearance of agriculture, in human fields.

4. C4 photosynthesis arose more than 20 times in Poaceae, yet all in the PACMAD clade. How can this be explained?

   **Answer** C4 photosynthesis requires a series of coordinated changes in leaf anatomy and enzyme systems, and its origin presupposes some "preadaptations": a small distance between veins, fairly large bundle sheath cells, and many organelles in the bundle sheath, which make it possible for the bundle sheath to take over the function of the second fixation of CO₂. The ancestor of the PACMAD clade already had these characters, so under the conditions of declining atmospheric CO₂ and drying climate since the Oligocene C4 photosynthesis could appear repeatedly and independently in it; the BOP clade has a large distance between veins and lacks these preadaptations, and mostly grows in forests, wetlands or cold temperate regions where the advantage of C4 photosynthesis is not obvious, so no C4 lineages arose there.

5. Compare maize and teosinte, and explain what morphological changes occurred in crop domestication and what their genetic basis is.

   **Answer** Teosinte is much branched, with a small ear of only 5–12 seeds in two ranks, each enclosed in a hard fruitcase, and the rachis breaks at each node at maturity; maize has only one main culm with shortened lateral branches, the ear borne at the tip of a short lateral branch enclosed in husks, with hundreds of naked kernels in several rows, and does not disarticulate at maturity. These changes are caused mainly by mutations in a few genes: variation in the regulatory region of *tb1* raised its expression and suppresses growth of lateral branches, so that the plant has a single main culm and the ear is borne on shortened lateral branches; one amino acid substitution in *tga1* causes the fruitcase to degenerate and the kernels to be naked. In addition, the increased number of kernel rows and the non-breaking rachis are also controlled by a few genes. This shows that large morphological changes in domestication can arise from mutations in a few regulatory genes together with continued human selection.
