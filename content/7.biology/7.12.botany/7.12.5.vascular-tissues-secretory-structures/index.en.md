The meristems, protective tissues and ground tissues of the previous chapter have counterparts in both bryophytes and vascular plants. The vascular tissues treated in this chapter are unique to vascular plants. Xylem carries water and mineral salts, and phloem carries organic nutrients. Together they form the *vascular tissue*, which is built from conducting cells that are elongated, tube-like and joined end to end, forming a continuous system throughout the plant body. Vascular tissue allows a plant to grow tall and to rise above the moist ground, and it is one of the most important innovations of the colonization of land. Its origin and the evolution of the stele are discussed in [7.12.16](/7.12.16). This chapter also treats the secretory structures scattered through many tissues, and finally groups all tissues into three tissue systems, preparing for the chapters on root, stem and leaf ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)–[7.12.23](/7.12.23)). The physiological mechanisms of water and assimilate transport (the cohesion–tension theory of transpiration and the pressure-flow hypothesis) belong to plant physiology.

## 5.1. Xylem

**Definition 5.1.1 (Xylem)** The complex tissue that consists of tracheids, vessel elements (water-conducting cells), wood fibers and xylem parenchyma cells, transports mainly water and the mineral salts dissolved in it, and also gives mechanical support, is called the **xylem**.

| Cell type | At maturity | Main function |
|---|---|---|
| Tracheid | Dead | Water conduction, with some support |
| Vessel element (joined into a vessel) | Dead | Water conduction |
| Wood fiber | Mostly dead | Support |
| Xylem parenchyma cell (including the parenchyma cells of xylem rays) | Living | Storage, lateral transport |

Tracheids and vessel elements are together called tracheary elements, the most important members of the xylem. Both are thick-walled, elongated cells that lack a living protoplast at maturity. Their secondary walls bear lignified thickenings of various patterns, which appear on the wall as annular, helical (spiral), scalariform, reticulate and pitted types (Figure 1).

### 5.1.1. Tracheids

**Definition 5.1.2 (Tracheid)** A *tracheid* is a single tube-like dead cell with wedge-shaped or obliquely tapered ends and no perforations in the end walls. Its secondary wall is lignified and thickened in various patterns, and water passes from one cell to the next through pits in the wall.

When tracheids are arranged lengthwise in an organ, the ends of cells above and below overlap closely and obliquely. Water moves upward through the pits in the overlapping side walls (mostly bordered pits, [7.12.3](/7.12.3)). Each tracheid is a separate conducting unit, generally $1$–$5\,\mathrm{mm}$ long. The walls are thick and the oblique ends overlap, so tracheids also provide support. All vascular plants have tracheids. In most ferns and gymnosperms the water-conducting cells are tracheids only. In angiosperms tracheids are relatively few and usually occur together with vessels.

### 5.1.2. Vessel Elements and Vessels

**Definition 5.1.3 (Vessel Elements, Vessels and Perforation Plates)** A tube-like dead cell whose end wall dissolves during development, leaving one or several large openings (**perforations**), is called a **vessel element**; the end wall bearing perforations is called a **perforation plate**. A longitudinal series of many vessel elements communicating directly through their perforations is called a **vessel**.

Development of a vessel element: the cell that will form a vessel element first enlarges rapidly, and its nucleus enlarges too, often becoming polyploid through **endoreplication**, which increases gene copy number and speeds the synthesis of wall material. Once the cell and nucleus stop enlarging, the side walls undergo secondary thickening and lignification in a specific pattern, whereas the future perforation sites have only a slightly thickened primary wall. After the secondary wall is complete, the tonoplast ruptures and the hydrolytic enzymes released digest the protoplast (a form of **programmed cell death**). The unlignified primary wall and middle lamella of the end wall are also hydrolyzed, forming the perforation, and the non-cellulosic polysaccharides in the pit membranes of the side walls are partly hydrolyzed while the cellulose framework remains. A vessel element is therefore a cell that completes its differentiation by "suicide", and its function is carried out entirely by the wall left after death.

The water-conducting capacity of a vessel made of many vessel elements depends only on its diameter and perforations, not on the length of the individual elements. Vessels vary in length from a few centimeters to about a meter, and in some lianas they reach several meters. After reaching a certain length a vessel closes, forming a blind end. Successive vessels overlap at their blind ends, and water flows from one vessel to the next through pits in the side walls, so the flow in vessels is not a straight line but somewhat tortuous. Vessels are generally wider than tracheids and therefore conduct water more efficiently. In angiosperms, except for a few primitive groups, the xylem consists mainly of vessels, which is one of the important reasons angiosperms are better adapted to terrestrial environments.

Types of perforation plates: usually the end wall of a vessel element almost completely disappears, leaving only a rim around it. Such a plate has a single opening and is called a simple perforation plate; it is mostly horizontal. In many woody plants the end wall of the vessel element has several to many parallel openings arranged like the rungs of a ladder, forming a scalariform perforation plate (a kind of multiple perforation plate), which is more or less oblique. In *Ephedra* and some others the end wall has many small round holes, forming an ephedroid (foraminate) perforation plate. Reticulate perforation plates also occur.

Types of secondary-wall thickening: the secondary wall of tracheary elements has five patterns of thickening, which in one organ generally differentiate in the following order (Figure 1):

| Type | Pattern of the secondary wall | Capacity to elongate | Where it occurs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annular | Parallel rings spaced well apart, with unlignified primary wall between them | Can be stretched as the organ elongates | Earliest protoxylem |
| Helical (spiral) | Spiral bands, sometimes branched | Can be stretched | Protoxylem |
| Scalariform | Transverse bars, like a denser spiral with a low pitch and longitudinal connections between neighboring turns, looking like the rungs of a ladder | Slight capacity to elongate | Metaxylem |
| Reticulate | Thickened walls interwoven into an irregular net; the meshes are unthickened areas | Cannot elongate | Metaxylem |
| Pitted | Wall thickened as a continuous sheet, with only pits left (mostly bordered pits, also simple pits) | Cannot elongate | Metaxylem and secondary xylem (the most common type) |

The earliest-differentiating **protoxylem** matures while the organ is still elongating. Its annular and helical elements can be stretched without hindering growth. The **metaxylem** that differentiates after elongation has stopped, and the secondary xylem, consist mainly of reticulate and pitted elements, which are strong and conduct water efficiently. The five types do not necessarily all occur in a given plant or organ, and there are intermediate types between two patterns, such as scalariform–reticulate and reticulate–pitted. The annular and helical vessels of the protoxylem are often torn as the organ continues to elongate, leaving a cavity (protoxylem lacuna) in the vascular bundles of grass stems and roots.

### 5.1.3. Wood Fibers, Xylem Parenchyma and Tyloses

Fibers in the xylem are called wood fibers. They are elongated cells with sharp ends, and within the same plant their walls are generally thicker than those of tracheids and strongly lignified. The protoplast is usually dead at maturity, though in some plants wood fibers stay alive for a long time. Wood fibers give the xylem a supporting function. According to their pits, wood fibers are divided into fiber-tracheids and libriform fibers ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)).

The living parenchyma cells of the xylem are called xylem parenchyma cells. Their walls usually also lignify late in development, and they often contain starch and crystals and function in storage. Together with the parenchyma cells of the xylem rays, they are the only living cells in the xylem, responsible for lateral transport and storage and for responding when vessels are damaged.

When the vessels of many trees age, or are injured or infected, neighboring xylem parenchyma or ray parenchyma cells grow balloon-like outgrowths through the pits into the vessel lumen. These are called *tyloses* (singular *tylosis*). The walls of tyloses may lignify, and resin, tannins and other substances may accumulate in them. They plug the vessel so that it loses its conducting ability, but they also prevent pathogens and air from spreading through the wood. Heartwood often contains large numbers of tyloses ([7.12.22](/7.12.22)). The vessels of white oak wood are blocked by tyloses and the wood is impermeable to liquids, which is why it is suitable for wine and whiskey barrels, whereas red oak wood has few tyloses and cannot be used for barrels.

### 5.1.4. Evolution of Water-Conducting Cells

The tracheid is the most primitive water-conducting cell. In the evolution of vascular plants it specialized in two directions. In one the cell wall became thicker and the pits narrower, giving fiber-tracheids and libriform fibers specialized for support. In the other the end walls dissolved and perforations appeared, giving vessel elements specialized for conduction.

Repeated origins of vessels: vessels are not found only in angiosperms. They occur in the lycophyte genus *Selaginella*, in *Equisetum*, in ferns such as bracken (*Pteridium aquilinum*) and *Marsilea*, and among gymnosperms in the gnetophytes (*Ephedra*, *Gnetum* and *Welwitschia*). The vessels of these groups arose from different tracheids and differ in form. Gnetophyte vessels derive from the dissolution of the pit membranes of tracheids with circular bordered pits, so their perforation plates are the multiperforate ephedroid type. Angiosperm vessels are generally thought to have arisen from tracheids with scalariform pitting. Vessels have therefore originated independently at least several times among vascular plants, a classic case of **convergent evolution**, and cannot be taken as evidence that *Selaginella* and *Equisetum* are closely related to angiosperms. Conversely, a few angiosperm lineages lack vessels and have only tracheids: *Amborella trichopoda* (the first branch to diverge in the angiosperm phylogenetic tree), Winteraceae, Trochodendraceae and others. The vessel-less condition of *Amborella* is regarded as primitive for angiosperms, while that of the latter two may be a secondary loss ([7.12.34](/7.12.34)).

Evolutionary trends of vessel elements: by comparing the secondary xylem of many angiosperms, Bailey and Tupper (1918) and Frost (1930) identified the following trends in the evolution of vessel elements from tracheids:

| Feature | More primitive | More advanced |
|---|---|---|
| Length | Long (close to tracheids) | Short |
| Diameter | Narrow, polygonal in cross section | Wide, circular in cross section |
| End wall | Oblique | Transverse |
| Perforation plate | Scalariform, with many bars | Simple |
| Lateral wall pitting | Scalariform pitting | Opposite pitting, then alternate pitting |

Plants whose vessel elements are long and narrow, with oblique end walls and scalariform perforation plates, are considered more primitive in evolutionary systematics. These "Bailey trends" are the basic framework of angiosperm wood anatomy and agree broadly with molecular phylogenies. Modern phylogenetic analyses have also shown, however, that in some groups a simple perforation plate can revert to a scalariform one (for example in cold, moist habitats), so the state of a single character cannot be used mechanically to judge primitive or advanced status, and must be interpreted on a phylogenetic tree ([7.12.33](/7.12.33)).

The trade-off between efficiency and safety: water flow in vessels approximately obeys the **Hagen–Poiseuille law**. For a circular tube of radius $r$ and length $L$ under a pressure difference $\Delta p$, the volume flow rate is

$$
Q = \frac{\pi r^4}{8\eta L}\,\Delta p,
\tag{5.1}
$$

where $\eta$ is the viscosity of water. The flow rate is proportional to the fourth power of the radius: a vessel of twice the diameter conducts 16 times as much water, and one vessel of diameter $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ is equivalent to ten thousand tracheids of diameter $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. This is why vessels are more efficient than tracheids, and it explains why a few large vessels carry most of the water flow of a tree. But wide vessels are also more vulnerable. The water in xylem is under negative pressure (tension), and during freezing and thawing or drought dissolved gases come out of solution and form bubbles. Once a vessel is blocked by an air embolism, the whole vessel stops functioning, and wide, long vessels are more prone to freeze–thaw embolism. Tracheids are each only a few millimeters long, and bordered pits with a torus confine an embolism to a single cell ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)), so conifers can dominate cold and dry environments. Ring-porous trees, which produce new wide vessels each spring and let them become embolized and plugged with tyloses after use ([7.12.22](/7.12.22)), represent another solution to this trade-off.

**Example 5.1.1** Suppose the secondary xylem of an angiosperm has two kinds of vessels, with diameters $40\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $160\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, in the numerical ratio $20 : 1$ in cross section. By Eq. (5.1), the ratio of the flow rates of single vessels is $(160/40)^4 = 256$. The wide vessels, although they make up less than 5% of all vessels, therefore carry $256/(256 + 20) \approx 93\%$ of the flow. The actual flow is also affected by the resistance of pits and perforation plates and is usually well below the theoretical value, but the conclusion that wide vessels dominate holds.

## 5.2. Phloem

**Definition 5.2.1 (Phloem)** The complex tissue that consists of sieve-tube elements or sieve cells, companion cells, phloem parenchyma cells and phloem fibers, and transports mainly organic nutrients, is called the **phloem**.

Unlike in the xylem, the sieve-tube elements and sieve cells that carry out the transport in the phloem are living cells.

### 5.2.1. Sieve-Tube Elements and Sieve Tubes

**Definition 5.2.2 (Sieve-Tube Elements, Sieve Tubes and Sieve Plates)** In the phloem of angiosperms, a tube-like cell with only a primary wall, with many large pores (sieve pores) differentiated in its end walls, and with a living protoplast but no nucleus at maturity is called a **sieve-tube element**. Sieve-tube elements are joined lengthwise into long cell series called **sieve tubes**, the structures that transport photosynthetic products over long distances in angiosperms. An end wall bearing sieve pores is called a **sieve plate**, and a region where the pores are concentrated is called a *sieve area*. A plate with one sieve area is a simple sieve plate, and one with several sieve areas is a compound sieve plate.

The wall of a sieve-tube element is a primary wall, composed mainly of pectin and cellulose, often thick and shiny (nacreous wall). Sieve pores are enlarged channels of plasmodesmata, up to $1$–$15\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter. Thick strands of cytoplasm (connecting strands) pass through the pores and join the protoplasts of successive sieve-tube elements closely. The side walls of a sieve-tube element bear many specialized primary pit fields, also called sieve areas, whose pores are larger than those of ordinary parenchyma primary pit fields and are crossed by cytoplasmic strands thicker than plasmodesmata, allowing close exchange between the sieve-tube element and the neighboring cells. Simple sieve plates are mostly horizontal, whereas the sieve areas of compound plates are usually arranged in parallel like a ladder on an oblique wall (Figure 2D). The evolutionary trend of sieve plates is exactly the same as that of vessel perforation plates: from oblique to horizontal and from compound to simple, and sieve-tube elements change from long and narrow to short and wide.

**Definition 5.2.3 (Callose)** The β-1,3-glucan that lines the sieve pores and accumulates in quantity on sieve plates is called **callose**.

The mature sieve-tube element: during development the nucleus breaks down and the tonoplast disappears, and organelles such as ribosomes, the Golgi apparatus and microtubules disintegrate. Only a thin layer of cytoplasm remains against the wall, retaining mitochondria, plastids, smooth endoplasmic reticulum and P-protein (Figure 2A).

- P-protein (phloem protein) is a protein specific to the sieve-tube elements of angiosperms. Its form varies among species: tubular, fibrillar, granular or crystalline. It is usually dispersed in the cytoplasm. When the phloem is disturbed or injured, it is swept by the surge of sap toward the wound to the sieve pores, where it aggregates into a slimy plug that blocks the pores and prevents loss of sap (Figure 2B). Legume sieve tubes also contain a crystalline P-protein (the forisome) that swells and shrinks reversibly with the concentration of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and opens and closes the sieve pores like a valve. The older view, found in Chinese textbooks, that P-protein bodies are contractile proteins taking part in transport is no longer accepted.
- The plastids of sieve-tube elements differ in content and shape from those of other cells: some contain only starch (S-type), others protein crystals or filaments (P-type). P-type plastids occur in all monocots and in a few other groups such as Caryophyllales. Different subtypes can be distinguished under the electron microscope, and they are one of the characters used to delimit larger taxa (Behnke's work).
- The functional life of a sieve tube is short, only one growing season in most plants. In some woody plants (grape, linden) sieve tubes can persist for two years or more. In aging or dormant sieve tubes, callose accumulates heavily on the sieve plates, forming a pad-like callus that seals the pores. When the sieve tubes become active again the following spring, the callus dissolves and the connecting strands are reopened. The sieve tubes of woody monocots without secondary growth (such as palms) are used for life, and may remain functional for decades or even more than a hundred years. When the plant is wounded, sieve-tube elements synthesize callose within seconds to minutes to seal the sieve pores and prevent loss of nutrients. A sieve tube that has died loses turgor and is crushed by the surrounding living tissue.

Organic substances (mainly sucrose) move in sieve tubes at speeds of generally tens of centimeters to about a meter per hour, far faster than diffusion. The driving mechanism (the pressure-flow hypothesis) belongs to plant physiology.

### 5.2.2. Companion Cells

**Definition 5.2.4 (Companion Cell)** In the phloem of angiosperms, a narrow, elongated parenchyma cell that is adjacent to a sieve-tube element, arises from the same mother cell, and has a nucleus and abundant organelles is called a **companion cell**.

In forming a sieve-tube element, the mother cell undergoes one unequal longitudinal division, producing a large cell and a small cell: the large cell develops into the sieve-tube element and the small one into the companion cell (Figure 2E). In some plants the large cell divides longitudinally again, so that one sieve-tube element has several companion cells beside it. In the secondary phloem of some woody plants the small cell divides transversely several times, giving a vertical file of companion cells beside one sieve-tube element. The companion cell has a large nucleus and dense cytoplasm rich in mitochondria and ribosomes. The wall between it and the sieve-tube element bears dense plasmodesmata that branch on the companion-cell side and merge into a single pore on the sieve-tube side (the pore–plasmodesmata unit), so the two are together called the sieve-tube element–companion cell complex. A sieve-tube element, which lacks a nucleus and ribosomes, depends on its companion cell for protein and energy, and when the sieve tube dies, the adjacent companion cells die with it. In minor veins of some plants, companion cells develop into transfer cells ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)) that load sugars from the mesophyll into the sieve tubes.

Some study notes describe a "callose sheath" as a structure of vessels. This is wrong: callose lines the sieve pores and connecting strands of sieve tubes, whereas vessels are dead cells and contain no callose.

### 5.2.3. Sieve Cells and Albuminous Cells

**Definition 5.2.5 (Sieve Cell)** In the phloem of gymnosperms and ferns, a living cell with tapered, obliquely overlapping ends that transports organic substances, whose walls have only sieve areas and no sieve plates, and whose protoplast contains no P-protein, is called a **sieve cell**.

Gymnosperms and ferns generally lack sieve tubes, and the cells that transport organic substances are sieve cells. The relation of the sieve cell to the sieve-tube element resembles that of the tracheid to the vessel element: it acts singly, with sieve areas on its overlapping ends and side walls, and the pores of each sieve area are small and traversed by membranes of the endoplasmic reticulum. There are no companion cells beside sieve cells, but the phloem parenchyma or phloem rays of gymnosperms contain *albuminous cells* (Strasburger cells), which develop from phloem parenchyma or ray parenchyma cells. They have dense cytoplasm rich in endoplasmic reticulum, ribosomes and mitochondria, stain darkly and do not store starch. Many plasmodesmata cross the wall between an albuminous cell and a sieve cell, branching on the albuminous-cell side. Albuminous cells and sieve cells usually do not share a common origin, but albuminous cells are functionally equivalent to companion cells, and when a sieve cell dies the adjacent albuminous cells die with it. The sieve cells of ferns have neither companion cells nor typical albuminous cells beside them.

### 5.2.4. Phloem Fibers and Phloem Parenchyma

The supporting cells of the phloem are the phloem fibers. Their walls are weakly lignified or unlignified, so they are tough and strongly resistant to bending, and they are important textile raw materials (ramie, flax, jute and others, [7.12.4](/7.12.4)). The parenchyma cells of the phloem function mainly in storage and lateral transport and often contain crystals and various storage products. In some plants the phloem parenchyma also contains secretory structures such as laticifers and secretory canals (Section 5.4).

## 5.3. Vascular Bundles

Xylem and phloem are two complex tissues consisting mainly of conducting cells (vessel elements, tracheids and sieve-tube elements, sieve cells), and morphologically they are called vascular tissue, separately or together. In many organs the vascular tissue differentiates from the procambium and is distributed in bundles within the ground tissue.

**Definition 5.3.1 (Vascular Bundle)** A strand-like structure that differentiates from the procambium and consists of primary xylem and primary phloem together is called a **vascular bundle**. In some vascular bundles a layer of fascicular cambium is retained between the xylem and the phloem.

The vascular bundle is the basic unit of vascular tissue in stems, leaves (leaf veins) and floral parts. It is often surrounded by a bundle sheath of one or several layers of parenchyma or sclerenchyma cells (for the bundle sheath of grasses and C4 plants see [7.12.23](/7.12.23)). Vascular bundles are classified in two ways (Figure 3).

By the presence of cambium: in a closed bundle the procambium differentiates entirely into primary xylem and primary phloem, no cambium is retained, and no secondary growth is possible, as in the stems of most monocots. An open bundle retains a fascicular cambium between xylem and phloem and is capable of secondary growth, producing secondary xylem and secondary phloem, as in the stems of gymnosperms and most eudicots.

By the arrangement of xylem and phloem:

| Type | Arrangement | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Collateral | Phloem outside and xylem inside, side by side; according to the presence of cambium, further divided into open collateral and closed collateral bundles | Stems of most seed plants; open collateral in most eudicots, closed collateral in most monocots |
| Bicollateral | Phloem on both the inner and outer sides of the xylem, with cambium between the outer phloem and the xylem | Stems of Cucurbitaceae (pumpkin), Solanaceae (tomato, potato), Convolvulaceae (sweet potato), Apocynaceae and Myrtaceae |
| Amphicribral | Xylem in the center, surrounded by phloem | Rhizomes of most ferns, a few parts of angiosperms such as stamen filaments, and the stem of *Begonia* |
| Amphivasal | Phloem in the center, surrounded by xylem | Rhizomes of some monocots, such as *Acorus* and *Convallaria*; secondary bundles of *Dracaena* and others |
| Radial | Primary xylem and primary phloem alternate on different radii and are not connected | Primary structure of the root ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)) |

Amphicribral and amphivasal bundles are together called concentric bundles. Strictly speaking, the "radial bundle" of the root is not a bundle at all, but an arrangement in a stele in which xylem and phloem are separate.

The relation among vascular bundle, vascular tissue and stele is as follows. Vascular tissue is the collective name for xylem and phloem. The vascular bundle is the unit in which vascular tissue is distributed in strands in many organs. The *stele* is the central axis of a stem or root consisting of all tissues inside the cortex (the vascular tissue together with the intervening parenchyma, the pith and the pericycle), a concept that describes, from the viewpoint of phylogeny, the overall arrangement of vascular tissue in the axis. The stele types of ferns and seed plants (protostele, siphonostele, dictyostele, eustele, atactostele) are treated in [7.12.16](/7.12.16).

## 5.4. Secretory Structures

Certain plant cells synthesize special organic or inorganic substances and either excrete them from the body, secrete them outside the cell, or accumulate them inside the cell. This phenomenon is called secretion. Plant secretions are extremely varied: sugars, essential oils, organic acids, alkaloids, tannins, resins, oils, proteins, enzymes, bactericidal substances, growth substances, vitamins and many mineral salts. They serve many roles in the life of the plant. Roots secrete organic acids, enzymes and other substances into the soil, converting sparingly soluble salts into absorbable forms and attracting particular microbes to form the rhizosphere microbiome. Flowers secrete nectar and fragrant oils to attract animal pollinators. Some secretions inhibit or kill pathogens and other plants (allelopathy), or are toxic to animals and so protect the plant itself, and some are excretory or storage products. Many secretions have major economic value, such as rubber, lacquer, aromatic oils, nectar and drugs.

**Definition 5.4.1 (Secretory Structure)** The cells or cell assemblies in the plant body that produce, store or discharge secretions are collectively called **secretory structures**. According to whether the secretion is discharged from the body, they fall into two broad classes: **external secretory structures** and **internal secretory structures**.

The cells that produce secretions have diverse origins and forms. Some are scattered singly in other tissues, while others are concentrated or specialized into definite structures. Secretory cells generally have dense cytoplasm and a large nucleus, with well-developed endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus or plastids (which synthesize terpenoids), and are often transfer cells.

### 5.4.1. External Secretory Structures

The cells of external secretory structures discharge their secretions onto the surface of the plant. The common ones are glandular epidermis, glandular hairs, nectaries, hydathodes and salt glands.

Glandular epidermis: in some parts of the plant the epidermal cells are glandular and secretory. For example, the stigma epidermal cells of petunia and sumac form papillae with dense cytoplasm and a thin cuticle, and secrete a mucilage containing sugars, amino acids and phenolic compounds that favors pollen adhesion and germination ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)).

Glandular hairs: a glandular hair is a secretory hair-like outgrowth of the epidermis, generally consisting of a head and a stalk. The head consists of one or several secretory cells, and the stalk of non-secreting cells, attached to the epidermis. In many glandular hairs the side walls of the basal stalk cells are completely cutinized or suberized, much like the Casparian strip of the endodermis, which prevents the secretion from flowing back through the walls of the stalk cells. The secreted substances often accumulate first between the wall of the head cells and the cuticle, blistering the cuticle, and are then released through rupture of the cuticle or through pores (Figure 4A). Examples:

- The peltate glandular hairs of mint, lavender and geranium secrete essential oils (monoterpenes), the glandular hairs of tobacco and cotton secrete various secondary metabolites, and the glandular hairs on the female bracts of hemp secrete a cannabinoid-containing resin.
- Young leaves of pear (*Pyrus*), hickory (*Carya*), birch (*Betula*) and others bear mucilage hairs (colleters) that secrete gums covering the bud and protecting it.
- The glandular hairs of carnivorous plants secrete nectar, mucilage and digestive enzymes to attract, trap and digest insects. The "tentacles" on the leaves of sundew (*Drosera*) are outgrowths with vascular bundles and glandular tips ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)).
- The stinging hairs of nettles (*Urtica*) break on contact with skin, penetrate it and inject irritating substances ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)), and are often counted as glandular hairs.

**Definition 5.4.2 (Nectary)** An external secretory structure that secretes a sugar solution (nectar) is called a **nectary**. Those in flowers are called floral nectaries, and those on vegetative organs such as stems, leaves, petioles, stipules and bracts are called extrafloral nectaries.

Floral nectaries are found on the calyx, petals, stamens, base of the pistil or receptacle of insect-pollinated flowers, for example the receptacle epidermis between stamens and pistil in milkvetch (*Astragalus*), the inner epidermis of the spur of nasturtium (*Tropaeolum*), and the nectar pits at the petal bases of buttercup (*Ranunculus*). Extrafloral nectaries include the purple-black spot on the stipules of broad bean, the glands at the tip of the petiole of cherry and peach, the glands on the petiole of castor bean and those on the petiole of passionflower. The ants they attract protect the plant against herbivores. Some ferns (such as bracken) also have nectaries on their fronds. The secretory tissue of a nectary consists of the epidermis and several layers of parenchyma beneath it. The cells are small, with dense cytoplasm and a large nucleus and well-developed endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, sometimes developing into transfer cells, and vascular bundles usually lie near the secretory tissue. The raw material of nectar comes from phloem sap, so the ratio of phloem to xylem in the bundle is related to the composition of the nectar: when phloem is well developed the nectar has a high sugar content, and when xylem is well developed the nectar contains more water. The sugars in nectar are mainly sucrose, glucose and fructose, in proportions that vary with plant and pollinator, along with amino acids and other substances.

**Definition 5.4.3 (Hydathode)** A structure that discharges excess water from the plant body in liquid form is called a **hydathode**, and the phenomenon is called *guttation*. A hydathode consists of a water pore, an epithem and vascular bundle endings. The water pores are mostly at leaf tips or margins and are modified stomata whose guard cells have lost the ability to close. Beneath the pore is modified mesophyll tissue called the epithem, whose cells are loosely arranged and lack chloroplasts. The vascular bundle ends as a small vein containing only tracheids and no phloem (Figure 4B).

On warm, humid nights or early mornings, when the soil is well supplied with water and the air is humid so that transpiration is very weak, root pressure forces water from the xylem into the intercellular spaces of the epithem, from where it is discharged through the water pores and forms droplets on leaf tips and margins. Nasturtium, cabbage, tomato, strawberry and burnet (*Sanguisorba*), floating-leaved plants such as water chestnut (*Trapa*) and water lily, and many grasses show conspicuous guttation.

Salt glands: some plants growing on saline soils and seashores have salt glands on the leaf surface that excrete excess salt. A gland consists of two to many cells, and the secretory cells are rich in mitochondria because salt secretion requires energy. Tamarisk (*Tamarix*), sea lavender (*Limonium*), gray mangrove (*Avicennia marina*) and grasses such as cordgrass (*Spartina*) have salt glands. The leaf surfaces of saltbush (*Atriplex*) and relatives bear bladder-like salt hairs, in which salt accumulates in the swollen terminal cell and is shed when the cell bursts. The physiology of salt secretion belongs to plant physiology, and the adaptations of halophytes are treated in [7.12.32](/7.12.32).

### 5.4.2. Internal Secretory Structures

Secretory structures whose secretions are not discharged from the body are called internal secretory structures. They include secretory cells, secretory cavities and canals, and laticifers.

Secretory cells: secretory cells may be living or dead, but in all of them the lumen holds a particular secretion. They are generally parenchyma cells scattered singly among other cells and are often clearly larger than their neighbors, especially in length, so they are easily recognized. Such cells, which differ conspicuously in form and contents from the surrounding cells, are collectively called **idioblasts**. According to the secretion, they are divided into:

| Secretory cell | Secretion | Representative families (APG IV treatment in parentheses) |
|---|---|---|
| Oil cell | Essential oil | Lauraceae, Magnoliaceae, Calycanthaceae, Piperaceae, Araceae (*Acorus*, placed in Acoraceae in APG IV) |
| Mucilage cell | Mucilage (polysaccharide) | Cactaceae, Malvaceae (including the former Tiliaceae) |
| Crystal cell | Calcium oxalate crystals ([7.12.3](/7.12.3)) | Moraceae, Amaryllidaceae, Commelinaceae |
| Tannin cell | Tannin | Vitaceae, Crassulaceae, Fabaceae, Rosaceae |
| Myrosin cell | Myrosinase (stored separately from glucosinolates; when the cell is damaged the two meet and form pungent, toxic isothiocyanates) | Brassicaceae, Capparaceae (split into Capparaceae and Cleomaceae in APG IV, both in Brassicales) |

The walls of oil cells often have a suberin layer that keeps the essential oil from diffusing into neighboring cells.

Secretory cavities and canals: these are cavities or ducts that store secretions in the plant body and are essentially a kind of intercellular space. By mode of formation there are three kinds (the three modes of intercellular-space formation in [7.12.3](/7.12.3)):

- Lysigenous: formed by the breakdown of some cells. For example, the translucent yellow dots ("oil glands") commonly seen in the leaves and fruit peel of citrus first form aromatic oil in some cells; later these cells rupture and release their contents into the lysigenous cavity, around which partly damaged cells can be seen (Figure 4C).
- Schizogenous: formed when the middle lamella between secretory cells dissolves and the cells separate. The resin canals in the xylem and leaves of conifers, the lacquer canals in the phloem of the lacquer tree (*Toxicodendron vernicifluum*) and the oil tubes (vittae) in the fruits of Apiaceae are all schizogenous. Intact secretory cells (epithelial cells) line the canal, and the resin or lacquer is discharged by these cells into the duct, where it accumulates (Figure 4D). After wounding, conifer wood can also form additional traumatic resin canals.
- Schizolysigenous: formed by a combination of the two, as in the secretory canals in the leaves and stems of *Mangifera*.

**Definition 5.4.4 (Laticifer)** A tube-like cell or cell series that secretes and stores latex is called a **laticifer**. Its wall is a primary wall and is not lignified. At maturity it is mostly multinucleate, with no clear boundary between vacuole and cytoplasm, and the protoplast surrounds the latex.

There are two types of laticifers (Figure 4E):

- Non-articulated laticifers: formed by a single cell that keeps elongating and branching as the plant grows, reaching several meters or more in length; each is a huge coenocyte. The laticifers of Apocynaceae (including the former Asclepiadaceae), Moraceae and *Euphorbia* are of this type.
- Articulated laticifers: formed by many tube-like cells that join one another during development, after which the connecting walls dissolve; they often interconnect into a network. The laticifers of Asteraceae (the ligulate-flowered group, such as lettuce and dandelion), Papaveraceae, Caricaceae, Musaceae, Convolvulaceae, and the rubber tree (*Hevea brasiliensis*) of the Euphorbiaceae are of this type. Chinese textbooks record that the primary phloem of the rubber tree has non-articulated laticifers and the secondary phloem has articulated ones. The former are destroyed very early as the stem develops, whereas the latter persist for a long time, and the rubber harvested in tapping is secreted by the articulated laticifers of the secondary phloem.

The composition of latex is extremely complex, and it often contains sugars, proteins, fats, tannins, alkaloids, salts, resins and rubber. The composition and color of latex differ among plants. The latex of opium poppy contains large amounts of alkaloids (morphine, codeine), that of *Lactuca* (Asteraceae) often contains bitter sesquiterpene lactones, and papaya latex contains the enzyme papain. The latex of many families and genera contains rubber, which is cis-1,4-polyisoprene (a polyterpene) suspended in small particles in the latex. Species rich in rubber are sources of natural rubber, most famously the rubber tree, and also the Indian rubber fig (*Ficus elastica*), Russian dandelion (*Taraxacum kok-saghyz*) and guayule (*Parthenium argentatum*). Gutta-percha from hardy rubber tree (*Eucommia ulmoides*) is trans-1,4-polyisoprene, hard rather than elastic, the same as gutta-percha proper. Latex flows out and coagulates when the plant is wounded, sealing the wound, and its toxins and sticky substances deter feeding insects.

## 5.5. Tissue Systems

Every plant organ is built of certain kinds of tissue, and the kinds and arrangement of tissues differ among organs of different function. But the plant is an organic whole, and the organs are not only functionally connected but must also be continuous and unified in internal structure. To stress this view, botany uses the concept of the tissue system (Sachs, 1875).

**Definition 5.5.1 (Tissue System)** A unit formed, in a whole plant or an organ, by one tissue or several tissues in structure and function is called a **tissue system**. The tissues of vascular plants can be grouped into three tissue systems: the **dermal tissue system** (dermal system), the **vascular tissue system** (vascular system) and the **ground tissue system** (ground system).

| Tissue system | Tissues included | Origin | Distribution and role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dermal system | Epidermis (primary), periderm (secondary) | Protoderm; cork cambium | Covers the surface of every organ, forming a continuous protective layer around the whole plant |
| Vascular system | Xylem, phloem (primary and secondary) | Procambium; vascular cambium | Runs continuously through the whole plant, connecting regions of growth and development with regions of synthesis and storage of organic nutrients |
| Ground system | Various parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma | Ground meristem | The basic substance of all parts of the plant, filling the space between dermal and vascular systems |

The structure of the whole plant is thus a vascular system embedded in a ground system and covered by a dermal system. Apart from the epidermis or periderm always covering the outermost layer, the structural differences among organs consist chiefly in the different relative distribution of vascular and ground tissue. In the root the vascular tissue is concentrated in the center, forming a solid stele. In the stem the vascular bundles are arranged in a ring or scattered, and the ground tissue is differentiated into cortex and pith. In the leaf the vascular bundles form the veins, distributed as a network in the ground tissue of the mesophyll. Secretory structures are scattered through all three systems. The division into these three tissue systems unifies all the tissues of the previous chapter and this one, and is also the framework for describing organ structure in the chapters on root, stem and leaf.

## Summary

Items:

- Xylem (Definition 5.1.1) = tracheids, vessel elements, wood fibers and xylem parenchyma. Tracheids (Definition 5.1.2) conduct water through pits and also support. Vessel elements, vessels and perforation plates (Definition 5.1.3); vessel elements complete differentiation through programmed cell death and hydrolysis of the end wall.
- Five thickening patterns: annular and helical (protoxylem, extensible) then scalariform, reticulate and pitted (metaxylem and secondary xylem). Perforation plates: simple, scalariform, ephedroid, reticulate.
- Tyloses: parenchyma cells grow through pits into the vessel lumen and plug the vessel, preventing the spread of pathogens and air.
- Vessels arose independently (convergently) in angiosperms, gnetophytes, *Selaginella*, *Equisetum* and a few ferns; *Amborella*, Winteraceae and Trochodendraceae lack vessels. Bailey trends: shorter, wider, end walls from oblique to transverse, scalariform to simple perforation plates, lateral pitting from scalariform to alternate. Flow $\propto r^4$, Eq. (5.1); wide vessels are efficient but prone to embolism.
- Phloem (Definition 5.2.1) = sieve-tube elements (or sieve cells), companion cells (or albuminous cells), phloem parenchyma and phloem fibers. Sieve-tube elements, sieve tubes and sieve plates (Definition 5.2.2); callose (Definition 5.2.3) belongs to sieve tubes, not to vessels; P-protein plugs pores; sieve tubes usually function for one growing season only, up to a century in palms.
- The companion cell (Definition 5.2.4) shares its origin with the sieve-tube element (one unequal longitudinal division); the sieve cell (Definition 5.2.5) has only sieve areas and is accompanied by albuminous cells (Strasburger cells) of different origin.
- Vascular bundle (Definition 5.3.1): closed and open; collateral, bicollateral, amphicribral, amphivasal, radial; vascular tissue, vascular bundle and stele distinguished.
- Secretory structures (Definition 5.4.1): external (glandular epidermis, glandular hairs, nectaries (Definition 5.4.2), hydathodes (Definition 5.4.3), salt glands) and internal (secretory cells, secretory cavities and canals, laticifers (Definition 5.4.4)).
- Tissue systems (Definition 5.5.1): dermal, vascular and ground systems; the vascular system is embedded in the ground system and covered by the dermal system.

## Questions

1. From the structure and composition of the conducting tissues, explain why angiosperms are said to be better adapted to terrestrial life than gymnosperms. Does this conclusion have exceptions or limits?

   **Answer** The xylem of angiosperms consists mainly of vessels: vessel elements have perforated end walls, are joined end to end into long tubes and have large diameters, and by Eq. (5.1) the flow is proportional to the fourth power of the radius, so water conduction is far more efficient than in the tracheids of gymnosperms. Wood fibers specialize in support, dividing the labor of conduction and support. The phloem consists mainly of sieve tubes: the pores of the sieve plates are large, and they are closely coupled with companion cells of common origin, so transport is also more efficient than in gymnosperm sieve cells, which have only sieve areas. This lets angiosperms support large leaf areas and rapid growth. The limit is that wide vessels are prone to embolism in freezing and drought, whereas gymnosperm tracheids and bordered pits with a torus confine embolisms to single cells and conduct water more safely, so conifers still dominate cold, dry, high-latitude and high-altitude regions. Nor are vessels unique to angiosperms (they arose independently in gnetophytes, *Selaginella*, *Equisetum* and others), and some angiosperms such as *Amborella* have no vessels.

2. In a longitudinal section of a pumpkin stem, a vascular bundle contains one kind of vessel whose wall has a ring-shaped thickening at regular intervals, another with a spiral thickening, and another whose wall is thickened throughout with only many small holes left. What are they, and what regularity is there in their position within the bundle? Why?

   **Answer** They are annular, helical and pitted vessels, respectively. The pumpkin stem has bicollateral bundles, with the primary xylem between the two layers of phloem; the protoxylem lies on the inner side (toward the pith; primary xylem of the stem is endarch) and the metaxylem on the outer side. Annular and helical vessels are the earliest-differentiating protoxylem, lying on the inner side; scalariform, reticulate and pitted vessels come later and lie outside, with pitted vessels closest to the fascicular cambium. The reason is that protoxylem matures while the stem is still elongating, and the unlignified primary wall between the rings or spirals of the secondary wall allows it to be stretched with the organ. Metaxylem differentiates after elongation stops, and its thickenings join into a net or a continuous sheet, strong and efficient in conducting water but unable to elongate further.

3. Compare the sieve-tube element–companion cell complex with the sieve cell–albuminous cell pair. Why can sieve-tube elements and companion cells be said to "live and die together"?

   **Answer** Similarities: in both, a living cell of the phloem that transports organic substances is paired with an adjacent auxiliary cell with dense cytoplasm; the conducting cells lack nuclei and depend on the auxiliary cell for protein and energy; numerous branched plasmodesmata connect the two; and when the conducting cell ceases to function the auxiliary cell dies with it. Differences: the sieve-tube element has sieve plates (with large pores) on its end walls and contains P-protein, and sieve-tube elements are joined end to end as sieve tubes; the sieve cell has only sieve areas (with small pores), no P-protein, and individual cells overlap at their tips. The companion cell and sieve-tube element arise from the same mother cell by an unequal longitudinal division, whereas the albuminous cell develops from a phloem parenchyma or ray cell and generally does not share its origin with the sieve cell. "Live and die together" means that the companion cell and sieve-tube element come from one mother cell, that the sieve-tube element loses its nucleus and ribosomes at maturity and depends entirely on the companion cell for survival, and that the companion cell dies when the sieve-tube element ceases to function.

4. By which secretory structures are the oil dots on citrus peel, the resin of pine, the latex of the rubber tree and the lacquer of the lacquer tree produced? How do their modes of formation differ?

   **Answer** The oil dots of citrus are lysigenous secretory cavities: some cells accumulate aromatic oil and then break down, releasing the oil into the cavity, which is surrounded by partly broken-down cells. Pine resin is produced by schizogenous resin canals: the middle lamellae between secretory cells dissolve, the cells separate to form the duct, and a ring of intact epithelial cells secretes resin into it. Lacquer of the lacquer tree also comes from schizogenous lacquer canals in the phloem. The latex of the rubber tree is produced by articulated laticifers in the secondary phloem: many tube-like cells are joined end to end and the connecting walls dissolve, forming a network of ducts. The latex contains suspended particles of cis-polyisoprene and flows out when the bark is cut. In the first three the secretion is stored in intercellular spaces (cavities or canals), whereas latex is stored within the laticifer cells themselves.

5. Are the water drops at a leaf tip necessarily dew? How can guttation be distinguished from dew by morphology? What conditions does guttation require?

   **Answer** Not necessarily. Guttation is water from inside the plant discharged through the water pores of hydathodes. The drops appear only at fixed positions where the pores are located, such as leaf tips and the tips of marginal teeth, often one drop per tooth, and they contain small amounts of mineral salts and organic substances, so a white salt residue may remain after drying. Dew is water vapor from the air condensing over the whole cool leaf surface, evenly distributed and not restricted to the margin. Guttation requires vigorous water uptake by the roots producing high root pressure together with very weak transpiration, and typically occurs on warm nights and early mornings when the soil is moist and the air humid. The water pores of a hydathode cannot close and the vascular bundle ends with only tracheids, so water in the xylem is driven by root pressure directly out through the epithem.
