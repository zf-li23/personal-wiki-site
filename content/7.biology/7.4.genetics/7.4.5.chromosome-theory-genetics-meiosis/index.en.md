Mendel's laws ([7.4.2](/7.4.2)) were rules about invisible "factors"; this chapter shows where the factors are. Between 1902 and 1916 cytology and genetics converged on one statement: genes are parts of chromosomes, and the regularities of transmission are the regularities of chromosome behavior in **mitosis** and **meiosis**. We take the cell mechanics of these divisions from [7.3.21](/7.3.21) (spindle, kinetochores, cohesin, synaptonemal complex) and the structure of chromosomes and the karyotype from [7.3.17](/7.3.17), and ask only what follows genetically: which **alleles** end up together in which cells, and with what probabilities. The answers are the bridge from cell biology to genetics. Segregation and independent assortment follow from the pairing and orientation of homologues; crossing over, the third consequence of meiosis, is the subject of linkage and mapping in [7.4.8](/7.4.8) and [7.4.9](/7.4.9); and the failures of segregation (nondisjunction) are both the decisive proof of the theory ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)) and the origin of aneuploidy ([7.4.16](/7.4.16)). The chapter ends with gametogenesis, which links meiosis to the maternal and paternal age effects of later chapters.

## 5.1. Chromosomes Carry the Genes

### 5.1.1. Homologues, Sister Chromatids and the Karyotype

By the 1880s microscopists had seen that the nuclei of egg and sperm fuse at fertilization and contribute equally to the zygote, and that basic dyes stain threadlike bodies in the dividing nucleus, the chromosomes (from Greek *chroma*, color, and *soma*, body). Their number is constant in the cells of a species (46 in humans, 8 in *Drosophila melanogaster*), halved in its gametes and restored at fertilization. Cells with one set of chromosomes are **haploid**, those with two sets **diploid**; the number of chromosomes in one set is written $n$, so a human somatic cell has $2n = 46$.

**Definition 5.1.1 (Homologous Chromosomes and Sister Chromatids)** **Homologous chromosomes** (homologues, homologs) are the two members of a chromosome pair in a diploid cell, one inherited from each parent, which match in size, centromere position and banding pattern and carry the same genes in the same order, though possibly different alleles of them. **Sister chromatids** are the two identical copies of one chromosome produced by its replication in S phase, held together by cohesin and joined at the **centromere** until they separate in anaphase of mitosis or meiosis II.

The distinction is the key to everything that follows: sister chromatids carry the same alleles (apart from new mutations and crossing over), homologues may carry different ones. Arranging the metaphase chromosomes of a cell in homologous pairs of decreasing size gives the **karyotype** ([7.3.17](/7.3.17#sec-17.4.1)). In a human male karyotype 22 pairs match and one does not.

**Definition 5.1.2 (Autosome)** An **autosome** is any chromosome that is not a sex chromosome: autosomes are present in two copies in both sexes of a diploid species and do not differ between them. Humans have 22 pairs of autosomes and one pair of **sex chromosomes** (XX in females, XY in males; [7.4.6](/7.4.6)).

Chromosome numbers vary without any relation to complexity; the haploid numbers of the main genetic model organisms are worth knowing, because $n$ fixes the number of independently assorting units in a cross (Section 5.4):

### 5.1.2. The Chromosome Theory of Inheritance

Walter Sutton (1902–1903, from grasshopper spermatocytes) and Theodor Boveri (1902–1904, from sea urchin embryos with abnormal chromosome sets) noticed that chromosomes behave exactly as Mendel's factors must. Sutton's argument was a list of parallels:

| Mendel's factors ([7.4.2](/7.4.2)) | Chromosomes in meiosis and fertilization |
|---|---|
| two copies of each factor in a plant, one from each parent | two homologues of each chromosome, one from each parent |
| the two copies separate in gamete formation (segregation) | homologues go to different cells in meiosis I |
| each gamete receives one copy | each gamete receives one homologue of each pair |
| factors for different traits assort independently | different pairs of homologues orient independently on the meiosis I spindle |
| the zygote restores two copies | fertilization restores the pairs |

Boveri added that a sea urchin embryo develops normally only if every cell receives a complete set of chromosomes, so that chromosomes are individually different and individually necessary.

**Definition 5.1.3 (Chromosome Theory of Inheritance)** The **chromosome theory of inheritance** is the theory that genes are located on chromosomes, at fixed positions (loci) in a fixed linear order, and that the transmission of genes from parents to offspring follows from the behavior of chromosomes in meiosis and fertilization.

The parallels were circumstantial. The theory became a demonstrated fact when particular genes were shown to travel with particular, cytologically recognizable chromosomes: Morgan's X-linked *white* gene (1910) and, decisively, Bridges' demonstration (1916) that exceptional progeny arising from **nondisjunction** of the X chromosomes have exactly the abnormal chromosome sets that their phenotypes predict ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)). The theory also predicted that genes on the same chromosome should not assort independently, which Morgan's group confirmed and turned into the genetic map ([7.4.8](/7.4.8)). The main steps:

| Year | Step | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| 1902–1903 | Sutton and Boveri: chromosomes behave as Mendel's factors | 5.1.2 |
| 1905 | Bateson, Saunders and Punnett: sweet-pea factors that do not assort independently ("coupling") | [7.4.8](/7.4.8) |
| 1909 | Johannsen introduces the words gene, genotype and phenotype | [7.4.1](/7.4.1) |
| 1910 | Morgan: *white* follows the X chromosome in *Drosophila* | [7.4.6](/7.4.6) |
| 1911 | Morgan: linkage and crossing over explain the excess of parental types | [7.4.8](/7.4.8) |
| 1913 | Sturtevant: the first genetic map, five genes on the X | [7.4.8](/7.4.8) |
| 1916 | Bridges: nondisjunction of the X proves the theory | [7.4.6](/7.4.6) |
| 1931 | Creighton and McClintock (maize), Stern (*Drosophila*): recombination is accompanied by physical exchange | [7.4.8](/7.4.8) |
| 1933 | Nobel Prize to Morgan for the role of chromosomes in heredity | |

## 5.2. Mitosis Preserves the Genotype

**Mitosis** distributes one copy of each replicated chromosome to each daughter nucleus ([7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.1)); because sister chromatids are copies of one DNA molecule, the two daughter cells receive the same chromosomes and the same alleles as the mother cell. Genetically, mitosis is a copying operation: a zygote of **genotype** $Aa\,Bb$ gives rise to a body whose cells are all $Aa\,Bb$. This constancy is why a genotype can be assigned to an organism at all, and why a blood sample reveals the genotype of the germline.

The exceptions are informative rather than embarrassing, and each has its own chapter. A new **mutation** in a dividing cell is passed to its clone of descendants and makes the organism a somatic mosaic ([7.4.12](/7.4.12)); crossing over between homologues in a mitotic cell can make a heterozygous cell give rise to two homozygous daughter clones, the twin spots of mitotic recombination ([7.4.9](/7.4.9)); mitotic nondisjunction produces aneuploid clones ([7.4.16](/7.4.16)); and loss of heterozygosity by these routes is a central step in cancer ([7.4.21](/7.4.21)).

## 5.3. Meiosis and Mendel's Laws

**Meiosis** ([7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.7)) consists of one round of DNA replication followed by two divisions. In prophase I the homologues pair and form a **bivalent** of four chromatids held by a **synaptonemal complex**; at least one crossover per bivalent leaves a **chiasma**, which together with arm cohesion holds the homologues together until anaphase I. At metaphase I the two homologues of each bivalent attach to opposite poles; at anaphase I they separate. Meiosis II separates the sister chromatids. Three genetic consequences follow, one for each law of transmission.

### 5.3.1. Segregation

Consider a heterozygote $Aa$. Its two alleles lie on the two homologues of one pair, so after replication the bivalent carries $A$ on both chromatids of one homologue and $a$ on both chromatids of the other. Meiosis I sends the homologues to different cells, and meiosis II sends one chromatid of each to each gamete. Every gamete therefore receives exactly one allele, and of the four products of a meiosis two carry $A$ and two carry $a$. This is the **law of segregation** in cellular form; it is stronger than Mendel's statement, because it holds not only on average over many gametes but meiosis by meiosis (tetrad analysis in fungi, where the four products of one meiosis are recovered together, shows the $2{:}2$ ratio in every tetrad; [7.4.9](/7.4.9)).

**Definition 5.3.1 (Reductional and Equational Division)** A **reductional division** is a nuclear division that separates homologous centromeres (and the chromosome segments attached to them), so that each daughter nucleus receives one member of each homologous pair and the chromosome number is halved; meiosis I is the reductional division of meiosis. An **equational division** separates sister centromeres, so that each daughter receives one chromatid of every chromosome present and the chromosome number is unchanged; mitosis and meiosis II are equational divisions.

For the centromeres the assignment is fixed: they segregate reductionally in meiosis I. For a gene it depends on crossing over. If no crossover occurs between a locus and its centromere, the alleles $A$ and $a$ stay with their centromeres and are separated at meiosis I; if a crossover occurs in that interval, each homologue leaving meiosis I carries one $A$ chromatid and one $a$ chromatid, and the alleles are separated only at meiosis II.

![When are two alleles separated? A: without a crossover between the centromere and the locus, the homologue with A (blue, paternal) and the homologue with a (red, maternal) go to different cells in meiosis I, so each product of meiosis I is AA or aa and the alleles are separated at the first division. B: one crossover between the centromere and the locus exchanges the distal segments of two non-sister chromatids; each product of meiosis I now carries A on one chromatid and a on the other, and the alleles are separated only in meiosis II. In both cases the four gametes are two A and two a](./plots/mi-mii-segregation.py)

The frequency of second-division segregation therefore measures the distance between a gene and its centromere; in fungi with ordered tetrads it can be read directly from the order of spores in the ascus ([7.4.9](/7.4.9)). The same fact matters in human genetics: the genotype of the first polar body depends on whether a crossover occurred between the locus and the centromere (Example 5.6.1).

### 5.3.2. Independent Assortment

Now consider a dihybrid $Aa\,Bb$ whose two loci lie on different (nonhomologous) chromosomes, with $A$ and $B$ inherited from the father (on the blue homologues) and $a$ and $b$ from the mother. At metaphase I the bivalent of the first pair can place either homologue toward a given pole, and so can the bivalent of the second pair, independently. The two orientations of the second bivalent relative to the first are equally likely.

**Proposition 5.3.1 (Independent Assortment of Nonhomologous Chromosomes)** If loci $A$ and $B$ lie on different pairs of homologues, and the orientations of different bivalents at metaphase I are independent and each homologue faces a given pole with probability $1/2$, then a dihybrid $Aa\,Bb$ produces the four gametes $AB$, $Ab$, $aB$, $ab$ each with probability $1/4$, whatever the phase in which it received the alleles from its parents.

**Proof** Fix the pole that will receive the homologue carrying $A$. The homologue carrying $B$ goes to the same pole with probability $1/2$ (orientation 1) and to the other pole with probability $1/2$ (orientation 2). Orientation 1 produces two $AB$ and two $ab$ products, orientation 2 two $Ab$ and two $aB$. Averaging over orientations, each of the four types has probability $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14$. Crossing over does not change this: a crossover between $A$ and its centromere only moves the separation of $A$ from $a$ to meiosis II, and the chromatid that a gamete receives from each pair is still a random one of the four, independently between pairs. $\square$

![Independent orientation of bivalents. A cell heterozygous for A/a on one pair of chromosomes and B/b on another. A: in orientation 1 the two paternal homologues (blue, A and B) face the same pole, and the meiosis produces two AB and two ab gametes. B: in orientation 2 the paternal homologue of the second pair faces the other pole, giving two Ab and two aB gametes. The two orientations are equally likely, so over many meioses the four gamete types are equally frequent: the law of independent assortment](./plots/independent-orientation.py)

This is the **law of independent assortment** derived from the cell. It also says when the law fails: loci on the same chromosome travel together unless separated by crossing over, which is **linkage** ([7.4.8](/7.4.8)). Mendel's seven genes lie on only four of the seven pea chromosomes; the pairs that share a chromosome are either so far apart that they recombine freely (seed color and flower color on chromosome 1) or were not reported together in a dihybrid cross (pod shape and stem length on chromosome 4), so he saw no linkage.

### 5.3.3. Crossing Over and Recombinant Gametes

**Definition 5.3.2 (Crossing Over)** **Crossing over** is the reciprocal exchange of corresponding segments between two non-sister chromatids of a pair of homologous chromosomes, occurring in prophase I of meiosis (and rarely in mitotic cells). Each crossover involves two of the four chromatids of a bivalent and produces two recombinant chromatids, each made of a segment of one homologue joined to the adjacent segment of the other.

Cytologically a crossover is seen as a chiasma ([7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.7.2)); molecularly it is the crossover resolution of a double Holliday junction in **homologous recombination** ([7.2.9](/7.2.9#sec-9.5.2)). Genetically it is defined by its products.

Prophase I is divided by the appearance of the chromosomes into leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, diplotene and diakinesis (table in [7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.7.2)), and the genetic events can be placed on this timeline. Recombination begins in leptotene with programmed double-strand breaks; homologues synapse in zygotene, forming bivalents; crossovers are completed in pachytene; when the synaptonemal complex disassembles in diplotene, the homologues remain joined only at the chiasmata, which are the first cytological sign of the crossovers; and in diakinesis the bivalents reach maximal condensation. Older accounts describe chiasmata as sliding toward the chromosome ends during diakinesis (terminalization). In the organisms examined with modern markers, chiasmata mostly stay at the sites of the crossovers, and apparent terminalization reflects condensation and the progressive release of arm cohesion; chiasma positions can therefore be used as crossover positions, which is the basis of cytological crossover counts ([7.4.8](/7.4.8#sec-8.8.1)). Before prophase, premeiotic S phase is typically longer than a mitotic S phase, and it is then that the meiotic cohesin that will later hold chiasmata and sister centromeres together is loaded ([7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.7)).

**Definition 5.3.3 (Recombinant Gamete)** For an individual that inherited the alleles of two or more loci in particular combinations from its two parents (the parental combinations, for example $AB$ from the father and $ab$ from the mother), a **recombinant gamete** is a gamete that carries a combination of alleles not present in either parental chromosome set ($Ab$ or $aB$); gametes carrying a parental combination are parental gametes.

Recombinant gametes arise in two ways. For loci on different chromosomes, independent assortment makes half the gametes recombinant (Proposition 5.3.1). For loci on the same chromosome, only crossing over in the interval between them does so, and because a single crossover involves two of the four chromatids, at most half of the products of a meiosis with one crossover in the interval are recombinant. The proportion of recombinant gametes, the **recombination frequency**, therefore never exceeds $1/2$, and it measures the distance between linked loci ([7.4.8](/7.4.8)).

The three consequences of meiosis can be summarized as three sources of genetic variation among gametes:

| Event | Stage | Genetic consequence | Studied in |
|---|---|---|---|
| separation of homologues | anaphase I | segregation of alleles ($1{:}1$) | [7.4.2](/7.4.2) |
| independent orientation of bivalents | metaphase I | independent assortment of unlinked loci | [7.4.2](/7.4.2), Section 5.4 |
| crossing over | prophase I | recombination of linked loci | [7.4.8](/7.4.8), [7.4.9](/7.4.9) |

## 5.4. Counting Gametes

The arithmetic of meiosis is the arithmetic of independent coin tosses, one per chromosome pair, plus the crossovers within each pair. Two levels must be distinguished: which parental homologue (paternal or maternal centromere) a gamete receives from each pair, and which alleles it receives at loci along each chromosome.

**Formula 5.4.1 (Chromosome Combinations)** Ignoring crossing over, a diploid with $n$ pairs of chromosomes can produce $2^n$ different gametes, according to which homologue of each pair they receive, each with probability $2^{-n}$. The number of gametes that receive exactly $k$ paternal and $n - k$ maternal chromosomes is $\binom{n}{k}$, so the number $K$ of paternal chromosomes in a random gamete has the **binomial distribution** $K \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, 1/2)$. Two parents can produce $2^n \cdot 2^n = 4^n$ chromosome combinations in their zygotes.

**Example 5.4.1** For humans, $n = 23$. A person can produce $2^{23} = 8\,388\,608 \approx 8.4\times10^6$ gametes differing in their chromosome combinations, and a couple $2^{46} \approx 7.0\times10^{13}$ zygotes; this already exceeds the number of humans who have ever lived (about $10^{11}$) by a factor of several hundred. The probability that a gamete carries only the chromosomes the person received from one specified parent is $2^{-23} \approx 1.2\times10^{-7}$; the probability that it carries exactly 12 paternal and 11 maternal chromosomes, the most likely split, is $\binom{23}{12}2^{-23} = 1\,352\,078/8\,388\,608 \approx 0.161$.

The same reasoning applies at the level of genes. A genotype heterozygous at $m$ unlinked loci produces $2^m$ kinds of gametes in equal proportions; a cross between two such heterozygotes produces $3^m$ genotypes and, with complete dominance, $2^m$ phenotypes. These counts are the core of the probability toolbox of [7.4.2](/7.4.2), and Section 5.3.2 is their justification.

Crossing over multiplies the variety: every bivalent has at least one crossover, so the chromosome a gamete receives from a pair is in general a mosaic of its two homologues, and the number of distinguishable gametes is practically unlimited. A quantitative consequence concerns ancestry. Let $G$ be the fraction of a gamete's autosomal DNA that comes from the person's father (the child's grandfather).

**Formula 5.4.2 (Grandparental Share of a Gamete)** If autosome $i$ has physical length $L_i$ and receives from the grandfather a fraction $g_i$ of its length, then $G = \sum_i L_i g_i / \sum_i L_i$ and $\mathbb{E}[G] = 1/2$. Without crossing over $g_i \in \{0, 1\}$ with probability $1/2$ each, independently, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}(G) = \frac{1}{4}\,\frac{\sum_i L_i^2}{\left(\sum_i L_i\right)^2},
\tag{5.1}
$$
which for $n$ chromosomes of equal length is $1/(4n)$. Crossovers make each $g_i$ a fraction strictly between 0 and 1 and reduce the variance further.

**Example 5.4.2** For the 22 human autosomes with their actual lengths, Eq. (5.1) gives a standard deviation of $G$ of $0.116$ (for equal lengths it would be $1/(2\sqrt{22}) = 0.107$). Simulating crossovers as a Poisson process along each chromosome with the human map lengths (about 27 crossovers per male and about 42–44 per female meiosis) reduces it to about $0.078$ in sperm and $0.067$ in eggs (figure below). A child receives half of its genome from each parent, so its share from one grandparent is $G/2$: on average $1/4$, with a standard deviation of about $0.035$–$0.04$. Two children of the same parents differ correspondingly in how much of each grandparent they carry, which is why genetic relatedness between relatives of the same nominal degree varies measurably (the variance in identity by descent between siblings, used in [7.4.24](/7.4.24)).

![Simulated fraction of a human gamete's autosomal DNA that comes from the grandfather (20 000 gametes per curve). Without crossing over (gray) each of the 22 autosomes is inherited whole from one grandparent, and the share varies widely (standard deviation 0.116). Crossing over, modeled as Poisson crossovers with the approximate map length of each autosome, breaks chromosomes into grandparental segments and narrows the distribution: about 27 crossovers per male meiosis (blue) and about 44 per female meiosis (red) give standard deviations of 0.078 and 0.067. ## 5.5. DNA Content and Chromosome Number

Two quantities are often confused in problems: the number of chromosomes in a nucleus and the amount of DNA in it. The **C value** is the DNA content of one unreplicated haploid set (for humans about $3.1\,\mathrm{Gb}$, or about $3.3\,\mathrm{pg}$); chromosome number is counted in centromeres, so a replicated chromosome with two sister chromatids still counts as one chromosome.

**Formula 5.5.1 (Chromosomes, Chromatids and DNA Content)** For a diploid organism with $2n$ chromosomes:

| Stage | Chromosomes | Chromatids per chromosome | DNA molecules | DNA content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| G1 (and gamete precursor before S) | $2n$ | 1 | $2n$ | $2C$ |
| G2, mitotic prophase and metaphase, prophase I, metaphase I | $2n$ | 2 | $4n$ | $4C$ |
| each daughter of mitosis | $2n$ | 1 | $2n$ | $2C$ |
| each product of meiosis I (metaphase II) | $n$ | 2 | $2n$ | $2C$ |
| each product of meiosis II (gamete) | $n$ | 1 | $n$ | $1C$ |
| zygote after fertilization (before S) | $2n$ | 1 | $2n$ | $2C$ |

At metaphase I there are $n$ bivalents; the number of kinetochores is $4n$ in mitosis (two per chromosome) but behaves as $2n$ functional units in meiosis I, because sister kinetochores are co-oriented ([7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.7.3)).

**Example 5.5.1** A human primary spermatocyte at metaphase I contains 46 chromosomes, 92 chromatids and 92 DNA molecules (4C, about $13\,\mathrm{pg}$ of DNA), arranged as 23 bivalents. A secondary spermatocyte at metaphase II contains 23 chromosomes and 46 chromatids (2C); a sperm contains 23 chromosomes (1C). The DNA content of a secondary spermatocyte equals that of a somatic G1 cell, but its chromosome number is half: the two quantities are not proportional, which is the point of the table.

![DNA content (C, solid) and chromosome number (n, dashed) per nucleus. A: in the mitotic cycle replication doubles the DNA content during S phase without changing the chromosome number, and mitosis halves the DNA content again. B: in meiosis one replication is followed by two divisions: meiosis I halves both the chromosome number (2n to n) and the DNA content (4C to 2C), meiosis II halves only the DNA content (2C to 1C), and fertilization restores 2n and 2C in the zygote](./plots/dna-content.py)

## 5.6. The Genetics of Gametogenesis

**Definition 5.6.1 (Gametogenesis, Spermatogenesis and Oogenesis)** **Gametogenesis** is the formation of gametes from germ-line cells: mitotic proliferation of diploid precursors, meiosis, and the differentiation of its products. In animals **spermatogenesis** is the formation of sperm, in which each primary spermatocyte gives four functional spermatids, and **oogenesis** the formation of eggs, in which each primary oocyte gives one ovum and two or three polar bodies.

The cell biology of the two processes is compared in [7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.7.4) and their development is treated in [7.5](/7.5) and [7.14](/7.14). Genetically, three differences matter.

1. Which products survive. All four products of a male meiosis become sperm, so the segregation ratio among sperm is a direct sample of meiosis. Only one product of a female meiosis becomes the egg; the others are discarded as **polar bodies**. Normally the choice is random with respect to alleles, so Mendelian ratios hold in eggs too; when it is not, an allele that preferentially stays in the egg is transmitted to more than half of the offspring (female meiotic drive, [7.4.17](/7.4.17)).
2. Timing and arrest. Human oocytes enter prophase I in the fetal ovary; about 6–7 million oocytes are present at about 20 weeks of gestation, 1–2 million at birth and about 300 000–400 000 at puberty, and of these only about 400–500 are ovulated. Each remains arrested in diplotene with its bivalents held by chiasmata and cohesin for 12 to 50 years. The loss of cohesion with age underlies the sharp rise of nondisjunction with maternal age ([7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.7.5), [7.4.16](/7.4.16)): about $0.05\,\%$ of births to women in their mid-20s have trisomy 21, about $0.3\,\%$ at 35 and about $3\,\%$ at 45. Meiosis in males starts at puberty and continues throughout life, one round of spermatogenesis taking about 64–74 days.
3. Number of replications before meiosis. An oocyte is the product of about 22–24 mitotic divisions, all completed before birth. Spermatogonial stem cells divide throughout adult life: about 30 divisions precede puberty and about 23 more occur per year afterward, so a sperm of a 40-year-old man descends from roughly 600 mitoses (Crow's estimate). Because most new point mutations arise from replication errors, the number of new mutations transmitted by a father rises by about 1.5 per year of paternal age, against about 0.4 per year for mothers: the paternal age effect ([7.4.12](/7.4.12)).

**Example 5.6.1 (Inferring the Egg Genotype from Polar Bodies)** In preimplantation genetic testing ([7.4.14](/7.4.14)) the polar bodies can be biopsied and genotyped while the egg is left intact. Suppose a woman is heterozygous $Aa$ for a disease allele $a$. If the first polar body is $AA$ (both chromatids carry $A$), no crossover occurred between the locus and the centromere, the secondary oocyte is $aa$, and the egg will be $a$ whatever the second polar body; if the first polar body is $aa$, the egg will be $A$. If the first polar body is heterozygous ($Aa$, after a crossover in the centromere–locus interval), the secondary oocyte also carries one $A$ and one $a$ chromatid, and the egg carries the allele not found in the second polar body. The test thus depends on Section 5.3.1: the more distant the locus from its centromere, the more often the second polar body is needed.

The difference between the sexes extends to recombination: human oocytes have about 1.6 times as many crossovers as spermatocytes, and in some species one sex has no crossing over at all (male *Drosophila*, female silkworms), with consequences for mapping that are taken up in [7.4.8](/7.4.8). In plants and fungi meiosis produces spores rather than gametes, and the gametes arise later by mitosis in a haploid generation; the place of meiosis in the life cycles of model organisms (gametic, sporic and zygotic meiosis) is described in [7.4.1](/7.4.1).

## Summary

- Terms: Homologues carry the same loci, possibly different alleles; sister chromatids are replicated copies (Definition 5.1.1). Autosomes are the chromosomes other than the sex chromosomes (Definition 5.1.2). The chromosome theory of inheritance (Definition 5.1.3), argued by Sutton and Boveri from parallels and proven by Morgan and Bridges, places genes at loci on chromosomes.
- Mitosis copies, so all somatic cells share the zygote's genotype; exceptions (somatic mutation, mitotic recombination, mitotic nondisjunction) make mosaics.
- Meiosis explains Mendel:

  | Law | Cellular basis |
  |---|---|
  | segregation $1{:}1$ | homologues separate (reductional division, Definition 5.3.1); exactly two of four products carry each allele |
  | independent assortment | independent orientation of bivalents (Proposition 5.3.1) |
  | recombination of linked loci | crossing over between non-sister chromatids (Definitions 5.3.2, 5.3.3); recombination frequency $\le 1/2$ |

- First or second division: Alleles separate at meiosis I if there is no crossover between locus and centromere, at meiosis II if there is one (figure in Section 5.3.1; tetrads in [7.4.9](/7.4.9)).
- Counting (Formula 5.4.1): $2^n$ chromosome combinations per gamete, $4^n$ per zygote, paternal chromosomes in a gamete $\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n, 1/2)$; for humans $2^{23} \approx 8.4\times10^6$. The grandparental share of a gamete has mean $1/2$ and variance $\frac14\sum L_i^2/(\sum L_i)^2$ without crossovers (Eq. (5.1)), smaller with them (Example 5.4.2).
- DNA content and chromosome number (Formula 5.5.1): G2 and meiosis I $2n$, $4C$; after meiosis I $n$, $2C$; gametes $n$, $1C$.
- Prophase I: breaks in leptotene, synapsis in zygotene, crossovers in pachytene, chiasmata visible from diplotene (Section 5.3.3).
- Gametogenesis (Definition 5.6.1): four sperm per spermatocyte, one egg plus polar bodies per oocyte; decades-long arrest of oocytes (maternal age effect on nondisjunction) versus hundreds of spermatogonial divisions (paternal age effect on point mutations); polar bodies reveal the egg's genotype (Example 5.6.1).

## Questions

1. A diploid species has $2n = 12$. How many chromosomes, chromatids and DNA molecules, and how much DNA (in C), are there in (a) a cell in G2, (b) a cell at metaphase I, (c) a cell at metaphase II, (d) a gamete?

   **Answer** (a) 12 chromosomes, 24 chromatids, 24 DNA molecules, 4C. (b) The same: 12 chromosomes as 6 bivalents, 24 chromatids, 4C. (c) 6 chromosomes, 12 chromatids, 12 DNA molecules, 2C. (d) 6 chromosomes, 6 chromatids (unreplicated), 6 DNA molecules, 1C.
2. Why does Sutton's argument (Section 5.1.2) prove less than Bridges' experiment?

   **Answer** Sutton showed that chromosomes behave as Mendel's factors would, which makes the theory plausible but does not exclude other carriers with the same behavior. Bridges showed that a particular gene (*white*) followed a particular chromosome (the X) even when that chromosome behaved abnormally: flies with an unusual genotype at *white* had exactly the unusual X chromosome content predicted from their phenotype. A correlation in the exceptions is much harder to explain without the theory.
3. In a species with $n = 4$, how many chromosome combinations can a gamete have (ignoring crossing over)? What is the probability that a gamete receives all four of its chromosomes from the individual's mother? Exactly two from the mother?

   **Answer** $2^4 = 16$ combinations. All four maternal: $(1/2)^4 = 1/16$. Exactly two maternal: $\binom{4}{2}/16 = 6/16 = 3/8$.
4. Prove that at most half of the gametes from meioses with exactly one crossover between loci $A$ and $B$ are recombinant, and that exactly half are.

   **Answer** A crossover involves two of the four chromatids of the bivalent. The two chromatids not involved keep their parental combinations ($AB$ and $ab$); the two involved exchange the segments beyond the crossover and become $Ab$ and $aB$. Each meiosis therefore gives two recombinant and two parental products, and since a gamete receives a random one of the four (or, in males, all four become gametes), exactly half of the gametes from such meioses are recombinant. Meioses without a crossover in the interval contribute none, so overall the recombinant fraction is at most $1/2$ (multiple crossovers are considered in [7.4.8](/7.4.8)).
5. A heterozygote $Aa$ shows second-division segregation of $A/a$ in 30 % of its meioses. In what fraction of meioses was there a crossover (an odd number of crossovers) between the centromere and the locus, and what fraction of its first polar bodies (if it is a female mammal) are heterozygous?

   **Answer** Second-division segregation occurs exactly when an odd number of crossovers falls between centromere and locus (an even number restores the original arrangement of the alleles relative to the centromere), so in 30 % of meioses. In those meioses each product of meiosis I, including the first polar body, carries $A$ on one chromatid and $a$ on the other, so 30 % of first polar bodies are heterozygous and 70 % are homozygous ($AA$ or $aa$, 35 % each).
6. An individual is $Aa\,Bb\,Cc$, with the three loci on three different chromosomes, having received $ABC$ from its father and $abc$ from its mother. What fraction of its gametes is $AbC$? What fraction is recombinant (non-parental for at least one pair of loci)?

   **Answer** By Proposition 5.3.1 applied to each pair, all $2^3 = 8$ gametes are equally frequent: $AbC$ has probability $1/8$. Only $ABC$ and $abc$ are parental, so $1 - 2/8 = 3/4$ are recombinant.
7. Use Eq. (5.1) to compute the standard deviation of the grandparental share $G$ for an organism with $n = 4$ autosomes of relative lengths $4{:}3{:}2{:}1$ and no crossing over. What is the probability that a gamete carries no DNA at all from the grandfather?

   **Answer** $\sum L_i = 10$, $\sum L_i^2 = 16 + 9 + 4 + 1 = 30$, so $\operatorname{Var}(G) = \frac14 \cdot 30/100 = 0.075$ and $\mathrm{SD} = 0.274$. No grandpaternal DNA requires all four maternal chromosomes to come from the grandmother: $(1/2)^4 = 1/16 \approx 0.063$. (With an obligatory crossover on every chromosome this probability is zero.)
8. Explain why the number of crossovers per meiosis cannot be less than the haploid number $n$ in a species like humans, and estimate the minimum genetic map length of the human autosomes in centimorgans.

   **Answer** Every bivalent needs at least one chiasma to be held together and to bi-orient at metaphase I (the obligatory crossover; [7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.7.2)); achiasmate bivalents missegregate. So there are at least 22 autosomal crossovers per meiosis. A crossover involves 2 of 4 chromatids, so one crossover per bivalent corresponds to an average of 0.5 crossovers per chromatid, i.e. a map length of 50 cM per chromosome: at least $22 \times 50 = 1100\,\mathrm{cM}$. The real lengths (about $2700\,\mathrm{cM}$ in males and about $4400\,\mathrm{cM}$ in females, or 27 and 44 crossovers per meiosis) are larger.
9. A woman heterozygous $Aa$ undergoes polar body biopsy. The first polar body is heterozygous and the second polar body carries $A$. What is the egg's allele? What would you conclude if the first polar body were $AA$?

   **Answer** A heterozygous first polar body means the secondary oocyte carries one $A$ and one $a$ chromatid; the second polar body takes one of them ($A$), so the egg is $a$. If the first polar body were $AA$, the secondary oocyte would be $aa$ and the egg $a$, with no need to examine the second polar body.
10. In a man of 20, about $30 + 23\times 5 = 145$ germ-line divisions precede a sperm; at 45, about $30 + 23\times 30 = 720$. If each division adds on average $0.1$ new point mutations to the haploid genome transmitted, how many paternally derived new mutations does a child of a 20-year-old and of a 45-year-old father carry? Compare with the observed slope.

    **Answer** About $14.5$ and $72$ respectively, a slope of $(72 - 14.5)/25 \approx 2.3$ per year. The observed slope is about 1.5 per year of paternal age (Jónsson et al. 2017), so the per-division rate implied by this crude model is about $0.065$; the model captures why the slope is linear and why it is much steeper for fathers than for mothers, whose oocytes undergo a fixed number of divisions.
11. (Comprehensive) An individual of a species with $n = 3$ has genotype $Aa\,Bb\,Dd$, where $A$ and $B$ are on the same chromosome and $D$ on another. In what fraction of its meioses must a crossover occur between $A$ and $B$ for the gamete $Ab\,D$ to have frequency $1/16$? (Assume the individual received $AB\,D$ from its father and $ab\,d$ from its mother.)

    **Answer** $D$ assorts independently of the $A$–$B$ chromosome, so $\mathbb{P}(Ab\,D) = \mathbb{P}(Ab)\cdot\frac12$, and we need $\mathbb{P}(Ab) = 1/8$. The recombinant classes $Ab$ and $aB$ are equally frequent, so the recombination frequency is $r = 1/4$. If crossovers in the interval are single (at most one per meiosis), half of the products of a meiosis with a crossover are recombinant, so a crossover must occur in $2r = 1/2$ of meioses.
12. Explain, using Formula 5.5.1, why a cell with the DNA content $2C$ can be either diploid or haploid, and how one could tell them apart in a flow-cytometry experiment that measures only DNA content.

    **Answer** A diploid G1 cell ($2n$, one chromatid each) and a haploid cell at metaphase II or a secondary spermatocyte ($n$, two chromatids each) both contain $2C$. DNA content alone cannot distinguish them. One must add another measurement: chromosome counting, centromere or kinetochore staining (number of centromeres $2n$ versus $n$), or a marker of the cell type or cell cycle stage; in testis samples the $1C$ peak of spermatids is unambiguous, while the $2C$ peak mixes spermatogonia in G1 and secondary spermatocytes.
13. How many primary spermatocytes and primary oocytes are needed, at least, to produce 400 offspring in a mammal? How many microsporocytes (pollen mother cells) and megasporocytes are needed for 400 seeds in a flowering plant?

    **Answer** Each primary spermatocyte gives four sperm and each primary oocyte one egg, and each offspring needs one of each: 100 spermatocytes and 400 oocytes. In a flowering plant each microsporocyte gives four pollen grains, and one pollen grain delivers the two sperm of a double fertilization, while each megasporocyte gives one functional megaspore and thus one embryo sac: 100 microsporocytes and 400 megasporocytes.
14. Rice has $2n = 24$. At pachytene, how many bivalents, chromosomes (centromeres) and chromatids are there? How many chromosome combinations can a gamete have (ignoring crossing over), and what is the probability that a gamete carries only maternal centromeres?

    **Answer** 12 bivalents, 24 chromosomes (24 centromeres) and 48 chromatids. $2^{12} = 4096$ combinations; all maternal centromeres with probability $2^{-12} = 1/4096 \approx 2.4 \times 10^{-4}$.
15. In a plant $AB/ab$, a chiasma is seen between the two loci in $30\,\%$ of pollen mother cells and never more than one. What are the gamete frequencies? What would they be if the loci were on different chromosomes?

    **Answer** A meiocyte with one crossover in the interval gives two recombinant and two parental products, so $r = 0.30/2 = 0.15$: $AB$ and $ab$ $0.425$ each, $Ab$ and $aB$ $0.075$ each. On different chromosomes all four gametes would be $0.25$ (Proposition 5.3.1), whatever the frequency of chiasmata.
