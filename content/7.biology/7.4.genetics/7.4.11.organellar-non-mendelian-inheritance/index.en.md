Mendel's rules rest on three assumptions: both parents contribute equally to the offspring, each **heterozygote** passes either **allele** to a gamete with probability $1/2$, and the outcome of a cross does not depend on which parent was the mother. All three follow from the behavior of nuclear **chromosomes** in **meiosis** ([7.4.5](/7.4.5)). **Genes** outside the nucleus, in **mitochondria** and **plastids**, break all three: they are usually transmitted by one parent only, they are present in many copies that are partitioned at random rather than segregated by a **spindle**, and the outcome of a cross depends on its direction. This chapter treats these exceptions as a branch of transmission genetics with its own quantitative rules. The structure of the **organelle** **genomes** and the **cell biology** of the germline bottleneck were described in [7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.5) and [7.1.23](/7.1.23#sec-23.5.4); here we ask how organelle **mutations** are recognized in crosses, how their frequency drifts within cells and through generations, and how this determines the risk of mitochondrial disease. The chapter ends with three other phenomena that also make **reciprocal crosses** differ, though for different reasons: maternal effects (the egg's cytoplasm is made under the control of the mother's nuclear genes; this connects to the maternal-effect genes of development in 7.4.20), cytoplasmic male sterility (an interaction between mitochondrial and nuclear genes exploited for hybrid seed) and infectious heredity by endosymbiotic bacteria such as *Wolbachia*. Genomic imprinting, the fifth source of parent-of-origin effects, is treated in 7.4.18.

## 11.1. Criteria for Non-Mendelian Inheritance

### 11.1.1. What Mendelian Transmission Predicts

For an **autosomal** gene, a cross gives the same result whichever parent carries the **mutant** allele, and in the F2 the mutant **phenotype** reappears in a fixed proportion (1/4 for a **recessive** allele) in both sexes. In a **testcross** of a **heterozygote**, the two **alleles** appear in a 1:1 ratio, and in fungi with ordered asci a heterozygous **locus** segregates 4:4 in every **ascus** ([7.4.9](/7.4.9)). Departures from these patterns signal one of a small number of mechanisms.

**Definition 11.1.1 (Non-Mendelian Inheritance)** **Non-Mendelian inheritance** is the transmission of a trait in a pattern that does not follow the segregation of nuclear chromosomes in **meiosis**: typically the results of **reciprocal crosses** differ, a heterozygote does not give 1:1 segregation, and the trait follows one parent (usually the mother). Its main sources are genes in organelle genomes (cytoplasmic or extranuclear inheritance), maternal effects of nuclear genes, genomic imprinting and inherited endosymbionts.

**X-linked** inheritance also makes reciprocal crosses differ, but it is Mendelian: the difference is between sons and daughters, and it disappears when the **sex chromosomes** are followed ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)). The other mechanisms are distinguished by how the trait behaves in later generations and by experiments that separate nucleus from cytoplasm.

| Mechanism | Reciprocal F1 | Later generations | Decisive test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autosomal (Mendelian) | identical | 3:1 or 1:1 ratios, both sexes | — |
| **X-linked** | differ between the sexes (**criss-cross inheritance**) | ratios differ by sex | follow X and Y |
| Organellar | all progeny like the mother | always like the mother; no segregation in backcrosses to the father, however many generations | transfer of nucleus or cytoplasm; mapping to organelle DNA; sorting-out of mixed cytoplasm |
| Maternal effect | all progeny have the **phenotype** of the mother's **genotype** | the trait follows nuclear genes with a delay of one generation: 3:1 among broods in F3 | phenotype predicted by the mother's genotype, not by the zygote's |
| Genomic imprinting (7.4.18) | differ according to the parent who transmitted the allele | the allele is silent or active according to its parental origin, and the imprint is reset in each generation | parent-of-origin of the allele |
| Infectious (endosymbiont) | progeny like the mother | can be cured by antibiotics or heat; sometimes transmissible by injection | curing; detection of the symbiont |

**Algorithm 11.1.1 (Analysis of a Reciprocal-Cross Difference)** Given a trait whose reciprocal F1 differ:

1. If sons and daughters differ in a way that follows the X chromosome of the parents, the gene is **sex-linked**.
2. Otherwise backcross the F1 repeatedly to the paternal line (as males). If the trait of the original mother persists in every generation while the nuclear markers of the father are substituted, the determinant is cytoplasmic; cure the line with antibiotics to distinguish an endosymbiont from an organelle gene, and look for heteroplasmic sectors (variegation, sorting-out) as a sign of an organelle genome.
3. If instead the trait segregates in the F3 as 3:1 among broods, with each brood uniform, the determinant is a nuclear gene acting through the mother (maternal effect).
4. If the trait depends on the parental origin of a nuclear allele and reverses when the allele passes through the other sex, consider imprinting.

**Formula 11.1.1 (Nuclear Substitution by Recurrent Backcrossing)** If a line A is used once as the mother and its descendants are then crossed as mothers to males of line B in every generation, the expected fraction of the nuclear genome derived from B after $g$ crosses to B (the first cross included) is

$$
1 - \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{g},
$$
because each cross halves the remaining share of A. The cytoplasm, transmitted through the eggs, remains that of A throughout.

After $g = 6$ crosses 98.4 % of the nuclear genome comes from B, and after 10 crosses 99.9 %; a trait of A that persists through such a program is cytoplasmic. Line A's alleles linked to a selected nuclear marker are lost more slowly (**linkage** drag), so the test is cleanest when no nuclear marker of A is selected. Because organelle genes do not segregate in meiosis, they cannot be placed on the nuclear **linkage map**; where they recombine at all (in yeast and *Chlamydomonas* zygotes), they are mapped on the organelle genome itself.

## 11.2. Genetic Features of Organelle Genomes

Mitochondria and plastids descend from bacteria (**endosymbiotic theory**, [7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.8)) and keep reduced genomes: human **mitochondrial DNA** is a circle of $16\,569\,\mathrm{bp}$ with 37 genes (13 **polypeptides** of the **respiratory chain**, 2 rRNAs, 22 **tRNAs**); the yeast **mtDNA** is about $86\,\mathrm{kb}$ but encodes fewer **proteins**; land-plant mtDNAs range from about $200\,\mathrm{kb}$ to more than $10\,\mathrm{Mb}$ with much noncoding DNA and frequent recombination between repeats; **chloroplast** genomes are $120$–$160\,\mathrm{kb}$ with about 100–130 genes. Organelle genes are expressed with their own **ribosomes**, sometimes with **RNA editing** and with deviations from the standard **genetic code**, and most organelle proteins are encoded in the nucleus ([7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.5), [7.2.2](/7.2.2)). For transmission genetics, five properties matter.

| Property | Nuclear genes | Organelle genes | Genetic consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copy number per cell | 2 (**diploid**) | $10^2$–$10^4$ (mtDNA); $10^3$–$10^4$ (cpDNA in a leaf cell) | a cell can carry a mixture of genotypes |
| Partition at division | spindle, exact | random among organelles and genomes | the mixture drifts and sorts out |
| Contribution of the gametes | equal | usually one parent (most often the mother) | uniparental inheritance; reciprocal crosses differ |
| Recombination | regular, in meiosis | rare (animals) to frequent (plants, yeast) | animal mtDNA is inherited as one haplotype |
| Mutation rate | about $10^{-8}$ per bp per generation in humans | about 10 to 100 times higher for animal mtDNA; lower for plant organelle DNA | many mtDNA **polymorphisms**; mtDNA as a **molecular clock** for maternal lineages |

Because human mtDNA is maternally inherited and does not recombine, it records maternal lineages: a grandchild and the mother's mother share the same mtDNA sequence apart from new mutations, which is used in forensic identification and in population history ([7.10](/7.10)).

## 11.3. Maternal, Biparental and Paternal Inheritance

### 11.3.1. *Neurospora* poky

The first organelle mutation identified with a gene was *poky* in *Neurospora crassa* (Mitchell and Mitchell 1952), a slow-growing mutant with defective **cytochromes**. In a cross between strains of opposite mating type, one parent acts as the female: it forms the large protoperithecium that contributes the cytoplasm of the zygote, while the other contributes essentially only a nucleus. When a *poky* female carrying the nuclear allele $ad^{+}$ is fertilized by a $poky^{+}$, $ad^{-}$ strain, the nuclear marker segregates 4:4 in every ascus, as meiosis requires, but all eight **ascospores** are *poky*; in the reciprocal cross all eight are $poky^{+}$. The *poky* mutation turned out to be a 4-bp deletion in the mitochondrial gene for the small-subunit rRNA, which reduces mitochondrial **translation**. The experiment contains the essential logic: a nuclear marker shows that meiosis was normal, and the cytoplasmic trait ignores it.

A second test separates nucleus and cytoplasm without meiosis. Hyphae of filamentous fungi fuse, mixing their cytoplasm but not their nuclei, to form a **heterokaryon**. When a *poky* strain and a **wild-type** strain carrying different nuclear markers form a **heterokaryon**, the conidia that it later produces each receive nuclei of one kind, but the cytoplasm has been mixed: some conidia with the nucleus of the wild-type strain grow slowly, and some with the nucleus of the *poky* strain grow normally. The phenotype travels with the cytoplasm and not with the nucleus. Such a heterokaryon test is the fungal version of **nuclear transfer**.

### 11.3.2. Variegation, Heteroplasmy and Sorting-Out

Carl Correns (1909) crossed flowers on green, white and variegated branches of the four-o'clock *Mirabilis jalapa*. The phenotype of the progeny depended only on the branch that bore the seed: seeds from green branches gave green seedlings, seeds from white branches gave white seedlings (which die), and seeds from variegated branches gave green, white and variegated seedlings in variable proportions, whatever the source of the pollen.

| Branch bearing the flower (seed parent) | Pollen from | Progeny |
|---|---|---|
| green | green, white or variegated | green |
| white | green, white or variegated | white (seedlings die) |
| variegated | green, white or variegated | green, white and variegated, in no fixed ratio |
 In *Mirabilis* plastids are transmitted through the egg only; a variegated branch has cells with a mixture of normal and defective plastids, and the egg of such a branch may receive only normal, only mutant or mixed plastids.

A cell with more than one organelle genotype is in a state of **heteroplasmy** ([7.1.23](/7.1.23#sec-23.5.4)).

**Definition 11.3.1 (Homoplasmy and Vegetative Segregation)** A cell, tissue or individual that contains only one genotype of an organelle genome is **homoplasmic**; its state is **homoplasmy**. **Vegetative segregation** (also cytoplasmic segregation, sorting-out) is the change of the proportions of organelle genotypes among the descendants of a heteroplasmic cell during mitotic divisions, which partition organelles and their genomes at random; it produces homoplasmic cell lineages from heteroplasmic ones, visible in plants as green and white sectors.

Sorting-out is the organelle counterpart of segregation, but it is probabilistic and gradual rather than exact and immediate; its kinetics is the subject of Section 11.4. Not every variegation is maternally inherited. In maize, the nuclear **recessive** mutation *iojap* (*ij*, on chromosome 7; Rhoades 1943) causes some plastids to lose their ribosomes; once formed, the defective plastids are inherited maternally as organelles, even in the progeny of $Ij\,Ij$ plants. The reciprocal crosses show both kinds of inheritance in one experiment:

| Cross | $\mathrm{F}_1$ | Later generations |
|---|---|---|
| green $Ij\,Ij$ (female) × striped $ij\,ij$ (male) | all green ($Ij\,ij$) | $\mathrm{F}_2$ $3$ green : $1$ striped or white, a Mendelian ratio |
| striped $ij\,ij$ (female) × green $Ij\,Ij$ (male) | green, striped and white plants in no fixed ratio | striped $\mathrm{F}_1$ backcrossed as females to $Ij\,Ij$ males for several generations still give the three classes, after *ij* has been eliminated from the nucleus |

A nuclear gene can thus create a cytoplasmic mutation, and the two kinds of inheritance appear in the same **pedigree**. The Iojap protein is associated with the large **subunit** of the plastid ribosome; plastids that lose their ribosomes in $ij\,ij$ tissue cannot rebuild them, because many ribosomal components are encoded in the plastid genome and need plastid ribosomes to be made. The autonomy of an organelle is therefore relative: its genome is necessary, but most of its proteins come from the nucleus ([7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.5)).

### 11.3.3. *Chlamydomonas* and Yeast

The unicellular alga *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* has isogamous gametes of two mating types, $\mathrm{mt}^{+}$ and $\mathrm{mt}^{-}$, of equal size. Ruth Sager (1954) found that resistance to streptomycin encoded in the chloroplast DNA is inherited from the $\mathrm{mt}^{+}$ parent in more than $95\,\%$ of zygotes. Uniparental inheritance here is not a matter of gamete size: within an hour of zygote formation the chloroplast DNA of the $\mathrm{mt}^{-}$ parent is degraded, while that of the $\mathrm{mt}^{+}$ parent is protected, under the control of genes in the mating-type locus. Mitochondrial DNA of *Chlamydomonas* is inherited from the opposite parent, $\mathrm{mt}^{-}$. The direction of uniparental inheritance is therefore a property of the organelle and the species, not a general law about mothers.

The budding yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* shows the other extreme: both **haploid** cells of a mating contribute mitochondria, the zygote is heteroplasmic, and its diploid buds become homoplasmic for one or the other parental mtDNA within about 20 divisions (biparental inheritance followed by rapid vegetative segregation). Yeast respiratory mutants form small colonies on glucose, on which they can ferment, and cannot grow on nonfermentable carbon sources such as glycerol (Ephrussi 1949). Petites arise spontaneously at the unusually high rate of about $0.1$–$1\,\%$ per cell generation, and almost all cells become petite after growth in ethidium bromide, which blocks mtDNA **replication**; their colonies are a third to a half the size of wild-type (grande) colonies, and the cells lack cytochromes $a$, $a_3$ and $b$, which require mitochondrially encoded subunits. A petite colony never gives rise to grande cells, whereas grande colonies continually throw off petites.

**Definition 11.3.2 (Petite Mutant)** A **petite mutant** of yeast is a respiratory-deficient mutant that forms small colonies on glucose. *Segregational* petites carry a mutation in a nuclear gene for a mitochondrial function and segregate 2 grande : 2 petite in **tetrads** of a cross with wild type; *neutral* petites ($\rho^{0}$) have lost their mtDNA, and a cross with wild type gives only grande diploids and 4 grande : 0 petite tetrads; *suppressive* petites ($\rho^{-}$) carry large deletions of mtDNA that replicate preferentially, so that a cross with wild type gives a high, strain-specific proportion of petite diploids.

The three classes are a compact lesson: Mendelian segregation identifies a nuclear gene; the disappearance of a defect in a cross identifies the loss of an organelle genome, which the partner restores; and the **dominance** of a defect in a cross identifies a defective genome that outcompetes the wild type within the cell.

### 11.3.4. Mechanisms of Uniparental Inheritance

The default in animals and most flowering plants is **maternal inheritance** ([7.3.12](/7.3.12#def-12.5.3)). It is ensured by several mechanisms acting together: dilution (a human oocyte contains about $10^{5}$–$10^{6}$ copies of mtDNA, a sperm about $10^{2}$), reduction of mtDNA in the male germline, and active elimination of paternal mitochondria after fertilization by ubiquitylation and **autophagy**. Plastids are inherited paternally in most conifers and biparentally in a minority of angiosperms (*Pelargonium*, *Oenothera*). In mussels, doubly uniparental inheritance transmits a female mtDNA to all offspring and a separate male mtDNA only to sons. Rare paternal leakage is detected in crosses of mice and *Drosophila* with distinguishable mtDNAs at frequencies of about $10^{-4}$ per offspring. A report of biparental mtDNA transmission in three human families (Luo et al. 2018) was later explained largely by large nuclear insertions of mtDNA (NUMTs) inherited in a Mendelian way (Wei et al. 2020): an apparent violation of **maternal inheritance** must exclude nuclear copies of organelle DNA.

## 11.4. A Quantitative Model of Heteroplasmic Segregation

### 11.4.1. Drift over Generations

The cellular mechanism of the germline bottleneck was described in [7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.5.3), where a single generation of sampling gave the distribution of a child's **heteroplasmy** ([Formula 12.5.1 in 7.3.12](/7.3.12#fml-12.5.1)). Over several generations of a maternal lineage, or several rounds of division in a cell lineage, the sampling repeats. The simplest model treats each generation as a draw of $N$ segregating units (genomes or nucleoids) from the mutant fraction of the previous generation, followed by amplification that does not change the fraction. This is the Wright–Fisher model of neutral drift in a population of size $N$ (7.4.23); the mutant fraction $h_t$ is a **Markov chain** with **binomial distribution** transitions $N h_{t+1} \mid h_t \sim \mathrm{Bin}(N, h_t)$.

**Formula 11.4.1 (Heteroplasmy Variance over Generations)** For neutral sampling of $N$ units per generation from an initial mutant fraction $h_0$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[h_t] = h_0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(h_t) = h_0(1 - h_0)\left[1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\right)^{t}\right], \tag{11.1}
$$
and the expected heteroplasmy index $H_t = \mathbb{E}[2h_t(1 - h_t)]$ decays as $H_t = 2h_0(1 - h_0)(1 - 1/N)^{t}$.

**Proof** The conditional mean of $h_{t+1}$ given $h_t$ is $h_t$, so $\mathbb{E}[h_t] = h_0$. The conditional **variance** is $h_t(1 - h_t)/N$. Let $u_t = \mathbb{E}[h_t(1 - h_t)]$. Since $\mathbb{E}[h_{t+1}^2 \mid h_t] = h_t^2 + h_t(1 - h_t)/N$, we have $\mathbb{E}[h_{t+1}(1 - h_{t+1}) \mid h_t] = h_t(1 - h_t)(1 - 1/N)$, hence $u_t = h_0(1 - h_0)(1 - 1/N)^t$. By the **law of total variance**, $\operatorname{Var}(h_t) = \mathbb{E}[h_t^2] - h_0^2 = h_0 - u_t - h_0^2 = h_0(1 - h_0) - u_t$, which is Eq. (11.1). $\square$

Three consequences follow. First, the mean does not change: drift alone neither favors nor removes a mutant, and the probability that a lineage eventually becomes homoplasmic for the mutant is $h_0$, the probability of loss $1 - h_0$ (a martingale argument, the same as for the fixation of a neutral allele in 7.4.23). Second, the variance approaches its maximum $h_0(1 - h_0)$, the variance of a mixture of homoplasmic lineages, with a time constant of about $N$ generations: a narrow bottleneck sorts out quickly. Third, the normalized variance $V_t = \operatorname{Var}(h_t)/[h_0(1 - h_0)] = 1 - b$ with $b = (1 - 1/N)^t$ depends on $N$ and $t$ only through $b$; for one generation $V = 1/N$, so $1/V$ estimates the effective number of segregating units.

The full distribution of $h_t$ is obtained by multiplying the initial distribution by the $(N + 1) \times (N + 1)$ transition matrix $t$ times. Kimura (1955) solved the diffusion approximation of this chain; the resulting two-parameter *Kimura distribution*, with parameters $h_0$ and $b$, has continuous mass on $(0, 1)$ and point masses at $0$ and $1$, and it fits the heteroplasmy of mouse and human oocytes and offspring better than ad hoc distributions (Wonnapinij and colleagues 2008). The diffusion limit itself is an equation of the type treated in [2.5](/2.5); here we use the exact discrete chain.

### 11.4.2. Estimating the Bottleneck and Predicting Offspring

**Example 11.4.1 (Estimating N from Siblings)** A woman with heteroplasmy $h_0 = 0.35$ for a neutral mtDNA variant has six children with heteroplasmies $0.12, 0.35, 0.48, 0.22, 0.61, 0.30$. The **sample variance** is $s^2 = 0.0313$, so $V = s^2/[h_0(1 - h_0)] = 0.0313/0.2275 = 0.138$ and $N \approx 1/V \approx 7$. Six children give a crude estimate: the sampling error of a variance from $n$ observations is about $s^2\sqrt{2/(n - 1)}$, here $63\,\%$ of the estimate. Pooled data from many families and from single oocytes give effective bottlenecks of the order of 10–100 units in humans and mice, smaller than the number of mtDNA copies in primordial germ cells (about 200).

**Example 11.4.2 (Risk over One and Two Generations)** With $N = 20$ and a threshold of $0.6$: a woman with $h_0 = 0.4$ has children with $\operatorname{Var}(h) = 0.4 \times 0.6/20 = 0.012$ (**standard deviation** $0.11$), and the probability that a child has $h \geq 0.6$ is $\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.4) \geq 12) = 0.057$. For her mother's mother at $h_0 = 0.3$, the probability that a daughter exceeds the threshold is $0.005$, but that a granddaughter does is $0.035$, and that a great-granddaughter does, $0.070$ (by the transition matrix); in the same three generations $3.7\,\%$ of lineages have lost the mutant altogether. A low-level variant in a family can therefore produce affected individuals suddenly, after several unaffected generations.

The neutral model is a baseline. Severe mutations are selected against in the female germline, so their transmitted heteroplasmy is lower than predicted, while some variants (and many deletions) have a replicative advantage within cells. The model also applies to somatic tissues: heteroplasmy drifts among cell lineages during development and life, declines with age in blood (where cells with high mutant load are selected against) and rises in postmitotic muscle and neurons. The heteroplasmy measured in blood is therefore an imperfect guide to that in the oocytes.

## 11.5. The Genetics of Mitochondrial Disease

### 11.5.1. Three Genetic Classes

Mitochondrial diseases are disorders of **oxidative phosphorylation**; they affect about 1 in 4300 adults (Gorman and colleagues 2015), and about 1 in 200 newborns carries a pathogenic mtDNA variant, mostly at low heteroplasmy (Elliott and colleagues 2008). Genetically they fall into three classes, with different recurrence risks.

| Class | Inheritance | Examples | Recurrence risk for a sibling or child |
|---|---|---|---|
| mtDNA point mutations | maternal; homoplasmic or heteroplasmic | LHON (m.11778G>A in *MT-ND4*, m.3460G>A in *MT-ND1*, m.14484T>C in *MT-ND6*: about $90\,\%$ of cases); MERRF (m.8344A>G in $\mathrm{tRNA^{Lys}}$, about $80$–$90\,\%$ of cases); MELAS (m.3243A>G in $\mathrm{tRNA^{Leu(UUR)}}$); NARP and Leigh syndrome (m.8993T>G in *MT-ATP6*) | none from fathers; from mothers, all children inherit the variant, and the risk of disease depends on the transmitted level |
| Single large-scale mtDNA deletions | usually sporadic (arising in the mother's germline or early embryo) | Kearns–Sayre syndrome, chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia, Pearson syndrome | low (about 1 in 24 for children of affected women) |
| Nuclear genes for mitochondrial proteins (more than 350 genes) | Mendelian, mostly autosomal recessive; some X-linked or dominant | most mitochondrial disease of childhood; *POLG* mutations causing mtDNA depletion and multiple deletions | 1/4 for siblings in recessive cases |

The third class is a reminder that the mitochondrial **proteome** is mostly nuclear: a mitochondrial phenotype in a family does not by itself mean mtDNA inheritance. A pedigree in which affected women transmit the disease to all children (with variable **penetrance** and **expressivity**) and affected men to none points to mtDNA; one with affected siblings of unaffected parents, or transmission through fathers, points to a nuclear gene.

### 11.5.2. Homoplasmic Disease with Incomplete Penetrance: LHON

The LHON mutations are hypomorphic: a cell homoplasmic for them survives but has reduced **Complex I** activity, and the retinal ganglion cells, whose long axons are unmyelinated within the retina and depend heavily on their mitochondria, degenerate in young adults, causing painless loss of central vision. Most patients are homoplasmic, so all children of a carrier mother inherit the mutation, but **penetrance** is incomplete and sex-dependent: about $50\,\%$ of male and $10\,\%$ of female carriers lose vision. The difference implies **modifiers**: nuclear genes (an X-linked modifier has long been suspected), estrogen, which improves mitochondrial biogenesis, and environmental factors such as smoking. Gene therapy with a nuclear-encoded, mitochondrially targeted *ND4* (allotopic expression) injected into the eye has had modest effects in trials.

### 11.5.3. Heteroplasmic Disease and the Threshold Effect

**Definition 11.5.1 (Threshold Effect)** The **threshold effect** is the appearance of a biochemical defect and of disease only when the fraction of mutant mtDNA in a cell or tissue exceeds a critical value, typically $60$–$90\,\%$, below which the remaining wild-type genomes supply enough function; the threshold is lower in tissues with a higher dependence on **oxidative phosphorylation**.

The threshold exists because mitochondria of a cell fuse and exchange gene products ([7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.3)): a minority of wild-type genomes complements the mutant ones in the same network, much as a wild-type allele masks a recessive one, so that activity stays near normal until the mutant fraction is high and then falls steeply. Thresholds vary with the mutation (point mutations in tRNA genes usually need more than $80$–$90\,\%$ in muscle; large deletions about $60\,\%$) and with the tissue. MERRF illustrates the consequences: patients are heteroplasmic (cells homoplasmic for the tRNA mutation would die), muscle fibers with high mutant load appear as ragged red fibers, and the severity of myoclonic epilepsy, myopathy, deafness and ataxia correlates with the mutant fraction in each tissue. The same variant, m.3243A>G, causes maternally inherited **diabetes** and deafness at lower levels and MELAS at higher levels. For m.8993T>G the relation is particularly sharp: carriers below about $60$–$70\,\%$ are usually unaffected, those around $70$–$90\,\%$ develop NARP (neuropathy, ataxia and retinitis pigmentosa), and those above $90\,\%$ Leigh syndrome, a fatal neurodegeneration of infancy.

**Formula 11.5.1 (Risk of Disease for a Child)** If the child's heteroplasmy has the distribution of Formula 11.4.1 (one generation, $N h \sim \mathrm{Bin}(N, h_0)$) and disease of a given severity requires $h \geq \theta$, then

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{child affected}) = \sum_{k \geq \lceil \theta N \rceil} \binom{N}{k} h_0^{k}(1 - h_0)^{N - k}.
$$
The risk is a steep function of $h_0 - \theta$ measured in units of the standard deviation $\sqrt{h_0(1 - h_0)/N}$.

**Example 11.5.1 (m.8993T>G)** A mother with $h_0 = 0.5$ and an assumed bottleneck of $N = 20$: the probabilities that a child has $h < 0.7$, $0.7 \leq h < 0.9$ and $h \geq 0.9$ are $0.942$, $0.057$ and $0.0002$. A mother with $h_0 = 0.8$ instead has children with $\mathbb{P}(h \geq 0.9) = \mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.8) \geq 18) = 0.21$. Empirical data for this mutation show the same steep dependence on the maternal level, though with more spread than a single-$N$ model predicts, because $N$ itself varies.

### 11.5.4. Prevention: Prenatal Testing and Mitochondrial Replacement

**Genetic counseling** for heteroplasmic mutations has to work with these distributions. Preimplantation genetic testing measures the heteroplasmy of a few cells biopsied from each embryo, which predicts that of the fetus well because heteroplasmy is roughly uniform among blastomeres; a meta-analysis found that embryos below about $18\,\%$ mutant load have a probability of about $95\,\%$ or more of being unaffected (Hellebrekers and colleagues 2012). For a woman with high heteroplasmy, or with a homoplasmic mutation, no embryo may qualify.

**Definition 11.5.2 (Mitochondrial Replacement Therapy)** **Mitochondrial replacement therapy** prevents transmission of an mtDNA disease by transferring the nuclear genome of the affected mother into an enucleated oocyte or zygote of a donor with normal mtDNA: in *maternal spindle transfer* the **metaphase** II spindle with its chromosomes is moved between unfertilized oocytes; in *pronuclear transfer* the two pronuclei are moved between zygotes after fertilization. The child receives its nuclear genome from its parents and its mtDNA from the donor.

The United Kingdom permitted the techniques by law in 2015 (first licence 2017); the first child born after spindle transfer was reported in 2016, from a clinic in Mexico. In 2025 the Newcastle group reported eight children born after pronuclear transfer, all healthy at the time of the report; in five the maternal mtDNA was undetectable and in three it was present in blood at low levels (up to about $16\,\%$). The genetic risk lies in carryover: the small amount of maternal mtDNA transferred with the nucleus (typically below $2\,\%$) passes through the germline bottleneck of a daughter, where it can drift (Formula 11.4.1) or, in some **embryonic stem cell** lines, increase by selection ("reversion"). With carryover $h_0 = 0.02$ and $N = 20$, a daughter's oocytes carry the maternal mtDNA with probability $1 - 0.98^{20} = 0.33$, but the probability that one of them exceeds $0.6$ by drift alone is negligible ($< 10^{-15}$); the concern is selection, not neutral drift. Editing of the mitochondrial genome itself, by mitochondrially targeted nucleases that destroy mutant genomes and shift heteroplasmy toward the wild type, or by **base editors**, is described in [7.2.24](/7.2.24#sec-24.6.3).

## 11.6. Other Non-Mendelian Phenomena

### 11.6.1. Maternal Effect

**Definition 11.6.1 (Maternal Effect)** A **maternal effect** is the determination of an offspring's phenotype by the genotype of its mother rather than by its own genotype, because the mother's nuclear genes produce substances (**mRNAs**, proteins, metabolites) deposited in the egg that act in the embryo. The responsible genes segregate in a Mendelian way, but their phenotypic expression is shifted by one generation.

The classic case is the direction of shell coiling in the pond snail *Lymnaea* (Boycott and Diver 1923; explained by Sturtevant 1923). Dextral (right-handed) coiling is controlled by a dominant allele $D$ and sinistral by $d$, but the coiling of a snail is set by the orientation of the spindles at the third cleavage division, which depends on cytoplasmic factors in the egg. A $dd$ female (sinistral) fertilized by a $DD$ male gives F1 snails that are all $Dd$ but sinistral, because their mother was $dd$. Self-fertilized F1 snails (*Lymnaea* is a hermaphrodite) produce F2 with genotypes $DD : Dd : dd = 1 : 2 : 1$, all dextral because their mother was $Dd$. Only when each F2 snail is self-fertilized do the broods reveal the genotypes: the $DD$ and $Dd$ snails produce dextral broods and the $dd$ snails sinistral broods, in the ratio 3:1 among broods. The gene was identified in 2016 as a duplicated formin gene, *Lsdia1*, whose maternal mRNA is present in dextral but not sinistral eggs of *L. stagnalis* (Davison and colleagues; Kuroda and colleagues); **formins** nucleate **actin filaments**, which tilt the cleavage spindles ([7.3.13](/7.3.13)).

**Formula 11.6.1 (Maternal-Effect Phenotypes)** For a maternal-effect gene with a dominant allele $D$, an offspring has the phenotype of $D$ with probability equal to the probability that its mother carries $D$. Hence the phenotype frequencies of generation $t$ equal the genotype classes of generation $t - 1$: if the mothers of generation $t$ have genotype frequencies $P_{DD}, P_{Dd}, P_{dd}$, the fraction of offspring with the dominant phenotype is $P_{DD} + P_{Dd}$, regardless of the offspring's own genotypes.

![Maternal-effect inheritance of shell coiling in *Lymnaea* (Formula 11.6.1), shells drawn with Raup's coiling model. A sinistral dd female crossed with a dextral DD male gives F1 snails that are all Dd but sinistral, because their mother was dd. The self-fertilized F1 produce F2 in the ratio 1 DD : 2 Dd : 1 dd, all dextral because their mother was Dd. When each F2 snail is self-fertilized, the DD and Dd snails produce dextral broods and the dd snails sinistral broods: the 3:1 ratio appears among broods, one generation late.](./plots/snail-coiling.py)

Because the phenotype of the offspring is set before fertilization, maternal effect was formerly also called predetermination, and the one-generation delay of the Mendelian ratio "delayed inheritance". The *Lymnaea* effect is persistent: the coiling set in the egg lasts for life.

Maternal effects can also be transient. In the flour moth *Ephestia kuehniella*, the dominant allele $a^{+}$ is needed to make kynurenine, a precursor of eye and skin pigment. Larvae of genotype $aa$ from an $a^{+}a$ mother are pigmented, because kynurenine was deposited in the egg, but the pigment is diluted during growth and the adults have the light (red) eyes of their genotype instead of the brown eyes of $a^{+}$ moths. Reciprocal **testcrosses** of the $\mathrm{F}_1$ therefore differ only in the larvae:

| Testcross | Larvae | Adults |
|---|---|---|
| $a^{+}a$ female × $aa$ male | all pigmented | $\frac12$ brown-eyed ($a^{+}a$), $\frac12$ red-eyed ($aa$) |
| $aa$ female × $a^{+}a$ male | $\frac12$ pigmented ($a^{+}a$), $\frac12$ unpigmented ($aa$) | $\frac12$ brown-eyed, $\frac12$ red-eyed |

A maternal effect resembles cytoplasmic inheritance in its reciprocal F1, but it is caused by nuclear genes of the mother: it disappears after one generation and gives Mendelian ratios among the broods of the next. Maternal-effect genes that pattern the embryo, such as *bicoid* in *Drosophila*, are analyzed genetically in 7.4.20, and their mechanisms in 7.5.

### 11.6.2. Cytoplasmic Male Sterility and Hybrid Seed

Male sterility, the failure of a plant to produce functional pollen while its pistils remain normal, has been found in hundreds of species. Genetically it falls into two classes. *Genic male sterility* is caused by nuclear genes alone, usually recessive alleles of genes needed for anther or pollen development, and segregates in a Mendelian way; it is hard to propagate, because a sterile $ms\,ms$ plant pollinated by a fertile $Ms\,ms$ sibling gives only half sterile progeny, except when sterility is conditional on the environment (below). Cytoplasmic male sterility is caused by the mitochondrial genome. Many plant species carry mitochondrial genomes that cause pollen abortion, typically through a chimeric **open reading frame** created by recombination between repeats in the plant mtDNA. The effect can be suppressed by nuclear genes; when no restorer is known, the sterile cytoplasm is transmitted to all progeny, which remain sterile, and some older texts treat such cases as a separate "cytoplasmic" type, distinct from the "nucleo-cytoplasmic" type with restorers. Both are cytoplasmic male sterility in current usage.

**Definition 11.6.2 (Cytoplasmic Male Sterility and Restorer Genes)** **Cytoplasmic male sterility** (CMS) is the failure to produce functional pollen caused by a mitochondrial genotype (the sterile cytoplasm $S$), in plants whose female fertility is normal. A **restorer gene** ($Rf$) is a nuclear gene whose dominant allele suppresses the effect of the CMS cytoplasm and restores pollen fertility; plants with normal cytoplasm $N$ are fertile whatever their $Rf$ genotype. Genotypes are written as cytoplasm(nuclear genotype), for example $S(rf\,rf)$.

For one restorer locus and two cytoplasms there are six genotypes, of which only one is sterile:

| Cytoplasm | $Rf\,Rf$ | $Rf\,rf$ | $rf\,rf$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $N$ | fertile | fertile | fertile |
| $S$ | fertile | fertile | sterile |

Since the progeny always receive the cytoplasm of the sterile mother, the five fertile genotypes fall into three classes as pollen parents of an $S(rf\,rf)$ line: $N(rf\,rf)$ gives sterile $S(rf\,rf)$ progeny (it maintains the sterility); $N(Rf\,Rf)$ and $S(Rf\,Rf)$ give fertile $S(Rf\,rf)$ progeny (they restore fertility); and $N(Rf\,rf)$ and $S(Rf\,rf)$ give a $1 : 1$ segregation of fertile and sterile progeny (heterozygous restorers, with a sporophytic restorer).

Many restorer genes encode pentatricopeptide repeat proteins, sequence-specific RNA-binding proteins imported into mitochondria that cleave or block translation of the CMS transcript. Restoration can act in two ways. A *sporophytic* restorer acts in the diploid tissues of the anther, so that an $S(Rf\,rf)$ plant makes fully fertile pollen; a *gametophytic* restorer acts in each haploid pollen grain, so that only $Rf$ pollen of an $S(Rf\,rf)$ plant is viable (half of its pollen aborts).

**Example 11.6.1 (Fertility in the F2)** The F1 hybrid $S(Rf\,rf)$ is self-fertilized. With a sporophytic restorer, the F2 genotypes are $S(Rf\,Rf) : S(Rf\,rf) : S(rf\,rf) = 1 : 2 : 1$, and $3/4$ of the plants are fertile. With a gametophytic restorer, only $Rf$ pollen functions, so the F2 are $S(Rf\,Rf) : S(Rf\,rf) = 1 : 1$; all plants set seed, but the $S(Rf\,rf)$ half shed $50\,\%$ aborted pollen. A testcross of the F1 as pollen parent onto an $S(rf\,rf)$ line gives $1/2$ fertile progeny in the sporophytic case and all fertile progeny in the gametophytic case.

CMS makes large-scale production of hybrid seed possible in self-pollinating crops, where emasculation by hand is impractical. In the *three-line system* developed for rice by Yuan Longping and colleagues (wild-abortive CMS found in 1970, the first commercial hybrids in 1976), a CMS line provides the female parent, a maintainer line with the same nuclear genome but normal cytoplasm propagates it, and a restorer line ($N(Rf\,Rf)$ or $S(Rf\,Rf)$) supplies pollen that gives fertile $F_1$ hybrids. A new sterile line is obtained by backcrossing a chosen cultivar, as the recurrent male, onto a CMS line for several generations (Formula 11.1.1), which transfers the sterile cytoplasm into the cultivar's nuclear background and makes the cultivar its maintainer. Seed is produced in two isolated plots: in the multiplication plot rows of the A line alternate with the B line, and the seed set on the A rows is new A-line seed; in the hybrid plot rows of the A line alternate with the R line, and the seed on the A rows is the hybrid, while the B and R lines are propagated by their own selfed seed. The *two-line system* replaces CMS by environment-sensitive genic male sterility, starting from Shi Mingsong's discovery in 1973 of a photoperiod-sensitive male-sterile plant in the japonica cultivar Nongken 58: nuclear mutations such as *pms3* (photoperiod-sensitive, in Nongken 58S) and *tms5* (thermosensitive) make plants sterile in long days or at high temperature and fertile otherwise, so that one line serves as both sterile female and its own maintainer. Hybrid vigor itself is treated quantitatively in 7.4.24. Genetic uniformity has a cost: in 1970 about $85\,\%$ of the US maize crop carried Texas (T) cytoplasm, whose CMS gene *T-urf13* also makes mitochondria sensitive to a toxin of *Bipolaris maydis* race T, and the southern corn leaf blight epidemic destroyed about $15\,\%$ of the harvest.

![The three-line system of hybrid seed production with cytoplasmic male sterility (Definition 11.6.2). The A line, S(rf rf), is male sterile and serves as the female parent. Pollinated by the maintainer B line, N(rf rf), which has the same nuclear genotype in a normal cytoplasm, it produces seed that is again S(rf rf), so the A line is propagated. ### 11.6.3. Infectious Heredity

Some cytoplasmic traits are caused by inherited microorganisms. In *Paramecium aurelia*, "killer" strains (Sonneborn 1943) release particles that kill sensitive cells; the trait requires a dominant nuclear gene $K$ and cytoplasmic kappa particles, later identified as the bacterium *Caedibacter*, which carries a defective phage that produces the toxin. Kappa can be maintained only in cells with $K$: a $kk$ cell loses its kappa within a few fissions, and a $KK$ cell that lacks kappa cannot make it de novo, so the killer phenotype requires both. *Paramecium* conjugates in pairs: each partner makes haploid micronuclei by meiosis, the partners exchange one, and each fuses the received nucleus with its own, so both exconjugants have the same new diploid genotype; the cytoplasm is exchanged only if the pair stays joined for longer than usual. Autogamy, the fusion of two identical meiotic products in one cell, makes any cell **homozygous**.

| Cross $KK$ + kappa (killer) × $kk$ (sensitive) | Exconjugants | After autogamy of an exconjugant |
|---|---|---|
| short **conjugation**, no cytoplasmic exchange | $Kk$ + kappa (killer) and $Kk$ without kappa (sensitive) | from the killer, $\frac12$ $KK$ + kappa (killer) and $\frac12$ $kk$, which loses kappa (sensitive); from the sensitive, $KK$ or $kk$, both sensitive |
| long conjugation, cytoplasm exchanged | both $Kk$ + kappa (killers) | from each, $\frac12$ killer, $\frac12$ sensitive |

That the two exconjugants of a short conjugation have the same nuclear genotype but different phenotypes, according to the cytoplasm each kept, was the decisive evidence for a cytoplasmic factor.

In *Drosophila*, the sigma rhabdovirus makes flies sensitive to $\ce{CO2}$: normal flies recover quickly from $\ce{CO2}$ anesthesia, infected ones are paralyzed or die. Sensitive females crossed with resistant males give sensitive progeny; sensitive males crossed with resistant females give mostly resistant progeny with a few sensitive ones, because the **virus** is transmitted efficiently through the egg and only occasionally through the sperm. Maternally transmitted *Spiroplasma* kills male embryos early in development, so infected "sex-ratio" females produce almost only daughters, which pass the trait on. The most widespread is *Wolbachia*, an α-proteobacterium in the cytoplasm of the germline of an estimated $40$–$65\,\%$ of insect species, transmitted through the eggs. *Wolbachia* manipulates host reproduction to favor infected females, by male killing, feminization of genetic males, induction of parthenogenesis or, most commonly, cytoplasmic incompatibility.

**Definition 11.6.3 (Cytoplasmic Incompatibility)** **Cytoplasmic incompatibility** (CI) is the death of embryos produced when sperm from a male infected with an endosymbiont such as *Wolbachia* fertilizes an egg of an uninfected female (or one infected with an incompatible strain); crosses of infected females with infected or uninfected males are compatible. The sperm is modified during **spermatogenesis** by two *Wolbachia* genes, *cifA* and *cifB*, and rescued only by *cifA* in the egg.

CI gives infected females an advantage without any benefit to the host, because uninfected females lose part of their progeny. Let $p$ be the frequency of infected females, $s_{\mathrm{f}}$ the relative reduction of their fecundity, $s_{\mathrm{h}}$ the fraction of incompatible embryos that die, and $\mu$ the fraction of ova of infected mothers that lack the bacterium.

**Formula 11.6.2 (Spread of Cytoplasmic Incompatibility)** With random mating and discrete generations,

$$
p' = \frac{p(1 - s_{\mathrm{f}})(1 - \mu)}{1 - s_{\mathrm{f}}p - s_{\mathrm{h}}p(1 - p) - \mu s_{\mathrm{h}}p^2(1 - s_{\mathrm{f}})}. \tag{11.2}
$$
For perfect transmission ($\mu = 0$) the equilibria are $p = 0$ (stable), $\hat p = s_{\mathrm{f}}/s_{\mathrm{h}}$ (unstable) and $p = 1$ (stable); the infection invades only if introduced above the threshold $\hat p$.

**Proof** Infected females produce $1 - s_{\mathrm{f}}$ offspring, of which a fraction $1 - \mu$ are infected and survive regardless of the father; their uninfected fraction $\mu$ survives with probability $1 - s_{\mathrm{h}}p$ (the probability of mating with an uninfected male, plus incompatible matings that survive). Uninfected females produce offspring surviving with probability $1 - s_{\mathrm{h}}p$. The total output is $p(1 - s_{\mathrm{f}})[(1 - \mu) + \mu(1 - s_{\mathrm{h}}p)] + (1 - p)(1 - s_{\mathrm{h}}p)$, which simplifies to the denominator of Eq. (11.2); the numerator is the output of infected offspring. Setting $p' = p$ with $\mu = 0$ gives $1 - s_{\mathrm{f}} = 1 - s_{\mathrm{f}}p - s_{\mathrm{h}}p(1 - p)$, that is $s_{\mathrm{f}}(1 - p) = s_{\mathrm{h}}p(1 - p)$, with solutions $p = 1$ and $p = s_{\mathrm{f}}/s_{\mathrm{h}}$. Below $\hat p$ the fecundity cost outweighs the CI advantage and $p' < p$; above it the reverse holds. $\square$

The model is a population-genetic analysis of selection on a cytoplasmic element, with the frequency-dependent advantage typical of CI (the more infected males, the more uninfected embryos die). It is used to plan releases. The *w*Mel strain from *Drosophila melanogaster*, transferred to the dengue mosquito *Aedes aegypti*, causes nearly complete CI, reduces female fecundity by a few to about ten percent and, decisively, blocks the replication of dengue and other viruses in the mosquito. Releases in Cairns (from 2011) established the infection at more than $90\,\%$, and a cluster-randomized trial in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, found a $77\,\%$ reduction in the incidence of virologically confirmed dengue (Utarini and colleagues 2021).

**Example 11.6.2** For $s_{\mathrm{f}} = 0.1$ and $s_{\mathrm{h}} = 0.9$ the threshold is $\hat p = 0.111$. Released at $p = 0.10$, the infection declines ($0.099$, $0.098$, ...); released at $p = 0.15$ it increases slowly at first ($0.155$, $0.161$, $0.168$, ...) and then rapidly, and from $p = 0.2$ it exceeds $0.95$ within 11 generations. With $\mu = 0.03$ the threshold rises to $0.146$ and the stable equilibrium falls to $0.996$; a strain with $s_{\mathrm{f}} = 0.2$ has a threshold of $0.222$.

![Spread of a *Wolbachia* infection that induces cytoplasmic incompatibility (Formula 11.6.2). A: change in infection frequency per generation as a function of the frequency, for CI strength sh = 0.9 and fecundity cost sf = 0.1 or 0.2 with perfect maternal transmission, and sf = 0.1 with 3% of uninfected ova (mu = 0.03); open circles mark the unstable equilibria, the invasion thresholds, near sf/sh. ## Questions

1. A trait differs in reciprocal crosses of two inbred lines of maize. Outline a crossing program that distinguishes sex linkage, an organelle gene, a maternal effect and an endosymbiont.

   **Answer** Maize has no sex chromosomes, so sex linkage is excluded. Backcross the F1 from the cross with mother A repeatedly to line B as the male parent, recovering line B's nuclear genome (about $1 - 2^{-g}$ after $g$ backcrosses). If the trait of line A persists through all generations with the nucleus of B, it is cytoplasmic; test with antibiotic or heat treatment and microscopy or PCR for bacteria to exclude an endosymbiont, and look for variegated sectors or sequence differences in the organelle DNA. If instead the F1 resemble the mother's genotype but the trait segregates among the broods of the F3 (3:1 of uniform broods) and cosegregates with a nuclear marker, it is a maternal effect (Algorithm 11.1.1).

2. In *Neurospora*, a *poky* strain of mating type A carrying $ad^{+}$ is used as the female in a cross with a $poky^{+}$, $ad^{-}$ strain of mating type a. Give the expected composition of an ascus, and the composition of an ascus from the reciprocal cross.

   **Answer** Female *poky* $ad^{+}$: 8 spores, 4 $ad^{+}$ : 4 $ad^{-}$, all *poky*; mating types also 4 A : 4 a. Reciprocal (female $poky^{+}$ $ad^{-}$): 4 $ad^{+}$ : 4 $ad^{-}$, all $poky^{+}$. The nuclear markers segregate 4:4 and the mitochondrial trait follows the female.

3. A woman has heteroplasmy $h_0 = 0.4$ for a pathogenic mtDNA mutation; assume neutral sampling with $N = 20$ and disease at $h \geq 0.6$. (a) What are the mean and standard deviation of her children's heteroplasmy? (b) What is the probability that a child is affected? (c) Her brother has the same heteroplasmy. What is the risk for his children?

   **Answer** (a) Mean $0.4$, variance $0.4 \times 0.6/20 = 0.012$, standard deviation $0.110$ (Formula 11.4.1 with $t = 1$). (b) $\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.4) \geq 12) = 0.057$ (Formula 11.5.1). (c) Essentially zero: mtDNA is not transmitted by fathers.

4. In the family of Question 3, the woman's maternal grandmother had $h_0 = 0.3$. Using the same model, compute the variance of heteroplasmy among the granddaughters of the grandmother (two generations), and the probability that a granddaughter exceeds $0.6$. Why may a family appear to have no affected members for several generations and then several affected ones?

   **Answer** $\operatorname{Var}(h_2) = 0.21[1 - 0.95^2] = 0.21 \times 0.0975 = 0.0205$. The probability of $h_2 \geq 0.6$ from the two-step transition matrix is $0.035$ (compared with $0.005$ for a daughter). The variance grows with each generation while the mean stays at $0.3$, so the tails reach the threshold only after some generations; in the same time many lineages lose the mutation (about $1.2\,\%$ after two and $3.7\,\%$ after three generations). Sudden appearance of affected individuals is expected under drift, without any new mutation.

5. The children of a woman with $h_0 = 0.35$ for a neutral mtDNA variant have heteroplasmies $0.12, 0.35, 0.48, 0.22, 0.61, 0.30$. Estimate the effective number of segregating units and comment on its precision.

   **Answer** Sample variance $s^2 = 0.0313$ (with $n - 1 = 5$ in the denominator); $N \approx h_0(1 - h_0)/s^2 = 0.2275/0.0313 = 7.3$. The relative **standard error** of a variance from six values is about $\sqrt{2/5} = 0.63$, so $N$ is uncertain by more than a factor of two; using the **sample mean** $0.347$ instead of $0.35$ hardly changes it. Many families or single-oocyte measurements are needed (Example 11.4.1).

6. A woman is a homoplasmic carrier of m.11778G>A (LHON). Assume penetrance $0.5$ in males and $0.1$ in females and equal sex ratio. (a) What is the probability that her next child develops LHON? (b) She has three sons: what is the probability that at least one is affected? (c) What is the risk for the children of an affected man?

   **Answer** (a) All children inherit the homoplasmic mutation; $\mathbb{P} = \frac12(0.5) + \frac12(0.1) = 0.30$. (b) $1 - 0.5^3 = 0.875$. (c) Practically zero, because mtDNA is maternally inherited; his daughters' children are also unaffected.

7. In *Lymnaea* (dextral $D$ dominant, maternal effect), a sinistral snail is self-fertilized and all its progeny are dextral. (a) What are the genotypes of this snail and of its mother? (b) What proportion of its progeny will produce sinistral broods when they are in turn self-fertilized?

   **Answer** (a) A sinistral snail had a $dd$ mother; its own genotype determines the coiling of its progeny, which are dextral, so it carries $D$: it is $Dd$ (it cannot be $DD$, since its mother was $dd$). (b) Its progeny are $DD : Dd : dd = 1 : 2 : 1$, all dextral; the $dd$ quarter will produce sinistral broods (Formula 11.6.1).

8. A cross $S(rf\,rf)$ × $N(Rf\,Rf)$ gives a fertile F1. (a) Give the cytoplasmic and nuclear genotype of the F1. (b) Give the fertility of the F2 from self-fertilization, for a sporophytic and for a gametophytic restorer. (c) What fraction of progeny are fertile when the F1 is used as the pollen parent onto $S(rf\,rf)$, in each case?

   **Answer** (a) $S(Rf\,rf)$: the cytoplasm comes from the female. (b) Sporophytic: $1 : 2 : 1$ genotypes, $3/4$ fertile plants, $1/4$ sterile. Gametophytic: only $Rf$ pollen functions, F2 are $S(Rf\,Rf) : S(Rf\,rf) = 1 : 1$, all set seed; the heterozygotes have $50\,\%$ aborted pollen. (c) Sporophytic: the F1 sheds both $Rf$ and $rf$ pollen, progeny $S(Rf\,rf) : S(rf\,rf) = 1 : 1$, half fertile. Gametophytic: only $Rf$ pollen fertilizes, all progeny $S(Rf\,rf)$, all fertile (with half-aborted pollen) (Example 11.6.1).

9. Why does the three-line system need a maintainer line, and why does the two-line system not need one?

   **Answer** The A line is male sterile and cannot be selfed; to obtain more A-line seed it must be pollinated by a fertile line that does not restore fertility and does not change its cytoplasm: $N(rf\,rf)$ pollen gives $S(rf\,rf)$ seed. In the two-line system sterility is nuclear and conditional (photoperiod- or temperature-sensitive): the same line is sterile under one environment, where it is used as the female for hybrid seed, and fertile under another, where it is selfed. The restorer requirement also disappears, because any normal pollen parent gives a fertile hybrid.

10. A *Wolbachia* strain causes CI with $s_{\mathrm{h}} = 0.6$ and reduces fecundity by $s_{\mathrm{f}} = 0.15$, with perfect transmission. (a) What is the invasion threshold? (b) A wild population has 10 000 adult females; how many infected females must be released (with matching males) to exceed the threshold by 5 percentage points in the first generation? (c) What happens if a host mutation reduces $s_{\mathrm{h}}$ to $0.1$?

    **Answer** (a) $\hat p = s_{\mathrm{f}}/s_{\mathrm{h}} = 0.25$ (Formula 11.6.2). (b) Target $p = 0.30$: $f/(f + 10\,000) = 0.30$, so $f = 0.3 \times 10\,000/0.7 \approx 4300$ infected females. (c) Then $s_{\mathrm{f}} > s_{\mathrm{h}}$: the interior equilibrium $\hat p = 1.5$ lies outside $[0, 1]$ and $p' < p$ for all $p < 1$, so the infection declines from any frequency below 1, since $p' > p$ requires $s_{\mathrm{h}}p > s_{\mathrm{f}}$; the strain cannot invade or persist.

11. Yeast crosses: a haploid grande strain is crossed with three different petite strains. Cross 1 gives only grande diploids, which sporulate to give 4 grande : 0 petite tetrads; cross 2 gives grande diploids and 2 grande : 2 petite tetrads; cross 3 gives $70\,\%$ petite diploids. Classify the petites and explain.

    **Answer** Cross 1: neutral petite ($\rho^{0}$): it lacks mtDNA, the grande parent supplies mtDNA to the zygote, and all spores inherit it. Cross 2: segregational petite: a nuclear recessive mutation, masked in the diploid and segregating 2:2 in meiosis. Cross 3: suppressive petite ($\rho^{-}$): its deleted mtDNA replicates faster than wild-type mtDNA in the heteroplasmic zygote and takes over in most diploid lineages (Definition 11.3.2).

12. After pronuclear transfer, a girl carries $2\,\%$ maternal (mutant) mtDNA. (a) Under neutral sampling with $N = 20$ per generation, what fraction of her oocytes carry any maternal mtDNA, and how likely is a child above $60\,\%$? (b) Why is the risk nevertheless monitored?

    **Answer** (a) $\mathbb{P}(\text{at least one of 20 units mutant}) = 1 - 0.98^{20} = 0.33$; $\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.02) \geq 12) < 10^{-15}$. Most oocytes with the mutation carry 1/20 to 2/20, and a child above threshold would need several generations of drift. (b) Because the neutral model ignores selection: carried-over mtDNA can have a replicative advantage over the donor mtDNA (as seen in some embryonic stem **cell lines** and in mismatched nuclear–mitochondrial combinations), which can raise its level rapidly; the heteroplasmy of children born after the procedure is therefore followed, and haplotype matching of donor and mother has been proposed.

13. (Open question) *Wolbachia* that cause CI spread even though they reduce host fecundity. Is this an adaptation of the host, of the bacterium, or neither? What does the model of Formula 11.6.2 imply about the evolution of CI strength and of fecundity cost within an infected population?

    **Answer** Outline: CI is a trait of the bacterium that increases its own transmission through the female line (a selfish genetic element, 7.4.17); the host gains nothing from it. Once the infection is near fixation, almost all females are infected, CI no longer kills embryos, and selection on the bacterium acts mainly on its effect on female fecundity and transmission ($s_{\mathrm{f}}$, $\mu$): variants that reduce the cost or increase transmission are favored, while CI strength is maintained only by weak selection (it matters only against uninfected immigrants) and may decay. Hosts are selected for suppressors of CI and of the fecundity cost. Field data from *Drosophila simulans* in California showed an infection spreading across the state in a few years and subsequent evolution toward a fecundity benefit.

14. A trait of line A persists after line A, used as the mother, has been crossed to males of line B in every generation. (a) How many crosses to B are needed before the expected fraction of B's nuclear genome exceeds 99 %? (b) After 6 crosses, what fraction of A's nuclear genome is expected to remain? (c) Why is persistence of the trait evidence for a **cytoplasmic determinant**, but not for an organelle gene?

    **Answer** (a) $1 - 2^{-g} \geq 0.99$ requires $2^{-g} \leq 0.01$, so $g \geq 7$ ($2^{-7} = 0.0078$) (Formula 11.1.1). (b) $2^{-6} = 1.6\,\%$. (c) Only the cytoplasm of A has been transmitted unchanged; it may contain organelle genomes or endosymbionts (or, rarely, a stable epigenetic state), so curing with antibiotics or heat and sequencing of organelle DNA are still needed (Algorithm 11.1.1).

15. In maize, a striped $ij\,ij$ plant is used (a) as the pollen parent and (b) as the seed parent in crosses with a green $Ij\,Ij$ plant. Give the $\mathrm{F}_1$ and $\mathrm{F}_2$ in each case. (c) Striped $\mathrm{F}_1$ plants from cross (b) are backcrossed as females to $Ij\,Ij$ males for five generations. What is expected, and what does it show?

    **Answer** (a) $\mathrm{F}_1$ all green $Ij\,ij$ (normal plastids from the green mother); $\mathrm{F}_2$ $3$ green : $1$ $ij\,ij$, which become striped or white as they generate defective plastids. (b) The $\mathrm{F}_1$ are all $Ij\,ij$, but their eggs carried plastids from the striped mother, some normal and some defective: green, striped and white plants in no fixed ratio, according to the plastids each egg received. (c) Green, striped and white progeny continue to appear in variable proportions although *ij* has been eliminated from the nucleus (after five generations the $Ij\,Ij$ genotype predominates): defective plastids, once produced by the nuclear gene, are inherited maternally as organelles and do not revert.

16. A *Paramecium* killer of genotype $KK$ with kappa conjugates with a sensitive $kk$ cell. (a) Give the genotypes and phenotypes of the exconjugants after a short and after a long conjugation. (b) Each exconjugant of a short conjugation undergoes autogamy. What fraction of all resulting clones are killers? (c) A killer clone is $Kk$; what fraction of its autogamous progeny are killers, and why do the others lose kappa?

    **Answer** (a) Short: both $Kk$; the former killer keeps its kappa (killer), the former sensitive has none (sensitive). Long: both $Kk$ with kappa, both killers. (b) Autogamy of the $Kk$ killer gives $\frac12 KK$ + kappa (killer) and $\frac12 kk$ (kappa lost, sensitive); the sensitive exconjugant gives only sensitive clones. Over both exconjugants, $\frac12 \times \frac12 = \frac14$ of clones are killers. (c) $\frac12$; the $kk$ clones cannot maintain kappa, which needs the product of $K$, and are diluted to kappa-free cells in a few fissions.

17. A male-sterile line $S(rf\,rf)$ is pollinated by three fertile plants. Plant X gives only sterile progeny, plant Y only fertile progeny, and plant Z equal numbers of fertile and sterile progeny (sporophytic restorer). (a) What can be said about the genotypes of X, Y and Z? (b) How would you decide whether Y has $N$ or $S$ cytoplasm?

    **Answer** (a) X is $N(rf\,rf)$, a maintainer (an $S(rf\,rf)$ plant would itself be sterile). Y is $Rf\,Rf$, a restorer, with $N$ or $S$ cytoplasm. Z is $Rf\,rf$, with either cytoplasm. (b) Use Y as the female, since cytoplasm is transmitted only through the egg: cross Y × $N(rf\,rf)$ and self the $Rf\,rf$ $\mathrm{F}_1$. If Y has $S$ cytoplasm, $\frac14$ of the $\mathrm{F}_2$ are $S(rf\,rf)$ and sterile; if $N$, all $\mathrm{F}_2$ are fertile.

18. Suppose that fertility of a CMS cytoplasm is restored sporophytically by dominant alleles at two unlinked loci, $Rf3$ and $Rf4$. Give the proportions of fertile plants in the $\mathrm{F}_2$ of $S(rf3\,rf3\,rf4\,rf4) \times N(Rf3\,Rf3\,Rf4\,Rf4)$, and in the testcross of the $\mathrm{F}_1$ as pollen parent onto the sterile line, (a) if one dominant allele at either locus restores fertility, (b) if dominant alleles at both loci are needed.

    **Answer** The $\mathrm{F}_1$ is $S(Rf3\,rf3\,Rf4\,rf4)$, fertile in both models. (a) Fertile unless $rf3\,rf3\,rf4\,rf4$: $\mathrm{F}_2$ $15$ fertile : $1$ sterile; testcross $3 : 1$. (b) Fertile only if $Rf3\_\,Rf4\_$: $\mathrm{F}_2$ $9 : 7$; testcross $1 : 3$. These are the duplicate-gene and complementary-gene ratios of [7.4.4](/7.4.4), with the cytoplasm fixed as $S$ in every plant.

19. In the flour moth, an $a^{+}a$ female is mated with an $aa$ male, and in the reciprocal cross an $aa$ female with an $a^{+}a$ male. Give the phenotypes of larvae and adults in each cross. Why is this called a transient maternal effect?

    **Answer** $a^{+}a$ female × $aa$ male: all larvae pigmented (kynurenine supplied in every egg), adults $\frac12$ brown-eyed $a^{+}a$ and $\frac12$ red-eyed $aa$. $aa$ female × $a^{+}a$ male: larvae $\frac12$ pigmented and $\frac12$ unpigmented, adults $\frac12$ brown and $\frac12$ red. The maternal product affects only the early stages of $aa$ offspring and is used up or diluted by growth, after which the offspring's own genotype determines the phenotype; in *Lymnaea* the maternal effect is persistent because coiling, set at the third cleavage, cannot change later.

20. In *Lymnaea* (dextral $D$ dominant, maternal effect), a dextral $Dd$ snail is used as the female and fertilized by a $dd$ male. (a) What are the phenotypes and genotypes of the progeny? (b) Each progeny snail is self-fertilized. What fraction of the broods are sinistral? (c) The reciprocal cross is made ($dd$ female × $Dd$ male); answer (a) and (b) again.

    **Answer** (a) All dextral (mother $Dd$); genotypes $\frac12 Dd$, $\frac12 dd$. (b) $Dd$ snails produce dextral broods and $dd$ snails sinistral broods: $\frac12$ sinistral. (c) All progeny sinistral (mother $dd$), with genotypes $\frac12 Dd$, $\frac12 dd$; on selfing, again $\frac12$ of broods dextral ($Dd$ mothers) and $\frac12$ sinistral (Formula 11.6.1). Reciprocal crosses differ in the progeny phenotype but not in the next generation's broods.

21. $\ce{CO2}$-sensitive *Drosophila* males are crossed with resistant females, and $3\,\%$ of the progeny are sensitive; the reciprocal cross gives almost $100\,\%$ sensitive progeny. (a) Why do these results exclude a dominant nuclear allele (autosomal or X-linked) for sensitivity? (b) How could you show that the factor is infectious?

    **Answer** (a) A dominant nuclear allele carried by the sensitive parent would reach at least half of the progeny in both reciprocal crosses alike (if autosomal), or all daughters of a sensitive father (if X-linked). The asymmetric $3\,\%$ against $100\,\%$ matches a cytoplasmic factor transmitted efficiently through eggs and rarely through sperm. (b) Inject extracts of sensitive flies into resistant flies: they become sensitive, and the agent (sigma virus) can be passaged serially from fly to fly, which no nuclear gene can do.

22. A recessive genic male-sterile mutant $ms\,ms$ is maintained by pollinating it with fertile $Ms\,ms$ sibling plants. (a) What fraction of the progeny are sterile, and what must a seed producer do in each generation? (b) How does a photoperiod-sensitive genic male sterility avoid this problem?

    **Answer** (a) $ms\,ms \times Ms\,ms$ gives $\frac12$ sterile $ms\,ms$ and $\frac12$ fertile $Ms\,ms$; if the line is used as the female for hybrid seed, the fertile half must be identified and removed before flowering, or it will self and contaminate the hybrid seed. (b) Sterility is conditional: under long days the line is male sterile and is used as the female in the hybrid plot, under short days it is fertile and is propagated by selfing, so all of its progeny are $ms\,ms$ and no maintainer or roguing is needed (two-line system).
