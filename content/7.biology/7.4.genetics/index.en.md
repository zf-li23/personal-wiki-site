Genetics is the science of heredity and variation, and its method is inference from counting. A **gene** cannot be seen in a pea plant or a fruit fly, but the offspring of a controlled cross can be sorted and counted, and from their frequencies one infers hidden genotypes, the number of genes involved, how their **alleles** interact and where the genes lie on **chromosomes**. The core questions of the subject follow from this method. How are alleles transmitted from parents to offspring, and why does one number, the probability $1/2$ that a heterozygote passes on each allele, turn the prediction of offspring into a calculation? How does the mapping from **genotype** to **phenotype** reshape the expected ratios? How can the frequency of recombinant offspring measure the distance between two genes? Where do new alleles come from, and what happens to transmission when chromosomes break, change in number, carry elements that move or cheat, or are marked epigenetically? And how do the same rules, applied to whole populations and to traits that depend on many genes, explain allele frequencies, the resemblance between relatives and the response to selection? The course gives equal weight to the two sides of the subject: the phenomena and their mechanisms, and the calculations, from modified Mendelian ratios and map distances to pedigree risks, Hardy–Weinberg frequencies and heritabilities.

The chapters follow the order in which genetic analysis extends its reach. After the logic of inference from crosses and the **model organisms** that make it practical, Mendel's two laws are derived together with the probability toolkit that every later chapter uses, and then complicated by the interactions between alleles of one gene and between different genes, whose modified ratios are read backward as clues to how gene products work together. The **chromosome theory of inheritance** gives the laws a physical basis in **meiosis**, and its first test cases are sex determination and sex linkage; human **pedigrees** apply the same rules to families that cannot be designed, with risks computed as conditional probabilities. **Crossing over** then turns the exceptions to independent assortment into a measuring instrument, first in the counts of linkage mapping, then in the tetrads of fungi that show single meioses, and finally in the gene transfers of bacteria and phages; organellar inheritance and maternal effects follow as the cases in which the parents do not contribute equally. With the transmission rules established, the course turns to the material they act on: **mutation**, its kinds, rates and randomness; the dissection of genes by complementation and fine-structure mapping; whole genomes and the variation between them, used as markers to find disease genes; changes in the structure and number of chromosomes; transposable and selfish elements that move or bias their own transmission; and epigenetic states that are inherited without a change of sequence. Two chapters on the engineering of genomes and the design of screens, and two on the genetics of development and of cancer, show the toolkit at work. The last part moves from individuals to populations: the Hardy–Weinberg law as the null model, the forces of mutation, migration, selection and drift, the variance components of quantitative genetics, and the mapping of complex traits by QTL and genome-wide association. The course summary collects the calculation methods and the decision procedure for modes of inheritance, places the course between cell and developmental biology, and asks how far genetics is as simple and as finished as it is often said to be.

The cellular machinery behind transmission, the spindle, the synaptonemal complex and cohesin, the karyotype and X inactivation, comes from [cell biology](/7.3), and the molecular nature of genes, their expression, repair and recombination from [molecular biology](/7.2); a few explanations of dominance draw on the pathways and metabolic control of [biochemistry](/7.1). The mathematics comes from statistics: conditional probability, the binomial and multinomial distributions and the central limit theorem of [probability](/3.1), likelihood, χ² tests and multiple testing from [statistical inference](/3.2), and regression from [linear regression](/3.3). In turn, the mutants and screens of this course are the starting point of [developmental biology](/7.5), which explains the phenotypes that the genetic analysis of development only classifies; the coalescent, molecular tests of selection and speciation continue the population genetics in [evolutionary biology](/7.10); the physiology and ecology of bacterial gene transfer belong to [microbiology](/7.11); the statistical and computational treatment of genomic data is taken further in [biostatistics](/7.7) and [bioinformatics](/7.8), and genome-wide genetic-interaction maps in [systems biology](/7.9).

Olympiad genetics and university genetics stress different things. Olympiad problems are above all calculations, variants of segregation and independent assortment, recombination frequencies and map distances, and they require a feel for numerical ratios; only a part of the questions test concepts. The university course weighs the other way: calculation counts for less, and the variety of genetic phenomena and their mechanisms for more. These pages try to serve both, explaining each mechanism and giving the methods of calculation with worked examples. Of the Chinese textbooks, the *Genetics* of Zudong Liu and colleagues, now in its fourth edition, is very well suited to a first encounter with the subject, while the *Genetics* of Zhuohua Dai and Yafu Wang, in its third edition, covers considerably more ground and is worth reading in full for anyone who wants to go further. Among the English books I regard Hartwell's *Genetics: From Genes to Genomes* and Snustad and Simmons' *Principles of Genetics* as the classics; both are available in Chinese translation.

Before these pages were written I summed up my view of genetics in three claims that I knew to be provocative: that, compared with the many intricate and fragmented facts of **biochemistry**, molecular biology and **cell biology**, genetics needs only a few simple mathematical rules, at roughly the level of middle-school mathematics; that classical genetics has clearly stopped developing, its living parts, molecular and population genetics, being pursued mainly within molecular and evolutionary biology; and that the structure of the discipline will therefore not change much, which makes genetics the easiest of the biology courses. Looking back over the course ([26.5](/7.4.26#sec-26.5)), I would keep the core of each claim and move its boundary. The rules of transmission are indeed elementary, and their economy is real; but inferring the rules from data and following them through populations requires **likelihood**, **Bayesian inference**, Poisson processes, **Markov chains** and variance components, which lie well beyond middle school. The Mendel–Morgan framework has not been overturned and needs no new law, yet it has not stopped developing either: association studies at biobank scale, polygenic prediction, somatic mosaicism and genome-wide CRISPR screens are its classical concepts applied at the scale of whole genomes. What I still hold is that transmission genetics, the first half of the course, is the most economical of the biological subjects, with a few principles explaining a very large range of phenomena, while its statistical half is open and changing quickly. I also continue to see cell biology, genetics and development as one system that tells the story from the level of the cell to the level of the individual: the cell supplies the machinery that copies and partitions chromosomes, genetics follows the consequences of that machinery for transmission and variation, and [developmental biology](/7.5) asks how one genome builds an organism.
