Mendelian genetics treats an allele as a fixed sequence: whichever parent transmitted it, whatever cell it sits in, the same allele has the same effect. This chapter is about the exceptions in which the same DNA sequence exists in two heritable states. A gene can be silent on the chromosome that came from the mother and active on the one from the father; a gene moved next to heterochromatin can be off in some cells and on in their neighbours, each choice copied through many divisions; a state of methylation acquired by one generation can, in some organisms, reappear in the next. The molecular machinery behind these states (histone modification, **DNA methylation**, Polycomb repression and the read–write feedback that stores a chromatin state) was described in [7.2.15](/7.2.15), and the cytology of **heterochromatin** and X inactivation in [7.3.17](/7.3.17). Here we ask the geneticist's questions: how an epigenetic state reveals itself in crosses and pedigrees, how its stability through cell divisions and generations can be measured and modeled, and what it means for risk in human families. The chapter extends the parent-of-origin effects that broke the rules of [7.4.11](/7.4.11) (maternal and cytoplasmic inheritance) to nuclear genes, uses the pedigree calculus of [7.4.7](/7.4.7) and the uniparental disomy of [7.4.16](/7.4.16), and prepares the genetic screens of [7.4.19](/7.4.19) and [7.4.20](/7.4.20), since modifier screens for variegation are among the most successful examples of the method. Development uses these mechanisms everywhere ([7.5](/7.5)).

## 18.1. What Makes Inheritance Epigenetic

**Epigenetics** ([7.2.15](/7.2.15#def-15.8.1)) studies heritable changes of gene function that are not changes of DNA sequence. For a geneticist, three properties distinguish an epigenetic state from a mutation:

1. Heritability through divisions. The state is copied when the cell divides (mitotic inheritance) and in some cases passes through meiosis to the next generation (meiotic or germline inheritance). A transient response to the environment, such as induction of an enzyme by its substrate, is not epigenetic.
2. No change in sequence. Sequencing the gene in its two states shows the same bases, or the two states segregate in crosses as if they were alleles of one locus that are nevertheless identical in sequence.
3. Reversibility at an appreciable rate. Epigenetic states switch far more often than DNA mutates, from about $10^{-4}$ per site per generation for single CpG sites in plants to nearly every generation for imprints, which are reset in each germ line. Reversion at high frequency, often in response to the sex of the parent, development or environment, is the strongest genetic sign that a heritable variant is not a sequence change.

| Phenomenon | What is inherited | Through mitosis | Through meiosis | Reset |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Genomic imprinting (18.2) | allele-specific silencing according to the sex of the parent | yes | yes, but reset according to the sex of the transmitting parent | every generation, in the germ line |
| X inactivation ([7.4.6](/7.4.6#sec-6.5)) | silencing of one X in female mammals | yes (clonal patches) | no | in the germ line |
| Position-effect variegation (18.4) | silencing of a gene near heterochromatin, chosen cell by cell | yes (clonal patches) | no (rechosen in each embryo) | each generation |
| Polycomb memory (18.5) | repression of developmental genes where they were off | yes | mostly no | each generation |
| Epialleles and paramutation (18.6) | methylation state of a locus | yes | yes, often for many generations | rarely; spontaneous reversion |

The table orders the phenomena by how far the state travels. Imprints are the most regular: they always pass through meiosis but are always reset, so the rules of their inheritance are as precise as Mendel's. The other states are transmitted less predictably, and their inheritance has to be described by switching rates rather than ratios (18.7).

## 18.2. Genomic Imprinting

### 18.2.1. The Evidence

Mammals cannot reproduce by parthenogenesis, although a mouse egg can be activated to divide without sperm. In 1984 the groups of Davor Solter (McGrath and Solter) and Azim Surani (Surani, Barton and Norris) asked why, by pronuclear transplantation: they removed one pronucleus from a fertilized mouse egg and replaced it with a pronucleus of the other sex or of the same sex. Eggs with one maternal and one paternal pronucleus developed normally. Gynogenetic eggs, with two maternal pronuclei, formed small embryos with poorly developed extraembryonic tissues (placenta and yolk sac); androgenetic eggs, with two paternal pronuclei, formed placental tissue but little embryo. Both died. The two parental genomes are therefore not equivalent, although they carry the same genes: each is marked, in the germ line of its parent, in a way that the other cannot replace. Human pathology shows the same: a complete hydatidiform mole, a proliferation of placental trophoblast without an embryo, is diploid with both genomes from the father (usually one sperm whose genome has doubled), whereas an ovarian teratoma, a disorganized tumor of embryonic tissues, is derived from a parthenogenetically activated oocyte with only maternal genomes.

Individual genes with parent-specific expression were found in the following years. DeChiara, Robertson and Efstratiadis (1991) made mice heterozygous for a targeted null allele of *Igf2*, which encodes insulin-like growth factor 2, a fetal growth factor. Heterozygotes that had inherited the null allele from their father were about 60 % of normal size, as small as homozygotes; heterozygotes that had inherited it from their mother were normal. The maternal copy of *Igf2* is silent, so the phenotype depends only on the paternal allele. In the same year the receptor that degrades IGF2, *Igf2r*, was found to be expressed only from the maternal allele, and the noncoding *H19*, next to *Igf2*, also from the maternal allele.

**Definition 18.2.1 (Genomic Imprinting)** **Genomic imprinting** is the epigenetic marking of a gene, in the germ line of a parent, according to the sex of that parent, so that in the offspring the gene is expressed from only one parental allele: a maternally expressed gene is silent on the paternally inherited chromosome, and a paternally expressed gene is silent on the maternally inherited chromosome. A phenotype that depends on which parent transmitted an allele is a **parent-of-origin effect**.

The terms "maternally imprinted" and "paternally imprinted" are used in opposite senses in the literature (some authors mean the silenced allele, others the marked one, which need not be the same), so this chapter always says which allele is expressed: *Igf2* is paternally expressed, *H19* and *Igf2r* are maternally expressed. In mice and humans about 150–200 genes are imprinted, out of about 20 000; about four-fifths of them lie in a dozen or so clusters of 0.1–4 Mb, each containing several imprinted genes, some maternally and some paternally expressed, controlled together.

Imprinting is found in placental mammals and marsupials and, independently, in the endosperm of flowering plants, in both cases where offspring are nourished by the mother after fertilization. It is not found in birds, egg-laying mammals or fish, and does not occur in most model invertebrates. This distribution is one of the facts that any evolutionary explanation must account for (18.2.5).

### 18.2.2. The Pedigree Signature

An imprinted gene is transmitted like any autosomal gene: each parent passes one of its two alleles to each child with probability $1/2$. What changes is the expression of the allele in the child, which depends only on the sex of the transmitting parent, not on where the allele was before. The imprint is erased and rewritten in each generation (18.2.4), so a silent allele inherited from a mother becomes an active allele in the sperm of her son.

**Proposition 18.2.1 (Transmission of a Mutation in an Imprinted Gene)** Let a loss-of-function mutation $m$ in a paternally expressed gene be fully penetrant whenever it is the expressed allele. Then a child of a heterozygous father is affected with probability $1/2$, and a child of a heterozygous mother is affected with probability $0$ but is a carrier with probability $1/2$; the risk to a grandchild through an unaffected carrier son is $1/2$ for each of his children, and through an unaffected carrier daughter $0$. For a maternally expressed gene, exchange "father" and "mother".

**Proof** Each heterozygous parent transmits $m$ with probability $1/2$ (law of segregation). In the child the paternally inherited allele is the only expressed one; the child is affected exactly when the paternal allele is $m$, which has probability $1/2$ if the father is the carrier and $0$ if only the mother is. A carrier child of either sex carries $m$ on one chromosome; in the germ line the imprint is reset according to the child's sex, so a son passes $m$ as a paternal (expressed) allele and a daughter as a maternal (silent) allele, each with probability $1/2$. $\square$

**Algorithm 18.2.1 (Recognizing Imprinting in a Pedigree)**

1. Check that the trait follows autosomal transmission: both sexes are affected and carriers transmit to children of both sexes.
2. Group the affected children by the sex of the parent through whom the allele came (the parent who is a carrier or descends from the carrier line).
3. If affected children always received the allele from their father, while carrier mothers have only unaffected children who themselves can transmit through their sons, suspect a paternally expressed gene; the converse pattern suggests a maternally expressed gene.
4. Exclude the alternatives that also give sex-of-parent asymmetries: mitochondrial and other maternal inheritance (transmission only from mothers, to all children, [7.4.11](/7.4.11)); maternal effect (the child's phenotype follows the mother's genotype, not its own); X linkage (sex of the child matters, father-to-son transmission absent, [7.4.6](/7.4.6)); anticipation of a repeat expansion that is larger through one sex ([7.4.12](/7.4.12)).
5. Confirm with molecular tests: allele-specific expression of a heterozygous polymorphism (only one allele in the transcript), or a parent-specific methylation pattern at the gene's control region.

The mutation of the grandmother I-2 is silent in her and in her children, whatever their sex, because it was inherited from or passed through a female; it is expressed in the children of her carrier son II-2 (III-1, III-3), but not in those of her carrier daughter II-4, whose children III-5 and III-6 are unaffected carriers. B: a maternally expressed gene: the reverse, affected children arise only through carrier mothers (II-2's children are carriers, II-4's are affected). The pattern is easily mistaken for incomplete **penetrance** of a dominant trait, with "skipped" generations. The difference is that the skipping is not random: it follows the sex of the transmitting parent with probability one.

**Example 18.2.1** (a) A woman's father has a disorder caused by a mutation in a paternally expressed gene; she is unaffected. Any allele she received from her father is expressed in her, so with full penetrance an unaffected daughter cannot have received the mutation: her carrier risk is $0$, and so is the risk to her children. (b) Suppose instead that the gene is maternally expressed and her father is affected (he received the mutation from his mother). An allele from the father is silent in his daughter, so her being unaffected gives no information: she is a carrier with probability $1/2$, and each of her children is affected with probability $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$ before she is tested, $\tfrac12$ if she is shown to be a carrier. (c) Her brother in case (b) is also a carrier with probability $1/2$, but his children are never affected; only his daughters' children can be.

### 18.2.3. Imprinting Control Regions

Within an imprinted cluster, the parental origin is read at a short DNA element.

**Definition 18.2.2 (Imprinting Control Region)** An **imprinting control region** (ICR) is a DNA element, typically a few kilobases rich in CpG dinucleotides, that is methylated in the germ line of one sex only, keeps this differential methylation on the two parental chromosomes in all somatic cells, and is required in *cis* for the imprinted expression of the genes of its cluster.

Deleting an ICR abolishes imprinting of the whole cluster on the chromosome that carries the deletion, which is how ICRs were identified; the effect depends on which parent transmits the deletion, a parent-of-origin effect of the second order. Of the roughly two dozen known germline ICRs of the mouse, most are methylated in the oocyte and only three (at *H19*–*Igf2*, *Dlk1*–*Dio3* and *Rasgrf1*) in sperm. The ICR controls its neighbours by two mechanisms.

The first mechanism is an insulator, at *H19* and *Igf2*. *Igf2* and *H19* lie about 90 kb apart on mouse chromosome 7 (human 11p15.5) and share enhancers downstream of *H19*. Between *Igf2* and *H19* lies the ICR (the H19 differentially methylated region). On the maternal chromosome the ICR is unmethylated and binds the zinc-finger protein CTCF, which forms an **insulator** ([7.2.14](/7.2.14#ex-14.7.1)): it organizes the chromatin into loops that confine the enhancers to *H19*, so *H19* is expressed and *Igf2* is not. On the paternal chromosome the ICR is methylated, CTCF cannot bind, methylation spreads to the *H19* promoter and silences it, and the enhancers activate *Igf2*. A single methylation difference thus switches two genes in opposite directions. Deleting the ICR from the maternal chromosome makes *Igf2* biallelic and produces overgrowth.

The second mechanism is a noncoding RNA, at *Igf2r* (with *Airn*) and *Kcnq1* (with *Kcnq1ot1*). In the *Igf2r* cluster the ICR lies in an intron of *Igf2r* and contains the promoter of *Airn*, a **long noncoding RNA** transcribed in the antisense direction. On the maternal chromosome the ICR is methylated, *Airn* is silent and *Igf2r* is expressed. On the paternal chromosome *Airn* is transcribed, and its transcription through the *Igf2r* promoter, together with the RNA, which recruits silencing complexes to the chromatin around it, silences *Igf2r* and two more distant genes in *cis*. The *Kcnq1* cluster works in the same way: the paternally expressed lncRNA *Kcnq1ot1* silences about ten genes over 1 Mb, among them *Cdkn1c*, which encodes the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p57. Truncating *Airn* or *Kcnq1ot1* relieves the silencing of the whole cluster, which is how the RNAs were shown to be required.

### 18.2.4. The Life Cycle of an Imprint

An imprint must survive every somatic division and yet be reversible in the germ line, since a man transmits his mother's chromosomes as paternal ones. The cycle has three phases.

1. Erasure. In primordial germ cells, about embryonic days 10.5–12.5 in the mouse, genome-wide demethylation (passive, by replication without maintenance, and active, by TET enzymes, [7.2.15](/7.2.15#sec-15.7.2)) erases the imprints of both parental chromosomes.
2. Establishment. De novo methyltransferases (DNMT3A with its partner DNMT3L) methylate the ICRs according to the sex of the germ line: in male prospermatogonia before birth, in growing oocytes after birth. Mice lacking DNMT3L in the oocyte produce embryos without maternal imprints, which die at mid-gestation with the imprinted genes of every cluster expressed as if both chromosomes were paternal (Bourc'his and colleagues 2001).
3. Maintenance. After fertilization most of the genome is demethylated again (the paternal genome actively, the maternal passively), but ICRs are protected: the zinc-finger protein ZFP57 binds their methylated hexamer TGCCGC and recruits KAP1 (TRIM28) and maintenance methylation, so the parental marks pass intact into all cells of the embryo. Thereafter DNMT1 copies them at each replication.

The cycle explains Proposition 18.2.1. It also predicts that defects in any phase produce imprinting disorders: failure of erasure or establishment in a parent's germ line (an imprinting defect affecting one child, or recurrent if it is caused by an ICR deletion), and failure of maintenance in the embryo (often mosaic, since it occurs after the first divisions). Maternal-effect mutations in the genes that protect imprints, such as *ZFP57* and the subcortical maternal complex genes *NLRP2*, *NLRP7* and *KHDC3L*, cause multilocus imprinting disturbances and recurrent hydatidiform moles in the children of homozygous women: the woman's genotype determines the imprints of her eggs.

### 18.2.5. Why Imprinting?

Most imprinted genes affect growth of the fetus and placenta, postnatal feeding, or behavior, and their parental direction is not random: paternally expressed genes (*Igf2*, *Peg1*, *Peg3*) tend to promote growth and maternally expressed genes (*Igf2r*, *H19*, *Cdkn1c*, *Grb10*) to restrain it. The kinship (parental conflict) theory of David Haig explains this pattern. In a species where a mother can have offspring by several fathers, a gene expressed from the paternal chromosome in a fetus gains if it extracts more maternal resources for this fetus, at the expense of half-siblings with whom it shares no paternal genes; a gene expressed from the maternal chromosome is equally related to all the mother's offspring and gains by moderating extraction. Imprinting evolves where the offspring draw on maternal resources after fertilization (placenta, endosperm), which matches the taxonomic distribution. The *Igf2*–*Igf2r* pair is the textbook case: the paternally expressed growth factor and the maternally expressed receptor that degrades it act in opposite directions on fetal size, and knockout of *Igf2r* (inherited from the mother) gives mice about 30 % larger than normal. The evolutionary dynamics of such conflicts are treated in [7.10](/7.10).

## 18.3. Imprinting Disorders

### 18.3.1. Prader–Willi and Angelman Syndromes

Two very different disorders map to the same region of human chromosome 15.

**Definition 18.3.1 (Prader–Willi and Angelman Syndromes)** **Prader–Willi syndrome** (PWS) is a disorder caused by loss of expression of the paternally expressed genes of 15q11–q13 (notably the snoRNA cluster *SNORD116*), with neonatal hypotonia and poor feeding followed from early childhood by insatiable appetite and obesity, short stature, hypogonadism and mild to moderate intellectual disability. **Angelman syndrome** (AS) is a disorder caused by loss of function of the maternally expressed gene *UBE3A* in 15q11–q13, with severe intellectual disability, absent speech, ataxia, seizures and a happy demeanor with frequent laughter.

Each affects about 1 in 15 000 births. Both are usually caused by the same deletion, of about 5–6 Mb between clusters of low-copy repeats at breakpoints BP1 or BP2 and BP3, which recombine unequally in meiosis ([7.4.15](/7.4.15)); the difference is the parent from whom the deleted chromosome came. In neurons, *UBE3A* is expressed only from the maternal chromosome because the paternal copy is silenced by a long antisense transcript, part of the paternally expressed *SNHG14* transcription unit; most other tissues express both copies. A child who lacks the paternal 15q11–q13 has no expression of *SNORD116* and the other paternally expressed genes and has PWS; a child who lacks the maternal region has no neuronal *UBE3A* and has AS.

Four kinds of genetic lesion remove the expressed copies:

| Mechanism | PWS (paternal genes missing) | AS (maternal *UBE3A* missing) | Recurrence risk for sibs |
|---|---|---|---|
| deletion of 15q11–q13 | paternal deletion, about 65–75 % | maternal deletion, about 70 % | < 1 % (de novo); higher if a parent carries a balanced rearrangement of 15q |
| uniparental disomy 15 | maternal UPD, about 20–30 % | paternal UPD, about 2–7 % | < 1 % (unless a parent carries a Robertsonian translocation involving 15) |
| imprinting defect | maternal pattern on both chromosomes, about 1–3 % | paternal pattern on both, about 3 % | up to 50 % if caused by a deletion of the imprinting center inherited from the parent of the relevant sex; < 1 % otherwise |
| mutation of a single gene | rare (microdeletions of *SNORD116*) | *UBE3A* mutation, about 10 % | 50 % if the mother carries the mutation |
| unknown | | about 10 % | |

The proportions are those reported in large diagnostic series and vary somewhat between them. Uniparental disomy is the most instructive mechanism: a child with two maternal chromosomes 15 and none from the father has two normal copies of every gene, no sequence change at all, and PWS, because both copies carry the maternal imprint. Most cases arise by trisomy rescue ([7.4.16](/7.4.16)): a maternal meiosis I nondisjunction produces a disomic egg, the trisomic zygote loses one chromosome 15 in an early mitosis, and if the lost chromosome is the paternal one (probability $1/3$ for a random loss), the embryo has maternal UPD. This is why maternal UPD 15, like trisomy, increases with maternal age, and why placental mosaicism for trisomy 15 found at prenatal diagnosis is a reason to test the fetus for UPD.

**Algorithm 18.3.1 (Diagnosis and Recurrence Risk in PWS or AS)**

1. Test methylation at the *SNRPN* promoter in the imprinting center (methylation-specific PCR or MLPA): a normal person has one methylated (maternal) and one unmethylated (paternal) allele. Only methylated alleles: PWS; only unmethylated: AS. This single test detects deletion, UPD and imprinting defect, about 99 % of PWS and 80 % of AS.
2. Distinguish the mechanisms: copy number of 15q11–q13 (array or MLPA) for a deletion; polymorphic markers in the child and both parents for UPD; if neither, an imprinting defect, then search for a deletion of the imprinting center.
3. If the methylation is normal in a child with AS, sequence *UBE3A* and test the mother.
4. Assign the recurrence risk from the mechanism (table above), and the risk to the extended family: a *UBE3A* mutation or an imprinting-center deletion carried silently by a parent can also be carried by that parent's siblings, with risks for their children that follow Proposition 18.2.1.

**Example 18.3.1** A boy has Angelman syndrome with a normal methylation pattern; his mother carries the same *UBE3A* mutation and is unaffected, as is her mother. (a) The mother must have inherited the mutation from her father: in her it is a paternal allele, silent in neurons, whereas a maternally inherited copy would have made her affected. (b) Each further child of the mother is affected with probability $1/2$. (c) The mother's brother, if he is a carrier (probability $1/2$, since their father is a carrier), passes the mutation as a paternal allele: his children are carriers with probability $1/2$ but unaffected; his daughters' children are then at risk $1/2 \cdot 1/2 = 1/4$ each, or $1/2 \cdot 1/2 \cdot 1/2 = 1/8$ before his own carrier status is known.

### 18.3.2. Beckwith–Wiedemann and Silver–Russell Syndromes

The *H19*/*Igf2* and *Kcnq1* clusters at 11p15.5 (18.2.3) give a second pair of opposite disorders. Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome (BWS), about 1 in 10 000 births, is an overgrowth disorder with macroglossia, abdominal wall defects, hemihypertrophy and an increased risk of embryonal tumors (Wilms tumor, hepatoblastoma) in childhood. It is caused by an excess of paternal and a deficit of maternal function: loss of methylation at the *Kcnq1* ICR on the maternal chromosome (about half of cases, which silences maternal *CDKN1C*), paternal UPD of 11p15 (about 20 %, almost always mosaic, from a postzygotic mitotic recombination), gain of methylation at the maternal *H19* ICR (about 5–10 %, which turns on a second copy of *IGF2*), and *CDKN1C* mutations inherited from the mother (about 5 % of sporadic and 40 % of familial cases). Silver–Russell syndrome, with intrauterine and postnatal growth restriction, is the mirror image: loss of methylation at the paternal *H19* ICR (about 40 %) or maternal UPD of chromosome 7 (about 10 %). The two syndromes confirm the conflict pattern of 18.2.5 in humans: the paternal chromosome promotes growth, the maternal restrains it.

Assisted reproduction (in vitro fertilization and intracytoplasmic sperm injection) has been associated with a several-fold increase in the relative risk of BWS and other imprinting disorders, although the absolute risk stays below 1 in 1000; whether the cause is the culture of embryos during the reprogramming window or the subfertility of the parents is still debated.

## 18.4. Position-Effect Variegation

### 18.4.1. The Phenomenon in Genetic Terms

In 1930 Hermann Muller, screening X-irradiated *Drosophila*, found mutants with mottled eyes: patches of red and white facets. The best-studied, $In(1)w^{m4}$, is an inversion that moves the *white* gene, needed for red pigment, from euchromatin to a position next to the pericentric heterochromatin of the X chromosome. The *white* gene itself is intact: a crossover that restores the normal order restores the red eye. The mottling is a **position effect** of the kind defined in [7.2.15](/7.2.15#def-15.6.1), **position-effect variegation**: the spreading of heterochromatin across the breakpoint silences *white* in some cells and not in others, the choice is made early in development and inherited clonally, so that each patch descends from one cell.

Three genetic properties make PEV a quantitative phenotype, measurable by counting pigmented facets or by extracting the eye pigment:

1. Distance. Genes closer to the breakpoint are silenced more often; genes far into euchromatin escape. Silencing spreads from heterochromatin in a polar way.
2. Dosage of heterochromatin. Adding a Y chromosome (almost entirely heterochromatin) suppresses variegation (more red), and removing it (XO males) enhances it. The extra heterochromatin titrates proteins that are present in limiting amounts, so less is available to spread across the breakpoint.
3. Environment. Raising flies at higher temperature suppresses variegation; lower temperature enhances it.

The titration effect predicts that genes for heterochromatin proteins are dosage-sensitive: halving their dose should suppress variegation, increasing it should enhance.

### 18.4.2. Modifier Screens for Variegation

**Definition 18.4.1 (Suppressors and Enhancers of Variegation)** A **suppressor of variegation**, *Su(var)*, is a mutation that, heterozygous, reduces the silencing of a variegating gene (more cells express it); an **enhancer of variegation**, *E(var)*, increases silencing. The genes identified by such mutations are named after them.

Screens of mutagenized flies carrying $w^{m4}$ (Gunter Reuter, Pierre Spierer, Lori Wallrath and others, from the 1980s) recovered more than a hundred modifier loci, several hundred alleles, from a single phenotype. The screen design is that of the modifier screen of [7.4.20](/7.4.20): a sensitized genotype, here a gene poised between on and off, in which a twofold change in the dose of a component shifts the phenotype visibly. Most *Su(var)* loci encode heterochromatin components or the enzymes that place their marks: *Su(var)2-5* encodes HP1, the chromodomain protein that reads H3K9 methylation, and *Su(var)3-9* the H3K9 methyltransferase ([7.2.15](/7.2.15#sec-15.6.3)). Their dosage behavior shows that they are structural parts of silencing: *Su(var)2-5* heterozygotes suppress, extra copies of the gene enhance, and a triplo-enhancer, haplo-suppressor locus is the genetic definition of a limiting component of a complex. *E(var)* loci include genes for proteins of active chromatin, such as members of the trithorax group, which antagonize the spreading.

PEV has analogues in other organisms: telomere position effect in budding yeast ([7.2.15](/7.2.15#sec-15.6.1)), variegated expression of transgenes integrated near heterochromatin in mice (the reason why transgenic mouse lines must be compared by several independent insertions, [7.4.19](/7.4.19)), and the mosaic, clonally inherited silencing of repeated transgenes in plants. In humans, position effects of rearrangements explain some disorders in which the breakpoint lies outside the gene, up to a megabase away ([7.4.15](/7.4.15)).

## 18.5. Developmental Memory: Polycomb and X Inactivation

Two epigenetic systems keep developmental decisions through the many divisions of a body, and both were first understood genetically.

*Polycomb* (*Pc*) mutations in *Drosophila* were found because heterozygous males have extra sex combs on the second and third legs: the second and third thoracic segments partly take on the identity of the first. The *Hox* genes that give each segment its identity ([7.4.20](/7.4.20)) are switched on in the correct segments by the segmentation genes, whose products disappear a few hours later; thereafter the pattern is maintained by the Polycomb group (PcG), which keeps *Hox* genes off where they have not been activated, and by the trithorax group (trxG), which keeps them on where they have. Loss of PcG function derepresses *Hox* genes in anterior segments, which take posterior identities; loss of trxG function causes the opposite transformations. Many PcG mutations have a maternal effect, since the maternal product sustains the embryo through the stage at which the pattern is first locked in. The chromatin mechanism, H3K27 methylation by PRC2 and its read–write feedback, is described in [7.2.15](/7.2.15#sec-15.6.3); its genetic signature is that the *Hox* pattern is set by transient inputs and then inherited clonally.

**X-chromosome inactivation** in female mammals ([7.4.6](/7.4.6#sec-6.5), [7.3.17](/7.3.17#sec-17.1.3)) is the clearest epigenetic state at the level of whole chromosomes: which X is inactivated is chosen at random in each cell of the early embryo and inherited by all its descendants, so that heterozygous females are mosaics of clones. In mouse extraembryonic tissues, inactivation is imprinted (the paternal X is always inactive), a link between the two systems. X inactivation is reversed in the female germ line, before meiosis. Its consequences (calico cats, manifesting heterozygotes, the clonal origin of tumors, [7.4.21](/7.4.21)) are those of a stable epigenetic state that is chosen once per cell lineage.

## 18.6. Epigenetic Inheritance Across Generations

### 18.6.1. Intergenerational and Transgenerational Effects

A parent's environment can affect its offspring in many ways that involve no inheritance of epigenetic states: through the uterus, the milk, behavior or the cytoplasm of the egg (maternal effects, [7.4.11](/7.4.11#sec-11.6.1)). A pregnant female exposes not only herself but also her fetus and the germ cells inside it. A claim that an epigenetic state is inherited must therefore be tested in a generation that was never directly exposed.

**Definition 18.6.1 (Intergenerational and Transgenerational Inheritance)** An effect of an exposure of the parental generation F0 that appears in a generation that was itself directly exposed, as embryo or as germ cells, is **intergenerational inheritance**. An effect that persists into the first generation that was not directly exposed is **transgenerational epigenetic inheritance**.

**Formula 18.6.1 (The First Unexposed Generation)** If the exposed generation is F0, the first generation that can show transgenerational inheritance is

| Who is exposed | Directly exposed generations | First unexposed generation |
|---|---|---|
| a male, or a female before pregnancy | F0 and its germ cells, which form F1 | F2 |
| a pregnant female | F0; the fetus F1; the germ cells of the fetus, which form F2 | F3 |

The counting is simple but has decided many debates. Many reports of "transgenerational" effects of diet, stress or toxins in mammals concern F1 or F2 after maternal exposure, which are intergenerational effects; fewer withstand testing in F3, with controlled maternal environment (embryo transfer or cross-fostering) and with the parent of origin varied.

### 18.6.2. The Agouti Viable Yellow Allele

The best-studied mammalian epiallele is in the mouse *agouti* gene, which encodes a signaling protein that makes hair follicles switch from black to yellow pigment, giving the banded agouti hair. The lethal yellow allele $A^{y}$ of [7.4.3](/7.4.3#sec-3.4.1), a deletion that puts *agouti* under a ubiquitous promoter, is a sequence change. The viable yellow allele $A^{vy}$ is different: it carries an IAP **retrotransposon** inserted upstream of the coding exons, whose long terminal repeat contains a cryptic promoter that drives *agouti* everywhere. When the IAP is unmethylated, the mouse is yellow, obese, diabetic and prone to tumors; when it is methylated, *agouti* is expressed only in its normal hair-cycle pattern and the mouse is agouti-colored ("pseudoagouti") and lean. Cells choose the state early in development and inherit it, so isogenic $A^{vy}/a$ mice, of identical genotype, range from yellow through mottled to pseudoagouti.

The state is partly inherited through the female germ line. Morgan, Whitelaw and colleagues (1999) found that yellow $A^{vy}/a$ mothers have more yellow and fewer pseudoagouti offspring than pseudoagouti mothers, although both mothers transmit the same allele; through fathers there was no such effect. Embryo transfer into mothers of the other phenotype did not change the result, so the effect is carried by the gamete, not the uterus. Diet changes the distribution: methyl-donor supplements (folic acid, vitamin B12, choline, betaine) fed to $a/a$ mothers carrying $A^{vy}/a$ offspring shift the offspring toward pseudoagouti, with higher methylation of the IAP (Wolff and colleagues 1998; Waterland and Jirtle 2003). In the terms of Definition 18.6.1 the dietary effect is intergenerational: the offspring were exposed in utero. Later work showed that the methylation of the $A^{vy}$ IAP is erased after fertilization in both parental genomes and re-established in the embryo; what is inherited through the oocyte is not the methylation itself but a tendency to re-establish it, which depends on maternal-effect chromatin proteins. A related epiallele, $Axin^{Fu}$ (kinked tail, also an IAP insertion), is transmitted through both parents.

### 18.6.3. Paramutation and Epialleles in Plants

Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance is common in flowering plants, whose germ line is set aside late, from somatic cells of the flower, and is not reprogrammed as thoroughly as that of mammals.

**Definition 18.6.2 (Epiallele and Paramutation)** An **epiallele** is one of two or more heritable states of a locus that have the same DNA sequence and differ in chromatin, usually in DNA methylation, and in expression. **Paramutation** is an interaction between two alleles of a locus in which one allele (paramutagenic) heritably changes the expression state of the other (paramutable), which then becomes paramutagenic itself.

Paramutation was discovered by Alexander Brink (1956) at the maize *r1* locus and is best understood at *b1*, which controls purple anthocyanin pigment in the plant. The *B-I* allele is strongly expressed; it changes spontaneously, at about 1–10 % per generation, into *B′*, weakly expressed, with the same sequence. In a *B′*/*B-I* heterozygote, *B-I* is converted to *B′* with a frequency near 100 %, so that the F1 is lightly pigmented and every F2 plant is light: the ratio is not 3:1 but 1:0. Converted *B′* converts further *B-I* alleles in later crosses. The interaction requires seven tandem copies of an 853-bp sequence about 100 kb upstream of the gene, which are methylated in *B′*, and the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase encoded by *mop1*: small RNAs from the repeats direct methylation to the homologous repeats of the other allele ([7.2.18](/7.2.18#sec-18.7)). Paramutation breaks Mendel's assumption that alleles separate unchanged from the heterozygote.

The first natural epiallele was found in toadflax, *Linaria vulgaris*. Linnaeus described in 1744 a radially symmetric ("peloric") form of this normally bilaterally symmetric flower and thought it a new species. Cubas, Vincent and Coen (1999) showed that it carries the same sequence as the wild type at *Lcyc*, the homolog of the *CYCLOIDEA* gene for floral asymmetry, but *Lcyc* is methylated and silent; it is inherited as a recessive Mendelian allele and occasionally reverts, in somatic sectors and in progeny, to wild type. In *Arabidopsis*, epigenetic recombinant inbred lines (epiRILs) made from crosses between wild-type plants and mutants of the chromatin remodeler DDM1, which lose much of their methylation, have nearly identical genomes but different methylation patterns; they show heritable variation in flowering time and root length, and the variation maps to differentially methylated regions as if they were genetic loci (Johannes and colleagues 2009; Cortijo and colleagues 2014). Such epialleles behave as alleles with a high mutation rate in both directions (18.7).

### 18.6.4. Small RNAs and Inheritance in Nematodes

In *C. elegans*, **RNA interference** ([7.2.18](/7.2.18#sec-18.5)) triggered by feeding worms double-stranded RNA silences the target gene not only in the treated animals but often in their descendants, typically for three to five generations and for some genes indefinitely. The silencing is carried by secondary small interfering RNAs, amplified by RNA-dependent RNA polymerases and bound by the germline nuclear Argonaute HRDE-1, which direct H3K9 methylation to the target; piRNAs ([7.2.18](/7.2.18#sec-18.7)) initiate similar inherited silencing of foreign sequences. Mutants in *hrde-1* lose the inheritance and become progressively sterile over generations ("mortal germline"), which shows that the system has an endogenous role in germline maintenance. Inherited silencing is actively limited: dedicated genes reset it, so that its duration is itself a regulated, heritable trait.

### 18.6.5. Mammals: Reasons for Caution

In mammals, two waves of reprogramming, after fertilization and in the primordial germ cells (18.2.4), erase most methylation, and most sequences that escape erasure are imprinted regions and some young retrotransposons such as the IAPs of 18.6.2. This is why well-documented transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in mammals is rare and mostly concerns retrotransposon-associated epialleles. Human epidemiological studies of famine and other exposures (for example, children conceived during the Dutch Hunger Winter of 1944–1945 had slightly lower methylation at the *IGF2* region six decades later) show intergenerational associations whose mechanism, and whose separation from confounding, remain uncertain. A claim of epigenetic inheritance in mammals requires the controls of Definition 18.6.1, exclusion of genetic differences (including in the mitochondria and the microbiome) and a molecular carrier that survives reprogramming.

## 18.7. A Markov Model of Epiallele Stability

Epialleles switch too often to be described by the mutation rates of [7.4.12](/7.4.12) and too regularly to be ignored. Their inheritance over generations is a two-state **Markov chain** ([3.5.9](/3.5.9)).

**Formula 18.7.1 (Two-State Epiallele Model)** Let a site (a CpG, or an epiallele) be methylated ($M$) or unmethylated ($U$) in each generation, and let it change from $M$ to $U$ with probability $\alpha$ and from $U$ to $M$ with probability $\beta$ per generation, independently of the past. Let $m_t$ be the probability that the site is methylated in generation $t$. Then

$$
m_{t+1} = (1-\alpha)\,m_t + \beta\,(1 - m_t), \qquad
m_t = \pi + (1 - \alpha - \beta)^t\,(m_0 - \pi), \qquad
\pi = \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}.
\tag{18.1}
$$

The equilibrium methylation level is $\pi$; deviations from it decay geometrically with ratio $\lambda = 1 - \alpha - \beta$, so that the characteristic time is about $1/(\alpha + \beta)$ generations, and the probability that a site in state $M$ is still in state $M$ after $t$ generations is $\pi + (1 - \pi)\lambda^t$.

**Proof** In generation $t+1$ the site is methylated if it was methylated and did not lose methylation, probability $m_t(1-\alpha)$, or was unmethylated and gained it, probability $(1-m_t)\beta$. Subtracting the fixed point $\pi = (1-\alpha)\pi + \beta(1 - \pi)$, which gives $\pi = \beta/(\alpha+\beta)$, yields $m_{t+1} - \pi = (1-\alpha-\beta)(m_t - \pi)$; iterating gives Eq. (18.1). The conditional probability starting from $M$ is the case $m_0 = 1$. $\square$

The model has three uses.

- Estimating rates. In mutation-accumulation lines of *Arabidopsis*, propagated by single-seed descent from one ancestor for 30 generations and profiled by bisulfite sequencing, Schmitz, Becker and colleagues (2011) and van der Graaf and colleagues (2015) estimated per-generation rates for CG sites of about $\beta \approx 3 \times 10^{-4}$ (gain) and $\alpha \approx 6 \times 10^{-4}$ (loss). The DNA mutation rate in the same lines is about $7 \times 10^{-9}$ per base per generation ([7.4.12](/7.4.12#sec-12.5)), so single CG sites switch about $10^4$–$10^5$ times more often than bases mutate. Fitting Eq. (18.1) to the divergence of methylation between lines as a function of generations separates the two rates.
- Persistence. From Eq. (18.1), a methylated state is a reliable mark of ancestry only over times short compared with $1/(\alpha+\beta)$: about $1\,000$ generations for single CG sites in *Arabidopsis*, but a few generations for *b1* (where $B$-$I$ changes to $B'$ at about 1–10 % per generation and back much more rarely) or for inherited RNAi in worms.
- Response to selection. A trait determined by an epiallele responds to selection, but the response decays at rate $\lambda$ when selection stops, unlike a response based on sequence variants; this is the basis of the argument that epialleles contribute mainly to short-term, reversible adaptation, discussed with population genetics in [7.4.23](/7.4.23).

The model is generational. Within an organism, a chromatin state is copied at each cell division with an error rate per division; the maintenance-and-de-novo model of DNA methylation ([7.2.15](/7.2.15#fml-15.7.1)) and the read–write model of regional memory ([7.2.15](/7.2.15#ex-15.6.1)) describe that level, and the generational rates of Eq. (18.1) are their net result over a germline cycle.

**Example 18.7.1** For a CG site with $\alpha = 6 \times 10^{-4}$ and $\beta = 3 \times 10^{-4}$: (a) $\pi = 3/9 = 0.33$; (b) $\lambda = 1 - 9 \times 10^{-4}$, so the deviation of a population of sites from equilibrium halves in $\ln 2/(9 \times 10^{-4}) \approx 770$ generations; (c) a site methylated today is still methylated after 30 generations with probability $\pi + (1-\pi)\lambda^{30} = 0.333 + 0.667 \times 0.9734 = 0.982$, so about 1.8 % of the sites methylated in the ancestor have lost methylation in a line after 30 generations, and two such lines differ at about $2 \times 0.982 \times 0.018 \approx 3.5\,\%$ of them. In a genome with $5 \times 10^6$ methylated CG sites, that is about $9 \times 10^4$ changed sites per line, against fewer than one new base substitution per $10^8$ bases per generation.

## Questions

1. Pronuclear transplantation produces mouse eggs with two maternal (gynogenetic) or two paternal (androgenetic) genomes. (a) Why does this experiment show that imprinting exists, but not which genes are imprinted? (b) Predict the parental composition of a complete hydatidiform mole and of an ovarian teratoma.

   **Answer** (a) Gynogenetic and androgenetic eggs contain the same genes as normal eggs, only from one parent; their failure shows that the two parental genomes are functionally different, which requires a parental mark. Which genes carry the marks needs allele-specific tests, such as the *Igf2* knockout transmitted through each parent. (b) A complete mole is androgenetic (diploid, both genomes from the father; extraembryonic tissue overgrows, as in androgenetic mouse embryos); an ovarian teratoma is parthenogenetic, with only maternal genomes (embryonic tissues without trophoblast).

2. A man carries a loss-of-function mutation in a paternally expressed gene, which he inherited from his mother; the disease is fully penetrant when the mutant allele is expressed. He has children with an unrelated woman. (a) Is he affected? (b) What is the probability that each of his children is affected? (c) A carrier daughter of his has children: what is their risk? (d) And the children of a carrier son?

   **Answer** (a) No: his mutant allele is maternal and therefore silent; his paternal allele is normal and expressed. (b) In his children the allele is paternal and expressed: each child is affected with probability $1/2$. (c) A daughter transmits the allele as a maternal, silent allele: risk $0$ of being affected, $1/2$ of being a carrier. (d) A son transmits it as a paternal allele: risk $1/2$ for each child.

3. Allele-specific expression: a mouse cross is made between two inbred strains, A and B, which differ by a SNP in the transcript of a gene. In the F1 of A female × B male, 98 % of the RNA-seq reads carry the B allele; in the reciprocal cross, 97 % carry the A allele. (a) Which parental allele is expressed? (b) Why are reciprocal crosses essential? (c) What result would a strain-specific (*cis*-acting) expression difference give?

   **Answer** (a) In both crosses the paternal allele (B in the first, A in the second) gives most of the reads: the gene is paternally expressed. (b) In a single cross, preferential expression of one allele could reflect either parent of origin or a *cis*-acting sequence difference between the strains; only the reversal of the expressed allele when the parents are exchanged identifies imprinting. (c) The same allele (say B) would dominate in both reciprocal crosses, at a ratio that is the same whichever parent transmits it.

4. A child has Prader–Willi syndrome with maternal uniparental disomy 15 resulting from trisomy rescue. (a) Explain why the child has PWS although both chromosomes 15 are normal in sequence. (b) If the trisomic zygote loses one of its three chromosomes 15 at random, what fraction of rescued zygotes from a maternal meiotic nondisjunction have maternal UPD? (c) Why does the risk of maternal UPD 15 increase with maternal age?

   **Answer** (a) Both chromosomes carry the maternal imprint, so the paternally expressed genes of 15q11–q13 (*SNORD116* and others) are silent on both: there is no expressed copy. (b) The zygote has two maternal chromosomes and one paternal; losing the paternal one (probability $1/3$) gives maternal UPD; losing a maternal one (probability $2/3$) gives a normal biparental karyotype. (c) The initiating event is maternal meiotic nondisjunction, whose frequency rises with maternal age like that of trisomies ([7.4.16](/7.4.16)).

5. The parents of a child with Angelman syndrome ask for the risk to their next child. Give the risk for each of the following findings: (a) a de novo maternal deletion of 15q11–q13; (b) paternal uniparental disomy with normal parental karyotypes; (c) an imprinting defect caused by a deletion of the imprinting center that the mother carries on the chromosome she inherited from her father; (d) a *UBE3A* mutation also present in the mother.

   **Answer** (a) Below 1 % (germline mosaicism only). (b) Below 1 %. (c) 50 %: the mother's deletion is silent in her (it was paternal), but when she transmits it (probability $1/2$) it prevents the switch to a maternal imprint. (d) 50 %: maternal *UBE3A* is the expressed copy in neurons, and each child inherits the mutation from her with probability $1/2$.

6. Methylation-specific PCR of the *SNRPN* region gives, for a normal person, a methylated (maternal) and an unmethylated (paternal) product. Predict the result for: (a) PWS with paternal deletion; (b) PWS with maternal UPD; (c) AS with maternal deletion; (d) AS with a *UBE3A* point mutation. Which of these can the test not detect?

   **Answer** (a) Methylated product only (the remaining chromosome is maternal). (b) Methylated only. (c) Unmethylated only. (d) Both products, as in a normal person: the imprint is normal and only the gene is mutated. The test cannot detect (d), which needs sequencing of *UBE3A*.

7. In *Drosophila* with the variegating allele $w^{m4}$, a mutation $S$ on chromosome 2 increases the fraction of red facets from 20 % to 70 % when heterozygous, and has no visible phenotype in flies with a normal *white* gene. (a) Is $S$ a suppressor or an enhancer of variegation? (b) A duplication of the wild-type region containing the $S$ locus reduces the red fraction to 5 %. What does this dosage behavior suggest about the product of the gene? (c) Why is a $w^{m4}$ background a good sensitized background for a genetic screen?

   **Answer** (a) A suppressor, *Su(var)*: it reduces silencing. (b) Haplo-suppressor and triplo-enhancer: the product is a limiting structural component of heterochromatin, whose amount sets how far silencing spreads (as for HP1, *Su(var)2-5*). (c) The *white* gene in $w^{m4}$ is poised between on and off, so a twofold change in a silencing component shifts the fraction of red facets visibly in heterozygotes; mutations that would be invisible in a normal background can be scored in the F1, a dominant screen.

8. Paramutation at *b1*: a *B′*/*B′* plant (light) is crossed to a *B-I*/*B-I* plant (dark). (a) Predict the F1 and F2 phenotypes, assuming that conversion of *B-I* in the heterozygote is complete. (b) Contrast this with the F2 expected for a recessive sequence allele. (c) What would a backcross of an F1 plant to *B-I*/*B-I* give?

   **Answer** (a) F1: light (the *B-I* allele is converted to *B′*). F2: all light, since every F1 gamete carries *B′* (the original or the converted allele): a ratio of 1:0 instead of 3:1. (b) A recessive sequence allele would give a dark F1 and 3 dark : 1 light in the F2. (c) Each progeny is *B′*/*B-I* (the F1 transmits only *B′*), so again all light; the converted alleles convert the *B-I* from the backcross parent.

9. A dietary exposure of pregnant mice changes a phenotype of their offspring. (a) In which generation must the change be found to qualify as transgenerational epigenetic inheritance? (b) If only fathers are exposed, before mating? (c) Name two further controls needed in either case.

   **Answer** (a) F3: the exposed mother is F0, her fetuses F1 and their germ cells (the future F2) were exposed directly (Formula 18.6.1). (b) F2: the exposed father F0 and his sperm (the future F1) were exposed, F2 was not. (c) Transmission through each parental sex separately (to separate gamete from uterine and behavioral effects, with embryo transfer or cross-fostering), exclusion of genetic and microbiome differences between the lines, and identification of a molecular carrier in the gametes.

10. An epiallele switches from $M$ to $U$ with probability $\alpha = 0.04$ and from $U$ to $M$ with $\beta = 0.01$ per generation. (a) What fraction of a large population of independent lines is methylated at equilibrium? (b) A line is methylated today. What is the probability that it is methylated 10 generations later? (c) After how many generations has the deviation of a population of lines, all methylated now, from equilibrium halved?

    **Answer** (a) $\pi = 0.01/0.05 = 0.2$. (b) $\lambda = 1 - 0.05 = 0.95$; $\pi + (1-\pi)\lambda^{10} = 0.2 + 0.8 \times 0.5987 = 0.679$. (c) $0.95^t = 0.5$ gives $t = \ln 0.5/\ln 0.95 = 13.5$ generations.

11. Single CG sites in *Arabidopsis* gain methylation at $\beta \approx 3 \times 10^{-4}$ and lose it at $\alpha \approx 6 \times 10^{-4}$ per generation. Two lines descend from one ancestor for 30 generations each. (a) For a site methylated in the ancestor, what is the probability that the two lines differ at it? (b) For $10^6$ such sites, how many differences are expected? (c) Compare with the number of base differences expected between the lines in a $1.2 \times 10^8$ bp genome with a mutation rate of $7 \times 10^{-9}$ per base per generation.

    **Answer** (a) Each line is still methylated with probability $p = \pi + (1-\pi)\lambda^{30}$ with $\pi = 1/3$, $\lambda = 1 - 9 \times 10^{-4}$: $p = 0.333 + 0.667 \times 0.9734 = 0.9823$. The lines differ with probability $2p(1-p) = 2 \times 0.9823 \times 0.0177 = 0.0348$. (b) About $3.5 \times 10^4$ sites. (c) $2 \times 30 \times 7 \times 10^{-9} \times 1.2 \times 10^8 \approx 50$ base differences: methylation differences outnumber sequence differences by almost three orders of magnitude, even though only a fraction of all bases are methylatable sites.

12. (Pedigree) A woman has two children with Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome caused by a loss-of-function mutation in *CDKN1C*, which is maternally expressed. She is unaffected, as is her father; her father's sister is unaffected but has an affected child. (a) Through which of the woman's grandparents did the mutation most likely enter the family? (b) What is the risk for each further child of the woman? (c) The woman's brother is shown to be a carrier. What is the risk for his children, and for the children of his daughters?

    **Answer** (a) Through her paternal grandfather. The woman and her father's sister are unaffected carriers who transmit the disease, so each carries the mutation on a silent, paternally derived chromosome: the woman received it from her father, and her father and his sister from their father. Had the father received it from his mother, it would be expressed in him and he would be affected. (b) $1/2$ per child: she transmits the allele as a maternal, expressed allele. (c) His children are carriers with probability $1/2$ but unaffected (paternal transmission). Each daughter is a carrier with probability $1/2$ and transmits as a maternal allele, so the risk for each of her children is $1/4$ before she is tested, $1/2$ if she is a carrier.
