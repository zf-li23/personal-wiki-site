Mendel's laws assume that every allele has the same chance, one half, of being transmitted, and that alleles stay where they are. Transposable elements break the second assumption: they move, insert into genes, and multiply within genomes until they make up half of the DNA of humans and most of the DNA of maize. Other elements break the first: they cheat in meiosis and are transmitted to more than half of the gametes. Both are selfish in the sense that their spread does not depend on any benefit to the organism. The molecular mechanisms of transposition, the classes of elements and their host restraints are described in [7.2.9](/7.2.9#sec-9.8), where **transposon**, **DNA transposon**, **retrotransposon** and **transposition** are defined, and the repeated sequences they leave in genomes in [7.2.2](/7.2.2#sec-2.2.4). This chapter asks what transposition looks like to a geneticist: unstable mutations and variegated phenotypes, reciprocal crosses that differ, alleles that rise in frequency against the interest of their carriers, and the uses of transposons for finding and manipulating genes. It builds on the chromosomal rearrangements of [7.4.15](/7.4.15), many of which are caused by transposons, and prepares the gene drives of [7.4.19](/7.4.19) and the population genetics of [7.4.23](/7.4.23).

## 17.1. McClintock's Controlling Elements

### 17.1.1. *Ds*, *Ac* and Variegated Kernels

In the 1940s Barbara McClintock studied maize strains in which the short arm of chromosome 9 broke frequently, always at the same place. Breakage was visible in the kernels: a kernel heterozygous for the dominant alleles *C* (colored aleurone), *Sh* (plump endosperm) and *Wx* (amylose starch) on the broken chromosome, and the recessive alleles on its homologue, showed sectors with the recessive phenotypes wherever the chromosome had broken and lost its distal part. She traced the breakage to a factor at the site of the break, which she called "Dissociation" (*Ds*), and found that *Ds* broke the chromosome only if a second factor, which she named "Activator" (*Ac*), was present somewhere in the genome. She then discovered that *Ds* could move: it appeared at new sites, and when it inserted into the *C* gene it produced a colorless mutant allele, $c^{\mathit{Ds}}$, which in the presence of *Ac* reverted to *C* in some cells, giving colorless kernels with purple spots. *Ac* could move too, and *Ac* alone, without *Ds*, could cause unstable mutations. Her conclusion, published in 1950–1953, that genomes contain mobile "controlling elements" that regulate the genes near which they land, was confirmed at the molecular level in the 1980s; she received the Nobel Prize in 1983.

**Definition 17.1.1 (Autonomous and Nonautonomous Elements)** An **autonomous element** encodes the functions needed for its own transposition (the transposase of a DNA transposon, the reverse transcriptase and endonuclease of a retrotransposon) and can move by itself; a **nonautonomous element** lacks a functional copy of these genes but keeps the sequences (such as terminal inverted repeats) recognized by the enzymes, so that it moves only when an autonomous element of the same family is present elsewhere in the genome and supplies them in trans.

*Ac* is a $4.6\,\mathrm{kb}$ DNA transposon encoding a transposase; the *Ds* elements are members of the same family with internal deletions or unrelated internal sequences, $0.4$ to $4\,\mathrm{kb}$ long, sharing the $11\,\mathrm{bp}$ terminal inverted repeats of *Ac*. In the same way the human Alu SINEs are nonautonomous elements mobilized by the enzymes of the autonomous L1 LINEs ([7.2.2](/7.2.2#sec-2.2.4)), and defective P elements are mobilized by complete ones (17.3).

The genetics of *Ac*/*Ds* follows from Definition 17.1.1. A strain with $c^{\mathit{Ds}}$ and no *Ac* is stably colorless; crossing in *Ac* produces spotted kernels; the spotting segregates with *Ac*, not with $c^{\mathit{Ds}}$, and *Ac* can be mapped independently. Excision of *Ds* from the 5′ untranslated region of *C* restores the gene. Excision from a coding exon usually does not, because excision leaves a few base pairs of the target-site duplication behind (a footprint) and these shift or change the reading frame; such excisions produce stable new alleles, some with partial activity, which made the elements a source of allelic variation.

### 17.1.2. A Developmental-Timing Model of Spotting

The size of a spot tells when the excision happened, and their number how often. The aleurone layer of the endosperm grows from a single triploid cell by repeated cell divisions; a cell in which *Ds* excises from $c^{\mathit{Ds}}$ passes the restored *C* allele to all its descendants, which form one purple clone.

**Formula 17.1.1 (Spot Sizes)** Model the aleurone as growing from one cell by $K$ rounds of synchronous division, and let excision occur in each cell that still carries the insertion with probability $\varepsilon_k$ at division $k$. An excision at division $k$ gives a spot of $2^{K - k}$ cells. If $\varepsilon_k$ is small, the expected number of spots of this size per kernel is about $\varepsilon_k 2^k$, and the expected fraction of the aleurone that is pigmented is about $\sum_k \varepsilon_k$, which equals $K\varepsilon$ for a constant rate $\varepsilon$.

**Proof** After division $k$ there are $2^k$ cells, nearly all still carrying the insertion when $\varepsilon$ is small, so the expected number of excisions is $\varepsilon_k 2^k$, each pigmenting $2^{K - k}$ of the $2^K$ final cells: a fraction $\varepsilon_k 2^k \cdot 2^{K-k}/2^K = \varepsilon_k$ per division. $\square$

With a constant rate, each halving of spot size doubles the number of spots: small spots are many, large ones rare, and every division contributes the same pigmented area. A change in the timing of excision therefore changes the pattern without necessarily changing the total pigment.

![Spotted kernels in a developmental-timing model (Formula 17.1.1). The aleurone grows from one cell by 14 rounds of division; Ds excises from the c gene in a cell at division k with probability ε_k, and the descendants of that cell form a purple spot of 2^(14 − k) cells. A: one simulated kernel with one dose of Ac, with excision possible at every division (ε = 1.5 × 10⁻³): few spots, some large. B: with two doses of Ac excision is delayed to the last six divisions (at a higher rate): many small spots and no large ones. C: spot-size distributions over 400 simulated kernels of each kind (bars) and the expectation ε_k 2^k (dashes), on a logarithmic scale](./plots/kernel-spots.py)

McClintock observed that adding copies of *Ac* (in the triploid endosperm, $1$, $2$ or $3$ doses, controlled by the cross) delays excision: spots become later, smaller and more numerous. This negative dosage effect is now attributed to the aggregation of excess transposase. The model shows how such data are read: the sizes of spots measure the developmental time of the events, as the sizes of twin spots do for mitotic recombination ([7.4.9](/7.4.9)) and the sizes of mutant clones do in the fluctuation test ([7.4.12](/7.4.12)).

## 17.2. Transposable Elements of Bacteria

**Definition 17.2.1 (Insertion Sequence and Composite Transposon)** An **insertion sequence** (IS element) is a bacterial transposable element, $0.7$–$2.5\,\mathrm{kb}$ long, that carries only the gene for its transposase between short terminal inverted repeats. A **composite transposon** consists of a segment of DNA, often carrying antibiotic resistance genes, flanked by two copies of an IS element (in direct or inverted orientation); the transposase of one IS element moves the whole structure by acting on the outer ends of the two IS elements.

IS elements were discovered through polar mutations in the *gal* operon of *E. coli* that reverted spontaneously and contained inserted DNA (1967–1969). Composite transposons such as Tn10 (tetracycline resistance, flanked by IS10) and Tn5 (kanamycin resistance, IS50) and simple, non-composite transposons such as Tn3 (ampicillin resistance, transposase and resolvase between $38\,\mathrm{bp}$ inverted repeats) carry resistance genes from one replicon to another; on conjugative plasmids ([7.4.10](/7.4.10)) they spread between cells, strains and species. The combination of transposition (within a cell), plasmid conjugation (between cells) and selection by antibiotics explains how resistance to new drugs appears in many pathogens within a few years of their introduction. Integrons, which capture resistance gene cassettes by site-specific recombination, are frequently carried on transposons as well.

IS elements also reshape bacterial genomes: recombination between two copies of an IS element integrates the F plasmid into the chromosome to form an Hfr strain (Definition 10.4.3), and between copies on the chromosome causes deletions and inversions (non-allelic homologous recombination, Definition 15.1.2).

## 17.3. P Elements and Hybrid Dysgenesis

### 17.3.1. Reciprocal Crosses That Differ

In the 1970s Margaret and James Kidwell and John Sved found that crosses between some laboratory strains of *Drosophila melanogaster* and recently caught wild strains gave offspring with high rates of mutation, chromosome breakage and male recombination (normally absent in *Drosophila*), and with atrophied gonads, but only when the father came from the wild strain.

**Definition 17.3.1 (Hybrid Dysgenesis and Cytotype)** **Hybrid dysgenesis** is a syndrome of germline abnormalities (sterility, high mutation and chromosome-breakage rates, male recombination) in the offspring of crosses between certain strains of *Drosophila*, caused by the transposition in the germline of elements introduced by one parent into a genome and cytoplasm that do not restrain them. The **cytotype** is the maternally transmitted state of the egg that determines whether the elements are repressed (P cytotype) or not (M cytotype).

![P–M hybrid dysgenesis. Of the four crosses between P strains (yellow flies), which carry P elements and transmit P cytotype, and M strains (gray), which have neither, only that of an M female with a P male gives dysgenic offspring: the paternal P elements enter an egg without the maternal piRNAs that would repress them, and they transpose in the germline of the offspring. The reciprocal cross is normal, although the offspring carry the same P elements in their nuclear genomes, so the difference lies in what the mother deposits in the egg](./plots/hybrid-dysgenesis.py)

The responsible element, the P element, was cloned in 1982 by Bingham, Rubin and colleagues from dysgenesis-induced mutations of the *white* gene, by transposon tagging (Definition 17.7.1). The complete P element is $2.9\,\mathrm{kb}$ long, with $31\,\mathrm{bp}$ terminal inverted repeats and a transposase gene of four exons; P strains carry about 30–50 copies, most of them defective nonautonomous derivatives. That the cytotype is inherited through the mother but becomes established over generations was a puzzle for twenty years; it is now explained by PIWI-interacting RNAs (**PIWI-interacting RNA**, [7.2.18](/7.2.18)). Small RNAs complementary to P elements are produced from P-element copies inserted in piRNA clusters of P strains, are loaded into PIWI proteins in the ovary and deposited in the egg; in the germline of the offspring they silence P elements transcriptionally and post-transcriptionally and seed the production of further piRNAs. An M female deposits none, so the paternal P elements in her offspring are free to transpose until their own piRNA production builds up.

Two further controls restrict P activity to the germline. The third intron of the transposase gene is spliced only in germ cells; in somatic cells the unspliced RNA encodes a truncated protein that represses transposition by binding the element ends (the repressor described in [7.2.9](/7.2.9#sec-9.8)). The tissue specificity of splicing explains why dysgenesis affects only the germline.

### 17.3.2. A Recent Invasion

M strains are old laboratory strains, collected before about 1950; nearly all wild populations collected since the 1970s are P strains. *D. melanogaster* acquired P elements during the twentieth century by horizontal transfer from *Drosophila willistoni*, a distant species of the American tropics whose P elements differ from those of *D. melanogaster* by a single base pair; the probable vector is a mite that feeds on fly eggs. Within a few decades the element spread through the world population of the species, an invasion that the population genetics of 17.6 explains. A second invasion, of *Drosophila simulans*, began around 2010 and was followed in real time. The I–R system of *D. melanogaster*, caused by a LINE-like element called I, shows the same logic with the sexes reversed in name (inducer males, reactive females) and the same maternal piRNA control.

## 17.4. Transposable Elements in the Human Genome

![Approximate composition of the human genome by class of transposable element, with protein-coding sequence for comparison (values as in 7.2.2). Retrotransposons dominate; of the roughly half million L1 copies about a hundred can still retrotranspose, and they also mobilize Alu elements and make processed pseudogenes, whereas all DNA transposons and nearly all endogenous retroviruses of humans are inactive](./plots/human-te-content.py)

About $45$–$50\,\%$ of the human genome derives from transposable elements, most of it very old and inactive. Only L1, Alu and the composite SVA elements (which L1 also mobilizes) still move. New germline insertions of Alu occur in about one of every 20–40 births and of L1 in about one of every 100–200; they are polymorphic markers whose ancestral state is known (absence), so they have been used to trace human population history.

About 130 human disease mutations caused by new insertions are known. The first (Kazazian and colleagues, 1988) were two L1 insertions into exon 14 of the *F8* gene in boys with hemophilia A; their parents did not carry them. Since L1 is active in the early embryo and in some neuronal precursors, somatic insertions also occur, and many cancers (especially of the esophagus and colon) carry tens to hundreds of somatic L1 insertions, which occasionally disrupt tumor suppressor genes ([7.4.21](/7.4.21)). In the germline, L1 is held in check by DNA methylation and by piRNAs: mice lacking components of the piRNA pathway reactivate L1 in spermatogonia and are sterile, and human infertility variants in these genes are being classified by measuring L1 expression.

## 17.5. Genetic Consequences of Transposition

1. Insertional mutations. Most spontaneous visible mutations of *Drosophila* are insertions of transposable elements (the classic *white-apricot* allele $w^{a}$ is an insertion of the retrotransposon *copia* into an intron of *white*). Mendel's wrinkled peas carry an $0.8\,\mathrm{kb}$ insertion, related to *Ac*/*Ds*, in the gene for starch-branching enzyme I ([7.4.2](/7.4.2)), and the *carbonaria* allele that made the peppered moth black during the industrial revolution is a $22\,\mathrm{kb}$ insertion of a tandemly repeated DNA transposon into an intron of the gene *cortex*.
2. Unstable alleles. Alleles caused by insertions of DNA transposons revert when the element excises (precisely, or leaving a footprint), giving variegation when the autonomous element is present, as in the kernels of 17.1. Revertants arise at rates that can exceed $10^{-3}$ per gamete, far higher than for point mutations.
3. Effects on expression. Insertions upstream of a gene can supply new promoters, enhancers or splice sites, or spread silent chromatin into the gene; in the long term, transposon sequences are co-opted as regulatory elements: in mammals many enhancers and binding sites of transcription factors derive from transposons, and the placental expression of many genes depends on endogenous retroviruses (reviewed by Fueyo, Feschotte, Wysocka and colleagues, 2022).
4. Gene relocation and new genes. L1 reverse-transcribes its RNA together with downstream sequence that is read through its weak polyadenylation signal (3′ transduction), and so copies exons and whole genes to new places; processed pseudogenes and retrogenes arise in the same way. Two DNA transposons that insert near each other can mobilize the segment between them, as composite transposons do in bacteria.
5. Rearrangements. Recombination between two copies of the same element produces deletions, duplications, inversions and translocations by non-allelic homologous recombination (Definition 15.1.2); excision and insertion of *Ac*/*Ds* at the two ends of a segment (alternative transposition) generate deletions and inversions directly.
6. Domestication. Some transposase genes have become host genes: RAG1, the recombinase of **V(D)J recombination**, and the centromere protein CENP-B derive from DNA transposases, telomerase is related to retrotransposon reverse transcriptases, and the syncytins of the placenta are retroviral envelope proteins ([7.2.9](/7.2.9#sec-9.8)). In *Drosophila*, telomeres are maintained not by telomerase but by specialized retrotransposons (HeT-A, TART) that insert at chromosome ends.

## 17.6. Selfish Genetic Elements

**Definition 17.6.1 (Selfish Genetic Element)** A **selfish genetic element** is a DNA sequence that increases its own transmission relative to the rest of the genome by means that do not benefit, and often harm, the organism that carries it: by making extra copies of itself (transposable elements), by being transmitted to more than half of the gametes of heterozygotes (meiotic drivers, B chromosomes, homing endonuclease genes), or by killing or sterilizing the offspring that do not carry it (cytoplasmic incompatibility, Definition 11.6.3).

The term was introduced by Doolittle and Sapienza and by Orgel and Crick (1980), who argued that much repetitive DNA exists because it can replicate itself, not because it serves the organism. The idea is a statement about population genetics: an element spreads if its transmission advantage exceeds its cost to the carrier.

### 17.6.1. The Copy Number of Transposable Elements

A transposon whose copies duplicate within the genome increases its number of copies each generation; if copies were harmless, it would spread without limit. Copy number is limited by excision, by the host's regulation of transposition, and by selection against individuals with many copies (through insertional mutations and ectopic recombination between copies).

**Formula 17.6.1 (Transposon Copy Number)** Let $n$ be the mean number of copies per haploid genome, $u$ the rate of transposition per copy per generation, $v$ the rate of excision (loss) per copy, and let the log fitness of a genome with $n$ copies be $\ln w(n) = -an - \tfrac12 bn^2$, with $b > 0$ when copies interact (synergistic epistasis, as through ectopic recombination). If copies are distributed among genomes approximately as a **Poisson distribution**, the mean copy number changes approximately as

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}t} = n\bigl(u - v - a - bn\bigr),
$$
with a stable equilibrium

$$
n^{*} = \frac{u - v - a}{b} \quad (u > v + a).
$$
If $u \le v + a$ the element is lost; if $b = 0$ and $u > v + a$ it increases without limit.

**Proof** Transposition and excision change $n$ by $(u - v)n$ per generation. Selection changes the mean of a trait by the covariance between the trait and relative fitness; for $\ln w$ approximately linear around the mean, $\Delta n_{\mathrm{sel}} \approx \operatorname{Var}(n)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln w}{\mathrm{d}n} = -\operatorname{Var}(n)(a + bn)$, and with Poisson variance $\operatorname{Var}(n) = n$. The equilibrium follows by setting the bracket to zero; it is stable because the bracket decreases in $n$. $\square$

![Copy-number dynamics of a transposon (Formula 17.6.1) with u = 0.01, v = 0.001, a = 0.001 and b = 10⁻⁴. A: from one, 20 or 150 copies the mean copy number approaches the equilibrium n* = (u − v − a)/b = 80 (solid lines); after an invasion from a single copy it rises logistically over several hundred generations. With self-regulation, u(n) = u0/(1 + n/20), as when host piRNAs increase with the number of copies, the equilibrium is lower (dashed). B: the equilibrium grows linearly with the transposition rate above the threshold v + a and falls with the strength of synergistic selection b](./plots/te-copy-number.py)

The model explains the logistic course of the P-element invasion, why elements are lost when their transposition rate falls below the rate at which copies are removed (which is the fate of most families, which then decay by mutation), and why host repression of transposition (piRNAs, methylation) lowers the equilibrium. It also predicts a role for sex: in asexual lineages, copies cannot be sorted into different genomes by recombination and segregation, so selection cannot remove them efficiently, while new families cannot invade from other individuals.

### 17.6.2. Meiotic Drive

**Definition 17.6.2 (Meiotic Drive)** **Meiotic drive** (segregation distortion) is the transmission of one allele or chromosome of a heterozygote to more than half of its functional gametes, caused by the allele itself, either by biased segregation in female meiosis (true meiotic drive in the narrow sense) or by the destruction or disabling of the gametes that do not carry it (gamete killing, usually in males). Its strength is measured by the transmission ratio $k$, the fraction of the offspring of a heterozygote that receive the driving allele.

Two systems have been analyzed in detail.

- *Segregation Distorter* (*SD*) of *Drosophila melanogaster*. Males heterozygous for an *SD* chromosome 2 and a sensitive homologue transmit *SD* to $95$–$99\,\%$ of their offspring; the sperm carrying the homologue fail to condense their chromatin and are lost. The driver *Sd* is a truncated duplication of the gene for RanGAP, and its target *Rsp* (*Responder*) is a block of satellite DNA in the pericentric heterochromatin of the sensitive chromosome; insensitive alleles have few repeats. Enhancers of drive and the insensitive *Rsp* are held together with *Sd* by inversions, a supergene (Definition 15.4.3).
- The *t* haplotypes of the house mouse. A *t* haplotype is a variant form of about $40\,\mathrm{Mb}$ of chromosome 17, held together by four nonoverlapping inversions, that is transmitted by heterozygous $+/t$ males to up to $90$–$99\,\%$ of their offspring. Several distorter loci on the haplotype encode deregulated signaling proteins that act on all spermatids through the shared cytoplasm of the syncytium, and a responder locus on the haplotype protects the *t*-bearing sperm, so that $+$ sperm swim poorly. Most *t* haplotypes carry recessive lethal mutations, and $t/t$ males that survive are sterile.

A driver that is lethal or sterile when homozygous cannot fix, and the balance between drive and homozygous cost sets an equilibrium.

**Formula 17.6.2 (Equilibrium of a Driving Allele)** Let a driving allele $D$ have frequency $p$ in the gametes, with random mating, viability $w_{DD}$ of $DD$ relative to $1$ for $Dd$ and $dd$, and transmission ratio $k$ from heterozygotes of both sexes. Then

$$
p' = \frac{w_{DD}\,p^2 + 2kpq}{w_{DD}\,p^2 + 2pq + q^2}.
$$
For $w_{DD} = 0$ and $k > 1/2$, the stable equilibrium is $p^* = 2k - 1$, at which a fraction $(2k - 1)/k$ of adults are carriers. If drive occurs in males only (females transmit with ratio $1/2$), the equilibrium fraction of carriers among adults is

$$
h^* = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{1 - k}{k}}.
$$

**Proof** After viability selection, $D$ is carried by $DD$ survivors (frequency $w_{DD}p^2$, transmitting $D$ always) and heterozygotes ($2pq$, transmitting it with probability $k$); dividing by the mean viability gives $p'$. With $w_{DD} = 0$, $p' = p$ with $p > 0$ requires $2kq = 2pq + q^2 = q(1 + p)$, so $2k = 1 + p$. Adults are then $Dd$ or $dd$ in the ratio $2pq : q^2 = 2p : q$, so the carrier fraction is $2p/(1 + p) = (2k - 1)/k$. For male-only drive, let $h$ be the carrier fraction among adults; males produce $D$ sperm at frequency $hk$ and females $D$ eggs at frequency $h/2$, and among the surviving zygotes (all except $DD$) the carrier fraction is $h' = \bigl[hk(1 - h/2) + \tfrac{h}{2}(1 - hk)\bigr]/(1 - h^2k/2)$. Setting $h' = h$ and dividing by $h$ gives $\tfrac{k}{2}h^2 - kh + k - \tfrac12 = 0$, whose root in $(0, 1)$ is $h^* = 1 - \sqrt{(1 - k)/k}$. $\square$

A: the fraction of heterozygous carriers among adults over generations, starting from 1 %, when heterozygous males transmit the driver to a fraction k of their offspring and females are Mendelian (as for t haplotypes), for k = 0.6, 0.8 and 0.95, and (dashed) with drive in both sexes, k = 0.8. B: the equilibrium carrier frequency as a function of k, for male-only drive, 1 − √((1 − k)/k), and for drive in both sexes, (2k − 1)/k](./plots/meiotic-drive.py)

**Example 17.6.1** For *t* haplotypes with $k = 0.95$ in males and lethality of $t/t$, the model predicts that $1 - \sqrt{0.05/0.95} = 77\,\%$ of adult mice carry *t*, so that the frequency of *t* among chromosomes 17 is about $39\,\%$. In wild populations carriers are usually only $10$–$25\,\%$. The discrepancy shows what the model leaves out: females mate with several males, and the sperm of $+/t$ males compete poorly with those of $+/+$ males, so the effective transmission ratio is lower; small, inbred demes lose *t* by drift or are founded without it; and females may avoid $+/t$ males. Each of these mechanisms has been documented, and population cages and wild populations in which polyandry is prevented show higher *t* frequencies.

Drive in female meiosis works through the asymmetry of the divisions: only one of four products becomes the egg ([7.3.21](/7.3.21#sec-21.7.4)), and a chromosome whose centromere binds the spindle more strongly tends to stay in the egg instead of going to the polar body. Such centromere drive may explain the rapid evolution of centromeric DNA and of centromeric histones, and the preferential transmission of Robertsonian fusions in some mammals ([7.4.15](/7.4.15)). **B chromosomes** ([7.3.17](/7.3.17#sec-17.5.3)) accumulate by directed nondisjunction at the first pollen mitosis of rye and maize, which places both chromatids of the B in the generative nucleus that forms the sperm: their transmission ratio through pollen exceeds one half, which balances the loss of fertility they cause when numerous.

### 17.6.3. Homing Endonuclease Genes

**Definition 17.6.3 (Homing Endonuclease Gene)** A **homing endonuclease gene** is a selfish element that encodes a sequence-specific endonuclease cutting the allele that lacks the element at the site where the element is inserted in its own allele; repair of the break by homologous recombination ([7.2.9](/7.2.9)) using the element-bearing homologue as the template copies the element into the cut allele, converting a heterozygote into a homozygote (homing).

Homing endonuclease genes are found in self-splicing introns and inteins of fungi, algae, organelles and phages (the first, in the mitochondrial ribosomal RNA gene of yeast, was the ω locus, whose "unidirectional gene conversion" was a genetic puzzle of the 1970s). If homing occurs in the germline with efficiency $e$, a heterozygote transmits the element to a fraction $(1 + e)/2$ of its gametes. With no cost, the frequency of the element rises as $p' = p + epq$, almost as fast as an allele with a selective advantage $e$ that is always expressed, which is why Austin Burt proposed in 2003 to use engineered homing genes to spread traits through wild populations; with CRISPR–Cas9 as the endonuclease these are the gene drives of [7.4.19](/7.4.19).

## 17.7. Transposons as Genetic Tools

**Definition 17.7.1 (Transposon Tagging and Enhancer Trap)** **Transposon tagging** is the identification of a gene through a mutation caused by insertion of a known transposable element: the element serves as a molecular tag by which the flanking DNA of the mutant gene can be cloned. An **enhancer trap** is a transposable element carrying a reporter gene with a weak (minimal) promoter; when the element inserts near an enhancer, the reporter is expressed in the pattern that the enhancer drives, revealing the expression pattern of nearby genes.

- Mutagenesis and tagging. In *Drosophila*, P elements mobilized in a dysgenic cross or by a stable transposase source (the element $\Delta2\text{–}3$, whose third intron is removed so that it makes transposase in all tissues but cannot itself move) produce insertion mutations whose positions are found by inverse PCR or sequencing. The *Drosophila* Gene Disruption Project collected insertions in most genes with P, *piggyBac* and *Minos* elements. In maize, *Mutator* and *Ac*/*Ds* serve the same purpose, and the first plant genes were cloned by tagging. In *Arabidopsis* and rice, T-DNA insertions from *Agrobacterium* play the role of the transposon ([7.4.19](/7.4.19)).
- Transformation. P elements carrying a gene of interest between their ends, injected into embryos with a source of transposase, integrate into the germline (Rubin and Spradling, 1982), the first method of making transgenic animals other than mice. *Sleeping Beauty* (a fish transposon reconstructed from inactive copies) and *piggyBac* (from a moth) work in mammalian cells and are used in gene therapy and for delivering CAR genes into T cells ([7.2.24](/7.2.24)).
- Enhancer traps and GAL4 lines. Enhancer-trap insertions carrying the yeast transcription factor gene *GAL4* create thousands of lines that express GAL4 in particular tissues, which can drive any gene placed under a UAS promoter (the GAL4/UAS system, [7.4.19](/7.4.19)).
- Saturating insertion screens. In bacteria, random transposon insertions in a pool of millions of mutants, followed by sequencing of the insertion sites before and after growth under a condition (Tn-seq), measure the fitness contribution of every gene at once: genes with no insertions in the surviving pool are essential under that condition.

## Questions

1. In the model of Formula 17.1.1 with $K = 14$ and a constant excision rate $\varepsilon = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}$, how many single-cell spots and how many spots of $1\,024$ cells are expected per kernel? How many spots in total, and what fraction of the aleurone is pigmented?

   **Answer** Single-cell spots come from excision at the last division ($k = 14$): $\varepsilon 2^{14} = 24.6$. Spots of $1\,024 = 2^{10}$ cells come from $k = 4$: $\varepsilon 2^4 = 0.024$ (one kernel in about 40 has one). Total $\varepsilon(2 + 4 + \dots + 2^{14}) = \varepsilon(2^{15} - 2) \approx 49$ spots. Pigmented fraction $K\varepsilon = 0.021$, i.e. $2.1\,\%$, of which each division contributes the same share.
2. Kernels of a $c^{\mathit{Ds}}$ strain crossed with an *Ac* line have on average $4.2\,\%$ of their aleurone pigmented, and the largest spots contain about $2^{11}$ cells. Estimate $\varepsilon$ (assuming a constant rate over $K = 14$ divisions) and the division at which excisions begin. If a second dose of *Ac* gives the same pigmented fraction but no spot larger than 32 cells, what has changed?

   **Answer** $K\varepsilon = 0.042$ gives $\varepsilon = 3 \times 10^{-3}$. Spots of $2^{11}$ cells come from division $k = 3$, so excision is possible from about the third division on. With two doses the largest spots, $2^5$ cells, mean that excision begins only at division $9$; the same total over six divisions requires a rate of $0.042/6 = 7 \times 10^{-3}$ per division. The timing has changed, not the total amount of excision: the negative dosage effect of *Ac*.
3. A colorless maize kernel of genotype $c^{\mathit{Ds}}/c^{\mathit{Ds}}/c$ (triploid aleurone) is planted. Its progeny from a cross with a line carrying *Ac* show spotted kernels in half of the offspring. Was *Ac* homozygous or heterozygous in the pollen parent? How would you show that the spotting is due to a factor unlinked to *C*?

   **Answer** Half the kernels receiving *Ac* means the pollen parent was heterozygous for *Ac* (or had one copy of an *Ac* not present on its homologue). Testcross a spotted plant with a $c/c$ line without *Ac* and score kernels for spotting and for the alleles at *C*: spotting appears in half of the colorless-background kernels independently of the *C*-linked markers if *Ac* is unlinked, and its own position can be mapped with markers on other chromosomes.
4. Predict the fertility of the offspring of (a) P female × M male, (b) M female × P male, (c) the daughters of cross (b) crossed with M males, if their cytotype is still M-like. Explain using piRNAs.

   **Answer** (a) Fertile: the egg contains maternal piRNAs against P, so the P elements of the offspring (all from the mother) and any from the father are silenced. (b) Dysgenic and largely sterile: paternal P elements enter an egg without P-directed piRNAs, and transpose in the germline. (c) If these daughters are fertile at all, they carry P elements but deposit few piRNAs; their offspring with M males receive P elements from the mother but are only partly protected. Over generations the P copies that land in piRNA clusters make piRNAs, and the line acquires P cytotype: cytotype is inherited maternally but builds up.
5. Using Formula 17.6.1 with $u = 0.01$, $v = 0.001$, $a = 0.001$ and $b = 10^{-4}$, compute $n^*$. If a host mutation in the piRNA pathway raises $u$ to $0.02$, what is the new equilibrium? Why do such mutations make flies sterile long before the new equilibrium is reached?

   **Answer** $n^* = (0.01 - 0.002)/10^{-4} = 80$; with $u = 0.02$, $n^* = 180$. The approach takes many generations, but the immediate effect is a burst of transposition in the germline of every fly, with DNA breaks and insertional mutations that arrest germ cell development: the sterility is caused by transposition itself, not by the eventual copy number.
6. A new transposon enters a genome with one copy, with $u = 0.01$, $v = 0.001$ and $a = 0.001$. Ignoring $b$ at first, approximately how many generations does it take to reach 50 copies? (Use $n(t) = \mathrm{e}^{rt}$ with $r = u - v - a$.)

   **Answer** $r = 0.008$; $\ln 50/0.008 = 3.912/0.008 \approx 490$ generations. With $b = 10^{-4}$ it takes longer, since the growth rate at $n = 50$ is $0.008 - 0.005 = 0.003$; the logistic solution of Formula 17.6.1 gives about $610$ generations to reach $50$ (consistent with the figure).
7. A driving allele is lethal when homozygous and is transmitted with ratio $k = 0.75$ by heterozygotes of both sexes. Find the equilibrium gametic frequency and the carrier fraction among adults. Compare with male-only drive at the same $k$.

   **Answer** $p^* = 2(0.75) - 1 = 0.5$; carriers $(2k - 1)/k = 0.5/0.75 = 0.667$. Male-only drive: $h^* = 1 - \sqrt{0.25/0.75} = 1 - 0.577 = 0.423$.
8. Wild house mice carrying *t* haplotypes are found at about $15\,\%$. Use the male-only formula to find the transmission ratio $k$ that would produce this equilibrium, and compare with the laboratory value of about $0.9$. What does the comparison suggest?

   **Answer** $1 - \sqrt{(1 - k)/k} = 0.15$ gives $(1 - k)/k = 0.7225$, $k = 1/1.7225 = 0.58$. The effective transmission ratio in the wild is far below the laboratory value, which points to factors that reduce the success of $+/t$ males in the wild (sperm competition under polyandry, female choice) or to population structure and drift that the model ignores.
9. A homing element with germline efficiency $e = 0.9$ and no fitness cost is released at a frequency of $1\,\%$. Iterate $p' = p + epq$ to find how many generations it takes to exceed $50\,\%$.

   **Answer** $p$: $0.01 \to 0.0189 \to 0.0356 \to 0.0665 \to 0.122 \to 0.219 \to 0.373 \to 0.584$. Seven generations. An allele with Mendelian inheritance and no advantage would stay at $1\,\%$.
10. About 130 million children are born each year. Estimate the number of new germline Alu insertions per year, and the expected number that land in protein-coding sequence (about $1.2\,\%$ of the genome). Why is the real number of coding insertions lower than this estimate?

    **Answer** At one insertion per 20–40 births, about $3$–$6.5$ million new Alu insertions per year; if insertion were uniform, $1.2\,\%$, about $40\,000$–$80\,000$, would fall in coding sequence. Insertion is not uniform: L1 endonuclease prefers T-rich sites, which are underrepresented in exons, and open chromatin differs between sites; moreover, insertions that disrupt essential genes are lost before birth or remain undetected unless they cause disease.
11. Mendel's wrinkled-seed allele $r$ is an insertion in the gene for starch-branching enzyme I. Explain why $r$ is recessive, why $rr$ seeds are wrinkled and sweet, and why the allele is stable even though it was caused by a transposon.

    **Answer** The insertion disrupts the enzyme, so $rr$ seeds make no branched starch (amylopectin); sugars accumulate, the seeds take up more water during development and shrink and wrinkle on drying. One functional copy makes enough enzyme ($Rr$ seeds are round): the allele is a recessive loss of function ([7.4.2](/7.4.2)). The insertion belongs to an *Ac*/*Ds*-like family, but without an active autonomous element in pea it cannot excise, and the element has probably lost its own ends or transposase; insertions of defective elements are as stable as other mutations.
12. Design a transposon-tagging experiment to clone a maize gene defined by a recessive mutation $m$ for which no molecular information exists, using an active *Ac* line.

    **Answer** Cross plants carrying active *Ac* (in a wild-type $M/M$ background) as pollen parents to $m/m$ females, and screen the progeny for mutant individuals ($m/m^{*}$), where $m^{*}$ is a new mutant allele induced by an *Ac* insertion into $M$ in the germline of the *Ac* parent. Confirm that the new allele is unstable (somatic reversion gives wild-type sectors or revertant progeny in the presence of *Ac*). Then find an *Ac*-hybridizing restriction fragment, or an insertion site identified by sequencing, that cosegregates with $m^{*}$ in the progeny of the mutant, clone the DNA flanking the element, and confirm the gene by showing that independent alleles carry insertions in the same gene or that revertants have lost the element.
