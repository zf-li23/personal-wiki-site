Sponges are sessile, filter-feeding animals without a gut, muscles or nerves. Their body is a system of water canals lined by flagellated collar cells, which drive water through the animal and strain food from it. With about 9,300 described species, almost all marine, they range from millimetre-sized crusts to barrel sponges over a metre across and glass sponges that live for thousands of years. Sponges were once treated as a side branch of the animal kingdom, the "Parazoa", that never reached the level of tissues. We now know that they undergo gastrulation, have epithelia, and share with other animals most of the genes for cell adhesion, signalling and development. On the current animal tree they are either the first or the second lineage to branch off ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)), so the way a sponge works tells us what is possible for an animal built at the simplest grade of construction (the cellular grade of [7.17.2](/7.17.2)).

This chapter describes the cells and skeleton of sponges (§7.2), the aquiferous system and the physics of filter feeding (§7.3), reproduction, larvae and cell plasticity (§7.4), and the four classes (§7.5). It ends with the position of sponges in the animal tree (§7.6), their ecology (§7.7), and a comparison with the classification found in older textbooks (§7.8). The next chapter treats the comb jellies and placozoans, the other non-bilaterian phyla apart from the Cnidaria ([7.17.9](/7.17.9)).

## 7.1. Sponges at a Glance

**Definition 7.1.1 (Porifera)** The **Porifera** (sponges) are sessile, mostly marine metazoans in which water is pumped through a system of pores, canals and chambers by flagellated collar cells (choanocytes); they have no gut, no nervous system and no true muscle cells, and their skeleton, when present, consists of mineral spicules and/or collagenous fibres secreted in a middle layer of motile cells.

The name means "pore bearer". The diagnostic features, which together distinguish sponges from all other animals, are:

- choanocytes and an aquiferous system through which the animal feeds, respires and excretes;
- a mesohyl, a middle layer containing motile, largely pluripotent cells and skeletal elements;
- the absence of a gut, neurons, gap junctions and an anterior–posterior axis in the adult (the larva is polarized; many adults have an apical–basal polarity defined by the osculum);
- a high degree of **pluripotency** of the cells, two of whose lineages (archaeocytes and choanocytes) behave as stem cells.

Sponges share with other animals the defining traits of the **Metazoa**: multicellularity achieved through embryonic layering, cell junctions (adherens junctions are present in some species), actin–myosin contraction, type IV **collagen** and a basement membrane in at least one class, and oogenesis and spermatogenesis of the animal type. About 220 species live in fresh water (family Spongillidae and relatives); the rest are benthic marine animals found from the intertidal zone to the abyss, most abundant on reefs and rocky coasts.

## 7.2. Cells and Skeleton

### 7.2.1. The Cell Layers

A sponge wall has three layers: an outer *pinacoderm*, an inner *choanoderm* lining the flagellated chambers or the central cavity, and between them the mesohyl. The outer layer consists of flat *pinacocytes*. Pinacocytes that line the outer surface are exopinacocytes; those lining the internal canals are endopinacocytes; those at the base secrete collagen and attach the sponge to the substrate. In calcareous sponges with the simplest construction, single tubular *porocytes* span the wall and form the incurrent pores (ostia).

**Definition 7.2.1 (Choanocyte)** A **choanocyte** (collar cell) is a sponge cell bearing a single flagellum surrounded by a collar of microvilli linked by a fine mucous mesh. The beating flagellum drives water through the collar, which retains food particles; the particles are then taken up by phagocytosis.

Choanocytes are the pumps and the filters of the sponge. Their structure is so similar to that of choanoflagellates that this resemblance was the classical argument for **choanoflagellates** being the closest relatives of animals, a relationship now confirmed by phylogenomics ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)). The similarity is not proof of homology between the two cell types, however: single-cell transcriptomes show that sponge choanocytes and choanoflagellates express different sets of genes, and some biologists regard the collar–flagellum unit as a design that arose more than once.

**Definition 7.2.2 (Mesohyl)** The **mesohyl** is the middle layer of a sponge, between pinacoderm and choanoderm: a collagenous extracellular matrix containing motile amoeboid cells, skeletal elements and often symbiotic microorganisms.

The mesohyl is not merely a jelly. It is the sponge's connective tissue and contains several cell types:

| Cell | Function |
|---|---|
| Archaeocytes | Large amoeboid cells with a prominent nucleolus; digest food passed on by choanocytes, transport nutrients, store reserves, give rise to gametes and to every other cell type (totipotent stem cells) |
| Sclerocytes | Secrete spicules |
| Spongocytes | Secrete spongin fibres |
| Collencytes and lophocytes | Secrete dispersed collagen fibrils |
| Myocytes | Contractile cells around oscula and large canals |
| Gland and pigment cells | Mucus, toxins, pigments |

The mesohyl of sponges is called "mesoglea" in some older texts, but it is not equivalent to the **mesoglea** of cnidarians and ctenophores: it is full of migrating cells and is produced by them, not secreted by two epithelia that face each other.

### 7.2.2. Spicules and Spongin

**Definition 7.2.3 (Spicule)** A **spicule** is a skeletal element of a sponge, secreted by sclerocytes, made either of calcium carbonate (calcite, in Calcarea) or of hydrated amorphous silica (biosilica, in the other classes).

Siliceous spicules are deposited around an axial filament of the enzyme silicatein, which polymerizes silicic acid from seawater; calcareous spicules have no axial filament and are deposited within a collagen sheath by two or more cooperating cells. Spicules are classified by size and by the number of axes and rays:

| Category | Description | Typical of |
|---|---|---|
| Megascleres | Large structural spicules (0.1 mm to several cm, up to 3 m in the anchoring spicules of *Monorhaphis*) | All spicule-bearing sponges |
| Microscleres | Small spicules (10–100 µm) scattered in the mesohyl | Demospongiae, Hexactinellida |
| Monaxons | One axis (straight or curved rods, pointed at one or both ends) | Demospongiae, Calcarea |
| Triactines and tetractines | Three or four rays | Calcarea, Homoscleromorpha |
| Tetraxons | Four rays not in one plane | Demospongiae, Homoscleromorpha |
| Triaxons (hexactines) | Three perpendicular axes, six rays | Hexactinellida only |

In many demosponges the spicules are cemented together, or entirely replaced, by *spongin*, a flexible collagen rich in iodine and bromine. Bath sponges (*Spongia*, *Hippospongia*) have a skeleton of spongin fibres alone. Spicule shape and arrangement remain the main characters in sponge taxonomy at the species level, although molecular phylogenies have shown that many spicule types evolved more than once.

The glass sponges show how much a few spicules can achieve. In the Venus's flower basket *Euplectella*, spicules are fused into a lattice with diagonal bracing, and individual spicules are built of concentric layers of silica separated by thin organic layers. This laminated structure stops cracks from spreading and makes the spicules much tougher than solid glass. The basal spicules also conduct light like optical fibres.

### 7.2.3. Do Sponges Have Tissues?

Traditional textbooks describe sponges as animals "at the cellular level" without true tissues. The distinction has become blurred. The pinacoderm and choanoderm are **epithelial tissue** in the functional sense: they separate the internal medium from seawater, and in homoscleromorphs they rest on a type IV collagen **basement membrane** and are joined by **adherens junctions**, exactly as in other animals. Freshwater sponges have sealing junctions that maintain a transepithelial electrical resistance. What sponges lack is a stable commitment of cells to tissues: choanocytes and archaeocytes can transform into other cell types, and the whole sponge continuously remodels itself. It is therefore more accurate to say that sponges have simple, plastic tissues than that they have none.

## 7.3. The Aquiferous System and Filter Feeding

### 7.3.1. Three Grades of Construction

**Definition 7.3.1 (Aquiferous System)** The **aquiferous system** (canal system) of a sponge is the network of incurrent pores (ostia), canals, flagellated chambers or cavities lined by choanocytes, and excurrent openings (oscula) through which choanocytes drive water.

Water always follows the same sequence: in through many small ostia, past the choanocytes, and out through one or a few large oscula. Three classical grades of construction differ in where the choanocytes sit, as shown in the figure below:

| Type | Path of water | Choanocytes | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asconoid | Ostia (porocytes) → spongocoel → osculum | Line the whole central cavity (spongocoel) | *Leucosolenia*, *Clathrina* (Calcarea) |
| Syconoid | Incurrent pores → incurrent canals → prosopyles → radial canals → apopyles → spongocoel → osculum | Line the radial canals, which are evaginations of the wall | *Sycon*, *Grantia* (Calcarea) |
| Leuconoid | Ostia → incurrent canals → prosopyles → flagellated chambers → apopyles → excurrent canals → atrium → osculum | Line small spherical chambers embedded in a thick mesohyl | Most sponges: all Demospongiae and Hexactinellida, many Calcarea |

![The three grades of the sponge aquiferous system and the cells of the sponge wall. (A) Longitudinal sections: in the asconoid grade choanocytes (red) line the central spongocoel; in the syconoid grade the wall is folded into radial canals lined by choanocytes; in the leuconoid grade choanocytes are confined to small chambers within a thick mesohyl, fed and drained by branching canals. Arrows show the direction of water flow, from the ostia to the osculum. (B) The wall in section (inside on the left): choanocytes, the mesohyl with amoebocytes (archaeocytes), a sclerocyte (scleroblast) secreting a spicule, an oocyte, a porocyte forming an ostium, and the outer pinacocytes. (C) A choanocyte: a collar of microvilli around a single flagellum.](./plots/sponge-body-plans.py)

The series asconoid → syconoid → leuconoid is a series of increasing folding, not a phylogenetic sequence. All three occur within the Calcarea, and leuconoid construction has evolved independently several times. Its significance is geometric. In an asconoid tube the choanocyte surface grows only in proportion to the area of the wall, while the volume of water in the spongocoel, which must be expelled through a single osculum, grows faster; an asconoid sponge therefore cannot exceed a few millimetres in diameter. In a leuconoid sponge the number of chambers increases in proportion to the volume of the mesohyl, so pumping capacity keeps pace with body size. All large sponges are leuconoid. Two further constructions, the solenoid (anastomosing tubes in *Leucascus*) and the sylleibid (chambers arranged around invaginations of the atrium, in some calcareans and homoscleromorphs), are variations on the same principle.

### 7.3.2. The Hydrodynamics of Flow

Water is effectively incompressible, so in a steady state the same volume of water passes every cross-section of the canal system per unit time. If $Q$ is the volume flow rate, $A_k$ the total cross-sectional area of all parallel channels at stage $k$ (all ostia, all incurrent canals, all chambers, ...) and $v_k$ the mean speed at that stage, then

$$
Q = A_k v_k \quad\text{for every } k, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{v_k}{v_{\mathrm{osc}}} = \frac{A_{\mathrm{osc}}}{A_k}. \tag{7.1}
$$

Each choanocyte chamber is small, but there are so many of them that their combined cross-section is hundreds to thousands of times larger than that of the osculum. The water therefore slows down as it spreads through the branching incurrent canals, is slowest in the chambers, and accelerates again as the excurrent canals merge and leave through the osculum, as the figure below shows. Both ends of this profile are adaptive. Slow flow at the collars gives time for particles to be captured. A fast oscular jet, typically several centimetres per second, carries filtered water and wastes away from the sponge, so that it does not refilter its own exhalant water.

![The principle of continuity in the aquiferous system of a leuconoid sponge (schematic; the relative areas are illustrative, not measurements of a particular species). Bars: total cross-sectional area of each stage relative to the osculum (log scale). Line: mean flow speed relative to the oscular speed, the reciprocal of the area because the volume flow $Q$ is the same at every stage (Eq. (7.1)). The water is slowest in the choanocyte chambers, where the total cross-section is largest, and fastest at the osculum.](./plots/canal-flow.py)

At the scale of a choanocyte, about 5–10 µm, and at speeds of tens of micrometres per second, the **Reynolds number** is far below 1. Viscous forces dominate, inertia is negligible, and a particle stops the instant the water stops pushing it ([7.14.2](/7.14.2#sec-2.3)). The collar does not work as a sieve that intercepts particles by momentum but as a filter through which the flagellum draws water by viscous pumping. The flagellum of a sponge choanocyte has a lateral vane that increases its thrust. The pressure the pumps generate is small, a few pascals, and the canal system is built to minimize resistance.

Sponges also use the ambient current. Water flowing past a tall osculum is faster than water near the bottom, where the ostia lie. By Bernoulli's principle the pressure is lower at the osculum, and this pressure difference draws water through the sponge even when the choanocytes are inactive (Vogel 1977). Many sponges orient their oscula to exploit this passive flow.

**Example 7.3.1** Suppose the osculum of a sponge has a diameter of $1\,\mathrm{cm}$ and the oscular jet has a speed of $5\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$. Estimate the flow rate, and the speed through the choanocyte chambers if their combined cross-section is $1000$ times that of the osculum.

The oscular area is $A_{\mathrm{osc}} = \pi (0.5\,\mathrm{cm})^2 \approx 0.79\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, so $Q = A_{\mathrm{osc}} v_{\mathrm{osc}} \approx 3.9\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}$, about $14\,\mathrm{L\,h^{-1}}$. By Eq. (7.1) the mean speed in the chambers is $v = 5\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}/1000 = 50\,\mathrm{\mu m\,s^{-1}}$. A sponge of $1\,\mathrm{L}$ pumping at this rate passes its own volume of water every four to five minutes. Measured pumping rates range from 0.002 to 0.84 mL of water per second per cubic centimetre of sponge, so the most active sponges filter their own volume every 10–20 s.

### 7.3.3. Feeding, Respiration and Excretion

Sponges are suspension feeders that take very small particles. Particles of 0.1–2 µm, mostly bacteria and picoplankton, are trapped on the collar microvilli and phagocytosed by choanocytes. Larger particles, up to about 50 µm, are taken up by pinacocytes and archaeocytes lining the incurrent canals, because they cannot pass the small prosopyles. Sponges remove up to 99% of the bacteria from the water they pump and also absorb dissolved organic matter, which in many reef sponges supplies most of their carbon. Digestion is entirely intracellular: food vacuoles formed in choanocytes are passed to archaeocytes, which digest them and carry nutrients through the mesohyl. In glass sponges a single syncytium distributes the products by cytoplasmic streaming.

The sponge has no respiratory or excretory organs. Each cell exchanges oxygen, carbon dioxide and ammonia with the water flowing past it. Freshwater sponges, which live in a hypo-osmotic medium, have contractile vacuoles in their choanocytes and pinacocytes, as freshwater protists do.

Some sponges have abandoned filter feeding. About 150 species, most in the deep-sea family Cladorhizidae, are carnivores. They have lost the aquiferous system and capture small crustaceans on filaments covered with hook-shaped microscleres; archaeocytes migrate to the prey, engulf it and digest it within a few days.

### 7.3.4. Coordination without Neurons

Sponges have no neurons, yet they behave. A sponge can close its ostia and oscula, slow its pumping when the water is full of sediment, and perform slow whole-body contractions, called "sneezes", that expel clogging material from the canals. In cellular sponges these responses are propagated slowly, at speeds below 0.04 cm s⁻¹, by contraction of pinacocytes and by diffusing signals such as glutamate, GABA and nitric oxide. The primary cilia that line the oscula of freshwater sponges act as flow sensors, the only sensory organ identified in sponges (Ludeman et al. 2014).

Glass sponges are an exception. Their trabecular syncytium conducts electrical impulses at about 0.3 cm s⁻¹, and an impulse arrests the flagella of the whole sponge within seconds (Leys & Mackie 1997). This is the fastest response of any sponge, and it is possible because the tissue is a continuous cytoplasm, not because it contains nerves.

The genome of sponges contains many genes of the postsynaptic density, the molecular scaffold of the receiving side of a synapse, but no synapses. In the freshwater sponge *Spongilla lacustris* single-cell transcriptomes have revealed "neuroid" cells that express part of this toolkit and extend processes to the choanocyte chambers (Musser et al. 2021). Such cells may show what the cellular precursors of neurons were like; the origin of nervous systems is discussed in [7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.1).

## 7.4. Reproduction and Development

### 7.4.1. Asexual Reproduction and Gemmules

Sponges reproduce asexually by fragmentation, when pieces broken off by waves or predators reattach and grow, and by budding, in which buds either separate or remain attached and give rise to a branching individual. Many freshwater and some marine sponges also form resistant asexual bodies.

**Definition 7.4.1 (Gemmule)** A **gemmule** is an asexual resting body of a sponge: a cluster of archaeocytes packed with yolk reserves and enclosed in a protective coat of collagen, usually reinforced with spicules, with an opening (micropyle) through which the cells emerge when conditions improve.

Gemmules are formed in autumn or before a dry season, survive freezing and drying when the parent sponge dies, and hatch in spring: the archaeocytes stream out of the micropyle and differentiate into all the cell types of a new sponge. Archaeocytes in a gemmule are arrested in a dormant state, much like seeds or diapausing insect eggs, and are released from dormancy by low temperature or rewetting. The spicules of the gemmule coat (amphidiscs in *Ephydatia*, for example) are important taxonomic characters of freshwater sponges.

### 7.4.2. Sexual Reproduction

Most sponges are hermaphrodites that produce eggs and sperm at different times, which favours cross-fertilization. Sperm develop from choanocytes, which lose their collars, or from transformed choanocyte chambers; oocytes develop from archaeocytes (or, in Calcarea, also from choanocytes) and remain in the mesohyl. Sperm are released through the osculum and drawn into another sponge with the inhalant current. In many calcareans a choanocyte captures a sperm, loses its collar and flagellum, migrates to the oocyte as a carrier cell, and transfers the sperm nucleus into the egg. Many demosponges and all calcareans and homoscleromorphs studied are viviparous: they brood their embryos in the mesohyl and release swimming larvae. Other sponges are oviparous and spawn eggs into the water, sometimes in spectacular synchronized "smoking" events on reefs.

### 7.4.3. Larvae and the "Inversion" Problem

Sponge larvae are lecithotrophic: they live on their yolk, swim for hours to days, settle on a suitable surface and metamorphose. They have an anterior–posterior axis, pigment spots and sometimes a ring of long cilia that serves for phototaxis. The larval types correspond to the classes:

| Larva | Structure | Class |
|---|---|---|
| Parenchymella | Solid; flagellated outer cells around an inner mass of cells | Most Demospongiae |
| Calciblastula | Hollow, entirely flagellated | Calcarea: Calcinea |
| Amphiblastula | Hollow; flagellated anterior half, unflagellated posterior half | Calcarea: Calcaronea |
| Trichimella | Solid, with a belt of multiciliated cells | Hexactinellida |
| Cinctoblastula | Hollow, uniformly flagellated epithelium on a basement membrane, with a posterior belt of distinct cells | Homoscleromorpha |

**Definition 7.4.2 (Amphiblastula)** The **amphiblastula** is the larva of calcaronean sponges: a hollow blastula whose anterior half consists of small flagellated cells and whose posterior half consists of large unflagellated granular cells.

The development of the amphiblastula in *Sycon* contains a curious step. The embryo first forms a hollow ball inside the parent, with the flagella of its small cells pointing into the cavity. An opening then forms among the large cells, and the embryo turns itself inside out through it, so that the flagella come to face outward. This inversion (excurvation) is reminiscent of the inversion of *Volvox* colonies ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5.2)). After the free-swimming larva settles, the flagellated cells move inward and become choanocytes, while the large cells overgrow them and form the pinacoderm.

Because the flagellated cells, which are on the outside of the larva, end up inside the adult, nineteenth-century zoologists spoke of a "reversal of the germ layers": in other animals the outer layer of the larva becomes the epidermis. This interpretation assumed that the choanoderm was equivalent to endoderm and the pinacoderm to ectoderm. Recent work on cell lineages and gene expression does not support a simple equivalence. Sponges have their own type of gastrulation, at the stage of cleavage or of metamorphosis depending on the group, and the comparison of sponge cell layers with the germ layers of other animals remains unsettled. What is clear is that sponges do have an embryonic stage in which cells separate into layers, the defining feature of the **Metazoa**.

### 7.4.4. Cell Plasticity and Reaggregation

In 1907 H. V. Wilson pressed sponges through fine silk cloth, dissociating them into single cells and small clumps. Within hours the cells crawled together, sorted out and within days reconstituted functional sponges with canals and chambers. When the cells of two species of different colours were mixed, each species reaggregated with its own kind. The species-specific adhesion is mediated by a large extracellular proteoglycan, the aggregation factor, and its cell-surface receptor. These experiments founded the study of cell recognition and adhesion and were among the first to show self-organization of a multicellular body from dissociated cells.

The same plasticity allows sponges to regenerate almost any part (**regeneration**, see [7.5.35](/7.5.35)), to fuse with genetically identical neighbours while rejecting non-identical ones (an allorecognition system), and to convert choanocytes into archaeocytes and back. In some species choanocytes divide every five to six hours and are shed almost as fast, one of the fastest rates of cell turnover known in an animal (De Goeij et al. 2009). This continuous turnover explains how sponges keep their filter working in water full of particles and bacteria.

## 7.5. Classification

Molecular phylogenies support four classes, arranged in two clades: Calcarea + Homoscleromorpha, and Hexactinellida + Demospongiae (the "Silicea" in the narrow sense). Because the siliceous Homoscleromorpha are the sister group of the calcareous Calcarea, siliceous spicules either evolved twice (in Homoscleromorpha and in Silicea) or were present in the ancestral sponge and replaced by calcite in Calcarea; the spicules of the two siliceous lineages differ in shape and development, which favours independent origins.

| Class | Skeleton | Construction | Larva | Species (described) | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calcarea (calcareous sponges) | Calcite spicules (1-, 3- or 4-rayed), no axial filament | Asconoid, syconoid, leuconoid, solenoid, sylleibid | Calciblastula (Calcinea), amphiblastula (Calcaronea) | ~790 | *Leucosolenia*, *Clathrina*, *Sycon*, *Grantia* |
| Hexactinellida (glass sponges) | Siliceous hexactines, often fused into a rigid lattice; spongin absent | Leuconoid, the soft tissue a syncytium | Trichimella | ~680 | *Euplectella*, *Hyalonema*, *Monorhaphis* |
| Demospongiae (demosponges) | Siliceous spicules (not hexactines) and/or spongin, or none | Leuconoid | Parenchymella (most) | ~7,400 (81% of species) | *Spongia*, *Spongilla*, *Tethya*, *Cliona*, *Chondrosia* |
| Homoscleromorpha | Small tetraxon siliceous spicules (calthrops) all of one size, or none | Sylleibid or leuconoid; epithelia with basement membrane | Cinctoblastula | ~120 | *Oscarella*, *Plakina* |

Within the classes:

- Calcarea has two subclasses distinguished by larva and by choanocyte structure: Calcinea (calciblastula, nucleus basal in the choanocyte) and Calcaronea (amphiblastula, nucleus apical, flagellum arising directly from the nucleus). All calcareans are marine, mostly in shallow water.
- Hexactinellida are almost all deep-sea animals, most common at 300–600 m, with subclasses Amphidiscophora (anchored in soft sediment by a tuft of long spicules, as in *Hyalonema*) and Hexasterophora (attached to hard substrates, as in *Euplectella*). Their soft tissue is a multinucleate syncytium in which the choanocytes are replaced by collar bodies budded from a few nucleated choanoblasts. The difference is so great that the glass sponges were once proposed as a separate subphylum ("Symplasma"). Some deep-sea hexactinellids are among the oldest animals known: a specimen of *Monorhaphis chuni* was estimated to be about 11,000 years old from the growth layers of its giant spicule.
- Demospongiae comprise three subclasses: Keratosa (spongin skeleton only, including bath sponges), Verongimorpha (no skeleton or spongin fibres with a pith) and Heteroscleromorpha (siliceous spicules, often with spongin; more than 5,000 species, including the freshwater sponges, the boring sponges *Cliona*, and the carnivorous cladorhizids). The older division into Tetractinomorpha and Ceractinomorpha by mode of reproduction is polyphyletic and has been abandoned. The "coralline sponges" (formerly Sclerospongiae), which deposit a solid calcareous base under a demosponge or calcarean tissue, are the survivors of Paleozoic and Mesozoic reef builders and belong to these two classes.
- Homoscleromorpha were long included in the Demospongiae and were raised to class rank in 2012 (Gazave et al.). Their epithelia with a basement membrane and adherens junctions are the most "eumetazoan-like" of any sponge.

## 7.6. Sponges in the Animal Tree

For two decades it was debated whether sponges form a clade or a grade. Early rRNA trees placed Calcarea closer to the other animals than to demosponges, which would have made the sponges paraphyletic and implied that the ancestor of all animals was itself a sponge. Phylogenomic data have firmly established that Porifera is monophyletic.

The remaining question is whether sponges or ctenophores are the sister group of all other animals. Most phylogenomic analyses with well-fitting models favour ctenophores branching first, and the strongest evidence so far comes from the arrangement of genes on chromosomes: sponges, placozoans, cnidarians and bilaterians share chromosome fusions, followed by mixing of the fused gene sets, that ctenophores lack (Schultz et al. 2023). If this is correct, the simplicity of sponges is partly secondary: sponges would have lost neurons and muscles that were present in the ancestor of all animals, or the nervous systems of ctenophores and of other animals evolved independently. The alternative, sponges first, is discussed with the rest of the animal tree in [7.17.6](/7.17.6).

The fossil record of sponges begins securely in the early Cambrian, about 535 million years ago, with hexactinellid spicules and whole-body fossils such as those of the Chengjiang and Burgess Shale faunas. Older claims rest on Ediacaran spicule-like structures and on 24-isopropylcholestane, a sterane found in rocks over 635 million years old that was interpreted as a demosponge biomarker; whether such molecules can also be made by algae is disputed. The archaeocyaths, cup-shaped calcareous fossils that built the first animal reefs in the early Cambrian, were very probably sponges.

## 7.7. Ecology and Uses

Sponges are major components of reef and deep-sea communities. Glass sponges form reefs up to 20 m high off British Columbia, a habitat thought extinct since the Jurassic until its discovery in 1987. Boring sponges of the family Clionaidae etch galleries into coral skeletons and mollusc shells with acid secreted by specialized cells, removing chips of carbonate; they are among the main agents of reef bioerosion.

Many sponges harbour dense communities of bacteria and archaea in the mesohyl, up to 35–40% of the sponge's volume in "high microbial abundance" sponges, along with cyanobacteria and dinoflagellates in species of well-lit water. The symbionts contribute to nitrogen fixation, nitrification, photosynthesis and the production of defensive compounds, and the sponge with its microbes is studied as a **holobiont** ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)).

On coral reefs much of the primary production is released as dissolved organic matter, which most animals cannot use. Sponges absorb it, convert it into cells, and shed choanocytes and other cells as detritus at a high rate; the detritus is eaten by snails, crustaceans and worms. This "sponge loop" (de Goeij et al. 2013) returns energy and nutrients to the reef food web and helps explain how reefs thrive in nutrient-poor tropical water.

Being sessile and soft, sponges defend themselves chemically. They are the richest animal source of bioactive natural products. Nucleosides from the Caribbean sponge *Tectitethya crypta* (spongothymidine and spongouridine) led to the synthetic antiviral vidarabine and the anticancer drug cytarabine; halichondrin B from the demosponge *Halichondria okadai* led to the anticancer drug eribulin. Sponges are also eaten by hawksbill turtles, angelfishes and nudibranchs, some of which recycle sponge toxins for their own defence.

The atrium of *Euplectella* often houses a pair of shrimps (*Spongicola*) that enter as juveniles and grow too large to leave; in Japan the sponge is a traditional wedding gift symbolizing lifelong fidelity. The skeletons of bath sponges have been harvested since antiquity and are now also farmed.

## 7.8. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

Older textbooks, including the standard Chinese ones, differ from the account above mainly in the points below; the facts are the same, but some interpretations have changed.

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Sponges form a side branch, the "Parazoa", that never evolved tissues | Sponges are metazoans with gastrulation and simple, plastic epithelia; "Parazoa" is abandoned |
| Three classes: Calcarea, Hexactinellida, Demospongiae | Four classes; Homoscleromorpha (e.g. *Oscarella*, formerly a demosponge) separated in 2012 |
| The middle layer is called mesoglea | The mesohyl, a cellular connective tissue not equivalent to the cnidarian mesoglea |
| Collencytes or stellate cells may be primitive nerve cells | No neurons; conduction is non-nervous, though "neuroid" cells with synaptic genes exist |
| Inversion of the amphiblastula as a "reversal of germ layers" | Sponge cell layers cannot be simply equated with ectoderm and endoderm |
| Asconoid → syconoid → leuconoid as an evolutionary series | Grades of construction that evolved repeatedly; all three in Calcarea |
| About 10,000 species | About 9,300 described; perhaps half the living species still undescribed |

## Summary

- Sponges (Definition 7.1.1) are sessile metazoans at the simplest grade of construction: a pinacoderm, a choanoderm of choanocytes (Definition 7.2.1) and a cellular mesohyl (Definition 7.2.2) with archaeocytes, sclerocytes and skeletal elements; no gut, nerves or true muscles.
- The skeleton consists of calcite or silica spicules (Definition 7.2.3) and/or spongin. Hexactines are diagnostic of glass sponges.
- The aquiferous system (Definition 7.3.1) is asconoid, syconoid or leuconoid. Leuconoid construction lets the pumping surface scale with volume and is found in all large sponges.
- By continuity (Eq. (7.1)), flow is slowest in the choanocyte chambers, which have the largest total cross-section, and fastest at the osculum; collars filter at very low Reynolds number. Ambient currents add passive flow.
- Sponges feed on bacteria and dissolved organic matter with intracellular digestion; carnivorous cladorhizids have lost the aquiferous system. They coordinate behaviour without neurons; glass sponges conduct electrical impulses through a syncytium.
- Asexual reproduction by fragmentation, budding and gemmules (Definition 7.4.1); sexual reproduction mostly hermaphroditic and viviparous, with class-specific larvae (parenchymella, calciblastula, amphiblastula (Definition 7.4.2), trichimella, cinctoblastula).
- Dissociated sponge cells reaggregate species-specifically; choanocytes and archaeocytes are stem cells, and sponges regenerate readily.
- Four classes: Calcarea, Hexactinellida, Demospongiae, Homoscleromorpha. Porifera is monophyletic; whether sponges or ctenophores branched first is still debated, with recent gene-order data favouring ctenophores.
- Sponges build reefs, erode carbonate, host dense microbial symbioses, recycle dissolved organic matter through the sponge loop, and are a major source of drugs.

## Questions

1. Why can an asconoid sponge not grow large, while a leuconoid sponge can?

   **Answer** In an asconoid sponge the choanocytes line the spongocoel, so the pumping surface grows with the area of the wall ($\propto L^2$), while the volume of water in the spongocoel that must be moved through the single osculum grows as $L^3$; the area available per unit volume falls as $1/L$, and the flow becomes too sluggish to feed and ventilate the animal. Folding the wall into radial canals (syconoid) postpones the problem; placing choanocytes in chambers distributed throughout a thick mesohyl (leuconoid) makes the number of chambers proportional to the volume, so pumping capacity scales with body size.
2. The oscular jet of a sponge has a speed of $10\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$ and the speed through the choanocyte chambers is $20\,\mathrm{\mu m\,s^{-1}}$. What is the ratio of the total cross-section of the chambers to the area of the osculum? Why is each extreme useful to the sponge?

   **Answer** By continuity, $A_{\mathrm{ch}}/A_{\mathrm{osc}} = v_{\mathrm{osc}}/v_{\mathrm{ch}} = 10\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}/(20 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}) = 5000$. Slow flow at the collars gives time to capture particles and exchange gases; the fast jet throws used water well clear of the sponge so that it is not drawn back into the ostia.
3. In what sense is the "reversal of germ layers" in *Sycon* a misnomer?

   **Answer** The idea assumes that the flagellated cells of the larva correspond to ectoderm and the choanocytes they become to endoderm. Sponge cell layers cannot be equated with the germ layers of eumetazoans: there is no gut, and lineage tracing and gene expression do not show a one-to-one correspondence. What happens is that the cells of an embryo rearrange into the two adult layers, a form of gastrulation peculiar to sponges, together with an earlier inversion of the embryo that brings the flagella to the outside.
4. Sponges have no neurons. How, then, does a glass sponge stop its pumping within seconds of being disturbed, and what does the existence of "neuroid" cells in *Spongilla* suggest?

   **Answer** The soft tissue of glass sponges is a continuous syncytium. An electrical impulse propagates through it at about $0.3\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$ and arrests the flagella of all collar bodies; no specialized conducting cells are needed. Neuroid cells that express postsynaptic genes and contact the choanocyte chambers suggest that parts of the synaptic machinery, and perhaps cells that regulate other cells by secretion, existed before neurons. Whether they are the precursors of neurons or a sponge-specific innovation is not known.
