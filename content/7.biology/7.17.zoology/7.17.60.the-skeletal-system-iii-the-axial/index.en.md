The axial skeleton is the skeleton of the body axis: the notochord and the vertebral column that replaces it, the ribs, the sternum and, in some reptiles, the gastralia. With the skull, which caps it in front, it is the oldest part of the vertebrate endoskeleton and gives the group its name. It has three functions that pull in different directions: it must be stiff enough to resist the compression produced by the trunk muscles and to support the body against gravity, flexible enough to bend in swimming, walking and turning the head, and strong enough to protect the spinal cord and, through the ribs, the heart and lungs.

The vertebral column is a serially repeated structure, and its history is one of differentiation of the series. In a fish there are only trunk and tail vertebrae; in a mammal five regions with different shapes and functions, two specialized vertebrae at the front that carry and turn the head, and a sacrum that transmits the weight of the body to the hind limbs. The regional identity of vertebrae is specified by the **Hox genes** expressed in the somites ([7.5.23](/7.5.23#sec-23.9)), and the Hox code provides the clearest example of how the serial homology of a skeletal series ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2.1)) is established and modified.

Section 60.3 compares the vertebral columns of fishes, the shapes of centra and the vertebrae of early tetrapods; §60.4 treats regionalization, the atlas–axis complex and the Hox code, with the constancy of the mammalian neck. Ribs, sternum and gastralia follow in §60.5, the functional morphology of the column in §60.6, and a comparison across the classes in §60.7. The muscles of the axis are treated in [7.17.62](/7.17.62), the somites and their segmentation in [7.5.23](/7.5.23) and the formation of vertebrae in [7.5.24](/7.5.24#sec-24.8).

## 60.1. Notochord and Vertebrae

### 60.1.1. The Notochord

The **notochord** ([7.17.43](/7.17.43)) is the first axial skeleton of every chordate. It is a rod of large vacuolated cells enclosed in a thick sheath of collagen and elastic fibres; the pressure of the fluid in the vacuoles against the inextensible sheath makes the rod stiff in compression but flexible in bending, a hydrostatic skeleton in the sense of [7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.2.1). In lancelets the notochord contains muscle cells whose contraction stiffens it further. It extends from the tail to the midbrain, below the neural tube and above the gut, and it acts in the embryo as a signalling centre that induces the floor plate of the neural tube and the sclerotome of the somites.

The notochord persists throughout life as the main axial support in cyclostomes, sturgeons and paddlefishes, lungfishes and coelacanths, all of which have vertebral arches but no centra or only small ones. In most other vertebrates the notochord is constricted or replaced by the centra: remnants persist between the vertebrae in fishes and amphibians, and in mammals as the gelatinous nucleus pulposus at the centre of each intervertebral disc.

### 60.1.2. The Parts of a Vertebra

A vertebra consists of a spool-shaped body below the spinal cord and arches above and, in the tail, below it.

**Definition 60.1.1 (Centrum)** The **centrum** (vertebral body) is the cylindrical or spool-shaped body of a vertebra that surrounds or replaces the notochord; the centra form the weight-bearing column of the vertebral column, and their articular faces with neighbouring centra are either flat, concave or convex.

The other parts of a vertebra, which are present in most vertebrates, are:

- the *neural arch* (dorsal arch), which encloses the spinal cord in the vertebral canal and is topped by a *neural spine*;
- the *haemal arch* (ventral arch) in the tail, which encloses the caudal artery and vein and continues in a *haemal spine*; in amniotes the haemal arches are separate Y-shaped *chevron bones* attached between successive caudal centra;
- *zygapophyses*, paired articular processes of the neural arch, a front pair (prezygapophyses) facing up or inward and a back pair (postzygapophyses) facing down or outward, which overlap those of the neighbouring vertebrae and guide and limit their movement;
- *transverse processes* projecting laterally, which in tetrapods carry the ribs: the *diapophysis* on the neural arch articulates with the tubercle of a two-headed rib, the *parapophysis* on the centrum with its head;
- additional processes in particular groups: the *zygosphene* and *zygantrum*, an extra articulation above the zygapophyses in snakes and some lizards; the *hypapophysis*, a ventral keel on the cervical centra of many reptiles and birds; the *metapophysis* (mammillary process) and *anapophysis* (accessory process) of mammalian lumbar vertebrae.

## 60.2. Development

### 60.2.1. Sclerotome and Resegmentation

Each **somite** of the trunk divides into a dorsolateral **dermomyotome**, which forms the muscles and the dermis of the back, and a ventromedial **sclerotome**, whose cells migrate around the notochord and the neural tube and form the vertebrae and ribs ([7.5.23](/7.5.23#sec-23.10)). The sclerotome of each somite is divided into an anterior (cranial) half of loosely packed cells and a posterior (caudal) half of densely packed cells; the motor axons and the neural crest cells that form the dorsal root ganglia pass only through the anterior halves.

**Resegmentation** ([7.5.24](/7.5.24#sec-24.8)) then recombines the halves: the posterior half of one sclerotome joins the anterior half of the next to form one vertebra. Each vertebra is therefore offset by half a segment from the somites, and each myotome, which remains in the position of its somite, spans two vertebrae and attaches to both; contraction of a myotome bends the column at the joint between the two. The spinal nerves, which grew out through the anterior sclerotome halves, emerge between successive vertebrae, and the intervertebral discs form at the level of the middle of each somite.

Resegmentation explains why the number of vertebrae equals the number of trunk somites even though no vertebra corresponds to a single somite, and why there are eight cervical spinal nerves but seven cervical vertebrae in mammals: the first nerve emerges between the skull and the atlas. The Chinese textbooks note that the existence of resegmentation was disputed in the twentieth century (Baur 1969, Verbout 1974); quail–chick chimaeras and lineage tracing in the chick and mouse have since confirmed it in amniotes, while in teleosts the centra form partly from the notochord sheath without a comparable recombination, so resegmentation may not apply in the same way to all vertebrates.

### 60.2.2. Arch Elements and the Centrum

The vertebrae of fishes develop from several pairs of separate cartilages, the *arcualia*, around the notochord. Classical comparative anatomy (Gadow 1933) recognized in each segment four pairs: basidorsals and basiventrals, in the posterior part of each sclerotome, and interdorsals and interventrals, in the anterior part. The basidorsals form the neural arch, the basiventrals the haemal arch (in the tail) or the bases of the ribs (in the trunk); the interdorsals and interventrals form intercalary arches between successive vertebrae in chondrichthyans and sturgeons, or contribute to the centrum, or disappear.

The centrum can form in three ways: by ossification of the notochordal sheath itself (chordacentrum, in teleosts and some chondrichthyans), by chondrification and ossification of mesenchyme around the notochord (perichordal centrum, in tetrapods), or by expansion of the bases of the arch elements (arcocentrum, in chondrichthyans and many fishes). The proportions vary between groups, and the centra of different groups are therefore not homologous in every detail. The order of appearance in development, neural arches first, then haemal arches, then centra, parallels the order of their appearance in evolution: lampreys have only small neural arch elements, sturgeons neural and haemal arches without centra, and most other gnathostomes complete vertebrae.

The vertebral column of fishes has only two regions. Trunk vertebrae carry ribs and lack haemal arches; caudal vertebrae have haemal arches, and in teleosts the last few vertebrae are modified into the hypural plates and other elements that support the rays of the tail fin. Because the head is attached to the shoulder girdle, fishes have no neck, and because the pelvic fins are not attached to the column, no sacrum.

### 60.3.2. The Shapes of Centra

The articular faces of successive centra can be concave, convex or flat. The shape determines how freely the joints can move and how much of the notochord remains between them; it is also a classical character in the classification of reptiles and amphibians.

**Definition 60.3.1 (Types of Centra)** By the shape of their articular faces, centra are:

- **amphicoelous**: concave at both ends, the spaces between adjacent centra filled with notochordal tissue or cartilage, so that a sagittal section shows a beaded notochord;
- **procoelous**: concave at the anterior end and convex at the posterior end, forming a ball-and-socket joint with the ball behind;
- **opisthocoelous**: convex at the anterior end and concave at the posterior end, forming a ball-and-socket joint with the ball in front;
- **heterocoelous**: with saddle-shaped articular faces, concave from side to side and convex from top to bottom at the front and the reverse at the back, so that each joint permits flexion in two planes but prevents rotation about the long axis;
- **acoelous** (amphiplatyan): flat at both ends, the centra separated by fibrocartilaginous intervertebral discs.

| Type | Mobility | Occurrence |
|---|---|---|
| Amphicoelous | Limited bending in all directions; the joint is an elastic cushion of notochord | Most fishes; early tetrapods; many salamanders; tuatara and geckos; caecilians (some) |
| Procoelous | Free bending in all directions, with rotation | Most frogs (presacral vertebrae 1–7), most lizards and all snakes, crocodylians (since the Cretaceous) |
| Opisthocoelous | Free bending, ball in front | Gars; many salamanders (salamandrids); some frogs (discoglossids, pipids); cervical vertebrae of ungulates such as horses and camels; some dinosaurs (sauropod presacrals) |
| Heterocoelous | Flexion in the sagittal and horizontal planes; no rotation within one joint | Cervical vertebrae of birds; also the cervical vertebrae of some turtles |
| Acoelous (amphiplatyan) | Movement by deformation of the discs; limited, but strong and shock-absorbing | Mammals; also many dinosaurs and early reptiles |

Several statements in the Chinese textbooks need qualification. In frogs the vertebrae are mostly procoelous, but the eighth presacral is often amphicoelous and the sacral vertebra has two condyles behind; the "two pairs of arches per segment" of lampreys are the paired dorsal arcualia, not complete arches. The procoelous centra of crocodylians are not ancestral for archosaurs, which had amphicoelous centra; procoely evolved in the crocodylian lineage in the Cretaceous. And acoelous centra are not unique to mammals, although they are characteristic of them.

### 60.3.3. The Vertebrae of Early Tetrapods

The vertebrae of osteolepiform fishes and early tetrapods consisted of separate pieces: a neural arch above and, below and around the notochord, a crescentic *intercentrum* in front and a pair of small *pleurocentra* behind. The intercentrum is thought to correspond to the basiventrals, the pleurocentra to the interventrals or interdorsals. In later tetrapods different lineages enlarged different pieces, and the vertebral types were once used to classify Palaeozoic amphibians.

| Type | Main element of the centrum | Other element | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rhachitomous | Large crescentic intercentrum | Small paired pleurocentra | *Eusthenopteron*, *Ichthyostega*, temnospondyls such as *Eryops* |
| Stereospondylous | Intercentrum, a complete disc | Pleurocentra lost | Triassic temnospondyls (*Mastodonsaurus*) |
| Embolomerous | Intercentrum and pleurocentrum equal, both complete discs | — | Anthracosaurs (*Archeria*), aquatic embolomeres |
| Gastrocentrous | Pleurocentrum, a complete spool | Intercentrum reduced to a small wedge or lost | Amniotes, seymouriamorphs; the condition of all amniotes |
| Holospondylous (lepospondylous) | A single spool-shaped centrum formed directly around the notochord | — | Lepospondyls; living amphibians |

In amniotes the intercentra persist as small ventral wedges in some lizards and the tuatara, and as the ventral arch of the atlas (§60.4.2) and the chevron bones of the tail, which are therefore intercentral elements. The origin of the single centrum of living amphibians is uncertain: if lissamphibians descend from temnospondyls, the centrum might derive from the pleurocentrum or the intercentrum; if from lepospondyls, it is a holospondylous centrum that forms directly. Developmental evidence (direct perichordal ossification without a cartilage stage in salamanders) favours the second interpretation for some groups, and the Chinese textbook's report of disagreement remains accurate.

### 60.3.4. The Columns of the Representative Animals

- *Lamprey.* A persistent notochord with a thick sheath, and on each side of the spinal cord two small cartilages (arcualia) per segment; no centra, no ribs.
- *Dogfish shark.* Calcified amphicoelous centra with notochordal remnants, neural arches alternating with intercalary (interdorsal) plates, haemal arches in the tail, short ribs; the vertebral column turns up into the upper lobe of the heterocercal tail.
- *Carp.* About 36–38 amphicoelous vertebrae, divided into trunk vertebrae with long ventral ribs and caudal vertebrae with haemal spines; many Y-shaped intermuscular bones in the myosepta; the first four vertebrae modified as the Weberian ossicles connecting the swim bladder with the inner ear; the last vertebrae and hypurals support the homocercal tail.
- *Frog.* Eight procoelous presacral vertebrae (the first, the atlas, with two cotyles for the occipital condyles and no transverse processes; the eighth often amphicoelous), one sacral vertebra with broad transverse processes articulating with the long ilia, and the rod-like urostyle; short ribs fused to the transverse processes in ranids.
- *Lizard.* Procoelous vertebrae: eight cervicals including atlas and axis, about 18 dorsals with ribs, two sacrals, and a long tail with chevron bones and, in many species, fracture planes for autotomy.
- *Pigeon.* About 12 heterocoelous cervicals with fused cervical ribs; thoracic vertebrae partly fused into a notarium, bearing ribs with uncinate processes; the synsacrum fused with the pelvis; five or six free caudals and the pygostyle.
- *Rabbit.* Vertebral formula C7 T12–13 L7 S4 Cd15–16, with long lumbar transverse processes for the large back muscles used in hopping and a sacrum of four fused vertebrae.

### 60.3.5. Vertebral Number and Body Size in Fishes

The number of vertebrae varies much more among fishes than among tetrapods: from about 16 in some ocean sunfishes and pufferfishes to more than 200 in eels and some deep-sea fishes. Within species and among related species, vertebral number often increases toward higher latitudes and colder water (Jordan's rule, 1892) and with body length (pleomerism, Lindsey 1975). Experiments with several species show that lower temperature during development increases the number of somites, because the segmentation clock and axis elongation respond differently to temperature ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)). Elongated fishes such as eels swim by anguilliform undulation, in which many short segments allow the body to form several waves at once; short-bodied fishes with few vertebrae are typically manoeuvrable reef fishes or fast swimmers that confine bending to the tail. Vertebral number is thus both an adaptation to the mode of swimming and a developmental response to the environment.

## 60.4. Regionalization

### 60.4.1. Regions of the Column

With the move to land, the vertebral column became divided into regions with different functions:

- *Cervical* vertebrae form the neck, allow the head to move independently of the trunk and bear short ribs or none.
- *Thoracic* (dorsal) vertebrae carry ribs that reach the sternum and form the rib cage; in reptiles and many other tetrapods the trunk vertebrae between neck and sacrum, all bearing ribs, are called *dorsal* without distinction between thoracic and lumbar.
- *Lumbar* vertebrae, between the rib cage and the sacrum, lack free ribs (their transverse processes contain fused rib rudiments); they are characteristic of mammals.
- *Sacral* vertebrae attach the pelvic girdle to the column through expanded transverse processes and sacral ribs; they are usually fused into a sacrum.
- *Caudal* vertebrae form the tail, with chevrons below in many tetrapods.

The number of vertebrae in each region varies widely among tetrapods. The vertebral formula, giving the number in each region, is used in mammals as a taxonomic character.

### 60.4.2. The Atlas and Axis

The first two vertebrae of amniotes are specialized for the movement of the head. In amphibians the single cervical vertebra, the atlas, has two cotyles that receive the two occipital condyles and permit only nodding; there is no axis. In amniotes the atlas is a ring formed by the intercentrum of the first vertebra (the ventral arch) and its paired neural arches; its pleurocentrum has fused to the second vertebra, the axis, as the dens (odontoid process), which projects forward into the ring of the atlas and is held against its ventral arch by a transverse ligament. The atlas articulates with the occipital condyle(s), and the atlanto-occipital joint permits nodding; the atlantoaxial joint, around the dens, permits rotation of the head. In some reptiles a small *proatlas*, the remnant of an additional neural arch, lies between the atlas and the skull.

The atlas–axis complex is an amniote synapomorphy. In mammals with two occipital condyles nodding ("yes") occurs at the atlanto-occipital joint and rotation ("no") at the atlantoaxial joint. In birds a single hemispherical condyle on a hemispherical atlas permits extensive rotation already at the first joint, which, with the long, flexible neck, allows owls to turn their heads through some 270°.

### 60.4.3. The Sacrum

The sacrum appeared with the tetrapod hind limb. In fishes the pelvic girdle is free in the body wall; in early tetrapods such as *Acanthostega* it was attached to a single vertebra by a sacral rib; in living amphibians one sacral vertebra with expanded transverse processes supports the ilia, in frogs by a movable joint used in jumping. In some salamanders the sacral vertebra is not fixed: in *Necturus*, the Chinese textbooks report, the pelvic girdle may attach to the 18th, 19th or 20th vertebra, even differently on the two sides, a sign of the weak supporting role of the hind limbs.

Amniotes have at least two sacral vertebrae, and their sacral ribs fuse with the vertebrae; mammals have three to five, fused into a sacrum, with up to 13 in some xenarthrans. In birds the sacrum is part of the *synsacrum*, a single bone formed by the fusion of the last thoracic, the lumbar, the sacral and the first caudal vertebrae, which is fused with the ilia into a rigid pelvis; together with the fused thoracic vertebrae of many birds (the notarium) it stiffens the trunk for flight and bipedal walking.

### 60.4.4. The Tail

The tail is the region behind the cloaca or anus. Its vertebrae become simpler toward the tip, losing first the transverse processes and zygapophyses and then the neural and haemal arches, until only spool-shaped centra remain. Specializations include the urostyle of frogs, a rod formed from the fused postsacral vertebrae; the *pygostyle* of birds, formed by the fusion of the last four to ten caudal vertebrae, which supports the tail feathers and their muscles; and the coccyx of apes and humans, three to five rudimentary vertebrae fused into one. In many lizards the caudal vertebrae have a fracture plane through the centrum, at which the tail breaks off when grasped (caudal autotomy), and the regenerated tail is supported by an unsegmented cartilage tube rather than vertebrae ([7.5.35](/7.5.35)).

## 60.5. The Hox Code and the Neck

### 60.5.1. Hox Genes and Vertebral Identity

The identity of each vertebra, cervical, thoracic or lumbar, is determined in the presomitic mesoderm by the combination of Hox genes that it expresses ([7.5.23](/7.5.23#sec-23.9)). Because the Hox genes are expressed in the order of their position in the clusters (**Hox colinearity**), more posterior somites express more posterior genes. Three experiments established the code:

1. Burke et al. (1995) showed that the boundary between cervical and thoracic vertebrae lies at the anterior limit of *Hoxc6* expression in the somites in all tetrapods examined, although it is at the 7th–8th somite-derived vertebra in the mouse, the 14th–15th in the chick and the 3rd in the frog. The position of the boundary along the body differs, but its relation to the Hox code is constant: the number of cervical vertebrae evolves by shifting the expression domain.
2. Wellik and Capecchi (2003) deleted all three *Hox10* paralogues in the mouse and obtained mice with ribs on every vertebra behind the neck, including the lumbar and sacral ones: *Hox10* genes repress rib formation and specify lumbar identity. Deleting the *Hox11* paralogues transformed sacral vertebrae into lumbar ones.
3. Mutations of individual Hox genes cause homeotic transformations of single vertebrae, such as a seventh cervical vertebra with ribs, which shows that the code acts vertebra by vertebra (**homeotic transformation**).

The long bodies of snakes were once explained by an expansion of the thoracic Hox code and a loss of regional boundaries (Cohn and Tickle 1999). More recent analyses of vertebral shape (Head and Polly 2015) show that snakes retain cryptic regions along the column similar to those of lizards, and that the elongation resulted mainly from an increase in the number of somites produced by a faster segmentation clock relative to the rate of axis growth (Gomez et al. 2008; [7.5.23](/7.5.23)).

### 60.5.2. Seven Cervical Vertebrae

Almost all mammals have seven cervical vertebrae: the mouse and the giraffe, the bat and the whale. Only manatees (six), the two-toed sloths (*Choloepus*, five to seven) and the three-toed sloths (*Bradypus*, eight to ten) are regular exceptions. Birds, by contrast, have between about 11 and 25 cervical vertebrae, and reptiles between about 7 and more than 70 (in elasmosaurid plesiosaurs). The constancy in mammals is striking because the number of thoracic and lumbar vertebrae varies freely among them.

![The number of cervical vertebrae in mammals, birds and other reptiles, plotted for representative species (values from the literature: mammals from Narita and Kuratani 2005 and Galis 1999; birds from Böhmer et al. 2019 and standard anatomies; reptiles from standard anatomies and Soul and Benson 2017 for plesiosaurs). Mammals cluster at seven except sloths and manatees; birds and other reptiles span a wide range. Points are species values or typical counts, not population distributions.](./plots/neck-counts.py)

Galis (1999) proposed that the constraint is developmental and pleiotropic. In mammals, changes in the identity of the cervical vertebrae, a seventh cervical vertebra with ribs (cervical rib), are associated with changes in the expression of Hox genes that also take part in the development of other organs, and in humans cervical ribs are strongly associated with stillbirth, childhood cancers and other congenital abnormalities: in one study, cervical ribs were found in a large majority of stillborn fetuses but in a small minority of live-born infants (Galis et al. 2006). Selection against the side effects of the mutations that would change the number would thus keep it constant.

The exceptions support the hypothesis in an indirect way. Sloths and manatees have low metabolic rates, and their skeletons show other abnormalities (fused vertebrae, rudimentary ribs, delayed ossification) that suggest a general relaxation of the developmental constraints, perhaps because of their low rates of cell proliferation, and perhaps because their slow life allows them to survive defects that would be fatal in other mammals (Varela-Lasheras et al. 2011). In sloths the extra or missing "cervical" vertebrae are not a change in the number of neck vertebrae but a homeotic shift: some vertebrae with cervical shape carry rudimentary ribs, and the boundary between the regions is displaced relative to the vertebral count. Why birds and reptiles are free of the constraint is less clear; the association of cervical ribs with cancer may be specific to mammals, or the relevant Hox genes may have fewer pleiotropic roles in sauropsids.

**Example 60.5.1** The flexibility of the neck depends on the number of joints and on the range of motion at each. A bird with 14 heterocoelous cervical joints, each permitting about $20^\circ$ of dorsoventral flexion, can bend its neck through $14 \times 20^\circ = 280^\circ$ in the sagittal plane in total, enough to tuck the head between the wings or to reach the preen gland at the base of the tail; distributing the bending in an S-shaped curve allows the head to be held still in space while the body moves (head-bobbing in pigeons). A mammal with six cervical joints of comparable range has a total of only about $120^\circ$ in the neck, and turns its body to look backward. A giraffe, with seven elongated vertebrae, has a long but not especially flexible neck: its range of motion is increased mainly at the joint between the last cervical and first thoracic vertebrae, which in giraffes has the shape of a cervical joint, a homeotic change within the constant count.

## 60.6. Ribs, Sternum and Gastralia

### 60.6.1. Dorsal and Ventral Ribs

Ribs are skeletal elements that project from the vertebrae into the connective tissue septa of the trunk. Two kinds are distinguished by their position (Wake 1979).

- *Dorsal ribs* (intermuscular ribs) develop where the myosepta, the sheets of connective tissue between successive myomeres, meet the horizontal septum that separates the epaxial from the hypaxial muscles. They lie between the muscle masses, extending laterally and downward.
- *Ventral ribs* (subperitoneal or pleural ribs) develop where the myosepta meet the lining of the body cavity, so that they lie just outside the peritoneum, encircling the viscera.

Most teleosts have only ventral ribs, as well as small intermuscular bones in the myosepta (epineurals and epipleurals, the "Y-shaped fish bones" of carp). Chondrichthyans have short ribs in the horizontal septum, usually interpreted as dorsal ribs. Bichirs (*Polypterus*) have both dorsal and ventral ribs on the same vertebrae. The ribs of tetrapods are generally regarded as dorsal ribs, which extended ventrally around the trunk when the ventral ribs were lost. The ventral ribs of fishes are continuous with the haemal arches of the tail: in the transitional vertebrae the haemal arch opens and its halves become ribs.

### 60.6.2. Tetrapod Ribs

Tetrapod ribs typically have two heads: a ventral *capitulum* articulating with the parapophysis on the centrum (or between two centra) and a dorsal *tuberculum* articulating with the diapophysis on the transverse process; such ribs are *bicipital* (dicephalous). Single-headed (unicipital) ribs occur in frogs, snakes and the posterior trunk of many reptiles. Between the two heads runs the vertebral artery in the neck, and where cervical ribs fuse to the vertebrae, as in birds and mammals, the opening between them becomes the transverse foramen.

| Group | Ribs |
|---|---|
| Early tetrapods | Long ribs on most presacral vertebrae, often with overlapping uncinate flanges (*Ichthyostega*) |
| Frogs | Ribs very short or fused to the transverse processes, not reaching the sternum |
| Salamanders | Short two-headed ribs, not reaching the sternum |
| Caecilians | Short ribs on all vertebrae except the first |
| Lizards | Ribs on cervical (short), dorsal and sacral vertebrae; some posterior dorsal ribs free (floating); in *Draco* elongated ribs support a gliding membrane |
| Snakes | Ribs on all precloacal vertebrae except the first two; connected to the ventral scales by muscles, used in locomotion |
| Turtles | Ten pairs of broad trunk ribs fused with the dermal plates of the carapace ([7.17.51](/7.17.51)); cervical vertebrae without ribs; sacral ribs to the ilium |
| Crocodylians | Two-headed ribs with cartilaginous uncinate processes; gastralia behind |
| Birds | Vertebral and sternal segments, both ossified and joined by a joint; bony uncinate processes overlapping the next rib, which serve as levers for the inspiratory muscles (Codd et al. 2005) |
| Mammals | Bony vertebral segment and cartilaginous costal cartilage; true ribs reach the sternum, false ribs join the costal cartilage of the rib in front, floating ribs end freely; cervical and lumbar ribs fused to the transverse processes |

### 60.6.3. The Sternum

The sternum is a ventral, median skeletal element of tetrapods that connects the ventral ends of the ribs and supports the pectoral girdle. Fishes have no sternum. Among amphibians, frogs have a well-developed sternum (an omosternum in front of the pectoral girdle and a xiphisternum behind, partly cartilaginous), salamanders a small plate of cartilage overlapped by the coracoids, and caecilians none. Lizards and crocodylians have a cartilaginous or partly calcified sternum to which several ribs attach; snakes and turtles lack it (the plastron of turtles is dermal). Birds have a large bony sternum with a **keel** (carina) for the flight muscles, absent in ratites; bats and moles have a keeled manubrium. Mammals have a segmented sternum of sternebrae: the manubrium in front, the body and the xiphoid process behind.

The origin of the sternum has long been debated. The Chinese textbooks list three hypotheses: fusion of the distal ends of ribs, derivation from the pectoral girdle, and independent origin in the ventral connective tissue. Developmental studies favour the third: in birds and mammals the sternum develops from paired bands of lateral plate mesoderm (sternal bands) that fuse in the midline, independent of the ribs, which only later join it; and grafting and lineage studies in the chick show its origin from the lateral plate, not from the somites. The sternum is thus, by its embryonic origin, closer to the appendicular skeleton than to the axial skeleton, although by position and function it belongs with the ribs.

### 60.6.4. Gastralia

The *gastralia* (abdominal ribs) are dermal bones in the ventral body wall behind the sternum, arranged in transverse rows of slender rods, each row a chevron of several pieces. They are not ribs, since they are not endochondral and do not articulate with vertebrae, but derived from the ventral scales of early tetrapods ([7.17.57](/7.17.57#sec-57.6.4)). They occur in tuatara and crocodylians among living reptiles, and in many extinct groups including theropod dinosaurs and early birds such as *Archaeopteryx*, and they support the abdominal wall and take part in breathing.

### 60.6.5. Ribs and Breathing

In amniotes the ribs became the main pump for ventilating the lungs, replacing the buccal pump of amphibians ([7.14.17](/7.14.17)). In lizards and snakes the intercostal muscles rotate the ribs forward and outward, enlarging the body cavity; in turtles, whose ribs are fused to the shell, abdominal and limb-girdle muscles move the viscera instead. In crocodylians a diaphragmaticus muscle pulls the liver backward like a piston, with the gastralia and the pubis bracing the abdominal wall. Birds swing the sternum and the ossified sternal ribs downward and forward, pivoting at the joints between vertebral and sternal ribs, and the uncinate processes act as levers that increase the moment arm of the inspiratory muscles (Codd et al. 2005); the movement ventilates the air sacs, which move air through the rigid lung. Mammals combine rib movement with the muscular diaphragm, whose motor nerves (phrenic nerves) arise from the cervical segments 3–5, a reminder of the cervical origin of its muscle.

These differences explain several features of the rib cage. The rib cage of mammals is restricted to the thoracic region and is short behind, leaving the lumbar region free to flex in running; in birds it is rigid and connected to a large sternum; in crocodylians the gastralia continue it backward over the belly; and in snakes ribs along the whole trunk both ventilate the lung and take part in locomotion and in constriction of prey.

## 60.7. Functional Morphology of the Column

### 60.7.1. Swimming Fishes

In a swimming fish the myomeres on one side contract in a wave that passes backward along the body, bending the body into a travelling wave ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)). The vertebral column does not need to resist the weight of the body, which is supported by water, but it must resist compression: without a stiff axis the contracting muscles would shorten the body instead of bending it. The notochord or the chain of centra provides this stiffness, and the amphicoelous joints with their notochordal cushions store and release elastic energy during each tail beat. In fast-swimming fishes such as tunas the column is stiffened by interlocking processes and by the tendons of the myomeres, which transmit the force of the trunk muscles to the tail and confine the bending to its base (thunniform swimming).

### 60.7.2. Sprawling Tetrapods

In salamanders and lizards the body bends laterally during walking, in a standing wave whose nodes lie at the girdles: the trunk curves to the left when the right forelimb and left hindlimb are forward, and to the right in the next step. The lateral bending lengthens the stride and is produced by the same axial muscles used in swimming. The zygapophyses of these animals are nearly horizontal and allow lateral bending; the procoelous joints of lizards allow bending in all directions.

As discussed in [7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.4.2), lateral bending interferes with breathing when the axial muscles are used for both (Carrier's constraint). The solutions of the two amniote lineages left their mark on the column: mammals moved the limbs under the body and bent the column in the vertical plane, and the diaphragm took over much of the work of breathing; birds stiffened the trunk with the notarium and synsacrum and moved the limbs from the hips and the knees.

### 60.7.3. Mammals

The column of a quadrupedal mammal can be modelled as a bow and string (Slijper 1946): the arched vertebral column, braced by the epaxial muscles and ligaments, is the bow; the abdominal muscles, the linea alba and the sternum are the string; and the body hangs from the bow between the fore and hind limbs. The neural spines of the anterior thoracic vertebrae are long, for the attachment of the nuchal ligament and muscles that support the head; at the *anticlinal vertebra* in the mid-thorax the spines change from posterior to anterior inclination, reflecting the change in the direction of the pull of the muscles.

The lumbar vertebrae of mammals carry the main bending of the trunk in galloping. Their zygapophyses are vertical, interlocking in the sagittal plane, which permits flexion and extension but resists rotation and lateral bending. In the gallop of a cheetah the column flexes and extends at each stride, adding its movement to that of the limbs and lengthening the stride by about a third; in a horse, whose lumbar column is stiff, the stride depends more on the limbs, and the rigid back transmits the thrust of the hind limbs efficiently and supports the heavy gut. In humans the upright posture gave the column four curvatures, the cervical and lumbar lordoses and the thoracic and sacral kyphoses, which place the centre of mass above the hip joints and act as springs; the lumbar vertebrae are large and wedge-shaped, and the intervertebral discs absorb loads that make lumbar disc herniation a common human disease.

### 60.7.4. Snakes and Limbless Tetrapods

Limblessness evolved more than 25 times in squamates, as well as in caecilians and some salamanders, and each time the vertebral column took over locomotion. Snakes have 120 to more than 400 vertebrae, with procoelous centra, zygosphene–zygantrum articulations that resist torsion while permitting lateral bending, and ribs on almost every vertebra. In lateral undulation the body pushes against irregularities of the ground; in rectilinear locomotion the ventral scales are lifted and moved forward by muscles from the ribs and pressed against the ground; in concertina locomotion the body is alternately bunched and extended; and in sidewinding the body is lifted in sections. The number of vertebrae, and hence of segments that can be controlled independently, is part of what makes these modes possible.

### 60.7.5. Long Necks

Very long necks evolved repeatedly: in sauropod dinosaurs (up to about 15 m in *Mamenchisaurus* and *Supersaurus*), in the Triassic protorosaur *Tanystropheus*, in elasmosaurid plesiosaurs, in giraffes and camels, in swans and herons. They are built in two ways: by increasing the number of cervical vertebrae, as in birds, plesiosaurs (up to 76 in *Albertonectes*) and sauropods (up to 19), or by elongating a fixed number, as in giraffes and *Tanystropheus* (13 cervicals, each up to 1 m long). Mammals, constrained to seven, can only use the second way.

A long neck held out horizontally is a cantilever whose bending moment grows with the square of its length. Sauropods reduced the weight of the neck with extensive pneumatic cavities in the vertebrae, which in some species made up some 60–70 % of the volume of the cervical bone, and supported it by ligaments and by bracing with long, overlapping cervical ribs that were ossified tendons of the neck muscles (Taylor and Wedel 2013). Giraffes support the neck with a massive nuchal ligament from the elongated neural spines of the anterior thoracic vertebrae, and their arterial blood pressure, about twice that of other mammals, is needed to supply the brain two metres above the heart ([7.14.13](/7.14.13)).

### 60.8.2. Trends

The Chinese textbooks summarize the evolution of the vertebral column as a progression from notochord to vertebral column, from two regions to five, from limited mobility (amphicoelous) to greater mobility (procoelous and opisthocoelous) and finally to highly mobile (heterocoelous) or strong and elastic (acoelous) joints, with the appearance of the neck and sacrum on land. The comparative table supports the general direction, but three qualifications follow from the modern phylogeny. The types of centra are adaptations to particular modes of movement and evolved repeatedly (procoely in frogs, squamates and crocodylians independently; opisthocoely in gars, salamanders and horses), so that they are not a single ladder. The loss of centra in sturgeons, lungfishes and coelacanths is secondary, not primitive. And regionalization is set by a Hox code shared by all vertebrates, which was present before the regions became distinct in shape; what evolved on land was the morphological expression of a regional pattern that is already present, in cryptic form, in fishes.

## Summary

- The axial skeleton comprises the **notochord**, the vertebral column, ribs, sternum and gastralia. A vertebra has a **centrum** (Definition 60.1.1), neural and haemal arches, zygapophyses and transverse processes (diapophysis and parapophysis for two-headed ribs).
- Vertebrae form from the **sclerotome**; by **resegmentation** each vertebra is made of the posterior half of one sclerotome and the anterior half of the next, so vertebrae alternate with myotomes and spinal nerves emerge between them. Arches appear before centra in development and in evolution (lamprey arcualia, sturgeon arches, gnathostome centra).
- Centra (Definition 60.3.1) are **amphicoelous** (fishes, early tetrapods, many salamanders), **procoelous** (frogs, squamates, crocodylians), **opisthocoelous** (gars, salamandrids, ungulate necks), **heterocoelous** (bird necks) or acoelous (mammals); each type evolved repeatedly. Early tetrapod centra were rhachitomous; amniotes have gastrocentrous vertebrae with a dominant pleurocentrum.
- Tetrapods have cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral and caudal regions; amniotes an atlas–axis complex (the dens is the atlas pleurocentrum) and two or more sacral vertebrae; birds a synsacrum and pygostyle.
- The Hox code specifies regional identity (*Hoxc6* at the cervical–thoracic boundary; *Hox10* lumbar, *Hox11* sacral). Mammals almost always have seven cervical vertebrae, probably because changes are coupled with harmful pleiotropic effects (Galis); sloths and manatees are exceptions with relaxed constraints, while birds and reptiles vary widely.
- Ribs are dorsal (intermuscular) or ventral (subperitoneal); teleosts have ventral ribs, tetrapods dorsal ribs, often bicipital, with uncinate processes in birds. The sternum, absent in fishes, arises from lateral plate mesoderm; birds have a keeled sternum. Gastralia are dermal.
- The column resists compression in swimming fishes, bends laterally in sprawling tetrapods, flexes dorsoventrally in running mammals (bow-and-string model, vertical lumbar zygapophyses) and is stiffened in birds.

## Questions

1. Explain why there are eight cervical spinal nerves but only seven cervical vertebrae in mammals.

   **Answer** Spinal nerves grow out through the anterior halves of the sclerotomes. By resegmentation each vertebra forms from the posterior half of one sclerotome and the anterior half of the next, so each nerve emerges between two vertebrae, at the level where the anterior half of a sclerotome lies. The first cervical nerve emerges through the anterior half of the first cervical sclerotome, which in mammals contributes to the occipital region and the atlas, so it passes between the skull and the atlas. Each following nerve passes between successive vertebrae; the eighth emerges between the seventh cervical and the first thoracic vertebra. There are therefore one more cervical nerves than cervical vertebrae, while in the thoracic and lumbar regions each nerve is named after the vertebra in front of it.

2. Which type of centrum would you expect in (a) a teleost, (b) a frog, (c) the neck of a goose, (d) a human, and why?

   **Answer** (a) Amphicoelous: in water the column needs to resist compression and permit moderate bending; the notochordal cushions between the concave faces store elastic energy. (b) Procoelous (presacral vertebrae 1–7): ball-and-socket joints permit free movement in a short trunk; the eighth is often amphicoelous and the sacral vertebra has a double condyle behind. (c) Heterocoelous: saddle-shaped joints permit flexion in two planes and give the long neck great mobility while preventing twisting at each joint. (d) Acoelous with intervertebral discs: flat faces with fibrocartilaginous discs give a strong, load-bearing column in which movement is produced by deformation of the discs, which also absorb shocks during upright walking and running.

3. A mutation in a mouse causes ribs to form on all lumbar vertebrae. Which genes are likely affected, and what does the result show about the specification of vertebral identity?

   **Answer** The phenotype resembles that of mice lacking all three *Hox10* paralogues (*Hoxa10*, *Hoxc10*, *Hoxd10*; Wellik and Capecchi 2003), in which ribs form on every vertebra behind the neck. *Hox10* genes are expressed in the presomitic mesoderm of the lumbar region and repress the rib-forming programme; without them, the lumbar vertebrae adopt thoracic identity. The result shows that regional identity is specified by Hox genes acting in the paraxial mesoderm before the vertebrae form, that rib formation is a default for trunk vertebrae suppressed in the lumbar region, and that paralogues act redundantly, so that single mutants have weak phenotypes.

4. Compare the sternum of a frog, a lizard, a pigeon and a rabbit, and evaluate the evidence that the sternum belongs to the appendicular rather than the axial skeleton.

   **Answer** Frog: an omosternum in front of the pectoral girdle and a xiphisternum behind, partly bony, connected to the girdle; ribs do not reach it. Lizard: a cartilaginous plate to which several pairs of ribs attach, with the interclavicle in front. Pigeon: a large bony sternum with a high keel for the flight muscles, articulating with the coracoids and with the ossified sternal ribs. Rabbit: a series of sternebrae (manubrium, body, xiphoid process) joined to the costal cartilages of the true ribs. Evidence for an appendicular affinity: in birds and mammals the sternum develops from paired sternal bands of lateral plate mesoderm, the tissue that forms the limbs and girdles, rather than from the sclerotome; it develops independently of the ribs, which join it secondarily, and it is present in frogs, which have almost no ribs. Against: by position and function it is part of the thoracic cage and it is absent where there are no limbs only inconsistently (snakes lack it, but so do turtles with limbs). The developmental origin is the stronger argument for classifying it with the appendicular skeleton.

5. (Open) The near constancy of seven cervical vertebrae in mammals is often cited as a developmental constraint. Design a comparative study to test the hypothesis that the constraint is due to pleiotropic effects of Hox genes.

   **Answer** Points to consider: (a) Predictions: if changes in cervical number are selected against because of pleiotropic effects, the frequency of cervical ribs and transitional vertebrae should be higher in species or populations where selection is relaxed (low metabolic rates, small populations, captivity) and should be associated with other abnormalities. (b) Data: survey large museum collections of mammals for cervical ribs and homeotic transformations, with metabolic rate, body size and life history as covariates (Varela-Lasheras et al. 2011 did this for sloths and manatees); compare fetal and adult frequencies in humans and other species to measure selection against them. (c) Comparisons with birds and reptiles: test whether the corresponding Hox genes have the same pleiotropic roles (for example in cancer susceptibility or organogenesis) in sauropsids. (d) Experiments: examine mouse mutants that shift the cervical–thoracic boundary for associated defects. (e) Alternative hypotheses to test: the constraint may arise from the integration of the neck with the diaphragm (its innervation from cervical segments 3–5) and the shoulder girdle, which would predict associated changes in these structures.
