Almost every living vertebrate has jaws. The roughly 120 species of hagfishes and lampreys of [7.17.45](/7.17.45) are the only survivors of the jawless grade; the jawed vertebrates, or gnathostomes ("jaw mouths"), comprise the other 99.8 % of vertebrate species, from sharks and bony fishes to tetrapods. Jaws let a vertebrate bite, grasp, crush and cut, and so opened a range of foods and ways of life that jawless fishes could not use; but jaws came in a package with other novelties: a second pair of paired fins, a skeleton with joints between the gill arches, an inner ear with three semicircular canals, myelinated nerves, a new adaptive immune system and a second duplication of the genome.

This chapter first defines the gnathostome body plan (§46.1), then asks how jaws and paired fins evolved, combining comparative anatomy, development and fossils (§46.2). The earliest jawed vertebrates, the armoured placoderms and the spiny acanthodians, are treated in §46.3; several of the most informative fossils come from the Silurian of southern China. The rest of the chapter is about the cartilaginous fishes, the sharks, rays and chimaeras (§46.4–§46.5), the sister group of the bony fishes, which are the subject of [7.17.47](/7.17.47) and [7.17.48](/7.17.48). The skull and the suspension of the jaws are compared across vertebrates in [7.17.58](/7.17.58) and [7.17.59](/7.17.59), the fins and girdles in [7.17.61](/7.17.61); the physiology of shark osmoregulation, electroreception and reproduction is in [7.14](/7.14), to which the relevant sections link.

## 46.1. The Gnathostome Body Plan

### 46.1.1. Characters of Jawed Vertebrates

**Definition 46.1.1 (Gnathostomata)** The **Gnathostomata** (gnathostomes, jawed vertebrates) are the clade of vertebrates comprising the last common ancestor of the chondrichthyans and the osteichthyans and all its descendants, together with the extinct stem groups closer to them than to cyclostomes and osteostracans that possess jaws. Their jaws are formed by the skeleton of the first pharyngeal arch (upper palatoquadrate and lower Meckel's cartilage) and bear teeth; they have two pairs of paired fins (pectoral and pelvic) supported by girdles, an articulated gill skeleton medial to the gills, myomeres divided by a horizontal septum into epaxial and hypaxial muscles, an inner ear with three semicircular canals, paired nasal sacs, myelin sheaths around axons, and an adaptive immune system based on immunoglobulins, T-cell receptors and RAG-mediated gene recombination.

The table compares cyclostomes and gnathostomes. Several gnathostome characters appear to have been acquired in a stepwise fashion along the stem: paired pectoral fins and cellular bone were already present in osteostracans ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.4.4)), whereas jaws, pelvic fins and the third semicircular canal appear only with the first gnathostomes.

| Character | Cyclostomes | Gnathostomes |
|---|---|---|
| Mouth | Oral apparatus without jaws; horny teeth on a lingual cartilage | Jaws of the mandibular arch, hinged at a joint between palatoquadrate and Meckel's cartilage; teeth of dentine and enamel or enameloid, replaced continuously or periodically |
| Gill skeleton | Unjointed branchial basket lateral to the gill pouches | Jointed arches (pharyngo-, epi-, cerato-, hypo- and basibranchials) medial to the gills; gills on interbranchial septa or filaments |
| Fins | Median fins only | Median fins and two pairs of paired fins with girdles, typically tribasic (pro-, meso- and metapterygium) |
| Axial skeleton and muscles | Notochord with small arcualia; no horizontal septum | Neural and haemal arches, in crown gnathostomes centra and ribs; horizontal septum, epaxial and hypaxial muscles |
| Nasal organs | One median nostril and nasohypophyseal opening | Paired nostrils and nasal sacs; hypophysis opening into the mouth in the embryo |
| Inner ear | One (hagfishes) or two (lampreys) semicircular canals | Three canals, including a horizontal canal |
| Nervous system | Unmyelinated axons; no sympathetic chain in hagfishes | Myelin sheaths (Schwann cells, oligodendrocytes), faster conduction; sympathetic chain ganglia |
| Circulation | Heart of sinus venosus, atrium and ventricle | Conus arteriosus added; spleen as a discrete organ |
| Reproduction | No gonoducts; gametes shed through the body cavity | Sperm conducted by the archinephric duct; eggs by an oviduct (Müllerian duct) |
| Immune system | Variable lymphocyte receptors | Immunoglobulins, T-cell receptors, MHC molecules, RAG enzymes, a thymus ([7.15.3](/7.15.3)) |
| Genome | 1R and an independent triplication | 1R and 2R; four *Hox* clusters |

Myelin illustrates the functional significance of these changes. Conduction velocity in an unmyelinated axon increases roughly with the square root of its diameter, but in a myelinated axon it increases approximately linearly with its diameter, so a myelinated fibre of $10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ (about $60\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$) conducts faster than the squid giant axon, which is fifty times thicker. Myelin allowed fast reflexes and large, active bodies without giant axons; it appeared with the first gnathostomes and is absent in cyclostomes. The fossil braincases of placoderms already show the large canals of cranial nerves typical of myelinated fibres.

### 46.1.2. What Jaws Do

Jaws are a pair of levers. The upper jaw is fixed to (or slung from) the braincase; the lower jaw rotates at the jaw joint. Muscles of the mandibular arch, the adductor mandibulae, close the mouth by pulling on the lower jaw between the joint and the teeth; muscles of the hypobranchial region and the coracomandibularis open it.

**Example 46.1.1 (The Jaw as a Lever)** Let the adductor muscle insert on the lower jaw at a distance $a$ from the jaw joint (the in-lever) and exert a force $F_{\mathrm{m}}$ perpendicular to the jaw, and let a tooth lie at a distance $b$ from the joint (the out-lever). Balancing the moments about the joint gives the bite force at the tooth,

$$
F_{\mathrm{b}} = F_{\mathrm{m}}\,\frac{a}{b},
$$
and the ratio $a/b$ is the mechanical advantage. A muscle that shortens at speed $v$ moves the tooth at speed $v\,b/a$. With $a = 2\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $b = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$, a muscle force of $500\,\mathrm{N}$ produces a bite of $100\,\mathrm{N}$ at the front teeth but $250\,\mathrm{N}$ at a tooth $4\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the joint, while the front of the jaw moves five times faster than the muscle shortens. Jaws thus trade force against speed along their length: the posterior teeth crush, the anterior teeth snap. Fishes that eat hard prey (the crushing tooth plates of horn sharks, rays and chimaeras) have short jaws with a high mechanical advantage; fast snapping predators have long jaws with a low one. The same lever analysis is applied to the evolution of mammal jaws in [7.17.54](/7.17.54).

:::

Jaws are also part of the ventilation pump. When a fish expands its mouth and pharynx, water flows in; when it compresses them with the mouth closed, water is forced out over the gills. Jointed gill arches and a hinged mouth that can be closed make this two-pump system far more effective than the velar pump of lampreys. Jon Mallatt (1996) proposed that the first function of jaws was ventilatory: forceful closing of the mouth during the expulsion phase of a more active, more oxygen-demanding fish. Only later would the jaws, already large and muscular, have been used to seize prey. The hypothesis explains why the jaw muscles are serially homologous with the constrictor muscles of the gill arches.

## 46.2. The Origin of Jaws and Paired Fins

### 46.2.1. The Gill-Arch Hypothesis

The classical answer to the question of where jaws came from is that they are a modified gill arch. In a shark, each gill arch consists of a series of jointed cartilages on each side, from dorsal to ventral the pharyngobranchial, epibranchial, ceratobranchial and hypobranchial, joined ventrally by median basibranchials. The arches bend forward at the joint between epibranchial and ceratobranchial, so that each has the shape of a chevron. The palatoquadrate and Meckel's cartilage of the jaws look like an enlarged epibranchial and ceratobranchial; the hyomandibula and ceratohyal of the second arch, which support the jaws, look like the corresponding elements of the hyoid arch; and between the jaws and the hyoid arch lies a small opening, the *spiracle*, which looks like a reduced gill slit. Carl Gegenbaur (1878) concluded that the jaws were a former gill arch, the mandibular arch, and that the spiracle was the remains of the gill slit between it and the hyoid arch.

In its simple form, the hypothesis has three problems. First, the skeleton of the cyclostome gill basket lies lateral to the gill pouches, whereas the gnathostome gill arches lie medial to the gills; the two skeletons may not be directly homologous, and the jawless ancestor of gnathostomes need not have had arches like those of sharks. Second, some versions assumed that one or two arches in front of the mandibular arch had been lost ("premandibular arches"), for which there is no developmental or fossil evidence. Third, no fossil shows an intermediate between a gill arch and a jaw: the osteostracans, the closest jawless relatives, have no jaws, and the earliest gnathostomes already have fully formed ones.

What remains of the hypothesis is the serial homology of the pharyngeal arches. In every vertebrate embryo the head develops a series of **pharyngeal arches**, each with a core of neural crest mesenchyme that forms its skeleton, a core of mesoderm that forms its muscles, a cranial nerve and an aortic arch, separated by pharyngeal pouches and clefts. The jaws develop from the first arch in the same way as the gill arches develop from the posterior arches ([7.5.20](/7.5.20#sec-20.7)). Jaws are the skeleton of the first arch, specialized; they are not a gill arch that was once used for respiration in an adult.

### 46.2.2. The Developmental Origin of Jaws

Three findings from developmental biology explain how the first arch became different.

1. *The Hox-free first arch.* The neural crest that fills the first arch expresses no *Hox* genes, while the crest of the second and more posterior arches expresses *Hox* genes in a nested pattern. Forcing *Hoxa2* into the first arch transforms its skeleton toward that of the second arch; deleting *Hoxa2* in mice transforms second-arch elements into a mirror copy of first-arch elements ([7.5.20](/7.5.20#sec-20.7)). A *Hox*-free state is required for the jaw.
2. *The Dlx code.* Within each arch the genes of the *Dlx* family are expressed in a nested dorsoventral pattern: *Dlx1* and *Dlx2* throughout, *Dlx5* and *Dlx6* in the ventral half, *Dlx3* and *Dlx4* in the most ventral part. The signal endothelin-1 from the ventral ectoderm and mesoderm induces the ventral genes. In mice lacking *Dlx5* and *Dlx6*, the lower jaw is transformed into a mirror image of the upper jaw (Depew et al. 2002; Beverdam et al. 2002), and blocking endothelin signalling has the same effect. The nested code thus distinguishes an upper element from a lower one; a joint-forming gene (*Nkx3.2*, also called *Bapx1*) is expressed where the two meet. The *Dlx* genes duplicated in the genome duplications ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1.5)), and lampreys have a nested *Dlx* expression in their pharyngeal arches too, so the dorsoventral code is older than jaws; what gnathostomes added was the joint and the differentiation of the first arch into two articulating levers.
3. *Heterotopy.* Shigeru Kuratani and colleagues compared the head of lamprey and gnathostome embryos. In lampreys the crest cells of the first arch form the upper and lower lips and the velum around a single median nasohypophyseal placode, which develops in front of the mouth and blocks the forward migration of crest cells beneath the forebrain. In gnathostomes the nasal placodes are paired and lateral, and the hypophyseal placode is displaced into the roof of the mouth (Rathke's pouch); crest cells can then migrate forward between the two nasal placodes, forming the trabeculae of the braincase and the front of the face, while the mandibular arch crest forms the jaws behind them. The evolution of jaws thus involved a shift in the position of tissue interactions (heterotopy) made possible by the separation of the nasal and hypophyseal placodes. The galeaspid *Shuyu* ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.4.3)) shows this separation in a jawless fish, as the hypothesis predicts.

### 46.2.3. The Origin of Paired Fins

Two hypotheses about paired fins have been debated since the nineteenth century. Gegenbaur's archipterygium hypothesis derived the paired fins and their girdles from a gill arch with its rays; the gill-arch skeleton and the girdles are in fact both shaped by similar genes, but the fins develop from the lateral plate mesoderm of the trunk, not from the pharyngeal arches, and the hypothesis has few followers today. The fin-fold hypothesis of Thacher, Mivart and Balfour (1876–1881) derived the paired fins from continuous lateral fin folds running along the ventrolateral body wall, which were later divided into pectoral and pelvic portions, as the median fin fold was divided into dorsal, caudal and anal fins.

Development supports a modified fin-fold hypothesis. In the embryo the paired fins arise from the lateral plate mesoderm at two positions along the body, where the T-box genes *Tbx5* (forelimb, pectoral fin) and *Tbx4* (hindlimb, pelvic fin) are activated under the control of the *Hox* code of the trunk, and FGF signalling sets up an outgrowth like a limb bud ([7.5.31](/7.5.31)); in some embryos (sharks) a continuous lateral ridge of competent tissue precedes the two buds. Fossils record the sequence: paired ventrolateral folds in the galeaspid *Tujiaaspis*, pectoral fins with an internal skeleton in osteostracans, and both pectoral and pelvic fins in the placoderms and all later gnathostomes. The pectoral fins evolved first, the pelvic fins later, and the two pairs share much of their genetic programme (they are serial homologues).

## 46.3. Stem Gnathostomes: Placoderms and Acanthodians

### 46.3.1. Placoderms

The *placoderms* ("plate skins") are extinct jawed fishes of the Silurian and Devonian in which the head and the front of the trunk were covered by two shields of dermal bone, a head shield and a thoracic shield, connected by a joint in many groups so that the head could be raised. Most were bottom dwellers; some were large pelagic predators. They were the most diverse vertebrates of the Devonian, with more than 300 genera, and disappeared at the end of that period. Because they have no living descendants among them, the group is defined by its armour; most phylogenetic analyses since 2013 find it paraphyletic, a series of stem gnathostomes (the tree below).

- *Antiarchs* (*Bothriolepis*, *Pterichthyodes*, the Chinese *Yunnanolepis* and *Sinolepis*) had a box-like trunk armour and pectoral appendages enclosed in jointed tubes of dermal bone, like the legs of a crab; their small eyes and nostrils lay close together on top of the head, and they probably browsed on detritus and algae on the bottom of lakes and coastal waters. Antiarchs were abundant in China, where the Devonian rocks of Yunnan and Guangxi have yielded many genera.
- *Arthrodires* ("jointed necks") had a joint between head and thoracic shields. The largest, *Dunkleosteus* of the Late Devonian, reached a length of about 4 m (older estimates of 6–10 m are now considered too large); instead of teeth it had self-sharpening bony gnathal plates, and biomechanical models give bite forces of several thousand newtons and an exceptionally fast jaw opening by a four-bar linkage of the head joint.
- *Ptyctodonts* were small, with tooth plates for crushing. In *Materpiscis* from the Late Devonian Gogo Formation of Australia, an embryo was found inside the mother, connected by what appears to be an umbilical cord (Long et al. 2008), and males had pelvic claspers: placoderms practised internal fertilization and viviparity 380 million years ago. Claspers were later reported in the antiarch *Microbrachius*.

### 46.3.2. Silurian Jawed Fishes from China

The earliest well-preserved jawed vertebrates come from the Silurian of southern China, and they have changed the picture of early gnathostome evolution.

| Fossil | Locality and age | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| *Qianodus duplicis* | Guizhou, about 439 million years (early Silurian) | Tooth whorls, the oldest known teeth of a jawed vertebrate (Andreev et al. 2022); jawed vertebrates had diversified by the early Silurian |
| *Fanjingshania renovata* | Guizhou, about 436 million years | A spiny chondrichthyan relative with fin spines and scales of acanthodian type, among the oldest chondrichthyans |
| *Xiushanosteus mirabilis*, *Shenacanthus vermiformis* | Chongqing (Xiushan), about 436 million years | The oldest complete jawed fishes (Zhu et al. 2022): *Xiushanosteus* a small placoderm with the skull roof pattern of later forms, *Shenacanthus* a chondrichthyan with large dermal plates on the trunk, showing that early chondrichthyans had placoderm-like armour |
| *Entelognathus primordialis* | Kuanti Formation, Qujing, Yunnan, about 423 million years (late Silurian) | A placoderm with the marginal dermal jaw bones of osteichthyans: premaxilla, maxilla and dentary (Zhu et al. 2013) |
| *Qilinyu rostrata* | Same formation | Intermediate between *Entelognathus* and the other placoderms in its jaw bones (Zhu et al. 2016) |
| *Guiyu oneiros* | Same formation | The oldest articulated bony fish, with a mosaic of actinopterygian and sarcopterygian characters ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)) |

*Entelognathus* resolved an old puzzle. In placoderms the jaws were covered by gnathal plates of dermal bone inside the mouth; in bony fishes and tetrapods the jaws carry marginal dermal bones on the outside, the premaxilla and maxilla above and the dentary below, which bear the teeth. These bones were thought to be an osteichthyan novelty unrelated to the placoderm plates. *Entelognathus* has a placoderm skull and body but, in addition, marginal jaw bones with the shape and position of the premaxilla, maxilla and dentary. The bony-fish jaw bones therefore originated in placoderms, and our own maxilla and dentary can be traced to Silurian placoderms. Since osteichthyans share these bones with *Entelognathus* and *Qilinyu*, while chondrichthyans lack dermal bone altogether, the chondrichthyan condition appears to be a loss.

### 46.3.3. Acanthodians

The *acanthodians* ("spiny sharks") lived from the Silurian to the Permian. Most were small, slender fishes with large eyes, a heterocercal tail, tiny square scales and a stout spine in front of every fin except the caudal fin, often with additional pairs of spines between the pectoral and pelvic fins (*Climatius*); some, like *Acanthodes* of the Permian, had gill rakers and no teeth and probably fed on plankton. Their mixture of characters made them hard to place: their scales and braincase resemble those of osteichthyans, their fin spines and teeth those of sharks. Fossils such as *Doliodus* from the Early Devonian of Canada, a shark with paired fin spines, and the analyses that followed the discoveries in China place acanthodians on the stem of the chondrichthyans: early chondrichthyans were spiny, and the living sharks lost most of the spines. A separate class Acanthodii is therefore a paraphyletic group.

## 46.4. Cartilaginous Fishes

### 46.4.1. Characters of Chondrichthyans

**Definition 46.4.1 (Chondrichthyes)** The **Chondrichthyes** (chondrichthyans, cartilaginous fishes) are the clade of jawed vertebrates comprising the Elasmobranchii (sharks and batoids) and the Holocephali (chimaeras), together with their extinct stem relatives. Their internal skeleton is cartilage covered by a layer of prismatic calcified cartilage (small mineralized tiles, the tesserae) and contains no endochondral or perichondral bone; their skin bears placoid scales; their teeth develop on a dental lamina and are replaced in rows; males have claspers on the pelvic fins, and fertilization is internal; they have no lungs or swim bladder.

The absence of bone is not a primitive condition: bone was present in osteostracans and placoderms, and chondrichthyans lost it, keeping bone-like tissue only in the bases of their scales and teeth. The genome of the elephant shark *Callorhinchus milii*, a chimaera, lacks a family of genes (the secretory calcium-binding phosphoproteins) needed for bone mineralization in bony fishes (Venkatesh et al. 2014). Further chondrichthyan characters are an intestine with a *spiral valve*, a *rectal gland* that secretes salt, blood made nearly isosmotic with seawater by urea and trimethylamine oxide (§46.5.3), an oil-rich liver (§46.5.1), and a conus arteriosus with several rows of valves.

**Definition 46.4.2 (Placoid Scale)** A **placoid scale** (dermal denticle) is the scale of chondrichthyans: a small tooth-like structure with a basal plate of bone-like tissue anchored in the dermis and a spine of dentine projecting through the epidermis, capped with enameloid and enclosing a pulp cavity. Placoid scales do not grow; they are shed and replaced as the fish grows.

Placoid scales are odontodes ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1.3)), and they share their structure and developmental genes with teeth: they form at a placode of epidermis over a condensation of neural crest-derived mesenchyme. On the body of a fast shark their crowns carry fine ridges aligned with the flow, which reduce turbulent skin friction slightly; the "shark-skin" swimsuits and riblet surfaces of engineering imitate them. The scales of other fishes and their evolution are compared in [7.17.57](/7.17.57).

Teeth are formed continuously on a dental lamina, a fold of epithelium on the inner side of each jaw, and move outward in rows as on a conveyor belt; each position is occupied by a tooth family, a series of developing teeth behind the functional one. Shed teeth are replaced every few days to weeks; a large shark may use tens of thousands of teeth during its life, which is why shark teeth are among the most common vertebrate fossils. The shapes are diverse: the serrated triangular blades of the great white shark *Carcharodon carcharias* for cutting large prey, the pointed grasping teeth of the sand tiger, the pavement of flat crushing teeth in the back of the mouth of horn sharks (*Heterodontus*) and many rays, and the minute teeth of the filter-feeding whale shark, basking shark and megamouth, which strain plankton with gill rakers.

**Definition 46.4.3 (Clasper)** A **clasper** (myxopterygium) is the copulatory organ of male chondrichthyans, an elongated, grooved extension of the skeleton of each pelvic fin; during mating one clasper is flexed forward and inserted into the female's cloaca, and seminal fluid flows along its groove, often flushed by seawater from a subcutaneous siphon sac.

### 46.4.2. Holocephali: Chimaeras

**Definition 46.4.4 (Holocephali)** The **Holocephali** (holocephalans, chimaeras, ratfishes) are chondrichthyans in which the palatoquadrate is fused to the braincase (autostylic, or holostylic, suspension), the teeth are fused into a few ever-growing tooth plates, the gill slits are covered by a fleshy operculum with one external opening on each side, and the skin is mostly naked; there is a venomous spine in front of the first dorsal fin, and males have a clasper-like tenaculum on the forehead and additional prepelvic claspers.

About 50 living species of chimaeras live in deep water on continental slopes worldwide. They swim by flapping their large pectoral fins, eat hard-shelled invertebrates with their tooth plates and lay large horny egg cases. The name "whole head" refers to the fusion of the upper jaw to the skull. The Chinese textbooks cite *Chimaera phantasma* (silver chimaera), found in the East and South China Seas. The elephant shark *Callorhinchus milii* of southern Australia has the most slowly evolving vertebrate genome sequenced so far and is used as a reference for ancestral gnathostome gene content. Holocephalans have a rich fossil record; in the Carboniferous they were more diverse than sharks.

### 46.4.3. Elasmobranchii: Sharks and Batoids

**Definition 46.4.5 (Elasmobranchii)** The **Elasmobranchii** (elasmobranchs) are chondrichthyans with five to seven pairs of gill slits that open separately to the outside, the palatoquadrate not fused to the braincase, continuously replaced teeth, placoid scales over the body and usually a spiracle; they comprise the sharks (Selachii) and the batoids (Batoidea: skates, rays, guitarfishes and sawfishes).

Living elasmobranchs number about 1200 species, roughly 540 sharks and 650 batoids. Molecular phylogenies show that sharks and batoids are sister groups. An earlier morphological hypothesis (the "Hypnosqualea") derived the batoids from within the squalomorph sharks, close to angel sharks and sawsharks, because these sharks share a flattened body with the batoids; the shared flattening is convergent.

Sharks swim by lateral undulation of the body and tail; batoids by undulating or flapping their enlarged pectoral fins. Their gill slits lie on the underside, and since a ray resting on the bottom cannot take water in through the mouth, it inhales through the large dorsal spiracles and exhales through the ventral gill slits. Most batoids are bottom-living predators of invertebrates and fishes, crushing shells with pavement teeth; the mantas and devil rays are filter feeders in open water, with cephalic fins that funnel plankton into the mouth. In electric rays, modified branchial muscles form electric organs on each side of the head, up to a sixth of the body mass, producing discharges of up to about 200 V that stun prey. Sawfishes, with a long saw-like rostrum edged with modified dermal denticles, detect prey electrically with the rostrum and slash it; all five species are critically endangered or endangered.

### 46.4.4. The Body of a Shark

The Chinese textbooks do not use a shark as the representative fish, but a spiny dogfish (*Squalus*) is the classic dissection animal of comparative anatomy courses, and its organization is the reference for the cartilaginous fishes.

- *External features.* A fusiform body with a pointed snout and a ventral mouth; two dorsal fins each with a spine in front, a heterocercal caudal fin, paired pectoral and pelvic fins and no anal fin; five external gill slits on each side and a spiracle behind the eye; paired ventral nostrils, each divided by a flap into incurrent and excurrent openings; a cloaca between the pelvic fins, and claspers in males. The lateral line runs along each side, and the pores of the ampullae of Lorenzini are scattered over the snout.
- *Skin.* Placoid scales cover the body; the dermis is thick, with layers of collagen fibres wound helically around the body, which act as an external tendon for the swimming muscles.
- *Skeleton.* The chondrocranium is a single cartilaginous box with nasal and otic capsules and an orbit; the jaws are slung from it by the hyomandibula (hyostylic suspension, [7.17.58](/7.17.58)). The vertebrae have calcified centra (amphicoelous, with remnants of the notochord between them) and neural and haemal arches; small ribs project into the horizontal septum. The pectoral girdle is a U-shaped scapulocoracoid not attached to the skull; the pelvic girdle is a bar of cartilage. The fins are supported by basal and radial cartilages and, in the fin web, by horny rays (ceratotrichia).
- *Muscles.* Myomeres in a series of nested cones, divided by the horizontal septum into epaxial and hypaxial muscles; branchiomeric muscles of the jaws and gill arches ([7.17.62](/7.17.62)).
- *Digestion.* A large J-shaped stomach, a short intestine with a spiral valve that increases the absorptive surface and slows the passage of food, a large two-lobed liver with a gall bladder, a pancreas and a spleen. The rectal gland opens into the rectum.
- *Respiration.* The gill arches carry gill filaments on each side of interbranchial septa that extend to the body surface and form the flaps of the gill slits; water is pumped by a buccal pump and parabranchial pumps (the chambers outside the gills), and in fast-swimming sharks by **ram ventilation**, in which forward motion drives water through the open mouth ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.2)). Some pelagic sharks must swim continuously to breathe.
- *Circulation.* The heart has a sinus venosus, an atrium, a ventricle and a muscular conus arteriosus with several rows of valves; the ventral aorta supplies five pairs of afferent branchial arteries, and the efferent arteries collect into the dorsal aorta ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)). There is a renal portal system.
- *Excretion and reproduction.* Long opisthonephric kidneys; in males the anterior part of the kidney and the archinephric duct carry sperm, and the ducts open into the cloaca. Females have paired ovaries (only the right one functional in some species) and oviducts with a shell gland and a uterus ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)).
- *Nervous system and sense organs.* A brain with large olfactory bulbs and forebrain, a large cerebellum; ten pairs of cranial nerves plus a terminal nerve. Large eyes with a reflecting tapetum behind the retina, an inner ear with three canals and a large sacculus, the lateral line, and the ampullae of Lorenzini (§46.5.2).

## 46.5. Life in Water: Sharks and Rays

### 46.5.1. Buoyancy and the Heterocercal Tail

Cartilage and calcified cartilage are lighter than bone, but shark tissues are still denser than seawater, and chondrichthyans have no gas-filled swim bladder. They reduce their density with a large liver rich in low-density oils: triglycerides and, in many deep-sea sharks, the hydrocarbon squalene (density about $0.86\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$) and alkoxy-diglycerides. In deep-sea dogfishes and the basking shark the liver may make up a quarter of the body mass. The model below shows how large a liver is needed.

**Example 46.5.1 (How Big a Liver for Neutral Buoyancy?)** Let a shark consist of lean tissue of density $\rho_{\mathrm{b}}$ and a liver of density $\rho_{\mathrm{L}}$ occupying a fraction $\varphi$ of the body volume. Its mean density is $\bar\rho = \varphi\rho_{\mathrm{L}} + (1 - \varphi)\rho_{\mathrm{b}}$, and it is neutrally buoyant in seawater of density $\rho_{\mathrm{w}}$ when $\bar\rho = \rho_{\mathrm{w}}$, that is when

$$
\varphi^* = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{b}} - \rho_{\mathrm{w}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{b}} - \rho_{\mathrm{L}}}.
$$
With $\rho_{\mathrm{b}} = 1.075\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$, $\rho_{\mathrm{w}} = 1.026\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ and a squalene-rich liver of $\rho_{\mathrm{L}} = 0.90\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$, $\varphi^* = 0.049/0.175 = 0.28$; as a fraction of body mass, $\varphi^*\rho_{\mathrm{L}}/\rho_{\mathrm{w}} \approx 0.25$. A liver of triglycerides ($\rho_{\mathrm{L}} \approx 0.95$) would have to fill 39 % of the body, which is anatomically impossible; such a shark remains negatively buoyant and must generate lift. Its net weight in water is the fraction $(\bar\rho - \rho_{\mathrm{w}})/\bar\rho$ of its weight in air, a few per cent, and the lift $\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathrm{w}}U^2SC_{L}$ of its fins and body (area $S$, lift coefficient $C_{L}$) balances it at a minimum speed $U$. For a $10\,\mathrm{kg}$ shark with $S = 0.06\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and $C_{L} = 0.4$, a lean body needs about $0.6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, roughly one body length per second, which is close to the cruising speed of many sharks.

:::

![A model of buoyancy from an oil-rich liver. (A) Net weight in seawater, as a fraction of weight in air, as a function of the fraction of body volume occupied by the liver, for a liver of density 0.90 (rich in squalene, as in deep-sea sharks) and 0.95 (rich in triglycerides); the dot marks neutral buoyancy, reached with a liver of about 28 % of body volume in the first case and not within anatomically possible sizes in the second. (B) The minimum swimming speed at which the lift of fins and body balances the net weight, for a 10 kg shark with a lifting area of 0.06 m² and a lift coefficient of 0.4. Densities and dimensions are round illustrative values (Example 46.5.1), not measurements of a particular species.](./plots/shark-buoyancy.py)

The trade-off is visible across species. Deep-sea squaloid sharks, which move slowly in a food-poor environment, have huge squalene-rich livers and are nearly neutrally buoyant; fast pelagic sharks such as the mako and blue shark have smaller livers, are denser than seawater and generate lift by swimming; bottom-living sharks and rays, which rest on the seafloor, are the densest. The sand tiger shark gulps air into its stomach to adjust its buoyancy.

The asymmetrical *heterocercal tail*, in which the vertebral column turns up into the dorsal lobe, was classically thought to produce a forward and upward thrust that lifts the tail, balanced by the lift of the flat head and the pectoral fins in front of the centre of mass. Flow measurements with particle image velocimetry on swimming dogfishes and leopard sharks (Wilga and Lauder 2002) confirmed that the tail pushes water backward and downward, producing lift at the rear, but showed that the pectoral fins of sharks swimming steadily produce little lift; the body itself, held at a small positive angle of attack, generates the forward lift. Sharks adjust the body angle to their buoyancy and speed. Fast pelagic sharks (the lamnids) have nearly symmetrical, crescent-shaped (lunate) tails, as tunas do, which produce thrust efficiently.

### 46.5.2. Sense Organs and Electroreception

Sharks find food with a series of senses that operate at decreasing distances. Smell works over hundreds of metres in a current: the nasal sacs, through which water flows continuously, detect amino acids and other substances at concentrations of about $10^{-9}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$; the "blood-smelling" sensitivity of sharks is similar to that of bony fishes. Hearing of low-frequency sounds and the **lateral line** detect movements of prey over tens of metres down to a few body lengths ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.1)). Vision is good in dim light because of a tapetum lucidum, a reflecting layer of guanine crystals behind the retina; many sharks have colour-blind retinas with a single cone type. At the last moment of an attack, at a distance of a few tens of centimetres, **electroreception** takes over.

The electroreceptors of chondrichthyans are the *ampullae of Lorenzini*, described by Stefano Lorenzini in 1678. Each is a canal filled with a conductive jelly that opens at a pore in the skin and ends in an ampulla lined with sensory hair cells; the canals radiate from clusters beneath the skin of the head, so that each canal measures the potential difference between its pore and the inside of the body. Adrian Kalmijn showed in the early 1970s that a shark buried in sand attacked a flatfish hidden beneath agar, which blocks smell and vision but not electric fields, and that it attacked buried electrodes producing the field of a fish in preference to buried pieces of fish. Their threshold is of the order of $5\,\mathrm{nV\,cm^{-1}}$, the most sensitive electroreception known. Sharks also respond to the electric fields induced by their own motion through the Earth's magnetic field and may use them for orientation. The flattened head of hammerheads (the cephalofoil) spreads the ampullae over a wide area. The physiology of electroreceptors is in [7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5.3).

### 46.5.3. Osmoregulation with Urea

Marine chondrichthyans keep the concentration of sodium chloride in their plasma at about half that of seawater, like bony fishes, but they make up the osmotic difference with organic solutes: urea at about $350\text{–}400\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and trimethylamine oxide (TMAO) at about half that concentration. Their body fluids are thus slightly hyperosmotic to seawater, and water enters across the gills by osmosis; it is excreted by the kidney with little effort. Excess sodium chloride is secreted by the rectal gland, and urea is retained by the gills, which are almost impermeable to it, and by the kidney, which reabsorbs it actively ([7.14.21](/7.14.21#sec-21.5.4)). Urea at these concentrations destabilizes proteins, and TMAO counteracts it; the two are present in the ratio of about 2:1 at which their effects on protein structure cancel. Chimaeras and the coelacanth use the same strategy ([7.17.48](/7.17.48)). A few elasmobranchs enter fresh water: the bull shark *Carcharhinus leucas* lowers its urea concentration and can live in rivers for long periods, and the South American river stingrays (Potamotrygonidae) are permanent freshwater fishes that have lost the ability to retain urea.

### 46.5.4. Reproduction and Life History

All chondrichthyans have internal fertilization. Mating can be violent: the male grasps the female's pectoral fin or flank with his teeth, which is why females of many species have thicker skin than males. The modes of reproduction range from egg laying to placental viviparity, and most species practise one of the forms of viviparity ([7.14.38](/7.14.38#sec-38.9.1)).

| Mode | Nutrition of the embryo | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Oviparity | Yolk only; large egg cases of collagen ("mermaid's purses") with tendrils or horns, attached to the bottom; development several months to more than a year | All chimaeras and skates (Rajidae), horn sharks, catsharks (about 40 % of chondrichthyans) |
| Yolk-sac viviparity (formerly "ovoviviparity") | Yolk only; the embryos develop in the uterus and are born as miniature adults | Spiny dogfish (gestation 18–24 months), whale shark (up to 300 embryos in one female), angel sharks |
| Histotrophy | Uterine secretion ("uterine milk") rich in lipids and proteins, secreted by villi (trophonemata) of the uterine wall | Stingrays and mantas |
| Oophagy and embryophagy | The embryos eat unfertilized eggs ovulated by the mother; in the sand tiger the largest embryo in each uterus eats its siblings | Mackerel sharks (Lamniformes): great white, mako, sand tiger |
| Placental viviparity | After the yolk is used up, the yolk sac fuses with the uterine wall to form a yolk-sac placenta that transfers nutrients from the mother | Requiem sharks (Carcharhinidae), hammerheads, smooth-hounds such as *Mustelus* |

Viviparity has evolved many times in elasmobranchs from oviparous ancestors, and matrotrophy (maternal provision beyond yolk) several times within the viviparous lineages.

The life history of chondrichthyans is slow: they grow slowly, mature late, produce few, large young after long gestations, and live long. The spiny dogfish matures at about 10–20 years; radiocarbon dating of the eye lens nucleus of Greenland sharks gave ages of at least 272 years and probably about 400 years for the largest females (Nielsen et al. 2016), the longest lifespan known in vertebrates. Such species recover very slowly from fishing: a population whose females first breed at 15 years and produce a few young every two years cannot sustain fishing mortality of more than a few per cent per year.

### 46.5.5. Sharks and People

About 37 % of the roughly 1200 chondrichthyan species are threatened with extinction according to the IUCN Red List (Dulvy et al. 2021), mainly because of overfishing, including the large bycatch of fisheries for other species and the trade in shark fins, of which East Asia has been the largest market. Oceanic sharks declined by about 70 % in abundance between 1970 and 2018 (Pacoureau et al. 2021). Sawfishes, guitarfishes and wedgefishes are the most threatened. Several species, including the whale shark, the basking shark, the great white shark, the hammerheads and the mantas, are listed in CITES, which regulates their international trade. In China, the whale shark is a nationally protected animal, shark fin was removed from official banquets in 2013, and consumption of fins in China has fallen considerably since then.

## 46.6. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

The Chinese textbooks treat the cartilaginous fishes as a class of the "Pisces" (fishes) with two subclasses, Holocephali and Elasmobranchii, the latter divided into the superorders Selachomorpha (sharks) and Batoidea (rays), and list the orders with Chinese examples. The structure of this classification agrees with the current one; the differences are listed below.

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Class Pisces (fishes) containing Chondrichthyes and Osteichthyes | "Fishes" are a paraphyletic group: the osteichthyans include the tetrapods, and the chondrichthyans are the sister group of the osteichthyans as a whole |
| Placoderms and acanthodians as separate extinct classes, Placodermi and Acanthodii | Both are paraphyletic: placoderms are stem gnathostomes, acanthodians stem chondrichthyans; *Entelognathus* links placoderms to the osteichthyan jaw bones |
| About 846 species of chondrichthyans, more than 260 in China | About 1200 species, with many species of deep-water sharks and rays described since the 1990s |
| Batoids derived from sharks | Sharks and batoids are sister groups |
| Isuriformes (= Lamniformes); Pristiformes; Rajiformes in a broad sense | Lamniformes; Rhinopristiformes (sawfishes with guitarfishes and wedgefishes); Rajiformes (skates) |
| *Notorhynchus platycephalus*, *Pristis cuspidata*, *Dasyatis akajei*, *Mobula japanica* | *Notorynchus cepedianus*, *Anoxypristis cuspidata*, *Hemitrygon akajei*, *Mobula mobular* (current names) |
| "Ovoviviparity" as a separate reproductive mode | Classified as yolk-sac viviparity (lecithotrophic viviparity); other forms of viviparity are matrotrophic |
| Chondrichthyan skeleton "primitive", cartilage preceding bone | The skeleton of chondrichthyans lost bone; bone appeared earlier in jawless stem gnathostomes |

The textbook characters of the class (cartilaginous endoskeleton, placoid scales, five to seven gill slits opening directly to the outside, usually a heterocercal tail, no swim bladder, a spiral valve, claspers and internal fertilization) and of the two subclasses (in holocephalans the fusion of the upper jaw to the braincase, four gill slits covered by an operculum, a fin spine, the cephalic and prepelvic claspers, no cloaca) are included in §46.4.

## Summary

- Gnathostomes (Definition 46.1.1) add to the vertebrate body plan jaws of the first pharyngeal arch with teeth, pelvic fins and girdles, jointed gill arches medial to the gills, a horizontal septum, three semicircular canals, paired nasal sacs, myelin, gonoducts, RAG-based adaptive immunity and the second genome duplication. Jaws are levers that trade force for speed (Example 46.1.1) and also serve ventilation.
- The jaws are the specialized skeleton of the first pharyngeal arch, serially homologous with the gill arches; they did not evolve from an adult gill arch used for breathing. Development explains their origin: a *Hox*-free first arch, the nested *Dlx* code that distinguishes upper and lower elements with a joint between them, and the heterotopic shift of crest migration made possible by the separation of the nasal and hypophyseal placodes.
- Paired fins arose from the lateral plate mesoderm, pectoral fins before pelvic fins (modified fin-fold hypothesis).
- Placoderms are a paraphyletic series of stem gnathostomes (antiarchs, arthrodires, ptyctodonts with internal fertilization); the Silurian *Entelognathus* and *Qilinyu* of Yunnan show the origin of the osteichthyan marginal jaw bones; the early Silurian fishes of Chongqing and Guizhou are the oldest jawed vertebrates and teeth. Acanthodians are stem chondrichthyans.
- Chondrichthyans (Definition 46.4.1): prismatic calcified cartilage without bone, placoid scales (Definition 46.4.2), teeth replaced in rows, claspers (Definition 46.4.3), internal fertilization. Holocephali (Definition 46.4.4): autostylic jaws, tooth plates, operculum. Elasmobranchii (Definition 46.4.5): sharks and batoids, sister groups.
- Buoyancy from an oily liver (Example 46.5.1), lift from the body and the heterocercal tail; senses from smell to electroreception by the ampullae of Lorenzini; osmoregulation with urea and TMAO and a rectal gland; oviparity, yolk-sac viviparity and several forms of matrotrophy; slow life histories make sharks vulnerable to overfishing.

## Questions

1. What evidence supports the idea that jaws are serial homologues of the gill arches, and what are the main objections to deriving them from an adult gill arch?

   **Answer** Supporting evidence: the jaws and gill arches develop from the same kind of embryonic pharyngeal arch, each with a neural crest skeleton, mesodermal muscles, a cranial nerve and an aortic arch; the palatoquadrate and Meckel's cartilage correspond in position to the dorsal and ventral elements of a gill arch; the jaw muscles are serially homologous with the gill-arch constrictors; the spiracle lies in the position of a gill slit between the mandibular and hyoid arches; and the nested *Dlx* code is used in all arches. Objections: the cyclostome gill skeleton lies lateral to the gills, the gnathostome one medial, so the jawless ancestor may not have had shark-like arches; no fossil shows an intermediate; there is no evidence for lost premandibular arches; and the mandibular arch never bore gills in any known vertebrate. The jaws are the first arch, specialized, not a converted respiratory arch.
2. Explain the heterotopy theory of jaw origin and how the galeaspid *Shuyu* bears on it.

   **Answer** In lampreys a single median nasohypophyseal placode forms in front of the mouth and prevents neural crest cells from migrating forward beneath the forebrain; the mandibular crest forms the upper lip, lower lip and velum around this complex. In gnathostomes the nasal placodes are paired and lateral and the hypophyseal placode moves into the roof of the mouth, so crest cells can migrate forward between the nasal placodes to form the trabeculae and the front of the face, and the mandibular arch crest behind them forms the upper and lower jaws. The change in where tissues meet, a heterotopy, was thus the key step. *Shuyu*, a jawless galeaspid from the Silurian, already has separate paired olfactory organs and a hypophyseal duct opening into the mouth: the predicted precondition existed before jaws, in the jawless sister lineage of gnathostomes.
3. Why is the absence of bone in chondrichthyans considered a derived condition?

   **Answer** Because bone is present in groups that branch earlier on the gnathostome stem: the dermal armour of heterostracans and osteostracans, the cellular and perichondral bone of osteostracans, the extensive dermal and perichondral bone of placoderms, and the marginal jaw bones of *Entelognathus*. The most parsimonious explanation is that the common ancestor of chondrichthyans and osteichthyans had bone and that chondrichthyans lost it, retaining bone-like tissue only in the bases of scales and teeth. The elephant shark genome supports this: it lacks genes for secretory calcium-binding phosphoproteins that bony fishes use to mineralize bone.
4. Using Example 46.5.1, explain why fast pelagic sharks are negatively buoyant while deep-sea dogfishes are nearly neutrally buoyant.

   **Answer** Neutral buoyancy requires a liver fraction $\varphi^* = (\rho_{\mathrm{b}} - \rho_{\mathrm{w}})/(\rho_{\mathrm{b}} - \rho_{\mathrm{L}})$, about a quarter of body mass with a squalene-rich liver. A liver that large adds bulk and drag and stores energy as oil rather than as muscle; for a fast predator that must accelerate and swim continuously for ventilation, it is cheaper to carry a smaller liver and generate the small lift needed (a few per cent of body weight) from the body and fins, since at cruising speeds this lift comes almost free. In the food-poor deep sea, sharks swim slowly and save energy; for them lift from swimming would be expensive relative to their low metabolic rate, and neutral buoyancy from a huge low-density liver is favoured.
5. How do marine elasmobranchs avoid losing water to seawater, and why do they need TMAO?

   **Answer** They retain urea (about $350\text{–}400\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$) and TMAO in their body fluids, raising the total osmotic concentration to slightly above that of seawater while keeping sodium chloride at about half the seawater concentration. Water therefore enters by osmosis instead of leaving, and the kidney excretes the excess; the rectal gland secretes the salt that diffuses in. Urea is retained because the gills are nearly impermeable to it and the kidney reabsorbs it. At such concentrations urea destabilizes proteins; TMAO stabilizes them, and at a ratio of about two urea to one methylamine the effects cancel, so enzymes function normally.
6. (Open) Design a field experiment to test whether a shark uses electroreception in the final phase of an attack, and predict the result.

   **Answer** Following Kalmijn: in a large tank or sheltered sea area, bury a live flatfish in sand under (a) a chamber of agar, which passes electric fields but blocks chemical and visual cues, and (b) the same chamber covered with a thin electrically insulating plastic film, which blocks electric fields; also bury pairs of electrodes passing a weak current that mimics the bioelectric field of a fish, next to pieces of fish in chambers that release odour. Record approaches and strikes with cameras. Prediction: sharks attracted by odour from a distance will strike at the agar-covered fish and at the active electrodes, even preferring the electrodes to the odour source in the last few tens of centimetres, but will not strike the insulated fish. Controls: inactive electrodes, and sharks with the ampullae temporarily blocked or anaesthetized locally.
7. Why are sharks and rays more vulnerable to overfishing than most bony fishes of similar size?

   **Answer** Because of their life history: late maturity (often after a decade or more), low fecundity (a few to a few dozen young per litter rather than millions of eggs), long gestation and often breeding only every second or third year. Their populations therefore have a low maximum rate of increase, and the fishing mortality they can sustain is small; a bony fish population with very high fecundity can recover quickly when early survival improves, a shark population cannot. In addition, many sharks are caught as bycatch in fisheries managed for other species, and high value of fins encourages retaining them.
