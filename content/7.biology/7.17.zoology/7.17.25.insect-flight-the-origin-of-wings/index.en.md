Insects were the first animals to fly, more than 100 million years before pterosaurs, and they remain the most diverse fliers. Flight explains much of their success: it allows escape from predators, the search for scattered food, mates and egg-laying sites, and the colonization of new habitats ([7.17.22](/7.17.22)). It is also one of the most demanding activities in biology. Insect flight muscle has the highest known metabolic rates of any tissue, and the air flows around a beating insect wing are so unsteady that classical aerodynamics, developed for aircraft, cannot explain how a bumblebee stays aloft.

The chapter follows the problem from origin to mechanism. Section 25.1 asks where wings came from, Section 25.2 how the thorax and its muscles move them, Section 25.3 how flapping wings produce force and at what energetic cost, and Section 25.4 why so many insects have folded or lost their wings. The anatomy of the thorax and wings is described in [7.17.24](/7.17.24); the physiology of synchronous and asynchronous flight muscle in [7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.6.5); the physics of flow at low Reynolds numbers in [7.6.22](/7.6.22).

## 25.1. The Origin of Wings

### 25.1.1. The Fossil Record

All winged insects descend from a single winged ancestor: the wings of all Pterygota share venation, articulation and development, and wings are a synapomorphy of the clade ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)). Yet their origin is poorly documented. Hexapods are known from the Early Devonian (about 410 Ma), and the Devonian *Rhyniognatha*, known only from mandibles, has been interpreted as a dicondylic insect and possibly a winged one, though some authors regard it as a myriapod. Between the Middle Devonian and the mid-Carboniferous there is a gap of some 60 million years with almost no insect fossils (the "hexapod gap"), and when winged insects appear, about 325 million years ago, they are already diverse, with fully formed wings and several orders.

Carboniferous and Permian insects include the Palaeodictyoptera, with sucking beaks and small winglet-like lobes on the prothorax, the Meganisoptera (griffinflies), relatives of dragonflies, one of which, *Meganeuropsis permiana*, had a wingspan of about 70 cm ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)), and early relatives of mayflies, cockroaches and orthopterans. Nymphs of some Palaeozoic species had articulated wing pads on all three thoracic segments and curved wing pads on the abdomen, which suggests that wing-like structures were once serially repeated.

### 25.1.2. Three Hypotheses

Two old hypotheses and a recent synthesis compete.

The *paranotal* (tergal) hypothesis, proposed by Müller in 1873, holds that wings evolved from rigid lateral extensions of the thoracic terga, the paranota, which first improved stability during falls and gliding and only later became articulated and muscled. The prothoracic lobes of Palaeodictyoptera and the lateral expansions of the terga of silverfish were cited in support. Its weakness is that it must invent the articulation and muscles anew.

The *exite* (pleural, gill) hypothesis, revived by Wigglesworth and Kukalová-Peck, derives wings from an exite, a lateral outgrowth of a basal leg segment (the epicoxa), which was incorporated into the body wall together with the basal leg segments themselves. The wing would then inherit an articulation and muscles from the leg. Abdominal gill plates of mayfly nymphs and the coxal styli of bristletails are interpreted as serial homologues of such exites; the epicoxal and subcoxal leg segments would have become the pleural sclerites and the axillary region.

The *dual origin* hypothesis combines the two. It is based on developmental genetics. The wing primordium in *Drosophila* forms from a group of cells that separates early from the leg primordium, and expresses genes that are also expressed in crustacean leg exites (the transcription factors *vestigial*, *apterous* and *nubbin*). In the beetle *Tribolium castaneum*, knocking down wing genes reduced not only the wings but also structures of the terga of segments without wings and of the pleura, suggesting that both tergal and pleural tissues contribute to the wing (Clark-Hachtel and Tomoyasu 2016 and later work). A study of the expression of leg-patterning genes in crustaceans and insects concluded that the insect body wall incorporates proximal leg segments, so that a leg exite and the tergum could both lie close to where the wing forms (Bruce and Patel 2020). In this view the wing blade has a tergal origin while its hinge, muscles and part of its gene network were recruited from the leg, an example of **co-option** and of **deep homology** between insect wings and crustacean exites ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

### 25.1.3. Functional Intermediates

A wing too small to fly is useful only if each intermediate stage had a function. Several have been proposed and tested in living insects:

- *Aerial righting and gliding*: wingless arboreal jumping bristletails (Archaeognatha) that fall from tree trunks can steer their descent back to the trunk using their legs and caudal filament (Yanoviak and colleagues 2009), showing that directed aerial descent is possible without wings; small lateral lobes would improve it.
- *Surface skimming*: some stoneflies and mayflies move across the water surface by beating their wings while their bodies or legs remain in the water; with smaller wings the speed decreases gradually instead of failing abruptly (Marden and Kramer 1994). Surface skimming has been proposed as an intermediate between aquatic gills and aerial flight.
- *Thermoregulation*: models of insects with lobes of different sizes showed that small lobes improve heating in sunlight, whereas only larger lobes improve aerodynamic performance, and that the transition from one function to the other depends on body size (Kingsolver and Koehl 1985).
- *Gills or ventilation*: in an aquatic ancestor, movable exites could have ventilated gills and later produced thrust.

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. Phylogeny suggests that the immediate ancestor of the Pterygota was terrestrial: its closest relatives, the silverfish and bristletails, are terrestrial, and the aquatic nymphs of mayflies and dragonflies are probably secondarily aquatic.

## 25.2. Flight Muscles and the Thorax

### 25.2.1. Direct and Indirect Flight Muscles

Each wing is a lever whose inner end is attached to the margin of the notum and which rests a little further out on the pleural wing process, the fulcrum ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)). Muscles can move it in two ways.

**Definition 25.2.1 (Indirect Flight Muscle)** **Indirect flight muscles** are the flight muscles of insects that are not attached to the wings but move them by deforming the thorax: the dorsal longitudinal muscles, running between the phragmata of successive segments, whose contraction bows the notum upwards and lowers the wings, and the dorsoventral muscles, running from the notum to the sternum or leg bases, whose contraction pulls the notum down and raises the wings. **Direct flight muscles** are those that insert on sclerites at the wing base (the basalare, subalare and axillary sclerites) and move the wings directly.

In Neoptera the indirect muscles supply almost all the power, and the direct muscles adjust the angle of attack, the amplitude and the folding of the wing (the third axillary muscle folds the wing back). In dragonflies, by contrast, the downstroke is powered directly by the large basalar and subalar muscles, which pull on the wing base outside the fulcrum, and the upstroke by dorsoventral muscles inserting on the wing base inside the fulcrum, so that each wing is driven independently. Fore- and hindwings of dragonflies can be operated with a phase difference, which allows dragonflies to hover and to turn sharply in pursuit of prey. Mayflies and the more primitive Neoptera are intermediate; cockroaches and locusts use indirect muscles for the downstroke and direct muscles that assist the upstroke and control.

### 25.2.2. The Elastic Thorax

A wing must be accelerated and decelerated twice in each cycle, and if the kinetic energy of the wing were lost at the end of each half-stroke, the inertial cost would be comparable to the aerodynamic cost. Most of it is recovered by elastic storage: at the end of a stroke the wing's kinetic energy deforms the thorax and the wing hinge, and the energy is returned in the next half-stroke. The thoracic box, its pleural ridges and especially resilin-rich ligaments of the wing hinge act as springs; the wing–thorax system is a resonant oscillator whose natural frequency is close to the wingbeat frequency. In flies, the thorax was long thought to snap between two stable positions through an unstable middle position ("click mechanism"); later work showed that a bistable click is not essential, but the principle of a spring-loaded thorax with a nonlinear stiffness remains.

### 25.2.3. Synchronous and Asynchronous Muscle

In **asynchronous flight muscle** the contraction frequency is not set by the motor nerve: nerve impulses arrive only every few cycles to keep the muscle activated, and the oscillation is maintained by delayed stretch activation and the mechanical resonance of the thorax ([7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.6.5)). Synchronous muscle, found in dragonflies, mayflies, Orthoptera, Lepidoptera and other groups, contracts once per nerve impulse and is limited to frequencies below about 100 Hz, mostly 10–50 Hz, by the time needed to release and resequester calcium. Asynchronous muscle has evolved independently several times (at least seven times on most estimates), in Diptera, Hymenoptera, Coleoptera, Hemiptera, Thysanoptera and others, and it allows wingbeat frequencies of 100 to 1000 Hz; the record, just over 1000 Hz, belongs to tiny ceratopogonid midges (*Forcipomyia*, Sotavalta 1953).

Flight muscle works only above a minimum temperature. Large insects such as bumblebees, hawkmoths and locusts warm their thorax before take-off by contracting the antagonistic flight muscles simultaneously, which shivers the thorax without moving the wings; bumblebees maintain thoracic temperatures above 30 °C in cool air ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)). Heat produced by the flight muscles is retained by insulating hairs or scales and, in some species, carried to the abdomen by the hemolymph to prevent overheating.

## 25.3. The Aerodynamics of Flapping Flight

### 25.3.1. Reynolds Numbers

The flow around a wing is characterized by the **Reynolds number**, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces,

$$
Re = \frac{U c}{\nu}, \tag{25.1}
$$

where $U$ is the speed of the wing, $c$ its chord and $\nu \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ the kinematic viscosity of air. Insect wings work between $Re \approx 10$ (thrips and tiny parasitoid wasps less than 1 mm long) and $Re \approx 10^4$ (large moths, dragonflies and locusts), compared with about $10^6$–$10^7$ for an aircraft wing. At the lowest Reynolds numbers air is "viscous" for the insect, and the bristled wings of thrips and featherwing beetles act like solid plates because the boundary layers around neighbouring bristles overlap ([7.6.22](/7.6.22)).

### 25.3.2. The Quasi-Steady Test: the "Bumblebee Paradox"

A wing moving steadily through air at speed $U$ produces a lift force

$$
L = \tfrac12 \rho\, C_L\, S\, U^2, \tag{25.2}
$$

where $\rho$ is the density of air, $S$ the wing area and $C_L$ the lift coefficient, which depends on wing shape, angle of attack and Reynolds number. Steady insect wings in wind tunnels reach maximum lift coefficients of about 0.8–1.2 before they stall. The *quasi-steady assumption* treats a flapping wing at each instant as if it were moving steadily at its instantaneous velocity and integrates Eq. (25.2) along the span (blade-element theory) and over the stroke. For hovering with a sinusoidal stroke of amplitude $\Phi$ (radians) and frequency $f$, the angular velocity of the wing has mean square $(\pi \Phi f)^2/2$; integrating over the span with $r_2$ the radius of the second moment of wing area (as a fraction of the wing length $R$), the mean lift equals the weight $mg$ when

$$
\bar C_L = \frac{4 m g}{\rho\, S\, r_2^2 R^2 \pi^2 \Phi^2 f^2}, \tag{25.3}
$$

with $S$ the area of both wings. This is the mean lift coefficient required for hovering.

**Example 25.3.1** For *Drosophila melanogaster*, $m = 1.0\,\mathrm{mg}$, $R = 2.5\,\mathrm{mm}$, area of one wing about $1.9\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ (so $S = 3.8 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$), $\Phi = 2.6\,\mathrm{rad}$ (150°), $f = 220\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $r_2 = 0.55$. Eq. (25.3) gives

$$
\bar C_L = \frac{4 \times 10^{-6} \times 9.81}{1.2 \times 3.8\times 10^{-6} \times 0.30 \times 6.25 \times 10^{-6} \times 9.87 \times 6.76 \times 4.84\times 10^{4}} \approx 1.4.
$$

The mean speed at $r_2$ is $U \approx 2\Phi f r_2 R \approx 1.6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and the mean chord about $0.8\,\mathrm{mm}$, so $Re \approx 80$. A mean $C_L$ of 1.4 means that over much of the stroke the wing must produce more than its steady maximum; quasi-steady aerodynamics cannot support the fly.

### 25.3.3. Unsteady Mechanisms

Experiments with tethered insects, smoke visualization around a flapping hawkmoth (Ellington and colleagues 1996) and dynamically scaled robotic wings in mineral oil (the "robofly" of Dickinson, Lehmann and Sane 1999) revealed several unsteady mechanisms.

**Definition 25.3.1 (Leading-Edge Vortex)** A **leading-edge vortex** is a vortex formed by flow separation at the leading edge of a wing moving at a high angle of attack, which remains attached over the upper surface throughout the stroke and enhances lift by lowering the pressure above the wing.

On an aircraft wing at a high angle of attack a leading-edge vortex grows and is shed within a few chord lengths, and lift collapses (stall). The leading-edge vortex of an insect wing does not detach during the half-stroke, because a revolving wing travels only a few chord lengths per stroke and because the rotation of the wing about its base stabilizes the vortex (the centripetal and Coriolis accelerations of a revolving wing, characterized by a low Rossby number, and an outward flow along the span which drains vorticity towards the tip). This "delayed stall" is the main source of lift in most insects and also in hovering hummingbirds and bats, and it gives the high lift coefficients observed in robotic wings.

Further mechanisms add force at the ends of each half-stroke, when the wing flips over:

- *Rotational circulation*: when the wing rotates about its long axis while still translating, it generates additional circulation, as a spinning ball does (Kramer effect); rotation slightly before stroke reversal increases lift.
- *Wake capture*: at the start of the new half-stroke the wing meets the wake it shed during the previous one, recovering some of the energy left in it.
- *Added mass*: the wing must accelerate the air around it, which produces transient forces at stroke reversal.

**Definition 25.3.2 (Clap-and-Fling)** **Clap-and-fling** is a lift-enhancing mechanism of flapping flight in which the two wings touch each other above the body at the end of the upstroke (clap) and then separate by rotating about their trailing edges (fling), so that air rushing into the widening gap establishes circulations of opposite sign around the two wings at the start of the downstroke.

Clap-and-fling was discovered by Weis-Fogh (1973) in the tiny parasitoid wasp *Encarsia formosa*, which uses it at every stroke. It solves a problem of starting a wing from rest: the circulation around an aerofoil normally builds up slowly (the Wagner effect), but in the fling each wing starts with a circulation already established, equal and opposite to that of its partner, so the total circulation remains zero as required by Kelvin's theorem. The clap also expels a jet of air backwards, adding thrust. The price is wear of the wings that touch at every stroke. Clap-and-fling is used regularly by thrips, small wasps and whiteflies, and occasionally by butterflies, locusts and *Drosophila* in tethered flight or during take-off; a partial version, the "clap and peel", is used by larger insects.

![The clap-and-fling (Weis-Fogh mechanism), schematic: wing sections (chords) seen along the span, leading edge marked by a dot, at the top of the stroke. 1–2, clap: the wings approach dorsally, the leading edges meet first and the closing gap pushes air out backwards as a jet, adding thrust. 3–4, fling: the wings rotate apart about their trailing edges; air rushes into the opening gap, and each wing begins the downstroke with a bound circulation already established (circular arrows, opposite in sign), so that lift develops at once without the slow build-up of circulation (Wagner effect). ### 25.3.4. Power

The power required for flight has three components. *Induced power* is the rate at which kinetic energy is given to the downward jet of air that supports the weight; *profile power* is the work against drag on the wings; *inertial power* is the cost of accelerating the wing mass, largely recovered by elastic storage (Section 25.2.2). Induced power can be estimated from momentum theory. A hovering animal is treated as an actuator disc of area $A$ (the area swept by the wings) that accelerates air downward; by conservation of momentum the thrust equals the weight $W = mg$ when the induced velocity at the disc is $w = \sqrt{W/(2\rho A)}$, and the induced power is

$$
P_{\mathrm{ind}} = W w = \sqrt{\frac{W^3}{2 \rho A}}, \tag{25.4}
$$

the Rankine–Froude estimate (a minimum; real wakes are less uniform, and corrections increase it by 10–30%). For two wings of length $R$ with a stroke amplitude $\Phi$ the swept area is $A = \Phi R^2$. Eq. (25.4) shows that the cost of hovering rises with the *disc loading* $W/A$: the specific induced power $P_{\mathrm{ind}}/m$ is proportional to $\sqrt{W/A}$.

**Example 25.3.2** A bumblebee worker with $m = 175\,\mathrm{mg}$ ($W = 1.7\,\mathrm{mN}$), $R = 13\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\Phi = 2.0\,\mathrm{rad}$ has $A = 3.4 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Eq. (25.4) gives $P_{\mathrm{ind}} = \sqrt{(1.7\times 10^{-3})^3/(2 \times 1.2 \times 3.4 \times 10^{-4})} \approx 2.5\,\mathrm{mW}$, about 14 W per kilogram of body mass. With profile power of a similar order and a muscle efficiency of about 10%, the metabolic rate of hovering is several hundred watts per kilogram of body mass, consistent with measured rates that are 50–100 times the resting rate. Bumblebees loaded with nectar or pollen up to nearly their own mass must raise their disc loading, and the power required increases as $W^{3/2}$.

### 25.3.5. Scaling of Wingbeat Frequency

How should wingbeat frequency change with size? If insects were geometrically similar (wing length $R \propto \ell$, wing area $\propto \ell^2$, mass $\propto \ell^3$) and flew with the same stroke amplitude and lift coefficient, then weight support, $mg \propto \rho\, S (f \Phi R)^2 \propto f^2 \ell^4$, would require $f \propto \ell^{-1/2}$, that is $f \propto m^{-1/6}$. Observed frequencies fall faster with size, roughly as $f \propto R^{-0.7}$ across insects of very different form, because large insects have relatively larger wings (lower wing loading), different stroke amplitudes, and different muscle physiology: in small insects asynchronous muscle allows frequencies far above the synchronous limit.

### 25.3.6. Stability and Control

A flapping insect is inherently unstable and must correct its attitude continuously. Information comes from the compound eyes (optic flow), the ocelli (the horizon), the antennae (air speed and flow direction, sensed by Johnston's organ) and, in flies, the **halteres**. The vibrating haltere acts as a gyroscope: when the body rotates, a Coriolis force

$$
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{C}} = -2 m_{\mathrm{h}}\, \boldsymbol{\Omega} \times \boldsymbol{v} \tag{25.5}
$$

acts on the knob of mass $m_{\mathrm{h}}$ moving with velocity $\boldsymbol{v}$ in the body frame rotating at angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$, bending the haltere stalk out of its plane of beating; campaniform sensilla at the haltere base detect this bending, and their signals reach motor neurons of the wing steering muscles within a single wingbeat. Because the Coriolis force oscillates at the beat frequency while other forces on the haltere are at twice the frequency or constant, the neural circuit can separate rotation from other signals. Halteres also act as clocks that time the activity of the steering muscles.

Steering is achieved by small asymmetries between the left and right wings in stroke amplitude, angle of attack and timing, produced by direct flight muscles attached to the axillary sclerites. *Drosophila* changes direction in rapid body saccades of about 90° completed in less than 50 ms, and recovers from perturbations within a few wingbeats.

## 25.4. Wing Folding and the Loss of Flight

### 25.4.1. Wing Folding

Neoptera fold their wings back over the abdomen using the third axillary sclerite and its muscle ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)); Palaeoptera, which lack this mechanism, hold their wings spread (dragonflies) or upright (mayflies, damselflies). Folded wings can be carried into crevices, under bark, into soil and among dense vegetation, and the forewings can be hardened as covers for the hindwings (tegmina, elytra, hemelytra). About 98% of living insect species are neopterans, and wing folding is considered a **key innovation** of the group ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

### 25.4.2. Secondary Flightlessness

Flight has been lost many times. Its costs are the energy and materials of wings and flight muscles, which can make up 10–20% of the body mass, and the risk of being carried away by wind. Flightlessness is common:

- on oceanic islands and in windy places, as Darwin noted for the many flightless beetles of Madeira, and on sub-Antarctic islands, where flightless flies and moths are common;
- at high altitudes, in cold climates and in caves;
- in parasites that live on their hosts (fleas, lice, bat flies, the females of Strepsiptera);
- in social insects, where workers are wingless and reproductives shed their wings after the mating flight (ants and termites), using the flight muscles as a source of nutrients for the first brood;
- in females of many species whose males fly (bagworm moths, winter moths, scale insects), a form of sexual dimorphism linked to fecundity.

Many species are *wing-polymorphic*, with long-winged (macropterous) and short-winged (brachypterous) or wingless (apterous) forms. In crickets of the genus *Gryllus* and in planthoppers the short-winged form reproduces earlier and produces more eggs, while the long-winged form disperses, a trade-off between flight and reproduction (the oogenesis–flight syndrome). Wing form may be determined genetically, by crowding and food quality, or by photoperiod; in aphids, crowding induces winged forms that disperse to new host plants ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)). After dispersal many insects break down their flight muscles (histolysis) and redirect the resources to reproduction.

## Questions

1. Why can a wing that is lowered by contraction of muscles not attached to it still be controlled precisely? Which muscles do the controlling?

   **Answer** The indirect muscles deform the thorax and so drive both wings symmetrically and at the resonant frequency, but the wing's angle of attack, amplitude and the left–right differences are set by small direct muscles on the basalare, subalare and axillary sclerites, which change the geometry of the hinge and so how the thoracic oscillation is transmitted to each wing. Power and control are thus separated: large asynchronous power muscles and small synchronous steering muscles that fire once per beat and can change within a single beat.

3. Using Eq. (25.4), explain why a bumblebee carrying a load of nectar equal to half its body mass needs about 80% more induced power.

   **Answer** At constant swept area $P_{\mathrm{ind}} \propto W^{3/2}$; increasing $W$ by a factor 1.5 multiplies the power by $1.5^{3/2} \approx 1.84$, an increase of about 84%. The bee can partly compensate by increasing the stroke amplitude (larger $A$) and frequency, which is what loaded bees are observed to do.

4. Why does clap-and-fling offer the greatest advantage to the smallest insects?

   **Answer** At low Reynolds numbers viscous effects are large and lift coefficients of steady wings are low, and a wing starting from rest builds circulation only slowly as it travels several chord lengths (Wagner effect); small insects with short strokes would spend much of each half-stroke building lift. The fling creates circulation immediately, so lift is available from the start of the downstroke. The cost, wear and extra drag when the wings are pulled apart in viscous air, is acceptable at these sizes; in large insects, which reach high lift through the leading-edge vortex, the benefit is smaller.

5. What observations would distinguish between the paranotal and the exite hypotheses of wing origin, and why have developmental genetic studies favoured a dual origin?

   **Answer** The paranotal hypothesis predicts that wings derive from tergal tissue only, so that wing genes should be expressed only in tergal cells and wing-like structures should form from terga without contribution from leg-derived tissue; the exite hypothesis predicts that wing cells derive from a leg exite, with the wing gene network shared with crustacean leg exites and with the leg primordium. Experiments showed both: the wing primordium separates from the leg primordium and shares gene expression with crustacean exites (supporting the exite view), but knockdown of wing genes in wingless segments affects tergal and pleural structures, and lineage analyses show tergal contributions to the blade. A wing formed by merging a tergal blade with an exite-derived hinge accounts for both kinds of evidence.

6. (Open question) Among female winter moths the wings are reduced to stubs, whereas males fly. Propose an explanation and a test.

   **Answer** Females that do not fly can put the resources saved from wings and flight muscles into eggs (fecundity increases with body mass), and in winter, with low temperatures and few predators active, dispersal by females may be less valuable, since males can find females by their pheromones. A test: compare the egg load and body mass of flightless females with those of related fully winged species of similar size, and measure dispersal and the genetic structure of populations; across a phylogeny of moths, test whether female flightlessness has evolved more often in species active in cold seasons than expected by chance (a comparative test, [7.10.26](/7.10.26)).
