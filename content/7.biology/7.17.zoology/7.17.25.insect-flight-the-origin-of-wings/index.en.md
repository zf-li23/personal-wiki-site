Insects were the first animals to fly, more than 100 million years before pterosaurs, and they remain the most diverse fliers. Flight explains much of their success: it allows escape from predators, the search for scattered food, mates and egg-laying sites, and the colonization of new habitats ([7.17.22](/7.17.22)). It is also one of the most demanding activities in biology. Insect flight muscle has the highest known metabolic rates of any tissue, and the air flows around a beating insect wing are so unsteady that classical aerodynamics, developed for aircraft, cannot explain how a bumblebee stays aloft.

The chapter follows the problem from origin to mechanism. Section 25.1 asks where wings came from, Section 25.2 how the thorax and its muscles move them, Section 25.3 how flapping wings produce force and at what energetic cost, and Section 25.4 why so many insects have folded or lost their wings. The anatomy of the thorax and wings is described in [7.17.24](/7.17.24); the physiology of synchronous and asynchronous flight muscle in [7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.6.5); the physics of flow at low Reynolds numbers in [7.6.22](/7.6.22).

## 25.1. The Origin of Wings

### 25.1.1. The Fossil Record

All winged insects descend from a single winged ancestor: the wings of all Pterygota share venation, articulation and development, and wings are a synapomorphy of the clade ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)). Yet their origin is poorly documented. Hexapods are known from the Early Devonian (about 410 Ma), and the Devonian *Rhyniognatha*, known only from mandibles, has been interpreted as a dicondylic insect and possibly a winged one, though some authors regard it as a myriapod. Between the Middle Devonian and the mid-Carboniferous there is a gap of some 60 million years with almost no insect fossils (the "hexapod gap"), and when winged insects appear, about 325 million years ago, they are already diverse, with fully formed wings and several orders.

Carboniferous and Permian insects include the Palaeodictyoptera, with sucking beaks and small winglet-like lobes on the prothorax, the Meganisoptera (griffinflies), relatives of dragonflies, one of which, *Meganeuropsis permiana*, had a wingspan of about 70 cm ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)), and early relatives of mayflies, cockroaches and orthopterans. Nymphs of some Palaeozoic species had articulated wing pads on all three thoracic segments and curved wing pads on the abdomen, which suggests that wing-like structures were once serially repeated.

The earliest winged insects show that flight was already sophisticated when it first appears in the record.

| Group | Age | Characters relevant to flight |
|---|---|---|
| Palaeodictyoptera | Mid-Carboniferous to end-Permian; *Delitzschala* (about 325 Ma) among the oldest | Two pairs of similar, broad, net-veined wings held outstretched; small articulated prothoracic winglets; piercing-sucking beak used on plant reproductive organs |
| Megasecoptera | Carboniferous–Permian | Slender, often stalked wings, held outstretched; long cerci |
| Diaphanopterodea | Carboniferous–Permian | Palaeopteran relatives that could fold their wings back over the abdomen, by a mechanism different from that of Neoptera |
| Meganisoptera (griffinflies) | Carboniferous–Permian | Relatives of dragonflies; wingspans up to about 70 cm; aerial predators with spiny legs |
| Early mayfly relatives | Late Carboniferous–Permian | Fore- and hindwings of similar size (living mayflies have small hindwings); nymphs with articulated wing pads |
| Early Neoptera (cockroach relatives, early orthopterans) | From the Late Carboniferous | Wings folded over the abdomen; tegmina-like forewings |

The fossils thus show that the Palaeoptera–Neoptera split, wing folding (twice, in Diaphanopterodea and Neoptera) and very large size had all evolved within the first twenty million years of the record. The giants are discussed together with the oxygen hypothesis in [7.17.26](/7.17.26). Wings are also preserved far more often than bodies, because their tough, flat cuticle survives transport in water; much of Palaeozoic insect classification therefore rests on venation ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)).

### 25.1.2. Three Hypotheses

Two old hypotheses and a recent synthesis compete.

The *paranotal* (tergal) hypothesis, proposed by Müller in 1873, holds that wings evolved from rigid lateral extensions of the thoracic terga, the paranota, which first improved stability during falls and gliding and only later became articulated and muscled. The prothoracic lobes of Palaeodictyoptera and the lateral expansions of the terga of silverfish were cited in support. Its weakness is that it must invent the articulation and muscles anew.

The *exite* (pleural, gill) hypothesis, revived by Wigglesworth and Kukalová-Peck, derives wings from an exite, a lateral outgrowth of a basal leg segment (the epicoxa), which was incorporated into the body wall together with the basal leg segments themselves. The wing would then inherit an articulation and muscles from the leg. Abdominal gill plates of mayfly nymphs and the coxal styli of bristletails are interpreted as serial homologues of such exites; the epicoxal and subcoxal leg segments would have become the pleural sclerites and the axillary region.

The *dual origin* hypothesis combines the two. It is based on developmental genetics. The wing primordium in *Drosophila* forms from a group of cells that separates early from the leg primordium, and expresses genes that are also expressed in crustacean leg exites (the transcription factors *vestigial*, *apterous* and *nubbin*). In the beetle *Tribolium castaneum*, knocking down wing genes reduced not only the wings but also structures of the terga of segments without wings and of the pleura, suggesting that both tergal and pleural tissues contribute to the wing (Clark-Hachtel and Tomoyasu 2016 and later work). A study of the expression of leg-patterning genes in crustaceans and insects concluded that the insect body wall incorporates proximal leg segments, so that a leg exite and the tergum could both lie close to where the wing forms (Bruce and Patel 2020). In this view the wing blade has a tergal origin while its hinge, muscles and part of its gene network were recruited from the leg, an example of **co-option** and of **deep homology** between insect wings and crustacean exites ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

The genetic evidence comes from several kinds of experiment. In *Tribolium* the first thoracic segment and the abdominal segments carry no wings, yet they contain groups of cells that express *vestigial* and other wing genes; when the Hox genes that normally suppress wings in these segments are knocked down, these cells grow into ectopic wings (Ohde, Yaginuma and Niimi 2013; Clark-Hachtel, Linz and Tomoyasu 2013). The pronotum of beetles and the gin traps (pinching structures between pupal abdominal segments) are therefore wing serial homologues, modified by Hox genes, as the treehopper helmet has been proposed to be ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)). In the mayfly *Cloeon dipterum* the abdominal gills of the nymph express a set of genes that overlaps with that of the wings (Almudi and coauthors 2020), which fits the old idea that the gills and wings are serial homologues. In cockroaches knockdown of *vestigial* reduces both wings and the lateral expansions of the pronotum (Elias-Neto and Belles 2016).

### 25.1.3. Functional Intermediates

A wing too small to fly is useful only if each intermediate stage had a function. Several have been proposed and tested in living insects:

- *Aerial righting and gliding*: wingless arboreal jumping bristletails (Archaeognatha) that fall from tree trunks can steer their descent back to the trunk using their legs and caudal filament (Yanoviak and colleagues 2009), showing that directed aerial descent is possible without wings; small lateral lobes would improve it.
- *Surface skimming*: some stoneflies and mayflies move across the water surface by beating their wings while their bodies or legs remain in the water; with smaller wings the speed decreases gradually instead of failing abruptly (Marden and Kramer 1994). Surface skimming has been proposed as an intermediate between aquatic gills and aerial flight.
- *Thermoregulation*: models of insects with lobes of different sizes showed that small lobes improve heating in sunlight, whereas only larger lobes improve aerodynamic performance, and that the transition from one function to the other depends on body size (Kingsolver and Koehl 1985).
- *Gills or ventilation*: in an aquatic ancestor, movable exites could have ventilated gills and later produced thrust.

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. Phylogeny suggests that the immediate ancestor of the Pterygota was terrestrial: its closest relatives, the silverfish and bristletails, are terrestrial, and the aquatic nymphs of mayflies and dragonflies are probably secondarily aquatic.

The interpretation of these intermediate stages depends on phylogeny. James Marden's surface-skimming stoneflies are an attractive model, but stoneflies are neopterans whose ancestors flew, so their skimming may be a secondary simplification rather than a relic, and critics have argued that the behaviour says more about what wings can do than about how they arose. The thermoregulation hypothesis is supported by physical models but is difficult to test on fossils. Most authors now see the evolution of flight as a sequence in which lobes on the thorax, perhaps already movable and serially repeated, were first used in falls and on surfaces and then enlarged in step with their aerodynamic usefulness.

## 25.2. Flight Muscles and the Thorax

### 25.2.1. Direct and Indirect Flight Muscles

Each wing is a lever whose inner end is attached to the margin of the notum and which rests a little further out on the pleural wing process, the fulcrum ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)). Muscles can move it in two ways.

**Definition 25.2.1 (Indirect Flight Muscle)** **Indirect flight muscles** are the flight muscles of insects that are not attached to the wings but move them by deforming the thorax: the dorsal longitudinal muscles, running between the phragmata of successive segments, whose contraction bows the notum upwards and lowers the wings, and the dorsoventral muscles, running from the notum to the sternum or leg bases, whose contraction pulls the notum down and raises the wings. **Direct flight muscles** are those that insert on sclerites at the wing base (the basalare, subalare and axillary sclerites) and move the wings directly.

In Neoptera the indirect muscles supply almost all the power, and the direct muscles adjust the angle of attack, the amplitude and the folding of the wing (the third axillary muscle folds the wing back). In dragonflies, by contrast, the downstroke is powered directly by the large basalar and subalar muscles, which pull on the wing base outside the fulcrum, and the upstroke by dorsoventral muscles inserting on the wing base inside the fulcrum, so that each wing is driven independently. Fore- and hindwings of dragonflies can be operated with a phase difference, which allows dragonflies to hover and to turn sharply in pursuit of prey. Mayflies and the more primitive Neoptera are intermediate; cockroaches and locusts use indirect muscles for the downstroke and direct muscles that assist the upstroke and control.

### 25.2.2. The Elastic Thorax

A wing must be accelerated and decelerated twice in each cycle, and if the kinetic energy of the wing were lost at the end of each half-stroke, the inertial cost would be comparable to the aerodynamic cost. Most of it is recovered by elastic storage: at the end of a stroke the wing's kinetic energy deforms the thorax and the wing hinge, and the energy is returned in the next half-stroke. The thoracic box, its pleural ridges and especially resilin-rich ligaments of the wing hinge act as springs; the wing–thorax system is a resonant oscillator whose natural frequency is close to the wingbeat frequency. In flies, the thorax was long thought to snap between two stable positions through an unstable middle position ("click mechanism"); later work showed that a bistable click is not essential, but the principle of a spring-loaded thorax with a nonlinear stiffness remains.

**Example 25.2.1** Is elastic storage worth having? Take a *Drosophila* wing of mass $m_{\mathrm{w}} \approx 2.4\,\mathrm{\mu g}$ with a radius of gyration of about 1 mm about the wing base, so that its moment of inertia is $I \approx m_{\mathrm{w}} r_{\mathrm{g}}^2 \approx 2.4 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$. For a sinusoidal stroke of amplitude $\Phi = 2.6\,\mathrm{rad}$ at $f = 220\,\mathrm{Hz}$, the peak angular velocity is $\omega_{\max} = \pi \Phi f \approx 1800\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, and the peak kinetic energy of one wing is $\tfrac12 I \omega_{\max}^2 \approx 3.9 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{J}$. If this energy had to be supplied anew and then destroyed in each half-stroke, the two wings would cost $4 f \times 3.9 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 3.4\,\mathrm{\mu W}$. The induced power of hovering from Eq. (25.4) below is about $5\,\mathrm{\mu W}$ for the same fly.

Without elastic recovery, the inertial cost (larger still when the air accelerated with the wing is included) would therefore be of the same order as the aerodynamic cost; with the high elasticity of resilin and the thoracic cuticle, most of it is recovered, and measurements of flight metabolism in *Drosophila* indicate that only a small part of the inertial power is actually paid.

### 25.2.3. Synchronous and Asynchronous Muscle

In **asynchronous flight muscle** the contraction frequency is not set by the motor nerve: nerve impulses arrive only every few cycles to keep the muscle activated, and the oscillation is maintained by delayed stretch activation and the mechanical resonance of the thorax ([7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.6.5)). Synchronous muscle, found in dragonflies, mayflies, Orthoptera, Lepidoptera and other groups, contracts once per nerve impulse and is limited to frequencies below about 100 Hz, mostly 10–50 Hz, by the time needed to release and resequester calcium. Asynchronous muscle has evolved independently several times (at least seven times on most estimates), in Diptera, Hymenoptera, Coleoptera, Hemiptera, Thysanoptera and others, and it allows wingbeat frequencies of 100 to 1000 Hz; the record, just over 1000 Hz, belongs to tiny ceratopogonid midges (*Forcipomyia*, Sotavalta 1953).

Typical wingbeat frequencies show the division between the two muscle types (rounded values from many sources; frequencies vary with temperature, load and individual).

| Insect | Muscle | Wingbeat frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Cabbage white butterfly (*Pieris*) | Synchronous | about 10 Hz |
| Desert and migratory locusts | Synchronous | about 17–20 Hz |
| Hawkmoths (Sphingidae) | Synchronous | about 25–40 Hz |
| Dragonflies (Aeshnidae, Libellulidae) | Synchronous | about 25–40 Hz |
| Cockchafer (*Melolontha*) | Asynchronous | about 45–50 Hz |
| House fly (*Musca domestica*) | Asynchronous | about 190–200 Hz |
| *Drosophila melanogaster* | Asynchronous | about 200–250 Hz |
| Honey bee worker | Asynchronous | about 230 Hz |
| *Aedes aegypti* (female / male) | Asynchronous | about 450–500 Hz / 600–700 Hz |
| Biting midges (*Forcipomyia*) | Asynchronous | up to about 1000 Hz |

The flight tone is also a signal. Male mosquitoes locate females by the sound of their wingbeat, detected by Johnston's organ ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)), and in *Aedes aegypti* a male and a female that approach each other adjust their wingbeat frequencies until a harmonic of the male's tone matches a harmonic of the female's (near 1200 Hz), a "harmonic convergence" that precedes mating (Cator and coauthors 2009). Wingbeat frequency is therefore under sexual as well as aerodynamic selection in these insects.

Steering is done by small muscles that work in a different way from the power muscles. In *Drosophila* each wing has about a dozen steering muscles inserting on the basalare and the axillary sclerites; some fire once in every wingbeat and shift their timing by fractions of a millisecond to change the amplitude, while others are recruited only for large manoeuvres. Imaging of calcium activity in all the steering muscles of tethered flies (Lindsay, Sustar and Dickinson 2017) showed that they are organized in a few functional groups with distinct roles in amplitude, rotation and timing, a small "motor alphabet" from which all manoeuvres are composed.

Flight muscle works only above a minimum temperature. Large insects such as bumblebees, hawkmoths and locusts warm their thorax before take-off by contracting the antagonistic flight muscles simultaneously, which shivers the thorax without moving the wings; bumblebees maintain thoracic temperatures above 30 °C in cool air ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)). Heat produced by the flight muscles is retained by insulating hairs or scales and, in some species, carried to the abdomen by the hemolymph to prevent overheating.

**Example 25.2.2** How long does a bumblebee need to warm up? Take a worker of 175 mg with a thorax of about 60 mg (specific heat about $3.4\,\mathrm{J\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$), whose shivering flight muscles produce about 50 mW, of which about 35 mW are retained in the thorax at the start of warm-up. The thorax then warms at $\dfrac{0.035\,\mathrm{W}}{0.06\,\mathrm{g} \times 3.4\,\mathrm{J\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}} \approx 0.17\,\mathrm{K\,s^{-1}}$, about $10\,\mathrm{K}$ per minute, and would reach the $35\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ needed for flight from $10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ in about two and a half minutes. In reality heat loss grows as the difference between thoracic and air temperature increases, so warming slows as the bee approaches flight temperature, and measured warm-up rates in cool air are a few degrees per minute. Bernd Heinrich's studies of bumblebees showed that queens incubating their brood in early spring use the same mechanism, pressing the warm abdomen onto the brood clump, and that a countercurrent arrangement of blood vessels in the narrow petiole can be switched off to unload heat into the abdomen when the thorax overheats.

:::

The power that flight muscle actually delivers was measured by the work-loop technique of Robert Josephson (1985). An isolated muscle is lengthened and shortened sinusoidally at the wingbeat frequency and stimulated at a chosen phase of the cycle; the force plotted against length forms a loop, whose area is the net work per cycle. Multiplied by the frequency it gives the mechanical power, which for insect flight muscles in sustained operation is of the order of 100 W per kilogram of muscle. Since the metabolic rate of flying insects is about ten times higher, the efficiency of flight muscle in converting chemical energy to mechanical work is only about 10%, much of the rest heating the thorax.

### 25.2.4. Fuel, Oxygen and the Flight Motor

Flight muscle is built for sustained power. Mitochondria occupy up to about 40% of the fiber volume in the asynchronous muscles of flies and bees, and tracheoles indent the muscle cells so that no mitochondrion is more than a few micrometres from an air-filled tube ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)); oxygen reaches the muscle in the gas phase, and flight metabolism is almost entirely aerobic. The fuels differ among orders, as the classic wind-tunnel studies of Weis-Fogh on locusts and later work on many species showed.

| Fuel | Orders and examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate (trehalose, glycogen) | Diptera, Hymenoptera (honey bees use only sugar) | High power, short reserves; a honey bee without access to nectar exhausts its reserves within about a quarter of an hour of flight |
| Carbohydrate, then lipid | Locusts and other Orthoptera, many Lepidoptera | Carbohydrate during the first minutes, then fat mobilized from the fat body by adipokinetic hormone and carried as diacylglycerol on lipophorin |
| Proline | Tsetse flies, Colorado potato beetle, some other beetles | Partially oxidized to alanine, which the fat body converts back to proline |

Fat yields about 39 kJ per gram, more than twice as much as carbohydrate (about 17 kJ per gram), and is stored without water, which is why long-distance fliers use it. As an estimate, a locust of 2 g carrying 0.3 g of fat and flying at a metabolic rate of about 80 W per kilogram of body mass has enough fuel for some 20 hours of flight; at an airspeed of about 4 m s⁻¹ this is about 300 km, and with a tail wind much more ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)).

The rhythm of flight is generated in the nervous system. Donald Wilson (1961) removed the sensory input from the wings of locusts and found that the thoracic ganglia still produced the alternating pattern of elevator and depressor motor activity, at about half the normal frequency, when the animal was stimulated by wind on the head; this was one of the first demonstrations of a **central pattern generator**. A single stretch receptor neuron at the base of each wing, firing at the top of each upstroke, then restores the normal frequency by phase-dependent feedback. In flies with asynchronous muscle the nervous system sets only the level of activation and the steering commands; the frequency is set by the mechanics of the thorax (§25.2.3).

## 25.3. The Aerodynamics of Flapping Flight

### 25.3.1. Reynolds Numbers

The flow around a wing is characterized by the **Reynolds number**, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces,

$$
Re = \frac{U c}{\nu}, \tag{25.1}
$$

where $U$ is the speed of the wing, $c$ its chord and $\nu \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ the kinematic viscosity of air. Insect wings work between $Re \approx 10$ (thrips and tiny parasitoid wasps less than 1 mm long) and $Re \approx 10^4$ (large moths, dragonflies and locusts), compared with about $10^6$–$10^7$ for an aircraft wing. At the lowest Reynolds numbers air is "viscous" for the insect, and the bristled wings of thrips and featherwing beetles act like solid plates because the boundary layers around neighbouring bristles overlap ([7.6.22](/7.6.22)).

The table gives representative values, with the mean wing speed estimated as $U \approx 2\Phi f r_2 R$ for hovering (with $r_2 = 0.55$) and the mean chord $c$; the morphological values are typical published figures and the results are rounded.

| Insect | Wing length $R$ | Mean chord | Wingbeat frequency | Mean wing speed | $Re$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Encarsia formosa* (parasitoid wasp) | 0.6 mm | 0.2 mm | about 400 Hz | 0.7 m s⁻¹ | about 10 |
| *Drosophila melanogaster* | 2.5 mm | 0.8 mm | 220 Hz | 1.6 m s⁻¹ | about 80 |
| *Culex* mosquito | 2.8 mm | 0.7 mm | about 700 Hz (small amplitude) | 1.5 m s⁻¹ | about 70 |
| Honey bee | 9.5 mm | 3 mm | 230 Hz | 3.9 m s⁻¹ | about 800 |
| Bumblebee | 13 mm | 4 mm | 155 Hz | 4.4 m s⁻¹ | about 1200 |
| Hawkmoth *Manduca sexta* | 48 mm | 18 mm | 25 Hz | 2.6 m s⁻¹ | about 3000 |

The smallest fliers have been studied only recently. The featherwing beetle *Paratuposa placentis*, about 0.4 mm long, flies with bristled hindwings that sweep through a large, figure-of-eight-shaped stroke, clap together below and above the body, and are slowed at each reversal by the opened elytra, which act as brakes that stabilize the body; despite its size the beetle flies about as fast as related beetles three times longer (Farisenkov and coauthors 2022). Bristled wings weigh much less than membranous ones of the same area, which reduces the inertial power that dominates at these sizes.

### 25.3.2. The Quasi-Steady Test: the "Bumblebee Paradox"

A wing moving steadily through air at speed $U$ produces a lift force

$$
L = \tfrac12 \rho\, C_L\, S\, U^2, \tag{25.2}
$$

where $\rho$ is the density of air, $S$ the wing area and $C_L$ the lift coefficient, which depends on wing shape, angle of attack and Reynolds number. Steady insect wings in wind tunnels reach maximum lift coefficients of about 0.8–1.2 before they stall. The *quasi-steady assumption* treats a flapping wing at each instant as if it were moving steadily at its instantaneous velocity and integrates Eq. (25.2) along the span (blade-element theory) and over the stroke. For hovering with a sinusoidal stroke of amplitude $\Phi$ (radians) and frequency $f$, the angular velocity of the wing has mean square $(\pi \Phi f)^2/2$; integrating over the span with $r_2$ the radius of the second moment of wing area (as a fraction of the wing length $R$), the mean lift equals the weight $mg$ when

$$
\bar C_L = \frac{4 m g}{\rho\, S\, r_2^2 R^2 \pi^2 \Phi^2 f^2}, \tag{25.3}
$$

with $S$ the area of both wings. This is the mean lift coefficient required for hovering.

**Example 25.3.1** For *Drosophila melanogaster*, $m = 1.0\,\mathrm{mg}$, $R = 2.5\,\mathrm{mm}$, area of one wing about $1.9\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ (so $S = 3.8 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$), $\Phi = 2.6\,\mathrm{rad}$ (150°), $f = 220\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $r_2 = 0.55$. Eq. (25.3) gives

$$
\bar C_L = \frac{4 \times 10^{-6} \times 9.81}{1.2 \times 3.8\times 10^{-6} \times 0.30 \times 6.25 \times 10^{-6} \times 9.87 \times 6.76 \times 4.84\times 10^{4}} \approx 1.4.
$$

The mean speed at $r_2$ is $U \approx 2\Phi f r_2 R \approx 1.6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and the mean chord about $0.8\,\mathrm{mm}$, so $Re \approx 80$. A mean $C_L$ of 1.4 means that over much of the stroke the wing must produce more than its steady maximum; quasi-steady aerodynamics cannot support the fly.

:::

The claim that "according to aerodynamics the bumblebee cannot fly" goes back to a calculation reported by the French entomologist Antoine Magnan in 1934, which treated the bee's wings as fixed aircraft wings. The careful quasi-steady analyses of Weis-Fogh (1973) and Ellington (1984) showed that the required mean lift coefficients of many hovering insects exceed the steady maxima, sometimes by a factor of two; the paradox therefore points to aerodynamic mechanisms that steady theory leaves out.

### 25.3.3. Unsteady Mechanisms

Experiments with tethered insects, smoke visualization around a flapping hawkmoth (Ellington and colleagues 1996) and dynamically scaled robotic wings in mineral oil (the "robofly" of Dickinson, Lehmann and Sane 1999) revealed several unsteady mechanisms.

The robotic approach deserves description, because it turned a field of speculation into one of measurement. Dickinson's group built a pair of acrylic model wings, about 25 cm long and shaped like those of *Drosophila*, driven by computer-controlled motors through the measured wing kinematics, and immersed them in a tank of mineral oil. By the principle of dynamic similarity, a large wing moving slowly in a viscous liquid experiences the same flow as a small wing moving fast in air if the Reynolds number is the same; but each wingbeat now lasts several seconds, and the forces, measured with strain gauges at the wing base, are large enough to record accurately. Changing one kinematic parameter at a time, for instance the timing of wing rotation, showed directly how each mechanism contributes to lift. Flow fields were visualized with particles and lasers (particle image velocimetry).

**Definition 25.3.1 (Leading-Edge Vortex)** A **leading-edge vortex** is a vortex formed by flow separation at the leading edge of a wing moving at a high angle of attack, which remains attached over the upper surface throughout the stroke and enhances lift by lowering the pressure above the wing.

On an aircraft wing at a high angle of attack a leading-edge vortex grows and is shed within a few chord lengths, and lift collapses (stall). The leading-edge vortex of an insect wing does not detach during the half-stroke, because a revolving wing travels only a few chord lengths per stroke and because the rotation of the wing about its base stabilizes the vortex (the centripetal and Coriolis accelerations of a revolving wing, characterized by a low Rossby number, and an outward flow along the span which drains vorticity towards the tip). This "delayed stall" is the main source of lift in most insects and also in hovering hummingbirds and bats, and it gives the high lift coefficients observed in robotic wings.

The leading-edge vortex was first seen on a real insect by Charles Ellington and colleagues (1996), who released smoke from a rake in front of hawkmoths (*Manduca sexta*) flying tethered in a wind tunnel and filmed it with high-speed cameras; a mechanical model, the "flapper", then showed that the vortex spirals out along the span and is shed only near the tip. On the robotic *Drosophila* wing the lift coefficient rose within a fraction of a chord length of travel to about 1.8 and stayed there for the rest of the half-stroke, instead of collapsing as on a translating wing. Mosquitoes, whose stroke amplitude is only about 40°, cannot rely on a leading-edge vortex alone, because the wing does not travel far enough for it to develop fully: Bomphrey and coauthors (2017) found that a large part of their lift comes from rotational mechanisms, a vortex at the trailing edge formed by wake capture and a rotational drag produced by the fast pitching of the wing at each reversal.

Insect wings are not rigid plates. They bend and twist passively during the stroke, because inertial and aerodynamic forces deform the corrugated membrane and veins ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)), and the deformation changes the angle of attack along the span and the camber of the wing. In most insects the twist is produced by inertia at stroke reversal rather than by muscles, and models show that moderate flexibility can increase aerodynamic efficiency. The flexible zones, lines of resilin in the veins and the flexion lines between them, are therefore part of the flight apparatus, and their pattern is constant within groups.

Further mechanisms add force at the ends of each half-stroke, when the wing flips over:

- *Rotational circulation*: when the wing rotates about its long axis while still translating, it generates additional circulation, as a spinning ball does (Kramer effect); rotation slightly before stroke reversal increases lift.
- *Wake capture*: at the start of the new half-stroke the wing meets the wake it shed during the previous one, recovering some of the energy left in it.
- *Added mass*: the wing must accelerate the air around it, which produces transient forces at stroke reversal.

**Definition 25.3.2 (Clap-and-Fling)** **Clap-and-fling** is a lift-enhancing mechanism of flapping flight in which the two wings touch each other above the body at the end of the upstroke (clap) and then separate by rotating about their trailing edges (fling), so that air rushing into the widening gap establishes circulations of opposite sign around the two wings at the start of the downstroke.

Clap-and-fling was discovered by Weis-Fogh (1973) in the tiny parasitoid wasp *Encarsia formosa*, which uses it at every stroke. It solves a problem of starting a wing from rest: the circulation around an aerofoil normally builds up slowly (the Wagner effect), but in the fling each wing starts with a circulation already established, equal and opposite to that of its partner, so the total circulation remains zero as required by Kelvin's theorem. The clap also expels a jet of air backwards, adding thrust. The price is wear of the wings that touch at every stroke. Clap-and-fling is used regularly by thrips, small wasps and whiteflies, and occasionally by butterflies, locusts and *Drosophila* in tethered flight or during take-off; a partial version, the "clap and peel", is used by larger insects.

Butterflies show how the mechanism changes with size and wing flexibility. In free-flying butterflies filmed in a wind tunnel, Johansson and Henningsson (2021) found that at the end of the upstroke the flexible wings clap together in a cupped shape, trapping and expelling a pocket of air backwards as a jet; this produces a large forward thrust at take-off and in fast acceleration, and the flexibility of the wings makes the clap more efficient than it would be with rigid wings. The large, broad wings and slow wingbeat of butterflies, which make them look inefficient fliers, are thus suited to quick, erratic take-offs that help them escape birds.

### 25.3.4. Power

The power required for flight has three components. *Induced power* is the rate at which kinetic energy is given to the downward jet of air that supports the weight; *profile power* is the work against drag on the wings; *inertial power* is the cost of accelerating the wing mass, largely recovered by elastic storage (Section 25.2.2). Induced power can be estimated from momentum theory. A hovering animal is treated as an actuator disc of area $A$ (the area swept by the wings) that accelerates air downward; by conservation of momentum the thrust equals the weight $W = mg$ when the induced velocity at the disc is $w = \sqrt{W/(2\rho A)}$, and the induced power is

$$
P_{\mathrm{ind}} = W w = \sqrt{\frac{W^3}{2 \rho A}}, \tag{25.4}
$$

the Rankine–Froude estimate (a minimum; real wakes are less uniform, and corrections increase it by 10–30%). For two wings of length $R$ with a stroke amplitude $\Phi$ the swept area is $A = \Phi R^2$. Eq. (25.4) shows that the cost of hovering rises with the *disc loading* $W/A$: the specific induced power $P_{\mathrm{ind}}/m$ is proportional to $\sqrt{W/A}$.

**Example 25.3.2** A bumblebee worker with $m = 175\,\mathrm{mg}$ ($W = 1.7\,\mathrm{mN}$), $R = 13\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\Phi = 2.0\,\mathrm{rad}$ has $A = 3.4 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Eq. (25.4) gives $P_{\mathrm{ind}} = \sqrt{(1.7\times 10^{-3})^3/(2 \times 1.2 \times 3.4 \times 10^{-4})} \approx 2.5\,\mathrm{mW}$, about 14 W per kilogram of body mass. With profile power of a similar order and a muscle efficiency of about 10%, the metabolic rate of hovering is several hundred watts per kilogram of body mass, consistent with measured rates that are 50–100 times the resting rate. Bumblebees loaded with nectar or pollen up to nearly their own mass must raise their disc loading, and the power required increases as $W^{3/2}$.

### 25.3.5. Scaling of Wingbeat Frequency

How should wingbeat frequency change with size? If insects were geometrically similar (wing length $R \propto \ell$, wing area $\propto \ell^2$, mass $\propto \ell^3$) and flew with the same stroke amplitude and lift coefficient, then weight support, $mg \propto \rho\, S (f \Phi R)^2 \propto f^2 \ell^4$, would require $f \propto \ell^{-1/2}$, that is $f \propto m^{-1/6}$. Observed frequencies fall faster with size, roughly as $f \propto R^{-0.7}$ across insects of very different form, because large insects have relatively larger wings (lower wing loading), different stroke amplitudes, and different muscle physiology: in small insects asynchronous muscle allows frequencies far above the synchronous limit.

The scaling argument also explains why asynchronous muscle evolved repeatedly in small insects. Below a wing length of a few millimetres, weight support requires frequencies of a few hundred hertz, at which a synchronous muscle would have to release and resequester calcium within a millisecond; the sarcoplasmic reticulum needed would leave little room for myofibrils and mitochondria. Asynchronous muscle, activated tonically and oscillating by stretch activation, needs very little sarcoplasmic reticulum and can devote its volume to power production. Within a species, wingbeat frequency rises with thoracic temperature in insects that do not regulate it, and it changes as the wings wear: bumblebees whose wing area has been experimentally reduced beat their wings faster to compensate, but can carry less.

### 25.3.6. Stability and Control

A flapping insect is inherently unstable and must correct its attitude continuously. Information comes from the compound eyes (optic flow), the ocelli (the horizon), the antennae (air speed and flow direction, sensed by Johnston's organ) and, in flies, the **halteres**. The vibrating haltere acts as a gyroscope: when the body rotates, a Coriolis force

$$
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{C}} = -2 m_{\mathrm{h}}\, \boldsymbol{\Omega} \times \boldsymbol{v} \tag{25.5}
$$

acts on the knob of mass $m_{\mathrm{h}}$ moving with velocity $\boldsymbol{v}$ in the body frame rotating at angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$, bending the haltere stalk out of its plane of beating; campaniform sensilla at the haltere base detect this bending, and their signals reach motor neurons of the wing steering muscles within a single wingbeat. Because the Coriolis force oscillates at the beat frequency while other forces on the haltere are at twice the frequency or constant, the neural circuit can separate rotation from other signals. Halteres also act as clocks that time the activity of the steering muscles.

Steering is achieved by small asymmetries between the left and right wings in stroke amplitude, angle of attack and timing, produced by direct flight muscles attached to the axillary sclerites. *Drosophila* changes direction in rapid body saccades of about 90° completed in less than 50 ms, and recovers from perturbations within a few wingbeats.

Take-off is a separate problem. When a looming object approaches, a fruit fly does not simply jump: in the 200 ms before take-off it shifts its middle legs and adjusts its posture so that the jump carries it away from the direction of the threat, a sequence that is computed from visual input and shows that even escape is planned (Card and Dickinson 2008). The jump itself is produced by the middle legs, and wing beating starts only after the fly has left the ground. Large insects such as locusts take off with a jump and then open the wings; hawkmoths and bumblebees, which must warm up first, start from rest by beating the wings.

Classic experiments established the role of the halteres. Gottfried Fraenkel (1939) found that blowflies whose halteres had been removed could no longer fly stably, but that flight was partly restored when a thread was glued to the tip of the abdomen, which added passive stability like the tail of a kite; J. W. S. Pringle (1948) then analysed the haltere as a vibrating gyroscope and showed how the campaniform sensilla at its base could measure Coriolis forces. In *Drosophila* flight simulators, in which a tethered fly controls the rotation of a visual panorama by its own steering torque (developed by Karl Götz, Martin Heisenberg and Reinhard Wolf), the visual and mechanosensory control of flight and even visual learning can be studied in a single animal.

Dragonflies are the champions of aerial pursuit. Hunting dragonflies intercept flying prey rather than chasing it: they steer so as to keep the image of the prey at a constant position on the dorsal part of the eye, which brings them on a collision course, and they adjust their head and body to the prey's movements in less than a wingbeat. In a study under controlled conditions they caught more than 90% of the flies released to them (Combes and coauthors 2012), a success rate far higher than that of most vertebrate predators.

**Example 25.3.4** How fast can an insect fly? In 1927 the entomologist Charles Townsend reported that males of the deer bot fly *Cephenemyia* flew at about 1300 km h⁻¹ (366 m s⁻¹), a figure that was repeated in newspapers and encyclopaedias. Irving Langmuir (1938) showed by simple estimates that it is impossible. The power needed to overcome the drag of the body is $P = \tfrac12 \rho\, C_D A\, U^3$. For a body of frontal area $A \approx 5 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ (half a square centimetre) and a drag coefficient $C_D \approx 0.5$, at $U = 366\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $P \approx 0.5 \times 1.2 \times 0.5 \times 5 \times 10^{-5} \times 366^3 \approx 740\,\mathrm{W}$, more than two megawatts per kilogram of a fly of 0.3 g, ten thousand times what flight muscle can deliver. At 10 m s⁻¹ the same body needs only about 15 mW, a plausible value. Langmuir also whirled a fly-sized lump of solder on a thread: it was a blur at about 20 km h⁻¹ and could not be seen at all at about 100 km h⁻¹, so a fly at the claimed speed would have been invisible. Reliable measurements put the fastest insects, large dragonflies and horse flies, at about 10–15 m s⁻¹ in short dashes.

### 25.3.7. Forward Flight, Gliding and Migration

Most of what is described above concerns hovering, which is the most demanding case. In forward flight the wings meet air that also moves past the body, the stroke plane tilts forwards and the induced power falls as speed increases, because the wings act on a larger mass of air per unit time. Weis-Fogh and Jensen (1956) measured the forces of desert locusts flying tethered in a wind tunnel at about 3–4 m s⁻¹, with wingbeat frequencies of about 17–20 Hz, and found that their lift could be explained largely by quasi-steady aerodynamics: unlike small hoverers, a fast-flying locust does not depend on unsteady mechanisms, although the leading-edge vortex is present also in locusts.

Large insects with low wing loading glide. Many dragonflies alternate flapping and gliding; butterflies and some locusts glide during migration; and gliding is cheaper than flapping because no muscle work is done on the wings. The globe skimmer dragonfly *Pantala flavescens* migrates across the Indian Ocean between India and East Africa, a multigenerational circuit of about 14,000–18,000 km for which it uses monsoon winds at heights of up to about 1 km (Anderson 2009). Entomological radar has shown how common such wind-borne migration is: over southern England alone about 3.5 trillion insects migrate high above the ground each year (Hu and coauthors 2016), and many large moths, butterflies and hoverflies select favourable winds and adjust their headings to compensate for drift (Chapman and coauthors 2010). In East Asia the brown planthopper and other rice pests migrate north each spring on the south-westerly winds of the monsoon ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)). The ecology and physiology of **insect migration** are discussed in [7.17.29](/7.17.29).

## 25.4. Wing Folding and the Loss of Flight

### 25.4.1. Wing Folding

Neoptera fold their wings back over the abdomen using the third axillary sclerite and its muscle ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)); Palaeoptera, which lack this mechanism, hold their wings spread (dragonflies) or upright (mayflies, damselflies). Folded wings can be carried into crevices, under bark, into soil and among dense vegetation, and the forewings can be hardened as covers for the hindwings (tegmina, elytra, hemelytra). About 98% of living insect species are neopterans, and wing folding is considered a **key innovation** of the group ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

The folding mechanisms differ among neopteran orders, which suggests that the details were refined independently after the basic hinge evolved. Orthoptera, cockroaches, mantises and stoneflies fold the hindwing longitudinally like a fan along radiating lines of the vannus; beetles and earwigs fold it also transversely, so that a hindwing longer than the body is packed under the short elytra or tegmina; wasps fold the forewings longitudinally along their length (Vespidae); and many moths and caddisflies hold their wings roof-like over the body. Wings that are simply laid flat on the abdomen, as in flies, bees and bugs, need only the rotation at the hinge provided by the third axillary sclerite. Mayflies and dragonflies, which cannot fold, are limited to open habitats near water; among living palaeopterans only damselflies bring their wings together above the abdomen, by raising them rather than folding them back.

### 25.4.2. Secondary Flightlessness

Flight has been lost many times. Its costs are the energy and materials of wings and flight muscles, which can make up 10–20% of the body mass, and the risk of being carried away by wind. Flightlessness is common:

- on oceanic islands and in windy places, as Darwin noted for the many flightless beetles of Madeira, and on sub-Antarctic islands, where flightless flies and moths are common;
- at high altitudes, in cold climates and in caves;
- in parasites that live on their hosts (fleas, lice, bat flies, the females of Strepsiptera);
- in social insects, where workers are wingless and reproductives shed their wings after the mating flight (ants and termites), using the flight muscles as a source of nutrients for the first brood;
- in females of many species whose males fly (bagworm moths, winter moths, scale insects), a form of sexual dimorphism linked to fecundity.

Many species are *wing-polymorphic*, with long-winged (macropterous) and short-winged (brachypterous) or wingless (apterous) forms. In crickets of the genus *Gryllus* and in planthoppers the short-winged form reproduces earlier and produces more eggs, while the long-winged form disperses, a trade-off between flight and reproduction (the oogenesis–flight syndrome). Wing form may be determined genetically, by crowding and food quality, or by photoperiod; in aphids, crowding induces winged forms that disperse to new host plants ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)). After dispersal many insects break down their flight muscles (histolysis) and redirect the resources to reproduction.

The island case has been tested more carefully since Darwin. Thomas Wollaston found that about 200 of the 550 beetle species he collected on Madeira could not fly, and Darwin interpreted this as selection against individuals that were blown out to sea. Across the sub-Antarctic islands of the Southern Ocean, where flightless flies, moths and beetles are especially common (the Kerguelen fly *Anatalanta aptera*, for example, has lost its wings entirely), Leihy and Chown (2020) found that the proportion of flightless species is best explained by wind speed combined with low temperature: in cold, windy places flight is energetically expensive and rarely possible, so the resources saved by not building wings and flight muscles outweigh the advantages of dispersal. Being blown away seems to be less important than the cost of flying.

Can wings, once lost, be regained? Whiting, Bradler and Maxwell (2003) inferred from a phylogeny of stick insects that fully winged species arose several times from wingless ancestors, which would mean that the developmental program for wings was kept intact for millions of years in wingless lineages. The inference depends on the tree and on the model of character change, and it has been disputed; but the experiments described in §25.1.2, in which wings grow from wing serial homologues when Hox genes are knocked down, show that the program for wings can persist in segments that do not express it.

Reproductives of ants and termites illustrate how flight muscle can become a resource. A young ant queen flies once, on her mating flight, then breaks off her wings at preformed lines of weakness at their base and digs a nest. In many species she then raises her first brood without feeding, using amino acids from the histolysis of her large flight muscles and storage proteins of the fat body ([7.17.38](/7.17.38)).

## Questions

1. Why can a wing that is lowered by contraction of muscles not attached to it still be controlled precisely? Which muscles do the controlling?

   **Answer** The indirect muscles deform the thorax and so drive both wings symmetrically and at the resonant frequency, but the wing's angle of attack, amplitude and the left–right differences are set by small direct muscles on the basalare, subalare and axillary sclerites, which change the geometry of the hinge and so how the thoracic oscillation is transmitted to each wing. Power and control are thus separated: large asynchronous power muscles and small synchronous steering muscles that fire once per beat and can change within a single beat.

2. (Calculation) A honey bee with $m = 100\,\mathrm{mg}$, $R = 9.5\,\mathrm{mm}$, one wing area $25\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ (forewing plus hindwing), $\Phi = 1.6\,\mathrm{rad}$ and $f = 230\,\mathrm{Hz}$ hovers. Compute the required mean lift coefficient from Eq. (25.3) with $r_2 = 0.55$, and the induced power from Eq. (25.4).

   **Answer** $S = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}$; denominator $1.2 \times 5.0\times10^{-5} \times 0.3025 \times 9.03\times10^{-5} \times 9.87 \times 2.56 \times 5.29\times10^{4} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-3}$; numerator $4 \times 10^{-4} \times 9.81 = 3.9 \times 10^{-3}$; $\bar C_L \approx 1.8$. Swept area $A = 1.6 \times (9.5\times10^{-3})^2 = 1.44 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$; $P_{\mathrm{ind}} = \sqrt{(9.81\times10^{-4})^3/(2 \times 1.2 \times 1.44\times10^{-4})} \approx 1.6\,\mathrm{mW}$, about 16 W kg⁻¹. The small stroke amplitude of honey bees raises both the required lift coefficient and the induced power; bees compensate by a high frequency.

3. Using Eq. (25.4), explain why a bumblebee carrying a load of nectar equal to half its body mass needs about 80% more induced power.

   **Answer** At constant swept area $P_{\mathrm{ind}} \propto W^{3/2}$; increasing $W$ by a factor 1.5 multiplies the power by $1.5^{3/2} \approx 1.84$, an increase of about 84%. The bee can partly compensate by increasing the stroke amplitude (larger $A$) and frequency, which is what loaded bees are observed to do.

4. Why does clap-and-fling offer the greatest advantage to the smallest insects?

   **Answer** At low Reynolds numbers viscous effects are large and lift coefficients of steady wings are low, and a wing starting from rest builds circulation only slowly as it travels several chord lengths (Wagner effect); small insects with short strokes would spend much of each half-stroke building lift. The fling creates circulation immediately, so lift is available from the start of the downstroke. The cost, wear and extra drag when the wings are pulled apart in viscous air, is acceptable at these sizes; in large insects, which reach high lift through the leading-edge vortex, the benefit is smaller.

5. What observations would distinguish between the paranotal and the exite hypotheses of wing origin, and why have developmental genetic studies favoured a dual origin?

   **Answer** The paranotal hypothesis predicts that wings derive from tergal tissue only, so that wing genes should be expressed only in tergal cells and wing-like structures should form from terga without contribution from leg-derived tissue; the exite hypothesis predicts that wing cells derive from a leg exite, with the wing gene network shared with crustacean leg exites and with the leg primordium. Experiments showed both: the wing primordium separates from the leg primordium and shares gene expression with crustacean exites (supporting the exite view), but knockdown of wing genes in wingless segments affects tergal and pleural structures, and lineage analyses show tergal contributions to the blade. A wing formed by merging a tergal blade with an exite-derived hinge accounts for both kinds of evidence.

6. (Open question) Among female winter moths the wings are reduced to stubs, whereas males fly. Propose an explanation and a test.

   **Answer** Females that do not fly can put the resources saved from wings and flight muscles into eggs (fecundity increases with body mass), and in winter, with low temperatures and few predators active, dispersal by females may be less valuable, since males can find females by their pheromones. A test: compare the egg load and body mass of flightless females with those of related fully winged species of similar size, and measure dispersal and the genetic structure of populations; across a phylogeny of moths, test whether female flightlessness has evolved more often in species active in cold seasons than expected by chance (a comparative test, [7.10.26](/7.10.26)).

7. (Calculation) Using the values of Example 25.3.3, estimate by what factor the induced power per unit mass of a desert locust exceeds that of a cabbage white butterfly, and why the locust can nevertheless migrate for hours on its fat reserves.

   **Answer** The specific induced power scales as $\sqrt{W/S}$: $\sqrt{6/0.3} \approx 4.5$, so the locust pays roughly four to five times more per unit mass for weight support at the same stroke geometry. It can still fly for many hours because it flies forwards at 3–4 m s⁻¹, where the induced power is much lower than in hovering (the wings act on a large mass of air passing by), because its wings work at high Reynolds numbers where quasi-steady lift is efficient, and because it burns fat (about 39 kJ g⁻¹, twice as much as carbohydrate), mobilized from the fat body by adipokinetic hormone. With a fat load of about 15% of body mass the estimate of §25.2.4 gives about 20 hours of flight.

8. Why did Dickinson's group build a 25-cm model wing and move it slowly through mineral oil, rather than measuring forces on a real fruit fly? What must be kept equal between the model and the fly?

   **Answer** The forces on a real *Drosophila* wing are of the order of micronewtons and change within a fraction of the 4.5-ms wingbeat, which makes them almost impossible to measure directly and to attribute to particular phases of the stroke. By dynamic similarity, the flow pattern around geometrically similar wings is the same when the Reynolds number $Re = Uc/\nu$ (and, for flapping, the reduced frequency or advance ratio and the kinematics expressed as angles over the stroke cycle) are equal. A model wing a hundred times longer, beating about a thousand times less often in a mineral oil whose kinematic viscosity is about eight times that of air, has the same $Re$, but the cycle lasts several seconds and the forces are large enough to measure; the dimensionless force coefficients obtained can be scaled back to the insect.
