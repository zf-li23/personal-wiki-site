More than 99% of described animal species are bilaterians: animals with a head and a tail, a back and a belly, and three germ layers. The bilaterian body plan is so familiar that it is easy to forget that it had an origin, at some point before the Cambrian, in an animal that we can reconstruct only by comparing its descendants with each other and with the cnidarians, the sister group of the Bilateria ([7.17.9](/7.17.9)). How complex was that ancestor? Did it have a brain, a through-gut, a body cavity, segments? The answer depends critically on a group of small marine worms, the Xenacoelomorpha, that lack most of these features and whose position in the animal tree has been disputed for twenty years.

This chapter first describes the characters shared by bilaterians (§10.1) and the attempts to reconstruct their last common ancestor (§10.2). It then introduces the Nephrozoa (§10.3) and the division into protostomes and deuterostomes (§10.4). The second half treats the Xenacoelomorpha (§10.5), whose two possible positions on the tree are compared in §10.5.4. The animal tree as a whole is in [7.17.6](/7.17.6); the developmental concepts (cleavage, gastrulation, the fate of the blastopore) are in [7.17.5](/7.17.5) and [7.5.6](/7.5.6).

## 10.1. The Bilaterian Body Plan

The **Bilateria** are animals with **bilateral symmetry**: a single plane, the sagittal (median) plane, divides the body into mirror-image halves. Three body axes are defined, anterior–posterior, dorsal–ventral and left–right ([7.17.3](/7.17.3)). The other characters usually listed as bilaterian synapomorphies are:

| Character | Comment |
|---|---|
| Mesoderm | A third germ layer between ectoderm and endoderm, giving rise to muscles and connective tissue; bilaterians are **triploblasts** |
| An anterior–posterior axis aligned with the direction of movement | Movement head first is associated with **cephalization**, the concentration of sense organs and nerve cells at the anterior end |
| A dorsoventral axis patterned by BMP and its antagonist Chordin | The same molecular system patterns the axis in insects and vertebrates, but inverted ([7.5](/7.5)) |
| A Hox cluster with anterior, central and posterior genes | Cnidarians have anterior- and posterior-type Hox genes but no central class; the cluster of bilaterians had at least seven genes ([7.10](/7.10)) |
| Muscles of mesodermal origin, separate from the epithelia | Including longitudinal and circular body-wall musculature |
| Gonads of mesodermal origin and a germ line set aside early | Not universal, but widespread |

Bilateral symmetry is associated with directed locomotion. A radially symmetrical animal meets its environment equally from all sides, which suits sessile or drifting life. An animal that crawls or swims in one direction meets new food, prey and danger at its front end, so natural selection favours sense organs and a brain there, a mouth nearby, and an elongated body that offers little resistance. The ventral side faces the substrate and is specialized for locomotion, the dorsal side for protection.

Not every bilaterian is bilaterally symmetrical as an adult. Echinoderms are pentaradial ([7.17.42](/7.17.42)), many sessile animals (barnacles, tunicates) have lost most traces of bilateral organization, and gastropods are asymmetric through torsion. In all of them the larva or the embryo is bilateral, and the adult symmetry is secondary.

Bilateral organization did not appear from nothing at the base of the Bilateria. Anthozoan cnidarians such as the sea anemone *Nematostella vectensis* have, besides their oral–aboral axis, a second, "directive" axis perpendicular to it, marked by the slit-shaped mouth and pharynx and by the arrangement of the mesenteries, so that the polyp is in fact bilaterally symmetrical. Hox genes are expressed in staggered domains along this directive axis, and BMP signalling with its antagonist Chordin patterns it, as along the dorsoventral axis of bilaterians (Finnerty et al. 2004; Genikhovich et al. 2015). Whether the directive axis of anthozoans and the dorsoventral axis of bilaterians are homologous, and hence whether the common ancestor of cnidarians and bilaterians was already bilateral, is disputed: the medusozoans are radial, the molecular systems may have been recruited independently, and the oral–aboral axis of cnidarians has been compared both with the anterior–posterior axis of bilaterians and with their dorsoventral axis. What is clear is that the genetic machinery for two body axes predates the Bilateria.

The other defining innovation, mesoderm, is a germ layer in the embryological sense but also a set of new cell types: true muscle cells separate from epithelia, fibroblasts producing connective tissue, and in many lineages blood cells, nephrocytes and gonads. The jellyfish has striated muscle in the subumbrella, and *Nematostella* expresses many "mesodermal" genes (*twist*, *snail*, *Mef2*) in its endoderm, which suggests that the bilaterian mesoderm arose by segregation of part of an ancestral endomesoderm ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)). Mesoderm made larger bodies possible: a muscular body wall acting on a fluid-filled space or on parenchyma is a **hydrostatic skeleton** ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)) that allows burrowing and crawling, the modes of life recorded in the earliest bilaterian trace fossils (§10.2.2).

## 10.2. Reconstructing the First Bilaterian

### 10.2.1. Simple or Complex?

Because the cnidarians are the sister group of the Bilateria, any character found in both cnidarians and in the main bilaterian lineages was present in their common ancestor. Characters found in protostomes and deuterostomes but not in cnidarians were acquired in the bilaterian stem lineage. The difficulty is that many organ systems — a through-gut, a central nervous system, a body cavity, segments, a heart, excretory organs — are present in some protostomes and some deuterostomes but absent in others. They may have been present in the ancestor and lost many times, or absent and gained independently.

Two contrasting pictures have been proposed:

| | Simple ancestor ("acoeloid") | Complex ancestor ("urbilaterian") |
|---|---|---|
| Body | Small, ciliated, flat worm without body cavity, gliding on cilia | Larger, coelomate, possibly segmented worm with muscular locomotion |
| Gut | Sac-like, mouth only | Through-gut with mouth and anus |
| Nervous system | Basiepidermal nerve net with an anterior condensation | Brain and paired ventral (or dorsal) nerve cords |
| Excretory organs | None | Nephridia |
| Life cycle | Direct development | Possibly a ciliated larva (in Nielsen's trochaea theory) |
| Supporters | Von Graff, Hyman (the "planuloid–acoeloid" theory); acoels as early-branching bilaterians | Remane and others (archicoelomate theory); evo-devo arguments from shared gene expression (De Robertis and Sasai 1996, "Urbilateria") |

The two pictures descend from older theories of the origin of the bilaterians, each tied to a theory of the first animals ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)):

- The *planuloid–acoeloid theory* (von Graff 1882, developed by Hyman 1951) derives the bilaterians from a planula-like, solid, ciliated cnidarian larva that settled on the bottom, flattened and crept forward; its descendants were acoel-like worms, from which flatworms and then the animals with body cavities arose. This theory underlies the progression "acoelomate, pseudocoelomate, coelomate" of many textbooks.
- The *archicoelomate (enterocoel) theory* (Sedgwick 1884, Remane 1950, Jägersten 1955) derives the coelom from the gastric pouches of an anthozoan-like ancestor: the bilaterian ancestor was a coelomate animal with three pairs of coelomic sacs, as still seen in brachiopods, phoronids and the larvae of hemichordates and echinoderms, and acoelomates are secondarily simplified.
- The *trochaea theory* (Nielsen 1979, 2012) derives the protostomes from a pelagic, gastrula-like ancestor with a ring of downstream-collecting cilia around the blastopore, whose lateral closure produced a through-gut with mouth and anus; the trochophore larva would recapitulate this ancestor.

None of these theories can be tested directly, but their predictions about where acoelomate worms sit in the tree can. Molecular phylogenies placed the flatworms deep within the Spiralia, which removed the main living example of the planuloid–acoeloid series, but placed the acoels outside the Nephrozoa, which revived it in a new form.

The complex view gained strength in the 1990s from developmental genetics. The same genes pattern the eyes (*Pax6*), the heart (*tinman*/*Nkx2.5*), the anterior–posterior regions of the nervous system (*otx*, *gbx* and the Hox genes) and the dorsoventral axis (BMP and Chordin) in flies and vertebrates. Gene expression patterns in the developing nervous system of the annelid *Platynereis* are so similar to those of vertebrates, including mediolateral columns of neuron types along the nerve cord, that a centralized nervous system in the common ancestor seemed likely (Denes et al. 2007). Shared genes, however, do not prove shared organs: the same regulatory genes may have been recruited independently to similar functions (**deep homology**). Many such genes are expressed in comparable patterns in the nerve nets of cnidarians and acoels, which have no central nervous system.

The current consensus is intermediate. The first bilaterian was probably a small, ciliated worm, perhaps a few millimetres long, living on or in the sediment, with mesoderm, a Hox cluster, a concentration of nerve cells at the anterior end, photoreceptors with opsins and probably a gut. Whether that gut had an anus, and whether the nervous system was centralized, depends on whether the Xenacoelomorpha are the sister group of all other bilaterians (§10.5.4). Segmentation and the coelom are unlikely to have been present.

The question of the nervous system was tested directly by Martín-Durán et al. (2018), who examined the expression of the genes that pattern the vertebrate neural tube and the annelid nerve cord in mediolateral columns (*nk2.1*, *nk6*, *pax6*, *msx* and others) in a wide range of spiralians, ecdysozoans and deuterostomes, including animals with one, two or several nerve cords. If the nerve cords of *Platynereis* and vertebrates were homologous, the columnar arrangement should be found wherever there is a ventral or dorsal nerve cord. It was not: most species showed no conserved mediolateral code, and the few that did were scattered over the tree. The authors concluded that nerve cords evolved independently in several lineages from an ancestral nervous system that had an anterior concentration but no cords, and that the similar expression in annelids and vertebrates is convergent or reflects deep homology of cell types rather than of the organ.

### 10.2.2. The Fossil Record

The oldest evidence of bilaterians is trace fossils: horizontal burrows a few millimetres wide in Ediacaran sediments about 560 million years old, which indicate animals that moved directionally through sediment, using muscles against a hydrostatic skeleton. *Ikaria wariootia*, a body fossil a few millimetres long from the Ediacara Member of South Australia (about 555 million years old), may be the burrow-maker: a rice-grain-shaped animal with a distinct front and back (Evans et al. 2020). *Kimberella*, with a muscular foot and feeding traces made by a radula-like structure, is often interpreted as an early mollusc or a stem-group bilaterian. Larger and more diverse bilaterian burrows appear at the base of the Cambrian, about 539 million years ago, and the body fossils of most bilaterian phyla follow in the **Cambrian explosion** ([7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.4)). Molecular clocks place the origin of the Bilateria somewhat earlier, around 600–650 million years ago, with wide confidence intervals.

Other Ediacaran fossils bear on the question. *Kimberella quadrata* of the White Sea in Russia (about 555 million years) is an oval animal up to 15 cm long with a muscular foot and a dorsal, non-mineralized shell or mantle, associated with fan-shaped scratch marks (*Kimberichnus*) left as it grazed microbial mats with a toothed organ; it is a stem-group bilaterian or a stem-group mollusc. *Dickinsonia*, a flat, quilted organism of the **Ediacaran biota**, was shown to be an animal by the cholesterol derivatives preserved in its organic remains (Bobrovskiy et al. 2018); its trace fossils show that it moved over microbial mats, and it may be a stem bilaterian or placozoan-grade animal.

From the Shibantan Member of the Dengying Formation in the Yangtze Gorges area of Hubei (about 551–539 million years), *Yilingia spiciformis* is a segmented, bilaterally symmetrical animal up to 27 cm long whose body fossils lie at the end of its own trackways, the strongest Ediacaran evidence for segmented, mobile bilaterians (Chen et al. 2019). Earlier claims, such as the 580-million-year-old phosphatized microfossil *Vernanimalcula* from the Doushantuo Formation of Guizhou, described as a coelomate bilaterian, were shown to be artefacts of mineralization (Bengtson & Budd 2004).

The trace fossil record changes markedly across the Ediacaran–Cambrian boundary. Ediacaran traces are simple, horizontal and shallow, made just beneath the microbial mats that covered the sea floor. At the base of the Cambrian, defined by the first appearance of the branching burrow *Treptichnus pedum*, traces become more diverse, deeper and vertical, and bioturbation begins to mix the sediment. This "agronomic revolution" destroyed the mat-dominated ecosystems of the Ediacaran and is one of the ways in which bilaterians transformed the environment ([7.10.29](/7.10.29)).

### 10.2.3. Reconstructing Ancestral States

Whether the first bilaterian had a given organ is a problem of ancestral state reconstruction on a tree ([7.10.26](/7.10.26#sec-26.6.1)). Parsimony gives the simplest answer: under the Nephrozoa hypothesis an anus requires one gain (in the nephrozoan stem), under the Xenambulacraria hypothesis one gain at the base of Bilateria and one loss in Xenacoelomorpha, or two independent gains, in the protostomes and in the Ambulacraria: one step on the first tree and two on the second. The first bilaterian is reconstructed without an anus on the first tree and, if one prefers a single gain followed by a loss, with one on the second. But parsimony treats gains and losses as equally likely, and many zoologists think that complex organs are lost more easily than they are gained, as the many gutless parasites and the anus-less flatworms suggest.

A likelihood reconstruction makes the assumption explicit. Let a binary character have state 0 (mouth only) and 1 (through-gut), with gain rate $g$ ($0 \to 1$) and loss rate $l$ ($1 \to 0$). Along a branch of length $t$ the probabilities of change are

$$
P_{01}(t) = \frac{g}{g+l}\left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-(g+l)t}\right), \qquad P_{10}(t) = \frac{l}{g+l}\left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-(g+l)t}\right),
\tag{10.1}
$$
and the probability of the tip states for each state of an internal node is computed by the pruning algorithm. Two lessons follow. First, when gains are at least as likely as losses, both trees give a probability below about one third and differ little; the topology becomes decisive only if losses are assumed to be much easier than gains. Second, under the Xenambulacraria tree the answer depends strongly on the assumed ratio of loss to gain rates, which cannot be estimated from a single character on five taxa. In practice the character is studied together with developmental evidence: how the anus forms in different groups (§10.4) and whether the genes that pattern the hindgut are expressed in xenacoelomorphs.

## 10.3. Nephrozoa

**Definition 10.3.1 (Nephrozoa)** The **Nephrozoa** are the clade comprising Protostomia and Deuterostomia, that is, all bilaterians except (on most analyses) the Xenacoelomorpha; their ancestral features include excretory organs (nephridia: protonephridia or metanephridia) and a through-gut with mouth and anus.

The name refers to the nephridia. **Protonephridia** — tubules ending blindly in terminal cells with cilia or flagella that drive fluid along the tubule, as in the **flame cell** of flatworms — are found in flatworms, rotifers, nemerteans, larval annelids and molluscs, and some other protostomes, and also in the cephalochordate *Branchiostoma* (as solenocytes). Metanephridia, open to the body cavity, occur in coelomate protostomes and in some deuterostomes. The excretory organs of the two groups differ in many details, so whether nephridia were present in the common ancestor of Nephrozoa, or evolved more than once, is still debated; but the absence of any nephridia in the Xenacoelomorpha fits with the idea that excretory organs appeared after their divergence. The principles of filtration and reabsorption common to all these organs are described in [7.17.4](/7.17.4) and [7.14.22](/7.14.22#sec-22.8.1).

The two kinds of nephridia work on different principles, summarized in the following table:

| | Protonephridium | Metanephridium |
|---|---|---|
| Inner end | Closed by terminal cells (flame cells with many cilia, solenocytes with one flagellum) | Open to the coelom by a ciliated funnel (nephrostome) |
| Filtration | Across a filter of the terminal cell (a weir of microvilli or a slit diaphragm), driven by the negative pressure produced by the beating cilia | Across the wall of blood vessels into the coelom (podocytes), driven by blood pressure; the coelomic fluid then enters the funnel |
| Typical body plan | Small, acoelomate or pseudocoelomate animals, larvae | Larger coelomate animals with a circulatory system |
| Examples | Flatworms, rotifers, nemerteans, trochophore larvae, *Branchiostoma* | Annelids, molluscs (as the kidney opening into the pericardium), brachiopods |

The proposal that all nephridia share one origin was strengthened by a comparative study of gene expression (Gąsiorowski et al. 2021). In a broad sample of spiralians and ecdysozoans, the developing protonephridia and metanephridia express the same combination of transcription factors (including *Sall*, *Eya*, *Six1/2* and *Pou3*), and the filtration cells express homologues of proteins of the vertebrate kidney filter such as nephrin and kirre. The authors concluded that the ultrafiltration-based excretory organs of all Nephrozoa are homologous, with protonephridia and metanephridia as two derived forms of one ancestral organ. How xenacoelomorphs excrete without them was examined by Andrikou et al. (2019): in acoels, nemertodermatids and *Xenoturbella*, ammonium transporters, aquaporins and other proteins of the vertebrate kidney tubule are expressed in the digestive tissue, which removes ammonia by active transport. Excretion across the gut is therefore probably older than the nephridia, which arose as specialized organs for filtering body fluid once bodies became larger and more active.

The through-gut is equally characteristic. Almost all nephrozoans have an anus or have lost it secondarily (flatworms, many parasites). Xenacoelomorphs have only a mouth. If they are the sister group of the Nephrozoa, the anus evolved once in the nephrozoan ancestor; if they are deuterostomes, it was lost in their lineage.

## 10.4. Protostomes and Deuterostomes

The Nephrozoa divide into two great clades, the **Protostomia** (protostomes: arthropods, nematodes, molluscs, annelids, flatworms and their relatives) and the **Deuterostomia** (deuterostomes: echinoderms, hemichordates and chordates). The names mean "mouth first" and "mouth second" and refer to the fate of the blastopore: in the classical protostome the blastopore becomes the mouth, and in the deuterostome it becomes the anus, the mouth forming later at the opposite end.

The classical contrast between the two groups involved several characters:

| Character | Classical protostome | Classical deuterostome |
|---|---|---|
| Blastopore | Becomes the mouth (or mouth and anus by lateral closure) | Becomes the anus |
| Cleavage | Spiral, determinate (**spiral cleavage**) | Radial, indeterminate (**radial cleavage**) |
| Mesoderm and coelom | From a single mesoderm precursor (the 4d cell), coelom by schizocoely (**schizocoely**) | From outpockets of the archenteron, coelom by enterocoely (**enterocoely**) |
| Nervous system | Ventral nerve cords, brain dorsal to the mouth | Dorsal nerve cord (chordates) or nerve net |
| Larva | **Trochophore** (in spiralians) | **Dipleurula**-type larva (in ambulacrarians) |

Molecular phylogenetics has confirmed the two clades but undermined most of the characters on which they were founded. The blastopore fate varies within groups: in some protostomes (priapulids, some nematodes, some molluscs and annelids) the blastopore becomes the anus or closes entirely, and in many animals the mouth and the anus both form from the elongated, slit-like blastopore that closes in the middle (amphistomy). Spiral cleavage occurs only in one part of the protostomes, the **Spiralia**, and has been lost in some of them; the other part, the **Ecdysozoa**, has very varied cleavage patterns. Enterocoely occurs in some protostomes (brachiopods, chaetognaths). Several phyla once regarded as deuterostomes on embryological grounds (chaetognaths, brachiopods, phoronids, bryozoans) are protostomes. The protostome–deuterostome split is real, but it is defined by the tree, not by embryology.

Two studies of the 2000s and 2010s showed how labile the blastopore is. In the priapulid *Priapulus caudatus*, a member of the Ecdysozoa and so a protostome, the blastopore becomes the anus and the mouth forms secondarily at the anterior end, exactly as in a textbook deuterostome; the genes *brachyury* and *foxA*, expressed around the blastopore, mark the future anus (Martín-Durán et al. 2012). Deuterostomy thus also occurs in protostomes, and the ancestral condition of the Nephrozoa may well have been deuterostomic. In the acoel *Convolutriloba longifissura*, which has only one gut opening, the mouth forms at the site of the blastopore and expresses the genes of the bilaterian foregut and mouth, whereas genes that mark the hindgut and anus in other bilaterians are expressed in posterior tissues near the male gonopore. Hejnol and Martindale (2008) concluded that the mouth of acoels corresponds to the mouth of other bilaterians and that the anus evolved later, as a second opening at the posterior end, perhaps from a connection between the gut and the genital ducts.

The three great clades of the Nephrozoa recognized today are characterized as follows:

| Clade | Defining characters | Members | Chapters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spiralia (Lophotrochozoa in the wide sense) | Spiral quartet cleavage with 4d mesentoblast; trochophore larva in many; lophophore or trochophore-related larvae; ciliated epidermis | Annelids, molluscs, nemerteans, flatworms, rotifers and other gnathiferans, bryozoans, brachiopods, phoronids, entoprocts | [7.17.11](/7.17.11)–[7.17.16](/7.17.16) |
| Ecdysozoa | Cuticle moulted under control of ecdysteroids; no locomotory cilia; no primary larva | Nematodes, nematomorphs, priapulids, kinorhynchs, loriciferans, tardigrades, onychophorans, arthropods | [7.17.17](/7.17.17)–[7.17.40](/7.17.40) |
| Deuterostomia | Pharyngeal gill slits (ancestrally); enterocoelous mesoderm in many; radial cleavage | Echinoderms, hemichordates, chordates (and possibly Xenacoelomorpha) | [7.17.41](/7.17.41)–[7.17.43](/7.17.43), [7.17.45](/7.17.45)–[7.17.68](/7.17.68) |

Within the Protostomia, the Spiralia (or Lophotrochozoa in a wide sense: annelids, molluscs, flatworms, rotifers, nemerteans, lophophorates and others, [7.17.11](/7.17.11)–[7.17.16](/7.17.16)) are defined by spiral cleavage and its derivatives. The quartet pattern of spiral cleavage, with its characteristic 4d mesentoblast, is so conserved across phyla that individual blastomeres can be identified as homologous across annelids, molluscs, nemerteans and polyclad flatworms ([7.5.6](/7.5.6#sec-6.3)). The Ecdysozoa (nematodes, arthropods and their relatives, [7.17.17](/7.17.17)–[7.17.21](/7.17.21)) moult their cuticle.

## 10.5. Phylum Xenacoelomorpha

**Definition 10.5.1 (Xenacoelomorpha)** The **Xenacoelomorpha** are a phylum of small, mostly marine, bilaterally symmetrical worms without anus, protonephridia, circulatory system or body cavity, with a ciliated epidermis whose cilia have a characteristic stepped tip, a basiepidermal nervous system, and a sac-like gut or a digestive syncytium; they comprise the Xenoturbellida, the Nemertodermatida and the Acoela.

There are about 430 described species. The Acoela (about 400 species, the bulk of the phylum) and the Nemertodermatida (about 20 species) together form the Acoelomorpha, which is the sister group of the Xenoturbellida (six species, all in *Xenoturbella*). No fossils are known.

### 10.5.1. Body Plan

Xenacoelomorphs are flattened or cylindrical worms, mostly 1–5 mm long, which glide on cilia over sediment, algae or other animals. Their body organization is simple:

- The epidermis is a multiciliated epithelium. Each cilium has a characteristic "shelf" near its tip where the outer doublet microtubules 4–7 end, and the ciliary rootlets form a network. Worn-out epidermal cells sink into the body and are resorbed as "pulsatile bodies". These two features are unique to the phylum.
- There is no basement membrane under the epidermis in acoels, and the space between epidermis and gut is filled with muscle cells and parenchymal cells derived from mesoderm, as in flatworms: xenacoelomorphs are **acoelomates**.
- In Xenoturbellida and Nemertodermatida the gut is a sac lined by an epithelium; in Acoela it is replaced by a *digestive syncytium*, a multinucleate mass of cytoplasm without a lumen in which prey is enclosed and digested. The name *Acoela* ("without cavity") refers to this. There is only one opening, the mouth, on the ventral side.
- There are no protonephridia and no circulatory or respiratory system; gas exchange and excretion are by diffusion.
- The nervous system is a basiepidermal plexus, condensed in many acoels into an anterior commissural brain and several pairs of longitudinal nerve cords. A statocyst with one statolith (or two in nemertodermatids) lies in the brain region and is the main sense organ; some species have ocelli.
- A *frontal organ*, a cluster of gland cells opening at the anterior tip, secretes mucus.
- Acoels and nemertodermatids are hermaphrodites with internal fertilization; there are no permanent gonads, and gametes form from scattered cells in the parenchyma. Many acoels have copulatory organs and inject sperm through the body wall (hypodermic insemination). Development is direct.

The cleavage of acoels is a spiral pattern in which the blastomeres divide in pairs (duet cleavage) rather than in quartets; it resembles spiral cleavage but is probably not homologous to it. The mesoderm arises from cells derived from the vegetal macromeres.

### 10.5.2. *Xenoturbella*

*Xenoturbella bocki* was described in 1949 from a fjord in Sweden: a pale, mouth-only worm up to 4 cm long, with a ring furrow and a side furrow but no organs other than a nerve net, a statocyst and a sac-like gut. In 2016 four new species were described from the deep eastern Pacific, one of them, *X. monstrosa*, 20 cm long (Rouse et al. 2016). *Xenoturbella* feeds on the eggs and larvae of bivalves. For a few years it was even thought to be a degenerate mollusc, because DNA extracted from it contained molluscan sequences; these turned out to come from its food. Later analyses placed it first among the deuterostomes (Bourlat et al. 2006) and then with the acoelomorphs.

The body wall of *Xenoturbella* consists of a ciliated epidermis with the characteristic stepped cilia and pulsatile bodies, a basiepidermal nerve net without a brain, a thin layer of circular and longitudinal muscle and a loose mesenchyme around a gut lined by a simple epithelium of phagocytic cells; there are no gonads, and eggs and sperm develop in the gut wall and are probably released through the mouth. A sixth species, *X. japonica*, was found in 2017 off the Pacific coast of Japan. Development, studied in *X. bocki* (Nakano et al. 2013), is direct: the embryo becomes a free-swimming, uniformly ciliated juvenile without a mouth, which resembles the hatchlings of acoels, one of the developmental arguments for uniting the two groups.

### 10.5.3. Acoela and Nemertodermatida

**Definition 10.5.2 (Nemertodermatida)** The **Nemertodermatida** are acoelomorph xenacoelomorphs, a few millimetres long, with a gut lined by an epithelium (and in some species an intermittent mouth), a statocyst with two statoliths, a basiepidermal nervous system and uniflagellate sperm.

Nemertodermatids (about 20 species) live interstitially in marine sediments; *Meara stichopi* lives in the foregut of sea cucumbers. The Acoela (about 400 species) are the most diverse and best known xenacoelomorphs, and the three groups can be compared as follows:

| Group | Species | Gut | Statocyst | Nervous system | Habitats |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Xenoturbellida | 6, all *Xenoturbella* | Sac with epithelium | Present, of unusual structure | Basiepidermal net, no brain | Fjords (*X. bocki*, 20–100 m) and deep sea, to 3,700 m |
| Nemertodermatida | ~20 | Epithelial (partly syncytial) | Two statoliths | Basiepidermal, with an anterior concentration and longitudinal cords | Interstitial; in sea cucumbers |
| Acoela | ~400 | Digestive syncytium, no lumen | One statolith | Internalized brain (commissural), several pairs of nerve cords | Sediments, algae, corals; a few in brackish water and sea cucumbers |

Acoels are predators of diatoms, ciliates and small crustaceans, which they engulf whole into the digestive syncytium. Several are noteworthy, and one of them, *Symsagittifera roscoffensis*, has been studied for more than a century as an example of animal–algal symbiosis.

**Example 10.5.1 (*Symsagittifera roscoffensis*)** The Roscoff worm is a green acoel 2–4 mm long that lives on the sandy beaches of the Atlantic coast of Europe, from Wales and the Channel Islands to Brittany and Portugal, in the upper intertidal zone.

- *Body.* A flattened worm with a ciliated epidermis, a frontal organ and a statocyst at the anterior end; beneath the epidermis lie layers of circular, longitudinal and diagonal muscles and a parenchyma packed with green algal cells. The digestive syncytium occupies the centre of the body, and the ventral mouth is small.
- *Symbiosis.* Each adult carries tens of thousands of cells of the prasinophyte alga *Tetraselmis convolutae*, which lose their cell wall and flagella inside the host and lie between the muscle and parenchymal cells. The algae are not passed through the eggs: each generation of hatchlings, which are colourless, must take up free-living *Tetraselmis* from the water within a few days, and juveniles that fail to do so die. Once colonized, the worms stop feeding and live on the products of photosynthesis. Classic experiments by Keeble and Gamble (1903–1905) established that the green cells are algae and that the animal depends on them; later, Douglas (1983) showed that the algae take up the uric acid produced by the host, so that nitrogenous waste is recycled within the partnership.
- *Behaviour.* At low tide, millions of worms rise to the surface of the sand and form dark green patches in seepage streams, where they photosynthesize; before the tide returns they sink into the sand. The rhythm is tidal and endogenous: it persists for days in the laboratory without tides. The worms are phototactic and orient to gravity with the statocyst.
- *Reproduction and regeneration.* The worms are hermaphrodites with internal fertilization; eggs are laid in mucous capsules in the sand. Like other acoels they regenerate: animals cut behind the statocyst regenerate a head with a new brain and recover phototaxis (Sprecher et al. 2015).

:::

- *Hofstenia miamia*, the three-banded panther worm of the Caribbean, regenerates a whole animal from small fragments, including the head with its brain. Its regeneration depends on pluripotent stem cells (neoblasts) that express *piwi*, like that of planarians, and on Wnt signalling at the posterior end and BMP signalling along the dorsoventral axis (Srivastava et al. 2014). Gehrke et al. (2019) sequenced its genome and followed the changes in chromatin accessibility after amputation; the first gene activated, within hours, was the transcription factor *egr* (early growth response), which opens the chromatin of hundreds of downstream regeneration genes, and knockdown of *egr* blocks regeneration. Because acoels and planarians are not closely related, the shared features (neoblasts, Wnt polarity, *egr* as an early wound-induced gene) are candidates for an ancient bilaterian regeneration program.
- *Convolutriloba* reproduces asexually by fission and budding, and *Waminoa* lives on the surface of reef corals with its own dinoflagellate symbionts; *Paratomella*, about 0.5 mm long, is one of the smallest bilaterians.

### 10.5.4. Where Do Xenacoelomorphs Belong?

Acoels and nemertodermatids were for a century classified as orders of the class Turbellaria of the flatworms. Their simple organization and the absence of an anus were interpreted as primitive features shared with flatworms. Molecular data overturned this: Acoelomorpha are not flatworms, and their rRNA genes placed them outside the Nephrozoa, as the sister group of all other bilaterians (Ruiz-Trillo et al. 1999). By 2009–2011 phylogenomic analyses had grouped *Xenoturbella* with them as the Xenacoelomorpha (Hejnol et al. 2009; Philippe et al. 2011). Two hypotheses are now debated (see the figure below):

1. The Nephrozoa hypothesis: Xenacoelomorpha is the sister group of the Nephrozoa. Many phylogenomic analyses support it, especially those with the largest gene samples (Cannon et al. 2016). On this tree, the xenacoelomorph body plan approximates that of the first bilaterians, and the anus, nephridia and centralized nervous system evolved in the nephrozoan stem lineage.
2. The Xenambulacraria hypothesis: Xenacoelomorpha is the sister group of Ambulacraria (echinoderms and hemichordates) within the Deuterostomia (Philippe et al. 2011, 2019). On this tree, xenacoelomorphs descend from deuterostome ancestors with an anus, nephridia (or their equivalents) and probably gill slits, and their simplicity is secondary.

The problem is a classic case of possible **long-branch attraction** ([7.10.21](/7.10.21)). Acoels have rapidly evolving genomes and long branches in molecular trees; long branches tend to be drawn toward the root of a tree, which is itself at the end of a long branch leading to the outgroups. Analyses that remove the fastest-evolving genes, use site-heterogeneous models or include only the slowly evolving *Xenoturbella* and nemertodermatids tend to favour Xenambulacraria; analyses with more genes and standard models favour the Nephrozoa hypothesis. Some morphological and genomic characters support each: xenacoelomorphs lack several microRNA families and some Hox genes shared by all Nephrozoa (acoels have only three Hox genes), which fits an early divergence but could also reflect loss, while a few genes otherwise found only in deuterostomes support Xenambulacraria. A simulation study found that systematic errors bias analyses more strongly toward the Nephrozoa hypothesis than toward Xenambulacraria, which weakens the main support for the former (Kapli & Telford 2020); the question remains open.

The evidence can be summarized as follows:

| Evidence | Favours Nephrozoa hypothesis | Favours Xenambulacraria |
|---|---|---|
| Phylogenomics with standard (site-homogeneous) models, many genes | Yes (Hejnol et al. 2009; Cannon et al. 2016) | |
| Phylogenomics with site-heterogeneous models, slowly evolving genes, without the fast acoels | | Yes (Philippe et al. 2011, 2019) |
| Simulations of systematic error | | Errors favour Nephrozoa more strongly (Kapli & Telford 2020) |
| Gene content: Hox genes, microRNAs | Fewer Hox genes and nephrozoan microRNAs absent (fits early divergence) | Absences may be losses, as in other simplified animals |
| Mitochondrial genome of *Xenoturbella* | | Gene order and codes resembling deuterostomes (weak) |
| Morphology | No anus, nephridia or gill slits (fits primitive simplicity) | No known shared derived character with Ambulacraria; simplicity could be secondary |

The stakes are high. If Xenacoelomorpha is the sister group of Nephrozoa, it is the best living model for the first bilaterians, and the reconstruction in §10.2.1 tilts toward the simple ancestor. If it is a deuterostome, the first bilaterians could have been more complex, and the Xenacoelomorpha become an example of extreme simplification, like the parasites of [7.17.12](/7.17.12).

(A) Nephrozoa hypothesis: Xenacoelomorpha is the sister group of all other bilaterians, so the anus (1), nephridia (2) and a centralized nervous system (3) arose once in the stem lineage of Nephrozoa, and Xenacoelomorpha never had them. (B) Xenambulacraria hypothesis: Xenacoelomorpha is the sister group of Ambulacraria within the deuterostomes; the same characters arose at the base of Nephrozoa (= Bilateria) and were lost (crossed bars) in the xenacoelomorph lineage. ## 10.6. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Acoels (order Acoela) and nemertodermatids as turbellarian flatworms (class Turbellaria, Platyhelminthes) | A separate phylum, Xenacoelomorpha, not related to flatworms; flatworms are spiralian protostomes ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)) |
| Flatworms as the most primitive bilaterians, the first triploblasts and first bilateral animals | The position of "first bilaterians" is taken, if by anyone, by Xenacoelomorpha; flatworms are derived spiralians whose simple anatomy is partly secondary |
| Protostomes and deuterostomes defined by the fate of the blastopore, cleavage and coelom formation | Clades defined by molecular phylogeny; embryological characters vary within both |
| Lophophorates, chaetognaths as deuterostomes | Protostomes ([7.17.12](/7.17.12), [7.17.16](/7.17.16)) |
| A progression of body cavities, acoelomate → pseudocoelomate → coelomate, as stages of evolution | Three grades of organization; each arose or was lost several times, and the coelom was probably not present in the first bilaterian ([7.17.3](/7.17.3)) |
| Bilateral symmetry as an adaptation that arose with the change from floating to crawling on the bottom | Retained as a functional explanation; the molecular machinery for a second body axis already existed in the cnidarian–bilaterian ancestor (§10.1) |

The Chinese textbooks introduce bilateral symmetry and the mesoderm with the flatworms, which are presented as the first triploblastic, bilateral animals; the mesoderm is credited with the origin of muscles, the parenchyma that stores water and food, and an excretory system, and so with making a more active, ultimately terrestrial life possible. These functional arguments remain valid: they describe what mesoderm and a head end do for an animal. What has changed is the genealogy. The animals that best show the bilaterian grade of organization in its simplest form are now the xenacoelomorphs, not the flatworms, and the turbellarian "order Acoela" of the older literature corresponds to the Acoela of this chapter, removed from the flatworms and placed in a phylum of its own. The account of the flatworms themselves follows in [7.17.11](/7.17.11).

## Summary

- Bilaterians are characterized by bilateral symmetry with three axes, mesoderm (triploblasty), cephalization, a Hox cluster with anterior, central and posterior genes, and BMP–Chordin patterning of the dorsoventral axis; radial adults (echinoderms) are secondarily so.
- The first bilaterian was probably a small, ciliated, acoelomate worm with mesoderm, a Hox cluster, anterior neural concentration and photoreceptors; whether it had an anus, nephridia or a centralized nervous system depends on the position of Xenacoelomorpha. Shared developmental genes indicate deep homology, not necessarily homologous organs.
- Anthozoans already have a second (directive) body axis patterned by Hox genes and BMP–Chordin, and many mesodermal genes are expressed in the cnidarian endoderm: the toolkit for bilateral organization and mesoderm predates the Bilateria.
- Historical theories: planuloid–acoeloid (simple ancestor), archicoelomate (coelomate ancestor) and trochaea; mediolateral patterning genes are not conserved among nerve cords (Martín-Durán et al. 2018), favouring independent origins of nerve cords.
- The earliest bilaterian traces are Ediacaran burrows of about 560 million years ago; *Ikaria* may be a burrow-maker, *Kimberella* a stem bilaterian or mollusc, and *Yilingia* from the Yangtze Gorges a segmented mobile bilaterian; the base of the Cambrian is marked by complex burrows (*Treptichnus pedum*).
- Ancestral state reconstruction (Eq. (10.1)): under the Nephrozoa topology the first bilaterian probably lacked an anus whatever the ratio of loss to gain rates; under Xenambulacraria the answer depends on that ratio.
- Nephrozoa (Definition 10.3.1) = Protostomia + Deuterostomia, ancestrally with nephridia and a through-gut. Protonephridia and metanephridia share developmental genes and are probably homologous; xenacoelomorphs excrete through the gut.
- The blastopore criterion, spiral versus radial cleavage and schizocoely versus enterocoely describe classical protostomes and deuterostomes but vary within both clades; Spiralia (spiral cleavage) and Ecdysozoa (moulting) are the two protostome clades.
- Xenacoelomorpha (Definition 10.5.1): Xenoturbellida, Nemertodermatida and Acoela; ciliated epidermis with pulsatile bodies, no anus, protonephridia or circulatory system, statocyst, basiepidermal nervous system, digestive syncytium in acoels. *Symsagittifera roscoffensis* (Example 10.5.1) acquires its algae anew each generation and recycles its uric acid through them; *Hofstenia* regenerates through neoblasts, Wnt and BMP signalling and the early gene *egr*. Nemertodermatida (Definition 10.5.2) have a gut epithelium and two statoliths.
- Xenacoelomorpha is either the sister group of Nephrozoa (simplicity primitive) or of Ambulacraria (simplicity secondary); long-branch attraction makes the question hard.

## Questions

1. A gene expressed in the anterior nervous system of both a fly and a mouse is also expressed in the anterior nerve net of an acoel. What does this tell us about the last common ancestor of bilaterians, and what does it not tell us?

   **Answer** It suggests that the gene was already used in anterior neural development in the common ancestor of these animals, that is, that the ancestor had an anterior–posterior axis and some anterior specialization of the nervous system. It does not show that the ancestor had a brain in the sense of a centralized organ: the same gene can pattern a nerve net, and its role in brains may have evolved independently in different lineages by recruitment of an ancestral regulatory program (deep homology). Organ homology requires correspondence of structure and position, not only shared genes.
2. Explain why the position of Xenacoelomorpha matters for reconstructing the first bilaterian, using the anus as an example.

   **Answer** If Xenacoelomorpha is the sister group of Nephrozoa, the mouth-only gut of xenacoelomorphs and of cnidarians (the outgroup) implies that the first bilaterian had no anus, and the anus evolved once in the nephrozoan stem lineage. If Xenacoelomorpha is the sister group of Ambulacraria, the first bilaterian is the common ancestor of protostomes and deuterostomes, both of which have an anus, so the first bilaterian probably had one, and it was lost in xenacoelomorphs. The same reasoning applies to nephridia and a centralized nervous system.
3. Why might acoels be drawn toward the base of the bilaterian tree in a phylogenetic analysis even if they are in fact deuterostomes?

   **Answer** Acoel genes evolve fast, so acoels sit on a long branch. In models that do not account for heterogeneity in rates and in amino acid composition across sites and lineages, convergent substitutions accumulated on long branches are mistaken for shared ancestry, so long branches are attracted to each other. The longest branch in the tree is usually the one leading to the distant outgroups (cnidarians, sponges, choanoflagellates), so a fast-evolving ingroup taxon tends to be pulled toward the root. Removing fast-evolving sites or taxa and using better-fitting models reduces the effect.
4. Are the terms "protostome" and "deuterostome" still useful if many protostomes do not form the mouth from the blastopore?

   **Answer** Yes, as names of clades: molecular phylogenies consistently recover Protostomia and Deuterostomia as monophyletic sister groups. What has lost its value is the embryological definition. The blastopore fate, cleavage type and mode of coelom formation are labile and vary within each clade, so they cannot be used to assign an animal to one group or the other; membership is decided by the phylogeny.
5. In the model of Eq. (10.1), why does the probability that the first bilaterian had an anus stay below about one half on the Nephrozoa tree, even when losses are assumed to be a hundred times more likely than gains?

   **Answer** On the Nephrozoa tree the first bilaterian is joined to two lineages without an anus (the cnidarian outgroup, through the common ancestor fixed in state 0, and the xenacoelomorphs) and one lineage with an anus (the Nephrozoa). If the bilaterian ancestor had an anus, it must have been lost in the xenacoelomorphs and gained on the short stem from the cnidarian–bilaterian ancestor; if it lacked one, a single gain on the short nephrozoan stem suffices. When losses are cheap, the first scenario costs little on the xenacoelomorph branch, but it still requires a gain on the short bilaterian stem, which is as improbable as the gain on the nephrozoan stem; the two scenarios become comparably likely, and the probability approaches a value set by the relative lengths of the two stems and by the chance of a loss on the xenacoelomorph branch, rather than 1. On the Xenambulacraria tree, by contrast, the first bilaterian is joined to the protostomes and to the deuterostomes, both with an anus, and a single cheap loss in xenacoelomorphs explains the data.
6. *Hofstenia miamia* and planarians both regenerate whole bodies from neoblasts using Wnt signalling for polarity. What can be concluded about the regeneration of the first bilaterian, and what further evidence would strengthen the conclusion?

   **Answer** Acoels and planarians belong to different branches of the bilaterian tree (Xenacoelomorpha and Spiralia), so features they share are either inherited from their common ancestor, the first bilaterian or the first nephrozoan depending on the position of Xenacoelomorpha, or convergent. The shared use of pluripotent stem cells, Wnt-based anterior–posterior polarity and the early wound gene *egr* suggests that a regeneration program with these elements is ancestral. The conclusion would be strengthened by finding the same components, with the same regulatory connections (for example *egr* binding sites at the same downstream genes), in other regenerating animals such as annelids, nemerteans, echinoderms and cnidarians, and by showing that animals that cannot regenerate have lost parts of the program rather than never having had it.
