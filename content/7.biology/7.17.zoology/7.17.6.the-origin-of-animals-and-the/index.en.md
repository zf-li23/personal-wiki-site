The previous chapters described animals by their grades of organization: symmetry, germ layers, body cavities, segmentation and modes of development. This chapter places them on the tree of descent, which is the framework of the rest of the course: every later chapter on a group of animals begins by locating it on the tree drawn here. We begin with the closest relatives of animals and the hypotheses about how a multicellular animal arose from a single cell, then deal with the traditional "Protozoa", which Chinese textbooks of zoology still treat as the first animal phylum, and finally examine the evidence for the current animal tree and its uncertain nodes. The phylogenetic methods were treated in evolutionary biology ([7.10.2](/7.10.2), [7.10.21](/7.10.21)), the origin of eukaryotes and of multicellularity in [7.10.28](/7.10.28), the fossil record of early animals in [7.10.29](/7.10.29), and the protists themselves in [7.11.20](/7.11.20); here we take from them only what the animal tree requires.

## 6.1. The Closest Relatives of Animals

### 6.1.1. Holozoa

Animals are opisthokonts ([7.17.1](/7.17.1#sec-1.1.1)). Within the opisthokonts they belong, with three lineages of unicellular organisms, to the clade *Holozoa*. These relatives are small and inconspicuous, but because they bracket the origin of animals they are the key to which features of animals are new and which were inherited.

**Definition 6.1.1 (Choanoflagellata)** The **choanoflagellates** (Choanoflagellata) are free-living, heterotrophic unicellular or colonial opisthokonts whose cell bears a single flagellum surrounded by a collar of actin-supported microvilli; the beating flagellum draws water through the collar, which traps bacteria. They are the sister group of the Metazoa; together the two form the clade Choanozoa.

About 250 species live in the sea and in fresh water, some naked, some in an organic cup (theca) or a basket of siliceous strips (lorica). Several species form colonies by incomplete division, so that the cells remain connected by cytoplasmic bridges: *Salpingoeca rosetta* forms rosette-shaped colonies when it detects a sulfonolipid made by the bacteria it eats (Alegado and colleagues, 2012), and can also form chains or pass through a sexual phase. The resemblance between the choanoflagellate cell and the choanocyte of sponges was noted in 1841 and long taken as evidence that sponges descend from colonial choanoflagellates ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)); whether the two cell types are homologous is still debated, because the choanocytes of sponges share fewer genes with choanoflagellates than their morphology suggests.

The other unicellular holozoans are the *Filasterea*, amoeboid cells with long filopodia such as *Capsaspora owczarzaki*, a symbiont of freshwater snails that can aggregate into a multicellular stage, and the *Ichthyosporea*, mostly parasites of fishes and invertebrates that grow as multinucleate coenocytes and then cellularize, in a process that resembles the cellularization of the insect blastoderm (*Sphaeroforma*). Holozoans thus show all three ways of becoming multicellular in a single lineage: clonal division (choanoflagellates), aggregation (filastereans) and cellularization of a syncytium (ichthyosporeans).

### 6.1.2. The Genome of the First Animal

The genomes of these relatives, sequenced since 2008, have changed the picture of animal origins. Many gene families once thought to be animal inventions already existed in unicellular ancestors: cadherins and tyrosine kinases in choanoflagellates; integrins and the components of the integrin adhesion complex in *Capsaspora*; transcription factors such as p53, Myc, Brachyury and T-box genes, and the RNA-binding proteins that regulate them. What animals added was less a new set of building blocks than new ways to use them: several signaling pathways in complete form (Wnt, TGF-β, Hedgehog, Notch), large families of homeobox and other developmental transcription factors, the **Hox genes** of ParaHoxozoa (Section 6.5.2), distal enhancers and three-dimensional organization of the chromatin, and new classes of small regulatory RNAs (**microRNAs**). Rather than a single key innovation, the origin of animals was the integration of a pre-existing toolkit into programs that produce many stable cell types from one zygote ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5)).

## 6.2. How Did Multicellular Animals Arise?

### 6.2.1. Classical Hypotheses

Before molecular phylogenetics, the origin of animals was argued from embryology and from the simplest living animals. Four main hypotheses were proposed.

**Definition 6.2.1 (Gastraea Hypothesis)** The **gastraea hypothesis** of Ernst Haeckel (1872–1874) holds that the ancestor of all metazoans was a gastraea, a hollow spherical colony of flagellated cells (like a blastula) in which one side invaginated to form a two-layered, cup-shaped animal with a gut and a blastopore, resembling the gastrula of living embryos.

| Hypothesis | Author | Ancestor | Assumptions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gastraea | Haeckel 1874 | Invaginated colony, a two-layered sac with a gut | Gastrulation by invagination is ancestral; recapitulated in development |
| Planuloid ancestor (phagocytella) | Metschnikoff 1886 | Solid ciliated colony in which cells that had taken up food migrated inward (ingression) | The planula of cnidarians resembles the ancestor; intracellular digestion preceded a gut |
| Placula | Bütschli 1884, revived by Grell | Flat two-layered disc that fed with its lower surface | The placozoan *Trichoplax* resembles the ancestor |
| Syncytial (cellularization) | Hadži 1953, Hanson 1977 | Multinucleate ciliate that became cellular | The first animals were bilateral, acoel-like flatworms; radial animals derived |
| Symbiotic | Various | Several kinds of protist living together | Different cell types arose from different organisms |

The colonial hypotheses (gastraea, planuloid, placula) differ in the shape of the ancestor but agree that it arose from a colony of flagellated cells, the view that Chinese textbooks call the colonial theory. The hypothesis of an aggregate of a flagellated cell type which then differentiated is the best supported, for several reasons. Animals are the sister group of choanoflagellates, which form colonies by cell division; animal sperm and many embryonic and epithelial cells carry a single flagellum, as choanoflagellates do; and the development of every animal begins with the division of one cell, not with aggregation or symbiosis.

The syncytial hypothesis has been rejected. Ciliates are alveolates, not relatives of animals ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.4)); acoels, which the hypothesis considered the most primitive animals, are bilaterians (Section 6.5.3); and no animal embryo cellularizes a multinucleate ciliate-like cell. The symbiotic hypothesis is incompatible with the genetics of animals, all of whose cells carry one genome. Between the gastraea and the planuloid hypothesis the evidence is less decisive: invagination and ingression both occur in cnidarians and sponges, and the root of the animal tree (Section 6.5.1) affects which condition is ancestral. Many authors now prefer a version in which the earliest animal had a life cycle with flagellated feeding cells and a temporal sequence of cell types, from which spatial differentiation arose by keeping the cell types of different stages together in one body.

### 6.2.2. Fossils and Molecular Clocks

The fossil record of the first animals is sparse and ambiguous ([7.10.29](/7.10.29)). Molecular clocks calibrated with many fossils place the origin of crown-group animals between about 800 and 650 million years ago, in the Tonian or Cryogenian, long before unambiguous animal fossils. Possible traces of sponges include steroid biomarkers (24-isopropylcholestanes) from about 660–635 million years ago, but these may also be made by algae. Among the **Ediacaran biota** (575–539 Mya), *Dickinsonia* preserves cholesterol derivatives and is probably an animal, perhaps of placozoan or stem-bilaterian grade; *Kimberella*, with scratch marks of a radula-like organ, is often interpreted as a stem-group mollusc; and trace fossils of bilaterian burrowers appear from about 560 Mya. Skeletons appear at the end of the Ediacaran (*Cloudina*, about 550 Mya).

The **Cambrian explosion** brought the first fossils that can be assigned with confidence to most living phyla, in sites of exceptional preservation: Chengjiang in Yunnan (about 518 Mya), Sirius Passet in Greenland, the Burgess Shale (about 508 Mya). Many Cambrian animals are stem-group representatives, with some but not all of the features of the living crown group, such as the radiodonts and lobopodians on the stem of the arthropods ([7.17.18](/7.17.18)). Such stem groups show the order in which the characters of a body plan were assembled.

## 6.3. "Protozoa": A Historical Group

### 6.3.1. The Protozoa of Zoology Textbooks

**Definition 6.3.1 (Protozoa)** **Protozoa** is the traditional name for the unicellular, mostly motile and heterotrophic eukaryotes formerly classified as the lowest phylum (or subkingdom) of animals. It is not a clade but a polyphyletic grade of **protists**, and it is no longer used as a formal taxon; the word survives informally for heterotrophic protists.

The classification still used in the textbooks of Liu Lingyun and Xu Chongren divides the phylum Protozoa into four classes by their organelles of locomotion: flagellates (Mastigophora), amoebae (Sarcodina), sporozoans (Sporozoa, which have no locomotory organelle in most stages) and ciliates (Ciliata). These classes do not correspond to lineages: each is distributed over several eukaryotic supergroups, and some former protozoa are not protists at all (see the figure below).

The textbooks themselves note that molecular data have overturned the scheme and keep it for convenience. Two of their specific statements need correction. The myxosporeans, listed among the sporozoans, are cnidarians that have been reduced to a few cells: their polar capsules are homologous to cnidarian **cnidae**, and their genomes place them, with the parasite *Polypodium*, as the sister group of the medusozoans ([7.17.9](/7.17.9)). And the "mesozoans", which the same textbooks place between protozoa and metazoa and relate to ciliates on the basis of the base composition of their DNA, are two unrelated lineages of reduced spiralian bilaterians: the dicyemids, parasites in the kidneys of cephalopods, and the orthonectids, which are annelids ([7.17.12](/7.17.12)). Base composition is not evidence of relationship.

### 6.3.2. Animal Functions at the Level of a Cell

The protists that zoology textbooks use as representatives remain instructive, because they perform within one cell the functions that animals perform with organ systems. Their cell biology is treated in [7.11.20](/7.11.20); the comparison with animals is summarized here.

| Function | *Euglena* | *Amoeba* | *Paramecium* | *Plasmodium* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Support and shape | Pellicle of protein strips under the membrane, allowing euglenoid movement | None; shape changes constantly | Pellicle with alveoli | Pellicle with inner membrane complex |
| Locomotion | Flagellum | Pseudopodia | Cilia in rows, beating in metachronal waves | Gliding of sporozoites and merozoites |
| Feeding | Photosynthesis, or osmotrophy in the dark (mixotrophy) | Phagocytosis, food vacuoles | Cilia drive food into the oral groove and cytostome; food vacuoles; cytoproct | Ingests host hemoglobin in a food vacuole |
| Osmoregulation | Contractile vacuole | Contractile vacuole | Two contractile vacuoles with radial canals | Not needed in host fluids |
| Sensing | Eyespot shading a photoreceptor at the base of the flagellum | Chemical and mechanical stimuli | Membrane potential changes reverse ciliary beating (avoidance reaction) | Host recognition by the apical complex |
| Defense | — | Encystment | Trichocysts | Antigenic variation |
| Reproduction | Longitudinal binary fission | Binary fission | Transverse fission; conjugation with exchange of micronuclei | Schizogony, gametogony and sporogony in two hosts |

The ciliate's division of labor between a diploid micronucleus (germ line) and a polyploid macronucleus (somatic expression) is a unicellular parallel to the separation of germ line and soma in animals. Its avoidance reaction, in which a calcium action potential reverses the beating of the cilia, uses the same kind of voltage-gated channels as animal neurons ([7.16.53](/7.16.53)).

## 6.4. Evidence for Animal Relationships

### 6.4.1. Kinds of Evidence

Until the late twentieth century animal phylogeny was inferred from morphology and embryology: body cavities, cleavage patterns, larvae, the structure of cuticles, nephridia and nervous systems. Such characters are informative within phyla but often ambiguous between them, because grades of organization evolve repeatedly ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.6)). Molecular data have transformed the field in stages:

- Ribosomal RNA. Sequences of 18S rRNA (Field and colleagues, 1988, and later studies) separated the protostomes into the Lophotrochozoa (Halanych and colleagues, 1995) and the Ecdysozoa (Aguinaldo and colleagues, 1997), against the Articulata hypothesis.
- Phylogenomics. Since about 2008 data sets of hundreds to thousands of genes from transcriptomes and genomes (Dunn and colleagues, 2008; Laumer and colleagues, 2019) have resolved most relationships among phyla.
- Rare genomic changes. Features that change rarely and are unlikely to arise twice, such as the gain of particular microRNA families (Sempere and colleagues, 2006), the structure of the Hox cluster, the order of genes in mitochondrial genomes and, most recently, the conserved linkage of genes on chromosomes (**synteny**), test relationships independently of sequence models.

### 6.4.2. Sources of Error

The deepest nodes of the animal tree are difficult because the branches leading to them were short (the phyla diverged within tens of millions of years more than 550 million years ago) and the branches since then are long. Two systematic errors are especially important ([7.10.21](/7.10.21)):

- **Long-branch attraction**: rapidly evolving lineages tend to be grouped together, or drawn toward the root, by models that underestimate multiple substitutions. Nematodes and flatworms, whose genes evolve fast, were long placed at the base of the bilaterians for this reason, and fast-evolving ctenophores may be attracted to the long branch of the outgroup.
- Compositional heterogeneity: lineages with different amino-acid compositions can be grouped by composition rather than descent.

Remedies include site-heterogeneous substitution models such as CAT-GTR, recoding amino acids into groups of similar ones (Dayhoff-6 recoding), removing fast genes or distant outgroups, and denser taxon sampling. Laumer and colleagues (2019), the first study with genomic data from every animal phylum, showed that several nodes, such as the position of placozoans and the root of the tree, change with these choices, while most others are robust.

### 6.5.1. The Root: Ctenophores or Sponges First?

The first split within the animals separates either the ctenophores or the sponges from all others. The question matters because ctenophores have neurons, muscles and a through gut, whereas sponges have none of these: if ctenophores branched first, either these features arose twice, once in ctenophores and once in the lineage of cnidarians and bilaterians, or they were present in the first animals and lost in sponges and placozoans (see the figure below).

![Two hypotheses for the root of the animal tree. (A) Ctenophores first: either neurons and muscles evolved twice (bars, in ctenophores and in ParaHoxozoa), or they evolved once and were lost in sponges and placozoans (crosses). (B) Sponges first: a single origin in the common ancestor of ctenophores and ParaHoxozoa (Eumetazoa) and one loss in placozoans suffice](./plots/two-roots.py)

Phylogenomic analyses have supported both answers, depending on models, genes and outgroups. Analyses with standard models and many genes usually favor ctenophores first; site-heterogeneous models with recoding sometimes favor sponges first (Pisani and colleagues, 2015; Simion and colleagues, 2017). Schultz and colleagues (2023) brought evidence of a different kind. Comparing the chromosomes of ctenophores, sponges, cnidarians, placozoans, bilaterians and unicellular outgroups, they found ancestral groups of linked genes that are preserved in ctenophores and in the outgroups, but that in sponges, cnidarians, placozoans and bilaterians have been fused and mixed in the same way. Such fusion-with-mixing is practically irreversible, so these four lineages form a clade, *Myriazoa*, and ctenophores are the sister group of all other animals. Most specialists now accept this result.

Ctenophores first does not settle how nervous systems evolved. Ctenophore neurons differ from those of other animals in their transmitters and in the syncytial nerve net recently described in *Mnemiopsis* (Burkhardt and colleagues, 2023), which favors an independent origin, but losses in sponges and placozoans cannot be excluded ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.1)). Sponges, in either case, are not a model of the ancestral animal in every respect.

### 6.5.2. ParaHoxozoa and Planulozoa

The remaining non-bilaterians are the placozoans and the cnidarians. Placozoans, cnidarians and bilaterians share Hox or ParaHox genes and form the clade *ParaHoxozoa*. Cnidarians and bilaterians share a further set of characters, among them a body axis patterned by Wnt signaling with the blastopore at one end, ciliated larvae with an apical organ and a fully differentiated nervous system, and are grouped as *Planulozoa*; some analyses with recoded data place placozoans instead as the sister group of cnidarians. The cnidarians include the **Myxozoa**, reduced parasites once classified among the protozoa ([7.17.9](/7.17.9)).

### 6.5.3. Bilateria

**Definition 6.5.1 (Bilateria)** The **Bilateria** (bilaterians) are the clade of animals descended from the common ancestor of xenacoelomorphs, protostomes and deuterostomes. Their synapomorphies include bilateral symmetry with anteroposterior and dorsoventral axes, a mesoderm, a centralized nervous system and an expanded set of Hox genes; most also have a through gut and excretory organs.

The position of the **Xenacoelomorpha** (acoels, nemertodermatids and *Xenoturbella*), small worms without an anus or nephridia, is the main open question within the Bilateria. Most analyses place them as the sister group of all other bilaterians, which are then called **Nephrozoa** after their nephridia; this implies that the first bilaterian may have been a small, simple worm. Others place them within the deuterostomes as the sister group of Ambulacraria (the Xenambulacraria hypothesis), which implies extensive secondary simplification ([7.17.10](/7.17.10)).

### 6.5.4. Deuterostomia

The **Deuterostomia** contain only three phyla: echinoderms and hemichordates, which form **Ambulacraria**, and chordates ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#def-5.2.1)). Within Chordata, lancelets (**Cephalochordata**) are the sister group of tunicates (**Tunicata**) plus vertebrates (together **Olfactores**), a result of phylogenomics that reversed the traditional view of lancelets as the closest relatives of vertebrates ([7.17.43](/7.17.43)). Their shared ancestral character, the pharyngeal slits, was lost in echinoderms. Groups once placed here, the lophophorates and chaetognaths, are protostomes.

### 6.5.5. Protostomia: Ecdysozoa and Spiralia

The **Protostomia** are the larger branch of the Bilateria, with more than 90% of animal species. They consist of two clades.

**Definition 6.5.2 (Ecdysozoa)** The **Ecdysozoa** are the clade of protostomes that grow by molting a cuticle under the control of ecdysteroid hormones and lack motile cilia on the body surface. They comprise the scalidophorans (priapulids, kinorhynchs, loriciferans), the nematoids (nematodes, nematomorphs) and the panarthropods (tardigrades, onychophorans, arthropods).

**Definition 6.5.3 (Spiralia, Lophotrochozoa)** The **Spiralia** are the clade of protostomes that includes all animals with spiral cleavage and their descendants: the Gnathifera (rotifers with acanthocephalans, gnathostomulids, micrognathozoans and probably chaetognaths), flatworms, gastrotrichs and the Lophotrochozoa. The **Lophotrochozoa** in the narrow sense are the spiralians with a lophophore or a trochophore larva, or descended from such: molluscs, annelids, nemerteans, brachiopods, phoronids, bryozoans, entoprocts and cycliophorans.

Within the Ecdysozoa the panarthropods are generally recovered, with onychophorans as the sister group of arthropods, but the position of tardigrades and the relationships among scalidophorans and nematoids are less certain; Laumer and colleagues placed loriciferans with nematodes. Within the arthropods, the **Mandibulata** (myriapods and **Pancrustacea**) and the origin of hexapods within a paraphyletic Crustacea are robust ([7.17.18](/7.17.18), [7.17.22](/7.17.22)). Within the Spiralia, the **Gnathifera** branch first; whether flatworms and gastrotrichs form a clade (Rouphozoa) is uncertain; and the relationships among the lophotrochozoan phyla, apart from the clade **Lophophorata** (bryozoans, phoronids, brachiopods) and the pairing of entoprocts with cycliophorans, are still unsettled. Spiralia is sometimes called Lophotrochozoa in a broad sense; in this course Lophotrochozoa is used in the narrow sense of Definition 6.5.3.

## 6.6. Reading the Course along the Tree

The order of the chapters follows the tree from its root. Part II treats the non-bilaterian lineages: sponges, ctenophores with placozoans, and cnidarians. Part III begins with the origin of the Bilateria and the xenacoelomorphs and continues through the spiralians: flatworms and gastrotrichs, the gnathiferans and minor phyla, molluscs, annelids, and the nemerteans and lophophorates. Part IV treats the ecdysozoans, from the cuticle-bearing worms to the arthropods, and Part V the hexapods in detail. Part VI returns to the deuterostomes, from hemichordates and echinoderms to the invertebrate chordates, and Parts VII and VIII treat the vertebrates. Each chapter places its group on the tree above and notes which characters arose on the branch leading to it; the course summary ([7.17.70](/7.17.70)) returns to the tree to trace how the body plans of the phyla were assembled.

## Summary

- Animals belong to Holozoa with three unicellular lineages; their sister group is the **choanoflagellates** (Definition 6.1.1). Much of the animal genetic toolkit predates animals; what is new is its integration into developmental programs.
- Of the classical hypotheses for the origin of animals, the colonial ones derived from a flagellated ancestor are supported, the **gastraea hypothesis** (Definition 6.2.1) and the planuloid hypothesis differing in whether invagination or ingression was ancestral; the syncytial and symbiotic hypotheses are rejected.
- **Protozoa** (Definition 6.3.1) is a polyphyletic grade of protists; its four classes are distributed over the eukaryotic supergroups, microsporidia are fungi and myxosporeans cnidarians.
- Animal phylogeny rests on phylogenomics and rare genomic changes, corrected for long-branch attraction and compositional bias.
- The current tree: ctenophores are probably the sister group of all other animals (synteny); ParaHoxozoa and Planulozoa; **Bilateria** (Definition 6.5.1) with Xenacoelomorpha as sister to Nephrozoa or within deuterostomes; Deuterostomia (Ambulacraria, Chordata); Protostomia with **Ecdysozoa** (Definition 6.5.2) and **Spiralia**, including **Lophotrochozoa** (Definition 6.5.3).
- Many traditional groups (Coelenterata, Aschelminthes, Articulata, Mesozoa) are not clades; their members are scattered over the tree.

## Questions

1. Why does the discovery that choanoflagellates, not some other protists, are the sister group of animals favor the colonial over the syncytial hypothesis?

   **Answer** The sister group shows which features the ancestor of animals inherited. Choanoflagellates are uninucleate flagellated cells that form colonies by incomplete cell division; animals develop from one cell by division and have flagellated sperm and epithelial cells. This fits the derivation of animals from a colony of flagellated cells. The syncytial hypothesis requires a multinucleate ciliate ancestor, but ciliates are alveolates, far from animals on the eukaryote tree, and no close relative of animals is a ciliate.

2. Explain why a fusion of two ancestral chromosomal linkage groups followed by mixing of their genes is stronger evidence of relationship than a single shared amino-acid substitution.

   **Answer** An amino-acid substitution can arise independently in two lineages (homoplasy) or be reversed, and its signal can be swamped by long-branch attraction. A fusion with mixing of genes from two linkage groups is a complex event that is very unlikely to occur twice in the same way, and once the genes are intermixed, the original separate groups cannot be restored by later rearrangements. Lineages sharing such a derived state are therefore almost certainly a clade, as Schultz and colleagues argued for sponges, cnidarians, placozoans and bilaterians.

3. A textbook lists the myxosporeans as a subclass of Sporozoa. What evidence places them among the cnidarians?

   **Answer** Their polar capsules, used to attach the spore to the host, are built like the nematocysts of cnidarians and contain homologous proteins (such as minicollagens); phylogenomic analyses place them within the Cnidaria, together with *Polypodium* as the sister group of the medusozoans; and they are multicellular, with spores formed by several cells, including the capsulogenic cells. Their resemblance to sporozoans results from convergent reduction to intracellular parasitism.

4. If xenacoelomorphs were the sister group of Ambulacraria rather than of Nephrozoa, what would follow for the ancestors of the deuterostomes and of the bilaterians?

   **Answer** Xenacoelomorphs would then be deuterostomes that lost many features: a through gut with an anus, nephridia, pharyngeal slits and perhaps a coelom. The common ancestor of bilaterians would no longer be reconstructed as a simple acoel-like worm; it would more likely have had these organs, and the simplicity of xenacoelomorphs would be secondary. The hypothesis thus shifts the reconstruction from a simple to a complex bilaterian ancestor.
