The previous chapters described animals by their grades of organization: symmetry, germ layers, body cavities, segmentation and modes of development. This chapter places them on the tree of descent, which is the framework of the rest of the course: every later chapter on a group of animals begins by locating it on the tree drawn here. We begin with the closest relatives of animals and the hypotheses about how a multicellular animal arose from a single cell, then deal with the traditional "Protozoa", which Chinese textbooks of zoology still treat as the first animal phylum, and finally examine the evidence for the current animal tree and its uncertain nodes. The phylogenetic methods were treated in evolutionary biology ([7.10.2](/7.10.2), [7.10.21](/7.10.21)), the origin of eukaryotes and of multicellularity in [7.10.28](/7.10.28), the fossil record of early animals in [7.10.29](/7.10.29), and the protists themselves in [7.11.20](/7.11.20); here we take from them only what the animal tree requires.

## 6.1. The Closest Relatives of Animals

### 6.1.1. Holozoa

Animals are opisthokonts ([7.17.1](/7.17.1#sec-1.1.1)). Within the opisthokonts they belong, with three lineages of unicellular organisms, to the clade *Holozoa*. These relatives are small and inconspicuous, but because they bracket the origin of animals they are the key to which features of animals are new and which were inherited.

**Definition 6.1.1 (Choanoflagellata)** The **choanoflagellates** (Choanoflagellata) are free-living, heterotrophic unicellular or colonial opisthokonts whose cell bears a single flagellum surrounded by a collar of actin-supported microvilli; the beating flagellum draws water through the collar, which traps bacteria. They are the sister group of the Metazoa; together the two form the clade Choanozoa.

About 250 species live in the sea and in fresh water, some naked, some in an organic cup (theca) or a basket of siliceous strips (lorica). Several species form colonies by incomplete division, so that the cells remain connected by cytoplasmic bridges: *Salpingoeca rosetta* forms rosette-shaped colonies when it detects a sulfonolipid made by the bacteria it eats (Alegado and colleagues, 2012), and can also form chains or pass through a sexual phase. The resemblance between the choanoflagellate cell and the choanocyte of sponges was noted in 1841 and long taken as evidence that sponges descend from colonial choanoflagellates ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)); whether the two cell types are homologous is still debated, because the choanocytes of sponges share fewer genes with choanoflagellates than their morphology suggests.

The other unicellular holozoans are the *Filasterea*, amoeboid cells with long filopodia such as *Capsaspora owczarzaki*, a symbiont of freshwater snails that can aggregate into a multicellular stage, and the *Ichthyosporea*, mostly parasites of fishes and invertebrates that grow as multinucleate coenocytes and then cellularize, in a process that resembles the cellularization of the insect blastoderm (*Sphaeroforma*). Holozoans thus show all three ways of becoming multicellular in a single lineage: clonal division (choanoflagellates), aggregation (filastereans) and cellularization of a syncytium (ichthyosporeans).

| Lineage | Cell form | Multicellular stages | Notable genomic features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Choanoflagellata | Flagellum surrounded by a microvillar collar | Clonal colonies (rosettes, chains) by incomplete division | Cadherins, receptor tyrosine kinases, many signaling components | *Monosiga brevicollis*, *Salpingoeca rosetta* |
| Filasterea | Amoeba with long filopodia | Aggregation into a multicellular stage, cysts | Integrin adhesion complex, Brachyury and other transcription factors | *Capsaspora owczarzaki*, *Ministeria* |
| Ichthyosporea | Walled cells; amoeboid or flagellated dispersal stages | Coenocytes that cellularize | Cell-cycle and cellularization genes resembling the insect blastoderm | *Sphaeroforma arctica*, *Creolimax* |
| Metazoa | Many cell types, epithelia, flagellated sperm | Clonal development from a zygote, with cell differentiation | Complete Wnt, TGF-β, Hedgehog and Notch pathways; Hox/ParaHox genes in ParaHoxozoa; microRNAs | Animals |

The free-living *Corallochytrium* and its relatives, close to the ichthyosporeans, complete the picture of a holozoan ancestor with a complex life cycle of amoeboid, flagellated and encysted stages. The common ancestor of animals and choanoflagellates was therefore probably not a simple flagellate but a cell able to switch between several states, a point that matters for the origin of animal cell types (Section 6.2.1).

### 6.1.2. The Genome of the First Animal

The genomes of these relatives, sequenced since 2008, have changed the picture of animal origins. Many gene families once thought to be animal inventions already existed in unicellular ancestors: cadherins and tyrosine kinases in choanoflagellates; integrins and the components of the integrin adhesion complex in *Capsaspora*; transcription factors such as p53, Myc, Brachyury and T-box genes, and the RNA-binding proteins that regulate them. What animals added was less a new set of building blocks than new ways to use them: several signaling pathways in complete form (Wnt, TGF-β, Hedgehog, Notch), large families of homeobox and other developmental transcription factors, the **Hox genes** of ParaHoxozoa (Section 6.5.2), distal enhancers and three-dimensional organization of the chromatin, and new classes of small regulatory RNAs (**microRNAs**). Rather than a single key innovation, the origin of animals was the integration of a pre-existing toolkit into programs that produce many stable cell types from one zygote ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5)).

## 6.2. How Did Multicellular Animals Arise?

### 6.2.1. Classical Hypotheses

Before molecular phylogenetics, the origin of animals was argued from embryology and from the simplest living animals. Four main hypotheses were proposed.

**Definition 6.2.1 (Gastraea Hypothesis)** The **gastraea hypothesis** of Ernst Haeckel (1872–1874) holds that the ancestor of all metazoans was a gastraea, a hollow spherical colony of flagellated cells (like a blastula) in which one side invaginated to form a two-layered, cup-shaped animal with a gut and a blastopore, resembling the gastrula of living embryos.

| Hypothesis | Author | Ancestor | Assumptions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gastraea | Haeckel 1874 | Invaginated colony, a two-layered sac with a gut | Gastrulation by invagination is ancestral; recapitulated in development |
| Planuloid ancestor (phagocytella) | Metschnikoff 1886 | Solid ciliated colony in which cells that had taken up food migrated inward (ingression) | The planula of cnidarians resembles the ancestor; intracellular digestion preceded a gut |
| Placula | Bütschli 1884, revived by Grell | Flat two-layered disc that fed with its lower surface | The placozoan *Trichoplax* resembles the ancestor |
| Syncytial (cellularization) | Hadži 1953, Hanson 1977 | Multinucleate ciliate that became cellular | The first animals were bilateral, acoel-like flatworms; radial animals derived |
| Symbiotic | Various | Several kinds of protist living together | Different cell types arose from different organisms |

The colonial hypotheses (gastraea, planuloid, placula) differ in the shape of the ancestor but agree that it arose from a colony of flagellated cells, the view that Chinese textbooks call the colonial theory. The hypothesis of an aggregate of a flagellated cell type which then differentiated is the best supported, for several reasons. Animals are the sister group of choanoflagellates, which form colonies by cell division; animal sperm and many embryonic and epithelial cells carry a single flagellum, as choanoflagellates do; and the development of every animal begins with the division of one cell, not with aggregation or symbiosis.

The syncytial hypothesis has been rejected. Ciliates are alveolates, not relatives of animals ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.4)); acoels, which the hypothesis considered the most primitive animals, are bilaterians (Section 6.5.3); and no animal embryo cellularizes a multinucleate ciliate-like cell. The symbiotic hypothesis is incompatible with the genetics of animals, all of whose cells carry one genome. Between the gastraea and the planuloid hypothesis the evidence is less decisive: invagination and ingression both occur in cnidarians and sponges, and the root of the animal tree (Section 6.5.1) affects which condition is ancestral. Many authors now prefer a version in which the earliest animal had a life cycle with flagellated feeding cells and a temporal sequence of cell types, from which spatial differentiation arose by keeping the cell types of different stages together in one body.

This view, sometimes called the temporal-to-spatial hypothesis, has several modern variants. Claus Nielsen's choanoblastaea is a spherical colony of choanoflagellate-like cells from which, by invagination, a gastraea-like animal with a gut arose; it updates Haeckel's idea in the light of the sister-group relationship with choanoflagellates. Gösta Jägersten's bilaterogastraea derives the bilaterians from a gastraea that settled on the sea floor, crawled on its blastoporal side and developed a mouth and anus from the ends of a slit-like blastopore ([7.17.10](/7.17.10)). All these hypotheses agree that the first animals were small, ciliated or flagellated, and fed on bacteria, and that the gut, nerves and muscles were added later; they differ in the order of these additions, which the root of the tree (Section 6.5.1) bears on directly.

### 6.2.2. Fossils and Molecular Clocks

The fossil record of the first animals is sparse and ambiguous ([7.10.29](/7.10.29)). Molecular clocks calibrated with many fossils place the origin of crown-group animals between about 800 and 650 million years ago, in the Tonian or Cryogenian, long before unambiguous animal fossils. Possible traces of sponges include steroid biomarkers (24-isopropylcholestanes) from about 660–635 million years ago, but these may also be made by algae. Among the **Ediacaran biota** (575–539 Mya), *Dickinsonia* preserves cholesterol derivatives and is probably an animal, perhaps of placozoan or stem-bilaterian grade; *Kimberella*, with scratch marks of a radula-like organ, is often interpreted as a stem-group mollusc; and trace fossils of bilaterian burrowers appear from about 560 Mya. Skeletons appear at the end of the Ediacaran (*Cloudina*, about 550 Mya).

The **Cambrian explosion** brought the first fossils that can be assigned with confidence to most living phyla, in sites of exceptional preservation: Chengjiang in Yunnan (about 518 Mya), Sirius Passet in Greenland, the Burgess Shale (about 508 Mya). Many Cambrian animals are stem-group representatives, with some but not all of the features of the living crown group, such as the radiodonts and lobopodians on the stem of the arthropods ([7.17.18](/7.17.18)). Such stem groups show the order in which the characters of a body plan were assembled.

The discrepancy between molecular and fossil dates has narrowed but not disappeared. Early molecular clocks, calibrated with few fossils and a strict clock, placed the divergence of protostomes and deuterostomes 1,000 million years ago or more; relaxed-clock analyses with many calibrations and with the uncertainty of each calibration expressed as a prior distribution give younger and broader estimates, roughly 800–650 Mya for crown-group animals and 650–560 Mya for crown-group bilaterians ([7.10.24](/7.10.24#sec-24.4)). Part of the remaining gap is expected even if the clocks are right, because a lineage becomes recognizable as fossils only after it has acquired the characters that identify it, and because small soft-bodied animals are rarely preserved. Part may reflect faster rates of molecular evolution during the early radiation, which relaxed clocks model only partly. The two kinds of evidence are therefore read together: fossils give minimum ages for nodes, and the clock gives a probability distribution of how much older each node may be.

### 6.2.3. The Ediacaran Record

Animal fossils before the large Ediacaran forms are few and contested. The phosphorites of the Doushantuo Formation at Weng'an in Guizhou, about 600 million years old, preserve microscopic spheres dividing like cleaving embryos, with cellular detail; some may be early animal embryos, others are encysting protists or holozoans, and a supposed small bilaterian from the same deposits (*Vernanimalcula*) has been shown to be an artifact of mineralization. Seaweeds and possible animals occur in the early Ediacaran Lantian biota of Anhui.

The Ediacaran macrofossils fall into three assemblages that succeed one another in time and environment:

| Assemblage | Age (Mya) | Setting | Characteristic organisms | Animal affinities proposed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Avalon | About 575–560 | Deep water, below the reach of light (Newfoundland, England) | Rangeomorph fronds (*Charnia*, *Fractofusus*), discs | Fronds of uncertain affinity; *Haootia*, with muscle-like fibers, and *Auroralumina*, with a cup and tentacles, are candidate cnidarians |
| White Sea | About 560–550 | Shallow seas with microbial mats (Russia, South Australia) | *Dickinsonia*, *Kimberella*, *Spriggina*, *Tribrachidium*, trace fossils | *Dickinsonia* an animal (cholesterol derivatives) of uncertain position; *Kimberella* a probable stem mollusc; *Ikaria*, a millimeter-sized worm, a probable bilaterian trace maker |
| Nama | About 550–539 | Shallow seas, often with carbonate (Namibia, South China) | Tubular *Cloudina*, cup-shaped *Namacalathus*, the segmented *Yilingia* | First biomineralized animals; *Yilingia*, a segmented bilaterian associated with its own trails |

Two features of this record are important for the animal tree. First, motile bilaterians, the makers of horizontal burrows, existed by about 560 Mya, so the split between protostomes and deuterostomes must be older. Second, many Ediacaran organisms, such as the rangeomorphs and the triradial *Tribrachidium*, do not fit any living phylum; they may be stem-group animals or extinct lineages outside the crown groups of living phyla, and their disappearance near the base of the Cambrian may have been caused by competition with, or disturbance of the sea floor by, burrowing and grazing bilaterians.

### 6.2.4. The Cambrian Explosion and the Chengjiang Biota

The **Cambrian explosion** ([7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.4)) is recorded in a sequence of faunas. The base of the Cambrian, 538.8 Mya, is defined by the first appearance of the trace fossil *Treptichnus pedum*, the branching burrow of a bilaterian probing the sediment. The first stage (Fortunian) is dominated by the small shelly fauna, millimeter-sized skeletal elements of early molluscs, hyoliths, brachiopods and sponges and the grasping spines (*Protohertzina*) of early chaetognaths. From the same interval comes the phosphatized microfauna of the Kuanchuanpu Formation in Shaanxi, about 535 Mya, which includes *Saccorhytus*, a sac-like millimeter-sized animal first described as an early deuterostome with pharyngeal openings and later, with better material, reinterpreted as an ecdysozoan without such openings, an example of how preservation can mislead.

Trilobites appear about 521 Mya, and from about 518 Mya a series of sites with soft-part preservation (Konservat-Lagerstätten) records the whole fauna: Chengjiang and Qingjiang in Yunnan and Hubei, Sirius Passet in Greenland, and later the Burgess Shale in Canada. The Chengjiang biota, discovered by Hou Xianguang at Maotianshan in 1984 and now a World Heritage site, contains more than 250 species, with guts, eyes, nerves and even brains preserved.

| Group | Chengjiang representatives | Significance | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sponges | *Leptomitus*, other demosponges and hexactinellids | Sponges were abundant in the early Cambrian | [7.17.7](/7.17.7) |
| Ctenophores | Comb jellies, some with skeletal plates and spokes (scleroctenophores) | Cambrian ctenophores were more diverse in body plan than living ones | [7.17.8](/7.17.8) |
| Lobopodians | *Microdictyon*, *Luolishania*, *Hallucigenia fortis* | Stem-group panarthropods with lobopod legs and dorsal plates or spines | [7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.2) |
| Arthropods | Radiodonts (*Amplectobelua*, *Anomalocaris*), *Fuxianhuia* with a preserved brain, trilobites, *Naraoia* | Stem and crown arthropods: the assembly of the head and appendages | [7.17.18](/7.17.18) |
| Priapulids and relatives | Palaeoscolecids and other priapulid-like worms | Scalidophorans were abundant burrowers | [7.17.17](/7.17.17) |
| Brachiopods | Lingulids preserved with their pedicle and lophophore | Soft parts link Cambrian and living brachiopods | [7.17.16](/7.17.16) |
| Vetulicolians and yunnanozoans | *Vetulicola*, *Yunnanozoon*, *Haikouella* | Possible stem deuterostomes or chordates; interpretations differ | [7.17.43](/7.17.43) |
| Vertebrates | *Myllokunmingia*, *Haikouichthys* | The oldest vertebrates, with myomeres, a notochord and gill pouches | [7.17.45](/7.17.45) |

The Chengjiang fauna shows that the phyla were distinct by 518 Mya but that many of their members were stem-group forms with only part of the crown-group body plan. The arthropod lineage, for example, can be read from lobopodians with soft legs, through radiodonts with jointed frontal appendages, to euarthropods with a jointed exoskeleton on the whole body ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.2)). Such series of stem groups are the main fossil evidence for the order in which the characters of a phylum were assembled, and they are used to calibrate the molecular clock of the animal tree.

## 6.3. "Protozoa": A Historical Group

### 6.3.1. The Protozoa of Zoology Textbooks

**Definition 6.3.1 (Protozoa)** **Protozoa** is the traditional name for the unicellular, mostly motile and heterotrophic eukaryotes formerly classified as the lowest phylum (or subkingdom) of animals. It is not a clade but a polyphyletic grade of **protists**, and it is no longer used as a formal taxon; the word survives informally for heterotrophic protists.

The classification still used in the textbooks of Liu Lingyun and Xu Chongren divides the phylum Protozoa into four classes by their organelles of locomotion: flagellates (Mastigophora), amoebae (Sarcodina), sporozoans (Sporozoa, which have no locomotory organelle in most stages) and ciliates (Ciliata). These classes do not correspond to lineages: each is distributed over several eukaryotic supergroups, and some former protozoa are not protists at all (see the figure below).

The textbooks themselves note that molecular data have overturned the scheme and keep it for convenience. Two of their specific statements need correction. The myxosporeans, listed among the sporozoans, are cnidarians that have been reduced to a few cells: their polar capsules are homologous to cnidarian **cnidae**, and their genomes place them, with the parasite *Polypodium*, as the sister group of the medusozoans ([7.17.9](/7.17.9)). And the *Mesozoa* ("mesozoans"), which the same textbooks place between protozoa and metazoa and relate to ciliates on the basis of the base composition of their DNA, are two unrelated lineages of reduced spiralian bilaterians: the dicyemids, parasites in the kidneys of cephalopods, and the orthonectids, which are annelids ([7.17.12](/7.17.12)). Base composition is not evidence of relationship.

### 6.3.2. Animal Functions at the Level of a Cell

The protists that zoology textbooks use as representatives remain instructive, because they perform within one cell the functions that animals perform with organ systems. Their cell biology is treated in [7.11.20](/7.11.20); the comparison with animals is summarized here.

| Function | *Euglena* | *Amoeba* | *Paramecium* | *Plasmodium* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Support and shape | Pellicle of protein strips under the membrane, allowing euglenoid movement | None; shape changes constantly | Pellicle with alveoli | Pellicle with inner membrane complex |
| Locomotion | Flagellum | Pseudopodia | Cilia in rows, beating in metachronal waves | Gliding of sporozoites and merozoites |
| Feeding | Photosynthesis, or osmotrophy in the dark (mixotrophy) | Phagocytosis, food vacuoles | Cilia drive food into the oral groove and cytostome; food vacuoles; cytoproct | Ingests host hemoglobin in a food vacuole |
| Osmoregulation | Contractile vacuole | Contractile vacuole | Two contractile vacuoles with radial canals | Not needed in host fluids |
| Sensing | Eyespot shading a photoreceptor at the base of the flagellum | Chemical and mechanical stimuli | Membrane potential changes reverse ciliary beating (avoidance reaction) | Host recognition by the apical complex |
| Defense | — | Encystment | Trichocysts | Antigenic variation |
| Reproduction | Longitudinal binary fission | Binary fission | Transverse fission; conjugation with exchange of micronuclei | Schizogony, gametogony and sporogony in two hosts |

The ciliate's division of labor between a diploid micronucleus (germ line) and a polyploid macronucleus (somatic expression) is a unicellular parallel to the separation of germ line and soma in animals. Its avoidance reaction, in which a calcium action potential reverses the beating of the cilia, uses the same kind of voltage-gated channels as animal neurons ([7.16.53](/7.16.53)).

## 6.4. Evidence for Animal Relationships

### 6.4.1. Kinds of Evidence

The first trees of animals were drawn by Ernst Haeckel (1866), who arranged the phyla on a trunk rising from protozoa through coelenterates and worms to vertebrates, guided by the gastraea theory and the biogenetic law. For the next century the main framework was a sequence of grades: Libbie Hyman's treatise on the invertebrates (1940–1967) organized the phyla by symmetry, germ layers and body cavity, and the idea that complexity increased step by step shaped every textbook. Willi Hennig's phylogenetic systematics (1950, 1966) changed the logic of the question, requiring groups to be defined by shared derived characters, and the first cladistic analyses of animal phyla in the 1990s already questioned several grades.

Until the late twentieth century animal phylogeny was inferred from morphology and embryology: body cavities, cleavage patterns, larvae, the structure of cuticles, nephridia and nervous systems. Such characters are informative within phyla but often ambiguous between them, because grades of organization evolve repeatedly ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.6)). Molecular data have transformed the field in stages:

- Ribosomal RNA. Sequences of 18S rRNA (Field and colleagues, 1988, and later studies) separated the protostomes into the Lophotrochozoa (Halanych and colleagues, 1995) and the Ecdysozoa (Aguinaldo and colleagues, 1997), against the Articulata hypothesis.
- Phylogenomics. Since about 2008 data sets of hundreds to thousands of genes from transcriptomes and genomes (Dunn and colleagues, 2008; Laumer and colleagues, 2019) have resolved most relationships among phyla.
- Rare genomic changes. Features that change rarely and are unlikely to arise twice, such as the gain of particular microRNA families (Sempere and colleagues, 2006), the structure of the Hox cluster, the order of genes in mitochondrial genomes and, most recently, the conserved linkage of genes on chromosomes (**synteny**), test relationships independently of sequence models.

### 6.4.2. Sources of Error

The deepest nodes of the animal tree are difficult because the branches leading to them were short (the phyla diverged within tens of millions of years more than 550 million years ago) and the branches since then are long. Two systematic errors are especially important ([7.10.21](/7.10.21)):

- **Long-branch attraction**: rapidly evolving lineages tend to be grouped together, or drawn toward the root, by models that underestimate multiple substitutions. Nematodes and flatworms, whose genes evolve fast, were long placed at the base of the bilaterians for this reason, and fast-evolving ctenophores may be attracted to the long branch of the outgroup.
- Compositional heterogeneity: lineages with different amino-acid compositions can be grouped by composition rather than descent.

Remedies include site-heterogeneous substitution models such as CAT-GTR, recoding amino acids into groups of similar ones (Dayhoff-6 recoding), removing fast genes or distant outgroups, and denser taxon sampling. Laumer and colleagues (2019), the first study with genomic data from every animal phylum, showed that several nodes, such as the position of placozoans and the root of the tree, change with these choices, while most others are robust.

### 6.4.3. Rare Genomic Changes

Rare genomic changes complement sequence data because their signal does not depend on a substitution model. Each type has its own strengths and pitfalls.

| Marker | Example of its use | Pitfalls |
|---|---|---|
| Hox gene signatures | Lophotrochozoans share the posterior Hox genes *Lox4*, *Lox5*, *Post1* and *Post2*; ecdysozoans share a particular *Ubx*/*abdA* peptide motif; deuterostomes have their own posterior Hox genes (de Rosa and colleagues 1999) | Hox clusters are lost or broken in some lineages (tunicates, nematodes, flatworms) |
| MicroRNA families | New families accumulate in the stem lineages of Bilateria, Protostomia, Ecdysozoa, Lophotrochozoa and vertebrates, and are rarely lost (Sempere and colleagues 2006) | Losses do occur (flatworms, tunicates), so absence is weak evidence; ctenophores and placozoans lack microRNAs altogether |
| Mitochondrial gene order | Rearrangements shared by groups of arthropods support Pancrustacea | Gene order evolves very fast in some lineages and converges |
| Intron positions and gene structure | Shared intron positions support relationships among some lineages | Intron gain and loss can be parallel |
| Chromosomal synteny | Ancestral linkage groups and their fusions place ctenophores as sister to all other animals (Schultz and colleagues 2023) and support relationships within phyla | Requires chromosome-level genomes, still unavailable for many phyla |

### 6.4.4. Morphology and Fossils in a Molecular Age

Molecular data do not make morphology obsolete. First, characters must be mapped onto the tree to say anything about the evolution of body plans, and the reconstruction of ancestral states ([7.10.21](/7.10.21#sec-21.5)) depends on knowing the characters of living species precisely. Second, fossils can be placed only by their morphology: the stem groups of the Cambrian are attached to the tree by the characters they share with crown groups, and they provide the calibrations of molecular clocks ([7.10.24](/7.10.24#sec-24.4)). Third, morphology provides an independent test of molecular results: the grasping spines of chaetognaths resemble the jaws of gnathiferans in their fine structure, and the Cambrian *Amiskwia*, with jaws of gnathiferan type on a chaetognath-like body, supports the molecular placement of chaetognaths within Gnathifera (Vinther and Parry 2019). Combined analyses of molecules and morphology, and tip-dated analyses that include fossils as terminals ([7.10.24](/7.10.24#sec-24.5)), are increasingly used for groups with rich fossil records such as arthropods and echinoderms.

### 6.5.1. The Root: Ctenophores or Sponges First?

The first split within the animals separates either the ctenophores or the sponges from all others. The question matters because ctenophores have neurons, muscles and a through gut, whereas sponges have none of these: if ctenophores branched first, either these features arose twice, once in ctenophores and once in the lineage of cnidarians and bilaterians, or they were present in the first animals and lost in sponges and placozoans (see the figure below).

![Two hypotheses for the root of the animal tree. (A) Ctenophores first: either neurons and muscles evolved twice (bars, in ctenophores and in ParaHoxozoa), or they evolved once and were lost in sponges and placozoans (crosses). (B) Sponges first: a single origin in the common ancestor of ctenophores and ParaHoxozoa (Eumetazoa) and one loss in placozoans suffice](./plots/two-roots.py)

Phylogenomic analyses have supported both answers, depending on models, genes and outgroups. Analyses with standard models and many genes usually favor ctenophores first; site-heterogeneous models with recoding sometimes favor sponges first (Pisani and colleagues, 2015; Simion and colleagues, 2017). Schultz and colleagues (2023) brought evidence of a different kind. Comparing the chromosomes of ctenophores, sponges, cnidarians, placozoans, bilaterians and unicellular outgroups, they found ancestral groups of linked genes that are preserved in ctenophores and in the outgroups, but that in sponges, cnidarians, placozoans and bilaterians have been fused and mixed in the same way. Such fusion-with-mixing is practically irreversible, so these four lineages form a clade, *Myriazoa*, and ctenophores are the sister group of all other animals. Most specialists now accept this result.

The question has a turbulent history. The first large phylogenomic analysis of animals (Dunn and colleagues 2008) found ctenophores as the sister group of all other animals, and the genomes of *Mnemiopsis* (Ryan and colleagues 2013) and *Pleurobrachia* (Moroz and colleagues 2014) seemed to confirm it. Critics argued that the result was an artifact of **long-branch attraction**, since ctenophore genes evolve fast and the choanoflagellate outgroup is distant. Analyses with site-heterogeneous models, with distant outgroups removed or with amino acids recoded into fewer categories recovered sponges first (Pisani and colleagues 2015; Simion and colleagues 2017; Feuda and colleagues 2017; Redmond and McLysaght 2021), while others found the ctenophore-first result robust to the same corrections (Whelan and colleagues 2015, 2017). The synteny evidence is valuable because it does not depend on any of these modeling choices.

Traditionally, the animals with nerves, muscles and a gut (ctenophores, cnidarians and bilaterians) were united as the *Eumetazoa*, with sponges as their sister group (Parazoa). If ctenophores branch first, the Eumetazoa in this sense are not a clade, and the name is used either for all animals except sponges and placozoans, as a grade, or abandoned in favor of Myriazoa and ParaHoxozoa. Ctenophores also lack several features that the other three lineages share: they have no Hox or ParaHox genes and no microRNAs, their neurons use few of the small-molecule transmitters of other animals, and their adhesive colloblasts and comb plates have no counterpart elsewhere ([7.17.8](/7.17.8#sec-8.4)).

Ctenophores first does not settle how nervous systems evolved. Ctenophore neurons differ from those of other animals in their transmitters and in the syncytial nerve net recently described in *Mnemiopsis* (Burkhardt and colleagues, 2023), which favors an independent origin, but losses in sponges and placozoans cannot be excluded ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.1)). Sponges, in either case, are not a model of the ancestral animal in every respect.

### 6.5.2. ParaHoxozoa and Planulozoa

The remaining non-bilaterians are the placozoans and the cnidarians. Placozoans, cnidarians and bilaterians share Hox or ParaHox genes and form the clade *ParaHoxozoa*. Cnidarians and bilaterians share a further set of characters, among them a body axis patterned by Wnt signaling with the blastopore at one end, ciliated larvae with an apical organ and a fully differentiated nervous system, and are grouped as *Planulozoa*; some analyses with recoded data place placozoans instead as the sister group of cnidarians. The cnidarians include the **Myxozoa**, reduced parasites once classified among the protozoa ([7.17.9](/7.17.9)).

Placozoans were long known from a single species, *Trichoplax adhaerens*, described in 1883 from the walls of a seawater aquarium. Genomic sampling has since revealed a group of genetically distinct lineages, now classified in four genera (*Trichoplax*, *Hoilungia*, *Polyplacotoma* and *Cladtertia*), which differ little in morphology. Their small genomes contain many genes for developmental signaling and for neuronal secretion, although placozoans have neither nerves nor muscles; their peptide-secreting cells, which coordinate ciliary gliding and feeding, are a possible model of how neurons arose. The alternative placements of placozoans, as sister to Planulozoa or to Cnidaria (Laumer and colleagues 2018), imply different reconstructions: in the second case the planula larva and the axial patterning shared by cnidarians and bilaterians would have been lost in placozoans.

### 6.5.3. Bilateria

**Definition 6.5.1 (Bilateria)** The **Bilateria** (bilaterians) are the clade of animals descended from the common ancestor of xenacoelomorphs, protostomes and deuterostomes. Their synapomorphies include bilateral symmetry with anteroposterior and dorsoventral axes, a mesoderm, a centralized nervous system and an expanded set of Hox genes; most also have a through gut and excretory organs.

The position of the **Xenacoelomorpha** (acoels, nemertodermatids and *Xenoturbella*), small worms without an anus or nephridia, is the main open question within the Bilateria. Most analyses place them as the sister group of all other bilaterians, which are then called **Nephrozoa** after their nephridia; this implies that the first bilaterian may have been a small, simple worm. Others place them within the deuterostomes as the sister group of Ambulacraria (the Xenambulacraria hypothesis), which implies extensive secondary simplification ([7.17.10](/7.17.10)).

The debate illustrates the difficulty of placing fast-evolving lineages. Acoels were long classified as the most primitive flatworms, until rRNA sequences separated them from the Platyhelminthes and placed them near the base of the Bilateria (Ruiz-Trillo and colleagues 1999). *Xenoturbella*, a worm with a sac-like gut without an anus, a nerve net without a brain and no discrete gonads, was first mistaken for a relative of molluscs because of mollusc DNA in its gut, then placed with deuterostomes, and finally united with acoels and nemertodermatids in the Xenacoelomorpha. Phylogenomic analyses with more taxa have placed the clade as sister to Nephrozoa (Hejnol and colleagues 2009; Cannon and colleagues 2016; Rouse and colleagues 2016) or, with better-fitting models and fewer fast genes, as sister to Ambulacraria (Philippe and colleagues 2011, 2019; Kapli and Telford 2020). Because acoels have very long branches, the first result could be long-branch attraction toward the root; because the second is sensitive to the choice of genes, neither is yet decisive.

### 6.5.4. Deuterostomia

The **Deuterostomia** contain only three phyla: echinoderms and hemichordates, which form **Ambulacraria**, and chordates ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#def-5.2.1)). Within Chordata, lancelets (**Cephalochordata**) are the sister group of tunicates (**Tunicata**) plus vertebrates (together **Olfactores**), a result of phylogenomics that reversed the traditional view of lancelets as the closest relatives of vertebrates ([7.17.43](/7.17.43)). Their shared ancestral character, the pharyngeal slits, was lost in echinoderms. Groups once placed here, the lophophorates and chaetognaths, are protostomes.

Ambulacraria is well supported by molecules and by shared morphology: a dipleurula-type larva with an upstream ciliary band, a coelom divided into three pairs of compartments, and an axial complex that includes a filtering organ near the heart ([7.17.41](/7.17.41)). The monophyly of Deuterostomia itself, by contrast, has been questioned: some recent analyses find little support for a clade of Ambulacraria and Chordata, and the two may be successive branches near the base of the Bilateria (Kapli and colleagues 2021). If so, characters shared by ambulacrarians and chordates, such as pharyngeal slits and deuterostomy, would be ancestral for the Bilateria and lost in protostomes, a striking change in the reconstruction of the first bilaterian.

### 6.5.5. Protostomia: Ecdysozoa and Spiralia

The **Protostomia** are the larger branch of the Bilateria, with more than 90% of animal species. They consist of two clades.

**Definition 6.5.2 (Ecdysozoa)** The **Ecdysozoa** are the clade of protostomes that grow by molting a cuticle (in arthropods under the control of ecdysteroid hormones) and lack motile cilia on the body surface. They comprise the scalidophorans (priapulids, kinorhynchs, loriciferans), the nematoids (nematodes, nematomorphs) and the panarthropods (tardigrades, onychophorans, arthropods).

**Definition 6.5.3 (Spiralia, Lophotrochozoa)** The **Spiralia** are the clade of protostomes that includes all animals with spiral cleavage and their descendants: the Gnathifera (rotifers with acanthocephalans, gnathostomulids, micrognathozoans and probably chaetognaths), flatworms, gastrotrichs and the Lophotrochozoa. The **Lophotrochozoa** in the narrow sense are the spiralians with a lophophore or a trochophore larva, or descended from such: molluscs, annelids, nemerteans, brachiopods, phoronids, bryozoans, entoprocts and cycliophorans.

The Ecdysozoa were first recognized in an analysis of 18S rRNA by Aguinaldo and colleagues (1997), who used a slowly evolving nematode to avoid the long-branch artifact that had placed nematodes near the root. The hypothesis contradicted the Articulata, the union of annelids and arthropods by segmentation, and was strongly debated for a decade before phylogenomic data, microRNAs and Hox signatures confirmed it. The morphological characters shared by ecdysozoans are mostly features of the cuticle and its molting: a three-layered cuticle with an outer epicuticle, molting by apolysis and ecdysis, the absence of motile cilia on the epidermis, and in many a terminal mouth surrounded by an eversible introvert or pharynx. The Spiralia, by contrast, are defined by spiral cleavage with a quartet pattern of micromeres and mesoderm from the 4d cell ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#sec-5.1)), which is modified or lost in many members (bryozoans, brachiopods, gnathiferans, flatworms other than polyclads), and many of them have a trochophore larva or a ciliary band of trochophore type.

Within the Ecdysozoa the panarthropods are generally recovered, with onychophorans as the sister group of arthropods, but the position of tardigrades and the relationships among scalidophorans and nematoids are less certain; Laumer and colleagues placed loriciferans with nematodes. Within the arthropods, the **Mandibulata** (myriapods and **Pancrustacea**) and the origin of hexapods within a paraphyletic Crustacea are robust ([7.17.18](/7.17.18), [7.17.22](/7.17.22)). An alternative that grouped myriapods with chelicerates (Myriochelata), found in some early molecular analyses, has been abandoned. Within the Pancrustacea three clades are recognized: the Oligostraca (ostracods, branchiurans and the parasitic pentastomids), the Multicrustacea (malacostracans, copepods and barnacles) and the Allotriocarida, in which the cave-dwelling remipedes, or remipedes with cephalocarids, are the closest relatives of the hexapods ([7.17.21](/7.17.21#sec-21.2)). The insects are thus highly modified crustaceans that colonized land, a conclusion supported also by the structure of their eyes and nervous systems. Within the molluscs, a split between the spicule-bearing Aculifera (aplacophorans and chitons) and the shelled Conchifera is now well supported ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.2)). Within the Spiralia, the **Gnathifera** branch first; whether flatworms and gastrotrichs form a clade (Rouphozoa) is uncertain; and the relationships among the lophotrochozoan phyla, apart from the clade **Lophophorata** (bryozoans, phoronids, brachiopods) and the pairing of entoprocts with cycliophorans, are still unsettled. Spiralia is sometimes called Lophotrochozoa in a broad sense; in this course Lophotrochozoa is used in the narrow sense of Definition 6.5.3.

Several older groupings of small protostomes have dissolved. The Platyzoa, a supposed clade of small acoelomate or blastocoelomate spiralians (flatworms, gastrotrichs, gnathiferans), proved to be a grade: Gnathifera branch first in Spiralia, and flatworms with gastrotrichs (**Rouphozoa**) are the sister group of Lophotrochozoa (Struck and colleagues 2014; Laumer and colleagues 2015). The scalidophorans and nematoids are often combined as the *Cycloneuralia*, worms with a collar-shaped brain around the pharynx, but this group is not consistently recovered. Within the Lophotrochozoa, analyses have proposed a clade of molluscs with entoprocts (Tetraneuralia, from their four longitudinal nerve cords), a clade of nemerteans with brachiopods and phoronids (Kryptotrochozoa) and a clade of annelids with nemerteans, but only the Lophophorata (with brachiopods and phoronids as Brachiozoa) is recovered regularly. The two groups of former mesozoans have been placed by genomic data: orthonectids within the annelids and dicyemids among the spiralians, possibly close to the Rouphozoa ([7.17.12](/7.17.12#sec-12.6)).

### 6.5.9. What the Tree Says about Body Plans

Mapping the characters of [7.17.3](/7.17.3) and [7.17.4](/7.17.4) onto the tree gives a provisional history of the animal body plan. The table lists the node at which each major character probably arose and the main lineages that lost it; the reconstructions depend on the contested nodes discussed above and are revisited in [7.17.44](/7.17.44) and [7.17.70](/7.17.70).

| Character | Probable origin | Main losses or exceptions | Uncertainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multicellularity with epithelia, collagen and sperm with one flagellum | Metazoa | — | — |
| Neurons and muscle cells | Ctenophores and Planulozoa separately, or Metazoa with losses in sponges and placozoans | Sponges, placozoans | Depends on the root |
| Hox and ParaHox genes | ParaHoxozoa | Reduced in several lineages | — |
| Through gut | Ctenophores (anal pores) and Nephrozoa, or Bilateria | Xenacoelomorphs (if derived), flatworms, many parasites | Position of Xenacoelomorpha |
| Bilateral symmetry, mesoderm | Bilateria (perhaps with a bilateral precursor in the cnidarian–bilaterian ancestor) | Adult echinoderms (pentaradial) | — |
| Nephridia | Nephrozoa | Lost in echinoderms, tunicates, many small animals | Homology of protonephridia and metanephridia |
| Coelom | Nephrozoa once, or several times | Rouphozoa, gnathiferans (except chaetognaths), cycloneuralians | Number of origins |
| Pharyngeal slits | Deuterostomia | Echinoderms | Deuterostome monophyly |
| Segmentation | Annelida, Arthropoda (Panarthropoda), Chordata separately | Sipunculans, echiurans; reduced in mites and barnacles | Possible shared components |
| Spiral cleavage, trochophore | Spiralia (trochophore perhaps in Lophotrochozoa) | Modified in bryozoans, brachiopods, cephalopods | Ancestral larva of Spiralia |
| Molted cuticle | Ecdysozoa | — | Cuticle chemistry differs (collagen in nematodes, chitin in arthropods) |

The table makes two general points. Many characters that the grade system arranged in a ladder appear as innovations at particular nodes, followed by losses in lineages that became small, sessile or parasitic; simplification is as common in animal evolution as increase in complexity. And the uncertain nodes of the tree are exactly those on which the reconstruction of the earliest animals and the earliest bilaterians depends, which is why the root and the position of the Xenacoelomorpha remain the most studied questions in animal phylogeny.

## Summary

- Animals belong to Holozoa with three unicellular lineages; their sister group is the **choanoflagellates** (Definition 6.1.1). Much of the animal genetic toolkit predates animals; what is new is its integration into developmental programs.
- Of the classical hypotheses for the origin of animals, the colonial ones derived from a flagellated ancestor are supported, the **gastraea hypothesis** (Definition 6.2.1) and the planuloid hypothesis differing in whether invagination or ingression was ancestral; the syncytial and symbiotic hypotheses are rejected.
- **Protozoa** (Definition 6.3.1) is a polyphyletic grade of protists; its four classes are distributed over the eukaryotic supergroups, microsporidia are fungi and myxosporeans cnidarians.
- The fossil record of animals begins with disputed Cryogenian biomarkers and Doushantuo embryos; the Ediacaran assemblages (Avalon, White Sea, Nama) include probable cnidarians, stem molluscs and bilaterian trace makers; the Cambrian explosion, recorded in the small shelly fauna and in the Chengjiang and Burgess biotas, shows most phyla with many stem-group members.
- Animal phylogeny rests on phylogenomics and rare genomic changes (Hox signatures, microRNAs, gene order, synteny), corrected for long-branch attraction and compositional bias; morphology remains necessary for fossils and for reconstructing ancestral states.
- The current tree: ctenophores are probably the sister group of all other animals (synteny); ParaHoxozoa and Planulozoa; **Bilateria** (Definition 6.5.1) with Xenacoelomorpha as sister to Nephrozoa or within deuterostomes; Deuterostomia (Ambulacraria, Chordata); Protostomia with **Ecdysozoa** (Definition 6.5.2) and **Spiralia**, including **Lophotrochozoa** (Definition 6.5.3).
- Contested nodes include the root (ctenophores or sponges first, and so the status of **Eumetazoa**), the position of placozoans and xenacoelomorphs, the monophyly of Deuterostomia, and the relationships within Lophotrochozoa.
- Many traditional groups (Coelenterata, **Aschelminthes**, Articulata, **Mesozoa**, Platyzoa) are not clades; their members are scattered over the tree. The table of Section 6.5.8 gives the diagnostic features of the thirty-odd living phyla.

## Questions

1. Why does the discovery that choanoflagellates, not some other protists, are the sister group of animals favor the colonial over the syncytial hypothesis?

   **Answer** The sister group shows which features the ancestor of animals inherited. Choanoflagellates are uninucleate flagellated cells that form colonies by incomplete cell division; animals develop from one cell by division and have flagellated sperm and epithelial cells. This fits the derivation of animals from a colony of flagellated cells. The syncytial hypothesis requires a multinucleate ciliate ancestor, but ciliates are alveolates, far from animals on the eukaryote tree, and no close relative of animals is a ciliate.

2. Explain why a fusion of two ancestral chromosomal linkage groups followed by mixing of their genes is stronger evidence of relationship than a single shared amino-acid substitution.

   **Answer** An amino-acid substitution can arise independently in two lineages (homoplasy) or be reversed, and its signal can be swamped by long-branch attraction. A fusion with mixing of genes from two linkage groups is a complex event that is very unlikely to occur twice in the same way, and once the genes are intermixed, the original separate groups cannot be restored by later rearrangements. Lineages sharing such a derived state are therefore almost certainly a clade, as Schultz and colleagues argued for sponges, cnidarians, placozoans and bilaterians.

3. A textbook lists the myxosporeans as a subclass of Sporozoa. What evidence places them among the cnidarians?

   **Answer** Their polar capsules, used to attach the spore to the host, are built like the nematocysts of cnidarians and contain homologous proteins (such as minicollagens); phylogenomic analyses place them within the Cnidaria, together with *Polypodium* as the sister group of the medusozoans; and they are multicellular, with spores formed by several cells, including the capsulogenic cells. Their resemblance to sporozoans results from convergent reduction to intracellular parasitism.

4. If xenacoelomorphs were the sister group of Ambulacraria rather than of Nephrozoa, what would follow for the ancestors of the deuterostomes and of the bilaterians?

   **Answer** Xenacoelomorphs would then be deuterostomes that lost many features: a through gut with an anus, nephridia, pharyngeal slits and perhaps a coelom. The common ancestor of bilaterians would no longer be reconstructed as a simple acoel-like worm; it would more likely have had these organs, and the simplicity of xenacoelomorphs would be secondary. The hypothesis thus shifts the reconstruction from a simple to a complex bilaterian ancestor.

5. Molecular clocks place the origin of crown-group animals at about 800–650 Mya, but the oldest generally accepted animal body fossils are about 560 million years old. Give three explanations for the gap and say what kind of fossil would close it.

   **Answer** First, the earliest animals were probably small (millimeters or less), soft-bodied and lacked mineralized parts, so they had little chance of fossilization outside exceptional deposits such as the Doushantuo phosphorites. Second, the molecular dates may be too old, because rates of molecular evolution were possibly faster in early lineages and because the calibrations and priors of relaxed-clock models are uncertain ([7.10.24](/7.10.24)). Third, some early animals may have been preserved but not recognized, because stem-group animals lacked the diagnostic characters of crown groups (the Ediacaran fronds may be an example). The gap would be closed by unambiguous animal body fossils, for example sponges with spicules or cnidarians with cnidae, in Cryogenian or early Ediacaran rocks, or by molecular dates that are consistent across many independent calibrations.

6. A newly discovered microscopic worm has a ciliated epidermis, a blind gut, no body cavity, protonephridia with flame cells and cuticular jaws in its pharynx. Using the table of Section 6.5.8, to which clades might it belong, and which observations would decide among them?

   **Answer** The combination of cuticular jaws in a muscular pharynx with an acoelomate body points to Gnathifera, most likely Gnathostomulida or Micrognathozoa, within Spiralia; the ciliated epidermis and protonephridia are consistent with this. A rotifer would have a syncytial epidermis with an intracellular lamina, a corona and a blastocoelom, and a flatworm would lack jaws (and usually has a branched gut and neoblasts). Decisive observations would be the ultrastructure of the jaws (whether they are built of rods like gnathiferan trophi), the type of epidermal ciliation (monociliated cells in gnathostomulids) and, above all, molecular sequences analyzed with a well-sampled data set of spiralians.
