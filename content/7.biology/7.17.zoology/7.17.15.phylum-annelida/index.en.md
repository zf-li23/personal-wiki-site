Annelids are the segmented worms: earthworms in the soil, leeches in ponds and wet forests, and a bewildering variety of marine worms that crawl, swim, burrow, build tubes, bore into rock or live in symbiosis with other animals. About 20,000 species are described, from interstitial forms less than half a millimetre long to Australian earthworms and marine eunicids of more than 3 m. Annelids are among the most abundant animals of marine sediments, where they make up a large share of the macrofauna, and earthworms are the dominant animals of many soils by biomass, the "intestines of the earth" in Aristotle's phrase.

On the animal tree, annelids are spiralian protostomes within the **Lophotrochozoa**, related to the molluscs, nemerteans and lophophorates ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.5.5)). They show the characteristic features of the clade: **spiral cleavage**, mesoderm from the 4d cell, **schizocoely** and, in marine species, a **trochophore** larva ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)). Their own innovation is a body built of repeated **segments**, each with its pair of coelomic sacs, its septa, nephridia, ganglion and, in many, its pair of parapodia with **chaetae**. **Segmentation** of the annelid kind was long taken as evidence that annelids and arthropods are close relatives (the Articulata of Cuvier); the current view is that the two phyla belong to different branches of the protostomes and that their segmentation is convergent ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.5.2)).

Molecular phylogenetics has also transformed the classification within the phylum. The three classes of the Chinese textbooks — Polychaeta, Oligochaeta and Hirudinea — are not equivalent clades: leeches are modified oligochaetes, the clitellates as a whole arose from within the "polychaetes", and several groups long treated as separate phyla (Sipuncula, Echiura, Pogonophora and Vestimentifera) are annelids that have lost most or all of their segmentation. The chapter describes the body plan (§15.1), the phylogeny (§15.2), the errant (§15.3) and sedentary (§15.4) polychaetes, the annelids without segments (§15.5), the clitellates with the earthworm as representative animal (§15.6), the leeches (§15.7) and the economic importance of annelids (§15.8), and ends with the comparison with the traditional classification (§15.9). The physiology of annelid muscle and peristalsis is treated in [7.17.4](/7.17.4) and [7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.6.3); here we concentrate on structure, diversity and evolution.

## 15.1. The Annelid Body Plan

**Definition 15.1.1 (Annelida)** The **Annelida** (annelids, segmented worms) are bilaterally symmetrical, coelomate spiralian worms whose body consists of a presegmental prostomium, a peristomium around the mouth, a series of segments added from a growth zone in front of the terminal pygidium, and the pygidium bearing the anus; typically each segment contains a pair of coelomic sacs separated from those of its neighbours by septa, a pair of metanephridia, a ganglion of the ventral nerve cord and paired bundles of chitinous chaetae, and the blood circulates in a closed system of vessels. Segmentation, chaetae or both are reduced or lost in several lineages.

### 15.1.1. Prostomium, Segments and Pygidium

An annelid body has three kinds of regions with different developmental origins. The *prostomium*, in front of the mouth, carries the brain and, in polychaetes, the eyes, antennae, palps and nuchal organs; the *peristomium* surrounds the mouth; the *pygidium* at the rear carries the anus. These regions derive from the episphere, the mouth region and the hyposphere of the trochophore. Between peristomium and pygidium lie the segments, which are formed one after another by a growth zone just in front of the pygidium. The mesoderm of each segment comes from a pair of mesodermal bands produced by **teloblasts**, descendants of the 4d cell, and the bands split into paired coelomic sacs, one pair per segment (schizocoely). Because new segments are added at the rear, the oldest segment is the first one behind the head and the youngest is the last one in front of the pygidium. Prostomium and pygidium are not segments, since they do not arise from the growth zone, although in many species one or more anterior segments fuse with the head.

Most annelid segments are alike (homonomous segmentation), a condition that the Chinese textbooks call the beginning of "true segmentation". The repeated organs of each segment are **serial homologues**: they arise from the same developmental programme repeated along the axis, and in the polychaete *Platynereis* the segment boundaries are marked by stripes of *engrailed* and Wnt signalling much as in arthropods ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.5.2)). Heteronomy is common nonetheless. Tube-dwelling polychaetes have a thorax and an abdomen with different chaetae and parapodia, the lugworm has a head region, a gill-bearing middle and a tail without gills, and in leeches the segments are fixed in number and grouped into regions (§15.7).

Segmentation has advantages that explain its repeated evolution and its persistence. A worm divided into compartments of constant volume can change the shape of one part of its body independently of the others, which allows peristalsis (§15.1.3); local damage is confined to a few segments, which helps regeneration; and serially repeated units can be specialized for different functions without rebuilding the whole body.

### 15.1.2. Body Wall, Chaetae and Parapodia

The body wall consists of a thin collagenous cuticle secreted by a single-layered epidermis rich in gland cells, an outer layer of circular muscle, an inner layer of longitudinal muscle usually divided into four bands, and the somatic peritoneum. Unlike the cuticle of arthropods and nematodes, the annelid cuticle is not moulted; it consists of crossing layers of collagen fibres like those of the Clark–Cowey lattice ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.2.2)), with microvilli of the epidermis passing through it. Many annelids have motile cilia on parts of the body surface, a spiralian feature absent in ecdysozoans.

**Definition 15.1.2 (Chaeta)** A **chaeta** (plural chaetae) of an annelid is an extracellular bristle of β-chitin and protein formed in an epidermal follicle by a single basal cell, the chaetoblast, whose microvilli act as a template: the bristle grows from its base as the microvilli extend and withdraw, leaving a bundle of parallel canals inside it. Muscles attached to the follicle protrude, retract and rotate the chaetae.

Chaetae are among the most useful characters in annelid classification. They may be simple (capillaries, spines, hooks, and the S-shaped uncini of tube dwellers, which grip the wall of the tube) or compound, with a jointed blade. Earthworms have few short, S-shaped chaetae per segment, in four pairs in lumbricids or in a complete ring in the perichaetine megascolecids. The Chinese textbooks rightly insist on the term "chaeta" for annelids and "seta" for the sensory bristles of arthropods, which are cellular processes with a different structure ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)). Chaetae with the same mode of formation are found in brachiopods and in the larvae of some bryozoans ([7.17.16](/7.17.16)), and whether they are homologous in these phyla is still debated.

**Definition 15.1.3 (Parapodium)** A **parapodium** (plural parapodia) is a lateral, unjointed, muscular outgrowth of the body wall of a polychaete segment, typically one pair per segment, divided into a dorsal notopodium and a ventral neuropodium, each bearing a bundle of chaetae supported internally by one or more stout chaetae, the aciculae, and often a dorsal and a ventral cirrus; the coelom extends into it.

Parapodia are paddles, legs and gills at once. In errant polychaetes the notopodia and neuropodia act as oars and levers during crawling and swimming, the dorsal cirri are often expanded into gills or, in scale worms, into protective scales (elytra), and the dense capillaries in the parapodia serve for gas exchange. In tube dwellers and burrowers the parapodia are reduced to low ridges with rows of hooks, and in clitellates they are absent: the chaetae emerge directly from the body wall. Parapodia are not homologous with the legs of arthropods, which evolved from the lobopods of panarthropods ([7.17.18](/7.17.18)).

### 15.1.3. The Septate Coelom as a Hydrostatic Skeleton

The coelom of an annelid is the largest and most regular of any invertebrate. In each segment it forms a pair of sacs, lined by peritoneum, separated in the midline by dorsal and ventral mesenteries and from the neighbouring segments by two-layered septa, so that the body is a chain of fluid-filled compartments. The coelomic fluid contains coelomocytes, which phagocytose particles and bacteria, form clots and in some species carry respiratory pigment. In earthworms the septa have sphincter-controlled openings, and dorsal pores between segments let coelomic fluid escape to moisten the skin.

The septate coelom is the **hydrostatic skeleton** of the annelid ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.3.1)). Coelomic fluid is incompressible, so each compartment keeps its volume: when the circular muscles of a segment contract, the segment becomes long and thin, and when the longitudinal muscles contract it becomes short and thick. The quantitative relation is simple.

**Formula 15.1.1 (Constant-Volume Segment)** Treat a segment as a cylinder of length $l$ and radius $r$ whose volume $V = \pi r^2 l$ is fixed. Then

$$
r = \sqrt{\frac{V}{\pi l}}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}r}{r} = -\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}l}{l},
\tag{15.1}
$$
so a relative change in length produces half that relative change in radius, in the opposite direction.

The model shows why the septa matter. In a worm without septa the fluid could flow freely along the body, and a contraction in one place would dilate the body elsewhere rather than locally; peristalsis would require coordination of the whole body. Annelids that do not crawl by peristalsis, such as many tube dwellers and the lugworm, which burrows with its proboscis, have incomplete septa, and the unsegmented sipunculans and echiurans have none (§15.5). Burrowing in sediment adds a second mechanism. Radiographs of ragworms digging in gelatin, a transparent analogue of mud, show that the worm extends a crack ahead of it by pressing the sides of its head against the walls, driving the crack forward like a wedge: burrowing is a fracture process, not only a pushing one (Dorgan et al. 2005).

### 15.1.4. Circulation and Respiratory Pigments

Annelids have a **closed circulatory system** ([7.14.12](/7.14.12#sec-12.1.3)). The two main vessels run the length of the body in the mesenteries: a contractile dorsal vessel, in which blood is pumped forward by peristaltic waves, and a ventral vessel, in which it flows backward. Segmental vessels connect them through capillary beds in the body wall, the gut, the nephridia and the parapodia, and in earthworms several pairs of enlarged, contractile connecting vessels in the oesophageal region, the "hearts" or aortic arches, maintain the pressure in the ventral vessel. A gut sinus around the intestine collects absorbed food. The vessels are spaces in the extracellular matrix between the coelomic sacs: in this sense the lumen of an annelid vessel is a remnant of the blastocoel, as the Chinese textbooks state, and the vessel wall is formed by the peritoneum of the adjacent coelomic sacs.

Most annelids have dissolved respiratory pigments, and the phylum is the most varied of all animals in this respect ([7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.6.1)):

| Pigment | Metal and colour | Location | Annelids |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hemoglobin (giant extracellular "erythrocruorin", about 3.6 MDa, of 144 globin chains and linker chains) | Iron in heme; red | Dissolved in the blood plasma | Earthworms, most polychaetes, siboglinids |
| Intracellular hemoglobin | Iron in heme; red | Coelomocytes in the coelomic fluid | Glycerids, capitellids, echiurans |
| Chlorocruorin (a hemoglobin with a modified heme) | Iron; green in dilute solution, red when concentrated | Dissolved in the blood plasma | Sabellids, serpulids, flabelligerids |
| Hemerythrin (non-heme) | Two iron atoms per subunit; violet when oxygenated | Coelomocytes | Magelonids, sipunculans (also in brachiopods and priapulids) |

The giant extracellular hemoglobins have very high oxygen affinity and strong cooperativity, which suits animals that live in burrows with little oxygen; the hemoglobin of the vent tube worm *Riftia* binds hydrogen sulfide at separate sites, so that oxygen and sulfide can be carried to the symbiotic bacteria at once without poisoning the worm (§15.5.1). Gas exchange takes place through the body surface in most small annelids and in earthworms, whose moist skin is covered by a capillary network just under the cuticle, and through parapodia or special gills, branched outgrowths of the parapodia or of the head, in larger polychaetes. Small interstitial species often lack blood vessels altogether.

### 15.1.5. Excretion and Chloragogen Tissue

The excretory organs of most annelids are **metanephridia** ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#def-4.6.2)), one pair per segment. Each opens into the coelom by a ciliated funnel, the nephrostome, passes through the septa behind it as a coiled tubule enveloped by capillaries, and opens to the outside by a nephridiopore on the next segment. The funnel draws coelomic fluid into the tubule, the tubule reabsorbs salts and organic solutes and receives wastes from the capillaries, and the final urine is hypotonic in freshwater and terrestrial species, which must eliminate water ([7.14.22](/7.14.22#sec-22.8.1)). Some polychaetes, especially small ones and larvae, retain **protonephridia** whose terminal cells, the solenocytes, carry a single long flagellum surrounded by a filter of microvilli. In many polychaetes the nephridium is combined with a separate coelomoduct, the gonoduct of mesodermal origin, into a compound organ (nephromixium) that releases both urine and gametes.

Earthworms of the family Megascolecidae, including *Amynthas*, have replaced the large paired nephridia by hundreds of small micronephridia of three kinds: integumentary ones opening on the body wall, septal ones on the septa that open into the intestine, and pharyngeal ones opening into the pharynx. Excretion into the gut, where water and salts can be reabsorbed, saves water, an adaptation to life in soil.

On the outer surface of the intestine and the dorsal vessel lies the chloragogen tissue, a layer of yellowish peritoneal cells, the chloragocytes. These cells were once thought to be excretory; they are in fact a metabolic organ comparable to the liver or the fat body of insects: they store glycogen and lipids, synthesize hemoglobin, deaminate amino acids and make urea, and accumulate heavy metals and toxins, which makes earthworms useful indicators of soil pollution. Loaded chloragocytes break away into the coelomic fluid and are eventually removed through the nephridia or deposited in the body wall.

### 15.1.6. Nervous System and Sense Organs

The central nervous system consists of a brain (cerebral ganglia) in the prostomium or, in earthworms, behind it above the pharynx, circumpharyngeal connectives, and a **ventral nerve cord** ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#def-4.7.1)) with a ganglion in each segment. In some polychaetes the two longitudinal cords remain separate and connected by commissures, like the rungs of a ladder; in most annelids they are fused into one cord, but the paired origin is seen in development. Each segmental ganglion sends nerves to the body wall, the parapodia and the nephridia and can coordinate local reflexes, so that segments of a decapitated worm still crawl; the brain controls responses to stimuli and initiates locomotion through the first ventral ganglia. The brain of polychaetes is divided into regions supplying the palps, antennae, eyes and nuchal organs; in errant species it contains mushroom bodies, which have been compared with the **mushroom bodies** of arthropods, and in *Platynereis* the expression of a set of transcription factors in them resembles that of the arthropod and vertebrate learning centres, although whether the structures are homologous is disputed.

Running in the ventral nerve cord are giant fibres, axons of large diameter (up to about 0.1 mm in earthworms, more in some polychaetes) that conduct at up to about 30 m s⁻¹, many times faster than ordinary fibres, and trigger the contraction of the whole longitudinal musculature at once ([7.14.4](/7.14.4)). In the earthworm one median giant fibre responds to stimuli at the front end and two lateral ones to stimuli at the rear, so that a worm touched at the front withdraws backward into its burrow, and one touched at the rear contracts in the opposite direction. Such escape circuits evolved independently in annelids, squid, crayfish and fishes.

Sense organs are richest in errant polychaetes: the eyes of nereids and alciopids are cups with a lens and many photoreceptors, the latter large enough to form images; the nuchal organs, ciliated pits or folds behind the prostomium, are chemosensory; statocysts occur in some burrowers. Earthworms have no eyes, but photoreceptor cells scattered in the epidermis, most numerous at the front and rear ends, make them avoid light; their skin is densely supplied with chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors. The larva of *Platynereis* has become a model for the evolution of eyes and of the nervous system, because its simple eyes and its whole nervous system have been mapped cell by cell.

### 15.1.7. Reproduction and Development

Polychaetes are mostly gonochoristic. Their gonads are temporary proliferations of the peritoneum in many segments; gametes mature in the coelomic fluid and are released through the nephridia, the coelomoducts or ruptures of the body wall. Fertilization is usually external. Cleavage is spiral and determinate ([7.5.6](/7.5.6)), and in many species the embryo becomes a planktonic trochophore with a prototroch, an apical organ and a pair of protonephridia; as segments are added from the growth zone, the larva becomes a metatrochophore and then a nectochaete, which swims with the parapodia of its first segments before it settles ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#def-5.4.2)). Many species have lecithotrophic larvae or brood their eggs in tubes or under scales. The clitellates, by contrast, are hermaphrodites with permanent gonads in a few segments, internal fertilization within a cocoon and direct development (§15.6).

Asexual reproduction by fission or budding occurs in many polychaetes and aquatic oligochaetes, and regeneration of lost anterior or posterior segments is widespread. In syllids, stolons, posterior parts of the body that produce gametes, are budded off and swim away to spawn while the stock regenerates; the most remarkable, *Ramisyllis multicaudata*, which lives inside a sponge, has a single head and a body that branches hundreds of times, every branch tip budding stolons. Asexual budding and regeneration both rely on the posterior growth zone and on pluripotent cells, and they show the developmental flexibility of the segmented body plan.

## 15.2. Phylogeny and Classification

### 15.2.1. From Three Classes to a Tree

The traditional classification, still used in the Chinese textbooks, recognized three classes of annelids: Polychaeta, Oligochaeta and Hirudinea, with Archiannelida (small interstitial forms) sometimes added as a fourth. The Polychaeta were further divided into Errantia and Sedentaria according to their way of life.

**Definition 15.2.1 (Polychaeta)** The **Polychaeta** (polychaetes) are, in the traditional sense, the annelids without a clitellum, mostly marine, usually with parapodia bearing numerous chaetae, a head with sensory appendages, separate sexes, external fertilization and a trochophore larva; in phylogenetic terms the traditional Polychaeta is a **paraphyletic group** from which the clitellates and the formerly separate phyla Sipuncula, Echiura and Pogonophora are derived, and the name is now either abandoned or used for the whole phylum or for the clade Pleistoannelida.

Two discoveries undermined the old scheme. First, analyses of morphology (Rouse and Fauchald 1997) and then of molecules showed that the clitellates arose from within the polychaetes, which makes "Polychaeta" in the old sense equivalent to "Annelida minus Clitellata". Second, phylogenomic studies (Struck et al. 2011; Weigert et al. 2014; Andrade et al. 2015; Helm et al. 2018) placed the sipunculans, echiurans and siboglinids inside the annelids and found a well-supported topology of the deep branches.

The names Errantia and Sedentaria of the old classification have therefore survived as the names of the two great clades, although their composition is not that of the nineteenth-century groups: some sedentary families belong to the Errantia and some mobile ones to the Sedentaria, and the clitellates, whose way of life is neither, are sedentarians. Myzostomids, small parasites of crinoids, and the parasitic orthonectids ([7.17.12](/7.17.12)) are probably highly modified annelids as well, though their position is not settled.

### 15.2.3. The Fossil Record

Soft-bodied worms fossilize poorly, and the annelid record depends on exceptional deposits and on the hard jaws of eunicids. The oldest unquestioned annelids are from the Early Cambrian Sirius Passet biota of Greenland (*Phragmochaeta*, about 518 million years ago), followed by polychaetes of the Chengjiang and Burgess Shale faunas (*Canadia*, *Burgessochaeta*, *Kootenayscolex*), which already have parapodia with chaetae; *Kootenayscolex* shows that the peristomium of early annelids carried chaetae, which suggests that the head was originally a modified segment (Nanglu and Caron 2018). Sipunculans with the modern body form are known from Chengjiang. From the Ordovician onward, the isolated jaws of eunicids (scolecodonts) are common microfossils used in biostratigraphy, and calcareous serpulid tubes are abundant from the Triassic. Clitellates have almost no fossil record, apart from cocoons and leeches in amber; molecular clocks place their origin in the Palaeozoic, and their radiation on land probably followed the spread of forest soils.

## 15.3. Errantia: Ragworms and Their Relatives

### 15.3.1. The Ragworm: A Representative Polychaete

The representative polychaete of the Chinese textbooks is the ragworm, the clam worm of North America (*Nereis* in the broad sense; many species are now in *Hediste*, *Alitta*, *Perinereis* and *Neanthes*). Ragworms live in burrows in the sand and mud of the intertidal zone and in estuaries, emerging at night to hunt, scavenge and graze algae; the common Chinese species include *Perinereis aibuhitensis* and *Tylorrhynchus heterochaetus*, the latter living in brackish rice fields of southern China.

| System | Structure |
|---|---|
| External form | Body up to 20–30 cm, of up to 200 similar segments; prostomium with two pairs of eyes, a pair of short antennae and a pair of large, two-jointed palps; peristomium with four pairs of tentacular cirri; nuchal organs at the rear of the prostomium; pygidium with a pair of anal cirri |
| Body wall and parapodia | Cuticle, epidermis, circular muscle, four bands of longitudinal muscle, oblique muscles moving the parapodia; biramous parapodia, notopodium and neuropodium each with an aciculum, compound chaetae and cirri |
| Feeding and digestion | An eversible pharynx (proboscis) armed with a pair of strong chitinous jaws, hardened by zinc, and rows of small denticles (paragnaths) whose pattern identifies the species; oesophageal glands; straight intestine |
| Circulation and respiration | Closed system with dorsal and ventral vessels and segmental vessels; capillaries in the parapodia; dissolved hemoglobin; gas exchange through parapodia and body surface |
| Excretion | One pair of metanephridia per segment |
| Nervous system | Brain in the prostomium; ventral nerve cord with segmental ganglia and giant fibres |
| Reproduction | Sexes separate; gametes from the peritoneum, maturing in the coelom; many species transform into swimming epitokes and spawn in swarms; trochophore and nectochaete larvae |

### 15.3.2. Epitoky and Swarming

Many errant polychaetes spawn only once, in a transformation of the adult called *epitoky*: the sexually mature form (the epitoke) differs from the immature benthic form (the atoke) in having enlarged eyes, reduced head appendages, a degenerated gut, enlarged parapodia with paddle-like chaetae and a body packed with gametes. In nereids the whole worm transforms ("heteronereis"); in eunicids and syllids only the posterior part does, and it may separate from the anterior part. Epitokes leave the bottom and swim to the surface in synchronized swarms, where males and females release their gametes, guided by pheromones, and then die.

Swarming is timed by the moon. The Samoan palolo worm *Palola viridis* spawns at dawn on one or two days a week after the full moon in October or November, and islanders gather the swarming epitokes as a delicacy; the Atlantic palolo spawns in summer near the last quarter moon. *Platynereis dumerilii* swarms in the days after the new moon, and laboratory studies of this species have shown that a circalunar clock, entrained by moonlight, acts together with circadian clocks to time maturation and swarming. In nereids the transformation is controlled by a brain hormone that inhibits maturation as long as the worm is young; its decline releases the gonads and triggers epitoky. Synchronized spawning in a dense swarm increases the chance of fertilization in the open water and satiates predators.

Amphinomids, the fireworms, are not errantians but belong to a basal lineage; they are mentioned here because they look like errant polychaetes. The bearded fireworm *Hermodice carunculata* feeds on corals, and its hollow calcareous chaetae break off in the skin of a predator or a careless diver and inject a painful toxin.

## 15.4. Sedentaria: Burrowers and Tube Dwellers

Sedentarians have given up active hunting for feeding on deposits or on particles suspended in the water. Their bodies are often divided into regions, their parapodia reduced to ridges with hooked chaetae that grip the tube, and their head bears the feeding apparatus: tentacles, palps or a crown of radioles.

| Group | Way of life | Feeding |
|---|---|---|
| Lugworms (Arenicolidae) | In an L- or U-shaped burrow in sand flats, marked by coiled casts at the tail end and a depression above the head; *Arenicola marina* up to 20 cm, gills on the middle segments | Swallows sand at the head end, digesting microorganisms and organic matter; pumps water through the burrow by peristalsis |
| Capitellidae | Earthworm-like burrowers of organically enriched mud | Deposit feeders; *Capitella teleta* is a classic indicator of organic pollution and a model of spiralian development |
| Spionidae | Tube dwellers in sediment with a pair of long grooved palps | Deposit and suspension feeders; *Polydora* bores into mollusc shells and damages cultured oysters, scallops and pearl oysters |
| Chaetopteridae (basal, see §15.2.2) | U-shaped parchment tube; middle segments form fans that pump water | A mucous bag filters particles, is rolled into a ball and eaten; bioluminescent |
| Terebellidae (spaghetti worms) | Tubes of sand grains; long extensible tentacles spread over the sediment | Deposit feeders: particles travel along a ciliated groove of each tentacle |
| Sabellidae (fan worms) | Flexible tubes of mucus and sediment; a crown of feathery radioles | Suspension feeders: cilia on the radioles create a current and sort particles by size; the crown withdraws in a fraction of a second |
| Serpulidae | Calcareous tubes, an operculum that closes the tube; *Hydroides elegans* is a major fouling organism of ships, *Spirorbis* forms tiny coiled tubes on seaweed | Suspension feeders with a radiolar crown |
| Sabellariidae | Build reefs of cemented sand tubes | Suspension feeders |

The tube is the sedentarian's external skeleton and refuge. It is secreted as mucus, protein or calcium carbonate by glands of the body wall, often incorporating sand grains or shell fragments selected by size, and it allows the worm to ventilate its burrow and to withdraw from predators. Sabellids and serpulids illustrate the trade-off between feeding and safety: their radioles must be extended to feed but are bitten off by fish, so they carry eyespots and withdraw at the passing of a shadow, and regenerate lost radioles quickly.

## 15.5. Annelids That Lost Their Segments

Three groups long regarded as separate phyla are now placed inside the annelids, and each has reduced or lost segmentation in the adult. They demonstrate that a body plan character as fundamental as segmentation can be lost.

### 15.5.1. Siboglinidae: Beard Worms and Vent Tube Worms

Siboglinids are tube-dwelling annelids without a mouth or gut in the adult; they obtain their food from chemoautotrophic bacteria that they house in a special organ, the *trophosome*. About 200 species are known, in four groups:

| Group | Habitat | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Frenulata (beard worms, the former Pogonophora) | Soft sediments rich in organic matter, from shallow water to more than 9,000 m depth | Very thin, a fraction of a millimetre to a few millimetres wide and up to tens of centimetres long, with one to many long tentacles; the rear end, with chaetae and septa, anchors the tube |
| Vestimentifera (vent and seep tube worms) | Hydrothermal vents and cold seeps | *Riftia pachyptila*, up to 2 m long, with a red plume of gill filaments and a body cavity filled with trophosome |
| *Sclerolinum* | Rotting wood and sediments | Intermediate features |
| *Osedax* (bone-eating worms) | Bones of dead whales on the sea floor | Females send root-like extensions into the bone and house heterotrophic bacteria that digest collagen and lipids; the males are microscopic dwarfs living inside the female's tube |

*Riftia* was discovered in 1977 at the Galápagos Rift and became the emblem of vent ecosystems. Its plume takes up oxygen, carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulfide from the mixing zone of vent fluid and seawater; the hemoglobin carries both oxygen and sulfide (§15.1.4) to the trophosome, where the bacteria (*Candidatus* Endoriftia persephone) oxidize sulfide and fix carbon dioxide by the Calvin cycle and the reverse tricarboxylic acid cycle, and the worm digests part of the bacterial population ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)). With this supply *Riftia* grows faster than almost any other marine invertebrate, more than 80 cm a year. The larva and juvenile still have a transient mouth and gut; the free-living symbionts infect the young worm through its skin, migrate to the mesoderm around the gut, which develops into the trophosome, and the gut degenerates (Nussbaumer et al. 2006).

The beard worms, first dredged by the Dutch *Siboga* expedition in 1900 and described in 1914, were for most of the twentieth century treated as a separate phylum, even as deuterostomes, because the anterior end of the body, with its tentacles, was mistaken for the whole animal; the segmented, chaetigerous posterior end was usually broken off and lost during collection. Its discovery in 1964 showed the annelid affinity, which molecular data later confirmed.

### 15.5.2. Echiurans: Spoon Worms

Echiurans, about 160 species now classified as the family Thalassematidae close to the capitellids, are sausage-shaped marine worms with a large, non-retractile prostomium, the proboscis, which is often spoon-shaped or forked and can be extended many times the length of the trunk to collect deposits. The trunk has one pair of ventral hooked chaetae and, in some, rings of chaetae around the anus; the coelom is spacious and undivided; the gut is very long and coiled; the blood vessels are simple, and coelomocytes contain hemoglobin. *Urechis unicinctus* of the coasts of the Yellow and Bohai Seas lives in a U-shaped burrow, through which it pumps water by peristalsis; a mucus net spun across the burrow filters particles, and the net is eaten with its catch. It is a delicacy in northern China.

*Bonellia viridis* of European coasts is a classic case of **environmental sex determination**. The female has a trunk of a few centimetres and a forked proboscis up to more than 1 m long; the male, 1–3 mm long, is ciliated and lives in the female's genital sac, where it does nothing but produce sperm. A larva that settles on the sea floor alone becomes a female; one that settles on the proboscis of a female becomes a male, under the influence of a substance (bonellin) in the female's skin.

Echiurans have no segments as adults, but during development their ventral nerve cord forms as a series of paired ganglion rudiments that later fuse, and in some species the mesoderm forms segmental blocks that disappear. These traces are consistent with their phylogenetic position: echiurans descend from segmented ancestors.

### 15.5.3. Sipunculans: Peanut Worms

Sipunculans, about 150 species, are unsegmented marine worms without chaetae, with a trunk and a slender anterior introvert that can be retracted into the trunk by retractor muscles and everted by the pressure of the coelomic fluid. The mouth at the tip of the introvert is surrounded by ciliated tentacles that collect deposits or suspended particles, and the introvert often carries hooks. The gut is U-shaped and coiled, with the anus on the dorsal side of the anterior trunk; there are no blood vessels, but a separate fluid system serves the tentacles, and coelomocytes contain hemerythrin. A pair of large metanephridia releases gametes as well as urine, and development passes through a trochophore and, in many species, a long-lived planktonic larva, the pelagosphera. Sipunculans live in burrows in sediment, in crevices of rock and coral, and in empty shells; some bore into coral and limestone. *Sipunculus nudus* of the coasts of southern China, called "sand worm" in local markets, is farmed on tidal flats in Guangxi and Fujian.

The Chinese textbooks treat sipunculans and echiurans as separate phyla, close to the annelids. Phylogenomic studies place sipunculans as the sister group of the amphinomids, near the base of the annelid tree, and show in their larvae a transient serial arrangement of neurons in the ventral nerve cord (Kristof et al. 2008), a vestige of the segmentation that their ancestors had.

### 15.5.4. Is Segmentation Reversible?

The annelid tree shows segmentation lost at least twice (sipunculans and echiurans) and greatly modified in the siboglinids, whose segmented region is a short opisthosome. This overturns a common assumption of older zoology, that complex characters, once acquired, are not lost, and that unsegmented coelomates like sipunculans and echiurans must therefore represent stages before segmentation evolved. When a character can be lost, its absence in a group is not evidence that the group is primitive. The same lesson recurs throughout the course: the acoelomate condition of flatworms ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)), the reduced coelom of molluscs ([7.17.13](/7.17.13)) and the absent segmentation of most spiralians must each be judged against the phylogeny, not against an imagined ladder of increasing complexity. It also cautions against using the presence or absence of segmentation to decide the relationships of annelids, arthropods and chordates ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.5.2)).

## 15.6. Clitellata

**Definition 15.6.1 (Clitellata, Clitellum)** The **Clitellata** (clitellates: oligochaetes and leeches) are annelids with a **clitellum**, a girdle of thickened, glandular epidermis on a fixed group of segments near the front of the body, which in sexually mature animals secretes the mucus that binds partners in copulation and the cocoon in which the eggs are fertilized and develop; clitellates are hermaphrodites with gonads in a few fixed anterior segments, have no parapodia and few or no chaetae, and develop directly without a larva.

The clitellates, about 8,500 species, are primarily animals of fresh water and soil, with a few marine species. Their phylogenetic position within the Sedentaria implies that they descend from marine polychaetes that colonized fresh water and then land; the cocoon, which protects the embryos from desiccation and osmotic stress, and direct development, which avoids a vulnerable larva, are adaptations to these environments. The traditional class Oligochaeta is paraphyletic with respect to the leeches, whose closest relatives are the freshwater Lumbriculidae; the name "oligochaetes" is used informally for clitellates other than leeches.

**Definition 15.6.2 (Oligochaeta)** The **Oligochaeta** (oligochaetes, earthworms and their kin) are the clitellates other than the leeches and their immediate relatives: segmented worms with few chaetae per segment, a reduced head without appendages, a septate coelom and a clitellum; the group is paraphyletic, because the leeches arose from within it.

### 15.6.1. The Earthworm: A Representative Clitellate

The representative earthworm of the Chinese textbooks is *Pheretima* in the old broad sense, the commonest earthworms of East Asia; most Chinese species are now placed in *Amynthas* and *Metaphire* of the family Megascolecidae (for example the medicinal *Amynthas aspergillum* and *Metaphire guillelmi*). Western textbooks use the European *Lumbricus terrestris* (Lumbricidae), which differs in a few details given in brackets.

| System | Structure |
|---|---|
| External form | About 100 or more similar segments, commonly 15–30 cm (the largest Chinese species, from Hainan, reach about 70 cm); prostomium a small lobe without appendages above the mouth; chaetae in a ring around the middle of each segment (perichaetine) [four pairs per segment in *Lumbricus*]; dorsal pores between segments from about the 11th/12th onward |
| Clitellum | Ring-shaped, on segments 14–16, without intersegmental grooves [saddle-shaped, on about segments 32–37 in *Lumbricus*] |
| Genital openings | A single female pore ventrally on segment 14; a pair of male pores on segment 18 [on segment 15 in *Lumbricus*]; two to four pairs of spermathecal pores in the intersegmental grooves of segments 5/6 to 8/9, the number and position characteristic of the species |
| Body wall and coelom | Cuticle, epidermis with mucous and sensory cells, circular and longitudinal muscle (obliquely striated), peritoneum; septa with sphincters, absent in the first few segments and in the gizzard region |
| Digestive system | Mouth, muscular pharynx that sucks in soil, oesophagus with calciferous glands that excrete excess calcium as calcite and regulate the pH of the blood, a thin-walled crop and a muscular gizzard lined with cuticle that grinds the food, and a long intestine with a dorsal infolding, the typhlosole, which enlarges the absorptive surface; a pair of intestinal caeca at about segment 26 |
| Circulation | Dorsal vessel (blood forward), ventral vessel (blood backward), subneural and lateral oesophageal vessels; four or five pairs of contractile lateral vessels (hearts) in the oesophageal region; dissolved giant hemoglobin |

| Reproduction | Two pairs of testes in testis sacs in segments 10 and 11, seminal vesicles in 11 and 12, vasa deferentia joined by prostate glands near the male pores; one pair of ovaries on the septum 12/13 in segment 13, with oviducts opening on segment 14; spermathecae in the anterior segments |

### 15.6.2. Copulation and the Cocoon

Earthworms are simultaneous hermaphrodites but do not fertilize themselves. Two worms lie with their ventral surfaces together and head to tail, bound by mucus from the clitellum, and each transfers sperm to the spermathecae of the other; in *Amynthas* the male pores of each are pressed against the spermathecal pores of the partner, while in *Lumbricus* the sperm runs along external seminal grooves. After the partners separate, each worm secretes around its clitellum a tube of mucus and a protein sheath, into which the clitellum releases albumin. The worm backs out of the tube; as the tube slides forward over the female pore it receives eggs, and over the spermathecal pores it receives the stored sperm of the partner, so that fertilization takes place inside the tube. When the tube slips off the head, its ends close and it becomes a lemon-shaped cocoon a few millimetres long, containing one to twenty embryos that feed on the albumin. In most species only one or a few hatch, after two to several weeks, as miniature worms.

Some earthworms, including several pest and invasive species, are parthenogenetic, and many have a high capacity for regeneration of the posterior end; asexual fission is common in aquatic oligochaetes such as *Nais* and *Aeolosoma* (the latter, often listed among the oligochaetes in older textbooks, is not a clitellate).

### 15.6.3. Diversity of Oligochaetes

| Group | Habitat and features | Examples |

Earthworm ecologists classify species by their way of life, which matters more for their role in soil than their taxonomy: epigeic species (such as *Eisenia fetida*) live in leaf litter and compost and do not burrow deeply; endogeic species (many *Aporrectodea*) burrow horizontally in the mineral soil and feed on soil organic matter; anecic species (*Lumbricus terrestris*) make permanent vertical burrows down to 2 m and drag leaves into them from the surface.

### 15.6.4. Earthworms and Soil

Darwin's last book, *The Formation of Vegetable Mould through the Action of Worms* (1881), showed by forty years of measurements that earthworms bury objects lying on the surface — stones, ruins, the chalk spread on his fields — under a layer of their casts, and estimated that the worms of an English pasture bring up on the order of 10 tonnes of soil per acre each year. Modern studies have confirmed the magnitude and the consequences. Earthworms mix organic matter into the mineral soil, create stable aggregates and continuous macropores that increase infiltration and aeration, stimulate microbial activity, and accelerate the decomposition of leaf litter; they are the classic example of ecosystem engineers.

**Example 15.6.1 (How Fast Do Earthworms Turn Over a Soil?)** Suppose the earthworms of a pasture produce casts at a rate of $25\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}\,a^{-1}}$ (Darwin's 10 tonnes per acre) and work the top $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ of soil, whose bulk density is $1.2\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. The mass of soil in this layer is

$$
m = 0.20\,\mathrm{m} \times 10^4\,\mathrm{m^2\,ha^{-1}} \times 1200\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} = 2.4 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}} = 2400\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}},
$$
so the whole layer passes through the guts of the worms in about $2400/25 \approx 100$ years. Casting rates measured in temperate pastures range from a few to more than $50\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}\,a^{-1}}$, and in some tropical grasslands they exceed $200\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}\,a^{-1}}$, corresponding to turnover times from decades to a few centuries. If the casts are deposited on the surface, they raise it by $25\,\mathrm{t}/(1.2\,\mathrm{t\,m^{-3}} \times 10^4\,\mathrm{m^2}) \approx 2\,\mathrm{mm}$ per year, which is the order of the burial rates Darwin measured (he estimated about 5 mm).

:::

Because earthworms change soils so strongly, their introduction can transform ecosystems. The hardwood forests of the formerly glaciated northern United States and Canada had no native earthworms after the last glaciation; European lumbricids and, more recently, Asian *Amynthas* ("jumping worms") introduced with soil and fishing bait consume the thick forest floor of leaf litter, change the soil structure, release nutrients and harm understorey plants, salamanders and ground-nesting birds. The same animal that improves an agricultural soil is an invasive ecosystem engineer in a forest adapted to its absence.

## 15.7. Leeches

**Definition 15.7.1 (Hirudinea)** The **Hirudinea** (leeches and their relatives) are clitellates with a fixed number of segments, each subdivided externally into several annuli, an anterior sucker around the mouth and a posterior sucker formed from the last seven segments, no parapodia and (except in *Acanthobdella*) no chaetae, and a coelom largely filled by connective and botryoidal tissue and reduced to a system of channels (sinuses) that in most leeches replaces the blood vessels.

### 15.7.1. Body Plan

The body of a leech is dorsoventrally flattened and muscular, with segments of the same number in all true leeches (usually counted as 32, or 34 when the head region is included; textbooks give 32–34), so that the segment and the annulus can be used as landmarks for comparing species. The head comprises the prostomium and the first few segments fused into the anterior sucker, bearing 2–10 pairs of eyes; the posterior sucker comprises the last seven segments. Between the epidermis and the circular muscle there is a thick dermis with pigment cells, and diagonal and dorsoventral muscles allow the body to flatten and twist. Leeches move by swimming with dorsoventral undulations or by looping: the anterior sucker attaches, the body extends, the posterior sucker attaches behind it and the body contracts.

The reduction of the septa and the coelom ends the segment-by-segment hydrostatic skeleton of other annelids; instead, the whole body works as a single fluid-filled cylinder, which allows looping and undulation but not peristalsis. In the most primitive living leech, *Acanthobdella peledina*, a parasite of salmonid fishes in cold lakes of northern Europe and Siberia, the anterior segments still have chaetae and a septate coelom and there is no anterior sucker; in the rhynchobdellids the original blood vessels persist inside the coelomic channels, and in the arhynchobdellids the channels have taken over the function of the circulation entirely. These conditions form a series that shows how the leech body plan was derived from that of an oligochaete.

### 15.7.2. Feeding

About three-quarters of leech species feed on blood or body fluids of vertebrates and invertebrates, usually as temporary ectoparasites; the others are predators that swallow small invertebrates whole.

| Group | Feeding apparatus | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Rhynchobdellida (proboscis leeches) | A muscular proboscis protruded through a pore in the anterior sucker and pushed into the host's tissue; blood vessels retained in the coelomic channels | Glossiphoniidae (*Glossiphonia*, *Helobdella*, *Placobdella*, on turtles, amphibians and snails); fish leeches Piscicolidae; turtle leeches Ozobranchidae (*Ozobranchus*, with finger-like gills on the sides) |
| Arhynchobdellida: jawed leeches | No proboscis; three toothed jaws in the buccal cavity that make a Y-shaped incision | Medicinal leeches *Hirudo*; land leeches *Haemadipsa* of moist tropical and subtropical forests |
| Arhynchobdellida: jawless | Large muscular pharynx; predators | *Erpobdella*; *Whitmania*, which feeds on snails and other invertebrates |

A blood-feeding leech can take a meal several times its own weight; the excess water is removed quickly by the nephridia, and the concentrated blood is stored in the lateral caeca of the crop and digested slowly over months, with the help of symbiotic bacteria (*Aeromonas veronii* in *Hirudo*). Leeches have few digestive proteases of their own, and a medicinal leech can survive more than a year on a single meal. Their saliva is a pharmacy: hirudin, a specific inhibitor of thrombin, prevents clotting; other components inhibit platelet aggregation, dilate vessels and anaesthetize the wound.

### 15.7.3. The Medicinal Leech

The medicinal leech of Europe (*Hirudo medicinalis*, and the closely related *H. verbana*, which is the species mostly supplied commercially) and the Japanese medicinal leech *H. nipponia* of East Asia are the representative leeches of the textbooks. The dried bodies of *Whitmania pigra*, *Hirudo nipponia* and *Whitmania acranulata* are used in traditional Chinese medicine. Bloodletting with leeches, practised since antiquity, nearly exterminated *H. medicinalis* in Europe in the nineteenth century, when tens of millions were imported each year into France alone. Today leeches are used after microsurgery: when a reattached finger or ear or a skin flap fills with blood because its veins have not yet reconnected, leeches applied to it remove the stagnant blood and the continuing bleeding from the bite keeps the tissue alive until venous drainage is restored; in 2004 the United States approved them as a medical device. Hirudin, produced by recombinant DNA technology, and its synthetic analogue bivalirudin are clinical anticoagulants.

The medicinal leech is also a classic model of neurobiology. Each of its 21 midbody ganglia contains about 400 neurons, most of them identifiable from animal to animal by size, position and function, so that circuits for swimming, crawling, heartbeat and the local bending reflex have been described neuron by neuron. These studies, together with those of the giant neurons of *Aplysia* ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.1.6)), established the idea that behaviour can be explained by small, identifiable circuits ([7.14.6](/7.14.6)).

### 15.7.4. Reproduction

Leeches are hermaphrodites with internal fertilization. The medicinal leech has about ten pairs of testes in successive segments and one pair of ovaries; the male gonopore with an eversible penis opens on one segment and the female gonopore on the next, in the clitellar region. Jawed leeches copulate with the penis; many rhynchobdellids and erpobdellids instead implant spermatophores into the body wall of the partner (hypodermic impregnation), from where the sperm migrate through the tissues to the ovaries. Eggs are laid in cocoons formed by the clitellum, as in earthworms. Glossiphoniids show parental care: the parent broods the eggs on its ventral surface and carries the young until they can feed. The glossiphoniid *Helobdella* has become a model of spiralian development, in which the teloblast lineages that form the segments can be followed cell by cell ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)).

## 15.8. Annelids and Humans

- Food and bait: ragworms and lugworms are major baits of sea angling and are farmed for it, as in the culture of *Perinereis aibuhitensis* in northern China; polychaetes and their larvae are essential food for fishes and for shrimp larvae in hatcheries. Palolo, *Urechis* and *Sipunculus* are eaten in parts of Asia and the Pacific.
- Soil and waste: earthworms improve soil fertility (§15.6.4); vermicomposting with *Eisenia fetida* and *Perionyx excavatus* converts manure, sewage sludge and food waste into fertilizer; earthworms accumulate heavy metals and pesticides and are standard animals in tests of soil toxicity.
- Medicine: the dried earthworm ("earth dragon") of traditional Chinese medicine; lumbrokinase, a group of fibrinolytic enzymes from earthworms used as a thrombolytic in China; leeches and hirudin (§15.7.3).
- Harm: fouling by serpulids and sabellids on ships and in cooling systems; shell-boring spionids (*Polydora*) in shellfish culture; blood-feeding leeches on livestock and people, especially land leeches in the forests of southern China and Southeast Asia; earthworms as intermediate hosts of some parasites of birds and pigs, and as invasive species in northern forests (§15.6.4).
- Environmental monitoring: *Capitella* and tubificids as indicators of organic pollution, the diversity of polychaete assemblages as an index of the health of marine sediments.

Several details in the Chinese textbooks need updating. The number of described annelid species is about 20,000 (older figures range from 9,000 to 16,500). The giant hemoglobin of earthworms is sometimes confused with hemerythrin, which some translations call "earthworm hemoglobin"; hemerythrin does not occur in earthworms but in sipunculans, magelonids, brachiopods and priapulids (§15.1.4). Most East Asian species of *Pheretima* have been transferred to *Amynthas* and *Metaphire*. *Aeolosoma*, listed among the aquatic oligochaetes, is not a clitellate. The representative species of the Chinese textbooks (*Nereis*, *Pheretima*, *Hirudo*) and the other species named there (*Arenicola*, *Chaetopterus*, *Serpula*, *Spirorbis*, *Terebella*, *Glycera*, *Harmothoe*, *Syllis*, *Hermodice*, *Polydora*, *Tubifex*, *Limnodrilus*, *Branchiura*, *Eisenia*, *Drawida*, *Whitmania*, *Haemadipsa*, *Glossiphonia*, *Erpobdella*, *Urechis*, *Bonellia*, *Sipunculus* and the pogonophorans) are treated in the sections above.

## Summary

- Annelids (Definition 15.1.1) are segmented coelomate spiralians whose segments, added from a posterior growth zone between the prostomium-peristomium and the pygidium, each contain paired coelomic sacs, septa, nephridia, a ganglion and chaetae (Definition 15.1.2); polychaetes carry them on parapodia (Definition 15.1.3).
- The septate coelom is a hydrostatic skeleton of constant-volume compartments: $\mathrm{d}r/r = -\tfrac12\,\mathrm{d}l/l$ (Eq. (15.1)). Backward waves of anchoring (short, thick) segments produce forward peristaltic crawling; burrowing in mud also works by crack propagation.
- Circulation is closed, with a forward-pumping dorsal and a backward-flowing ventral vessel; pigments include giant extracellular hemoglobin, chlorocruorin and hemerythrin. Excretion is by metanephridia (micronephridia in *Amynthas*), protonephridia in some polychaetes; chloragogen tissue is a liver-like store and detoxifying tissue.
- The nervous system is a brain, connectives and a ventral nerve cord with segmental ganglia and giant fibres; polychaetes have eyes, nuchal organs and mushroom bodies.
- Polychaetes are mostly gonochoristic broadcast spawners with trochophore, metatrochophore and nectochaete larvae; many nereids, eunicids and syllids spawn as epitokes in moon-timed swarms (palolo, *Platynereis*).
- Phylogeny: Oweniida, Chaetopteridae and Amphinomida + Sipuncula branch first; the crown clade Pleistoannelida comprises Errantia (ragworms, scale worms, glycerids, syllids, eunicids) and Sedentaria (spionids, sabellids and serpulids, siboglinids, terebellids, lugworms, capitellids with echiurans, and clitellates). Polychaeta (Definition 15.2.1) is paraphyletic.
- Siboglinids lost the gut and live on chemosymbiotic bacteria in a trophosome (*Riftia*, *Osedax*); echiurans (*Urechis*, *Bonellia* with environmental sex determination) and sipunculans (*Sipunculus*) lost segmentation: segmentation can be lost, and its absence is not evidence of primitiveness.
- Clitellates (Definition 15.6.1) are hermaphrodites with a clitellum, cocoons and direct development; "Oligochaeta" (Definition 15.6.2) is paraphyletic. The earthworm (*Amynthas*/*Pheretima*) has its clitellum on segments 14–16, male pores on 18, calciferous glands, crop, gizzard and typhlosole; earthworms are ecosystem engineers that can turn over the topsoil within a century (Example 15.6.1) and invade forests that lack them.
- Leeches (Definition 15.7.1) have a fixed number of annulated segments, two suckers, a coelom reduced to channels and no chaetae; rhynchobdellids feed with a proboscis, jawed leeches (*Hirudo*) with three jaws and anticoagulant saliva (hirudin). The medicinal leech is used in microsurgery and is a model of identified neural circuits.

## Questions

1. Why are the prostomium and the pygidium not counted as segments? Where are new segments formed, and which segment is the oldest?

   **Answer** Segments are the units produced by the posterior growth zone, each with mesoderm derived from the teloblastic mesodermal bands, its own pair of coelomic sacs, ganglion, nephridia and chaetae. The prostomium develops from the episphere of the trochophore (in front of the prototroch) and the pygidium from the region around the larval anus; neither arises from the growth zone, and neither has coelomic sacs of the segmental kind. New segments are formed by the growth zone just in front of the pygidium, so the youngest segment is the last one before the pygidium and the oldest is the first segment behind the peristomium. In regeneration and in asexual budding the same zone is re-established at the cut end.
2. An earthworm segment shortens from 4 mm to 3 mm. By how much does its diameter change? Explain why peristalsis requires septa.

   **Answer** At constant volume, $r \propto l^{-1/2}$ (Eq. (15.1)), so $r_2/r_1 = (4/3)^{1/2} \approx 1.15$: the diameter increases by about 15%. Peristalsis requires that a contraction of the longitudinal muscles in one group of segments thickens those segments and presses them against the burrow, while other segments are lengthened by their circular muscles. If the coelom were continuous, coelomic fluid displaced by a contraction would flow to other parts of the body and the effect would not be local; the septa divide the fluid skeleton into independent compartments, so each segment changes shape according to its own muscles. Annelids that do not crawl by peristalsis often have incomplete septa.
3. Compare the respiratory pigments of annelids. What is unusual about the hemoglobin of *Riftia*?

   **Answer** Most annelids have giant extracellular hemoglobins (erythrocruorins) dissolved in the blood plasma, of very high molecular mass and high oxygen affinity; some, such as glycerids and echiurans, have intracellular hemoglobin in coelomocytes; sabellids, serpulids and flabelligerids have chlorocruorin, an iron-containing pigment with a modified heme that appears green in dilute solution; and magelonids and sipunculans have hemerythrin, a non-heme iron protein in coelomocytes. The hemoglobin of *Riftia* binds oxygen at its heme groups and hydrogen sulfide at separate sites (free cysteine residues and zinc ions), so it can transport both substrates of its sulfur-oxidizing symbionts in the same blood without the sulfide reacting with oxygen or poisoning the worm's cytochrome c oxidase.
4. Why is the traditional class Polychaeta no longer recognized as a natural group? What does the current classification of annelids look like?

   **Answer** Phylogenetic analyses show that the Clitellata (oligochaetes and leeches), as well as the sipunculans, echiurans and siboglinids, arose from within the polychaetes. A group that contains the common ancestor but excludes some of its descendants is paraphyletic; "Polychaeta" in the old sense is defined only by the absence of the clitellum and by ancestral features (parapodia, many chaetae, separate sexes, trochophore). The current tree has small basal lineages (Oweniida, then Chaetopteridae, then Amphinomida with Sipuncula) and a crown clade Pleistoannelida with two subclades: Errantia (mostly mobile forms with well-developed parapodia and jaws) and Sedentaria (mostly tube dwellers and burrowers), the latter including the siboglinids, the echiurans and the clitellates.
5. Echiurans and sipunculans were once taken as unsegmented ancestors or relatives of the annelids. What evidence shows that they are annelids that lost segmentation, and what general lesson follows?

   **Answer** Phylogenomic analyses place echiurans within the Sedentaria, close to the capitellids, and sipunculans as sister group of the amphinomids within the annelid tree; both are therefore nested among segmented annelids, and a single loss in each is more parsimonious than several independent gains of segmentation in the other annelids. Developmental evidence agrees: the ventral nerve cord of echiurans forms from serially repeated ganglion rudiments that fuse, and the larval nervous system of sipunculans shows a transient serial arrangement of neurons. Echiurans also retain annelid chaetae. The lesson is that complex characters can be lost, so the absence of a character in a group does not show that the group is primitive; character polarity must be inferred from the phylogeny.
6. Describe the formation of the cocoon in an earthworm. Why is fertilization delayed until after copulation?

   **Answer** During copulation two worms exchange sperm, which each stores in its spermathecae. Later each worm's clitellum secretes a mucous tube and a protein sheath filled with albumin; the worm moves backward out of it, so the tube passes forward over the body. As it passes the female pore (segment 14 in *Amynthas*) it receives eggs, and as it passes the spermathecal pores further forward it receives the partner's sperm, so eggs are fertilized inside the tube. When the tube slips off the head its ends close, forming the cocoon, in which the embryos develop directly on the albumin. Storing sperm and fertilizing later allows the eggs to be fertilized and enclosed in a protective case in one step, away from the dangers of desiccation, and lets a worm produce several cocoons over weeks from one copulation; it also ensures that eggs are fertilized by a partner's sperm, not by the worm's own.
7. How does the blood-feeding of a medicinal leech depend on its saliva, its gut and its symbionts?

   **Answer** The leech attaches with its anterior sucker and makes a Y-shaped cut with three toothed jaws. Its saliva contains anaesthetic substances, vasodilators, inhibitors of platelet aggregation and hirudin, a specific inhibitor of thrombin, so the bite is not felt and the blood keeps flowing during the long meal and afterwards. The leech can swallow several times its body weight; water and salts are removed rapidly through the nephridia, and the concentrated blood is stored in the caeca of the crop. Because leeches produce few digestive proteases, digestion is slow and depends on symbiotic bacteria (*Aeromonas veronii*), which also prevent the growth of other bacteria in the stored blood; a single meal can sustain the leech for many months.
