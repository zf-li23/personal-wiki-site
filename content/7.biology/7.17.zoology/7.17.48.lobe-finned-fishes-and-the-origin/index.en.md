The transition from water to land is one of the best-documented major transitions in the history of life. Every tetrapod (amphibian, reptile, bird and mammal) descends from a lobe-finned fish of the Devonian that had lungs, a fleshy fin with a single bone at its base and an internal nostril, and over about 20 million years the descendants of such fishes acquired limbs with digits, a neck, a strengthened vertebral column and new ways of feeding, breathing and hearing. The fossils that record this transition, many found since 1990, show that the change happened largely in water: limbs with digits evolved in animals that still had gills and lived in shallow, weedy waters, and were only later used for walking on land.

This chapter treats the lobe-finned fishes (Sarcopterygii), the second lineage of bony fishes ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)). It describes the living coelacanths and lungfishes (§48.1), then the tetrapodomorph fishes and the first tetrapods of the Devonian (§48.2), and the transformation of fins into limbs (§48.3). Section 48.4 asks what problems life on land posed and how they were solved, and §48.5 surveys the early tetrapods of the Carboniferous and Permian. The living amphibians follow in [7.17.49](/7.17.49), the amniotes in [7.17.50](/7.17.50). The developmental genetics of the limb is in [7.5.31](/7.5.31) and [7.5.32](/7.5.32); the comparative anatomy of girdles and limbs across tetrapods in [7.17.61](/7.17.61); the physiology of air breathing in [7.14.17](/7.14.17).

## 48.1. Lobe-Finned Fishes

### 48.1.1. Characters of Sarcopterygians

**Definition 48.1.1 (Sarcopterygii)** The **Sarcopterygii** (sarcopterygians, lobe-finned fishes) are the clade of osteichthyans comprising the coelacanths (Actinistia), the lungfishes (Dipnoi), the tetrapodomorphs and the tetrapods. Their paired fins (or limbs) have a fleshy lobe supported by an internal skeleton with a single proximal bone (the humerus or femur) articulating with the girdle and a branching series of distal bones, moved by muscles that extend into the fin; ancestrally they have two dorsal fins, cosmoid scales, teeth with true enamel, and an evaginated forebrain with paired hemispheres.

The paired fins are the key character. In a ray-finned fish, a row of short radials at the base of the fin carries the dermal fin rays, and the fin is moved by muscles in the body wall; in a lobe-finned fish the fin skeleton is a chain of bones extending out from the body along a main axis (the metapterygial axis), with side branches, and the fin has its own muscles. The pattern of one bone, then two, then many is already visible in the fins of fossil sarcopterygians and became the pattern of the tetrapod limb: **stylopod**, **zeugopod** and **autopod** ([7.5.31](/7.5.31)).

Early sarcopterygians were diverse in the Devonian and include some of the earliest bony fishes, several from the Early Devonian of Yunnan, China (*Psarolepis*, *Achoania*, *Youngolepis*, *Diabolepis*, *Styloichthys*). Today only eight species of sarcopterygian fishes survive: two coelacanths and six lungfishes. Their few survivors contrast with the 35 000 ray-finned fishes, but sarcopterygians as a whole, including the tetrapods, are as diverse as actinopterygians.

The early sarcopterygians of Yunnan were studied above all by Chang Mee-mann (Zhang Miman) and her students at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing. *Youngolepis* and *Diabolepis*, from the Early Devonian Xitun and Xujiachong formations of Qujing and Wuding, are stem lungfishes: *Diabolepis* already has a palate with fields of teeth that foreshadow the lungfish tooth plates, and Chang's serial grinding of the braincase of *Youngolepis* in the 1980s showed that it had no choana, which contradicted the view of Jarvik and others that the internal nostrils of lungfishes and tetrapods were homologous and that *Youngolepis*-like fishes linked them. *Psarolepis* and *Achoania* combine sarcopterygian and actinopterygian characters (a pectoral girdle with a long spine, cosmine-covered skull bones) and show that the earliest sarcopterygians resembled the earliest actinopterygians more closely than any living species does. *Styloichthys* has been placed close to the split between lungfishes and tetrapodomorphs. Together with the Silurian *Guiyu* ([7.17.47](/7.17.47#sec-47.1.2)) these fossils make southern China the most important region for the origin of the bony fishes.

The relationships of the three living lineages were debated for a century. Lungfishes have lungs, internal nostrils and a heart with a partially divided atrium, and nineteenth-century zoologists considered them the closest relatives of amphibians; after the discovery of the coelacanth, its fleshy fins and the resemblance of its fossil relatives to the tetrapodomorphs suggested the coelacanth instead. Molecular data, culminating in the coelacanth and lungfish genomes (Amemiya et al. 2013; Meyer et al. 2021), consistently place lungfishes as the **sister group** of tetrapods, with coelacanths branching first. The tetrapodomorph fishes, all extinct, are closer to tetrapods than lungfishes are.

### 48.1.2. Coelacanths

**Definition 48.1.2 (Coelacanth)** **Coelacanths** (Actinistia) are sarcopterygian fishes with a diphycercal tail bearing a small median epicaudal lobe (three-lobed tail), lobed paired fins and a lobed second dorsal and anal fin, an intracranial joint dividing the braincase into an anterior and a posterior part, a rostral organ in the snout, a persistent, fluid-filled notochord, and a lung reduced to a fat-filled vestige in the living species.

Coelacanths have a fossil record from the Devonian to the Late Cretaceous, about 410 to 66 million years ago, with more than 120 species, and they were believed to have gone extinct with the dinosaurs. On 22 December 1938 Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer, curator of the museum in East London, South Africa, found a strange blue fish 1.5 m long among the catch of a trawler near the mouth of the Chalumna River; the ichthyologist J. L. B. Smith recognized it as a coelacanth and named it *Latimeria chalumnae*. A second specimen was found only in 1952, in the Comoro Islands between Madagascar and Mozambique, where local fishermen had long caught it occasionally. In 1997 a second species, *L. menadoensis*, was discovered at a fish market in Sulawesi, Indonesia, 10 000 km away.

*Biology.* Coelacanths reach about 2 m and 90 kg. By day they rest in caves on steep volcanic slopes at depths of 150–250 m and at night drift slowly near the bottom to feed on fishes and cephalopods, often in a head-down posture. Divers in submersibles observed that they move their paired fins in a diagonal pattern, the left pectoral with the right pelvic, like the limbs of a walking tetrapod, though they swim rather than walk; they hold position with the paddle-like second dorsal and anal fins. The rostral organ, a gel-filled cavity with pores in the snout, is an electroreceptor. The intracranial joint allows the front of the skull to rotate upward when the mouth opens, increasing the gape; it was present in many early sarcopterygians and lost in lungfishes and tetrapods. Like chondrichthyans, coelacanths retain urea in their blood for osmoregulation ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.5.3)). They are viviparous: eggs as large as an orange develop in the oviduct, nourished mainly by their yolk, and females give birth to up to 26 young about 35 cm long. Recent ageing from scale rings suggests a lifespan of about 100 years, maturity at about 55 years and a gestation of about five years (Mahé et al. 2021), among the slowest life histories of any fish.

*Anatomy.* The skeleton of *Latimeria* is largely cartilaginous, with little ossification of the braincase and vertebrae; the notochord is a wide, fluid-filled tube under pressure that serves as the main axial support, and the vertebral column consists only of neural and haemal arches. The body is covered with large, overlapping cosmoid-derived scales with small denticles, and beneath the head lies a pair of gular plates. The braincase is unusual in that the brain occupies only about 1 % of the cranial cavity in adults, the rest filled with fat. The heart is a straight tube, without the folding seen in other vertebrates, a condition that may be primitive. The lung, in which fossil coelacanths had an ossified wall, persists in the embryo as a lung and becomes a vestigial, fat-filled organ beside the oesophagus in the adult; the fat may also contribute to buoyancy. Fossil coelacanths lived in fresh water as well as the sea and ranged in size from a few centimetres to more than 5 m (the Cretaceous *Mawsonia* of South America and Africa).

*A "living fossil".* The coelacanth is the classic **living fossil**: a living species that closely resembles fossils tens or hundreds of millions of years old. The term needs care. *Latimeria* is not identical to its Devonian relatives; it is a member of a group whose body plan changed slowly, and its genome evolves more slowly than those of most vertebrates, in its protein-coding genes as in the amount of transposon activity (Amemiya et al. 2013). It is not an ancestor of tetrapods but a distant cousin with its own long history and many specializations (deep-water life, the fat-filled lung, viviparity). Both species are threatened: the Comoros population numbers a few hundred adults.

### 48.1.3. Lungfishes

**Definition 48.1.3 (Lungfish)** **Lungfishes** (Dipnoi) are sarcopterygian fishes with lungs used for air breathing, crushing tooth plates on the palate and the lower jaw instead of marginal teeth, an upper jaw (palatoquadrate) fused to the braincase, a nostril system in which the excurrent nostril opens inside the mouth, a partially divided atrium of the heart with pulmonary veins, and, in living species, a diphycercal tail formed by the fusion of the dorsal, caudal and anal fins.

Lungfishes were abundant and diverse in Devonian seas, with many species with heavy skulls and cosmoid scales; living lungfishes are freshwater fishes of the southern continents.

| Genus and species | Distribution | Lungs and breathing | Paired fins | Aestivation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Neoceratodus forsteri* (Australian lungfish) | Mary and Burnett rivers, Queensland | One lung; mainly gill breathing, uses air when water is poor in oxygen | Broad, paddle-like, with a central axis and two rows of side branches | No |
| *Protopterus* (African lungfishes, four species: *P. annectens*, *P. aethiopicus*, *P. amphibius*, *P. dolloi*) | Tropical Africa | Paired lungs; obligate air breathers as adults; reduced gills | Long, thin, filamentous | Yes, in a mucous cocoon in the dried mud |
| *Lepidosiren paradoxa* (South American lungfish) | Amazon and Paraná basins | Paired lungs; obligate air breathers | Thin, short | Yes, in burrows, without a cocoon |

When their pools dry up, African lungfishes burrow into the mud, secrete a mucous cocoon that hardens around the body, leave a breathing tube to the surface and fall into a dormant state, aestivation. Their metabolic rate falls by up to 90 %, they stop eating, and they convert ammonia to less toxic urea, which accumulates in the body; they can survive in this state for months and, in the laboratory, for several years, until rain dissolves the cocoon. Their larvae have external gills, as amphibian larvae do. Lungfishes have the largest genomes of all animals: about 43 Gb in *Neoceratodus* and 40 Gb in *Protopterus annectens*, more than 13 times the human genome, mainly because of an expansion of transposable elements (Meyer et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2021). Fossil lungfishes are known from China, including Devonian forms from Yunnan and Mesozoic ceratodontids from Sichuan.

Because they are the closest living relatives of tetrapods, lungfishes are used to infer the ancestral state of characters at the origin of tetrapods. For example, lungfish fins express *Hoxd13* in a late phase at the tip of the fin, as limbs do in the autopod (§48.3), and the West African lungfish *P. annectens* walks on the bottom with its thin pelvic fins in an alternating gait, lifting its body clear of the substrate (King et al. 2011).

## 48.2. Tetrapodomorphs and the First Tetrapods

**Definition 48.2.1 (Tetrapodomorpha)** The **Tetrapodomorpha** (tetrapodomorphs) are the clade comprising the tetrapods and all sarcopterygians more closely related to them than to lungfishes. The extinct, non-tetrapod members, the "tetrapodomorph fishes" (stem tetrapods), share with tetrapods a **choana** (an internal nostril opening into the roof of the mouth), a humerus with a characteristic pattern of muscle attachments, an enlarged entepicondyle, and in more derived forms a flattened head with dorsal eyes.

### 48.2.1. The Origin of the Choana

Tetrapods breathe through their nostrils: air enters by the external nostril and passes through the nasal cavity to the choana, an internal opening in the roof of the mouth. Most fishes have two external nostrils on each side, an incurrent and an excurrent one, through which water flows past the olfactory epithelium. Where did the choana come from? The answer came from the Early Devonian of Yunnan. *Kenichthys campbelli*, a small tetrapodomorph fish about 395 million years old, has its posterior (excurrent) nostril at the margin of the upper jaw, between the premaxilla and the maxilla, halfway between its position on the face in other fishes and the choana in the palate (Zhu and Ahlberg 2004). The choana is therefore the posterior external nostril that migrated through the gap between the marginal jaw bones into the roof of the mouth. Lungfishes, whose excurrent nostril also opens inside the mouth, evolved this condition independently; their internal nostril is not homologous with the choana, as had long been argued.

### 48.2.2. Tetrapodomorph Fishes

The tetrapodomorph fishes of the Middle and Late Devonian document the transition step by step.

| Taxon | Age and locality | Characters |
|---|---|---|
| *Kenichthys* | About 395 Ma, Yunnan | Posterior nostril at the jaw margin |
| Osteolepiforms: *Osteolepis*, *Gogonasus*, *Eusthenopteron* | 390–380 Ma; *Eusthenopteron foordi* from Miguasha, Quebec (about 385 Ma) | Fusiform predators up to 1.5 m, with cosmoid or rhombic scales, two dorsal fins and a three-lobed tail; pectoral fin with humerus, radius, ulna and an ulnare continuing the axis; labyrinthine infolding of the dentine of the teeth (shared with early tetrapods); a large spiracle; well studied by Erik Jarvik over 50 years |
| Tristichopterids and rhizodonts | Middle and Late Devonian | Large predators; parallel radiations |
| Elpistostegalians: *Panderichthys* (Latvia, about 380 Ma), *Elpistostege* (Miguasha, about 380 Ma), *Tiktaalik* (Ellesmere Island, Canadian Arctic, about 375 Ma) | Late Devonian | Flattened, crocodile-like head with dorsal eyes and a long snout, no dorsal and anal fins, a reduced gill cover, and in *Tiktaalik* a neck, robust ribs and a wrist |

*Tiktaalik roseae* was discovered in 2004 by Edward Daeschler, Neil Shubin and Farish Jenkins, who searched deliberately in Late Devonian rocks of the right age and environment (river deltas) to find an intermediate between fishes like *Panderichthys* and early tetrapods like *Acanthostega*; the discovery is a classic example of a prediction from phylogeny and stratigraphy. *Tiktaalik* was up to 2.7 m long, with scales, fin rays and gills like a fish, but it had a flat skull with eyes on top, a large spiracular notch that may have been used for breathing air, no bony gill cover, so that the head was separated from the shoulder girdle and could move on a neck, and overlapping ribs that supported the trunk. Its pectoral fin had a humerus, radius and ulna, and distal elements that formed joints allowing the fin to bend at a "wrist" and prop the body up on the bottom, in a push-up posture; the pelvic girdle was large and the pelvic fin robust, so the hind fins also contributed to support (Shubin et al. 2014). Specimens of *Elpistostege* found in 2020 have distal radials arranged in rows that resemble the bones of digits, still embedded in a fin web with rays (Cloutier et al. 2020).

### 48.2.3. Devonian Tetrapods

**Definition 48.2.2 (Tetrapoda)** The **Tetrapoda** (tetrapods) are the clade of sarcopterygians with limbs bearing digits (an autopod with digits instead of fin rays); in the narrow sense used by many palaeontologists, the name is restricted to the crown group, the last common ancestor of living amphibians and amniotes and all its descendants, the limbed stem forms being called stem tetrapods or Tetrapoda in the broad sense.

The definition matters because the earliest animals with limbs and digits are not members of the crown group: they branched before the split between the ancestors of amphibians and those of amniotes. This chapter uses "tetrapods" in the broad, apomorphy-based sense (animals with digits) and says "crown tetrapods" when the distinction is needed.

The best-known Devonian tetrapods come from the Late Devonian of East Greenland, about 365 million years old.

- *Acanthostega gunnari*, about 60 cm long, had eight digits on each forelimb and hindlimb, a tail with a fin supported by fin rays, a lateral line, and grooved ceratobranchials that indicate internal gills. Its forelimbs could not bear its weight on land: the elbow could not be straightened, and the wrist bones were not ossified. Jennifer Clack and Michael Coates (1990) concluded that it lived in shallow water and used its limbs for paddling and clambering through vegetation, and that limbs with digits evolved in water, not as adaptations for walking on land.
- *Ichthyostega*, up to 1.5 m, had seven toes on the hindlimb (the forelimb hand is unknown), massive overlapping ribs and a powerful shoulder girdle and forelimbs. Its vertebral column was regionalized in an unusual way, and reconstructions suggest it moved on land like a seal or mudskipper, dragging the body with its forelimbs, rather than walking with alternating limbs (Pierce et al. 2012).
- *Tulerpeton* from Russia had six digits on the hand and foot; *Ventastega* from Latvia and *Elginerpeton* from Scotland are known from skulls and limb bones.
- *Sinostega pani* from the Late Devonian of Ningxia, China (Zhu et al. 2002), known from a lower jaw, showed that Devonian tetrapods were not restricted to the continents around the North Atlantic of that time.

The polydactyly of the Devonian tetrapods (six to eight digits) shows that pentadactyly, the five-digit limb (the pentadactyl limb of the Chinese textbooks), was not primitive for tetrapods. It became fixed in the ancestors of crown tetrapods during the Carboniferous, perhaps because the development of the autopod became more constrained once digits acquired separate identities.

### 48.2.4. Trackways Older than the Bones

In 2010 Grzegorz Niedźwiedzki and colleagues described trackways from Zachełmie in the Holy Cross Mountains of Poland, in marine tidal flat sediments of the early Middle Devonian, about 395 million years old. Some footprints show digits, and the tracks have the pattern of alternating limb movements of a quadruped with a trunk up to 2.5 m long. They are about 18 million years older than the oldest known tetrapod body fossils and about 10 million years older than *Tiktaalik* and *Panderichthys*.

![Ages of key fossils of the fish–tetrapod transition (approximate). The tetrapodomorph fish *Kenichthys* with its forming choana (Yunnan, about 395 Ma); the osteolepiforms *Eusthenopteron* and *Gogonasus* (about 385–380 Ma); the elpistostegalians *Panderichthys* and *Elpistostege* (about 380 Ma) and *Tiktaalik* (about 375 Ma); the limbed tetrapods *Acanthostega*, *Ichthyostega* and *Tulerpeton* (about 365 Ma) and *Sinostega* from Ningxia (about 360 Ma). The Zachełmie trackways (red), made by an animal with digits about 395 Ma, are older than all body fossils of elpistostegalians and tetrapods.](./plots/devonian-timeline.py)

The trackways do not overturn the sequence of character acquisition shown by the body fossils: the order in which characters evolved (choana, flattened head, loss of the gill cover, neck, wrist, digits) is read from the phylogeny, not from the ages of the fossils. They show that the stratigraphic ages of fossils are a poor guide to the times of origin. If the tracks were made by tetrapods, then the lineage of elpistostegalians and the lineage leading to tetrapods had already separated by 395 million years ago, and *Tiktaalik* and *Panderichthys* are late-surviving members of a group whose earlier members are not yet known (a ghost lineage, [7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.2)). They also suggest that tetrapods may have originated in marine tidal environments rather than in rivers and lakes. Some authors have questioned whether the Zachełmie prints are trackways of tetrapods or of fishes, but most accept them.

### 48.3.1. Homology of the Limb Bones

The bones of the stylopod and zeugopod are clearly homologous between tetrapodomorph fishes and tetrapods: the humerus of *Eusthenopteron* has the same joints and muscle attachments as the humerus of an amphibian, and the radius and ulna are recognizable by their positions and shapes. The question is the autopod. In sarcopterygian fins the main axis runs through the ulna and ulnare and continues into a series of axial elements, with radials branching from its preaxial side. In the classical interpretation, the digits of tetrapods are the distal radials of this axis, bent forward ("the digital arch"), and the fin rays were lost. An alternative view holds that the autopod is a new structure, a neomorph, added to the distal end of the fin.

Developmental genetics supports a partial version of the second view ([7.5.32](/7.5.32#sec-32.7.1)). In the tetrapod limb bud, *Hox* genes of groups 9–13 are expressed in two phases: an early phase that patterns the stylopod and zeugopod, and a late phase, controlled by enhancers in a regulatory landscape on the other side of the *HoxD* cluster, in which *Hoxa13* and *Hoxd13* are expressed in the autopod. The late phase is present in fish fins too, in the distal part of the fin bud of zebrafish, sharks and paddlefish, although the autopod-specific enhancers are less active. When the late-phase enhancers of fish are tested in mice, they drive expression in the proximal limb, not the digits. And in zebrafish, cells that express *hox13* genes form the dermal fin rays; deleting the *hox13* genes shortens the fin rays and increases the number of distal endochondral bones (Nakamura et al. 2016). The interpretation is that digits and fin rays share a developmental origin in the most distal part of the appendage: as the dermal fin rays were reduced in the elpistostegalians, the distal domain shifted from making rays to making endochondral bones, the digits. The digits are new in being a distinct, segmented series of endochondral elements, but they arise from an ancient distal compartment of the fin.

### 48.3.2. The Girdles

The pectoral girdle of fishes is attached to the back of the skull by a chain of dermal bones (the posttemporal, supracleithrum and cleithrum), and the endochondral scapulocoracoid on its inner side bears the fin. In tetrapods the dermal bones connecting the girdle to the skull were lost, beginning in *Tiktaalik*, which freed the head to move on a neck and allowed the forelimbs to bear weight independently of the head. The scapulocoracoid enlarged, and the dermal clavicle and interclavicle formed a ventral brace. The pelvic girdle of fishes is a small plate embedded in the body wall, not connected to the vertebral column; in tetrapods it enlarged into an ilium, ischium and pubis on each side, and the ilium became attached by a sacral rib to one (or more) sacral vertebrae, so that the hindlimbs transmit thrust to the vertebral column. This connection is a key innovation for walking with the hindlimbs, and in early tetrapods the hindlimbs became the main propulsive limbs. The comparison of girdles across tetrapods is in [7.17.61](/7.17.61).

## 48.4. The Problems of Life on Land

Life on land differs from life in water in almost every physical respect. The table lists the problems and how early tetrapods solved them; the following subsections explain the most important.

| Problem | In water | On land | Solutions in tetrapods |
|---|---|---|---|
| Support | Buoyancy carries the body | The full weight acts on the skeleton | Stronger vertebral column with interlocking zygapophyses; ribs supporting the trunk; limbs and girdles; sacrum |
| Locomotion | Lateral undulation against water | Friction against the ground; undulation alone is ineffective | Limbs with digits; alternating gait combined with lateral bending |
| Feeding | Suction of prey with water | Air is too thin for suction | Grasping and biting with the jaws; a mobile tongue on the hyoid apparatus; neck for head movements |
| Breathing | Gills; water holds little oxygen | Air holds 30 times more oxygen; gills collapse; CO₂ is hard to eliminate | Lungs ventilated by a buccal pump; CO₂ released through the skin; later rib ventilation |
| Hearing | Particle motion detected by the inner ear | Sound in air does not enter the body without a transformer | Hyomandibula became the stapes; tympanic middle ears evolved later and independently in several lineages |
| Vision | Short distances in turbid water | Light travels far; the cornea refracts strongly in air | Enlarged eyes with dorsal position; eyelids and lacrimal glands to keep the eye moist (in crown tetrapods) |
| Water and ions | Osmotic exchange through gills and skin | Desiccation by evaporation | Keratinized epidermis (in amniotes); behaviour; urea excretion |
| Lateral line | Detects water flow | Useless in air | Lost in terrestrial adults; kept in aquatic forms and larvae |

### 48.4.1. Support and Locomotion

In water a fish weighs almost nothing; on land the whole weight bears down on the body.

**Example 48.4.1 (Weight in Water and on Land)** A fish of mean density $\rho_{\mathrm{b}} = 1.05\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ in fresh water ($\rho_{\mathrm{w}} = 1.00$) without a gas bladder has a net weight in water of $(\rho_{\mathrm{b}} - \rho_{\mathrm{w}})/\rho_{\mathrm{b}} \approx 5\,\%$ of its weight in air. Leaving the water multiplies the load on its skeleton by $1/0.05 = 20$; with a partly filled lung, which makes it nearly neutrally buoyant, by much more. A 1 m tetrapodomorph weighing about 10 kg thus goes from a load of half a kilogram to ten kilograms. The skeleton must also resist bending: a body supported at its two girdles is a beam loaded by its own weight, and the bending moment at its middle grows with the square of its length. The vertebral column of fishes is a flexible chain of centra and arches designed for lateral bending; in tetrapods the vertebrae acquired interlocking articular processes (zygapophyses) that resist sagging and twisting, ribs became longer and stiffer, and the column was linked to the hindlimbs by the sacrum.

:::

The vertebrae of early tetrapods were built of several pieces: a wedge-shaped intercentrum in front, a pair of pleurocentra behind, and a neural arch above ([7.17.60](/7.17.60)). Different lineages enlarged different pieces: in temnospondyls the intercentrum became the main body of the vertebra, in amniotes the pleurocentrum. Early tetrapods moved with a sprawling posture, the humerus and femur held horizontally, combining lateral bending of the trunk with alternating movements of the limbs (a walking trot, the diagonal limbs moving together), as salamanders still do.

### 48.4.2. Feeding

Suction feeding, the main method of fishes ([7.17.47](/7.17.47#sec-47.3.2)), does not work in air, which is 800 times less dense than water. Terrestrial tetrapods must seize food with the jaws, move it with the tongue and swallow it. The skulls of early tetrapods show a shift from long, flat skulls with jaws adapted to suction and snapping to skulls with stronger jaw-closing muscles. A study of the skull sutures of *Tiktaalik* and early tetrapods, which reflect the loading of the skull, showed that the skulls of tetrapods became adapted to biting at the transition (Markey and Marshall 2007). The hyoid apparatus, which in fishes expands the mouth for suction and supports the gills, became the support of a muscular tongue.

### 48.4.3. Breathing Air

Lungs were present in the earliest osteichthyans ([7.17.47](/7.17.47#sec-47.1.1)), so air breathing did not need to be invented at the transition; but tetrapods had to breathe air alone, without gills. Fishes and amphibians ventilate their lungs with a **buccal pump**: the floor of the mouth is lowered to draw air in through the nostrils, the nostrils are closed, and the floor is raised to force the air into the lungs ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.3)). The choana allowed air to enter with the mouth closed. Amniotes later evolved aspiration breathing, in which the ribs (and in mammals the diaphragm) expand the thorax and draw air into the lungs ([7.17.50](/7.17.50)).

Eliminating carbon dioxide is harder than taking up oxygen. Air contains about 30 times more oxygen than the same volume of water, so an air breather needs to ventilate much less to obtain the same oxygen; but CO₂ is far more soluble in water than oxygen, and a water breather eliminates it easily through the gills, whereas an air breather with low ventilation retains it. Blood CO₂ pressure in fishes is typically 0.2–0.4 kPa, in air-breathing tetrapods 3–5 kPa, ten times higher, and the bicarbonate concentration of the blood rises to buffer it. Early tetrapods probably released much CO₂ through their skin, as modern amphibians do ([7.17.49](/7.17.49)). The internal gills of *Acanthostega* and the spiracles of elpistostegalians suggest that the early tetrapods breathed with both gills and lungs.

### 48.4.4. Hearing

The hyomandibula of fishes, the upper element of the hyoid arch, supports the jaw joint and the operculum. In tetrapods it was freed from both functions (the jaw articulated directly with the braincase, and the operculum was lost) and became the stapes, which spans the gap between the braincase and the side of the head near the spiracular notch. In early tetrapods such as *Acanthostega* the stapes was a massive bone that braced the braincase against the palate and was probably not used for hearing airborne sound. The tympanic ear, a membrane in an otic notch connected to a slender stapes, evolved independently in several lineages later in the Palaeozoic and Mesozoic: in the ancestors of frogs, in reptiles, and in mammals ([7.14.42](/7.14.42)). The fate of the hyomandibula, from jaw support to stapes, is followed through all vertebrates in [7.17.58](/7.17.58).

### 48.4.5. Vision

Malcolm MacIver and colleagues (2017) found that the eyes of tetrapodomorphs tripled in size before the origin of digits, in the elpistostegalians, and moved to the top of the head. In water, even when it is clear, light is absorbed and scattered over a few metres, so a larger eye gains little range; in air, an animal can see objects tens of metres away, and a larger eye increases the range and the number of objects visible by a large factor. MacIver suggested that elpistostegalians lived like crocodiles, with their eyes above the water surface, looking for prey on the shore, and that the advantage of seeing prey on land drove the evolution of limbs that could carry the animal there ("buena vista" hypothesis). The hypothesis is debated, but it links a sensory change to the ecological opportunity of land, where invertebrates had already lived for tens of millions of years.

### 48.4.6. Why Go onto Land?

Why did tetrapodomorphs leave the water? Several hypotheses have been proposed, and they are not exclusive.

1. *Drying pools.* Alfred Romer proposed in the 1950s that Devonian fishes living in seasonal pools in a semi-arid climate used their fins to crawl from a drying pool to another; limbs would have been favoured as a way of staying in water. The idea fits the red sandstones of many Devonian sites, but *Acanthostega* and *Tiktaalik* lived in wet, well-vegetated floodplains and deltas, and the Zachełmie trackways come from a marine tidal flat, so seasonal drought was not the general setting.
2. *Shallow, oxygen-poor water.* The Middle and Late Devonian saw the spread of forests (*Archaeopteris*) and of plants with deep roots, which increased the input of plant debris and nutrients into rivers and lakes; decay of organic matter in warm, shallow, densely vegetated water lowered its oxygen content. In such waters air breathing at the surface, a flattened head with dorsal eyes and nostrils, and fins strong enough to prop the body up and push through vegetation would have been advantageous, and limbs with digits evolved in this context (Clack 2012).
3. *Food and escape.* Arthropods had colonized land in the Silurian, and the margins of waters offered prey that fishes could not reach; at the same time the shallowest waters and the shore provided refuge from large predatory fishes (the rhizodonts and tristichopterids). The visual hypothesis (§48.4.5) is a version of this idea.
4. *Tidal flats.* The Zachełmie trackways and some later tetrapod sites suggest intertidal environments, where animals stranded at low tide would have been favoured if they could move across exposed sediment and breathe air; tidal pools offered abundant stranded prey. Mudskippers ([7.17.47](/7.17.47#sec-47.4.4)) are a living analogue.
5. *Warmth.* Basking in shallow water or on the shore raises body temperature and could speed digestion and the growth of eggs and juveniles, as in many living ectotherms.

What is clear is that the transition was gradual and that each step was useful in water: lungs and air breathing were inherited from early osteichthyans, the flattened head with dorsal eyes evolved in elpistostegalians living at the water surface, the neck and the wrist in animals that propped themselves up in shallow water, and limbs with digits in animals that still had gills. Terrestrial locomotion and life away from water came last, in the Carboniferous.

## 48.5. Early Tetrapods after the Devonian

The early Carboniferous (Tournaisian, 359–347 million years ago) was long known as "Romer's gap", an interval with almost no tetrapod fossils. Discoveries since the 2000s, especially in Scotland (*Pederpes* and the fossils of the TW:eed project; Clack et al. 2016), have filled it with a diverse fauna of small and large tetrapods, many with five digits, showing that tetrapods diversified rapidly after the end-Devonian extinctions. By the mid-Carboniferous, tetrapods had split into the two lineages that lead to the living groups: the stem of the amphibians and the stem of the amniotes. The traditional names for the extinct groups are:

| Group | Age | Characters | Relationships |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Temnospondyls* (*Eryops*, *Mastodonsaurus*, *Cacops*) | Early Carboniferous to Early Cretaceous | Flat, wide skulls with large palatal openings; four fingers in the hand; vertebrae with large intercentra; many aquatic or semi-aquatic predators, some terrestrial; up to 6 m | Most diverse early tetrapods; the dissorophoids among them are the most likely relatives of lissamphibians ([7.17.49](/7.17.49)) |
| Anthracosaurs and embolomeres (*Proterogyrinus*) | Carboniferous to Permian | Deep skulls, vertebrae with large pleurocentra | Traditionally on the amniote stem; position debated |
| Seymouriamorphs (*Seymouria*) and diadectomorphs (*Diadectes*) | Permian | Robust terrestrial tetrapods; diadectomorphs the earliest large herbivorous tetrapods | Close to amniotes ([7.17.50](/7.17.50)) |
| *Lepospondyls* (*Diplocaulus*, microsaurs, aïstopods) | Carboniferous to Permian | Small; spool-shaped centra formed directly in bone; some limbless, some with boomerang-shaped heads | Probably a mixture of groups; some may be related to amniotes, others to lissamphibians |

The diversification of tetrapods in the Carboniferous coincided with the coal forests: high atmospheric oxygen (perhaps up to 30 %), warm and humid tropical swamps and abundant arthropods, including giant dragonfly relatives and millipedes ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)). Some early tetrapods became secondarily aquatic again, with lateral lines on their skulls and long bodies (the colosteids, many temnospondyls), others small terrestrial insectivores (microsaurs), and some returned to an eel-like, limbless form (aïstopods). The first amniotes appear in the same forests around 318 million years ago ([7.17.50](/7.17.50)).

The Chinese textbooks call the early tetrapods "labyrinthodonts", for the folded dentine of their teeth, which they share with tetrapodomorph fishes, and divide the early amphibians into Apsidospondyli (with vertebrae formed from several pieces, including the temnospondyls) and Lepospondyli. "Labyrinthodontia" and Apsidospondyli are paraphyletic: they include the ancestors of both lissamphibians and amniotes, characterized by primitive characters.

## 48.6. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Coelacanths and lungfishes as two subclasses of Osteichthyes, sometimes with the extinct "Crossopterygii" (fringe-finned fishes: osteolepiforms and coelacanths) | Sarcopterygii: coelacanths, lungfishes, tetrapodomorphs and tetrapods; "Crossopterygii" is paraphyletic and not used |

The textbooks' account of the differences between water and land (density, oxygen content, temperature variation, light, sound) and the problems tetrapods had to solve (support and locomotion, breathing air, avoiding desiccation, sensing in air, reproduction away from water) agrees with §48.4. Their characterization of the coelacanth *Latimeria chalumnae* (persistent notochord, gular plate, three-lobed tail, fat-filled swim bladder, spiral valve, viviparity) and of the lungfish families is included in §48.1, with the updates in the table.

## Summary

- Sarcopterygians (Definition 48.1.1) have lobed fins with a single proximal bone and a branching skeleton, the basis of the tetrapod limb. Eight living species of fishes: two coelacanths and six lungfishes; lungfishes are the sister group of tetrapods.
- Coelacanths (Definition 48.1.2): *Latimeria* discovered in 1938 (Comoros, Sulawesi), intracranial joint, rostral electroreceptor, fat-filled lung, viviparity and very slow life history; a "living fossil" with a slowly evolving genome but its own specializations.
- Lungfishes (Definition 48.1.3): air-breathing with lungs, tooth plates, aestivation in cocoons (*Protopterus*), the largest animal genomes.
- Tetrapodomorphs (Definition 48.2.1): the choana arose from the posterior nostril (*Kenichthys*, Yunnan); osteolepiforms (*Eusthenopteron*), elpistostegalians (*Panderichthys*, *Elpistostege*, *Tiktaalik* with neck, ribs and wrist).
- Tetrapods (Definition 48.2.2): Devonian *Acanthostega* (eight digits, internal gills, aquatic), *Ichthyostega*, *Tulerpeton*, *Sinostega*; limbs with digits evolved in water. The Zachełmie trackways, 395 million years old, predate the body fossils.
- Fin to limb: stylopod and zeugopod homologous with fish fin bones; the autopod arose from the distal compartment that forms fin rays in fishes (*Hox13* late phase); the pectoral girdle separated from the skull, the pelvic girdle joined the sacrum.
- Problems of land: support (Example 48.4.1), locomotion, biting instead of suction, buccal-pump lung ventilation and CO₂ elimination, the hyomandibula becoming the stapes, enlarged eyes for vision in air, water loss.
- After the Devonian: temnospondyls, anthracosaurs, seymouriamorphs, lepospondyls; "labyrinthodonts" are paraphyletic.

## Questions

1. Lungfishes are the closest living relatives of tetrapods, yet tetrapods are not descended from lungfishes. Explain.

   **Answer** "Closest living relative" means that lungfishes and tetrapods share a more recent common ancestor than either does with coelacanths or ray-finned fishes; it says nothing about either descending from the other. The common ancestor of lungfishes and tetrapods lived in the Early Devonian and was neither a lungfish nor a tetrapod. After the split, the lungfish lineage evolved its own specializations (tooth plates, fused palatoquadrate, reduced bones), while the tetrapod lineage passed through tetrapodomorph fishes such as *Eusthenopteron* and *Tiktaalik*. Those fishes are on the tetrapod side of the split and are therefore more closely related to tetrapods than lungfishes are, but none of them survives.
2. What does *Kenichthys* show about the origin of the choana, and why does this matter for the old argument that tetrapods are related to lungfishes?

   **Answer** In *Kenichthys* the posterior external nostril lies at the edge of the mouth, between the premaxilla and maxilla, intermediate between the facial position of the excurrent nostril in other fishes and the palatal position of the choana in tetrapods. It shows that the choana is the posterior external nostril that migrated into the mouth through the gap between the marginal jaw bones. Lungfishes also have an excurrent nostril inside the mouth, which was once taken as evidence that they are close to tetrapods; *Kenichthys* shows that the condition arose separately in the tetrapodomorph lineage, so the internal nostril of lungfishes is convergent and not evidence of a special relationship. The sister-group relationship of lungfishes and tetrapods rests on other evidence (molecular data and other characters).
3. Why is *Acanthostega* important for understanding the origin of limbs?

   **Answer** *Acanthostega* had limbs with eight digits but also internal gills (grooved gill arches), a tail fin with fin rays, a lateral line, and forelimbs that could not support the body on land (unossified wrist, elbow that could not straighten). It therefore lived in water and used its limbs to move through shallow, vegetated water. This showed that limbs with digits evolved in an aquatic animal and were only later used for walking on land: limbs were an exaptation for terrestrial locomotion, not an adaptation that arose for it.
4. Explain, with numbers, why the transition to land created a problem with carbon dioxide rather than with oxygen.

   **Answer** Air contains about 210 mL of oxygen per litre, air-saturated water at 20 °C about 6–7 mL, roughly 30 times less. An air breather therefore obtains its oxygen with much less ventilation than a water breather, and moving air over a lung is cheap. But CO₂ is very soluble in water, so a fish ventilating large volumes of water through its gills removes CO₂ easily and keeps its blood CO₂ pressure low (0.2–0.4 kPa). An animal ventilating its lungs only enough for oxygen removes CO₂ less effectively, and its blood CO₂ pressure rises roughly tenfold (3–5 kPa), shifting the acid–base balance; the blood bicarbonate must rise to compensate. Early tetrapods likely solved this partly by excreting CO₂ through moist skin, as amphibians do.
5. The Zachełmie trackways are older than *Tiktaalik*. Does this mean that *Tiktaalik* cannot be a transitional form?

   **Answer** No. *Tiktaalik* is transitional in its characters: it combines fish characters (fin rays, gills, scales) with tetrapod characters (neck, wrist, flat head, ribs), and its position on the tree between *Panderichthys* and the limbed tetrapods is unaffected by its age. A transitional fossil need not be an ancestor; it is a member of a side branch that preserves an intermediate combination of characters. The trackways imply that the lineage leading to tetrapods with digits existed by 395 million years ago, so the elpistostegalian lineage must be at least that old, and *Tiktaalik* is a late member of it. They reveal a ghost lineage and indicate that the transition began earlier, possibly in marine tidal settings, but the order of character acquisition is still read from the phylogeny.
6. Describe how the pectoral and pelvic girdles changed between tetrapodomorph fishes and early tetrapods, and explain the functional significance of each change.

   **Answer** Pectoral girdle: the dermal bones connecting it to the skull (posttemporal, supracleithrum, extrascapulars) were lost, beginning with *Tiktaalik*; the head became free to move on a neck, and the shock of the forelimbs striking the ground was no longer transmitted to the skull. The endochondral scapulocoracoid enlarged for limb muscles, and the clavicles and interclavicle formed a ventral brace. Pelvic girdle: in fishes it is a small plate in the body wall; in tetrapods it became a large structure of ilium, ischium and pubis, and the ilium was attached by a sacral rib to the vertebral column. The hindlimbs could then push the body forward and transmit their thrust to the axial skeleton, making them the main propulsive limbs.
7. (Open) The "buena vista" hypothesis proposes that improved vision in air favoured the evolution of limbs. What evidence would test it, and how could it be distinguished from the hypothesis that limbs evolved for moving in shallow, vegetated water?

   **Answer** Evidence for the vision hypothesis: the increase in orbit size and the dorsal position of the eyes should precede, on the phylogeny, the origin of weight-bearing limbs and digits (MacIver et al. found the orbit enlargement in elpistostegalians); the environments of elpistostegalians should include shallow water with exposed shores and terrestrial prey (arthropods); the visual range advantage can be modelled from eye size and the optical properties of air and water. Evidence for the aquatic-locomotion hypothesis: the first limbs with digits should show adaptations for pushing against substrates and vegetation under water, as in *Acanthostega* (internal gills, non-weight-bearing joints), and their environments should be vegetated shallow waters. The hypotheses are not exclusive: limbs may have evolved for moving in shallow water, and vision in air may have made excursions onto land profitable. Testing would require dated phylogenies with eye size, limb robustness and habitat data mapped on the same tree, and biomechanical analysis of the earliest limbs.
