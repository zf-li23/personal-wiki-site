The *Antliophora* are the holometabolous clade of scorpionflies (**Mecoptera**), fleas (**Siphonaptera**) and true flies (**Diptera**). Their males share a *sperm pump*, a muscular, sclerotized chamber of the ejaculatory duct that drives sperm into the female during copulation, and many details of the mouthparts and larval head. They form, with caddisflies and moths ([7.17.36](/7.17.36)), the Mecopterida ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)). The three orders differ enormously in diversity: about 600–800 species of scorpionflies, a little over 2500 fleas, and about 160,000 described flies, perhaps a million in all. Flies are among the most important insects for people: they include the vectors of malaria, dengue, sleeping sickness, leishmaniasis and river blindness, the decomposers of carrion and dung, the second most important pollinators after bees, the parasitoids of many pests, and the fruit fly *Drosophila melanogaster*, the best-studied animal in biology.

This chapter treats the three orders in order of their branching: the scorpionflies, a small, old order from which fleas probably arose; the fleas, the most specialized ectoparasites among insects; and the flies. Mouthpart types are defined in [7.17.23](/7.17.23), **halteres** in [7.17.24](/7.17.24) and their role in flight control in [7.17.25](/7.17.25); the medical importance of flies and fleas is summarized in [7.17.40](/7.17.40).

## 35.1. Mecoptera: Scorpionflies and Hangingflies

### 35.1.1. Characters

**Definition 35.1.1 (Mecoptera)** The **Mecoptera** (scorpionflies, hangingflies and relatives) are holometabolous insects with the head prolonged ventrally into a rostrum formed by the clypeus, genae, maxillae and labium, with small chewing mandibles at its tip; long filiform antennae; usually two pairs of similar, narrow membranous wings with many veins; five-segmented tarsi; short cerci; and, in most families, males with bulbous genitalia; larvae usually eruciform, often with compound eyes, and the pupa decticous and exarate; about 600–800 species in nine families.

Scorpionflies are medium-sized insects of moist, shaded forest, most diverse in the temperate Northern Hemisphere; China, with several hundred species of *Panorpa*, *Neopanorpa* and *Dicerapanorpa*, is a centre of their diversity. The order was much more diverse in the Permian and Mesozoic, and Jurassic and Cretaceous mecopterans from northeastern China include species with long siphonate mouthparts, probably feeding on pollination drops of gymnosperms before the rise of flowering plants (Ren and coauthors 2009).

Mecopteran larvae are unusual among holometabolous insects in having compound eyes, each composed of several ommatidia, instead of simple stemmata (in Panorpidae and Bittacidae); they live in soil, litter or moss and feed on dead insects or plant material, and eruciform larvae of Panorpidae bear abdominal prolegs. The pupae are decticous and exarate, with functional mandibles, as in Neuropterida.

### 35.1.2. Families and Mating Systems

| Family | Characters | Biology and examples |
|---|---|---|
| Panorpidae (common scorpionflies; about 480 species) | Wings spotted or banded; males with the genital bulb curled upward over the back like a scorpion's sting; the female abdomen tapering | Scavengers on dead insects, including those in spider webs; *Panorpa*, *Neopanorpa* |
| Bittacidae (hangingflies; about 270) | Long, slender legs; tarsi raptorial, the last tarsomere folding back on the fourth like a pocket knife; hang from vegetation by the forelegs | Predators: catch flying insects with the hind tarsi |
| Boreidae (snow scorpionflies; about 30) | Small, dark; wings vestigial (bristle-like hooks in males); females with an ovipositor | Active on snow in winter; adults and larvae feed on mosses |
| Meropeidae, Choristidae, Apteropanorpidae, Panorpodidae, Eomeropidae | Small relict families | Scattered, often disjunct distributions |
| Nannochoristidae | Small, southern (Australia, New Zealand, Chile) | Larvae aquatic, elongate, predatory; possibly the sister group of fleas |

The mating systems of scorpionflies are a classic system for the study of sexual selection, described by R. Thornhill in the 1970s and 1980s. In the hangingfly *Hylobittacus apicalis*, a male captures an insect, hangs from vegetation and releases a pheromone from abdominal vesicles; a female approaching him inspects the nuptial gift and copulates only if it is large enough, feeding on it during copulation. Copulation lasts up to about 20 minutes with a large gift; if the female finishes eating within about five minutes she breaks off, before the male has transferred sperm, and males whose gifts allow copulations of more than 20 minutes gain little more. The size of the gift thus determines how many eggs the male fertilizes, and males steal gifts from other males by imitating females. Males of *Panorpa* offer either a dead insect or a mass of hardened saliva produced from enlarged salivary glands; males without either attempt forced copulation, holding the female with a clamp (notal organ) on the abdomen. The behaviour of nuptial feeding is treated more generally in [7.17.28](/7.17.28).

## 35.2. Siphonaptera: Fleas

### 35.2.1. Characters

**Definition 35.2.1 (Siphonaptera)** The **Siphonaptera** (fleas) are wingless holometabolous insects, 1–8 mm long, with a laterally compressed, heavily sclerotized body bearing backward-directed setae and often combs (ctenidia) of flat spines on the head and pronotum; short, clubbed antennae lying in grooves on the sides of the head; compound eyes reduced or absent; piercing-sucking mouthparts formed by the unpaired epipharynx and the paired laciniae of the maxillae, sheathed by the labial palps; long legs, the hind legs adapted for jumping; a sensory plate (sensilium) on abdominal tergite 10; and legless larvae with a head capsule and chewing mouthparts; all adults are blood-feeding ectoparasites of mammals or birds; about 2600 species.

Every character of the flea fits life in the fur or feathers of a host. The flattened body slides between hairs; the backward-pointing setae and combs anchor it against the host's grooming, and the shape and spacing of the combs match the diameter and spacing of the hairs of its usual hosts. Eyes are reduced, and the antennae, folded into grooves, are raised in males to clasp the female during mating. The sensilium, a plate of long trichobothria on the tenth tergite, detects air currents and perhaps the approach of a host. About 94% of species live on mammals, especially rodents, and 6% on birds; most are associated more with the nest or burrow of the host than with its body, and many species can feed on several host species.

The mouthparts differ from those of all other blood-feeding insects: there are no mandibles. The epipharynx, a single median stylet, and the two serrated laciniae form a fascicle; the laciniae cut through the skin, and blood is drawn up a food canal between them and the epipharynx by the cibarial and pharyngeal pumps, while saliva flows down a separate channel. The labial palps form a sheath that does not enter the skin.

### 35.2.2. The Jump

A cat flea can jump about 20 cm horizontally, a hundred times its body length; the rabbit flea *Spilopsyllus cuniculi* accelerates at about 1350 m s⁻² and leaves the ground at about 1 m s⁻¹, in less than a millisecond (Bennet-Clark and Lucey 1967). No muscle can contract that fast. For a take-off at constant acceleration to speed $v$ in time $t$, the kinetic energy per unit body mass is $v^2/2$, so the mean power is $v^2/(2t)$ and the peak power $v^2/t$; for the flea this is about 1300 W kg⁻¹, while insect muscle delivers at most a few hundred watts per kilogram of muscle, and the jumping muscles are only a fraction of body mass.

![Why fleas need a catapult. Peak power per unit body mass needed to reach take-off speed v within time t, $v^2/t$, for three take-off speeds, compared with the range that muscles acting directly can deliver (shaded; about 10–100 W per kilogram of body mass). The rabbit flea (point; take-off at about 1 m/s in about 0.75 ms; Bennet-Clark and Lucey 1967) needs over ten times more than muscle could provide. The deficit is removed by storing energy slowly in an elastic pad of resilin and releasing it suddenly by a catch.](./plots/flea-jump.py)

The solution is a catapult. Before the jump, the trochanteral depressor muscle of the hind leg contracts slowly over about 0.1 s and compresses a pad of the rubber-like protein resilin in the pleural arch of the thorax, the remnant of the wing hinge of a winged ancestor; a catch holds the leg cocked. Releasing the catch returns the stored energy within about a millisecond, and the force is transmitted through the leg to the ground by the tibia and tarsus (Sutton and Burrows 2011). The time over which power is delivered is thus decoupled from the time of muscle contraction, an example of power amplification found also in froghoppers ([7.17.32](/7.17.32)), click beetles ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)) and the mandibles of trap-jaw ants.

### 35.2.3. Life Cycle

Flea eggs are laid on the host and fall off into the nest or bedding, where the legless, bristly larvae feed on organic debris and, essentially, on the dried blood excreted by the adults. The larvae pass through three instars and spin a silken cocoon incorporating sand and debris. The adult develops inside but may remain there, as a pharate adult in the cocoon, for months until vibration, warmth or carbon dioxide signals the presence of a host; this explains why a long-empty house can produce a sudden swarm of hungry fleas when it is reoccupied. Rabbit fleas synchronize their reproduction with that of the host: their ovaries mature only after the flea feeds on a pregnant doe, under the influence of her corticosteroids, and the fleas move to the newborn young.

### 35.2.4. Fleas and Disease

| Species | Hosts | Importance |
|---|---|---|
| *Xenopsylla cheopis* (oriental rat flea, Pulicidae) | Rats, people | The main vector of plague (*Yersinia pestis*) and of murine typhus (*Rickettsia typhi*) |
| *Pulex irritans* (human flea) | People, pigs, carnivores | Formerly common in dwellings; possible vector of plague between people |
| *Ctenocephalides felis* (cat flea) | Cats, dogs, many mammals | The most common flea on pets worldwide; vector of *Rickettsia felis* and of *Bartonella henselae* (cat-scratch disease, transmitted in flea feces); intermediate host of the dog tapeworm *Dipylidium caninum* |
| *Ctenocephalides canis* (dog flea) | Dogs | Similar to the cat flea |
| *Tunga penetrans* (chigoe, jigger) | People, pigs | Females burrow into skin, mostly of the feet, and swell to the size of a pea (tungiasis) |
| *Oropsylla silantiewi* and other Ceratophyllidae | Marmots and ground squirrels | Vectors in the natural plague foci of central Asia, including the Tibetan Plateau |

Plague is transmitted by "blocked" fleas, a mechanism discovered by A. W. Bacot and C. J. Martin in 1914. *Yersinia pestis* forms a biofilm in the proventriculus, the spined valve at the end of the flea's foregut, and gradually blocks it. The starving flea feeds repeatedly; blood cannot pass the block, and as the flea stops pumping it flows back, with bacteria, into the bite. Plague remains endemic in wild rodents in many regions; China has natural foci in marmots, gerbils and ground squirrels, and the third plague pandemic, which began in Yunnan in the mid-19th century and spread from Hong Kong in 1894, is the one during which A. Yersin isolated the bacterium.

Fossils from the Middle Jurassic and Early Cretaceous of northeastern China include giant, flattened insects with long siphons and claws (*Pseudopulex*, *Saurophthirus*), interpreted as ectoparasites of feathered dinosaurs or pterosaurs and as stem-group fleas (Gao and coauthors 2012; Huang and coauthors 2012), although whether they belong to the flea lineage is debated.

## 35.3. Diptera: True Flies

### 35.3.1. Characters

**Definition 35.3.1 (Diptera)** The **Diptera** (true flies) are holometabolous insects with one pair of membranous wings on the mesothorax and the hind wings transformed into **halteres**; a greatly enlarged mesothorax containing the flight muscles, the pro- and metathorax reduced; a large, mobile head with large compound eyes; mouthparts adapted for sucking liquids, the labium usually forming a proboscis with terminal labella, and mandibles present only in blood-feeding females of some families; no true ovipositor (often a telescopic tip of the abdomen); and legless larvae, with a well-developed, reduced or no head capsule; about 160,000 described species.

The halteres, small club-shaped organs that beat in antiphase with the wings, are gyroscopic sensors: they detect rotations of the body by the Coriolis forces acting on their knobs and provide flight stabilization within a few milliseconds ([7.17.25](/7.17.25)). A consequence of the two-winged design is that the whole flight motor is in the mesothorax, which in higher flies dominates the body. Flies are the fastest and most manoeuvrable fliers among insects; their asynchronous flight muscles beat at up to about 1000 Hz in some midges.

The wing venation is the most important character after antennae for identifying families. In the Comstock–Needham system ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)) a fly wing has the costa running all round the wing or ending near the tip; the subcosta; three branches of the radius (R1, R2+3 and R4+5); two or three branches of the media; the anterior cubitus; and one anal vein. A closed discal cell is present in most families. In many acalyptrate flies the costa has one or two breaks near the base, where the wing bends; the posterior basal part of the wing forms a lobe (alula), and, in calyptrate flies, two further lobes, the calypters (squamae), which cover the halteres.

The head is also diagnostic. Males of many families are holoptic (the compound eyes meet on top of the head), females dichoptic. In the Schizophora the adult escapes from the puparium by pushing off its front end with the *ptilinum*, a sac everted from the head by blood pressure; when the ptilinum is withdrawn, a horseshoe-shaped ptilinal suture remains above the antennae, the diagnostic character of the clade.

### 35.3.2. Larvae and Pupae

Fly larvae are legless, although many have prolegs, creeping welts or pseudopods, and they occupy every moist habitat: water, mud, decaying plant and animal matter, dung, living plants and the bodies of other animals. The head shows a progressive reduction:

| Larval head | Characters | Groups |
|---|---|---|
| Eucephalic | A complete, sclerotized head capsule with mandibles moving sideways | Most of the nematoceran grade (mosquitoes, midges, crane flies, fungus gnats) |
| Hemicephalic | Head capsule reduced and partly retracted into the thorax; mandibles moving vertically | Lower Brachycera (horse flies, soldier flies, robber flies) |
| Acephalic (maggot) | No external head capsule; an internal cephalopharyngeal skeleton bearing a pair of mouthhooks that move vertically, used to rasp food that is liquefied by saliva and taken up | Cyclorrhapha (house flies, blow flies, fruit flies) |

Chinese textbooks describe the mouthparts of fly maggots as a distinct "rasping" type. Pupae are obtect in the nematoceran grade and lower Brachycera; mosquito pupae are mobile and swim actively with a pair of paddles ("tumblers"). In the Cyclorrhapha the last larval cuticle is not shed but hardens into a barrel-shaped case, the *puparium*, inside which the larva molts to a pupa: the **coarctate pupa** of [7.17.29](/7.17.29). The adult of Cyclorrhapha leaves the puparium through a circular opening at its front end, while lower Brachycera emerge through a T-shaped split, the basis of the old division into Cyclorrhapha and "Orthorrhapha".

### 35.3.3. Classification and Antennae

Traditionally Diptera were divided into two suborders, the Nematocera, with long antennae of many similar segments (mosquitoes, midges, crane flies), and the Brachycera, with short antennae (all others). The Brachycera are monophyletic, but the Nematocera are a grade: molecular data place one nematoceran lineage, the Bibionomorpha (March flies, fungus gnats and gall midges), as the sister group of Brachycera (Wiegmann and coauthors 2011). Within the Brachycera the "lower Brachycera" (horse flies, soldier flies, robber flies, bee flies, dance flies) form a grade leading to the Cyclorrhapha, which in turn comprise the Aschiza (hover flies, scuttle flies and others without a ptilinum, also a grade) and the Schizophora; the Schizophora are divided into acalyptrate flies (without calypters: fruit flies, vinegar flies, leaf miners and many small families) and the Calyptratae (house flies, blow flies, flesh flies, tachinids, bot flies and the louse flies and tsetse).

![Antennae of Diptera (schematic; scape, pedicel and flagellum coloured). The many-segmented antennae of the nematoceran grade (crane flies, with filiform antennae; male mosquitoes, with plumose antennae bearing whorls of long setae; March flies, with short bead-like antennae) contrast with the short antennae of Brachycera, whose flagellum is reduced to a compact first flagellomere with an annulated tip (horse flies), a terminal stylus (robber flies) or a dorsal bristle, the arista (Cyclorrhapha, such as house flies).](./plots/fly-antennae.py)

In the Brachycera the flagellum consists of a large first flagellomere and a terminal stylus or arista of a few small segments; in Cyclorrhapha the arista arises dorsally from the first flagellomere and is feathered (plumose) in many families, including *Drosophila*. The antenna of flies is also a hearing organ: male mosquitoes detect the wing tone of females, about 400–600 Hz, with their plumose antennae and **Johnston's organ** ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)), and the arista of *Drosophila* vibrates in the near field of the male's courtship song.

### 35.3.4. Mouthparts and Blood Feeding

The ancestral fly mouthparts were probably those of a blood or nectar feeder with blade-like mandibles and maxillae. From this ground plan two very different types evolved.

In the **sponging mouthparts** of higher flies ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)), mandibles and maxillary stylets are lost; the proboscis consists of a basal rostrum, a median haustellum (labium) and the paired labella, whose underside is crossed by channels (pseudotracheae) through which liquids are drawn by capillarity and the cibarial pump. House flies dissolve solid food with regurgitated crop contents and saliva before taking it up, which makes them efficient mechanical vectors of enteric pathogens. In blood-feeding Calyptratae (stable flies *Stomoxys*, horn flies, tsetse, louse flies), both sexes pierce skin with the labium itself, whose labella bear rasping teeth: a secondary piercing mouthpart derived from the sponging type.

In the **piercing-sucking mouthparts** of blood-feeding nematocerans and horse flies, the females retain the ancestral stylets. In mosquitoes the fascicle consists of six stylets enclosed by the grooved labium.

![The proboscis of a female mosquito in cross-section (schematic). Six stylets form the fascicle that enters the skin: the labrum, rolled into the food canal through which blood is drawn by the cibarial and pharyngeal pumps; two thin mandibles; two maxillae (laciniae) with toothed tips that cut and anchor; and the hypopharynx, carrying the salivary canal through which anticoagulants and vasodilators are injected. The labium is a grooved sheath that bends back as the stylets enter the skin; its tip, the labella, guides the fascicle. Colours follow the mouthpart homology used in this course: labrum yellow, mandibles orange, maxillae blue, labium green.](./plots/mosquito-fascicle.py)

A probing mosquito saws the maxillae alternately into the skin, bends the fascicle in the tissue in search of a small blood vessel, and then sucks blood directly from it (vessel feeding), injecting saliva with anticoagulants, an apyrase that breaks down the ADP released by platelets, and vasodilators. Horse flies, black flies, biting midges and sand flies have broader, blade-like mandibles that slash the skin; they feed from the pool of blood that forms (pool feeding). Blood feeding has evolved several times within Diptera and in most families is limited to females, which need protein for egg production; males feed on nectar.

## 35.4. Families of the Nematoceran Grade

### 35.4.1. Mosquitoes

Mosquitoes (Culicidae; about 3700 species) are slender flies with a long proboscis, scales on the wing veins and the wing margin, and long legs. They have two subfamilies, the Anophelinae and the Culicinae (with *Culex*, *Aedes* and many other genera). Females of most species need a blood meal to mature each batch of eggs; they find hosts by the carbon dioxide plume, which activates host-seeking, then by skin odours (lactic acid, ammonia, carboxylic acids), heat and vision. Larvae ("wrigglers") live in standing water, from ponds and rice fields to tree holes, leaf axils and discarded tyres; most filter or browse food particles with mouth brushes and breathe air at the surface. The four larval instars are followed by a mobile, comma-shaped pupa that breathes through two respiratory trumpets on the thorax.

Mating takes place in swarms that form at dusk over landmarks such as bushes or a pale patch of ground. The males detect the wing tone of a female entering the swarm with their plumose antennae; both sexes then adjust their wingbeat frequencies so that a harmonic of the male's tone converges with one of the female's (harmonic convergence), a sign of mutual recognition in *Aedes* and *Culex*. Females usually mate once and store enough sperm for life, which is why the release of sterile or incompatible males can suppress a population.

The three genera of greatest medical importance differ at every stage, and these differences are used to identify them and to target their control:

| Stage | *Culex* | *Aedes* | *Anopheles* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egg | Laid together in floating rafts on the water surface | Laid singly on damp surfaces just above the water line; resist drying for months and hatch when flooded | Laid singly on the water, each with a pair of lateral floats |
| Larva | Hangs at an angle from the surface by a long respiratory siphon | Hangs at an angle; siphon short and stout | Lies parallel to the surface; no siphon, spiracles on a dorsal plate; palmate float hairs |
| Pupa | Respiratory trumpets long and narrow | Trumpets short | Trumpets short, broad, funnel-shaped |
| Adult at rest | Body parallel to the surface, abdomen not raised | Body parallel to the surface; black with white bands and lines (*Ae. aegypti*, *Ae. albopictus*) | Body and proboscis in a straight line, at an angle to the surface; palps as long as the proboscis in both sexes; wings often spotted |
| Biting | Mostly at night | Mostly by day (*Ae. aegypti*, *Ae. albopictus*) | At night and at dusk |
| Main diseases | West Nile virus, Japanese encephalitis (*Cx. tritaeniorhynchus*), lymphatic filariasis (*Cx. pipiens pallens*, *Cx. quinquefasciatus*) | Dengue, Zika, chikungunya, yellow fever | Malaria (*Plasmodium*; only anophelines transmit human malaria) |

China was certified malaria-free by the World Health Organization in 2021, after reducing about 30 million cases a year in the 1940s to none; the main vectors had been *Anopheles sinensis* (vivax malaria in the plains), *An. lesteri* (formerly *An. anthropophagus*), and *An. minimus* and *An. dirus* in the south. Lymphatic filariasis was eliminated in 2007. Dengue, transmitted mainly by *Aedes albopictus*, remains a problem in southern China; an outbreak in Guangdong in 2014 had more than 40,000 cases. An instructive application of insect biology comes from Guangzhou: releases of *Ae. albopictus* males infected with a *Wolbachia* strain that makes their matings with wild females sterile (cytoplasmic incompatibility; [7.17.28](/7.17.28)), combined with low-dose irradiation to sterilize any released females, nearly eliminated the mosquito from two river islands (Zheng and coauthors 2019).

Why some mosquito species are far more dangerous than others can be expressed by the *vectorial capacity*, the number of potentially infective bites that arise from one infected person in one day (Macdonald 1957; Garrett-Jones 1964):

$$
C = \frac{m a^2 p^n}{-\ln p},
$$

where $m$ is the number of female mosquitoes per person, $a$ the number of bites on people per mosquito per day (the product of biting frequency and the proportion of bites taken on humans), $p$ the daily probability of survival of a mosquito, and $n$ the extrinsic incubation period in days, the time the pathogen needs to develop in the mosquito before it reaches the salivary glands (about 10–14 days for *Plasmodium falciparum* at 25 °C). The factor $a$ appears twice, because the mosquito must bite twice, once to acquire the pathogen and once to transmit it; $p^n$ is the probability of surviving the incubation period; and $1/(-\ln p)$ is the expected remaining lifespan in which it can transmit. Vectorial capacity is extremely sensitive to $p$: if daily survival falls from 0.9 to 0.8 with $n = 12$, $p^n/(-\ln p)$ falls from about 2.7 to about 0.31, a ninefold reduction, while the number of mosquitoes only enters linearly. This is why insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor residual spraying, which kill old, infective mosquitoes, have reduced malaria so effectively, and why highly anthropophilic, long-lived vectors such as *Anopheles gambiae* in Africa sustain far more transmission than zoophilic species such as *An. sinensis*. Multiplied by the duration of human infectiousness and the probability of transmission per bite, vectorial capacity gives the **basic reproduction number** of a vector-borne infection ([7.11.35](/7.11.35)).

Robber flies and the other predatory flies show how vision and flight combine in pursuit. Like dragonflies ([7.17.31](/7.17.31)), robber flies such as *Holcocephala* take off toward a passing insect on an interception course, keeping the angle between their line of sight and the prey constant (a rule known from missile guidance as proportional navigation), and they correct their course with a delay of about 30 ms; the acute zone of their compound eye, with large facets and closely spaced ommatidia, lets them detect prey of 1 mm at distances of half a metre (Wardill and coauthors 2017). Male hover flies and many other flies chase females in a similar way, and the visual neurons of flies that detect small moving targets are among the best-studied neurons of insects.

### 35.5.2. Aschiza

Hover flies (Syrphidae; about 6000 species) are identified by a false vein, the *vena spuria*, running between the radius and the media, and by a closed cell near the posterior margin. Many are striped black and yellow and imitate bees and wasps (**Batesian mimicry**, [7.17.39](/7.17.39)), from honey-bee-like *Eristalis* drone flies to wasp-like *Chrysotoxum*; their mimicry is often imperfect, a puzzle studied in mimicry theory. Adults feed on nectar and pollen and are major pollinators. The larvae of Syrphinae are predators of aphids, the European *Episyrphus balteatus* among the most important natural enemies of aphids in China; those of *Eristalis* are aquatic filter feeders in polluted water with a telescopic breathing tube ("rat-tailed maggots"), and those of *Microdon* live in ant nests. Billions of hover flies migrate south each autumn across Europe.

Scuttle flies (Phoridae) are small, humpbacked flies that run rapidly; the larvae of *Megaselia scalaris* feed on almost any decaying matter, and species of *Pseudacteon* are parasitoids of ants that decapitate their hosts and were released in the southern United States against fire ants. Big-headed flies (Pipunculidae), whose head consists almost entirely of the eyes, are parasitoids of leafhoppers and planthoppers.

*Drosophila melanogaster* is the most important **model organism** among animals. T. H. Morgan's discovery of the sex-linked white-eye mutation in 1910 founded the chromosome theory of heredity; H. J. Muller showed in *Drosophila* that X-rays cause mutations; the screens of C. Nüsslein-Volhard and E. Wieschaus identified the genes that pattern the embryo, and E. B. Lewis's analysis of the homeotic genes led to the discovery of the **Hox genes** ([7.5.14](/7.5.14), [7.5.15](/7.5.15)); the Toll pathway of innate immunity ([7.15](/7.15)) and the molecular clock of circadian rhythms were also discovered in flies, each recognized by a Nobel Prize. The advantages that made *Drosophila* a model are biological: a generation time of ten days, small size, easy culture on yeast food, polytene chromosomes in the larval salivary glands, only four chromosome pairs, and a genetics free of recombination in males. The development of its imaginal discs is treated in [7.5.33](/7.5.33).

The New World screwworm illustrates the most successful use of the *sterile insect technique*, devised by E. F. The method works best for species whose females mate once and whose populations can first be reduced; it is used today against fruit flies, tsetse and mosquitoes ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)).

## 35.6. Flies and People

The economic and medical importance of Diptera is greater than that of any other order. Flies are vectors of the major tropical diseases (malaria, dengue, yellow fever, leishmaniasis, sleeping sickness, onchocerciasis, lymphatic filariasis) and of many viral diseases of livestock; they cause myiasis, damage crops as fruit flies, gall midges, leaf miners and root maggots, and are nuisance insects. They are also indispensable: larvae of flies are among the main decomposers of carrion, dung and plant litter; adult flies pollinate many plants, including cacao, mango and many alpine and arctic flowers, where flies outnumber bees; tachinids, hover flies and gall midges are important natural enemies of pests; black soldier fly larvae recycle organic waste; and fly larvae are a basic food of fish, birds and bats. In aquatic ecosystems, chironomid larvae are often the most numerous animals of the bottom fauna and are used as indicators of water quality and, from their subfossil head capsules in lake sediments, of past climates.

## Summary

- Antliophora (Mecoptera, Siphonaptera, Diptera) share a male sperm pump.
- **Mecoptera** (Definition 35.1.1) have a beak-like head with terminal chewing mouthparts and, uniquely among Holometabola, larvae with compound eyes; hangingflies and scorpionflies are models of nuptial gifts and mate choice.
- **Siphonaptera** (Definition 35.2.1) are laterally compressed, wingless blood feeders with combs, mouthparts of epipharynx and laciniae, and a resilin catapult that amplifies muscle power for jumping; larvae feed on debris and adult feces, and pharate adults wait in the cocoon. *Xenopsylla cheopis* transmits plague by regurgitation from a blocked proventriculus.
- **Diptera** (Definition 35.3.1) have one pair of wings and **halteres**, an enlarged mesothorax, sucking mouthparts, and legless larvae whose head is complete (eucephalic), reduced (hemicephalic) or internal with mouthhooks (acephalic maggots); Cyclorrhapha pupate in a puparium, and Schizophora escape from it with a ptilinum.
- Nematocera (long, many-segmented antennae) are a grade; Brachycera (short antennae with a stylus or arista) are a clade, within which Cyclorrhapha and Schizophora (Acalyptratae and Calyptratae) are nested.
- Blood-feeding females of nematocerans and horse flies retain stylets: the mosquito fascicle of labrum, mandibles, maxillae and hypopharynx inside the labial sheath. *Culex*, *Aedes* and *Anopheles* differ in eggs, larval siphon, resting posture and the diseases they transmit.
- The vectorial capacity of a mosquito population, $m a^2 p^n/(-\ln p)$, depends most strongly on the daily survival $p$ and the human-biting rate $a$.
- Important families include crane flies, moth and sand flies, midges, biting midges, black flies, gall midges, horse flies, soldier flies, robber flies, hover flies, fruit flies, *Drosophila*, house flies, blow flies, flesh flies, tachinids, bot flies and tsetse; the sterile insect technique and *Wolbachia* releases are biological methods of controlling them.

## Questions

1. A small wingless insect from a cat's bedding is flattened from side to side and jumps strongly. Which order is it, and which features distinguish it from a louse?

   **Answer** It is a flea (Siphonaptera), most likely the cat flea *Ctenocephalides felis*, which has both genal and pronotal combs. Fleas are laterally compressed, with long legs and enlarged hind legs for jumping, short antennae in grooves, piercing-sucking mouthparts and a sensilium on the tenth tergite; they are holometabolous, with legless larvae living off the host. Lice (Psocodea) are flattened dorsoventrally, cannot jump, have legs with claws for grasping hair, and are hemimetabolous, all stages living on the host.

2. Why can a flea not jump simply by contracting its leg muscles, and how does it solve the problem?

   **Answer** The flea reaches about 1 m/s in less than a millisecond; the peak power required, $v^2/t$ per unit mass, is of the order of 1000 W/kg of body mass, far more than muscle, which delivers at most a few hundred watts per kilogram of muscle, can produce, and muscle cannot shorten within a millisecond. The flea contracts the muscle slowly beforehand, storing elastic energy in a pad of resilin in the thorax while a catch locks the leg; releasing the catch returns the energy within a millisecond. Power is amplified because the energy is accumulated over about 0.1 s and released in about 1 ms.

3. A mosquito larva hangs at an angle below the water surface, breathing through a long siphon; nearby, another larva lies parallel to the surface without a siphon. To which genera might they belong, and what does this mean for the diseases in the area?

   **Answer** The first is a culicine, probably *Culex* (long siphon; eggs laid in rafts) or, if the siphon is short and the water is in a container, *Aedes*; the second is *Anopheles*. *Culex* species transmit West Nile virus, Japanese encephalitis and lymphatic filariasis, *Aedes* transmit dengue, Zika, chikungunya and yellow fever, and *Anopheles* are the only vectors of human malaria. The presence of *Anopheles* larvae therefore indicates potential for malaria transmission if infected people are present, while container-breeding *Aedes* indicate risk of dengue.

4. Explain why the old suborder Nematocera is no longer recognized, while Brachycera is.

   **Answer** Nematocera were defined by long antennae of many similar segments, eucephalic larvae and other characters that are ancestral for flies; the short antennae of Brachycera are derived. Molecular and morphological phylogenies show that one nematoceran lineage, Bibionomorpha, is more closely related to Brachycera than to other nematocerans, so that a group including all long-horned flies but excluding Brachycera is paraphyletic. Brachycera are monophyletic, defined by shared derived characters (short antennae with a compact flagellum, hemicephalic or acephalic larvae and others).

5. A body is found with blow fly larvae of known species at a known developmental stage. Explain how forensic entomologists estimate the minimum time since death, and what may make the estimate inaccurate.

   **Answer** Blow flies lay eggs on a body within hours of death. The development time of each stage at different temperatures is known from laboratory rearing and is expressed as an accumulated number of degree-days above a developmental threshold ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)). Using temperature records from the site, investigators count back the degree-days needed to reach the observed stage, giving the minimum time since the eggs were laid. Errors arise from differences between site and weather-station temperatures, the heat produced by masses of maggots, delayed access of flies (indoors, buried or wrapped bodies, night, cold), drugs in the tissues that alter development, and misidentification of species.

6. (Open question) Why might so many lineages of flies have evolved blood feeding, while no beetles and very few moths have?

   **Answer** Blood feeding requires mouthparts that can pierce skin and suck liquids, and the ability to find warm-blooded hosts. Flies already had fluid-feeding adults with stylet-like mandibles and maxillae (ancestral in the order and retained in females of several families), strong flight, and sensory systems tuned to carbon dioxide and odours; blood provides the protein needed for egg production. Beetles have chewing mouthparts adapted to solid food, and moths have lost mandibles and turned the maxillae into a soft proboscis; only a few *Calyptra* moths pierce skin, using a proboscis with hooks evolved from fruit-piercing. Testing this idea would involve mapping the origins of blood feeding on a phylogeny of flies and asking whether they occurred in lineages with nectar- or fluid-feeding, stylet-bearing ancestors.
