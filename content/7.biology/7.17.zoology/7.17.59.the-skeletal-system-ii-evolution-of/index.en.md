The previous chapter ([7.17.58](/7.17.58)) took the skull apart into its three components and followed each pharyngeal arch element through the vertebrates. This chapter puts the skull together again and follows it as a whole, from the cartilaginous braincase of a shark to the fused, akinetic skull of a mammal. Along the way it treats the features that change from one group to the next and that the Chinese textbooks use to characterize the classes: the number of bones, the occipital condyles, the shape of the braincase, the temporal fenestrae, the palate, and the movable joints within the skull.

The organizing idea is functional. A skull must protect the brain and sense organs, but its shape is dominated by feeding: by how food is captured (suction, biting, swallowing whole), processed (crushing, shearing, chewing) and swallowed, and by how the jaw muscles are housed and anchored. Each major transition in skull form, the origin of the tetrapod skull, the opening of temporal fenestrae, the kinetic skulls of squamates and birds, the akinetic skull of mammals, can be read as a change in the mechanics of feeding, combined with constraints inherited from the ancestral pattern of bones.

The chapter first follows the skull through the fishes (§59.1), the transition to land (§59.2), the amniotes and their temporal fenestrae (§59.3) and the kinetic skulls of lepidosaurs and birds (§59.4). Section 59.5 treats turtles, crocodylians and birds, §59.6 the origin and composition of the mammalian skull. Sections 59.7–59.9 then take the comparative characters one at a time: the number of bones and Williston's law, the occipital condyles and the braincase types, and the palate. Section 59.10 summarizes the skull of the classes in one table.

## 59.1. The Skulls of Fishes

### 59.1.1. Jawless Vertebrates and Chondrichthyans

The skulls of living cyclostomes consist of a cartilaginous trough beneath the brain, the capsules of a single nasal organ and two ears, and the cartilages of the oral apparatus and branchial basket ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.3.3)). The ostracoderms of the Palaeozoic added a head shield of dermal bone, a single plate (osteostracans, galeaspids) or a mosaic of plates (heterostracans), but no internal ossification of the braincase except a lining of perichondral bone in osteostracans. Their skulls show the paired eyes and the pineal opening between them, the single median nostril of osteostracans, and canals for the lateral line and for cranial nerves, which have allowed detailed reconstructions of the brain of 420-million-year-old fishes from tomographic scans ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.4)).

The chondrichthyan skull is a single box of cartilage, its surface reinforced by a mosaic of mineralized tiles (tesserae). It has a rostrum in front, nasal capsules, large orbits, otic capsules containing the inner ears, and an occipital region articulating with the vertebral column. The jaws are the cartilaginous primary jaws, suspended by the hyomandibula (hyostylic in most modern sharks and rays, amphistylic in sixgill and sevengill sharks) or fused to the braincase (holostylic chimaeras) ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.5)). Because the skull has no dermal bones and few joints, the variation among sharks lies mainly in its proportions, in the length of the rostrum, and in the shape of the jaws and teeth.

### 59.1.2. Actinopterygians

The skull of ray-finned fishes contains all three components in abundance. A teleost skull, with the bones of the jaws, the opercular series and the branchial arches, has more than 130 separate elements, the largest number in any vertebrate. Much of this number reflects the many moving parts of the feeding apparatus. A teleost captures most of its food by suction: it rapidly expands the buccal and opercular cavities, so that water rushes into the mouth carrying the prey with it. The expansion is produced by a chain of linked bones: the neurocranium is lifted by the epaxial muscles, the hyoid apparatus and the floor of the mouth are pulled down by the sternohyoideus attached to the shoulder girdle, the suspensoria (the hyomandibula, quadrate and the bones between them) swing outward, and the opercula are abducted.

**Formula 59.1.1 (Suction Flow)** In the simplest model, water entering the mouth must fill the increase in volume of the buccal cavity, so the mean flow speed across the mouth opening is

$$
u = \frac{1}{A}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t},
$$
where $V$ is the volume of the buccal and opercular cavities and $A$ the area of the mouth opening.

**Example 59.1.1** A sunfish that expands its buccal cavity by $2\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ in $20\,\mathrm{ms}$ through a mouth opening of $0.5\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ generates a mean flow of $(2/0.02)/0.5 = 200\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$, or $2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, enough to engulf a small swimming prey. Doubling the speed of expansion doubles the flow; narrowing the mouth to a small, round opening also increases the flow speed, at the cost of a smaller volume of water drawn in from in front of the head. The protrusible mouth of advanced teleosts, in which the premaxilla slides forward on an ascending process ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)), brings the opening closer to the prey and concentrates the flow on it, since the velocity of suction flow decays steeply with distance from the mouth (approximately as the cube of the distance for a point sink).

:::

The skull of early actinopterygians (palaeoniscoids) had a long, robust maxilla firmly attached to the cheek and an oblique suspensorium, and the mouth gaped widely. In the evolution of the neopterygians and then of the teleosts the maxilla was freed from the cheek bones, the suspensorium became more vertical, and the premaxilla became mobile, steps that are interpreted as successive improvements in suction feeding and in the precision of prey capture (Lauder 1982). The diversity of teleost skulls, from the elongated jaws of needlefishes to the crushing jaws of parrotfishes and the extremely protrusible jaws of the slingjaw wrasse *Epibulus*, is a diversity of variations on the same set of linkages.

### 59.1.3. Sarcopterygians and the Intracranial Joint

In the osteolepiforms, the fishes closest to tetrapods, and in coelacanths the braincase is divided into two units, an anterior ethmosphenoid unit carrying the snout, palate and upper jaw, and a posterior otico-occipital unit, joined by a movable *intracranial joint*. The roof of the skull is divided along the same line. When the mouth opened, the anterior unit was raised relative to the posterior unit, increasing the gape; in the living coelacanth *Latimeria* the joint is moved by a unique basicranial muscle beneath the braincase. Lungfishes and tetrapods lost the joint independently, in tetrapods by the fusion of the two units into one braincase.

The skull of osteolepiforms such as *Eusthenopteron* shows most of the bones that the tetrapod skull inherited. In the skull roof the bones over the brain (called "frontals" and "parietals" in older literature, but the parietals and postparietals of tetrapods, [7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2.3)) are flanked by intertemporals, supratemporals and tabulars; the cheek has a squamosal, quadratojugal, preopercular and the circumorbital bones; behind the roof lie extrascapular bones and, at the back of the cheek, the opercular and subopercular. The palate contains vomers, palatines, ectopterygoids, pterygoids and a parasphenoid, and the internal nostril (choana) opens between the vomer and the palatine, a feature that lobe-finned fishes share with tetrapods ([7.17.48](/7.17.48)).

## 59.2. The Tetrapod Skull

### 59.2.1. From Fish to Tetrapod

The series of Devonian fossils from *Eusthenopteron* through *Panderichthys* and *Tiktaalik* to *Acanthostega* and *Ichthyostega* documents the transformation of the skull ([7.17.48](/7.17.48#sec-48.3)).

| Change | Fishes (*Eusthenopteron*) | Early tetrapods (*Acanthostega*) | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proportions | Long postorbital region, short snout | Long snout, short postorbital region, eyes on top of the head | Capture of prey by biting and lunging in shallow water or at its surface rather than by suction |
| Opercular series | Opercular, subopercular, gular plates | Lost; the preopercular persists | Loss of the opercular pump and of water flow over gills in adults |
| Extrascapulars | Present, linking skull and shoulder | Lost | The head separated from the shoulder girdle (already in *Tiktaalik*), forming a neck |
| Intracranial joint | Present | Fused | A rigid braincase for biting and for transmitting forces from the jaws |
| Hyomandibula | Jaw support and opercular support | Stapes, bracing braincase against the palate | Jaw suspension autostylic; stapes later involved in hearing |
| Spiracle | Spiracular opening behind the eye | Otic (spiracular) notch between the squamosal and tabular | The notch housed a spiracle used in breathing, later in some lineages a tympanum |
| Lateral line | Canals in the bones | Grooves on the surface of the bones | Still aquatic; the lateral line was lost in terrestrial adults later |

The skull of early tetrapods was flat and broad, with a solid roof over the jaw muscles, small paired nostrils near the front, orbits on top, a pineal foramen and a notch at the back of each side. The braincase was low, the palate broad and covered with teeth, with large fangs on the vomer, palatine and ectopterygoid, and the occiput attached to the vertebral column by a single condyle formed by the basioccipital and the exoccipitals.

### 59.2.2. Living Amphibians

The skulls of frogs, salamanders and caecilians are greatly simplified compared with those of their Palaeozoic relatives. Frogs and salamanders have lost many dermal bones (the postparietals, tabulars, supratemporals, intertemporals and postfrontals; in frogs also the postorbital and jugal; in salamanders often the lacrimal and quadratojugal), so that the orbits and the temporal regions are open and the skull is a light framework of bars. The braincase is only partly ossified; the frog has a single sphenethmoid, paired prootics and exoccipitals, and fused frontoparietals. Caecilians, by contrast, have compact, heavily ossified skulls with fused elements, used as rams for burrowing, in which the eyes are reduced and covered by skin or bone.

The simplification of the skull of living amphibians has been explained by **paedomorphosis** ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)): the skulls of small temnospondyls resemble the juvenile skulls of larger relatives, and the bones lost in frogs and salamanders are those that ossify last in the development of temnospondyls. The open skulls also accommodate the large eyes, which in frogs bulge into the roof of the mouth and help to push food down the throat during swallowing.

## 59.3. The Amniote Skull

### 59.3.1. Early Amniotes

The skull of the earliest amniotes, such as *Hylonomus* and *Paleothyris* ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.1)), differed from that of earlier tetrapods in several ways: it was relatively deeper and narrower; the otic notch was absent, the squamosal meeting the tabular and supratemporal at the back of the skull; the intertemporal was lost; a flange of the pterygoid projected ventrally in front of the jaw joint, providing an origin for a new jaw muscle, the pterygoideus; and the jaw muscles were larger and could close the jaw on prey with more force (kinetic-inertial feeding gave way to static pressure feeding, Olson 1961). The braincase was fully ossified, with a single condyle at its base.

### 59.3.2. Temporal Fenestrae

The openings in the temporal region of the dermal roof are defined and classified in [7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.3): **temporal fenestrae** absent (anapsid), one lower (synapsid), two (diapsid) and one upper (euryapsid and parapsid). The fenestrae evolved independently in synapsids and diapsids and were modified repeatedly afterwards.

| Lineage | Ancestral pattern | Later modifications |
|---|---|---|
| Synapsids | One lower fenestra, bounded by postorbital and squamosal above, jugal and squamosal below | Fenestra enlarged until the postorbital bar was lost and the orbit merged with the temporal fossa; the lower bar became the zygomatic arch; the upper margin, the sagittal crest of the braincase |
| Lepidosaurs | Diapsid | Squamates lost the lower temporal bar (quadratojugal), freeing the quadrate; many lost the upper bar too (snakes, amphisbaenians); tuatara has a complete lower bar, secondarily re-formed |
| Archosaurs | Diapsid, with an antorbital fenestra in front of the orbit and a mandibular fenestra in the lower jaw | Crocodylians retain both temporal fenestrae but closed the antorbital fenestra; birds merged the upper temporal fenestra and orbit and reduced the bars |
| Turtles | Diapsid in stem turtles | Fenestrae closed; temporal emarginations from behind (most cryptodires) or from below (cheek emargination) |
| Marine reptiles | Diapsid | Ichthyosaurs and sauropterygians: lower fenestra closed (euryapsid and parapsid skulls) |

The repeated opening of fenestrae has been explained in several ways: by the need for space for bulging jaw muscles during contraction, by the provision of strong edges for muscle attachment, and by the removal of bone where stresses are low. Finite-element models of skulls show that the bars around the fenestrae carry the principal stresses of biting, while the regions where the fenestrae open are relatively unstressed, which supports a combination of the last two explanations ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.3.3)).

### 59.3.3. The Lizard Skull as a Representative

The lizard used as a representative animal in the Chinese textbooks (in China usually a lacertid such as *Eremias* or *Takydromus*, or the gecko *Gekko*) shows the squamate pattern of the diapsid skull. The skull is relatively high and narrow, with a large orbit separated from its partner by an interorbital septum (a tropibasic skull, §59.8.2). Behind the orbit the upper temporal fenestra is bounded by the parietal medially, the postorbital and postfrontal in front, and the squamosal and supratemporal laterally and behind; the upper temporal bar (postorbital and squamosal) is complete. The lower temporal bar is absent: the jugal ends behind the orbit, the quadratojugal is lost, and the quadrate is exposed on the side of the skull, suspended from the squamosal and supratemporal and free to rotate.

The braincase is fully ossified except in the ethmoid region, with a single occipital condyle formed by the basioccipital and the two exoccipitals. The parietals are fused into one bone with a parietal foramen for the parietal eye ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)). The palate retains the vomers, palatines, pterygoids and ectopterygoids, and a slender epipterygoid (the columella cranii of older texts) rises from the pterygoid to the braincase on each side. The lower jaw has the dentary, splenial, coronoid, angular, surangular and the compound articular (articular fused with the prearticular), and the teeth are attached to the inner side of the jaw (pleurodont) or to its crest (acrodont, in agamids and chameleons).

In geckos the postorbital and the upper temporal bar are lost, so the temporal region is fully open; in amphisbaenians and many burrowing lizards the skull is consolidated into a compact, akinetic unit for head-first burrowing, in convergence with caecilians. The tuatara (*Sphenodon*) differs from lizards in having a complete lower temporal bar, which recent fossils show to be secondarily reacquired, a fixed quadrate and acrodont teeth on the maxilla that shear against a double row on the dentary and palatine, a unique propalinal chewing mechanism in which the lower jaw slides forward during closure.

## 59.4. Cranial Kinesis

### 59.4.1. Definition and Types

The skull of most vertebrates is a set of bones firmly united by sutures, except for the joint of the lower jaw. In some groups, however, parts of the skull move relative to each other.

**Definition 59.4.1 (Cranial Kinesis)** **Cranial kinesis** is movement between parts of the skull other than the movement of the lower jaw on the skull, made possible by flexible joints within the skull. It is described by the position of the main hinge:

- *metakinesis*: movement between the braincase and the dermal skull roof at the back of the skull (lizards);
- *mesokinesis*: movement at a transverse hinge between the frontal and parietal bones, behind the orbits (many lizards);
- *prokinesis*: movement of the upper jaw as a unit at a hinge between the nasal and frontal bones, in front of the orbit (most birds);
- *rhynchokinesis*: flexion within the upper jaw itself, at hinges along the nasal bars (some birds, such as shorebirds and hummingbirds);
- *streptostyly*: rotation of the quadrate about its dorsal articulation with the skull, which moves the lower jaw joint forward and backward (squamates, birds).

The term *akinetic* is used for skulls without significant kinesis: those of crocodylians, turtles and mammals.

### 59.4.2. Lizards and Snakes

In lizards, a typical kinetic cycle has been documented by cineradiography (Frazzetta 1962; Herrel and colleagues): as the mouth opens, the quadrate swings forward (streptostyly), pushing the pterygoids and the palate forward, and the snout rotates upward at the mesokinetic joint; as the mouth closes on the prey, the snout rotates downward, so that the teeth of the upper jaw strike the prey more nearly perpendicular to its surface and the bite is distributed along the tooth row. The extent of kinesis varies greatly among lizards, and in some families (chameleons, many iguanians) it is small or absent. Its functions are still debated: proposals include an increase in gape, better grip on struggling prey, and shock absorption during strong biting.

Snakes have the most kinetic skulls of all vertebrates. The braincase is a rigid tube, but the upper jaws (maxilla, palatine and pterygoid) on each side, the quadrates, the supratemporals and the two halves of the lower jaw move independently, linked by joints and ligaments. The two mandibles are joined at the front only by elastic tissue, so that they can be spread apart, and each can be advanced separately. A snake swallows prey larger than its head by "walking" its jaws over it, alternately advancing the left and right sides of the upper and lower jaws while the teeth of the other side hold the prey (the pterygoid walk). In vipers the short maxilla bearing the fang rotates on the prefrontal, erecting the fang when the mouth opens ([7.17.51](/7.17.51)). The loss of the bars and of most sutures in the skull of snakes is the anatomical basis of this mechanism.

### 59.4.3. Birds

In most birds the upper bill moves as a unit at a flexible zone at its base, between the nasal and frontal bones (prokinesis). The movement is driven by the quadrate: when the jaw opens, the protractor muscles rotate the quadrate forward, and the quadrate pushes on two bony rods that run forward from it, the jugal bar laterally and the pterygoid–palatine bar medially, which in turn push the base of the upper bill and raise it. Because the jugal bar is a thin bone connecting the quadrate with the upper jaw, and because the postorbital bar of reptiles has disappeared, the orbit and the temporal region are open behind, leaving the quadrate free to rotate.

The functions of avian kinesis include increasing the gape, maintaining the orientation of the upper bill relative to the visual field while the lower jaw opens, absorbing shock during pecking (woodpeckers), and providing fine control of the bill tip for manipulating small objects. In parrots the hinge is a true synovial joint, and the upper bill can be raised strongly to crack seeds; in shorebirds that probe in mud (snipes, sandpipers) rhynchokinesis allows the tip of the bill alone to open underground to seize prey.

## 59.5. Turtles, Crocodylians and Birds

### 59.5.1. Turtles

The skull of turtles is compact and akinetic, with no teeth and a horny beak (rhamphotheca) on the premaxillae, maxillae and dentary. It lacks temporal fenestrae; instead, many turtles have emarginations, notches cut into the posterior or lower margin of the skull roof that expose the jaw muscles. The embryology of the skull, molecular phylogenies and the diapsid skulls of stem turtles such as *Pappochelys* and *Eorhynchochelys* show that this condition is a secondary closure of a diapsid skull ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.3.4), [7.17.51](/7.17.51)).

A special feature of turtles is the *trochlear mechanism* of the jaw-closing muscle. The adductor mandibulae, which in other reptiles runs directly from the skull roof to the lower jaw, in turtles runs backward over the otic region and then turns downward around a pulley (trochlea) to the jaw, lengthening the muscle and allowing a larger muscle to pass through the confined space inside the shell when the head is retracted. The pulley is formed by the otic capsule (trochlear process of the prootic) in cryptodires, which retract the neck vertically, and by the pterygoid in pleurodires, which fold it sideways; the two mechanisms evolved independently.

### 59.5.2. Crocodylians

The crocodylian skull is a robust, flat, diapsid skull with both temporal fenestrae, an akinetic braincase and a fixed quadrate. Its most distinctive feature is a complete bony secondary palate formed by the premaxillae, maxillae, palatines and pterygoids, which carries the internal nostrils to the back of the mouth, behind a fold of tissue, the palatal valve, that closes off the throat. A crocodile can breathe with its mouth open under water, holding prey, as long as the nostrils are above the surface. The secondary palate also stiffens the long snout against the bending and twisting forces of struggling prey: in the long-snouted gharial and in crocodiles that twist prey by rolling, the palate forms a closed tube with the snout roof, which resists torsion much better than an open trough (Busbey 1995).

Crocodylians also show archosaur features: an antorbital fossa in front of the orbit (the antorbital fenestra closed in living species), a mandibular fenestra in the lower jaw, teeth in sockets (**thecodont** teeth), and a braincase with extensive air spaces connected to the middle ear.

### 59.5.3. Birds

The bird skull is a modified diapsid skull. The braincase is enlarged and rounded, the orbits are very large and separated only by a thin interorbital septum, and the occiput has rotated under the braincase, so that the foramen magnum faces downward and backward. The dermal and endochondral bones fuse early in life, the sutures disappear and many bones are pneumatized from the middle ear and nasal cavity, so that the skull is light; in pigeons and chickens the separate bones can be distinguished only in the young. The postorbital bone is lost, the upper temporal fenestra has merged with the orbit, and the lower temporal bar is reduced to the slender jugal bar (jugal and quadratojugal).

The palate of birds shows two main patterns. In the palaeognaths (ratites and tinamous) the vomer is large and the pterygoid and palatine are rigidly united with each other and with the basisphenoid (dromaeognathous palate); in the neognaths the pterygoid–palatine joint is movable and slides on the base of the braincase during kinesis. Older classifications distinguished several neognath palates by the relation of the maxillopalatine processes to the vomer and to each other: the *schizognathous* palate, in which the maxillopalatines do not meet in the midline and leave a cleft (pigeons, gulls, chickens), and the *desmognathous* palate, in which they fuse across the midline (ducks, parrots, birds of prey). These palate types, still listed in the Chinese textbooks, are useful descriptions but are poor guides to phylogeny. Birds have no bony secondary palate: the slit between the palatal bones remains open.

## 59.6. The Mammalian Skull

### 59.6.1. From Synapsids to Mammals

The skull of the earliest synapsids, such as *Archaeothyris* and the pelycosaurs, had a single lower temporal fenestra, a complete postorbital bar, many dermal bones in the roof (including postfrontals, postorbitals, tabulars and supratemporals), a lower jaw of six or seven bones with the jaw joint between the quadrate and the articular, a single occipital condyle, and a primary palate. The changes from this pattern to that of mammals occurred in steps through therapsids and cynodonts, in a mosaic pattern described in [7.17.54](/7.17.54).

| Feature | Early synapsids | Therapsids and early cynodonts | Mammaliaforms and mammals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temporal fenestra | Small, behind the orbit | Enlarged; the parietal forms a sagittal crest | Confluent with the orbit; postorbital bar lost (re-formed in primates, ungulates and others) |
| Dermal roof bones | Postfrontal, postorbital, supratemporal, tabular present | Progressively lost | Lost; postparietals fused into the interparietal |
| Lower jaw | Dentary and six postdentary bones of similar importance | Dentary enlarged with a coronoid process; postdentary bones reduced and loosely attached | Dentary only |
| Jaw joint | Quadrate–articular | Quadrate–articular, with a second contact between dentary and squamosal in advanced cynodonts (*Diarthrognathus*, *Probainognathus*) | Dentary–squamosal; quadrate and articular in the middle ear as incus and malleus |
| Palate | Primary palate | Partial secondary palate (maxillary and palatine shelves) | Complete secondary palate with a soft palate |
| Occipital condyle | Single | Double in cynodonts | Double, on the exoccipitals |
| Teeth | Many similar, continuously replaced | Incisors, canines, postcanines; precise occlusion begins | Heterodont, diphyodont, with precise occlusion |
| Braincase | Narrow, low | Wall partly formed by the epipterygoid | Enlarged; side wall formed by the alisphenoid (epipterygoid) and the parietal and squamosal |
| Zygomatic arch | Lower temporal bar (jugal, squamosal) | Bowed outward for the enlarged jaw muscles | Zygomatic arch (zygomatic process of the squamosal and the jugal); masseter attached to it |

The sequence of the jaw joint and the ear is one of the best-documented transformations in the fossil record. In the Jurassic mammaliaforms and early mammals from the Yanliao and Jehol biotas of northern China (*Liaoconodon*, *Yanoconodon*, *Maotherium*), the postdentary bones remained attached to the dentary by an ossified Meckel's cartilage even when they no longer formed the jaw joint, an intermediate stage between the reptilian jaw and the definitive mammalian middle ear, which was detached from the jaw independently in monotremes and therians (Meng et al. 2011; [7.10.32](/7.10.32)).

### 59.6.2. Composition of the Mammalian Skull

The mammalian skull consists of fewer bones than that of any other tetrapod: about 30 in a rabbit or dog, 22 in an adult human (without the ear ossicles and the hyoid). The reduction results both from the loss of dermal bones and from the fusion of endochondral and dermal elements into compound bones. Each compound bone has a history that can be traced to separate elements of other vertebrates.

| Mammalian bone | Components | Component of the skull | Homologue in reptiles |
|---|---|---|---|
| Occipital | Basioccipital, two exoccipitals, supraoccipital; interparietal (often fused) | Chondrocranium; interparietal dermal | Basioccipital, exoccipitals, supraoccipital; postparietals |
| Temporal | Squamous part (squamosal); petrous part (petrosal = prootic + opisthotic + epiotic); tympanic part (ectotympanic); styloid process; in many mammals the entotympanic of the bulla | Dermal (squamosal, tympanic); chondrocranium (petrosal); splanchnocranium (styloid) | Squamosal; prootic and opisthotic; angular; ceratohyal; entotympanic is a mammalian novelty |

### 59.6.3. The Akinetic Skull and Chewing

The mammalian skull is akinetic, but it permits the most elaborate processing of food among vertebrates: chewing (mastication), in which the cheek teeth of the upper and lower jaws occlude precisely and food is cut and ground before it is swallowed. Several features of the skull work together to make this possible.

- *A single, stable jaw joint* between the condyle of the dentary and the glenoid fossa of the squamosal, often with an articular disc, allows complex movements: hinge rotation in carnivores, whose transversely elongated condyle sits in a deep fossa that permits little sideways movement; transverse grinding in ungulates, with flat joints; and anteroposterior movement in rodents, whose condyles slide in longitudinal grooves.
- *A divided jaw-closing musculature*: the temporalis, inserting on the coronoid process, and the masseter and medial pterygoid, inserting on the angle of the dentary, form a sling around the jaw. By activating the muscles of the two sides unequally, a mammal moves the lower jaw laterally and grinds food on one side at a time (unilateral chewing), with the jaw of the working side moving across the upper teeth.
- *A complete secondary palate and a soft palate* separate the food in the mouth from the airway, so that the animal can breathe while it chews; the cheeks and a muscular tongue keep food between the teeth.
- *A strong, sutured braincase and zygomatic arch* resist the forces of the jaw muscles, which in carnivores can reach several thousand newtons.

The proportions of the skull follow the diet. The temporalis dominates in carnivores, which have a large coronoid process, a high sagittal crest and a jaw joint at the level of the tooth row, maximizing force at the carnassials ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.5.3)). The masseter dominates in herbivores, with a high jaw joint above the tooth row, so that the cheek teeth meet simultaneously along their length; in rodents a part of the masseter passes through or in front of the orbit and pulls the jaw forward for gnawing. The comparative anatomy of teeth is in [7.17.63](/7.17.63), of the jaw muscles in [7.17.62](/7.17.62).

### 59.6.4. Skull Proportions and Allometry

Within the constraints of a common set of bones, the proportions of the skull vary greatly with size, diet and development. Two allometric patterns are well documented in mammals.

- *Brain and face.* The braincase scales with the brain, which grows with strong negative allometry relative to body mass (brain mass $\propto M^{0.6}$–$M^{0.75}$ across mammals, [7.14.46](/7.14.46)), while the face scales with the dentition and the jaw muscles, nearly isometrically. Larger species therefore have relatively smaller braincases and longer faces. In many groups of placental mammals and in marsupials the larger species have relatively longer snouts, a pattern called cranial evolutionary allometry (Cardini and Polly 2013).
- *Juvenile and adult.* In the individual, the braincase is relatively large at birth and the face grows more, so that the snout lengthens with age (ontogenetic allometry). Paedomorphosis in the proportions of the skull, retention of juvenile proportions in adults, has been proposed for the domestic dog, many of whose breeds have short faces and rounded braincases compared with the wolf, and for humans, whose flat face and rounded braincase resemble those of juvenile apes.

The skulls of domestic dogs show how much variation the same set of bones can produce in a few centuries. The length of the face relative to the braincase varies more among dog breeds than among all wild species of Carnivora, from the long-snouted (dolichocephalic) borzoi to the short-snouted (brachycephalic) bulldog (Drake and Klingenberg 2010). Short faces in brachycephalic breeds are associated with mutations affecting the growth of the face, such as a variant of the gene *SMOC2*, and with the obstructive breathing problems of these breeds, because the soft tissues of the nasal cavity and the soft palate do not shrink with the bones. The example illustrates that the skull is a developmental system, not a set of independent parts: selection on one proportion changes the shape of many bones at once, in the manner of Thompson's transformation grids ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.4.3)).

## 59.7. The Number of Bones and Williston's Law

### 59.7.1. The Trend

Across the history of tetrapods the number of separate bones in the skull has decreased. The paleontologist Samuel Williston (1914) generalized the observation: "the parts in an organism tend toward reduction in number, with the fewer parts greatly specialized in function". *Williston's law* is not a law but a trend, and its causes are discussed below. The skulls of early sarcopterygians had more than 50 dermal bones in the skull roof, cheek and opercular region alone; early tetrapods about 40–45 bones in the skull and lower jaw on each side; early amniotes slightly fewer; mammals and birds, after fusion, fewer still.

| Group | Approximate number of bones in the skull (adult, including the lower jaw) |
|---|---|
| Teleosts | 100–180, including the opercular and branchial skeleton |
| Early tetrapods and amniotes; living reptiles | 50–90 |
| Living amphibians | About 30–50 (fewer in frogs) |
| Mammals (dog, rabbit) | About 30–35 |
| Humans | 22 (29 with ear ossicles and hyoid) |

The numbers in the table are approximate and depend on what is counted (paired bones once or twice, separate or fused elements, juvenile or adult skulls); they are the order of magnitude given in the textbooks. Studies that counted skull bones consistently across a phylogeny, such as Sidor's (2001) count for about 100 synapsid genera, found a clear decrease in the number of bones from pelycosaurs to mammals, but also that the decrease was not steady: it was concentrated at particular nodes and accompanied by reversals, and the rate of loss differed among lineages.

![The number of bones in the skull of the vertebrate classes, as ranges reported in comparative anatomy textbooks (Kardong 2019; Yang & Cheng), on a logarithmic axis, arranged in the order of the phylogeny from teleosts to humans. The decrease from fishes to mammals is the trend that Williston generalized; the ranges overlap, and the counts depend on whether paired bones, fused elements and juvenile skulls are counted, so the figure shows orders of magnitude, not measurements of single species.](./plots/bone-counts.py)

### 59.7.2. Causes and Exceptions

Bones are lost in two ways. *Fusion* reduces the number of separate elements while preserving their tissue, as in the occipital, temporal and sphenoid of mammals; it often follows a reduction of mobility, since sutures that no longer move are no longer needed, and is common where the skull is subjected to large forces or where it encloses an enlarged brain. *Loss* removes an element entirely, as with the postfrontal, postorbital and supratemporal of mammals; it often follows the reduction of an element in size and function, as in the postdentary bones that became small before they became ear bones.

Proposed causes include functional integration (fewer, larger bones form a stronger structure), developmental economy (bones that form late in development are easily lost by paedomorphosis, as in living amphibians), and the enlargement of the brain, which expands the braincase until neighbouring bones meet and fuse. Not all lineages follow the trend: teleosts increased the number of bones in their feeding apparatus as they evolved new linkages; ichthyosaurs and many whales increased the number of phalanges; and new elements arise in many skulls as sesamoids, fontanelle bones or ossifications of soft tissue (the entotympanic of mammals, the os rostri of pigs). Williston's law describes a tendency in the dermal skull of tetrapods, not a universal direction of evolution.

## 59.8. Occipital Condyles and Braincase Types

### 59.8.1. Occipital Condyles

The joint between the skull and the vertebral column is formed by one or two condyles on the occipital bones around the foramen magnum.

**Definition 59.8.1 (Occipital Condyle)** An **occipital condyle** is an articular surface on the occipital region of the skull that articulates with the first vertebra (the atlas in amniotes). A single condyle is formed by the basioccipital, often with contributions from the exoccipitals (tripartite condyle); paired condyles are formed by the two exoccipitals on either side of the foramen magnum.

| Group | Condyle | Formed by | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclostomes | None | — | Notochord continuous into the braincase |
| Chondrichthyans | Paired condylar surfaces on the occipital cartilage, or the skull fused to the vertebrae | Occipital cartilage | Small movement at most |
| Teleosts | Single, concave, like the face of a vertebral centrum | Basioccipital (with exoccipitals) | |
| Early tetrapods | Single | Basioccipital and exoccipitals | Ancestral tetrapod state |
| Living amphibians | Two | Exoccipitals (basioccipital unossified) | Allows nodding but little rotation; the atlas has two cotyles |
| Reptiles and birds | Single, rounded | Basioccipital (with exoccipitals) | Allows rotation and nodding; in birds a hemispherical condyle permits wide head movements |
| Synapsids | Single in early forms, double in cynodonts and mammals | Exoccipitals | Mammals: nodding at the atlanto-occipital joint, rotation at the atlanto-axial joint ([7.17.60](/7.17.60)) |

The double condyles of amphibians and of mammals are convergent: they evolved from a single condyle independently, in the lissamphibian lineage by loss of ossification of the basioccipital, in cynodonts by division of the condyle with enlargement of the exoccipital contributions. Summaries that list two condyles for cartilaginous fishes should be read with care: their condylar surfaces do not form a true occipital joint in most species.

### 59.8.2. Platybasic and Tropibasic Braincases

The shape of the braincase between the orbits depends on the trabeculae of the embryonic chondrocranium ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.2.1)).

**Definition 59.8.2 (Platybasic and Tropibasic Skulls)** A **platybasic** skull is one in which the trabeculae remain separate and broad, so that the braincase extends forward between the orbits and the orbits are widely separated. A **tropibasic** skull is one in which the trabeculae fuse into a median bar, the orbits are large and close together, separated by a thin interorbital septum, and the brain lies behind them.

Platybasic skulls are found in chondrichthyans, lungfishes, living amphibians and mammals; tropibasic skulls in most actinopterygians, in reptiles and in birds. The distribution is not a simple phylogenetic sequence. The tropibasic skull is associated with large eyes and a small anterior brain, conditions found in many fishes, lizards and birds; the platybasic skull of living amphibians is associated with their wide, flat heads; and the platybasic skull of mammals is secondary, arising from tropibasic ancestors as the forebrain expanded forward between the orbits. Some study summaries classify mammals as tropibasic; this is not the usual interpretation, which places mammals among platybasic skulls with a secondary origin.

## 59.9. The Palate

### 59.9.1. Primary and Secondary Palates

The *primary palate* is the roof of the mouth formed by the dermal bones beneath the braincase: vomers, palatines, ectopterygoids, pterygoids and parasphenoid ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.6.3)). In fishes and early tetrapods it is the only palate, and in tetrapods the internal nostrils open through it, near the front of the mouth, so that air and food share the oral cavity.

**Definition 59.9.1 (Secondary Palate)** The **secondary palate** is a horizontal shelf of bone, formed by medial processes of the premaxilla, maxilla and palatine (and in crocodylians also the pterygoid), that lies below the primary palate and separates the nasal passage from the mouth, carrying the internal nostrils posteriorly toward the pharynx. In mammals it is continued backward by the muscular soft palate.

The secondary palate evolved independently in several lineages: in therapsids (partially in therocephalians, completely in advanced cynodonts and mammals), in crocodyliforms (completely only in eusuchians, the group that includes living crocodylians), in some turtles, and in some lizards (partially, by soft tissue folds). Its functions differ.

| Function | Explanation | Groups |
|---|---|---|
| Breathing while food is in the mouth | Air passes above the palate to the pharynx while the mouth holds or chews food | Mammals (chewing), crocodylians (holding prey under water) |
| Suckling | The young seal the mouth around the nipple and suck while breathing through the nose | Mammals |
| Strengthening the snout | A closed box of palate and roof resists bending and torsion | Crocodylians with long snouts; mammals with strong jaws |
| Support for the tongue in processing food | The tongue presses food against a hard surface | Mammals; palatal ridges (rugae) |

The secondary palate is linked with endothermy in synapsids, because continuous breathing is needed to sustain high metabolic rates, and its appearance in therocephalians and cynodonts is one of the indirect lines of evidence for elevated metabolism in these groups. In crocodylians, which are ectotherms, the palate evolved in connection with aquatic predation and the mechanical demands of a long snout. The comparison shows that a structure can be homoplastic and serve different roles in different lineages ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.3.1)). In humans, failure of the palatal shelves to fuse during development produces cleft palate, one of the commonest birth defects, which interferes with suckling and speech.

### 59.9.2. The Internal Nostrils and the Nasal Cavity

The internal nostril (**choana**) originated in tetrapodomorph fishes, in which it opened between the vomer and the palatine, near the front of the palate. In amphibians and most reptiles it remains in that position. A secondary palate moves it backward, and in mammals the nasal cavity enlarged above the palate, becoming filled with the scroll-like turbinals: maxilloturbinals in the airway, covered with respiratory epithelium that warms and humidifies the inspired air and recovers heat and water from the expired air, and ethmoturbinals and nasoturbinals in the olfactory recess, covered with olfactory epithelium. The maxilloturbinals are closely associated with endothermy; their presence in cynodonts has been taken as evidence for elevated metabolic rates ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)). The olfactory system and the vomeronasal organ are treated in [7.17.68](/7.17.68).

### 59.10.2. Trends and Their Explanation

The Chinese textbooks summarize the evolution of the skull as a set of trends: the number of bones decreases; cartilage is progressively replaced by bone and bones fuse; the braincase enlarges relative to the face; the splanchnocranium is transformed after the loss of gills; and the joints of the skull become tighter. Each trend holds over the main line from fishes to mammals and has a functional basis: the enlargement of the brain in birds and mammals, the change from suction feeding in water to biting and chewing on land, and the reorganization of the jaw muscles. But the trends are not universal. Kinesis increased in squamates and birds; the number of bones increased in the feeding apparatus of teleosts; amphibians simplified their skulls by paedomorphosis, not by the fusions of mammals; and many features, the double occipital condyles, the secondary palate, the platybasic braincase, evolved convergently in different lineages. The skull, more than any other part of the skeleton, shows how the same few components can be rearranged to solve different problems.

## Summary

- Cyclostome and chondrichthyan skulls are cartilaginous; ostracoderms and placoderms had dermal head shields. Teleost skulls have more than 130 elements, organized into linkages for suction feeding (Formula 59.1.1: flow speed $u = (\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}t)/A$) and jaw protrusion. Osteolepiforms and coelacanths have an intracranial joint.
- The transition to tetrapods lengthened the snout, lost the opercular and extrascapular bones (freeing the head from the shoulder), fused the intracranial joint, converted the hyomandibula into the stapes and left an otic notch; living amphibians simplified the skull, probably by paedomorphosis.
- Amniotes lost the otic notch and the intertemporal, gained a pterygoid flange and stronger jaw muscles, and evolved **temporal fenestrae** independently in synapsids and diapsids, later modified in each lineage.
- **Cranial kinesis** (Definition 59.4.1): metakinesis, mesokinesis, prokinesis, rhynchokinesis and streptostyly; extreme in snakes, prokinetic in most birds; crocodylians, turtles and mammals are akinetic.
- Turtles secondarily closed their fenestrae and evolved a trochlear jaw-muscle mechanism; crocodylians have a complete secondary palate that permits breathing with prey in the mouth and stiffens the snout; birds have fused, pneumatized skulls with large orbits, an interorbital septum and no bony secondary palate.
- The mammalian skull arose from the synapsid pattern by enlargement of the fenestra, loss of roof bones, enlargement of the dentary, a new dentary–squamosal joint, transfer of the quadrate and articular to the ear, a double condyle and a secondary palate. Its compound bones (occipital, temporal, sphenoid) combine chondrocranial, dermal and splanchnocranial elements.
- Williston's law: the number of skull bones decreases by fusion and loss, with exceptions; **occipital condyles** (Definition 59.8.1) are single in teleosts, early tetrapods, reptiles and birds and double, convergently, in lissamphibians and mammals; platybasic and tropibasic skulls (Definition 59.8.2) depend on the trabeculae; the **secondary palate** (Definition 59.9.1) evolved independently in mammals and crocodylians.

## Questions

1. A teleost increases the volume of its buccal cavity by $1.5\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ in $15\,\mathrm{ms}$. Calculate the mean flow speed at the mouth for mouth openings of $0.3\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ and $1.2\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, and explain why a fish feeding on small evasive prey benefits from a small, protrusible mouth.

   **Answer** $\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}t = 1.5/0.015 = 100\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}$. With $A = 0.3\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, $u \approx 333\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}} = 3.3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; with $A = 1.2\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, $u \approx 83\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$. A small mouth concentrates the same volume flow into a higher speed, which is needed to overcome the escape speed of small prey. Because suction flow decays steeply with distance from the mouth, protruding the mouth toward the prey brings the region of high flow to it; the combination of a small, round, protrusible mouth with rapid expansion is typical of planktivorous and small-prey specialists. A large mouth draws in a larger volume at lower speed, suitable for large or slow prey.

2. Explain why the double occipital condyles of frogs and of mammals are considered convergent and not homologous.

   **Answer** The ancestral state for tetrapods, inferred from early tetrapods, early amniotes, early synapsids and reptiles, is a single condyle formed by the basioccipital and exoccipitals. Within synapsids, the single condyle persisted through pelycosaurs and early therapsids and became double only in cynodonts, by enlargement of the exoccipital contributions; within the amphibian lineage, the basioccipital ceased to ossify, leaving the two exoccipitals as condyles. Because the many groups between frogs and mammals on the tree have single condyles, the double condition arose at least twice, and the two conditions also differ in development (unossified basioccipital in frogs, a reduced basioccipital contribution in mammals). They fail Patterson's congruence test as homologues.

3. Compare the functions of the secondary palate in crocodylians and in mammals. Why is its presence in cynodonts used as evidence of endothermy, but not in crocodylians?

   **Answer** In both groups the palate separates the airway from the mouth. In crocodylians, with a palatal valve, it allows breathing while the mouth is open under water holding prey, and it stiffens the long snout against torsion when struggling prey is shaken or rolled. In mammals it allows breathing during prolonged chewing and during suckling, supports the tongue in food processing and strengthens the face against chewing forces. Endotherms need continuous ventilation because their metabolic rate is about ten times that of ectotherms, so interruption of breathing during long feeding bouts is costly; the palate in cynodonts, together with turbinals and other features, is consistent with elevated metabolism. Crocodylians show that a secondary palate can evolve in ectotherms for other reasons, so the palate alone is weak evidence; it is persuasive only in combination with other characters.

4. Describe how the quadrate moves the upper bill in a prokinetic bird, and why the loss of the postorbital bar was necessary for this mechanism.

   **Answer** When the depressor muscles open the lower jaw, the protractor muscles of the quadrate rotate it forward about its dorsal articulation with the braincase. The quadrate pushes two struts forward: laterally the thin jugal bar (jugal and quadratojugal), medially the pterygoid and palatine, which slide along the base of the braincase. Both struts push against the base of the upper bill, which rotates upward at the flexible nasofrontal hinge. Closing reverses the movement. The mechanism requires the quadrate and the struts to be free to move relative to the braincase; in a diapsid with a complete postorbital bar and upper temporal bar, the jugal and quadratojugal would be braced against the skull roof and could not slide. The loss of the postorbital and the reduction of the bars left the jugal bar as a mobile rod.

5. (Open) Williston's law states that serially repeated parts tend to become fewer and more specialized. Discuss whether this is a law of evolution, citing evidence from skulls and from other parts of the skeleton.

   **Answer** Points to consider: (a) Evidence for the trend: loss of dermal skull bones from sarcopterygians to tetrapods and from synapsids to mammals (Sidor 2001); fusion in birds; reduction of the vertebral regions and of tooth number in mammals; reduction of digits in horses. (b) Evidence against generality: increase of bones in the teleost feeding apparatus; hyperphalangy in ichthyosaurs and whales; increase in vertebral number in snakes and eels; new elements (sesamoids, entotympanic). (c) Possible causes: functional integration, developmental economy (late-forming elements are lost by paedomorphosis), fusion under load; but reductions are often irreversible (Dollo's law) and lineages may become trapped in reduced states, which would create a trend without directional selection. (d) Conclusion: a statistical tendency in particular structures and lineages, explained by several processes, not a law in the physical sense.
