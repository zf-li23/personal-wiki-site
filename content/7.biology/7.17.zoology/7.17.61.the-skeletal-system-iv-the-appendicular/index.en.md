The appendicular skeleton comprises the skeleton of the paired appendages, fins in fishes and limbs in tetrapods, and the girdles that connect them to the body: the pectoral girdle in front and the pelvic girdle behind. Median fins, which are not paired, are usually treated with them because they share the same tissues. The appendages are the organs of locomotion of most vertebrates, and their history, from the fin of a shark to the hand of a human or the wing of a bat, is one of the best-documented examples of how one structure can be remodelled for different functions while keeping its basic plan.

That plan is the tetrapod limb with its three segments, the **stylopod**, **zeugopod** and **autopod** ([7.5.31](/7.5.31)), and its serial homology between forelimb and hindlimb ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2.1)). The girdles have a different and more complicated history: the pectoral girdle is a compound of dermal and cartilage bones, like the skull, while the pelvic girdle is purely endochondral. The transition from fins to limbs is described in [7.17.48](/7.17.48#sec-48.3), the development of limbs in [7.5.31](/7.5.31) and [7.5.32](/7.5.32); this chapter compares the structures across the vertebrates and relates them to the ways vertebrates move.

The chapter first discusses the origin of paired fins and the skeleton of fins (§61.1). Section 61.2 follows the pectoral girdle and §61.3 the pelvic girdle from fishes to mammals. Section 61.4 describes the tetrapod limb, its carpals, tarsals and digits, §61.5 posture and gait with a quantitative model of dynamic similarity, and §61.6 the specializations for running, jumping, digging, climbing, swimming and flight, including the three vertebrate wings. Section 61.7 compares the appendicular skeleton across the classes.

## 61.1. Fins

### 61.1.1. The Origin of Paired Fins

Cyclostomes have no paired fins; the earliest paired appendages appear in jawless stem gnathostomes, as pectoral fins in osteostracans and as paired ventrolateral folds in some galeaspids ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.2.3)). Three classical hypotheses have been proposed for their origin.

| Hypothesis | Proposed by | Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gill-arch (archipterygium) hypothesis | Gegenbaur (1870s) | The girdles are modified gill arches and the fins their rays; the pelvic girdle migrated backward | Fins develop from lateral plate mesoderm of the trunk, not from pharyngeal arches; but Gillis et al. (2009) showed that the gill rays of skates are patterned by *Shh* like the fins, suggesting a co-option of the same genetic programme rather than homology of the structures |
| Fin-fold hypothesis | Thacher, Mivart, Balfour (1876–1881) | Paired fins are the remnants of continuous ventrolateral fin folds | Consistent with fossils (*Tujiaaspis* with paired folds; acanthodians with several pairs of intermediate spines) and with development (a lateral ridge of competent tissue, the fin buds at positions set by Hox genes); the most widely accepted framework, in modified form |
| Fin-spine hypothesis | Various | Fins evolved from spines projecting from the body wall, with a web behind them | Acanthodians have paired spines in front of fins, but spines appear in parallel with fins, not as their precursors |

The Chinese textbooks favour the fin-fold hypothesis and state that the girdles arose by the extension of the basal fin skeleton toward the ventral midline, which is still a reasonable summary. Developmental genetics adds that the pectoral and pelvic fins arise at the levels where the Hox code of the lateral plate activates *Tbx5* and *Tbx4* respectively, and that the pelvic fins appeared later in evolution than the pectoral ones: osteostracans have only pectoral fins, and pelvic fins first appear in placoderms.

### 61.1.2. The Skeleton of Paired Fins

The fin skeleton consists of an endoskeleton of cartilage or cartilage bone at the base and a dermal skeleton in the web.

- The *basals* (basipterygia) articulate with the girdle. In chondrichthyans there are typically three in the pectoral fin, the propterygium in front, the mesopterygium in the middle and the metapterygium behind; the metapterygium carries an axis of segments.
- The *radials*, rows of rods distal to the basals, support the fleshy base of the web.
- The *fin rays* stiffen the web: ceratotrichia (unsegmented rays of elastoidin) in chondrichthyans, **lepidotrichia** (jointed bony rays derived from scales) in osteichthyans, with fine actinotrichia at the growing margin ([7.17.57](/7.17.57#sec-57.6.3)).

Three types of paired fin are distinguished by the arrangement of the endoskeleton.

| Type | Skeleton | Occurrence |
|---|---|---|
| Tribasal fin | Three basals (pro-, meso- and metapterygium), radials, ceratotrichia | Sharks and rays |
| Ray fin | Basals reduced or absent; a row of short radials; the lepidotrichia form most of the fin and articulate almost directly with the girdle | Actinopterygians |
| Lobe fin (archipterygium) | A central axis of segments (the metapterygial axis) with radials on one or both sides, inside a fleshy lobe; lepidotrichia at the edge | Sarcopterygians: lungfishes (biserial, radials on both sides), coelacanths, osteolepiforms (uniserial, radials on the preaxial side) |

The fin of tetrapodomorph fishes shows the beginning of the tetrapod limb: the first segment of the metapterygial axis corresponds to the humerus, the second segment and the first preaxial radial to the ulna and radius, and the more distal elements to the wrist ([7.17.48](/7.17.48#sec-48.3)). The digits have no clear counterpart in the fin; their development uses the late phase of *Hoxa13* and *Hoxd13* expression, which in fishes patterns the distal fin and the fin rays ([7.5.32](/7.5.32)). The Chinese textbooks call the chondrichthyan fin a "fin-fold fin" and that of actinopterygians a "ray fin"; the corresponding English names are given in the table.

In teleosts the position of the pelvic fins changed in evolution: abdominal (in the middle of the belly) in primitive teleosts such as carps and salmon, thoracic (below the pectoral fins) in perch-like fishes, and jugular (in front of the pectoral fins) in cods and some others, with the pelvic girdle attached to the cleithrum of the pectoral girdle. The position is an important taxonomic character.

### 61.1.3. Median Fins and Tails

The median fins, the dorsal, anal and caudal fins, are older than the paired fins: lancelets have a continuous median fin fold, and lampreys have dorsal and caudal fins. They are supported by radials (pterygiophores) that interdigitate with the neural and haemal spines, and by fin rays. Their function is mainly to stabilize the body against roll and yaw, and in many fishes to provide or redirect thrust.

The caudal fin has four main forms, distinguished by the relation of the vertebral column to the fin lobes:

- protocercal (diphycercal in the strict sense): the column runs straight to the tip of a symmetrical fin (larval fishes, lampreys; secondarily in lungfishes and coelacanths, often called diphycercal);
- **heterocercal**: the column turns up into a larger upper lobe (sharks, sturgeons, early actinopterygians), producing a lift force at the tail ([7.17.46](/7.17.46));
- abbreviated heterocercal: externally nearly symmetrical, with a short upturned column (gars, bowfin);
- **homocercal**: externally symmetrical, the column ending in a set of modified elements (urostyle, hypurals) at the base of the fin, which is supported mainly by the hypurals and rays (teleosts) ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)).

## 61.2. The Pectoral Girdle

### 61.2.1. Dual Origin

**Definition 61.2.1 (Pectoral and Pelvic Girdles)** The **pectoral girdle** is the skeletal support of the pectoral fins or forelimbs, composed of an endochondral part (the scapulocoracoid, or the scapula, coracoid and procoracoid derived from it) bearing the articulation with the fin or limb (glenoid fossa), and, in osteichthyans, of dermal bones (cleithrum, clavicle, interclavicle, supracleithrum, postcleithrum, anocleithrum and posttemporal) that brace it externally and connect it to the skull. The **pelvic girdle** is the skeletal support of the pelvic fins or hindlimbs, composed only of cartilage or cartilage bone (in tetrapods the ilium, ischium and pubis), bearing the articulation with the limb (acetabulum) and, in tetrapods, connected to the vertebral column through the sacrum.

The endochondral part of the pectoral girdle is present in all gnathostomes; it is the whole pectoral girdle of chondrichthyans. The dermal part is derived from the dermal armour behind the head: in placoderms and early osteichthyans a ring of dermal plates encircled the body at the back of the gill chamber, and these plates became the dermal girdle. The two parts thus have the same relation as the chondrocranium and dermatocranium of the skull.

### 61.2.2. From Fishes to Mammals

| Group | Endochondral elements | Dermal elements | Connection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chondrichthyans | Scapulocoracoid: a U-shaped bar across the ventral side, with scapular processes and glenoid; often a suprascapular cartilage | None | Free in the body wall |
| Actinopterygians (teleosts) | Scapula, coracoid; mesocoracoid (in lower teleosts) | Cleithrum (the largest), supracleithrum, posttemporal, postcleithra; clavicle lost in teleosts | Posttemporal articulates with the skull; the girdle forms the back wall of the gill chamber |
| Sarcopterygian fishes (*Eusthenopteron*) | Small scapulocoracoid on the inner side of the cleithrum | Clavicle, cleithrum, supracleithrum, anocleithrum, posttemporal; interclavicle in some | Attached to the skull |
| Early tetrapods (*Acanthostega*) | Larger scapulocoracoid | Cleithrum, clavicle, interclavicle; posttemporal and supracleithrum lost (from *Tiktaalik*) | Free from the skull; a neck |
| Living amphibians | Scapula with a cartilaginous suprascapula, coracoid, procoracoid; in frogs epicoracoid cartilages | Clavicle (frogs, in front of the procoracoid); cleithrum (frogs, on the suprascapula); salamanders have no dermal elements | Muscles; frogs with a sternum |
| Reptiles | Scapula (and suprascapula in lizards), coracoid (in lizards also an anterior coracoid, in turtles a procoracoid process) | Clavicle and interclavicle (lizards); crocodylians lack clavicles; turtles incorporate clavicles and interclavicle into the plastron | Muscles and the sternum |
| Birds | Long blade-like scapula, strut-like coracoid braced against the sternum | Clavicles fused into the furcula (wishbone); interclavicle incorporated in the furcula's symphysis or lost | Coracoid–sternum joint; triosseal canal for the tendon of the supracoracoideus |
| Monotremes | Scapula, coracoid, procoracoid ("epicoracoid") | Clavicles and interclavicle | Coracoid reaches the sternum, as in reptiles |
| Therian mammals | Scapula with a spine and acromion; coracoid reduced to the coracoid process of the scapula; procoracoid lost | Clavicle (present in primates, bats, rodents, insectivores; reduced or absent in carnivorans, ungulates and other cursorial mammals); interclavicle lost | Muscles only (a sling of serratus and other muscles); the clavicle, when present, braces the shoulder against the sternum |

Three trends run through the table. The dermal part of the girdle was reduced, from five or six bones in fishes to the clavicle alone in therian mammals; the bones that connected the girdle to the skull were lost first, freeing the head. The endochondral part enlarged, especially the scapula, which in mammals carries most of the shoulder muscles and is free to rotate on the thorax, so that it functions as an additional limb segment that lengthens the stride. And the connections of the girdle to the trunk became muscular, so that the forelimbs of a running mammal act as shock-absorbing struts rather than being rigidly attached.

### 61.2.3. Special Cases

The pectoral girdle of frogs takes two forms, which have been used in classification. In the *arciferous* girdle (toads, tree frogs), the epicoracoid cartilages of the two sides overlap and can slide on each other; in the *firmisternal* girdle (ranids, the Chinese true frogs), they are fused in the midline into a rigid bar. The firmisternal girdle is thought to absorb landing forces better in jumping, but both types occur in jumping species, and they have evolved more than once.

The flight apparatus of birds is braced by the strut-like coracoids, which transmit the downward pull of the pectoralis and the reaction forces of the wing to the sternum, and by the furcula, which acts as a spring that is bent during the downstroke and recoils during the upstroke (Jenkins et al. 1988, by cineradiography of a starling). The supracoracoideus, the main elevator of the wing, lies below the wing on the sternum and raises it by a tendon that passes through the triosseal canal between coracoid, scapula and furcula, a pulley that reverses the direction of its pull.

The clavicle of mammals is a good example of the relation between form and locomotion. It is well developed in mammals that use the forelimbs for many movements (climbing, grasping, digging, flying) because it holds the shoulder away from the thorax and allows the limb to move in many directions; it is reduced or absent in runners and jumpers, whose forelimbs move mainly fore and aft, because a free scapula can slide forward and backward on the thorax and lengthen the stride. The clavicle of the cat is a small splint in the muscles, that of the dog a nodule or absent, that of the horse absent; the clavicle of humans, bats and moles is strong.

## 61.3. The Pelvic Girdle

### 61.3.1. Fishes and Early Tetrapods

The pelvic girdle of chondrichthyans is a transverse bar of cartilage across the belly, the puboischiadic bar, with a small iliac process on each side; the girdle of actinopterygians a pair of small endochondral plates (basipterygia), not connected to the vertebral column. In sarcopterygian fishes each half of the girdle is a plate with an acetabulum and a short iliac process.

In early tetrapods each half of the girdle became a broad plate with three parts meeting at the acetabulum: the *ilium* above, extending to the vertebral column; the *pubis* in front and below; and the *ischium* behind and below. The two halves met in a ventral symphysis, and the ilium was attached, through a sacral rib, to one vertebra. With this connection the hindlimbs could push the body forward and support its weight, and the hindlimb became the main propulsive limb in most tetrapods.

### 61.3.2. The Pelvis in Tetrapods

| Group | Pelvic girdle | Connection to the sacrum |
|---|---|---|
| Salamanders | Ilium, ischium (ossified), a puboischiadic plate with a cartilaginous pubis; in front of it a Y-shaped cartilage (ypsiloid cartilage), whose shape differs among species | One sacral vertebra, sometimes variable in position |
| Frogs | Very long ilia extending forward to the sacral vertebra; small ischium and pubis behind, forming a disc with the acetabulum | Movable iliosacral joint, used in jumping and swimming |
| Lizards | Ilium, ischium and pubis with a large puboischiadic fenestra (the thyroid fenestra) between pubis and ischium | Two sacral vertebrae |
| Crocodylians | Ilium; pubis excluded from the acetabulum and movable, used in breathing | Two sacral vertebrae |
| Dinosaurs | Saurischians: pubis pointing forward (triradiate pelvis); ornithischians: pubis rotated backward parallel to the ischium, with a forward prepubic process (tetraradiate pelvis) ([7.17.52](/7.17.52)); perforate acetabulum | Three or more sacral vertebrae |
| Birds | Ilium, ischium and pubis fused with each other and with the synsacrum; pubis directed backward; the two pubes and ischia do not meet ventrally (open pelvis) | Synsacrum |
| Mammals | The three bones fused into the hip bone (os coxae, innominate bone); a large obturator foramen between pubis and ischium; pubic symphysis (closed pelvis); epipubic bones in monotremes and marsupials | Sacrum of three to five fused vertebrae |

The open pelvis of birds is often explained by the passage of large hard-shelled eggs, but it is also found in theropod dinosaurs in which the pubes met distally in a "boot", and its evolution is linked to the backward rotation of the pubis and the reorganization of the hindlimb muscles. The epipubic bones of monotremes and marsupials, once interpreted as supports of the pouch, are present in both sexes and also in pouchless species; they stiffen the trunk during locomotion and breathing (Reilly and White 2003) and were present in early eutherians, so that their loss is a placental feature, perhaps associated with the expansion of the abdomen in long pregnancy.

In whales and sirenians the pelvic girdle is reduced to a pair of small bones in the body wall, no longer connected to the vertebral column. In whales these bones anchor the muscles of the penis, and their size correlates with the intensity of sexual selection among species (Dines et al. 2014), an example of a "vestigial" structure that retains a function.

### 61.3.3. The Human Pelvis and Bipedalism

The human pelvis is one of the most strongly modified structures of the human skeleton. Compared with the pelvis of chimpanzees, the ilia are short and broad and curve forward around the sides of the abdomen, which brings the gluteus medius and minimus to the side of the hip, where they stabilize the pelvis over the stance leg in walking instead of extending the thigh; the sacrum is broad and the sacroiliac joint is close to the hip joint, shortening the lever through which the weight of the trunk acts; and the birth canal is short from front to back and twisted, its inlet wider from side to side and its outlet from front to back. The fossil *Australopithecus afarensis* ("Lucy", about 3.2 million years old) already had a short, broad ilium, showing that bipedal walking preceded the enlargement of the brain by more than a million years.

The narrow, twisted birth canal and the large head of the human newborn make human birth unusually difficult; the fetal head must rotate as it passes through. This conflict between the demands of bipedal walking, which favour a narrow pelvis, and of giving birth to large-brained infants, which favour a wide one, has been called the *obstetric dilemma* (Washburn 1960). It has been used to explain the sexual dimorphism of the human pelvis (wider inlet, larger subpubic angle in women, as the Chinese textbooks describe) and the relatively immature state of human newborns, although metabolic limits on the energy a mother can supply during pregnancy may contribute as much as the pelvis to the timing of birth (Dunsworth et al. 2012).

## 61.4. The Tetrapod Limb

### 61.4.1. The Basic Plan

The tetrapod limb consists of the stylopod (humerus or femur), the zeugopod (radius and ulna, or tibia and fibula) and the autopod, which is subdivided into the basipodium (carpals or tarsals), the metapodium (metacarpals or metatarsals) and the acropodium (phalanges of the digits). The terms come from the development of the limb bud, in which the three segments are specified along the proximodistal axis ([7.5.31](/7.5.31)).

The carpus of a generalized amniote consists of a proximal row of three bones, the radiale, intermedium and ulnare, one or two centralia, a row of five distal carpals, and a pisiform, a sesamoid on the ulnar side. The tarsus is built on the same plan, but in amniotes the proximal row is consolidated into two bones: the *astragalus* (talus), formed by the fusion of the tibiale, intermedium and a centrale, and the *calcaneum*, the former fibulare, which bears a heel process in mammals for the attachment of the Achilles tendon. The astragalus is an amniote synapomorphy ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.1.2)).

The number of phalanges in each digit is expressed as a *phalangeal formula*, from digit I (thumb, hallux) to digit V. The primitive amniote formula is 2-3-4-5-3 in the hand and 2-3-4-5-4 in the foot, retained in many lizards; mammals have 2-3-3-3-3 in both, a reduction that occurred in the cynodont–mammal transition.

### 61.4.2. Polydactyly and the Origin of Five Digits

The earliest tetrapods were polydactyl: *Acanthostega* had eight digits on the hand, *Ichthyostega* seven on the foot, *Tulerpeton* six. Pentadactyly, five digits, became fixed in the stem of crown tetrapods in the Early Carboniferous and has not been exceeded since, except by the hyperdactyly of ichthyosaurs and the "extra digits" (sesamoids) of moles and pandas. This stability, like that of the mammalian neck ([7.17.60](/7.17.60#sec-60.5.2)), suggests a developmental constraint: the digits are specified by a Shh gradient from the posterior margin of the limb bud and by a Turing-type reaction–diffusion mechanism that sets their number and spacing ([7.5.32](/7.5.32)), and increasing their number beyond five may interfere with the specification of digit identity.

Reductions of digit number, in contrast, are common. Living amphibians have four digits on the hand (frogs and most salamanders) and five or fewer on the foot; birds have three digits in the hand; horses one functional digit, artiodactyls two; and many lizards have lost all digits independently.

### 61.4.3. Digits and Ankles of Particular Groups

- Frogs have a radioulna and a tibiofibula, each a single bone formed by fusion; the astragalus and calcaneum (tibiale and fibulare) are elongated into an additional segment of the hindlimb, which lengthens the jumping leg.
- Birds have a fused carpometacarpus with three digits (the alular, major and minor digits) and two free carpals (radiale and ulnare); in the hindlimb the proximal tarsals fuse with the tibia into a tibiotarsus, and the distal tarsals with the fused metatarsals II–IV into a tarsometatarsus, so that the ankle joint lies between the two rows of tarsals (*mesotarsal* or intertarsal joint), and the fibula is a thin splint. The identity of the three digits of the bird hand (I-II-III by their position in the adult, as in theropods, or II-III-IV by their position in the embryo) was long debated; the current resolution is a shift of digit identity relative to position during evolution, the "frame-shift" hypothesis (Wagner and Gauthier 1999; Tamura et al. 2011), with **digit identity** determined by gene expression rather than by position.
- Archosaurs differ in the ankle: crocodylians have a *crurotarsal* ankle, with the joint between the astragalus (attached to the tibia) and the calcaneum (moving with the foot), which allows the foot to rotate in a semi-erect gait; pterosaurs and dinosaurs, including birds, have a *mesotarsal* ankle, a simple hinge between the proximal tarsals (fused to the tibia) and the distal ones, suited to an erect posture with the limbs moving in a parasagittal plane.
- Mammals have a lower ankle joint between astragalus and calcaneum and an upper joint between tibia and astragalus; the calcaneal heel lengthens the lever of the calf muscles. Mammals also have a patella, a sesamoid in the tendon of the quadriceps, which evolved independently in mammals, birds and lizards.

Several mammals form new digit-like elements from sesamoids. The giant panda grasps bamboo with an enlarged radial sesamoid, the "panda's thumb", opposed to the five true digits; moles have an os falciforme beside the thumb that widens the digging hand. These elements are the Chinese textbooks' "sixth finger" of the panda; they are not digits, because they develop as sesamoids in a tendon, without the digit programme of the autopod.

### 61.4.4. The Limbs of the Representative Animals

- *Carp.* Pectoral fins on the girdle behind the gill cover, supported by a few short radials and the lepidotrichia; pelvic fins in the abdominal position, on small free pelvic bones; dorsal fin with a serrated spine in front; homocercal tail.
- *Frog.* Forelimb with humerus, radioulna, six carpals and four digits (a rudimentary prepollex beside the first); hindlimb with femur, tibiofibula, elongated astragalus and calcaneum, small distal tarsals, five toes and a prehallux; arciferous or firmisternal pectoral girdle with clavicles, coracoids, scapulae and suprascapulae; pelvic girdle with long ilia meeting the sacral vertebra.
- *Lizard.* Sprawling pentadactyl limbs with claws; phalangeal formulae 2-3-4-5-3 (hand) and 2-3-4-5-4 (foot); mesotarsal ankle with fused astragalocalcaneum; pectoral girdle with scapula, suprascapula, fenestrated coracoid, clavicles and a T-shaped interclavicle; pelvis with a thyroid fenestra, two sacral vertebrae.
- *Pigeon.* Wing with humerus, radius, ulna, radiale, ulnare, carpometacarpus and three digits; leg with femur, tibiotarsus, fibula, tarsometatarsus and four toes (anisodactyl: three forward, one back) with phalangeal counts 2-3-4-5; pectoral girdle with scapula, coracoid and furcula braced against a keeled sternum; open pelvis fused with the synsacrum.
- *Rabbit.* Plantigrade to digitigrade limbs adapted for hopping: long hindlimbs with fused tibia and fibula distally, long metatarsals and four toes; forelimbs with five digits; a small clavicle; a scapula with spine, acromion and a metacromion; hip bones fused, with a pubic symphysis.

Bird feet are classified by the arrangement of toes: anisodactyl (three toes forward, the hallux back; most perching birds), zygodactyl (II and III forward, I and IV back; parrots, woodpeckers, cuckoos, owls with a reversible toe), heterodactyl (III and IV forward, I and II back; trogons), syndactyl (II–IV forward, partly fused; kingfishers), pamprodactyl (all four forward; swifts, mousebirds), and the reduced feet of ratites (three toes in rheas and emus, two in the ostrich, the only bird with two toes). These types are taxonomic characters and adaptations to perching, climbing and running.

## 61.5. Posture and Gait

### 61.5.1. Sprawling, Semi-Erect and Erect Postures

The posture of a tetrapod, the position of its limbs relative to the body, determines how its weight is supported. In the *sprawling* posture of salamanders and lizards the humerus and femur project sideways and move in a nearly horizontal plane, the body is close to the ground, and the weight must be supported by muscles that hold the elbow and knee joints bent, at a high muscular cost. In the *semi-erect* posture of crocodylians walking on land (the "high walk") the limbs are held partly under the body. In the *erect* posture of mammals, dinosaurs and birds the limbs move in a parasagittal plane beneath the body, and the weight is borne largely along the bones.

The erect posture evolved independently in the synapsid and archosaur lineages. In mammals it involved a rotation of the limbs described in the Chinese textbooks as "limb torsion": the hindlimb rotated forward so that the knee points forward, and the forelimb rotated backward so that the elbow points backward, with the forearm crossed (radius over ulna) so that the hand faces forward in the pronated position. Primates and some other mammals can rotate the radius around the ulna and turn the hand palm upward (supination); most mammals have the forearm fixed in pronation.

### 61.5.2. Gaits and the Froude Number

A gait is a pattern of limb movements, described by the sequence and timing of footfalls. In a *walk* each foot is on the ground for more than half the stride; in a *trot* diagonal limbs move together; in a *gallop* or *bound* the fore and hind pairs move nearly together, with a phase in which all feet are off the ground. Animals change gait at characteristic speeds, and the speeds at which they change scale with size.

R. McNeill Alexander (1976, 1989) explained the scaling by *dynamic similarity*: two animals of different size move in a dynamically similar way when their speeds are such that the ratio of inertial to gravitational forces is the same. The ratio is the Froude number.

**Formula 61.5.1 (Froude Number)** For an animal of leg length $l$ (height of the hip above the ground) moving at speed $v$ under gravitational acceleration $g$, the Froude number is

$$
\mathrm{Fr} = \frac{v^{2}}{g\,l}.
$$
In walking, the body vaults over the stance leg like an inverted pendulum, and the centripetal acceleration $v^{2}/l$ needed to keep it on the arc cannot exceed $g$; walking is therefore impossible above $\mathrm{Fr} = 1$, and humans and other bipeds switch from walking to running at $\mathrm{Fr} \approx 0.5$. Quadrupeds switch from walking to trotting at $\mathrm{Fr} \approx 0.3$–$0.5$ and from trotting to galloping at $\mathrm{Fr} \approx 2$–$3$ (Alexander and Jayes 1983).

**Example 61.5.1** For a human with $l = 0.9\,\mathrm{m}$, the walk–run transition at $\mathrm{Fr} = 0.5$ is predicted at $v = \sqrt{0.5 \times 9.81 \times 0.9} \approx 2.1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, close to the observed $2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. A child with legs half as long changes gait at $2.1/\sqrt{2} \approx 1.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. On the Moon, with $g = 1.62\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, the transition is predicted at $\sqrt{0.5 \times 1.62 \times 0.9} \approx 0.85\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, which is why the Apollo astronauts preferred to hop. A horse with $l = 1.5\,\mathrm{m}$ is predicted to change from trot to gallop at $\mathrm{Fr} = 2.5$, at $v = \sqrt{2.5 \times 9.81 \times 1.5} \approx 6.1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

:::

Alexander (1976) also used the relation between stride length and speed, which is similar in dynamically similar animals, to estimate the speeds of dinosaurs from fossil trackways: the relative stride length $\lambda/l$ (stride length divided by hip height, estimated as about four times the footprint length) is related to the Froude number, and empirically $v \approx 0.25\, g^{0.5}\, \lambda^{1.67}\, l^{-1.17}$. Most dinosaur trackways give walking speeds of $1$–$2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; a few theropod trackways indicate running at up to about $10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

## 61.6. Specializations of the Limbs

### 61.6.1. Running

Cursorial (running) mammals have converged on a set of limb features that increase stride length and stride frequency and reduce the energy cost of moving the limbs.

| Feature | Effect | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Long distal segments (metapodials, zeugopod) relative to the stylopod | Longer stride; distal segments move fast with small muscles | Horses, antelopes, cheetahs, ostriches |
| Digitigrade or unguligrade foot posture | Effectively adds a segment to the limb | Dogs and cats (digitigrade), ungulates (unguligrade) |
| Reduction and fusion of digits and metapodials | Lighter distal limb; a strong single strut | Horse (digit III, cannon bone with splint bones II and IV); artiodactyls (III and IV fused into a cannon bone) |
| Muscles concentrated near the body, distal limb moved by long tendons | Lower moment of inertia of the limb, so it can be swung faster with less energy | Horses, deer |
| Elastic tendons and ligaments | Store and return energy at each stride | The suspensory ligament of horses, the Achilles tendon of kangaroos and humans |
| Joints restricted to the sagittal plane | Stability at speed | Tongue-and-groove joints of ungulate ankles |
| Free scapula, reduced clavicle | Scapula acts as an extra limb segment | Carnivorans, ungulates |

The foot posture of mammals is described by which part of the foot touches the ground (the terms are used in the Chinese textbooks as well): *plantigrade*, with the whole sole, including the heel, on the ground (primates, bears, many rodents, insectivores; the ancestral condition); *digitigrade*, on the digits with the metapodials raised (dogs, cats); and *unguligrade*, on the tips of the digits, capped by hooves (horses, artiodactyls). Each step from plantigrade to unguligrade lengthens the functional limb.

### 61.6.2. Jumping, Digging and Climbing

Jumping animals have long hindlimbs, often with an elongated additional segment: the elongated tarsals and ilia of frogs, the long metatarsals of kangaroos and jerboas, the long tibiotarsus and tarsometatarsus of birds. The force of the jump is produced by the extensor muscles, but in many small jumpers (frogs, bushbabies, fleas among invertebrates) the muscles are too slow, and energy is stored in elastic tendons before the jump and released quickly, a catapult mechanism ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)).

Digging (fossorial) mammals, such as moles, have short, powerful forelimbs with large in-levers ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.5.1)): a broad humerus with large crests for muscles, a long olecranon on the ulna for the triceps, short distal segments, a broad hand with long claws and, in moles, the os falciforme. Their clavicles are strong, and the forelimb is often rotated so that the palm faces outward and the hand pushes soil sideways.

Climbing (arboreal) animals grasp with claws (squirrels, many lizards) or with opposable digits (primates, chameleons, which have two bundles of fused digits, and tree frogs, with adhesive toe pads). Many primates have long arms and mobile shoulders: gibbons swing beneath branches (brachiation) with a fully mobile shoulder joint, a long clavicle and a scapula on the back of the thorax, features that humans inherited from arboreal apes and that make the human shoulder so versatile.

### 61.6.3. Swimming

Tetrapods returned to the water many times, and their limbs became paddles or flippers. In ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs and whales the limbs became stiff hydrofoils with short, flattened proximal bones and many phalanges (*hyperphalangy*: up to 14 or more phalanges per digit in some whales, more than 20 in some ichthyosaurs), sometimes with extra digits (*hyperdactyly* in ichthyosaurs). In seals the hindlimbs, in sea lions the forelimbs, provide thrust. Sea turtles fly under water with long, wing-like forelimbs; penguins and auks do the same with wings whose bones are flattened and stiffened at the joints. Whales lost the external hindlimbs, but a small pelvis and occasionally a femur remain in the body wall (§61.3.2), and early whales such as *Ambulocetus* and *Rodhocetus* document the transition from a running ancestor with hooves ([7.10.32](/7.10.32)).

### 61.6.4. Flight: Three Wings

Powered flight evolved three times in vertebrates, in pterosaurs, birds and bats, and in each case from the forelimb. The three wings are homologous as forelimbs and convergent as wings ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2.2)), and they differ in the way the airfoil is supported.

| Feature | Pterosaurs | Birds | Bats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airfoil | Skin membrane (brachiopatagium) stiffened by actinofibrils, from the tip of digit IV to the hindlimb | Flight feathers (primaries on the hand, secondaries on the ulna) | Skin membrane (patagium) with elastic fibres and muscles, between digits II–V, the body and the hindlimb |
| Hand | Digit IV enormously elongated (wing finger, four phalanges); digits I–III small, clawed; a pteroid bone at the wrist supporting the forewing membrane | Digits fused into the carpometacarpus; three digits (alular, major, minor), few phalanges; the alula, on the first digit, controls airflow at low speed | Digits II–V elongated (metacarpals and phalanges), digit I short, clawed and free |
| Forearm | Radius and ulna both robust | Ulna larger than radius, with quill knobs for the secondaries | Radius long, ulna reduced and fused to it |
| Shoulder and sternum | Keeled sternum; scapulocoracoid fused to the notarium in large forms | Keeled sternum, strut-like coracoid, furcula | Keeled manubrium in some; clavicle strong |
| Hindlimb | Part of the wing; quadrupedal on the ground | Separate from the wing; bipedal | Part of the wing; rotated so that the knee faces backward; hanging by the feet |
| Bone structure | Thin-walled, pneumatic | Pneumatic in many | Not pneumatic; thin distal bones, poorly mineralized to allow flexing |

The differences have functional consequences. The feathered wing of birds can be partly folded and its feathers rotated, so that the upstroke is aerodynamically inactive in slow flight and the wing shape changes with speed; feathers are dead structures that are replaced in molt and can be damaged without impairing the wing's structure. The membranes of bats and pterosaurs are living skin with muscles and sensory receptors, whose camber and tension can be controlled continuously, giving bats exceptional manoeuvrability at low speed; but a torn membrane is a serious injury. The bird wing separates the forelimb from walking, which allowed the bipedal running of the theropod ancestors to continue; bats and pterosaurs, whose hindlimbs are part of the wing, are poor walkers (except for vampire bats and pterosaurs, which walked quadrupedally).

### 61.6.5. Limb Proportions as a Measure of Cursoriality

Because running mammals lengthen the distal segments of their limbs, the ratio of distal to proximal segment lengths is a simple index of cursorial adaptation. The most widely used is the ratio of metatarsal length to femur length (the MT/F ratio). It is about 0.2–0.3 in plantigrade mammals such as bears, humans and many rodents, about 0.4–0.6 in digitigrade carnivorans such as dogs and cats, and about 0.7–1.0 or more in unguligrade runners such as horses, antelopes and deer (values after Garland and Janis 1993). Garland and Janis showed that across mammals the MT/F ratio correlates with maximum running speed, but more weakly than expected once body size and phylogeny are accounted for: elongated metatarsals reduce the cost of locomotion as much as they raise maximum speed, and some fast runners (cheetahs) have only moderately elongated feet. The ratio is nonetheless useful for inferring the locomotor habits of fossil mammals and dinosaurs; the long metatarsals of ornithomimid and tyrannosaurid theropods suggest good runners, the short metatarsals of sauropods a graviportal, slow-moving habit.

### 61.6.6. Limb Reduction and Loss

Limbless or nearly limbless body forms have evolved more than 25 times in squamates, as well as in caecilians, several salamanders (the eel-like *Amphiuma* and *Siren*), amphisbaenians and, among extinct forms, aïstopod lepospondyls. Limb reduction usually proceeds in a regular sequence: digits are lost first, from the posterior or anterior side, then the distal segments, and the forelimbs are often lost before the hindlimbs. In skinks of the genus *Lerista* in Australia the full series from five-toed to limbless species occurs within a single genus. The reduction is associated with elongation of the body, an increase in the number of presacral vertebrae ([7.17.60](/7.17.60)) and life in dense vegetation, sand or soil, where limbs are of little use.

Snakes lost their forelimbs completely, but many retain vestiges of the pelvic girdle and femur, visible in boas and pythons as spurs beside the cloaca. The Cretaceous snakes *Najash* and *Haasiophis* had well-formed hindlimbs. The genetic basis of limb loss in snakes involves the degeneration of an enhancer (the ZRS) of *Shh* that drives its expression in the limb bud: replacing the mouse ZRS with the python sequence produces mice with severely truncated limbs (Kvon et al. 2016), an example of evolution by the loss of a regulatory element rather than of the gene itself, whose other functions are preserved ([7.5.32](/7.5.32)).

### 61.7.2. Trends

The appendicular skeleton shows the clearest relation between form and mode of life in the vertebrate body. In fishes the fins are steering and stabilizing organs and the body provides most of the thrust; the girdles are small and either free in the body wall or attached to the skull. In tetrapods the limbs became the propulsive and supporting organs: the girdles enlarged, the pelvic girdle attached to the column through the sacrum, the pectoral girdle lost its connection to the skull and gained a muscular sling, the limbs moved under the body in mammals and dinosaurs, and the distal segments were modified for the many modes of locomotion that tetrapods evolved.

The Chinese textbooks emphasize that the pectoral girdle, with its dermal components, changes more than the pelvic girdle, which consists of three bones that are present whenever the hindlimbs are present. The comparison confirms this, and adds that the conservatism of the pelvic girdle is relative: the orientation of the pubis, the number of sacral vertebrae, the opening of the pelvis and the fusion of its bones differ among the groups and are among the most important characters for classifying fossil archosaurs and mammals.

## Summary

- Paired fins arose in stem gnathostomes, pectoral fins before pelvic fins; the fin-fold hypothesis, modified by developmental genetics (Hox-dependent *Tbx5*/*Tbx4* fields in the lateral plate), is preferred to the gill-arch hypothesis. Fins have basals, radials and fin rays (ceratotrichia or **lepidotrichia**); fins are tribasal (sharks), ray fins (actinopterygians) or lobe fins (sarcopterygians). Tails are protocercal, **heterocercal**, abbreviated heterocercal, **homocercal** or diphycercal.
- The **pectoral girdle** (Definition 61.2.1) has an endochondral part (scapulocoracoid; scapula, coracoid, procoracoid) and a dermal part (cleithrum, clavicle, interclavicle, supracleithrum, posttemporal); the dermal part was reduced from fishes to mammals, the connection to the skull lost in early tetrapods, and the coracoid reduced to a process in therians. Birds have a furcula and strut-like coracoids.
- The **pelvic girdle** is endochondral: ilium, ischium and pubis, attached to the sacrum in tetrapods; dinosaurs have triradiate (saurischian) or tetraradiate (ornithischian) pelves; birds an open pelvis fused with the synsacrum; mammals a fused hip bone and a closed pelvis.
- The tetrapod limb has a stylopod, zeugopod and autopod; amniotes have an astragalus and calcaneum and a phalangeal formula of 2-3-4-5-3 (hand), mammals 2-3-3-3-3. Early tetrapods had six to eight digits; five became fixed; reductions are frequent. Ankles are crural (mammals), crurotarsal (crocodylians) or mesotarsal (dinosaurs and birds).
- Posture changed from sprawling to erect independently in synapsids and archosaurs. Gait transitions follow dynamic similarity: Formula 61.5.1, $\mathrm{Fr} = v^{2}/(gl)$, with walk–run at $\mathrm{Fr} \approx 0.5$ and trot–gallop at about 2–3.
- Specializations: running (long distal segments, digitigrade and unguligrade feet, digit reduction, proximal muscles, elastic tendons), jumping, digging (large in-levers), climbing, swimming (hyperphalangy), and flight with three convergent wings built on the homologous forelimb of pterosaurs, birds and bats.

## Questions

1. Why is the pectoral girdle described as having a "dual origin", and how does this compare with the skull?

   **Answer** The pectoral girdle combines an endochondral element, the scapulocoracoid, which is present in all gnathostomes and forms the joint with the fin or limb, with dermal bones (cleithrum, clavicle, interclavicle, supracleithrum, posttemporal) that derive from the dermal armour encircling the body behind the head of early fishes and that brace the endochondral part from outside and connect it to the skull. The situation parallels the skull, which combines the endochondral chondrocranium and splanchnocranium with the dermal dermatocranium; in both, the dermal elements were reduced or lost in later lineages while the endochondral elements persisted (in therian mammals the clavicle is the only dermal element left in the girdle). The pelvic girdle, in contrast, has no dermal part.

2. Using the Froude number, predict the speed at which a 40 cm tall (hip height) dog should switch from trotting to galloping, and at which an ostrich with a hip height of 1.2 m should switch from walking to running.

   **Answer** Dog: $v = \sqrt{2.5 \times 9.81 \times 0.4} \approx 3.1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (with $\mathrm{Fr} = 2.5$; the range $\mathrm{Fr} = 2$–$3$ gives $2.8$–$3.4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$). Ostrich: $v = \sqrt{0.5 \times 9.81 \times 1.2} \approx 2.4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The predictions assume dynamic similarity, which holds approximately across mammals and birds; deviations arise from differences in limb design (the ostrich's long distal segments and crouched knee) and from the arbitrary definition of leg length.

3. Compare the ankle joints of a lizard, a crocodile, a chicken and a horse, and relate the differences to posture.

   **Answer** Lizard: a mesotarsal joint between the proximal tarsals (astragalus and calcaneum, fused in many lizards) and the distal tarsals, permitting the flexion and rotation needed in a sprawling gait. Crocodile: a crurotarsal joint between the astragalus, which moves with the tibia, and the calcaneum, which moves with the foot, with a peg-and-socket articulation that allows the foot to rotate relative to the leg as the limb swings in a semi-erect posture. Chicken: a mesotarsal (intertarsal) hinge between the tibiotarsus (proximal tarsals fused to the tibia) and the tarsometatarsus (distal tarsals fused to the metatarsals), allowing movement only in the parasagittal plane, suited to erect bipedal walking. Horse: a crural joint between the tibia and the astragalus with a deep pulley (trochlea) restricting movement to the sagittal plane, and a fused, almost immobile set of lower tarsal joints, suited to erect, fast parasagittal movement.

4. A paleontologist finds an isolated forelimb with an elongated fourth digit and a small bone at the wrist pointing toward the body. What animal is it likely to be, and how would its wing have differed from that of a bat?

   **Answer** A pterosaur: the elongated digit IV (the wing finger) supported the wing membrane, and the pteroid, a bone unique to pterosaurs, supported the forewing membrane (propatagium) in front of the arm. The pterosaur wing was supported by a single digit, so the membrane was stiffened internally by fibres (actinofibrils) and tensioned by the hindlimb; a bat wing is supported by four elongated digits (II–V) that act as ribs and allow the camber and tension of the membrane to be adjusted finger by finger. The pterosaur's digits I–III remained free and clawed for walking and grasping; the bat has only a free thumb. Both wings are homologous with each other as forelimbs and convergent as wings.

5. (Open) The clavicle is absent in horses, reduced in cats and well developed in humans and moles. Develop a functional explanation and propose how you would test it with a comparative data set.

   **Answer** Points to consider: (a) Hypothesis: the clavicle braces the shoulder joint away from the thorax and stabilizes it against forces in many directions; it is favoured in mammals whose forelimbs move in many planes (climbing, grasping, digging, flying, swimming with the forelimbs) and disfavoured in mammals whose forelimbs move mainly fore and aft, because a free scapula can slide and rotate on the thorax, lengthening the stride and absorbing shocks. (b) Test: compile clavicle presence and relative size for a large sample of mammals with known locomotor modes; use phylogenetic comparative methods (phylogenetic logistic regression or independent contrasts, [7.10.2](/7.10.2)) to test for an association between cursoriality and clavicle loss while controlling for shared ancestry; count independent losses on the tree. (c) Predictions: repeated, independent losses in cursorial lineages (carnivorans, ungulates, lagomorphs), retention in arboreal, fossorial and volant ones. (d) Complications: the clavicle may be reduced to a ligament or a nodule rather than lost; body size and the role of the clavicle in muscle attachment may confound the pattern.
