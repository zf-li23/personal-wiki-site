Insects are the most species-rich group of organisms on Earth: about one million species have been described, more than all other animals together, and the true number is several times larger. This chapter opens the part of the course devoted to entomology (7.17.22–7.17.40). It defines the hexapods and the insects by their shared characters, places them on the arthropod tree, introduces the three small wingless orders that are hexapods but not insects, and gives a first map of insect classification that later chapters fill in. It then asks two quantitative questions that have shaped modern biodiversity science: how many insect species exist, and why there are so many.

The arthropod body plan (segmentation, tagmata, the jointed **exoskeleton** and its **cuticle**, appendages, **molting**) is treated in [7.17.18](/7.17.18) and is assumed here. External anatomy follows in [7.17.23](/7.17.23) and [7.17.24](/7.17.24), flight in [7.17.25](/7.17.25), internal anatomy in [7.17.26](/7.17.26); the phylogeny and fossil history of insects are in [7.17.30](/7.17.30) and the orders in [7.17.31](/7.17.31)–[7.17.36](/7.17.36). The place of arthropods on the animal tree is in [7.17.6](/7.17.6).

## 22.1. What Is an Insect?

### 22.1.1. Hexapods

**Definition 22.1.1 (Hexapoda)** The **Hexapoda** are the arthropods whose body is divided into three **tagmata**, a head, a thorax of exactly three segments each bearing one pair of uniramous walking legs, and an abdomen of at most eleven segments (plus a telson in the embryo) without walking legs; the head bears one pair of antennae and three pairs of mouthpart appendages (mandibles, maxillae and a labium formed by the fused second maxillae).

The thorax fixed at three leg-bearing segments is the defining apomorphy of the group, and it gives the hexapods their name. Other characters of the ground plan are:

- a head of six embryonic segments (the labral or ocular region, the antennal, intercalary, mandibular, maxillary and labial segments), fused into a capsule; the intercalary segment, which carries the second antennae in crustaceans, has no appendage in hexapods;
- legs of six articles: coxa, trochanter, femur, tibia, tarsus and pretarsus (with claws);
- an internal head skeleton, the tentorium, formed by invaginations of the head wall;
- gas exchange through a **tracheal system** opening by spiracles;
- excretion by **Malpighian tubules** that grow out of the ectodermal hindgut (the arachnid tubules are endodermal and convergent);
- a large fat body, mainly in the abdomen;
- gonopores at the end of the abdomen (on segments 7, 8 or 9 in insects).

Hexapods are primarily terrestrial: all aquatic hexapods (mayfly and dragonfly nymphs, water beetles, mosquito larvae) are secondarily aquatic and breathe either with a modified tracheal system or with air carried down from the surface ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)). The oldest undisputed hexapod fossils are springtails from the Early Devonian Rhynie chert (about 410 Ma); molecular clocks place the origin of hexapods in the Ordovician and that of insects near the Silurian ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

### 22.1.2. Insects

Not every hexapod is an insect. Three small groups of soil animals, the springtails, proturans and diplurans, keep their mouthparts in a pocket of the head (§22.2); the remaining hexapods have exposed mouthparts and a set of further innovations.

**Definition 22.1.2 (Insecta)** The **Insecta** (Ectognatha) are the hexapods whose mouthparts project freely from the head capsule (ectognathy), whose antenna has muscles only in the basal segment (scape), with an unmuscled, annulated flagellum and a chordotonal organ (Johnston's organ) in the second segment (pedicel), and whose females have an ovipositor formed by appendages of abdominal segments 8 and 9; a median caudal filament between the two cerci and tarsi subdivided into tarsomeres also belong to the ground plan.

The antenna is a good example of how a small structural change opens a large functional field. In the entognathan orders every antennal segment has its own muscles, as in a leg. In insects the flagellum is moved only as a whole from its base, and the bending of the flagellum against the pedicel is measured by **Johnston's organ**; the antenna becomes a sensitive mechanical sensor of air flow, sound and gravity ([7.17.23](/7.17.23), [7.17.27](/7.17.27)). The **ovipositor** allowed eggs to be inserted into soil, plant tissue or other animals ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)); it was later lost or remodelled in many orders (the sting of bees and wasps is an ovipositor).

Most living insects have wings as adults. The **Pterygota** (winged insects) are defined in [7.17.30](/7.17.30); the two primitively wingless insect orders, the jumping bristletails (Archaeognatha) and the silverfish (Zygentoma), were once united as "Apterygota" ([7.17.31](/7.17.31)), which is a **paraphyletic group** defined only by the absence of wings. Wingless species within the Pterygota (fleas, lice, worker ants, many scale insects) have lost their wings secondarily ([7.17.25](/7.17.25)).

### 22.1.3. A Representative Insect: the Migratory Locust

The East Asian migratory locust, *Locusta migratoria manilensis* (Orthoptera, Acrididae), the classic representative insect of Chinese textbooks and a historically devastating pest of cereals in China, shows the insect body plan with few specializations. ### 22.1.4. Hexapods among the Arthropods

Older classifications, including the textbooks on which many readers learned zoology, grouped hexapods with myriapods as "Uniramia" or "Atelocerata" (antennae of one pair, uniramous legs, tracheae, Malpighian tubules). Molecular phylogenies and the structure of the compound eye and nervous system have overturned this: hexapods are nested within the crustaceans, forming the clade **Pancrustacea** (Tetraconata), and the similarities with myriapods are convergent adaptations to life on land ([7.17.18](/7.17.18), [7.17.20](/7.17.20)). Shared derived characters of Pancrustacea include ommatidia of **compound eyes** with four crystalline-cone cells, and particular neuroblast and brain structures. Among living crustaceans the closest relatives of hexapods are probably the Remipedia (blind cave crustaceans), alone or together with the Cephalocarida ([7.17.21](/7.17.21)); since **Crustacea** in the traditional sense then excludes hexapods, it is paraphyletic.

The crustacean ancestry explains several hexapod structures. The labium corresponds to the second maxillae of crustaceans, fused in the midline. The intercalary segment of the hexapod head, without appendages, corresponds to the segment that bears the second antennae in crustaceans. The insect wing may contain material of a crustacean leg exite ([7.17.25](/7.17.25)).

## 22.2. The Entognathan Orders

**Definition 22.2.1 (Entognatha)** The **Entognatha** are the hexapods whose mandibles and maxillae are withdrawn into a pouch of the head (the gnathal pouch) formed by oral folds that fuse with the labium, so that only their tips protrude; they comprise the orders Collembola, Protura and Diplura.

Entognathans are small (mostly 0.5–5 mm, some diplurans to 50 mm), wingless and live in soil, leaf litter and similar moist habitats. Their development is ametabolous: juveniles resemble adults and continue to molt after sexual maturity. They lack the insect antenna (in Protura antennae are absent altogether) and the ovipositor.

Whether Entognatha is a **clade** is not settled. Entognathy itself may have evolved more than once (the gnathal pouches of Diplura differ in detail from those of Collembola and Protura), and some analyses place Diplura as the **sister group** of Insecta. Phylogenomic studies mostly recover Entognatha as monophyletic, but differ on the internal arrangement: Collembola + Protura ("Ellipura", supported by morphology) or Protura + Diplura ("Nonoculata", eyeless, supported by several molecular data sets).

### 22.2.1. Springtails (Collembola)

**Definition 22.2.2 (Collembola)** **Collembola** (springtails) are entognathous hexapods with an abdomen of only six segments that bears a ventral tube (collophore) on the first segment, a catch (retinaculum) on the third and a jumping organ (furcula) on the fourth.

Springtails are among the most abundant animals in soil: densities of $10^4$–$10^5$ individuals per square meter are common, and hundreds of thousands per square meter occur in some forest and tundra soils. About 6000–9000 species are described. They feed on fungal hyphae, decaying plant matter, bacteria and algae and are important in decomposition and in the dispersal of fungal spores. The ventral tube, the "glue peg" that gave the order its name (Greek *colla*, glue, *embolon*, peg), takes up water and ions and helps the animal adhere to smooth surfaces. Many species lack tracheae and breathe through the cuticle, and Malpighian tubules are absent. A postantennal organ behind each antenna is a sensory organ of uncertain function, and the eyes, where present, are clusters of at most eight ommatidia on each side.

At rest the forked furcula is folded forward under the abdomen and held by the retinaculum. Hemolymph pressure and elastic energy stored in the cuticle build up tension; when the catch is released the furcula strikes the ground and throws the animal into the air, often tumbling. The jump is an escape from predators such as mites, pseudoscorpions and ants.

**Example 22.2.1** How large is the acceleration of a springtail jump? A jump of height $h$ needs a take-off speed $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. If the furcula pushes against the ground over a distance $d$ at constant acceleration, then

$$
a = \frac{v^2}{2d} = \frac{gh}{d}, \qquad t = \frac{2d}{v}.
$$

For $h = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$ (the order of magnitude reported for larger springtails) and $d = 0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ (the length of the furcula of a 2 mm animal), $v \approx 1.4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $a \approx 200g$ and $t \approx 0.7\,\mathrm{ms}$. This calculation ignores air resistance, which for an animal a few millimeters long reduces the height reached considerably; the real take-off speed must therefore be somewhat higher. Muscle cannot shorten fast enough to deliver such power in less than a millisecond ([7.14.10](/7.14.10)); like fleas, froghoppers and click beetles, springtails use a catapult in which slowly loaded elastic energy is released by a catch.

### 22.2.2. Proturans (Protura)

**Definition 22.2.3 (Protura)** **Protura** are minute (below 2 mm), pale entognathous hexapods without antennae or eyes, which walk on the middle and hind legs and hold the sensilla-bearing forelegs forward as feelers, and whose abdomen grows by the addition of segments at molts from nine to twelve segments.

The post-embryonic addition of segments (anamorphic development) is unique among hexapods and recalls myriapods. Proturans have small appendages (styli) on the first three abdominal segments and no cerci; tracheae are present in some families. About 800 species are known. They live in moist soil and litter and are thought to feed on mycorrhizal fungi.

### 22.2.3. Diplurans (Diplura)

**Definition 22.2.4 (Diplura)** **Diplura** are eyeless entognathous hexapods with long moniliform antennae, styli and eversible vesicles on several abdominal segments, and a pair of cerci that are either long and filiform or short and forceps-like.

The two main families differ in their cerci: Campodeidae (such as *Campodea*) have long, many-segmented cerci and are soft-bodied scavengers and fungivores; Japygidae have sclerotized, forceps-like cerci used to seize small arthropods, and some exceed 5 cm. Diplurans have relatively well-developed tracheae, and the abdominal styli are generally interpreted as remnants of abdominal limbs. About 1000 species are known.

## 22.3. A First Map of Insect Diversity

### 22.3.1. The Major Lineages

Within the Insecta the first split separates the jumping bristletails (Archaeognatha), whose mandibles articulate with the head by a single condyle, from the Dicondylia, whose mandibles have two articulations and move like a hinge: the silverfish (Zygentoma) and the winged insects. The winged insects are traditionally divided into the **Palaeoptera** (mayflies and dragonflies, which cannot fold their wings over the abdomen) and the **Neoptera**, in which a special sclerite at the wing base allows the wings to be folded back. Within the Neoptera three large clades are recognized:

- **Polyneoptera**: stoneflies, earwigs, grasshoppers and crickets, stick insects, mantises, cockroaches and termites, and several small orders; mostly chewing insects with an enlarged anal fan in the hindwing;
- **Paraneoptera** (Acercaria): bark lice and lice, thrips, and the true bugs, cicadas, aphids and scale insects (Hemiptera); mouthparts often modified for sucking;
- **Holometabola**: insects with a pupal stage, among them the four largest orders, beetles, flies, moths and butterflies, and wasps, bees and ants.

These names are defined, and the evidence for them is discussed, in [7.17.30](/7.17.30); the orders are described in [7.17.31](/7.17.31)–[7.17.36](/7.17.36). Most current classifications recognize 28 living insect orders, which together with the three entognathan orders gives 31 hexapod orders, as many as there are animal phyla. The figure below shows how unevenly species are distributed among them.

### 22.3.2. Correspondence with Older Classifications

Classifications of insects in older textbooks differ from the current one mainly in four ways. (1) The winged insects were split into Exopterygota (wings develop externally, hemimetabolous) and Endopterygota (wings develop internally, holometabolous); the Endopterygota are the Holometabola, but the Exopterygota are paraphyletic. (2) Termites formed the order Isoptera; they are now a subgroup of the cockroaches (Blattodea), nested among wood-eating cockroaches. (3) Sucking lice (Anoplura) and chewing lice (Mallophaga) formed one or two orders separate from the bark lice (Psocoptera); lice arose within the bark lice, and the combined order is Psocodea. (4) Cicadas, leafhoppers, aphids and scale insects formed the order Homoptera; they are paraphyletic with respect to the true bugs (Heteroptera), and both belong to Hemiptera. The diagnostic structures used throughout, mouthparts, wings and type of metamorphosis, remain the same; only the grouping has changed.

## 22.4. How Many Insect Species Are There?

### 22.4.1. Described Species

About one million hexapod species have been described (Brusca and coauthors estimate about 996,000 for 2023). Since Linnaeus's tenth edition of *Systema Naturae* (1758) about 3500 new species have been described per year on average, and in recent decades about 7000 per year. Beetles alone account for about 387,000 species, more than a quarter of all described animals; a single beetle family, the weevils (Curculionidae), has about 65,000 described species, nearly 5% of all known animals. Most undescribed species are small, tropical and in megadiverse groups such as parasitoid wasps, flies, beetles and moths.

### 22.4.2. Erwin's Extrapolation

In 1982 Terry Erwin fogged the canopies of 19 individuals of the tropical tree *Luehea seemannii* in Panama with insecticide and collected about 1200 beetle species. His estimate of the total number of tropical arthropod species is a chain of multiplications:

1. Of the 1200 beetle species, he estimated that 163 were host-specific to this tree species (13.6%).
2. Multiplying by about 50,000 species of tropical trees gives $163 \times 50{,}000 \approx 8.2$ million canopy beetle species specific to trees.
3. If beetles are 40% of canopy arthropod species, canopy arthropods number about 20 million.
4. If the canopy holds two thirds of the arthropod species of a forest, the total is about 30 million.

The estimate was enormously influential, and it is wrong in an instructive way: every factor is uncertain and their errors multiply. Later work showed that host specificity is much lower than Erwin assumed. Many "specific" beetles are found on several related trees, and specificity should be measured as effective specificity, weighting each beetle by how strongly it is concentrated on one host. The fraction of beetles among canopy arthropods and the ratio of canopy to ground fauna also vary. When each factor is varied over a range of plausible values, the product spreads over an order of magnitude, and the final distribution is skewed: a few high values of several factors together produce very large totals.

![Erwin's extrapolation as a Monte Carlo calculation. Each simulation draws the five factors of the chain (beetle species per tree species, effective host-specific fraction, number of tropical tree species, beetles as a fraction of canopy arthropods, canopy share of the total fauna) from uniform distributions over the ranges shown, which are illustrative assumptions spanning values discussed by later authors rather than measurements, and multiplies them. The histogram of 200,000 products is shown on a logarithmic axis, with the median and the 5% and 95% quantiles (dashed). Erwin's point values give about 30 million; the green band is the estimate of Stork (2018) for insects alone, 5.5 million (range 2.6–7.8 million), which rests on independent methods. The host-specific fraction dominates the uncertainty: it alone spans a factor of seven.](./plots/erwin-extrapolation.py)

Several other approaches have since been used: scaling from well-known groups (if the ratio of described to estimated species is known for butterflies in one region, apply it to beetles); the rate at which known species are found in new samples; and taxonomists' expert judgement. They converge on a narrower range. Stork (2018) summarized them as about 5.5 million insect species (range 2.6–7.8 million) and about 7 million species of terrestrial arthropods, of which about 1.5 million are beetles. Thus perhaps only one insect species in five has a name. The argument shows a general point about multiplicative estimates: on a logarithmic scale the errors of the factors add, so the factor with the widest relative range must be measured first.

### 22.4.3. Abundance and Biomass

Insects are as remarkable in numbers as in species. Estimates of the number of insects alive at any time are of the order of $10^{18}$–$10^{19}$, about $10^8$ per human; E. O. Wilson estimated $10^{15}$ ants alone. In tropical forests ants and termites make up a large share of the animal biomass, and in many terrestrial ecosystems insects are the main herbivores, predators of other invertebrates, decomposers of wood and dung, and food of birds, bats, lizards and amphibians.

## 22.5. Why Are There So Many Insects?

### 22.5.1. Candidate Explanations

No single cause explains insect diversity; the candidates are features that may have raised speciation rates, lowered extinction rates, or allowed insects to divide resources more finely. The evidence, from fossils and from comparisons of sister clades, is discussed in [7.17.30](/7.17.30) and in the evolutionary background of [7.10.31](/7.10.31).

| Feature | Proposed effect | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Small body size | Many niches in one habitat (one leaf can hold a miner, a galler, a sap feeder and their parasitoids); small populations become isolated easily | Most insects are a few millimeters long |
| Exoskeleton with waterproof cuticle | Protection and resistance to desiccation on land ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)) | Shared with other arthropods |
| Flight | Escape, dispersal, colonization of patchy resources ([7.17.25](/7.17.25)) | Insects were the first flying animals |
| Wing folding (Neoptera) | Access to narrow spaces while keeping wings | About 98% of insect species are neopterans |
| Complete metamorphosis | Larva and adult exploit different resources and habitats ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)) | Holometabola contain about 80% of insect species |
| Herbivory and coevolution with flowering plants | Specialization on hosts, diversification with angiosperms ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)) | Phytophagous clades are often richer than their non-phytophagous sister clades |
| Low extinction | Many insect families have persisted since the Mesozoic | Insects passed several mass extinctions with few family-level losses |

Four of these have been proposed as **key innovations**: flight, wing folding, complete metamorphosis and plant feeding. A key innovation is tested by comparing species numbers of clades that have the trait with those of their sister clades that lack it; the comparison must be replicated across independent origins, because a single pair of clades can differ in size for any reason. For phytophagy such replication exists (Mitter, Farrell and Wiegmann 1988 found that in most of 13 comparisons the plant-feeding clade was more species-rich); for wings and metamorphosis each trait arose only once, and the test is weaker ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

### 22.5.2. Why Beetles?

J. B. S. Haldane is said to have answered a question about what biology reveals of the Creator with "an inordinate fondness for beetles". Several factors have been proposed for beetle diversity: hardened forewings (elytra) that protect the folded hindwings and the body and allow life in soil, wood and under bark; a long history, with many lineages surviving since the Jurassic and few extinctions (Hunt and coauthors 2007); and repeated radiations of plant feeders, especially weevils and leaf beetles on flowering plants (Farrell 1998). Beetles are therefore diverse probably because they accumulated lineages for a long time, rather than because they speciate unusually fast ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)).

## 22.6. Insects and People

### 22.6.1. Benefits and Harm

Insects provide services on which human life depends. About three quarters of the leading food crops benefit from animal pollination, mainly by insects, accounting for about a third of global crop production by volume (Klein and coauthors 2007). Insects decompose dead wood, leaf litter, dung and carcasses, aerate soils, disperse seeds (more than 3000 plant species are dispersed by ants), and control other insects as predators and parasitoids. Silk (*Bombyx mori*), honey and wax, shellac and carmine are insect products; insects are eaten by about two billion people, and they are increasingly used as feed and in waste conversion.

On the other side, herbivorous insects destroy a substantial fraction of potential harvests, in the field and in storage; locust plagues have shaped the history of agriculture in China and Africa. Blood-feeding insects transmit some of the most serious infectious diseases: malaria (*Anopheles*), dengue, Zika and yellow fever (*Aedes*), lymphatic filariasis, sleeping sickness (tsetse flies), Chagas disease (triatomine bugs) and plague (fleas). The **vectors**, pest management and insect conservation are the subject of [7.17.40](/7.17.40).

Several insects are major model organisms: *Drosophila melanogaster* for genetics and development ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)), the honey bee for learning and social behavior, the silkworm for endocrinology, the locust and cockroach for neurophysiology.

### 22.6.2. The Branches of Entomology

Entomology, the study of insects, covers every level of biology. Insect morphology and anatomy describe structure and homology ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)–[7.17.26](/7.17.26)); insect physiology studies function (in this site mainly within [7.14](/7.14)); insect systematics names, classifies and reconstructs the phylogeny of insects ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)–[7.17.36](/7.17.36)); insect ecology studies populations and communities, and insect behavior includes communication and social life ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)–[7.17.39](/7.17.39)). The applied branches are agricultural and forest entomology, medical and veterinary entomology, and forensic entomology, which estimates the time since death from the succession of insects on a corpse ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)). Closely related disciplines are acarology (mites and ticks, which are arachnids, [7.17.19](/7.17.19)) and arachnology.

## Summary

## Questions

1. Which characters distinguish an insect from a springtail? Why is it misleading to call springtails "primitive insects"?

   **Answer** Springtails are hexapods (three tagmata, six legs, labium, tentorium), but not insects: their mouthparts are withdrawn into a head pouch (entognathy), every antennal segment is muscled, and they have no Johnston's organ, no ovipositor, no median caudal filament, and an abdomen of only six segments with collophore, retinaculum and furcula. "Primitive insect" suggests an ancestor of insects, but springtails are a separate lineage as old as the insects, with many specializations of their own (the jumping apparatus, the reduced abdomen, the ventral tube). They retain some ancestral hexapod characters (ametaboly, molting as adults) and have lost others.

2. Explain why the similarities between hexapods and myriapods (one pair of antennae, uniramous legs, tracheae, Malpighian tubules) do not show that they are each other's closest relatives.

   **Answer** Uniramous legs and a single pair of antennae are absences or reductions that can arise many times; tracheae and Malpighian tubules are adaptations to air breathing and water conservation on land, which each lineage needed when it left the sea and which evolved independently (their detailed structure and developmental origin differ, and arachnids also evolved tracheae and Malpighian tubules convergently). Molecular phylogenies, the four-cone ommatidia and neural characters place hexapods within Pancrustacea, so the shared terrestrial characters are homoplasies.

3. A colleague repeats Erwin's calculation with a host-specific fraction of 2.5% instead of 13.6% and keeps the other factors. What is the new estimate? Which factor would you measure first to reduce the uncertainty, and why?

   **Answer** The estimate scales linearly with the fraction: $30 \times 2.5/13.6 \approx 5.5$ million, close to current estimates. In a product, the relative uncertainty of the result is roughly the sum of the relative uncertainties of the factors on a logarithmic scale, so the factor with the widest relative range (host specificity, here spanning about a factor of seven, against factors of 1.5–2 for the others) dominates and should be measured first, for example by sampling the same beetle species across many tree species and computing effective specificity.

4. (Calculation) A click beetle 1 cm long rises 30 cm after its click, and the thoracic hinge accelerates the body over about 0.6 mm. Estimate the take-off speed and the mean acceleration. Why does it need a catch mechanism?

   **Answer** $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.3} \approx 2.4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; $a = v^2/(2d) \approx 5.9/0.0012 \approx 4900\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \approx 500g$; the push lasts $t = 2d/v \approx 0.5\,\mathrm{ms}$. Muscle cannot complete a contraction in half a millisecond, and the power required per unit muscle mass exceeds what muscle can deliver. A catch lets the muscle contract slowly against a locked joint, storing elastic energy in cuticle (often with resilin) that is released almost instantaneously when the catch slips.

5. Why is a comparison of species numbers between one clade with complete metamorphosis and its sister clade weak evidence that metamorphosis is a key innovation? What would make it stronger?

   **Answer** With a single origin there is only one comparison, and the two clades may differ in species number for many reasons: chance (birth–death processes produce very unequal sister clades), other traits that arose on the same branch, or differences in age and extinction history. Evidence becomes stronger when the trait has arisen independently many times and the trait-bearing clade is richer in most replicate pairs (a sign test), as for phytophagy, or when diversification models fitted to a dated phylogeny show a shift in rates associated with the trait while accounting for other traits ([7.10.31](/7.10.31)).

6. (Open question) The migratory locust and *Drosophila melanogaster* are both representative insects in textbooks. List three ways in which each departs from the insect ground plan.

   **Answer** Locust: the hindlegs are saltatorial with an enlarged femur; the forewings are tegmina; the female ovipositor is a pair of short digging valves rather than a long blade; there are tympanal organs on the first abdominal segment; locusts show density-dependent phases. *Drosophila*: complete metamorphosis with a pupa (the ground plan of insects is ametabolous); the hindwings are reduced to halteres; the antenna is aristate with a reduced flagellum; the mouthparts are sponging, without mandibles; the abdomen has fewer visible segments, with the terminal segments forming the genitalia. Both have lost the median caudal filament.
