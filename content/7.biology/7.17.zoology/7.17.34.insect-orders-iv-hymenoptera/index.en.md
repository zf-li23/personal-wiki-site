The **Hymenoptera** (sawflies, wasps, ants and bees) are one of the four great holometabolous orders, with about 150,000 described species and probably several times as many undescribed, most of them small parasitoid wasps. They include the most important pollinators of flowering plants, the most diverse natural enemies of other insects, and three of the four independent origins of advanced **eusociality** in insects (ants, bees, social wasps; the fourth is in termites). The order is old: sawflies of the family Xyelidae occur in the Late Triassic, more than 220 million years ago, and the living xyelids still resemble them closely.

Molecular phylogenies place the Hymenoptera as the sister group of all other **Holometabola** ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)). The history of the order can be read as a sequence of innovations, each opening a new way of life: plant-feeding larvae with a saw-like **ovipositor** (sawflies); a wood-boring life with fungal symbionts (wood wasps); a parasitoid larva feeding on another insect, followed by the narrow "wasp waist" that frees the ovipositor to probe in all directions (Apocrita); the transformation of the ovipositor into a venom-injecting sting (Aculeata); nest building and provisioning of the young with paralysed prey; pollen feeding in bees; and, several times, colonies with a reproductive division of labour. This chapter follows that sequence. Social organization is treated in [7.17.38](/7.17.38), parasitoid–host dynamics in [7.17.39](/7.17.39), and figs and fig wasps in [7.17.37](/7.17.37).

## 34.1. Characters of the Hymenoptera

### 34.1.1. Diagnosis

**Definition 34.1.1 (Hymenoptera)** The **Hymenoptera** are holometabolous insects with two pairs of membranous wings, the hind wings smaller and coupled to the forewings by a row of hooks (**hamuli**) on their costal margin, and with reduced venation enclosing a few large cells; chewing or chewing-lapping mouthparts with functional mandibles; three ocelli; a female ovipositor formed by the appendages of abdominal segments 8 and 9 and modified for sawing, drilling or stinging; the first abdominal segment usually fused with the metathorax; no cerci or very short ones; haplodiploid sex determination; and larvae either caterpillar-like (sawflies) or legless (Apocrita); about 150,000 described species.

Hymenopteran wings are held flat over the abdomen at rest, or folded lengthwise in the Vespidae. The forewing usually has a dark **pterostigma** on the anterior margin, behind which lie a marginal cell and one to three submarginal cells; venation is reduced in small species, and in many chalcid wasps only a single short vein remains along the front margin, the wing surface carrying rows of microtrichia. This reduction is a scaling effect: at the low **Reynolds numbers** of insects 1 mm long, the wing works more like a paddle than an aerofoil, and fine fringes of setae replace membrane in the smallest species, as in thrips ([7.17.32](/7.17.32)) and featherwing beetles ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)). Coupling by hamuli makes the two wings on each side act as one surface, so that the functional flight system is two-winged, as in flies, though the hind wing is retained.

The mouthparts keep functional **mandibles** in all groups: sawflies chew leaves, wasps chew prey and build nests of paper or mud, ants carry brood and dig, and bees manipulate wax, resin and pollen. In bees and many wasps the **maxillae** and **labium** form a labiomaxillary complex that can be swung forward and retracted; in long-tongued bees the glossa is elongated and hairy and laps nectar in a combination of capillary loading and rapid protraction, the **chewing-lapping mouthparts** of [7.17.23](/7.17.23).

Antennae are filiform in sawflies and many parasitoids, often with more than 20 segments, and become elbowed (geniculate) in ants, many chalcid wasps and bees, with a long scape. In the Aculeata the number of antennal segments is nearly constant: 12 in females and 13 in males. Many parasitoids have a second, small trochanter-like ring at the base of the femur (the trochantellus), formerly described as a two-segmented trochanter.

### 34.1.2. Haplodiploidy and Sex Determination

All Hymenoptera are haplodiploid: males develop from unfertilized eggs and are haploid, females from fertilized eggs and are diploid (arrhenotoky, [7.17.28](/7.17.28)). **Haplodiploidy** has three consequences that run through the biology of the order.

First, a mated female controls the sex of each egg by releasing or withholding sperm from her **spermatheca** as the egg passes. She can therefore adjust the **sex ratio** of her offspring to circumstances, and parasitoid wasps do so with remarkable precision. Second, sisters share on average three quarters of their genes by descent through their haploid father, a relatedness asymmetry that W. D. Hamilton connected with the repeated origins of eusociality; the argument and its limits are treated in [7.17.38](/7.17.38) and [7.10.16](/7.10.16). Third, deleterious recessive alleles are exposed in haploid males and purged efficiently, so that inbreeding is less costly in many hymenopterans than in diploid insects.

The molecular basis of sex determination differs among groups. In the honey bee and many other species sex is set by *complementary sex determination*: individuals heterozygous at a single locus, *csd* in *Apis mellifera* (Beye and coauthors 2003), develop as females, and hemizygous (haploid) or homozygous individuals develop as males. Under inbreeding, homozygous diploid males arise; in honey bees they are eaten by workers soon after hatching, and in small, inbred populations of bees they reduce colony growth. The jewel wasp *Nasonia vitripennis*, which routinely mates with its brothers, has no complementary sex determination; there a maternally imprinted signal decides whether a fertilized egg becomes female. Some species have lost males altogether and reproduce by thelytoky, in several *Trichogramma* and other parasitoids because of infection by *Wolbachia* bacteria ([7.17.28](/7.17.28)); workers of the Cape honey bee *Apis mellifera capensis* lay unfertilized eggs that develop into females.

### 34.1.3. Local Mate Competition

Hamilton (1967) explained why many parasitoid wasps produce strongly female-biased broods. In Fisher's theory a 1 : 1 sex ratio is stable because each sex provides half of the genes of the next generation ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)). The argument assumes that sons compete for mates in a large population. If, however, the offspring of a few females mate with one another in the patch where they develop, before the mated daughters disperse, then sons compete with their own brothers for their sisters: an extra son adds little to the mother's fitness, and an extra daughter adds a whole mated disperser. This is **local mate competition**.

With $n$ foundresses laying eggs in a patch, the evolutionarily stable proportion of sons is

$$
x^* = \frac{n-1}{2n} \quad \text{(diploids)}, \qquad x^* = \frac{(n-1)(2n-1)}{n(4n-1)} \quad \text{(haplodiploids)}.
$$

Here $n$ measures how local mating is: with one foundress, the stable brood consists of just enough sons to inseminate all their sisters, and as $n$ grows the advantage of sons approaches that in a large random-mating population and $x^*$ approaches 1/2. The haplodiploid value is lower than the diploid one, because in a haplodiploid a mother transmits her genes through daughters more efficiently relative to sons when sibling matings occur (with sibling mating a mother's sons-in-law are her own sons, so the sperm stored by a daughter also carries the mother's genes, whereas a son's genes reach the next generation only through his mates).

![The evolutionarily stable proportion of sons under local mate competition as a function of the number of foundresses laying eggs in a patch (theory: Hamilton 1967 for diploids; Hamilton 1979 and Taylor and Bulmer 1980 for haplodiploids). With one foundress the stable brood has only a few sons, enough to inseminate their sisters; as the number of foundresses rises, the stable proportion approaches the Fisherian 1 : 1 ratio. The curve for haplodiploids lies below that for diploids. Curves are theory, not data.](./plots/lmc-sex-ratio.py)

The prediction has been tested many times. Females of *Nasonia vitripennis*, which lay clutches in fly puparia where their offspring mate on emergence, produce about 10% sons when alone on a host and increase the proportion of sons when they lay on a host already parasitized by another female, the more so the smaller their own clutch is relative to the first (Werren 1980). In fig wasps (Agaonidae), whose males are wingless and mate inside the fig before the females leave ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)), sex ratios across species rise with the typical number of foundresses entering a fig, close to the theoretical curve (Herre 1985). Local mate competition is one of the most successful quantitative predictions in evolutionary biology, and haplodiploid control of fertilization is what makes such fine adjustment possible.

## 34.2. Phylogeny and Classification

Traditional classifications divided the order into two suborders, the Symphyta (sawflies), with a broad junction between thorax and abdomen, and the Apocrita (wasps, bees and ants), with a narrow waist; the Apocrita were further divided into the parasitic "Parasitica" (or Terebrantia) and the stinging Aculeata. Phylogenomic studies (Peters and coauthors 2017; Branstetter and coauthors 2017; Blaimer and coauthors 2023) confirm that Apocrita and Aculeata are monophyletic but show that the other two groups are grades.

![Relationships of the main lineages of Hymenoptera, simplified from phylogenomic studies (Peters and coauthors 2017; Branstetter and coauthors 2017; Blaimer and coauthors 2023). The sawflies and wood wasps ("Symphyta", dashed bracket) form a grade leading to the parasitoid wood wasps (Orussidae) and Apocrita, which together form Parasitoidida: the parasitoid way of life arose once in their common ancestor. Within Apocrita the parasitoid superfamilies form a grade, from which the stinging wasps (Aculeata, shaded) arose; ants are the sister group of Apoidea, and bees arose within the apoid wasps. Marks give characters gained along the branches.](./plots/hymenoptera-tree.py)

The tree contains three lessons. The parasitoid habit arose once, in the ancestor of Orussidae and Apocrita (Parasitoidida), whose larvae feed on wood-boring beetle larvae; the Orussidae are therefore the closest living relatives of the Apocrita, although they lack a waist. Second, the sting evolved once, in the Aculeata, from the ovipositor of a parasitoid ancestor. Third, bees are not the sister group of wasps but a lineage of apoid wasps that switched from hunting insects to collecting pollen for their larvae, probably in the Early Cretaceous; their closest relatives among living wasps may be the minute Ammoplanidae (Sann and coauthors 2018).

|---|---|---|
| Sawflies and wood wasps ("Symphyta", grade) | Xyeloidea, Tenthredinoidea, Pamphilioidea, Cephoidea, Siricoidea, Xiphydrioidea | Larvae phytophagous: leaf feeders, stem borers, wood borers |
| Orussoidea | Orussidae (parasitoid wood wasps) | Larvae ectoparasitoids of wood-boring beetle larvae |
| Parasitoid Apocrita (grade) | Ichneumonoidea, Chalcidoidea, Cynipoidea, Platygastroidea, Proctotrupoidea, Diaprioidea, Ceraphronoidea, Evanioidea, Stephanoidea, Trigonaloidea, Mymarommatoidea | Mostly parasitoids of other insects and spiders; some gall makers and seed feeders |
| Aculeata | Chrysidoidea, Vespoidea, Tiphioidea, Thynnoidea, Pompiloidea, Scolioidea, Formicoidea, Apoidea | Ovipositor a sting; parasitoids, predators that provision nests, pollen feeders; eusociality in Vespidae, Formicidae and some bees |

## 34.3. The Wasp Waist and the Ovipositor

### 34.3.1. Propodeum, Petiole and Gaster

**Definition 34.3.1 (Wasp waist)** In the Apocrita the first abdominal segment is fused with the metathorax as the **propodeum**, and the second abdominal segment forms a narrow stalk, the petiole, between the propodeum and the rest of the abdomen. The body behind the head is therefore divided into a **mesosoma** (the three thoracic segments plus the propodeum) and a **metasoma** (abdominal segments 2 onward), whose enlarged part behind the petiole is called the gaster.

In sawflies the first abdominal segment is a normal segment, broadly joined to the second. The constriction of the Apocrita lies between the first and second abdominal segments, not between thorax and abdomen, which is why entomologists use the terms mesosoma and metasoma for hymenopterans. In ants the petiole bears an upright scale or node, and in Myrmicinae and several other subfamilies the third abdominal segment forms a second node, the postpetiole; together they make the ant's waist very flexible and allow it to bend the gaster forward under the body to sting or spray.

![The wasp waist of Apocrita compared with the broad junction of sawflies (schematic lateral view, legs and wings omitted). In sawflies the first abdominal segment (A1) is a normal abdominal segment broadly joined to A2. In Apocrita A1 is fused to the metathorax as the propodeum, so that the functional thorax (mesosoma) includes A1; the constriction lies between A1 and A2, which forms the petiole, and the gaster behind it is the rest of the metasoma.](./plots/wasp-waist.py)

The waist evolved together with the parasitoid larva. A female parasitoid must locate hosts hidden in wood, in plant tissue, in other insects' cocoons or inside their bodies, and she must direct the ovipositor accurately. A flexible joint in front of the ovipositor-bearing segments allows the metasoma to be swung beneath the body, to the sides or forward between the legs, so that the ovipositor can probe at any angle while the female stands still. The Orussidae, which lack a waist, solve the same problem differently: they locate beetle larvae in wood by tapping the surface with their antennae and detecting the echoes (vibrational sounding), and they drill straight down. The second correlate of the waist is the legless larva of Apocrita, whose midgut is closed behind until the last instar (the waste is voided as a meconium before pupation), an adaptation to feeding inside a host without fouling it.

### 34.3.2. From Ovipositor to Sting

The hymenopteran ovipositor is the best example of the general insect ovipositor ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)). It is built from the appendages of abdominal segments 8 and 9. The first valvifers, on segment 8, carry the first valvulae, two slender blades below; the second valvifers, on segment 9, carry the second valvulae, fused into a single dorsal stylet, and the third valvulae, which form a pair of sheaths that enclose the shaft at rest. The two first valvulae slide independently along rails on the underside of the stylet, each locked to it by a tongue-and-groove joint (the olistheter), so that they can be advanced alternately; with backward-pointing teeth near their tips they work themselves into a substrate by a reciprocating movement, without any push from the body.

In sawflies (Tenthredinidae) the valvulae are broad and toothed and cut pockets in leaves or stems, hence the name. In wood wasps and in the ichneumonids that parasitize them, the ovipositor drills through wood: females of *Megarhyssa* and *Rhyssa* insert a shaft several centimetres long into a tree trunk to reach a wood wasp larva, and the tips of their valvulae are hardened by incorporated manganese and zinc. Chalcid and ichneumonid parasitoids of concealed hosts use the ovipositor as a sense organ as well, with chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors at its tip that assess the host before an egg is laid.

![From ovipositor to sting (schematic, not to scale). (A) In a parasitoid wasp the shaft is exserted: the first valvifers (abdominal segment 8) move the paired lancets (first valvulae), the second valvifers (segment 9) carry the dorsal stylet (fused second valvulae) and the sheaths (third valvulae), and the egg passes down the canal between lancets and stylet. (B) In a stinging wasp or bee the same parts are withdrawn into a sting chamber at the tip of the abdomen; the egg leaves at the base of the sting, and the canal carries venom from the venom gland and its reservoir; Dufour's gland opens nearby. In honey bee workers the lancets are barbed. (C) Cross-section of the shaft: each lancet slides on a rail of the stylet (olistheter) around the central canal.](./plots/sting-homology.py)

In the Aculeata the ovipositor has become a sting. The parts are the same, but the shaft is withdrawn into a sting chamber inside the seventh abdominal segment, eggs no longer pass down it but leave through the genital opening at its base, and the canal between stylet and lancets carries venom. The venom gland, a pair of filaments with a muscular reservoir, is derived from an accessory gland of the female reproductive tract, as are the venom glands of parasitoids, whose secretions paralyse hosts or regulate their physiology. Dufour's gland, also opening at the sting base, secretes hydrocarbons and esters used in nest lining and as trail and recognition pheromones. Only females sting; males of aculeates lack the apparatus, although some press spines of their genitalia into an attacker.

The sting of honey bee workers is specialized for defence of the colony. Each lancet bears about ten large barbs; in the elastic skin of a vertebrate the barbs hold, and when the bee pulls away the whole sting apparatus, with venom reservoir, muscles and its ganglion, tears from her body and continues to pump venom while releasing an alarm pheromone (isopentyl acetate and other compounds) that recruits further defenders. The worker dies, but she is sterile, and her death costs the colony little. Venoms differ among groups: honey bee venom consists largely of the membrane-lytic peptide melittin with phospholipase A2, hyaluronidase and the neurotoxin apamin; wasp venoms contain kinins and mastoparans; ant venoms range from proteinaceous venoms like those of wasps to the piperidine alkaloids of fire ants (*Solenopsis*), and in Formicinae the sting is lost and formic acid is sprayed from an acidopore.

## 34.4. Sawflies and Wood Wasps

The "Symphyta" (about 8500 species) are the sawflies, leaf-rolling and web-spinning sawflies, stem sawflies, wood wasps and horntails. Adults have no waist, a well-developed venation with many closed cells, and a saw-like or drill-like ovipositor; they are short-lived and feed little, on pollen, nectar or small insects. The larvae are herbivores, except in Orussidae.

Sawfly larvae resemble caterpillars, and confusing them with lepidopteran larvae is a classical error in pest identification. The differences are reliable:

| Character | Sawfly larva (Tenthredinoidea) | Caterpillar (Lepidoptera) |
|---|---|---|
| Stemmata | Usually one on each side of the head | Usually six on each side |
| Abdominal prolegs | Six to eight pairs, from abdominal segment 2 (on A2–A8 and A10) | At most five pairs, on A3–A6 and A10 |
| Crochets on prolegs | Absent | Present, in rows or circles |
| Adfrontal sclerites of the head | Absent | Present, beside the frons |

Tenthredinidae (common sawflies, about 6000 species) feed on leaves of many plants; the turnip sawfly *Athalia rosae* and the related *A. japonica* attack crucifers, and the wheat sawfly *Dolerus tritici* feeds on wheat leaves in northern China and pupates in an earthen cell in soil. Many sawfly larvae feed gregariously and raise the abdomen in unison when disturbed, and the larvae of pine sawflies (Diprionidae) sequester resin acids from pine needles in foregut pouches and regurgitate them against predators. Larvae of Pamphiliidae live in webs or rolled leaves; those of Cephidae bore in grass stems (the wheat stem sawfly *Cephus cinctus*, a pest in North America and northern China). Argidae and Cimbicidae are other common families.

Wood wasps (Siricidae; horntails) are large, cylindrical insects whose females have a long drill-like ovipositor and a terminal horn. A female drills into the wood of a weakened conifer and injects, with each egg, spores of a symbiotic white-rot fungus (*Amylostereum*) carried in a pair of intersegmental sacs at the base of the ovipositor, together with a phytotoxic mucus that weakens the tree's defences. The larva tunnels in the wood for one to three years and digests the fungus-softened wood with the help of fungal enzymes. The European *Sirex noctilio* has become a major pest of pine plantations in Australasia, South America, South Africa and North America, and was found in northeastern China in 2013; it is controlled by a nematode, *Deladenus siricidicola*, which feeds on the same fungus and sterilizes female wood wasps by entering their eggs.

The parasitoid wood wasps (Orussidae) are the sister group of Apocrita. Their larvae are legless ectoparasitoids of the larvae of wood-boring beetles and wood wasps, and the adult female, as described above, finds hosts in wood by vibrational sounding. They show what the ancestral parasitoid of the Apocrita may have been like: a wood-dweller that switched from eating wood or fungus to eating the other larvae in it.

## 34.5. Parasitoid Wasps

### 34.5.1. Life Histories

Most Apocrita are **parasitoids**: their larvae feed on or in a single host insect or spider and kill it, unlike true parasites, which usually do not kill their host, and unlike predators, which eat many prey ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)). Parasitoid wasps are the most species-rich group of Hymenoptera and perhaps of all insects; Forbes and coauthors (2018) argued that, since most insect species support several specialist parasitoid wasps, the Hymenoptera may contain more species than the beetles. Their life histories are classified along several independent axes:

| Axis | Categories | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Where the larva feeds | Ectoparasitoid (outside the host); endoparasitoid (inside) | Ectoparasitoids often attack concealed hosts in tunnels, galls or cocoons |
| Whether the host continues to develop | Idiobiont: the host is killed or permanently paralysed at oviposition; koinobiont: the host continues to feed and grow after parasitism | Most idiobionts are ectoparasitoids of concealed hosts; most koinobionts are endoparasitoids and more host-specific |
| Number of larvae per host | Solitary; gregarious (several to thousands) | Gregarious broods arise from several eggs or by polyembryony |
| Host stage attacked | Egg, larval, pupal or adult parasitoids; egg–larval and larval–pupal parasitoids complete development in a later stage | Egg parasitoids (Trichogrammatidae, Mymaridae, Scelionidae) are among the smallest insects |
| Trophic level | Primary parasitoid; hyperparasitoid (a parasitoid of another parasitoid) | Hyperparasitoids reduce the effectiveness of biological control |

A koinobiont endoparasitoid must solve two problems: it must escape the host's immune response, which encapsulates foreign objects in layers of hemocytes ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)), and it must keep the host alive and growing until its own larva has finished feeding. Females inject, with the egg, venom and ovarian secretions that suppress the host's immunity and redirect its development. In about 50,000 species of Braconidae and Ichneumonidae these secretions include *polydnaviruses*: virus-like particles produced in the wasp's ovary from genes integrated in the wasp genome, which infect host cells and express genes that block encapsulation and alter growth. The bracoviruses of braconids derive from a nudivirus captured by an ancestor about 100 million years ago (Bézier and coauthors 2009); the ichnoviruses of ichneumonids arose independently. The wasp and its domesticated virus are inherited together, the virus no longer replicating on its own, an extreme case of **mutualism** ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)).

**Polyembryony** ([7.17.28](/7.17.28)) is a further solution for a small egg in a large host: in the encyrtid *Copidosoma floridanum* a single egg laid in a moth egg divides, as the caterpillar grows, into up to about 2000 embryos, most of which develop into larvae that consume the caterpillar, while a minority develop precociously into sterile "soldier" larvae that kill competitors from other broods.

Females locate hosts in a hierarchical sequence: they find the host's habitat, often by plant volatiles; then the host itself, by its odours, frass, vibrations or the volatiles released by plants in response to herbivore feeding (in a classic case, caterpillar-damaged maize attracts the braconid *Cotesia marginiventris*; Turlings and coauthors 1990); and finally they assess the host with antennae and ovipositor. Many reject hosts already parasitized, recognizing marking pheromones; when they accept them (superparasitism), the larvae fight or compete physiologically.

### 34.5.2. The Main Parasitoid Superfamilies

| Superfamily and family | Characters | Biology and examples |
|---|---|---|
| Ichneumonidae (ichneumon wasps; about 25,000 species, perhaps 100,000) | Antennae long, with more than 16 segments; forewing with a second recurrent vein; often a long ovipositor | Mostly koinobiont endoparasitoids of holometabolous larvae and pupae; *Rhyssa* and *Megarhyssa* drill into wood for wood wasp larvae; *Xanthopimpla* in moth pupae |
| Braconidae (about 20,000) | Like ichneumonids but without a second recurrent vein; abdominal tergites 2 and 3 fused | *Cotesia glomerata*, gregarious in caterpillars of the cabbage white; *C. vestalis* in the diamondback moth; Aphidiinae, solitary in aphids, leaving the dead aphid as a swollen "mummy"; polydnaviruses |
| Chalcidoidea (about 22,500 described, perhaps 500,000) | Minute to small; antennae elbowed; wing venation reduced to one vein; often metallic; prepectus present | The most diverse parasitoids; also gall makers, seed feeders and fig pollinators; see below |
| Cynipoidea: Cynipidae (gall wasps); Figitidae | Small, laterally compressed metasoma; reduced venation | Cynipids induce galls, most on oaks, often with alternation of a sexual and an asexual generation; figitids parasitize fly larvae (*Leptopilina* in *Drosophila*) |
| Platygastroidea: Scelionidae, Platygastridae | Minute, black; elbowed antennae | Scelionids are egg parasitoids of bugs, grasshoppers, moths and spiders; *Trissolcus japonicus* attacks eggs of the brown marmorated stink bug |
| Proctotrupoidea, Diaprioidea, Ceraphronoidea | Small, mostly black | Parasitoids of beetle and fly larvae |
| Evanioidea: Evaniidae (ensign wasps) | Metasoma tiny, attached high on the propodeum and flagging up and down | Develop in cockroach oothecae |

The Chalcidoidea include the most important agents of biological control. The Trichogrammatidae are egg parasitoids under 1 mm long; species of *Trichogramma*, mass-reared on the eggs of the Chinese oak silkmoth *Antheraea pernyi* or of grain moths, are released in China over millions of hectares each year against the Asian corn borer, rice leafrollers and pine caterpillars, one of the largest biological control programmes in the world. The Aphelinidae include *Encarsia formosa*, used since the 1920s against greenhouse whitefly, and *Aphelinus* in aphids. The Encyrtidae include *Anagyrus lopezi*, introduced from South America in the 1980s, which brought the cassava mealybug *Phenacoccus manihoti* under control across tropical Africa and protected the staple food of some 200 million people. The fairyflies (Mymaridae) are egg parasitoids with stalked, fringed wings; the wingless, blind males of *Dicopomorpha echmepterygis*, 0.14 mm long, are the smallest known insects. The jewel wasp *Nasonia vitripennis* (Pteromalidae), a gregarious parasitoid of fly pupae, is a genetic model for the study of sex determination, sex-ratio adjustment and speciation by *Wolbachia*-induced incompatibility. The fig wasps (Agaonidae) are the obligate pollinators of figs ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)).

Gall wasps (Cynipidae, about 1400 species) induce some of the most complex plant galls; the larval secretions reprogram plant tissue into a structured gall with a nutritive layer, a sclerenchyma shell and often external hairs or spines. Many oak gall wasps alternate between a sexual generation and an all-female asexual generation that induce galls of different form on different organs or even on different oak species. The chestnut gall wasp *Dryocosmus kuriphilus*, native to China, is an invasive pest of chestnuts in Japan, Europe and North America and is controlled by its Chinese parasitoid *Torymus sinensis* (Torymidae), introduced deliberately.

## 34.6. Aculeata: Stinging Wasps, Ants and Bees

### 34.6.1. Stinging Wasps

The early aculeates were parasitoids that used the sting to paralyse a host and laid an egg on it. Nest building arose when females moved the paralysed host to a protected place; provisioning several prey per larva in a constructed cell, the habit of most solitary wasps, followed. The main families of wasps are:

| Family | Characters | Biology and examples |
|---|---|---|
| Chrysididae (cuckoo wasps) | Brilliant metallic blue and green; metasoma concave below, with three or four visible tergites; can roll into a ball | Cleptoparasites or parasitoids in nests of other wasps and bees |
| Dryinidae | Females often wingless, with chelate (pincer-like) fore tarsi | Parasitoids of leafhoppers and planthoppers, grasping the host with the chelae; *Haplogonatopus* on rice planthoppers |
| Bethylidae | Flattened, prognathous head | Ectoparasitoids of beetle and moth larvae in concealed sites; *Sclerodermus guani*, mass-reared in China against longhorn beetle larvae in wood |
| Mutillidae (velvet ants) | Females wingless, densely hairy, brightly coloured, with a very long sting | Parasitoids in nests of bees and wasps; aposematic and stridulating |
| Scoliidae, Tiphiidae | Robust, hairy; females dig | Parasitoids of scarab grubs in soil; *Campsomeris* introduced against Japanese beetle grubs |
| Pompilidae (spider wasps) | Long legs; females run with flicking wings | Each larva is provisioned with one paralysed spider; *Pepsis* (tarantula hawks) attack tarantulas |
| Vespidae (vespid wasps; about 5000 species) | Eyes notched (kidney-shaped); pronotum reaching the tegulae; wings folded lengthwise at rest | Eumeninae (potter wasps) build mud nests provisioned with caterpillars; Polistinae (paper wasps) and Vespinae (hornets, yellowjackets) are social and build paper nests of chewed wood fibre; Stenogastrinae (hover wasps) |
| Sphecidae, Crabronidae, Ampulicidae (apoid wasps) | Pronotum short, separated from the tegulae by a rounded lobe | Solitary hunters: *Ammophila* (sand wasps) provision burrows with caterpillars; *Philanthus* (beewolves) hunt honey bees; *Ampulex compressa* (emerald cockroach wasp) |

The hunting behaviour of solitary wasps impressed J.-H. Fabre and N. Tinbergen, who used digger wasps to study homing by landmarks. The emerald cockroach wasp *Ampulex compressa* stings a cockroach first into the prothoracic ganglion, briefly paralysing the forelegs, and then precisely into the brain, abolishing the cockroach's escape responses without paralysing it; the wasp then leads the docile cockroach by one antenna into a burrow, lays an egg on it and seals it in alive (Libersat and coauthors).

Social vespids are the familiar wasps. Paper wasps (*Polistes*) build an open comb of paper cells hanging from a stalk; colonies are founded by one or a few females, which establish a dominance hierarchy, and the dominant female lays most of the eggs; the system is the classic model of the transition from cooperative breeding to division of labour. Hornets and yellowjackets (Vespinae) build envelope-covered nests, sometimes of thousands of workers. The Asian giant hornet *Vespa mandarinia*, the largest hornet, preys on honey bee colonies, killing the guards one by one and then occupying the nest. Colonies of the eastern honey bee *Apis cerana* defend themselves by surrounding a hornet with a ball of workers that vibrate their flight muscles and raise the temperature to about 45–46 °C, lethal to the hornet but tolerated by the bees, a defence that the introduced western honey bee lacks (Ono and coauthors 1995). The yellow-legged hornet *Vespa velutina*, accidentally introduced from China to France around 2004, has spread through western Europe.

### 34.6.2. Ants

**Definition 34.6.1 (Formicidae)** The **Formicidae** (ants) are eusocial aculeate Hymenoptera with geniculate antennae with a long scape, a petiole bearing a node or scale (and in many subfamilies a second node, the postpetiole), a metapleural gland opening above the hind coxae, and colonies with winged queens and males and wingless workers; about 14,000 described species.

Ants arose in the Cretaceous and became dominant in terrestrial ecosystems in the Cenozoic; in tropical forests they may make up a quarter of the animal biomass. The metapleural gland, unique to ants, secretes antimicrobial compounds that protect workers and brood in the humid nest. The major subfamilies are Formicinae (no sting, formic acid; *Formica*, *Camponotus*, the weaver ants *Oecophylla*), Myrmicinae (the largest, with postpetiole; *Pheidole*, the leafcutter ants *Atta* and *Acromyrmex*, which cultivate fungus gardens, the fire ants *Solenopsis* and the pharaoh ant *Monomorium pharaonis*), Ponerinae (powerful stings, mostly predators), Dolichoderinae (no sting, defensive secretions from anal glands; the Argentine ant *Linepithema humile*) and Dorylinae (army ants, with nomadic colonies of millions).

The weaver ant *Oecophylla smaragdina* builds nests by binding leaves with silk produced by its larvae, which workers hold in their mandibles like shuttles. Its use against citrus pests in southern China is described in *Nanfang Caomu Zhuang* (304 CE), in which citrus growers bought nests of the ants and connected trees with bamboo bridges; it is the oldest known record of biological control. The red imported fire ant *Solenopsis invicta*, native to South America, spread to the United States in the 1930s, to Taiwan in 2003 and to Guangdong in 2004, and is now established in much of southern China; its sting causes pustules and sometimes anaphylaxis, and its colonies damage crops and electrical equipment. Ant colony organization, castes and communication are treated in [7.17.38](/7.17.38).

### 34.6.3. Bees

**Definition 34.6.2 (Anthophila)** The bees (**Anthophila**) are apoid Hymenoptera whose larvae are fed on pollen and nectar collected by the adult female; they have branched (plumose) body hairs, a broadened hind basitarsus, and structures for carrying pollen: a scopa of dense hairs on the hind legs or under the metasoma, or a corbicula (pollen basket) on the hind tibia; about 20,000 described species in seven families.

Branched hairs hold pollen grains, which bees groom into their pollen-carrying structures; they also carry an electrostatic charge that helps pollen to jump onto the bee. The switch from prey to pollen required no change in the larval body plan, since the larva is fed in a cell in either case, but it tied the bees to flowering plants: bees and angiosperms have diversified together since the Cretaceous, and bees are the dominant pollinators of most floras ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)).

The corbiculate bees, with a pollen basket on the hind tibia in which pollen is packed with nectar into a pellet, include the four tribes of Apini, Bombini, Meliponini and Euglossini; three of them are eusocial. Bumble bees can vibrate their flight muscles to shake pollen out of flowers with pored anthers (buzz pollination), which honey bees cannot do; *Bombus terrestris* and native species are reared commercially for pollinating greenhouse tomatoes. Euglossine males collect volatile compounds from orchids and store them in swollen hind tibiae as a courtship perfume, the orchids being pollinated in return.

The honey bees, genus *Apis*, comprise about ten species in Asia, with one, *A. mellifera*, also native to Europe and Africa: the western honey bee *A. mellifera*, now kept worldwide; the eastern (Chinese) honey bee *A. cerana*, native to China and southern and eastern Asia; the giant honey bees *A. dorsata* and the Himalayan cliff bee *A. laboriosa*, which build a single huge comb in the open; and the dwarf honey bee *A. florea*. The parasitic mite *Varroa destructor* is native to *A. cerana*, whose workers groom it off and whose brood resists it; after switching to *A. mellifera* in the 20th century, it and the viruses it transmits became the main cause of colony losses of western honey bees worldwide. The biology of honey bee colonies, castes and the waggle dance is treated in [7.17.38](/7.17.38).

## 34.7. Hymenoptera and People

No other insect order is as useful to people. Insect **pollination**, mostly by bees, was valued at about 153 billion euros a year worldwide around 2005, about a tenth of the value of agricultural food production (Gallai and coauthors 2009), and about three quarters of the leading crop species benefit from animal pollination. Honey, wax, propolis and royal jelly are products of *Apis*; China is the world's largest producer of honey. Parasitoid wasps are the main agents of classical and augmentative biological control ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)); among the successes are *Trichogramma* against corn borers in China, *Encarsia formosa* in greenhouses, *Anagyrus lopezi* against cassava mealybug in Africa, and *Torymus sinensis* against the chestnut gall wasp.

The harmful Hymenoptera are fewer: plant-feeding sawflies and wood wasps in forests and crops, gall wasps on chestnut, invasive ants (fire ants, the Argentine ant, the yellow crazy ant *Anoplolepis gracilipes*, which devastated the red crab populations of Christmas Island) and invasive hornets. Stings cause pain, and in sensitized people anaphylaxis; in China, mass attacks by *Vespa* hornets cause dozens of deaths in some years. The declines of wild bees and of managed honey bees, attributed to habitat loss, pesticides (notably neonicotinoids), pathogens and parasites, and climate change, have made the conservation of pollinators a public concern.

The textbook characters remain correct: two pairs of membranous wings coupled by hooks, the hind pair smaller; mouthparts chewing or chewing-lapping; the first abdominal segment joined to the thorax and, in Apocrita, a narrow waist; an ovipositor that saws, pierces or stings; and complete metamorphosis with caterpillar-like larvae in sawflies and legless larvae in the others.

## Summary

- **Hymenoptera** (Definition 34.1.1) have membranous wings coupled by **hamuli**, reduced venation, functional mandibles, an ovipositor of the appendages of abdominal segments 8 and 9, and haplodiploidy; they are the sister group of all other Holometabola.
- **Haplodiploidy** lets females choose the sex of each egg; under **local mate competition** the stable proportion of sons falls to $(n-1)(2n-1)/[n(4n-1)]$ with $n$ foundresses, a prediction confirmed in *Nasonia* and fig wasps. Sex is determined by a complementary locus (*csd* in honey bees) in many species, by other mechanisms in *Nasonia*.
- Sawflies and wood wasps form a grade ("Symphyta") without a waist; their larvae are plant feeders, distinguished from caterpillars by one stemma on each side, more prolegs and no crochets. Wood wasps carry symbiotic fungi.
- The parasitoid habit arose once, in Orussidae plus Apocrita. The wasp waist (Definition 34.3.1) lies between the propodeum (abdominal segment 1, fused with the thorax) and the petiole (segment 2) and frees the ovipositor to probe; apocritan larvae are legless.
- Parasitoid wasps are classified as ecto- or endoparasitoids, idiobionts or koinobionts, solitary or gregarious; koinobionts suppress host immunity with venoms and, in braconids and ichneumonids, domesticated polydnaviruses. Chalcidoidea, Ichneumonidae and Braconidae are the main agents of biological control.
- In Aculeata the ovipositor became a sting: lancets (first valvulae) sliding on a stylet (fused second valvulae) deliver venom, and eggs leave at the base. Stinging wasps include cuckoo, spider, vespid and apoid wasps; Formicidae (Definition 34.6.1) and Anthophila (Definition 34.6.2) are aculeate lineages, bees arising within the apoid wasps.
- Hymenoptera are the most important pollinators and natural enemies of pests; invasive ants and hornets and the declines of bees are the main problems associated with them.

## Questions

1. A green larva with three pairs of thoracic legs and seven pairs of abdominal prolegs is feeding on a crucifer leaf. Is it a caterpillar? Which further characters would you check?

   **Answer** It is almost certainly a sawfly larva (Tenthredinidae, for example *Athalia*). Caterpillars have at most five pairs of prolegs, on abdominal segments 3–6 and 10; sawfly larvae have six to eight pairs, beginning on segment 2. To confirm, check the prolegs for crochets (present in caterpillars, absent in sawflies), count the stemmata (usually one on each side of the head in sawflies, six in caterpillars), and look for adfrontal sclerites beside the frons (present only in caterpillars). The distinction matters in pest control, because some insecticides used against caterpillars, such as *Bacillus thuringiensis* strains active on Lepidoptera, do not kill sawflies.

2. Explain why the waist of a wasp lies between the first and second abdominal segments, and how it is thought to be related to the parasitoid way of life.

   **Answer** In Apocrita the first abdominal segment (propodeum) is fused with the metathorax and functions as part of the thorax, so the constriction lies behind it, between abdominal segments 1 and 2 (the petiole). The waist evolved in a parasitoid ancestor whose females had to insert the ovipositor into hidden hosts; a mobile joint in front of the ovipositor-bearing segments lets the female swing the metasoma under her body and probe at different angles while standing still. The parasitoid wood wasps (Orussidae), which share the parasitoid habit but not the waist, show that parasitism came first; the waist then improved the control of the ovipositor. It was retained in aculeates, where it gives the same mobility to the sting.

3. A female *Nasonia vitripennis* lays eggs alone on a fly puparium; a second female lays a smaller clutch on the same puparium later. Predict the sex ratios of the two clutches, and explain the prediction.

   **Answer** The first female, alone, should produce a strongly female-biased clutch with just enough sons to mate with her daughters (about 10% sons in experiments), because her sons compete with each other for mates in the puparium (local mate competition) and extra daughters, which disperse after mating, add more to her fitness. The second female's sons will compete mainly with the first female's sons and will have many unrelated females to mate with, so sons are worth more to her; she should produce a much higher proportion of sons, and the higher, the smaller her clutch relative to the first. Werren (1980) found exactly this pattern. Haplodiploidy makes the adjustment possible, since the female controls fertilization of each egg.

4. Why do honey bee workers die after stinging a person, while wasps and queens can sting repeatedly, and why might this trait have evolved?

   **Answer** The lancets of honey bee workers bear large backward-pointing barbs that anchor the sting in the elastic skin of birds and mammals; when the bee flies off, the sting apparatus, with venom reservoir, muscles and ganglion, tears out and continues to pump venom and release alarm pheromone. Wasps and honey bee queens have smaller barbs or none and can withdraw the sting. Autotomy increases the dose of venom and marks the target for other defenders; the worker's death costs little in fitness because workers are sterile and their genes are transmitted through the queen, so selection on the colony favours self-sacrificial defence against large vertebrates that raid nests for honey and brood.

5. A koinobiont endoparasitoid larva develops inside a caterpillar for two weeks without being killed by the host's immune system. Which mechanisms may protect it?

   **Answer** At oviposition the female injects venom and ovarian secretions with the egg; in many braconids and ichneumonids these include polydnavirus particles, made from viral genes integrated in the wasp genome, which infect host hemocytes and other tissues and express genes that prevent hemocytes from spreading and encapsulating the egg and that alter the host's growth and hormones. The egg or larva may also bear surface coats that are not recognized as foreign, and teratocytes released from the embryonic membranes in some species secrete immunosuppressive and nutritive factors. Together they keep the host alive but immunologically compromised until the parasitoid larva completes feeding.

6. (Open question) Parasitoid wasps may be more species-rich than beetles. What kinds of data would support or refute this claim?

   **Answer** One needs estimates of the number of insect host species and of the average number of specialist parasitoid wasp species per host: for example, rearing all parasitoids from well-sampled host species in a region (as in the work of Forbes and coauthors on gall wasps and other hosts), combined with DNA barcoding to separate cryptic species, and then extrapolating to the number of host species. Supporting evidence would be consistently high numbers of host-specific parasitoids per host, including on beetles themselves; refuting evidence would be extensive host sharing (generalist parasitoids) and fewer parasitoids on many hosts. Biases to consider include the poor description of small parasitoids (taxonomic impediment), cryptic species, and the fact that many hosts in tropical regions have never been reared.
