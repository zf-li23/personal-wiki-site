Insects and flowering plants are the two largest groups of multicellular organisms on land, and much of the diversity of each is a consequence of the other. About half of all insect species feed on living plants, and these phytophagous lineages are richer in species than their non-phytophagous sister groups ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)); conversely, about nine in ten flowering plants depend on animals, mostly insects, for **pollination** ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)). The interaction ranges from antagonism (a caterpillar eating a leaf) through exploitation that also helps the plant (a seed-eating fig wasp that pollinates the flowers it lays in) to intimate **mutualism** (ants that live in and defend a tree).

This chapter is written from the insect's point of view. It first classifies the ways in which insects use plants and the breadth of their diets, then describes how an insect finds and accepts a host, the defences of plants and the counter-adaptations of insects, and the evolutionary theory that links the two, the escape-and-radiate **coevolution** of Ehrlich and Raven, whose general theory is given in [7.10.17](/7.10.17). It then turns to the mutualisms: pollination (the floral side is treated in [7.12.29](/7.12.29)), nursery pollination in figs and leafflowers, ant–plants and seed dispersal by ants. It ends with galls, the most intimate antagonistic interaction, and with the use of insects against weeds. Mouthpart types are defined in [7.17.23](/7.17.23), the stylet feeding of bugs in [7.17.32](/7.17.32), and the community and ecosystem consequences of herbivory are left to ecology ([7.19](/7.19)).

## 37.1. Phytophagy

### 37.1.1. Why Plants Are Difficult Food

Plants are abundant but poor food. Leaves contain 1–5% nitrogen by dry mass, against about 10% in animal tissue, and much of their mass is cellulose and lignin that insects cannot digest without symbionts; phloem sap is rich in sugar but contains little amino nitrogen, and xylem sap is a dilute solution of minerals and amino acids, about a hundred times poorer than phloem. Plants also lack sterols in the form insects need (insects cannot synthesize sterols and must convert phytosterols to cholesterol). An insect living on a plant must, in addition, cling to a surface that may be waxy, hairy or wind-swept, resist desiccation in the open air, and overcome defences that the plant has evolved against it.

Phytophagous insects meet these problems in characteristic ways. Many grow slowly and eat large amounts: a caterpillar may eat its own mass of leaves each day, and its efficiency of conversion of digested food into body mass is lower on old, tough, nitrogen-poor leaves than on young ones. Sap feeders depend on obligate bacterial symbionts in **bacteriocytes**, which synthesize essential amino acids and vitamins ([7.17.26](/7.17.26), [7.11.31](/7.11.31)), and excrete the surplus sugar as **honeydew**. Xylem feeders (cicadas, spittlebugs, sharpshooter leafhoppers) are at the extreme: to extract enough nitrogen from a liquid under negative pressure they need a powerful cibarial pump, which gives them a swollen face, and they process up to several hundred times their body mass of sap in a day; the froth of spittlebug nymphs is made of the excess water, mixed with air and a secretion of the Malpighian tubules.

**Definition 37.1.1 (Phytophagy)** **Phytophagy** (herbivory) is feeding on living tissues of plants: leaves, stems, roots, sap, buds, flowers, pollen, nectar, fruits or seeds. Insects that feed on dead plant material are detritivores (saprophages), not phytophages.

Phytophagy has evolved many times in insects. Plant feeding by sucking and chewing is documented from Late Carboniferous fossils of seed-fern and tree-fern leaves with feeding damage, and the Palaeodictyopterida of the Palaeozoic had a beak for piercing plant organs ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)). Among living insects the main plant-feeding groups are the Orthoptera (most Caelifera), Phasmatodea, Thysanoptera, most Hemiptera, and the four great holometabolous orders, in which phytophagy is concentrated in the Lepidoptera (nearly all), the phytophagous beetles of Chrysomeloidea and Curculionoidea, the sawflies, and several families of flies. In a classic comparison of phytophagous clades with their non-phytophagous sister groups, Mitter, Farrell and Wiegmann (1988) found the plant feeders to be more diverse in 11 of 13 comparisons, which suggests that phytophagy is a key to insect diversity, not merely a correlate.

### 37.1.2. Feeding Guilds

The way an insect feeds determines what it damages, what defences it meets and how it can be controlled. Entomologists classify phytophagous insects into feeding guilds:

| Guild | How the insect feeds | Main groups | Examples, with important species in China |
|---|---|---|---|
| External leaf chewers | Bite and chew leaf tissue with mandibles | Lepidoptera larvae, Orthoptera, leaf beetles, sawfly larvae, stick insects | Oriental armyworm *Mythimna separata*, cotton bollworm *Helicoverpa armigera*, diamondback moth *Plutella xylostella*, fall armyworm *Spodoptera frugiperda* (invasive since 2019), the migratory locust *Locusta migratoria*, pine caterpillars *Dendrolimus* |
| Sap suckers | Insert stylets into phloem, xylem or mesophyll cells and suck | Hemiptera, Thysanoptera | Brown planthopper *Nilaparvata lugens* and white-backed planthopper *Sogatella furcifera* on rice, cotton aphid *Aphis gossypii*, the whitefly *Bemisia tabaci*, western flower thrips *Frankliniella occidentalis* |
| Leaf miners | Larvae live and feed between the two epidermal layers of a leaf | Only four holometabolous orders: Diptera, Lepidoptera, Coleoptera, Hymenoptera | Vegetable leaf miners *Liriomyza sativae* and *L. huidobrensis* (Agromyzidae), citrus leafminer *Phyllocnistis citrella* (Gracillariidae) |
| Stem and wood borers | Tunnel in stems, shoots, twigs, trunks or roots | Longhorn beetles, jewel beetles, weevils and bark beetles; Cossidae, Sesiidae and Crambidae among moths; Cephidae and Siricidae among sawflies | Asian corn borer *Ostrinia furnacalis*, striped rice stem borer *Chilo suppressalis*, Asian longhorned beetle *Anoplophora glabripennis* in poplars, Japanese pine sawyer *Monochamus alternatus*, vector of the pinewood nematode |
| Gall inducers | Induce the plant to form a gall in which the insect feeds | Cecidomyiidae, Cynipidae, aphids, psyllids, scale insects, thrips, some Tephritidae, sawflies, moths and weevils | Chinese gall aphid *Schlechtendalia chinensis* on *Rhus chinensis*, orange wheat blossom midge *Sitodiplosis mosellana*, grape phylloxera *Daktulosphaira vitifoliae* (Section 37.6) |
| Seed and fruit feeders | Larvae develop in seeds or fruit; adults or nymphs suck developing seeds | Bruchine seed beetles, weevils, Tephritidae, Tortricidae; seed bugs; harvester ants | Pea weevil *Bruchus pisorum*, rice weevil *Sitophilus oryzae*, oriental fruit fly *Bactrocera dorsalis*, peach fruit moth *Carposina sasakii* |
| Root feeders | Feed on roots underground | Larvae of scarabs ("white grubs"), click beetles ("wireworms"), cicadas; mole crickets; root aphids | White grubs of *Holotrichia* and the mole cricket *Gryllotalpa orientalis*, the soil pests of northern Chinese farmland |
| Flower visitors | Eat pollen, nectar or floral tissue | Bees, butterflies and moths, flies, beetles, thrips | Mostly mutualists (Section 37.4); some, such as blossom beetles, are pests |

The guilds differ in visible damage. Chewing removes tissue, and damage is easy to measure as leaf area lost: in temperate forests, mean annual loss of leaf area to insects is about 9%, rarely more than 20% outside outbreaks (compilation of Landsberg and Ohmart 1989). Sap feeders leave the cell walls intact, but they drain resources, inject toxic saliva (the "hopperburn" of rice attacked by the brown planthopper, in which whole patches of the crop collapse) and transmit plant viruses: aphids transmit about half of the known insect-borne plant viruses ([7.17.32](/7.17.32), [7.11.26](/7.11.26)). Miners and borers are protected from many natural enemies and from contact insecticides, which is why their control relies on systemic insecticides, resistant cultivars, cultural measures and parasitoids ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)). A stem borer that girdles a growing point or a fruit borer that destroys a fruit can cause losses out of proportion to the tissue it eats.

**Definition 37.1.2 (Leaf Mine)** A **leaf mine** is the tunnel or blotch formed in the interior of a leaf by a larva that feeds on the mesophyll between the two epidermal layers; its form (linear, serpentine, blotch or tentiform), the deposition of frass in it and the host plant are usually species-specific and are used to identify the miner.

Leaf mining is confined to holometabolous larvae, because only a larva that can grow without moulting into an adult form in the confined space can complete development there; miners are flattened, often with reduced legs and a prognathous head. Many mining moths change habit with age: the gracillariid *Phyllonorycter* larva begins as a sap-feeder with blade-like mandibles that slice open cells and later becomes a tissue feeder that makes a tentiform mine. A serpentine mine records the life of the larva, widening as it grows, and the frass line shows its feeding.

### 37.1.3. Host Range

**Definition 37.1.3 (Host Range of Herbivores)** The set of plant species on which an insect feeds and develops is its **host range**. An insect is **monophagous** if it feeds on one plant species or genus, **oligophagous** if it feeds on several genera of one family (or a few related families), and **polyphagous** if it feeds on plants of many families.

The silkworm *Bombyx mori*, which feeds on mulberry (*Morus*, Moraceae) and accepts few other plants, is nearly monophagous; the cabbage white *Pieris rapae* and the diamondback moth *Plutella xylostella* are oligophagous on Brassicaceae and a few other glucosinolate-bearing families; the cotton bollworm *Helicoverpa armigera* and the gypsy moth *Lymantria dispar* are polyphagous, with recorded hosts in dozens of families. Gall wasps of the Cynipini are almost all confined to oaks, and nearly every species is restricted to a few oak species. Most phytophagous insects are specialists: in compilations of rearing records, the majority of species feed on plants of a single family, and the frequency distribution of diet breadth is strongly right-skewed (Forister and coauthors 2015). Grasshoppers are an exception, many species being generalists that mix plants in their diet.

Why specialization should be so common is an old problem. Four explanations, not mutually exclusive, have been proposed:

- *Physiological trade-offs*: adaptation to the defences of one plant lowers performance on others ("jack of all trades, master of none"). Experiments have often failed to find such trade-offs among host-plant genotypes, but the enzymes and receptors that cope with one class of compounds are of little use against another.
- *Neural constraints* (Bernays): a specialist can recognize and assess its host faster and with fewer errors, because it needs to process less information. Specialist aphids and leaf beetles make decisions faster than generalists, and they spend less time exposed to predators.
- *Enemy-free space*: a specialist can use the host's chemistry or appearance for protection, as monarch caterpillars sequester milkweed cardenolides (Section 37.2.3) or as caterpillars matching their host's foliage escape birds.
- *Phylogenetic inertia*: related insects feed on related plants because host shifts are rare and usually to chemically similar plants, so specialization is inherited.

Within a species, a female's choice of oviposition site often predicts the performance of her offspring, the preference–performance hypothesis ("mother knows best"), most clearly in insects whose larvae cannot move between plants, such as gall makers and miners. Host specialization can lead to speciation. The apple maggot *Rhagoletis pomonella*, originally on hawthorn in North America, formed a *host race* on introduced apples in the mid-19th century; the two races mate on their host fruit and differ in the timing of emergence, which is adjusted to the earlier fruiting of apple, so that host choice reduces gene flow ([7.10.17](/7.10.17), [7.10.12](/7.10.12)).

### 37.1.4. Finding and Accepting a Host

Host selection is a sequence of behavioural steps, each guided by different cues:

1. Habitat location and orientation at a distance. Flying insects orient upwind in odour plumes of plant volatiles: green-leaf volatiles (six-carbon alcohols and aldehydes released by damaged leaves), terpenoids and specific compounds such as the isothiocyanates that attract crucifer specialists. Visual cues act at closer range: aphids and whiteflies are attracted to yellow, which mimics the reflectance of young leaves, which is why yellow sticky traps catch them.
2. Contact and evaluation. After landing, the insect drums the surface with its tarsi and antennae, tastes it with contact chemoreceptors on the tarsi, mouthparts and ovipositor ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)), and may take a test bite or probe with the stylets. Specialists have receptor neurons tuned to the defensive compounds of their hosts, which act as **kairomones**: caterpillars of *Pieris* have taste cells on the maxillae that fire in response to glucosinolates such as sinigrin, and the compounds stimulate feeding; for a non-adapted insect the same compounds are deterrent. Generalist caterpillars instead have "deterrent cells" that respond to many bitter compounds.
3. Acceptance and continued feeding or oviposition, decided by the balance of stimulating and deterrent inputs, and by nutrients (sugars and amino acids).

The tobacco hornworm *Manduca sexta*, a specialist on Solanaceae, learns its host: larvae reared on one host refuse others, a form of induced preference. Oviposition is often more selective than larval feeding. Female swallowtails "taste" leaves with their fore tarsi before laying an egg, and in *Heliconius* butterflies females avoid passion-vine shoots that already carry eggs; some passion vines have yellow stipular structures that resemble *Heliconius* eggs, a deterrent that Gilbert interpreted as egg mimicry by the plant.

## 37.2. Plant Defences and Insect Counter-Adaptations

### 37.2.1. Physical Defences

The first barrier is the plant surface. Hairs (**trichomes**) impede small insects and, when they are glandular (**glandular trichomes**), release sticky or toxic secretions; the hooked trichomes on leaves of beans impale the legs of leafhoppers and aphids, and glandular hairs of wild potatoes and tomatoes trap aphids in exudate. Silica in the leaves of grasses wears down mandibles and lowers digestibility; tough, fibrous, sclerophyllous leaves (as in eucalypts and mature oak leaves) are eaten slowly and mainly by insects with strong, often specialized mandibles. Epicuticular wax crystals on leaves and stems make surfaces slippery: many insects cannot hold on to waxy cabbage leaves or to the waxy stems of some *Macaranga*, which are climbed only by the plant's symbiotic ants (Section 37.5).

Canal systems that store latex or resin under pressure are physical and chemical defences at once. When a caterpillar bites a milkweed leaf, latex, which contains toxic cardenolides, floods out and glues its mouthparts. Many specialists disarm this defence behaviourally: monarch caterpillars and other milkweed feeders first cut a trench across the leaf or chew through the midrib, so that the part beyond the cut is drained of latex, and then feed beyond it (vein cutting and trenching; Dussourd and Eisner 1987). Similar behaviour evolved independently in beetles and moths on lettuce, squash and other latex- or resin-bearing plants. Bark beetles meet the resin of conifers by mass attack, coordinated by aggregation **pheromones**, which exhausts the resin defences of a tree.

### 37.2.2. Chemical Defences

**Definition 37.2.1 (Secondary Plant Compounds)** **Secondary plant compounds** (plant secondary metabolites, allelochemicals) are compounds that are not required for growth and development but act in the interactions of a plant with other organisms. Against insects they act as repellents, feeding and oviposition deterrents, toxins and digestibility reducers.

About 200,000 such **secondary metabolites** are known, and plant families are often characterized by particular classes ([7.10.17](/7.10.17), [7.13](/7.13)):

| Class | Examples and plants | Action on insects | Specialists that cope with them |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alkaloids** | Nicotine (tobacco), caffeine (tea, coffee), pyrrolizidine alkaloids (*Senecio*, Boraginaceae), tomatine (tomato) | Neurotoxins; nicotine is an agonist of the **nicotinic acetylcholine receptor** | *Manduca sexta* excretes nicotine rapidly; arctiid moths sequester pyrrolizidine alkaloids |
| Cyanogenic glycosides | Cassava, clover, passion vines, almond | Release HCN, which blocks cytochrome oxidase, when tissue is damaged | Burnet moths (*Zygaena*) and *Heliconius* sequester them or synthesize their own |
| **Glucosinolates** | Brassicales: crucifers, capers | Hydrolysed by myrosinase on tissue damage to toxic isothiocyanates (the "mustard-oil bomb") | Pierid butterflies divert hydrolysis with nitrile-specifier protein ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)); the diamondback moth removes the sulfate with a gut sulfatase so that myrosinase cannot act |
| Cardenolides (cardiac glycosides) | Milkweeds (*Asclepias*), oleander, foxglove | Inhibit the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase | Monarchs and milkweed bugs carry an insensitive ATPase and sequester the compounds |
| Furanocoumarins | Apiaceae (wild parsnip, celery), Rutaceae (citrus) | Bind DNA when activated by ultraviolet light | The black swallowtail *Papilio polyxenes* metabolizes them with cytochrome P450 enzymes of the CYP6B family |
| **Terpenoids** (isoprenoids) | Resins of conifers, essential oils, pyrethrins (*Tanacetum*), azadirachtin (neem), phytoecdysteroids | Toxic, deterrent, or disrupt moulting | Bark beetles use host terpenes as precursors of their pheromones |
| Phenolics, tannins, lignin | Oak leaves, eucalypts, tea | Bind proteins, reduce digestibility; oxidize to toxic quinones at high gut pH | Caterpillars on oaks have an alkaline gut and surfactants that limit tannin binding |
| Protein defences | Proteinase inhibitors, lectins, chitinases | Block digestion of protein in the gut | Many caterpillars switch to inhibitor-insensitive proteases |
| Non-protein amino acids | L-canavanine (seeds of *Canavalia* and other legumes) | Incorporated into proteins in place of arginine | The bruchine *Caryedes brasiliensis* has an arginyl-tRNA synthetase that discriminates against canavanine and degrades it as a nitrogen source |

The chemical-ecology theory of the 1970s distinguished two strategies. Feeny's *apparency hypothesis* held that large, long-lived plants, which herbivores will certainly find ("apparent" plants, such as oak trees), should invest in quantitative, dose-dependent defences that reduce digestibility (tannins, lignin, toughness), which no herbivore can easily overcome; whereas short-lived, scattered herbs ("unapparent" plants, such as crucifers) should rely on qualitative toxins effective in small doses (glucosinolates, alkaloids), which escape generalists at little cost but are vulnerable to specialists that evolve tolerance. Feeny's own example was the contrast between oak, whose leaves become tough and tannin-rich in early summer, when the spring feeding of the winter moth *Operophtera brumata* ends, and crucifers. The scheme is a useful approximation, though many apparent plants have toxins too; later theories emphasize resource availability (fast-growing plants of rich soils invest less in defence and replace lost tissue; the growth–defence trade-off of [7.13](/7.13)) and the distinction between constitutive and induced defence.

### 37.2.3. Induced Defences and Indirect Defences

Defence costs resources, and attack is intermittent. Many plants therefore mount an **induced defense** only when attacked. Green and Ryan (1972) showed that wounding tomato leaves by Colorado potato beetles causes the accumulation of proteinase inhibitors in the wounded and in unwounded leaves; the signal is mediated by **jasmonic acid** and its conjugate jasmonoyl-isoleucine, which act through the COI1 receptor and the degradation of JAZ repressors ([7.13](/7.13)). Plants distinguish herbivory from mechanical damage by elicitors in the oral secretions of insects, such as volicitin (an amino-acid conjugate of a fatty acid) in caterpillar regurgitant, and by the pattern of damage. Mutants of tomato and *Nicotiana attenuata* that cannot synthesize or perceive jasmonate are heavily attacked in the field, and in *N. attenuata* the jasmonate-regulated production of nicotine and trypsin inhibitors is required for the plant to survive its herbivores (Baldwin and colleagues).

Defence can also be indirect. Leaves damaged by herbivores emit blends of volatile terpenoids and green-leaf volatiles that attract the parasitoids and predators of the herbivores: maize leaves fed on by caterpillars attract the braconid *Cotesia marginiventris* (Turlings and coauthors 1990; [7.17.34](/7.17.34)), and lima bean leaves infested by spider mites attract predatory mites. These volatiles are synomones in the classification of semiochemicals ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)), benefiting both emitter and receiver. Wild tobacco plants release volatiles that cause predatory bugs to remove more than 90% of the eggs of *Manduca* (Kessler and Baldwin 2001). Extrafloral nectar, food bodies and domatia that recruit ants (Section 37.5) are constitutive indirect defences; in some plants nectar secretion from extrafloral nectaries also rises after damage.

### 37.2.4. How Insects Overcome Plant Defences

Insects that feed on chemically defended plants use one or more of five mechanisms:

| Mechanism | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Avoidance | Feeding on plant parts or stages with little defence; disarming canals | Feeding on young leaves before tannins accumulate; vein cutting on milkweed and latex plants (Section 37.2.1) |
| Rapid excretion | The compound passes through the gut or is excreted by the Malpighian tubules before it does harm | Nicotine in *Manduca sexta*; caffeine and many alkaloids in specialists |
| Metabolic detoxification | Enzymes modify the compound to a less toxic, more soluble product: cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, glutathione S-transferases, carboxylesterases, UDP-glycosyltransferases | P450s of *Papilio* induced by furanocoumarins; the gut sulfatase of *Plutella xylostella* that inactivates glucosinolates (Ratzka and coauthors 2002); the P450 CYP6AE14 of *Helicoverpa armigera* induced by gossypol of cotton |
| Target-site insensitivity | Amino acid substitutions make the molecular target insensitive to the toxin | Substitutions in the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase of cardenolide-feeding insects; the canavanine-discriminating tRNA synthetase of *Caryedes* |
| Sequestration | The compound is stored, often in special tissues or glands, and used against predators | Cardenolides in the monarch; pyrrolizidine alkaloids in arctiid moths; aristolochic acids in the pipevine swallowtail *Battus philenor* and the Chinese *Byasa* and *Atrophaneura* |

**Definition 37.2.2 (Sequestration)** **Sequestration** is the uptake of defensive compounds from food and their storage in the body, in a form that protects the insect against its own enemies.

Target-site insensitivity provides one of the clearest examples of repeated evolution at the molecular level. Cardenolides bind a pocket on the α subunit of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. In the monarch *Danaus plexippus*, the milkweed bug *Oncopeltus fasciatus*, the milkweed leaf beetle and others, independent substitutions at the same few amino acid positions (most often 111, 119 and 122) make the enzyme less sensitive to the toxin; in some lineages the gene was duplicated first, so that a resistant copy could evolve in the gut and nervous tissue while another copy kept full activity (Zhen and coauthors 2012). Karageorgi and coauthors (2019) edited the three substitutions of the monarch into the ATPase of *Drosophila melanogaster* with CRISPR: the edited flies survived on cardenolide-containing food as adults and as larvae, and could even store the compounds, showing that these few changes are sufficient to explain the resistance. The order in which the substitutions arose in the monarch lineage is constrained by pleiotropy: each later substitution is tolerated only in the background of the earlier ones, a case of epistasis in adaptation ([7.10.5](/7.10.5)).

Sequestration turns a plant's defence into the insect's. The monarch caterpillar stores cardenolides from its milkweed host and passes them to the adult; blue jays that eat a monarch reared on a cardenolide-rich milkweed vomit within minutes and afterwards refuse monarchs on sight (Brower 1969), a classic demonstration of **aposematism** ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)). Butterflies reared on cardenolide-free milkweeds are palatable, and the protection of a population depends on the chemistry of its host plants. In the ornate moth *Utetheisa ornatrix*, the larvae sequester pyrrolizidine alkaloids from *Crotalaria* seeds; males pass part of their supply to the female in the spermatophore, and she transfers it to the eggs, which are then rejected by ants and coccinellids (Eisner and Meinwald). Females prefer males that advertise a large supply with a courtship pheromone derived from the alkaloids, so that a plant defence has become the basis of a sexual signal.

## 37.3. Coevolution of Insects and Plants

### 37.3.1. Escape and Radiate

Ehrlich and Raven (1964), comparing the host plants of butterflies, observed that related butterflies feed on related plants that share a class of secondary compounds, and that butterflies of different families often converge on the same chemically defined group of plants. They proposed the stepwise sequence later called escape-and-radiate **coevolution**: a plant lineage that evolves a new chemical defence escapes most herbivores and radiates in this enemy-free space; an insect lineage that evolves a way to tolerate the defence colonizes the new clade, escapes competition, and radiates on it in turn. The theory predicts that defensive innovations and counter-innovations should each be followed by bursts of diversification, and that host use should be phylogenetically conserved and should track the chemistry of plants rather than their phylogeny.

![Escape-and-radiate coevolution (schematic, after Ehrlich and Raven 1964). Left: a plant lineage gains a new defensive compound (marker on its branch), escapes its herbivores and radiates. Right: later, an insect lineage gains a mechanism to tolerate the compound (marker), colonizes the defended plants and radiates on them; dashed lines connect insects with their hosts. The best-documented case is the Brassicales, whose glucosinolates arose about 90 million years ago, and the pierid butterflies, which colonized them about 70–80 million years ago after evolving the nitrile-specifier protein; later glucosinolate classes and further detoxification genes were each followed by increased rates of diversification (Edger and coauthors 2015).](./plots/escape-radiate.py)

The best test, the study of Brassicales and pierid butterflies by Edger and coauthors (2015), is described in [7.10.17](/7.10.17); it found, as predicted, repeated escalation in which new classes of glucosinolates and new copies of the butterflies' detoxification genes were each associated with higher rates of diversification. Comparable evidence comes from the leaf beetles *Blepharida* and their hosts *Bursera* in Mexico. Some *Bursera* species store terpenoid resins in canals under pressure and squirt them at a biting beetle, while others have lost the squirt and evolved more complex resin chemistry; the beetles have evolved vein cutting and resin tolerance, and, across the two phylogenies, beetle host shifts track plant chemistry rather than plant phylogeny, and the timing of diversification of the two groups is concordant (Becerra 2003).

The theory also has limits. Most plants are attacked by many insects and most insects feed on a few plants, so that the selection pressures are usually diffuse rather than pairwise. Host shifts are often not to the newest plant clades but between chemically similar old ones, and phylogenies of herbivorous insects are usually not congruent with those of their hosts: insects colonized already diversified plant groups, often long after the plants arose (**cophylogeny**, [7.10.17](/7.10.17)). The oscillation hypothesis (Janz and Nylin) proposes that host ranges expand and contract repeatedly: a lineage expands its range, which opens new hosts, and then fragments into specialists on different hosts, producing new species in each phase of contraction.

### 37.3.2. Did Insects and Angiosperms Diversify Together?

Comparisons of beetle lineages have shown that clades feeding on angiosperms are much richer in species than their sister clades on conifers and cycads (Farrell 1998), and the Lepidoptera, Chrysomeloidea and Curculionoidea, the largest groups of herbivores, are dominated by angiosperm feeders. Yet the fossil record and dated phylogenies show that the major insect orders and most insect feeding guilds are older than the angiosperms; plant–insect associations of the Mesozoic included cycads and their weevils and thrips, Jurassic scorpionflies and lacewings with long siphons that probably took pollination drops from gymnosperms (Ren and coauthors 2009, from Middle Jurassic deposits of Inner Mongolia), and gymnosperm-feeding beetles. As discussed in [7.17.30](/7.17.30), the current view is that the angiosperms entered an already diverse insect fauna, and that particular insect lineages radiated on them afterwards; the increase in diversity of phytophagous insects owes more to the many new host lineages and plant organs offered by angiosperms than to a change in the insects themselves.

## 37.4. Pollination

### 37.4.1. The Pollinating Insects

Pollination by insects is older than flowers. Cycads are pollinated today by weevils and thrips that feed in their cones, and the fossil record of pollen-carrying insects begins in the Permian. The oldest direct evidence of insect pollination of angiosperms is a tumbling flower beetle (Mordellidae) preserved in Burmese amber about 99 million years old with pollen grains of a eudicot on its body (Bao and coauthors 2019). The traits of flowers adapted to each class of pollinators, the **pollination syndromes**, are described in [7.12.29](/7.12.29); here we summarize the pollinators.

Bees are the most effective pollinators because both sexes visit flowers throughout their adult life and females collect pollen for their larvae; their branched hairs, which hold grains electrostatically, deposit pollen on stigmas when they visit the next flower. Bumble bees even detect the weak electric fields around flowers, which change when a flower has just been visited (Clarke and coauthors 2013). Bees see ultraviolet, blue and green ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)), learn colours, scents and shapes rapidly (honey bees learn a colour associated with sugar water after one to three rewards, violet and blue fastest), and many species are oligolectic, collecting pollen from a few related plants. Insect-pollinated crops depend on them: about three quarters of the leading crop species benefit from animal pollination, although these crops account for about a third of world crop production by volume, since the staples (cereals) are wind-pollinated or selfing (Klein and coauthors 2007). In some orchards of southwestern China, where wild pollinators have become scarce and pesticide use is heavy, pear and apple flowers are pollinated by hand, an illustration of the work insects normally do free.

**Definition 37.4.1 (Flower Constancy)** **Flower constancy** is the tendency of an individual pollinator to visit flowers of one plant species during a foraging trip, bypassing other rewarding species.

Aristotle noted that honey bees keep to one kind of flower on each trip. Constancy benefits the plant, since a constant visitor carries conspecific pollen and does not waste it on other species. For the pollinator, constancy appears to be a consequence of the limits of learning and memory: handling a familiar flower type is faster, and switching costs time while the bee retrieves the motor routine for another type. Constancy creates reproductive isolation between plants with different flowers, and shifts between pollinators with different preferences are a major cause of speciation in angiosperms ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)).

### 37.4.2. The Economics of a Foraging Trip

A pollinator's visits are governed by energy. A bumble bee of 0.2 g in flight spends about 0.1 W (Heinrich 1979), so a trip of 30 minutes costs about 180 J, the energy content of about 11 mg of sugar; a flower of a bumble-bee plant typically offers 0.1–2 mg of sugar in its nectar. The plant must offer enough to make visits worthwhile, but not so much that the bee is satiated after a few flowers and carries pollen to few plants; nectar volumes are therefore small and variable, and many plants refill their nectaries slowly, which keeps pollinators moving. Bumble bees regulate their thoracic temperature by shivering their flight muscles before take-off and by controlling the flow of hemolymph between thorax and abdomen ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)), and can forage at air temperatures near 0 °C, which makes them the main pollinators of arctic and alpine plants and of early spring flowers. The rules by which foraging pollinators leave patches of flowers follow predictions of optimal foraging theory, treated in animal behaviour ([7.18](/7.18)).

### 37.4.3. Specialization and Coevolution in Pollination

The match between the length of the proboscis and the depth of the floral tube is the most famous case of apparent coevolution. Darwin predicted, from the 30-cm nectar spur of the Madagascan comet orchid *Angraecum sesquipedale*, a moth with a proboscis long enough to reach the nectar, which was found in 1903 as *Xanthopan morganii praedicta* ([7.12.29](/7.12.29)). Field experiments on other systems show reciprocal selection: in long-tubed irises pollinated by the long-proboscid fly *Moegistorhynchus longirostris*, plants with longer tubes receive more pollen and flies with longer proboscises obtain more nectar (Pauw and coauthors 2009; [7.10.17](/7.10.17)). But a match can also arise by one-sided adaptation: a moth may evolve a long proboscis to feed while hovering away from ambushing spiders, and the orchid may then adapt to the moth. In most pollination systems the plant is strongly selected by its pollinators while the pollinators, which visit many plants, are hardly selected by any one of them.

### 37.4.4. Figs and Fig Wasps

The most specialized pollination systems are nursery pollination mutualisms, in which the pollinator's larvae develop in the flowers it pollinates and eat some of the seeds. The fig–fig wasp system is the largest and most studied.

**Definition 37.4.2 (Fig–Fig Wasp Mutualism)** The **fig–fig wasp mutualism** is the obligate association between the about 800 species of *Ficus* (Moraceae) and their pollinators, the fig wasps (Agaonidae, Chalcidoidea): the flowers of a fig are enclosed in an urn-shaped inflorescence, the syconium (**hypanthodium**, [7.12.27](/7.12.27)), which only fig wasps can enter, and the wasps can develop only in fig ovaries.

The life cycle of a monoecious fig (with male and female flowers in each syconium) runs in phases that are synchronous within a tree but not among trees:

1. Receptive phase. The young syconium releases a species-specific blend of volatiles. A female wasp, about 1–2 mm long and carrying pollen, finds it and forces her way through the ostiole, a narrow channel lined with overlapping bracts; she usually loses her wings and parts of her antennae on the way and never leaves.
2. Pollination and oviposition. Inside, she walks over the stigmas of the female flowers, inserting her ovipositor down the styles and laying eggs in some ovules, and pollinates the flowers. Each egg induces a gall in which the larva eats the developing endosperm. She dies inside the fig.
3. Interfloral phase. Over several weeks, the galled flowers produce wasps and the pollinated, ungalled flowers produce seeds.
4. Male phase. Wingless males emerge first, chew into the galls containing females, mate with them through the gall wall (so that matings are among siblings and cousins, the basis of **local mate competition**, [7.17.34](/7.17.34)), and then chew an exit tunnel through the fig wall. At this time the male flowers open. Females emerge, load pollen, and leave through the tunnel to find a receptive syconium on another tree, living only one to two days as adults.
5. Ripening. The syconium ripens into the "fruit" eaten by bats, birds and monkeys, which disperse the seeds.

![The life cycle of a fig wasp in a monoecious fig (schematic). A pollen-carrying female enters a receptive syconium through the ostiole, pollinates the female flowers and lays eggs in some ovules, which become galls; she dies inside. Weeks later, wingless males emerge, mate with the females still in their galls and chew an exit hole; the females collect pollen from the now-open male flowers and fly to a receptive syconium on another tree, which must be found within one or two days. Ovules that received pollen but no egg become seeds. In gynodioecious figs (about half of all species, including the cultivated fig) the functions are separated: wasps develop only in the syconia of functionally male trees, and seeds form only on female trees, whose syconia the wasps enter but cannot breed in.](./plots/fig-wasp-cycle.py)

The system is stable although the wasps eat seeds. A fig wasp's ovipositor is short, and it can usually lay eggs only in ovules with short styles, which lie in the inner layer of the syconium; the outer, long-styled ovules are protected and become seeds. In gynodioecious figs, about half of the species, this division is complete: female trees produce syconia with long-styled flowers only, which wasps enter and pollinate but cannot lay eggs in, so that the wasps that enter them die without offspring, deceived by the identical scent. In actively pollinating fig wasps, two thirds of the species, females collect pollen from the anthers into pockets on the thorax with their coxal combs and deliberately deposit it on the stigmas; passively pollinating species carry pollen loosely on the body, and their figs produce more pollen (Kjellberg and coauthors 2001). Figs sanction cheaters: in experiments with *Ficus* in Panama, syconia entered by wasps that carried no pollen were often aborted, killing the wasp's offspring, and pollen-free wasps were rare in species whose figs sanctioned most strongly (Jandér and Herre 2010).

Specificity is high but not absolute. The classical rule "one fig species, one wasp species" has many exceptions: many figs have two or more pollinator species, some wasps pollinate two figs, and host switches and hybridization have occurred. Molecular phylogenies show a broad congruence of the two groups at the level of genera and sections and an age of the mutualism of about 75 million years, with many local departures from strict cospeciation (Cruaud and coauthors 2012). The wasps must find a receptive fig of their own species, often scattered in a tropical forest; tiny females ride the wind above the canopy, and genetic analysis of the pollinators of the desert fig *Ficus sycomorus* in Namibia showed dispersal over more than 100 km (Ahmed and coauthors 2009). Because a fig population must flower asynchronously to keep its pollinators alive through the year, and because figs fruit in seasons when other food is scarce, figs are keystone resources for fruit-eating vertebrates in tropical forests.

Syconia also host non-pollinating fig wasps of several chalcid families: gallers that lay eggs from outside through the fig wall with ovipositors several times their body length, and parasitoids of the pollinators and of the gallers. In China, figs are diverse in the south (Xishuangbanna, Hainan), where the fig–wasp system has been studied extensively; the creeping fig *Ficus pumila*, whose seeds are used to make the jelly dessert *aiyu* in Taiwan, is pollinated by *Wiebesia pumilae*, and the Chinese banyan *F. microcarpa* by *Eupristina verticillata*, which was carried with planted banyans to Florida and other regions, where the trees now set seed and spread.

Nursery pollination has evolved repeatedly. Yuccas and yucca moths ([7.10.17](/7.10.17), [7.12.29](/7.12.29)) are the classic North American case. In the leafflower plants *Glochidion* and *Breynia* (Phyllanthaceae) of tropical and subtropical Asia, including southern China, female moths of the genus *Epicephala* (Gracillariidae) actively pollinate the flowers with a proboscis covered in sensory hairs and lay eggs in the ovary, and their larvae eat a few of the seeds; some 500 species of *Glochidion* may each have their own moth (Kato and coauthors 2003). Globeflowers (*Trollius*) and *Chiastocheta* flies, and senita cacti and *Upiga* moths are other examples. All share active pollination, larvae that eat only part of the seed crop, and plant traits that limit how many seeds the pollinators destroy.

## 37.5. Ants and Plants

### 37.5.1. Ant–Plants

**Definition 37.5.1 (Myrmecophyte, Domatium, Extrafloral Nectary)** A **myrmecophyte** (ant–plant) is a plant that houses ant colonies in **domatia**, cavities such as hollow thorns, stems or swollen petioles that develop without the ants; many also feed the ants with food bodies or with nectar from **extrafloral nectaries**, nectar glands outside the flowers that are not involved in pollination.

The classic study is Janzen's (1966) of the bull-horn acacia *Vachellia* (formerly *Acacia*) *cornigera* and its ant *Pseudomyrmex ferrugineus* in Mexico. The plant produces swollen hollow stipular thorns in which the ants nest, extrafloral nectaries on the petioles, and protein- and lipid-rich food bodies (Beltian bodies) on the leaflet tips, which feed the larvae. The ants patrol the plant day and night, attack herbivorous insects and vertebrates, and cut vines and seedlings of other plants that touch it. When Janzen removed the ants with insecticide or by cutting out the thorns, the acacias were heavily damaged by insects, grew less and were overgrown by competitors; most died within a year. The acacia lacks the cyanogenic glycosides of its non-myrmecophytic relatives, which suggests that ant protection replaced chemical defence.

The interaction contains manipulation. Extrafloral nectar of the ant-acacias contains no sucrose, because the plant adds an invertase that splits it into glucose and fructose; adult workers of the obligate ant partners have lost their own gut invertase activity, so they cannot use sucrose-rich nectar from other sources and are tied to their host, and the invertase is inhibited in nectar by a chitinase-like protein that also protects it from microbial spoilage (Heil and coauthors 2005, 2014). Other myrmecophytes have evolved the same system independently: *Cecropia* trees with *Azteca* ants in the Neotropics, which feed the ants with glycogen-rich Müllerian bodies; *Macaranga* in Southeast Asia, many of whose species have hollow stems inhabited by *Crematogaster* ants and waxy stems that other ants cannot climb; and epiphytic ant-plants such as *Myrmecodia* and *Dischidia*, whose domatia absorb nutrients from the ants' refuse. Extrafloral nectaries are far more widespread than domatia, known from about 4000 species in more than 100 families of vascular plants (Weber and Keeler 2013); they attract opportunistic ants, which reduce herbivory on average but not always.

### 37.5.2. Trophobiosis

Many ants tend sap-sucking Hemiptera (aphids, scale insects, mealybugs, treehoppers) and caterpillars of lycaenid butterflies for their sugary excretions, **honeydew** and the secretions of special glands. In return the ants protect them from predators and parasitoids, remove honeydew that would otherwise foster sooty moulds, and sometimes carry them to new feeding sites or into their nests; tended aphid colonies grow faster, and some aphids have lost the anal cauda that flicks away honeydew in untended species. The association, called trophobiosis, affects plants indirectly: tended aphids damage the plant more, while the ants may also deter chewing herbivores. In citrus orchards of southern China the weaver ant *Oecophylla smaragdina* has been used against pests since at least the 4th century ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)), but ants that tend scale insects and aphids, such as *Lasius* and the invasive Argentine ant, often increase pest damage and are controlled in orchards with sticky bands on the trunks. About three quarters of lycaenid caterpillars associate with ants; in the large blue *Phengaris* (formerly *Maculinea*) the caterpillar is carried into the nest of a *Myrmica* ant by mimicking the ants' brood odour and then eats the brood, a social parasite treated in [7.17.38](/7.17.38).

### 37.5.3. Seed Dispersal by Ants

**Definition 37.5.2 (Myrmecochory)** **Myrmecochory** is the dispersal of seeds by ants, which carry seeds bearing a lipid-rich appendage, the **elaiosome**, to their nests, feed the elaiosome to their larvae and discard the intact seed in the nest or on a refuse pile.

Myrmecochory has evolved independently more than 100 times and occurs in about 11,000 plant species (Lengyel and coauthors 2010), especially in spring herbs of temperate deciduous forests (violets, *Corydalis*, trilliums) and in the sclerophyll shrublands of Australia and South Africa. Elaiosomes contain oleic acid and diglycerides that resemble the chemical signals of dead insects, which ants carry home as food. The plant benefits through dispersal away from the parent, escape from seed predators and fire in underground nests, and germination in nutrient-enriched soil. Unlike the fig system, myrmecochory is a diffuse mutualism: no plant depends on a single ant species, and the ants show no special adaptations to it.

## 37.6. Galls

**Definition 37.6.1 (Insect Gall)** An **insect gall** (zoocecidium) is an abnormal growth of plant tissue, by enlargement (hypertrophy) and multiplication (hyperplasia) of cells, that is induced by an insect and in which the insect lives and feeds.

About 13,000 gall-inducing insect species have been described, about 2% of insects (Gullan and Cranston), and estimates of the total number run to more than 100,000. The main groups are gall midges (Cecidomyiidae, the most species-rich), cynipid gall wasps (mostly on oaks and roses), sternorrhynchan Hemiptera (aphids, psyllids, scale insects and phylloxerans) and thrips, with smaller numbers of tephritid flies, chalcid wasps (including the fig wasps), sawflies (*Pontania* on willows), moths and weevils. Galls range from simple swellings to highly organized structures: cynipid galls on oak have an inner layer of nutritive cells rich in protein, lipid and sugar on which the larva feeds, a hard sclerenchyma layer, a spongy outer layer and often hairs, spines or sticky coatings, and their form is so species-specific that species are often identified from the gall alone. A gall is an extended phenotype of the insect: the plant's genes build it, but its form is determined by the genes of the insect.

| Type | Formation | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Covering gall | Plant tissue grows around the insect and encloses it | Cynipid galls on oaks; gall-forming scale insects |
| Pouch gall | The leaf blade bulges on one side around a feeding nymph, forming a pocket open on the other | Many psyllids and aphids |
| Roll and fold galls | Differential growth rolls or folds a leaf around the insect | Many aphids and thrips |
| Mark gall | The egg is laid inside the tissue, and the gall develops around the developing larva | Sawfly galls on willow |
| Bud and rosette galls | A bud enlarges or produces many small leaves | Gall midges on willows ("willow roses") |

Gall induction is a manipulation of plant development. Plant hormones are involved: gall-inducing sawflies, aphids and gall midges synthesize auxin and cytokinins, and galls are strong sinks for photosynthates. The insect controls the outcome through secreted proteins. Larvae of the Hessian fly *Mayetiola destructor* inject saliva rich in small secreted proteins, effectors analogous to those of plant pathogens, which are recognized by the products of resistance genes in resistant wheat cultivars (a gene-for-gene relation like that of rust fungi; Zhao and coauthors 2015). In the witch-hazel gall aphid *Hormaphis cornu*, a family of hundreds of rapidly evolving salivary-gland genes, named *bicycle* genes after a pair of cysteine–tyrosine motifs, is expressed specifically in the gall-forming generation, and allelic variation in one of them determines the colour of the gall (Korgaonkar and coauthors 2021).

Three advantages of gall living have been proposed: a nutritious diet, since gall tissue concentrates nutrients and is low in defensive compounds (the nutrition hypothesis); a protected microenvironment that buffers desiccation (the microenvironment hypothesis); and protection against natural enemies (the enemy hypothesis). The last is the weakest: galls are attacked by specialized parasitoids, and also by inquilines, other insects that cannot induce galls themselves and live in the galls of others, so that a single oak gall may contain a community of a dozen species.

Some galls are economically important. The grape phylloxera *Daktulosphaira vitifoliae*, native to eastern North America, has a holocycle on its native vines that includes leaf-galling and root-galling generations; introduced into Europe in the 1860s with American vines, its root-feeding forms destroyed most of the vineyards of France within three decades, and European viticulture was saved only by grafting European cultivars onto resistant American rootstocks, still the practice today. The Chinese gall aphid *Schlechtendalia chinensis* induces galls on the leaf rachis of the sumac *Rhus chinensis*; the dried galls, *wubeizi* (Chinese gallnuts), contain 50–70% tannin and have been a source of tannic acid, gallic acid, dyes and medicine in China for centuries. Its life cycle alternates between the sumac, on which the fundatrix induces the gall and her offspring multiply inside it, and mosses (*Plagiomnium*), on which the aphids overwinter; cultivating the moss under sumac trees is the basis of gallnut production in Hubei, Hunan, Guizhou and Sichuan.

## 37.7. Insects Against Weeds

Many weeds are plants introduced without their natural enemies. In classical biological control of weeds, host-specific herbivores are collected in the weed's native range, tested for specificity in quarantine, and released. The model case is the prickly pear cactus (*Opuntia stricta* and others) in Australia, which by 1925 covered about 24 million hectares of Queensland and New South Wales. After the release of the Argentine cactus moth *Cactoblastis cactorum* from 1926, whose caterpillars tunnel in the cladodes in groups and admit rot, the cactus collapsed within about seven years, and the land was resettled; a memorial hall in Queensland was named after the moth. Other successes include the weevil *Cyrtobagous salviniae* against the floating fern *Salvinia molesta*, which had choked lakes and rivers in Africa, Asia and Australasia, and the flea beetle *Agasicles hygrophila*, introduced into China from the United States in 1986, against the invasive alligator weed *Alternanthera philoxeroides* in water bodies of southern China. The ragweed leaf beetle *Ophraella communa*, which arrived in China accidentally around 2001, is now mass-reared and released against common ragweed *Ambrosia artemisiifolia*, a source of allergenic pollen.

The method carries the risk that an agent attacks native plants. *Cactoblastis cactorum*, spread to the Caribbean and then to Florida, now threatens native *Opuntia* in North America; the thistle weevil *Rhinocyllus conicus*, released in North America against introduced thistles, also attacks native thistles (Louda and coauthors 1997). Modern programmes therefore test candidate agents on a wide range of plants related to the target and release only highly specific ones; on the whole, about a third to a half of established agents contribute to the control of their target weed, and severe effects on non-target plants have been rare when agents were selected for specificity.

## Summary

- About half of insect species are phytophagous (Definition 37.1.1). Plant food is poor in nitrogen and sterols and defended; phytophagous clades are nevertheless more diverse than their sister groups.
- Feeding guilds: leaf chewers, sap suckers (phloem, xylem, mesophyll), leaf miners (Definition 37.1.2; only holometabolous larvae), borers, gall inducers, seed and fruit feeders, root feeders and flower visitors. The guild determines damage and control.
- Host range (Definition 37.1.3): most herbivores are mono- or oligophagous; explanations are physiological trade-offs, neural constraints, enemy-free space and phylogenetic inertia. Host races (*Rhagoletis*) show that host shifts can lead to speciation. Host selection proceeds by distant orientation to volatiles and colours, contact chemoreception and acceptance.
- Plant defences are physical (trichomes, silica, toughness, wax, latex canals) and chemical (secondary compounds, Definition 37.2.1: alkaloids, cyanogenic glycosides, glucosinolates, cardenolides, furanocoumarins, terpenoids, tannins, proteinase inhibitors). Defences may be constitutive or induced by jasmonate signalling, and direct or indirect (volatiles attracting enemies, extrafloral nectar).
- Insects counter with avoidance (vein cutting), excretion, detoxification (P450s, GSTs, sulfatase, nitrile-specifier protein), target-site insensitivity (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase substitutions, sufficient for cardenolide resistance in edited *Drosophila*) and sequestration (Definition 37.2.2; monarch, *Utetheisa*).
- Escape-and-radiate coevolution (Ehrlich and Raven) is supported by the Brassicales–Pierinae and *Bursera*–*Blepharida* systems, but most interactions are diffuse, and insects colonized already diversified plant groups.
- Pollinators are beetles, flies, bees, wasps, Lepidoptera and thrips; bees are the most effective, through hairy bodies, pollen collection, colour learning and flower constancy (Definition 37.4.1). Floral rewards are balanced against pollinator energy needs.
- Fig–fig wasp mutualism (Definition 37.4.2): pollinators breed in short-styled ovules of the syconium; sib-mating inside the fig; active pollination; sanctions by abortion; broad but imperfect cospeciation over about 75 million years. Yucca moths and *Epicephala* leafflower moths are other nursery pollinators.
- Ant–plants (Definition 37.5.1) house and feed defending ants (*Vachellia*–*Pseudomyrmex*, *Cecropia*–*Azteca*, *Macaranga*–*Crematogaster*); ants tend honeydew producers (trophobiosis) and disperse elaiosome-bearing seeds (Definition 37.5.2).
- Insect galls (Definition 37.6.1) are induced by effector proteins and hormones and are extended phenotypes of the insect; phylloxera and the Chinese gall aphid are economically important. Host-specific insects such as *Cactoblastis*, *Cyrtobagous* and *Agasicles* control invasive weeds, with a risk to non-target plants.

## Questions

1. A leaf shows a narrow, winding tunnel that widens gradually and contains a black central line. Which kinds of insects could have made it, and which could not? What does the widening tell you?

   **Answer** It is a serpentine leaf mine, made by a larva feeding between the two epidermal layers. Only holometabolous larvae make leaf mines: flies (mostly Agromyzidae), moths (Gracillariidae, Nepticulidae and others), beetles (some leaf beetles, jewel beetles and weevils) and sawflies. Nymphs of hemimetabolous insects (bugs, grasshoppers, thrips) do not mine. The widening records the growth of the larva as it fed, and the black line is its frass. The host plant and the shape of the mine and frass line usually identify the species; agromyzid larvae, for example, feed with their mouth hooks lying on one side and leave frass in two alternating rows.

2. Explain how a monarch caterpillar can feed on milkweed, which contains cardenolides and latex, and why a blue jay that has eaten one adult monarch may refuse others.

   **Answer** The caterpillar first cuts a trench across the leaf or chews through the midrib, draining the latex canals beyond the cut, and then feeds beyond it, avoiding being glued by latex. Cardenolides inhibit the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, but the monarch's ATPase carries amino acid substitutions (at positions 111, 119 and 122) that make it insensitive. The caterpillar sequesters the cardenolides and passes them to the adult. A jay that eats a cardenolide-rich adult vomits, learns to associate the orange-and-black pattern with illness and then avoids monarchs on sight; this is aposematism. Monarchs reared on cardenolide-poor milkweeds are palatable, so protection depends on the host plant.

3. Feeny proposed that oak trees and cabbages defend themselves differently. State the hypothesis and evaluate it.

   **Answer** The apparency hypothesis holds that large, long-lived, abundant plants (apparent plants such as oaks) cannot escape herbivores in space or time and should invest in quantitative, dose-dependent defences (tannins, toughness, low nitrogen) that reduce digestibility for all herbivores and are hard to evolve around; small, ephemeral, scattered plants (unapparent plants such as crucifers) escape many herbivores and should use cheap, qualitative toxins effective in small doses (glucosinolates), which generalists avoid but specialists can overcome. Oak leaves do become tough and tannin-rich after the spring flush, and crucifers do support specialist pierids and diamondback moths adapted to glucosinolates. But many apparent plants also contain potent toxins, quantitative and qualitative defences grade into each other, and apparency is hard to measure; resource availability (growth rate) and induced defence explain much variation that apparency does not.

4. In a monoecious fig, why do the fig wasps not destroy all the seeds, and why does a pollen-free female fig wasp have fewer offspring than a pollinating one?

   **Answer** The wasp's ovipositor is short, and it can lay eggs only in ovules with short styles, in the inner layer of flowers; the long-styled outer ovules, if pollinated, become seeds. The plant therefore retains a share of its ovules. Pollen-free females (cheaters) save the cost of collecting and depositing pollen, but many figs abort syconia that receive no pollen, killing the larvae inside, and in some species the galled ovules develop better when pollinated; cheaters therefore leave fewer offspring. These sanctions, together with the limited reach of the ovipositor, stabilize the mutualism.

5. How did Janzen show that the ants of the bull-horn acacia benefit the plant, and what indicates that the ants are not free agents in the mutualism?

   **Answer** He removed the ants from some plants (by insecticide or by cutting off the occupied thorns) and compared them with plants that kept their ants. Unoccupied plants suffered much more damage by herbivorous insects, grew more slowly, were overgrown by neighbouring plants and mostly died, while occupied plants thrived. The plant controls its partners: its extrafloral nectar contains invertase, so it is sucrose-free, and the obligate ant partners have lost their own invertase activity as adults; they cannot use other sucrose-rich nectars and depend on their host. The acacia also provides food bodies only for the larvae of its ants.

6. (Open question) The *Drosophila* experiment of Karageorgi and coauthors showed that three amino acid substitutions can confer cardenolide resistance. Why did the substitutions arise in a particular order in the monarch's ancestors, and what does this imply for predicting adaptation?

   **Answer** Each substitution affects not only binding of the toxin but also the normal function of the ATPase; one of the resistance substitutions alone impairs the enzyme and causes neurological defects, and is tolerated only after an earlier, permissive substitution has been fixed. The order is therefore constrained by epistasis and pleiotropy: the fitness effect of a mutation depends on the genetic background. The same positions were changed in many independent cardenolide-feeding insects, which shows that adaptation can be repeatable and predictable at the level of the target site, but the path is restricted to a few orders of mutations, and in some lineages gene duplication relaxed the constraint by letting one copy specialize. Predicting adaptation requires knowledge of the fitness landscape, including such interactions, not only of the selective agent.
