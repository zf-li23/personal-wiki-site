Every vertebrate must move oxygen from the environment into its blood and carbon dioxide out of it, across a thin, moist, well-perfused surface that is ventilated by water or air. Water and air pose opposite problems ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.2.3)). Water is dense and viscous and holds about 30 times less oxygen than air at the same partial pressure, so water breathers must pass large volumes over their exchange surfaces, preferably in one direction; air is light and rich in oxygen but dries the surfaces, so air breathers keep them inside the body in sacs ventilated by pumping. The vertebrate answers are gills, pumped by the pharynx, and lungs, outgrowths of the pharynx that are filled by pumps of increasing sophistication.

This chapter first surveys the respiratory surfaces and their homologies (§64.1), then compares gills (§64.2), discusses the origin of lungs and of the swim bladder (§64.3), and follows the lungs through the tetrapods (§64.4), with a separate section on the unique system of birds (§64.5). Section 64.6 treats the airways and vocal organs, §64.7 the evolution of the ventilation mechanisms and §64.8 the class-by-class comparison. The physics of gas exchange, the structure of alveoli, gas transport and the control of breathing are in [7.14.17](/7.14.17)–[7.14.20](/7.14.20); the swim bladder as a buoyancy organ in [7.17.47](/7.17.47); the development of the lung in [7.5.30](/7.5.30#sec-30.7). The blood vessels that supply gills and lungs, the aortic arches, are the subject of [7.17.65](/7.17.65).

## 64.1. Respiratory Organs and Their Homologies

### 64.1.1. The Respiratory Surfaces

All vertebrate respiratory organs share the same physical design: a large surface, a thin barrier between medium and blood, a rich capillary supply and a mechanism that renews the medium at the surface. They differ in their position and origin.

| Organ | Position and origin | Medium | Main users |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internal gills | Walls of the pharyngeal pouches and slits; gill arches | Water | Cyclostomes, fishes, larval amphibians (internal gills of tadpoles) |
| External gills | Outgrowths of the skin over the pharyngeal arches | Water | Larvae of lungfishes, bichirs, amphibians; paedomorphic salamanders |
| Skin | Integument with capillaries close to the surface | Water or air | Amphibians (all; the only organ of lungless salamanders), many fishes, sea snakes, turtles |
| Buccopharyngeal mucosa | Lining of mouth and pharynx | Air or water | Frogs (oscillations of the mouth floor), some air-breathing fishes, softshell turtles |
| Lungs | Ventral outgrowth of the pharynx | Air | Lungfishes, bichirs, tetrapods |
| Swim bladder | Dorsal outgrowth of the foregut | Air (in air breathers) | Gars, bowfin, many teleosts as accessory air-breathing organ |
| Accessory organs | Suprabranchial chambers (labyrinth organ of gouramis, arborescent organ of air-breathing catfishes), vascular stomach or intestine (loaches, armoured catfishes), cloacal bursae (some turtles) | Air or water | Air-breathing fishes, turtles |

Most vertebrates use more than one surface, and the partition between them depends on the medium and on the gas: in amphibians the skin eliminates most of the carbon dioxide, which is far more soluble in water than oxygen, while the lungs take up most of the oxygen (**cutaneous respiration**, [7.17.49](/7.17.49#sec-49.6)).

### 64.1.2. Gills and Lungs Are Analogous

Gills and lungs are functionally similar but not homologous. Both develop from the pharynx, but gills develop from the lateral walls of the pharyngeal pouches and the arches between them, lungs from a ventral outgrowth of the floor of the pharynx behind the last pouch. In fishes the blood flows from the heart through the gills and then to the body; in tetrapods the lungs receive blood from the sixth aortic arch and return it to the heart ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)). The swim bladder and the lungs, by contrast, are homologous: both are outgrowths of the foregut supplied by branches of the sixth arch (§64.3). The traditional formulation, "gills and lungs are analogous, the swim bladder and lungs homologous", remains correct; what has changed is the direction of the transformation between swim bladder and lung.

## 64.2. Gills

### 64.2.1. Structure of a Gill

A vertebrate gill is a series of **pharyngeal slits** separated by gill arches. Each arch carries a skeletal bar, an afferent branchial artery from the ventral aorta, an efferent branchial artery to the dorsal aorta, branchial muscles and a branch of a cranial nerve (IX or X) ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.5); [7.17.65](/7.17.65)). On each face of an arch sits a row of *gill filaments* (primary lamellae), and each filament bears on its upper and lower surfaces many thin plates, the *secondary lamellae*, which are the actual exchange surface. A secondary lamella is a sheet of blood channels between two thin epithelia, held apart by pillar cells; the barrier between water and blood is only $1$–$10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ thick. Each arch with its two rows of filaments is a *holobranch*; a row on one face only is a *hemibranch*.

The arrangement of filaments and lamellae sets up countercurrent exchange: water passes between the lamellae from the pharyngeal side to the outside, while blood flows through each lamella in the opposite direction, from the efferent to the afferent side of the filament. Blood leaving a lamella has met the freshest water, and teleosts extract up to $80\,\%$ of the oxygen from the water passing their gills ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.3.3)). The gills are also the main site of nitrogen excretion (as ammonia) and, through their ionocytes (chloride cells), of ion regulation in fishes ([7.14.21](/7.14.21)).

The gill surfaces of fishes are said to be ectodermal in origin in older texts (the gill filaments forming on the outer, skin-covered parts of the arches). Lineage tracing in fishes and lampreys shows that the respiratory epithelium of the gill filaments derives from pharyngeal endoderm, as does that of the lungs; only the external gills of larvae are covered by ectoderm.

### 64.2.2. Three Types of Gills

*Pouched gills of cyclostomes.* Lampreys have seven pairs of spherical gill pouches, each lined with gill folds and connected by a narrow inner opening to the pharynx and by an outer opening to the outside. In the adult the pharynx divides into a dorsal oesophagus and a ventral blind respiratory tube from which the pouches open; while the lamprey is attached to its host by the oral disc, water is pumped in and out through the external openings by the elastic branchial basket and the constrictor muscles. Hagfishes have 5–16 pairs of gill pouches whose ducts join into a common external opening on each side in some species; water enters through the single nostril and is pumped by a velum.

*Septal gills of elasmobranchs.* In sharks and rays each gill arch carries a long interbranchial septum of connective tissue and muscle that extends to the skin and ends as a flap covering the next gill slit; the filaments are attached along the faces of the septum. There are five (in some species six or seven) external gill slits, and in front of them the spiracle, a reduced first gill slit between the mandibular and hyoid arches that carries a small pseudobranch. Rays and skates, lying on the bottom, take in water through the dorsal spiracles and release it through ventral gill slits. Chimaeras have a fleshy operculum covering four gill openings.

*Opercular gills of bony fishes.* In actinopterygians the interbranchial septum is reduced to a short base, so the two hemibranchs of each arch project freely as V-shaped rows of filaments; the arches are covered by a bony operculum, and the four gill arches open into a common opercular cavity with one external opening on each side. A reduced septum lets the filaments of neighbouring arches touch at their tips, forming a sieve through which all water must pass between the lamellae. Sturgeons and other basal actinopterygians have longer septa than teleosts, which shows the transition.

The reduction of the interbranchial septum (long in elasmobranchs, shorter in sturgeons, almost absent in teleosts) and the covering of the gill slits by an operculum are two trends from the condition of early gnathostomes. The number of gill slits also decreased: seven pouches in lampreys, five slits plus a spiracle in most elasmobranchs, four gill arches behind the hyoid in teleosts; in tetrapods gill slits break through only in larval amphibians.

### 64.2.3. Ventilation of Gills

Fish gills are ventilated by a double pump: a buccal pressure pump in front (the mouth cavity compressed by the hypobranchial and branchiomeric muscles, [7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.5)) and an opercular (or parabranchial) suction pump behind (the opercular cavity expanded by the opercular muscles), working so that water flows almost continuously from the mouth over the gills to the outside, with valves at the mouth and under the opercula preventing backflow. Fast swimmers (tunas, many sharks, mackerels) switch to ram ventilation: they keep the mouth open and let their forward motion drive water through the gills; some tunas and sharks cannot pump and must swim to breathe ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.2)).

### 64.2.4. Gill Design and Activity

The dimensions of gills follow the activity of the fish. Gas uptake across a respiratory surface obeys Fick's law ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.2.2)),

$$
\dot M_{\ce{O2}} = K\,\frac{A}{d}\,\Delta P_{\ce{O2}},
$$
where $A$ is the surface area, $d$ the thickness of the barrier, $\Delta P_{\ce{O2}}$ the mean partial-pressure difference between water and blood and $K$ Krogh's diffusion constant of the tissue. A fish can raise its oxygen uptake by enlarging $A$ (more and longer filaments, more closely spaced lamellae), by thinning $d$, and by keeping $\Delta P_{\ce{O2}}$ large through countercurrent flow and high ventilation.

**Example 64.2.1 (Gill Morphometry and Diffusing Capacity)** Take a sluggish bottom-dwelling fish with a barrier of about $5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and compare it with a tuna, whose lamellar barrier is about $0.5$–$1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ thick and whose gill area per unit body mass is roughly an order of magnitude larger. The ratio $A/d$, the anatomical diffusing capacity, is then about $10 \times (5/0.75) \approx 70$ times higher in the tuna, which matches the large difference in maximum metabolic rate between the two lifestyles. The cost is that a large, thin gill also exchanges ions and water with the environment, which increases the cost of osmoregulation; fishes adjust the functional surface by recruiting more lamellae when oxygen demand is high (lamellar recruitment) and by remodelling the gill in some species (crucian carp grow a cell mass between the lamellae in cold, well-oxygenated water and lose it in hypoxia).

The same equation explains the trend of barrier thickness across air breathers: about $0.5$–$1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in reptiles and amphibians, about $0.6\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ (arithmetic mean) in mammals and $0.1$–$0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in birds, the most active aerobic vertebrates. Birds achieve this extremely thin barrier because their rigid air capillaries are not stretched and folded during breathing, unlike the compliant alveoli of mammals ([7.14.18](/7.14.18#sec-18.7.3)).

### 64.2.5. External Gills

External gills are filamentous outgrowths of the skin over the third to fifth pharyngeal arches, supplied by loops of the aortic arches. They occur in the larvae of lungfishes (*Protopterus*, *Lepidosiren*), bichirs and amphibians, and are therefore probably an ancestral feature of osteichthyan larvae. Frog tadpoles first have three pairs of external gills, which are soon covered by a fold of skin, the operculum, and replaced by internal gills in a branchial chamber opening through a single spiracle (usually on the left). Salamander larvae keep external gills until metamorphosis, and paedomorphic salamanders (axolotl, *Necturus*, olm) keep them throughout life ([7.17.49](/7.17.49)). Amniote embryos form pharyngeal pouches but no gills; in reptiles and birds some pouches briefly open as slits, in mammals they do not open.

## 64.3. Lungs and the Swim Bladder

### 64.3.1. Lungs Are Ancestral in Bony Fishes

Traditional accounts derive the lungs of tetrapods from the swim bladder of ancestral fishes, which would have become respiratory in the oxygen-poor waters of the Devonian. The comparative and developmental evidence now points in the opposite direction: lungs are the ancestral condition of the Osteichthyes, and the swim bladder of most ray-finned fishes is a derived, dorsally displaced lung.

- *Distribution.* The most basal living actinopterygians, the bichirs (Polypteridae), have paired ventral lungs that open by a ventral glottis into the floor of the pharynx and are supplied by the sixth aortic arch, exactly like those of lungfishes and tetrapods. Lungfishes (paired lungs in *Protopterus* and *Lepidosiren*, a single lung in *Neoceratodus*), the coelacanth (a vestigial lung beside a fat-filled organ, relatively larger in the embryo) and tetrapods all have ventral lungs. On the phylogeny ([7.17.47](/7.17.47), [7.17.48](/7.17.48)) the simplest reconstruction is a ventral lung in the common ancestor of all bony fishes.
- *Development.* Lungs and swim bladders arise from the same region of the foregut and share the expression of the same genes (*Tbx4*, *Nkx2.1* in part, *Shh*), but the swim bladder bud forms on the dorsal side. In the bichir and in tetrapods the lung bud is ventral and paired.
- *Blood supply.* The lungs of bichirs, lungfishes and tetrapods and the respiratory swim bladders of gars and the bowfin are supplied by arteries from the sixth aortic arch (pulmonary arteries); in teleosts the swim bladder is usually supplied by the coeliac or dorsal aorta, a derived condition.
- *Fossils.* Ossified lung walls are preserved in fossil coelacanths of the Mesozoic, which shows that their ancestors had large functional lungs, and the anatomy of Devonian lungfishes and tetrapodomorphs (spiracles, skull roofs, ribs) indicates air breathing in both actinopterygian and sarcopterygian lineages of that time.

Air breathing thus evolved once, at the base of the bony fishes, perhaps in the warm, stagnant waters of the Silurian and Devonian. In the actinopterygian lineage the lung moved to the dorsal side, became single and was transformed into a buoyancy organ, the **swim bladder**; in the sarcopterygian lineage it remained ventral and paired and became the lung of tetrapods. The two problems that troubled the traditional view, that the swim bladder is dorsal and single while lungs are ventral and paired, are thereby resolved: the dorsal single bladder is the derived state.

### 64.3.2. The Swim Bladder

The swim bladder lies dorsal to the gut, below the kidneys and vertebral column, and is filled with gas (mostly oxygen and nitrogen). In basal actinopterygians (sturgeons, gars, bowfin) and in the basal teleosts (osteoglossomorphs, eels, herrings, cyprinids, catfishes, salmonids) it is connected to the oesophagus by a pneumatic duct throughout life: these are **physostomous** fishes, which fill the bladder by gulping air at the surface and release gas through the duct. In most acanthomorph teleosts the duct closes after the larval stage: these **physoclistous** fishes regulate the volume of the bladder by secreting gas from the blood into it through a gas gland supplied by a countercurrent **rete mirabile**, and resorb gas through a thin, well-vascularized area, the oval (oval body), which can be closed off by a muscular diaphragm ([7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.6.4)). The gas gland can fill the bladder against pressures of more than 100 atmospheres in deep-sea fishes, thanks to the Root effect of fish haemoglobins and the countercurrent multiplication in the rete.

The bladder is often divided into two chambers (cyprinids), or bears anterior extensions that contact the inner ear (herrings) or connect to it by the chain of **Weberian apparatus** ossicles (Otophysi: carp, catfishes, characins), so that it serves as a resonator and pressure-to-sound transducer ([7.17.47](/7.17.47); [7.17.68](/7.17.68)). Drum muscles on the bladder produce sounds in croakers, toadfishes and gurnards. The swim bladder has been lost many times in bottom-dwelling fishes (flatfishes, many gobies, anglerfishes) and in fishes that move rapidly up and down (mackerels, some tunas), where a gas-filled organ would change volume too quickly.

In gars and the bowfin the gas bladder has a spongy, alveolar inner wall and is used for air breathing; many teleosts in warm, hypoxic waters have also returned to air breathing, either with the swim bladder (the arapaima, *Arapaima*; the tarpon) or with new accessory organs.

### 64.3.3. Air-Breathing Organs of Fishes

Air breathing evolved independently at least 38–67 times among living fishes (estimates depend on how lineages are counted). The organs used include, besides lungs and swim bladders, the suprabranchial labyrinth organ of gouramis and climbing perches (anabantoids) and the arborescent organs of air-breathing catfishes (*Clarias*), outgrowths of the opercular cavity supported by folded bone; the vascularized pharynx of snakeheads; the stomach of armoured catfishes; and the posterior intestine of loaches (*Misgurnus*), which swallow air and release the used gas through the anus. These organs share the requirements of a lung (thin barrier, rich blood supply, a way to renew air) and are classic examples of convergence.

## 64.4. The Lungs of Tetrapods

### 64.4.1. Increasing Internal Subdivision

A lung is a sac whose inner surface is enlarged by partitions (septa) into compartments of diminishing size. The comparison from amphibians to mammals is a series of increasing subdivision, with two different architectures at the end: the parabronchial lung of birds and the alveolar lung of mammals.

- *Amphibians.* The lungs are paired thin-walled sacs. In salamanders such as *Necturus* they are smooth inside, in frogs the walls bear low septa that divide the inner surface into a honeycomb of shallow chambers. The lungs are small relative to the skin: a frog's lung surface is less than its skin surface. The lungs are reduced or lost in many salamanders, completely in the lungless salamanders (Plethodontidae, about two-thirds of all salamander species), which breathe through the skin and the buccopharynx (**Plethodontidae**, [7.17.49](/7.17.49)); one caecilian (*Atretochoana*) is also lungless.
- *Reptiles.* Lungs of lepidosaurs and turtles range from single-chambered (unicameral) lungs with a central lumen surrounded by honeycomb-like compartments, the faveoli, in tuataras and most lizards, to multichambered (multicameral) lungs subdivided by large septa into many chambers, each with faveolar walls, in monitor lizards, turtles and crocodylians. Snakes and many elongate lizards have one functional lung: the left lung is reduced or absent in most snakes, and the right lung is long, with a respiratory front part and a thin-walled, non-respiratory saccular posterior part that stores air; many snakes also have a vascularized "tracheal lung" along the trachea. Chameleons have lung diverticula that inflate the body in defensive display.
- *Mammals.* The bronchi branch repeatedly into a bronchial tree of about 23 generations in humans, ending in alveolar ducts and alveoli, cup-shaped chambers about $0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter whose walls carry the capillary network ([7.14.18](/7.14.18#sec-18.1)). The human lung has about 480 million alveoli with a total gas-exchange surface of about $100$–$140\,\mathrm{m^2}$, some 70 times the body surface. The lungs are lobed: in humans the right lung has three lobes and the left two; in rabbits the left lung has two lobes and the right four.

Several functional trends run through this series ([7.14.18](/7.14.18#sec-18.7.4)). Subdivision increases the surface per volume of lung and shortens the diffusion distance from the air in the lumen to the capillaries; it also increases the volume of air that must be moved through narrowing airways, and creates dead space. The ancestral tetrapod lung, like that of a lungfish, had internal septa and faveoli, so the smooth sacs of salamanders are probably simplified rather than primitive.

### 64.4.2. Unidirectional Airflow outside Birds

Birds were long thought to be unique in having air flow in one direction through their lungs. Measurements of airflow and the anatomy of the bronchi showed in 2010–2014 that the lungs of alligators, monitor lizards and green iguanas also have unidirectional flow: air moves along a ring of secondary bronchi in the same direction during inspiration and expiration, guided only by the geometry of the branches (aerodynamic valving), without valves or air sacs. Unidirectional flow is therefore probably ancestral for archosaurs and perhaps for a much larger group of reptiles (diapsids), and it evolved before the air sacs of birds. Its original advantage may lie less in gas exchange than in efficient mixing and in breathing during locomotion.

## 64.5. The Respiratory System of Birds

The lungs of birds are small, rigid and attached to the dorsal body wall and the ribs, which indent their dorsal surface; they hardly change volume during breathing. Ventilation is driven by a system of thin-walled air sacs that act as bellows ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.4)).

The trachea divides at the syrinx into two primary bronchi (the mesobronchi), which run through each lung to the posterior air sacs. From each primary bronchus arise two groups of secondary bronchi: the ventrobronchi (cranially) and the dorsobronchi (caudally). These are connected by hundreds of parallel tubes, the **parabronchi** ($0.5$–$2\,\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter), which form the bulk of the lung (the paleopulmo, present in all birds); in most birds a second, irregular network of parabronchi, the neopulmo, connects the primary bronchus to the posterior air sacs. From the wall of each parabronchus radiate atria and infundibula that lead into a meshwork of air capillaries, $3$–$10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter, interlaced with blood capillaries; the blood–gas barrier is only about $0.1$–$0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ thick, thinner than in mammals ([7.14.18](/7.14.18#sec-18.7.3)).

Most birds have nine air sacs: an unpaired clavicular sac, paired cervical, cranial thoracic, caudal thoracic and abdominal sacs. The cranial group (cervical, clavicular, cranial thoracic) connects to the ventrobronchi, the caudal group (caudal thoracic, abdominal) to the primary bronchus. The air sacs have few blood vessels and take no part in gas exchange. Extensions of them penetrate the vertebrae, the humerus and other bones (**pneumatic bones**, [7.17.53](/7.17.53)); the same bone pneumaticity in saurischian dinosaurs and pterosaurs shows that air sacs evolved in their common ancestors.

Air flows through the parabronchi in the same direction in both phases of breathing. During inspiration the sternum moves down and forward, all air sacs expand, fresh air flows through the primary bronchus into the caudal air sacs and partly into the parabronchi via the dorsobronchi, while air from the parabronchi is drawn into the cranial sacs. During expiration the sacs are compressed; air from the caudal sacs is driven through the dorsobronchi into the parabronchi, and air from the cranial sacs leaves through the ventrobronchi and trachea. There are no anatomical valves; the direction of flow is set by the geometry of the bronchial openings and the velocity of the air (aerodynamic valving). A volume of air thus needs two breathing cycles to pass through the system, and the parabronchi receive fresh air during both inspiration and expiration (the "double respiration" of Chinese textbooks). The blood capillaries cross the parabronchi at right angles, a crosscurrent arrangement that lets the blood leaving the lung reach an oxygen partial pressure higher than that of the exhaled air ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.3.3)).

## 64.6. Airways and Vocal Organs

The airway from the nostrils to the lungs passes through the nasal cavity (with the choanae, [7.17.63](/7.17.63#sec-63.4.1)), the pharynx, the larynx, the trachea and the bronchi. Its skeleton comes from the last pharyngeal arches.

- *Nasal cavity and turbinates.* The nasal cavity of amniotes contains scroll-like turbinal bones (conchae) covered with mucosa. In mammals the maxilloturbinals (respiratory turbinates) in the main airway warm and humidify inspired air and recover heat and water from expired air by countercurrent exchange, while the ethmoturbinals carry the olfactory epithelium ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)). Birds have analogous respiratory conchae of cartilage or bone. Respiratory turbinates are found in almost all mammals and birds but not in reptiles, and because high ventilation rates of endotherms would otherwise cause heavy losses of water and heat, their presence (or the space they need, preserved in fossil snouts) has been used as evidence for endothermy in cynodonts ([7.17.54](/7.17.54); [7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.2)).
- *Larynx.* The opening of the lungs into the pharynx, the glottis, is guarded by a pair of arytenoid cartilages in amphibians and reptiles, with a ring-shaped cricoid cartilage behind them; these laryngeal cartilages derive from the sixth (and probably fifth) arch skeleton, and the muscles that open and close the glottis are vagal branchiomeric muscles ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.5.4)). Mammals add a thyroid cartilage, derived from the fourth and fifth arches, which shields the larynx in front, and an epiglottis of elastic cartilage that folds over the glottis in swallowing. The vocal folds of mammals stretch between the thyroid and arytenoid cartilages. In most mammals the larynx lies high and the epiglottis touches the soft palate, so air passes from the nose to the trachea while milk or food passes around it; in humans, and in deer, lions and some other roaring mammals, the larynx descends and lengthens the vocal tract.
- *Trachea and bronchi.* Amphibians have only a short laryngotracheal chamber. A trachea stiffened by cartilage rings, and two bronchi, appear in amniotes. The trachea is long in long-necked reptiles and birds; in cranes, swans and birds of paradise it may form loops in the sternum or under the skin that lower the pitch of calls. The rings are complete in birds and partly ossified, C-shaped and open dorsally in mammals.
- *Vocal organs.* Frogs produce sounds with vocal cords in the larynx, and males of many species have vocal sacs, inflatable pouches of the floor or sides of the mouth that radiate the sound. Most reptiles are silent or hiss; geckos and crocodylians vocalize with the larynx. Birds sing with a **syrinx**, an organ unique to birds at the junction of the trachea and bronchi, where the air flow sets vibrating membranes (the medial and lateral labia) in each bronchus; up to seven or eight pairs of syringeal muscles control them. Because each bronchus has its own sound source, songbirds can produce two independent notes at once. The larynx of birds has no vocal folds. Fossil evidence of a syrinx (in *Vegavis*, Late Cretaceous) suggests that it evolved late in the avian stem lineage.

## 64.7. The Evolution of Ventilation

### 64.7.1. From the Buccal Pump to Aspiration

The mechanisms that move air into lungs evolved in a series of steps that can be followed in living animals ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.3)).

1. *Buccal pumping.* Air-breathing fishes and amphibians force air into the lungs by positive pressure: the floor of the mouth is lowered to draw air in through the mouth or nostrils, the mouth or nostrils close, and the floor is raised to push air through the glottis into the lungs (**buccal pump**). In frogs the cycle has distinct phases: the floor of the mouth oscillates to ventilate the buccal cavity, exhalation is driven by the elastic recoil of the lungs and contraction of the body wall, and the expired air mixes only partly with the fresh air in the mouth. The buccal pump is a modification of the gill ventilation pump of fishes, driven by the same hyoid and hypobranchial muscles ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.4.1)), and was inherited by the earliest tetrapods, whose short, straight ribs could not have ventilated the lungs.
2. *Costal aspiration.* Amniotes fill their lungs by negative pressure: the intercostal and other hypaxial muscles rotate the ribs outward and expand the thorax, and the lungs follow. Costal aspiration frees the head and neck from breathing, which allowed longer necks and the separation of feeding from ventilation; it also made possible larger tidal volumes and a drier, scaly skin, since the lungs alone could cover the gas exchange. The fossil ribs of early amniotes are long and curved, consistent with costal aspiration.
3. *The locomotor constraint.* Lizards and salamanders walk with lateral bending of the trunk, which compresses one lung while expanding the other; they cannot breathe effectively while running (Carrier's constraint). Monitor lizards solve this by gular pumping, a buccal pump that supplements costal breathing during running. Mammals and birds, with upright limbs, solved it by dorsoventral bending (mammals) or by a rigid trunk (birds), and by coupling breaths to strides.

### 64.7.2. Diverse Amniote Solutions

The costal pump was modified differently in each amniote lineage.

### 64.7.3. Changing Media: Metamorphosis and Return to Water

The transition from water to air breathing is repeated in the life of every frog. A tadpole breathes with internal gills ventilated by a buccal pump that draws water in through the mouth and expels it through the spiracle, supplemented by the skin and, in later stages, by gulping air into the developing lungs. At metamorphosis, under the control of thyroid hormone ([7.14.34](/7.14.34#sec-34.7.1)), the gills and the gill arches are resorbed, the branchial arteries are remodelled into the carotid, systemic and pulmocutaneous arches ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)), the lungs enlarge and the buccal pump switches from water to air. The respiratory control system changes accordingly, from a drive set mainly by oxygen to one set mainly by carbon dioxide ([7.14.20](/7.14.20#sec-20.7.1)).

Many amniotes returned to water but none re-evolved gills; they remain air breathers that hold their breath. Their respiratory organs are modified in parallel ways. The nostrils move to the top of the head in whales (the blowhole), crocodylians and hippopotamuses, and can be closed by muscles or valves. The trachea of diving mammals is reinforced by cartilage and, in deep divers such as sperm whales, beaked whales and elephant seals, the lungs are small and collapse at depth, which pushes the air into the stiff airways where no nitrogen is absorbed; this protects the animals against decompression sickness ([7.14.18](/7.14.18#sec-18.7.5)). Oxygen is stored mainly in the blood and muscles, not in the lungs ([7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.6.3)). Sea snakes have a lung that extends almost the whole length of the body, with a posterior saccular part used as an oxygen store and buoyancy organ, and they can take up a fifth or more of their oxygen through the skin; some freshwater turtles that overwinter under ice exchange gases through the pharynx and the paired cloacal bursae.

## 64.9. Comparison with Traditional Accounts

Several points of the traditional account need revision in the light of current evidence, without changing the overall picture.

| Traditional statement | Current view |
|---|---|
| Lungs evolved from the swim bladder of early bony fishes | Paired ventral lungs are ancestral for Osteichthyes; the swim bladder is a derived dorsal lung of actinopterygians (§64.3.1). Lungs and swim bladder remain homologous |
| The gill filaments are ectodermal | The respiratory epithelium of internal gills is endodermal, like that of the lungs; external gills are covered by ectoderm (§64.2.1) |
| Physostomous fishes are primitive, physoclistous fishes derived | Correct; the duct closes in most acanthomorph teleosts, and the condition can change within lineages |
| Adult reptiles have no cutaneous respiration | Most do not, but sea snakes take up a substantial part of their oxygen through the skin, and some freshwater turtles exchange gas through the skin, pharynx and cloacal bursae during long dives |
| Bronchi appear first in reptiles; birds have "double respiration" | Correct in substance: bronchi are an amniote character; in birds the parabronchi receive fresh air in both phases of breathing, with unidirectional flow, which is now known also in crocodylians and some lizards (§64.4.2) |
| The diaphragm first appears in mammals | Correct for the muscular diaphragm; crocodylians have a non-homologous diaphragmaticus muscle (§64.7.2) |

## Summary

- Gills and lungs are analogous respiratory organs from different parts of the pharynx; the swim bladder and lungs are homologous foregut outgrowths supplied by the sixth aortic arch.
- Gills consist of filaments with secondary lamellae on the pharyngeal arches, arranged for countercurrent exchange. Pouched gills (cyclostomes), septal gills (elasmobranchs) and opercular gills (bony fishes) form a series in which the interbranchial septum is reduced and the slits are covered by an operculum.
- Paired ventral lungs are ancestral for bony fishes (bichirs, lungfishes, tetrapods); the dorsal swim bladder of actinopterygians is a derived lung that became a buoyancy organ (physostomous fishes keep the pneumatic duct, physoclistous fishes regulate gas by a gas gland, rete and oval). This reverses the traditional view that lungs evolved from the swim bladder.
- Tetrapod lungs increase in internal subdivision from amphibian sacs to the faveolar lungs of reptiles; birds have rigid lungs of parabronchi ventilated by air sacs with unidirectional flow, mammals alveolar lungs ventilated tidally. Unidirectional flow also occurs in crocodylians and some lizards and predates air sacs.
- Ventilation evolved from the buccal pump (fishes, amphibians) to costal aspiration (amniotes), and then to lineage-specific pumps: abdominal and limb-girdle pumps in turtles, the hepatic piston in crocodylians, the air-sac bellows in birds and the diaphragm in mammals.
- The laryngeal cartilages derive from the posterior pharyngeal arches; the syrinx of birds and the larynx of mammals are independent vocal organs.

## Questions

1. State three lines of evidence that paired ventral lungs, not the swim bladder, are the ancestral air organ of bony fishes.

   **Answer** First, distribution on the phylogeny: the most basal actinopterygians (bichirs), lungfishes and tetrapods have paired ventral lungs, so the most parsimonious ancestral state is a ventral lung, with the dorsal bladder derived within actinopterygians. Second, blood supply: the lungs of bichirs, lungfishes and tetrapods and the respiratory bladders of gars and bowfin are supplied by branches of the sixth aortic arch, the pulmonary arteries, whereas the teleost bladder is supplied by derived vessels. Third, development: lungs and swim bladder arise from the same foregut region with shared gene expression, the bud being ventral and paired in bichirs and tetrapods and dorsal in derived actinopterygians, which is a shift in position rather than a new organ.

2. Explain how the structure of a teleost gill makes countercurrent exchange possible, and why the reduction of the interbranchial septum improved it.

   **Answer** Water flows from the pharynx between the secondary lamellae of adjacent filaments to the opercular cavity, while blood flows within each lamella in the opposite direction, from the efferent to the afferent side; along the whole lamella blood meets water with a higher oxygen partial pressure, so blood can approach the oxygen tension of incoming water. In elasmobranchs the long septum restricts the free movement of the filaments and leaves channels through which some water passes without contacting lamellae. In teleosts the reduced septum lets the filament tips of adjacent arches meet, forming a continuous sieve so that all water passes between lamellae, and the operculum makes a single, controllable outflow that the opercular pump can use.

3. Compare the ventilation of a frog, a lizard and a mammal. Why can a running lizard not breathe effectively, and how have mammals and birds overcome this?

   **Answer** A frog uses a positive-pressure buccal pump: the floor of the mouth draws air in through the nostrils and pushes it into the lungs with the nostrils closed. A lizard uses costal aspiration: intercostal muscles expand the rib cage to draw air in by negative pressure. A mammal mainly uses the diaphragm, aided by intercostals. A running lizard bends its trunk laterally, which compresses the lung on one side while expanding the other, so air shuttles between lungs instead of being exchanged (Carrier's constraint). Mammals bend the trunk dorsoventrally and have the diaphragm, coupling breaths to strides; birds have a rigid trunk and lungs ventilated by air sacs; monitor lizards add gular pumping.

4. Describe the path of a single breath through the respiratory system of a bird over two respiratory cycles.

   **Answer** First inspiration: the air enters through the trachea and primary bronchus and flows mostly into the caudal air sacs. First expiration: the caudal sacs are compressed and push this air through the dorsobronchi into the parabronchi, where gas exchange occurs. Second inspiration: as all sacs expand, the air in the parabronchi is drawn into the cranial air sacs. Second expiration: the cranial sacs are compressed and the air leaves through the ventrobronchi, primary bronchus and trachea. The parabronchi receive air flowing in the same direction in both phases.

5. Why have many teleost lineages lost the swim bladder, and why did many others evolve accessory air-breathing organs?

   **Answer** A gas-filled bladder changes volume with depth (Boyle's law), so fishes that move rapidly up and down (mackerels, some tunas) would lose control of buoyancy, and bottom-dwelling fishes (flatfishes, gobies, anglerfishes) have no need of neutral buoyancy; these lost it. Fishes in warm, stagnant, oxygen-poor fresh waters, where water holds little oxygen, benefit from taking oxygen from air; they evolved air-breathing organs many times from whatever thin, vascularized surface was available: swim bladder, pharynx, opercular chamber (labyrinth and arborescent organs), stomach or intestine.

6. (Open question) The lungs of salamanders are smooth sacs. Is this the ancestral tetrapod condition? What evidence would you consider?

   **Answer** Probably not. Lungfishes and many reptiles have lungs with internal septa and faveoli, and the lungs of frogs are partitioned; parsimony on the phylogeny therefore suggests that the ancestral tetrapod lung was already subdivided and that the smooth lungs of some salamanders are simplified, consistent with their reliance on cutaneous respiration (and the complete loss of lungs in plethodontids). Evidence to consider includes the lung structure in all lungfish genera and in caecilians, the developmental origin of septa, the relation between lung complexity and body size and metabolism within salamanders, and fossil indications of rib structure and ventilation in early tetrapods.
