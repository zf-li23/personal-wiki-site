The synapsids are the lineage of amniotes that includes the mammals. For about 70 million years, from the Late Carboniferous to the middle of the Triassic, synapsids were the dominant large land animals: the sail-backed *Dimetrodon*, the tusked dicynodonts and the sabre-toothed gorgonopsians were the top herbivores and predators of the Permian. They were long called "mammal-like reptiles", but they are not reptiles: they diverged from the sauropsids at the origin of the amniotes ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.4.1)), and the mammals are simply their surviving members.

The transition from early synapsids to mammals is one of the best-documented major transitions in the fossil record. Over 100 million years the characters that define mammals — a lower jaw made of a single bone, three middle-ear bones, differentiated teeth replaced only once, a secondary palate, a diaphragm, hair and probably endothermy — were acquired one after another in a series of fossil groups. The jaw joint and middle ear in particular, where bones that formed the jaw joint of reptiles became the auditory ossicles of mammals, are a textbook example of change of function. This chapter treats the synapsid lineage (§54.1–54.2), the acquisition of mammalian characters (§54.3–54.4), and the early mammaliaforms and mammals (§54.5), up to the origin of the crown group Mammalia (§54.6). The macroevolutionary interpretation of this transition is in [7.10.32](/7.10.32#sec-32.1.2); here we give the anatomical detail. Living mammals follow in [7.17.55](/7.17.55); the comparative anatomy of the skull, ear and teeth is in [7.17.59](/7.17.59), [7.17.63](/7.17.63) and [7.17.68](/7.17.68).

## 54.1. The Pelycosaur Grade

### 54.1.1. Early Synapsids

The earliest synapsids, such as *Archaeothyris* from the Late Carboniferous of Nova Scotia (about 306 million years ago), were lizard-sized insectivores or carnivores, hardly distinguishable from early sauropsids except by the lower temporal fenestra behind the eye ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.3.2)). In the Late Carboniferous and Early Permian, synapsids radiated into a variety of forms that are collectively called "pelycosaurs", a paraphyletic **grade** of early synapsids that are not therapsids. The main groups were:

- *Caseasaurs*: small-headed, barrel-bodied herbivores (*Cotylorhynchus*, up to 6 m and 500 kg), with a huge gut for fermenting plants; they were among the first large terrestrial herbivores, together with the diadectomorphs.
- *Ophiacodontids*: carnivores with long skulls, possibly semi-aquatic fish-eaters (*Ophiacodon*).
- *Edaphosaurids*: herbivores with a tall dorsal sail of elongated neural spines bearing short side branches (*Edaphosaurus*).
- *Sphenacodontids*: the top predators of the Early Permian, including *Dimetrodon* (up to 4 m), which also had a sail. Sphenacodontids had teeth of different sizes along the jaw, with enlarged canine-like teeth on the maxilla: the beginning of differentiated (heterodont) dentition. Their angular bone bore a reflected lamina, a thin flange whose function is unknown but which, in later synapsids, may have supported a tympanic membrane (§54.3.3).

The sails of *Dimetrodon* and *Edaphosaurus* evolved independently. They have been interpreted as thermoregulatory devices that would warm the body quickly in the morning sun, but the sail of juveniles was too small to have much effect, and display is an equally plausible function. Pelycosaurs were ectothermic, with sprawling limbs and a reptile-like posture.

### 54.1.2. Pelycosaurs and the Permian World

In the Early Permian, pelycosaurs made up about 70 % of the genera of terrestrial amniotes known, and the classic faunas of Texas and Oklahoma (the "Red Beds") are dominated by them. They declined during the Middle Permian, in a turnover between about 275 and 270 million years ago (Olson's extinction), and were replaced by the therapsids, which had evolved from sphenacodont-like ancestors.

## 54.2. Therapsids

### 54.2.1. Characters and Diversity

The *therapsids* appeared in the Middle Permian (about 275–270 million years ago) and dominated terrestrial faunas of the Late Permian. Compared with pelycosaurs, they had a larger temporal fenestra and more powerful jaw muscles; a more upright posture of the hindlimbs, with the femur held more vertically and the knee turned forward, while the forelimbs remained more sprawling; longer limbs and a reduced tail; and in many groups a pineal foramen that became smaller, perhaps reflecting a change in the role of the parietal eye in thermoregulation. Bone histology shows faster growth than in pelycosaurs. The main groups were:

| Group | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Biarmosuchia | The most primitive therapsids; medium-sized carnivores with large canines | *Biarmosuchus* (Russia) |
| Dinocephalia ("terrible heads") | Large carnivores and herbivores of the Middle Permian with thick-boned skulls, possibly used in head butting; up to 5 m | *Moschops*, *Titanophoneus*; *Sinophoneus* (Gansu) |
| Anomodontia (with the dicynodonts) | Herbivores with a horny beak; in dicynodonts ("two dog teeth") the only teeth were a pair of upper tusks, and the jaw slid back and forth to shear plants; from rat-sized burrowers to the 9-tonne Triassic *Lisowicia* | *Dicynodon*, *Lystrosaurus*, *Kannemeyeria*; Chinese *Sinokannemeyeria* |
| Gorgonopsia | The top predators of the Late Permian, with sabre-like upper canines and a huge gape | *Inostrancevia* (3.5 m), *Gorgonops* |
| Therocephalia | Carnivores and some herbivores; several mammal-like features (secondary palate in some, maxillary turbinate ridges); some survived into the Triassic | *Euchambersia* (possibly venomous) |
| Cynodontia | The lineage leading to mammals (§54.2.3) | *Procynosuchus*, *Thrinaxodon* |

The Middle Permian fauna of Dashankou in Yumen, Gansu (about 268 million years ago) is one of the oldest therapsid faunas in the world. It includes *Raranimus*, one of the most primitive known therapsids, early anomodonts and the dinocephalian *Sinophoneus*, and it shows that therapsids were diverse at high latitudes in the northern continent of Laurasia early in their history.

### 54.2.2. The End-Permian Extinction and *Lystrosaurus*

The end-Permian extinction, 252 million years ago, was the largest **mass extinction** in the history of life, eliminating about 80–90 % of marine species and about 70 % of terrestrial vertebrate families ([7.10.31](/7.10.31#sec-31.4.1)). Massive volcanic eruptions in Siberia released carbon dioxide and sulphur, causing extreme global warming, acid rain and the loss of oxygen from the oceans; on land, the vegetation collapsed and the climate became hot and arid. Gorgonopsians, dinocephalians (already extinct) and most dicynodonts disappeared.

One dicynodont, *Lystrosaurus*, survived and became, briefly, the most abundant land vertebrate that has ever lived: in the earliest Triassic of South Africa it makes up more than 90 % of the fossils of terrestrial vertebrates. *Lystrosaurus*, a pig-sized burrowing herbivore, is found in South Africa, India, Antarctica, Russia and the Junggar Basin of Xinjiang in China, and its distribution across these now-separate continents is classic evidence of Pangaea. Its survival has been attributed to its burrowing, its small body and fast growth, and its ability to breathe in low-oxygen air (a large chest and nasal turbinates are debated). Other survivors included therocephalians and the cynodonts, which radiated in the Triassic.

### 54.2.3. Cynodonts

**Definition 54.2.1 (Cynodontia)** The **Cynodontia** (cynodonts, "dog teeth") are the clade of therapsids that includes mammals and all therapsids more closely related to mammals than to therocephalians. Cynodonts have an enlarged dentary that forms most of the lower jaw, with a coronoid process for the attachment of the temporalis muscle; a zygomatic arch flaring outward, beneath which the masseter muscle originated; postcanine teeth with several cusps; a bony secondary palate; and ribs reduced or absent in the lumbar region.

The earliest cynodonts, such as *Procynosuchus*, lived in the Late Permian. After the extinction, *Thrinaxodon* from the Early Triassic of South Africa and Antarctica, a cat-sized animal, already shows many mammalian traits. A *Thrinaxodon* was found curled up in its burrow together with an injured amphibian (*Broomistega*) that apparently sheltered with it, a fossil of a burrowing cynodont at rest.

The Triassic cynodonts divided into two groups:

- *Cynognathia*, including the large carnivore *Cynognathus* (about 1.2 m) and the traversodontids, herbivores with broad, grinding cheek teeth.
- *Probainognathia*, the lineage leading to mammals, including *Probainognathus* from Argentina, the tritylodontids (herbivores with rodent-like teeth that survived into the Early Cretaceous), the tritheledontids and the brasilodontids, small insectivores that are the closest relatives of mammaliaforms.

The tritylodontid *Bienotherium* and the related *Oligokyphus* lived in the Early Jurassic of Lufeng, Yunnan, alongside the early mammaliaforms (§54.5.1). Tritylodontids were so mammal-like that their placement inside or outside the mammaliaforms was debated; they differ by having retained a functional articular–quadrate jaw joint.

### 54.2.4. Feeding of Dicynodonts and Gorgonopsians

The two most characteristic therapsid groups of the Late Permian show how far synapsids had diverged from their pelycosaur ancestors in feeding. Dicynodonts had lost all teeth except, in most species, a pair of upper tusks, and the front of both jaws bore a horny beak like that of a turtle. The jaw articulation allowed the lower jaw to slide forward and backward: the jaw muscles, inserted far back, pulled the lower jaw backward as it closed, and the sharp edges of the lower beak sliced plant material against the palate. This fore-and-aft (propalinal) chewing, also found in tuatara and some rodents, made dicynodonts efficient herbivores of tough vegetation such as horsetails and seed ferns, and they became the most abundant large herbivores of the Late Permian and Triassic. Some species dug burrows, as preserved spiral burrows with skeletons show, and the tusks may have served in digging and display.

Gorgonopsians were the opposite: predators with very long, blade-like upper canines and a jaw that could open to about 90°, a gape made possible by a jaw joint that allowed the quadrate to move and by a lower jaw whose muscles were arranged to act at wide angles. Like the sabre-toothed cats of the Cenozoic, which evolved the same combination independently, they probably killed large prey with slashing bites. The sabre-tooth design evolved at least four times in synapsids (gorgonopsians, some therocephalians, the marsupial *Thylacosmilus* and the sabre-toothed cats), a recurrent example of **convergent evolution**.

### 54.2.5. Triassic Synapsid Faunas

In the Early and Middle Triassic, synapsids recovered from the extinction and shared the land with the early archosaurs. Large dicynodonts (kannemeyeriiforms) were the dominant herbivores, cynognathians and traversodontids the medium-sized carnivores and herbivores. In northern China, the Ermaying and Tongchuan formations of Shanxi and Shaanxi preserve such faunas, with the dicynodont *Sinokannemeyeria*, the traversodontid *Sinognathus* and early archosaurs; similar faunas in South Africa (the Karoo Basin), Argentina and Russia show that the same groups were spread across Pangaea.

The decline of the large synapsids in the Late Triassic coincided with the rise of dinosaurs ([7.17.52](/7.17.52#sec-52.4.2)). The surviving cynodont lineages became small: by the end of the Triassic, the probainognathians closest to mammals weighed tens of grams, and the earliest mammaliaforms lived in a world dominated by large archosaurs. The miniaturization along the stem lineage of mammals, like that along the stem lineage of birds, may have helped the evolution of new characters: small animals have relatively large brains and sense organs, lose heat rapidly (favouring insulation and endothermy), and live in microhabitats (burrows, leaf litter, trees) where hearing and smell matter more than vision.

## 54.3. The Jaw Joint and the Middle Ear

### 54.3.1. From Many Bones to One

The lower jaw of a pelycosaur, like that of other early amniotes, consisted of several bones on each side: the tooth-bearing dentary in front, and behind it the surangular, angular, articular, prearticular, coronoid and splenial. The jaw joint was formed by the articular, at the back of the lower jaw, against the quadrate of the skull; the stapes, which conducted sound, rested against the quadrate. In mammals, the lower jaw is a single bone, the dentary, which articulates with the squamosal of the skull, and the articular and quadrate are in the middle ear as the malleus and incus.

Along the synapsid series, the dentary grew larger and the postdentary bones smaller (figure). In sphenacodontids the dentary makes up about half of the jaw; in therapsids it grows a coronoid process; in early cynodonts it forms most of the jaw, and the postdentary bones are a slender rod lying in a trough on the inside of the dentary; in late probainognathians the postdentary bones are tiny. At the same time the quadrate became smaller and was loosely attached to the squamosal.

### 54.3.2. Why the Dentary Enlarged: A Lever Model

The changes in the jaw were linked to changes in the jaw muscles. In pelycosaurs the jaw was closed by an adductor muscle that pulled upward and backward on the back of the jaw. In cynodonts this muscle divided into the temporalis, attached to the coronoid process of the dentary and pulling upward and backward, and the masseter, attached to the outer side of the dentary and originating on the zygomatic arch, pulling upward and forward. Together the two muscles produce a resultant force that is nearly vertical and acts close to the bite point, so the force transmitted through the jaw joint is reduced (Crompton 1963; Bramble 1978).

The effect can be seen in a simple lever model. Treat the lower jaw as a rigid lever with the joint at one end. If a muscle exerts a vertical force $F$ at a distance $a$ in front of the joint and the bite point lies at a distance $b$, the bite force is $F_{\mathrm{b}} = F\,a/b$, and the joint must supply a reaction

$$
R = F - F_{\mathrm{b}} = F\left(1 - \frac{a}{b}\right),
$$
so moving the effective line of action of the muscles forward, toward the bite point ($a \to b$), increases the bite force and reduces the load on the joint.

**Example 54.3.1** A jaw 10 cm long bites at its front ($b = 10$ cm). In a pelycosaur-like arrangement, the muscle force $F = 100$ N acts 2 cm in front of the joint: the bite force is $100 \times 2/10 = 20$ N and the joint reaction 80 N. In a cynodont-like arrangement, the combined masseter and temporalis act at an effective distance of 5 cm: the bite force rises to $100 \times 5/10 = 50$ N and the joint reaction falls to 50 N. If the resultant muscle force is also oriented so that its line passes near the bite point, as in advanced cynodonts, the joint reaction approaches zero during biting.

:::

A jaw joint that carries little force can be small and loosely built. The reduction of the articular and quadrate was thus made possible by the reorganization of the jaw muscles, and once reduced, these bones were free to become specialized for transmitting sound. The real situation is more complex — the jaw is loaded in three dimensions, the muscles of the two sides act together, and the balance changes during the bite — but three-dimensional models of cynodont skulls support the conclusion that the mammalian jaw arrangement reduces joint loads (Lautenschlager et al. 2017).

### 54.3.3. Two Joints at Once

How could the jaw joint change from articular–quadrate to dentary–squamosal without a stage in which the jaw had no joint at all? The fossils answer: for a time there were two joints side by side. In the probainognathian *Diarthrognathus* ("two-jointed jaw") from the Early Jurassic of South Africa and in the brasilodontids, a process of the dentary (or the surangular) contacted the squamosal beside the old articular–quadrate joint. In *Morganucodon* (§54.5.1) a fully developed dentary–squamosal joint, with a rounded condyle on the dentary fitting into a glenoid fossa on the squamosal, functioned alongside a reduced articular–quadrate joint just medial to it. The two joints lay on the same transverse axis, so the jaw could open and close around both. Later the articular and quadrate lost their contact with the jaw.

The old joint was not lost but transformed: the joint between the articular and the quadrate is the joint between the malleus and the incus in your ear.

### 54.3.4. The Formation of the Middle Ear

The middle ear of mammals has three ossicles in a chain, from the eardrum to the inner ear:

| Mammalian ossicle | Homologue in other amniotes | Pharyngeal arch |
|---|---|---|
| Malleus (hammer) | Articular (with the prearticular forming its anterior process, the goniale) | First (mandibular) arch, posterior end of Meckel's cartilage |
| Incus (anvil) | Quadrate | First arch, posterior end of the palatoquadrate |
| Stapes (stirrup) | Stapes (columella) | Second (hyoid) arch; homologous with the hyomandibula of fishes ([7.17.48](/7.17.48#sec-48.4.4)) |
| Ectotympanic (tympanic ring, holding the eardrum) | Angular, including its reflected lamina | Dermal bone of the lower jaw |

The homologies were first recognized from embryology. Karl Reichert (1837) observed that in pig embryos the malleus and incus develop from the posterior end of the first pharyngeal arch, the malleus continuous with Meckel's cartilage, the cartilage of the embryonic lower jaw; Ernst Gaupp (1913) added the homology of the tympanic with the angular. In a newborn opossum, which is born very early in development, the dentary–squamosal joint has not yet formed, and the young marsupial suckling on the teat uses the articular–quadrate (malleus–incus) joint as its jaw joint for the first weeks, before the ossicles separate. Development therefore recapitulates the sequence documented by the fossils, a rare case where von Baer's observations of embryos and the fossil record tell the same story ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

The fossil record shows that the middle ear separated from the jaw in stages, and that the final separation happened more than once:

1. *Attached middle ear*: the postdentary bones lie in a trough on the dentary and conduct sound received by a membrane supported by the reflected lamina of the angular, as well as by bone conduction through the jaw (in cynodonts and early mammaliaforms such as *Morganucodon*).
2. *Transitional middle ear*: the ossicles have detached from the dentary but are still connected to it by an ossified Meckel's cartilage, which runs in a groove on the inside of the dentary. Fossils of this stage were first found in China: *Yanoconodon* (2007) and *Liaoconodon* (2011) from the Jehol biota, and *Origolestes* (2019), whose well-preserved ossicles show that the malleus–incus joint was a flat, overlapping contact (Mao et al. 2020).
3. *Definitive mammalian middle ear*: the ossicles are completely separated from the jaw and suspended from the skull, enclosed by the ectotympanic and, in many mammals, by a bony bulla.

The definitive middle ear evolved independently in monotremes, in therians and in multituberculates, from a common ancestor with an attached or transitional ear (Luo 2011). The separation was driven by selection for hearing: the small, light ossicles, freed from the jaw, can vibrate at high frequencies, and mammals hear frequencies (up to 100 kHz or more in bats and rodents) far above those audible to reptiles and birds (rarely above 10 kHz). The lever and area ratios of the ossicles and eardrum match the impedance of air to that of the fluid-filled cochlea ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.3)). The tympanic ear itself evolved independently in mammals, in lepidosaurs, in archosaurs and in frogs: the ancestral amniotes had no eardrum, and the reflected lamina of the angular, once interpreted as the support of an ancient synapsid eardrum, was probably a muscle attachment in pelycosaurs and only later supported a tympanum.

### 54.3.5. The Developmental Genetics of the Jaw Joint

The genes that pattern the first pharyngeal arch explain why the jaw joint could be converted into an ear joint. In fishes, amphibians and birds, the homeobox gene *Nkx3.2* (*Bapx1*) is expressed at the site where the jaw joint forms, between the quadrate and the articular; knocking it down in zebrafish fuses the upper and lower jaw cartilages. In mice, *Nkx3.2* is expressed not at the dentary–squamosal joint but in the developing malleus and incus, and mice lacking the gene have defects of the malleus (Tucker et al. 2004). The genetic programme of the ancestral jaw joint thus persists in the middle ear, while the new jaw joint is built by a separate mechanism, a secondary cartilage on the dentary that forms against the squamosal. The neural crest cells of the first arch that form the ossicles are patterned by the endothelin–*Dlx* code that distinguishes upper and lower jaws ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)), and the separation of the ossicles from Meckel's cartilage depends on the controlled breakdown of the cartilage in late development, regulated by the bone-resorbing osteoclasts; when this fails in mice, the ossicles remain connected to the jaw, a condition resembling the transitional middle ear of Cretaceous mammals (Urban et al. 2017).

## 54.4. Other Mammalian Characters

### 54.4.1. Skull and Teeth

*Secondary palate.* In cynodonts, shelves of the maxilla and palatine grew toward the midline below the original roof of the mouth, forming a bony **secondary palate** that separates the nasal passage from the mouth ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)). A continuation of soft tissue, the soft palate, extends it backward to the pharynx. The secondary palate lets an animal breathe while food is in its mouth and is chewed, and allows suckling, which requires that the mouth form a sealed chamber. It also strengthens the snout against the stresses of biting. Crocodylians evolved a secondary palate independently.

*Occipital condyles.* Pelycosaurs, like reptiles, have a single occipital condyle on the basioccipital; probainognathian cynodonts have two condyles on the exoccipitals, as in mammals. Two condyles allow up-and-down nodding of the head on the atlas, while rotation takes place between the atlas and axis ([7.17.60](/7.17.60)).

*Zygomatic arch and braincase.* The temporal fenestra enlarged and the bone between it and the orbit was reduced, so that in many mammals orbit and temporal fossa form one space; the bar below it, the zygomatic arch (jugal and squamosal), gives origin to the masseter. The braincase expanded, and its side wall was formed by a new bone, the alisphenoid, derived from the epipterygoid of the first arch.

*Heterodonty and occlusion.* Synapsid teeth became differentiated into incisors, canines and postcanines, and the postcanines into premolars and multicusped molars ([7.17.55](/7.17.55)). In cynodonts the postcanines acquired several cusps in a row; in mammaliaforms upper and lower molars occluded precisely, with wear facets that show they sheared against each other in a fixed relation. Precise occlusion requires that teeth not be replaced constantly, and mammaliaforms became **diphyodont**: one set of deciduous ("milk") teeth replaced once by permanent teeth, and the molars, which erupt behind the deciduous teeth, never replaced. Diphyodonty is associated with determinate growth (the jaw stops growing at maturity) and with lactation: young that suckle do not need teeth, and their adult teeth can erupt when the jaw has almost reached its final size.

*The tribosphenic molar.* In the therian lineage (Boreosphenida) the molars became tribosphenic ("three-cusped crushing"): each upper molar has three main cusps, the paracone, metacone and the inner protocone; each lower molar has a front triangle of three cusps (the trigonid, with protoconid, paraconid and metaconid) and a rear basin (the talonid). When the jaws close, the trigonid of the lower molar shears against the upper molars, and the protocone of the upper molar crushes food in the talonid basin like a pestle in a mortar. This molar combines cutting and grinding and is the starting point from which the molars of all living therians evolved. The Jurassic *Shuotherium* from China has a "pseudotribosphenic" molar with the basin in front of the trigonid instead of behind it, a convergent solution.

### 54.4.2. Postcranial Skeleton and the Diaphragm

The vertebral column became regionalized. In pelycosaurs all trunk vertebrae bore ribs; in cynodonts the ribs of the posterior trunk became short and then were lost, giving a thoracic region with ribs and a lumbar region without. The change is associated with the evolution of the muscular diaphragm separating the thoracic and abdominal cavities, which in mammals is the main muscle of inspiration ([7.17.64](/7.17.64)). The diaphragm itself does not fossilize, but the loss of lumbar ribs and the change in the vertebrae suggest that it was present in late cynodonts. The posture became more erect, with the limbs beneath the body; the calcaneus developed a heel for the calf muscles ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.1.2)); and the phalangeal formula was reduced from the 2-3-4-5-3 of early amniotes to the mammalian 2-3-3-3-3, the extra phalanges of the third and fourth digits becoming tiny disks in therapsids and then disappearing.

The pectoral girdle lost dermal elements: the cleithrum disappeared, and in therians the interclavicle and the coracoid as a separate bone were lost (it survives as the coracoid process of the scapula), while monotremes retain an interclavicle, coracoid and procoracoid. The scapula of therians developed a spine and a supraspinous fossa for new muscles, and became mobile on the rib cage.

### 54.4.3. Hair, Glands and Lactation

Hair and mammary glands rarely fossilize. Their origin is inferred from several lines of evidence:

- *Direct evidence of fur* in Jurassic mammaliaforms from China: the docodont *Castorocauda* from the Yanliao biota (2006), a beaver-like swimmer with a flat, scaly tail, was covered with guard hairs and underfur. Hair is also preserved in Early Cretaceous mammals (*Spinolestes* from Spain, with spines like a hedgehog's), and hair-like structures have been reported in Late Permian coprolites (Bajdek et al. 2016).
- *Vibrissae*: a branching canal in the maxilla for the infraorbital nerve, which in mammals supplies the whiskers, takes its mammalian form in probainognathian cynodonts (Benoit et al. 2016), which may therefore have had whiskers.
- *Developmental and genetic evidence*: hair develops from epidermal **placodes**, as do feathers and scales ([7.17.57](/7.17.57)), and mammary glands, sweat glands and sebaceous glands develop from the same kind of epidermal appendage. Mammary glands probably evolved from apocrine-like glands associated with hair follicles that originally secreted fluid onto eggs, moistening them and protecting them against microbes (Oftedal 2002). Monotremes still secrete milk onto patches of hair from which the young lap it; the nipples of therians are a later addition. The milk proteins (caseins) evolved from genes for secreted calcium-binding proteins before the divergence of monotremes and therians ([7.14.38](/7.14.38#sec-38.9.4)).

### 54.4.4. Endothermy and the Brain

When synapsids became endothermic is inferred from indirect evidence ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.5.2)). Bone histology shows that therapsids grew faster than pelycosaurs; ridges in the nasal cavity of therocephalians and cynodonts suggest respiratory turbinates, which recover heat and water from exhaled air (Hillenius 1992); oxygen isotopes in bone indicate elevated body temperatures in Late Permian dicynodonts and cynodonts (Rey et al. 2017); and the size of the semicircular canals, whose fluid becomes less viscous at higher temperature, suggests that mammal-like endothermy arose rather suddenly in the Late Triassic, about 233 million years ago, near the base of the Mammaliamorpha (Araújo et al. 2022). Endothermy seems to have evolved in steps over more than 50 million years, in concert with the reorganization of breathing (diaphragm, secondary palate), posture and growth.

The brain enlarged in two pulses. Computed tomography of the skulls of *Morganucodon* and *Hadrocodium* shows that the brain of mammaliaforms was larger than that of cynodonts relative to body size, and that the enlargement affected first the olfactory bulbs and cortex and then the neocortex and the areas receiving touch from the hair (Rowe et al. 2011). The small, nocturnal early mammals, living under the dominance of dinosaurs, relied on smell, hearing and touch rather than vision — the "nocturnal bottleneck" hypothesis, which also explains why most mammals have only two cone types in their retinas, having lost the ultraviolet and other cones of their ancestors ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)).

### 54.4.5. Mosaic Evolution

The characters of mammals did not evolve together, and different lineages of cynodonts acquired them in different combinations; some characters (the secondary palate, the reduction of the postdentary bones, the definitive middle ear) evolved more than once. The transition is a classic example of **mosaic evolution** ([7.10.32](/7.10.32#sec-32.1.2)): the "mammal" emerges gradually from a series of cynodonts, and where to draw the line is a matter of definition. Traditionally the dentary–squamosal joint was used as the defining character of mammals; modern phylogenetic systematics instead defines the crown group by its living members (§54.6), and the extinct relatives outside it are mammaliaforms or stem mammals.

## 54.5. Mammaliaforms and Mesozoic Mammals

### 54.5.1. Mammaliaforms

**Definition 54.5.1 (Mammaliaformes)** The **Mammaliaformes** (mammaliaforms) are the clade comprising *Morganucodon*, the crown-group mammals and all descendants of their last common ancestor, together with the docodonts and other extinct relatives. Mammaliaforms have a dentary–squamosal jaw joint, diphyodont dentition with precise molar occlusion, a petrosal bone enclosing an elongated cochlea, and an enlarged brain.

The oldest mammaliaforms are from the Late Triassic (about 205–200 million years ago) of Europe, and among the best known are those of the Early Jurassic Lower Lufeng Formation of Yunnan, China (about 195 million years ago), where they lived with *Lufengosaurus* ([7.17.52](/7.17.52#sec-52.8)) and tritylodontids:

- *Morganucodon*, known from Wales and from Lufeng (*M. oehleri*), was a shrew-sized (about 10 cm, 20–30 g) insectivore with a double jaw joint and an attached middle ear, and probably nocturnal; its growth, inferred from tooth replacement and bone histology, was slower than in living mammals, and it may have lived as long as 14 years, much longer than a modern shrew (Newham et al. 2020).
- *Sinoconodon*, from Lufeng, larger and more primitive, still replaced its teeth several times, like a reptile.
- *Hadrocodium wui*, from Lufeng (described in 2001), was tiny (about 2 g) and had a very large braincase for its size and a lower jaw without a postdentary trough, suggesting that its middle ear was already separate from the jaw.

The docodonts, with complex molars for crushing, were a diverse Middle Jurassic group; several from the Yanliao biota of northeastern China show that they were ecologically varied: the swimming *Castorocauda*, the burrowing *Docofossor*, the tree-climbing *Agilodocodon*, and *Microdocodon*, whose fossil preserves a hyoid apparatus like that of mammals, allowing swallowing of chewed food (Zhou et al. 2019).

### 54.5.2. The Jurassic and Cretaceous Radiations of China

The Jurassic and Cretaceous mammaliaforms and mammals of China have overturned the old picture of Mesozoic mammals as small, uniform, shrew-like insectivores in the shadow of the dinosaurs. They show that early mammals had already explored many ecological niches:

| Taxon | Age and biota | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| *Castorocauda lutrasimilis* | Middle Jurassic, Yanliao | Docodont swimmer with fur, webbed feet and a flat tail |
| *Volaticotherium antiquum* | Middle–Late Jurassic, Yanliao | A gliding eutriconodont with a fur-covered patagium |
| *Maiopatagium*, *Vilevolodon*, *Arboroharamiya* | Middle–Late Jurassic, Yanliao | Gliding and arboreal haramiyidans |

Most Mesozoic mammals belonged to groups that are now extinct: the eutriconodonts, the spalacotheroids and dryolestoids, and the multituberculates (§54.5.3).

### 54.5.3. Multituberculates

The multituberculates (Allotheria) were the most successful Mesozoic mammals, with more than 200 known species over about 130 million years, from the Middle Jurassic to the early Oligocene. They had enlarged, procumbent lower incisors like those of rodents, a gap (diastema) behind them, a blade-like lower premolar in many species that sliced plant material, and upper and lower molars with two or three parallel rows of many cusps, which ground food with a backward power stroke of the lower jaw, the reverse of the direction in most other mammals. Many were arboreal or terrestrial omnivores and herbivores the size of mice and rats; the Late Cretaceous radiation of flowering plants coincided with an increase in their diversity and in the complexity of their teeth (Wilson et al. 2012). Their narrow pelvis suggests that they gave birth to tiny, undeveloped young, as marsupials do. Multituberculates have a definitive middle ear and are crown mammals; their position relative to monotremes and therians, and whether the haramiyidans are their relatives, is debated. Several species are known from China, including the Jehol *Sinobaatar*, one of the oldest well-preserved multituberculates, and Late Cretaceous forms from Inner Mongolia and Henan. They survived the K–Pg extinction in North America and Asia and declined in the Eocene, probably in competition with rodents and primates.

## 54.6. The Crown Group Mammalia

Under the definition used in this course, the **mammals** (Mammalia) are the crown group: the last common ancestor of living monotremes and therians and all its descendants ([7.17.55](/7.17.55)). With this definition, *Morganucodon* and the docodonts are not mammals but mammaliaforms; the multituberculates, eutriconodonts and other groups that branch off after the monotremes are mammals. The older definition, by the dentary–squamosal joint, includes *Morganucodon* and some brasilodontids, and is still used in many textbooks. The two definitions differ only in where they draw the line on the stem; the sequence of characters is the same.

The living mammals divide into two lineages that diverged in the Jurassic:

- the **monotremes** (Monotremata), on the australosphenidan lineage of the southern continents, whose earliest members (*Teinolophos*, *Steropodon*) are from the Early Cretaceous of Australia;
- the therians (Theria), which split into the metatherians (with the **marsupials**) and the eutherians (with the placental mammals), an event dated by molecular clocks and fossils to about 160–170 million years ago.

The diversification of placental mammals into their living orders, and the evolution of the marsupials, are treated in [7.17.55](/7.17.55). Molecular clocks suggest that several placental lineages (Afrotheria, Xenarthra, Laurasiatheria, Euarchontoglires) were already separate in the Late Cretaceous, but the great ecological radiation of placentals followed the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs 66 million years ago.

## 54.7. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

Chinese textbooks describe the origin of mammals from the "theriodonts" (Theriodontia) of the "mammal-like reptiles", a branch of the pelycosaur descendants in which the basioccipital no longer takes part in the occipital condyle (which became double), the teeth are differentiated, a secondary palate is present and the dentary is enlarged, and which died out in the Triassic. They place the earliest mammals in the Triassic and Jurassic and describe the three subclasses of living mammals (Prototheria, Metatheria and Eutheria) and their adaptive radiation.

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Synapsids as "mammal-like reptiles", a subclass of Reptilia | Synapsids are the mammal lineage, the sister group of sauropsids; they are not reptiles |
| Pelycosauria and Therapsida as orders | "Pelycosaurs" are a paraphyletic grade; Therapsida is a clade within it, with cynodonts and mammals inside |
| Theriodontia (cynodonts and others) as the direct ancestors of mammals, extinct in the Triassic | Mammals arose within cynodonts (probainognathians); tritylodontids survived into the Cretaceous |
| Mammals defined by the dentary–squamosal joint, hair and mammary glands | The crown group Mammalia is defined by its living members; the dentary–squamosal joint diagnoses the larger Mammaliaformes; hair and mammary glands are inferred to have evolved earlier |
| The three ossicles of the middle ear as a defining character of mammals | The definitive middle ear evolved in stages and independently in monotremes, therians and multituberculates |
| Mesozoic mammals as small, insectivorous, nocturnal animals | Many were small, but Jurassic and Cretaceous mammals included swimmers, gliders, burrowers, climbers and predators of small dinosaurs |
| Prototheria, Metatheria and Eutheria as subclasses | Monotremata (on the australosphenidan lineage), Metatheria (with Marsupialia) and Eutheria (with Placentalia); Theria = Metatheria + Eutheria |

The textbooks' account of the advanced characters of mammals and of the reptile–mammal transition (double occipital condyle, secondary palate, differentiated teeth, enlarged dentary) is included in §54.3–54.4, with the addition of the developmental and fossil evidence for the origin of the ossicles.

## Summary

- Synapsids diverged from sauropsids in the Late Carboniferous. Pelycosaurs (caseasaurs, ophiacodontids, edaphosaurids, sphenacodontids such as *Dimetrodon*) are a paraphyletic grade that dominated the Early Permian.
- Therapsids (biarmosuchians, dinocephalians, anomodonts with dicynodonts, gorgonopsians, therocephalians, cynodonts) dominated the Late Permian; the Dashankou fauna of Gansu is one of the oldest. *Lystrosaurus* survived the end-Permian extinction and dominated the earliest Triassic.
- Cynodonts (Definition 54.2.1): enlarged dentary with coronoid process, zygomatic arch and masseter, secondary palate, multicusped postcanines, loss of lumbar ribs; Cynognathia and Probainognathia, the latter leading to mammals.
- Jaw and ear: the dentary enlarged and the postdentary bones shrank as the temporalis and masseter reduced the load on the jaw joint (lever model: $R = F(1 - a/b)$); a double jaw joint (*Diarthrognathus*, *Morganucodon*) preceded the dentary–squamosal joint; articular → malleus, quadrate → incus, angular → tympanic ring, documented by embryology (Reichert–Gaupp) and by Chinese fossils of the transitional middle ear (*Yanoconodon*, *Liaoconodon*, *Origolestes*); the definitive middle ear evolved independently in several lineages.
- Other characters: secondary palate, double occipital condyle, diphyodonty and precise occlusion, the tribosphenic molar, regionalized vertebrae and diaphragm, reduced phalanges, hair (*Castorocauda*), whiskers, glands and lactation, endothermy in steps (turbinates, histology, isotopes, inner ear), brain enlargement led by olfaction; a mosaic.
- Mammaliaforms (Definition 54.5.1): *Morganucodon*, *Sinoconodon* and *Hadrocodium* of Lufeng; docodonts. Jurassic and Cretaceous mammals of the Yanliao and Jehol biotas were ecologically diverse.
- Crown Mammalia: monotremes and therians (metatherians and eutherians), diverging in the Jurassic.

## Questions

1. Explain how the jaw joint of synapsids could change from the articular–quadrate joint to the dentary–squamosal joint without passing through a stage without a functional joint.

   **Answer** The change took place through a stage with two joints side by side. As the dentary enlarged, its posterior part (in some forms the surangular) came into contact with the squamosal next to the articular–quadrate joint. In advanced probainognathian cynodonts such as *Diarthrognathus* and in mammaliaforms such as *Morganucodon*, a dentary condyle articulated with a glenoid fossa on the squamosal, while the reduced articular still met the quadrate just medially; the two joints lay on the same axis, so the jaw rotated about both. Once the new joint was established and carried the load, the old joint was released from its jaw function and could become specialized for sound transmission. The joint between the articular and the quadrate persists as the malleus–incus joint of the mammalian middle ear.
2. Using the lever model, a muscle force of 200 N acts 3 cm in front of the jaw joint, and the animal bites at 12 cm. Calculate the bite force and the joint reaction. How would they change if the effective point of the muscle force moved to 6 cm?

   **Answer** With $a = 3$ cm and $b = 12$ cm: bite force $F_{\mathrm{b}} = 200 \times 3/12 = 50$ N, joint reaction $R = 200 - 50 = 150$ N. With $a = 6$ cm: $F_{\mathrm{b}} = 200 \times 6/12 = 100$ N, $R = 100$ N. Moving the muscle's line of action forward doubles the bite force and reduces the joint load by one third; in cynodonts, the division of the adductor into temporalis and masseter with different directions of pull moved the resultant force forward and made it nearly vertical, further reducing the load at the joint. This allowed the articular and quadrate to become smaller and eventually free for hearing.
3. What evidence shows that the malleus and incus are homologous with the articular and quadrate?

   **Answer** (a) Embryology: in mammalian embryos the malleus and incus develop from the posterior end of the first pharyngeal arch, the malleus continuous with Meckel's cartilage (the embryonic lower jaw cartilage), exactly where the articular and quadrate develop in reptiles (Reichert 1837); the anterior process of the malleus ossifies from a dermal element corresponding to the prearticular, and the tympanic ring from a dermal element corresponding to the angular (Gaupp 1913). (b) The marsupial newborn uses the malleus–incus joint as its jaw joint before the dentary–squamosal joint forms. (c) Fossils: a series of cynodonts and mammaliaforms shows the postdentary bones shrinking and moving toward the ear, the double jaw joint of *Morganucodon*, and in *Yanoconodon*, *Liaoconodon* and *Origolestes* ossicles detached from the jaw but still connected to it by ossified Meckel's cartilage. (d) Gene expression: the same genes (such as *Bapx1*) that pattern the jaw joint in other vertebrates are expressed in the malleus–incus joint of mammals.
4. Why is diphyodonty linked to lactation and precise occlusion in mammals?

   **Answer** Precise occlusion, in which the cusps of upper and lower molars fit against each other in a fixed relation, requires that teeth remain in place and that the jaws stop growing; continuously replaced teeth of changing size and position, as in reptiles, cannot maintain such a fit. Mammals solved this by replacing the teeth only once (diphyodonty) and by having determinate growth. A newborn mammal fed with milk does not need to chew, so it can delay the eruption of most teeth until its jaw is nearly adult size; the deciduous teeth bridge the period of growth, and the permanent teeth erupt when the jaw has almost reached its final size. Lactation thus allowed the evolution of a dentition that is set once and fits precisely, and molars, which erupt last, are never replaced.
5. (Open) Hair and mammary glands do not fossilize. Discuss how we can estimate when they evolved, and what the uncertainties are.

   **Answer** Approaches: (a) phylogenetic bracketing — monotremes and therians both have hair and milk, so their common ancestor (Jurassic) had them; this gives a minimum date. (b) Direct fossil evidence — fur in *Castorocauda* (Middle Jurassic, a docodont outside the crown group) pushes hair to the base of the mammaliaforms; hair-like structures in Permian coprolites, if correctly identified, would push it to therapsids. (c) Osteological correlates — the maxillary canal for whisker nerves (probainognathians), snout foramina, turbinates (insulation needed by endotherms), patterns of tooth replacement consistent with lactation (diphyodonty in mammaliaforms suggests lactation). (d) Molecular evidence — the genes for milk proteins and hair keratins can be dated by comparing their families across amniotes. Uncertainties: correlates can be ambiguous (snout foramina occur in reptiles without whiskers), fossil identification of hair in coprolites is debated, and molecular dates have wide confidence intervals; different characters (vibrissae, body fur, glands) may have evolved at different times.
