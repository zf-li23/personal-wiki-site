Vertebrates are the chordates with a head. The lancelets and tunicates of [7.17.43](/7.17.43) filter food with cilia and mucus and sense the world with a few scattered receptors; a vertebrate has a skull enclosing a large brain, paired eyes, ears and nose, a muscular pharynx that pumps water over gills, a heart with several chambers, kidneys that filter blood, and a skeleton of cartilage and, in most groups, bone. Almost all these novelties are built in the embryo from two cell populations that are absent, or present only in rudimentary form, in other chordates: the **neural crest** and the cranial **placodes**. With them came an active life, larger bodies and eventually predation. The roughly 70 000 living species of vertebrates, from hagfishes to humans, share this body plan.

This chapter opens the part of the course on vertebrate diversity ([7.17.45](/7.17.45)–[7.17.55](/7.17.55)). It first defines the vertebrate characters and explains how they are built (§45.1), then outlines the phylogeny and fossil record of early vertebrates (§45.2). The living jawless vertebrates, hagfishes and lampreys, are treated in §45.3, with a lamprey as the representative animal; the extinct jawless groups, which document the steps toward jawed vertebrates, follow in §45.4, and the chapter ends with a comparison with the traditional classification (§45.5). The jaws themselves and the cartilaginous fishes are in [7.17.46](/7.17.46). The developmental mechanisms of the neural crest and the placodes are in [7.5.20](/7.5.20) and [7.5.22](/7.5.22); the comparative anatomy of each organ system across vertebrates is in [7.17.56](/7.17.56)–[7.17.68](/7.17.68), to which this chapter links instead of repeating it.

## 45.1. What Is a Vertebrate?

### 45.1.1. The Defining Characters

All vertebrates share the five chordate characters of [7.17.43](/7.17.43): a **notochord**, a dorsal hollow nerve cord, **pharyngeal slits**, an **endostyle** (transformed into the thyroid gland in adults) and a muscular postanal tail with **myomeres**. To these they add a long list of their own.

**Definition 45.1.1 (Vertebrata)** The **Vertebrata** (vertebrates, craniates) are the clade of chordates comprising the cyclostomes (hagfishes and lampreys) and the jawed vertebrates, the sister group of the tunicates within the **Olfactores**. They have a distinct head with a cranium (a cartilaginous or bony capsule around the brain and the paired sense organs), a brain divided into forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain, paired eyes, an inner ear with semicircular canals, a muscular pharynx with gills of endodermal origin, a heart with at least three chambers, kidneys of segmental tubules with glomeruli, cartilage, and at least rudimentary vertebral elements beside the notochord; in the embryo they form a migratory neural crest and cranial placodes.

The name refers to the vertebrae, the segmental skeletal elements around the notochord and nerve cord. For several decades an alternative name, Craniata, was preferred for the whole group, because hagfishes were thought to have a cranium but no vertebral elements at all; "Vertebrata" was then restricted to lampreys and jawed vertebrates. Two findings removed the reason for the distinction: hagfishes and lampreys were shown to be each other's closest relatives (§45.3.1), and hagfish embryos were found to form cartilaginous vertebral elements in the tail (Ota et al. 2011). Today Vertebrata and Craniata are used as synonyms, and this course uses Vertebrata.

### 45.1.2. Neural Crest, Placodes and the "New Head"

The **neural crest** is a population of cells that arises at the border between the neural plate and the epidermis, leaves the closing neural tube by an epithelial–mesenchymal transition and migrates through the embryo. In the trunk it forms sensory and autonomic neurons, glia (including the Schwann cells that make myelin in jawed vertebrates), pigment cells and the adrenal medulla. In the head it forms much more: the cartilage and bone of the pharyngeal arches and the front of the skull, the dentine-forming cells of teeth, the connective tissue of the face, and the walls of the aortic arch arteries. Because its derivatives are so diverse, the crest is sometimes called a fourth germ layer. Its gene regulatory network and the experiments that revealed its potency are in [7.5.20](/7.5.20).

The cranial **placodes** are thickenings of the head ectoderm next to the crest ([7.5.22](/7.5.22)). The olfactory placode forms the sensory epithelium of the nose; the lens placode, the lens; the otic placode, the inner ear; the lateral line placodes, the neuromasts and electroreceptors of the lateral line; the epibranchial and trigeminal placodes, the sensory neurons of several cranial nerve ganglia; the adenohypophyseal placode, the anterior pituitary.

In 1983 Carl Gans and Glenn Northcutt combined these facts into the *new head hypothesis*. They argued that the vertebrate head is a new structure added to the front of a lancelet-like chordate, and that it was built largely from the two new tissues. The ancestral chordates were filter feeders that pumped water with cilia; the first vertebrates shifted to a more active life, pumping water with muscles of the pharyngeal wall (which allowed larger bodies and more forceful ventilation) and eventually seizing larger food particles and prey. Paired distance senses (eyes, nose, lateral line) located food at a distance, an enlarged brain processed their information, and a skeleton of crest-derived cartilage supported the muscular pharynx and protected brain and sense organs. In this view the neural crest and placodes were the developmental innovations that made a predatory vertebrate possible.

The hypothesis has been revised in two respects. First, the new tissues did not appear from nothing. The tunicates, the sister group of vertebrates, have neural plate border cells that express crest genes and migrate to form pigment cells, and ectodermal regions that express placode genes and form sensory cells ([7.17.43](/7.17.43#sec-43.4)). The vertebrate crest seems to have evolved by adding programmes for migration over long distances and for skeleton formation to a pre-existing border cell population; lancelets already have the border genes but not the migratory cells. Second, the lancelet-like head of the vertebrate ancestor is not entirely without counterparts: lancelets have a frontal eye homologous with the vertebrate paired eyes and a forebrain-like region, so the vertebrate brain was enlarged and elaborated rather than added.

The ecological part of the hypothesis has also been tested against fossils. The larval lamprey, the ammocoete, is a filter feeder with an endostyle and was long taken as a model of the ancestral vertebrate. But fossil lampreys from the Devonian and Carboniferous had no ammocoete stage: their hatchlings already had the adult oral disc and eyes (§45.3.3). The ammocoete is probably a derived larval specialization of modern lampreys, and the earliest vertebrates were small, active animals that took larger food particles, as the new head hypothesis proposed.

### 45.1.3. Cartilage, Bone and the Mineralized Tissues

Vertebrates have two kinds of skeletal tissue unknown in other animals in this form: cartilage of the vertebrate type, and mineralized tissues with crystals of hydroxyapatite, a calcium phosphate, deposited in a collagenous matrix. Calcium carbonate skeletons, common in invertebrates (the spicules of sponges, the shells of molluscs, the ossicles of echinoderms), are not used.

*Cartilage* is a tissue of chondrocytes embedded in a matrix of type II collagen and proteoglycans, firm but flexible and able to grow from within. Cartilage-like tissues occur in some invertebrates (the odontophore of snails, the head cartilage of cephalopods, the lophophore skeleton of brachiopods), and even lancelets express part of the cartilage gene network in their oral cirri, but the cellular cartilage of the vertebrate pharyngeal arches is built by neural crest cells activating *Sox9* and *Col2a1*. Cyclostomes have several kinds of cartilage, some of them built mainly from proteins other than collagen (the elastin-like lamprin of lampreys).

The mineralized tissues differ in their cells and in how they are deposited:

| Tissue | Cells and origin | Structure | Occurrence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bone | Osteoblasts (mesoderm or neural crest), trapped as osteocytes in cellular bone | Collagen I matrix with hydroxyapatite, vascularized; remodelled throughout life | Aspidin (acellular) in heterostracans; cellular bone from osteostracans onward |
| Calcified cartilage | Chondrocytes | Cartilage matrix impregnated with apatite, often as small tiles (tesserae) | Cartilaginous fishes, early gnathostomes |
| Dentine | Odontoblasts (neural crest), which stay outside the tissue and send processes into tubules | More mineralized than bone, no cells inside, not remodelled | Odontodes, teeth, placoid scales |
| Enamel | Ameloblasts (epidermis, ectoderm) | The most mineralized vertebrate tissue (about 96 % mineral), almost no protein | Teeth of sarcopterygians, ganoine of ganoid scales |
| Enameloid | Odontoblasts and ameloblasts together | Hard capping tissue with a collagenous matrix that is later removed | Teeth and scales of sharks and of teleosts |
| Cementum | Cementoblasts | Bone-like tissue that anchors teeth | Mammals and other amniotes with socketed teeth |

Bone forms in three ways ([7.5.24](/7.5.24)). **Dermal bone** forms directly in the dermis by intramembranous ossification, without a cartilage precursor; the earliest vertebrate bone was dermal, in the armour of jawless fishes. Perichondral bone forms as a sheath on the surface of a cartilage element. Endochondral bone (**cartilage bone**) replaces cartilage from inside; among living vertebrates it is found only in bony fishes and tetrapods. The distinction between dermal bone and cartilage bone organizes the whole comparative anatomy of the skull ([7.17.58](/7.17.58)).

The basic unit of the early dermal skeleton was the *odontode*, a small tooth-like element of dentine, usually capped with enameloid, sitting on a base of bone. The armour of early jawless and jawed fishes consisted of odontodes fused to underlying bone; the placoid scales of sharks are isolated odontodes ([7.17.46](/7.17.46)). Teeth are odontodes in the mouth. An "outside-in" hypothesis derives teeth from skin odontodes that spread into the mouth at the margins of the jaws; an "inside-out" hypothesis derives them from tooth-like structures of the pharynx. The developmental programmes of teeth and scales share most genes, and both hypotheses may be partly true ([7.5.22](/7.5.22#sec-22.4.2)).

Why did vertebrates mineralize their skeleton with calcium phosphate? Bone serves as armour and as a framework for muscles, but it is also a store of calcium and phosphate that can be mobilized under hormonal control, a function it has in all bony vertebrates. Phosphate is scarce in seawater, and a reservoir would have been an advantage to active animals with a high demand for ATP. Both functions may have favoured the earliest dermal skeleton; which came first is unknown.

### 45.1.4. Muscles, Pharynx and Organs

The myomeres of vertebrates are W-shaped in side view (V-shaped in lancelets), with forward and backward pointing cones. Each myomere spans several vertebral segments, so that the contraction of one block bends a long stretch of the body and the muscle fibres of different depths shorten by similar amounts during undulation. In jawed vertebrates a horizontal septum divides the myomeres into dorsal epaxial and ventral hypaxial parts; cyclostomes have no horizontal septum ([7.17.62](/7.17.62)).

The pharynx is muscular and pumps water. In lancelets the water current is driven by cilia on the gill bars; in vertebrates it is driven by muscles of the pharyngeal arches, which expand and compress the pharynx against an elastic skeleton of cartilage bars. Gill filaments with thin secondary lamellae, richly supplied with blood, line the pouches between the arches and are the main site of gas exchange; they derive from the endoderm of the pharyngeal pouches, unlike the external gills of amphibian larvae, which are ectodermal ([7.17.64](/7.17.64)). Each arch carries its own skeletal element, muscles, nerve and artery, and the fates of these arch components can be followed from fishes to mammals ([7.17.58](/7.17.58)); in a fish the arches are numbered from front to back, the mandibular arch being the first and the hyoid arch the second.

The heart is a muscular tube folded into a series of chambers: sinus venosus, atrium and ventricle in cyclostomes, with a conus arteriosus added in jawed fishes. It pumps venous blood forward into the ventral aorta, which branches into aortic arches running through the gills to the dorsal aorta ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)). The blood carries red cells with haemoglobin, and the whole system is closed. The kidney filters blood plasma at glomeruli and reabsorbs useful solutes from the filtrate in tubules, which allows the regulation of water and ions independent of the environment ([7.14.21](/7.14.21)); it develops from the intermediate mesoderm in a front-to-back series of pronephros, mesonephros and, in amniotes, metanephros ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)).

### 45.1.5. Two Rounds of Genome Duplication

Vertebrate genomes contain many families of paralogous genes where invertebrates have one: four *Hox* clusters in humans where lancelets have one, four *Dlx* bigene pairs, several fibroblast growth factor receptors, and so on. Susumu Ohno proposed in 1970 that the vertebrate genome had been duplicated as a whole, and the "2R hypothesis" of two rounds of **whole-genome duplication** early in vertebrate history is now supported by the arrangement of paralogous genes in conserved blocks of synteny: comparing the human genome with the lancelet genome, most lancelet chromosomal regions correspond to four regions in humans, as expected after two duplications (Putnam et al. 2008).

Where the duplications occurred relative to the split between cyclostomes and jawed vertebrates was long unclear, because cyclostome genomes are rich in GC and hard to align. Analyses of the hagfish and lamprey genomes published in 2024 (Marlétaz et al.; Yu et al.) concluded that the first duplication (1R) took place in the common ancestor of all vertebrates, the second (2R) only in the lineage leading to jawed vertebrates, and that cyclostomes underwent an independent genome triplication of their own. Teleost fishes added a third duplication (3R) about 320 million years ago ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)), and salmonids and some carps a fourth.

**Example 45.1.1 (Counting Hox Clusters)** Assume that the chordate ancestor had one *Hox* cluster, as lancelets do, and that each whole-genome duplication doubles every cluster. After 1R and 2R a jawed vertebrate should have $1 \times 2 \times 2 = 4$ clusters, as found in humans, the coelacanth and the gar (HoxA, HoxB, HoxC and HoxD). A third duplication in teleosts predicts $4 \times 2 = 8$; zebrafish have seven, having lost one copy of HoxD (HoxDb), and pufferfishes also seven, with different losses. Sharks and rays have three because the HoxC cluster was lost in the elasmobranch lineage, although chimaeras retain it. Lampreys, with 1R followed by an independent triplication, would be expected to have $1 \times 2 \times 3 = 6$; at least six *Hox* clusters have been found in the sea lamprey genome. The counts thus match the duplication history, and the departures from the simple products are explained by the loss of whole clusters after duplication.

Duplicated genes are released from some of the constraints of a single copy. One copy may keep the old function while the other acquires a new function (neofunctionalization), or the two may divide the old functions between them (subfunctionalization; the fates of duplicates are discussed in [7.10.18](/7.10.18#sec-18.2)). It has often been suggested that the genome duplications provided the raw material for vertebrate innovations such as the neural crest, the jaws and the adaptive immune system. The timing fits for some of them: the four *Dlx* pairs used in coding the jaw ([7.17.46](/7.17.46)) appeared with 2R. But cyclostomes have a neural crest, placodes and a complex brain after only one shared duplication, and many duplicates were lost soon after duplication. The duplications enlarged the toolkit; they did not by themselves create the vertebrate body plan.

## 45.2. Vertebrate Phylogeny and the Fossil Record

### 45.2.1. The Earliest Vertebrates

The oldest known vertebrates come from the early Cambrian Chengjiang biota of Yunnan, China, about 518 million years old. *Haikouichthys ercaicunensis* and *Myllokunmingia fengjiaoa* (probably the same animal) were fish-shaped, about 3 cm long, with a dorsal fin, a ventral fin fold, W-shaped myomeres, six or seven gill pouches, paired eyes and a cartilaginous skeleton of the head and gill region (Shu et al. 1999, 2003). *Metaspriggina walcotti* from the Burgess Shale of Canada (about 508 million years) shows paired eyes, a pair of nasal capsules and gill bars. These animals had no mineralized tissues. They show that the vertebrate head with its sense organs evolved before bone, and that vertebrates were already present at the time of the **Cambrian explosion** ([7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.4)).

Mineralized vertebrate tissues first appear as fragments of dermal armour in the Late Cambrian and Ordovician (the identity of the oldest, *Anatolepis*, is disputed), and complete armoured fishes in the Middle and Late Ordovician, about 470–445 million years ago. Jawed vertebrates are known from scales in the Ordovician and from complete fossils in the Silurian, about 435 million years ago. From the late Silurian through the Devonian, jawless and jawed fishes lived side by side for about 50 million years; most jawless groups disappeared in the Late Devonian extinctions, about 372 and 359 million years ago, together with many marine invertebrates and early jawed fishes.

Several characters can be placed on the tree. Bone (aspidin, without cells) first appears in the pteraspidomorphs; paired fins, cellular bone, perichondral bone, sclerotic rings around the eyes and a heterocercal tail fin in the osteostracans; jaws, a second pair of paired fins, three semicircular canals and true teeth in the gnathostomes. The relationships of the cyclostomes to the fossil groups are less certain. The conodonts are placed by some analyses on the gnathostome stem (as in the figure), by others as stem cyclostomes (Miyashita et al. 2019); a few authors doubt that they are vertebrates at all.

### 45.2.3. Marine or Freshwater Origin?

The vertebrate kidney excretes large volumes of dilute urine while retaining ions and organic solutes, which is what a freshwater fish must do. This led Homer Smith and others to argue that vertebrates evolved in fresh water. Three observations now favour a marine origin: all other deuterostomes are marine; the earliest vertebrate fossils (Chengjiang, the Ordovician armoured fishes) come from marine or nearshore marine sediments; and hagfishes, the most marine of vertebrates, have body fluids nearly isosmotic with seawater, as invertebrate deuterostomes do. The glomerular kidney may have evolved for regulating the volume of extracellular fluid and divalent ions, and only later proved useful in fresh water. Lampreys, teleosts and many other groups invaded fresh water independently and evolved hypoosmotic regulation.

## 45.3. Cyclostomes: Hagfishes and Lampreys

### 45.3.1. Cyclostome Characters and Monophyly

**Definition 45.3.1 (Cyclostomata)** The **Cyclostomata** (cyclostomes) are the clade of living jawless vertebrates, comprising the hagfishes and the lampreys. They lack jaws, paired fins, mineralized tissues, a horizontal septum and specialized gonoducts; they have a single median nostril and nasohypophyseal opening, a skeleton of the gill region that forms an unjointed branchial basket lateral to the gill pouches, a muscular velum that pumps water, a tongue-like apparatus with horny teeth of keratin moved by a lingual cartilage, and an adaptive immune system based on variable lymphocyte receptors.

Whether hagfishes and lampreys form a clade was one of the long debates of vertebrate zoology. Morphological cladistic analyses from the 1970s to the 1990s (Løvtrup, Janvier and others) found the lampreys closer to jawed vertebrates than to hagfishes: lampreys share with gnathostomes vertebral elements along the whole body, a lateral line with neuromasts, electroreceptors, an innervated heart, extrinsic eye muscles, two or more semicircular canals and a hypoosmotic regulation of body fluids, all absent in hagfishes. If hagfishes were the sister group of all other vertebrates, the cyclostomes would be paraphyletic and hagfishes would be the best model of the earliest vertebrate.

Molecular data told another story. Phylogenies of ribosomal RNA genes, then of hundreds of nuclear genes and of microRNA families (Heimberg et al. 2010), consistently unite hagfishes and lampreys. New morphological evidence also supports the clade. Hagfish embryos, studied for the first time with modern methods in the 2000s (*Eptatretus burgeri* in Japan; Ota et al. 2007), form a migratory neural crest and placodes like those of lampreys; hagfishes have rudimentary vertebral elements (§45.1.1); and the Cretaceous fossil hagfish *Tethymyxine* shows that slime glands and periocular trunk muscles were present in stem hagfishes. Many "primitive" characters of hagfishes, such as their poorly developed eyes and lateral line, are now interpreted as losses in a burrowing, scavenging lineage rather than as the ancestral vertebrate state.

Monophyly has a consequence for interpreting the ancestor. Characters shared by cyclostomes and gnathostomes, such as the neural crest, placodes, a cranium and the gills of the pharyngeal pouches, were present in the last common ancestor of all vertebrates. Characters that differ between them, such as the cyclostome velum and branchial basket or the gnathostome jointed gill arches, cannot be polarized from the living groups alone; the fossil stem gnathostomes (§45.4) are needed. Living cyclostomes are not ancestors but a specialized branch with a history as long as ours.

### 45.3.2. Hagfishes

**Definition 45.3.2 (Hagfish)** **Hagfishes** (Myxini, order Myxiniformes) are marine, eel-shaped cyclostomes without fins other than a low median fin fold around the tail, with a terminal median nostril that opens into the pharynx and is used for ventilation, three or four pairs of barbels around the nostril and mouth, two rows of horny teeth on a protrusible dental plate that opens and closes horizontally, five to sixteen pairs of gill pouches, rows of slime glands along the body, poorly developed eyes beneath the skin, and body fluids isosmotic with seawater.

There are about 80 species in one family, Myxinidae, in two main subfamilies: Myxininae (*Myxine* and relatives), whose gill pouches open by a single common external opening on each side, and Eptatretinae (*Eptatretus*), with separate openings for each pouch. They live on soft bottoms of the continental shelves and slopes of temperate seas, mostly in cold water, often at depths of hundreds of metres; there are none in fresh water and few in the polar seas. Adults are mostly 30–80 cm long. Along the coasts of China live *Eptatretus burgeri* (in the East China Sea, Yellow Sea and around Japan and Korea), *E. yangi* (formerly *Paramyxine yangi*, from Taiwan and the southern coast) and several other species described from the waters around Taiwan.

*Feeding.* Hagfishes locate food mainly by smell and touch, with the barbels and the olfactory organ. They feed on polychaetes, shrimps and other invertebrates in the sediment and scavenge on dead and dying vertebrates, the carcasses of whales and fishes that sink to the bottom ("whale falls"), into which they burrow head first, feeding from the inside. Video recordings also show them catching small fishes from burrows. A hagfish has no jaws but a protrusible dental plate with two pairs of rows of horny teeth; the plate is everted, the rows spread apart, then pulled back and closed, grasping food in a horizontal pinch. To tear off a piece, a hagfish ties its body into a knot and slides the knot forward to brace against the food; the same knotting is used to escape from a grip and to wipe off slime. Hagfishes can also absorb dissolved amino acids across the skin and gills, unique among vertebrates, which helps when buried in a carcass.

*Slime.* Along each side of the body, 90–200 slime glands open by pores. When a hagfish is attacked, the glands release mucus and thread cells; each thread cell contains a single coiled protein thread made of intermediate filaments, about 1–3 µm thick and up to 15 cm long. In seawater the threads unravel within a fraction of a second and, with the mucus, trap a large volume of water in a gel. The slime is one of the most dilute gels known, containing only a few milligrams of solids per litre of water. It clogs the gills of a predator: films show sharks and other fishes that bite a hagfish releasing it immediately and choking. The protein threads are strong and elastic, and their structure is studied as a model for synthetic fibres.

*Body fluids and circulation.* Hagfishes are **osmoconformers**: the osmotic concentration of their plasma is close to that of seawater (about $1000\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$), with sodium and chloride as the main ions, like the fluids of marine invertebrates ([7.14.21](/7.14.21#sec-21.5.2)). They regulate divalent ions (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, SO₄²⁻) and acid–base balance. The heart has no nervous innervation (it lacks vagal fibres), and accessory hearts help the circulation: a portal heart pumps blood from the intestine and anterior cardinal veins into the liver, and cardinal and caudal hearts move venous blood in the head and tail. Blood pressure is the lowest of any vertebrate, the blood volume large. Hagfishes tolerate hypoxia and even anoxia for many hours, an adaptation to feeding inside carcasses and living in oxygen-poor mud.

*Sense organs and nervous system.* The eyes lie beneath unpigmented skin and have no lens, cornea or extrinsic muscles; the retina has photoreceptors and can detect light but not form images. Light-sensitive cells also occur in the skin of the head and cloaca. There is one semicircular canal on each side, interpreted either as the original condition or as two vertical canals fused into a ring with two ampullae; the lateral line consists of few lines of sensory cells without typical neuromasts; there are no electroreceptors. The brain is relatively large, with a well-developed olfactory region, but the cerebellum is absent.

*Excretion and reproduction.* A functional pronephros persists in the front of the body beside the heart, and a mesonephric kidney (opisthonephros) of segmental, large glomeruli drains into the archinephric ducts. There is one median gonad. Juvenile gonads are undifferentiated, and the anterior part may develop into an ovary and the posterior into a testis; adults are usually of one sex, although some species are reported to be hermaphrodites. Sex ratios are often strongly biased toward females. Eggs are few (usually fewer than 30 per female) and large, 2–3 cm long, enclosed in a horny capsule with anchor filaments at both ends; development is direct and very slow, without a larva. The difficulty of obtaining embryos kept hagfish development unknown for a century, until the laboratory spawning of *Eptatretus burgeri* in Japan.

*Hagfishes and people.* In Korea and Japan *Eptatretus burgeri* is fished for food and for its skin, which is tanned into soft "eel skin" leather; overfishing has depleted Asian stocks, and fisheries have spread to the North Pacific coast of America. Very little is known about the population biology of hagfishes, which makes the fisheries hard to manage.

### 45.3.3. Lampreys

**Definition 45.3.3 (Lamprey)** **Lampreys** (Petromyzontida, order Petromyzontiformes) are cyclostomes with an eel-shaped body, one or two dorsal fins and a caudal fin; a ventral suctorial oral disc (buccal funnel) lined with horny teeth around the mouth, with a rasping tongue supported by a piston cartilage; a median nasohypophyseal opening on top of the head that ends blindly in a nasohypophyseal sac; well-developed lateral eyes and a pineal eye; seven pairs of gill pouches opening separately behind the head; and a life cycle with a filter-feeding larva, the **ammocoete**, that metamorphoses into the adult.

There are about 40–50 species in three families: Petromyzontidae of the Northern Hemisphere (most species), and Geotriidae (the pouched lamprey *Geotria*) and Mordaciidae (*Mordacia*) of the temperate Southern Hemisphere. All spawn in fresh water. In anadromous species the adults live in the sea and ascend rivers to breed; others spend their whole life in rivers and lakes. About half the species are parasitic as adults, feeding on the blood and tissues of fishes; the others, the brook lampreys, do not feed after metamorphosis and live only a few months to breed. Many brook lampreys form "paired species" with a parasitic species from which they evolved repeatedly, such as the European river lamprey *Lampetra fluviatilis* and brook lamprey *L. planeri*, which are hardly distinguishable as larvae.

*Representative animal: the northeastern lamprey.* The Chinese textbooks describe *Lethenteron morii* (northeastern lamprey; often listed as *Lampetra morii*), a small freshwater species about 15–30 cm long from the Yalu and Liao rivers and other rivers of northeastern China and Korea. Its body plan is that of all lampreys.

- *External features.* The eel-shaped body is divided into head, trunk and tail; the tail is laterally compressed. The skin is soft, smooth and scaleless, with many mucous cells. On top of the head lies the single nasohypophyseal opening, behind it a pale patch of skin over the pineal eye. Seven round external gill openings lie behind each eye. The oral disc on the underside of the head is fringed with small papillae and studded with horny teeth; the tongue also bears teeth and acts as a rasp. There are two dorsal fins and a symmetrical (protocercal) caudal fin, without paired fins; the anus and, behind it, a urogenital papilla lie at the base of the tail.
- *Skeleton and muscles.* The notochord persists throughout life as the main axial support, with a thick fibrous sheath. Small paired cartilages (arcualia) along its dorsal side, two pairs per segment, are rudiments of neural arches. The neurocranium is incomplete: a cartilaginous floor beneath the brain with low side walls, and separate olfactory and otic capsules. The gill region is supported by a branchial basket, a continuous lattice of cartilage bars under the skin, lateral to the gill pouches, ending behind in a pericardial cartilage that protects the heart. Annular and piston cartilages support the oral disc and the tongue. The myomeres are W-shaped, without a horizontal septum.
- *Digestion.* The mouth opens at the bottom of the oral disc. Oral glands secrete an anticoagulant (lamphredin) that keeps the host's blood flowing. Behind the mouth the pharynx divides into a dorsal oesophagus and a ventral, blind respiratory tube (branchial tube), separated by the velum; food passes along the oesophagus while water moves in and out of the respiratory tube. There is no stomach; the straight intestine has a longitudinal fold (typhlosole) that increases its surface. The liver has no gall bladder in adults, and the pancreatic tissue is scattered in the gut wall.
- *Respiration.* Each side of the respiratory tube has seven internal gill openings, each leading into a spherical gill pouch lined with gill filaments; each pouch opens to the outside by an external gill opening. While attached to a host, an adult cannot take water in through the mouth; it pumps water in and out through the external gill openings by contracting and relaxing the branchial basket (tidal ventilation). Larvae and free-swimming adults draw water in through the mouth and pump it out over the gills (flow-through ventilation).
- *Circulation.* The heart lies behind the gill pouches in a pericardial cavity and consists of a sinus venosus, an atrium and a ventricle. The ventral aorta gives off arteries to the gill pouches; oxygenated blood collects in the dorsal aorta. There is a hepatic portal vein but no renal portal system.
- *Nervous system.* The brain has the five regions of the vertebrate brain (telencephalon, diencephalon, mesencephalon, cerebellum, medulla oblongata), lying in one plane without flexures. The olfactory bulbs are large, the cerebral hemispheres small; the cerebellum is a narrow band. There are ten pairs of cranial nerves; the spinal nerves have dorsal roots with ganglia and ventral roots that remain separate. The axons have no myelin sheaths (myelin is a gnathostome character).
- *Sense organs.* The single nostril leads to one olfactory sac (paired in the embryo), from which the nasohypophyseal sac extends backward beneath the brain; part of its wall forms the adenohypophysis. The inner ear has two vertical semicircular canals. The eyes have a lens and a retina; accommodation is achieved by flattening the cornea with a special muscle. The pineal and parapineal organs on the roof of the diencephalon are light-sensitive. Neuromasts of the lateral line lie in shallow grooves on the head and body, and electroreceptors (end buds) are present in the skin.
- *Urogenital system.* A pair of long mesonephric kidneys drain by archinephric ducts into a urogenital sinus. The sexes are separate; the single gonad has no ducts, and eggs or sperm are shed into the body cavity and leave through genital pores into the urogenital sinus and out by the urogenital papilla. Fertilization is external.

*Feeding of parasitic adults.* A parasitic lamprey attaches to a fish with its oral disc by suction, rasps a wound through the skin and scales with the toothed tongue, and feeds on blood and tissue fluids, sometimes on muscle. Feeding may continue for days on one host. Hosts often survive but may die from the wound or from secondary infection, especially when small. The diet is easily digested, and the gut is simple.

*Life cycle.* In spring adults migrate upstream to gravelly stretches of clear rivers. Using their oral discs, pairs move stones to dig a shallow nest; the female attaches to a stone at the upstream edge, the male attaches to her head and wraps around her, and eggs and sperm are shed together. A female of *Lethenteron* lays some 10 000–20 000 eggs, a sea lamprey up to 100 000. The adults die after spawning: lampreys are semelparous (**semelparity**). The eggs, about 1 mm in diameter and moderately yolky, undergo holoblastic but unequal cleavage. After a few weeks the larvae hatch, drift downstream and burrow into soft mud of slow backwaters.

The larva was originally described as a separate genus of "primitive" chordates, and the Latin name of that genus is now used as the common name of the larva itself. The ammocoete is worm-shaped, with an oral hood, eyes hidden beneath the skin, a continuous fin fold, and a large pharynx with seven pairs of gill slits and an endostyle. It lives buried in the mud with only the oral hood exposed and filters organic particles, diatoms and bacteria from the water, which is pumped by the velum and pharyngeal muscles; mucus from the endostyle and the pharyngeal walls traps the particles, as in a lancelet. The larval period lasts three to seven years, longer than the adult life.

At metamorphosis, which takes several months, the oral hood becomes the oral disc with teeth, the eyes develop and appear at the surface, the pharynx divides into oesophagus and branchial tube, the endostyle transforms into the follicles of the thyroid gland, the gut is remodelled, and the kidney changes. Unlike amphibian **metamorphosis**, which is driven by a rise of **thyroid hormones** ([7.14.34](/7.14.34#sec-34.7.1)), lamprey metamorphosis begins with a sharp fall in thyroid hormone levels, and it can be induced experimentally by blocking thyroid hormone synthesis. Thyroid hormone controls metamorphosis in both groups, but the sign of the signal is reversed.

**Example 45.3.1 (Survival in a Semelparous Life Cycle)** A female sea lamprey lays about $n = 60\,000$ eggs and dies. If the population is to remain constant on average, each female must be replaced by one breeding female in the next generation; with a sex ratio of 1:1 she must produce on average two breeding adults. The probability that an egg survives through the larval, metamorphic and parasitic phases to spawn is therefore

$$
s = \frac{2}{n} = \frac{2}{6 \times 10^{4}} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-5}.
$$
If the total generation time of about seven years is divided into one year as an egg and young larva, five further larval years, half a year of metamorphosis and one year of parasitic feeding, and if survival is the same in each year, the annual survival is $s^{1/7.5} \approx 0.25$. In reality mortality is concentrated in the egg and early larval stages; once a lamprey reaches the parasitic phase its survival is high. The calculation shows how small an increase in early survival is needed for a population to grow: doubling egg-to-larva survival doubles the next generation, which is why a population freed of predators and competitors, like the sea lamprey in the upper Great Lakes, can expand rapidly.

*Lampreys and people.* Sea lampreys (*Petromyzon marinus*) entered the upper Great Lakes of North America through the Welland Canal, which bypasses Niagara Falls; between the 1920s and 1950s they spread through all the lakes and, together with overfishing, contributed to the collapse of the lake trout fisheries. Control since the 1950s combines a lampricide (TFM) that kills larvae in the spawning streams, barriers and traps at river mouths, and the release of sterilized males; pheromones that guide migrating adults (bile acids released by larvae, and a male sex pheromone) are being tested as lures. In Europe, by contrast, lampreys have declined because dams block their migrations, and they are protected. In China, *Lethenteron camtschaticum* (Arctic lamprey, the "Japanese lamprey" of older Chinese literature, *Lampetra japonica*), *L. reissneri* and *L. morii* live in the rivers of the northeast; their populations have declined with dams and pollution.

*Fossil lampreys.* Lampreys have a sparse fossil record because they lack bone: *Priscomyzon* from the Late Devonian of South Africa (about 360 million years old), *Mayomyzon* and *Pipiscius* from the Carboniferous Mazon Creek beds of Illinois, and *Mesomyzon mengae* from the Early Cretaceous Jehol biota of Liaoning, China, the first fossil lamprey from fresh water (Chang et al. 2006). A growth series of *Priscomyzon* showed that its hatchlings already had a large oral disc and eyes and were not filter-feeding ammocoetes (Miyashita et al. 2021). The ammocoete stage evolved later, perhaps when lampreys moved into fresh water to breed.

### 45.3.4. A Second Adaptive Immune System

The **adaptive immunity** of jawed vertebrates recognizes antigens with immunoglobulins and T-cell receptors, whose variable regions are assembled by the recombination of gene segments under the control of the RAG enzymes ([7.15.3](/7.15.3)). Lampreys and hagfishes have no immunoglobulins, T-cell receptors, MHC molecules or RAG genes, yet they make specific antibody responses and have immunological memory. Their lymphocytes carry *variable lymphocyte receptors* (VLRs) built from leucine-rich repeat modules. During lymphocyte development, a cytidine deaminase related to the activation-induced deaminase of jawed vertebrates copies segments from a large library of flanking modules into an incomplete germline *VLR* gene, generating a repertoire of perhaps $10^{14}$ different receptors (Pancer et al. 2004). Three classes of VLR are expressed in three lymphocyte lineages: VLRB on B-like cells, which secrete it as a multimeric antibody, and VLRA and VLRC on T-like cells, one of which matures in a thymus-like tissue at the tips of the gill filaments (the thymoid). Jawless and jawed vertebrates thus evolved adaptive immune systems independently on a shared foundation of lymphocyte lineages, and the comparison shows that very different molecules can solve the problem of generating diverse antigen receptors.

## 45.4. Extinct Jawless Vertebrates

### 45.4.1. Conodonts

Conodont elements are microscopic (mostly 0.1–2 mm) tooth-like fossils of calcium phosphate, extremely abundant in marine rocks from the Late Cambrian to the end of the Triassic. Because their shapes evolved rapidly, they are among the most important index fossils for dating Palaeozoic and Triassic strata. For more than a century the animals that bore them, the *conodonts*, were unknown. Fossils with soft parts found in the 1980s (*Clydagnathus* from the Carboniferous of Scotland; Briggs et al. 1983) and later the large *Promissum* from the Ordovician of South Africa showed an eel-shaped animal a few centimetres long, with large eyes, myomeres, a notochord and a fin with rays, the elements forming a complex grasping and slicing apparatus in the pharynx.

The animal has the general form of a vertebrate, and conodonts were widely accepted as the earliest vertebrates with a mineralized skeleton. Studies with synchrotron tomography, however, found that the hard tissues of conodont elements grew differently from vertebrate dentine and enamel and probably evolved convergently (Murdock et al. 2013). If conodonts are vertebrates, their position is unresolved: on the gnathostome stem, or as stem cyclostomes.

### 45.4.2. Pteraspidomorphs, Anaspids and Thelodonts

The Pteraspidomorphi include the earliest armoured fishes. The arandaspids of the Ordovician (*Sacabambaspis* from Bolivia, *Arandaspis* from Australia) and the related *Astraspis* from North America were 12–35 cm long, with a head and trunk covered by plates and scales of acellular bone (aspidin) with dentine tubercles, a row of gill openings, small eyes at the front and a fin-less trunk ending in a tail fin. The heterostracans of the Silurian and Devonian (*Pteraspis*, *Doryaspis*, *Drepanaspis*) had large dorsal and ventral head shields with a single gill opening on each side; many had a hypocercal tail, with the lower lobe larger than the upper, and the body shapes range from streamlined swimmers to flattened bottom dwellers. Their mouths were ringed with small oral plates, and they probably fed on small particles and soft prey.

The anaspids (*Birkenia*, *Pharyngolepis*, *Jamoytius*) of the Silurian and Devonian were small, laterally compressed, with fine scales, a row of gill openings, a hypocercal tail and paired ventrolateral fin folds or spines; some authors have related them to lampreys. The thelodonts were covered by tiny scales with dentine crowns; some were flattened, others fork-tailed and deep-bodied. Their isolated scales are common microfossils in Silurian and Devonian rocks, and the internal anatomy of the group is poorly known.

### 45.4.3. Galeaspids: the Chinese Jawless Fishes

The *galeaspids* lived from the early Silurian to the Late Devonian, about 436–372 million years ago, and are known almost only from China (Zhejiang, Hunan, Hubei, Yunnan, Guangxi, Ningxia and Xinjiang) and northern Vietnam, a region that was then a separate continental block, the South China plate. More than 80 species have been described, mostly by the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing, beginning with the work of Liu Yuhai in the 1960s. They had a single solid head shield of acellular bone, often with elaborate horns and processes, and a large median opening on top of the head in front of the eyes, which led into the nasopharyngeal cavity and probably served for water intake. The internal skeleton of the head was calcified cartilage, preserving the shapes of brain cavities, nerves and blood vessels.

The galeaspids have shed light on two transitions. In *Shuyu* from the Silurian of Zhejiang, tomographic reconstruction of the braincase showed two separate olfactory organs and a hypophyseal duct that opened into the mouth, separated from the nasal openings (Gai et al. 2011). In cyclostomes the nasal sac and the adenohypophyseal duct form one nasohypophyseal complex that opens at the top of the head; in gnathostomes they are separated, the paired nostrils at the front and the hypophysis opening into the mouth. The separation is thought to have been a precondition for the forward growth of the neural crest cells that form the upper jaw between the two nasal placodes (the "heterotopy" theory of Kuratani; see [7.17.58](/7.17.58)). *Shuyu* shows the separation without jaws. The second transition concerns paired fins: *Tujiaaspis vivus* from the Silurian of Hunan has paired, continuous ventrolateral fin folds behind the head (Gai et al. 2022), consistent with the hypothesis that paired fins arose from continuous lateral fin folds that later divided into pectoral and pelvic fins ([7.17.46](/7.17.46)).

### 45.4.4. Osteostracans: the Sister Group of Jawed Vertebrates

The *osteostracans* (*Cephalaspis*, *Hemicyclaspis*, *Tremataspis*, *Zenaspis*) were bottom-dwelling fishes of the Silurian and Devonian, mostly 10–60 cm long, with a horseshoe-shaped head shield, dorsal eyes close together around a pineal opening, a median nasohypophyseal opening in front of them, and a ventral mouth. Three fields of small plates on the dorsal and lateral parts of the shield, connected to the inner ear by large canals, were sensory fields, probably mechanosensory or electroreceptive. The braincase, ossified with perichondral bone, preserves the canals of nerves and vessels in detail; Erik Stensiö's serial grinding of *Cephalaspis* skulls in the 1920s and 1930s, reconstructing the brain and cranial nerves of a 400-million-year-old fish, was one of the founding achievements of vertebrate palaeontology.

Osteostracans share with gnathostomes a series of characters absent in other jawless vertebrates: paired pectoral fins with an internal skeleton and muscles, attached behind the head shield; cellular bone and perichondral bone; a sclerotic ring of bone around the eye; a heterocercal (epicercal) tail with the axis bent upward into the upper lobe; and two dorsal fins in some species. They are therefore regarded as the sister group of the jawed vertebrates. They lacked pelvic fins, jaws, teeth and the gnathostome inner ear with three canals.

### 45.4.5. The Sequence of Characters

Combining the living and fossil groups, the steps from a lancelet-like chordate to a jawed vertebrate can be ordered as follows.

| Node | Characters acquired | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Vertebrata | Head with cranium, tripartite brain, paired eyes and olfactory organs, inner ear; neural crest and placodes; muscular pharynx with gill pouches; chambered heart; glomerular kidney; vertebral elements; cartilage; first genome duplication | Living cyclostomes and gnathostomes; Chengjiang and Burgess fossils |
| Stem gnathostomes above conodonts | Dermal skeleton of acellular bone and dentine (odontodes) | Arandaspids, heterostracans |
| Galeaspids + osteostracans + gnathostomes | Mineralized braincase; separation of the olfactory organs from the hypophyseal duct (at least in galeaspids) | *Shuyu* |
| Osteostracans + gnathostomes | Paired pectoral fins, cellular and perichondral bone, sclerotic ring, heterocercal tail | Osteostracans |
| Gnathostomata | Jaws and teeth, pelvic fins, three semicircular canals, myelin, horizontal septum, separate gonoducts, second genome duplication, RAG-based immunity | [7.17.46](/7.17.46) |

The sequence shows that the vertebrate body plan was assembled in steps over about 100 million years: the head first, then the dermal skeleton, then paired fins and an ossified internal skeleton, and only then jaws. Bone evolved as armour in the skin before it was used in the internal skeleton. Jaws were added to an animal that already had a complex head, paired fins and an active, bottom-feeding or swimming life.

## 45.5. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

The Chinese textbooks treat the living jawless vertebrates as the class Cyclostomata with two orders, Myxiniformes and Petromyzontiformes, and the representative animal *Lampetra morii*. Most of their description agrees with the current view; the differences are these.

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Class Cyclostomata within subphylum Vertebrata, "the most primitive living vertebrates"; also called Agnatha, Monorhina (single nostril) or Marsipobranchii (pouched gills) | Cyclostomata is a clade of two orders, a specialized sister group of the jawed vertebrates; "Agnatha" (all jawless vertebrates) is paraphyletic, because the extinct jawless groups are stem gnathostomes |
| Hagfishes sometimes placed as the sister group of all other vertebrates, the whole group then called Craniata | Hagfishes and lampreys are sister groups (molecular and morphological evidence); Vertebrata and Craniata are synonyms |
| Hagfishes "parasitic", sometimes found inside fishes | Hagfishes are scavengers and predators of invertebrates; they enter dead or dying fishes but are not parasites |
| Four pairs of barbels in hagfishes; hagfish hermaphrodites, sex determined by which part of the gonad develops | Three or four pairs depending on the genus; most hagfishes are of separate sexes as adults, with undifferentiated juvenile gonads |
| *Lampetra morii*, *L. japonica*, *L. reissneri* | Usually placed in *Lethenteron*; *L. japonica* is a synonym of *L. camtschaticum* |
| No fossils of the living orders | Fossil hagfishes (*Myxinikela*, *Tethymyxine*) and lampreys (*Priscomyzon*, *Mayomyzon*, *Mesomyzon* from China) are known |
| Ostracoderms as one group of early jawless fishes, with cyclostomes as their descendants, living in fresh water | Ostracoderms are a paraphyletic grade of stem gnathostomes; the earliest vertebrates were marine; the osteostracans are the sister group of the jawed vertebrates |
| The ammocoete as a model of the primitive vertebrate | The ammocoete is a derived larva of modern lampreys; Palaeozoic lampreys hatched with adult features |
| Ten pairs of cranial nerves in lampreys | Agrees; the numbering of cranial nerves across vertebrates is compared in [7.17.67](/7.17.67) |

The textbook characterization of lamprey anatomy (notochord with arcualia, incomplete cranium, branchial basket, oral disc and piston tongue, respiratory tube separate from the oesophagus, heart of three chambers, five-part brain without flexures, two semicircular canals, single gonad without ducts) remains accurate and is included in §45.3.3. Their account of the ostracoderm characters (no jaws, head armour, single nostril and pineal opening, two semicircular canals in osteostracans, paired pectoral flaps in cephalaspids) also agrees with current descriptions; what has changed is the interpretation, from ancestors of cyclostomes to a series of stem gnathostomes.

## Summary

- Vertebrates (Definition 45.1.1) are the chordates with a head: a cranium, a tripartite brain, paired eyes, nose and ears, a muscular pharynx with endodermal gills, a chambered heart, glomerular kidneys, cartilage and vertebral elements. Most of the head is built from the neural crest and the placodes; the new head hypothesis links these tissues to a shift from ciliary filter feeding to active muscular pumping and predation (§45.1.2).
- Mineralized tissues (bone, calcified cartilage, dentine, enamel and enameloid) are vertebrate innovations; the earliest bone was dermal armour made of odontodes; endochondral bone is found only in bony fishes and tetrapods.
- Two rounds of whole-genome duplication: 1R in all vertebrates, 2R in jawed vertebrates, an independent triplication in cyclostomes, 3R in teleosts; the number of *Hox* clusters follows this history (Example 45.1.1).
- The earliest vertebrates (*Haikouichthys*, *Myllokunmingia*, about 518 million years) had no bone. Bone, paired fins and jaws were acquired in that order along the gnathostome stem; the armoured "ostracoderms" are a paraphyletic grade.
- Cyclostomes (Definition 45.3.1) are monophyletic. Hagfishes (Definition 45.3.2): marine scavengers with slime glands, osmoconformers, reduced eyes and lateral line, direct development. Lampreys (Definition 45.3.3): oral disc and rasping tongue, separate respiratory tube, seven gill pouches, semelparous breeding in fresh water, a filter-feeding ammocoete larva and metamorphosis triggered by a fall of thyroid hormone (Example 45.3.1).
- Cyclostomes have an independently evolved adaptive immune system with variable lymphocyte receptors.
- Extinct jawless groups: conodonts (disputed), pteraspidomorphs, anaspids, thelodonts, the Chinese galeaspids (separation of nose and hypophysis in *Shuyu*, paired fin folds in *Tujiaaspis*) and osteostracans, the sister group of the jawed vertebrates.

## Questions

1. Which vertebrate characters are built from the neural crest or the placodes? Use your answer to explain the new head hypothesis.

   **Answer** From the neural crest: the cartilage and bone of the pharyngeal arches and of the front of the skull, the dentine-forming cells of teeth and odontodes, the connective tissue and dermis of the face, pigment cells, the sensory and autonomic neurons of the peripheral nervous system and their glia, the adrenal medulla and the walls of the aortic arch arteries. From the placodes: the olfactory epithelium, the lens, the inner ear, the neuromasts and electroreceptors of the lateral line, the sensory neurons of several cranial ganglia and the adenohypophysis. Together these are most of the head: its skeleton, its sense organs and much of its innervation. The new head hypothesis holds that the vertebrate head was added to a lancelet-like chordate when these tissues evolved, and that it was favoured by a change in life from ciliary filter feeding to muscular ventilation and active feeding on larger particles and prey, which required distance senses, a larger brain and a skeleton to support a muscular pharynx.
2. Why is "Agnatha" no longer used as a formal taxon, while "Cyclostomata" is?

   **Answer** Agnatha, "the jawless vertebrates", contains the cyclostomes and all extinct jawless groups. The extinct groups (pteraspidomorphs, anaspids, galeaspids, osteostracans and others) are successively closer to the jawed vertebrates than to cyclostomes; a group containing them and the cyclostomes but not the gnathostomes is paraphyletic and defined only by the absence of jaws, a primitive character. Cyclostomata, the hagfishes and lampreys, is supported as a clade by molecular data and by shared derived characters (velum, horny teeth on a lingual apparatus, branchial basket, variable lymphocyte receptors, periocular trunk muscles), so it is a natural group.
3. The kidney of vertebrates is well suited to life in fresh water. Why do most zoologists nevertheless think that vertebrates originated in the sea?

   **Answer** Because all other deuterostomes, including the closest relatives of vertebrates, are marine; because the earliest vertebrate fossils (Chengjiang, the Ordovician armoured fishes) come from marine sediments; and because hagfishes keep body fluids isosmotic with seawater, as marine invertebrates do, which is probably the ancestral condition. The ability of the glomerular kidney to excrete much water may have been an exaptation that originally served other functions (volume regulation, excretion of divalent ions) and later allowed the repeated invasion of fresh water.
4. A lamprey attached to a host cannot take water in through its mouth. How does it breathe, and what structure keeps food and respiratory water apart?

   **Answer** It ventilates tidally through the external gill openings: contraction of the muscles around the gill pouches and the elastic recoil of the branchial basket move water in and out of the same openings. In the adult the pharynx is divided into a dorsal oesophagus, through which food passes, and a ventral blind respiratory tube connected to the gill pouches; the velum at the entrance of the respiratory tube closes it from the mouth, so that water and food do not mix while the lamprey feeds.
5. How do the fossils *Shuyu* and *Tujiaaspis* bear on the origin of jaws and paired fins?

   **Answer** In *Shuyu*, a Silurian galeaspid, the paired olfactory organs are separate from the hypophyseal duct, which opened into the mouth. In cyclostomes these structures are combined in one median nasohypophyseal complex that blocks the forward growth of neural crest cells between the nasal placodes; in gnathostomes their separation allows crest cells to grow forward and form the upper jaw region. *Shuyu* shows that this separation occurred before jaws evolved, as a precondition. *Tujiaaspis*, another galeaspid, has paired continuous fin folds along the ventrolateral body, supporting the fin-fold hypothesis that paired fins originated as continuous lateral folds that were later divided into pectoral and pelvic fins.
6. Lampreys and hagfishes have neither antibodies nor T-cell receptors. How can they have adaptive immunity, and what does this imply about the evolution of the immune system?

   **Answer** Their lymphocytes express variable lymphocyte receptors built from leucine-rich repeat modules. In developing lymphocytes a cytidine deaminase inserts randomly chosen modules from a large flanking library into the germline gene by a gene conversion-like process, producing an enormous diversity of receptors; each lymphocyte expresses one, clones expand after antigen binding, and VLRB is secreted as an antibody. Three receptor classes in distinct lineages resemble the B and T cells of jawed vertebrates. The implication is that the last common ancestor of vertebrates already had lymphocyte lineages with B-like and T-like division of labour, and that the two lineages then evolved different molecular systems for generating diverse receptors (VLRs in cyclostomes, RAG-mediated V(D)J recombination of immunoglobulin-domain genes in gnathostomes): convergent solutions to the same problem.
7. (Open) The sea lamprey thrives in the Great Lakes but is endangered in parts of Europe. Using Example 45.3.1, explain which stages of the life cycle a control programme should target, and why the same reasoning helps conservation.

   **Answer** In a semelparous species with high fecundity, population size is very sensitive to survival in the early stages, where most mortality occurs: the egg-to-adult survival needed for replacement is only about $3 \times 10^{-5}$, so small changes in larval survival or in the number of females that reach spawning grounds change the population growth rate strongly. Control therefore targets the spawning and larval stages: barriers that stop adults from reaching spawning streams, lampricides that kill larvae in the streams, and trapping of migrating adults with pheromone lures; sterile-male releases reduce effective fecundity. Conservation in Europe uses the same logic in reverse: restoring access to spawning gravels (fish passes, dam removal) and protecting larval habitats in fine sediment has the greatest effect, because these stages limit recruitment.
