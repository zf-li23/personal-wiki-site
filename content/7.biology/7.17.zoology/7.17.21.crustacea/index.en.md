Crustaceans are the arthropods of the water. About 70 000 species have been described, from interstitial copepods a tenth of a millimeter long to the Japanese spider crab with a leg span of nearly 4 m, and they dominate the zooplankton of the oceans and of fresh water, the sea floor, the intertidal zone and many caves; a few groups live on land. Their morphological disparity exceeds that of any other arthropod subphylum, including the insects: barnacles, which Darwin spent eight years describing, look nothing like shrimps, and some parasitic crustaceans are reduced to a sac of gonads.

The chapter has four parts. First comes the body plan shared by crustaceans, with the crayfish and the prawn as representative animals: two pairs of antennae, biramous limbs, the carapace, the nauplius larva, and the endocrine control of molting and growth. Then the main lineages are described in the order of the classification of BG 4e. Malacostraca, the largest class with the crabs, shrimps, krill, amphipods and isopods, receives most space, including a full account of the serial homology of the crayfish appendages. The chapter closes with the place of crustaceans in marine food webs and the phylogenetic result that the insects arose from within them, so that Crustacea in the traditional sense is a **paraphyletic group**, the crustacean part of the **Pancrustacea** ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.9.2)).

## 21.1. The Crustacean Body Plan

### 21.1.1. Head, Trunk and Appendages

**Definition 21.1.1 (Crustacea)** **Crustacea** is the traditional subphylum of mandibulate arthropods with a head of the ocular region and five appendage-bearing segments carrying two pairs of antennae (antennules and antennae), the mandibles and two pairs of maxillae (maxillules and maxillae); usually biramous trunk limbs; a head shield or carapace; gas exchange by gills or through the body surface; excretion by antennal or maxillary glands; and a nauplius larva at some stage of development. It is paraphyletic with respect to the hexapods.

The two pairs of antennae are the simplest diagnostic character: no other living arthropods have them. In the head homologies of [7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.6), the antennules are deutocerebral and correspond to the antennae of insects and myriapods and to the chelicerae of chelicerates, and the antennae are tritocerebral and correspond to the pedipalps of chelicerates and to the appendage-less intercalary segment of insects. Behind the head, the trunk is usually divided into a thorax and an abdomen, whose segment numbers differ between classes; in remipedes the trunk is uniform and in ostracods it is greatly reduced. One to three anterior thoracic segments may be fused with the head, their limbs then becoming feeding limbs called maxillipeds.

Crustacean limbs show the arthropod limb in its most complete form, with a protopod bearing endites and exites and two rami, the endopod and the exopod ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.3.4)). Flattened, leaflike limbs with setose margins, as in branchiopods, serve for swimming, filtering and gas exchange at once; in malacostracans the limbs along the body are specialized for different tasks, from sensing to walking and copulation, while remaining **serial homologues** of one another (Section 21.4.3).

**Definition 21.1.2 (Carapace)** The **carapace** of crustaceans is a fold of the body wall that grows backward from the posterior margin of the head and covers part or all of the thorax, and sometimes the whole body; its inner wall is thin and can serve for gas exchange, and the space it encloses at the sides forms the gill chambers in decapods.

The carapace takes many forms: a short head shield in cephalocarids, a bivalved shell enclosing the whole body in ostracods, clam shrimps and water fleas, the mantle of barnacles that secretes calcareous plates, and in decapods a single hard shield fused dorsally with all the thoracic segments. It has been reduced or lost in several lineages, such as fairy shrimps, amphipods and isopods.

### 21.1.2. Internal Organization

Internal anatomy is described here for a decapod such as the prawn or the crayfish.

- Gut: the foregut, lined with cuticle, forms a large cardiac stomach with a gastric mill of calcified teeth that grinds food, and a pyloric stomach with setal filters that sort fine particles into the midgut gland (hepatopancreas) and coarse ones into the intestine. The hepatopancreas, a mass of blind tubules, secretes digestive enzymes, absorbs nutrients and stores reserves.
- Gas exchange: decapods have up to four series of gills on each side, attached to the coxae (podobranchs), to the arthrodial membranes (arthrobranchs) and to the body wall above the legs (pleurobranchs), enclosed in a gill chamber under the carapace. The exopod of the second maxilla, the scaphognathite, beats to draw water through the chamber from behind and below and to expel it forward. Small crustaceans exchange gases through the body surface and the inner wall of the carapace.
- Circulation: the heart is a compact sac in a pericardial sinus with three pairs of ostia; arteries supply the head, the gut and the limbs, and in decapods the arterial system is so extensive that the circulation is sometimes called partly closed; the hemolymph carries hemocyanin, or hemoglobin in some branchiopods and copepods.
- Excretion and osmoregulation: a pair of antennal glands (green glands) open at the bases of the second antennae, maxillary glands in many other crustaceans and in larvae ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.5.2)). Each has a coelomic end sac, where hemolymph is filtered, and a tubule that modifies the filtrate. Nitrogen is excreted mainly as ammonia across the gills, so the glands serve chiefly for ion and water balance, which is important in freshwater crustaceans ([7.14.21](/7.14.21#sec-21.5)).
- Nervous system and sense organs: a brain, circumesophageal connectives and a ventral nerve cord, with progressive fusion of ganglia that culminates in crabs, where all thoracic ganglia form one mass. The compound eyes are usually on movable stalks, and many decapods have reflecting superposition eyes with square facets. A statocyst at the base of each antennule contains sand grains or secreted statoliths; it is lined with cuticle and refilled after each molt, which allowed classic experiments in which shrimps given iron filings oriented to a magnet. The larvae, and the adults of some groups, have a median naupliar eye of three or four cups.
- Color change: **chromatophores** in the hypodermis concentrate or disperse pigment under the control of eyestalk hormones; the pigment astaxanthin bound to the protein crustacyanin gives the blue-green color of living lobsters and prawns, and its release when the protein is denatured by cooking turns them red.

### 21.1.3. Reproduction and Larvae

Most crustaceans have separate sexes; barnacles, cephalocarids and remipedes are hermaphrodites, and many branchiopods and ostracods reproduce by **parthenogenesis** at least part of the time. Fertilization is internal or occurs as the eggs are laid, and most crustaceans brood their eggs, attached to the pleopods in decapods, in a brood pouch in peracarids and water fleas, or in egg sacs in copepods.

The characteristic larva is the **nauplius** ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)), with an unsegmented body, a median eye and three pairs of appendages, the antennules, the antennae and the mandibles, used for swimming. With successive molts segments and limbs are added from a posterior growth zone (anamorphic development). In many malacostracans the nauplius stage is passed in the egg and the hatchling is a later larva:

| Group | Larval sequence |
|---|---|
| Penaeid prawns (Dendrobranchiata) | nauplius → protozoea → zoea (mysis) → postlarva |
| Crabs (Brachyura) | zoea (swimming with thoracic exopods, carapace spines) → megalopa (abdomen extended, pleopods for swimming) → juvenile crab |
| Spiny lobsters (Achelata) | phyllosoma, a flat, transparent, leaf-shaped larva that drifts for months |
| Barnacles (Cirripedia) | nauplius → cypris (bivalved, nonfeeding, settles head first) |
| Crayfish, isopods, amphipods | direct: hatch as juveniles |

### 21.1.4. Molting and Its Control

Crustaceans molt throughout life, and the cycle of intermolt, premolt, ecdysis and postmolt ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.4)) involves large movements of calcium, because the cuticle of most crustaceans is mineralized. In premolt, calcium is resorbed from the old cuticle and stored, in crayfish in a pair of gastroliths on the stomach wall ([7.14.36](/7.14.36#sec-36.10.3)); in postmolt the animal takes up water to expand and hardens the new cuticle with stored and absorbed calcium within hours to days. Molting is triggered and controlled by hormones (see the figure below).

**Ecdysone** is secreted by the Y-organs, a pair of glands in the anterior thorax (near the mandibles in decapods), the counterparts of the prothoracic glands of insects ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)). The Y-organs are held in check by molt-inhibiting hormone (MIH), a neuropeptide made by neurosecretory cells of the X-organ in the eyestalk and released from the sinus gland, a neurohemal organ also in the eyestalk. MIH belongs to the same family as the crustacean hyperglycemic hormone (CHH), which mobilizes glucose and also affects molting. When MIH release falls, in response to growth, limb loss, temperature or photoperiod, the Y-organs secrete ecdysone, converted in the tissues to 20-hydroxyecdysone, and premolt begins. The sinus gland also releases vitellogenesis-inhibiting hormone (VIH), which delays ovarian maturation. Removal of the eyestalks therefore induces molting and ovarian maturation, and unilateral eyestalk ablation has long been used to induce spawning in shrimp aquaculture, a practice now being replaced on welfare grounds by hormonal and genetic methods.

Neurosecretory cells of the X-organ in the eyestalk project to the sinus gland, which releases molt-inhibiting hormone (MIH) into the hemolymph; MIH suppresses the Y-organs, which secrete ecdysone, converted to the active 20-hydroxyecdysone. ### 21.1.5. Discontinuous Growth

Because the cuticle is hard between molts, body size increases in steps: it stays constant during the intermolt and jumps at each molt, as the animal expands its new cuticle by taking up water and then replaces the water by tissue during the following intermolt. Growth in a crustacean is therefore described by two quantities, the molt increment (how much the animal grows at a molt) and the intermolt period (how often it molts).

A simple model reproduces the typical pattern (see the figure below). Brooks (1886) proposed that each molt multiplies body length by a constant ratio, $L_{n+1} = b L_n$ with $b > 1$, the crustacean counterpart of **Dyar's rule** for insect head widths ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)). Measured molt increments usually fall with size, and are better described by a linear relation between post-molt and pre-molt length, the Hiatt growth diagram,

$$
L_{n+1} = a + b L_n ,
$$
in which the relative increment $(L_{n+1} - L_n)/L_n = a/L_n + b - 1$ declines toward $b - 1$ in large animals. At the same time the intermolt period lengthens with size, $T_n = c\,L_n^{k}$. Together these give an apparently smooth, decelerating growth curve composed of ever larger steps separated by ever longer intervals, as in the figure. In some crustaceans, such as many crabs, growth ends with a terminal molt at puberty; in others, such as lobsters and crayfish, molting continues for life with very long intermolt periods in large adults.

![A model of discontinuous crustacean growth (schematic, illustrative parameters). Left: body length against age; length is constant between molts and jumps at each molt (blue), and the dashed line joins the post-molt sizes. Molt increments follow a Hiatt diagram (right), post-molt length $L' = a + bL$ with $a = 1.2$ mm and $b = 1.05$, so the relative increment falls from over 50 % in the smallest juveniles to about 12 % at a length of 16 mm, while the intermolt period lengthens with size ($T = cL^{k}$, $c = 3$ d, $k = 1.1$). The grey dashed line in the Hiatt diagram is $L' = L$ (no growth). Brooks' rule (constant ratio, $a = 0$) would give geometric growth in size.](./plots/molt-growth.py)

## 21.3. Oligostraca, Multicrustacea (Except Malacostraca) and Allotriocarida

### 21.3.1. Ostracods, Fish Lice and Tongue Worms

Ostracods (seed shrimps, about 13 000 living species and nearly 65 000 fossil ones) are small crustaceans, mostly 0.1–2 mm, whose whole body is enclosed in a bivalved carapace hinged dorsally and closed by an adductor muscle; the trunk is reduced to at most three pairs of limbs, and they swim and crawl mainly with the antennae. They live in every aquatic habitat and in moist soil. Their calcified valves, molted at each instar, make them one of the best fossil groups for dating rocks and reconstructing past salinity and temperature. Some marine ostracods (*Vargula*) produce a blue light with luciferin and luciferase, used in courtship displays.

Fish lice (Branchiura, such as *Argulus*) are ectoparasites of fish with a flattened carapace and the first maxillae transformed into suckers. Tongue worms (Pentastomida, about 140 species) are wormlike parasites of the lungs and nasal passages of vertebrates, mainly reptiles; they have only two pairs of hooked limbs near the mouth and are so modified that they were long treated as a separate phylum. Their sperm structure and molecular data place them close to the fish lice in a clade Ichthyostraca, and larvae resembling them are known from the late Cambrian. *Linguatula serrata*, a parasite of dogs, and *Armillifer* species of snakes occasionally infect humans.

### 21.3.2. Copepods

**Definition 21.3.1 (Copepoda)** **Copepoda** is a class of small crustaceans, usually 0.5–5 mm long, with a cylindrical body divided into a prosome and a narrow urosome, a single median naupliar eye, no compound eyes and no carapace, long antennules used for swimming (and in males for clasping), four or five pairs of biramous swimming legs joined across the midline by a coupling plate, and eggs usually carried in one or two egg sacs.

With about 14 000 described species, copepods are the most abundant multicellular animals in the sea and probably the most numerous animals on Earth after nematodes. Calanoid copepods are the principal grazers of phytoplankton and the main food of larval fish, herring and many baleen whales: in the Yellow Sea and the East China Sea *Calanus sinicus* is the dominant species, and in the North Atlantic *C. finmarchicus*. Many copepods store wax esters for the winter and migrate daily between deep water by day and surface water by night, the largest daily movement of biomass on the planet. Cyclopoids such as *Cyclops* are common in fresh water and serve as intermediate hosts of the guinea worm ([7.17.17](/7.17.17#sec-17.5.3)) and of fish tapeworms. Parasitic copepods, such as the salmon louse *Lepeophtheirus salmonis* in salmon farms, are highly modified.

### 21.3.3. Barnacles and Rhizocephalans

**Definition 21.3.2 (Cirripedia)** **Cirripedia** is the group of thecostracan crustaceans that settle as cypris larvae on their head, attached by the antennules, and metamorphose into sessile adults enclosed in a mantle (the carapace) that in most species secretes calcareous plates; the six pairs of thoracic limbs are long, curled, biramous cirri used to filter food, and the abdomen is lost. It includes the barnacles (Thoracica), the burrowing barnacles (Acrothoracica) and the parasitic Rhizocephala.

The barnacle has been described as a shrimp standing on its head and kicking food into its mouth with its legs. Acorn barnacles (*Balanus*, *Amphibalanus*, *Chthamalus*) have a ring of fused wall plates and an operculum of movable plates that closes at low tide; goose barnacles (*Lepas*, *Pollicipes*) hang on a fleshy stalk. Most barnacles are hermaphrodites that cross-fertilize with a penis that can be many times the length of the body; in some species small dwarf males live on the hermaphrodites, as Darwin discovered. Barnacles dominate rocky shores, where their zonation and competition were the subject of classic experiments in ecology (Connell 1961), and their cement, which works under water, and their fouling of ship hulls, which increases drag and fuel use, are of practical interest.

Rhizocephalans, such as *Sacculina carcini* on shore crabs, show how far parasitism can transform a crustacean. The female cyprid settles on a crab and injects a small mass of cells into its hemocoel, which grows a root system (the interna) through the host's body; later a sac (the externa) containing the ovary emerges under the crab's abdomen, where the crab's own egg mass would be. The host stops molting, is castrated, and if male, is feminized and broods the parasite's larvae as if they were its own. Only the nauplius and cypris larvae reveal that the parasite is a crustacean.

### 21.3.4. Branchiopods

**Definition 21.3.3 (Branchiopoda)** **Branchiopoda** is a class of mostly freshwater crustaceans with numerous flattened, leaflike thoracic limbs (phyllopods) used for swimming, filter feeding and gas exchange, reduced second maxillae and a limbless abdomen; it comprises the fairy shrimps and brine shrimps (Anostraca), the tadpole shrimps (Notostraca) and the "bivalved" Diplostraca (clam shrimps and water fleas).

Branchiopods are characteristic of temporary pools and saline lakes without fish. Their eggs survive drying and freezing for years in a state of cryptobiosis and hatch when the pool fills again. The brine shrimp *Artemia* lives in salt lakes at salinities up to saturation, and its dried cysts, which hatch within a day in seawater, are the standard live food in the rearing of fish and shrimp larvae. Tadpole shrimps (*Triops*) have a broad shield-like carapace and up to 70 pairs of limbs and have changed little in form since the Mesozoic; they are common in rice fields.

Water fleas (Cladocera, such as *Daphnia*) are 0.5–5 mm long, with a bivalved carapace that leaves the head free and large biramous second antennae for swimming. They reproduce by cyclic parthenogenesis: in favorable conditions females produce diploid eggs that develop without fertilization into more females, so that populations grow rapidly; when food declines, temperature falls or days shorten, males and sexual eggs are produced, and the fertilized eggs are enclosed in a resistant case (ephippium) that survives the winter. *Daphnia* also shows striking phenotypic plasticity: in water low in oxygen it makes more hemoglobin and turns red, and in the presence of chemical cues from predators it grows helmets or neck teeth. Water fleas are key grazers in lakes, important food for fish, and **model organisms** for ecotoxicology and ecological genomics.

### 21.3.5. Cephalocarids and Remipedes

Two small classes, discovered only in the second half of the twentieth century, have played a large role in ideas about crustacean evolution. Cephalocarids (horseshoe shrimps, about 12 species), first found in 1955 in marine sediments off New England, are blind, 2–4 mm long, with a horseshoe-shaped head shield and a series of similar thoracic limbs that combine walking, swimming and filtering; their simple, uniform limbs made them a candidate for the most "primitive" living crustacean. Remipedes (about 30 species), first found in 1979 in a marine cave in the Bahamas, are blind, centipede-like swimmers up to 4.5 cm long that live in anchialine caves with layers of fresh and salt water; they have 16 to over 40 trunk segments with similar biramous swimming limbs, and their first maxillae carry fangs through which a venom is injected into prey.

Phylogenomic analyses place remipedes, possibly together with cephalocarids, as the closest living relatives of the hexapods. If this is right, the hexapods arose from an animal with a brain organization and many-segmented trunk like those of remipedes, which have well-developed olfactory centers and a brain more similar to that of insects than to that of most crustaceans, a remarkable correspondence for an animal that lives in darkness in caves.

## 21.4. Malacostraca

### 21.4.1. The Malacostracan Body Plan

**Definition 21.4.1 (Malacostraca)** **Malacostraca** is the largest class of crustaceans, with a body of usually 19 segments and a telson: a head of the ocular region and five segments, a thorax (pereon) of eight segments and an abdomen (pleon) of six segments (seven in Leptostraca); the thoracic limbs (pereiopods, of which the anterior ones may be maxillipeds) are mostly for walking or grasping, the first five abdominal limbs (pleopods) for swimming, ventilation, brooding or copulation, and the sixth pair (uropods) forms with the telson a tail fan; the female gonopores open on the sixth and the male on the eighth thoracic segment.

About 40 000 species belong to the Malacostraca, including all large crustaceans. Their constant number of segments and the regular division of labor among the limbs make them the clearest example of tagmosis and serial homology among crustaceans. Many have a caridoid escape reaction: a rapid flexion of the muscular abdomen, triggered by giant neurons in the ventral nerve cord, that drives the animal backward with the tail fan, the reaction that makes a startled crayfish or shrimp shoot away.

The main orders are grouped into three subclasses:

| Subclass, superorder | Orders | Distinctive features |
|---|---|---|
| Phyllocarida | Leptostraca | bivalved carapace with an adductor muscle; seven abdominal segments; leaflike thoracic limbs |
| Hoplocarida | Stomatopoda (mantis shrimps) | second maxillipeds enlarged as raptorial claws; complex compound eyes |
| Eumalacostraca: Eucarida | Euphausiacea (krill), Decapoda | carapace fused with all thoracic segments; stalked eyes |
| Eumalacostraca: Peracarida | Amphipoda, Isopoda, Mysida, Cumacea, Tanaidacea and others | females with a brood pouch (marsupium) formed by plates (oostegites) on the thoracic limbs; direct development |
| Eumalacostraca: Syncarida | Bathynellacea, Anaspidacea | no carapace; groundwater and Tasmanian streams |

### 21.4.2. Mantis Shrimps and Krill

Mantis shrimps (Stomatopoda), about 500 species of shallow tropical and subtropical seas, are ambush predators that strike with their raptorial second maxillipeds. "Spearers" impale fish and shrimps with spiny dactyls, while "smashers" such as *Odontodactylus scyllarus* break snail shells and crab carapaces with a club, accelerating it with a spring-and-latch mechanism to over 20 m/s in less than a millisecond; the strike is so fast that it produces cavitation bubbles whose collapse adds a second impact (Patek et al. 2004). Their compound eyes have a midband of specialized ommatidia with twelve classes of color photoreceptors and receptors for circularly polarized light.

**Definition 21.4.2 (Euphausiacea)** **Euphausiacea** (krill) is an order of pelagic, shrimplike eucarid crustaceans, 1–6 cm long, whose carapace does not enclose the gills, which are exposed as podobranchs on the thoracic limbs; there are no maxillipeds, the thoracic limbs bear setal baskets for filter feeding, and most species have photophores, light organs on the eyestalks, thorax and abdomen.

The Antarctic krill *Euphausia superba* forms swarms with densities of over 10 000 individuals per cubic meter and has a total biomass estimated at several hundred million tonnes (about 380 million in one estimate; Atkinson et al. 2009), one of the largest of any animal species. It is the central link of the Southern Ocean food web, between phytoplankton and the baleen whales, seals, penguins, fish and squid that eat it, and it is also the target of a growing fishery. Its decline in parts of its range with the loss of sea ice is one of the main concerns of Antarctic ecology.

### 21.4.3. Decapoda and the Serial Appendages of the Crayfish

**Definition 21.4.3 (Decapoda)** **Decapoda** is the order of eucarid malacostracans with a carapace fused to all eight thoracic segments that encloses lateral gill chambers, three pairs of maxillipeds, and five pairs of pereiopods (walking legs), of which the first one to three pairs are often chelate; it includes the prawns, shrimps, lobsters, crayfish, hermit crabs and crabs.

With about 15 000 species, decapods include most of the crustaceans used as food. The crayfish (or the prawn, the representative animal of Chinese textbooks) shows the malacostracan body plan clearly: 19 pairs of appendages, all modifications of one biramous limb, each adapted to a function (see the figure below).

The prawn of Chinese textbooks, *Fenneropenaeus chinensis* (formerly *Penaeus orientalis*), differs in being a swimmer: its body is laterally compressed, its pleopods are large swimming paddles, its first three pairs of pereiopods all carry small chelae, the male's first pleopods are joined into a copulatory organ (petasma) and the female has a sperm receptacle (thelycum) between the last legs. It is a dendrobranchiate, which releases its eggs into the water to hatch as nauplii, and it migrates seasonally between the Yellow Sea and the Bohai Sea to spawn.

Decapods are divided into two suborders:

- Dendrobranchiata, the penaeid prawns, with branched (dendrobranchiate) gills, eggs released into the water, and a nauplius larva.
- Pleocyemata, all other decapods, which brood their eggs on the pleopods and hatch at a later stage. They include the shrimps (Caridea, such as the freshwater *Macrobrachium nipponense*, the oriental river prawn of China); the lobsters and crayfish (Astacidea; the red swamp crayfish *Procambarus clarkii*, introduced into China in the 1920s–1930s, is now both a major aquaculture species and an invasive one); the spiny and slipper lobsters (Achelata, with phyllosoma larvae); the Anomura (hermit crabs, porcelain crabs, king crabs and the coconut crab *Birgus latro*); and the true crabs (Brachyura).

In crabs the abdomen is short, flattened and folded tightly under the cephalothorax, and the carapace is broad; the center of mass lies over the legs, which allows rapid sideways walking. Brachyurans include the Chinese mitten crab *Eriocheir sinensis*, which lives in rivers and migrates to estuaries to breed and has invaded rivers in Europe and North America, and the swimming crab *Portunus trituberculatus*, whose last legs are flattened paddles. Such a crablike body has evolved at least five times independently among decapods, in brachyurans and several times among anomurans (porcelain crabs, king crabs), a process called carcinization, which shows how strongly a body plan can be molded by convergent selection for a compact, protected, agile form.

### 21.4.4. Amphipods and Isopods

**Definition 21.4.4 (Peracarida)** Peracarida is the superorder of malacostracans in which the female carries the eggs and young in a brood pouch (marsupium) formed by plates (oostegites) on the bases of the thoracic limbs, and the young hatch as juveniles (mancas); its largest orders are the **Amphipoda**, laterally compressed peracarids without a carapace and with sessile eyes, whose pleon bears three pairs of swimming pleopods and three pairs of stiff uropods for jumping, and the **Isopoda**, dorsoventrally flattened peracarids without a carapace, whose pereiopods are all similar and whose pleopods serve for gas exchange.

Amphipods (about 10 000 species) are abundant in the sea and in fresh water: *Gammarus* in streams, and an endemic radiation of over 350 species in Lake Baikal. Beach hoppers (Talitridae) live in the drift line, and some have moved into the leaf litter of forests. The deep-sea *Hirondellea* lives in the deepest ocean trenches. Isopods (about 11 000 species) range from the deep-sea *Bathynomus*, up to 50 cm long, through the wood-boring gribble *Limnoria* and fish parasites such as *Cymothoa exigua*, which replaces the tongue of its host fish, to the woodlice (Oniscidea, about 3 700 species), the most successful terrestrial crustaceans ([7.17.20](/7.17.20#sec-20.2.3)).

## 21.5. Crustaceans and People

Crustaceans matter to people in many ways. They are central to aquatic food webs, as grazers (copepods, krill, water fleas), filter feeders (barnacles), predators and scavengers (decapods, amphipods, isopods) and decomposers. Shrimps, prawns, crabs, lobsters, crayfish and krill support major fisheries and aquaculture, China being by far the largest producer. Some are pests or invaders: wood-boring gribbles and fouling barnacles damage structures and ships, and the mitten crab and the red swamp crayfish have spread far beyond their native ranges. A few are vectors or hosts of disease, such as copepods as intermediate hosts of guinea worm and tapeworms and freshwater crabs as hosts of lung flukes ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)). Many shellfish allergies are caused by crustacean tropomyosin.

## 21.6. Crustacea, Pancrustacea and the Chinese Textbooks

The traditional Crustacea is a paraphyletic group: the hexapods arose from within it, probably from a lineage related to the remipedes (Section 21.3.5). The terrestrial insects are therefore descendants of crustaceans, and characters shared by insects and crustaceans, such as ommatidia with four cone cells, homologous neuroblasts and the details of the nervous system, are inherited from their common pancrustacean ancestor ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.9.2)). For practical purposes the name Crustacea remains in use, as Reptilia does for the non-avian reptiles, but it designates a grade.

Chinese textbooks divide the Crustacea into six classes, Remipedia, Cephalocarida, Branchiopoda, Ostracoda, Maxillopoda and Malacostraca, with the tongue worms as a subclass of Maxillopoda or as a separate class, or into about ten classes including Copepoda and Cirripedia. The correspondence with the modern classification is:

| Chinese textbook | Modern treatment |
|---|---|
| Malacostraca, with Decapoda (prawn, crayfish, crabs), Stomatopoda, Isopoda, Amphipoda, Euphausiacea | unchanged; Malacostraca in Multicrustacea |
| Maxillopoda (copepods, barnacles, fish lice, mystacocarids; tongue worms included or separate) | abandoned as polyphyletic: copepods and thecostracans (barnacles) in Multicrustacea, fish lice, mystacocarids and tongue worms in Oligostraca |
| Ostracoda | Oligostraca |
| Branchiopoda | Allotriocarida |
| Remipedia, Cephalocarida (as the most primitive crustaceans) | Allotriocarida, the closest relatives of the hexapods; their simple limbs are not evidence of a basal position |
| Pentastomida as an obscure group or phylum | a class of crustaceans, sister to fish lice |
| Crustacea as a subphylum distinct from Hexapoda | Crustacea is paraphyletic; Pancrustacea = crustacean lineages + Hexapoda |

The textbook account of the prawn, including its appendages, the gills and gill chamber, the gastric mill and hepatopancreas, the antennal (green) glands, ammonia excretion through the gills, the larval sequence from nauplius to postlarva, and the X-organ–sinus gland and Y-organ control of molting and reproduction, is in agreement with the modern account above.

## Questions

1. Using the Hiatt relation $L' = 1.2 + 1.05L$ (mm), compute the length after three molts of a juvenile 5 mm long, and the relative increment at the first and third molts. Why do relative increments decline?

   **Answer** $L_1 = 1.2 + 5.25 = 6.45$ mm, $L_2 = 1.2 + 6.77 = 7.97$ mm, $L_3 = 1.2 + 8.37 = 9.57$ mm. The relative increments are $(6.45 - 5)/5 = 29\,\%$ at the first molt and $(9.57 - 7.97)/7.97 = 20\,\%$ at the third. The relation has a positive intercept, so the relative increment $a/L + b - 1$ falls toward $b - 1 = 5\,\%$ as $L$ increases. Biologically, the size of the jump is limited by the water a molting animal can take up and by the tissue it can build during the intermolt; larger animals invest more in reproduction and molt less often, and the absolute amount of new cuticle needed grows faster than the gain in length.

2. A crab larva is captured in a plankton sample. Which characters would identify it as a zoea or a megalopa, and why are these larval forms considered adaptations to dispersal?

   **Answer** A zoea has a large carapace, often with long dorsal and rostral spines, swims with the setose exopods of its maxillipeds and has a slender, extended abdomen without functional pleopods; its compound eyes are sessile or nearly so. A megalopa has a crablike carapace without the long spines, functional chelipeds and walking legs, and an extended abdomen with setose pleopods used for swimming; it is the transitional stage that settles to the bottom. Both are planktonic, nonbenthic stages that are carried by currents for weeks, spreading the species far from the adults and allowing colonization of new habitats; the spines of the zoea reduce sinking and deter small fish predators.

3. Explain why the antennule of a crayfish, the antenna of a grasshopper and the chelicera of a spider are homologous, while the crayfish antenna has no homologue among the appendages of the grasshopper.

   **Answer** The crayfish antennule, the grasshopper antenna and the spider chelicera are all on the deutocerebral segment, innervated from the deutocerebrum and lying on the first segment without Hox gene expression; they are the same appendage specialized differently. The crayfish antenna is on the tritocerebral segment; in insects this segment (the intercalary segment) persists in the embryo and its neuromere forms the tritocerebrum, but it has no appendage, so the grasshopper has no counterpart of the crayfish antenna ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.6)).

4. *Sacculina* has no segments, appendages or gut as an adult. What evidence shows that it is a crustacean, and more precisely a relative of barnacles?

   **Answer** Its life cycle includes a typical crustacean nauplius with three pairs of limbs and a naupliar eye, and a cypris larva identical in structure to that of barnacles, which settles on its antennules on the host; the female cyprid then injects the cells that become the internal root system. Molecular phylogenies confirm that rhizocephalans are nested within Thecostraca close to the barnacles. The adult's loss of all crustacean features is a consequence of its endoparasitic life, in which nutrients are absorbed through the roots and only reproduction remains.

5. (Open question) The crablike body form evolved several times independently among decapods. Propose a hypothesis for why this form is favored and how one might test it.

   **Answer** Hypothesis: folding the abdomen under the cephalothorax and broadening the carapace protects the soft abdomen, lowers and centers the body mass over the legs, and improves stability and agility in walking and defense; this is favored in benthic decapods that walk rather than swim, in habitats with many crushing predators. Tests: compare, within anomuran clades containing both crablike and non-crablike species, the habitats, predation pressure and locomotor performance (speed, turning, resistance to overturning measured in flow tanks); use phylogenetic comparative methods to test whether transitions to the crab form are correlated with shifts to benthic walking or to predator-rich habitats; and examine whether the developmental pathways of abdomen folding are similar in different lineages, which would indicate a predisposition (developmental bias) as well as selection.
