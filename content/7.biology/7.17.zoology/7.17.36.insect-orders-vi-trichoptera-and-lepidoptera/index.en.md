The *Amphiesmenoptera*, the caddisflies (**Trichoptera**) and the moths and butterflies (**Lepidoptera**), are sister orders whose common ancestor lived in the Triassic. They share hairy wings, in which the hairs became scales in Lepidoptera; a larval labium and hypopharynx fused into a single lobe carrying at its tip the opening of the silk glands (the spinneret); heterogametic females (ZW or Z0 sex determination, unlike the XY males of most insects); and details of wing venation and of the larval head. Silk, produced by modified labial glands, is the thread of their evolution: caddisfly larvae use it under water to build nets and cases, caterpillars to make shelters, safety lines and cocoons, and people have bred one moth, the silkworm, for at least five thousand years for its cocoons.

Lepidoptera, with about 157,000 described species and probably several hundred thousand in all, are one of the four great orders. They are the most important plant-feeding insects: their caterpillars are the main herbivores on many plants and include most of the worst crop pests. Butterflies, the best-known insects, are a single lineage nested among the moths. This chapter treats the caddisflies and then the Lepidoptera, ending with the silkworm. Scale colours are introduced in [7.17.23](/7.17.23), tympanal organs and bat–moth interactions in [7.17.27](/7.17.27), mimicry and warning colour in [7.17.39](/7.17.39), and insect–plant interactions in [7.17.37](/7.17.37).

## 36.1. Trichoptera: Caddisflies

### 36.1.1. Characters

**Definition 36.1.1 (Trichoptera)** The **Trichoptera** (caddisflies) are holometabolous insects whose adults resemble small moths, with two pairs of membranous wings covered with hairs (not scales) and held roof-like over the abdomen at rest, long setaceous antennae, reduced mouthparts with vestigial mandibles but well-developed maxillary and labial palps and a short haustellum for taking up liquids, and long legs with tibial spurs; the larvae are aquatic, campodeiform or eruciform, with a pair of anal prolegs bearing hooks, and use silk from the labial glands to build retreats, nets or portable cases; the pupa is decticous and exarate; about 16,000 species.

Adult caddisflies are mostly nocturnal, live from days to a few weeks and drink water and nectar; they are often seen in swarms over streams and come to lights. The pharate adult cuts its way out of the pupal case with its mandibles, swims to the surface or the shore, and molts there.

### 36.1.2. Larvae, Silk and Cases

Caddisfly larvae occur in almost every freshwater habitat, most abundantly in cool, running water, and are, with mayflies and stoneflies, the EPT taxa used to assess water quality ([7.17.31](/7.17.31)). A few species live in damp moss on land, and one family, the Chathamiidae of New Zealand and Australia, lives in marine rock pools. They breathe through the body surface or filamentous tracheal gills, and case-building larvae ventilate the case by undulating the abdomen, drawing a current through it.

Their silk is an underwater adhesive. The heavy-chain fibroin of caddisflies contains runs of phosphorylated serines that bind calcium ions; the calcium bridges make the fibre stick and set in water (Stewart and Wang 2010). The larvae use it in three ways that define the main groups:

| Group | Larval habit | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Annulipalpia (retreat-makers) | Fixed shelters of silk and debris, usually with a capture net in front; filter feeders and collectors | Hydropsychidae (net-spinning caddisflies, with nets whose mesh is finer in slower water), Philopotamidae (finger-net caddisflies, with very fine sac-like nets), Polycentropodidae (trumpet nets for drifting prey), Psychomyiidae, Stenopsychidae (*Stenopsyche*, large predatory net-spinners of East Asian mountain streams) |
| Free-living and "cocoon-making" families | Without cases until the last instar or with simple shelters | Rhyacophilidae (free-living predators, with gills; build a cocoon only for pupation); Glossosomatidae (saddle-case makers, grazing on stones); Hydroptilidae (micro-caddisflies, 2–4 mm, free-living in early instars and building purse-like cases in the last) |
| Integripalpia (portable-case makers) | Tubular portable cases of sand, stones or plant pieces; mostly shredders and grazers | Limnephilidae (northern caddisflies, the largest family; cases of many materials), Phryganeidae (large, with spiral cases of plant pieces), Leptoceridae (long-horned caddisflies), Sericostomatidae, Helicopsychidae (cases of sand grains coiled like a snail shell) |

The cases are adaptive in several ways: they protect the soft abdomen from predators such as fish and stoneflies; they improve gas exchange, because the larva can pump water through a tube; and heavy mineral cases act as ballast in fast water, while light plant cases float in still water. Building behaviour is species-specific and is often more useful in identifying larvae than morphology. The middle group of the table, once the suborder "Spicipalpia", is paraphyletic: phylogenomic data place these families with the Integripalpia, so that portable cases and fixed retreats represent two diverging uses of silk from an ancestor that spun only a cocoon for pupation (Thomas and coauthors 2020).

Caddisfly larvae are among the dominant animals of stream benthos, shredding leaf litter, grazing algae, filtering drift and preying on other invertebrates; adults and larvae are important food for fish, and anglers imitate them with artificial flies. Chinese textbooks call the larvae "stone silkworms". In China the fauna is rich in mountain streams: the large net-spinning *Stenopsyche* (Stenopsychidae) and *Hydropsyche* and *Cheumatopsyche* (Hydropsychidae) are often the most abundant insects on the stones of rivers, *Rhyacophila* (Rhyacophilidae) is common in cold torrents, and *Limnephilus* and *Phryganea* build cases in ponds and lakes. Larvae are classified into feeding guilds: filterers (hydropsychids, philopotamids), scrapers (glossosomatids, many limnephilids), shredders of leaf litter (limnephilids, sericostomatids, lepidostomatids), and predators (rhyacophilids, polycentropodids). Net-spinners show how silk extends the reach of a small animal: the capture net of a hydropsychid has a regular mesh whose size, from a few to several hundred micrometres, depends on species, instar and current speed, and it filters particles the larva could not otherwise collect; caddisflies thereby link the drift of organic particles in streams to fish and birds.

## 36.2. Lepidoptera: Characters

**Definition 36.2.1 (Lepidoptera)** The **Lepidoptera** (moths and butterflies) are holometabolous insects whose body, legs and two pairs of membranous wings are covered with flattened scales; the adult mouthparts, except in a few basal families with functional mandibles, form a coiled proboscis of the two maxillary galeae, the mandibles being vestigial or absent, with prominent labial palps; the fore and hind wings are coupled by a jugum, a frenulum or an overlap of the wings; the larvae are caterpillars with chewing mouthparts, a spinneret on the labium, three pairs of thoracic legs and up to five pairs of abdominal prolegs (on segments 3–6 and 10) bearing crochets; the pupa is obtect in most families; about 157,000 described species.

### 36.2.1. Scales and Colour

Scales are flattened setae, each made by one epidermal cell and inserted in a socket formed by a second cell, as in the mechanosensory bristles from which they evolved. They cover the wings in overlapping rows, like roof tiles: large cover scales over smaller ground scales. A scale has a flat lower lamina and an upper lamina of longitudinal ridges joined by cross-ribs, with windows between them.

![The scales of a butterfly wing (schematic). (A) Scales are inserted in rows on the wing membrane and overlap like roof tiles, cover scales over ground scales. (B) A single scale: a stalk (pedicel) in its socket and a lamina whose upper surface bears longitudinal ridges joined by cross-ribs, with windows between them. (C) Cross-section of the upper lamina: in structurally coloured scales the ridges are built of overlapping lamellae, layers of cuticle separated by air, whose spacing produces colour by multilayer interference.](./plots/wing-scales.py)

Wing colour patterns are made of thousands of single-coloured scales, like a mosaic. Colours are of two kinds ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)). Pigmentary colours come from melanins (black and brown), ommochromes (reds and oranges of nymphalids), pterins (the whites and yellows of Pieridae, unique among butterflies) and flavonoids taken up from the larval food plant. Structural colours arise from the nanoarchitecture of the scale: the ridge lamellae of *Morpho* act as multilayer reflectors producing a brilliant blue; the scales of some swallowtails combine a multilayer with concavities that mix blue and yellow into green; and the green hairstreaks (*Callophrys*) contain three-dimensional gyroid photonic crystals of cuticle. Scales also make the wing water-repellent and protect moths in spider webs, where scales come off and the moth slips free.

Specialized scent scales (androconia), often grouped in patches on the wings or in eversible hair pencils on the abdomen, release male courtship pheromones; the males of milkweed butterflies (*Danaus*) make their pheromone from pyrrolizidine alkaloids collected from plants. The development of wing patterns, including the eyespots of *Bicyclus*, is treated in [7.5.39](/7.5.39).

### 36.2.2. The Proboscis and Nectar Feeding

The proboscis consists of the two greatly elongated galeae, each a C-shaped half-tube, linked along their length by interlocking rows of small plates (legulae) above and hooks below, so that the two halves enclose a single food canal. At rest it is coiled like a watch spring under the head, held by the elasticity of its cuticle and by intrinsic muscles; it is uncoiled by an increase of hemolymph pressure within it. Fluid enters through the drinking region near the tip, where the spaces between the dorsal legulae act as a sponge that wicks liquid in by capillarity, and is drawn up the canal by the cibarial pump in the head (Monaenkova and coauthors 2012).

Because the canal is a narrow tube, the uptake of nectar is governed by viscous flow. For a tube of radius $r$ and length $L$ under a suction pressure $\Delta p$, the Hagen–Poiseuille law gives the volume flow

$$
Q = \frac{\pi r^4 \Delta p}{8 \mu L},
$$

where $\mu$ is the viscosity of the nectar. The rate of sugar intake is $Q$ times the sugar mass per volume, $c\rho$. Concentrated nectar carries more sugar per volume, but its viscosity rises steeply with concentration, so the intake rate passes through a maximum.

![A Hagen–Poiseuille model of nectar uptake through a proboscis. At fixed tube dimensions and suction, the rate of sugar intake is proportional to $c\rho/\mu$, where c is the sugar concentration, ρ the density and μ the viscosity of the solution. Viscosity (dashed; points are handbook values for sucrose solutions at 20 °C, CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics) rises steeply above about 40% sugar, so that the intake rate (solid) peaks at about 22% and falls at higher concentrations. The curve is a model, not data on any species.](./plots/nectar-viscosity.py)

The model explains two observations. Flowers pollinated by butterflies and moths tend to offer relatively dilute nectar, often around 20–30% sugar, while bees, which lap nectar with a hairy tongue and are less limited by viscosity, take more concentrated nectars; and butterflies feeding on very concentrated or dried sugar first dilute it with saliva. The steep dependence on $r^4$ and the inverse dependence on $L$ mean that a long proboscis is costly in feeding rate, so that very long proboscides should evolve only where they give access to protected nectar. The classic case is Darwin's prediction (1862) that the Madagascan orchid *Angraecum sesquipedale*, with a nectar spur about 30 cm long, must be pollinated by a moth with a proboscis of similar length; the hawk moth *Xanthopan praedicta* (formerly a subspecies of *X. morganii*), with a proboscis over 20 cm long, was described in 1903 and was filmed pollinating the orchid only in the 1990s.

Some moths feed on other liquids: many male butterflies drink at mud puddles and dung to obtain sodium, which they pass to females with the spermatophore; moths of the genus *Calyptra* (Erebidae) pierce fruit, and a few species pierce the skin of mammals and drink blood, using a proboscis armed with hooks; and some Madagascan moths drink the tears of sleeping birds. The adults of many moths, including the silkworm moth and saturniids, have a reduced proboscis and do not feed, living on reserves accumulated by the larva.

### 36.2.3. Wings, Ears and Genitalia

Fore and hind wings are coupled in one of three ways:

| Coupling | Mechanism | Groups |
|---|---|---|
| Jugate | A lobe at the base of the forewing (jugum) lies under the hind wing | Micropterigidae, Hepialidae and other basal moths |
| Frenate | A bristle or group of bristles on the base of the hind wing (frenulum: one stout bristle in males, several in females) is held by a hook (retinaculum) or a tuft of scales on the underside of the forewing | Most moths |
| Amplexiform | The enlarged humeral lobe of the hind wing overlaps the forewing; no frenulum | Butterflies (except one Australian skipper), and several large moths such as silkworm moths and saturniids |

The basal families are also homoneurous (fore and hind wings with similar venation), whereas most Lepidoptera are heteroneurous, with reduced venation in the hind wing. A large discal cell in the middle of the wing, closed or open, is present in most families and is used, with the branching pattern of the veins around it, in identification.

Hearing organs evolved independently many times, mostly in response to bats ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)): on the metathorax in Noctuoidea, at the base of the abdomen in Pyraloidea, Geometroidea and Drepanidae, and at the base of the forewing in the nocturnal moth-butterflies (Hedylidae) and in some nymphalid butterflies. Many tiger moths (Erebidae: Arctiinae) produce ultrasonic clicks with tymbals on the thorax, which warn bats of their toxicity or, in some species, jam the bat's echolocation.

The female genitalia are of three types, which define major lineages: a single genital opening for copulation and egg-laying (the "monotrysian" basal groups); two separate openings, one on the eighth segment for copulation (the ostium bursae) and one on the ninth for egg-laying, connected internally by a sperm duct (Ditrysia); and an external groove carrying sperm between the two openings (Exoporia: Hepialidae). The complex male genitalia (valvae, uncus, aedeagus) and the female genitalia are the most important characters for species identification in moths.

### 36.2.4. Caterpillars and Pupae

The caterpillar is an eruciform larva with a hardened head, six stemmata on each side, short antennae, chewing mandibles and a median spinneret through which silk from the labial glands is extruded. Its three pairs of thoracic legs are segmented; the abdominal prolegs, fleshy and unsegmented, end in a sole with rows or circles of small hooks (crochets), whose arrangement is a family character. Caterpillars are distinguished from sawfly larvae by the number of prolegs and the crochets ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)). The arrangement of the primary setae (chaetotaxy) is used to identify larvae.

Most caterpillars feed on the leaves of seed plants, externally or as leaf miners, stem borers, root feeders, fruit borers or gall makers; some feed on mosses, lichens, fungi, detritus, wool, wax or other insects. Departures from the basic plan reflect habit: loopers (Geometridae) have prolegs only on segments 6 and 10 and move by drawing the abdomen up to the thorax; slug caterpillars (Limacodidae) lack prolegs and glide on a ventral sucker; leaf miners are flattened and reduce their legs. Defences include urticating hairs and spines (tussock moths, lappet moths, slug caterpillars), eversible forked glands (osmeteria) releasing terpenoids in swallowtail caterpillars, resemblance to twigs, bird droppings or snakes, and sequestration of plant toxins ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)).

Pupae are obtect, with appendages glued to the body, in the Ditrysia; the basal Micropterigidae and some other non-ditrysians have decticous exarate pupae with movable mandibles. Moth pupae lie in cocoons or earthen cells; the naked pupa of a butterfly (chrysalis) hangs from a silk pad by a hooked terminal spine (cremaster), held head-down in Nymphalidae or upright by a silk girdle in Papilionidae, Pieridae and Lycaenidae.

## 36.3. Phylogeny and the Basal Lineages

The Lepidoptera are among the best-resolved insect orders. Fossil scales from the Triassic–Jurassic boundary, about 200 million years old, include hollow scales of the type found in proboscis-bearing moths, suggesting that the proboscis evolved before the flowering plants, perhaps for drinking water or the pollination drops of gymnosperms (van Eldijk and coauthors 2018). Most of the diversity, however, arose in the Cretaceous and Cenozoic together with the angiosperms ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

**Definition 36.3.1 (Micropterigidae)** The **Micropterigidae** (mandibulate archaic moths) are small, often metallic moths, the sister group of all other Lepidoptera, whose adults have functional chewing mandibles and no proboscis and feed on pollen or fern spores, and whose larvae, with long antennae and eight pairs of abdominal prolegs without crochets, feed on liverworts, mosses or detritus in damp places; about 160 species.

The first branches of the lepidopteran tree, Micropterigidae, the New Zealand kauri moths Agathiphagidae (whose larvae live in kauri seeds and can remain in diapause for up to twelve years) and the South American Heterobathmiidae, all have adult mandibles. The remaining moths, the Glossata, have a proboscis. Among the non-ditrysian glossatans are the Eriocraniidae (leaf miners with mandibulate pupae), the ghost moths and swift moths (Hepialidae), whose larvae feed on roots and wood, the pigmy moths (Nepticulidae), leaf miners including the smallest Lepidoptera, with wingspans of about 3 mm, and the fairy moths (Adelidae), whose males have antennae several times the length of the body.

Larvae of the ghost moth genus *Thitarodes*, feeding on roots in alpine meadows at 3000–5000 m on the Tibetan Plateau, are infected by the fungus *Ophiocordyceps sinensis*, which kills them and grows a fruiting body from the head: the caterpillar fungus, one of the most valuable natural products of the region and the subject of intense harvesting ([7.11.21](/7.11.21)).

The Ditrysia contain about 98% of lepidopteran species. The following tables give the most important families in the order of the classification.

### 36.4.2. Macroheterocera

The large moths form a clade, the Macroheterocera, which includes the superfamilies Geometroidea, Lasiocampoidea, Bombycoidea and Noctuoidea of the following table; the closest relatives of the butterflies among the moth superfamilies remain uncertain.

| Family | Characters | Biology, examples and importance |
|---|---|---|
| Geometridae (geometer moths; about 24,000) | Slender body; broad wings held flat; tympanal organs at the base of the abdomen | Larvae are loopers (inchworms) with prolegs on segments 6 and 10 only, often twig mimics; the peppered moth *Biston betularia*, the classic example of industrial melanism, whose dark form is caused by a transposon insertion in the gene *cortex* (van't Hof and coauthors 2016; [7.10.3](/7.10.3)) |
| Lasiocampidae (lappet moths, tent caterpillars) | Stout, hairy; no frenulum; proboscis reduced | Larvae hairy, often urticating; the Masson pine caterpillar *Dendrolimus punctatus* and related species are the most destructive defoliators of pine forests in China |
| Bombycidae (silkworm moths) | Stout, with pectinate antennae; no functional proboscis | *Bombyx mori*, the domesticated silkworm (Section 36.6), and its wild ancestor *B. mandarina* |
| Saturniidae (giant silk moths; about 3400) | Large; often eyespots on all four wings; males with broad pectinate antennae; adults not feeding | The Chinese oak silkmoth *Antheraea pernyi* (tussah silk), the eri silkmoth *Samia ricini*, the moon moths *Actias*, and the atlas moth *Attacus atlas*, one of the largest moths |
| Sphingidae (hawk moths; about 1500) | Stout, streamlined; narrow forewings; long proboscis; very fast fliers | Hover at flowers and are important pollinators of night-blooming flowers; larvae with a horn on segment 8 ("hornworms"); *Manduca sexta*, a model of insect physiology and endocrinology; the soybean hawk moth *Clanis bilineata*, whose larvae are eaten in parts of eastern China; the day-flying hummingbird hawk moth *Macroglossum stellatarum* |
| Notodontidae (prominents) | Stout; hind wing without a frenulum hook in some | Includes the processionary moths (*Thaumetopoea*), with urticating larval hairs |
| Erebidae (about 25,000) | Metathoracic ears | Tiger moths (Arctiinae), many aposematic and clicking, including the fall webworm *Hyphantria cunea*, invasive in China since 1979; tussock moths (Lymantriinae), including the spongy moth *Lymantria dispar*; underwing moths (*Catocala*); fruit-piercing and blood-drinking *Calyptra* |
| Noctuidae (owlet moths; about 12,000) | Stout, dull-coloured; metathoracic ears; forewing pattern with orbicular and reniform spots | The cotton bollworm *Helicoverpa armigera*, the target of Bt cotton in China since 1997; the oriental armyworm *Mythimna separata*, which migrates northward each spring across eastern China; the fall armyworm *Spodoptera frugiperda*, native to the Americas, which reached Yunnan in 2019 and spread over most of China within a year; the tobacco cutworm *S. litura*; the black cutworm *Agrotis ipsilon* |

Many of these pests are long-distance migrants. Radar studies over China and Europe show that noctuid moths such as *Helicoverpa armigera*, *Mythimna separata* and *Agrotis ipsilon* fly at hundreds of metres above the ground and select altitudes and headings that use favourable winds, covering hundreds of kilometres in a night ([7.17.29](/7.17.29) for insect migration).

## 36.5. Butterflies

### 36.5.1. Butterflies and Moths

Butterflies (Papilionoidea, including skippers and the moth-butterflies Hedylidae; about 19,000 species) are a monophyletic group of diurnal Lepidoptera nested among the moth superfamilies. "Moths" is therefore a name for all Lepidoptera except butterflies, a paraphyletic assemblage, and no single character separates all butterflies from all moths. The usual field characters work for most species:

| Character | Butterflies | Most moths |
|---|---|---|
| Antennae | Filiform with a terminal club; club with a recurved hook in skippers | Filiform, setaceous or pectinate (feathery in males), without a club (a few moths, such as Castniidae, have clubbed antennae) |
| Wing coupling | Amplexiform; no frenulum (except in one skipper) | Frenulum and retinaculum (absent in some large moths) |
| Resting posture | Wings closed vertically above the body (skippers hold them partly open) | Wings held roof-like, flat or rolled round the body; some geometrids rest like butterflies |
| Activity | Diurnal (Hedylidae nocturnal) | Mostly nocturnal; many diurnal moths |
| Pupa | A naked chrysalis suspended by a cremaster | Usually in a cocoon or an earthen cell |

![Characters used to tell butterflies from moths (schematic). (A) Antennae: clubbed in butterflies, with a hooked club in skippers, filiform or pectinate (especially in males) in most moths. (B) Wing coupling: in most moths a bristle on the base of the hind wing (frenulum, red) is caught by a retinaculum on the underside of the forewing (frenate coupling); in butterflies the frenulum is lost and the enlarged humeral lobe of the hind wing overlaps the forewing (amplexiform coupling).](./plots/butterfly-moth.py)

Two migrations of butterflies have become model systems. Monarchs of eastern North America fly up to about 4000 km in autumn to a few overwintering sites in the mountain fir forests of central Mexico, where tens of millions cluster on the trees; the return in spring takes three or four generations. The migrants navigate with a time-compensated sun compass: the azimuth of the sun, sensed by the eyes, is corrected by a circadian clock located partly in the antennae, and antennectomized butterflies fly in the wrong direction (Merlin and coauthors 2009). The painted lady *Vanessa cardui* makes an even longer multigenerational circuit between tropical Africa and northern Europe, up to about 12,000–15,000 km a year, most of it at high altitude out of sight, as shown by radar and by stable isotopes in the wings of migrants (Stefanescu and coauthors 2013; Talavera and coauthors 2018).

*Heliconius* butterflies of the American tropics are unusual in feeding on pollen: they collect pollen on the proboscis, moisten it with saliva and drink the dissolved amino acids. The protein-rich diet allows adults to live for six months or more and to lay eggs continuously, and it supports large mushroom bodies used in learning the positions of flowers along foraging routes. Different species of *Heliconius* share identical warning patterns in each region (Müllerian mimicry), and genomic studies have shown that the same few genes (*optix*, *WntA*, *cortex*) control the patterns in all of them, some pattern alleles having passed between species by hybridization.

Lycaenid larvae and ants form some of the most intricate associations between insects. The caterpillar's dorsal nectary organ on abdominal segment 7 secretes droplets of sugars and amino acids that ants drink, while a pair of eversible tentacle organs on segment 8 releases volatile signals that alert ants, and vibratory organs on the pupa and larva produce substrate-borne calls. The ants guard the caterpillars against parasitoids and predators. In the social parasites of the genus *Phengaris* the relationship turns into exploitation: the late-instar caterpillar drops from its food plant, mimics the surface hydrocarbons and the sounds of *Myrmica* larvae and queens, is carried by workers into the nest and there eats the ant brood or is fed by the workers like their own larvae, until it pupates and emerges the following summer.

Butterflies are the best-studied insects in ecology and conservation. Their diurnal activity, conspicuous colours and well-known taxonomy make them standard subjects for population monitoring, studies of the effects of climate change on distributions, and models of speciation and of colour pattern genetics (*Heliconius*, *Papilio*, *Bicyclus*). Batesian and Müllerian **mimicry** in butterflies ([7.17.39](/7.17.39), [7.10.17](/7.10.17)) was described by H. W. Bates and F. Müller from Amazonian butterflies in the 1860s and 1870s.

### 36.5.3. Host Plants and Pheromones

Most caterpillars are specialists: in surveys of tropical and temperate forests, the majority of lepidopteran species feed on plants of a single family or genus, and closely related butterflies tend to feed on related plants. P. R. Ehrlich and P. H. Raven (1964) proposed from this pattern that butterflies and plants have diversified in a stepwise arms race: a plant lineage that evolves a new chemical defence escapes its herbivores and radiates, and an insect lineage that evolves a way to detoxify the defence then radiates onto it. Pierids feeding on glucosinolate-bearing crucifers, which they tolerate with a nitrile-specifier protein that redirects the breakdown of glucosinolates, and swallowtails on aristolochic-acid-bearing pipevines are classic examples; the theory and its tests are treated in [7.17.37](/7.17.37) and [7.10.17](/7.10.17).

Moths find mates by female sex **pheromones**, usually blends of a few long-chain unsaturated alcohols, acetates or aldehydes derived from fatty acids, in species-specific ratios; males detect them with thousands of sensilla on their antennae and fly upwind in a zigzag path within the odour plume ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)). Because the blends are species-specific and active in minute amounts, synthetic pheromones are used for monitoring pests with traps and for control by mating disruption: dispensers releasing pheromone throughout an orchard make it impossible for males to locate females. Mating disruption is used against the codling moth, the oriental fruit moth, the pink bollworm and others on hundreds of thousands of hectares, without effects on natural enemies.

## 36.6. The Silkworm and Silk

The domesticated silkworm *Bombyx mori* descends from the wild silkmoth *B. mandarina* of China. Genomic data indicate a single domestication in China, and archaeology shows the use of silk from the Neolithic onward: biomolecular traces of silk from tombs in Henan are about 8500 years old, and woven silk fabrics from Zhejiang date to about 4700 years ago. Silk was exported along the Silk Road from the Han dynasty, and sericulture spread to Korea, Japan, India, Byzantium and Europe, but China remains the largest producer of raw silk. Domestication changed the moth profoundly: adults cannot fly, larvae do not wander from food, and they produce several times more silk than the wild form.

The silkworm passes through five larval instars over about four weeks, feeding only on mulberry leaves, and then spins a cocoon over two to three days from a single continuous filament about one to one and a half kilometres long. Silk is produced in a pair of tubular labial glands. The posterior part of each gland synthesizes fibroin, a complex of a heavy chain of about 390 kDa, a light chain and a glycoprotein; the heavy chain consists largely of repeats of the motif Gly-Ala-Gly-Ala-Gly-Ser, which stack into β-sheet crystals that make the fibre strong. The middle part adds sericin, a gummy coat that glues the threads together. The liquid silk solution is stored in a concentrated, liquid-crystalline state and solidifies into fibre as it is drawn out through the spinneret under tension and shear. To reel silk, cocoons are heated to kill the pupae and soaked to soften the sericin, and the threads of several cocoons are reeled together.

The silkworm is also a model organism. It was the subject of the first demonstration that a microorganism causes an animal disease, when A. Bassi showed in 1835 that the muscardine disease is caused by the fungus now named *Beauveria bassiana*, and of L. Pasteur's work on pébrine, a microsporidian disease that threatened French sericulture in the 1860s. The first insect pheromone, bombykol, was identified from silkworm females by A. Butenandt in 1959 ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)), and the silkworm genome was sequenced by Chinese and Japanese groups in 2004. Other moths are also used for silk: the Chinese oak silkmoth *Antheraea pernyi*, reared outdoors on oak in Liaoning and Henan, produces tussah silk, and its pupae are eaten and its eggs used to rear *Trichogramma* parasitoids for biological control ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)); the eri silkmoth *Samia ricini* is reared on castor in India and southern China.

## 36.7. Lepidoptera and People

Caterpillars are the most important insect pests of crops: corn borers, stem borers, armyworms, cutworms, bollworms, the diamondback moth, codling moth and stored-product moths cause losses of tens of billions of dollars a year. Transgenic crops expressing *Bacillus thuringiensis* toxins, introduced in the 1990s, greatly reduced the use of insecticides against *Helicoverpa* in Chinese cotton; their durability depends on delaying resistance with refuges of non-Bt plants, in northern China provided by the mosaic of other crops on which bollworms also feed. Sex pheromones are used to monitor moth populations and, in mating disruption, to control them; mass trapping, light traps, *Trichogramma* releases and baculoviruses ([7.11.26](/7.11.26)) are other biological methods. Forest defoliators (pine caterpillars, the spongy moth, the fall webworm) cause outbreaks over large areas.

On the other side are silk, the pollination of many night-blooming and long-tubed flowers by hawk moths and other moths, the role of caterpillars as the main food of nestling birds in temperate forests, and edible caterpillars, an important food in parts of Africa and Asia. Many butterflies and moths have declined with the loss of habitat and agricultural intensification, and butterflies are among the indicator groups used to monitor biodiversity.

## Summary

- Amphiesmenoptera share hairy wings, a larval spinneret fed by labial silk glands, and heterogametic females.
- **Trichoptera** (Definition 36.1.1) are moth-like insects with hairy wings and aquatic larvae that build silk nets and fixed retreats (Annulipalpia) or portable cases (Integripalpia); their silk sets under water by calcium bridges between phosphorylated serines.
- **Lepidoptera** (Definition 36.2.1) have scaled wings and bodies, a coiled proboscis of the maxillary galeae, jugate, frenate or amplexiform wing coupling, and caterpillars with crochet-bearing prolegs on segments 3–6 and 10.
- Scale colours are pigmentary (melanins, ommochromes, pterins, flavonoids) or structural (multilayer ridge lamellae, photonic crystals); scent scales release courtship pheromones.
- Nectar uptake follows viscous flow, $Q = \pi r^4 \Delta p/(8\mu L)$, so the sugar intake rate peaks at intermediate concentrations; long proboscides are costly and evolve where nectar is deep, as in Darwin's prediction of *Xanthopan praedicta*.
- **Micropterigidae** (Definition 36.3.1) are the mandibulate sister group of all other Lepidoptera; the proboscis defines Glossata, and two female genital openings define the Ditrysia, about 98% of species.
- Important moth families include Tortricidae, Plutellidae, Gelechiidae, Crambidae, Pyralidae, Geometridae, Lasiocampidae, Bombycidae, Saturniidae, Sphingidae, Erebidae and Noctuidae; butterflies (Papilionidae, Hesperiidae, Pieridae, Lycaenidae, Riodinidae, Nymphalidae) are one lineage within the moths, differing in clubbed antennae, amplexiform wing coupling and diurnal habits.
- *Bombyx mori*, domesticated in China, spins a cocoon from fibroin and sericin produced in the labial glands; it is a model organism for genetics, pheromone biology and insect pathology.

## Questions

1. A small moth-like insect with long antennae and hairy, roof-like wings is caught at a light near a stream. How would you confirm whether it is a caddisfly or a moth?

   **Answer** Look at the wings and mouthparts with a lens. Caddisflies have wings covered with hairs, not flattened scales (scales rub off as coloured dust; hairs do not), and lack a coiled proboscis: their mouthparts are reduced to palps and a short haustellum, while most moths have a coiled proboscis between the labial palps. Caddisflies also usually have conspicuous tibial spurs and very long antennae, and their venation lacks the discal cell typical of moths. Rearing the larva would confirm it: caddisfly larvae are aquatic and build retreats or cases.

2. Explain why a moth feeding through a proboscis takes up sugar most quickly from nectar of intermediate concentration, and how the length of the proboscis enters the argument.

   **Answer** Flow through the narrow food canal is viscous, $Q = \pi r^4 \Delta p/(8\mu L)$, so at a given suction pressure the volume intake falls as viscosity rises. The sugar intake is $Q c\rho$: increasing concentration adds sugar per volume, but the viscosity of sugar solutions rises steeply above about 30–40%, so the product $c\rho/\mu$ has a maximum at intermediate concentrations (about 20–25% in the simple model). A longer proboscis increases $L$ and therefore lowers $Q$ proportionally; an insect with a long proboscis must suck harder, take more dilute nectar or feed more slowly, so very long proboscides are expected only where they give access to nectar unavailable to others.

3. Why are "moths" not a natural group, and what characters nonetheless allow most butterflies to be distinguished from most moths?

   **Answer** Phylogenies place butterflies (Papilionoidea, including skippers and Hedylidae) as one lineage within the ditrysian moths, closer to some moth superfamilies than these are to the basal moths; a group "moths" that excludes butterflies leaves out descendants of their common ancestor and is paraphyletic. Most butterflies differ from most moths in having clubbed antennae (hooked in skippers), amplexiform wing coupling without a frenulum, a resting posture with wings closed above the body, diurnal activity and a naked chrysalis; but each character has exceptions (clubbed antennae in Castniidae, no frenulum in Bombycoidea, nocturnal Hedylidae, a frenulum in one skipper).

4. What evidence shows that the Micropterigidae are the sister group of all other Lepidoptera, and what does that suggest about the ancestral lepidopteran?

   **Answer** Molecular phylogenies consistently place Micropterigidae outside a clade containing all other Lepidoptera. Morphologically, they lack the derived characters of the rest: adults have functional mandibles and no proboscis, larvae have eight pairs of prolegs without crochets, and the pupa is decticous, while they share with other Lepidoptera the scales and other ordinal characters, and with caddisflies ancestral features of the wings and genitalia. The ancestral lepidopteran was therefore probably a small, mandibulate moth that chewed pollen or spores as an adult and whose larvae lived in moist places, the proboscis and the leaf-feeding caterpillar evolving later.

5. A field of cabbage is attacked by green caterpillars; a farmer sprays a *Bacillus thuringiensis* product, but the damage continues. Suggest two biological explanations.

   **Answer** First, the pest may be the diamondback moth *Plutella xylostella*, which has evolved resistance to Bt toxins in many regions, often through changes in the midgut receptors to which the toxin binds; resistance would be favoured by repeated spraying. Second, the caterpillar-like larvae may not be Lepidoptera at all but sawfly larvae (Tenthredinidae, for example *Athalia*), which have more prolegs without crochets and are not susceptible to the lepidopteran-active Bt strains. Other possibilities include poor coverage of leaf undersides, degradation of the spray in sunlight, or larvae too old to be killed.

6. (Open question) The silkworm was domesticated from a wild moth. Which changes would you expect artificial selection to have produced, and how could genomic data be used to find the genes involved?

   **Answer** Expected changes include increased silk production (larger silk glands, more fibroin), tolerance of crowding and handling, loss of wandering and escape behaviour, reduced flight and pigmentation in adults, and synchronized development; some traits may be by-products of selection on others. Comparing the genomes of many domestic strains with wild *B. mandarina* can identify regions of reduced diversity and strong differentiation (selective sweeps) in the domesticated form; genes in these regions (for example those expressed in the silk gland or involved in behaviour and development) are candidates, which can then be tested by gene editing in the silkworm, a species in which transgenesis and genome editing are routine.
