An insect grows inside a cuticle that cannot grow with it. It therefore develops in steps, molting from one instar to the next, and in most insects these steps also transform the body: the wingless caterpillar becomes a pupa and then a moth, the aquatic dragonfly naiad an aerial hunter. How an insect spends the months or years between egg and adult, how fast it develops at a given temperature, when it stops developing to wait out winter or drought and when it moves to another region, together make up its life history. These questions matter for the biology of almost every insect and are of direct practical importance: forecasting the emergence of a pest, timing a control measure, or predicting how many generations a species will complete as climates warm all depend on them.

This chapter follows postembryonic development from the hatchling to the adult. Section 29.1 deals with molting and growth, 29.2 and 29.3 with the types of metamorphosis and the forms of larvae and pupae, 29.4 with the hormones and transcription factors that decide the character of each molt, and 29.5 with the evolution of complete metamorphosis. Sections 29.6–29.9 turn to the seasonal life history: temperature and development, dormancy and diapause, migration and polyphenism. The embryonic development that precedes all this is described in [7.17.28](/7.17.28#sec-28.7); the cuticle and the molt in [7.17.23](/7.17.23) and [7.17.18](/7.17.18); the endocrine organs in [7.17.26](/7.17.26). The developmental mechanisms of **metamorphosis**, the ecdysone cascade and the **imaginal discs** are treated in [7.5.33](/7.5.33), and the physiology of the insect endocrine system in [7.14.32](/7.14.32#sec-32.9.3); here we use them without repeating them.

## 29.1. Molting and Growth

### 29.1.1. Stages and Instars

Postembryonic development begins with hatching, when the young insect leaves the egg, and ends with the final molt to the adult, called eclosion in holometabolous insects and the imaginal molt in others. Between these events the insect passes through a series of molts.

**Definition 29.1.1 (Instar)** An **instar** is the form of an insect between two successive molts; the first instar is the hatchling, each molt adds one, and the last instar is the adult (imago). The time spent in an instar is the stadium (plural stadia), and the stage of development (egg, nymph or larva, pupa, adult) comprises one or more instars.

For practical reasons the instar is counted from ecdysis to ecdysis, when the old cuticle is shed. Physiologically, however, a new instar begins earlier, at apolysis, when the epidermis separates from the old cuticle and begins to secrete the new one ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)). Between apolysis and ecdysis the insect is pharate, enclosed in the cuticle of the previous instar. Many insects spend long periods in this state, for example pharate first-instar larvae of mosquitoes inside the eggshell, or pharate adults of moths inside the pupal cuticle, because the double cuticle reduces water loss while the insect waits for favourable conditions.

The number of instars is characteristic of a species but varies widely among groups:

| Group | Usual number of immature instars | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| Silverfish (Zygentoma) and bristletails (Archaeognatha) | About 9–14 before maturity | Adults continue to molt, in silverfish up to several dozen times |
| Mayflies (Ephemeroptera) | 10–50 nymphal instars | Followed by the winged subimago (Section 29.2.2) |
| Dragonflies (Odonata) | 9–16 | |
| Grasshoppers and locusts (Acrididae) | 5–6 | *Locusta migratoria* usually 5; females of some species one more |
| Hemiptera | 5 (aphids 4) | |
| Lepidoptera | Usually 5 larval instars | The silkworm *Bombyx mori* has 5 (4 larval molts); some races 4 |
| Most Coleoptera and Hymenoptera | 3–5 larval instars | |
| Higher Diptera (Cyclorrhapha) | 3 larval instars | |

The number is not always fixed. Poor food, low temperature or short days can add supernumerary instars, and in some species males and females differ (females are larger and have one more instar). The silkworm shows the hormonal basis of the number: implanting extra corpora allata into a last-instar larva produces an additional larval instar ([7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.3)).

### 29.1.2. Growth and Dyar's Rule

Between molts, the sclerotized parts of the cuticle cannot expand, but the arthrodial membranes and, in caterpillars and fly larvae, most of the body cuticle can, so that body mass increases more or less continuously while the head capsule and other sclerites grow only at the molt. This is why the width of the head capsule, not body length, is used to determine the instar of a larva collected in the field: it falls into discrete size classes, one per instar.

**Definition 29.1.2 (Dyar's Rule)** **Dyar's rule** (Dyar 1890) states that the linear dimensions of a sclerotized part, typically the width of the head capsule, increase at each molt by a constant ratio characteristic of the species, so that the size $x_i$ in instar $i$ follows a geometric progression,

$$
x_i = x_1 g^{\,i-1}, \qquad \ln x_i = \ln x_1 + (i-1)\ln g,
\tag{29.1}
$$
with a growth ratio $g$ that is usually between 1.2 and 1.9 (often near 1.4).

Dyar based the rule on the head capsules of caterpillars of 28 species of Lepidoptera, and it holds approximately in many insects and in crustaceans. A related empirical rule, Przibram's rule, states that body mass doubles in each instar, so that if the shape stays the same, linear dimensions grow by $2^{1/3} \approx 1.26$ per molt. Neither is a law: the ratio varies between instars and with food and temperature, and it is often larger in the early instars. Their value lies in fitting a simple model against which deviations can be recognized.

**Example 29.1.1** Larvae of a moth collected in the field have head capsules in four clear size classes, with mean widths of 0.32, 0.46, 0.95 and 1.35 mm. Are these four consecutive instars? The ratios of successive classes are 1.44, 2.07 and 1.42: the middle ratio is close to the square of the others ($1.43^2 \approx 2.05$), so an instar of width about $0.46 \times 1.43 \approx 0.66\,\mathrm{mm}$ is probably missing from the sample, for example because it is short or lives in a different microhabitat. Plotting $\ln x$ against instar number (Eq. (29.1)) makes the test visual: assigning the classes to instars 1, 2, 4 and 5 puts all points on a straight line with slope $\ln g \approx 0.36$, whereas numbering them 1–4 produces a kink. Gaps in a Dyar plot are the standard way of detecting instars that were missed, and the rule also predicts the size of the final instar of species whose last stages are rarely collected.

If the four measured size classes (0.32, 0.46, 0.95 and 1.35 mm) are taken as instars 1–4 (open circles, dashed line), the plot shows a kink; assigned to instars 1, 2, 4 and 5 (filled circles) they lie on one straight line with growth ratio g ≈ 1.43, and the missing third instar is predicted at about 0.66 mm (star). ### 29.1.3. The Molt

A molt is a sequence of events in the epidermis and the nervous system that takes from a day to more than a week. After apolysis the epidermal cells divide, enlarging the epidermis, and secrete a molting fluid into the space between themselves and the old cuticle. The fluid contains inactive chitinases and proteases. Only when the epidermis has laid down the outer, protective layer of the new epicuticle are the enzymes activated; they then digest the old endocuticle, whose products are resorbed, while the new procuticle is being deposited beneath them. What is shed at ecdysis is the indigestible remainder, the old epicuticle and exocuticle and the cuticular linings of the foregut, hindgut and tracheae.

Ecdysis itself is a fixed motor program triggered by a cascade of neuropeptides, best studied in the tobacco hornworm, *Manduca sexta*. When the ecdysteroid titer falls at the end of the molt, the Inka cells on the tracheae release ecdysis-triggering hormone (ETH), and neurosecretory cells of the brain release eclosion hormone (EH); the two stimulate each other's release in a positive feedback loop. They act on the central nervous system to start pre-ecdysis behaviour (loosening the old cuticle, seeking a suitable site), and EH releases crustacean cardioactive peptide (CCAP), which switches to ecdysis proper: waves of contraction that split the old cuticle along the ecdysial line ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)) and push the insect out. After ecdysis the insect swallows air or water to expand the new cuticle, and the neurohormone bursicon first makes the cuticle briefly more plastic, allowing the wings of a newly eclosed adult to expand, and then triggers its tanning ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)). The newly molted, soft, pale insect is called teneral.

## 29.2. Types of Metamorphosis

### 29.2.1. Ametaboly

The amount of change between hatchling and adult differs greatly among insects, and entomologists have long classified development by it. The simplest case is found in the primitively wingless insects, the bristletails and silverfish ([7.17.31](/7.17.31#sec-31.2)). Their hatchlings resemble the adult except in size, the proportions of some parts and the lack of genitalia; they grow by a series of molts that continue after sexual maturity, so that the adult alternates feeding, mating and molting. This development without metamorphosis is called ametaboly (called epimorphosis in some Chinese textbooks). The entognathan hexapods (springtails, proturans and diplurans, [7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.2)) develop in the same way; proturans in addition add abdominal segments at the first molts, from 9 in the hatchling to 12 in the adult, a form called anamorphosis, which is also found in myriapods ([7.17.20](/7.17.20)). Continued molting in the adult is the ancestral condition of hexapods and of arthropods in general.

### 29.2.2. The Subimago of Mayflies

The winged insects (**Pterygota**, [7.17.30](/7.17.30)) do not molt once they have functional wings, with one exception. In mayflies the last nymphal instar molts into a winged subimago (dun), which flies, usually only a short distance from the water, and then molts once more, often within a day, into the sexually active adult (spinner) with clear, shining wings, longer legs and cerci. The subimago is the only winged insect instar that molts. In a few species (for example in the genera *Dolania* and *Palingenia*) the females mate and lay eggs as subimagos and never molt again.

The subimago is usually interpreted as a vestige of the molting adults of the ancestors of winged insects: fossil nymphs of Palaeozoic insects with articulated wing pads suggest that the early pterygotes passed through several winged instars ([7.17.25](/7.17.25#sec-25.1.1)). The development of mayflies has been called prometaboly.

### 29.2.3. Hemimetaboly

In most hemimetabolous insects the wings appear as external wing pads, which grow at each molt and become functional wings at the last one. The young resemble the adult in body plan, compound eyes and mouthparts.

**Definition 29.2.1 (Hemimetaboly)** **Hemimetaboly** (incomplete metamorphosis) is the development of pterygote insects in which the immatures resemble the adults in general body plan, have compound eyes and develop the wings externally as wing pads, and the change to the adult form is completed at the final molt without a pupal stage.

The immatures of hemimetabolous insects have two names in English and Chinese entomology, depending on their habitat and how much they differ from the adult.

**Definition 29.2.2 (Nymph and Naiad)** A **nymph** is an immature stage of a hemimetabolous insect that lives in the same habitat as the adult and resembles it except in size, wings and genitalia (grasshoppers, cockroaches, true bugs). A **naiad** is the aquatic immature stage of a mayfly, dragonfly or stonefly, which differs from the terrestrial or aerial adult in having adaptations for aquatic life, such as tracheal gills, and, in dragonflies, the prehensile labial mask.

In the English literature the word naiad is now rarely used, and the aquatic immatures of mayflies, dragonflies and stoneflies are usually called nymphs or even larvae. Chinese textbooks keep the distinction, and they also distinguish two kinds of hemimetabolous development: paurometaboly, in which nymph and adult share habitat and food, as in grasshoppers, mantises and bugs, and hemimetaboly in the narrow sense, in which aquatic naiads differ from aerial adults, as in dragonflies and stoneflies. Both are hemimetaboly in the sense of Definition 29.2.1; the narrow sense is a matter of ecology, not of developmental mechanism.

Several hemimetabolous groups have evolved one or more non-feeding, quiescent instars before the adult, in which the wings and other adult structures develop internally: thrips have one or two (a "propupa" and a "pupa"), the fourth instar of whiteflies is enclosed in a puparium, and male scale insects pass through a prepupa and a pupa. This development, sometimes called neometaboly, resembles complete metamorphosis, but it evolved independently in Paraneoptera ([7.17.32](/7.17.32#sec-32.1)); the quiescent stages are convergent with the pupa of the Holometabola, not homologous to it.

### 29.2.4. Holometaboly

In the Holometabola the immature and adult stages differ radically, and a pupal stage lies between them.

**Definition 29.2.3 (Holometaboly)** **Holometaboly** (complete metamorphosis) is the development of the Holometabola, in which the larva differs from the adult in form, usually in food and habitat, has no compound eyes (only stemmata) and no external wing pads, the wings and many other adult structures developing internally from imaginal discs; the larva molts into a pupa, a non-feeding instar in which the adult body is formed, and the pupa molts into the adult.

The larva is specialized for feeding and growth, the adult for dispersal and reproduction. A caterpillar is a chewing, leaf-eating tube with stemmata, thoracic legs and abdominal prolegs ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)); the butterfly into which it turns has compound eyes, a coiled sucking proboscis, wings and no prolegs, and drinks nectar. About 85–90% of described insect species are holometabolous ([7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.3)), and their success is often attributed to this separation of functions (Section 29.5).

In some holometabolous insects whose larvae must find a host, successive larval instars differ markedly from one another, a condition called hypermetamorphosis. The blister beetles (Meloidae) are the textbook example. The first instar of *Epicauta*, whose larvae feed on grasshopper eggs, is an active, long-legged triungulin that searches the soil for an egg pod; once it has found one, it molts into a series of fleshy, short-legged, grub-like instars that feed on the eggs; the last of these becomes a rigid, immobile "pseudopupa" (coarctate larva) that overwinters, molts into another active grub and then pupates. In species that develop in bee nests the triungulins climb flowers and cling to visiting bees. Strepsiptera, mantispid lacewings, some parasitoid flies (Acroceridae, Bombyliidae) and wasps have similar active first instars ("planidia") that seek a host, followed by sedentary, legless feeding instars.

Chinese zoology textbooks classify development in exactly these terms. Two statements found there need qualification. First, the parthenogenetic, viviparous aphids are not ovoviviparous: their embryos are nourished by the mother (pseudoplacental viviparity, [7.17.28](/7.17.28#sec-28.4)). Second, the mayfly subimago is described as sexually mature; its gonads are mature, but in most species mating takes place only after the final molt.

## 29.3. Larvae and Pupae

### 29.3.1. Larval Types

Holometabolous larvae are classified by the development of their legs and head. The scheme used in Chinese entomology goes back to Berlese (1913), who interpreted the types as larvae hatching at different stages of embryonic development (Section 29.5):

| Type | Characters | Subtypes and examples |
|---|---|---|
| Protopod | Embryo-like, with rudimentary appendages and incomplete segmentation; living in a nutrient-rich medium | First instars of many endoparasitoid wasps (Platygastridae and others) |
| Polypod | Thoracic legs and abdominal prolegs | Eruciform: caterpillars of Lepidoptera (prolegs with crochets, usually on segments 3–6 and 10), sawfly larvae (prolegs without crochets, on more segments), Mecoptera |
| Oligopod | Thoracic legs, no abdominal prolegs | Campodeiform (long legs and cerci, prognathous, predatory: Neuroptera, Carabidae, Dytiscidae, triungulins); scarabaeiform (C-shaped, fleshy: white grubs of Scarabaeidae); elateriform (slender, hard, short legs: wireworms); platyform (flattened: Psephenidae) |
| Apodous | No legs | Eucephalous (complete head capsule: mosquito larvae, fleas, weevils, Buprestidae, Cerambycidae); hemicephalous (head partly retracted into the thorax: crane flies, horse flies); acephalous (no head capsule, only mouth hooks: maggots of Cyclorrhapha) |

The forms reflect habits rather than ancestry. Apodous larvae evolved many times in insects that live surrounded by their food, inside wood, seeds, fruits, dung or hosts, and polypod larvae among free-living plant feeders that need to grip a leaf; the relationship of the forms to the orders is given in [7.17.33](/7.17.33#sec-33.1). Larval stages may also change during development (hypermetamorphosis, Section 29.2.4).

### 29.3.2. Pupae

**Definition 29.3.1 (Pupa)** The **pupa** is the instar of a holometabolous insect between the last larval instar and the adult: it does not feed, is usually immobile, has external wing pads, legs and antennae of adult form, and within it larval tissues are broken down and the adult body is built from the imaginal discs and other imaginal cells.

The pupa is far from inactive: it is the stage in which the adult is constructed. Pupae are classified by whether the appendages are free, by whether the mandibles are functional and by the presence of a puparium.

**Definition 29.3.2 (Exarate, Obtect and Coarctate Pupae)** An **exarate pupa** has its appendages free from the body (not glued down). An **obtect pupa** has the appendages firmly cemented to the body by a secretion produced at the larval–pupal molt, so that the pupa is a smooth, rigid case. A **coarctate pupa** is an exarate pupa enclosed in a puparium, the hardened, barrel-shaped cuticle of the last larval instar, which is not shed.

Exarate pupae are found in most Coleoptera, Hymenoptera, Neuropterida, Mecoptera, Trichoptera and Siphonaptera; obtect pupae in most Lepidoptera (the chrysalis of butterflies), in nematoceran Diptera (the mobile, comma-shaped pupae of mosquitoes) and in a few beetles (ladybirds). The coarctate pupa is characteristic of the cyclorrhaphan flies (house flies, blow flies, fruit flies), whose puparium opens at the anterior end along a circular line of weakness when the adult emerges by inflating a head sac, the ptilinum. Pupae with functional, articulated mandibles (decticous pupae: Megaloptera, Raphidioptera, Neuroptera, Mecoptera, Trichoptera and the most primitive moths, Micropterigidae) use them to cut their way out of the cocoon or cell; they are always exarate and are thought to be ancestral. All other pupae are adecticous, and the adult escapes by other means. The pupae of caddisflies and of some primitive moths are even mobile, swimming or crawling to the surface before the adult emerges.

Pupae are vulnerable, and most are protected. The larvae of many Lepidoptera, Trichoptera and Hymenoptera spin a cocoon of silk produced by the labial (salivary) glands, the silkworm's cocoon being the most familiar; the larvae of lacewings spin theirs with silk made by the Malpighian tubules and extruded from the anus. Others pupate in a cell in soil or wood, under bark, or hang exposed as chrysalides protected by camouflage or toxicity. The final larval instar usually stops feeding, empties its gut and wanders to a pupation site; this "prepupal" period, often called prepupa in Chinese textbooks, is part of the last larval instar, during which the larva becomes pharate pupa.

### 29.3.3. Inside the Pupa

What happens inside the pupal cuticle differs among tissues and among orders. In the higher flies, most larval tissues are destroyed by programmed cell death and the adult epidermis and appendages are built almost entirely from the imaginal discs and small nests of imaginal cells (histoblasts) that were set aside in the embryo ([7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.4)). In beetles and moths, by contrast, imaginal discs are formed later, some only in the last larval instar, and many larval organs (the Malpighian tubules, parts of the gut and the tracheal system) are remodelled rather than replaced. Larval muscles are mostly broken down and the adult flight muscles develop from myoblasts associated with the discs and the nerves.

The nervous system is remodelled rather than rebuilt. Most larval motor neurons survive metamorphosis, retract their larval branches and grow new ones to the adult muscles, and new neurons produced by neuroblasts during larval life mature in the pupa, forming most of the adult optic lobes and the enlarged **mushroom bodies** ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.6)). Because so much of the neural circuitry persists, some learned associations survive metamorphosis: moths trained as caterpillars to avoid an odour avoid it as adults (Blackiston and colleagues 2008).

## 29.4. Hormonal Control

### 29.4.1. Ecdysteroids and Juvenile Hormone

Molting and metamorphosis are controlled by two effector hormones whose actions were discovered by classical endocrine experiments and are described in [7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.3). The steroid **ecdysone**, made by the prothoracic glands under the stimulation of the brain neuropeptide prothoracicotropic hormone (PTTH) and converted to 20-hydroxyecdysone (20E) in peripheral tissues, initiates each molt. The sesquiterpenoid **juvenile hormone** (JH), made by the corpora allata ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.7)), determines its character: when JH is present at the time of the ecdysteroid rise, the epidermis makes another cuticle of the same kind ("status quo" action); when JH is absent, the epidermis switches to a new program.

In a holometabolous insect this gives three kinds of molt. Larval–larval molts occur with high JH; in the last larval instar JH disappears, and a small "commitment" pulse of ecdysteroid in its absence reprograms the epidermis for pupal development, followed by a large pulse that starts the pupal molt; a brief reappearance of JH before pupation prevents premature adult differentiation, and the pupal–adult molt occurs in the complete absence of JH. In a hemimetabolous insect JH is present in all nymphal molts and absent in the last nymphal instar, whose molt produces the adult. JH thus does not drive metamorphosis; it prevents it, and metamorphosis occurs when it ceases.

Some Chinese textbooks call PTTH a "molt-promoting hormone" and describe it as acting on both the prothoracic glands and the corpora allata; in fact PTTH acts on the prothoracic glands (through the receptor tyrosine kinase Torso), whereas the corpora allata are regulated by other neuropeptides, allatotropins and allatostatins.

### 29.4.2. The Transcriptional Switch

The JH and ecdysone signals converge on a small set of transcription factors that specify the stage-specific programs. Three of them are central in all insects studied (Belles and Santos 2014; Truman and Riddiford 2022):

- *Krüppel homolog 1* (*Kr-h1*), induced by JH through its receptor Methoprene-tolerant (Met); it represses the genes of the next stage and so mediates the status quo action of JH;
- *Broad* (*Br-C*), induced by 20E in the absence of JH, which specifies the pupa in holometabolous insects;
- *E93*, induced by 20E when Kr-h1 is absent, which specifies the adult in both hemimetabolous and holometabolous insects; Kr-h1 represses *E93* directly (the "MEKRE93 pathway": Met → Kr-h1 ⊣ E93).

The differences between hemimetaboly and holometaboly lie largely in when these genes are expressed. In hemimetabolous insects such as the milkweed bug *Oncopeltus* and the cockroach *Blattella*, Broad is expressed throughout nymphal life and promotes the stepwise growth of the wing pads; in the last nymphal instar Kr-h1 disappears, E93 appears and the adult is formed. In holometabolous insects Broad is restricted to the transition from larva to pupa, and a third factor, *chinmo*, maintains the larval state and is repressed by Broad and E93. Knocking down *E93* in the cockroach or the beetle *Tribolium* causes supernumerary nymphal or pupal instars instead of adults; knocking down *Kr-h1* or *Met* in young nymphs or larvae causes precocious metamorphosis. A conserved hormonal system is thus deployed with different timing in the two kinds of development.

### 29.4.3. Hormones as Insecticides

Because JH and ecdysone have no exact counterparts in vertebrates, compounds that mimic or block them are among the most selective insecticides, the "insect growth regulators" ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)):

| Class | Examples | Action |
|---|---|---|
| JH analogues (juvenoids) | Methoprene, pyriproxyfen, fenoxycarb | Applied to last-instar larvae, they prevent normal metamorphosis (supernumerary larvae, larval–pupal intermediates); used against mosquito larvae, fleas, whiteflies and scale insects |
| Ecdysone agonists | Tebufenozide, methoxyfenozide (diacylhydrazines) | Bind the ecdysone receptor of caterpillars and trigger a premature, lethal molt |
| Chitin synthesis inhibitors | Diflubenzuron, hexaflumuron (benzoylureas) | Block the formation of the new cuticle at the molt |
| Anti-JH compounds | Precocenes from the plant *Ageratum* | Destroy the corpora allata, causing precocious metamorphosis in some bugs (Bowers 1976) |

Juvenoids were first found by accident: paper towels made from balsam fir prevented the metamorphosis of European bugs of the genus *Pyrrhocoris* in an American laboratory, because the wood contains juvabione, a JH mimic (Sláma and Williams 1965). Plants thus discovered the endocrine weakness of insects long before chemists did.

## 29.5. The Evolution of Complete Metamorphosis

Complete metamorphosis evolved once, in the ancestor of the Holometabola, and the oldest holometabolous larvae are known from the Late Carboniferous, about 310 million years ago (*Metabolarva bella*, Nel and colleagues 2013); the group thus originated before the Permian. How a larva and a pupa evolved from the nymphs of a hemimetabolous ancestor has been debated for over a century, and three hypotheses have been proposed.

| Hypothesis | Larva corresponds to | Pupa corresponds to | Main evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hinton (1963) and earlier "nymphal" hypotheses | The nymphal instars, with wing development internalized | The final nymphal instar, specialized for the transition to the adult | The pupa resembles the adult; wings are visible on it |
| Berlese (1913), revived as the pronymph hypothesis (Truman and Riddiford 1999) | The pronymph, the embryonic stage of hemimetabolous insects that forms the first cuticle, prolonged and made to feed | All nymphal instars, compressed into one | Hormone titers and Broad expression (Section 29.4.2) |
| Intermediate views | Early nymphs, modified | The last nymphal instars | Neometaboly of Paraneoptera shows that quiescent pre-adult stages evolve readily |

The pronymph hypothesis has gained support from developmental endocrinology. In hemimetabolous embryos the first embryonic cuticle, that of the pronymph, is produced before JH appears, and JH then directs the embryo to form a nymph with adult-like proportions; in holometabolous embryos JH appears earlier, and the embryo is diverted into a larva, a simplified, feeding version of the pronymph. Broad, which in hemimetabolous insects is expressed throughout the nymphal instars, is in holometabolous insects confined to the pupa, as if the nymphal program had been compressed into a single instar. On this view the holometabolous larva is a new, intercalated stage, not a modified nymph, which explains why larvae lack compound eyes and external wing pads and why their form is so different from that of the adult.

Why was holometaboly so successful? The most widely accepted explanation is adaptive decoupling (Moran 1994): because larva and adult are built from largely separate sets of tissues, each can evolve to its own ecological role without compromise, the larva as a feeding machine in a habitat rich in food, the adult as a dispersing and reproducing machine. Larvae and adults of the same species rarely compete for food. Internal wing development also allows larvae to live inside plant tissues, soil or hosts where external wing pads would be damaged. Comparative studies of diversification support the view that holometaboly is a **key innovation** ([7.10.31](/7.10.31#def-31.5.2)), though the four largest orders also owe their diversity to other traits, notably herbivory and parasitism ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)). Whether the pupa itself is an advantage or the price of reorganization is still debated; it is the most vulnerable stage of many insects, and much of the cost of holometaboly is borne in it.

## 29.6. Temperature and Development

### 29.6.1. The Developmental Threshold and the Degree-Day Rule

Insects are **ectotherms**, and the rate of their development depends mainly on temperature. Over the middle range of temperatures experienced by a species, the rate of development, the reciprocal of the developmental time, increases approximately linearly with temperature; extrapolating this line downward defines a temperature at which development would stop.

**Definition 29.6.1 (Developmental Threshold Temperature)** The **developmental threshold temperature** (lower developmental threshold, developmental zero) $C$ of a stage of an insect is the temperature below which no measurable development occurs; operationally it is estimated as the temperature at which the linear regression of developmental rate on temperature reaches zero.

**Definition 29.6.2 (Degree-Day Rule)** The **degree-day rule** (law of effective accumulated temperature, thermal summation rule) states that, within the linear range, a stage of development is completed when the sum of the temperatures above the developmental threshold, accumulated over the time of development, reaches a constant $K$, the thermal constant of the stage:

$$
K = N(T - C),
\tag{29.2}
$$
where $N$ is the developmental time in days at constant temperature $T$ and $K$ is measured in degree-days ($^\circ\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{d}$). Under varying temperatures the rule becomes $\sum_{j} \max(T_j - C, 0) = K$, summed over days $j$.

Equation (29.2) is equivalent to a linear relation between temperature and the rate of development $v = 1/N$,

$$
v = \frac{T - C}{K} = -\frac{C}{K} + \frac{1}{K}\,T,
\tag{29.3}
$$
so that $C$ and $K$ are obtained from a linear regression of $v$ on $T$: the slope is $b = 1/K$ and the intercept $a = -C/K$, giving $K = 1/b$ and $C = -a/b$. Degree-days are a measure of "physiological time": at 20 °C a stage with $C = 10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $K = 100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{d}$ takes 10 days, at 15 °C 20 days, at 30 °C 5 days.

**Example 29.6.1** Eggs of a moth kept at constant temperatures of 15, 20, 25 and 30 °C hatch after 50, 25, 16.7 and 12.5 days. The rates are 0.020, 0.040, 0.060 and 0.080 per day, which lie exactly on a line of slope $b = 0.004\,\mathrm{d^{-1}\,{}^\circ C^{-1}}$ and intercept $a = -0.040\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$. Hence $K = 1/0.004 = 250\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{d}$ and $C = 0.040/0.004 = 10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. If the mean temperature in the field is 18 °C, the eggs should hatch after $250/(18 - 10) \approx 31$ days. With real data the points scatter and the regression gives standard errors for $C$ and $K$ ([3.3](/3.3)); the threshold estimated by extrapolation is usually a few degrees higher than the lowest temperature at which development is actually possible, because the true relationship flattens near the threshold (Section 29.6.2).

Thermal constants are compiled for many pests and are the basis of forecasting in plant protection: from the date at which a stage was observed and a running sum of the daily degree-days above $C$, the date of the next stage can be predicted, and control measures, for example the release of egg parasitoids against corn borers or spraying against hatching caterpillars, timed accordingly. The method was introduced into Chinese plant protection in the 1950s, and the formula $K = N(T - C)$ is a standard part of Chinese entomology textbooks.

### 29.6.2. Nonlinear Models and Fluctuating Temperatures

The linear model fails at both ends. Near the threshold, development continues slowly at temperatures below the extrapolated $C$, so the rate curve is concave upward; above an optimum, typically 28–35 °C, the rate declines sharply and development stops at an upper lethal limit. The whole curve is described by nonlinear models, such as the model of Brière and colleagues (1999),

$$
v(T) = a\,T\,(T - T_{\mathrm{L}})\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{H}} - T}, \qquad T_{\mathrm{L}} \le T \le T_{\mathrm{H}},
\tag{29.4}
$$
with a lower threshold $T_{\mathrm{L}}$, an upper limit $T_{\mathrm{H}}$ and a scale parameter $a$; more mechanistic models (Sharpe and DeMichele 1977; Schoolfield and colleagues 1981) describe the curve by the thermodynamics of a rate-controlling enzyme that is inactivated at high and low temperatures.

Because the rate curve is not linear, the mean temperature does not predict the mean rate. By Jensen's inequality the mean of a convex function exceeds the function of the mean, so insects living in fluctuating temperatures develop faster than at the constant mean temperature where the rate curve is convex (near the threshold) and more slowly where it is concave (near the optimum); this is the Kaufmann effect. In the degree-day method the same consideration requires that degree-days be computed from the daily course of temperature rather than the daily mean. For a day with mean 12 °C and a sinusoidal amplitude of 8 °C and a threshold of 10 °C, the daily mean gives 2.0 degree-days, whereas integrating $\max(T - C, 0)$ over the sinusoidal day gives 3.6; on a day with mean 8 °C the daily mean gives none, the integration 1.6. The single-sine method used in practice (Baskerville and Emin 1969) computes degree-days from the daily minimum and maximum in this way.

(29.4), lower threshold 8 °C, upper limit 36 °C) fitted to the same mid-range; the models agree between about 15 and 28 °C, but the nonlinear curve predicts slow development below C and a decline above the optimum near 30 °C. ### 29.6.3. Voltinism

Temperature, the length of the season and diapause together determine how many generations a species completes in a year.

**Definition 29.6.3 (Voltinism)** **Voltinism** is the number of generations an insect population completes per year: univoltine populations have one, bivoltine two, multivoltine (polyvoltine) more than two, and semivoltine populations need more than one year per generation.

Voltinism often varies within a species along gradients of latitude and altitude. The Asian corn borer *Ostrinia furnacalis*, the most important pest of maize in China, completes one generation a year in the north-east of China and up to six or seven in the far south (Guangxi, Hainan), and the oriental armyworm and many aphids show similar clines. A simple prediction of the number of generations is the annual sum of degree-days divided by the thermal constant of one generation, but real populations usually complete fewer, because the generation that would emerge too late to complete development before winter enters diapause instead (Section 29.7). Very long life cycles occur in insects with poor food: wood-boring beetles may take several years and occasionally more than 20, and the periodical cicadas of eastern North America (*Magicicada*) develop on the xylem sap of tree roots for 13 or 17 years and emerge synchronously in enormous numbers, a long cycle that has been interpreted as an escape from predators that cannot track such rare, prime-numbered events.

Rising temperatures increase the number of generations of multivoltine species, extend the ranges of species limited by heat, and can desynchronize insects from their host plants. Degree-day models, combined with climate scenarios, are used to predict these changes ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)).

## 29.7. Dormancy: Quiescence and Diapause

### 29.7.1. Quiescence and Diapause

Insects in seasonal climates must survive periods of cold, heat or drought in which development and reproduction are impossible. They do so by dormancy, which takes two forms. Quiescence is an immediate response to unfavourable conditions: development slows or stops when the temperature falls below the threshold or water is lacking and resumes as soon as conditions improve. Diapause is different.

**Definition 29.7.1 (Diapause)** **Diapause** is a hormonally controlled state of arrested development or reproduction, with lowered metabolism and increased resistance to environmental extremes, that is entered at a species-specific stage in response to token cues predicting the onset of unfavourable conditions (such as day length), before those conditions arrive, and is not terminated simply by the return of favourable conditions but only after a period of physiological processing (diapause development).

The key property of diapause is anticipation. An insect that waited until winter to stop developing would be caught in an unprotected stage; by responding to day length, which changes in the same way every year, it prepares for winter weeks in advance, accumulating reserves and cryoprotectants and moving to a sheltered site. Diapause can occur in any stage, but each species has a fixed diapause stage:

| Diapause stage | Examples |
|---|---|
| Egg (embryonic diapause) | The silkworm *Bombyx mori* (bivoltine races), the migratory locust (in northern China), gypsy moth, aphids (the overwintering fertilized egg), many grasshoppers and *Aedes* mosquitoes |
| Larva | The European and Asian corn borers (mature larva in the stalk), many caterpillars, some beetles |
| Pupa | Many moths (*Hyalophora cecropia*, *Antheraea*, *Helicoverpa armigera*), flesh flies |
| Adult (reproductive diapause) | Ladybirds, the Colorado potato beetle, mosquitoes of the genus *Culex*, the monarch butterfly (migrant generation) |

Obligatory diapause occurs in every generation regardless of conditions, typically in univoltine species; facultative diapause occurs only in the generation exposed to the inducing cues, as in multivoltine species, in which the summer generations develop without interruption. Diapause passes through phases: induction (the sensitive stage receives the cues, often well before the diapause stage), preparation, maintenance, termination (often requiring a long period of cold, or chilling, in winter) and post-diapause quiescence, in which development waits only for warm temperatures. Diapause in summer, in response to heat or drought, is called aestivation; many ladybirds and some moths aestivate in the mountains or under bark.

### 29.7.2. Photoperiodism and the Critical Photoperiod

Day length is the principal cue of diapause induction in temperate insects, a form of **photoperiodism** ([7.13](/7.13)). The response of a population is measured by rearing samples at a range of constant day lengths and recording the proportion that enters diapause.

**Definition 29.7.2 (Critical Photoperiod)** The **critical photoperiod** (critical day length) of a photoperiodic response is the day length at which 50% of a population enters diapause. In long-day insects, which develop without diapause in long days and enter diapause in short days, diapause is induced when the day length falls below the critical photoperiod.

Most temperate insects are long-day insects, since short days announce winter. Some, which aestivate in summer and develop in the cool season, show the reverse, short-day response. The day length is measured by photoreceptors in the brain, not by the compound eyes ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.5.1)), and the measurement involves the circadian clock: in many insects the photoperiodic response depends on the timing of light relative to an endogenous **circadian rhythm** (the Bünning hypothesis), and mutations in clock genes alter or abolish it. The response typically rises from almost no diapause at long days to nearly complete diapause over a range of only one or two hours, so that a population responds sharply to small changes in day length.

The critical photoperiod increases with latitude. Classical experiments by Danilevskii (1961) on populations of the moth *Acronicta rumicis* from the Black Sea coast to near Saint Petersburg showed an increase of roughly 1–1.5 h for every 5° northward, and similar clines have since been found in many species. The geometry of day length explains the direction of the cline.

**Example 29.7.1** In the northern hemisphere, the day length (sunrise to sunset, including refraction) on 5 August is about 13.5 h at 30°N, 14.1 h at 40°N and 15.0 h at 50°N. A population that should enter diapause on that date, a few weeks before the first frosts, must therefore have a critical photoperiod near 13.5 h at 30°N but near 15.0 h at 50°N. Because winter also arrives earlier in the north, the northern population must in fact enter diapause earlier, at a still longer day length; a critical photoperiod of 13.5 h, appropriate at 30°N, would induce diapause at 50°N only at the end of August, too late. The steep clines found by Danilevskii are thus a predictable result of selection on the timing of diapause. Measured critical photoperiods are usually an hour or more longer than the astronomical day length, because insects perceive part of the twilight as light.

The critical photoperiod is heritable, and it evolves fast. In the pitcher-plant mosquito *Wyeomyia smithii* of North America the critical photoperiod of northern populations decreased measurably between 1972 and 1996, a genetic response to the longer growing seasons of a warming climate (Bradshaw and Holzapfel 2001).

A: photoperiodic response curves of three populations of a long-day insect from 35°, 45° and 55°N (logistic curves with critical photoperiods of 13.5, 15.5 and 17.5 h, steepness modelled on classical clines such as those of Danilevskii 1961): the proportion entering diapause rises from near 0 to near 100% over about two hours of day length. ### 29.7.3. The Endocrine Basis of Diapause

Diapause is a hormonal state, and the hormone involved depends on the stage. Larval and pupal diapause are usually caused by the failure of the brain to release PTTH, so that ecdysone is not produced and no molt occurs. Williams showed this in classic experiments on pupae of the cecropia moth, *Hyalophora cecropia*, in the 1940s and 1950s: a diapausing pupa resumes development only after its brain has been chilled for several weeks, and a chilled brain implanted into a diapausing pupa terminates its diapause. Adult reproductive diapause is caused by the lack of JH, which in the adult acts as a gonadotropin ([7.17.28](/7.17.28#sec-28.7.5)): in short days the corpora allata are inactive, the ovaries do not develop and the fat body stores reserves; injecting JH breaks the diapause.

Egg diapause of the silkworm is controlled by the mother. A neuropeptide, diapause hormone, made by neurosecretory cells of the subesophageal ganglion of the female pupa, acts on the developing ovary and causes the eggs to enter diapause as early embryos. Whether a female releases it depends on the temperature and day length at which she herself was incubated as an embryo: a maternal effect that spans a generation. The commercial importance of silk led to detailed studies of this system in Japan and China, and sericulturists control the production of diapausing and non-diapausing eggs by regulating incubation conditions or by breaking diapause with dilute hydrochloric acid.

### 29.7.4. Cold Hardiness

Insects that overwinter in exposed sites must survive temperatures below the freezing point of their body fluids. Their strategies, discussed physiologically in [7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.7), form a spectrum:

| Strategy | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Freeze avoidance | Remain liquid by **supercooling**: the gut is emptied of ice-nucleating food, ice nucleators are removed from the hemolymph, polyols (glycerol, sorbitol) and sugars (trehalose) and **antifreeze proteins** are accumulated; death occurs at the supercooling point, when ice forms spontaneously | Many overwintering caterpillars and pupae; the goldenrod gall moth *Epiblema scudderiana*; the fire-coloured beetle *Dendroides canadensis* |
| Freeze tolerance | Survive the formation of ice in the extracellular fluids, which is initiated at mild subzero temperatures by ice-nucleating proteins in the hemolymph, so that ice forms slowly and the cells dehydrate instead of freezing; polyols protect membranes | The goldenrod gall fly *Eurosta solidaginis* (larvae survive about −40 °C), some Arctic caterpillars, the Antarctic midge *Belgica antarctica* |
| Chill tolerance and chill susceptibility | Supercool but are damaged by prolonged cold above the supercooling point | Many temperate insects; summer aphids |

A curious relative of cold hardiness is anhydrobiosis. Larvae of the African chironomid *Polypedilum vanderplanki*, which live in rock pools that dry out, lose almost all their body water, replacing it by the sugar trehalose, which vitrifies; in this state they survive for years and tolerate temperatures from about −270 to over 100 °C, and they revive within an hour of being wetted.

## 29.8. Migration

### 29.8.1. Migratory Flight

Diapause allows an insect to wait in place for good conditions; migration takes it to them.

**Definition 29.8.1 (Insect Migration)** **Insect migration** is persistent, straightened-out movement, undistracted by resources and other stimuli that would normally stop the insect (food, mates, oviposition sites), effected by the insect's own locomotion or by its active embarkation on wind or water currents, and associated with a distinct physiological state and with behaviours that precede and follow the movement; it typically carries a population to a different region where it breeds, and its progeny may return.

The definition, following the behavioural concept of Kennedy and Dingle, emphasizes the state of the migrant rather than the distance: an aphid that rises from a crop and lets itself be carried a few kilometres by the wind is migrating, a butterfly that patrols a meadow all day is not. Migrants are often young adults in a pre-reproductive state, in which energy is directed to flight muscles and fat rather than to the ovaries, a pattern called the oogenesis–flight syndrome (Johnson 1969); juvenile hormone, low during migration and rising at its end, links the two. Many migrants fly in reproductive diapause.

Most migrating insects are carried by the wind. Small insects whose airspeed is less than the wind speed can direct their movement only within the "flight boundary layer" near the ground (Taylor 1974); above it they drift. Larger migrants, moths, butterflies, locusts and dragonflies, select altitudes and nights with favourable winds and orient within the airstream, partly compensating for drift. Radar studies have shown that the scale of windborne migration is enormous: about 3.5 trillion insects, weighing some 3,200 tonnes, migrate each year at high altitude over southern Britain alone (Hu and colleagues 2016).

**Example 29.8.1** A noctuid moth flies at an airspeed of $4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ at an altitude of 500 m, where the nocturnal low-level jet blows from the south-west at $12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. If the moth heads north-east, downwind, its ground speed is $16\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and in a flight of 8 hours it covers $16 \times 3600 \times 8 \approx 460\,\mathrm{km}$. If it heads north, 45° from the wind, its ground speed is $\sqrt{12^2 + 4^2 + 2\cdot12\cdot4\cos45^\circ} \approx 15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, so it loses little distance but turns its track about 11° towards the north. Three or four such nights carry a migrant across eastern China from the Yangtze to the north-east plain. Selecting favourable winds is therefore far more effective than flying fast: the moth's own airspeed contributes only a quarter of the distance.

### 29.8.2. Migrants of East Asia and Elsewhere

East Asia has a system of insect migration driven by the monsoon. Several of the most important crop pests of China cannot overwinter in the north and reinvade it every year from the south:

- The oriental armyworm, *Mythimna separata* (Noctuidae), overwinters only south of about 33°N. In spring the moths migrate north in several waves, generation by generation, reaching the north-east of China and the Korean peninsula in summer, and return south in autumn. Its long-distance migration was established in the early 1960s by Chinese entomologists through mass marking and recapture of moths over much of eastern China, one of the first demonstrations of long-distance insect migration.
- The brown planthopper, *Nilaparvata lugens*, and the white-backed planthopper, *Sogatella furcifera* (Delphacidae), which cannot overwinter north of about 25°N, are carried each spring and early summer from Indochina and southern China to the Yangtze valley, Korea and Japan on the south-westerly winds of the monsoon, and migrate back in autumn; the rice leaf roller *Cnaphalocrocis medinalis* follows a similar route. A radar station on an island in the Bohai Strait has monitored the passage of many such species between northern and north-eastern China.
- The fall armyworm, *Spodoptera frugiperda*, an American species that reached China from Myanmar in 2019, spread over much of China within a year by the same seasonal migration routes.

Elsewhere, the monarch butterfly, *Danaus plexippus*, of eastern North America migrates up to about 4,000 km in autumn to a few overwintering sites in the fir forests of central Mexico, where it clusters by the millions in reproductive diapause; it navigates with a time-compensated sun compass, whose clock lies partly in the antennae, and a magnetic compass, and the return to the north in spring takes several generations. The painted lady, *Vanessa cardui*, migrates in successive generations between tropical Africa and Europe in an annual circuit of over 10,000 km, and the globe skimmer dragonfly, *Pantala flavescens*, follows the intertropical convergence zone across the Indian Ocean.

## 29.9. Polyphenism

### 29.9.1. Phase Polyphenism of Locusts

Many insects produce alternative forms in response to environmental conditions, a phenomenon called **polyphenism** ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)). The best-known case is that of locusts, grasshoppers of a few species of Acrididae that change their behaviour, colour, morphology and physiology with population density.

| Character | Solitarious phase | Gregarious phase |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviour | Avoids other locusts; nymphs cryptic, adults fly at night | Attracted to others; nymphs march in bands, adults fly in swarms by day |
| Nymphal colour | Green or brown, matching the vegetation | Black pattern on yellow or orange (aposematic in the desert locust) |
| Adult morphology | Convex pronotal crest; hind femora long relative to the tegmina and head | Flat or concave pronotum; relatively longer tegmina (higher E/F ratio), shorter femora |
| Physiology | Fewer, larger eggs; more nymphal instars in some species | More active, higher lipid stores for flight; eggs fewer per pod but larger hatchlings in some species |

The change is graded and cumulative. In the desert locust, *Schistocerca gregaria*, crowding switches solitarious nymphs to gregarious behaviour within a few hours; the trigger is tactile stimulation of the hind femora by other locusts, together with their sight and smell, and the switch is mediated by a rise of serotonin in the thoracic ganglia (Anstey and colleagues 2009). In the migratory locust, *Locusta migratoria*, dopamine signalling and the aggregation pheromone 4-vinylanisole ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.4.3)) play the corresponding roles (Guo and colleagues 2020). Colour and morphology change only over successive molts, and the phase state is partly transmitted to the next generation through factors added by the mother to the eggs and egg pod.

The East Asian migratory locust, *Locusta migratoria manilensis* ([7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.1.3)), devastated cereal crops in China for millennia; Chinese historical records document hundreds of outbreaks. Ecological studies in the 1950s showed that outbreaks arose in the reed-covered floodplains and lake shores of the North China Plain, where alternating flooding and drought create bare breeding grounds, and the transformation of these habitats by water control and cultivation, together with monitoring and chemical control, greatly reduced outbreaks.

### 29.9.2. Wing Polyphenism and Seasonal Forms

Wing polyphenism is common among insects that live on patchy resources. Parthenogenetic aphids produce winged daughters when the colony is crowded, the host plant deteriorates or predators appear (aphids that perceive their alarm pheromone, (*E*)-β-farnesene, produce more winged offspring), and wingless ones otherwise; the decision is made by the mother and acts on her embryos ([7.17.28](/7.17.28#sec-28.5.2)). Wingless morphs reproduce earlier and more, winged ones disperse, a trade-off that is regulated by JH and ecdysteroid signalling. Planthoppers, crickets and water striders show similar density- or photoperiod-dependent dimorphisms between short-winged and long-winged forms; in the brown planthopper the long-winged form, which migrates (Section 29.8.2), is produced under crowding through the insulin signalling pathway.

Seasonal polyphenism produces different forms in different seasons. The European map butterfly, *Araschnia levana*, has an orange spring form (*levana*), whose larvae developed in short days and overwintered as diapausing pupae, and a black-and-white summer form (*prorsa*), produced in long days; the difference is caused by the timing of the ecdysteroid release in the pupa. The African butterfly *Bicyclus anynana* has large eyespots in the wet season and small ones in the dry season, a response to developmental temperature also mediated by ecdysteroids, which has made it a model of the developmental basis of plasticity ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)). The castes of social insects are the most elaborate polyphenisms of all ([7.17.38](/7.17.38)); the dimorphic males of horned beetles were described in [7.17.28](/7.17.28#sec-28.1.2).

## Questions

1. A larva of an unknown beetle has a head capsule 2.1 mm wide. In the same species the first-instar head width is 0.5 mm and the growth ratio is 1.6. Which instar is it, and how would you check the assignment?

   **Answer** By Eq. (29.1), $i = 1 + \ln(x_i/x_1)/\ln g = 1 + \ln 4.2/\ln 1.6 = 1 + 1.435/0.470 \approx 4.05$, so the larva is in the fourth instar (predicted width $0.5 \times 1.6^3 = 2.05\,\mathrm{mm}$). To check, measure many larvae: the widths should cluster into discrete classes whose logarithms are evenly spaced. A gap twice the usual ratio would reveal an unsampled instar, and a continuous distribution would indicate that the growth ratio is variable (for example with food quality), in which case the rule cannot assign individuals reliably.

2. Why is the subimago of mayflies considered an ancestral feature of winged insects rather than a specialization of mayflies?

   **Answer** All other arthropods, including the wingless insects, continue to molt as adults, so molting in a winged stage is the expected ancestral condition and its absence in other pterygotes a derived loss. Fossil nymphs of Palaeozoic insects had articulated wing pads, suggesting several winged instars in early pterygotes. The subimago is thus interpreted as the last remnant of a series of winged molts, retained in the oldest surviving lineage of winged insects, rather than a new stage. Its short duration and the tendency of some mayflies to mate as subimagos suggest that it is being reduced.

3. In what sense is the "pupa" of thrips or whiteflies not a pupa?

   **Answer** It is a non-feeding, quiescent instar before the adult in which wings and adult structures develop internally, and functionally it resembles the pupa of holometabolous insects. But thrips and whiteflies belong to the Paraneoptera, whose other members (bugs, bark lice) are hemimetabolous, and the Holometabola form a separate clade; the quiescent stages therefore evolved independently, by convergence, and are not homologous to the holometabolan pupa. They also arise in the last immature instars of a series of nymphs, whereas the holometabolan larva has no compound eyes or external wing pads.

4. (Calculation) Using Example 29.6.1 ($C = 10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $K = 250\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{d}$), predict the hatching time in a week of constant 14 °C followed by constant 22 °C. Then explain why the prediction might be wrong if the 14 °C were a mean of nights at 6 °C and days at 22 °C.

   **Answer** In the first week the eggs accumulate $7 \times (14 - 10) = 28$ degree-days. The remaining $222$ degree-days at 22 °C require $222/12 = 18.5$ days, so hatching occurs about 25.5 days after the start. If the temperature fluctuated between 6 and 22 °C, the hours below 10 °C contribute nothing in the degree-day sum but the hours above contribute more than the mean suggests: with a rectangular day–night cycle of equal halves, each day gives $0.5 \times 0 + 0.5 \times 12 = 6$ degree-days instead of 4, and the first week gives 42. Moreover, near the threshold the true rate curve is curved (Kaufmann effect), so the linear model is only an approximation; field degree-days should be computed from the daily temperature course.

5. Why must an insect use day length, rather than temperature, as the main cue to enter diapause, and why does the critical photoperiod increase with latitude?

   **Answer** Temperature fluctuates irregularly from day to day and year to year, so a warm spell in autumn could make an insect continue to develop until it is caught by frost in a vulnerable stage; day length changes in exactly the same way every year and predicts the approach of winter weeks in advance. At higher latitudes summer days are longer and winter arrives earlier, so a population must enter diapause at a date when the day length is still long: in Example 29.7.1, the day length on 5 August increases from 13.5 h at 30°N to 15.0 h at 50°N, and the northern population must respond even earlier. Selection therefore produces critical photoperiods that increase northward by roughly 1–1.5 h per 5° of latitude.

6. A pest moth has a critical photoperiod of 14 h. A warming climate lengthens the growing season, but the photoperiod of a given date does not change. Predict the evolutionary response and name an observed example.

   **Answer** With a fixed critical photoperiod the population would enter diapause on the same date as before, although the warmer autumn would allow another generation, or at least further growth, before winter. Selection should favour genotypes that enter diapause later, at shorter day lengths, so the critical photoperiod should decrease, and voltinism may increase. This has been observed in the pitcher-plant mosquito *Wyeomyia smithii*, whose northern populations evolved shorter critical photoperiods between 1972 and 1996 (Bradshaw and Holzapfel 2001), a genetic rather than plastic response to climate change.

7. (Calculation) A planthopper migrating on a south-westerly low-level jet of $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ cannot fly against the wind (airspeed about $0.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$). How far can it travel in a 30-hour flight, and what determines where it lands?

   **Answer** Its ground speed is essentially the wind speed, $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, giving $15 \times 3600 \times 30 \approx 1600\,\mathrm{km}$, the distance from northern Vietnam to the Yangtze valley or beyond; its own airspeed changes this by only about 5%. Where it lands is determined by the weather: migrants descend at dawn, when they reach the limit of their flight time, or are brought down by rain and descending air, for example along the monsoon front, which is why planthopper immigrations into a region are concentrated on particular nights and can be forecast from wind trajectories.

8. In what respects do locust phases illustrate the difference between polyphenism and genetic polymorphism?

   **Answer** Solitarious and gregarious locusts can be produced from the same genotype, even from the same egg pod, by rearing them at different densities, and a solitarious nymph can be switched to gregarious behaviour within hours by crowding; the forms are therefore environmentally induced alternatives of one genotype, not alleles in a population. Their genetic basis lies in the developmental and neural machinery that responds to density (serotonin, dopamine, pheromone reception), which is the target of selection. In a genetic polymorphism, by contrast, alternative forms are determined by different alleles and cannot be switched by the environment.
