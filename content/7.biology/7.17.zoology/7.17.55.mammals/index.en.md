Mammals are the synapsids that survived ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)). About 6 700 living species are known, from the bumblebee bat (*Craseonycteris thonglongyai*, about 2 g) and the Etruscan shrew (*Suncus etruscus*, under 2 g) to the blue whale (*Balaenoptera musculus*), at more than 150 t the largest animal that has ever lived. They live on every continent and in every ocean, fly (bats), glide, burrow, swim and dive to 3 000 m (Cuvier's beaked whale), and are the dominant large animals of most terrestrial ecosystems. Humans are mammals, and much of medicine, agriculture and human history depends on other mammals: our domestic animals, our laboratory models and the reservoirs of many of our diseases.

This chapter first lists the characters of mammals (§55.1) and their teeth (§55.2), on which mammal classification has long rested. It then treats the three living lineages: monotremes (§55.3), marsupials (§55.4) and placentals (§55.5), with the four superorders of placentals and their orders. Section 55.6 describes foot posture and locomotion; §55.7 the primates; §55.8 the domestic rabbit, the representative mammal of Chinese textbooks; §55.9 the mammals of China and their conservation; and §55.10 the correspondence with the traditional classification. The physiology of mammals is treated throughout 7.14, the placenta in [7.14.38](/7.14.38#sec-38.9); the comparative anatomy of each organ system in [7.17.57](/7.17.57)–[7.17.68](/7.17.68); human evolution in [7.10.33](/7.10.33).

## 55.1. Characters of Mammals

### 55.1.1. Definition and Characters

**Definition 55.1.1 (Mammalia)** The **Mammalia** (mammals) are the crown group of synapsids: the last common ancestor of the living monotremes and therians and all its descendants. Mammals have hair; mammary glands that secrete milk; sweat and sebaceous glands; a lower jaw formed by the dentary alone, articulating with the squamosal; three middle-ear ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes); heterodont teeth in sockets replaced once (diphyodont); two occipital condyles; a muscular diaphragm; a four-chambered heart with a left aortic arch; red blood cells without nuclei in the circulation; and a neocortex in the cerebral hemispheres.

The characters are distributed over all organ systems, and most are compared with those of other vertebrates in the comparative chapters:

| System | Mammalian characters | Compared in |
|---|---|---|
| Integument | Hair (guard hairs, underfur, vibrissae); sweat (eccrine and apocrine), sebaceous, scent and mammary glands; claws, nails, hooves; horns and antlers in some | [7.17.57](/7.17.57) |
| Skull | Dentary only; dentary–squamosal joint; three ossicles; secondary palate; two occipital condyles; zygomatic arch; enlarged braincase | [7.17.59](/7.17.59) |
| Teeth | Heterodont (incisors, canines, premolars, molars); thecodont; diphyodont; precise occlusion (§55.2) | [7.17.63](/7.17.63) |
| Axial skeleton | Seven cervical vertebrae in almost all species; thoracic, lumbar, sacral and caudal regions; atlas–axis joint for rotation | [7.17.60](/7.17.60) |
| Appendicular skeleton | Limbs beneath the body; mobile scapula with a spine; pelvis of fused ilium, ischium and pubis (innominate) | [7.17.61](/7.17.61) |
| Respiration and circulation | Diaphragm; branching bronchial tree ending in alveoli; four-chambered heart with left aortic arch; anucleate red blood cells | [7.17.64](/7.17.64), [7.17.65](/7.17.65) |

The constancy of the number of cervical vertebrae is remarkable: giraffes and mice both have seven. The only exceptions are sloths (six to ten) and manatees (six). Galis (1999) proposed that the number is constrained because changes in the *Hox* genes that specify the identity of cervical vertebrae are linked to early developmental defects and childhood cancers in mammals; birds and reptiles, whose cervical counts vary, do not show this linkage ([7.17.60](/7.17.60)).

Mammals are **endotherms** with body temperatures mostly between 35 and 39 °C, lower in monotremes (about 32 °C) and in many marsupials and xenarthrans. They maintain them with insulation (fur, fat), shivering and non-shivering heat production in brown fat (a mammalian innovation), and lose heat by sweating, panting and blood flow to the skin ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)). Many small mammals save energy by daily torpor or hibernation.

### 55.1.2. Body Size and Life History

Mammals vary in body mass by a factor of about 100 million, and many features of their biology scale with mass. Basal metabolic rate scales approximately as $M^{3/4}$ (**Kleiber's law**, [7.14.30](/7.14.30#sec-30.3.1)), so the metabolic rate per gram falls as $M^{-1/4}$: a gram of shrew burns energy about 30 times faster than a gram of elephant. Life-history traits scale in parallel: gestation time, age at maturity and maximum life span all increase roughly as $M^{1/4}$, while heart rate and breathing rate decrease as $M^{-1/4}$, so that most mammals live for a similar number of heartbeats (on the order of $10^9$). A mouse matures in six weeks and lives two years; an elephant matures at 10–15 years and lives 60–70.

At a given body mass, however, mammals differ widely along a "fast–slow" continuum. Bats, for example, live about three times as long as non-flying mammals of the same size (the Brandt's bat *Myotis brandtii*, weighing 7 g, has been recorded at 41 years), and so do naked mole-rats and primates; species with low extrinsic mortality — fliers, burrowers, those living in trees or on islands, and large-brained species — tend to have slow life histories, as predicted by life-history theory ([7.10.15](/7.10.15)). Litter size ranges from one (most primates, bats, whales, ungulates) to more than 20 (the tenrec *Tenrec ecaudatus* can bear 32), and the neonates range from altricial (blind, naked young of rodents, carnivorans, rabbits) to highly precocial (ungulates, guinea pigs, whales, which can walk or swim within hours of birth).

## 55.2. Teeth

### 55.2.1. Tooth Classes and the Dental Formula

Mammalian teeth are of four kinds, distinguished by position as much as by shape: *incisors* in the premaxilla and the front of the dentary, *canines* (one on each side of each jaw) at the front of the maxilla and behind the incisors below, *premolars* (which have deciduous predecessors) and *molars* (which do not, erupting behind the deciduous teeth). Because the number of each kind is fixed within a species and differs among groups, it is a classic tool of mammalian taxonomy.

**Definition 55.2.1 (Dental formula)** The **dental formula** of a mammal gives the number of incisors, canines, premolars and molars on one side of the upper and lower jaws, written as $\mathrm{I}\,\frac{i}{i'}\,\mathrm{C}\,\frac{c}{c'}\,\mathrm{P}\,\frac{p}{p'}\,\mathrm{M}\,\frac{m}{m'}$, or as $\frac{i.c.p.m}{i'.c'.p'.m'}$; the total number of teeth is twice the sum of all the numbers.

The ancestral formula of placental mammals is $\frac{3.1.4.3}{3.1.4.3} = 44$, retained in the pig and the mole; that of marsupials is $\frac{5.1.3.4}{4.1.3.4} = 50$, as in the opossum. In most lineages teeth have been lost:

| Species | Formula | Total |
|---|---|---|
| Pig (*Sus scrofa*) | 3.1.4.3 / 3.1.4.3 | 44 |
| Dog (*Canis familiaris*) | 3.1.4.2 / 3.1.4.3 | 42 |
| Cat (*Felis catus*) | 3.1.3.1 / 3.1.2.1 | 30 |
| Human, Old World monkeys, apes | 2.1.2.3 / 2.1.2.3 | 32 |
| New World monkeys (most) | 2.1.3.3 / 2.1.3.3 | 36 |
| Horse (*Equus caballus*) | 3.1.3–4.3 / 3.1.3.3 | 40–42 (canines often absent in mares) |
| Cattle, sheep (ruminants) | 0.0.3.3 / 3.1.3.3 | 32 (the lower canine incisiform; the upper incisors replaced by a horny pad) |
| Rabbit (*Oryctolagus cuniculus*) | 2.0.3.3 / 1.0.2.3 | 28 |
| Rat, mouse | 1.0.0.3 / 1.0.0.3 | 16 |
| Opossum (*Didelphis*) | 5.1.3.4 / 4.1.3.4 | 50 |

Some mammals have departed from the pattern altogether. Toothed whales have many identical, peg-like teeth (homodonty, up to about 260 in some dolphins) and do not replace them; baleen whales have no teeth as adults; anteaters and pangolins have none; elephants have one pair of upper incisors (the tusks) and molars that are replaced horizontally, one after another from the back of the jaw, six in a lifetime. The narwhal's tusk is a spiralled upper canine, usually only the left, in males.

### 55.2.2. Tooth Types and Diet

The shape of the cheek teeth reflects diet:

- *Tribosphenic* molars, with cutting and crushing parts ([7.17.54](/7.17.54#sec-54.4.1)), are retained in insectivorous marsupials, shrews, hedgehogs and many bats.
- *Bunodont* molars, with low, rounded cusps, crush and grind mixed food: pigs, bears, primates including humans.
- *Selenodont* molars, with crescent-shaped ridges of enamel on the cusps, grind tough plant material with sideways jaw movement: ruminants, camels.
- *Lophodont* molars, with cusps joined into transverse ridges (lophs), shear and grind plants: rhinoceroses, tapirs, kangaroos; elephants have molars made of many plates (lamellae).
- *Secodont* (sectorial) teeth, with blade-like crests, cut flesh: in carnivorans the upper fourth premolar and lower first molar form a pair of *carnassials* that slice like scissors.

![(A) The heterodont dentition of a generalized placental mammal (dental formula I 3/3, C 1/1, P 4/4, M 3/3 = 44, close to that of the pig), with tooth classes coloured, and the dental formulae of some representative species. (B) Crown views of the main types of molars (schematic): tribosphenic, with paracone, metacone and protocone; bunodont, with low rounded cusps; selenodont, with crescent-shaped ridges; lophodont, with transverse ridges; and the secodont carnassial blade of carnivorans.](./plots/teeth.py)

Herbivores that eat abrasive food have high-crowned (*hypsodont*) cheek teeth, which wear down slowly over a lifetime, instead of the low-crowned (*brachydont*) teeth of omnivores and browsers. Hypsodonty evolved in many grazing lineages (horses, bovids, rodents) as grasslands spread in the Miocene, 20–10 million years ago, and as silica-rich grasses and dust abraded their teeth. In rodents and lagomorphs, and in the cheek teeth of some voles, the teeth grow continuously from open roots (hypselodont, ever-growing teeth); the incisors of rodents have enamel only on the front surface, so the softer dentine behind wears faster and keeps the edge chisel-sharp.

## 55.3. Monotremes

**Definition 55.3.1 (Monotremata)** The **Monotremata** (monotremes) are the egg-laying mammals: the platypus and the echidnas, the surviving members of the australosphenidan lineage. They lay leathery-shelled eggs; have a cloaca into which the intestinal, urinary and reproductive tracts open; secrete milk from mammary glands without nipples; retain an interclavicle, coracoid and procoracoid in the pectoral girdle and epipubic bones in the pelvis; and lack teeth as adults, the jaws bearing horny pads or a beak.

Only five species survive, in Australia and New Guinea: the platypus (*Ornithorhynchus anatinus*), the short-beaked echidna (*Tachyglossus aculeatus*) and three long-beaked echidnas (*Zaglossus*) of New Guinea. They are not "primitive mammals" in every respect: they are highly specialized survivors of a lineage that separated from therians in the Jurassic.

*The platypus* is a semi-aquatic animal of the streams of eastern Australia, about 50 cm long. It dives with eyes, ears and nostrils closed and finds its prey — insect larvae, crustaceans, worms — with its bill, which carries about 40 000 electroreceptors and 60 000 mechanoreceptors; the electroreceptors detect the weak electric fields generated by the muscles of prey, and the bill is swept from side to side to locate them ([7.14.43](/7.14.43)). Males have a spur on each hind ankle connected to a venom gland, active in the breeding season and probably used in fights between males; the venom causes intense pain in humans. The juveniles have molar teeth, lost as they grow. The platypus genome (Warren et al. 2008; Zhou et al. 2021) is a mosaic of reptile-like, bird-like and mammal-like features; platypuses have ten sex chromosomes (five X and five Y in males) forming a chain at meiosis, and their sex-determining system shares genes with the Z chromosome of birds rather than with the X of therians.

*Echidnas* are terrestrial, covered with spines and fur, and feed on ants and termites with a long sticky tongue; the short-beaked echidna is widespread in Australia. Their beak is also electrosensitive, but with far fewer receptors than the platypus bill.

*Reproduction.* Monotremes lay one to three small eggs (about 1.5 cm long) with a leathery shell, after a period of about 3 weeks in the uterus during which the egg grows by absorbing uterine secretions. The platypus incubates its eggs in a nesting burrow for about 10 days, curled around them; the echidna lays its single egg directly into a temporary pouch on its belly. The hatchling is tiny (about 0.3–0.4 g) and undeveloped, like a newborn marsupial, and feeds on milk that oozes from glandular areola patches with hair on the mother's belly, from which it laps or sucks it. Lactation lasts 3–4 months in the platypus and up to 7 months in the echidna. Monotremes have a body temperature of about 32 °C, a low metabolic rate, and echidnas hibernate.

The fossil record of monotremes begins in the Early Cretaceous of Australia (*Steropodon*, *Teinolophos*, about 110–120 million years ago); the Miocene platypus *Obdurodon* retained teeth as an adult, and a fossil platypus tooth from Paleocene Patagonia (*Monotrematum*) shows that monotremes once lived in South America, when it was connected to Australia through Antarctica.

## 55.4. Marsupials

### 55.4.1. Characters and Reproduction

**Definition 55.4.1 (Marsupialia)** The **Marsupialia** (marsupials) are the crown group of metatherians: the living opossums, shrew opossums, monito del monte and the Australasian marsupials, with their common ancestor and all its descendants. Metatherians and marsupials give birth after a short gestation to very small, undeveloped young that complete their development attached to a teat, often in a pouch (marsupium); the placenta is formed mainly by the yolk sac; females have paired uteri and lateral vaginae; and epipubic bones project forward from the pelvis.

The distinctive feature of marsupials is their mode of reproduction. Gestation is short — 12–13 days in the Virginia opossum (*Didelphis virginiana*), 33 days in the red kangaroo (*Osphranter rufus*), shorter than the oestrous cycle in many species. The placenta is formed by the yolk sac against the uterine wall (a choriovitelline placenta); only bandicoots form a chorioallantoic placenta as well, late in gestation ([7.14.38](/7.14.38#sec-38.9.3)). The newborn is tiny (about 0.8 g in the red kangaroo, a body mass less than 1/30 000 of the mother's), with an embryonic hindlimb, unfused eyelids and undeveloped lungs and kidneys, but with strong forelimbs, a precocious tongue and a developed sense of smell. It climbs unaided from the urogenital opening to a teat, which it takes in its mouth; the teat swells so that the newborn becomes firmly attached for weeks.

Development is then completed during a long lactation, during which the composition of the milk changes from dilute, carbohydrate-rich milk for the attached young to lipid-rich milk for the joey that leaves the pouch. A female red kangaroo can feed two young of different ages from different teats at the same time with different milks (concurrent asynchronous lactation), and she may carry a third offspring as a dormant blastocyst. This **embryonic diapause** is controlled by the suckling of the young in the pouch: suckling stimulates prolactin, which suppresses the corpus luteum and keeps the blastocyst dormant; when the joey leaves the pouch or dies, the blastocyst resumes development, and a new young is born a month later. Marsupial reproduction thus invests little in pregnancy and much in lactation, so a female can abandon a young cheaply in drought, which may be an advantage in the unpredictable Australian climate.

The female reproductive tract is double: two uteri and two lateral vaginae, used to transport sperm, and a median birth canal (pseudovaginal canal) that forms before each birth (and permanently in kangaroos). Males of many species have a bifid penis and a scrotum in front of the penis. Epipubic bones, also present in monotremes and Mesozoic eutherians, support the abdominal wall and may help in locomotion.

### 55.4.2. Diversity and Biogeography

About 380 species of marsupials live today:

| Order | Distribution | Representatives |
|---|---|---|
| Didelphimorphia | Americas | Opossums (~120 species), including the Virginia opossum, the only marsupial of North America |
| Paucituberculata | Andes | Shrew opossums (7) |
| Microbiotheria | Chile and Argentina | Monito del monte (*Dromiciops*), closer to Australian marsupials than to other American ones |
| Dasyuromorphia | Australia, New Guinea | Quolls, dunnarts, the numbat (*Myrmecobius*), the Tasmanian devil (*Sarcophilus harrisii*), the extinct thylacine (*Thylacinus cynocephalus*) |
| Peramelemorphia | Australia, New Guinea | Bandicoots and bilbies |
| Notoryctemorphia | Australian deserts | Marsupial moles (*Notoryctes*) |
| Diprotodontia | Australia, New Guinea | Kangaroos and wallabies, koala, wombats, possums and gliders (*Petaurus*), the honey possum; extinct *Diprotodon* (2.7 t) |

Marsupials (metatherians) arose in the Northern Hemisphere, where Cretaceous metatherians were common in North America and Asia, and reached South America in the latest Cretaceous or Paleocene. From South America they crossed Antarctica, then still forested and connected to both continents, and arrived in Australia by the Early Eocene (about 55 million years ago, the fossils of Murgon, Queensland). When Australia separated from Antarctica, about 45–35 million years ago, its marsupials evolved in isolation from placentals other than bats and, much later, rodents that arrived from Asia. They radiated into a remarkable variety of forms that parallel placental mammals elsewhere — one of the most striking examples of **convergent evolution**:

![Convergent evolution of Australian marsupials and placental mammals of other continents (silhouettes, not to scale): the thylacine and the grey wolf, cursorial carnivores; the sugar glider and the flying squirrel, gliders with a membrane between fore- and hindlimbs; the marsupial mole and the mole, burrowers with reduced eyes; the numbat and the giant anteater, eaters of ants and termites.](./plots/convergence.py)

The convergence extends to details: the skull of the thylacine is so similar to that of a wolf that students confuse them, although it has marsupial characters such as inflected angles of the lower jaw and palatal vacuities; the gliding membrane of possums evolved independently at least three times among Australian marsupials. Other niches were filled differently: kangaroos, the large herbivores of Australia, hop on their hindlimbs, a gait that becomes more economical as speed increases because energy is stored in the long tendons.

Australia lost almost all of its megafauna (marsupials over about 45 kg, giant birds and reptiles) between about 50 000 and 40 000 years ago, soon after human arrival, and since European settlement more than 30 Australian mammal species have become extinct, the highest rate in the world, mostly because of introduced cats and foxes and habitat change. The thylacine was hunted to extinction; the last known individual died in Hobart Zoo in 1936.

## 55.5. Placental Mammals

### 55.5.1. Characters and the Four Superorders

**Definition 55.5.1 (Eutheria)** The **Eutheria** (eutherians) are the therian mammals more closely related to placental mammals than to marsupials; their crown group, the Placentalia (placental mammals), comprises all living eutherians. Placental mammals have a long gestation during which the embryo is nourished through a chorioallantoic placenta; the trophoblast protects the embryo from rejection by the mother's immune system; they lack epipubic bones; and the young are born at a more advanced stage than those of marsupials.

The placenta varies greatly among placentals, in the shape of the contact zone (diffuse in pigs and horses, cotyledonary in ruminants, zonary in carnivorans, discoid in rodents and primates) and in the number of tissue layers between maternal and fetal blood (epitheliochorial in pigs, endotheliochorial in carnivorans, haemochorial in rodents and primates); the capture of retroviral envelope genes (syncytins) for forming the syncytiotrophoblast happened independently in several lineages ([7.14.38](/7.14.38#sec-38.9.2)).

The classification of placental mammals was transformed in the late 1990s and early 2000s, when molecular phylogenies (Madsen et al. 2001; Murphy et al. 2001) revealed four major groups that partly contradicted the traditional orders based on morphology, but corresponded well to geography:

- *Afrotheria*, which evolved in Africa when it was an island continent: elephants, sirenians, hyraxes, the aardvark, elephant shrews, tenrecs and golden moles.
- *Xenarthra*, which evolved in South America: armadillos, sloths and anteaters.
- *Euarchontoglires*: rodents, lagomorphs, treeshrews, colugos and primates.
- *Laurasiatheria*: shrews, moles and hedgehogs, bats, pangolins, carnivorans, perissodactyls and cetartiodactyls (including whales).

Euarchontoglires and Laurasiatheria together form the Boreoeutheria, the placentals of the northern continents; Afrotheria and Xenarthra are usually grouped as Atlantogenata, which reflects the opening of the Atlantic Ocean between Africa and South America. Whether the root of the placental tree lies between Atlantogenata and Boreoeutheria, or within one of these groups, was hard to resolve, because the four groups diverged within a few million years in the Late Cretaceous, about 90–80 million years ago, according to molecular clocks. The fossil record shows few undoubted placentals before the K–Pg boundary, and a rapid radiation of placental body forms in the Paleocene and Eocene after it.

### 55.5.2. Afrotheria

Afrotherians are so different from one another in form — elephants and golden moles, sea cows and elephant shrews — that only molecular data revealed their kinship. They share a few anatomical features, such as a late eruption of the permanent teeth and a high number of thoracolumbar vertebrae.

- *Proboscidea*: the elephants (African savanna elephant *Loxodonta africana*, African forest elephant *L. cyclotis*, and Asian elephant *Elephas maximus*), the largest living land animals (savanna elephant males up to 6 t or more). The trunk is a fusion of the nose and upper lip, with no bones and more than 40 000 muscle fascicles, used for breathing, smelling, drinking, feeding, communication and touch. The tusks are enlarged upper incisors. Elephants communicate with infrasonic calls carried kilometres, and live in matriarchal family groups. The fossil proboscideans include mammoths, mastodons and the Chinese "shovel-tusked" *Platybelodon*. Asian elephants survive in China only in southern Yunnan (Xishuangbanna and neighbouring areas), about 300 animals; in 2020–2021 a herd of 15 travelled more than 500 km north to the outskirts of Kunming before returning, a journey followed by millions of people.
- *Sirenia*: the dugong (*Dugong dugon*) and three manatees (*Trichechus*), fully aquatic herbivores of coasts and rivers that feed on seagrasses and other plants, with dense bones as ballast and a very low metabolic rate. Steller's sea cow (*Hydrodamalis gigas*), 8 m long, was exterminated in 1768, 27 years after its discovery. The dugong once lived along the coasts of southern China; surveys of fishers in 2019 found no reliable sighting for decades, and it is considered functionally extinct in Chinese waters (Lin et al. 2022).
- *Hyracoidea*: hyraxes, rabbit-sized African and Middle Eastern herbivores, close relatives of elephants and sirenians.
- *Tubulidentata*: the aardvark, an African termite eater with peculiar teeth made of tubes of dentine, without enamel.
- *Afrosoricida* and *Macroscelidea*: the tenrecs of Madagascar, which have radiated into shrew-, hedgehog- and otter-like forms; the golden moles of southern Africa, convergent with true moles; and the elephant shrews (sengis).

### 55.5.3. Xenarthra

Xenarthrans ("strange joints") are named for the extra articulations (xenarthrales) between their lumbar vertebrae, which brace the spine for digging. They have low metabolic rates and body temperatures (30–34 °C), and their teeth, if present, are simple pegs without enamel.

- *Cingulata*: armadillos (about 22 species), with a carapace of bony plates covered with horny scales, arranged in movable bands. The nine-banded armadillo (*Dasypus novemcinctus*) gives birth to identical quadruplets: a single fertilized egg splits into four embryos (polyembryony).
- *Pilosa*: the tree sloths (six species in two genera, *Bradypus* and *Choloepus*, whose similarity is convergent), which hang upside down from branches, have a very slow metabolism and digestion, and whose fur harbours algae, moths and fungi; and the anteaters, toothless, with a long sticky tongue extending up to 60 cm in the giant anteater.

During the Cenozoic, when South America was an island, xenarthrans were far more diverse: ground sloths (*Megatherium*, the size of an elephant) and glyptodonts, armadillo relatives as large as a small car with a solid carapace. When the Isthmus of Panama formed about 3 million years ago, faunas crossed between the Americas in the Great American Biotic Interchange ([7.10.30](/7.10.30)); ground sloths and glyptodonts reached North America, and many South American mammals, including the large marsupial and native ungulate predators and herbivores, were replaced by northern carnivorans and ungulates. The giant xenarthrans died out at the end of the Pleistocene.

### 55.5.4. Euarchontoglires

*Rodentia* are the largest order of mammals, with about 2 600 species, 40 % of all mammals. Their defining character is a single pair of ever-growing, chisel-like incisors in each jaw, separated from the cheek teeth by a gap (diastema) into which the lips can be drawn, so that a rodent can gnaw without swallowing debris. The jaw muscles are arranged in different ways, used in traditional classification: in squirrels the masseter extends onto the snout in front of the zygomatic arch (sciuromorphous), in porcupines and guinea pigs part of it passes through an enlarged infraorbital foramen (hystricomorphous), and in rats and mice both arrangements are combined (myomorphous); molecular phylogenies show that these arrangements evolved more than once. Rodents include squirrels, beavers, porcupines, rats and mice, voles, hamsters, gerbils, jerboas, mole rats and the capybara, at 60 kg the largest living species. The naked mole-rat (*Heterocephalus glaber*) of East Africa lives in colonies with a single breeding female, one of two **eusocial** mammals; it resists cancer and hypoxia and lives for over 30 years. The house mouse and the brown rat are the main laboratory mammals ([7.5.2](/7.5.2)) and, with the black rat, among the most destructive pests and reservoirs of human disease, including plague, which still circulates among wild marmots and gerbils in western and northern China.

*Lagomorpha* comprise the rabbits and hares (Leporidae) and the pikas (Ochotonidae). They differ from rodents in having a second, small pair of peg-like upper incisors behind the first (formerly the basis of the name Duplicidentata), enamel around the whole incisor, and a fenestrated skull. Lagomorphs digest plant material by fermentation in a large caecum and reingest the soft faecal pellets produced from it (caecotrophy), a second pass through the gut that recovers vitamins and microbial protein. The plateau pika (*Ochotona curzoniae*) is a keystone species of the Tibetan Plateau: its burrows shelter birds and lizards, and it is the main prey of many predators; poisoning campaigns against it as a pest of grasslands have reduced biodiversity.

*Scandentia*, the treeshrews of Southeast Asia, are small, squirrel-like animals. The northern treeshrew (*Tupaia belangeri*), common in southern China, has become a laboratory model for virology and neuroscience in China because it is more closely related to primates than mice are. *Dermoptera* are the two species of colugos ("flying lemurs") of Southeast Asia, gliders with the most extensive gliding membrane of any mammal, extending to the fingers and tail; they are the sister group of primates.

### 55.5.5. Laurasiatheria

*Eulipotyphla* (about 550 species) include the shrews, moles, hedgehogs, solenodons and desmans. Shrews are tiny, have the highest metabolic rates among mammals, and must eat almost continuously; some (*Blarina*, the solenodons) have venomous saliva, and a few echolocate with high-pitched calls. Moles have short, broad forelimbs turned outward for digging, with an extra "thumb", the enlarged radial sesamoid bone. The traditional order Insectivora combined these animals with tenrecs, golden moles and elephant shrews, which are afrotherians; its members shared only the retention of ancestral features (small size, insectivorous diet, simple teeth), so the group was paraphyletic and polyphyletic, a **grade** rather than a clade.

*Chiroptera*, the bats (about 1 480 species, the second-largest order), are the only mammals capable of powered flight. The wing is a double membrane (the patagium) stretched between the elongated fingers II–V, the body and the hindlimbs, and often the tail; the thumb is free and clawed ([7.17.61](/7.17.61)). Most bats are nocturnal insectivores that locate prey by **echolocation**, emitting high-frequency calls through the mouth or the nose and analysing the echoes ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.5)). Bats divide into two lineages: the Yinpterochiroptera, including the Old World fruit bats (Pteropodidae, which navigate by vision and smell and do not echolocate with the larynx) and the horseshoe bats and their relatives, and the Yangochiroptera, the majority of insectivorous bats. Molecular phylogeny thus showed that the traditional division into "megabats" (fruit bats) and "microbats" (echolocating bats) is not natural: laryngeal echolocation either evolved twice or was lost in fruit bats. Bats also include nectar feeders that pollinate plants, fishing bats, and the vampire bats of the Americas, which feed on blood. Bats host many viruses, including coronaviruses related to SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2, which has made their ecology and immunology a subject of intense study ([7.11.27](/7.11.27)). China has about 140 species of bats.

*Pholidota*, the pangolins (eight species in Africa and Asia), are toothless ant and termite eaters covered with overlapping keratin scales, convergent with anteaters. They are the most heavily trafficked wild mammals in the world, for their meat and their scales, which are used in traditional medicine; the Chinese pangolin (*Manis pentadactyla*) is Critically Endangered and has been a Class I protected species in China since 2020, when pangolin scales were also removed from the national pharmacopoeia.

*Carnivora* (about 300 species) are united by the carnassial pair of teeth (upper P4 and lower M1). They divide into the cat-like Feliformia (cats, civets, mongooses, hyenas) and the dog-like Caniformia (dogs, bears, the giant panda, the red panda, raccoons, weasels and their relatives, and the seals, sea lions and walrus, formerly a separate order Pinnipedia). The carnivorans range from the least weasel (about 50 g) to the polar bear and the southern elephant seal (up to 4 t). Many have become omnivores or herbivores: the giant panda (§55.9) and the red panda eat bamboo, and both have an enlarged radial sesamoid bone that works as a "false thumb" for gripping stems, an example of convergence between two not closely related caniforms.

*Perissodactyla* ("odd-toed ungulates", 17 species): horses, zebras and asses, rhinoceroses and tapirs. The axis of the foot passes through the third digit (mesaxonic foot), which in horses is the only digit left. Perissodactyls are hindgut fermenters, digesting cellulose with bacteria in a large caecum and colon. The horse family is the classic example of an evolutionary trend in the fossil record, from the dog-sized, four-toed *Hyracotherium* (*Eohippus*) of the Eocene to the single-toed, high-crowned horses of today — not a straight line but a branching bush of lineages ([7.10.32](/7.10.32#sec-32.5.2)). Przewalski's horse (*Equus ferus przewalskii*), extinct in the wild by the 1960s, has been reintroduced to Mongolia and to the Kalamaili reserve in Xinjiang from captive stock; genetic studies show it descends from horses herded by humans 5 500 years ago in Kazakhstan, which became feral.

*Cetartiodactyla* ("even-toed ungulates and whales", about 350 species) have an astragalus with a pulley-like surface at both ends (double-trochleated), which restricts the ankle to fore-and-aft movement, and a foot whose axis passes between the third and fourth digits (paraxonic). They include the pigs and peccaries (Suina), camels and llamas (Tylopoda), ruminants (deer, giraffes, bovids such as cattle, sheep, goats and antelopes), and the hippopotamuses and whales (Whippomorpha). Ruminants ferment plant material in a four-chambered stomach — rumen, reticulum, omasum and abomasum, the last being the true glandular stomach — and regurgitate and rechew their food (rumination; **ruminant stomach**, [7.14.26](/7.14.26#sec-26.10.4), [7.17.63](/7.17.63)). Bovids have horns, a bony core covered by a permanent keratin sheath, in both sexes in many species; deer have antlers, bony structures that are shed and regrown every year, usually only in males ([7.17.57](/7.17.57)).

### 55.5.6. The Placental Radiation after the K–Pg Extinction

When the non-avian dinosaurs disappeared 66 million years ago, the largest mammals weighed a few kilograms. Within about 10 million years, mammals had evolved into herbivores and predators of up to several hundred kilograms, and by the Oligocene the largest land mammal of all time, the hornless rhinoceros *Paraceratherium* (about 15–20 t, known from Inner Mongolia, Gansu and Central Asia), roamed Asia. The maximum body size of mammals rose rapidly on all continents after the extinction and levelled off after about 25 million years, a pattern consistent with an **adaptive radiation** into vacated ecological space (Smith et al. 2010).

The Paleocene faunas were dominated by "archaic" groups that left few or no descendants: multituberculates, the condylarths (a grade of early ungulate-like mammals), the large herbivorous pantodonts and dinoceratans, and early carnivorous groups (creodonts and mesonychids). Many modern orders — primates, artiodactyls, perissodactyls, bats — appear abruptly in the fossil records of North America, Europe and Asia at the beginning of the Eocene, about 56 million years ago, during the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum, a brief interval of global warming by 5–8 °C; dispersal across high-latitude land bridges in the warm climate spread them across the northern continents, and early horses became temporarily smaller during the warm interval.

Southern China preserves important Paleocene faunas, notably in the Qianshan Basin of Anhui and the Nanxiong Basin of Guangdong, which record endemic Asian groups. Among them are the earliest relatives of rodents and lagomorphs (*Heomys*, *Mimotona*, *Tribosphenomys*) and the anagalids, which suggest that the Glires originated in Asia. The extinct "South American ungulates" (litopterns and notoungulates), which evolved in isolation in South America and include the camel-like *Macrauchenia* and the rhinoceros-like *Toxodon* described by Darwin, were shown by ancient collagen sequences to be relatives of perissodactyls (Welker et al. 2015), not of the afrotherians or of an independent group.

### 55.5.7. The Origin of Whales

That whales are artiodactyls — that the closest living relatives of whales are hippopotamuses — was first proposed from molecular data in the 1990s and is now supported by fossils ([7.10.32](/7.10.32)). The transition from land to sea took place in about 10 million years, in the Eocene of India and Pakistan, then on the shores of the Tethys Sea:

| Fossil | Age (million years ago) | Features |
|---|---|---|
| *Indohyus* (a raoellid) | 48 | Small, deer-like artiodactyl with thickened bones (ballast for wading) and a thickened ear bone like that of whales; the sister group of whales |
| *Pakicetus* | 50 | Wolf-sized, terrestrial, with a whale-like ear region and teeth; lived near water |
| *Ambulocetus* ("walking whale") | 49 | Crocodile-like amphibious predator with large hindfeet for paddling |
| *Rodhocetus* | 47 | Short limbs, still a double-pulley astragalus (artiodactyl ankle), probably swam with its hindlimbs |
| *Basilosaurus*, *Dorudon* | 40 | Fully aquatic, with a tail fluke and tiny hindlimbs that could not support the body |

Living whales divide into the toothed whales (Odontoceti: dolphins, porpoises, sperm whales, beaked whales), which echolocate with clicks produced in the nasal passages and focused by the melon, a fatty organ in the forehead, and the baleen whales (Mysticeti), which filter krill and small fish with plates of keratin (baleen) hanging from the upper jaw. In whales the nostrils have moved to the top of the head (blowhole), the hindlimbs are reduced to vestigial pelvic bones, and the tail fluke, without bones, is beaten up and down.

## 55.6. Locomotion and Foot Posture

Mammals stand and move on their limbs in three postures, depending on how much of the foot touches the ground ([7.17.61](/7.17.61)):

- *Plantigrade* animals walk on the whole sole, from the heel to the toes: primates including humans, bears, rodents, insectivores. The posture is stable and suits climbing and digging.
- *Digitigrade* animals walk on their toes, with the heel and metatarsals raised: dogs, cats, many rodents when running. The effective length of the limb increases.
- *Unguligrade* animals walk on the tips of the toes, which are enclosed by hooves: perissodactyls and artiodactyls. The metatarsals and metacarpals are long, often fused into a cannon bone, and the number of digits is reduced.

![Foot postures of mammals (schematic left hindlimbs, not to scale). Plantigrade: the whole sole rests on the ground. Digitigrade: the heel and metatarsals are raised and the animal stands on its toes. The progression from plantigrade to unguligrade lengthens the distal parts of the limb relative to the proximal parts, increasing stride length, and concentrates the muscles near the body so the light distal limb can be swung quickly: both are adaptations for fast and economical running (cursorial locomotion). Other specializations include the long, spring-like tendons of kangaroos and ungulates that store elastic energy, the flexible spine of the cheetah that adds to its stride, the burrowing limbs of moles, the brachiating arms of gibbons, the flippers of seals and whales, and the wings of bats.

## 55.7. Primates

### 55.7.1. Characters

Primates (about 520 species) are mostly tropical arboreal mammals. They are characterized by a combination of traits associated with life in trees and visual foraging: grasping hands and usually feet with an opposable first digit; nails instead of claws on most digits; forward-facing eyes with overlapping fields that give stereoscopic vision, and a postorbital bar or closed orbit; a reduced sense of smell in many groups; large brains relative to body size; long gestation, usually a single young, slow development and long life; and complex social behaviour. Several hypotheses explain the origin of these traits: life on fine terminal branches (the arboreal hypothesis), visual predation on insects (the visual predation hypothesis) and feeding on fruits and flowers of the first angiosperm forests.

China has about 30 species of primates: macaques (the rhesus macaque, the Tibetan macaque and others), the snub-nosed monkeys (§55.9), langurs, gibbons, slow lorises and the pygmy slow loris. The skywalker hoolock gibbon (*Hoolock tianxing*), described in 2017 from western Yunnan, was the first new ape species described by Chinese scientists.

### 55.7.3. Primate Fossils from China

China has yielded key fossils for the early history of primates. *Archicebus achilles* from the Early Eocene of Jingzhou, Hubei (about 55 million years ago), described in 2013, is the oldest nearly complete primate skeleton; it is a tiny (about 30 g) haplorhine close to the divergence of tarsiers and anthropoids, with a mixture of tarsier-like and monkey-like features. *Eosimias* from the Middle Eocene of Jiangsu and Shanxi (about 45 million years ago) is one of the earliest anthropoids, supporting an Asian origin of the anthropoids, which later reached Africa. Among apes, *Lufengpithecus* from the Late Miocene of Yunnan is an Asian hominid close to orangutans, and *Gigantopithecus blacki*, known from more than 2 000 teeth and four lower jaws from caves in Guangxi and neighbouring provinces, was the largest primate that has ever lived, perhaps 3 m tall; it died out between 295 000 and 215 000 years ago as forests became seasonal (Zhang et al. 2024). Human evolution, including the fossils of *Homo erectus* from Zhoukoudian (Peking Man) and the Denisovans, is treated in [7.10.33](/7.10.33).

## 55.9. Mammals of China and Their Conservation

China has about 690 species of mammals, more than any other country of the Northern Hemisphere, about 150 of them endemic, concentrated in the mountains of the southwest and on the Tibetan Plateau. Its fauna straddles the Palaearctic and Oriental realms ([7.17.69](/7.17.69)). Several Chinese mammals have become symbols of conservation.

*Giant panda* (*Ailuropoda melanoleuca*). A bear (Ursidae) that has become a bamboo specialist: more than 99 % of its diet is bamboo, which it eats for 10–16 hours a day, digesting only about 17 % of it. It retains the short, simple gut of a carnivore and depends on its gut microbes, which are carnivore-like and poorly suited to cellulose; the gene for the umami taste receptor (*Tas1r1*) has become a pseudogene, perhaps related to the loss of interest in meat (Li et al. 2010). Its adaptations for bamboo include a "false thumb" (the enlarged radial sesamoid bone), broad, flat molars and powerful jaw muscles. Pandas are solitary, and females are fertile for only two to three days a year; the newborn weighs only about 100 g, 1/900 of its mother, one of the smallest relative birth sizes of any placental. The fourth national survey (2015) counted 1 864 wild pandas in Sichuan, Shaanxi and Gansu, an increase of 17 % over the previous survey, and the species was downgraded from Endangered to Vulnerable by IUCN in 2016. The Giant Panda National Park, established in 2021, joins fragmented populations across 22 000 km².

*Snub-nosed monkeys* (*Rhinopithecus*). Colobine monkeys with upturned noses that live in cold mountain forests. The golden snub-nosed monkey (*R. roxellana*) of Sichuan, Shaanxi (Qinling), Gansu and Hubei (Shennongjia) lives in multilevel societies of several hundred animals, made of harem units of one male and several females that associate in bands. The black-and-white snub-nosed monkey (*R. bieti*) of the Yunnan–Tibet border lives at altitudes up to 4 700 m, higher than any other non-human primate, and feeds largely on lichens in winter; the grey snub-nosed monkey (*R. brelichi*) survives only on Mount Fanjing in Guizhou, about 400 animals; the black snub-nosed monkey (*R. strykeri*) was described in 2010 from Myanmar and later found in Yunnan.

*Cetaceans of the Yangtze.* The baiji or Yangtze river dolphin (*Lipotes vexillifer*), the only member of its family, lived only in the middle and lower Yangtze. It was nearly blind, finding prey by echolocation. Its numbers fell from several hundred in the 1980s, through bycatch in fishing gear (particularly electrofishing and rolling hooks), collisions with boats, dams and pollution; an intensive six-week survey in 2006 found none, and it was declared functionally extinct (Turvey et al. 2007), the first cetacean driven to extinction by humans. The Yangtze finless porpoise (*Neophocaena asiaeorientalis asiaeorientalis*), which shares its habitat, declined to about 1 000 animals by 2017; a ten-year fishing ban across the Yangtze basin, in force since 2021, and protected oxbow lakes have stabilized it, and the 2022 survey counted about 1 250.

*Gibbons.* The Hainan gibbon (*Nomascus hainanus*), restricted to the Bawangling forest of Hainan Island, was reduced to about ten animals in two groups in the 1980s and is the rarest ape in the world. Protection of its forest has allowed it to increase to about 40 animals in several groups.

*Ungulates.* Père David's deer (*Elaphurus davidianus*), a deer of the marshes of the lower Yangtze, became extinct in the wild in China about 1900; the last captive herd in the imperial hunting park of Nanhaizi in Beijing was destroyed in 1900, but animals taken to Europe survived at Woburn Abbey in England. Reintroduced to Nanhaizi in 1985 and to Dafeng in Jiangsu and Shishou in Hubei, they now number more than 10 000, with free-living populations. The Tibetan antelope (*Pantholops hodgsonii*), a bovid of the high plateau, was poached for its fine wool (shahtoosh) to about 70 000 animals in the 1990s; protection, including the Hoh Xil reserve (a World Heritage Site since 2017), has allowed it to recover to about 300 000. Other notable ungulates include the wild yak, the Tibetan wild ass, the wild Bactrian camel of the Lop Nur region, the musk deer (whose males have tusks and a musk gland instead of antlers) and the Chinese water deer (*Hydropotes inermis*), also tusked and antlerless.

*Large carnivores.* The South China tiger (*Panthera tigris amoyensis*) has not been seen in the wild for decades and survives only in captivity. The Amur tiger and Amur leopard have recovered in the forests of the Changbai Mountains along the border with Russia, now protected in the Northeast China Tiger and Leopard National Park (2021). The snow leopard of the Tibetan Plateau and the mountains of the west is relatively widespread in China, which holds about 60 % of its range.

China protects its wildlife under the Wildlife Protection Law with two levels of state protection (Class I and Class II), revised in 2021 to include about 980 species and groups.

## 55.10. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

Chinese textbooks divide living mammals into three subclasses — Prototheria (monotremes), Metatheria (marsupials) and Eutheria (placentals) — and the placentals into about 14 orders: Insectivora, Scandentia, Chiroptera, Primates, Edentata, Pholidota, Lagomorpha, Rodentia, Cetacea, Carnivora, Pinnipedia, Proboscidea, Perissodactyla and Artiodactyla (with Sirenia and Hyracoidea in some editions).

| Traditional order | Current classification |
|---|---|
| Insectivora | Split: Eulipotyphla (shrews, moles, hedgehogs; Laurasiatheria), Afrosoricida (tenrecs, golden moles) and Macroscelidea (elephant shrews), both Afrotheria; treeshrews and colugos already separate |
| Edentata (Xenarthra plus pangolins and the aardvark) | Split: Cingulata and Pilosa (Xenarthra), Pholidota (Laurasiatheria, close to Carnivora), Tubulidentata (Afrotheria) |
| Chiroptera with Megachiroptera and Microchiroptera | One order, divided into Yinpterochiroptera (fruit bats with horseshoe bats and relatives) and Yangochiroptera |

| Pinnipedia as a separate order | Pinnipeds are caniform carnivorans, close to bears and mustelids |

The textbooks' description of the mammalian body (skin and its derivatives, skeleton, muscles, the oral cavity and digestion, respiration, circulation, excretion, reproduction with the types of placenta and uterus, the brain and the twelve cranial nerves, sense organs and endocrine glands) corresponds to §55.1, §55.8 and the comparative chapters ([7.17.57](/7.17.57)–[7.17.68](/7.17.68)). Their discussion of the significance of viviparity and lactation, of the dental formula as a taxonomic character, of the adaptive radiation of mammals into ecological types (terrestrial, burrowing, arboreal, aerial and aquatic) and of the conservation and sustainable use of wildlife is included in §55.2, §55.4–55.6 and §55.9.

## Summary

- Mammals (Definition 55.1.1): crown-group synapsids with hair, mammary glands, a single-bone lower jaw, three ossicles, diphyodont heterodont teeth, diaphragm, left aortic arch, anucleate red blood cells and neocortex; seven cervical vertebrae; about 6 700 species.
- Teeth: incisors, canines, premolars, molars; the dental formula (Definition 55.2.1), ancestrally 3.1.4.3/3.1.4.3 in placentals; tribosphenic, bunodont, selenodont, lophodont and secodont molars; hypsodonty in grazers; ever-growing incisors in rodents and lagomorphs.
- Monotremes (Definition 55.3.1): platypus and echidnas; leathery eggs, cloaca, milk without nipples, electroreception, venomous spur, ten sex chromosomes.
- Marsupials (Definition 55.4.1): short gestation with a yolk-sac placenta, a tiny newborn that attaches to a teat, long lactation, embryonic diapause; about 380 species; dispersal from the north through South America and Antarctica to Australia; convergence with placentals (thylacine and wolf, sugar glider and flying squirrel).
- Placentals (Definition 55.5.1): chorioallantoic placenta of varied types; four superorders, Afrotheria (elephants, sirenians, hyraxes, aardvark, tenrecs), Xenarthra (armadillos, sloths, anteaters), Euarchontoglires (rodents, lagomorphs, treeshrews, colugos, primates) and Laurasiatheria (eulipotyphlans, bats, pangolins, carnivorans, perissodactyls, cetartiodactyls).
- Whales are artiodactyls, sister group of hippos; the land-to-sea transition is documented by *Indohyus*, *Pakicetus*, *Ambulocetus*, *Rodhocetus* and *Basilosaurus*.
- Foot postures: plantigrade, digitigrade and unguligrade; mesaxonic perissodactyls and paraxonic artiodactyls.
- Primates: grasping hands, nails, stereoscopic vision, large brains; Strepsirrhini and Haplorhini; Chinese fossils *Archicebus*, *Eosimias*, *Lufengpithecus*, *Gigantopithecus*.
- China: about 690 species; the giant panda's recovery, snub-nosed monkeys, the extinction of the baiji, the Yangtze finless porpoise and the fishing ban, the Hainan gibbon, the return of Père David's deer and the Tibetan antelope.

## Questions

1. A skull has the dental formula of 3.1.4.2 / 3.1.4.3. What is the total number of teeth, and what kind of animal could it be? What would you expect the carnassials to be?

   **Answer** Total = $2 \times (3 + 1 + 4 + 2 + 3 + 1 + 4 + 3) = 2 \times 21 = 42$. This is the formula of the dog family (Canidae) and of several other caniform carnivorans: a full set of incisors, large canines, four premolars, and reduced molars (two upper, three lower). As a carnivoran, it should have carnassials formed by the upper fourth premolar (P4) and the lower first molar (M1), with blade-like crests that shear past each other; the molars behind the carnassials retain crushing surfaces, reflecting the omnivorous diet of canids compared with cats, whose formula is 3.1.3.1/3.1.2.1 = 30 has almost no crushing molars.
2. Explain why the placental orders recognized by molecular phylogenies correspond better to geography than the traditional orders based on morphology.

   **Answer** The four superorders diverged in the Late Cretaceous, when the continents were separating: Afrotheria evolved on Africa, isolated as an island continent for much of the early Cenozoic, Xenarthra on South America, and Boreoeutheria on the northern continents. Each lineage then radiated within its region into animals of many forms and ways of life, and similar ecological roles were filled independently in different regions: insect-eating burrowers (golden moles in Africa, true moles in Eurasia), ant eaters (aardvark, anteaters, pangolins), large herbivores (elephants, perissodactyls). Traditional orders such as Insectivora, Edentata and the "ungulates" grouped animals by similar adaptations and retained ancestral features, which are often convergent, whereas molecular data from many independent genes reflect descent. The correspondence between molecular clades and the continents on which they evolved is strong independent evidence that the molecular tree is correct.
3. What is embryonic diapause in kangaroos, how is it controlled, and what might be its advantage?

   **Answer** After a female red kangaroo gives birth, she mates again within days; the resulting embryo develops to a blastocyst of about 100 cells and then stops developing while the young in the pouch is suckling. The suckling stimulus causes release of prolactin from the pituitary, which suppresses the corpus luteum, so progesterone stays low and the uterus does not support the blastocyst's development. When the pouch young begins to suckle less (as it leaves the pouch) or dies, prolactin falls, the corpus luteum becomes active, and the blastocyst resumes development, being born about a month later. Advantages: a replacement young is ready if the pouch young dies, saving the time of a new pregnancy, and the female can produce young in quick succession when conditions are good, while in drought she can lose young cheaply and wait. Embryonic diapause also occurs in many placentals (bears, seals, roe deer, mustelids), where it times birth to the season.
4. Describe the evidence that whales evolved from artiodactyls, and explain why the ankle bone was decisive.

   **Answer** Molecular evidence: phylogenies of many genes and of retroposon insertions (SINEs, which are nearly free of homoplasy) placed whales within Artiodactyla, as the sister group of hippopotamuses, in the 1990s. Morphological evidence was then needed, because modern whales lack hindlimbs. The artiodactyl ankle is diagnostic: the astragalus has a pulley-shaped surface at both its upper and lower ends (double-trochleated), a character found only in artiodactyls. The Eocene whales *Rodhocetus* and *Artiocetus* from Pakistan, described in 2001, had such astragali, together with whale-like skulls and ears, confirming the molecular result. *Indohyus*, a deer-like raoellid artiodactyl, shares with whales the thickened involucrum of the ear bone and dense limb bones, and is their sister group. The fossils from *Pakicetus* to *Basilosaurus* then document the steps of the transition to water.
5. (Open) The giant panda is often described as an evolutionary dead end, poorly adapted to its bamboo diet. Evaluate this view using the evidence in this chapter and in 7.14.

   **Answer** Points for: the panda has a carnivore's short gut and microbiome, digests only about 17 % of the bamboo it eats, must feed for most of the day, has low reproductive rates (short oestrus, one young, tiny neonates), and depends on bamboo forests that die back synchronously after flowering. Points against: pandas have been bamboo specialists for at least 2 million years (fossils of *Ailuropoda microta* from southern China), so the strategy has been successful; they compensate for low digestion by high intake, very low metabolic rate (about 38 % of that expected for their size, Nie et al. 2015, achieved with low thyroid hormone levels, reduced organ sizes and thick fur) and low activity; they have specific adaptations (false thumb, robust skull and molars, loss of *Tas1r1*). Their decline was caused by habitat loss and fragmentation by humans, and populations have increased under protection. Specialization limits flexibility but is not maladaptation; whether a lineage is a "dead end" can only be judged in hindsight. A complete answer should distinguish adaptation to a niche from vulnerability to rapid human-caused change.
