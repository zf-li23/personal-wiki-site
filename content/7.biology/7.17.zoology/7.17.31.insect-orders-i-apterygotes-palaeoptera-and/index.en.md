This chapter begins the survey of the living insect orders that occupies [7.17.31](/7.17.31)–[7.17.36](/7.17.36). It first explains which characters are used to place an insect in its order and gives a key to the orders of adult insects; it then treats the orders at the base of the insect tree: the two wingless orders, the two orders of Palaeoptera, whose wings cannot be folded, and the ten orders of Polyneoptera, mostly chewing, hemimetabolous insects with an enlarged anal fan in the hind wing. The remaining orders follow: Paraneoptera in [7.17.32](/7.17.32), and Holometabola in [7.17.33](/7.17.33)–[7.17.36](/7.17.36).

The morphological terms (mouthpart types, wing types, **wing venation**, leg types, **cerci**, **ovipositor**) are defined in [7.17.23](/7.17.23) and [7.17.24](/7.17.24) and used here without repetition; types of development are treated in [7.17.29](/7.17.29), and the relationships among orders and the fossil record in [7.17.30](/7.17.30). The classification follows Brusca, Giribet and Moore (2023), who recognize 28 living orders of insects; species numbers are approximate recent counts of described species and differ between catalogues. Older names that appear in many Chinese textbooks (Thysanura, Isoptera, Grylloblattaria and others) are given in the correspondence table of Section 31.5.

## 31.1. Identifying Insects to Order

### 31.1.1. Diagnostic Characters

An insect order is recognized by a combination of characters, none of which works alone. The most useful are:

| Character | What to look at | Examples of diagnostic states |
|---|---|---|
| Wings | Number of pairs; texture of the forewing; folding at rest; venation; coupling | Forewings as **elytra** (Coleoptera), **tegmina** (Orthoptera, Blattodea, Mantodea), **hemelytra** (Heteroptera); hind wings as **halteres** (Diptera); wings not folding (Palaeoptera); scaled (Lepidoptera), hairy (Trichoptera), fringed (Thysanoptera) |
| Mouthparts | Type; which parts form stylets or the proboscis | **Chewing mouthparts** in most polyneopterans and in beetles; **piercing-sucking mouthparts** in Hemiptera; **rasping-sucking mouthparts** in thrips; **siphoning mouthparts** in Lepidoptera; **sponging mouthparts** in higher Diptera; **chewing-lapping mouthparts** in bees |
| Development | Ametabolous, hemimetabolous or holometabolous; aquatic or terrestrial immatures | **Hemimetaboly** with aquatic **naiads** (mayflies, dragonflies, stoneflies); **holometaboly** with a **pupa** (Holometabola) |
| Antennae | Type; number of segments | Short and bristle-like (Palaeoptera, cicadas); moniliform (termites, Zoraptera); geniculate (ants, weevils); lamellate (scarabs); aristate (higher flies) |
| Legs | Leg types; number of tarsomeres | Raptorial forelegs (Mantodea); saltatorial hind legs (Orthoptera, fleas); silk-spinning fore tarsi (Embioptera) |
| Abdominal apex | Cerci (present, long, short, forceps); median caudal filament; ovipositor | Three "tails" (Archaeognatha, Zygentoma, most Ephemeroptera); forceps (Dermaptera); long sword-shaped ovipositor (katydids) |
| Head | Position of mouthparts; eyes; ocelli; rostrum | Prognathous head of earwigs; rostrum of Mecoptera and weevils; huge contiguous eyes of dragonflies |

The **tarsus** is often written as a *tarsal formula*, the number of tarsomeres on the fore, middle and hind legs: 5-5-5 in most beetles, Hymenoptera, Diptera and cockroaches; 5-5-4 in the "heteromerous" beetles (darkling beetles, blister beetles); 5-5-5 but apparently 4-4-4 in leaf beetles, longhorn beetles and weevils, whose fourth tarsomere is minute and hidden in the bilobed third (pseudotetramerous tarsi); 3-3-3 in acridid grasshoppers; 2-2-2 or 3-3-3 in Hemiptera and bark lice. The count is a family-level character in beetles ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)) and an order-level character in some small orders (Zoraptera 2, Embioptera 3).

Immature insects are harder to identify than adults, and many keys are for adults only. Larvae of Holometabola are identified by head capsule, legs, prolegs and their crochets, and spiracles; nymphs of hemimetabolous orders usually resemble the adults of their order, apart from wing pads and genitalia, except the aquatic naiads, whose gills and mouthparts are specialized.

### 31.1.2. A Key to the Orders of Adult Insects

Wingless members of usually winged orders (worker ants, female scale insects, wingless stick insects, female bagworm moths, wingless flies such as keds) are keyed only where they are common; a specimen that fits no lead well is often such a case, and is best identified by its other characters. The entognathan orders (Collembola, Protura, Diplura), with mouthparts withdrawn into the head, are not insects and are treated in [7.17.22](/7.17.22).

## 31.2. The Wingless Insects: Archaeognatha and Zygentoma

The two orders of primitively wingless insects are what remains of a wider radiation before the origin of wings. Together they were long classified as the order Thysanura ("bristle tails"), and with the entognathans as the subclass **Apterygota**; both groups are paraphyletic. They share ectognathous mouthparts, a long multisegmented median caudal filament between the cerci, ventral styli and eversible vesicles on the abdomen (the remnants of abdominal limbs), indirect sperm transfer by a spermatophore or sperm droplets, and ametabolous development: the young resemble small adults and adults continue to molt throughout life, up to 50 or more times, unlike any winged insect.

**Definition 31.2.1 (Archaeognatha)** The **Archaeognatha** (jumping bristletails) are wingless insects with a cylindrical body and humped thorax, large compound eyes meeting on top of the head, three ocelli, monocondylic mandibles (a single articulation with the head), long seven-segmented maxillary palps, styli on the middle and hind coxae and on abdominal segments 2–9, and a median caudal filament longer than the cerci; about 500 species in two families.

The single mandibular condyle is the reason the order is placed as the sister group of all other insects (Dicondylia, [7.17.22](/7.17.22)). Bristletails live under bark and stones and in leaf litter, coastal rocks and grass, feed on algae, lichens and detritus, and escape by jumping: the arched thorax and flexed abdomen strike the ground, launching the animal several body lengths. The families are Machilidae (worldwide, including the Chinese genera *Pedetontus* and *Petrobiellus*) and Meinertellidae (mainly southern continents). Males in many species deposit a sperm droplet on a silk thread spun from the genitalia, which the female takes up.

**Definition 31.2.2 (Zygentoma)** The **Zygentoma** (silverfish and firebrats) are wingless, dorsoventrally flattened insects usually covered with silvery scales, with small, widely separated compound eyes or none, dicondylic mandibles, five-segmented maxillary palps, styli on some abdominal segments, and three caudal appendages of about equal length; about 550 species in five families.

The dicondylic mandible, shared with the winged insects, makes the Zygentoma the sister group of Pterygota. The best-known family, Lepismatidae, contains the synanthropic silverfish *Lepisma saccharina*, the four-lined silverfish *Ctenolepisma lineata* and the firebrat *Thermobia domestica*, which feed on paper, glue, starch and textiles and are among the few animals that produce their own cellulase. Other species live in caves, ant and termite nests (Nicoletiidae, Ateluridae) or in deserts. Males deposit a spermatophore on the ground and guide the female over it with a courtship of touching and pursuit. The relict genus *Tricholepidion* from California (Lepidotrichidae) retains ocelli and other ancestral characters and was once considered intermediate between the two orders.

The abdominal styli and eversible vesicles of both orders are the remnants of abdominal limbs: the styli are the distal parts of appendages, moved by muscles and used as runners that lift the abdomen off the ground, and the vesicles, everted by blood pressure and withdrawn by muscles, take up water from dew and moist surfaces. Both orders molt throughout adult life, a silverfish passing through as many as 60 molts in a life of up to several years, and they can regenerate lost appendages at each molt; reproductive and molting cycles alternate. Their indirect sperm transfer, with a spermatophore or a sperm droplet taken up by the female without copulation, is the ancestral condition of insects, retained also by the entognathan hexapods ([7.17.28](/7.17.28)). Bristletails are among the oldest insects in the fossil record, and Carboniferous and Permian fossils of the extinct Monura and of archaeognathan-like insects show that the wingless body plan was in place before the origin of wings ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

## 31.3. Palaeoptera: Mayflies and Dragonflies

The two orders of the **Palaeoptera** cannot fold their wings over the abdomen: their wing base lacks the third axillary sclerite with its folding muscle ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)). Both have aquatic immatures (**naiads**, often called larvae or nymphs in the English literature), short bristle-like antennae in the adult, and wings with a dense network of veins. Whether the two orders form a clade, or whether mayflies or dragonflies are the sister group of Neoptera, is still debated ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

### 31.3.1. Ephemeroptera

**Definition 31.3.1 (Ephemeroptera)** The **Ephemeroptera** (mayflies) are palaeopteran insects whose adults have vestigial mouthparts and do not feed, triangular forewings and much smaller or no hind wings held vertically over the body at rest, short bristle-like antennae, and two long cerci usually with a median caudal filament; the aquatic naiads have chewing mouthparts and abdominal tracheal gills, and the last naiad molts into a winged, sexually immature *subimago*, which molts once more into the adult (imago); about 3500 species.

The subimago is unique: in all other insects the winged stage never molts. It has dull, translucent wings covered with water-repellent microtrichia, perhaps an adaptation for emerging through the water surface, and usually lasts a few hours to a day. Adults live from less than an hour to a few days; their gut is filled with air and lightens the body for the nuptial flight. Males swarm, often over water at dusk, and seize females from below; their forelegs are long for clasping, and in Baetidae the eyes are divided, the upper part (turbinate eyes) raised on a stalk to spot females against the sky.

Naiads pass through 10–50 molts over months to two years. Their gills, plate-like or feathery on abdominal segments 1–7, both take up oxygen and create ventilation currents. Body form reflects habitat: flattened clingers on stones in fast streams (Heptageniidae), streamlined swimmers (Baetidae, Siphlonuridae), burrowers in mud with mandibular tusks and feathery gills (Ephemeridae, Palingeniidae, Polymitarcyidae), sprawlers in silt with operculate gills covering the others (Caenidae). Synchronized mass emergences of burrowing mayflies (*Hexagenia* on the North American Great Lakes, *Palingenia longicauda* on the Tisza river) can be detected by weather radar. Mayfly, stonefly and caddisfly naiads (the EPT taxa) are sensitive to low oxygen and pollution, and their richness in a stream sample is a standard index of water quality. Common Chinese genera include *Ephemera* (Ephemeridae), *Ecdyonurus* and *Epeorus* (Heptageniidae) and *Baetis* (Baetidae).

Mayfly wings have a distinctive venation, with many intercalary veins between the main branches and crossveins forming a dense network; the forewing is triangular and the anal area reduced. The fluting of the wing, with alternating convex and concave veins, preserves features thought to be close to the ancestral pterygote wing ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)). Adult mayflies are among the most important foods of stream fish and of birds and bats at the time of emergences, and imitations of their subimagos ("duns") and imagos ("spinners") are the basis of fly fishing.

### 31.3.2. Odonata

**Definition 31.3.2 (Odonata)** The **Odonata** (dragonflies and damselflies) are palaeopteran insects with a large, mobile head bearing huge compound eyes, short bristle-like antennae and strong chewing mandibles; two pairs of elongate membranous wings, each with a nodus and usually a pterostigma, moved by direct flight muscles; a long slender abdomen; secondary male copulatory organs on the ventral side of abdominal segments 2–3; and aquatic naiads whose labium forms an extensible prehensile *mask* for catching prey; about 6400 species.

Adults and naiads are predators. The adult catches prey in flight, the six legs held forward as a basket. Its eyes contain up to about 30,000 ommatidia each, with a dorsal region of high resolution that tracks prey against the sky, and target-selective neurons steer an interception course rather than a direct pursuit. Each wing is moved by its own direct muscles, so that fore and hind wings can beat in different phases; this gives dragonflies exceptional manoeuvrability and the ability to fly backward ([7.17.25](/7.17.25)).

Reproduction is unusual. Before mating the male transfers sperm from the gonopore on segment 9 to the secondary genitalia on segments 2–3. He grasps the female behind the head (dragonflies) or by the prothorax (damselflies) with the terminal appendages, forming a tandem, and the female curls her abdomen forward to the male's secondary genitalia: the copulatory "wheel". In many species the male's penis first removes or displaces sperm stored by the female from earlier matings (Waage 1979, *Calopteryx maculata*), a well-known case of **sperm competition** ([7.17.28](/7.17.28)). Eggs are dropped into water or inserted into plant tissue with an ovipositor (damselflies, Aeshnidae).

The naiad mask is the labium with an elongate prementum hinged to the postmentum; at rest it is folded under the head, and it shoots forward within about 10–20 milliseconds, the palps closing on the prey like pincers. The strike is driven by elastic energy stored in the cuticle and released by a catch, assisted by hemolymph pressure from abdominal contraction. Dragonfly naiads breathe with gills in the rectum and can expel the water for jet propulsion; damselfly naiads have three leaf-like caudal lamellae.

| | Anisoptera (dragonflies) | Zygoptera (damselflies) |
|---|---|---|
| Wings | Hind wing broader at the base than the forewing; held horizontally at rest | Fore and hind wings similar, narrowed at the base; held together above the abdomen at rest (half-open in Lestidae) |
| Discoidal cell | Divided into a triangle and supratriangle | A quadrilateral |

A third group, the relict genus *Epiophlebia* (two or three species in the Himalaya, Japan and northeastern China), combines a dragonfly-like body with damselfly-like wings; it is the sister group of Anisoptera, and the two together form Epiprocta. Major dragonfly families are Aeshnidae (hawkers, such as *Anax*), Gomphidae (clubtails, eyes separated), Cordulegastridae, Corduliidae and Libellulidae (skimmers and darters, the largest family, with *Sympetrum*, *Orthetrum*, *Crocothemis* and *Pantala*). The wandering glider *Pantala flavescens*, common in China in summer, is the world's most widely distributed dragonfly; successive generations cross the Indian Ocean on monsoon winds, a multigenerational migration of up to about 18,000 km. Major damselfly families are Calopterygidae (jewelwings, with metallic bodies and coloured wings), Lestidae (spreadwings), Platycnemididae and Coenagrionidae (narrow-winged damselflies, the largest family, with *Ischnura*). Palaeozoic relatives of the Odonata, the griffenflies (Meganisoptera) such as *Meganeuropsis permiana*, reached wingspans of about 70 cm ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

The odonate wing is a stiff, corrugated, highly veined structure with several features unique to the order. The nodus, a short, strong crossvein complex about halfway along the anterior margin, acts as a shock absorber and allows the wing to twist in the upstroke; the **pterostigma**, a weighted, pigmented cell near the tip, damps flutter at high speeds; and the discoidal cell (triangle or quadrilateral) near the base distinguishes the suborders. The legs are held forward under the head and are used for perching and catching prey rather than walking. The thorax is skewed: the pleura slope backward so that the legs lie forward and the wings backward, an arrangement that places the wings above the centre of mass.

Common species in China include the green darner *Anax parthenope* (Aeshnidae), the scarlet skimmer *Crocothemis servilia*, the white-tailed skimmer *Orthetrum albistylum* and the butterfly-like *Pseudothemis zonata* (Libellulidae), the wandering glider *Pantala flavescens*, and the damselflies *Ischnura senegalensis* (Coenagrionidae) and *Matrona basilaris* (Calopterygidae, with dark metallic wings). Male dragonflies of many species defend territories at water and patrol along the bank; in Calopterygidae the male courts with wing displays and guards the female during oviposition. Naiads are top predators of ponds and paddy fields, eating mosquito larvae, tadpoles and even small fish, and they are themselves food for fish and birds.

## 31.4. Polyneoptera

The **Polyneoptera** comprise ten orders, mostly with **chewing mouthparts**, hemimetabolous development, many-segmented cerci and an enlarged anal fan (vannus) in the hind wing, folded like a fan beneath leathery forewings; many have tarsal pads (euplantulae). Their monophyly, long doubted because these characters are ancestral, is now strongly supported by phylogenomic data (Misof and coauthors 2014; Wipfler and coauthors 2019). Within it, the following groupings are well supported: Dictyoptera (Mantodea and Blattodea), Eukinolabia (Phasmatodea and Embioptera), Xenonomia or Notoptera (Grylloblattodea and Mantophasmatodea), and, less firmly, Haplocercata (Zoraptera and Dermaptera). The positions of Plecoptera and Orthoptera among them are less certain. The sections below follow these groupings.

### 31.4.1. Plecoptera

**Definition 31.4.1 (Plecoptera)** The **Plecoptera** (stoneflies) are soft-bodied polyneopteran insects with long filiform antennae, chewing mouthparts often reduced in the adult, two pairs of membranous wings held flat over and partly around the abdomen at rest, the hind wing with a large anal lobe, three-segmented tarsi, long multisegmented cerci, no ovipositor, and aquatic naiads usually with thoracic or abdominal gills; about 3800 species.

Stonefly naiads live in cool, well-oxygenated streams and lake shores, as shredders of fallen leaves, grazers of algae or predators (Perlidae, Perlodidae). They are the most pollution-sensitive of the EPT taxa. Adults are weak fliers, short-lived and often active in winter or early spring (winter stoneflies, Capniidae and Taeniopterygidae, emerge onto snow). Males and females find each other by drumming: they tap the abdomen on the substrate and exchange species-specific vibrational duets. The order is divided into the southern Antarctoperlaria (with gills of a distinctive type) and the northern Arctoperlaria, a pattern reflecting the separation of Gondwana and Laurasia; important families include Perlidae, Nemouridae, Pteronarcyidae (giant stoneflies) and Peltoperlidae. In China *Kamimuria* and *Neoperla* (Perlidae) and *Amphinemura* (Nemouridae) are common.

The aquatic immatures of the three orders that dominate stream samples are easily confused at first. They differ consistently:

### 31.4.2. Dermaptera

**Definition 31.4.2 (Dermaptera)** The **Dermaptera** (earwigs) are elongate, flattened polyneopteran insects with a prognathous head, chewing mouthparts, short leathery forewings without veins, semicircular membranous hind wings folded beneath them in a complex fan-and-transverse pattern, three-segmented tarsi, and unsegmented cerci forming forceps in the adult; about 2000 species.

The forceps, larger and curved in males, are used in defence, in capturing prey, in courtship and in folding the hind wings, whose folding pattern combines radial and transverse folds and is opened by elastic resilin joints. The folded hind wing is one of the most compact folding patterns known in insects. Many earwigs rarely fly. Females guard their eggs in a burrow, clean them of fungal spores and feed the young nymphs, one of the best-studied cases of maternal care in insects. Most species are nocturnal omnivores; the European earwig *Forficula auricularia* (Forficulidae) is a garden pest and also a predator of aphids, and the shore earwig *Labidura riparia* (Labiduridae) is a widespread predator, and the maritime earwig *Anisolabis maritima* (Anisolabididae) lives under debris on seashores worldwide. Two former suborders, the blind, wingless Arixeniidae (on Southeast Asian bats) and Hemimeridae (on African giant rats), are earwigs specialized as ectoparasites or commensals, with viviparous reproduction and a placenta-like nutrition of the embryo.

### 31.4.3. Zoraptera

**Definition 31.4.3 (Zoraptera)** The **Zoraptera** (angel insects) are minute (under 4 mm) polyneopteran insects with nine-segmented moniliform antennae, chewing mouthparts, two-segmented tarsi, one-segmented cerci, and in each species both wingless, eyeless forms and winged forms with eyes and two pairs of small paddle-shaped wings with reduced venation that are shed after dispersal; about 50 species in the genus *Zorotypus* sensu lato.

Zorapterans live in colonies under the bark of rotting logs, feeding on fungal hyphae and spores and on small arthropods. Wing dimorphism and the shedding of wings resemble those of termites, but there are no castes. They are among the rarest insects in collections; in China *Zorotypus sinensis* and *Z. medoensis* were described from Tibet (Hwang 1974, 1976). Their position within Polyneoptera was long called "the Zoraptera problem"; phylogenomic studies place them as the sister group of earwigs. Living zorapterans have recently been divided into two families (Spiralizoridae and Zorotypidae) on the basis of male genitalia.

### 31.4.4. Orthoptera

**Definition 31.4.4 (Orthoptera)** The **Orthoptera** (grasshoppers, locusts, crickets and katydids) are polyneopteran insects with chewing mouthparts, a large saddle-shaped pronotum extending down over the pleura, forewings usually forming tegmina and hind wings with a large anal fan, hind legs usually saltatorial with an enlarged femur, short unsegmented cerci, a well-developed ovipositor of three pairs of valves, and usually tympanal hearing organs and stridulatory sound production; about 29,000 species.

The order is divided into two suborders, which are among the clearest-cut examples of how antennae, ears, sound production and ovipositor vary together ([7.17.27](/7.17.27) for the sense organs):

| | Caelifera | Ensifera |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Grasshoppers and locusts (Acrididae), pygmy grasshoppers (Tetrigidae), gaudy grasshoppers (Pyrgomorphidae) | Katydids or bush crickets (Tettigoniidae), crickets (Gryllidae), mole crickets (Gryllotalpidae), camel crickets (Rhaphidophoridae) |
| Antennae | Shorter than the body, usually fewer than 30 segments | Usually longer than the body, many more than 30 segments |
| Tympanal organs | On the sides of abdominal segment 1 | On the fore tibiae, connected to a prothoracic spiracle by an acoustic trachea |
| Stridulation | Usually femoro-tegminal: a row of pegs on the inner hind femur rubbed against a raised vein of the tegmen (some species crepitate in flight or drum) | Tegmino-tegminal: a file of teeth on the underside of one tegmen rubbed by a scraper on the other |
| Ovipositor | Short, with hooked valves that dig a hole in soil; eggs laid in a froth-covered pod | Long, blade-like (katydids) or needle-like (crickets), inserting eggs into plant tissue or soil |
| Tarsi | Three segments or fewer | Three (crickets) or four (katydids) segments |
| Diet | Mostly herbivorous, many on grasses | Herbivorous, omnivorous or predatory |

Ensiferan song is a by-product of wing closure: as the scraper passes along the file, each tooth produces one cycle of sound, so that the carrier frequency equals the rate of tooth impacts. Field crickets keep this rate nearly constant (an "escapement" mechanism, in which the resonating harp of the wing paces the scraper), producing a pure tone of about 4–5 kHz; katydids produce broadband or ultrasonic songs up to over 100 kHz. Crickets close the right tegmen over the left, katydids the left over the right. Only males sing in most species; females approach the calling song, which is species-specific in its temporal pattern and has been a model for studying the neural recognition of signals ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)). It is a common misconception that orthopterans rub their hind legs together.

Locusts are grasshoppers (Acrididae) that show phase **polyphenism**: at low density they develop as cryptic, solitary *solitarious* individuals; crowding, sensed through touch of the hind femora and the sight and smell of other locusts, induces within hours a behavioural switch to gregarious behaviour, mediated by a rise of serotonin in the thoracic ganglia, followed over generations by changes of colour, shape and physiology. Gregarious hoppers march in bands and adults migrate in swarms that may cover hundreds of square kilometres. The desert locust *Schistocerca gregaria* is the most destructive; in eastern China the oriental migratory locust *Locusta migratoria manilensis*, the representative insect of [7.17.22](/7.17.22), caused recorded outbreaks for over two thousand years, mainly in the floodplains of the Yellow and Huai rivers, and its control by draining breeding grounds and by monitoring has been one of the achievements of Chinese entomology.

Other important families and species: Acrididae (the rice grasshopper *Oxya chinensis*, the long-headed grasshopper *Acrida cinerea*), Pyrgomorphidae (*Atractomorpha sinensis*), Tetrigidae (pygmy grasshoppers, with a pronotum covering the whole abdomen), Tettigoniidae (*Gampsocleis* katydids, kept as singing pets in northern China; *Mecopoda* bush crickets), Gryllidae (the field cricket *Teleogryllus emma*, the fighting cricket *Velarifictorus micado*, central to the old Chinese pastime of cricket fighting), and Gryllotalpidae (mole crickets, *Gryllotalpa orientalis* and *G. unispina*, soil pests with fossorial forelegs that sing from a burrow shaped as an acoustic horn). Many orthopterans are eaten by people, and several species are farmed.

Locust phase change is one of the best-studied phenotypic switches in insects, and Chinese laboratories have contributed much of the recent work on *Locusta migratoria*. The genome of the migratory locust, about 6.5 billion base pairs, was sequenced in 2014 and was then the largest animal genome known (Wang and coauthors 2014). The aggregation pheromone 4-vinylanisole, released by gregarious locusts and detected by the olfactory receptor OR35, attracts both nymphs and adults; mutant locusts lacking the receptor do not respond, and the compound could be used to trap swarms (Guo and coauthors 2020). Dopamine and serotonin signalling, small RNAs and epigenetic marks maintain the phases.

### 31.4.5. Phasmatodea and Embioptera

**Definition 31.4.5 (Phasmatodea)** The **Phasmatodea** (stick and leaf insects) are elongate, cylindrical or leaf-like polyneopteran insects with chewing mouthparts, a short prothorax bearing a pair of defensive glands, a greatly elongated mesothorax and metathorax, wings absent or with short tegmina and fan-like hind wings, unsegmented cerci, and herbivorous habits; about 3500 species.

Phasmids are the most perfect plant mimics among animals: twig-like body, legs and antennae, leaf-like expansions in the leaf insects (Phylliidae), lichen- and moss-like textures, and swaying movements that imitate leaves in the wind. They include the longest insects: a specimen of *Phryganistria chinensis* from Guangxi, China, measured 62.4 cm with the legs extended, and *Phobaeticus chani* from Borneo has a body of about 36 cm. Females drop or flick eggs, which in many species resemble plant seeds, with a knob (capitulum) that ants carry to their nests, as they do the elaiosomes of seeds. Facultative **parthenogenesis** is widespread, and some species consist of females only. Nymphs can regenerate lost legs at the following molts. The western North American *Timema*, wingless and short-bodied, is the sister group of all other phasmids (Euphasmatodea) and a model in the study of asexual lineages. Euphasmatodea include the Phasmatidae, Diapheromeridae and Lonchodidae, among them the giant stick insects of Southeast Asia and China (*Phryganistria*, *Phobaeticus*); the leaf insects (Phylliidae; *Phyllium* in Yunnan and Hainan); and the Lord Howe Island stick insect *Dryococelus australis*, believed extinct after rats reached the island in 1918 and rediscovered in 2001 on the sea stack Ball's Pyramid, from which a captive population has been bred. Phasmid eggs have hard, species-specific capsules with a lid (operculum) and a micropylar plate that are used in taxonomy.

**Definition 31.4.6 (Embioptera)** The **Embioptera** (webspinners) are small, elongate polyneopteran insects with a prognathous head, chewing mouthparts, three-segmented tarsi with the basal segment of the fore tarsi swollen and containing silk glands, enlarged hind femora, and two-segmented cerci; females and nymphs are wingless, and the males of most species have two pairs of soft wings stiffened by blood sinuses; about 400 species.

Webspinners live in colonies in silk galleries on bark, rocks and in litter, spinning silk from many hollow setae on the fore tarsi at every stage. They run backward as fast as forward. Females guard eggs and young; males leave the galleries to disperse and do not feed. Their wings are flexible in the gallery and stiffened for flight by hemolymph pressure. Phylogenomic data unite Embioptera and Phasmatodea (Eukinolabia).

### 31.4.6. Grylloblattodea and Mantophasmatodea

**Definition 31.4.7 (Grylloblattodea)** The **Grylloblattodea** (ice crawlers or rock crawlers) are wingless, pale, soft-bodied polyneopteran insects 15–35 mm long with a prognathous head, reduced or absent compound eyes, no ocelli, long filiform antennae, five-segmented tarsi, long five- to nine-segmented cerci and a sword-shaped ovipositor; about 35 species in one family, Grylloblattidae, in cold habitats of western North America and eastern Asia.

Ice crawlers are active near 0 °C, and many die at temperatures not much above 10 °C; North American species scavenge on insects blown onto snowfields and glaciers, while Asian species (*Galloisiana*, including *G. sinensis* from the Changbai Mountains of Jilin) live in cool forest soil and caves. Development is very slow, and a generation may take up to seven years.

**Definition 31.4.8 (Mantophasmatodea)** The **Mantophasmatodea** (heel-walkers) are wingless polyneopteran insects 1–3 cm long with a hypognathous head, long filiform antennae, chewing mouthparts, no ocelli, five-segmented tarsi with a large arolium, and one-segmented cerci, which walk with the distal tarsomeres and arolium raised off the ground; about 20 species in southern Africa.

The order was described in 2002 (Klass and coauthors) from Baltic amber fossils and museum specimens from Namibia and Tanzania, the first new insect order recognized since the Grylloblattodea in 1914, and living populations were then found in Namibia and South Africa. Jurassic fossils from Inner Mongolia show that the lineage once occurred in Asia. Heel-walkers are nocturnal predators of other insects; the sexes find each other by drumming the abdomen on the substrate. Molecular data show that Mantophasmatodea and Grylloblattodea are sister groups, and some authors unite them as one order, Notoptera; the two are kept separate here, following Brusca and coauthors.

### 31.4.7. Dictyoptera: Mantodea and Blattodea

Mantises, cockroaches and termites form the well-supported clade Dictyoptera. They share an egg case (*ootheca*), formed from secretions of the female accessory glands around a double row of eggs (reduced in most termites), a perforation in the tentorium of the head, and features of the proventriculus and genitalia. Some authors rank Dictyoptera as an order with Mantodea and Blattodea as suborders.

**Definition 31.4.9 (Mantodea)** The **Mantodea** (mantises) are predatory polyneopteran insects with a triangular, very mobile head bearing large, widely separated compound eyes, an elongate prothorax, raptorial forelegs whose spiny tibia closes on the spiny femur, forewings usually forming tegmina, and eggs laid in a foam ootheca; about 2500 species.

Mantises are ambush or stalking predators that judge distance by binocular vision (they were the first insects in which stereopsis was demonstrated, by fitting them with 3D glasses), striking in about 50–100 ms. Many are cryptic, resembling leaves, bark, lichen or flowers; the orchid mantis *Hymenopus coronatus* (Hymenopodidae) attracts pollinating insects by resembling a flower more strongly than real flowers do. Sexual cannibalism, in which the female eats the male during or after copulation, occurs in many species, though less often than in the laboratory. The foam ootheca hardens in air and protects the eggs over winter; in China the egg cases of *Tenodera sinensis*, *Hierodula patellifera* and *Statilia maculata* are a traditional medicine. Mantises are generalist predators and are used in biological control with limited effect.

The classification of mantises was revised in 2019 on molecular and genital characters into about 29 families (Schwarz and Roy 2019); among the most familiar are the Mantidae (*Tenodera*, *Hierodula*, *Mantis religiosa*), the flower mantises (Hymenopodidae, including *Creobroter* and *Hymenopus*), and the dead-leaf and stick-like mantises (Deroplatyidae, Toxoderidae). The ootheca, formed of a foam secreted by the accessory glands and shaped by the abdominal tip, contains rows of eggs in chambers that open along a central zone, through which the nymphs emerge; the first-instar nymph (pronymph) is enclosed in a cuticle that it sheds immediately after emergence. Some mantises guard their oothecae and young.

**Definition 31.4.10 (Blattodea)** The **Blattodea** (cockroaches and termites) are polyneopteran insects with a hypognathous head, chewing mouthparts, filiform or moniliform antennae, multisegmented or short cerci, and (in cockroaches) a dorsoventrally flattened body with a large shield-like pronotum, cursorial legs with five-segmented tarsi, leathery forewings and an ootheca, or (in termites) eusocial colonies with castes, a soft body, moniliform antennae and equal, deciduous wings in the reproductives; about 4500 species of cockroaches and 3000 of termites.

Of the cockroaches, fewer than 40 species live with people, among them the American cockroach *Periplaneta americana* and the oriental cockroach *Blatta orientalis* (Blattidae) and the German cockroach *Blattella germanica* (Ectobiidae), carriers of pathogens and allergens and among the most insecticide-resistant insects. Most species live in tropical forest litter, under bark and in caves. Females carry the ootheca until shortly before hatching (Ectobiidae), deposit it (Blattidae), or retain it in a brood sac (ovoviviparity, Blaberidae); in *Diploptera punctata* the embryos are fed a protein-rich "milk", true viviparity. The wingless females of *Eupolyphaga sinensis* (Corydiidae) are an important traditional Chinese medicine, farmed on a large scale. The wood roaches *Cryptocercus*, with species in the mountains of China and in North America, live as biparental families in rotting logs and digest wood with flagellates in the hindgut.

| Family | Characters | Biology and examples |
|---|---|---|
| Blattidae | Large; ootheca dropped or glued soon after formation | *Periplaneta americana*, *P. fuliginosa*, *Blatta orientalis* |
| Ectobiidae (formerly Blattellidae) | Small to medium; ootheca carried until hatching | *Blattella germanica*, the most important indoor pest; many forest species |
| Blaberidae | Ootheca retracted into a brood sac; ovoviviparous or viviparous | *Diploptera punctata*; the Madagascar hissing cockroach *Gromphadorhina portentosa*, which hisses by expelling air through spiracles |
| Corydiidae (formerly Polyphagidae) | Females often wingless, rounded; males winged | Desert and soil species; *Eupolyphaga sinensis*; some live in ant nests |
| Cryptocercidae | Wingless, robust | Wood-feeding families; sister group of termites |
| Nocticolidae and other small families | Small, pale | Including blind cave cockroaches |


**Termites** were formerly the order Isoptera. Comparative morphology and molecular data show that they are the sister group of *Cryptocercus*: both share wood-feeding, the same lineages of symbiotic gut flagellates (parabasalids and oxymonads, [7.11.20](/7.11.20)), transmitted between individuals by anal feeding (proctodeal trophallaxis), and long-lived family groups. Termites are therefore eusocial cockroaches, and cockroaches without them are paraphyletic. They are now ranked as the epifamily Termitoidae (or infraorder Isoptera) within Blattodea. The most basal living termite, the Australian *Mastotermes darwiniensis*, still lays eggs in an ootheca-like mass and has a hind wing with an anal lobe, like a cockroach.

![Relationships within Dictyoptera, simplified from phylogenomic and molecular studies (Inward and coauthors 2007; Bourguignon and coauthors 2015; Evangelista and coauthors 2019). Termites (shaded) are the sister group of the wood roaches (Cryptocercidae), with which they form the clade Tutricablattae, and lie within the cockroaches; Blattodea in the current sense (bar) includes them. Marks on the branches give characters gained (green, yellow) or lost (red) along them. Only representative families are shown; termites comprise about nine families.](./plots/dictyoptera-tree.py)

Termite families fall into "lower termites", which retain gut flagellates, and the "higher termites" (Termitidae, about 70% of species), which lost them and rely on gut bacteria, fungi or both. Important groups are Kalotermitidae (drywood termites, small colonies in sound wood), Archotermopsidae (dampwood termites), Rhinotermitidae (subterranean termites, including the Formosan subterranean termite *Coptotermes formosanus*, the most destructive pest of buildings in southern China and an invasive species elsewhere, and *Reticulitermes*) and Termitidae (including the fungus-growing Macrotermitinae, such as *Macrotermes* and *Odontotermes formosanus*, which cultivate the fungus *Termitomyces* on combs of predigested plant material, and soil-feeding and grass-harvesting species). The termite families differ in the characters of the soldiers and imagos and in colony structure:

| Family | Characters | Biology |
|---|---|---|
| Mastotermitidae | Large; hind wing with an anal lobe; eggs in an ootheca-like mass | One living species, *Mastotermes darwiniensis* of northern Australia, a destructive pest |
| Archotermopsidae, Stolotermitidae, Hodotermitidae | Large; compound eyes in imagos well developed | Dampwood termites in rotting logs; harvester termites (Hodotermitidae) in African grasslands |
| Kalotermitidae | Soldiers with large mandibles or plug-like heads that block galleries (phragmosis); no true workers | Drywood termites, small colonies in dry wood; *Cryptotermes* in furniture |
| Rhinotermitidae | Soldiers with a frontal gland pore (fontanelle) secreting defensive liquid | Subterranean termites with large colonies connected to soil; *Coptotermes*, *Reticulitermes* |
| Termitidae (higher termites) | No gut flagellates; true worker caste; soldiers diverse, including nasutes that squirt sticky secretion from a snout | Fungus growers (Macrotermitinae), soil feeders, grass and wood feeders; the largest mounds |

Termite workers and soldiers are immatures or arrested forms whose development is controlled by pheromones and hormones; castes, colony foundation and mound architecture are treated in [7.17.38](/7.17.38). Termites are among the most important decomposers of wood and litter in the tropics and a major source of atmospheric methane from their gut archaea.

| Zoraptera (Polyneoptera, probably sister to Dermaptera) | Zoraptera sometimes placed near the Psocodea among the "hemipteroid" orders | Molecular data place it in Polyneoptera |

The diagnostic characters taught in these textbooks, such as the moniliform antennae and equal wings of termites, the saltatorial hind legs and tympana of Orthoptera, and the subimago of mayflies, remain exactly as described; only the grouping of the orders has changed.

## Summary

- An insect is placed in its order by a combination of wing, mouthpart, developmental, antennal, leg and abdominal characters; the tarsal formula is used at order level in small orders and at family level in beetles. A key to the 28 orders of adult insects is given in Section 31.1.2.
- **Archaeognatha** (Definition 31.2.1; monocondylic mandible, eyes meeting, humped thorax) and **Zygentoma** (Definition 31.2.2; flattened, dicondylic) are wingless, ametabolous and molt as adults; the old Thysanura and Apterygota are paraphyletic.
- **Palaeoptera** cannot fold their wings and have aquatic naiads: **Ephemeroptera** (Definition 31.3.1), with a winged subimago that molts again and non-feeding adults, and **Odonata** (Definition 31.3.2), predators with direct flight muscles, secondary male genitalia and a naiad labial mask; Anisoptera and Zygoptera differ in wings, eyes and gills.
- **Polyneoptera** are mostly chewing, hemimetabolous insects with a hind-wing anal fan: **Plecoptera** (Definition 31.4.1), **Dermaptera** (Definition 31.4.2), **Zoraptera** (Definition 31.4.3), **Orthoptera** (Definition 31.4.4), **Phasmatodea** (Definition 31.4.5), **Embioptera** (Definition 31.4.6), **Grylloblattodea** (Definition 31.4.7), **Mantophasmatodea** (Definition 31.4.8), **Mantodea** (Definition 31.4.9) and **Blattodea** (Definition 31.4.10).
- Caelifera (short antennae, abdominal tympana, femoro-tegminal stridulation, short digging ovipositor) and Ensifera (long antennae, tibial tympana, tegminal file and scraper, long ovipositor) are the two suborders of Orthoptera; locusts are grasshoppers with density-dependent phase polyphenism.
- Termites are eusocial cockroaches, the sister group of *Cryptocercus*; the former order Isoptera is a subgroup of Blattodea, which with Mantodea forms Dictyoptera.

## Questions

1. A small, wingless insect from under bark has three long tails, a scaly body and widely separated small eyes. Which order is it, and which characters distinguish it from the order it most resembles?

   **Answer** It is a silverfish (Zygentoma). The similar jumping bristletails (Archaeognatha) have a cylindrical body with a humped thorax, large compound eyes meeting on top of the head, long maxillary palps, a median caudal filament distinctly longer than the cerci and a monocondylic mandible; silverfish are flattened, their eyes are small and separated or absent, the three tails are about equally long, and the mandible has two condyles.

2. Why is the subimago of mayflies considered remarkable, and what might be its function?

   **Answer** In all other pterygote insects the stage with functional wings is the adult, which never molts again; mayflies molt once after acquiring functional wings, from the subimago to the imago. The subimago may be a remnant of the ancestral condition in which early winged insects continued to molt as adults (as apterygotes do); its water-repellent microtrichia may help the insect emerge through the water surface, and the final molt allows the adult to acquire long legs and caudal filaments needed in mating flights that could not be formed in the naiad cuticle.

3. Name four characters by which a katydid can be distinguished from a grasshopper, and explain which of them relate to sound communication.

   **Answer** Katydids (Ensifera) have antennae longer than the body, tympanal organs on the fore tibiae, a file and scraper on the forewings, and a long blade-like ovipositor; grasshoppers (Caelifera) have short antennae, tympanal organs on abdominal segment 1, pegs on the hind femur rubbed against the tegmen, and a short ovipositor of digging valves. The positions of the ears and of the stridulatory apparatus relate to sound communication; they arose independently in the two suborders, as their different positions show.

4. On what evidence are termites placed within the cockroaches rather than in their own order?

   **Answer** Molecular phylogenies consistently recover termites as the sister group of the wood roach *Cryptocercus*, inside a clade of cockroach families, so that an order of cockroaches excluding termites would be paraphyletic. Independent evidence supports this: termites and *Cryptocercus* share wood feeding, closely related lineages of symbiotic hindgut flagellates transmitted by proctodeal trophallaxis, and long-lived family groups; the basal termite *Mastotermes* retains an ootheca-like egg mass and an anal lobe in the hind wing, cockroach characters lost in other termites.

5. Use the key in Section 31.1.2 to identify an insect with one pair of membranous forewings, tiny club-like hind wings and a sponging proboscis, and explain why couplet 3 asks about the mouthparts.

   **Answer** Couplet 1 (wings present) → 2 (one pair of wings) → 3 (halteres and a functional proboscis): Diptera. The mouthparts are included because male scale insects also have one pair of wings and small hind-wing remnants (hamulohalteres), but their mouthparts are absent; asking for a functional proboscis prevents them from being keyed out as flies, and they are keyed instead at couplet 4″.

6. A naiad collected from a stream has two long tails, tufts of gills between the legs and two claws on each tarsus. To which order does it belong, and which features would distinguish it from a mayfly naiad?

   **Answer** It is a stonefly naiad (Plecoptera). Mayfly naiads usually have three tails (two cerci and a median caudal filament, sometimes only two), gills as plates or filaments on the sides or back of abdominal segments 1–7, and a single claw on each tarsus; stonefly naiads have two cerci, gills (when present) on the thorax, at the leg bases or at the base of the abdomen, and two tarsal claws. Odonate naiads are excluded by the absence of a labial mask.

7. (Open question) Phasmatodea are often described as "the most perfect plant mimics". Which observations could test whether a particular stick insect's resemblance to twigs reduces predation, and what alternative explanations should be considered?

   **Answer** Experiments with natural predators (birds, lizards) can compare detection or attack rates of stick insects on matching and mismatching backgrounds, of models differing in shape, or of live insects that sway versus those held still. Comparative studies can test whether twig-like forms evolved repeatedly in lineages exposed to visually hunting predators. Alternative explanations to consider are that an elongate shape is favoured for other reasons (movement through foliage, thermoregulation), that resemblance mainly prevents recognition rather than detection (masquerade versus crypsis), and that chemical defences from the prothoracic glands contribute to survival.
