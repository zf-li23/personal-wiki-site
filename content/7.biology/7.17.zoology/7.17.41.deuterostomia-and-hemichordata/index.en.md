The deuterostomes are the smallest of the three great clades of bilaterians, with only three phyla, but they include the most familiar animals of the sea shore, the echinoderms, and the phylum to which we ourselves belong, the chordates. The third phylum, the hemichordates, is known to few people outside zoology: about 140 species of acorn worms that burrow in marine sediments and of tiny colonial pterobranchs that live in secreted tubes. Yet hemichordates hold a key place in the history of zoology. They have pharyngeal gill slits like chordates, a short dorsal nerve cord that develops by rolling up like a neural tube, and a stiff rod in the head once mistaken for a notochord; their larva closely resembles that of echinoderms. For more than a century they were treated as the link between the invertebrates and the chordates.

This chapter first describes the deuterostomes as a whole: their composition, their shared characters and their earliest fossils (§41.1). It then treats the hemichordates: their trimeric body plan (§41.2), the acorn worms (§41.3) and the pterobranchs (§41.4). Section 41.5 asks which hemichordate characters are really shared with chordates, and §41.6 compares the current view with the traditional classification. The fate of the blastopore and the classical protostome–deuterostome contrast were discussed in [7.17.5](/7.17.5) and [7.17.10](/7.17.10); the position of the deuterostomes on the animal tree is in [7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.5.4). The echinoderms follow in [7.17.42](/7.17.42), the invertebrate chordates in [7.17.43](/7.17.43).

## 41.1. The Deuterostomes

### 41.1.1. Composition and Shared Characters

The **Deuterostomia** were named for the fate of the blastopore, which in their embryos becomes the anus or closes near it while the mouth breaks through secondarily at the opposite end of the archenteron. As shown in [7.17.5](/7.17.5#sec-5.2.2), this criterion has exceptions on both sides, and the clade is now defined by molecular phylogeny. It contains three phyla: echinoderms, hemichordates and chordates. Four phyla formerly placed here on embryological grounds, the brachiopods, phoronids, bryozoans and chaetognaths, are protostomes ([7.17.12](/7.17.12), [7.17.16](/7.17.16)).

The body plans of the three phyla diverge so strongly that few anatomical characters unite them all. The candidates are:

| Character | Distribution | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Pharyngeal slits | Hemichordates, chordates; some fossil stem-group echinoderms (disputed) | Homologous by their development and gene expression; lost in crown-group echinoderms |
| Tripartite coelom (archimery): protocoel, mesocoel, metacoel | Hemichordates, echinoderm larvae; not recognizable in chordates | Possibly a synapomorphy of Ambulacraria rather than of all deuterostomes |
| Coelom by **enterocoely** | Most hemichordates and echinoderms, amphioxus | Vertebrates form the coelom by splitting of lateral plate mesoderm; some hemichordate cavities form by schizocoely |
| **Radial cleavage**, mouth not from the blastopore | All three phyla, with exceptions | Plesiomorphic: also found outside the clade |
| Mesodermal endoskeleton | Echinoderms (calcite), vertebrates (cartilage, bone) | Not homologous between the two |

The strongest evidence for the clade is genomic. Hemichordate genomes contain a cluster of six genes in a fixed order, four of them transcription factors (*nkx2.1*, *nkx2.2*, *pax1/9*, *foxA*) expressed in the pharyngeal endoderm during the development of the gill slits; the same cluster is conserved in amphioxus, vertebrates and echinoderms but not outside the deuterostomes (Simakov et al. 2015). Deuterostomes also share a set of novel gene families with putative functions in filter feeding, suggesting that their common ancestor fed by pumping water through its pharynx. Several posterior **Hox genes** of deuterostomes (*Hox9–13*) were duplicated independently of those of protostomes.

### 41.1.2. Ambulacraria and Chordata

Within the deuterostomes, phylogenomic analyses consistently recover two clades. The chordates (cephalochordates, tunicates and vertebrates) form one; the echinoderms and hemichordates form the other.

**Definition 41.1.1 (Ambulacraria)** The **Ambulacraria** (ambulacrarians) are the clade of deuterostomes comprising the echinoderms and the hemichordates, the sister group of the Chordata. Their proposed synapomorphies are a larva of the dipleurula type with a single circumoral ciliary band, an axial complex (an excretory organ formed by a protocoel and a blood sinus with a pore to the exterior), a tripartite coelom with a hydropore, and a mitochondrial genetic code in which AUA codes for isoleucine rather than methionine.

The name comes from the ambulacra, the radial grooves of echinoderms; it had been coined in the nineteenth century, when Metschnikoff noticed the similarity of the tornaria larva of acorn worms to echinoderm larvae (§41.3.4). The pairing is morphologically surprising, because adult echinoderms share almost nothing visible with acorn worms, while hemichordates share gill slits and a dorsal nerve cord with chordates. The phylogeny implies that the characters shared by hemichordates and chordates, if homologous, were present in the last common ancestor of all deuterostomes and were lost in echinoderms. This simple inference, which inverts the classical reading of hemichordates as "half chordates", is the key to the evolutionary interpretation in §41.5.

The position of the Xenacoelomorpha is the main open question at the base of the deuterostomes. If the Xenambulacraria hypothesis is correct, these small worms without an anus are the sister group of Ambulacraria and descend from a deuterostome ancestor with gill slits ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.5.4)). Most recent analyses place them outside, as the sister group of Nephrozoa, and the tree in the figure follows this view.

### 41.1.3. Cambrian Deuterostomes

Echinoderms, hemichordates, tunicates and vertebrates are all present by the early Cambrian, and molecular clocks place the origin of the clade in the Ediacaran. Several extinct groups from the Chengjiang biota of Yunnan (about 518 million years ago) and the Burgess Shale have been interpreted as deuterostomes, but their positions are disputed:

- *Vetulicolians* had a bipartite body: a barrel-shaped anterior part with five pairs of lateral openings, interpreted as gill slits, and a segmented tail without appendages. They have been placed in the stem groups of deuterostomes, of chordates or of tunicates.
- *Yunnanozoans* (*Yunnanozoon*, with *Haikouella* probably a synonym) are fusiform animals a few centimetres long with serial pharyngeal bars and a segmented trunk. They have been interpreted as stem deuterostomes, hemichordates, stem chordates or, on the basis of cellular cartilage in their pharyngeal arches, as stem vertebrates (Tian et al. 2022). Chinese textbooks treat *Haikouella* as an early hemichordate.
- *Cambroernids* (*Herpetogaster*, the discoidal eldoniids) had a coiled gut in a coelomic sac and tentacles, and may be stem ambulacrarians.

*Saccorhytus coronarius*, a millimetric sac-like fossil from the earliest Cambrian of Shaanxi, was described in 2017 as the oldest deuterostome on the basis of lateral pores interpreted as precursors of gill slits. Restudy of better-preserved material showed that the pores are the bases of spines and that *Saccorhytus* is an early ecdysozoan without gill slits (Liu et al. 2022). The oldest secure deuterostomes are therefore Cambrian Series 2 fossils such as the earliest echinoderms (about 520 million years ago) and the vertebrates *Haikouichthys* and *Myllokunmingia* ([7.17.45](/7.17.45)).

## 41.2. Phylum Hemichordata

**Definition 41.2.1 (Hemichordata)** The **Hemichordata** (hemichordates) are a phylum of marine, bilaterally symmetrical deuterostomes whose body is divided into three regions, a prosome (proboscis or cephalic shield), a mesosome (collar) and a metasome (trunk), each with its own coelomic compartment; they have pharyngeal slits (or a single pair), a stomochord (a preoral diverticulum of the gut), a heart–glomerulus complex in the prosome serving excretion, and a short dorsal nerve cord in the collar.

The name, "half chordates", was given by Bateson in 1885, who saw in the stomochord a short notochord; for decades hemichordates were classified as a subphylum of Chordata. The phylum has two classes of very different appearance: the solitary, burrowing **Enteropneusta** (acorn worms, about 120 species) and the colonial, tube-dwelling **Pterobranchia** (about 30 living species, and many fossil graptolites). Phylogenomic data support the monophyly of both classes and of the phylum (Cannon et al. 2014). Earlier analyses based on ribosomal genes placed pterobranchs within the enteropneusts, which led Nielsen to treat the two as separate phyla; this is no longer necessary.

| Class | Body | Habit | Gill slits | Nerve cord | Development |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enteropneusta | Vermiform, 1 mm to over 2 m; proboscis, collar, long trunk | Solitary, free-living; burrowing or epibenthic | Many pairs (1 to over 100), U-shaped, with tongue bars | Dorsal collar cord, hollow in some; dorsal and ventral trunk cords | Direct, or indirect via a tornaria larva |
| Pterobranchia | Sac-like zooids under 1 cm; cephalic shield, collar with tentaculate arms, trunk and stalk; U-shaped gut | Colonial or aggregated, in secreted tubes (coenecium) | One pair (*Cephalodiscus*) or none (*Rhabdopleura*) | Collar ganglion, intraepidermal | Short-lived, nonfeeding larva; budding |

### 41.2.1. The Trimeric Body Plan

Both classes share the tripartite organization that is thought to be ancestral for ambulacrarians. The three regions are, from front to back:

1. The prosome (proboscis in enteropneusts, cephalic shield in pterobranchs) contains a single, unpaired protocoel, which opens to the exterior through one or two pores. It houses the heart–glomerulus complex and the stomochord.
2. The mesosome (collar) contains a pair of mesocoels, which open through collar pores. In pterobranchs the mesocoels extend into the arms and tentacles and drive them hydraulically.
3. The metasome (trunk) contains a pair of metacoels without openings; it holds the pharynx, the gut and the gonads, and in pterobranchs continues into a contractile stalk.

The coelomic cavities arise by **enterocoely**: in the direct-developing acorn worm *Saccoglossus kowalevskii* each of the five cavities pinches off separately from the archenteron (Kaul-Strehlow and Stach 2013). In indirect developers only the protocoel forms in the embryo; the mesocoels and metacoels appear late in the larva. The coelomic fluid of the proboscis and collar, contained by muscular walls, acts as a **hydrostatic skeleton**, the main support of the body.

## 41.3. Class Enteropneusta: Acorn Worms

**Definition 41.3.1 (Enteropneusta)** The **Enteropneusta** (acorn worms) are hemichordates with an elongate, vermiform body divided into a muscular proboscis, a short collar bearing the ventral mouth, and a long trunk with two dorsolateral rows of gill pores and a terminal anus; they are solitary and free-living.

Acorn worms live in U-shaped burrows in sand and mud, under stones or among algal holdfasts, from the intertidal zone to the deep sea; deep-sea species of the family Torquaratoridae crawl over the sediment surface and can swim. The proboscis gives the class its common name: protruding from the collar, it resembles an acorn in its cup. Chinese textbooks use *Balanoglossus* as the representative animal; *Saccoglossus kowalevskii* of the North American Atlantic coast and *Ptychodera flava* of the Pacific have become developmental models, and several Chinese species, among them *Glossobalanus polybranchioporus* of Xiamen and *Saccoglossus hwangtauensis* of Qingdao, are protected.

### 41.3.1. Body Wall and Support

The epidermis is a tall, multiciliated epithelium rich in gland cells, with a nerve plexus at its base. It secretes copious mucus, used for feeding and for lining the burrow, which in many species has an iodine-like or bromine-like smell from halogenated phenols that may deter predators. The muscles, which derive from the coelomic linings, are circular and longitudinal in the proboscis and collar but mainly longitudinal in the trunk. Peristaltic waves of the proboscis, braced against the collar, move the worm through the sediment; the proboscis is withdrawn against the collar to close the mouth "like a cork in a bottle".

Two kinds of supporting structures stiffen the body. The proboscis skeleton, a Y-shaped plate of thickened basement membrane, lies beneath the stomochord and extends two horns into the collar; similar collagenous rods support the gill bars. The *stomochord* is a hollow, blind outgrowth of the roof of the buccal cavity that projects forward into the proboscis stalk. Its wall is a vacuolated epithelium, which makes it a stiff, turgid rod against which the contractile heart vesicle presses. Because it is endodermal, lies anterior to the mouth and does not express the transcription factors that specify the notochord along the dorsal axis, it is not homologous with the chordate notochord (§41.5).

### 41.3.2. Feeding, the Pharynx and Gill Slits

Most acorn worms are deposit feeders that can also filter suspended particles. Particles stick to the mucus on the proboscis and are carried backward by cilia toward the mouth; large particles are rejected over the collar, and food passes ventrally over a horseshoe-shaped preoral ciliary organ, probably chemosensory, into the mouth as a mucous cord. Sediment-swallowing species such as *Balanoglossus* leave coiled casts at the exit of their burrows.

The pharynx occupies the anterior trunk. Its dorsal half is perforated by two rows of U-shaped slits, from one pair in the meiofaunal *Meioglossus* to more than a hundred in large species; new slits are added at the posterior end as the worm grows. Each slit is divided by a tongue bar (a secondary gill bar) descending from its dorsal margin, and adjacent slits are separated by primary bars; in ptychoderids small cross-bridges, the synapticles, join the two. Water drawn into the mouth by the lateral cilia of the bars passes through the slits into branchial sacs and leaves through dorsolateral gill pores. The slits thus separate the feeding current from the food: particles are retained in pharyngeal mucus, rolled into a cord and passed into the oesophagus, as in the **filter feeding** of amphioxus. Their role in gas exchange, long assumed by analogy with fish gills, is doubtful; most gas exchange is probably across the body surface. Behind the pharynx the gut is a straight tube with a hepatic region of dark green or brown sacculations, where digestion and absorption take place.

### 41.3.3. Circulation, Excretion and the Nervous System

The circulatory system is open, of vessels and blood sinuses between the basement membranes ([7.14.12](/7.14.12)). Blood flows forward in a dorsal vessel and backward in a ventral vessel, the reverse of the direction in chordates. At the base of the proboscis the dorsal vessel widens into a central sinus, which lies between the stomochord below and the heart vesicle above. The heart vesicle is a closed sac with myoepithelial walls; its contraction squeezes the central sinus against the stiff stomochord and forces blood into the glomerulus. The glomerulus is a set of finger-like folds of the protocoel wall whose lining cells are podocytes, which filter blood into the protocoel; the primary urine leaves through the proboscis pore. This heart–glomerulus complex is a filtration kidney like the axial complex of echinoderms and, functionally, like a vertebrate glomerulus, a striking example of the general principle that animal excretory organs filter first and modify the filtrate afterward ([7.14.21](/7.14.21)).

The nervous system is largely a basiepidermal nerve plexus with condensations along the dorsal and ventral midlines of the trunk. In the collar, however, the dorsal cord lies beneath the epidermis as a separate collar cord, formed during development by an infolding of the dorsal epidermis in a way that resembles chordate **neurulation** ([7.5.18](/7.5.18)); in some species it contains a central canal or isolated lumina and opens to the exterior at both ends by neuropores. Adult acorn worms have no eyes and few sense organs, but the tornaria has eyespots.

### 41.3.4. Reproduction and Development

The sexes are separate. Numerous sac-like gonads lie in the anterior trunk, in ptychoderids within lateral genital wings, each opening by its own pore; fertilization is external. Acorn worms regenerate well, and some reproduce asexually by transverse fission; *Ptychodera flava* can regenerate an entire proboscis, collar and nervous system from a piece of trunk.

Cleavage is radial, holoblastic and nearly equal, and gastrulation is by invagination. The blastopore marks the posterior end but closes, and both mouth and anus form later. There are two modes of development:

- Direct development, from yolky eggs, in the Harrimaniidae (*Saccoglossus*): a short-lived, nonfeeding larva elongates and becomes a small worm within days.
- Indirect development, from small eggs, in the Ptychoderidae and Spengelidae (*Balanoglossus*, *Ptychodera*): the embryo becomes a planktotrophic tornaria larva that feeds in the plankton for weeks to months.

**Definition 41.3.2 (Tornaria)** The **tornaria** is the planktotrophic larva of indirectly developing enteropneusts: a transparent, bilaterally symmetrical larva with an apical plate bearing eyespots and a ciliary tuft, a through-gut, a protocoel with a dorsal hydropore, a looped circumoral ciliary band that circles the mouth field and collects food particles, and, in later stages, a posterior ring of compound cilia, the telotroch, for swimming.

The tornaria was described by Johannes Müller in 1850 as an echinoderm larva. Its circumoral ciliary band, made of monociliated cells that create the feeding current and capture particles upstream of the band, runs in the same course as that of the auricularia of sea cucumbers and the bipinnaria of sea stars ([7.17.42](/7.17.42)), and its protocoel with a hydropore corresponds to the axocoel of echinoderm larvae. These larvae are the basis of the idealized **dipleurula** of [7.17.5](/7.17.5#def-5.4.2). At metamorphosis the tornaria sinks, the proboscis and collar grow out, the gill slits form as pouches of the pharynx that open to the exterior (the first on the left side), and the young worm begins to burrow.

![The tornaria of an enteropneust compared with the bipinnaria of a sea star, schematic ventral views (anterior up), with the ciliary bands green and the gut yellow. In both larvae a single looped ciliary band (circumoral band) surrounds the mouth field and drives the feeding current; the gut runs from mouth to anus; an anterior coelomic sac (protocoel or axocoel) opens through a dorsal hydropore. The tornaria adds an apical plate with eyespots and, later, a posterior telotroch; the bipinnaria later separates a preoral loop from the postoral band and develops the arms of the brachiolaria. The similarity of the two larvae, first noted by Metschnikoff in 1869, is among the oldest evidence for the Ambulacraria.](./plots/larvae-comparison.py)

Molecular patterning has changed the interpretation of the tornaria. Genes that pattern the anterior–posterior axis of the adult are expressed in the larva only in their anterior domains, and the posterior Hox genes are not expressed until metamorphosis: the tornaria is essentially a "swimming head", and the trunk is added at metamorphosis (Gonzalez et al. 2017). The same is probably true of echinoderm larvae, which therefore cannot be compared region by region with a chordate tadpole, as the classical hypotheses of chordate origin assumed ([7.17.43](/7.17.43)).

## 41.4. Class Pterobranchia

**Definition 41.4.1 (Pterobranchia)** The **Pterobranchia** (pterobranchs) are minute (usually less than 1 cm), colonial or aggregated hemichordates whose zooids have a ciliated cephalic shield (the prosome), a collar bearing one to nine pairs of arms fringed with ciliated tentacles, a sac-like trunk with a U-shaped gut and a dorsal anus near the collar, and a contractile stalk; the zooids live in tubes secreted by the cephalic shield, which together form the colony's coenecium.

There are two living genera. *Rhabdopleura* forms true colonies: its zooids, each in its own tube, are connected by a living stolon and have two arms and no gill slits. *Cephalodiscus* forms aggregations of zooids in a common, spiny tubular housing; the zooids are not permanently connected, bear up to nine pairs of arms and have one pair of gill slits. Pterobranchs feed like bryozoans and other tentaculate filter feeders: the arms and tentacles form a sphere or funnel, cilia drive water through it, and particles caught in mucus are carried down food grooves to the mouth. This resemblance to lophophorates, although convergent, explains why the first *Rhabdopleura* were described as bryozoans in 1866.

Colonies grow by budding from the stalk or stolon, beginning with a single sexually produced zooid. Sexual reproduction is poorly known; in *Rhabdopleura* the embryos are brooded in the tube and released as short-lived, ciliated, nonfeeding larvae that settle within a day, enclose themselves in a cocoon and metamorphose.

Pterobranchs are the living remnants of a group that was abundant in the Paleozoic seas: the graptolites. Graptolite colonies (rhabdosomes) were built of collagenous tubes (thecae) with a characteristic growth pattern of half-rings and zigzag sutures, the fusellar structure, which is also found in the tubes of *Rhabdopleura*. Benthic, branching graptolites (Dendroidea) appeared in the middle Cambrian; planktonic graptoloids, which evolved in the Ordovician, diversified rapidly into many colony shapes and spread across the oceans, and their rapid succession of species makes them some of the most important index fossils of the Ordovician and Silurian. The graptoloids died out in the Early Devonian, the dendroids in the Carboniferous. Phylogenetic analyses treat *Rhabdopleura* as a living graptolite, so that Pterobranchia and Graptolithoidea are now regarded as the same clade (Mitchell et al. 2013; Maletz 2014).

## 41.5. Hemichordates and the Origin of Chordates

### 41.5.1. Which Characters Are Shared?

Because hemichordates are ambulacrarians, any character they share with chordates by homology must have been present in the ancestral deuterostome and lost by echinoderms. Each candidate must therefore be examined on its own:

| Hemichordate character | Chordate counterpart | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Pharyngeal slits with supporting bars | **Pharyngeal slits** | Homologous: same position in the pharyngeal endoderm, expression of *pax1/9*, *six1/2* and *foxC*, the conserved gene cluster; tongue bars and synapticles also in amphioxus |
| Stomochord | **Notochord** | Not homologous: endodermal, preoral, different gene expression; the notochord forms from dorsal mesoderm |
| Collar cord, formed by infolding | **Dorsal hollow nerve cord** | Disputed: similar morphogenesis, but the collar cord is short and acorn worms have an extensive nerve net and ventral cord; the chordate cord is induced by the notochord |
| Iodine-binding cells in the pharynx, epibranchial ridge | **Endostyle** | Possibly homologous as an iodine-concentrating pharyngeal epithelium, but hemichordates have no endostyle as such |
| Postanal region of juvenile harrimaniids | Muscular postanal tail | Probably not homologous; it corresponds to the pterobranch stalk, although posterior Hox genes are expressed in both |
| Heart–glomerulus complex | Glomerulus of the vertebrate kidney | Analogous filtration organs |

The table leaves two characters with strong support, the pharyngeal slits and the pharyngeal gene cluster, and a probable one, an iodine-binding pharyngeal epithelium. Together they indicate that the ancestral deuterostome was a filter feeder that pumped water through its pharynx, a conclusion also supported by the deuterostome-specific gene families for filter feeding. The notochord, the dorsal hollow nerve cord and the muscular tail, by contrast, are chordate innovations.

### 41.5.2. The Ectoderm of an Acorn Worm as a Map of the Vertebrate Brain

A second line of evidence comes from developmental genes. In *Saccoglossus* embryos the transcription factors that pattern the vertebrate neural plate from front to back, among them *six3*, *otx*, *dlx*, *emx*, *gbx*, *engrailed* and the Hox genes, are expressed in the same order along the body, but in rings that encircle the whole ectoderm rather than on its dorsal side (Lowe et al. 2003). Signalling centres that organize the vertebrate brain, such as the anterior neural ridge, the zona limitans intrathalamica and the isthmus, have counterparts expressing *fgf8*, *sonic hedgehog* and *wnt1* in acorn worm ectoderm (Pani et al. 2012). The anteroposterior map of the deuterostome body is therefore older than the chordate nervous system; what chordates added was the concentration of neural tissue on one side.

The dorsoventral axis is patterned in acorn worms, as in protostomes, by BMP on the dorsal side and its antagonist Chordin on the ventral side, the reverse of chordates. This observation, together with the dorsal position of the gill pores and the direction of blood flow, supports the hypothesis that the chordate body is dorsoventrally inverted relative to that of other bilaterians, which is discussed in [7.17.43](/7.17.43).

### 41.5.3. The Ancestral Deuterostome

The current reconstruction, consistent with the phylogeny, the shared genes and the Cambrian fossils, is a solitary, worm-like, bilaterally symmetrical animal with a tripartite body, a through-gut with pharyngeal slits used for filter feeding, a diffuse basiepidermal nervous system with some longitudinal condensations, and probably a feeding larva. It resembled an acorn worm more than a pterobranch or an echinoderm. Older scenarios derived the deuterostomes from a sessile, tentaculate, pterobranch-like ancestor, because pterobranchs were thought to be the earliest branch of hemichordates; with the two classes as sister groups and colonial tube-dwelling now known also in Cambrian and deep-sea enteropneusts, there is no reason to place a pterobranch at the base.

## 41.6. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Hemichordates as a subphylum of Chordata (19th century) | A separate phylum, sister group of echinoderms in Ambulacraria |
| The stomochord as a rudimentary notochord | A preoral diverticulum of the gut, not homologous with the notochord |
| Hemichordates as "transitional forms" between invertebrates and chordates | A sister lineage of echinoderms; characters shared with chordates (gill slits) are ancestral deuterostome features lost in echinoderms |
| Deuterostomes defined by the fate of the blastopore, radial cleavage and enterocoely; chaetognaths and lophophorates included | Defined by molecular phylogeny and the pharyngeal gene cluster; only three phyla |
| Chordates derived from an echinoderm-like or hemichordate-like ancestor (the "echinoderm theory") | Ambulacraria and Chordata are sister groups; neither is ancestral to the other |
| Creatine and arginine phosphagens as evidence for kinship: chordates with creatine, invertebrates with arginine, echinoderms and hemichordates with both | The distribution of phosphagens is scattered: creatine kinase occurs in sponges, cnidarians, annelids and sea urchin sperm, so the character has no phylogenetic value |
| *Haikouella* as the oldest hemichordate | A yunnanozoan of disputed position (stem deuterostome, stem chordate or stem vertebrate) |

Chinese textbooks describe the tornaria as resembling the bipinnaria of sea stars or the auricularia of sea cucumbers; both comparisons are correct, because the two echinoderm larvae are variants of the same dipleurula plan. The representative animal *Balanoglossus* is treated in §41.3 in the same terms as in those textbooks: proboscis, collar and trunk; proboscis, collar and trunk coeloms opening by pores; stomochord; U-shaped gill slits; open circulation with the glomerulus as an excretory organ; dorsal and ventral nerve cords joined by a ring in the collar.

## Summary

- Deuterostomes comprise three phyla in two clades, Ambulacraria (echinoderms and hemichordates, Definition 41.1.1) and Chordata. Their most robust shared characters are pharyngeal slits and a conserved pharyngeal gene cluster; enterocoely, a tripartite coelom and a secondary mouth are widespread but not universal. Lophophorates and chaetognaths are protostomes.
- Cambrian vetulicolians, yunnanozoans and cambroernids are probable deuterostomes of uncertain position; *Saccorhytus* is an ecdysozoan.
- Hemichordates (Definition 41.2.1) have a prosome, mesosome and metasome, each with its coelom (protocoel, paired mesocoels and metacoels), a stomochord, a heart–glomerulus complex and a dorsal collar cord.
- Acorn worms (Definition 41.3.1): burrowing deposit and filter feeders with U-shaped gill slits and tongue bars, an open circulation with the blood flowing forward dorsally, a glomerulus that filters into the protocoel, a collar cord formed by neurulation-like infolding, and direct development or a tornaria larva (Definition 41.3.2), a "swimming head" resembling echinoderm larvae.
- Pterobranchs (Definition 41.4.1): tiny colonial tube-dwellers with tentaculate arms (*Rhabdopleura*, *Cephalodiscus*); they are living graptolites.
- Homologous with chordates: pharyngeal slits and the anteroposterior map of the ectoderm. Not homologous: stomochord and notochord. The ancestral deuterostome was probably a solitary, filter-feeding worm with gill slits.

## Questions

1. Why does the sister-group relationship of hemichordates and echinoderms change the interpretation of the gill slits of acorn worms?

   **Answer** If hemichordates were the sister group of chordates, the gill slits could be a synapomorphy of a hemichordate–chordate clade, and echinoderms would never have had them. Because hemichordates are the sister group of echinoderms, the gill slits shared by hemichordates and chordates, being homologous (same developmental genes and position), must have been present in the last common ancestor of all deuterostomes and were lost in the lineage of crown-group echinoderms. They are a deuterostome character, not evidence that hemichordates are "half chordates".
2. Give three reasons why the stomochord is not regarded as homologous with the notochord.

   **Answer** (a) Origin: the stomochord is an outgrowth of the gut endoderm (the roof of the buccal cavity), whereas the notochord forms from dorsal mesoderm (chordamesoderm) separate from the gut. (b) Position: the stomochord lies in the proboscis, anterior to the mouth, whereas the notochord lies dorsal to the gut along the trunk and tail. (c) Gene expression and function: the stomochord does not express the notochord program (*brachyury* and its targets in a dorsal axial strip) and does not induce or support a nerve cord; it braces the heart vesicle. The superficial similarity, a rod of vacuolated cells, is convergent.
3. In an acorn worm, blood flows forward in the dorsal vessel; in amphioxus, backward. How does this observation bear on the hypothesis of dorsoventral inversion?

   **Answer** In annelids and acorn worms the main contractile vessel is dorsal and carries blood forward, and the ventral vessel carries it backward. In chordates the heart and the contractile ventral aorta lie ventrally and carry blood forward, and the dorsal aorta carries it backward. If the chordate body is the acorn-worm body turned upside down, the vessels keep their direction of flow but exchange positions, which is what is observed. The same reversal is seen in the BMP and Chordin domains and in the dorsal position of the gill pores in hemichordates versus the ventral position of the slits' external openings and the endostyle in chordates. Each observation alone is weak; together they support the inversion.
4. The heart–glomerulus complex of acorn worms and the glomerulus of the vertebrate kidney are both filtration organs. Why are they considered analogous rather than homologous?

   **Answer** They occupy different positions (the hemichordate glomerulus in the proboscis, associated with the protocoel; the vertebrate glomeruli in the trunk, associated with the nephric tubules of intermediate mesoderm), develop from different tissues, and are not connected by intermediate forms in amphioxus or tunicates, which have other excretory organs. Both use podocytes because ultrafiltration across a basement membrane covered by podocyte-like cells is a general solution found in many animals (also in the axial organ of echinoderms and the nephridia of amphioxus). Similar function and cell type are not enough for homology without positional and developmental correspondence.
5. Graptolites are excellent index fossils. Which properties of the group explain this, and why is their fossil record so much better than that of enteropneusts?

   **Answer** Graptoloids were planktonic, so a species spread quickly over wide oceanic areas; they evolved rapidly, so each species existed for a short time; and they were abundant and easily recognized by the shape of their colonies. These properties allow rocks on different continents to be correlated within narrow time intervals. Their colonies were built of decay-resistant collagenous tubes (the rhabdosome), which fossilize well as carbonized films in shales, whereas enteropneusts are soft-bodied worms that decay within days and are preserved only in exceptional deposits such as the Burgess Shale.
