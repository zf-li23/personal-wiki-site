Sixty-nine chapters have described the animals one group at a time and, for the vertebrates, one organ system at a time. This final chapter does not repeat them. It reads the course again along the single thread that ran through it, the animal tree of [7.17.6](/7.17.6), and asks three questions of the material. Where on the tree did each feature of the major body plans arise, and in what order were the body plans assembled? Which features arose once and were inherited, and which arose again and again as independent answers to the same functional problem? And what general patterns, the extraordinary diversity of insects, the transformation of the vertebrate head and its arches, the replacement of a ladder of grades by a branching tree, emerge only when the chapters are put side by side?

The chapter is organized accordingly. Section 70.1 recalls how the tree replaced the ladder of the older textbooks and how an innovation is placed on it. Section 70.2 walks up the tree from the first animals to birds and mammals, node by node. Section 70.3 collects the cases of **convergent evolution** into one table and asks why convergence is so common. Section 70.4 assembles the explanation of insect diversity from the entomology chapters, and Section 70.5 draws together five threads of vertebrate comparative anatomy that run through Parts VII and VIII. Section 70.6 sets the phyla and classes of the Chinese textbooks against the modern system in one table, and Section 70.7 lists the links to the other biology courses on this site. Every statement is linked to the chapter and section where it is developed; the summary adds no new material, only a new arrangement.

## 70.1. From a Ladder to a Tree

### 70.1.1. Grades and Clades

The zoology of the twentieth century arranged animals in a ladder of grades of organization ([7.17.2](/7.17.2#sec-2.1), [7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.6)): protozoa, then sponges at the cellular grade, cnidarians at the tissue grade with two germ layers, flatworms as the first triploblastic, acoelomate bilaterians, "pseudocoelomates" with a body cavity and a through gut, and finally the coelomates, divided into protostomes and deuterostomes, with annelids and arthropods united as Articulata. Each rung was given an "evolutionary significance": the mesoderm of flatworms, the complete gut of the pseudocoelomates, the coelom and segmentation of annelids, the notochord of chordates. Chinese textbooks of general zoology still teach the phyla in this order, and the order is pedagogically sound, because it proceeds from simple to complex.

The molecular phylogenies of the last thirty years ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.4)) showed that the ladder is not the history. A **grade** (Definition 3.1.2) is a level of organization that may be reached several times or abandoned; a clade is a group of all descendants of one ancestor. On the current tree ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.5)) Bilateria, Protostomia, Deuterostomia, Ecdysozoa and Spiralia are clades, whereas Radiata, Acoelomata, **Aschelminthes**, Coelomata and Articulata are not ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.6)). Flatworms are not the most primitive bilaterians but spiralians related to molluscs and annelids ([7.17.11](/7.17.11#sec-11.8)); the "pseudocoelomates" are scattered over both protostome clades ([7.17.12](/7.17.12#sec-12.7), [7.17.17](/7.17.17#sec-17.7)); annelids and arthropods are in different superclades, so their segmentation evolved separately ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.5.2)).

The change did not make the old characters wrong. The descriptions of what each feature does, a coelom as a hydrostatic skeleton, a through gut as an assembly line, a centralized nervous system for directed movement, remain valid functional explanations ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.11.4)). What changed is their reading as a sequence. A character that the ladder placed on one rung now appears as an innovation at a particular node of the tree, often followed by losses in lineages that became small, sessile or parasitic, and often reappearing elsewhere by convergence. Simplification is as common in animal evolution as increase in complexity ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.5.9)).

### 70.1.2. Placing an Innovation on the Tree

Three kinds of reasoning, used throughout the course, place a character on the tree. The first is parsimony on a known topology: a character shared by all members of a clade and absent from its sister group most probably arose on the branch leading to the clade, as a synapomorphy. The second is explicit ancestral state reconstruction with rates of gain and loss, which shows that the answer can depend on the ratio of the two rates, as for the anus of the first bilaterian ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.2.3), Eq. (10.1)). The third, for soft tissues of fossils, is phylogenetic bracketing: a feature present in the two closest living relatives of a fossil lineage, such as the keratinized skin of amniotes or the air sacs of dinosaurs, was probably present in it too ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.3.4), [7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.1.3)).

Fossils add stem groups, extinct lineages that branch below the crown of a living clade and show in which order its characters were acquired. The Cambrian lobopodians, gilled lobopodians, *Opabinia* and radiodonts show the arthropod body plan assembled from claws, then compound eyes and frontal appendages, then jointed limbs and sclerites ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.2)); the armoured jawless fishes show that bone, paired fins and jaws were acquired in that order ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.4.5)); the synapsids show the mammalian jaw joint and middle ear emerging step by step over a hundred million years ([7.17.54](/7.17.54#sec-54.3)). A character complex that defines a living group, the **body plan** (Definition 3.1.1) of the textbooks, is therefore not an indivisible type but the end of a series of steps, a case of **mosaic evolution** ([7.17.54](/7.17.54#sec-54.4.5)).

Two cautions apply to every statement in the following section. The uncertain nodes of the animal tree, the root (ctenophores or sponges first), the position of the Xenacoelomorpha and the relationships within Lophotrochozoa ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.5.6)), are exactly those on which the reconstruction of the earliest animals depends. And a shared gene network is not a shared organ: the expression of Pax6 in eyes or of *Distal-less* in appendages across phyla shows **deep homology** of genetic circuits, not homology of the eyes or limbs themselves ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2.4), [7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.11.2)).

### 70.2.1. The First Animals: Cells That Cooperate

Animals are opisthokonts, and their sister group is the **choanoflagellates** (Definition 6.1.1), unicellular and colonial filter feeders whose collared cells resemble the choanocytes of sponges ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.1)). Much of the genetic toolkit that animals use to build bodies, cadherins, integrins, tyrosine kinases, many transcription factor families, is older than animals; what is new in the **Metazoa** (Definition 1.1.1) is its integration into a developmental program that makes a multicellular body with differentiated cell types, epithelia resting on a collagenous extracellular matrix, and an embryo that cleaves and, in most animals, gastrulates ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.1.2), [7.17.2](/7.17.2#sec-2.2)). The colonial hypotheses of animal origin, the **gastraea hypothesis** (Definition 6.2.1) of Haeckel and the planuloid hypothesis, both derive animals from a flagellated colony and are consistent with this picture; the syncytial and symbiotic hypotheses are rejected ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.2.1)).

The earliest branches show how little of the later body plans was present at the start. Sponges pump water through an aquiferous system lined by choanocytes and digest food inside cells, with no gut, nerves or muscles ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)); their three grades of construction, asconoid, syconoid and leuconoid, show the problem that will recur in every larger animal, that a surface (here the choanocyte surface) must keep pace with a volume, and the leuconoid design solves it ([7.17.7](/7.17.7#sec-7.3)). Placozoans are flat discs of a few cell types that coordinate gliding by secreted peptides ([7.17.8](/7.17.8#sec-8.3)). Ctenophores, probably the sister group of all other animals, already have a gut with anal pores, muscles and a nerve net, but no Hox genes; if they did branch first, nerves and muscles either evolved twice or were lost in sponges and placozoans ([7.17.8](/7.17.8#sec-8.4)). Either way, the simplicity of sponges and placozoans is at least partly secondary, the first of many simplifications on the tree.

### 70.2.2. ParaHoxozoa, Planulozoa and the Cnidarians

Placozoans, cnidarians and bilaterians share Hox and ParaHox genes (node 2), and cnidarians and bilaterians share a Wnt-patterned body axis with the blastopore at one end and a ciliated larva with an apical organ (node 3; [7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.5.2)). Cnidarians are the outgroup that tells us what the bilaterian ancestor inherited. They are diploblastic, with an epidermis and a gastrodermis separated by a mesoglea, a **gastrovascular cavity** with one opening, a nerve net and radial or biradial symmetry ([7.17.9](/7.17.9#sec-9.1)); their unique innovation, the **cnidocyte** (Definition 9.2.1), is the most explosive cell in the animal kingdom ([7.17.9](/7.17.9#sec-9.2.2)). Their life cycle alternates a sessile polyp with a swimming medusa (**metagenesis**, Definition 9.3.1), but the medusa arose only within Medusozoa, and the ancestral cnidarian was polyp-like ([7.17.9](/7.17.9#sec-9.7)).

The cnidarians also show that the toolkit of the Bilateria predates them. Anthozoans have a second, directive body axis patterned by Hox genes and BMP–Chordin, and many genes that make mesoderm in bilaterians are expressed in the cnidarian endoderm ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.1)). Even camera eyes, the ultimate organ of the large active bilaterians, are found in the rhopalia of box jellyfish ([7.17.9](/7.17.9#sec-9.5.2)). The step to the Bilateria was therefore less the invention of new genes than the use of old ones to build a new body.

### 70.2.3. Bilateria: Symmetry, Mesoderm and a Head

The **Bilateria** (Definition 6.5.1, node 4) are characterized by **bilateral symmetry** with three body axes, a third germ layer, the **mesoderm**, **cephalization** (Definition 3.2.2), an expanded Hox cluster with anterior, central and posterior genes, and BMP–Chordin patterning of the dorsoventral axis ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.1)). These characters belong together functionally. An animal that moves in one direction meets the world with one end, where sense organs and nervous tissue concentrate, and needs a back and a belly as well as a front and a rear ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.2.3)); mesoderm supplies the muscles that move it and, later, the circulatory, excretory and reproductive organs ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.3.1)).

What the first bilaterian looked like depends on the position of the **Xenacoelomorpha** (Definition 10.5.1). If these small worms, without an anus, nephridia or circulatory system, are the sister group of all other bilaterians, the first bilaterian may have been a small, ciliated, acoelomate worm with a blind gut; if they belong near the Ambulacraria, their simplicity is secondary and the first bilaterian may have been more complex ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.5.4)). The fossil record does not decide the question: the first bilaterian traces are Ediacaran burrows of about 560 million years ago, and *Kimberella* and *Yilingia* are probable stem bilaterians ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.2.2)). The historical theories of a simple planuloid–acoeloid ancestor and of a complex archicoelomate ancestor are thus still alive as hypotheses about this one node.

### 70.2.4. Nephrozoa: A Through Gut and Nephridia

All bilaterians except the xenacoelomorphs, the **Nephrozoa** (Definition 10.3.1, node 5), ancestrally have a **complete digestive tract** (Definition 4.4.2), with an ectodermal foregut and hindgut and an endodermal midgut, and excretory organs of the nephridial type ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.3)). The through gut allows food to be processed in sequence, by regions specialized for storage, grinding, digestion and absorption; the nephridia, whether **metanephridia** draining the coelom or blind protonephridia with flame cells, share developmental genes and molecules of the filter (nephrin, kirre) and are probably homologous ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.7.2)). From this node on, the excretory organs of the course can be read as variations of one filtration organ, to which the vertebrate nephron also belongs ([7.17.66](/7.17.66#sec-66.2.1)), while the Malpighian tubules of land arthropods are secretory organs added independently ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.7.3)).

The **coelom**, by contrast, cannot be placed with confidence. Body cavities of all three grades, acoelomate, blastocoelomate and coelomate, occur in unrelated clades, and the coelom forms by **schizocoely** or **enterocoely** in both protostomes and deuterostomes ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.4.5), [7.17.5](/7.17.5#sec-5.3)). It may have arisen once in the Nephrozoa and been lost in the small acoelomate and blastocoelomate lineages, or several times; either way, it is not the rung of a ladder.

### 70.2.5. Protostomes I: Spiralia

The protostomes divide into two clades, defined by development and by the body covering ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.4)). The **Spiralia** (node 6) share **spiral cleavage** with the 4d cell as the source of mesoderm ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#sec-5.1.2)), and many have a **trochophore** larva with a prototroch ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#sec-5.4.2)). Within them the course met almost every body plan that the old ladder placed on its lower and middle rungs. Flatworms are flat because flatness keeps every cell within diffusion distance of the surface, and their parasitic Neodermata replaced the epidermis with a syncytial tegument ([7.17.11](/7.17.11#sec-11.4)); rotifers, gnathostomulids and arrow worms share cuticular jaws (Gnathifera, [7.17.12](/7.17.12#sec-12.1)); the Lophotrochozoa unite the trochozoans with the lophophorates, whose **lophophore** (Definition 16.2.1) is an upstream-collecting filter of ciliated tentacles ([7.17.16](/7.17.16#sec-16.2)).

Two spiralian body plans dominate in species and in the textbooks. Molluscs (Definition 13.1.1) combine a head-foot, a visceral mass and a **mantle** that secretes a shell and overhangs a mantle cavity with ctenidia, and feed with a **radula** ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.1)); from this plan, torsion produced the gastropods, lateral compression and enlarged gills the filter-feeding bivalves, and a chambered buoyant shell, arms and a funnel the cephalopods, whose closed circulation, camera eyes and large brains converge on those of vertebrates ([7.17.14](/7.17.14#sec-14.7)). Annelids (Definition 15.1.1) are segmented coelomates whose segments, each with paired coelomic sacs, septa, nephridia, a ganglion and chaetae, are added from a posterior growth zone ([7.17.15](/7.17.15#sec-15.1)); the septate coelom is a hydrostatic skeleton of constant-volume compartments, the mechanical reason for segmentation in a burrowing worm ([7.17.15](/7.17.15#sec-15.1.3)). That sipunculans and echiurans are annelids that lost their segments shows that **segmentation** can be lost and its absence is not evidence of primitiveness ([7.17.15](/7.17.15#sec-15.5.4)).

### 70.2.6. Protostomes II: Ecdysozoa and the Arthropods

The **Ecdysozoa** (Definition 6.5.2, node 7) share a three-layered **cuticle** (Definition 17.1.1) that is molted, and they have lost locomotory cilia and ciliated larvae ([7.17.17](/7.17.17#sec-17.1)). A molted cuticle imposes growth in steps and a way of moving without cilia: nematodes, the most abundant animals, move by undulation with longitudinal muscles against a pressurized blastocoelom and a cuticle of crossed fibres ([7.17.17](/7.17.17#sec-17.3.3)). The **Panarthropoda** (node 8) add paired, clawed limbs, a **hemocoel** and segments with *engrailed* at their borders ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.1.1)).

The arthropods (node 9) turned the cuticle into a jointed **exoskeleton** of sclerotized plates and tubes, with muscles inserted on its inner surface and limbs of articulated segments ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.3)). Three consequences follow from it. The segments were grouped into **tagmata** under Hox control, so that one body plan could be specialized into the prosoma and opisthosoma of chelicerates, the head and trunk of myriapods and the head, thorax and abdomen of crustaceans and insects ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.3.1)); the head problem, the homology of head appendages, was solved by tracing each pair to its segment and its part of the brain, so that the chelicerae of spiders correspond to the antennae of insects ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.6)). Growth requires **molting** under the control of ecdysteroids and juvenile hormone, with all its costs and risks ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.4)). And the hard surface made the **compound eye** possible, an eye of many small optical units whose resolution is limited by diffraction and therefore suited to small animals ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.7.2)). The living arthropods divide into Chelicerata and **Mandibulata** (Definition 18.9.1), and the mandibulates into Myriapoda and **Pancrustacea** (Definition 18.9.2), within which the hexapods are terrestrial crustaceans closest to the remipedes ([7.17.21](/7.17.21#sec-21.6)).

The hexapods carry the tree further in three steps that will matter for Section 70.4. Wings arose once, at the base of the **Pterygota** (Definition 30.2.1, node 10), by about 325 million years ago, probably by combining a tergal lobe with pleural exite genes ([7.17.25](/7.17.25#sec-25.1)); the **Neoptera** (Definition 30.2.2, node 11) can fold their wings back over the abdomen; and the **Holometabola** (Definition 30.2.5, node 12) insert a pupa between a larva and an adult that are built from different tissues, the adult largely from imaginal discs ([7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.2.4)).

### 70.2.7. Deuterostomes and the First Chordates

The deuterostomes (node 13) comprise two clades, Ambulacraria and Chordata, whose most robust shared characters are **pharyngeal slits** and a conserved pharyngeal gene cluster; enterocoely, a tripartite coelom and a secondary mouth are widespread but not universal ([7.17.41](/7.17.41#sec-41.1.1)). The ancestral deuterostome was probably a solitary, filter-feeding worm with gill slits, close to an acorn worm in body plan ([7.17.41](/7.17.41#sec-41.5.3)). Echinoderms built from this ancestry the strangest body plan of the course: a pentaradial adult made from a bilateral larva, a skeleton of porous calcite ossicles, mutable collagenous tissue that stiffens under nervous control, and a **water vascular system** (Definition 42.3.1) that operates the **tube feet** as closed hydraulic units ([7.17.42](/7.17.42)). Their radial symmetry is secondary, a reversal of the bilaterian condition associated with a sessile ancestry ([7.17.42](/7.17.42#sec-42.7.2)).

The chordates (Definition 43.1.1, node 14) combine a **notochord**, a **dorsal hollow nerve cord**, pharyngeal slits, an **endostyle** and a muscular postanal tail with myomeres. The characters form two complexes, one for swimming (notochord, myomeres, tail, nerve cord) and one for low-Reynolds-number mucus filter feeding (slits, endostyle, ciliated tracts), and the lancelet shows both in nearly ancestral form ([7.17.43](/7.17.43#sec-43.1.2)). Two revisions of the older textbooks concern this node. Tunicates, not lancelets, are the sister group of vertebrates (**Olfactores**, Definition 43.4.1), sharing neural crest-like cells and a cardiopharyngeal field, so that the ancestral chordate was a free-living, lancelet-like animal and the sessile ascidian is derived ([7.17.43](/7.17.43#sec-43.4)). And the dorsoventral axis of chordates is inverted relative to that of other bilaterians, which explains why the nerve cord is dorsal and the heart ventral ([7.17.43](/7.17.43#sec-43.5.2)).

### 70.2.8. Vertebrates: A New Head

The vertebrates (node 15) are the chordates with a head: a cranium, a tripartite brain, paired eyes, nose and ears, a muscular pharynx with endodermal gills and a chambered heart ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1.1)). Most of the head is built from two embryonic tissues, the **neural crest** and the ectodermal **placode** series, and the new head hypothesis relates their origin to a shift from ciliary filter feeding to muscular pumping and predation ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1.2)). Mineralized tissues, bone, dentine and enamel, are a second vertebrate innovation; the first bone was dermal armour of odontodes ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1.3), [7.17.57](/7.17.57#sec-57.6.1)). A third is genomic: two rounds of **whole-genome duplication** at the base of the vertebrates and of the jawed vertebrates supplied paralogues that were recruited into these new structures ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1.5)).

The jawed vertebrates (node 16) add jaws of the first pharyngeal arch, pelvic fins and girdles, three semicircular canals, myelin and RAG-based adaptive immunity ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.1.1)). The origin of jaws, long read as the transformation of an adult gill arch, is now explained by development: a Hox-free first arch, a nested *Dlx* code that distinguishes upper and lower elements, and a shift of crest migration made possible by the separation of the nasal and hypophyseal placodes ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.2.2)). The bony fishes (node 17) add endochondral bone, marginal dermal jaw bones and lungs, from which the swim bladder of ray-finned fishes was derived, reversing the older view that lungs came from the swim bladder ([7.17.47](/7.17.47#sec-47.1), [7.17.64](/7.17.64#sec-64.3.1)).

### 70.2.9. Tetrapods and Amniotes: Life on Land

Limbs with digits (node 18) evolved in water, in Devonian tetrapodomorphs such as *Acanthostega*, before the first tetrapods walked on land; the autopod is a new distal compartment, specified by a late phase of *Hox13* expression, that replaced the fin rays ([7.17.48](/7.17.48#sec-48.3.1)). The move onto land then reorganized almost every system: the pectoral girdle separated from the skull, the hyomandibula became the stapes, the buccal pump ventilated lungs, the eyes were enlarged for vision in air ([7.17.48](/7.17.48#sec-48.4)). Living amphibians are a specialized sister lineage of the amniotes, not their ancestors, and still depend on water for eggs and skin ([7.17.49](/7.17.49)).

The **amniotic egg** (Definition 50.2.1, node 19) freed reproduction from water, with an amnion and chorion from the somatopleure and a yolk sac and allantois from the splanchnopleure ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.2)). Amniotes split early into synapsids and sauropsids (Definition 50.4.1), which solved the problems of land in parallel but differently: posture, a diaphragm versus accessory pumps and one-way lungs, a divided heart versus a shunting one, urea versus uric acid ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.4.2)). On the synapsid line, hair, mammary glands, a single-bone lower jaw and three ear ossicles define the mammals (node 20; [7.17.55](/7.17.55#sec-55.1.1)); on the sauropsid line, feathers arose in stages in theropod dinosaurs long before flight, and birds (node 21) are the surviving flying dinosaurs ([7.17.52](/7.17.52#sec-52.7)). Endothermy evolved independently on the two lines ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.5)).

| Tetrapoda, Amniota | Limbs with digits; amniotic egg | Limbs lost in snakes, caecilians | [7.17.48](/7.17.48)–[7.17.50](/7.17.50) |
| Mammalia, Aves | Hair, milk, three ossicles; feathers, flight | Flight lost in ratites, penguins | [7.17.53](/7.17.53)–[7.17.55](/7.17.55) |

The table shows a regularity that the ladder concealed. Major innovations are concentrated at a few nodes, but they are not complete at those nodes: each body plan was assembled in steps along its stem, many of whose members are known only as fossils, and then modified, reduced or partly lost in its descendants.

## 70.3. Same Problem, Many Solutions

The second reading of the course looks across the tree instead of up it. The same functional problems confront every animal of a given size, medium and way of life: how to see, how to move a long body, how to breathe and keep water on land, how to fly, how to keep a high body temperature. Many of the solutions recur in unrelated lineages. This section collects them; the figure maps the main cases onto one cladogram.

![Twelve functional designs mapped on a cladogram of the main lineages of the course (topology simplified from 7.17.6 and the class chapters). A bar spanning several rows marks one origin shared by those lineages; separate dots in a column mark independent origins; the hollow dot marks the regional endothermy of tunas. Camera eyes, closed circulation, flight, endothermy, divided ventricles, tympanic ears, eusociality and jet propulsion each arose in several unrelated lineages; compound eyes and segmentation are homologous within arthropods (and within chordates for segmentation) but convergent between phyla; tracheae and Malpighian tubules evolved separately in several arthropod lineages that colonized land.](./plots/convergence-matrix.py)

### 70.3.1. Eyes

Eyes are the classic case. At the level of the organ they evolved between 40 and 65 times, but they share parts at lower levels: opsins, the two kinds of photoreceptor cell (ciliary and rhabdomeric) and the transcription factor Pax6 are common to all of them, so that eyes are deeply homologous and organ-level convergent at once ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.9.1)). The optical designs are far fewer than the eyes: the **camera eye** (Definition 44.9.2) with a spherical graded-index lens evolved at least four to six times, in cephalopods, fishes, alciopid polychaetes, heteropod snails and box jellyfish, because a single lens in water can only work that way ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.9.3)). Coleoid and vertebrate eyes are nearly identical in optics but differ in development, in the orientation of the retina (everted against inverted) and in the receptor type (rhabdomeric against ciliary), the signatures of independent origin ([7.17.14](/7.17.14#sec-14.7.2), [7.17.68](/7.17.68#sec-68.4.1)). Compound eyes arose within the arthropods once and, independently, in ark clams and sabellid fan worms; the four functional classes of photoreception (Definition 44.9.1) show why every step from an eyespot to a camera eye could be favoured on its own ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.9.2)).

### 70.3.2. Segmentation

Segmentation (Definition 3.5.1) occurs in three major lineages: annelids, which add segments from teloblasts in a posterior growth zone; panarthropods, which use a growth zone or the subdivision of a blastoderm; and chordates, whose somites form under a clock and wavefront ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.5.2), [7.17.60](/7.17.60#sec-60.2.1)). On the tree these lineages are separated by unsegmented relatives, and the most parsimonious reading is three origins, with some genes (*engrailed*, Notch signalling, the **segmentation clock** of some arthropods) shared as components of deep homology. The functional reasons differ as well: compartments of constant volume for burrowing in annelids, a jointed exoskeleton in arthropods, serial muscles bending an axial rod in swimming chordates ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.5.4)). Repetition of single organs, as in chitons, monoplacophorans and tapeworm proglottids, is not segmentation ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.5.3)).

### 70.3.3. Life on Land

The colonization of land is the best replicated experiment of the course. Arthropods and their relatives came ashore at least five times, as onychophorans, arachnids, myriapods, hexapods and malacostracans (woodlice and land crabs), and each lineage had to solve the same problems of desiccation, gas exchange, nitrogen excretion, support and fertilization ([7.17.20](/7.17.20#sec-20.2.2)). The solutions converged: a wax layer on the cuticle that lowers its permeability about a hundredfold ([7.17.20](/7.17.20#sec-20.2.5)); tracheae, which evolved at least five or six times with different arrangements of openings ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.5.3)); **Malpighian tubules** (Definition 4.6.3), endodermal in arachnids and ectodermal in insects and myriapods ([7.17.19](/7.17.19), [7.17.20](/7.17.20)); uric acid or guanine as nitrogenous waste; and internal fertilization or spermatophores.

Snails and vertebrates repeated the experiment with different materials: a lung from the mantle cavity in pulmonate snails ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.7.5)), and in amniotes a keratinized waterproof skin, a lung ventilated by aspiration, a concentrating or uric-acid-excreting kidney and the amniotic egg ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.1.3)). The terrestrial syndrome of [7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.11.1) is thus a set of functions, each fulfilled by organs of different origin in each lineage. Within the vertebrates, a smaller convergent syndrome on land is the separation of air and food: a **secondary palate** (Definition 59.9.1) evolved independently in mammals and crocodylians, and a complete separation of the lungs from the abdominal cavity independently in mammals, crocodylians and birds ([7.17.59](/7.17.59#sec-59.9.1), [7.17.63](/7.17.63#sec-63.1.3)).

### 70.3.4. Flight

Powered flight evolved four times, in insects, pterosaurs, birds and bats ([7.17.25](/7.17.25), [7.17.61](/7.17.61#sec-61.6.4)). The insect wing is a new structure, a double layer of cuticle with veins, moved largely by indirect muscles that deform the thorax ([7.17.25](/7.17.25#sec-25.2)); the three vertebrate wings are built on the homologous forelimb but are convergent as wings: a membrane on the fourth finger in pterosaurs, feathers on a fused hand in birds, a membrane between four elongated fingers in bats. The principles of the earlier chapters apply to all four. Small fliers work at low Reynolds numbers and rely on unsteady mechanisms such as the leading-edge vortex ([7.17.25](/7.17.25#sec-25.3.3)), large ones on quasi-steady lift and the power curve of birds ([7.17.53](/7.17.53#sec-53.5.2)); all fliers reduce weight, birds by pneumatic and fused bones ([7.17.53](/7.17.53#sec-53.4.1)), pterosaurs by pneumatic bones as well ([7.17.52](/7.17.52#sec-52.3.1)).

Flightlessness is equally convergent: it evolved repeatedly in island insects, in parasites (fleas, lice), in social castes and in females of many insects, and in ratites, penguins and island rails among birds ([7.17.25](/7.17.25#sec-25.4.2), [7.17.53](/7.17.53#sec-53.4.4)). Both directions are expected where flight muscles are costly and the benefits of dispersal small.

### 70.3.5. Circulation, Hearts and Body Temperature

Large active animals need to move oxygen faster than diffusion allows ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.1.2)). A **closed circulatory system** evolved several times, in annelids, nemerteans, cephalopods and chordates, while arthropods and most molluscs kept an **open circulatory system** in which the hemocoel replaces the coelom ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.6.2)). The trade-off between vessel size and pressure (Formula 44.6.2) explains why closed systems accompany high metabolic rates and filtration kidneys with podocytes ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.6.3)).

Within the vertebrates the heart was divided progressively as air breathing developed: partial septa in lungfishes, two atria in amphibians, a ventricle with three cava in lepidosaurs and turtles, a complete septum in crocodylians and four-chambered hearts, convergently, in birds and mammals ([7.17.65](/7.17.65#sec-65.2)). The complete double circulation is associated with **endothermy**, which also evolved twice, in the synapsids and the archosaurs, and was inferred in fossils from insulation, bone histology, nasal turbinates, isotopes and the size of the semicircular canals ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.5.2)). Regional endothermy in tunas, lamnid sharks and the opah, and the pre-flight warm-up of large insects ([7.17.25](/7.17.25#sec-25.2.4)), show that heat production in muscle can be selected for many times wherever activity in cold water or air pays.

### 70.3.6. Hearing, Electric Organs and Social Life

The tympanic ear, a thin membrane backed by air that transmits sound to receptors, evolved at least 20 times in insects from proprioceptive chordotonal organs ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2.4)) and independently in frogs, lepidosaurs, turtles, archosaurs and mammals among tetrapods ([7.17.68](/7.17.68#sec-68.3.3)). Electric organs evolved at least six times in fishes, from different muscles or nerves ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.8)), and electroreception was lost and reinvented in teleosts and monotremes ([7.17.68](/7.17.68#sec-68.2.2)). Eusociality, with a reproductive division of labour, evolved at least a dozen times in insects, in termites, ants, vespid wasps and several lineages of bees, aphids, thrips and beetles, and also in snapping shrimps and naked mole-rats ([7.17.38](/7.17.38#sec-38.1.2)). Viviparity evolved more than 100 times in squamates alone ([7.17.51](/7.17.51#sec-51.3)).

### 70.3.8. Why Convergence Is So Common

Three reasons recur in the course. The first is physics. The laws of diffusion, of flow at low and high Reynolds numbers, of optics and of mechanics admit only a few good designs for a given task, and the same designs are found again and again: the spherical lens with Matthiessen's ratio in water ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.9.3)), the helical fibre lattice at $54^\circ 44'$ in worms and nematodes ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.2.2), [7.17.17](/7.17.17#sec-17.3.2)), the countercurrent exchanger in gills, swim bladders and kidneys ([7.17.64](/7.17.64#sec-64.2.1), [7.17.47](/7.17.47#sec-47.4.2)), the latch-and-spring catapult in trap-jaw ants, fleas and frogs ([7.17.23](/7.17.23#sec-23.5), [7.17.35](/7.17.35#sec-35.2), [7.17.49](/7.17.49#sec-49.2.1)).

The second is the shared toolkit. Because all animals inherited the same families of cell types and gene networks, organs built for the same function in unrelated lineages tend to use the same components: opsins for light, Pax6 to specify eyes, *tinman* to specify hearts, *Distal-less* for outgrowths. This is the meaning of **deep homology**, and it makes convergence at the level of organs easier and more likely ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2.4)). The third is the correlation of characters: organs evolve together as syndromes of a way of life, so that the large and active, the small and interstitial, the terrestrial, the sessile and the parasitic animals each converge on whole sets of characters at once ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.11.1)).

The consequence for method is the distinction between **homology** and **homoplasy** at a stated level ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2)). The wings of birds and bats are homologous as forelimbs and convergent as wings; the eyes of squids and fishes are convergent as eyes and deeply homologous in their genes. Remane's criteria of position, special quality and continuity propose a homology, and Patterson's tests of similarity, conjunction and congruence with the tree test it ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2.3)).

## 70.4. Why Are There So Many Insects?

Entomology occupied nineteen chapters, more than any other group, because insects are most of animal diversity. About a million insect species are described, of an estimated five to six million, and four holometabolous orders, the beetles, flies, moths and wasps, make up about four fifths of them ([7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.4), [7.17.30](/7.17.30#sec-30.4.1)). The course has examined the candidate explanations one by one; here they are combined.

### 70.4.1. Properties of the Body Plan

Several explanations concern the arthropod and hexapod body plan as a whole and are shared by all insects. The exoskeleton with a waxy epicuticle protects against desiccation and predators and supports a small body efficiently ([7.17.23](/7.17.23#sec-23.1)); small size, enforced by tracheal diffusion and by the mechanics of molting, lets many species divide a single resource (one tree offers niches to hundreds of species, on the bark, in the wood, the leaves, the seeds, the galls and the bodies of other insects) and allows large populations in small areas ([7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.1.5), [7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.4.3)). Tagmosis gave a head with modular mouthparts, which became chewing, piercing-sucking, siphoning or sponging tools for different diets ([7.17.23](/7.17.23#sec-23.5)), a thorax for locomotion and an abdomen for reproduction. Short generation times and high fecundity, as in aphids with telescoped generations ([7.17.28](/7.17.28#sec-28.5)), allow fast adaptation, and dormancy in diapause allows survival through unfavourable seasons ([7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.7)).

### 70.4.2. Key Innovations

Four traits have been proposed as **key innovation**s: wings, wing folding, complete metamorphosis and plant feeding ([7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.5.1)). Insects were the first flying animals, and flight opened dispersal, escape and the colonization of patchy resources ([7.17.25](/7.17.25)); wing folding gave access to narrow spaces with the wings protected, and about 98% of insect species are neopterans ([7.17.25](/7.17.25#sec-25.4.1)); complete metamorphosis decoupled larva and adult, which exploit different foods and habitats, and the Holometabola contain about 80% of insect species ([7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.5), [7.17.33](/7.17.33#sec-33.1)). Plant feeding, which about half of insect species practise, is a poor but enormous resource, and **phytophagy** (Definition 37.1.1) was followed by specialization on host plants, host shifts and speciation ([7.17.37](/7.17.37#sec-37.1)).

The traits differ in how well they can be tested. A key innovation is tested by comparing a clade that has it with its sister group, which is of the same age ([7.17.30](/7.17.30#sec-30.4.2)). For phytophagy, which arose many times, replicated comparisons exist, and in most of thirteen pairs the plant-feeding clade was richer (Mitter and colleagues 1988). Wings and complete metamorphosis arose once each, and a single sister-group comparison has little power: under the equal-rates model, the probability that the smaller of two sister clades is as small as observed is $2n/(N-1)$ (Eq. (30.2)), which is very small for beetles against Strepsiptera but not significant for Lepidoptera against Trichoptera (Example 30.4.1). Analyses of diversification across dated trees of all insect families associate complete metamorphosis with faster diversification and find further rate increases within the large holometabolous orders ([7.17.30](/7.17.30#sec-30.4.2)).

### 70.4.3. Low Extinction and Long History

A clade is rich because it diversified fast, went extinct slowly, or is old. The fossil record of insects points to low extinction as a major factor: about 84% of insect families living in the Early Cretaceous still exist, and apart from the end-Permian crisis insects suffered no mass extinction at the family level ([7.17.30](/7.17.30#sec-30.4.1)). Beetles illustrate the combination. Their elytra protect the folded hind wings and let them live in soil, wood and under bark; many beetle lineages have survived since the Jurassic; and phytophagous weevils and leaf beetles radiated repeatedly on flowering plants ([7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.5.2), [7.17.33](/7.17.33#sec-33.3)). Beetles are diverse probably because they accumulated lineages over a long time with little loss, not because they speciate unusually fast.

### 70.4.4. Ecological Opportunity and Coevolution

The association with flowering plants is the explanation most often given in popular accounts, and the course has qualified it. The major orders originated in the Permian or Triassic, before the angiosperms, and insect family diversity did not accelerate when angiosperms appeared; but several herbivorous and pollinating lineages, phytophagous beetles, butterflies and moths, bees, aphids and leafhoppers, did radiate with their angiosperm hosts ([7.17.30](/7.17.30#sec-30.4.3)). Escape-and-radiate **coevolution** is supported in particular systems, the Brassicales and the cabbage whites, *Bursera* and its flea beetles, but most interactions between insects and plants are diffuse ([7.17.37](/7.17.37#sec-37.3)). Parasitoidism and **eusociality** added further dimensions: parasitoid wasps may be among the most species-rich insects because their hosts are diverse and their host ranges narrow ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.2)), and social insects, a small fraction of species, dominate many ecosystems in biomass ([7.17.38](/7.17.38#sec-38.8)).

### 70.4.5. A Synthesis

| Level | Factor | Effect on diversity | Evidence | Chapters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arthropod body plan | Exoskeleton, tagmata, modular mouthparts | Protection, desiccation resistance, many diets | Shared with other arthropods, which are also diverse | [7.17.18](/7.17.18), [7.17.23](/7.17.23) |
| Size | Small body, fine-grained niches | More species per resource; large populations | Species–size distributions | [7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.1.5), [7.17.30](/7.17.30#sec-30.4.1) |

The answer to the question is thus not one trait but a hierarchy. The arthropod body plan and small size made a high diversity possible; the hexapod innovations of flight, wing folding and complete metamorphosis raised the rate at which lineages accumulated; ecological specialization on plants and hosts multiplied the niches; and low extinction let the result accumulate over the whole Phanerozoic since the Devonian. Each factor is shared by many other insects, and none alone explains why one order is richer than its sister group; the explanation must be sought in their combination in particular lineages.

Five threads run through several of them and through the class chapters, and together they tell the story of the vertebrate head and trunk as they passed from water to land. ### 70.5.1. The Fate of the Pharyngeal Arches

Each **pharyngeal arch** has a skeleton from the neural crest, branchiomeric muscles, a cranial nerve and an aortic arch, and the four components can be followed together through all vertebrates ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.3.1)). The first arch, the **mandibular arch**, became the primary jaws of gnathostomes: the palatoquadrate above and Meckel's cartilage below ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.4)). In bony fishes and tetrapods, dermal bones (premaxilla, maxilla, dentary) took over the biting margin as secondary jaws, and the arch cartilages ossified at the back of the jaws as the quadrate and articular, which formed the jaw joint ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.4.2)). The second arch, the **hyoid arch**, gave the **hyomandibula**, which suspended the jaw in many fishes (hyostyly, [7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.5)) and became the stapes or columella of tetrapods. The posterior arches gave the gill skeleton, then the hyoid horns and the laryngeal cartilages ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.3.4)).

| Arch | Nerve | Fishes | Non-mammalian tetrapods | Mammals |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Mandibular | V | Palatoquadrate and Meckel's cartilage (jaws); quadrate and articular at the joint | Quadrate, articular, epipterygoid | Incus, malleus, alisphenoid; muscles of mastication |
| 2 Hyoid | VII | Hyomandibula (jaw support), ceratohyal | Stapes (columella), hyoid | Stapes, styloid, lesser horns of hyoid; facial muscles |
| 3 | IX | First gill arch | Hyoid horns | Greater horns of hyoid |
| 4–6 | X | Gill arches | Laryngeal cartilages | Thyroid and other laryngeal cartilages |

The muscles follow the skeleton because muscles keep their nerve ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.2.3)): the mammalian muscles of mastication, the tensor tympani and the anterior digastric are mandibular-arch muscles of the trigeminal nerve, and the facial muscles and stapedius belong to the facial nerve of the hyoid arch ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.5)). The nerves themselves, one branchial nerve for each arch (V, VII, IX, X), explain the numbering of the cranial nerves ([7.17.67](/7.17.67#sec-67.5.4)). The endocrine derivatives of the pharyngeal pouches, the thyroid from the endostyle and the parathyroids from tissue related to fish gills, complete the picture of a pharynx that was converted, organ by organ, from a filter and a gill into a jaw, an ear, a larynx and a set of glands ([7.17.67](/7.17.67#sec-67.7)).

### 70.5.2. Reduction and Fusion of Skull Bones

The vertebrate skull has three components, the **chondrocranium**, the **splanchnocranium** and the **dermatocranium** ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.1)), whose contributions shift from cartilage in cyclostomes and sharks to dermal bone in mammals. Among bony vertebrates the number of separate bones declined: teleosts have more than a hundred elements, organized into linkages for suction feeding and jaw protrusion ([7.17.59](/7.17.59#sec-59.1.2)); early tetrapods lost the opercular and extrascapular bones that tied the head to the shoulder ([7.17.59](/7.17.59#sec-59.2.1)); amniotes lost the intertemporal and evolved **temporal fenestra**e independently in synapsids and diapsids ([7.17.59](/7.17.59#sec-59.3.2)); mammals lost the postorbital, postfrontal and supratemporal and fused many elements into the compound occipital, temporal and sphenoid ([7.17.59](/7.17.59#sec-59.6.2)). Williston's law generalizes the trend, but it is a tendency with exceptions, concentrated at particular nodes and reversed in some lineages, not a law ([7.17.59](/7.17.59#sec-59.7)).

The causes discussed in the chapter, functional integration, developmental economy and the expansion of the braincase, apply differently on each line. Birds fused their skull roof into a light, pneumatized box with large orbits, mammals closed the skull around a large brain and made it akinetic for chewing, and squamates kept or increased the mobility of skull elements for swallowing large prey, the extreme **cranial kinesis** (Definition 59.4.1) of snakes ([7.17.59](/7.17.59#sec-59.4)). Reduction of bone number thus accompanied specialization of the remaining bones, which is what Williston meant.

### 70.5.3. The Aortic Arches

Six pairs of **aortic arch**es (Definition 65.3.1), one in each pharyngeal arch, connect the ventral and dorsal aortae in all vertebrate embryos ([7.17.65](/7.17.65#sec-65.3.1)). In fishes the persisting arches are interrupted by gill capillaries: arches II–VI in sharks, III–VI in teleosts. In tetrapods arches III, IV and VI persist as the carotid, systemic and pulmonary arches, and the lineages differ in which systemic arch they keep: both in amphibians and reptiles, the right in birds, the left in mammals ([7.17.65](/7.17.65#sec-65.3.3)). The dorsal part of arch VI, the **ductus arteriosus**, bypasses the lungs in the mammalian fetus and closes at birth into the ligamentum arteriosum ([7.17.65](/7.17.65#sec-65.5)).

The arches illustrate descent with modification in its plainest form. The recurrent laryngeal nerve, the nerve of the sixth arch, still loops behind the sixth arch artery on its way to the larynx; in a giraffe it runs several metres down the neck and back, a detour explained only by the descent of the heart from the pharyngeal region into the thorax ([7.17.65](/7.17.65#sec-65.3.1)). Why birds kept the right systemic arch and mammals the left is not explained by function; what both share is the loss of one arch once the ventricle was completely divided and the two arches became redundant.

### 70.5.4. Kidneys and Genital Ducts

The vertebrate kidney develops from front to back in three generations from one nephrogenic cord: the **pronephros** (the larval kidney of anamniotes), the **mesonephros** (the adult kidney of anamniotes, as the **opisthonephros**, and the embryonic kidney of amniotes) and the **metanephros** (the adult kidney of amniotes, drained by a ureter) ([7.17.66](/7.17.66#sec-66.2.2)). Across them nephrons become more numerous, lose their nephrostomes and change from external to internal glomeruli; the sequence preserves a developmental mechanism rather than the kidneys of three ancestral adults ([7.17.66](/7.17.66#sec-66.2.4)). Loops of Henle and the ability to concentrate urine appear only in mammals and in some nephrons of birds ([7.17.66](/7.17.66#sec-66.3.1)).

The genital ducts were borrowed from the kidney. The first duct of the kidney, the archinephric duct, carries sperm in males of most gnathostomes, and in amniote males the mesonephros becomes the epididymis and the Wolffian duct the vas deferens, leaving the ureter to the metanephros ([7.17.66](/7.17.66#sec-66.6.1)). In females the **Müllerian duct** forms oviducts, uteri and vagina, fused to different degrees in mammals; teleosts, by contrast, have gonoducts of gonadal origin, not homologous with these ducts ([7.17.66](/7.17.66#sec-66.6.2)). Copulatory organs evolved independently many times, from claspers and gonopodia to hemipenes and the amniote phallus ([7.17.66](/7.17.66#sec-66.7.2)).

### 70.5.5. The Ear Ossicles

The last thread connects the first two. In early tetrapods the hyomandibula, freed from jaw support, became the stapes, a rod that transmits vibrations from the skull or a tympanum to the inner ear ([7.17.48](/7.17.48#sec-48.4.4)). Tympanic middle ears with a single ossicle, the columella or stapes, then evolved independently in frogs, lepidosaurs, turtles and archosaurs ([7.17.68](/7.17.68#sec-68.3.3)). On the synapsid line the dentary enlarged and the postdentary bones shrank as the jaw muscles were rearranged; a double jaw joint preceded the new dentary–squamosal joint, and the old joint bones, the articular and the quadrate, together with the angular, became the malleus, the incus and the tympanic ring ([7.17.54](/7.17.54#sec-54.3)). Embryology (the Reichert–Gaupp theory) and a series of fossils, many from China, document the transition, and the definitive mammalian middle ear separated from the jaw independently in several Mesozoic lineages ([7.17.54](/7.17.54#sec-54.3.4)).

The three-ossicle ear is the most striking example in the course of **exaptation**: bones that worked as a jaw joint for more than a hundred million years became a lever system for hearing high frequencies, and the colours of the figure follow them from the palatoquadrate and Meckel's cartilage of a shark to the incus and malleus of a mammal ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.8.2)).

### 70.5.6. What the Threads Have in Common

Four features recur in the five threads. Each begins with a segmental, repeated plan in the embryo (arches, arteries, nephrons), serially homologous in the sense of **serial homology** (Definition 56.2.1), and the adult structures arise by loss, fusion and specialization of the repeated parts. Each passes through the transition from water to land, which removed the gills and made their skeleton, arteries and muscles available for new uses. Each shows convergence between synapsids and sauropsids on the same functional goal (an undivided aorta on one side, a concentrating kidney, a tympanic ear) reached by different routes. And each illustrates the reading of development used throughout Part VIII: the embryo does not repeat the adult ancestors, as the **biogenetic law** (Definition 5.6.1) asserted, but it conserves the developmental mechanisms from which the ancestors' structures were made ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#sec-5.6)).

## Summary

- The tree replaced the ladder ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)): grades such as acoelomates, pseudocoelomates and coelomates are levels reached and abandoned in several lineages; Bilateria, Nephrozoa, Protostomia (Spiralia, Ecdysozoa) and Deuterostomia are clades. The functional explanations of the old textbooks remain valid; their reading as one sequence does not.
- Body plans were assembled in steps at a few nodes and along their stems (Section 70.2.10): epithelia and cell types (Metazoa); Hox genes (ParaHoxozoa); bilateral symmetry, mesoderm and a head (Bilateria); through gut and nephridia (Nephrozoa); spiral cleavage (Spiralia); molted cuticle (Ecdysozoa); jointed exoskeleton and tagmata (Arthropoda); wings, wing folding and the pupa (Pterygota, Neoptera, Holometabola); pharyngeal slits (Deuterostomia); notochord and dorsal nerve cord (Chordata); neural crest and placodes (Vertebrata); jaws (Gnathostomata); lungs and endochondral bone (Osteichthyes); digits (Tetrapoda); the amniotic egg (Amniota); hair and three ossicles (Mammalia); feathers and flight (in the dinosaur lineage leading to birds). Losses and simplifications are as common as gains.
- Convergence is the rule at the level of organs (Section 70.3.7): camera eyes, compound eyes, segmentation, tracheae, Malpighian tubules, closed circulation, flight, endothermy, divided hearts, tympanic ears, secondary palates, electric organs, eusociality and viviparity each evolved several times. Physical constraints, a shared toolkit of cell types and genes (deep homology) and correlated syndromes of a way of life make it common; homology and convergence must be judged at a stated level.
- Insect diversity (Section 70.4.5) results from a hierarchy of factors: the arthropod body plan and small size, the innovations of flight, wing folding and complete metamorphosis, ecological specialization (phytophagy, parasitoidism), short generations and diapause, and low extinction over 400 million years.
- Five threads of vertebrate comparative anatomy (Section 70.5): the pharyngeal arches became jaws, ear bones, hyoid and larynx; skull bones decreased by loss and fusion (Williston's law, a trend); aortic arches III, IV and VI persist in tetrapods, with the right systemic arch in birds and the left in mammals; kidneys succeed one another as pronephros, mesonephros and metanephros, and the archinephric duct became the vas deferens; the quadrate, articular and angular became the incus, malleus and tympanic ring of mammals.
- The phyla and classes of the Chinese textbooks (Section 70.6) changed by removal (Protozoa), absorption (Sipuncula, Pogonophora, Acanthocephala, Myxozoa, birds within reptiles) and splitting of grades (Coelenterata, Mesozoa, Aschelminthes, Reptilia, protochordates); their descriptions and representative animals remain the material of the course.

## Questions

1. The older textbooks say that "the true coelom first appears in annelids, which is a major step in animal evolution". Reformulate the statement so that it agrees with the current tree, and say what remains true of it.

   **Answer** On the current tree a coelom occurs in annelids, molluscs (reduced), nemerteans (rhynchocoel), lophophorates, arthropods (reduced, with a hemocoel), echinoderms, hemichordates and chordates, and it forms by schizocoely or enterocoely in both protostomes and deuterostomes ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.4.5)). Annelids are spiralians, not the ancestors of arthropods or chordates, so the coelom did not "first appear" in them; it either arose once at the base of the Nephrozoa and was lost in acoelomate and blastocoelomate lineages, or arose several times. A correct formulation is: "a coelom divided by septa into segmental compartments is characteristic of annelids, where it acts as a hydrostatic skeleton for peristaltic burrowing." What remains true is the functional explanation: a fluid-filled cavity separates the gut from the body wall, serves as a hydrostatic skeleton and provides space for gonads and excretory organs ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.4.3)).

2. Why can the camera eyes of squids and fishes be called both convergent and homologous? Give the evidence for each statement.

   **Answer** As organs they are convergent: they develop differently (the cephalopod eye from an invagination of the surface ectoderm, the vertebrate eye from an outgrowth of the brain together with a lens placode), the cephalopod retina is everted with rhabdomeric receptors and no blind spot, the vertebrate retina inverted with ciliary receptors, and the closest relatives of each (other molluscs, lancelets and tunicates) have no camera eyes ([7.17.14](/7.17.14#sec-14.7.2), [7.17.68](/7.17.68#sec-68.4.1)). At lower levels they are homologous: both use opsins of families present in the bilaterian ancestor, both are specified with Pax6, and both photoreceptor types were present in that ancestor ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.9.1)). This is deep homology: homologous components assembled independently into analogous organs. The similarity of the optics (a spherical graded-index lens) is a further convergence imposed by the physics of a lens in water.

3. Use the tree of Section 70.2 to decide in how many steps the characters of a lizard were acquired from the first animal. Group the steps by node, and name one character of the lizard that was acquired by convergence rather than inherited from a node of the figure.

   **Answer** Following the figure from the root: Metazoa (epithelia, collagen), ParaHoxozoa (Hox genes), Planulozoa (axis, nervous system), Bilateria (bilateral symmetry, mesoderm, head), Nephrozoa (through gut, nephridia), Deuterostomia (pharyngeal slits, embryonically), Chordata (notochord, dorsal nerve cord, endostyle as the thyroid), Olfactores, Vertebrata (neural crest, cranium, bone), Gnathostomata (jaws, paired fins), Osteichthyes (endochondral bone, lungs), Sarcopterygii (lobed limb skeleton), Tetrapoda (digits), Amniota (amniotic egg, keratinized skin), Sauropsida and Lepidosauria (skin shedding, transverse cloacal slit; [7.17.51](/7.17.51#sec-51.1)): about fifteen nodes. Characters acquired by convergence include the tympanic middle ear, which evolved independently in lepidosaurs ([7.17.68](/7.17.68#sec-68.3.3)), viviparity in many lizards, and the procoelous vertebrae, which evolved several times ([7.17.60](/7.17.60#sec-60.3.2)).

4. A student argues: "insects are diverse because they can fly". Evaluate the claim using the methods of Section 70.4.

   **Answer** Flight may have contributed, but the claim cannot be tested directly, because wings evolved only once: the only sister-group comparison, Pterygota against Zygentoma, is a single event, and the small size of the silverfish lineage could be due to extinction or chance (Example 30.4.1, [7.17.30](/7.17.30#sec-30.4.2)). Other evidence points to several factors: most insect diversity is within the Neoptera (wing folding) and the Holometabola (complete metamorphosis); phytophagous clades are richer than their sister groups in replicated comparisons; and low family-level extinction allowed diversity to accumulate. Many winged insects are not especially diverse, and flight was lost repeatedly in rich lineages. The claim should be restated: flight is one of several features whose combination, with small size, specialization and low extinction, explains insect diversity.

5. Trace the hyoid arch from a shark to a human through skeleton, muscle, nerve and artery.

   **Answer** Skeleton: in a shark the dorsal element of the hyoid arch is the hyomandibula, which supports the jaw (hyostyly), and the ventral element the ceratohyal; in tetrapods the hyomandibula became the stapes (columella), and in humans the arch gives the stapes, the styloid process and the lesser horns and upper body of the hyoid ([7.17.58](/7.17.58#sec-58.3.4)). Muscle: the hyoid-arch muscles (constrictors, interhyoideus) of fishes became the stapedius, the posterior belly of the digastric, the stylohyoid and the platysma and facial muscles of mammals ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.5.3)). Nerve: all are supplied by the facial nerve (VII), the nerve of the hyoid arch ([7.17.67](/7.17.67#sec-67.5.4)). Artery: aortic arch II supplies the hyoid hemibranch in sharks, is lost in teleosts and tetrapods, and leaves the stapedial artery in mammals, which in many species runs through the ring of the stapes ([7.17.65](/7.17.65#sec-65.3.3)).

6. Why do the comparative chapters still use the seven traditional columns (cyclostomes to mammals) if "bony fishes" and "reptiles" are paraphyletic? What precautions does this require?

   **Answer** The columns are a convenient arrangement of the data that follows the Chinese textbooks and the older comparative anatomies, and five of them (cyclostomes, chondrichthyans, lissamphibians, birds, mammals) are clades ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.8.1)). The precautions: a feature shared by reptiles and birds is a sauropsid feature, not a "reptilian" one; a feature found in reptiles but not in mammals is not thereby primitive, because the two lineages have evolved separately since the Carboniferous; "bony fishes" in the tables means ray-finned fishes, and the lungfishes, which are closer to tetrapods, must be considered separately when a character's origin is at issue (the lungs, [7.17.64](/7.17.64#sec-64.3.1)). Evolutionary sequences must be read from the tree, not from the order of the columns.

7. (Open question) Choose one functional problem not treated in Section 70.3, for example adhesion to surfaces, venom injection or bioluminescence, and assemble the evidence from the course that it was solved repeatedly.

   **Answer** Bioluminescence is a good example. In the course it appears in ctenophores (photocytes with photoproteins, [7.17.8](/7.17.8#sec-8.1)), cnidarians (the source of GFP, [7.17.9](/7.17.9#sec-9.8)), cephalopods with symbiotic bacteria (*Euprymna*–*Aliivibrio*, [7.17.14](/7.17.14#sec-14.7.5)), fireflies and click beetles, whose luciferases were recruited independently from acyl-CoA synthetases ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.7)), and deep-sea fishes. The chemistry differs (photoproteins, luciferin–luciferase systems, bacterial light), the light organs differ in origin, and the functions differ (warning, mating signals, counter-illumination, lures). A complete answer lists the lineages, the light-producing system in each, the evidence for independent origin (different enzymes or symbionts in close relatives) and the functional reasons. Similar answers can be given for venom (cnidae, cone snail radulae, spider chelicerae, centipede forcipules, snake fangs, the spur of the platypus) or adhesion (tube feet, gecko setae, insect pads).
