The circulatory system of vertebrates is closed: blood stays within a continuous system of vessels, driven by a single chambered heart. Its plan is the same in all vertebrates. A heart lying ventrally behind the pharynx pumps blood forward into a ventral aorta, through a series of arches running up between the gill slits to a dorsal aorta, which carries it back along the body; veins return it to the heart. What changes in evolution is the respiratory organ that the blood visits on its way. In fishes the gills lie in series between the heart and the body, and the heart pumps only deoxygenated blood. In tetrapods the lungs receive blood from one pair of arches and return it to the heart, which must then keep two streams apart and pump them to two destinations. The history of the heart, of the aortic arches and of the great veins is largely the history of this change from a single to a double circulation.

This chapter first sets out the general plan (§65.1). It then follows the heart from fishes to mammals (§65.2), the aortic arches and the main arteries (§65.3) and the veins (§65.4), with the fetal circulation (§65.5) and the lymphatic system (§65.6); §65.7 compares these structures class by class and with traditional accounts. Blood and its pigments, cardiac physiology, haemodynamics and circulatory regulation, including the functional analysis of cardiac shunts, are in [7.14.11](/7.14.11)–[7.14.16](/7.14.16); the development of the heart and vessels in [7.5.28](/7.5.28) and [7.5.29](/7.5.29); the respiratory organs that the arches supply in [7.17.64](/7.17.64); and the pharyngeal arches, whose arteries the aortic arches are, in [7.17.58](/7.17.58) and [7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.5).

## 65.1. The Plan of the Circulatory System

### 65.1.1. Components

The circulatory system comprises the cardiovascular system (heart, arteries, capillaries, veins and blood) and the lymphatic system (lymphatic vessels, lymph and lymphoid organs). The heart, vessels and blood cells derive from mesoderm: the heart from the splanchnic mesoderm of the lateral plate, the vessels from angioblasts that arise in many places in the mesoderm and assemble into vascular networks (vasculogenesis) that then sprout and remodel (angiogenesis; [7.5.29](/7.5.29)).

*Arteries* carry blood away from the heart, *veins* toward it; the terms refer to direction, not to oxygen content (the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood, the pulmonary veins oxygenated blood). Arteries have thick walls with elastic fibres and smooth muscle; veins have thinner walls and often valves. Between them lie the capillaries, whose walls of endothelium alone exchange substances with the tissues ([7.14.15](/7.14.15)). Lancelets have a closed vessel system without a heart, in which several contractile vessels propel the blood ([7.17.43](/7.17.43)); vertebrates add a chambered heart, erythrocytes with haemoglobin and an endothelium that lines all vessels.

### 65.1.2. Portal Systems

A **portal system** is a set of veins that collects blood from one capillary bed and delivers it to a second capillary bed before it returns to the heart. Vertebrates have three: the *hepatic portal system*, which carries blood from the capillaries of the stomach, intestine, pancreas and spleen to the sinusoids of the liver, so that absorbed nutrients and toxins are processed before reaching the general circulation ([7.14.29](/7.14.29#sec-29.7)); the *renal portal system* of non-mammalian vertebrates, which carries blood from the tail and hindlimbs to the peritubular capillaries of the kidney (§65.4); and the hypothalamo–hypophyseal portal system, which carries releasing hormones from the median eminence to the anterior pituitary ([7.17.67](/7.17.67)). The hepatic portal system is present in all vertebrates and the most constant part of the venous system; the renal portal system is large in fishes and amphibians and disappears in mammals.

### 65.1.3. Single and Double Circulation

In a *single circulation* the blood passes through the heart once in each circuit: heart → gills → body → heart. The heart contains only deoxygenated venous blood. Because the gill capillaries lie between the heart and the body, the pressure generated by the heart is partly spent in the gills, and the blood reaches the body at a lower pressure than that leaving the heart ([7.14.16](/7.14.16#sec-16.8)).

In a *double circulation* the blood passes through the heart twice in each circuit: once in the pulmonary circuit (heart → lungs → heart) and once in the systemic circuit (heart → body → heart). The two circuits can run at different pressures: low in the lungs, where a thin barrier must not be flooded, high in the body, where blood must be pushed through long resistant vessels. A double circulation is incompletely divided in lungfishes, amphibians and most reptiles, which can shunt blood between the circuits, and completely divided in crocodylians (except for the foramen of Panizza), birds and mammals.

**Example 65.1.1 (Pressure in Series and in Parallel)** In a resting rainbow trout the ventral aortic pressure is about $5\,\mathrm{kPa}$ (about $40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$) and the dorsal aortic pressure about $3.5\,\mathrm{kPa}$: roughly a third of the pressure generated by the heart is lost in the gills before blood reaches the body. If the gill vasculature has resistance $R_{\mathrm{g}}$ and the systemic vasculature $R_{\mathrm{s}}$, both carry the full cardiac output $Q$ in a single circulation, and the ventricle must generate $\Delta P = Q(R_{\mathrm{g}} + R_{\mathrm{s}})$; the systemic perfusion pressure is only $QR_{\mathrm{s}}$. In a double circulation the two circuits are in series through the heart, which is two pumps: the right ventricle generates $QR_{\mathrm{p}}$ for the lungs and the left ventricle $QR_{\mathrm{s}}$ for the body, so a low pulmonary pressure (about $2\,\mathrm{kPa}$ in mammals) protects the thin blood–gas barrier while the systemic pressure (about $13\,\mathrm{kPa}$ mean arterial pressure) drives blood through the body. A divided heart thus allows a high systemic pressure without high pressure in the gas-exchange organ, a precondition for the high blood flows of endotherms. Amphibians and reptiles, with one ventricle, have nearly equal pulmonary and systemic pressures (except monitor lizards and pythons with functional separation) and lower systemic pressures, around $4$–$6\,\mathrm{kPa}$.

## 65.2. The Heart

### 65.2.1. The Heart as a Tube of Chambers

The heart develops from paired endocardial tubes beneath the pharynx that fuse into a single straight tube in the pericardial cavity ([7.5.28](/7.5.28#sec-28.4)). Cells of the first heart field form the left ventricle and parts of the atria; the second heart field adds cells to both ends, forming the outflow tract and parts of the right ventricle and atria (**second heart field**). The tube is divided into four chambers in series, from back to front: the *sinus venosus*, a thin-walled chamber receiving the great veins; the *atrium*; the *ventricle*, the thick muscular pump; and the outflow tract, which is a contractile *conus arteriosus* with rows of valves in chondrichthyans and many basal bony fishes, and a non-contractile elastic *bulbus arteriosus* (a swelling of the ventral aorta lined by smooth muscle and elastin) in teleosts. As the tube grows it loops into an S, which brings the atrium dorsal and anterior to the ventricle; the chambers then lie side by side rather than in a line, the condition from which all vertebrate hearts are derived.

The chambers are separated by valves that keep blood flowing forward: sinoatrial valves, atrioventricular valves and the semilunar valves of the outflow tract. The heartbeat starts in the sinus venosus, whose pacemaker becomes the sinoatrial node of birds and mammals when the sinus is incorporated into the right atrium ([7.14.13](/7.14.13)).

### 65.2.2. Hearts of Fishes

- *Cyclostomes.* The heart has a sinus venosus, one atrium and one ventricle, with no conus. Hagfishes have a low-pressure circulation with large blood sinuses and accessory hearts: a portal heart that pumps blood from the intestine and cardinal veins into the liver, cardinal hearts in the head and caudal hearts in the tail. The main heart is aneural, its rate set by its own pacemaker and by filling.
- *Chondrichthyans.* The heart has the four chambers in series, with a muscular conus arteriosus containing two to several rows of semilunar valves; the atrium lies dorsal to the ventricle. The rigid pericardium allows the heart to suck blood into the atrium by creating negative pressure when the ventricle contracts ([7.17.63](/7.17.63#sec-63.1.2)).
- *Actinopterygians.* Basal groups (bichirs, sturgeons, gars, bowfin) have a conus with several rows of valves. In teleosts the conus is reduced to a ring with one pair of valves, and the outflow is a bulbus arteriosus whose elastic wall smooths the pulsatile output of the ventricle, like the compliant arteries of mammals ([7.14.14](/7.14.14)). The ventricle has a spongy inner layer of trabeculae supplied by venous blood and, in active fishes (salmonids, tunas), a compact outer layer supplied by coronary arteries.
- *Lungfishes.* The lungs return oxygenated blood through pulmonary veins to the left side of the atrium, which is partly divided by a septum; the ventricle also has a partial septum, and a spiral fold in the conus directs the two streams to different arches. Lungfishes thus have the beginnings of a double circulation, which evolved with air breathing.

### 65.2.3. Hearts of Amphibians

Amphibians have a sinus venosus, two atria, one ventricle and a conus arteriosus. The atria are completely separated by an interatrial septum in frogs and most salamanders: the right atrium receives deoxygenated blood from the body (via the sinus venosus) and, in frogs, oxygenated blood from the skin, the left atrium oxygenated blood from the lungs via the pulmonary veins. The single ventricle has no septum, but its wall is deeply trabeculated, with spongy muscular columns that hold the two streams partly apart. The conus contains a spiral valve, a twisted fold that tends to direct the blood ejected first (mainly deoxygenated, from the right side) to the pulmocutaneous arches and the later ejected blood (mainly oxygenated) to the systemic and carotid arches.

The separation is incomplete, and amphibians vary the distribution of blood between lungs and body according to need: during diving or when the skin covers most of the gas exchange, blood bypasses the lungs ([7.14.16](/7.14.16#sec-16.8)). In lungless salamanders (Plethodontidae) the atrial septum is reduced or perforated and the pulmonary veins are absent, since there is no lung to separate ([7.17.49](/7.17.49)); in caecilians and some salamanders the septum is fenestrated.

### 65.2.4. Hearts of Lepidosaurs and Turtles

Lizards, snakes and turtles have two completely separate atria and a ventricle divided incompletely into three interconnected compartments (cava): the cavum venosum (receiving blood from the right atrium), the cavum arteriosum (receiving blood from the left atrium) and the cavum pulmonale (leading to the pulmonary artery). A muscular ridge partly separates the cavum pulmonale from the cavum venosum. The outflow tract has divided into three trunks that leave the ventricle separately: the pulmonary trunk from the cavum pulmonale, and the left and right systemic arches (aortae) from the cavum venosum. The sinus venosus is still a distinct chamber.

During ventricular contraction the muscular ridge presses against the ventricular wall, so that deoxygenated blood in the cavum venosum and cavum pulmonale is pushed into the pulmonary trunk and oxygenated blood from the cavum arteriosum, crossing the cavum venosum, into the systemic arches. Under resting conditions mixing is small; in many lizards less than 10–20 %. By changing the resistance of the pulmonary circuit (with smooth-muscle sphincters in the pulmonary artery), turtles and snakes can shunt blood from right to left, bypassing the lungs during breath-holding and diving. Monitor lizards and pythons have a well-developed ridge and generate high systemic and low pulmonary pressures in the same ventricle, behaving functionally almost like a divided heart. Physiologists now regard these hearts not as imperfect intermediates between amphibian and mammalian hearts but as adaptive designs for intermittent breathing ([7.14.16](/7.14.16#sec-16.8.3)).

### 65.2.5. The Crocodylian Heart

Crocodylians have a four-chambered heart with a complete interventricular septum: right and left atria and right and left ventricles. Three trunks leave the ventricles. The pulmonary trunk and the left aorta (the left systemic arch) both leave the right ventricle; the right aorta (right systemic arch, which also gives the carotid and subclavian arteries) leaves the left ventricle. The two aortae communicate through a small opening, the **foramen of Panizza**, where they cross at their bases, and again by an anastomosis behind the heart.

At rest the pressure in the right ventricle is lower than in the left aorta, the valve of the left aorta stays closed, and blood from the right ventricle goes only to the lungs; the left aorta is then filled with oxygenated blood from the right aorta through the foramen of Panizza. During diving the pulmonary resistance rises, partly through a cog-tooth valve of nodules of connective tissue in the outflow of the right ventricle that closes it actively, the right-ventricular pressure exceeds the aortic pressure, and deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle is ejected into the left aorta, bypassing the lungs. The left aorta supplies mainly the stomach and intestine; one hypothesis is that sending acid-rich, carbon dioxide-rich blood to the stomach aids gastric acid secretion after large meals. The crocodylian heart shows that complete ventricular separation evolved in archosaurs before birds, together with a mechanism for shunting.

### 65.2.6. Hearts of Birds and Mammals

Birds and mammals have four-chambered hearts with complete atrial and ventricular septa, a complete separation of the pulmonary and systemic circuits and a single systemic arch: the right one in birds, the left one in mammals (§65.3.3). The sinus venosus is incorporated into the wall of the right atrium, where it forms the region where the caval veins enter and the sinoatrial node; in birds a small sinus venosus remains distinct in some species. The conus arteriosus is split into the bases of the aorta and the pulmonary trunk with their semilunar valves. The ventricular walls are compact and supplied by coronary arteries, the left ventricle much thicker than the right, which pumps against the low resistance of the lungs.

The four-chambered hearts of birds and mammals are convergent. Their common ancestor was an early amniote with an incompletely divided ventricle like that of a lizard, and the septum was completed independently in synapsids and archosaurs. Details reflect the independence: the right atrioventricular valve is a muscular flap in birds and a fibrous tricuspid valve with chordae tendineae in mammals; birds keep the right systemic arch, mammals the left. The developmental basis of septation is shared, however: in both, the interventricular septum forms where the transcription factor *Tbx5* (expressed on the left side) meets regions without it, and in turtles and lizards *Tbx5* is expressed in a shallow gradient across the whole ventricle, which correlates with the incomplete septum. Raising or sharpening the *Tbx5* boundary experimentally changes the extent of septation, an example of deep homology of a mechanism underlying convergent structures ([7.5.28](/7.5.28#sec-28.7)).

### 65.2.7. Myocardium, Coronary Supply and the Position of the Heart

The wall of the ventricle consists of two kinds of myocardium. *Spongy* (trabecular) myocardium is a meshwork of muscle columns bathed by the blood in the lumen, from which it takes its oxygen; *compact* myocardium is a solid outer layer that needs its own blood supply through coronary arteries. Cyclostomes, most teleosts and amphibians have mainly spongy ventricles that live on luminal blood, which in a fish heart is deoxygenated venous blood: the heart of a fish works on the oxygen left over by the tissues. Elasmobranchs and active teleosts (salmonids, tunas) add a compact layer supplied by coronary arteries from the efferent branchial arteries, that is with oxygenated blood just leaving the gills; in tunas the compact layer makes up more than half the ventricular mass. Crocodylians, birds and mammals have predominantly compact ventricles fed by coronary arteries from the base of the aorta, and their spongy myocardium is reduced to a thin layer of trabeculae carneae during development; persistent spongy myocardium in humans is a congenital cardiomyopathy ("non-compaction"), a reminder of the ancestral condition.

The heart lies in the pericardial cavity behind the gills in fishes and moves back into the thorax with the lengthening of the neck in amniotes ([7.17.63](/7.17.63#sec-63.1.3)). In snakes its position varies with way of life: about a quarter of the body length behind the head in arboreal species, which must maintain blood flow to the head when climbing vertically, further back in terrestrial and aquatic species, where hydrostatic pressure gradients are smaller or are cancelled by the surrounding water ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.7.2)). The heart itself is innervated by the vagus (slowing) and, in tetrapods, by sympathetic nerves (accelerating); in hagfishes it has no nerves at all ([7.17.67](/7.17.67)).

## 65.3. The Arterial System and the Aortic Arches

### 65.3.1. The Basic Pattern

In all vertebrate embryos the arterial system begins with the same plan. A ventral aorta runs forward from the heart beneath the pharynx; paired dorsal aortae run along the roof of the pharynx and join behind it into a single dorsal aorta that runs back beneath the vertebral column; and a series of arches connects the two, one in each pharyngeal arch.

**Definition 65.3.1 (Aortic Arch)** An **aortic arch** (pharyngeal arch artery) is one of the paired arteries that connect the ventral aorta with the dorsal aortae through the pharyngeal arches. Vertebrate embryos form six pairs, numbered I–VI from front to back in the mandibular arch, hyoid arch and the four following arches; in gill-breathing vertebrates the arches are interrupted by the capillaries of the gills into afferent and efferent branchial arteries.

The six pairs do not exist at the same time: arches I and II form first and regress while the posterior ones form. In fishes the persisting arches carry blood through the gills; in tetrapods the third, fourth and sixth arches survive in modified form in every adult, as the carotid, systemic and pulmonary arches, and the fate of the arches is one of the most often cited examples of descent with modification. The numbering refers to the pharyngeal arches, not to the gill slits: aortic arch III runs in the third pharyngeal arch, which in fishes is the first gill arch, and arch VI in the sixth pharyngeal arch, the fourth gill arch of a teleost ([7.17.58](/7.17.58)).

The same arch can be followed through all vertebrates because it keeps its relations with the skeleton, the nerve and the muscles of its pharyngeal arch: the glossopharyngeal nerve runs behind arch III, the vagus behind arches IV–VI, and the recurrent laryngeal nerve, the nerve of the sixth arch, loops behind the sixth arch artery (in mammals the ductus arteriosus on the left, and on the right, where the distal sixth arch disappears, the subclavian artery, derived from arch IV) on its way back up to the larynx ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.5.4)). In giraffes this nerve runs several metres down the neck and back up, a detour that records the descent of the heart from the pharyngeal region into the thorax.

### 65.3.2. Fishes and Amphibians

In fishes each persisting arch is divided by the gill capillaries into an afferent branchial artery from the ventral aorta and an efferent branchial artery to the dorsal aorta. The lost arches and their remnants are:

- *Chondrichthyans.* Arch I is lost in the adult, although a small artery from the first efferent branchial artery supplies the spiracle (the pseudobranch); arches II–VI pass through five pairs of gills (the hyoid hemibranch and four holobranchs). In sharks the efferent arteries of each gill form loops around the gill slit, and the carotid arteries arise from the efferent vessels of the first gills.
- *Teleosts.* Arches I and II are lost; arches III–VI supply the four gill arches. The internal carotid arteries continue from the dorsal aortae forward.
- *Lungfishes and bichirs.* As in teleosts, but a pulmonary artery branches from each sixth efferent artery to the lung, which therefore receives blood that has already passed through the gills. In the African lungfish, whose posterior gill arches lack filaments, blood can flow directly through arches III and IV to the body and through the gill-less arches V and VI to the lungs.

Amphibian larvae have a fish-like pattern with gill arches III–VI. At metamorphosis the gill capillaries degenerate, the afferent and efferent arteries of each arch join into a continuous arch, and the arches transform:

- *Frogs.* Arch III becomes the carotid arch (common carotid → internal carotid, with a carotid body, a spongy swelling at its base that acts as a pressure and chemical sensor and helps direct flow); arch IV becomes the systemic arch, which on each side joins the dorsal aorta; arch V disappears; arch VI becomes the *pulmocutaneous arch*, which divides into the pulmonary artery to the lung and the cutaneous artery to the skin. The segment of the dorsal aorta between arches III and IV (the *ductus caroticus*) disappears, and so does the dorsal part of arch VI beyond the pulmonary branch (the *ductus arteriosus*).
- *Salamanders.* Arch V usually persists as a small vessel joining the systemic arch, and the ductus arteriosus and ductus caroticus are retained in many species, so that more connections between the circuits remain than in frogs.

### 65.3.3. Amniotes

In amniotes the outflow of the heart divides into three trunks, and arches III, IV and VI persist; arches I, II and V disappear.

- *Reptiles.* The right systemic arch (IV) leaves the left side of the ventricle and gives off the carotid arteries; the left systemic arch (IV) leaves the right side (the cavum venosum); the pulmonary trunk supplies both sixth arches, which become the pulmonary arteries. The two systemic arches join behind the heart to form the dorsal aorta. Lizards retain the ductus caroticus in many species; turtles and the tuatara can retain the ductus arteriosus.
- *Birds.* The left fourth arch disappears; the right fourth arch is the only systemic arch, curving over the right bronchus. The left and right common carotids arise from brachiocephalic trunks, which also give the large subclavian arteries to the flight muscles. The ductus arteriosus closes at hatching.
- *Mammals.* The left fourth arch is the systemic arch (the arch of the aorta, curving over the left bronchus); the right fourth arch forms the base of the right subclavian artery (the brachiocephalic trunk in humans), and the right dorsal aortic root behind it disappears. The third arches give the common carotid arteries and the proximal internal carotids; the external carotids sprout from their bases. The left sixth arch gives the left pulmonary artery and the ductus arteriosus (duct of Botallo) to the aorta, which closes after birth into the ligamentum arteriosum; the right sixth arch gives the right pulmonary artery and loses its connection to the dorsal aorta. Arches I and II leave small remnants: arch I part of the maxillary artery, arch II the stapedial artery, which in many mammals (and in human embryos) passes through the ring of the stapes.

Why birds keep the right and mammals the left arch is not explained by function; the choice was probably fixed by chance in each lineage once the two systemic arches of their ancestors became redundant with a complete septum. What both share is the loss of one of the two systemic arches, which is consistent with the hypothesis that two systemic arches are useful only when the heart can shunt blood.

### 65.3.4. Branches of the Dorsal Aorta

Behind the arches the dorsal aorta gives off paired and unpaired branches whose pattern is broadly constant. Paired segmental (intercostal and lumbar) arteries supply the body wall; the subclavian arteries supply the forelimbs, and the iliac arteries the hindlimbs, continuing as the caudal artery in the haemal canal of the tail. Paired visceral branches supply the kidneys, gonads and adrenals. Unpaired branches in the dorsal mesentery supply the gut: the coeliac artery (stomach, liver, spleen, pancreas), the superior (cranial) mesenteric artery (intestine) and the inferior (caudal) mesenteric artery (posterior large intestine). In fishes the umbilical arteries are absent; in amniote embryos the umbilical (allantoic) arteries arise from the iliac arteries and carry blood to the allantois or placenta (§65.5).

Many vertebrates have retia mirabilia, dense networks of parallel arteries and veins that work as countercurrent exchangers (**rete mirabile**): in the swim bladder of physoclistous fishes ([7.17.64](/7.17.64#sec-64.3.2)), in the red muscle of tunas and lamnid sharks, which keeps them warm (**countercurrent heat exchange**, [7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.5)), in the legs of birds and the flippers of whales, and in the carotid rete of artiodactyls, which cools the blood reaching the brain.

### 65.3.5. Arteries of the Head and the Brain

The internal carotid artery, the forward continuation of the dorsal aorta beyond arch III, supplies the brain; the external carotid, a branch of the third arch near its base, supplies the face, jaws and tongue in amniotes. In fishes the corresponding territories are supplied by the efferent branchial arteries of the first gill arches and by the hyoidean and mandibular arteries. At the base of the brain the internal carotids join the vertebral and basilar arteries (in mammals) into an arterial ring, the circle of Willis, which allows blood to reach all parts of the brain if one supply is blocked. Birds have a similar ring and, in many species, the two internal carotids fused into a single vessel in a groove of the neck vertebrae; long-necked birds and giraffes have special adaptations (valves, a carotid rete or high blood pressure) to supply a brain far above the heart ([7.14.14](/7.14.14)). In mammals the stapedial artery, a remnant of the second arch, supplies much of the dura and face in the embryo; in many adult mammals (rodents, some insectivores) it persists and passes through the ring of the stapes, whereas in humans it regresses and its territory is taken over by branches of the external carotid (the middle meningeal artery).

## 65.4. The Venous System

### 65.4.1. The Basic Pattern in Fishes

The venous system of a shark shows the ancestral pattern of four groups of veins draining into the sinus venosus.

1. *The cardinal veins.* Paired anterior cardinal veins drain the head, paired posterior cardinal veins the body wall, kidneys and gonads; on each side they join into a common cardinal vein (duct of Cuvier) that enters the sinus venosus through the septum transversum. This H-shaped arrangement of four cardinal veins is the basic vertebrate pattern.
2. *The hepatic portal system.* Veins from the stomach, intestine, spleen and pancreas, derived from the embryonic subintestinal vein, form the hepatic portal vein to the liver; hepatic veins carry the blood from the liver to the sinus venosus.
3. *The renal portal system.* The caudal vein from the tail divides into renal portal veins to the kidneys, where the blood passes through the peritubular capillaries before entering the posterior cardinal veins. In the shark embryo the posterior cardinals first connect with the caudal vein directly; the connection is then interrupted, which diverts the caudal blood through the kidneys.
4. *The lateral abdominal veins.* Paired veins in the lateral body wall drain the pelvic fins and body wall and join the subclavian veins from the pectoral fins before entering the common cardinal veins.

Teleosts have a similar system, but most lack lateral abdominal veins; the blood from the posterior body is collected by the posterior cardinals, and the renal portal system is often reduced, with the caudal vein continuing directly into the right posterior cardinal. Cyclostomes have cardinal and hepatic portal veins but no renal portal system; lampreys have a single inferior jugular vein, and hagfishes their accessory hearts in the venous circulation.

### 65.4.2. The Caval System of Tetrapods

In tetrapods the cardinal veins are largely replaced by caval veins, and pulmonary veins return blood from the lungs. The changes are:

1. *The anterior vena cava* (precava) on each side forms from the common cardinal vein and the proximal anterior cardinal vein; it receives the internal jugular vein (the rest of the anterior cardinal), the external jugular and the subclavian vein.
2. *The posterior vena cava* (postcava) is a new vessel formed by the joining of segments of several embryonic veins: the hepatic veins and a new vessel in the dorsal mesentery near the liver anteriorly, and parts of the subcardinal (and in mammals the supracardinal) veins, which run medial to the kidneys, posteriorly. It collects blood from the kidneys, gonads and posterior body and replaces the posterior cardinal veins as the main return route. Lungfishes already have a posterior vena cava, which is therefore an ancestral feature of sarcopterygian air breathers.
3. *The posterior cardinal veins* regress in adult frogs and most amniotes; in mammals their remnants in the thorax form the azygos (right) and hemiazygos (left) veins, which drain the thoracic wall into the anterior vena cava.
4. *The pulmonary veins* carry blood from the lungs to the left atrium; they are homologous with the veins of the lung of lungfishes and bichirs.
5. *The abdominal veins* change: in amphibians the paired lateral abdominal veins of the embryo lose their connection with the subclavian veins and fuse into a single *ventral abdominal vein* in the ventral midline, which carries blood from the hindlimbs to the hepatic portal vein. Reptiles keep paired ventral abdominal veins entering the liver; birds and mammals lose them in the adult. In mammals the embryonic umbilical (allantoic) veins, which correspond to the abdominal veins, carry blood from the placenta through the liver to the postcava (§65.5).
6. *The renal portal system* declines from amphibians to mammals. In amphibians and reptiles the iliac veins from the hindlimbs divide between renal portal veins and the abdominal veins. In birds the renal portal veins are connected with the iliac and caudal veins by a ring, and smooth-muscle renal portal valves at the entry of the iliac veins into the postcava decide whether blood flows through the kidney or bypasses it; a coccygeomesenteric vein, unique to birds, connects the renal portal ring with the hepatic portal vein. In mammals the renal portal system is absent: the kidneys receive only arterial blood, which matches the large glomerular filtration of mammalian kidneys ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)).

Two details of mammals deserve correction of a common simplification. Many mammals (monotremes, marsupials, rodents, rabbits, insectivores, many bats) keep both anterior venae cavae throughout life; in others (carnivores, ungulates, primates) the left precava is reduced to the coronary sinus, which drains the veins of the heart into the right atrium, and the left side of the head and forelimb drains through a brachiocephalic vein to the right precava. And the posterior vena cava of mammals runs through the liver, not just past it, receiving the hepatic veins.

### 65.4.4. Blood Cells and Haematopoiesis

The erythrocytes of all non-mammalian vertebrates are oval and nucleated; those of mammals lose their nucleus and organelles during maturation and are small biconcave discs (oval in camels and llamas), which increases their surface relative to volume and lets them pass through narrow capillaries. Erythrocyte size varies inversely with metabolic rate and genome size: the largest erythrocytes, up to $70\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ long, belong to salamanders such as *Amphiuma*, which have very large genomes, while those of mammals are $5$–$10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ across and those of birds $10$–$15\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Antarctic icefishes (Channichthyidae) have no erythrocytes and no haemoglobin at all and compensate with large hearts, high blood volume and the high solubility of oxygen in cold water, a remarkable exception that shows how far a circulatory system can be modified ([7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.6.1)).

The sites of blood-cell formation change with the skeleton. Fishes form blood cells in the kidney (the head kidney of teleosts), the spleen and the intestinal wall, since their bones mostly lack marrow cavities; amphibians in the spleen, kidney and, in frogs after metamorphosis, the bone marrow; amniotes mainly in the red bone marrow, after an embryonic phase in the yolk sac, liver and spleen ([7.5.29](/7.5.29#sec-29.7)). The thymus, spleen and, in birds, the cloacal bursa house lymphocytes (§65.6).

## 65.5. The Fetal Circulation

In amniote embryos gas exchange takes place in extraembryonic membranes, the chorioallantois of reptiles and birds and the placenta of therian mammals ([7.5.5](/7.5.5); [7.17.50](/7.17.50)), and the lungs are filled with fluid and receive little blood. The **fetal circulation** of mammals therefore has shunts that bypass the lungs and liver, and the story of their closure at birth recapitulates, in outline, the structures of the ancestral heart and arches ([7.5.28](/7.5.28#sec-28.9)).

| Fetal structure | Function | Fate after birth |
|---|---|---|
| Umbilical vein (one, left) | Carries oxygenated blood from the placenta to the fetus | Round ligament of the liver (in the falciform ligament) |
| Ductus venosus | Shunts umbilical blood past the liver sinusoids into the posterior vena cava | Ligamentum venosum |
| Foramen ovale (with the valve of the septum primum) | Passes blood from the right to the left atrium, bypassing the lungs | Closes when left atrial pressure exceeds right; fossa ovalis |
| Ductus arteriosus | Carries blood from the pulmonary trunk to the aorta, bypassing the lungs | Constricts within hours to days (rising oxygen, falling prostaglandins); ligamentum arteriosum |
| Umbilical arteries (paired) | Carry deoxygenated blood from the internal iliac arteries to the placenta | Proximal parts persist as superior vesical arteries; distal parts become the medial umbilical ligaments |

At the first breaths the lungs expand, pulmonary resistance falls, pulmonary blood flow rises and the pressure in the left atrium exceeds that in the right, which presses the valve of the foramen ovale shut. The ductus arteriosus is the remnant of the dorsal part of the left sixth aortic arch, and the foramen ovale recalls the communication between the atria of animals with an incomplete atrial septum. In birds the embryo breathes through the chorioallantoic membrane supplied by allantoic arteries and veins; just before hatching it pierces the air cell and starts breathing with its lungs, and the two ductus arteriosi close.

## 65.6. The Lymphatic System

The lymphatic system collects excess interstitial fluid filtered from the capillaries and returns it to the veins as lymph, and it carries absorbed fats from the intestine and lymphocytes between lymphoid organs ([7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.3); development in [7.5.29](/7.5.29#sec-29.4.3)). Lymphatic vessels arise from venous endothelium in the embryo and are therefore a derived part of the venous system.

Its anatomy varies with blood pressure and with the way lymph is propelled.

- *Fishes.* The homology of the fish lymphatic system has been debated, since a "secondary vascular system" of fine vessels branching from arteries carries a cell-poor fluid in teleosts; zebrafish nevertheless have true lymphatic vessels that develop from veins as in mammals. Lymph hearts occur in the tail of some teleosts.
- *Amphibians and reptiles.* Lymphatic vessels have few or no valves and are expanded into large subcutaneous lymph sacs (in frogs separating the skin from the muscles over most of the body). Contractile *lymph hearts* of striated muscle pump the lymph into veins: frogs have two pairs (anterior, near the third vertebra, and posterior, near the urostyle), some salamanders and caecilians up to dozens along the body; reptiles have one pair at the base of the tail emptying into the renal portal veins ([7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.5.4)). Lymph hearts are needed because the low blood pressure of amphibians and their high fluid filtration through the skin produce much lymph.
- *Birds.* Embryos have lymph hearts, which persist in the adult only in some birds (ostriches, some others). Lymph vessels have valves, and a pair of thoracic ducts drain into the anterior venae cavae; true lymph nodes are few, found mainly in waterfowl. The cloacal bursa (bursa of Fabricius), a dorsal diverticulum of the cloaca, is the primary lymphoid organ in which B cells mature ([7.17.63](/7.17.63#sec-63.8); [7.15](/7.15)).
- *Mammals.* Lymphatic vessels have many valves and are propelled by contraction of their smooth muscle and by the movement of surrounding tissues. They pass through numerous lymph nodes, which filter the lymph and house lymphocytes, and join into the thoracic duct, which begins in the abdomen as the cisterna chyli and drains into the junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins, and a smaller right lymphatic duct. Lymph hearts are absent.

Lymphoid organs are present in all gnathostomes: the **thymus**, a derivative of the pharyngeal pouches (pouches 3 and 4 in mammals, more in fishes and amphibians) where T cells mature, and the **spleen**, a lymphoid organ and blood reservoir in the dorsal mesogaster ([7.17.67](/7.17.67)). Cyclostomes have no discrete spleen or thymus but have lymphoid tissue in the gut wall and in thymus-like structures (thymoids) at the tips of the gill filaments of lamprey larvae, where their variable lymphocyte receptor-bearing T-like cells mature ([7.15.3](/7.15.3)).

### 65.7.2. Trends and Their Interpretation

Four trends run through the comparison.

1. *From single to double circulation.* With lungs, the pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood to the heart, and septa in the atria, ventricle and outflow tract increasingly separate the two streams. Complete separation evolved twice (synapsids, archosaurs), and incomplete separation is not a deficiency but allows controlled shunting in animals that breathe intermittently, dive or have low metabolic rates.
2. *Reduction of the aortic arches.* Of six embryonic pairs, fishes keep four or five as gill arches; tetrapods keep three (III, IV, VI) as carotid, systemic and pulmonary arches; birds and mammals keep only one of the two systemic arches. The arches that disappear (I, II, V, and the ductus caroticus and ductus arteriosus) leave remnants that betray their former presence.
3. *From cardinal to caval veins.* The H-shaped cardinal system of fishes is replaced by anterior venae cavae and a new posterior vena cava; the posterior cardinals survive in mammals only as the azygos system.
4. *Decline of the renal portal system.* It is large in fishes and amphibians, reduced and bypassable in reptiles and birds and absent in mammals, as the kidney shifts from tubular secretion supplied with venous blood to glomerular filtration of arterial blood ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)).

Some statements found in older accounts should be refined. The hearts of lizards and turtles are not merely a step toward the mammalian heart but a design that permits shunting; birds and mammals did not inherit their four-chambered hearts from a common ancestor; the sinus venosus does not simply vanish in birds and mammals but becomes part of the right atrium and its pacemaker; and many mammals keep two anterior venae cavae.

## Summary

- The vertebrate circulation is closed, with a ventral heart, a ventral aorta, six pairs of aortic arches (Definition 65.3.1) and a dorsal aorta in the basic plan; the hepatic portal system is constant, the renal portal system declines and is lost in mammals.
- The heart is a tube of chambers in series (sinus venosus, atrium, ventricle, conus or bulbus arteriosus). With air breathing it was divided progressively: partial septa in lungfishes, two atria and a spiral valve in amphibians, a ventricle with three cava and three outflow trunks in lepidosaurs and turtles, a complete septum with the foramen of Panizza in crocodylians, and convergent four-chambered hearts in birds and mammals.
- Fishes keep arches II–VI or III–VI as gill arches. Tetrapods keep III (carotid), IV (systemic) and VI (pulmonary or pulmocutaneous); amphibians lose V (except salamanders), birds the left and mammals the right systemic arch. The ductus arteriosus (dorsal part of VI) closes into the ligamentum arteriosum in mammals.
- The H-shaped cardinal venous system of fishes gives way to the anterior venae cavae and a composite posterior vena cava in tetrapods; pulmonary veins appear with lungs; the lateral abdominal veins become the ventral abdominal vein of amphibians and the umbilical veins of mammalian embryos; the posterior cardinals become the azygos system.
- The mammalian fetal circulation bypasses the lungs and liver through the ductus venosus, foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus, which close at birth.
- Lymph hearts pump lymph in amphibians and reptiles; mammals have valved lymph vessels, lymph nodes and a thoracic duct; birds have a cloacal bursa.

## Questions

1. Number the aortic arches of a teleost from the pharyngeal arch in which each runs, and explain why the first gill arch of a teleost carries aortic arch III.

   **Answer** Aortic arches are numbered by pharyngeal arch: I in the mandibular arch, II in the hyoid arch, III–VI in the following four arches. In teleosts arches I and II are lost (the mandibular arch forms the jaws, the hyoid arch the hyoid and opercular apparatus), and the first gill-bearing arch is the third pharyngeal arch, so the four gill arches of a teleost carry aortic arches III–VI. The numbering expresses homology with the arches of other vertebrates rather than position among the gills.

2. Trace the fate of the fourth and sixth aortic arches from a frog to a lizard, a bird and a mammal.

   **Answer** Arch IV: in the frog both fourth arches are systemic arches joining the dorsal aorta; in the lizard both persist as left and right systemic arches leaving different parts of the ventricle; in birds only the right persists; in mammals the left forms the arch of the aorta and the right only the base of the right subclavian artery. Arch VI: in the frog it is the pulmocutaneous arch to lung and skin, with the ductus arteriosus lost; in the lizard it forms the pulmonary arteries (ductus retained in some turtles and the tuatara); in birds the pulmonary arteries, with ductus closing at hatching; in mammals the pulmonary arteries, with the left ductus arteriosus open in the fetus and closed after birth into the ligamentum arteriosum.

3. Why is the recurrent laryngeal nerve on the left side of a mammal looped around the arch of the aorta, while on the right it loops around the subclavian artery?

   **Answer** The nerve belongs to the sixth pharyngeal arch and passes posterior to the sixth arch artery to reach the larynx. On the left the dorsal part of the sixth arch persists as the ductus arteriosus (ligamentum arteriosum) next to the arch of the aorta, so the nerve hooks around it; on the right the dorsal part of the sixth arch and the fifth arch disappear, and the nerve slips forward until it is caught by the next persisting vessel, the right fourth arch, which forms the base of the right subclavian artery. As the heart descended into the thorax the nerve was dragged with these vessels.

4. Explain how a crocodile can send deoxygenated blood to its body during a dive although its ventricles are completely separated.

   **Answer** The left aorta leaves the right ventricle next to the pulmonary trunk. At rest, the pressure in the right ventricle stays below that in the left aorta, which is filled with oxygenated blood from the right aorta through the foramen of Panizza, so the left aortic valve stays closed and all right-ventricular blood goes to the lungs. During a dive pulmonary resistance rises (with active closure of the cog-tooth valve in the right outflow tract), right-ventricular pressure rises above aortic pressure, the left aortic valve opens and deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle is ejected into the left aorta and the systemic circulation, bypassing the lungs.

5. Why are the four-chambered hearts of birds and mammals considered convergent? What evidence connects them at the level of developmental mechanism?

   **Answer** The common ancestor of birds and mammals was an early amniote whose closest living models (lizards, turtles) have an incompletely divided ventricle, and the phylogeny places complete septation independently in synapsids and in archosaurs; differences in detail (muscular right atrioventricular valve in birds, fibrous tricuspid in mammals; right systemic arch in birds, left in mammals) support independence. At the level of mechanism, in both groups the ventricular septum forms at a sharp boundary of *Tbx5* expression, whereas in turtles and lizards *Tbx5* forms a shallow gradient; experimental changes in the *Tbx5* pattern change septation, so the same gene network was used twice (deep homology).

6. The renal portal system is present in fishes, amphibians, reptiles and birds but absent in mammals. Relate its decline to the evolution of the kidney.

   **Answer** In fishes and amphibians the kidney tubules receive much of their blood from the renal portal veins, which bring venous blood from the tail and hindlimbs; this low-pressure supply serves tubular secretion and reabsorption, while glomeruli receive arterial blood. In reptiles and birds, which excrete uric acid largely by tubular secretion, the portal supply persists but can be bypassed (valves in birds), adjusting renal blood flow. Mammals rely on high glomerular filtration of arterial blood followed by reabsorption, and their kidneys receive only arterial blood: the renal portal veins are lost, and the posterior vena cava collects blood from the hindlimbs directly.
