The chapters on the invertebrate phyla ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)–[7.17.43](/7.17.43)) described each group as a whole: its body plan, its organs and its place on the tree. This chapter reads the same material in the other direction. It takes one organ system at a time and asks, across all the phyla, how many basic designs exist, where on the animal tree each design occurs, which similarities are inherited from a common ancestor and which arose independently, and what physical or functional constraints made a design possible or necessary. The two chapters on animal architecture ([7.17.3](/7.17.3), [7.17.4](/7.17.4)) introduced the vocabulary and a catalog of solutions before the phyla were described; here, with the phyla in hand, the catalog becomes a comparative anatomy.

The chapter is the invertebrate counterpart of the comparative anatomy of vertebrates ([7.17.56](/7.17.56)–[7.17.68](/7.17.68)), but the comparison works at a different scale. Among vertebrates almost every bone and vessel can be followed from fishes to mammals, and comparative anatomy is the study of homologous parts. Among invertebrates the phyla differ so much that most organs cannot be matched part for part; what can be compared are designs, and the main question is how often each design evolved. The physiology of the organs is treated in animal physiology ([7.14](/7.14)), their development in developmental biology ([7.5](/7.5)) and the methods of phylogenetic comparison in evolutionary biology ([7.10](/7.10)); this chapter links to them and concentrates on structure, distribution and evolution.

## 44.1. Comparing Organ Systems across Phyla

### 44.1.1. Homology at Different Levels

Two structures in different animals are homologous when they derive from the same structure in their common ancestor ([7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.3.2)). Classical comparative anatomy recognized homology by three criteria: similar position in the body plan, similar detailed composition ("special quality"), and continuity through intermediate forms in related species or in development. All three work well within a phylum, where the parts of a crayfish limb can be matched to those of a lobster or a copepod; between phyla they often fail, because body plans are so different that position has no common reference and intermediates are extinct. The decisive test is then the phylogeny: a character that is present in two phyla but absent from their closest relatives, which branch between them, is more probably convergent than homologous, whatever its similarity.

Modern work adds a complication. A structure can be homologous at one level of organization and convergent at another. The camera eyes of octopuses and vertebrates evolved independently, but their photoreceptor cells express homologous opsins inherited from the common bilaterian ancestor, and their development begins with the same transcription factor, Pax6 ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.4.1)). The segments of annelids, arthropods and vertebrates evolved separately, yet parts of the gene networks that make them are shared ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.5.2)). Such sharing of the genetic toolkit by organs that are not themselves homologous is **deep homology** ([7.5.39](/7.5.39#sec-39.3)). For each organ system, therefore, three questions must be kept apart:

| Level | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cell type | Did the specialized cells (photoreceptors, podocytes, striated muscle cells) arise once? | Ciliary and rhabdomeric photoreceptors both present in the bilaterian ancestor |
| Gene network | Is the developmental program shared? | Pax6 in eyes, *tinman*/Nkx2-5 in hearts, Six/Eya/Sall in nephridia |
| Organ | Was the organ itself present in the common ancestor? | Compound eyes homologous within arthropods, convergent in sabellid fan worms |
| Design | Is the overall solution (closed circulation, through gut, tracheae) the same? | Tracheae in insects, arachnids, onychophorans and woodlice: same design, separate origins |

A similarity between phyla at the level of design is usually convergence; one at the level of cell type or gene network is often homology. Much of the apparent contradiction in the older literature, where the same organ was declared homologous by one author and convergent by another, disappears once the level of comparison is stated.

### 44.1.2. The Physical Constraints

The repeated convergence of designs is not an accident. All animals live under the same physics, and a handful of scaling relations, most of them introduced in [7.17.4](/7.17.4), set the limits within which each organ system works:

| Relation | Form | What it constrains | Section |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surface and volume | Area $\propto L^2$, volume $\propto L^3$ | Every exchange organ: gills, guts, kidneys, skin | [7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.1.1) |
| Diffusion time | $t \approx x^2/2D$ (Formula 4.1.1) | Body thickness without circulation; distance from vessels to cells; tracheal length | [7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.1.2), Section 44.6 |
| Helical fiber geometry | $V \propto \sin^2\theta\cos\theta$ (Formula 4.2.1) | Shape changes of worms with hydrostatic skeletons | [7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.2.2) |
| Bending of a tube | Stiffness $\propto r^3 t$ | Exoskeleton versus endoskeleton | Section 44.2 |
| Reynolds number | $Re = \rho U L/\mu$ | Ciliary versus muscular locomotion and feeding | [7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.3.2) |
| Poiseuille flow | Pressure drop $\propto \mu v L/r^2$ at fixed velocity | Open versus closed circulation | Section 44.6 |
| Diffraction and geometric blur | Blur $\approx \lambda/A$ and $A/f$ | Pinhole, lens and compound eyes | Section 44.9 |

Because the same constraint acts in every phylum, the same few designs keep reappearing: flattening or a circulation when bodies grow thick, tubes that filter body fluid and then modify it, lenses once eyes are large enough to profit from them. Convergence is the expected outcome when many lineages explore a small space of physically workable solutions.

### 44.1.3. Reading the Organ Systems on the Tree

The figure below arranges the adult states of seven organ systems on the animal tree used throughout this course ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.5)). Two patterns can be read from it. Some states are confined to one clade and appear in all or most of its members: the dorsal hollow nerve cord in chordates, the molted cuticle in ecdysozoans, compound eyes in arthropods, the water vascular system and stereom in echinoderms. These are the synapomorphies of the clades, and the organs they describe are homologous within them. Other states are scattered across distant branches with different states in between: closed circulatory systems in annelids, nemerteans, cephalopods and chordates; lens eyes in cnidarians, molluscs, annelids, onychophorans and vertebrates; air-breathing organs in several arthropod lineages, snails and vertebrates. Scattered states are either convergent or ancestral and repeatedly lost, and parsimony on the tree, together with the detailed structure of the organs, decides between the two ([7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.6.1)).

The table also shows the correlations between systems that will recur in every section. The phyla without circulation are those without specialized gas-exchange organs, small or flat or thin. Sessile filter feeders (bryozoans, brachiopods, tunicates) combine a U-shaped gut with a reduced nervous system and few sense organs. Organ systems evolve as parts of a body plan, and the final section of the chapter returns to these linked characters.

## 44.2. Body Wall and Skeleton

### 44.2.1. Three Kinds of Body Covering

The outer surface of an invertebrate is an epidermis of a single cell layer, and the phyla differ mainly in what that epidermis does at its free surface. Three conditions cover almost all of them.

- *A motile ciliated epidermis.* Cnidarian larvae and polyps, ctenophores (whose comb plates are fused cilia), xenacoelomorphs, free-living flatworms, gastrotrichs, nemerteans, the larvae and parts of the adults of annelids, molluscs, brachiopods and echinoderms, and the larvae of lancelets all bear motile cilia on the body surface. The ciliated epidermis is the ancestral condition of the Eumetazoa in the broad sense: it serves for locomotion and feeding in small animals and for moving water over the surface.
- *A cuticle that is not molted.* Annelids secrete a thin collagenous cuticle through which epidermal microvilli pass ([7.17.15](/7.17.15#sec-15.1.2)); rotifers and acanthocephalans have instead an intracellular skeletal lamina inside a syncytial epidermis ([7.17.12](/7.17.12)); parasitic flatworms replace the epidermis by a syncytial tegument ([7.17.11](/7.17.11#sec-11.4.1)). None of these is shed, and the animal grows continuously.
- *A molted cuticle.* All ecdysozoans have a layered cuticle with an outer epicuticle, shed at each molt under the control of ecdysteroid-related hormones, and none has motile cilia on its surface ([7.17.17](/7.17.17#sec-17.1)). In panarthropods and scalidophorans the cuticle contains chitin, in nematodes it is mainly collagen.

The loss of surface cilia and the evolution of a molted cuticle are two sides of one change at the base of the Ecdysozoa. A cuticle thick enough to protect and to serve as a skeleton cannot be crossed by cilia, and once cilia are lost, every ecdysozoan, however small, must move with muscles: nematodes by undulation, tardigrades on legs, kinorhynchs with their introvert. Spiralians, which kept cilia, include many microscopic animals that glide on ciliated soles (gastrotrichs, small flatworms, rotifers on their corona). The cuticle is therefore a character that constrained the whole subsequent evolution of the ecdysozoan body, including growth in steps by molting ([7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.1)) and the use of sensilla that pierce the cuticle as sense organs ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.1)).

Chitin itself is much older than the Ecdysozoa. Chitin synthase genes are present in choanoflagellates and sponges, and chitin forms the chaetae of annelids and brachiopods, the radula and the periostracum of molluscs, the jaws of gnathiferans and the tube of siboglinid annelids. The distribution suggests an ancestral capacity to secrete chitin extracellularly that different phyla deployed in different structures, not one ancestral chitinous cuticle.

### 44.2.2. The Hydrostatic Skeleton as the Default

Most bilaterians support their bodies with fluid. A **hydrostatic skeleton** needs only an incompressible core and antagonistic muscles around it, and different phyla use whatever enclosed volume their body plan offers:

| Fluid volume | Muscles | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gastrovascular cavity, closed by the mouth | Epitheliomuscular cells, longitudinal and circular | Sea anemones, *Hydra* | The skeleton and the gut are the same space ([7.17.9](/7.17.9#sec-9.1.2)) |
| Parenchyma and the fluid between its cells | Circular, longitudinal and diagonal layers | Flatworms, xenacoelomorphs | Shape changes are limited; flatworms stay flat ([7.17.16](/7.17.16#sec-16.1.2)) |
| Coelomic rhynchocoel | Proboscis musculature | Nemerteans | A coelom used only to evert the proboscis |
| Blastocoelom under high pressure | Longitudinal muscles only | Nematodes | The stiff cuticle acts as the antagonist; the worm can only bend ([7.17.17](/7.17.17#sec-17.3.3)) |
| Septate coelom, one compartment per segment | Circular and longitudinal | Earthworms, many polychaetes | Local control of each segment allows peristalsis ([7.17.15](/7.17.15#sec-15.1.3)) |
| Undivided coelom | Circular and longitudinal | Sipunculans, echiurans, enteropneusts | Burrowing by waves of the whole body |
| Hemocoel | Body-wall and pedal muscles | Mollusc foot and siphons, caterpillars, extension of spider legs | Combined with other skeletons |
| Water vascular system | Ampullae and tube-foot muscles | Echinoderms | A separate hydraulic system for each tube foot ([7.17.42](/7.17.42#sec-42.3.2)) |
| Muscle tissue itself | Muscles in three directions | Cephalopod arms, tongues, elephant trunks | **Muscular hydrostat** (Definition 4.2.1) |

The list shows why the hydrostatic skeleton is better regarded as a principle than as an organ. It evolved wherever a muscular body wall surrounds an enclosed volume, and it was available to the first bilaterians, whose trace fossils record burrowing and crawling ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.2.2)). The only question that the hydrostatic skeleton raises for the phylogeny is that of the coelom: R. B. Clark argued that the coelom evolved, perhaps several times, as a hydrostatic skeleton for burrowing worms, which would explain why coelomate phyla are scattered on the tree among acoelomate and blastocoelomate ones ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.4.5)). Compartmentation adds a second step. A single fluid space transmits pressure throughout the body, so a contraction anywhere affects the whole animal; septa confine pressure to a segment and allow local control, and the septate coelom of annelids is part of the explanation for their segmentation.

### 44.2.3. Mineralized Skeletons: Many Origins

Mineral skeletons appeared in the fossil record within a short interval of geological time, between the latest Ediacaran (*Cloudina*, about 550 million years ago) and the early Cambrian, in a dozen or more lineages at once ([7.10.29](/7.10.29)). Their materials and modes of growth show that they evolved independently in each phylum.

**Definition 44.2.1 (Biomineralization)** **Biomineralization** is the formation of mineral deposits by living organisms under biological control, typically by secretion of an organic matrix of proteins and polysaccharides into an enclosed space, where the matrix nucleates crystals of a particular mineral and polymorph and determines their orientation and form.

| Skeleton | Mineral | Location and origin | Growth | Phyla |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spicules | Amorphous silica; calcite in calcareous sponges | Inside sclerocytes in the mesohyl, around an axial filament | Each spicule grows from one or a few cells | Porifera ([7.17.7](/7.17.7#sec-7.2.2)) |
| Coral skeleton | Aragonite | Secreted by the basal ectoderm beneath the polyp | Accretion; the polyp rises on its skeleton | Scleractinian corals; calcite spicules in octocorals ([7.17.9](/7.17.9#sec-9.4.2)) |
| Shell | Aragonite and calcite, in layers on an organic periostracum | Secreted by the mantle epidermis into the extrapallial space | Accretion at the margin | Molluscs ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.1.2)) |
| Shell | Calcite, or calcium phosphate in linguliform brachiopods | Secreted by the mantle epidermis | Accretion at the margin | Brachiopods ([7.17.16](/7.17.16#sec-16.4.1)) |
| Zooecium | Calcite or aragonite in the body wall | Epidermis of each zooid | Each zooid adds its own wall | Bryozoans ([7.17.16](/7.17.16#sec-16.5.1)) |
| Calcified tube | Calcite and aragonite | Secreted by glands of the collar | Accretion | Serpulid annelids |
| Calcified cuticle | Calcite and amorphous calcium carbonate in the procuticle | Epidermis, outside the cells | Molting | Crustaceans, millipedes ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.3.2)) |
| Stereom | Magnesian calcite, each ossicle a single crystal | Inside syncytia of sclerocytes in the mesodermal dermis | Internal growth and resorption | Echinoderms ([7.17.42](/7.17.42#sec-42.2.1)) |
| Bone, dentine | Hydroxyapatite (calcium phosphate) | Mesodermal and neural-crest cells | Internal growth and remodeling | Vertebrates ([7.17.58](/7.17.58)) |

Three observations argue for independent origins. The minerals differ: silica, calcite, aragonite and two forms of calcium phosphate, and even closely related groups use different polymorphs. The sites differ: inside cells (spicules, stereom), in a space under an epithelium (shells, coral skeletons) or in an extracellular matrix (cuticle, bone). And the matrix proteins that control crystallization are mostly lineage-specific, although some general components, such as carbonic anhydrases that supply carbonate and acidic proteins that bind calcium, are shared and probably ancient. Biomineralization is therefore a classic case of a common toolkit used to build structures that are not homologous, comparable to the case of eyes.

The mode of growth is as important as the mineral. A shell that grows by accretion at its margin can enlarge without interruption and records the history of the animal in its growth lines, and its geometry follows simple rules of coiling ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.6)). A cuticle hardened outside the cells cannot grow and must be molted, with all the consequences described for arthropods. A skeleton inside living tissue, like stereom and bone, can grow, be resorbed and be repaired, which is why only internal skeletons combine large size with continuous growth.

### 44.2.4. Exoskeleton and Endoskeleton

Arthropods and vertebrates are the two great groups of animals with articulated skeletons and muscles working on levers, one with the skeleton outside, the other inside. The mechanical difference between them follows from the geometry of tubes and rods.

**Formula 44.2.1 (Tube versus Rod)** A cylindrical skeletal element of length $l$ resists bending in proportion to its flexural rigidity $EI$, where $E$ is Young's modulus and $I$ the second moment of area of its cross section. For a thin-walled tube of radius $r$ and wall thickness $t \ll r$, and for a solid rod of radius $r_{\mathrm{s}}$,

$$
I_{\mathrm{tube}} \approx \pi r^3 t, \qquad I_{\mathrm{rod}} = \frac{\pi r_{\mathrm{s}}^4}{4}.
$$
If the two contain the same amount of material, $\pi r_{\mathrm{s}}^2 = 2\pi r t$, then

$$
\frac{I_{\mathrm{tube}}}{I_{\mathrm{rod}}} = \frac{r}{t}.
\tag{44.1}
$$

:::

A tube whose radius is ten times its wall thickness is thus ten times stiffer than a rod of the same mass. An exoskeleton is such a tube, and for small animals it is the lightest possible skeleton, which also protects the soft parts and, in arthropods, carries a waterproof wax layer. The advantage shrinks with size for three reasons. A thin-walled tube fails by local buckling of its wall, so the wall must thicken as loads grow; the weight to be carried rises with the cube of length, the strength of the skeleton only with the square; and at each molt the animal is soft and must support itself by hydrostatic pressure until the new cuticle hardens. The largest arthropods live in water, where buoyancy carries most of the weight. Vertebrate long bones are themselves hollow tubes with marrow cavities, so the contrast is less in the shape of the elements than in their position: an internal skeleton is surrounded by muscle, grows continuously and does not limit the size of the soft parts.

Hard skeletal elements within the body are not confined to echinoderms and vertebrates. Cephalopods have cartilage around the brain and at the base of the arms, cellular and with a matrix of collagen and chondroitin sulfate like vertebrate cartilage, but evolved independently; lancelets support their gill bars and oral cirri with cartilage-like collagenous rods; and the chordate notochord is a rod stiffened by turgor in vacuolated cells. The clearest homologies of skeletons are thus within phyla, and the clearest convergences between them.

## 44.3. Locomotion

### 44.3.1. Cilia, Muscle and Body Size

Locomotion follows the Reynolds number ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.3.2)). Animals smaller than about a millimeter, and the larvae of many phyla, swim or glide with cilia; larger animals move with muscles. The switch can be followed within single phyla. Small flatworms glide on cilia over a mucous trail, large polyclads and land planarians crawl by muscular waves; ctenophores use comb rows of fused cilia even at several centimeters, the largest ciliary swimmers known, but large ctenophores also flap their oral lobes; annelid and mollusc larvae swim with ciliary bands, the adults with muscles. Ciliary locomotion survives in large animals mainly where speed does not matter, in the gliding of snails, which combine cilia and muscular pedal waves, and of nemerteans.

The ecdysozoans show what happens when cilia are lost (Section 44.2.1). Even microscopic ecdysozoans, kinorhynchs, loriciferans and tardigrades about 0.1–1 mm long, move with muscles and cuticular structures, and nematodes undulate at Reynolds numbers well below one, where undulation works because the body is long and the wave asymmetric. The absence of cilia also changed the sense organs, which in ecdysozoans are built from modified cilia only inside the cuticle (amphids, sensilla) and never as motile ciliary bands.

### 44.3.2. Muscle Cells

Three kinds of muscle cell are found among invertebrates: smooth muscle, without regular sarcomeres; obliquely striated muscle, with sarcomeres staggered at an angle, so that the bands run helically around the fiber; and cross-striated muscle, with sarcomeres in register.

| Muscle type | Main occurrence | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Smooth | Most phyla in the gut and many body walls; catch muscles of bivalves | Slow, economical; catch muscle holds tension with little energy |
| Obliquely striated | Body wall of nematodes, annelids, many molluscs (squid mantle), chaetognaths, nemerteans | Large changes in length, intermediate speed; suits hydrostatic skeletons |
| Cross-striated | Arthropods, vertebrates, chaetognaths (in part), the swimming muscle of medusae, tentacles of some ctenophores, fast muscles of scallops | Fast, powerful contraction over a limited range of length |

The distribution of cross-striated muscle is a striking example of convergence of a cell type. Steinmetz and colleagues (2012) showed that the striated muscles of medusae lack several characteristic proteins of bilaterian striated muscle, including components of the troponin complex and of the Z-disc, and that the core contractile genes, such as the heavy chain of myosin II, had duplicated into "striated" and "smooth" paralogues before the origin of animals. Striated muscle, made from these ancient parts, therefore evolved independently in cnidarians and bilaterians, and probably several times within the Bilateria. Obliquely striated muscle is typical of animals whose bodies change length greatly, those with hydrostatic skeletons, whereas cross-striated muscle is typical of animals whose muscles act on rigid levers. The muscle type follows the skeleton.

### 44.3.3. Appendages and Their Homology

Paired appendages for locomotion are rare among invertebrates and evolved a few times:

| Appendage | Structure | Phyla | Homology |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parapodia | Unjointed, muscular, with chaetae and aciculae, the coelom extending inside | Polychaete annelids | Homologous within annelids; not homologous with arthropod legs ([7.17.15](/7.17.15#sec-15.1.2)) |
| Lobopods | Unjointed, hydrostatic, with terminal claws | Onychophorans, tardigrades, Cambrian lobopodians | Ancestral panarthropod legs ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.2.1)) |
| Arthropodia | Jointed, with an exoskeleton and intrinsic muscles | Arthropods | Derived from lobopods; the **biramous appendage** is ancestral for the crown group |
| Tube feet | Hydraulic, with an ampulla, in rows along the ambulacra | Echinoderms | Part of the water vascular system ([7.17.42](/7.17.42#sec-42.3)) |
| Fins | Median fins of lancelets and chaetognaths; paired fins of vertebrates | Chordates, chaetognaths | Independent in the two phyla |

The muscular foot of molluscs, the introvert of scalidophorans and the jet of cephalopods and medusae are locomotory organs of other kinds, built on hydrostatic principles. Remarkably, all outgrowths of the body wall, whether parapodia, arthropod legs, tube feet, the ampullae of ascidians or vertebrate limbs, express the homeobox gene *Distal-less* (*Dlx*) at their tips during development (Panganiban and colleagues 1997). The gene marks proximodistal outgrowth in general, and its expression in appendages that are clearly not homologous is another case of deep homology: it shows that the capacity to grow out a body-wall appendage is ancient, not that the bilaterian ancestor had legs.

## 44.4. Feeding and Digestion

### 44.4.1. From Intracellular Digestion to a Through Gut

Three stages in the evolution of digestion can be read from the tree, although the order in which they appear in textbooks is not the order of descent in every lineage:

1. *Intracellular digestion only*, by cells that phagocytose particles: the choanocytes and archaeocytes of sponges ([7.17.7](/7.17.7#sec-7.3.3)) and the lower epithelium of placozoans, which digests algae under the body by secreting enzymes and absorbing the products, a kind of external digestion.
2. *A gut with a single opening*, the **gastrovascular cavity** (Definition 4.4.2) of cnidarians and the blind gut of flatworms, xenacoelomorphs and some smaller groups, in which digestion begins extracellularly and is completed inside gut cells. In acoels the gut is replaced by a digestive syncytium without a lumen ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.5.1)).
3. *A through gut* with mouth and anus, the **complete digestive tract** of nearly all nephrozoans, through which food passes in one direction while digestion and absorption are divided among successive regions.

The origin of the anus is one of the oldest problems of invertebrate morphology. In the classical view, the blastopore became the mouth of protostomes, and the anus formed later as a new opening, whereas in deuterostomes the blastopore became the anus. In the amphistomy hypothesis the blastopore of the ancestor was slit-like and closed in the middle, its ends giving rise to mouth and anus at once, as in some annelids and onychophorans. Current data point to a third view: the mouth is homologous throughout the Bilateria, because the same genes (*foxA*, *goosecoid*, *brachyury*) mark the oral region in cnidarians, acoels and nephrozoans, whereas the anus, marked by different genes and formed in different ways, may have arisen more than once (Hejnol and Martindale 2008). Ctenophores have anal pores, which in *Mnemiopsis* form only when needed ([7.17.8](/7.17.8#sec-8.1.4)), and they are widely regarded as independent of the bilaterian anus. If xenacoelomorphs are the sister group of Nephrozoa, a through gut evolved at the base of Nephrozoa; if they are deuterostomes, they lost the anus.

The through gut is not irreversible. It has been lost or reduced in many lineages:

| Change | Lineages | Associated way of life |
|---|---|---|
| Gut lost entirely | Tapeworms, acanthocephalans, adult nematomorphs, male rotifers in many species, adult siboglinid annelids (replaced by a trophosome of symbiotic bacteria), *Osedax* | Absorption from a host, or symbiotic chemosynthesis |
| Anus lost | Brittle stars, articulate brachiopods, some flatworms | A blind gut in which indigestible matter is expelled through the mouth |
| U-shaped gut, anus near the mouth | Phoronids, bryozoans, brachiopods, entoprocts, sipunculans, pterobranchs, ascidians | Life in a tube, burrow or attached, with only one end exposed |

The U-shaped gut is a clear convergence imposed by a way of life: an animal that lives in a tube or is fixed to the substrate with only its anterior end free must void its feces near its mouth, and the lophophorates, entoprocts, sipunculans, pterobranchs and tunicates achieved this independently, the lophophorates with the anus outside the tentacle crown, the entoprocts with the anus inside it ([7.17.16](/7.17.16#sec-16.2)).

### 44.4.2. Regions of the Gut

A through gut is built from the same three parts in most phyla. The foregut (stomodeum) forms by invagination of the ectoderm at the mouth, the hindgut (proctodeum) by invagination at the anus, and the midgut from the endoderm. In ecdysozoans the ectodermal foregut and hindgut are lined by cuticle and molted, so that digestion and absorption are confined to the midgut ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.5.2)). The functional regions that develop from these parts are strikingly convergent among phyla with similar diets:

| Function | Structure (phylum) | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| Grinding | Gizzard of earthworms; proventriculus of insects; gastric mill of decapods; mastax of rotifers; Aristotle's lantern of sea urchins; gizzard of birds | Foregut muscle and cuticle, or calcite teeth (sea urchins), independently in each |
| Storage | Crop of earthworms, insects, leeches (with caeca), birds | Foregut |
| Digestive gland | Digestive gland of molluscs; hepatopancreas of crustaceans; pyloric caeca of sea stars; gastric caeca of insects; hepatic caecum of lancelets | Midgut diverticula; the lancelet caecum may be homologous to the vertebrate liver |
| Sorting of particles | Stomach with ciliated sorting fields, crystalline style and style sac (bivalves, many gastropods); pyloric filter of decapods | Midgut (molluscs), foregut (crustaceans) |
| Protection of the epithelium | Peritrophic membrane of arthropods; mucus-bound food strings of filter feeders | Midgut secretion |
| Water and ion recovery | Rectum with rectal pads of insects; hindgut of millipedes | Hindgut |

The repeated invention of a grinding chamber, a storage crop and a digestive gland in phyla whose guts are not homologous region by region is the digestive counterpart of the convergence of eyes. In the bivalve stomach, the crystalline style is a rotating rod of enzymes, turned by cilia of the style sac, which winds in the food string and releases amylase as it wears against a gastric shield; it is the most elaborate solution to the problem of processing a continuous stream of small particles ([7.17.14](/7.17.14#sec-14.1.3)).

### 44.4.3. Feeding Structures: Jaws, Radulae and Filters

Jaws evolved many times, from many different starting points:

| Jaws | Material and origin | Phyla |
|---|---|---|
| Cuticular jaws in a muscular pharynx (trophi, jaws of gnathostomulids) | Cuticle secreted by the pharyngeal epithelium | Gnathifera: rotifers, gnathostomulids, micrognathozoans; the grasping spines of chaetognaths are probably homologous ([7.17.12](/7.17.12#sec-12.5)) |
| Eversible jaws of the pharynx | Sclerotized protein with metals (zinc, copper) | Errant polychaetes (nereids, eunicids, glycerids) |
| Beak | Chitin and protein, hardened with a gradient of tanning | Cephalopods |
| Radula | Chitinous teeth on a membrane, moved over an odontophore | Molluscs except bivalves ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.1.3)) |
| Mandibles and other mouthparts | Modified appendages | Arthropods |
| Aristotle's lantern | Calcite stereom teeth and pyramids | Sea urchins |
| Vertebrate jaws | Cartilage and bone of the first pharyngeal arch | Gnathostomes ([7.17.46](/7.17.46)) |

None of these is homologous to another except within the Gnathifera. The arthropod case is the most distinctive: arthropod jaws are legs, appendages of head segments turned to feeding, so that the evolution of the feeding apparatus in arthropods is the evolution of a series of homologous limbs ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.6)).

**Filter feeding** evolved still more often, and its mechanisms are listed in [7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.4.3). Two points matter for comparative anatomy. First, ciliary filter feeding comes in two types that differ in ciliary architecture: downstream collecting by opposed bands of compound cilia (trochophores, rotifers, entoprocts, many molluscs and annelids) and upstream collecting by single cilia (lophophorates, echinoderm and hemichordate larvae, pterobranchs). These are among the few feeding characters that are informative about phylogeny ([7.17.16](/7.17.16#sec-16.2)). Second, the pharyngeal filter of deuterostomes, slits in the pharynx with a mucus net secreted by an endostyle or a homologous gland, is the one filter shared by several phyla: enteropneusts, lancelets and tunicates. The **pharyngeal slit** is homologous among them, and its primary function was feeding and moving water, not gas exchange ([7.17.41](/7.17.41#sec-41.5.1)).

## 44.5. Gas Exchange

### 44.5.1. Water and Air

Every respiratory organ is a thin, moist, well-ventilated surface, and its form depends first on the medium. The physical differences between water and air are summarized in the figure; their physiological consequences are treated in [7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.2.3).

![Water against air as a respiratory medium: ratios of physical properties, water divided by air, at 20 °C (fresh water in equilibrium with air; values from the CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics). Blue bars to the left of 1: water holds about 31 times less oxygen, oxygen diffuses in it about 10,000 times more slowly, and Krogh's diffusion constant, the product of the two that governs diffusion across a layer of the medium, is about 310,000 times smaller. Red bars to the right: water is about 830 times denser and 55 times more viscous, so moving it costs far more, and its heat capacity per volume is about 3,500 times larger, so a surface ventilated by water also loses body heat to it](./plots/water-air.py)

**Formula 44.5.1 (Diffusive Uptake through a Layer of Medium)** By Fick's law in the form used in respiratory physiology, the rate at which oxygen crosses a layer of thickness $x$ and area $A$ under a partial pressure difference $\Delta p$ is

$$
\dot n = K A \frac{\Delta p}{x}, \qquad K = D\beta,
\tag{44.2}
$$
where $D$ is the diffusion coefficient and $\beta$ the capacitance (the change of concentration per unit partial pressure) of oxygen in the medium; $K$ is Krogh's diffusion constant.

:::

Because $K$ in water is about $3\times10^5$ times smaller than in air, an unstirred layer of water a few micrometers thick over a gill is as great a barrier as an air layer of a meter. Gills must therefore be ventilated so that fresh water flows right over their surface, their lamellae must be closely spaced, and they work best with **countercurrent exchange**, which keeps a partial pressure gradient along the whole surface. In air the medium is not limiting; the problem becomes water loss, because a respiratory surface moist enough for gases to dissolve also evaporates water.

**Example 44.5.1 (The Cost of Moving the Medium)** To obtain 1 mmol of oxygen while extracting half of what the medium contains, an animal must pass $1/(0.5 \times 0.284) \approx 7.0\,\mathrm{L}$ of air-saturated fresh water over its gills, a mass of 7 kg, but only $1/(0.5 \times 8.71) \approx 0.23\,\mathrm{L}$ of air, a mass of 0.28 g. The mass of medium moved differs by a factor of about 25,000, and water is also 55 times more viscous.

:::

The two media thus impose opposite designs. Aquatic gas-exchange organs are evaginations of the body surface, gills hanging into the water, ventilated by cilia, by muscular pumping or by the animal's movement. Aerial ones are invaginations, lungs and tracheae, whose openings can be narrowed or closed to limit evaporation. This geometrical rule holds across all the phyla that breathe both media and is the most reliable generalization about respiratory organs.

### 44.5.2. Gills: Many Origins

Gills evolved in almost every phylum of large aquatic animals, from different parts of the body:

| Gill | Origin | Flow | Phyla | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Secondary gills | Outgrowths of the mantle or the dorsal body wall | Various | Nudibranchs, limpets (pallial gills) | [7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.7.8) |

The table contains only two cases of homology between phyla, the **pharyngeal slit** of deuterostomes and, within arthropods, the limb-borne gills of crustaceans and the book gills of horseshoe crabs, both derived from appendages. The others are independent outgrowths. Tracheal gills are a special case: they were derived within insects, secondarily, from a tracheal system that had evolved on land, and they show that an aquatic respiratory organ can evolve from an aerial one. Whether the abdominal gills of mayfly larvae are serial homologues of the insect wing is part of the debate on the origin of wings ([7.17.25](/7.17.25#sec-25.1.2)).

### 44.5.3. Breathing Air: Lungs and Tracheae

Air breathing evolved in every lineage that colonized land, and the organs of each lineage are its own:

| Lineage | Organ | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| Onychophorans | Tracheae | Tufts of unbranched tubes from many small spiracles over the body; spiracles cannot close |
| Arachnids | **Book lungs**; tracheae | Book lungs once, in the ancestor of scorpions and tetrapulmonates; tracheae several times (sieve and tube tracheae) ([7.17.19](/7.17.19#sec-19.6)) |
| Myriapods | Tracheae | Spiracles in different positions in the classes; dorsal unpaired spiracles in scutigeromorph centipedes |
| Hexapods | Tracheae with closable spiracles | Branched system ending in **tracheoles** ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.4.1)) |
| Woodlice | Pleopodal lungs | Air-filled invaginations of the abdominal limbs |
| Land crabs, coconut crab | Branchiostegal lungs | Vascularized lining of the gill chamber, with reduced gills |
| Land snails and slugs | Lung | Vascularized mantle cavity opening through a pneumostome; evolved separately in pulmonates and in several caenogastropod and neritimorph families |
| Vertebrates | Lungs | Outgrowths of the pharynx ([7.17.64](/7.17.64)) |

The **tracheal system** of the insects is the most radical of these designs. It carries gas in air-filled tubes to within micrometers of every cell, so that the respiratory medium, not the blood, makes the last part of the journey, and the hemolymph is released from transporting oxygen. Tracheae evolved at least five or six times among panarthropods, in onychophorans, several times in arachnids, in myriapods and in hexapods, and the arrangement of their openings differs in each lineage ([7.17.20](/7.17.20#sec-20.2.2)). The reason for the repeated convergence is the diffusion constant of air (Formula 44.5.1): only in air can diffusion along a tube supply tissue millimeters to centimeters away, and the limit it sets is computed in [7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.4.3). Tracheae are also why insects did not need a respiratory pigment and could make do with an open circulation of low pressure.

### 44.5.4. Respiratory Pigments

The oxygen carriers of invertebrates ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.5.2)) have their own evolutionary history, which does not follow the phylogeny of the animals. Globins are present in all domains of life, and hemoglobins were recruited as oxygen carriers in many lineages separately: vertebrates, many annelids, a few molluscs, branchiopod crustaceans, midge larvae, phoronids and nemerteans, often in cells in some and dissolved in others. Hemocyanins evolved twice, from different families of copper proteins: arthropod hemocyanins are hexamers related to the phenoloxidases and to the storage hexamerins of insects, mollusc hemocyanins are huge cylindrical decamers of multi-domain subunits related to tyrosinases. The similarity of their copper-binding sites is convergence on the same chemistry. Hemerythrin, a non-heme iron protein, occurs in sipunculans, priapulids, brachiopods and a few polychaetes. Respiratory pigments thus illustrate homology at the molecular level (globins, copper proteins) combined with independent recruitment at the level of function.

## 44.6. Circulation

### 44.6.1. Why Circulate?

Diffusion alone supplies tissue within about a millimeter of a surface ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.1.2)). Bulk flow carries substances much farther in the same time, and the comparison between the two is expressed by a dimensionless number.

**Formula 44.6.1 (Péclet Number)** For transport over a distance $L$ by a fluid moving at velocity $v$, with diffusion coefficient $D$ of the transported substance, the Péclet number

$$
\mathrm{Pe} = \frac{vL}{D}
\tag{44.3}
$$
is the ratio of the time for diffusion over $L$ (of order $L^2/D$) to the time for flow over $L$ ($L/v$). For $\mathrm{Pe} \gg 1$, convection dominates.

:::

For oxygen in tissue fluid ($D \approx 2\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$) and a slow flow of $1\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$, $\mathrm{Pe} \approx 0.5$ over $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ but about 500 over $1\,\mathrm{mm}$: a circulation makes sense only above a size of tens of micrometers, which is exactly the boundary between the animals that have one and those that do not. Sponges and cnidarians solve the same problem with the water of the environment, pumped through the aquiferous system or the gastrovascular cavity; flatworms with a branched gut and a flat body; and nematodes by the movement of their pressurized body fluid. A circulatory system proper appears in the animals that are both thick and active.

### 44.6.2. The Distribution of Open and Closed Systems

Closed circulatory systems occur in annelids, nemerteans, phoronids, cephalopods and chordates; open systems in arthropods, most molluscs, brachiopods, hemichordates and tunicates; and the remaining phyla have none. The pattern on the tree (Section 44.1.3) is not the one expected from a single origin of either type. Within molluscs the closed system of cephalopods is surely derived from the open system of other molluscs, which shows that a closed system can evolve from an open one; within annelids some small species have lost the vessels altogether.

Comparative histology suggests why the two types interconvert so easily. In all invertebrates the blood-vascular system develops as spaces in the extracellular matrix between the basement membranes of adjacent epithelia, the remnants of the blastocoel, and the vessels have no endothelium of their own: their walls are the coelomic linings, or the gut epithelium, on either side (Ruppert and Carle 1983). Only vertebrates have a continuous endothelium, and cephalopods a partial one. Contractile vessels and hearts are formed from the coelomic epithelium, whose cells are myoepithelial: the pericardial wall of molluscs, the dorsal vessel of annelids and the heart of arthropods are all coelomic muscle. A closed system is thus a set of channels in the matrix between coelomic sacs; where the coelom is reduced and the spaces fuse, the system becomes open. The chapter on the coelom ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.4.3)) showed how closely the two body cavities are linked.

The heart illustrates deep homology again. The *Drosophila* gene *tinman*, required for the formation of the dorsal vessel, has a vertebrate orthologue, *Nkx2-5*, required for the heart (Bodmer 1993), and several further transcription factors are shared. Hearts in the two phyla lie on opposite sides of the body and are built differently, but the specification of contractile mesoderm that pumps body fluid may be inherited from the bilaterian ancestor. The direction of flow supports the dorsoventral inversion of chordates ([7.17.43](/7.17.43#sec-43.5.2)): blood runs forward in the dorsal vessel and backward in the ventral one in annelids, arthropods and hemichordates, the reverse in chordates.

### 44.6.3. Pressure, Vessel Size and the Two Designs

The physical difference between open and closed systems is easiest to see in a model. Blood moves through channels of radius $r$ and length $L$ at a mean velocity $v$, fast enough to renew the fluid near the cells.

**Formula 44.6.2 (Pressure to Drive Flow through a Channel)** For laminar flow of a fluid of viscosity $\mu$ at mean velocity $v$ through a cylindrical channel of radius $r$ and length $L$, the **Hagen–Poiseuille law** gives the pressure drop

$$
\Delta p = \frac{8\mu v L}{r^2}.
\tag{44.4}
$$

:::

At a given velocity the required pressure grows as $1/r^2$. Fine channels such as capillaries need high pressures; wide sinuses need almost none. The benefit of fine channels is on the other side of the exchange: if the channels occupy a fixed fraction of the tissue, their spacing is proportional to their radius, and the diffusion time to the farthest cell grows as $r^2$ (see the figure).

![The trade-off between pressure and diffusion distance (model with illustrative parameters: mean velocity $v = 0.5\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$, channel length $L = 1\,\mathrm{mm}$, viscosity $\mu = 1.5\,\mathrm{mPa\,s}$, channels occupying 10% of the tissue volume, oxygen diffusion coefficient $D = 2\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$). (A) The pressure drop needed to drive the flow, Eq. (44.4), falls as $1/r^2$: some hundreds of pascals in capillaries a few micrometers wide, a fraction of a pascal in sinuses. A closed system buys short diffusion paths with high pressure; an open system works at low pressure but leaves the tissues far from the flow](./plots/vessel-tradeoff.py)

**Example 44.6.1** With the parameters of the figure, capillaries of radius $3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ need $\Delta p = 8 \times 1.5\times10^{-3} \times 5\times10^{-4} \times 10^{-3}/(3\times10^{-6})^2 \approx 670\,\mathrm{Pa}$, about 5 mmHg, over one millimeter, and leave a diffusion distance of about $8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, crossed in about $0.02\,\mathrm{s}$. Sinuses of radius $200\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ need only about $0.15\,\mathrm{Pa}$ but leave a distance of about $0.56\,\mathrm{mm}$, which takes over a minute.

:::

The model explains the distribution of the two designs. A closed system with fine vessels serves large, active animals with high metabolic rates: squid, which also need high pressure for the filtration of their kidneys (Section 44.7), annelids, whose closed system also supplies the gills and the pressurized coelom, and vertebrates. Open systems serve animals in which oxygen reaches the tissues by another route (the tracheae of insects), in which metabolism is low (bivalves, brachiopods), or in which large, active animals compensate with a high cardiac output and a well-developed arterial system that delivers blood close to the tissues before it enters the sinuses, as in decapod crustaceans, whose system is sometimes called partly closed ([7.17.21](/7.17.21#sec-21.1.2)). The open hemolymph also takes on other roles that a closed system could not: the hydraulic skeleton of spider legs and of molting arthropods, and the storage of water in desert insects.

## 44.7. Excretion and Osmoregulation

### 44.7.1. Two Kinds of Tubes: Nephridia and Coelomoducts

The excretory organs of invertebrates are tubes that link the interior of the body to the outside, and two kinds were distinguished by E. S. Goodrich (1945). *Nephridia* begin inside the body, are formed from ectoderm or from cells of the body wall, and serve for excretion: the **protonephridium** (Definition 4.6.1) and the **metanephridium** (Definition 4.6.2). *Coelomoducts* are outgrowths of the coelomic wall to the exterior, of mesodermal origin, primarily gonoducts that carry gametes out of the coelom. In many polychaetes the two fuse into compound organs, and the arthropod segmental organs (antennal, maxillary and coxal glands) are generally interpreted as coelomoducts that took over excretion.

**Definition 44.7.1 (Coelomoduct, Podocyte)** A **coelomoduct** is a duct that grows outward from the lining of a coelomic cavity and opens to the exterior, carrying gametes (a gonoduct) or, in some animals, urine. A **podocyte** is an epithelial cell with interdigitating foot processes, separated by slits bridged by a diaphragm, which rests on a basement membrane over a blood space and forms the filter through which blood pressure drives an ultrafiltrate into a coelomic cavity or a capsule.

The excretory organs of the phyla can then be sorted by where filtration takes place and what drives it:

| Organ | Filtration site | Driving force | Phyla |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protonephridium with flame cells or cyrtocytes | Weir or slit diaphragm of the terminal cell | Negative pressure from ciliary beating | Flatworms, gastrotrichs, rotifers, nemerteans, priapulids, trochophore larvae, some small annelids |
| Metanephridium | Podocytes on blood vessels, into the coelom | Blood pressure | Annelids, sipunculans, molluscs (heart–kidney complex), brachiopods, phoronids |
| Coelomic end sac with podocytes | End sac of the segmental organ | Hemolymph pressure | Crustaceans (antennal and maxillary glands), arachnids (coxal glands), onychophorans (segmental nephridia) |
| Glomerulus and axial complex | Podocytes of the protocoel or the axial sinus | Contraction of the heart vesicle or the dorsal sac | Enteropneusts ([7.17.41](/7.17.41#sec-41.3.3)), echinoderms ([7.17.42](/7.17.42#sec-42.4.2)) |
| Nephridia of lancelets | Basement membrane under cyrtopodocytes, into the coelom | Blood pressure | Lancelets ([7.17.43](/7.17.43#sec-43.2.3)) |
| Nephron | Podocytes of the glomerulus, into Bowman's capsule | Blood pressure | Vertebrates ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)) |
| **Malpighian tubule** | None; active secretion of ions | Ion pumps | Insects, myriapods, arachnids |

The table makes the functional logic clear. Where an animal has a pressurized circulation, the filter is made of podocytes on its blood vessels and the filtrate collects in a coelomic space, from which a metanephridium or a coelomoduct leads it out; this is the arrangement of annelids, molluscs, crustaceans, enteropneusts, lancelets and vertebrates. Where there is no such circulation, the terminal cell of a protonephridium makes its own filtrate by drawing fluid through a filter with its cilia. Nemerteans show the transition: their flame cells press against the walls of blood vessels and filter blood ([7.17.16](/7.17.16#sec-16.1.2)). The fundamental principle common to all, filtration followed by modification of the filtrate along a tube, is described in [7.14.22](/7.14.22#sec-22.8.1).

### 44.7.2. One Origin of Filtration Kidneys?

Whether protonephridia and metanephridia, and the nephrons of vertebrates, share a common origin has been debated since the nineteenth century. The case for homology has grown stronger with molecular data. Developing protonephridia and metanephridia of spiralians and ecdysozoans express the same set of transcription factors (*Six1/2*, *Eya*, *Sall*, *Pou3*) as the vertebrate kidney, and their filtration cells express orthologues of nephrin and kirre, the proteins of the slit diaphragm of vertebrate podocytes (Gąsiorowski and colleagues 2021; [7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.3)). The nephrocytes of *Drosophila*, cells that filter the hemolymph and take up its proteins, have slit diaphragms built from the same two proteins (Weavers and colleagues 2009). On this evidence the filtration organs of all Nephrozoa derive from one ancestral organ, a protonephridium or a podocyte-like filtration cell, whose forms diverged as body cavities and circulations evolved; metanephridia, in which the filtering cells moved from the tip of the nephridium to the wall of the blood vessels, may have arisen several times from protonephridia, and annelid larvae, which have protonephridia before their adult metanephridia form, retain both stages in one life cycle.

Two exceptions support this reading. Xenacoelomorphs, the sister group of Nephrozoa on most analyses, have no nephridia and excrete through the gut, which expresses ammonium transporters and other proteins of the kidney tubule (Andrikou and colleagues 2019); excretion across the gut epithelium is therefore older than nephridia. And echinoderms and tunicates, which have lost nephridia, are marine osmoconformers that need not regulate their volume, as are many small marine animals with reduced protonephridia. Where excretory organs are present, they are best developed in freshwater animals, which must remove the water entering by osmosis: protonephridia are mainly osmoregulatory organs in freshwater flatworms and rotifers, and contractile vacuoles do the same in the cells of freshwater sponges.

### 44.7.3. Secretory Kidneys on Land

**Malpighian tubules** (Definition 4.6.3) work without filtration: their cells pump potassium and other ions into the lumen, and water and wastes follow. They evolved at least three times, from the ectodermal hindgut in insects and, independently, in myriapods, and from the endodermal midgut in arachnids ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.5.2)), and tardigrades have comparable glands at the junction of midgut and hindgut whose homology is uncertain. Secretion suits animals with an open circulation of low pressure, which could not drive a filter, and it suits life on land: the tubules empty into the hindgut, where the rectum reabsorbs water and ions, and nitrogen leaves as nearly dry uric acid or, in arachnids, guanine. The cryptonephric systems of beetle larvae and caterpillars, in which the tubules are bound to the rectum, extract water even from dry feces ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.3.1)).

Several lineages excrete without tubules altogether. Chloragogen tissue in annelids, the fat body and nephrocytes in insects, the coelomocytes of echinoderms and the renal sac of ascidians, which stores urate concretions throughout life (and in molgulid ascidians houses a symbiotic apicomplexan, *Nephromyces*), are storage excretion or processing organs rather than kidneys. The Chinese textbooks group the invertebrate organs into contractile vacuoles, protonephridia, metanephridia and Malpighian tubules; this sequence is a useful summary of designs, but it is not a phylogenetic series, since protonephridia and metanephridia coexist in one phylum and in one life cycle, and Malpighian tubules evolved separately in two arthropod lineages.

| Ganglionated ventral nerve cord | Below the gut, one pair of ganglia per segment | Annelids, arthropods, tardigrades; onychophorans with widely separated cords | Ganglia fuse in many arthropods ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.6)) |
| Lateral cords | Two main cords at the sides | Nemerteans | Brain around the rhynchodaeum |
| Ring and radial nerves | Around the mouth, along the ambulacra | Echinoderms | No brain; ectoneural, hyponeural and entoneural systems ([7.17.42](/7.17.42#sec-42.4.4)) |
| Dorsal hollow nerve cord | Above the notochord, formed by neurulation | Chordates; a hollow collar cord in enteropneusts | Brain vesicle in lancelets, three-part brain in vertebrates |

*Internalization*: in cnidarians, xenacoelomorphs and enteropneusts the nervous system lies in the base of the epidermis, in contact with the basement membrane; in most bilaterians it has sunk below the epidermis into the connective tissue, protected by its own sheath, and in chordates it has been rolled into a tube. *Centralization*: the number of longitudinal cords falls (from the many cords of an orthogon to one pair), the cell bodies concentrate in ganglia, and an anterior brain grows in size. Both trends accompany active, directional locomotion and **cephalization** ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.2.3)). The reverse trend, decentralization, is as common: sessile and slow animals reduce their brains and nerve cords, as do ascidians, which keep only a ganglion as adults, bivalves, which have lost the head, and echinoderms, whose pentaradial body has no front end.

### 44.8.2. Was the First Bilaterian Centralized?

The main question about the evolution of nervous systems is whether the bilaterian ancestor had a centralized nerve cord, inherited by both protostomes and chordates, or a diffuse net from which cords evolved several times. Two lines of evidence pull in opposite directions.

- For a single origin: the ventral cord of the annelid *Platynereis* and the dorsal neural tube of vertebrates are patterned from the midline outward by the same series of transcription factors (*nk2.1/nk2.2*, *nk6*, *pax6*, *msx* and others), in the same order, and give rise to similar neuron types (Denes and colleagues 2007). With the inverted dorsoventral axis of chordates ([7.17.43](/7.17.43#sec-43.5.2)) the two cords would then be homologous, and the urbilaterian would have had a centralized cord.
- For several origins: when the same genes were studied in a broader sample of bilaterians, including many spiralians, xenacoelomorphs and hemichordates, the mediolateral pattern was found in only some of the species with cords, and was absent from others whose cords lie in the same position (Martín-Durán and colleagues 2018). Enteropneusts, close relatives of chordates, have a mostly diffuse basiepidermal net with only a short collar cord. And the phylum at the base of the Bilateria, the Xenacoelomorpha, has a basiepidermal net with anterior condensations of very varied form.

The current view is that nerve cords evolved more than once, from an ancestor with a basiepidermal nervous system that already had regional patterning genes and perhaps an anterior concentration, and that the shared patterning of annelid and vertebrate cords reflects the repeated use of an ancient gene network rather than an ancestral cord (Hejnol and Lowe 2015). The question is not settled, and it is closely tied to the reconstruction of the first bilaterian as a small, simple worm or a larger, segmented one ([7.17.10](/7.17.10#sec-10.2)).

### 44.8.3. Ganglia, Giant Axons and Brains

Within the centralized systems the same functional demands produced convergent structures. Giant axons, fibers of unusually large diameter, conduct faster because conduction velocity in unmyelinated axons grows with the square root of the diameter ([7.14.4](/7.14.4#sec-4.9.2)); they evolved independently in the escape circuits of squid, earthworms and other annelids, crayfish, cockroaches and fishes (the Mauthner cells). Myelin, the other solution, evolved separately in gnathostomes, annelids, copepods and penaeid shrimps ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.2.2)). Learning centers of similar architecture, with many small parallel intrinsic neurons receiving sensory input and output neurons onto which they converge, are the **mushroom bodies** of arthropods and errant polychaetes and the vertical lobe of cephalopods; the first pair may be homologous, the cephalopod lobe is certainly not.

The size of nervous systems varies over seven orders of magnitude:

| Animal | Approximate number of neurons | Organization |
|---|---|---|
| *Caenorhabditis elegans* (hermaphrodite) | 302 | Nerve ring and cords, invariant lineage |
| Hydra polyp | A few thousand | Nerve net |
| Medicinal leech | About 10,000 (32 segmental ganglia of about 400 each, and the head ganglia) | Ventral cord of identifiable neurons |
| Sea hare *Aplysia* | About 20,000 | Paired ganglia with giant identifiable neurons |
| *Drosophila* (adult brain) | About 140,000 | Fused cerebral ganglia, mapped connectome |
| Honey bee worker (brain) | About one million | Large mushroom bodies |
| *Octopus vulgaris* | About 500 million, two thirds in the arms | Brain of fused ganglia around the oesophagus |
| Human (for comparison) | About 86 billion | Neural tube |

The octopus, with a brain larger than that of many fishes and arm ganglia that control movement locally, shows that a large, centralized brain can evolve from a ganglionic mollusc system, as it did from the neural tube of vertebrates. Among invertebrates, large brains evolved only in cephalopods and, at a smaller absolute size, in insects and some other arthropods, all active predators or foragers with complex sense organs.

The Chinese textbooks classify invertebrate nervous systems as net-like, ladder-like and chain-like, and some include the molluscan nervous system among the chain-like systems. In the sense of this chapter, the molluscan system is not a ventral ganglionated cord: it is a tetraneurous system in aculiferans and a system of paired ganglia linked by connectives in conchiferans, with no segmental chain ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.1.6)). The three Chinese categories correspond to the nerve net, the orthogon and the ventral nerve cord of the table; the others are separate architectures.

## 44.9. Eyes and Other Sense Organs

### 44.9.1. Shared Parts of Independent Eyes

Eyes are the most famous example of repeated evolution, and they show clearly why homology must be judged level by level. At the level of molecules, all animal eyes use opsins, which arose early in animal evolution (they are present in ctenophores, placozoans, cnidarians and bilaterians, but not in sponges) and diversified into the c-opsins of **ciliary photoreceptors** and the r-opsins of **rhabdomeric photoreceptors** ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.4.1)). At the level of cell types, both kinds of photoreceptor were present in the bilaterian ancestor, and different lineages built their main eyes from one or the other: vertebrates and cubozoan jellyfish from ciliary cells, arthropods and cephalopods from rhabdomeric ones ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.2)). At the level of development, the transcription factor Pax6 and its partners specify eyes in insects, cephalopods and vertebrates, a case of **deep homology**. At the level of the organ, the eyes themselves evolved many times: Salvini-Plawen and Mayr (1977), counting the structurally distinct photoreceptor organs, estimated between 40 and 65 independent origins.

### 44.9.2. Four Stages of Visual Function

Nilsson (2009, 2013) proposed a functional classification that orders the diversity of eyes by the tasks they can perform.

**Definition 44.9.1 (Classes of Photoreception)** Four classes of photoreceptive function are distinguished: *non-directional photoreception*, the measurement of ambient light intensity by unshielded photoreceptors (circadian and depth control); *directional photoreception*, by photoreceptors shaded on one side by screening pigment, which indicates the direction of light (phototaxis); *low-resolution vision*, by many photoreceptors in a cup or array, each receiving light from a different direction, which resolves large objects and allows orientation to the environment; and *high-resolution vision*, by eyes with focusing optics that resolve small objects, as needed for finding prey, mates and predators at a distance.

The classes form a sequence of increasing information, each requiring the previous one, and the eyes of living animals represent all of them:

| Class | Structure | Examples | Phyla |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-directional | Scattered photoreceptors | Skin photoreceptors of earthworms, sea urchin tube feet, extraocular photoreceptors of many animals | Almost all |
| Directional | Eyespot: one photoreceptor and one pigment cell | Larval eyes of trochophores and of many other larvae, the frontal eye of lancelets | Most bilaterians, cnidarian larvae |
| Low resolution | Pigment cup with many photoreceptors; compound eyes of few ommatidia | Planarian eyes, eyes of limpets, nemerteans, sabellid fan worms, many cubozoan and scyphozoan ocelli, ark clams | Cnidaria, Platyhelminthes, Nemertea, Mollusca, Annelida, Arthropoda, Echinodermata (dispersed photoreceptors of brittle stars) |
| High resolution | Camera eyes with a lens or mirror; compound eyes with many ommatidia | Cephalopods, alciopid polychaetes, heteropod snails, scallops (mirror), jumping spiders, insects and crustaceans, vertebrates | Mollusca, Annelida, Arthropoda, Chordata, Onychophora (small lens eyes); Cnidaria (cubozoan lens eyes, underfocused) |

Image-forming eyes occur in only six of the 30-odd animal phyla, the cnidarians, molluscs, annelids, onychophorans, arthropods and chordates, but these phyla contain about 96% of all animal species (Land and Nilsson 2012). Within the molluscs alone, the ladder from eyespot to camera eye is spread across living species: pigment cups in limpets, pinhole eyes in *Nautilus*, lensed eyes of increasing quality in snails, and the camera eyes of coleoid cephalopods. That all stages are functional on their own is the reason a camera eye could evolve by small steps in a geologically short time ([7.10.32](/7.10.32#sec-32.3.2)).

### 44.9.3. Optical Designs

**Definition 44.9.2 (Pinhole Eye, Camera Eye)** A **pinhole eye** is an eye in which an image is formed on the retina by a small aperture without any refracting structure, each point of the scene illuminating a patch of retina as wide as the aperture. A **camera eye** is a single-chambered eye in which a lens (or a cornea and a lens) focuses an image of the scene on a retina at the back of the chamber.

The optical designs of eyes are fewer than the eyes themselves, and each design evolved several times:

| Design | Image formation | Examples | Independent origins |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pigment cup | No image; direction from shading | Flatworms, nemerteans, limpets, many polychaetes | Very many |
| Pinhole | Small aperture | *Nautilus*; the eyes of abalones approach it | At least once in cephalopods, in some gastropods |
| Camera eye with a spherical graded-index lens | Refraction by a lens whose index falls from center to margin | Cephalopods, fishes, alciopid polychaetes, gastropods (strombids, heteropods), cubozoans | At least four to six times |
| Camera eye with cornea and lens | Refraction mainly at a curved cornea in air | Terrestrial vertebrates, jumping spiders (principal eyes, with a telephoto component), onychophorans, some insect ocelli | Several times on land |
| Concave mirror | Reflection by a multilayer of guanine crystals behind the retina | Scallops (up to about 200 eyes on the mantle edge, with two retinas, one ciliary and one rhabdomeric), the deep-sea ostracod *Gigantocypris* | Several times |
| Apposition compound eye | Each ommatidium sees one direction | Insects, crustaceans, trilobites (with calcite lenses), sabellid fan worms, ark clams | At least three times (arthropods, sabellids, arcid bivalves) |
| Superposition compound eye | Light from many facets focused on one rhabdom, by refraction or reflection | Moths, krill, decapod shrimps and crayfish | Several times within arthropods |

Two lessons emerge. Lens eyes in water converge on a spherical lens with a refractive-index gradient, which corrects spherical aberration and gives a focal length about 2.5 times the lens radius (Matthiessen's ratio); fishes, cephalopods and alciopid polychaetes all reached this solution, because it is the only one that a single lens in water allows ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.3)). And compound eyes evolved in small animals, where their limitation, the trade-off between facet size and diffraction ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.7.2)), matters least: a compound eye with the resolution of a camera eye of the same size would have to be enormous.

The eyes of onychophorans illustrate how detailed comparison can decide homology between phyla. These small lens eyes on the head of velvet worms were long compared with the lateral compound eyes of arthropods. Their innervation from the brain, their development and their opsins instead match the median eyes (ocelli) of arthropods, and they are now regarded as homologous with those (Mayer 2006). Lateral compound eyes are thus an arthropod innovation, and median eyes are the older panarthropod eyes.

### 44.9.4. Why a Lens? The Optics of Pinhole Eyes

The pinhole eye of *Nautilus* is the classic intermediate, and a simple optical model shows both what it achieves and why other lineages improved on it.

**Formula 44.9.1 (Blur in a Pinhole Eye)** In a pinhole eye of focal length (depth) $f$ with an aperture of diameter $A$, a point of the scene illuminates a patch of retina of width about $A$, an angular blur $A/f$; diffraction at the aperture adds a blur of about $\lambda/A$. Combining the two,

$$
\Delta\rho \approx \sqrt{\left(\frac{A}{f}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\lambda}{A}\right)^2},
\tag{44.5}
$$
which is smallest at $A^* = \sqrt{\lambda f}$, where $\Delta\rho_{\min} = \sqrt{2\lambda/f}$. The light received by each photoreceptor is proportional to $(A/f)^2$.

:::

**Example 44.9.1 (*Nautilus*)** The eye of *Nautilus* is about 10 mm deep. For $\lambda = 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, the optimal aperture is $A^* = \sqrt{0.5\times10^{-6} \times 10^{-2}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.07\,\mathrm{mm}$ and the best resolution about $0.01\,\mathrm{rad}$, or $0.6^\circ$. *Nautilus*, which lives at depths of a few hundred meters in dim light, instead opens its pupil between about 0.4 and 2.8 mm (Land 1981). At 1 mm the blur is about $0.1\,\mathrm{rad}$, nearly $6^\circ$, ten times worse than optimal, but the eye receives about 200 times more light. A lens of the same 1 mm aperture would remove the geometric blur and leave only diffraction and the receptor spacing, about $5\,\mu\mathrm{m}/10\,\mathrm{mm}$: a blur of about $0.04^\circ$, over a hundred times sharper at the same brightness.

:::

Why *Nautilus* never acquired a lens in some 400 million years of its lineage's history is a puzzle; its eye is open to seawater, which may prevent the formation of a closed lens vesicle, and its way of life may depend more on smell than on vision. The model shows, however, why most lineages whose eyes reached the size of millimeters did evolve a lens: any refracting body in the aperture shifts the curve of panel B downward, so each step toward a lens improves vision without cost in light.

### 44.9.5. Statocysts and Other Mechanoreceptors

**Definition 44.9.3 (Statocyst)** A **statocyst** is a sense organ of balance consisting of a fluid-filled vesicle lined by mechanosensory ciliated cells, containing a dense body, the statolith (a single secreted concretion) or statoconia (many small particles or sand grains), whose weight deflects the sensory cilia and so signals the direction of gravity.

Statocysts evolved in nearly every phylum of mobile animals: in hydromedusae, the rhopalia of scyphozoans and cubozoans, the apical organ of ctenophores (one statolith on four groups of balancer cilia), acoels and some flatworms, nemerteans, molluscs (at the pedal ganglia), sedentary and burrowing polychaetes, decapod crustaceans (at the base of the antennules, refilled with sand grains after each molt) and mysids (in the tail), and holothurians. The vertebrate inner ear is a comparable organ. The cells that sense the movement of the statolith, ciliated mechanoreceptors, are ancient and probably homologous across animals; the organs are mostly convergent, since their positions, innervation and statoliths differ. The cephalopod statocyst illustrates how far convergence can go: it has maculae that sense linear acceleration and gravity and cristae with cupulae in a system of canals that sense rotation, the functional equivalent of the vertebrate utricle and semicircular canals, built independently in a mollusc.

Arthropods could not use surface cilia as mechanoreceptors, and all their external mechanoreceptors and chemoreceptors are sensilla, cuticular structures innervated by ciliated sensory neurons below the cuticle ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.1)). The **chordotonal organ**, a stretch receptor of ciliated neurons inside the body, takes over the functions of a statocyst and of hearing organs in insects; its development uses the gene *atonal*, whose vertebrate homologue *Atoh1* specifies the hair cells of the inner ear, another case of a conserved cell-type program in organs that are not homologous.

## 44.10. Reproduction, Development and Larvae

### 44.10.1. Gonads and Gonoducts

The reproductive system shows the same history of convergence on a few designs, against a background of shared cell types. Germ cells in many bilaterians are set aside early and carry a conserved set of germ-plasm genes (*vasa*, *nanos*, *piwi*), but whether they are specified by inherited germ plasm or by induction varies within phyla; induction is thought to be ancestral, and germ plasm evolved several times (Extavour and Akam 2003). The gonads are absent in sponges and placozoans, form in the gastrodermis or epidermis in cnidarians and in the parenchyma of flatworms, and in coelomates arise from or beside the coelomic lining. In the gonocoel theory the coelom itself began as the cavity of serially repeated gonads ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.4.5)), and many polychaetes still release gametes into the coelom, from which they leave through metanephridia, coelomoducts or by rupture of the body wall.

The ducts follow the excretory organs (Section 44.7.1). Where gametes leave through nephridia, excretory and reproductive systems share their outlets, as in many polychaetes and in the molluscan heart–kidney–gonad complex, where the gonads of chitons and many gastropods open through or beside the kidneys. The vertebrate urogenital system, in which the archinephric duct of the kidney becomes the male gonoduct, is the most familiar case of the same association ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)).

### 44.10.2. Fertilization and the Structures for It

The ancestral mode of fertilization in animals is the release of sperm and often eggs into the water. The sperm of such spawners, the aquasperm with a round head, a few mitochondria and a simple flagellum, is so similar in sponges, cnidarians, many polychaetes, molluscs, echinoderms and fishes that its form is taken as the ancestral animal sperm. Internal fertilization evolved many times, especially in animals that moved to fresh water or land, and each time with its own structures:

| Structure | Origin | Lineages |
|---|---|---|
| Spermatophores deposited on the ground | Glandular products of the male ducts | Many arachnids, myriapods, collembolans and other apterygote hexapods, salamanders ([7.17.28](/7.17.28#sec-28.2.1)) |
| Intromittent organs from appendages | Modified limbs | Pedipalps of spiders, gonopods of millipedes and decapods, the hectocotylus arm of cephalopods, the secondary genitalia of male dragonflies |
| Intromittent organs from the gonoduct | Eversible penis or aedeagus | Flatworms, gastropods, insects, rotifers, amniotes |
| Hypodermic insemination | Penis or stylet that pierces the body wall | Some flatworms and acoels, leeches, rotifers, bed bugs |
| Sperm storage | Seminal receptacle, spermatheca | Annelids, molluscs, insects, crustaceans |

Copulatory organs derived from legs, mouthparts and arms are the clearest case of convergence: arthropods and cephalopods, which have series of appendages, repeatedly turned one pair into a sperm-transfer device, while worms without appendages evolved penises from their ducts. Hermaphroditism follows ways of life rather than phylogeny: it is common in sessile, slow-moving and parasitic animals (barnacles, land snails, earthworms, flatworms, tunicates), where mates are rare, and it is the ancestral condition in no large clade.

### 44.10.3. Larvae across the Tree

The larval types of the phyla were compared in [7.17.5](/7.17.5#sec-5.4.2). In the comparative perspective of this chapter, three points stand out. First, primary ciliated larvae with an apical organ occur in cnidarians, spiralians and ambulacrarians, and the apical organ expresses a common set of genes in all of them, which suggests an ancient larval sense organ ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#sec-5.4.4)). Second, larvae are repeatedly lost when lineages move to land or fresh water, where planktonic larvae have no food and are washed away: earthworms and leeches, land snails and most freshwater molluscs (freshwater mussels instead have a parasitic glochidium), onychophorans and terrestrial arthropods all develop directly from large eggs, or within a cocoon. Third, the secondary larvae of insects, the caterpillar and the maggot, are not ciliated larvae at all but juveniles whose development was divided by complete metamorphosis ([7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.5)); they evolved on land long after the loss of the primary larvae.

## 44.11. Organ Systems and the Body Plan

### 44.11.1. Linked Characters

Organ systems do not evolve independently of one another. The comparison shows several recurrent syndromes, combinations of characters that appear together in unrelated lineages because they solve the problems of one way of life:

| Way of life | Characters that appear together | Lineages |
|---|---|---|
| Large and active | Closed circulation, gills with countercurrent flow, filtration kidneys with podocytes, a large brain, camera or compound eyes | Cephalopods, vertebrates; in part decapods and errant polychaetes |
| Small, in water or sediment | Body surface for gas exchange, no circulation, protonephridia or none, ciliary locomotion, eyespots or none | Gastrotrichs, rotifers, small flatworms, interstitial annelids |
| Terrestrial | Waterproof cuticle or keratinized skin, air-breathing invaginations (tracheae, lungs), uric acid or guanine, Malpighian tubules or concentrating kidneys, internal fertilization, direct development | Insects, arachnids, myriapods, onychophorans, land snails, amniotes |
| Sessile filter feeder | U-shaped gut, ciliary or mucus filter, reduced head and brain, few sense organs, colonies or budding, hermaphroditism | Bryozoans, entoprocts, pterobranchs, ascidians, sponges; barnacles among arthropods |
| Internal parasite | Gut reduced or lost, absorption through the body surface (tegument), reduced sense organs, very high fecundity, complex life cycles with intermediate hosts | Tapeworms, flukes, acanthocephalans, nematomorphs, rhizocephalan barnacles, pentastomids |

These syndromes explain much of the scatter on the tree. Characters that the grade classification once used to arrange the phyla in a ladder, a coelom, a circulation, a through gut, a centralized brain, often follow from size and way of life, and they are gained and lost together. The terrestrial syndrome in particular arose at least five times in arthropods and onychophorans alone ([7.17.20](/7.17.20#sec-20.2.2)), and once more in snails and in vertebrates, each time with organs of different origin performing the same functions.

The table collects the conclusions of the preceding sections.

The general pattern is consistent. The cell types and gene networks of animals are old and largely shared; the organs built from them are much more often convergent; and the physical constraints of size, medium and optics channel the convergence into a few recurrent designs.

### 44.11.4. Correspondence with the Chinese Textbooks

Chinese textbooks of general zoology present the invertebrates as a ladder of grades and attach an "evolutionary significance" to each new feature. The statements are useful as functional explanations, and most remain true in that sense; what has changed is their reading as a historical sequence.

| Textbook statement | Current interpretation |
|---|---|
| Bilateral symmetry and mesoderm appear first in flatworms, which are the most primitive bilaterians | Bilateral symmetry and mesoderm arose at the base of the Bilateria; the first branch is probably the Xenacoelomorpha, and flatworms are spiralians, probably simplified ([7.17.10](/7.17.10)) |
| The pseudocoel and the complete gut appear in the "pseudocoelomates" | The blastocoelomate condition evolved several times in small animals of Spiralia and Ecdysozoa; the through gut is ancestral for Nephrozoa ([7.17.12](/7.17.12#sec-12.7)) |
| The true coelom, metamerism, the closed circulation and metanephridia appear in annelids, a major step in evolution | All four are present in annelids, but coelom and closed circulation also occur elsewhere and may have arisen several times; segmentation evolved separately in annelids, arthropods and chordates ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.5.2)) |
| Parapodia are primitive appendages, the forerunners of arthropod limbs | Parapodia and arthropod legs are not homologous; arthropod legs derive from panarthropod lobopods ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.2.1)) |
| The nervous system evolves from net-like to ladder-like to chain-like | These are three of several architectures; centralization probably occurred more than once, and the molluscan system is not chain-like (Section 44.8) |
| Excretory organs evolve from contractile vacuoles through protonephridia and metanephridia to Malpighian tubules | Protonephridia and metanephridia are probably variants of one filtration organ; Malpighian tubules are secretory and convergent in insects and arachnids (Section 44.7) |
| The circulation evolves from none (nemerteans have the first blood vessels) to open and closed systems | Open and closed systems interconvert; closed systems arose several times (Section 44.6) |
| Hemerythrin is given a name meaning "earthworm hemoglobin" | Hemerythrin occurs in sipunculans, priapulids, brachiopods and a few polychaetes; earthworms use giant extracellular hemoglobin (erythrocruorin) |

The second column does not overturn the first. The textbooks describe correctly what each feature does for the animal that has it, and the comparative anatomy of this chapter confirms that these functions explain why the same features evolved again and again. The difference is that the features are no longer read as steps of one ladder but as repeated answers, drawn from a shared toolkit of cells and genes, to the problems that size, medium and way of life pose to every animal.

## Questions

1. Using Eq. (44.1), compare a tubular arthropod leg segment of radius 0.5 mm and wall thickness 0.05 mm with a solid rod of the same material and mass. What happens to the advantage if the animal is scaled up tenfold in length while keeping the same proportions?

   **Answer** The ratio $I_{\mathrm{tube}}/I_{\mathrm{rod}} = r/t = 10$: the tube is ten times stiffer in bending. With isometric scaling $r/t$ stays 10, so the shape advantage is unchanged, but the loads grow faster than the strength: weight scales as $L^3$, while the stress the skeleton must resist at a given shape scales as weight divided by area, that is as $L$. A tenfold larger animal would have stresses ten times higher in the same proportions; the wall must thicken disproportionately, which reduces $r/t$ and the advantage, and the soft phase after molting becomes more dangerous. This is why large arthropods are aquatic and why the size advantage of exoskeletons is confined to small animals.

2. Explain from Formula 44.5.1 why aquatic animals have evaginated gills and terrestrial animals invaginated lungs or tracheae, and give one exception to the rule.

   **Answer** In water the diffusion constant of oxygen is about $3\times10^5$ times lower than in air, so the medium itself limits uptake: the surface must be large, thin, close to the flowing water and ventilated constantly, which is best achieved by thin structures that hang into the water. In air the medium is not limiting, but a moist exchange surface loses water by evaporation; placing it inside the body, behind a narrow, closable opening, keeps the air around it saturated and reduces the loss. Exceptions include the tracheal gills of aquatic insect larvae, which are external but contain air-filled tracheae, and the gills of land crabs and woodlice, which persist in reduced form but must be kept moist; the vascularized lungs of these crustaceans follow the rule.

3. A worm 2 mm in diameter has a closed circulation with capillaries of radius $4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. Using Eq. (44.4) with $\mu = 1.5\,\mathrm{mPa\,s}$, $v = 0.5\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$ and $L = 1\,\mathrm{mm}$, estimate the pressure needed. Why could an animal with an open circulation and sinuses of radius 0.1 mm not use metanephridia that depend on podocyte filtration?

   **Answer** $\Delta p = 8 \times 1.5\times10^{-3} \times 5\times10^{-4} \times 10^{-3}/(4\times10^{-6})^2 \approx 375\,\mathrm{Pa}$, about 3 mmHg over one millimeter of capillary, a pressure that contractile vessels can produce. In an open system the hemolymph flows through sinuses with almost no resistance, so the heart cannot build up the pressure difference that podocyte filtration requires across a filter as fine as a slit diaphragm. Arthropods filter only in the end sacs of their segmental organs, which are supplied directly by arteries, and the insects rely instead on Malpighian tubules, which secrete their fluid and need no pressure.

4. The eye of a small snail is 0.3 mm deep and has no lens. What is the best resolution it can achieve as a pinhole eye, and what aperture does that require? Why would a lens help less in this eye than in the eye of *Nautilus*?

   **Answer** With $f = 0.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\lambda = 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, the optimal aperture is $A^* = \sqrt{\lambda f} = \sqrt{0.5\times10^{-6} \times 3\times10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 12\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and the best resolution is $\sqrt{2\lambda/f} \approx 0.058\,\mathrm{rad} \approx 3.3^\circ$. In a lens eye of the same size the diffraction limit $\lambda/A$ remains, and with an aperture of, say, 0.1 mm it is about $0.3^\circ$; but the receptors, a few micrometers wide, subtend about $1^\circ$ at a focal length of 0.3 mm, so the receptor mosaic limits the image. The gain from a lens grows with eye size, which is why only eyes of the order of millimeters and larger profit fully from one, and why small animals often have compound eyes or simple cups.

5. Name three pairs of organs in different phyla that are convergent at the level of the organ but share a homologous component at a lower level, and state the shared component.

   **Answer** The camera eyes of cephalopods and vertebrates (convergent organs) share opsins and the Pax6 developmental program; the hearts of insects and vertebrates share the transcription factor *tinman*/Nkx2-5; the legs of arthropods, the parapodia of annelids and the limbs of vertebrates share *Distal-less* expression at their tips. Other valid answers include the statocysts of molluscs and the vertebrate inner ear (ciliated mechanoreceptors), the chordotonal organs of insects and vertebrate hair cells (*atonal*/*Atoh1*), and the nephrocytes of insects and vertebrate podocytes (nephrin and kirre in the slit diaphragm).

6. (Open question) Choose one of the syndromes of Section 44.11.1 and explain, organ system by organ system, how it arose in two unrelated lineages. Which of its characters would you expect to be easiest, and which hardest, to reverse?

   **Answer** Taking the terrestrial syndrome in insects and land snails: the body covering became a waxy cuticle in insects and a mucus-covered skin with a shell in snails; the respiratory organ became tracheae in insects and a vascularized mantle cavity in snails; nitrogen excretion shifted to uric acid in both, by Malpighian tubules in insects and by the kidney in snails; fertilization became internal in both, with copulation, and planktonic larvae were lost. Characters that depend on a single physiological switch, such as the nitrogenous waste, are relatively easy to reverse (aquatic insects and freshwater snails excrete ammonia), and air-breathing organs can be returned to water as plastrons or tracheal gills in insects and as secondary gills in some snails. Complex developmental changes such as the loss of the primary larva are hardest to reverse: no terrestrial lineage that returned to water has re-evolved a trochophore or a nauplius.
