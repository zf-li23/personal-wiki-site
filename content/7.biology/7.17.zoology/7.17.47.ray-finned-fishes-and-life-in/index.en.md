More than half of all living vertebrate species are ray-finned fishes: about 35 000 species, roughly as many as all tetrapods together, living in every aquatic habitat from mountain torrents to the deep-sea floor at 8000 m, from Antarctic waters at −1.9 °C to hot springs at 40 °C, and ranging in size from gobies that mature at 8 mm to ocean sunfishes of two tonnes. Almost all of them, more than 99 %, are teleosts. Their success rests on a body plan that is efficient in water, a dense, viscous medium that supports the body but resists motion and holds little oxygen, and on an unusual capacity for evolutionary diversification in feeding, reproduction and form.

This chapter first characterizes the bony fishes, the Osteichthyes, which include the ray-finned fishes and the lobe-finned fishes with their descendants, the tetrapods (§47.1). It then describes the ray-finned fishes, from the surviving early lineages (bichirs, sturgeons, gars, bowfins; §47.2) to the teleosts and their diversity, with the Chinese freshwater fishes and the carp as the representative animal (§47.3). Section 47.4 treats the physical problems of life in water (buoyancy, swimming, sensing, breathing and osmoregulation) and how fishes solve them, with quantitative models; §47.5 their reproduction and life histories. The chapter ends with a comparison with the traditional classification (§47.6). The lobe-finned fishes follow in [7.17.48](/7.17.48); the physiology of fish gills, swim bladders and osmoregulation is in [7.14.17](/7.14.17), [7.14.19](/7.14.19) and [7.14.21](/7.14.21); the skulls, scales, fins and organ systems are compared with those of other vertebrates in [7.17.57](/7.17.57)–[7.17.68](/7.17.68).

## 47.1. The Bony Fishes

### 47.1.1. Characters of Osteichthyans

**Definition 47.1.1 (Osteichthyes)** The **Osteichthyes** (osteichthyans, bony fishes) are the clade of jawed vertebrates comprising the Actinopterygii and the Sarcopterygii (including the tetrapods). Their internal skeleton is at least partly ossified by endochondral bone; their fins are supported by **lepidotrichia**, segmented and often branched dermal fin rays; they have air-filled sacs that develop from the gut (lungs or a swim bladder); the gill chamber is covered on each side by a bony **operculum** with branchiostegal rays below it; and the upper and lower jaws carry marginal dermal bones (premaxilla, maxilla and dentary) bearing teeth, with a pattern of dermal skull-roof bones that can be traced through all later bony vertebrates.

The name means "bony fishes", but in the phylogenetic sense the Osteichthyes include the tetrapods, which are lobe-finned fishes that came onto land ([7.17.48](/7.17.48)); in this sense humans are osteichthyans. The term "bony fishes" in the ordinary sense, the osteichthyans other than tetrapods, refers to a **paraphyletic group**.

Several osteichthyan characters have changed their meaning with new fossils. The marginal jaw bones were once considered an osteichthyan novelty, but the Silurian placoderms *Entelognathus* and *Qilinyu* have them ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.3.2)). Bone itself is older. The truly new features of the osteichthyans are endochondral bone in the internal skeleton (**cartilage bone**), the lepidotrichia, the operculum and the gas-filled sacs of the gut.

The gas-filled sacs are central to the biology of bony fishes. All living osteichthyans that branch early in both lineages (bichirs among actinopterygians, lungfishes among sarcopterygians) have paired lungs that open on the ventral side of the pharynx, and they breathe air. The lungs are therefore an osteichthyan character, evolved in fishes living in warm, shallow waters that were often poor in oxygen, and the swim bladder of teleosts is a modified lung that has moved to a dorsal position and lost its respiratory function in most species. Darwin had argued the reverse, that lungs evolved from a swim bladder; the phylogeny shows that the lung came first.

### 47.1.2. Early Bony Fishes and the Split between Rays and Lobes

The earliest osteichthyans are known from the late Silurian and Early Devonian of Yunnan, China. *Guiyu oneiros* from the Kuanti Formation of Qujing, about 425 million years old, is the oldest articulated bony fish (Zhu et al. 2009): a fish about 33 cm long with rhombic scales, a pectoral fin with a fleshy lobe and spines in front of its median fins, combining characters of actinopterygians and sarcopterygians. *Psarolepis* and *Achoania* from the Early Devonian of Yunnan, and *Meemannia*, whose braincase has an actinopterygian-like structure combined with sarcopterygian features, show that the split between the two lineages happened by about 420 million years ago and that the earliest representatives of both resembled each other more than their living descendants do.

The two lineages differ above all in their fins:

| | Actinopterygii | Sarcopterygii |
|---|---|---|
| Paired fins | Fin rays supported directly by a row of short radials at the base; muscles mostly inside the body wall | A fleshy lobe with a single basal bone (the future humerus or femur) and a branching series of bones, with muscles extending into the fin |
| Scales | Ganoid scales with a shiny surface of ganoine (enamel) in early forms; thin bony scales without ganoine in teleosts | Cosmoid scales with a layer of cosmine (dentine with pore canals) in early forms |
| Teeth | Capped with acrodin, an enameloid | Capped with true enamel |
| Brain | Forebrain everted (its roof folded outward) | Forebrain evaginated (paired hemispheres as in tetrapods) |
| Nostrils | Paired incurrent and excurrent openings | Excurrent opening moved to the margin of the mouth or into the palate (the choana in tetrapodomorphs) |
| Diversity | About 35 000 living species | 8 living species of fishes (coelacanths and lungfishes) and about 35 000 tetrapods |

The types of fish scales and their tissues are compared in [7.17.57](/7.17.57): **ganoid scales**, **cosmoid scales**, and the **cycloid scales** and **ctenoid scales** of teleosts.

## 47.2. Ray-Finned Fishes and Their Early Lineages

**Definition 47.2.1 (Actinopterygii)** The **Actinopterygii** (actinopterygians, ray-finned fishes) are the clade of osteichthyans whose paired fins are supported by lepidotrichia arising from a row of radials at the base of the fin, without a fleshy lobe; ancestrally they have a single dorsal fin, ganoid scales with ganoine, teeth capped with acrodin and an everted forebrain.

The ray-finned fishes appear in the Late Silurian and became abundant in the Devonian (*Cheirolepis*). The Palaeozoic forms, traditionally called "palaeoniscoids", were fusiform fishes with large eyes, heavy ganoid scales, a heterocercal tail and jaws suspended at a vertical angle with a large gape. During the Mesozoic, several lineages independently evolved features that culminated in the teleosts: more flexible jaws, a reduced and lighter scale cover, a more symmetrical tail. The living ray-finned fishes form five lineages that branched successively.

### 47.2.1. Bichirs

The bichirs and the reedfish (Polypteriformes; 12 species of *Polypterus* and *Erpetoichthys*) live in the fresh waters of tropical Africa, in swamps and river margins often poor in oxygen. They are covered with thick ganoid scales, have a row of five to eighteen separate dorsal finlets each with a spine in front, and fleshy-based pectoral fins that are used as props on the bottom. They have paired ventral lungs and must breathe air; they take air through the mouth and also, by recoil of the ganoid scale armour, through the spiracles on top of the head (Graham et al. 2014). Their larvae have a pair of external gills, as amphibian larvae do. When juvenile *Polypterus* were reared on land for eight months, they walked more effectively and changed the shape of their pectoral girdle in the direction of early tetrapods (Standen et al. 2014), an example of developmental plasticity in a trait that evolved in the fish–tetrapod transition.

### 47.2.2. Sturgeons and Paddlefishes

The Acipenseriformes comprise about 25 species of sturgeons (Acipenseridae) and the paddlefishes (Polyodontidae), large fishes of the Northern Hemisphere that spawn in fresh water. Their internal skeleton is largely cartilaginous, but this is a secondary reduction: their Jurassic ancestors had ossified skeletons. They keep a persistent notochord without centra, a heterocercal tail, a spiral valve in the intestine, a spiracle and a conus arteriosus. Sturgeons have five longitudinal rows of large bony scutes on the body, a long snout with four barbels in front of a ventral, toothless mouth that is protruded like a tube to suck up invertebrates and small fishes from the bottom. They are long-lived and mature late (some species after 15–20 years), and they produce the eggs sold as caviar; overfishing for caviar, dams that block their spawning migrations and pollution have made them the most threatened group of animals assessed by the IUCN: all 26 species are threatened, two-thirds of them critically endangered.

*The Chinese sturgeon.* *Acipenser sinensis* is a large anadromous sturgeon up to 4–5 m long and more than 500 kg, which lives in the coastal waters of the East and South China Seas and enters the Yangtze River to spawn. Adults ascend the river in summer and autumn, stay in the river for more than a year without feeding while their gonads mature, and spawn in autumn of the following year on gravel beds. Before 1981 the spawning grounds were in the upper Yangtze and the lower Jinsha River, more than 2500 km from the sea. The Gezhouba Dam at Yichang, closed in 1981, cut off this route; the sturgeons then spawned on a single site of a few kilometres below the dam. After the Three Gorges Dam began operating in 2003, which lowered the autumn water temperature at that site, natural spawning became irregular and was not detected in several years after 2013. The species is critically endangered. It has been bred artificially since the 1980s and through a complete captive life cycle since 2009, and millions of juveniles have been released into the Yangtze. Together with the ten-year fishing ban on the Yangtze that began in 2021, these measures are the main hope for the wild population. The Yangtze sturgeon *A. dabryanus*, a smaller freshwater species, is extinct in the wild and survives in captivity, and the kaluga *Huso dauricus* and the Amur sturgeon *A. schrenckii* of the Heilongjiang (Amur) River are critically endangered.

*The Chinese paddlefish.* *Psephurus gladius*, a giant endemic of the Yangtze that reached 7 m, had a long, sword-like rostrum and was a predator of fishes, unlike the filter-feeding American paddlefish. Its decline followed overfishing in the 1970s and the Gezhouba Dam, which separated adults from their spawning grounds upstream. No young fish have been seen since 1995, the last adult was seen in 2003, and a study of its history concluded that it went extinct between 2005 and 2010 (Zhang et al. 2020); the IUCN declared it extinct in 2022. Its loss is the first documented extinction of one of the great freshwater fishes of the world in recent times. The surviving American paddlefish *Polyodon spathula* of the Mississippi feeds on zooplankton with its gill rakers and uses its paddle-shaped rostrum, covered with tens of thousands of electroreceptive ampullae, to detect plankton.

### 47.2.3. Gars and Bowfins

The Holostei comprise the gars (Lepisosteiformes; seven species of *Lepisosteus* and *Atractosteus* in North and Central America and Cuba) and the bowfins (Amiiformes; *Amia calva* and *A. ocellicauda*, distinguished in 2022, in eastern North America). Gars are elongated predators with long toothed jaws, heavy rhombic ganoid scales and a vascularized swim bladder used for air breathing; the alligator gar *Atractosteus spatula* reaches 3 m. Bowfins have thinner scales, a long dorsal fin and a lung-like gas bladder. Both live in warm, still waters and can survive low oxygen. Morphological studies often treated holosteans as a paraphyletic grade leading to teleosts, but molecular data support them as a clade, the sister group of teleosts, and their slowly evolving genomes (the spotted gar *Lepisosteus oculatus*) are used as a reference for interpreting the duplicated genomes of teleosts.

Gars are sold in China as aquarium fishes. Released or escaped alligator gars have been found in rivers, lakes and park ponds; in 2022 the draining of a park lake in Henan to remove a single alligator gar attracted national attention, and the species is now listed among China's invasive alien species.

## 47.3. Teleosts

### 47.3.1. Characters of Teleosts

**Definition 47.3.1 (Teleostei)** The **Teleostei** (teleosts) are the clade of ray-finned fishes characterized by a mobile premaxilla, a homocercal caudal fin whose symmetrical lobes are supported by expanded haemal spines (hypurals) and modified vertebrae with uroneural bones, thin cycloid or ctenoid scales without ganoine, pharyngeal tooth plates of the gill arches used as a second set of jaws, and a third whole-genome duplication (the teleost-specific duplication) in their common ancestor.

Teleosts appeared in the Triassic and radiated in the Cretaceous and Cenozoic; today there are about 33 000 species, more than 99 % of ray-finned fishes. Four complexes of characters account for much of their success.

1. *The tail.* In the *homocercal tail* the vertebral column ends in a few upturned vertebrae with expanded hypural bones that support a symmetrical caudal fin. The fin rays can be spread or folded and moved independently, so the tail can change its area and stiffness.
2. *The jaws.* The premaxilla separated from the braincase and became the main tooth-bearing bone of the upper jaw, with the maxilla behind it acting as a lever; in many groups the upper jaw can be protruded (§47.3.2). The pharyngeal jaws (upper and lower pharyngeal tooth plates on the gill arches) process food separately from the oral jaws.
3. *The skeleton and scales.* The heavy ganoid scales were replaced by thin, flexible scales of acellular bone and collagen, and the skeleton became light and ossified.
4. *The swim bladder.* The lung of the ancestor became a hydrostatic organ with a gas gland that can secrete gas against high pressures (§47.4.2).

The teleost genome duplication (3R) occurred about 320 million years ago, after the split from holosteans and before the radiation of crown teleosts. About 15–20 % of the duplicated genes were retained in both copies, and some of them diversified into new functions: the colour patterns and pigment cells, parts of the visual system, and the electric organs of electric fishes ([7.10.18](/7.10.18#sec-18.2.1)). Whether the duplication caused the diversity of teleosts is debated, because the great radiations of teleost families came 150–200 million years later; as with the vertebrate 2R ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1.5)), the duplication expanded the genetic toolkit rather than triggering diversification directly. Two teleosts, the zebrafish *Danio rerio* and the medaka *Oryzias latipes*, are major **model organisms** of developmental biology and genetics.

### 47.3.2. Suction Feeding and Jaw Protrusion

Most fishes feed by suction: they expand the mouth cavity rapidly, so that the pressure inside falls below that of the surrounding water and a jet of water carrying the prey flows into the mouth. Water is about 800 times denser than air, and a prey item moves with the water around it; a fish that simply lunges forward with an open mouth pushes a bow wave that pushes the prey away. Suction overcomes this. In a largemouth bass the pressure in the mouth falls by several tens of kilopascals within 20–40 ms of the start of the strike.

Protrusion of the upper jaw improves suction in three ways. It moves the mouth opening toward the prey quickly, by up to a quarter of the head length, without the whole body having to accelerate; it turns the mouth into a round, tube-like opening that concentrates the flow in front; and it closes the gaps at the sides of the mouth, so that water enters from the front. In spiny-finned fishes the premaxilla has a long ascending process that slides in a groove on the snout; when the lower jaw drops, a ligament pulls the lower end of the maxilla forward, and the maxilla rotates and pushes the premaxilla forward.

**Example 47.3.1 (What Jaw Protrusion Gains)** A fish 20 cm long protrudes its upper jaw by 1 cm in 15 ms, at an average speed of about $0.7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, while the body moves forward at $0.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The mouth thus approaches the prey at $1.2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, more than twice the speed of the body. Because the flow speed produced by suction falls off steeply with distance from the mouth (roughly with the cube of the distance for a point sink, since the flow through a sphere of radius $r$ around the mouth is constant: $u \propto Q/r^2$, and its effect on a small particle decreases further with distance), bringing the mouth 1 cm closer to a prey 2 cm away, that is halving the distance, increases the suction velocity acting on the prey about fourfold. Protrusion is therefore one of the most effective ways of increasing feeding performance, and it evolved independently several times in teleosts (in cyprinids, in the acanthomorphs and in other groups), each time with a different mechanism.

:::

The pharyngeal jaws add a second processing system. In cichlids the lower pharyngeal bones are fused, and the upper pharyngeal jaws articulate with the braincase and are moved by their own muscles; the oral jaws capture food while the pharyngeal jaws crush, grind or shred it. The decoupling of capture and processing is thought to have contributed to the extraordinary diversity of cichlid feeding modes in the African lakes ([7.10.12](/7.10.12)). In carps, which have no teeth in the oral jaws, strong pharyngeal teeth crush food against a horny pad on the base of the skull; their shape distinguishes the Chinese carps (§47.3.5).

### 47.3.3. The Diversity of Teleosts

**Definition 47.3.2 (Weberian Apparatus)** The **Weberian apparatus** is the chain of small bones, derived from parts of the first four vertebrae, that connects the anterior wall of the swim bladder to the perilymphatic spaces of the inner ear in ostariophysan fishes; from back to front the tripus, intercalarium, scaphium and claustrum transmit the vibrations of the gas-filled bladder, which acts as a pressure-to-displacement transducer, to the fluid of the ear.

Most fishes hear by the movement of their otoliths relative to the hair cells of the inner ear, which detects particle motion in water at frequencies below about 500–1000 Hz; the pressure component of sound is lost because the fish body has nearly the density of water. A gas bladder converts sound pressure into motion, but only if it is coupled to the ear. In ostariophysans the Weberian apparatus provides this coupling and extends hearing up to 3–5 kHz with much lower thresholds; goldfish and catfishes are standard animals for experiments on fish hearing ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.2)). The apparatus may have contributed to the success of ostariophysans in fresh water, where turbid water favours acoustic communication and detection. Other teleosts evolved different couplings independently: extensions of the swim bladder that contact the ear in herrings, cods and squirrelfishes, analogous to the Weberian apparatus.

Ostariophysans share a second characteristic, the fright reaction (*Schreckreaktion*) discovered by Karl von Frisch: injured skin releases an alarm substance from specialized club cells, and other fish of the shoal respond with flight or freezing. The chemical, at least in part, is a chondroitin fragment.

*Eels.* The freshwater eels (*Anguilla*) are catadromous: they grow in rivers and lakes and migrate to the sea to spawn. The Japanese eel *Anguilla japonica*, which lives in the rivers of China, Korea and Japan, spawns in the open Pacific west of the Mariana Islands, more than 2000 km from the coast; eggs were collected there for the first time in 2009 (Tsukamoto et al. 2011). The leptocephalus larvae drift back with the North Equatorial and Kuroshio currents for several months, metamorphose into transparent glass eels, enter the estuaries and become pigmented elvers. Adults return to the sea after 5–15 years and die after spawning. Eel farming in East Asia depends entirely on wild glass eels, since captive breeding is not yet economical, and catches of glass eels have fallen to a fraction of their levels in the 1960s; the species is endangered.

### 47.3.4. The Representative Animal: the Carp

The Chinese textbooks describe the common carp *Cyprinus carpio* as the representative fish. Its body is typical of a generalized teleost.

- *External features.* A laterally compressed, spindle-shaped body divided into head, trunk and tail; a terminal mouth with two pairs of barbels; paired nostrils, each with an incurrent and an excurrent opening, leading to blind olfactory sacs; eyes without eyelids; the operculum covering the gills on each side, with the gill opening behind it. One long dorsal fin with a serrated spine in front, a similar anal fin, a homocercal, forked caudal fin; pectoral fins behind the gill opening and pelvic fins in an abdominal position (a primitive teleost character). Cycloid scales overlap like roof tiles, their free edges backward; the lateral line runs along each side as a row of pierced scales.
- *Skin.* An epidermis with many mucous cells and a dermis containing the scales and pigment cells; scales grow by adding rings (circuli) at the margin, faster in summer, so that annual marks (annuli) record the age of the fish.
- *Skeleton.* A skull of numerous bones (more than 100 elements in the skull and jaws, a mixture of dermal and cartilage bones); amphicoelous vertebrae with remnants of the notochord, divided into trunk vertebrae with ribs and caudal vertebrae with haemal arches; the first four vertebrae modified into the Weberian apparatus. Intermuscular bones (the small Y-shaped "fish bones" of carp meat) ossify in the myosepta. The pectoral girdle is attached to the skull by the posttemporal; the pelvic girdle is a pair of plates embedded in the body wall ([7.17.61](/7.17.61)).
- *Muscles.* W-shaped myomeres in nested cones, epaxial and hypaxial divided by the horizontal septum; mostly white (fast, glycolytic) fibres with a thin band of red (slow, oxidative) fibres along the lateral line.
- *Digestion.* No teeth in the jaws; pharyngeal teeth in three rows on the fifth ceratobranchial crush food against a horny pad on the basioccipital. There is no stomach; the long, coiled intestine (two to three times the body length, as in most omnivores and herbivores) leads to the anus. The liver and pancreas are combined into a diffuse hepatopancreas around the intestine; a gall bladder is present. *Gill rakers* on the gill arches retain food particles.
- *Swim bladder.* Two chambers, anterior and posterior, joined by a narrow duct; the pneumatic duct connects the posterior chamber with the oesophagus (physostomous condition, §47.4.2). The anterior chamber is connected with the inner ear by the Weberian apparatus.
- *Respiration.* Four pairs of gills on the gill arches, each with two rows of gill filaments (a hemibranch on each side of the arch, two hemibranchs forming a holobranch), with secondary lamellae; the interbranchial septum is reduced, so the filaments project freely into the opercular chamber. Water is pumped by the buccal and opercular pumps with a nearly continuous flow over the gills, in the direction opposite to blood flow in the lamellae (**countercurrent exchange**, §47.4.4). A pseudobranch lies on the inside of the operculum.
- *Circulation.* A heart of sinus venosus, atrium, ventricle and bulbus arteriosus (an elastic, non-contractile swelling of the ventral aorta that replaces the conus arteriosus in teleosts); a single circulation through the gills to the body. Four pairs of afferent branchial arteries carry blood to the gills; the efferent arteries join the dorsal aorta ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)). There is a hepatic portal system and a renal portal system.
- *Excretion and osmoregulation.* A pair of long, dark red mesonephric kidneys beneath the vertebral column, fused at the back; the ureters join and open behind the anus. A freshwater fish produces large volumes of dilute urine; most nitrogen is excreted as ammonia across the gills (**ammonotelic**, §47.4.5).
- *Reproduction.* Paired gonads continuous with their ducts (the teleost gonoduct is a new duct formed from the gonad wall, not the Müllerian duct); separate sexes; external fertilization. Carp spawn in spring in shallow water among plants, laying sticky eggs that adhere to vegetation; a large female lays several hundred thousand eggs.
- *Nervous system and sense organs.* A brain with small cerebral hemispheres (the everted telencephalon), large optic lobes and cerebellum, and paired vagal lobes in the medulla that process taste; ten pairs of cranial nerves. The inner ear has three semicircular canals and three otoliths (lapillus, sagitta, asteriscus); the lateral line canals on the head and body; taste buds on the barbels, lips and gill rakers and over the body surface.

### 47.3.5. Chinese Freshwater Fishes and the Four Major Carps

China has about 1500 species of freshwater fishes, more than two-thirds of them Cypriniformes. The fauna is rich because of the size of the river systems, the long isolation of their headwaters on the margins of the Tibetan Plateau and the subtropical climate of the south. Many groups are endemic: the schizothoracine carps of the Tibetan Plateau, the loaches and balitorids of mountain torrents (with ventral suckers formed from paired fins), and the cave-dwelling golden-line fishes *Sinocyclocheilus* of the karst of Guangxi, Guizhou and Yunnan, with more than 70 species, many blind and depigmented, the largest radiation of cave fishes in the world.

The "four major Chinese carps" (sì dà jiā yú, the four domestic fishes) have been cultured together in ponds since the Tang dynasty, after an imperial ban on the farming of common carp (whose name was a homophone of the imperial family name) led farmers to the native species of the Yangtze.

| Species | Feeding niche | Adaptations | Position in the water column |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black carp *Mylopharyngodon piceus* | Snails and freshwater mussels | One row of molariform pharyngeal teeth that crush shells | Bottom |
| Grass carp *Ctenopharyngodon idella* | Aquatic plants and grass | Two rows of comb-like pharyngeal teeth that cut and grind plants; one of the few herbivorous teleosts able to live on higher plants | Middle to upper |
| Silver carp *Hypophthalmichthys molitrix* | Phytoplankton | Gill rakers fused into a fine spongy sieve that retains particles down to about $10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ | Upper |
| Bighead carp *H. nobilis* | Zooplankton | Long, fine gill rakers, not fused; large head (about a third of the body length) | Middle to upper |

The four species divide the food resources of a pond among them, and in polyculture their combined yield exceeds that of any one of them alone: the grass carp feeds on plants added by the farmer, and its faeces fertilize the pond, which promotes phytoplankton (eaten by silver carp) and zooplankton (eaten by bighead carp), while black carp eat the snails. China produces about 60 % of the world's farmed fish, and the grass carp is the most produced freshwater fish in the world, with about 5–6 million tonnes per year.

The four carps spawn in the main channels of the Yangtze and other large rivers during floods in late spring and early summer, when rising water and temperature (above about 18 °C) trigger spawning in turbulent stretches. Their eggs are semi-buoyant: they swell with water and drift downstream for one to two days while the embryos develop, and the larvae settle in the lakes and floodplains connected to the river. Until the 1950s, pond culture relied on fry collected from the rivers. In 1958 Zhong Lin and colleagues in Guangdong induced spawning of silver and bighead carp in ponds by injecting pituitary extracts and human chorionic gonadotropin, and artificial propagation of all four species followed; it is one of the important achievements of Chinese fisheries science. Damming of rivers and the separation of lakes from rivers have reduced the natural spawning, and the number of drifting eggs passing below the Three Gorges Dam fell sharply after 2003.

Silver, bighead, black and grass carps were introduced to North America in the 1960s and 1970s for aquaculture and the control of algae, snails and weeds. They escaped into the Mississippi basin, where silver and bighead carp now make up the majority of fish biomass in parts of some rivers; the "Asian carps" are a major problem there, and electric barriers on the Chicago canals are meant to keep them out of the Great Lakes. The same species that are threatened in their native rivers by dams are invasive pests elsewhere.

Other Chinese freshwater fishes mentioned in the textbooks include the common carp and the crucian carp *Carassius auratus*, whose domesticated form, the goldfish, was selected in China from the Song dynasty onward; the blunt-snout bream *Megalobrama amblycephala* ("Wuchang fish") of the middle Yangtze; the Chinese bitterling *Rhodeus sinensis*, whose females lay eggs through a long ovipositor into the gills of freshwater mussels, where the embryos develop protected; the Chinese sucker *Myxocyprinus asiaticus*, the only Asian member of the suckers (Catostomidae), whose relatives live in North America; the oriental weatherfish (loach) *Misgurnus anguillicaudatus*, which breathes air by swallowing it and absorbing oxygen in the hindgut; the mandarin fish *Siniperca chuatsi*, a predator that feeds only on live fishes; the rice-field eel *Monopterus albus* (§47.5.3); and the snakeheads *Channa*, with an air-breathing suprabranchial organ.

## 47.4. Life in Water

### 47.4.1. Water as a Medium

Water differs from air in ways that shape every aspect of fish biology. It is about 800 times denser and 50 times more viscous, so it supports the body (the weight of a fish is almost balanced by buoyancy) but makes motion expensive and demands a streamlined form. It holds about 1/30 as much oxygen per litre as air at the same partial pressure, and oxygen diffuses through it about 10 000 times more slowly; the respiratory surfaces must be large and thin and the ventilation flow high ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.2.3)). Water has a high heat capacity and conducts heat well, so most fishes are **ectotherms** at the temperature of the water. And it transmits sound about 4.5 times faster than air with little loss, and light poorly, so that hearing, the lateral line and chemical senses are often more important than vision.

### 47.4.2. Buoyancy and the Swim Bladder

**Definition 47.4.1 (Swim Bladder)** The **swim bladder** (gas bladder) is a gas-filled sac of ray-finned fishes, derived from the lungs of early osteichthyans, that lies dorsal to the gut beneath the vertebral column and serves mainly for buoyancy, in some groups also for hearing, sound production and respiration; it is connected to the oesophagus by a pneumatic duct in **physostomous** fishes and closed in **physoclistous** fishes, where gas is secreted by a gas gland and resorbed in a vascularized region (the oval).

Fish tissues are denser than water (bone about $2\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$, muscle about $1.05$), and a fish without a gas bladder sinks. A bladder of about 5 % of body volume makes a marine teleost neutrally buoyant, and about 7 % a freshwater one (fresh water is less dense than seawater, about 1.000 compared with 1.026). Neutral buoyancy saves the energy that would otherwise be spent generating lift, and it allows a fish to hover and manoeuvre at low speed.

Gas in a bladder obeys Boyle's law: at constant temperature, its volume is inversely proportional to the pressure. The pressure in water increases by about one atmosphere ($101\,\mathrm{kPa}$) for every 10 m of depth.

**Example 47.4.1 (Boyle's Law and Depth)** A fish is neutrally buoyant at its home depth $d_{\mathrm{n}}$ with a bladder of volume $V_{\mathrm{n}}$. If it moves to depth $d$ without adding or removing gas, the bladder volume becomes

$$
\frac{V}{V_{\mathrm{n}}} = \frac{p_{\mathrm{n}}}{p} = \frac{10\,\mathrm{m} + d_{\mathrm{n}}}{10\,\mathrm{m} + d}.
$$
A fish neutral at 10 m that swims up to the surface doubles its bladder volume; one neutral at 50 m that rises to the surface has a bladder six times larger. The relative change of volume per metre of vertical movement is

$$
\frac{1}{V}\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}d} = -\frac{1}{10\,\mathrm{m} + d},
$$
10 % per metre near the surface but only 0.5 % per metre at 200 m. Near the surface, buoyancy is therefore unstable: a fish that sinks a little loses buoyancy and sinks further, one that rises gains buoyancy and rises further, and it must correct by swimming or by adjusting the gas. Fish hauled up from depth by a net or line suffer barotrauma: the bladder expands, may burst and can push the stomach out of the mouth. A physoclistous fish neutral at 50 m that could resorb gas at the rate needed would take hours to adjust to the surface, because gas exchange in the oval is slow; physostomous fishes can release gas through the pneumatic duct quickly.

:::

![Boyle's law and the swim bladder (model). (A) The volume of a swim bladder with a fixed amount of gas, relative to its volume at the depth where the fish is neutrally buoyant, for fishes neutral at the surface, at 10 m and at 50 m (dots); a fish rising above its home depth gains buoyancy, one sinking below it loses buoyancy. (B) The fractional change of bladder volume per metre of vertical movement, $1/(10\,\mathrm{m} + d)$: about 10 % per metre at the surface, less than 1 % per metre below 100 m, so buoyancy control is most demanding near the surface.](./plots/swim-bladder.py)

*Physostomous and physoclistous fishes.* In physostomous fishes, including most of the early-branching teleosts (eels, herrings, carps, catfishes, salmon), the pneumatic duct remains open in the adult; they fill the bladder by gulping air at the surface and release gas through the duct. In physoclistous fishes (most acanthopterygians) the duct closes after the larva has first filled the bladder. Gas is then secreted into the bladder by a gas gland, a patch of glandular epithelium supplied by a countercurrent capillary network, the **rete mirabile**. The gland cells produce lactic acid and CO₂, which lower the pH of the blood; acidification causes haemoglobin of the Root type to release oxygen (the **Root effect**: a fall in the maximum oxygen saturation of haemoglobin at low pH), and the rete multiplies the local oxygen partial pressure by countercurrent multiplication, so that oxygen can be deposited into the bladder against pressures of tens or hundreds of atmospheres ([7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.6.4)). Gas is resorbed through the oval, a thin, richly vascularized region that can be opened or closed by a muscular sphincter. Deep-sea fishes of the continental slopes have bladders filled with oxygen at pressures of up to 100 atmospheres or more; many fishes of greater depths have lost the bladder and use lipids or watery, poorly ossified tissues for buoyancy instead.

Many bottom-living fishes (flatfishes, gobies, many blennies), very fast swimmers that change depth rapidly (some tunas and mackerels) and some deep-sea fishes have lost the swim bladder or reduced it; for them buoyancy is less important than staying on the bottom or tolerating rapid pressure changes.

### 47.4.3. Swimming

Most fishes swim by passing a wave of bending along the body from head to tail, faster than the fish moves forward. Each segment of the body pushes water backward and sideways; the backward momentum given to the water is the thrust. The swimming modes differ in how much of the body takes part:

| Mode | Body undulation | Examples | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anguilliform | The whole body, with more than one wavelength along the body | Eels, lampreys, many sharks, loaches | Efficient at low speed; manoeuvring in confined spaces; swimming backward |
| Subcarangiform | Rear half to two-thirds of the body | Trout, carp, cod | General purpose; acceleration |
| Carangiform | Rear third; amplitude rises sharply toward the tail | Jacks, herrings, mackerel | Fast cruising |
| Thunniform | Only the narrow caudal peduncle and a stiff, crescent-shaped (lunate) tail; the body is stiff | Tunas, lamnid sharks, swordfishes, and convergently dolphins and ichthyosaurs | Fast, efficient long-distance cruising |
| Ostraciiform | Body rigid; the tail fin oscillates like a paddle | Boxfishes | Slow, stable swimming |
| Median and paired fin propulsion | Undulation or oscillation of the dorsal and anal fins (balistiform, amiiform, gymnotiform), or of the pectoral fins (rajiform in rays; labriform rowing or flapping in wrasses) | Triggerfishes, bowfin, knifefishes, rays, wrasses, seahorses | Precise manoeuvring near reefs and the bottom; the body stays straight (useful in electric fishes, which keep their electric field stable) |

![Swimming modes of fishes, dorsal view of the body with the head to the left (model). For each mode the midline is a travelling wave whose amplitude grows from head to tail (dashed envelope); grey lines are the midlines at ten instants through one tailbeat and the shaded body is the outline at one instant. From anguilliform to thunniform swimming, the wavelength of the body wave increases (from about 0.6 to 1.3 body lengths) and the undulation is restricted to an increasingly short rear part of the body. The amplitude envelopes and wavelengths are schematic values chosen to match typical published kinematics, not measurements of particular fishes.](./plots/swimming-modes.py)

The efficiency of swimming depends on the frequency $f$ and the peak-to-peak amplitude $A$ of the tailbeat relative to the swimming speed $U$, combined in the dimensionless *Strouhal number*

$$
\mathrm{St} = \frac{fA}{U}.
$$
Flapping and undulating foils produce thrust most efficiently when $\mathrm{St}$ lies between about 0.2 and 0.4, where the vortices shed by the tail form a jet behind the fish. Across many swimming and flying animals, from fishes and dolphins to birds, bats and insects, cruising animals operate in this range (Taylor, Nudds and Thomas 2003).

**Example 47.4.2 (Strouhal Number of a Cruising Fish)** A trout 30 cm long cruises at 1.5 body lengths per second, $U = 0.45\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, with a tailbeat frequency of $4\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and a peak-to-peak tail amplitude of 0.2 body lengths, $A = 0.06\,\mathrm{m}$. Then $\mathrm{St} = 4 \times 0.06/0.45 = 0.53$, somewhat above the optimal range; at a higher speed of $0.75\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ with $f = 5\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and the same amplitude, $\mathrm{St} = 0.40$. Measurements show that the tail-beat amplitude of fishes stays nearly constant at about 0.2 body lengths over a wide range of speeds, while frequency increases nearly in proportion to speed; $\mathrm{St}$ therefore falls toward a constant value at higher speeds, in the efficient range. The Reynolds number of this trout, $\operatorname{Re} = UL/\nu = 0.45 \times 0.3/(1.0 \times 10^{-6}) \approx 1.4 \times 10^{5}$, is in the inertial regime, where streamlining matters; fish larvae a few millimetres long swim at Reynolds numbers of 10–100, where viscosity is important (**Reynolds number**).

:::

Fish muscle is divided into fibre types. A thin layer of red, slow oxidative fibres beneath the skin, concentrated along the horizontal septum, powers sustained cruising; the bulk of the myomeres consists of white, fast glycolytic fibres used in bursts, which fatigue in seconds. In tunas, some lamnid sharks and billfishes the red muscle lies deep in the body, close to the vertebral column, and is warmed by its own metabolism; countercurrent heat exchangers (**countercurrent heat exchange** in the retia of the lateral vessels) keep the heat from being carried to the gills, and the red muscle of a bluefin tuna can be 10–20 °C warmer than the water. This regional endothermy evolved independently in tunas and lamnid sharks ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)).

The fastest burst starts of fishes, used in escaping predators, are triggered by a pair of giant neurons in the hindbrain, the Mauthner cells, which fire a single action potential that contracts all the muscles of one side, bending the fish into a C within 10–20 ms; the second stage propels it away. The C-start is one of the best-understood escape circuits in neurobiology.

### 47.4.4. Sensing the Water and Breathing

The **lateral line** detects water movements relative to the body: the displacement of the cupula of each neuromast, a group of hair cells with a gelatinous cap, by the flow ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.1)). Superficial neuromasts on the skin respond to the velocity of flow; canal neuromasts in the lateral line canals of the head and along the body respond to its acceleration (pressure gradients) and are less disturbed by steady currents. With the lateral line, fishes detect prey and predators nearby, avoid obstacles, keep their position in schools and orient to currents (rheotaxis). Blind cave fishes such as *Sinocyclocheilus* and the Mexican tetra *Astyanax* have more and larger neuromasts than their surface relatives.

The otoliths of the inner ear grow by daily and annual layers of calcium carbonate. Counting these layers gives the age of a fish in days (in larvae) or years (in adults), and their chemistry records the waters the fish has lived in; otoliths are the main tool for ageing fishes in fisheries science.

Gills extract oxygen efficiently because water flows over the secondary lamellae in the direction opposite to the blood (countercurrent exchange), so that blood leaving the lamella meets incoming water with the highest oxygen content; teleosts extract 50–80 % of the oxygen from the water they ventilate, compared with about 25 % for humans breathing air ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.2)). The buccal and opercular pumps maintain a nearly continuous flow; fast swimmers such as tunas and mackerels ventilate by ram ventilation and suffocate if they stop.

Air breathing evolved independently in more than 60 lineages of teleosts living in warm, oxygen-poor fresh waters, using many different organs: the swim bladder (gars, bowfin, arowanas), the stomach or intestine (loaches, some armoured catfishes), the mouth and pharynx (the rice-field eel, mudskippers), suprabranchial chambers with labyrinthine plates (the labyrinth fishes such as gouramis and the climbing perch *Anabas*), and branched outgrowths of the gill chamber (snakeheads, walking catfishes *Clarias*). Some, including the snakeheads and the climbing perch, are obligate air breathers that drown if denied access to the surface. Mudskippers (*Periophthalmus*, *Boleophthalmus*) of the Chinese and Southeast Asian mudflats spend much of their time out of water, breathing through their skin and through the moist mouth and gill chamber, and move on land with their pectoral fins.

### 47.4.5. Osmoregulation

The body fluids of teleosts have an osmotic concentration of about $300\text{–}400\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$, about a third of that of seawater and far above that of fresh water. Teleosts therefore regulate their ions and water against the environment ([7.14.21](/7.14.21#sec-21.5.3)).

| | Marine teleosts | Freshwater teleosts |
|---|---|---|
| Osmotic problem | Lose water by osmosis, gain salts by diffusion | Gain water by osmosis, lose salts by diffusion |
| Drinking | Drink seawater continuously; absorb NaCl and water in the intestine | Drink little |
| Gills | Ionocytes (chloride cells) secrete Na⁺ and Cl⁻ | Ionocytes take up Na⁺, Cl⁻ and Ca²⁺ from the water |
| Kidney | Little urine, nearly isosmotic; excretes divalent ions (Mg²⁺, SO₄²⁻); glomeruli reduced or absent in some species | Large volume of dilute urine; well-developed glomeruli |
| Nitrogen excretion | Ammonia across the gills (ammonotelic), some urea | Ammonia across the gills |

Euryhaline fishes, such as salmon, eels, tilapia and the Chinese sturgeon, move between fresh water and the sea and switch between the two modes over days or weeks. The switch is controlled by hormones: cortisol and growth hormone prepare the gills for seawater, prolactin for fresh water; in salmon the transformation of the freshwater parr into the seawater-tolerant smolt before the downstream migration is controlled by thyroid hormones, growth hormone and cortisol ([7.14.34](/7.14.34#sec-34.7.2)).

### 47.4.6. Electric Fishes

Electric organs evolved at least six times in fishes: in electric rays and skates among chondrichthyans, and among teleosts in the African elephantfishes (Mormyridae) and the South American knifefishes (Gymnotiformes), both weakly electric, and in the electric catfish *Malapterurus* and the stargazers (Uranoscopidae). Electric organs consist of modified muscle (or in one family of knifefishes, nerve) cells, the electrocytes, stacked in series so that their voltages add. Weakly electric fishes generate a continuous electric field around themselves, with discharges of less than one volt, and detect distortions of the field by objects of different conductivity with tuberous electroreceptors in the skin (active electrolocation); they also communicate with their discharges, which differ between species and sexes. Strong discharges stun prey: the electric eels (*Electrophorus*, three species, gymnotiforms despite the name) produce discharges of up to about 860 V in *E. voltai*. The independent evolution of electric organs in mormyrids and gymnotiforms involved the same duplicated sodium channel gene from the teleost genome duplication (**neofunctionalization**, [7.10.18](/7.10.18#sec-18.2.1)).

## 47.5. Reproduction and Life Histories

### 47.5.1. Fecundity, Eggs and Larvae

Most teleosts fertilize externally and produce large numbers of small eggs: a cod lays several million, an ocean sunfish up to 300 million. Many marine teleosts have pelagic eggs, buoyant because of a large fluid-filled space or an oil droplet, and pelagic larvae that drift in the plankton for weeks or months before settling. Survival from egg to adult is typically $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$, and small changes in larval survival, caused by food supply, temperature and currents, produce large fluctuations in recruitment from year to year. This variability, first studied by Johan Hjort in the Norwegian cod and herring fisheries in 1914, is a central problem of fisheries management.

### 47.5.2. Migration

Many fishes migrate regularly between habitats for spawning, feeding and overwintering; the Chinese textbooks distinguish breeding, feeding and overwintering migrations. Migrations between fresh water and the sea are called diadromous: anadromous fishes, such as salmon, sturgeons, shad and lampreys, grow in the sea and spawn in fresh water; catadromous fishes, such as freshwater eels, grow in fresh water and spawn in the sea; amphidromous fishes move between the two habitats at other stages, not for breeding. Pacific salmon, including the chum salmon *Oncorhynchus keta* that ascends the Heilongjiang, Wusuli and Songhua rivers in autumn, return to the stream where they hatched, guided by olfactory imprinting on the odour of the stream learned as smolts (Hasler's experiments with plugged nostrils, 1950s), and die after spawning. The large yellow croaker *Larimichthys crocea*, once the most important fishery species of the East China Sea, gathered in huge spawning aggregations in spring and summer in coastal waters, where it produces loud drumming sounds with muscles attached to the swim bladder; "knocking" fisheries of the 1950s and 1960s, which killed whole schools by exploiting the sound sensitivity of the fish, and intense trawling of the spawning and overwintering aggregations in the 1970s caused the collapse of the wild stocks, and the yellow croaker on the market today is almost entirely farmed.

### 47.5.3. Parental Care, Sex Change and Unisexual Species

About a quarter of teleost families show some form of parental care, and in most of them the male provides it. Sticklebacks (*Gasterosteus*, and in China *Pungitius sinensis*) build nests of plant material glued with a kidney secretion, in which the male fans the eggs; mouthbrooding cichlids carry eggs and young in the mouth; in seahorses and pipefishes the female deposits eggs in a brood pouch of the male, which supplies them with oxygen and nutrients and "gives birth". Male care is common because external fertilization leaves the male with the eggs, and a territorial male can guard eggs while courting further females.

Teleosts are the only vertebrates in which sequential hermaphroditism is widespread. In protogynous species, individuals first function as females and later as males: the rice-field eel *Monopterus albus*, a Chinese food fish, is female first and changes to male after spawning once or twice, so that large individuals are males; many wrasses and groupers change sex when the dominant male of a group disappears. In protandrous species, individuals are first male: in anemonefishes (*Amphiprion*) the largest fish of a group is the female, and when she dies the largest male becomes female. The size-advantage model explains the direction: sex change is favoured when reproductive success increases with size faster in one sex than in the other. A large male wrasse that monopolizes spawning on a reef gains more from size than a large female, which gains only in fecundity; protogyny follows. In anemonefishes, where mating is monogamous and fecundity rises steeply with female size, protandry follows.

A few fishes reproduce without males of their own. The Amazon molly *Poecilia formosa* and the silver crucian carp *Carassius gibelio* (in China and northern Asia) are gynogenetic: their eggs must be activated by the sperm of a related species, but the sperm's genome is usually not incorporated (in *C. gibelio*, a triploid, it can occasionally be). Viviparity, with internal fertilization by a modified anal fin (the gonopodium), occurs in the livebearers (Poeciliidae: guppies, mollies, swordtails) and several other families. In some deep-sea anglerfishes the tiny male attaches to a female, fuses with her skin and circulation and becomes a permanent sperm-producing appendage (sexual parasitism), possible because these anglerfishes have lost parts of their adaptive immune system that would reject the male tissue.

## 47.6. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

The Chinese textbooks place the bony fishes in the class (or group) Osteichthyes of the Pisces, with three subclasses: Coelacanthimorpha (or Crossopterygii, "fringe-finned fishes"), Dipnoi (lungfishes) and Actinopterygii, the last with three superorders, Chondrostei (bichirs and sturgeons), Holostei (gars and bowfin) and Teleostei. The classification of teleost orders follows the older system in which Perciformes contained most spiny-finned fishes.

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Osteichthyes as a class of fishes, excluding tetrapods | Osteichthyes is a clade that includes the tetrapods; "bony fishes" in the usual sense are paraphyletic |
| Three subclasses: Coelacanthimorpha, Dipnoi, Actinopterygii | Two lineages, Actinopterygii and Sarcopterygii; coelacanths and lungfishes are sarcopterygians, together with the tetrapods ([7.17.48](/7.17.48)) |
| Chondrostei containing Polypteriformes and Acipenseriformes | Polypteriformes and Acipenseriformes are separate early-branching lineages; Chondrostei is paraphyletic if the bichirs are included |
| Holostei with gars and bowfin as the intermediate grade before teleosts | Holostei is a clade, the sister group of teleosts (molecular evidence) |
| "Superorder Teleostei" with about 96 % of living fishes | Teleostei: about 33 000 species, more than 99 % of actinopterygians |
| Swim bladder as an organ of fishes from which lungs evolved | Lungs are the ancestral osteichthyan organ; the swim bladder is a modified lung |
| Perciformes with about 9300 species, including Serranidae, Sciaenidae, Gobiidae, Scombridae, Cichlidae, Channidae | Perciformes in the narrow sense (perches, sea basses, scorpionfishes and relatives); gobies (Gobiiformes), mackerels and tunas (Scombriformes), cichlids (Cichliformes), snakeheads (Anabantiformes), jacks (Carangiformes) in separate orders of Percomorpha |
| Gasterosteiformes including seahorses | Sticklebacks in Perciformes in the narrow sense; seahorses and pipefishes in Syngnathiformes |
| Salmoniformes containing salmon, smelts, pikes and others | Salmoniformes (salmon, trout, whitefishes); Esociformes (pikes) and Osmeriformes (smelts) separate |
| Clupeiformes including the anchovies; Cypriniformes and Siluriformes as separate orders | Agrees; Clupeiformes and Ostariophysi together form the Otomorpha |
| *Aristichthys nobilis* (bighead carp), *Lepidosteus*, *Amia calva* as the only bowfin | *Hypophthalmichthys nobilis*; *Lepisosteus*; two species of bowfin |
| Chinese paddlefish as a living endangered species | Extinct (IUCN, 2022) |

The textbook account of the carp (external form, cycloid scales and the lateral line, the skull, vertebrae and Weberian apparatus, pharyngeal teeth, two-chambered physostomous swim bladder, gills and gill rakers, heart with bulbus arteriosus, mesonephric kidneys, brain and sense organs) and of migration is included in §47.3.4 and §47.5.2. The textbooks' characterization of the teleosts by the "bulbus arteriosus instead of conus", "no spiral valve" and "homocercal tail", and of the Chondrostei by the cartilaginous skeleton, persistent notochord, spiral valve and conus, remains accurate as description; the cartilaginous skeleton of sturgeons is a secondary reduction.

## Summary

- Osteichthyes (Definition 47.1.1): endochondral bone, lepidotrichia, lungs (the swim bladder is a modified lung), operculum, marginal jaw bones; the earliest bony fishes (*Guiyu*, *Psarolepis*, *Meemannia*) are from the Silurian and Devonian of Yunnan. The two lineages, ray-finned and lobe-finned, differ in fins, scales, teeth and brain.
- Actinopterygii (Definition 47.2.1): bichirs (air-breathing African fishes with ganoid scales), sturgeons and paddlefishes (secondarily cartilaginous; the critically endangered Chinese sturgeon, the extinct Chinese paddlefish), gars and bowfins (Holostei, sister group of teleosts).
- Teleostei (Definition 47.3.1): mobile premaxilla and protrusible jaws (Example 47.3.1), homocercal tail, thin scales, pharyngeal jaws, a physoclistous swim bladder in many, and the 3R genome duplication; diversity from eels and bony-tongues to ostariophysans with the Weberian apparatus (Definition 47.3.2) and the spiny-finned fishes.
- The carp is the representative animal; the four major Chinese carps divide the food of a pond (molluscs, plants, phytoplankton, zooplankton) and spawn drifting eggs in floods of the large rivers.
- Life in water: buoyancy by a swim bladder (Definition 47.4.1) governed by Boyle's law (Example 47.4.1), gas secretion by the gas gland with the Root effect and countercurrent multiplication; swimming modes from anguilliform to thunniform with Strouhal numbers of 0.2–0.4 (Example 47.4.2); the lateral line, Weberian hearing and otoliths; countercurrent gills and repeated evolution of air breathing; hypo- and hyperosmotic regulation; electric organs.
- Reproduction: high fecundity and variable recruitment, diadromous migrations (eels, salmon, the yellow croaker), male parental care, sex change explained by the size-advantage model, gynogenesis and viviparity.

## Questions

1. Why do zoologists say that lungs are older than swim bladders, and how do the bichirs support this view?

   **Answer** The earliest-branching living members of both osteichthyan lineages breathe air with paired, ventral lungs: the bichirs among actinopterygians and the lungfishes among sarcopterygians, and tetrapods have lungs too. Mapping the character on the tree shows that ventral, paired air-breathing lungs were present in the common ancestor of osteichthyans. The swim bladder of teleosts is dorsal, unpaired and mostly hydrostatic, a modification within actinopterygians; intermediate states are seen in gars and bowfins, whose bladders are dorsal but respiratory. Darwin's view, that lungs evolved from a swim bladder, reverses the direction.
2. A physoclistous fish neutrally buoyant at 30 m is chased upward to 10 m. By how much does its swim bladder expand, and what does the fish do?

   **Answer** By Boyle's law $V/V_{\mathrm{n}} = (10 + 30)/(10 + 10) = 2$: the bladder doubles in volume, if the fish cannot remove gas quickly. The fish becomes positively buoyant and tends to rise further. It counteracts this by swimming downward or holding its fins at a negative angle of attack, by contracting muscles around the bladder to compress it slightly, and over a longer time by resorbing gas through the oval into the blood. Because resorption is slow, physoclistous fishes generally restrict the speed of their vertical movements; if forced up fast, as in a net, the bladder may burst.
3. Explain how a gas gland can secrete oxygen into a swim bladder at a pressure many times higher than that of the oxygen in arterial blood.

   **Answer** The gas gland produces lactic acid and carbon dioxide, which acidify the blood in its capillaries. Teleost haemoglobins show a Root effect: at low pH their oxygen capacity falls, so they release oxygen, raising the oxygen partial pressure in the blood locally; lactate also lowers the solubility of gases (salting-out effect). The rete mirabile, a bundle of parallel arterial and venous capillaries with blood flowing in opposite directions, then acts as a countercurrent multiplier: the oxygen released in the venous capillaries leaving the gland diffuses into the arterial capillaries entering it, so oxygen is trapped and accumulates at the gland end, building up partial pressures high enough to diffuse into the bladder even at great depth.
4. Why does a fish that cruises efficiently keep its tail amplitude roughly constant and increase its tailbeat frequency with speed?

   **Answer** Thrust efficiency of an oscillating tail is greatest when the Strouhal number $fA/U$ lies near 0.2–0.4. If amplitude stays at about 0.2 body lengths, frequency must rise in proportion to speed to keep $\mathrm{St}$ in this range. Amplitude is also limited by the body's structure and by the drag of large lateral excursions (recoil of the anterior body). Increasing frequency at constant amplitude therefore keeps the wake in the efficient jet configuration over a range of speeds.
5. The four major Chinese carps are cultured together. Explain the ecological basis of the polyculture and why damming the Yangtze threatens their wild populations.

   **Answer** The four species occupy different feeding niches: black carp eat snails and mussels with molariform pharyngeal teeth, grass carp eat plants with comb-like pharyngeal teeth, silver carp filter phytoplankton with fused, spongy gill rakers, bighead carp filter zooplankton with fine gill rakers; they also feed at different depths. Grass carp faeces fertilize the water and promote plankton, so the combined yield exceeds that of monoculture. In the wild, all four spawn in flowing river channels during floods, and their semi-buoyant eggs must drift for one to two days in turbulent water to hatch; larvae need floodplain lakes. Dams reduce flood pulses, change water temperature, shorten the free-flowing stretches available for drifting eggs and cut rivers off from lakes, so spawning and recruitment decline.
6. Use the size-advantage model to predict the direction of sex change in a coral reef fish in which a few large males defend territories and spawn with many females.

   **Answer** In such a mating system, male reproductive success depends strongly on size, because only large males can defend territories and they mate with many females, whereas female success increases only modestly with size (through fecundity). An individual therefore maximizes its lifetime reproduction by being female while small and becoming male when large enough to hold a territory: protogyny is predicted, as observed in many wrasses, parrotfishes and groupers. The removal experiment confirms the social control: when the dominant male is removed, the largest female changes sex within days.
7. (Open) The Chinese sturgeon has been bred in captivity and released by the million, but the wild population has not recovered. Suggest reasons and propose measures, using the biology of the species.

   **Answer** Reasons: the species matures late (males at about 8–18 years, females at 14–26 years) and the released fish have not yet had time to reach maturity in numbers; survival of released juveniles in the river and sea may be low (bycatch, ship strikes, pollution, prey depletion); the only spawning site below the Gezhouba Dam has unsuitable conditions in many years, especially autumn water temperatures raised by the release of warmer water from the Three Gorges reservoir, so adults reaching the river may not spawn; captive breeding may reduce genetic diversity or fitness. Measures: manage dam releases to provide suitable temperature and flow during the spawning season; restore or create additional spawning substrate; enforce the fishing ban and reduce bycatch at sea; mark released fish to measure their survival; maintain genetic diversity in the captive stock and release at stages and sites with high survival; protect the estuary nursery habitat.
