Sense organs connect the brain to the world, and their structure is shaped directly by the physics of the stimuli they receive: light behaves differently in air and water, sound is transmitted differently through water, air and bone, and odour molecules dissolve or float. The sense organs of vertebrates therefore carry a particularly clear record of the transition from water to land. The same basic receptor cells, the hair cells of the lateral line and inner ear, the photoreceptors of the retina and pineal, the chemoreceptors of the nose and taste buds, were used throughout, while the accessory structures that deliver the stimulus to them (lenses, middle ears, vomeronasal organs) were rebuilt again and again.

This chapter proceeds from the skin inward. Section 68.1 surveys the general receptors of the skin, §68.2 the lateral line and electroreceptors, §68.3 the ear, §68.4 the eye, §68.5 the median eyes, §68.6 the chemical senses and §68.7 other senses; §68.8 compares the classes. The physiology of sensation is in [7.14.39](/7.14.39)–[7.14.43](/7.14.43) and [7.16](/7.16); the development of the placodes, eye and ear in [7.5.22](/7.5.22); the bones of the middle ear in the context of the skull and of the origin of mammals in [7.17.58](/7.17.58), [7.17.59](/7.17.59) and [7.17.54](/7.17.54). The sense organs of invertebrates (**statocysts**, ocelli, **pinhole eyes** and **camera eyes** of cephalopods) are compared in [7.17.44](/7.17.44).

## 68.1. General Receptors of the Skin

The skin and the deeper tissues contain receptors for touch, pressure, vibration, temperature and pain, supplied by the general sensory fibres of the spinal nerves and of the trigeminal, facial, glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves ([7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.5)). They are of two kinds.

- *Free nerve endings* branch among the cells of the epidermis and dermis; they detect pain (nociceptors), temperature and crude touch. They are the only receptors in many fishes and amphibians.
- *Encapsulated endings* are enclosed in capsules of connective tissue or associated with special cells that filter the stimulus. Merkel discs (a nerve ending on a Merkel cell in the basal epidermis) respond to sustained pressure and occur in all vertebrates from fishes onward. Pacinian (lamellated) corpuscles, with an onion-like capsule, detect high-frequency vibration in the dermis and deeper tissues of mammals; the Herbst corpuscles of birds, in the bill of ducks and in the legs, are their avian counterparts, and Grandry corpuscles in the bills of ducks and geese detect velocity. Meissner corpuscles in the dermal papillae of the hairless skin of primates detect light touch. Ruffini endings respond to stretch of the skin; Krause end bulbs were classically assigned to cold, but their function is uncertain.

Some vertebrates have tactile organs concentrated in special structures: the barbels of catfishes and sturgeons (which also bear taste buds), the bill of the platypus with its mechano- and electroreceptors, the push-rod organs of the nose of moles (Eimer's organs; the star-nosed mole has about 25,000 on 22 fleshy rays), the integumentary sense organs on the scales of crocodylians and lizards, and the vibrissae (sinus hairs) of mammals, whose follicles are surrounded by blood sinuses and dense innervation ([7.17.57](/7.17.57)).

## 68.2. The Lateral Line System and Electroreceptors

### 68.2.1. Neuromasts and Canals

The **lateral line** system is a set of mechanoreceptive organs, the *neuromasts*, distributed over the head and along the trunk of cyclostomes, fishes and aquatic amphibians ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.1)). Each neuromast is a cluster of **hair cells** with supporting cells, whose hair bundles project into a gelatinous cupula. The hair bundles are polarized, each with a row of stereocilia graded in height and one kinocilium at the tall end; deflection toward the kinocilium excites, away from it inhibits. Neuromasts are arranged in pairs of opposite polarity, so that each line detects water movement in both directions along its axis.

Neuromasts occur either freely on the skin surface (superficial neuromasts, sensitive to the velocity of water flow) or in canals within the dermis or dermal bones of the head and in the lateral line scales of the trunk, connected to the surface by pores (canal neuromasts, sensitive to pressure gradients and acceleration, and protected from noise caused by the fish's own swimming). The canals of the head follow characteristic courses (supraorbital, infraorbital, mandibular, preopercular and temporal canals), which can be traced in fossil skull bones and have been used to identify homologous bones ([7.17.58](/7.17.58)). The lateral line detects the movements of prey, predators and neighbours in a school, and the water flow around obstacles; the blind cave fish *Astyanax* navigates by enlarged superficial neuromasts.

The lateral line develops from lateral line placodes, which are distinct from the otic placode, and is supplied by the anterior and posterior lateral line nerves ([7.17.67](/7.17.67#sec-67.5.1)). It is present in larval and aquatic adult amphibians and lost at metamorphosis in frogs and terrestrial salamanders; it was lost in the ancestor of amniotes, and no amniote has a lateral line. Amniotes that returned to water use other senses: the vibrissae of seals detect the hydrodynamic trails of fish.

### 68.2.2. Electroreceptors

**Electroreception**, the detection of weak electric fields, is a sense of most non-teleost aquatic vertebrates ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5.3)). Its receptors are modified hair cells of lateral line origin, lying at the bottom of jelly-filled canals open to the skin surface (*ampullary organs*). In chondrichthyans these are the **ampullae of Lorenzini**, whose long canals, concentrated on the snout, make sharks and rays sensitive to fields of a few nanovolts per centimetre, enough to locate prey buried in sand by the fields of their muscles and gills. Similar ampullary organs occur in lampreys, bichirs, sturgeons and paddlefishes (whose long rostrum is covered with them), coelacanths, lungfishes and aquatic salamanders and caecilians; this distribution shows that electroreception is ancestral for vertebrates.

Electroreception was lost in the ancestors of neopterygians (gars, bowfin, teleosts) and in frogs and amniotes, and then reinvented at least twice in teleosts (in osteoglossomorphs such as mormyrids and knifefishes, and in siluriforms and gymnotiforms), with ampullary organs of a different structure and, in the weakly electric fishes, *tuberous organs* tuned to their own electric discharges ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.8)). Among mammals the monotremes (platypus and echidnas) evolved electroreceptors from mucous glands of the bill or snout, innervated by the trigeminal nerve, and the Guiana dolphin has electroreceptive pits derived from vibrissal follicles; these are convergent.

## 68.3. The Ear

### 68.3.1. The Membranous Labyrinth

The inner ear develops from the otic placode, which invaginates into an otic vesicle and differentiates into the *membranous labyrinth*, a closed system of sacs and canals filled with endolymph, suspended in perilymph within the bony (or cartilaginous) otic capsule ([7.5.22](/7.5.22#sec-22.1)). It has a vestibular part, which senses gravity and acceleration, and, in tetrapods, an auditory part.

- *Semicircular canals.* Each canal has an ampulla with a crest (crista) of hair cells covered by a gelatinous cupula, which is deflected by the flow of endolymph when the head rotates in the plane of the canal (**semicircular canals**; [7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.5)). Gnathostomes have three canals in three perpendicular planes (anterior, posterior and horizontal); lampreys have two (anterior and posterior) and hagfishes one, apparently a single torus formed from two.
- *Otolithic organs.* The utricle, saccule and lagena each contain a sensory patch, the macula, covered by a gelatinous membrane loaded with calcium carbonate crystals, the otoconia or, in teleosts, large solid otoliths. Gravity and linear acceleration displace the otoliths relative to the hair cells. Teleost otoliths grow in daily and annual layers and are used to determine the age of fishes.
- *Auditory papillae.* Tetrapods add patches of hair cells specialized for sound: the papilla amphibiorum and papilla basilaris in amphibians, and in amniotes a basilar papilla that elongates into the cochlear duct. The basilar papilla lies on a basilar membrane that vibrates with sound, and the hair cells are covered by a tectorial membrane. In birds and crocodylians the duct is straight or slightly curved and up to a few millimetres long; in therian mammals it is coiled into the cochlea (about 2.5 turns in humans, up to 4 in some rodents) and contains the organ of Corti with inner and outer hair cells. Monotremes have a curved, uncoiled cochlea with a lagena at its tip, which recalls the condition of reptiles and birds. Mammals have lost the lagena; birds and reptiles retain it.

In fishes the endolymphatic duct opens to the surface of the head in elasmobranchs (allowing sand grains to enter as otoconia); in tetrapods it is a blind sac, and in frogs it forms large calcareous endolymphatic sacs alongside the vertebral column.

### 68.3.2. Hearing in Fishes

Sound in water is a pressure wave accompanied by particle motion. A fish's body has about the density of water and moves with the particle motion, while the denser otoliths lag behind, so the otolithic organs detect particle motion directly: all fishes hear low frequencies (up to a few hundred hertz) in this way ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.2)). Pressure is detected only if a gas-filled chamber, which is compressed and expanded by sound, is coupled to the ear. Many teleosts have such coupling, achieved independently in several ways:

- In otophysan fishes (carp, catfishes, characins; about two-thirds of freshwater fishes) the **Weberian apparatus**, a chain of four small bones derived from the first vertebrae and ribs (tripus, intercalarium, scaphium and claustrum), transmits vibrations of the swim bladder to a perilymphatic sinus of the inner ear; these fishes hear up to several kilohertz ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)).
- Herrings and their relatives have anterior extensions of the swim bladder that enter the skull and contact the utricle; some shads detect ultrasound up to $180\,\mathrm{kHz}$, probably to avoid echolocating dolphins.
- Squirrelfishes, cichlids and others have anterior horns of the swim bladder touching the skull; mormyrids have a gas bubble detached from the swim bladder next to the saccule.

### 68.3.3. The Middle Ear and Its Repeated Origin

On land the inner ear faces an impedance problem: sound in air carries little energy into the dense fluids of the inner ear, and about 99.9 % is reflected at an air–water interface. A tympanic middle ear overcomes this by collecting sound with a large, light *tympanic membrane* and transmitting it through a lever of one or more ossicles to the small oval window of the otic capsule; the ratio of areas (about 17–20 in humans) and the leverage of the ossicles amplify the pressure ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.2.2)). The middle-ear cavity develops from the first pharyngeal pouch, which remains connected with the pharynx by the Eustachian (auditory) tube, the derivative of the **spiracle** of fishes ([7.17.63](/7.17.63#sec-63.3.1)).

A tympanic middle ear evolved independently at least five times in tetrapods, in the Triassic, roughly 100 million years after the origin of tetrapods ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.3); [7.17.54](/7.17.54)): in anurans, in lepidosaurs, in archosaurs (birds and crocodylians), probably in turtles, and in mammals. Early tetrapods had a massive stapes that braced the braincase against the palate and probably transmitted low-frequency vibrations from the ground or water rather than airborne sound; the "otic notch" at the back of the skull of temnospondyls, long interpreted as the seat of an eardrum, was probably a spiracular opening.

- *The single-ossicle ear.* In amphibians and sauropsids the only ossicle is the *columella* (stapes), the descendant of the **hyomandibula** of fishes, a rod from the oval window to the eardrum, often with a cartilaginous extracolumella at its outer end. In frogs the eardrum lies on the side of the head behind the eye (enormous in bullfrog males); salamanders and caecilians have no tympanum and a second ossicle, the operculum, connected to the shoulder girdle by the opercularis muscle, which transmits substrate vibrations. In lizards the eardrum lies in a shallow depression behind the jaw joint, in crocodylians under a protective flap, in birds at the bottom of a short external ear canal covered by specialized feathers (ear coverts). Snakes have lost the tympanum and middle-ear cavity; their columella connects to the quadrate, and they hear low-frequency airborne sound and ground vibrations through the jaw.
- *The mammalian ear.* Mammals have three ossicles: the malleus (homologous with the articular bone of the reptilian lower jaw), the incus (homologous with the quadrate), and the stapes (homologous with the columella), and the tympanic membrane is supported by the tympanic bone (homologous with the angular bone of the lower jaw). The transfer of the jaw-joint bones into the middle ear, during the transition from cynodonts to mammals, is documented by fossils in which the postdentary bones were still attached to the lower jaw by an ossified Meckel's cartilage (*Yanoconodon*, *Liaoconodon*) and by embryology, in which the malleus and incus form from the posterior end of Meckel's cartilage and the palatoquadrate ([7.17.58](/7.17.58); [7.17.54](/7.17.54)). The detachment of the ossicles from the jaw (the definitive mammalian middle ear) happened independently in monotremes, in therians and in some extinct groups. The ossicles became light and loosely suspended, which allowed mammals to hear high frequencies (above $10\,\mathrm{kHz}$, up to $150\,\mathrm{kHz}$ in some bats and toothed whales), unlike all other vertebrates, which are limited to below about $10\,\mathrm{kHz}$.

### 68.3.4. The Outer Ear

An external auditory meatus, a canal leading inward to the tympanic membrane, is present in crocodylians, birds and mammals. Only mammals have a pinna (auricle), a movable flap of elastic cartilage supported by muscles (supplied by the facial nerve, [7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.6)), which collects sound and, by its directional filtering, helps to locate sound sources in elevation. The pinna is enormous in bats, fennec foxes and elephants (in which it also radiates heat), reduced in many aquatic mammals and absent in true seals, whales and many burrowing mammals. Owls have no pinnae, but the facial disc of feathers works as a sound reflector, and the ear openings of barn owls are asymmetric in height, which lets them localize sound in elevation as well as azimuth ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.6)).

## 68.4. The Eye

### 68.4.1. Development and Structure

The vertebrate eye develops from two sources ([7.5.22](/7.5.22#sec-22.2)): the optic vesicle, an outgrowth of the diencephalon that folds into a double-walled optic cup, whose inner wall becomes the neural retina and outer wall the pigment epithelium; and the lens placode, a thickening of the head ectoderm induced by the optic vesicle, which forms the lens. The surrounding mesenchyme (neural crest and mesoderm) forms the outer coats. The vertebrate eye is a **camera eye** with an inverted retina: the photoreceptors lie in the outer layer of the retina, against the pigment epithelium, and light must pass through the layers of neurons first; the axons of the ganglion cells leave through the optic disc, which produces a blind spot. The camera eyes of cephalopods, which develop as invaginations of the skin, have an everted retina without a blind spot; the two are a textbook case of convergence ([7.17.44](/7.17.44); [7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.3)).

The wall of the eye has three coats.

- *Fibrous tunic.* The sclera, of dense connective tissue, often reinforced by cartilage (chondrichthyans, many teleosts, amphibians, reptiles, birds) or by a ring of bony *scleral ossicles* (many teleosts, most reptiles except snakes and crocodylians, birds, many extinct reptiles including ichthyosaurs and dinosaurs), and in front the transparent cornea.
- *Vascular tunic (uvea).* The choroid, with blood vessels and pigment; the ciliary body, with the ciliary muscle and the processes that secrete aqueous humour and hold the lens; and the iris, a pigmented diaphragm around the pupil, with sphincter and dilator muscles (of neural ectoderm origin; striated in birds and reptiles, smooth in mammals).
- *Retina.* Rods (for dim light) and cones (for bright light and colour), bipolar and ganglion cells and interneurons ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.2)). Lampreys already have two types of photoreceptors and a retina with the same layers as gnathostomes, so the vertebrate eye was complete in the common ancestor of all living vertebrates; hagfish eyes are degenerate, without lens, iris or eye muscles.

### 68.4.2. Photoreceptors and Colour Vision

The retina of the earliest vertebrates already had rods and four classes of cones, with the visual pigments (opsins) LWS (red), RH2 (green), SWS2 (blue) and SWS1 (violet or ultraviolet), and lampreys have this set ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.4); [7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.5)). Most teleosts, reptiles and birds retain four cone types and are tetrachromatic; birds and turtles add coloured oil droplets in their cones, which filter light and sharpen colour discrimination, and double cones, used for luminance and motion vision. Mammals passed through a long nocturnal phase in the Mesozoic, during which they lost the RH2 and SWS2 opsins and the oil droplets; most mammals are therefore dichromatic, with only LWS and SWS1 cones, and their retinas are rod-dominated. Old World primates (catarrhines) regained trichromacy by a duplication of the LWS gene on the X chromosome, which produced separate red and green pigments; in many New World monkeys trichromacy depends on allelic variation of a single X-linked gene, so that only heterozygous females are trichromats. Deep-sea fishes have rod-only retinas with pigments tuned to blue light, and several of them have multiple rod opsins; cetaceans and some nocturnal mammals have lost cone function nearly completely.

### 68.4.4. Accommodation

To focus objects at different distances an eye must change its refractive power or the distance between lens and retina ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.1)). Water and the cornea have nearly the same refractive index, so an aquatic eye loses the refraction of the cornea and must rely on a strong, nearly spherical lens; in air the cornea provides most of the refraction (about two-thirds in humans) and the lens can be flatter. Vertebrates use four mechanisms, distributed according to habitat and phylogeny.

1. *Moving the lens backward.* Teleosts have a spherical lens, set for near vision at rest; the retractor lentis muscle, at the end of the falciform process, pulls the lens back toward the retina to focus on distant objects.
2. *Moving the lens forward.* Lampreys flatten the cornea with a cornealis muscle and so push the lens back; elasmobranchs and amphibians have a protractor lentis muscle that pulls the lens forward for near vision (in elasmobranchs the eye at rest is set for distance). Snakes, whose ciliary muscle has been lost, accommodate by contracting muscles at the base of the iris, which raises the pressure of the vitreous and pushes the lens forward. This complete reinvention of accommodation is one of the arguments that snakes passed through a burrowing or nocturnal ancestry in which the eye was reduced.
3. *Changing the curvature of the lens by squeezing.* Lizards, turtles, crocodylians and birds have a soft lens and a striated ciliary muscle that squeezes the lens equator through an annular pad, increasing the curvature of the front surface. Birds and some reptiles also change the curvature of the cornea with a separate corneal muscle (Crampton's muscle), and diving birds such as cormorants and diving ducks accommodate strongly by squeezing the lens through the iris, which compensates for the loss of corneal refraction under water.
4. *Changing the curvature of the lens by relaxing it.* In mammals the lens is held by zonular fibres attached to the ciliary body. At rest the zonule is taut and the lens is flattened for distance; contraction of the smooth ciliary muscle relaxes the zonule and the elastic lens rounds up for near vision. Accommodation is limited or absent in many nocturnal mammals and rodents and declines with age in humans (presbyopia).

### 68.4.5. Eyelids, Glands and Eye Position

Fishes have no movable eyelids, except a nictitating membrane in some sharks; their eyes are bathed by water. Terrestrial vertebrates protect and moisten the cornea with eyelids and glands, which first appear in amphibians.

- *Eyelids and nictitating membrane.* Frogs have a fixed upper lid, a movable lower lid and a transparent nictitating membrane folded up from the lower lid; when the eye is retracted by the retractor bulbi, the nictitating membrane covers it. Reptiles and birds have upper and lower lids (in birds the lower usually closes the eye) and a nictitating membrane that sweeps across the eye horizontally from the inner corner, pulled by the pyramidalis and quadratus muscles ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.4.2)). Snakes and many geckos have lost movable lids; their eye is covered by a transparent fused lid, the spectacle (brille), shed with each moult, which makes them briefly blind before shedding. Mammals have muscular upper and lower lids with eyelashes; the nictitating membrane is reduced to a fold (the plica semilunaris in humans) except in some carnivores and ungulates.
- *Glands.* The Harderian gland (at the inner corner, lubricating the nictitating membrane) appears in amphibians and is well developed in reptiles, birds and many mammals (rodents, rabbits), reduced in primates. Lacrimal glands (at the outer corner, secreting tears) appear in reptiles; sea turtles use them as salt glands ([7.17.66](/7.17.66#sec-66.4.3)). Mammals add the tarsal (Meibomian) glands of the lids, which secrete an oily film. A nasolacrimal duct drains the tears into the nasal cavity in tetrapods; in squamates the tears drain to the vomeronasal organ.
- *Position.* Eyes on the sides of the head give a wide field of view (almost 360° in rabbits and woodcocks); forward-facing eyes give binocular overlap and depth perception, in predators and arboreal animals (owls, cats, primates). The optic nerves of mammals with binocular vision cross only partially at the optic chiasm.

## 68.5. The Pineal and Parietal Eyes

The roof of the diencephalon of many vertebrates carries photoreceptive median organs ([7.17.67](/7.17.67#sec-67.3.4)). Lampreys have both a pineal and a parapineal organ with photoreceptors; teleosts and amphibians a photosensitive pineal organ (in frogs a frontal organ beneath the skin of the head); and many lizards and the tuatara a well developed *parietal eye*, derived from the parapineal organ, which lies in the parietal foramen of the skull roof beneath a transparent scale and has a lens, a retina with photoreceptors and a nerve to the brain. The parietal eye detects light intensity and the colour balance of skylight (its photoreceptors contain both a blue-sensitive and a green-sensitive opsin with antagonistic effects) and helps regulate exposure to the sun and daily rhythms. A parietal foramen is widespread in fossil early tetrapods, early amniotes and therapsids, and was lost in many lineages, including crocodylians, birds and mammals ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)). Birds and mammals keep the pineal organ as an endocrine gland that secretes melatonin ([7.17.67](/7.17.67#sec-67.7.5)).

## 68.6. The Chemical Senses

### 68.6.1. Olfaction

The olfactory organ develops from the olfactory placodes, which invaginate into olfactory pits lined with the **olfactory epithelium** of ciliated receptor neurons; their axons form the olfactory nerve to the olfactory bulb ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.1)). In fishes each olfactory sac opens to the outside only, through incurrent and excurrent nostrils, and water flows through it; the epithelium forms folds (a rosette) that enlarge the surface. Cyclostomes have a single median nostril; hagfishes have a nasopharyngeal duct opening into the pharynx through which they breathe, lampreys a blind nasohypophyseal sac. In sarcopterygians the excurrent nostril moved into the mouth as the **choana** ([7.17.48](/7.17.48)), and in tetrapods the nasal cavity became part of the airway.

In amniotes the nasal cavity contains turbinals (conchae), scroll-like bones that carry the olfactory epithelium on their posterior parts (ethmoturbinals) and, in mammals and birds, the respiratory epithelium on the anterior ones ([7.17.64](/7.17.64#sec-64.6)). The extent of the olfactory epithelium varies with the importance of smell: large in rodents, carnivores and ungulates, small in primates and most birds, and absent in toothed whales, in parallel with the size of the olfactory bulbs ([7.17.67](/7.17.67#sec-67.3.6)). The number of functional olfactory receptor genes follows the same pattern: more than 1,000 in mice and rats, about 2,000 in African elephants, about 400 in humans and very few in dolphins ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.3.3)).

### 68.6.2. The Vomeronasal Organ

The **vomeronasal organ** (Jacobson's organ) is a second olfactory organ in the anterior roof of the mouth or floor of the nasal cavity, lined with receptor neurons whose axons project to the accessory olfactory bulb ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.3)). It detects pheromones and prey odours and appears first in amphibians (as a diverticulum of the nasal cavity), which suggests that it evolved with life on land, although homologous receptor genes and cells occur in the olfactory epithelium of fishes.

In squamates the vomeronasal organ is a pair of domed chambers above the palate, opening into the mouth by ducts and separate from the nasal cavity. The forked tongue of snakes and monitor lizards collects odour particles from the air and the ground; the tips are then pressed against the openings of the organs, or the particles are transferred by pads on the floor of the mouth. Because each tip samples a different point, the forked tongue allows a snake to compare the concentrations on the two sides and to follow a scent trail (tropotaxis). The vomeronasal organ is well developed in most mammals (rodents, carnivores, ungulates; male ungulates perform flehmen, curling the upper lip to draw pheromones into the organ), and absent or vestigial in birds, crocodylians, catarrhine primates (Old World monkeys, apes and humans, in which the receptor genes are pseudogenes) and cetaceans. Chinese textbooks state that adult mammals lack the organ; this is true only of these groups.

### 68.6.3. Taste

**Taste buds** are clusters of elongated receptor cells (not neurons) and supporting cells, which develop from the local epithelium (ectoderm or endoderm) and are innervated by the facial, glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.4)). They occur in all vertebrates. In fishes they lie in the mouth and pharynx and, in many species, over the lips, barbels, fins and the whole body surface; a catfish is a "swimming tongue" with up to 200,000 taste buds outside the mouth, supplied by the facial nerve ([7.17.67](/7.17.67#sec-67.3.1)). Amphibians have taste buds on the tongue, palate and pharynx; reptiles and birds few, mainly on the palate and the base of the tongue (chickens about 300). Mammals have most of their taste buds on the papillae of the tongue (fungiform, foliate and circumvallate) and on the soft palate and epiglottis ([7.17.63](/7.17.63#sec-63.4.2)). Taste receptor genes have been lost in lineages with specialized diets: cats and other carnivores lack a functional sweet receptor, many birds lack it but hummingbirds re-evolved sweet taste from the umami receptor, and whales and dolphins have lost most taste receptors.

## 68.7. Other Senses

- *Infrared detection.* Pit vipers (Crotalinae) have a pair of loreal pit organs between the eye and nostril, each a cavity divided by a thin membrane, about $15\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ thick, densely supplied by trigeminal nerve endings that express the heat-sensitive channel TRPA1; boas and pythons have labial pits along the lips. The pits form a coarse infrared image of warm prey, projected to the optic tectum in register with the visual map ([7.17.67](/7.17.67#sec-67.3.7)). Vampire bats have infrared-sensitive nose pits based on TRPV1; the two snake lineages evolved their pits independently.
- *Magnetoreception.* Migratory birds, sea turtles, salmon and many other vertebrates orient with the geomagnetic field (**magnetoreception**; [7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5.4)). Two mechanisms are proposed: a light-dependent radical-pair mechanism in cryptochrome proteins of the retina, supported by the dependence of bird orientation on light and on visual brain areas, and a mechanism based on magnetite crystals, whose putative receptor cells in the beak of pigeons turned out to be macrophages. No magnetoreceptive organ has yet been identified anatomically.
- *Echolocation.* Bats and toothed whales locate objects by the echoes of their own calls ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.5)). The anatomical adaptations lie in the sound-producing organs (the larynx of bats, the nasal "phonic lips" and fatty melon of toothed whales), in the ears (enlarged cochleae, ears acoustically isolated from the skull in toothed whales, which receive sound through the fat-filled lower jaw) and in the auditory brain.

## Summary

- Cutaneous receptors (free endings, Merkel, Pacinian, Herbst, Meissner corpuscles) are general; specialized tactile organs evolved in barbels, bills, snouts and vibrissae.
- The lateral line of neuromasts and the ampullary electroreceptors are ancestral sense organs of aquatic vertebrates, lost on land. Electroreception was lost in neopterygians and reinvented in teleosts and monotremes.
- The inner ear has one or two semicircular canals in cyclostomes and three in gnathostomes. Fishes hear with their otolithic organs, aided in otophysans by the Weberian apparatus; tetrapods added auditory papillae, which became the cochlear duct of archosaurs and the coiled cochlea of therian mammals.
- The tympanic middle ear evolved independently in frogs, lepidosaurs, archosaurs, turtles and mammals. Non-mammals have one ossicle, the columella (stapes, from the hyomandibula); mammals have malleus (articular), incus (quadrate) and stapes, with the tympanic bone (angular), and can hear high frequencies.
- The camera eye with an inverted retina was complete in the first vertebrates. Accommodation is achieved by moving the lens back (teleosts) or forward (elasmobranchs, amphibians, snakes), by squeezing a soft lens with striated muscles (reptiles, birds) or by relaxing the zonule of an elastic lens (mammals). Accessory structures (tapetum, falciform process, conus papillaris, pecten, scleral ossicles, eyelids and glands) reflect habitat and lineage.
- The pineal and parietal eyes are photoreceptive in non-mammals; the parietal eye persists in lizards and the tuatara.
- Olfaction moved into the airway with the choana; the vomeronasal organ appears in amphibians and is elaborated in squamates (forked tongue) and many mammals; taste buds are ancient and widespread, and taste genes are lost with diet.

## Questions

1. Why do fishes without a swim-bladder connection to the ear hear only low frequencies, and how does the Weberian apparatus extend their range?

   **Answer** A fish's tissues have nearly the density of water and move with the particle motion of a sound wave; the denser otoliths lag behind, so the hair cells detect particle motion, which is significant only near the source and at low frequencies. Sound pressure, which carries information over larger distances and higher frequencies, compresses a gas bubble far more than tissue. The Weberian apparatus couples the swim bladder, which vibrates with sound pressure, through a chain of modified vertebral bones to the perilymph of the inner ear, so that pressure is converted into motion of the endolymph and hair cells; otophysans hear up to several kilohertz with better sensitivity.

2. Trace each of the three mammalian ear ossicles and the tympanic bone back to its precursor in the jaw or pharyngeal arches of non-mammals.

   **Answer** The stapes is the columella of amphibians and reptiles and the hyomandibula of fishes, the dorsal element of the second (hyoid) arch. The incus is the quadrate, the ossified posterior end of the palatoquadrate of the first (mandibular) arch, which formed the upper jaw joint. The malleus is the articular, the ossified posterior end of Meckel's cartilage, the lower part of the first arch, which formed the lower jaw joint. The tympanic bone is the angular, a dermal bone of the lower jaw. Embryology (malleus and incus from Meckel's cartilage and palatoquadrate) and fossils (postdentary bones attached to the jaw by ossified Meckel's cartilage in early mammals) support these homologies.

3. Explain why the lens of a fish is spherical and why the fish accommodates by moving the lens, while a bird changes its lens shape.

   **Answer** The cornea has almost the same refractive index as water, so in water it provides no refraction; all refraction must be done by the lens, which must be strongly curved, spherical and of high refractive index. A spherical lens cannot easily be made more or less curved, so fishes focus by changing its distance from the retina (teleosts pull it back for distance). In air the cornea provides most of the refraction, and the lens needs only to fine-tune; birds have a soft lens and striated ciliary muscles that change its curvature rapidly (and a corneal muscle), which suits fast focusing in flight.

4. Give evidence that snakes reinvented accommodation and discuss what this implies about their history.

   **Answer** Lizards accommodate by squeezing the lens with a ciliary muscle, as do other reptiles and birds; snakes lack a ciliary muscle and instead push the lens forward by raising vitreous pressure with muscles at the iris root, a unique mechanism. Snakes also lack scleral ossicles, have a spectacle instead of movable lids and have retinal and lens features unlike lizards. Because snakes are nested within lizards, these differences indicate that the ancestral lizard eye was reduced in the snake lineage, consistent with a burrowing or nocturnal phase, and that a new mechanism of accommodation was built when vision became important again.

5. How does the forked tongue of a snake help it follow a scent trail?

   **Answer** The two tips of the tongue sample odour molecules at two points separated by several millimetres. They deliver the samples to the left and right vomeronasal organs (or ducts), so the brain can compare the concentrations on the two sides at each tongue-flick. If one side receives more scent, the snake turns toward it; this comparison (tropotaxis) allows it to stay on a trail left by prey or a mate.

6. Lateral line and electroreceptors were lost in amniotes. Which amniotes have evolved functionally similar senses, and how?

   **Answer** Pinnipeds use their vibrissae to detect hydrodynamic trails left by swimming fish, a mechanical analogue of the lateral line based on hair follicles. Monotremes evolved electroreceptors from mucous glands in the skin of the bill or snout, innervated by the trigeminal nerve, which detect prey muscle fields; the Guiana dolphin has electroreceptive pits derived from vibrissal follicles. Crocodylians have integumentary sense organs on the jaws that detect ripples on the water surface. These are convergent solutions using skin structures and nerves other than the lateral line placodes and nerves.
