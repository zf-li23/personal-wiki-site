Excretion and reproduction are functionally unrelated, but in vertebrates they share their embryonic origin, their position and, in large part, their ducts, so that anatomists treat them together as the urogenital system. Kidneys and gonads both arise from the mesoderm beside the dorsal mesentery, and the first duct of the kidney, the archinephric duct, later carries sperm in males of most vertebrates; a second duct running parallel to it becomes the oviduct of females. The history of the urogenital system is therefore a history of ducts changing owners: as new kidneys replaced old ones, ducts that had drained urine were taken over by the reproductive system, and new ducts were added for urine.

This chapter begins with the embryonic source of kidneys and gonads (§66.1), then follows the kidney through its three generations (§66.2), the structure of nephrons and kidneys (§66.3), and the bladder, excretory products and salt glands (§66.4). The reproductive part covers the gonads (§66.5), the genital ducts and their homologies (§66.6) and the accessory glands and copulatory organs (§66.7); §66.8 compares the classes. The physiology of renal function and osmoregulation is in [7.14.21](/7.14.21)–[7.14.24](/7.14.24), reproductive physiology and placentation in [7.14.37](/7.14.37)–[7.14.38](/7.14.38), and the development of the kidney and gonads, including sex determination, in [7.5.25](/7.5.25)–[7.5.27](/7.5.27). The cloaca, in which the ducts end in most vertebrates, was described in [7.17.63](/7.17.63#sec-63.8); invertebrate excretory and reproductive organs are compared in [7.17.44](/7.17.44).

## 66.1. Origin of the Urogenital System

The mesoderm on each side of the neural tube divides into paraxial mesoderm (somites), intermediate mesoderm and lateral plate ([7.17.63](/7.17.63#sec-63.1.1)). The intermediate mesoderm forms a longitudinal cord, the nephrogenic cord, in which the kidneys develop; in the head and anterior trunk it is segmented into nephrotomes, one per somite, and further back it is unsegmented. Medial to it, the coelomic epithelium thickens into the genital ridges, which are colonized by primordial germ cells migrating from far away (the endoderm of the yolk sac in mammals, the hindgut region in birds and others; [7.5.27](/7.5.27#sec-27.4)) and form the gonads. Kidney and gonad therefore lie side by side on a common urogenital ridge that bulges into the dorsal body cavity on each side of the mesentery, covered by peritoneum.

In the primitive condition each nephrotome contains a small coelomic cavity (the nephrocoel) connected with the general coelom by a ciliated funnel, the nephrostome, and the tubule of the nephrotome runs laterally to join a longitudinal duct. Such a coelom-connected tubule resembles the **coelomoducts** and the metanephridia of invertebrates, which also drain fluid from the coelom ([7.17.44](/7.17.44)); whether the vertebrate kidney tubules are homologous with them is uncertain.

## 66.2. The Kidney Series

### 66.2.1. Nephrons and the Change from Coelomic to Vascular Filtration

The functional unit of a vertebrate kidney is the **nephron**: a renal corpuscle, in which a tuft of capillaries (the glomerulus) is invaginated into the double-walled Bowman's capsule, followed by a long tubule that modifies the filtrate by reabsorption and secretion ([7.14.22](/7.14.22#sec-22.3)). The inner wall of the capsule consists of **podocytes**, cells whose interdigitating foot processes form the filtration barrier, the same cell type that forms the filtration sites of invertebrate excretory organs. In the most primitive nephrons the glomerulus is not enclosed in a capsule but projects into the coelom or the nephrocoel (an *external glomerulus*), and the filtrate enters the tubule through the nephrostome from the body cavity: filtration is coelomic. In advanced nephrons the glomerulus is inside the capsule (an *internal glomerulus*), the nephrostome is lost and filtrate passes directly from blood into the tubule: filtration is vascular.

The trend across the kidney generations runs in this direction: from segmental to non-segmental arrangement, from few to many nephrons, from nephrostomes to their loss, and from external to internal glomeruli; at the same time the kidney develops progressively further back in the body.

### 66.2.2. Three Generations of Kidney

Vertebrate kidneys develop from front to back in waves along the nephrogenic cord, and the comparison of embryos and adults across the classes distinguishes three generations ([7.5.25](/7.5.25#sec-25.2)). They are not three separate organs but regions of one continuous cord that become active at different times.

- *Holonephros.* A hypothetical ancestral kidney extending along the whole length of the coelom, with one segmental nephron per segment, each with a nephrostome, all draining into one archinephric duct. The larvae of hagfishes and caecilians have kidneys that come close to this pattern, which is the starting point from which the other kidneys are thought to have differentiated.
- **Pronephros**. The anterior part of the cord. Its few nephrons (often 2–7 pairs) have nephrostomes and external glomeruli or one combined glomus, and drain into the archinephric duct, which forms first in this region and grows backward to the cloaca. The pronephros is the functional kidney of the larvae of lampreys, teleosts and amphibians, and the adult kidney of hagfishes (together with a posterior kidney) and a few teleosts; in amniotes it is rudimentary and non-functional in the embryo.
- **Mesonephros**. The middle part of the cord. Its nephrons are more numerous, not strictly segmental, and have internal glomeruli; they drain into the same archinephric duct, which is then called the mesonephric (Wolffian) duct. It is the functional embryonic kidney of amniotes (with a short life in mammals) and, extended backward, the adult kidney of anamniotes.
- **Metanephros**. The posterior part of the cord, which in amniotes forms a compact kidney with up to millions of nephrons, without nephrostomes. It is induced by the ureteric bud, a new outgrowth of the posterior archinephric duct (**ureteric bud**), which branches within the metanephric mesenchyme to form the collecting system and is drained by a new duct, the ureter ([7.5.25](/7.5.25#sec-25.3)).

In anamniotes the adult kidney is formed by the mesonephric region and the region behind it, without a separate metanephros; this extended mesonephros is called the *opisthonephros* ("posterior kidney"). It is drained by the archinephric duct, sometimes supplemented by accessory ducts. Its anterior part is often modified in males to carry sperm (§66.6), and its posterior part does most of the excretion. In amniotes the metanephros is the adult kidney, and the mesonephros is taken over by the male reproductive system as the epididymis.

### 66.2.3. The Kidneys of the Classes

| | Embryo or larva | Adult kidney | Urinary duct of the adult |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hagfishes | Pronephros (larva close to a holonephros) | Pronephros (persists anteriorly) and opisthonephros of segmental glomerular units | Archinephric duct |
| Lampreys | Pronephros (functional in the ammocoete) | Opisthonephros | Archinephric duct |
| Chondrichthyans | Pronephros | Opisthonephros; the anterior part serves the reproductive tract in males | Archinephric duct in females; accessory urinary ducts in males |
| Teleosts | Pronephros (functional in larvae; the "head kidney" persists as lymphoid and endocrine tissue) | Opisthonephros, often fused into one organ along the dorsal body wall | Archinephric ducts, separate from the genital ducts |
| Amphibians | Pronephros (tadpoles) | Opisthonephros | Archinephric duct (in male salamanders anterior tubules carry sperm, a posterior part drains urine) |
| Amniotes | Pronephros (vestigial), mesonephros (functional) | Metanephros | Ureter |

Two points are worth noting. The "head kidney" of teleosts, the persisting anterior pronephric region, has no excretory function in adults but is the main haematopoietic and lymphoid organ and contains the interrenal and chromaffin tissue (the fish adrenal, [7.17.67](/7.17.67)). And the mesonephros is not equally important in all amniote embryos: it is large and functional for a long time in reptile and bird embryos and in pigs, small and short-lived in humans and rodents, where the placenta takes over excretion.

### 66.2.4. Interpreting the Sequence

The sequence pronephros → mesonephros → metanephros in amniote embryos was a favourite example of the biogenetic law: the embryo seemed to pass through the kidneys of its ancestors. The modern interpretation is more modest. The three kidneys are regions of one nephrogenic cord that differentiate in an anterior-to-posterior wave, as do the somites and many other axial structures, and the early, anterior regions are used where a small embryo or larva needs a kidney quickly. The pronephros is the larval kidney of anamniotes because their larvae hatch early and live in fresh water, where they must excrete water immediately; amniote embryos, enclosed in membranes, can rely on the mesonephros and later the metanephros. The archinephric duct, laid down by the pronephros, is reused by each subsequent kidney and finally, in amniotes, gives rise to the ureteric bud. What the sequence preserves is therefore a developmental mechanism, a duct-first, front-to-back induction of nephrons, rather than a series of ancestral adult kidneys ([7.5.25](/7.5.25#sec-25.6)).

## 66.3. Structure of Nephrons and Kidneys

### 66.3.1. Comparative Nephron Structure

All vertebrate nephrons share a renal corpuscle, a proximal tubule (reabsorbing most of the filtered salts, water, glucose and amino acids, and secreting organic ions), an intermediate segment and a distal tubule, followed by collecting ducts. They differ mainly in the size of the glomeruli and in the presence of a loop of Henle ([7.14.22](/7.14.22); [7.14.24](/7.14.24)).

- *Fishes.* Freshwater teleosts have large glomeruli and produce copious dilute urine to remove water gained by osmosis. Marine teleosts, which lose water, have small glomeruli, and about 30 species from several families (toadfishes, pipefishes and seahorses, goosefishes, some icefishes) have *aglomerular kidneys* in which urine is formed by tubular secretion alone. Marine elasmobranchs have large nephrons with complex tubules that reabsorb urea, which they retain as an osmolyte ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.5.3)).
- *Amphibians and reptiles.* Nephrons lack loops of Henle and produce urine that is at most isosmotic with plasma; amphibians conserve water by reabsorption in the bladder and skin, reptiles by excreting uric acid, which precipitates ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)).
- *Birds.* Two kinds of nephrons: numerous small *reptile-type* nephrons without loops in the cortex, and fewer *mammal-type* nephrons with loops of Henle in medullary cones, which allow birds to produce urine about twice as concentrated as plasma.
- *Mammals.* All nephrons have loops of Henle, which descend into the medulla and set up the countercurrent multiplication that concentrates urine up to 4 times (humans) and more than 20 times (desert rodents) the osmolality of plasma ([7.14.23](/7.14.23)). Long loops and a thick medulla go together with high concentrating ability: the relative medullary thickness of a kangaroo rat is more than five times that of a beaver.

**Example 66.3.1 (Loop Length and Concentrating Ability)** The loop of Henle concentrates urine by countercurrent multiplication ([7.14.23](/7.14.23)). In a simple model the medulla is divided into $n$ levels, and at each level the ascending limb pumps salt out until it maintains a transverse difference $\Delta$ (the single effect, of the order of $200\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$) between the ascending limb and the surrounding fluid, which equilibrates with the descending limb. Because the fluid flows down one limb and up the other, the transverse differences add up along the loop, and at steady state the osmolality at the tip rises approximately in proportion to the number of levels, that is to the length of the loop, $C_{\mathrm{tip}} \approx C_0 + n\Delta/2$ for small $n\Delta/C_0$. Doubling the length of the loops (and of the medulla) roughly doubles the excess osmolality that can be reached at the tip and therefore in the final urine, which is equilibrated with the papilla by water reabsorption from the collecting ducts. This is why concentrating ability correlates with the relative thickness of the medulla across mammals: desert rodents with long papillae reach $5{,}000$–$9{,}000\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$, humans about $1{,}200$, beavers and other aquatic mammals with short loops only about $500$. Birds, with few and short loops in medullary cones, reach about twice plasma osmolality, and reptiles without loops cannot exceed it.

### 66.3.2. The Mammalian Kidney

The metanephric kidney of mammals is bean-shaped and divided into an outer *cortex*, with the renal corpuscles and convoluted tubules, and an inner *medulla*, with the loops of Henle and collecting ducts arranged in conical renal pyramids whose tips (papillae) open into the renal pelvis, the funnel-shaped upper end of the ureter. Kidneys of small mammals (rodents, rabbits, carnivores) are unipyramidal, with one papilla. Kidneys of humans and pigs have many pyramids (multipyramidal) but a smooth surface. In cattle the kidney is lobulated externally, and in whales, seals, otters and bears it is divided into hundreds of small units, the renculi, each a miniature kidney with cortex, medulla and calyx; renculi are an adaptation of large animals, perhaps keeping diffusion and tubule lengths short. A human kidney has about a million nephrons.

The kidneys of non-mammalian amniotes are lobulated: reptile kidneys are elongated or lobed organs in the posterior body cavity, bird kidneys lie in depressions of the synsacrum and are divided into three divisions (cranial, middle, caudal) with many lobules, each with cortical and medullary parts.

## 66.4. Ureters, Bladder and Extrarenal Excretion

### 66.4.1. Urinary Ducts and Bladders

The urine of anamniotes leaves through the archinephric ducts, that of amniotes through the ureters; both open into the cloaca or the urogenital sinus. A urinary bladder stores urine and, in many species, reabsorbs water. Three kinds of bladder are distinguished by their origin.

| Type | Origin | Found in |
|---|---|---|
| Duct (tubal) bladder | Dilation of the fused posterior ends of the archinephric ducts | Many teleosts |
| Cloacal bladder | Ventral outgrowth of the cloaca | Lungfishes, amphibians, monotremes |
| Allantoic bladder | Base of the embryonic allantois, a ventral outgrowth of the cloaca or hindgut | Turtles, tuatara, most lizards, therian mammals |

The cloacal and allantoic bladders are probably homologous (both derive from the ventral wall of the cloacal region), the distinction reflecting whether an allantois develops. Snakes, crocodylians and almost all birds (except ostriches and rheas, which have a urine-storing coprodeum) have no bladder; urine flows from the ureters into the cloaca, where water is reabsorbed. Frogs use their bladder as a water reservoir: some desert frogs store water amounting to 30 % of their body mass and can reabsorb it under the control of antidiuretic hormone (arginine vasotocin) during drought ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)). In therian mammals the ureters open into the bladder and the urethra drains it; in monotremes the ureters open into the urogenital sinus and urine flows back into the bladder.

### 66.4.2. Excretory Products

Vertebrates excrete nitrogen as ammonia, urea or uric acid, and the choice depends on the availability of water and on the developmental environment ([7.14.21](/7.14.21#sec-21.4)). Teleosts and aquatic amphibian larvae are **ammonotelic**, excreting ammonia mainly across the gills. Elasmobranchs, adult amphibians, mammals and many turtles are **ureotelic**; elasmobranchs and the coelacanth retain urea in their blood as an osmolyte. Squamates, crocodylians (which excrete both ammonia and uric acid) and birds are **uricotelic**: uric acid is excreted as a paste with little water, a precondition for the cleidoic egg, in which waste accumulates without poisoning the embryo ([7.17.50](/7.17.50)). Some amphibians are exceptions: the arboreal frogs *Phyllomedusa* and *Chiromantis* excrete uric acid, and the aquatic African clawed frog *Xenopus* is largely ammonotelic.

### 66.4.3. Extrarenal Salt Excretion

Vertebrates that live in or drink sea water, and many that eat salty food, cannot excrete all excess salt through the kidney, because no non-mammalian kidney produces urine saltier than plasma. They have evolved salt-secreting organs independently many times.

| Organ | Animals | Origin | Secretion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionocytes (chloride cells) of the gills | Marine teleosts (and freshwater teleosts, which take up ions) | Gill epithelium | NaCl, actively |
| **Rectal gland** | Elasmobranchs, chimaeras | Diverticulum of the rectum | NaCl solution isosmotic with plasma |
| Nasal salt glands | Marine birds (petrels, gulls, penguins, pelicans), lizards (marine iguana, many desert lizards) | Lateral nasal gland (birds, lizards) | NaCl (birds), KCl and NaCl (lizards) |
| Lachrymal salt glands | Sea turtles | Orbital gland | NaCl ("tears") |
| Sublingual salt glands | Sea snakes, file snakes | Posterior sublingual gland | NaCl |
| Lingual salt glands | Saltwater crocodiles | Glands of the tongue | NaCl |
| Sweat glands | Mammals | Skin | Mainly thermoregulatory; salt loss rather than salt excretion |

The diversity of salt glands in reptiles, which derive from different glands of the head in different lineages, shows that the same physiological solution, a secretory epithelium with Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and the Na⁺–K⁺–2Cl⁻ cotransporter, was built repeatedly from available glands ([7.14.21](/7.14.21#sec-21.5)). Marine mammals have no salt glands; they produce concentrated urine.

## 66.5. The Gonads

### 66.5.1. Development and Structure

The gonads develop from the genital ridges as bipotential organs that become testes or ovaries according to the sex-determining signals ([7.5.26](/7.5.26)): genetic in mammals (*SRY*), birds (dosage of *DMRT1* on the Z chromosome) and many others, temperature-dependent in crocodylians, many turtles and some lizards, and social or environmental in many fishes. In the bipotential gonad the medulla gives rise to the testis cords, the cortex to the ovarian follicles.

The *testes* produce sperm in seminiferous tubules (in amniotes) or in cysts or lobules (in anamniotes), with Sertoli (supporting) cells and androgen-secreting interstitial (Leydig) cells. In most vertebrates sperm leave the testis through efferent ductules, derived from anterior mesonephric tubules, into the archinephric duct (§66.6). Teleosts are an exception: their testes are drained by a sperm duct that develops from the gonad itself, not from the kidney.

The *ovaries* produce eggs in follicles. They are solid in amniotes and most fishes, hollow and sac-like in amphibians and some teleosts, whose eggs are shed into an internal cavity. In teleosts the ovarian cavity continues into an oviduct formed by the ovary wall (a "cystovarian" ovary), not a Müllerian duct; in salmonids and a few others the eggs are shed into the body cavity and leave through genital pores. Cyclostomes have a single median gonad and no genital ducts; gametes are shed into the coelom and leave through abdominal pores into the urogenital sinus.

### 66.5.2. Asymmetry and Descent

Gonads are paired in most vertebrates but often asymmetric. Most birds have only a left functional ovary and oviduct; the right ovary regresses in the embryo, though it persists in some raptors and kiwis. In platypuses only the left ovary is functional; one ovary is reduced in many bats and in some sharks (only the right ovary develops in some catsharks), and cyclostomes have a single median gonad. The testes are paired in all jawed vertebrates and usually symmetrical, though the left testis of birds is often larger.

The testes of many mammals descend from their abdominal position into a scrotum, a pouch of skin outside the abdominal cavity, where they are kept a few degrees cooler than the body core; the *gubernaculum*, a ligament from the testis to the inguinal region, guides the descent. The testes remain in the abdomen (testicond mammals) in monotremes, afrotherians (elephants, hyraxes, tenrecs, golden moles), xenarthrans, cetaceans and some others, and are scrotal in marsupials (which have a scrotum in front of the penis) and most boreoeutherians. Why spermatogenesis in scrotal mammals requires lower temperatures is not settled; the scrotum may be a secondary adaptation (to galloping, which pressurizes the abdomen, or to signalling), with the temperature dependence evolving afterward. Ovaries also descend in mammals, but only into the pelvis.

### 66.5.3. Hermaphroditism and Sex Change

Most vertebrates are gonochoristic, but several hundred species of teleosts in about 27 families are hermaphrodites ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)). *Simultaneous* hermaphrodites produce eggs and sperm at the same time: some sea basses (*Serranus*, *Hypoplectrus*) and the mangrove rivulus *Kryptolebias marmoratus*, which fertilizes itself. *Sequential* hermaphrodites change sex during life. *Protogynous* species start as females and change to males when large, as in wrasses, parrotfishes, groupers and the rice-field eel *Monopterus albus* (a common food fish in China, all of which are females when young). *Protandrous* species start as males and become females, as in anemonefishes (*Amphiprion*) and black porgies, where the largest individual of a group is female. The direction of change fits the size-advantage model: the sex that gains more from large size is the later one, males in species where large males monopolize mating, females where fecundity rises with size and mating is random.

Amphibians and amniotes do not change sex naturally, but traces of the other sex's tissue exist. Male toads (Bufonidae) have a Bidder's organ, a rudimentary ovary at the anterior end of each testis, which develops into a functional ovary if the testes are removed. In birds, the regressed right gonad of females can develop into a testis-like organ if the left ovary is destroyed by disease, which is the explanation of the historical records of hens that began to crow and grow combs.

## 66.6. Genital Ducts

### 66.6.1. Male Ducts: The Archinephric Duct Becomes the Vas Deferens

In males of all jawed vertebrates except teleosts, sperm pass from the testis through efferent ductules into the archinephric duct, which therefore serves as a sperm duct.

- *Elasmobranchs.* The anterior part of the opisthonephros (Leydig's gland) is modified to secrete seminal fluid, and its tubules and the archinephric duct (a convoluted ductus deferens ending in a seminal vesicle and sperm sac) carry sperm; urine from the posterior kidney is drained by accessory urinary ducts.
- *Amphibians.* The archinephric duct carries both urine and sperm in frogs; in salamanders the anterior tubules carry sperm and a posterior kidney portion with its own collecting ducts carries urine.
- *Amniotes.* With the metanephros and ureter taking over excretion, the archinephric duct (now the **Wolffian duct**) is used only for sperm: its anterior end with the mesonephric tubules becomes the epididymis, coiled and long (up to 6 m in humans), where sperm mature and are stored, and the rest becomes the vas deferens (ductus deferens), which in mammals ends in the urethra; the seminal vesicle buds from its distal end.

The use of a kidney duct for sperm explains why the testes are connected to the kidney in vertebrates, and why teleosts, whose sperm ducts are new structures, show a different arrangement.

### 66.6.2. Female Ducts: The Müllerian Duct

The oviduct of jawed vertebrates (except teleosts) is the **Müllerian duct** (paramesonephric duct), a second duct parallel to the archinephric duct. In elasmobranchs it forms by longitudinal splitting of the archinephric duct, in other gnathostomes from a groove of the coelomic epithelium beside the archinephric duct, which guides its growth backward. Its anterior end remains open to the coelom as a funnel, the ostium (infundibulum), which catches eggs released from the ovary into the body cavity; the egg is not shed directly into the duct, since ovary and oviduct are not connected.

Along its length the Müllerian duct differentiates according to the mode of reproduction ([7.14.38](/7.14.38)).

- *Elasmobranchs.* The two oviducts share one anterior ostium; each has a shell gland (nidamental gland), which secretes the egg case in oviparous species, and a posterior uterus, in which the embryos develop in viviparous species (with yolk-sac or uterine nutrition, or a yolk-sac placenta; [7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.5.4)).
- *Amphibians.* The oviducts are long and coiled; their glandular walls coat the eggs with jelly layers. A few live-bearing salamanders and caecilians retain embryos in the posterior oviduct, where caecilian fetuses scrape the lining with special fetal teeth.
- *Reptiles.* The oviducts have an infundibulum, a glandular uterus that secretes the shell membranes and shell, and a vagina opening into the cloaca; viviparous squamates (about 20 % of species) form placentae in the uterus.
- *Birds.* Only the left oviduct is usually functional. It has five regions in sequence: infundibulum (fertilization and the chalazae), magnum (albumen), isthmus (shell membranes), shell gland or uterus (shell and pigment, during about 20 hours) and vagina, opening into the urodeum of the cloaca.
- *Mammals.* Each Müllerian duct becomes an oviduct (uterine tube, Fallopian tube) and a uterus, and their fused posterior ends a vagina. Monotremes have separate uteri that secrete the shell and open into the urogenital sinus. Marsupials have two uteri, two lateral vaginae (through which sperm ascend) and, in many species, a median vaginal canal (pseudovaginal canal) through which the young are born.

The degree of fusion of the two Müllerian ducts in placental mammals defines the types of uterus.

| Uterus type | Structure | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Duplex | Two separate uteri, each with its own cervix, opening into one vagina | Rabbits, many rodents, elephants, aardvarks |
| Bipartite | Uteri fused posteriorly into a short body with one cervix; a median septum persists | Many carnivores (cats), horses, pigs in some accounts |
| Bicornuate | A common body with one cervix and two long horns | Ruminants, pigs, dogs, insectivores, bats, whales |
| Simplex | A single uterine body, no horns | Primates (including humans), armadillos, sloths |

The assignment of species to the bipartite and bicornuate types varies among textbooks, since the two types grade into each other. Long horns go with multiple young carried in a row (litter-bearing species); a simplex uterus with one large fetus. In female amniotes the Wolffian duct regresses, leaving remnants (epoophoron and paroophoron near the ovary, Gartner's duct in the wall of the vagina); in males the Müllerian duct regresses under anti-Müllerian hormone from the Sertoli cells, leaving the appendix testis and the prostatic utricle.

### 66.6.3. Reproductive Modes and the Female Tract

The anatomy of the female tract reflects where the embryo develops and how it is nourished ([7.14.38](/7.14.38)). *Oviparous* animals lay eggs; *viviparous* animals give birth to young that develop in the female tract. Within viviparity, *lecithotrophy* (nutrition from the yolk only) and *matrotrophy* (nutrition supplied by the mother during gestation) are the endpoints of a continuum. Chinese textbooks distinguish ovoviviparity (eggs retained and hatched in the female without maternal nutrition), "pseudo-viviparity" and "true viviparity" (with a placenta); current usage replaces these with the combinations of viviparity and lecithotrophy or matrotrophy, because retained eggs usually receive at least water, gases and some nutrients from the mother, so that a sharp boundary between "ovoviviparity" and viviparity does not exist.

Viviparity has evolved about 150 times among vertebrates, more than 100 times in squamates alone, and only once in mammals (in therians). The organs of maternal nutrition differ accordingly:

| Mode | Structure | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Uterine milk (histotrophy) | Uterine secretions absorbed by the embryo, through villi (trophonemata) in the uterine wall | Stingrays and other batoids |
| Oophagy and intrauterine cannibalism | Embryos eat unfertilized eggs or their siblings | Lamnid sharks; sand tiger shark |
| Yolk-sac placenta | Yolk sac attached to the uterine wall | Requiem and hammerhead sharks |
| Oviductal feeding | Fetuses scrape the lining with fetal teeth | Viviparous caecilians |
| Ovarian gestation | Embryos develop in the ovarian cavity, nourished through follicles or trophotaeniae | Viviparous teleosts (surfperches, goodeids) |
| Chorioallantoic placenta | Extraembryonic membranes apposed to the uterine wall | Viviparous squamates (*Chalcides*, *Mabuya* with complex placentae), therian mammals ([7.14.38](/7.14.38#sec-38.9)) |

In teleosts the uterus is absent, since their oviduct is not Müllerian: the embryos develop in the ovary itself, and some species (goodeids) feed their embryos through ribbon-like trophotaeniae, extensions of the embryonic gut. The Müllerian uterus was thus not a precondition for viviparity, which used whatever chamber was available.

## 66.7. Accessory Glands and Copulatory Organs

### 66.7.1. Accessory Glands

Accessory sex glands add secretions to the sperm or eggs. In male mammals they are the seminal vesicles (from the vas deferens), the prostate (from the urethra), and the bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands; their secretions activate and nourish sperm and form a copulatory plug in rodents. Their presence varies: dogs have only a prostate, cetaceans lack seminal vesicles, and monotremes and marsupials lack seminal vesicles. Male elasmobranchs have Leydig's gland (the modified anterior kidney) and siphon sacs that flush sperm along the clasper. Female birds have sperm-storage tubules at the junction of uterus and vagina, which keep sperm alive for days to weeks; many reptiles, including turtles and snakes, store sperm for months or years.

### 66.7.2. Copulatory Organs

Internal fertilization evolved many times among vertebrates, and the copulatory organs that transfer sperm have different origins in different lineages.

| Organ | Animals | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| Claspers (myxopterygia) | Male chondrichthyans | Modified posterior part of the pelvic fins, with a groove that guides sperm ([7.17.46](/7.17.46)) |
| Gonopodium | Live-bearing poeciliid fishes (guppies, mosquitofish), some others | Modified anal fin with elongated rays |
| Phallodeum | Caecilians | Eversible posterior part of the cloaca |
| "Tail" | Tailed frogs (*Ascaphus*) | Extension of the cloaca |
| Hemipenes | Male squamates | Paired eversible pouches at the base of the tail, beside the cloaca; grooved and often ornamented with spines |
| Phallus | Turtles, crocodylians, ratites, tinamous, ducks and geese | Unpaired erectile organ on the ventral wall of the cloaca, with a sperm groove; in ducks long, coiled and everted explosively |
| Penis | Mammals | Erectile organ formed from the genital tubercle, with corpora cavernosa and corpus spongiosum; the urethra runs through it; bifid in monotremes and many marsupials; with a bone (baculum, os penis) in rodents, carnivores, bats and many primates |

Most birds (about 97 % of species, including all Neoaves) and the tuatara have no intromittent organ and transfer sperm by cloacal contact (the "cloacal kiss"). In galliforms a phallus begins to develop in the embryo, but its growth is halted by apoptosis induced by *Bmp4* signalling, so the loss of the bird phallus occurred by a developmental modification rather than by the loss of the primordium.

Whether the copulatory organs of amniotes are homologous is still debated. Developmental studies show that the paired genital tubercles of squamates arise from the same population of cells that forms the hindlimbs, while in mammals and birds they arise from tissue nearer the tail, both under the control of signals from the cloacal endoderm and of *Hox* genes shared with limb development. The external genitalia may therefore be homologous as structures at the cloaca–limb junction but derived from different cell sources, an example of the flexibility of developmental origin.

## Summary

- Kidneys and gonads develop side by side from the intermediate mesoderm and the genital ridges; the kidney's first duct, the archinephric duct, is used for sperm in males of most gnathostomes, and the Müllerian duct forms the oviduct.
- Three kidney generations develop from front to back: the pronephros (larval kidney of anamniotes, vestigial in amniotes), the mesonephros (adult kidney of anamniotes as the opisthonephros, embryonic kidney of amniotes) and the metanephros (adult kidney of amniotes, with the ureter from the ureteric bud). Across them nephrons become more numerous, lose their nephrostomes and change from external to internal glomeruli.
- Loops of Henle occur only in mammals and in some nephrons of birds, the vertebrates that can concentrate urine; marine teleosts have small or no glomeruli, freshwater teleosts large ones.
- Bladders are duct bladders (teleosts), cloacal bladders (amphibians, monotremes) or allantoic bladders (amniotes); snakes, crocodylians and most birds have none. Salt glands evolved repeatedly (rectal gland, gill ionocytes, nasal, lachrymal, sublingual and lingual glands).
- In amniote males the mesonephros becomes the epididymis and the Wolffian duct the vas deferens; in females the Müllerian ducts become oviducts, uteri and vagina, fused to different degrees in duplex, bipartite, bicornuate and simplex uteri. Teleosts have gonoducts of gonadal origin, cyclostomes none.
- Copulatory organs evolved independently (claspers, gonopodia, phallodeum, hemipenes, phallus, penis); most birds have lost the phallus by a developmental mechanism of apoptosis.

## Questions

1. Compare the pronephros, mesonephros, opisthonephros and metanephros in position, nephron structure, duct and the vertebrates in which each is functional.

   **Answer** The pronephros lies most anteriorly, has few segmental nephrons with nephrostomes and external glomeruli, drains into the archinephric duct, and functions in larval lampreys, teleosts and amphibians and in adult hagfishes; it is vestigial in amniotes. The mesonephros lies in the middle trunk, has more numerous, non-segmental nephrons with internal glomeruli, drains into the same duct (Wolffian duct) and is the embryonic kidney of amniotes. The opisthonephros is the mesonephric region extended backward, the adult kidney of anamniotes, drained by the archinephric duct (and accessory ducts in male elasmobranchs). The metanephros is the posterior, compact kidney of adult amniotes, with very many nephrons without nephrostomes, induced by and drained through the ureter.

2. Explain why the male reproductive tract of a mammal uses a former kidney duct and what remains of the mesonephros in males and females.

   **Answer** In ancestral vertebrates the testis discharged sperm through anterior kidney tubules into the archinephric duct, which drained the kidney. When amniotes evolved a new kidney (metanephros) with a new duct (ureter), the archinephric duct was freed from excretion and kept only its reproductive role as the Wolffian duct, forming the epididymis and vas deferens, while the anterior mesonephric tubules became efferent ductules. In females the mesonephric tubules and Wolffian duct regress to vestiges: epoophoron, paroophoron and Gartner's duct.

3. Why do some marine teleosts have kidneys without glomeruli, while freshwater teleosts have large glomeruli?

   **Answer** Freshwater teleosts gain water by osmosis and must excrete large volumes of dilute urine; large glomeruli filter much fluid, from which salts are reabsorbed. Marine teleosts lose water to the hyperosmotic sea, drink sea water and must conserve water; filtering a lot would waste water, and their divalent ions can be excreted by tubular secretion. Reducing or losing glomeruli minimizes water loss, and urine is formed by secretion of ions (magnesium, sulfate) into the tubules, while monovalent salts are excreted by the gills.

4. Distinguish duplex, bipartite, bicornuate and simplex uteri and relate them to litter size.

   **Answer** They reflect increasing fusion of the two Müllerian ducts. Duplex: two separate uteri with two cervices (rabbits, rodents). Bipartite: uteri fused posteriorly into a short body with one cervix, separated internally by a septum (cats, horses). Bicornuate: a common body and two long horns (ruminants, pigs, dogs). Simplex: a single body without horns (primates, armadillos). Species with long separate horns or uteri typically carry litters of several young spaced along the horns, while the simplex uterus is associated with one or few large young.

5. Salt glands of reptiles derive from different glands in different lineages. What does this imply, and what feature do all salt-secreting organs share?

   **Answer** It implies convergent evolution: salt secretion evolved independently in sea turtles (lachrymal gland), sea snakes (sublingual gland), crocodiles (lingual glands), marine iguanas and other lizards (nasal gland), as it did in birds (nasal gland), elasmobranchs (rectal gland) and teleosts (gill ionocytes). All share a transporting epithelium with basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Na⁺–K⁺–2Cl⁻ cotransporters and an apical chloride channel, which secretes a salt solution more concentrated than plasma (isosmotic in the rectal gland).

6. (Open question) Most birds have lost the phallus, but ratites and ducks retain it. Outline how you would test whether this loss occurred once or several times.

   **Answer** Map the presence of a phallus on the bird phylogeny: it is present in palaeognaths (ratites, tinamous) and in Anseriformes, and absent in Galliformes and Neoaves; since Galloanserae contains both ducks (with) and galliforms (without), the simplest reconstruction is loss at least twice (in galliforms and in Neoaves) or once with re-evolution in ducks (unlikely). Developmental comparison helps: galliform embryos initiate a phallus that regresses by *Bmp4*-induced apoptosis; if neoavian embryos show the same early primordium and the same apoptotic mechanism, the same genetic change could have been co-opted independently, whereas different mechanisms would strongly support independent losses. Comparative genomics of the regulatory regions controlling *Bmp4* and other genes in the genital tubercle would test whether the same changes occurred in both lineages.
