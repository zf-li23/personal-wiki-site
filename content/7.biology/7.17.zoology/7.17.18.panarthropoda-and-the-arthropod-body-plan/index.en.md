The third lineage of the **Ecdysozoa** ([7.17.17](/7.17.17)) is the *Panarthropoda*: the water bears (Tardigrada), the velvet worms (Onychophora) and the arthropods. They share paired, clawed limbs on a segmented body, and in the arthropods these limbs and the body wall became jointed and hardened, a change called arthropodization. With well over a million described species, and estimates of several million more, the arthropods are by far the largest animal phylum: insects alone make up more than half of all described species of organisms.

This chapter builds the common ground for the remaining invertebrate chapters. It first describes the two small panarthropod phyla and the Cambrian fossils that show how the arthropod body plan was assembled step by step, then the arthropod body plan itself: segments and tagmata, the exoskeleton and its molting, limbs, internal organs and eyes, and the "head problem" of which segments and appendages are homologous across the subphyla. It ends with the trilobites, the phylogeny of the living arthropods and the question of why the phylum is so diverse. The four subphyla are treated in [7.17.19](/7.17.19) (Chelicerata), [7.17.20](/7.17.20) (Myriapoda), [7.17.21](/7.17.21) (Crustacea) and from [7.17.22](/7.17.22) on (Hexapoda). The physiology of arthropod muscles, circulation, tracheae, excretion, hormones and vision is in 7.14 and is linked rather than repeated.

## 18.1. Panarthropoda

### 18.1.1. What Panarthropods Share

Panarthropods are segmented, at least in the trunk, and each trunk segment carries a pair of ventrolateral limbs ending in claws. Their muscles form discrete bands rather than continuous sheets, the body cavity is a **hemocoel**, formed during development when the embryonic coelomic sacs break down and merge with the primary body cavity, and the segment-polarity gene *engrailed* is expressed in the posterior part of each forming segment in all three phyla. The degree of arthropodization separates them:

| Phylum | Described species | Limbs | Brain neuromeres | Compound eyes | Malpighian tubules |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tardigrada | about 1 300 | 4 pairs, unjointed, telescopic | 1 | no | endodermal |
| Onychophora | about 200 | 13–43 pairs, unjointed lobopods | 2 | no | none (segmental nephridia) |
| Arthropoda | over 1 000 000 | jointed, with intrinsic muscles | 3 | yes (lost in some) | in chelicerates (endodermal) and in myriapods and hexapods (ectodermal) |

Most phylogenomic analyses place onychophorans as the sister group of arthropods and tardigrades as the sister group of both, although some data sets group tardigrades with nematodes, probably because both are fast-evolving.

### 18.1.2. Phylum Tardigrada

**Definition 18.1.1 (Tardigrada)** **Tardigrada** (water bears) is a phylum of microscopic panarthropods, usually 0.1–0.5 mm long, with a body of a head and four trunk segments, each trunk segment bearing a pair of unjointed, telescopic legs ending in claws or adhesive discs; a buccal–pharyngeal apparatus with a pair of piercing stylets and a muscular sucking pharynx; a thin chitinous cuticle molted throughout life; and no circulatory or respiratory organs.

Tardigrades live in the water films on mosses, lichens and leaf litter, in fresh water and in marine sediments; moss cushions may hold more than a million per square meter. Most feed by piercing plant, algal or animal cells with their stylets and sucking out the contents, and the stylets are shed and re-formed at each molt. Two main classes are recognized: Heterotardigrada, mostly marine, with cephalic sensory cirri and often dorsal plates, and Eutardigrada, mostly in fresh water and on land, without cirri and with three Malpighian tubules.

Tardigrades are famous for *cryptobiosis*, a reversible state in which metabolism becomes undetectable. When its habitat dries out, a limnoterrestrial tardigrade retracts its head and legs, loses almost all its water and becomes a barrel-shaped *tun*, in which the water content falls to a few percent of the body mass and metabolism drops below detectable levels. Entering and leaving the tun takes minutes to hours and requires slow drying; tardigrades dried too fast die, because they need time to synthesize their protective proteins. In this state tardigrades have survived years in museum specimens, temperatures near absolute zero and above 100 °C for minutes, and ten days of exposure to the vacuum and radiation of low Earth orbit (Jönsson et al. 2008). Unlike many anhydrobiotic animals they accumulate little or no trehalose; instead they express intrinsically disordered proteins (cytoplasmic abundant heat-soluble or CAHS proteins) that form a gel and vitrify as the cell dries (Boothby et al. 2017), and *Ramazzottius varieornatus* has a nuclear protein, Dsup, that protects DNA from damage by X-rays (Hashimoto et al. 2016). Tardigrade genomes have lost several Hox genes that in arthropods pattern the middle of the trunk, suggesting that the tardigrade body corresponds to only the head region of an arthropod.

### 18.1.3. Phylum Onychophora

**Definition 18.1.2 (Onychophora)** **Onychophora** (velvet worms) is a phylum of terrestrial panarthropods with a soft, annulated cuticle covered with papillae, 13–43 pairs of unjointed, hydrostatically operated lobopod legs ending in claws, a head with one pair of annulated antennae, a pair of jaws within the mouth and a pair of oral (slime) papillae from which an adhesive secretion is squirted, numerous small, unbranched tracheae and segmental nephridia.

Velvet worms, 0.5–20 cm long, live in humid forests of the tropics and of the southern temperate continents. The two living families reflect the breakup of Pangaea: Peripatidae are circumtropical, Peripatopsidae are restricted to former parts of Gondwana (South Africa, Chile, Australia, New Zealand). Their cuticle is thin and permeable and their tracheae open through hundreds of small spiracles that cannot be closed, so they lose water quickly and are confined to moist microhabitats.

All are predators of small invertebrates. A velvet worm captures prey by ejecting two jets of slime from the oral papillae, fired from huge slime glands by a sudden rise in hemolymph pressure; the slime, a fluid of proteins that hardens on contact with air into sticky threads, entangles the prey, which is then bitten with the jaws, injected with saliva and sucked out. The legs have no intrinsic muscles: they are extended by hydrostatic pressure and moved by body-wall muscles. Many species are viviparous, and in some neotropical Peripatidae the embryo is nourished through a placenta-like connection with the uterine wall.

Onychophorans were long regarded as "missing links" between annelids and arthropods because they combine a soft, annelid-like body wall and segmental nephridia with arthropod-like claws, tracheae, an open circulation with a dorsal heart with ostia, and a molted chitinous cuticle. Their position as the sister group of arthropods within Ecdysozoa means that the annelid-like features are plesiomorphies of protostomes and the arthropod-like ones are panarthropod characters.

## 18.2. Cambrian Stem Groups and the Assembly of the Arthropod Body Plan

### 18.2.1. Lobopodians

Exceptionally preserved Cambrian faunas, above all Chengjiang in Yunnan (about 518 million years ago), Sirius Passet in Greenland and the Burgess Shale in Canada (about 508 million years ago), contain many soft-bodied panarthropods that show how arthropod characters were acquired one by one along the stem lineage.

**Definition 18.2.1 (Lobopodia)** **Lobopodia** (lobopodians) are Cambrian panarthropods with an annulated, unsclerotized trunk and paired, unjointed lobopod legs ending in claws, without the jointed limbs and segmental sclerites of arthropods; they form a paraphyletic grade along the stems of onychophorans, tardigrades and arthropods rather than a clade.

*Hallucigenia sparsa* of the Burgess Shale, first reconstructed upside down and back to front, walked on slender clawed legs and carried two rows of dorsal spines; its claws and head structures show it to be a stem onychophoran (Smith and Caron 2015). Other lobopodians, such as *Aysheaia*, *Microdictyon* and *Onychodictyon*, bore dorsal plates or spines and lived on sponges and other sessile animals. The lobopodian grade shows that the ancestral panarthropod was a soft-bodied worm with lobopod legs, and that onychophorans retained this body form while the arthropod lineage hardened it.

### 18.2.2. Gilled Lobopodians and Radiodonts

The next stem arthropods were swimmers. The gilled lobopodians *Kerygmachela* and *Pambdelurion* of Sirius Passet had lateral swimming flaps along the body as well as lobopod legs, and a pair of large, spiny frontal appendages for grasping prey. *Opabinia* of the Burgess Shale had five stalked eyes and a long proboscis ending in a claw, now interpreted as fused frontal appendages. The radiodonts, among them *Anomalocaris* (up to half a meter or more), *Amplectobelua* and the Ordovician filter feeder *Aegirocassis* (about 2 m), were the largest animals of their time: they had a circular mouth with plates (the oral cone), a pair of segmented frontal appendages, a series of swimming flaps and stalked compound eyes with over 16 000 lenses each in *Anomalocaris* (Paterson et al. 2011).

The frontal appendages of these animals were sclerotized and segmented, but their trunk was not. Their frontal appendages are innervated from the protocerebrum and correspond to the antennae of onychophorans, appendages that have been lost in all living arthropods (Section 18.6.1).

### 18.2.3. The Upper Stem and the Crown

The first animals with a fully sclerotized, segmented trunk and jointed trunk limbs, such as *Fuxianhuia* of Chengjiang, are the upper stem of Euarthropoda (true arthropods); *Fuxianhuia* even preserves a three-part brain (Ma et al. 2012). The "great-appendage" arthropods (Megacheira, such as *Leanchoilia* and *Alalcomenaeus*) and the trilobites (Section 18.8) are close to or within the crown group by the early Cambrian. Arthropodization was thus a sequence: lobopod legs and claws, then compound eyes and sclerotized frontal appendages, then swimming flaps, then jointed trunk limbs and segmental sclerites (see the figure below).

![Panarthropod relationships with Cambrian stem groups (dagger: extinct; after Giribet and Edgecombe 2020 and Brusca, Giribet and Moore 2023). Tardigrades are the sister group of onychophorans plus arthropods. Lobopodians form a paraphyletic grade: *Hallucigenia* is a stem onychophoran, the gilled lobopodians (*Kerygmachela*, *Pambdelurion*), *Opabinia* and the radiodonts (*Anomalocaris*) are successive stem arthropods (green band: total-group Arthropoda). These fossils are the main reason why the arthropod stem is better known than that of almost any other phylum, and why the debate on the origin of arthropods moved from comparing living groups (annelids versus nematodes) to arranging fossils along a single lineage. Their wider context, the **Cambrian explosion**, is in [7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.4).

## 18.3. The Arthropod Body Plan

### 18.3.1. Segments and Tagmata

**Definition 18.3.1 (Arthropoda)** **Arthropoda** is the phylum of panarthropods whose body is covered by a sclerotized exoskeleton divided into segmental plates and whose segments primitively each bear a pair of jointed appendages moved by intrinsic muscles; they have a head of fused segments with a three-part brain, lateral compound eyes and median ocelli, a hemocoel with an open circulatory system and a dorsal tubular heart with ostia, and grow by ecdysteroid-controlled molting.

The arthropod body is a series of **segments**. Each segment is typically boxed by a dorsal sclerite (tergite) and a ventral one (sternite), joined at the sides by the flexible pleural region, in which smaller sclerites may lie and in which the legs articulate. Segments arise in sequence from a posterior growth zone, and the segment-polarity genes, notably *engrailed*, define their boundaries ([7.5.15](/7.5.15#sec-15.4)). The front end, the ocular or protocerebral region, and the terminal telson that bears the anus are not counted as true segments in most accounts.

The segments of arthropods are not all alike: groups of them are fused or specialized into **tagmata** (**heteronomous segmentation**, in contrast to the **homonomous segmentation** of most annelids). All arthropods have a head; the trunk is divided further in characteristic ways:

| Subphylum | Tagmata | Head appendages behind the eyes |
|---|---|---|
| Chelicerata | prosoma (cephalothorax), opisthosoma | chelicerae, pedipalps, then 4 pairs of legs on the prosoma |
| Myriapoda | head, trunk | antennae, mandibles, first and second maxillae |
| Crustacea | head (cephalon), thorax, abdomen (variously fused, often with a carapace) | first and second antennae, mandibles, first and second maxillae |
| Hexapoda | head, thorax, abdomen | antennae, mandibles, maxillae, labium |

The homology of head segments is taken up in Section 18.6, and the tagmata of each subphylum in the following chapters. **Tagmosis** is one expression of a deeper principle: the segments and their appendages are serially homologous structures that can be specialized independently, under the control of **Hox genes** expressed in different combinations along the body axis ([7.5.15](/7.5.15#sec-15.5)).

### 18.3.2. The Exoskeleton

**Definition 18.3.2 (Exoskeleton)** The **exoskeleton** of arthropods is the cuticle in its role as a skeleton: hardened plates (sclerites) connected by thin, flexible arthrodial membranes at the joints, with inward projections (apodemes) to which muscles attach, so that muscles act on a system of levers enclosing the body.

The arthropod **cuticle** has the layers common to all ecdysozoans (Definition 17.1.1 in [7.17.17](/7.17.17#def-17.1.1); see the figure below). The epicuticle, at most a few micrometers thick, contains no chitin; in insects it consists of an inner epicuticle, a cuticulin layer that is the first to be secreted at a molt, a wax layer of long-chain hydrocarbons and esters, and, in many species, an outer cement layer. The wax layer is the main barrier to water loss on land ([7.17.20](/7.17.20)). The procuticle, which makes up most of the thickness, is a composite of **chitin** microfibrils, about 3 nm thick, embedded in a protein matrix ([7.1.10](/7.1.10#sec-10.3.3)). The microfibrils lie parallel within thin sheets whose orientation rotates slightly from one sheet to the next, a helicoidal or "twisted plywood" arrangement that gives the lamellate appearance in section and makes the cuticle tough in every direction in its plane. Pore canals carry material from the epidermis to the surface.

![The arthropod integument (schematic, not to scale; insect-like). A: the epidermis, a single cell layer on a basal lamina, secretes the cuticle: the epicuticle without chitin, with a wax layer, and the chitin–protein procuticle, divided into the sclerotized exocuticle and the lamellate endocuticle; pore canals run through the procuticle, a dermal gland opens on the surface, and a seta is formed by a shaft-forming (trichogen) and a socket-forming (tormogen) cell. B: a joint. The sclerotized exocuticle is interrupted at an arthrodial membrane, where the procuticle remains unsclerotized and folded; an apodeme, an inward fold of cuticle, gives a muscle its attachment.](./plots/cuticle.py)

The mechanical properties of cuticle cover a range of about seven orders of magnitude in stiffness, from soft caterpillar cuticle (Young's modulus near 1 kPa when the larva is between molts and its body is held up by pressure) to sclerotized mandibles of beetles and locusts (about 10–20 GPa). Two processes produce the hard cuticle:

- **Sclerotization**: proteins of the procuticle are cross-linked by quinones derived from catecholamines such as *N*-acetyldopamine, secreted into the cuticle by the epidermis and oxidized by phenoloxidases. Sclerotization dehydrates the cuticle and usually darkens it; the sclerotized layer is the exocuticle, and the endocuticle below remains unsclerotized. It occurs in all arthropods and is treated for insects in [7.17.23](/7.17.23).
- Mineralization: calcium carbonate (calcite and amorphous calcium carbonate) or, rarely, calcium phosphate is deposited in the procuticle, mainly in crustaceans, millipedes and some other groups. The calcium budget of molting crustaceans is described in [7.14.36](/7.14.36#sec-36.10.3).

Where flexibility is needed, at the joints, between abdominal segments and in the wing hinges, the exocuticle is absent and the procuticle forms arthrodial membranes. Some joints and hinges contain resilin, a protein rubber that stores and releases elastic energy with very little loss, used in the jumps of fleas and froghoppers and in the wing hinges of locusts and dragonflies.

An exoskeleton is a tube, and a tube is the most efficient form for resisting bending for a given mass of material: its stiffness depends on the fourth power of its radius, while its mass depends only on the radius times the wall thickness. For small animals an exoskeleton is therefore lighter for its strength than an internal skeleton of rods. As size increases, however, an exoskeleton must thicken to resist buckling and support the weight of the animal during the soft phase after each molt, and the molt itself becomes slower and more dangerous. This is one reason why the largest living arthropods are aquatic, such as the Japanese spider crab (*Macrocheira kaempferi*, leg span about 4 m), and why even the Paleozoic giants (the eurypterid *Jaekelopterus*, about 2.5 m; the millipede-relative *Arthropleura*, over 2 m) are rare exceptions.

### 18.3.3. Joints and Muscles

In a typical leg joint the two segments articulate at one or two points (condyles); a joint with two condyles is a hinge that moves in one plane, so the leg as a whole gains its freedom from successive joints whose axes lie in different planes. Each hinge is operated by antagonistic flexor and extensor muscles that run inside the segment and insert on apodemes of the next. A few joints lack extensor muscles, notably two leg joints of spiders, which are extended hydraulically by raising hemolymph pressure in the body ([7.17.19](/7.17.19)).

Arthropod muscles are cross-striated and attach to the cuticle through tendon cells of the epidermis. Their control differs from that of vertebrates: a muscle may be supplied by only one to a few motor neurons, each of which forms many endplates along every fiber, and gradations of force come from graded synaptic potentials and from the interaction of fast, slow and inhibitory motor neurons rather than from recruitment of many motor units ([7.14.8](/7.14.8#sec-8.8.3)). The asynchronous flight muscle of many insects is described in [7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.6.5).

### 18.3.4. Appendages

The basic arthropod limb has two parts (see the figure below). The basal part, the protopod, attaches to the body; its first article is always called the coxa, and in crustaceans a second article, the basis, follows. The distal part, the telopod, is a series of articles (podomeres). The protopod can bear lobes on its inner side, called endites, which in many arthropods form jaws or chewing gnathobases, and on its outer side, called exites, which form gills, gill cleaners or swimming paddles; large exites are often called epipods.

**Definition 18.3.3 (Biramous and Uniramous Appendages)** A **biramous appendage** is one in which the protopod (the basis) carries two rami, an inner endopod and an outer exopod; a **uniramous appendage** has a single series of articles distal to the protopod, without an exopod.

![Arthropod limbs (schematic). A: a biramous limb of a crustacean: the protopod (coxa and basis, grey-blue) carries an endite (here a gnathobase) and an exite (epipod, a gill), and distally two rami, the endopod (teal) and the setose exopod (orange). B: a uniramous limb, an insect leg: coxa, trochanter (often homologized with the basis), femur, tibia, tarsus and pretarsus with claws, without an exopod.](./plots/appendages.py)

Biramous limbs are typical of crustaceans, especially of their swimming and filter-feeding limbs and of the nauplius larva, and of trilobites. Uniramous limbs are the walking legs of insects, myriapods and chelicerates, and also many crustacean limbs, in which the exopod has been lost. Uniramy was once considered a fundamental character uniting insects, myriapods and onychophorans as "Uniramia" (Manton) and treated as separating them from the "biramous" groups. Both the fossils (stem arthropods such as *Fuxianhuia* have biramous limbs) and the expression of genes that pattern limbs show instead that the uniramous condition arose several times by loss of the exopod; the proximodistal patterning of all arthropod limbs, including the expression of *Distal-less* in the telopod, is shared (Section 18.9).

The specialization of serially homologous limbs along one body, for sensing, feeding, walking, swimming, gas exchange, copulation and brood care, is the most obvious expression of the arthropod body plan. It is described in detail for the crayfish and shrimp in [7.17.21](/7.17.21) and for insects in [7.17.23](/7.17.23) and [7.17.24](/7.17.24).

## 18.4. Molting

Growth in an arthropod is a cycle of four phases, named for crustaceans after Drach and applying in principle to all arthropods ([7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.1) for the general concept of **molting**):

1. Intermolt: the cuticle is complete and hard; the animal feeds and stores reserves.
2. Premolt (proecdysis): the epidermis detaches from the old cuticle (apolysis) and secretes a new epicuticle; molting fluid with chitinases and proteases is secreted into the space between old and new cuticle and digests the old endocuticle, the products being resorbed; the new procuticle is deposited beneath the new epicuticle. In crustaceans, calcium is resorbed from the old cuticle and stored.
3. Ecdysis: the old cuticle, now reduced to epicuticle and exocuticle, splits along preformed lines of weakness (ecdysial lines), and the animal pulls itself out, leaving the exuviae, which include the linings of the foregut, the hindgut and, in insects, the tracheae.
4. Postmolt: the animal swallows water or air to expand the new, soft cuticle to its new size before it is sclerotized or mineralized; endocuticle continues to be laid down afterward.

Drach divided the crustacean cycle further into stages that can be recognized under the microscope by the state of the setae and the cuticle: A and B (early and late postmolt, the cuticle still soft and then paper-thin), C (intermolt, with C4 the fully hardened state), D0–D4 (premolt, from apolysis to the formation of new setae inside the old ones) and E (ecdysis itself). The intermolt C4 occupies most of the cycle in an adult crab, whereas in a fast-growing juvenile the animal passes almost directly from postmolt into the next premolt.

The cuticle cannot stretch much once it is hardened, so each molt must provide the space for the growth of the following intermolt. A linear increase of 20 % at one molt, for instance, corresponds to a rise of $1.2^3 \approx 1.73$, or 73 %, in body volume, which the animal fills with tissue during the intermolt, while immediately after ecdysis the extra volume is occupied by water or, in insects, by air. The material lost with the exuviae is considerable even after resorption, and many crustaceans and insects eat their own cast cuticle; a lobster recovers much of the calcium it needs from the water and from its exuviae.

The number of molts differs greatly. Insects usually pass through 4–8 larval instars, but mayflies may molt more than 20 times; the American lobster molts about 20–25 times in its first five to seven years and then once a year or less; and some crabs stop at a terminal molt at maturity. Each molt also offers an opportunity to change form, which arthropods use in metamorphosis ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)).

The cycle is under endocrine control. In insects a neuropeptide from the brain, prothoracicotropic hormone, stimulates the prothoracic glands to secrete **ecdysone**, which is converted to the active 20-hydroxyecdysone; a pulse of 20-hydroxyecdysone starts apolysis, and its decline releases ecdysis-triggering hormone and eclosion hormone, which run the motor program of ecdysis; the hormone bursicon then triggers sclerotization of the expanded cuticle. **Juvenile hormone** decides whether a molt leads to another larval stage or to metamorphosis ([7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.3), [7.14.32](/7.14.32#sec-32.9.3)). In crustaceans the ecdysteroid is produced by the Y-organs, and molting is held back between molts by molt-inhibiting hormone released from the X-organ–sinus gland complex of the eyestalk ([7.17.21](/7.17.21)).

Molting is costly and dangerous: the animal cannot feed for part of the cycle, is soft and almost defenseless in early postmolt, and may die trapped in its old cuticle. Many arthropods molt in hiding, and some, such as horseshoe crabs and spiders, molt in a characteristic posture. Insects stop molting at the adult stage (except the subimago of mayflies), while many crustaceans and some arachnids continue to molt as adults. Molting also allows lost appendages to be regenerated; many arthropods can shed a limb at a preformed breakage plane (autotomy) and grow it back in one or several molts.

## 18.5. Internal Organization

### 18.5.1. Hemocoel and Open Circulation

The main body cavity of arthropods is the hemocoel, and the coelom survives only as the cavities of the gonads and of the end sacs of the excretory organs. Its fluid, hemolymph, bathes the organs directly. The heart is a dorsal tube lying in a pericardial sinus; hemolymph enters it through paired, valved openings (ostia) when it relaxes and is pumped forward, in many crustaceans and chelicerates through well-developed arteries, before it percolates back through sinuses. The details and the comparison with closed systems are in [7.14.12](/7.14.12#sec-12.1.3) under **open circulatory system**.

The respiratory pigment, where present, is dissolved in the hemolymph: hemocyanin, a copper protein, in chelicerates, many crustaceans and a few insects; hemoglobin in some branchiopod crustaceans and in midge larvae. In most insects the hemolymph does not transport oxygen at all, because the **tracheal system** delivers air directly to the tissues ([7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.5.2), [7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.5)).

### 18.5.2. Gut and Excretory Organs

The gut has three regions with different origins: the foregut (stomodeum) and hindgut (proctodeum) arise from ectoderm and are lined with cuticle, which is molted, and the midgut (mesenteron) arises from endoderm and is the site of most enzyme secretion and absorption, often enlarged into digestive ceca or a digestive gland (the hepatopancreas of crustaceans). In many arthropods the midgut contents are enclosed in a **peritrophic membrane** of chitin and protein, which protects the epithelium ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)).

Two kinds of excretory organs occur, of different origin:

| Organ | Origin | Where it occurs |
|---|---|---|
| Coxal glands | segmental nephridia: end sac (coelomic remnant) and duct opening at a leg base | chelicerates |
| Antennal (green) glands and maxillary glands | the same, opening at the second antennae or second maxillae | crustaceans (antennal in adult decapods, maxillary in many others and in larvae) |
| Malpighian tubules | blind tubules opening into the gut at the midgut–hindgut junction | arachnids (from endoderm), myriapods and hexapods (from ectoderm), tardigrades |

The nephridial organs filter hemolymph into the end sac and modify the filtrate along the duct; Malpighian tubules secrete potassium-rich fluid into the gut, which is then modified by the hindgut ([7.14.23](/7.14.23#sec-23.7.2)). Because their embryonic origin differs, the **Malpighian tubules** of arachnids and of insects and myriapods are regarded as convergent, one of the characters of the repeated colonization of land discussed in [7.17.20](/7.17.20).

### 18.5.3. Nervous System

The central nervous system has the protostome pattern: a dorsal brain above the foregut, connectives around it and a paired, ganglionated **ventral nerve cord**, with ganglia that are often fused, as in the single thoracic ganglion mass of crabs and of many flies. The arthropod brain consists of three neuromeres:

- the protocerebrum, which receives the optic nerves and contains the optic lobes, the **mushroom bodies** and the central complex, the main centers of integration;
- the deutocerebrum, which receives the antennal nerves in mandibulates and contains the antennal lobes (olfactory centers), and innervates the chelicerae in chelicerates;
- the tritocerebrum, which innervates the second antennae of crustaceans, the pedipalps of chelicerates and the labrum, and gives rise to the connectives around the foregut and to the stomatogastric nervous system.

The tritocerebrum arises behind the mouth in the embryo and only later moves forward; this, and the homology of the appendages it innervates, is central to the head problem (Section 18.6).

## 18.6. The Head Problem and the Homology of Head Appendages

### 18.6.1. Segments of the Head

How many segments the arthropod head contains, and which appendages are homologous across the subphyla, was disputed for more than a century: the chelicerae of spiders, the antennae of insects and the two pairs of antennae of crustaceans differ in form, function and position. Three lines of evidence now give a consistent answer:

1. Neuroanatomy: the nerves of each appendage can be traced to a brain neuromere, in embryos as well as in adults.
2. Gene expression: *engrailed* marks the posterior border of each segment in the embryo, and the anterior Hox genes are expressed in a nested series. In the spider *Cupiennius* and the mite *Archegozetes*, the chelicera segment expresses no Hox gene, like the insect antennal segment, and the pedipalp segment expresses *labial* and *proboscipedia*, like the appendage-less intercalary segment of insects and the second-antennal segment of crustaceans (Damen et al. 1998; Telford and Thomas 1998).
3. Fossils: stem arthropods with preserved brains and appendages, such as radiodonts and *Fuxianhuia*, show which appendages were added or lost along the stem.

The result is summarized in the figure below. The first, protocerebral region bears the eyes; its appendages are the antennae of onychophorans and the frontal appendages of radiodonts, and they have been lost in all living arthropods, perhaps surviving as the labrum, which develops from paired buds. The deutocerebral segment carries the chelicerae of chelicerates, the chelifores of sea spiders and the (first) antennae of myriapods, crustaceans and insects, so that a spider's chelicera is homologous with an insect's antenna. The tritocerebral segment carries the pedipalps in chelicerates and the second antennae in crustaceans, and has no appendages in myriapods and insects, where it is called the intercalary segment. From the fourth segment on, chelicerates have legs and mandibulates have the mandibles, followed by two pairs of maxillae (in insects the second maxillae fuse into the labium).

![Homology of the anterior segments and their appendages in the panarthropods (after Brusca, Giribet and Moore 2023, based on neuroanatomy and *engrailed* and Hox gene expression). Each row is one segment, identified by its brain neuromere; appendages in the same row are homologous and share a colour. The protocerebral antennae of onychophorans have no counterpart in living arthropods. The deutocerebral appendages (jaws, chelifores, chelicerae, the antennae of myriapods and insects, the first antennae of crustaceans) are homologous; the tritocerebral segment bears pedipalps in chelicerates and second antennae in crustaceans, but no appendages in myriapods and hexapods (the intercalary segment). Dash: no appendage. Brackets: the absence of antennae in chelicerates, and the mandible on the fourth segment of mandibulates.](./plots/head-segments.py)

### 18.6.2. Consequences

This scheme removes two old puzzles. First, chelicerates do not "lack antennae": they carry the deutocerebral appendage as a feeding chelicera instead of a sensory antenna. Second, myriapods and insects do not "lack the first antennae": they lack the appendages of the tritocerebral (second-antennal) segment, so the name Atelocerata ("incomplete antennae") for myriapods plus insects describes a real character, but a loss that can have happened more than once. The scheme also shows how flexible arthropod heads are: the number of segments fused into the head differs between groups (about six in mandibulates, a prosoma of seven in chelicerates), and further trunk segments are added to the feeding apparatus in several crustaceans, whose maxillipeds are thoracic limbs ([7.17.21](/7.17.21)).

## 18.7. Eyes

### 18.7.1. Median and Lateral Eyes

Arthropods have two kinds of eyes, innervated from the protocerebrum. Median eyes (ocelli) are small simple eyes with a cup of photoreceptors and, in some, a lens; they include the naupliar eye of crustaceans, the median eyes of chelicerates and the dorsal ocelli of insects. Lateral eyes are primitively compound; they have been modified into groups of simple eyes (stemmata) in the larvae of holometabolous insects, in many myriapods and in arachnids, whose lateral eyes are derived from compound eyes by reduction.

### 18.7.2. Compound Eyes

**Definition 18.7.1 (Compound Eye)** A **compound eye** is a lateral eye made up of many similar optical units, the **ommatidia**, each consisting of a cuticular corneal lens, a crystalline cone formed by cone cells (four in crustaceans and insects), a small group of photoreceptor (retinula) cells, usually eight, whose rhabdomeres together form a light-sensitive rhabdom, and pigment cells that optically isolate the ommatidium.

Each ommatidium samples light from a small solid angle, and the image is a mosaic of these samples. The rhabdoms are made of microvilli of **rhabdomeric photoreceptors** ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.2)); their regular orientation makes many arthropods sensitive to the plane of polarized light. In an **apposition eye** each rhabdom receives light only through its own lens, which suits animals active in bright light; in a **superposition eye**, typical of nocturnal insects and many crustaceans, a clear zone separates the cones from the retina, and light from many facets is focused onto each rhabdom ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.4), [7.17.27](/7.17.27)). The two designs are compared in the figure and the table.

![Apposition and superposition compound eyes (schematic sections). (A) Apposition eye: each ommatidium is isolated by screening pigment, and its corneal lens and crystalline cone focus the light from a small angle onto the rhabdom directly beneath, so that each rhabdom receives light only through its own facet. (B) Superposition eye: a wide clear zone separates the cones from the retina; parallel rays from one point of the visual field enter many facets and are redirected by the cones (by refraction in insects, by reflection in decapod crustaceans) so that they converge on a single rhabdom, which makes the eye up to several hundred times more sensitive](./plots/eye-types.py)

| Eye type | Optics | Sensitivity | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Focal apposition | Lens and cone focus light onto the rhabdom beneath; fused rhabdom | Low | Bees, dragonflies, butterflies, many crabs |
| Neural superposition | Apposition optics, but the rhabdomeres are separate (open rhabdom); the six photoreceptors in six neighboring ommatidia that look at the same point send their signals to one cartridge in the lamina | Higher than focal apposition at the same resolution | Flies (Diptera) |
| Refracting superposition | Graded-index cones bend rays across the clear zone | High | Moths, many beetles, caddisflies, krill |
| Reflecting superposition | Square facets and mirror-lined cones reflect rays across the clear zone | High | Shrimps, crayfish, lobsters |
| Parabolic superposition | A combination of a lens and a parabolic reflector | High | Some crabs and hermit crabs |

The gain of superposition can be estimated with the standard expression for the optical sensitivity of an eye to an extended source, the number of photons absorbed per receptor for a given radiance,

$$
S = \left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^2 A^2 \left(\frac{d}{f}\right)^2 \left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-kl}\right),
$$

where $A$ is the diameter of the aperture, $d$ the diameter of the rhabdom, $f$ the focal length, $k$ the absorption coefficient of the photopigment and $l$ the length of the rhabdom (Land 1981). In an apposition eye the aperture is a single facet, about 20 µm across; in a superposition eye the light from tens to hundreds of facets is pooled, so that the effective aperture is ten or more times wider and, because $S$ grows with $A^2$, the eye is about a hundred times more sensitive. Many nocturnal insects add a further trick: screening pigment migrates into the clear zone by day, turning a superposition eye into a functional apposition eye, and withdraws at night.

The resolution of a compound eye is limited by two effects that pull in opposite directions. The angle between neighboring ommatidia, which sets how finely the visual field is sampled, is $\Delta\varphi = D/R$ for an eye of radius $R$ with facets of diameter $D$; smaller facets sample more finely. But diffraction at a lens of diameter $D$ blurs the image of each ommatidium over an angle of about $\lambda/D$, so smaller facets also see less sharply. The total blur

$$
\Delta\rho \approx \sqrt{\left(\frac{D}{R}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\lambda}{D}\right)^2}
$$
is smallest when the two terms are equal, at

$$
D^* = \sqrt{\lambda R}, \qquad \Delta\rho_{\min} = \sqrt{\frac{2\lambda}{R}} .
$$

For light of $\lambda = 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and an eye radius of 1 mm, $D^* \approx 22\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and the best resolution is about 1.8° (see the figure below). Honeybees, with facets of about 20–25 µm, are close to this optimum (Barlow 1952). Because $\Delta\rho_{\min}$ falls only as $R^{-1/2}$, halving the blur requires an eye four times larger, and a compound eye with the 0.017° resolution of the human fovea would need a radius of tens of meters. Compound eyes are instead superb at what small eyes can do well: a wide field of view and a temporal resolution of more than 200 flickers per second in fast-flying flies.

![A model of compound-eye resolution (after Barlow 1952 and Kirschfeld 1976). For each eye radius $R$ (0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 mm; light of wavelength 0.5 µm) the angular blur combines sampling by the ommatidial mosaic ($D/R$, rising with facet size) and diffraction at each facet ($\lambda/D$, falling with facet size). The optimum facet diameter $D^* = \sqrt{\lambda R}$ (dots) grows with the square root of eye radius, and the best attainable resolution improves only as $R^{-1/2}$.](./plots/compound-eye-optics.py)

The ommatidia of crustaceans and insects share precise details, such as exactly four cone cells secreting the crystalline cone and two primary pigment cells, which are absent from the lateral eyes of chelicerates (the horseshoe crab has ommatidia with many photoreceptors and a different cone) and of myriapods (whose ocelli and the compound-like eyes of *Scutigera* have many cone cells). This is one of the characters behind the name Tetraconata for the crustacean–insect clade (Section 18.9). Compound eyes themselves are much older: they are preserved in radiodonts and trilobites, and are a character of the arthropod stem (see the tree above).

## 18.8. Trilobites

**Definition 18.8.1 (Trilobite)** **Trilobites** (Trilobita) are extinct marine arthropods, from the early Cambrian (about 521 million years ago) to the end of the Permian (252 million years ago), with a calcified dorsal exoskeleton divided lengthwise into a raised axial lobe and two pleural lobes and crosswise into a head shield (cephalon), a thorax of articulated segments and a tail shield (pygidium); the head bore one pair of antennae and paired compound eyes, and the body a series of similar biramous limbs.

With over 20 000 described species, trilobites are the most diverse group of fossil arthropods and among the commonest Paleozoic fossils; in the Cambrian of China, for example, *Redlichia* and many other genera are used to define biostratigraphic zones. Their calcified dorsal exoskeleton fossilized readily, whereas the thin ventral cuticle and the limbs are preserved only in exceptional deposits (Chengjiang, Burgess Shale, the pyritized fossils of Beecher's Trilobite Bed). The limbs were all alike behind the antennae, each with a walking endopod, a gill-bearing exopod and a spiny protopod that passed food forward along the midline to the mouth.

Several features show that the trilobite exoskeleton was molted like any other: the cephalon was crossed by facial sutures along which it split at ecdysis, and many fossils are exuviae. Development passed from a minute protaspis larva through meraspid stages, in which thoracic segments were released one by one from the pygidium, to the holaspid stage with the full number of thoracic segments, a rare example of a fossil ontogeny known in detail. Many trilobites could enroll into a ball for protection, and the eyes had lenses of calcite, a single crystal per lens oriented with its optic axis along the line of sight: holochroal eyes had many small lenses under a common cornea, schizochroal eyes (in Phacopida) fewer, larger lenses, each with a doublet structure that corrected spherical aberration.

The dorsal shield gives the characters by which trilobites are classified. The cephalon has a raised central glabella, flanked by the fixed cheeks (fixigenae); the facial sutures separate these from the free cheeks (librigenae) that carry the visual surface of the eyes. By the course of the sutures, trilobites are proparian (the suture reaches the lateral margin in front of the genal angle), opisthoparian (behind it) or gonatoparian (at it). On the ventral side a calcified plate, the hypostome, lay under the glabella in front of the mouth. The thorax had from two (agnostids) to more than 60 segments, and the pygidium was formed by fused segments behind the thorax. Trilobites ranged in length from about 1 mm to over 70 cm (*Isotelus rex*, Ordovician of Canada).

| Order | Range | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Agnostida | Early Cambrian–Late Ordovician | Tiny, eyeless, with two thoracic segments and a pygidium as large as the cephalon; their trilobite status is debated |
| Redlichiida | Early–Middle Cambrian | Many thoracic segments, small pygidium, crescent-shaped eyes; *Redlichia*, *Eoredlichia* of Chengjiang |
| Corynexochida | Cambrian–Devonian | Long glabella, often spiny |
| Ptychopariida | Cambrian–Ordovician | The generalized Cambrian type, very diverse |
| Asaphida | Cambrian–Silurian | Large pygidium; many pelagic and burrowing forms |
| Phacopida | Ordovician–Devonian | Schizochroal eyes; enrollment common |
| Lichida | Cambrian–Devonian | Spiny, with granular exoskeletons |
| Proetida | Ordovician–Permian | The last order, the only one to survive the Late Devonian crisis |

Trilobites occupied many ways of life. Most were benthic deposit feeders or scavengers that plowed through the sediment, and their traces, such as the resting impressions *Rusophycus* and the crawling trails *Cruziana*, are common in Paleozoic sandstones. Others, with powerful gnathobases, were predators of worms; olenids of oxygen-poor bottoms may have harbored symbiotic bacteria; and some, with huge eyes and streamlined bodies, swam in the open water. Their diversity peaked in the Cambrian and Ordovician, fell in each of the great extinctions, and the last proetids disappeared at the end of the Permian. In China, the swallowtail trilobite *Drepanura* of the late Cambrian of Shandong, whose pygidial spines spread like the tail of a swallow, has been collected since the Song dynasty, and its slabs are still known as "swallow stones".

Trilobites belong to the Artiopoda, a clade of Paleozoic arthropods with similar limbs. Whether artiopodans are closer to chelicerates (the old Arachnomorpha hypothesis, supported by some fossil analyses) or to mandibulates (Antennulata, based on the antennae) is unresolved, which is why they are shown in a polytomy in the tree above.

## 18.9. The Phylogeny of Living Arthropods

### 18.9.1. Chelicerata and Mandibulata

The living arthropods fall into four subphyla, whose monophyly is well supported: Chelicerata, Myriapoda, Crustacea (in the traditional sense) and Hexapoda. Their relationships were the subject of several competing hypotheses:

| Hypothesis | Grouping | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Uniramia (Manton) | onychophorans, myriapods and hexapods, with arthropodization evolved several times | rejected: arthropods are monophyletic and onychophorans are their sister group |
| Atelocerata or Tracheata | myriapods + hexapods (one pair of antennae, tracheae, Malpighian tubules) | rejected: the shared characters are convergent adaptations to land |
| Schizoramia | crustaceans + trilobites + chelicerates (biramous limbs) | rejected: biramy is plesiomorphic |
| Myriochelata (Paradoxopoda) | myriapods + chelicerates | recovered by some molecular analyses, now regarded as an artifact |
| Mandibulata | myriapods + crustaceans + hexapods | supported by morphology and phylogenomics |
| Pancrustacea (Tetraconata) | crustaceans + hexapods, hexapods nested within crustaceans | strongly supported |

**Definition 18.9.1 (Mandibulata)** **Mandibulata** is the clade of arthropods comprising myriapods, crustaceans and hexapods, characterized by mandibles (gnathobasic jaws) on the fourth head segment, followed by two pairs of maxillae, and by antennae on the deutocerebral segment.

The mandible of mandibulates is a limb whose gnathobase (an endite of the coxa) became the jaw, while the telopod was reduced to a palp or lost; it is the same structure in a millipede, a crab and a beetle. The sister group of Mandibulata, the Chelicerata, retained legs on the fourth to sixth segments and feeds with the deutocerebral chelicerae.

### 18.9.2. Pancrustacea

**Definition 18.9.2 (Pancrustacea)** **Pancrustacea** (also called Tetraconata) is the clade comprising the crustaceans and the hexapods, within which the hexapods arose from one crustacean lineage; it is characterized by ommatidia with four cone cells and two primary pigment cells, by the formation of the nervous system from ventral neuroblasts that divide asymmetrically, and by strong support in phylogenomic analyses.

Pancrustacea makes the traditional Crustacea a **paraphyletic group** that excludes one of its own descendant lineages, as Reptilia excludes birds. Insects can be regarded, in the words of Brusca and colleagues, as "crustaceans of the land". The closest crustacean relatives of the hexapods appear to be the remipedes, blind cave crustaceans, possibly together with the cephalocarids ([7.17.21](/7.17.21)). The characters once shared by myriapods and insects, such as tracheae, Malpighian tubules and the loss of the second antennae, were evolved independently on land, as discussed in [7.17.20](/7.17.20).

The resulting tree, which the following chapters follow, is:

$$
\text{Arthropoda} = \text{Chelicerata} + \text{Mandibulata}, \qquad \text{Mandibulata} = \text{Myriapoda} + \text{Pancrustacea}, \qquad \text{Pancrustacea} = \text{crustacean lineages} + \text{Hexapoda}.
$$

### 18.9.3. Correspondence with Chinese Textbooks

Chinese textbooks use the same subphyla with different interpretations:

| Chinese textbook | Its system | Modern equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Liu Lingyun and Zheng Guangmei | five subphyla: Trilobitomorpha, Crustacea, Chelicerata, Myriapoda, Hexapoda; Onychophora and Tardigrada as separate phyla related to arthropods; annelids and arthropods treated as closely related (the Articulata view), Ecdysozoa presented as an alternative | Trilobites as extinct crown arthropods; the Articulata is rejected in favor of Ecdysozoa ([7.17.17](/7.17.17)); the close relationship of crustaceans and hexapods, which this textbook also accepts, is the Pancrustacea clade |
| Xu Chongren and Cheng Hong | four subphyla: Trilobita, Chelicerata, Crustacea, Uniramia (myriapods + insects) | Uniramia is polyphyletic in the sense of combining two lineages that independently became uniramous and terrestrial; myriapods are the sister group of Pancrustacea |

The diagnostic characters these textbooks give for the arthropods, namely heteronomous segmentation, a chitinous exoskeleton with molting, jointed appendages, striated muscle in bundles, a mixocoel (hemocoel) with open circulation and a ventral nerve cord, are all retained here; only their phylogenetic interpretation has changed. The textbook explanation of arthropod success, a protective and waterproof exoskeleton, jointed and specialized appendages, tracheae, metamorphosis that separates larval and adult niches, small size and complex sense organs, is also sound, although the exoskeleton and small size are linked: the same cuticle that protects the animal limits its size (Section 18.3.2).

## 18.10. Why Are There So Many Arthropods?

The arthropods include about 80 % of described animal species, and the insects alone more than a million; estimates of the true number of insect species center on about 5.5 million (Stork 2018). Several properties of the body plan are usually offered as explanations, and they act together:

| Factor | How it promotes diversity |
|---|---|
| Serially homologous, independently specializable segments and limbs | The same limb can become an antenna, jaw, leg, paddle, gill or copulatory organ under Hox control, so new ways of feeding and moving evolve by modifying existing parts |
| The exoskeleton | Protection against predators and desiccation, rigid levers for precise movement, and a surface that can be sculptured into setae, scales, sense organs and wings |
| Small size | Many niches are available only to small animals; a single plant can host hundreds of species feeding on different parts |
| Flight (insects) | Dispersal, escape and access to scattered resources, and the colonization of new habitats |
| Metamorphosis | Larvae and adults use different resources and do not compete (holometabolous insects make up about 85 % of insect species) |
| Coevolution with plants and animals | Specialization on particular host plants, hosts or prey, especially in herbivorous beetles, moths and flies and in parasitoid wasps |
| High fecundity and short generations | Rapid adaptation and frequent speciation; large populations resist extinction |
| Age and low extinction rates | Arthropods have existed for over 520 million years; many insect families have persisted since the Mesozoic, so diversity has accumulated |

These factors explain why arthropods can be diverse, but the diversity is unevenly distributed. Within insects, it is concentrated in four holometabolous orders, the beetles, flies, wasps and moths, and within them in lineages that feed on flowering plants or parasitize other insects. Comparisons of sister groups show that shifts to herbivory on angiosperms and to parasitism are associated with higher diversity, which indicates that ecological opportunity, not the body plan alone, has driven the radiations. The insect orders and their diversity are treated in [7.17.30](/7.17.30)–[7.17.36](/7.17.36), and their interactions with plants in [7.17.37](/7.17.37).

## Questions

1. An insect antenna, a spider chelicera and a crustacean first antenna look and work very differently. What evidence shows that they are homologous, and which appendages of a spider and a crayfish correspond to the insect's intercalary segment?

   **Answer** All three are innervated from the deutocerebrum, and in embryos they lie on the first segment behind the ocular region, which expresses no Hox gene in spiders, insects and crustaceans; the following segment expresses *labial* (and *proboscipedia* in chelicerates) in all three. Fossils with preserved brains show the same order. The insect intercalary segment, which has no appendages, therefore corresponds to the spider's pedipalp segment and the crayfish's second-antennal segment; all three are tritocerebral.

2. Using $D^* = \sqrt{\lambda R}$ and $\Delta\rho_{\min} = \sqrt{2\lambda/R}$, estimate the optimum facet diameter and resolution for a fruit fly eye of radius about 0.2 mm, and explain why the fly's facets (about 16 µm) are larger than this optimum.

   **Answer** With $\lambda = 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $R = 200\,\mu\mathrm{m}$: $D^* = \sqrt{100}\,\mu\mathrm{m} = 10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $\Delta\rho_{\min} = \sqrt{2 \times 0.5/200} = 0.071\,\mathrm{rad} \approx 4^\circ$. Larger facets than the diffraction optimum collect more light per photoreceptor, which improves the signal-to-noise ratio and the temporal resolution of vision; a small fly that flies fast in varying light gains more from sensitivity than from a slightly finer sampling, so its eye trades spatial resolution for photon capture. The simple model optimizes only spatial blur, not sensitivity.

3. Why is the exoskeleton advantageous for small animals but limiting for large ones? Give two separate reasons why the largest arthropods are aquatic.

   **Answer** A hollow cylinder resists bending with a stiffness proportional to the fourth power of its radius but costs material in proportion to radius times wall thickness, so at small size an external tube is stronger for its mass than internal rods, and it also protects and waterproofs. As size increases, the wall must thicken more than in proportion to resist buckling under the body's weight, and the animal must survive the soft postmolt phase, during which the new cuticle cannot yet bear loads. Water supports the body by buoyancy, so a soft, freshly molted animal does not collapse; and water allows calcium carbonate to be taken up and the cuticle to be hardened quickly. In addition, on land gas exchange by tracheae and the risk of desiccation during the molt impose further limits.

4. Explain why Chinese textbooks that recognize a subphylum Uniramia and those that recognize Pancrustacea cannot both be right, and what kind of evidence decided the question.

   **Answer** Uniramia groups myriapods and insects as sister groups, with crustaceans outside; Pancrustacea places insects inside or next to a crustacean lineage, so that myriapods are outside the insect–crustacean clade. The two trees are incompatible. The characters supporting Uniramia (one pair of antennae, uniramous limbs, tracheae, Malpighian tubules) are either losses or features related to life on land, which can evolve convergently; the characters supporting Pancrustacea are detailed similarities with no obvious functional link to a habitat (four cone cells and two primary pigment cells per ommatidium, neuroblast-based neurogenesis), together with consistent phylogenomic evidence from hundreds of genes. Shared complex details and independent molecular data outweigh adaptive similarities that can be convergent.

5. (Open question) Onychophoran legs have no intrinsic muscles and are extended by hydrostatic pressure, and spiders extend two leg joints hydraulically. Discuss what this tells us about the evolution of arthropod locomotion, and suggest an experiment to measure the role of hydraulics in spider walking.

   **Answer** The ancestral panarthropod limb was a hydrostatic lobopod, moved by body-wall muscles and pressure; arthropodization added sclerites and intrinsic flexor and extensor muscles, but hydraulic mechanisms could persist where extensor muscles were lost or never evolved, as in spider leg joints, so the arthropod leg is a mosaic of muscular and hydraulic control. Experiment: insert a pressure transducer into the prosoma of a spider and record hemolymph pressure during walking, running and jumping, together with high-speed video of leg-joint angles; the hydraulic hypothesis predicts pressure peaks coinciding with extension of the femur–patella and tibia–metatarsus joints, increasing with speed. Puncturing the cuticle to lower pressure, or loading the spider, should impair extension at these joints but not flexion. Such measurements (Parry and Brown 1959; later work) show pressures of tens of kilopascals during fast movement.

6. Using $S \propto A^2$, compare the sensitivity of an apposition eye with facets 25 µm across and a superposition eye in which the light from a patch of facets 300 µm across converges on each rhabdom, other things being equal. Why is the gain not obtained without cost?

   **Answer** The ratio is $(300/25)^2 = 144$, so the superposition eye is about 140 times more sensitive. The cost is spatial resolution and complexity: the image formed by many facets is blurred by imperfections of the optics and by light spreading in the clear zone, so superposition eyes usually resolve less finely than apposition eyes of the same size; they also need a deep eye with a clear zone and precisely graded cones or mirrors, and many species need pigment migration to protect the eye in daylight.
7. A shore crab 40 mm wide molts with a linear increment of 25 %. By how much must it increase its volume immediately after ecdysis, and where does the extra volume come from at first and later?

   **Answer** The volume increases by a factor $1.25^3 \approx 1.95$, nearly doubling. Immediately after ecdysis, before the new cuticle hardens, the crab takes up water through the gut and gills to stretch the soft cuticle to its new size; during the following intermolt the water is gradually replaced by new tissue grown from food, while the cuticle hardens by mineralization and the endocuticle thickens.
