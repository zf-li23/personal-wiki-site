Most insects die young, and most of them are eaten. The enemies of insects include vertebrates (birds, bats, lizards, frogs, fishes), spiders and other arthropods, nematodes, fungi, protists, bacteria and viruses, but above all other insects: predatory insects in almost every order, and the parasitoids, insects whose larvae develop on or in a single host and kill it. Predation and parasitism are among the strongest selective forces in the lives of insects, and they have shaped the bewildering array of camouflage, warning colours, mimicry, spines and chemical weapons that insects carry. They also regulate insect populations, which is why the natural enemies of pests are the basis of biological control ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)).

This chapter first treats insects as predators, then the parasitoids and the dynamics of host–parasitoid interactions, and finally the defences of insects. It builds on the classification of parasitoid life histories in [7.17.34](/7.17.34) (idiobionts and koinobionts, polydnaviruses, the diversity of parasitoid wasps), on the definitions of **aposematism**, **mimicry**, **Batesian mimicry** and **Müllerian mimicry** in [7.10.17](/7.10.17), where Müller's model of shared predation is derived, and on the sequestration of plant toxins described in [7.17.37](/7.17.37). The general population ecology of predators and prey belongs to ecology ([7.19](/7.19)); the host–parasitoid models given here are its best-studied special case.

## 39.1. Predatory Insects

### 39.1.1. Who the Predators Are

Predation is the ancestral habit of several orders and has arisen repeatedly in others:

| Order | Predatory groups | Prey and method |
|---|---|---|
| Odonata | All (adults and nymphs) | Adults catch flying insects in the air with spiny legs held as a basket; nymphs seize prey with the extensible labial mask |
| Mantodea | All | Sit-and-wait ambush with raptorial forelegs |
| Hemiptera | Assassin bugs (Reduviidae), ambush bugs (Phymatinae), damsel bugs (Nabidae), minute pirate bugs (Anthocoridae), predatory stink bugs (Asopinae); aquatic Nepomorpha (water scorpions, giant water bugs, backswimmers) | Pierce prey with the rostrum and inject saliva that paralyses and liquefies tissue |
| Neuroptera | Larvae of lacewings, antlions, mantispids; many adults | Larvae with sucking jaws formed by mandible and maxilla; antlion pits ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)) |
| Coleoptera | Ground beetles and tiger beetles, diving beetles, rove beetles, ladybirds, fireflies (larvae), checkered beetles | Chewing; extra-oral digestion in many larvae |
| Diptera | Robber flies, dance flies, long-legged flies; larvae of hover flies, gall midges (*Aphidoletes*) and some others | Robber flies inject saliva through the hypopharynx; hover fly larvae suck out aphids |
| Hymenoptera | Ants; solitary and social wasps that provision prey | Sting and venom; group foraging in ants |
| Mecoptera | Hangingflies | Catch prey with raptorial hind tarsi |

Among the most important predators in Chinese agriculture are ladybirds (the seven-spot ladybird *Coccinella septempunctata* and the harlequin ladybird *Harmonia axyridis* on aphids), lacewings (*Chrysoperla sinica*), minute pirate bugs (*Orius*), the mirid *Cyrtorhinus lividipennis* on rice planthopper eggs, ground beetles in fields, and spiders, which are not insects but are the most numerous predators in rice paddies.

### 39.1.2. Morphological Adaptations

Predators must detect, catch and subdue prey, and the same solutions have evolved repeatedly.

- *Raptorial forelegs* ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)), with a spined femur and tibia that close like a jack-knife, evolved independently in mantids, mantispid lacewings, ambush and assassin bugs, water scorpions, some flies (Empididae) and others, a textbook case of **convergent evolution**. In mantids the strike lasts 50–100 ms.
- *The labial mask* of dragonfly and damselfly nymphs is the prementum and postmentum of the labium, folded under the head at rest and shot forward to seize prey with two hooked palps in about 10–25 ms. The strike is powered by a spring-loaded mechanism: muscles load elastic structures (with **resilin**) while a latch holds the mask, and release of the latch provides the power, a catapult like that of fleas and click beetles (Büsse and coauthors 2021).
- *Piercing-sucking predators* digest their prey outside the body. Assassin bugs inject saliva containing proteases, phospholipases and neurotoxic peptides that paralyse prey within seconds; the same saliva makes their bites very painful. Larvae of lacewings and antlions have sickle-shaped jaws, each formed by a mandible grooved below and a maxilla fitting into the groove, enclosing a canal through which they inject digestive enzymes and suck out the liquefied contents ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)).
- *Vision* is the main sense of aerial and ambush predators. Dragonflies and robber flies have acute zones with large facets and small interommatidial angles, and intercept prey by keeping the bearing of the prey constant (interception, [7.17.35](/7.17.35)). Praying mantids judge strike distance by stereopsis: mantids fitted with miniature coloured filters and shown three-dimensional films struck at virtual prey only when the image disparity indicated that it was within reach (Nityananda and coauthors 2016), the first demonstration of stereo vision in an invertebrate; unlike human stereopsis, it is based on changes in the image, not on matching static details.
- *Lures* are used by a few. Females of the firefly *Photuris* imitate the flash responses of females of *Photinus* and eat the males they attract (aggressive mimicry), and acquire from them the defensive lucibufagins they cannot make themselves; larvae of the fungus gnat *Arachnocampa* lure prey with light ([7.17.35](/7.17.35)). The orchid mantis *Hymenopus coronatus* of Southeast Asia, once interpreted as camouflaged among flowers, is itself more attractive to pollinating insects than real flowers, a lure in the form of a flower (O'Hanlon and coauthors 2014).

### 39.1.3. Foraging Strategies and the Functional Response

Predators either sit and wait (mantids, antlions, ambush bugs, larval dragonflies in many species) or search actively (ladybirds, ground beetles, lacewing larvae, ants). An actively searching ladybird larva walks rapidly along leaf edges and veins until it encounters an aphid; after a capture it turns more often and walks more slowly, concentrating its search in the vicinity, *area-restricted search*, which is efficient because aphids occur in clusters.

How many prey a predator eats depends on prey density. Holling (1959) distinguished three forms of the functional response, the number of prey eaten per predator per unit time as a function of prey density $N$:

$$
f_{\mathrm{I}}(N) = aN, \qquad f_{\mathrm{II}}(N) = \frac{aN}{1 + ahN}, \qquad f_{\mathrm{III}}(N) = \frac{bN^2}{1 + bhN^2}. \tag{39.1}
$$

Here $a$ is the attack rate (the area searched per unit time, times the probability of capture on encounter), and $h$ the handling time per prey (pursuing, subduing, eating and digesting). The type II form, Holling's *disc equation*, follows from dividing the predator's time between searching and handling: in time $T$, the predator searches for $T_s = T - h N_e$ and catches $N_e = a N T_s$ prey, from which $N_e/T = aN/(1 + ahN)$. It saturates at $1/h$ prey per unit time. Most insect predators and parasitoids show a type II response in the laboratory. A type III (sigmoid) response arises when predators attack rare prey less efficiently, for instance because they learn to search for common prey or switch between prey types.

![Functional responses of a predator (Eq. 39.1). Type I: intake proportional to prey density. Type II (Holling's disc equation, a = 0.5 per day per unit density, h = 0.05 day per prey): intake saturates at 1/h = 20 prey per day because of handling time. Type III: sigmoid, with intake low at low density. Lower panel: the proportion of prey eaten (intake divided by density). It declines with density in type II, so that a predator with a type II response alone cannot regulate prey (inverse density dependence), and rises at first in type III, which can stabilize prey numbers at low density.](./plots/functional-response.py)

The shape matters for pest control. With a type II response, the proportion of prey eaten falls as prey become more abundant, so that a predator alone cannot prevent an outbreak; with type III the proportion first rises, providing density-dependent mortality at low prey density. Predators also respond numerically, by aggregating where prey are abundant and by reproducing more when well fed. Ladybirds are efficient against aphids in part because their larvae need many aphids to complete development (several hundred for *Harmonia axyridis*), so that females lay eggs near aphid colonies that are young enough to support the larvae, and in part because adults aggregate at aphid outbreaks.

## 39.2. Parasitoids

### 39.2.1. Definition and Diversity

**Definition 39.2.1 (Parasitoid)** A **parasitoid** is an insect whose larva develops by feeding on or in the body of a single host arthropod (or another invertebrate) and eventually kills it, while the adult is free-living. Parasitoids are thus intermediate between predators, which kill and eat many prey during their lives, and true parasites, which feed on one or several hosts usually without killing them.

The defining traits of the parasitoid life history are that the host is a single individual that supports the complete development of one parasitoid (or a brood), that the host dies, and that the adult female, not the larva, chooses the host. Parasitoids include about 10% of all described insect species, and most of the undescribed ones may be parasitoid wasps. Parasitoidism has evolved once in Hymenoptera (in the ancestor of Orussidae and Apocrita; [7.17.34](/7.17.34)), about two dozen times in flies, several times in beetles, and in Strepsiptera, Neuroptera and a few moths:

| Group | Hosts | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Parasitoid Hymenoptera (Ichneumonoidea, Chalcidoidea, Platygastroidea and others; about 75% of all parasitoids) | Eggs, larvae, pupae and adults of nearly all orders, and spiders | Endo- and ectoparasitoids, idiobionts and koinobionts ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)) |
| Tachinidae (Diptera; about 8500 species) | Mostly caterpillars, beetles, bugs, grasshoppers | Eggs laid on the host, injected, or deposited as larvae on food plants that the host eats |
| Other flies: Bombyliidae, Phoridae, Pipunculidae, Acroceridae, Conopidae, some Sarcophagidae | Bee and wasp larvae, grasshopper eggs; ants (*Pseudacteon*), leafhoppers, spiders, bees in flight | Many lay eggs or first instars in flight |
| Coleoptera: Meloidae, Ripiphoridae, some Staphylinidae (*Aleochara*), Carabidae (*Lebia*, *Brachinus* larvae) | Bee nests, grasshopper egg pods, fly puparia, beetle pupae | First instars are active triungulins that find hosts ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)) |
| Strepsiptera | Bees, wasps, bugs, grasshoppers and others | Females remain permanently in the host ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)) |
| Neuroptera: Mantispidae | Spider egg sacs | Larvae board female spiders and enter the egg sac when it is made |
| Lepidoptera: Epipyropidae | Planthoppers, cicadas | Larvae feed externally on the host's abdomen under a waxy cover |

Parasitoids are classified as primary (on herbivores or predators), hyperparasitoids (on other parasitoids, facultatively or obligately) and, in some systems, tertiary parasitoids; communities of parasitoids on a single host species often contain ten to twenty species, organized in food webs that are analysed as a model of food-web structure in ecology.

### 39.2.2. Finding the Host

A parasitoid female must find hosts that are small, scattered, often hidden, and under selection to be inconspicuous. She searches hierarchically: first the habitat, then the host's food plant, then the host itself, using cues that become more reliable but harder to detect as she approaches:

| Stage | Cues | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Habitat and plant | Plant volatiles, especially those induced by herbivore feeding (synomones); visual shape and colour of the plant | Herbivore-induced maize volatiles attract *Cotesia marginiventris* ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)); egg deposition by a herbivore induces volatiles that attract egg parasitoids |
| Host vicinity | Odours of host frass, silk, scales, sex and aggregation **pheromones** of the host (kairomones) | *Trichogramma* respond to the sex pheromone of their moth hosts and to wing scales; bark beetle parasitoids to aggregation pheromones |
| Host itself | Vibrations, sounds, heat, contact chemicals on the host cuticle | *Ormia ochracea* locates singing crickets by sound ([7.17.35](/7.17.35)); orussids and some ichneumonids by vibrational sounding; host-marking pheromones show that a host is already parasitized |

Parasitoids learn. Females of *Cotesia* and *Microplitis* that have oviposited in a host on a given plant species, or that have smelled host frass, are later attracted more strongly to the odour of that plant; this associative learning lets generalist parasitoids track whatever hosts are locally abundant, and it is exploited in biological control by exposing mass-reared parasitoids to the target odours before release. Some parasitoids hitch rides on their hosts (phoresy): females of the egg parasitoids *Trichogramma* and some scelionids cling to adult female moths or bugs and are carried to the place where eggs are laid.

### 39.2.3. Overcoming and Regulating the Host

After finding a host the female assesses it with antennae and ovipositor, often rejecting hosts that are too small, already parasitized or of the wrong stage, and decides the number and sex of eggs to lay (clutch size and sex allocation; **local mate competition**, [7.17.34](/7.17.34)). Her offspring must then survive the host's defences.

Insects defend themselves against parasitoid eggs and larvae by *encapsulation*: hemocytes ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)) recognize the foreign object, adhere to it in layers, and the phenoloxidase cascade deposits melanin, which kills the parasitoid by asphyxiation and toxic quinones. In *Drosophila* larvae attacked by the figitid wasp *Leptopilina boulardi*, a special class of large flat hemocytes, lamellocytes, differentiates within hours to form the capsule. Hosts also defend themselves behaviourally: caterpillars thrash, regurgitate, drop on silk threads or bite; aphids kick and walk away; and *Drosophila* larvae infected by parasitoids prefer food containing ethanol, which kills the wasp larvae more than the fly larvae, a form of self-medication (Milan, Kacsoh and Schlenke 2012). In the pea aphid *Acyrthosiphon pisum*, resistance to the braconid *Aphidius ervi* is provided largely by a facultative bacterial symbiont, *Hamiltonella defensa*, and its bacteriophage APSE, which encodes toxins that kill the developing wasp (Oliver and coauthors 2003): resistance is inherited maternally, with the symbiont, and its frequency in field populations rises when parasitoids are abundant.

Parasitoids counter these defences. Koinobiont endoparasitoids suppress encapsulation with venom, ovarian proteins, teratocytes (cells released from the serosa of the parasitoid embryo) and, in braconids and ichneumonids, domesticated **polydnaviruses** ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)). They also regulate the host's development, arresting its metamorphosis, altering its endocrine system (lowering JH or ecdysteroid titres) and changing its nutrition to their own needs; a caterpillar parasitized by *Cotesia congregata* stops growing at a size that will just support the parasitoid brood.

### 39.2.4. Manipulating Host Behaviour

Several parasitoids change their hosts' behaviour in ways that benefit the parasitoid, examples of **host manipulation**:

- The braconid *Glyptapanteles* develops in caterpillars of the geometrid moth *Thyrinteina leucocerae*. When the wasp larvae have left the host and spun their cocoons, the caterpillar, still alive with one or two wasp larvae inside it, stops feeding, stands over the cocoons and violently swings its head at approaching predators; guarded broods suffer half as much predation as unguarded ones (Grosman and coauthors 2008).
- The braconid *Dinocampus coccinellae* develops in adult ladybirds; after its larva emerges and spins a cocoon between the ladybird's legs, the ladybird remains on top of the cocoon, partially paralysed and twitching, and its warning colours deter predators of the cocoon. The paralysis is associated with a virus that the wasp transmits, which replicates in the ladybird's nervous system (Dheilly and coauthors 2015).
- The ichneumonid *Hymenoepimecis argyraphaga*, an ectoparasitoid of the orb-weaving spider *Plesiometa argyra*, induces its host on the night before it kills it to build a special "cocoon web" with a few strong radial threads, which supports the wasp cocoon and protects it from rain (Eberhard 2000).
- The emerald cockroach wasp stings into the cockroach brain and leads the docile host to its burrow ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)).

Manipulation is not confined to parasitoids: hairworms drive crickets into water ([7.17.17](/7.17.17)), and fungi of the genus *Ophiocordyceps* make infected carpenter ants climb vegetation and bite into a leaf vein before they die, a position from which the fungal fruiting body releases spores onto the foraging trails below. The general problem, how genes in one organism act through the behaviour of another, is discussed with the extended phenotype in evolutionary biology ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)).

### 39.2.5. Clutch Size, Superparasitism and Hyperparasitoids

A gregarious parasitoid must decide how many eggs to lay in a host. Larger clutches give more offspring per host but smaller, less fecund adults, because the larvae share a fixed amount of food. The clutch that maximizes the fitness gained from one host (the Lack clutch) is larger than the clutch that maximizes fitness per egg, and when hosts are scarce relative to the female's egg supply, selection favours clutches near the Lack clutch; when hosts are abundant and eggs are limiting, smaller clutches are expected (Charnov and Skinner). Females of *Trichogramma* adjust clutch size to the size of the host egg, laying one egg in a small moth egg and several in a large one, and they measure host size by walking over the egg and timing the turns at its edge.

Superparasitism, laying in a host already parasitized by a conspecific, is usually avoided by females that detect the marking pheromone of a previous female, because in solitary parasitoids only one larva survives: first-instar larvae of many braconids and ichneumonids have large mandibles and kill rivals, or one larva suppresses the others physiologically. Superparasitism is favoured nevertheless when unparasitized hosts are rare, when many females exploit the same patch (so that a rejected host will be taken by a competitor), and, in some species, when a second egg improves the chance of overcoming the host's encapsulation response. Multiparasitism, by different parasitoid species, leads to similar contests.

Parasitoids are themselves hosts. A caterpillar of the cabbage white *Pieris rapae* may support the gregarious braconid *Cotesia glomerata*, whose cocoons are in turn attacked by hyperparasitoids of the genera *Lysibia* and *Acrolyta* (Ichneumonidae), which locate them by the volatiles that plants emit when the caterpillars inside them are parasitized (Poelman and coauthors 2012). In rice fields of southern China, the eggs of planthoppers are attacked by mymarid wasps of the genus *Anagrus* and by trichogrammatids, which can parasitize a large share of the eggs laid; their populations persist in the margins of fields on alternative hosts, which is one reason why preserving flowering vegetation and grassy margins around paddies is part of ecological engineering against rice pests ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)). Hyperparasitoids reduce the effectiveness of biological control, so programmes of classical biological control introduce primary parasitoids only after removing their hyperparasitoids in quarantine.

## 39.3. Host–Parasitoid Population Dynamics

### 39.3.1. The Nicholson–Bailey Model

Parasitoids are ideal subjects for population models: each parasitized host produces parasitoids instead of hosts, generations are often synchronized, and the number of searching females is easy to relate to the number of hosts parasitized. Nicholson and Bailey (1935) proposed the first such model.

**Formula 39.3.1 (Nicholson–Bailey Model)** Let $H_t$ and $P_t$ be the numbers of hosts and of adult female parasitoids in generation $t$. Suppose each parasitoid searches an area $a$ during its life (the *area of discovery*), at random and independently of the others, so that the number of encounters of a given host follows a Poisson distribution with mean $aP_t$, and the probability that a host escapes parasitism is $\mathrm{e}^{-aP_t}$. Unparasitized hosts reproduce with net rate $\lambda > 1$, and each parasitized host produces $c$ female parasitoids. Then

$$
H_{t+1} = \lambda H_t\,\mathrm{e}^{-aP_t}, \qquad P_{t+1} = c\,H_t\left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-aP_t}\right). \tag{39.2}
$$

The model has an equilibrium with both species present. Setting $H_{t+1} = H_t = H^*$ gives $\mathrm{e}^{-aP^*} = 1/\lambda$, so

$$
P^* = \frac{\ln\lambda}{a}, \qquad H^* = \frac{\lambda\ln\lambda}{(\lambda - 1)\,ac}. \tag{39.3}
$$

The equilibrium parasitoid density depends only on the host's rate of increase and the parasitoid's searching efficiency, and the host equilibrium is lower the more efficient the parasitoid: a parasitoid with a large area of discovery holds the host at low density, the goal of biological control. But the equilibrium is unstable for all $\lambda > 1$, $a$ and $c$. Linearizing Eq. (39.2) at the equilibrium gives a Jacobian matrix whose determinant is $\lambda\ln\lambda/(\lambda - 1) > 1$, so that the product of the two eigenvalues exceeds one in modulus; since they are complex conjugates, both lie outside the unit circle, and small perturbations grow into oscillations of increasing amplitude until one species is lost (compare the neutral cycles of the continuous **Lotka–Volterra model**, [2.4.11](/2.4.11)). The parasitoid overexploits the host: when hosts are abundant, parasitoids increase, parasitize nearly all hosts, and crash with them.

### 39.3.2. Aggregation and Other Stabilizing Mechanisms

Real host–parasitoid systems persist, sometimes for decades at low, steady densities, which shows that something in the model is missing. The most important missing element is heterogeneity in the risk of parasitism. Parasitoids aggregate in patches of high host density, hosts in some patches are hard to reach, and parasitoids interfere with each other. May (1978) replaced the Poisson escape term by the zero term of a **negative binomial distribution**, which describes clumped attacks:

$$
H_{t+1} = \lambda H_t\left(1 + \frac{aP_t}{k}\right)^{-k}, \qquad P_{t+1} = cH_t\left[1 - \left(1 + \frac{aP_t}{k}\right)^{-k}\right]. \tag{39.4}
$$

The parameter $k > 0$ measures clumping: as $k \to \infty$ attacks become random and Eq. (39.4) reduces to Eq. (39.2); small $k$ means that attacks are concentrated on a few hosts while many escape. The equilibrium of Eq. (39.4) is stable if and only if $k < 1$. Hassell, May, Pacala and Chesson (1991) generalized the result: in a wide class of models, the interaction is stabilized when the squared coefficient of variation of the density of searching parasitoids around hosts, $\mathrm{CV}^2$, exceeds about 1 (the "$\mathrm{CV}^2 > 1$ rule"). Aggregation stabilizes not because parasitoids prefer dense host patches as such, but because it creates a partial refuge: a fraction of hosts experiences little parasitism, so the parasitoids cannot overexploit the host population, and the effect is density-dependent because the refuge matters most when parasitoids are abundant.

Other stabilizing mechanisms are host density dependence (self-limitation of the host, as in Beddington's model), a physical refuge (hosts in bark crevices, gall walls or deep in fruit), a type III functional response, interference among searching parasitoids, and spatial structure: in models on a lattice of patches connected by local dispersal, local extinctions are compensated by recolonization, and the regional populations persist with moving waves and spirals of abundance (Hassell and coauthors 1991).

Laboratory systems illustrate the theory. Utida (1957) kept the azuki bean weevil *Callosobruchus chinensis* with the braconid *Heterospilus prosopidis* for more than 100 generations; the populations oscillated with a period of about six to seven generations but did not go extinct, because the weevil larvae hidden in beans and the host's own density dependence provided stability. Huffaker (1958) found that a predatory mite and its prey mite went extinct quickly on a single cluster of oranges, but persisted for several cycles in a large array of oranges where barriers slowed dispersal, the first experimental demonstration that spatial structure can stabilize enemy–victim interactions.

The main lesson for biological control is that the best agents combine a high searching efficiency (low $H^*$) with aggregation or other stabilizing features. The winter moth *Operophtera brumata*, accidentally introduced into Nova Scotia, was brought under control in the 1950s by two introduced European parasitoids, the tachinid *Cyzenis albicans* and the ichneumonid *Agrypon flaveolatum*, and its densities fell by more than an order of magnitude and remained low; analysis showed that predation of pupae in the soil, a density-dependent mortality, contributed to the stability (Roland 1994).

## 39.4. Defence

Defences are usually divided into primary defences, which act before a predator detects or attacks the prey (crypsis, masquerade, aposematism, mimicry, avoiding the predator's active times and places), and secondary defences, which act during an encounter (flight, startle displays, mechanical and chemical defence, death feigning).

### 39.4.1. Crypsis and Masquerade

**Definition 39.4.1 (Crypsis and Masquerade)** **Crypsis** is the prevention of detection by a predator: by **background matching** (resembling the colour and pattern of the background), **disruptive coloration** (contrasting marks that break up the outline), **countershading** (darker above than below, which cancels the shading by overhead light) or transparency. **Masquerade** is the prevention of recognition: the animal is detected but mistaken for an inedible object, such as a twig, leaf, thorn, stone or bird dropping.

Background matching is the commonest defence of insects. Grasshoppers of grasslands are green or straw-coloured, and those of burnt ground are black; many species have both forms, determined by the background during development (homochromy) or by humidity. The peppered moth *Biston betularia* is the textbook example of natural selection by bird predation on crypsis ([7.10.5](/7.10.5)): melanic forms (*carbonaria*) spread in industrial regions of Britain in the 19th century, where tree bark was blackened by soot, and declined after clean-air legislation from the 1950s. The melanic allele is caused by the insertion of a transposable element into the gene *cortex* around 1819 (van't Hof and coauthors 2016), the same gene that controls wing patterns in *Heliconius* (Section 39.4.4). Majerus's experiment of releasing about 4800 moths over six years showed that birds ate more of the conspicuous form, as predicted (Cook and coauthors 2012). The caterpillars of the peppered moth match the colour of the twigs they rest on, even when blindfolded, by sensing light with opsins expressed in the skin (Eacock and coauthors 2019).

Masquerade is perfected in stick insects and leaf insects ([7.17.31](/7.17.31)), in the leaf-like katydids whose forewings have veins, simulated fungal spots and even "bite marks", in the orange oakleaf *Kallima inachus* of southern China, whose closed wings resemble a dead leaf with midrib and petiole, in thorn-mimicking treehoppers, and in the young caterpillars of many swallowtails (including the common Chinese citrus swallowtails *Papilio xuthus* and *P. polytes*), which resemble bird droppings before becoming green in later instars. Masquerade works through recognition: domestic chicks that had experience of real twigs were slower to attack twig-mimicking caterpillars than chicks that had not, although they detected both equally (Skelhorn and coauthors 2010). Some insects decorate themselves: larvae of green lacewings carry the empty skins of their aphid prey and plant debris on their backs, and nymphs of some assassin bugs stack the corpses of their ant prey into a "backpack" that protects them from spiders.

### 39.4.2. Startle Displays and Eyespots

When discovered, many cryptic insects suddenly expose conspicuous colours or eyespots, a *deimatic* or startle display: the eyed hawkmoth *Smerinthus ocellata* raises its forewings to reveal two large blue eyespots on the hind wings; saturniid moths (*Automeris*, *Antheraea*) and many mantids flash eyespots or bright wing colours; and some caterpillars inflate their thorax to display false eyes resembling a snake's head. Experiments with the peacock butterfly *Aglais io*, which flicks open its wings to show four large eyespots and produces a hissing sound by rubbing the wing veins, showed that blue tits fled from butterflies whose eyespots were intact more often than from butterflies whose eyespots had been painted over (Vallin and coauthors 2005). Large eyespots appear to work by intimidating predators, perhaps because they resemble the eyes of the predators' own enemies, perhaps simply because they are conspicuous and unexpected.

Small marginal eyespots work differently: they deflect attacks away from the vital body. The African butterfly *Bicyclus anynana* has large ventral eyespots in the wet season, when it is active among green vegetation, and small ones in the dry season, when it rests cryptically on dead leaves; the development of the eyespots and this seasonal plasticity are explained in [7.5.39](/7.5.39) and [7.17.29](/7.17.29). In experiments with praying mantids as predators, wet-season butterflies with large eyespots were attacked first at the eyespots and escaped with damaged wings, while dry-season forms were attacked less often but at the body (Prudic and coauthors 2015). The false head of many hairstreak butterflies (Lycaenidae), with thread-like tails that resemble antennae on the hind wings, an eyespot and a habit of moving the hind wings while at rest, has the same function.

### 39.4.3. Mechanical and Chemical Defences

Mechanical defences include a hard cuticle (weevils are so heavily armoured that birds often swallow them whole and pass them alive), spines and urticating hairs (tussock moths, lappet moths, slug caterpillars; [7.17.36](/7.17.36)), detachable appendages (the legs of stick insects and crane flies break off at preformed joints, autotomy), cases and shelters (caddis larvae, bagworms, leaf rollers, the froth of spittlebugs, the waxy threads of woolly aphids), jumping (fleas, flea beetles, click beetles, springtails), death feigning (thanatosis; in the red flour beetle *Tribolium castaneum*, selection lines that feign death for longer survive predatory spiders better; Miyatake and coauthors 2004) and the escape responses of insects that hear bats ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)).

Chemical defences are of three origins: synthesized by the insect itself; sequestered from food plants ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)); or produced by symbionts. They are discharged from many kinds of exocrine glands, by reflex bleeding of toxic hemolymph (ladybirds, blister beetles, some grasshoppers), or carried in the tissues as a deterrent to anything that bites. Examples:

| Insect | Compounds | Origin and delivery |
|---|---|---|
| Stink bugs (Pentatomidae) and other Heteroptera | (*E*)-2-alkenals (hexenal, octenal, decenal) and alkanes | Metathoracic scent glands in adults, dorsal abdominal glands in nymphs |
| Ants (Formicinae) | Formic acid (up to 50–60% of the poison gland fluid) | Sprayed from the acidopore ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)) |
| Blister beetles (Meloidae) | Cantharidin | Synthesized by males and passed to females at mating; reflex bleeding; causes skin blisters (the "Chinese blister beetle" *Mylabris* in traditional medicine) |
| Rove beetles *Paederus* | Pederin, an amide that inhibits protein synthesis | Produced by an endosymbiotic *Pseudomonas* bacterium in females; crushing the beetle on the skin causes *Paederus* dermatitis, common in southern China in summer |
| Burnet moths (*Zygaena*) | Cyanogenic glycosides | Synthesized de novo and sequestered from *Lotus* |
| Ladybirds | Alkaloids (coccinelline, harmonine) | Synthesized; reflex bleeding from the knee joints |
| Termite soldiers (Nasutitermitinae) | Diterpenes in a sticky glue | Sprayed from the frontal gland ([7.17.38](/7.17.38)) |
| Bombardier beetles (Brachininae, Paussinae) | Benzoquinones, ejected hot | Pygidial glands with a reaction chamber (below) |

The bombardier beetles have the most remarkable chemical weapon. Each pygidial gland consists of a reservoir, which stores an aqueous solution of hydroquinones and hydrogen peroxide, and a reaction chamber with thick cuticular walls, lined with cells that secrete catalases and peroxidases. When the beetle is attacked, muscles force reservoir fluid into the reaction chamber, where two reactions take place explosively:

$$
\ce{2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2}, \qquad \ce{C6H4(OH)2 + H2O2 -> C6H4O2 + 2H2O}.
$$

Catalase decomposes hydrogen peroxide, and the oxygen released raises the pressure; peroxidase oxidizes hydroquinone to *p*-benzoquinone, the irritant. Both reactions are strongly exothermic, and the mixture is ejected at about 100 °C through an opening at the abdominal tip that the beetle can aim in almost any direction, even over its back. In *Brachinus* the discharge is pulsed at about 500 pulses per second: each pulse of reaction pressure closes the inlet valve from the reservoir, and the chamber refills between pulses, which protects the beetle from its own heat (Dean and coauthors 1990; Arndt and coauthors 2015).

**Example 39.4.1 (The Heat Budget of the Bombardier Spray)** Take a reservoir fluid of 1 kg containing about 25% hydrogen peroxide and 10% hydroquinones by mass, values of the order measured in *Brachinus* (Aneshansley and coauthors 1969). It contains $250/34.0 \approx 7.4\,\mathrm{mol}$ of $\ce{H2O2}$ and $100/110 \approx 0.91\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydroquinone. With standard reaction enthalpies of about $-203\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for the oxidation of hydroquinone by hydrogen peroxide and $-94.6\,\mathrm{kJ}$ per mole of $\ce{H2O2}$ decomposed to water and oxygen, the oxidation releases $0.91 \times 203 \approx 185\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and the decomposition of the remaining $6.5\,\mathrm{mol}$ of peroxide about $615\,\mathrm{kJ}$, together about $0.8\,\mathrm{MJ}$. Heating 1 kg of aqueous solution from 25 °C to 100 °C requires about $4.2\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \times 75\,\mathrm{K} \approx 315\,\mathrm{kJ}$; the remaining $\approx 0.5\,\mathrm{MJ}$ can vaporize about $0.5/2.26 \approx 0.2\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water. The calculation shows that the reactions are more than sufficient to bring the spray to the boiling point, as measured, with part of it vaporized, which contributes to the propulsion of the spray.

:::

Many defensive chemicals are effective at very low doses because they act on conserved targets of predators: pain and irritant receptors (quinones, formic acid, aldehydes activate the TRPA1 channel of vertebrates and the homologous channels of arthropods), sodium channels (alkaloids), or the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (cardenolides). The cost of chemical defence has been demonstrated in several insects: sequestering or synthesizing toxins reduces growth or fecundity, which is one reason why many insects rely on cheaper cryptic defences instead.

### 39.4.4. Aposematism and Mimicry

Chemically defended insects are often conspicuous: black and yellow or orange (wasps, many beetles, burnet moths), red and black (ladybirds, pyrrhocorid bugs, lycid beetles), or brightly patterned (*Heliconius*, monarchs, many caterpillars of toxic plants). **Aposematism** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)) works because predators learn faster to avoid conspicuous unpalatable prey than cryptic ones, remember them longer, and are often innately wary of particular colour combinations (domestic chicks avoid black-and-yellow stripes without experience). Warning signals are often multimodal: the hiss of the peacock butterfly, the ultrasonic clicks of tiger moths that warn bats of their distastefulness ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)), and pyrazine odours shared by many aposematic insects. Comparative analyses support a predicted sequence in the evolution of warning signals: in butterfly larvae, gregariousness evolved after unpalatability and aposematism more often than before them (Sillén-Tullberg 1988), consistent with the idea that the first conspicuous mutants were protected more easily in groups.

Mimicry arises from aposematism. In **Batesian mimicry** palatable species resemble defended models: hover flies, clearwing moths (Sesiidae) and longhorn beetles imitate wasps and bees ([7.17.35](/7.17.35), [7.17.36](/7.17.36)); many beetles, bugs, spiders and flies imitate ants (myrmecomorphy); and the females of *Papilio polytes*, the common Mormon of southern China and southern Asia, have one non-mimetic form and two or more forms that mimic toxic swallowtails of the genus *Pachliopta*, while males are non-mimetic. The polymorphism is controlled by alternative alleles of the sex-determination gene *doublesex*, an inversion that holds together a supergene (Kunte and coauthors 2014; Nishikawa and coauthors 2015). In **Müllerian mimicry** defended species share a pattern: species of *Heliconius* form mimicry rings with each other and with other distasteful butterflies, and the two Müllerian co-mimics *H. erato* and *H. melpomene* have converged on the same 25 or so geographic races across the Neotropics, the same few genes (*optix*, *WntA*, *cortex* and *aristaless*) controlling the patterns in both species (Reed and coauthors 2011).

The frequency dependence that distinguishes the two forms of mimicry can be seen in a simple model of predator learning.

**Formula 39.4.1 (A Learning Model of Batesian Mimicry)** Suppose a predator attacks a prey item with a given warning pattern with probability $q$. After eating a palatable mimic it raises $q$ by a fraction $\alpha$ of the remaining distance to 1, $q \mapsto q + \alpha(1 - q)$; after tasting an unpalatable model it lowers $q$ by a fraction $\beta$, $q \mapsto q - \beta q$. If a proportion $m$ of the prey with the pattern are mimics, the expected change of $q$ per attack is zero when $m\alpha(1 - q) = (1 - m)\beta q$, so that the long-run attack probability is

$$
q^* = \frac{m\alpha}{m\alpha + (1 - m)\beta} = \frac{m}{m + (1 - m)\beta/\alpha}. \tag{39.5}
$$

Here $\beta/\alpha$ measures the unpalatability of the model relative to the reward of eating a mimic: a highly noxious model teaches avoidance faster than a mimic reverses the lesson. The attack probability, and so the mortality of both mimics and models, rises with the proportion of mimics. A Batesian mimic is therefore favoured when rare and loses protection as it becomes common, negative frequency dependence that can maintain polymorphic mimicry, as in *Papilio polytes* and *P. dardanus*, and that harms the model. The more noxious the model, the more mimics it can support.

Protection declines as mimics become common, and noxious models protect more mimics. In Müllerian mimicry, by contrast, both species are unpalatable, every attack on either lowers $q$, and a shared pattern reduces the number of individuals of each species sacrificed to educate predators (Müller's model, [7.10.17](/7.10.17)); selection is positively frequency-dependent and drives co-mimics to converge on the commonest local pattern, which explains the geographic races of *Heliconius*. Real systems lie on a spectrum: co-mimics differ in noxiousness, and a weakly defended species can act as a partial Batesian parasite of a strongly defended one (quasi-Batesian mimicry).

Mimicry is often imperfect. Many hover flies resemble wasps only vaguely. Proposed explanations are that predators generalize broadly so that a rough resemblance suffices, that imperfect mimics resemble several models at once, and that selection for accurate mimicry is weak in small species, which are less profitable prey: across 38 species of hover flies, mimetic accuracy increased with body size (Penney and coauthors 2012), as the last explanation predicts.

### 39.4.5. Collective Defence

Living in groups can protect insects in several ways. Grouping dilutes the risk for each individual, and an individual surrounded by others is safer (the "selfish herd"); aposematic larvae in groups present a larger, more memorable signal; groups can mount coordinated displays, as sawfly larvae that rear up in unison and regurgitate resin ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)); and colonies can defend themselves actively, as social insects do with stings, soldiers and the heat-balling of hornets by Asian honey bees ([7.17.38](/7.17.38), [7.17.34](/7.17.34)). An extreme form of predator satiation is shown by the periodical cicadas *Magicicada* of eastern North America, which emerge synchronously after 13 or 17 years underground in densities of up to several million per hectare; birds and mammals eat only a small fraction, and the long prime-numbered cycles may have evolved to prevent predators or parasitoids with shorter cycles from tracking them.

## Summary

- Insect predators occur in many orders; raptorial forelegs evolved convergently; dragonfly nymphs strike with a spring-loaded labial mask; piercing predators digest prey externally; mantids use stereopsis; some predators lure prey (aggressive mimicry).
- The functional response (Eq. 39.1): type I linear, type II (disc equation, saturating at $1/h$), type III sigmoid; only type III gives density-dependent mortality at low prey density.
- Parasitoids (Definition 39.2.1) kill a single host that supports their development; about 10% of insect species, mostly Hymenoptera, also flies, beetles, Strepsiptera and others. Females locate hosts hierarchically by plant volatiles, host kairomones and host signals, and learn cues.
- Hosts resist by encapsulation (lamellocytes, melanization), behaviour (self-medication) and protective symbionts (*Hamiltonella* in aphids); parasitoids counter with venoms, teratocytes and polydnaviruses, regulate host development and sometimes manipulate host behaviour (*Glyptapanteles*, *Dinocampus*, *Hymenoepimecis*).
- The Nicholson–Bailey model (Formula 39.3.1) has equilibrium $P^* = \ln\lambda/a$, $H^* = \lambda\ln\lambda/[(\lambda-1)ac]$, which is always unstable. Aggregation of risk (negative binomial with $k < 1$, the $\mathrm{CV}^2 > 1$ rule), refuges, host density dependence, type III responses and spatial structure stabilize host–parasitoid interactions.
- Defences: crypsis and masquerade (Definition 39.4.1; peppered moth and *cortex*, *Kallima*, bird-dropping caterpillars), startle displays and deflective eyespots, mechanical defences, chemical defences (synthesized, sequestered, or from symbionts as pederin). The bombardier beetle's catalase and peroxidase reactions heat the quinone spray to 100 °C (Example 39.4.1).
- Aposematism is reinforced by predator learning and innate wariness; Batesian mimicry is negatively frequency-dependent (Formula 39.4.1, Eq. 39.5), Müllerian mimicry positively; supergenes (*doublesex* in *Papilio polytes*) and a few shared genes (*optix*, *WntA*, *cortex*) control mimetic patterns. Group living dilutes risk and allows collective defence and predator satiation.

## Questions

1. A ladybird larva has an attack rate $a = 0.5$ per day (in units of area searched per aphid density) and a handling time of $h = 0.05$ day per aphid. How many aphids per day does it eat at densities of 10, 100 and 1000 aphids per unit area? What fraction of the aphids is eaten at each density, and what does this imply for biological control?

   **Answer** By the disc equation $f = aN/(1 + ahN)$: at $N = 10$, $f = 5/(1 + 0.25) = 4$ aphids per day; at $N = 100$, $f = 50/(1 + 2.5) \approx 14.3$; at $N = 1000$, $f = 500/(1 + 25) \approx 19.2$, close to the maximum $1/h = 20$. The fractions eaten per day are 0.4, 0.14 and 0.019. The proportion eaten falls as aphids become more abundant (inverse density dependence), so a fixed number of ladybirds cannot stop a rapidly growing aphid outbreak; control requires releasing or attracting predators early, at low aphid density, or a numerical response (more predators where aphids are abundant).

2. In the Nicholson–Bailey model with $\lambda = 3$, $a = 0.01$ and $c = 1$, find the equilibrium numbers of hosts and parasitoids. If a more efficient parasitoid with $a = 0.02$ were introduced instead, how would the host equilibrium change? Why is the equilibrium of the model not observed in nature?

   **Answer** $P^* = \ln 3/0.01 \approx 110$ and $H^* = 3\ln 3/(2 \times 0.01 \times 1) \approx 165$. Doubling $a$ halves both: $P^* \approx 55$, $H^* \approx 82$; a more efficient parasitoid suppresses the host more, the principle behind choosing agents for biological control. The equilibrium is unstable for any parameter values: deviations grow into oscillations of increasing amplitude, because random search lets the parasitoids overexploit the hosts when they are common. Real systems persist because of mechanisms absent from the model: aggregation of parasitism in some patches (negative binomial $k < 1$), refuges, host density dependence, interference among parasitoids and spatial structure.

3. Using Example 39.4.1, explain why the bombardier beetle does not cook itself. What anatomical and physiological features make the spray possible?

   **Answer** The reactants are stored in a reservoir without the enzymes, where they are stable; the reaction occurs only in a separate reaction chamber with thick, sclerotized cuticular walls that tolerate heat and pressure, and its enzymes (catalase and peroxidase) are secreted by gland cells into the chamber. Only small amounts of fluid enter the chamber at a time: in *Brachinus* the discharge is pulsed at about 500 Hz, each burst of pressure closing the valve from the reservoir and ejecting the hot products, after which the chamber refills and cools; heat is therefore produced in small portions and carried away with the spray, and vaporization of part of the water (the remaining energy in the heat budget) limits the temperature to the boiling point.

4. A palatable butterfly species mimics a toxic model. Using Eq. (39.5), describe how the mimic's protection changes as it becomes more common, and predict what happens when a second, differently patterned mimetic form arises in the same species.

   **Answer** The attack probability $q^* = m/[m + (1 - m)\beta/\alpha]$ increases with the proportion $m$ of mimics among prey with the pattern: when mimics are rare, predators mostly encounter models and avoid the pattern; as mimics become common, predators are rewarded more often and attack more. Mimics are thus favoured when rare. A new form resembling a different, locally abundant model starts with a low $m$ relative to its own model and enjoys higher protection, so it spreads until the protection of the two forms is equal. This negative frequency-dependent selection can maintain a polymorphism of several mimetic forms, each matching a different model, as in females of *Papilio polytes* and *P. dardanus*; a supergene keeps the components of each pattern together.

5. In pea aphid populations, the proportion of aphids carrying *Hamiltonella defensa* rises in summers when parasitoids are abundant and falls when they are scarce. Explain this pattern and its implications for biological control with *Aphidius*.

   **Answer** *Hamiltonella* and its phage kill developing *Aphidius* larvae, so infected aphids survive attack better; when parasitoids are abundant, infected matrilines increase. The symbiont is costly in the absence of parasitoids (infected aphids have lower fecundity or are more susceptible to other enemies), so it declines when parasitoids are rare; this fluctuating selection maintains the infection at intermediate frequencies. For biological control, a high prevalence of protective symbionts can reduce the effectiveness of releases of *Aphidius*; monitoring symbiont frequencies and using parasitoid strains or species that overcome the defence, or combining parasitoids with predators that are not affected by it, can help.

6. (Open question) Why might many hover flies be only poor mimics of wasps? Design a field or laboratory study to test one explanation.

   **Answer** Candidate explanations: (a) predators generalize broadly, so rough resemblance is sufficient; (b) relaxed selection on small species, which are less profitable for predators; (c) imperfect mimics are intermediate between several models (multimodel mimicry); (d) imperfect resemblance to human eyes may be good resemblance in the visual system of birds (ultraviolet patterns, movement); (e) constraints or trade-offs with thermoregulation and other functions. A test of (b): measure mimetic accuracy (with image analysis of pattern similarity to wasps, ideally modelled in bird colour space) and body size across many hover fly species, controlling for phylogeny; then offer birds artificial prey of different sizes and accuracies in the field to see whether the advantage of accurate mimicry increases with prey size. A test of (d): compare similarity measured in human and avian visual models and in behaviour (wing-waving, buzzing).
