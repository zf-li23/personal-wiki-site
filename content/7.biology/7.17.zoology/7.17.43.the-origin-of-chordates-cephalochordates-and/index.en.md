The chordates include the vertebrates and two groups of small marine invertebrates: the lancelets, fish-shaped animals a few centimetres long that burrow in clean sand, and the tunicates, sea squirts and their planktonic relatives, whose adults are filter-feeding bags that hardly look like animals at all. Before the vertebrates evolved a head, a skull and a backbone, the chordate body plan was already in place: a stiff rod along the back, a hollow nerve cord above it, a pharynx perforated by slits for filter feeding, a ventral gland that secretes the feeding mucus, and a muscular tail behind the anus that swims by lateral undulation. These characters are best seen in the lancelets, which keep them throughout life, and in the swimming larvae of sea squirts, which lose most of them at metamorphosis. This chapter is the bridge between the invertebrates of [7.17.7](/7.17.7)–[7.17.42](/7.17.42) and the vertebrates of [7.17.45](/7.17.45) onward: the chordate characters defined here are used in all later chapters.

The chapter first defines the chordate characters and describes their development (§43.1). It then treats the cephalochordates, with amphioxus as the representative animal (§43.2), and the tunicates (§43.3). Section 43.4 explains why tunicates, not lancelets, are the closest relatives of vertebrates, and §43.5 reviews how the chordate body plan may have arisen. The chapter ends with a comparison with the traditional classification (§43.6). The deuterostomes as a whole and the hemichordates, whose gill slits are homologous with those of chordates, are treated in [7.17.41](/7.17.41); the developmental mechanisms that build the chordate body (the organizer, neural induction, neurulation, somitogenesis) are in [7.5.16](/7.5.16)–[7.5.23](/7.5.23).

## 43.1. The Chordate Body Plan

### 43.1.1. The Defining Characters

**Definition 43.1.1 (Chordata)** The **Chordata** (chordates) are the clade of deuterostomes comprising the Cephalochordata, the Tunicata and the Vertebrata. At least at some stage of their life they have a notochord, a dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits, an endostyle (or its derivative, the thyroid gland) and a muscular postanal tail; ancestrally their body-wall muscles form serially repeated blocks, the myomeres.

The five characters are defined here; their fates in the vertebrates are treated in the comparative anatomy chapters ([7.17.56](/7.17.56)–[7.17.68](/7.17.68)).

**Definition 43.1.2 (Notochord)** The **notochord** is a flexible, rod-shaped skeletal organ that runs along the dorsal midline between the gut and the nerve cord; it derives from the dorsal midline mesoderm (chordamesoderm) of the embryo and consists of cells containing large fluid-filled vacuoles, or (in lancelets) of stacked muscle plates, enclosed in a tough sheath of collagen and elastic fibres.

The notochord works as a hydrostatic skeleton in the form of a rod. The turgor of its vacuolated cells, pressing against the inextensible sheath, makes it stiff in compression but flexible in bending. When the segmental muscles of one side contract, the notochord prevents the body from shortening, so that the contraction bends it sideways, and its elasticity helps to straighten it again. In embryos the notochord is also a signalling centre: it secretes Sonic hedgehog and other signals that pattern the ventral neural tube and the somites ([7.5.18](/7.5.18)). It persists throughout life in lancelets, larvaceans, lampreys and hagfishes and in some fishes (sturgeons, lungfishes, the coelacanth); in other vertebrates it is replaced by the vertebral column, and in mammals its remnant forms the nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral discs.

**Definition 43.1.3 (Dorsal Hollow Nerve Cord)** The **dorsal hollow nerve cord** of chordates is a tubular central nervous system lying dorsal to the notochord, with a central canal (neurocoel); it forms from the dorsal ectoderm of the embryo, a neural plate that folds into a neural tube (neurulation), and in vertebrates its anterior part becomes the brain.

The chordate nerve cord differs from the ventral nerve cords of annelids and arthropods ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.7)) in three ways: it is dorsal to the gut, it is hollow, and it is single rather than paired. Its formation by the rolling up of a neural plate is induced by signals from the underlying chordamesoderm, the organizer of [7.5.16](/7.5.16) and [7.5.17](/7.5.17).

**Definition 43.1.4 (Pharyngeal Slits)** **Pharyngeal slits** (pharyngeal gill slits, pharyngeal clefts) are paired openings in the wall of the pharynx, formed where outpocketings of the pharyngeal endoderm (pharyngeal pouches) meet the ectoderm and break through, which connect the lumen of the pharynx with the exterior or with a surrounding atrium.

The slits are older than the chordates: they are shared with hemichordates ([7.17.41](/7.17.41#sec-41.5.1)). In lancelets and tunicates they serve for filter feeding, and in fishes they became respiratory gills. In tetrapods the **pharyngeal pouches** form in the embryo but usually do not open; they and the **pharyngeal arches** between them give rise to the middle ear cavity, the tonsils, the thymus, the parathyroid glands and, through the skeleton of the arches, to the jaws, the hyoid and the ear ossicles ([7.17.58](/7.17.58)).

**Definition 43.1.5 (Endostyle)** The **endostyle** is a longitudinal ciliated and glandular groove in the floor of the pharynx of tunicates, lancelets and larval lampreys; it secretes the mucus sheet used to trap food particles in filter feeding, and some of its cells concentrate iodine and synthesize iodinated tyrosines.

The endostyle is the evolutionary precursor of the vertebrate thyroid gland. In the ammocoete larva of lampreys it is still a pharyngeal feeding groove; at metamorphosis part of it is transformed into thyroid follicles that secrete **thyroid hormones** ([7.14.34](/7.14.34#sec-34.7.3)). The iodine-binding pharyngeal cells of acorn worms suggest that iodine chemistry in the pharynx is older still.

The *postanal tail* is the part of the body that extends behind the anus, containing the notochord, the nerve cord and muscle but not the gut; in protostomes and hemichordates the anus is at or near the posterior tip. With the *myomeres* (serially repeated blocks of body-wall muscle on each side, separated by connective-tissue septa, the myosepta), it forms a propulsive organ that swims by lateral undulation. The myomeres derive from the somites of the embryo, which in vertebrates arise from the paraxial mesoderm under the control of the segmentation clock ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)). Myomeres are present in lancelets and vertebrates but absent in tunicates, whose tail muscles are not segmented; because tunicates are the sister group of vertebrates, segmented muscles were probably present in the ancestral chordate and lost in tunicates (§43.4).

### 43.1.2. A Plan for Swimming and Filter Feeding

The characters form two functional complexes:

| Complex | Characters | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Locomotor | Notochord, myomeres, postanal tail, dorsal nerve cord with segmental nerves | Swimming by lateral undulation: alternate contraction of the myomeres on the two sides bends the stiff notochord; the tail extends the propulsive surface behind the body cavity |
| Feeding | Pharyngeal slits, endostyle, ciliated pharyngeal tracts, atrium | Filter feeding: cilia draw water into the mouth and out through the slits; particles are trapped on a mucus sheet secreted by the endostyle and carried to the gut |

In the first chordates both complexes probably belonged to the same animal, an active, burrowing or swimming filter feeder like a lancelet. Tunicates separated them in time, the larva swimming and the adult filter feeding; vertebrates changed the feeding complex into a predatory one, with a muscular pharynx, gills used for respiration and, later, jaws.

Filter feeding with a mucus net works at very small scales, where viscosity dominates. The mesh of the mucus sheet of ascidians and the spaces between the cilia of the gill bars are of the order of a micrometre, and the water moves through them at about a millimetre per second.

**Example 43.1.1 (Flow through a Mucus Filter)** With a mesh width $d = 0.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, a flow speed $U = 1\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$ and the kinematic viscosity of seawater $\nu = \mu/\rho \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, the **Reynolds number** of the flow through the mesh is

$$
Re = \frac{U d}{\nu} = \frac{10^{-3} \times 0.5 \times 10^{-6}}{10^{-6}} = 5 \times 10^{-4}.
$$

Inertia is negligible ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.3.2)): particles are not sieved by impact but follow streamlines until they touch a fibre of the net and stick to it, and the resistance to flow grows steeply as the mesh becomes finer. This is why tunicates and lancelets drive water with cilia, which generate low pressures over a large surface, and why the filters can retain particles much smaller than the mesh, down to bacteria of about $1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in ascidians and even smaller in larvaceans.

## 43.2. Subphylum Cephalochordata: Lancelets

**Definition 43.2.1 (Cephalochordata)** The **Cephalochordata** (cephalochordates, lancelets, amphioxus) are small, laterally compressed, fish-shaped marine chordates that retain all the chordate characters throughout life; their notochord, built of muscular lamellae, extends forward beyond the nerve cord to the tip of the snout; they have serial myomeres and gonads, an oral hood with cirri and a ciliated wheel organ, a pharynx with numerous slits enclosed in an atrium that opens by a ventral atriopore, and no cranium, vertebrae, paired fins or heart.

There are about 30–35 species in three genera, *Branchiostoma* (with paired rows of gonads) and *Asymmetron* and *Epigonichthys* (gonads on the right side only). They live in shallow tropical and temperate seas, buried in clean, coarse sand ("amphioxus sand") with the front end protruding, and swim in short bursts, mostly at night. The common European species *Branchiostoma lanceolatum* gave the old name amphioxus ("pointed at both ends"), and the American *B. floridae* has become the main laboratory species. In China, *B. belcheri* and *B. japonicum* occur on the coasts of Xiamen and Qingdao; the amphioxus of Liuwudian near Xiamen supported the only commercial lancelet fishery in the world until the mid-twentieth century, and both species are now nationally protected. Lancelets are 4–7 cm long and live two to four years.

### 43.2.1. Body Wall, Skeleton and Muscles

The body is covered by a one-layered epidermis secreting a thin cuticle, over a thin dermis of gelatinous connective tissue. Fins are median only: a low dorsal fin, a caudal fin, and a ventral (preanal) fin from the atriopore to the anus, supported by fin-ray boxes of connective tissue. Paired metapleural folds hang from the ventral sides of the pharyngeal region; they form the floor of the atrium and are sites of gas exchange.

The main skeleton is the notochord. Uniquely among chordates, its plates are modified muscle cells (with paramyosin-like filaments resembling the catch muscles of bivalves), innervated by the nerve cord, which can stiffen the rod by contracting inside its sheath, perhaps an aid in burrowing. Cartilage-like connective tissue supports the oral cirri, the velar tentacles and the gill bars. About 60 pairs of chevron-shaped myomeres, their apices pointing forward, run along the sides. The myomeres of the left and right sides are offset by half a segment, as are their nerves; this asymmetry probably suits the alternating contractions of swimming. A peculiarity of lancelets is that the muscle cells send fine processes, the muscle tails, to the surface of the nerve cord, where synapses form; the "ventral roots" of the nerve cord are thus bundles of muscle processes rather than motor axons.

### 43.2.2. Feeding and Digestion

Lancelets are ciliary suspension feeders. Water and particles enter through the oral hood, whose cirri exclude sand grains, into the vestibule. On the walls of the vestibule, ciliated bands with brownish epithelium form the *wheel organ*, whose beating cilia appear to rotate under the microscope and sweep particles toward the mouth. In the roof of the vestibule, a ciliated mucous groove ends in Hatschek's pit, which secretes mucus and contains cells producing gonadotropin-releasing hormone and other neuropeptides; it is regarded as homologous with the adenohypophysis of vertebrates. The mouth is an opening in a membranous velum, surrounded by velar tentacles.

The pharynx occupies about half of the body length and has up to 180–200 pairs of slits, added throughout life. Primary gill bars, which contain an extension of the coelom, alternate with secondary (tongue) bars that grow down across each slit, the same arrangement as in acorn worms, and the bars are joined by synapticles. Lateral cilia on the bars drive the water through the slits into the atrium and out of the atriopore; the gills are not the main respiratory surface. Food is trapped by the mucus secreted by the endostyle, carried up the walls of the pharynx by cilia to the dorsal epibranchial groove, which carries it backward into the gut as a cord; the peripharyngeal ciliated bands at the front of the pharynx connect the endostyle to the epibranchial groove. Behind the pharynx the gut forms a midgut, from which a large blind hepatic caecum projects forward on the right side of the pharynx, then an ileocolic ring, where cilia rotate the food cord, and a hindgut opening by the anus, slightly to the left of the ventral midline in front of the caudal fin. The hepatic caecum secretes digestive enzymes, absorbs and stores nutrients and synthesizes the yolk protein vitellogenin; it is probably homologous with the vertebrate liver.

### 43.2.3. Circulation, Excretion and the Coelom

The circulation is closed and follows the vertebrate pattern, but there is no heart and the blood has no respiratory pigment and few cells. Blood flows forward beneath the pharynx in the ventral aorta, which, with small contractile swellings at the bases of the branchial arteries (the "branchial hearts"), pumps it up through the gill bars into paired dorsal aortae; these join behind the pharynx into a single dorsal aorta that runs backward. Blood from the intestine collects in a subintestinal vein, which divides into capillaries in the hepatic caecum, forming a hepatic portal system like that of vertebrates; from there and from the paired anterior and posterior cardinal veins of the body wall it returns through the sinus venosus to the ventral aorta. Gas exchange takes place mainly across the body surface, especially the metapleural folds, aided by the thinness of the animal.

Excretion is by about 90–100 pairs of nephridia lying above the pharynx, one at each primary gill bar, which open into the atrium. Their terminal cells, cyrtopodocytes (formerly called solenocytes), each bear a long flagellum in a collar of microvilli and stand on the wall of the coelom, around blood vessels; fluid is filtered from the blood across the basement membrane into the coelom and from there into the nephridium. They therefore work like the filtration nephridia of protostomes and resemble the podocytes of vertebrate glomeruli, but their homology with the **protonephridia** of other bilaterians is doubtful. A single anterior nephridium, Hatschek's nephridium, lies on the left near the mouth.

The coelom forms by enterocoely in the anterior part of the embryo (§43.2.5); in the adult it is reduced to narrow canals by the growth of the myomeres and the atrium. The atrium, which surrounds the pharynx, is not a coelom: it is a cavity lined by ectoderm, formed when the metapleural folds grow down and fuse beneath the pharynx in the larva.

### 43.2.4. Nervous System and Sense Organs

The nerve cord is a hollow tube without a distinct brain; its anterior end, the cerebral vesicle, is only slightly enlarged. In larvae it opens at the front by a neuropore; in adults a small depression, Kölliker's pit, remains. The cord gives off dorsal and ventral nerve roots at each segment; unlike those of vertebrates, the dorsal roots have no ganglia and carry both sensory and motor fibres to the skin and the gut, and they do not join the ventral roots. The epidermis contains many sensory cells, concentrated on the oral cirri.

Detailed comparisons of gene expression and of the electron-microscopic structure of the cerebral vesicle have shown that it corresponds to the vertebrate diencephalon and parts of the midbrain, and that its anterior pigment spot, the frontal eye, is homologous with the paired eyes of vertebrates: like the vertebrate retina, its photoreceptors are ciliary, express a ciliary opsin and the same transcription factors (*Pax6*, *Otx*, *Rx*), and they are shaded by a pigment cell expressing *Mitf* (Vopalensky et al. 2012). Other photoreceptors, the Joseph cells and the dorsal ocelli (Hesse organs: cups of a photoreceptor and a pigment cell along the nerve cord), are rhabdomeric and express melanopsin, like the intrinsically photosensitive ganglion cells of the vertebrate retina. The small lamellar body in the cerebral vesicle has been compared with the vertebrate pineal organ.

### 43.2.5. Reproduction and Development

The sexes are separate. About 26 pairs of gonads (fewer in *Asymmetron*, on one side only) lie in the atrial wall. In the breeding season, at dusk, the gonad walls rupture, gametes are released into the atrium and out through the atriopore, and fertilization is external. The eggs are small (about $0.1\text{–}0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$) and isolecithal; cleavage is radial and holoblastic, a coeloblastula forms and gastrulates by invagination. The neural plate forms on the dorsal side and is covered by the epidermis before it rolls into a tube; for a time the neural tube connects with the archenteron through the blastopore (the neurenteric canal), and the blastopore then closes and the anus forms nearby. The notochord forms from the dorsal midline of the archenteron roof; the somites form on either side by enterocoely, as pouches of the archenteron, in the anterior part of the body, but posterior somites form from the tail bud without pouching, as in vertebrates.

The larva is planktotrophic and strikingly asymmetrical: the mouth opens on the left side, the first gill slits on the right side, and a "club-shaped gland" lies beside the pharynx; at metamorphosis, after weeks to months in the plankton, the slits are rearranged into two symmetrical rows, the metapleural folds enclose the atrium and the young lancelet settles. The asymmetry is controlled by the same Nodal–Pitx2 left–right pathway that acts in vertebrates.

Amphioxus is the best living proxy for the ancestral chordate genome. Its genome, sequenced in 2008, has a single Hox cluster of 15 genes and a single copy of most gene families that are represented by two to four paralogues in vertebrates, which confirmed that vertebrate genomes underwent two rounds of **whole-genome duplication** after they separated from the lancelet lineage (Putnam et al. 2008). The genes are still arranged on chromosomes in an order recognizable in vertebrates, in hemichordates and even in cnidarians, so that the "chordate ancestral linkage groups" can be reconstructed.

## 43.3. Subphylum Tunicata

**Definition 43.3.1 (Tunicata)** The **Tunicata** (tunicates, urochordates) are marine chordates whose body is enclosed in a tunic, a secreted covering of a cellulose polymer (tunicin) and proteins that contains living cells; the pharynx is enlarged into a branchial basket perforated by many slits (stigmata) and, except in larvaceans, enclosed in an ectodermal atrium; the notochord and nerve cord are restricted to the tail of the larva (and of the adult larvaceans), the body-wall muscles are not segmented, and the coelom is reduced to the pericardium and epicardial sacs.

Tunicates (about 3,000 species) are among the most common animals of hard surfaces in the sea and of the plankton. They comprise three traditional classes: the sessile ascidians or sea squirts, the pelagic salps and their relatives (Thaliacea), and the planktonic larvaceans (Appendicularia). The name Urochordata ("tail chordates") refers to the notochord being confined to the tail.

The tunic is unique: tunicates are the only animals that synthesize cellulose, using a cellulose synthase acquired by horizontal gene transfer from a bacterium, probably an actinobacterium, in the ancestor of all tunicates (Nakashima et al. 2004; Sasakura et al. 2005). The tunic is secreted by the epidermis but contains wandering blood cells and in many species blood vessels; it may be gelatinous, leathery or reinforced with calcareous spicules, and serves for support and protection.

### 43.3.1. Class Ascidiacea: Sea Squirts

**Definition 43.3.2 (Ascidiacea)** The **Ascidiacea** (ascidians, sea squirts) are tunicates whose adults are sessile, attached to the substrate, solitary or colonial, with an oral (branchial) siphon and an atrial siphon usually both opening upward; the large branchial basket has rows of stigmata and the U-shaped gut opens into the atrium; development is usually through a swimming, nonfeeding tadpole larva.

Ascidians range from solitary species a few centimetres to over 20 cm tall (*Ciona*, *Styela*, *Halocynthia*, *Pyura*) to colonial forms in which many small zooids share a common tunic and sometimes a common atrial opening (*Botryllus*, *Didemnum*). *Styela clava*, the representative animal of the Chinese textbooks, a stalked, leathery species of the western Pacific, is abundant on piers, rafts and aquaculture installations and has spread to many other coasts as an invasive species.

(A) Tadpole larva (lateral view, anterior to the left): the trunk contains the sensory vesicle with the ocellus and the otolith, the visceral ganglion, the rudiments of the pharynx and endostyle, and three adhesive papillae at the front; the tail contains the notochord (about 40 cells in *Ciona*), the dorsal hollow nerve cord and rows of unsegmented muscle cells; the larval tunic forms a fin. (B) Metamorphosis: the larva attaches by its papillae, the tail is resorbed with the notochord and most of the nerve cord, and the body rotates so that the siphons come to point away from the substrate. The adult has the following organization, which is best understood as a lancelet's feeding apparatus in a sessile body:

- Body wall and tunic. The tunic covers the mantle (the body wall), in which muscle bands run along the body and sphincters around the siphons; when disturbed, the animal contracts and squirts water from both siphons.
- Feeding. Cilia on the stigmata draw water through the oral siphon, past a ring of oral tentacles, into the branchial basket and through the stigmata into the atrium. The endostyle on the ventral side secretes a mucus net that is carried up both sides of the basket, collects particles as water flows through, and is rolled into a cord by the dorsal lamina or languets and passed into the oesophagus. The gut is U-shaped: oesophagus, stomach, intestine and anus opening into the atrium near the atrial siphon. Ascidians filter as efficiently as mussels and compete with cultured bivalves.
- Circulation. The circulation is open. A tubular heart in a pericardial sac (a coelomic remnant) beats by peristaltic waves that start from a pacemaker at one end, and every few minutes the direction is reversed, so that the same vessels alternately carry blood to the branchial basket and to the viscera ([7.14.13](/7.14.13#sec-13.7.2)). The blood contains many cell types; in some ascidians certain cells accumulate vanadium to concentrations ten million times those of seawater, for reasons still unknown.
- Excretion. There are no nephridia; nitrogenous wastes are stored as urate concretions in renal vesicles near the intestine or lost by diffusion.
- Nervous system. A cerebral ganglion lies in the body wall between the siphons. Beneath it lies the neural gland, which opens into the pharynx by a ciliated funnel; it has been compared with the pituitary, although its homology is uncertain. Sensory cells are concentrated around the siphons, including secondary mechanoreceptor cells that may be homologues of the hair cells of the vertebrate ear and lateral line.
- Reproduction. Ascidians are hermaphrodites, with an ovary and a testis near the gut loop and ducts opening into the atrium; most avoid self-fertilization by releasing eggs and sperm at different times or by a self-incompatibility system. Colonial species also bud, and the compound ascidian *Botryllus* has become a model of allorecognition: colonies that meet fuse if they share an allele at a polymorphic fusibility locus and reject each other otherwise.

Solitary ascidians spawn small eggs; cleavage is bilateral and determinate, and the development of a few hundred cells into a tadpole within a day, with cells fated as early as the 8-cell stage, made ascidians classical subjects of the study of autonomous specification ([7.5.6](/7.5.6#sec-6.2)). The tadpole larva, about 1–2 mm long and composed of about 2,600 cells, is a chordate in miniature: its tail has a notochord of exactly 40 cells in *Ciona*, a hollow nerve cord and muscle, and the trunk has a sensory vesicle with an ocellus and an otolith, a visceral ganglion, and the rudiments of the pharynx and endostyle. It does not feed and swims for hours to a day, first toward the light and then, when ready to settle, toward shade and downward. It attaches by its adhesive papillae and metamorphoses within a day: the tail is resorbed, the notochord, the tail nerve cord and the sensory organs disappear, the body rotates about 90° so that the siphons point upward, the branchial basket enlarges and the filtering apparatus becomes functional. Because the adult lacks most of the chordate characters of the larva, this change is called *retrogressive metamorphosis*. The term describes the anatomy, not a step backward in evolution: the sessile adult is as derived as the larva.

### 43.3.2. Class Thaliacea: Salps, Doliolids and Pyrosomes

**Definition 43.3.3 (Thaliacea)** The **Thaliacea** (thaliaceans: salps, doliolids and pyrosomes) are pelagic tunicates without a free-swimming larva in which the oral and atrial siphons lie at opposite ends of a barrel-shaped body, so that the water current used for feeding also propels the animal; their life cycles usually alternate solitary sexual and colonial or aggregated asexual generations.

Salps and doliolids swim by jet propulsion: bands of circular muscle contract and expel water from the atrial siphon, and the elastic tunic refills the body through the oral siphon, which is closed by a valve during the stroke. Pyrosomes are colonies of many small zooids embedded in a common tunic around a central cavity, into which the atrial siphons open; the colony, from a few centimetres to many metres long, moves slowly by the combined ciliary currents and glows brilliantly with bacterial or intrinsic bioluminescence.

The life cycle of salps is a **metagenesis**, an alternation of diploid asexual and sexual generations like that of cnidarians ([7.17.9](/7.17.9#sec-9.3.1)). A solitary oozooid, developed from a fertilized egg brooded in the parent like a mammalian embryo with a placenta, produces by budding a chain of aggregate blastozooids, which are hermaphrodites and reproduce sexually; each sexual zooid broods one embryo. Doliolids add a third stage with specialized feeding and nursing zooids on the "tail" of the oozooid. Budding allows salps to multiply very rapidly when food is abundant, forming blooms of thousands per cubic metre that graze the phytoplankton and produce fast-sinking faecal pellets, an important route of carbon to the deep sea.

### 43.3.3. Class Appendicularia: Larvaceans

**Definition 43.3.4 (Appendicularia)** The **Appendicularia** (larvaceans) are small (mostly less than 1 cm) planktonic tunicates whose adult retains a tadpole-like form, with a trunk and a muscular tail containing the notochord and nerve cord; they have no atrium, a pharynx with one pair of slits opening directly to the exterior, and live inside a temporary, secreted mucous house that serves as a feeding filter.

The larvacean *Oikopleura* secretes its house from a field of epidermal cells on the trunk within minutes. Beating of the tail inside the house draws water through coarse inlet filters, which exclude large particles, and through a fine, folded food-concentrating filter, from which concentrated particles are sucked into the mouth; the water exits through an outlet, slowly moving the house. When the filters clog, every few hours, the animal escapes through an emergency exit and inflates a new house from a pre-formed rudiment. Larvaceans capture particles down to about $0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, including bacteria; they are among the most abundant animals of the plankton, and their discarded houses, sinking as "marine snow", carry a substantial part of the carbon fixed in the upper ocean to the deep sea. *Oikopleura dioica* has a life cycle of about a week, a compact genome of about 70 Mb that has lost many genes and its Hox cluster, and is a model of rapid genome evolution.

### 43.3.4. Relationships within Tunicates

Phylogenomic analyses place the larvaceans as the sister group of all other tunicates and the thaliaceans within the ascidians, probably as the sister group of the phlebobranch ascidians (Delsuc et al. 2018; Kocot et al. 2018). Two consequences follow. First, the Ascidiacea in the traditional sense are paraphyletic, and thaliaceans are pelagic descendants of sessile ancestors. Second, the free-living, tailed larvaceans are not ascidian larvae that became sexually mature, as had long been assumed. The tadpole-like body of the adult larvacean is the ancestral form of tunicates, and sessile adults with a retrogressive metamorphosis evolved within the group. The traditional three classes are kept here as convenient, with this caveat.

| Group | Habit | Adult tail and notochord | Atrium | Larva | Reproduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Appendicularia | Planktonic, in a mucous house | Present | Absent; one pair of slits open to the exterior | None (direct development to a tadpole-like adult) | Sexual only; mostly hermaphrodite |
| Ascidiacea | Sessile, solitary or colonial | Absent | Present, siphons usually both upward | Tadpole, nonfeeding | Hermaphrodite; colonial species also bud |
| Thaliacea | Pelagic, solitary and chain-forming | Absent | Present, siphons at opposite ends | None (tailed but encapsulated in doliolids) | Metagenesis: sexual and budding generations |

## 43.4. Olfactores: Tunicates as the Sister Group of Vertebrates

For most of the twentieth century, lancelets were regarded as the closest relatives of vertebrates. They share with vertebrates a long notochord, segmented muscles, a closed circulation with a vertebrate pattern, a hepatic caecum and a fish-like way of swimming, and they seemed an obvious model of the vertebrate ancestor. Tunicates, with their sessile adults, appeared to be a side branch.

Phylogenomic analyses reversed this view. With large numbers of genes, tunicates group with vertebrates and lancelets branch first (Delsuc et al. 2006, 2008); the earlier result had been an artefact of long-branch attraction, tunicate genes evolving very rapidly.

**Definition 43.4.1 (Olfactores)** The **Olfactores** are the clade comprising the tunicates and the vertebrates, the sister group of the cephalochordates within the Chordata.

The name was coined by Jefferies (1991), who had predicted the relationship from fossils with a supposed olfactory organ. Several developmental characters support it:

- *Neural crest-like cells*. In the ascidians *Ecteinascidia* and *Ciona*, cells at the border of the neural plate express the neural crest genes (*Snail*, *FoxD*, *Pax3/7*, *Msx*), delaminate and migrate to form pigment cells (Jeffery et al. 2004; Abitua et al. 2012). Lancelets have neural plate border genes but no migratory crest cells.
- *Placode-like ectoderm*. Ascidian larvae have anterior ectodermal regions that express the genes of vertebrate cranial placodes (*Six1/2*, *Eya*) and give rise to sensory cells of the siphons and the neural gland.
- *A cardiopharyngeal field*. In *Ciona* the heart and the muscles of the atrial siphon arise from common progenitor cells, as the second heart field and the pharyngeal (branchiomeric) muscles of vertebrates do ([7.5.28](/7.5.28)).

The tree has two implications for the ancestral chordate. Characters shared by lancelets and vertebrates but absent from tunicates, the segmented myomeres, the long notochord and the closed circulation, were probably present in the ancestral chordate and lost in tunicates. And tunicates, rather than being primitive, are the most derived chordates: they have lost the segmented body, much of the genome (their Hox genes are dispersed and incomplete) and, in ascidians, the free-living adult. The ancestral chordate was a free-living, lancelet-like filter feeder, not a sessile, ascidian-like animal.

## 43.5. The Origin of Chordates

### 43.5.1. Classical Hypotheses

The question of how the chordate body plan arose from an invertebrate one has been debated for two centuries.

1. *Inverted annelids or arthropods*. In 1822 Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire argued that a vertebrate is an arthropod turned on its back: the ventral nerve cord of an arthropod, with the heart above it, corresponds to the dorsal nerve cord and ventral heart of a vertebrate. Gaskell and Patten developed the idea in the early twentieth century, deriving vertebrates from eurypterids or other arthropods. The hypothesis failed because protostome nerve cords are solid and paired, the mouth of an inverted annelid would face upward, and the embryology of chordates shows no trace of such a reversal; protostomes and chordates are not closely related.
2. *Garstang's larval hypothesis*. Garstang (1894, 1928) proposed that chordates arose from an echinoderm-like dipleurula larva: the ciliary bands of the larva moved dorsally and fused into the neural tube, the adoral band became the endostyle, and the elongated larva developed a muscular tail with a notochord. Through paedomorphosis, the precocious maturation of a larva, the swimming stage escaped the sessile adult; vertebrates would descend from an ascidian tadpole that never metamorphosed ([7.5.39](/7.5.39#sec-39.4.2)). For much of the twentieth century this was the dominant textbook view.
3. *The echinoderm and calcichordate hypotheses*. Because echinoderms and chordates are both deuterostomes, many authors derived chordates from echinoderm-like ancestors, and Jefferies saw in the Paleozoic stylophoran carpoids calcite-plated ancestors of chordates ([7.17.42](/7.17.42#sec-42.7.2)).

All three are rejected by current evidence. The phylogeny places lancelets at the base of the chordates and tunicates as the sister group of vertebrates; a sessile, ascidian-like adult is derived within tunicates, so the ancestral chordate was a free-living adult, and no tadpole needed to escape a sessile adult. The tornaria and the echinoderm larvae are "swimming heads" that do not express the trunk program ([7.17.41](/7.17.41#sec-41.3.4)), so their ciliary bands cannot be the precursors of a chordate nerve cord running along the trunk. And echinoderms and hemichordates are the sister group of chordates, not their ancestors.

### 43.5.2. Dorsoventral Inversion

One element of Geoffroy's idea has nevertheless returned, in a new form. In all bilaterians the dorsoventral axis is patterned by a gradient of BMP signalling opposed by the BMP antagonist Chordin (Sog in *Drosophila*). In *Drosophila*, annelids and acorn worms, BMP (Dpp) is expressed dorsally and Chordin ventrally, and the central nervous system forms on the side with Chordin, the ventral side. In chordates the gradient is reversed: BMP is ventral, Chordin is secreted by the organizer on the dorsal side, and the nervous system forms dorsally ([7.5.14](/7.5.14), [7.5.16](/7.5.16#sec-16.6), [7.5.17](/7.5.17)). The molecular coordinates of the body are therefore inverted relative to the anatomical ones (De Robertis and Sasai 1996; Arendt and Nübler-Jung 1994).

Anatomical observations agree with an inversion within the deuterostomes, between the ambulacrarian-like ancestor and the chordates:

| Feature | Acorn worms (and protostomes) | Chordates |
|---|---|---|
| BMP domain | Dorsal | Ventral |
| Chordin domain | Ventral | Dorsal |
| Main nerve cord | Ventral (acorn worms: dorsal and ventral cords) | Dorsal |
| Gill pores | Dorsal | Slits open ventrally into the atrium (lancelets) |
| Blood flow in the dorsal vessel | Forward | Backward (dorsal aorta) |
| Blood flow in the ventral vessel | Backward | Forward (ventral aorta, heart) |
| Epibranchial ridge (enteropneusts) vs endostyle | Dorsal | Ventral |

The mouth does not fit: it is ventral in both. The inversion therefore required either a migration of the mouth around the body or a new mouth on the new ventral side; the asymmetric larva of amphioxus, whose mouth opens on the left side, has been cited as a trace of such a migration. The hypothesis does not imply that chordates descend from an inverted animal; it says that during the origin of chordates, the side of the body that faced the substrate changed. One scenario is that the ancestor, a worm-like filter feeder with gill slits and a diffuse nerve net, came to swim or rest with its former dorsal side down, perhaps lying in sediment like a lancelet; the nervous system then concentrated on the side facing away from the bottom, under the influence of a Chordin-secreting organizer. Whether the hemichordate collar cord corresponds to the chordate nerve cord (which would argue against inversion of the whole nervous system) remains debated.

Shading of the body wall shows the BMP signalling domain (dark) and the Chordin domain (light) of the embryo; in the vessels, a dot marks blood flowing toward the head and a cross blood flowing toward the tail. In the acorn worm BMP is dorsal and Chordin ventral; the main nerve cord lies ventrally, the gill pores open dorsally and the dorsal vessel carries blood forward. (C) The interpretation: turning the ancestral body upside down brings its nerve cord, vessels and gill openings into the chordate positions, but the mouth, ventral in both, must have moved or formed anew.](./plots/dv-inversion.py)

### 43.5.3. Cambrian Chordates

Soft-bodied chordates are preserved in the Cambrian Lagerstätten:

| Fossil | Deposit and age | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| *Pikaia gracilens* | Burgess Shale, about 508 million years ago | A stem chordate with myomeres, long regarded as a cephalochordate; restudy described a small head with a pair of tentacles and a series of possible pharyngeal appendages (Conway Morris and Caron 2012), but its notochord and the orientation of its body remain debated |
| *Cathaymyrus diadexus* | Chengjiang, about 518 million years ago | Lancelet-like, with pharyngeal slits and myomeres; possibly a stem cephalochordate |
| *Yunnanozoon*, *Haikouella* | Chengjiang | Yunnanozoans of disputed position, between stem deuterostomes and stem vertebrates ([7.17.41](/7.17.41#sec-41.1.3)) |
| *Shankouclava* | Chengjiang | A sac-like animal with a branchial basket and two siphons, interpreted as the oldest tunicate; the status of the earlier "*Cheungkongella*" is disputed |
| *Haikouichthys*, *Myllokunmingia*, *Metaspriggina* | Chengjiang, Burgess Shale | Early jawless vertebrates with a head, paired eyes, gill pouches and zigzag myomeres ([7.17.45](/7.17.45)) |

The fossils show that by about 518 million years ago chordates and even vertebrates already existed with the essential features of their body plans. They do not document the transition from a non-chordate deuterostome, which must have taken place in the Ediacaran.

## 43.6. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Three chordate characters (notochord, dorsal nerve tube, gill slits), sometimes with the postanal tail and a ventral heart | Five characters: notochord, dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits, endostyle, postanal tail; gill slits are shared with hemichordates and are a deuterostome character |
| Protochordates (Urochordata + Cephalochordata) as a natural group, "invertebrate chordates" | A paraphyletic grade: tunicates are closer to vertebrates than to lancelets |
| Cephalochordates as the most advanced protochordates and the sister group of vertebrates | Cephalochordates are the sister group of Olfactores (tunicates + vertebrates) |
| Larvaceans as paedomorphic ascidian larvae; ascidians primitive | Larvaceans are the sister group of the other tunicates; the sessile adult evolved within tunicates |
| Three tunicate classes of equal rank | Thaliaceans nested within ascidians; Ascidiacea paraphyletic |
| Chordates derived from echinoderm-like ancestors via an ascidian tadpole (Garstang) | The ancestral chordate was a free-living, lancelet-like animal; ambulacrarians are a sister group |
| Arginine phosphate in invertebrates, creatine phosphate in chordates | Phosphagens are scattered across phyla and have no phylogenetic value |
| Amphioxus about 25 species in two genera | About 30–35 species in three genera (*Branchiostoma*, *Asymmetron*, *Epigonichthys*) |
| Pigment spot of amphioxus as a vestigial balance organ or a shade for the ocelli | The frontal eye, homologous with vertebrate eyes |
| Nephridia with solenocytes, compared with protonephridia | Filtration nephridia with cyrtopodocytes; homology with protonephridia doubtful |
| Hatschek's pit and the neural gland of ascidians as pituitary homologues | Hatschek's pit is probably homologous with the adenohypophysis; the homology of the ascidian neural gland is uncertain |

The representative animals of the Chinese textbooks, *Branchiostoma belcheri* and *Styela clava*, are described in §43.2 and §43.3.1 in the same terms as in those textbooks. The textbook account of amphioxus development (coeloms by enterocoely in the front of the body and from the tail bud behind) and of the circulation (no heart, branchial hearts, hepatic portal vein) agrees with current descriptions.

## Summary

- Chordates (Definition 43.1.1) have a notochord (Definition 43.1.2), a dorsal hollow nerve cord (Definition 43.1.3), pharyngeal slits (Definition 43.1.4), an endostyle (Definition 43.1.5) and a muscular postanal tail, with myomeres ancestrally. They form a locomotor complex (notochord, myomeres, tail, nerve cord) and a feeding complex (slits, endostyle, ciliated tracts) for low-Reynolds-number mucus filtration (Example 43.1.1).
- Cephalochordates (Definition 43.2.1): about 30–35 species of lancelets; notochord of muscle lamellae to the snout; oral hood, wheel organ, Hatschek's pit, pharynx with up to 200 pairs of slits in an atrium; closed circulation without heart or pigment, hepatic portal system; nephridia with cyrtopodocytes; cerebral vesicle and frontal eye; asymmetrical larva; a genome without the vertebrate duplications.
- Tunicates (Definition 43.3.1): cellulose tunic acquired by horizontal gene transfer; branchial basket; notochord only in the larval tail. Ascidians (Definition 43.3.2; *Styela*, *Ciona*, *Botryllus*): sessile adults, reversing heart, tadpole larva, retrogressive metamorphosis. Thaliaceans (Definition 43.3.3): pelagic jet-propelled salps, doliolids, pyrosomes with metagenesis. Larvaceans (Definition 43.3.4): tadpole-like adults in mucous houses.
- Olfactores (Definition 43.4.1): tunicates are the sister group of vertebrates; they share neural crest-like cells, placode-like ectoderm and a cardiopharyngeal field. Larvaceans branch first within tunicates; the ancestral chordate was a free-living, lancelet-like animal.
- Classical hypotheses of chordate origin (inverted arthropods, Garstang's larva, echinoderm ancestors) are rejected; the dorsoventral axis of chordates is inverted relative to that of other bilaterians (BMP–Chordin, vessels, gill openings).
- Cambrian chordates: *Pikaia*, *Cathaymyrus*, *Shankouclava*, and the early vertebrates of Chengjiang.

## Questions

1. Which of the five chordate characters are found outside the chordates, and which are true chordate synapomorphies?

   **Answer** Pharyngeal slits are shared with hemichordates (and probably some stem echinoderms) and are a deuterostome character; an iodine-binding pharyngeal epithelium related to the endostyle may also be older. A dorsal nerve cord occurs in a short form in the collar of acorn worms, but its homology is disputed. The notochord, the muscular postanal tail with myomeres, the endostyle as a defined organ and the hollow dorsal nerve cord along the whole trunk induced by the notochord are chordate synapomorphies. Characters shared by chordates and hemichordates are evidence about the ancestral deuterostome, not about a hemichordate–chordate clade.
2. Why does the position of tunicates as the sister group of vertebrates make it unlikely that the ancestral chordate was sessile?

   **Answer** The ancestral chordate is reconstructed from its three descendant lineages. Lancelets are free-living as adults, vertebrates are free-living, and within tunicates the earliest-branching group, the larvaceans, are free-living adults with a tail and notochord, while sessile adults occur only in the ascidians (and secondarily pelagic thaliaceans nested within them). The most parsimonious reconstruction is a free-living ancestor, with a sessile adult evolving once within tunicates. If lancelets had been the sister group of vertebrates and tunicates the earliest branch, with ascidians at their base, a sessile ancestor with a tadpole larva (as in Garstang's scheme) would have been more plausible.
3. A lancelet has gill slits but uses them mainly for feeding, and its blood carries no pigment. Where does it take up oxygen, and why is this possible?

   **Answer** Mainly across the body surface, especially the thin metapleural folds and the atrial wall, by diffusion. A lancelet is small (a few millimetres thick), slender and not very active, so the diffusion distance from the surface to the tissues is short and the oxygen demand low ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.1.2)). The blood serves mainly to distribute nutrients; dissolved oxygen in the plasma is enough for the small requirement. The gill bars, covered with cilia that pump water and mucus, are not specialized for gas exchange, unlike the gill filaments of fishes, which evolved when the pharynx became muscular and respiratory.
4. Explain why the term "retrogressive metamorphosis" does not mean that ascidians are evolving backward.

   **Answer** The term describes the anatomy of metamorphosis: the larva has a notochord, a dorsal nerve cord, a tail and sense organs, and the adult loses them, so the adult appears "simpler" than the larva in chordate characters. But both stages are adaptations of the same life cycle: a short-lived, nonfeeding larva for dispersal and choice of settlement site, and a sessile adult specialized for filter feeding, with an enlarged branchial basket, a tunic, siphons and a complex reproductive and immune system. Phylogenetically the ascidian life cycle is derived within tunicates, not ancestral; evolution has no direction toward more or fewer structures.
5. Using the Reynolds number, explain why ciliary pumps rather than muscles drive the feeding currents of lancelets and ascidians, while fishes ventilate their gills with muscles.

   **Answer** Filter feeding on bacteria and phytoplankton requires a fine filter, with pores of about a micrometre. Flow through such pores is at a Reynolds number far below 1 (Example 43.1.1), dominated by viscosity, so the pressure needed is proportional to the flow speed and inversely to the square of the pore size; to filter a large volume at acceptable pressure, the filter must be large and the water must move slowly through it. Cilia generate small pressures but act over a very large surface (the whole branchial basket), matching the low-pressure, high-volume requirement efficiently. Fish gills are not fine filters: they pass water at high speed over gill filaments for gas exchange, at higher Reynolds numbers and higher pressures, which muscular pumps produce efficiently; cilia could not.
6. List the observations that support a dorsoventral inversion in the chordate lineage, and the main difficulty of the hypothesis.

   **Answer** Supporting observations: BMP is expressed dorsally and Chordin ventrally in protostomes and acorn worms but the reverse in chordates; the central nervous system develops on the Chordin side in both, ventrally in protostomes and dorsally in chordates; the main contractile vessel carries blood forward on the dorsal side of annelids and acorn worms but on the ventral side (heart, ventral aorta) of chordates; the gill pores of acorn worms are dorsal while the endostyle and the openings of the chordate pharynx are ventral; and the epibranchial ridge of acorn worms may correspond to the ventral endostyle. The main difficulty is the mouth, which is ventral in both groups: an inversion would place it dorsally, so it must have migrated around the body or formed anew, for which the embryology of chordates provides only indirect hints (the left-sided mouth of the amphioxus larva). The homology of the hemichordate collar cord with the chordate nerve cord, if confirmed, would also complicate the picture.
