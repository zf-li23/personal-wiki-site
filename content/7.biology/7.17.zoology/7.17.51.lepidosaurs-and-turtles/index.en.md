The turtles, the tuatara and the squamates — lizards, amphisbaenians and snakes — are the living sauropsids that are not archosaurs. They are not each other's closest relatives: the lepidosaurs (tuatara and squamates) are the first branch of living reptiles, and the turtles are the **sister group** of the archosaurs ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.4.1)). They are treated together here because both are ectothermic, both retain many ancestral reptile features, and both are the "reptiles" of everyday language and of the traditional class Reptilia. With about 12 000 species, squamates are the second-largest group of tetrapods after birds; turtles, with about 360, are a small group with one of the most remarkable body plans among vertebrates.

The chapter has two parts. The first (§51.1–51.3) treats the lepidosaurs: their characters, the tuatara, the diversity and phylogeny of squamates, the feeding apparatus with its mobile skull, the snakes, and the special abilities of lizards. The second (§51.4–51.6) treats the turtles: the shell and how it develops, the origin of turtles from fossils, their diversity and reproduction, including temperature-dependent sex determination. Section 51.7 summarizes the lepidosaurs and turtles of China, and §51.8 compares the modern classification with the traditional one. The temporal fenestrae that distinguish these lineages are explained in [7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.3); the comparative anatomy of the skull and its kinesis in [7.17.59](/7.17.59); sense organs such as the vomeronasal organ and pit organs in [7.17.68](/7.17.68); the physiology of thermoregulation in [7.14.31](/7.14.31).

## 51.1. Lepidosaurs

### 51.1.1. Characters of Lepidosaurs

**Definition 51.1.1 (Lepidosauria)** The **Lepidosauria** (lepidosaurs) are the crown clade of the tuatara (Rhynchocephalia) and the squamates (Squamata); with their fossil stem relatives they form the **Lepidosauromorpha**, the sister group of the Archelosauria (turtles and archosaurs) among living reptiles. Synapomorphies include a transverse cloacal opening, skin shed periodically as a whole or in large pieces from a regenerating epidermis, fracture planes in the caudal vertebrae that allow the tail to break off, a fused astragalus and calcaneum, epiphyses (secondary centres of ossification) at the ends of the long bones, and teeth attached to the inner side (pleurodont) or the crest (acrodont) of the jaw rather than in sockets.

Several of these characters affect the whole biology of lepidosaurs:

- *Skin and shedding.* The epidermis of lepidosaurs alternates between a resting phase and a renewal phase in which a complete new set of keratin layers (rich in β-keratins, [7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.1.3)) forms beneath the old one; then the old layers separate and are shed. Snakes shed the whole skin, including the transparent spectacle over the eye, in one piece; lizards in patches. Turtles and crocodylians wear their scales continuously and do not shed in this way.
- *Tail autotomy.* Each caudal vertebra (beyond the first few) has a plane of weakness across the centrum, and the muscles and blood vessels are arranged so that the tail can be broken off at that plane with little loss of blood. The detached tail twitches for minutes and distracts the predator. The regenerated tail has a cartilage tube instead of vertebrae. Autotomy is lost in chameleons, monitors and snakes.
- *Growth and epiphyses.* As in mammals, but independently, the long bones of lepidosaurs have epiphyses that fuse with the shaft at maturity, so growth is largely determinate.

The fossil record of lepidosauromorphs begins in the Early to Middle Triassic. *Megachirella* from the Middle Triassic of the Italian Alps (about 240 million years ago) is the oldest known squamate (Simões et al. 2018), and rhynchocephalians were diverse and widespread in the Late Triassic and Jurassic, including the marine, long-bodied pleurosaurs.

### 51.1.2. The Tuatara

The tuatara, *Sphenodon punctatus*, is the only surviving rhynchocephalian. It lives on about 30 small offshore islands of New Zealand, where it reaches 60 cm and about 1 kg (males are larger). It shares burrows with nesting seabirds such as petrels and shearwaters, whose guano supports the invertebrates it eats; it also eats seabird eggs and chicks. Introduced rats eliminated it from the main islands and many smaller ones, and conservation since the 1990s has depended on eradicating rats and reintroducing tuatara to predator-free sanctuaries.

The tuatara is not a lizard; it differs from squamates in many features:

| Feature | Tuatara | Squamates |
|---|---|---|
| Lower temporal bar | Complete (regained secondarily) | Lost; lower fenestra open below |
| Quadrate | Fixed to the skull | Movable (streptostyly) |
| Teeth | Acrodont, fused to the jaw crest, not replaced; a tooth-like chisel of the premaxilla; two rows of upper teeth (maxilla and palatine) between which the single lower row fits | Pleurodont (most) or acrodont (agamids, chameleons), replaced continually |
| Jaw action | Closing followed by a forward slide of the lower jaw (propalinal shearing) that saws food between the tooth rows | No propalinal shearing |
| Copulatory organ | None; sperm transferred by cloacal apposition | Paired hemipenes |
| Parietal eye | Well developed, with lens and retina, beneath a scale on the head of hatchlings | Present in many lizards, absent in snakes |
| Body temperature | Active at 5–20 °C, the lowest of any reptile | Mostly 25–40 °C |

The tuatara lives slowly. It grows for 35 years, reaches sexual maturity at 10–20 years and can live for more than 100; females lay eggs only every two to five years, and the eggs incubate for 11 to 16 months, the longest incubation of any reptile. Sex is determined by temperature, with males produced at higher incubation temperatures (§51.6.2). Its genome of about 5 Gb is one of the largest among amniotes (Gemmell et al. 2020). The tuatara is often called a **living fossil**, but its similarity to Jurassic relatives is in general form: it has its own specializations, notably the shearing dentition and the regained lower temporal bar, which braces the quadrate against the forces of shearing.

### 51.1.3. Squamates

**Definition 51.1.2 (Squamata)** The **Squamata** (squamates: lizards, amphisbaenians and snakes) are the lepidosaurs with paired hemipenes, a movable quadrate (streptostyly), loss of the lower temporal bar, and a skull in which several joints allow movement between its parts (cranial kinesis).

"Lizards" are not a natural group: snakes and amphisbaenians evolved from within the lizards, and a lizard is any squamate that is not a snake. The main groups, with approximate numbers of species (Reptile Database 2024), are:

| Group | Species | Representatives | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dibamidae | ~25 | blind lizards (Southeast Asia, Mexico) | Limbless or nearly so, burrowing, eyes under skin |
| Gekkota | ~2 300 | geckos, flap-footed lizards (Pygopodidae) | Mostly nocturnal; many with adhesive toe pads; vocal; spectacle over the eye in most |
| Scincoidea | ~1 800 | skinks, girdled lizards, night lizards | Smooth, overlapping scales with osteoderms; frequent limb reduction; many viviparous |
| Lacertoidea | ~1 000 | lacertids, teiids (whiptails, tegus), amphisbaenians (~200) | Active foragers; amphisbaenians limbless burrowers with annular scales |
| Iguania | ~2 000 | iguanas, anoles, agamids, chameleons | Mostly sit-and-wait predators or herbivores; tongue used to capture prey; visual signalling (dewlaps, head-bobbing) |
| Anguimorpha | ~250 | monitors (*Varanus*), anguids (glass lizards), Gila monster (*Heloderma*), crocodile lizard (*Shinisaurus*) | Forked tongue in monitors; venom glands in *Heloderma*; the largest living lizard, the Komodo dragon (*Varanus komodoensis*, 3 m, 70 kg) |
| Serpentes | ~4 000 | snakes | Limbless, elongate, highly kinetic skull |

Squamates range from geckos (*Sphaerodactylus*) and chameleons (*Brookesia nana*) about 2 cm long to the reticulated python (*Malayopython reticulatus*), which exceeds 6 m. They occupy every continent except Antarctica and include burrowers, climbers, gliders (*Draco*, which glides on wings supported by elongated ribs), aquatic forms (sea snakes, the marine iguana *Amblyrhynchus*) and desert specialists.

### 51.1.4. Squamate Phylogeny: Morphology against Molecules

Squamate relationships are the best-known case of a deep conflict between morphological and molecular phylogenies. Morphological analyses, from Estes, de Queiroz and Gauthier (1988) to Gauthier et al. (2012), placed the Iguania as the sister group of all other squamates, the Scleroglossa ("hard tongue"), which share a tongue that is not used to capture prey, jaw prehension and well-developed chemosensory abilities. They also grouped the limbless, burrowing squamates — snakes, amphisbaenians and dibamids — together.

Molecular phylogenies, from Townsend et al. (2004) and Vidal and Hedges (2005) to analyses of hundreds of nuclear loci and genomes (Pyron et al. 2013; Burbrink et al. 2020), give a different tree. Dibamids and geckos branch first; iguanians are nested deep in the tree as the sister group of anguimorphs, and together with snakes they form a clade, the Toxicofera; amphisbaenians are lacertoids, related to the lacertids.

![Two hypotheses of squamate relationships. (A) The morphological tree places Iguania as the sister group of all other squamates (Scleroglossa, blue bar) and groups the limbless burrowers (snakes, amphisbaenians, dibamids). (B) The molecular tree places dibamids and geckos at the base, groups iguanians with anguimorphs and snakes in Toxicofera (orange band) and places amphisbaenians among the lacertoids.](./plots/squamate-trees.py)

The conflict is explained by **homoplasy** in the morphological data. Burrowing selects for the same set of changes in unrelated lineages — elongation, loss of limbs, reduction of the eyes, a compact, solidly built skull — so the characters that united snakes, amphisbaenians and dibamids are **convergent evolution**. Similarly, the "scleroglossan" features of the tongue and jaws are probably ancestral for squamates and were modified in iguanians, which evolved tongue prehension secondarily. Recent analyses that combine morphology with molecules and fossils find the molecular tree and reinterpret the morphology accordingly (Simões et al. 2018).

The Toxicofera ("toxin bearers") were named for the hypothesis that venom evolved once in their common ancestor: oral glands producing proteins similar to snake venom toxins occur in monitors, the Gila monster and some iguanians (Fry et al. 2006). This is debated. Many of the "toxin" proteins have other functions and are expressed in other tissues, the glands of most iguanians do not deliver venom, and the venom systems of snakes and helodermatids differ in gland position and delivery. The clade itself is well supported; the single origin of venom is not.

The origin of snakes is related to these debates. A burrowing origin is supported by the structure of the snake eye, which suggests that the eyes were reduced and then rebuilt, and by the fossil *Najash rionegrina* from the Late Cretaceous of Argentina, which had a sacrum and hindlimbs and lived on land. A marine origin was suggested by the mosasaurs, giant marine anguimorphs of the Late Cretaceous, and by Cretaceous marine snakes with hindlimbs (*Pachyrhachis*, *Haasiophis*, *Eupodophis*), but these are now considered derived snakes that entered the sea. The oldest snake fossils are from the Middle Jurassic (about 167 million years ago). *Tetrapodophis*, described in 2015 as a four-legged snake from Brazil, is now considered more likely a dolichosaur, a marine lizard.

### 51.1.5. Feeding: Skull Kinesis, Tongue and Foraging Mode

**Cranial kinesis**, movement between parts of the skull other than the jaw joint, is highly developed in squamates. Three types are distinguished ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)):

- *Streptostyly*: the quadrate is attached to the skull only at its upper end and swings forward and backward, changing the position of the jaw joint;
- *Mesokinesis*: a hinge across the skull roof between the frontal and the parietal lets the snout rise and fall relative to the back of the skull;
- *Metakinesis*: a joint between the braincase and the skull roof at the back of the head.

In lizards these movements are small and help the jaws close on prey at a wide gape and hold it as the head moves. In snakes they are extreme (§51.2.2).

The tongue reflects a division within squamates. Iguanians capture prey with the tongue (lingual prehension), as the tuatara does, and chameleons have turned this into a ballistic projectile (§51.3.2). Other squamates seize prey with the jaws, and their tongue is free for chemoreception: it picks up odour molecules and carries them to the **vomeronasal organ**, a pair of chemosensory organs that open in the roof of the mouth ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)). In monitors and snakes the tongue is deeply forked, and because each tine samples a separate point, the animal can compare concentrations on the two sides and follow a scent trail (tropotaxis).

Lizards have two broad foraging modes, which are correlated with many other characters:

| Character | Sit-and-wait (ambush) foragers | Active (widely foraging) foragers |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Iguanians (anoles, agamids, chameleons), many geckos | Lacertids, teiids, skinks, varanids |
| Movement while foraging | Rarely; waits on a perch and dashes at passing prey | Constantly; searches crevices and leaf litter |
| Prey | Mobile, conspicuous prey (flying insects) | Sedentary, hidden prey (larvae, termites, eggs) |
| Prey detection | Vision | Chemoreception (tongue flicking and vomeronasal organ) |
| Body form | Stocky, often cryptic, short tail | Slender, streamlined, long tail |
| Physiology | Low aerobic capacity, high capacity for bursts | High aerobic capacity and endurance |
| Predators | Avoids detection by crypsis | Detected easily; relies on speed and vigilance |
| Reproduction | Large clutches relative to body mass | Smaller relative clutch mass, which would slow a moving lizard |

The modes are ends of a continuum, but the correlations show that a choice of foraging behaviour reorganizes morphology, physiology and life history together.

## 51.2. Snakes

### 51.2.1. The Snake Body Plan

The snake body is built from a long series of nearly identical vertebrae: about 120–300 in most snakes and up to about 500 in some, compared with 25–30 trunk vertebrae in a lizard. Each vertebra bears a pair of ribs, and the vertebrae articulate by two extra joints (the zygosphene and zygantrum) that prevent twisting. The internal organs are rearranged to fit a long, narrow body: the left lung is reduced or absent and the right is long, often with a posterior air-sac portion; the kidneys and gonads are staggered one behind the other; and the gall bladder lies far from the liver.

Elongation and limb loss have been studied as examples of evolution by changes in development ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)). In pythons, the expression domains of the **Hox genes** that specify thoracic identity (*Hoxc6*, *Hoxc8*) extend along almost the whole body, so that nearly every vertebra has a rib-bearing, thoracic identity and there is no neck (Cohn and Tickle 1999), and the number of segments is increased by a faster segmentation clock relative to growth ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)). Limbs are lost in steps. Pythons and boas retain a vestigial pelvis and femur with a small spur beside the cloaca. The limb buds of python embryos start to form and then regress, because the ZRS enhancer, which drives *Sonic hedgehog* in the posterior limb bud ([7.5.32](/7.5.32)), has accumulated mutations in snakes; when the snake version of the ZRS replaces the mouse version, mouse limbs are severely truncated, and restoring a 17-base-pair sequence missing in snakes rescues them (Kvon et al. 2016).

Snakes move without limbs in four main ways: lateral undulation, in which waves of bending push against irregularities of the ground; concertina locomotion in tunnels, in which parts of the body are anchored while others extend; rectilinear locomotion, used by heavy snakes, in which the belly scales are lifted and pulled forward in waves without bending the body; and sidewinding on loose sand, in which the body touches the ground at only two or three points.

### 51.2.2. Feeding

Snakes swallow their prey whole, often prey heavier than themselves, without chewing. Their skull is adapted for this ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)):

- The two halves of the lower jaw are joined at the chin only by elastic ligaments and muscle, and can move independently.
- Each lower jaw is articulated with a long quadrate, which hangs from the supratemporal, itself attached loosely to the braincase; the jaw joint lies far behind the head and the gape is very wide.
- The tooth-bearing bones of the upper jaw — the maxilla and the palatopterygoid bar (palatine and pterygoid) — are each attached to the skull by ligaments and can move independently.
- The braincase is closed in front by bone, protecting the brain from struggling prey.

Swallowing proceeds by "pterygoid walking": the left and right palatopterygoid bars and lower jaws move alternately forward over the prey, their backward-pointing teeth gripping it, so the head ratchets itself forward over the prey. The trachea opens far forward in the floor of the mouth and can be extended so the snake breathes during a long swallow.

Many snakes kill prey before swallowing. Constrictors (boas, pythons, many colubrids) coil around the prey and tighten each time it exhales. Constriction was long thought to suffocate prey, but measurements in rats show that it stops blood circulation within seconds, causing cardiac arrest and brain failure well before suffocation (Boback et al. 2015).

### 51.2.3. Venom and Fangs

Venomous snakes inject a secretion of the venom gland, a modified oral gland (Duvernoy's gland in rear-fanged snakes), through grooved or hollow teeth. Four types of dentition are distinguished:

| Type | Teeth | Families and examples | Venom delivery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aglyphous | Solid teeth without grooves | Pythons, boas, most colubrids (rat snakes *Elaphe*) | No venom fangs |
| Opisthoglyphous ("rear-fanged") | One or more enlarged grooved teeth at the rear of the maxilla | Some colubrids: boomslang *Dispholidus*, the Chinese red-necked keelback *Rhabdophis* | Venom flows along the groove while chewing |
| Proteroglyphous ("front-fanged") | Short, fixed, hollow (enclosed-groove) fangs at the front of the maxilla | Elapidae: cobras (*Naja*), kraits (*Bungarus*), mambas, sea snakes, coral snakes | Venom injected by a quick bite |
| Solenoglyphous | Long, hollow fangs on a short maxilla that rotates; folded back at rest | Viperidae: vipers and pit vipers (*Deinagkistrodon*, *Gloydius*, *Trimeresurus*, rattlesnakes); also *Atractaspis* | Fangs swing forward as the mouth opens and inject deeply |

The solenoglyphous mechanism is a lever system: the quadrate swings forward as the mouth opens, pushing the pterygoid and ectopterygoid forward, and the ectopterygoid rotates the short maxilla on its joint with the prefrontal so that the fang erects. Because the fang folds against the roof of the mouth, it can be very long (up to 5 cm in the Gaboon viper).

![Cranial kinesis and fang erection in a viper (schematic left lateral views). (A) Mouth closed: the fang lies folded back along the roof of the mouth. (B) Mouth open: the quadrate swings forward from its joint with the supratemporal, pushing the pterygoid and ectopterygoid forward (arrows); the short maxilla rotates on the prefrontal and the fang erects; the lower jaw (dentary and compound bone) drops at the quadrate joint. Open circles are joints.](./plots/viper-skull.py)

Venoms are mixtures of tens to hundreds of proteins. Elapid venoms are dominated by neurotoxins (three-finger toxins that block the acetylcholine receptor, β-bungarotoxin that destroys nerve terminals) and kill by respiratory paralysis; viperid venoms are dominated by enzymes (metalloproteinases, serine proteases, phospholipases A₂) that destroy tissue and interfere with blood coagulation. Venom composition evolves rapidly by gene duplication and positive selection, and differs between and even within populations according to diet. Venom is primarily a means of subduing prey; its use in defence is secondary.

### 51.2.4. Infrared Pit Organs

Pit vipers (Crotalinae) have a deep pit on each side of the head between the eye and the nostril; pythons and some boas have a row of shallower pits along the lips. The pit is a cavity crossed by a membrane 10–15 μm thick, richly supplied with blood vessels and with the endings of the trigeminal nerve. Infrared radiation from a warm animal heats the membrane, and a heat-sensitive ion channel, TRPA1, which in mammals responds to irritant chemicals, opens and excites the nerve endings (Gracheva et al. 2010). Because the membrane is so thin and has so little mass, a change of 0.003 °C can be detected. The pit works like a pinhole camera: its opening casts an infrared image on the membrane, and the trigeminal signals are mapped in the optic tectum in register with the visual map, so the snake "sees" warm prey in darkness and strikes accurately at it. The pits of pit vipers and of boas and pythons evolved independently.

## 51.3. Lizard Specializations and Reproduction

### 51.3.1. Gecko Adhesion

Many geckos climb smooth vertical surfaces and run upside down on ceilings. Their toes bear plates (lamellae) covered with about 14 400 hair-like setae per mm²; each seta, about 100 μm long, branches into hundreds of spatulae only 0.2 μm wide. The spatulae come so close to the surface that they are held by van der Waals forces, the weak attractions between molecules at close range (Autumn et al. 2000, 2002). A single seta can generate about 200 μN of adhesion, and if all the setae of a 50 g tokay gecko were engaged at once they could support more than 100 kg, far more than needed; in practice only a small fraction contacts the surface at once. The adhesion depends on the angle of the seta: pulling the seta toward the body engages it, and peeling it off at an angle above 30° releases it, so the gecko attaches and detaches its toes in milliseconds by curling them. The structure has inspired synthetic dry adhesives. Toe pads have evolved independently several times in geckos and also in anoles and some skinks.

### 51.3.2. Chameleons: Colour and the Ballistic Tongue

Chameleons (Chamaeleonidae, about 220 species, mostly in Africa and Madagascar) are arboreal, slow-moving ambush predators with zygodactylous feet (toes fused in opposing bundles), a prehensile tail, turret eyes that move independently, and two famous specializations.

*Colour change.* Many lizards change colour by moving melanin in melanophores ([7.17.57](/7.17.57)). Chameleons also change colour by changing structure. Their skin has two layers of iridophores containing nanocrystals of guanine; the upper layer has crystals arranged in a regular lattice that acts as a photonic crystal, reflecting light of a wavelength set by the spacing between crystals. When a male panther chameleon (*Furcifer pardalis*) becomes excited, the lattice spacing increases and the reflected colour shifts from blue to yellow and red; combined with yellow pigment in the overlying xanthophores, this changes the skin from green to orange (Teyssier et al. 2015). Colour change in chameleons serves mainly in social signalling, less in camouflage.

*The ballistic tongue.* A chameleon can shoot its tongue to more than its body length in a few hundredths of a second. The tongue's core is the entoglossal process, a rod of the hyoid; around it lie the accelerator muscle and, inside that, sheaths of collagen. Before the strike, the accelerator muscle contracts slowly and stretches the collagen sheaths like springs; the strike releases this stored energy at once, as a catapult does. Because the power comes from elastic recoil rather than muscle contraction during the strike, the tongue reaches accelerations of over 2 500 m s⁻² in small species (Anderson 2016), and the performance is little affected by cold, which slows muscle. The tip of the tongue forms a suction cup coated with very viscous mucus that holds the prey.

### 51.3.3. Defence

Squamates defend themselves by crypsis, flight, tail autotomy (§51.1.1), threat displays (the frill of the Australian frilled lizard *Chlamydosaurus*, the hood of cobras, the hissing and rattling of rattlesnakes), venom, and, in horned lizards (*Phrynosoma*), by squirting blood from the eyes, which deters canid predators. Some snakes feign death; others mimic venomous species (the coral snake mimicry complex, an example of **Batesian mimicry** and **Müllerian mimicry**, [7.10.17](/7.10.17)).

### 51.3.4. Reproduction

Squamates are oviparous or viviparous, and the transition from egg-laying to live birth has occurred more than 100 times, more often than in all other vertebrates combined. Viviparity is particularly common in cold climates and at high altitudes ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.2.4)). Many viviparous skinks have complex placentas formed by the chorioallantois and yolk sac against the oviduct wall; in some (*Mabuya*, *Pseudemoia*) the embryo obtains most of its nutrients from the mother, as in a mammal.

**Parthenogenesis** occurs in about 40 species of squamates. In the whiptail lizards (*Aspidoscelis*) of North America and some lacertids and geckos, all-female species arose by hybridization between two sexual species; the hybrid females produce unreduced eggs by doubling their chromosomes before meiosis, so their daughters are clones and keep the heterozygosity of the hybrid. In whiptails, females take turns performing male-like courtship, which stimulates ovulation. Facultative parthenogenesis, in which females of normally sexual species occasionally produce offspring without mating, is known in Komodo dragons, boas and pythons.

Sex determination in squamates is genetic in most species, with male (XY) or female (ZW) heterogamety, but it is temperature-dependent in some lizards (many agamids, geckos) and switches between genetic and temperature control in the bearded dragon *Pogona vitticeps*, in which high temperatures reverse ZZ males into females. The **temperature-dependent sex determination** of turtles and the tuatara is treated in §51.6.2.

## 51.4. Turtles: Characters and the Shell

### 51.4.1. Characters of Turtles

**Definition 51.4.1 (Testudines)** The **Testudines** (turtles; Chelonia) are the reptiles whose trunk is enclosed in a bony shell formed by a dorsal carapace, incorporating the dorsal vertebrae and ribs, and a ventral plastron, incorporating the clavicles and interclavicle; the pectoral and pelvic girdles lie inside the rib cage. Living turtles have a toothless beak covered by keratin (rhamphotheca), a skull without temporal fenestrae, and no movement of the ribs in breathing.

The shell is the defining feature of turtles and has made them one of the most conservative vertebrate body plans: turtles 210 million years old would be recognized at a glance. The shell has also constrained the rest of their biology: they cannot breathe by moving the ribs, their neck and limbs must fold into or beside the shell, and their reproduction (a fixed body volume for eggs) and locomotion are shaped by it.

### 51.4.2. Bones and Scutes

The shell consists of bony plates covered by keratinous scutes (horny shields):

| Part | Bones | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| Carapace, midline | Nuchal plate (front), neural plates (fused to the neural spines of 8–10 trunk vertebrae), suprapygal and pygal plates (rear) | Dermal bones fused with the vertebrae |
| Carapace, sides | Costal plates (8 pairs), each fused with a broadened rib | Ribs expanded by bone formed in the dermis |
| Carapace, margin | Peripheral plates (11 pairs) | Dermal ossifications |
| Plastron | Paired epiplastra (front), unpaired entoplastron, paired hyoplastra, hypoplastra and xiphiplastra | Epiplastra = clavicles; entoplastron = interclavicle; hyo-, hypo- and xiphiplastra probably from gastralia (abdominal ribs) |
| Bridge | Connection of carapace and plastron between fore- and hindlimbs | Processes of the hyo- and hypoplastra meeting the peripherals |

The scutes are epidermal: on the carapace a row of vertebral scutes, paired rows of pleural (costal) scutes and a rim of marginals; on the plastron paired gular, humeral, pectoral, abdominal, femoral and anal scutes. The seams between scutes do not coincide with the sutures between bones, so the shell is stronger than either layer alone. Scutes grow by adding keratin at their margins, and in many species growth rings (annuli) can be counted. The softshell turtles (Trionychidae) have lost the scutes and the peripheral bones, and their shell is covered by leathery skin; the leatherback sea turtle (*Dermochelys coriacea*) has a carapace of thousands of small dermal ossicles embedded in thick, oily skin.

### 51.4.3. How the Shoulder Got Inside the Ribs

In every other tetrapod, the scapula lies outside the rib cage. In turtles it lies inside, under the carapace. This was long cited as a puzzle: how could a structure move from outside to inside the ribs? Developmental studies resolved it (Burke 1989; Nagashima et al. 2009). In turtle embryos, a ridge of thickened ectoderm and mesenchyme, the *carapacial ridge*, forms along each side of the back. The ribs, instead of growing ventrally around the body wall as in other amniotes, are drawn laterally into the dermis of the back, guided by signals (FGF, Wnt) from the ridge, and grow outward over the region where the scapula forms. The scapula does not move: the ribs grow above it and fold the body wall over it. The relative positions of muscles and bones are preserved, but because the rib cage has been displaced dorsally and laterally ("axial arrest" of rib growth), the shoulder ends up inside.

![Transverse sections at the level of the shoulder girdle (schematic). In a lizard (left) the ribs encircle the trunk and the scapula lies outside them. In a turtle (right) the ribs grow laterally into the dermis of the back and fuse with dermal costal plates to form the carapace, covered by keratin scutes; the scapula and coracoid lie inside the rib cage. The plastron is formed by dermal bones, anteriorly the clavicles (epiplastra) and interclavicle (entoplastron); the bridge joins plastron and carapace. Not to scale.](./plots/turtle-shell.py)

The ribs then ossify, and dermal bone forms around them to produce the costal plates. The plastron develops separately from dermal ossification centres in the ventral body wall. The genetic changes that redirect rib growth have been sought in the expression of *Hox* and of genes such as *Wnt5a* in the carapacial ridge, but the developmental mechanism is still incompletely understood.

### 51.4.4. Breathing in a Box

A turtle cannot expand its rib cage. It ventilates its lungs, which lie dorsally under the carapace and are attached to it, by changing the volume of the visceral cavity with sheets of muscle at the openings of the shell. Contraction of the transverse abdominal muscle and the pectoralis pushes the viscera against the lungs and expels air; contraction of the oblique abdominal and serratus muscles enlarges the body cavity at the limb pockets and draws air in. Movements of the limbs and neck also help. Fossils show that the abdominal breathing muscles were in place before the shell: the broadened ribs of the stem turtle *Eunotosaurus* would have stiffened the trunk and prevented costal ventilation, implying a shift to abdominal ventilation early in turtle evolution (Lyson et al. 2014).

Aquatic turtles have additional means of gas exchange. Some take up oxygen through the skin and the lining of the pharynx (softshells) or through the cloaca (the Australian Fitzroy River turtle *Rheodytes leukops*, which can obtain most of its oxygen from water pumped through cloacal bursae). The painted turtle (*Chrysemys picta*) and other northern freshwater turtles survive months of winter submerged in ice-covered ponds with almost no oxygen; their metabolism falls by more than 90 %, and the lactic acid produced by anaerobic metabolism is buffered by calcium carbonate released from the shell and stored in it.

## 51.5. The Origin of Turtles

### 51.5.1. The Fossil Sequence

Until 2008 the oldest known turtle, *Proganochelys* from the Late Triassic of Germany (about 210 million years ago), already had a complete shell, and the origin of turtles was one of the great gaps of the vertebrate fossil record. Several discoveries since then, two of them in China, have filled it:

| Fossil | Age and place | Shell | Other features |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Eunotosaurus* | Middle Permian, about 260 million years ago, South Africa | No shell; nine pairs of very broad, T-shaped trunk ribs | Short trunk; strong forelimbs; probably a burrower (Lyson et al. 2016) |
| *Pappochelys* | Middle Triassic, about 240 million years ago, Germany | No shell; broad ribs; gastralia (abdominal ribs) thickened and paired | Diapsid skull with both temporal fenestrae |
| *Eorhynchochelys* | Late Triassic, about 228 million years ago, Guizhou, China | No shell; broad ribs | 2.5 m long; a toothless beak at the front of the jaws; diapsid skull |
| *Odontochelys semitestacea* | Late Triassic, about 220 million years ago, Guizhou, China | A complete plastron; no carapace, but neural plates and broad ribs | Teeth on the jaws; found in marine deposits; tail long |
| *Proganochelys* | Late Triassic, about 210 million years ago, Germany | Complete shell with carapace and plastron | Teeth on the palate only; beak; head and limbs not retractable; spiked tail |

*Odontochelys*, the "toothed turtle with half a shell", described by Li Chun and colleagues in 2008 from the Triassic of Guanling, Guizhou, was the decisive find: it shows that the plastron evolved before the carapace, and that the carapace began with broadened ribs and neural plates without the dermal costal plates. Because *Odontochelys* lived in or near the sea, its discoverers suggested that the plastron first protected the belly of an aquatic animal from below. The burrowing habits inferred for *Eunotosaurus* suggest instead that the broad ribs evolved as a stiff trunk for digging, and that the shell was later co-opted for protection; the two hypotheses are not mutually exclusive.

### 51.5.2. Turtles among the Reptiles

The position of turtles among amniotes has been argued for a century. Their anapsid skull suggested a relationship with early amniotes or with the parareptiles (pareiasaurs, procolophonids); some morphological analyses placed them with lepidosaurs. Molecular data — mitochondrial and nuclear genes, microRNAs, ultraconserved elements and whole genomes — consistently place turtles with archosaurs (Archelosauria) (Crawford et al. 2015). The fossils support this: *Pappochelys* and *Eorhynchochelys* had diapsid skulls, so the turtle skull lost its fenestrae secondarily, probably as the skull became heavily roofed and was emarginated from behind ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.3.4)).

## 51.6. Diversity and Reproduction of Turtles

### 51.6.1. The Living Turtles

The about 360 living species fall into two clades, distinguished by how they withdraw the head:

- *Pleurodira* (side-necked turtles, about 95 species) bend the neck sideways and tuck the head under the front edge of the shell. Their pelvis is fused to the carapace and plastron. They live in fresh water in South America, Africa, Madagascar and Australasia; they include the matamata (*Chelus*) and the snake-necked turtles (Chelidae).
- *Cryptodira* (hidden-necked turtles, about 270 species) retract the head straight back by folding the neck into a vertical S-bend. They include most familiar turtles:

| Family | Species (approx.) | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Testudinidae (tortoises) | 60 | Terrestrial, high-domed shell, elephantine hind feet; giant tortoises of Galápagos and Aldabra |
| Geoemydidae (Old World pond turtles) | 70 | Most Asian freshwater and semi-terrestrial turtles, including box turtles (*Cuora*) with a hinged plastron |
| Emydidae (New World pond turtles) | 50 | Sliders (*Trachemys*), painted turtles, box turtles (*Terrapene*) |
| Trionychidae (softshells) | 30 | Flat, leathery shell without scutes; long snorkel snout; Chinese softshell turtle (*Pelodiscus sinensis*) |
| Cheloniidae (sea turtles) | 6 | Flippers; cannot retract head; green, loggerhead, hawksbill, ridleys, flatback |
| Dermochelyidae | 1 | Leatherback, the largest turtle (up to 900 kg), dives deeper than 1 000 m, feeds on jellyfish |
| Chelydridae, Kinosternidae, Platysternidae and others | — | Snapping turtles, mud turtles, the big-headed turtle (*Platysternon megacephalum*) of southern China, which cannot withdraw its head |

### 51.6.2. Temperature-Dependent Sex Determination

All turtles lay eggs, usually buried in a nest dug by the female with her hind feet; the eggs are abandoned and the hatchlings dig themselves out. In many turtles, all crocodylians, the tuatara and some lizards, the sex of the embryo is not set by sex chromosomes but by the temperature of incubation during a sensitive period in the middle third of development ([7.5.26](/7.5.26#sec-26.6.1)). Bull (1980) distinguished three patterns:

- *Pattern Ia*: males at low temperatures, females at high temperatures (many turtles, including sea turtles, sliders and painted turtles);
- *Pattern Ib*: females at low, males at high temperatures (tuatara, some lizards);
- *Pattern II*: females at both low and high temperatures, males at intermediate ones (crocodylians, snapping turtles, some lizards and turtles).

The response is described by a reaction norm, the proportion of males as a function of constant incubation temperature $T$. For pattern Ia a logistic curve fits well:

$$
m(T) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(\dfrac{T - T_{\mathrm{piv}}}{s}\right)},
$$
where $T_{\mathrm{piv}}$, the *pivotal temperature*, gives a 1:1 sex ratio and $s$ sets the width of the *transitional range* of temperatures that give mixed sexes (about $4s$ wide for $0.12 < m < 0.88$). Pattern Ib has the opposite sign, and pattern II is the product of a rising and a falling logistic.

![Model: logistic reaction norms for the three patterns of temperature-dependent sex determination, with approximate literature parameters. Pattern Ia (loggerhead turtle, pivotal temperature about 29 °C), males from cool nests; pattern Ib (tuatara, about 22 °C), males from warm nests; pattern II (American alligator), males only around 32–33 °C. Schematic.](./plots/tsd-curves.py)

For the loggerhead turtle, $T_{\mathrm{piv}} \approx 29\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and the transitional range is only a few degrees wide, so a small change in nest temperature can change the sex ratio from all-male to all-female. The molecular mechanism has been worked out in the red-eared slider (*Trachemys scripta*): at male-producing temperatures, the histone demethylase KDM6B removes a repressive histone mark from the promoter of the male gene *Dmrt1*, which is then expressed; at female-producing temperatures, this does not happen. Knocking down *Kdm6b* at male temperature produces females (Ge et al. 2018). Temperature may act through calcium and redox signalling in the gonad.

The adaptive significance of TSD is debated. The Charnov–Bull hypothesis proposes that TSD is favoured when incubation temperature affects the fitness of males and females differently; experiments with the jacky dragon (*Amphibolurus muricatus*), in which hormonally sex-reversed embryos were incubated at different temperatures, supported it (Warner and Shine 2008). TSD makes turtles and crocodylians vulnerable to climate warming: in the northern Great Barrier Reef, more than 99 % of young green turtles are now female (Jensen et al. 2018). Females can partly compensate by nesting earlier or choosing shaded sites.

### 51.6.3. Navigation and Migration

Sea turtles migrate thousands of kilometres between feeding grounds and the beaches where they hatched. Hatchling loggerheads entering the sea from Florida swim into the North Atlantic gyre and circle it for years before returning. They navigate by the geomagnetic field: hatchlings placed in a tank and exposed to the inclination and intensity of the field at different points along the gyre swim in the direction that would keep them in the current, so they carry an inherited magnetic map (Lohmann et al. 2001). Adult females return to nest on or near their natal beach (natal homing), apparently by imprinting on the magnetic signature of the coast; green turtles nesting on Ascension Island, a speck in the South Atlantic, swim there from feeding grounds off Brazil, 2 000 km away. The behavioural mechanisms of orientation are treated in 7.18.

### 51.6.4. Turtles in Trouble

More than half of all turtle species are threatened with extinction, a higher proportion than in any other major vertebrate group (Stanford et al. 2020). The life history of turtles makes them vulnerable: late maturity, long life and very high mortality of eggs and hatchlings mean that populations depend on the survival of adults, and the removal of even a few per cent of adults per year causes decline. In Asia, collection for food and traditional medicine has emptied whole regions of turtles since the 1990s (the "Asian turtle crisis"). Sea turtles are killed as bycatch in fisheries and their beaches are lost to development and rising seas.

## 51.7. Lepidosaurs and Turtles of China

China has about 500 species of lizards and snakes and about 35 species of turtles, concentrated in the south.

*Lizards.* The tokay gecko (*Gekko gecko*), the largest gecko of southern China, is collected in large numbers for traditional medicine. Toad-headed agamas (*Phrynocephalus*) are characteristic of the deserts of the northwest and the Tibetan Plateau, where *P. vlangalii* is viviparous ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.2.4)). Grass lizards (*Takydromus*), racerunners (*Eremias*), skinks (*Plestiodon*) and the agamids *Diploderma* (*Japalura*) and *Calotes* are common. The Chinese crocodile lizard (*Shinisaurus crocodilurus*), the sole member of its family, lives along streams in Guangxi and Guangdong and in northern Vietnam; it is endangered and a Class I state-protected species.

*Snakes.* China has about 300 snake species, about 60 of them venomous. The medically most important are the many-banded krait (*Bungarus multicinctus*) and the Chinese cobra (*Naja atra*) among elapids, the king cobra (*Ophiophagus hannah*), which feeds on other snakes, and among vipers the sharp-snouted pit viper (*Deinagkistrodon acutus*, the "hundred-pace snake"), the Chinese green tree viper (*Trimeresurus stejnegeri*), the mamushis (*Gloydius*) and Russell's viper (*Daboia siamensis*). Shedao (Snake Island), a 0.73 km² island off Lüshun in Liaoning, is home to some 20 000 Shedao pit vipers (*Gloydius shedaoensis*), an endemic species that feeds on migrating birds stopping on the island in spring and autumn and fasts for the rest of the year.

*Snakebite.* Venomous snakes cannot be recognized reliably by appearance: the triangular head often cited is shared by harmless snakes, and elapids have narrow heads. Fang marks, the progression of symptoms (local swelling and bleeding in viper bites, paralysis in elapid bites) and identification of the snake guide treatment. Current first aid is to keep the victim still, immobilize the bitten limb, remove rings and tight clothing and reach a hospital quickly; incision, suction, ice and tight tourniquets are harmful. Antivenom, prepared from the plasma of horses immunized with venom and specific to the snake or its group, is the only specific treatment.

*Turtles.* The Chinese softshell turtle (*Pelodiscus sinensis*) is farmed in enormous numbers for food. The Chinese pond turtle (*Mauremys reevesii*), the golden coin turtle (*Cuora trifasciata*), the big-headed turtle and many others have declined drastically in the wild through collection. The Yangtze giant softshell turtle (*Rafetus swinhoei*), the largest freshwater turtle in the world, is on the verge of extinction: the last known female died at Suzhou Zoo in 2019, and only one or two individuals are known in the wild in Vietnam. Five species of sea turtles occur in Chinese waters, and green turtles still nest in the Xisha Islands.

## 51.8. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

Chinese textbooks recognize four living orders of reptiles: Chelonia (turtles), Rhynchocephalia (the tuatara), Squamata, divided into the suborders Lacertilia (lizards) and Serpentes (snakes), and Crocodylia. Turtles are divided into four families: tortoises (Testudinidae), leatherbacks (Dermochelyidae), sea turtles (Cheloniidae) and softshells (Trionychidae).

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Turtles as anapsids, a relict of the earliest reptiles | Turtles are diapsids, sister group of archosaurs; the skull closed secondarily (§51.5.2) |
| Turtles in four families, with the Asian pond turtles in the Testudinidae | About 14 families in Pleurodira and Cryptodira; Asian pond and box turtles are Geoemydidae, separate from the tortoises |
| Squamata with two suborders, lizards (Lacertilia) and snakes (Serpentes) | Lacertilia is paraphyletic: snakes arose within lizards, as the sister group of iguanians and anguimorphs (Toxicofera); amphisbaenians are lacertoids |
| The tuatara as a "living fossil", the most primitive reptile | The tuatara is the sole surviving rhynchocephalian, sister group of squamates; it has its own specializations (shearing jaw, regained lower temporal bar) |
| Venomous and non-venomous snakes distinguished by head shape, tail shape and colour | These features are unreliable; fangs and venom glands are diagnostic |
| Snakes without pectoral girdle or sternum, with the halves of the lower jaw joined by ligament and a movable quadrate | Confirmed (§51.2.2); add the movable upper-jaw bones and pterygoid walking |

The textbooks' description of the four types of fangs and the venom apparatus (venom gland, duct and fang), of the pit organ of pit vipers, of the scaled skin shed by moulting and of the autotomy of lizard tails is included in §51.1–51.3.

## Summary

- Lepidosaurs (Definition 51.1.1): tuatara and squamates; transverse cloacal slit, periodic skin shedding, tail autotomy, epiphyses. The tuatara is the sole rhynchocephalian: acrodont teeth, propalinal shearing, regained lower temporal bar, low body temperature, very slow life history.
- Squamates (Definition 51.1.2): hemipenes, streptostylic quadrate, kinetic skull; about 12 000 species. Molecular phylogenies place iguanians with anguimorphs and snakes (Toxicofera); the morphological grouping of burrowers was convergent.
- Feeding: kinesis (streptostyly, meso- and metakinesis); tongue prehension in iguanians versus a chemosensory, forked tongue linked to the vomeronasal organ; sit-and-wait versus active foraging as integrated syndromes.
- Snakes: elongation by extended thoracic Hox expression and a fast segmentation clock; limb loss through ZRS mutations; jaws for swallowing large prey; constriction stops circulation; four fang types (aglyphous, opisthoglyphous, proteroglyphous, solenoglyphous); TRPA1-based infrared pits.
- Lizard specializations: van der Waals adhesion of gecko setae; photonic-crystal colour change and elastic-recoil tongue of chameleons; parthenogenesis of hybrid origin; viviparity evolved over 100 times.
- Turtles (Definition 51.4.1): shell of carapace (vertebrae, ribs, dermal plates) and plastron (clavicles, interclavicle, gastralia) covered by scutes; girdles inside the ribs because the ribs grow laterally over the scapula (carapacial ridge); abdominal-muscle breathing; anoxia tolerance.
- Origin of turtles: *Eunotosaurus*, *Pappochelys*, *Eorhynchochelys*, *Odontochelys* (plastron first, Guizhou) and *Proganochelys*; turtles are diapsids close to archosaurs.
- Temperature-dependent sex determination: patterns Ia, Ib and II; logistic reaction norm with pivotal temperature; KDM6B–*Dmrt1* mechanism; vulnerability to warming. Sea turtles navigate by a magnetic map. More than half of turtle species are threatened.

## Questions

1. Explain why the morphological and molecular phylogenies of squamates disagree, and which characters were misleading.

   **Answer** Morphological analyses placed iguanians as the sister group of all other squamates and united snakes, amphisbaenians and dibamids, because they share features of the tongue and jaws (in non-iguanians) and features of burrowing (elongation, limb loss, reduced eyes, a compact skull). Molecular data from many independent genes instead nest iguanians within Toxicofera with anguimorphs and snakes and place amphisbaenians among lacertoids and dibamids at the base. The burrowing characters are convergent: they evolved independently each time a lineage adapted to life underground, because the same functional demands produce the same solutions. The tongue and jaw characters of "scleroglossans" are probably ancestral for squamates, while the tongue prehension of iguanians is a reversal. Such suites of functionally correlated characters behave like one character repeated many times, and can mislead parsimony analyses; independent molecular loci are less subject to this bias.
2. Describe the lever mechanism that erects the fang of a viper and compare it with the fangs of a cobra.

   **Answer** In a viper the maxilla is short and carries a long, hollow fang; it is hinged to the prefrontal. When the mouth opens, the quadrate swings forward, pushing the pterygoid forward; the pterygoid pushes the ectopterygoid, which presses on the back of the maxilla and rotates it about the prefrontal joint, swinging the fang from horizontal to vertical. Closing reverses the motion and folds the fang against the palate, so it can be long without obstructing the mouth. In a cobra (proteroglyphous) the fang is short and permanently erect, fixed on a longer maxilla that does not rotate much; venom is delivered by a quick bite, sometimes followed by chewing, and penetration is shallower. Vipers inject deeper, with large fangs, matched to venoms that act on tissues and blood; the elapid fang system serves venoms that act on nerves and need only enter the bloodstream.
3. A loggerhead turtle population nests on a beach where the mean nest temperature during the sensitive period is 29.0 °C, the pivotal temperature, with $s = 0.45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Using the logistic model, calculate the proportion of males if the mean nest temperature rises by 1 °C and by 2 °C. What does this imply?

   **Answer** $m(T) = 1/(1 + \exp((T - 29)/0.45))$. At 30 °C: $\exp(1/0.45) = \exp(2.22) = 9.2$, so $m = 1/10.2 \approx 0.10$, about 10 % males. At 31 °C: $\exp(4.44) = 85$, $m \approx 0.012$, about 1 % males. A warming of 1–2 °C, within the range projected for this century, would shift the population from a balanced sex ratio to almost all females. Because turtles are long-lived and males can mate with several females, the population may not decline immediately, but in the long term too few males and higher embryo mortality at high temperatures threaten it. Responses could include nesting earlier in the season, choosing shaded nest sites or evolution of the pivotal temperature, but the heritable variation and generation time of turtles make evolutionary response slow; conservation measures include shading nests or relocating eggs.
4. What does *Odontochelys* tell us about the evolution of the turtle shell, and how does *Eunotosaurus* complicate the story?

   **Answer** *Odontochelys* (Late Triassic, Guizhou) has a complete plastron but no carapace: only broadened ribs and neural plates on the back, without dermal costal plates. This shows that the shell was assembled in steps, with the plastron first and the carapace later, and that the carapace began as broadening of the ribs and vertebrae, the endoskeletal part, before dermal bone was added. Because it was found in marine sediments, the plastron was interpreted as protection for an aquatic animal attacked from below. *Eunotosaurus* (Permian, 260 million years ago) has broad ribs but no shell and features of a burrower (strong forelimbs, a stiff trunk). It suggests that broadened ribs first evolved to stiffen the trunk as a base for digging, and that their protective function was secondary — an exaptation. The two fossils together show that the broad ribs came first, and that the environment in which the shell evolved remains debated.
5. (Open) The tuatara is called a living fossil. Discuss whether the term is appropriate.

   **Answer** In favour: the tuatara is the only survivor of a group (Rhynchocephalia) that was diverse and widespread in the Triassic and Jurassic, and its general body form, skull with two temporal fenestrae and acrodont teeth resemble those of Mesozoic relatives; its lineage separated from squamates about 240 million years ago, so it is a long, isolated branch. Against: it is not an ancestor of squamates or a primitive reptile; it has many specializations (propalinal shearing, the secondarily complete lower temporal bar, the loss of the copulatory organ) and its genome and physiology have evolved as long as any other lineage's; molecular rates in tuatara are not unusually low. "Living fossil" describes a combination of low present diversity, long separate history and morphological similarity to old fossils, not an absence of evolution. A better description is "the sole survivor of an ancient lineage", which emphasizes its importance for reconstructing the ancestral lepidosaur without implying that it stopped evolving.
