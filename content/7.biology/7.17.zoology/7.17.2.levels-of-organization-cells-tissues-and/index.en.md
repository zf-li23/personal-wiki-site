An animal body is built from cells, and the cells are organized at several levels: they combine into tissues, tissues into organs, and organs into organ systems. The levels are not merely a convenient hierarchy of description. Each level depends on inventions of the animal lineage (cell junctions, a collagenous extracellular matrix, epithelia, contractile and excitable cells), and the animal phyla differ in how far up the hierarchy their bodies are built. This chapter introduces the grades of organization, the features of animal cells that make tissues possible, the four basic tissues and their invertebrate variants, and the organ systems that later chapters compare across the phyla. Cell structure itself is the subject of cell biology ([7.3](/7.3)), and the function of each organ system that of animal physiology ([7.14](/7.14)); here we stress what is shared and what varies among animals. The next two chapters ([7.17.3](/7.17.3), [7.17.4](/7.17.4)) build on these levels to describe the architecture of the whole body, and [7.17.44](/7.17.44) returns to the organ systems of invertebrates after all the phyla have been described.

## 2.1. Grades of Organization

Zoologists traditionally recognize five grades of structural organization. They describe how the work of the body is divided among its parts:

| Grade | Division of labor | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Protoplasmic (unicellular) | All functions within one cell, divided among organelles | Protists, such as ciliates and amoebae ([7.11.20](/7.11.20)) |
| Cellular | Several cell types, each with its own task, but cells not organized into tissues | Sponges ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)) |
| Cell–tissue | Cells organized into tissues, mainly two epithelia | Cnidarians, ctenophores ([7.17.8](/7.17.8), [7.17.9](/7.17.9)) |
| Tissue–organ | Tissues combined into organs (pharynx, protonephridia, gonads) | Flatworms ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)) |
| Organ system | Organs linked into systems (circulatory, excretory, nervous) | Most bilaterians, such as annelids, molluscs, arthropods, vertebrates |

![Grades of organization in animals, illustrated by living representatives (silhouettes): a choanoflagellate (one cell, sometimes a colony of similar cells), a sponge (cellular grade: several cell types but no true tissues), *Hydra* (tissue grade: two epithelia with a nerve net, separated by mesoglea), a planarian (organ grade: organs composed of several tissues) and an earthworm (organ-system grade). The series is a sequence of grades, not a line of descent: the living species are not ancestors of one another](./plots/levels-of-organization.py)

The boundaries are not sharp. Sponges have epithelium-like layers (the pinacoderm and the choanoderm) joined by junctions, and homoscleromorph sponges even have a basal lamina; cnidarians have organ-like structures such as gonads and the rhopalia of jellyfish. More importantly, grades are not clades. The placozoans and sponges are simple, but phylogenomics leaves open whether their simplicity is ancestral or partly secondary, and parasitic or minute bilaterians, such as the dicyemids with only a few dozen cells, have lost organ systems their ancestors had ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)). The grades describe the levels a body can reach, not a ladder that evolution climbed once.

What changes from one grade to the next is the degree of integration. In a colonial choanoflagellate every cell can feed, divide and give rise to a new colony. In a sponge the cells are specialized, but many of them can still transform into one another, and a sponge pressed through a fine sieve reaggregates into a functional animal. In a cnidarian the epithelial cells are permanently committed, and coordination is taken over by a nerve net. In bilaterians whole organs depend on one another through a circulation, a nervous system and hormones, and the loss of one organ is fatal. Higher grades thus buy efficiency and size at the price of reduced autonomy of the parts.

### 2.1.1. Animal Tissues

A **tissue** is a group of cells of similar origin and structure, together with the extracellular material they produce, that carries out a common function. The botanical definition ([7.12.4](/7.12.4#def-4.1.1)) stresses common origin from a meristem; in animals tissues are usually classified by structure and function, and one organ contains tissues derived from different germ layers. Histologists recognize four basic tissues in vertebrates, and the same four categories describe the tissues of most other animals (see the figure below).

![The four basic tissues of animals and their representative cells. Epithelial tissue: cells packed in sheets on a basal lamina and polarized, here a simple columnar epithelium, with a mucus-secreting goblet cell. Connective tissue: few cells in an abundant extracellular matrix; fibroblasts secrete collagen fibres, adipocytes store fat, and blood is a connective tissue with a fluid matrix. Muscle tissue: skeletal muscle fibres (multinucleate, striated), cardiomyocytes (branched, striated, joined by intercalated discs) and smooth muscle cells (spindle-shaped, without striations). Nervous tissue: neurons and glial cells, such as the oligodendrocytes that myelinate axons in the vertebrate central nervous system](./plots/four-tissues.py)

The four tissues differ above all in the ratio of cells to extracellular material and in the way their cells are attached:

| Tissue | Cells | Extracellular material | Typical attachment | Main roles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Epithelial | Densely packed, polarized | Very little, except the basal lamina beneath | To each other by junctions; to the basal lamina below | Covering, lining, secretion, absorption, sensing |
| Connective | Scattered | Abundant: fibers and ground substance, or a fluid | To the matrix by integrins | Support, binding, storage, transport, defense |
| Muscle | Elongated, contractile | Little; each fiber has its own basal lamina | To each other and to tendons or cuticle | Movement, shape change, pumping |
| Nervous | Neurons with long processes, glia | Little | To each other at synapses | Conduction, integration, control |

The germ layers do not map one-to-one onto the tissues. Epithelia arise from all three layers (the epidermis from ectoderm, the gut lining from endoderm, the lining of the coelom and of blood vessels from mesoderm); connective tissue and muscle come mostly from mesoderm, but in the vertebrate head much of the connective tissue, cartilage and bone derives from the **neural crest**, an ectodermal population ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)); and nervous tissue comes from ectoderm. The germ layers themselves are introduced in [7.17.3](/7.17.3).

## 2.2. What Is Special About Animal Cells

Animal cells have the eukaryotic organelles common to all protists, fungi and plants ([7.3](/7.3)). What distinguishes them is a set of features that allow cells to cooperate in tissues. Most of these features are older than the animals: cadherins, integrins and some matrix proteins are found in choanoflagellates and other unicellular relatives, where they serve for feeding and attachment, and they were recruited for multicellularity in the first animals ([7.17.1](/7.17.1)).

### 2.2.1. Cell Junctions

Animal cells adhere to each other through **adherens junctions** built of cadherins and to the matrix through integrins; sealing junctions close the spaces between epithelial cells; anchoring junctions resist tension; and gap junctions couple the cytoplasm of neighboring cells ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)). The types differ between vertebrates and invertebrates in ways that matter for comparative histology:

| Junction | Function | Vertebrates | Invertebrates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Occluding | Seals the space between epithelial cells; separates apical and basolateral membrane | **Tight junction** at the apex (claudins, occludin) | **Septate junction** below the apex, a ladder of septa in section (pleated septate junctions in arthropods and molluscs, smooth septate junctions in the arthropod midgut); vertebrates keep septate-like junctions only at the paranodes of myelinated axons |
| Adherens | Links the actin cytoskeleton of neighboring cells; a belt below the apex | Adherens junction (zonula adherens) of classical cadherins and catenins | The same; it is the most conserved junction and is present in sponges |
| Desmosome | Spot welds anchoring intermediate filaments (keratins in epithelia) | **Desmosomes** with desmosomal cadherins; abundant in skin and heart | Absent: desmosomes are a vertebrate invention; arthropods have no cytoplasmic intermediate filaments at all |
| Gap | Channels that pass ions and small molecules between cells | **Gap junctions** of connexins (tunicates and vertebrates) | Gap junctions of innexins, an unrelated family; none in sponges and placozoans |
| Cell–matrix | Anchors the basal surface to the basal lamina | **Hemidesmosomes** (integrin α6β4, keratin) and focal adhesions (integrins, actin) | Integrin-based adhesions; in insect epidermis hemi-adherens junctions anchor the muscle-attachment fibers that cross the epidermis |

Chinese textbooks describe the septate junction of invertebrates as a "honeycomb desmosome", because in tangential section the septa form a honeycomb pattern; it is an occluding junction, not a desmosome. The difference in position is useful in practice: a section of an insect epithelium shows the adherens belt at the apex and the septate junction beneath it, whereas in a vertebrate epithelium the tight junction lies above the adherens belt.

### 2.2.2. The Extracellular Matrix

All animals secrete an **extracellular matrix**, and its principal protein is **collagen**. Collagens are defined by their triple helix of three chains with the repeat Gly–X–Y, and they occur in two main forms. Fibrillar collagens assemble into banded fibrils and give tissues their tensile strength; vertebrates have several (type I in bone, tendon and skin, type II in cartilage, type III in reticular fibers). Network-forming type IV collagen, with laminin, nidogen and the proteoglycan perlecan, forms the **basal lamina**, a sheet 50–100 nm thick beneath every epithelium and around every muscle fiber.

The basal lamina is the oldest form of animal matrix that we can recognize. Its proteins are present in homoscleromorph sponges, ctenophores, cnidarians and all bilaterians, but most demosponges and the placozoans lack a basal lamina, which may be a secondary loss. Other matrix components are more restricted: the spongin of demosponges is a collagen; the cuticles of nematodes and annelids are made of special cuticular collagens ([7.17.17](/7.17.17), [7.17.15](/7.17.15)); fibronectin is found only in vertebrates and **elastin**, the rubbery protein of arteries and lungs, only in jawed vertebrates. Invertebrates with high-pressure circulations, such as octopuses, use other elastomeric proteins in their arteries. The ground substance between the fibers consists of **proteoglycans** and hyaluronan, which bind water and resist compression.

### 2.2.3. Cilia, Flagella and Cell Types

Animal sperm swims with a single flagellum, and many epithelia are ciliated: ciliated cells drive water through sponges, propel larvae and small flatworms, and move mucus in vertebrate airways. Cnidarian and bilaterian sensory cells often carry a modified cilium ([7.3.14](/7.3.14)). An old comparative observation is that epithelia with one cilium per cell (monociliated) prevail in sponges, cnidarians and most deuterostomes, whereas epithelia with many cilia per cell (multiciliated) prevail in ctenophores and spiralians, such as flatworms, annelids and molluscs; the larval ciliary bands of the two groups also differ ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)). The choanocytes of sponges, with a flagellum surrounded by a collar of microvilli, resemble choanoflagellates, and similar collared cells occur in other phyla, from the epidermis of cnidarians to the nephridia of some invertebrates.

Animal cells have no cell wall. Without a rigid wall they can change shape, migrate during development and engulf particles. Support comes instead from the cytoskeleton, the matrix and, in larger animals, skeletons.

The number of cell types increases with the grade of organization: sponges have about a dozen, placozoans at least six, cnidarians a few dozen, and vertebrates several hundred. Single-cell sequencing now defines cell types by their expression profiles, which makes it possible to compare them across phyla and to ask which types are homologous ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.4.1)). In this view a cell type is an evolutionary unit with its own history, defined by a core regulatory complex of transcription factors, and tissues are communities of cell types that work together.

## 2.3. Epithelial Tissue

**Definition 2.3.1 (Epithelial Tissue)** An **epithelium** (epithelial tissue) is a sheet of closely packed cells joined by junctions, with little intercellular material, that covers an external or internal surface of the body or lines a cavity. Its cells are polarized, with an apical surface facing the outside or the lumen and a basal surface resting on a basal lamina, below which lies connective tissue.

Epithelia separate the internal environment from the outside and control what passes between them: they protect, absorb, secrete, excrete and sense. They are avascular and are nourished by diffusion from the connective tissue beneath. Because they line every surface, epithelia are the first tissue to have evolved; the body of a cnidarian is essentially two epithelia, and the epidermis and gut lining of all animals are epithelia. By function, epithelia are classified as covering, glandular and sensory epithelia.

### 2.3.1. Covering Epithelia

Covering epithelia are classified by the number of cell layers and the shape of the cells at the surface (see the figure below).

![Types of covering epithelium. Simple epithelia have one layer of cells: squamous (flattened; the endothelium of blood vessels and the lining of lung alveoli, where diffusion distances must be short), cuboidal (kidney tubules, ducts of glands) and columnar (intestinal lining). A pseudostratified epithelium is a single layer of cells of unequal height whose nuclei lie at different levels, so that it appears layered; that of the airways is ciliated. Stratified squamous epithelium has many layers renewed from the basal layer; in land vertebrates its outer cells become keratinized (skin)](./plots/epithelium-types.py)

| Type | Structure | Example | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple squamous | One layer of flat cells | Endothelium, alveolar wall, peritoneum | Diffusion, filtration |
| Simple cuboidal | One layer of cube-shaped cells | Kidney tubules, gland ducts | Secretion, absorption |
| Simple columnar | One layer of tall cells, often with microvilli or cilia | Intestine; ciliated oviduct | Absorption, secretion, transport |
| Pseudostratified columnar | One layer of cells of unequal height | Airways (ciliated) | Secretion and mucus transport |
| Stratified squamous | Many layers, flat at the surface | Vertebrate epidermis (keratinized), esophagus (not keratinized) | Protection against abrasion and water loss |
| Transitional | Many layers whose surface cells change shape when stretched | Urinary bladder | Distension |

The simple squamous epithelia that line blood vessels and the body cavities are given special names: the endothelium of the heart and vessels and the mesothelium of the peritoneum, pleura and pericardium. Both derive from mesoderm, which is one reason why some histologists treat them separately, but structurally they are epithelia.

The free surface of an epithelium is specialized for its task. Microvilli, finger-like extensions supported by bundles of actin filaments, increase the absorptive area up to twentyfold; they form the brush border of the kidney tubules and the striated border of intestinal cells. Cilia move fluid or mucus over the surface. In stratified epithelia the surface cells may fill with **keratin** and die (keratinization), which makes the skin of land vertebrates resistant to water loss. Where the epithelium faces the matrix, the basal surface is folded in transporting epithelia, which increases the area for ion pumps, as in the salt glands of birds and the rectal pads of insects.

### 2.3.2. The Epidermis Across the Phyla

The epidermis of most invertebrates is a simple epithelium, cuboidal or columnar, and secretes an extracellular covering: a collagenous cuticle in annelids and nematodes, the chitinous **cuticle** of arthropods ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)), the shell and periostracum of molluscs. In arthropods the living epidermis (older texts call it the hypodermis) and the nonliving cuticle it secretes together form the integument. Some epithelia are syncytial: the outer covering of parasitic flatworms, the tegument, is a continuous cytoplasm without cell boundaries ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)), and so are much of the nematode epidermis and most of the tissue of glass sponges.

| Group | Epidermis | Secreted covering |
|---|---|---|
| Sponges | Pinacoderm of flat pinacocytes, without a basal lamina in most species | None |
| Cnidarians | Simple columnar, mostly of epitheliomuscular cells, with cnidocytes and gland cells | Thin mucus; chitinous perisarc in hydroids; aragonite skeleton in stony corals |
| Free-living flatworms | Simple, ciliated (at least ventrally), with rhabdites and adhesive glands | None |
| Parasitic flatworms | Syncytial tegument with sunken nuclei | Glycocalyx |
| Nematodes | Thin, partly syncytial hypodermis with four longitudinal cords | Multilayered collagenous cuticle, molted |
| Annelids | Simple columnar, with gland cells; chaetae formed in epidermal follicles | Thin collagenous cuticle, not molted |
| Molluscs | Simple; the mantle epithelium secretes the shell in layers | Periostracum (protein), prismatic and nacreous layers (calcium carbonate) |
| Arthropods | Simple cuboidal or columnar | Chitin–protein cuticle, sclerotized or mineralized, molted |
| Echinoderms | Simple, often ciliated, over a dermis with calcite ossicles | Thin cuticle |
| Vertebrates | Stratified; mucous and unkeratinized in fishes and amphibian larvae, keratinized in amniotes | Keratinized layers, scales, feathers, hair (from the epidermis); bony scales from the dermis |

The contrast between a single-layered epidermis that secretes a dead covering (most invertebrates) and a multilayered epidermis whose own cells die and form the covering (land vertebrates) is the basic difference between the two integuments. The vertebrate integument is compared in [7.17.57](/7.17.57).

### 2.3.3. Glandular and Sensory Epithelia

Glands are epithelial cells specialized for secretion, and most glandular epithelia are simple cuboidal or columnar. A unicellular gland, such as the goblet cell that secretes mucus, lies among other epithelial cells; multicellular glands are epithelia folded into tubes or sacs. An **exocrine gland** releases its products through ducts onto a surface; an **endocrine gland** has lost its ducts and releases hormones into the blood. Glands are classified by shape and by the mode of release:

| Criterion | Types | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Number of cells | Unicellular; multicellular | Goblet cells, the gland cells of the earthworm epidermis; salivary glands |
| Shape of the secretory part | Tubular (straight or coiled), alveolar (acinar, sac-shaped), tubuloalveolar | Intestinal crypts, sweat glands; sebaceous glands; salivary and mammary glands |
| Branching of the duct | Simple (one duct); compound (branched ducts) | Sweat gland; pancreas, mammary gland |
| Mode of release | Merocrine (exocytosis of vesicles); apocrine (part of the apical cytoplasm is shed); holocrine (the whole cell disintegrates) | Most glands; mammary gland; sebaceous gland |
| Product | Serous (watery, with enzymes); mucous (viscous mucins); mixed | Parotid gland; goblet cells; submandibular gland |

Invertebrates have a great variety of epidermal glands: adhesive glands of flatworms and gastrotrichs, silk glands of spiders and caterpillars, the ink sac of cephalopods, the venom glands of centipede forcipules, and the clitellum of earthworms, which secretes the cocoon. Many invertebrate endocrine organs are neurosecretory rather than epithelial, such as the corpora cardiaca of insects and the X-organ of crustaceans, which release neurohormones from axon terminals ([7.17.26](/7.17.26), [7.17.21](/7.17.21)).

Sensory epithelia contain receptor cells among supporting cells: olfactory epithelium, taste buds, the retina (a derivative of the brain), and the hair-cell epithelia of the vertebrate inner ear and lateral line. Receptor cells are of two kinds. Primary sensory cells are neurons whose axons run to the central nervous system, as in the olfactory epithelium; secondary sensory cells, such as hair cells and taste cells, lack axons and pass their signal at a synapse to a sensory neuron. Many invertebrate sense organs are likewise epithelial, for example the sensilla of insects, built of a few epidermal cells around a sensory neuron ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)).

## 2.4. Connective Tissue

**Definition 2.4.1 (Connective Tissue)** A **connective tissue** consists of relatively few cells dispersed in an abundant extracellular matrix of protein fibers and ground substance, which the cells secrete. It supports and binds other tissues, fills spaces between organs, stores energy and, in the form of blood, transports material.

The properties of a connective tissue are determined mostly by its matrix. The fibers are collagen fibers (tensile strength), elastic fibers of elastin (recoil) and reticular fibers (fine collagen networks that support soft organs); the ground substance contains glycosaminoglycans and proteoglycans that bind water and resist compression. Collagen fibers, white in fresh tissue, are flexible but barely extensible and dissolve into gelatin when boiled; elastic fibers, yellow, can be stretched to about one and a half times their length and resist boiling and weak acids. A tendon can carry about 100 MPa before it breaks, comparable to a steel wire of the same cross-section at one-fifth its mass.

The resident cells are fibroblasts, which make and maintain the matrix and are activated in wound healing, together with **macrophages** (histiocytes) that engulf debris and microbes, **mast cells** that release histamine and heparin in inflammation, adipocytes, and **plasma cells** that secrete antibodies. Wandering cells of the blood pass through the tissue. All of them derive in the embryo from mesenchyme, a loose tissue of star-shaped cells that is the common source of the connective tissues ([7.15](/7.15) for the immune cells).

### 2.4.1. Types of Connective Tissue in Vertebrates

| Type | Matrix | Cells | Location and role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loose (areolar) | Loosely woven fibers in abundant ground substance | Fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells | Beneath epithelia, around vessels and nerves; packing tissue |
| Dense regular | Parallel collagen bundles | Rows of fibroblasts | Tendons, ligaments |
| Dense irregular | Collagen bundles in all directions | Fibroblasts | Dermis, capsules of organs |
| Elastic | Parallel elastic fibers or fenestrated sheets | Fibroblasts, smooth muscle | Ligamentum nuchae of grazing mammals, walls of large arteries |
| Reticular | Network of reticular fibers | Reticular cells | Framework of lymph nodes, spleen, bone marrow |
| Adipose | Little matrix | Adipocytes | Energy store, insulation; **brown adipose tissue** produces heat ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)) |
| Cartilage | Firm gel of collagen and proteoglycans, no blood vessels | Chondrocytes in lacunae | Embryonic skeleton, joints, ear, larynx; the adult skeleton of sharks |
| Bone | Collagen mineralized with hydroxyapatite | Osteoblasts, osteocytes in lacunae, osteoclasts | Skeleton; calcium store ([7.14.36](/7.14.36)) |
| Blood and lymph | Fluid plasma | Red and white blood cells, platelets | Transport, defense ([7.14.11](/7.14.11)) |

Adipose tissue consists of large cells whose cytoplasm is filled by one fat droplet (white fat) or by many small droplets with abundant mitochondria (brown fat); loose connective tissue divides it into lobules. Besides storing energy at about 37 kJ g⁻¹, twice the yield of carbohydrate, white fat is an endocrine organ that secretes leptin ([7.14.30](/7.14.30)).

### 2.4.2. Cartilage and Bone

Cartilage consists of chondrocytes in cavities (lacunae) of a firm matrix; the cells of one lacuna, often two to four, are the offspring of one cell (an isogenous group). Cartilage has no blood vessels or nerves; it is nourished by diffusion from the perichondrium, a sheath of dense connective tissue, and therefore heals slowly. It comes in three forms: hyaline cartilage, the most common, with fine collagen fibrils (articular surfaces, ribs, trachea, the embryonic skeleton); elastic cartilage, with elastic fibers (external ear, epiglottis); and fibrocartilage, with dense collagen bundles (intervertebral discs, the pubic symphysis). The skeleton of sharks and rays is cartilage, often reinforced by a surface layer of mineralized tiles (tesserae) ([7.17.46](/7.17.46)).

Bone is a connective tissue whose collagen matrix is mineralized with crystals of hydroxyapatite, a calcium phosphate, which make up about two-thirds of its dry mass. Three cell types maintain it: osteoblasts deposit new matrix; osteocytes, osteoblasts walled up in their own matrix, lie in lacunae and communicate with each other through cytoplasmic processes in fine canaliculi, sensing mechanical strain; and osteoclasts, multinucleate cells derived from the macrophage lineage, resorb bone. The balance of deposition and resorption is **bone remodeling** ([7.14.36](/7.14.36)).

Bone is either compact or spongy. In compact bone the matrix is laid down in lamellae: outer and inner circumferential lamellae beneath the periosteum and around the marrow cavity, and between them many *Haversian systems* (osteons), cylinders of concentric lamellae around a central canal that carries blood vessels and nerves and runs along the long axis of the bone; transverse canals connect the central canals with each other. Spongy bone, a lattice of trabeculae with marrow in its spaces, fills the ends of long bones and the interior of short bones, and its trabeculae are aligned with the main lines of stress. Not all bone has osteocytes: the bone of most teleost fishes is acellular. The skeletal tissues and their development (**endochondral ossification** and **intramembranous ossification**, [7.5.24](/7.5.24)) are compared across the vertebrate classes in [7.17.56](/7.17.56) and [7.17.58](/7.17.58).

The hardest tissues of vertebrates, dentine and enamel, are found in teeth and in the scales of many fishes. Dentine is a bone-like tissue secreted by odontoblasts, derived from the neural crest, whose processes run in tubules through the matrix; enamel is almost pure hydroxyapatite secreted by the epidermis. The combination of a dentine layer covered by an enamel-like cap is older than jaws: it is present in the dermal armor of Paleozoic jawless fishes ([7.17.45](/7.17.45)).

### 2.4.3. Blood

Blood is a connective tissue in which the matrix, the plasma, is fluid. When blood clots, the plasma protein fibrinogen polymerizes into fibers of fibrin; the fluid left after clotting, the serum, corresponds to the ground substance. The cellular elements and their roles are:

| Element | Structure | Number in human blood | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Erythrocytes | Biconcave discs about 7.5 µm across, without nucleus in mammals; oval and nucleated in other vertebrates | About $5 \times 10^{12}$ per liter | Transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide by hemoglobin |
| Neutrophils | Lobed nucleus, fine granules | 50–70 % of white cells | Phagocytosis of bacteria |
| Eosinophils | Bilobed nucleus, red-staining granules | 1–4 % | Defense against parasitic worms; allergy |
| Basophils | Large dark granules | Below 1 % | Release of histamine |
| Lymphocytes | Round nucleus, little cytoplasm | 20–40 % | Adaptive immunity (B and T cells) |
| Monocytes | Kidney-shaped nucleus; the largest white cells | 2–8 % | Become macrophages in the tissues |
| Platelets | Cell fragments 2–3 µm across, without nucleus (mammals); nucleated thrombocytes in other vertebrates | $1.5$–$4 \times 10^{11}$ per liter | **Hemostasis** |

All blood cells arise from **hematopoietic stem cells**, in mammals in the red bone marrow ([7.14.11](/7.14.11), [7.15](/7.15)). The lack of a nucleus in mammalian erythrocytes and platelets is a derived character of mammals; camels and their relatives have oval, but still anucleate, red cells.

In invertebrates the body fluid contains **hemocytes**, mostly phagocytic and clotting cells; respiratory pigments, where present, are usually dissolved in the fluid rather than packed in cells. Exceptions are the hemoglobin-filled cells in the coelom of some polychaetes and of sipunculans. The amoebocytes of horseshoe crabs release a clotting cascade on contact with bacterial endotoxin, and their lysate is the standard test for endotoxin in medical products. In insects, the hemocytes encapsulate parasitoid eggs and form nodules around bacteria ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)).

### 2.4.4. Connective Tissues of Invertebrates

Mineralized bone is unique to vertebrates, but invertebrates have connective tissues of their own.

**Definition 2.4.2 (Mesoglea)** The **mesoglea** is the gelatinous layer between the epidermis and the gastrodermis of cnidarians and ctenophores. It is an extracellular matrix of water, collagen fibers and proteoglycans, secreted by the two epithelia, and is either nearly acellular (in *Hydra* a thin sheet) or contains wandering cells and, in ctenophores and many medusae, muscle cells.

The mesoglea is not a germ layer, although it lies where the mesoderm lies in bilaterians: it arises as a secretion of ectoderm and endoderm, and the few cells that enter it migrate from these layers. In large jellyfish it makes up most of the body volume, which keeps the metabolic rate per unit volume low and the body nearly neutrally buoyant ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)). Sponges have a comparable layer, the mesohyl, which contains several types of wandering cells, collagen and skeletal spicules ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)). In acoelomate flatworms the space between gut and body wall is filled by parenchyma (mesenchyme), cells and matrix of mesodermal origin that store food and contain stem cells ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)).

Other invertebrate connective tissues include the cartilage of cephalopods, which supports the brain capsule and has chondrocytes with branched processes; the cartilage-like tissues of horseshoe crabs and some polychaetes; the mutable collagenous tissue of echinoderms, which can stiffen or soften within seconds under nervous control ([7.17.42](/7.17.42)); the chloragogen tissue of earthworms, which stores glycogen and fat and processes nitrogenous waste around the gut ([7.17.15](/7.17.15)); and the **fat body** of insects, which stores reserves and makes proteins of the hemolymph ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)). In animals with an **open circulatory system** the body fluid, hemolymph, combines the roles of blood and interstitial fluid.

## 2.5. Muscle Tissue

**Definition 2.5.1 (Muscle Tissue)** A **muscle tissue** consists of elongated cells (muscle fibers) specialized for contraction, in which filaments of actin and myosin slide past each other to shorten the cell and develop force; the cells are excitable and are controlled by nerves, hormones or their own pacemakers.

Muscle cells are classified by the arrangement of their contractile filaments (see the figure below). In striated muscle the myosin and actin filaments are aligned in sarcomeres in register across the cell, which produces the cross-striation; in smooth muscle they are not aligned; in obliquely striated muscle the sarcomeres are staggered so that the bands run helically around the fiber.

![Three arrangements of the contractile filaments in animal muscle cells (schematic). (A) Cross-striated muscle: the sarcomeres of neighboring myofibrils lie in register, so the dark A bands and the light I bands, divided by Z lines, form transverse stripes; vertebrate skeletal fibers are multinucleate with peripheral nuclei. Below, one sarcomere: thick myosin filaments in the middle, thin actin filaments anchored at the Z lines and overlapping them. (B) Obliquely striated muscle of nematodes, annelids and many molluscs: the sarcomeres of neighboring rows are staggered, so the bands run obliquely; dense bodies take the place of Z lines. (C) Smooth muscle: spindle-shaped cells with a central nucleus, in which thick and thin filaments are not aligned and thin filaments are anchored to dense bodies in the cytoplasm and at the membrane](./plots/muscle-striation.py)

|---|---|---|---|
| Skeletal (striated) | Long multinucleate fibers formed by fusion of myoblasts; sarcomeres in register | Vertebrate skeletal muscle; arthropod muscles (also striated, but mononucleate or syncytial fibers in some) | [7.14.9](/7.14.9) |

| Epitheliomuscular cells | Epithelial cells with contractile basal processes | Cnidarian epidermis and gastrodermis | [7.17.9](/7.17.9) |

### 2.5.1. Vertebrate Muscle Tissues

A skeletal muscle is organized hierarchically. The whole muscle, wrapped in connective tissue, consists of bundles (fascicles) of muscle fibers; each fiber is a single multinucleate cell up to several centimeters long, with a hundred or more nuclei beneath its membrane (the sarcolemma), formed in the embryo by the fusion of myoblasts. The fiber is filled with myofibrils, about 1 µm in diameter, which are chains of sarcomeres. Within each sarcomere the thick filaments of myosin form the dark A band; the thin filaments of actin, anchored at the Z lines, extend into the A band from both sides, and the region with only thin filaments is the light I band; the middle of the A band, where thin filaments do not reach, is the H zone, bisected by the M line. During contraction the thin filaments slide between the thick ones, the I band and H zone narrow and the A band keeps its width, which is the sliding filament mechanism ([7.14.8](/7.14.8), [7.14.9](/7.14.9)). Skeletal muscle is under voluntary control.

Cardiac muscle cells are short, branched and usually have one central nucleus. Their myofibrils are striated like those of skeletal muscle, but the cells are joined end to end by **intercalated discs**, stepped junctions that combine adherens-type junctions anchoring the myofibrils, desmosomes, and gap junctions through which excitation spreads from cell to cell. The heart therefore contracts as a functional unit, and its rhythm is generated by its own pacemaker cells ([7.14.13](/7.14.13)).

Smooth muscle cells are spindle-shaped, 20–500 µm long, with a central nucleus. Their thick and thin filaments do not form sarcomeres; thin filaments are anchored to dense bodies in the cytoplasm and dense plaques at the membrane, which correspond to Z lines, and contraction makes the cell twist and shorten like a corkscrew. Smooth muscle contracts slowly and economically and can sustain tension for long periods. In single-unit smooth muscle, as in the gut, uterus and bladder, the cells are coupled by gap junctions and contract together; in multi-unit smooth muscle, as in the iris and the large arteries, each cell is controlled by its own nerve terminal. Myoepithelial cells, contractile epithelial cells around the secretory units of glands, are a smooth-muscle-like cell type of ectodermal origin.

### 2.5.2. Muscle in Invertebrates

Striated muscle has evolved more than once. Jellyfish swimming muscles are striated, but the genes that make their sarcomeres partly differ from those of bilaterian striated muscle, and comparative genomics indicates that striated muscle arose independently in cnidarians and bilaterians from a common contractile cell type (Steinmetz and colleagues, 2012). Ctenophore muscles are smooth and lie in the mesoglea. Sponges and placozoans have no muscle cells, although their cells contract with actin and myosin.

Within the bilaterians, the arrangement of filaments is suited to the mechanical demands on each muscle:

- **Obliquely striated muscle**, with its staggered sarcomeres, can shorten over a far greater range than cross-striated muscle and still develop force, which suits the body walls of worms and the mantle of squids, where muscles act on a hydrostatic skeleton ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)). In nematodes the muscle cells send processes (muscle arms) to the nerve cords instead of receiving nerve branches ([7.17.17](/7.17.17)).
- Arthropod muscles are cross-striated, and their sarcomeres are often longer than those of vertebrates (up to 10 µm in some crustacean claw muscles), which gives slower but stronger contraction. The asynchronous flight muscle of many insects contracts many times per nerve impulse, driven by stretch activation ([7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.6.5)).
- The **catch muscle** of bivalve molluscs, such as the adductor that holds a mussel or oyster shut, contains thick filaments with a core of paramyosin. After contraction it can lock in a "catch" state and maintain tension for hours with almost no expenditure of energy, released by a serotonin-mediated signal ([7.17.14](/7.17.14)).
- Echinoderms have smooth muscles in the tube feet and body wall, but much of their posture is held by mutable collagenous tissue rather than by muscle.

## 2.6. Nervous Tissue

**Definition 2.6.1 (Nervous Tissue)** A **nervous tissue** consists of neurons, cells specialized to receive stimuli and transmit signals rapidly to other cells through electrical impulses and chemical synapses, together with glial cells that support, nourish and insulate them.

### 2.6.1. Neurons

A **neuron** has a cell body (soma) with the nucleus, usually several dendrites that receive signals, and an axon that conducts impulses away from the cell body. The soma and dendrites contain Nissl bodies, stacks of rough endoplasmic reticulum that stain with basic dyes, but the axon and the axon hillock from which it arises do not, so the axon can be recognized in stained sections. A neuron has many dendrites but only one axon, which may branch at its end; axons range from a few micrometers to more than a meter in the motor neurons of large mammals. Neurons are classified by shape and by function:

| Criterion | Types | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Number of processes | Unipolar (one process); pseudounipolar (one process that divides into a peripheral and a central branch); bipolar (two); multipolar (one axon, many dendrites) | Most invertebrate central neurons; vertebrate sensory neurons of the dorsal root ganglia; retinal bipolar cells; motor neurons, **Purkinje cells** |
| Function | Sensory (afferent), interneurons, motor (efferent) | Mechanoreceptors; most neurons of the brain; neurons innervating muscles |
| Transmitter | Cholinergic, glutamatergic, GABAergic, aminergic, peptidergic | Vertebrate motor neurons (acetylcholine); arthropod motor neurons (glutamate) |

The cellular basis of signaling is treated in [7.14.4](/7.14.4)–[7.14.5](/7.14.5) and [7.16.4](/7.16.4). A structural difference between the central nervous systems of vertebrates and of most invertebrates deserves mention here. In arthropods, annelids and molluscs, the central neurons are mostly unipolar: their cell bodies lie in a rind at the surface of each ganglion and send a single process into a central neuropil, where all synapses are made. In vertebrates, multipolar neurons receive synapses on their cell bodies and dendrites throughout the gray matter.

### 2.6.2. Glia and Myelin

**Glial cells** outnumber neurons in many parts of the vertebrate brain and perform many tasks:

| Glial cell | Location | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Astrocytes | Central nervous system | Regulate ions and transmitters at synapses; part of the **blood–brain barrier**; supply metabolites |
| Oligodendrocytes | Central nervous system | Each myelinates segments of many axons |
| Microglia | Central nervous system | Immune defense; pruning of synapses (of macrophage lineage, not neural) |
| Ependymal cells | Lining of the ventricles and central canal | Produce and move cerebrospinal fluid |
| Schwann cells | Peripheral nerves | Each myelinates one segment of one axon, or ensheathes several unmyelinated axons |
| Satellite cells | Peripheral ganglia | Surround neuronal cell bodies |

**Myelin** is a spiral wrapping of glial membrane that insulates the axon; it is interrupted at regular intervals by the **nodes of Ranvier**, where the action potential is regenerated, which speeds conduction many times over that of an unmyelinated axon of the same diameter. Invertebrates also have glia, which ensheath axons and form a blood–brain barrier in insects, where the barrier is sealed by septate junctions between glial cells.

### 2.6.3. Nervous Tissue Across the Phyla

Nervous tissue is present in all animals except sponges and placozoans. In cnidarians the neurons form a **nerve net** in both epithelia; in ctenophores the subepithelial nerve net is a syncytium of fused neurons, one reason to think that their nervous system may have evolved separately ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.1)). Myelin evolved independently in vertebrates, annelids and copepod crustaceans; cephalopods and many arthropods achieve fast conduction instead with giant axons, such as the squid giant axon of up to 1 mm diameter in which the action potential was first analyzed. The arrangement of nervous tissue into nerve nets, nerve cords and brains is described in [7.17.4](/7.17.4).

## 2.7. Organs and Organ Systems

### 2.7.1. Organs

An organ is a structure composed of several tissues that together perform a particular function and have a characteristic form. The small intestine of a vertebrate, for example, has an absorptive columnar epithelium, loose connective tissue with blood vessels and lymphatics, layers of smooth muscle and a nerve plexus; none of these tissues alone can digest and absorb food. The tissues of an organ are not a mechanical aggregate: the epithelium digests and absorbs, the connective tissue supports and carries the vessels that bring blood and take away absorbed food, the smooth muscle mixes and moves the contents, and the nerves coordinate them.

The tissues of an organ are of two kinds: the parenchyma, the tissue that carries out the organ's specific function (the hepatocytes of the liver, the nephrons of the kidney), and the stroma, the connective tissue framework with vessels and nerves. Organs are either hollow, with a wall of concentric layers around a lumen, or solid, with parenchyma packed within a capsule. The wall of the vertebrate digestive tube shows the layered pattern of hollow organs:

| Layer | Tissues | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Mucosa | Epithelium, loose connective tissue (lamina propria), a thin muscle layer | Secretion and absorption |
| Submucosa | Dense irregular connective tissue with vessels, a nerve plexus and in places glands | Support and supply |
| Muscularis externa | Inner circular and outer longitudinal smooth muscle with a nerve plexus between them | Peristalsis and mixing |
| Serosa or adventitia | Mesothelium with connective tissue, or connective tissue alone | Covering and attachment |

The same principle, an epithelium on the inside and muscle and connective tissue outside, organizes the airways, blood vessels, ducts and the uterus. In invertebrates the gut wall is often simpler, with an epithelium resting directly on a thin layer of muscle and connective tissue, but in annelids, molluscs and arthropods it shows the same basic pattern of an inner epithelium and outer muscle layers.

### 2.7.3. How Organ Systems Are Built in Different Phyla

Each organ system can be built in a few basic ways, and the phyla combine them differently. The following table anticipates the comparative chapter [7.17.44](/7.17.44); the phylum chapters describe the details.

| Function | Without a special system | Invertebrate solutions | Vertebrate solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Support | Turgor of cells and matrix (sponges, cnidarians) | **Hydrostatic skeleton** (worms, cnidarians), **exoskeleton** (arthropods, mollusc shells), calcite endoskeleton (echinoderms) | Endoskeleton of cartilage and bone |
| Digestion | Intracellular, in choanocytes (sponges) | **Gastrovascular cavity** with one opening; **complete digestive tract** | Complete tract with large glands (liver, pancreas) |
| Gas exchange | Diffusion across the surface | Gills (molluscs, crustaceans, polychaetes), book lungs, tracheae, the skin (earthworms) | Gills, lungs |
| Internal transport | Diffusion, the gastrovascular cavity | Coelomic fluid; **open circulatory system** (arthropods, most molluscs); closed system (annelids, cephalopods) | Closed system with a chambered heart |
| Excretion and osmoregulation | Diffusion of ammonia; contractile vacuoles in sponge cells of fresh water | **Protonephridia** (flatworms, rotifers, larvae), **metanephridia** (annelids, molluscs), Malpighian tubules (insects, spiders) | Kidneys of nephrons |
| Control | Local signaling between cells | Nerve net; **ladder-like nervous system**; ganglionated **ventral nerve cord** with a brain | **Dorsal hollow nerve cord** with a brain |

Organ systems are not independent. The coelom of annelids is at once a skeleton, a circulatory space and part of the excretory system; the vertebrate pharynx serves feeding in lancelets and respiration in fishes; the insect fat body combines the roles of liver and adipose tissue. Following such shifts of function through the phyla is one of the main tasks of comparative zoology.

### 2.7.4. Correspondence with Chinese Textbooks

Chinese textbooks of general zoology open with the same material: the cell, the four basic tissues with the same subdivision of each, and the concepts of organ and system. They list the muscle types as striated (skeletal), cardiac, obliquely striated and smooth, exactly as above, and give the obliquely striated muscle of roundworms and earthworms as the invertebrate example. Two points are presented differently here. The "honeycomb desmosome" of invertebrate epithelia is a septate junction (Section 2.2.1). And the grades of organization are presented as levels of complexity rather than as stages in a single line of evolution, because several phyla have become simpler secondarily.

| Level | Characteristic | Lowest grade where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| Cell | Organelles; animal cells add junctions, collagenous matrix, flagellated sperm | Protists (protoplasmic grade) |
| Cell types | Division of labor among cells | Sponges (cellular grade) |
| Tissue | Cells of similar structure and their matrix, performing a common function | Cnidarians (tissue grade) |
| Organ | Several tissues performing a function | Flatworms (organ grade) |
| Organ system | Organs linked in one function | Most bilaterians |

- Animal cells cooperate through junctions (adherens junctions in all animals; tight junctions in vertebrates and septate junctions in invertebrates; desmosomes only in vertebrates; gap junctions of connexins or innexins) and a collagenous extracellular matrix with a basal lamina of type IV collagen and laminin.
- The four basic tissues are **epithelial tissue** (Definition 2.3.1; covering, glandular and sensory epithelia, classified by layers and cell shape), **connective tissue** (Definition 2.4.1; loose, dense, elastic, reticular, adipose, cartilage, bone and blood), **muscle tissue** (Definition 2.5.1; skeletal, cardiac, smooth and obliquely striated) and **nervous tissue** (Definition 2.6.1; neurons and glia).
- Bone: osteoblasts, osteocytes and osteoclasts; compact bone of Haversian systems and circumferential lamellae, spongy bone of trabeculae. Blood: erythrocytes, five kinds of leukocytes and platelets in plasma.
- Invertebrate variants include the cuticle-secreting simple epidermis, syncytial teguments, the **mesoglea** of cnidarians and ctenophores (Definition 2.4.2), the mesohyl of sponges, the parenchyma of flatworms, cephalopod cartilage, chloragogen tissue, the insect fat body, echinoderm mutable collagenous tissue, epitheliomuscular cells, obliquely striated muscle and the catch muscle of bivalves.
- Organs combine parenchyma and stroma; hollow organs, such as the gut, have layered walls of mucosa, submucosa, muscularis and serosa.
- Grades of organization are levels, not clades: striated muscle, myelin and perhaps nervous systems evolved more than once, and simple bodies may be secondarily simplified.

## Questions

1. Why is mesoglea not counted as a third germ layer, although it lies between the epidermis and gastrodermis as mesoderm lies between ectoderm and endoderm?

   **Answer** A germ layer is a layer of embryonic cells set aside during gastrulation that gives rise to a defined set of tissues. Mesoglea is an extracellular matrix secreted by the two epithelia; the cells found in it are immigrants from those epithelia, not a separate population of embryonic cells. Mesoderm, in contrast, forms from its own cell lineage (for example from the 4d cell in spiralians) and gives rise to muscle, connective tissue and the coelomic lining.

2. Blood contains no fibers and its cells are not fixed. Why is it classified as a connective tissue?

   **Answer** By its structure and origin: blood consists of cells dispersed in an abundant extracellular matrix (plasma, whose fibrinogen polymerizes into fibers during clotting), its cells come from the same mesenchymal stem cell lineages that give rise to other connective tissues in the embryo, and it connects the other tissues functionally by transport. The defining features of connective tissue are few cells and much matrix, not a solid matrix.

3. An insect's flight muscle and a vertebrate's leg muscle are both striated. What evidence would decide whether striated muscle in the two is homologous?

   **Answer** Striation itself is a mechanical arrangement that can evolve repeatedly. The test is phylogenetic and molecular: map the character on the animal tree and compare the proteins that build the sarcomere. Arthropods and vertebrates are both bilaterians, and bilaterian striated muscles share a core set of sarcomeric proteins (such as the striated-muscle myosin heavy chain and titin-like proteins), which supports homology of the bilaterian striated muscle cell type, whereas in cnidarians several of these components are missing and others were recruited independently, which indicates convergence there.

4. A section of an unknown epithelium shows an apical adherens belt with a ladder-like junction beneath it, and no desmosomes. What can you infer about the animal?

   **Answer** The ladder-like junction is a septate junction, the occluding junction of invertebrates; in vertebrates the occluding tight junction lies above the adherens belt, and desmosomes are common in vertebrate epithelia. The animal is therefore an invertebrate. If, in addition, the cells contain no cytoplasmic intermediate filaments and secrete a chitinous cuticle, it is probably an arthropod.

5. Explain in mechanical terms why the body-wall muscles of earthworms and nematodes are obliquely striated rather than cross-striated.

   **Answer** These muscles act on a hydrostatic skeleton, and the body wall must shorten and lengthen greatly during peristalsis or undulation. In cross-striated muscle force falls steeply when the sarcomeres are stretched or shortened far from their optimum length, because filament overlap is lost or filaments collide. In obliquely striated muscle the staggered sarcomeres can shear past each other, so the cell can shorten to a small fraction of its rest length while the filaments keep overlapping, and force is maintained over a much wider range of lengths. The cost is lower speed of contraction, which matters little for slow burrowing or crawling.

6. Compact bone has blood vessels in its Haversian canals, whereas cartilage has none. What consequences does this have for growth, repair and the maximum thickness of the two tissues?

   **Answer** Cartilage cells are nourished only by diffusion through the matrix from the perichondrium, so cartilage cannot be very thick, its cells have a low metabolic rate, and it repairs slowly; it can grow from within, because its matrix is not mineralized (interstitial growth). Bone has a mineralized matrix through which diffusion is negligible, so its osteocytes depend on the canals and canaliculi near the vessels; this allows thick, metabolically active bone that can be remodeled and repaired quickly, but bone can grow only by deposition on surfaces (appositional growth) or by replacing cartilage, as in endochondral ossification.
