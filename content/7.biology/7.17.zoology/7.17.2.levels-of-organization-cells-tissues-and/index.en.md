An animal body is built from cells, and the cells are organized at several levels: they combine into tissues, tissues into organs, and organs into organ systems. The levels are not merely a convenient hierarchy of description. Each level depends on inventions of the animal lineage (cell junctions, a collagenous extracellular matrix, epithelia, contractile and excitable cells), and the animal phyla differ in how far up the hierarchy their bodies are built. This chapter introduces the grades of organization, the features of animal cells that make tissues possible, the four basic tissues and their invertebrate variants, and the organ systems that later chapters compare across the phyla. Cell structure itself is the subject of cell biology ([7.3](/7.3)), and the function of each organ system that of animal physiology ([7.14](/7.14)); here we stress what is shared and what varies among animals. The next two chapters ([7.17.3](/7.17.3), [7.17.4](/7.17.4)) build on these levels to describe the architecture of the whole body.

## 2.1. Grades of Organization

Zoologists traditionally recognize five grades of structural organization. They describe how the work of the body is divided among its parts:

| Grade | Division of labor | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Protoplasmic (unicellular) | All functions within one cell, divided among organelles | Protists, such as ciliates and amoebae ([7.11.20](/7.11.20)) |
| Cellular | Several cell types, each with its own task, but cells not organized into tissues | Sponges ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)) |
| Cell–tissue | Cells organized into tissues, mainly two epithelia | Cnidarians, ctenophores ([7.17.8](/7.17.8), [7.17.9](/7.17.9)) |
| Tissue–organ | Tissues combined into organs (pharynx, protonephridia, gonads) | Flatworms ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)) |
| Organ system | Organs linked into systems (circulatory, excretory, nervous) | Most bilaterians, such as annelids, molluscs, arthropods, vertebrates |

![Grades of organization in animals, illustrated by living representatives (silhouettes): a choanoflagellate (one cell, sometimes a colony of similar cells), a sponge (cellular grade: several cell types but no true tissues), *Hydra* (tissue grade: two epithelia with a nerve net, separated by mesoglea), a planarian (organ grade: organs composed of several tissues) and an earthworm (organ-system grade). The series is a sequence of grades, not a line of descent: the living species are not ancestors of one another](./plots/levels-of-organization.py)

The boundaries are not sharp. Sponges have epithelium-like layers (the pinacoderm and the choanoderm) joined by junctions, and homoscleromorph sponges even have a basal lamina; cnidarians have organ-like structures such as gonads and the rhopalia of jellyfish. More importantly, grades are not clades. The placozoans and sponges are simple, but phylogenomics leaves open whether their simplicity is ancestral or partly secondary, and parasitic or minute bilaterians, such as the dicyemids with only a few dozen cells, have lost organ systems their ancestors had ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)). The grades describe the levels a body can reach, not a ladder that evolution climbed once.

### 2.1.1. Animal Tissues

A **tissue** is a group of cells of similar origin and structure, together with the extracellular material they produce, that carries out a common function. The botanical definition ([7.12.4](/7.12.4#def-4.1.1)) stresses common origin from a meristem; in animals tissues are usually classified by structure and function, and one organ contains tissues derived from different germ layers. Histologists recognize four basic tissues in vertebrates, and the same four categories describe the tissues of most other animals (see the figure below).

![The four basic tissues of animals and their representative cells. Epithelial tissue: cells packed in sheets on a basal lamina and polarized, here a simple columnar epithelium, with a mucus-secreting goblet cell. Connective tissue: few cells in an abundant extracellular matrix; fibroblasts secrete collagen fibres, adipocytes store fat, and blood is a connective tissue with a fluid matrix. Muscle tissue: skeletal muscle fibres (multinucleate, striated), cardiomyocytes (branched, striated, joined by intercalated discs) and smooth muscle cells (spindle-shaped, without striations). Nervous tissue: neurons and glial cells, such as the oligodendrocytes that myelinate axons in the vertebrate central nervous system](./plots/four-tissues.py)

## 2.2. What Is Special About Animal Cells

Animal cells have the eukaryotic organelles common to all protists, fungi and plants ([7.3](/7.3)). What distinguishes them is a set of features that allow cells to cooperate in tissues.

- Cell junctions. Animal cells adhere to each other through **adherens junctions** built of cadherins and to the matrix through integrins; sealing junctions close the spaces between epithelial cells, namely **tight junctions** in vertebrates and septate junctions in invertebrates; **desmosomes** anchor intermediate filaments and resist tension; and gap junctions couple the cytoplasm of neighboring cells (built of connexins in chordates and of the unrelated innexins in other animals) ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)).
- The **extracellular matrix**. All animals secrete **collagen**, and most have a **basal lamina** of type IV collagen and laminin beneath their epithelia. The matrix gives tissues tensile strength, guides cell migration and stores signals.
- Cilia and flagella. Animal sperm swims with a single flagellum, and many epithelia are ciliated: ciliated cells drive water through sponges, propel larvae and small flatworms, and move mucus in vertebrate airways. Cnidarian and bilaterian sensory cells often carry a modified cilium ([7.3.14](/7.3.14)).
- No cell wall. Without a rigid wall, animal cells can change shape, migrate during development and engulf particles. Support comes instead from the cytoskeleton, the matrix and, in larger animals, skeletons.

The number of cell types increases with the grade of organization: sponges have about a dozen, placozoans at least six, cnidarians a few dozen, and vertebrates several hundred. Single-cell sequencing now defines cell types by their expression profiles, which makes it possible to compare them across phyla and to ask which types are homologous ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.4.1)).

## 2.3. Epithelial Tissue

**Definition 2.3.1 (Epithelial Tissue)** An **epithelium** (epithelial tissue) is a sheet of closely packed cells joined by junctions, with little intercellular material, that covers an external or internal surface of the body or lines a cavity. Its cells are polarized, with an apical surface facing the outside or the lumen and a basal surface resting on a basal lamina, below which lies connective tissue.

Epithelia separate the internal environment from the outside and control what passes between them: they protect, absorb, secrete, excrete and sense. They are avascular and are nourished by diffusion from the connective tissue beneath. Because they line every surface, epithelia are the first tissue to have evolved; the body of a cnidarian is essentially two epithelia, and the epidermis and gut lining of all animals are epithelia.

### 2.3.1. Covering Epithelia

Covering epithelia are classified by the number of cell layers and the shape of the cells at the surface (see the figure below).

![Types of covering epithelium. Simple epithelia have one layer of cells: squamous (flattened; the endothelium of blood vessels and the lining of lung alveoli, where diffusion distances must be short), cuboidal (kidney tubules, ducts of glands) and columnar (intestinal lining). A pseudostratified epithelium is a single layer of cells of unequal height whose nuclei lie at different levels, so that it appears layered; that of the airways is ciliated. Stratified squamous epithelium has many layers renewed from the basal layer; in land vertebrates its outer cells become keratinized (skin)](./plots/epithelium-types.py)

| Type | Structure | Example | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple squamous | One layer of flat cells | Endothelium, alveolar wall, peritoneum | Diffusion, filtration |
| Simple cuboidal | One layer of cube-shaped cells | Kidney tubules, gland ducts | Secretion, absorption |
| Simple columnar | One layer of tall cells, often with microvilli or cilia | Intestine; ciliated oviduct | Absorption, secretion, transport |
| Pseudostratified columnar | One layer of cells of unequal height | Airways (ciliated) | Secretion and mucus transport |
| Stratified squamous | Many layers, flat at the surface | Vertebrate epidermis (keratinized), esophagus (not keratinized) | Protection against abrasion and water loss |
| Transitional | Many layers whose surface cells change shape when stretched | Urinary bladder | Distension |

The epidermis of most invertebrates is a simple epithelium, cuboidal or columnar, and secretes an extracellular covering: a collagenous cuticle in annelids and nematodes, the chitinous **cuticle** of arthropods ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)), the shell and periostracum of molluscs. In arthropods the living epidermis (older texts call it the hypodermis) and the nonliving cuticle it secretes together form the integument. Some epithelia are syncytial: the outer covering of parasitic flatworms, the tegument, is a continuous cytoplasm without cell boundaries ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)), and so are much of the nematode epidermis and most of the tissue of glass sponges.

### 2.3.2. Glandular and Sensory Epithelia

Glands are epithelial cells specialized for secretion. A unicellular gland, such as the goblet cell that secretes mucus, lies among other epithelial cells; multicellular glands are epithelia folded into tubes or sacs. Exocrine glands release their products through ducts onto a surface; endocrine glands have lost their ducts and release hormones into the blood. By the mode of release, glands are merocrine (exocytosis of vesicles, as in most glands), apocrine (part of the apical cytoplasm is shed, as in mammary glands) or holocrine (the whole cell disintegrates, as in sebaceous glands). Invertebrates have a great variety of epidermal glands: adhesive glands of flatworms and gastrotrichs, silk glands of spiders and caterpillars, the ink sac of cephalopods, the venom glands of cnidarian tentacles and centipede forcipules.

Sensory epithelia contain receptor cells among supporting cells: olfactory epithelium, taste buds, the retina (a derivative of the brain), and the hair-cell epithelia of the vertebrate inner ear and lateral line. Many invertebrate sense organs are likewise epithelial, for example the sensilla of insects, built of a few epidermal cells around a sensory neuron ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)).

## 2.4. Connective Tissue

**Definition 2.4.1 (Connective Tissue)** A **connective tissue** consists of relatively few cells dispersed in an abundant extracellular matrix of protein fibers and ground substance, which the cells secrete. It supports and binds other tissues, fills spaces between organs, stores energy and, in the form of blood, transports material.

The properties of a connective tissue are determined mostly by its matrix. The fibers are collagen (tensile strength), elastic fibers of elastin (recoil) and reticular fibers (fine collagen networks that support soft organs); the ground substance contains glycosaminoglycans and proteoglycans that bind water and resist compression. The resident cells are fibroblasts, which make and maintain the matrix, together with macrophages, mast cells and adipocytes, and wandering cells of the blood.

### 2.4.1. Types of Connective Tissue in Vertebrates

| Type | Matrix | Cells | Location and role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loose (areolar) | Loosely woven fibers in abundant ground substance | Fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells | Beneath epithelia, around vessels and nerves; packing tissue |
| Dense regular | Parallel collagen bundles | Rows of fibroblasts | Tendons, ligaments |
| Dense irregular | Collagen bundles in all directions | Fibroblasts | Dermis, capsules of organs |
| Adipose | Little matrix | Adipocytes | Energy store, insulation; brown fat produces heat ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)) |
| Cartilage | Firm gel of collagen and proteoglycans, no blood vessels | Chondrocytes in lacunae | Embryonic skeleton, joints, ear, larynx; the adult skeleton of sharks |
| Bone | Collagen mineralized with hydroxyapatite | Osteoblasts, osteocytes in lacunae, osteoclasts | Skeleton; calcium store ([7.14.36](/7.14.36)) |
| Blood and lymph | Fluid plasma | Red and white blood cells, platelets | Transport, defense ([7.14.11](/7.14.11)) |

Cartilage comes in three forms: hyaline cartilage, the most common, with fine collagen fibrils; elastic cartilage, with elastic fibers (external ear); and fibrocartilage, with dense collagen bundles (intervertebral discs). Bone is either compact, in which concentric lamellae surround central canals carrying blood vessels (the Haversian systems or osteons), or spongy, a lattice of trabeculae filled with marrow. The skeletal tissues and their development (**endochondral ossification** and **intramembranous ossification**, [7.5.24](/7.5.24)) are compared across the vertebrate classes in [7.17.55](/7.17.55) and [7.17.57](/7.17.57).

### 2.4.2. Connective Tissues of Invertebrates

Mineralized bone is unique to vertebrates, but invertebrates have connective tissues of their own.

**Definition 2.4.2 (Mesoglea)** The **mesoglea** is the gelatinous layer between the epidermis and the gastrodermis of cnidarians and ctenophores. It is an extracellular matrix of water, collagen fibers and proteoglycans, secreted by the two epithelia, and is either nearly acellular (in *Hydra* a thin sheet) or contains wandering cells and, in ctenophores and many medusae, muscle cells.

The mesoglea is not a germ layer, although it lies where the mesoderm lies in bilaterians: it arises as a secretion of ectoderm and endoderm, and the few cells that enter it migrate from these layers. In large jellyfish it makes up most of the body volume, which keeps the metabolic rate per unit volume low and the body nearly neutrally buoyant ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)). Sponges have a comparable layer, the mesohyl, which contains several types of wandering cells, collagen and skeletal spicules ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)). In acoelomate flatworms the space between gut and body wall is filled by parenchyma (mesenchyme), cells and matrix of mesodermal origin that store food and contain stem cells ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)).

Other invertebrate connective tissues include the cartilage of cephalopods, which supports the brain capsule and has chondrocytes with branched processes; the cartilage-like tissues of horseshoe crabs and some polychaetes; the mutable collagenous tissue of echinoderms, which can stiffen or soften within seconds under nervous control ([7.17.42](/7.17.42)); and the fat body of insects, which stores reserves and makes proteins of the hemolymph ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)). In animals with an **open circulatory system** the body fluid, hemolymph, combines the roles of blood and interstitial fluid.

## 2.5. Muscle Tissue

**Definition 2.5.1 (Muscle Tissue)** A **muscle tissue** consists of elongated cells (muscle fibers) specialized for contraction, in which filaments of actin and myosin slide past each other to shorten the cell and develop force; the cells are excitable and are controlled by nerves, hormones or their own pacemakers.

Muscle cells are classified by the arrangement of their contractile filaments. In striated muscle the myosin and actin filaments are aligned in sarcomeres in register across the cell, which produces the cross-striation; in smooth muscle they are not aligned; in obliquely striated muscle the sarcomeres are staggered so that the bands run helically around the fiber.

| Type | Structure | Occurrence | Physiology |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skeletal (striated) | Long multinucleate fibers formed by fusion of myoblasts; sarcomeres in register | Vertebrate skeletal muscle; arthropod muscles (also striated, but mononucleate or syncytial fibers in some) | [7.14.9](/7.14.9) |
| Cardiac | Branched, usually mononucleate striated cells joined by intercalated discs | Vertebrate heart | [7.14.10](/7.14.10) |
| Smooth | Spindle-shaped mononucleate cells without striation | Vertebrate viscera and vessels; many invertebrate muscles | [7.14.10](/7.14.10) |
| Obliquely striated | Staggered sarcomeres in helical bands | Body wall of nematodes, annelids, many molluscs | [7.14.10](/7.14.10) |
| Epitheliomuscular cells | Epithelial cells with contractile basal processes | Cnidarian epidermis and gastrodermis | [7.17.9](/7.17.9) |

Striated muscle has evolved more than once. Jellyfish swimming muscles are striated, but the genes that make their sarcomeres partly differ from those of bilaterian striated muscle, and comparative genomics indicates that striated muscle arose independently in cnidarians and bilaterians from a common contractile cell type (Steinmetz and colleagues, 2012). Ctenophore muscles are smooth and lie in the mesoglea. Sponges and placozoans have no muscle cells, although their cells contract with actin and myosin.

## 2.6. Nervous Tissue

**Definition 2.6.1 (Nervous Tissue)** A **nervous tissue** consists of neurons, cells specialized to receive stimuli and transmit signals rapidly to other cells through electrical impulses and chemical synapses, together with glial cells that support, nourish and insulate them.

A neuron has a cell body with the nucleus, usually several dendrites that receive signals, and an axon that conducts impulses away from the cell body; by the number of processes neurons are unipolar (pseudounipolar), bipolar or multipolar. Glial cells outnumber neurons in vertebrates and perform many tasks: in vertebrates oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells form the myelin sheath, astrocytes regulate the environment of synapses, and microglia defend the brain. The cellular basis of signaling is treated in [7.14.4](/7.14.4)–[7.14.5](/7.14.5) and [7.16.4](/7.16.4).

Nervous tissue is present in all animals except sponges and placozoans. In cnidarians the neurons form a **nerve net** in both epithelia; in ctenophores the subepithelial nerve net is a syncytium of fused neurons, one reason to think that their nervous system may have evolved separately ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.1)). Myelin evolved independently in vertebrates, annelids and copepod crustaceans; cephalopods and many arthropods achieve fast conduction instead with giant axons. The arrangement of nervous tissue into nerve nets, nerve cords and brains is described in [7.17.4](/7.17.4).

## 2.7. Organs and Organ Systems

An organ is a structure composed of several tissues that together perform a particular function. The small intestine of a vertebrate, for example, has an absorptive columnar epithelium, loose connective tissue with blood vessels and lymphatics, layers of smooth muscle and a nerve plexus; none of these tissues alone can digest and absorb food. Organs whose functions are linked form an organ system, such as the digestive system of mouth, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, intestine, liver and pancreas.

Organ systems are not independent. The coelom of annelids is at once a skeleton, a circulatory space and part of the excretory system; the vertebrate pharynx serves feeding in lancelets and respiration in fishes; the insect fat body combines the roles of liver and adipose tissue. Following such shifts of function through the phyla is one of the main tasks of comparative zoology.

## Summary

| Level | Characteristic | Lowest grade where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| Cell | Organelles; animal cells add junctions, collagenous matrix, flagellated sperm | Protists (protoplasmic grade) |
| Cell types | Division of labor among cells | Sponges (cellular grade) |
| Tissue | Cells of similar structure and their matrix, performing a common function | Cnidarians (tissue grade) |
| Organ | Several tissues performing a function | Flatworms (organ grade) |
| Organ system | Organs linked in one function | Most bilaterians |

- The four basic tissues are **epithelial tissue** (Definition 2.3.1; sheets of polarized cells on a basal lamina), **connective tissue** (Definition 2.4.1; few cells in an abundant matrix, including cartilage, bone and blood), **muscle tissue** (Definition 2.5.1; skeletal, cardiac, smooth and obliquely striated) and **nervous tissue** (Definition 2.6.1; neurons and glia).
- Invertebrate variants include the cuticle-secreting simple epidermis, syncytial teguments, the **mesoglea** of cnidarians and ctenophores (Definition 2.4.2), the mesohyl of sponges, the parenchyma of flatworms, cephalopod cartilage, echinoderm mutable collagenous tissue, and epitheliomuscular cells.
- Grades of organization are levels, not clades: striated muscle, myelin and perhaps nervous systems evolved more than once, and simple bodies may be secondarily simplified.
- Organ systems are compared across the vertebrate classes in 7.17.56–7.17.67 and their functions explained in 7.14.

## Questions

1. Why is mesoglea not counted as a third germ layer, although it lies between the epidermis and gastrodermis as mesoderm lies between ectoderm and endoderm?

   **Answer** A germ layer is a layer of embryonic cells set aside during gastrulation that gives rise to a defined set of tissues. Mesoglea is an extracellular matrix secreted by the two epithelia; the cells found in it are immigrants from those epithelia, not a separate population of embryonic cells. Mesoderm, in contrast, forms from its own cell lineage (for example from the 4d cell in spiralians) and gives rise to muscle, connective tissue and the coelomic lining.

2. Blood contains no fibers and its cells are not fixed. Why is it classified as a connective tissue?

   **Answer** By its structure and origin: blood consists of cells dispersed in an abundant extracellular matrix (plasma, whose fibrinogen polymerizes into fibers during clotting), its cells come from the same mesenchymal stem cell lineages that give rise to other connective tissues in the embryo, and it connects the other tissues functionally by transport. The defining features of connective tissue are few cells and much matrix, not a solid matrix.

3. An insect's flight muscle and a vertebrate's leg muscle are both striated. What evidence would decide whether striated muscle in the two is homologous?

   **Answer** Striation itself is a mechanical arrangement that can evolve repeatedly. The test is phylogenetic and molecular: map the character on the animal tree and compare the proteins that build the sarcomere. Arthropods and vertebrates are both bilaterians, and bilaterian striated muscles share a core set of sarcomeric proteins (such as the striated-muscle myosin heavy chain and titin-like proteins), which supports homology of the bilaterian striated muscle cell type, whereas in cnidarians several of these components are missing and others were recruited independently, which indicates convergence there.
