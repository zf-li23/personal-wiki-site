An insect lives inside a cuticle that is hard, waterproof and insensitive. Everything it learns about the world passes through small windows in that armour: hairs, pegs, pits, domes and thin membranes, each with one or a few sensory neurons beneath it. These cuticular sense organs, the sensilla, are among the most numerous structures on an insect's body, and their form tells a trained eye what they detect. Insects use them to sense touch, gravity, air currents, sound, substrate vibration, temperature, humidity, infrared radiation, taste, odours and light, and many species produce signals for the senses of others: songs, vibrations, pheromones and flashes of light.

This chapter describes the structure, types and evolution of insect sense organs and signalling organs. It builds on the anatomy of the integument and the antenna ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)), the head eyes ([7.17.23](/7.17.23#sec-23.3.4)) and the brain ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.6)), and on the compound eye of arthropods in general ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.7.2)). The cellular mechanisms of transduction are treated in [7.14](/7.14): mechanoreception in [7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.5), hearing and insect ears in [7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.4), vision and compound-eye optics in [7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.4), chemical senses in [7.14.43](/7.14.43); the neural computations of insect olfaction and motion vision in [7.16.23](/7.16.23#sec-23.4) and [7.16.17](/7.16.17#sec-17.6.3), the evolution of the insect odorant receptors in [7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.3.2), and the physical limits of eyes in [7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.6.6). Here we concentrate on the organs themselves, the physical constraints that shape them, and the signals they serve. Behaviour, its mechanisms and its evolution belong to 7.18.

## 27.1. The Sensillum

### 27.1.1. Structure and Development

**Definition 27.1.1 (Sensillum)** A **sensillum** (plural sensilla) is the elementary sense organ of insects and other arthropods: a unit of one or a few bipolar sensory neurons together with the epidermal cells that enclose them and usually a specialized cuticular part (a hair, peg, plate, pit or dome) through which the stimulus reaches the dendrites. In a typical cuticular sensillum three accessory cells surround the neuron: the innermost thecogen cell, which forms a sheath around the dendrite; the trichogen cell, which secretes the hair shaft or peg; and the tormogen cell, which secretes the socket.

The neurons of sensilla are primary sensory cells: each has a dendrite that receives the stimulus and an axon that runs without synapse to the central nervous system. In type I neurons the dendrite carries a modified cilium (with a basal body and a ciliary segment) at whose tip transduction occurs; all cuticular sensilla and chordotonal organs have type I neurons. Type II neurons are multipolar, have no cilium and no cuticular structure; they form stretch receptors in muscles, the gut and the body wall, and the nociceptive multidendritic neurons of larval epidermis.

The accessory cells are not passive. The tormogen and trichogen cells secrete the sensillum lymph that bathes the outer segment of the dendrite. Its potassium concentration is high and the space is electrically positive with respect to the hemolymph, so that the receptor current that flows when channels open in the dendrite is driven by both the potential and the concentration gradient: the epithelium acts as a battery that increases sensitivity ([7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.5)). In olfactory and gustatory sensilla the lymph also contains odorant-binding proteins (Section 27.4).

A sensillum develops from a single epidermal cell, the sensory organ precursor. In the *Drosophila* bristle it divides asymmetrically: one daughter gives the shaft and socket cells, the other the neuron and the sheath cell (and a glial cell that migrates away). Which daughter becomes which depends on the **Notch signaling pathway** and on the unequal inheritance of the Notch inhibitor Numb ([7.5.19](/7.5.19)). Because the four cells of a sensillum are sisters, adding, removing or modifying sensilla is a simple developmental change; this is one reason why sensilla are so diverse and why new sense organs, such as ears, have evolved repeatedly from existing ones.

### 27.1.2. Morphological Types

Entomologists classify sensilla by the form of their cuticular part. The classical names (Schenk 1903; Snodgrass 1935) are still used in descriptive work:

| Type | Cuticular part | Typical function | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trichoid (sensillum trichodeum) | Long hair in a flexible socket, or a long thin-walled hair | Touch, air movement; pheromone reception (thin-walled) | Body hairs, cercal filiform hairs, moth antennae |
| Chaetic (sensillum chaeticum) | Stout bristle | Touch, contact chemoreception | Legs, mouthparts |
| Basiconic (sensillum basiconicum) | Short peg | Olfaction | Antennae of most insects |
| Coeloconic (sensillum coeloconicum) | Peg in a shallow pit | Olfaction (acids, amines), temperature and humidity | Antennae |
| Ampullaceous (sensillum ampullaceum) | Peg at the bottom of a deep flask-shaped pit | Carbon dioxide, humidity, temperature | Antennae of ants and bees |
| Placoid (sensillum placodeum) | Thin plate flush with the cuticle | Olfaction | Pore plates of bee and aphid antennae |
| Styloconic (sensillum styloconicum) | Peg on a cuticular style | Taste | Maxillary galea of caterpillars |
| Campaniform (sensillum campaniformium) | Thin dome or cap in the cuticle, no projection | Strain in the cuticle | Leg joints, wing bases, halteres |
| Scolopophorous (chordotonal) | None: subcuticular | Stretch, vibration, sound | Joints, Johnston's organ, ears |

The form matters less for physiology than the structure of the wall, which determines how a stimulus reaches the dendrite. Three classes are distinguished by their pores:

| Class | Wall | Neurons | Modality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aporous | No pores | One mechanosensory neuron ending at the base of the hair; or two or three neurons in an inflexible peg | Mechanoreception; temperature and humidity |
| Uniporous | A single pore at the tip | Usually two to five gustatory neurons plus one mechanosensory neuron | Contact chemoreception (taste) |
| Multiporous | Many pores in a thin wall (single-walled), or slits between fused fingers (double-walled) | One to many olfactory neurons with branched dendrites | Olfaction |

## 27.2. Mechanoreception

### 27.2.1. Hairs and Air Currents

The simplest mechanoreceptors are trichoid sensilla with a single neuron whose dendrite ends at the base of the hair. When the hair is deflected in its socket, the dendrite tip is compressed and the neuron fires; many hairs respond only to movement (phasic receptors), some also to maintained deflection (tonic receptors). Hairs are directional: the socket and the attachment of the dendrite usually allow movement in one plane, and the neuron responds to deflection in one direction within it.

Among the most sensitive mechanoreceptors known are the filiform hairs on the cerci of crickets and cockroaches. A cricket cercus carries several hundred hairs, from about 30 µm to 1.5 mm long, arranged in rows of different preferred directions. The air displaced by an approaching predator deflects them; their neurons project onto giant interneurons of the terminal abdominal ganglion that trigger an escape run within a few tens of milliseconds, and the pattern of active hairs encodes the direction of the air flow. Similar hairs on caterpillars respond to the wingbeat of approaching parasitoid wasps.

**Example 27.2.1** Why are filiform hairs so long, and why are they of different lengths? Air oscillating with angular frequency $\omega$ over a surface is slowed in a viscous boundary layer whose thickness is

$$
\delta = \sqrt{\frac{2\nu}{\omega}},
\tag{27.1}
$$
where $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity, $1.5 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ for air. At $f = 100\,\mathrm{Hz}$, $\omega = 628\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\delta = \sqrt{3.0\times10^{-5}/628} \approx 0.22\,\mathrm{mm}$; at $10\,\mathrm{Hz}$, $\delta \approx 0.7\,\mathrm{mm}$. A hair that is much shorter than $\delta$ sits in nearly still air and is hardly moved, so a hair must protrude through the boundary layer to sense low-frequency air movements. Long hairs have more inertia and drag and resonate at lower frequencies, short hairs at higher ones; an array of hairs of graded length therefore samples a range of frequencies, and in the most sensitive hairs the mechanical work needed to make the neuron fire is close to the energy of thermal noise, $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ (Shimozawa and colleagues 2003).

### 27.2.2. Proprioceptors

Insects measure the positions of their own body parts with three kinds of receptors (proprioceptors):

- *hair plates*, fields of short hairs at joints (the neck, the coxae, the base of the antennae), which are bent by the adjacent segment as the joint flexes; they signal joint angle and are part of the reflexes that hold the head level and control walking;
- *campaniform sensilla*, thin domes of cuticle with a single neuron, which respond to strain in the cuticle rather than to its movement; they lie in groups where cuticle bends under load, on the legs near the joints, at the wing bases and on the halteres of flies, where several fields of them measure the Coriolis forces on the beating haltere ([7.17.24](/7.17.24), [7.17.25](/7.17.25));
- *stretch receptors* with type II multipolar neurons, and chordotonal organs spanning joints, such as the femoral chordotonal organ, which measures the angle and movement of the femur–tibia joint.

### 27.2.3. Chordotonal Organs

**Definition 27.2.1 (Chordotonal Organ)** A **chordotonal organ** is a subcuticular mechanoreceptor of insects and crustaceans composed of one to many thousands of scolopidia stretched between two points of the body wall or between the body wall and an internal structure. Each scolopidium consists of one to three type I sensory neurons whose ciliated dendrite is enclosed by a scolopale cell, a sheath stiffened by actin-filled rods (scolopales), and attached at its tip to a cap (attachment) cell; a ligament or glial cell anchors the base. Stretching or vibration of the organ is transmitted along the cilium, where it opens mechanosensitive channels.

Chordotonal organs have no external cuticular structure and can therefore be placed anywhere in the body where the cuticle or an internal membrane moves. The mechanotransduction channels of the cilium include members of the TRP family (NompC and the TRPV heteromer Nanchung–Inactive); in *Drosophila* the same genes are needed for hearing, gravity sensing and proprioception. Chordotonal organs are the source of most insect hearing organs:

- **Johnston's organ** in the antennal pedicel ([7.17.23](/7.17.23#def-23.4.2)) measures movements of the flagellum. In *Drosophila* it contains about 480 neurons in groups that detect gravity and wind (slow, maintained deflections) and the near-field courtship song of the male (oscillations of a few hundred hertz). In male mosquitoes and chironomid midges the pedicel is swollen and holds about 15,000 neurons; the plumose flagellum resonates at the frequency of the female wingbeat, and the male hears her flight tone at a distance of several centimetres. The neurons of the mosquito ear are active amplifiers: they move the flagellum themselves and tune it, like the outer hair cells of the vertebrate cochlea ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.3.4)).
- The *subgenual organ*, a fan of scolopidia in the proximal tibia of each leg, attached to the tracheal wall and the cuticle, detects vibrations of the substrate with thresholds of a nanometre or less. It is present in most orders but absent in beetles and flies.

### 27.2.4. Tympanal Organs

**Definition 27.2.2 (Tympanal Organ)** A **tympanal organ** (insect ear) is a hearing organ consisting of a thin, cuticular tympanal membrane (tympanum) backed by an air-filled tracheal sac, and a chordotonal organ attached to the inner side of the membrane or to the sac; sound pressure moves the membrane, whose vibration stimulates the scolopidia. Tympanal organs detect sound pressure in the far field, at distances of many wavelengths from the source.

Tympanal ears are the clearest example of convergent evolution among insect sense organs. They have arisen independently at least 20 times in at least seven orders, and in almost every part of the body:

| Location | Taxa |
|---|---|
| Fore tibia | Crickets and bush crickets (Orthoptera: Ensifera) |
| First abdominal segment | Grasshoppers and locusts (Orthoptera: Caelifera); cicadas (Hemiptera); geometrid and pyraloid moths; tiger beetles |
| Metathorax | Noctuoid moths (noctuids, erebids including tiger moths, notodontids) |
| Ventral midline between the metathoracic legs | Mantises (a single "cyclopean" ear) |
| Base of the forewing (radial vein) | Green lacewings (Chrysopidae); nymphalid butterflies (Vogel's organ) |
| Prosternum | Parasitoid flies of the tribe Ormiini (Tachinidae) and some flesh flies |
| Cervical membrane | Some scarab beetles |

Neuroanatomy indicates that each ear evolved from a chordotonal organ that already existed at that position as a proprioceptor or vibration receptor: in moths, the ear is innervated by the same nerve branch that innervates a stretch receptor in earless relatives; in the bush cricket, the crista acustica in the fore tibia lies beside a subgenual organ that is also present, without a tympanum, in the mid and hind legs. The transition requires only that a thin patch of cuticle overlies an air space, so that the cuticle can vibrate freely; the tracheal system supplies the air space everywhere in the body.

Three ears show the range of designs:

- Bush crickets (Tettigoniidae) have two tympana on each fore tibia, just below the knee. Sound reaches their inner face through a horn-shaped acoustic trachea that opens at a large spiracle on the prothorax; the trachea amplifies the sound as a horn does. A row of up to 60 scolopidia, the crista acustica, lies on the trachea and is tonotopically organized, the distal receptors responding to higher frequencies, from about 5 kHz to over 50 kHz.
- Locusts (Acrididae) have a tympanum on each side of the first abdominal segment, with Müller's organ, a cluster of about 70–80 scolopidia, attached to its inner face at several sclerotized points. Different groups of receptors respond to different frequencies because travelling waves on the membrane peak at different places for different frequencies, a mechanical frequency analysis comparable to that of the basilar membrane.
- Noctuoid moths have an ear on each side of the metathorax with only two auditory receptor neurons, A1 and A2 (and a non-auditory B cell). Both respond broadly to ultrasound; A2 has a threshold about 20 dB higher than A1. The moth ear does not discriminate frequencies; it is a bat detector.

Small insects face a problem in localizing sound: their two ears are so close that the differences in arrival time and intensity are tiny. Many solve it with pressure-difference receivers, in which sound reaches each tympanum from both sides through internal air passages, so that the membrane responds to the difference of pressures and is strongly directional. The most remarkable solution, the mechanically coupled ears of the fly *Ormia ochracea*, is analysed in [7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.4).

### 27.2.5. Bats and Moths

Insectivorous bats hunt flying insects at night by **echolocation** ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#def-42.6.2)) with calls of about 20–200 kHz. The ears of moths, lacewings, mantises, beetles and several orthopterans are most sensitive in this range, and hearing insects respond to bat calls with a two-stage defence: faint calls (detected by the sensitive A1 cell in moths) make the moth turn away, and loud calls of an approaching bat (also exciting A2) trigger erratic loops, dives or a free fall. Field experiments by Roeder in the 1960s showed that moths with intact ears escape attacks considerably more often than deafened moths.

Bats and insects have since been locked in an evolutionary arms race. Tiger moths (Erebidae: Arctiinae) answer bat calls with ultrasonic clicks made by metathoracic tymbals (Section 27.6); in chemically protected species the clicks are acoustic warning signals (**aposematism**), in others they mimic those of protected species, and in *Bertholdia trigona* they jam the bat's sonar at close range (Corcoran and colleagues 2009). Some hawkmoths produce ultrasound by rubbing scales on their genitalia, and the long hindwing tails of luna moths deflect bat attacks away from the body. On the other side, the barbastelle bat (*Barbastella barbastellus*) hunts eared moths with calls 10–100 times weaker than those of other bats, so that moths hear it only when it is a few metres away (Goerlitz and colleagues 2010).

Why can a moth hear a bat before the bat detects the moth? The answer is in the geometry of sound propagation.

**Example 27.2.2** A bat emits a call of source level $L_{\mathrm{s}}$ (in decibels sound pressure level at the reference distance $r_0 = 0.1\,\mathrm{m}$). Over a distance $r$ the sound loses $20\log_{10}(r/r_0)$ decibels by spherical spreading and $\alpha r$ by atmospheric absorption, which is strong for ultrasound ($\alpha \approx 1\,\mathrm{dB\,m^{-1}}$ at 40 kHz). The moth hears the call if the level arriving at it exceeds its threshold $T_{\mathrm{m}}$, and the bat hears the echo if the echo, which travels the path twice and is reduced by the target strength $S_{\mathrm{t}}$ of the moth (the echo level at $r_0$ relative to the incident level), exceeds the bat's threshold $T_{\mathrm{b}}$:

$$
L_{\mathrm{s}} - 20\log_{10}\frac{r}{r_0} - \alpha r \ge T_{\mathrm{m}}, \qquad
L_{\mathrm{s}} - 2\left(20\log_{10}\frac{r}{r_0} + \alpha r\right) + S_{\mathrm{t}} \ge T_{\mathrm{b}}.
\tag{27.2}
$$
With $L_{\mathrm{s}} = 120\,\mathrm{dB}$, $\alpha = 1\,\mathrm{dB\,m^{-1}}$, $T_{\mathrm{m}} = 60\,\mathrm{dB}$, $S_{\mathrm{t}} = -20\,\mathrm{dB}$ and $T_{\mathrm{b}} = 20\,\mathrm{dB}$, the first inequality holds up to $r \approx 16\,\mathrm{m}$ and the second up to $r \approx 5\,\mathrm{m}$. Because the echo suffers the losses twice, the prey's warning range is several times the predator's detection range for any loud call. A bat that calls 26 dB more quietly ($L_{\mathrm{s}} = 94\,\mathrm{dB}$) is heard only within about 3.4 m and detects the moth within about 1.8 m: whispering costs the bat detection range but removes most of the moth's warning time.

![The sonar equation for a bat and an eared moth (Eq. (27.2); illustrative parameters: source level 120 dB SPL at 10 cm, absorption 1 dB per metre at about 40 kHz, moth hearing threshold 60 dB, target strength −20 dB, bat echo threshold 20 dB). A: the level of the bat call arriving at the moth (solid) and of the echo returning to the bat (dashed) against distance; the moth hears the bat out to about 16 m, the bat detects the moth only within about 5 m. B: the same for a "whispering" bat with a source level of 94 dB: the moth's warning range shrinks to about 3.4 m, close to the bat's own detection range of about 1.8 m.](./plots/bat-moth.py)

The model is crude (calls are directional, thresholds depend on frequency, and many bats use calls louder than 120 dB), but its conclusion is robust and agrees with field measurements: moths detect most bats at tens of metres, bats detect moths at a few metres.

## 27.3. Temperature, Humidity and Infrared

Temperature and humidity are sensed by small aporous pegs, usually sunk in coeloconic pits on the antennae, each containing a characteristic triad of neurons: a cold cell, which increases its firing when temperature falls, a moist cell and a dry cell, which respond in opposite directions to changes in humidity. In *Drosophila*, humidity detection requires ionotropic receptors (IR25a, IR93a with IR40a or IR68a) in such sensilla. The antennae are the main site of temperature sensing in most insects studied, with additional receptors on the tarsi (in the cockroach on the arolium and pulvilli) and probably central temperature sensors in the brain and thoracic ganglia. The molecular thermosensors, including TRP channels, are discussed in [7.14.40](/7.14.40#sec-40.6.2).

Blood-feeding insects are drawn to the heat of their hosts. Mosquitoes combine carbon dioxide (detected by ampullaceous or basiconic sensilla on the maxillary palps), skin odours and heat, and triatomine bugs (*Rhodnius*, *Triatoma*) find a warm host by its radiant heat with sensilla on the antennae.

The most specialized heat sense is that of the jewel beetles of the genus *Melanophila* (Buprestidae), whose larvae develop in freshly burnt wood. Each beetle has a pair of pit organs beside the mesothoracic coxae, each containing about 50–100 dome-shaped sensilla. An infrared photon of 3–4 µm wavelength (the emission maximum of a forest fire) is absorbed by a fluid-filled cuticular sphere inside the dome, which expands minutely and stimulates a mechanoreceptor: a photomechanical infrared receptor. Beetles arrive at forest fires from distances of kilometres.

## 27.4. Chemoreception and Chemical Communication

### 27.4.1. Taste

Contact chemoreceptors (gustatory sensilla) are uniporous hairs or pegs on the mouthparts, the tarsi, the antennae, the ovipositor and, in flies, the wing margins. Each usually contains a few taste neurons with different response spectra, for example a sugar cell, a bitter (deterrent) cell, a water cell and a salt cell, and one mechanosensory neuron that signals when the hair touches a surface. Because the dendrites end below a single pore at the tip, a recording electrode slipped over the hair tip can both deliver a stimulus solution and record the neurons' impulses (the tip-recording method, Hodgson and colleagues 1955), which made the insect taste hair the first taste receptor whose single cells could be studied.

Tarsal taste explains striking behaviour. A hungry blow fly or butterfly that steps into sugar solution extends its proboscis automatically, and a female butterfly "drums" a leaf with her foretarsi, whose sensilla taste the host plant's compounds, before she lays eggs. The gustatory receptors (Gr) of insects, like their odorant receptors, are ligand-gated ion channels unrelated to the taste receptors of vertebrates.

### 27.4.2. Olfaction

Olfactory sensilla are multiporous: odour molecules adsorb onto the cuticle, diffuse into pores 10–50 nm wide, pass through pore tubules into the sensillum lymph and are carried by odorant-binding proteins to the receptors on the dendrites. The neurons express **odorant receptors**, which in insects form heteromeric ion channels with the coreceptor Orco ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.3.2)), or ionotropic receptors (mainly in coeloconic sensilla), and each neuron expresses one or a few receptors. All neurons expressing the same receptor converge on one glomerulus of the antennal lobe ([7.16.23](/7.16.23#sec-23.4.2)).

The antenna is designed as a filter for capturing molecules from air. The pectinate antennae of male silk moths are an open sieve with a large surface: each antenna of a male *Bombyx mori* carries about 17,000 long trichoid sensilla, each with two neurons, one tuned to the main component of the female sex pheromone, bombykol, the other to its aldehyde, bombykal. The sensilla are long and thin and spaced so that a substantial fraction of the pheromone molecules in the air passing through the antenna are caught; a single bombykol molecule can elicit a nerve impulse, and a few hundred molecules arriving within a second can elicit a behavioural response.

### 27.4.3. Pheromones and Other Semiochemicals

Chemicals that carry information between organisms are called semiochemicals. Those acting within a species are **pheromones** ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#def-43.3.1)); those acting between species are allelochemicals, classified by who benefits.

| Category | Acts between | Benefit | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sex pheromone | Members of a species | Mate attraction, courtship | Bombykol of the silk moth; most moth females; male scents of butterflies |
| Aggregation pheromone | Members of a species | Joint use of a resource, mass attack, mating | Bark beetles; stored-grain beetles; 4-vinylanisole of the migratory locust |
| Spacing (epideictic) pheromone | Members of a species | Avoidance of crowding | Oviposition-deterring pheromone of tephritid fruit flies |
| Trail pheromone | Members of a species | Recruitment to food, orientation | Ants, termites |
| Alarm pheromone | Members of a species | Dispersal or defence | Aphids ((*E*)-β-farnesene), honey bee sting pheromone, ants |
| Primer pheromone | Members of a species | Long-term physiological change | Queen mandibular pheromone of the honey bee |
| Allomone | Different species | Emitter | Defensive secretions; orchid flowers imitating a female bee's sex pheromone |
| **Kairomone** ([7.5.38](/7.5.38#def-38.8.3)) | Different species | Receiver | Host odours used by herbivores; pheromones of prey exploited by predators and parasitoids |
| Synomone | Different species | Both | Volatiles of damaged plants that attract the parasitoids of the herbivores |

Pheromones are usually distinguished as releasers, which trigger immediate behaviour, and primers, which change physiology or development over hours to days. The first insect pheromone to be identified was bombykol, (10*E*,12*Z*)-hexadecadien-1-ol, isolated by Butenandt and colleagues in 1959 from the glands of about half a million female silk moths. Since then the sex pheromones of several thousand species have been identified, most of them unsaturated fatty acid derivatives (alcohols, acetates, aldehydes of 10–18 carbon atoms) synthesized from ordinary fatty acids by a few desaturases and chain-shortening enzymes in a gland between abdominal segments 8 and 9 of the female.

Species specificity usually comes not from unique compounds but from blends: the same compound occurs in many species, in different ratios and combinations. The European corn borer, *Ostrinia nubilalis*, has two strains that use the same two isomers of 11-tetradecenyl acetate in nearly opposite ratios, about 97:3 (*Z*:*E*) in one and about 1:99 in the other, and males of each strain respond mainly to their own blend. The Asian corn borer, *O. furnacalis*, a major maize pest in China, uses instead the 12-tetradecenyl acetates, a shift caused by the activation of a different desaturase gene present in the genome of the genus (Roelofs and colleagues 2002). Small changes in the biosynthetic enzymes thus change the signal, and changes in the receptors of the male track them; the species specificity of pheromones is a major premating isolating barrier ([7.10.11](/7.10.11)).

The practical importance of insect pheromones is large. Synthetic sex pheromones are used to monitor pest populations with traps, to remove males by mass trapping, and to disrupt mating by saturating the air of a crop so that males cannot find females ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)).

### 27.4.4. The Active Space of a Pheromone

How far a pheromone signal reaches depends on the emission rate, the sensitivity of the receiver and the movement of the air. Bossert and Wilson (1963) defined the active space of a pheromone as the region in which its concentration exceeds the behavioural threshold $K$ of the receiver, and showed that its size and duration depend mainly on the ratio of the emitted amount to the threshold.

**Example 27.4.1 (Bossert–Wilson model)** A puff of $M$ molecules released instantaneously at a point in still air spreads by diffusion with diffusion coefficient $D$:

$$
C(r, t) = \frac{M}{(4\pi D t)^{3/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{r^2}{4Dt}\right).
\tag{27.3}
$$
The active space is the sphere in which $C \ge K$. Its radius grows, reaches a maximum and shrinks to zero; setting $C(0, t) = K$ gives its lifetime,

$$
t_{\mathrm{fade}} = \frac{1}{4\pi D}\left(\frac{M}{K}\right)^{2/3},
\qquad
r_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2\pi \mathrm{e}}}\left(\frac{M}{K}\right)^{1/3} \approx 0.42\left(\frac{M}{K}\right)^{1/3}.
\tag{27.4}
$$
For $M/K = 10^4\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ and $D = 0.07\,\mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ (a molecule of the size of a pheromone in air), $r_{\max} \approx 9\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $t_{\mathrm{fade}} \approx 9\,\mathrm{min}$ in perfectly still air, much less in moving air. Because $r_{\max}$ grows only as the cube root of $M/K$, a thousandfold increase in emission enlarges the signal only tenfold: a small, short-lived active space requires a low $M/K$ and suits an alarm signal that should stay local and stop soon after the danger. For a continuous source in wind of speed $u$, the time-averaged plume is a narrow cone downwind of the source whose length increases as the square root of the emission rate divided by $uK$. A female moth emitting continuously and a male with a threshold of a few hundred molecules per cubic centimetre produce a plume that can be followed over hundreds of metres.

The time-averaged plume is a fiction for the receiver: turbulence breaks a plume into filaments, and a male moth flying upwind meets brief bursts of high concentration separated by clean air. Males respond to each burst by surging upwind and to its loss by casting across the wind, a strategy that works because the olfactory receptor neurons of moths can follow pulses at several per second. The neural mechanisms of this behaviour belong to 7.18.

## 27.5. Vision

### 27.5.1. Photoreceptors and Eyes

All insect photoreceptors are rhabdomeric: the visual pigment, an **opsin** bound to a retinal chromophore, lies in a brush of microvilli (the rhabdomere) along one side of the cell, and phototransduction works through Gq and phospholipase C ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#def-53.4.2), [7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.2)). The rhabdomeres of several neighbouring cells together form a rhabdom. Insects have four kinds of light sensors:

- *dermal and internal photoreceptors* without optics: light-sensitive neurons in the brain of aphids and moths measure day length for the photoperiodic control of reproduction and diapause ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)), and swallowtail butterflies have photoreceptors on the genitalia that are used in copulation and oviposition;
- **stemmata**, the lateral eyes of holometabolous larvae;
- **ocelli**, the dorsal simple eyes of adults and nymphs;
- **compound eyes** of adults and nymphs.

Stemmata and ocelli are both called simple eyes but are not homologous to each other: stemmata are larval versions of the compound eyes (they arise in the same region of the head and their neurons project to the optic lobes), whereas the ocelli are separate median eyes with their own nerves.

### 27.5.2. Stemmata and Ocelli

Caterpillars have usually six stemmata on each side of the head, each a single lens over a small group of receptors, so each sees one broad patch of the world; by swinging its head the caterpillar scans its surroundings. Some predatory larvae have stemmata that form true images: the larva of a tiger beetle, waiting in its burrow, has two large stemmata on each side with an extended retina that judges the size and distance of prey. In *Drosophila* the larval eye (Bolwig's organ, about 12 receptors) is remodelled at metamorphosis into a small group of photoreceptors behind the compound eye of the adult, used in setting the circadian clock.

Most adult insects with good flight have three ocelli in a triangle on the vertex, one median and two lateral ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)). The lens of each ocellus focuses light behind the retina, so the image is blurred, and many receptors (about 675 in the median ocellus of the dragonfly *Sympetrum*) converge onto a few large second-order neurons. Ocelli are therefore very sensitive and fast but poor at resolution: they act as horizon detectors that signal the pitch and roll of the body in flight. The statement in many textbooks that ocelli "only detect light and cannot form images" is broadly correct for most insects, but the ocelli of dragonflies resolve the horizon as a line, and those of some night-flying bees and wasps are large.

### 27.5.3. Ommatidia and the Two Optical Designs

The compound eye consists of ommatidia ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#def-18.7.1)), from a few in some ant workers and about 750 in *Drosophila* to about 5,500 in a honey bee worker and nearly 30,000 in large dragonflies. Each ommatidium has a corneal facet lens secreted by two corneagen cells, a crystalline cone formed by four cone (Semper) cells, eight (sometimes seven or nine) retinula cells whose rhabdomeres form the rhabdom, and two primary and several secondary pigment cells. The cone varies among orders: a hard intracellular crystalline cone (eucone eyes of most insects), an extracellular liquid-filled pseudocone (flies), no cone at all, the cone cells being clear (acone eyes of many bugs and some beetles), or a cone formed by the cornea (exocone eyes of fireflies and click beetles).

Two optical principles are realized (Exner 1891).

**Definition 27.5.1 (Apposition Eye)** An **apposition eye** is a compound eye in which each ommatidium is optically isolated by screening pigment, and the rhabdom lies directly below the crystalline cone, so that each rhabdom receives light only through its own facet from a small angle of the visual field; the image is a mosaic of as many points as there are ommatidia.

**Definition 27.5.2 (Superposition Eye)** A **superposition eye** is a compound eye in which a wide, unpigmented clear zone separates the cones from a deep retina, and the crystalline cones act as graded-index lenses that redirect light so that rays entering many facets are focused onto a single rhabdom; the resulting erect image is much brighter than in an apposition eye of the same size.

Apposition eyes are typical of day-active insects: bees, flies, butterflies, dragonflies, most bugs and grasshoppers. Superposition eyes are typical of nocturnal and crepuscular insects, most moths, many beetles (especially scarabs), caddisflies and lacewings. In many superposition eyes pigment migrates into the clear zone in bright light, isolating the ommatidia and turning the eye functionally into an apposition eye; in darkness it withdraws. A reflecting tapetum of tracheae behind the retina in moths reflects unabsorbed light back through the rhabdoms; it produces the eyeshine of moths in a torch beam.

Flies have a third arrangement. In their open rhabdom the rhabdomeres of the eight cells are separate: six peripheral ones (R1–R6) and two central ones stacked one above the other (R7 above R8). Because the rhabdomeres lie at different positions in the focal plane of the facet lens, the six peripheral cells of one ommatidium look in six different directions, and each direction is also viewed by one cell in each of five neighbouring ommatidia. The axons of the six cells that look in the same direction converge on the same cartridge of the lamina. This neural superposition (Kirschfeld 1967) gives six times the light signal of a single receptor without loss of resolution, optically an apposition eye with superposition in the wiring.

### 27.5.4. Resolution and Sensitivity

Two angles describe the spatial vision of a compound eye: the interommatidial angle $\Delta\phi = D/R$, the angle between the axes of neighbouring ommatidia (facet diameter $D$, local radius of curvature $R$), which sets the sampling density; and the acceptance angle $\Delta\rho$ of a single ommatidium, which sets the blur. Diffraction at the facet makes $\Delta\rho$ at least about $\lambda/D$, so smaller facets sample more finely but blur more; this trade-off sets an optimum facet size and explains why a compound eye with the resolution of the human eye would have a radius of tens of metres ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#eq-18.12)). Typical interommatidial angles are about 5° in *Drosophila*, 1°–2° in the honey bee and as little as 0.25° in the forward-looking acute zone of large dragonflies.

The product $p = D\,\Delta\phi$ (in micrometres, $\Delta\phi$ in radians), the eye parameter (Snyder 1977), measures the compromise. In bright light, resolution is limited by diffraction and the best design has $p$ close to half the wavelength, about 0.3 µm, as in bees and day-flying butterflies. In dim light, photon noise limits vision, and it pays to give up resolution for sensitivity: crepuscular insects have $p$ of 1–3 µm, with larger facets or wider rhabdoms. Many eyes are not uniform: acute zones with large facets and small $\Delta\phi$ point in the direction where resolution matters most, forward and upward in male flies and bees that chase females against the sky.

Sensitivity can be calculated from the optics. Land's formula gives the number of photons absorbed by one photoreceptor per second per unit of luminance:

$$
S = \left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^2 A^2 \left(\frac{d}{f}\right)^2 \left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-kl}\right),
\tag{27.5}
$$
where $A$ is the diameter of the aperture through which light reaches the receptor, $d$ the diameter of the rhabdom, $f$ the focal length, $l$ the rhabdom length and $k \approx 0.0067\,\mathrm{\mu m^{-1}}$ its absorption coefficient; $S$ has units of µm² sr.

**Example 27.5.1** For an apposition eye with $A = D = 20\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, $d = 2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, $f = 60\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and $l = 320\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ (values of the order found in honey bees), Eq. (27.5) gives $S \approx 0.62 \times 400 \times (1/30)^2 \times 0.88 \approx 0.24\,\mathrm{\mu m^2\,sr}$. For a superposition eye whose aperture is the pupil of many facets, $A = 300\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, with $d = 8\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, $f = 200\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and $l = 200\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, $S \approx 0.62 \times 9 \times 10^4 \times (0.04)^2 \times 0.74 \approx 66\,\mathrm{\mu m^2\,sr}$, nearly 300 times more. A luminance of $1\,\mathrm{cd\,m^{-2}}$ corresponds to about $4 \times 10^{3}$ photons $\mathrm{s^{-1}\,sr^{-1}\,\mu m^{-2}}$ (at 555 nm), so in an integration time of 20 ms the apposition receptor absorbs $N \approx 0.24 \times 4\times10^3 \times 0.02 \approx 20$ photons at $1\,\mathrm{cd\,m^{-2}}$ (dusk), the superposition receptor about 5,000. The signal-to-noise ratio of photon counting is $\sqrt N$, so at starlight ($10^{-3}\,\mathrm{cd\,m^{-2}}$) the apposition receptor, absorbing 0.02 photons per integration time, can no longer see contrasts, whereas the superposition receptor still absorbs a few photons.

Nocturnal insects with apposition eyes compensate in the ways the example suggests: the sweat bee *Megalopta genalis*, which forages in the rain forest of Panama in twilight, has large facets and wide rhabdoms, slow photoreceptors (longer integration time) and neurons in the lamina that sum the signals of neighbouring ommatidia, trading resolution for sensitivity (Warrant and colleagues 2004).

### 27.5.5. Colour, Ultraviolet and Polarization

Most insects have at least three spectral classes of photoreceptors, typically sensitive to ultraviolet, blue and green light; the honey bee's peak at about 344, 436 and 544 nm (Peitsch and colleagues 1992). Bees see ultraviolet but not red: flowers that look uniformly yellow to us often have ultraviolet patterns (nectar guides) visible to bees, and red flowers pollinated by bees are rare. Butterflies have added receptor classes by duplicating opsin genes and by filtering pigments: the Japanese swallowtail *Papilio xuthus* has six classes and uses at least four in colour vision, and the bluebottle *Graphium sarpedon* has fifteen (Chen and colleagues 2016). Red receptors have evolved several times, in butterflies, some dragonflies and a few beetles.

Because the microvilli of each rhabdomere are aligned and the pigment molecules lie along them, insect photoreceptors are inherently sensitive to the plane of polarized light. In most of the eye this is suppressed by twisting the rhabdom, but in a narrow dorsal rim area the rhabdoms are straight, the microvilli of different cells perpendicular, and the receptors analyse the polarization pattern of the sky. This pattern depends on the position of the sun and serves bees, ants and locusts as a compass even when the sun is hidden; the central complex of the brain integrates it ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.6)). Aquatic insects use horizontally polarized light reflected from water surfaces to find water, which is why they sometimes land on shiny car roofs and asphalt. The dung beetle *Scarabaeus zambesianus* orients by the polarization pattern around the moon.

### 27.5.6. Time and Motion

Compound eyes are fast. The photoreceptors of flies follow flicker above 200 Hz, three to four times faster than human cones, partly because the microvillar transduction machinery is fast and partly because flying insects can afford the metabolic cost of fast receptors. Their visual systems are specialized for motion: the optic lobes compute local motion by comparing signals from neighbouring ommatidia with a delay, the operation described by the **Reichardt detector** ([7.16.17](/7.16.17#sec-17.6.3)), and large tangential neurons of the lobula plate combine local motion into the patterns of optic flow produced by rotation and translation of the body. Distance is judged from motion parallax rather than from binocular disparity, since the two eyes are close and coarse; praying mantises, which strike at prey from a stationary perch, are the one insect group shown to use stereopsis (Nityananda and colleagues 2016).

## 27.6. Producing Sound and Vibration

### 27.6.1. Stridulation

Most insect sounds are made by stridulation, in which a row of teeth, ridges or pegs (the file) is rubbed against a sharp edge (the scraper or plectrum). Each tooth strike produces a brief vibration; to radiate sound efficiently the vibration must drive a larger resonant surface.

The field cricket shows the principle in its purest form. Its two tegmina overlap, the right over the left; on the underside of the right tegmen a thickened vein carries a file of about 100–250 teeth, and the edge of the left tegmen forms the scraper. Sound is produced on the closing stroke, as the scraper runs along the file. A triangular cell of the tegmen, the harp, vibrates at its resonant frequency (about 4–5 kHz in *Gryllus*) and radiates the sound. The harp in turn controls the rate at which teeth engage, as the escapement of a clock controls its gear: each oscillation of the harp releases the next tooth, so that the tooth-strike rate equals the resonant frequency and the song is an almost pure tone (Elliott and Koch 1985).

**Example 27.6.1** A field cricket sings at a carrier frequency of 4.5 kHz, and each sound pulse lasts 18 ms. If each tooth strike drives one cycle of the harp, the scraper strikes $4500 \times 0.018 \approx 80$ teeth per pulse, a little over half of a typical file. Chirps of three or four pulses repeated a few times per second give the species-specific temporal pattern, which females use to recognize males of their species.

Bush crickets (Tettigoniidae) stridulate in the same way but with the left tegmen over the right, the file on the left and a membranous "mirror" resonator on the right; many tropical species sing in ultrasound, up to about 150 kHz. Grasshoppers (Acrididae: Gomphocerinae) rub a row of pegs on the inner face of the hind femur against a raised vein of the tegmen, producing broadband rasping sounds. Stridulatory organs of other kinds occur in beetles (elytra against abdomen, head against pronotum), ants (on the gaster), bugs, butterfly pupae and many aquatic insects; in the water bug *Micronecta scholtzi* (Micronectidae) the male rubs a ridge on its genitalia against the abdomen, producing at about 2 mm body length one of the loudest sounds relative to body size known.

A small source radiates sound poorly. For a source of radius $a$ that is small compared with the wavelength $\lambda$ (that is, $ka = 2\pi a/\lambda < 1$), the radiated power at a given vibration velocity increases as $(ka)^2$ if only one face radiates (a monopole) and as $(ka)^4$ if both faces vibrate in opposite phase (a dipole), as a free wing does: the pressure produced by one face is cancelled by the other. A cricket tegmen of radius about 5 mm singing at 4.5 kHz ($\lambda = 7.6\,\mathrm{cm}$) has $ka \approx 0.4$, so it radiates as a leaky dipole. Insects improve radiation by baffles: male tree crickets (*Oecanthus*) chew a hole in a leaf and sing with their wings in the hole, so that the leaf separates the two faces; mole crickets sing from the bulb of a burrow shaped as a double exponential horn that couples the small source to the air (Bennet-Clark 1970).

### 27.6.2. Tymbals

Cicadas (Hemiptera: Cicadidae) produce the loudest insect sounds with tymbals, a pair of ribbed cuticular membranes on the sides of the first abdominal segment of males. A large tymbal muscle pulls the tymbal inward; the ribs buckle in sequence, each producing a click, and the tymbal springs back when the muscle relaxes. The clicks excite a large abdominal air sac that resonates near the song frequency, usually 4–7 kHz, and the tympana, which lie below the tymbals, act as outlets of the resonator. Some species exceed 100 dB at 50 cm; in China the black cicada *Cryptotympana atrata* is the loudest common species. Tymbals are widespread in the other Auchenorrhyncha (leafhoppers, planthoppers), but these smaller insects lack the resonant air sac and transmit their signals as vibrations through the plant. Tiger moths have tymbals on the metathorax (Section 27.2.5).

### 27.6.3. Substrate Vibration and Flight Tones

Airborne sound is the exception. Most insects that communicate mechanically do so by vibrations of the substrate, plants, soil or water surface, which travel well at low frequencies that small insects can produce efficiently (Cocroft and Rodríguez 2005). Leafhoppers, planthoppers and stink bugs court with duets of plant-borne vibrations; the brown planthopper *Nilaparvata lugens* is a well-studied example. Termite soldiers drum their heads against the gallery walls in alarm, water striders send ripples across the surface film, and lacewings court by tremulating the abdomen.

Flight itself produces sound at the wingbeat frequency. The flight tones of mosquitoes are species- and sex-specific: in *Aedes aegypti* females beat their wings at about 400 Hz and males at about 600 Hz, and during courtship the partners adjust their wingbeats until a harmonic of each, near 1,200 Hz, matches (Cator and colleagues 2009). The males detect the female's tone with Johnston's organ (Section 27.2.3). *Drosophila* males court with a "song" produced by vibrating one wing, consisting of pulses at species-specific intervals and of sine song; the intervals are one of the cues by which females recognize males of their own species.

## 27.7. Bioluminescence

Light production, **bioluminescence**, has evolved in a few insect groups: in beetles (fireflies, Lampyridae; click beetles, Elateridae; railroad worms, Phengodidae), in larvae of fungus gnats (*Arachnocampa*, Keroplatidae) that glow in caves and on stream banks, and in a few springtails.

The light organs of fireflies lie on the ventral side of the last abdominal segments (segments 6 and 7 in males of many species). They consist of a ventral layer of photocytes, richly supplied with tracheae whose tracheoles end between the cells, and a dorsal reflector layer of cells filled with urate crystals. In the photocytes the enzyme luciferase oxidizes the substrate luciferin with oxygen and ATP; the excited oxyluciferin emits yellow-green light of 550–570 nm. The reaction is one of the most efficient light sources known, with a quantum yield of about 0.4 (Ando and colleagues 2008); its chemistry is treated in [7.1.16](/7.1.16). Flashes are switched by nerve impulses: octopamine released by the nerves stimulates the production of nitric oxide, which inhibits the respiration of the mitochondria that lie between the tracheoles and the peroxisomes containing luciferase, so that oxygen reaches the luciferase and a flash is produced (Trimmer and colleagues 2001).

Fireflies use their flashes in courtship. Males of North American *Photinus* fly at dusk emitting species-specific patterns of flash duration, number and interval; a female in the vegetation answers with a flash after a species-specific delay, and the male approaches by a dialogue of flashes (Lloyd 1966). Females of the genus *Photuris* exploit this code: they answer the flashes of *Photinus* males, lure them and eat them, acquiring with them the defensive steroids (lucibufagins) that they cannot make themselves. In Southeast Asia, males of *Pteroptyx* gather in trees by the thousands and flash in synchrony, a natural example of coupled oscillators.

Phylogenetic studies indicate that bioluminescence in the firefly lineage began in larvae, where glowing is a warning signal of distastefulness (**aposematism**), and was later retained in adults and co-opted for courtship. Firefly luciferase belongs to the family of fatty acyl-CoA synthetases and arose by gene duplication; the genomes of fireflies and click beetles suggest that their luciferases were recruited independently (Fallon and colleagues 2018), so that bioluminescence evolved at least twice within the elateroid beetles.

## 27.8. Comparing the Channels of Communication

Insects signal through every physical channel available to them, and each channel has properties that suit some messages and not others. The comparison below summarizes the physical constraints discussed in this chapter.

| Channel | Range | Speed and duration | Localization by receiver | Main constraints | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chemical (airborne) | Centimetres (alarm) to hundreds of metres downwind (sex attractants) | Slow; persists for seconds to hours | Upwind flight along the plume; poor in still air | Wind direction; cannot be switched off quickly | Mate attraction, aggregation, alarm |
| Chemical (deposited) | Contact to metres along a trail | Persists for minutes to days | Following the trail | Must be renewed | Trails, territory, oviposition marking, recognition of nestmates |
| Airborne sound | Metres to over 100 m (cicadas, bush crickets) | Fast; switched on and off instantly | Good for large insects, difficult for small ones (Section 27.2.4) | Small radiators are inefficient at low frequencies; high frequencies are absorbed; eavesdropping by predators and parasitoids | Calling songs, courtship, aggression, anti-bat signals |
| Substrate vibration | Centimetres to a few metres within one plant | Fast | Moderate, by comparing arrival at different legs | Attenuation and dispersion depend on the plant | Courtship duets of leafhoppers, planthoppers and bugs, alarm in termite colonies |
| Near-field sound and air movement | A few body lengths | Fast | Good at close range | Falls off steeply with distance | Courtship songs of *Drosophila*, mosquito flight tones |
| Visual (reflected light) | Metres to tens of metres in daylight | Fast | Good | Needs ambient light and line of sight; conspicuous to predators | Wing displays, ultraviolet patterns of butterflies, species recognition |
| Bioluminescence | Metres to tens of metres at night | Fast, coded in time | Good | Only at night or in darkness; costly | Firefly flash codes, warning glow of larvae |

Two physical regularities explain much of the table. The intensity of a signal spreading in three dimensions falls with the square of distance, 6 dB for each doubling, whatever the channel, and on top of this sound loses energy by absorption roughly in proportion to the square of its frequency, so that the ultrasonic songs of small bush crickets and the clicks of tiger moths carry only a few metres (Example 27.2.2). A chemical signal, by contrast, travels with the air, and its range depends on the wind and on the ratio of emission rate to threshold (Example 27.4.1) rather than on the power of the sender. Small insects can therefore signal over long distances only chemically; acoustic long-distance signals are the domain of the larger crickets, bush crickets and cicadas, which can produce loud sounds at low frequencies.

Every public signal can be exploited by an unintended receiver. Parasitoid flies of the tribe Ormiini locate singing crickets by ear, and in Hawaii, where the fly was introduced, a population of the field cricket *Teleogryllus oceanicus* became largely silent within some 20 generations through a mutation that removes the file from the male tegmen (Zuk and colleagues 2006). Predatory *Photuris* fireflies mimic the answers of *Photinus* females (Section 27.7), bats home in on the songs of bush crickets, and many parasitoids and predators find their hosts by the hosts' pheromones, which act on them as kairomones (Section 27.4.3). The design of signals thus reflects a compromise between reaching the intended receiver and escaping the unintended ones; the evolutionary theory of signalling belongs to 7.18.

## Questions

1. Why do nearly all insect sense organs except the eyes have the same basic cellular composition? What does this imply for their evolution?

   **Answer** All cuticular sensilla and chordotonal organs develop from a single sensory organ precursor cell that produces, by a fixed sequence of asymmetric divisions, the neuron(s), the sheath cell and the cells that secrete the hair and socket. Differences between sensilla lie in how these few cells are shaped (the form of the cuticular part, pores, attachment of the dendrite) and in which receptor proteins the neuron expresses. New sense organs can therefore arise by modifying an existing module rather than building a new structure: ears from proprioceptive chordotonal organs, thermosensors from pegs. This explains the repeated, convergent origins of similar organs at different places on the body.

2. (Calculation) Using Eq. (27.1), estimate the thickness of the boundary layer at 300 Hz in air and in water ($\nu = 1.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$). What does the result imply for the length of hairs that detect water movements in aquatic insects?

   **Answer** In air $\delta = \sqrt{2 \times 1.5\times10^{-5}/(2\pi\times300)} = \sqrt{1.6\times10^{-8}} \approx 0.13\,\mathrm{mm}$; in water $\delta = \sqrt{2\times10^{-6}/1885} \approx 33\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Because water is much less viscous in the kinematic sense, its boundary layer is about four times thinner, and much shorter hairs suffice to reach moving fluid; on the other hand water is 800 times denser, so the forces on a hair are larger. Aquatic insects detect water movements with short, stiff hairs or with the antennae.

3. (Calculation) In the model of Example 27.2.2, how much quieter must a bat call be for the moth's warning range to equal the bat's detection range?

   **Answer** Let $g(r) = 20\log_{10}(r/r_0) + \alpha r$. The ranges are equal at the distance $r^*$ where $L_{\mathrm{s}} - g = T_{\mathrm{m}}$ and $L_{\mathrm{s}} - 2g + S_{\mathrm{t}} = T_{\mathrm{b}}$ hold simultaneously. Subtracting gives $g(r^*) = T_{\mathrm{m}} + S_{\mathrm{t}} - T_{\mathrm{b}} = 60 - 20 - 20 = 20\,\mathrm{dB}$, so $r^* \approx 0.9\,\mathrm{m}$ (since $20\log_{10}9 \approx 19$ and $\alpha r \approx 0.9$). Then $L_{\mathrm{s}} = T_{\mathrm{m}} + g = 80\,\mathrm{dB}$, 40 dB below the loud call. Below this source level the bat would detect the moth before being heard, but only at less than a metre, which leaves it little time to intercept; real whispering bats sit near this balance.

4. Why does the size of a pheromone's active space depend so weakly on the amount released, and why is this appropriate for alarm pheromones?

   **Answer** In three-dimensional diffusion the peak concentration at distance $r$ after a release of $M$ molecules falls as $M/r^3$, so the radius within which a threshold $K$ is exceeded grows only as $(M/K)^{1/3}$ (Eq. (27.4)). An alarm signal should alert nearby nestmates and fade quickly once the danger is past, so that the colony does not stay alarmed; a modest $M/K$ gives a small active space that fades within seconds to minutes. Sex attractants, in contrast, are emitted continuously into the wind, where advection carries them far, and receivers with very low thresholds extend the plume.

5. Compare apposition and superposition eyes with respect to resolution and sensitivity, and explain why flies achieve high sensitivity without giving up apposition optics.

   **Answer** In an apposition eye each rhabdom receives light only through its own small facet, which gives a sharp image limited by facet diffraction and sampling but low sensitivity, because the aperture is a single facet. In a superposition eye many facets focus light on one rhabdom, so the aperture is many facets wide and sensitivity is hundreds of times higher, at the cost of some blur from imperfect superposition. Flies have open rhabdoms: the six peripheral rhabdomeres of one ommatidium look in different directions, and the six receptors in different ommatidia that share a direction are pooled in one lamina cartridge. The signal is six times larger while each point is still sampled through small facets, so resolution is unchanged.

6. A student claims that insects "cannot see red". Evaluate the claim.

   **Answer** It is true of many insects, including honey bees, which have ultraviolet, blue and green receptors only; a red flower appears dark or, if it reflects ultraviolet, ultraviolet-coloured to a bee. But red-sensitive receptors have evolved repeatedly, in many butterflies (by additional long-wavelength opsins or red filtering pigments), some dragonflies and some beetles, and these insects can discriminate red. Colour vision depends on the set of receptor classes and their comparison in the brain, which differs among groups, so the statement must be restricted to particular taxa.

7. Why did bioluminescence evolve first as a warning signal in larvae rather than as a mating signal in adults, according to the phylogenetic evidence? What kind of evidence supports this inference?

   **Answer** Ancestral-state reconstruction on the phylogeny of fireflies and their relatives places luminescence in larvae at the base of the group, with adult light organs arising within it; luminous larvae of many species are distasteful, and predators learn to avoid glowing prey. A warning signal is advantageous even without a specialized organ or a code, whereas a mating code requires coordinated evolution of signal and receiver; the existing light production of the larvae could later be retained in the adult and elaborated into a species-specific courtship code. The evidence is comparative: the distribution of larval and adult luminescence on a phylogeny, the toxicity of luminous larvae and experiments showing that predators avoid glowing prey.
