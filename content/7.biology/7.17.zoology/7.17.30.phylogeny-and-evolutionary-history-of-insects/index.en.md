Insects have a history of more than 400 million years, longer than that of any other group of land animals except the arachnids and myriapods. They were the first animals to fly, by some 100 million years, and they already formed complex communities of herbivores, predators and parasites in the coal forests of the Carboniferous. Most of the living orders had appeared by the Triassic, and the fauna of the Cretaceous ambers would look largely familiar to a modern entomologist. This chapter reconstructs that history from two kinds of evidence, the fossil record and the phylogeny of living insects, and asks what they say about the causes of insect diversity.

Section 30.1 surveys the fossil record from the Devonian to the Cenozoic. Section 30.2 describes the phylogeny of the hexapods as it is understood from phylogenomic studies and defines the major clades of winged insects, which the chapters on the orders ([7.17.31](/7.17.31)–[7.17.36](/7.17.36)) take as their framework; Section 30.3 relates the modern system to the traditional classifications. Section 30.4 discusses the key innovations proposed to explain insect diversity and the methods used to test them. The diagnostic characters of hexapods and insects are in [7.17.22](/7.17.22), the origin of wings in [7.17.25](/7.17.25#sec-25.1), the giant Palaeozoic insects in [7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.4.3) and the evolution of metamorphosis in [7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.5). The methods of phylogenetic inference, the **molecular clock** and diversification analysis belong to [7.10](/7.10) ([7.10.20](/7.10.20)–[7.10.26](/7.10.26), [7.10.31](/7.10.31)), and the general history of life to [7.10.29](/7.10.29).

## 30.1. The Fossil Record

### 30.1.1. How Insects Fossilize

Insects have no mineralized skeleton, and their **fossil record** depends on exceptional circumstances. Most fossil insects are compressions or impressions in fine-grained sediments of lakes, lagoons and estuaries, where bodies that fell or were washed into still, oxygen-poor water were buried before they decayed. The wings are the toughest part and are often the only part preserved, which is why fossil insects are so often classified by their venation ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)). Exceptional preservation of whole bodies occurs in siderite nodules (Mazon Creek, Illinois, Late Carboniferous), in fine lake deposits such as those of Daohugou and Jiulongshan in Inner Mongolia and Hebei (Middle to Late Jurassic, about 165–160 Ma) and of the Jehol Biota in western Liaoning (Early Cretaceous, about 125 Ma), and in fossil resin.

Amber, fossilized tree resin, preserves insects in three dimensions, often with microscopic detail of setae, sensilla and even muscles. The major amber deposits span the last 130 million years: Lebanese amber (Early Cretaceous, about 130 Ma), the very rich Kachin amber of northern Myanmar (about 99 Ma), Baltic amber (Eocene, about 44 Ma) and Dominican amber (Miocene, about 16–20 Ma). Amber samples insects that lived on or near resin-producing trees and is biased towards small species. The trade in Kachin amber has been linked to armed conflict in Myanmar, and since 2020 several scientific societies and journals have stopped accepting studies of amber mined there after 2017.

Despite these limits, the insect fossil record is rich at the level of families: about 1,260 families are known as fossils, more than for tetrapod vertebrates. Because insect families are long-lived, their first and last appearances can be used to estimate rates of origination and extinction through time (Labandeira and Sepkoski 1993), and family-level diversity has risen almost continuously since the Carboniferous, interrupted by only one major crisis, at the end of the Permian.

### 30.1.2. The Devonian: First Hexapods and the Hexapod Gap

The oldest certain hexapods are springtails of the Early Devonian Rhynie chert of Scotland, about 410 million years old (*Rhyniella praecursor*), preserved with plants and arachnids in silica deposited by hot springs. The same deposit yielded *Rhyniognatha hirsti*, known only from a pair of mandibles, which was described as a dicondylic, possibly winged insect; a later study interpreted its mandibles as those of a centipede (Haug and Haug 2017), and its identity remains disputed. *Strudiella devonica* from the Late Devonian of Belgium (about 370 Ma) has been interpreted as a wingless insect nymph, but its preservation is poor.

After the Devonian there is a gap of about 60 million years, from the Middle Devonian to the mid-Carboniferous, with almost no insect fossils, the "hexapod gap". Molecular clocks, calibrated with other fossils, place the origin of insects in the Early Ordovician (about 480 Ma) and that of the winged insects in the Early Devonian (about 405 Ma) (Misof and colleagues 2014). If these dates are correct, insects diversified on land in a period from which almost no fossils are known, and the gap is a gap in preservation rather than in insects ([7.10.24](/7.10.24)).

### 30.1.3. The Carboniferous: the First Fliers

Winged insects appear abruptly in the Serpukhovian stage of the Early Carboniferous, about 325 million years ago; one of the oldest is *Delitzschala bitterfeldensis* from Germany, a palaeodictyopteran. By the Late Carboniferous (about 315–300 Ma) the fauna was diverse and included groups unlike any living order:

| Group | Characters | Fate |
|---|---|---|
| Palaeodictyopterida (Palaeodictyoptera, Megasecoptera, Diaphanopterodea) | Piercing-sucking beak for feeding on plant sap, spores and ovules; prothoracic winglets; wings held outstretched; nymphs with articulated wing pads | Diverse until the Permian, extinct at the end-Permian extinction |
| Meganisoptera (griffinflies) | Relatives of dragonflies, predators; *Meganeura monyi* from Commentry, France, with a wingspan of about 65 cm, and *Meganeuropsis permiana* (Permian of Kansas) of about 70 cm | Extinct at the end of the Permian |
| Stem-group mayflies (for example Syntonopteridae) | Large, with equal fore- and hindwings | The ancestors of living mayflies |
| "Roachoids" (stem-group Dictyoptera) | Cockroach-like, with a long external ovipositor | Abundant: the Carboniferous is sometimes called the "age of cockroaches" |
| Archaeorthoptera | Early relatives of grasshoppers and crickets | Gave rise to Orthoptera |

The Carboniferous record already shows insects feeding on plants in all major ways: piercing and sucking by palaeodictyopterans, chewing of leaves, boring in wood, and eating spores, documented by damage on fossil plants and by spores in the guts of insects. The oldest larvae of holometabolous insects are also Carboniferous (*Metabolarva bella*, about 311 Ma; [7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.5)). The large size of some Carboniferous and Permian insects, and the hypothesis that it was permitted by high atmospheric oxygen, are discussed in [7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.4.3).

The Palaeodictyopterida deserve a closer look, because they were the first large group of herbivorous insects and have no living descendants. Their head bore a long beak of five stylets, homologous to the mandibles, maxillae and hypopharynx, with which they pierced the cones and stems of seed ferns and cordaites to suck sap or to reach the developing seeds; their prothorax carried a pair of small, veined winglets, and their nymphs had curved wing pads that grew at each molt, as in the hemimetabolous insects of today. Some species had wingspans of over 50 cm. They were so diverse that they made up about half of the Carboniferous insect fauna known from some deposits, yet the whole group disappeared at the end of the Permian, and with it a mode of feeding on seed plants that was not taken up again until the true bugs evolved.

### 30.1.4. The Permian and the End-Permian Crisis

The Permian (299–252 Ma) saw the radiation of the Holometabola and the Paraneoptera. Early relatives of beetles (Coleopterida, with the Tshekardocoleidae from about 295 Ma onward), scorpionflies, lacewings, caddisfly relatives and the first Hemiptera are known from the Early Permian, and by the Late Permian holometabolans made up a substantial part of the fauna. Several Permian deposits, such as Elmo in Kansas and Chekarda in the Urals, have yielded thousands of specimens.

At the end of the Permian, about 252 million years ago, the largest **mass extinction** in the history of life ([7.10.31](/7.10.31#sec-31.4.1)) eliminated about eight insect orders, among them the Palaeodictyopterida and the griffinflies, and a large fraction of families. It is the only mass extinction that clearly affected insects at the level of orders. The Triassic fauna that followed was dominated by lineages that belong to, or are close to, the living orders.

### 30.1.5. The Mesozoic: Origin of the Modern Orders

Most living orders are known by the Triassic or Jurassic. The first true flies (Diptera) appear in the Middle Triassic (about 240 Ma) and the first sawflies of the family Xyelidae, which still lives today, in the Late Triassic; scales of moths from the Triassic–Jurassic boundary (about 201 Ma) document Lepidoptera earlier than any body fossil (van Eldijk and colleagues 2018). Many modern families appear in the Jurassic.

Long before flowering plants existed, insects were feeding on the pollination drops and pollen of gymnosperms. Middle Jurassic scorpionflies from Daohugou and related deposits of China had long, siphonate proboscides, which they probably used to take fluids from the reproductive organs of seed plants (Ren and colleagues 2009), and similar long-proboscid insects (kalligrammatid lacewings with eyespots on the wings, nectar-feeding flies) are known from the Jurassic and Early Cretaceous. Insect pollination is therefore older than the **angiosperms** and was probably one of the conditions for their success ([7.12](/7.12)).

The Cretaceous saw the rise of flowering plants (from about 130 Ma), of social insects and of many herbivorous lineages that are now species-rich. The oldest termites are Early Cretaceous; the oldest ants are known from Kachin amber (about 99 Ma), in which several extinct "hell ants" (Haidomyrmecinae) with bizarre vertically moving mandibles occur alongside workers of crown-group ants; the oldest bees are of similar age. The diversification of ants, of the leaf beetles and weevils, of butterflies and of many flies and wasps accelerated in the Late Cretaceous and the Paleogene, in part alongside the angiosperms (Section 30.4.3).

Not every Mesozoic insect belongs to a living order. Several extinct orders and suborders flourished in the Triassic and Jurassic and then disappeared, and their structures show how varied the early experiments were:

| Extinct group | Age | Characters |
|---|---|---|
| Titanoptera | Triassic | Giant relatives of Orthoptera with wingspans of up to about 35 cm, raptorial forelegs and large stridulatory areas in the forewings |
| Chresmodidae | Jurassic–Cretaceous | Long-legged, possibly water-surface skating polyneopterans |
| Kalligrammatidae | Jurassic–Cretaceous | "Butterflies of the Jurassic": large lacewings with eyespots on scaly wings and long siphonate mouthparts, convergent with butterflies |
| Permopsocida | Permian–Cretaceous | Paraneopterans with an elongate head and piercing mouthparts; in Cretaceous amber with pollen in the gut |
| Tarachoptera | Cretaceous | Small amphiesmenopterans in amber, related to the common ancestor of caddisflies and moths |

The kalligrammatids are a striking example of **convergent evolution**: lacewings, not related to butterflies, evolved the same combination of scaled wings, eyespots and nectar-feeding mouthparts about 50 million years before the first butterflies, and perhaps visited the reproductive organs of Bennettitales as butterflies now visit flowers (Labandeira and colleagues 2016).

Amber adds behaviour to morphology. Specimens in Kachin amber include ants of extinct subfamilies with vertically closing, scythe-like mandibles, sometimes preserved holding their prey; termites of several castes trapped together; ticks entangled in the feathers of dinosaurs; beetles with pollen of cycads attached to their bodies; and lacewing larvae carrying debris on their backs as camouflage, a behaviour of modern green lacewing larvae. Such "frozen behaviour" shows that many ecological roles of modern insects were already established in the mid-Cretaceous.

### 30.1.6. The Cenozoic

The end-Cretaceous extinction (66 Ma), which eliminated the non-avian dinosaurs, had a modest effect on insect families, though fossil leaves from North America show a sharp decline in the diversity of insect feeding damage across the boundary, followed by recovery over several million years. The Eocene ambers and lake deposits (Baltic amber, Messel in Germany, the Green River Formation and Florissant in the United States) contain a fauna of essentially modern families, many of them with living genera; Dominican amber of the Miocene contains a largely modern fauna of the West Indies, including stingless bees and ants of living genera. During the warm interval at the Paleocene–Eocene boundary (about 56 Ma), the frequency and diversity of insect damage on fossil leaves increased, an indication that warming increases herbivory (Currano and colleagues 2008).

### 30.1.7. Fossil Insects of China

China has become one of the richest sources of fossil insects, and a large share of the new Mesozoic taxa described in recent decades comes from Chinese deposits. Four are especially important:

| Deposit | Age | Setting and fauna |
|---|---|---|
| Xiaheyan, Ningxia | Late Carboniferous (Pennsylvanian), about 315 Ma | Compression fossils in shales of a coastal plain: palaeodictyopterans, griffinflies, early orthopteroids and roachoids, often with colour patterns of the wings preserved; one of the richest Carboniferous insect faunas outside Europe and North America |
| Tongchuan, Shaanxi | Middle–Late Triassic, about 237 Ma | Lake deposits of the Ordos Basin, documenting the recovery after the end-Permian extinction, with early Mesozoic beetles, cockroaches, hemipterans and the first representatives of several modern families |
| Daohugou and the Jiulongshan Formation, Inner Mongolia, Hebei and Liaoning | Middle to Late Jurassic, about 165–160 Ma | Volcanic lake sediments with a fauna of well over a hundred families: long-proboscid scorpionflies, kalligrammatid lacewings, early fleas of giant size, cicadas, beetles, sawflies and some of the oldest crown-group families of many orders; also feathered dinosaurs, salamanders and early mammals |
| Yixian Formation (Jehol Biota), western Liaoning | Early Cretaceous, about 125 Ma | Lake shales with a fauna dominated by aquatic insects (mayfly nymphs of the genus *Ephemeropsis*, water beetles) and with early angiosperms (*Archaefructus*); feathered dinosaurs and early birds |

The Jurassic and Cretaceous "giant fleas" of China, up to 2 cm long, with long serrated piercing mouthparts, are interpreted as ectoparasites of feathered dinosaurs or early mammals; they show that the flea lineage had become parasitic by the Middle Jurassic, although their exact relationship to modern fleas is debated. The Eocene amber of Fushun in Liaoning (about 50 Ma), together with the Cambay amber of India, provides a window on the Paleogene insects of Asia comparable to the Baltic amber of Europe.

### 30.1.8. How Old Is a Lineage? Confidence Limits from the Fossil Record

The oldest fossil of a lineage is almost never its first member. How far back should its origin be extended? If fossil finds of a lineage are distributed randomly and uniformly through its true time range, a simple statistical argument gives a confidence interval for the true time of origin.

**Example 30.1.1 (Strauss–Sadler Confidence Interval)** Suppose that a lineage is known from $H$ fossil horizons spanning an observed range of $R$ million years, and that horizons are a uniform random sample of the true range. The probability that the observed range falls short of the true range by more than a fraction $x$ at one end is $(1 + x)^{-(H-1)}$. Setting it equal to $1 - C$ gives the length $r_C$ by which the range must be extended at that end to obtain a confidence level $C$ (Strauss and Sadler 1989):

$$
r_C = R\left[(1 - C)^{-1/(H-1)} - 1\right].
\tag{30.1}
$$
For an order known from $H = 10$ horizons over $R = 100$ million years, the 95% limit extends the range by $100\,[0.05^{-1/9} - 1] \approx 39.5$ million years; with $H = 30$ horizons the extension shrinks to $100\,[0.05^{-1/29} - 1] \approx 10.9$ million years. For the winged insects, which are absent from the 60-million-year hexapod gap and are known from many horizons only after 325 Ma, the interval reaches well into the Devonian, consistent with the molecular estimate of about 405 Ma.

The assumption of uniform preservation is rarely true: the hexapod gap shows that preservation potential can drop to almost nothing for tens of millions of years, and the earliest members of a lineage may have lived in habitats with no chance of fossilization. Formal methods that combine fossil occurrences with the branching process, such as the **fossilized birth–death process** ([7.10.24](/7.10.24)), relax these assumptions, but the qualitative conclusion is the same: the oldest fossils are minimum ages, and the true origins of most insect orders lie tens of millions of years earlier.

## 30.2. The Phylogeny of Hexapods

### 30.2.1. Evidence

The modern framework of insect phylogeny was established by morphological cladistics, notably by Hennig (1969) and Kristensen (1975, 1991), who recognized most of the groups used today on the basis of the head, the mouthparts, the wing base and development. Molecular phylogenetics, first with ribosomal RNA and then with growing sets of nuclear genes, confirmed many of them and overturned others, most strikingly by placing the Hymenoptera as the sister group of all other holometabolans, the Strepsiptera with the beetles, the termites within the cockroaches and the lice within the bark lice. Some problems remained intractable because of **long-branch attraction** and compositional heterogeneity ([7.10.21](/7.10.21)), for example the position of the fast-evolving Strepsiptera, which early molecular studies placed with the flies.

The landmark phylogenomic study of Misof and colleagues (2014) analysed 1,478 single-copy nuclear protein-coding genes from the transcriptomes of 144 species, representing all orders, and estimated divergence times with a relaxed clock calibrated by 37 fossils. Its tree has been the basis of later classifications, including the one used in this course (Brusca, Giribet and Moore 2023), and later studies focused on particular groups (Polyneoptera: Wipfler and colleagues 2019; Paraneoptera: Johnson and colleagues 2018; Hymenoptera: Peters and colleagues 2017; Coleoptera: McKenna and colleagues 2019; Lepidoptera: Kawahara and colleagues 2019) have confirmed most of its results and refined the rest. The main points of agreement and disagreement are as follows.

The history of the Strepsiptera illustrates both the difficulty and the progress. These small endoparasites, in which males have club-like forewings and fan-shaped hindwings and females are larviform and never leave their host, combine characters of beetles (hindwing flight, the first-instar triungulin larva of many beetles) with highly derived traits found nowhere else. Early analyses of ribosomal RNA placed them as the sister group of flies, the "Halteria" hypothesis, because both have a pair of wings modified into balancing organs; but strepsipteran genes evolve fast, and the grouping with flies was a typical artefact of long-branch attraction. Analyses of whole genomes with models that account for rate and compositional variation placed them firmly as the sister group of beetles (Niehuis and colleagues 2012), in agreement with morphology: in flies it is the hindwing that becomes the haltere, in Strepsiptera the forewing, so the "halteres" of the two groups are not even positionally homologous.

Two general lessons follow. First, molecular data are not free of artefacts, and the most reliable conclusions are those supported by different kinds of evidence, including rare genomic changes such as gene rearrangements and intron positions. Second, the conflict between data sets is concentrated at a few nodes, the relationships of the Palaeoptera, the position of the Psocodea and the internal relationships of the Polyneoptera, where lineages diverged within short intervals long ago; most of the tree is now stable.

### 30.2.2. Hexapoda, Insecta and Dicondylia

The base of the hexapod tree is described in [7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.3): the three entognathan orders (springtails, proturans, diplurans), whose monophyly is uncertain, and the insects, within which the jumping bristletails (Archaeognatha), with monocondylic mandibles, are the sister group of the Dicondylia. Within the Dicondylia the silverfish (Zygentoma) are the sister group of the winged insects; the relict *Tricholepidion gertschi* of California, often placed in its own family, belongs to the silverfish lineage as its earliest branch. The hexapods as a whole are nested within the crustaceans as part of the **Pancrustacea** ([7.17.18](/7.17.18)), probably as the sister group of the remipedes.

The relationships of the three entognathan orders are one of the most persistent problems at the base of the tree. Morphology has suggested three groupings: Ellipura (springtails and proturans, which share a reduced abdomen and a particular structure of the mouthparts), Nonoculata (proturans and diplurans, both blind), and Cercophora (diplurans and insects, which share cerci and some features of the head). Molecular analyses have favoured each in turn. If the diplurans are the sister group of the insects, entognathy evolved twice or was lost in insects, and the Entognatha are paraphyletic; the uncertainty is the reason why this course treats the entognathans as an informal group ([7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.2)). In all hypotheses the earliest hexapods were small, soft-bodied inhabitants of soil and litter, a way of life that the entognathans have kept for over 400 million years.

### 30.2.3. Pterygota

**Definition 30.2.1 (Pterygota)** The **Pterygota** (winged insects) are the clade of insects whose adults primitively bear two pairs of articulated wings on the mesothorax and metathorax; other synapomorphies are the absence of molts after the wings become functional (except for the subimago of mayflies) and the loss of the abdominal styli in adults.

Wings evolved once, and all wingless members of the Pterygota (lice, fleas, worker ants, female scale insects) are secondarily wingless ([7.17.25](/7.17.25#sec-25.4.2)). Within the Pterygota the first question is the relationship of the two palaeopterous orders.

**Definition 30.2.2 (Palaeoptera and Neoptera)** The **Neoptera** are the clade of winged insects that can fold their wings back over the abdomen at rest, by means of a third axillary sclerite and an associated muscle at the wing base ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)); they comprise all living winged insects except the mayflies and the dragonflies. The **Palaeoptera** are the mayflies (Ephemeroptera) and the dragonflies and damselflies (Odonata), which cannot fold their wings; whether they form a clade is unresolved.

The inability to fold the wings is ancestral and cannot by itself show that the two orders form a clade. Three hypotheses have been defended, each with some morphological and molecular support:

| Hypothesis | Grouping | Supporting characters |
|---|---|---|
| Palaeoptera | (Ephemeroptera + Odonata) + Neoptera | Similarities of the wing base and venation (intercalary veins between the main veins); short, bristle-like adult antennae; the analysis of Misof and colleagues (2014), with modest support |
| Metapterygota | Ephemeroptera + (Odonata + Neoptera) | Loss of the subimago; a fixed anterior articulation of the mandible (Staniczek 2000); loss of the median caudal filament; some molecular analyses |
| Chiastomyaria | Odonata + (Ephemeroptera + Neoptera) | Direct sperm transfer by copulation in mayflies and Neoptera, whereas the secondary copulatory apparatus of dragonflies is unique; crossed thoracic muscles; some molecular analyses |

The problem persists because the three lineages diverged within a short interval in the Devonian, about 400 million years ago, leaving few characters on the internal branches, and because each lineage has since evolved for a long time on its own. The figure below shows the three hypotheses; this course, following Brusca, Giribet and Moore (2023), treats the relationship as unresolved.

### 30.2.4. Polyneoptera

**Definition 30.2.3 (Polyneoptera)** The **Polyneoptera** are the clade of neopteran insects comprising ten orders: Plecoptera, Dermaptera, Zoraptera, Orthoptera, Phasmatodea, Embioptera, Grylloblattodea, Mantophasmatodea, Mantodea and Blattodea; they are hemimetabolous, mostly with chewing mouthparts, many-segmented cerci and an enlarged, fan-folded anal area of the hindwing, and share tarsal adhesive pads (euplantulae) and characters of the wing base.

The Polyneoptera include some of the oldest and the most recently discovered of the insect orders: the Mantophasmatodea (heel-walkers) were described only in 2002, from museum specimens from Africa and a fossil in Baltic amber, and living populations were found in Namibia the same year. Their monophyly was long doubted because their shared characters were thought to be ancestral, but phylogenomic studies support it strongly (Wipfler and colleagues 2019). Within the group the cockroaches, termites and mantises form the Dictyoptera, the stick insects and webspinners the Eukinolabia, and the ice crawlers and heel-walkers the Xenonomia; the details and the position of termites are treated in [7.17.31](/7.17.31#sec-31.4).

The relationships among the polyneopteran orders are less certain than their monophyly. Analyses of transcriptomes and morphology agree that the Dictyoptera, Eukinolabia and Xenonomia are clades, and that the termites arose within the cockroaches, as the sister group of the wood-eating cockroach *Cryptocercus*, with which they share wood-digesting gut protists transmitted from parent to offspring. The termites are relatively young: their oldest fossils are Early Cretaceous and molecular dates place their origin in the Late Jurassic, about 150 million years ago. The positions of the stoneflies, the earwigs and the tiny, rare Zoraptera, which have been placed in almost every possible position, remain the least stable part of the polyneopteran tree; recent studies suggest that Zoraptera and Dermaptera are sister groups (Haplocercata) and that stoneflies are near the base of the group.

### 30.2.5. Paraneoptera

**Definition 30.2.4 (Paraneoptera)** The **Paraneoptera** (Acercaria) are the clade of neopteran insects comprising the Psocodea (bark lice, book lice and lice), the Thysanoptera (thrips) and the Hemiptera; they lack cerci, have at most three tarsomeres, a reduced number of Malpighian tubules (four), concentrated abdominal ganglia, an enlarged cibarial pump with a bulging postclypeus and a slender, rod-like lacinia.

The characters are described in [7.17.32](/7.17.32#sec-32.1). Thrips and Hemiptera, which have stylet mouthparts, form the Condylognatha. The monophyly of the Paraneoptera is supported by morphology and by the largest phylogenomic analysis of the group (Johnson and colleagues 2018), whereas the study of Misof and colleagues placed the Psocodea as the sister group of the Holometabola, with weak support. Either way the Paraneoptera and the Holometabola together probably form a clade, the Eumetabola, the sister group of the Polyneoptera.

### 30.2.6. Holometabola

**Definition 30.2.5 (Holometabola)** The **Holometabola** (Endopterygota) are the clade of neopteran insects with complete metamorphosis ([7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.2.4)): their larvae lack compound eyes and external wing pads, the wings and other adult structures develop from imaginal discs, and a pupal stage separates larva and adult. They comprise eleven orders: Hymenoptera, Raphidioptera, Megaloptera, Neuroptera, Coleoptera, Strepsiptera, Mecoptera, Siphonaptera, Diptera, Trichoptera and Lepidoptera.

The relationships within the Holometabola are among the best resolved in insect phylogeny and are treated in [7.17.33](/7.17.33#sec-33.1): the Hymenoptera are the sister group of all others (Aparaglossata), which form two clades, the Neuropteroidea (the three neuropterid orders and the Coleopterida, beetles and Strepsiptera) and the Mecopterida (Antliophora: scorpionflies, fleas and flies; Amphiesmenoptera: caddisflies and moths). Two long-standing problems have been solved by phylogenomics: the Strepsiptera, parasites with a bizarre morphology, are the sister group of the beetles (Niehuis and colleagues 2012), and the fleas are highly modified, parasitic members of the scorpionfly lineage, possibly nested within the Mecoptera as the sister group of the small southern family Nannochoristidae (Tihelka and colleagues 2020), which would make the Mecoptera in their traditional sense paraphyletic.

### 30.2.7. Characters of the Major Clades

The groups recognized by phylogenomics are not defined by molecules alone; most of them have morphological synapomorphies, many recognized before molecular data existed. The table summarizes them; for some nodes, notably the Eumetabola and the Neuropteroidea, convincing morphological characters are still lacking.

| Clade | Main synapomorphies |
|---|---|
| Insecta (Ectognatha) | Exposed mouthparts; flagellum of the antenna without intrinsic muscles; Johnston's organ in the pedicel; ovipositor of segments 8 and 9; tarsus subdivided into tarsomeres; median caudal filament |
| Dicondylia | Mandible articulated with the head by two condyles, moving as a hinge in one plane; ovipositor with an interlocking (olistheter) mechanism |
| Pterygota | Wings on the meso- and metathorax; no molts after the wings become functional (except the subimago); loss of the abdominal styli in adults |
| Neoptera | Wing folding by the third axillary sclerite and its muscle; jugal lobe of the wing |
| Polyneoptera | Tarsal pads (euplantulae); enlarged anal fan of the hindwing; characters of the egg and the wing base |
| Paraneoptera | Enlarged cibarial pump with a bulging postclypeus; rod-like lacinia; at most three tarsomeres; no cerci; four Malpighian tubules |
| Holometabola | Complete metamorphosis with a pupa; larvae with stemmata only and without external wing pads; imaginal discs |
| Aparaglossata (Holometabola other than Hymenoptera) | Loss of the paraglossae of the larval labium |
| Coleopterida | Flight powered mainly by the hindwings (posteromotorism), the forewings transformed into elytra (beetles) or small club-like halteres (Strepsiptera) |
| Antliophora | A muscular sperm pump in the male genitalia |
| Amphiesmenoptera | Hairy or scaly wings; silk from the larval labial glands; female heterogamety (ZW); paired glands on the fifth abdominal sternum |

Many of these characters have been modified or lost in particular groups, as synapomorphies often are: the fleas and lice have lost the wings, beetles the external ovipositor of the ground plan, and the higher flies the larval head capsule. A synapomorphy marks the ancestor of a clade, not every one of its members.

### 30.2.8. From Crustaceans to Insects

The hexapods are terrestrial crustaceans. Phylogenomic analyses place them within the **Pancrustacea**, most often as the sister group of the Remipedia, blind crustaceans of flooded coastal caves ([7.17.21](/7.17.21)), and the hexapod body plan, with its three tagmata, six legs and labium formed from the second maxillae, is a terrestrial modification of a crustacean one ([7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.1.4)). The structure of the ommatidia, with four crystalline cone cells, and of the brain, with its optic neuropils, are shared with crustaceans and not with myriapods, whose tracheae, Malpighian tubules and uniramous legs evolved independently on land ([7.17.20](/7.17.20)).

When did the hexapod ancestor leave the water? The molecular dates place the split of hexapods from their crustacean relatives in the Cambrian or Ordovician, and the origin of the insects in the Early Ordovician, a time when the only land plants were small bryophyte-like forms and the soil was a thin crust. The first hexapods were probably small detritivores and fungivores of moist soil and litter, as the springtails and diplurans still are. Their successful terrestrialization depended on the waterproof epicuticle, the tracheal system and internal fertilization or indirect sperm transfer, all of which evolved convergently in arachnids and myriapods ([7.17.18](/7.17.18)).

### 30.2.9. A Dated Tree

Combining the phylogeny with molecular dates gives a time tree of the insect orders. The dates have large uncertainties, of tens of millions of years for the deep nodes, because they depend on the calibration fossils, the clock model and the treatment of rate variation ([7.10.24](/7.10.24)). The broad pattern, however, is consistent among studies: insects originated in the Ordovician; winged insects in the Devonian; the three neopteran lineages and the Holometabola by the Early Carboniferous; most living orders by the Permian or Triassic, often tens of millions of years before their oldest fossils.

![A dated tree of the major lineages of insects (chronogram; node ages approximate and rounded, after Misof and colleagues 2014 and later studies, with uncertainties of 20–40 Myr for many nodes). Polyneoptera are shown as a single tip; the three-way split among Ephemeroptera, Odonata and Neoptera is drawn as unresolved. Marks show key characters: wings (Pterygota), wing folding (Neoptera) and complete metamorphosis (Holometabola). The geological time scale is shown below the tree.](./plots/insect-timetree.py)

Molecular and fossil dates for the same nodes often disagree, and the disagreement is instructive. The Lepidoptera are a good example. Their oldest body fossils are Early Jurassic, about 190 Ma, but scales from the Triassic–Jurassic boundary (Section 30.1.5) extend the record to about 201 Ma, and molecular analyses place the split between moths and caddisflies near 300 Ma (Kawahara and colleagues 2019), in the Late Carboniferous or Early Permian. The butterflies, whose body fossils are only Paleogene, are estimated to be about 100 million years old, so that they probably originated in the Cretaceous with the angiosperms. Differences of this size arise from several sources: the molecular dates depend on which fossils are used as calibrations and how much rate variation the clock model allows ([7.10.24](/7.10.24)); the fossil record of small, soft-bodied insects is patchy; and early members of a lineage may lack the characters by which palaeontologists recognize it.

## 30.3. Correspondence with Traditional Classifications

Traditional classifications, including those of most Chinese textbooks, divided the insects into the subclasses Apterygota and Pterygota, and the Pterygota into the Exopterygota (wings develop externally, hemimetabolous) and the Endopterygota (wings develop internally, holometabolous); the number of orders recognized was usually 33–34. The modern system keeps the diagnostic characters but changes the groups:

| Traditional group | Modern status | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Class Insecta in the broad sense, including Collembola, Protura and Diplura | Hexapoda; Insecta in the narrow sense (Ectognatha) | Entognathans lack the insect antenna and ectognathy; whether they are monophyletic is uncertain ([7.17.22](/7.17.22)) |
| Subclass Apterygota (wingless insects) | Paraphyletic: Archaeognatha and Zygentoma are successive sister groups of Pterygota | Defined only by the absence of wings |
| Thysanura | Paraphyletic: split into Archaeognatha and Zygentoma | Silverfish share dicondylic mandibles with winged insects |
| Subclass Pterygota | Clade (Definition 30.2.1) | Wings evolved once |
| Palaeoptera | Possibly paraphyletic (Section 30.2.3) | The non-folding wing is ancestral |
| Exopterygota (Hemimetabola) | Paraphyletic: Polyneoptera and Paraneoptera, plus Palaeoptera | Defined by ancestral characters |
| Endopterygota (Holometabola) | Clade (Definition 30.2.5) | Complete metamorphosis evolved once |
| "Orthopteroid orders" | Polyneoptera (clade) | Supported by phylogenomic data |
| "Hemipteroid orders" | Paraneoptera (clade, with some doubt about Psocodea) | |
| Isoptera (termites) | Within Blattodea | Termites are the sister group of the wood roach *Cryptocercus* ([7.17.31](/7.17.31)) |
| Mallophaga and Anoplura (lice) | Within Psocodea | Lice arose from bark lice ([7.17.32](/7.17.32)) |
| Homoptera | Paraphyletic; within Hemiptera | Heteroptera are nested among its suborders ([7.17.32](/7.17.32)) |
| Mecoptera and Siphonaptera as separate orders | Fleas probably nested within Mecoptera | Kept as two orders here pending further evidence ([7.17.35](/7.17.35)) |

Chinese zoology textbooks generally present the older two-subclass scheme: the subclass Apterygota with four orders (Protura, Collembola, Diplura and Thysanura) and the subclass Pterygota divided into Palaeoptera and Neoptera, with about 30 orders of winged insects, some of them (Homoptera, Isoptera, Anoplura and Mallophaga) since merged into other orders. The schematic tree in these textbooks already shows the Palaeoptera–Neoptera split and the separation of the endopterygote orders, which remain valid in substance; the main differences lie at the base (entognathans and the split of Thysanura) and in the merging of the orders listed in the table. The developmental classification (ametaboly, hemimetaboly, holometaboly), which these textbooks combine with the classification of orders, is discussed in [7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.2.5).

The number of orders has therefore fallen, from 33–34 in older Chinese textbooks to 28 insect orders (31 hexapod orders) today, even though two orders, Mantophasmatodea and the separation of Zygentoma from Archaeognatha, were added. Readers who learned the older system will find all the old diagnostic statements, "termites have equal wings and moniliform antennae", "the mouthparts of Homoptera arise from the back of the head", still true; only the conclusion that these characters define separate orders has changed.

## 30.4. Key Innovations and the Diversity of Insects

### 30.4.1. Why Are There So Many Insects?

Insects comprise about 60% of all described animal species ([7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.4)), and four holometabolous orders, the beetles, flies, moths and wasps, account for about four fifths of the insects. The traditional explanations, outlined in [7.17.22](/7.17.22), are of two kinds: properties of insects as a whole (small size, the exoskeleton, short generation times) and particular innovations that opened new ways of life (wings, wing folding, complete metamorphosis, herbivory, parasitism). Diversity depends on the rates of speciation and extinction and on time; a clade is rich because it diversified fast, went extinct slowly, or is old. These factors can be separated only by comparing clades.

The fossil record points to low extinction as a major cause. Insect families, once they appear, rarely disappear: about 84% of the insect families living in the Early Cretaceous still exist, whereas tetrapod families of that age are mostly extinct, and apart from the end-Permian crisis insects have suffered no mass extinction at the family level (Labandeira and Sepkoski 1993). Insect richness has accumulated over 400 million years with little loss. The factors that give insects their low extinction rates, wide distributions, large populations, the ability to survive unfavourable periods in diapause and to disperse by flight, are properties of the whole group rather than of particular lineages.

Small size is a property of insects as a whole that probably contributes to their diversity in another way. The median body length of insects is a few millimetres, and the number of species in a body-size class increases towards small sizes down to about 1–3 mm before declining. A small animal perceives its environment at a finer grain: a single tree offers niches to hundreds of insect species, on the bark, in the wood, in the leaves, in the flowers and seeds, in the galls of other insects and in the bodies of other insects. Many more species can coexist on a given resource if each uses only a small part of it, and populations of small animals are large enough to persist in small areas. This partitioning of the environment, together with the narrow host ranges of many herbivores and parasitoids, explains why tropical forests contain such vast numbers of insect species ([7.17.22](/7.17.22#sec-22.4)).

### 30.4.2. Testing Key Innovations

A **key innovation** ([7.10.31](/7.10.31#def-31.5.2)) is a trait that raises the **diversification rate** of the lineages that have it. Because a single clade that has the trait and is diverse could be diverse for any number of other reasons, the standard test compares a clade with its **sister group**: sister groups are of the same age by definition, so a difference in species number reflects a difference in net diversification rate, not in time. When the trait has evolved many times, as herbivory has in insects, a set of independent sister-group comparisons can be tested with a sign test, as in the classic study of Mitter and colleagues (1988), which is worked through in [7.10.31](/7.10.31#sec-31.5.2). When the trait has evolved only once, as wings or complete metamorphosis have, one can still ask whether a single split is more uneven than expected by chance.

**Example 30.4.1 (Equal-Rates Test for One Sister Pair)** Suppose that in a clade of $N$ species all lineages speciate at the same rate and none go extinct (a **Yule process**, [7.10.31](/7.10.31)). Then the number of species descended from one of the two daughters of the root is uniformly distributed on $1, 2, \dots, N - 1$ (Slowinski and Guyer 1989). The probability that the smaller of the two sister clades contains $n$ species or fewer is therefore

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{smaller clade} \le n) = \frac{2n}{N - 1}, \qquad n < \frac{N}{2}.
\tag{30.2}
$$
For the beetles (about 387,100 described species) and their sister group, the Strepsiptera (about 600), $N \approx 387{,}700$ and $\mathbb{P} \approx 1{,}200/387{,}700 \approx 0.003$: so uneven a split is very unlikely under equal rates, and the beetle lineage has diversified faster. For the moths and butterflies (157,400) and the caddisflies (12,000), $\mathbb{P} \approx 24{,}000/169{,}400 \approx 0.14$: although the Lepidoptera are thirteen times richer, the split is not significantly uneven by itself.

The second result illustrates the low power of the test for a single pair: even a large ratio of species numbers can arise by chance in a Yule process, because the distribution of clade sizes is so broad. In terms of rates, the stem-age estimator of the net diversification rate, $r = \ln N / t$ (with $t$ the age of the split), gives for two sister clades the ratio $r_1/r_2 = \ln N_1/\ln N_2$, independent of $t$: for beetles and Strepsiptera $12.87/6.40 \approx 2.0$, for Lepidoptera and Trichoptera $11.97/9.39 \approx 1.3$. A twofold difference in rate, sustained over 300 million years, produces a 600-fold difference in species number.

![Sister-group comparisons among insect lineages (described species, approximate counts given by Brusca, Giribet and Moore 2023; sister relationships as in the dated tree of Section 30.2.9). For each pair the bars show the species numbers on a logarithmic scale and the right column gives the probability, under the equal-rates Yule model (Eq. (30.2)), that the smaller clade is as small as observed or smaller. Pairs: Pterygota versus Zygentoma (wings); Holometabola versus Paraneoptera (complete metamorphosis, assuming Eumetabola); Coleopterida versus Neuropterida; Coleoptera versus Strepsiptera; Diptera versus Mecoptera with Siphonaptera; Lepidoptera versus Trichoptera; Hemiptera versus Thysanoptera. Several large differences are not significant on their own (P > 0.05), which illustrates the low power of single comparisons.](./plots/sister-groups.py)

The comparisons show a mixed picture. Wings are associated with a vastly uneven split (winged insects against silverfish), but this is a single event, and the silverfish lineage may simply have suffered extinction. Complete metamorphosis is not significantly associated with an uneven split against its sister group in this crude test, even though holometabolans outnumber the Paraneoptera eightfold; analyses of richness across a dated tree of all insect families, which use far more information, do associate complete metamorphosis with faster diversification, and find further increases of rate within the beetles, flies, wasps and moths (Rainford and colleagues 2014). The key innovations, if they are such, seem to have acted together with later traits: herbivory in the beetles and moths, parasitism in the wasps and flies, and the association with flowering plants.

Parasitism, in particular, is a puzzle. Parasitoid wasps are one of the most species-rich groups of insects, yet a sister-group study found no consistent increase of diversity in parasitoid lineages compared with their predatory or herbivorous relatives (Wiegmann and colleagues 1993); the diversity of the parasitoid Hymenoptera may depend more on the diversity of their hosts and on the narrow host ranges of individual species than on parasitism as such ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)).

### 30.4.3. Did Insects Radiate with the Flowering Plants?

The flowering plants and the insects are the two most species-rich groups of terrestrial organisms, and many insects depend on angiosperms as food or as sources of nectar and pollen. It is natural to suppose that the two radiated together. The evidence is mixed:

- At the level of families, the fossil record shows no acceleration of insect diversification when angiosperms appeared in the Early Cretaceous; insect family diversity had been increasing steadily since the Triassic (Labandeira and Sepkoski 1993).
- The major orders, including the herbivorous Lepidoptera and the pollinating Hymenoptera and Diptera, originated in the Permian or Triassic, long before angiosperms, and pollination by insects is documented on gymnosperms in the Jurassic (Section 30.1.5).
- On the other hand, several herbivorous lineages did diversify in the Cretaceous and Paleogene alongside their angiosperm hosts: the phytophagous beetles (leaf beetles and weevils) (McKenna and colleagues 2009), the butterflies and several groups of moths (Kawahara and colleagues 2019), the bees, and many families of aphids and leafhoppers.

The current view is therefore that insects did not radiate because of angiosperms; rather, an already diverse insect fauna, which included pollinators and herbivores of gymnosperms, provided the ecological setting in which angiosperms diversified, and some insect lineages then radiated on the new hosts. This process of reciprocal evolutionary change and diversification between plants and their insect associates, the escape-and-radiate **coevolution** of Ehrlich and Raven ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)), is the subject of [7.17.37](/7.17.37).

### 30.4.4. Major Ecological Transitions

The history of insects can also be told as a series of ecological transitions, each of which happened once or many times and changed the ecological roles of insects in terrestrial ecosystems.

| Transition | Number of origins | Earliest evidence | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Herbivory on vascular plants | At least 50 times | Spore feeding and piercing in the Devonian and Carboniferous | Insects became the main consumers of living plant tissue on land |
| Flight | Once | About 325 Ma (fossils), about 405 Ma (molecular) | Dispersal, escape from predators, access to plants of all heights |
| Leaf mining and galling | Many times (mining in Lepidoptera, Diptera, Coleoptera, Hymenoptera) | Galls in the Late Carboniferous, mines in the Triassic | Herbivores protected inside plant tissues |
| Pollination | Many times | Jurassic long-proboscid insects on gymnosperms | Mutualisms with seed plants ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)) |
| Aquatic life of immatures | Several dozen times in about a dozen orders | Carboniferous mayfly relatives | Insects dominate freshwater invertebrate faunas; secondarily aquatic respiration ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.4.4)) |
| Parasitoidism | Once in Hymenoptera (Apocrita), several times in Diptera, Coleoptera, Strepsiptera | Jurassic parasitoid wasps | A major cause of mortality of other insects ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)) |
| Blood feeding and ectoparasitism | Many times (fleas, lice, bed bugs, triatomines, mosquitoes and several other flies) | Jurassic giant fleas | Vectors of disease ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)) |
| Eusociality | Once in termites, once in ants, several times in bees and wasps, and in a few aphids, thrips and a beetle | Cretaceous termites and ants | Social insects dominate the animal biomass of many habitats ([7.17.38](/7.17.38)) |

Plant–insect interactions passed through four broad phases, documented by the fossil record of insect damage on plants (Labandeira 2006): a Silurian–Devonian phase of arthropods feeding on spores and piercing the stems of early land plants; a late Palaeozoic phase in which all the major functional feeding groups (external leaf feeding, piercing and sucking, boring, galling) appeared on seed ferns, conifers and cordaites; a Mesozoic phase dominated by gymnosperms, in which leaf mining and pollination appeared; and the phase that began in the mid-Cretaceous, dominated by angiosperms. Each phase was largely a re-assembly of existing feeding modes on new plants rather than the origin of new ones.

### 30.4.5. Relict Groups and Biogeography

Ancient lineages that have survived in only a few places are a characteristic feature of the insect fauna. The ice crawlers (Grylloblattodea) live in cold habitats of north-eastern Asia (including the Changbai Mountains of north-eastern China) and western North America, the moss bugs (Coleorrhyncha: Peloridiidae) only in the mossy temperate forests of southern South America, New Zealand and Australia, the scorpionfly family Nannochoristidae in the same southern lands, and the heel-walkers in southern and eastern Africa. Such disjunct distributions are classic subjects of **biogeography** ([7.10.30](/7.10.30)): the southern pattern corresponds to the fragments of Gondwana and was long explained by vicariance, the splitting of populations as the supercontinent broke up; dated phylogenies show that some splits are indeed old enough for this, whereas others are younger and imply dispersal across oceans.

Some lineages tell the same story by their fossils. The snakeflies (Raphidioptera) and the scorpionflies were much more diverse and widespread in the Mesozoic than today, and the relict genus *Epiophlebia* of the Himalaya and East Asia ([7.17.31](/7.17.31#sec-31.3.2)) is the last survivor of a group of Mesozoic dragonflies. Relicts are valuable for phylogenetics because they break up long branches and preserve ancestral character combinations, and for conservation because each represents a large share of evolutionary history in a few species.

### 30.4.6. Insect History and the Present Crisis

Knowledge of insect history gives a perspective on present changes. Over 400 million years insects survived the end-Permian extinction with the loss of about eight orders and passed through the end-Cretaceous extinction with little loss at the family level. Present-day declines of insect abundance and diversity, documented in long-term monitoring in Europe and North America and caused mainly by the loss and degradation of habitats, the intensification of agriculture, pesticides, light pollution and climate change, are therefore not unprecedented in kind; what is new is their speed and their cause. Because insects pollinate most flowering plants, decompose litter and dung and are food for most terrestrial vertebrates, their decline affects whole ecosystems; the conservation of insects is treated in [7.17.40](/7.17.40).

## Questions

1. Why are molecular estimates of the age of insects and of winged insects older than their oldest fossils, and is this a contradiction?

   **Answer** A fossil gives a minimum age: the lineage must be at least as old as its oldest known member, and the first members of a lineage are rare, small and often lack the diagnostic characters, so they are seldom found. The Devonian–Carboniferous hexapod gap shows that a long interval may be almost unrecorded. Molecular dates estimate the split between lineages, which precedes the appearance of the recognizable crown group. A difference of tens of millions of years is therefore expected; it would be a contradiction only if fossils were older than the molecular estimate. The molecular dates themselves are uncertain, because they depend on calibrations and clock models.

2. Explain why the inability of mayflies and dragonflies to fold their wings is not evidence that they form a clade.

   **Answer** A clade is supported only by shared derived characters (synapomorphies). The non-folding wing is the ancestral condition of winged insects, retained in the two oldest lineages, whereas wing folding is the derived character of Neoptera. Sharing an ancestral character (symplesiomorphy) says nothing about whether two lineages are more closely related to each other than to a third, so the Palaeoptera hypothesis must be supported by other characters, such as features of the larval mouthparts and the wing veins, or by molecular data, and these are weak and conflicting.

3. Which traditional groups of insects are paraphyletic, and why have the corresponding orders been merged rather than kept as separate groups of convenience?

   **Answer** Apterygota, Thysanura and Exopterygota are paraphyletic, as were Homoptera, Psocoptera without lice, Blattodea without termites, and probably Mecoptera without fleas. In the last four cases a recognized order (lice, termites, fleas) arose from within another; keeping both orders would make the parent order paraphyletic, defined only by lacking the specializations of its derived subgroup. Modern classification aims at monophyletic taxa so that names convey phylogenetic information; therefore the derived group is included in the larger order (lice in Psocodea, termites in Blattodea, Homoptera in Hemiptera), and the traditional groups survive only as informal names.

4. (Calculation) The Diptera have about 159,000 described species and their sister group, Mecoptera including fleas, about 3,300. Using Eq. (30.2), test the equal-rates hypothesis. Then compute the ratio of the stem-age net diversification rates.

   **Answer** $N \approx 162{,}300$ and $n = 3{,}300$, so $\mathbb{P} = 2 \times 3{,}300/162{,}300 \approx 0.041$, below 0.05: the split is more uneven than expected under equal rates, though not overwhelmingly. The rate ratio is $\ln 159{,}000/\ln 3{,}300 = 11.98/8.10 \approx 1.5$. Fleas are highly specialized parasites and scorpionflies a relict group, while flies have radiated into almost every aquatic, terrestrial and parasitic way of life, which is consistent with, but does not prove, a role for traits of flies such as their larval diversity.

5. Why is the low extinction rate of insect families an alternative to key innovations as an explanation of insect diversity, and how could the two be distinguished?

   **Answer** Diversity at present depends on net diversification, speciation minus extinction, integrated over time. A group with ordinary speciation rates but very low extinction accumulates species over hundreds of millions of years, whereas a key innovation increases the rate in particular lineages. The two predict different patterns: low extinction acting on all insects predicts that most insect lineages, with and without the innovation, are rich compared with other animals of the same age, and that fossil families persist; a key innovation predicts that lineages with the trait are richer than their sister groups. Fossil data on family durations test the first; sister-group comparisons and trait-dependent diversification models on dated trees test the second.

6. (Calculation) The oldest fossils of a family of beetles are 150 million years old, and the family is known from 6 fossil horizons spread between 150 and 50 Ma. A molecular study dates its origin to 230 Ma. Is the molecular date compatible with the fossil record at the 95% level, assuming uniform preservation?

   **Answer** By Eq. (30.1), with $R = 100$ million years and $H = 6$, $r_{0.95} = 100\,[0.05^{-1/5} - 1] = 100\,[1.821 - 1] \approx 82$ million years, so the 95% confidence limit for the origin is about $150 + 82 = 232$ Ma. The molecular date of 230 Ma lies just inside the interval and is not contradicted by the fossils. With so few horizons the interval is very wide; the result also depends on the assumption of uniform preservation, which is questionable for amber-dependent records of small beetles.

7. Why are the halteres of flies and the club-like wings of Strepsiptera evidence against, rather than for, a close relationship of the two groups?

   **Answer** In flies the hindwings are reduced to halteres and the forewings fly; in Strepsiptera the forewings are reduced to clubs and the hindwings fly. The structures are therefore on different segments and are not positionally homologous: they are a convergent solution to the same aerodynamic problem, balancing a flight with one pair of wings. Hindwing flight (posteromotorism) is shared by Strepsiptera with beetles, whose forewings are elytra, and supports the Coleopterida, which phylogenomic studies confirm.

8. What evidence suggests that insect pollination is older than the flowering plants?

   **Answer** Insects with long, siphonate mouthparts (mesopsychid and aneuretopsychid scorpionflies, kalligrammatid lacewings, some flies) are known from the Jurassic and Early Cretaceous, before or at the very beginning of the angiosperm record; their mouthparts resemble those of nectar feeders, and some carry gymnosperm pollen. Gymnosperms of the time (Bennettitales, Gnetales, some seed ferns) produced pollination drops that such insects could feed on. Together with the occurrence of pollen-feeding insects since the Carboniferous, this shows that insect pollination evolved on gymnosperms and was available when angiosperms appeared.
