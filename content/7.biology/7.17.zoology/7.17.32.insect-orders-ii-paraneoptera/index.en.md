The **Paraneoptera** (also called Acercaria or hemipteroid orders) are the second great lineage of neopteran insects, with about 120,000 described species in three orders: the bark lice and lice (Psocodea), the thrips (Thysanoptera) and the bugs (Hemiptera). Their evolution is a story of mouthparts. Starting from chewing ancestors with a powerful sucking pump in the head, they turned the maxillae and mandibles into stylets and became liquid feeders, on plant cells, phloem and xylem sap, the hemolymph of other insects and the blood of vertebrates. Hemiptera, with more than 100,000 species, are the largest order of hemimetabolous insects, and among them are the most important vectors of plant viruses and some of the worst pests of crops.

This chapter follows [7.17.31](/7.17.31), where the key to orders and the polyneopteran orders are given, and precedes the Holometabola ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)–[7.17.36](/7.17.36)). Mouthpart types are defined in [7.17.23](/7.17.23), the **hemelytron** in [7.17.24](/7.17.24), and reproductive modes such as **parthenogenesis** and **cyclical parthenogenesis** in [7.17.28](/7.17.28); the role of insects as vectors of human disease is treated in [7.17.40](/7.17.40).

## 32.1. Characters of the Paraneoptera

Paraneopterans share a combination of derived characters that reflect the shift to sucking liquids:

| Character | State in Paraneoptera |
|---|---|
| Cibarium | Enlarged cibarial dilator muscles, attached to a large, often bulging postclypeus: a powerful sucking pump |
| Lacinia | Slender, rod-like, detached from the stipes (in Hemiptera and thrips part of the stylets) |
| Tarsi | Three or fewer tarsomeres |
| Cerci | Absent |
| Hind wing | Anal fan reduced; venation reduced |
| Abdominal ganglia | Concentrated in one or two ganglionic masses |
| Development | Hemimetabolous; some groups with one or more quiescent, non-feeding stages before the adult (thrips, whiteflies, male scale insects) |
| Sperm | A single flagellum with two centrioles in some groups; sperm cells often in bundles |

Psocodea retain chewing mandibles; Thysanoptera and Hemiptera (together Condylognatha) have stylet mouthparts and are sister groups. The quiescent stages of thrips, whiteflies and male scale insects, in which wings and adult structures develop inside a non-feeding instar, are often called "pupae"; they evolved independently of the pupa of Holometabola, as convergent responses to the large difference between immature and adult ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)).

## 32.2. Psocodea: Bark Lice, Booklice and Lice

**Definition 32.2.1 (Psocodea)** The **Psocodea** are paraneopteran insects with chewing mandibles, a rod-like lacinia, a bulging postclypeus and one-, two- or three-segmented tarsi, comprising the free-living bark lice and booklice, with long filiform antennae and (when present) membranous wings held roof-like at rest, and the lice, wingless, dorsoventrally flattened, permanent ectoparasites of birds and mammals with short antennae, reduced or no eyes and legs adapted for grasping hair or feathers; about 11,000 species.

### 32.2.1. Bark Lice and Booklice

Free-living psocodeans (about 5600 species, formerly the order Psocoptera) are 1–10 mm long, with a large head, bulging postclypeus, long antennae, and two pairs of wings with a pterostigma and reduced venation (wings are reduced or absent in many species). They graze on algae, lichens, fungi and organic debris on bark, leaves, rocks and in litter; some live in birds' nests and human dwellings. Many take up water vapour from unsaturated air through a pair of hypopharyngeal sclerites, against which the rod-like lacinia is braced. The booklice *Liposcelis* (Liposcelididae), wingless and flattened, are cosmopolitan pests of stored grain, flour, herbaria and insect collections and are resistant to many fumigants. Living bark lice are divided into three suborders, Trogiomorpha, Troctomorpha (including Liposcelididae) and Psocomorpha (the largest, with Psocidae and many families of bark-dwellers).

The forewing of winged bark lice has a characteristic reduced venation: a thickened pterostigma, a radial sector and media that often fuse for a short distance, and in many families a closed cell (the areola postica) formed by a loop of the cubitus; wings are held roof-like and coupled in flight by a hook on the forewing margin. Many species spin silk from labial glands to cover their eggs or to form communal webs on tree trunks, in which some tropical species live gregariously in hundreds. Parthenogenesis is common. About 5600 species are described, but the fauna of tropical forest canopies is poorly known; in China several hundred species are recorded, many described in the last decades.

### 32.2.2. Lice

The parasitic lice (about 5000 species, formerly the order Phthiraptera or the orders Mallophaga and Anoplura) arose within the free-living psocodeans: molecular phylogenies place them close to the booklice (Liposcelididae), within Troctomorpha, so that "Psocoptera" without lice is paraphyletic. Some recent studies even suggest that parasitism arose twice, once in the ancestors of the bird-and-mammal chewing lice and once in another lineage, but this is not settled. Lice spend their entire life on one host individual, glue their eggs (nits) to hairs or feathers, and pass through three nymphal instars. The four groups are:

| Group | Mouthparts and feeding | Hosts and examples |
|---|---|---|
| Amblycera | Chewing; mandibles horizontal; maxillary palps present; antennae in grooves | Birds and mammals; feed on skin and blood (*Menopon gallinae* of poultry) |
| Ischnocera | Chewing; mandibles vertical; antennae exposed | Mostly birds (feather lice), also mammals (*Bovicola bovis*) |
| Rhynchophthirina | Chewing mandibles at the tip of a long rostrum | Three species on elephants and warthogs (*Haematomyzus*) |
| Anoplura (sucking lice) | Three piercing stylets retracted into a sac in the head; feed on blood | Placental mammals only; human lice |

The chewing groups, Amblycera and Ischnocera, were the "Mallophaga", which is paraphyletic: Ischnocera, Rhynchophthirina and Anoplura together form a clade. Because blood is poor in B vitamins, sucking lice carry obligate bacterial symbionts (*Riesia* in human lice) in special mycetomes, as phloem-sucking bugs do for amino acids (Section 32.4.1).

Lice show a suite of adaptations to permanent life on a host. The body is flattened and wingless, the eyes are reduced or absent, the antennae short, and each tarsus ends in a single strong claw that closes against a thumb-like process of the tibia, clasping a hair or feather barb of a diameter matched to the louse; spiracles can close, so that lice survive the host's swimming or bathing. Most lice have three nymphal instars, and the whole life cycle, from egg to egg-laying adult, takes about three to four weeks. A female head louse lays about five to ten eggs a day and glues each to a hair close to the scalp with a cement produced by her accessory glands; the eggs hatch after about a week. Lice die within a day or two away from the host, and transmission requires close contact (head lice), shared clothing and bedding (body lice) or sexual contact (crab lice).

Lice are among the most host-specific of all parasites: many species occur on one host species, and related hosts often carry related lice, a pattern known as Fahrenholz's rule. The phylogenies of lice and hosts are therefore often congruent, but host switches occur, especially between hosts that share nests or dust baths, and the louse fauna of a bird or mammal is a historical record of its contacts.

Three kinds of lice live on people. The head louse and the body louse are forms of one species, *Pediculus humanus* (subspecies *capitis* and *humanus*); the body louse lives in clothing and lays its eggs there, and it is the vector of epidemic typhus (*Rickettsia prowazekii*), trench fever (*Bartonella quintana*) and louse-borne relapsing fever (*Borrelia recurrentis*), diseases of war and famine that killed millions until the 20th century. The divergence of body lice from head lice has been used to estimate when people began to wear clothing, roughly 80,000–170,000 years ago (Toups and coauthors 2011). The crab louse *Pthirus pubis* is most closely related to the gorilla louse *P. gorillae*, from which it was acquired by host switching about 3–4 million years ago. Lice often speciate in step with their hosts; the phylogeny of the chewing lice of pocket gophers mirrors that of the gophers (Hafner and Nadler 1988), a classic example of cospeciation ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)).

## 32.3. Thysanoptera: Thrips

**Definition 32.3.1 (Thysanoptera)** The **Thysanoptera** (thrips) are minute (0.5–15 mm, most 1–2 mm), slender paraneopteran insects with an asymmetrical mouth cone formed by the labrum and labium enclosing the left mandible and two maxillary stylets (the right mandible is absent), six- to ten-segmented antennae, one- or two-segmented tarsi with an eversible adhesive vesicle (arolium) at the tip, and, when winged, two pairs of narrow strap-like wings fringed with long setae; about 6000 species.

The mouthparts are of the rasping-sucking type ([7.17.23](/7.17.23)): the single mandible punches a hole in a plant cell, and the maxillary stylets, locked together into a food tube, are inserted and the cell contents sucked out ("punch and suck"). Damaged cells fill with air, giving the silvery scars typical of thrips damage. The fringed wings work at the low **Reynolds numbers** of insects this small, where a comb of setae is almost as effective as a membrane and much lighter ([7.17.25](/7.17.25)).

Development includes two feeding larval instars followed by one or two non-feeding instars, the propupa and pupa (Terebrantia) or a propupa and two pupal instars (Tubulifera), in which wings form externally as pads; it is intermediate between hemimetaboly and holometaboly and sometimes called remetaboly. Thrips are haplodiploid: males develop from unfertilized eggs (arrhenotoky), and many populations consist of females only.

| Suborder | Characters | Families and examples |
|---|---|---|
| Terebrantia | Female with a saw-like ovipositor of four valves; abdomen tip conical; forewing with veins and microtrichia | Thripidae: the western flower thrips *Frankliniella occidentalis*, invasive worldwide since the 1980s; onion thrips *Thrips tabaci*; melon thrips *T. palmi* |
| Tubulifera | No external ovipositor; abdominal segment 10 tubular; wings without veins; eggs laid on surfaces | Phlaeothripidae: fungus feeders, predators, gall-makers (*Kladothrips*, with soldier castes in Australian acacia galls) |

Most thrips feed on flowers, leaves, fungal spores or pollen; some are predators of mites and other thrips. As plant pests they are important mainly as vectors: tospoviruses (such as tomato spotted wilt virus) are acquired only by first-instar larvae feeding on infected plants, multiply in the thrips and are transmitted by the adult, which disperses. Thrips also pollinate some plants, among them cycads and some dipterocarp trees of Southeast Asian rain forests.

The western flower thrips *Frankliniella occidentalis*, native to western North America, spread worldwide with the trade in ornamental plants after the 1970s and reached China around 2003; it now damages vegetables and flowers in protected cultivation across the country and is resistant to many insecticides. The melon thrips *Thrips palmi*, the onion thrips *T. tabaci*, the chilli thrips *Scirtothrips dorsalis* and the rice thrips *Stenchaetothrips biformis* are other important pests in China. Their small size, cryptic habits, rapid development (about two weeks at 25 °C), haplodiploidy and the ability of females to reproduce without males make thrips populations difficult to control. Biological control uses predatory mites (*Neoseiulus cucumeris*, *Amblyseius swirskii*) and minute pirate bugs (*Orius*).

## 32.4. Hemiptera

**Definition 32.4.1 (Hemiptera)** The **Hemiptera** (bugs, cicadas, leafhoppers, planthoppers, aphids, scale insects and their relatives) are paraneopteran insects with **piercing-sucking mouthparts** in which the labium forms a segmented, grooved beak (rostrum) sheathing two mandibular and two maxillary stylets, the maxillary stylets interlocking to form a food canal and a salivary canal; maxillary and labial palps are absent; forewings are uniform in texture or hemelytra; there are no cerci; about 105,000 species.

### 32.4.1. Stylet Feeding

The stylets are long, flexible cuticular rods, the mandibular stylets outside, the maxillary pair inside, held together by tongue-and-groove joints so that they slide on one another. The tips of the mandibular stylets are barbed and anchor the bundle while the maxillary stylets advance; the beak itself does not enter the tissue but bends or telescopes back as the stylets penetrate. The cibarial pump in the head draws fluid up the food canal, and the salivary pump injects saliva down the salivary canal.

Hemipterans feed in two principal ways. Sheath feeders (most Sternorrhyncha and Auchenorrhyncha, many Heteroptera) secrete a gelling saliva that hardens into a sheath around the stylets as they move between plant cells to the phloem or xylem; they then take up sap from a single sieve element or vessel for hours. Cell-rupture feeders (plant bugs, Miridae; many predators) move the stylets back and forth, inject watery saliva rich in enzymes and suck up the liquefied tissue (lacerate and flush), a form of extra-oral digestion that reaches its extreme in predatory assassin bugs, whose saliva paralyses and digests prey. Stylet movements and the fluid taken up can be followed electrically by the electrical penetration graph, which records the potential across a circuit through plant and insect.

Plant sap is an unbalanced food. Phloem sap is rich in sugars, poor in essential amino acids and under positive pressure, so that phloem feeders take up and excrete large volumes; the surplus sugar is excreted as *honeydew*, on which sooty moulds grow and which ants harvest. Many phloem feeders concentrate food by a filter chamber, in which the anterior midgut is looped against the hindgut and water short-circuits to the rectum ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)). The amino acids they lack are supplied by obligate bacterial endosymbionts living in specialized cells (bacteriocytes): *Buchnera aphidicola* in aphids, *Portiera* in whiteflies, *Carsonella* in psyllids. Xylem sap is even poorer and under negative pressure; xylem feeders (cicadas, spittlebugs, sharpshooter leafhoppers) have a very large cibarial pump and carry two complementary symbionts, *Sulcia* and a second partner (*Hodgkinia* in cicadas), whose genomes are among the smallest known ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)).

Feeding by stylets makes hemipterans the main vectors of plant viruses and phytoplasmas ([7.11.26](/7.11.26)). Transmission modes are classified by how long the vector remains infective:

| Mode | Where the virus is retained | Acquisition and retention | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-persistent | Stylet tips | Seconds to minutes in brief probes of epidermal cells; lost within hours | Potyviruses and cucumber mosaic virus by aphids |
| Semi-persistent | Foregut | Minutes to hours; lost at molting | Citrus tristeza virus by aphids; criniviruses by whiteflies |
| Persistent circulative | Crosses the gut into the hemolymph and reaches the salivary glands, without multiplying | Hours to acquire, latent period of days; retained for life | Luteoviruses by aphids; begomoviruses (tomato yellow leaf curl virus) by the whitefly *Bemisia tabaci* |
| Persistent propagative | Multiplies in the vector, sometimes passed to the eggs | Long latent period; lifelong | Rice stripe virus by the small brown planthopper *Laodelphax striatellus*; rice dwarf virus by leafhoppers |

Non-persistent viruses are spread by aphids that probe a plant briefly and leave, often species that do not colonize the crop; insecticides therefore control them poorly, since the virus is transmitted before the aphid dies. Bacteria are transmitted in similar ways: *Xylella fastidiosa*, the cause of Pierce's disease of grapevines and of olive quick decline, by xylem-feeding sharpshooters and spittlebugs; *Candidatus* Liberibacter asiaticus, the agent of citrus greening (huanglongbing), by the Asian citrus psyllid *Diaphorina citri*.

The four suborders differ above all in the position of the beak and the texture of the forewings:

| Suborder | Beak | Antennae | Forewings | Main groups |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sternorrhyncha | Arising between or behind the fore coxae | Long, filiform (reduced in scale insects) | Uniform, membranous; often reduced; held roof-like | Psyllids, whiteflies, aphids, scale insects |
| Auchenorrhyncha | Arising from the back of the head capsule, ventrally | Short, with a bristle-like flagellum | Uniform, membranous or slightly thickened; held roof-like | Cicadas, spittlebugs, leafhoppers, treehoppers, planthoppers |
| Coleorrhyncha | Arising from the front of the head, ventrally | Short, concealed | Uniform, with a network of cells; held flat | Moss bugs (Peloridiidae) |
| Heteroptera | Arising from the front of the head; a gula behind it | Usually four or five segments, visible | Hemelytra, folded flat with the membranes overlapping | True bugs |

### 32.4.2. Sternorrhyncha

Sternorrhyncha (about 17,000 species) are small, often sedentary phloem feeders with complex life cycles, and the source of many of the worst pests of crops and glasshouses.

- Psylloidea (jumping plant lice, psyllids; about 4000 species). Adults jump with enlarged hind coxae and look like tiny cicadas; nymphs are flattened and many secrete wax or form lerps (protective covers) or galls. Most are specialized on one host genus. Psyllids are among the most host-specific insects, and their nymphs excrete honeydew coated in wax as pellets or threads, keeping the nymph clean. In China the pear psylla *Cacopsylla chinensis* and the jujube psyllid are serious fruit pests, and gall-forming psyllids induce characteristic pit and blister galls on the leaves of hackberries and other trees. The Asian citrus psyllid *Diaphorina citri* transmits huanglongbing, the most destructive disease of citrus in southern China and elsewhere.
- Aleyrodoidea (whiteflies; about 1600 species). Adults are 1–3 mm, covered with white wax powder; first-instar nymphs (crawlers) settle and the next instars are sessile scales; the last instar stops feeding and the adult develops inside it (the "puparium"). The sweetpotato whitefly *Bemisia tabaci*, a complex of many cryptic species of which MEAM1 and MED have spread worldwide, and the greenhouse whitefly *Trialeurodes vaporariorum* are major pests and vectors of begomoviruses.
- Aphidoidea (aphids, Section 32.4.3; about 5000 species).
- Coccoidea (scale insects; about 8000 species). Females are neotenic: after the mobile first instar (crawler) they settle, lose legs, eyes and antennae in many families, and secrete a cover of wax or a hard scale; they are little more than a feeding and egg-laying sac. Males pass through quiescent prepupal and pupal instars and become minute, short-lived adults with one pair of wings, hind wings reduced to hamulohalteres, no mouthparts and long waxy tail filaments. Important families are Monophlebidae (the cottony cushion scale *Icerya purchasi*), Pseudococcidae (mealybugs), Coccidae (soft scales), Diaspididae (armoured scales, the largest family; the San José scale *Diaspidiotus perniciosus*), Kerriidae (lac insects, *Kerria lacca*, source of shellac) and Dactylopiidae (cochineal, *Dactylopius coccus*, source of the red dye carmine). In China the white wax scale *Ericerus pela* (Coccidae), cultured on privet and ash, has been farmed for wax for over a thousand years.

The control of *Icerya purchasi* in California by the vedalia beetle *Novius cardinalis* (formerly *Rodolia*, Coccinellidae), imported from Australia in 1888–1889, saved the Californian citrus industry within two years and is the classic success of classical biological control ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)).

### 32.4.3. Aphids

Aphids are soft-bodied, 1–5 mm phloem feeders with long antennae (usually six segments, the last with a long terminal process, and with rings of olfactory sensilla, the rhinaria), a pair of siphunculi (cornicles) on the fifth or sixth abdominal segment that secrete defensive droplets and alarm pheromone, and a tail-like cauda. Most belong to the family Aphididae; the Adelgidae (on conifers) and Phylloxeridae lay eggs in all generations. A species may produce up to eight distinct morphs in one year (fundatrix, wingless and winged parthenogenetic females, winged migrants, sexual females, males of one or two forms), which differ in wings, eyes, antennae, rhinaria and reproductive anatomy while being genetically identical: aphids are the extreme example of insect polymorphism. Their life cycles combine **cyclical parthenogenesis** with seasonal polymorphism, viviparity and, in many species, alternation of host plants:

- *Holocyclic* species pass through one sexual generation per year. A fertilized egg overwinters on the primary host (often a woody plant); in spring it hatches into a wingless female, the fundatrix, which produces daughters by parthenogenesis and viviparity; several parthenogenetic generations follow, wingless when food is abundant and winged when crowding or deteriorating host quality, sensed through tactile and nutritional cues, induces the winged morph (a **polyphenism**). In heteroecious species the winged females migrate in early summer to a herbaceous secondary host and return in autumn; there short days induce the production of sexual females (oviparae) and males, which mate, and the oviparae lay the overwintering eggs. The cotton aphid *Aphis gossypii* overwinters as eggs on woody primary hosts such as *Hibiscus syriacus* or *Zanthoxylum* and spends the summer on cotton, cucurbits and many other crops.
- *Anholocyclic* lineages, common in warm climates and in many pest species, have lost the sexual phase and reproduce by parthenogenesis all year.

Parthenogenetic aphids are apomictic: the eggs develop without meiosis, so offspring are clones of the mother. Their embryos begin to develop in the mother before her own birth, and the developing daughter already carries granddaughter embryos, a *telescoping of generations* that shortens the effective generation time to about a week. Sex determination is XO: males arise when one X chromosome is eliminated in a special maturation division, and only X-bearing sperm are functional, so the sexual eggs all produce females.

![A life-table model of aphid population growth (illustrative parameters of the order typical for aphids near 20 °C, not data for one species: development to first birth in 7 days, then 4 daughters per day for 20 days, no mortality). The intrinsic rate of increase r is the root of the Euler–Lotka equation $\sum_x \mathrm{e}^{-rx} l_x m_x = 1$. (A) Numbers descended from one founder: viviparous parthenogenesis (r = 0.37 per day, doubling in under two days) is about twice as fast as the same life history with an egg stage, and faster still than sexual reproduction with an egg stage, in which half the offspring are sons that do not bear young. (B) r falls and doubling time rises with the time to first birth: telescoping of generations, which shortens this time, has a larger effect on r than fecundity.](./plots/aphid-growth.py)

The model in the figure shows why aphids are such effective colonists of crops. Under exponential growth the rate of increase depends more on the age at first reproduction than on the number of offspring, because daughters born early start reproducing early; parthenogenesis also avoids the "twofold cost" of producing sons ([7.10.14](/7.10.14)), and viviparity removes the egg stage. The same features make aphids the most important vectors of plant viruses, with about 275 viruses transmitted. Notable species include the green peach aphid *Myzus persicae*, a vector of over 100 viruses; the pea aphid *Acyrthosiphon pisum*, a model for the genomics of polyphenism and symbiosis; the grape phylloxera *Daktulosphaira vitifoliae* (Phylloxeridae), which, introduced from North America, destroyed most European vineyards after the 1860s until vines were grafted onto resistant American rootstocks; and the Chinese gall aphid *Schlechtendalia chinensis*, whose galls on *Rhus chinensis*, harvested for tannin as the Chinese gallnut, were a traditional source of ink and medicine. Some gall-forming aphids (Hormaphidinae, Pemphiginae) have sterile soldier nymphs that defend the gall, an independent origin of **eusociality** ([7.17.38](/7.17.38)).

### 32.4.4. Auchenorrhyncha

Auchenorrhyncha (about 43,000 species) are active, mostly jumping sap feeders with short, bristle-tipped antennae and the beak arising far back on the underside of the head. Almost all communicate by vibrations, produced by tymbals, paired ribbed membranes on the first abdominal segment that buckle when pulled by a muscle; in most species the signals travel through the plant and are inaudible to us, but cicadas amplify them into loud airborne songs. The suborder has two infraorders, recovered as sister groups by phylogenomic data.

Cicadomorpha:

- Cicadidae (cicadas; about 3000 species): large, with three ocelli, membranous wings and males with large tymbals over air-filled abdominal resonators and tympana for hearing; nymphs live underground for years feeding on xylem of roots, with fossorial forelegs, and emerge to molt on tree trunks. The periodical cicadas *Magicicada* of eastern North America emerge synchronously every 13 or 17 years in densities up to millions per hectare, a predator-satiation strategy; that both periods are prime numbers has been interpreted as minimizing synchrony with predator or competitor cycles. In China the black cicada *Cryptotympana atrata* is the commonest large species; its exuviae are a traditional medicine.

  The prime-number argument can be made precise. A predator or parasite with a life cycle of $p$ years coincides with an emergence of cicadas with period $T$ every $\operatorname{lcm}(T, p)$ years. For $T = 17$ and any $p$ from 2 to 16 this is $17p$ years, the largest possible value, whereas a 16-year cycle would coincide every 16 years with predators of period 2, 4, 8 or 16; for $T = 13$ the coincidences are likewise rare. Prime periods also minimize the hybridization of broods with different periods when they emerge together, since 13- and 17-year broods coincide only every 221 years. Whether either effect explains the evolution of prime periods is unresolved, but the arithmetic shows why prime cycles are less often matched than composite ones. Male cicadas sing with tymbals that buckle in a series of ribs, each rib producing a sound pulse; the hollow abdomen resonates at the carrier frequency, and some species exceed 100 dB at close range, among the loudest insect sounds ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)).
- Cercopoidea (spittlebugs or froghoppers): nymphs hide in a froth produced by blowing air into anal excretions mixed with secretions of the Malpighian tubules; adults include the best jumpers relative to size among insects (the froghopper *Philaenus spumarius* accelerates at up to about 4000 m s⁻² using a catapult; Burrows 2003).
- Membracoidea: leafhoppers (Cicadellidae, about 22,000 species, the largest family of Hemiptera), recognized by rows of spines on the hind tibiae and by brochosomes, tiny protein–lipid spheres from the Malpighian tubules that they spread over the body as a water repellent; and treehoppers (Membracidae), whose enlarged pronotum forms spectacular helmets mimicking thorns, ants or seeds.

Fulgoromorpha (planthoppers; about 13,000 species) have antennae inserted below the eyes, with an enlarged pedicel bearing many plate-like sensilla, and two ocelli. The Delphacidae include the most important rice pests of Asia: the brown planthopper *Nilaparvata lugens*, the white-backed planthopper *Sogatella furcifera* and the small brown planthopper *Laodelphax striatellus*. They occur as long-winged (macropterous) and short-winged (brachypterous) morphs, a density- and insulin-signalling-controlled polyphenism; the macropters of *N. lugens* migrate each spring on monsoon winds from Indochina into southern and eastern China, and outbreaks cause "hopperburn", the drying and collapse of rice patches, as well as transmitting viruses. Other important cicadomorphan pests in China are the green rice leafhopper *Nephotettix cincticeps*, vector of rice dwarf virus, which multiplies in the leafhopper and passes to its eggs, and tea green leafhoppers (*Empoasca onukii* and relatives), whose feeding on young tea shoots, oddly, induces the volatiles that give the prized "Oriental Beauty" oolong of Taiwan its honey-like flavour. Fulgoridae (lanternflies) include the spotted lanternfly *Lycorma delicatula*, native to China and an invasive pest of vineyards and trees in the United States since 2014.

### 32.4.5. Coleorrhyncha

The moss bugs (Peloridiidae; about 37 species) are flat, 2–5 mm bugs living in wet moss in temperate forests of Australia, New Zealand, New Caledonia and southern South America, a Gondwanan distribution. They have a network of cells on the head, pronotum and forewings, and they communicate by substrate vibration. Small and cryptic, they would be a curiosity were it not for their position as the sister group of Heteroptera, which makes them important for reconstructing the origin of the true bugs.

### 32.4.6. Heteroptera

The true bugs (Heteroptera, about 42,000 species) are recognized by hemelytra, folded flat over the abdomen with their membranous tips overlapping, a beak arising from the front of the head (separated from the prothorax by a gula), usually four-segmented, and scent glands: dorsal abdominal glands in nymphs and metathoracic glands opening near the hind coxae in adults. The glands produce aldehydes, alkanes and esters that deter predators and act as alarm and aggregation pheromones. Seven infraorders are recognized; the most important are:

| Infraorder | Habits and characters | Important families and species |
|---|---|---|
| Gerromorpha (semiaquatic bugs) | On the water surface, supported by hydrofuge hairs; antennae long, visible | Gerridae (water striders; *Halobates*, the only insects of the open ocean), Veliidae, Hydrometridae |
| Nepomorpha (aquatic bugs) | Under water; antennae short, hidden in grooves under the eyes; forelegs often raptorial; mostly predatory | Nepidae (water scorpions, with a long respiratory siphon at the tip of the abdomen), Belostomatidae (giant water bugs, males carrying the eggs on the back), Notonectidae (backswimmers), Corixidae (water boatmen, with a plastron) |
| Leptopodomorpha | Shore bugs, large eyes, jumping | Saldidae |
| Cimicomorpha | Diverse, many predators; forewing membrane with few cells | Reduviidae (assassin bugs), Cimicidae (bed bugs), Miridae (plant bugs, the largest family, about 11,000 species), Tingidae (lace bugs), Anthocoridae (minute pirate bugs) |
| Pentatomomorpha | Mostly plant feeders; trichobothria on the abdomen; membrane with many veins | Pentatomidae (stink bugs), Tessaratomidae, Coreidae (leaf-footed bugs), Lygaeidae (seed bugs), Pyrrhocoridae (cotton stainers), Alydidae |

Water striders stand on the surface film without breaking it. A thin leg pressed into the surface is supported by surface tension acting along both sides of the line of contact; the maximum supporting force per unit length of contact is about $2\gamma$, where $\gamma \approx 0.072\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ is the surface tension of water, or about 0.14 mN per millimetre of leg. A water strider of 10 mg weighs about 0.1 mN, so less than a millimetre of fully loaded contact would hold it. Its legs, covered with millions of oriented hydrophobic microsetae that trap air (making them superhydrophobic), rest on the water along several centimetres, and a single leg can support about fifteen times the insect's weight (Gao and Jiang 2004). Water striders row with the middle legs, which push on the surface and shed small vortices that carry momentum backward (Hu and coauthors 2003), and they communicate by ripples.

The aquatic bugs breathe air in various ways: water scorpions through a siphon formed by two grooved filaments at the tip of the abdomen; backswimmers from an air bubble held by hairs on the ventral abdomen, which also serves as a hydrostatic organ; water boatmen with a plastron ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)). Giant water bugs (*Lethocerus*, up to 12 cm, eat fish and frogs) show paternal care: in Belostomatinae the female glues the eggs to the male's back, and he aerates them at the surface. The pygmy water boatman *Micronecta scholtzi* stridulates with its genitalia at up to about 99 dB, the loudest sound relative to body size known in animals (Sueur and coauthors 2011).

Bed bugs (*Cimex lectularius* in temperate regions, *C. hemipterus* in the tropics) feed on human blood at night and practise traumatic insemination: the male pierces the female's abdominal wall with his paramere and injects sperm into the hemocoel, where a special organ (the spermalege) reduces the damage. Their worldwide resurgence since the late 1990s is driven by insecticide resistance and travel. Assassin bugs are predators with a curved, three-segmented beak; the kissing bugs (Triatominae: *Triatoma*, *Rhodnius*, *Panstrongylus*), blood feeders on vertebrates in the Americas, transmit *Trypanosoma cruzi*, the agent of Chagas disease, in their feces ([7.11.20](/7.11.20)). *Rhodnius prolixus* was the experimental animal in which V. B. Wigglesworth discovered the hormonal control of molting and metamorphosis ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)).

Many Pentatomomorpha and Miridae are crop pests. In China, plant bugs such as *Apolygus lucorum* became the main pests of cotton after the adoption of Bt cotton in the late 1990s, because the reduced spraying against bollworms released them (Lu and coauthors 2010), an instructive case of secondary pest outbreak. The litchi stink bug *Tessaratoma papillosa* damages litchi and longan; the brown marmorated stink bug *Halyomorpha halys*, native to East Asia, has become a major invasive pest in North America and Europe. Bean bugs of the genus *Riptortus* (Alydidae) acquire their gut symbiont *Burkholderia* from the soil anew in each generation. Predatory bugs, including minute pirate bugs (*Orius*) and some mirids, are used in biological control in glasshouses, and the predatory stink bug *Arma chinensis* and the assassin bug *Sycanus croceovittatus* are mass-reared in China against caterpillars.

Several other heteropterans common in China are worth knowing. The rice bugs *Leptocorisa* (Alydidae) suck developing rice grains; the green stink bug *Nezara viridula* and the sloe bug *Dolycoris baccarum* feed on many crops; the pear lace bug *Stephanitis nashi* (Tingidae) whitens the leaves of pears; and the kudzu bug *Megacopta cribraria* (Plataspidae), whose mothers deposit capsules of symbiotic bacteria beside the eggs for the nymphs to eat, has spread from Asia to North America. In southern China the assassin bug *Triatoma rubrofasciata*, a cosmopolitan species associated with rats, can carry trypanosomes and occasionally bites people.

### 32.4.7. Hemiptera and People

More crop pests belong to Hemiptera than to any order except Lepidoptera, and their importance comes from three features shared by many species: small size and concealed feeding, parthenogenesis or short generation times that allow explosive increase, and the transmission of plant pathogens. The following table summarizes the main hemipteran pests of crops in China and the kind of damage they do:

| Crop | Main hemipteran pests | Damage |
|---|---|---|
| Rice | Brown planthopper *Nilaparvata lugens*, white-backed planthopper *Sogatella furcifera*, small brown planthopper *Laodelphax striatellus*, green rice leafhopper *Nephotettix cincticeps*, rice bugs *Leptocorisa* | Hopperburn; transmission of rice stripe virus, southern rice black-streaked dwarf virus (by *S. furcifera*) and rice dwarf virus; empty grains |
| Wheat and maize | Grain aphids *Sitobion miscanthi* and *Rhopalosiphum padi*, corn leaf aphid *R. maidis* | Sap removal; barley yellow dwarf virus |
| Cotton | Cotton aphid *Aphis gossypii*, mirid bugs *Apolygus lucorum* and *Adelphocoris* spp., whiteflies | Shedding of buds and bolls; honeydew contaminating fibre |
| Vegetables | Green peach aphid *Myzus persicae*, whiteflies *Bemisia tabaci* and *Trialeurodes vaporariorum* | Viruses (potyviruses, begomoviruses, criniviruses); sooty mould |
| Citrus | Asian citrus psyllid *Diaphorina citri*, scale insects, whiteflies | Huanglongbing (citrus greening); weakening of trees |
| Fruit and forest trees | San José scale, the pear psylla *Cacopsylla chinensis*, the spotted lanternfly, cicadas and spittlebugs | Weakening and death of branches; honeydew |
| Tea | Tea green leafhoppers, tea mosquito bugs (*Helopeltis*) | Scorching and distortion of shoots |

Hemipteran pests are a textbook case of insecticide resistance: *Myzus persicae* has evolved resistance to most classes of insecticides through gene amplification of detoxifying esterases and changes in target sites, and *Bemisia tabaci* MED, which displaced MEAM1 in China within a few years after 2003, did so partly because of its greater resistance to neonicotinoids. Integrated management therefore combines resistant varieties (the brown planthopper resistance genes *Bph* in rice, of which several were cloned in China), conservation of natural enemies such as spiders, mirid predators and parasitoids, reduction of virus sources, and selective insecticides.

The order also includes useful species. Lac insects (*Kerria lacca*), cultured on host trees in India, Thailand and southern China (Yunnan), secrete the resin from which shellac is made, used in varnishes, food glazes and coatings of pills. Cochineal insects (*Dactylopius coccus*), cultured on *Opuntia* cacti, yield carminic acid, the red food dye carmine (E120), formerly one of the most valuable exports of Mexico and Peru. The white wax scale *Ericerus pela* has provided insect white wax in China since the Tang dynasty. Gall aphids yield Chinese gallnuts (*wubeizi*), a source of tannin and gallic acid. Several cochineal species have been used successfully in the biological control of invasive *Opuntia* cacti, and aphid- and scale-feeding lady beetles were the first great successes of biological control ([7.17.33](/7.17.33)).

## Summary

- **Paraneoptera** share an enlarged cibarial pump with a bulging postclypeus, a rod-like lacinia, three or fewer tarsomeres and no cerci; thrips and hemipterans have stylet mouthparts.
- **Psocodea** (Definition 32.2.1) include free-living bark lice and booklice and the parasitic lice, which arose within them; Amblycera and Ischnocera (the old Mallophaga) chew, Anoplura suck blood. The human body louse transmits epidemic typhus, trench fever and relapsing fever.
- **Thysanoptera** (Definition 32.3.1) have an asymmetrical mouth cone with only the left mandible, fringed wings and quiescent propupal and pupal instars; Terebrantia have a saw-like ovipositor, Tubulifera a tubular abdominal tip. Thrips transmit tospoviruses.
- **Hemiptera** (Definition 32.4.1) feed through a labial beak sheathing four stylets, as sheath feeders on phloem or xylem or as cell-rupture feeders and predators; phloem and xylem feeders depend on bacterial endosymbionts and transmit plant viruses in non-persistent, semi-persistent, persistent circulative and persistent propagative modes.
- The four suborders differ in the origin of the beak and the forewings: Sternorrhyncha (psyllids, whiteflies, aphids, scale insects), Auchenorrhyncha (cicadas, spittlebugs, leafhoppers, treehoppers, planthoppers), Coleorrhyncha (moss bugs) and Heteroptera (true bugs, with hemelytra and scent glands).
- Aphids combine cyclical parthenogenesis, viviparity, telescoped generations, wing polyphenism and often host alternation; the short time to first reproduction gives them very high rates of increase.
- Water striders are supported by surface tension, about $2\gamma$ per unit length of contact on each side of a superhydrophobic leg.
- The order Homoptera is abolished because it is paraphyletic with respect to Heteroptera.

## Questions

1. Why are the quiescent "pupal" instars of thrips and whiteflies not regarded as homologous with the pupa of beetles or flies?

   **Answer** Thrips and whiteflies are paraneopterans, nested among hemimetabolous orders, and the Holometabola form a separate clade whose pupa evolved once in their common ancestor ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)). The quiescent stages of thrips, whiteflies and male scale insects arose independently in each lineage; in thrips the wings develop as external pads, not from internal imaginal discs as in Holometabola. The similarity is convergent: a non-feeding stage allows large remodelling between a sedentary or cryptic juvenile and a mobile adult.

2. A grower notices that a non-persistently transmitted potyvirus spreads in his pepper field although insecticides kill the aphids on the crop within hours. Explain why, and suggest a better control method.

   **Answer** In non-persistent transmission the virus is carried on the stylet tips and is acquired and inoculated in brief probes of a few seconds, often by winged aphids of species that do not colonize pepper and only test the plant before leaving. The virus is transmitted before an insecticide can act, and repellent effects of some insecticides may even increase probing and movement. Better methods aim at preventing probing or reducing virus sources: mineral oil sprays that interfere with retention on the stylets, reflective mulches or barrier crops that intercept aphids, insect-proof nets, removal of infected plants and weed hosts, and resistant varieties.

3. Using the Euler–Lotka equation, explain why halving the age at first reproduction raises the rate of increase of an aphid more than doubling its daily fecundity.

   **Answer** The intrinsic rate r solves $\sum_x \mathrm{e}^{-rx} m_x = 1$ (with survival 1). Offspring born at age $x$ are discounted by $\mathrm{e}^{-rx}$, so for large r only the earliest births matter. Doubling fecundity doubles every term and increases r roughly by $(\ln 2)/T$, where $T$ is the mean age of reproduction weighted by these discounted births (about the age at first birth for a fast-growing population); halving the age at first reproduction halves $T$ for every term and roughly doubles r. In the model of the figure, a 7-day development gives r ≈ 0.37 per day; halving it to 3.5 days raises r to about 0.62 per day, while doubling daily fecundity from 4 to 8 daughters raises it much less, to about 0.44 per day.

4. How can a true bug be distinguished from a cicada or leafhopper in the field, and from a beetle?

   **Answer** A true bug (Heteroptera) has a beak arising from the front of the head, usually visible as a segmented rod lying beneath the body, forewings that are hemelytra (thickened base, membranous tip) folded flat with overlapping tips, often a large triangular scutellum, and usually conspicuous four- or five-segmented antennae. Cicadas and leafhoppers have the beak arising from the back of the head, short bristle-like antennae and uniform wings held roof-like. Beetles have chewing mouthparts and elytra meeting in a straight midline suture without a membranous part.

5. A water strider of 15 mg rests on four legs whose contact lines with the water total 4 cm. Estimate whether surface tension can support it, and with what safety margin.

   **Answer** The weight is $15 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{kg} \times 9.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{N}$. The maximum supporting force of surface tension is about $2\gamma L = 2 \times 0.072 \times 0.04 \approx 5.8 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{N}$, about 40 times the weight. The insect therefore uses only a small fraction of the available support; the large margin lets it jump, row and carry prey without breaking through, provided the legs stay water-repellent.

6. The divergence of body lice from head lice has been used to date the origin of clothing. What assumptions does this argument require?

   **Answer** It assumes that body lice, which live and lay eggs in clothing, could only arise after people regularly wore clothes; that the divergence time of the two lineages can be estimated from their DNA with a reliable molecular clock calibrated, for example, by the human–chimpanzee split of their hosts' lice; that the two forms have not exchanged genes since (they in fact interbreed and body lice may arise repeatedly from head lice, so the estimate dates the oldest surviving body-louse lineage); and that the estimate refers to tailored or regularly worn clothing rather than occasional coverings.
