Social insects are a small fraction of insect species, perhaps 2% of the described species, but in many terrestrial habitats they make up most of the insect biomass: ants and termites together are thought to account for a third or more of the animal biomass of tropical rainforest. A colony of honey bees, army ants or fungus-growing termites behaves in many ways like a single organism, with reproductive and somatic parts, internal communication and homeostasis, which is why it has been called a superorganism. The rise of such colonies from solitary ancestors is one of the **major evolutionary transitions**, and the social insects are the best-studied case of altruism explained by **kin selection** ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)).

This chapter treats the biology of insect societies: the levels of sociality, the groups in which **eusociality** evolved, castes and how they develop, the life cycle and architecture of colonies, the genetic relationships within colonies and the conflicts they create, the organization of work, the scaling of colonies as superorganisms, and the dance language of honey bees. The general theory of **inclusive fitness** and **Hamilton's rule** is given in [7.10.16](/7.10.16) and is used here without derivation; the classification of the social Hymenoptera is in [7.17.34](/7.17.34) and that of termites in [7.17.31](/7.17.31). The behavioural mechanisms of communication, learning and decision making in insect societies, and the general theory of social behaviour, belong to animal behaviour ([7.18](/7.18)).

## 38.1. Levels of Sociality

### 38.1.1. From Solitary Life to Eusociality

Most insects never meet their offspring: a female lays eggs on food and leaves. Parental care has nevertheless evolved many times, and the forms of group living have been arranged in a series of levels (Michener; Wilson 1971):

| Level | Shared nest | Cooperative brood care | Reproductive division of labour | Overlap of generations | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solitary | No | No | No | No | Most insects; many solitary bees and wasps |
| Subsocial | Parent and young | Parent cares for its own young | No | Parent with immature young | Earwigs, burying beetles, passalid beetles, the wood roach *Cryptocercus*, treehoppers, shield bugs |
| Communal | Yes | No | No | No | Some mining bees (Andrenidae) nesting in one burrow, each provisioning her own cells |
| Quasisocial | Yes | Yes | No (all females lay) | No | Rare; some halictid and euglossine bees |
| Semisocial | Yes | Yes | Yes | No (same generation) | Some halictid bees, associations of *Polistes* foundresses |
| Eusocial | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Termites, ants, social wasps and bees, soldier aphids, gall thrips, an ambrosia beetle |

**Eusociality** combines the three criteria of the last row ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)). Two routes to it have been proposed. In the *subsocial route*, a mother caring for her young lives long enough for her daughters to remain and help her rear further offspring, so that the colony is a family; this is the route taken, as far as we know, by all eusocial insects. In the *parasocial route*, females of the same generation that share a nest (communal, quasisocial and semisocial associations) come to divide reproduction; this occurs in some bees and wasps, but the colonies with a permanent sterile caste all arose within families.

**Definition 38.1.1 (Caste; Primitive and Advanced Eusociality)** A **caste** is a set of individuals in a colony that are specialized for particular functions, distinguished by reproductive role (reproductives versus workers) and often by morphology, size or age. A society is **primitively eusocial** if queens and workers are similar in form and can exchange roles, caste being determined mainly by social interactions in adult life (paper wasps, sweat bees, bumble bees); it is **advanced** (highly) **eusocial** if queens and workers differ in morphology, caste is fixed during development, and workers cannot become fully functional queens (honey bees, stingless bees, most ants, termites of the Termitidae).

Advanced eusociality has been described as a point of no return: once workers have lost the ability to mate or to found colonies, and queens the ability to forage or to rear a brood alone, neither caste can return to solitary life. Reversals from primitive eusociality to solitary life are common in sweat bees, but none is known from advanced societies.

### 38.1.2. Independent Origins

Eusociality has evolved at least a dozen times in insects (and also in snapping shrimps of the genus *Synalpheus*, in two African mole-rats and, in a reduced form, in some spiders):

| Lineage | Origins | Age | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Termites (Blattodea: Termitoidae) | Once | Late Jurassic or Early Cretaceous, about 150 million years ago | Diploid; arose from wood-feeding cockroaches resembling *Cryptocercus*, its sister group, which lives in family groups in rotting logs and transfers gut symbionts by proctodeal feeding ([7.17.31](/7.17.31)) |
| Ants (Formicidae) | Once | Early Cretaceous, about 140–120 million years ago | All species eusocial (with secondarily workerless social parasites) |
| Vespid wasps (Polistinae + Vespinae; Stenogastrinae) | One or two | Cretaceous | Paper wasps and hornets |
| Corbiculate bees (honey bees, stingless bees, bumble bees) | One or two | Cretaceous | Orchid bees are solitary or communal |
| Halictid bees | At least two, with many reversals | Eocene and later | Facultative eusociality in some species |
| Allodapine and xylocopine bees | Several, weak | — | Small colonies |
| Crabronid wasps (*Microstigmus*) | Once | — | Small colonies in leaf nests |
| Aphids (Pemphigidae, Hormaphididae) | Several | — | First instar "soldiers" defend galls (Aoki 1977) |
| Gall thrips (*Kladothrips*) | Once or more | — | Soldiers with enlarged forelegs in phyllode galls of Australian *Acacia* (Crespi 1992) |
| Ambrosia beetle *Austroplatypus incompertus* | Once | — | Females tunnel in living eucalypt trunks, and unmated daughters help for years (Kent and Simpson 1992) |

The list shows common ecological features. Almost all eusocial insects live in a defensible, valuable nest that is costly to build or acquire: a gall, a tunnel in wood, a burrow, or a constructed comb. The "fortress defenders" (termites, gall aphids, gall thrips, the ambrosia beetle) live inside their food and need no foraging; their first specialized caste is the soldier, which defends the nest. The "life insurers" (Hymenoptera) forage outside the nest, and their workers ensure that the brood survives the death of any one forager or of the mother. In both cases, a helper at home can contribute to the reproduction of relatives more surely than it could reproduce alone.

## 38.2. The Major Groups

### 38.2.1. Termites

All termites are eusocial, and their castes differ from those of Hymenoptera in four ways: both sexes are represented in every caste; the colony is founded by a king and a queen, who mate repeatedly throughout life; the immature stages (nymphs and larvae) work in the colony; and caste is not fixed at hatching, because development is hemimetabolous and an individual can change course at several moults.

| Caste | Origin | Characters and function |
|---|---|---|
| Primary reproductives (king and queen) | Winged adults (alates) from a dispersal flight | Shed wings along basal sutures after the flight; a pair founds the colony in a royal chamber. The queen of higher termites becomes physogastric, her abdomen swelling with ovaries and fat body; a *Macrotermes* queen may be more than 10 cm long and lay tens of thousands of eggs a day for a decade or more |
| Supplementary (neotenic) reproductives | Nymphs with wing buds (nymphoid) or workers (ergatoid) that mature without becoming alates | Replace or supplement the primary pair; common in lower termites and *Reticulitermes* |
| Workers | In lower termites, pseudergates: late-instar nymphs or larvae that remain totipotent; in Termitidae and some others, true workers that have left the reproductive line | Forage, build, feed the other castes by trophallaxis, tend the brood and the fungus garden |
| Soldiers | Workers or nymphs that pass through a presoldier moult | Sterile; head enlarged, heavily sclerotized; mandibulate (biting or snapping), phragmotic (blocking tunnels with the head), or nasute (Nasutitermitinae, which spray a sticky, toxic terpenoid secretion from a frontal gland on a snout); fed by workers |

Caste differentiation in termites is controlled by **juvenile hormone** (JH) and by pheromones of the reproductives and soldiers. A high JH titre in a worker induces the presoldier moult; soldiers in turn suppress the production of further soldiers, which keeps their proportion at a level typical of the species (often 1–10%). Reproductives secrete inhibitory pheromones that prevent nymphs and workers from developing into neotenics; when a queen dies, the inhibition is lifted, and supplementary reproductives develop within weeks. In the Japanese subterranean termite *Reticulitermes speratus*, the queen pheromone has been identified as a blend of n-butyl n-butyrate and 2-methyl-1-butanol, which is also present on her eggs (Matsuura and coauthors 2010). The same species has an extraordinary system of queen succession: the primary queen produces her replacements, the secondary queens, parthenogenetically, so that they carry only her genome, while workers and alates are produced sexually, so that the colony maintains genetic diversity while the queen's genes are passed on in full (Matsuura and coauthors 2009).

Termites feed on lignocellulose (wood, litter, grass, humus, fungus). Lower termites digest it with the help of flagellate protists in the hindgut paunch, to which Chinese textbooks often point as a classic case of symbiosis ([7.11.20](/7.11.20)): termites whose protists have been killed by heat continue to eat wood but starve. Higher termites (Termitidae, about 70% of the species) have lost the protists and rely on bacteria; the subfamily Macrotermitinae of Africa and Asia cultivates the basidiomycete *Termitomyces* on combs of predigested plant material in its nest. The fruiting bodies of *Termitomyces*, which rise from termite mounds after the rains, are the prized *jizong* mushrooms of Yunnan and Sichuan. The mounds of *Macrotermes* in African savannas reach several metres in height. They do not act as the thermosiphon that Lüscher proposed in the 1960s; measurements in *M. michaelseni* showed that the daily heating and cooling of the mound wall drives a flow of air that reverses between day and night, flushing the nest of carbon dioxide (King, Ocko and Mahadevan 2015).

About 3000 species of termites are known, and in China the most important are the Formosan subterranean termite *Coptotermes formosanus*, a pest of buildings and trees in the south; species of *Reticulitermes*, the commonest termites of houses in the Yangtze basin; and the black-winged subterranean termite *Odontotermes formosanus*, a fungus grower whose extensive tunnels weaken embankments and dams, so that levees on the Yangtze and its tributaries are surveyed for termite nests. As decomposers, termites are among the main recyclers of dead plant matter in the tropics, and their gut archaea release methane, about 1–3% of global emissions.

### 38.2.2. Ants

Ants are the most diverse and ecologically dominant social insects ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)). An estimate based on 489 field studies gave about $2\times10^{16}$ ants on Earth, with a dry biomass of about 12 million tonnes of carbon, more than that of wild birds and wild mammals combined (Schultheiss and coauthors 2022). A colony consists of one or more queens, winged males (produced seasonally) and wingless, sterile or partly fertile female workers. In about a third of ant genera, workers are polymorphic, with size classes (minors, medias, majors) that differ allometrically: in the majors the head grows faster than the body, so that a major worker of *Pheidole* has a disproportionately large head with powerful mandibular muscles used for defence or for cracking seeds. In the leafcutter *Atta*, workers vary about 200-fold in mass, from the minims that tend the fungus garden to the soldiers that guard the trails.

Caste determination in most ants is environmental. Whether a female larva becomes a queen depends on how much and what it is fed, on temperature, on the age and condition of the queen, and on whether the larva reaches a critical size before a JH-sensitive period; queen pheromones from a fertile queen reduce the rearing of new queens. The development of worker subcastes is controlled in a similar way, by JH and by the growth of imaginal discs: in workers of *Pheidole*, which lack wings, rudimentary wing discs still form transiently, and their growth is required for the large head of soldiers, so that a vestige of the ancestral queen's developmental programme is used to build a new worker caste (Rajakumar and coauthors 2012). In some ants caste is genetically determined: in certain *Pogonomyrmex* harvester ants, two interbreeding lineages coexist, and daughters of crosses between lineages become workers while daughters from within a lineage become queens. In the fire ant *Solenopsis invicta*, a supergene on a "social chromosome" (Gp-9 region) determines whether a colony has one queen (monogyne) or many (polygyne): workers carrying the *Sb* variant kill queens that lack it (Wang and coauthors 2013).

Ant colonies are founded in two ways. In claustral founding, a mated queen sheds her wings, seals herself in a chamber and raises the first workers with food derived from the histolysis of her flight muscles and fat body ([7.17.25](/7.17.25)); in dependent founding, she returns to her nest or joins another, and colonies may bud. Many invasive ants (the Argentine ant *Linepithema humile*, the little fire ant, the yellow crazy ant) form supercolonies of many queens and nests whose workers do not fight each other; the European supercolony of the Argentine ant stretches some 6000 km along the coast from Italy to Portugal.

Two specializations of ants deserve particular mention. Army ants (*Eciton* in the Neotropics, *Dorylus* in Africa and southern Asia) have huge colonies, up to millions of workers in *Dorylus*, with wingless, permanently physogastric queens; they hunt in swarm raids and alternate, in *Eciton*, between nomadic and stationary phases synchronized with the development of the brood. Leafcutter ants (*Atta*, *Acromyrmex*) cut fresh leaves on which they grow a fungus (*Leucoagaricus gongylophorus*), fertilizing it with their faeces and weeding out alien fungi; the specialized parasite of the garden, the fungus *Escovopsis*, is suppressed by antibiotics produced by actinobacteria (*Pseudonocardia*) carried in cuticular crypts of the workers, a three- or four-partner mutualism (Currie and coauthors 1999). Agriculture by ants evolved once, about 55–65 million years ago, in the New World attines.

### 38.2.3. Honey Bees

A colony of the western honey bee *Apis mellifera* contains one queen, 20,000–60,000 workers and, in spring and summer, several hundred to a few thousand drones. Queens develop in 16 days from egg to adult, workers in 21 and drones in 24. Workers and queens both develop from fertilized eggs; drones from unfertilized ones (**haplodiploidy**).

| Caste | Development and diet | Morphology | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Queen | Fertilized egg in a large, vertical queen cell; fed royal jelly throughout larval life | Long abdomen with large ovaries (about 150–180 ovarioles per ovary) and a spermatheca; smooth, curved sting; no pollen baskets; reduced hypopharyngeal and wax glands | Lays up to about 1500–2000 eggs a day; produces queen pheromone |
| Worker | Fertilized egg in a worker cell; fed brood food (worker jelly) and, from the third day, a diet with honey and pollen | Few ovarioles (2–12 per ovary), no functional spermatheca; pollen baskets, wax glands, hypopharyngeal glands; barbed sting | Cleaning, nursing, building, guarding, foraging (Section 38.4) |
| Drone | Unfertilized egg in a larger drone cell | Large eyes meeting at the top of the head, long antennae with more sensilla; no sting | Mates with queens on mating flights; dies at mating; expelled from the colony in autumn |

**Definition 38.2.1 (Royal Jelly)** **Royal jelly** is the protein-rich secretion of the hypopharyngeal and mandibular glands of young worker honey bees (nurses), fed in abundance to the larvae destined to become queens; its major components are the major royal jelly proteins, sugars, lipids including 10-hydroxy-2-decenoic acid, and vitamins.

How a diet makes a queen has been studied intensively. Larvae from worker cells transferred into queen cells before about three days of age develop into queens, which is how beekeepers rear queens; later transfers produce intermediates. The difference lies in both the quantity of food, which affects insulin/TOR nutrient signalling and the JH titre, and its composition: worker brood food contains less sugar and, after the third day, pollen and honey whose phenolic acid p-coumaric acid down-regulates genes for ovary development (Mao, Schuler and Berenbaum 2015). Two widely cited mechanisms have proved less secure than they first appeared. Silencing the DNA methyltransferase gene *Dnmt3* in newly hatched larvae caused about 70% of them to develop as queen-like adults (Kucharski and coauthors 2008), suggesting that **DNA methylation** fixes caste; but later work found small, partly inconsistent differences in methylation between castes, and the causal role of methylation in caste development remains debated. A single protein of royal jelly, royalactin, was reported to induce queen development (Kamakura 2011), but the effect could not be reproduced (Buttstedt and coauthors 2016). The current view is that caste depends on a network of nutrient-sensing pathways responding to the whole diet rather than on a single "queen substance" in food.

The queen controls the colony chemically. Her mandibular glands produce the queen mandibular pheromone, a blend including (*E*)-9-oxodec-2-enoic acid (9-ODA), which workers lick from her body and spread through the colony by contact and by trophallaxis, the exchange of food mouth to mouth. It inhibits workers from rearing new queens and from activating their ovaries, attracts drones to the queen on mating flights, and keeps a swarm together. Chinese textbooks call it the "caste-regulating pheromone". Its effect is reinforced by brood pheromone from the larvae. When the queen dies or the colony grows so large that the pheromone is too diluted, workers build queen cells.

Honey bee queens mate in the air, at drone congregation areas, with on average about 12 drones (range about 6–30), during one or a few mating flights in the first weeks of adult life; the sperm is stored in the spermatheca, about 5–6 million cells, and used over the queen's life of two to five years. The drone's endophallus everts explosively during mating and breaks off in the queen, and the drone dies. Colonies reproduce by swarming: in late spring the old queen leaves with about half of the workers, and a daughter queen takes over the old nest. The swarm hangs as a cluster while scout bees search for a cavity, report candidate sites by dancing, and reach a decision by building up a sufficient number of scouts at one site (a quorum of about 20–30 bees), which triggers the swarm's departure (Seeley 2010). Workers hold the brood nest at 34–35 °C by shivering their flight muscles and by fanning and evaporative cooling; in winter, the colony clusters and survives on stored honey.

The eastern honey bee *Apis cerana*, native to China, differs from *A. mellifera* in smaller colonies, frequent absconding, the ability to forage at lower temperatures, resistance to the mite *Varroa destructor*, and the heat-balling defence against hornets ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)). Chinese beekeeping is based on both species, with *A. mellifera* introduced in the early 20th century now kept in most commercial apiaries.

### 38.2.4. Social Wasps and Other Bees

Paper wasps (*Polistes*) are the model of primitive eusociality. In spring, a colony is founded by one female or by several cofoundresses, often sisters, which fight and establish a dominance hierarchy; the dominant female (queen) lays most of the eggs, eats eggs laid by subordinates and does little foraging, while the subordinates forage. The hierarchy is maintained by aggression and, in *Polistes dominula*, partly by visual badges: variable black spots on the clypeus correlate with body size and dominance in some populations (Tibbetts and Dale 2004), though not in all, and *Polistes fuscatus* recognizes individual faces, a capacity unusual in insects (Sheehan and Tibbetts 2011). Daughters emerging in summer usually become workers; in autumn males and future queens are produced, the queens mate and hibernate, and the colony dies. Hornets and yellowjackets (Vespinae) form larger annual colonies in envelope-covered paper nests, with queens larger than workers and fixed caste, an intermediate level of eusociality.

Bumble bees (*Bombus*) have annual colonies of tens to a few hundred workers founded by a single hibernated queen, who builds wax pots of nectar and pollen and incubates her first brood by pressing her warm abdomen against it. Stingless bees (Meliponini; about 600 species in the tropics, including *Tetragonula* and *Lepidotrigona* in southern China) have perennial colonies of hundreds to tens of thousands of workers, mass-provision cells and seal them, have queens that cannot found colonies alone, and swarm by gradual transfer of workers and materials to a new nest. In sweat bees (Halictidae), eusociality can vary within a species: *Halictus rubicundus* and *Lasioglossum calceatum* are eusocial in warm places with a long season, where a first brood of workers can be raised before reproductives, and solitary at high latitudes or altitudes, which shows how readily the social phenotype responds to the length of the season.

### 38.2.5. Soldier Aphids, Thrips and Beetles

Some gall-forming aphids of the families Pemphigidae and Hormaphididae produce soldiers: first-instar or second-instar nymphs with enlarged forelegs and sharp frontal horns that attack predators (such as syrphid larvae) and do not develop further, or develop slowly (Aoki 1977). Because aphids in a gall are a clone produced parthenogenetically, the soldiers are genetically identical to the individuals they defend ($r = 1$), and soldiers of some species also clean the gall of honeydew and repair breaches with their body fluids, which clot and harden. Soldier thrips in Australian *Acacia* galls are similar, but they are haplodiploid and inbred, with relatedness of about 0.75 or more within a gall. The ambrosia beetle *Austroplatypus incompertus* bores tunnels in living eucalypts where colonies last more than 30 years; the unmated daughters of the founding female lose the terminal tarsal segments that they would need to leave and found nests, and work for years maintaining the gallery and the fungus gardens. These cases show that eusociality does not require haplodiploidy; what they share with the Hymenoptera is high relatedness within the group and a valuable, defensible home.

## 38.3. Relatedness and Conflict

### 38.3.1. Relatedness in Hymenopteran Colonies

In haplodiploid Hymenoptera a female inherits her father's whole haploid genome, so full sisters share all of their paternal genes and half of their maternal genes, and their relatedness is $\tfrac34$ ([Example 16.3.1](/7.10.16#ex-16.3.1) in 7.10.16). The relatedness of a female worker to other colony members is:

| Relative | Relatedness $r$ (worker to relative) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mother (queen) | $\tfrac12$ | |
| Full sister | $\tfrac34$ | Same father |
| Half sister | $\tfrac14$ | Different fathers |
| Brother (queen's son) | $\tfrac14$ | Brother developed from an unfertilized egg |
| Own son or daughter | $\tfrac12$ | |
| Full nephew (full sister's son) | $\tfrac38$ | |
| Half nephew | $\tfrac18$ | |

If the queen mates with $k$ males who contribute equally to the offspring, two workers are full sisters with probability $1/k$ and half sisters otherwise, so the average relatedness among workers is

$$
\bar r = \frac{1}{k}\cdot\frac34 + \left(1 - \frac1k\right)\frac14 = \frac14 + \frac{1}{2k}. \tag{38.1}
$$

With single mating ($k = 1$), $\bar r = \tfrac34$; with $k = 2$, $\tfrac12$, no more than to the worker's own offspring; with the twelve or so matings of a honey bee queen, about 0.29. Multiple queens reduce it further. Hamilton's haplodiploidy hypothesis, that the high relatedness of sisters predisposes Hymenoptera to eusociality, therefore cannot apply to the many species with multiply mated queens.

B: the average relatedness among workers as a function of the number of males with which the queen mated, Eq. (38.1), with equal paternity; the dashed line marks r = 1/2, the relatedness of a female to her own offspring. Values for honey bees (about 12 matings) and for many ants and primitively eusocial bees and wasps (one mating) are marked. The comparative evidence points in another direction. When Hughes and coauthors (2008) mapped mating frequency onto the phylogenies of eusocial Hymenoptera, they found that all ancestral states at the origins of eusociality were single mating, and that multiple mating arose only in lineages that already had advanced eusociality and sterile workers, such as honey bees, *Atta* leafcutters, army ants and *Vespula*. This supports the *monogamy hypothesis* (Boomsma 2007): with a monogamous mother, a female is related to her full siblings exactly as to her own offspring on average ($\tfrac12$, averaging sisters and brothers), so that any ecological advantage of helping, however small, tips the balance toward staying at home. Lifetime monogamy, rather than haplodiploidy, is the common factor shared by the eusocial Hymenoptera and termites (whose king and queen pair for life). Haplodiploidy may still have helped by allowing sex-ratio adjustment.

### 38.3.2. Conflicts Within Colonies

Kin selection predicts cooperation but also conflicts, wherever the inclusive fitness interests of queen and workers differ. The best-documented ones concern sex allocation and the production of males.

- *Sex allocation.* A queen is equally related to her sons and daughters ($\tfrac12$), and her optimal investment ratio in reproductive females to males is 1 : 1. A worker in a colony with a singly mated queen is related to sisters by $\tfrac34$ and to brothers by $\tfrac14$; if she controls allocation, the stable investment ratio is 3 : 1 in favour of females (Trivers and Hare 1976). Investment ratios in monogynous ant species are female-biased, often near 3 : 1, as the workers' interest predicts. In the ant *Formica exsecta*, colonies differ in whether their queen mated once or twice: workers in colonies of singly mated queens, which are more related to their sisters, raised mainly females, and those of doubly mated queens raised mainly males (Sundström and coauthors 1996), and the workers achieved this by eliminating male larvae.
- *Male production.* Workers of many species have ovaries and can lay unfertilized eggs that develop into males. A worker is related by $\tfrac12$ to her own sons, $\tfrac14$ to brothers, and $\tfrac38$ or $\tfrac18$ to nephews. If the queen mated multiply, a worker is more related to her brothers than to the average worker's son ($\tfrac14$ against about $\tfrac18$ to $\tfrac38$ averaged), and workers are expected to destroy each other's eggs, **worker policing** (Ratnieks 1988; [7.10.16](/7.10.16)). In honey bees, workers eat almost all worker-laid eggs, which they recognize by the absence of a chemical mark laid by the queen, and only about 0.1% of males are workers' sons, although some workers' ovaries are active. In colonies of species whose queens mate once, workers do produce many males.
- *Queen replacement.* When several new queens emerge, they fight, and the first queen to emerge kills her rivals in their cells; workers influence which queen survives.

## 38.4. The Organization of Work

### 38.4.1. Division of Labour

Within the worker caste, tasks are divided by morphology (in ants and termites with polymorphic workers) and by age. In honey bees, a worker passes through a sequence of tasks during her adult life of about five to six weeks in summer (age polyethism): cleaning cells (days 1–3), nursing larvae with secretions of the hypopharyngeal glands (about days 3–12), building comb with wax from the abdominal wax glands, processing nectar and storing pollen (about days 12–18), guarding the entrance (about days 18–21), and foraging until death. The sequence is flexible. If young bees are removed, foragers revert to nursing and regrow their hypopharyngeal glands; if foragers are removed, young bees begin foraging early. The transition is controlled by a mutual inhibition between vitellogenin, a yolk protein made in the fat body, and JH: nurses have high vitellogenin and low JH, foragers the reverse, and the inhibitory pheromone ethyl oleate, made by foragers and passed to nurses by trophallaxis, delays their maturation. Winter bees, which do not rear brood, have high vitellogenin and live for months.

A simple model captures how colonies allocate workers to tasks without central control. In the *response threshold model* (Bonabeau, Theraulaz and Deneubourg 1996), a stimulus $s$ associated with a task (the hunger signals of larvae, the temperature of the nest) rises when the task is neglected and falls when it is performed; a worker with threshold $\theta$ performs the task with probability

$$
P(s) = \frac{s^n}{s^n + \theta^n},
$$

where $n$ sets the steepness of the response. If workers differ in their thresholds, because of age, size, genotype (different patrilines in a multiply mated colony) or experience, low-threshold individuals become specialists at a task, and others join only when the stimulus rises further. The model explains why removing specialists causes others to switch, and why genetically diverse colonies, such as those of multiply mated honey bee queens, regulate nest temperature more precisely: patrilines differ in their thresholds for fanning, so that the number of fanning bees increases gradually rather than all at once (Jones and coauthors 2004). Mattila and Seeley (2007) showed that colonies headed by multiply mated queens build comb, forage and grow faster than colonies of singly mated queens, a benefit of genetic diversity that helps to explain the evolution of multiple mating in honey bees.

### 38.4.2. Nests

Insect nests are built without plans by local rules: each worker responds to the structure it encounters, so that the work in progress directs further work, a mechanism called stigmergy. The hexagonal cells of honey bee comb, made of wax secreted from glands on the underside of the abdomen, are an economical solution to storing the maximum volume with the minimum wax; the bees build circular cells that become hexagonal partly because the warm wax flows into the shape that minimizes surface, and partly by the workers' shaping. Comb hangs vertically, with cells tilted upward by about 13° so that nectar does not run out. Paper wasps chew wood fibres and plant stems into paper; *Vespa* nests may contain more than ten combs inside an envelope; termite mounds of *Macrotermes* contain fungus gardens, royal chamber, ventilation channels and a thick wall; ant nests range from a few chambers in soil to the leaf nests of weaver ants and the excavations of *Atta*, which can reach 8 m depth and move 40 tonnes of soil.

## 38.5. The Colony as a Superorganism

**Definition 38.5.1 (Superorganism)** A **superorganism** is a colony of social insects in which the reproductive and the non-reproductive (worker) castes are analogous to the germ line and the soma of an organism, so that the colony, rather than the individual, is the unit that reproduces and on which selection acts most directly.

Wheeler (1911) introduced the comparison, and Hölldobler and Wilson (2009) developed it: in advanced eusocial colonies, workers cannot reproduce independently and conflicts are suppressed, so that the colony functions as a single organism with parts specialized for reproduction, defence, food processing and thermoregulation. The comparison can be made quantitative. In individual organisms, metabolic rate scales with body mass $M$ as $B = B_0 M^{b}$ with $b \approx \tfrac34$ (**Kleiber's law**, [7.14.30](/7.14.30)). If a colony were merely a collection of independent workers, its metabolic rate should be proportional to the number of workers and scale with colony mass with an exponent of 1. Measurements of whole colonies show instead that colony metabolic rate scales hypometrically, with exponents near three quarters: in the harvester ant *Pogonomyrmex californicus*, the exponent for colonies of different sizes was about 0.75–0.8, while workers isolated from their colonies respired in proportion to their mass (Waters and coauthors 2010); across colonies of ants, bees, wasps and termites, colony metabolic rate, lifetime and growth scale with colony mass with exponents similar to those of single organisms (Hou and coauthors 2010).

Why larger colonies use less energy per worker is not settled. A larger proportion of workers in large colonies is inactive at any moment, and the activity of workers decreases with colony size; the transport of food through the colony by trophallaxis is analogous to the distribution network that has been invoked to explain the exponent in organisms. Whatever the mechanism, the convergence of scaling laws supports the view that colonies are integrated systems.

## 38.6. Communication

### 38.6.1. Chemical Communication

Social insects communicate mainly by **pheromones** ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)), produced by a large number of exocrine glands. An ant may have more than 20 glands, from the mandibular glands in the head (alarm pheromones) to the Dufour's and poison glands associated with the sting (trail and recruitment pheromones) and the pygidial and sternal glands of the gaster. Fire ant workers that find food lay a trail from the sting with a Dufour's gland secretion that evaporates within minutes, so that trails to exhausted food sources fade; a trail is reinforced as long as returning foragers are successful, a positive feedback by which the colony concentrates its foragers on the best food sources, a principle copied in "ant colony optimization" algorithms. Nestmates recognize each other by blends of cuticular hydrocarbons, which are shared within the colony by grooming and trophallaxis and learned by each worker; an intruder with a different blend is attacked. Termites lay trails from a sternal gland, and soldiers of many species alarm others by drumming their heads against the gallery walls, a vibrational signal.

### 38.6.2. The Waggle Dance

K. von Frisch discovered that a honey bee forager returning from a rich food source communicates its direction and distance to nestmates by a dance on the vertical comb in the dark hive (Nobel Prize 1973).

**Definition 38.6.1 (Waggle Dance)** The **waggle dance** of honey bees is a repeated figure-of-eight run in which the dancer moves straight ahead while waggling her abdomen from side to side (the waggle run) and then circles back alternately to the left and right to start again. The angle of the waggle run relative to the upward vertical on the comb equals the angle between the direction of the food source and the direction of the sun's azimuth, and the duration of the waggle run increases with the distance to the source.

The encoding transfers a horizontal angle relative to the sun into an angle relative to gravity: a run straight up means "fly toward the sun", straight down "away from the sun", and a run 60° to the right of vertical means "60° to the right of the sun". Because the sun's azimuth moves during the day, dancers correct their dances as the sun moves, using their time-compensated sun compass, and when the sky is overcast they use the polarization pattern of the sky ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)). The dwarf honey bee *Apis florea*, which builds a single comb in the open, dances on the horizontal top of the comb and points the run directly toward the food. Distance is coded by the duration of the waggle phase, roughly a second for each kilometre, with differences between subspecies; for close sources, the waggle runs become so short that the dance looks like a circle, the "round dance" of older descriptions, which current work treats as the short-distance form of the same dance. The distance information comes from optic flow: bees trained to fly through a narrow tunnel lined with a patterned wall, which creates a large image motion over a short distance, signalled distances several times longer than the real flight (Srinivasan and coauthors 2000).

![The encoding of the waggle dance. Left: in the field, the food source lies at an angle α to the right of the sun's azimuth as seen from the hive. Right: on the vertical comb inside the hive, the dancer performs waggle runs at the same angle α to the right of the upward vertical, returning alternately left and right (figure-of-eight). The dance has been tested in several ways. Recruits search near the indicated location, and their searches spread in proportion to the distance; robot dancers moved in the hive recruited bees to the indicated directions; and recruits tracked by harmonic radar flew in the direction indicated by the dance before searching locally (Riley and coauthors 2005). Dance information is combined with odour carried by the dancer and with the recruit's own memory, and its value varies with the landscape: in experiments in which dances were disoriented by placing hives horizontally under diffuse light, colonies foraged less efficiently in tropical habitats with patchy, rich food sources but not in temperate habitats with widespread forage (Dornhaus and Chittka 2004). Stingless bees recruit by other means, many by depositing scent marks along the route or by guiding nestmates. The neural basis, learning and evolution of dance communication are treated in animal behaviour ([7.18](/7.18)).

## 38.7. Social Parasites and Guests

The resources of social insect colonies, stored food and brood guarded by workers and organized by shared signals, have attracted parasites that break into the communication system.

- *Inquilines* are social parasites that live permanently in the host colony, often without a worker caste: queens of the ant *Teleutomyrmex schneideri* ride on the host queen and are fed by host workers. By Emery's rule, social parasites are often close relatives of their hosts, sometimes sister species, which suggests that some arose within the host species by sympatric speciation.
- *Slave-making ants* (*Polyergus*, some *Formica*, *Temnothorax*) raid nests of related species and carry off pupae, which become workers in the raider's nest; *Polyergus* workers have sickle-shaped mandibles for fighting and cannot feed themselves.
- *Cuckoo bumble bees* (subgenus *Psithyrus* of *Bombus*) have no workers; the female enters a colony of a host bumble bee, kills or subdues the queen, and has her own eggs reared by the host workers.
- *Myrmecophiles*, guests of ants, include beetles (rove beetles of *Lomechusa*, paussine ground beetles), the caterpillars of lycaenid butterflies such as *Phengaris*, which mimic the brood odour and sounds of *Myrmica* ants and are carried into the nest, where they are fed or eat the brood ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)), crickets, silverfish and mites; many mimic the hydrocarbons of the host colony.
- *Honey bee parasites* include the mite *Varroa destructor* ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)), the small hive beetle and the Cape honey bee *Apis mellifera capensis*, whose workers lay diploid eggs that develop into females by thelytoky and can take over colonies of other subspecies as social parasites.

## 38.8. The Success of Social Insects

Social insects dominate many ecosystems. Ants are the main predators of other small arthropods in many habitats, turn more soil than earthworms in some tropical regions, and disperse seeds ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)); termites decompose a large fraction of dead wood and litter in tropical forests and savannas; bees pollinate most flowering plants; leafcutter ants are the dominant herbivores of the Neotropics. The advantages of colonies are those of organisms over single cells: division of labour among specialists, parallel rather than serial work (a colony can forage, defend and build at the same time, and a task is completed even if one worker dies), homeostasis of the nest environment, and a much longer lifetime of the colony than of its workers. Social insects also illustrate the costs. Dense colonies of related individuals are vulnerable to pathogens, against which they have evolved "social immunity": grooming, removal of infected brood, antimicrobial secretions such as those of the metapleural gland of ants, the use of plant resins (propolis) in bee nests, and the confinement of sick workers.

## Summary

- Social levels range from subsocial (parental care) through communal, quasisocial and semisocial to eusocial (cooperative brood care, overlapping generations, reproductive division of labour). Castes (Definition 38.1.1) are fixed in advanced eusocial insects and flexible in primitively eusocial ones.
- Eusociality evolved at least a dozen times: termites, ants, vespid wasps, several bee lineages, a crabronid wasp, gall aphids, gall thrips and an ambrosia beetle. All live in valuable, defensible nests; "fortress defenders" began with soldiers, "life insurers" with foraging workers.
- Termite castes include both sexes and are developmentally flexible: primary and supplementary reproductives, pseudergates or true workers, and soldiers; JH and pheromones regulate them. Macrotermitinae cultivate *Termitomyces*.
- Ant workers are often polymorphic; caste is environmental in most species (food, JH, rudimentary wing discs in *Pheidole* soldiers) and genetic in some (*Pogonomyrmex*, the social chromosome of fire ants).
- Honey bee queens develop on royal jelly (Definition 38.2.1); caste depends on nutrient signalling and diet composition; the roles of DNA methylation and royalactin are contested. Queen pheromone, multiple mating and swarming organize the colony.
- Under haplodiploidy full sisters are related by 3/4, but average worker relatedness is 1/4 + 1/(2k) with k matings (Eq. 38.1). Ancestral monogamy, not haplodiploidy alone, characterizes the origins of eusociality. Conflicts over sex allocation (3 : 1 under worker control) and male production (worker policing) follow from relatedness asymmetries.
- Work is divided by morphology and age; response threshold models explain flexible allocation; genetic diversity improves colony homeostasis.
- Colonies are superorganisms (Definition 38.5.1) whose metabolism scales with colony mass with exponents near 3/4, as in organisms.
- The waggle dance (Definition 38.6.1) encodes the direction of food relative to the sun as an angle relative to gravity and distance as waggle duration, measured by optic flow.
- Social parasites and guests exploit colony communication; social insects dominate many ecosystems through division of labour, parallel work and homeostasis, and defend themselves against disease by social immunity.

## Questions

1. A honey bee queen has mated with 15 drones of equal paternity. What is the average relatedness between two of her workers? Would a worker gain more inclusive fitness by rearing sisters or her own daughters, other things equal? Why, then, are honey bee workers sterile?

   **Answer** By Eq. (38.1), $\bar r = \tfrac14 + \tfrac{1}{2\cdot15} = 0.25 + 0.033 \approx 0.28$, which is less than her relatedness to her own offspring ($\tfrac12$). By relatedness alone, rearing her own daughters would be favoured. But honey bee workers cannot mate (they lack a functional spermatheca), so they could only produce sons, and their sterility evolved in an ancestor with a monogamous queen; multiple mating arose afterwards, in an already obligately eusocial lineage in which workers had lost the capacity for independent reproduction. Once workers are sterile, multiple mating is favoured by the benefits of genetic diversity (disease resistance, homeostasis), and worker policing keeps workers from laying male eggs, since a worker is more related to her brothers than to the sons of other workers.

2. Explain how a forager indicates a food source 2 km away, 40° to the left of the sun, and how the dance would change if the forager danced three hours later to the same source.

   **Answer** On the vertical comb she performs waggle runs directed 40° to the left of straight up, with a waggle phase lasting about 2 s (with the approximate calibration of 1 s per km), returning alternately to the left and right between runs. Three hours later the sun's azimuth will have moved (on average about 15° per hour, faster near noon in summer), so the angle between food and sun changes by the same amount; if the sun has moved 45° away from the food, she will dance about 85° to the left of vertical. The duration of the waggle run stays the same, because the distance has not changed.

3. Why does the presence of soldiers in clonal aphid galls and in diploid termites argue against the view that haplodiploidy is necessary for eusociality? What do these groups share with eusocial Hymenoptera?

   **Answer** Soldier aphids are produced parthenogenetically and are genetically identical to the colony members they defend, and termites are diploid; neither has the relatedness asymmetry of haplodiploidy, yet both have a sterile caste. What they share with the eusocial Hymenoptera is high relatedness among colony members (a clone; a family founded by a lifelong monogamous pair; the offspring of a singly mated queen) and an ecological situation in which helping is effective: a valuable, defensible home (gall, wood, nest) that can be defended cheaply by a few individuals against predators, and in which independent reproduction is risky. Hamilton's rule can be satisfied with $r = \tfrac12$ or $r = 1$ if the benefit-to-cost ratio is high; haplodiploidy is neither necessary nor sufficient.

4. In a monogynous ant species with a singly mated queen, the investment ratio of new queens to males is found to be 3 : 1 by mass. What does this suggest about who controls sex allocation? How could you test it further?

   **Answer** The queen's optimum is 1 : 1, because she is equally related to her daughters and sons; the workers' optimum is 3 : 1, because they are related by $\tfrac34$ to sisters and $\tfrac14$ to brothers. A 3 : 1 ratio suggests that workers control allocation, for example by eliminating male larvae or by feeding female larvae to become queens. Tests: compare colonies within a population that differ in relatedness (queens that mated once versus twice, or colonies with one versus several queens) and check whether colonies with lower relatedness asymmetry produce relatively more males (as in *Formica exsecta*); track the primary sex ratio of eggs laid by the queen and the secondary ratio among reared reproductives to see where the bias arises.

5. (Open question) Colony metabolic rate scales with colony mass with an exponent near 3/4. Suggest mechanisms for this pattern and an experiment that could distinguish them.

   **Answer** Possible mechanisms: a higher proportion of inactive workers in large colonies; lower activity of workers when they are crowded or when tasks are fewer per worker; constraints of the internal distribution of food (trophallactic networks) or of nest ventilation that limit the supply rate per worker; and a change of colony composition with size (more brood, more stored food, larger workers). An experiment could manipulate colony size by splitting colonies and measuring respiration of the whole colony, of groups of workers removed from the colony, and of individuals, while recording activity with video tracking; if per-worker metabolic rate in large colonies is restored when workers are isolated, the effect is social (behavioural); if activity-adjusted metabolism per worker still declines with colony size, a supply constraint is more likely. Altering colony composition (adding brood) tests the composition hypothesis.
