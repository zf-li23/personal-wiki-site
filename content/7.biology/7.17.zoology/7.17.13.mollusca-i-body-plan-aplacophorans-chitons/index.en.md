Molluscs — snails, slugs, clams, mussels, chitons, squid and octopuses — are the second largest animal phylum after the arthropods, with about 73,000 described living species and a comparable number of fossil species. They range from millimetre-long interstitial worms covered with spicules to the colossal squid, and they live in the deep sea, on rocky shores, in rivers and lakes, and on land from deserts to mountain tops. Their fossil record, built on hard shells, is one of the best in the animal kingdom and has made them central to palaeontology and evolutionary theory.

On the animal tree, molluscs are spiralian protostomes within the **Lophotrochozoa**, together with annelids, nemerteans and the lophophorates ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.5.5)). They develop by **spiral cleavage**, form their mesoderm and body cavities by **schizocoely**, and many have a **trochophore** larva ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)). What sets them apart is a body plan of unusual plasticity: a few shared components — mantle, shell, foot, radula and gills — have been modified into eight classes so different in appearance that their unity was recognized only in the nineteenth century.

This chapter describes the molluscan body plan (§13.1), the phylogeny of the phylum and its fossil origins (§13.2), and the three small classes of aplacophorans (§13.3), chitons (§13.4) and monoplacophorans (§13.5). A model of shell coiling (§13.6) prepares the treatment of the largest class, the gastropods (§13.7), with torsion, the modern classification and representative species. The chapter ends with a comparison with the traditional classification (§13.8). Bivalves, tusk shells and cephalopods follow in [7.17.14](/7.17.14).

## 13.1. The Molluscan Body Plan

**Definition 13.1.1 (Mollusca)** The **Mollusca** (molluscs) are unsegmented, bilaterally symmetrical (or secondarily asymmetrical) spiralian protostomes whose dorsal body wall forms a mantle that secretes calcareous spicules, plates or a shell and overhangs a mantle cavity containing the gills (ctenidia); whose ventral body wall forms a muscular foot; whose mouth contains a radula; whose coelom is reduced to the pericardium, the cavities of the gonads and the nephridia; and whose main body cavity is a hemocoel.

Four regions can be distinguished in most molluscs: a head with the mouth and sense organs, a muscular foot, a visceral mass containing most of the internal organs, and the mantle that covers the visceral mass. Head and foot together are often called the head-foot. The figure below shows a generalized mollusc and how the classes have modified it.

(A) A generalized mollusc: a dorsal shell secreted by the mantle covers the visceral mass; the mantle overhangs a posterior mantle cavity containing a pair of ctenidia, into which the anus and the nephridiopores open; the heart lies in the pericardium, the remnant of the coelom; the radula lies in the buccal cavity; the foot forms a creeping sole. (B) How the classes modify the plan: aplacophorans (worm-like, spicules instead of a shell, foot reduced to a ridge in a groove or lost); chitons (eight plates, ctenidia in grooves along the sides of the foot); monoplacophorans (a cap-shaped shell, repeated gills); gastropods (visceral mass coiled and twisted by torsion, mantle cavity anterior); bivalves (two valves, laterally compressed, head reduced); scaphopods (tubular shell open at both ends); cephalopods (chambered shell, foot transformed into arms and funnel)](./plots/mollusc-body-plans.py)

### 13.1.1. Mantle and Mantle Cavity

**Definition 13.1.2 (Mantle, Mantle Cavity)** The **mantle** (pallium) is the dorsal body wall of a mollusc, a fold of epidermis, connective tissue and muscle whose outer epithelium secretes the shell or the spicules. The **mantle cavity** is the space between the overhanging mantle and the body, open to the outside, in which lie the ctenidia, the osphradia and the openings of the anus, nephridia and gonoducts.

The mantle cavity is the respiratory chamber and the outlet of the body. Cilia on the gills and the mantle drive a current of water through it: in the plesiomorphic arrangement water enters at the sides, passes over the gills, and leaves after picking up faeces, urine and gametes, so that the outgoing current carries waste away from the incoming one. Two sense and gland organs accompany the gills. The *osphradium*, a patch of sensory epithelium at the entrance of the incoming current, samples the water and is chemosensory and possibly mechanosensory; it is largest in predatory and scavenging snails, which track prey by smell. The *hypobranchial gland* on the roof of the cavity secretes mucus that binds sediment; in murex snails its secretion is the source of Tyrian purple, the dye of antiquity. In land snails the mantle cavity has become a lung (§13.7.5), and in cephalopods its muscular wall pumps water for jet propulsion ([7.17.14](/7.17.14)).

### 13.1.2. The Shell

The shell is secreted by the mantle as an extracellular mineralized structure. In conchiferan molluscs (§13.2) it has three layers:

| Layer | Composition | Secreted by |
|---|---|---|
| Periostracum | Thin organic layer of quinone-tanned protein (conchiolin), coloured, resistant to acid | A groove between the outer and middle folds of the mantle margin |
| Prismatic layer | Columns of calcium carbonate (calcite or aragonite) set vertically in an organic matrix | The outer fold of the mantle margin |
| Inner layer: nacreous (mother-of-pearl) or crossed-lamellar | Thin tablets of aragonite in sheets separated by organic matrix ("brick and mortar"), or crossed lamellae | The whole outer epithelium of the mantle |

The margin of the mantle has three folds with different functions: the outer fold secretes the periostracum and the prismatic layer, the middle fold is sensory (in scallops it bears the eyes), and the inner fold is muscular and controls the flow of water. The first two layers therefore grow only at the margin, so the shell grows in area by adding material at its edge, and they do not thicken; the inner layer is deposited over the whole inner surface and thickens throughout life. Mineralization takes place in a thin space between the mantle and the shell, the extrapallial space, in which an organic matrix of chitin, silk-like proteins and acidic glycoproteins controls whether calcite or aragonite crystallizes. Nacre is up to 3,000 times tougher than pure aragonite, because cracks are deflected along the organic layers between tablets.

Growth is not uniform: seasonal and daily variation in the activity of the mantle produces growth lines, and in many bivalves daily, tidal and annual increments can be counted in sections of the shell, as in tree rings.

A pearl forms when a fragment of the outer mantle epithelium comes to lie in the connective tissue of the mantle, carried in by a parasite, a grain of sand or an injury. The displaced cells proliferate into a closed pearl sac and deposit shell layers, usually nacre, around the nucleus, so a pearl has the same structure as the inner shell. The common statement that a grain of sand alone produces a pearl is incomplete: without the epithelial cells no pearl sac forms. Cultured pearls are made by implanting a bead together with a small piece of mantle from a donor animal into the gonad of a pearl oyster (*Pinctada*), or by implanting pieces of mantle alone into the mantle of freshwater mussels, as in the triangle-shell mussel *Hyriopsis cumingii* that produces most Chinese freshwater pearls.

### 13.1.3. Radula and Gut

**Definition 13.1.3 (Radula)** The **radula** is the feeding organ of molluscs: a chitinous ribbon bearing transverse rows of teeth, lying in the floor of the buccal cavity on a muscular and cartilaginous support, the odontophore, which protrudes it from the mouth and draws it back so that the teeth scrape, cut or grasp food. New rows are continually formed in a radular sac at its posterior end and move forward to replace the worn teeth.

The radula is a synapomorphy of molluscs, lost only in bivalves and some specialized gastropods. Its teeth are described by a radular formula giving the number of marginal, lateral and central teeth in a row: the Chinese mystery snail has $2\cdot1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot2$, two marginals, one lateral, the central and the corresponding teeth on the other side. The type of radula reflects diet and is important in classification:

| Type | Teeth per row | Examples and diet |
|---|---|---|
| Docoglossan | Few, strong, mineralized teeth | Limpets (Patellogastropoda) scraping algae from rock |
| Rhipidoglossan | Many fan-like marginals | Abalone, top shells, nerites; brushing microalgae |
| Taenioglossan | Seven ($2\cdot1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot2$) | Most caenogastropods, grazers and carnivores |
| Rachiglossan (stenoglossan) | Three or one | Whelks, murex snails; predators |
| Toxoglossan | Single hollow harpoon-like marginal, used once | Cone snails, injecting venom |
| Polyplacophoran | Seventeen, the major laterals capped with magnetite | Chitons scraping algae and rock |

The teeth of chitons are capped with magnetite, an iron oxide, and those of limpets contain fibres of goethite: limpet teeth have the highest tensile strength measured for any biological material (Barber et al. 2015). Chitons and limpets erode the rock surface as they feed and are significant agents of bioerosion on rocky shores.

The gut is complete and regionally specialized. Salivary glands lubricate the radula; the oesophagus often bears glands; the stomach receives the ducts of a large *digestive gland*, the main site of enzyme secretion, absorption and intracellular digestion. In microphagous molluscs — grazers of microalgae and filter feeders — the stomach contains a rotating rod of mucus and enzymes, the *crystalline style*, secreted in a style sac and turned by cilia. The style winds the mucous food string into the stomach like a windlass, grinds against a chitinous gastric shield, and releases digestive enzymes (mainly amylases) as its tip dissolves. Ciliated sorting fields send fine particles to the digestive gland and coarse ones to the intestine. The style is present in many bivalves and herbivorous gastropods and absent in carnivores.

### 13.1.4. Ctenidia

**Definition 13.1.4 (Ctenidium)** A **ctenidium** is the gill of molluscs: an axis projecting into the mantle cavity, containing afferent and efferent blood vessels, nerves and muscles, that bears a row of flattened leaflets (filaments) on each side (bipectinate) or on one side (monopectinate); lateral cilia on the leaflets drive the respiratory current, and blood flows through the leaflets opposite to the water.

The opposite flow of water and blood makes the ctenidium a **countercurrent exchange** organ ([7.14.17](/7.14.17)). Plesiomorphically there is one pair of bipectinate ctenidia in the posterior mantle cavity, but the number varies: many pairs in chitons, three to six in monoplacophorans, two pairs in *Nautilus*, one in most gastropods, and none in scaphopods and many sea slugs, which breathe through the body surface or secondary gills. In bivalves the ctenidia are enlarged into sheets that filter food from the water ([7.17.14](/7.17.14)).

### 13.1.5. Coelom, Circulation and Excretion

The coelom of molluscs is small. It persists as the pericardium around the heart, the lumina of the gonads and the nephridia, connected in the plesiomorphic condition so that gametes leave through the nephridia. The main body cavity is a **hemocoel**: the heart pumps blood through arteries into sinuses among the tissues, and the blood returns through the gills to the heart, an **open circulatory system** except in cephalopods. The heart lies dorsally in the pericardium and consists of a ventricle and one or two atria (more in monoplacophorans and *Nautilus*), each atrium receiving blood from the gill on its side. The blood pigment is usually hemocyanin, dissolved in the plasma, which turns blue when oxygenated; a few bivalves (ark clams) and planorbid snails use hemoglobin. The blood also works as a **hydrostatic skeleton**: snails extend the head and foot from the shell, and bivalves extend the foot, by pumping blood into them.

The excretory organs, usually called kidneys, are **metanephridia** ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#def-4.6.2)) that open into the pericardium through a ciliated renopericardial canal and to the mantle cavity through a nephridiopore. Together with the heart they form a heart-kidney complex: blood pressure filters fluid across the wall of the atria, which bears podocytes, into the pericardium; the kidney collects this primary urine, reabsorbs useful solutes and adds wastes by secretion. Pericardial glands on the atrial walls add further secretions. Most molluscs are ammonotelic; land snails excrete uric acid, which saves water.

### 13.1.6. Nervous System and Sense Organs

The plesiomorphic molluscan nervous system, retained in aplacophorans and chitons, is a nerve ring around the oesophagus with two pairs of longitudinal cords, the pedal cords to the foot and the lateral (pleurovisceral) cords to the mantle and viscera, linked by commissures — a pattern called tetraneury, similar to a **ladder-like nervous system**. In conchiferans the nerve cells are concentrated into paired ganglia: cerebral (head and sense organs), pleural (mantle), pedal (foot), and visceral or parietal ganglia (viscera, gills), linked by connectives. The trend toward concentration culminates in the brain of cephalopods, in which all ganglia fuse around the oesophagus ([7.17.14](/7.17.14)).

Sense organs include tentacles, eyes of many types (from pigment cups in limpets to lensed eyes in snails, the mantle-margin eyes of scallops and the camera eyes of cephalopods), statocysts near the pedal ganglia, the osphradia, and in chitons the aesthetes, sensory organs in the shell plates. The giant axons of squid ([7.14.4](/7.14.4)) and the large identifiable neurons of the sea hare *Aplysia*, in which the cellular basis of learning was discovered, have made molluscs model organisms of neurobiology.

### 13.1.7. Reproduction and Development

Most molluscs are gonochoristic with external fertilization, the plesiomorphic condition retained in chitons, many bivalves and the vetigastropods. Internal fertilization, copulatory organs and hermaphroditism evolved repeatedly, especially in gastropods. Cleavage is spiral, except in cephalopods, whose large yolky eggs undergo discoidal cleavage. In marine species the embryo becomes a **trochophore** and then, in gastropods and bivalves, a **veliger** ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#def-5.4.2)), with a shell gland that secretes the larval shell (protoconch), a foot rudiment and a velum. Chitons have a trochophore without a velum, and cephalopods, many freshwater and all land molluscs develop directly. The shell gland arises as an invagination of the dorsal ectoderm whose cells express the genes *engrailed* and *Dpp* at its margin, a molecular signature shared by molluscs and brachiopods.

## 13.2. Phylogeny and Origin of the Molluscs

### 13.2.1. The "Hypothetical Ancestral Mollusc"

Generations of textbooks have described a "hypothetical ancestral mollusc" (HAM): a small, limpet-like animal with a cap-shaped dorsal shell, a creeping sole, a posterior mantle cavity with a pair of ctenidia, a radula and a tetraneurous nervous system, from which each class could be derived by changing proportions. The HAM was a useful teaching device, but it was constructed by averaging the features of living classes, not by phylogenetic analysis, and it carried hidden assumptions: that the ancestor had a single shell, that it was a creeping grazer, and that monoplacophorans were the closest living approximation. Lindberg and Ghiselin (2003) showed that it was effectively a generalized gastropod. Current work reconstructs ancestral states on an explicit tree and treats the fossil record independently.

### 13.2.2. Aculifera and Conchifera

Phylogenomic analyses (Kocot et al. 2011, 2020; Smith et al. 2011) divide the molluscs into two clades. The *Aculifera* are the molluscs with calcareous spicules or sclerites in the cuticle of the mantle: the two aplacophoran classes and the chitons. The *Conchifera* are the molluscs with a shell formed by a single shell gland region, a larval shell, a three-layered shell wall and a mantle margin with three folds: monoplacophorans, gastropods, bivalves, scaphopods and cephalopods. Within Conchifera, the best supported arrangement places monoplacophorans as the sister group of cephalopods and unites gastropods, bivalves and scaphopods, though the relationships among the last three are not settled. The older division of conchiferans into Cyrtosoma (gastropods and cephalopods, with coiled shells and a strong head) and Diasoma (bivalves and scaphopods, burrowers with a reduced head), followed by many textbooks, is not supported by molecular data.

The aculiferan hypothesis has a consequence for the evolution of the shell. If aplacophorans and chitons are sister groups, the worm-like aplacophorans need not be the most primitive molluscs, as traditional schemes held; they may be derived from chiton-like ancestors that lost their plates. Fossils support this. *Kulindroplax* from the Silurian of England (Sutton et al. 2012) is an aplacophoran-like worm with seven chiton-like plates, and Palaeozoic multiplacophorans combine chiton plates with spines. The ancestor of all molluscs may therefore have carried both sclerites and plates, and the single shell of conchiferans and the eight plates of chitons are alternative consolidations of an armour of sclerites.

### 13.2.3. Fossils

Molluscan shells are among the first mineralized fossils. Tiny cap-shaped and coiled shells (helcionelloids) occur in the earliest Cambrian, about 535 million years ago, among the "small shelly fossils" that mark the beginning of the **Cambrian explosion**. The Ediacaran *Kimberella* (about 555 million years ago), a bilaterian that left scratch marks while feeding, was once interpreted as a mollusc with a radula, but it is now usually treated as a stem lophotrochozoan of uncertain affinity. The Cambrian *Wiwaxia*, *Halkieria* and *Odontogriphus*, covered with sclerites and some bearing a radula-like feeding apparatus, are interpreted by most palaeontologists as stem-group molluscs or stem aculiferans, though some see them as relatives of annelids. Bivalves (*Pojetaia*, *Fordilla*) and coiled univalves appear in the Early Cambrian, chitons and the first cephalopods in the Late Cambrian, and all shelled classes radiated in the Ordovician. The scaphopods are the exception: Ordovician records are doubtful, and unquestioned tusk shells are known only from the late Palaeozoic. Aplacophorans, without a mineralized shell, have almost no fossil record.

## 13.3. Aplacophorans

**Definition 13.3.1 (Aplacophora)** The **Aplacophora** (aplacophorans) are worm-like marine molluscs without a shell, whose body is covered by a cuticle containing calcareous spicules, whose foot is reduced to a ciliated ridge in a ventral groove or absent, and whose mantle cavity is a small posterior chamber; they comprise the Solenogastres and the Caudofoveata, often ranked as two separate classes.

Aplacophorans are mostly less than 5 cm long and live in sediments and on hydroids and corals from the shallow shelf to the abyss; there are about 300 described species of Solenogastres and 150 of Caudofoveata, with many more undescribed. They lack eyes, tentacles, statocysts and nephridia (the gonoducts, which arise from the pericardium, serve for excretion as well). The two groups differ as follows:

| | Solenogastres (Neomeniomorpha) | Caudofoveata (Chaetodermomorpha) |
|---|---|---|
| Foot | A narrow ciliated ridge in a ventral groove (pedal groove) | Absent; no ventral groove |
| Anterior end | Mouth with a vestibulum of sensory papillae | Mouth surrounded by a cuticular oral shield used for burrowing |
| Mantle cavity and gills | Posterior cavity without ctenidia, often with folds | Posterior cavity (the "caudal pit") with one pair of ctenidia |
| Spicules | Of many shapes, often hollow, sometimes in several layers | Overlapping scale-like spicules |
| Habit | Epibenthic, crawling on and feeding on cnidarians | Burrowers, deposit feeders or feeders on foraminifera |
| Reproduction | Hermaphrodites, internal fertilization, copulatory spicules | Gonochoristic, external fertilization |

The names describe these characters: Solenogastres means "groove-belly" and Caudofoveata "tail-pit", and the Chinese names ("groove-belly class" and "tail-cavity class") translate them. The radula is present in most species but small or reduced. Despite their simple appearance, aplacophorans have the full molluscan set of organs, including a radula, a heart with a ventricle and an atrium in a pericardium, and a tetraneurous nervous system, which marks them as true molluscs rather than primitive worms. Examples are *Neomenia* and *Proneomenia* (Solenogastres), the latter recorded from the South China Sea, and *Chaetoderma* and *Falcidens* (Caudofoveata).

## 13.4. Chitons

**Definition 13.4.1 (Polyplacophora)** The **Polyplacophora** (chitons) are dorsoventrally flattened, oval marine molluscs whose shell consists of eight overlapping, articulated plates (valves) embedded in a thick muscular mantle girdle bearing spicules, scales or bristles; they have a broad creeping foot, many pairs of ctenidia in the mantle grooves along the sides of the foot, a radula with magnetite-capped teeth, and sensory aesthetes penetrating the plates.

Chitons, about 1,000 living species, are mostly 2–10 cm long; the gumboot chiton *Cryptochiton stelleri* of the North Pacific reaches about 36 cm and has the plates completely covered by the girdle. Most live on rocks in the intertidal and shallow subtidal, where they cling with the foot and the girdle, which acts as a suction cup; a few live in the deep sea on sunken wood. Common Chinese species belong to *Acanthochitona*, *Lepidozona* and *Ischnochiton*.

The eight plates articulate with one another: the anterior head plate, six intermediate plates and the posterior tail plate. Each plate has an upper layer, the tegmentum, unique to chitons and perforated by canals with the aesthetes, and a lower layer, the articulamentum, which forms projections that insert into the girdle. Because the plates can move relative to each other, a chiton dislodged from the rock rolls up like a woodlouse. The aesthetes are photosensory and secretory organs; in some species, such as *Acanthopleura granulata*, hundreds of them carry eyes with lenses made of aragonite, which can form crude images (Speiser et al. 2011).

The head lacks eyes and tentacles. Chitons graze algae and the biofilm on rocks with their radula, whose 17 teeth per row include a pair of large laterals capped with magnetite; a new row matures every one to two days. The many pairs of ctenidia hang in the mantle groove on each side of the foot, and water enters the groove anteriorly, passes between the gills and leaves posteriorly, near the anus and the nephridiopores. The heart has a ventricle and two atria; there is a pair of nephridia. The nervous system is tetraneurous, with a nerve ring and pedal and lateral cords connected by commissures, without distinct ganglia. Sexes are separate; eggs are shed singly or in strings and fertilized in the water, and the trochophore settles without passing through a veliger stage.

The serial repetition of plates and ctenidia, and the eight pairs of pedal retractor muscles in chitons and in monoplacophorans, raise the same question as the monoplacophorans (§13.5): is it a trace of segmentation in the ancestor of molluscs?

## 13.5. Monoplacophorans

**Definition 13.5.1 (Monoplacophora)** The **Monoplacophora** (monoplacophorans) are limpet-like marine molluscs with a single cap-shaped shell, a broad foot surrounded by a mantle groove, and serially repeated organs: three to six pairs of ctenidia, three to seven pairs of nephridia, eight pairs of pedal retractor muscles, one or two pairs of gonads and two pairs of atria.

Monoplacophorans were known only from Cambrian to Devonian fossils until 1952, when the Danish Galathea expedition dredged living specimens from 3,570 m off the Pacific coast of Costa Rica; Lemche named them *Neopilina galatheae* in 1957. About 35 living species are now known, 1–37 mm long, from depths of about 200 m to more than 6,000 m. They feed on detritus, foraminifera and diatoms with a radula, have a pair of statocysts but no eyes, and a nerve ring with lateral and pedal cords. The living species belong to the order Tryblidiida, which has a fossil record in the Palaeozoic but none at all for the last 375 million years; they have evolved throughout this gap and are not unchanged survivors of the Cambrian forms. They are a **living fossil** in the sense that they represent an old body type, not an unchanged lineage.

The discovery revived an old hypothesis. Lemche and Wingstrand (1959) interpreted the repeated organs of *Neopilina* as evidence that molluscs descended from segmented ancestors, close to annelids, the clade Articulata extended to Mollusca. Several lines of evidence now speak against it:

- The repeated structures of monoplacophorans are not aligned with one another: there are more pairs of muscles than of gills or nephridia, and they do not correspond to units with their own coelom, ganglia and blood vessels as true segments do ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#def-3.5.1)).
- Molluscs and annelids are not sister groups; annelids lie in a separate branch of the Lophotrochozoa, and annelid **segmentation** is not present in their closest relatives (§13.8, [7.17.15](/7.17.15)).
- Repetition of organs occurs elsewhere in molluscs (chitons, *Nautilus*) and in other unsegmented phyla, and in chitons it arises during development by addition, not by the subdivision of a segmented germ band.

Serial repetition in molluscs is therefore best interpreted as having arisen independently in some lineages, a tendency rather than a heritage of segmented ancestors. Early molecular analyses that united monoplacophorans and chitons as "Serialia" were affected by a contaminated sequence and have not been confirmed by phylogenomic data.

## 13.6. The Geometry of Shell Coiling

Most conchiferan shells are cones that grow at the aperture, and many are coiled. Moseley (1838) noted that coiled shells follow a logarithmic spiral, and D'Arcy Thompson (1917) showed why: a structure that grows only by adding material at its edge in constant proportion keeps its shape as it grows, and the only curve with this property is the logarithmic (equiangular) spiral. Raup (1966) turned this into a model with which shells of every type can be generated on a computer.

**Formula 13.6.1 (Raup's Coiling Model)** A shell is the surface swept out by a generating curve, the outline of the aperture, as it revolves about a fixed coiling axis. If $\theta$ is the angle of revolution and $r(\theta)$ the distance of the outer edge of the generating curve from the axis, the shell is defined by three parameters:

$$
r(\theta) = r_0\, W^{\theta/2\pi}, \qquad d(\theta) = D\, r(\theta), \qquad z(\theta) = T\, r(\theta),
\tag{13.1}
$$
where $W$ is the whorl expansion rate (the factor by which the generating curve grows in one revolution), $D$ the relative distance of the inner edge of the generating curve from the axis, and $T$ the translation rate, the ratio of the displacement along the axis to the distance from it. A fourth parameter, the shape of the generating curve, sets the cross-section of the whorls.

:::

The three parameters have simple morphological meanings. Shells with $T = 0$ are planispiral (the coiled cephalopods, the ram's-horn snails), and shells with large $T$ are high-spired (turret shells). Small $W$ gives many tight whorls; a large $W$ gives a rapidly expanding aperture, and when $W$ exceeds about $10^3$, less than one whorl is formed, as in the valves of bivalves and brachiopods and the cap of a limpet. The parameter $D$ controls whether successive whorls touch: if $D > 1/W$, the whorls do not overlap and the shell is open (evolute), with an open umbilicus; if $D = 0$ the shell is coiled around a solid axis, the columella.

**Example 13.6.1 (Measuring a Shell)** The parameters can be read from a real shell. In a median section of a *Nautilus* shell, the distance from the coiling axis to the outer edge of the aperture is $r = 60\,\mathrm{mm}$, and one whorl earlier, along the same radius, it is $20\,\mathrm{mm}$. Then $W = 60/20 = 3$. A half whorl earlier the distance is $r_0 W^{1/2} = 20 \times 1.73 \approx 35\,\mathrm{mm}$, which can be checked on the same section; a constant ratio between successive half whorls is the test that the spiral is logarithmic. Because the inner edge of each whorl lies almost on the axis ($D \approx 0$), $D < 1/W$ and the last whorl covers the previous ones, as in all involute shells. For a high-spired snail the same ratio on a section along the axis gives $W$, and $T$ is the ratio of the distance of a point of the suture from the apex, measured along the axis, to its distance from the axis: about 0.5–1 in a low top shell, about 5 in a slender turret shell.

:::

The value of the model lies in the comparison between the shells that are possible and those that exist. Raup represented the parameters as the axes of a cube, a theoretical *morphospace*, and plotted living and fossil species in it.

![Raup's morphospace of coiled shells. (A) Shells generated by Eq. (13.1) on a grid of the whorl expansion rate $W$ (columns, logarithmic) and the translation rate $T$ (rows), with $D = 0.1$ and a circular generating curve. Shaded areas: the approximate regions occupied by real shells (after Raup 1966): coiled cephalopods ($T = 0$, low $W$), gastropods (low $W$, positive $T$), bivalves and brachiopods (very high $W$, little or no translation). (B) The planispiral plane ($T = 0$) for different $W$ and $D$: the curve $D = 1/W$ separates shells whose whorls overlap (below the curve, involute) from open, non-overlapping coils (above it); most ammonoids and *Nautilus* lie below or near it](./plots/raup-morphospace.py)

Two conclusions emerged. First, the classes occupy separate regions of the space, as expected from their different modes of life, and the boundaries between occupied and unoccupied regions can often be explained functionally: an open coil with whorls not touching ($D > 1/W$) is mechanically weak and rare except in some ammonoids, and a high-spired shell with a large aperture would topple. Second, much of the morphospace is empty. Some empty regions correspond to shells that would be useless or impossible to build, others to shells that would work but never evolved, which raises the question of developmental and historical constraints. Raup's study became the model for the field of theoretical morphology, and the same approach has since been applied to ammonoid shells, horns, branching corals and the shapes of leaves.

## 13.7. Gastropoda

**Definition 13.7.1 (Gastropoda)** The **Gastropoda** (gastropods: snails, slugs and limpets) are molluscs in which the visceral mass and mantle undergo torsion during development, so that the mantle cavity lies anteriorly above the head and the visceral nerve cords cross (or are secondarily uncrossed); they usually have a single, spirally coiled shell into which the animal can withdraw, closed in many species by an operculum on the foot, a distinct head with tentacles and eyes, a creeping foot, and a radula.

Gastropods are by far the largest class, with about three-quarters of all living molluscs, and the only molluscs that have colonized land on a large scale. They include marine grazers, predators, filter feeders and parasites, pelagic forms such as sea butterflies and heteropods, freshwater snails, and terrestrial snails and slugs.

### 13.7.1. Shell and Foot

A gastropod shell is a cone coiled about a central axis, the columella. The apex is the oldest part, formed by the larval shell; the whorls of the spire increase in size toward the last, the body whorl, which opens in the aperture with an inner and an outer lip. Whorls meet at the suture. Where the whorls do not fuse at the axis, a hollow umbilicus remains at the base. Holding the shell with the apex up and the aperture facing the observer, the aperture is on the right in a dextral shell, the condition of about 90% of species, and on the left in a sinistral one. The direction of coiling is set at the first cleavages by the chirality of the spiral cleavage pattern and is inherited as a maternal effect in *Lymnaea* ([7.4.11](/7.4.11)).

The foot is a muscular sole on which the snail glides on a film of mucus by waves of contraction (pedal waves) or, in small species, by cilia. The operculum, a horny or calcareous disc on the back of the foot, closes the aperture when the animal withdraws. Shells have been reduced or lost many times, producing sea slugs and land slugs; in cowries and several other groups the mantle lobes cover the shell from outside, which keeps it glossy.

### 13.7.2. Torsion

**Definition 13.7.2 (Torsion)** **Torsion** is a developmental rotation, unique to gastropods, by which the visceral mass and mantle turn counterclockwise (viewed from above) by up to 180° relative to the head and foot, so that the mantle cavity with its gills, anus and nephridiopores comes to lie anteriorly above the head, the gut becomes U-shaped, and the two pleurovisceral nerve connectives cross in a figure of eight (streptoneury).

In the classical description, based on the abalone *Haliotis* and the limpet *Patella*, torsion takes place in the veliger in two phases: a rapid rotation of about 90° within a few hours, caused by the contraction of an asymmetric larval retractor muscle, and a slower rotation of the remaining 90° by differential growth. Later studies have shown that the process varies among gastropods. In several caenogastropods and heterobranchs there is no rapid phase, and the mantle cavity first forms as an invagination on the right side and moves to its dorsal-anterior position by differential growth, so that Page (2006) proposed that torsion is better described as an asymmetric growth process than as a rotation of a pre-formed mantle cavity. The outcome is the same: pre-torsional right organs (ctenidium, atrium, osphradium, nephridium) are now on the left, and the visceral loop is twisted.

![Torsion in gastropods (schematic dorsal views, anterior up, the animal's right on the right; foot and head pink, visceral mass grey, mantle cavity green, gut yellow, pleural and visceral ganglia with their connectives red; the pre-torsional right ctenidium purple, the left one blue). (A) The pre-torsional condition, as in a hypothetical untorted ancestor: the mantle cavity with a pair of ctenidia, the anus and the nephridiopores lies posteriorly, and the two pleurovisceral connectives run parallel from the pleural ganglia to the visceral ganglion. (B) Halfway through torsion (90°): the mantle cavity lies on the right side. (C) After 180° of torsion: the mantle cavity opens anteriorly above the head, the gut is U-shaped, the ctenidium originally on the right is on the left, and the connectives cross in a figure of eight (in the animal one passes above the gut and the other below it). (D) Detorsion in heterobranchs: the mantle cavity moves back toward the right side or the rear and the connectives become uncrossed (euthyneury), but the gill lost after torsion is not regained](./plots/torsion.py)

Why should an animal twist its body? Several hypotheses have been proposed:

| Hypothesis | Proposed advantage | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Larval protection (Garstang 1928) | The veliger can withdraw its head and velum into the mantle cavity, protecting them from predators | Torted and untorted abalone veligers were eaten equally often by planktonic predators (Pennington and Chia 1985); does not explain why adults keep torsion |
| Anterior mantle cavity | Gills and osphradia face forward into clean water ahead of the crawling animal and can sample it; the head can be withdrawn first into the shell | Plausible advantages for the adult; difficult to test |
| Balance of the coiled shell | Torsion brings a coiled shell that protruded to one side into line with the body axis, improving balance | Coiling may have preceded torsion in the fossil record, as this hypothesis requires |
| Developmental by-product | Torsion follows from asymmetric growth and was later exploited | Consistent with the variable developmental mechanisms |

None is accepted by all. What is clear is the cost: with the anus and nephridiopores above the head, waste could foul the gills and mouth. Much of the history of gastropods can be read as a sequence of solutions to this sanitation problem. In early gastropods water entered both sides of the mantle cavity and left through a slit or a row of holes in the shell above the anus, as in the slit shells (Pleurotomariidae) and abalones; keyhole limpets have a single hole at the apex. In most later lineages the right ctenidium (the pre-torsional left one), its atrium and osphradium, and the right nephridium were lost, and water flows across the mantle cavity from left to right, over the single left gill first and then past the anus and nephridiopore on the right. A single monopectinate ctenidium attached to the mantle roof, with an inhalant siphon at the left, characterizes the caenogastropods.

Torsion was secondarily reversed in many heterobranchs. In *detorsion* the mantle cavity returns toward the right side or the rear and the visceral nerve connectives become uncrossed (euthyneury); in many sea slugs the mantle cavity and ctenidium have disappeared altogether, and secondary gills developed on the back. Organs lost on one side during torsion did not reappear, so detorted gastropods remain asymmetrical internally.

### 13.7.3. Classification

Traditional zoology, including the Chinese textbooks, divided the gastropods into three subclasses based on the position of the gill and the nervous system: Prosobranchia ("front gills", torted, mostly marine and with an operculum), Opisthobranchia ("rear gills", detorted sea slugs) and Pulmonata (air-breathing snails with a lung). Molecular and anatomical studies (Bouchet et al. 2017) have shown that the prosobranchs are a **paraphyletic group** from which the other two arose, and that the opisthobranchs and pulmonates together form one clade, the Heterobranchia, within which neither is monophyletic as traditionally defined. The current classification recognizes five main clades:

| Clade | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Patellogastropoda (true limpets) | Cap-shaped shell without coiling in the adult; docoglossan radula; often one or no ctenidium, with secondary gills | *Patella*, *Lottia*, *Cellana* |
| Vetigastropoda | Often two bipectinate ctenidia, shell with nacre, slit or holes; rhipidoglossan radula | Abalones *Haliotis*, slit shells *Pleurotomaria*, top shells *Trochus*, keyhole limpets *Fissurella* |
| Neritimorpha | Rhipidoglossan radula, calcareous operculum; marine, freshwater and terrestrial | Nerites *Nerita*, *Neritina* |
| Caenogastropoda | One monopectinate ctenidium, siphon, taenioglossan or more reduced radula; about 60% of living gastropods; marine, freshwater, terrestrial | Periwinkles *Littorina*, river snails *Cipangopaludina*, *Oncomelania*, apple snails *Pomacea*, cowries *Cypraea*, moon snails *Neverita*, whelks *Rapana*, cone snails *Conus* |
| Heterobranchia | Heterostrophic protoconch (larval shell coiled differently from the adult); usually hermaphroditic; often detorted and euthyneurous | Sea hares *Aplysia*, nudibranchs, sea butterflies; freshwater pond and ramshorn snails *Lymnaea*, *Planorbis*; land snails and slugs *Cornu*, *Achatina*, *Agriolimax* |

Within Heterobranchia, the Euthyneura comprise the Nudipleura (nudibranchs and side-gilled slugs), the Euopisthobranchia (bubble shells, sea hares and pteropods), and the Panpulmonata, which include the sap-sucking sea slugs (Sacoglossa), the freshwater Hygrophila (*Lymnaea*, *Biomphalaria*, planorbids) and the Eupulmonata, with the land snails and slugs (Stylommatophora). A lung evolved within Panpulmonata, and sea slugs evolved several times among the euthyneurans.

### 13.7.4. The Chinese Mystery Snail: A Representative Gastropod

The Chinese mystery snail *Cipangopaludina chinensis* (Caenogastropoda: Viviparidae) lives in lakes, ponds, rivers and rice fields throughout China and East Asia and has been introduced to North America. Its shell, 4–6 cm high with six to seven convex whorls, is dextral and closed by a horny operculum with concentric growth lines.

| System | Structure |
|---|---|
| External form | Head with a snout, a pair of long tentacles with eyes on swellings at their outer bases; broad foot with operculum; neck lobes form a short left inhalant and a longer right exhalant siphon |
| Feeding and digestion | Radula $2\cdot1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot2$ (taenioglossan); salivary glands; stomach and large digestive gland in the upper whorls; the U-shaped intestine ends at the anus on the right side of the mantle cavity |
| Respiration and circulation | One monopectinate ctenidium on the left of the mantle cavity; heart with one atrium (in front) and one ventricle in the pericardium; open circulation with hemocyanin |
| Excretion | One kidney in front of the pericardium, connected to it by a renopericardial canal, with a ureter opening near the exhalant siphon; ammonia |
| Nervous system | Cerebral, pleural, pedal, oesophageal (supra- and sub-oesophageal) and visceral ganglia; the pleurovisceral connectives cross in a figure of eight; statocysts beside the pedal ganglia, one osphradium |
| Reproduction | Sexes separate; the right tentacle of the male is thickened and serves as the copulatory organ; internal fertilization; the female broods embryos in a uterus and gives birth to young snails (ovoviviparity, hence "mystery" and Viviparidae) |

Other caenogastropods of medical and economic importance include the amphibious *Oncomelania hupensis*, the only intermediate host of *Schistosoma japonicum*, and *Parafossarulus* and *Bithynia*, first **intermediate hosts** of the Chinese liver fluke ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)); and the South American golden apple snail *Pomacea canaliculata*, introduced to Asia for food in the 1980s, now a pest of rice and an intermediate host of the rat lungworm *Angiostrongylus cantonensis*.

### 13.7.5. Land Snails and Slugs

The Stylommatophora, with more than 20,000 species, are the most successful terrestrial molluscs. Their mantle cavity is a lung: the gill has been lost, the roof of the cavity is richly vascularized, and the cavity opens through a small contractile pore, the pneumostome, which limits water loss. The head bears two pairs of retractile tentacles, the upper pair with eyes at their tips. Land snails are simultaneous hermaphrodites with a single gonad producing both eggs and sperm (ovotestis), and a complex reproductive tract; in many helicid snails the partners stab each other with a calcareous love dart before reciprocal copulation, and the mucus it carries increases the number of sperm stored, an example of **sexual selection** acting on hermaphrodites. Development is direct, in eggs with a shell or jelly.

Life on land requires protection from desiccation. Snails become inactive in dry weather, sealing the aperture with a membrane of dried mucus (an epiphragm), and they excrete uric acid. Slugs, without the protection of a shell, depend on mucus and on moist microhabitats, and in compensation can enter narrow crevices and avoid the calcium cost of a shell. Introduced species, such as the giant African snail *Achatina* (*Lissachatina*) *fulica* in southern China, are serious pests.

The land snail of the Chinese textbooks is a generalized stylommatophoran; the commonest species in Chinese gardens and fields is *Bradybaena similaris*, a small, dextral, low-spired snail. Its organization differs from that of the mystery snail in the features that land life demands:

| System | Structure |
|---|---|
| External form | Shell without operculum; head with two pairs of retractile tentacles, eyes at the tips of the upper (posterior) pair, the lower pair tactile and olfactory; a single foot gliding on mucus secreted by a pedal gland beneath the mouth |
| Feeding | A horny jaw on the roof of the buccal cavity and a radula with more than a hundred small teeth per row, rasping leaves; crop and stomach; digestive gland in the upper whorls |
| Respiration and circulation | Lung (mantle cavity without a ctenidium, its roof a vascular network); pneumostome on the right side of the mantle collar; heart with one atrium and one ventricle |
| Excretion | One kidney beside the pericardium, its ureter opening near the pneumostome; uric acid stored or excreted as a paste |
| Nervous system | Ganglia concentrated in a ring around the oesophagus; visceral loop short and uncrossed (euthyneury, from detorsion) |
| Reproduction | Simultaneous hermaphrodite: ovotestis, hermaphrodite duct, albumen gland, a combined sperm-oviduct dividing into vas deferens with penis and oviduct with vagina, a spermatheca (bursa copulatrix), a dart sac with mucous glands; common genital pore behind the right tentacles; reciprocal copulation, eggs laid in soil, direct development |

Freshwater pulmonates (Hygrophila) have returned to water from land. Pond snails (*Lymnaea*) surface to fill the lung with air, or fill it with water in deep or cold lakes; the planorbids (*Planorbis*, *Hippeutis*, *Biomphalaria*) have hemoglobin in their blood, which supports life in poorly oxygenated water. They matter medically as intermediate hosts of flukes: *Lymnaea* (*Radix*, *Galba*) of the liver fluke *Fasciola hepatica*, *Hippeutis* and *Segmentina* of the giant intestinal fluke *Fasciolopsis buski*, and *Biomphalaria* of *Schistosoma mansoni* in Africa and South America ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)). The intertidal onchidiid slugs (*Onchidium*, common on Chinese mudflats) are air-breathing euthyneurans that have lost the shell and carry eyes on dorsal papillae.

### 13.7.6. Cone Snails and Conotoxins

The cone snails (*Conus* and related genera, nearly 1,000 species) are predatory caenogastropods of tropical seas that hunt worms, other snails or fish. Each marginal radular tooth is a hollow harpoon, loaded into the tip of the proboscis, thrust into the prey and used to inject venom; a new tooth is used for each strike. The venom of a single species contains 100–200 different small peptides, the conotoxins, each typically 10–40 amino acids long and stabilized by disulfide bonds, which block or modulate specific ion channels and receptors of the prey's nervous system. Fish-hunting species such as *C. geographus* can kill humans. Because each conotoxin is highly selective, they have become tools of neuroscience and sources of drugs: the synthetic version of $\omega$-conotoxin MVIIA from *C. magus*, which blocks N-type calcium channels, was approved in 2004 as the painkiller ziconotide. *C. geographus* even releases a fish-like insulin into the water to sedate a school of fish before engulfing it (Safavi-Hemami et al. 2015). The rapid evolution of conotoxin genes, driven by changes in prey, is a classic example of molecular diversification under positive selection.

### 13.7.7. Marine Snails of Rocky Shores and Soft Sediments

The marine gastropods named in the Chinese textbooks illustrate the main ways of life of the class:

| Species or group | Clade | Way of life and notable features |
|---|---|---|
| Abalones (*Haliotis discus hannai*, *H. diversicolor*) | Vetigastropoda | Grazers of macroalgae on rocky shores; spire reduced, a huge foot, a row of holes for the exhalant current; two bipectinate ctenidia; farmed on a large scale in China; the nacreous shell is a traditional medicine |
| Slit shells (*Pleurotomaria*, *Perotrochus*) | Vetigastropoda | Deep-water relicts (100–1,000 m) with an exhalant slit in the body whorl, a lineage with a rich Palaeozoic and Mesozoic fossil record, long called living fossils; northern Taiwan is one of their main localities |

Gastropod predation leaves a record. Drill holes made by moon snails and muricids are found in fossil shells from the Cretaceous onward, and the proportion of drilled shells, their position and their success rate have been used to measure predation intensity through time, one of the lines of evidence for the escalation of predators and prey discussed in [7.17.14](/7.17.14#sec-14.9).

### 13.7.8. Sea Slugs: Defence without a Shell

The loss of the shell, completed independently in several lineages of heterobranchs, exposed the animal to predators, and sea slugs have compensated with chemical and borrowed defences. The sea hare *Aplysia*, whose shell is a thin internal plate, releases ink and an opaline secretion that contain repellents and confuse the chemical senses of lobsters. Many nudibranchs sequester toxins from the sponges, cnidarians and bryozoans they eat and advertise them with bright warning colours (**aposematism**). Aeolid nudibranchs go further: they eat hydroids and sea anemones, pass the undischarged **nematocysts** of their prey through the gut into the tips of the dorsal cerata, and store them in cnidosacs, where they are kept functional and discharged against predators (kleptocnidae; on nematocysts see [7.17.9](/7.17.9)).

The sacoglossan sea slugs suck the contents of algal cells. Some, such as *Elysia chlorotica* and *Plakobranchus*, keep the chloroplasts of their algal food in cells of the digestive gland, where the plastids continue to photosynthesize for weeks to months (kleptoplasty). Claims that algal nuclear genes had been transferred to the slug genome to maintain the plastids have not been confirmed by genome sequencing, and the plastids appear to survive because they are unusually robust; the contribution of their photosynthesis to the nutrition of the slug is still debated. Sea slugs show how much of the protective function of a shell can be replaced by chemistry and behaviour, and why shell loss evolved repeatedly once torsion had been reversed.

Several details of the Chinese textbooks need correction. Liu and Zheng attach the name and the character of the "ventral groove" to Caudofoveata and the posterior mantle cavity to Solenogastres; the reverse is correct, as the Latin names indicate (§13.3), and the Chinese names should be paired accordingly. The type species of the living monoplacophorans is *Neopilina galatheae* (named after the *Galathea*, not "galathea"). The time course of torsion over "tens of millions of years" given in two textbooks is a reconstruction, not an observation (see the table). The estimates of 100,000–115,000 living molluscan species given in older sources include many synonyms; the current count of described valid species is about 73,000. The representative species of the Chinese textbooks treated in this chapter are the Chinese mystery snail (§13.7.4), the land snail (§13.7.5), the other gastropods listed there (§13.7.5–13.7.8) and the chitons and aplacophorans named above; the river mussel and cuttlefish follow in [7.17.14](/7.17.14).

## Summary

- Molluscs (Definition 13.1.1) are unsegmented spiralians with a head-foot, a visceral mass, a mantle that secretes a shell or spicules and overhangs a mantle cavity (Definition 13.1.2), a radula (Definition 13.1.3), ctenidia (Definition 13.1.4), a reduced coelom (pericardium, gonads, nephridia) and an open circulation.
- The conchiferan shell has a periostracum, a prismatic layer and a nacreous or crossed-lamellar layer; it grows at the mantle margin and thickens from the whole mantle. Pearls form in a pearl sac of displaced mantle epithelium.
- Radula types (docoglossan, rhipidoglossan, taenioglossan, rachiglossan, toxoglossan) reflect diet; microphagous molluscs have a crystalline style. Excretion is by a heart-kidney complex: filtration across the atria into the pericardium, modification in metanephridial kidneys.
- The nervous system is tetraneurous in aculiferans and ganglionated (cerebral, pleural, pedal, visceral) in conchiferans. Development is spiral, with trochophore and veliger larvae, except in cephalopods.
- The "hypothetical ancestral mollusc" is a teaching model, not a reconstruction. The phylum divides into Aculifera (aplacophorans, chitons) and Conchifera (monoplacophorans, gastropods, bivalves, scaphopods, cephalopods).
- Aplacophorans (Definition 13.3.1): Solenogastres with a pedal groove, Caudofoveata without a foot and with an oral shield. Chitons (Definition 13.4.1): eight plates, girdle, multiple ctenidia, magnetite teeth, aesthetes. Monoplacophorans (Definition 13.5.1): a cap-shaped shell with serially repeated organs that are not segments.
- Raup's model, Eq. (13.1), generates shells from the whorl expansion rate $W$, the distance from the axis $D$ and the translation rate $T$; the classes occupy distinct regions of morphospace, and much of it is empty.
- Gastropods (Definition 13.7.1) undergo torsion (Definition 13.7.2), bringing the mantle cavity forward and crossing the visceral nerves; its adaptive significance is debated. Its sanitation problem was solved by shell slits and by loss of the right gill; heterobranchs underwent detorsion.
- Modern classes of gastropods: Patellogastropoda, Vetigastropoda, Neritimorpha, Caenogastropoda and Heterobranchia; Prosobranchia is paraphyletic and Opisthobranchia and Pulmonata are not natural groups.
- The Chinese mystery snail is an ovoviviparous caenogastropod with one gill and crossed nerves; land snails breathe with a lung, are hermaphrodites and resist desiccation; cone snails inject conotoxins with harpoon-like radular teeth.
- Marine gastropods range from algal grazers (abalones, limpets) and splash-zone periwinkles to drilling predators (moon snails, whelks) and pelagic sea butterflies; freshwater pulmonates are intermediate hosts of flukes. Sea slugs replaced the shell with chemical defences, stolen nematocysts (aeolids) and stolen chloroplasts (sacoglossans).

## Questions

1. Why does the prismatic layer of a shell not thicken, while the nacreous layer does? How can the shell of a bivalve record its age?

   **Answer** The periostracum and the prismatic layer are secreted only by the folds of the mantle margin, so they are added only at the edge of the shell as it grows outward; once a part of the shell lies inside the margin, its outer layers receive no more material. The nacreous (inner) layer is secreted by the whole outer epithelium of the mantle and is deposited over the entire inner surface throughout life, so it thickens. Because marginal growth varies with season, tide and day, it leaves growth lines; in sections, annual bands (and in some species daily or tidal increments) can be counted like tree rings, and long-lived bivalves such as *Arctica islandica* have been aged at more than 500 years in this way.
2. Explain the heart-kidney complex of molluscs. How does it resemble the vertebrate nephron?

   **Answer** Blood pressure in the heart forces fluid across the wall of the atria, where podocytes form a filter, into the pericardium, which is a remnant of the coelom. This primary urine passes through the renopericardial canal into the kidney, a metanephridium, where useful solutes are reabsorbed and wastes secreted before it leaves through the nephridiopore into the mantle cavity. The vertebrate nephron follows the same principle: ultrafiltration across podocytes from the blood into a coelom-derived space (Bowman's capsule), followed by modification of the filtrate in a tubule. In both, filtration and tubular modification are separated, and the filter lies between blood and a coelomic space.
3. In Raup's model, what values of $W$, $D$ and $T$ correspond to (a) a high-spired turret shell, (b) a *Nautilus*, (c) a limpet or a bivalve valve? Why might shells with $D > 1/W$ be rare?

   **Answer** (a) A turret shell has a low whorl expansion rate (many whorls, $W \approx 1.2$–2), a small $D$ (whorls coiled around a columella) and a large translation rate $T$. (b) *Nautilus* is planispiral ($T = 0$) with a moderate $W$ (about 3) and $D \approx 0$, so each whorl envelops the previous one (involute). (c) A limpet or bivalve valve has a very large $W$ (of the order of $10^3$ or more), so that less than one whorl is formed, and small $T$. If $D > 1/W$, successive whorls do not touch; such open coils lack the mutual support of adjoining whorls, are mechanically weaker and more fragile, and require more shell material per volume enclosed, so they are uncommon (some Cretaceous heteromorph ammonoids are exceptions).
4. Describe the consequences of torsion for the organs of the mantle cavity and the nervous system. Which problem did torsion create, and how was it solved in different gastropod lineages?

   **Answer** Torsion turns the visceral mass and mantle by up to 180° counterclockwise. The mantle cavity with the gills, osphradia, anus and nephridiopores moves from behind to above the head; organs originally on the right come to lie on the left; the gut becomes U-shaped; and the pleurovisceral connectives cross in a figure of eight. The anus and nephridiopores now discharge above the head and gills, so waste could foul the respiratory current and the mouth. Early gastropods (vetigastropods such as abalones and slit shells) direct the exhalant current through a slit or holes in the shell above the anus; keyhole limpets have an apical hole. Most later gastropods lost the right gill and right nephridium, so water flows across the cavity from left to right, over the gill first and then past the anus. Many heterobranchs underwent detorsion, moving the mantle cavity back toward the rear and uncrossing the nerves.
5. Why is the traditional division of gastropods into Prosobranchia, Opisthobranchia and Pulmonata no longer used, and what replaces it?

   **Answer** Phylogenetic analyses show that the "prosobranchs" are not a clade: the opisthobranchs and pulmonates evolved from within them, so Prosobranchia is paraphyletic, defined only by the retention of ancestral features (anterior gill, crossed nerves). Opisthobranchs and pulmonates together form the clade Heterobranchia, but within it the traditional pulmonates are nested among opisthobranch lineages (for example, sacoglossan sea slugs are close to pulmonates), so neither group is monophyletic. The current classification recognizes Patellogastropoda, Vetigastropoda, Neritimorpha, Caenogastropoda and Heterobranchia, with Euthyneura (Nudipleura, Euopisthobranchia, Panpulmonata) within Heterobranchia.
6. Are the repeated organs of *Neopilina* evidence that molluscs descend from segmented ancestors?

   **Answer** Probably not. The repeated organs are not arranged in matching units: there are eight pairs of pedal retractors but fewer pairs of gills, nephridia and gonads, and there are no corresponding coelomic compartments, ganglia or septa as in annelid segments. Phylogenomics places molluscs and annelids in separate branches of Lophotrochozoa, and the closest relatives of the annelids are unsegmented, so a segmented common ancestor would require many losses. Serial repetition also occurs in chitons and *Nautilus* and in other unsegmented phyla, and the living monoplacophorans belong to a Mesozoic radiation. Repetition of organs is therefore best explained as having evolved independently, a tendency rather than an inherited segmentation.
7. How would you determine the whorl expansion rate $W$ of a planispiral shell from a photograph, and how would you test whether the shell is a logarithmic spiral?

   **Answer** Measure, along one radius from the coiling axis, the distances to the outer edge of successive whorls, $r_1, r_2, r_3, \dots$. $W$ is the ratio $r_{k+1}/r_k$ of distances one revolution apart. For a logarithmic spiral this ratio is the same for every pair, and the ratio over any fixed angle $\theta$ is $W^{\theta/2\pi}$, so distances half a whorl apart differ by $\sqrt{W}$ (Example 13.6.1). Plotting $\ln r$ against the angle of revolution gives a straight line of slope $\ln W/2\pi$; curvature in this plot would show that the rate of expansion changes during growth, as it does at maturity in many ammonoids and snails, whose last whorl departs from the spiral.
8. Aeolid nudibranchs and sacoglossans both keep functional parts of their food. Compare the two cases and explain why both are more common in shell-less gastropods.

   **Answer** Aeolids eat cnidarians and transport the undischarged nematocysts through the digestive gland into cnidosacs at the tips of the cerata, where they are discharged at attackers: a borrowed weapon. Sacoglossans pierce algal cells, suck out the contents and retain the chloroplasts in digestive cells, where some continue to photosynthesize for weeks or months (kleptoplasty): borrowed metabolism, whose nutritional value is debated. Both are found in heterobranch sea slugs that have lost the shell: without a shell the animal needs other defences against predators, and the exposed, often flattened body with dorsal outgrowths (cerata, parapodial flaps) carries stored nematocysts or exposes chloroplasts to light. Shell-less snails also profit from distastefulness and warning colours, which a heavy shell makes less necessary.
