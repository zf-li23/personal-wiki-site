This chapter brings together several small phyla of the Spiralia that share either an unusual anatomy or an unusual history. The Gnathifera are microscopic animals with complex jaws in the pharynx: the gnathostomulids and micrognathozoans, discovered only in the twentieth century, and the rotifers, among the most abundant animals in fresh water. The thorny-headed worms (Acanthocephala), parasites without a gut, turn out to be highly modified rotifers. The arrow worms (Chaetognatha), predators of the plankton, were long classified as deuterostomes and are now placed close to the Gnathifera. Finally, the dicyemids and orthonectids are parasites so simplified that they were once considered intermediate between protists and animals.

Many of these animals were grouped in older textbooks as "pseudocoelomates" (Aschelminthes), worms with a body cavity derived from the blastocoel. Molecular phylogenies have distributed them among the Spiralia and the Ecdysozoa; the last section of this chapter traces where each went. The flatworms and gastrotrichs, the other small-bodied spiralians, were treated in [7.17.11](/7.17.11), the general features of Spiralia in [7.17.10](/7.17.10), and the nematodes and other ecdysozoan "pseudocoelomates" follow in [7.17.17](/7.17.17).

## 12.1. Gnathifera

**Definition 12.1.1 (Gnathifera)** The **Gnathifera** are a clade of spiralians whose pharynx contains complex jaws made of cuticular elements built from tubular rods with an electron-dense core; it comprises the Gnathostomulida, the Micrognathozoa and the Rotifera (including the Acanthocephala), and, in a wider sense, the Chaetognatha.

The homology of the jaws was proposed on the basis of electron microscopy (Ahlrichs 1995; Kristensen & Funch 2000): in all three phyla the jaw elements are secreted by pharyngeal epithelial cells and contain the same kind of rods in cross-section. Phylogenomic analyses have confirmed the clade, and they place Gnathifera as the sister group of all other spiralians (Marlétaz et al. 2019; Laumer et al. 2019), which makes it important for reconstructing the spiralian ancestor. Gnathiferans do not show typical spiral cleavage: rotifers have a modified pattern, and nothing is known of the cleavage of gnathostomulids and micrognathozoans.

| Phylum | Size | Habitat | Jaws | Species |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gnathostomulida | 0.5–4 mm | Marine sediments, often anoxic | Paired jaws and an unpaired basal plate | ~100 |
| Micrognathozoa | ~0.15 mm | Freshwater mosses and springs | Highly complex, several paired elements | 2 described |
| Rotifera (excluding Acanthocephala) | 0.05–2 mm | Mostly fresh water, also moist soil and the sea | Trophi of the mastax | ~2,150 |
| Acanthocephala | 1 mm to over 60 cm | Endoparasites of arthropods and vertebrates | None (lost) | ~1,300 |
| Chaetognatha | 2 mm to 12 cm | Marine plankton and benthos | Grasping spines and teeth on the head | ~130 |

### 12.1.1. Gnathostomulida

Gnathostomulids ("jaw-mouth worms") were described in 1956 by Peter Ax from the sands of the Baltic Sea. They are slender, transparent worms living between sand grains, often in the sulphide-rich, oxygen-poor layers beneath the surface. Each epidermal cell bears a single cilium, a rare condition among bilaterians, by which the worm glides. The ventral mouth leads into a muscular pharynx with a pair of toothed jaws and a basal plate, with which the worm scrapes fungi, bacteria and diatoms from sand grains. The gut is blind, although a transient connection to the epidermis has been reported. Gnathostomulids are hermaphrodites; some have a hollow, stylet-like penis and inject sperm through the body wall of the partner. Development is direct. There are two orders, Filospermoidea (elongate, sperm filiform) and Bursovaginoidea (with a bursa and vagina).

### 12.1.2. Micrognathozoa

The only described micrognathozoan, *Limnognathia maerski*, was found in 1994 in a cold spring on Disko Island, Greenland, and described in 2000; it has since been found on the subantarctic Crozet Islands. It is about 150 µm long, with a head, thorax and abdomen, and moves by ciliary plates on the ventral surface, which form a ciliated "sole". Its jaws are the most complex of any gnathiferan, with about 15 sclerotized elements. Only females have been found, and the animal probably reproduces by parthenogenesis, laying thin-shelled eggs in summer and thick-shelled resting eggs. Its mixture of rotifer-like and gnathostomulid-like features made it the key to recognizing the Gnathifera.

## 12.2. Rotifera: Body Plan

**Definition 12.2.1 (Rotifera)** The **Rotifera** (rotifers) are gnathiferans with an intracytoplasmic lamina in a syncytial epidermis, an anterior ciliated corona used for locomotion and feeding, and a muscular pharynx (mastax) with jaws (trophi); the group includes the Acanthocephala, which have lost the corona and the mastax.

Rotifers ("wheel bearers") were among the first animals seen under the microscope, by Leeuwenhoek in 1702, who described the beating corona as two rotating wheels. Most are 100–500 µm long, as small as large ciliates, but they are multicellular animals with about 1,000 nuclei, muscles, a nervous system, a gut and excretory organs.

### 12.2.1. Body Regions and Integument

The body is divided into a head with the corona, a trunk and a tapering foot, which ends in one to four toes with *pedal glands* that secrete a cement for temporary attachment. The epidermis is a syncytium whose outer cytoplasm contains a stiff, protein-rich layer, the *intracytoplasmic lamina*. In many species the lamina is thickened over the trunk into a rigid shell, the *lorica*, often with spines, and the foot and head can be withdrawn into it. The intracytoplasmic lamina is the main synapomorphy of rotifers and acanthocephalans, and it is the reason the two together are called Syndermata ("joined skin").

**Definition 12.2.2 (Corona)** The **corona** of a rotifer is the ciliated region at the anterior end of the head, typically consisting of a circle of cilia around the mouth (the circumapical band) and an apical field, whose beating propels the animal and draws food particles to the mouth.

The shape of the corona varies with the mode of life. In the common bdelloid *Philodina* it forms two ciliated discs (trochal discs), whose metachronal beating creates the illusion of rotating wheels; in sessile species such as *Collotheca* it is a funnel fringed with long, stiff setae that trap prey; in predatory species such as *Asplanchna* it is reduced.

### 12.2.2. Mastax and Trophi

**Definition 12.2.3 (Mastax)** The **mastax** is the muscular pharynx of a rotifer, containing the trophi, a set of hard cuticular jaws whose elements (the median fulcrum and paired rami, unci and manubria) grind, cut or grasp food.

The structure of the trophi reflects diet and is the main character in rotifer taxonomy:

| Type | Structure and use | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Malleate | Rami and unci with many teeth; grinding of suspended particles | *Brachionus*, *Keratella* |
| Ramate | Large semicircular rami with ridges, unci reduced; grinding | Bdelloids (*Philodina*, *Adineta*) |
| Virgate | Long manubria and fulcrum; acts as a piston pump that sucks out prey contents | *Synchaeta*, *Trichocerca* |
| Forcipate | Long pincer-like rami protruded through the mouth to seize prey | *Dicranophorus* |
| Incudate | Large rami like forceps, protruded to grasp prey | *Asplanchna* |
| Uncinate | Few hooked teeth; for grasping prey caught by the corona | *Collotheca* |

### 12.2.3. Internal Organs

Behind the mastax, the oesophagus leads into a stomach with gastric glands, an intestine and a cloaca, which opens on the dorsal side at the base of the foot; some predatory species have lost the intestine. The body cavity between gut and body wall is a fluid-filled space without a mesodermal lining, persisting from the blastocoel: in the terminology of [7.17.3](/7.17.3), a **pseudocoel**. It serves as a hydrostatic skeleton and for transport.

The excretory system consists of a pair of protonephridia whose terminal cells (flame bulbs) open into a contractile bladder that empties into the cloaca. They are mainly osmoregulatory: a freshwater rotifer may excrete its own volume of water in a few minutes. The brain is a ganglion dorsal to the mastax, with a pair of ventral nerve cords; sense organs include one or more eyespots, antennae with sensory bristles and a *retrocerebral organ* of uncertain function.

Many rotifers are eutelic: the number of nuclei in each organ is constant within a species and cell division ceases after embryonic development, so that growth is by cell enlargement and the animal cannot regenerate lost parts.

## 12.3. Rotifera: Reproduction and Diversity

### 12.3.1. The Classes

Free-living rotifers are divided into three groups, to which the Acanthocephala (§12.4) are added:

| Group | Characteristics | Reproduction | Species |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seisonidea | Marine, epizoic on the gills of the leptostracan crustacean *Nebalia*; elongate, corona reduced | Sexual; males and females similar | 4 |
| Bdelloidea | Fresh water, moss, soil, lichens; two trochal discs; ramate trophi; move like leeches | Only parthenogenetic; no males known | ~460 |
| Monogononta | Mostly freshwater plankton and littoral; one ovary; males small and short-lived | Cyclical parthenogenesis | ~1,600 |

Molecular phylogenies suggest that Acanthocephala is the sister group of Seisonidea, or of Bdelloidea, which makes the "rotifers" in the traditional sense paraphyletic; for this reason some authors call the whole clade Syndermata and reserve "Rotifera" for the free-living groups.

### 12.3.2. Cyclical Parthenogenesis

The life cycle of monogonont rotifers alternates between asexual and sexual reproduction. Under favourable conditions, the population consists of *amictic* females that produce diploid eggs by mitosis, which develop without fertilization into further amictic females; a female can produce a new generation every few days, so populations grow exponentially. Under certain conditions, notably high population density, some eggs develop into *mictic* females, which produce haploid eggs by meiosis. If a haploid egg is not fertilized, it develops into a male, which is haploid, small and has no gut. If it is fertilized by a male, it becomes a thick-shelled *resting egg*, which sinks to the bottom, survives drying and freezing, and can remain viable for decades before hatching into an amictic female.

In *Brachionus* the switch to sex is triggered by a protein that the females release into the water; when its concentration exceeds a threshold, a fraction of the daughters become mictic (Snell et al. 2006). Density therefore acts as a signal of a population that is about to exhaust its food and is likely to experience a crash. The following model captures the logic. Let $A$ and $M$ be the densities of amictic and mictic females, $N = A + M$, and $E$ the number of resting eggs produced:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}t} &= b(F)\,A\left[1 - m(N)\right] - \delta A, &\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} &= b(F)\,A\,m(N) - \delta M, \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}t} &= -aNF, &\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} &= cM,
\end{aligned}
\tag{12.1}
$$
with $b(F) = rF/(F + F_{\mathrm{h}})$ and $m(N) = m_{\max}N^h/(N^h + N_{\mathrm{c}}^h)$.

Here $F$ is the food supply of a pond (relative to its initial value), consumed by the females at rate $a$ per female and not renewed; $b(F)$ is the birth rate, which saturates at the maximum $r$ when food is abundant ($F_{\mathrm{h}}$ is the food level at which it is halved); $\delta$ is the death rate; and $m(N)$ is the fraction of daughters that become mictic, which rises steeply (Hill coefficient $h$) around a critical density $N_{\mathrm{c}}$. Mictic females produce resting eggs at rate $c$. With realistic parameters (birth rates of 0.3–0.8 per day, lifespans of one to two weeks), the model shows that sex is postponed until the population is large, so that asexual growth first multiplies the number of females, and that resting eggs are produced mainly around the population peak, just before the decline caused by food depletion. Delaying sex too long risks a crash before many resting eggs are made; switching too early sacrifices asexual growth. Field and laboratory populations show the same pattern, and the threshold for mixis evolves rapidly when the length of the growing season changes (Smith & Snell 2012).

### 12.3.3. Bdelloids: Ancient Asexuals

Bdelloid rotifers have been the most famous problem for theories of the evolution of sex ([7.10.14](/7.10.14#sec-14.3.2)). No male has ever been found among the hundreds of thousands examined; the class has diversified into some 460 species over tens of millions of years (they are known from amber tens of millions of years old); and asexual lineages are generally expected to go extinct quickly because they accumulate harmful mutations and adapt slowly. Several peculiarities may explain their success:

- Their genomes are degenerate tetraploids in which pairs of gene copies have diverged, but other pairs remain nearly identical, which suggests that the copies are kept similar by gene conversion, a form of recombination without meiosis.
- About 8–10% of their genes were acquired by horizontal gene transfer from bacteria, fungi and plants, the highest proportion known in animals (Gladyshev et al. 2008). Uptake of foreign DNA may occur when the animals dry out and their membranes and DNA are damaged and repaired.
- They survive desiccation at any stage of life (anhydrobiosis), in a dormant state in which metabolism stops entirely; they can be revived after years, and *Adineta* survived 24,000 years in Siberian permafrost (Shmakova et al. 2021). Desiccation also allows them to escape parasitic fungi, which die, and to be dispersed by wind.

Evidence of some genetic exchange between individuals has been found in natural populations, so bdelloids may not be entirely asexual; but they remain the best example of an animal clade that has persisted and diversified for tens of millions of years without conventional sex.

### 12.3.4. Ecology

Rotifers are one of the three main groups of freshwater zooplankton, with cladocerans and copepods. They graze bacteria, algae and detritus, reproduce faster than crustaceans and are eaten by fish larvae and predatory invertebrates. Their density in eutrophic ponds can reach thousands per litre. *Brachionus plicatilis* and *B. calyciflorus* are cultured in large quantities as live food for the larvae of fish and crustaceans in aquaculture, and rotifers are used as indicators of water quality and in ecotoxicology.

## 12.4. Acanthocephala: Thorny-Headed Worms

**Definition 12.4.1 (Acanthocephala)** The **Acanthocephala** (thorny-headed worms) are endoparasitic syndermatans without a gut, with an eversible anterior proboscis armed with recurved hooks, which attach in the intestine of a vertebrate definitive host; their larvae develop in an arthropod intermediate host.

Acanthocephalans are 1 mm to more than 60 cm long (*Macracanthorhynchus hirudinaceus* of pigs, whose females reach 65 cm). Their body consists of a presoma, with the proboscis, its receptacle and a neck, and a trunk. The proboscis can be retracted into the receptacle by muscles and everted by hydrostatic pressure, driven by fluid in two elongated sacs, the *lemnisci*, that hang from the neck into the body cavity. The tegument is a thick syncytium with the intracytoplasmic lamina of rotifers, penetrated by a system of fluid-filled lacunar canals, and it takes up nutrients from the host's gut, as in tapeworms. There is no gut at any stage.

The sexes are separate. After copulation, the male seals the female's genital opening with a secretion of its cement glands. In the female the ovary breaks up into ovarian balls that float in the body cavity; fertilized eggs develop into shelled larvae (acanthors) and are sorted by a unique organ, the *uterine bell*, which passes mature eggs to the uterus and returns immature ones to the body cavity.

The life cycle involves two hosts:

1. Eggs, each containing an acanthor larva with hooks, are released with the faeces of the vertebrate host.
2. The eggs are eaten by an arthropod (an amphipod, isopod, ostracod or insect larva), in whose body cavity the acanthor develops into an infective *cystacanth*.
3. The definitive host is infected when it eats the arthropod. Some species pass through paratenic hosts, in which the cystacanth survives without developing.

Many acanthocephalans alter the behaviour of their intermediate host in ways that increase the chance that it will be eaten by the definitive host. Freshwater amphipods (*Gammarus*) infected by *Pomphorhynchus laevis* lose their avoidance of light and are attracted rather than repelled by the odour of fish, the definitive host; amphipods infected by *Polymorphus paradoxus*, whose definitive hosts are ducks, skim along the water surface and cling to vegetation when disturbed, so that they are easily caught by dabbling ducks. The behavioural changes are mediated in part by changes in the serotonin system of the host. Such *host manipulation* is a recurrent theme in parasites with trophic transmission, from acanthocephalans to trematodes ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)) and nematomorphs ([7.17.17](/7.17.17)), and is an example of an extended phenotype (Dawkins 1982).

## 12.5. Chaetognatha: Arrow Worms

**Definition 12.5.1 (Chaetognatha)** The **Chaetognatha** (arrow worms) are marine, transparent, torpedo-shaped predatory worms whose head bears chitinous grasping spines and teeth that can be covered by a hood, with lateral and caudal fins supported by rays; they are hermaphrodites with direct development.

Chaetognaths (about 130 species) are among the most abundant predators of the marine plankton, in places second only to copepods in numbers; most are 1–3 cm long. The body is divided by transverse septa into head, trunk and tail. The head bears two eyes, a vestibule leading to the ventral mouth, rows of anterior and posterior teeth, and on each side 4–14 large, curved grasping spines made of α-chitin. A fold of the body wall, the hood, can be drawn over the spines during swimming to reduce drag and pulled back to strike. The trunk and tail have one or two pairs of lateral fins and a caudal fin, with a mesh of ciliary fence receptors (sensory hair cells) that detect the vibrations of swimming prey. Arrow worms hover motionless, then dart forward by a flick of the longitudinal muscles and seize copepods and fish larvae with the spines; some species, and the bacteria associated with them, produce tetrodotoxin, which paralyses the prey.

The nervous system has a cerebral ganglion and a large ventral ganglion in the trunk, connected by circumoesophageal connectives. There is no circulatory or excretory system. Each individual has ovaries in the trunk and testes in the tail; sperm is exchanged as spermatophores. The eggs develop directly into juveniles.

Few phyla have moved so far in the animal tree. Classical embryology placed chaetognaths among the deuterostomes: their cleavage is radial, the blastopore becomes the anus, and the body cavities form by folding of the archenteron, as in enterocoely. Molecular data from the 2000s placed them firmly among the protostomes, and recent phylogenomic analyses put them as the sister group of Gnathifera (Marlétaz et al. 2019). Their grasping spines and the jaws of gnathiferans may be homologous, and the Cambrian fossil *Amiskwia*, once thought to be a chaetognath or a nemertean, has been reinterpreted as a gnathiferan with jaws (Caron & Cheung 2019). The Cambrian protoconodonts, spine-like microfossils, are probably the grasping spines of stem chaetognaths, so arrow worms have been among the predators of the plankton for over 500 million years. Their body cavities, which line the trunk, are not homologous to the enterocoelic coelom of deuterostomes.

## 12.6. Dicyemida and Orthonectida

Dicyemids and orthonectids were once united as "Mesozoa", animals supposed to be intermediate between protists and metazoans because they consist of only a few dozen cells. Both are in fact highly reduced spiralian parasites, and they are probably not closely related to each other.

The Dicyemida (about 120 species) live in the renal sacs of cephalopods, attached by their anterior end to the kidney epithelium, where they feed on urine. An adult is a worm 0.5–7 mm long made of only 10–40 cells: a single long axial cell surrounded by a constant number of ciliated peripheral cells. Inside the axial cell, agametes develop asexually into vermiform embryos (when the host population is small) or, at high density, into hermaphroditic gonads, the infusorigens, which produce ciliated *infusoriform larvae* that leave with the host's urine. How the next cephalopod is infected is unknown. Dicyemids have the smallest number of cells of any animal and have lost almost all organs, but they retain spiralian genes, including Hox genes.

The Orthonectida (about 25 species) parasitize flatworms, nemerteans, molluscs, annelids, brittle stars and other invertebrates. Inside the host they form a multinucleate plasmodium, which produces free-living males and females, a few hundred cells each, that leave the host, mate and release ciliated larvae. The genome of *Intoshia variabili* is among the smallest in animals and has lost many genes, but it retains a reduced nervous system of about ten neurons. Phylogenomic analyses place orthonectids within or close to the Annelida (Schiffer et al. 2018), and dicyemids elsewhere in the Spiralia; both show how far parasitism can simplify a spiralian body.

## 12.7. The Fate of the "Pseudocoelomates"

In older classifications, including the standard Chinese textbooks, the phyla with a fluid-filled body cavity not lined by mesoderm (a **pseudocoel**) were united as the "pseudocoelomates" or Aschelminthes (Hyman 1951), sometimes as one phylum with several classes. The groups share, besides the type of body cavity, a cuticle, a muscular pharynx, a constant number of cells in many species and small size. Molecular phylogenetics shows that these features evolved repeatedly: the "pseudocoelomates" are a **grade**, not a clade, and their members belong to two different branches of the protostome tree.

| Rotifera, Acanthocephala | Spiralia: Gnathifera (Syndermata) | This chapter |

Phyla traditionally placed in this group are marked with dots on the current protostome tree (simplified, after Laumer et al. 2019 and Marlétaz et al. 2019): they belong to two different branches, the Ecdysozoa (moulting animals: nematodes, nematomorphs and the scalidophorans) and the Spiralia (gnathiferans, gastrotrichs, entoprocts and cycliophorans). The pseudocoel itself can be understood as a consequence of small size and of the loss of mesodermal linings, a condition that recurs whenever development is simplified. Many small spiralians and ecdysozoans are thought to have evolved from larger ancestors by miniaturization and progenesis, retaining the body cavity of a larva in the adult. "Pseudocoelomate", like "acoelomate", is therefore a useful descriptive term for a type of organization, but not a name for a group of related animals.

## Summary

- Gnathifera (Definition 12.1.1) are spiralians with homologous cuticular jaws: Gnathostomulida (interstitial, monociliated epidermis), Micrognathozoa (*Limnognathia*, the most complex jaws) and Rotifera with Acanthocephala; Chaetognatha are their sister group. Gnathifera is the sister group of all other spiralians.
- Rotifers (Definition 12.2.1) have a syncytial epidermis with an intracytoplasmic lamina (often a lorica), a corona (Definition 12.2.2), a mastax with trophi (Definition 12.2.3) whose types reflect diet, a pseudocoel, protonephridia with a bladder, and often eutely.
- Seisonidea (sexual), Bdelloidea (only parthenogenetic) and Monogononta (*cyclical parthenogenesis*). In monogononts, amictic females multiply asexually; density-dependent mixis (Eq. (12.1)) produces males and resting eggs near the population peak.
- Bdelloids are ancient asexuals with degenerate tetraploid genomes, extensive horizontal gene transfer and anhydrobiosis.
- Acanthocephala (Definition 12.4.1) are gutless parasitic rotifers with a hooked proboscis, lemnisci, a uterine bell, and a two-host life cycle with arthropod intermediate hosts whose behaviour they manipulate.
- Chaetognatha (Definition 12.5.1) are planktonic predators with chitinous grasping spines; their deuterostome-like embryology misled classification, and they are now related to Gnathifera.
- Dicyemida and Orthonectida ("Mesozoa") are highly reduced spiralian parasites.
- The "pseudocoelomates" are a grade: their members belong to Ecdysozoa (nematodes, nematomorphs, scalidophorans) and Spiralia (gnathiferans, gastrotrichs, entoprocts).

## Questions

1. Why were chaetognaths classified as deuterostomes, and what evidence moved them to the protostomes?

   **Answer** Their embryology resembles that of classical deuterostomes: radial cleavage, the blastopore becoming the anus, the mouth forming secondarily, and body cavities formed by folding from the archenteron in a manner resembling enterocoely. Molecular phylogenies (rRNA, then large sets of nuclear genes) consistently group them with protostomes, and recent phylogenomic analyses place them as the sister group of Gnathifera, consistent with features such as the chitinous grasping spines that may correspond to gnathiferan jaws and with Hox genes of protostome type. The embryological characters are now seen as labile and convergent.
2. In the model of Eq. (12.1), what would be the consequence for a rotifer population in a short-lived pond if the critical density for mixis $N_{\mathrm{c}}$ were much higher than the carrying capacity $K$? And if it were very low?

   **Answer** If $N_{\mathrm{c}} \gg K$, the density never reaches the threshold, $m(N)$ stays near zero, no mictic females appear and no resting eggs are produced; when the pond dries up, the population dies without leaving survivors. If $N_{\mathrm{c}}$ is very low, mixis begins early, a large fraction of daughters become mictic while the population is still small, asexual growth is curtailed, and the total number of resting eggs is small because few females are available to produce them. An intermediate threshold maximizes the number of resting eggs; populations in ponds with short seasons evolve lower thresholds.
3. How can an acanthocephalan that lives in a fish benefit from changing the behaviour of an amphipod?

   **Answer** The parasite can reach its definitive host only if the amphipod in which its cystacanth develops is eaten by a fish. Amphipods normally avoid light and fish odour, which keeps them hidden. A parasite that makes its host swim toward light or toward fish increases the chance that the host is eaten by the right predator, and so increases its own transmission. Because the genes of the parasite affect the behaviour of the host, this is an extended phenotype favoured by natural selection on the parasite.
4. In what sense are rotifers "pseudocoelomates", and why is this no longer a reason to classify them with nematodes?

   **Answer** Their body cavity is a fluid-filled space between the gut and body wall that is not lined by mesodermal epithelium and derives from the embryonic blastocoel, which is the definition of a pseudocoel. Nematodes have the same type of cavity. But the molecular phylogeny places rotifers among the spiralians and nematodes among the ecdysozoans, so the pseudocoel must have arisen independently, probably as a result of small size and simplified development. A shared condition that arose more than once is a grade character, not evidence of relationship.
