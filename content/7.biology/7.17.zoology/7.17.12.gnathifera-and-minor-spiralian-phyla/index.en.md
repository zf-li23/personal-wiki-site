This chapter brings together several small phyla of the Spiralia that share either an unusual anatomy or an unusual history. The Gnathifera are microscopic animals with complex jaws in the pharynx: the gnathostomulids and micrognathozoans, discovered only in the twentieth century, and the rotifers, among the most abundant animals in fresh water. The thorny-headed worms (Acanthocephala), parasites without a gut, turn out to be highly modified rotifers. The arrow worms (Chaetognatha), predators of the plankton, were long classified as deuterostomes and are now placed close to the Gnathifera. Finally, the dicyemids and orthonectids are parasites so simplified that they were once considered intermediate between protists and animals.

Many of these animals were grouped in older textbooks as "pseudocoelomates" (Aschelminthes), worms with a body cavity derived from the blastocoel. Molecular phylogenies have distributed them among the Spiralia and the Ecdysozoa; the last section of this chapter traces where each went. The flatworms and gastrotrichs, the other small-bodied spiralians, were treated in [7.17.11](/7.17.11), the general features of Spiralia in [7.17.10](/7.17.10), and the nematodes and other ecdysozoan "pseudocoelomates" follow in [7.17.17](/7.17.17).

## 12.1. Gnathifera

**Definition 12.1.1 (Gnathifera)** The **Gnathifera** are a clade of spiralians whose pharynx contains complex jaws made of cuticular elements built from tubular rods with an electron-dense core; it comprises the Gnathostomulida, the Micrognathozoa and the Rotifera (including the Acanthocephala), and, in a wider sense, the Chaetognatha.

The homology of the jaws was proposed on the basis of electron microscopy (Ahlrichs 1995; Kristensen & Funch 2000): in all three phyla the jaw elements are secreted by pharyngeal epithelial cells and contain the same kind of rods in cross-section. Phylogenomic analyses have confirmed the clade, and they place Gnathifera as the sister group of all other spiralians (Marlétaz et al. 2019; Laumer et al. 2019), which makes it important for reconstructing the spiralian ancestor. Gnathiferans do not show typical spiral cleavage: rotifers have a modified pattern, spiral cleavage has been reported but not studied in detail in gnathostomulids, and nothing is known of the development of micrognathozoans.

| Phylum | Size | Habitat | Jaws | Species |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gnathostomulida | 0.5–4 mm | Marine sediments, often anoxic | Paired jaws and an unpaired basal plate | ~100 |
| Micrognathozoa | ~0.15 mm | Freshwater mosses and springs | Highly complex, several paired elements | 1 described |
| Rotifera (excluding Acanthocephala) | 0.05–2 mm | Mostly fresh water, also moist soil and the sea | Trophi of the mastax | ~2,150 |
| Acanthocephala | 1 mm to over 60 cm | Endoparasites of arthropods and vertebrates | None (lost) | ~1,300 |
| Chaetognatha | 2 mm to 12 cm | Marine plankton and benthos | Grasping spines and teeth on the head | ~130 |

### 12.1.1. Gnathostomulida

**Definition 12.1.2 (Gnathostomulida)** The **Gnathostomulida** (jaw worms) are a phylum of minute, acoelomate, hermaphroditic marine worms of interstitial habitats, with a monociliated epidermis, a muscular pharyngeal bulb containing a pair of cuticular jaws and an unpaired basal plate, a blind gut and protonephridia with monociliated terminal cells.

Gnathostomulids ("jaw-mouth worms") were described as flatworms by Peter Ax in 1956 from the sands of the Baltic Sea and raised to a phylum by Riedl in 1969. About 100 species are known from the intertidal zone to depths of several hundred metres. They are slender, transparent worms 0.5–1 mm long (up to 4 mm), divided into a head, sometimes with a mobile rostrum, a slightly constricted neck and a trunk tapering into a tail. They live between sand grains, often in the sulphide-rich, oxygen-poor layers beneath the surface, where few other animals survive.

- *Body wall.* Each epidermal cell bears a single cilium, a rare condition among bilaterians shared with some gastrotrichs, by which the worm glides; there is no cuticle. Beneath a thin basement membrane lie circular muscles and, inside them, usually three pairs of longitudinal muscles, which bend, twist and shorten the body. The space between gut and body wall is filled with a few cells; there is no body cavity.
- *Feeding.* The ventral mouth leads into a muscular pharyngeal bulb with a comb-like basal plate in the lower lip and a pair of toothed jaws in the side walls. Contraction of the pharyngeal muscles pushes the basal plate out of the mouth to scrape bacteria, fungi and diatoms from sand grains, and the jaws snap shut to pass the food into the gut. The jaws are secreted by pharyngeal epithelial cells and consist of rods with an electron-dense core surrounded by a clear sheath, as in rotifers and micrognathozoans.
- *Gut.* The gut is blind. In some species a tissue connection between the posterior end of the gut and the epidermis has been interpreted as a transient anus, a vestige of a lost anus or an incompletely developed one.
- *Excretion and nerves.* Two to five pairs of protonephridia lie along the sides, each with a monociliated terminal cell, a canal cell and a pore cell. The nervous system lies within the epidermis: a brain, a buccal ganglion supplying the pharynx, and one to three pairs of longitudinal nerve cords; ciliary pits and stiff sensory cilia on the head are the sense organs.
- *Reproduction.* The worms are hermaphrodites, with a single ovary in the anterior trunk and one or two testes behind it. Some have a bursa that stores received sperm; some have a hollow, stylet-like penis and inject sperm through the body wall of the partner. Fertilized eggs are laid singly in the sediment; cleavage has been reported as spiral, and development is direct. There are two orders, Filospermoidea (elongate, with filiform sperm and no bursa) and Bursovaginoidea (with a bursa and often a vagina).

**Definition 12.1.3 (Micrognathozoa)** The **Micrognathozoa** are a phylum of microscopic freshwater gnathiferans with a head, a pleated thorax and an abdomen, a ventral ciliated sole of multiciliated cells, dorsal epidermal plates stiffened by an intracellular lamina, and the most complex pharyngeal jaws of any gnathiferan.

The only described micrognathozoan, *Limnognathia maerski*, was found in 1994 among mosses in a cold spring on Disko Island, Greenland, and described by Kristensen and Funch in 2000; it has since been found on the subantarctic Crozet Islands. It is about 150 µm long and moves by ciliary plates (ciliophores) on the ventral surface, which form a ciliated "sole", and by ciliary tufts on the head. Its jaws consist of about 15 sclerotized elements, which can be protruded through the mouth to scrape bacteria and diatoms. The dorsal and lateral epidermis is not ciliated but carries plates of intracellular lamina, like the integument of rotifers, whereas the ciliated ventral epidermis resembles that of gnathostomulids. There are protonephridia, a brain and a pair of ventral nerve cords; the gut is a simple sac with a dorsal anal pore that opens only periodically. Only females have been found, and the animal probably reproduces by parthenogenesis, laying thin-shelled eggs in summer and thick-shelled resting eggs. Its mixture of rotifer-like and gnathostomulid-like features made it the key to recognizing the Gnathifera.

## 12.2. Rotifera: Body Plan

**Definition 12.2.1 (Rotifera)** The **Rotifera** (rotifers) are gnathiferans with an intracytoplasmic lamina in a syncytial epidermis, an anterior ciliated corona used for locomotion and feeding, and a muscular pharynx (mastax) with jaws (trophi); the group includes the Acanthocephala, which have lost the corona and the mastax.

Rotifers ("wheel bearers") were among the first animals seen under the microscope, by Leeuwenhoek in 1702, who described the beating corona as two rotating wheels. Most are 100–500 µm long, as small as large ciliates, but they are multicellular animals with about 1,000 nuclei, muscles, a nervous system, a gut and excretory organs.

### 12.2.1. Body Regions and Integument

The body is divided into a head with the corona, a trunk and a tapering foot, which ends in one to four toes with *pedal glands* that secrete a cement for temporary attachment. The epidermis is a syncytium whose outer cytoplasm contains a stiff, protein-rich layer, the *intracytoplasmic lamina*. In many species the lamina is thickened over the trunk into a rigid shell, the *lorica*, often with spines, and the foot and head can be withdrawn into it. The intracytoplasmic lamina is the main synapomorphy of rotifers and acanthocephalans, and it is the reason the two together are called Syndermata ("joined skin").

**Definition 12.2.2 (Corona)** The **corona** of a rotifer is the ciliated region at the anterior end of the head, typically consisting of a circle of cilia around the mouth (the circumapical band) and an apical field, whose beating propels the animal and draws food particles to the mouth.

The shape of the corona varies with the mode of life. In the common bdelloid *Philodina* it forms two ciliated discs (trochal discs), whose metachronal beating creates the illusion of rotating wheels; in sessile species such as *Collotheca* it is a funnel fringed with long, stiff setae that trap prey; in predatory species such as *Asplanchna* it is reduced. The main types are:

| Corona type | Structure | Function | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buccal field | Ventral ciliated field around the mouth, weak circumapical band | Creeping on substrates and feeding on deposited particles | *Notommata*, *Dicranophorus* |
| Trochal discs | Two raised discs fringed by cilia (trochus) with a girdle (cingulum) below | Swimming and creating feeding currents in bdelloids | *Philodina*, *Rotaria* |
| Trochus–cingulum | A preoral ring (trochus) and a postoral ring (cingulum) of compound cilia, with a food groove between them | Planktonic suspension feeding (a downstream-collecting system, like the prototroch and metatroch of a trochophore) | *Brachionus*, *Keratella*, *Conochilus* |
| Funnel | Corona expanded into a funnel or lobes with long setae, few or no beating cilia | Passive trapping of swimming prey by sessile species | *Collotheca*, *Stephanoceros* |
| Reduced | Small ciliary tufts | Pelagic predators that seize prey with the trophi | *Asplanchna* |

Swimming rotifers are small enough that viscous forces dominate ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)), so they move like ciliates: at a few body lengths per second, stopping as soon as the cilia stop, and steering by beating the cilia asymmetrically.

### 12.2.2. Mastax and Trophi

**Definition 12.2.3 (Mastax)** The **mastax** is the muscular pharynx of a rotifer, containing the **trophi**, a set of hard cuticular jaws whose elements (the median fulcrum and paired rami, unci and manubria) grind, cut or grasp food.

The structure of the trophi reflects diet and is the main character in rotifer taxonomy:

| Type | Structure and use | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Malleate | Rami and unci with many teeth; grinding of suspended particles | *Brachionus*, *Keratella* |
| Ramate | Large semicircular rami with ridges, unci reduced; grinding | Bdelloids (*Philodina*, *Adineta*) |
| Virgate | Long manubria and fulcrum; acts as a piston pump that sucks out prey contents | *Synchaeta*, *Trichocerca* |
| Forcipate | Long pincer-like rami protruded through the mouth to seize prey | *Dicranophorus* |
| Incudate | Large rami like forceps, protruded to grasp prey | *Asplanchna* |
| Uncinate | Few hooked teeth; for grasping prey caught by the corona | *Collotheca* |

### 12.2.3. Internal Organs

Behind the mastax, the oesophagus leads into a stomach with gastric glands, an intestine and a cloaca, which opens on the dorsal side at the base of the foot; some predatory species have lost the intestine. The body cavity between gut and body wall is a fluid-filled space without a mesodermal lining, persisting from the blastocoel: in the terminology of [7.17.3](/7.17.3), a **pseudocoel**. It serves as a hydrostatic skeleton and for transport.

The excretory system consists of a pair of protonephridia whose terminal cells (flame bulbs) open into a contractile bladder that empties into the cloaca. They are mainly osmoregulatory: a freshwater rotifer may excrete its own volume of water in a few minutes. The brain is a ganglion dorsal to the mastax, with a pair of ventral nerve cords; sense organs include one or more eyespots, antennae with sensory bristles and a *retrocerebral organ* of uncertain function.

Many rotifers are eutelic: the number of nuclei in each organ is constant within a species and cell division ceases after embryonic development, so that growth is by cell enlargement and the animal cannot regenerate lost parts.

**Example 12.2.1 (*Brachionus calyciflorus*)** This monogonont, about 0.2–0.5 mm long, is one of the commonest rotifers of ponds and lakes in China and elsewhere, and it is cultured as live food and used as a standard test organism in ecotoxicology. Its organization illustrates that of the planktonic rotifers.

- *Integument and form.* The trunk is enclosed in a rigid, transparent lorica, flattened dorsoventrally, with two to six spines at its anterior dorsal margin and often a pair of posterior spines. The head with the corona can be withdrawn into the lorica, and a long, annulated, retractile foot, ending in two toes with pedal glands, emerges from an opening at the rear.
- *Corona and feeding.* A trochus of long compound cilia creates a current that brings particles of 1–20 µm (algae, bacteria, detritus) toward the head; they are retained by the cingulum and transported along the ciliated buccal funnel to the mouth. The malleate trophi grind the particles in the mastax, which pumps continuously; a female clears several microlitres of water per hour.
- *Gut.* An oesophagus leads into a ciliated stomach with a pair of gastric glands, the main site of digestion; a short intestine leads to the cloaca, which opens dorsally at the base of the foot.
- *Excretion.* A pair of protonephridia, each with several flame bulbs, opens into a contractile bladder that empties into the cloaca every few minutes; in fresh water the protonephridia remove the water that enters the body by osmosis.
- *Nerves and senses.* A dorsal brain above the mastax, with an eyespot, connects to two ventral nerve cords; sensory organs include a dorsal antenna, a pair of lateral antennae and sensory bristles on the corona.
- *Reproduction.* The female has a single *germovitellarium*: a syncytial ovary in which eggs are formed and a vitellarium that supplies them with yolk. Eggs pass through a short oviduct to the cloaca and are carried attached to the rear of the lorica until they hatch. In summer an amictic female produces some 20 eggs in her life of one to two weeks, and the daughters reach maturity in two to three days. Males are about a third the length of females, lack a gut, live a day or two and inseminate females by piercing their body wall with the penis.

## 12.3. Rotifera: Reproduction and Diversity

### 12.3.1. The Classes

**Definition 12.3.1 (Classes of Free-Living Rotifers)** Free-living rotifers are divided into three classes: the **Seisonidea**, marine epizoic rotifers with paired gonads and regular sexual reproduction; the **Bdelloidea**, with paired germovitellaria, two trochal discs and ramate trophi, which reproduce only by parthenogenesis; and the **Monogononta**, with a single germovitellarium, which reproduce by cyclical parthenogenesis.

The Chinese textbooks unite the Seisonidea and Bdelloidea, both with paired ovaries, as a class Digononta, a grouping not supported by molecular phylogenies. The Acanthocephala (§12.4) are added to these groups:

| Group | Characteristics | Reproduction | Species |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seisonidea | Marine, epizoic on the gills of the leptostracan crustacean *Nebalia*; elongate, corona reduced | Sexual; males and females similar | 4 |
| Bdelloidea | Fresh water, moss, soil, lichens; two trochal discs; ramate trophi; move like leeches | Only parthenogenetic; no males known | ~460 |
| Monogononta | Mostly freshwater plankton and littoral; one ovary; males small and short-lived | Cyclical parthenogenesis | ~1,600 |

Molecular phylogenies suggest that Acanthocephala is the sister group of Seisonidea, or of Bdelloidea, which makes the "rotifers" in the traditional sense paraphyletic; for this reason some authors call the whole clade Syndermata and reserve "Rotifera" for the free-living groups. Analyses of nuclear genes and mitochondrial gene order (Sielaff et al. 2016) unite Seisonidea and Acanthocephala as Pararotatoria, which with the Bdelloidea forms the Hemirotifera, the sister group of Monogononta; the epizoic, sexual seisonids then represent the closest free-living relatives of the parasites.

Monogononts are by far the most diverse class, and many of their genera are common in Chinese waters; the names below are those used by Chinese faunistic works:

| Genus | Form and habits | Trophi |
|---|---|---|
| *Brachionus* | Loricate, planktonic and littoral; spines inducible (§12.3.4) | Malleate |
| *Keratella* | Lorica with a mosaic of facets and anterior spines; among the commonest planktonic rotifers | Malleate |
| *Asplanchna* | Large (up to 1.5 mm), sac-like, transparent, without intestine or anus; predator of other rotifers | Incudate |
| *Polyarthra* | Planktonic, with bundles of feather-like paddles that make it jump | Virgate |
| *Filinia* | Planktonic, with two long anterior and one posterior bristle | Malleoramate |
| *Conochilus* | Spherical, free-swimming colonies of up to 100 individuals | Malleoramate |
| *Limnias*, *Floscularia* | Sessile in self-built tubes on aquatic plants | Malleoramate |
| *Collotheca* | Sessile in a gelatinous tube; funnel-shaped corona traps prey | Uncinate |

### 12.3.2. Cyclical Parthenogenesis

**Definition 12.3.2 (Cyclical Parthenogenesis)** **Cyclical parthenogenesis** is a life cycle in which generations reproducing by parthenogenesis alternate with a sexual generation. In monogonont rotifers, **amictic females** produce diploid eggs without meiosis, which develop into females, and **mictic females** produce haploid eggs by meiosis, which develop into males if unfertilized and into diploid resting eggs if fertilized.

The same pattern is found in cladocerans such as *Daphnia* ([7.17.21](/7.17.21)) and in aphids ([7.17.28](/7.17.28)), all animals of ephemeral or seasonal habitats. In monogonont rotifers, under favourable conditions, the population consists of amictic females that produce diploid eggs by mitosis, which develop without fertilization into further amictic females; a female can produce a new generation every few days, so populations grow exponentially. Under certain conditions, notably high population density, some eggs develop into mictic females, which produce haploid eggs by meiosis. If a haploid egg is not fertilized, it develops into a male, which is haploid, small and has no gut. If it is fertilized by a male, it becomes a thick-shelled *resting egg*, which sinks to the bottom, survives drying and freezing, and can remain viable for decades before hatching into an amictic female.

In *Brachionus* the switch to sex is triggered by a protein that the females release into the water; when its concentration exceeds a threshold, a fraction of the daughters become mictic (Snell et al. 2006). Density therefore acts as a signal of a population that is about to exhaust its food and is likely to experience a crash. The following model captures the logic. Let $A$ and $M$ be the densities of amictic and mictic females, $N = A + M$, and $E$ the number of resting eggs produced:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}t} &= b(F)\,A\left[1 - m(N)\right] - \delta A, &\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} &= b(F)\,A\,m(N) - \delta M, \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}t} &= -aNF, &\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} &= cM,
\end{aligned}
\tag{12.1}
$$
with $b(F) = rF/(F + F_{\mathrm{h}})$ and $m(N) = m_{\max}N^h/(N^h + N_{\mathrm{c}}^h)$.

Here $F$ is the food supply of a pond (relative to its initial value), consumed by the females at rate $a$ per female and not renewed; $b(F)$ is the birth rate, which saturates at the maximum $r$ when food is abundant ($F_{\mathrm{h}}$ is the food level at which it is halved); $\delta$ is the death rate; and $m(N)$ is the fraction of daughters that become mictic, which rises steeply (Hill coefficient $h$) around a critical density $N_{\mathrm{c}}$. Mictic females produce resting eggs at rate $c$. With realistic parameters (birth rates of 0.3–0.8 per day, lifespans of one to two weeks), the model shows that sex is postponed until the population is large, so that asexual growth first multiplies the number of females, and that resting eggs are produced mainly around the population peak, just before the decline caused by food depletion. Delaying sex too long risks a crash before many resting eggs are made; switching too early sacrifices asexual growth. Field and laboratory populations show the same pattern.

(B) The fraction of daughters becoming mictic, $m(N)$, and the cumulative number of resting eggs, most of which are produced around the population peak](./plots/cyclical-parthenogenesis.py)

The model also shows why the threshold should depend on the habitat. If the pond dries before the population peaks, only eggs produced before that date count, and a lower threshold, which starts sex earlier at the cost of slower growth, yields more resting eggs. In long seasons the population reaches its peak before the pond dries in any case, and the best threshold is close to the peak density that food allows. A threshold above the maximum density reached is fatal, because no resting eggs are made at all. In the shortest season the best threshold is low, about 12 females per mL, because the pond dries before a large population can build up; in longer seasons it lies near 60–70 females per mL, close to the peak density; thresholds above the peak density produce almost no resting eggs. Experiments agree with this reasoning. In *Brachionus* the mixis signal is a protein released by the females themselves, so that its concentration reflects density, and clones from different habitats differ heritably in their sensitivity to it. When laboratory populations were kept in regular cycles of short "hydroperiods", in which they were killed and restarted from resting eggs every few weeks, the propensity for sex and the production of resting eggs increased within a few dozen generations (Smith & Snell 2012).

### 12.3.3. Bdelloids: Ancient Asexuals

Bdelloid rotifers have been the most famous problem for theories of the evolution of sex ([7.10.14](/7.10.14#sec-14.3.2)). No male has ever been found among the hundreds of thousands examined; the class has diversified into some 460 species over tens of millions of years (they are known from amber tens of millions of years old); and asexual lineages are generally expected to go extinct quickly because they accumulate harmful mutations and adapt slowly. Several peculiarities may explain their success:

- Their genomes are degenerate tetraploids in which pairs of gene copies have diverged, but other pairs remain nearly identical, which suggests that the copies are kept similar by gene conversion, a form of recombination without meiosis.
- About 8–10% of their genes were acquired by horizontal gene transfer from bacteria, fungi and plants, the highest proportion known in animals (Gladyshev et al. 2008). Uptake of foreign DNA may occur when the animals dry out and their membranes and DNA are damaged and repaired.
- They survive desiccation at any stage of life (anhydrobiosis), in a dormant state in which metabolism stops entirely; they can be revived after years, and *Adineta* survived 24,000 years in Siberian permafrost (Shmakova et al. 2021). Desiccation also allows them to escape parasitic fungi and to be dispersed by wind: when Wilson and Sherman (2010) infected bdelloid populations with a lethal fungus, populations that were dried and stored for several weeks were cleared of the parasite, which dies when dry, and dried animals blown in a wind tunnel founded new, uninfected populations. Drying and dispersal may thus provide the escape from coevolving parasites that sex provides in other animals (the Red Queen hypothesis, [7.10.14](/7.10.14)).

Anhydrobiosis in bdelloids does not depend on trehalose, the disaccharide that accumulates in drying nematodes, brine shrimp cysts and yeast and is often cited as the key to cryptobiosis; no trehalose can be detected in drying or dried bdelloids (Lapinski & Tunnacliffe 2003). They rely instead on intrinsically disordered proteins such as LEA (late embryogenesis abundant) proteins and on efficient repair of DNA broken during drying. The general mechanisms of **cryptobiosis** are compared with those of tardigrades in [7.17.18](/7.17.18).

Evidence of some genetic exchange between individuals has been found in natural populations, so bdelloids may not be entirely asexual; but they remain the best example of an animal clade that has persisted and diversified for tens of millions of years without conventional sex.

### 12.3.4. Ecology

Rotifers are one of the three main groups of freshwater zooplankton, with cladocerans and copepods. They graze bacteria, algae and detritus, reproduce faster than crustaceans and are eaten by fish larvae and predatory invertebrates. Their density in eutrophic ponds can reach thousands per litre. Their populations change seasonally in density, species composition and form. Many planktonic rotifers show *cyclomorphosis*, seasonal changes of shape within a species, and some of these changes are defences induced by predators. In a classic series of experiments begun in the 1960s, Gilbert showed that *Brachionus calyciflorus* produces daughters with long posterolateral spines when cultured in water that has contained the predatory rotifer *Asplanchna*; the spines are induced by a chemical released by the predator (a **kairomone**), and spined individuals are rejected more often when caught. *Asplanchna* itself is polymorphic: females fed on large prey or on food containing α-tocopherol (vitamin E) produce large, humped or cruciform daughters that can eat larger prey, including the spined *Brachionus*, a chain of induced responses between predator and prey.

*Brachionus plicatilis* and *B. calyciflorus* are cultured in large quantities as live food for the larvae of fish and crustaceans in aquaculture, and rotifers are used as indicators of water quality and in ecotoxicology.

## 12.4. Acanthocephala: Thorny-Headed Worms

**Definition 12.4.1 (Acanthocephala)** The **Acanthocephala** (thorny-headed worms) are endoparasitic syndermatans without a gut, with an eversible anterior proboscis armed with recurved hooks, which attach in the intestine of a vertebrate definitive host; their larvae develop in an arthropod intermediate host.

Acanthocephalans are 1 mm to more than 60 cm long (*Macracanthorhynchus hirudinaceus* of pigs, whose females reach 65 cm). Their body consists of a presoma, with the proboscis, its receptacle and a neck, and a trunk. The proboscis can be retracted into the receptacle by muscles and everted by hydrostatic pressure, driven by fluid in two elongated sacs, the *lemnisci*, that hang from the neck into the body cavity. The tegument is a thick syncytium with the intracytoplasmic lamina of rotifers, penetrated by a system of fluid-filled lacunar canals, and it takes up nutrients from the host's gut, as in tapeworms. There is no gut at any stage.

The sexes are separate. After copulation, the male seals the female's genital opening with a secretion of its cement glands. In the female the ovary breaks up into ovarian balls that float in the body cavity; fertilized eggs develop into shelled larvae (acanthors) and are sorted by a unique organ, the *uterine bell*, which passes mature eggs to the uterus and returns immature ones to the body cavity.

The life cycle involves two hosts:

1. Eggs, each containing an acanthor larva with hooks, are released with the faeces of the vertebrate host.
2. The eggs are eaten by an arthropod (an amphipod, isopod, ostracod or insect larva), in whose body cavity the acanthor develops into an infective *cystacanth*.
3. The definitive host is infected when it eats the arthropod. Some species pass through paratenic hosts, in which the cystacanth survives without developing.

Many acanthocephalans alter the behaviour of their intermediate host in ways that increase the chance that it will be eaten by the definitive host. Freshwater amphipods (*Gammarus*) infected by *Pomphorhynchus laevis* lose their avoidance of light and are attracted rather than repelled by the odour of fish, the definitive host; amphipods infected by *Polymorphus paradoxus*, whose definitive hosts are ducks, skim along the water surface and cling to vegetation when disturbed, so that they are easily caught by dabbling ducks. The behavioural changes are mediated in part by changes in the serotonin system of the host. Such *host manipulation* is a recurrent theme in parasites with trophic transmission, from acanthocephalans to trematodes ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)) and nematomorphs ([7.17.17](/7.17.17)), and is an example of an extended phenotype (Dawkins 1982).

That manipulation actually increases transmission was shown by field and laboratory experiments. Bethel and Holmes (1973, 1977) compared two acanthocephalans of *Gammarus lacustris* in Canadian lakes: amphipods with cystacanths of *Polymorphus paradoxus*, whose hosts are mallards and muskrats that feed at the surface, became positively phototactic and clung to floating material, whereas those with *P. marilis*, whose hosts are diving ducks, stayed in the illuminated middle of the water column; in each case the infected amphipods were eaten disproportionately by the appropriate host. Moore (1983) showed that terrestrial isopods (*Armadillidium vulgare*) infected with *Plagiorhynchus cylindraceus*, a parasite of starlings, left shelter, rested on light-coloured surfaces and were much more often found among the prey brought by starlings to their nestlings than uninfected isopods, although they made up only a small fraction of the isopod population. The behavioural change begins only when the cystacanth becomes infective; before that, some species even make their hosts more cautious, which protects the parasite from being eaten by a host in which it could not develop.

Four classes are recognized, which differ in their hosts and in the structure of the body wall:

| Class | Hosts | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Archiacanthocephala | Birds and mammals; insects or myriapods as intermediate hosts | Large; lacunar canals dorsal and ventral; protonephridia in some | *Macracanthorhynchus*, *Moniliformis* |
| Palaeacanthocephala | Fishes, birds, aquatic mammals; amphipods and isopods as intermediate hosts | Lacunar canals lateral; trunk often spiny | *Pomphorhynchus*, *Polymorphus*, *Acanthocephalus* |
| Eoacanthocephala | Fishes, amphibians, turtles; ostracods and copepods as intermediate hosts | Small; few giant nuclei in the tegument | *Neoechinorhynchus* |
| Polyacanthocephala | Fishes and crocodilians | Many rows of proboscis hooks | *Polyacanthorhynchus* |

**Example 12.4.1 (*Macracanthorhynchus hirudinaceus*)** The giant thorny-headed worm of pigs is the largest acanthocephalan and the one described in Chinese textbooks as the representative species.

- *Form.* Females reach 20–65 cm and males 5–10 cm; the milky white, slightly flattened, transversely wrinkled trunk tapers backward. The small, globular proboscis carries about six spiral rows of six recurved hooks and is retracted into a receptacle by retractor muscles; it is everted by the pressure of the lemnisci and of the fluid in the body cavity.
- *Body wall.* There is no gut. The syncytial tegument contains a small number of giant nuclei, a network of lacunar canals that distributes nutrients, and numerous crypts that increase the absorptive surface; it is covered by a glycocalyx that protects it from the host's enzymes. Beneath it lie circular and longitudinal muscles, which are themselves tubular and connected with the lacunar system.
- *Reproduction.* The male has two testes, cement glands and a copulatory bursa; the female's ovary breaks up into ovarian balls in the body cavity, and the uterine bell sorts mature eggs, each with an acanthor, from immature ones. A female may contain millions of eggs and release hundreds of thousands a day.
- *Life cycle.* The thick-shelled eggs survive for years in the soil. They are eaten by the larvae of scarab beetles (dung beetles and chafers), in whose body cavity the acanthor becomes an acanthella and then an infective cystacanth in two to three months. Pigs, and occasionally other mammals, are infected by eating the grubs or adult beetles. The worm attaches to the wall of the small intestine with the proboscis and causes inflammation, ulcers and sometimes perforation.
- *Medical importance.* Human infections occur, mostly in children who have eaten raw or roasted beetles; most reported cases come from northern and eastern China, especially Liaoning and Shandong. The infection causes abdominal pain and can lead to intestinal perforation.

## 12.5. Chaetognatha: Arrow Worms

**Definition 12.5.1 (Chaetognatha)** The **Chaetognatha** (arrow worms) are marine, transparent, torpedo-shaped predatory worms whose head bears chitinous grasping spines and teeth that can be covered by a hood, with lateral and caudal fins supported by rays; they are hermaphrodites with direct development.

Chaetognaths (about 130 species) are among the most abundant predators of the marine plankton, in places second only to copepods in numbers; most are 1–3 cm long. The body is divided by transverse septa into head, trunk and tail. The head bears two eyes, a vestibule leading to the ventral mouth, rows of anterior and posterior teeth, and on each side 4–14 large, curved grasping spines made of α-chitin. A fold of the body wall, the hood, can be drawn over the spines during swimming to reduce drag and pulled back to strike. The trunk and tail have one or two pairs of lateral fins and a caudal fin, with a mesh of ciliary fence receptors (sensory hair cells) that detect the vibrations of swimming prey. Arrow worms hover motionless, then dart forward by a flick of the longitudinal muscles and seize copepods and fish larvae with the spines; some species, and the bacteria associated with them, produce tetrodotoxin, which paralyses the prey.

The nervous system has a cerebral ganglion and a large ventral ganglion in the trunk, connected by circumoesophageal connectives. There is no circulatory or excretory system. Each individual has ovaries in the trunk and testes in the tail; sperm is exchanged as spermatophores. The eggs develop directly into juveniles.

Several features of chaetognaths are unusual among invertebrates. The epidermis is multilayered over most of the body, a stratified epithelium otherwise known only in vertebrates. Behind the eyes, a looped band of ciliated cells on the dorsal surface, the corona ciliata, is probably a chemosensory or water-current organ. The body wall muscles are organized in four quadrants of longitudinal fibres, of two types: primary muscles, which are cross-striated and among the fastest of any animal, and secondary muscles for slower movement; there are no circular muscles. The gut is a straight tube from the mouth to an anus at the junction of trunk and tail; prey is swallowed whole and digested within an hour or two. The body cavities of head, trunk and tail are lined by a thin peritoneum and separated by septa; the trunk cavity contains the ovaries, the tail cavity the testes, and the fluid in them serves for transport in the absence of a circulatory system.

The sperm mature in the tail cavity, are packed into spermatophores in a pair of seminal vesicles at the sides of the tail, and are transferred to a partner or to the same individual: self-fertilization occurs. In the planktonic species the eggs float freely or are attached to the body; in the benthic *Spadella* they are glued to the substrate. The hatchling already has the body plan of the adult.

About 130 species are divided into two orders: Phragmophora, with transverse ventral muscles (phragms) in the trunk, including the benthic *Spadella* and the deep-water *Eukrohnia*, and Aphragmophora, without them, including most of the familiar planktonic species of *Sagitta* and related genera. Chaetognaths are important predators in all seas and can consume a large fraction of the daily production of copepods. In the East China Sea 21 species have been recorded; the dominant species, *Flaccisagitta enflata* (formerly *Sagitta enflata*), occurs from the surface to 250 m but has about 75% of its individuals in the upper 50 m, and its abundance tracks warm currents, which makes it an indicator of water masses. Many species migrate vertically each day, rising at night with their copepod prey.

Few phyla have moved so far in the animal tree. Classical embryology placed chaetognaths among the deuterostomes: their cleavage is radial, the blastopore becomes the anus, and the body cavities form by folding of the archenteron, as in enterocoely. Molecular data from the 2000s placed them firmly among the protostomes, and recent phylogenomic analyses put them as the sister group of Gnathifera (Marlétaz et al. 2019). Their grasping spines and the jaws of gnathiferans may be homologous, and the Cambrian fossil *Amiskwia*, once thought to be a chaetognath or a nemertean, has been reinterpreted as a gnathiferan with jaws (Caron & Cheung 2019). The Cambrian protoconodonts, spine-like microfossils, are probably the grasping spines of stem chaetognaths, so arrow worms have been among the predators of the plankton for over 500 million years. Their body cavities, which line the trunk, are not homologous to the enterocoelic coelom of deuterostomes.

## 12.6. Dicyemida and Orthonectida

Dicyemids and orthonectids were once united as the **Mesozoa**, animals supposed to be intermediate between protists and metazoans because they consist of only a few dozen cells; Chinese textbooks treat them as a phylum Mesozoa with two classes, Rhombozoa and Orthonectida, placed before the sponges. Both are in fact highly reduced spiralian parasites, and they are probably not closely related to each other.

**Definition 12.6.1 (Dicyemida)** The **Dicyemida** (Rhombozoa) are minute endoparasites of the renal sacs of cephalopods whose body consists of a single long axial cell surrounded by a fixed number of ciliated peripheral cells, without organs; they reproduce asexually within the axial cell and sexually through hermaphroditic infusorigens that produce ciliated infusoriform larvae.

The Dicyemida (about 120 species) live in the renal sacs of cephalopods, attached by their anterior end to the kidney epithelium, where they feed on urine. An adult is a worm 0.5–7 mm long made of only 10–40 cells: a single long axial cell surrounded by a constant number of ciliated peripheral cells. Inside the axial cell, agametes develop asexually into vermiform embryos (when the host population is small) or, at high density, into hermaphroditic gonads, the infusorigens, which produce ciliated *infusoriform larvae* that leave with the host's urine. How the next cephalopod is infected is unknown. Dicyemids have the smallest number of cells of any animal and have lost almost all organs, but they retain spiralian genes, including Hox genes. The infusoriform larva, of 37–40 cells in a fixed arrangement, has two large refringent bodies of magnesium inositol hexaphosphate that make it heavy, so that it sinks after leaving the host; it has been suggested that it infects young cephalopods on the sea floor. Each species of dicyemid is specific to one or a few species of host, and several species can live in the same kidney, segregated by the position at which they attach.

**Definition 12.6.2 (Orthonectida)** The **Orthonectida** are endoparasites of marine invertebrates that live in the host as a multinucleate plasmodium, which produces free-living, ciliated males and females of a few hundred cells, with muscle and nerve cells but no gut; after mating outside the host, the female releases ciliated larvae that infect new hosts.

The Orthonectida (about 25 species) parasitize flatworms, nemerteans, molluscs, annelids, brittle stars and other invertebrates. Inside the host they form a multinucleate plasmodium, which produces free-living males and females, a few hundred cells each, that leave the host, mate and release ciliated larvae. The genome of *Intoshia variabili* is among the smallest in animals and has lost many genes, but it retains a reduced nervous system of about ten neurons. Phylogenomic analyses place orthonectids within or close to the Annelida (Schiffer et al. 2018), and dicyemids elsewhere in the Spiralia; both show how far parasitism can simplify a spiralian body.

## 12.7. The Fate of the "Pseudocoelomates"

In older classifications, including the standard Chinese textbooks, the phyla with a fluid-filled body cavity not lined by mesoderm (a **pseudocoel**) were united as the "pseudocoelomates" or Aschelminthes (Hyman 1951), sometimes as one phylum with several classes. The groups share, besides the type of body cavity, a cuticle, a muscular pharynx, a constant number of cells in many species and small size. Molecular phylogenetics shows that these features evolved repeatedly: the "pseudocoelomates" are a **grade**, not a clade, and their members belong to two different branches of the protostome tree.

|---|---|---|

| Priapulida, Kinorhyncha, Loricifera | Ecdysozoa: Scalidophora | [7.17.17](/7.17.17) |
| Rotifera, Acanthocephala | Spiralia: Gnathifera (Syndermata) | This chapter |
| Gnathostomulida (sometimes included) | Spiralia: Gnathifera | This chapter |
| Gastrotricha | Spiralia: Rouphozoa, sister group of flatworms | [7.17.11](/7.17.11) |
| Entoprocta, Cycliophora | Spiralia: near Bryozoa and other lophophorates | [7.17.16](/7.17.16) |
| Chaetognatha (treated with the deuterostomes) | Spiralia: sister group of Gnathifera | This chapter |
| Mesozoa (a phylum with Rhombozoa and Orthonectida, at the base of the Metazoa) | Two separate groups of reduced spiralians | This chapter |

The Chinese textbooks already discuss the molecular results that dismantled the pseudocoelomates: they report that rotifers belong to the Lophotrochozoa and nematodes to the Ecdysozoa, and that gnathostomulids, micrognathozoans, rotifers and acanthocephalans form the Gnathifera. They nevertheless keep the pseudocoelomates as a chapter, arguing that a single gene (the small-subunit rRNA) should not override the evidence of body organization. Phylogenomic analyses of hundreds of genes have since confirmed the rRNA results, so the reservation no longer applies; the chapter is best read as a description of a grade.

Phyla traditionally placed in this group are marked with dots on the current protostome tree (simplified, after Laumer et al. 2019 and Marlétaz et al. 2019): they belong to two different branches, the Ecdysozoa (moulting animals: nematodes, nematomorphs and the scalidophorans) and the Spiralia (gnathiferans, gastrotrichs, entoprocts and cycliophorans). The pseudocoel itself can be understood as a consequence of small size and of the loss of mesodermal linings, a condition that recurs whenever development is simplified. Many small spiralians and ecdysozoans are thought to have evolved from larger ancestors by miniaturization and progenesis, retaining the body cavity of a larva in the adult. "Pseudocoelomate", like "acoelomate", is therefore a useful descriptive term for a type of organization, but not a name for a group of related animals.

## Summary

- Gnathifera (Definition 12.1.1) are spiralians with homologous cuticular jaws: Gnathostomulida (Definition 12.1.2: interstitial, sulphide sediments, monociliated epidermis, basal plate and jaws, blind gut), Micrognathozoa (Definition 12.1.3: *Limnognathia*, the most complex jaws, a periodic anal pore) and Rotifera with Acanthocephala; Chaetognatha are their sister group. Gnathifera is the sister group of all other spiralians.
- Rotifers (Definition 12.2.1) have a syncytial epidermis with an intracytoplasmic lamina (often a lorica), a corona (Definition 12.2.2), a mastax with trophi (Definition 12.2.3) whose types reflect diet, a pseudocoel, protonephridia with a bladder, and often eutely.
- Corona types (buccal field, trochal discs, trochus–cingulum, funnel, reduced) and trophi types reflect the mode of feeding; *Brachionus calyciflorus* (Example 12.2.1) shows the planktonic monogonont organization.
- Classes (Definition 12.3.1): Seisonidea (sexual), Bdelloidea (only parthenogenetic) and Monogononta (cyclical parthenogenesis, Definition 12.3.2). In monogononts, amictic females multiply asexually; density-dependent mixis (Eq. (12.1)) produces males and resting eggs near the population peak, and the best threshold is lower in short-lived habitats.
- Predator-induced spines in *Brachionus* (kairomones from *Asplanchna*) and the polymorphism of *Asplanchna* are classic examples of inducible defences.
- Bdelloids are ancient asexuals with degenerate tetraploid genomes, extensive horizontal gene transfer and anhydrobiosis without trehalose; drying and wind dispersal let them escape parasitic fungi.
- Acanthocephala (Definition 12.4.1) are gutless parasitic rotifers with a hooked proboscis, lemnisci, a uterine bell, and a two-host life cycle with arthropod intermediate hosts whose behaviour they manipulate (shown experimentally for *Polymorphus* and *Plagiorhynchus*); four classes; *Macracanthorhynchus hirudinaceus* (Example 12.4.1) of pigs, with scarab beetle larvae as intermediate hosts, occasionally infects people.
- Chaetognatha (Definition 12.5.1) are planktonic predators with chitinous grasping spines; their deuterostome-like embryology misled classification, and they are now related to Gnathifera. They have a stratified epidermis, fast striated muscles in four quadrants and a tail cavity with the testes; *Flaccisagitta enflata* dominates the East China Sea.
- Dicyemida (Definition 12.6.1) and Orthonectida (Definition 12.6.2), the former "Mesozoa", are highly reduced spiralian parasites of cephalopod kidneys and of various invertebrates.
- The "pseudocoelomates" are a grade: their members belong to Ecdysozoa (nematodes, nematomorphs, scalidophorans) and Spiralia (gnathiferans, gastrotrichs, entoprocts).

## Questions

1. Why were chaetognaths classified as deuterostomes, and what evidence moved them to the protostomes?

   **Answer** Their embryology resembles that of classical deuterostomes: radial cleavage, the blastopore becoming the anus, the mouth forming secondarily, and body cavities formed by folding from the archenteron in a manner resembling enterocoely. Molecular phylogenies (rRNA, then large sets of nuclear genes) consistently group them with protostomes, and recent phylogenomic analyses place them as the sister group of Gnathifera, consistent with features such as the chitinous grasping spines that may correspond to gnathiferan jaws and with Hox genes of protostome type. The embryological characters are now seen as labile and convergent.
2. In the model of Eq. (12.1), what would be the consequence for a rotifer population in a short-lived pond if the critical density for mixis $N_{\mathrm{c}}$ were much higher than the carrying capacity $K$? And if it were very low?

   **Answer** If $N_{\mathrm{c}} \gg K$, the density never reaches the threshold, $m(N)$ stays near zero, no mictic females appear and no resting eggs are produced; when the pond dries up, the population dies without leaving survivors. If $N_{\mathrm{c}}$ is very low, mixis begins early, a large fraction of daughters become mictic while the population is still small, asexual growth is curtailed, and the total number of resting eggs is small because few females are available to produce them. An intermediate threshold maximizes the number of resting eggs; populations in ponds with short seasons evolve lower thresholds.
3. How can an acanthocephalan that lives in a fish benefit from changing the behaviour of an amphipod?

   **Answer** The parasite can reach its definitive host only if the amphipod in which its cystacanth develops is eaten by a fish. Amphipods normally avoid light and fish odour, which keeps them hidden. A parasite that makes its host swim toward light or toward fish increases the chance that the host is eaten by the right predator, and so increases its own transmission. Because the genes of the parasite affect the behaviour of the host, this is an extended phenotype favoured by natural selection on the parasite.
4. In what sense are rotifers "pseudocoelomates", and why is this no longer a reason to classify them with nematodes?

   **Answer** Their body cavity is a fluid-filled space between the gut and body wall that is not lined by mesodermal epithelium and derives from the embryonic blastocoel, which is the definition of a pseudocoel. Nematodes have the same type of cavity. But the molecular phylogeny places rotifers among the spiralians and nematodes among the ecdysozoans, so the pseudocoel must have arisen independently, probably as a result of small size and simplified development. A shared condition that arose more than once is a grade character, not evidence of relationship.
5. *Brachionus calyciflorus* grown in water from an *Asplanchna* culture produces daughters with long spines. Design an experiment to test whether the spines are an adaptive defence, and explain why the spines are not produced all the time.

   **Answer** Rear sister females of one clone in water with and without the *Asplanchna* signal, obtaining spined and unspined daughters of the same genotype. Offer equal numbers of each to hungry *Asplanchna* and record encounters, captures and rejections; if the spines are a defence, spined individuals should be rejected or released more often and survive better. To find the cost, compare the birth rates, swimming speeds and lifespans of spined and unspined females in the absence of predators. Spines are produced only when the predator is present because they are costly (material and energy for a longer lorica, possibly slower swimming or lower fecundity) and useless without the predator; an inducible defence is favoured when the predator is variable in time and its presence can be detected reliably by a chemical cue.
6. Why do gnathostomulids, which live among sand grains a fraction of a millimetre wide, have a monociliated epidermis and no body cavity, and why would they be difficult to discover?

   **Answer** Animals between sand grains must be very small, slender and able to move in films of water; ciliary gliding is effective at this size, where viscous forces dominate, and a body without a cavity, with the space between gut and body wall filled by a few cells, is sufficient for an animal whose tissues are all close to the surface and can exchange gases and wastes by diffusion. Their small size, transparency and habitat, the sulphide-rich sediments below the oxidized surface layer where most sampling methods do not reach and which require special extraction techniques, explain why the phylum was recognized only in the second half of the twentieth century.
