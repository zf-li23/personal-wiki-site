Birds are the living dinosaurs ([7.17.52](/7.17.52)): feathered, toothless, endothermic, bipedal archosaurs whose forelimbs are wings. With about 11 000 species they are the most species-rich class of terrestrial vertebrates, found on every continent and ocean, from the Antarctic ice to tropical forests, and from sea level to over 8 000 m on migration. Flight shapes almost every part of their body, from the fused skeleton and the one-way lungs to the reduction of the right ovary, and even flightless birds (ostriches, penguins, kiwis) are descended from fliers and retain most of the flight design. At the same time birds are among the best-studied animals, and much of what is known about migration, navigation, song learning, sexual selection and population ecology was first learned from them.

This chapter first defines birds and outlines their phylogeny and classification (§53.1–53.2), then treats feathers (§53.3), the skeleton and muscles of flight (§53.4) and the mechanics of flight (§53.5). Sections 53.6–53.8 cover feeding and locomotion, the internal organ systems and the senses; §53.9 reproduction; §53.10 migration. Section 53.11 gives the structure of the domestic pigeon, the representative bird of Chinese textbooks, §53.12 the birds of China, and §53.13 the correspondence with the traditional classification. The physiology of the bird lung, of digestion in crop and gizzard and of thermoregulation is treated in 7.14 ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.4), [7.14.26](/7.14.26#sec-26.10.3), [7.14.31](/7.14.31)); the behaviour of birds (song, courtship, the mechanisms of orientation) is left to 7.18.

## 53.1. Characters of Birds

**Definition 53.1.1 (Aves)** The **Aves** (birds) are the crown group of avialan dinosaurs: the last common ancestor of all living birds and all its descendants (Neornithes). Birds have feathers; forelimbs modified as wings with a carpometacarpus and reduced digits; a toothless beak covered by keratin; a keeled sternum (reduced in some flightless forms); a furcula; a pygostyle; a synsacrum fused with the ilium; a tibiotarsus and tarsometatarsus with an intertarsal ankle joint; pneumatic bones connected to air sacs; a completely divided heart with a right aortic arch; and endothermy.

The broader group Avialae includes all dinosaurs closer to living birds than to deinonychosaurs, among them *Archaeopteryx*, *Confuciusornis* and the enantiornithines ([7.17.52](/7.17.52#sec-52.7.5)); "bird" in everyday usage, and in the traditional class Aves, covers both. Most avian characters were acquired step by step in theropods: hollow bones and the furcula in early theropods, feathers in coelurosaurs, the wing in paravians, the pygostyle in pygostylians, the keel in ornithothoracines, the toothless beak in crown birds.

Birds are remarkably uniform in body plan compared with mammals. Body size ranges from the bee hummingbird (*Mellisuga helenae*, 2 g) to the common ostrich (*Struthio camelus*, up to 150 kg), and extinct flightless birds were larger still: the elephant birds of Madagascar (*Vorombe*, about 650 kg) and the moas of New Zealand (*Dinornis*, up to 3.6 m tall), both exterminated by humans within the last thousand years. Among flying birds, the heaviest living species are bustards and swans of 15–18 kg, and the extinct soaring *Argentavis* (Miocene, Argentina) reached about 70 kg with a wingspan of about 6–7 m.

## 53.2. Phylogeny and Classification

### 53.2.1. The Major Lineages

Living birds divide into two groups, first recognized by their palates:

- *Palaeognathae* (about 65 species): the flightless ratites (ostriches, rheas, emus, cassowaries, kiwis, and the extinct moas and elephant birds) and the tinamous of Central and South America, which can fly. Their palate retains a rigid connection between the vomer and the pterygoid (palaeognathous palate), and their sternum lacks a keel in ratites. Molecular studies showed that the tinamous are nested within the ratites, as the sister group of the moas, and that the kiwis are the sister group of the elephant birds of Madagascar rather than of the moas of New Zealand (Mitchell et al. 2014). Flightlessness therefore evolved several times among palaeognaths, from flying ancestors that dispersed between continents.
- *Neognathae* (all other birds), with a flexible palate. They divide into the *Galloanserae* — the gamebirds (Galliformes) and waterfowl (Anseriformes) — and the *Neoaves*, the vast majority of birds.

The relationships within Neoaves were long unresolved. Large phylogenomic studies (Jarvis et al. 2014; Prum et al. 2015; Stiller et al. 2024, with genomes of 363 species) found several stable groups: the Strisores (nightjars, swifts and hummingbirds), the Aequornithes ("core water birds": loons, penguins, tubenoses, storks, cormorants, pelicans and herons) and the Telluraves ("core land birds": hawks, owls, kingfishers, woodpeckers, falcons, parrots and passerines). Unexpected results include the grouping of flamingos with grebes, of falcons with parrots and passerines (not with hawks), and of hoopoes with hornbills. The order of branching at the base of Neoaves remains uncertain, because the main lineages diverged within a few million years after the K–Pg extinction ([7.17.52](/7.17.52#sec-52.7.5)), a rapid **adaptive radiation** that left little phylogenetic signal.

## 53.3. Feathers

### 53.3.1. Structure

A typical contour feather ([7.17.52](/7.17.52#sec-52.7.2) for its development) consists of:

- the *calamus* (quill), a hollow tube inserted in the follicle;
- the *rachis*, the solid shaft above the calamus, which bears the vane;
- the *vane*, made of a row of *barbs* on each side of the rachis; each barb bears two rows of *barbules*. The distal barbules (those on the side of the barb toward the feather tip) carry tiny hooks (hooklets, barbicels) that catch on the grooved proximal barbules of the next barb, like a zip fastener, so the vane forms a closed, coherent surface. When the barbs separate, the bird restores the vane by preening, drawing the feather through its bill.

Feathers are made of β-keratins (corneous β-proteins), and the follicle is innervated and supplied with muscles that erect or flatten the feather. Feathers are arranged in tracts (pterylae) separated by bare areas (apteria) except in penguins, ratites and a few others, which are evenly covered. A bird has from about 1 000 feathers (hummingbirds) to 25 000 (swans), and the plumage weighs two to three times as much as the skeleton.

### 53.3.2. Types of Feathers

| Type | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Contour feathers | Closed vane, rachis | Body covering, streamlining, colour; flight feathers are specialized contour feathers |
| Flight feathers: remiges (primaries on the hand, secondaries on the ulna) and rectrices (tail feathers on the pygostyle) | Long, stiff, asymmetrical vanes in remiges | Airfoil, thrust and steering |
| Down feathers (plumules) | Short or no rachis, loose barbs without hooklets | Insulation beneath the contour feathers; the natal down of chicks |
| Semiplumes | Rachis long, vane loose | Insulation, buoyancy in waterbirds |
| Filoplumes | A hair-like shaft with a few barbs at the tip, beside contour feathers; with sensory receptors at the base | Monitor the position of contour feathers |
| Bristles | Stiff rachis with few or no barbs | Protection of eyes and nostrils, sensing (around the mouths of nightjars and flycatchers) |
| Powder down | Barb tips disintegrate into a fine waxy powder | Waterproofing and cleaning (herons, parrots) |

Chinese textbooks distinguish three types of feathers: contour feathers, down and filoplumes (hair-like feathers); the others are subdivisions of these.

The number of primaries is usually 9–12, attached to the carpometacarpus and the phalanges of the second (major) digit; the secondaries, attached by their bases to the ulna (whose quill knobs often show on the bone), number from about 6 in hummingbirds to more than 30 in albatrosses. The tail usually has 12 rectrices. The small feathers of the first digit form the alula, which acts as a slot at the leading edge of the wing and delays stall at low speeds and high angles of attack.

### 53.3.3. Moult, Care and Colour

Feathers wear and are replaced by moult, usually once or twice a year, under the control of thyroid and sex hormones and of day length. Most birds replace their flight feathers in sequence, a few at a time on each wing, so they can keep flying; ducks, geese, grebes and many other waterbirds shed all their flight feathers at once and are flightless for 3–5 weeks, often in safe moulting grounds. A plumage of small birds costs a considerable amount of protein and energy, and moult is scheduled between breeding and migration. Birds care for their feathers by preening, bathing and dusting, and most have a single skin gland, the uropygial (preen) gland above the base of the tail, whose waxy secretion conditions the feathers ([7.17.57](/7.17.57)).

Feather colour has two sources:

- *Pigments*: melanins (black, brown, grey and reddish brown), produced by the bird's own melanocytes and deposited in melanosomes; carotenoids (red, orange and yellow), obtained from the diet and modified by the bird (the pink of flamingos from the carotenoids of algae and brine shrimp); and rarer pigments such as the psittacofulvins of parrots and the porphyrins and turacins of turacos.
- *Structural colours*: produced by the scattering of light by nanostructures of keratin, melanosomes and air. Non-iridescent blues (blue jays, kingfishers) come from a spongy keratin matrix in the barbs that scatters blue light coherently; iridescent colours (peacocks, hummingbirds, the neck of the pigeon) from thin layers or lattices of melanosomes in the barbules, whose colour changes with viewing angle.

Birds see ultraviolet light (§53.8), and many plumages that look uniform to us have ultraviolet patterns. Plumage serves in camouflage, species recognition, signalling of condition and **sexual selection** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)); in many species males are brightly coloured and females cryptic.

## 53.4. Skeleton and Muscles for Flight

### 53.4.1. The Skeleton

The avian skeleton combines lightness and rigidity. Many bones are fused, so that the body forms a rigid frame to which the wings and legs are attached, while the neck is long and flexible:

- *Skull*: thin bones fused into a single unit; large orbits; a toothless beak formed by the premaxilla and dentary covered by a keratin sheath (rhamphotheca); a mobile upper bill hinged to the braincase (prokinesis, [7.17.59](/7.17.59)); a single occipital condyle.
- *Neck*: 11–25 cervical vertebrae (14 in the pigeon, 25 in swans) with saddle-shaped (heterocoelous) articular surfaces that allow great flexibility; the head can be turned through 270° in owls.
- *Trunk*: thoracic vertebrae often fused into a notarium (in pigeons, chickens, hawks); ribs with uncinate processes that overlap the next rib, stiffening the rib cage and serving as levers for breathing muscles; the synsacrum, 10–23 fused lumbar, sacral and caudal vertebrae fused also with the ilium; a short series of free caudal vertebrae and the pygostyle, which supports the tail feathers and their muscles.
- *Sternum*: large, with a keel in flying birds (below).
- *Pectoral girdle*: a strut-like coracoid that braces the shoulder against the sternum and resists the compression produced by the downstroke; a blade-like scapula; the furcula ("wishbone"), formed by the fused clavicles, which flexes like a spring during the wingbeat.
- *Wing*: a long humerus, radius and ulna; two free carpal bones (radiale and ulnare); the carpometacarpus, formed by fusion of the distal carpals and three metacarpals; three reduced digits. Which digits they are — the first three (I–III), as in theropods, or the second to fourth (II–IV), as embryological condensations suggest — is a long-standing debate; the "frame-shift" hypothesis proposes that digits II–IV of the embryo developed the identities of I–III (Wagner and Gauthier 1999).
- *Hindlimb*: the femur is short and nearly horizontal, held close to the body; the tibia is fused with the proximal tarsals into the tibiotarsus; the fibula is reduced to a splint; the distal tarsals and metatarsals II–IV are fused into the tarsometatarsus; the ankle joint lies between them (intertarsal joint). The "backward knee" of a bird is its ankle.

**Definition 53.4.1 (Pneumatic bone)** A **pneumatic bone** is a bone whose marrow cavity is replaced by air spaces connected with the respiratory system; in birds the air spaces are diverticula of the air sacs that enter through foramina in the bone wall, and in pterosaurs and saurischian dinosaurs they are inferred from such foramina.

Pneumaticity varies with way of life: it is most extensive in large soaring birds (pelicans, vultures, hornbills, in which even the bones of the toes may be pneumatized) and reduced in diving birds (penguins, loons) and in small birds. Pneumatic bones are often said to make birds light, but the total mass of the skeleton relative to body mass is similar in birds and mammals of the same size, because bird bones are denser (Dumont 2010); the advantage lies in a light, stiff, thin-walled structure, and the air sacs also lower the body's density.

### 53.4.2. The Keel and the Flight Muscles

**Definition 53.4.2 (Keel)** The **keel** (carina) of birds is the median ventral ridge of the sternum, on whose sides and edge the two main flight muscles, the pectoralis and the supracoracoideus, originate.

The *pectoralis*, the largest muscle of a flying bird (15–25 % of body mass in strong fliers such as pigeons), originates on the keel and sternum and inserts on the underside of the humerus; it powers the downstroke. The *supracoracoideus*, lying beneath it against the keel, raises the wing. Although it lies below the wing, its tendon passes up and through the triosseal canal, an opening formed by the coracoid, scapula and furcula, and inserts on the upper side of the humerus, so it pulls the wing up like a rope over a pulley. The supracoracoideus is relatively large in birds that need a powered upstroke (hummingbirds, in which it is about half the mass of the pectoralis) and small in others (about one-tenth in pigeons), which raise the wing largely by aerodynamic forces and elastic recoil.

The keel is reduced or absent in flightless birds — the ratites, flightless rails, the kakapo — whereas in penguins, which "fly" underwater, it is well developed. Because the flight muscles of the pectoral region and the leg muscles of the pelvic region lie near the centre of gravity, and the distal parts of wings and legs are moved by long tendons, the limbs are light and the body compact.

### 53.4.3. Legs and the Perching Mechanism

The legs of birds carry the body on land, and in most birds the femur is held nearly horizontal and the knee lies within the body contour, so the centre of gravity lies above the feet. Many perching birds can sleep on a branch without falling off. Two mechanisms are involved. The tendons of the toe flexors pass behind the ankle, so that crouching (flexing the ankle) pulls the toes closed around the perch; and in passerines a ribbed surface on the flexor tendons engages ridges on the inside of their sheath, a ratchet-like locking mechanism that holds the toes closed with little muscle activity.

### 53.4.4. The Evolution of Flightlessness

Flight has been lost many times: in the palaeognaths at least four or five times, in rails perhaps more than 30 times, and in pigeons (the dodo and the Rodrigues solitaire), parrots (the kakapo), cormorants (the Galápagos cormorant), ducks (steamer ducks), grebes, auks (the great auk, exterminated in 1844), penguins and many other groups. Flightless birds share a set of changes: a reduced or absent keel, smaller flight muscles and wings, a lighter and less fused pectoral girdle, loss of the interlocking structure of the flight feathers, and larger legs and body size.

Most flightless birds evolved on islands without terrestrial mammalian predators, such as New Zealand, Madagascar, Mauritius and Hawaii, and in such places the costs of flight — the energy to maintain flight muscles, which are expensive tissue, and the energy spent in flying — outweigh its benefits. Flightless rails and ducks evolved within a few hundred thousand years on islands colonized by flying ancestors. The same conditions made them vulnerable when humans and introduced rats, cats and pigs arrived: most of the bird species exterminated in historic times were flightless island birds.

The genetic changes behind flightlessness have been studied in the ratites. Their genomes show accelerated evolution in many conserved non-coding elements, regulatory sequences near genes involved in limb and sternum development, and the same elements were affected independently in different ratite lineages, a case of convergent evolution through changes in gene regulation rather than in proteins (Sackton et al. 2019; [7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

## 53.5. Flight

### 53.5.1. Lift and Wing Loading

A wing generates lift because air flowing over its cambered, inclined surface is deflected downward. The lift is

$$
L = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho\,V^2 S\,C_L,
$$
where $\rho$ is air density, $V$ airspeed, $S$ the wing area and $C_L$ the lift coefficient, which increases with the angle of attack up to stall (about 1.2–1.6 for bird wings). In steady level flight lift equals weight, so the speed required rises with the *wing loading* $Mg/S$: birds with heavy bodies and small wings (ducks, loons, auks) must fly fast, while those with light wing loading (swallows, terns, butterflies among insects) can fly slowly. Under geometric similarity, mass scales as length cubed and wing area as length squared, so wing loading increases as $M^{1/3}$ and so does the minimum flight speed: large birds need long runs or high places to take off.

A second key parameter is the *aspect ratio*, the ratio of span to mean chord, $b^2/S$. A long, narrow wing (high aspect ratio, 15–18 in albatrosses) generates lift with less induced drag (the drag associated with the vortices shed at the wingtips) and is efficient for gliding; a short, broad wing (low aspect ratio, 4–6 in pheasants and many forest birds) allows rapid take-off and manoeuvring among obstacles.

### 53.5.2. The Power Curve

The power that a bird must produce to fly at a given speed was analysed by Colin Pennycuick (1968, 2008) with a model borrowed from helicopter aerodynamics. The mechanical power has three components:

- **Induced power**, the cost of producing lift by accelerating air downward; for a wing of span $b$ sweeping a disc of area $S_{\mathrm{d}} = \pi b^2/4$,

  $$
  P_{\mathrm{ind}} = \frac{k\,(Mg)^2}{2\,\rho\,V\,S_{\mathrm{d}}},
  $$
  which falls as speed increases (more air passes over the wings per unit time), with $k \approx 1.2$ a correction for non-ideal wakes;
- *Parasite power*, the cost of overcoming the drag of the body, $P_{\mathrm{par}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^3 S_{\mathrm{b}} C_{\mathrm{Db}}$, which rises steeply with speed ($S_{\mathrm{b}}$ is the frontal area of the body and $C_{\mathrm{Db}}$ its drag coefficient);
- *Profile power*, the cost of overcoming the drag of the flapping wings, roughly constant over the normal range of speeds.

The sum is a U-shaped curve. Two speeds are special. At the *minimum-power speed* $V_{\mathrm{mp}}$, the bottom of the curve, the bird uses the least energy per unit time; it is the best speed for staying aloft, as when searching for food. At the *maximum-range speed* $V_{\mathrm{mr}}$, found where a line from the origin is tangent to the curve, the bird uses the least energy per unit distance; it is the best speed for migration. For a pigeon the model gives $V_{\mathrm{mp}} \approx 13\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{mr}} \approx 20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, close to the speeds of free-flying pigeons. Measurements in wind tunnels (on cockatiels, magpies and others) confirmed the U shape, although it is shallower than predicted in some species.

![Model: Pennycuick's power curve. (A) Mechanical power required by a pigeon (0.4 kg, span 0.67 m) against airspeed: induced power falls and parasite power rises with speed, profile power is taken as constant; the minimum-power speed (filled point) and the maximum-range speed (open point, where a line from the origin is tangent to the curve) are marked. (B) Scaling under geometric similarity: the minimum power required per unit mass rises as the 1/6 power of mass, while the power available from the flight muscles per unit mass falls as about the −1/3 power; the curves cross (schematically placed at 12–15 kg) near the mass of the heaviest flapping fliers.](./plots/flight-power.py)

The model explains why there is an upper limit to the size of flapping birds. For geometrically similar birds, $V_{\mathrm{mp}} \propto M^{1/6}$ and the minimum power required scales as $M^{7/6}$, or $M^{1/6}$ per unit mass. The power available from the flight muscles is the work per contraction (proportional to muscle mass, $M$) times the wingbeat frequency, which falls with size as about $M^{-1/3}$; the available power per unit mass therefore falls as $M^{-1/3}$. The curves cross at a mass above which a bird cannot sustain level flapping flight. The heaviest flying birds, the great bustard (*Otis tarda*, males up to about 18 kg), swans and the kori bustard, are near this limit; they fly with little margin and take off with difficulty. Larger birds can fly only by soaring, as *Argentavis* did, or not at all.

### 53.5.3. Wing Shapes and Flight Modes

Wing shapes reflect ways of flying (Savile 1957):

| Wing type | Shape | Flight | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elliptical | Short, broad, low aspect ratio, slotted primaries | Rapid take-off, manoeuvring in vegetation | Pheasants, many passerines, woodpeckers |
| High-speed | Long, pointed, swept back, little slotting | Fast flapping, long-distance flight | Falcons, swifts, swallows, shorebirds, ducks |
| High-aspect-ratio (active soaring) | Very long and narrow | Dynamic soaring over the sea | Albatrosses, shearwaters, gulls |
| Slotted high-lift (passive soaring) | Broad, with deeply slotted primaries | Thermal soaring over land at low speed | Eagles, vultures, storks, cranes |

Birds use several modes of flight:

- *Flapping flight*: the downstroke produces most of the lift and thrust; in the upstroke the wing is flexed (and in small birds partly folded) to reduce drag.
- *Gliding*: flight without flapping, losing height at an angle set by the lift-to-drag ratio (about 10:1 in eagles, 20:1 or more in albatrosses).
- *Soaring*: gliding in rising air. Eagles, vultures and storks circle in thermals; albatrosses use *dynamic soaring*, gaining energy from the gradient of wind speed above the sea surface by climbing into the wind and descending downwind, and can travel thousands of kilometres with little flapping. Migrating raptors, storks and cranes follow routes that provide thermals and avoid crossing open sea.
- *Hovering*: in hummingbirds the wing rotates at the shoulder so that it produces lift on both strokes, the upstroke providing about a quarter of the weight support (Warrick et al. 2005); wingbeat frequencies are 20–80 Hz. Hovering is the most expensive mode of flight, and the metabolic rate of a hovering hummingbird is the highest of any vertebrate ([7.14.30](/7.14.30#sec-30.6.4)).
- *Intermittent flight*: small birds alternate flapping with gliding (flap-gliding) or with brief periods with the wings folded (bounding flight, in finches and woodpeckers), which saves power at high speeds.
- *Formation flight*: geese, cranes and ibises flying in V formation save energy by flying in the upwash of the wingtip vortices of the bird ahead; northern bald ibises adjust their wingbeats in phase with the bird in front to exploit it (Portugal et al. 2014).

## 53.6. Feet, Bills and Feeding

### 53.6.1. Feet and Locomotion

The feet of birds are as diverse as their bills, and toe arrangement is used in classification:

| Foot type | Arrangement | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Anisodactyl | Three toes forward, the hallux (first toe) back | Most birds, passerines |
| Zygodactyl | Toes 2 and 3 forward, 1 and 4 back | Parrots, woodpeckers, cuckoos; owls and the osprey can turn toe 4 back |
| Heterodactyl | Toes 3 and 4 forward, 1 and 2 back | Trogons |
| Syndactyl | Toes 2 and 3 (or 3 and 4) joined at the base | Kingfishers, hornbills |
| Pamprodactyl | All four toes forward | Swifts |
| Palmate | Three front toes joined by webs | Ducks, gulls |
| Totipalmate | All four toes joined by webs | Pelicans, cormorants |
| Lobate | Toes with lobes of skin | Grebes, coots |
| Cursorial | Toes reduced to three or two (ostrich) | Ratites, bustards |

Raptors have strong, curved talons for grasping prey; ostriches run at 70 km/h on two toes, the longer with a hoof-like nail. Penguins swim with flipper-like wings, loons and grebes with feet placed far back on the body, which makes them clumsy on land.

### 53.6.2. Bills

The bill is a bony core covered with a keratin sheath that grows continuously. Its shape reflects diet, and the adaptive radiation of bills is among the clearest examples of form matching function: the conical seed-cracking bills of finches and grosbeaks (with the powerful bite of the hawfinch), the crossed mandibles of crossbills that pry open conifer cones, the hooked bills of raptors and shrikes, the probe of hummingbirds and sandpipers, whose bill tips are sensitive to pressure changes in mud, the filter of flamingos, which feed with the head upside down, and the pouch of pelicans. The Galápagos finches show how bill shape evolves within years under natural selection, and how genes such as *BMP4* and *CaM* control the depth and length of the bill ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)).

### 53.6.3. Digestion

Birds have no teeth, and the gut does the work of chewing ([7.14.26](/7.14.26#sec-26.10.3), [7.17.63](/7.17.63)):

- The *crop*, a dilation of the oesophagus, stores food in many birds (gamebirds, pigeons, raptors). In pigeons, both sexes produce *crop milk*, a secretion of fat- and protein-rich cells sloughed from the crop lining under the control of prolactin, to feed the young; flamingos and male emperor penguins produce analogous secretions.
- The stomach has two parts: the glandular *proventriculus*, which secretes acid and pepsin, and the muscular *gizzard*, lined with a tough keratin-like cuticle, which grinds food with the help of swallowed grit. The gizzard is very muscular in seed eaters and reduced in fruit and nectar eaters.
- The intestine is relatively short; paired caeca are large in gamebirds and waterfowl, where bacteria ferment plant material, and in the grouse they are essential for digesting winter diets of conifer needles. The hoatzin (*Opisthocomus hoazin*) of South America ferments leaves in an enlarged crop, like a ruminant.
- The gut ends in the cloaca, which receives urine and faeces and the products of the gonads. The dorsal wall of the cloaca of young birds bears the *bursa of Fabricius*, the organ in which B lymphocytes mature, named after its discoverer and the source of the "B" in B cell ([7.15.1](/7.15.1)).

Birds process food quickly: fruit passes through a small frugivore in 15–30 minutes. Many birds regurgitate indigestible remains as pellets (owls, raptors, gulls), from which their diet can be studied.

## 53.7. Respiration, Circulation and Excretion

*Respiration.* The lungs of birds are small, rigid and nearly constant in volume, attached to the ribs; they are ventilated by a system of air sacs that act as bellows. Most birds have nine air sacs: a cervical pair, an unpaired clavicular sac, cranial and caudal thoracic pairs and an abdominal pair, with extensions into the bones. Air flows in one direction through the lung's gas-exchange tubes, the **parabronchi**, during both inspiration and expiration: on inspiration, fresh air passes to the caudal sacs and through the lung; on expiration, air from the caudal sacs passes through the lung again; the cranial sacs receive air that has passed through the lung and expel it. The cross-current arrangement of blood capillaries and air capillaries gives an efficiency of oxygen uptake higher than in mammals, important at high altitude: bar-headed geese fly over the Himalaya at more than 6 000 m, and Rüppell's griffon vulture has been recorded at 11 000 m. The physiology is treated in [7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.4); the anatomy is compared across vertebrates in [7.17.64](/7.17.64). The sound-producing organ of birds is the *syrinx*, at the junction of the trachea and bronchi, rather than the larynx; songbirds control its two sides independently and can produce two notes at once.

*Circulation.* The heart is four-chambered, with complete separation of the pulmonary and systemic circulations, as in mammals but independently derived ([7.14.16](/7.14.16#sec-16.8.4)); the systemic aorta is the right fourth aortic arch ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)). Bird hearts are relatively larger than those of mammals and pump more blood per beat; the heart of a small hummingbird beats more than 1 200 times a minute in flight. The red blood cells are nucleated.

*Excretion.* Birds are **uricotelic**: they excrete nitrogen as uric acid, which precipitates in the ureters and cloaca and leaves as a white paste with the faeces, with little loss of water ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.4.2)). The kidneys are three-lobed, and only some of their nephrons have loops of Henle, so birds can concentrate urine only about two to three times the osmotic concentration of plasma; urine is modified further in the cloaca and colon, where water and salt are reabsorbed. There is no urinary bladder except in the ostrich. Marine birds drink seawater and excrete the excess salt through *salt glands* above the eyes, which secrete a fluid twice as salty as seawater that drips from the nostrils ([7.14.24](/7.14.24#sec-24.7.3)).

*Thermoregulation.* Birds are **endotherms** with body temperatures of 39–42 °C, higher than those of most mammals. They insulate themselves with plumage, lose heat by panting and gular fluttering (birds have no sweat glands) and through unfeathered legs, which have countercurrent heat exchangers. Some, including hummingbirds, swifts and nightjars, save energy by entering torpor, and the common poorwill (*Phalaenoptilus nuttallii*) hibernates. Body size and appendage length vary with climate according to **Bergmann's rule** and **Allen's rule** ([7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.8)).

## 53.8. Senses and Brain

*Vision* is the dominant sense of most birds. The eyes are very large (in some raptors and owls, larger than the human eye) and often fill most of the skull. Birds have four types of cone, one sensitive to ultraviolet or violet light, and coloured oil droplets in the cones that sharpen colour discrimination; they are tetrachromatic. Raptors have two foveae in each eye, one for forward binocular vision and one for lateral vision, and a density of photoreceptors that gives an acuity two to three times that of humans in large eagles. A pleated, vascular structure, the pecten, projects from the retina into the vitreous body and is thought to nourish the retina, which lacks blood vessels. Birds accommodate by changing the curvature of the cornea and the lens with striated muscles ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)). Migratory birds sense the geomagnetic field, probably through a light-dependent reaction in the protein cryptochrome 4 in the retina (§53.10).

*Hearing* is acute in the frequency range of bird song (1–5 kHz). The barn owl (*Tyto alba*) locates prey in total darkness by sound alone: its facial disc of feathers focuses sound like a parabolic dish, and its ears are asymmetrically placed, one higher than the other, so that the difference in intensity between the ears gives elevation and the difference in arrival time gives azimuth ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.6)). Oilbirds (*Steatornis*) of South America and cave swiftlets (*Aerodramus*) of Asia, including the edible-nest swiftlets of southern China and Southeast Asia, navigate in dark caves by **echolocation** with audible clicks.

*Smell* was long thought to be weak in birds, but kiwis, which hunt earthworms at night, have nostrils at the tip of the bill; turkey vultures find carrion by smell; and albatrosses and petrels locate patches of plankton by the scent of dimethyl sulphide released when phytoplankton is grazed.

*Brain.* Bird brains are relatively large, comparable to those of mammals of the same size, with a large cerebellum (for flight) and optic lobes. The forebrain lacks the layered cortex of mammals, but its pallium has similar circuitry arranged in nuclei, and parrots and corvids pack neurons twice as densely as primates, so their forebrains contain as many neurons as those of monkeys (Olkowicz et al. 2016). Crows and parrots use and make tools, solve multi-step problems and recognize individual humans. Vocal learning — the learning of song by imitation, with a sensitive period, specialized brain nuclei and dialects — evolved independently in songbirds, parrots and hummingbirds and is a model for human speech; it is treated in 7.18.

## 53.9. Reproduction

### 53.9.1. Sex, Gonads and Eggs

Birds have ZW sex chromosomes: males are ZZ, females ZW ([7.5.26](/7.5.26#sec-26.4)). In most species only the left ovary and oviduct develop in females; the right regresses in the embryo. The loss is often interpreted as a weight saving for flight, but crocodylians have two functional ovaries and some early birds probably had one (fossil follicles in *Jeholornis*), and raptors often retain a small right ovary. Males have paired internal testes, which in seasonal breeders enlarge several hundredfold during the breeding season. Most birds mate by a "cloacal kiss", pressing the cloacae together for a second or two. About 3 % of species, among them ratites, tinamous, ducks, geese and cracids, have a phallus, which in some ducks is long and corkscrew-shaped (up to 42 cm in the Argentine lake duck, *Oxyura vittata*), matched by a corkscrew-shaped vagina that twists in the opposite direction, a result of sexual conflict over fertilization (Brennan et al. 2007).

An egg is formed in about a day: the yolk is released from the ovary, fertilized in the infundibulum of the oviduct, and coated with albumen, shell membranes and shell on its way down ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.2.1)). For the shell, a laying hen mobilizes calcium from medullary bone, a special bone formed in the marrow cavities under the influence of oestrogen before laying; its presence in fossils identifies egg-laying females ([7.17.52](/7.17.52#sec-52.7.5)).

### 53.9.2. Nests, Incubation and Development

Most birds build nests, ranging from a scrape in the ground (plovers) to the woven nests of weavers, the mud nests of swallows, the saliva nests of swiftlets and the communal nests of sociable weavers that house hundreds of birds. Clutch size ranges from one (albatrosses, many seabirds) to 15–20 (partridges, ducks). David Lack proposed that clutch size is set by the number of young the parents can raise, so it is larger at higher latitudes, where long summer days give more time for foraging, and in species whose young feed themselves.

Eggs are incubated at about 37–38 °C by one or both parents, who develop a bare, vascularized *brood patch* on the belly in which the skin warms the eggs directly. Incubation lasts from 10–11 days in small passerines to 80 days in albatrosses and kiwis. Megapodes (mound-builders of Australasia) do not incubate: they bury their eggs in mounds of rotting vegetation or in sand heated by the sun or volcanic activity, and the male regulates the temperature by adding or removing material; the young hatch fully feathered and independent.

Hatchlings range along a spectrum from precocial to altricial, which Chinese textbooks summarize as precocial and altricial young:

| | Precocial | Altricial |
|---|---|---|
| At hatching | Eyes open, covered with down, can walk and often feed themselves | Eyes closed, naked or nearly so, cannot regulate temperature, fed by the parents |
| Egg | Large, with much yolk (35–50 % of contents) | Smaller, less yolk (20–25 %) |
| Incubation | Longer | Shorter |
| Growth after hatching | Slower | Faster |
| Examples | Ducks, gamebirds, shorebirds, ratites | Passerines, pigeons, parrots, woodpeckers |

Intermediate states include semi-precocial (gulls, which can walk but are fed) and semi-altricial young (herons, hawks, owls, which are downy but stay in the nest).

### 53.9.3. Mating Systems and Parental Care

About 90 % of bird species are socially monogamous, and both parents care for the young, a much higher proportion than in mammals, where females alone feed the young with milk. Genetic paternity analysis has shown, however, that in most "monogamous" species some young are fathered by males other than the social partner (extra-pair paternity). Polygyny (one male with several females) occurs in species whose young need little care from the male (many gamebirds, manakins and birds of paradise, which display at leks), and polyandry in some shorebirds (phalaropes, jacanas), in which females are larger and brightly coloured and males incubate.

About 1 % of bird species are obligate *brood parasites*: they lay their eggs in the nests of other species and never raise their own young. The common cuckoo (*Cuculus canorus*) parasitizes more than 100 host species in Eurasia, including the reed warblers of China; each female lays eggs that mimic those of her host, and the cuckoo chick, hatching first, pushes the host's eggs out of the nest. Hosts reject odd eggs, and cuckoos evolve better mimicry: a classic **coevolution** arms race ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). The evolution of such behaviour is treated in 7.18.

## 53.10. Migration and Navigation

### 53.10.1. Patterns of Migration

About 40 % of the world's birds are regular migrants, moving seasonally between breeding and non-breeding areas. Chinese textbooks classify birds according to their seasonal status at a given place:

- *Residents*, present all year;
- *Summer visitors*, which breed and leave in autumn (swallows, cuckoos, many warblers in northern China);
- *Winter visitors*, which breed farther north and spend the winter (cranes, swans, many ducks);
- *Passage migrants*, which pass through in spring and autumn (many shorebirds);
- *Vagrants*, which appear outside their normal range.

Migration is fuelled by fat, stored before departure by hyperphagia: small songbirds double their body mass in a few weeks, and some shorebirds carry fat equal to their lean mass. Migrants remodel their bodies, shrinking the gut and liver before a long flight and rebuilding them at stopover sites. The records are extraordinary. A bar-tailed godwit (*Limosa lapponica*) tracked by satellite in 2022 flew nonstop from Alaska to Tasmania, 13 560 km in 11 days. Arctic terns (*Sterna paradisaea*) travel about 70 000 km a year between Arctic breeding grounds and the Antarctic. Common swifts (*Apus apus*) stay airborne for up to ten months, eating and sleeping on the wing (Hedenström et al. 2016).

### 53.10.2. Navigation

To migrate, a bird needs a compass (to keep a direction) and, to return to a particular place, a map. Experiments since the 1950s have identified several compasses:

- *Sun compass*: birds orient by the sun's azimuth, compensating for its movement by their circadian clock; shifting the clock of a pigeon by six hours shifts its homing direction by about 90° (Kramer, Schmidt-Koenig).
- *Star compass*: nocturnal migrants learn the centre of rotation of the night sky; indigo buntings raised under a planetarium sky rotating around a different star oriented relative to that star (Emlen 1967).
- *Magnetic compass*: birds sense the inclination (dip angle) of the geomagnetic field, distinguishing "poleward" from "equatorward" rather than north from south (Wiltschko and Wiltschko 1972); the mechanism is probably a radical-pair reaction in cryptochrome 4 in the retina.
- *Map cues*: homing pigeons use olfactory cues carried by winds and possibly magnetic intensity and inclination gradients to determine their position; young birds on their first migration rely on an inherited programme of direction and duration, whereas adults correct for displacement.

The physiological and behavioural mechanisms of orientation are treated in 7.18.

### 53.10.3. Flyways of China

China lies on three of the world's major flyways. The East Asian–Australasian Flyway, the richest in species, connects breeding grounds in Siberia, Mongolia and northeast China with wintering grounds in Southeast Asia and Australasia; about 50 million waterbirds use it. Its bottleneck is the intertidal mudflats of the Yellow Sea and Bohai Gulf, where shorebirds such as the bar-tailed godwit, the great knot and the critically endangered spoon-billed sandpiper (*Calidris pygmaea*) refuel; land reclamation destroyed more than a third of these mudflats between the 1950s and 2010s, and the decline of shorebird populations on the flyway has been linked to it. The Yellow Sea–Bohai Gulf migratory bird sanctuaries were inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2019 (with a second phase in 2024). The Central Asian Flyway crosses western China and the Tibetan Plateau, and the bar-headed goose (*Anser indicus*) crosses the Himalaya on it. The Beijing population of the common swift (*Apus apus pekinensis*), nesting in the gate towers of the old city, was tracked with geolocators from 2014 and shown to winter in southern Africa, after a journey of more than 15 000 km each way across Central Asia and the Arabian Peninsula.

The study of migration has been transformed by tracking technology: bird rings (banding, since 1899), radar, geolocators weighing less than a gram, GPS and satellite transmitters, and networks of automated radio receivers.

## 53.12. Birds of China and Their Conservation

China has about 1 500 species of birds, about one-seventh of the world's total, more than 100 of them endemic or nearly endemic. The Galliformes are particularly rich: China has more than 60 species of pheasants and partridges, including the endemic golden pheasant (*Chrysolophus pictus*), Reeves's pheasant (*Syrmaticus reevesii*), the brown eared pheasant (*Crossoptilon mantchuricum*) and the Chinese monal (*Lophophorus lhuysii*); the domestic chicken was domesticated from the red junglefowl (*Gallus gallus*) in Southeast Asia and southern China. The mountains of southwest China, the Hengduan Mountains in particular, are among the richest areas for birds in the temperate world.

The recovery of the crested ibis (*Nipponia nippon*) is a celebrated conservation success. Once found across East Asia, the species was thought extinct in the wild until seven birds were rediscovered in 1981 in Yangxian, Shaanxi. Protection of their nesting trees and the rice paddies where they feed, a ban on pesticides in the area, captive breeding and reintroductions have raised the population to several thousand birds, and the ibis has been reintroduced in Japan and Korea. Other species are less fortunate: the Chinese crested tern (*Thalasseus bernsteini*) numbers fewer than 100 birds; Baer's pochard (*Aythya baeri*), which breeds in northeast China, has declined to a few hundred; the yellow-breasted bunting (*Emberiza aureola*), once one of the most abundant birds of Eurasia, declined by about 90 % in two decades, largely through trapping for food on migration in southern China, and is now Critically Endangered.

China holds most of the world's populations of several cranes. The red-crowned crane (*Grus japonensis*) breeds in the wetlands of Zhalong in Heilongjiang and the Sanjiang Plain and winters on the coast of Jiangsu at Yancheng. The black-necked crane (*Grus nigricollis*) is the only crane that breeds on a high plateau, in the wetlands of the Tibetan Plateau, and winters in Guizhou, Yunnan and southern Tibet. Almost the entire population of the Siberian crane (*Leucogeranus leucogeranus*), about 4 000 birds, winters at Poyang Lake in Jiangxi, where its feeding depends on the water level that controls the growth of submerged plants.

Birds are also hosts of avian influenza viruses, which circulate naturally in waterfowl; the spread of highly pathogenic strains between poultry and wild birds along flyways is a problem for both conservation and public health ([7.11.27](/7.11.27)).

## Summary

- Birds (Definition 53.1.1): crown-group avialan dinosaurs with feathers, wings, a toothless beak, keel, furcula, pygostyle, synsacrum, pneumatic bones, a four-chambered heart and endothermy; about 11 000 species.
- Phylogeny: Palaeognathae (ratites, tinamous; flightlessness evolved repeatedly) and Neognathae (Galloanserae and Neoaves, which radiated rapidly after the K–Pg extinction); about 40 orders, with major changes from the traditional classification (falcons with parrots and passerines, flamingos with grebes, penguins with tubenoses).
- Feathers: calamus, rachis, barbs, barbules with hooklets; contour, flight, down, semiplume, filoplume, bristle and powder-down feathers; tracts; moult; pigment and structural colour.
- Skeleton: fusions (skull, notarium, synsacrum, pygostyle, carpometacarpus, tibiotarsus, tarsometatarsus), pneumatic bones (Definition 53.4.1), the keel (Definition 53.4.2) for the pectoralis and supracoracoideus, whose tendon runs through the triosseal canal as over a pulley; perching mechanism.
- Flight: lift and wing loading; aspect ratio; Pennycuick's power curve with induced, parasite and profile power; minimum-power and maximum-range speeds; an upper size limit of flapping flight near 15 kg; wing types and flight modes (flapping, gliding, thermal and dynamic soaring, hovering, intermittent and formation flight).
- Feeding and internal organs: diverse feet and bills; crop, proventriculus and gizzard; air sacs and parabronchi with one-way flow; syrinx; right aortic arch; uric acid and salt glands.
- Senses: tetrachromatic vision with ultraviolet cones, double foveae in raptors; sound localization in owls; echolocation in oilbirds and swiftlets; smell in kiwis, vultures and petrels; dense forebrain neurons in parrots and corvids.
- Reproduction: ZW sex, left ovary only, cloacal kiss or phallus; nests, brood patch, precocial and altricial young; social monogamy with extra-pair paternity; brood parasitism.
- Migration: fat storage and body remodelling; record flights; sun, star and magnetic compasses and map cues; the East Asian–Australasian Flyway and the Yellow Sea mudflats.
- China: about 1 500 species, rich in pheasants; recovery of the crested ibis; cranes; threatened species such as the Chinese crested tern and yellow-breasted bunting.

## Questions

1. Using the power-curve model, explain why a bird migrating over a long distance should fly faster than one searching for food, and why a headwind should make it fly faster still.

   **Answer** A bird searching for food stays aloft as long as possible for a given amount of fuel and should fly at the speed that minimizes power, $V_{\mathrm{mp}}$, the bottom of the U-shaped curve. A migrating bird should minimize the energy spent per unit distance, power divided by speed, which is minimized where a straight line from the origin touches the curve, $V_{\mathrm{mr}}$; because the curve rises slowly near its minimum, this point lies at a higher speed than $V_{\mathrm{mp}}$ (for the pigeon, about 20 instead of 13 m s⁻¹). In a headwind of speed $w$, ground speed is $V - w$, so the cost per unit ground distance is $P/(V - w)$; the tangent must then be drawn from the point $(w, 0)$ instead of the origin, and touches the curve at a higher airspeed. Birds should therefore fly faster into headwinds and slower with tailwinds, as radar studies of migrants confirm.
2. Why do the traditional superorders Ratitae and Impennes not correspond to natural groups?

   **Answer** Both were defined by features related to the loss of aerial flight. Ratites were grouped by a flat sternum without a keel and reduced wings, and penguins by flipper-like wings used for swimming. Molecular phylogenies place the tinamous, which fly and have a keel, inside the ratites, so the ratites without tinamous are paraphyletic, and flightlessness with keel loss evolved several times independently from flying ancestors (in ostriches, rheas, emus and cassowaries, kiwis and elephant birds, moas). Penguins are closely related to albatrosses and petrels within the Aequornithes and are derived from flying seabirds; their keel is large, because they fly underwater. Features associated with flightlessness are convergent adaptations and cannot define groups.
3. The pectoralis of a pigeon weighs about ten times as much as the supracoracoideus, while in a hummingbird the supracoracoideus is about half the mass of the pectoralis. Explain the difference.

   **Answer** The pectoralis powers the downstroke and the supracoracoideus the upstroke. In most birds, including pigeons in forward flight, the upstroke produces little aerodynamic force: the wing is flexed and partly folded, and it is raised by aerodynamic forces on the moving bird and elastic recoil, so the supracoracoideus needs to be only large enough to reposition the wing, and is especially important during take-off and slow flight. A hovering hummingbird has no forward speed, so the wing must produce lift on both strokes, inverting during the upstroke so that it pushes air downward; the upstroke provides about a quarter of the weight support, and the supracoracoideus must do substantial work. Muscle mass reflects the work each stroke must do.
4. A cuckoo chick is fed by its host parents even though it looks nothing like their own chicks. What evolutionary processes explain this, and why do hosts reject foreign eggs but rarely foreign chicks?

   **Answer** Brood parasitism imposes a high fitness cost on hosts, which lose their entire brood when the cuckoo chick ejects their eggs. Selection on hosts favours recognizing and rejecting cuckoo eggs, and selection on cuckoos favours eggs that mimic those of the host: a coevolutionary arms race. At the chick stage, the cuckoo chick has already destroyed the host's brood, and its begging calls (in the common cuckoo, sounding like a whole brood of host chicks) and wide gape stimulate the parents' feeding instinct. Rejecting chicks is hard to evolve because learning what one's own chicks look like is risky: a host that imprinted on a cuckoo chick in its first brood would afterwards reject its own chicks, a cost greater than that of occasionally raising a cuckoo. Egg recognition can be learned from the first eggs laid, which are the host's own. Some hosts in Australia do reject cuckoo chicks, and some cuckoo chicks mimic their hosts' young.
5. (Open) Birds and mammals both evolved endothermy, a four-chambered heart and high aerobic capacity. Compare how the two groups solved the problem of supplying oxygen during sustained activity, and discuss which solution is better suited to flight at high altitude.

   **Answer** Points to consider: (a) Ventilation — mammals use a tidal lung with blind alveoli ventilated by the diaphragm; birds use air sacs as bellows and rigid lungs with one-way flow through parabronchi. (b) Gas exchange — the cross-current arrangement in birds can bring arterial oxygen pressure above that of exhaled air, while the alveolar lung cannot exceed alveolar pressure; birds have very thin blood–gas barriers. (c) Circulation — both have completely divided hearts, but birds have larger hearts and higher cardiac output for their size. (d) Blood — bird haemoglobins of high-altitude species (bar-headed goose) have high oxygen affinity; mammals at altitude (yaks, deer mice) show similar adaptations. (e) The bird system is better at altitude, as shown by birds flying over the Himalaya where mammals of similar size could not exercise; the mammalian system is cheaper to build and allows vocalization through the larynx and a flexible thorax for suckling. Both evolved from the costal ventilation of early amniotes ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.4.2)), and the bird system may build on the unidirectional flow already present in archosaurs.
