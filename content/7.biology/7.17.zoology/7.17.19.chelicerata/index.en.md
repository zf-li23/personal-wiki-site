The Chelicerata are the sister group of all other living arthropods, the Mandibulata ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.9)). They comprise three very different groups: the marine sea spiders, the marine horseshoe crabs, and the arachnids, which are mostly terrestrial and include spiders, scorpions, harvestmen, mites and ticks. With about 113 000 described living species, chelicerates are second in diversity only to the insects, and mites alone may number in the millions of undescribed species.

The chapter follows the scheme of the arthropod chapters: first the body plan that defines the subphylum, emphasizing the features that differ from those of mandibulates (no antennae, no mandibles, external digestion, book gills and book lungs), then the groups in phylogenetic order. Spiders receive the most space, because their silk is one of the most remarkable materials made by any organism and its properties can be measured and compared quantitatively. Mites and ticks are introduced as **vectors** of disease, a subject that returns with the insects in [7.17.40](/7.17.40). The chapter ends with the question whether the arachnids form a clade, and with the adaptations that made arachnids among the first animals on land, a theme continued in [7.17.20](/7.17.20).

## 19.1. The Chelicerate Body Plan

### 19.1.1. Prosoma and Opisthosoma

**Definition 19.1.1 (Chelicerata)** **Chelicerata** is the subphylum of arthropods whose body consists of two tagmata, a prosoma of the ocular region and six appendage-bearing segments, usually covered by a dorsal shield (carapace), and an opisthosoma of up to 12 segments and a telson; the prosomal appendages are, in order, the chelicerae, the pedipalps and four pairs of walking legs, and there are no antennae and no mandibles.

The prosoma and opisthosoma are often called cephalothorax and abdomen, as in Chinese textbooks, but they are not homologous with the tagmata of the same name in crustaceans and insects: the chelicerate prosoma combines the segments of a mandibulate head with the first legs, and there is no separate "head" in chelicerates. In horseshoe crabs, scorpions and the extinct eurypterids the opisthosoma is further divided into a broader mesosoma and a narrow, limbless metasoma; in spiders the two tagmata are joined by a narrow pedicel; in mites they are fused.

**Definition 19.1.2 (Chelicerae and Pedipalps)** The **chelicerae** are the first pair of prosomal appendages, on the deutocerebral segment, small and usually two- or three-segmented, used for seizing and tearing food; the **pedipalps** are the second pair, on the tritocerebral segment, and function in different groups as legs, sensory organs, grasping claws or, in male spiders, copulatory organs.

The homology of these appendages with the antennae and second antennae of mandibulates is explained in [7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.6). Chelicerae come in two main forms. In chelate chelicerae, with three articles, the last two form a pincer, as in horseshoe crabs, scorpions, harvestmen and many mites. In spiders the chelicera has two articles, a stout basal one and a curved fang through whose tip the venom gland opens; the fang folds against the base like the blade of a jackknife. The pedipalps of scorpions and pseudoscorpions are large chelate claws; in horseshoe crabs they are walking legs; in spiders they are short and leglike in females and carry a complex copulatory bulb in males.

### 19.1.2. Feeding

Most chelicerates are predators, and most cannot swallow solid food. Arachnids digest their prey outside the body: they tear it with the chelicerae, flood it with digestive enzymes from the midgut and suck the liquefied tissue into the mouth through a filtering preoral cavity, using a muscular pharynx and a sucking stomach operated by muscles attached to the carapace. The midgut has extensive diverticula that fill much of the opisthosoma and store food, so that many spiders and scorpions can survive months without eating. Harvestmen and some mites are exceptions that take particulate food.

Horseshoe crabs chew food with the spiny bases (gnathobases) of their walking legs, which surround the mouth; this was presumably also the feeding mode of the earliest chelicerates.

### 19.1.3. Gas Exchange: Book Gills, Book Lungs and Tracheae

**Definition 19.1.3 (Book Gills and Book Lungs)** A **book gill** is a gill of aquatic chelicerates formed by a flaplike opisthosomal appendage bearing on its posterior surface a stack of thin, hemolymph-filled lamellae, like the pages of a book, over which water flows. A **book lung** is the terrestrial counterpart: a ventral pocket of the opisthosoma (an atrium) opening by a slit, from whose wall a stack of air-filled lamellae projects, held apart by cuticular struts, with hemolymph flowing inside the lamellae.

Horseshoe crabs have five pairs of book gills on opisthosomal segments 3–7, moved rhythmically to ventilate them and also used for swimming. Scorpions have four pairs of book lungs on the mesosomal segments 3–6, and mygalomorph spiders two pairs; in most araneomorph spiders the anterior pair remains as book lungs and the posterior pair has become tracheae. Book lungs are ventilated weakly or not at all, and gas exchange depends largely on diffusion through the spiracle, which can be narrowed to reduce water loss.

The position and structure of book gills and book lungs suggest that book lungs evolved from book gills when the appendages were sunk into the body, and the detailed similarity of the book lungs of scorpions and tetrapulmonate arachnids (spiders, whip spiders, whip scorpions) supports a single origin within the clade Arachnopulmonata (Section 19.5). Tracheae have evolved independently several times in arachnids: as sieve tracheae (bundles of tubules from an atrium) in spiders, pseudoscorpions and some mites, and as tube tracheae in harvestmen, sun spiders, ricinuleids and many mites ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7)). They are not homologous with the tracheae of insects or myriapods.

### 19.1.4. Circulation and Excretion

The heart is a tube in the anterior opisthosoma with two to seven pairs of ostia, and the hemolymph carries hemocyanin; arteries are well developed in large forms such as scorpions and horseshoe crabs, whose book gills and book lungs need a steady flow. Excretion is by coxal glands, saccate nephridial organs opening at the bases of the first or third legs, and, in arachnids, by one or two pairs of **Malpighian tubules**. Arachnid Malpighian tubules arise from the endoderm of the midgut, whereas those of insects and myriapods arise from the ectoderm of the hindgut, one reason to regard them as convergent ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.5.2)).

Terrestrial arachnids excrete nitrogen mainly as guanine (spiders, scorpions and harvestmen) or uric acid (many mites), both nearly insoluble and nontoxic, so they can be voided as a paste with little water, as in other **uricotelic** animals ([7.14.21](/7.14.21#sec-21.4.1)). Together with a waxy epicuticle and the ability to close the spiracles, this makes scorpions and many spiders among the most desiccation-resistant animals: desert scorpions lose water through their cuticle as slowly as any terrestrial arthropod.

### 19.1.5. Nervous System and Sense Organs

The central nervous system of arachnids is highly concentrated: all the ganglia of the ventral nerve cord have moved forward and fused with the subesophageal ganglion into a single mass in the prosoma, pierced by the esophagus. Only scorpions retain separate ganglia in the opisthosoma. The eyes are simple: median eyes (one pair in most groups) and lateral eyes, which in arachnids are small groups of simple eyes derived from compound eyes; only horseshoe crabs retain compound lateral eyes ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.7)).

Arachnids rely mainly on mechanoreception and chemoreception. Trichobothria, long, extremely thin hairs set in a socket, respond to air currents and to the near-field sound of a flying insect; slit sensilla, slits in the cuticle covered by a membrane and innervated by a sensory cell, measure the strain of the cuticle, and in spiders they are grouped into lyriform organs near the leg joints, sensitive to vibrations of the substrate or the web. Contact chemoreceptors on the pedipalps and front legs give taste and smell.

### 19.1.6. Reproduction

The sexes are separate and the gonopore lies on the second opisthosomal segment. Copulation by an intromittent organ is rare (harvestmen and some mites). Most arachnids transfer sperm indirectly in a **spermatophore**, a packet of sperm deposited on the ground and taken up by the female, often after an elaborate courtship in which the male guides the female over it, as in the "dance" of scorpions. Male spiders spin a small sperm web, deposit a drop of sperm on it and draw it into the copulatory bulbs of their pedipalps, with which they inseminate the female; the bulbs and the corresponding female genital plate (epigyne) are so species-specific that they are the main characters for identifying spiders.

## 19.2. Pycnogonida: Sea Spiders

**Definition 19.2.1 (Pycnogonida)** **Pycnogonida** (**sea spiders**) is a class of marine chelicerates with a very small, narrow trunk and long legs, usually four pairs, sometimes five or six; a large anterior proboscis with a terminal mouth; chelifores (the deutocerebral appendages), palps, and in males (and often females) a pair of ovigers on which the male carries the eggs; and extensions of the gut and the gonads into the legs.

The roughly 1 400 species of sea spiders range from a few millimeters to deep-sea and polar species with a leg span of 70 cm, such as *Colossendeis*. They feed by sucking the tissues of cnidarians, sponges, bryozoans and soft-bodied invertebrates with the proboscis. The trunk is so slender that most of the organs lie in the legs: diverticula of the midgut and the gonads extend into them, and the gonopores open on the leg bases. Gas exchange is by diffusion through the cuticle, and the heart is weak; in some species the hemolymph is moved mainly by peristalsis of the gut diverticula in the legs (Woods et al. 2017).

Males take the egg masses from the female during mating, often from several females, and carry them on their ovigers until the young hatch, usually as a protonymphon larva with three pairs of appendages. Larvae of sea spiders are known from the late Cambrian, and phylogenomic analyses place pycnogonids as the sister group of all other living chelicerates, the Euchelicerata.

## 19.3. Merostomata: Horseshoe Crabs and Eurypterids

**Definition 19.3.1 (Merostomata)** **Merostomata** is a group of aquatic chelicerates with a large, horseshoe-shaped prosomal carapace and opisthosomal appendages modified as book gills, comprising the living horseshoe crabs (order Xiphosura) and, in most classifications, the extinct sea scorpions (Eurypterida).

Only four species of horseshoe crabs survive: *Limulus polyphemus* on the Atlantic coast of North America, and three Asian species, *Tachypleus tridentatus* (the Chinese horseshoe crab of the coasts of southern China and Southeast Asia), *T. gigas* and *Carcinoscorpius rotundicauda*. Their body consists of a domed prosoma bearing a pair of compound lateral eyes and median ocelli, a hexagonal opisthosoma with movable marginal spines, and a long, mobile telson used to right the animal when it is overturned and as a lever when burrowing. On the underside, small chelicerae are followed by five pairs of legs with chelate tips (the first four pairs) and gnathobases around the mouth; the last pair carries leaflike processes for pushing through sand. A pair of small chilaria behind the legs is the remnant of the appendages of the first opisthosomal segment. The opisthosoma bears a genital operculum and five pairs of book gills.

Horseshoe crabs spawn on beaches at high spring tides, where the female digs a nest and the male clinging to her fertilizes the eggs externally. The larva has been called a "trilobite larva" because of its resemblance to trilobites, although trilobites are not their ancestors. Horseshoe crabs have changed little in body form since the Paleozoic, which led to their description as "living fossils"; their genomes, however, have undergone several rounds of whole-genome duplication.

Two discoveries made with *Limulus* are part of the history of biology and medicine. H. K. Hartline studied the large ommatidia of the lateral eyes and discovered lateral inhibition between neighboring photoreceptors, the basis of contrast enhancement (Nobel Prize 1967). F. B. Bang and J. Levin found that the amebocytes of the hemolymph clot in the presence of bacterial endotoxin, through a cascade beginning with the protein factor C; the Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) test, and the Tachypleus amebocyte lysate test made from the Chinese horseshoe crab, became the standard method of detecting endotoxin in drugs and medical devices. Bleeding for lysate production, harvesting for bait and loss of spawning beaches have reduced populations; *T. tridentatus* is listed as endangered, and a recombinant factor C test now offers an alternative that does not require animals.

The Paleozoic eurypterids (Ordovician to Permian) were aquatic predators with a large pair of paddle-shaped last legs for swimming; *Jaekelopterus* of the Devonian, about 2.5 m long, was one of the largest arthropods that ever lived.

## 19.4. Arachnida

### 19.4.1. Overview

**Definition 19.4.1 (Arachnida)** **Arachnida** is the traditional class of mostly terrestrial chelicerates in which the opisthosoma lacks external limbs except as book lungs, spinnerets, pectines or genital structures, gas exchange is by book lungs or tracheae or through the cuticle, and digestion is largely preoral; it contains about 110 000 described species in about 16 living orders.

The main orders are compared below; the next sections describe scorpions, spiders and mites in more detail.

| Order | Common name | Species | Distinctive features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scorpiones | scorpions | about 2 800 | chelate pedipalps; mesosoma and tail-like metasoma ending in a sting; pectines; four pairs of book lungs; viviparous |
| Araneae | spiders | about 52 000 | pedicel; fanged chelicerae with venom glands; male palpal organs; spinnerets and silk glands |
| Opiliones | harvestmen, daddy longlegs | about 6 700 | prosoma and opisthosoma broadly joined; one pair of eyes on a turret; no silk or venom; tracheae; penis and ovipositor; defensive scent glands |
| Pseudoscorpiones | false scorpions | about 4 000 | small; chelate pedipalps with venom glands; silk glands in the chelicerae; ride on insects (phoresy) |
| Solifugae | sun spiders, camel spiders | about 1 200 | enormous chelate chelicerae; fast desert predators; tube tracheae |
| Amblypygi | whip spiders | about 260 | flattened; spiny raptorial pedipalps; first legs as long sensory "whips" |
| Uropygi (Thelyphonida) | whip scorpions, vinegaroons | about 120 | long tail flagellum; spray acetic acid |
| Schizomida, Palpigradi, Ricinulei | | each a few hundred or fewer | small tropical soil and cave animals |
| Acari (Acariformes, Parasitiformes) | mites and ticks | over 55 000 | prosoma and opisthosoma fused; gnathosoma; six-legged larva |

The fossil record of arachnids begins in the Silurian: the extinct trigonotarbids, spiderlike arachnids without silk glands, from about 420 million years ago, are among the oldest land animals, and scorpions are known from the Silurian as well, although the earliest forms may have been aquatic or amphibious. Spiders appear in the Carboniferous, preceded in the Devonian by uraraneids, which had silk-producing spigots but a tail.

### 19.4.2. Scorpions

**Definition 19.4.2 (Scorpiones)** **Scorpiones** is the order of arachnids with large chelate pedipalps, a short prosoma, an opisthosoma of a broad seven-segmented mesosoma and a narrow five-segmented metasoma ending in a bulbous telson with a venom gland and sting, a pair of comblike sensory pectines on the ventral side of the second mesosomal segment, and four pairs of book lungs.

Scorpions catch prey with the pedipalps and, when needed, subdue it with the sting. The pectines carry thousands of chemosensory and mechanosensory pegs that touch the ground and probably detect the texture and chemistry of the substrate, including the trail of a mate. Courtship is a "dance" in which the male grasps the female's pedipalps with his own and leads her back and forth until she is positioned over a spermatophore he has deposited. All scorpions are viviparous; the young are born after months of development in the female and ride on her back until their first molt.

The cuticle of scorpions fluoresces blue-green under ultraviolet light, owing to compounds in its outer hyaline layer, which makes them easy to find at night. Of some 2 800 species only about 25, mostly in the family Buthidae (*Androctonus*, *Leiurus*, *Centruroides*, *Tityus*), have venoms dangerous to humans. The East Asian scorpion *Mesobuthus martensii* (often listed as *Buthus martensii*) is widely used in traditional Chinese medicine, is farmed on a large scale, and its genome was sequenced in 2013 (Cao et al. 2013); its venom peptides, which act on sodium and potassium channels, are tools of neurophysiology.

### 19.4.3. Spiders

**Definition 19.4.3 (Araneae)** **Araneae** (spiders) is the order of arachnids in which the prosoma and opisthosoma are joined by a narrow pedicel; the chelicerae consist of a basal article and a fang, through which a venom gland opens in nearly all species; the male pedipalps carry copulatory bulbs; and the opisthosoma, unsegmented except in the Mesothelae, bears spinnerets connected to internal silk glands.

The internal anatomy of a spider is shown in the figure below. Living spiders fall into three lineages. The Mesothelae (the family Liphistiidae of East and Southeast Asia, including *Heptathela* in southern China) retain a segmented opisthosoma with tergites and spinnerets in the middle of the underside; they are sister to all other spiders, the Opisthothelae. Within these, the Mygalomorphae (tarantulas, trapdoor spiders, funnel-web spiders) have chelicerae that move parallel to the body axis (orthognath) and two pairs of book lungs, and the Araneomorphae, about 94 % of species, have chelicerae that move toward each other (labidognath), usually one pair of book lungs and a pair of tracheae.

Spiders are unusual among arthropods in lacking extensor muscles at two leg joints (femur–patella and tibia–metatarsus): these are extended by hemolymph pressure generated by muscles in the prosoma, which is why a dead spider curls its legs, and jumping spiders leap by a sudden pressure increase ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.3.3)). Jumping spiders (Salticidae) also have the best eyes of any arthropod of their size: a pair of large principal eyes with a long tube, a tiered retina and movable retinae, giving them a resolution comparable to that of some mammals and the ability to recognize prey and mates at a distance.

Nearly all spiders are venomous, but only a few species are dangerous to humans: the widow spiders (*Latrodectus*), whose α-latrotoxin causes massive transmitter release at synapses; the recluse spiders (*Loxosceles*), whose sphingomyelinase D causes necrotic lesions; the Australian funnel-web spiders (*Atrax*, *Hadronyche*); and the Brazilian wandering spiders (*Phoneutria*). The vast majority are harmless to people and important predators of insects in every terrestrial ecosystem: the spiders of the world have been estimated to kill 400–800 million tonnes of prey a year (Nyffeler and Birkhofer 2017).

### 19.4.4. Silk and Webs

**Definition 19.4.4 (Spinneret)** A **spinneret** is one of the paired, movable, fingerlike appendages at the rear of the opisthosoma of spiders, derived from the limbs of the fourth and fifth opisthosomal segments, whose surface carries many spigots through which the silk glands release their products.

Most spiders have three pairs of spinnerets. In the cribellate spiders, the anterior median pair is fused into a flat sieve plate, the cribellum, through which very fine dry fibers are combed by a row of bristles on the fourth legs to form woolly, adhesive capture threads. A single orb-weaving spider has up to seven types of silk glands, each producing a silk with different properties for a different task:

| Gland | Silk | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Major ampullate | dragline | safety line, web frame and radii |
| Minor ampullate | auxiliary | temporary spiral during web construction |
| Flagelliform | capture spiral core | highly elastic sticky spiral of orb webs |
| Aggregate | aqueous glue | sticky droplets on the capture spiral |
| Piriform | attachment cement | attachment discs joining threads to each other and to surfaces |
| Aciniform | wrapping silk | wrapping prey, sperm web, egg sac lining |
| Tubuliform (cylindrical) | egg-case silk | outer wall of the egg sac |

Silk proteins, spidroins, are very large proteins (often over 250 kDa) with a central region of short repeated motifs and conserved terminal domains. In dragline spidroins, stretches of alanine (poly-A) or alternating glycine and alanine form β-sheet nanocrystals that give strength, while glycine-rich motifs (GGX, GPGXX) form an amorphous, partly helical matrix that gives extensibility. The silk is stored in the gland as a highly concentrated liquid-crystalline solution (the dope) and is converted into a solid fiber only in the spinning duct, by the removal of water, a fall in pH from about 7.6 to below 6, exchange of ions and the shear forces of drawing; the terminal domains respond to these changes by linking the proteins into fibers. Silk spinning is thus a process at room temperature and in water, in contrast to the high temperatures and toxic solvents used to spin synthetic fibers.

The mechanical properties of silks explain their uses (see the figure below). Dragline silk has a tensile strength of about 1.1 GPa, close to that of high-tensile steel, but stretches by about 27 % before breaking, so the energy it absorbs before failure, its toughness of about 160 MJ m⁻³, is three times that of Kevlar and some 25 times that of steel. The capture spiral of an orb web is weaker but stretches about threefold, absorbing a similar energy in a different way. In an orb web, the stiff, strong radii absorb most of the kinetic energy of an insect flying into the web, dissipating it mostly as heat, because silk is viscoelastic and returns only a small part of the stored energy; the stretchy, sticky capture spiral then holds the insect instead of catapulting it back out (Sensenig et al. 2012). The dragline of Darwin's bark spider (*Caerostris darwini*), which spans rivers in Madagascar with webs up to 25 m long, is the toughest biological material measured.

Orb webs are built by two groups, the cribellate Uloboridae and Deinopidae, whose capture threads are dry and woolly, and the ecribellate Araneoidea, which coat the capture spiral with glue. The similarity of their building behavior suggests that the orb web arose once in their common ancestor, but many descendant lineages have abandoned it for sheet webs, cobwebs (Theridiidae), bolas or hunting without webs; web form is not a reliable guide to relationships. Silk is used for much more than capture: as a dragline while walking, for retreats and egg sacs, for nuptial gifts, and for dispersal by ballooning, in which young spiders release threads and are carried by air currents and atmospheric electric fields for hundreds of kilometers.

### 19.4.5. Mites and Ticks

**Definition 19.4.5 (Acari)** **Acari** (mites and ticks) is the group of arachnids in which the body is not divided into prosoma and opisthosoma but into an anterior gnathosoma (capitulum), formed by the chelicerae, the pedipalps and the surrounding body wall, and a posterior idiosoma bearing the legs; the larva has only three pairs of legs. It comprises two lineages, Acariformes and Parasitiformes, whose relationship to each other is debated.

Mites are mostly under 1 mm long and live in every habitat: soil (where oribatid mites reach densities of hundreds of thousands per square meter and help decompose litter), fresh water, the sea, plants, stored food, and the bodies of other animals. Their life cycle passes through an egg, a six-legged larva, up to three eight-legged nymphal stages and the adult. Their economic and medical importance is great:

- Plant feeders: spider mites (Tetranychidae) such as *Tetranychus* suck plant cells and are major pests of crops and greenhouses; predatory mites (Phytoseiidae) such as *Phytoseiulus persimilis* are used against them in biological control ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)).
- Parasites of animals: the itch mite *Sarcoptes scabiei* burrows in the skin and causes scabies; follicle mites (*Demodex*) live in human hair follicles; *Varroa destructor*, which feeds on the fat body of honeybee larvae and transmits viruses, is the main cause of honeybee colony losses worldwide ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)).
- Allergens: house dust mites (*Dermatophagoides*) feed on shed skin, and their feces contain proteins that are the most common cause of allergic asthma.
- Vectors: larval trombiculid mites (chiggers) of the genus *Leptotrombidium* transmit *Orientia tsutsugamushi*, the agent of scrub typhus, in East and Southeast Asia, including much of southern China.

Ticks (order Ixodida, in Parasitiformes) are large mites that feed only on blood. Hard ticks (Ixodidae) have a dorsal shield and feed for days; most need three hosts, one each for the larva, nymph and adult, and drop to the ground to molt between them. Soft ticks (Argasidae) lack the shield and feed for minutes to hours. A tick finds a host by "questing" on vegetation with its front legs held out, sensing carbon dioxide, heat and odors with Haller's organ on the first tarsi, and it anchors itself with a barbed hypostome and cement while injecting saliva with anticoagulant, anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive substances. Because they feed on several hosts over a long life, ticks are among the most important **vectors** of disease: *Ixodes* transmits Lyme disease (*Borrelia burgdorferi* in the broad sense) and, as *I. persulcatus* in the forests of northeastern China, tick-borne encephalitis; *Haemaphysalis longicornis* transmits severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome, a viral **zoonosis** first described in China in 2009–2011; other ticks transmit rickettsial spotted fevers, babesiosis and Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever. The epidemiology of vector-borne infection is treated in [7.11.35](/7.11.35).

### 19.4.6. Harvestmen and Other Orders

Harvestmen (Opiliones) look like long-legged spiders but differ in many ways: the prosoma and opisthosoma are broadly joined into an oval body, there is only one pair of eyes, usually on a turret, there are no silk or venom glands, the male has a penis and the female an ovipositor, and many species can eat solid food, including dead animals and plant matter. They defend themselves with secretions of scent glands opening on the prosoma and by shedding legs. Their legs contain long sensory and motor nerves that let a detached leg twitch for minutes, distracting a predator.

Pseudoscorpions (Pseudoscorpiones) are small arachnids under 8 mm long, common in leaf litter, under bark and in books. They have venom glands in the chelate pedipalps, spin silk from glands in the chelicerae for molting and brood chambers, and disperse by phoresy, clinging with their pedipalps to the legs of flies, beetles and harvestmen. The other small orders are listed in the table of Section 19.4.1.

## 19.5. Are the Arachnids a Clade?

The traditional view, shown in panel A of the figure below, treats the terrestrial arachnids as a clade and the aquatic horseshoe crabs as their sister group, which implies a single colonization of land by the arachnid ancestor. Arachnid characters such as external digestion, book lungs or tracheae, Malpighian tubules, guanine excretion, slit sensilla and trichobothria fit this view.

Phylogenomic analyses have challenged it. Many analyses since 2014 place Xiphosura among the arachnid orders (panel B), often close to the ricinuleids or to a group of mites and pseudoscorpions (Ballesteros and Sharma 2019). If correct, either land was colonized more than once by arachnid lineages, or horseshoe crabs returned to the sea from terrestrial ancestors, which would make their book gills secondarily aquatic book lungs. Other analyses, which remove fast-evolving genes and taxa such as mites and pseudoscorpions with long branches, recover Arachnida as a clade (Lozano-Fernandez et al. 2019). The question is not resolved; BG and this course keep the traditional classification while noting the alternative.

![Two hypotheses of chelicerate relationships. A: the traditional tree, in which the mostly terrestrial arachnids form a clade (green bar) and the marine horseshoe crabs (Xiphosura) are their sister group. B: many phylogenomic analyses place Xiphosura among the arachnid orders, which would make Arachnida paraphyletic and imply either several colonizations of land or a return to the sea. In both, sea spiders (Pycnogonida) are the sister group of the other chelicerates, and scorpions and spiders form the clade Arachnopulmonata (red branches), united by homologous book lungs; relationships among the other orders are poorly resolved (polytomies).](./plots/chelicerate-trees.py)

Two results are robust. Scorpions belong with the spiders, whip spiders and whip scorpions in Arachnopulmonata, sharing the detailed structure of the book lungs and a whole-genome duplication that gave them two copies of many Hox genes; scorpions are therefore not the most "primitive" arachnids, as their Silurian age and aquatic-looking ancestors once suggested. And the two lineages of mites, Acariformes and Parasitiformes, may not be each other's closest relatives, so that the similar body plans of mites could be the product of convergent miniaturization.

## 19.6. Arachnid Adaptations to Land

Whatever the number of colonizations, arachnids show a coherent set of adaptations to life on land, which can be compared with those of myriapods and insects ([7.17.20](/7.17.20)):

| Problem | Arachnid solution |
|---|---|
| Gas exchange in air | book lungs (invaginated book gills), independently evolved tracheae, closable spiracles |
| Water loss | waxy epicuticle, internal respiratory surfaces, guanine and uric acid excretion, water reabsorption in the hindgut |
| Excretion | Malpighian tubules (endodermal) and coxal glands |
| Feeding without water to suspend food | preoral digestion and a sucking stomach |
| Fertilization without water | spermatophores, palpal organs, internal fertilization |
| Support | stiff exoskeleton, hydraulic leg extension |
| Sensing | trichobothria, slit sensilla, contact chemoreceptors |

## 19.7. Correspondence with Chinese Textbooks

Chinese textbooks divide the subphylum Chelicerata into three classes, the Merostomata (with the horseshoe crabs, including the three Asian species found in China), the Arachnida and the Pycnogonida (also Pantopoda), and treat mites and ticks as a single order (Acarina). The modern classification differs in three ways:

| Chinese textbook | Modern treatment | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Merostomata, Arachnida, Pycnogonida as classes | Pycnogonida and Euchelicerata (Merostomata + Arachnida) | sea spiders are the sister group of all other chelicerates |
| Acarina as one order | Acari with two superorders, Acariformes and Parasitiformes, each with several orders | the two may not be sister groups |
| Scorpions treated first as "primitive" arachnids | scorpions within Arachnopulmonata, with spiders | Silurian age does not mean basal position |
| "Cephalothorax and abdomen" | prosoma and opisthosoma | not homologous with the tagmata of the same name in crustaceans and insects |

The textbook descriptions of the spider body (pedicel, chelicerae with venom, book lungs and tracheae, spinnerets and silk glands, coxal glands and Malpighian tubules, guanine excretion, concentrated nervous system) and of scorpions (pectines, metasoma and sting, viviparity) agree with the modern account.

## Questions

1. A fly of mass 20 mg hits a web at 1 m/s. Estimate how long a piece of dragline silk of diameter 4 µm could absorb its kinetic energy before breaking, and comment on the result.

   **Answer** The kinetic energy is $\frac12 m v^2 = \frac12 \times 2\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{kg} \times (1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})^2 = 1\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{J}$. The cross-section of the thread is $\pi (2\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m})^2 \approx 1.26\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, so 1 cm of thread has a volume of $1.26\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm{m^3}$ and, at a toughness of $1.6\times10^{8}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$, can absorb about $2\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{J}$ before breaking. A centimeter of dragline is therefore enough in principle; in a real web the energy is shared among several radii, so each is loaded far below its breaking point, and most of the energy is dissipated as heat rather than returned, which keeps the fly from bouncing out.

2. Explain why the prosoma of a spider is not homologous with the cephalothorax of a crayfish, although both are often called "cephalothorax".

   **Answer** The spider prosoma comprises the ocular region and six segments: the deutocerebral (chelicerae), tritocerebral (pedipalps) and four leg-bearing segments. The crayfish cephalothorax comprises the six-segmented head (first and second antennae, mandibles, two pairs of maxillae) fused with eight thoracic segments under a carapace. By the head homologies of [7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.6), the spider's four leg segments correspond to the mandibular, two maxillary and first thoracic segments of the crayfish, so the two tagmata contain different sets of segments and arose independently by different fusions; they share only a name and a general position.

3. Give the arguments for and against the view that book lungs evolved from book gills, and state what the nested position of horseshoe crabs among arachnids would imply for this view.

   **Answer** For: both are stacks of lamellae on the posterior side of opisthosomal appendages or their pockets, in the same segments, with hemolymph inside the lamellae, and developmental studies show book lungs forming from limb buds that sink into the body; the lamellae of fossil scorpions are intermediate. Against: the details of lamellar support and ventilation differ, and book lungs could in principle have formed from the body wall. If horseshoe crabs are nested among arachnids, the common ancestor of arachnids and xiphosurans may already have been terrestrial with book lungs, and the book gills of horseshoe crabs would be book lungs returned to water, reversing the direction of evolution; alternatively, book gills evolved into book lungs several times. Either way, the homology of the two organs would stand, but not the sequence gill → lung in a single step.

4. Why are ticks more efficient vectors of pathogens than most mites, and why do hard ticks with three hosts in particular transmit zoonoses to humans?

   **Answer** Ticks feed only on blood and take large meals over days, so they acquire and inoculate pathogens efficiently; they live long, and many pathogens are maintained through the molts (transstadial transmission) and sometimes to the eggs (transovarial transmission). A three-host hard tick feeds as larva, nymph and adult on different, often unrelated hosts: larvae and nymphs typically on small mammals or birds, which are reservoirs of *Borrelia*, tick-borne encephalitis virus or SFTS virus, and nymphs and adults on larger mammals, including humans, who are accidental hosts. Each stage therefore links wild reservoirs with people, which makes these ticks the typical vectors of zoonoses.

5. (Open question) Spider silk is stronger for its weight than steel and tougher than Kevlar, yet it is not mass-produced. What properties of spiders and spidroins make this difficult, and how might biotechnology overcome them?

   **Answer** Spiders are territorial and cannibalistic, so they cannot be farmed at high density like silkworms, and each produces little silk. Spidroin genes are very long and repetitive, difficult to clone and express, and the proteins tend to aggregate in expression hosts; the native fiber's properties depend not only on the sequence but on the spinning process (dope concentration, pH and ion gradients, shear and drawing) that converts it into oriented β-sheet nanocrystals. Approaches: expressing shortened, engineered spidroins with the native terminal domains in bacteria, yeast or transgenic silkworms, and spinning them in biomimetic devices that reproduce the pH gradient and shear of the spinning duct; such fibers now approach native dragline in some properties, but matching its combination of strength and extensibility remains a research goal.
