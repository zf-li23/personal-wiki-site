Insects are short-lived, small and often widely dispersed, yet they must find a mate of their own species at the right time and place, transfer sperm across a cuticle-covered body, and lay eggs that survive desiccation and attack. The solutions are extraordinarily varied: songs and pheromones that reach mates kilometres away, sperm packages and sperm stores that last for years, male genitalia that differ between closely related species more than any other structure, and a repertoire of reproductive modes, from virgin birth to one egg producing two thousand embryos, that is unmatched among animals. This diversity is also of practical importance: the ability of aphids to multiply parthenogenetically, or of a single cottony cushion scale to found a population, makes them formidable pests.

This chapter follows the reproductive cycle from mate finding to the hatching of the embryo. Section 28.1 deals with mate finding and courtship, 28.2 with sperm transfer and the evolution of genitalia, 28.3 and 28.4 with eggs, oviposition and viviparity, 28.5 with atypical modes of reproduction, 28.6 with sex determination and the reproductive manipulators of the genus *Wolbachia*, and 28.7 with embryogenesis. The anatomy of the reproductive organs is described in [7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.8), the external genitalia and the ovipositor in [7.17.24](/7.17.24#sec-24.4.3), the signals used in courtship in [7.17.27](/7.17.27). The theory of sexual selection is treated in [7.10.13](/7.10.13), the evolution of sex and parthenogenesis in [7.10.14](/7.10.14), the genetics of sex determination in [7.4.6](/7.4.6), and the molecular control of segmentation in the *Drosophila* embryo in [7.5.14](/7.5.14) and [7.5.15](/7.5.15).

## 28.1. Finding a Mate

### 28.1.1. Encounter Sites and Signals

The first problem of sexual reproduction is encounter. Insects meet in three main ways:

- at a resource that both sexes visit: a host plant, a fresh dung pat, a carcass or a flower; dung flies and many beetles mate where the female will lay her eggs;
- at a landmark that is independent of resources: hilltops (hilltopping butterflies and flies), the tops of bushes, the edges of forests, or a visual marker above which males swarm; mayflies, midges, mosquitoes and many flies form swarms consisting mostly of males, and a female that flies into the swarm is seized at once;
- by long-distance signals: sex pheromones (most moths), calling songs (crickets, bush crickets, cicadas), flashes (fireflies) or vibrations through the substrate (leafhoppers, stoneflies) ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)).

When males defend small display territories that contain no resources needed by females, and females visit the aggregation to choose a mate, the mating system is a **lek** ([7.18](/7.18)); leks are common in fruit flies of the families Drosophilidae (the Hawaiian *Drosophila*) and Tephritidae.

### 28.1.2. Courtship and Secondary Sexual Characters

Courtship is the close-range exchange of signals that precedes and often accompanies mating. It serves species recognition, but it is also an arena of **sexual selection** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#def-13.2.1)): females choose among males, and males compete for access to females. Many striking structures of insects are secondary sexual characters shaped in this way:

| Character | Examples | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Horns and enlarged mandibles | Stag beetles (Lucanidae), rhinoceros beetles (*Trypoxylus dichotomus*), dung beetles (*Onthophagus*) | Fights between males for tunnels, sap flows or females |
| Eye stalks | Stalk-eyed flies (Diopsidae) | Female choice of males with wide eye span; fights |
| Enlarged eyes | Males of many flies (holoptic eyes meeting on the vertex), drones of honey bees, the divided eyes of male Bibionidae | Detecting females against the sky |
| Plumose or pectinate antennae | Male mosquitoes, midges, silk moths | Hearing the female's wingbeat; capturing pheromone |
| Wing colour and ultraviolet patterns | Butterflies, damselflies | Display and species recognition |
| Scent organs | Hair pencils of danaine butterflies, coremata of arctiine moths | Male courtship pheromones |
| Claspers and grasping legs | Water striders, diving beetles (suction cups on male fore tarsi) | Holding the female |

Horned dung beetles show how such characters develop. In *Onthophagus taurus* males above a threshold body size grow long horns, smaller males almost none: the scaling of horn length on body size is a steep sigmoid, a developmental switch regulated by nutrition and juvenile hormone during the last larval instar. Small hornless males do not fight; they sneak into the tunnels guarded by horned males, an alternative tactic maintained by frequency-dependent selection. Horns are costly: in species with large head horns the eyes or antennae next to them are smaller, and in species with thoracic horns the wings, a developmental trade-off for limited resources within the pupa (Emlen 2001).

### 28.1.3. Nuptial Gifts and Sexual Cannibalism

In many insects the male gives the female something besides sperm. Male hangingflies (Mecoptera: Bittacidae) catch an insect and offer it to the female, who mates only while she feeds on it; copulations with small gifts are cut short before the full ejaculate has been transferred, so female choice selects for males that bring large prey (Thornhill 1976). Male empidid flies offer prey, some wrapped in silk or replaced by an empty silk balloon. Male bush crickets transfer a large spermatophore with a sperm-free, proteinaceous part, the spermatophylax, which the female eats while the sperm drain from the sperm ampulla into her genital tract; the spermatophylax can amount to a quarter of the male's body mass. Male butterflies and moths transfer sodium, amino acids or defensive alkaloids with the spermatophore, and males of many Lepidoptera "puddle" at wet ground to collect the sodium that they pass to their mates.

Sexual cannibalism by female mantises is the extreme gift. It is frequent in the laboratory and occurs in the field; females in poor condition eat males more often and produce larger oothecae when they do. Decapitated males copulate more vigorously, because the subesophageal ganglion normally inhibits the copulatory movements. Male mantises approach females cautiously, which suggests that being eaten is not usually in their interest.

## 28.2. Sperm Transfer and Storage

### 28.2.1. From Indirect to Direct Sperm Transfer

The ancestral mode of sperm transfer in hexapods is indirect. Male springtails, diplurans and silverfish deposit a stalked droplet of sperm, a spermatophore, on the ground, and the female picks it up with her genital opening; male bristletails (Archaeognatha) attach sperm droplets to a thread and guide the female to them. Copulation with an intromittent organ arose in the ancestor of the winged insects and allowed sperm to be transferred directly, without exposure to air.

**Definition 28.2.1 (Spermatophore)** A **spermatophore** is a package of sperm enclosed in a capsule secreted by the male accessory glands, transferred to the female during copulation (or deposited on the substrate in apterygotes and entognathans) and usually placed in the female's bursa copulatrix or at the opening of the spermathecal duct, from which the sperm migrate to the spermatheca.

Spermatophores are used by most orders; the males of many Heteroptera, Coleoptera, Diptera and Hymenoptera transfer free sperm in seminal fluid instead. In Lepidoptera a new spermatophore is formed for each mating and its neck remains in the female's bursa for life, so dissecting a female shows how often she has mated; such counts show that females of most moth and butterfly species mate more than once.

Dragonflies and damselflies (Odonata) have a unique system. The male transfers sperm from the genital opening on abdominal segment 9 to a secondary copulatory apparatus on the underside of segments 2 and 3 before mating. He grasps the female behind the head with the claspers at the tip of his abdomen (the tandem position), and the female bends her abdomen forward to receive sperm from his secondary genitalia, so that the pair forms a closed loop (the wheel position).

### 28.2.2. The Spermatheca

**Definition 28.2.2 (Spermatheca)** The **spermatheca** (receptaculum seminis) is an ectodermal sac of the female reproductive tract, lined with cuticle and opening into the genital chamber, in which sperm received at mating are stored alive, nourished by the secretions of a spermathecal gland, and released in small numbers to fertilize eggs as they pass the opening.

Sperm storage separates mating from fertilization in time. The queen of a honey bee mates on one or a few nuptial flights with 10–20 drones early in life and stores about five million sperm, enough to fertilize eggs for several years; she releases only about 10–20 sperm for each fertilized egg, and by opening or closing the spermathecal duct she decides whether an egg will be fertilized and thus become a female, or not, and become a male (Section 28.6). Queens of leaf-cutter ants store sperm for more than ten years, the longest sperm storage known in animals. Eggs are fertilized as they pass the opening of the spermathecal duct in the genital chamber: the micropyle, a narrow canal through the eggshell, is turned towards the duct, and one or more sperm enter.

### 28.2.3. The Diversity of Genitalia

The genitalia of male insects are often the most complex, species-specific structures of the body, and taxonomists in many groups identify species mainly by them. Two related species that cannot be told apart externally, such as *Drosophila simulans* and *D. mauritiana*, differ clearly in the shape of the male's genital processes. Why should organs with such a simple function evolve so fast?

The oldest explanation, the lock-and-key hypothesis (Dufour 1844), holds that the genitalia of the two sexes fit only within a species and so prevent hybridization. It is supported in a few cases: in Japanese ground beetles of the subgenus *Ohomopterus* (*Carabus*) the male's copulatory piece fits a pocket of the female's vagina, and matings between species damage both partners. But in general it fails: the female genitalia are often simple and do not vary in step with those of males, interspecific matings are often mechanically possible, and genitalia also differ between species that never meet.

Most entomologists now attribute genital diversity mainly to sexual selection acting after copulation has begun (Eberhard 1985):

- **cryptic female choice** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.2.3)): genitalia stimulate the female, and she uses the sperm of males that stimulate her best; in over a third of insect species studied, males perform movements during copulation (copulatory courtship) that look like stimulation;
- **sperm competition** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#def-13.2.2)): the male's genitalia remove or displace sperm of earlier males (Section 28.2.4);
- sexual conflict ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.2.5)): males evolve devices that override female control, and females evolve counter-adaptations. The penis of male seed beetles (*Callosobruchus*) carries spines that wound the female's genital tract; male bed bugs pierce the female's body wall with the aedeagus and inject sperm into her hemocoel (traumatic insemination), and females have evolved a special organ, the spermalege, at the usual site of piercing, which reduces the damage.

Comparative evidence supports the sexual-selection view: in butterflies of the genus *Heliconius*, genitalia are more diverse in species in which females mate repeatedly, and termites, in which a king and queen form a lifelong monogamous pair, have almost no external genitalia.

### 28.2.4. Sperm Competition

When a female mates with several males and stores their sperm, the males' ejaculates compete for her eggs. The share of offspring sired by the last male to mate, $P_2$, is measured with the sterile-male technique: a female is mated to a normal male and to a male whose sperm have been made unable to produce viable embryos by irradiation, in both orders.

**Example 28.2.1** Suppose that females mated only to normal males produce eggs of which 90% hatch, and that females mated first to a normal male and then to an irradiated male produce eggs of which 18% hatch. The eggs fertilized by the irradiated male fail to hatch, so the fraction fertilized by the first male is $0.18/0.90 = 0.20$, and the second (irradiated) male has fertilized $P_2 = 1 - 0.18/0.90 = 0.80$ of the eggs. In general

$$
P_2 = 1 - \frac{x}{p},
\tag{28.1}
$$
where $x$ is the hatching rate when the second male is sterile and $p$ the hatching rate of normal matings. Values of $P_2$ above 0.5, last-male precedence, are typical of many insects; in some species sperm are mixed ($P_2 \approx 0.5$) and in a few the first male has precedence.

The mechanisms of last-male precedence are known in a few cases. Male damselflies of the genus *Calopteryx* spend most of a copulation removing the sperm of previous males from the female's sperm stores with a penis armed with backward-pointing spines, and introduce their own sperm only at the end (Waage 1979). In other dragonflies the male's penis pushes earlier sperm to the back of the storage organ. Males also prevent later matings: by mating plugs that seal the female's genital opening, by guarding her until she lays her eggs (the tandem flights of dragonflies), by prolonged copulation (some stick insects copulate for weeks), and by substances in the seminal fluid that make the female unreceptive and stimulate egg laying, such as the sex peptide of *Drosophila* ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#sec-26.8.2)).

## 28.3. Eggs and Oviposition

### 28.3.1. The Egg

The insect egg is large and yolk-rich relative to the female, from about 0.02 mm in some parasitoid wasps to more than 10 mm in some bees. It is completed in the ovariole ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#def-26.8.1)), where the follicle cells secrete two envelopes around the oocyte: the thin vitelline membrane and the chorion, the eggshell proper, often of several layers and sculptured with the outlines of the follicle cells that made it. The chorion has to reconcile two incompatible requirements, the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide and the retention of water. Terrestrial eggs solve this with a waxy layer and a meshwork in the chorion that holds a thin film of air connected to the atmosphere by narrow pores, the aeropyles; when the egg is flooded, the air film acts as a plastron ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#def-26.4.4)). Eggs that develop in water or damp soil have respiratory horns or a crown of processes (water scorpions, many flies). Sperm enter through the micropyles; eggs of lice and stick insects have a lid, the operculum, through which the larva hatches.

### 28.3.2. Oviposition

The site of oviposition determines the survival of the offspring, and females choose it carefully, using taste receptors on the tarsi, antennae and ovipositor. Eggs are laid in four main ways:

- inserted into soil, plant tissue or a host with an appendicular ovipositor formed by appendages of segments 8 and 9 ([7.17.24](/7.17.24#def-24.4.2)): grasshoppers (into soil), bush crickets and cicadas (into stems), sawflies (into leaves), parasitoid wasps (into hosts); the long ovipositor of the ichneumonid *Megarhyssa* drills several centimetres into wood to reach a wood-wasp larva;
- laid with a substitutional ovipositor, the telescoping posterior segments of the abdomen, in most beetles, flies and moths;
- glued to a surface with the secretions of the female accessory (colleterial) glands: the eggs of lice on hairs (nits), of ladybird beetles on leaves;
- enclosed in a protective case: the tanned purse-like ootheca of cockroaches, the foamy ootheca of mantises, which hardens in air, and the egg pod of grasshoppers, a mass of eggs embedded in froth in the soil.

In stinging Hymenoptera the ovipositor has become a sting and has lost its egg-laying function: eggs leave through the genital opening at its base. The giant water bugs (Belostomatidae) show the rare case of paternal care: females lay eggs on the back of the male, who keeps them aerated by stroking water over them; he mates repeatedly with the female before she lays, which ensures that the eggs he carries are his own.

## 28.4. Ovoviviparity and Viviparity

Most insects are oviparous: embryonic development begins after the egg is laid. In others the mother retains the developing eggs or young; four types are usually distinguished:

| Type | Development | Nutrition of the embryo | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ovoviviparity | Eggs with eggshell incubated in the genital tract; they hatch at or just before laying | Yolk only | Some cockroaches (*Blaberus*), flesh flies (Sarcophagidae), many tachinid flies, some thrips and beetles |
| Pseudoplacental viviparity | Yolk-poor eggs develop in the genital tract; the mother forms a placenta-like tissue | Maternal secretions | Parthenogenetic aphids, the earwigs *Hemimerus* and *Arixenia*, the bat bugs Polyctenidae, some Psocodea |
| Hemocoelous viviparity | Embryos develop free in the mother's hemocoel and leave through a brood canal or by eating the mother | Hemolymph | Female Strepsiptera; paedogenetic gall midges |
| Adenotrophic viviparity | A single larva hatches in the "uterus" and feeds on milk glands (modified accessory glands) until fully grown; it pupariates immediately after birth | Milk gland secretion | Tsetse flies (*Glossina*), louse flies (Hippoboscidae), bat flies (Nycteribiidae, Streblidae) |

The tsetse fly is the extreme. A female produces one larva about every nine days, each nearly as heavy as herself, and only eight to ten offspring in her life, an exceptionally low reproductive rate for an insect. This is why tsetse populations can be suppressed by releasing sterile males, which was used to eliminate *Glossina austeni* from Zanzibar in the 1990s ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)).

## 28.5. Parthenogenesis and Other Unusual Modes

### 28.5.1. Parthenogenesis

**Parthenogenesis** ([7.10.14](/7.10.14#def-14.1.1)) occurs in almost every insect order. It is classified by the sex of the offspring produced from unfertilized eggs:

| Type | Unfertilized eggs give | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Arrhenotoky | Males only (fertilized eggs give females) | All Hymenoptera, thrips, whiteflies, some scale insects and beetles: the basis of haplodiploidy (Section 28.6) |
| Thelytoky | Females only | Many stick insects, bark lice, some aphids, weevils, scale insects, some parasitoid wasps (often induced by *Wolbachia*) |
| Deuterotoky (amphitoky) | Both sexes | Some thrips and sawflies; aphids in autumn |

Thelytoky is mechanistically of two kinds. In apomictic thelytoky the egg forms without meiosis and the offspring are clones of the mother. In automictic thelytoky meiosis occurs and diploidy is restored afterwards, by fusion of meiotic products or by duplication of the chromosomes of the egg nucleus (gamete duplication); this leads to homozygosity and loss of genetic variation. Parthenogenesis can be obligate or facultative (a female mates if a male is available). Purely parthenogenetic populations are often found at the edges of the range of related sexual forms, at high latitudes, high altitudes or on islands (geographic parthenogenesis), perhaps because a single female can found a population there.

### 28.5.2. Cyclical Parthenogenesis in Aphids

Aphids alternate many parthenogenetic generations with one sexual generation each year, a form of **cyclical parthenogenesis** ([7.17.12](/7.17.12#sec-12.3.2)) combined with complex polymorphism and, in many species, an alternation of host plants. The holocycle of a host-alternating aphid such as the bird cherry–oat aphid, *Rhopalosiphum padi*, is as follows:

1. A fertilized egg overwinters on the primary (winter) host, usually a woody plant, here bird cherry. In spring it hatches into a wingless female, the fundatrix, which reproduces parthenogenetically.
2. After one or two parthenogenetic generations on the primary host, winged females (alate migrants) are produced, which fly to the secondary (summer) hosts, grasses and cereals.
3. On the summer hosts many generations of wingless and winged viviparous parthenogenetic females follow; winged forms are produced when the colony is crowded or the plant deteriorates and spread the infestation.
4. In autumn, shortening days and falling temperatures induce winged females (gynoparae) that fly back to the primary host and produce sexual females (oviparae), and winged males that are produced on the secondary host also fly back.
5. Oviparae mate and lay the overwintering eggs, which are cold-hardy, unlike the parthenogenetic stages.

![The annual cycle (holocycle) of a host-alternating aphid (schematic). On the primary woody host, a fertilized overwintering egg hatches in spring into the fundatrix, which produces parthenogenetic daughters; winged spring migrants fly to the secondary herbaceous host, where many generations of viviparous parthenogenetic females (wingless and, when crowded, winged) follow in summer. In autumn short days induce winged gynoparae and males that return to the primary host; the gynoparae produce sexual females (oviparae), which mate with the males and lay the eggs. Only the egg is fertilized and only the autumn generation is sexual; all other generations are female and parthenogenetic. In mild climates many populations skip the sexual phase and overwinter as parthenogenetic females (anholocycly).](./plots/aphid-cycle.py)

In mild climates many clones lose the sexual phase and overwinter as active parthenogenetic females (anholocycly); in China, as elsewhere, aphid populations in the south are largely anholocyclic and the sexual phase is restricted to the north. Parthenogenetic aphids develop their embryos in a telescoped way: the embryos in a newborn female already contain the embryos of the next generation, so that a mother carries her granddaughters. Together with viviparity, which eliminates the egg stage, this gives aphids some of the highest rates of increase of any animal.

**Example 28.5.1** An aphid becomes adult 7 days after birth and then produces 4 daughters per day for 10 days (illustrative values at about 20 °C). The intrinsic rate of increase $r$ satisfies the Euler–Lotka equation

$$
\sum_{x} l_x m_x \mathrm{e}^{-r x} = 1,
\tag{28.2}
$$
with survival $l_x = 1$ and fecundity $m_x = 4$ at ages $x = 7, 8, \dots, 16$ days. Numerically $r \approx 0.36\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$, a doubling time of $\ln 2 / r \approx 1.9$ days: without mortality a single aphid would give rise to about $\mathrm{e}^{30r} \approx 5 \times 10^4$ descendants in a month. Because daughters are born only seven days after their mother, $r$ depends far more on the age at first reproduction than on lifetime fecundity, and telescoped development, by shortening the generation time, is the main reason for the explosive growth.

### 28.5.3. Paedogenesis and Neoteny

**Definition 28.5.1 (Paedogenesis)** **Paedogenesis** is reproduction by an immature stage, a larva or a pupa, whose ovaries develop precociously and produce eggs or offspring, usually parthenogenetically; adults may be produced only under unfavourable conditions.

Larval paedogenesis has evolved several times in gall midges (Cecidomyiidae: *Miastor*, *Heteropeza*), whose larvae live in decaying wood or fungi. The eggs develop in the hemocoel of the mother larva and the daughter larvae eat the mother from within and emerge; in this way a population builds up rapidly while the food lasts, and winged adults are produced when the substrate is exhausted or crowded. The beetle *Micromalthus debilis*, which lives in decaying wood, has the most complex life cycle known in beetles, with paedogenetic larvae that produce either female larvae or a single male egg; the male larva eats its mother. Neoteny is different: a juvenile-looking form mates and reproduces conventionally. The wingless, larva-like adult females of scale insects and of Strepsiptera, and the larviform females of some glow-worm beetles, are neotenic.

### 28.5.4. Polyembryony

**Definition 28.5.2 (Polyembryony)** **Polyembryony** is the development of two or more embryos from a single egg by division of the early embryo, so that the offspring are genetically identical; in insects it occurs in some endoparasitoid wasps and in Strepsiptera.

Polyembryony evolved independently in four families of parasitoid wasps (Encyrtidae, Platygastridae, Braconidae, Dryinidae). The best studied species, *Copidosoma floridanum* (Encyrtidae), lays one or two eggs in the egg of a moth (a looper caterpillar). The yolk-poor egg divides into a mass of up to about 2,000 embryos, which develop slowly as the host caterpillar grows, enclosed in an extraembryonic membrane (the trophamnion) that takes nutrients from the host's hemolymph. A minority of the embryos develop early into "soldier" larvae with large mandibles that do not pupate and die with the host; they kill larvae of competing parasitoids and, when a host contains a male and a female egg, preferentially kill their brothers, which shifts the sex ratio of the brood towards females (Giron and colleagues 2004). Polyembryony thus produced a sterile caste in a group without social behaviour, a striking case of kin-selected altruism among clones.

### 28.5.5. Hermaphroditism

Hermaphroditism is almost unknown in insects. The exception is the cottony cushion scale *Icerya purchasi* (Monophlebidae) and its relatives, in which the "females" have an ovotestis and fertilize themselves; males occur rarely. The testis tissue of these hermaphrodites is haploid and derives from a lineage of paternal cells that is passed on from generation to generation within the body, an arrangement that has been interpreted as an infectious male cell line (Gardner and Ross 2011). Self-fertilization lets a single individual found a population, which helped *Icerya purchasi* to spread from Australia to citrus-growing regions worldwide; its control in California by the introduced vedalia ladybird in 1888–1889 was the first great success of biological control ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)).

## 28.6. Sex Determination

Insects show nearly every known mechanism of **sex determination** ([7.4.6](/7.4.6#def-6.1.2)):

| System | Male | Female | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Male heterogamety XX/XY | XY | XX | Most Diptera; some beetles and bugs |
| Male heterogamety XX/X0 | X0 | XX | Orthoptera, Odonata, many cockroaches, Hemiptera (Heteroptera), some beetles |
| Female heterogamety ZZ/ZW | ZZ | ZW (or Z0) | Lepidoptera and Trichoptera |
| Haplodiploidy (arrhenotoky) | Haploid, from an unfertilized egg | Diploid | Hymenoptera, Thysanoptera, whiteflies, some scale insects, some beetles (bark beetles of the Xyleborini, *Micromalthus*) |
| Paternal genome elimination | Diploid at fertilization, the paternal set inactivated or eliminated | Diploid | Many scale insects (lecanoid and Comstockiella systems), some springtails and lice; fungus gnats (Sciaridae) |
| Single-locus or environmental | Varies | Varies | A dominant male-determining factor (M) on an autosome in house flies and mosquitoes |

Haplodiploidy ([7.4.6](/7.4.6#sec-6.1.2)) has evolved about a dozen times in insects. In the honey bee, sex is determined by the complementary sex determiner locus: individuals heterozygous at *csd* are female, hemizygous (haploid) individuals male, and diploid homozygotes become sterile diploid males that workers eat as larvae; the expected loss of diploid brood is analysed in [7.4.6](/7.4.6#sec-6.1.2). Haplodiploidy has profound consequences: females control the sex ratio of their offspring by fertilizing or not fertilizing each egg, which allows the female-biased sex ratios of parasitoid wasps under local mate competition ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.3.2)), and the relatedness asymmetries of haplodiploid families were Hamilton's original argument for the repeated evolution of **eusociality** in Hymenoptera ([7.10.16](/7.10.16#sec-16.3.3)).

The molecular cascade varies at the top and is conserved at the bottom. In all insects studied, sex is finally executed by sex-specific splicing of the transcription factor *doublesex* (*dsx*), which is regulated in most orders by *transformer* (*tra*). The primary signal that sets *tra* differs: the number of X chromosomes acting through *Sex-lethal* in *Drosophila* ([7.4.6](/7.4.6#sec-6.3)), the male-determining genes *Mdmd* in the house fly and *Nix* in the mosquito *Aedes aegypti*, heterozygosity at *csd* in the honey bee, and in the silk moth a small piRNA from the W chromosome (*Fem*), which silences the masculinizing gene *Masc* (Kiuchi and colleagues 2014). Sex determination is thus a striking example of developmental system drift: a conserved output reached from inputs that change rapidly in evolution.

### 28.6.1. *Wolbachia* and Other Reproductive Manipulators

*Wolbachia* is an intracellular α-proteobacterium living in the cytoplasm of the cells of a large fraction of insect species (estimates range from 40% to 65%) and of other arthropods and filarial nematodes. It is transmitted from mother to offspring in the egg cytoplasm, but not through sperm, so males are a dead end for it. Its spread is therefore favoured by any effect that increases the production or survival of infected females, and *Wolbachia* manipulates its hosts' reproduction in four ways ([7.10.16](/7.10.16#sec-16.4.2)):

- **cytoplasmic incompatibility** ([7.4.11](/7.4.11#def-11.6.3)): embryos die when an infected male fertilizes an uninfected female (or one infected with an incompatible strain);
- induction of thelytokous parthenogenesis in haplodiploid hosts (*Trichogramma* and other parasitoid wasps), by gamete duplication that makes unfertilized eggs diploid and female; antibiotic treatment restores males;
- feminization of genetic males (woodlice; some butterflies, such as *Eurema mandarina* in Japan);
- male killing in the embryo (also caused by *Spiroplasma* and other bacteria), which benefits the infected sisters through reduced competition or by eating their dead brothers (ladybirds).

Cytoplasmic incompatibility can make *Wolbachia* spread through a population even though infected females may be slightly less fecund. The dynamics follow from bookkeeping of the offspring of the two kinds of females (Turelli and Hoffmann 1995).

**Example 28.6.1** Let $p$ be the frequency of infected females (equal to that of infected males under random mating), $F$ the fecundity of infected females relative to uninfected ones, $\mu$ the fraction of the offspring of infected females that fail to receive the bacterium, and $s_{\mathrm h}$ the fraction of embryos killed in incompatible crosses (uninfected egg, infected sperm). Infected females produce $F$ offspring, of which $(1-\mu)F$ are infected and always viable, and $\mu F$ are uninfected and survive with probability $1 - s_{\mathrm h} p$; uninfected females produce offspring that survive with probability $1 - s_{\mathrm h} p$. Hence

$$
p' = \frac{pF(1-\mu)}{pF\left[1 - \mu s_{\mathrm h} p\right] + (1-p)(1 - s_{\mathrm h} p)}.
\tag{28.3}
$$
With perfect transmission ($\mu = 0$) the equation has an unstable internal equilibrium at

$$
\hat p = \frac{1-F}{s_{\mathrm h}},
\tag{28.4}
$$
above which the infection spreads to fixation and below which it is lost. For $F = 0.9$ and $s_{\mathrm h} = 0.9$, $\hat p \approx 0.11$; with imperfect transmission, $\mu = 0.03$, the unstable threshold rises to about 0.15 and the infection settles at a stable frequency of about 0.996 instead of fixation. The threshold explains why *Wolbachia* spreads in waves once it is common locally: an introduced strain must be released in numbers large enough to pass $\hat p$.

![Spread of a cytoplasmic-incompatibility Wolbachia (Eq. (28.3), illustrative parameters F = 0.9, s_h = 0.9). A: the change in frequency per generation, p′ − p, against the frequency p for perfect (μ = 0) and imperfect (μ = 0.03) maternal transmission; equilibria lie where the curve crosses zero: p = 0 is stable, the unstable threshold is p̂ ≈ 0.11 (μ = 0) or 0.15 (μ = 0.03), and the upper equilibrium is fixation (μ = 0) or about 0.996 (μ = 0.03). B: frequency over generations from different initial frequencies (μ = 0.03): releases well above the threshold sweep through the population within about ten generations, releases just above it take longer, and releases below it disappear.](./plots/wolbachia-ci.py)

This threshold dynamics is the basis of a method of controlling dengue. The strain *w*Mel, transferred from *Drosophila melanogaster* into the mosquito *Aedes aegypti*, causes complete cytoplasmic incompatibility and reduces the mosquito's ability to transmit dengue, Zika and chikungunya viruses. Released in large enough numbers in Cairns, Australia, it spread to near fixation and has persisted for more than a decade (Hoffmann and colleagues 2011), and in a randomized trial in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, *w*Mel releases reduced the incidence of symptomatic dengue by 77% (Utarini and colleagues 2021). A different strategy, used in Guangzhou, releases *Wolbachia*-infected males sterilized by irradiation to suppress mosquito populations: their matings with wild females are incompatible.

## 28.7. Embryogenesis

### 28.7.1. Cleavage and the Blastoderm

Insect eggs are centrolecithal ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)): the yolk fills the egg, with a thin layer of yolk-free cytoplasm at the surface (the periplasm). Development is activated by fertilization or, in parthenogenetic species, by the passage of the egg through the oviduct. The zygote nucleus divides repeatedly without cytokinesis, a form of superficial cleavage ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)). The nuclei, each in an island of cytoplasm (an energid), migrate to the periphery, where they form a syncytial blastoderm; cell membranes then grow in between them and produce a cellular blastoderm, a single layer of cells around the yolk. Some nuclei remain in the yolk as vitellophages that digest it. In many insects, among them *Drosophila*, the first cells to form, at the posterior pole, are the pole cells, the precursors of the germ line, which inherit the germ plasm deposited in the egg during oogenesis ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)). In *Drosophila* the whole sequence to cellularization takes 13 rapid nuclear cycles and about 2.5 hours.

### 28.7.2. The Germ Band

The cells of the blastoderm do not all contribute to the embryo. On the ventral side a thickened region, the germ anlage, forms the embryo proper; the remaining thin cells become extraembryonic.

**Definition 28.7.1 (Germ Band)** The **germ band** is the elongated, ventral plate of the insect embryo that develops from the germ anlage of the blastoderm after gastrulation and gives rise to the body of the embryo; it becomes segmented from front to back, forms limb buds on its segments and later grows around the yolk at the sides and back to close the body.

Insects differ in how much of the body is specified in the germ anlage. In long-germ insects (flies, most Hymenoptera, some beetles) the anlage occupies most of the egg and contains the primordia of all segments, which are specified simultaneously; in short-germ insects (grasshoppers, many hemimetabolous orders) the anlage is small and forms only the head and the first segments, the rest being added one after the other from a posterior growth zone; intermediate-germ insects (the flour beetle *Tribolium*, many beetles and bugs) are in between. Short-germ development is ancestral, and the long-germ mode evolved several times; the developmental genetics of this transition is discussed in [7.5.39](/7.5.39#sec-39.5.2).

![Long-, intermediate- and short-germ development (schematic, anterior to the left, ventral side down; not to scale). Top row: the germ anlage on the ventral side of the blastoderm, coloured by the body regions whose primordia it already contains. A: in a long-germ insect such as *Drosophila* the anlage spans most of the egg and contains the primordia of all segments of the head, thorax and abdomen. B: in an intermediate-germ insect such as the flour beetle *Tribolium* it contains the head and thorax and a posterior growth zone. C: in a short-germ insect such as a grasshopper it contains little more than the head lobes and the growth zone. Bottom row: the fully segmented germ band; in B and C the segments behind the anlage have been added one after another from the growth zone (arrows), whereas in A all segments were specified simultaneously.](./plots/germ-bands.py)

Gastrulation begins with the invagination of a ventral strip of the germ band, which forms the inner layer, mainly mesoderm, while the outer layer becomes the ectoderm; the endoderm of the midgut arises from anterior and posterior invaginations at the ends of the germ band. The ectoderm forms the epidermis and, by delamination of neuroblasts, the nervous system; invaginations form the stomodeum (foregut), the proctodeum (hindgut and Malpighian tubules), the tracheal system and the salivary glands. At the stage of maximum segmentation, all insect embryos look remarkably similar, with a head, three thoracic and up to eleven abdominal segments each bearing paired limb buds. This phylotypic stage of insects ([7.5.39](/7.5.39#sec-39.1.2)) is followed by the loss or modification of abdominal limb buds, except for the pleuropodia of the first abdominal segment, glandular organs of many embryos that secrete enzymes for hatching, and the prolegs of larvae ([7.17.24](/7.17.24#sec-24.4.2)).

### 28.7.3. Extraembryonic Membranes and Blastokinesis

Insect embryos are enclosed in two extraembryonic membranes. As the germ band forms, folds of the blastoderm at its edges grow over its ventral surface and fuse; the inner layer of the folds becomes the amnion, which encloses a fluid-filled amniotic cavity over the ventral side of the embryo, and the outer layer becomes the serosa, which surrounds the whole embryo and yolk beneath the chorion. Insect amnion and serosa are analogous but not homologous to the membranes of the same names in amniote vertebrates ([7.5.9](/7.5.9)): they arose independently, and like the vertebrate amnion they protect the embryo in a fluid compartment.

The serosa protects the egg against desiccation and infection. In many insects it secretes a cuticle of its own, the serosal cuticle, beneath the chorion, and in *Tribolium* eggs in which the serosa is removed by knocking down the gene *zerknüllt* survive in humid but not in dry air (Jacobs and colleagues 2013); serosal cells also produce antimicrobial peptides. Higher flies (Schizophora), whose eggs develop in moist food within a day, have reduced the two membranes to a single amnioserosa on the dorsal side, a derived condition.

In many hemimetabolous insects the embryo moves within the egg, a process called blastokinesis. During anatrepsis the germ band sinks into the yolk tail first, so that it lies reversed, with its ventral side facing the dorsal side of the egg; later, during katatrepsis, the amnion and serosa rupture and contract, and the embryo is pulled back to the surface, turning so that it lies in its final orientation. The serosa then contracts to a small dorsal organ and is absorbed, and the sides of the germ band grow over the yolk to close the back of the embryo (dorsal closure). In long-germ insects, which have no large movements, the germ band instead extends around the posterior end of the egg (germ-band extension) and retracts again.

### 28.7.4. Hatching

Before hatching the embryo of a hemimetabolous insect often swallows the amniotic fluid and air that diffuses into the egg and so increases its volume; it breaks the eggshell with an egg burster, a cuticular tooth or ridge on the head of the embryonic cuticle, or with pressure, often along a line of weakness or by pushing off the operculum. In many Orthoptera and other hemimetabolous insects the hatchling is a pronymph (or vermiform larva) still enclosed in the embryonic cuticle, which crawls up through the soil from the egg pod and molts to the first nymphal instar at the surface. Embryonic development lasts from less than a day (*Drosophila* at 25 °C, 22 h) to many months in species whose eggs overwinter in diapause ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)), and its rate depends on temperature in the same way as the rate of postembryonic development.

### 28.7.5. Hormones and Reproduction

The reproductive cycle is under hormonal control, varying among orders ([7.14.32](/7.14.32#sec-32.9.3)). The yolk protein precursor vitellogenin is synthesized by the **fat body** ([7.17.26](/7.17.26#def-26.3.1)) and taken up by the growing oocyte; its synthesis is stimulated by **juvenile hormone** in most insects (locusts, cockroaches, many beetles and butterflies) and by ecdysteroids produced by the ovary in mosquitoes and flies. In blood-feeding mosquitoes (anautogenous species) the blood meal triggers the release of a brain neuropeptide, ovary ecdysteroidogenic hormone, which starts the gonotrophic cycle: one batch of eggs per blood meal, a fact used to estimate the age of female mosquitoes by counting the relics of laid eggs in their ovarioles. Juvenile hormone, the hormone that keeps larvae juvenile ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)), is thus reused in the adult as a gonadotropin.

## Questions

1. Why is the spermatophore of terrestrial apterygote hexapods deposited on the ground considered the ancestral mode of sperm transfer, and what problems does it pose on land?

   **Answer** It occurs in the entognathan orders and in both apterygote insect orders, the earliest branches of the hexapod tree, and resembles the indirect sperm transfer of many other terrestrial arthropods (myriapods, many arachnids); copulation with an intromittent organ is found only in Pterygota, which suggests that it evolved once in their ancestor. On land a free spermatophore is exposed to desiccation and to being lost or eaten, and transfer depends on the female finding it; such species live in humid microhabitats, and the males often guide the female to the spermatophore. Copulation removes both problems.

2. (Calculation) In a sperm competition experiment, normal matings give 85% hatching. Females mated first to an irradiated male and then to a normal male give 60% hatching; females mated first to a normal male and then to an irradiated male give 17% hatching. Estimate $P_2$ from each design and comment.

   **Answer** With the second male normal, the fraction of eggs fertilized by him is the fraction that hatches relative to normal: $P_2 = 0.60/0.85 \approx 0.71$. With the second male irradiated, Eq. (28.1) gives $P_2 = 1 - 0.17/0.85 = 0.80$. The two estimates should agree if irradiated and normal sperm compete equally; the difference suggests that irradiated sperm are somewhat less competitive, or that the order of mating interacts with sperm quality. Both designs indicate last-male precedence.

3. Why do some entomologists argue that genital diversity results mostly from sexual selection rather than from selection against hybridization?

   **Answer** Selection against hybridization (lock and key) predicts that differences should be largest between species that meet (reinforcement) and that female genitalia should diverge with male genitalia; neither is generally true. Genitalia differ even between allopatric species, female structures are often simple, and males stimulate females during copulation. Diversity is greatest where females mate with several males, and genitalia are reduced in monogamous termites, as expected if competition among males for fertilization within the female (cryptic female choice, sperm competition, sexual conflict) drives their evolution.

4. (Calculation) Using Eq. (28.2), how would the intrinsic rate of increase of the aphid in Example 28.5.1 change if each mother produced 3 daughters per day instead of 4, and if instead the age at first reproduction were 6 days? What does the comparison show?

   **Answer** Numerical solution of $\sum_{x=7}^{16} 3\mathrm{e}^{-rx} = 1$ gives $r \approx 0.33\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$, a reduction of about 9% for a 25% reduction in fecundity. With 4 daughters per day from day 6 to day 15, $r \approx 0.41\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$, an increase of about 13% for a one-day shortening of development. Because early offspring contribute most to growth (they are discounted least by $\mathrm{e}^{-rx}$), a one-day change in generation time matters more than a 25% change in fecundity; this is why rapid development, viviparity and telescoped generations are so effective.

5. In the model of Example 28.6.1 with $\mu = 0$, show that $\hat p = (1-F)/s_{\mathrm h}$, and explain why the infection cannot invade from a low frequency.

   **Answer** Setting $p' = p$ in Eq. (28.3) with $\mu = 0$ and $p \ne 0$ gives $F = pF + (1-p)(1 - s_{\mathrm h}p)$, so $F(1-p) = (1-p)(1 - s_{\mathrm h}p)$ and, for $p \ne 1$, $F = 1 - s_{\mathrm h}p$, i.e. $p = (1-F)/s_{\mathrm h}$. When infected individuals are rare, infected males rarely meet uninfected females, so the cost they impose on uninfected females (and the relative advantage of being infected) is proportional to $p$ and small, while the fecundity cost $1 - F$ of infected females is independent of $p$. Infected females are therefore at a net disadvantage below $\hat p$ and at an advantage above it: the system is bistable, and the infection must be introduced above the threshold or arrive by chance.

6. What is the function of the serosa, and why have higher flies been able to reduce it?

   **Answer** The serosa encloses the embryo and yolk, secretes a protective serosal cuticle and produces immune factors; experiments in *Tribolium* show that eggs without a serosa are vulnerable to desiccation. Higher flies (Schizophora) lay eggs in moist, food-rich substrates where desiccation is not a risk, and their embryos develop within a day, so the protective function is less important; their reduced amnioserosa still participates in germ-band retraction and dorsal closure.

7. Polyembryonic wasps such as *Copidosoma* produce soldier larvae that never reproduce. Why is the evolution of such a sterile caste easier to explain here than in social insects?

   **Answer** The embryos derived from one egg are a clone, so their relatedness is 1: a soldier that dies while increasing the survival of its clone-mates loses nothing in inclusive fitness, and Hamilton's rule is satisfied for any positive benefit. In social insects, workers are related to the brood they raise by less than 1 (for example, 3/4 to full sisters in haplodiploid colonies with a singly mated queen, less with multiple mating), so the benefits must be larger. When male and female eggs share a host, soldiers are related to their sisters (clone-mates) more than to their brothers from another egg, which explains why they preferentially kill brothers.
