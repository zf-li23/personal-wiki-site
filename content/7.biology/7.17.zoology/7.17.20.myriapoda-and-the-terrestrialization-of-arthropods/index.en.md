The Myriapoda, centipedes, millipedes and two classes of small soil animals, are the sister group of the Pancrustacea within the **Mandibulata** ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.9)). They are entirely terrestrial, and the oldest known body fossils of land animals are millipedes. This makes them the natural place to ask how arthropods came onto land, a step that was taken not once but several times: by onychophorans, by arachnids, by myriapods, by hexapods and, more recently, by several groups of crustaceans.

The first half of the chapter describes the myriapod body plan and its four classes, comparing them with the textbook descriptions. The second half follows the problem of life on land across all panarthropods: what the problems are, how each lineage solved them, why the similar solutions are regarded as convergent, when land was colonized, and how body size and the permeability of the cuticle set the time an animal can survive in dry air. The physiology of tracheae and Malpighian tubules is in [7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.5) and [7.14.23](/7.14.23#sec-23.7.2), and that of insects in particular in [7.17.26](/7.17.26).

## 20.1. Myriapoda

### 20.1.1. The Myriapod Body Plan

**Definition 20.1.1 (Myriapoda)** **Myriapoda** is the subphylum of mandibulate arthropods whose body consists of a head and a long trunk of many similar segments, each primitively with one pair of uniramous walking legs; the head bears one pair of antennae, the mandibles and one or two pairs of maxillae; gas exchange is by tracheae, excretion by ectodermal Malpighian tubules, and development is direct.

About 16 000 species have been described. Myriapods live in leaf litter, soil, rotting wood and caves, mostly in humid habitats, from the tropics to the tundra. Their segments are much less specialized than those of insects or crustaceans, and the trunk is not divided into thorax and abdomen. The head has the mandibulate set of segments ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.6)): an ocular region with groups of simple eyes (ocelli) or no eyes, the antennal (deutocerebral) segment, an appendage-less intercalary (tritocerebral) segment, the mandibles and two maxillary segments, whose appendages are fused in different ways in the different classes. Many myriapods have a pair of sensory organs of Tömösváry at the base of the antennae, probably sensitive to humidity or vibrations.

The tracheae open through spiracles, usually one pair per segment on the sides or the underside. In most myriapods the spiracles cannot be closed, and the epicuticle contains little wax, so that water is lost rapidly; this is the main reason why most myriapods are confined to moist microhabitats and are active at night (Section 20.2.5). The heart is a long tube with ostia in each segment, and the nitrogenous waste is mainly uric acid in dry-living species and ammonia in species of very moist habitats. The sexes are separate and sperm are transferred indirectly in a **spermatophore** in most groups. Development is direct, but in many groups the juveniles hatch with fewer segments and legs than the adult and add them at successive molts (anamorphic development); in others they hatch with the full number (epimorphic development).

### 20.1.2. Chilopoda: Centipedes

**Definition 20.1.2 (Chilopoda)** **Chilopoda** (centipedes) is the class of myriapods with a flattened trunk of segments each bearing one pair of legs, an odd number of leg pairs (15 to 191), and the legs of the first trunk segment modified into a pair of large, pincerlike venom claws, the **forcipules**, with which they capture prey; the gonopores open at the posterior end of the trunk.

All centipedes are predators of other arthropods, earthworms and, in the largest tropical species of *Scolopendra*, small vertebrates. The forcipules are trunk limbs, not mouthparts, and the venom gland in each opens near its tip. Centipede venoms are mixtures of peptides and enzymes; one peptide from *Scolopendra subspinipes mutilans* blocks the KCNQ potassium channels of the heart and nerves, which explains the severe effects of some bites (Luo et al. 2018). This species is common in central and southern China, is farmed, and the dried animal is used in traditional medicine.

Five living orders are recognized:

| Order | Leg pairs | Development | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scutigeromorpha | 15 | anamorphic | very long legs, fast runners; dorsal unpaired spiracles leading to tracheal lungs; compound-like eyes (the house centipede *Scutigera* and *Thereuopoda*) |
| Lithobiomorpha | 15 | anamorphic | stout, brown, under stones and bark (*Lithobius*) |
| Craterostigmomorpha | 15 | anamorphic (hatch with 12) | two species in Tasmania and New Zealand |
| Scolopendromorpha | 21 or 23 | epimorphic | large; females guard their eggs and young (*Scolopendra*) |
| Geophilomorpha | 27–191 | epimorphic | long, thin, blind burrowers; legs always in odd numbers |

The fact that no centipede has an even number of leg pairs, although the number varies from 15 to 191, is a classic puzzle of development: it reflects the way segments are produced in pairs by a double-segment periodicity in the embryo, with the final count always odd. In the geophilomorph *Strigamia maritima*, whose genome was sequenced in 2014, the segmentation genes are first expressed with a two-segment periodicity in the posterior growth zone and each primary stripe is then split into two, so that segments are added in pairs on top of the odd number of anterior segments (Chipman et al. 2004). Leg number in geophilomorphs varies within species, increases with temperature during development in some of them, and is often higher in females than in males, but it always changes by steps of two.

The two subclasses differ in the position of the spiracles. Notostigmophora (only Scutigeromorpha) have a single unpaired spiracle in the middle of the hind margin of each of seven long tergites; it opens into a short atrium from which two fans of very short, blind-ending tracheae project into the pericardial sinus, so that oxygen is loaded into the blood next to the heart and carried by hemocyanin, as in a lung. Pleurostigmophora (the other four orders) have paired lateral spiracles above the leg bases and branched tracheae that run to the tissues as in insects. The long and short tergites of lithobiomorphs and scolopendromorphs alternate along the trunk, and the spiracles lie on the segments with long tergites; the alternation reverses at segments 7 and 8, a peculiarity regarded as a synapomorphy of the centipedes.

**Example 20.1.1 (*Scolopendra subspinipes mutilans*)** The Chinese red-headed centipede is a scolopendromorph about 11 cm long, with a dark green to blackish trunk and a reddish-brown head and first tergite; it lives under stones and in leaf litter in the hills of the middle and lower Yangtze basin. Its organization can be described system by system.

- *External form.* A flattened head capsule carries a pair of antennae of 17 articles, four ocelli on each side, the mandibles, and two pairs of maxillae whose bases are fused into a platelike structure under the mouth. The first trunk segment carries the forcipules, whose large coxosternum covers the mouthparts from below; behind it follow 21 leg-bearing segments, a legless pregenital segment, the genital segment and the telson. The last legs are long, directed backward and used as sensory and defensive organs, not for walking.
- *Digestive system.* The forcipules stab the prey and inject venom from a gland in the basal articles; the maxillae hold the prey while the mandibles cut it. A long foregut (pharynx and oesophagus) with salivary glands leads into a straight midgut, the main site of digestion and absorption, lined by a peritrophic membrane, and a short hindgut into which a pair of Malpighian tubules opens.
- *Circulation and gas exchange.* The dorsal tubular heart has one pair of ostia in each segment and pumps hemolymph forward into the aorta and the lateral arteries; tracheae open through paired lateral spiracles on alternate segments (segments 3, 5, 8, 10, 12 and so on), each spiracle sunk in an atrium lined with bristles.
- *Nervous system.* The brain (protocerebrum, deutocerebrum and tritocerebrum) is connected to a subesophageal ganglion that controls the mouthparts and to a ventral nerve cord with one ganglion pair per segment, scarcely fused.
- *Reproduction.* The sexes are separate. The male spins a small silk web from glands of the genital segment, deposits a spermatophore on it, and the female picks it up after a courtship in which the partners tap each other with the antennae. The female lays a cluster of a few dozen eggs in a cavity in soil or wood and coils around them, holding them against her ventral side and cleaning them of fungi with her mouthparts, until the young disperse after their first moults. Development is epimorphic: the young hatch with the full number of 21 leg pairs.
- *Ecology and uses.* It hunts at night for insects, spiders, earthworms and small vertebrates. The dried animal (wugong) is a traditional Chinese medicine; the species is farmed in Hubei, Zhejiang and other provinces, and its venom contains, besides the KCNQ-blocking peptide, many other neurotoxins and enzymes.

:::

The house centipede, *Scutigera coleoptrata* of the Mediterranean region, now spread with people around the world, and the East Asian *Thereuopoda* species, common in houses in China, represent the scutigeromorphs: a short, domed body with eight large tergites covering 15 leg-bearing segments, 15 pairs of extremely long, many-jointed legs that break off easily, antennae longer than the body and large compound eyes of up to 200 ommatidia. They are the fastest myriapods, running at up to 42 cm per second, and when they run only a few legs touch the ground at a time, the hind legs stepping outside the front ones so that they do not interfere. The brown stone centipedes of the genus *Lithobius*, common under stones and bark in Europe and northern China, represent the lithobiomorphs: 15 leg pairs, alternating long and short tergites, and a defensive behaviour in which they kick the last legs toward an attacker and throw sticky droplets from glands on the last four pairs of legs.

Most centipedes spin silk webs for the spermatophore (scutigeromorphs deposit it on the ground), and three orders brood their eggs: craterostigmomorphs and scolopendromorphs coil around them ventrally, geophilomorphs dorsally. Lithobiomorphs and scutigeromorphs lay single eggs coated with soil and leave them.

### 20.1.3. Diplopoda: Millipedes

**Definition 20.1.3 (Diplopoda)** **Diplopoda** (millipedes) is the class of myriapods whose trunk, after a short anterior region, consists of **diplosegments**, rings formed by the fusion of two segments that each bear two pairs of legs, two pairs of spiracles and two pairs of ostia; the first trunk ring (collum) is legless, the first maxillae are fused into a platelike **gnathochilarium** and the second maxillae are absent; the gonopores open on the third trunk ring.

Millipedes are slow-moving detritivores that eat decaying leaves and wood, together with the bacteria and fungi on them, and in many forest soils they are among the most important animals in the breakdown of litter. With some 12 000 described species, they are the largest class of myriapods. Most have a cylindrical body, and the cuticle of the tergites is hardened with calcium carbonate. The legs of a diplosegment move in waves that pass along the body; pushing with many legs at a time gives the force to burrow through litter and soil. The record for leg number belongs to *Eumillipes persephone*, a subterranean millipede from Western Australia with 1 306 legs (Marek et al. 2021).

Millipedes defend themselves by rolling into a coil or ball and by chemical weapons. Repugnatorial glands on the sides of many rings (ozopores) secrete benzoquinones, phenols, alkaloids or, in the flat-backed Polydesmida, hydrogen cyanide released from stored mandelonitrile. The males of most millipedes transfer sperm with gonopods, modified legs of the seventh trunk ring that pick up sperm from the gonopores and introduce it into the female; their shape is the main character for identifying species. The small bristly millipedes (Polyxenida) lack calcification and gonopods.

The anterior trunk departs from the diplosegment pattern: the first ring, the collum, is legless and forms a collar behind the head, and rings 2–4 each have a single pair of legs and are counted as true single segments. Whether a diplosegment is really two fused segments has been questioned, because in embryos the dorsal (tergal) and ventral (leg-bearing) parts of the trunk are patterned out of register, the ventral side with twice as many units as the dorsal; the diplosegment may thus reflect a decoupling of dorsal and ventral segmentation rather than a fusion of complete segments (Janssen et al. 2004).

The classification of millipedes rests on the cuticle, the gonopods and the shape of the rings:

| Group | Characters | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Penicillata (Polyxenida) | Minute, soft, uncalcified cuticle with tufts of serrate bristles; no gonopods, no repugnatorial glands; bristles detached as a defence against ants | *Polyxenus* |
| Pentazonia | Ventral plates divided in the midline; last legs of the male modified into claspers (telopods), no gonopods on ring 7; Glomerida and Sphaerotheriida short (12–13 rings) and able to roll into a ball, Glomeridesmida flat | Pill millipedes *Glomeris* (Glomerida), giant pill millipedes (Sphaerotheriida) |
| Helminthomorpha: Colobognatha | Mouthparts reduced to a suctorial cone in some; gonopods from legs 9 and 10; females coil around the eggs | *Polyzonium*, *Platydesmus* |
| Helminthomorpha: Eugnatha | The bulk of the diversity; gonopods from leg 8 (and 9); cylindrical (Julida, Spirobolida, Spirostreptida) or flat-backed with lateral keels (Polydesmida) | *Julus*, *Narceus*, *Oxidus*, *Desmoxytes* |

Two Chinese species illustrate the extremes. *Oxidus gracilis*, a polydesmidan about 2 cm long, dark brown with yellow lateral keels, is common in gardens and greenhouses and has been spread from East Asia to every continent; like other polydesmidans it is eyeless and releases benzaldehyde and hydrogen cyanide when disturbed, which give it the smell of bitter almonds. The large cylindrical spirobolidan and spirostreptidan millipedes of southern China, 10 cm or more long and often reddish, coil into a tight spiral and secrete benzoquinones that stain the skin brown; they are the "shanqiong" (mountain millipedes) of Chinese textbooks. The shocking pink dragon millipede *Desmoxytes purpurosea* of Thailand advertises its cyanide with aposematic colours, and the Californian xystodesmid *Motyxia* glows green at night with a photoprotein in its cuticle, the only known case of bioluminescence by a photoprotein on land.

The legs of a millipede move in metachronal waves from the rear forward, the left and right legs of a pair in phase, and most of them stand on the ground at any moment. This low, powerful gait, which pushes the head through soil and litter like a ram, contrasts with the fast gait of centipedes, in which the two legs of a pair are in opposite phase and fewer than a third touch the ground (Manton 1965). The different body forms are adapted to different ways of burrowing: julids and spirostreptids bulldoze head first with the smooth, cylindrical body; polydesmidans open crevices by pushing with the dorsal keels; pill millipedes wedge themselves under bark and stones.

Most millipedes are detritivores, and their role in soil formation is large. They fragment leaf litter, mix it with soil and microorganisms in the gut and deposit it as faecal pellets that are colonized by fungi and bacteria; in tropical forests without many earthworms they may be the main soil-forming animals. A few groups are predators (some Callipodida and Chordeumatida), and a few, such as the Siphonophorida, suck the juices of plants or fungi.

In reproduction, the male bends the anterior body to fill the gonopods with sperm from the gonopores on the coxae of the second legs (ring 3), then coils around the female and inserts the gonopods into her vulvae, which also lie on the third ring. The eggs, from a few to 300, are laid in soil, often in a nest of soil or faeces. Development is anamorphic (§20.1.6).

### 20.1.4. Symphyla and Pauropoda

The two remaining classes are small, pale, blind soil animals, rarely noticed but often abundant: hundreds to thousands per square metre in the upper soil of forests and grasslands.

**Definition 20.1.4 (Symphyla)** **Symphyla** is the class of small (1–9 mm), eyeless, unpigmented myriapods whose trunk has 12 leg-bearing segments, a thirteenth segment with a pair of spinnerets and a pair of long sensory hairs (trichobothria), and a minute telson; the second maxillae are fused into a labium, a single pair of spiracles opens on the sides of the head, and the gonopore is on the third trunk segment.

Symphylans (about 200 species) look like small centipedes, with long, many-jointed antennae and fast-moving legs, and have 15–24 tergites, more than the number of segments, because some tergites are divided. At the base of most legs there is an eversible coxal sac, which takes up water, and a small stylus; similar coxal vesicles and styli occur in the bristletails and other primitively wingless hexapods, and the similarity of the mouthparts (mandibles, first maxillae and a labium) is the reason why symphylans were once considered close to the ancestors of insects. The tracheae from the head spiracles supply only the first three trunk segments. Their fertilization is unusual: the male deposits spermatophores on stalks; a female that finds one bites it off and stores the sperm in pouches in her mouth cavity, then takes each egg from her gonopore with the mouthparts, smears it with sperm and fixes it to moss or soil. The young hatch with six or seven pairs of legs and add a pair at each moult; adults keep moulting, up to some 40 times in *Scutigerella immaculata*, without adding segments. *S. immaculata*, the garden symphylan, feeds on root hairs and seedlings and reaches densities of hundreds of millions per hectare in greenhouses and vegetable fields, where it is a serious pest.

**Definition 20.1.5 (Pauropoda)** **Pauropoda** is the class of minute (0.3–2 mm), eyeless, soft-bodied myriapods with branched antennae carrying flagella and a peculiar globular sense organ, a trunk of 11–12 segments with usually nine pairs of legs, tergites that each cover two segments, a gnathochilarium formed by the first maxillae and no second maxillae; most lack tracheae and a heart, and the gonopores open on the third trunk segment.

Pauropods (about 900 species) are among the smallest arthropods with a long trunk; their thin cuticle and small size allow gas exchange through the body surface, which explains the loss of tracheae and heart. A pair of pseudoculi, disc-shaped sense organs on the sides of the head, may correspond to the organs of Tömösváry. They feed on fungal hyphae and decaying matter; *Millotauropus* eats springtails and their eggs. The male hangs a spermatophore on silk threads between soil particles, and the female takes it up. The young hatch as a motionless "pupoid" stage with traces of antennae and legs, then pass through active juvenile stages with three, five, six and eight pairs of legs before the adult with nine pairs.

### 20.1.5. Phylogeny and Classification

Molecular and morphological data agree that the Myriapoda are monophyletic and that the centipedes are the sister group of the other three classes.

**Definition 20.1.6 (Progoneata)** **Progoneata** is the clade of myriapods comprising Symphyla, Pauropoda and Diplopoda, whose gonopores open near the front of the trunk, on the third trunk segment, in contrast to the posterior gonopores of the centipedes (Opisthogoneata).

Within Progoneata, pauropods and millipedes are united as Dignatha by the gnathochilarium, the loss of the second maxillae, a legless first trunk segment (the collum of millipedes), spiracles at the bases of the legs, a motionless pupoid stage after hatching and a first active juvenile with three pairs of legs:

$$
\text{Myriapoda} = \text{Chilopoda} + \text{Progoneata}, \qquad \text{Progoneata} = \text{Symphyla} + (\text{Pauropoda} + \text{Diplopoda}).
$$

Some molecular analyses of the 2000s grouped the myriapods with the chelicerates (Myriochelata or Paradoxopoda), probably an artefact of long-branch attraction and of the few genes analysed; phylogenomic analyses with many genes, and morphological characters such as the mandible and the structure of the ommatidia, support Mandibulata, with myriapods as the sister group of Pancrustacea. Within Chilopoda, Scutigeromorpha (Notostigmophora) are the sister group of the remaining orders (Pleurostigmophora), and the epimorphic Scolopendromorpha and Geophilomorpha form a clade (Epimorpha).

The Chinese textbooks treat the group in two ways, both compatible with the descriptions above once the higher classification is updated:

| Textbook treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Subphylum Myriapoda with three classes, Chilopoda, Diplopoda and Symphyla (Liu Lingyun); pauropods not treated | Subphylum Myriapoda with four classes, Pauropoda included; Chilopoda sister to Progoneata |
| Subphylum Uniramia containing Diplopoda, Pauropoda, Chilopoda, Symphyla and Insecta (Xu Chongren) | Uniramia, and the Atelocerata (Myriapoda + Hexapoda) on which it rests, are polyphyletic: hexapods belong to Pancrustacea ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.9.3)) |
| Myriapods characterized by tracheae, Malpighian tubules and a single pair of antennae, "like insects" | The same characters, but the tracheae and Malpighian tubules evolved independently in the two groups (§20.2.2), and the single pair of antennae is a loss shared by convergence |
| *Pneumodesmus* as a Middle Silurian millipede about 425 million years old | Redated to the earliest Devonian, about 414 million years ago (§20.1.7) |

The characters that myriapods share with insects, a single pair of antennae, tracheae, Malpighian tubules and uniramous legs, are either inherited from the mandibulate ancestor or convergent adaptations to land. The descriptions of the classes in these textbooks, such as the poison claws of centipedes and the double segments of millipedes, are retained unchanged.

### 20.1.6. Postembryonic Development

All myriapods develop directly, without a larva, but most do not hatch with the adult number of segments. Segments and legs are added at successive moults from a growth zone in front of the telson, a mode of development called *anamorphosis*; in the alternative, epimorphic development, the full number is formed in the embryo. Several variants are distinguished:

| Mode | Course | Groups |
|---|---|---|
| Epimorphosis | Hatch with all segments and legs; later moults only enlarge the animal | Scolopendromorpha, Geophilomorpha |
| Hemianamorphosis | Segments added at the first moults until the final number is reached, then further moults without addition | Scutigeromorpha, Lithobiomorpha, Craterostigmomorpha; pill millipedes (Pentazonia); Symphyla |
| Teloanamorphosis | Segments added until the final number, which coincides with the last (adult) moult | Polydesmida and some other helminthomorphs |
| Euanamorphosis | Segments added at every moult, even after maturity | Some Julida and other helminthomorphs |

Anamorphosis is also found in pycnogonids, many crustaceans (whose naupliar development is an extreme form of it) and the proturans among hexapods ([7.17.22](/7.17.22)), and it is probably the ancestral mode of arthropod development; epimorphosis evolved within the centipedes once, in the ancestor of Epimorpha, which also brood their eggs. The two modes reflect a trade-off: an epimorphic animal invests more yolk in each egg and hatches as a larger, complete juvenile, while an anamorphic one hatches early from a smaller egg and builds the posterior body while feeding.

![Postembryonic addition of legs in myriapods: the number of leg pairs at successive stadia (stadium 1 = the first free-living stage). The anamorphic centipedes *Scutigera* (from 4 pairs) and *Lithobius* (from 7) reach the final 15 pairs after five or six moults and *Craterostigmus* after one; the epimorphic *Scolopendra* hatches with its 21 pairs. The symphylan *Scutigerella* hatches with 6 pairs and adds one per moult to 12; the pauropod *Pauropus* passes through active stages with 3, 5, 6 and 8 pairs to the adult with 9 (the preceding immobile pupoid stage is not shown). Moults continue after the last addition. Counts after Lewis (1981) and Brusca et al. (2023).](./plots/anamorphosis.py)

The males of some European julids undergo periodomorphosis: after mating, a mature male moults into an intercalary stage with regressed gonopods, which cannot copulate, and regains functional gonopods at a later moult, alternating between reproductive and non-reproductive periods.

### 20.1.7. The Fossil Record of Myriapods

Millipedes, with their calcified cuticle, have the best fossil record of the class. The oldest body fossils are Silurian and Early Devonian millipedes from Scotland: *Kampecaris obanensis*, about 425 million years old, and *Pneumodesmus newmani*, whose rings show spiracles and which therefore breathed air. *Pneumodesmus* was long regarded as Middle Silurian (about 428 million years) and is still given this age in many textbooks, but uranium–lead dating of zircons from its formation placed it at about 414 million years, in the earliest Devonian (Suarez et al. 2017; Brookfield et al. 2020).

Several extinct groups flourished in the late Palaeozoic: the Archipolypoda, millipedes with rings alternating in size and often long spines, and the Arthropleurida, of debated position within the myriapods, whose largest species, *Arthropleura*, reached a length of more than 2 m in the coal forests of the Carboniferous, the largest land arthropod known. Its size is often attributed to the high oxygen concentration of the Carboniferous air, which would have allowed tracheae to supply a larger body ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)), although the absence of large terrestrial vertebrate predators and herbivores may have mattered as much.

Centipedes, without calcium carbonate in the cuticle, are rarer: the scutigeromorph *Crussolum* is known from fragments of legs from the late Silurian to the Devonian, and *Devonobius* from the Middle Devonian of New York (about 380 million years). The well-preserved fossils of the Rhynie chert and of later ambers show that the principal orders of centipedes and millipedes existed by the late Palaeozoic or the Mesozoic, and the organization of the myriapods has changed little since.

## 20.2. The Terrestrialization of Arthropods

### 20.2.1. The Problems of Life in Air

An aquatic animal that moves onto land faces several problems at once, all of them consequences of the physical properties of air:

| Problem | Why it arises in air | Solutions in arthropods |
|---|---|---|
| Desiccation | air is a dry medium; the body loses water by evaporation through the surface and the respiratory organs | waterproof epicuticle with a wax layer; respiratory surfaces sunk into the body; closable spiracles; water reabsorption in the hindgut; nocturnal and cryptic habits |
| Gas exchange | gills collapse and dry in air, but oxygen diffuses about 10 000 times faster in air than in water | book lungs, tracheae, pleopodal lungs, branchial chambers |
| Nitrogen excretion | ammonia is toxic and needs much water to be removed | uric acid or guanine (**uricotelic** excretion), excreted as paste |
| Excretion of salts and water balance | no water to flush excretory organs | **Malpighian tubules** emptying into the gut, with reabsorption in the rectum |
| Support | no buoyancy | stiff exoskeleton; small body size |
| Fertilization | gametes cannot be shed into water | spermatophores, internal fertilization; eggs with a resistant shell |
| Temperature and radiation | large daily fluctuations; ultraviolet light | behavior, pigmented cuticle |

A molted cuticle is a preadaptation for life on land: it already provides a support system that does not depend on water and a barrier that needs only to be waterproofed. This helps explain why panarthropods colonized land so often, while among the other ecdysozoans only nematodes, which live in water films, are found widely in soil.

### 20.2.2. Several Independent Colonizations

When the land adaptations of the panarthropods are mapped on their phylogeny ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.9)), they turn out to be scattered over separate branches whose closest relatives are aquatic (see the figure below). The minimum number of separate colonizations of land is five: onychophorans, arachnids (perhaps more than once if horseshoe crabs are nested among them, [7.17.19](/7.17.19#sec-19.5)), myriapods, hexapods and malacostracan crustaceans; within the last, woodlice, land crabs, terrestrial amphipods and land hermit crabs each moved onto land independently. A few mites, copepods and ostracods live in soil or moss water films without being truly terrestrial.

![Independent colonizations of land by panarthropods (stars), mapped on the phylogeny of BG 4e, with the main land adaptations of each lineage. Each terrestrial lineage is nested among aquatic relatives: onychophorans, arachnids (sister to the marine horseshoe crabs and sea spiders), myriapods, hexapods (sister to the marine cave-dwelling remipedes within Pancrustacea) and, within Malacostraca, woodlice and land crabs. Their respiratory and excretory organs differ in structure and origin and are therefore convergent.](./plots/terrestrialization-tree.py)

The organs that solve the same problems in these lineages differ in their details, which is the evidence that they are **convergent evolution** and not inherited from a common terrestrial ancestor:

| Character | Onychophora | Arachnida | Myriapoda | Hexapoda | Woodlice (Isopoda) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Respiratory organ | many small tufts of unbranched tracheae, spiracles scattered and unclosable | book lungs; sieve or tube tracheae opening on the opisthosoma | tracheae opening on the trunk, position differing between classes (lateral, ventral, dorsal in *Scutigera*) | branched tracheae with closable spiracles on thorax and abdomen | pleopodal lungs (pseudotracheae) in the abdominal limbs |
| Malpighian tubules | none (segmental nephridia) | endodermal | ectodermal | ectodermal | none (maxillary glands) |
| Main nitrogenous waste | ammonia, urate stores | guanine | uric acid | uric acid | ammonia, released as gas |
| Sperm transfer | spermatophores on the body surface | spermatophores, palpal organs | spermatophores, gonopods | spermatophores, copulation | copulation |

The similarity between the tracheae and Malpighian tubules of myriapods and insects is closer than that between either and the arachnid organs, which is why these characters were long taken as evidence for the Atelocerata. Their convergence follows from the strong evidence for Pancrustacea, and from the fact that insect tracheae open on the thorax and abdomen whereas those of centipedes open on segments that are not homologous to them in any simple way.

### 20.2.3. Woodlice and Land Crabs: Crustaceans in Transition

The crustacean lineages that came onto land more recently show intermediate stages. Woodlice (terrestrial Isopoda, Oniscidea) are the only crustaceans fully independent of water for reproduction: the eggs develop in a brood pouch (marsupium) under the female's thorax. They conduct water over the body surface by capillary channels, release ammonia as a gas, and in many species breathe through pleopodal lungs, air-filled invaginations of the abdominal limbs that function like tracheae. Many can roll into a ball (conglobation) to reduce water loss and protect themselves. Still, most woodlice lose water much faster than insects and are restricted to moist places and night activity.

Land crabs (Gecarcinidae, the coconut crab *Birgus latro* and others) breathe air through enlarged, vascularized branchial chambers that act as lungs, but nearly all must still return to the sea to release their larvae. They illustrate that the last step to full terrestriality, a life cycle independent of water, is the hardest.

### 20.2.4. When Did Arthropods Come onto Land?

Three kinds of evidence date the colonization of land ([7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.5.2)):

1. Trace fossils: trackways of arthropods on tidal flats and coastal dunes appear in the late Cambrian and Ordovician, about 500–480 million years ago; some were probably made by euthycarcinoids, extinct mandibulates related to the hexapods, walking out of the water for short periods (Collette et al. 2012).
2. Body fossils: the oldest are late Silurian. The Scottish millipede *Kampecaris* (about 425 million years by zircon dating; Brookfield et al. 2020) and trigonotarbid arachnids from about 420 million years ago are the earliest fully terrestrial animals known; *Pneumodesmus newmani*, which already shows spiracles and therefore breathed air, is slightly younger, from the earliest Devonian (§20.1.7). The Early Devonian Rhynie chert (about 407 million years) preserves a whole ecosystem with trigonotarbids, mites, harvestmen, centipedes, springtails and possibly the earliest insect, *Rhyniognatha*.
3. Molecular clocks: dated phylogenies place the origins of the terrestrial lineages of arachnids, myriapods and hexapods in the Cambrian or Ordovician, earlier than the oldest body fossils and close to the first spores of land plants about 470 million years ago (Lozano-Fernandez et al. 2016).

The picture is of an early, gradual invasion of land by several arthropod lineages, closely following the land plants and the soil they produced. The first terrestrial arthropods were detritivores and predators in a world of mosses, liverworts and early vascular plants; herbivory on living plant tissue was rare until later in the Devonian and Carboniferous, and the great radiation of insects came with the forests of the late Paleozoic ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

### 20.2.5. Water Loss and Body Size

Desiccation is the main limit on small arthropods in air, and its dependence on size can be captured by a simple model. Treat an animal as a cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$. Its water content is proportional to its volume, $W = w\rho\pi r^2 L$ (with $w$ the fraction of body mass that is water and $\rho$ the density), while evaporation across the cuticle is proportional to its surface area, $J = P\,\Delta p\, 2\pi r L$, where $P$ is the cuticular permeability and $\Delta p$ the difference in water vapor pressure between the body surface and the air (the saturation deficit). The time needed to lose a fraction $f$ of body water is

$$
t = \frac{f W}{J} = \frac{f w \rho\, r}{2 P\,\Delta p} .
$$

The time is proportional to the radius and inversely proportional to the permeability and the saturation deficit. The figure below evaluates the model for a loss of 20 % of body water, which is lethal for many arthropods, in dry air at 25 °C ($\Delta p \approx 3.2\,\mathrm{kPa}$). With a permeability typical of unwaxed cuticle, an animal 1 mm thick would lose a fifth of its water in about 1.5 h; with a waxed cuticle a hundred times less permeable, it would survive for days. An insect half the size of another, with the same cuticle, has half the time.

![A model of desiccation and body size (schematic, not data). For a cylindrical animal the time to lose 20 % of its body water is proportional to its radius and inversely proportional to the permeability $P$ of its cuticle (in µg cm⁻² h⁻¹ mmHg⁻¹; dry air at 25 °C, saturation deficit 3.2 kPa; respiratory water loss ignored). The three permeabilities are illustrative values spanning the range reported for terrestrial arthropods, from waxed cuticles of desert scorpions and beetles to the more permeable cuticles of woodlice and many myriapods (Hadley 1994).](./plots/desiccation-scaling.py)

The role of the wax layer was shown in classical experiments. Wigglesworth (1945) found that insects whose cuticle had been lightly abraded with alumina dust, which scratches only the epicuticle, lost water many times faster than intact ones, and that dusts of the same hardness that adsorb lipids had the same effect. Beament (1945) measured the rate of water loss of insects and of wax films extracted from them as a function of temperature: the rate rises slowly up to a transition temperature, characteristic of each species and close to the melting point of its wax, and then increases steeply, which he interpreted as the disordering of a monolayer of oriented lipid molecules. Modern measurements with gas chromatography have shown that desert species have cuticular hydrocarbons with longer chains and fewer branches and double bonds, which melt at higher temperatures (Gibbs 1998).

The model explains several observations. Small arthropods with permeable cuticles, such as most myriapods, woodlice and springtails, survive only in saturated air in soil and litter, and are active at night when the saturation deficit is small. The great reduction of cuticular permeability by the wax layer was the key innovation that let insects and arachnids live in dry, open habitats; in many insects the waxes undergo a phase transition at a "critical temperature" above which permeability rises sharply. Size and permeability can trade off: larger animals tolerate a more permeable cuticle. The model ignores water lost through the spiracles, which becomes important in active animals and is reduced by closing the spiracles and by discontinuous gas exchange ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)), and water gained by eating, drinking or absorbing vapor from the air, which some mites and insects can do.

## Questions

1. Using the water-loss model, compare a springtail 0.3 mm thick with unwaxed cuticle ($P = 100$) and a tenebrionid beetle 5 mm thick with waxed cuticle ($P = 1$) in dry air at 25 °C. How long can each survive before losing 20 % of its water?

   **Answer** With $f = 0.2$, $w = 0.7$, $\rho = 10^6\,\mu\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $\Delta p = 24\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, $t = 0.07 \times 10^6\, r / (P \times 24)$ hours with $r$ in cm. Springtail: $r = 0.015\,\mathrm{cm}$, $t = 1050/(2400) \approx 0.44\,\mathrm{h}$, under half an hour. Beetle: $r = 0.25\,\mathrm{cm}$, $t = 17\,500/24 \approx 730\,\mathrm{h}$, about a month. The 1700-fold difference comes from a 17-fold difference in size and a 100-fold difference in permeability; it explains why springtails live only in soil and litter and desert beetles can walk in the open.

2. Why are the tracheae of insects and of spiders not homologous, although both are tubes of cuticle carrying air into the body?

   **Answer** Phylogeny: spiders and insects belong to different arthropod subphyla (Chelicerata and Mandibulata) whose closest relatives (horseshoe crabs and sea spiders; crustaceans) are aquatic and lack tracheae, so the most parsimonious explanation is independent origin. Structure and position: spider tracheae open on the opisthosoma, often through a single spiracle, and arise from the posterior pair of book lungs or from other opisthosomal appendages, whereas insect tracheae open through paired spiracles on the thoracic and abdominal segments and branch to the tissues; their development is different. They are therefore convergent solutions to the same problem.

3. Centipedes and millipedes are both "many-legged", but they move very differently. Relate the differences in leg arrangement and body form to their ways of life.

   **Answer** Centipedes are fast predators: they have one pair of long legs per segment, a flattened body with flexible joints, and in fast runners such as *Scutigera* very long posterior legs that prevent interference; their legs move with a low number of legs on the ground at any time, giving speed. Millipedes are burrowing detritivores: their diplosegments carry two pairs of short legs, so that many legs push at once in waves along the cylindrical, rigid, calcified body, generating a large force for forcing a way through litter and soil at low speed. The forcipules of centipedes and the defensive glands and coiling of millipedes are part of the same contrast between pursuit predation and protected, slow living.

4. (Open question) Molecular clocks place the origin of terrestrial myriapods in the Cambrian or Ordovician, but the oldest body fossils are Silurian. Discuss possible reasons for the gap and what kind of fossil would close it.

   **Answer** Possible reasons: terrestrial animals rarely fossilize, since they do not live where sediments accumulate, and small soft-bodied soil animals need exceptional preservation such as cherts or ambers, which are rare in the Early Paleozoic; the first terrestrial forms may have lived in coastal or humid microhabitats close to the water, so their remains would be mixed with aquatic faunas and difficult to recognize as terrestrial; molecular clocks may also be biased toward older dates by calibration choices, rate variation and the lack of early fossils to constrain them. The gap would be closed by an Ordovician fossil with clear terrestrial characters, such as spiracles or tracheae (as in *Pneumodesmus*), found together with the cryptospores of early land plants in a continental deposit.

5. A centipede of the genus *Lithobius* hatches with 7 pairs of legs and a geophilomorph with its full number. What are the advantages of each mode, and why are epimorphosis and brood care found in the same clade?

   **Answer** An anamorphic centipede can hatch from a small egg and early in development, so a female can produce more eggs for the same investment, and the young build their posterior segments while feeding. An epimorphic centipede must form all segments in the embryo, which needs a large, yolky egg and a long embryonic period, but it hatches as a complete, mobile juvenile. A long embryonic period exposes the eggs to fungi and predators, and brooding (the female coiling around the eggs and cleaning them) protects that investment; brooding is therefore expected to evolve together with large eggs and epimorphic development, as it did in the ancestor of Scolopendromorpha and Geophilomorpha.
6. Why do pauropods lack tracheae and a heart, although they are terrestrial arthropods?

   **Answer** Their body is less than 2 mm long and a few tenths of a millimetre thick, with a thin, weakly sclerotized cuticle. Over such distances diffusion through the body surface and through the body fluid is fast enough to supply oxygen to all tissues (the diffusion time grows with the square of the distance), so a tracheal system and a pump for the blood bring no advantage. The same size argument explains the loss of tracheae in many small mites and springtails. The price is a permeable surface: pauropods can live only in saturated air in the soil.
