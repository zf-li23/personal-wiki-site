The Myriapoda, centipedes, millipedes and two classes of small soil animals, are the sister group of the Pancrustacea within the **Mandibulata** ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.9)). They are entirely terrestrial, and the oldest known body fossils of land animals are millipedes. This makes them the natural place to ask how arthropods came onto land, a step that was taken not once but several times: by onychophorans, by arachnids, by myriapods, by hexapods and, more recently, by several groups of crustaceans.

The first half of the chapter describes the myriapod body plan and its four classes, comparing them with the textbook descriptions. The second half follows the problem of life on land across all panarthropods: what the problems are, how each lineage solved them, why the similar solutions are regarded as convergent, when land was colonized, and how body size and the permeability of the cuticle set the time an animal can survive in dry air. The physiology of tracheae and Malpighian tubules is in [7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.5) and [7.14.23](/7.14.23#sec-23.7.2), and that of insects in particular in [7.17.26](/7.17.26).

## 20.1. Myriapoda

### 20.1.1. The Myriapod Body Plan

**Definition 20.1.1 (Myriapoda)** **Myriapoda** is the subphylum of mandibulate arthropods whose body consists of a head and a long trunk of many similar segments, each primitively with one pair of uniramous walking legs; the head bears one pair of antennae, the mandibles and one or two pairs of maxillae; gas exchange is by tracheae, excretion by ectodermal Malpighian tubules, and development is direct.

About 16 000 species have been described. Myriapods live in leaf litter, soil, rotting wood and caves, mostly in humid habitats, from the tropics to the tundra. Their segments are much less specialized than those of insects or crustaceans, and the trunk is not divided into thorax and abdomen. The head has the mandibulate set of segments ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.6)): an ocular region with groups of simple eyes (ocelli) or no eyes, the antennal (deutocerebral) segment, an appendage-less intercalary (tritocerebral) segment, the mandibles and two maxillary segments, whose appendages are fused in different ways in the different classes. Many myriapods have a pair of sensory organs of Tömösváry at the base of the antennae, probably sensitive to humidity or vibrations.

The tracheae open through spiracles, usually one pair per segment on the sides or the underside. In most myriapods the spiracles cannot be closed, and the epicuticle contains little wax, so that water is lost rapidly; this is the main reason why most myriapods are confined to moist microhabitats and are active at night (Section 20.2.5). The heart is a long tube with ostia in each segment, and the nitrogenous waste is mainly uric acid in dry-living species and ammonia in species of very moist habitats. The sexes are separate and sperm are transferred indirectly in a **spermatophore** in most groups. Development is direct, but in many groups the juveniles hatch with fewer segments and legs than the adult and add them at successive molts (anamorphic development); in others they hatch with the full number (epimorphic development).

### 20.1.2. Chilopoda: Centipedes

**Definition 20.1.2 (Chilopoda)** **Chilopoda** (centipedes) is the class of myriapods with a flattened trunk of segments each bearing one pair of legs, an odd number of leg pairs (15 to 191), and the legs of the first trunk segment modified into a pair of large, pincerlike venom claws (forcipules), with which they capture prey; the gonopores open at the posterior end of the trunk.

All centipedes are predators of other arthropods, earthworms and, in the largest tropical species of *Scolopendra*, small vertebrates. The forcipules are trunk limbs, not mouthparts, and the venom gland in each opens near its tip. Centipede venoms are mixtures of peptides and enzymes; one peptide from *Scolopendra subspinipes mutilans* blocks the KCNQ potassium channels of the heart and nerves, which explains the severe effects of some bites (Luo et al. 2018). This species is common in central and southern China, is farmed, and the dried animal is used in traditional medicine.

Five living orders are recognized:

| Order | Leg pairs | Development | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scutigeromorpha | 15 | anamorphic | very long legs, fast runners; dorsal unpaired spiracles leading to tracheal lungs; compound-like eyes (the house centipede *Scutigera* and *Thereuopoda*) |
| Lithobiomorpha | 15 | anamorphic | stout, brown, under stones and bark (*Lithobius*) |
| Craterostigmomorpha | 15 | anamorphic (hatch with 12) | two species in Tasmania and New Zealand |
| Scolopendromorpha | 21 or 23 | epimorphic | large; females guard their eggs and young (*Scolopendra*) |
| Geophilomorpha | 27–191 | epimorphic | long, thin, blind burrowers; legs always in odd numbers |

The fact that no centipede has an even number of leg pairs, although the number varies from 15 to 191, is a classic puzzle of development: it reflects the way segments are produced in pairs by a double-segment periodicity in the embryo, with the final count always odd.

### 20.1.3. Diplopoda: Millipedes

**Definition 20.1.3 (Diplopoda)** **Diplopoda** (millipedes) is the class of myriapods whose trunk, after a short anterior region, consists of diplosegments, rings formed by the fusion of two segments that each bear two pairs of legs, two pairs of spiracles and two pairs of ostia; the first trunk ring (collum) is legless, the first maxillae are fused into a platelike gnathochilarium and the second maxillae are absent; the gonopores open on the third trunk ring.

Millipedes are slow-moving detritivores that eat decaying leaves and wood, together with the bacteria and fungi on them, and in many forest soils they are among the most important animals in the breakdown of litter. With some 12 000 described species, they are the largest class of myriapods. Most have a cylindrical body, and the cuticle of the tergites is hardened with calcium carbonate. The legs of a diplosegment move in waves that pass along the body; pushing with many legs at a time gives the force to burrow through litter and soil. The record for leg number belongs to *Eumillipes persephone*, a subterranean millipede from Western Australia with 1 306 legs (Marek et al. 2021).

Millipedes defend themselves by rolling into a coil or ball and by chemical weapons. Repugnatorial glands on the sides of many rings (ozopores) secrete benzoquinones, phenols, alkaloids or, in the flat-backed Polydesmida, hydrogen cyanide released from stored mandelonitrile. The males of most millipedes transfer sperm with gonopods, modified legs of the seventh trunk ring that pick up sperm from the gonopores and introduce it into the female; their shape is the main character for identifying species. The small bristly millipedes (Polyxenida) lack calcification and gonopods.

### 20.1.4. Symphyla and Pauropoda

The two remaining classes are small, pale, blind soil animals. Symphyla (about 200 species, 1–9 mm) resemble small centipedes: they have 12 pairs of legs, long antennae, a pair of spinnerets on the penultimate segment from which they spin silk, and, like insects, a labium formed by the fused second maxillae; some, such as *Scutigerella immaculata*, damage the roots of crops in greenhouses. Pauropoda (about 900 species, 0.5–2 mm) have usually 9 pairs of legs, branched antennae with two or three flagella, tergites that each cover two segments, and in most species no tracheae or heart; they feed on fungal hyphae. ### 20.1.5. Phylogeny and Classification

Molecular and morphological data agree that the Myriapoda are monophyletic and that the centipedes are the sister group of the other three classes, which share anterior gonopores and are called Progoneata. Within Progoneata, pauropods and millipedes, which share the gnathochilarium and the first walking legs on the second or third trunk segment, are united as Dignatha:

$$
\text{Myriapoda} = \text{Chilopoda} + \text{Progoneata}, \qquad \text{Progoneata} = \text{Symphyla} + (\text{Pauropoda} + \text{Diplopoda}).
$$

Chinese textbooks place the myriapods either as a subphylum with the classes Chilopoda, Diplopoda and Symphyla (pauropods not treated), or within a subphylum Uniramia together with the insects. The second view, which goes back to the Atelocerata hypothesis, is rejected in favor of Pancrustacea ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.9.3)): the characters that myriapods share with insects, a single pair of antennae, tracheae, Malpighian tubules and uniramous legs, are either inherited from the mandibulate ancestor or convergent adaptations to land. The descriptions of the classes in these textbooks, such as the poison claws of centipedes and the double segments of millipedes, are retained unchanged.

## 20.2. The Terrestrialization of Arthropods

### 20.2.1. The Problems of Life in Air

An aquatic animal that moves onto land faces several problems at once, all of them consequences of the physical properties of air:

| Problem | Why it arises in air | Solutions in arthropods |
|---|---|---|
| Desiccation | air is a dry medium; the body loses water by evaporation through the surface and the respiratory organs | waterproof epicuticle with a wax layer; respiratory surfaces sunk into the body; closable spiracles; water reabsorption in the hindgut; nocturnal and cryptic habits |
| Gas exchange | gills collapse and dry in air, but oxygen diffuses about 10 000 times faster in air than in water | book lungs, tracheae, pleopodal lungs, branchial chambers |
| Nitrogen excretion | ammonia is toxic and needs much water to be removed | uric acid or guanine (**uricotelic** excretion), excreted as paste |
| Excretion of salts and water balance | no water to flush excretory organs | **Malpighian tubules** emptying into the gut, with reabsorption in the rectum |
| Support | no buoyancy | stiff exoskeleton; small body size |
| Fertilization | gametes cannot be shed into water | spermatophores, internal fertilization; eggs with a resistant shell |
| Temperature and radiation | large daily fluctuations; ultraviolet light | behavior, pigmented cuticle |

A molted cuticle is a preadaptation for life on land: it already provides a support system that does not depend on water and a barrier that needs only to be waterproofed. This helps explain why panarthropods colonized land so often, while among the other ecdysozoans only nematodes, which live in water films, are found widely in soil.

### 20.2.2. Several Independent Colonizations

When the land adaptations of the panarthropods are mapped on their phylogeny ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.9)), they turn out to be scattered over separate branches whose closest relatives are aquatic (see the figure below). The minimum number of separate colonizations of land is five: onychophorans, arachnids (perhaps more than once if horseshoe crabs are nested among them, [7.17.19](/7.17.19#sec-19.5)), myriapods, hexapods and malacostracan crustaceans; within the last, woodlice, land crabs, terrestrial amphipods and land hermit crabs each moved onto land independently. A few mites, copepods and ostracods live in soil or moss water films without being truly terrestrial.

![Independent colonizations of land by panarthropods (stars), mapped on the phylogeny of BG 4e, with the main land adaptations of each lineage. Each terrestrial lineage is nested among aquatic relatives: onychophorans, arachnids (sister to the marine horseshoe crabs and sea spiders), myriapods, hexapods (sister to the marine cave-dwelling remipedes within Pancrustacea) and, within Malacostraca, woodlice and land crabs. Their respiratory and excretory organs differ in structure and origin and are therefore convergent.](./plots/terrestrialization-tree.py)

The organs that solve the same problems in these lineages differ in their details, which is the evidence that they are **convergent evolution** and not inherited from a common terrestrial ancestor:

| Character | Onychophora | Arachnida | Myriapoda | Hexapoda | Woodlice (Isopoda) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Respiratory organ | many small tufts of unbranched tracheae, spiracles scattered and unclosable | book lungs; sieve or tube tracheae opening on the opisthosoma | tracheae opening on the trunk, position differing between classes (lateral, ventral, dorsal in *Scutigera*) | branched tracheae with closable spiracles on thorax and abdomen | pleopodal lungs (pseudotracheae) in the abdominal limbs |
| Malpighian tubules | none (segmental nephridia) | endodermal | ectodermal | ectodermal | none (maxillary glands) |
| Main nitrogenous waste | ammonia, urate stores | guanine | uric acid | uric acid | ammonia, released as gas |
| Sperm transfer | spermatophores on the body surface | spermatophores, palpal organs | spermatophores, gonopods | spermatophores, copulation | copulation |

The similarity between the tracheae and Malpighian tubules of myriapods and insects is closer than that between either and the arachnid organs, which is why these characters were long taken as evidence for the Atelocerata. Their convergence follows from the strong evidence for Pancrustacea, and from the fact that insect tracheae open on the thorax and abdomen whereas those of centipedes open on segments that are not homologous to them in any simple way.

### 20.2.3. Woodlice and Land Crabs: Crustaceans in Transition

The crustacean lineages that came onto land more recently show intermediate stages. Woodlice (terrestrial Isopoda, Oniscidea) are the only crustaceans fully independent of water for reproduction: the eggs develop in a brood pouch (marsupium) under the female's thorax. They conduct water over the body surface by capillary channels, release ammonia as a gas, and in many species breathe through pleopodal lungs, air-filled invaginations of the abdominal limbs that function like tracheae. Many can roll into a ball (conglobation) to reduce water loss and protect themselves. Still, most woodlice lose water much faster than insects and are restricted to moist places and night activity.

Land crabs (Gecarcinidae, the coconut crab *Birgus latro* and others) breathe air through enlarged, vascularized branchial chambers that act as lungs, but nearly all must still return to the sea to release their larvae. They illustrate that the last step to full terrestriality, a life cycle independent of water, is the hardest.

### 20.2.4. When Did Arthropods Come onto Land?

Three kinds of evidence date the colonization of land ([7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.5.2)):

1. Trace fossils: trackways of arthropods on tidal flats and coastal dunes appear in the late Cambrian and Ordovician, about 500–480 million years ago; some were probably made by euthycarcinoids, extinct mandibulates related to the hexapods, walking out of the water for short periods (Collette et al. 2012).
2. Body fossils: the oldest are Silurian. Millipedes from Scotland, *Kampecaris* (about 425 million years by zircon dating; Brookfield et al. 2020) and *Pneumodesmus newmani*, which already shows spiracles and therefore breathed air, together with trigonotarbid arachnids from about 420 million years ago, are the earliest fully terrestrial animals known. The Early Devonian Rhynie chert (about 407 million years) preserves a whole ecosystem with trigonotarbids, mites, harvestmen, centipedes, springtails and possibly the earliest insect, *Rhyniognatha*.
3. Molecular clocks: dated phylogenies place the origins of the terrestrial lineages of arachnids, myriapods and hexapods in the Cambrian or Ordovician, earlier than the oldest body fossils and close to the first spores of land plants about 470 million years ago (Lozano-Fernandez et al. 2016).

The picture is of an early, gradual invasion of land by several arthropod lineages, closely following the land plants and the soil they produced. The first terrestrial arthropods were detritivores and predators in a world of mosses, liverworts and early vascular plants; herbivory on living plant tissue was rare until later in the Devonian and Carboniferous, and the great radiation of insects came with the forests of the late Paleozoic ([7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

### 20.2.5. Water Loss and Body Size

Desiccation is the main limit on small arthropods in air, and its dependence on size can be captured by a simple model. Treat an animal as a cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$. Its water content is proportional to its volume, $W = w\rho\pi r^2 L$ (with $w$ the fraction of body mass that is water and $\rho$ the density), while evaporation across the cuticle is proportional to its surface area, $J = P\,\Delta p\, 2\pi r L$, where $P$ is the cuticular permeability and $\Delta p$ the difference in water vapor pressure between the body surface and the air (the saturation deficit). The time needed to lose a fraction $f$ of body water is

$$
t = \frac{f W}{J} = \frac{f w \rho\, r}{2 P\,\Delta p} .
$$

The time is proportional to the radius and inversely proportional to the permeability and the saturation deficit. The figure below evaluates the model for a loss of 20 % of body water, which is lethal for many arthropods, in dry air at 25 °C ($\Delta p \approx 3.2\,\mathrm{kPa}$). With a permeability typical of unwaxed cuticle, an animal 1 mm thick would lose a fifth of its water in about 1.5 h; with a waxed cuticle a hundred times less permeable, it would survive for days. An insect half the size of another, with the same cuticle, has half the time.

![A model of desiccation and body size (schematic, not data). For a cylindrical animal the time to lose 20 % of its body water is proportional to its radius and inversely proportional to the permeability $P$ of its cuticle (in µg cm⁻² h⁻¹ mmHg⁻¹; dry air at 25 °C, saturation deficit 3.2 kPa; respiratory water loss ignored). The three permeabilities are illustrative values spanning the range reported for terrestrial arthropods, from waxed cuticles of desert scorpions and beetles to the more permeable cuticles of woodlice and many myriapods (Hadley 1994).](./plots/desiccation-scaling.py)

The model explains several observations. Small arthropods with permeable cuticles, such as most myriapods, woodlice and springtails, survive only in saturated air in soil and litter, and are active at night when the saturation deficit is small. The great reduction of cuticular permeability by the wax layer was the key innovation that let insects and arachnids live in dry, open habitats; in many insects the waxes undergo a phase transition at a "critical temperature" above which permeability rises sharply. Size and permeability can trade off: larger animals tolerate a more permeable cuticle. The model ignores water lost through the spiracles, which becomes important in active animals and is reduced by closing the spiracles and by discontinuous gas exchange ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)), and water gained by eating, drinking or absorbing vapor from the air, which some mites and insects can do.

## Questions

1. Using the water-loss model, compare a springtail 0.3 mm thick with unwaxed cuticle ($P = 100$) and a tenebrionid beetle 5 mm thick with waxed cuticle ($P = 1$) in dry air at 25 °C. How long can each survive before losing 20 % of its water?

   **Answer** With $f = 0.2$, $w = 0.7$, $\rho = 10^6\,\mu\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $\Delta p = 24\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, $t = 0.07 \times 10^6\, r / (P \times 24)$ hours with $r$ in cm. Springtail: $r = 0.015\,\mathrm{cm}$, $t = 1050/(2400) \approx 0.44\,\mathrm{h}$, under half an hour. Beetle: $r = 0.25\,\mathrm{cm}$, $t = 17\,500/24 \approx 730\,\mathrm{h}$, about a month. The 1700-fold difference comes from a 17-fold difference in size and a 100-fold difference in permeability; it explains why springtails live only in soil and litter and desert beetles can walk in the open.

2. Why are the tracheae of insects and of spiders not homologous, although both are tubes of cuticle carrying air into the body?

   **Answer** Phylogeny: spiders and insects belong to different arthropod subphyla (Chelicerata and Mandibulata) whose closest relatives (horseshoe crabs and sea spiders; crustaceans) are aquatic and lack tracheae, so the most parsimonious explanation is independent origin. Structure and position: spider tracheae open on the opisthosoma, often through a single spiracle, and arise from the posterior pair of book lungs or from other opisthosomal appendages, whereas insect tracheae open through paired spiracles on the thoracic and abdominal segments and branch to the tissues; their development is different. They are therefore convergent solutions to the same problem.

3. Centipedes and millipedes are both "many-legged", but they move very differently. Relate the differences in leg arrangement and body form to their ways of life.

   **Answer** Centipedes are fast predators: they have one pair of long legs per segment, a flattened body with flexible joints, and in fast runners such as *Scutigera* very long posterior legs that prevent interference; their legs move with a low number of legs on the ground at any time, giving speed. Millipedes are burrowing detritivores: their diplosegments carry two pairs of short legs, so that many legs push at once in waves along the cylindrical, rigid, calcified body, generating a large force for forcing a way through litter and soil at low speed. The forcipules of centipedes and the defensive glands and coiling of millipedes are part of the same contrast between pursuit predation and protected, slow living.

4. (Open question) Molecular clocks place the origin of terrestrial myriapods in the Cambrian or Ordovician, but the oldest body fossils are Silurian. Discuss possible reasons for the gap and what kind of fossil would close it.

   **Answer** Possible reasons: terrestrial animals rarely fossilize, since they do not live where sediments accumulate, and small soft-bodied soil animals need exceptional preservation such as cherts or ambers, which are rare in the Early Paleozoic; the first terrestrial forms may have lived in coastal or humid microhabitats close to the water, so their remains would be mixed with aquatic faunas and difficult to recognize as terrestrial; molecular clocks may also be biased toward older dates by calibration choices, rate variation and the lack of early fossils to constrain them. The gap would be closed by an Ordovician fossil with clear terrestrial characters, such as spiracles or tracheae (as in *Pneumodesmus*), found together with the cryptospores of early land plants in a continental deposit.
