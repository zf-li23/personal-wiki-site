The skin is the largest organ of most vertebrates and the boundary between the animal and its environment. It protects against abrasion, pathogens and radiation; it controls the loss and gain of water and salts; it takes part in respiration, thermoregulation, sensation, communication and, through its derivatives, in locomotion, feeding and fighting. Its derivatives are among the most varied structures in the vertebrate body: the bony armour of the first vertebrates, the scales of fishes and reptiles, the feathers of birds and the hair of mammals, the glands that make mucus, poison, sweat and milk.

All these derivatives come from two layers that are present in every vertebrate: an epidermis of ectoderm and a dermis of mesoderm and neural crest. The distinction between the two layers organizes the chapter, as the distinction between dermal bone and cartilage bone ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.6.2)) organizes the skeleton. The epidermis makes glands and the keratinized structures that dominate the skin of land vertebrates; the dermis makes bone, and the dermal bone of the skin is the origin of the dermatocranium and of the dermal shoulder girdle ([7.17.58](/7.17.58), [7.17.61](/7.17.61)). The two layers act together in the placodes that form teeth, placoid scales, feathers and hairs.

The chapter first describes the general structure of the skin and its embryonic origin (§57.1). It then treats the epidermal glands (§57.2), the keratinized structures (§57.3), with feathers and hair in their own sections (§57.4, §57.5), and the dermal skeleton of the skin, with the classification and evolution of fish scales (§57.6). Section 57.7 compares the horns and antlers of mammals, §57.8 pigment and colour, and §57.9 summarizes the skin of the vertebrate classes in one table, with a comparison to the Chinese textbooks. The amphibian skin as a respiratory organ is treated in [7.17.49](/7.17.49#sec-49.6), the thermoregulatory role of fur and feathers in [7.14.31](/7.14.31), and the development of skin placodes in [7.5.22](/7.5.22).

## 57.1. Structure and Origin of the Skin

### 57.1.1. Epidermis, Dermis and Hypodermis

The vertebrate skin consists of an outer epidermis, a stratified epithelium, and an inner dermis of dense connective tissue, separated by a basement membrane. Beneath the dermis lies the hypodermis (subcutis), a layer of loose connective tissue and fat that attaches the skin to the underlying muscles; it is usually not counted as part of the skin, but its fat is the main insulation of whales and seals and an energy store in hibernators.

In amphioxus the epidermis is a single layer of columnar cells that secretes a thin non-cellular cuticle, as in most invertebrates ([7.17.2](/7.17.2#sec-2.3.2)). In all vertebrates it is stratified: new cells are produced in a basal layer (stratum basale, or stratum germinativum) and move outward as they differentiate. In aquatic vertebrates the outer cells remain alive and secrete mucus; in terrestrial vertebrates they fill with keratin and die, forming a cornified layer (stratum corneum) that is shed continuously in flakes (mammals, birds) or periodically in sheets (amphibians, lepidosaurs).

The epidermis of mammals has four or five layers, from deep to superficial:

| Layer | Cells | Process |
|---|---|---|
| Stratum basale | One layer of cuboidal or columnar cells on the basement membrane, with stem cells, melanocytes and Merkel cells | Cell division |
| Stratum spinosum | Several layers of polygonal cells joined by many desmosomes ("spines" in sections) | Synthesis of keratin filaments |
| Stratum granulosum | Two or three layers of flattened cells with keratohyalin granules and lamellar bodies | Release of lipids into the intercellular space; cells begin to die |
| Stratum lucidum | A few layers of clear, flattened dead cells | Only in thick skin of palms and soles |
| Stratum corneum | Many layers of flat dead cells filled with keratin, embedded in lipid | Barrier; desquamation |

The barrier to water lies in the stratum corneum: the intercellular lipids, ceramides, cholesterol and fatty acids arranged in lamellae, make the skin of amniotes waterproof. Removing the lipids with solvents increases the evaporative water loss of reptile skin several-fold. The thickness of the cornified layer varies with the mechanical demand, from a few cell layers on the eyelids to more than a millimetre on the soles of the feet.

The dermis is a felt of collagen and elastic fibres with fibroblasts, blood vessels, nerves, nerve endings and pigment cells. In fishes and amphibians it consists of an outer loose layer (stratum spongiosum, or stratum laxum) and an inner compact layer (stratum compactum) of orthogonal plies of collagen that resist stretching in all directions while allowing the body to bend; in mammals of a papillary layer, whose papillae interdigitate with the epidermis, and a reticular layer. The dermis is the source of all the bone of the skin and of the dermal papillae that induce feathers and hairs.

### 57.1.2. Embryonic Origin

The epidermis derives from surface ectoderm; the dermis has three sources, by region ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.7.2)):

- the dermis of the back, from the dermomyotome of the somites;
- the dermis of the flanks, belly and limbs, from the somatic layer of the lateral plate mesoderm;
- the dermis of the face and the front of the head, from cranial neural crest.

The pigment cells of the skin, wherever they come to lie, all derive from the neural crest, as do the odontoblasts of teeth and of the odontodes of the head. The odontoblasts of the dermal denticles of the trunk in skates also derive from trunk neural crest (Gillis et al. 2017). The interaction between epidermis and dermis, in which the dermis specifies what the epidermis will make (a feather, a scale, a hair) and the epidermis produces it, is the general mechanism of skin appendage development and is described in [7.5.22](/7.5.22).

### 57.1.3. A Model of Evaporation through the Skin

The barrier function of the skin can be expressed by its resistance to the diffusion of water vapour. Evaporation from a wet surface into air is driven by the difference in water-vapour density between the surface, which is saturated at skin temperature, and the air. Two resistances act in series: the boundary layer of still air next to the body, $r_{\mathrm{b}}$, which depends on body size and wind, and the resistance of the skin itself, $r_{\mathrm{s}}$.

**Formula 57.1.1 (Cutaneous Evaporation)** The rate of evaporative water loss per unit area of skin is

$$
E = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{sat}}(T_{\mathrm{s}}) - h\,\rho_{\mathrm{sat}}(T_{\mathrm{a}})}{r_{\mathrm{b}} + r_{\mathrm{s}}},
$$
where $\rho_{\mathrm{sat}}(T)$ is the saturation vapour density at temperature $T$ (about $17.3\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-3}}$ at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$), $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ and $T_{\mathrm{a}}$ the skin and air temperatures, $h$ the relative humidity of the air, and the resistances are measured in $\mathrm{s\,m^{-1}}$ (often quoted in $\mathrm{s\,cm^{-1}}$, $1\,\mathrm{s\,cm^{-1}} = 100\,\mathrm{s\,m^{-1}}$).

The skin resistance of most frogs and salamanders is close to zero, so their skin behaves almost like an open water surface and evaporation is limited by the boundary layer alone ($r_{\mathrm{b}}$ of the order of $1\,\mathrm{s\,cm^{-1}}$ for a small frog in still air). The skin resistance of reptiles, birds and mammals is typically one to three orders of magnitude higher, of the order of $10$–$1000\,\mathrm{s\,cm^{-1}}$, largely owing to the lipids of the stratum corneum (orders of magnitude after the review of Lillywhite 2006). A few tree frogs of arid regions, such as *Phyllomedusa*, which wipes a waxy secretion over its body, and *Chiromantis*, reach reptile-like resistances of several hundred seconds per centimetre.

**Example 57.1.1** A frog with $r_{\mathrm{s}} \approx 0$ and $r_{\mathrm{b}} = 1\,\mathrm{s\,cm^{-1}} = 100\,\mathrm{s\,m^{-1}}$ sits in air at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and 50 % relative humidity, with its skin cooled by evaporation to $17\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{sat}} \approx 14.5\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-3}}$). The driving difference is $14.5 - 0.5 \times 17.3 \approx 5.9\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-3}}$, and $E \approx 5.9/100 = 0.059\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$, about $210\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}\,h^{-1}}$. A 20 g frog with about $40\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ of exposed skin loses about $0.85\,\mathrm{g}$ per hour, more than 4 % of its body mass. A lizard of the same size with $r_{\mathrm{s}} = 100\,\mathrm{s\,cm^{-1}}$ loses about $100$ times less. The calculation explains why amphibians must stay near water, seek humid shelters or adopt a water-conserving posture that reduces the exposed area, and why the evolution of a lipid barrier was essential for the colonization of dry land by amniotes.

## 57.2. Glands of the Epidermis

### 57.2.1. Unicellular and Multicellular Glands

All vertebrate skin glands derive from the epidermis, although multicellular glands sink into the dermis and open by ducts. In fishes the dominant glands are single cells within the epidermis: *mucous cells* (goblet cells), which keep the body slimy, reduce friction and protect against pathogens and abrasion; and in many teleosts *club cells*, which in the Ostariophysi (carps, catfishes, characins) release an alarm substance (Schreckstoff) when the skin is damaged, causing fright reactions in shoal mates (von Frisch 1938). Hagfishes have, in addition, large slime glands along the flanks that release mucus and thread cells; a single hagfish can turn several litres of water into slime in a fraction of a second.

Multicellular glands are rare in fishes. Some form venom glands in the grooves of fin spines (stingrays, scorpionfishes, stonefishes), and some deep-sea fishes and sharks have *photophores*, light organs of epidermal origin, with or without symbiotic bacteria, used for counterillumination and signalling (**bioluminescence**). In tetrapods all skin glands are multicellular.

### 57.2.2. The Glands of Tetrapods

The glands of amphibians are described in [7.17.49](/7.17.49#sec-49.6): mucous glands, which keep the skin moist for gas exchange, and granular (serous, poison) glands, which secrete alkaloids, peptides and amines for defence. The skin of amniotes is relatively dry, and glands are restricted to certain sites, which suggests that the waterproof cornified skin evolved at the expense of a glandular one.

| Gland | Type | Secretion and function | Occurrence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mucous gland | Simple alveolar | Mucus: moisture, gas exchange, protection | Amphibians |
| Granular (serous, poison) gland | Simple alveolar, with a myoepithelial sheath | Toxins (bufadienolides of toads, batrachotoxins of poison frogs, peptides), antimicrobial peptides | Amphibians; aggregated as the parotoid glands of toads |
| Femoral and precloacal glands | Holocrine | Waxy secretion with pheromones, marking substrate | Many lizards |
| Musk (scent) glands | Holocrine | Odorous secretion | Turtles (inguinal and axillary), crocodylians (throat and cloaca), snakes (cloacal) |
| Uropygial (preen) gland | Holocrine, bilobed, at the base of the tail | Waxes (monoester and diester waxes), spread on the feathers during preening | Most birds; absent in ratites, some parrots and pigeons |
| Sebaceous gland | Holocrine, opening into a hair follicle | Sebum (triglycerides, wax esters, squalene): lubrication and waterproofing of hair and skin | Mammals; modified as the meibomian (tarsal) glands of the eyelids |
| Sweat gland, eccrine | Simple coiled tubular, opening onto the surface | Watery sweat: evaporative cooling, grip | Mammals; on the whole body in humans, on foot pads in most others |
| Sweat gland, apocrine | Coiled tubular, opening into a hair follicle | Proteinaceous secretion, odour after bacterial action; cooling in horses and cattle | Mammals; in humans in axilla and groin |
| Mammary gland | Compound tubuloalveolar | Milk | Mammals |
| Scent glands of mammals | Modified sebaceous or apocrine glands | Pheromones and marking substances | Musk gland of musk deer, anal glands of mustelids, the preorbital glands of antelopes, the ceruminous glands of the ear canal |

The uropygial gland is the only significant skin gland of birds. Its secretion keeps the feathers flexible and water-repellent and has antimicrobial properties; in some species it contains volatile compounds used in communication. The statement, common in older textbooks, that its secretion contains a precursor of vitamin D that is converted on the feathers by sunlight is plausible but not firmly established, and birds without the gland are not generally rachitic.

**Definition 57.2.1 (Mammary Gland)** A **mammary gland** is a compound tubuloalveolar gland of the mammalian skin that secretes milk under the control of prolactin and releases it through ducts under the action of oxytocin on myoepithelial cells. It develops from paired ventrolateral thickenings of the embryonic epidermis, the milk lines, along which buds form at species-specific positions.

The mammary gland is thought to derive from glands associated with hair follicles, possibly apocrine glands that originally moistened parchment-shelled eggs; monotremes lack nipples and secrete milk onto areolar patches of skin with mammary hairs, from which the young lap it. In therian mammals nipples or teats form at the openings: in a nipple (primates, rodents) many ducts open separately on the tip, in a teat (cattle, goats) the ducts open into a cistern that drains through one canal. The number and position of nipples correlate with litter size: one pectoral pair in primates, bats and elephants, up to 27 in the tenrec *Tenrec ecaudatus*. Milk itself is treated in [7.14.38](/7.14.38).

## 57.3. Keratinized Structures

### 57.3.1. Keratins

Keratins are the intermediate-filament proteins of epithelial cells. Two families form the hard structures of the skin. The α-keratins (keratin intermediate filaments) occur in all vertebrates and make the soft epidermis and, in mammals, the hard hair, nails, claws, hooves and horn sheaths. The harder corneous β-proteins (formerly called β-keratins, although they are not keratins in the molecular sense) are small proteins with a β-sheet core that occur only in sauropsids; they form the hard scales, claws and beaks of reptiles and the feathers of birds. Their gene family, clustered in the epidermal differentiation complex, expanded in parallel with the diversification of feathers (Greenwalt et al. 2013).

Because the corneous β-proteins are a sauropsid feature, the hard scales of reptiles are not homologous with the softer scales of the tails and feet of rodents and pangolins, which are built of α-keratin. This was noted in [7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.1.3).

### 57.3.2. Horny Teeth, Beaks and Sheaths

Before keratin became widespread in the skin, it formed local cutting surfaces. The *horny teeth* of lampreys and hagfishes, on the oral disc and the tongue-like piston, are cones of keratinized epidermis that are replaced from below; they are not homologous with true teeth, which are odontodes. Tadpoles have horny jaw sheaths and rows of keratinized labial teeth, which they lose at metamorphosis.

Beaks (rhamphothecae) evolved independently in several lineages: turtles, birds, several groups of dinosaurs (ceratopsians, ornithopods), dicynodont synapsids and the platypus (whose bill is soft and sensory, with electroreceptors). In birds the loss of teeth and the formation of the beak are linked in development, and the ancestral state of crown birds is toothless with a keratin beak; Mesozoic birds such as *Hesperornis* and *Ichthyornis* had both teeth and a partial beak at the tip of the jaws. Baleen, the feeding apparatus of mysticete whales, consists of plates of α-keratin that grow from the palate in a dense series and fray on their inner edge into a filter; mysticete embryos develop tooth buds that are resorbed before birth.

### 57.3.3. Scales of Amniotes

The scales of reptiles are folds of the skin with a thick outer layer of corneous β-protein and a thinner, more flexible hinge region of α-keratin between them. They may be overlapping (imbricate, as in most snakes and many lizards), juxtaposed (granular, as in geckos) or large plates (scutes) without overlap (turtles, crocodylians), and they may be underlain by osteoderms (§57.6.4). The arrangement of the scales of the head and body is an important character in the identification of lizards and snakes. The enlarged ventral scales of snakes, each connected by muscles to a pair of ribs, take part in rectilinear and lateral locomotion.

Lepidosaurs shed the whole outer epidermal generation at once: below the old outer layer a new one forms, and the two separate along a cleavage zone, so that snakes shed their skin in one piece (including the transparent spectacle over the eye) several times a year. Turtles and crocodylians shed small flakes or wear their scutes away.

In birds the scales of the feet and of the base of the beak are of two kinds: scutate scales on the front of the tarsometatarsus, which develop from placodes like feathers, and reticulate scales on the sole, which lack a placode stage. Feathers and scutate scales can interconvert: mutant chickens with feathered feet and experiments manipulating the Shh and BMP pathways show that the same placodes can give rise to either (Dhouailly 2009).

### 57.3.4. Claws, Nails and Hooves

Claws first appeared in amniotes; the claw-like keratinized tips of the toes of *Xenopus* and some salamanders evolved independently. A claw (unguis) consists of a dorsal plate (the unguis proper), curved over the terminal phalanx and growing from a matrix at its base, and a softer ventral plate (the subunguis); because the dorsal plate grows faster than the ventral one and is harder, the claw curves and stays sharp as it wears. Mammals modified the claw in two directions.

| Structure | Dorsal plate | Ventral plate | Digital pad | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claw | Curved, laterally compressed | Narrow | Present behind the claw | Most reptiles, birds, carnivorans, rodents |
| Nail | Flat, broad | Reduced to a narrow strip | Large, sensitive pad below | Primates |
| Hoof | Curved around the tip of the digit, enclosing the ventral plate | Forms the sole | Forms the frog (cuneus) at the back | Ungulates: perissodactyls, artiodactyls, hyraxes and elephants (hoof-like nails) |

A horse's hoof grows about 6–10 mm per month and is worn at the same rate on hard ground; it encloses the distal phalanx, to which it is attached by interdigitating epidermal and dermal laminae that transmit the weight of the animal, about half a tonne on four hooves.

## 57.4. Feathers

### 57.4.1. Structure and Types

**Definition 57.4.1 (Feather)** A **feather** is a branched epidermal appendage of birds and some other theropod dinosaurs, made of corneous β-protein and grown from a tubular follicle. A typical feather consists of a hollow calamus (quill) inserted in the follicle and a rachis (shaft) that bears two vanes of barbs; each barb bears two rows of barbules, and the hooked distal barbules of one barb interlock with the grooved proximal barbules of the next, forming a closed vane.

Feather types differ in how much of this plan they develop:

| Type | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Contour feather | Rachis with closed (pennaceous) vanes, a downy basal part | Covers the body; streamlining, insulation, colour |
| Flight feathers: remiges and rectrices | Large contour feathers with asymmetric vanes (in flying birds), attached to the hand (primaries), forearm (secondaries) and tail | Lift and thrust; steering and lift by the tail |
| Down feather | Short calamus and rachis or none, a tuft of loose barbs with barbules that do not interlock | Insulation beneath the contour feathers; the natal down of chicks |
| Semiplume | Rachis with loose vanes | Insulation, buoyancy |
| Filoplume | Long thin shaft with a few barbs at the tip, many nerve endings at the base | Sensing the position of contour feathers |
| Bristle | Stiff rachis with few or no barbs | Protection of eyes and nostrils; around the mouth of nightjars |
| Powder down | Continuously growing down that disintegrates into a fine keratin powder | Waterproofing (herons, parrots, pigeons) |

The feathers are not evenly distributed but grow in tracts (pterylae) separated by bare spaces (apteria), except in ratites, penguins and a few others. A pigeon has about 3 000 feathers, a swan about 25 000. Feathers are replaced in molts, usually once or twice a year, under the control of thyroid and sex hormones and day length; many species have distinct breeding and non-breeding plumages, and most molt their flight feathers sequentially so that they never lose the ability to fly, although ducks and geese molt them all at once and are flightless for several weeks.

### 57.4.2. Development

A feather develops from a placode, an epidermal thickening over a dermal condensation. The placode grows into a cylindrical bud, the bud sinks at its base into the skin to form a follicle, and at the base of the follicle a ring of proliferating epidermal cells, the collar, produces the feather as a tube around a vascular dermal pulp. Within the tube the epidermis is divided into longitudinal *barb ridges*, each of which becomes a barb, while cells between them die and separate the barbs. In a radially symmetric feather (down) the barb ridges are parallel; in a bilaterally symmetric feather they grow helically around the collar toward the dorsal side, where they fuse into a rachis. When the feather emerges from its sheath, it unfurls into a flat vane.

This mode of growth explains the hierarchical branching of a feather and its evolution. Prum (1999) proposed a model in which feather complexity evolved in stages, each corresponding to a developmental novelty of the follicle and collar.

| Stage | Structure | Developmental novelty | Fossil example |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | An unbranched hollow cylinder | Follicle and collar | Filaments of *Sinosauropteryx*, of ornithischians such as *Psittacosaurus* and *Tianyulong* (homology uncertain) |
| II | A tuft of barbs from a short calamus | Barb ridges | Down-like filaments of *Beipiaosaurus*, *Yutyrannus* |
| III | A rachis bearing barbs (IIIa) or barbs bearing barbules (IIIb) | Helical displacement of barb ridges (rachis); differentiation of barbule plates | Filaments of *Sinornithosaurus* |
| IV | Closed pennaceous vane with interlocking barbules | Hooked and grooved barbules | *Caudipteryx*, *Microraptor*, *Anchiornis* |
| V | Asymmetric flight feathers and other specializations | Differential growth of the two vanes | *Microraptor*, *Archaeopteryx*, modern birds |

The many feathered dinosaurs of the Jehol biota of western Liaoning (about 125 million years old) and the slightly older Yanliao biota have confirmed the general order of the model: filaments are widespread in coelurosaurian theropods, and pennaceous feathers appear before flight, in non-flying maniraptorans with feathered arms and tails used for display and brooding. Filaments in pterosaurs (pycnofibres) and in some ornithischians raise the possibility that the follicle evolved once at the base of the Ornithodira, but their homology with feathers is still debated.

## 57.5. Hair

### 57.5.1. Structure and Types

**Definition 57.5.1 (Hair)** A **hair** is an unbranched keratinized filament of mammals, made of α-keratin and produced by a follicle, an invagination of the epidermis into the dermis around a dermal papilla. The shaft consists of an outer cuticle of overlapping scale-like cells, a cortex of keratinized spindle-shaped cells that carries most of the pigment and determines the mechanical properties, and, in thicker hairs, a central medulla of loosely arranged cells and air spaces.

The follicle is accompanied by a sebaceous gland, often an apocrine sweat gland, and a small smooth muscle, the arrector pili, that erects the hair (and produces "goose bumps" in humans). The hair is produced by the matrix cells of the hair bulb, which surrounds the dermal papilla; the papilla controls the size and growth of the hair. A typical mammalian coat (pelage) consists of long, stiff guard hairs and a dense underfur of fine, wavy hairs that trap air; specialized hairs include the vibrissae (whiskers), sinus hairs with a blood sinus and many nerve endings in the follicle, the spines of hedgehogs and porcupines, and the bristles of pigs.

Hair grows in cycles: a growth phase (anagen), a regression phase (catagen) in which the lower follicle degenerates, and a resting phase (telogen), after which a new hair grows in the same follicle and pushes the old one out. In most mammals the follicles cycle in waves, producing seasonal molts; in humans each follicle cycles independently, and the anagen of scalp hair lasts several years. The insulating value of fur increases with its depth, from about 2 cm in small mammals to 6–8 cm in arctic foxes, wolves and reindeer, and has an upper limit set by the need to move (Scholander et al. 1950, [7.14.31](/7.14.31)).

### 57.5.2. Origin of Hair

Hair is a synapsid novelty, but when it arose is uncertain because hair fossilizes rarely. The earliest direct evidence comes from Mesozoic mammaliaforms and mammals with preserved pelts: *Castorocauda* from the Middle Jurassic of Inner Mongolia (about 165 million years ago), with guard hairs and underfur, and *Spinolestes* from the Early Cretaceous of Spain, with hair, spines and even preserved follicles. Coprolites from the Late Permian of Russia contain hair-like structures that may be from therapsids. Indirect evidence comes from the infraorbital foramen and the turbinals, which have been used to infer vibrissae and endothermy in cynodonts ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)).

Two main hypotheses have been proposed for the origin of hair. One derives hairs from sensory bristles located at the hinge between scales, which multiplied when an insulating coat became useful (Maderson 1972); the other derives them from structures associated with glands, which secreted lipids onto the skin. Hairs and scales coexist on the tails of rats and pangolins, where hairs emerge in groups behind each scale, a pattern consistent with the first hypothesis. Developmental genetics shows that hairs, feathers and reptile scales all form from placodes with a common signalling network (Shh, BMP, Wnt, Eda/Edar), and anatomical placodes have been found in the development of crocodylian and lizard scales (Di-Poï and Milinkovitch 2016). The placode, at least, is therefore homologous across amniotes, while hair, feather and scale as such are separate amniote derivatives of it.

## 57.6. The Dermal Skeleton of the Skin

### 57.6.1. From Armour to Scales

The oldest vertebrate skeleton is the dermal armour of the ostracoderms of the Ordovician and Silurian ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.4)). In heterostracans it consisted of large plates over the head and trunk and small scales over the tail; each plate was built of four layers: a basal layer of lamellar bone (or acellular aspidin), a middle spongy layer with vascular spaces, a layer of dentine tubercles, and a thin superficial cap of enameloid. The tubercles were **odontodes** fused to the plate. Placoderms retained a heavy armour of bone, but in most later fishes the plates broke up into smaller scales and became thinner.

The scales of bony fishes are classified by their tissues. The classical sequence, which is also the order of the Chinese textbooks, runs from the cosmoid scale of early sarcopterygians through the ganoid scales of early actinopterygians to the thin scales of teleosts. It describes the loss of tissue layers in several lineages, not a single line of descent.

**Definition 57.6.1 (Bony Scales of Fishes)** The scales of osteichthyans are named by their structure:

- A **cosmoid scale** consists of a basal plate of lamellar bone (isopedine), a spongy vascular bone layer, and a superficial layer of cosmine, dentine traversed by a pore-canal system that opens to the surface, covered by a thin layer of enamel. Cosmoid scales are found in early sarcopterygians (osteolepiforms, porolepiforms, early lungfishes) and grow only at their margins.
- A **ganoid scale** consists of bone covered by many layers of ganoine, a shiny enamel deposited in successive layers as the scale grows. In the palaeoniscoid type (bichirs and early actinopterygians) a layer of dentine lies between the bone and the ganoine; in the lepisosteoid type (gars) the dentine is lost and the ganoine rests directly on the bone. Ganoid scales are usually rhombic and articulate with each other by a peg-and-socket joint.
- A **cycloid scale** is a thin, flexible elasmoid scale with a smooth posterior margin, made of a superficial layer of poorly mineralized bone-like tissue over a plywood of collagen fibres (elasmodine), and marked by concentric growth ridges (circuli).
- A **ctenoid scale** is an elasmoid scale like the cycloid scale whose exposed posterior margin bears small spines, the ctenii.

Cycloid and ctenoid scales lack enamel, dentine and bone cells, and overlap like roof tiles: each is embedded at its anterior end in a dermal pocket and covered by a thin epidermis, with only the posterior part exposed. Because they grow at their margins throughout life and growth slows in winter, their circuli are crowded into annual rings (annuli), which fisheries biologists count to determine the age of a fish. The ctenoid scale with separately formed ctenii (true ctenoid) characterizes acanthomorph teleosts (perch-like fishes); in some flatfishes the upper side bears ctenoid and the lower side cycloid scales.

| Scale | Layers from surface to base | Growth | Occurrence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Placoid scale** | Enameloid, dentine around a pulp cavity, basal plate of bone of attachment | Fixed size; replaced | Chondrichthyans |
| Cosmoid | Enamel, cosmine (dentine with pore canals), spongy bone, lamellar bone | Margins | Early sarcopterygians; reduced in later lungfishes and coelacanths |
| Ganoid, palaeoniscoid | Multilayered ganoine, dentine, vascular bone, lamellar bone | Margins and surface | Early actinopterygians, bichirs |
| Ganoid, lepisosteoid | Multilayered ganoine, lamellar bone | Margins and surface | Gars |
| Elasmoid: cycloid and ctenoid | Thin limiting layer, elasmodine (plywood of collagen) | Margins | Teleosts (cycloid in Cypriniformes, salmonids, clupeids; ctenoid in most acanthomorphs); bowfin (amioid scales) and living lungfishes and coelacanths have elasmoid scales derived independently |

The thin scales of the living coelacanth *Latimeria* and of lungfishes are reduced cosmoid scales without cosmine. The Chinese textbooks list *Latimeria* together with the bichirs as having palaeoniscoid ganoid scales; its scales lack ganoine and are classified today as elasmoid scales of sarcopterygian origin. The trend from heavy to light scales is often explained by selection for flexibility and speed: rhombic ganoid scales form a stiff jointed armour, while overlapping elasmoid scales allow the body to bend freely, and the evolution of thin scales coincided with the radiation of fast-swimming teleosts.

### 57.6.2. Why Scales Became Thin: a Simple Mechanical Model

The replacement of heavy ganoid armour by thin overlapping scales can be made quantitative. A layer of scales of thickness $t$ and density $\rho_{\mathrm{s}}$ covering the body adds a mass per unit area $\rho_{\mathrm{s}} t$; for a fish of length $L$ the body mass grows as $L^{3}$ and the surface as $L^{2}$, so the relative mass of the armour is proportional to $t/L$: at a given thickness, armour is relatively cheaper for a large fish, and halving the thickness of the scales halves its relative mass. Thick, highly mineralized ganoid scales are therefore a heavy investment for small fishes, and the thin, partly mineralized elasmoid scales of teleosts weigh only a fraction as much per unit area. The stiffness of a scaled body in bending is more important than its mass: rhombic ganoid scales joined by peg-and-socket articulations and ligaments form a jointed armour whose bending stiffness is set by the scale joints, and gars and bichirs swim with stiff bodies by undulating only the posterior part. Overlapping elasmoid scales slide over one another and add little bending stiffness, but still resist puncture because a penetrating tooth must pierce several overlapping layers; the plywood of collagen in each scale stops cracks, as shown by mechanical tests on the large scales of the arapaima (Meyers and colleagues). The trend toward thin scales therefore bought flexibility, and with it the efficient body-wave swimming of most teleosts ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)), at little cost in protection.

### 57.6.3. Fin Rays

The fin webs of fishes are supported by dermal rays. Chondrichthyans have *ceratotrichia*, long flexible unsegmented rays of elastoidin, a collagen-like protein (the "shark fin" of the soup trade). Osteichthyans have **lepidotrichia** ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)), paired, jointed rays of bone that are modified scales: each ray consists of two hemitrichia, one on each side of the fin, which the muscles can slide against each other to curve the fin. Teleost fins also contain *actinotrichia*, fine unmineralized rays of elastoidin at the growing edge of the fin, which are thought to be homologous with ceratotrichia. In acanthomorph teleosts the anterior rays of the dorsal and anal fins are unsegmented spines.

### 57.6.4. Osteoderms, Shells and Gastralia

Tetrapods lost most of the dermal scales of their fish ancestors, but bone formed again in the dermis of many lineages. Early tetrapods such as *Ichthyostega* had small bony scales on the belly; temnospondyls had ventral scales and, in some, dermal plates on the back; caecilians still have small dermal scales in pockets within the annular folds of the skin, which are probably homologous with the scales of early tetrapods.

*Osteoderms*, bony plates formed in the dermis under the epidermal scales, evolved independently in many amniotes: crocodylians (on the back, in rows), many lizards (skinks, anguids, cordylids, helodermatids), ankylosaurs and stegosaurs, glyptodonts and armadillos among mammals. They form by metaplastic ossification of the dermis, without a cartilage stage and without odontodes, and serve as armour, as a reserve of calcium for eggshell formation (crocodylians) and, in crocodylians, as a heat exchanger and a brace for the vertebral column.

The turtle shell combines dermal and endoskeletal bone ([7.17.51](/7.17.51)). The carapace is formed by the expanded ribs and neural spines of the trunk vertebrae fused with dermal bone, and edged by dermal peripheral plates; the plastron consists of dermal bones, of which the epiplastra and the entoplastron are probably derived from the clavicles and interclavicle, and the rest from gastralia. The horny scutes on the bony plates do not coincide with the plates, so that the joints of the scutes and of the bones are staggered and the shell is stronger. In soft-shelled turtles (Trionychidae) the scutes are replaced by leathery skin and the peripheral plates are lost.

*Gastralia* are rod-like dermal bones in the ventral body wall, in rows that form a series of chevrons between the pectoral and pelvic girdles. They occur in tuatara, crocodylians and many extinct reptiles including non-avian dinosaurs and pterosaurs, and they are derived from the ventral scales of early tetrapods. In crocodylians they take part in breathing by bracing the abdominal wall during the action of the diaphragmaticus muscle ([7.14.17](/7.14.17)).

### 57.6.5. Teeth and Placoid Scales

Teeth and placoid scales are the derivatives that both layers of the skin produce together. Each is an odontode ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1.3)): it develops at an epidermal placode over a condensation of neural crest mesenchyme, the epidermis forming the enamel organ and the mesenchyme the dental papilla, whose odontoblasts deposit dentine. The **placoid scale** of sharks ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.4.1)) has a spine of dentine capped with enameloid over a pulp cavity, and a basal plate of bone of attachment in the dermis; at the margin of the jaws the scales grade into teeth, which are replaced continuously from a dental lamina on the inner side of the jaw.

The structure of teeth and their comparative anatomy, including the modes of attachment and replacement, belong to the digestive system ([7.17.63](/7.17.63)). For the skin it is enough to note that the dermal skeleton originated as odontodes in the skin, that teeth retain the odontode structure, and that the bone of the skull roof of early vertebrates arose by fusion of odontode bases into plates, which then sank beneath the skin (§58).

## 57.7. Horns and Antlers

Several groups of mammals bear horn-like structures on the head, used in fights between males, in display and in defence. They are built in four different ways and are therefore not homologous with each other except as derivatives of skin and skull.

| Type | Core | Covering | Shed? | Branched? | Sex | Groups |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Horn (bovid horn) | Bony horn core, an outgrowth of the frontal, often pneumatized | Permanent sheath of α-keratin, growing at the base | No | No | Males and often females | Bovidae: cattle, sheep, goats, antelopes |
| Pronghorn horn | Bony core from the frontal | Keratin sheath with a forward prong, shed and regrown every year | Sheath only | The sheath is forked | Males; small in some females | Antilocapridae: the pronghorn *Antilocapra americana* |
| Antler | Bone only, grown each year from a permanent frontal pedicle | Skin with short hairs (velvet) during growth; dead, bare bone when mature | Yes, annually | Usually | Males (both sexes in reindeer; none in musk deer and Chinese water deer) | Cervidae |
| Rhinoceros horn | None; sits on a roughened nasal (and frontal) boss | Compact keratin fibres (tubules) cemented together, growing continuously | No | No | Both sexes | Rhinocerotidae |
| Ossicone | Ossified cartilage formed in the dermis, fused to the skull | Permanent skin and hair | No | No | Both sexes | Giraffidae: giraffe and okapi |

Antlers are the fastest-growing bone known: the antlers of a large red deer or wapiti grow up to 2 cm per day, and the antlers of the moose can weigh 30 kg. The Chinese textbooks classify the antler as a dermal derivative of the skin; it is more accurately an outgrowth of the frontal bone, regenerated each year from stem cells of the periosteum of the pedicle by a modified endochondral ossification, while its velvet is skin. The growth and casting of antlers is under the control of testosterone and day length, and the velvet antlers of deer are a traditional medicine in China ("velvet antler").

## 57.8. Pigment and Colour

### 57.8.1. Pigment Cells

All the pigment cells of the skin derive from the neural crest. In fishes, amphibians and reptiles several kinds of **chromatophores** lie in the dermis (and some in the epidermis); in birds and mammals the only pigment cells of the skin are melanocytes.

| Cell | Pigment or reflecting material | Colour | Occurrence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Melanophore | Melanin (eumelanin) in melanosomes | Black, brown | Fishes, amphibians, reptiles |
| Xanthophore, erythrophore | Pteridines and carotenoids in vesicles | Yellow, orange, red | Fishes, amphibians, reptiles |
| Iridophore | Stacks of guanine crystals | Reflecting, iridescent, white or blue by interference and scattering | Fishes, amphibians, reptiles; the silvery sides of fishes |
| Leucophore | Diffusely scattering crystals | White | Some fishes |
| Melanocyte | Eumelanin and phaeomelanin, transferred as melanosomes to keratinocytes, feather cells or hair cells | Black, brown, reddish, yellowish | Birds and mammals (also present in other vertebrates) |

In the skin of frogs and many lizards the chromatophores are stacked into a *dermal chromatophore unit*: xanthophores on top, iridophores in the middle and melanophores at the bottom, with processes reaching upward. Blue light scattered by the iridophores passes through the yellow filter of the xanthophores and appears green; when the melanophores disperse their melanosomes upward into the processes, the skin darkens. The rapid colour changes of chameleons are produced largely by changes in the spacing of guanine nanocrystals in two layers of iridophores, which shift the wavelength of reflected light (Teyssier et al. 2015).

Physiological colour change, the movement of pigment within existing cells, is controlled by hormones (melanocyte-stimulating hormone darkens, melatonin lightens) and, in many teleosts and chameleons, by nerves. Morphological colour change, a change in the number of pigment cells and the amount of pigment, is slower and underlies seasonal and ontogenetic changes. The fast colour changes of cephalopods are produced by a different, muscular mechanism ([7.17.14](/7.17.14)).

### 57.8.2. Colour in Birds and Mammals

Birds and mammals have no dermal chromatophores; their colours come from pigments deposited in feathers and hair and from the microstructure of feathers. Melanins produce black, grey, brown and rufous colours; carotenoids, obtained from food and deposited in the feathers, red, orange and yellow (the pink of flamingos, the yellow of goldfinches); porphyrins some browns and the red turacin of turacos. Structural colours arise from nanostructures of keratin and air in the barbs (non-iridescent blue of jays and bluebirds, by coherent scattering) and from arrays of melanosomes in the barbules (the iridescence of hummingbirds, peacocks and starlings).

Because melanosome shape correlates with melanin type and colour (elongated eumelanosomes in black and grey feathers, spherical phaeomelanosomes in reddish ones), melanosomes preserved in fossil feathers have been used to reconstruct the colours of dinosaurs: the alternating rufous and white rings on the tail of *Sinosauropteryx*, the black, white and rufous pattern of *Anchiornis* (Li et al. 2010), the iridescent black of *Microraptor*. Mammals are less colourful because they lack structural colours and carotenoid deposition; their colours are mostly combinations of eumelanin and phaeomelanin, controlled by the melanocortin 1 receptor and its antagonist agouti.

### 57.9.2. Trends and Their Explanation

The table shows two trends that run in opposite directions. The dermal skeleton is extensive in the earliest vertebrates and is reduced in most later lineages, especially in tetrapods, in which it survives mainly in the skull and shoulder girdle and reappears as osteoderms in some armoured groups. The epidermis, on the contrary, becomes more elaborate: from a living mucous epithelium in fishes to a cornified, waterproof layer in amniotes that produces hard appendages.

The functional explanation of both trends is the change from water to land. In water the skin is supported, is not in danger of drying out, and can serve for gas exchange and chemical defence; heavy bony armour protects against predators and is not costly to carry. On land the main danger is desiccation, and the solution is a dead, lipid-sealed outer layer, which also protects against abrasion; armour is heavy, and protection is achieved by speed, size or, in a few lineages, by keratin and bony plates. Amphibians are intermediate: their skin is thin and moist, with many glands, and serves for respiration and water uptake, at the price of high evaporative water loss.

The epidermal appendages of amniotes, scales, feathers and hair, are three solutions to the same problems of protection and, in endotherms, of insulation. They share their developmental origin in skin placodes, but the structures themselves evolved separately in sauropsids and synapsids. Their evolution is a clear example of the distinction between deep homology of a developmental module and the independent evolution of the structures it produces ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2.4)).

### 57.9.4. The Skin of the Representative Animals

The representative animals of the Chinese textbooks summarize the classes.

- *Lamprey.* A scaleless skin with a stratified, non-keratinized epidermis rich in mucous cells and granular club cells; a dermis of regular collagen plies; keratinized horny teeth on the oral disc and the piston. Lampreys lack the slime glands of hagfishes.
- *Dogfish shark* (*Squalus*). The skin is covered by placoid scales, each with a spine directed backward, which make the skin rough to a hand moved forward; at the edges of the jaws they grade into teeth. The dermis is thick and tough, with chromatophores; the fins are supported by ceratotrichia.
- *Carp* (*Cyprinus carpio*). Large cycloid scales arranged in rows; the lateral-line scales are pierced by the lateral-line canal, and their number (the lateral-line scale count) is a standard character in identification. The thin epidermis over the scales contains mucous and club cells; breeding males develop keratinized nuptial tubercles on the head. Domestic "mirror carp" have only a few large scales because of mutations in the gene *fgfr1a1*, which illustrates the genetic control of scale development.
- *Frog* (*Pelophylax nigromaculatus*, *Rana*). A thin skin without scales, loosely attached to the body by large subcutaneous lymph sacs; mucous and granular glands, concentrated in the dorsolateral folds; dermal chromatophore units responsible for green colour and its changes; a thin cornified layer shed periodically and usually eaten.
- *Lizard.* Overlapping horny scales of corneous β-protein, larger on the head, small and granular on the flanks in many species; femoral pores along the underside of the thighs in many males; sheets of shed skin; in some groups osteoderms under the scales.
- *Pigeon* (*Columba livia*). A thin, loose, dry skin with feathers in tracts and bare apteria; scutate and reticulate scales on the feet; a horny beak with a soft cere at its base; the uropygial gland at the base of the tail is small in pigeons, which also produce powder down; crop-milk is formed in the crop, not in skin glands.
- *Rabbit* (*Oryctolagus cuniculus*). A thick skin with dense fur of guard hairs and underfur, molted seasonally; sebaceous glands with every follicle, few sweat glands (on the lips), inguinal scent glands, and four or five pairs of nipples along the milk lines; long vibrissae on the snout.

## Summary

- The skin consists of an ectodermal, stratified epidermis and a dermis of mesodermal and neural crest origin; the hypodermis attaches it to the body. In land vertebrates the epidermis cornifies, and the lipids of the stratum corneum make amniote skin waterproof.
- All skin glands derive from the epidermis: unicellular mucous and club cells in fishes; multicellular mucous and granular glands in amphibians; few glands in reptiles; the uropygial gland in birds; sebaceous, eccrine and apocrine sweat, scent and mammary glands in mammals (Definition 57.2.1).
- Keratinized structures are built of α-keratins (all vertebrates) and corneous β-proteins (sauropsids only): horny teeth of cyclostomes, beaks, baleen, reptile scales, claws, nails and hooves.
- Feathers (Definition 57.4.1) grow from follicles in which barb ridges form a branched structure; Prum's five stages of feather evolution are supported by the feathered dinosaurs of China. Hair (Definition 57.5.1) is a synapsid novelty documented since the Middle Jurassic; scales, feathers and hairs share a placode module but are separate structures.
- The dermal skeleton began as ostracoderm armour of odontodes on bone. The scales of bony fishes (Definition 57.6.1) are cosmoid, ganoid (palaeoniscoid and lepisosteoid) and elasmoid (cycloid and ctenoid); **placoid scales** and teeth derive from both layers. Fin rays are ceratotrichia in chondrichthyans and **lepidotrichia** in osteichthyans. Tetrapods reduced the dermal skeleton but re-evolved osteoderms, the turtle shell and gastralia.
- Mammalian head weapons are built in four ways: bovid horn (bony core, permanent sheath), pronghorn (shed sheath), antler (shed bone from a pedicle), rhinoceros horn (keratin only), with the giraffe ossicone as a fifth type.
- Pigment cells derive from neural crest; ectotherms have several kinds of dermal **chromatophores** arranged in chromatophore units; birds and mammals have melanocytes only and add structural and carotenoid colours (birds). Fossil melanosomes reveal dinosaur colours.
- Two opposite trends run through the vertebrates: reduction of the dermal skeleton and elaboration of the cornified epidermis, both explained by the transition from water to land.

## Questions

1. Why are the scales of a lizard, the feathers of a bird and the hairs of a mouse described as "homologous as placodes but not as structures"?

   **Answer** All three develop from epidermal placodes over dermal condensations that use the same signalling network (Shh, BMP, Wnt, Eda/Edar), and anatomical placodes are found in the development of reptile scales as well as feathers and hairs; the placode is therefore inherited from the common ancestor of amniotes. But the structures differ in their keratins (corneous β-proteins in scales and feathers, α-keratin in hair), in their architecture (a skin fold, a branched follicular appendage, an unbranched follicular filament), and in their phylogenetic distribution: feathers arose within theropod dinosaurs and hair within synapsids, long after the two lineages separated. The ancestral amniote had placodes but neither feathers nor hair, so the structures evolved independently from a shared developmental module, a case of deep homology.

2. Arrange the scales of a bichir, a gar, a carp and a perch by their tissues, and explain why the sequence should not be read as a line of descent.

   **Answer** Bichir: palaeoniscoid ganoid scale (ganoine, dentine, bone). Gar: lepisosteoid ganoid scale (ganoine on bone, no dentine). Carp: cycloid elasmoid scale (thin bony surface layer and collagen plywood). Perch: ctenoid elasmoid scale (as cycloid, with ctenii). The order describes successive losses of tissue layers. But the four fishes are not ancestors and descendants: bichirs, gars and teleosts are separate lineages of living actinopterygians, each with a long independent history; elasmoid scales arose within the teleost lineage and independently in the bowfin and in living lungfishes and coelacanths. The sequence is a morphological series of grades, useful for understanding how scales simplified, but each living form is a derived end point of its own lineage.

3. A paleontologist finds a fossil skull with a pair of short bony projections from the frontal bones. What features would distinguish whether they bore bovid-type horns, antlers or ossicones?

   **Answer** Antlers: a pedicle of the frontal with a burr (coronet) at its top, where the antler was shed, and possibly shed antlers found separately; the projection would end in a rough, flat surface. Bovid horn cores: a continuous outgrowth of the frontal, without a burr, with a grooved and vascularized surface that anchored the keratin sheath, often hollow (pneumatized from the frontal sinus). Ossicones: initially separate ossifications fused to the skull, often at the frontoparietal suture, with a rounded tip and a surface of skin attachment; in giraffids they are present in both sexes. Histology helps: antlers show the structure of rapidly formed bone that died at maturity; horn cores and ossicones show living bone remodeled over many years.

4. Explain why the skin of amphibians is both an advantage and a limitation for life on land.

   **Answer** Advantages: the thin, moist, vascularized skin serves as a respiratory surface (in plethodontid salamanders the only one) and absorbs water, for example through the "seat patch" of toads; its granular glands produce powerful chemical defences, and its mucus has antimicrobial properties. Limitations: the same permeability that permits gas exchange lets water evaporate almost as freely as from an open water surface, so most amphibians are restricted to humid habitats and to activity at night or in rain; the skin cannot be armoured without losing its respiratory function; and the skin is a site of infection, as shown by the chytrid fungus *Batrachochytrium*, which disrupts electrolyte transport across the skin ([7.17.49](/7.17.49)). Some frogs have reduced water loss with lipid secretions or cocoons, but none has a fully waterproof skin.

5. (Open) Feathers evolved before flight. What evidence supports this, and what other roles could feathers have had in the first feathered dinosaurs?

   **Answer** Evidence: filamentous and branched feathers occur in many coelurosaurs that could not fly and were not close to birds (*Sinosauropteryx*, *Yutyrannus*, *Beipiaosaurus*), and pennaceous feathers in non-flying oviraptorosaurs (*Caudipteryx*) whose arms were too short for flight; the phylogenetic distribution places the origin of feathers well before the origin of birds. Possible roles: insulation (filaments and down-like feathers on small active animals with high metabolic rates, and on the large *Yutyrannus* from a cool environment), display and species recognition (colour patterns reconstructed from melanosomes, elongated tail feathers), brooding (oviraptorids preserved on nests with arms spread over the eggs), and later aerodynamic functions such as wing-assisted incline running before powered flight. The roles are not exclusive, and feathers are a classic case of exaptation ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.3.2)).
