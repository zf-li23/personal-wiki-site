The archosaurs ("ruling reptiles") include the largest animals ever to walk on land, the first vertebrates to fly, and the most diverse living tetrapods. Two lineages survive: about 28 species of crocodylians, and the birds, with about 11 000 species. Between them, in the Mesozoic, lived the pterosaurs and the non-avian dinosaurs, which dominated terrestrial ecosystems for 135 million years. That crocodiles are more closely related to birds than to lizards is one of the most counterintuitive and best-established facts of vertebrate phylogeny; it means that features shared by crocodylians and birds — a four-chambered heart, a muscular gizzard, nest building and parental care, unidirectional airflow through the lungs — were probably present in their common ancestor and in dinosaurs.

This chapter follows the archosaur tree. Section 52.1 defines the archosaurs and their two lineages; §52.2 treats the crocodile line, including the living crocodylians; §52.3 the pterosaurs; §52.4 and §52.5 the dinosaurs, their characters, origin and major groups; §52.6 their biology. Section 52.7 follows the bird lineage from feathered dinosaurs to the birds of the Mesozoic, and §52.8 summarizes the dinosaurs and early birds of China, many of them central to the questions of this chapter. The physiological consequences of the crocodylian heart are in [7.14.16](/7.14.16#sec-16.8.3); the macroevolutionary account of the origin of birds is in [7.10.32](/7.10.32#sec-32.1.3), and this chapter adds the anatomical detail; living birds follow in [7.17.53](/7.17.53).

## 52.1. Archosaurs

### 52.1.1. Characters of Archosaurs

**Definition 52.1.1 (Archosauria)** The **Archosauria** (archosaurs) are the crown clade comprising crocodylians and birds, their last common ancestor and all its descendants, including the pterosaurs and non-avian dinosaurs. Archosaurs and their closest fossil relatives (Archosauriformes) have an antorbital fenestra (an opening in the side of the snout in front of the orbit), a mandibular fenestra in the lower jaw, teeth set in sockets (thecodont), a fourth trochanter on the femur for the insertion of the caudofemoralis muscle, and an ossified laterosphenoid in the braincase.

Each character has a functional interpretation, though not all are certain:

- The *antorbital fenestra* housed an air sinus, part of a system of air-filled spaces in the skull connected to the nasal cavity, and lightened the skull. It is secondarily closed in living crocodylians and reduced in birds, where the antorbital sinus persists.
- The *mandibular fenestra* lies between the dentary, surangular and angular and gave space for jaw muscles.
- *Thecodont* teeth, set in sockets, are firmly anchored and replaced from below; archosaurs share this with mammals, independently ([7.17.63](/7.17.63)).
- The *fourth trochanter* marks the insertion of the caudofemoralis longus, a large muscle running from the tail to the femur that retracts the hindlimb, the main propulsive muscle of crocodylians and non-avian dinosaurs. Birds, with their short tails, have reduced it and walk mainly by flexing the knee.

The closest relatives of archosaurs include *Euparkeria*, a small, agile animal about 50 cm long from the Early Triassic of South Africa (about 245 million years ago), long used as a model of the ancestral archosaur, and the large carnivorous proterosuchids and erythrosuchids, which radiated after the end-Permian **mass extinction**. The archosauriforms radiated very rapidly in the Early Triassic, and by 247 million years ago both lines of archosaurs had appeared.

### 52.1.2. Two Lineages and Their Ankles

The archosaurs split into the crocodile line, the *Pseudosuchia* (or Crurotarsi), and the bird line, the *Avemetatarsalia* (or Ornithodira). The two lines are recognized above all by their ankles ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.1.2)):

| | Crocodile line (Pseudosuchia) | Bird line (Avemetatarsalia) |
|---|---|---|
| Ankle joint | "Crocodile-normal": a peg on the astragalus fits into a socket on the calcaneum; the joint passes between them and lets the foot rotate | "Advanced mesotarsal": astragalus and calcaneum tightly joined to the tibia; the joint passes between them and the distal tarsals, a simple hinge |
| Calcaneum | Large, with a heel (calcaneal tuber) for the calf muscles | Small, without a tuber |
| Foot | Metatarsals of moderate length; plantigrade | Metatarsals long and bunched; digitigrade; bipedal ancestrally |
| Posture | Sprawling to fully erect; quadrupedal or bipedal | Fully erect, with the femoral head turned in |
| Living members | Crocodylians | Birds |

The hinge-like ankle of the bird line is suited to fast running on erect limbs moving in a parasagittal plane, and the elongated foot of birds (the tarsometatarsus, [7.17.53](/7.17.53)) evolved from it.

## 52.2. The Crocodile Line

### 52.2.1. Triassic Pseudosuchians

In the Triassic the crocodile line, not the bird line, dominated the land. Pseudosuchians included most of the large predators and many herbivores:

- *Aetosaurs*: quadrupedal herbivores and omnivores up to 5 m long, covered with rows of bony plates (osteoderms), some with long spikes at the shoulders.
- *Poposauroids and "rauisuchians"*: large predators with deep skulls and erect limbs, such as *Postosuchus* (4–6 m); some poposauroids, such as *Effigia* and *Shuvosaurus*, were toothless, beaked bipeds that looked remarkably like ornithomimid dinosaurs — a case of **convergent evolution** between the two archosaur lines 80 million years apart.
- *Phytosaurs*: long-snouted, crocodile-like semi-aquatic predators with the nostrils near the eyes rather than at the tip of the snout, an example of convergence with crocodylians; they may lie just outside the crown Archosauria.

Most of these groups disappeared in the end-Triassic extinction, 201 million years ago, which also opened the way for the dinosaurs.

### 52.2.2. Crocodylomorphs

The only pseudosuchians to survive the end-Triassic extinction were the crocodylomorphs. Early crocodylomorphs (sphenosuchians such as *Hesperosuchus*) were small, slender, long-legged, fully terrestrial animals, very different from modern crocodiles. Through the Jurassic and Cretaceous the crocodylomorphs radiated into a remarkable range of forms:

- *Thalattosuchians*: marine crocodylomorphs; the teleosaurids looked like gharials, and the metriorhynchids had flippers, a tail fin and probably gave birth at sea.
- *Notosuchians*: terrestrial crocodylomorphs of the Cretaceous of Gondwana, some with mammal-like teeth (incisors, canines and cheek teeth in *Pakasuchus* and *Notosuchus*), some herbivorous (*Simosuchus*, with a short, pug-like head), some armoured like armadillos (*Armadillosuchus*). The sebecids survived to the Miocene in South America.
- *Neosuchians*: semi-aquatic forms including the giant *Sarcosuchus* (Cretaceous, Africa, 9–12 m) and *Deinosuchus* (Cretaceous, North America, up to 10 m), and the crown group Crocodylia, which appeared about 95 million years ago.

The diversity of Mesozoic crocodylomorphs shows that the "living fossil" image of crocodiles is misleading: the semi-aquatic ambush predator is only one of many ways of life the lineage explored, and the one that survived.

### 52.2.3. The Living Crocodylians

*Crocodylia* has about 28 living species in three families:

| Family | Species | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alligatoridae (alligators and caimans) | 8 | Broad snout; the fourth lower tooth fits into a pit in the upper jaw and is hidden when the mouth is closed; no lingual salt glands; mostly fresh water | American alligator, Chinese alligator, caimans |
| Crocodylidae (crocodiles) | ~18 | Narrower snout; the fourth lower tooth fits into a notch and is visible; lingual salt glands; many enter salt water | Nile crocodile, saltwater crocodile (*Crocodylus porosus*, the largest living reptile, over 6 m and 1 000 kg) |
| Gavialidae (gharials) | 2 | Very long, narrow snout; fish eaters | Indian gharial (*Gavialis*), false gharial (*Tomistoma*, placed here by molecular data) |

All crocodylians are semi-aquatic ambush predators, and their anatomy is adapted to that life:

- *Head*. The nostrils, eyes and ears lie on the top of the head, so the animal can breathe, see and hear with only these exposed. A bony **secondary palate** separates the nasal passage from the mouth, and a fold at the back of the tongue (the gular or palatal valve) closes the throat, so a crocodile can open its mouth underwater, holding prey, without flooding the airway. The secondary palate of crocodylians evolved independently of that of mammals ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)).
- *Skin*. Rows of bony osteoderms beneath the dorsal scales; small dome-shaped integumentary sensory organs on the jaws (and, in crocodiles, over the body) that detect pressure waves and vibrations in water and help to locate prey in darkness.
- *Heart and circulation*. The ventricle is completely divided, as in birds and mammals, but the left aorta arises from the right ventricle beside the pulmonary artery, and the two aortae communicate through the *foramen of Panizza* at their base. When the animal breathes air, pressure in the left ventricle keeps the right ventricle's outflow to the lungs; during a dive, a "cog-tooth" valve in the pulmonary outflow tract closes and blood from the right ventricle bypasses the lungs into the left aorta, which supplies the gut. The function of this shunt (saving energy, aiding digestion of a large meal by carrying acid-forming CO₂ to the stomach, regulating heat) is debated ([7.14.16](/7.14.16#sec-16.8.3), [7.17.65](/7.17.65)).
- *Breathing*. In addition to rib movements, crocodylians ventilate their lungs with a hepatic piston: the diaphragmaticus muscle, running from the pelvis to the liver, pulls the liver backward and enlarges the thoracic cavity. Airflow through the lung is unidirectional, as in birds (Farmer and Sanders 2010; [7.17.64](/7.17.64)).
- *Locomotion*. Crocodylians swim with the tail and use two modes on land: the belly crawl and the high walk, with the limbs held nearly vertically under the body. Small crocodiles (such as the Australian freshwater crocodile *Crocodylus johnstoni*) can gallop. The crocodile-normal ankle allows the foot to rotate in these different gaits.
- *Feeding*. Crocodylians have the strongest bite measured in living animals (about 16 000 N in a large saltwater crocodile; Erickson et al. 2012), but the muscles that open the jaws are weak. They cannot chew; large prey is dismembered by the "death roll", spinning the body while holding the prey. Stones in the gizzard, a muscular stomach as in birds, help grind food.
- *Other features*. Nucleated red blood cells and a haemoglobin that binds bicarbonate, releasing oxygen during long dives; a single penis; a longitudinal cloacal slit; gastralia (abdominal ribs) and uncinate processes on the ribs.

### 52.2.4. Reproduction and Parental Care

Crocodylians lay hard-shelled eggs in nests that the female builds and guards. Alligators and many crocodiles build mounds of vegetation that warm as they rot; others dig holes in sand. Sex is determined by temperature (pattern II, [7.17.51](/7.17.51#sec-51.6.2)): in the American alligator, males are produced in a narrow band near 32–33 °C and females at lower and higher temperatures. When the young are ready to hatch, they call from inside the egg; the mother opens the nest, helps them out, and carries them to the water in her mouth. She guards them for weeks or months, and in some species the young form a crèche. Courtship involves bellowing, head slapping and infrasonic vibrations that make the water above the back "dance".

Parental care, nest building, brooding and vocal communication between parents and young are shared by crocodylians and birds, and fossil evidence (nests, eggs, brooding adults) shows that many dinosaurs had them too (§52.6.3). By phylogenetic bracketing, these behaviours were probably present in the common ancestor of archosaurs.

### 52.2.5. The Chinese Alligator

The Chinese alligator (*Alligator sinensis*), the only crocodylian of China and the only alligator outside the Americas, is a small species (rarely more than 1.5–2 m) of the lower Yangtze, in southern Anhui, Zhejiang and Jiangsu. Its distribution is the most northerly of any crocodylian except the American alligator, and it survives cold winters by hibernating in burrows, which it digs with complex tunnels and chambers, from October or November to April. It feeds mainly on snails, mussels, fish and other small animals. Females lay 10–40 eggs in a mound of vegetation in July; the young hatch in September.

Historically the Chinese alligator lived along the middle and lower Yangtze; it is the "tuo" of ancient Chinese texts. Habitat conversion to rice fields and persecution reduced the wild population to a few hundred individuals scattered in small ponds by the end of the twentieth century, and it is classified as Critically Endangered and a Class I state-protected animal. A captive-breeding programme at the Anhui Research Center for Chinese Alligator Reproduction, begun in 1979, has produced more than ten thousand animals, and reintroductions into restored wetlands since 2003 have begun to rebuild wild populations. In addition, a gharial-like crocodylian, *Hanyusuchus sinensis*, lived in southern China until historical times and is thought to have been exterminated by humans within the last few thousand years (Iijima et al. 2022).

## 52.3. Pterosaurs

### 52.3.1. The Pterosaur Wing

Pterosaurs were the first vertebrates to evolve powered flight, appearing in the Late Triassic (about 215 million years ago, *Eudimorphodon*) and surviving until the end of the Cretaceous. They ranged from species with a wingspan of 25 cm to the giant azhdarchids such as *Quetzalcoatlus northropi*, with a wingspan of 10–11 m and a mass of perhaps 200–250 kg, the largest flying animals ever.

The pterosaur wing was a membrane (patagium) supported by an enormously elongated fourth finger, whose four phalanges formed the leading edge of the outer wing; the first three fingers were short and clawed, and the fifth was lost. The membrane consisted of three parts: the propatagium in front of the arm, supported by a unique bone, the pteroid, at the wrist; the main brachiopatagium from the wing finger to the hindlimb; and the uropatagium between the legs. The brachiopatagium was stiffened by thin fibres (actinofibrils) and contained muscle and blood vessels, as fossils from the Jehol biota and elsewhere show. Because the wing finger is a single strut, the wing could be folded compactly. Its structure differs fundamentally from the wings of birds, supported by feathers on the second and third fingers, and of bats, a membrane stretched between four fingers ([7.17.61](/7.17.61)): the three vertebrate wings are homologous as forelimbs but convergent as wings.

Pterosaurs had other adaptations for flight: **pneumatic bones** with very thin walls invaded by air sacs, as in birds; a large brain with enlarged flocculi (regions of the cerebellum that process information from the wing membranes and eyes); a keeled sternum; and a coat of hair-like filaments, the pycnofibres, which insulated the body. Some pycnofibres from Chinese specimens are branched like the simplest feathers (Yang et al. 2019), raising the possibility that feather-like integument was inherited from a common ancestor of pterosaurs and dinosaurs — a question still open.

### 52.3.2. Diversity and Life

Early pterosaurs ("rhamphorhynchoids", a paraphyletic group) had long tails with a vane at the tip, short metacarpals and short necks. The pterodactyloids, which dominated the Cretaceous, had short tails, long metacarpals and long necks, and many had elaborate head crests used in display. *Darwinopterus* from the Middle Jurassic of Liaoning (described in 2009) combines the skull and neck of a pterodactyloid with the body and tail of a rhamphorhynchoid, showing that the two parts of the body evolved as separate modules (modular evolution). Pterosaur diets included fish (many forms skimmed or plunged), insects, small terrestrial vertebrates (the azhdarchids, stalking on the ground like storks), and filter-feeding (*Pterodaustro*, with a thousand bristle-like teeth).

On the ground pterosaurs walked on all fours, as trackways show, and biomechanical analyses suggest that they launched into the air by vaulting on their powerful forelimbs (Habib 2008), a mechanism that may explain how giant species could take off. Pterosaurs laid soft-shelled eggs. At a site in the Turpan–Hami Basin of Xinjiang, hundreds of eggs and embryos of *Hamipterus tianshanensis* (described in 2014 and 2017) show that pterosaurs nested in colonies, that the eggshell was thin and flexible, and that the hindlimbs of embryos were better developed than the forelimbs, suggesting that hatchlings could walk but not yet fly.

The origin of pterosaurs was obscure until the lagerpetids, small Triassic dinosaur relatives, were identified as their closest relatives from shared features of the braincase, inner ear and limbs (Ezcurra et al. 2020).

### 52.3.3. Flight Performance

Pterosaur flight can be analysed with the same aerodynamics as bird flight ([7.17.53](/7.17.53)). Their wing shape — long, narrow and pointed in most pterodactyloids, with a high aspect ratio — resembles that of soaring seabirds such as albatrosses and frigatebirds, and indicates efficient gliding and soaring over the sea. The low wing loading allowed by the membrane wing and pneumatic skeleton meant that even giant azhdarchids could soar on thermals with little flapping, and estimates of their glide performance suggest they could travel thousands of kilometres. Their small hindlimbs were connected to the wing membrane, and the uropatagium may have helped to control pitch. The membrane, unlike a feathered wing, could not be partly folded or have slots between the primaries, so pterosaurs probably adjusted the wing camber and area by flexing the fingers and limbs and by tensioning the membrane with its muscle fibres.

The long-tailed early pterosaurs probably flew more like bats than like birds, with a broad wing extending to the ankle. The radiation of pterodactyloids in the Cretaceous coincided with that of birds, and the hypothesis that birds drove small pterosaurs to extinction by competition (leaving only large forms in the Late Cretaceous) has been proposed but is debated, because small pterosaurs of the latest Cretaceous may simply have left no fossils.

## 52.4. Dinosaurs: Definition and Origin

### 52.4.1. What Is a Dinosaur

**Definition 52.4.1 (Dinosauria)** The **Dinosauria** (dinosaurs) are the clade comprising the last common ancestor of *Passer domesticus* (the house sparrow) and *Triceratops horridus* and all its descendants. It includes the Ornithischia and the Saurischia, the latter including birds. Dinosaurs share a perforate (open) acetabulum, three or more sacral vertebrae, a long deltopectoral crest on the humerus, an inturned femoral head that fits into the acetabulum, and a fully erect posture with the limbs held beneath the body.

The name was coined by Richard Owen in 1842 ("terrible lizards") for *Megalosaurus*, *Iguanodon* and *Hylaeosaurus*, which he recognized as a group of reptiles with erect limbs and five sacral vertebrae. Because birds are dinosaurs, "dinosaurs" in everyday speech means the non-avian dinosaurs, a **paraphyletic group**; this chapter writes "non-avian dinosaurs" where the distinction matters.

The perforate acetabulum is the most visible character. In other reptiles the hip socket has a bony inner wall; in dinosaurs the wall is open, and the head of the femur, turned inward at a right angle to the shaft, is held by a strong rim (the supra-acetabular crest) above it. The limb is thus held vertically beneath the body, the femur moving in a parasagittal plane, and the body weight is transmitted through the top of the socket rather than its inner wall. The erect posture, also achieved independently by some pseudosuchians and by mammals, allows efficient walking and running and probably helped dinosaurs breathe while running ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.4.2)).

### 52.4.2. The Origin of Dinosaurs

The oldest undisputed dinosaurs are from the Late Triassic (Carnian), about 233–231 million years ago, from the Ischigualasto Formation of Argentina (*Eoraptor*, *Herrerasaurus*, *Eodromaeus*) and the Santa Maria Formation of southern Brazil (*Staurikosaurus*, *Buriolestes*, *Saturnalia*). These earliest dinosaurs were small to medium-sized bipeds, 1–6 m long, and already included early theropods, sauropodomorphs and possibly ornithischians; dinosaurs were rare, forming a small part of faunas dominated by pseudosuchians, rhynchosaurs and synapsids. *Nyasasaurus* from the Middle Triassic of Tanzania (about 243 million years ago) may be the oldest dinosaur or its closest relative.

The closest relatives of dinosaurs (together the Dinosauromorpha) were small, slender, bipedal or facultatively bipedal Triassic animals: *Lagosuchus* (*Marasuchus*) from Argentina and the silesaurids, quadrupedal herbivores or omnivores with a beak-like jaw tip that some analyses place within the Ornithischia. The rise of dinosaurs from rarity to dominance took place in two steps: after the Carnian Pluvial Episode, a period of humid climate about 234–232 million years ago that changed floras, dinosaurs diversified, especially the herbivorous sauropodomorphs; after the end-Triassic extinction (201 million years ago), which removed most pseudosuchians, they became the dominant large terrestrial animals for the rest of the Mesozoic. Dinosaurs were therefore not competitively superior from the start; they owed much of their success to surviving two crises (Brusatte et al. 2008).

## 52.5. The Major Groups of Dinosaurs

### 52.5.1. Saurischians and Ornithischians

Harry Seeley divided dinosaurs in 1888 into two orders by the structure of their pelvis:

**Definition 52.5.1 (Saurischia and Ornithischia)** The **Saurischia** ("lizard-hipped" dinosaurs) are the clade of dinosaurs comprising the Sauropodomorpha and the Theropoda (including birds); ancestrally their pelvis is triradiate, with the pubis pointing forward and down (propubic), as in other reptiles. The **Ornithischia** ("bird-hipped" dinosaurs) are the clade of herbivorous dinosaurs in which the pubis is rotated backward to lie parallel to the ischium (opisthopubic), usually with a forward prepubic process; they also have a predentary bone, an unpaired bone capping the front of the lower jaws, which bore a horny beak.

The names are misleading: birds are saurischians, but their pubis is rotated backward like that of ornithischians, a convergence (the rotation also occurred in therizinosaurs and dromaeosaurs). The backward rotation of the pubis enlarged the space for the gut in herbivorous ornithischians, and in theropods it may be related to breathing with a cuirassal pump of the gastralia.

Ornithischian: the pubis is rotated backward beside the ischium (postpubic rod), with a forward prepubic process. Other ornithischian characters include leaf-shaped cheek teeth set in from the edge of the jaw (perhaps bordered by cheeks), a palpebral bone over the eye, and a lattice of ossified tendons along the vertebral column that stiffened the back and tail.

The classic division has been challenged. Baron, Norman and Barrett (2017) analysed a large data set of early dinosaurs and found the theropods closer to ornithischians than to sauropodomorphs, uniting them as the *Ornithoscelida* (a name coined by Huxley in 1870) and leaving Saurischia for sauropodomorphs and herrerasaurids. Reanalysis of their data by others (Langer et al. 2017) restored the traditional groups, and the difference between the trees is small: the earliest members of the three lineages were similar, and few characters separate them. The question remains open; it matters for the inferred ancestral dinosaur (was it carnivorous or omnivorous, with or without a beak?) but not for the identity of the three lineages.

### 52.5.2. Sauropodomorphs and the Evolution of Gigantism

The sauropodomorphs began as bipedal, omnivorous or herbivorous animals a few metres long (*Saturnalia*, *Plateosaurus*, the Chinese *Lufengosaurus*), traditionally grouped as "prosauropods", a paraphyletic series. They evolved into the sauropods: quadrupedal herbivores with a small head, long neck and long tail, column-like limbs and an elephant-like foot. Sauropods were the largest terrestrial animals in the history of life: the titanosaurs *Argentinosaurus* and *Patagotitan* from the Cretaceous of Argentina reached 30–35 m and perhaps 50–70 t; *Mamenchisaurus sinocanadorum* from the Jurassic of Xinjiang had a neck about 15 m long, the longest of any known animal.

How sauropods became so large has been analysed as a cascade of traits (Sander et al. 2011):

| Trait | How it permitted large size |
|---|---|
| No chewing; food swallowed whole and processed in the gut (with gastroliths in some) | A small, light head on a long neck; feeding rate limited only by cropping |
| Long neck | Large feeding envelope without moving the massive body, saving energy |
| Bird-like air sac system with **pneumatic bones** | Light vertebrae (neck vertebrae up to 60–70 % air), efficient unidirectional ventilation of a long trachea, heat loss |
| Oviparity with many small eggs | Fast population recovery; no limit on body size from gestation, unlike large mammals |
| High basal metabolic rate and fast growth | Bone histology shows continuous, fast growth: adult size in 10–20 years |

The textbook image of sauropods wallowing in swamps to support their weight has been abandoned: trackways show they walked on land, their bodies were full of air sacs and would have floated awkwardly, and the high water pressure on the chest would have prevented breathing.

The size of extinct animals can be estimated from their skeletons. A widely used method relates body mass to the circumference of the limb bones, which carry the weight. For 200 living quadrupedal mammals and reptiles, Campione and Evans (2012) found

$$
\log_{10} M = 2.749\,\log_{10} C - 1.104,
$$
where $M$ is body mass in grams and $C$ the sum of the least circumferences of the humerus and femur in millimetres. The exponent 2.749 is less than 3, so mass rises more slowly than the cube of bone circumference: large animals have relatively thicker bones. Still, the circumference grows only as $M^{0.364}$, and the cross-sectional area as $M^{0.73}$, so the stress in the bones, proportional to $M/C^2$, rises as $M^{0.27}$. Large animals compensate by straightening their limbs, which reduces the bending moments acting on the bones and the muscle force needed to support the body.

![Model: body mass and limb-bone circumference. (A) The relation of Campione and Evans (2012) for living quadrupeds (solid line), compared with geometric similarity (mass proportional to the cube of circumference, dashed); the masses of a dog, a horse and an elephant and the range of large sauropods are marked. (B) The implied axial stress in the limb bones (mass divided by circumference squared) rises as the 0.27 power of mass; a 50 t sauropod loads its bones about seven times more per unit area than a 30 kg dog, unless posture and bone shape compensate.](./plots/limb-bone-scaling.py)

The equation is fitted to quadrupeds; for bipedal dinosaurs the femur alone is used with a corrected equation. Different methods (scaling equations, volumetric reconstructions of the skeleton with a fleshed-out body, for example by computer models) give estimates that differ by tens of per cent for the largest dinosaurs: *Tyrannosaurus* is estimated at 6–9 t, *Giraffatitan* (formerly *Brachiosaurus brancai*) at 25–50 t.

### 52.5.3. Theropods

The theropods ("beast-footed") were bipedal and, except for several groups that became herbivorous, carnivorous. Their characters include hollow, thin-walled bones, a hand with three main fingers (digits I–III), the fourth and fifth reduced or lost, a wishbone (furcula) formed by the fused clavicles, and an intramandibular joint in the lower jaw. Major groups are:

- *Coelophysoids* (Late Triassic–Early Jurassic, *Coelophysis*), slender predators 2–3 m long.
- *Ceratosaurs* (*Ceratosaurus*; abelisaurids of the Cretaceous of Gondwana, with tiny forelimbs, such as *Carnotaurus*).
- *Megalosauroids*, including the spinosaurids (*Spinosaurus*, *Baryonyx*), with crocodile-like snouts for catching fish; *Spinosaurus*, at about 15 m the longest theropod, may have been semi-aquatic, a debated interpretation.
- *Allosauroids*, the dominant large predators of the Late Jurassic and Early Cretaceous (*Allosaurus*, *Giganotosaurus*, and the Chinese *Sinraptor*).
- *Coelurosaurs*, the group that includes birds: tyrannosauroids (from small, crested forms such as *Guanlong* and *Dilong* to *Tyrannosaurus rex*, 12 m and 6–9 t, with a bite force estimated at 35 000–57 000 N), compsognathids, ornithomimosaurs ("ostrich mimics", toothless and fast), therizinosaurs (herbivores with huge claws, such as *Therizinosaurus* and the feathered *Beipiaosaurus*), alvarezsaurs (with a single enlarged finger, probably for breaking into insect nests), oviraptorosaurs (beaked omnivores; *Caudipteryx*, the giant *Gigantoraptor*) and the Paraves: troodontids, dromaeosaurids and birds.

The coelurosaurs had large brains and binocular vision (troodontids had the largest brains relative to body size among non-avian dinosaurs), and many of the skeletal features once considered unique to birds — the furcula, hollow bones, the folding wrist with a half-moon-shaped (semilunate) carpal, a backward-rotated pubis, feathers and brooding behaviour — appeared step by step within them.

### 52.5.4. Ornithischians

Ornithischians were the most diverse herbivores of the Jurassic and Cretaceous:

| Group | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Heterodontosaurids | Small, early bipeds with canine-like tusks; some with filaments on the body | *Heterodontosaurus*, *Tianyulong* (Liaoning) |
| Thyreophora: Stegosauria | Quadrupeds with two rows of plates or spines along the back and tail spikes | *Stegosaurus*; Chinese *Huayangosaurus*, *Tuojiangosaurus* |
| Thyreophora: Ankylosauria | Heavily armoured with osteoderms; some with tail clubs | *Ankylosaurus*, *Euoplocephalus* |
| Ornithopoda | Bipeds or facultative quadrupeds with complex chewing; hadrosaurs ("duck-billed dinosaurs") with dental batteries of hundreds of stacked teeth and hollow crests used as resonating chambers | *Iguanodon* (thumb spike), *Maiasaura*, *Parasaurolophus*; Chinese *Shantungosaurus*, *Tsintaosaurus* |
| Marginocephalia: Pachycephalosauria | Thick-domed skulls | *Pachycephalosaurus* |
| Marginocephalia: Ceratopsia | A rostral bone forming a parrot-like beak, a frill over the neck, and in later forms horns | *Yinlong* (Xinjiang), *Psittacosaurus*, *Protoceratops*, *Triceratops* |

Hadrosaurs and ceratopsians chewed with jaws that moved so that the cheek teeth ground across one another, an ability as complex as that of mammals, and their dental batteries continually replaced worn teeth. The function of the plates of stegosaurs (display, temperature regulation, defence) and of the frills and horns of ceratopsians (species recognition, sexual display, defence) is debated; their variability among species and growth stages suggests that display and **sexual selection** played a major role.

## 52.6. Dinosaur Biology

### 52.6.1. Growth

Dinosaur bones record their growth. Cross sections of long bones show fibrolamellar bone, a richly vascularized tissue that forms during rapid growth, interrupted by lines of arrested growth (LAGs) formed each year when growth slowed. Counting the LAGs gives age, and growth curves can be constructed from a series of specimens of different sizes (Erickson et al. 2004). *Tyrannosaurus* grew most rapidly between 14 and 18 years of age, gaining up to about 2 kg per day, and reached adult size at about 20 years; it rarely lived beyond 30. Sauropods grew faster in absolute terms, perhaps several tonnes per year. Such growth rates are higher than those of living reptiles and approach those of large mammals and birds, implying high metabolic rates.

Whether dinosaurs were endotherms like birds has been debated since the 1970s. The evidence (growth rates, fibrolamellar bone, insulating filaments in many groups, erect posture and high activity, an air-sac system, predator–prey ratios, the presence of dinosaurs in polar regions with dark winters) points to elevated metabolic rates in most dinosaurs. Body temperatures estimated from clumped isotopes of eggshells range from about 31 °C in oviraptorids to 36–38 °C in sauropods, and a comparison of growth rates suggested that dinosaurs may have been intermediate between ectotherms and endotherms, "mesotherms" (Grady et al. 2014). The question is not whether dinosaurs were like lizards or like birds, but where on the continuum each group lay ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.5.2)).

### 52.6.2. Eggs, Nests and Parental Care

Dinosaur eggs and nests are known from all continents; China has some of the richest sites, in Xixia (Henan), Heyuan (Guangdong), Ganzhou (Jiangxi) and Laiyang (Shandong). Eggs range from 7 cm to more than 50 cm long (in *Macroelongatoolithus*, attributed to giant oviraptorosaurs). The earliest dinosaurs probably laid soft-shelled eggs ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.2.2)); hard calcareous shells evolved independently in several lineages, and coloured eggs with protoporphyrin and biliverdin pigments, as in birds, evolved in eumaniraptorans (Wiemann et al. 2018).

Nests show varied parental behaviour. The hadrosaur *Maiasaura* ("good mother lizard") nested in colonies, and its hatchlings, with poorly ossified limbs, probably stayed in the nest and were fed. Oviraptorid skeletons have been found sitting on their nests with the forelimbs spread over the eggs, in the brooding posture of birds; the eggs were arranged in a ring with a hollow centre where the adult sat, and in some the brooding adult was probably the male, as in many living ground-nesting birds. "Baby Yingliang", an oviraptorid embryo found in Ganzhou (described in 2021), lies curled in the egg in a tucking posture like that of a bird embryo before hatching, suggesting that bird-like prehatching behaviour arose in non-avian theropods.

### 52.6.3. Feeding, Behaviour and Social Life

Trackways show that many dinosaurs walked in groups, and bone beds with hundreds of individuals of one species (*Psittacosaurus*, *Centrosaurus*, *Coelophysis*) suggest herds or aggregations. The troodontid *Mei long* from Liaoning, described in 2004, was preserved in the sleeping posture of birds, with the head tucked under the forelimb. Some dinosaurs swallowed stones (gastroliths), as ostriches do. Stomach contents and coprolites show diets: a juvenile *Psittacosaurus* inside the stomach of the mammal *Repenomamus* ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)), a bird in the stomach of *Microraptor*, a lizard in *Sinosauropteryx*.

### 52.6.4. Speeds from Trackways

Dinosaur footprints are far more abundant than skeletons and record behaviour directly: posture, gait, group movement and speed. China has many tracksites, among them Liujiaxia in Yongjing (Gansu), with sauropod, theropod and ornithopod tracks on Early Cretaceous lake shores, and Zhucheng in Shandong.

Robert McNeill Alexander (1976) estimated the speed of extinct animals from their trackways by using the principle of dynamic similarity: animals of different sizes moving in a geometrically similar way move at the same Froude number $v^2/(gh)$, where $v$ is speed, $g$ the acceleration of gravity and $h$ the height of the hip ([7.17.61](/7.17.61)). From measurements of living mammals and birds, the relative stride length $\lambda/h$ ($\lambda$ = stride, the distance between successive prints of the same foot) is a function of the Froude number, which gives

$$
v \approx 0.25\,g^{0.5}\,\lambda^{1.67}\,h^{-1.17}.
$$
The hip height is estimated from the length of the footprint $\ell$, about $h \approx 4\ell$ for many bipedal dinosaurs. Animals walk when $\lambda/h < 2$, trot between 2 and 2.9, and run above 2.9.

**Example 52.6.1** A theropod trackway has footprints 30 cm long and a stride of 2.0 m. Then $h \approx 1.2\,\mathrm{m}$, $\lambda/h \approx 1.7$, a walk, and $v \approx 0.25 \times 3.13 \times 2.0^{1.67} \times 1.2^{-1.17} \approx 0.25 \times 3.13 \times 3.18 \times 0.81 \approx 2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, about 7 km/h. A trackway of the same animal with a stride of 5.0 m ($\lambda/h \approx 4.2$, running) gives $v \approx 0.25 \times 3.13 \times 14.7 \times 0.81 \approx 9.3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, about 34 km/h.

:::

Most dinosaur trackways record walking speeds of 1–3 m s⁻¹, as expected because animals spend most of their time walking; a few theropod trackways from Texas and Spain indicate running at 8–12 m s⁻¹. The method assumes that dinosaurs moved like living animals and that hip height can be estimated from footprints, assumptions that introduce errors of perhaps 20–30 %. Biomechanical models of large dinosaurs give an upper bound: an adult *Tyrannosaurus* had too little leg muscle to run fast, and could probably move no faster than about 5–8 m s⁻¹ (Hutchinson and Garcia 2002; Sellers et al. 2017), while smaller theropods and ornithomimids were much faster.

### 52.6.5. Brains and Senses

The brains of dinosaurs are known from endocasts, casts of the braincase cavity made from fossils or, today, reconstructed from computed tomography. In crocodylians and most non-avian dinosaurs the brain fills only part of the braincase, so endocasts give an upper bound on brain size; in coelurosaurs and birds the brain fills it closely. Relative brain size, measured by the encephalization quotient (the ratio of actual brain mass to the mass expected for a reptile of that body size; [7.14.46](/7.14.46)), was low in sauropods and stegosaurs, whose brains weighed a few hundred grams in bodies of many tonnes, higher in ornithopods and large theropods, and highest in small coelurosaurs such as troodontids and dromaeosaurs, which approached early birds. The enlargement of the forebrain and cerebellum that characterizes birds began within maniraptoran theropods before flight evolved (Balanoff et al. 2013), another component of the mosaic.

Endocasts and skull structures also reveal senses. The olfactory bulbs of tyrannosaurids were very large relative to the brain, suggesting an acute sense of smell. The inner ear gives information on hearing: the length of the cochlear duct correlates with the range of frequencies heard, and most dinosaurs probably heard low frequencies best, as crocodylians and large birds do. The size of the orbit and of the ring of bony plates in the sclera (the scleral ring) shows the size of the pupil and lens: comparisons with living birds and reptiles suggest that some small theropods and many early ornithischians were active at night or around the clock (Schmitz and Motani 2011), and the alvarezsaur *Shuvuuia* combined very large eyes with an enlarged, owl-like inner ear for hearing faint sounds, evidence of nocturnal hunting (Choiniere et al. 2021). Such inferences rest on correlations measured in living archosaurs, and phylogenetic bracketing with crocodylians and birds remains the basis for reconstructing the soft anatomy and behaviour of dinosaurs.

## 52.7. The Origin of Birds

### 52.7.1. Birds as Dinosaurs

That birds are living theropod dinosaurs is supported by hundreds of skeletal characters and by fossils that bridge the gap step by step ([7.10.32](/7.10.32#sec-32.1.3)). Thomas Henry Huxley proposed the relationship in 1868; it was set aside for half a century after Heilmann's *The Origin of Birds* (1926) because theropods seemed to lack clavicles; John Ostrom revived it in the 1970s after his description of *Deinonychus*; and Jacques Gauthier's cladistic analysis (1986) established it. The decisive evidence since 1996 has come from feathered dinosaurs, mostly from China.

The flight apparatus of living birds was assembled piecemeal, an example of **mosaic evolution**, and many of its parts evolved for other purposes before flight, an example of **exaptation**:

| Character | First appeared in |
|---|---|
| Hollow bones, furcula, three-fingered hand | Theropoda |
| Air-sac system with pneumatic vertebrae | Saurischia (sauropods and theropods) |
| Filamentous feathers | Coelurosauria (perhaps earlier) |
| Pennaceous (vaned) feathers, brooding on nests | Pennaraptora (oviraptorosaurs and paravians) |
| Semilunate carpal allowing the hand to fold sideways | Maniraptora |
| Long forelimbs with wings of pennaceous feathers, reduced body size | Paraves |
| Asymmetrical flight feathers | Paraves (*Microraptor*) and Avialae |
| Short tail ending in a pygostyle | Pygostylia (*Confuciusornis* and later birds) |
| Keeled sternum, strut-like coracoid, alula | Ornithothoraces (enantiornithines and ornithuromorphs) |
| Toothless beak in crown birds | Neornithes (beaks evolved several times) |

### 52.7.2. The Origin of Feathers

Feathers are the most complex integumentary structures of vertebrates. A feather grows from a follicle, a cylindrical invagination of the epidermis in which a collar of cells produces a tube of keratin. Within the collar, longitudinal ridges (barb ridges) form the barbs; in pennaceous feathers the ridges grow helically around the tube and fuse on one side to form the rachis; cells in each barb ridge form the barbules, which in the vane of a contour feather interlock by hooklets ([7.17.53](/7.17.53)).

Richard Prum (1999) proposed that feathers evolved in a series of stages, each corresponding to one developmental novelty of the follicle, and each necessary for the next. The fossils of feathered dinosaurs found since then match the model closely: simple filaments (stage I) in *Sinosauropteryx* and *Beipiaosaurus*, tufts of barbs (stage II) in *Sinornithosaurus* and *Dilong*, pennaceous feathers with closed vanes (stage IV) in *Caudipteryx* and *Anchiornis*, and asymmetrical flight feathers (stage V) in *Microraptor* and *Archaeopteryx*. The model also explains why feathers are not modified scales: the follicle, with its collar, is a new structure, although feathers, hair and scales develop from homologous **placodes** ([7.5.22](/7.5.22), [7.17.57](/7.17.57)).

![The developmental model of feather evolution (Prum 1999, schematic). Stage I, a hollow filament; II, a tuft of barbs; IIIa, barbs fused to a rachis; IIIb, barbs with barbules; IIIa+b, an open pennaceous vane; IV, a closed vane in which barbules interlock; V, asymmetrical vanes of flight feathers and other specializations. Each stage depends on the follicle novelty of the previous one. Below: examples of fossil taxa with feathers of each stage.](./plots/feather-stages.py)

Where feathers began is debated. Filamentous structures occur in ornithischians (*Tianyulong* in China, *Kulindadromeus* in Siberia, bristles on the tail of *Psittacosaurus*) and in pterosaurs, some of them branched (§52.3.1). If these are homologous with feathers, feather-like integument evolved in the common ancestor of pterosaurs and dinosaurs (Ornithodira) and was lost in many large forms, which have scaly skin; if not, filaments evolved several times. Either way, feathers evolved long before flight, first for insulation and display: the colours of fossil feathers, reconstructed from the shape and arrangement of melanosomes, show banded tails (*Sinosauropteryx*), black-and-white wings with a red crest (*Anchiornis*) and iridescence (*Microraptor*), consistent with display.

### 52.7.3. *Archaeopteryx*

*Archaeopteryx*, from the Late Jurassic lithographic limestones of Solnhofen in Bavaria (about 150 million years ago), was found in 1861, two years after the publication of *On the Origin of Species*; about a dozen skeletons are now known. It was a crow-sized animal with a mixture of characters:

| Bird characters | Non-avian dinosaur characters |
|---|---|
| Asymmetrical flight feathers forming wings | Teeth in the jaws |
| Furcula | A long bony tail of 21–23 vertebrae |
| Backward-pointing first toe (hallux), perhaps | Three separate, clawed fingers |
| Feathered tail | No ossified sternum, no keel |
| | Unfused metatarsals, gastralia, a hyperextensible second toe as in deinonychosaurs |

The feathered paravians found in China, some older than *Archaeopteryx* (*Anchiornis*, about 160 million years ago), have shown that *Archaeopteryx* is one of many small feathered animals near the base of the bird lineage, and its position just inside or just outside the Avialae (birds in the broad sense) is debated. It remains important because of its age and because it shows that flight feathers and wings had evolved by the Late Jurassic.

### 52.7.4. The Origin of Flight

How flight began has been argued in terms of two classic hypotheses:

- The *arboreal* ("trees-down") hypothesis: ancestors of birds climbed trees, leapt and glided between branches, and gliding was gradually converted into flapping flight. Gravity provides free energy, and gliding is an intermediate stage seen in many living animals.
- The *cursorial* ("ground-up") hypothesis: ancestors of birds were fast-running bipeds that used their forelimbs while leaping after prey or running, and flapping generated thrust and lift. It agrees with the terrestrial, bipedal theropod ancestry of birds.

Two discoveries changed the debate. *Microraptor gui* (described by Xu Xing and colleagues in 2003) was a small dromaeosaur with long, asymmetrical flight feathers on both its forelimbs and hindlimbs, a "four-winged" animal that probably glided or flew between trees, which supports an arboreal phase. Kenneth Dial (2003) observed that chicks of the chukar partridge, unable to fly, flap their rudimentary wings while running up steep slopes and tree trunks; the wings press the feet against the surface, and the chicks can climb slopes of more than 90° (wing-assisted incline running, WAIR). This shows that partial wings can be useful at every stage and that the same flapping motion serves both climbing and, later, flight; it reconciles the two hypotheses.

The scansoriopterygids *Yi qi* (Hebei, 2015) and *Ambopteryx* (Liaoning, 2019) of the Yanliao biota were small paravian relatives with a bat-like membrane wing supported by a long rod of bone on the wrist; they show that the theropods leading to birds experimented with more than one kind of wing, and that membranous gliding wings evolved and died out independently of the feathered wing. Analyses of the wing loading of paravians suggest that flapping flight may have evolved more than once among them (Pei et al. 2020).

### 52.7.5. Mesozoic Birds

The Early Cretaceous Jehol biota of Liaoning, Hebei and Inner Mongolia (about 131–120 million years ago) has produced more than 50 species of birds, documenting the radiation of birds after *Archaeopteryx*:

- *Jeholornis* still had a long bony tail; seeds preserved in its stomach show a plant-eating diet.
- *Sapeornis* had long wings and a short tail with a pygostyle.
- *Confuciusornis sanctus*, described in 1995 and known from thousands of specimens, was the first bird known with a toothless beak and had a pygostyle; some individuals have two long ribbon-like tail feathers, probably males, and others medullary bone, the calcium reserve formed by egg-laying females.
- The *Enantiornithes* ("opposite birds"), named for the reversed articulation between the scapula and coracoid, were the dominant land birds of the Cretaceous worldwide, with teeth, clawed fingers and a keeled sternum; their hatchlings were well developed and probably could fly soon after hatching. Chinese examples include *Sinornis*, *Protopteryx* and the long-snouted *Longipteryx*.
- The *Ornithuromorpha*, the lineage of living birds, include *Yanornis*, *Yixianornis* and *Gansus yumenensis* from Gansu, an amphibious bird similar to a modern loon. In the Late Cretaceous they included the toothed, flightless diving *Hesperornis* and the tern-like, toothed *Ichthyornis*.

Crown birds (Neornithes) had appeared before the end of the Cretaceous: *Vegavis iaai* from Antarctica (about 69 million years ago), an anseriform, and *Asteriornis maastrichtensis* from Belgium (about 66.7 million years ago), a galloanseran with a skull like that of a chicken and a duck. At the K–Pg boundary, 66 million years ago, the impact of an asteroid at Chicxulub, Mexico, ended the Cretaceous ([7.10.31](/7.10.31#sec-31.4.2)). All non-avian dinosaurs, pterosaurs, enantiornithines and most other Mesozoic birds became extinct; the crown birds survived, perhaps because they were small, could eat seeds, and lived on the ground rather than in the forests that were destroyed worldwide (Field et al. 2018). Their radiation after the extinction produced the orders of living birds ([7.17.53](/7.17.53)).

## 52.8. Dinosaurs and Early Birds of China

China has yielded more dinosaur species than any other country (more than 300 named), from the Early Jurassic to the end of the Cretaceous:

| Fauna or site | Age | Highlights |

| Lufeng, Yunnan | Early Jurassic, about 195 million years ago | *Lufengosaurus huenei*, the first dinosaur skeleton mounted in China (Yang Zhongjian, 1941); also early mammaliaforms ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)) |
| Dashanpu, Zigong, Sichuan | Middle Jurassic, about 165 million years ago | Sauropods (*Shunosaurus*, *Omeisaurus*), the stegosaur *Huayangosaurus*, theropods; the Zigong Dinosaur Museum is built over the quarry |
| Shishugou Formation, Junggar Basin, Xinjiang | Late Jurassic, about 160 million years ago | *Guanlong* (an early crested tyrannosauroid), *Yinlong* (the earliest ceratopsian), *Mamenchisaurus sinocanadorum* |

The Jehol fossils were preserved in fine-grained lake sediments by volcanic ash falls, which killed and buried animals rapidly, preserving feathers, skin, stomach contents and even melanosomes. Chinese palaeontologists (among them Ji Qiang, Xu Xing, Zhou Zhonghe and their colleagues) have described most of the feathered dinosaurs and Mesozoic birds known today, and the Jehol and Yanliao biotas are the main source of evidence for the stepwise origin of birds.

## 52.9. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

Chinese textbooks treat the crocodylians as one of four living orders of reptiles (Crocodylia, with 22 species) and the extinct archosaurs among the "ancient reptiles": the thecodonts (Thecodontia) as the primitive stem of the archosaurs, from which pterosaurs, dinosaurs and crocodiles arose, and the dinosaurs as two orders, Saurischia and Ornithischia, distinguished by their pelvis. Birds are treated as a separate class.

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Thecodontia, the stem group of archosaurs, ancestors of dinosaurs, pterosaurs, crocodiles and birds; pseudosuchian thecodonts as ancestors of birds | Thecodontia is paraphyletic. The archosaurs divide into the crocodile line (Pseudosuchia) and the bird line (Avemetatarsalia); birds arose within theropod dinosaurs, not from pseudosuchians |
| Crocodylia with 22 species | About 28 species in three families; several cryptic species recognized by molecular data (e.g. the Nile crocodile split into two species) |
| Crocodylians with complete double temporal fenestrae, a mandibular fenestra, a secondary palate, a fixed quadrate, thecodont teeth, uncinate processes and gastralia, a longitudinal cloacal slit and a single penis | Confirmed; add the four-chambered heart with the foramen of Panizza, the hepatic piston, unidirectional airflow and the loss of the antorbital fenestra in the crown group |
| Saurischia and Ornithischia as two orders of reptiles, with ornithischians "bird-hipped" | Two clades of dinosaurs; birds are saurischians, with a convergently opisthopubic pelvis; the Ornithoscelida hypothesis questions the grouping (§52.5.1) |
| Sauropods as swamp-dwellers whose weight was supported by water; *Mamenchisaurus hochuanensis* 22 m and 50 t | Sauropods were terrestrial, with air-filled skeletons; modern estimates of the mass of *M. hochuanensis* are well below 50 t |
| *Lufengosaurus* as a theropod-like herbivore | A sauropodomorph ("prosauropod"), not a theropod |
| Dinosaurs as sluggish, cold-blooded reptiles that died out from many possible causes | Many dinosaurs were active, fast-growing, some feathered and with elevated metabolic rates; non-avian dinosaurs died out in the K–Pg extinction caused by an asteroid impact, with Deccan volcanism as a possible contributing factor; birds are surviving dinosaurs |
| Pterosaurs with a wing membrane supported by the fourth finger, not related to birds | Confirmed; pterosaurs are the sister group of dinosauromorphs within the bird line, closest to lagerpetids |

## Summary

- Archosaurs (Definition 52.1.1): antorbital and mandibular fenestrae, thecodont teeth, fourth trochanter; two lineages, the crocodile line (Pseudosuchia, crocodile-normal ankle) and the bird line (Avemetatarsalia, advanced mesotarsal ankle), split in the Early Triassic.
- The crocodile line dominated the Triassic (aetosaurs, poposauroids, phytosaurs); crocodylomorphs radiated into marine, terrestrial and semi-aquatic forms. Living crocodylians (about 28 species): secondary palate with palatal valve, four-chambered heart with foramen of Panizza and pulmonary bypass, hepatic piston, unidirectional airflow, TSD, parental care. The Chinese alligator survives in the lower Yangtze through captive breeding and reintroduction.
- Pterosaurs: the first flying vertebrates; wing membrane on the fourth finger, pteroid, actinofibrils, pneumatic bones, pycnofibres; closest relatives the lagerpetids; colonial nesting of *Hamipterus* in Xinjiang.
- Dinosaurs (Definition 52.4.1): perforate acetabulum, erect posture; origin about 233 million years ago, dominance after the Carnian Pluvial Episode and the end-Triassic extinction.
- Saurischians and ornithischians (Definition 52.5.1) by pelvis type; birds are saurischians with a convergent opisthopubic pelvis; Ornithoscelida is an alternative hypothesis. Sauropod gigantism as a cascade (no chewing, long neck, air sacs, many small eggs, fast growth). Mass from limb-bone circumference: $\log_{10} M = 2.749\log_{10} C - 1.104$; bone stress rises as $M^{0.27}$.
- Dinosaur biology: fast growth recorded in bone; elevated metabolism; eggs (soft-shelled ancestrally), colonial nesting, brooding and bird-like embryonic posture.
- Origin of birds: characters acquired stepwise in theropods; feathers evolved in stages (Prum's model) long before flight; *Archaeopteryx* one of many early paravians; flight origin reconciled by WAIR and four-winged *Microraptor*; membranous wings of scansoriopterygids; Mesozoic birds of the Jehol biota (*Jeholornis*, *Confuciusornis*, enantiornithines, ornithuromorphs); crown birds survived the K–Pg extinction.

## Questions

1. Crocodiles look more like lizards than like birds. Explain why they are nevertheless classified with birds, and name three features shared by crocodylians and birds that are probably inherited from their common ancestor.

   **Answer** Classification follows common descent, not overall similarity. Crocodylians and birds share derived characters not found in lizards: the antorbital fenestra (closed in living crocodylians but present in their ancestors), the mandibular fenestra, teeth in sockets in ancestral birds, a fourth trochanter, and many molecular characters; their lizard-like appearance results from shared ancestral features (quadrupedal posture, scaly skin) retained from the common ancestor of all reptiles, which do not indicate close relationship. Features shared by crocodylians and birds and probably inherited from the archosaur ancestor include a completely divided ventricle (four-chambered heart), a muscular gizzard with gastroliths, unidirectional airflow through the lungs, nest building and parental care with vocal communication between parents and young, and the absence of the parietal eye.
2. Using the equation of Campione and Evans, estimate the body mass of an animal whose humerus and femur have least circumferences summing to 1 000 mm. By how much does the estimate change if the circumferences are 10 % larger?

   **Answer** $\log_{10} M = 2.749 \times \log_{10} 1000 - 1.104 = 2.749 \times 3 - 1.104 = 7.143$, so $M = 10^{7.143}\,\mathrm{g} \approx 1.39 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{g} \approx 14\,\mathrm{t}$. With 1 100 mm: $\log_{10} M = 2.749 \times 3.0414 - 1.104 = 7.257$, $M \approx 1.81 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{g} \approx 18\,\mathrm{t}$, an increase of $1.1^{2.749} - 1 \approx 30\,\%$. A 10 % error in measuring bone circumference thus produces a 30 % error in mass, and the scatter of living animals around the regression line (prediction error about 25 %) adds further uncertainty; mass estimates of the largest dinosaurs should be read as ranges.
3. Why is the name "bird-hipped" for ornithischians misleading, and what does the pelvis of birds tell us about convergent evolution?

   **Answer** Ornithischians have a pubis rotated backward, like that of living birds, which inspired the name. But birds are theropods, which belong to the Saurischia, the "lizard-hipped" dinosaurs, whose pubis ancestrally pointed forward. The backward pubis of birds evolved within theropods (it appears in therizinosaurs, dromaeosaurs and early birds), independently of the ornithischian condition. The pelvis therefore shows that similar structures can arise independently in different lineages under similar functional demands — perhaps to make space for a larger gut or for changes in breathing mechanics — and that a single character, however conspicuous, must be interpreted in the light of a phylogeny based on many characters.
4. Explain how the feathered dinosaurs of China support Prum's developmental model of feather evolution, and why feathers are considered an exaptation for flight.

   **Answer** Prum's model predicts that simpler feathers (single filaments, then tufts of barbs, then barbs on a rachis, then closed vanes) should appear before complex, asymmetric flight feathers, and in less bird-like dinosaurs. The fossils show this order on the tree: *Sinosauropteryx*, a compsognathid far from birds, has simple filaments; *Dilong* and *Sinornithosaurus* tufted filaments; oviraptorosaurs such as *Caudipteryx* symmetrical vaned feathers; paravians such as *Microraptor* asymmetrical vanes. Because the earliest feathers were filaments that could not generate aerodynamic forces, and because they occur in flightless dinosaurs that were large or had short arms, feathers must have first served other functions — insulation, display (shown by the melanosome-based colour patterns), brooding — before some were co-opted for flight. A structure that evolved for one function and was later used for another is an **exaptation**.
5. (Open) Why did birds survive the K–Pg extinction when all other dinosaurs, the pterosaurs and most Mesozoic birds died out?

   **Answer** Possible factors: (a) size — crown birds were small, needed less food and could reproduce quickly; (b) diet — the beaks and gizzards of crown birds allowed them to eat seeds, which survive in the soil after plant productivity collapses, while larger herbivores and carnivores lost their food chains; (c) habitat — the impact destroyed forests worldwide (indicated by a spike in fern spores), and analyses of the ecology of fossil and living birds suggest that the surviving lineages were ground-dwelling, while arboreal enantiornithines perished (Field et al. 2018); (d) flight and dispersal allowed recolonization of refugia; (e) physiological features such as endothermy and faster development may have helped. Tests: map ecological traits (body size, diet, habitat) on dated phylogenies and compare survivors with victims; study the few fossil sites spanning the boundary. Survival was probably a combination of traits and luck: most bird lineages also died out, so being a bird was not sufficient.
6. Compare the wings of pterosaurs, birds and bats. In what sense are they homologous, and in what sense convergent?

   **Answer** As forelimbs, all three are homologous: they contain the same bones (humerus, radius and ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges) inherited from the pentadactyl limb of the common tetrapod ancestor. As wings they are convergent: flight evolved independently in each lineage from non-flying ancestors, and the airfoil is built differently. In pterosaurs a membrane is supported mainly by the hugely elongated fourth finger, with a pteroid bone supporting a forewing membrane; in birds the airfoil consists of feathers attached to a skeleton in which the hand is reduced and fused (carpometacarpus with digits II–IV or I–III); in bats a membrane is stretched between four elongated fingers (II–V) and the hindlimb. The same structures (forelimbs) were modified in different ways to serve the same function — a combination of homology at one level and convergence at another ([7.17.61](/7.17.61)).
