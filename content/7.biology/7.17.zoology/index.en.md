Zoology is the science of animals, a single clade of eukaryotes that contains about one and a half million described living species and probably several times as many still unnamed. Its central questions are those of form and history. How many fundamentally different ways of building an animal are there, each a **body plan** defined by its symmetry, germ layers, body cavities, segmentation, skeleton and mode of development? How are the groups that carry these body plans related, and in what order did their features arise? And why does each structure have the form it has: what is it made of, what does it do, and from which structure of an ancestor is it derived? For most of its history zoology answered the second question with a ladder of grades, from protozoans through "lower" to "higher" invertebrates and from fishes to mammals. Molecular phylogenetics has replaced the ladder by a branching tree: the **Bilateria** divide into **Protostomia** and **Deuterostomia**, the protostomes into **Spiralia** and **Ecdysozoa**, and groups long taught as natural units, such as the pseudocoelomates, the Articulata of annelids and arthropods, or the class Reptilia without birds, turn out to be grades or **paraphyletic groups**. Reading animal diversity on this tree, and asking which similarities were inherited and which evolved again and again, is the thread of the whole course.

The course follows the tree from its root upward. A general part introduces animals and the science of zoology, the levels of organization, animal architecture, from symmetry and the coelom to size, support and exchange, developmental patterns and larvae, and the origin of animals with the animal tree that every later chapter refers to. The invertebrates are then treated phylum by phylum, body plan and phylogeny together: the non-bilaterian sponges, ctenophores, placozoans and cnidarians; the origin of the Bilateria and the acoelomorphs; the spiralians, with flatworms, rotifers, molluscs, annelids and the lophophorates; and the ecdysozoans, from the scalidophorans and nematodes to the arthropod body plan, the chelicerates, myriapods and crustaceans. Entomology, the largest part of the course, follows: after the hexapods are delimited, nineteen chapters take the insects through external and internal anatomy, flight and the origin of wings, the senses and communication, reproduction, metamorphosis, phylogeny and fossil history, the orders, and their relations with plants, with each other in social colonies, with their enemies, and with humans. The deuterostomes, the echinoderms and the invertebrate chordates close the invertebrates, and a chapter of invertebrate comparative anatomy reads the phyla once more organ system by organ system. The vertebrates are treated twice. First their diversity and evolution, from the vertebrate body plan and the jawless vertebrates through the origin of jaws, the fishes, the origin of tetrapods, the amphibians and the amniotes to birds and mammals; then a comparative anatomy of the vertebrates that, after the principles of homology, design and development, follows each organ system from the integument and the skull to the sense organs across all classes, with tables of homologous parts in place of lists of facts. A chapter on zoogeography, with the faunal regions of China, and a course summary that assembles the innovations of the body plans along the tree conclude the course. Throughout, every phylum and class is set against the classification of the Chinese textbooks, so that a reader who learned the older system can see what has changed and why.

Zoology is the descriptive core of animal biology, and the mechanisms of its structures are left to other courses on this site. The methods of phylogenetics, the molecular clock, the history of life and the theory of macroevolution and biogeography come from [evolutionary biology](/7.10); cleavage, gastrulation, segmentation, the neural crest and the development of limbs from [developmental biology](/7.5); and the work of the organs compared here, muscle, circulation, respiration, excretion, digestion, thermoregulation and the senses, from [animal physiology](/7.14). The former protozoans and the microbial symbionts of animals, from the photosymbionts of corals to the bacteriocytes of aphids, are treated in [microbiology](/7.11); the plants on which half of all insects feed, and the flowers that insects pollinate, in [botany](/7.12); the origin of nervous systems and the sensory systems in [computational neuroscience](/7.16); and the Toll pathway, the bursa of Fabricius and the origin of adaptive immunity in jawed vertebrates in [immunology](/7.15). In turn, the diversity described here is the material of [animal behavior](/7.18) and [ecology](/7.19).

Zoology is one of my strongest subjects, and entomology is a passion of its own. Like many children, I think, I came to biology through nature: catching insects and keeping them, and watching documentaries; China has a great many amateur naturalists of this kind. Yet as a subject zoology is harder to learn systematically than botany. Many animals are hard to observe, and the insects alone have more described species than all other animals together. Botany has its well-known classification systems, from Engler and Cronquist to APG; zoology has nothing comparable. Molecular phylogenetics has rebuilt the relationships among the animal phyla no less thoroughly than it has rebuilt those of the flowering plants, but the result is distributed among the specialists of each group and has never been gathered into one named consensus system that a student can learn. Few Chinese universities teach zoology as a course of its own, and in olympiad training it was rarely taught systematically, so I learned it from textbooks. My introduction was the *General Zoology* of Lingyun Liu and Guangmei Zheng, comprehensive and clearly illustrated; the *Animal Biology* of Chongren Xu and Hong Cheng, which I read in its third edition, is more refined and more concise. Neither is enough for the details, so I also read closely the comparative anatomy of Anfeng Yang and coauthors and the invertebrate zoology of Shuxian Ren, especially the former. Vertebrates remained my weaker side: the bones of the skeleton in particular would not stay in my memory. Invertebrates were the opposite. In the anxious week before the national preliminary round, when I could hardly read anything, Ren's book was the only one that held my attention, and I finished my first reading of it in that week; that year's paper happened to contain an unusual number of questions on invertebrates, so luck was on my side. Before the national final I read the *General Entomology* of Wanzhi Cai and coauthors from cover to cover in the same spirit. Little of it was examined, but the final went well too. Invertebrates, and insects above all, have been my lucky star.

The arrangement of these pages follows from that experience. My interest in invertebrates grew out of entomology, so the insects take a full part of the course and the most prominent place among the invertebrates, from their anatomy and flight to their orders and their lives with plants, enemies and humans. The comparative anatomy of vertebrates, which I found hardest, is organized by organ system and by homology: once the bones of the skull are traced as dermal and cartilage bones through the classes, and the pharyngeal arches through their changing fates, the skeleton becomes a history to be followed rather than a list to be memorized. Although I did not study from them, the English textbooks are the main line here, because they use the most current phylogeny and are the most complete; the Chinese textbooks I learned from are fully covered, and wherever their system differs, each chapter explains how the old groups correspond to the new ones, so that the two accounts do not conflict.
