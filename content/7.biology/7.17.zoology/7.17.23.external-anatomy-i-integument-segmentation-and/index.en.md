The external anatomy of insects is the vocabulary of entomology: every identification key, every order and family description and every functional study uses it. This chapter and the next describe it from the body wall outwards. Here we treat the integument, which is both skeleton and skin; the way the body is divided into segments and sclerites; and the head with its antennae and mouthparts. The thorax, legs, wings and abdomen follow in [7.17.24](/7.17.24).

Two principles organize the description. The first is **homology**: the same parts recur, modified, across orders and on different segments of one animal (**serial homology**), so that a bug's beak and a butterfly's proboscis can be read as transformed versions of the jaws of a grasshopper. The second is function: the form of each structure is understood through what it does, for example the waterproofing of the cuticle or the pumping of liquid food. The arthropod exoskeleton in general, including its development and molting, is introduced in [7.17.18](/7.17.18); the physiology of molting is in [7.5.33](/7.5.33) and [7.14.32](/7.14.32#sec-32.9.3).

## 23.1. The Integument

### 23.1.1. Layers

**Definition 23.1.1 (Integument)** The **integument** of an insect is its body wall: a single-layered epidermis (in entomology often called hypodermis), resting on a basement membrane that faces the hemocoel, and the **cuticle** secreted by the epidermis. The cuticle consists of a thin outer **epicuticle**, which contains no chitin, and a thick **procuticle** of chitin and protein; in hardened regions the procuticle is differentiated into an outer, sclerotized **exocuticle** and an inner, unsclerotized **endocuticle**.

The layers, from the outside:

| Layer | Thickness | Composition and function |
|---|---|---|
| Cement layer | Very thin, often absent | Secreted by dermal glands; protects the wax |
| Wax layer | Monolayer to several µm | Hydrocarbons, wax esters, fatty alcohols; main barrier against water loss |
| Epicuticle (superficial, outer and inner epicuticle) | 0.1–3 µm | Lipoprotein and tanned protein, no chitin; inextensible; the first layer laid down at a molt |
| Exocuticle | Part of the procuticle (10 µm–0.5 mm in total) | Chitin microfibrils in a protein matrix, cross-linked by sclerotization; hard, dark, not digested at molting |
| Endocuticle | Usually the thickest layer | Chitin and protein, unsclerotized, flexible; digested and resorbed at molting |
| Epidermis | One cell layer | Secretes cuticle, waxes, cement and enzymes of molting; contains dermal glands and the cells of setae |
| Basement membrane | — | Extracellular matrix separating the epidermis from the hemolymph |

**Chitin** is an unbranched polymer of β-1,4-linked *N*-acetylglucosamine ([7.1](/7.1)). In the procuticle its chains are bundled into microfibrils about 3 nm thick, embedded in protein. Within each thin sheet the microfibrils are parallel, and successive sheets are rotated by a small angle, so that the orientation turns helically through the thickness (a helicoidal or Bouligand structure). In sections the helicoid appears as alternating light and dark lamellae, an optical effect of the turning fibers. The helicoid resists stress equally in all directions in the plane of the cuticle, as plywood does. In many insects the endocuticle laid down by night has helicoidal and that by day parallel layers, so that daily growth layers can be counted to estimate the age of an adult.

Fine *pore canals* run from the epidermis through the procuticle and branch into still finer wax canals in the epicuticle; they deliver lipids to the surface. Larger ducts of dermal glands carry wax and cement. Scale insects and mealybugs have elaborate wax glands whose secretions cover the body and the egg sac.

Not all cuticle is hard. Between sclerites, and over the whole body of many larvae, the cuticle remains thin, folded and flexible (*arthrodial membrane*, intersegmental membrane), with little or no exocuticle. It can expand greatly: the intersegmental membranes of the abdomen of an ovipositing female locust stretch by up to about twenty times, the abdomen of a termite queen or a honeypot ant repletes is distended many times by unfolding of its folded epicuticle, and the abdominal cuticle of the blood-sucking bug *Rhodnius* becomes plastic under neural control when the bug takes a meal of many times its own mass. Where elastic recoil is needed, in wing hinges, the jumping mechanism of fleas and the tendons of the thorax, the cuticle contains *resilin*, a rubber-like protein that returns about 97% of the stored elastic energy.

### 23.1.2. Sclerotization

**Definition 23.1.2 (Sclerotization)** **Sclerotization** (tanning) is the irreversible hardening and stiffening of the exocuticle after a molt, by which cuticular proteins are cross-linked to each other and to chitin by quinones derived from catecholamines, and the cuticle loses water.

The catecholamines *N*-acetyldopamine and *N*-β-alanyldopamine, synthesized from tyrosine via dopa and dopamine in the epidermis, are secreted into the new cuticle, where phenoloxidases (laccase-2 in particular) oxidize them to quinones and quinone methides; these react with nucleophilic side chains of proteins and form cross-links. Dehydration of the protein matrix contributes as much to stiffening as the cross-links. Sclerotization starts minutes after ecdysis, triggered by the hormone bursicon, so that a freshly molted (teneral) insect is soft and pale for a while ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)). Darkening by melanin uses the same precursor pathway but is a separate process: a cuticle can be hard and pale, or soft and dark.

Mechanically, sclerotized cuticle is a fiber composite with a stiffness of the order of 1–20 GPa, between that of bone and of wood; soft endocuticle and arthrodial membrane are a thousand times more compliant. Mandibles are among the hardest insect structures: their cutting edges have a hardness comparable to that of copper, and in many ants, termites, beetles and caterpillars they are further hardened by incorporated metals such as zinc and manganese.

### 23.1.3. Waterproofing

A small insect has a large surface relative to its volume, and in dry air it would lose its water within hours without a barrier. The barrier is the wax layer, mainly long-chain hydrocarbons (often 25–35 carbons, saturated, branched or with double bonds). Its permeability rises steeply above a *transition temperature* at which the ordered lipid packing melts, and desert insects have waxes with longer chains and higher transition temperatures. Spiracles are the other route of water loss; they are dealt with in [7.17.26](/7.17.26). The same cuticular hydrocarbons serve as contact **pheromones** and recognition cues (a colony odour in social insects, [7.17.38](/7.17.38)), and surface waxes give some insects a whitish bloom that reflects sunlight and repels water.

### 23.1.4. Cuticular Processes

Insects are covered with outgrowths of the cuticle. Four kinds are distinguished by their cellular origin:

- *spines*: multicellular outgrowths of the whole integument, immovable; if articulated at the base they are *spurs* (the tibial spurs of many insects);
- *setae* (hairs, macrotrichia): formed by three cells, a trichogen cell that secretes the shaft, a tormogen cell that forms the socket, and a sensory neuron; most setae are mechanoreceptors or chemoreceptors (trichoid sensilla, [7.17.27](/7.17.27)); large setae are bristles, and flattened setae are *scales* (butterflies and moths, some beetles and bristletails);
- *acanthae*: unicellular, non-articulated projections;
- *microtrichia*: tiny hair-like projections, several per cell, which make the wings of many flies and caddisflies hairy, waterproof surfaces and form the attachment hairs of many feet.

### 23.1.5. Colour

Insect colours are produced in two ways, often combined.

*Pigmentary colours* come from the absorption of light by molecules in the cuticle, epidermis, hemolymph or fat body. Melanins give black and brown; ommochromes (from tryptophan) give brown, red and yellow and are the screening pigments of the eyes; pteridines give the white, yellow and orange of pierid butterflies and the red eyes of *Drosophila*; carotenoids and flavonoids are sequestered from food plants; tetrapyrroles (bile pigments and breakdown products of chlorophyll) give the greens of many grasshoppers and caterpillars; anthraquinones such as carminic acid of cochineal insects, and the aphins of aphids, are found in scale insects and aphids. Insect pigments are made by the insect, taken from food, or, rarely, made by endosymbionts.

*Structural colours* arise from the interaction of light with structures of the cuticle of the scale of the wavelength. Scattering from structures larger than the wavelength reflects all wavelengths and gives white (pierid scales owe their whiteness partly to scattering by pterin beads); scattering from very small irregularities favours short wavelengths and gives blue. Interference in stacks of thin layers gives the metallic, angle-dependent colours of many beetles, dragonflies and butterflies; diffraction gratings and three-dimensional photonic crystals (in the scales of some weevils and of lycaenid and papilionid butterflies) give other effects. In a multilayer of alternating refractive indices $n_1, n_2$ and thicknesses $d_1, d_2$, reflections from successive interfaces reinforce at the wavelength

$$
\lambda_{\max} = 2\left(n_1 d_1 \cos\theta_1 + n_2 d_2 \cos\theta_2\right), \tag{23.1}
$$

where $\theta_i$ are the angles of refraction in the layers; at normal incidence the condition is that each layer be a quarter wave thick, $n_i d_i = \lambda/4$. The reflectance at the peak rises with the number of layers and with the contrast $n_1/n_2$. Thin-film interference in physics is treated in [5.4.1](/5.4.1#sec-1.6).

**Example 23.1.1** The iridescent blue of *Morpho* butterflies comes from ridges on the scales carrying stacks of chitin lamellae separated by air. Suppose the lamellae are 80 nm thick ($n_1 = 1.56$) and the air gaps 110 nm ($n_2 = 1.0$). By Eq. (23.1) at normal incidence, $\lambda_{\max} = 2(1.56 \times 80 + 110)\,\mathrm{nm} \approx 470\,\mathrm{nm}$, blue. Viewed at an angle the peak shifts to shorter wavelengths, so the colour turns violet: interference colours, unlike pigments, change with viewing angle. Wetting the wing with a liquid of refractive index 1.4 lowers the contrast and shifts the peak to about $2(125 + 154)\,\mathrm{nm} \approx 560\,\mathrm{nm}$, which is a test that distinguishes structural from pigmentary colours.

:::

Colours have many functions: camouflage and disguise, warning (aposematic) signals and mimicry ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)), sexual signals, thermoregulation (dark cuticle absorbs radiation) and protection from ultraviolet light.

## 23.2. Segmentation and Tagmosis

### 23.2.1. Primary and Secondary Segmentation

The insect body is composed of **segments**, but the segments seen on the exoskeleton of an adult are not exactly the embryonic ones. In soft-bodied larvae the boundaries of the primary segments are folds where the longitudinal muscles attach, as in annelids. In sclerotized insects, the plates of each segment extend forward over the fold of the segment in front, leaving the soft intersegmental membrane in the posterior part of the primary segment.

**Definition 23.2.1 (Secondary Segmentation)** **Secondary segmentation** is the arrangement of the insect exoskeleton in which each sclerotized ring begins with a narrow strip (the acrotergite dorsally, acrosternite ventrally) in front of the internal ridge (antecosta) on which the longitudinal muscles insert, so that the muscles attach to rigid cuticle and the flexible conjunctival (intersegmental) membrane lies within the original segment.

Secondary segmentation turns the segmented muscles into levers acting across hinges of membrane, so that the abdomen can telescope and bend. In the thorax of winged insects the acrotergites of the second and third segments are enlarged as the postnota, which carry internal plates (phragmata) for the attachment of the large longitudinal flight muscles ([7.17.24](/7.17.24), [7.17.25](/7.17.25)).

### 23.2.2. Sclerites, Sutures and Apodemes

A sclerotized plate is a *sclerite*. Each segment primitively has a dorsal plate, the *tergum* (plural terga; also notum in the thorax), a ventral plate, the *sternum* (sterna), and lateral regions, the *pleura* (singular pleuron), which bear the leg bases. Subdivisions are called tergites, sternites and pleurites. In the abdomen the pleura are usually membranous; in the thorax they are sclerotized and joined to terga and sterna into a box for the leg and flight muscles.

Lines on the surface have two different origins, and the distinction matters for homology. A *suture* in the strict sense is a line of fusion between originally separate sclerites (or a line along which the cuticle splits at molting). A *sulcus* is the external groove of an internal ridge of the cuticle that strengthens a sclerite. Most "sutures" of insect descriptions are sulci, but the traditional names are kept. Internal processes of the cuticle are *apodemes* (inflections, often plate-like) and *apophyses* (arm-like); they give rigidity and surfaces for muscle attachment, so that the exoskeleton also has an internal skeleton. External landmarks where an apophysis invaginates appear as pits.

Directions on the body follow three axes: anterior (cephalic) to posterior (caudal), dorsal to ventral, and medial (mesal) to lateral. On appendages, proximal (basal) is near the body and distal (apical) away from it.

### 23.2.3. Tagmosis

**Tagmata** are groups of fused or functionally integrated segments ([7.17.3](/7.17.3)). The insect body of about 20 segments forms a head of six segments, a thorax of three and an abdomen of eleven (with a telson in the embryo), although fusion and reduction mean the full complement is never visible in one animal. Tagmosis can be shifted secondarily: in apocritan Hymenoptera the first abdominal segment (propodeum) is fused to the thorax to form the mesosoma, and the narrow second segment (petiole) separates it from the gaster; in many flies and higher Hymenoptera, terminal abdominal segments are telescoped into the body.

## 23.3. The Head

### 23.3.1. The Head Capsule

The head is a rigid capsule with two openings: the occipital foramen behind, through which gut, nerve cord, aorta and tracheae pass into the neck (cervix, mostly derived from the prothorax), and the mouth, surrounded by the mouthparts. Its regions are delimited by sulci and sutures.

**Definition 23.3.1 (Ecdysial Line)** The **ecdysial line** (ecdysial suture, epicranial suture) is the line of weakness of the head cuticle along which the cuticle of the immature insect splits at molting; it usually has the form of an inverted Y, with a median stem (coronal suture) on the vertex and two arms (frontal sutures) that diverge towards the antennae and delimit the frons.

Molting cuticle splits along ecdysial lines because they lack exocuticle. They are best developed in nymphs and larvae; in many adults they are faint or absent. On the head the main regions are:

| Region | Position | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Vertex | Top of the head, between and behind the eyes | Bears the stem of the ecdysial line and often the lateral ocelli |
| Frons | Front, between the arms of the ecdysial line | Bears the median ocellus; the origin of muscles of the pharynx (cibarial dilators) |
| Clypeus | Below the frons, separated by the frontoclypeal (epistomal) sulcus | Often divided into postclypeus and anteclypeus; carries the cibarial pump muscles in sucking insects |
| Labrum | Hinged to the clypeus | The "upper lip", a movable flap covering the mandibles |
| Genae | Sides of the head below the eyes | Separated from the mouthpart bases by the subgenal sulcus |
| Occiput and postocciput | Back of the head around the occipital foramen | The postoccipital sulcus is the only external trace of a segment boundary (between maxillary and labial segments) |
| Gula | Ventral sclerite behind the mouth in prognathous heads | Formed where the postgenae meet ventrally |

Internally the head is braced by the *tentorium*, formed by two pairs of apophyses, the anterior and posterior tentorial arms, which invaginate at the anterior and posterior tentorial pits and fuse in the middle; dorsal arms often grow towards the frons. The tentorium supports the brain and the foregut and provides attachment for the muscles of the mandibles, maxillae and antennae. Its fused form is a character of Dicondylia; in jumping bristletails and entognathans the arms remain separate.

### 23.3.2. Head Orientation

The direction in which the mouthparts point relative to the long axis of the body defines three head types. A *hypognathous* head has the mouthparts directed downwards, the presumed ancestral condition, found in grasshoppers, cockroaches and many herbivores that feed on surfaces below them. A *prognathous* head has the mouthparts directed forwards; it is typical of predators and of insects that burrow or push through narrow spaces (ground beetles, earwigs, many beetle and neuropteran larvae), and the space between the posterior parts of the head is then closed below by the gula. An *opisthognathous* head has the mouthparts directed backwards between the forelegs; it is the condition of Hemiptera, whose beak points back along the body at rest.

### 23.3.3. The Segments of the Head

The head is formed by the fusion of six segments: from front to back, an anterior (labral or ocular) region, and the antennal, intercalary (postantennal), mandibular, maxillary and labial segments. The mouthparts are the appendages of the last three; the antenna corresponds to a whole appendage of the antennal segment; the intercalary segment has no appendage in insects but a ganglion (the tritocerebrum). The origin of the labrum is debated: its development, the paired origin of its muscles and the expression of limb-patterning genes such as *Distal-less* suggest that it is a fused pair of appendages of the anterior region. The hypopharynx is probably a lobe of the sternal region of the mandibular segment. The general arthropod "head problem" and the correspondence of head segments across arthropods are treated in [7.17.18](/7.17.18).

### 23.3.4. Eyes

Adult insects primitively have a pair of **compound eyes**, made of many **ommatidia** (a few in some ants, about 30,000 in large dragonflies), and three dorsal *ocelli* arranged in a triangle, one median and two lateral. Ocelli have a single lens over many photoreceptors; they do not form sharp images but are highly sensitive to changes in light intensity and help to stabilize flight attitude relative to the horizon. Larvae of holometabolous insects lack compound eyes and have instead lateral *stemmata* (larval ocelli), up to six or seven on each side of the head in caterpillars. The structure and optics of compound eyes and ocelli are the subject of [7.17.27](/7.17.27) (see also [7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.4)).

## 23.4. Antennae

### 23.4.1. Parts of the Antenna

**Definition 23.4.1 (Parts of the Antenna)** The insect antenna consists of three parts: the basal **scape**, articulated with the head in a membranous antennal socket and containing the muscles that move the antenna; the **pedicel**, the second segment, containing Johnston's organ; and the **antennal flagellum**, the distal part, usually divided into annuli called flagellomeres and without intrinsic muscles.

The scape articulates on a small process of the socket rim, the antennifer, so that the antenna can be swung in all directions by muscles arising from the tentorium. The flagellum carries most of the sensilla: mechanoreceptors, olfactory and gustatory receptors, and thermo- and hygroreceptors ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)). Its subdivision into flagellomeres is not a true segmentation, because the flagellomeres have no muscles; their number varies within orders and even within an individual's development.

**Definition 23.4.2 (Johnston's Organ)** **Johnston's organ** is a chordotonal organ in the pedicel of the insect antenna, composed of many scolopidia attached around the joint between pedicel and flagellum, which detects movements of the flagellum relative to the pedicel.

Johnston's organ, present in all insects but not in entognathans, has diverse functions. In male mosquitoes and midges, which have about 15,000 scolopidial cells in it, it detects the flight tone of females; in honey bees it detects the air vibrations of the waggle dance; in flies it senses air flow during flight and gravity; in water-surface insects it detects surface waves.

### 23.4.2. Antennal Types

The form of the flagellum is highly variable and a key character in identification. The standard types are:

| Type | Form of the flagellum | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| Setaceous (bristle-like) | Tapering from base to tip, segments short and thin | Dragonflies, cicadas, mayflies |
| Filiform (thread-like) | Segments of nearly equal width | Ground beetles, locusts, cockroaches (long setaceous-filiform) |
| Moniliform (bead-like) | Segments rounded, like a string of beads | Termites |
| Serrate (saw-toothed) | Segments triangular, projecting on one side | Click beetles, jewel beetles |
| Pectinate (comb-like) | Long lateral processes on one side (unipectinate) or both sides (bipectinate) | Fire-coloured beetles; many male moths (bipectinate) |
| Plumose (feathery, whorled) | Each flagellomere with a whorl of long setae | Male mosquitoes and midges |
| Clavate (clubbed) | Gradually thickened towards the tip | Butterflies, ladybirds, darkling beetles |
| Capitate (knobbed) | Abruptly ending in a distinct knob of a few segments | Sap beetles, bark beetles |
| Lamellate | Terminal segments flattened into plates that can be spread or closed | Scarab beetles, stag beetles (similar) |
| Geniculate (elbowed) | Long scape, with the flagellum at an angle to it | Ants, honey bees, weevils (often geniculate and clubbed) |
| Aristate | Flagellum reduced to an enlarged first flagellomere (postpedicel) bearing a dorsal bristle, the arista | House flies, *Drosophila* (cyclorrhaphan flies) |
| Stylate | Flagellum ending in a short terminal style | Horse flies, robber flies |

Antennae are often sexually dimorphic. Male moths with large bipectinate antennae sample a large volume of air for female sex **pheromones**, and the plumose antennae of male mosquitoes resonate at the frequency of the female wingbeat. The type names describe shape, not homology: similar types have evolved independently in unrelated groups.

## 23.5. Mouthparts

### 23.5.1. The Chewing Ground Plan

**Definition 23.5.1 (Chewing Mouthparts)** **Chewing mouthparts** (mandibulate mouthparts) are the ancestral insect mouthparts, used to bite and chew solid food, consisting of five components: the **labrum** (upper lip), a median flap hinged to the clypeus, whose inner surface is the epipharynx; the paired **mandibles**, unsegmented jaws with apical cutting teeth (incisor area) and a basal grinding area (molar area); the paired **maxillae**, each of a basal cardo and stipes bearing an inner lobe (lacinia), an outer lobe (galea) and a segmented maxillary palp; the **labium** (lower lip), formed by the fused second maxillae, of a basal postmentum and a distal prementum bearing inner glossae, outer paraglossae and a pair of labial palps; and the **hypopharynx**, a median tongue-like lobe on the floor of the preoral cavity.

The grasshopper shows the ground plan with little modification. The mandibles move transversely; in dicondylic insects each mandible has two articulations with the head (anterior and posterior condyles), so that it swings like a door on a fixed axis and is moved by a large adductor muscle from the head capsule and a smaller abductor. The maxillary palps usually have five segments and the labial palps three; they carry chemoreceptors and test food. The laciniae hold and macerate food, the galeae are sensory. The glossae and paraglossae together form the ligula. In prognathous insects the postmentum may be divided into submentum and mentum.

The space enclosed by the mouthparts in front of the true mouth is the preoral cavity. The hypopharynx divides it into a dorsal food pouch, the *cibarium*, opening into the mouth, and a ventral *salivarium*, into which the salivary duct opens. Muscles from the clypeus dilate the cibarium; in sucking insects this becomes the cibarial pump, the engine of liquid feeding.

Mouthparts are the appendages of the mandibular, maxillary and labial segments, and their serial homology with legs is shown by development and by homeotic transformations: the cardo and stipes correspond to the leg base (coxa), the palps to the telopodite. In what follows the derived types are described as transformations of these five components; the color code of the figures is the same throughout (labrum yellow, mandible orange, maxilla blue, labium green).

For example, the main sucking tube is maxillary in butterflies, maxillary in true bugs, labral in mosquitoes and labial in house flies: sucking mouthparts have evolved many times from different components, and the textbook "types" are not natural groups.

### 23.5.2. Chewing-Lapping Mouthparts

**Definition 23.5.2 (Chewing-Lapping Mouthparts)** **Chewing-lapping mouthparts** are mouthparts in which the mandibles remain as chewing or manipulating jaws while the maxillae and labium are elongated into a proboscis for taking up liquid, the fused glossae forming a hairy tongue that is dipped into nectar and retracted between the maxillary galeae and labial palps.

In the honey bee the tongue (glossa) ends in a small spoon, the flabellum, and is covered with rings of hairs that hold nectar by capillarity. Repeated protraction and retraction of the tongue, about five cycles per second, loads nectar into the tube formed by the galeae and labial palps, from which the cibarial pump draws it up. The spoon-shaped mandibles knead wax, manipulate resin and pollen, feed larvae, groom and fight. At rest the proboscis is folded back under the head.

Lapping and sucking have different optima. Suction through a narrow canal is limited by viscosity, which rises steeply with sugar concentration; dipping a hairy tongue is less sensitive to viscosity because more viscous nectar adheres in a thicker layer. Accordingly, the nectar concentration that maximizes the rate of energy intake is about 30–35% sucrose for suction feeders such as butterflies, and about 50–60% for viscous dippers such as bees (Kim, Gilet and Bush 2011), matching the nectar concentrations of flowers pollinated by each group.

### 23.5.3. Piercing-Sucking Mouthparts

**Definition 23.5.3 (Piercing-Sucking Mouthparts)** **Piercing-sucking mouthparts** are mouthparts in which some components are drawn out into fine needles (stylets) that pierce plant or animal tissue, together enclosing a food canal through which liquid is sucked up and a salivary canal through which saliva is injected, while another component forms a sheath that guides the stylets but does not enter the tissue.

Two quite different constructions are both called piercing-sucking.

In Hemiptera (cicadas, aphids, true bugs) the labium forms a segmented beak or rostrum (four segments in most bugs, three in cicadas and aphids) with a dorsal groove holding four stylets. The two mandibular stylets lie outside and are toothed at the tip; they cut and anchor. The two maxillary stylets lie inside and are interlocked along their length by ridges and grooves; their inner faces form two canals, a larger dorsal food canal and a smaller ventral salivary canal. As the stylets penetrate, the labium folds back and stays on the surface. Sap feeders lay down a sheath of hardened saliva around the stylet path and reach the phloem or xylem; predatory bugs inject digestive saliva and suck up the liquefied contents of their prey (extra-oral digestion). Phloem sap is under positive pressure and flows into the stylets almost unaided; xylem sap is under negative pressure, and xylem feeders such as cicadas and spittlebugs have a large cibarial pump muscled from an enlarged, bulging postclypeus.

In female mosquitoes (Culicidae) there are six stylets. The labrum is rolled into the food canal, closed below by the hypopharynx, which carries the salivary canal; the paired mandibles are fine blades, and the paired maxillae are toothed at the tip and do most of the cutting. The labium, ending in two small lobes (labella), is a gutter-like sheath that bends back as the stylet bundle enters the skin; the stylets probe for a capillary under the guidance of sensilla on the labrum tip. Males have reduced mandibles and maxillae and do not suck blood. Similar bundles occur in other blood-feeding Nematocera (black flies, biting midges, sand flies).

Fleas (Siphonaptera) pierce with an unpaired epipharyngeal stylet flanked by two blade-like laciniae; sucking lice (Anoplura) have three stylets of uncertain homology (dorsal, median salivary, ventral) retracted into a sac in the head. Bed bugs and kissing bugs are Hemiptera of the bug type.

### 23.5.4. Rasping-Sucking Mouthparts

**Definition 23.5.4 (Rasping-Sucking Mouthparts)** **Rasping-sucking mouthparts** are the asymmetrical mouthparts of thrips (Thysanoptera), in which a short mouth cone formed by the clypeus, labrum and labium encloses three stylets, the left mandible and the two maxillary laciniae, the right mandible being vestigial; the mandibular stylet punctures the cell and the paired maxillary stylets form the food canal.

Thrips feed by punching a hole into individual epidermal or mesophyll cells and sucking out their contents; the emptied cells fill with air and give damaged leaves a silvery appearance. Some thrips feed on pollen or fungal spores. The loss of the right mandible is an autapomorphy of the order.

### 23.5.5. Siphoning Mouthparts

**Definition 23.5.5 (Siphoning Mouthparts)** **Siphoning mouthparts** are the mouthparts of adult butterflies and moths, in which the mandibles are absent and the two greatly elongated maxillary galeae, each grooved on its inner face, are linked along their length to form a proboscis that encloses a food canal and is coiled under the head at rest.

The proboscis is coiled by the elasticity of the cuticle and by intrinsic muscles, and extended by a rise in hemolymph pressure, with a "knee" bend that allows the tip to be directed into flowers. Liquid is drawn up by the cibarial and pharyngeal pumps. The food canal is narrow, so viscosity limits intake; by the Hagen–Poiseuille law the volume flow through a tube of radius $r$ and length $L$ under a pressure difference $\Delta p$ is

$$
Q = \frac{\pi r^4 \Delta p}{8 \mu L}, \tag{23.2}
$$

so a canal twice as wide carries sixteen times more nectar, and a nectar three times more viscous (about 40% instead of 20% sucrose) is drunk three times more slowly. Many proboscises in fact take up liquid partly by capillarity through the gaps between the linking structures near the tip, which makes them usable on thin films of liquid and wet surfaces as well as in pools.

The length of the proboscis can be extraordinary. Darwin, seeing the Madagascan orchid *Angraecum sesquipedale* with its 30-cm nectar spur, predicted a moth with a proboscis of that length; the hawkmoth *Xanthopan morganii praedicta*, described in 1903, has a proboscis of over 20 cm. Some noctuid moths pierce fruit with a proboscis bearing tearing hooks and erectile barbs, and a few (*Calyptra*) pierce the skin of mammals and suck blood. The most basal moths, the Micropterigidae, still have functional mandibles and chew pollen ([7.17.36](/7.17.36)).

### 23.5.6. Sponging Mouthparts

**Definition 23.5.6 (Sponging Mouthparts)** **Sponging mouthparts** are the mouthparts of house flies and related higher flies, in which mandibles and maxillary stylets are absent and the labium forms a fleshy proboscis (haustellum) ending in a pair of large lobes, the labella, whose ventral surfaces are crossed by channels (pseudotracheae) through which liquid food is drawn by capillarity and suction to the food canal.

The proboscis of a house fly has three parts: a basal rostrum, carrying the maxillary palps and housing the cibarial pump; the haustellum, mainly labium, with the labrum and hypopharynx forming the food canal on its anterior face; and the labella. The pseudotracheae, reinforced by incomplete rings of cuticle (hence the name), open to the surface by fine slits that admit liquid but exclude particles. House flies regurgitate saliva and crop contents onto solid food to dissolve it, which is one reason they spread pathogens ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)). In blood-feeding calyptrate flies (stable flies, horn flies, tsetse flies) the labium itself became a rigid piercing organ with teeth on its labella, a secondary derivation from sponging mouthparts.

### 23.5.7. Mouthparts of Larvae and Other Types

Larvae often have mouthparts very different from those of their adults, because they feed on different food ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)). Caterpillars and beetle larvae have chewing mouthparts, and caterpillars spin silk from labial glands that open on a spinneret on the labium. Maggots of cyclorrhaphan flies have lost the external head capsule; they rasp and tear food with paired mouth hooks of an internal cephalopharyngeal skeleton, which Chinese textbooks treat as a separate mouthpart type. Larvae of antlions and lacewings have sucking jaws: each mandible and maxilla together form a channel through which the liquefied prey is sucked. Dragonfly and damselfly nymphs catch prey with a prehensile labium, the mask, which is folded under the head and shot forward by hydraulic pressure ([7.17.31](/7.17.31)). Mosquito larvae filter-feed with brushes on the labrum. Many adult insects do not feed at all and have vestigial mouthparts (mayflies, some moths, males of scale insects).

## Questions

1. Why does the cuticle need both an epicuticle and a procuticle? What would happen to an insect whose wax layer were removed?

   **Answer** The procuticle provides mechanical strength (chitin microfibrils in protein, stiffened by sclerotization) but is permeable to water; the epicuticle, inextensible and without chitin, carries the lipid barrier that limits water loss. Removing the wax (for example with abrasive dusts or solvents) increases transpiration many times, and a small insect in dry air would desiccate within hours; inert dusts such as diatomaceous earth kill stored-product insects in this way.

2. A beetle's metallic green turns blue when viewed obliquely, and its colour disappears when the elytron is ground into a powder. Explain both observations.

   **Answer** The colour is produced by multilayer interference in the exocuticle. By Eq. (23.1) the reflected peak is proportional to $\cos\theta$ in the layers, so it shifts to shorter wavelengths at oblique incidence (green to blue). Grinding destroys the ordered layers, so the coherent reflection is lost; a pigment colour would persist in the powder. Wetting with a liquid of similar refractive index, which removes the contrast between layers, would also shift or abolish a structural colour.

3. (Calculation) The scales of a butterfly reflect maximally at 550 nm at normal incidence. The reflecting structure is a stack of chitin lamellae ($n = 1.56$) separated by air, with chitin and air layers of equal optical thickness. Find the thickness of each layer.

   **Answer** Quarter-wave layers: $n_1 d_1 = n_2 d_2 = \lambda/4 = 137.5\,\mathrm{nm}$, so $d_1 = 137.5/1.56 \approx 88\,\mathrm{nm}$ of chitin and $d_2 = 137.5\,\mathrm{nm}$ of air; the period is about 226 nm, and $2(n_1d_1 + n_2d_2) = 550\,\mathrm{nm}$ as required.

4. Which mouthpart forms the food canal in (a) a butterfly, (b) a cicada, (c) a female mosquito, (d) a house fly? What does this imply about the evolution of sucking mouthparts?

   **Answer** (a) The two maxillary galeae; (b) the two maxillary stylets; (c) the labrum, closed by the hypopharynx; (d) the labrum and hypopharynx on the haustellum, with liquid reaching it through the pseudotracheae of the labial labella. Since different components form the tube in different orders, and the closest relatives of each sucking group chew, sucking mouthparts evolved independently many times; the mouthpart types are functional categories, not homologous structures.

5. Why do aphids feeding on phloem need no strong pump, whereas cicadas feeding on xylem have a bulging clypeus? Relate your answer to the pressures in the two tissues.

   **Answer** Phloem sap is under positive turgor pressure (up to about 1–2 MPa), so once the stylets enter a sieve tube the sap is pushed into the food canal. Xylem sap is under tension (negative pressure), so the insect must generate a pressure lower than that of the xylem to draw it up; this requires a powerful cibarial pump, whose dilator muscles arise from the enlarged postclypeus. Xylem sap is also very dilute, so xylem feeders process large volumes and excrete copious watery fluid.

6. (Open question) Using Eq. (23.2), explain why suction feeders prefer more dilute nectar than lapping bees, and why long proboscises might impose a cost.

   **Answer** For suction, intake rate is $Q \propto r^4 \Delta p/(\mu L)$; the energy intake rate is $Q$ times the sugar concentration $c$, and viscosity $\mu$ rises nearly exponentially with $c$. The product $c/\mu(c)$ has a maximum at intermediate concentrations (about 30–35% sucrose). Lappers load a film whose thickness increases with viscosity, partly compensating the slower flow, so their optimum lies at higher concentrations. Since $Q \propto 1/L$, a proboscis twice as long halves the rate at a given pressure difference (or requires a doubled pressure difference), so very long proboscises are favoured only where they give access to otherwise unexploited nectar, as in deep-spurred flowers.
