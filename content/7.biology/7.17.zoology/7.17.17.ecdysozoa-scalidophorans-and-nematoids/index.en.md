The second great branch of the **Protostomia** ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)) is the **Ecdysozoa**, the animals that grow by **molting**. It contains eight phyla and, because it includes the arthropods, about 83 % of all described animal species. This chapter opens the ecdysozoan part of the course with the features shared by the whole clade, then treats the smaller groups: the three scalidophoran phyla (priapulans, kinorhynchs, loriciferans), the nematodes and the horsehair worms. The next chapter turns to the third ecdysozoan lineage, the Panarthropoda, and builds the arthropod body plan on which the following four chapters and the whole entomological part of the course rest.

Two threads run through the chapter. The first is phylogenetic: the nematodes were long grouped with rotifers and other small worms as "pseudocoelomates", and the arthropods with annelids as "Articulata"; the Ecdysozoa hypothesis broke up both groupings. The second is mechanical: a body enclosed in a cuticle that cannot grow must molt, and a nematode, which has only longitudinal muscles and a cuticle of crossed fibers, moves by working against a pressurized **hydrostatic skeleton** in a way that can be written as a small geometric model. The physiology of nematode muscle is in [7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.6.2) and [7.14.8](/7.14.8#sec-8.8.4); the cell lineage of *Caenorhabditis elegans* is in [7.5.6](/7.5.6#sec-6.4); here we keep to structure, diversity and life cycles.

## 17.1. Ecdysozoa

### 17.1.1. The Molted Cuticle

All ecdysozoans are covered by an extracellular layer secreted by the epidermis. Unlike the thin, often ciliated surfaces of flatworms and nemerteans, this layer is thick, layered and stiff enough to serve as armor and as a skeleton, and it is shed and replaced as the animal grows.

**Definition 17.1.1 (Cuticle)** The **cuticle** of ecdysozoans is a noncellular, multilayered covering secreted by the epidermis and periodically molted. It consists of a thin outer **epicuticle**, formed at the tips of the epidermal microvilli and largely made of lipids and proteins, and a thicker **procuticle** below it, divided where differentiated into an outer **exocuticle** and an inner **endocuticle**. The fibrous component of the procuticle is chitin in panarthropods, scalidophorans and nematomorph larvae, and collagen in nematodes and adult nematomorphs.

Chitin is present in every ecdysozoan phylum, at least in some region or stage: even nematodes, whose body cuticle is made of collagens and cuticlins, line the pharynx and build the eggshell with **chitin**.

A cuticle protects, supports and gives muscles something rigid to pull on, but it cannot grow once it is hardened. Ecdysozoans therefore grow in steps: the epidermis detaches from the old cuticle (apolysis), secretes a new epicuticle beneath it, digests most of the old procuticle with molting fluid and resorbs the products, and finally the animal splits the remains of the old cuticle along preformed lines and crawls out (ecdysis), swallowing water or air to expand the new, still soft cuticle before it hardens. The developmental and endocrine control of **molting** is treated in [7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.1); the arthropod molting cycle, where it reaches its greatest complexity, is in [7.17.18](/7.17.18).

The cuticle explains the second ecdysozoan feature: the absence of locomotory cilia on the body surface. A ciliated epithelium cannot be covered by a cuticle, and ecdysozoans have no ciliated larva, no ciliated gliding sole and no spiral cleavage. Cilia survive only inside sensory organs, such as the sensilla of arthropods and the amphids of nematodes, where they have lost their motility.

### 17.1.2. The Ecdysozoa Hypothesis

For most of the twentieth century zoologists followed Cuvier in uniting annelids and arthropods as the Articulata, on the strength of their segmented bodies, the paired ventral nerve cord and the growth of segments from a posterior zone. Nematodes and the other small worms with a body cavity not lined by mesoderm were placed in a separate grade, the "Aschelminthes" or **pseudocoel** animals, below the coelomates.

In 1997 Aguinaldo and colleagues found, with 18S rRNA sequences from a slowly evolving nematode, that nematodes group with arthropods and that this clade, together with priapulans and kinorhynchs, is separate from annelids and molluscs. They called it Ecdysozoa. Phylogenomic analyses with hundreds of genes have supported it ever since, and the morphological case was found to be strong once looked for:

| Character | Ecdysozoa | Articulata (annelids + arthropods) |
|---|---|---|
| Molted, three-layered cuticle | all eight phyla | annelids do not molt |
| Locomotory epidermal cilia | absent | present in annelids |
| Larva | no ciliated larva | trochophore in annelids |
| Cleavage | never spiral | spiral in annelids |
| Segmentation | in arthropods, kinorhynchs (and onychophorans, tardigrades) | annelids and arthropods |
| Molecular data | strongly supported | rejected |

Segmentation, the main argument for the Articulata, is then either convergent in annelids and arthropods or inherited from a much older bilaterian ancestor and lost many times; the developmental evidence is discussed in [7.17.18](/7.17.18) and [7.17.3](/7.17.3).

Within the Ecdysozoa three clades are well characterized (see the figure below). *Scalidophora* comprises animals with an eversible introvert armed with scalids; *Nematoida* (nematodes and horsehair worms) comprises worms with only longitudinal body-wall muscles and a collagenous adult cuticle; Panarthropoda comprises animals with paired, clawed limbs. How the three are related is not settled. Morphologists have often united Scalidophora and Nematoida as *Cycloneuralia*, for their ring-shaped brain around the pharynx, but most molecular analyses do not recover it and sometimes place Nematoida as the sister group of Panarthropoda or priapulans as the sister group of all other ecdysozoans.

![Relationships of the eight ecdysozoan phyla. The molted cuticle and the loss of locomotory cilia are synapomorphies of Ecdysozoa; Scalidophora (introvert with scalids), Nematoida (body-wall muscles only longitudinal) and Panarthropoda (paired, clawed limbs) are each well supported, but their mutual relationships remain unresolved and are drawn as a polytomy.](./plots/ecdysozoa-tree.py)

The fossil record shows that ecdysozoans were among the first bilaterians to leave traces. The trace fossil *Treptichnus pedum*, a branching burrow whose first appearance marks the base of the Cambrian about 539 million years ago, was probably made by a priapulan-like worm, and Cambrian deposits such as the Burgess Shale and Chengjiang contain abundant priapulan-like worms (*Ottoia*, *Selkirkia*) and armored "palaeoscolecids", many of them stem-group scalidophorans or stem-group ecdysozoans. The **Cambrian explosion** ([7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.4)) was to a large extent an ecdysozoan radiation.

### 17.1.3. Are Ecdysozoan Molting Hormones Homologous?

If molting is an ecdysozoan synapomorphy, is its endocrine control homologous too? In arthropods molting is triggered by the steroid **ecdysone**, converted to 20-hydroxyecdysone, which binds a nuclear receptor (EcR with its partner USP) and starts a cascade of further nuclear receptors, among them DHR3 and βFTZ-F1 ([7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.3)). Nematodes do not use ecdysteroids: *C. elegans* cannot synthesize sterols at all, needs dietary cholesterol, and has no EcR. Its molts are nevertheless driven by nuclear receptors, and the two essential ones, NHR-23 and NHR-25, are the orthologs of DHR3 and FTZ-F1.

The most likely interpretation is that the downstream nuclear-receptor cascade that coordinates apolysis and cuticle synthesis is ancient and shared, whereas the ligand at its top differs between lineages. For scalidophorans the question is open: kinorhynchs and priapulans molt, but whether ecdysteroids are involved has not been shown. Homology of a process does not require homology of every molecule in it, a point that returns in the evolution of insect metamorphosis ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)).

## 17.2. Scalidophora

### 17.2.1. Introvert and Scalids

Scalidophorans are marine worms that live in or on sediment. The name refers to the scalids, cuticular spines on the introvert that are triradiate in section at some stage and carry monociliated sensory cells. The introvert itself is the anterior part of the body, which can be turned inside out and drawn back by retractor muscles; repeatedly everted and retracted, it anchors the animal and pulls it through the sediment, and in priapulans it also catches prey. Other shared features are introvert retractor muscles attached through very long epidermal cells (tanycytes), **protonephridia** with a compound filter built by several terminal cells, and flosculi, small sensory spots surrounded by cuticular micropapillae.

The scalidophoran brain is a collar around the pharynx, from which a ventral nerve cord runs back, and the three phyla share a mouth cone or buccal tube armed with cuticular teeth. Their body cavity is a fluid-filled space between body wall and gut; in priapulans it is lined by a noncellular membrane and not by a mesodermal epithelium, so these animals are no longer counted as coelomates.

### 17.2.2. Phylum Priapulida

**Definition 17.2.1 (Priapulida)** **Priapulida** is a phylum of about 20 living species of cylindrical, unsegmented, superficially annulated marine worms with a large, spiny introvert, a muscular pharynx with cuticular teeth and, in many species, one or two posterior caudal appendages; the larva lives in a cuticular lorica that is shed at the molt to the adult.

Large priapulans such as *Priapulus caudatus* and *Halicryptus spinulosus* (up to about 20 cm) live in cold, oxygen-poor muds of the North Atlantic and the Arctic and Antarctic seas, burrowing by peristalsis and introvert movements and seizing polychaetes and other worms with the everted pharyngeal teeth. Meiofaunal priapulans such as *Tubiluchus* and *Meiopriapulus* are millimeter-sized and live between sand grains. The sexes are separate; fertilization is external, cleavage is radial-like, and the blastopore gives rise to the mouth, a reminder that "protostomy" in the literal sense is not universal among protostomes ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)).

Today priapulans are a relict group. In the Cambrian they were among the commonest large predators of the sea floor: *Ottoia prolifica* of the Burgess Shale is preserved with the remains of hyolithids and of other *Ottoia* in its gut.

### 17.2.3. Phylum Kinorhyncha

**Definition 17.2.2 (Kinorhyncha)** **Kinorhyncha** is a phylum of about 300 species of marine meiofaunal animals, nearly all under 1 mm long, whose body consists of a head (a mouth cone with oral styles and an introvert bearing rings of spinoscalids), a neck of closing plates (placids) and a trunk of 11 segments, each formed by cuticular plates (one tergal and usually two sternal), with segmentally arranged muscles, nerves and glands.

Kinorhynchs ("movable snouts", or mud dragons) crawl through mud by extending the head, anchoring it with the spinoscalids and drawing the trunk forward; when disturbed they retract the head into the trunk and close it with the placids. They are one of only three animal groups with segmentation of both the cuticle and the internal organs, the others being annelids and panarthropods. They develop directly through six juvenile stages separated by molts, and the trunk segments are added during this growth. Two classes are recognized, Cyclorhagida and Allomalorhagida, distinguished by the number and form of the placids.

### 17.2.4. Phylum Loricifera

**Definition 17.2.3 (Loricifera)** **Loricifera** is a phylum of minute (mostly under 0.5 mm) marine meiofaunal animals, discovered in 1983, whose head bears up to nine rings of scalids around a telescoping mouth cone and whose abdomen is enclosed in a lorica, a girdle of cuticular plates or folds into which the head and neck can be withdrawn.

Reinhardt Kristensen described the first species, *Nanaloricus mysticus*, from coarse shell gravel; loriciferans cling so tightly to sediment grains that they are dislodged only by flushing samples with fresh water. About 40 species have been named and many more are known from samples. Their life cycles are among the most complex in the animal kingdom: a larva with locomotory toes (the Higgins larva) molts several times, and some species add a paedogenetic generation in which larvae or postlarvae produce eggs parthenogenetically.

In 2010 Danovaro and colleagues reported three species of *Spinoloricus*, *Rugiloricus* and *Pliciloricus* living in the permanently anoxic, sulfidic sediments of the L'Atalante basin of the Mediterranean, more than 3 km deep, with mitochondria replaced by hydrogenosome-like organelles. If confirmed, these would be the first animals to complete their life cycle without oxygen. Later work questioned whether the specimens had been alive in the anoxic sediment rather than sunk into it as carcasses, and the claim is still debated.

## 17.3. Phylum Nematoda: The Body Plan

### 17.3.1. The Most Abundant Animals

**Definition 17.3.1 (Nematoda)** **Nematoda** is a phylum of unsegmented, bilaterally symmetrical, cylindrical worms tapered at both ends, with a collagenous cuticle molted four times during development, a body wall containing only longitudinal muscle cells that send processes (muscle arms) to the nerve cords, a fluid-filled pseudocoel under high pressure, a complete gut with a triradiate muscular pharynx, a circumpharyngeal nerve ring, and no motile cilia, circular muscles or circulatory and respiratory organs.

About 25 000 species have been described, but estimates of the real number run from several hundred thousand to over a million. Nematodes are found wherever there is liquid water: marine sediments, fresh water, soil, hot springs, the deep subsurface and the bodies of nearly every plant and animal. A square meter of soil commonly holds millions of individuals, and a global survey of soils estimated about $4.4 \times 10^{20}$ nematodes in the topsoil of the world, with a biomass of roughly 0.3 Gt (van den Hoogen et al. 2019); by number, most animals on Earth are nematodes.

The phylum is remarkably uniform in body plan for its ecological range. Free-living nematodes are mostly 0.1–3 mm long and feed on bacteria, fungi, algae or other small animals; parasites range from microscopic forms to *Dioctophyme renale* of the dog kidney (up to 1 m) and *Placentonema gigantissima* of the sperm whale placenta (about 8 m). The differences between them lie mainly in the mouth (lips, teeth, stylets) and in the life cycle.

### 17.3.2. Cuticle, Hypodermis and Muscle

The cuticle of a nematode is made mainly of collagens, cross-linked by tyrosine bonds, together with insoluble cuticlins. In *Ascaris* it has several layers, two or three of which consist of fibers running in helices around the body that cross each other at about 75° to the long axis. This arrangement makes the cuticle almost inextensible lengthwise, while letting it bend and change its shape slightly (Section 17.3.3).

Beneath the cuticle the epidermis, here called the hypodermis, is in most species a syncytium. It bulges inward along four longitudinal ridges, the dorsal, ventral and two lateral cords, which contain the nuclei of the hypodermis; the dorsal and ventral cords carry the main nerve cords, and the lateral cords carry the excretory canals (see the figure below). Between the cords lie the body-wall muscles in four quadrants.

![Cross-section of a female nematode (schematic, not to scale, after *Ascaris*). From the outside in: the collagenous cuticle; the syncytial hypodermis, thickened into dorsal and ventral cords (each with a nerve cord) and two lateral cords (each with an excretory canal); the longitudinal muscle cells in four quadrants, each with an outer contractile part (pink strip) and an inner cytoplasmic bag from which a thin muscle arm runs to the dorsal or ventral nerve cord; the pseudocoel, filled with fluid under pressure, containing the reproductive tubes; and the intestine, a single layer of endodermal cells around the lumen. There are no circular muscles.](./plots/nematode-section.py)

There are only longitudinal muscles. Each muscle cell is spindle-shaped, with its obliquely striated contractile part against the hypodermis and a large cytoplasmic bag containing the nucleus and glycogen facing the pseudocoel. Nematode muscle has a feature that is rare among animals (it is also known in some flatworms and echinoderms): instead of motor axons growing to the muscle, each muscle cell sends one or more muscle arms to the dorsal or ventral nerve cord and forms synapses there. Excitatory inputs are cholinergic and inhibitory inputs are GABAergic; the anthelmintics levamisole and pyrantel act on a nematode-specific acetylcholine receptor and ivermectin on glutamate-gated chloride channels ([7.14.8](/7.14.8#sec-8.8.4)).

### 17.3.3. A Pressurized Hydrostatic Skeleton

The fluid in the pseudocoel is the nematode's skeleton. Because the cuticle cannot stretch and the muscles are always partly contracted, the fluid is under pressure: Harris and Crofton (1957) measured a mean of about 9 kPa (70 mmHg) in *Ascaris lumbricoides*, with peaks over 25 kPa during movement, roughly ten times the values recorded in most other soft-bodied invertebrates. Contraction of the dorsal or ventral muscles cannot shorten the worm; the force is transmitted through the fluid to the opposite side, the body bends, and when the muscles relax the elastic cuticle straightens it again. Alternating contraction waves running along the body give the characteristic dorsoventral undulation, in which the worm lies on its side and throws its body into S-shaped waves.

Why such a body bends instead of shortening can be seen from the geometry of a cylinder wound with inextensible fibers (Clark and Cowey 1958). If a fiber of length $D$ makes $n$ turns around a cylinder at angle $\theta$ to its long axis, the cylinder has length and circumference

$$
L = D\cos\theta, \qquad 2\pi r = \frac{D\sin\theta}{n},
$$
so that its largest possible volume is

$$
V_{\max}(\theta) = \pi r^2 L = \frac{D^3}{4\pi n^2}\,\sin^2\theta\,\cos\theta .
$$

$V_{\max}$ peaks at $\tan\theta = \sqrt 2$, $\theta \approx 54.7^\circ$. A worm whose actual volume is below $V_{\max}$ can take any fiber angle by flattening its cross-section, and so can lengthen and shorten freely; flatworms and nemerteans work this way. A nematode is circular in section, so its constant fluid volume equals $V_{\max}$ at its fiber angle of about 75°. Shortening would increase $\theta$ and decrease $V_{\max}$ below the volume of incompressible fluid inside, which is impossible; muscle pull is converted into pressure and, if it acts on one side only, into bending (see the figure below).

![The helically wound cylinder model. Blue: the largest volume that a cylinder wound with inextensible fibers can enclose, relative to its maximum at 54.7°; orange, dashed: its length relative to the fiber length. A body below the blue curve has a flattened section and can change length freely, as in flatworms and nemerteans. The cuticle fibers of *Ascaris* run at about 75° and its section is round, so the worm lies on the curve (red dot): shortening would require more volume than the cuticle can enclose, and longitudinal muscle contraction raises the internal pressure and bends the body instead. The same pressure explains several other features of nematodes. The pharynx is a powerful triradiate pump that must force food into a gut that the surrounding pressure tends to collapse, and the gut empties through the anus by pressure when the anal muscles open it. The pseudocoel fluid also carries nutrients, oxygen and wastes, replacing a circulatory system. The model and its parameters (fiber angle, number of turns) show how a single material property of the cuticle constrains the whole locomotor system, as the comparison of muscle and skeleton types in [7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.6.2) also emphasizes.

### 17.3.4. Gut, Excretion, Nervous System and Sense Organs

The gut is a straight tube. The mouth, usually surrounded by three or six lips with sensory papillae, opens into a buccal cavity armed in some groups with teeth or a hollow stylet; the pharynx (esophagus) is muscular, triradiate in section and often has bulbs with valves; the intestine is a single layer of endodermal cells with microvilli, and the short rectum is lined by cuticle. Digestion is mainly extracellular. Most parasites in the gut are not anaerobic in a strict sense: adult *Ascaris* has a functional respiratory chain adapted to low oxygen and switches to fumarate reduction when oxygen is scarce.

The excretory system has no flame cells and no cilia, and is therefore not a **protonephridium** in the usual sense. In primitive marine species it is one or two large gland cells (renette cells) opening by a ventral pore near the anterior end; in *Ascaris* and many parasites the renette has become an H-shaped system of intracellular canals running in the lateral cords and joined by a transverse canal near the excretory pore. Its main role is osmoregulation; nitrogen is lost largely as ammonia across the body wall and gut.

The central nervous system is a nerve ring around the pharynx with associated ganglia, from which longitudinal cords run back in the hypodermal cords, the ventral cord carrying most of the motor neurons. The main sense organs are the *amphids*, a pair of pockets at the anterior end lined with sensory cilia that detect chemicals and temperature, labial papillae, and in many groups a pair of phasmids, similar posterior chemoreceptors near the tail. Amphids are the basis of the chemotaxis studied so intensively in *C. elegans*.

### 17.3.5. Reproduction and Development

Most nematodes have separate sexes, with males usually smaller than females and having a curved tail. The gonads are long tubes coiled in the pseudocoel: in a female *Ascaris* the paired ovaries, oviducts and uteri join in a short vagina opening at a vulva about a third of the way back from the anterior end; in the male the single testis, sperm duct and seminal vesicle open into a cloaca, from which a pair of copulatory spicules is protruded to open the female's vulva during mating. Nematode sperm have no flagellum: they crawl like amoebae, using a pseudopod driven by polymerization of the major sperm protein (MSP) instead of actin.

A female *Ascaris* may contain 27 million eggs and lay about 200 000 a day. Development is direct: the juvenile hatches with the adult body plan and grows through four juvenile stages, J1 (L1) to J4, separated by four molts. In most species the somatic cell number is fixed early, a condition called *eutely*, and growth after hatching is by cell enlargement, not division; *C. elegans* is the extreme case, with every somatic division known (Section 17.4).

## 17.4. *Caenorhabditis elegans*

The soil nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*, about 1 mm long, was chosen by Sydney Brenner in the 1960s as a **model organism** for genetics of the nervous system. Its advantages are a short life cycle (about 3.5 days at 20 °C), hermaphrodites that self-fertilize, so that recessive mutations become homozygous without crosses, males for crosses, a transparent body, and freezability.

The adult hermaphrodite has 959 somatic nuclei and the male 1031. John Sulston and colleagues traced the complete **cell lineage** of both from the zygote under the microscope: it is invariant, so each cell has a name and a known history ([7.5.6](/7.5.6#sec-6.4)). In the hermaphrodite lineage 131 cells die by programmed cell death, which led to the discovery of the core genes of **apoptosis** (Nobel Prize 2002, Brenner, Horvitz and Sulston). The hermaphrodite nervous system of 302 neurons was the first whose complete wiring diagram was reconstructed from serial electron micrographs (White et al. 1986). *C. elegans* was also the first animal whose genome was sequenced (1998, about 100 Mb with some 20 000 protein-coding genes), and it was in this worm that **RNA interference** (Fire and Mello, Nobel Prize 2006), the first **microRNA** (*lin-4*, Ambros and Ruvkun, Nobel Prize 2024) and the use of GFP as a reporter in vivo (Chalfie, Nobel Prize 2008) were discovered.

The life cycle has a branch point that links development to ecology. Under good conditions the worm passes from L1 to L4 and adulthood. Crowding, signaled by a blend of secreted ascarosides (dauer pheromone), lack of food or high temperature during L1 instead divert the L2 into an alternative third stage, the *dauer larva*, which does not feed, has a sealed mouth and a thickened cuticle, is resistant to desiccation and can survive for months; when conditions improve it molts to a normal L4. The decision is controlled by the nuclear receptor DAF-12 and its steroid ligand dafachronic acid, a molecular logic comparable to that of the insect molting hormones (Section 17.1.3). Dauer-like resting larvae are also the infective stages of many parasitic nematodes.

## 17.5. Parasitic Nematodes

Parasitism has evolved independently many times in nematodes, by most counts more than a dozen times in all, both for parasites of animals and of plants. A few species are among the most widespread parasites of humans: soil-transmitted helminths alone (roundworm, whipworm and hookworms) infect about 1.5 billion people, a quarter of the world's population, mostly children in poor countries. The general concepts of transmission are in [7.11.35](/7.11.35); here we describe the life cycles that a zoologist must know.

### 17.5.1. *Ascaris lumbricoides*

*Ascaris lumbricoides*, the large roundworm of humans, is the representative nematode of Chinese textbooks. Females reach 20–35 cm, males 15–30 cm, with a tail curved ventrally; the mouth is surrounded by three lips (one dorsal and two ventrolateral) with papillae, and the cuticle shows a conspicuous lateral line on each side, the lateral cord seen through the body wall. The adults live in the small intestine and feed on its semidigested contents.

The life cycle is direct, with a single host, but includes a migration through the body (see the figure below). Eggs with a thick shell and an outer mammillated protein coat leave the host unembryonated in the feces; in moist, warm, oxygenated soil they develop in two to three weeks into infective eggs containing a third-stage larva, and they remain viable for years. When an infective egg is swallowed, the larva hatches in the small intestine, penetrates the gut wall and travels with the blood through the liver and heart to the lungs, where it grows and molts; it then breaks into the alveoli, is carried up the airways, swallowed, and matures in the small intestine about two to three months after infection. Heavy infections retard growth in children and can block the intestine or the bile duct, and many larvae passing through the lungs cause pneumonitis. The pig roundworm *A. suum* is morphologically almost identical and the two are probably one species or a very recent split; prevention depends on sanitation and the treatment of feces.

### 17.5.2. Hookworms, Pinworms, Whipworms and *Trichinella*

Hookworms, *Ancylostoma duodenale* and *Necator americanus*, attach to the wall of the small intestine with a buccal capsule armed with teeth (*Ancylostoma*) or cutting plates (*Necator*) and feed on blood; heavy infections cause iron-deficiency anemia. Their eggs hatch in soil, and the infective filariform L3 enters through the skin, usually of bare feet, and reaches the intestine by the same lung route as *Ascaris*.

The pinworm *Enterobius vermicularis* is the commonest worm of children in temperate countries. Females migrate out of the anus at night to lay eggs on the perianal skin; the itching leads to scratching, eggs on the fingers are swallowed, and the cycle is completed by direct reinfection, which is why whole families must be treated together. The whipworm *Trichuris trichiura* has a slender anterior part threaded into the mucosa of the large intestine and a thick posterior part.

*Trichinella spiralis* differs from all of these in that adults and larvae develop in the same host. Adults live briefly in the small intestine, and females give birth to larvae that travel in the blood to striated muscle, enter muscle fibers and transform them into nurse cells, in which the larva coils and remains infective for years. The cycle is completed only when another mammal eats the infected muscle; humans become infected by eating undercooked pork or game, and the disease (trichinellosis) is controlled by meat inspection and thorough cooking. Whipworms and *Trichinella* belong to a different nematode class from the other parasites of this section (Section 17.7).

### 17.5.3. Filariae

Filarial nematodes are slender worms of the tissues, blood and lymphatics, transmitted by blood-feeding insects. Lymphatic filariasis is caused by *Wuchereria bancrofti*, transmitted by *Culex*, *Anopheles* and *Aedes* mosquitoes, and by *Brugia malayi*, transmitted by *Mansonia* and *Anopheles*. The adults live coiled in the lymphatic vessels and nodes; the females release *microfilariae*, sheathed first-stage larvae, into the blood. In most strains the microfilariae show nocturnal periodicity, appearing in the peripheral blood at night when the vector bites and retreating to the lung capillaries by day. In the mosquito they develop in the thoracic muscles to the infective L3, which leaves the proboscis during the next blood meal and enters through the bite wound (see the figure above). Chronic infection damages the lymphatics and causes lymphedema, elephantiasis and hydrocele. In China, where both species were once widespread, the elimination of lymphatic filariasis was acknowledged by the World Health Organization in 2007, achieved through mass drug administration and vector control.

Most filariae harbor the endosymbiotic bacterium *Wolbachia*, which they need for development and fertility; the antibiotic doxycycline, by killing the bacteria, sterilizes and eventually kills the worms. Other filariae include *Onchocerca volvulus*, transmitted by blackflies (*Simulium*), whose microfilariae migrate in the skin and eye and cause river blindness; its control with ivermectin earned Campbell and Ōmura the 2015 Nobel Prize. The guinea worm *Dracunculus medinensis*, a related spirurid transmitted through copepods in drinking water, is close to eradication, with fewer than twenty human cases a year in the 2020s.

### 17.5.4. Plant-Parasitic Nematodes

Plant-parasitic nematodes feed with a hollow, protrusible stylet through which they inject secretions and withdraw cell contents. Root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne*) induce a few root cells to become multinucleate, metabolically very active giant cells and the surrounding tissue to form galls; cyst nematodes (*Heterodera*, *Globodera*) induce a syncytium, and the female body hardens after death into a cyst that protects hundreds of eggs in the soil for years. Together these sedentary endoparasites cause losses of a significant fraction of world crop production. The wheat seed gall nematode *Anguina tritici* replaces developing grains with galls full of dried juveniles that can survive in anhydrobiosis for decades. The pine wood nematode *Bursaphelenchus xylophilus*, native to North America, causes pine wilt in East Asia, where it is carried from tree to tree by longhorn beetles of the genus *Monochamus*; it has killed hundreds of millions of pines in China and Japan. Some soil dorylaimids, such as *Xiphinema*, transmit plant viruses.

## 17.6. Phylum Nematomorpha

**Definition 17.6.1 (Nematomorpha)** **Nematomorpha** is a phylum of about 350 species of very long, thin worms whose larvae are parasites of arthropods and whose free-living adults have a collagenous cuticle, only longitudinal body-wall muscles, a reduced, often nonfunctional gut and a single ventral nerve cord, and live only to reproduce.

The horsehair worms, or gordian worms, owe their names to the old belief that they arose from horsehairs fallen into water, and to the knots in which males and females twist together when mating. Adults of the freshwater Gordiida are 10 cm to over 1 m long but only 1–3 mm thick; the marine *Nectonema*, with rows of swimming bristles, parasitizes decapod crustaceans. Eggs laid in water hatch into a minute larva with an eversible proboscis armed with stylets, which enters an aquatic insect larva and often encysts there; when a terrestrial arthropod such as a cricket, grasshopper, mantis or beetle eats the transport host, the larva grows in its hemocoel to fill much of the body.

The mature worm must return to water, and it does so by changing the behavior of its host. Crickets infected by *Spinochordodes tellinii* seek out and jump into water, where the worm emerges within minutes and the host often drowns (Thomas et al. 2002); proteomic studies have found changes in host neural proteins and worm proteins resembling those of the host, but the mechanism is not fully understood. Manipulation of this kind is one of the clearest examples of a parasite's genes acting through the behavior of another organism, a theme taken up in [7.17.39](/7.17.39).

Nematomorphs and nematodes share the collagenous adult cuticle, body-wall musculature of longitudinal cells only, longitudinal epidermal cords, sperm without the standard flagellum in some species, and a cloaca in both sexes; molecular data unite them as the clade Nematoida.

## 17.7. Classification and the Traditional "Pseudocoelomates"

The modern classification of nematodes, based largely on small-subunit rRNA (De Ley and Blaxter 2002) and confirmed by phylogenomics, recognizes three major lineages often ranked as classes. Chinese textbooks still use the older division by the presence of phasmids:

| Older system (Chinese textbooks) | Modern lineage | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Adenophorea (= Aphasmida): no phasmids, glandular excretory system | Enoplia | many marine free-living nematodes; *Enoplus* |
| | Dorylaimia | *Trichinella*, *Trichuris*, *Xiphinema*, mermithids |
| Secernentea (= Phasmida): phasmids, canal-type excretory system | Chromadorea | *C. elegans*, *Ascaris*, hookworms, pinworms, filariae, *Meloidogyne*, *Heterodera*, *Bursaphelenchus* |

Secernentea is a natural group nested within Chromadorea; Adenophorea, defined by the absence of phasmids, is a **grade** and is not used today.

The "pseudocoelomates" of Chinese textbooks are split between distant branches of the tree:

| Phylum in the pseudocoelomate chapter | Modern position | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Nematoda, Nematomorpha | Ecdysozoa: Nematoida | this chapter |
| Kinorhyncha, Loricifera, Priapulida | Ecdysozoa: Scalidophora | this chapter |
| Rotifera, Acanthocephala | Spiralia: Gnathifera (acanthocephalans are parasitic rotifers) | [7.17.12](/7.17.12) |
| Gastrotricha | Spiralia: Rouphozoa | [7.17.11](/7.17.11) |
| Entoprocta, Cycliophora | Spiralia: Lophotrochozoa | [7.17.16](/7.17.16) |

The pseudocoel is therefore not a single invention but a condition that has arisen in small animals of several lineages, by retention of the blastocoel or by reduction of a coelom; the body cavity types are compared in [7.17.3](/7.17.3). What the textbook pseudocoelomates share, small size, a cuticle and eutely, are largely the consequences of being small. The traditional chapter is best read as a description of a grade, and the modern system explains why kinorhynchs, priapulans and nematodes molt, while rotifers and gastrotrichs do not.

## Questions

1. Why can a ciliated epithelium not coexist with a molted cuticle on the body surface, and what does this imply for the larvae of ecdysozoans?

   **Answer** Motile cilia must project into the surrounding water to beat; a cuticle secreted over the epidermis covers the cell surface and would bury them, and molting would tear them away. Ecdysozoans therefore move by muscles acting on the cuticle or on limbs, and their cilia survive only inside sense organs, where they are immotile. For the same reason no ecdysozoan has a ciliated swimming larva such as the trochophore: their larvae (the nauplius of crustaceans, the loricate larva of priapulans, the Higgins larva of loriciferans) are cuticularized and move with muscles and appendages.

2. Using the fiber-angle model, compute $V_{\max}(75^\circ)/V_{\max}(54.7^\circ)$, and explain why a nemertean, whose fiber angle changes over a wide range as it moves, can shorten to a small fraction of its length.

   **Answer** $\sin^2\theta\cos\theta$ is $0.933 \times 0.259 = 0.2415$ at 75° and $2/(3\sqrt3) = 0.385$ at 54.7°, so the ratio is about 0.63. A round nematode at 75° thus contains only 63 % of the largest volume its cuticle could enclose at the optimal angle, and moving further from 54.7° (shortening, $\theta$ increasing) would lower $V_{\max}$ below its fixed volume. A nemertean's body volume lies well below the curve at most angles, because its section can flatten: as it shortens, the fiber angle increases toward 80° or more, and the body becomes wider and flatter while keeping its volume, so large changes in length are possible.

3. Compare the life cycles of *Ascaris lumbricoides*, *Enterobius vermicularis* and *Wuchereria bancrofti* with respect to the number of hosts, the infective stage and the route of infection, and derive from this the most effective control measure for each.

   **Answer** *Ascaris* has one host; the infective stage is the embryonated egg containing an L3, which must mature in soil for weeks and is swallowed with contaminated food or soil, so sanitation (safe disposal of feces) and deworming break the cycle. *Enterobius* has one host and no development outside it beyond a few hours; eggs laid on the perianal skin are infective almost at once and are transmitted by fingers, clothing and bedding, often as direct reinfection, so simultaneous treatment of all family members and hygiene are needed. *Wuchereria* has two hosts; the infective L3 develops only in a mosquito and enters through the bite, so mass drug administration to remove microfilariae from the human population combined with mosquito control is effective, as in China's elimination program.

4. Kinorhynchs are segmented and molt; annelids are segmented and do not molt. Which of the two characters is a better guide to phylogeny here, and why?

   **Answer** Molting is the better guide. The molted three-layered cuticle is a complex character shared by all eight ecdysozoan phyla, consistent with molecular phylogenies, and it involves an orthologous nuclear-receptor cascade even where the hormones differ. Segmentation, by contrast, is distributed patchily (annelids among spiralians; kinorhynchs, onychophorans, tardigrades and arthropods among ecdysozoans), differs in its development between groups (kinorhynch segments are added during juvenile molts, annelid segments from a posterior growth zone), and conflicts with the molecular evidence, so it is best interpreted as convergent or as a very ancient feature lost many times. The Articulata grouping based on segmentation is rejected.

5. (Open question) The dauer larva of *C. elegans* and the infective L3 of many parasitic nematodes are both nonfeeding, stress-resistant third-stage larvae. Suggest how parasitism might have evolved from a free-living ancestor with a dauer stage, and what molecular evidence would test the idea.

   **Answer** Idea: a free-living nematode whose dauer larvae disperse by clinging to insects or other animals (phoresy, common in rhabditids) could begin to enter the host through openings or wounds and develop inside it, first facultatively, then obligately; the dauer would become the infective stage that resumes development when it senses host cues instead of food. Tests: if the hypothesis is right, the exit from arrest in infective larvae of parasites (hookworms, *Strongyloides*) should be controlled by the same pathway as dauer exit, namely insulin-like signaling and DAF-12 with its dafachronic acid ligand; treating infective larvae with dafachronic acid should trigger activation, and blocking the pathway should prevent it. Experiments in *Strongyloides* and hookworms have in fact shown such effects, supporting the "dauer hypothesis" of the origin of parasitism.
