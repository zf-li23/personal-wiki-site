This chapter continues the molluscs with the three remaining classes of the Conchifera ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.2.2)). Bivalves and cephalopods are the two extremes of the molluscan body plan. The bivalves have given up the head and the radula, enclosed the body between two valves and turned the ctenidia into a filter: they are sedentary suspension feeders, burrowers and borers, among the most important animals of coastal sediments and the most heavily farmed animals of the sea. The cephalopods have turned the foot into arms and a funnel, the mantle into a jet engine and the shell into a buoyancy device, and have evolved a closed circulation, camera eyes and the largest brains of all invertebrates. Between them, the small class of tusk shells shows a third solution, burrowing with a tubular shell.

The concepts of the molluscan body plan (mantle, mantle cavity, ctenidium, radula), the shell layers and the veliger larva were introduced in [7.17.13](/7.17.13) and [7.17.5](/7.17.5). The chapter treats the bivalves (§14.1–14.3), the tusk shells (§14.4), the cephalopods (§14.5–14.8) and the fossil record of molluscs (§14.9), and ends with a comparison with the traditional classification (§14.10).

## 14.1. The Bivalve Body Plan

**Definition 14.1.1 (Bivalvia)** The **Bivalvia** (bivalves: clams, mussels, oysters and scallops) are laterally compressed molluscs enclosed in a shell of two valves, left and right, joined dorsally by an elastic ligament and usually by interlocking hinge teeth and closed by one or two adductor muscles; the head is reduced, without radula, eyes or tentacles; the mantle lobes line the valves and enclose a large mantle cavity containing one pair of ctenidia, usually enlarged for filter feeding; the foot is laterally compressed and used for burrowing.

There are nearly 10,000 living species, in the sea at all depths and in fresh water.

### 14.1.1. Shell, Hinge and Ligament

The two valves are secreted by the right and left mantle lobes. The umbo (beak), the oldest part of each valve, lies on the dorsal margin and usually points forward; around it run the commarginal growth lines. Along the dorsal margin the hinge plate bears teeth and sockets that interlock and keep the valves aligned. The arrangement of the teeth is used in classification: many similar teeth in a row (taxodont, in arks and nut clams), a few cardinal teeth below the umbo and elongate lateral teeth (heterodont, in most clams), or teeth reduced or absent (in mussels and the river mussel *Anodonta*, whose name means "toothless").

The valves are opened by the ligament, an elastic structure of tanned protein (and, internally, calcium carbonate fibres) that lies behind the umbo. When the adductors contract and close the valves, they compress an internal part of the ligament or stretch an external part; when they relax, the ligament springs back and the valves gape. A dead clam therefore lies open. The ligament and the adductors are antagonists, like a spring and a muscle acting on a lever with the hinge as its fulcrum.

**Formula 14.1.1 (Balance of Adductor and Ligament)** Let the ligament exert a torque $\tau_{\mathrm{L}}(\phi)$ about the hinge axis, increasing as the gape angle $\phi$ decreases, and let the adductor act at a distance $r_{\mathrm{A}}$ from the hinge axis with force $F_{\mathrm{A}}$. The valves stay closed when

$$
F_{\mathrm{A}}\, r_{\mathrm{A}} \ge \tau_{\mathrm{L}}(0),
\tag{14.1}
$$
and at rest they open to the angle at which $\tau_{\mathrm{L}}(\phi)$ falls to zero or is balanced by the resistance of the sediment.

:::

Because the adductors lie far from the hinge relative to the ligament, they work with a long lever arm: a moderate force holds the valves shut against a stiff ligament, and conversely a predator such as a sea star must pull with sustained force to open a clam. The adductor muscles contain two parts: a fast, striated part that closes the valves quickly (in scallops, the swimming muscle) and a smooth "catch" part that holds them closed for hours with very little ATP consumption ([7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.6.4)). In scallops the internal ligament contains *abductin*, a rubber-like protein that returns about 90% of the stored elastic energy, so that each closing stroke stores the energy for the next opening.

Many bivalves have two adductors of equal size (the anterior and posterior adductors of *Anodonta* and most clams). In mussels the anterior adductor is reduced, and in oysters and scallops it has disappeared, leaving a single large posterior adductor near the centre of the shell, a sequence associated with life attached to the substrate. Scars of the adductors, the foot muscles and the attachment of the mantle margin (the pallial line) can be seen inside an empty shell; an indentation of the pallial line, the pallial sinus, marks the space into which siphons are withdrawn and indicates how deeply a fossil species burrowed.

### 14.1.2. Mantle, Siphons and Ctenidia

The mantle lobes are free along the ventral margin in primitive bivalves but are fused to various degrees in most, leaving openings for the foot, an inhalant and an exhalant aperture. In burrowing species the posterior apertures are drawn out into siphons that reach the surface of the sediment while the animal stays buried; the long siphons of the soft-shell clam *Mya* and the geoduck *Panopea* cannot be withdrawn completely into the shell.

The ctenidia are the bivalves' feeding and respiratory organs. Their evolution from small bipectinate gills to large filtering sheets produced four types:

| Type | Structure | Feeding | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protobranch | Small bipectinate gills with short flat leaflets, like the ancestral ctenidium | Mainly respiratory; feeding by palp proboscides that collect deposits from the sediment | Nut clams *Nucula*, *Yoldia*; *Solemya* |
| Filibranch | Each leaflet a long filament folded into a V (W for the two sides of a gill); adjacent filaments joined only by tufts of interlocking cilia | Suspension feeding | Mussels *Mytilus*, arks *Arca*, scallops, pearl oysters, true oysters |
| Eulamellibranch | Filaments joined by tissue bridges into perforated sheets (lamellae), the two lamellae of each demibranch connected by interlamellar junctions into water tubes | Suspension feeding | Most clams; freshwater mussels *Anodonta*, *Hyriopsis* |
| Septibranch | Gills reduced to a muscular, perforated horizontal septum that pumps water | Carnivory: small crustaceans sucked in through the inhalant siphon | *Cuspidaria*, *Poromya* (deep sea) |

The filibranch and eulamellibranch ctenidia differ in the connections between filaments, not in their basic shape. Each demibranch is a W-shaped sheet hanging from the gill axis, with a food groove along its ventral margin.

### 14.1.3. Filter Feeding

A suspension-feeding bivalve pumps water through its gills with the lateral cilia of the filaments. Particles are intercepted at the face of the gill, where cirri of compound cilia (laterofrontal cirri) flick them onto the frontal surface, and frontal cilia carry them, bound in mucus, to the food groove at the margin of the demibranch and forward to the labial palps. The palps, two pairs of ciliated flaps beside the mouth, sort the particles by size and quality: acceptable particles go to the mouth, the rest are bound in mucus and rejected as pseudofaeces. In the stomach the crystalline style ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.1.3)) and ciliary sorting fields complete the processing. A mussel 5 cm long pumps several litres of water per hour and retains nearly all particles larger than about 4 µm, which makes beds of mussels and oysters major filters of coastal waters; the oyster reefs of Chesapeake Bay are estimated to have filtered the volume of the bay in a few days before they were destroyed by overfishing in the nineteenth century. **Filter feeding** makes bivalves also accumulate pollutants and algal toxins, which is the basis of shellfish poisoning and of their use as monitors of water quality.

### 14.1.4. Foot and Burrowing

The foot of a burrowing bivalve is a muscular **hydrostatic skeleton** filled with hemolymph. Burrowing follows a cycle that Trueman (1966) called the dual-anchor mechanism:

1. With the valves pressed apart against the sediment (penetration anchor), the foot is extended downward by protractor muscles and by blood pressure.
2. The adductors contract, closing the valves; this forces blood from the visceral mass into the foot, whose tip swells to form a terminal anchor, and water from the mantle cavity loosens the sediment around the shell.
3. The pedal retractor muscles contract and pull the shell down toward the anchored foot.
4. The adductors relax, the ligament opens the valves to form a new penetration anchor, and the cycle repeats.

Razor clams (*Ensis*, and the Chinese razor clam *Sinonovacula constricta*), with long smooth shells and powerful feet, can burrow faster than a person can dig them out; they reduce the resistance of the sediment by fluidizing it around the shell.

### 14.1.5. Internal Organs

The gut begins at a mouth between the labial palps, without a buccal mass. The stomach with style sac and digestive gland, a coiled intestine in the visceral mass and foot, and a rectum that passes through the ventricle of the heart and opens near the exhalant aperture are typical. The heart has one ventricle and two atria, lies dorsally in the pericardium, and the paired kidneys open into the suprabranchial chamber. The nervous system is simple, with three pairs of ganglia: cerebropleural ganglia beside the mouth, pedal ganglia in the foot and visceral ganglia below the posterior adductor. Statocysts lie near the pedal ganglia; the osphradium is above the visceral ganglia; many bivalves have sensory tentacles and eyes on the mantle margin, which they use to detect shadows of approaching predators. Scallops have up to 200 eyes along the mantle edge, each with a concave mirror of guanine crystals behind the retina that focuses the image, an optical design unlike that of any other mollusc.

## 14.2. Modes of Life and Representative Bivalves

### 14.2.1. Diversity of Habits

Starting from a burrowing ancestor, bivalves have adopted several ways of life:

| Mode of life | Adaptations | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Shallow and deep burrowing | Smooth, often elongate shell; large foot; fused mantle margin and siphons | Cockles, venus clams *Meretrix* and *Ruditapes*, razor clams, *Mya* |
| Byssal attachment | A byssus of protein threads secreted by a gland in the foot; reduced anterior adductor | Mussels *Mytilus*, pearl oysters, ark clams |
| Cementation | One valve cemented to the substrate; single adductor; foot lost in adults | True oysters *Crassostrea*, *Ostrea*, jewel boxes |
| Swimming | Light shell; single large adductor; abductin in the ligament; mantle eyes | Scallops *Pecten*, *Chlamys farreri*, *Argopecten* |
| Boring in wood and rock | Shell reduced to rasping valves at the front; boring by rotation and chemical secretion | Shipworms *Teredo*, *Bankia*; piddocks *Pholas* |
| Photosymbiosis | Zooxanthellae in a hypertrophied mantle exposed to light | Giant clams *Tridacna* |
| Chemosymbiosis | Sulfur-oxidizing or methane-oxidizing bacteria in the gills; gut reduced | Lucinids, *Solemya*, deep-sea *Bathymodiolus* and *Calyptogena* |
| Parasitism, commensalism | Small size, brooding | *Entovalva* in the gut of sea cucumbers |

The byssal threads of mussels attach with adhesive proteins rich in the modified amino acid DOPA (3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine), which binds to wet surfaces and has inspired synthetic medical adhesives. Shipworms digest the wood they excavate with the help of cellulose-degrading bacteria housed in their gills, and have destroyed wooden ships and harbour pilings since antiquity. Giant clams farm symbiotic dinoflagellates in their mantle ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)) and reach more than 1 m and 200 kg.

Scallops are the only bivalves that swim well, and their swimming is a jet propulsion system built from the parts of an ordinary clam. Rapid contraction of the striated part of the single adductor claps the valves together; the mantle margins form a curtain (the velum) that closes the ventral gape, so water is expelled in two jets on either side of the hinge, and the animal moves with the gape forward, apparently "biting" its way through the water. The ligament with its abductin reopens the valves, and a cycle of four to five claps per second can be kept up for several seconds, enough to escape a sea star. Two species dominate Chinese scallop culture: the native Zhikong scallop *Chlamys farreri* and the bay scallop *Argopecten irradians*, introduced from the east coast of North America in 1982 and now farmed on a scale far larger than in its native range.

### 14.2.2. The Freshwater Mussel: A Representative Bivalve

The representative bivalve of the Chinese textbooks is the freshwater mussel *Sinanodonta woodiana* (formerly *Anodonta woodiana*; Unionida), which lives buried in the mud of lakes and rivers with only its siphons exposed.

| System | Structure |
|---|---|
| Shell | Two equal oval valves, umbo near the anterior end; no hinge teeth; external black ligament; periostracum brown with growth lines |
| Muscles | Anterior and posterior adductors of equal size; anterior and posterior foot retractors and a protractor, all leaving scars inside the shell |
| Mantle and siphons | Two mantle lobes joined dorsally; posterior margins form a ventral inhalant aperture with sensory papillae and a dorsal smooth exhalant aperture |
| Feeding and digestion | Labial palps; stomach with crystalline style, gastric shield and sorting fields; digestive gland; intestine coiled in the visceral mass; rectum passing through the ventricle |
| Respiration | Two eulamellibranch demibranchs on each side; water passes through ostia into water tubes and the suprabranchial chamber; the outer demibranchs of the female serve as brood pouches (marsupia) |
| Circulation and excretion | Heart of one ventricle and two atria; open circulation with hemocyanin; a pair of U-shaped kidneys (organs of Bojanus) opening into the suprabranchial chamber; pericardial glands |
| Nervous system | Cerebropleural, pedal and visceral ganglia; statocysts; an osphradium over the visceral ganglia |
| Reproduction | Sexes separate; eggs fertilized in the outer demibranchs by sperm drawn in with the inhalant current; larvae brooded until they are released as glochidia |

### 14.2.3. The Glochidium and the Life Cycle of Freshwater Mussels

Freshwater mussels (Unionida) have solved the problem of dispersal upstream in rivers, where planktonic larvae would be washed away, with a parasitic larva.

**Definition 14.2.1 (Glochidium)** A **glochidium** is the parasitic larva of freshwater mussels (Unionida): a minute larva, usually 0.05–0.4 mm, with a bivalved shell and an adductor muscle, often with hooks on the valve margins, that attaches to the gills, fins or skin of a fish, becomes encysted in the host's tissue, feeds on it, and metamorphoses into a juvenile mussel that drops off.

The fertilized eggs develop in the brood chambers of the outer demibranchs, where in *Sinanodonta* the larvae overwinter. Released glochidia snap shut when they touch a fish; the host's epithelium grows around them and forms a cyst, in which the larva lives on the host's tissues for 2–5 weeks before it metamorphoses and leaves. The fish carries the young mussel upstream and spreads it through the river system. Many species attract their host actively: in *Lampsilis* the female's mantle margin forms a lure that resembles a small fish, and when a predatory fish strikes it the mussel releases a cloud of glochidia into its mouth. The relationship can be mutual: bitterlings (*Rhodeus*), small cyprinid fishes, lay their eggs into the mantle cavity of mussels through a long ovipositor, and the mussel's glochidia attach to the bitterling. Because they depend on particular host fishes, freshwater mussels are among the most threatened animals in the world; dams that block fish migration have caused the extinction of many species.

### 14.2.4. Reproduction and Larvae of Marine Bivalves

Most marine bivalves are gonochoristic broadcast spawners with very high fecundity: a Pacific oyster *Crassostrea gigas* (now often placed in *Magallana*) releases tens of millions of eggs in a spawning. Spawning is synchronized by temperature and by chemical cues: sperm in the water triggers spawning of females nearby. Fertilization is external, cleavage spiral, and the embryo passes through a trochophore within a day and becomes a **veliger** within one to two days. The first veliger, called the D-larva or straight-hinge larva from the outline of its shell, feeds on phytoplankton with the cilia of the velum; as it grows, the umbos of its two valves become prominent (umbo stage). After two to four weeks the larva develops a foot and an eyespot, becoming a *pediveliger*, which alternately swims with the velum and crawls with the foot, testing the substrate. Settlement is triggered by chemical and physical cues, among them the presence of adults of the same species and bacterial biofilms; at metamorphosis the velum is lost, the gills and adult organs develop, and oysters cement their left valve to the substrate. The newly settled juveniles (spat) of mussels often detach again, drift on long byssal threads and resettle elsewhere.

Sex in bivalves is more labile than in most animals. Many oysters are protandric, first male and later female, and *Crassostrea* can change sex in either direction between seasons according to food supply, an example of sex allocation adjusted to the larger reproductive return of large females ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)). Flat oysters of the genus *Ostrea* are simultaneous or alternating hermaphrodites that brood their larvae in the mantle cavity. Pteriomorphs and heterodonts in cold or deep water often brood, and most freshwater bivalves lack a free larva altogether (the zebra and golden mussels, members of families of brackish or marine origin, are exceptions): unionids have the glochidium (§14.2.3), and fingernail and pea clams (Sphaeriidae) brood their young in gill pouches until they are released as miniature adults. Hatcheries rear the larvae of oysters, scallops and clams in culture and supply most of the seed used in Chinese and Japanese aquaculture.

### 14.2.5. Classification

Traditional classifications divided bivalves into three subclasses by gill type: Protobranchia, Lamellibranchia (filibranchs and eulamellibranchs) and Septibranchia. Phylogenetic analyses (Bieler et al. 2014; Lemer et al. 2019) show that the filibranch condition is paraphyletic and the septibranchs are a specialized group within the Anomalodesmata. The current classification is:

| Group | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Protobranchia | Protobranch gills; taxodont or no hinge teeth; deposit feeders or chemosymbiotic | *Nucula*, *Yoldia*, *Solemya* |
| Autobranchia: Pteriomorphia | Filibranch gills; often byssate or cemented; one adductor reduced or lost | Mussels, arks, pearl oysters, pen shells, oysters, scallops, file shells |
| Autobranchia: Heteroconchia: Palaeoheterodonta | Eulamellibranch gills; nacreous shell; Trigoniida (marine relicts) and Unionida (freshwater, glochidia) | *Neotrigonia*; *Sinanodonta*, *Hyriopsis*, *Unio*, *Margaritifera* |
| Autobranchia: Heteroconchia: Heterodonta | Heterodont hinge; eulamellibranch gills; siphons; the majority of living clams; includes the Anomalodesmata with the septibranchs | Cockles, venus clams, razor clams, giant clams, shipworms, *Mya*, *Corbicula*, *Cuspidaria* |

## 14.3. Economic Importance of Bivalves

Bivalves are the most valuable group of cultured animals in the sea. China produces most of the world's farmed molluscs, more than 15 million tonnes per year, chiefly oysters, clams (*Ruditapes philippinarum*), scallops, mussels and razor clams, grown on ropes, rafts and tidal flats. Their culture needs no feed, because the animals filter natural phytoplankton, and removes nitrogen and phosphorus from coastal waters. Pearls come from pearl oysters (*Pinctada*) in the sea and from the triangle-shell mussel *Hyriopsis cumingii* in fresh water ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.1.2)). Harmful species include shipworms and rock borers, fouling mussels in water pipes, and invasive freshwater species: the zebra mussel *Dreissena polymorpha*, spread from the Ponto-Caspian region to Europe and North America, and the golden mussel *Limnoperna fortunei*, spread from southern China to South America, clog intakes of power plants and alter whole lake ecosystems.

## 14.4. Scaphopoda: Tusk Shells

**Definition 14.4.1 (Scaphopoda)** The **Scaphopoda** (tusk shells) are marine, infaunal molluscs with a tubular, usually curved and tapering shell open at both ends, a conical burrowing foot protruding from the wide anterior opening, a head with numerous slender contractile tentacles (captacula) that capture prey, a radula, and a mantle cavity that runs the length of the body without ctenidia.

The roughly 600 species live in sand and mud from the intertidal to more than 7,000 m, buried obliquely with the narrow posterior end projecting above the surface. Water enters and leaves the mantle cavity through the posterior opening, driven by cilia and periodic contractions of the foot; gas exchange takes place across the mantle, and the heart is reduced to a muscular sinus. The captacula, each with a sticky adhesive knob, explore the sediment and carry foraminifera and other small organisms to the mouth, where the radula crushes them. The sexes are separate; the gametes leave through the right kidney, and development passes through a trochophore and a veliger. Two orders are recognized: the Dentaliida, with the largest shell diameter near the anterior aperture and a conical foot with lateral lobes (*Dentalium*, *Antalis*), and the Gadilida, often with the widest point in the middle of the shell and a foot that expands into a terminal disc (*Cadulus*). Along the Pacific coast of North America, the shells of *Antalis pretiosa* were traded and used as money by indigenous peoples.

Scaphopods resemble bivalves in their reduced head and burrowing foot, which led to the grouping of the two as "Diasoma". Their nervous system, the way their foot develops and the U-shaped gut with the anus behind the foot resemble cephalopods instead, and molecular analyses place them near gastropods and bivalves; their position within Conchifera remains uncertain ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.2.2)).

## 14.5. The Cephalopod Body Plan

**Definition 14.5.1 (Cephalopoda)** The **Cephalopoda** are marine molluscs in which the foot is transformed into a circle of prehensile arms or tentacles around the mouth and a muscular funnel (siphon) through which water is expelled from the mantle cavity for jet propulsion; the shell, when present, is divided by septa into chambers connected by a siphuncle and serves for buoyancy; they have a closed circulatory system, a large brain enclosed in a cartilaginous cranium, beak-like jaws in addition to the radula, and direct development from large yolky eggs.

Cephalopods are the most specialized molluscs: about 800 living species, all marine predators, from the 1.5 cm pygmy squids (*Idiosepius*) to the giant squid *Architeuthis* (up to about 12 m with its tentacles) and the colossal squid *Mesonychoteuthis* (the heaviest invertebrate, about 500 kg). The axis of the body is unusual. The head with arms points downward in the ancestral, *Nautilus*-like orientation; the visceral mass is drawn out into a long dorsal hump enclosed in the muscular mantle. In a swimming squid this hump points backward, so the morphological dorsal side faces forward and the funnel faces down, but by functional convention the arms are called anterior and the funnel ventral, as in the Chinese textbooks.

That the arms and funnel are derived from the foot is an old inference from their innervation by the pedal ganglia. Studies of the embryos of *Nautilus* and of coleoids (Shigeno et al. 2008) have refined it: the arms and the funnel arise from separate paired primordia that move toward the head during development and fuse with it, so the cephalopod "head" is a composite assembled from several molluscan body regions. The arm crown is therefore best regarded as a new structure of the cephalopod head-foot rather than a simple transformation of a creeping sole.

### 14.5.1. The Chambered Shell and Buoyancy

The ancestral cephalopod shell is a cone divided by transverse walls, the septa, into a series of chambers, the phragmocone, with the animal living in the last, largest chamber (the body chamber). A tube of living tissue, the siphuncle, runs through all the chambers. The chambers are filled at first with liquid; the epithelium of the siphuncle pumps ions out of this cameral liquid, water follows by osmosis, and gas diffuses into the space left behind at less than atmospheric pressure. By adjusting the amount of liquid, the animal makes its shell a float that balances the weight of its tissues and shell. The physics is simple.

**Formula 14.5.1 (Neutral Buoyancy of a Chambered Shell)** Let a cephalopod consist of soft tissue of volume $V_{\mathrm{t}}$ and density $\rho_{\mathrm{t}}$, a shell of volume $V_{\mathrm{s}}$ and density $\rho_{\mathrm{s}}$, and gas-filled chambers of volume $V_{\mathrm{g}}$ (gas density negligible), in seawater of density $\rho_{\mathrm{w}}$. The animal is neutrally buoyant when its mass equals the mass of the water it displaces:

$$
\rho_{\mathrm{t}} V_{\mathrm{t}} + \rho_{\mathrm{s}} V_{\mathrm{s}} = \rho_{\mathrm{w}}\,(V_{\mathrm{t}} + V_{\mathrm{s}} + V_{\mathrm{g}}),
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\frac{V_{\mathrm{g}}}{V_{\mathrm{t}}} = \frac{(\rho_{\mathrm{t}} - \rho_{\mathrm{w}}) + (\rho_{\mathrm{s}} - \rho_{\mathrm{w}})\, V_{\mathrm{s}}/V_{\mathrm{t}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{w}}}.
\tag{14.2}
$$

:::

With typical values ($\rho_{\mathrm{t}} \approx 1.06$, $\rho_{\mathrm{s}} \approx 2.6$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{w}} \approx 1.026\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$), a shell of 10% of the tissue volume requires gas chambers of about 19% of the tissue volume; a thicker shell requires a proportionally larger float, so that strength and buoyancy trade off against each other (see the figure). The gas in the chambers is under a pressure of about 0.9 atm, so the shell must resist the full hydrostatic pressure of the water: the shell of *Nautilus pompilius* implodes at about 800 m (Saunders and Wehman 1977), which limits the depth range of the living animal to about 100–700 m.

(A) The gas volume needed for neutral buoyancy, relative to the volume of soft tissue, as a function of shell volume, for shell densities of 2.2, 2.6 and 2.9 $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (from porous cuttlebone to dense aragonite). Liquid exchange is slow: *Nautilus* needs weeks to refill and empty its newest chamber after a new septum is formed, so the shell is a device for maintaining neutral buoyancy over the long term, not for rapid vertical movement. Its daily migrations, from depths of several hundred metres at day to about 100 m at night, are made by swimming.

### 14.5.2. Nautiloids, Ammonoids and Coleoids

Living cephalopods belong to two groups. The Nautiloidea are represented today only by *Nautilus* and *Allonautilus* (six to eight species) in the tropical Indo-West Pacific. They have an external, coiled, chambered shell, about 90 tentacles without suckers, a pinhole eye without a lens, two pairs of ctenidia, two pairs of nephridia and two pairs of atria; they are scavengers and predators of crustaceans at depth. The Coleoidea, all other living cephalopods, have an internal shell or none, eight arms (plus two tentacles in decabrachians) with suckers, one pair of ctenidia, lensed camera eyes, an ink sac and chromatophores.

The fossil history of the class is dominated by groups with external shells. The first cephalopods, such as *Plectronoceras*, appear in the late Cambrian of northern China about 500 million years ago, when a chambered shell with a siphuncle made it possible to leave the sea floor. Straight-shelled (orthoconic) nautiloids, some several metres long, were top predators of Ordovician seas. The Ammonoidea arose from straight-shelled bactritids in the Early Devonian, about 410 million years ago, coiled their shells, and radiated into thousands of species; they nearly disappeared in the end-Permian and end-Triassic mass extinctions, recovered each time from a few surviving lineages, and died out at the end of the Cretaceous, 66 million years ago. Their rapid evolution and wide distribution make ammonites the most important index fossils for dating Mesozoic rocks. The coleoids originated in the Palaeozoic from bactritid ancestors by enclosing the shell in the mantle; the belemnites, with a bullet-shaped calcite guard around the phragmocone, flourished in the Jurassic and Cretaceous.

### 14.5.3. The Evolution of Suture Lines

The line along which a septum meets the outer wall of the shell, the suture, is visible on the inside of the shell or on fossil internal moulds. In nautiloids the septa are simple curved plates and the sutures straight or gently curved. In ammonoids the margins of the septa became folded, and the sutures increasingly complex: in Devonian to Permian goniatites they form smooth saddles (pointing toward the aperture) and pointed lobes; in Triassic ceratites the lobes are finely toothed; in Jurassic and Cretaceous ammonites both saddles and lobes are subdivided again and again into a fractal pattern.

![Suture lines of shelled cephalopods (schematic; the aperture is up, so saddles point up and lobes down), in the order in which they dominate the fossil record. Orthoceratitic: simple, straight to gently curved (nautiloids, Ordovician to Recent). Goniatitic: smooth saddles and pointed lobes (goniatites, Devonian–Permian). Why did sutures become more complex? The classical hypothesis is mechanical: a folded septum buttresses the thin outer wall against hydrostatic pressure, so complex sutures would have allowed thinner shells or deeper habitats (Westermann 1975). Finite-element models have questioned this, showing that folding concentrates stress in the septum itself (Daniel et al. 1997), and other proposed functions include stronger attachment of the body, more surface for the retention of cameral liquid by capillarity, and faster buoyancy regulation. Statistical analyses have shown that the average complexity of sutures increased over time, but also that this trend can be produced in part by diffusion away from a lower limit of simple sutures, without directional selection (Boyajian and Lutz 1992). The question remains open, and sutures are an example of how hard it is to infer function from form in extinct animals.

### 14.5.4. The Internal Shell of Coleoids

In coleoids the shell is enclosed by the mantle and reduced:

| Group | Shell | Buoyancy |
|---|---|---|
| Spirula (ram's horn squid) | A small, coiled, chambered internal shell with a siphuncle | Gas-filled chambers, like a nautiloid |
| Cuttlefish (Sepiida) | The cuttlebone: a flat chambered plate of aragonite, with thin lamellae separated by pillars; the siphuncular epithelium covers its ventral surface | Cuttlebone about 9% of the body volume, its liquid–gas ratio adjusted within hours |
| Squids (Myopsida, Oegopsida) | The gladius (pen): a thin feather-shaped plate of chitin and protein, a support for the mantle muscles | Many deep-sea squids replace sodium with lighter ammonium ions in coelomic fluid or tissues |
| Octopods | Reduced to small rods (stylets) or lost; the female argonaut secretes a calcareous egg case with two arms, not homologous with a shell | Mostly benthic, negatively buoyant; some pelagic species have gelatinous tissues |

The cuttlebone resists implosion to depths of 150–600 m depending on species, and the strength of the cuttlebone correlates with the maximum depth at which each species lives (Sherrard 2000). The ammonium solution of the cranchiid and histioteuthid squids (density about 1.010 g cm⁻³) makes them neutrally buoyant without a shell, at the cost of a large volume of watery tissue and a slow, drifting way of life.

## 14.6. Locomotion: Jet Propulsion

The mantle of a coleoid is a muscular tube closed at the rear. Circular muscle fibres contract and compress the mantle cavity, the mantle margin locks against the base of the funnel by a pair of cartilaginous press-studs (the mantle-locking apparatus), and water is forced out through the funnel, whose direction the animal controls. Radial muscle fibres and the elastic recoil of collagen fibres in the mantle wall then re-expand the mantle, and water flows in around the margin. In squid, giant axons from the stellate ganglia ([7.14.4](/7.14.4)) ensure that the whole mantle contracts nearly at once for escape jets. Fins on the mantle provide lift and slow, efficient swimming; octopuses mostly crawl with their arms and jet only to escape.

Jetting is fast but expensive. The reason lies in a basic principle of propulsion.

**Formula 14.6.1 (Froude Efficiency of a Jet)** An animal swimming at constant speed $U$ draws in water that is at rest and expels it backward at speed $u_{\mathrm{j}}$ relative to the animal, at a mass flow rate $\dot m$. The thrust is $F = \dot m\,(u_{\mathrm{j}} - U)$, the useful power is $FU$, and the kinetic energy given to the water per unit time is $\tfrac{1}{2}\dot m\,(u_{\mathrm{j}} - U)^2$. The propulsive (Froude) efficiency is

$$
\eta = \frac{FU}{FU + \tfrac{1}{2}\dot m (u_{\mathrm{j}} - U)^2} = \frac{2}{1 + u_{\mathrm{j}}/U}.
\tag{14.3}
$$

:::

The efficiency approaches 1 when a large mass of water is accelerated slightly ($u_{\mathrm{j}}$ close to $U$) and falls when a small mass is accelerated strongly. A squid's funnel is small, so to produce enough thrust it must expel water at several times its swimming speed, and its efficiency is low; fish, whose tails accelerate a large volume of water slightly, achieve efficiencies of 0.7–0.9. Measured whole-cycle efficiencies of adult squid jetting are about 0.4 (Anderson and DeMont 2000), and squid use more energy than fish of the same size swimming at the same speed (O'Dor and Webber 1986). Juvenile squid and slow swimmers do better, because they produce vortex rings that add to the thrust (Bartol et al. 2009). In exchange, jet propulsion gives explosive acceleration, which suits ambush predators and escape, and the oceanic flying squids (*Todarodes*) can launch themselves out of the water and glide for tens of metres.

The points in (B) give the corresponding jet-speed ratios. ## 14.7. Organ Systems of Cephalopods

### 14.7.1. Circulation and Respiration

Cephalopods are the only molluscs with a closed, high-pressure circulation ([7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.5.3)). Blood leaves the systemic heart through arteries, passes through capillaries in the tissues, returns in veins, and is pumped through the gills by two branchial hearts at the base of the gills before returning to the atria of the systemic heart. The blood pigment is hemocyanin, dissolved in the plasma; because hemocyanin carries less oxygen than hemoglobin in cells, cephalopods depend on high cardiac output and on the uptake of oxygen through the skin, and active squids live close to their physiological limits. Their gills have a countercurrent arrangement and are ventilated by the jet cycle itself.

### 14.7.2. Eyes

The eyes of coleoids are camera eyes with a cornea (in most), an iris, a spherical lens and a retina, so similar to the eyes of fishes that they are the textbook example of **convergent evolution** ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.3)). The differences reveal their independent origin: the retina is everted, with the photoreceptors facing the light and the nerve fibres behind them, so there is no blind spot; the photoreceptors are rhabdomeric, with microvilli and the opsins of invertebrates; and the eye develops from an invagination of the epidermis, not from an outgrowth of the brain. Focusing is by moving the lens, as in fishes. *Nautilus*, by contrast, has an open pinhole eye without a lens, filled with seawater, which shows an intermediate stage of eye evolution in a living animal. Most cephalopods have only one visual pigment and are colour-blind, but they can detect polarized light.

### 14.7.3. Brain and Behaviour

The ganglia of coleoids are fused into a brain around the oesophagus, protected by a cartilaginous cranium that also encloses the statocysts. The brain of *Octopus vulgaris* has about 500 million neurons, about two-thirds of them in the arms, whose nerve cords control much of the movement locally ([7.14.7](/7.14.7)). Octopuses and cuttlefish learn by observation and by trial and error, solve simple problems, and have long- and short-term memory centred in the vertical lobe system ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.8.4)). This intelligence evolved independently of that of vertebrates, in short-lived animals, and is usually explained by the demands of predation without a protective shell.

### 14.7.4. Chromatophores and Camouflage

**Definition 14.7.1 (Chromatophore)** A **chromatophore** of cephalopods is a small elastic sac of pigment granules (yellow, red, brown or black) in the dermis, surrounded by radial muscle fibres innervated directly from the brain; contraction of the muscles stretches the sac into a broad disc and displays the pigment within a fraction of a second, and their relaxation lets it shrink back into a dot.

Beneath the chromatophores lie iridophores, cells with stacks of thin platelets that reflect light by interference and produce iridescent colours, and leucophores, which scatter all wavelengths and appear white. Because the chromatophores are controlled by nerves rather than hormones, as they are in fishes and amphibians, cephalopods can change colour and pattern faster than any other animal. Cuttlefish match their background by choosing among a few basic body patterns — uniform, mottled and disruptive — according to the size and contrast of visual features in their surroundings, and they raise skin papillae to change their texture. Patterns also serve in courtship and threat displays. How colour-blind animals match colours is debated; the skin itself contains opsins, and the unusual shape of the pupil may allow colour to be inferred from chromatic aberration.

The ink sac, a diverticulum of the rectum, releases a cloud of melanin and mucus that hides the animal or forms a dummy ("pseudomorph") that distracts a predator; the ink also interferes with the chemical senses of predators.

### 14.7.5. Light Organs, Venom and Genomes

Many cephalopods produce light. In most deep-sea squids the photophores are their own tissue, with lenses, reflectors and colour filters, and they are used for counter-illumination: light emitted downward matches the faint light from above and erases the animal's silhouette as seen by predators below. Other species borrow the light. The Hawaiian bobtail squid *Euprymna scolopes* houses the luminous bacterium *Aliivibrio fischeri* in a bilobed light organ on its ink sac; each juvenile acquires the bacteria from seawater within hours of hatching, the symbionts trigger the development of the organ, and every dawn the squid expels most of them and the rest regrow during the day ([7.11.17](/7.11.17)). This association has become a model of how animals select and maintain specific bacterial partners ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)).

All cephalopods have venomous salivary glands, which they use to subdue prey; in most species the venom is harmless to humans. The exception is the blue-ringed octopuses (*Hapalochlaena*) of the western Pacific, whose posterior salivary glands contain tetrodotoxin produced by symbiotic bacteria, enough to kill an adult human by paralysing the respiratory muscles. The brilliant rings they flash when disturbed are a warning display (**aposematism**).

The genomes of coleoids have unusual features that may be connected with their complex nervous systems. The *Octopus bimaculoides* genome (Albertin et al. 2015) shows large expansions of the protocadherins, cell adhesion molecules involved in neuronal wiring (an expansion paralleled independently in vertebrates), and of C2H2 zinc-finger transcription factors, as well as extensive rearrangement of gene order. Coleoids also edit their messenger RNA on an extraordinary scale, recoding tens of thousands of sites, mostly in neural transcripts, by the deamination of adenosine to inosine; the editing changes with temperature and appears to adjust the properties of ion channels, but its price is a slower rate of evolution of the edited coding sequences (Liscovitch-Brauer et al. 2017).

### 14.7.6. Reproduction and Life History

Sexes are separate. The male packs sperm into elaborate spermatophores and transfers them with a modified arm, the hectocotylus, into the mantle cavity or onto the body of the female; in argonauts the tip of the hectocotylus breaks off and remains in the female. The large yolky eggs undergo discoidal cleavage, and the young hatch as miniature adults, or as planktonic paralarvae in many squid and octopuses. Most coleoids grow rapidly, live one or two years and die after a single breeding season (semelparity). The female octopus guards her eggs without feeding until they hatch and then dies, a programme controlled by the optic gland, an endocrine organ comparable to the pituitary; removal of the optic glands extends the life of a brooding female. *Nautilus*, by contrast, grows slowly, matures after 10–15 years and breeds repeatedly for years.

The relationships among the decabrachian orders are still unsettled in phylogenomic analyses, whereas the octopuses are robustly the sister group of the vampire squid.

### 14.8.2. The Cuttlefish: A Representative Cephalopod

The golden cuttlefish *Sepia esculenta* and the spineless cuttlefish *Sepiella maindroni* live in the coastal waters of China and are the representative cephalopods of the Chinese textbooks.

### 14.8.3. Cephalopods of Chinese Seas

Chinese seas are rich in cephalopods, and several are of great economic importance. The cuttlefishes *Sepiella maindroni* and *Sepia esculenta* supported one of the four great traditional fisheries of the East China Sea, together with two croakers and the hairtail, until overfishing in the 1980s caused the collapse of the *Sepiella* stock. The neritic squids *Uroteuthis chinensis* and *U. edulis* are fished in the South China Sea, the oceanic flying squid *Todarodes pacificus* in the Yellow and East China Seas, and the long-armed octopus *Octopus minor* and the webfoot octopus *Amphioctopus fangsiao* on tidal flats. The tiny *Idiosepius paradoxus*, about 1 cm long, sticks to seagrass with an adhesive organ on its back. Chinese distant-water fleets are now among the largest harvesters of the jumbo squid *Dosidicus gigas* in the eastern Pacific and of the Argentine shortfin squid *Illex argentinus* in the southwest Atlantic. Cephalopod populations respond quickly to fishing and climate because their lives are short: a failed year class cannot be buffered by older age classes, and an exceptional year can produce a sudden boom.

## 14.9. The Fossil Record of Molluscs

The excellent fossil record of molluscs makes them central to the study of evolution in deep time. Gastropods, bivalves and cephalopods appear in the early to late Cambrian; Ordovician seas saw the radiation of all three, together with chitons and monoplacophorans. Bivalves became the dominant shelled animals of the sea floor after the end-Permian extinction, replacing the brachiopods in many habitats ([7.17.16](/7.17.16)). Cretaceous reefs were built in part by rudists, bizarre cemented bivalves with one valve shaped like a horn and the other like a lid, which disappeared at the end of the Cretaceous along with the ammonites and belemnites. Molluscan fossils have provided classic studies of evolutionary rates and patterns: the gradual and punctuated change of freshwater snails in the Lake Turkana basin, the escalation of shell defences against shell-crushing predators in the Mesozoic marine revolution (Vermeij 1977), and the use of ammonite zones, each lasting less than a million years, to correlate rocks across continents ([7.10.29](/7.10.29)).

## Summary

- Bivalves (Definition 14.1.1) are laterally compressed molluscs with two valves, a hinge, a ligament that opens the valves against adductors that close them (Eq. (14.1)), no head or radula, large ctenidia and a burrowing foot; catch muscle holds the valves closed cheaply.
- Ctenidia: protobranch (small, respiratory; palp proboscides for deposit feeding), filibranch (filaments joined by cilia), eulamellibranch (lamellae with ostia and water tubes), septibranch (pumping septum, carnivory). Particles are carried in mucus to the food grooves and labial palps.
- Marine bivalves spawn in huge numbers and develop through trochophore, D-shaped veliger and pediveliger to settlement and metamorphosis; oysters change sex, many cold-water and freshwater bivalves brood. Scallops swim by clapping the valves and expelling two jets beside the hinge.
- Burrowing uses the dual-anchor mechanism. Modes of life include burrowing, byssal attachment, cementation, swimming, boring, photosymbiosis and chemosymbiosis.
- The freshwater mussel *Sinanodonta woodiana* broods its larvae in the outer demibranchs and releases glochidia (Definition 14.2.1), which parasitize fish; classification: Protobranchia, Pteriomorphia, Palaeoheterodonta (Unionida), Heterodonta.
- Tusk shells (Definition 14.4.1): tubular shell open at both ends, conical foot, captacula, no ctenidia; position within Conchifera uncertain.
- Cephalopods (Definition 14.5.1): arms and funnel from the foot, chambered shell with siphuncle for buoyancy (Eq. (14.2)), closed circulation with branchial hearts, camera eyes, a large brain. Nautiloids keep the external shell; ammonoids (Devonian–Cretaceous) evolved increasingly complex sutures, whose function is debated; coleoids internalized or lost the shell.
- Jet propulsion is fast but inefficient, because the Froude efficiency $\eta = 2/(1 + u_{\mathrm{j}}/U)$ (Eq. (14.3)) is low for a small, fast jet.
- Coleoid eyes are convergent with vertebrate eyes but have an everted retina and rhabdomeric receptors; octopus brains have about 500 million neurons; chromatophores (Definition 14.7.1) under direct neural control allow rapid camouflage.
- Squid have a gladius, cuttlefish a cuttlebone, octopuses no shell; most coleoids are short-lived and semelparous. The hectocotylus is the left ventral arm in decabrachians and the right third arm in octopuses.
- Cephalopods produce light themselves or with symbiotic bacteria (*Euprymna*–*Aliivibrio*), all have venomous saliva (lethal in blue-ringed octopuses), and coleoid genomes show expanded neural gene families and extensive RNA editing.
- The molluscan fossil record (ammonite biostratigraphy, the bivalve–brachiopod replacement, rudists, escalation) is a main source of evidence on macroevolution.

## Questions

1. Why does a dead clam gape? Use Eq. (14.1) to explain why a sea star can open a clam even though its tube feet are individually weak.

   **Answer** The valves are held closed only by contraction of the adductor muscles against the elastic ligament, which is compressed or stretched when the valves close and stores energy. In a dead clam the adductors no longer produce force, $F_{\mathrm{A}} r_{\mathrm{A}} < \tau_{\mathrm{L}}$, and the ligament springs the valves open. A sea star attaches many tube feet to both valves and pulls continuously for minutes to hours. The clam's catch muscle can hold the valves closed cheaply for a long time, but the sea star's sustained pull, added to the ligament torque, eventually exceeds the adductor torque; even a gap of a fraction of a millimetre suffices, because the sea star everts its stomach through it and digests the clam inside its shell.
2. Compare protobranch, filibranch, eulamellibranch and septibranch ctenidia. How does the gill type relate to the way of feeding?

   **Answer** Protobranch gills are small bipectinate ctenidia like those of other molluscs; they serve mainly for respiration, and protobranchs feed on deposits collected by palp proboscides. Filibranch gills consist of long filaments folded into a W and joined only by ciliary junctions; eulamellibranch gills have the same shape but the filaments are fused by tissue into perforated lamellae with water tubes; both are large filters that pump water and capture suspended particles, which are carried in mucus to the labial palps. Septibranch gills have been replaced by a muscular septum that pumps water and sucks in small animals, a specialization for carnivory in the food-poor deep sea.
3. Use Eq. (14.2) to show why a cephalopod with a thicker shell needs a larger float. Why does the gas in the chambers make the shell vulnerable at depth?

   **Answer** The required gas volume is $V_{\mathrm{g}} = [(\rho_{\mathrm{t}} - \rho_{\mathrm{w}})V_{\mathrm{t}} + (\rho_{\mathrm{s}} - \rho_{\mathrm{w}})V_{\mathrm{s}}]/\rho_{\mathrm{w}}$. Because shell material is more than twice as dense as seawater, each additional unit of shell volume requires about $(\rho_{\mathrm{s}} - \rho_{\mathrm{w}})/\rho_{\mathrm{w}} \approx 1.5$ units of gas, so a stronger, thicker shell must be balanced by a larger phragmocone. The gas in the chambers is at less than atmospheric pressure, while the water outside presses with about 1 atm per 10 m of depth; the shell and septa must carry the full pressure difference, so below a critical depth (about 800 m for *Nautilus*) the shell implodes. A thicker shell resists more pressure but needs a bigger float, so strength, buoyancy and depth range trade off.
4. A squid swims at $U = 0.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and expels water from its funnel at $u_{\mathrm{j}} = 2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. What is its Froude efficiency? Why do squid nevertheless use jet propulsion?

   **Answer** By Eq. (14.3), $\eta = 2/(1 + 2/0.5) = 2/5 = 0.4$: only 40% of the power delivered to the water does useful work; the rest is lost as kinetic energy of the jet. Squid use jets because they allow very rapid acceleration from rest and changes of direction, which serve escape from predators and ambush predation, and because the mantle, which also ventilates the gills, can be both pump and propulsor. They also swim slowly and more efficiently with their fins, and juveniles gain efficiency from vortex rings.
5. In what ways are cephalopod and vertebrate eyes similar, and what shows that they evolved independently?

   **Answer** Both are camera eyes with a cornea, an iris that controls the pupil, a spherical lens of graded refractive index that forms an image on a retina, and muscles that move the eye; in both, focusing in water is done by the lens. The differences show independent origin: the cephalopod retina is everted (photoreceptors face the light, no blind spot) while the vertebrate retina is inverted; cephalopod photoreceptors are rhabdomeric with r-opsins and a depolarizing response, vertebrate rods and cones are ciliary with c-opsins and hyperpolarize; and the cephalopod eye develops from an epidermal invagination, the vertebrate eye mainly from an outgrowth of the brain. The phylogeny, in which molluscs and vertebrates are separated by many lineages with simple eyes, confirms convergence.
6. How does the life cycle of freshwater mussels solve the problem of dispersal in rivers, and why does it make them vulnerable?

   **Answer** In a river, free planktonic larvae would be carried downstream and lost. Unionid mussels brood their embryos and release glochidia that attach to fish, encyst and feed on the host's tissues for weeks; when they drop off as juveniles, they may be far upstream or in a different tributary, so the fish disperses the mussel in any direction. Many species attract specific host fish with lures. The dependence on a particular host makes the mussels vulnerable: if the host fish declines or is blocked by dams, the mussels stop recruiting, and because adults live for decades, populations may persist for years without reproducing before they disappear.
7. Describe the larval life of an oyster from fertilization to spat. Why do hatcheries matter for bivalve aquaculture?

   **Answer** Eggs and sperm are shed into the water and fertilized externally; spiral cleavage leads within a day to a trochophore and within one to two days to a feeding veliger with a D-shaped shell (straight-hinge larva). The veliger feeds on phytoplankton with the cilia of its velum and grows through an umbo stage. After two to four weeks a foot and an eyespot develop (pediveliger); the larva swims and crawls alternately, testing surfaces, and settles in response to cues such as adults of its species and bacterial films. At metamorphosis it loses the velum and cements its left valve to the substrate, becoming spat. Because larval mortality in the plankton is enormous and wild settlement varies from year to year, hatcheries that spawn broodstock, rear larvae on cultured algae and induce settlement on collectors give a reliable supply of seed, and allow the selection of fast-growing or disease-resistant strains.
8. Why do cephalopod fisheries fluctuate more strongly than fisheries of long-lived fishes?

   **Answer** Most coleoids live one or two years, grow very fast and die after spawning, so the population each year consists almost entirely of one year class. Its size depends on the survival of the paralarvae and juveniles in that year, which varies with temperature, currents and food. A long-lived fish population contains many age classes, and a poor year class is buffered by older fish; a squid population has no such buffer, so a bad year can cause a collapse and a good one a boom. Heavy fishing of spawning aggregations, as in the East China Sea cuttlefish fishery of the 1980s, can remove a large part of the single breeding generation and cause a collapse from which the stock may not recover.
