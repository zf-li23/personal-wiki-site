The living amphibians, frogs, salamanders and caecilians, are the descendants of one of the two great lineages of crown tetrapods ([7.17.48](/7.17.48)). They are not living versions of the first tetrapods: they are small, specialized animals with a long independent history, a moist, glandular skin through which they breathe and lose water, and in most species a life cycle that begins in water as a larva and ends on land after metamorphosis. With about 8800 species they are more diverse than mammals, and they range from the 7 mm frog *Paedophryne amauensis* of New Guinea, the smallest known vertebrate, to the Chinese giant salamander, which reaches 1.8 m.

The chapter first defines the group and discusses its origin (§49.1), then describes the three orders: frogs (§49.2), salamanders (§49.3) and caecilians (§49.4). Section 49.5 treats reproduction, larvae and metamorphosis; §49.6 the skin, the organ that most distinguishes amphibians, with its glands, toxins and role in respiration and water balance; §49.7 the frog as the representative animal. The chapter ends with the worldwide decline of amphibians (§49.8) and a comparison with the traditional classification (§49.9). The hormonal control of metamorphosis is in [7.14.34](/7.14.34#sec-34.7.1) and [7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.2), regeneration in [7.5.35](/7.5.35), the amniotes in [7.17.50](/7.17.50); the skull, vertebrae, heart and urogenital system of amphibians are compared with those of other vertebrates in [7.17.58](/7.17.58)–[7.17.66](/7.17.66).

## 49.1. The Living Amphibians and Their Origin

### 49.1.1. Characters of Lissamphibians

**Definition 49.1.1 (Lissamphibia)** The **Lissamphibia** (lissamphibians, living amphibians) are the clade comprising the frogs (Anura), salamanders (Caudata) and caecilians (Gymnophiona) and their extinct relatives closer to them than to amniotes. They share pedicellate teeth, in which the crown is separated from the base by a ring of uncalcified tissue; a smooth, scaleless skin rich in mucous and granular glands, used in gas exchange; a sensory papilla in the inner ear (the papilla amphibiorum) that detects low-frequency sound; an operculum in the oval window moved by a muscle from the shoulder girdle (the opercularis system, in frogs and salamanders); green rods in the retina; fat bodies associated with the gonads; and a life cycle that ancestrally includes an aquatic larva and metamorphosis.

The name "amphibian" (living a double life) refers to the aquatic larva and terrestrial adult, but in the broad, traditional sense "Amphibia" also included all Palaeozoic non-amniote tetrapods, a **paraphyletic group** ([7.17.48](/7.17.48#sec-48.5)). This course uses Lissamphibia for the clade of living amphibians, which the Chinese textbooks call the class Amphibia.

Several characters often listed for amphibians are not lissamphibian synapomorphies but plesiomorphies shared with early tetrapods (eggs without amniotic membranes, a three-chambered heart, cutaneous respiration as a supplement to the lungs) or losses (no scales, reduced skull bones, short ribs). The pedicellate teeth are the most frequently cited derived character; they occur also in some dissorophoid temnospondyls, which is one argument for the origin of lissamphibians among them.

Phylogenetic analyses of molecular data unite frogs and salamanders as the Batrachia, with caecilians as their sister group. The three orders diverged in the late Carboniferous or Permian, more than 300 million years ago according to molecular clocks, although the oldest fossils of each order are Triassic or Jurassic.

### 49.1.2. The Origin of Lissamphibians

Where among the early tetrapods the living amphibians arose is one of the longest-running debates in vertebrate palaeontology, because the gap between the Palaeozoic tetrapods and the first undoubted lissamphibians of the Triassic is long and the small, delicate skeletons of early lissamphibians are rarely preserved. Three hypotheses have been proposed:

| Hypothesis | Claim | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temnospondyl hypothesis | All lissamphibians arose from small dissorophoid temnospondyls (amphibamids, branchiosaurids) of the Carboniferous and Permian | Pedicellate teeth, skull shape with large orbits and palatal openings, four fingers in the hand, larvae with external gills (branchiosaurids); *Gerobatrachus hottoni* (Early Permian, Texas, about 290 million years), with a mixture of frog and salamander characters | Favoured by most current analyses, including the Triassic stem caecilian *Funcrusher* (Kligman et al. 2023) |
| Lepospondyl hypothesis | Lissamphibians arose from lepospondyls (microsaurs, lysorophians) | Spool-shaped vertebrae formed directly in bone; elongated bodies with reduced limbs in some forms | Supported by some analyses; may reflect convergence in small, burrowing animals |
| Polyphyly | Frogs and salamanders from temnospondyls, caecilians from microsaurs (Carroll) | The caecilian *Eocaecilia* compared with microsaurs | Weakened by molecular evidence for lissamphibian monophyly and by fossil caecilian relatives with temnospondyl features |

Molecular data support the monophyly of Lissamphibia strongly, and most morphologists now favour a single origin from dissorophoid temnospondyls, although the debate is not closed. The Chinese textbooks present the polyphyly hypothesis; the current view is summarized in §49.9.

The fossil record of the orders begins in the Triassic. *Triadobatrachus massinoti* from the Early Triassic of Madagascar, about 250 million years old, has a frog-like skull and a shortened trunk but a short tail and hindlimbs not yet lengthened; *Czatkobatrachus* from Poland is similar. The first jumping frog is *Prosalirus bitis* from the Early Jurassic of Arizona, with a urostyle and long hindlimbs. *Triassurus* from the Triassic of Kyrgyzstan is the oldest salamander relative. The Middle and Late Jurassic of northern China (the Daohugou beds of Inner Mongolia and the Jiulongshan Formation of Hebei and Liaoning, about 165–160 million years old) have yielded thousands of exquisitely preserved salamanders, many with skin, gills and stomach contents: *Chunerpeton tianyiensis*, a cryptobranchid relative of the giant salamanders; *Jeholotriton*, *Pangerpeton*, *Beiyanerpeton* (the oldest member of the salamandroids, the lineage of most living salamanders) and others (Gao and Shubin 2003, 2012). They show that the main lineages of living salamanders had separated by the Middle Jurassic and that East Asia was a centre of early salamander evolution. *Eocaecilia micropodia* from the Early Jurassic of Arizona is an elongated caecilian with small limbs, intermediate between limbed ancestors and the limbless living caecilians.

## 49.2. Anura: Frogs and Toads

### 49.2.1. The Jumping Body Plan

**Definition 49.2.1 (Anura)** The **Anura** (anurans, frogs and toads) are lissamphibians without a tail in the adult, with a short vertebral column of 5–9 presacral vertebrae and postsacral vertebrae fused into a rod-like **urostyle**, long ilia extending forward to the sacral vertebra, fused radius and ulna (radioulna) and fused tibia and fibula (tibiofibula), elongated proximal tarsals (astragalus and calcaneum) that form an additional segment of the hindlimb, and a larva, the **tadpole**, that differs strongly from the adult and is transformed at metamorphosis.

The frog body is built for jumping. When a frog jumps, it extends its long hindlimbs, each with four segments (femur, tibiofibula, the elongated tarsals and the foot), so that it accelerates over a longer distance than an animal with three-segmented limbs; the iliosacral joint between the long ilium and the sacral vertebra allows the trunk to be raised at an angle at take-off; the short, stiff trunk without ribs, braced by the urostyle, transmits the thrust; and the forelimbs and pectoral girdle absorb the shock of landing.

(B) Frog: a short trunk of eight presacral vertebrae without ribs, with long transverse processes and broad sacral diapophyses; the postsacral vertebrae fused into the urostyle (outlined); long ilia reaching forward to the sacral diapophyses; radius and ulna fused into a radioulna, tibia and fibula into a tibiofibula; the elongated astragalus and calcaneum add a segment to the hindlimb. **Example 49.2.1 (How Far Can a Frog Jump?)** A frog leaving the ground at speed $v$ and angle $\theta$ to the horizontal travels, if air resistance is neglected, a horizontal distance

$$
R = \frac{v^2 \sin 2\theta}{g},
$$
greatest at $\theta = 45^\circ$. A leopard frog 7 cm long jumps about 1 m, which requires $v = \sqrt{gR} \approx 3.1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. If the hindlimbs accelerate the body over an extension distance of $d = 6\,\mathrm{cm}$, roughly the leg length, the mean acceleration is $v^2/(2d) \approx 80\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, about $8g$, and the take-off lasts $2d/v \approx 40\,\mathrm{ms}$. The power needed per kilogram of body mass is the kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}v^2 \approx 4.8\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$ divided by this time, about $120\,\mathrm{W\,kg^{-1}}$, or several hundred watts per kilogram of the hindlimb muscles, which make up about a fifth of the body. In good jumpers such as tree frogs, the measured peak power output during take-off exceeds what the muscles can produce directly (Peplowski and Marsh 1997): the muscles stretch elastic tendons before take-off, and the stored energy is released in a short recoil, as in a catapult. The longer the limb (the larger $d$), the lower the acceleration needed for the same speed; the extra limb segment of frogs is part of this design.

:::

Not all frogs jump. Many toads (Bufonidae) walk or make short hops, burrowing frogs dig backward with spade-like tubercles on their hindfeet, tree frogs climb with adhesive toe pads that work by wet adhesion and friction, the aquatic clawed frogs (*Xenopus*) and other pipids swim with powerful hindlimbs and have lost the tongue, and some tree frogs glide with webbed feet ("flying frogs" such as *Rhacophorus* and *Zhangixalus* in southern China). The pectoral girdle is arciferal in some families (the two halves overlap and can slide past each other, absorbing the shock of landing) and firmisternal in others (fused at the midline); the Chinese textbooks use this distinction for classification.

Frogs catch prey with a projectile tongue attached at the front of the mouth, which is flipped forward and out in a few tens of milliseconds; its soft tissue and sticky saliva, which becomes less viscous under shear, allow it to stick to and hold insects. The eyes retract into the orbits during swallowing and help push food into the oesophagus. Males call to attract females with vocal sacs that inflate as resonators; calls are species-specific and are the main barrier between closely related species. The concave-eared torrent frog *Odorrana tormota* of Huangshan in Anhui, which lives along loud streams, communicates with ultrasonic calls of up to 128 kHz and has sunken tympanic membranes tuned to high frequencies (Feng et al. 2006), an exception among frogs.

### 49.2.2. Classification

Frogs make up about 88 % of lissamphibian species, with more than 55 families. The traditional division into "Archaeobatrachia" (primitive frogs) and Neobatrachia is still used informally, but archaeobatrachians are a paraphyletic grade at the base of the tree. Phylogenomic analyses found that most of the living diversity of Neobatrachia arose in three lineages that diversified rapidly after the end-Cretaceous mass extinction 66 million years ago, when forests recovered (Feng et al. 2017).

|---|---|---|---|
| Early-branching frogs ("archaeobatrachians") | Leiopelmatidae, Ascaphidae, Bombinatoridae, Alytidae, Pipidae, Pelobatidae, Megophryidae | Free ribs in some, amphicoelous or opisthocoelous vertebrae, arciferal girdle; pipids aquatic and tongueless | Fire-bellied toad *Bombina orientalis* (northeastern China), midwife toad *Alytes*, Surinam toad *Pipa pipa*, clawed frog *Xenopus laevis*, the Asian horned frogs *Megophrys* and moustache toads *Leptobrachium* of southern China |
| Hyloidea | Bufonidae, Hylidae, Leptodactylidae, Dendrobatidae, Eleutherodactylidae and others | Mostly arciferal | Asiatic toad *Bufo gargarizans*, Japanese tree frog *Dryophytes immaculatus*, poison frogs (*Dendrobates*, *Phyllobates*), coquí *Eleutherodactylus* |
| Ranoidea | Ranidae, Dicroglossidae, Rhacophoridae, Microhylidae, Mantellidae and others | Mostly firmisternal | Black-spotted pond frog *Pelophylax nigromaculatus*, Chinese brown frog *Rana chensinensis*, tiger frog *Hoplobatrachus chinensis*, spiny frog *Quasipaa spinosa*, rice frog *Fejervarya multistriata*, Dennys's whipping frog *Zhangixalus dennysi*, the boreal digging frog *Kaloula borealis* |

China has more than 600 species of amphibians, among the highest numbers of any country in Asia, concentrated in the mountains of the southwest and south; about two-thirds are frogs. Several Chinese frogs are used economically: the parotoid gland secretion of *Bufo gargarizans* is dried as *chansu* (toad venom) in Chinese medicine, rich in bufadienolides that act on the sodium–potassium pump like digitalis; the dried fat of the oviduct of the Chinese brown frog *Rana chensinensis* (*hasma*) is a traditional tonic; and the tiger frog and spiny frog are farmed for food.

## 49.3. Caudata: Salamanders

### 49.3.1. Characters and Diversity

**Definition 49.3.1 (Caudata)** The **Caudata** (urodeles, salamanders and newts) are lissamphibians with an elongated body and a tail throughout life, usually two pairs of limbs of similar size (forelimbs with four digits, hindlimbs with five or fewer), separate radius and ulna and tibia and fibula, and larvae that resemble the adults, with external gills and a tail fin.

Salamanders walk with a sprawling gait that combines lateral undulation with alternating limb movements, the form of locomotion inferred for the earliest tetrapods; robots built to reproduce salamander locomotion have been used to study how the spinal cord switches between swimming and walking (Ijspeert et al. 2007). About 830 species live mainly in the Northern Hemisphere, in North America, Europe and Asia; only the lungless salamanders reach South America.

| Family | Species | Characters | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cryptobranchidae (giant salamanders) | 6–8 | Very large, flattened, wrinkled skin; aquatic; adults lose external gills but keep larval features (no eyelids); external fertilization | Chinese giant salamanders *Andrias* spp., Japanese giant salamander *A. japonicus*, hellbender *Cryptobranchus alleganiensis* |
| Hynobiidae (Asian salamanders) | About 90 | External fertilization; eggs in paired sacs; mostly in East Asia | *Hynobius*, the Siberian salamander *Salamandrella keyserlingii*, *Batrachuperus* of the mountain streams of Sichuan and Yunnan, *Ranodon* of Xinjiang |
| Salamandridae (newts and true salamanders) | About 130 | Internal fertilization by spermatophores; toxic skin; many with aquatic and terrestrial phases | Chinese fire-bellied newt *Cynops orientalis*, *Pachytriton*, crocodile newts *Tylototriton*, warty newts *Paramesotriton*, fire salamander *Salamandra salamandra* |
| Ambystomatidae (mole salamanders) | About 35 | Robust, terrestrial adults with aquatic larvae; some paedomorphic | Tiger salamander, axolotl *Ambystoma mexicanum* |
| Plethodontidae (lungless salamanders) | About 500 | No lungs; nasolabial grooves; many with direct development | *Plethodon*, *Bolitoglossa*, *Hydromantes*; *Karsenia koreana*, the only Asian species (Korea) |
| Proteidae, Sirenidae, Amphiumidae | Few | Permanently aquatic, with external gills (proteids, sirens) or eel-like (amphiumas) | Olm *Proteus anguinus* of the caves of the Dinaric karst, mudpuppy *Necturus*, sirens *Siren* |

*The Chinese giant salamander.* *Andrias davidianus*, the largest living amphibian, can exceed 1.5 m and 50 kg and lives for more than 50 years. It inhabits cool, fast mountain streams with rocky beds in central and southern China, hiding by day under rocks or in burrows and ambushing fishes, crabs and frogs at night with a fast suction strike. It breathes mainly through the folded, richly vascularized skin along its flanks and rarely surfaces to use its lungs. In late summer the female lays strings of several hundred eggs in a burrow occupied by a male, who fertilizes them externally and guards them until the larvae hatch. Its call when disturbed, likened to a crying baby, gives it its Chinese name. Genetic studies revealed that what was treated as one species comprises several deeply divergent lineages, of which the South China giant salamander *A. sligoi* and the Jiangxi giant salamander *A. jiangxiensis* have been described as species (Turvey et al. 2019; Chai et al. 2022). A survey of 16 provinces from 2013 to 2016 found wild individuals at only a few sites, most of them escapes from farms (Turvey et al. 2018): the giant salamanders are critically endangered, mainly from overcollection for food and from the mixing of lineages in farms, which hold millions of animals of mixed origin. Releasing farmed animals into the wild threatens the genetic integrity of the remaining wild lineages.

*Salamanders without lungs.* The *Plethodontidae*, more than half of all salamander species, have no lungs and breathe entirely through the skin and the lining of the mouth. Lunglessness probably evolved in cool, fast streams of the Appalachian Mountains, where lungs would make a salamander buoyant and liable to be swept away, and where cold, oxygen-rich water allows skin breathing to meet the demand. Freed from the role of the hyoid apparatus in lung ventilation, several plethodontid lineages evolved ballistic tongues: in *Hydromantes* and *Bolitoglossa* the tongue is shot out to more than half the body length in a few milliseconds by elastic recoil of collagen in the tongue muscles, a power amplification similar to that of frog jumping. Many plethodontids have direct development, laying eggs on land from which miniature adults hatch, and the tropical *Bolitoglossa* radiated in the forests of Central and South America.

### 49.3.2. Paedomorphosis

In many salamanders the adults retain larval characters: external gills, tail fins, an aquatic life, lidless eyes. This **paedomorphosis**, the retention of juvenile features in the adult, arises by **neoteny** (slowed somatic development while reproductive development proceeds normally) or by progenesis (accelerated sexual maturation in a juvenile body) ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)). Obligate paedomorphs never metamorphose (the olm, mudpuppies, sirens; the cryptobranchids are partially paedomorphic). Facultative paedomorphs metamorphose or not depending on conditions: in the tiger salamander, larvae in permanent, cold, fishless ponds with abundant food often mature as gilled aquatic adults, while those in temporary ponds metamorphose and leave.

The axolotl *Ambystoma mexicanum*, from the lake system of Xochimilco near Mexico City, is an obligate paedomorph in nature: it does not metamorphose because its thyroid gland produces too little hormone, and it can be induced to metamorphose into a terrestrial salamander by treatment with thyroxine (an experiment first made in the 1870s). The axolotl is a major model of regeneration: it regenerates complete limbs, the tail with the spinal cord, parts of the heart and brain and the lens throughout life ([7.5.35](/7.5.35)). Wild axolotls are nearly extinct because of the drainage and pollution of Xochimilco and introduced fishes, while millions live in laboratories and aquaria.

## 49.4. Gymnophiona: Caecilians

**Definition 49.4.1 (Gymnophiona)** The **Gymnophiona** (caecilians, Apoda) are limbless, elongated lissamphibians without limb girdles, with ring-like folds (annuli) of the skin along the body, a short tail or none, small eyes covered by skin or bone, a protrusible chemosensory tentacle between the eye and the nostril, a compact, heavily ossified skull used for burrowing, and internal fertilization by a protrusible male copulatory organ (the phallodeum).

About 220 species of caecilians live in the tropics of South and Central America, Africa, the Seychelles and South and Southeast Asia, most of them burrowing in moist soil, some aquatic (*Typhlonectes* in South America). They range from 10 cm to 1.5 m. Their solid skull is used as a ram for burrowing; to compensate for the loss of space for jaw-closing muscles, caecilians have a second jaw-closing mechanism, in which a hypobranchial muscle (the interhyoideus posterior) pulls down a long retroarticular process behind the jaw joint. Some species keep small dermal scales in pockets of the skin, unique among living amphibians. The left lung is reduced, as in snakes, an adaptation to the elongated body.

Reproduction is diverse. Some caecilians lay eggs that hatch into aquatic larvae, others lay eggs on land with direct development, and about half are viviparous: the foetuses use specialized foetal teeth to scrape and eat secretions and cells of the oviduct wall. In the East African *Boulengerula taitanus* and the South American *Siphonops*, the mother's skin thickens and becomes rich in lipids, and the young peel it off with special teeth and eat it (maternal dermatophagy; Kupfer et al. 2006); in *Siphonops annulatus* the mother also releases a milk-like secretion from the cloaca for her young (Mailho-Fontana et al. 2024). In China the Banna caecilian *Ichthyophis bannanicus* lives in Yunnan, Guangxi and Guangdong; it lays eggs on land near water, the female coiling around them, and the larvae live in water.

## 49.5. Reproduction, Larvae and Metamorphosis

### 49.5.1. Eggs and Modes of Reproduction

Amphibian eggs have no shell or amniotic membranes ([7.17.50](/7.17.50)): they are surrounded by jelly layers secreted by the oviduct, which protect them and let water and gases through, but they dry out quickly and must be laid in water or in damp places. Fertilization is external in most frogs, in cryptobranchids and hynobiids, and internal in the other salamanders (through spermatophores, packets of sperm on a gelatinous base that the male deposits and the female takes up with her cloaca), in caecilians and in a few frogs (the tailed frog *Ascaphus*, whose "tail" is a copulatory organ). In frogs the male clasps the female in amplexus while she lays her eggs, either around the waist (inguinal amplexus, in early-branching frogs) or behind the forelimbs (axillary amplexus, in most neobatrachians).

More reproductive modes are known in frogs than in any other vertebrate group, about forty, ranging from eggs and tadpoles in ponds, through foam nests on vegetation above water (rhacophorids such as *Zhangixalus*, whose tadpoles drop into the water), eggs carried on the back of the female (the Surinam toad *Pipa*, where the young develop in pits of the skin and emerge as froglets), eggs wrapped around the legs of the male (the midwife toad *Alytes*), tadpoles carried to water by a parent (poison frogs), and tadpoles raised in the vocal sac of the male (Darwin's frog *Rhinoderma*), to direct development of terrestrial eggs into froglets (*Eleutherodactylus*, about 200 species) and true viviparity (*Nectophrynoides*, *Limnonectes larvaepartus*). The two species of gastric-brooding frogs (*Rheobatrachus*) of Australia, in which the female swallowed her eggs and raised the tadpoles in her stomach, went extinct in the 1980s. The direction of change is mostly from aquatic eggs and larvae toward reduced dependence on water, a series that parallels, in an incomplete way, the evolution of the amniotic egg.

### 49.5.2. The Tadpole

The frog tadpole is one of the most specialized larvae of vertebrates. It has a globular body with a coiled, long gut, a muscular tail with dorsal and ventral fins, no limbs at first, and a mouth with keratinized jaw sheaths and rows of keratinized denticles used to scrape algae; the arrangement of the denticles is used to identify species. The gills are first external, then covered by an operculum, a fold of skin that encloses them in a chamber opening to the outside by a spiracle, usually on the left side. The tadpole pumps water through the mouth and over the gill filters with the buccal floor, filtering particles and trapping them on mucus, as a larval lamprey does. Most tadpoles are herbivorous suspension feeders or grazers; some are carnivorous or cannibalistic (spadefoot toads), and the tadpoles of some *Megophrys* and other Asian horned frogs have funnel-shaped mouths for skimming food from the water surface. Salamander larvae, by contrast, are carnivorous, with true teeth, external gills, balancers in pond-dwelling species and limbs that develop early.

### 49.5.3. Metamorphosis

At **metamorphosis** the tadpole is transformed within days to weeks into a froglet. The tail is resorbed by apoptosis and its tissues recycled, the gills are lost and the lungs take over, the gut shortens to a third of its length as the diet changes from plants to animals, the mouth widens and develops jaws and a tongue, the skin thickens and develops glands, the eyes acquire eyelids, a nictitating membrane and new visual pigments, the lateral line disappears in most species, the limbs grow, the excretion of nitrogen changes from ammonia to urea (**ammonotelic** to **ureotelic**), and the haemoglobin changes from a larval to an adult form.

The whole programme is controlled by **thyroid hormones**, thyroxine (T₄) and the more active triiodothyronine (T₃), whose concentration rises to a peak at metamorphic climax under the control of corticotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus, which in amphibians stimulates the pituitary to release thyrotropin. The same hormone causes opposite responses in different tissues: proliferation in the limb buds, death in the tail and gills, remodelling in the gut, as determined by the local expression of thyroid hormone receptors and deiodinase enzymes ([7.14.34](/7.14.34#sec-34.7.1), [7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.2)). Corticosteroids synergize with thyroid hormones, and prolactin slows metamorphosis. Tadpoles in drying ponds accelerate metamorphosis at the cost of a smaller size by raising their stress hormones, an example of adaptive developmental plasticity. Chemicals that interfere with thyroid signalling, such as perchlorate, polychlorinated biphenyls and some pesticides, disrupt metamorphosis and are tested on *Xenopus* tadpoles in toxicological assays.

## 49.6. Skin, Toxins and Water

### 49.6.1. Structure and Glands

The amphibian skin consists of a thin epidermis of a few cell layers with a single thin keratinized outer layer, shed regularly in one piece and often eaten, and a dermis containing glands, pigment cells, blood vessels and, in caecilians, scales. The glands are of two kinds, both derived from the epidermis and lying in the dermis: mucous glands, small and numerous, whose secretion keeps the skin moist for gas exchange, makes it slippery and contains antimicrobial substances; and granular (serous, poison) glands, larger and often concentrated in particular regions such as the parotoid glands behind the eyes of toads and the dorsolateral ridges of frogs, which secrete defensive compounds ([7.17.57](/7.17.57)).

The diversity of skin compounds is enormous: peptides (bradykinins, antimicrobial peptides such as the magainins of *Xenopus*, opioid peptides such as dermorphin), biogenic amines (serotonin, bufotenine), steroids (the bufadienolides of toads), tetrodotoxin (in newts of the genus *Taricha*, the same toxin as in pufferfishes) and alkaloids. The poison frogs of tropical America (Dendrobatidae) and the mantellas of Madagascar sequester alkaloids from their diet of ants and mites; *Phyllobates terribilis*, whose skin contains batrachotoxin that keeps sodium channels open, is among the most toxic animals known, and its toxin is probably derived from small beetles in its diet. Frogs raised in captivity on other food are not toxic. Many toxic amphibians are brightly coloured, an example of **aposematism** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)); the fire-bellied toads (*Bombina*) show their red or orange belly in the unken reflex, arching the body and raising the limbs.

The colours of amphibians are produced by **chromatophores** in the dermis arranged in a dermal chromatophore unit: yellow xanthophores at the top, reflecting iridophores containing guanine platelets in the middle, and melanophores at the bottom, whose processes extend upward around the others. Green colour arises from blue light scattered by the iridophores and filtered through the yellow pigment of the xanthophores; frogs can change colour slowly by dispersing or aggregating melanin under hormonal control (melanocyte-stimulating hormone).

### 49.6.2. Cutaneous Respiration

All amphibians exchange gases through their skin (*cutaneous respiration*), which is thin, moist and richly supplied with capillaries; in frogs the skin receives blood from a cutaneous artery branching from the pulmocutaneous arch ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)). The skin typically handles a larger share of the CO₂ elimination than of the oxygen uptake, because CO₂ diffuses about 20 times more readily than O₂ through tissue; in many frogs more than half of the CO₂ leaves through the skin. The share of oxygen taken up through the skin depends on body size, temperature and activity, as the following model shows.

**Example 49.6.1 (Skin Breathing and Body Size)** Let the capacity of the skin for oxygen uptake be limited by diffusion and proportional to the skin area, $\dot V_{\mathrm{skin,max}} = c\,M^{2/3}$ for an animal of mass $M$ of constant shape, while the oxygen demand of resting metabolism scales as $\dot V_{\mathrm{O_2}} = b\,M^{3/4}$ (**Kleiber's law**, [7.14.30](/7.14.30)). The share that the skin can supply is

$$
\frac{\dot V_{\mathrm{skin,max}}}{\dot V_{\mathrm{O_2}}} = \frac{c}{b}\,M^{2/3 - 3/4} = \frac{c}{b}\,M^{-1/12}.
$$
The exponent is small: a thousandfold increase in mass reduces the share by a factor of only $1000^{1/12} \approx 1.8$. Temperature has a larger effect. Diffusion through the skin increases little with temperature ($Q_{10} \approx 1.2$), whereas metabolic rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C ($Q_{10} \approx 2.2$), so a rise from 5 to 25 °C reduces the share the skin can supply by a factor of $(2.2/1.2)^2 \approx 3.4$. Activity raises demand further. Skin breathing is therefore sufficient for small or large amphibians only at low temperatures and low activity, which is why lungless salamanders live in cool habitats, and why many amphibians that rely heavily on the skin increase its area with folds (the giant salamanders, the Lake Titicaca frog *Telmatobius culeus*) or with hair-like vascular papillae (the hairy frog *Trichobatrachus robustus*, males during the breeding season).

### 49.6.3. Water Balance

The permeable skin that serves respiration also loses water by evaporation about as fast as a free water surface of the same area. Most amphibians therefore live in moist places, are active at night or in rain, and reduce exposure by posture (pressing the body against the substrate) and by aggregating. They take up water through the skin rather than by drinking, especially through a "seat patch" of highly vascularized, permeable skin on the underside of the pelvic region, whose permeability is increased by arginine vasotocin, the amphibian antidiuretic hormone, through the insertion of aquaporin channels. The urinary bladder stores dilute urine as a water reserve, which can be reabsorbed in dry conditions.

Some frogs have evolved remarkable resistance to drying. The South American tree frogs *Phyllomedusa* wipe their skin with lipid secretions from skin glands and excrete uric acid instead of urea, becoming nearly as waterproof as reptiles; Australian water-holding frogs (*Ranoidea platycephala*, formerly *Cyclorana*) burrow and form a cocoon of shed skin layers during drought, surviving underground for months or years. Several species tolerate freezing: the wood frog *Lithobates sylvaticus* of North America and the Siberian salamander *Salamandrella keyserlingii*, which lives in northeastern China and survives temperatures of −35 °C, accumulate glucose or glycerol as cryoprotectants and survive the freezing of a large part of their body water ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)). Only a few amphibians tolerate salt water; the crab-eating frog *Fejervarya cancrivora* of the mangroves of Southeast Asia and southern China raises its blood urea concentration, like a shark, and lives in brackish water.

## 49.7. The Representative Animal: the Frog

The Chinese textbooks describe the black-spotted pond frog *Pelophylax nigromaculatus* (often listed as *Rana nigromaculata*), the common "green frog" of the paddy fields and ponds of eastern Asia, as the representative amphibian.

- *External features.* The body is divided into head and trunk, without neck or tail. Large eyes with movable eyelids and a nictitating membrane; a pair of external nostrils with valves at the tip of the snout; a round tympanic membrane behind each eye; in males a pair of vocal sacs at the corners of the mouth. The forelimbs have four digits, the longer hindlimbs five, with webs between the toes. The skin is smooth, with dorsolateral folds and a green or brown pattern with dark spots.
- *Skeleton.* The skull is flat and broad, with large orbits, reduced dermal bones and two occipital condyles; the vertebral column has eight procoelous presacral vertebrae (the eighth amphicoelous), one sacral vertebra with broad transverse processes, and the urostyle; there are no ribs. The pectoral girdle is firmisternal, with a sternum; the pelvic girdle has long ilia ([7.17.60](/7.17.60), [7.17.61](/7.17.61)).
- *Muscles.* The segmental myomeres of fishes are replaced in the trunk by sheets of muscle; the limb muscles are large, especially the hindlimb extensors.
- *Digestion.* The mouth has small conical teeth on the upper jaw (maxilla and premaxilla) and on the vomers, none on the lower jaw; a sticky tongue attached in front and free behind; a stomach, a coiled small intestine and a short large intestine opening into the cloaca; a three-lobed liver with a gall bladder and a pancreas.
- *Respiration.* The adult breathes with a pair of thin-walled, sac-like lungs with internal septa, ventilated by the buccal pump ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.3)), through the lining of the mouth and through the skin. Air enters through the nostrils and choanae; a larynx with vocal cords produces the calls.
- *Circulation.* The heart has a sinus venosus, two atria (the left receiving oxygenated blood from the lungs, the right deoxygenated blood from the body and oxygenated blood from the skin) and one ventricle, with a spiral valve in the conus arteriosus that separates the flows partly. Three pairs of arterial arches leave the conus: the carotid, systemic and pulmocutaneous arches; the circulation is double but incompletely separated ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)).
- *Excretion and reproduction.* A pair of mesonephric kidneys drain by the archinephric ducts (which also carry sperm in males) into the cloaca; a thin-walled urinary bladder opens from the cloaca. Females have paired ovaries and long, coiled oviducts that secrete the egg jelly; fat bodies at the top of the gonads store energy for breeding. Fertilization is external in amplexus; eggs are laid in clumps in shallow water in spring.
- *Nervous system and sense organs.* A brain with larger cerebral hemispheres than in fishes, large optic lobes and a small cerebellum; ten pairs of cranial nerves. The eye accommodates by moving the lens forward; the ear has a middle ear with the tympanic membrane and the columella (stapes), connected to the pharynx by the Eustachian tube, besides the operculum ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)); a vomeronasal organ in the nose; the lateral line is lost at metamorphosis.

## 49.8. The Decline of Amphibians

Amphibians are the most threatened class of vertebrates. The second global assessment of the IUCN found that 41 % of the assessed species are threatened with extinction (Luedtke et al. 2023), compared with about 26 % of mammals and 13 % of birds; at least 37 species have gone extinct since 1980, and many more have not been seen for decades. Habitat loss is the most widespread threat, followed by disease, climate change and overexploitation.

The most dramatic declines, beginning in the 1980s in remote and protected mountain forests of Central America and eastern Australia, were caused by chytridiomycosis, an infection of the skin by the chytrid fungus *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis* (Bd), described in 1998 ([7.11.21](/7.11.21)). The **chytrid** invades the keratinized layers of the skin and disrupts its exchange of electrolytes; infected frogs lose sodium and potassium, and die of cardiac arrest. Bd has contributed to the decline of at least 501 species and to the presumed extinction of about 90 (Scheele et al. 2019), the greatest loss of biodiversity attributable to a disease. Genomic analyses traced the origin of the pandemic lineage to East Asia, probably the Korean peninsula, from where it was spread around the world in the twentieth century by the international trade in amphibians (O'Hanlon et al. 2018). Asian amphibians, which coevolved with the fungus, tolerate it. A second species, *B. salamandrivorans* (Bsal), also from East Asia, where newts carry it without disease, was introduced into Europe with the pet trade and has killed most fire salamanders in parts of the Netherlands, Belgium and Germany since 2010; it is a threat to the salamanders of North America, the world centre of salamander diversity.

In China the main threats are habitat loss, pollution, overharvesting for food and medicine, and introduced species, especially the American bullfrog *Lithobates catesbeianus*, farmed since the 1960s, which has established feral populations in many provinces and competes with and preys on native frogs. The giant salamanders are critically endangered (§49.3.1).

## 49.9. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

The Chinese textbooks treat the amphibians as a class of tetrapods with three orders, Gymnophiona (or Apoda), Urodela and Anura, and describe the class as the transitional group between aquatic and terrestrial vertebrates, with the frog as the representative animal. The ordinal classification is unchanged; the differences concern the origin of the class, the numbers of species and the names of species.

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Amphibia including the extinct "labyrinthodonts" and "lepospondyls" | Lissamphibia is the clade of living amphibians; the Palaeozoic groups are stem tetrapods, stem amphibians and stem amniotes ([7.17.48](/7.17.48)) |
| Living amphibians possibly polyphyletic, with Urodela and Gymnophiona closer | Lissamphibia is monophyletic; frogs and salamanders form the Batrachia, with caecilians as the sister group; most likely derived from dissorophoid temnospondyls |
| About 2500 species (165 caecilians, 500 salamanders, 4800 frogs) | About 8800 species: 220 caecilians, 830 salamanders, 7800 frogs; many new species are described every year |
| Amphibians as a "transitional" group between fishes and reptiles | Living amphibians are a specialized sister lineage of the amniotes, not their ancestors; the transition from water to land took place in the stem tetrapods |
| Families Discoglossidae (with *Bombina*) and Ranidae in a broad sense | Bombinatoridae (*Bombina*) and Alytidae (*Alytes*); the broad Ranidae has been divided into Ranidae, Dicroglossidae (*Hoplobatrachus*, *Quasipaa*, *Fejervarya*) and others |
| *Bufo bufo*, *Rana nigromaculata*, *Rana plancyi*, *Hyla immaculata*, *Rhacophorus dennysi*, *Hynobius keyserlingi* | *Bufo gargarizans*, *Pelophylax nigromaculatus*, *Pelophylax plancyi*, *Dryophytes immaculatus*, *Zhangixalus dennysi*, *Salamandrella keyserlingii* (current names) |
| *Andrias davidianus* as one species | At least three species described (*A. davidianus*, *A. sligoi*, *A. jiangxiensis*), with further undescribed lineages |
| Climate warming as the main cause of chytrid outbreaks | Chytridiomycosis spread mainly by trade in infected animals from East Asia; climate may modulate outbreaks locally |

The textbook characterization of the amphibians (bare glandular skin used in respiration, pentadactyl limbs, two occipital condyles, a sacral vertebra, lungs ventilated by the buccal pump, a heart with two atria and one ventricle, incomplete double circulation, mesonephric kidneys, eggs and larvae in water and metamorphosis, a middle ear with tympanum and columella in frogs) and of the three orders and their main families (the Chinese giant salamander, the Hynobiidae, Salamandridae and Proteidae; the Pipidae, Bufonidae, Hylidae, Ranidae, Rhacophoridae and Microhylidae with their Chinese representatives) is included in §49.2–§49.7, with the current names.

## Summary

- Lissamphibians (Definition 49.1.1): pedicellate teeth, glandular skin used for gas exchange, the papilla amphibiorum and the opercularis system; about 8800 species. Monophyletic; Batrachia (frogs and salamanders) plus caecilians; probably derived from dissorophoid temnospondyls; Jurassic salamanders of northern China document early salamander lineages.
- Frogs (Definition 49.2.1): a jumping body plan with a short trunk, urostyle, long ilia, radioulna, tibiofibula and elongated tarsals; take-off requires elastic power amplification (Example 49.2.1); about 7800 species, most neobatrachians diversifying after the end-Cretaceous extinction.
- Salamanders (Definition 49.3.1): tails, similar limbs, carnivorous larvae with external gills; the critically endangered Chinese giant salamanders (several species); lungless salamanders with ballistic tongues; paedomorphosis by neoteny or progenesis, the axolotl as a model of regeneration.
- Caecilians (Definition 49.4.1): limbless burrowers with annuli, tentacles, a second jaw-closing mechanism, internal fertilization, viviparity and maternal dermatophagy.
- Reproduction: eggs without shells, external or internal fertilization, about forty reproductive modes in frogs, tadpoles and metamorphosis controlled by thyroid hormones, direct development and parental care.
- Skin: mucous and granular glands, toxins (bufadienolides, tetrodotoxin, dietary alkaloids), aposematic colours, chromatophore units; cutaneous respiration limited by temperature more than by size (Example 49.6.1); water uptake through the seat patch, waterproofing, cocoons and freeze tolerance.
- The frog (*Pelophylax nigromaculatus*) as the representative animal; 41 % of amphibians threatened; chytridiomycosis spread from East Asia by trade.

## Questions

1. Which characters unite the three orders of living amphibians, and why are characters such as "eggs without amniotic membranes" not evidence of their monophyly?

   **Answer** Pedicellate teeth, the papilla amphibiorum of the inner ear, the operculum moved by the opercularis muscle (in frogs and salamanders), green rods in the retina, fat bodies associated with the gonads and a glandular, scaleless skin used in respiration are derived characters shared by lissamphibians; molecular phylogenies also support the group. Eggs without amniotic membranes, a three-chambered heart and dependence on water for breeding are primitive characters inherited from the earliest tetrapods and fishes; they were present in the common ancestor of all tetrapods and are shared with many extinct non-amniotes. A group defined by such plesiomorphies could be paraphyletic, so they do not indicate that the three orders form a clade.
2. Explain how each feature of the frog skeleton contributes to jumping.

   **Answer** The elongated hindlimbs with an extra segment formed by the astragalus and calcaneum extend over a long distance, so the body reaches a high take-off speed with moderate acceleration. The long ilia articulating with the sacral diapophyses form a joint that lets the trunk tilt upward at take-off, directing the thrust. The short, stiff vertebral column without ribs, with the urostyle, transmits the force of the legs to the body without bending. The fused radioulna and tibiofibula are strong, light struts. The robust pectoral girdle and forelimbs absorb landing forces. Elastic tendons allow muscles to store energy before take-off and release it in a short recoil, producing more power than the muscles alone.
3. A lungless salamander is moved from a stream at 10 °C to one at 25 °C. Using Example 49.6.1, predict what happens and how it might respond.

   **Answer** Its oxygen demand rises by a factor of about $2.2^{1.5} \approx 3.3$, while the diffusive capacity of its skin rises only by $1.2^{1.5} \approx 1.3$. If its skin and mouth lining just met demand at 10 °C, it would now be able to supply only about 40 % of the demand at rest, and less during activity. It would become hypoxic, unless it reduced activity, sought cooler microhabitats (under rocks, in seepages), increased skin perfusion and buccal ventilation, or lowered its metabolism. In the long term, populations of lungless salamanders are limited to cool habitats, and warming could shrink their ranges.
4. Why do thyroid hormones cause the tail of a tadpole to degenerate but the limbs to grow?

   **Answer** The response depends on the tissue, not on the hormone. Thyroid hormone acts through nuclear receptors that regulate different target genes in different cells; in the tail, they activate genes for apoptosis and for matrix-degrading enzymes, in the limb buds genes for cell proliferation and differentiation. The timing also differs: limb tissues express receptors and the deiodinase that converts T₄ to the more active T₃ early, so they respond to the low hormone levels of premetamorphosis, while the tail responds only to the high levels at climax; tail tissue also expresses deiodinases that inactivate the hormone until late. The programmes are tissue-autonomous: a tail tip grafted onto the trunk still degenerates at metamorphosis, while an eye grafted to the tail survives.
5. Why are the Chinese giant salamanders still critically endangered although millions are kept in farms?

   **Answer** Farm populations are not a substitute for wild ones. The farms were founded with animals from different river systems and mixed lineages that are now recognized as distinct species, so farmed animals are genetic mixtures; releasing them, as has been done on a large scale, introduces foreign genes into the remaining wild populations and may spread diseases (ranavirus, chytrid). Demand for wild animals as food and for breeding continues, and the mountain streams they need are affected by dams, mining and pollution. Surveys found very few genuinely wild animals. Conservation requires identifying and protecting the remaining wild lineages, stopping releases of mixed farm stock, enforcing protection, and breeding programmes based on genetically identified founders.
6. How did the international trade in amphibians spread chytridiomycosis, and why do many Asian amphibians not die from it?

   **Answer** Genomic analyses of Bd isolates from around the world show the greatest genetic diversity and the oldest lineages in East Asia, with the pandemic lineage derived from there and spread in the twentieth century, coinciding with the expansion of trade in amphibians (for food, pets, laboratory animals such as *Xenopus*, and bullfrogs). Infected animals that tolerate the fungus carried it to new regions, where naive species without coevolved resistance died. Asian amphibians have coexisted with Bd for a long time, and selection has favoured immune and skin defences (antimicrobial peptides, skin microbiota, immune responses) that keep infections low, so they act as reservoirs; the same applies to Asian newts and Bsal.
7. (Open) The model of Example 49.6.1 predicts that skin breathing depends weakly on size but strongly on temperature. Design a study that tests this prediction with real animals.

   **Answer** Measure the partitioning of oxygen uptake between skin and lungs in several species of different sizes (for example small and large frogs and salamanders) at several temperatures, using a divided respirometer in which the head and lungs breathe from one chamber and the body skin from another (or masks that separate pulmonary from cutaneous exchange), at rest and during standardized activity. Fit the skin share against log body mass and temperature, within species (ontogenetic series) and across species with phylogenetic comparative methods. The model predicts a shallow negative slope with mass (exponent near −1/12) and a strong negative effect of temperature (a ratio of Q₁₀ values near 1.8). Deviations would reveal adaptations such as skin folds, changes in skin perfusion and capillary density, or temperature-dependent regulation of skin blood flow, which could be measured with microsphere methods.
