Inside the exoskeleton described in [7.17.23](/7.17.23) and [7.17.24](/7.17.24), the organs of an insect lie in a body cavity filled with hemolymph. This chapter describes their structure and arrangement, system by system, following the representative locust of [7.17.22](/7.17.22) and noting the main variations among orders. Two features distinguish insects from vertebrates and shape the whole internal organization: oxygen is delivered to the tissues in the gas phase through tubes, the tracheae, so the blood does not carry oxygen; and excretion and water balance are achieved by blind-ending tubules and the hindgut rather than by filtering kidneys.

The physiology of these systems is treated in [7.14](/7.14): circulation in [7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.5.2), the tracheal system in [7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.5), excretion in [7.14.23](/7.14.23#sec-23.7.2), digestion in [7.14.26](/7.14.26#sec-26.10.2) and hormones in [7.14.32](/7.14.32#sec-32.9.3). Here we concentrate on structure and on the quantitative questions that structure raises, above all the limits that diffusion places on the tracheal system. The sense organs follow in [7.17.27](/7.17.27) and the action of hormones in development in [7.17.29](/7.17.29).

## 26.1. The Body Cavity

The insect body cavity is a **hemocoel** ([7.17.3](/7.17.3)): a space between the organs, filled with hemolymph and bounded by the basement membrane of the body wall. It is divided by two horizontal fibromuscular sheets. The *dorsal diaphragm*, below the heart and attached to the terga, separates a dorsal pericardial sinus; the *ventral diaphragm*, above the nerve cord, separates a ventral perineural sinus; between them the perivisceral sinus contains the gut, the reproductive organs, the Malpighian tubules and most of the fat body. The diaphragms are perforated and pulse, so that they guide the circulation of hemolymph: hemolymph flows forward in the dorsal vessel, backward through the perivisceral sinus and the appendages, and up into the pericardial sinus, where it re-enters the heart.

## 26.2. The Digestive System

### 26.2.1. Foregut, Midgut and Hindgut

The insect gut is a tube from mouth to anus, divided into three regions of different embryonic origin. The foregut (stomodeum) and hindgut (proctodeum) are ectodermal invaginations lined with cuticle, which is shed at every molt; the midgut (mesenteron) is endodermal, without cuticle, and is the main site of digestion and absorption.

The *foregut* consists of the pharynx, which in sucking insects forms a pump together with the cibarium; the esophagus; the *crop*, a dilatation for storage; and the *proventriculus*. In chewing insects such as cockroaches, crickets and many beetles, the proventriculus is a gizzard with a muscular wall and cuticular teeth that grind food; in others it is a simple valve. In honey bees it is a valve that separates nectar stored in the crop ("honey stomach") from food passed to the midgut; in flies and moths the crop is a lateral diverticulum in which liquid food is stored. A stomodeal (cardiac) valve projects into the midgut and prevents backflow.

The *midgut* (ventriculus, called the stomach in Chinese textbooks) usually bears blind pouches, the *gastric caeca*, near its anterior end: six in the locust, from two to many in other insects. Its epithelium consists of columnar cells with microvilli, which secrete enzymes and absorb nutrients, endocrine cells, and regenerative cells in nests (crypts) from which the epithelium is renewed.

**Definition 26.2.1 (Peritrophic Membrane)** The **peritrophic membrane** (peritrophic matrix) is a thin, semipermeable sheath of chitin, proteins and glycoproteins that surrounds the food in the midgut of most insects, separating the lumen into an endoperitrophic space containing the food and an ectoperitrophic space next to the epithelium.

The peritrophic membrane protects the epithelium from abrasion, toxins and pathogens, and it compartmentalizes digestion. It is permeable to small molecules and to many enzymes; digestion begins inside it and is completed on the cell surface. Fluid secreted at the posterior midgut and absorbed at the anterior midgut and caeca flows forward in the ectoperitrophic space while food moves backward: this countercurrent flux recycles enzymes and increases the efficiency of digestion. Two types are distinguished: type I is delaminated from the whole midgut epithelium, often in response to a meal (many beetles, adult mosquitoes after a blood meal), and type II is secreted continuously as a sleeve by a ring of cells at the anterior end of the midgut (flies, caterpillars). Many sap-sucking Hemiptera have lost it and have instead a membrane of lipids around the microvilli (perimicrovillar membrane).

The *hindgut* begins at the pylorus, where the Malpighian tubules open, and consists of the ileum, colon and rectum. The rectum has thickened areas of epithelium, the *rectal pads* (rectal papillae, six in the locust), which reabsorb water, ions and other useful substances from the excreta before they are expelled (Section 26.3). The salivary glands are usually paired labial glands in the thorax, opening on the labium; in caterpillars and caddisfly larvae they produce silk, in mosquitoes anticoagulants, and in aphids and bugs the saliva that forms the stylet sheath and that contains digestive enzymes or effectors that manipulate the host plant ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)).

### 26.2.2. Variation with Diet

The gut reflects the food. Insects that feed on solid food have a well-developed proventriculus and a short gut; liquid feeders store food in the crop and have a powerful sucking pump. Insects that feed on large volumes of dilute sap must get rid of excess water. In many Hemiptera, especially xylem and phloem feeders, the anterior midgut, the posterior midgut, the beginning of the hindgut and the Malpighian tubules are bound together in a *filter chamber*, in which water passes directly from the anterior midgut to the hindgut, bypassing the absorptive midgut, so that the nutrients are concentrated. Lepidopteran larvae feeding on leaves rich in tannins have an extremely alkaline midgut (pH up to 11–12), which prevents tannins from binding to proteins. Wood-feeding termites have a large hindgut paunch, a fermentation chamber containing cellulose-digesting symbionts: flagellate protists in the "lower" termites and bacteria in the higher termites ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)).

Many insects that live on nutritionally poor diets, such as plant sap, blood or wood, depend on symbiotic microorganisms that supply amino acids, vitamins or digestive enzymes. Obligate symbionts are often housed inside specialized cells, *bacteriocytes* (mycetocytes), grouped into an organ, the bacteriome, in the body cavity or the gut wall, and transmitted to the eggs. The aphid–*Buchnera* symbiosis is treated in [7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.4).

## 26.3. Excretion and the Fat Body

### 26.3.1. The Malpighian Tubule–Rectal Complex

**Malpighian tubules** ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)) are slender blind tubules that open into the gut at the junction of midgut and hindgut and lie free in the hemolymph. Their number varies from two (some scale insects) or four (flies, many beetles) to more than 200; the locust has about 300, in bundles. They are absent in springtails and aphids. The tubules produce a primary urine by actively secreting potassium ions (by a proton-pumping V-ATPase coupled to a cation/proton exchanger), followed by chloride and water; uric acid, other nitrogenous wastes and foreign substances such as plant toxins are also secreted into the lumen. The urine flows into the hindgut, where the rectal pads reabsorb water, ions, sugars and amino acids according to the needs of the insect, and uric acid precipitates and is excreted with the feces. Most terrestrial insects excrete nitrogen as uric acid, which can be eliminated almost without water. The physiology of this system, including its hormonal control by diuretic and antidiuretic hormones, is in [7.14.23](/7.14.23#sec-23.7.2).

In the larvae of many beetles (such as mealworms, Tenebrionidae) and of Lepidoptera, the distal parts of the Malpighian tubules are closely applied to the rectum and enclosed by a perinephric membrane: in this *cryptonephric system*, the tubules extract water from the rectal contents by creating a high osmotic concentration around the rectum, so that the feces are extremely dry. Mealworms can even absorb water vapour from humid air through the rectum.

Other excretory cells are the *nephrocytes* (pericardial cells along the heart and garland cells around the esophagus), which take up proteins and colloids from the hemolymph by endocytosis, and the urate cells of the fat body.

### 26.3.2. The Fat Body

**Definition 26.3.1 (Fat Body)** The **fat body** is a loose tissue of cells lying in the hemocoel, mainly in the abdomen, beneath the body wall and around the viscera, which stores lipids, glycogen and proteins and is the main site of intermediary metabolism, of synthesis of hemolymph proteins and of the humoral immune response.

The main cells of the fat body are the trophocytes (adipocytes), which store triglycerides and glycogen and synthesize the hemolymph sugar trehalose, storage proteins (hexamerins) taken up again at metamorphosis, the yolk precursor vitellogenin and antimicrobial peptides. Urate cells (urocytes) store uric acid ("storage excretion", as in cockroaches), and in some insects bacteriocytes are embedded in the fat body. The fat body thus combines functions of the liver and adipose tissue of vertebrates. Its development is closely linked to the reproductive cycle and to metamorphosis, when it is reorganized ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)). Oenocytes, large cells near the epidermis or among the fat body, synthesize cuticular hydrocarbons and have liver-like functions in lipid metabolism.

## 26.4. The Respiratory System

### 26.4.1. Spiracles, Tracheae and Tracheoles

The **tracheal system** ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.5)) is a system of air-filled tubes that open on the body surface and branch to all tissues.

**Definition 26.4.1 (Spiracle)** A **spiracle** is an external opening of the tracheal system on the body wall, usually provided with an atrium, filtering hairs and a closing apparatus operated by muscles; the ground plan has ten pairs, on the meso- and metathorax and on abdominal segments 1–8.

**Definition 26.4.2 (Tracheole)** A **tracheole** is a fine terminal branch of the tracheal system, less than 1 µm in diameter, formed within a tracheal end cell, whose tip is in contact with or indented into the tissue cells and is filled with liquid at rest.

The tracheae arise as invaginations of the epidermis; they are lined with cuticle whose spiral thickenings, the *taenidia*, keep them open like the rings of a vacuum-cleaner hose. Their cuticle is shed with the exoskeleton at each molt; the tracheoles are not molted. Tracheae are connected by longitudinal trunks and transverse commissures, so that air can flow along the body. In many active insects parts of the tracheae are expanded into thin-walled *air sacs* without taenidia, which serve as bellows for ventilation, as reservoirs, and in flying insects reduce weight; they occupy much of the head, thorax and abdomen of bees and flies. The tracheoles are especially dense in flight muscle, where they penetrate between the myofibrils, and end close to the mitochondria.

The arrangement of functional spiracles defines respiratory types, which are used in particular to describe larvae:

| Type | Functional spiracles | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Holopneustic | All ten pairs (two thoracic, eight abdominal) | Most adults and many nymphs (locust) |
| Peripneustic | Nine pairs, one thoracic pair closed | Many larvae of Lepidoptera and Coleoptera |
| Amphipneustic | Only the first thoracic and the last abdominal pairs | Larvae of many higher flies |
| Metapneustic | Only the last abdominal pair | Mosquito larvae (at the tip of the siphon), larvae of some flies |
| Apneustic | None; closed tracheal system | Many aquatic larvae and nymphs (mayflies, damselflies, some midges) |

### 26.4.2. Diffusion and Ventilation

Oxygen diffuses in air about $2 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and in water about $2 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, ten thousand times more slowly, and air contains about thirty times more oxygen per volume than air-saturated water. By delivering oxygen in the gas phase almost to the mitochondria, the tracheal system avoids the slow step of diffusion through liquid over all but the last few micrometres. In small or inactive insects diffusion along the tracheae is sufficient (Krogh 1920). Large and active insects add convection: rhythmic contractions of the abdomen compress the air sacs and pump air in and out (in the locust air enters through the anterior spiracles and leaves through the posterior ones, so that it flows in one direction through the main trunks), and in flight the deformations of the thorax ventilate the thoracic air sacs. X-ray imaging of living insects showed that even the tracheae of the head and thorax of beetles and crickets are rhythmically compressed (Westneat and colleagues 2003). At rest, the liquid at the tips of the tracheoles limits diffusion; during intense activity metabolites raise the osmotic concentration of the surrounding tissue, the liquid is drawn out and air moves closer to the mitochondria.

Many insects at rest show discontinuous gas exchange: long periods with closed spiracles, a phase of rapid fluttering and a burst of open spiracles in which accumulated $\ce{CO2}$ is released ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.5)).

### 26.4.3. The Diffusion Limit to Body Size

Because the tracheal system relies on diffusion over at least part of its length, it may limit body size. A simple model shows the scaling. Consider a column of tissue of length $L$ supplied from one end by tracheae that occupy a fraction $\phi$ of its cross-section; the tissue consumes oxygen uniformly at a rate $M$ per unit volume. In the steady state the gradient of oxygen concentration in the tracheal gas, $c(x)$, must carry the consumption of all the tissue beyond $x$:

$$
\phi D \frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}x} = -M (L - x),
$$

which integrates to a total drop $\Delta c = M L^2/(2 \phi D)$. If the partial pressure must not fall below a minimum $P_{\min}$ at the end of the column, then with $c = P/(RT)$

$$
L_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{2\, \phi\, D\, (P_{\mathrm{O_2}} - P_{\min})}{M R T}}. \tag{26.1}
$$

The maximal length grows only with the square root of the atmospheric oxygen partial pressure and falls with the square root of the metabolic rate.

**Example 26.4.1** With $D = 2 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, $\phi = 0.1$, $P_{\mathrm{O_2}} = 21\,\mathrm{kPa}$, $P_{\min} = 2\,\mathrm{kPa}$, $T = 298\,\mathrm{K}$ and the oxygen consumption of active flight muscle $M \approx 1.5\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}\,s^{-1}}$, Eq. (26.1) gives $L_{\max} = \sqrt{2 \times 0.1 \times 2\times10^{-5} \times 1.9\times10^{4}/(1.5 \times 8.314 \times 298)} \approx 4.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. For resting tissue with a hundred times lower $M$, $L_{\max}$ is ten times larger, about 45 mm. A large insect must therefore ventilate its tracheae by convection over most of their length and restrict diffusion to short terminal branches, and the volume taken by tracheae grows with body size.

During the late Carboniferous and early Permian, about 300 million years ago, atmospheric oxygen probably reached about 30% (compared with 21% today, [7.10.29](/7.10.29)), and some insects were giants: griffinflies (Meganisoptera) with wingspans of 70 cm, mayflies of 45 cm, and huge relatives of cockroaches and palaeodictyopterans. The *oxygen hypothesis* attributes their size to the higher oxygen level, which would have relaxed the diffusion limit (Graham and colleagues 1995). Several observations support it. Many insects reared in hyperoxia grow larger and in hypoxia smaller, although the response varies between species; the proportion of the leg occupied by tracheae increases disproportionately with body size in beetles (Kaiser and colleagues 2007), so the largest living beetles appear close to a limit; and the maximum wing length of fossil insects tracked estimated atmospheric oxygen over 200 million years, until the Early Cretaceous, when it decoupled from oxygen and declined, perhaps because flying birds became predators of large insects (Clapham and Karr 2012). The model above shows why the effect of oxygen is modest: a 40% higher oxygen level extends the diffusion distance by less than a third, so oxygen probably permitted giants rather than causing them, and other factors (absence of flying vertebrate predators, warm climates) were involved. Larvae, aquatic in many giants' relatives, may have been more strongly limited than adults.

### 26.4.4. Respiration in Water

Insects have colonized fresh water many times, and their respiratory adaptations show the problems of breathing air in water and of extracting oxygen from water. Three solutions are found.

*Breathing atmospheric air through the surface.* Mosquito larvae and pupae hang from the surface film and breathe through siphons or respiratory trumpets with hydrofuge (water-repellent) margins; the rat-tailed maggot of *Eristalis* has a telescopic siphon several times the length of its body. Larvae of some leaf beetles (*Donacia*) and mosquitoes (*Mansonia*) pierce the air-filled tissues of aquatic plants with their spiracular siphons.

*Gills.* Insects with closed (apneustic) tracheal systems take up oxygen by diffusion through the cuticle, often through thin outgrowths rich in tracheae, the *tracheal gills*: abdominal gill plates of mayfly nymphs, caudal lamellae of damselfly nymphs, filamentous gills of stonefly nymphs and caddisfly larvae, and the rectal gills of dragonfly nymphs, which are ventilated by pumping water in and out of the rectum (and expelled forcefully for jet propulsion). Some midge larvae (bloodworms, *Chironomus*) living in oxygen-poor mud have hemoglobin in their hemolymph.

*Air stores.* Many aquatic beetles and bugs carry air down with them, under the elytra, among hydrofuge hairs or as a bubble on the body surface, into which the spiracles open.

**Definition 26.4.3 (Physical Gill)** A **physical gill** (compressible gas gill) is a bubble of air carried by a diving insect and connected with its spiracles, into which oxygen diffuses from the water as the insect uses the oxygen in the bubble, so that the insect obtains more oxygen than the bubble initially contained; the bubble shrinks as nitrogen diffuses out, and must be renewed at the surface.

The physical gill works because the gas exchange of a bubble is governed by partial pressures. As the insect consumes oxygen, the oxygen partial pressure in the bubble falls below that of the air-saturated water (21 kPa), and oxygen diffuses in; at the same time, the nitrogen partial pressure in the bubble rises above that in the water (80 kPa), both because oxygen is removed and because the bubble is under additional hydrostatic pressure, and nitrogen diffuses out. Since the permeation of oxygen into the bubble is about twice as fast as that of nitrogen out of it (the product of diffusion coefficient and solubility in water is about 2.1 times larger for oxygen), every mole of nitrogen lost lets about two moles of oxygen in. The bubble therefore supplies several times its initial oxygen before it collapses. The gain is largest near the surface and at moderate rates of consumption; at greater depths the excess hydrostatic pressure drives nitrogen out faster, and at very low consumption the bubble is lost by nitrogen diffusion before the oxygen gain has had time to accumulate.

**Definition 26.4.4 (Plastron)** A **plastron** is a thin, permanent layer of air held on the body surface of an aquatic insect by a dense pile of hydrofuge hairs or a cuticular meshwork, which resists collapse under hydrostatic pressure and therefore acts as an incompressible gas gill.

Because the plastron volume is fixed by the hairs, nitrogen cannot escape by compression of the bubble, and the plastron can serve indefinitely as long as the water is well oxygenated. The water bug *Aphelocheirus* has millions of hairs per square millimetre and remains submerged permanently; the eggs of many terrestrial insects that may be flooded, and the spiracular gills of some pupae, have plastrons as well. Plastron respiration requires well-aerated water and fails in stagnant, warm water, where a plastron would lose oxygen to the water.

## 26.5. The Circulatory System

### 26.5.1. The Dorsal Vessel and Accessory Pulsatile Organs

Insects have an **open circulatory system** ([7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.5.2)): hemolymph is pumped by a dorsal tube and flows freely through the hemocoel.

**Definition 26.5.1 (Dorsal Vessel)** The **dorsal vessel** is the main pumping organ of insects, a muscular tube along the dorsal midline above the dorsal diaphragm, divided into a posterior heart, perforated by paired valved openings (ostia) through which hemolymph enters from the pericardial sinus, and an anterior aorta without ostia that opens into the head.

The heart is held in place by alary muscles fanning out to the terga. Peristaltic waves of contraction move hemolymph forward; in many insects the direction of the heartbeat reverses periodically, so that hemolymph also flows backward and helps ventilation and the regulation of temperature. In the locust the heart has seven chambers (each with a pair of ostia) in the abdomen. Because the hemolymph pressure from the heart is low, *accessory pulsatile organs* at the bases of the antennae, wings and legs pump hemolymph through these long appendages. Hemolymph pressure, generated by contraction of the body wall, is also used hydraulically: to expand the wings after the final molt, to extend the proboscis of butterflies, to evert glands and genital sacs, and to split the old cuticle at ecdysis.

### 26.5.2. Hemolymph and Hemocytes

Hemolymph makes up 15–75% of the body volume in different insects. Since the tracheae deliver oxygen, it carries little or no oxygen (except in the few insects with hemoglobin) and its main transport functions are those of nutrients, hormones and wastes. Its composition differs from that of vertebrate plasma: the main sugar is the disaccharide trehalose; amino acids are at very high concentrations; and in many phytophagous insects potassium and magnesium are high and sodium low. Hemolymph is also a water reserve and, with its proteins and antimicrobial peptides, part of the immune system.

The *hemocytes* are of several types. Plasmatocytes phagocytose bacteria and form capsules around larger invaders; granular cells (granulocytes) participate in phagocytosis and coagulation; crystal cells contain prophenoloxidase, the enzyme of the melanization cascade that seals wounds and encapsulated parasites in melanin; and in *Drosophila* larvae lamellocytes differentiate in response to parasitoid wasp eggs and encapsulate them. Humoral immunity is mediated by antimicrobial peptides synthesized by the fat body in response to the Toll and Imd signalling pathways. The discovery in *Drosophila* that the Toll receptor mediates antifungal immunity (Lemaitre and colleagues 1996) led to the discovery of the Toll-like receptors of vertebrates ([7.15.6](/7.15.6#sec-6.2)).

## 26.6. The Nervous System

The central nervous system of insects is a **ventral nerve cord** ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)) with a brain above the esophagus. The brain (supraesophageal ganglion) consists of three parts, corresponding to the anterior head segments ([7.17.18](/7.17.18)):

- the *protocerebrum*, the largest, with the optic lobes (lamina, medulla and lobula complex) that process the input from the compound eyes, the ocellar nerves, the **mushroom bodies** and the central complex;
- the *deutocerebrum*, with the antennal lobes, organized into glomeruli, each receiving the axons of the olfactory receptor neurons of one receptor type, and the mechanosensory and motor centres of the antenna;
- the *tritocerebrum*, small, connected to the frontal ganglion and, by connectives that pass around the esophagus, to the subesophageal ganglion.

The *subesophageal ganglion* is formed by the fused ganglia of the mandibular, maxillary and labial segments and innervates the mouthparts and the neck. In the ventral nerve cord there are primitively three thoracic and eight abdominal ganglia (the last few fused), connected by paired connectives; in the locust there are three thoracic and five abdominal ganglia (the first abdominal ganglia fused with the metathoracic ganglion). The tendency to concentrate the ganglia is strongest in higher flies and many Hemiptera, in which all thoracic and abdominal ganglia are fused into a single mass in the thorax. A stomatogastric (visceral) nervous system, with the frontal ganglion in front of the brain and the hypocerebral ganglion behind it, controls the muscles of the foregut.

The adult brain of *Drosophila* contains about 140,000 neurons, whose complete wiring diagram has been mapped (the FlyWire connectome, 2024), and that of a honey bee about a million. The **mushroom bodies**, paired neuropils of the protocerebrum formed by thousands of intrinsic Kenyon cells, receive olfactory and, in many insects, visual information and are centres of associative learning and memory ([7.16.23](/7.16.23#sec-23.4.3), [7.16.38](/7.16.38#sec-38.4.2)); they are especially large in social Hymenoptera and in cockroaches. The *central complex*, a set of midline neuropils (protocerebral bridge, fan-shaped body, ellipsoid body, noduli), integrates sensory information for orientation and navigation: in *Drosophila* the ellipsoid body contains a ring of neurons whose activity encodes the head direction of the fly ([7.16.44](/7.16.44#sec-44.4.2)), and in locusts and bees the central complex represents the direction of polarized skylight used as a compass.

## 26.7. Endocrine Organs

Insect hormones are produced by neurosecretory cells of the brain and other ganglia (**neurosecretion**) and by several endocrine glands. The main ones form a complex behind the brain and a pair of glands in the prothorax.

**Definition 26.7.1 (Corpora Cardiaca)** The **corpora cardiaca** (singular corpus cardiacum) are a pair of neuroendocrine organs behind the brain, close to the aorta, which store and release the neurohormones produced by neurosecretory cells of the brain and also produce hormones of their own, including adipokinetic hormone.

**Definition 26.7.2 (Corpora Allata)** The **corpora allata** (singular corpus allatum) are a pair of small endocrine glands of epithelial origin behind the corpora cardiaca, connected to them by nerves, which secrete juvenile hormone.

**Definition 26.7.3 (Prothoracic Gland)** The **prothoracic glands** are a pair of diffuse endocrine glands of ectodermal origin in the prothorax of immature insects, which synthesize the molting hormone ecdysone in response to the brain neuropeptide prothoracicotropic hormone and usually degenerate after the final molt.

The brain's neurosecretory cells, mainly in the pars intercerebralis and pars lateralis of the protocerebrum, send their axons to the corpora cardiaca, which form a neurohemal organ comparable to the vertebrate neurohypophysis ([7.14.32](/7.14.32#sec-32.9.3)). The corpora cardiaca, corpora allata and the hypocerebral ganglion together form the retrocerebral complex. In larvae of higher flies, the corpora allata, corpora cardiaca and prothoracic glands are fused into a single ring gland around the aorta. Other endocrine cells are the Inka cells of the epitracheal glands, which secrete ecdysis-triggering hormone, and the endocrine cells of the midgut epithelium. In adult females, the ovaries become a major source of ecdysteroids. The hormones of development are treated in [7.17.29](/7.17.29): prothoracicotropic hormone stimulates the prothoracic glands to produce **ecdysone**, which initiates each molt, while **juvenile hormone** determines whether the molt is larval, pupal or adult ([7.5.33](/7.5.33)).

## 26.8. The Reproductive System

### 26.8.1. The Female Reproductive System

Each ovary of a female insect consists of a number of tubular ovarioles, which open into a lateral oviduct; the two lateral oviducts join into a common oviduct, which opens into a genital chamber (or vagina) behind abdominal segment 7 or 8.

**Definition 26.8.1 (Ovariole)** An **ovariole** is one of the egg tubes of which the insect ovary is composed, consisting of a terminal filament, a germarium in which oogonia divide, a vitellarium in which oocytes grow and take up yolk, each oocyte surrounded by a layer of follicle cells, and a pedicel connecting it to the lateral oviduct.

The number of ovarioles is from one per ovary in some flies to more than 2000 in termite queens; the queen honey bee has about 150–180 per ovary and a worker only a few. Within each ovariole the oocytes are arranged in order of age, the oldest at the base. Three types of ovarioles are distinguished by the presence and position of nurse cells, which supply the oocyte with RNA, ribosomes and other materials:

| Type | Nurse cells | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Panoistic | Absent; each oocyte synthesizes its own RNA | Bristletails, silverfish, mayflies, dragonflies, Orthoptera, cockroaches, stick insects, fleas |
| Polytrophic (meroistic) | A cluster of nurse cells, sister cells of the oocyte connected to it by ring canals, accompanies each oocyte down the ovariole | Earwigs, Psocodea, most Holometabola (flies, moths, bees and wasps) |
| Telotrophic (acrotrophic, meroistic) | Nurse cells remain in the germarium (the tropharium) and supply the oocytes through long nutritive cords | Hemiptera, polyphagan beetles |

Meroistic ovarioles produce eggs faster, because the nurse cells, often polyploid, supply the oocyte with large amounts of RNA and proteins; this may be one reason why they evolved independently several times. The follicle cells secrete the vitelline membrane and the eggshell (chorion), which has an opening for sperm entry, the micropyle ([7.17.28](/7.17.28)). The yolk protein precursor vitellogenin is synthesized by the fat body and taken up by the oocyte by endocytosis.

The female tract has two kinds of ectodermal appendages: the **spermatheca**, a sac with a cuticular lining in which sperm received at mating are stored, often for the whole life of the female (for years in queen ants and honey bees), with a spermathecal gland that keeps them alive; and the *accessory glands* (colleterial glands), which secrete adhesives that attach eggs to the substrate or form the egg case (ootheca) of cockroaches and mantises and the foam around the egg pod of locusts. In many Lepidoptera (Ditrysia) the female has two genital openings: a copulatory opening on segment 8 leading to a sac, the bursa copulatrix, that receives the spermatophore, and an egg-laying opening on segment 9.

### 26.8.2. The Male Reproductive System

The male has a pair of testes, each consisting of several sperm tubes (follicles) in which spermatogenesis proceeds in a sequence of cysts; the vasa deferentia carry sperm to seminal vesicles, where they are stored, and join a median ejaculatory duct that opens through the phallus ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)). Accessory glands of the male secrete the seminal fluid and, in many insects, the material of the **spermatophore**, a package in which sperm are transferred to the female. Seminal fluid proteins also affect the female: in *Drosophila* the sex peptide, transferred at mating, reduces the female's receptivity to other males and increases her egg laying. In the locust each testis is formed by many sperm tubes, the ejaculatory duct receives the secretions of about fifteen accessory glands on each side, and spermatophores are formed in a spermatophore sac and transferred into the female's spermatheca. Reproduction and the events of fertilization are treated in [7.17.28](/7.17.28).

## Questions

1. Why do insects shed the lining of the foregut, hindgut and tracheae at each molt, but not that of the midgut and tracheoles? What problem does this pose for a molting insect?

   **Answer** The foregut, hindgut and tracheae are ectodermal invaginations whose epidermis secretes cuticle continuous with the exoskeleton; at molting a new cuticle is laid down and the old one, including these linings, is shed (the tracheal linings are pulled out through the spiracles). The midgut is endodermal and has no cuticle; tracheoles are intracellular and are not molted. During the molt an insect cannot feed and its tracheal system is temporarily disrupted, so molting is a period of vulnerability and limited respiration.

2. (Calculation) Using Eq. (26.1) with the parameters of Example 26.4.1, by what factor would the maximum diffusion length increase if atmospheric oxygen rose from 21% to 35%? What does this imply for the oxygen hypothesis?

   **Answer** $L_{\max} \propto \sqrt{P_{\mathrm{O_2}} - P_{\min}}$; with $P_{\mathrm{O_2}} = 35.5$ and $21.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $P_{\min} = 2\,\mathrm{kPa}$, the factor is $\sqrt{33.5/19.3} \approx 1.32$. A two-thirds increase in oxygen extends the diffusion distance by only a third; it relaxes the constraint without removing it, so higher oxygen permitted larger insects but cannot alone explain the twofold to threefold larger linear dimensions of the Palaeozoic giants. The absence of aerial vertebrate predators and the dependence of aquatic larvae on dissolved oxygen are additional factors.

3. A diving beetle carries a bubble under its elytra. Explain why it obtains more oxygen than the bubble contained, why the bubble eventually disappears, and why it must surface more often when it dives deeper.

   **Answer** As the beetle consumes O₂, the O₂ partial pressure in the bubble falls below that of the water, and O₂ diffuses in; the bubble acts as a gill. N₂ partial pressure rises above that of the water (because O₂ is removed and the bubble is compressed by hydrostatic pressure), so N₂ diffuses out, and the bubble shrinks until it is too small to be useful. At greater depth the extra hydrostatic pressure raises the N₂ partial pressure further and accelerates N₂ loss, so the bubble collapses sooner and the gain in oxygen is smaller.

4. Compare the plastron and the compressible gas gill. In what kind of habitat is each favoured?

   **Answer** Both are air layers through which oxygen diffuses from the water to the spiracles, but the gas gill is a compressible bubble that loses nitrogen and must be renewed, whereas the plastron is held at constant volume by dense hydrofuge hairs and does not collapse, so it can last indefinitely. The plastron requires well-oxygenated water (fast streams, cool clear water), because in oxygen-poor water oxygen would diffuse out of it; the gas gill is used by insects that can surface regularly, in still or oxygen-poor water.

5. Which features of the insect circulatory system follow from the fact that hemolymph does not transport oxygen?

   **Answer** Because oxygen is delivered by the tracheae, the circulation does not have to be fast or high-pressure, and a single open tube with low pressure suffices; there are no capillaries, and many tissues are perfused only slowly. Hemolymph can therefore carry high concentrations of solutes (trehalose, amino acids), and its volume can vary greatly, serving as a water reserve and a hydraulic medium. Heart failure is not immediately fatal, and injury causes little loss of hemolymph. The exceptions, insects with hemoglobin, live in hypoxic water.

6. Why might meroistic (polytrophic and telotrophic) ovarioles have evolved independently several times?

   **Answer** In panoistic ovarioles each oocyte must transcribe from its own single (tetraploid) nucleus the large amounts of RNA and ribosomes needed for embryogenesis, which limits the speed of oogenesis. In meroistic ovarioles many polyploid nurse cells transcribe and transfer RNA, ribosomes, mitochondria and proteins into the oocyte, so that eggs mature faster; this is advantageous for insects with short life cycles and high fecundity. Since the advantage is general and the developmental change (incomplete cytokinesis of the germ-line cyst and differentiation of nurse cells) is simple, it could arise repeatedly, as the distribution among orders indicates.
