Animals of different phyla differ not only as adults but also in how they develop: in the way the egg divides, in the way the embryo forms its germ layers and body cavity, and in whether it passes through a free-living larva before it becomes an adult. These differences were the main evidence for animal relationships before molecular phylogenetics, and the division of the Bilateria into protostomes and deuterostomes still bears the names of an embryological character, the fate of the blastopore. This chapter surveys the developmental patterns that are used to characterize animal groups and the types of larvae and life cycles, and then asks how much of the traditional embryological classification survives on the current tree. The cellular and molecular mechanisms of cleavage, gastrulation and specification were treated in developmental biology ([7.5.5](/7.5.5), [7.5.6](/7.5.6), [7.5.9](/7.5.9)), and are only summarized here; the body cavities whose formation is described here were defined in [7.17.3](/7.17.3).

## 5.1. Eggs and Cleavage

### 5.1.1. Egg Types

The egg is polarized from the start. Its animal pole, where the polar bodies are given off, contains the nucleus and less yolk; its vegetal pole contains more yolk. The amount and distribution of yolk largely determine how the egg divides ([7.5.5](/7.5.5#def-5.3.1)): eggs are isolecithal (little, evenly distributed yolk: sea urchins, lancelets, mammals), **mesolecithal** (a moderate amount concentrated in the vegetal half: amphibians, lampreys), **telolecithal** (yolk filling most of the egg, with a small disc of cytoplasm at the animal pole: cephalopods, fishes, reptiles, birds) or **centrolecithal** (yolk in the center, surrounded by a thin layer of cytoplasm: most arthropods).

The amount of yolk is related to the mode of life. Small eggs with little yolk develop quickly into larvae that must feed; large yolky eggs develop longer, often into juveniles that need not feed, and are expensive to produce, so fewer of them are made (Section 5.5.3).

### 5.1.2. Patterns of Cleavage

During **cleavage** the zygote divides rapidly without growth into progressively smaller **blastomeres**, usually forming a hollow **blastula** ([7.5.5](/7.5.5)). Cleavage is holoblastic (complete) when the furrows pass through the whole egg, and meroblastic (incomplete) when they cut only the yolk-free cytoplasm. Holoblastic cleavage is equal when the blastomeres are of similar size and unequal when the vegetal ones are larger, as in amphibians. Meroblastic cleavage is **discoidal** in telolecithal eggs, where only a disc on top of the yolk divides, and superficial in centrolecithal eggs, where the nucleus divides many times within a syncytium before cell membranes form around the nuclei at the surface (see the figure below).

![Eggs and patterns of cleavage. Top row: egg types by the amount and distribution of yolk (beige): isolecithal, mesolecithal, telolecithal and centrolecithal. Bottom row: radial cleavage (8-cell stage, side view; the blastomeres of the upper tier lie directly above those of the lower tier), spiral cleavage (8-cell stage seen from the animal pole; the quartet of small micromeres, 1a–1d, is rotated by 45° and lies over the furrows between the macromeres), unequal holoblastic cleavage of an amphibian, discoidal meroblastic cleavage limited to the blastodisc on the yolk, and superficial cleavage of insects, in which the nuclei of a syncytium migrate to the cortex](./plots/cleavage-types.py)

Independently of yolk, the geometry of the division planes, which depends on the orientation of the mitotic spindles, defines several characteristic patterns:

**Definition 5.1.1 (Radial Cleavage)** In **radial cleavage** the first two divisions are meridional and at right angles, the third is equatorial, and each subsequent tier of blastomeres lies directly above the tier below it, so that the embryo is radially symmetrical about the animal–vegetal axis.

Radial cleavage is found in echinoderms, hemichordates and chordates, and also in cnidarians, sponges and some protostomes, among them the brachiopods, phoronids and chaetognaths. In **spiral cleavage**, the pattern of annelids, molluscs, nemerteans, polyclad flatworms and some other spiralians, the spindles of the third and later divisions are tilted alternately clockwise and counterclockwise, so that each quartet of daughter cells lies over the furrows between the cells below ([7.5.6](/7.5.6)). Spiral cleavage is so stereotyped that individual blastomeres can be named and their fates compared across phyla. By the convention of E. B. Wilson (1892), the four cells of the four-cell stage are A, B, C and D; they give off successive quartets of micromeres, 1a–1d, 2a–2d, 3a–3d and 4a–4d, while the macromeres are called 1A–1D, 2A–2D and so on. The cell 4d, the mesentoblast, gives rise to most of the mesoderm and to part of the gut in almost all spiral-cleaving animals, one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the clade Spiralia ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)).

Other patterns are bilateral cleavage, symmetrical about a single plane (ascidians), rotational cleavage, in which the two cells of the two-cell stage divide in perpendicular planes (mammals), and the biradial cleavage of ctenophores. Nematodes and arthropods have their own idiosyncratic patterns.

### 5.1.3. Determinate and Indeterminate Cleavage

**Definition 5.1.2 (Determinate Cleavage)** Cleavage is **determinate** (mosaic) when the developmental fate of each blastomere is fixed early, so that a blastomere isolated at the two- or four-cell stage forms only the part of the embryo it would normally form. It is indeterminate (regulative) when early blastomeres can still compensate: an isolated blastomere forms a complete, smaller embryo.

The classical experiments are those of Hans Driesch (1891), who separated the first two blastomeres of a sea urchin and obtained two complete larvae (indeterminate), and of the snail and annelid embryologists, who showed that the isolated D quadrant forms the mesoderm and posterior structures and the others do not (determinate, **mosaic development**, [7.5.6](/7.5.6)). The distinction is one of degree: all embryos combine cell-autonomous determinants with cell interactions, and the timing of commitment differs among species. Identical twins in humans and polyembryony in armadillos and parasitoid wasps are consequences of indeterminate cleavage.

## 5.2. Gastrulation and the Fate of the Blastopore

### 5.2.1. Modes of Gastrulation

**Gastrulation** rearranges the blastula into the germ layers ([7.5.9](/7.5.9)). The movements by which this is done are **invagination** (the vegetal wall folds inward, forming the archenteron and the **blastopore**), **ingression** (individual cells migrate into the blastocoel), **delamination** (cells divide parallel to the surface, splitting off an inner layer), **involution** (a sheet rolls inward over the lip of the blastopore) and epiboly (a sheet of animal cells spreads over the yolky vegetal cells). Most embryos combine several: invagination with epiboly in amphibians, ingression with delamination in many cnidarians. The mode depends heavily on the amount of yolk and is therefore of limited use in classification.

### 5.2.2. Protostomes and Deuterostomes

In the classical embryology of Grobben (1908), the blastopore of one group of bilaterians becomes the mouth and of the other the anus, and the two groups were named after this difference.

**Definition 5.2.1 (Protostomia, Deuterostomia)** The **Protostomia** (protostomes) and **Deuterostomia** (deuterostomes) are the two sister clades into which most bilaterians fall. The protostomes comprise the Ecdysozoa and Spiralia; their name refers to the development of the mouth from the blastopore or near it. The deuterostomes comprise the echinoderms, hemichordates and chordates; in them the blastopore becomes the anus (or closes near the future anus) and the mouth forms secondarily at the opposite end of the archenteron.

The definition is phylogenetic, because the embryological character does not follow the clades exactly. In many annelids and in onychophorans the blastopore elongates into a slit whose lateral lips fuse in the middle, leaving the mouth and anus at its two ends (amphistomy); in some molluscs the blastopore closes and the mouth forms near the site; and in priapulids and chaetognaths, both protostomes, the blastopore becomes the anus and the mouth forms secondarily, a deuterostome pattern (Martín-Durán and colleagues, 2012). The ancestral condition of the Bilateria may therefore have been amphistomy, from which both protostomy and deuterostomy derived.

The two groups were long characterized by a whole set of embryological and anatomical differences. These are listed in the table, which also shows how many exceptions are known.

| Character | Protostomes (traditional) | Deuterostomes (traditional) | Exceptions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blastopore | Becomes the mouth | Becomes the anus | Amphistomy in annelids, onychophorans; deuterostomy in priapulids, chaetognaths |
| Cleavage | Spiral | Radial | Spiral only in Spiralia; ecdysozoans not spiral; brachiopods and phoronids radial |
| Determination | Determinate | Indeterminate | Ascidians determinate; many spiralians regulate partly |
| Mesoderm | From the 4d mesentoblast (teloblasts) | From the wall of the archenteron | Ecdysozoans form mesoderm by other routes |
| Coelom formation | Schizocoely | Enterocoely | Vertebrates schizocoelous; brachiopods and chaetognaths enterocoelous |
| Larva | Trochophore | Dipleurula | Ecdysozoans have no ciliated larvae; chordates have no dipleurula |
| Nerve cord | Ventral | Dorsal (in chordates) | Echinoderms radial nerves; hemichordates dorsal and ventral cords |
| Skeleton | Cuticle or shell (ectodermal) | Mesodermal endoskeleton | Cephalopod cartilage is a mesodermal endoskeleton in a protostome |

Molecular phylogenies have confirmed that protostomes and deuterostomes are clades, but have redrawn their boundaries: the lophophorates (brachiopods, phoronids, bryozoans) and the chaetognaths, which were often placed with the deuterostomes because of their radial cleavage and enterocoely, are protostomes. The traditional characters therefore describe the ancestral conditions of the spiralians and of the deuterostomes rather than of protostomes and deuterostomes as a whole. The ecdysozoans, which have neither spiral cleavage nor trochophores, are protostomes by descent.

## 5.3. Mesoderm and Coelom Formation

The mesoderm forms between ectoderm and endoderm, and in coelomates a cavity, the coelom, opens within it. Two classical modes are distinguished (see the figure below).

**Definition 5.3.1 (Schizocoely, Enterocoely)** In **schizocoely** the mesoderm arises from cells that proliferate into solid bands between the ectoderm and the gut, and the coelom forms by splitting within each band. In **enterocoely** the mesoderm and coelom arise together as pouches of the wall of the archenteron that bulge out, pinch off and become coelomic sacs, whose walls are the mesoderm.

![The two classical modes of mesoderm and coelom formation (schematic transverse sections). Top, schizocoely, typical of spiralians: mesoderm arises from a pair of teloblasts derived from the 4d cell near the blastopore, proliferates into two solid mesodermal bands, and a cavity opens within each band by splitting. Bottom, enterocoely, typical of echinoderms, hemichordates and lancelets: pouches of the archenteron bulge out, pinch off and become coelomic sacs. Both modes yield a coelom lined by mesoderm on the body-wall and gut sides](./plots/coelom-formation.py)

Schizocoely in the strict sense, from teloblasts of the 4d lineage, is the mode of annelids and molluscs; Chinese textbooks call it the teloblast method. Enterocoely occurs in echinoderms, hemichordates, lancelets and also in the protostome brachiopods and chaetognaths. In enterocoelous deuterostomes the coelom is typically divided into three pairs of compartments, anterior to posterior, the protocoel, mesocoel and metacoel. Vertebrates form their mesoderm by ingression of cells through the blastopore or primitive streak, and their coelom by splitting of the lateral plate mesoderm, a schizocoely that is not homologous with that of spiralians. Because both modes occur in both major clades and intermediate forms exist, they are not reliable indicators of relationship, and the coelom itself may have evolved more than once ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.6)).

## 5.4. Larvae

### 5.4.1. Direct and Indirect Development

**Definition 5.4.1 (Indirect Development)** In **indirect development** the embryo hatches as a larva, a free-living developmental stage that differs from the adult in form, habitat or way of life and becomes the juvenile by **metamorphosis**. In direct development the embryo develops into a juvenile that resembles a small adult, without a larval stage.

Indirect development allows the two stages to exploit different resources (the larva often plankton, the adult the bottom) and the larva to disperse a sessile or slow adult. Larvae that feed are planktotrophic; larvae that live on yolk supplied with the egg are lecithotrophic. In mixed development the early embryos are brooded or enclosed in egg capsules and released as larvae.

### 5.4.2. The Main Larval Types

The marine larvae of different phyla have characteristic forms, many of which were described as separate genera of animals before their true nature was recognized. Five types are widespread enough to be used throughout this course (see the figure below).

**Definition 5.4.2 (Planula, Trochophore, Veliger, Nauplius, Dipleurula)** A **planula** is the ciliated, two-layered and usually mouthless larva of cnidarians. A **trochophore** is the top-shaped ciliated larva of annelids and many molluscs, with an apical organ, a preoral girdle of compound cilia (the prototroch), a complete gut and a pair of protonephridia. A **veliger** is the later larva of gastropods and bivalves, with a shell, a foot and a velum, a pair of ciliated lobes derived from the prototroch. A **nauplius** is the unsegmented larva of crustaceans, with three pairs of head appendages (first antennae, second antennae and mandibles) and a median eye. A **dipleurula** is the hypothetical generalized larva of echinoderms and hemichordates, bilaterally symmetrical, with a single looped ciliary band around the mouth field and a through gut.

### 5.4.3. Planktotrophic and Lecithotrophic Larvae

A planktotrophic species produces many small eggs, each cheap, but the larvae must feed for weeks in the plankton, where most of them die. A lecithotrophic species produces fewer, larger eggs, and its larvae spend less time in the plankton. The trade-off can be expressed by a simple model.

**Example 5.4.1 (Survival in the Plankton)** Suppose that larvae die in the plankton at a constant rate $m$ per day and must spend $T$ days there before they can settle. The fraction that survive is $\mathrm{e}^{-mT}$. With $m = 0.1\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$, a planktotrophic larva that needs $T = 30$ days survives with probability $\mathrm{e}^{-3} \approx 0.05$, a lecithotrophic larva of the same species that needs only $T = 5$ days with probability $\mathrm{e}^{-0.5} \approx 0.61$. If the lecithotrophic egg costs ten times as much energy, a female with a fixed budget produces ten times fewer eggs, and the numbers of settling juveniles per unit of energy are $0.05$ and $0.061$: nearly equal, and the balance shifts with the mortality rate. Higher mortality favors the larger egg.

:::

This reasoning, developed by R. R. Vance (1973) and extended by many later authors, explains why both strategies persist and why they change easily in evolution: a small change in larval mortality or food supply can tip the balance. The sea urchin *Heliocidaris erythrogramma* has a large egg and a nonfeeding larva that metamorphoses in four days, whereas its congener *H. tuberculata* has a small egg and a feeding pluteus; the switch, which involved the loss of the larval gut, arms and skeleton, took place within the last few million years (Raff and colleagues). Planktotrophy has been lost many times in echinoderms, molluscs and annelids, but apparently regained rarely if ever, because the complex feeding structures of a larva, once lost, are difficult to re-evolve (Strathmann 1978).

### 5.4.4. Primary and Secondary Larvae and the Origin of Larvae

The ciliated larvae of cnidarians, spiralians and ambulacrarians share several features: they swim with cilia, have an apical organ with a ciliary tuft formed from the most animal-pole blastomeres and expressing a common set of genes, and many have ciliary bands around the mouth. Such larvae are called primary larvae. Larvae that arose later within a group, from a juvenile or adult-like stage that took on a separate way of life, are secondary larvae: the nauplius, the caterpillar and maggot of insects ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)) and the tadpole of frogs are examples.

Two views of larval evolution compete:

- Larva first: the ancestor of eumetazoans was a small ciliated animal resembling a larva, and the larger benthic adult was added later to the end of development (terminal addition). Claus Nielsen's trochaea theory derives the bilaterian body from a ciliated, planktonic gastraea with a ring of ciliary bands around the blastopore, and treats the primary larvae of spiralians and deuterostomes as homologous survivors of this ancestor.
- Adult first (intercalation): the ancestors developed directly into small benthic adults, and larvae were inserted into development several times as an adaptation for dispersal and planktonic feeding. Their shared features are explained by the use of the same ciliary and apical-organ genes, recruited repeatedly.

The question is not settled. Molecular studies show both shared genes in apical organs (supporting deep homology of these structures) and many differences in the genes active in the bodies of the larvae; the distribution of feeding larvae on the tree also suggests many independent gains and losses ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

## 5.5. Life Cycles

### 5.5.1. Asexual and Sexual Generations

Many animals alternate between generations that reproduce in different ways. In cnidarians, the sessile polyp reproduces asexually by budding, and the free-swimming medusa sexually; both are diploid. This alternation of a polypoid and a medusoid generation, called **metagenesis**, is the animal "alternation of generations" of Chinese textbooks, but it differs fundamentally from the **alternation of generations** of plants and algae, in which a diploid sporophyte alternates with a haploid gametophyte ([7.17.9](/7.17.9)). Another kind of alternation is heterogony, the cyclical alternation of parthenogenetic and sexual generations, as in aphids, water fleas and monogonont rotifers, which multiply rapidly by **parthenogenesis** when conditions are good and produce sexual males and females, and resistant eggs, when they deteriorate.

Parasites often have complex life cycles with one or more intermediate hosts in which larvae develop or multiply asexually and a definitive host in which the adults reproduce sexually: the digenean flukes pass through a snail, in which their larvae multiply asexually, before reaching a vertebrate ([7.17.11](/7.17.11)). Each transfer between hosts is a habitat transition with high mortality, which the asexual multiplication and the enormous egg output of the adults offset.

### 5.5.2. Metamorphosis

Metamorphosis, the transformation of a larva into the juvenile, can be gradual, as in many crustaceans and the hemimetabolous insects, or catastrophic, as in the many marine invertebrates whose larval tissues are destroyed within hours and whose juvenile develops from groups of cells set aside in the larva (the imaginal discs of insects are a comparable case, [7.5.33](/7.5.33)). Metamorphosis of marine larvae is usually triggered by environmental cues on settlement: chemical signals from the substrate, from conspecific adults or from bacterial films, and in reef fishes and corals even sounds. Its endocrine control is best known in insects (ecdysone and juvenile hormone) and amphibians (thyroid hormone) ([7.14.38](/7.14.38), [7.17.29](/7.17.29)).

## 5.6. The Biogenetic Law and Its Revision

The observation that embryos of different animals resemble each other more than the adults do inspired one of the most influential ideas in nineteenth-century zoology.

**Definition 5.6.1 (Biogenetic Law)** The **biogenetic law** (recapitulation theory), formulated by Ernst Haeckel in 1866, states that ontogeny is a brief and rapid recapitulation of phylogeny: in its development an animal passes through stages that resemble the adult forms of its ancestors, so that the embryo of a mammal passes through a fish-like stage with gill slits.

Haeckel's law was more influential than accurate. Before him Karl Ernst von Baer had stated that embryos of related animals resemble each other in their general characters, that the special characters appear later, and that the embryo of a higher animal resembles the embryo, not the adult, of a lower one (**von Baer's laws**, [7.5.39](/7.5.39#def-39.1.1)). The pharyngeal pouches and arches of the human embryo correspond to the pharyngeal region of a fish embryo, not to the gills of an adult fish; they are later transformed into the middle ear cavity, tonsils, thymus and other structures. Walter Garstang (1922) put the reversal bluntly: ontogeny does not recapitulate phylogeny, it creates it.

The modern view keeps what is true in both laws and adds mechanisms:

- Evolution changes development, and new characters can be added at any stage, not only at the end. Larval adaptations (caenogenesis) have no ancestral adult counterpart.
- Changes in the timing of development, **heterochrony**, alter adult form. The axolotl is a salamander that becomes sexually mature in its larval form (**paedomorphosis** by **neoteny**), and paedomorphosis has been proposed for the origin of several groups, among them the appendicularian tunicates and, in Garstang's hypothesis, the vertebrates from the tadpole larva of an ascidian-like ancestor ([7.17.43](/7.17.43)).
- Embryos are most alike at a middle period of development, the phylotypic stage, and more diverse before and after it (the developmental hourglass, [7.5.39](/7.5.39#sec-39.1.2)), because the earliest stages are adapted to the egg and the conditions of development and the later stages to the adult way of life.

Chinese textbooks present the biogenetic law as an objective law of development. It is better understood as a description of a tendency: embryos often retain features of ancestral embryos, because changes in early, highly interconnected stages are more likely to be harmful, which makes comparative embryology valuable evidence of relationship, but there is no law that development must repeat the history of adult forms.

- The **biogenetic law** (Definition 5.6.1) has been replaced by von Baer's laws, heterochrony and the developmental hourglass: development evolves, and embryos resemble ancestral embryos rather than ancestral adults.

## Questions

1. A spiralian embryologist kills the 4d cell of a snail embryo with a laser. What do you expect, and what does it show about the type of cleavage?

   **Answer** The embryo lacks most of its mesoderm, including the heart, body-wall muscles and kidney, and part of the hindgut, because these derive from 4d; neighboring cells do not replace them. This is the result expected of determinate (mosaic) development: the fate of the cell is fixed by the stage at which it forms, and the embryo cannot regulate. In some spiralians partial regulation occurs, which shows that the distinction is quantitative.

2. Priapulids are protostomes, yet their blastopore becomes the anus. Does this observation refute the clade Protostomia?

   **Answer** No. Protostomia is recognized from molecular phylogenies and other characters; the name only records a character that was believed to be universal in the group. The priapulid observation shows that deuterostomy occurs in a protostome, so the mode of mouth formation is homoplastic: either deuterostomy is ancestral for bilaterians and protostomy evolved within protostomes, or amphistomy was ancestral and both conditions derived from it. It refutes the use of the character as a diagnostic, not the clade.

3. Using the model of Example 5.4.1, find the larval mortality rate $m$ at which a planktotrophic strategy ($T = 30$ d, egg cost 1) and a lecithotrophic strategy ($T = 5$ d, egg cost 10) give equal numbers of juveniles per unit of energy.

   **Answer** Equality requires $\mathrm{e}^{-30m} = \mathrm{e}^{-5m}/10$, that is $\mathrm{e}^{-25m} = 0.1$, so $m = \ln 10/25 \approx 0.092\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$. Above this mortality lecithotrophy is favored, below it planktotrophy.

4. Why does the presence of pharyngeal pouches in human embryos not support Haeckel's version of the biogenetic law?

   **Answer** Haeckel's law requires the embryo to resemble the adult ancestor. The pouches of the human embryo resemble those of a fish embryo, not the gills of an adult fish: they never bear gill filaments or open to function in respiration, and they give rise to structures of the human head and neck. This is what von Baer's laws predict, a resemblance among embryos due to shared developmental programs, not a repetition of ancestral adult stages.
