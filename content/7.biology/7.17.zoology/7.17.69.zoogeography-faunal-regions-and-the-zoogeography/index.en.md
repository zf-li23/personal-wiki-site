Every group treated in this course has a geography. Monotremes live only in Australia and New Guinea, salamanders are almost confined to the Northern Hemisphere, the ice crawlers survive in a few cold forests of north-eastern Asia and western North America, and the giant panda in six mountain ranges of western China. Zoogeography asks why animals live where they do. It is the part of **biogeography** that deals with animals, and its general theory — endemism, realms, dispersal and vicariance, phylogeography, island biogeography — was developed in [7.10.30](/7.10.30). This chapter does not repeat that theory. It applies it to the animals of this course and to one country: it reviews the zoogeographic realms of the world and the taxa that characterize them (§69.2), then treats the zoogeographic regionalization of China (§69.3), the history that produced it (§69.4) and its endemic and threatened animals (§69.5).

China is an unusually good place for this exercise. It is the only large country that spans two realms on one continent, the Palearctic and the Oriental, without an ocean between them; it contains the highest and youngest plateau on Earth; and during the Pleistocene it was never covered by continental ice sheets, so that ancient lineages that vanished elsewhere — the giant panda, the takin, the Chinese alligator, the Chinese giant salamander — survived in its mountains. The examples are drawn from the vertebrate chapters (amphibians [7.17.49](/7.17.49), lepidosaurs and turtles [7.17.51](/7.17.51), birds [7.17.53](/7.17.53), mammals [7.17.55](/7.17.55)) and from the insect chapters ([7.17.30](/7.17.30), [7.17.31](/7.17.31), [7.17.36](/7.17.36)). The principles of conservation are left to the ecology course; §69.5 only describes the Chinese situation and the categories of protection.

## 69.1. Concepts of Zoogeography

### 69.1.1. Habitat, Range and Fauna

**Definition 69.1.1 (Zoogeography)** **Zoogeography** is the study of the distribution of animals over the Earth and of the causes of that distribution. *Ecological zoogeography* explains distributions at short time scales and small spatial scales by present conditions — climate, habitat, food, competitors and the ability to disperse; *historical zoogeography* explains the distribution of species and higher taxa at the scale of continents and geological time by the origin of lineages, their dispersal and extinction, and the movement of land and sea.

Three spatial concepts are used throughout. The *habitat* of a species is the kind of place it lives in, with all the conditions it needs to survive and reproduce: a conifer forest, a desert, a mountain stream. Its range (distribution area) is the geographic area it occupies, drawn on a map by joining the outermost records. Habitat is an ecological concept, range a geographic one: a species occupies only the patches of suitable habitat within its range. The limits of a range usually coincide with a limit of tolerance. The slow lorises, gibbons, hornbills, sunbirds and Asian elephant of southern China, for example, occur only where frosts are absent or rare, so that the frost line is effectively the northern limit of their ranges.

Species differ in the breadth of their requirements. *Eurytopic* species tolerate a wide range of habitats and have large ranges: the brown rat (*Rattus norvegicus*), the Siberian weasel (*Mustela sibirica*), the Eurasian tree sparrow (*Passer montanus*), the Asiatic toad (*Bufo gargarizans*) and the common carp. *Stenotopic* species have narrow requirements and usually small ranges: the giant panda (*Ailuropoda melanoleuca*), which depends on bamboo forest at middle elevations, and the Chinese alligator (*Alligator sinensis*), which lives only in the wetlands of the lower Yangtze. Many ranges are discontinuous: the paddlefishes of the Mississippi and the Yangtze, the tiger in eastern and southern Asia, Cabot's tragopan (*Tragopan caboti*) in three separate mountain areas of south-eastern China, and the warty newt *Tylototriton asperrimus* in six. These are cases of **disjunct distribution**, explained by **vicariance** or by the extinction of intermediate populations.

**Definition 69.1.2 (Fauna)** The **fauna** of an area is the whole set of animal species living in it, regarded as a product of history: the species that originated there or arrived there over geological time and persist under the present conditions. A *faunal region* is an area whose fauna is relatively uniform and differs from that of other areas.

A fauna is more than a species list. It has a history (some components are old residents, others recent immigrants), a composition by origin (northern or southern elements, endemic or widespread ones) and a structure by habitat. Chinese zoogeography, following Zhang Rongzu, distinguishes the fauna of a region, defined historically by its taxonomic composition, from the *ecogeographic faunal groups* of the landscape zones within it — the conifer-forest fauna, the steppe fauna, the desert fauna, the alpine-meadow fauna — defined ecologically by the habitats and adaptations of the animals that dominate them. The two classifications largely coincide in China (Table in §69.3.1), because the boundaries of regions follow the major climatic and topographic boundaries.

### 69.1.2. Barriers and Dispersal Routes

Whether a species spreads beyond its present range depends on its powers of **dispersal** and on the barriers around it. Physical barriers are seas, mountain ranges, deserts and large rivers; biological barriers are the absence of food plants or of an intermediate host, and established competitors or predators. A barrier is always relative to the organism: a strait that stops a frog does not stop a bird, and a desert that stops a forest squirrel is a highway for a jerboa. **Definition 69.1.3 (Corridor, Filter Route and Sweepstakes Route)** A **corridor** is a route of continuous suitable habitat along which many species can move freely in both directions, so that the faunas at its two ends become similar (the forest and steppe belts across Eurasia). A **filter route** allows only species with particular adaptations to pass (a desert crossed only by drought-tolerant animals, a land bridge with a cold climate crossed only by cold-tolerant ones). A **sweepstakes route** is crossed only by chance, by rare individuals carried by storms or on floating vegetation, typically from a continent to a remote island.

The kind of route determines the kind of fauna. Faunas joined by corridors are *balanced*: they contain the same broad mixture of groups. Faunas formed by sweepstakes dispersal are *disharmonic*: they lack whole groups that could not make the crossing and are rich in those that could. Oceanic islands illustrate this: the Hawaiian Islands had no native land mammals except a bat, no amphibians and no freshwater fishes of their own, but many birds, insects and snails, all descended from rare overseas colonists ([7.10.30](/7.10.30#sec-30.4.3)). The Bering land bridge was a filter: during glacial periods it carried cold-tolerant mammals such as mammoths, bison, wolves and humans between Asia and North America, but not tropical forms. Islands also follow the species–area relation $S = cA^z$, where $S$ is the number of species on an island of area $A$ and $z$ usually lies between 0.20 and 0.35 for isolated islands; the equilibrium between immigration and extinction that underlies it is the **theory of island biogeography** ([7.10.30](/7.10.30#sec-30.7.2)).

The older idea that each taxon spread outward from a **center of origin** — the region with its oldest fossils or its greatest diversity — is no longer used as a principle. Old taxa may be extinct where they arose (camels arose in North America and now live in Asia, Africa and South America), and the place of greatest diversity is often where a lineage radiated, not where it originated. Modern historical zoogeography instead reconstructs the history of a group from a dated phylogeny and compares the histories of many groups: shared patterns point to common vicariant events, unique patterns to dispersal ([7.10.30](/7.10.30#sec-30.4)). Within species, the same questions are asked of gene genealogies by **phylogeography** ([7.10.30](/7.10.30#sec-30.5)), which has become the main tool for the history of the Chinese fauna (§69.4.3).

### 69.1.3. Measuring the Similarity of Faunas

Faunal regions are recognized by comparing species lists. For two areas, let $a$ be the number of taxa they share and $b$ and $c$ the numbers found only in the first or only in the second. Two common indices of similarity are

$$
J = \frac{a}{a + b + c}, \qquad \beta_{\mathrm{sim}} = \frac{\min(b, c)}{a + \min(b, c)}.
$$

The Jaccard index $J$ is the proportion of all taxa that are shared; $1 - J$ is a distance. The Simpson dissimilarity $\beta_{\mathrm{sim}}$ measures turnover independently of the difference in species richness: a small fauna that is a subset of a large one has $\beta_{\mathrm{sim}} = 0$, whereas its Jaccard distance can be large. A matrix of distances between all pairs of areas is then clustered, for example by UPGMA (average linkage: repeatedly join the two clusters with the smallest mean pairwise distance), and the clusters at a chosen level are the regions. Holt and colleagues (2013) went one step further and replaced shared species by shared branch length on the phylogenies of all amphibians, birds and mammals, so that two areas with different species of the same genus count as similar.

## 69.2. The Zoogeographic Realms of the World

### 69.2.1. Wallace's Six Realms and Their Revision

Philip Sclater (1858) divided the land into six regions from the distribution of birds, and Alfred Russel Wallace (1876) confirmed them with mammals and other groups. The six realms, defined in [7.10.30](/7.10.30#sec-30.2.1), are the Palearctic (Europe, Africa north of the Sahara, the Middle East and Asia north of the Himalaya), the Nearctic (North America to the highlands of Mexico), the Neotropical (Central and South America and the West Indies), the Afrotropical or Ethiopian (Africa south of the Sahara, southern Arabia and Madagascar), the Oriental (South and South-East Asia and southern China) and the Australian (Australia, New Guinea, New Zealand and the islands of the south-western Pacific). The Palearctic and Nearctic share so many groups that they are often combined as the *Holarctic*.

Between the Oriental and Australian realms lies the archipelago that gave biogeography its most famous boundary. **Wallace's line** runs between Bali and Lombok and between Borneo and Sulawesi; the faunas of Bali and Lombok, 35 km apart, are strikingly different. Two other lines were drawn later. *Lydekker's line* follows the edge of the Sahul shelf west of New Guinea and the Aru Islands: east of it the fauna is fully Australian. *Weber's line*, between Sulawesi and the Moluccas and between Timor and the islands to the east, was drawn by Max Weber as the line of faunal balance, where Oriental and Australian elements are equally represented. The islands between Wallace's and Lydekker's lines, which never joined either continental shelf, form a transition zone, *Wallacea*, with many endemics of its own (the babirusa, the anoas and the tarsiers of Sulawesi). The lines are sharp for mammals and freshwater fishes, which cannot cross deep water, and blurred for birds, bats and insects.

Holt and colleagues (2013), using the phylogenetic dissimilarity of 21,037 species of amphibians, birds and non-marine mammals, recognized 11 realms and 20 regions. Wallace's realms remain recognizable, but several transition zones are raised to realms: a *Saharo-Arabian* realm between the Palearctic and Afrotropical, a *Madagascan* realm, an *Oceanian* realm for the Pacific islands, a *Panamanian* realm for Central America and, most relevant here, a *Sino-Japanese* realm covering much of central and eastern China, Korea and Japan, intermediate between the Palearctic and the Oriental. This last result reflects in a quantitative analysis what Chinese zoogeographers had long described as a broad transition zone in eastern China (§69.3.2).

Three patterns run through the table. First, each southern realm is defined by endemic groups of high rank: monotremes and the marsupial radiation in Australia, xenarthrans in South America, afrotherians in Africa. These are exactly the three ancient groups that molecular phylogenies place at the base of the placental tree or outside it (**Eutheria**, [7.17.55](/7.17.55)): the deep splits of mammals follow the breakup of **Gondwana**. Second, the northern realms are poor in endemics of high rank but share many groups with one another, because they were repeatedly joined across Beringia and the North Atlantic. Third, the Oriental and Afrotropical realms share many groups (pangolins, elephants, rhinoceroses, lorises, hornbills, sunbirds, caecilians), a legacy of the forests that covered much of Africa, Arabia and southern Asia in the Miocene, before the Sahara and the Arabian deserts formed.

The flightless paleognaths — ostrich in Africa, rheas in South America, emu and cassowaries in Australia and New Guinea, kiwis and the extinct moas in New Zealand, the extinct elephant birds in Madagascar — were the classic example of vicariance by continental drift. Ancient DNA has overturned it: the closest relatives of the kiwis are the elephant birds of Madagascar, and the moas are closest to the flighted tinamous of South America, so flight was lost several times after the ancestors had dispersed by air ([7.17.53](/7.17.53)). The **lungfish** genera of three southern continents ([7.17.48](/7.17.48)) and the moss bugs and southern scorpionflies of South America, Australia and New Zealand ([7.17.30](/7.17.30#sec-30.4.5)) are better candidates for Gondwanan vicariance.

### 69.2.3. The Historical Explanation

The realms are products of Earth history ([7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.7.1)). After **Pangaea** split into **Laurasia** and Gondwana in the Jurassic, Gondwana broke up during the Cretaceous: Africa and South America separated from about 100 million years ago, India and Madagascar from Africa and then from each other, and Australia stayed connected with South America through a temperate Antarctica until about 35 million years ago. Marsupials reached Australia by this southern route, where placentals other than bats and later rodents never followed; in Laurasia, where marsupials were also present in the Cretaceous and early Paleogene, they disappeared.

South America then remained an island continent for most of the Cenozoic, with its own xenarthrans, marsupials, native hoofed mammals and terror birds, joined by monkeys and caviomorph rodents that rafted across the Atlantic from Africa in the Eocene. When the Isthmus of Panama closed about 3 million years ago, the *Great American Biotic Interchange* began: armadillos, opossums, porcupines and ground sloths moved north, and deer, camels, cats, dogs, bears, horses and mastodons moved south. Northern immigrants and their descendants now make up about half of the mammal genera of South America, whereas few southern groups established themselves in the north. Africa joined Eurasia through Arabia about 20 million years ago, and India collided with Asia about 50 million years ago, raising the Himalaya and the Tibetan Plateau, the barrier that now separates the Palearctic and Oriental realms in Asia (§69.4.2).

## 69.3. The Zoogeographic Regionalization of China

### 69.3.1. Two Realms, Three Subrealms, Seven Regions

**Definition 69.3.1 (Zoogeographic Regionalization of China)** The **zoogeographic regionalization of China** (Zhang Rongzu 1979, revised 1999 and 2011) divides the country into two realms, the *Palearctic* in the north and west and the *Oriental* in the south, three subrealms, seven regions and 19 subregions. The Palearctic realm comprises the *Northeast Asian subrealm*, with the Northeast and North China regions, and the *Central Asian subrealm*, with the Mongolia–Xinjiang and Qinghai–Tibet regions; the Oriental realm comprises the *Sino-Indian subrealm*, with the Southwest, Central China and South China regions.

The scheme was built mainly from the distributions of land mammals and birds, checked against amphibians, reptiles and insects, and combined with the physical geography. The table gives the hierarchy and the ecogeographic faunal groups of each region.

### 69.3.2. The Boundary between the Realms

In the east the boundary follows the Qinling Mountains and the Huai River, the line that separates northern and southern China in climate, soils and agriculture. The Qinling, an east–west range rising to 3,771 m, blocks the cold winter monsoon from the north; along it and the Huai run the January mean isotherm of 0 °C and the annual isohyet of about 800 mm, the northern limit of subtropical evergreen forest and of rice as the main crop. South of the line, frost is rare and many Oriental groups — macaques, civets, porcupines, bamboo rats, bulbuls, laughingthrushes, tree frogs of the family Rhacophoridae — become common. East of the Qinling, however, the North China Plain and the lower Yangtze form a continuous lowland without a barrier. There the two faunas interpenetrate over hundreds of kilometers: Palearctic species such as the Siberian weasel, the wolf and the azure-winged magpie (*Cyanopica cyanus*) extend far south, and Oriental species such as the masked palm civet (*Paguma larvata*) and the Chinese bamboo partridge (*Bambusicola thoracicus*) extend north to the Huai and beyond. The boundary drawn for mammals and birds is an average of many individual range limits; for insects it lies at about the same place but is equally blurred.

In the west the boundary is set by the Himalaya and the eastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau. Here it is vertical as much as horizontal: on the south slope of the Himalaya and in the Hengduan Mountains, Oriental faunas occupy the valleys and lower slopes, and Palearctic faunas the upper slopes and plateau. In the Hengduan region the line runs approximately along 30° N, from Zoigê through Heishui, Barkam, Kangding and Litang to Batang, but along the deep north–south valleys Oriental species penetrate far to the north and Palearctic ones far to the south. In the analysis of Holt and colleagues, much of the eastern transition zone together with the Qinling and the Sichuan Basin forms part of the Sino-Japanese realm (§69.2.1): the two schemes describe the same transition, one as a broad boundary zone and the other as a realm in its own right.

### 69.3.3. The Northeast Region

The Northeast region comprises the Greater and Lesser Khingan, the Changbai Mountains, the Songliao Plain and, as an outlier, the Altai of northern Xinjiang. Winters are long and very cold (Mohe, at the northern tip, is the coldest place in China), summers short and wet. The forests are of larch, spruce, fir and Korean pine, mixed in the south with birch, aspen, Mongolian oak and lime. The boreal-forest fauna is poor in species but rich in cold-adapted ones: moose, reindeer (herded by the Evenki in the Greater Khingan), sable, wolverine, Siberian musk deer, the Amur tiger, mountain hare, capercaillie and hazel grouse, black and three-toed woodpeckers, crossbills and the spotted nutcracker, with only a few amphibians and reptiles (the Siberian salamander, the long-tailed clawed salamander, the viviparous lizard, the common adder). Animals concentrate along rivers and forest edges; many depend on conifer seeds, berries and fungi, whose crops fluctuate from year to year and drive cycles in the numbers of squirrels, voles and their predators. Winter adaptations are conspicuous: white winter coats in the mountain hare, stoat, least weasel and willow ptarmigan, long legs and spreading hooves in moose and reindeer, comb-like fringes on the toes of the hazel grouse. The Songnen wetlands are a breeding ground of the red-crowned crane (*Grus japonensis*). Among insects, the Chinese ice crawler *Galloisiana sinensis* of the Changbai Mountains (**Grylloblattodea**, [7.17.31](/7.17.31#sec-31.4.6)) and the relict dragonfly *Epiophlebia sinensis* of Heilongjiang are Palearctic relicts.

### 69.3.4. The North China Region

The North China region lies between the Northeast and Mongolia–Xinjiang regions in the north and the Qinling–Huai line in the south, from the Lanzhou Basin to the Yellow Sea, and includes the Loess Plateau and the Huang–Huai Plain. The climate is warm-temperate with cold, dry winters and hot, wet summers. Cultivation for several thousand years has removed most of the forest, which survives in the Taihang, Yanshan, Qinling and Ziwu mountains. The fauna is relatively poor and has few endemics; forest species such as red deer and black bear have retreated to the mountains, while small mammals of fields and loess have flourished: the greater long-tailed hamster (*Tscherskia triton*), striped hamster (*Cricetulus barabensis*), mandarin vole (*Lasiopodomys mandarinus*) and zokors (*Myospalax*), many of them serious agricultural pests. Typical birds are the chukar, the black drongo, the azure-winged magpie and the endemic Père David's laughingthrush (*Pterorhinus davidi*); typical herptiles are Swinhoe's gecko (*Gekko swinhonis*), the mountain racerunner (*Eremias brenchleyi*), the Chinese brown frog (*Rana chensinensis*) and the boreal digging frog (*Kaloula borealis*). The brown eared pheasant (*Crossoptilon mantchuricum*) of the Lüliang and Taihang mountains and the complex-toothed flying squirrel (*Trogopterus xanthipes*) are endemic. The region's representative insect is the oriental migratory locust (*Locusta migratoria manilensis*), whose outbreaks on the flood plains of the Yellow and Huai rivers are recorded for over two thousand years ([7.17.31](/7.17.31#sec-31.4.4)). Père David's deer (*Elaphurus davidianus*), a swamp deer of these plains, disappeared in the wild long before the nineteenth century and survived only in the imperial hunting park south of Beijing (§69.5.2).

### 69.3.5. The Mongolia–Xinjiang Region

The Mongolia–Xinjiang region extends from the western foot of the Greater Khingan along the Yinshan, the northern Loess Plateau, the Qilian and the Kunlun to the western border, and includes the Inner Mongolian and Ordos plateaus, the Alxa desert, the Hexi Corridor and the Qaidam, Tarim and Junggar basins with the Tianshan. The climate is continental: little rain, hot summers, long winters with temperatures below −30 °C, and large daily ranges. A line from Jining to Erenhot and the south-western Ordos separates an eastern steppe from a western desert. The steppe fauna is dominated by burrowing rodents and fast runners: the Mongolian gazelle (*Procapra gutturosa*), Daurian ground squirrel, Mongolian marmot, Brandt's vole (*Lasiopodomys brandtii*), Mongolian gerbil (*Meriones unguiculatus*), the five-toed jerboa (*Orientallactaga sibirica*), Daurian pika, and among birds the Mongolian lark, great bustard (*Otis tarda*), demoiselle crane and Pallas's sandgrouse (*Syrrhaptes paradoxus*). Rodent numbers rise and fall with the grass crop and are controlled by weasels, polecats, foxes and raptors. Ground-living passerines such as wheatears and snowfinches nest in rodent burrows, the "birds and rodents in one burrow" of Chinese texts. Grasshoppers, above all *Oedaleus asiaticus* (**Orthoptera**), are the main insect herbivores and pests of the steppe. The desert fauna is poorer but more specialized: jerboas (*Dipus sagitta*, *Euchoreutes naso*), gerbils (*Rhombomys opimus*, *Meriones*), the goitered gazelle, the wild Bactrian camel (*Camelus ferus*), the Mongolian wild ass, Pallas's cat, the marbled polecat, the Xinjiang ground jay (*Podoces biddulphi*, endemic to the Tarim), toad-headed agamas (*Phrynocephalus*), racerunners and wonder geckos (*Teratoscincus*). Desert animals avoid heat by being nocturnal and burrowing, obtain water from food and metabolism and lose little through concentrated urine and dry feces ([7.14](/7.14)). Amphibians are almost absent; the Semirechensk salamander (*Ranodon sibiricus*) of the Tianshan is a relict. Przewalski's horse, extinct in the wild by the 1960s, has been reintroduced in the Junggar Basin (Kalamaili) and Gansu.

### 69.3.6. The Qinghai–Tibet Region

The Qinghai–Tibet region comprises the Tibetan Plateau proper, enclosed by the Hengduan Mountains in the east, the Himalaya in the south and the Kunlun, Altun and Qilian in the north, with an average elevation above 4,000 m. The climate has long winters and no summer, and the vegetation is alpine meadow, alpine steppe and cold desert. The fauna is poor in species but rich in endemics, most of them derived from Central Asian, that is Palearctic, stocks: the Tibetan antelope or chiru (*Pantholops hodgsonii*), wild yak (*Bos mutus*), kiang (*Equus kiang*), Tibetan gazelle, white-lipped deer (*Przewalskium albirostris*), Himalayan marmot, plateau pika (*Ochotona curzoniae*) and many other pikas — the plateau is the center of pika diversity — snow leopard and Tibetan fox; among birds the black-necked crane (*Grus nigricollis*, the only crane that breeds at high altitude), Tibetan snowcock, Tibetan partridge, the ground tit (*Pseudopodoces humilis*) and several snowfinches, which nest in the burrows of pikas. Reptiles and amphibians are few: the toad-headed agamas *Phrynocephalus vlangalii* and *P. theobaldi*, the hot-spring snake *Thermophis baileyi*, which lives around geothermal springs, the Xizang plateau frog (*Nanorana parkeri*) and the Tibetan toad. The best-known insects are the ghost moths (*Thitarodes*, Hepialidae; **Lepidoptera**), whose soil-dwelling larvae are infected by the fungus *Ophiocordyceps sinensis* to produce the valuable "caterpillar fungus". The animals' adaptations to hypoxia — high-affinity hemoglobins, large hearts and lungs, changes in the HIF pathway — are treated in [7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.6.2).

### 69.3.7. The Southwest Region

The Southwest region comprises western Sichuan, the eastern Qamdo area of Tibet, the northern part of the Hengduan Mountains in Yunnan and the south slope of the Himalaya below the conifer belt. It is a land of high mountains and deep gorges, with elevations mostly between 1,600 and 4,000 m in the inhabited valleys and slopes, and its fauna is arranged in altitudinal belts: Palearctic species such as pikas, the Himalayan marmot, the Chinese grouse (*Tetrastes sewerzowi*) and toad-headed agamas on the high slopes; Oriental species such as macaques, civets, bamboo rats, sunbirds and flowerpeckers in the valleys. The region is the stronghold of China's most celebrated mammals: the giant panda, the red panda (*Ailurus fulgens*), the golden snub-nosed monkey (*Rhinopithecus roxellana*) and the black snub-nosed monkey of Yunnan (*R. bieti*), the takin (*Budorcas taxicolor*), and on the Himalayan slope the Himalayan tahr and gray langurs. It is also the world center of pheasants — Chinese monal (*Lophophorus lhuysii*), Temminck's and satyr tragopans, blood pheasant (*Ithaginis cruentus*), golden pheasant (*Chrysolophus pictus*), white eared pheasant — and of laughingthrushes, and has the highest diversity of shrews and moles in China. Stream salamanders (*Batrachuperus*), lazy toads (*Oreolalax*, *Scutiger*) and cascade frogs (*Amolops*) characterize its streams, and the swallowtail *Bhutanitis thaidina* its forests. Why this region is so rich is discussed in §69.4.4.

### 69.3.8. The Central China Region

The Central China region covers the Yangtze basin east of the Sichuan Basin. Its western half, from the Qinling south to the upper Xi River, is mountainous and plateau country mostly above 1,000 m; its eastern half is the plain and lakes of the middle and lower Yangtze with the hills of the south-east (the Dabie, Huangshan and Wuyi mountains). The climate is subtropical and humid, and much of the land is farmed. The fauna is Oriental in composition, with an admixture of widespread Palearctic species, and has few sharp boundaries with the neighboring regions. Characteristic mammals are the Chinese water deer (*Hydropotes inermis*), the tufted deer, Reeves's muntjac, the endemic black muntjac (*Muntiacus crinifrons*), the Chinese pangolin (*Manis pentadactyla*), porcupines, the large Indian civet and the masked palm civet; characteristic birds are Elliot's pheasant (*Syrmaticus ellioti*), Cabot's tragopan, the Chinese bamboo partridge and the crested ibis (*Nipponia nippon*), whose last wild population survived on the south slope of the Qinling. The Yangtze itself had an endemic fauna of large animals: the baiji or Yangtze river dolphin (*Lipotes vexillifer*), the Yangtze finless porpoise, the Chinese sturgeon and the Chinese paddlefish (*Psephurus gladius*). Typical herptiles are the Chinese alligator, the big-headed turtle (*Platysternon megacephalum*), the Chinese giant salamander (*Andrias davidianus*), the Chinese fire-bellied newt (*Cynops orientalis*), the Chinese tree frog (*Hyla chinensis*) and the sharp-snouted pit viper (*Deinagkistrodon acutus*). The swallowtail *Luehdorfia chinensis*, which flies in early spring and feeds on wild ginger, is endemic, and the domestic silkworm was domesticated from the wild *Bombyx mandarina* of this region ([7.17.36](/7.17.36)).

### 69.3.9. The South China Region

The South China region includes southern Yunnan, Guangxi and Guangdong, the coast of Fujian, Taiwan, Hainan and the islands of the South China Sea. The climate is tropical and subtropical, with annual rainfall above 1,500 mm; the natural vegetation is tropical rain forest and monsoon forest, now largely replaced by secondary forest, scrub and farmland. This is the richest region of China in species, above all in the mountains of southern Yunnan (Xishuangbanna), where the Asian elephant, gibbons (*Nomascus*, *Hoolock*), the Bengal slow loris, the binturong, the clouded leopard, the green peafowl (*Pavo muticus*), the red junglefowl (*Gallus gallus*, the wild ancestor of the chicken), hornbills, broadbills, parrots and trogons, the Burmese python, flying lizards and the caecilian *Ichthyophis bannanicus* (the only caecilian in China) reach China at the northern edge of their ranges. Arboreal fruit-eaters dominate the mammal fauna: primates, squirrels and flying squirrels, fruit bats and civets. Insects include the Atlas moth (*Attacus atlas*), leaf insects (*Phyllium*), birdwings (*Troides*) and the golden Kaiser-i-Hind (*Teinopalpus aureus*), the only butterfly in the first class of national protection ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)). Each island has its endemics: on Hainan the Hainan gibbon (*Nomascus hainanus*), Hainan Eld's deer and the Hainan peacock-pheasant; on Taiwan the Formosan macaque, Swinhoe's pheasant and the Mikado pheasant. Taiwan's fauna also contains Palearctic elements, such as the Siberian weasel and the Taiwan salamanders (*Hynobius*), relics of glacial periods when the island was joined to the mainland. The Chinese crocodile lizard (*Shinisaurus crocodilurus*), sole member of its family, lives along forest streams in Guangxi and Guangdong and in northern Vietnam ([7.17.51](/7.17.51)).

## 69.4. The History of the Chinese Fauna

### 69.4.1. From the Hipparion Fauna to the Quaternary Faunas

In the late Miocene and Pliocene, between about 11 and 2.6 million years ago, much of Eurasia and Africa shared one mammal fauna, named after the three-toed horse *Hipparion*: horses, rhinoceroses, giraffids, antelopes, hyaenas, saber-toothed cats and ostriches ranged from Spain to northern China. All the mammal families now living in China were already present, but a north–south differentiation had begun: open steppe and woodland with grazers in the north, forests with browsers in the south.

In the Quaternary the two faunas diverged, as the figure shows. In the south the early Pleistocene *Gigantopithecus* fauna, named after the giant ape of the karst caves of Guangxi, developed into the middle and late Pleistocene *Ailuropoda–Stegodon* fauna, which already had an Oriental character: giant panda, stegodonts, orangutans, gibbons, tapirs, rhinoceroses, hyaenas. In warm phases it spread north to North China, and many of its members have since disappeared from China (orangutans, tapirs, hyaenas) or have retreated to small areas (gibbons, elephants, the giant panda). In the north the early Pleistocene Nihewan fauna, still with many species now found only in the south, was succeeded by the Zhoukoudian fauna associated with Peking Man and then by the late Pleistocene Salawusu fauna of the Ordos. At the end of the Pleistocene the northern fauna divided into a cold *Mammuthus–Coelodonta* (mammoth and woolly rhinoceros) fauna in the Northeast and the Upper Cave fauna of North China. Moose, wolves, the Mongolian gazelle and Przewalski's horse of these faunas still live in northern China, though in smaller ranges. By the beginning of the Holocene the regional differentiation of the modern fauna was essentially complete.

### 69.4.2. The Uplift of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau

The collision of India with Asia, beginning about 50 million years ago, raised the Himalaya and the Tibetan Plateau, the largest and highest plateau on Earth (about 2.5 million square kilometers, mostly above 4,000 m). The timing of the uplift is debated. Older Chinese work, followed in the Chinese zoology textbooks, emphasized a rapid uplift in the Pliocene and early Pleistocene; paleoaltimetry from fossil leaves and isotopes now suggests that parts of central Tibet were already high in the Eocene and Oligocene, whereas the margins — the Himalaya, the north-eastern plateau and the Hengduan Mountains — rose mainly in the Miocene and Pliocene. In either view, the uplift had three effects on the fauna. It created a barrier between the faunas of Central Asia and South Asia; it intensified the Asian monsoon, which brought rain to southern and eastern China, and desiccated Central Asia, where the Gobi and Taklamakan deserts formed; and it created a new cold, high environment, colonized mainly from the steppes and mountains to the north.

The plateau fauna is therefore young in its present form and Central Asian in its affinities: its species differ from those of the Mongolia–Xinjiang region mostly at the level of species and subspecies, not of genera. Some cold-adapted lineages may even have originated there. The oldest woolly rhinoceros, *Coelodonta thibetana*, about 3.7 million years old, was found in the Zanda Basin of south-western Tibet (Deng and colleagues 2011), suggesting that the woolly megafauna of the Pleistocene steppes was preadapted to cold on the plateau before the ice ages ("out of Tibet"). Genomes of plateau animals — yak, Tibetan antelope, the ground tit, the Tibetan mastiff — show parallel changes in genes of the hypoxia response such as *EPAS1*, a case of **convergent evolution** at the molecular level.

### 69.4.3. Glaciations and Refugia

During the Pleistocene glaciations ([7.10.29](/7.10.29#sec-29.7.2)) ice sheets covered northern Europe and North America, and their faunas were pushed south against the Alps and the Mediterranean or the Gulf of Mexico, with many extinctions. China had only mountain glaciers, on the plateau and the highest ranges; the lowlands were colder and drier but not ice-covered, and the main mountain ranges run in directions that allowed animals to shift up and down slopes and north and south along valleys rather than being trapped. This is the main reason why the Chinese fauna retains so many relicts.

**Definition 69.4.1 (Glacial Refugium)** A **glacial refugium** is an area in which a species or a community persisted during a glacial period while it disappeared from most of its former range, and from which it later recolonized its present range.

Refugia are inferred from fossils and from phylogeography: populations in a refugium have higher genetic diversity, older lineages and private haplotypes, whereas recolonized areas have low diversity and lineages that branch off recently and in a star-like pattern. For eastern Asia, phylogeographic studies of many vertebrates and insects have found not one southern refugium, as in Europe, but several, "refugia within refugia": the Hengduan Mountains, the Qinling–Daba mountains, the Nanling and the mountains of the south-east coast, and Hainan and Taiwan, which were joined to the mainland when sea level fell. Such fragmentation has itself been a source of divergence, giving many species several deeply separated lineages. The genome of the giant panda records repeated expansions and contractions of its population that track the glacial cycles, followed by a recent decline due to people (Zhao and colleagues 2013).

The relicts that survived in these refuges are among the best-known **living fossils** of the course: the giant panda, sole survivor of a lineage of bears that once ranged across Eurasia; the red panda, the only living member of the Ailuridae and the last of a group widespread in the Miocene of Eurasia and North America; the takin; the Chinese alligator, whose only relative is the American alligator ([7.17.52](/7.17.52)); the Chinese giant salamander, whose only relatives are the Japanese giant salamander and the hellbender of North America ([7.17.49](/7.17.49)); the crocodile lizard; the big-headed turtle; and among insects the ice crawlers and *Epiophlebia* ([7.17.30](/7.17.30#sec-30.4.5)). Several of these show the classic eastern Asia – eastern North America disjunction (giant salamanders, alligators, paddlefishes, and many plants), the remains of a Tertiary forest fauna that was continuous across Beringia and was later fragmented by the cooling climate.

### 69.4.4. The Hengduan Mountains

The Hengduan Mountains, the series of parallel north–south ranges and gorges where the Nu (Salween), Lancang (Mekong) and Jinsha (upper Yangtze) rivers run side by side within 100 km, are one of the richest temperate regions on Earth and the core of the biodiversity hotspot of the mountains of south-western China. Five properties combine to explain this richness:

- *Juxtaposition of faunas.* The north–south valleys are corridors that let Oriental species move north and Palearctic species move south, so that both faunas meet here.
- *Altitudinal zonation.* Elevation ranges of 3,000–4,000 m on a single slope produce a sequence of belts from subtropical valley to alpine meadow, each with its own species.
- *Isolation.* Deep gorges and high ridges divide populations of species that cannot cross them, especially those of cool forests and alpine habitats, and promote allopatric speciation.
- *Recent uplift.* Much of the relief formed in the late Miocene and Pliocene; phylogenies of several groups show bursts of speciation in this period, so that the region has acted as a "species pump".
- *Refuge.* Without ice sheets, species could survive the glacial cycles by moving short distances up and down the slopes.

The result is a center of both survival and diversification. Old lineages (giant panda, takin, shrew moles of the genus *Uropsilus*) survive beside young radiations: the voles of the genus *Eothenomys*, pheasants, laughingthrushes, parrotbills, lazy toads (*Oreolalax*, *Scutiger*) and cascade frogs (*Amolops*), and among insects many beetles, bumblebees and swallowtails. It is no exaggeration that China, through this region, is the world center of pheasants and of laughingthrushes.

## 69.5. Endemic and Threatened Animals of China

### 69.5.1. Diversity and Endemism

China is one of the dozen "megadiverse" countries. Recent checklists list about 690 species of mammals (Wei and colleagues 2021) and about 1,450 species of birds (Zheng 2017), with several hundred each of amphibians and reptiles and more than 3,000 freshwater and marine fishes; the counts of amphibians and reptiles have grown rapidly with molecular taxonomy. Insects and other invertebrates are far from fully known: about 100,000 insect species have been described from China, perhaps a tenth of the world total. Endemism among vertebrates is about 10 % overall, and highest in amphibians and in groups of the mountains of the south-west.

Endemics are of two kinds. *Paleoendemics* are relicts of formerly widespread lineages, now restricted: the giant panda, the baiji (sole member of the family Lipotidae), Père David's deer, the Chinese alligator, the crocodile lizard. *Neoendemics* are young species that have not spread from where they arose: the snowfinches and pikas of the plateau, the many *Phrynocephalus* of the deserts, and most of the endemic pheasants, laughingthrushes and frogs of the Hengduan Mountains. Mapping the two kinds separately distinguishes regions that are museums of old lineages from regions that are cradles of new ones; the south-western mountains are both.

### 69.5.2. Threats and Categories of Protection

The threats to the Chinese fauna are those of the rest of the world — loss and fragmentation of habitat, overexploitation, pollution, introduced species and climate change — acting on a dense population with a long history of farming, a large demand for wildlife products and rapid recent development. Their effects are clearest in the Yangtze. The baiji was declared functionally extinct after a survey in 2006 found none (Turvey and colleagues 2007), the Chinese paddlefish was declared extinct in 2020, and the Chinese sturgeon no longer reproduces naturally below the Gezhouba dam; a ten-year ban on fishing in the Yangtze began in 2021.

Protection in China uses several overlapping systems:

| System | Categories | Status |
|---|---|---|
| *List of Wildlife under Special State Protection* (first published 1989, revised 2021) | Class I and Class II | Legal: hunting, trade and use are prohibited or licensed; 980 species and 8 groups in 2021, about a quarter of them in Class I |
| IUCN Red List | Extinct, Extinct in the Wild; Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable (together "threatened"); Near Threatened, Least Concern; Data Deficient | Global scientific assessment of extinction risk by quantitative criteria (population size and decline, range size) |
| China Biodiversity Red List (vertebrates 2015, revised) | IUCN categories applied within China | National assessment; a species can be more threatened in China than globally |
| CITES | Appendices I, II and III | International trade: Appendix I trade prohibited, Appendix II regulated by permits |

The categories measure different things. The Red List category is an estimate of the risk of extinction; the national class is a legal decision that also weighs endemism, cultural value and the needs of enforcement. The giant panda was moved from Endangered to Vulnerable by the IUCN in 2016, after the fourth national survey counted 1,864 wild pandas, but it remains in Class I. Some recoveries show what protection can achieve. The crested ibis, reduced to seven birds found in Yangxian, Shaanxi, in 1981, now numbers several thousand in the wild and in captivity. Père David's deer, extinct in China after the last herd in the imperial park was lost around 1900, was returned from the herd kept at Woburn Abbey in England in 1985 and now lives in free-ranging herds at Dafeng and Shishou. The Tibetan antelope, reduced by poaching for its wool in the 1990s, has recovered to an estimated 300,000 and was moved from Endangered to Near Threatened in 2016. Others remain on the edge: the Hainan gibbon numbers fewer than 40 individuals in a single forest, the rarest ape in the world. The principles behind these cases — population viability, reserve design, connectivity — belong to conservation biology.

### 69.5.3. Notes on the Chinese Textbooks

The regionalization of §69.3 is the one used in the Chinese zoology textbooks, which take it from Zhang Rongzu; the names of regions, subregions and faunal groups correspond one to one. A few points are treated differently here. The red panda is placed in its own family, Ailuridae, not in the raccoon family Procyonidae: molecular phylogenies show that it is a separate lineage of the Musteloidea, and its disjunction is with fossil ailurids, not with the American raccoons. The crocodile lizard is not endemic to China, since a population was discovered in northern Vietnam in 2003. Continental drift is explained by plate tectonics, driven by mantle convection, not by tides or the centrifugal force of the Earth's rotation, as in Wegener's original proposal. The flightless paleognaths are no longer a clean example of vicariance (§69.2.2). Finally, the Hipparion fauna is dated here to the late Miocene and Pliocene, from about 11 million years ago, and the timing of the uplift of the plateau is presented as debated rather than as a single Pliocene–Pleistocene event.

## Questions

1. Why is the boundary between the Palearctic and Oriental realms sharp in western China but a broad transition zone in the east?

   **Answer** In the west it follows the Himalaya and the edge of the Tibetan Plateau, a barrier several thousand meters high with very different climates on either side; species meet along a steep altitudinal gradient, so the boundary is narrow in horizontal distance (though Oriental species penetrate along valleys). In the east the Qinling is a barrier only for its own length; east of it, the North China Plain and the lower Yangtze form a continuous lowland with a gradual climatic gradient, so each species' range limit lies at a different latitude and the faunas interpenetrate over hundreds of kilometers. The boundary there is a statistical average of many range limits.

2. Compute the Jaccard similarity and the Simpson dissimilarity for two faunas: area A has 40 species, area B has 10, and all 10 species of B also occur in A. What does the difference between the two indices tell you?

   **Answer** $a = 10$, $b = 30$, $c = 0$. $J = 10/40 = 0.25$, so the Jaccard distance is 0.75; $\beta_{\mathrm{sim}} = \min(30, 0)/(10 + 0) = 0$. The Jaccard index is low only because B is poorer than A; there is no turnover of species, B is a subset (nested) of A. $\beta_{\mathrm{sim}}$ separates true replacement of species from differences in richness, which is why it is preferred for delimiting faunal regions, where richness varies greatly with area and climate.

3. Explain why the Chinese fauna retains more relict lineages than the fauna of Europe at similar latitudes, and give three examples with their closest living relatives.

   **Answer** China had no continental ice sheets in the Pleistocene, only mountain glaciers, and its mountain ranges and valleys allowed species to track their habitats by short movements up and down slopes and along north–south valleys, with several separate refugia (Hengduan, Qinling–Daba, Nanling, the south-east coast). Europe was largely covered by ice in the north, and its east–west mountain barriers (Alps, Pyrenees) and the Mediterranean trapped species moving south, causing many extinctions. Examples: Chinese giant salamander (Japanese giant salamander, hellbender); Chinese alligator (American alligator); giant panda (other bears, as the earliest-diverging living lineage); red panda (no close living relatives, its own family); *Epiophlebia sinensis* (two other *Epiophlebia* species in the Himalaya and Japan).

4. The wild yak and the Tibetan antelope live on the Tibetan Plateau, but the region is placed in the Palearctic realm and in the same subrealm as the Mongolia–Xinjiang region. Why?

   **Answer** Realms and subrealms are defined by the historical relationships of faunas, not by present climate. The plateau fauna was colonized mainly from the steppes and deserts of Central Asia as the plateau rose; its species belong to genera that also occur there (pikas, marmots, gazelles, wild asses, toad-headed agamas, snowfinches) and differ from them at the species or subspecies level. It has few elements from the Oriental realm, which is separated by the Himalaya. The ecological differences (alpine meadow vs desert) are expressed in the ecogeographic faunal groups, not in the higher units of the regionalization.

5. (Open question) Design a study to test whether the Hengduan Mountains acted as a "species pump" for a group of frogs or birds.

   **Answer** Approach: build a dated, densely sampled phylogeny of the group (for example cascade frogs *Amolops* or laughingthrushes) from genomic data, with fossil or geological calibrations; reconstruct ancestral areas (Hengduan vs other regions) with a model of range evolution; estimate speciation and extinction rates through time and by region with lineage-through-time plots and state-dependent diversification models. Predictions of the pump hypothesis: speciation rates in Hengduan lineages higher than elsewhere, peaking in the late Miocene–Pliocene when the relief formed; frequent dispersal out of the Hengduan into neighboring regions (the region as a source); sister species separated by ridges or gorges, or by elevation. Controls: compare with a group of lowland species, and test whether results are robust to sampling and to the choice of calibration.
