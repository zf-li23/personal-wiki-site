The nervous and endocrine systems coordinate the body; the first by fast, addressed electrical signals, the second by slower chemical messages broadcast through the blood. In vertebrates the two are linked anatomically as well as functionally: the hypothalamus of the brain controls the pituitary, the adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion, and several endocrine glands grow from the same pharyngeal pouches that carry the cranial nerves and aortic arches. Comparative anatomy of the nervous system has a long tradition of ranking vertebrates on a "scale" from fishes to mammals, with the cerebral cortex at the top. Modern comparative neurobiology replaces the scale with a tree: every lineage elaborated different parts of a brain whose basic plan was already present in the earliest vertebrates, and large, complex brains evolved independently in several lineages (sharks, teleosts, birds, mammals).

This chapter first describes the organization and development of the nervous system (§67.1) and the spinal cord with its nerves (§67.2), then compares the five regions of the brain across the classes (§67.3), brain size and encephalization (§67.4), the cranial nerves (§67.5) and the autonomic nervous system (§67.6). The second part compares the endocrine glands (§67.7). Neurophysiology and the functions of brain regions are in [7.14](/7.14) ([7.14.7](/7.14.7), [7.14.44](/7.14.44)–[7.14.46](/7.14.46)) and [7.16](/7.16), the evolution of nervous systems from the first neurons onward in [7.16.53](/7.16.53), the development of the neural tube and neural crest in [7.5.18](/7.5.18)–[7.5.20](/7.5.20), and hormones and their actions in [7.14.32](/7.14.32)–[7.14.38](/7.14.38). The nervous systems of invertebrates are compared in [7.17.44](/7.17.44), the sense organs of vertebrates in [7.17.68](/7.17.68).

## 67.1. Organization and Development

### 67.1.1. Central and Peripheral Nervous System

The nervous system is divided into the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord), which develops from the neural tube, and the peripheral nervous system (cranial and spinal nerves with their ganglia, the autonomic and enteric nervous systems), which develops mostly from the neural crest and the ectodermal placodes ([7.5.20](/7.5.20); [7.5.22](/7.5.22#sec-22.1)). The neural tube of vertebrates is the **dorsal hollow nerve cord** of chordates ([7.17.43](/7.17.43)); the **neural crest** and the cranial **placodes** are vertebrate innovations that supply the sensory neurons of the spinal and most cranial ganglia, all peripheral glia (Schwann cells), the autonomic neurons and the chromaffin cells ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1)).

| Source | Neural derivatives |
|---|---|
| Neural tube | Brain and spinal cord, retina, neurohypophysis, pineal organ; motor neurons of all somatic and visceral motor nerves |
| Neural crest | Sensory neurons of spinal ganglia and of the proximal ganglia of V, VII, IX, X; Schwann cells; sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia; enteric nervous system; adrenal medulla (chromaffin cells) |
| Placodes | Olfactory epithelium (olfactory placode), lens, inner ear and lateral line organs (otic and lateral line placodes), sensory neurons of the distal (epibranchial) ganglia of VII, IX, X and of the trigeminal ganglion, adenohypophysis (adenohypophyseal placode) |

### 67.1.2. Brain Vesicles, Flexures and Ventricles

The anterior end of the neural tube expands into three primary vesicles, the prosencephalon (forebrain), mesencephalon (midbrain) and rhombencephalon (hindbrain), and then into five ([7.5.18](/7.5.18#sec-18.6.1)): the prosencephalon divides into the telencephalon (cerebrum) and diencephalon, the rhombencephalon into the metencephalon (cerebellum and, in mammals, the pons) and myelencephalon (medulla oblongata). The hindbrain is segmented in the embryo into rhombomeres, each with its own pattern of *Hox* gene expression and its own cranial nerve motor nuclei, which links the brain to the segmental pharyngeal arches ([7.5.18](/7.5.18#sec-18.6.3)). The tube bends at the cephalic (midbrain) and cervical flexures; the flexures are slight in fishes and amphibians, larger in reptiles and greatest in birds and mammals, where the enlarged forebrain folds over the rest of the brain.

The cavity of the neural tube persists as the ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord, filled with cerebrospinal fluid secreted by the choroid plexuses, vascular folds of the thin roofs of the ventricles. The lateral ventricles (I and II) lie in the cerebral hemispheres, the third in the diencephalon, the cerebral aqueduct in the midbrain (expanded into an optic ventricle under the tectum in non-mammals) and the fourth ventricle in the hindbrain.

### 67.1.3. Meninges

The brain and spinal cord are wrapped in connective-tissue membranes, the meninges, which increase in number in the lineage leading to mammals. Fishes have a single primitive meninx. Amphibians, reptiles and birds have an outer dura mater and an inner leptomeninx (secondary meninx). Mammals divide the leptomeninx into an arachnoid membrane and a pia mater, with the subarachnoid space between them containing cerebrospinal fluid, so that the brain floats in a fluid cushion; the meninges derive from both neural crest (forebrain) and mesoderm.

### 67.1.4. Functional Columns

Within the spinal cord and hindbrain, neurons are arranged in longitudinal columns according to function, a pattern that explains the composition of the cranial and spinal nerves (§67.5). Sensory (afferent) columns lie dorsally (the alar plate), motor (efferent) columns ventrally (the basal plate), separated by the sulcus limitans. Within each, the classical scheme distinguishes somatic and visceral components:

| Component | Abbreviation | Innervates or receives from |
|---|---|---|
| General somatic afferent | GSA | Skin (touch, temperature, pain), muscles and joints (proprioception) |
| Special somatic afferent | SSA | Inner ear, lateral line and electroreceptors; retina (classically) |
| General visceral afferent | GVA | Viscera |
| Special visceral afferent | SVA | Taste buds (and olfaction, classically) |
| General visceral efferent | GVE | Smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands (autonomic preganglionic neurons) |
| Special visceral (branchial) efferent | SVE, BE | **Branchiomeric muscles** of the pharyngeal arches |
| General somatic efferent | GSE | Somatic (myotomal) muscles: trunk, limbs, eye muscles, tongue |

The scheme is a useful summary of nerve components, although development shows that the branchial efferent column is not "visceral" in origin (its muscles derive from paraxial cranial mesoderm, [7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.2.1)), and the categories are now often simplified to somatic sensory, visceral sensory, somatic motor, branchial motor and visceral motor.

## 67.2. The Spinal Cord and Spinal Nerves

### 67.2.1. The Spinal Cord

In gnathostomes the spinal cord has a central H- or butterfly-shaped grey matter, with dorsal horns of sensory interneurons and ventral horns of motor neurons, surrounded by white matter of myelinated tracts. In lampreys and hagfishes the cord is flattened, its axons are unmyelinated (myelin is a gnathostome character, [7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.1)), and grey and white matter are not sharply separated; the cord is nourished by diffusion from the surface. Lampreys and many fishes and amphibian larvae have giant reticulospinal neurons, the Mauthner cells, in the hindbrain, whose axons run the length of the cord and trigger the rapid escape bend (the C-start).

The cord is uniform in fishes. In tetrapods it has cervical and lumbar enlargements where the motor neurons of the limbs lie; their size reflects the use of the limbs, so the cervical enlargement is huge in birds (wings), the lumbar one in kangaroos and ostriches, and both are absent in snakes and caecilians. Birds have a gelatinous glycogen body in a dorsal cleft of the lumbosacral enlargement, of unknown function; a similar expansion of the neural canal in the sacrum of some dinosaurs gave rise to the myth of a "second brain". In mammals the spinal cord grows less than the vertebral column, so that in adult humans it ends at the first or second lumbar vertebra and the lumbar and sacral roots descend as the cauda equina; in most fishes, amphibians and reptiles it extends to the end of the tail. Teleosts and elasmobranchs have a caudal neurosecretory organ, the urophysis, at the end of the cord (§67.7.6).

### 67.2.2. Spinal Nerves

A spinal nerve has a dorsal root, carrying sensory fibres from neurons in a dorsal root ganglion (neural crest), and a ventral root, carrying motor fibres from the ventral horn. In lancelets the dorsal and ventral roots leave the cord separately and alternately, and the dorsal roots also carry motor fibres to the visceral muscles. In lampreys the roots alternate and remain separate. In hagfishes and gnathostomes they join to form a mixed spinal nerve, which divides into a dorsal ramus (epaxial muscles and dorsal skin), a ventral ramus (hypaxial muscles, ventral skin and limbs) and, in gnathostomes, rami communicantes to the sympathetic chain ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.3.2)). In tetrapods the ventral rami of the nerves supplying the limbs interconnect into the brachial and lumbosacral plexuses, which redistribute fibres from several segments to each limb muscle; the plexuses form where limb muscle precursors from several somites mingle.

## 67.3. The Brain

### 67.3.1. Myelencephalon

The medulla oblongata continues the spinal cord into the head. It contains the nuclei of cranial nerves V–XII, the reticular formation that controls posture, breathing and heart rate ([7.14.20](/7.14.20#sec-20.2)), and the ascending and descending tracts between spinal cord and brain. Its roof is thin and forms a choroid plexus over the fourth ventricle. It is the least variable region of the brain, but its sensory areas enlarge with the sense they serve. Carp and goldfish have huge vagal lobes for the taste buds of the palatal organ and pharynx, which they use to sort food from mud; catfishes have large facial lobes for the taste buds covering their barbels and body surface; electric fishes have an electrosensory lateral line lobe; and fishes and aquatic amphibians have an octavolateral area for the inner ear and lateral line (**lateral line**).

### 67.3.2. Metencephalon: Cerebellum and Pons

The cerebellum arises from the dorsal part of the first rhombomere and coordinates movements, balance and timing ([7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.3)). Its size varies more than that of any other brain region and correlates with agility and with the use of electrosensory or vestibular information rather than with taxonomic position.

- *Cyclostomes.* Lampreys have only a narrow cerebellar plate; hagfishes have no distinct cerebellum.
- *Chondrichthyans.* The cerebellum is large, with a folded corpus and paired auricles (vestibular and lateral-line parts), especially in active pelagic sharks and rays.
- *Actinopterygians.* The corpus cerebelli is often large; in teleosts a part of it, the valvula, projects forward into the midbrain ventricle, and in mormyrid electric fishes the valvula is enormous, covering the whole brain and making up most of its mass, the largest relative cerebellum of any vertebrate ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.8)).
- *Amphibians.* Small, a transverse band, little more than in lampreys; consistent with their slow movements.
- *Reptiles.* Small in lizards and snakes, larger in crocodylians and turtles.
- *Birds.* Large and folded into transverse folia, with a central vermis and small lateral flocculi.
- *Mammals.* The largest cerebellum relative to brain size among amniotes, with a median vermis and two cerebellar hemispheres (neocerebellum) connected to the cerebral cortex through the pons, a ventral bulge of the metencephalon carrying the corticopontine and pontocerebellar fibres; the pons is a mammalian structure. In humans the cerebellum contains about 80 % of all neurons of the brain.

### 67.3.3. Mesencephalon

The roof of the midbrain, the tectum, is the main integrating centre of the brain in non-mammalian vertebrates. Its superficial part, the optic tectum, receives the retinal projection and forms a topographic map of visual space; deeper layers integrate visual, auditory, lateral line, somatosensory and, in pit vipers, infrared maps in register, and send motor commands for orienting movements of the eyes, head and body. The optic tectum forms the paired optic lobes, very large in visually guided teleosts, lizards and birds (in which they are pushed laterally by the large forebrain and cerebellum). Ventral to it lies the torus semicircularis, the auditory and lateral-line midbrain centre.

In mammals the tectum is the corpora quadrigemina: the superior colliculi (the optic tectum, which controls visual reflexes and eye movements) and the inferior colliculi (the auditory torus). Visual perception has moved to the thalamus and cortex, but the superior colliculus keeps the ancestral role in orienting. The floor of the midbrain (tegmentum) contains the nuclei of cranial nerves III and IV, the red nucleus and, in amniotes, the dopaminergic substantia nigra; in mammals the cerebral peduncles of descending cortical tracts form its ventral surface.

### 67.3.4. Diencephalon

The diencephalon surrounds the third ventricle and has three parts.

- *Epithalamus.* Its roof bears the pineal complex: the pineal organ (epiphysis) and, in front of it, a parapineal organ, which in lampreys and many lizards and the tuatara forms the parietal eye with a lens and retina beneath an opening in the skull (the parietal foramen, [7.17.68](/7.17.68)). The paired habenulae, which relay forebrain signals to the midbrain, are asymmetric in many fishes, amphibians and reptiles.
- *Thalamus.* Paired masses of nuclei in the lateral walls, which relay sensory information to the telencephalon. It is small in fishes and amphibians, which process much of their sensory information in the tectum, and large in birds and mammals, where it relays nearly all sensory input (except olfaction) to the pallium; in mammals the two thalami often meet across the ventricle in an interthalamic adhesion.
- *Hypothalamus.* The floor of the diencephalon, with the optic chiasm, the infundibulum leading to the pituitary, and in mammals the mammillary bodies. It controls the pituitary, body temperature, feeding, drinking and reproductive behaviour (**hypothalamus**; [7.14.34](/7.14.34#sec-34.1)). Fishes have large inferior lobes of the hypothalamus and, in chondrichthyans and many actinopterygians, a saccus vasculosus, a highly vascularized sac behind the pituitary lined by coronet cells; in salmonids it has been shown to act as a sensor of seasonal changes in day length.

### 67.3.5. Telencephalon

The telencephalon develops from the anterior end of the neural tube and consists of the olfactory bulbs and the cerebral hemispheres. Each hemisphere has a dorsal roof, the pallium, and a ventral floor, the subpallium, which forms the basal ganglia (striatum and pallidum) and the septum. Older texts held that the forebrain of fishes is "only smell-brain"; tracer studies show that the fish pallium receives visual, auditory, lateral-line and somatosensory input through the thalamus and that the olfactory input is a minor part, so the multisensory pallium is ancestral.

Two different developmental geometries produce the hemispheres:

1. *Evagination.* In most vertebrates the walls of the telencephalon bulge outward and the hemispheres enclose lateral ventricles; the pallium lies dorsal to the ventricle (cyclostomes, chondrichthyans, sarcopterygians including tetrapods). In amphibians the pallium remains thin and its neurons stay close to the ventricle (a periventricular pallium).
2. *Eversion.* In actinopterygians the dorsal walls of the telencephalon roll outward and down, so that the ventricle is spread over the dorsal surface as a thin membrane and the pallial zones are rearranged: the medial pallium lies laterally and the lateral pallium medially. Eversion explains the confusing topology of the teleost forebrain and the long debate about the homologies of its parts.

On the basis of gene expression (*Emx1*, *Tbr1*, *Lhx2*, *Dlx*) and connections, the pallium of tetrapods is divided into four sectors, whose homologues can be identified across the classes ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.6)):

| Pallial sector | Amphibians | Reptiles | Birds | Mammals |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Medial pallium | Medial pallium | Medial cortex | Hippocampal formation | Hippocampus |
| Dorsal pallium | Dorsal pallium | Dorsal cortex (three-layered) | Hyperpallium (Wulst) | Neocortex (isocortex), six-layered |
| Lateral pallium | Lateral pallium | Lateral (olfactory) cortex | Piriform cortex | Piriform (olfactory) cortex |
| Ventral pallium | Ventral pallium | Dorsal ventricular ridge (DVR) | Mesopallium, nidopallium, arcopallium | Pallial amygdala, claustrum (and in some interpretations parts of neocortex) |
| Subpallium | Striatum, pallidum, septum | Striatum, pallidum | Striatum, pallidum (including song nucleus Area X) | Basal ganglia (caudate, putamen, globus pallidus), septum |

In reptiles a large mass of neurons, the dorsal ventricular ridge, bulges into the lateral ventricle from the lateral and ventral pallium. In birds the DVR is enormous and forms most of the forebrain. Classical neuroanatomy called these masses "striatum" (paleostriatum, neostriatum, hyperstriatum), on the assumption that the avian forebrain was a hypertrophied basal ganglion, which also implied that birds lacked the equivalent of a cerebral cortex. Comparative studies of gene expression, connections and neurochemistry showed that only the ventral part is subpallial; in 2004–2005 the Avian Brain Nomenclature Consortium renamed the pallial parts hyperpallium, mesopallium, nidopallium and arcopallium. The avian pallium is nuclear rather than layered, but its circuits (sensory input from the thalamus to intercalated fields, then to association and motor regions) correspond to those of mammalian cortical layers, and parrots and corvids pack more neurons into their small forebrains than primates of the same brain mass. Complex cognition therefore evolved convergently in birds and mammals on pallial structures of different architecture.

The neocortex of mammals is a six-layered sheet derived from the dorsal pallium (**neocortex**; [7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.1)), which receives sensory input from the thalamus and contains motor and association areas. It is relatively small in monotremes, marsupials and insectivores and expanded in primates, cetaceans and carnivores, where it covers the other parts of the brain and is folded into gyri and sulci (gyrencephalic brains) once a certain size is reached; small brains are smooth (lissencephalic). Placental mammals connect the two hemispheres through a large commissure of neocortical fibres, the corpus callosum; monotremes and marsupials use the anterior commissure instead.

### 67.3.6. Olfactory Bulbs and the Sensory Balance of the Brain

The olfactory bulbs vary with the importance of smell. They are huge in hagfishes, sharks (where they lie at the ends of long olfactory tracts), salamanders and many mammals (rodents, insectivores, carnivores, ungulates), and small in most birds (except kiwis, vultures and procellariiform seabirds, which find food by smell), in primates and in seals. Toothed whales have lost the olfactory bulbs and nerves completely, and baleen whales retain only rudiments; birds and primates rely on vision, toothed whales on echolocation, and in each case the regions of the brain serving the dominant sense expanded while the olfactory regions regressed. Accessory olfactory bulbs, which receive input from the vomeronasal organ, are large in snakes and many mammals and absent in birds, crocodylians, catarrhine primates and whales ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)).

### 67.3.7. Sensory Specializations in the Brain

Because each sense projects to particular brain regions, sensory specializations can be read from brain anatomy, in living animals and, through endocasts of the braincase, in fossils.

- *Electroreception.* Mormyrid and gymnotiform electric fishes have an enlarged electrosensory lateral line lobe in the medulla and, in mormyrids, the giant cerebellar valvula; their electrosensory brains evolved convergently from the same octavolateral precursors ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5)).
- *Infrared sensing.* Pit vipers and boas have a special trigeminal nucleus (the nucleus of the lateral descending trigeminal tract) that relays signals from the pit organs to the optic tectum, where they form a map in register with the visual map ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)).
- *Hearing.* Barn owls have enlarged auditory nuclei (nucleus magnocellularis, laminaris, angularis) and a map of auditory space in the midbrain; echolocating bats and toothed whales have huge inferior colliculi.
- *Touch.* The neocortex of mammals contains maps of the body surface in which specialized organs occupy disproportionate areas: the whiskers of rodents (the barrel field, one cortical barrel per whisker), the bill of the platypus (most of its somatosensory cortex, including electroreceptive inputs), the nose of the star-nosed mole.
- *Vision.* Diurnal birds and lizards have large optic tecta, primates a large visual cortex occupying much of the occipital lobe; nocturnal and burrowing mammals have reduced visual areas.

Endocasts show, for example, that early mammaliaforms enlarged the olfactory bulbs and neocortex, that the brains of maniraptoran dinosaurs approached those of birds in relative size before flight, and that *Archaeopteryx* had a brain intermediate between those of non-avian theropods and modern birds ([7.17.53](/7.17.53); [7.17.54](/7.17.54)).

## 67.4. Brain Size and Encephalization

Brain mass $E$ scales with body mass $P$ as a power law, $E = kP^{\alpha}$, with an exponent $\alpha$ of about $2/3$ to $3/4$ among species of a class ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.8); **allometry**). On a log–log plot, the brains of mammals and birds lie on lines about ten times higher than those of fishes, amphibians and reptiles of the same body mass. Within a line, the vertical deviation of a species is a measure of relative brain size, the **encephalization quotient**, $\mathrm{EQ} = E/E_{\mathrm{exp}}$, the ratio of the actual brain mass to the mass expected from the allometric line of the group (for mammals, after Jerison, $E_{\mathrm{exp}} = 0.12\,P^{2/3}$ with masses in grams).

**Example 67.4.1 (Expected Brain Mass and EQ)** A human of $P = 65\,\mathrm{kg} = 65{,}000\,\mathrm{g}$ has an expected brain mass of $0.12 \times 65{,}000^{2/3} \approx 0.12 \times 1{,}617 \approx 194\,\mathrm{g}$. With an actual brain mass of about $1{,}350\,\mathrm{g}$, $\mathrm{EQ} \approx 7$. A cat of $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ has $E_{\mathrm{exp}} = 0.12 \times 3{,}000^{2/3} \approx 25\,\mathrm{g}$ and an actual brain of about $25$–$30\,\mathrm{g}$, $\mathrm{EQ} \approx 1$. The exponent matters: with $\alpha = 3/4$ the human EQ is smaller (about 5) and large mammals look relatively smaller-brained, so EQ values must always be compared with the same reference line.

EQ is a coarse measure. It correlates with behavioural flexibility within some groups, but the number of neurons and their density in the pallium are better predictors of cognitive abilities across groups: primate and corvid brains are more densely packed than those of rodents or ungulates of the same size. Within lineages, brain size increased many times independently (in primates, cetaceans, elephants, parrots, corvids, and among fishes in mormyrids and sharks).

Brains are expensive. Neural tissue consumes about ten times as much energy per gram as the body on average, and the human brain, 2 % of body mass, uses about 20 % of the resting metabolic rate. Encephalization therefore requires either a higher energy supply (a richer diet, a higher metabolic rate) or savings elsewhere (a smaller gut, as proposed for primates by the "expensive tissue hypothesis"), and the brains of endotherms are larger than those of ectotherms partly because endotherms have more energy to spend. Counts of neurons with the isotropic fractionator method have shown that brains of the same mass contain very different numbers of neurons: rodent brains increase in neuron size as they grow larger, whereas primate brains add neurons at nearly constant density, and the pallium of parrots and songbirds contains two to four times as many neurons as a primate cortex of the same mass (Olkowicz et al. 2016).

## 67.5. The Cranial Nerves

### 67.5.1. Definition and Numbering

**Definition 67.5.1 (Cranial Nerve)** A **cranial nerve** is a peripheral nerve that connects with the brain and leaves the skull through a foramen of its own. By convention the cranial nerves of tetrapods are numbered with Roman numerals from front to back, I–XII, in the order in which they leave the human brain; a nerve 0 (the terminal nerve) lies in front of I.

Anamniotes are traditionally said to have ten pairs of cranial nerves (0 and I–X) and amniotes twelve (0 and I–XII). The difference is one of definition. The accessory nerve (XI) is a branch of the vagus that becomes independent in amniotes, its motor neurons extending into the upper spinal cord; the hypoglossal nerve (XII) is formed from the most anterior spinal (spino-occipital) nerves, which were incorporated into the skull as the occipital region grew backward. Fishes and amphibians have the corresponding fibres in the vagus and in their first spinal nerves. Fishes, aquatic amphibians and lampreys also have separate lateral line nerves (anterior and posterior lateral line nerves), which were once counted as branches of VII and X but are distinct nerves with their own placodes and ganglia.

### 67.5.3. Cranial Nerves of Cyclostomes

Cyclostomes show both the ancient features and the specializations of the cranial nerve pattern. Lampreys have the full set of eye-muscle nerves (III, IV, VI), but the abducens supplies, besides the lateral rectus, the ventral (inferior) rectus, which in gnathostomes is supplied by the oculomotor nerve, and the trigeminal nerve is huge, supplying the muscles of the oral disc and the velum. Hagfishes, whose eyes are degenerate and covered by skin, have no extrinsic eye muscles and no oculomotor, trochlear or abducens nerves; their trigeminal nerve supplies the protractor and retractor muscles of the dental plate. Both groups have lateral line nerves and a vagus supplying the gill pouches, and both lack a separate accessory nerve and a hypoglossal nerve, consistent with the absence of a muscular tongue, while their hypobranchial muscles are supplied by spinal nerves. The pattern of the branchial nerves (V, VII, IX, X), each associated with an epibranchial placode and a pharyngeal arch, is the same in cyclostomes and gnathostomes and was therefore present in the earliest vertebrates.

### 67.5.4. Nerves and Arches

The cranial nerves fall into groups that match the organization of the head ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.2)).

1. *Special sensory nerves* (0, I, II, VIII and the lateral line nerves) serve the paired sense organs derived from the forebrain and from placodes.
2. *Somatic motor nerves* (III, IV, VI, XII) serve muscles of somitic or paraxial origin: the eye muscles and the tongue. They resemble the ventral roots of spinal nerves.
3. *Branchial nerves* (V, VII, IX, X, with XI) are the nerves of the pharyngeal arches. Each typically has a sensory ganglion (from neural crest and an epibranchial placode), a branchial motor component for the muscles of its arch, and, in fishes, a characteristic branching around a gill slit: a pretrematic branch in front of the slit, a posttrematic branch behind it and a pharyngeal branch to the lining. The trigeminal nerve serves the mandibular arch, the facial the hyoid arch, the glossopharyngeal the third arch and the vagus all the rest; as the arches were transformed into jaws, ear ossicles, hyoid and larynx, their nerves followed. The vagus is so large because the posterior arches fused into its territory and because it supplies the viscera of the thorax and abdomen.

This arrangement is the basis of the segmental theory of the head: the cranial nerves were once interpreted as dorsal and ventral roots of segmental nerves, like spinal nerves. The modern view is that the head has two kinds of segmentation, the rhombomeres of the hindbrain and the pharyngeal arches, which are coordinated by neural crest streams, while the head mesoderm is not segmented in the same way as the trunk; the cranial nerves are not simple equivalents of spinal nerves.

## 67.6. The Autonomic Nervous System

The **autonomic nervous system** controls smooth muscle, heart and glands through chains of two neurons: a preganglionic neuron in the brainstem or spinal cord and a postganglionic neuron in a peripheral ganglion derived from the neural crest ([7.14.7](/7.14.7#sec-7.4)). It has a sympathetic division, whose preganglionic neurons lie in the thoracic and lumbar spinal cord and whose ganglia form chains beside the vertebral column (paravertebral) and around the major arteries (prevertebral), and a parasympathetic division, with preganglionic neurons in the brainstem (nerves III, VII, IX and X) and in the sacral cord, and ganglia close to or within the target organs; the enteric nervous system in the gut wall works largely independently ([7.14.26](/7.14.26#sec-26.3.1)).

In mammals the preganglionic sympathetic neurons lie in the lateral horn of the thoracic and upper lumbar cord. Their myelinated axons leave through the ventral roots and white rami communicantes to the paravertebral chain, where they synapse or pass through as splanchnic nerves to prevertebral ganglia (coeliac, superior and inferior mesenteric); the unmyelinated postganglionic axons return to the spinal nerves through grey rami. The chain extends into the neck as the superior, middle and inferior (stellate) cervical ganglia, which supply the head and the heart. Parasympathetic preganglionic axons travel in the oculomotor (to the ciliary ganglion and the iris), facial (to the pterygopalatine and submandibular ganglia and the lacrimal and salivary glands), glossopharyngeal (to the otic ganglion and the parotid gland) and vagus nerves (to ganglia in the walls of the thoracic and abdominal organs), and in the pelvic nerves. The adrenal medulla is the one site where preganglionic sympathetic fibres end directly on neural-crest-derived endocrine cells.

Comparative anatomy shows a gradual assembly of this system.

| | Lancelets | Cyclostomes | Chondrichthyans | Teleosts | Amphibians | Amniotes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sympathetic chain | — | Absent; scattered sympathetic-like neurons (lampreys) | Segmental paravertebral ganglia, not linked into a chain; no sympathetic outflow to the head | Paired chains extending into the head | Chains with cranial extension | Paired chains from neck to sacrum; cervical ganglia |
| Cranial parasympathetic | Visceral fibres in dorsal roots | Vagus to the heart (excitatory in lampreys) and gut | III, VII, IX, X | III, VII, IX, X | III, VII, IX, X | III, VII, IX, X |
| Sacral (pelvic) outflow | — | — | Spinal visceral outflow to the cloaca | Pelvic nerves | Pelvic nerves | Sacral parasympathetic (pelvic splanchnic nerves) |
| Adrenal chromaffin tissue | — | In walls of veins | Segmental bodies along the kidney | Scattered in the head kidney | Within the interrenal tissue | Adrenal medulla (mammals) or mixed (reptiles, birds) |

Hagfish hearts have no autonomic innervation at all, lamprey hearts are accelerated (not slowed) by the vagus through nicotinic receptors, and elasmobranch hearts are slowed by the vagus but receive no sympathetic nerves; dual antagonistic innervation of the heart is fully developed only in bony fishes and tetrapods. The recognition of a "sacral parasympathetic" outflow as distinct from the sympathetic one has been questioned: in mouse embryos the sacral preganglionic neurons share the transcription-factor profile of thoracolumbar sympathetic neurons (Espinosa-Medina et al. 2016), which suggests that the pelvic autonomic outflow may be sympathetic and the parasympathetic system purely cranial.

## 67.7. The Endocrine Glands

The endocrine glands of vertebrates derive from all three germ layers and from the neural tube and neural crest. Several of them are vertebrate innovations built from structures already present in their chordate relatives: the thyroid from the **endostyle**, the adenohypophysis perhaps from Hatschek's pit of lancelets, the parathyroids from pharyngeal pouch tissue that in fishes forms parts of the gills.

### 67.7.1. The Pituitary

The pituitary (hypophysis) has two parts of different origin, which come together beneath the diencephalon ([7.14.34](/7.14.34#sec-34.1)). The **adenohypophysis** develops from Rathke's pouch, an outgrowth of the ectoderm of the roof of the stomodeum (from the adenohypophyseal placode); the **neurohypophysis** develops from the infundibulum, a downgrowth of the floor of the diencephalon. The adenohypophysis comprises the pars distalis (with cells secreting growth hormone, prolactin, thyroid-stimulating hormone, adrenocorticotropic hormone and the gonadotropins), the pars intermedia (melanocyte-stimulating hormone) and, in tetrapods, the pars tuberalis around the infundibular stalk. The neurohypophysis comprises the median eminence and the pars nervosa (posterior lobe), where the axons of hypothalamic neurosecretory neurons release vasotocin and its relatives (vasopressin and oxytocin in mammals).

Hypothalamic control of the adenohypophysis takes two forms. In tetrapods and most fishes, releasing hormones are secreted into the capillaries of the median eminence and carried by the hypothalamo–hypophyseal portal veins to the pars distalis (**hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal system**). In teleosts there is no median eminence or portal system; hypothalamic axons enter the pars distalis directly and end on or near its cells. In hagfishes the adenohypophysis is a cluster of cell islands in connective tissue, and in lampreys its parts are only loosely attached to the brain, with control by diffusion.

| | Cyclostomes | Chondrichthyans | Teleosts | Amphibians | Reptiles | Birds | Mammals |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Origin of adenohypophysis | Nasohypophyseal placode (shared with the single nostril) | Rathke's pouch | Solid ingrowth of oral ectoderm | Solid or hollow ingrowth | Rathke's pouch | Rathke's pouch | Rathke's pouch |
| Pars intermedia | Present (lampreys) | Present | Present | Large (colour change) | Present | Absent | Present in most; absent in whales, elephants, armadillos |
| Pars tuberalis | Absent | Absent | Absent | Present | Present | Present | Present |
| Control of pars distalis | Diffusion | Portal and ventral lobe | Direct innervation | Portal system | Portal system | Portal system | Portal system |
| Neurohypophysis | Thin floor of the diencephalon | Neurointermediate lobe | Interdigitating with pars intermedia | Median eminence, pars nervosa | Median eminence, pars nervosa | Median eminence, pars nervosa | Median eminence, posterior lobe |

The pars intermedia is large in amphibians and fishes that change colour by melanocyte-stimulating hormone, and reduced or absent in birds and in some mammals. The homology of the adenohypophysis with Hatschek's pit, a ciliated groove in the roof of the mouth of lancelets, is supported by the expression of *Pitx* and *Six* genes in both, but remains uncertain; tunicates have a neural gland and ciliated duct in a similar position.

### 67.7.2. The Thyroid

The thyroid develops in all vertebrates as an outgrowth of the floor of the pharynx between the first and second pharyngeal pouches. In the larval lamprey (ammocoete) the endostyle, a ciliated, mucus-secreting groove that binds iodine as in lancelets and tunicates, transforms at metamorphosis into scattered thyroid follicles: the clearest evidence that the thyroid is the vertebrate descendant of the endostyle ([7.17.43](/7.17.43); [7.17.45](/7.17.45)). The follicles store the hormone precursor thyroglobulin and release thyroxine and triiodothyronine, which regulate metabolism and development, including amphibian metamorphosis ([7.14.34](/7.14.34#sec-34.5)).

The form of the gland varies: diffuse follicles along the ventral aorta in lampreys and most teleosts (in some teleosts follicles even migrate to the kidney, heart or eye); a compact, unpaired gland in elasmobranchs; paired glands at the sides of the trachea in amphibians, lizards and birds; an unpaired gland in front of the heart in snakes and turtles; and a bilobed gland with an isthmus over the trachea, below the thyroid cartilage, in mammals.

### 67.7.3. Parathyroids and Ultimobranchial Bodies

The parathyroid glands, which raise blood calcium by secreting parathyroid hormone, occur only in tetrapods. They develop from the dorsal parts of pharyngeal pouches 3 and 4 (pouch 3 only in some mammals). Fishes lack parathyroids, but their gills contain cells expressing the same transcription factor (*Gcm2*) and parathyroid-hormone-like peptides, which suggests that the parathyroid evolved from a part of the gill epithelium when the gills were lost (Okabe and Graham 2004). The thymus develops from the ventral parts of the same pouches ([7.17.65](/7.17.65#sec-65.6)).

The ultimobranchial bodies develop from the last pharyngeal pouches and secrete calcitonin, which lowers blood calcium. They are separate glands in gnathostomes from chondrichthyans to birds; in mammals their cells migrate into the thyroid and become the parafollicular (C) cells. Cyclostomes lack ultimobranchial bodies.

### 67.7.4. The Adrenal: Interrenal and Chromaffin Tissue

The mammalian adrenal gland has two parts of different origin: the cortex, derived from mesoderm of the genital ridge region, which secretes corticosteroids, and the medulla, derived from the neural crest, whose chromaffin cells are modified sympathetic postganglionic neurons that secrete adrenaline and noradrenaline into the blood ([7.14.35](/7.14.35#sec-35.1)). In other vertebrates the homologous tissues are called interrenal tissue (steroid-secreting) and chromaffin tissue, and their arrangement is one of the clearest series in comparative endocrinology: from separate tissues to an intermingled gland to a concentric organ.

### 67.7.5. Pancreatic Islets, Pineal and Gonads

The *pancreatic islets* (endocrine pancreas) were described with the pancreas ([7.17.63](/7.17.63#sec-63.9.2)): a separate islet organ in cyclostomes, clusters around the ducts in chondrichthyans, large Brockmann bodies in many teleosts and scattered islets in tetrapods. The *pineal organ* is photoreceptive in fishes, amphibians and reptiles, which have a direct light input to their circadian system ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)); in birds it is partly photosensitive, and in mammals it is a secretory gland innervated by sympathetic nerves that receives light information indirectly from the retina through the suprachiasmatic nucleus. In all vertebrates it secretes melatonin at night, which signals day length and synchronizes daily and seasonal rhythms ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.4.5)). The *gonads* secrete steroids ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)); the placenta of mammals ([7.14.38](/7.14.38)), the gut ([7.14.26](/7.14.26#sec-26.3.3)), the heart (natriuretic peptides) and the kidney (renin, erythropoietin) are also endocrine organs.

### 67.7.6. Glands Peculiar to Fishes

- *Corpuscles of Stannius.* Small bodies on or in the kidneys of holostean and teleost fishes, derived from the archinephric duct, which secrete stanniocalcin, a hormone that lowers blood calcium by reducing calcium uptake through the gills.
- *Urophysis.* The caudal neurosecretory system of teleosts and elasmobranchs: large neurosecretory neurons (Dahlgren cells) at the end of the spinal cord whose axons end in a neurohaemal organ, the urophysis, at the base of the tail. They release urotensins (urotensin I, related to corticotropin-releasing hormone, and urotensin II), involved in osmoregulation and stress responses. It is a spinal counterpart of the hypothalamo-neurohypophyseal system.
- *Head kidney.* The interrenal and chromaffin tissue of teleosts lies in the head kidney, the persistent anterior pronephric region ([7.17.66](/7.17.66#sec-66.2.3)).

## 67.8. Comparison with Traditional Accounts

| Traditional statement | Current view |
|---|---|

| Birds have an enlarged striatum (neostriatum, hyperstriatum) and lack a cortex | Most of the avian forebrain is pallium (hyperpallium, mesopallium, nidopallium, arcopallium); its circuits correspond to those of mammalian cortex (§67.3.5) |

## Summary

- The vertebrate nervous system develops from the neural tube (brain, spinal cord, retina, neurohypophysis), the neural crest (sensory and autonomic ganglia, Schwann cells, chromaffin cells) and the placodes (olfactory and inner-ear epithelia, lateral line, parts of the cranial ganglia, adenohypophysis).
- The spinal cord gains myelin, a clear grey and white matter and mixed spinal nerves in gnathostomes; tetrapods add limb enlargements and plexuses.
- The five brain regions are present in all vertebrates. Their relative sizes follow the senses and behaviour of each lineage: vagal and facial lobes for taste, a huge cerebellar valvula in electric fishes, large optic tecta in visual animals, and an enlarged pallium in birds (nuclear, from the dorsal ventricular ridge) and mammals (six-layered neocortex). Actinopterygians have an everted, other vertebrates an evaginated telencephalon.
- Brain mass scales with body mass with an exponent of about 2/3–3/4; mammals and birds have brains about ten times larger than other vertebrates of the same size, and the encephalization quotient compares species with the allometric expectation (Example 67.4.1).
- The cranial nerves (Definition 67.5.1) are special sensory (0, I, II, VIII, lateral line nerves), somatic motor (III, IV, VI, XII) and branchial (V, VII, IX, X, XI), the latter one for each pharyngeal arch; the twelve nerves of amniotes and ten of anamniotes differ by the separation of XI from X and the incorporation of spino-occipital nerves as XII.
- The autonomic system assembles stepwise: sympathetic chains in gnathostomes, dual innervation of the heart in bony fishes and tetrapods.
- Endocrine glands come from all germ layers. The pituitary combines oral ectoderm and diencephalon, the thyroid descends from the endostyle, the parathyroids (tetrapods only) from pouch tissue related to fish gills, and interrenal and chromaffin tissues change from separate (fishes) to intermingled (amphibians, reptiles, birds) to cortex and medulla (mammals).

## Questions

1. Why is the avian forebrain no longer described as an enlarged "striatum"? What does the current view imply for the evolution of intelligence?

   **Answer** Gene expression (pallial markers such as *Tbr1* and *Emx1* versus subpallial markers such as *Dlx*), connections (thalamic sensory input, projections like those of cortical layers) and neurochemistry show that most of the avian forebrain (hyperpallium, mesopallium, nidopallium, arcopallium) is pallium, homologous as a field with the mammalian pallium; only the basal parts are striatum and pallidum. The old names implied that birds lacked a cortex-like structure. The current view implies that complex cognition in corvids and parrots is built on pallial circuits that are homologous in origin but differ in architecture (nuclei rather than layers) from the mammalian neocortex, so intelligence evolved convergently in the two lineages on a shared pallial foundation.

2. Using Example 67.4.1, calculate the EQ of a $5{,}000\,\mathrm{kg}$ elephant with a $4{,}800\,\mathrm{g}$ brain, and explain why EQ alone is a poor measure of intelligence.

   **Answer** $E_{\mathrm{exp}} = 0.12 \times (5\times 10^{6})^{2/3} = 0.12 \times 2.92\times 10^{4} \approx 3{,}500\,\mathrm{g}$, so $\mathrm{EQ} \approx 4{,}800/3{,}500 \approx 1.4$. EQ depends on the chosen exponent and reference group; it ignores differences in neuron number and density (an elephant brain has fewer cortical neurons than its size suggests, while corvid and primate brains are dense), the proportion of brain devoted to different functions, and differences in body composition (fat or water) that change body mass without changing the demand on the brain.

3. Classify the cranial nerves into special sensory, somatic motor and branchial nerves, and explain why the trigeminal nerve supplies the tensor tympani while the facial nerve supplies the stapedius.

   **Answer** Special sensory: 0, I, II, VIII and the lateral line nerves. Somatic motor: III, IV, VI, XII. Branchial: V, VII, IX, X (with XI). The tensor tympani inserts on the malleus, which is the articular bone, derived from the mandibular arch; its muscle belongs to the mandibular arch and keeps the nerve of that arch, the trigeminal. The stapedius inserts on the stapes, derived from the hyomandibula of the hyoid arch, and is supplied by the nerve of the hyoid arch, the facial.

4. Describe the arrangement of interrenal and chromaffin tissue from sharks to mammals and suggest a functional reason for their association.

   **Answer** In sharks the interrenal body and segmental chromaffin bodies are separate; in teleosts both lie in the head kidney, close together; in amphibians, reptiles and birds they are intermingled in one gland; in mammals the chromaffin tissue forms the medulla surrounded by the interrenal cortex. Glucocorticoids from the cortex flow through the medulla via the adrenal portal circulation and induce the enzyme phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase, which converts noradrenaline to adrenaline; a close association therefore increases adrenaline production, and the two tissues also cooperate in the stress response.

5. What is the evidence that the thyroid of vertebrates derives from the endostyle of chordates, and that the parathyroid derives from gill tissue?

   **Answer** In larval lampreys the endostyle, an iodine-binding groove like that of lancelets and tunicates, transforms at metamorphosis into thyroid follicles; thyroid and endostyle share the expression of thyroid transcription factors (*Nkx2.1*, *Pax2/8*) and the binding of iodine. For the parathyroid, fishes lack the gland but express its key transcription factor *Gcm2* in the gill epithelium together with parathyroid-hormone-like peptides; in tetrapods *Gcm2* is expressed in the parathyroid primordia of the third and fourth pouches; this suggests that the parathyroid is a gill-derived tissue that became a separate gland when gills were lost.

6. Compare the control of the pars distalis by the hypothalamus in teleosts and mammals.

   **Answer** In mammals (and most tetrapods and many fishes) hypothalamic neurons release releasing and inhibiting hormones into the capillaries of the median eminence, and the hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal veins carry them to the capillaries of the pars distalis, so control is neurovascular. In teleosts there is no median eminence or portal system; hypothalamic axons penetrate the pars distalis and release their peptides directly next to the target cells, a neuroglandular control. Both achieve high local concentrations of hypothalamic hormones at the pituitary.
