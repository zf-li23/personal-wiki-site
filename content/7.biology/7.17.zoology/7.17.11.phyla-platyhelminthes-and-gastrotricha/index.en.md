Flatworms are soft, flattened worms without a body cavity, anus, or circulatory and respiratory systems. Free-living flatworms glide over the bottom of streams and seas on a carpet of cilia, and some, the planarians, can regenerate a complete animal from a small fragment. But three-quarters of the roughly 30,000 described species are parasites: flukes and tapeworms that live in the guts, blood vessels, livers and lungs of vertebrates and pass through one or more intermediate hosts. They include some of the most important parasites of humans and livestock, and their life cycles are among the most complex in the animal kingdom.

For a century flatworms were regarded as the simplest bilaterians, intermediate between cnidarians and coelomate animals. Molecular phylogenies have changed this view: flatworms are spiralians, related to annelids and molluscs, and their simple organization is in part secondary ([7.17.10](/7.17.10)). Their closest relatives are the gastrotrichs, microscopic worms with which they form the clade Rouphozoa.

This chapter describes the flatworm body plan (§11.1) and the planarian as representative animal (§11.2), the classification of free-living flatworms (§11.3), the parasitic Neodermata and their adaptations (§11.4), the life cycles of the main parasites (§11.5) and a model of their transmission (§11.6). It ends with the gastrotrichs (§11.7) and a comparison with traditional classifications (§11.8).

## 11.1. The Flatworm Body Plan

**Definition 11.1.1 (Platyhelminthes)** The **Platyhelminthes** (flatworms) are dorsoventrally flattened, acoelomate spiralian worms whose space between the gut and the body wall is filled with mesodermal parenchyma and muscle; they have a gut with a single opening (or none), protonephridia with flame cells, an orthogonal nervous system with an anterior brain, totipotent stem cells (neoblasts) as the only dividing cells, and usually a complex hermaphroditic reproductive system.

### 11.1.1. Acoelomate Organization and Its Consequences

Flatworms are the classic **acoelomates**. Between the epidermis and the gut lies a layer of muscles and a loose tissue of cells and extracellular matrix, the *parenchyma*, in which the reproductive organs, protonephridia and nerves are embedded. Without a coelom or a blood-vascular system, every cell must lie close enough to the surface or the gut to be supplied by diffusion. The flattened shape is the solution: a flatworm 1 cm long may be less than 1 mm thick, and the branching gut of larger species reaches all parts of the body. This geometric constraint, discussed in [7.17.4](/7.17.4), limits free-living flatworms to sizes of millimetres to a few centimetres (the largest terrestrial planarians, which are long ribbons, reach 1 m); parasitic tapeworms, bathed in the nutrients of their host's gut, reach many metres.

The body wall consists of the epidermis, a basement membrane and layers of circular, longitudinal and diagonal muscles. In free-living flatworms the epidermis is ciliated, at least ventrally, and contains gland cells and *rhabdites*, rod-shaped secretory bodies that swell into mucus when discharged. Small flatworms glide on their cilia over a mucous trail, larger ones move by muscular waves.

### 11.1.2. Gut

The mouth lies on the ventral side, often in the middle of the body, and opens into a pharynx. Three types of pharynx are distinguished:

| Pharynx | Structure | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Simple (pharynx simplex) | A short ciliated tube | Catenulids, macrostomids |
| Bulbous | Muscular bulb separated from the parenchyma by a septum | Rhabdocoels, most parasites |
| Plicate | Long, folded muscular tube lying in a pharyngeal pocket and protrusible through the mouth | Polyclads, triclads |

The intestine is a blind sac or a branched tube without an anus: unbranched in small species, three-branched in triclads (one anterior and two posterior branches, hence the name), and many-branched in polyclads. Digestion begins extracellularly in the intestine and is completed intracellularly by phagocytic gut cells. Undigested remains leave by the mouth. Tapeworms have no gut at all.

### 11.1.3. Protonephridia

Flatworms have paired protonephridial systems that run along the sides of the body and branch into fine tubules, each ending in a terminal cell. The **protonephridium** of flatworms ends in a **flame cell**, a terminal cell with a tuft of cilia whose beating, seen as a flickering flame under the microscope, drives fluid into the tubule. The wall of the terminal cell and the first tubule cell interdigitate to form a weir, a sieve-like filtration barrier through which fluid is drawn from the parenchyma. Along the tubule, cells reabsorb useful solutes and water. Protonephridia are mainly osmoregulatory organs: they are best developed in freshwater species, which must remove water entering by osmosis, and reduced or absent in many marine forms. Most nitrogenous waste (ammonia) diffuses out across the body surface. The general principle of filtration followed by modification of the filtrate is introduced in [7.17.4](/7.17.4).

### 11.1.4. Nervous System and Sense Organs

The nervous system has a bilobed anterior brain (cerebral ganglion) and several pairs of longitudinal nerve cords linked by transverse commissures, a pattern called an orthogon. In triclads the two ventral cords dominate and, with their commissures, form the **ladder-like nervous system** ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)). Sense organs include pigment-cup ocelli, in which a cup of pigment cells shades rhabdomeric photoreceptor cells from one side so that the animal can detect the direction of light; statocysts in some species; and chemoreceptive and mechanoreceptive ciliated cells, concentrated in planarians on the lateral auricles of the head.

### 11.1.5. Reproduction

Most flatworms are simultaneous hermaphrodites with internal fertilization and elaborate copulatory organs. The reproductive system of a planarian is typical:

- Male system: numerous testes in the parenchyma, sperm ducts (vasa deferentia) leading to a seminal vesicle and a muscular penis in the genital atrium, which opens through a ventral gonopore.
- Female system: a pair of ovaries (germaria) producing eggs without yolk, oviducts receiving the products of numerous *vitellaria* (yolk glands), and a copulatory bursa that receives the partner's sperm.

Copulation is usually reciprocal. The eggs and yolk cells are packaged together in a capsule, the cocoon, which is laid in the water. Many free-living flatworms also reproduce asexually by transverse fission: a planarian holds its tail to the substrate and pulls itself apart, and each half regenerates the missing part.

The separation of yolk from the egg is a key character in flatworm classification. In the *archoophoran* condition (Catenulida, Macrostomorpha, Polycladida), the egg contains its own yolk (endolecithal), and the polyclads have quartet spiral cleavage like other spiralians, with a planktonic Müller's larva bearing eight ciliated lobes. In the *neoophoran* condition (most other flatworms, including all parasites), the yolk is supplied by separate vitelline cells (ectolecithal), and cleavage is so modified that the spiral pattern is no longer recognizable.

### 11.1.6. Neoblasts

Flatworms have a unique cell biology: their differentiated cells, including epidermal cells, never divide. The only proliferating cells are the **neoblasts**, small undifferentiated cells in the parenchyma, which replace all other cells throughout life ([7.5.35](/7.5.35#sec-35.3.2)). Single neoblasts transplanted into irradiated planarians, which have lost their own neoblasts, can restore the whole animal, so at least some neoblasts are pluripotent. Neoblasts make planarians extraordinary regenerators and probably also explain why the parasitic flatworms can produce such enormous numbers of eggs: the germ cells of tapeworms and the asexual multiplication of larval flukes depend on neoblast-like stem cells.

## 11.2. The Planarian: A Representative Flatworm

Freshwater planarians (Tricladida: Continenticola) are the representative flatworms of most textbooks. *Dugesia japonica*, the common planarian of streams in East Asia, and *Schmidtea mediterranea*, the laboratory model of regeneration, are 1–2 cm long, dark brown or black above and paler below. Planarians are carnivores and scavengers. They detect food chemically with the auricles, glide onto it, protrude the pharynx through the mouth, and suck in tissue fragments with peristaltic pumping. They avoid light (negative phototaxis) and are most active at night.

Their regeneration has been studied since the experiments of Morgan around 1900. A fragment as small as 1/279 of the body can regenerate a whole worm. A cut surface first closes by muscle contraction and epidermal spreading; neoblasts then proliferate and form a blastema, and the polarity of the new tissue (whether a head or a tail forms) is set by Wnt/β-catenin signalling. The molecular mechanisms are described in [7.5.35](/7.5.35#sec-35.3).

## 11.3. Classification of Free-Living Flatworms

Traditional zoology divided the flatworms into three classes: the free-living Turbellaria and the parasitic Trematoda and Cestoda (with the Monogenea sometimes separated). Molecular and morphological analyses show that the parasitic groups form a clade, the Neodermata, that arose from within the free-living flatworms.

**Definition 11.3.1 (Turbellaria)** The **Turbellaria** (turbellarians) are the free-living flatworms, with a ciliated epidermis throughout life; the group is paraphyletic, because it excludes the Neodermata, which evolved from free-living ancestors.

The main lineages, from the base of the tree upward, are:

| Lineage | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Catenulida | Small, freshwater; simple pharynx; one median protonephridium; often reproduce by budding into chains (catenulid = "small chain"); sperm without flagella | *Catenula*, *Stenostomum* |
| Rhabditophora | All other flatworms; rhabdites; lamellated rhabdites and biflagellate sperm; a duo-gland adhesive system | |
| Macrostomorpha | Small, marine and freshwater; simple pharynx; archoophoran | *Macrostomum* (a model of sexual selection), *Microstomum* |
| Polycladida | Marine, often large (up to 15 cm) and brightly coloured; many-branched gut, plicate pharynx; spiral cleavage, Müller's larva | *Planocera*, *Pseudoceros* |
| Prorhynchida, Gnosonesimida | Small groups with intermediate characters | |
| Rhabdocoela | Bulbous pharynx; sac-like gut; diverse; some symbiotic | *Mesostoma* |
| Proseriata | Marine interstitial; elongate | *Monocelis* |
| Tricladida | Three-branched gut; plicate pharynx; marine (Maricola), freshwater and terrestrial (Continenticola) | *Dugesia*, *Schmidtea*, *Bipalium* (land planarians) |
| Fecampiida, Prolecithophora, Bothrioplanida | Smaller groups; fecampiids parasitic in crustaceans | |
| Neodermata | Parasites with a syncytial tegument (§11.4) | Flukes, monogeneans, tapeworms |

The closest free-living relatives of the Neodermata are, on recent phylogenomic analyses, the freshwater Bothrioplanida (Laumer et al. 2015). Land planarians of the genus *Bipalium*, with a hammer-shaped head, and the New Guinea flatworm *Platydemus manokwari* have been spread by the horticultural trade and are invasive predators of earthworms and snails on several continents.

## 11.4. The Neodermata: Parasitic Flatworms

**Definition 11.4.1 (Neodermata)** The **Neodermata** are the clade of parasitic flatworms (Trematoda, Monogenea and Cestoda) in which the ciliated larval epidermis is shed at or soon after infection and replaced by a new syncytial body covering, the tegument (neodermis).

### 11.4.1. The Tegument

**Definition 11.4.2 (Tegument)** The **tegument** (neodermis) of parasitic flatworms is a syncytial, non-ciliated body covering whose outer cytoplasmic layer, without nuclei, lies on a basement membrane and is connected by cytoplasmic bridges to cell bodies (cytons) containing the nuclei, which lie sunk beneath the muscle layers in the parenchyma.

The tegument is the interface between parasite and host. Its outer membrane is covered by a glycocalyx and is continually renewed from vesicles produced in the cytons, which protects the worm against antibodies and complement. In schistosomes the outer surface is a double membrane that acquires host molecules, including blood-group antigens and MHC proteins, which further disguises the worm. In tapeworms, which lack a gut, the tegument is the absorptive surface: it bears *microtriches*, microvilli-like projections with electron-dense tips that increase the surface area many times and also anchor the worm against the flow of intestinal contents. Glucose and amino acids are taken up by active transport across the tegument, and the tapeworm also secretes inhibitors of the host's digestive enzymes.

### 11.4.2. Adaptations to Parasitism

The neodermatans illustrate the principal adaptations of parasites:

- Attachment organs: oral and ventral suckers (acetabulum) in flukes; a posterior haptor with hooks and clamps in monogeneans; a scolex with suckers, grooves (bothria) or hooks in tapeworms.
- Reduction of organs used by free-living animals: loss of cilia in the adult, reduction of sense organs (eyes are present only in larvae), loss of the gut in tapeworms.
- Anaerobic metabolism: worms in the gut and bile ducts, where oxygen is scarce, obtain energy by fermenting glucose, often producing succinate and propionate through a modified mitochondrial pathway.
- Enormous fecundity: a gravid proglottid of the pork tapeworm contains 30,000–50,000 eggs, and several proglottids are shed every day; a female schistosome produces 300–3,000 eggs per day. Very few eggs reach a new host, and high fecundity compensates.
- Asexual multiplication in the larval stages: one miracidium of a digenean can give rise to thousands of cercariae, and a hydatid cyst of *Echinococcus* contains thousands of protoscoleces.
- Complex life cycles with intermediate hosts (below), which are thought to have evolved by the addition of new hosts to simpler cycles, as in the trophic transmission of tapeworms.

**Definition 11.4.3 (Intermediate Host and Definitive Host)** The host in which a parasite reaches sexual maturity and reproduces sexually is the **definitive host**; a host in which the parasite lives only as a larva, or reproduces only asexually, is an **intermediate host**. When there are two intermediate hosts, they are distinguished as first and second intermediate hosts.

### 11.4.3. Class Trematoda: Flukes

**Definition 11.4.4 (Trematoda)** The **Trematoda** (flukes) are endoparasitic neodermatans with an oral sucker and usually a ventral sucker, a mouth and a forked blind gut; they comprise the Aspidogastrea, with a single large ventral sucker divided into compartments, and the Digenea, with a life cycle that alternates sexual adults in a vertebrate with asexually multiplying larvae in a mollusc.

The Digenea are by far the larger group (over 12,000 species). Almost all digenean life cycles start in a mollusc, usually a snail, which suggests that molluscs were the original hosts and vertebrates were added later. The Aspidogastrea (about 80 species) are parasites of molluscs and cold-blooded vertebrates, often with a single host, and are the sister group of the Digenea.

The larval stages of a digenean occur in a fixed sequence:

**Definition 11.4.5 (Miracidium)** The **miracidium** is the first larva of a digenean fluke: a ciliated, free-swimming or egg-enclosed larva that hatches from the egg and infects the first intermediate host, a mollusc, where it transforms into a sporocyst.

Inside the snail, the larvae multiply asexually from germ cells: the *sporocyst*, a sac without a gut, produces either daughter sporocysts or *rediae*, larvae with a pharynx and gut, which in turn produce the next larval stage.

**Definition 11.4.6 (Cercaria)** The **cercaria** is the larva of a digenean fluke produced asexually within the mollusc host: a free-swimming larva, usually with a muscular tail, suckers and penetration glands, which leaves the snail and either encysts or penetrates the next host.

**Definition 11.4.7 (Metacercaria)** The **metacercaria** is the encysted, resting stage of a digenean fluke, formed when a cercaria loses its tail and secretes a cyst wall on vegetation or within the tissues of a second intermediate host; it develops into an adult when eaten by the definitive host.

### 11.4.4. Monogenea

The Monogenea (several thousand species) are mostly ectoparasites of the gills and skin of fishes, with a few on amphibians and turtles. They have a single host and a direct life cycle: an egg hatches into a ciliated *oncomiracidium*, which swims to a new host. They attach with an anterior adhesive organ and a posterior *haptor* armed with hooks or clamps. *Gyrodactylus*, a skin parasite of salmon, is viviparous: each worm contains a developing embryo, which already contains the next embryo, like Russian dolls, and populations grow explosively; introduced *G. salaris* devastated wild salmon in Norwegian rivers. *Dactylogyrus* species infest the gills of carp in aquaculture. Molecular analyses suggest that the Monogenea may be paraphyletic, with the two subgroups (Monopisthocotylea and Polyopisthocotylea) not each other's closest relatives; their relationships to Trematoda and Cestoda are unresolved.

### 11.4.5. Class Cestoda: Tapeworms

**Definition 11.4.8 (Cestoda)** The **Cestoda** (tapeworms) are endoparasitic neodermatans without a gut, which absorb nutrients through a tegument bearing microtriches; most have an anterior attachment organ (scolex) and a body divided into a chain of reproductive units (proglottids), and their larvae (with six hooks, or ten in the Gyrocotylidea) develop in one or more intermediate hosts.

**Definition 11.4.9 (Scolex and Proglottid)** The **scolex** is the anterior attachment organ of a tapeworm, bearing suckers, grooves (bothria) or hooks; behind it a neck region continually produces **proglottids**, the serially repeated units of the tapeworm body, each with a complete set of male and female reproductive organs.

The body of a tapeworm behind the scolex is the *strobila*. New proglottids form at the neck, so the youngest are at the front; as they move back they mature (immature → mature → gravid proglottids), and the gravid ones, filled with a branched uterus packed with eggs, detach and leave the host with the faeces. The proglottids are not segments in the sense of annelids or arthropods: they are repeated reproductive units formed by budding, and the "segmentation" of tapeworms evolved independently.

The eggs contain a larva with six hooks, the *oncosphere* (hexacanth). When eaten by an intermediate host, the oncosphere hatches, bores through the gut wall and develops into a larval stage characteristic of the group:

| Larva | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Procercoid and plerocercoid | Solid elongate larvae in a copepod and then a fish | *Dibothriocephalus* (broad fish tapeworm) |
| Cysticercoid | Small, solid, with an invaginated scolex | *Hymenolepis* in insects |
| Cysticercus | A fluid-filled bladder with one invaginated scolex | *Taenia* in pig or cattle muscle |
| Coenurus | A bladder with many scoleces | *Taenia multiceps* in sheep brain |
| Hydatid cyst | A large, often multichambered cyst whose germinal layer produces brood capsules with thousands of protoscoleces | *Echinococcus* |

**Definition 11.4.10 (Cysticercus)** A **cysticercus** (bladder worm) is the larva of taeniid tapeworms in the tissues of the intermediate host: a fluid-filled bladder containing a single invaginated scolex, which everts and attaches when the bladder is eaten by the definitive host.

The cestodes include the Gyrocotylidea and Amphilinidea (Cestodaria), unsegmented worms with a single set of reproductive organs, and the Eucestoda, the true tapeworms, which include the orders Diphyllobothriidea (with bothria), Cyclophyllidea (with four suckers and often hooks; includes *Taenia*, *Echinococcus*, *Hymenolepis*) and many others in sharks and rays.

## 11.5. Life Cycles of Important Parasites

The life cycles below are those of the main flatworm parasites of humans in China. They are described as biology: the sequence of hosts and stages, the route of transmission, and the adaptations of each stage. Each life cycle shows the same logic: a sexually reproducing adult in a vertebrate that disperses eggs, and larval stages that find, multiply in and leave intermediate hosts, so that the parasite eventually returns to a definitive host, often because that host eats an intermediate host.

### 11.5.1. *Clonorchis sinensis*: The Chinese Liver Fluke

*Clonorchis sinensis* is a digenean 10–25 mm long that lives in the bile ducts of humans, cats, dogs and pigs in East Asia. Its name refers to the branched testes, which lie one behind the other in the posterior part of the body.

1. Eggs containing a fully formed miracidium pass with the bile into the intestine and leave with the faeces. They do not hatch in water.
2. The eggs are eaten by freshwater snails (*Parafossarulus*, *Bithynia*, *Alocinma*), the first intermediate hosts. The miracidium hatches in the snail's gut and transforms into a sporocyst, which produces rediae, which produce cercariae.
3. The cercariae, with a long tail, leave the snail, swim, and penetrate the skin of freshwater fishes, mostly cyprinids, the second intermediate hosts; they encyst in the muscles as metacercariae.
4. When fish is eaten raw or undercooked, the metacercariae excyst in the duodenum and the young flukes migrate up the bile duct, where they mature in about a month and can live for decades.

Transmission therefore depends on human or animal faeces reaching ponds with snails, on fish farming in the same ponds, and on the custom of eating raw fish. *Opisthorchis viverrini* in Southeast Asia has a similar cycle.

### 11.5.2. *Schistosoma japonicum*: The Oriental Blood Fluke

Schistosomes differ from other digeneans in several ways. They live in blood vessels, and the sexes are separate: the female, long and slender, lies in the gynaecophoric canal, a groove formed by the folded body of the shorter, broader male, and the pair remains together for life. There is no redia, no second intermediate host and no metacercaria: the cercariae penetrate the skin of the definitive host directly.

1. Adult pairs live in the veins of the mesentery and the hepatic portal system. The female lays eggs in the small venules of the intestinal wall; the egg, which lacks a prominent spine, secretes enzymes that help it pass through the gut wall into the lumen. Eggs that are carried by the bloodstream to the liver instead become trapped there.
2. Eggs that reach fresh water hatch into miracidia, which swim and penetrate the amphibious snail *Oncomelania hupensis*, the only intermediate host.
3. In the snail, a mother sporocyst produces daughter sporocysts, which produce thousands of cercariae with a forked tail over the life of the snail.
4. Cercariae leave the snail, swim at the water surface and penetrate the skin of a mammal in contact with the water, losing their tails; the resulting *schistosomulum* migrates through the bloodstream, the lungs and the liver to the portal veins, where males and females pair and mature.

*S. japonicum* infects more than 40 species of mammals, and water buffalo and cattle are the most important reservoir hosts in China. The other main species of humans, *S. mansoni* in Africa and South America (snail *Biomphalaria*) and *S. haematobium* in Africa (snail *Bulinus*, adults in the veins of the bladder), have similar cycles.

Schistosomiasis was among the most important parasitic diseases in China in the 1950s, with about 10 million infected people in the Yangtze basin. Because the parasite depends on a single species of snail with restricted habitat (marshlands and irrigation ditches that flood seasonally), its transmission can be interrupted by measures at several points of the cycle: modifying snail habitats (lining ditches with concrete, draining or ploughing marshland), preventing faeces of people and livestock from reaching water (sanitation, replacing water buffalo with machinery), and treating infected people and animals with the drug praziquantel. These measures have reduced the number of infections by more than 99%, and transmission has been interrupted in most formerly endemic counties.

### 11.5.3. *Fasciolopsis buski*: The Giant Intestinal Fluke

*Fasciolopsis buski*, up to 7.5 cm long, lives attached to the wall of the small intestine of pigs and humans. Its eggs hatch in water, the miracidia infect planorbid snails (*Segmentina*, *Hippeutis*), and after sporocyst and two generations of rediae the cercariae leave the snail and encyst as metacercariae on the surface of aquatic plants such as water caltrop and water chestnut. Pigs and people are infected when they eat these plants raw or peel them with their teeth. The related liver fluke *Fasciola hepatica* of sheep and cattle has the same type of cycle with lymnaeid snails and metacercariae on grass.

### 11.5.4. *Taenia*: Pork and Beef Tapeworms

The pork tapeworm *Taenia solium* (2–4 m long, about 1,000 proglottids) and the beef tapeworm *T. saginata* (4–10 m, over 1,000 proglottids) live in the small intestine of humans, their only definitive host. Their scoleces differ: *T. solium* has four suckers and a rostellum with two rows of hooks (an "armed" scolex), *T. saginata* four suckers and no hooks.

1. Gravid proglottids, each containing tens of thousands of eggs, detach and pass with the faeces (those of *T. saginata* can crawl out actively).
2. Eggs eaten by the intermediate host, the pig for *T. solium* and cattle for *T. saginata*, hatch in the intestine; the oncospheres penetrate the gut wall, travel in the blood and lodge in muscle, where each develops in about two months into a cysticercus.
3. When the definitive host eats raw or undercooked meat, the scolex of the cysticercus evaginates, attaches to the intestinal wall, and the strobila begins to grow.

*T. solium* differs in that humans can also act as its intermediate host: if a person swallows its eggs, cysticerci develop in the person's tissues, including the brain. The adult tapeworm causes few symptoms, but larvae in the wrong host are much more harmful, a general rule in parasitology.

### 11.5.5. *Echinococcus granulosus*: The Hydatid Tapeworm

*Echinococcus granulosus* is a tiny tapeworm, 3–6 mm long with only three or four proglottids, which lives in large numbers in the intestine of dogs and other canids. Its eggs, released with the dog's faeces, are eaten by herbivores (sheep, cattle, yaks, camels) and by people. The oncosphere develops in the liver or lungs into a hydatid cyst, a slowly growing bladder that may reach the size of an orange. Its inner germinal layer buds off brood capsules, each with many protoscoleces, so one egg gives rise to thousands of potential adult worms. When a dog eats the infected organs of a slaughtered sheep, each protoscolex develops into an adult. The cycle is maintained by the feeding of offal to dogs, and hydatid disease is common in the pastoral regions of western China. The related *E. multilocularis*, whose larva grows invasively like a tumour, cycles between foxes and rodents.

## 11.6. A Model of Transmission: Schistosome Breakpoints

The life cycle of a parasite can be turned into a model that predicts how the parasite population responds to control. For macroparasites such as worms, which do not multiply within the definitive host, the natural variable is the *mean worm burden* $M$, the average number of adult worms per host. Macdonald (1965) proposed the first such model for schistosomes; the version below follows the analysis of May (1977) and Anderson and May (1991).

Each adult worm dies at rate $\mu$ (schistosomes live 3–5 years, so $\mu \approx 0.2$–$0.3\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$). New worms are acquired at a rate proportional to the number of fertilized eggs released into the environment, which depends on three factors:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mu M \left[ R_0\, \Phi(M, k)\, f(M, k, z) - 1 \right]. \tag{11.1}
$$

- $R_0$ is the **basic reproduction number** of the parasite: the average number of adult worms produced by one adult worm in a population where no density effects operate ([7.11.35](/7.11.35#sec-35.3.1)). It combines worm fecundity, egg survival, snail infection, cercarial output and the contact of hosts with water.
- $\Phi(M, k)$ is the probability that a female worm is mated, which matters because schistosomes have separate sexes. Worms are not spread evenly among hosts: most hosts carry few worms and a few carry many, a pattern well described by the negative binomial distribution with *aggregation parameter* $k$ (smaller $k$, more aggregation; values of 0.1–0.5 are typical). With a 1:1 sex ratio and polygamous mating, May (1977) showed that

  $$
  \Phi(M, k) = 1 - \frac{(1-\alpha)^{1+k}}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{1 - \cos\theta}{(1 + \alpha\cos\theta)^{1+k}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta, \qquad \alpha = \frac{M}{M + k}.
  $$

  At low $M$, most worms are alone in their host, $\Phi$ falls toward zero, and reproduction fails.
- $f(M, k, z) = \left[1 + M(1 - z)/k\right]^{-(k+1)}$ is the mean reduction in egg output per worm due to crowding, where $z = \mathrm{e}^{-\gamma}$ measures the strength of density-dependent fecundity per worm.

Setting the bracket in Eq. (11.1) to zero gives the equilibria. Besides $M = 0$, there are two (see the figure): a stable endemic equilibrium $M^*$, where density dependence balances transmission, and an unstable breakpoint $M_{\mathrm{b}}$, below which the mating probability is too low for worms to replace themselves. If control measures reduce the mean worm burden below the breakpoint, the parasite population declines to extinction on its own, even if transmission continues.

Where a curve crosses 1 (dashed), the population is at equilibrium: the left crossing is the unstable breakpoint (open circle), the right crossing the stable endemic equilibrium (filled circle). The model has practical and biological implications. The breakpoint is very low for realistic parameters, typically well below one worm per host, and it moves even lower when worms are strongly aggregated (small $k$), because aggregation places male and female worms together in heavily infected hosts. Control therefore rarely eliminates schistosomes by reducing worm burdens alone, and in practice elimination in China required reducing $R_0$ itself, by snail habitat modification, sanitation and the treatment of reservoir animals. At the same time, the model explains why the parasite persists so stubbornly: the endemic equilibrium is stable, and a population knocked down by treatment alone returns to it, over years to decades, unless transmission is also reduced.

## 11.7. Phylum Gastrotricha

**Definition 11.7.1 (Gastrotricha)** The **Gastrotricha** are microscopic, aquatic, acoelomate worms with a ventral ciliated sole, a cuticle that covers even the cilia and is not moulted, paired adhesive tubes, a muscular sucking pharynx with a triradiate lumen, a through-gut, and protonephridia.

Gastrotrichs ("hairy bellies") are 0.06–3.5 mm long and live between sand grains in the sea (interstitial meiofauna) and on submerged vegetation and in the bottom detritus of fresh waters. There are about 860 species in two orders:

| Order | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Macrodasyida | Marine and brackish, interstitial; elongate, with many adhesive tubes along the sides; simultaneous hermaphrodites; pharynx with pores |
| Chaetonotida | Mostly freshwater; bowling-pin-shaped, with a forked tail bearing adhesive tubes and a cuticle covered with scales and spines; many freshwater species reproduce only parthenogenetically |

The body has a constant number of cells in many species. Gastrotrichs swim or glide on the ventral cilia and attach temporarily with the adhesive tubes, each of which contains a duo-gland system: one gland cell secretes the adhesive, the other a releasing substance, much as in the adhesive organs of flatworms. Food — bacteria, diatoms and detritus — is sucked in by the muscular pharynx, whose lumen is Y-shaped in cross-section like that of nematodes, a resemblance that once led to their classification among the "Aschelminthes". The life cycle is direct and short, often only a few days; freshwater chaetonotids lay two kinds of eggs, thin-shelled eggs that develop immediately and thick-shelled resting eggs that survive drying and freezing.

Flatworms and gastrotrichs together form the clade *Rouphozoa* (from the Greek for "to gulp", referring to the sucking pharynx; Struck et al. 2014). Molecular phylogenies support it consistently, though morphological synapomorphies are few; the duo-gland adhesive systems and the presence of a muscular pharynx have been suggested. Rouphozoa is part of the Platytrochozoa within the Spiralia, as the sister group of the Lophotrochozoa (annelids, molluscs, nemerteans and lophophorates).

## 11.8. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Flatworms as the most primitive bilaterians, ancestral to coelomates | Spiralians related to annelids and molluscs; their acoelomate condition is probably secondary |
| Class Turbellaria with orders Acoela, Macrostomida, Polycladida, Tricladida, etc. | Acoela belong to the Xenacoelomorpha ([7.17.10](/7.17.10)); "Turbellaria" is paraphyletic; Catenulida and Rhabditophora are the basal division |
| Class Trematoda with subclasses Monogenea, Aspidogastrea, Digenea | Monogenea a separate class (perhaps paraphyletic); Trematoda = Aspidogastrea + Digenea; all three in the clade Neodermata |
| Class Cestoda (Cestoidea) with Cestodaria and Eucestoda | Retained as a clade within Neodermata |
| Gastrotricha among the pseudocoelomates ("Aschelminthes") | Acoelomate spiralians, the sister group of Platyhelminthes (Rouphozoa) |
| Proglottids as a form of segmentation | Repeated reproductive units produced by budding, not homologous with segments |

The representative species of the Chinese textbooks (*Dugesia japonica*, *Clonorchis sinensis*, *Taenia solium*) and the parasites listed there (*Schistosoma japonicum*, *Fasciolopsis buski*, *Fasciola hepatica*, *Paragonimus*, *Taenia saginata*, *Echinococcus granulosus*, *Gyrodactylus*, *Dactylogyrus*) are treated in the sections above.

## Summary

- Flatworms (Definition 11.1.1) are flattened acoelomate spiralians with parenchyma, a gut with one opening (or none), protonephridia with flame cells, an orthogonal nervous system, neoblasts as the only dividing cells, and complex hermaphroditic reproduction; flatness keeps all cells within diffusion distance.
- Archoophoran flatworms (catenulids, macrostomids, polyclads) have yolky eggs and spiral cleavage; neoophorans supply yolk from vitellaria.
- The planarian has a plicate pharynx, a three-branched gut, lateral protonephridial canals, a brain with two ventral nerve cords and commissures, and regenerates from neoblasts.
- "Turbellaria" (Definition 11.3.1) is paraphyletic: Catenulida and Rhabditophora, the latter including polyclads, triclads, rhabdocoels and the parasitic Neodermata.
- Neodermata (Definition 11.4.1) replace the larval epidermis with a syncytial tegument (Definition 11.4.2); parasites show attachment organs, reduction, anaerobic metabolism, high fecundity, larval multiplication and complex life cycles with definitive and intermediate hosts (Definition 11.4.3).
- Trematoda (Definition 11.4.4): Aspidogastrea and Digenea; digenean stages are egg → miracidium (Definition 11.4.5) → sporocyst → redia → cercaria (Definition 11.4.6) → metacercaria (Definition 11.4.7) → adult. Monogenea are mostly fish ectoparasites with direct cycles.
- Cestoda (Definition 11.4.8): no gut, microtriches, scolex and proglottids (Definition 11.4.9), oncosphere larvae developing into procercoids, cysticercoids, cysticerci (Definition 11.4.10) or hydatid cysts.
- *Clonorchis*: snail → fish → raw fish; *Schistosoma japonicum*: separate sexes in portal veins, snail *Oncomelania*, skin-penetrating cercariae; *Fasciolopsis*: metacercariae on water plants; *Taenia*: pig or cattle cysticerci; *Echinococcus*: dogs and herbivores, hydatid cysts.
- The Macdonald–May model, Eq. (11.1), has a stable endemic equilibrium and an unstable breakpoint caused by the need for worms to mate; aggregation lowers the breakpoint, so elimination usually requires reducing $R_0$.
- Gastrotrichs (Definition 11.7.1) are microscopic interstitial and freshwater worms with adhesive tubes and a triradiate pharynx; with flatworms they form Rouphozoa.

## Questions

1. Why are free-living flatworms flat, and why can tapeworms nonetheless grow to several metres?

   **Answer** Flatworms have no coelom, blood vessels or respiratory organs, so oxygen reaches their cells only by diffusion from the surface, and nutrients by diffusion from the gut. Diffusion is effective only over distances of a fraction of a millimetre, so the body must be thin in at least one dimension; a flat ribbon can grow in length and width while staying thin. A tapeworm is also thin and ribbon-like, and it is bathed along its whole length in the nutrient-rich contents of the host's gut, which it absorbs directly through its tegument; each proglottid is supplied locally, so total length is not limited by internal transport.
2. Compare the life cycles of *Clonorchis sinensis* and *Schistosoma japonicum*. Which stages does the schistosome lack, and how is the definitive host infected in each case?

   **Answer** Both begin with eggs in faeces and pass through a snail in which sporocysts multiply asexually to produce cercariae. *Clonorchis* has rediae in the snail, a second intermediate host (a fish) in which the cercaria encysts as a metacercaria, and infection of the definitive host by eating raw fish. *Schistosoma* lacks the redia, the second intermediate host and the metacercaria: its fork-tailed cercariae penetrate the skin of the definitive host directly in water. Schistosomes also differ in having separate sexes and living in blood vessels rather than bile ducts.
3. In the Macdonald–May model, why does the breakpoint exist, and why does stronger aggregation of worms among hosts make elimination harder?

   **Answer** Schistosomes are dioecious, so a female worm reproduces only if a male is present in the same host. At low mean worm burdens most worms are alone in their host, the mating probability $\Phi$ falls toward zero, and $R_0\Phi f < 1$: the population declines. The burden at which $R_0\Phi f = 1$ on the rising part of the curve is the breakpoint. When worms are aggregated (small $k$), the few heavily infected hosts contain both sexes even when the mean burden is low, so $\Phi$ stays high at low $M$ and the breakpoint moves toward zero; treatment must then reduce burdens almost to zero, which is impractical, and reducing $R_0$ itself becomes necessary.
4. In what sense are the proglottids of a tapeworm not segments?

   **Answer** Segments of annelids and arthropods are units of the body plan formed during development, containing repeated parts of the muscles, nervous system, excretory organs and body cavity, and they are retained throughout life. Proglottids are reproductive units budded continuously from the neck behind the scolex, each containing mainly a set of reproductive organs; they mature, fill with eggs and detach. They arise by a different process, in a lineage nested among unsegmented flatworms, and are therefore convergent with, not homologous to, segments.
5. *Taenia solium* causes more harm as a larva in humans than as an adult. Suggest an evolutionary explanation.

   **Answer** The adult tapeworm lives in its normal definitive host, the human gut, where natural selection has favoured a parasite that absorbs nutrients and releases eggs without seriously harming the host on which its own survival depends. Humans are not the normal intermediate host: the cysticercus is adapted to the muscles of pigs, which are eaten by humans and so complete the cycle. In a human, the larva is in a dead-end host; there has been no selection to limit its effects in human tissues such as the brain, and the host's reaction to the dying larva causes much of the damage. Parasites in accidental or dead-end hosts are often more pathogenic than in their usual hosts.
