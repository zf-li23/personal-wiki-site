A zoologist meeting an unfamiliar animal asks a short list of questions: does it have a front and a back, how many embryonic layers form its body, is there a cavity between the gut and the body wall, and is the body divided into repeated segments? The answers describe its body plan, and for more than a century they were the basis of animal classification. Chinese and Western textbooks still arrange the phyla in a sequence of increasing complexity, from asymmetrical sponges through radial diploblasts to acoelomate, pseudocoelomate and coelomate bilaterians. This chapter defines these characters precisely, explains what each contributes to the working of the body, and then compares the traditional grades with the animal tree of molecular phylogenetics ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)), which shows that several of them evolved more than once. The developmental mechanisms behind germ layers, axes and segments were treated in developmental biology ([7.5.9](/7.5.9), [7.5.15](/7.5.15), [7.5.23](/7.5.23)); the next chapter ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)) continues with size, support, movement and the organ systems, and [7.17.5](/7.17.5) with the modes of development.

## 3.1. Body Plans and Grades

The idea that each major group of animals has a characteristic organization goes back to Cuvier's four *embranchements* ([7.17.1](/7.17.1#sec-1.3.2)). German morphologists called it the *Bauplan*.

**Definition 3.1.1 (Body Plan)** The **body plan** of a group of animals is the set of structural features shared by its members that define their overall organization: symmetry and body axes, number of germ layers, type of body cavity, presence and kind of segmentation, the arrangement of the gut, nervous system and appendages, and the type of skeleton. It is usually established early in development and conserved through the group's evolution.

The body plan is an abstraction: no species shows it in pure form, and many species have modified it (sessile barnacles are arthropods without obvious segments in the adult, tapeworms are flatworms without a gut). It is nonetheless useful because the features of a body plan are correlated: a coelom divided by septa allows a segmented worm to burrow by peristalsis, a hard jointed exoskeleton requires molting, and the phylotypic stage at which the members of a phylum look most alike is when their body plan is laid down ([7.5.39](/7.5.39#sec-39.1.2)).

**Definition 3.1.2 (Grade)** A **grade** (evolutionary grade) is a group of organisms that share a level of organization, whether or not they share a common ancestry that excludes other organisms. A grade may coincide with a clade, but it is often a paraphyletic group (such as invertebrates or "reptiles") or a polyphyletic group (such as acoelomate animals).

Grades describe how complex an organism is; clades describe how organisms are related. The traditional classification of animals by symmetry, germ layers and body cavities is a classification by grades. It remains a valuable descriptive vocabulary, and the terms are used throughout this course, but since grades can be reached independently by different lineages, they are not evidence of relationship until they are mapped onto a phylogeny (Section 3.6).

Each feature of a body plan can be examined in three ways, and this course asks all three questions of every structure. The first is structural: what the feature is, in which tissues, and how it differs among species. The second is functional: what the feature does for the living animal, which is the domain of physiology and biomechanics ([7.14](/7.14)). The third is historical: where the feature came from, whether it is inherited from a common ancestor (homology) or arose separately (**homoplasy**), and by what change of development ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)). A coelom, for example, is a cavity lined by mesoderm (structure), works as a hydrostatic skeleton and a space for organs (function), and arose in different ways in different lineages (history). The three answers constrain one another: a functional explanation suggests why a feature might have evolved more than once, and a phylogeny tests whether it did.

The diversity of body plans is limited. Living animals are divided into some 30–35 phyla, the number depending on whether groups such as acanthocephalans, sipunculans or pentastomids are ranked as phyla or placed within others ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)). Almost every phylum with a fossil record is known from the Cambrian, and no new phylum-level body plan has appeared since; the bryozoans, which appear unambiguously only in the Early Ordovician, are the main exception in the record. Two explanations, not exclusive, are offered: the developmental programs that lay down a body plan became entrenched, so that later changes act on details rather than on the plan itself (the phylotypic stage, [7.5.39](/7.5.39#sec-39.1.2)), and the ecological space for new kinds of animals was filled early, so that later innovations arose within existing lineages.

## 3.2. Symmetry

### 3.2.1. Types of Symmetry

Symmetry describes how the parts of a body are arranged around a center, an axis or a plane, so that the body can be divided into similar halves.

**Definition 3.2.1 (Radial Symmetry, Bilateral Symmetry, Pentaradial Symmetry)** A body has **radial symmetry** when its parts are arranged around a central oral–aboral axis so that any of several planes through that axis divides it into similar halves. It has **bilateral symmetry** when only one plane, the median (sagittal) plane, divides it into mirror-image halves, the left and right sides, with distinct anterior and posterior ends and dorsal and ventral surfaces. **Pentaradial symmetry** is radial symmetry in which the parts are repeated five times around the axis, as in adult echinoderms.

Five arrangements are usually distinguished (see the figure below):

- Asymmetry: no plane divides the body into similar halves. Many sponges grow irregularly, adapting their shape to substrate and current; some asymmetrical animals, such as snails and hermit crabs, are secondarily derived from bilateral ancestors.
- Radial symmetry: the body is a cylinder or a bell organized around the mouth. Planes through the oral–aboral axis divide it into similar parts; the number of repeats defines tetramerous (four parts, many medusae such as *Aurelia*), hexamerous (six, hexacorals) or octamerous (eight, octocorals) symmetry.
- Biradial symmetry: radial symmetry in which only two planes at right angles divide the body into similar halves, because some structures are paired. Ctenophores are biradial, with a pair of tentacles in one plane and the flattened pharynx in the other; sea anemones, with their slit-shaped mouth and siphonoglyphs, are biradial or bilateral.
- Pentaradial symmetry: five-fold symmetry of adult echinoderms. It is secondary: echinoderm larvae are bilateral, and the adult symmetry develops at metamorphosis around one side of the larval body ([7.17.42](/7.17.42)).
- Bilateral symmetry: one median plane. It characterizes the Bilateria, which make up more than 99% of animal species.

Symmetry is correlated with way of life. Radially symmetrical animals are sessile (polyps, sea lilies) or drift and swim slowly in all directions (medusae, ctenophores); a body that meets its environment equally on all sides suits them. Bilateral symmetry is associated with directed locomotion: one end leads, meets food and danger first, and concentrates the sense organs and the mouth.

### 3.2.2. Axes, Planes and Terms of Orientation

The anatomy of bilateral animals is described with a fixed vocabulary.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Anterior, posterior | Toward the head end, toward the tail end |
| Dorsal, ventral | Toward the back (upper surface in most animals), toward the belly |
| Lateral, medial | Away from the midline, toward it |
| Proximal, distal | Nearer to, farther from the body or the point of attachment of an appendage |
| Oral, aboral | Toward the mouth, away from it (radial animals) |
| Median (midsagittal) plane | The single plane of bilateral symmetry |
| Sagittal plane | Any plane parallel to the median plane |
| Frontal (coronal) plane | A plane at right angles to the median plane, separating dorsal and ventral halves |
| Transverse plane | A plane at right angles to the anteroposterior axis (a cross section) |

In human anatomy, because people stand upright, "superior" and "inferior" replace anterior and posterior and "anterior" means ventral; zoology uses the terms of the table for all animals, including humans, in comparative contexts.

The axes have a molecular basis that is shared across the Bilateria. The anteroposterior axis is patterned by the **Hox genes** and their nested domains of expression (**Hox colinearity**, [7.5.15](/7.5.15)), the dorsoventral axis by a gradient of BMP signaling opposed by its antagonist chordin (Dpp and Sog in *Drosophila*). The gradient is inverted between arthropods and vertebrates: BMP specifies dorsal in flies and ventral in vertebrates, and the nerve cord forms where BMP is low, ventrally in insects and dorsally in vertebrates. This supports the old hypothesis of Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire (1822) that the vertebrate is an inverted arthropod ([7.5.16](/7.5.16)). Remarkably, the sea anemone *Nematostella*, a cnidarian, has a second body axis at right angles to the oral–aboral axis, patterned by BMP and Hox genes as well: the molecular means of making a bilateral body may predate the Bilateria.

### 3.2.3. Cephalization

**Definition 3.2.2 (Cephalization)** **Cephalization** is the concentration of nervous tissue, sense organs and feeding structures at the anterior end of a bilaterally symmetrical animal, forming a head.

Cephalization follows from directed movement: information about the environment is most useful where the animal is going, and a mouth at the leading end meets food first. It proceeds by degrees. Acoel worms and flatworms have an anterior concentration of neurons, the cerebral ganglion, with sensory cells and eyespots; annelids have a prostomium bearing palps, antennae and eyes; arthropods fuse several anterior segments into a head with antennae, compound eyes and mouthparts; vertebrates have a brain of three primary parts enclosed in a skull, with paired eyes, ears and nose. Cephalization is often reduced in sessile or burrowing animals: bivalves have lost the head of the molluscan body plan, and adult tunicates and echinoderms have no centralized brain.

The degree of cephalization is not a simple measure of rank. Cephalopods, a lineage of molluscs, evolved the largest invertebrate brains (about 500 million neurons in *Octopus vulgaris*, most of them in the arms) from a body plan whose other members have modest ganglia ([7.17.14](/7.17.14#sec-14.7)). Conversely, the earliest chordates were probably lancelet-like animals with only a small cerebral vesicle, and the large vertebrate head, with its brain, paired sense organs and skull, was assembled later from new embryonic tissues, the neural crest and the placodes ([7.17.45](/7.17.45)). Within arthropods the number of segments incorporated into the head differs among groups and is one of the classic problems of arthropod morphology ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.6)).

The pattern is consistent: radial symmetry recurs in sessile animals and in animals that drift (polyps, crinoids, adult echinoderms), and bilateral symmetry recurs, even within radial lineages, when animals begin to move in one direction (anthozoan larvae, burrowing heart urchins, sea cucumbers crawling on one side). In the five-fold echinoderms the radial plan is superimposed on a bilateral ancestry, and the burrowing forms have returned to bilaterality in a new plane. Symmetry is therefore one of the most labile features of the body plan, a strong reason not to use it alone as evidence of relationship.

### 3.2.5. Left–Right Asymmetry

Even bilateral animals are seldom exactly symmetrical. Departures from symmetry between left and right fall into three statistically distinct classes, which differ in what they say about development.

**Definition 3.2.4 (Directional Asymmetry, Antisymmetry, Fluctuating Asymmetry)** In a population, a bilateral character shows **directional asymmetry** when it is consistently larger or differently formed on one side; **antisymmetry** when one side is consistently larger but which side varies at random among individuals, so that the distribution of left-minus-right differences is bimodal or flat-topped; and **fluctuating asymmetry** when the left-minus-right differences are small and normally distributed about zero.

Directional asymmetry includes the position of the vertebrate heart, stomach and liver, the dextral coiling of most snails, the single left tusk of the male narwhal, and the vertically offset ear openings of barn owls, which improve the localization of sound in the vertical plane. Antisymmetry is found in the claws of male fiddler crabs, in the crusher and cutter claws of lobsters, where the side of the crusher is decided by use during a juvenile stage, and in the crossed mandibles of crossbills. Fluctuating asymmetry reflects small random errors of development; because it increases under stress such as inbreeding, pollution or parasitism, it is used as a measure of developmental instability.

Directional asymmetry requires a mechanism that distinguishes left from right in the embryo. In vertebrates, a leftward flow of fluid produced by rotating cilia at the node triggers expression of the signaling protein Nodal and the transcription factor Pitx2 on the left side only ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)). Snails also express *nodal* and *Pitx* on one side, but there the chirality is set by the oblique spiral cleavage of the eight-cell stage: in the pond snail *Lymnaea stagnalis* it is controlled by a maternally acting gene encoding a formin, a protein that nucleates actin filaments, and mothers homozygous for the recessive sinistral allele produce sinistral offspring whatever their own coiling. That two phyla use Nodal to mark one side suggests that a left–right signal existed in the bilaterian ancestor, although the upstream mechanisms differ.

## 3.3. Germ Layers

During gastrulation the cells of the embryo are sorted into **germ layers**, the sheets or masses of cells from which the tissues of the adult develop ([7.5.9](/7.5.9#def-9.1.2)). The outer layer, the **ectoderm**, forms the epidermis and the nervous system; the inner layer, the **endoderm**, lines the gut and forms its glands; and in most animals a middle layer, the **mesoderm**, forms muscles, connective tissue, the lining of the coelom, blood vessels, kidneys and gonads.

**Definition 3.3.1 (Diploblasts and Triploblasts)** **Diploblasts** (diploblastic animals) are animals whose body develops from two germ layers, ectoderm and endoderm, with at most a noncellular or sparsely cellular layer between them; they are the cnidarians and ctenophores. **Triploblasts** (triploblastic animals) develop a third germ layer, the mesoderm, between ectoderm and endoderm; they are the Bilateria.

Sponges and placozoans fit neither category: their embryos and adults have distinct cell layers, but these are not readily homologized with the germ layers of other animals. The mesoglea of diploblasts is an extracellular matrix, not a germ layer ([7.17.2](/7.17.2#def-2.4.2)). The distinction is less absolute than the definition suggests. Many cnidarians have muscle cells and gonads derived from the endoderm, ctenophores have true muscle cells in the mesoglea that some authors regard as mesodermal, and in the cnidarian *Nematostella* the endoderm expresses many of the transcription factors (such as *twist*, *snail* and *brachyury*) that specify mesoderm in bilaterians. A common interpretation is that the bilaterian mesoderm arose from part of an ancestral endomesoderm, the inner layer of a diploblastic ancestor.

The mesoderm was a decisive innovation. It provides a third population of cells that can form tissues between the two epithelia, notably muscles that are not tied to an epithelium, connective tissues and skeletons, circulatory and excretory systems and, in many groups, the lining of a body cavity. Almost all the complexity of bilaterian organ systems is mesodermal.

The germ-layer theory has a history that explains both its power and its limits. Christian Pander (1817) described three layers in the chick embryo, Karl Ernst von Baer generalized them to vertebrates, and Robert Remak in the 1850s established the three layers and their main derivatives in their modern sense. Alexander Kowalevsky showed in the 1860s and 1870s that lancelets, tunicates and many invertebrates gastrulate and form comparable layers, which made the germ layers the basis of comparisons across phyla. The theory was later found to be too rigid as a rule of fate: some tissues come from different layers in different animals, and the vertebrate neural crest, an ectodermal population, forms cartilage and bone. Germ layers are best regarded as the first major subdivisions of the embryo, homologous across the Bilateria in their position and their regulatory genes, rather than as fixed sources of particular tissues.

| Germ layer | Vertebrates | Invertebrates (examples) |

| Neural ectoderm | Brain and spinal cord, retina, neurohypophysis; the neural crest forms peripheral neurons and glia, pigment cells, adrenal medulla, dentine and most of the skeleton and connective tissue of the face ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)) | Central and peripheral nervous system; in insects the neuroblasts delaminate from a ventral neuroectoderm |

| Endoderm | Epithelium of the gut, liver, pancreas, lungs and trachea, urinary bladder; thyroid, parathyroids and thymic epithelium (from the pharyngeal pouches) ([7.5.30](/7.5.30)) | Midgut epithelium and its glands, such as the digestive gland of molluscs and the hepatopancreas of crustaceans; gastric caeca of insects; Malpighian tubules of arachnids (from the midgut) |

Two entries in the table deserve attention. The Malpighian tubules of insects grow out of the ectodermal hindgut, those of arachnids out of the endodermal midgut: the two organs, similar in form and function, are not homologous, and the germ layer of origin is one of the arguments that they evolved independently ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.6)). And the germ cells do not belong to any germ layer: in many animals (flies, nematodes, frogs) they are set aside very early by inheriting a germ plasm, in others (mice) they are induced later from cells of the epiblast ([7.5.27](/7.5.27)). The neural crest is so important for the vertebrate head that it has been called a fourth germ layer (Hall 2000).

### 3.3.2. Where the Mesoderm Comes From

The mesoderm arises in different ways in different groups, which is why its homology across the Bilateria was once questioned.

| Group | Origin of the mesoderm | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| Spiralians (annelids, molluscs, nemerteans, flatworms) | Endomesoderm from the 4d cell (mesentoblast), whose daughters form a pair of **teloblasts** and mesodermal bands; ectomesoderm from micromeres of the second and third quartets | 4d mesoderm forms adult muscles, coelom and gonads; ectomesoderm forms mainly larval muscles and mesenchyme ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)) |
| Insects | A ventral band of the blastoderm invaginates as the ventral furrow | Specified by the transcription factors Twist and Snail in response to the dorsoventral gradient ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)) |
| Nematodes | Particular blastomere lineages (MS, C and D in *Caenorhabditis elegans*) | Fixed lineages of a mosaic embryo ([7.5.6](/7.5.6)) |
| Echinoderms, hemichordates | Mesenchyme cells ingress from the archenteron (in sea urchins the skeletogenic mesenchyme comes from the micromeres); coelomic pouches bud from the archenteron | Enterocoely |
| Lancelets | Pouches of the archenteron form the anterior somites; later somites arise from a posterior growth zone | [7.17.43](/7.17.43) |
| Vertebrates | Cells ingress through the blastopore lip (amphibians) or the primitive streak (amniotes) | Induced by Nodal signaling from the endoderm (**mesoderm induction**, [7.5.16](/7.5.16)) |

Despite these differences, the mesoderm of bilaterians expresses a shared set of regulatory genes, including *twist*, *snail*, *brachyury* around the blastopore, *Mox*, *FoxC* and *FoxF*, and the same genes specify muscle (*MyoD* and its relatives) and heart (*tinman*/*Nkx2.5*) in flies and vertebrates. This supports a single origin of the mesoderm at the base of the Bilateria, from which the various routes of formation diverged.

### 3.3.3. Layers in Non-Bilaterian Animals

The non-bilaterians show what the germ layers may have been before the mesoderm. Sponges have an outer pinacoderm and an inner choanoderm, separated by the mesohyl with its wandering cells; whether the choanoderm corresponds to the endoderm of other animals is unresolved ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)). Placozoans have a ciliated lower epithelium that digests food on the substrate and a thin upper epithelium, with fiber cells between ([7.17.8](/7.17.8#sec-8.3)). Cnidarians have an epidermis and a gastrodermis, both made largely of epitheliomuscular cells, separated by mesoglea; in hydromedusae the striated swimming muscle derives from the entocodon, a cell mass of the developing medusa bud that some authors compare with a mesoderm ([7.17.9](/7.17.9)). Ctenophores have true muscle cells and mesenchyme in the mesoglea, which in *Mnemiopsis* derive from the same early blastomeres as the endoderm (Martindale and Henry 1999). These observations fit the view that the inner layer of the diploblastic ancestor was a bifunctional endomesoderm, and that the bilaterian mesoderm became a separate layer later.

## 3.4. Body Cavities

### 3.4.1. Three Grades of Triploblastic Organization

In triploblastic animals the space between the body wall and the gut may be filled with tissue or occupied by a fluid-filled cavity (see the figure below).

**Definition 3.4.1 (Body Cavity)** A **body cavity** is a fluid-filled space between the body wall and the gut of a triploblastic animal, in which internal organs lie or into which they bulge.

**Definition 3.4.2 (Acoelomates, Pseudocoel, True Coelom)** Triploblastic animals without a body cavity, in which the space between epidermis and gut is filled with mesodermal tissue (parenchyma), are **acoelomates**. A **pseudocoel** (pseudocoelom, or blastocoelom) is a body cavity not lined by mesodermal epithelium: it is a persistent remnant of the embryonic blastocoel, bounded by the muscles of the body wall on one side and by the gut, often with no muscle layer of its own, on the other. A **true coelom** (eucoelom) is a body cavity that arises within the mesoderm and is completely lined by a mesodermal epithelium, the peritoneum, which covers both the inner surface of the body wall (the somatic or parietal layer) and the outer surface of the gut (the splanchnic or visceral layer).

| Grade | Space between gut and body wall | Gut wall | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acoelomate | Filled with parenchyma (mesenchyme), sometimes with small lacunae | Endoderm, often with mesodermal muscle | Xenacoelomorphs, flatworms, gastrotrichs, gnathostomulids, nemerteans (but see below) |
| Pseudocoelomate (blastocoelomate) | Persistent blastocoel, not lined by mesodermal epithelium | Endoderm only in many species | Nematodes, rotifers, kinorhynchs, loriciferans, priapulids (mostly), nematomorphs |
| Coelomate | Coelom within the mesoderm, lined by peritoneum | Endoderm with splanchnic mesoderm (muscle, peritoneum) | Annelids, molluscs (reduced), arthropods (reduced), brachiopods, phoronids, echinoderms, hemichordates, chordates |

Because the peritoneum covers the organs, organs of coelomates do not actually lie free in the coelomic fluid: they are suspended in double folds of peritoneum, the mesenteries, which carry their blood vessels and nerves. In a pseudocoelomate the organs are bathed directly in the body fluid.

Chinese textbooks call the pseudocoel the "primary body cavity" and the true coelom the "secondary body cavity", because the blastocoel appears first in development and the coelom later. Brusca and colleagues prefer the term blastocoelom to pseudocoelom, because the cavity is not "false" but simply of a different origin. Acoelomate and pseudocoelomate animals are often small or slender, so that diffusion suffices for internal transport ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)).

### 3.4.2. The Hemocoel

**Definition 3.4.3 (Hemocoel)** A **hemocoel** is a body cavity filled with blood (hemolymph) that forms part of an **open circulatory system**: the blood leaves the vessels and bathes the organs directly before returning to the heart. In arthropods and molluscs the hemocoel is the main body cavity, while the true coelom is reduced to small spaces around the gonads, the excretory organs and, in molluscs, the heart (the pericardial cavity).

Embryologically the hemocoel of arthropods derives from the blastocoel and from spaces between the mesodermal tissues; the coelomic sacs that appear in their embryos fuse with it or become the cavities of the gonads and nephridia. The adult arthropod therefore does not fit the scheme of Definition 3.4.2 well, although it develops coelomic cavities. The hemocoel reflects a different solution to support and transport: a hard exoskeleton replaces the coelom as skeleton, and a large blood space replaces the coelom as the medium of transport ([7.14.12](/7.14.12)).

### 3.4.3. What Body Cavities Do

A fluid-filled cavity between body wall and gut has several advantages, which explain why cavities arose and were lost in several lineages:

- Hydrostatic skeleton: an enclosed volume of incompressible fluid resists the contraction of muscles in the body wall and transmits their force, allowing the body to change shape, burrow and crawl. Coeloms divided by septa allow segments to work independently ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)).
- Freedom of the gut: separated from the body wall, the gut can move by peristalsis of its own splanchnic muscles independently of locomotion.
- Room for organs: gonads can grow large and gametes accumulate in the coelom before spawning; the gut can become long and coiled.
- Transport and storage: coelomic fluid circulates nutrients, wastes and coelomocytes; in many small coelomates it serves as the circulatory medium.
- Excretion and reproduction: the coelom opens to the outside through coelomoducts and is drained by metanephridia, which use it as the site of primary urine formation; gametes leave through the same ducts ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)).

Different coelomic compartments can serve different functions in one animal. In vertebrates the coelom is divided into the pericardial, pleural and peritoneal cavities, which allow the heart and lungs to move freely ([7.17.63](/7.17.63)); in deuterostomes such as echinoderms and hemichordates the coelom is partitioned from front to back into three compartments (protocoel, mesocoel and metacoel), one of which forms the water-vascular system of echinoderms.

The functions can also be seen in reverse, in the lineages that reduced the coelom. Leeches, which move by suckers and loops rather than by peristalsis, have lost the septa, and their coelom is largely filled with connective tissue, leaving a system of channels that in some species has taken over the role of the blood vessels ([7.17.15](/7.17.15#sec-15.7)). Arthropods, with a rigid exoskeleton, no longer need a coelomic hydrostatic skeleton. Small animals such as gastrotrichs, gnathostomulids and many meiofaunal annelids, in which diffusion suffices for transport, have little or no body cavity.

### 3.4.5. How the Coelom Arises and How Often It Evolved

In development a coelom forms in two classical ways, by splitting of mesodermal bands (**schizocoely**) or by outpocketing of the archenteron (**enterocoely**) ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#def-5.3.1)), with many variants: vertebrates split the lateral plate mesoderm, which is a schizocoely unrelated to that of spiralians, and lancelets form their anterior somites as gut pouches. The distribution of the two modes cuts across the major clades. Schizocoely from 4d teloblasts characterizes annelids and molluscs; enterocoely occurs in echinoderms, hemichordates and lancelets but also in the protostome brachiopods and, in a modified form, chaetognaths. The mode of formation is therefore not a reliable guide to relationship.

Several hypotheses on the evolutionary origin of the coelom were formulated in the age of comparative embryology, most of them assuming that it arose only once.

| Hypothesis | Main proponents | The first coelom was | Evidence offered | Difficulties |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enterocoel theory | Lankester (1877), Sedgwick (1884); Remane's archicoelomate concept; Jägersten's bilaterogastraea | Pouches of the gut of a cnidarian-like ancestor that pinched off | Enterocoely in living deuterostomes; the gastric pouches of anthozoans; a three-part coelom (protocoel, mesocoel, metacoel) | Requires that schizocoely was derived; three-part coeloms are not general |
| Gonocoel theory | Bergh, Hatschek, Meyer, Goodrich | The cavity of serially repeated gonads, persisting after the gametes were shed | Serial gonads in flatworms and nemerteans; annelid coelomic lining produces gametes | In no living coelomate do gonads form before the coelom |
| Nephrocoel theory | Lankester, Ziegler, Faussek, Snodgrass | Expanded inner ends of nephridia | Close association of nephridia and coelom | Nephridia and coelom have different origins in development |
| Schizocoel (functional) view | R. B. Clark (1964) | Spaces opening within the mesoderm as a hydrostatic skeleton for burrowing | Mechanical advantage of a fluid skeleton for peristaltic burrowing | Implies several independent origins, which Clark accepted |

The current tree ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)) favors the possibility Clark recognized. Coelomate phyla are scattered among acoelomate and blastocoelomate ones in both Spiralia and Ecdysozoa, and the Xenacoelomorpha, near the base of the Bilateria, have none. Either the coelom arose once in the ancestor of the Nephrozoa and was lost in flatworms, gastrotrichs, gnathiferans and cycloneuralian ecdysozoans, or it arose more than once, perhaps separately in protostomes and deuterostomes. The developmental data do not decide between these alternatives, but the many independent losses required by a single origin, and the correlation of coelom loss with small size, show at least that the coelom is a functional character that lineages acquire and discard as their way of life changes.

## 3.5. Segmentation

### 3.5.1. Segments and Tagmata

**Definition 3.5.1 (Segmentation)** **Segmentation** (metamerism) is the division of the body along the anteroposterior axis into a series of repeated units, the **segments** (metameres), each of which contains a set of serially repeated structures derived from more than one germ layer, such as body-wall muscles, nerve ganglia, coelomic compartments, excretory organs, appendages or skeletal elements.

The definition requires repetition in several tissues at once. The repetition of a single organ (the serial gills and eight shell plates of a chiton, the paired nephridia of a monoplacophoran) is often called pseudometamerism and is not counted as segmentation. In annelids the coelom is divided by transverse septa into a pair of compartments per segment, each with its own muscles, ganglion of the ventral nerve cord, pair of nephridia and, in polychaetes, pair of parapodia. In arthropods the segments are marked by the plates of the exoskeleton, the appendages and the ganglia, but the coelom has disappeared. In vertebrates segmentation is present in the embryonic somites and in their derivatives, the muscle blocks (myomeres), vertebrae, ribs and spinal nerves, but not in the epidermis or the gut.

Not every part of a segmented body is a segment. In annelids the prostomium in front of the mouth and the pygidium around the anus arise from the regions of the larva in front of and behind the growth zone, not from the growth zone itself, and are therefore not counted as segments; new segments are added one by one in a zone just in front of the pygidium, so that the youngest segment is the last and the oldest the first behind the head ([7.17.15](/7.17.15#sec-15.1.1)). Arthropods have an unsegmented terminal region, the telson, behind the last segment, and a front end (the acron or ocular region) whose segmental status is debated ([7.17.18](/7.17.18#sec-18.6)). The repeated organs of the segments are serial homologues (**serial homology**): copies of one developmental unit along the axis, which can diverge in form while keeping their correspondence.

Segments may be alike or different (Definition 3.5.3 below), and the extreme of difference is the fusion of groups of segments into functional regions.

**Definition 3.5.2 (Tagma)** A **tagma** (plural tagmata) is a functional region of the body of a segmented animal, formed by a group of adjacent segments that are specialized together, such as the head, thorax and abdomen of an insect. The formation of tagmata is called **tagmosis**.

| Group | Tagmata | Functions |
|---|---|---|
| Insects | Head (6 segments), thorax (3), abdomen (up to 11) | Sensing and feeding; locomotion (legs and wings); digestion and reproduction |
| Chelicerates (spiders, scorpions) | Prosoma (cephalothorax), opisthosoma (abdomen) | Feeding, sensing and walking; respiration, digestion, reproduction, silk |
| Crustaceans (decapods) | Head, thorax (often fused with the head into a cephalothorax under a carapace), pleon (abdomen) | Sensing and feeding; walking; swimming |
| Myriapods | Head, trunk | Little tagmosis behind the head |
| Polychaete annelids | Prostomium, peristomium, trunk, pygidium; in tube worms thorax and abdomen | Sensing; feeding; locomotion |
| Leeches | Head with anterior sucker, 21 midbody segments, posterior sucker of 7 fused segments | Feeding and attachment; locomotion by looping |
| Vertebrates | Head, trunk (with neck, thorax, lumbar and sacral regions in tetrapods), tail | Regions of the vertebral column differ in their vertebrae, set by Hox codes |

**Definition 3.5.3 (Homonomous and Heteronomous Segmentation)** Segmentation is **homonomous segmentation** when the segments along the trunk are nearly alike in structure and function, as in the trunk of an earthworm, and **heteronomous segmentation** when segments differ from region to region, as in insects, the extreme being the grouping of segments into tagmata.

Homonomy and heteronomy are ends of a continuum, and evolution has moved in both directions. Cambrian stem-group arthropods already had a differentiated head, and the long, uniform trunks of centipedes, millipedes and remipedes are specializations of their own lineages rather than the ancestral condition; each arthropod lineage has a characteristic tagmosis whose segments are specialized for feeding, walking, swimming or reproduction. Chinese textbooks treat the homonomous segmentation of annelids as the starting point of "true segmentation" and heteronomous segmentation as its further development in arthropods, a sequence that describes a trend in the structure of segments, not the descent of arthropods from annelids.

Tagmosis in arthropods is controlled by the Hox genes, whose combinations of expression give each segment its identity; changes in Hox expression accompany changes in tagmata, for example the shift of the boundary between the maxillipeds and the walking legs in crustaceans ([7.5.15](/7.5.15)). The arthropod body plan is discussed in [7.17.18](/7.17.18) and the insect segments in [7.17.23](/7.17.23).

### 3.5.2. Is Segmentation Homologous?

Three large phyla are segmented: annelids, arthropods and chordates. Cuvier united annelids and arthropods as Articulata, and the shared segmentation of the two was long taken as evidence that they are sister groups. Molecular phylogeny placed them instead in two different clades of protostomes, the Spiralia and the Ecdysozoa, each of whose closest relatives (molluscs and nemerteans, nematodes and priapulids) are unsegmented ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)). The chordates are deuterostomes. Segmentation in the three phyla is therefore either an ancestral bilaterian feature lost many times, or it evolved independently, two or three times.

Before weighing the evidence, it helps to compare how the three phyla actually make and use their segments (see the table and the figure below).

| Feature | Annelids | Arthropods | Vertebrates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Segmented tissues | Ectoderm (epidermis, chaetae, ganglia) and mesoderm (muscles, coelom, nephridia) | Ectoderm (cuticle plates, appendages, ganglia) and mesoderm (muscles, embryonic coelomic sacs) | Paraxial mesoderm only (somites and their derivatives); nerves and neural crest follow the somites |
| Segmental coelom | Paired coelomic sacs, separated by septa | Transient embryonic sacs; adult hemocoel | Somitocoel transient; adult coelom unsegmented |
| Mode of formation | Sequential, from a posterior growth zone with **teloblasts** | Sequential from a growth zone in most arthropods (short-germ); simultaneous in the blastoderm in long-germ insects such as *Drosophila* | Sequential, by budding of somites from the presomitic mesoderm |
| Primary layer | Ectoderm and mesoderm each segmented by their own lineages | Ectoderm first; the mesoderm is patterned partly by the ectoderm | Mesoderm; the ectodermal nervous system is segmented secondarily |

![Three ways of making segments (schematic, anterior to the left, not to scale). (A) Annelid: new segments are added one by one in a growth zone in front of the pygidium, where teloblasts bud off cells of the mesodermal and ectodermal bands; the prostomium and pygidium are not segments, and the youngest segment lies at the rear. (B) Arthropod: in a short-germ insect or a spider the anterior segments are laid down first and the rest are added from a posterior growth zone (cyclic pair-rule or Notch expression); in a long-germ insect such as *Drosophila* all segments are specified at once by a cascade of gap and pair-rule genes, which ends in fourteen stripes of *engrailed* at the parasegment boundaries. The comparison shows a mixture of similarities and differences. All three phyla add segments sequentially from a posterior zone (long-germ insects are a derived exception within arthropods, [7.5.39](/7.5.39#sec-39.5.2)), and posterior growth zones in all three express *caudal* and Wnt signaling. But the tissues that are segmented first differ, the segmentation of vertebrates is confined to one mesodermal layer, and the boundary genes of arthropods and annelids play no comparable role in the somites.

Developmental genetics has supplied arguments for both views ([7.5.39](/7.5.39#sec-39.3)):

- For a common origin: the gene *engrailed* is expressed in a stripe at the posterior boundary of each segment in arthropods and in the annelid *Platynereis* (Prud'homme and colleagues, 2003), together with Wnt and Hedgehog signaling; in vertebrates somites are produced by an oscillating Notch signaling clock (the **segmentation clock**, [7.5.23](/7.5.23)), and oscillating Notch and Hes expression also accompanies the addition of segments from a posterior growth zone in spiders (Stollewerk and colleagues, 2003), centipedes and cockroaches (Pueyo and colleagues, 2008). In the beetle *Tribolium* the pair-rule gene *odd-skipped* oscillates in the growth zone with a period corresponding to two segments (Sarrazin and colleagues, 2012), a clock-like mechanism comparable to that of vertebrates. Balavoine and Adoutte (2003) argued from such data for a segmented urbilaterian.
- For independent origins: the genes are general-purpose components of the developmental toolkit used in many unsegmented structures (Notch in neurogenesis, *engrailed* in the shell field of molluscs and in neurons); the mechanisms differ in detail (most insects form all segments at once in the syncytial blastoderm by a cascade of gap and pair-rule genes, while vertebrates add them sequentially); and the outgroups of each segmented phylum are unsegmented, so a single origin would require many losses. Chipman (2010) proposed an intermediate view: the segmented phyla independently co-opted an ancestral gene network for posterior elongation, so that segmentation evolved in parallel from a shared developmental starting point.

Most authors now treat the segmentation of annelids, arthropods and vertebrates as convergent at the level of the segment, while accepting that parts of the gene networks used, such as the posterior growth zone and the Notch oscillator, may be shared by **deep homology**. The question shows the general lesson of this chapter: similarity of body plans, even when backed by shared genes, must be tested against the phylogeny.

A clear example of how segments can be built from cell lineages is the leech ([7.17.15](/7.17.15#sec-15.7)). Its embryo has five bilateral pairs of teloblasts: one pair (M) produces the mesoderm, four pairs (N, O, P and Q) the ectoderm. Each teloblast divides repeatedly and asymmetrically, budding off a column of small blast cells; the M, O and P teloblasts produce one blast cell per segment, the N and Q teloblasts two. The columns join into a germinal band whose cells form the segments in strict register, and the fixed number of segments of a leech (32) is set by the number of blast cells the teloblasts produce. Annelid segments thus arise by counting cell divisions, vertebrate somites by a clock acting on a field of cells, and *Drosophila* segments by subdividing a syncytium: three mechanisms that give similar outcomes.

### 3.5.3. Serial Repetition without Segmentation

Many unsegmented animals repeat one organ or another along the body. Such repetition, which does not involve several tissues in register, is called pseudometamerism or serial repetition, and it was once read as a sign of descent from segmented ancestors.

| Group | Repeated structures | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Chitons | Eight shell plates; 6–88 pairs of gills; repeated pedal nerve commissures and dorsoventral muscles | The plates do not match the gills or nerves; molluscs are not segmented ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.4)) |
| Monoplacophorans | Eight pairs of pedal retractor muscles, three to six pairs of gills, three to seven pairs of nephridia | Mismatched numbers; once claimed as evidence for segmented molluscs |
| Nemerteans | Gonads, lateral gut diverticula and nerve commissures alternating along the body | Pseudometamerism ([7.17.16](/7.17.16#sec-16.1)) |
| Cestodes | Proglottids, each with a set of reproductive organs | Proglottids form at the neck behind the scolex, so the oldest is the last: the reverse of annelid segments ([7.17.11](/7.17.11#sec-11.4)) |
| Kinorhynchs | Eleven trunk segments (zonites) with cuticular plates, segmental muscles and serially arranged nerve cells | Segmented in cuticle, muscles and nerves; usually regarded as independent of arthropod segmentation ([7.17.17](/7.17.17#sec-17.2)) |
| Vertebrate hindbrain | Rhombomeres, with Hox-coded identities | Segmented ectoderm that is independent of the somites ([7.5.18](/7.5.18)) |

Chinese textbooks describe the repeated gonads and gut diverticula of nemerteans as pseudosegmentation that anticipates the true segmentation of annelids. The phylogeny reverses the reading: nemerteans are related to annelids within the Lophotrochozoa, but their repeated organs are a separate solution, and the serial repetition of chitons and monoplacophorans is a feature of molluscs that evolved after their divergence from annelids.

### 3.5.4. Why Be Segmented?

The functional advantages of segmentation differ among the three phyla, which is itself an argument that it evolved for different reasons.

- In annelids the septa divide the coelom into compartments of constant volume, so that each segment can lengthen or shorten independently of its neighbors; this allows peristaltic burrowing and crawling (Clark 1964), and it confines the loss of fluid after an injury to a few segments. Annelids that do not burrow by peristalsis, such as many tube dwellers, have incomplete septa.
- In arthropods the segments are the hinged units of an exoskeleton, each with a pair of jointed appendages; segmentation makes a rigid body flexible, and heteronomy allows the appendages of different segments to specialize ([7.17.18](/7.17.18)).
- In vertebrates the segmented muscle blocks (myomeres) pull on an incompressible notochord or vertebral column, producing the lateral undulations of swimming; the segmental arrangement of vertebrae, ribs and spinal nerves follows from that of the somites ([7.17.43](/7.17.43), [7.17.60](/7.17.60)).

Segmentation can also be lost. Sipunculans and echiurans, now placed within the annelids, are unsegmented as adults, although transient segmental patterns appear in their nervous systems during development ([7.17.15](/7.17.15#sec-15.5)). Such cases show that a segmented body plan, once acquired, is not irreversible.

## 3.6. Grades and Clades

The traditional system combined the characters of this chapter into a hierarchy of grades. With minor variations it is the system of Hyman's treatise and of the Chinese textbooks of Liu Lingyun and Xu Chongren: Parazoa (sponges) versus Eumetazoa; among the Eumetazoa, Radiata (diploblastic radial animals: "Coelenterata", that is cnidarians and ctenophores) versus Bilateria; among the Bilateria, Acoelomata (flatworms, nemerteans), Pseudocoelomata or *Aschelminthes* (the pseudocoelomate worms: rotifers, nematodes and their allies, united by a body cavity derived from the blastocoel and, in most, a cuticle and a constant number of cells) and Coelomata, the last divided into Protostomia and Deuterostomia. Two consequences follow. First, the grades remain correct as descriptions: a nematode does have a pseudocoel and a flatworm has no body cavity, and these facts matter for how the animals work. What has changed is that these states are no longer read as steps on a single ladder of progress. The pseudocoel of nematodes and that of rotifers arose separately, and the acoelomate condition of flatworms may be derived from a coelomate ancestor by reduction, as small body size made a cavity unnecessary. Second, the question of what the ancestral bilaterian was like, a small acoelomate worm resembling an acoel or a larger coelomate, segmented animal, became open again. Current evidence slightly favors a small, unsegmented, possibly acoelomate ancestor with a through gut ([7.17.10](/7.17.10)), but this remains under debate.

The grade system lasted for a century because it captured real correlations. Animals without a body cavity are small or flat; animals with a blastocoelom are mostly small worms with a cuticle and high internal pressure; animals with a spacious coelom are larger and have circulatory and metanephridial systems. The Chinese textbooks of general zoology arrange their chapters in this sequence: protozoa, sponges, coelenterates, flatworms, pseudocoelomates, annelids, molluscs, arthropods, lophophorates, echinoderms and chordates, each chapter beginning with the new characters that the group adds, such as bilateral symmetry and mesoderm in flatworms or the coelom and segmentation in annelids. Read on the current tree, these "new characters" are still the right ones to learn for each phylum, but they are innovations of particular lineages, sometimes acquired more than once, rather than rungs of a ladder. This course follows the tree ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)) and, in each chapter on a phylum, gives the correspondence with the traditional sequence.

| Traditional statement | Reading on the current tree |
|---|---|
| Flatworms are the first triploblastic, bilateral animals | Bilateral symmetry and mesoderm arose at the base of the Bilateria; xenacoelomorphs branch earlier than flatworms, which are spiralians and may be simplified |
| The pseudocoel is a step between the acoelomate and coelomate conditions | Blastocoelomate phyla are scattered in Spiralia and Ecdysozoa; the condition arose several times, often with small size |
| True segmentation begins with annelids, and arthropods develop it further | Annelid and arthropod segmentation probably evolved separately; arthropods are related to unsegmented nematodes and priapulids |
| The coelom of protostomes forms by schizocoely, that of deuterostomes by enterocoely | Both modes occur in both clades; brachiopods and chaetognaths are enterocoelous protostomes |
| Echinoderms are radial animals close to the base of the deuterostomes | Echinoderms are bilaterians whose five-fold symmetry is secondary |

## Summary

| Character | States | Distribution and status |
|---|---|---|
| Symmetry (Definitions 3.2.1, 3.2.3) | Asymmetric; radial (tetramerous, hexamerous, octamerous); biradial; pentaradial; bilateral; left–right asymmetries (Definition 3.2.4) | Bilateral symmetry defines the clade Bilateria; radial and biradial symmetry are grades; pentaradial symmetry of echinoderms is secondary |
| Cephalization (Definition 3.2.2) | None to a head with brain and sense organs | Follows directed locomotion; reduced in sessile groups |
| Germ layers (Definition 3.3.1) | Diploblastic; triploblastic | Diploblasts: cnidarians, ctenophores; triploblasts: Bilateria, whose mesoderm forms by different routes but with shared regulatory genes |
| Body cavity (Definitions 3.4.1–3.4.3) | Acoelomate; pseudocoelomate (blastocoelomate); true coelom; hemocoel (mixocoel) | Each grade occurs in unrelated clades; the coelom forms by schizocoely or enterocoely in both protostomes and deuterostomes, and was probably lost or gained more than once |
| Segmentation (Definition 3.5.1) | Unsegmented; homonomous or heteronomous (Definition 3.5.3), with tagmata (Definition 3.5.2) | Annelids (teloblasts and growth zone), arthropods (growth zone or blastoderm subdivision), vertebrates (somites from a clock and wavefront); probably convergent, with shared genetic components; serial repetition of single organs is not segmentation |

- A **body plan** (Definition 3.1.1) is the set of correlated structural features shared by a group; a **grade** (Definition 3.1.2) is a level of organization, which may be paraphyletic or polyphyletic.
- The body cavity serves as a hydrostatic skeleton, frees the gut, provides room for organs and gametes, and transports fluid; in arthropods and molluscs the hemocoel of an open circulatory system replaces the coelom.
- Symmetry is labile: radial symmetry recurs in sessile and drifting animals, bilaterality whenever animals move in one direction; directional asymmetry requires a left–right signal such as Nodal.
- Ectoderm forms epidermis and nervous system, mesoderm muscles, connective tissue, circulatory, excretory and reproductive organs, endoderm the gut and its glands; the same organ (Malpighian tubules) can arise from different layers in different lineages.
- On the current tree Bilateria, Protostomia and Deuterostomia are clades, whereas Radiata, Acoelomata, Pseudocoelomata (**Aschelminthes**), Coelomata and Articulata are not; the traditional characters of each phylum remain valid as descriptions of lineage-specific innovations.

## Questions

1. A sea star is pentaradial, a sea anemone is biradial, and both lack a head. Why is the sea star classified among the Bilateria and the anemone not?

   **Answer** The Bilateria are defined as a clade, not by the adult shape. The sea star is triploblastic, has a coelom formed from the mesoderm, and its larva (bipinnaria) is bilaterally symmetrical; its pentaradial symmetry develops at metamorphosis and is secondary. Molecular phylogeny places echinoderms among the deuterostomes. The sea anemone is a diploblastic cnidarian outside the Bilateria, although the BMP and Hox expression along its directive axis suggests that bilaterians inherited part of the molecular means of making a second axis from an earlier ancestor.

2. Why is the hemocoel of an insect not a pseudocoel, although neither is lined by peritoneum?

   **Answer** The two are defined by their place in the body plan, not only by the lining. A pseudocoel is a persistent blastocoel lying between body wall and gut, filled with body fluid that is not part of a circulatory system. The hemocoel is the blood space of an open circulatory system: it receives hemolymph from a heart and vessels and returns it to the heart. Insects also form coelomic sacs in the embryo, which contribute to the gonads and other organs, so they are coelomates whose coelom is reduced, and their main body cavity is a hemocoel.

3. List the observations that would be needed to show that the segments of annelids and arthropods are homologous, and explain which of them are currently missing.

   **Answer** One would need (a) a phylogeny in which a single origin of segmentation is at least as parsimonious as several, (b) shared, specific developmental mechanisms that are not used in unsegmented relatives, and (c) correspondence of the segments in more than one tissue. Shared expression of *engrailed*, Wnt and Hedgehog at segment boundaries and oscillating Notch in a growth zone satisfy (b) only partly, because these genes also act in unsegmented structures. Requirement (a) is not met: annelids and arthropods are nested within unsegmented clades (Spiralia and Ecdysozoa), so homology would require many independent losses of segmentation in molluscs, nemerteans, nematodes, priapulids and others.

4. Flatworms are acoelomate and annelids coelomate, and both are spiralians. Describe two scenarios for the evolution of their body cavities and the evidence that could distinguish them.

   **Answer** In the first scenario the ancestor of Spiralia was acoelomate and the coelom evolved within the group, in the lineage leading to annelids, molluscs and brachiopods. In the second it was coelomate and the cavity was lost in flatworms, perhaps as a consequence of small size, which also led to the loss of a circulatory system. Evidence comes from mapping the character on a well-resolved tree of Spiralia (the more coelomate lineages branch off near its base, the more likely the coelomate ancestor) and from development: if the mesodermal bands of flatworms derive from the same 4d mesentoblast and express the same genes as those that form coelomic sacs in annelids, a reduction becomes plausible.

5. In a sample of 200 male fiddler crabs, 103 have the large claw on the right and 97 on the left; in a sample of 200 sticklebacks, the number of lateral plates differs by at most one between the two sides and the differences are symmetrically distributed about zero. Classify the two asymmetries and say what each implies about development.

   **Answer** The crab claw is antisymmetry: every male has one greatly enlarged claw, but the side is close to random (103 against 97 is consistent with a ratio of one to one). Development must contain a mechanism that makes the two sides different, but the choice of side is not specified by a left–right signal of the embryo (in some fiddler crabs a male that loses its large claw regrows a small one there while the other claw enlarges). The stickleback plates show fluctuating asymmetry: small deviations centered on zero, which reflect random noise in the development of a character that is meant to be symmetrical; their variance can be used as a measure of developmental stability. Neither is directional asymmetry, which would require a consistent bias to one side, as in the vertebrate heart.

6. A cestode has hundreds of proglottids, each with a complete set of reproductive organs. Give two reasons why zoologists do not regard it as segmented in the sense of Definition 3.5.1.

   **Answer** First, the proglottids repeat essentially one organ system, the reproductive organs (with the excretory canals and nerve cords running continuously through them), whereas segments involve several tissues in register, such as muscles, ganglia, coelom and nephridia. Second, the mode of formation is the reverse of annelid segmentation: proglottids are budded from the neck region behind the scolex, so the oldest and most mature proglottids are at the posterior end and are shed, while annelid segments are added in front of the pygidium. The cestode strobila is better understood as a chain of reproductive units, and cestodes belong to the unsegmented flatworms.
