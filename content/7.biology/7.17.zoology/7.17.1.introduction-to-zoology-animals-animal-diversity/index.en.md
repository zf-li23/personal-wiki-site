Zoology is the science of animals: their structure, development, function, behavior, classification and evolutionary history. Its subject is a single clade of eukaryotes, the Metazoa, which contains about one and a half million described living species, more than all other named organisms together, and probably several times as many that have not yet been named. This chapter first asks what an animal is and where animals sit on the **tree of life**; it then surveys how animal diversity is distributed among the phyla and how its unknown total is estimated, sketches the branches and history of zoology, introduces the rules by which animals are named, and closes with the plan of the course. The general ideas of phylogeny and classification were set out in evolutionary biology ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)) and the tools of taxonomy in botany ([7.12.2](/7.12.2)); here we take the zoological viewpoint and add only what differs. The phylogenetic framework that the whole course follows is drawn in [7.17.6](/7.17.6).

## 1.1. What Is an Animal

### 1.1.1. Animals Among the Eukaryotes

In the two-kingdom system of Linnaeus an animal was any organism that moves and ingests food. That criterion put amoebae, ciliates and euglenoids among the animals, and older textbooks of zoology accordingly opened with a phylum "Protozoa" of single-celled animals. Molecular phylogenies have since shown that these organisms belong to almost every branch of the eukaryote tree, and that the motile, food-ingesting way of life evolved many times ([7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.7.3), [7.11.20](/7.11.20)). The word "animal" is now reserved for a single clade, the multicellular animals or Metazoa; the former protozoa are **protists**, and how the traditional classes of protozoa map onto the modern supergroups is shown in [7.17.6](/7.17.6).

Within the eukaryotes, animals belong to the **opisthokonts**, the group whose motile cells, where present, are pushed by a single posterior flagellum: the sperm of animals and the zoospores of chytrid fungi are the clearest examples. The opisthokonts divide into Holomycota (fungi and the nucleariid amoebae) and Holozoa (animals and three groups of unicellular relatives: the **choanoflagellates**, the filastereans and the ichthyosporeans). Animals are thus more closely related to mushrooms than to any plant, and their closest living relatives are the choanoflagellates, microscopic cells with a collar of microvilli around one flagellum (see the figure below).

### 1.1.2. The Defining Features of Animals

**Definition 1.1.1 (Metazoa)** The **Metazoa** (animals) are the clade of opisthokont eukaryotes comprising the most recent common ancestor of sponges, ctenophores, placozoans, cnidarians and bilaterians and all its descendants. They are multicellular heterotrophs that, with few exceptions, ingest their food; their cells are bound by an extracellular matrix rich in collagen; they are diploid organisms whose only haploid cells are the gametes, a small motile sperm and a large egg; and the zygote develops through cleavage into an embryo whose cells sort into layers.

The definition is phylogenetic: Metazoa is the crown group of the animals, and the characters listed are its synapomorphies, the derived features present in the common ancestor. Several of them are better understood as a syndrome than as a list:

- Multicellularity with differentiated cells: Animals are among the few lineages with complex **multicellularity** ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5)): their bodies are built of many cell types in a reproducible arrangement, and only the germ line contributes to the next generation.
- Epithelia and the extracellular matrix: Animal cells are joined into sheets by specialized cell junctions and rest on an **extracellular matrix** whose fibrous protein is **collagen**; most animals have a **basal lamina** of type IV collagen and laminin beneath their epithelia ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)). The sponges are a partial exception: only the homoscleromorphs have a true basal lamina.
- Heterotrophy by ingestion: Animals eat other organisms and digest them, intracellularly in sponges and placozoans, extracellularly in a gut in most others. Some have secondarily given up feeding by mouth: tapeworms absorb nutrients through their surface, and the siboglinid tube worms of hydrothermal vents live on symbiotic bacteria.
- The gametic life cycle: Meiosis gives rise directly to gametes, so the multicellular body is always diploid; the sperm swims with a single posterior flagellum, the opisthokont inheritance.
- Development through an embryo: Cleavage of the zygote produces a **blastula**, and in all animals except sponges and placozoans **gastrulation** sorts the cells into two or three **germ layers** ([7.5.5](/7.5.5), [7.5.9](/7.5.9)). The developmental toolkit of animal genes (Wnt, TGF-β and Hedgehog signaling, the homeobox transcription factors including the **Hox genes** of most animals) was largely assembled at the origin of the Metazoa ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

None of these features is unique in isolation: fungi are opisthokont heterotrophs, many algae are multicellular, and choanoflagellates make cadherins and tyrosine kinases. What is unique is the combination, and the fact that the animal lineage built from it a body that moves through the world, senses it and acts on it with muscles and nerves (present in all animals except sponges and placozoans).

### 1.1.3. Invertebrates and Vertebrates

The first division of animals that most people learn, into animals with and without a backbone, goes back to Lamarck, who in 1801 grouped Linnaeus's insects and worms as *animaux sans vertèbres*.

**Definition 1.1.2 (Invertebrates)** The **invertebrates** are all animals that are not vertebrates: the 30 or so phyla other than Chordata, together with the two invertebrate subphyla of chordates, the cephalochordates (lancelets) and urochordates (tunicates). They form a **paraphyletic group**, because the vertebrates descend from an invertebrate ancestor but are excluded.

"Invertebrate" thus names a grade of organization and a division of labor among zoologists rather than a natural group. The asymmetry is extreme: vertebrates make up about 4% of described animal species, and an arthropod and a sea star are more closely related to each other than either is to a sponge. Throughout the course the invertebrate phyla are therefore treated in phylogenetic order rather than as a block opposed to the vertebrates.

## 1.2. The Diversity of Animals

### 1.2.1. Described Species

About 1.45 million living animal species had been described by the early 2020s (see the figure below). They are very unevenly distributed. The arthropods alone account for 82%, and the insects and other hexapods for more than two thirds of all animals; arthropods, molluscs and chordates together make up 92%. Twenty-five of the roughly 34 phyla each contain less than 1% of known species, and some contain only a handful: two cycliophorans, one micrognathozoan, a few placozoan species. Small phyla are not unimportant: the sponges and cnidarians build reefs, the nematodes are the most numerous animals in soils and sediments, and several minute phyla hold the key to the early branching of the animal tree.

![Numbers of described living species in the animal phyla (logarithmic axis; the phylum Arthropoda is split into its four subphyla and Chordata into its three). Data: Brusca, Giribet and Moore 2023, Table 1.1 (1,453,163 species in total, compiled from Zhang 2011, 2013 and later counts). Bars are colored by the major clade to which the phylum belongs (see the animal tree in 7.17.6): non-bilaterian animals, Xenacoelomorpha, Spiralia, Ecdysozoa, Deuterostomia](./plots/species-by-phylum.py)

The distribution in space is uneven in a different way. Animals arose in the sea, and every phylum except the onychophorans has living marine members; about half the phyla are exclusively marine, among them the echinoderms, ctenophores, brachiopods, chaetognaths, hemichordates and the minute kinorhynchs and loriciferans. Only a few lineages have conquered dry land with a whole life cycle independent of free water: arthropods (above all insects, arachnids and myriapods), pulmonate snails, some annelids and nematodes, the tardigrades in their dormant states, and the amniote vertebrates. Most of the described diversity is terrestrial nonetheless, because the insects radiated on land together with the flowering plants.

Animals span about eight orders of magnitude in length, from loriciferans and coral-reef nematodes of less than 0.1 mm to blue whales of 30 m, and more than fifteen orders of magnitude in mass. Many phyla consist largely of meiofauna, animals that pass through a 1 mm sieve and live between grains of sediment; others include giants such as the giant squid (*Architeuthis*, about 13 m with its tentacles) and giant clams (*Tridacna*, over 200 kg).

### 1.2.2. How Many Species Are There

The number of described species is a measure of taxonomic effort as much as of nature. About 15,000 to 20,000 new animal species are described each year, mostly insects, and there is no sign of saturation: in samples of beetles from tropical canopies, of crustaceans and polychaetes from continental slopes or of mites from soils, most species are typically new to science. Estimating the total requires extrapolation, and the three main approaches give answers that differ by an order of magnitude.

The first approach scales up from an intensively sampled community. Its best-known example also started the modern debate.

**Example 1.2.1 (Erwin's Estimate)** In 1982 Terry Erwin fogged the canopies of 19 trees of *Luehea seemannii* in Panama with insecticide and collected about 1,200 species of beetles. He assumed that 13.5% of the herbivorous beetles (and smaller fractions of the other feeding guilds) were specialists of that tree species, which gave about 163 host-specific beetle species per tree species. Multiplying by about 50,000 species of tropical trees gave 8.15 million species of canopy beetles. Since beetles make up about 40% of the arthropod species in such samples, the tropical canopy would hold about 20 million arthropod species; with half as many again on the ground, the total came to 30 million species of tropical arthropods.

:::

Each factor in Erwin's product is uncertain, and later studies found the critical one, host specificity, to be much lower than assumed: many canopy herbivores feed on several related trees. Repeating the calculation with sampled distributions of specificity and the uncertainty of every factor, Hamilton and colleagues (2010) obtained a median of about 6 million species of tropical arthropods, and the review of Stork (2018) arrives at about 5.5 million species of insects (range 2.6 to 7.8 million) and 7 million of terrestrial arthropods.

The second approach uses ratios between groups: if the ratio of insect to vascular-plant species, or of undescribed to described species in a well-studied region such as Britain, is assumed to hold elsewhere, the global total of a poorly known group can be estimated from that of a well-known one. The third approach uses the history of description itself. The higher-taxon method of Mora and colleagues (2011) rests on the observation that the discovery of higher taxa (phyla, classes, orders, families, genera) is nearly complete: the cumulative number of families described per year has flattened, and its asymptote can be estimated by regression. The asymptotic totals of successive ranks increase in a regular way, so the relation can be extrapolated one rank further, to species (see the figure below).

![Schematic of the higher-taxon method of Mora et al. 2011 (all values illustrative). (A) The cumulative number of described taxa of one higher rank, here families, levels off over the history of description (points), and an asymptotic regression (dashed curve) gives the number expected when description is complete. (B) The logarithms of the asymptotic numbers of phyla, classes, orders, families and genera lie close to a straight line when plotted against rank; extending the line one step (dashed) predicts the number of species. The published estimate for animals is about 7.8 million species, of which about 12% had been catalogued](./plots/higher-taxon-extrapolation.py)

The method predicted about 8.7 million (± 1.3 million) eukaryotic species, of which about 7.8 million are animals, and concluded that some 86% of species on land and 91% in the sea remain undescribed. Its weakness is the same as its strength: it extrapolates the behavior of taxonomists. Groups in which higher taxa are defined finely relative to species (vertebrates) and groups in which they are coarse (mites, nematodes, parasitic wasps) do not follow one rule, and cryptic species distinguishable only by DNA, which molecular surveys keep revealing, are not anticipated at all. Most estimates of the total number of animal species therefore lie between 5 and 10 million, with arguments for considerably more if cryptic species, mites and host-specific parasites are fully counted.

At the present rate of description, naming even the lower estimates would take several centuries, while habitat loss is removing species faster than they are found. This is the practical background to the work of taxonomy: a species that has no name cannot be monitored, protected or studied.

### 1.3.2. A Short History

Systematic zoology begins with Aristotle (384–322 BCE), whose *History of Animals* describes some 500 kinds of animals and divides them into animals with blood (roughly the vertebrates) and without blood (the invertebrates), subdivided by mode of reproduction and body structure; his observations on the development of the chick and the reproduction of cephalopods were not surpassed for two thousand years. Renaissance encyclopedists such as Conrad Gessner compiled what was known; the microscope revealed in the seventeenth century the protists, the cells and the minute anatomy of insects (Malpighi, Swammerdam, Leeuwenhoek).

Carl Linnaeus fixed the methods of classification. In the tenth edition of his *Systema Naturae* (1758) he named some 4,400 species of animals with binomials and arranged them in six classes: Mammalia, Aves, Amphibia, Pisces, Insecta and Vermes, the last a catch-all for everything else without a backbone. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck broke up the Vermes, introduced the term invertebrates and was the first to arrange animals in an explicit evolutionary series (1809). Georges Cuvier, founder of comparative anatomy and paleontology, divided the animals in 1812 into four *embranchements* on the basis of their body plans: Vertebrata, Mollusca, Articulata (segmented animals with jointed or soft bodies) and Radiata. Cuvier believed species to be fixed, but his insistence on the correlation of parts made the body plan the central concept of zoology.

The nineteenth century added embryology (Karl Ernst von Baer and the germ layers), the cell theory and, with Darwin's *Origin of Species* (1859), a genealogical meaning for classification. Ernst Haeckel drew the first trees of animal phyla (1866), coined the terms phylogeny and ecology and proposed the "biogenetic law" ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)); Berthold Hatschek (1888) divided the bilaterians into groups that correspond to the modern protostomes, deuterostomes and chordates. In the twentieth century Libbie Hyman's six-volume treatise *The Invertebrates* (1940–1967) organized the phyla by grade (acoelomate, pseudocoelomate, coelomate), and Willi Hennig's phylogenetic systematics (1950, 1966) made the recognition of clades by shared derived characters the method of classification. From 1988 on, sequences of 18S rRNA and then whole transcriptomes and genomes rearranged the animal tree: the Ecdysozoa (1997), the Spiralia and the positions of ctenophores and xenacoelomorphs are discoveries of molecular phylogenetics ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)).

In China, knowledge of animals is ancient: silk moths and domestic animals were reared in prehistoric times, the dictionary *Erya* (third century BCE or earlier) has chapters on insects, fishes, birds, wild and domestic mammals, and the *Rites of Zhou* divide animals into five kinds, the furred, feathered, shelled, scaled and naked. Li Shizhen's *Compendium of Materia Medica* (completed 1578) describes more than 400 medicinal animals, grouped as insects, scaled animals, shelled animals, birds and beasts. Modern zoology arrived in the early twentieth century: Bing Zhi founded the Biological Laboratory of the Science Society of China in Nanjing in 1922 and, with Hu Xiansu, the Fan Memorial Institute of Biology in Beijing in 1928, and the Chinese Society of Zoology dates from 1934. Since the 1950s Chinese zoologists have documented the national fauna in the multivolume *Fauna Sinica*, and Chinese fossil sites, from the Cambrian Chengjiang biota (found in 1984) to the feathered dinosaurs of the Jehol biota, have become central to the study of animal evolution.

### 1.3.3. Methods

The basic method of zoology is description: careful observation of external form, internal anatomy, life history and behavior, recorded in words, drawings and measurements and anchored to preserved specimens in collections. Comparison of descriptions across species reveals homologies and shared characters and is the basis of classification and of comparative anatomy. Experiment tests explanations under controlled conditions, as in the experimental embryology of the late nineteenth century or the physiology of the twentieth.

Modern methods extend all three. Electron microscopy resolved cilia, cuticles and cell junctions; confocal microscopy of stained or transgenic specimens shows nerves and muscles in whole small animals; micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) images the anatomy of a specimen, even a fossil or a museum type, in three dimensions without dissection. DNA sequences are now routine characters: a standard fragment of the mitochondrial *COI* gene serves as a "DNA barcode" for identifying species (Hebert and colleagues, 2003), environmental DNA in water or soil reveals which animals live in a place, and phylogenomic data sets of hundreds of genes resolve relationships among phyla.

## 1.4. Classification and Nomenclature

### 1.4.1. Ranks

Animal classification uses the same hierarchy of **taxonomic ranks** as plant classification ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.2)): kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species, with intermediate ranks formed with the prefixes super-, sub- and infra- (superfamily, subclass, infraorder) and additional ranks such as cohort and tribe where needed. Two familiar species show the hierarchy.

| Rank | Wolf | Western honey bee |
|---|---|---|
| Kingdom | Animalia | Animalia |
| Phylum | Chordata | Arthropoda |
| Class | Mammalia | Insecta |
| Order | Carnivora | Hymenoptera |
| Superfamily | (not used) | Apoidea |
| Family | Canidae | Apidae |
| Subfamily | Caninae | Apinae |
| Tribe | Canini | Apini |
| Genus | *Canis* | *Apis* |
| Species | *Canis lupus* Linnaeus, 1758 | *Apis mellifera* Linnaeus, 1758 |

Names of family-group taxa have fixed endings added to the stem of the type genus: superfamily -oidea, family -idae, subfamily -inae, tribe -ini, subtribe -ina (Apoidea, Apidae, Apinae, Apini from *Apis*). Names above the family group are not regulated and have no fixed endings, although conventions exist (insect orders in -ptera, fish orders in -iformes, bird orders in -formes). Ranks are conventions: a family of beetles is not equivalent in age, diversity or disparity to a family of birds, and modern classifications increasingly name clades without assigning ranks.

### 1.4.2. The International Code of Zoological Nomenclature

**Definition 1.4.1 (International Code of Zoological Nomenclature)** The **International Code of Zoological Nomenclature** (ICZN) is the set of rules, adopted by the International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature and now in its fourth edition (1999, with later amendments), that governs the scientific names of animals in the family group, genus group and species group. Its aim is that every taxon has one valid name and that no name is used for two taxa; it regulates names, not the scientific judgment of which taxa to recognize.

The main principles are these.

- Binominal nomenclature: The name of a species is a combination of two names, the generic name and the specific name (*Canis lupus*), written in italics, the generic name capitalized; a subspecies adds a third name (*Passer montanus saturatus*). The Code speaks of "binominal" rather than **binomial nomenclature** to stress that each part is a single word.
- Starting point: Zoological nomenclature begins on 1 January 1758, the date assigned to the tenth edition of *Systema Naturae* (and to Clerck's work on Swedish spiders); earlier names are not available.
- Availability: A name becomes available only if it is published in a work that meets the Code's criteria, with a description or diagnosis (required since 1931) and, for new species since 2000, an explicitly designated name-bearing type. Since 2012 electronic-only publications qualify if they are registered in ZooBank, the official online register.
- Priority: Of two or more available names for the same taxon (synonyms), the valid name is the oldest; the **principle of priority** can be set aside only by the Commission, for example to conserve a name in long and universal use.
- Homonymy: The same name may not be used for two different taxa in the same group: a junior homonym must be replaced, even if the senior one is no longer in use.
- Typification: Every name is tied to a name-bearing type: a species to a **type specimen** (a **holotype**, or syntypes from which a lectotype may be chosen, or a neotype if the original types are lost), a genus to a type species, a family to a type genus.
- Coordination: A name established at one rank in a group is available with the same author and date at every other rank of that group: by publishing the family Apidae one has also established the subfamily Apinae and the superfamily Apoidea.

A species name is cited with its author and year (*Apis mellifera* Linnaeus, 1758). When the species is later moved to another genus, the author and year are enclosed in parentheses: the Eurasian tree sparrow was described as *Fringilla montana* and is now *Passer montanus* (Linnaeus, 1758).

### 1.4.3. Differences from the Botanical Code

Animals and plants are named under independent codes, so the same generic name may occur in both (*Aotus* is a genus of night monkeys and of Australian peas). The rules differ in many details ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.4)).

| Point | Zoological code (ICZN) | Botanical code (ICN) |
|---|---|---|
| Starting date | 1 January 1758 | 1 May 1753 (*Species Plantarum*) for most groups |
| Ranks regulated | Family group, genus group, species group only | All ranks up to division; endings fixed for family and above (-aceae, -ales, -phyta) |
| Family ending | -idae; subfamily -inae, tribe -ini | -aceae; subfamily -oideae, tribe -eae |
| Tautonyms (identical genus and species names) | Allowed (*Gorilla gorilla*, *Bufo bufo*) | Forbidden |
| Language of the diagnosis | Any language | Latin or English (since 2012) |
| Terms | Available name; valid name | Validly published name; correct name |
| Citation after transfer to a new genus | Original author in parentheses: (Linnaeus, 1758) | Original author in parentheses followed by the author of the new combination: (L.) Mill. |
| Subspecific ranks | Subspecies only | Subspecies, variety, form |
| Conservation of names | Decisions of the Commission, Official Lists | Lists approved by International Botanical Congresses, appendices of the Code |

## 1.5. The Plan of This Course

Animals have no single morphological system comparable to the classical systems of plant classification, because the body plans of the phyla are too different for one anatomy to describe them all. The course therefore follows three intertwined threads. The first is the phylogenetic framework: we read the animal tree from its root upward, and each chapter on a group begins by placing it on the tree of [7.17.6](/7.17.6) and ends with a comparison with the traditional classification of Chinese textbooks. The second is the evolution of the body plan: symmetry, germ layers, body cavities, segmentation, skeletons, larvae and nervous systems are defined in the general chapters 7.17.2–7.17.5 and then followed through the phyla. The third is the link between form, function and evolution: for each structure we ask what it is, what it does (with the physiology linked to [7.14](/7.14)) and where it comes from (with the developmental mechanisms linked to [7.5](/7.5)).

## Summary

- **Metazoa** (Definition 1.1.1) is a clade of opisthokont eukaryotes, the sister group of the choanoflagellates within Holozoa; its synapomorphies are multicellularity with differentiated cells, epithelia on a collagenous extracellular matrix, ingestive heterotrophy, a gametic life cycle with flagellated sperm, and an embryo that develops through cleavage and, in most animals, gastrulation.
- The former "Protozoa" are protists scattered across the eukaryote tree; **invertebrates** (Definition 1.1.2) are a paraphyletic group defined by the absence of a backbone.
- About 1.45 million animal species are described: 82% arthropods, more than two thirds hexapods. Estimates of the total, by extrapolation from samples (Example 1.2.1), by ratios or by the higher-taxon method of Mora and colleagues, mostly lie between 5 and 10 million.
- Zoology developed from Aristotle's description, Linnaeus's binomials and classes, Cuvier's body plans and comparative anatomy, through embryology, evolution and Hennig's phylogenetic systematics, to molecular phylogenetics.
- The **International Code of Zoological Nomenclature** (Definition 1.4.1) regulates names of the family, genus and species groups: binominal names from 1 January 1758, availability, priority, homonymy, name-bearing types and coordination. It differs from the botanical code in its starting date, family endings, tolerance of tautonyms and citation of authors.
- The course reads the animal tree from the root upward ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)), develops the body-plan concepts in 7.17.2–7.17.5, and treats vertebrates first as groups and then system by system.

## Questions

1. Why are sponges included in the Metazoa although they lack nerves, muscles and, in most species, a basal lamina, whereas colonial choanoflagellates are excluded?

   **Answer** Metazoa is defined by ancestry, not by a checklist: it is the clade descended from the common ancestor of sponges and the other animals. Molecular phylogenies place sponges inside this clade, and sponges share with other animals collagen, epithelia joined by junctions, a gametic life cycle with flagellated sperm and eggs, and embryos that develop through cleavage. Their lack of nerves and muscles is either primitive or a loss. Colonial choanoflagellates are multicellular in a simple sense, but they lie outside the clade as its sister group: their colonies arise by incomplete division of a single cell type and have no embryo or germ line.

2. Using Erwin's figures, by how much does the final estimate change if only 5% of the herbivorous beetles are specialists of one tree species, all else being equal?

   **Answer** If host-specific beetles scale with the assumed fraction, 163 specialist species per tree species fall to roughly $163 \times 5/13.5 \approx 60$; the other guilds contributed part of Erwin's 163, so the reduction is somewhat smaller in reality. With 60 specialists, $60 \times 50{,}000 = 3$ million canopy beetles, $3/0.4 = 7.5$ million canopy arthropods and, adding half again for the ground fauna, about 11 million tropical arthropods instead of 30 million. The estimate is proportional to the specificity factor, which is why later studies of host specificity reduced it so much.

3. A beetle described in 1801 as *Carabus nitens* Smith was later transferred to the genus *Notiophilus*, and in 1810 another author described the same species as *Notiophilus splendens* Jones (hypothetical names). Which name is valid and how is it cited?

   **Answer** By the principle of priority the older available name wins, so the species is called *Notiophilus nitens*, and because it was originally described in another genus the author and year are put in parentheses: *Notiophilus nitens* (Smith, 1801). *N. splendens* Jones, 1810 is a junior subjective synonym. Under the botanical code the citation would additionally name the author who made the new combination.

4. Why does the higher-taxon method risk underestimating the number of species in groups such as mites or parasitic wasps?

   **Answer** The method assumes that the ratio between the numbers of taxa at successive ranks follows one regular relation across groups. In poorly studied hyperdiverse groups, families and genera are broad and few relative to the species they contain, and many genera are themselves still undiscovered, so the asymptotes of the higher ranks are underestimated and the extrapolation to species is too low. Cryptic species that differ only genetically add to the shortfall, because the method extrapolates the history of morphological description.
