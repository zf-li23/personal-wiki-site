Zoology is the science of animals: their structure, development, function, behavior, classification and evolutionary history. Its subject is a single clade of eukaryotes, the Metazoa, which contains about one and a half million described living species, more than all other named organisms together, and probably several times as many that have not yet been named. This chapter first asks what an animal is and where animals sit on the **tree of life**; it then surveys how animal diversity is distributed among the phyla and how its unknown total is estimated, sketches the branches and history of zoology, introduces the rules by which animals are named, and closes with the plan of the course. The general ideas of phylogeny and classification were set out in evolutionary biology ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)) and the tools of taxonomy in botany ([7.12.2](/7.12.2)); here we take the zoological viewpoint and add only what differs. The phylogenetic framework that the whole course follows is drawn in [7.17.6](/7.17.6).

## 1.1. What Is an Animal

### 1.1.1. Animals Among the Eukaryotes

In the two-kingdom system of Linnaeus an animal was any organism that moves and ingests food. That criterion put amoebae, ciliates and euglenoids among the animals, and older textbooks of zoology accordingly opened with a phylum "Protozoa" of single-celled animals. Molecular phylogenies have since shown that these organisms belong to almost every branch of the eukaryote tree, and that the motile, food-ingesting way of life evolved many times ([7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.7.3), [7.11.20](/7.11.20)). The word "animal" is now reserved for a single clade, the multicellular animals or Metazoa; the former protozoa are **protists**, and how the traditional classes of protozoa map onto the modern supergroups is shown in [7.17.6](/7.17.6).

Within the eukaryotes, animals belong to the **opisthokonts**, the group whose motile cells, where present, are pushed by a single posterior flagellum: the sperm of animals and the zoospores of chytrid fungi are the clearest examples. The opisthokonts divide into Holomycota (fungi and the nucleariid amoebae) and Holozoa (animals and three groups of unicellular relatives: the **choanoflagellates**, the filastereans and the ichthyosporeans). Animals are thus more closely related to mushrooms than to any plant, and their closest living relatives are the choanoflagellates, microscopic cells with a collar of microvilli around one flagellum (see the figure below).

### 1.1.2. The Defining Features of Animals

**Definition 1.1.1 (Metazoa)** The **Metazoa** (animals) are the clade of opisthokont eukaryotes comprising the most recent common ancestor of sponges, ctenophores, placozoans, cnidarians and bilaterians and all its descendants. They are multicellular heterotrophs that, with few exceptions, ingest their food; their cells are bound by an extracellular matrix rich in collagen; they are diploid organisms whose only haploid cells are the gametes, a small motile sperm and a large egg; and the zygote develops through cleavage into an embryo whose cells sort into layers.

The definition is phylogenetic: Metazoa is the crown group of the animals, and the characters listed are its synapomorphies, the derived features present in the common ancestor. Several of them are better understood as a syndrome than as a list:

- Multicellularity with differentiated cells: Animals are among the few lineages with complex **multicellularity** ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5)): their bodies are built of many cell types in a reproducible arrangement, and only the germ line contributes to the next generation.
- Epithelia and the extracellular matrix: Animal cells are joined into sheets by specialized cell junctions and rest on an **extracellular matrix** whose fibrous protein is **collagen**; most animals have a **basal lamina** of type IV collagen and laminin beneath their epithelia ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)). The sponges are a partial exception: only the homoscleromorphs have a true basal lamina.
- Heterotrophy by ingestion: Animals eat other organisms and digest them, intracellularly in sponges and placozoans, extracellularly in a gut in most others. Some have secondarily given up feeding by mouth: tapeworms absorb nutrients through their surface, and the siboglinid tube worms of hydrothermal vents live on symbiotic bacteria.
- The gametic life cycle: Meiosis gives rise directly to gametes, so the multicellular body is always diploid; the sperm swims with a single posterior flagellum, the opisthokont inheritance.
- Development through an embryo: Cleavage of the zygote produces a **blastula**, and in all animals except sponges and placozoans **gastrulation** sorts the cells into two or three **germ layers** ([7.5.5](/7.5.5), [7.5.9](/7.5.9)). The developmental toolkit of animal genes (Wnt, TGF-β and Hedgehog signaling, the homeobox transcription factors including the **Hox genes** of most animals) was largely assembled at the origin of the Metazoa ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

None of these features is unique in isolation: fungi are opisthokont heterotrophs, many algae are multicellular, and choanoflagellates make cadherins and tyrosine kinases. What is unique is the combination, and the fact that the animal lineage built from it a body that moves through the world, senses it and acts on it with muscles and nerves (present in all animals except sponges and placozoans).

### 1.1.3. Invertebrates and Vertebrates

The first division of animals that most people learn, into animals with and without a backbone, goes back to Lamarck, who in 1801 grouped Linnaeus's insects and worms as *animaux sans vertèbres*.

**Definition 1.1.2 (Invertebrates)** The **invertebrates** are all animals that are not vertebrates: the 30 or so phyla other than Chordata, together with the two invertebrate subphyla of chordates, the cephalochordates (lancelets) and urochordates (tunicates). They form a **paraphyletic group**, because the vertebrates descend from an invertebrate ancestor but are excluded.

"Invertebrate" thus names a grade of organization and a division of labor among zoologists rather than a natural group. The asymmetry is extreme: vertebrates make up about 4% of described animal species, and an arthropod and a sea star are more closely related to each other than either is to a sponge. Throughout the course the invertebrate phyla are therefore treated in phylogenetic order rather than as a block opposed to the vertebrates.

### 1.1.4. Kingdoms, Domains and Supergroups

The boundary of the animal kingdom has been redrawn several times, and each redrawing changed what a zoology textbook had to cover. Chinese textbooks still present the succession of kingdom systems, so it is worth seeing how they relate to the current tree.

| System | Proposed by | Units | Place of animals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two kingdoms | Linnaeus 1735 | Plantae, Animalia | Every organism that moves and ingests food, including amoebae and ciliates |
| Three kingdoms | Hogg 1860; Haeckel 1866 | Protista (Protoctista), Plantae, Animalia | Single-celled organisms removed to Protista |
| Four kingdoms | Copeland 1938, 1956 | Monera, Protoctista, Plantae, Animalia | Multicellular animals only; prokaryotes separated |
| Five kingdoms | Whittaker 1969 | Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia | Multicellular eukaryotes that ingest their food |
| Six kingdoms | Chen Shixiang 1979 | Viruses; Bacteria, Cyanobacteria; Plantae, Fungi, Animalia (in three superkingdoms) | As in the five-kingdom system |
| Eight kingdoms | Cavalier-Smith 1989 | Eubacteria, Archaebacteria, Archezoa, Protozoa, Chromista, Plantae, Fungi, Animalia | As above |
| Three domains | Woese, Kandler and Wheelis 1990 | Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya | One lineage within Eukarya |
| Supergroups | Adl et al. 2019; Burki et al. 2020 | Unranked clades of eukaryotes (Amorphea, Diaphoretickes and others) | The clade Metazoa within Opisthokonta |

The *five-kingdom system* of Whittaker was the most influential of these. It combined two criteria: the level of organization (prokaryotic, unicellular eukaryotic, multicellular eukaryotic) and, among multicellular eukaryotes, the mode of nutrition, with plants as photosynthetic producers, fungi as absorptive decomposers and animals as ingestive consumers. Both criteria describe grades rather than clades. Protista is a **paraphyletic group** that contains the ancestors of the other three eukaryotic kingdoms, and ingestion of food is a way of life that evolved many times among protists; only its combination with complex multicellularity is unique to the animal clade.

Molecular phylogenies, beginning with the rRNA trees of Carl Woese, replaced this scheme with a tree of three domains, of which Eukarya is divided into a handful of supergroups ([7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.7.3), [7.11.20](/7.11.20)). The name Animalia survives and is now used strictly as a synonym of Metazoa. For the reader of older Chinese textbooks the practical consequence is simple: the "protozoa" treated at the start of those books are not animals, and the animal kingdom begins with the sponges, the first chapter on a phylum in this course ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)).

## 1.2. The Diversity of Animals

### 1.2.1. Described Species

About 1.45 million living animal species had been described by the early 2020s (see the figure below). They are very unevenly distributed. The arthropods alone account for 82%, and the insects and other hexapods for more than two thirds of all animals; arthropods, molluscs and chordates together make up 92%. Twenty-five of the roughly 34 phyla each contain less than 1% of known species, and some contain only a handful: two cycliophorans, one micrognathozoan, a few placozoan species. Small phyla are not unimportant: the sponges and cnidarians build reefs, the nematodes are the most numerous animals in soils and sediments, and several minute phyla hold the key to the early branching of the animal tree.

![Numbers of described living species in the animal phyla (logarithmic axis; the phylum Arthropoda is split into its four subphyla and Chordata into its three). Data: Brusca, Giribet and Moore 2023, Table 1.1 (1,453,163 species in total, compiled from Zhang 2011, 2013 and later counts). Bars are colored by the major clade to which the phylum belongs (see the animal tree in 7.17.6): non-bilaterian animals, Xenacoelomorpha, Spiralia, Ecdysozoa, Deuterostomia](./plots/species-by-phylum.py)

The distribution in space is uneven in a different way. Animals arose in the sea, and every phylum except the onychophorans has living marine members; about half the phyla are exclusively marine, among them the echinoderms, ctenophores, brachiopods, chaetognaths, hemichordates and the minute kinorhynchs and loriciferans. Only a few lineages have conquered dry land with a whole life cycle independent of free water: arthropods (above all insects, arachnids and myriapods), pulmonate snails, some annelids and nematodes, the tardigrades in their dormant states, and the amniote vertebrates. Most of the described diversity is terrestrial nonetheless, because the insects radiated on land together with the flowering plants.

Animals span about eight orders of magnitude in length, from loriciferans and coral-reef nematodes of less than 0.1 mm to blue whales of 30 m, and more than fifteen orders of magnitude in mass. Many phyla consist largely of meiofauna, animals that pass through a 1 mm sieve and live between grains of sediment; others include giants such as the giant squid (*Architeuthis*, about 13 m with its tentacles) and giant clams (*Tridacna*, over 200 kg).

### 1.2.2. How Many Species Are There

The number of described species is a measure of taxonomic effort as much as of nature. About 15,000 to 20,000 new animal species are described each year, mostly insects, and there is no sign of saturation: in samples of beetles from tropical canopies, of crustaceans and polychaetes from continental slopes or of mites from soils, most species are typically new to science. Estimating the total requires extrapolation, and the three main approaches give answers that differ by an order of magnitude.

The first approach scales up from an intensively sampled community. Its best-known example also started the modern debate.

**Example 1.2.1 (Erwin's Estimate)** In 1982 Terry Erwin fogged the canopies of 19 trees of *Luehea seemannii* in Panama with insecticide and collected about 1,200 species of beetles. He assumed that 13.5% of the herbivorous beetles (and smaller fractions of the other feeding guilds) were specialists of that tree species, which gave about 163 host-specific beetle species per tree species. Multiplying by about 50,000 species of tropical trees gave 8.15 million species of canopy beetles. Since beetles make up about 40% of the arthropod species in such samples, the tropical canopy would hold about 20 million arthropod species; with half as many again on the ground, the total came to 30 million species of tropical arthropods.

:::

Each factor in Erwin's product is uncertain, and later studies found the critical one, host specificity, to be much lower than assumed: many canopy herbivores feed on several related trees. Repeating the calculation with sampled distributions of specificity and the uncertainty of every factor, Hamilton and colleagues (2010) obtained a median of about 6 million species of tropical arthropods, and the review of Stork (2018) arrives at about 5.5 million species of insects (range 2.6 to 7.8 million) and 7 million of terrestrial arthropods.

The way such uncertainty accumulates in a product of factors can be made exact.

**Example 1.2.2 (Propagating the Uncertainty of a Product)** Erwin's estimate has the form

$$
N = \frac{T\,s}{b\,c}, \tag{1.1}
$$

where $T$ is the number of tropical tree species, $s$ the number of host-specific beetle species per tree species, $b$ the fraction of canopy arthropod species that are beetles and $c$ the fraction of tropical arthropod species that live in the canopy (Erwin used $T = 50{,}000$, $s = 163$, $b = 0.4$ and $c = 2/3$). Taking logarithms turns the product into a sum, $\ln N = \ln T + \ln s - \ln b - \ln c$. If the factors are uncertain and independent, the variances of their logarithms add,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\ln N) = \operatorname{Var}(\ln T) + \operatorname{Var}(\ln s) + \operatorname{Var}(\ln b) + \operatorname{Var}(\ln c),
$$

so $N$ is approximately log-normally distributed, its median is the product of the medians of the factors, and its spread is dominated by the factor whose logarithm is most variable. In practice one draws each factor at random from a distribution that covers its published range, computes $N$, and repeats the calculation many thousands of times (Monte Carlo simulation, see the figure below). Host specificity, which varies over an order of magnitude between studies, contributes most of the variance; the number of tree species, known within a factor of about 1.5, contributes little.

:::

(1.1) (schematic; the input distributions are illustrative ranges in the spirit of Hamilton et al. 2010, not their fitted values). Each of 100,000 runs draws the number of tree species $T$, the effective number of host-specific beetle species per tree species $s$, the beetle fraction $b$ and the canopy fraction $c$ at random and computes the total $N$. Hamilton and colleagues did exactly this, with distributions estimated from the literature for each factor, including the overlap of specialist beetles between related tree species that Erwin had ignored. The result was a median near 6 million with a 90% interval of roughly 4 to 11 million tropical arthropod species. The lesson is general: a precise-looking estimate built from several multiplied guesses is only as good as its most uncertain factor.

The second approach uses ratios between groups: if the ratio of insect to vascular-plant species, or of undescribed to described species in a well-studied region such as Britain, is assumed to hold elsewhere, the global total of a poorly known group can be estimated from that of a well-known one. The third approach uses the history of description itself. The higher-taxon method of Mora and colleagues (2011) rests on the observation that the discovery of higher taxa (phyla, classes, orders, families, genera) is nearly complete: the cumulative number of families described per year has flattened, and its asymptote can be estimated by regression. The asymptotic totals of successive ranks increase in a regular way, so the relation can be extrapolated one rank further, to species (see the figure below).

![Schematic of the higher-taxon method of Mora et al. 2011 (all values illustrative). (A) The cumulative number of described taxa of one higher rank, here families, levels off over the history of description (points), and an asymptotic regression (dashed curve) gives the number expected when description is complete. (B) The logarithms of the asymptotic numbers of phyla, classes, orders, families and genera lie close to a straight line when plotted against rank; extending the line one step (dashed) predicts the number of species. The published estimate for animals is about 7.8 million species, of which about 12% had been catalogued](./plots/higher-taxon-extrapolation.py)

The method predicted about 8.7 million (± 1.3 million) eukaryotic species, of which about 7.8 million are animals, and concluded that some 86% of species on land and 91% in the sea remain undescribed. Its weakness is the same as its strength: it extrapolates the behavior of taxonomists. Groups in which higher taxa are defined finely relative to species (vertebrates) and groups in which they are coarse (mites, nematodes, parasitic wasps) do not follow one rule, and cryptic species distinguishable only by DNA, which molecular surveys keep revealing, are not anticipated at all. Most estimates of the total number of animal species therefore lie between 5 and 10 million, with arguments for considerably more if cryptic species, mites and host-specific parasites are fully counted.

At the present rate of description, naming even the lower estimates would take several centuries, while habitat loss is removing species faster than they are found. This is the practical background to the work of taxonomy: a species that has no name cannot be monitored, protected or studied.

## 1.3. The Science of Zoology

### 1.3.1. Branches of Zoology

Chinese textbooks divide zoology by subject in a similar way but more finely. Animal morphology includes anatomy (the structure of organs and their relations), comparative anatomy (the comparison of organ systems among groups to reveal their evolutionary relationships), histology and cytology, and paleozoology, the study of fossil animals; taxonomy compares groups at every rank to infer their relationships; physiology studies the functions of organs and their responses to the environment, with endocrinology and immunology as offshoots; embryology, now enlarged into developmental biology, studies how the embryo forms; ecology studies animals and their environment at the levels of the individual, the population, the community and the ecosystem; zoogeography studies the distribution of species over the Earth; and animal genetics studies heredity and variation. The modern subject is organized as much by level as by discipline, from molecules and cells through tissues, organs and individuals to populations and ecosystems, and most research questions cross several levels.

Conservation biology, a synthetic field that grew out of ecology, genetics and systematics in the 1980s, studies how to protect species, genetic diversity and ecosystems and how to use biological resources sustainably. Its legal basis was laid by the Convention on Biological Diversity (1992), which defines biodiversity at the three levels of genes, species and ecosystems. Every one of these tasks depends on zoological knowledge: a species can be protected only if it can be identified, its habitat, food and breeding biology known, and its relationships to other species understood.

By group, zoologists speak of protozoology (now part of protistology), helminthology (parasitic worms), malacology (molluscs), carcinology (crustaceans), arachnology, entomology, ichthyology, herpetology (amphibians and reptiles), ornithology and mammalogy. Applied zoology includes parasitology and medical entomology, the control of agricultural pests, fisheries and aquaculture, sericulture and apiculture, and the conservation of animals and their habitats.

### 1.3.2. A Short History

Systematic zoology begins with Aristotle (384–322 BCE), whose *History of Animals* describes some 500 kinds of animals and divides them into animals with blood (roughly the vertebrates) and without blood (the invertebrates), subdivided by mode of reproduction and body structure; his observations on the development of the chick and the reproduction of cephalopods were not surpassed for two thousand years. Renaissance encyclopedists such as Conrad Gessner compiled what was known. Anatomy became an observational science with Andreas Vesalius's *De humani corporis fabrica* (1543), and William Harvey's demonstration of the circulation of the blood (1628) was the first great result of experimental physiology. The microscope revealed in the seventeenth century the cells (Robert Hooke, 1665), the protists and spermatozoa (Antonie van Leeuwenhoek) and the minute anatomy of insects: Marcello Malpighi described the silkworm, including the excretory tubules that bear his name, and Jan Swammerdam showed that the butterfly is already formed inside the pupa, so that metamorphosis is a development and not a transformation of one animal into another.

Carl Linnaeus fixed the methods of classification. In the tenth edition of his *Systema Naturae* (1758) he named some 4,400 species of animals with binomials and arranged them in six classes: Mammalia, Aves, Amphibia, Pisces, Insecta and Vermes, the last a catch-all for everything else without a backbone. Linnaeus used five ranks (class, order, genus, species and variety) and believed, like most naturalists of his time, that species were fixed. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck broke up the Vermes, introduced the term invertebrates and was the first to arrange animals in an explicit evolutionary series (*Philosophie zoologique*, 1809); his mechanism, the inheritance of characters acquired through use and disuse, has not survived, but his idea that species change has. Georges Cuvier, founder of comparative anatomy and paleontology, divided the animals in 1812 into four *embranchements* on the basis of their body plans: Vertebrata, Mollusca, Articulata (segmented animals with jointed or soft bodies) and Radiata. Cuvier believed species to be fixed and explained the succession of fossil faunas by catastrophes, but his principle of the correlation of parts, which allowed him to reconstruct an extinct animal from a few bones, made the body plan the central concept of zoology. His debate with Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire in 1830, over whether all animals share one plan of composition, anticipated the modern comparison of body plans across phyla, and Richard Owen gave the terms homology and analogy their present meanings in 1843.

The nineteenth century added embryology (Karl Ernst von Baer and the germ layers), the cell theory (Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann, 1838–1839) and, with Darwin's *Origin of Species* (1859), a genealogical meaning for classification. Ernst Haeckel drew the first trees of animal phyla (1866), coined the terms phylogeny and ecology and proposed the "biogenetic law" ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)); Berthold Hatschek (1888) divided the bilaterians into groups that correspond to the modern protostomes, deuterostomes and chordates. In the twentieth century Libbie Hyman's six-volume treatise *The Invertebrates* (1940–1967) organized the phyla by grade (acoelomate, pseudocoelomate, coelomate), and Willi Hennig's phylogenetic systematics (1950, 1966) made the recognition of clades by shared derived characters the method of classification. From 1988 on, sequences of 18S rRNA and then whole transcriptomes and genomes rearranged the animal tree: the Ecdysozoa (1997), the Spiralia and the positions of ctenophores and xenacoelomorphs are discoveries of molecular phylogenetics ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)).

Genetics entered zoology through Mendel's laws (1866, rediscovered in 1900) and Thomas Hunt Morgan's work on the fruit fly, which from 1910 placed genes on chromosomes. In the 1930s and 1940s the Modern Synthesis (Ronald Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane, Sewall Wright, Theodosius Dobzhansky, Ernst Mayr, George Gaylord Simpson) united genetics with natural selection, systematics and paleontology, and gave zoology the biological species concept ([7.10](/7.10)). Ethology, the comparative study of behavior, was founded by Konrad Lorenz, Niko Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch, who shared a Nobel Prize in 1973. The structure of DNA (Watson and Crick, 1953) opened the molecular era, and the discovery of the homeobox in 1984, shared by the developmental genes of flies and vertebrates, showed that very different body plans are built with the same genetic toolkit ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

In China, knowledge of animals is ancient: silk moths and domestic animals were reared in prehistoric times, the dictionary *Erya* (third century BCE or earlier) has chapters on insects, fishes, birds, wild and domestic mammals, and the *Rites of Zhou* divide animals into five kinds, the furred, feathered, shelled, scaled and naked. Li Shizhen's *Compendium of Materia Medica* (completed 1578) describes more than 400 medicinal animals, grouped as insects, scaled animals, shelled animals, birds and beasts. Modern zoology arrived in the early twentieth century: Bing Zhi founded the Biological Laboratory of the Science Society of China in Nanjing in 1922 and, with Hu Xiansu, the Fan Memorial Institute of Biology in Beijing in 1928, and the Chinese Society of Zoology dates from 1934. Since the 1950s Chinese zoologists have documented the national fauna in the multivolume *Fauna Sinica*, and Chinese fossil sites, from the Cambrian Chengjiang biota (found in 1984) to the feathered dinosaurs of the Jehol biota, have become central to the study of animal evolution.

Much early Chinese knowledge of animals was practical and is preserved in agricultural and medical works. The calendar *Xia Xiaozheng*, traditionally attributed to the Xia dynasty and preserved in a Han compilation, records month by month the appearance of animals such as mayflies and the retreat of ants into their nests; the *Book of Songs* mentions more than a hundred animals. Ji Han's *Nanfang Caomu Zhuang* (304 CE) describes how orange growers in the south bought nests of a predatory ant to protect their trees from insect pests, the oldest known record of the deliberate use of one animal to control another (the weaver ant *Oecophylla smaragdina* is still used in this way). Jia Sixie's *Qimin Yaoshu* (about 544 CE) gives detailed instructions for raising livestock, poultry and fish and for sericulture. In the twentieth century, besides the founding institutions named above, Chinese zoologists contributed classical experimental work, such as Tong Dizhou's nuclear-transfer experiments on fish and amphibian eggs in the 1960s and 1970s, and theoretical schemes such as the six-kingdom system of the entomologist Chen Shixiang (Section 1.1.4).

### 1.3.3. Methods

The basic method of zoology is description: careful observation of external form, internal anatomy, life history and behavior, recorded in words, drawings and measurements and anchored to preserved specimens in collections. Comparison of descriptions across species reveals homologies and shared characters and is the basis of classification and of comparative anatomy. Experiment tests explanations under controlled conditions, as in the experimental embryology of the late nineteenth century or the physiology of the twentieth.

Modern methods extend all three. Electron microscopy resolved cilia, cuticles and cell junctions; confocal microscopy of stained or transgenic specimens shows nerves and muscles in whole small animals; micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) images the anatomy of a specimen, even a fossil or a museum type, in three dimensions without dissection. DNA sequences are now routine characters: a standard fragment of the mitochondrial *COI* gene serves as a "DNA barcode" for identifying species (Hebert and colleagues, 2003), environmental DNA in water or soil reveals which animals live in a place, and phylogenomic data sets of hundreds of genes resolve relationships among phyla.

Chinese textbooks name three basic methods, and the distinction remains useful.

| Method | What it does | Classical tools | Modern tools |
|---|---|---|---|
| Description | Records the external form, internal structure, habits and uses of animals, in words, drawings and measurements | Dissection, light microscopy, illustration, museum specimens | Electron microscopy, confocal imaging, micro-CT, genome sequencing |
| Comparison | Seeks similarities and differences among animals to establish groups and their relationships | Comparative anatomy and embryology | Karyotypes, protein and DNA sequences, morphometrics, phylogenetic analysis |
| Experiment | Observes living animals under controlled conditions to test explanations | Ablation, transplantation, physiological recording | Isotope tracers, gene knockout and genome editing, tracking devices in the field |

Two institutions underlie all three methods. Natural history collections hold the preserved specimens on which names, descriptions and comparisons rest, including the type specimens of every species (Section 1.4.2); the largest museums hold tens of millions of specimens, and with their records of where and when each animal was collected they also document how distributions have changed. Field stations and long-term surveys provide the corresponding record of living populations. Modern species descriptions increasingly combine several kinds of evidence, morphology, DNA, behavior and geography, an approach called integrative taxonomy.

DNA barcoding shows how a molecular method becomes quantitative.

**Example 1.3.1 (The Barcode Gap)** Identification by a DNA barcode works if sequences from individuals of the same species differ less than sequences from different species. For each pair of sequences one computes a genetic distance, the proportion of differing sites corrected for multiple substitutions (often by the Kimura two-parameter model). If the distribution of intraspecific distances lies entirely below that of distances to the nearest other species, there is a *barcode gap*, and an unknown specimen can be assigned to the species whose sequences it most resembles. In many well-studied groups of animals intraspecific divergence in *COI* is usually below 1% and divergence between congeneric species usually above 2–3%; Hebert and colleagues (2004) proposed that a divergence more than ten times the mean intraspecific value signals a probable undescribed species. The gap is not universal: recently diverged species, hybridizing species and species with mitochondrial introgression overlap, and deeply divergent lineages within one named species may be cryptic species or merely old populations (see the figure below).

### 1.3.4. Zoology and Human Affairs

Zoology is a basic science, but few sciences have as many direct applications. The main ones can be grouped as follows.

- Resources and conservation: Animals supply food, fiber and medicine, and many species, from the giant panda to the crested ibis, depend on conservation programs that require knowledge of their habitat, diet and breeding. Because extinction is irreversible, zoological surveys of what lives where are the first step of any conservation policy.
- Agriculture and husbandry: Pest control relies on the life histories of the pests, which tell when they are most vulnerable, and on their natural enemies: the egg parasitoids of the genus *Trichogramma*, which lay their eggs in the eggs of moths such as the cotton bollworm, are mass-reared and released in China and elsewhere. Pheromone traps and the **sterile insect technique**, by which the New World screwworm fly was eliminated from North and Central America, came from the study of insect behavior and reproduction ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)). Insects pollinate a large share of crops, and the rearing of livestock, poultry, fish, honey bees and silkworms rests on their anatomy and physiology.
- Medicine: Parasites and vectors are animals. The five parasitic diseases that were once the main public-health problems of rural China (malaria, schistosomiasis, filariasis, visceral leishmaniasis and hookworm disease) were brought under control by interrupting the life cycles of the parasites, which required knowing their **intermediate hosts** and **vectors**: the snail *Oncomelania* for the blood fluke, mosquitoes for malaria and filariasis, sand flies for leishmaniasis. Animals are also a source of drugs: the anticoagulant hirudin from the medicinal leech, the blood-pressure drug captopril, developed from peptides in the venom of the Brazilian pit viper *Bothrops jararaca*, the analgesic ziconotide from the venom of a cone snail, the diabetes drug exenatide from the saliva of the Gila monster, and fibrinolytic enzymes from earthworms used in Chinese medicine. Animal models in the laboratory, from fruit flies to mice, are the basis of experimental medicine.
- Industry and technology: Silk (from the domestic silkworm *Bombyx mori* and the Chinese tussah moth *Antheraea pernyi*), wool, leather, pearls, shellac from the lac insect *Kerria lacca* and the white wax of the scale insect *Ericerus pela* are animal products. Biomimetics copies animal designs: the nose of a Japanese high-speed train was reshaped after the beak of the kingfisher to reduce the pressure wave in tunnels, and dry adhesives have been modeled on the setae of gecko feet.

## 1.4. Classification and Nomenclature

### 1.4.1. Ranks

Animal classification uses the same hierarchy of **taxonomic ranks** as plant classification ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.2)): kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species, with intermediate ranks formed with the prefixes super-, sub- and infra- (superfamily, subclass, infraorder) and additional ranks such as cohort and tribe where needed. Two familiar species show the hierarchy.

| Rank | Wolf | Western honey bee |
|---|---|---|
| Kingdom | Animalia | Animalia |
| Phylum | Chordata | Arthropoda |
| Class | Mammalia | Insecta |
| Order | Carnivora | Hymenoptera |
| Superfamily | (not used) | Apoidea |
| Family | Canidae | Apidae |
| Subfamily | Caninae | Apinae |
| Tribe | Canini | Apini |
| Genus | *Canis* | *Apis* |
| Species | *Canis lupus* Linnaeus, 1758 | *Apis mellifera* Linnaeus, 1758 |

Names of family-group taxa have fixed endings added to the stem of the type genus: superfamily -oidea, family -idae, subfamily -inae, tribe -ini, subtribe -ina (Apoidea, Apidae, Apinae, Apini from *Apis*). Names above the family group are not regulated and have no fixed endings, although conventions exist (insect orders in -ptera, fish orders in -iformes, bird orders in -formes). Ranks are conventions: a family of beetles is not equivalent in age, diversity or disparity to a family of birds, and modern classifications increasingly name clades without assigning ranks.

Chinese textbooks distinguish three schools of classification that arose in the twentieth century ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)). Numerical taxonomy (phenetics) groups organisms by overall similarity computed from many unweighted characters; its statistical methods survive, but similarity alone confuses shared ancestral characters with shared derived ones. Evolutionary systematics, the school of Mayr and Simpson, groups organisms by descent but also by degree of divergence, and so accepts **paraphyletic groups** such as "reptiles" that exclude a strongly divergent descendant (the birds). Cladistics, founded by Hennig, recognizes only clades, defined by **synapomorphies**. Current zoological classification is essentially cladistic: a named group should be a clade, and the traditional groups that are not, such as Protozoa, Invertebrata, Pisces and Reptilia in the old sense, are kept, if at all, as informal names for grades. Throughout this course each chapter therefore ends with a comparison between the clades used here and the groups of the traditional classification.

### 1.4.2. The International Code of Zoological Nomenclature

**Definition 1.4.1 (International Code of Zoological Nomenclature)** The **International Code of Zoological Nomenclature** (ICZN) is the set of rules, adopted by the International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature and now in its fourth edition (1999, with later amendments), that governs the scientific names of animals in the family group, genus group and species group. Its aim is that every taxon has one valid name and that no name is used for two taxa; it regulates names, not the scientific judgment of which taxa to recognize.

The main principles are these.

- Binominal nomenclature: The name of a species is a combination of two names, the generic name and the specific name (*Canis lupus*), written in italics, the generic name capitalized; a subspecies adds a third name (*Passer montanus saturatus*). The Code speaks of "binominal" rather than **binomial nomenclature** to stress that each part is a single word.
- Starting point: Zoological nomenclature begins on 1 January 1758, the date assigned to the tenth edition of *Systema Naturae* (and to Clerck's work on Swedish spiders); earlier names are not available.
- Availability: A name becomes available only if it is published in a work that meets the Code's criteria, with a description or diagnosis (required since 1931) and, for new species since 2000, an explicitly designated name-bearing type. Since 2012 electronic-only publications qualify if they are registered in ZooBank, the official online register.
- Priority: Of two or more available names for the same taxon (synonyms), the valid name is the oldest; the **principle of priority** can be set aside only by the Commission, for example to conserve a name in long and universal use.
- Homonymy: The same name may not be used for two different taxa in the same group: a junior homonym must be replaced, even if the senior one is no longer in use.
- Typification: Every name is tied to a name-bearing type: a species to a **type specimen** (a **holotype**, or syntypes from which a lectotype may be chosen, or a neotype if the original types are lost), a genus to a type species, a family to a type genus.
- Coordination: A name established at one rank in a group is available with the same author and date at every other rank of that group: by publishing the family Apidae one has also established the subfamily Apinae and the superfamily Apoidea.

A species name is cited with its author and year (*Apis mellifera* Linnaeus, 1758). When the species is later moved to another genus, the author and year are enclosed in parentheses: the Eurasian tree sparrow was described as *Fringilla montana* and is now *Passer montanus* (Linnaeus, 1758).

Several conventions of everyday practice follow from these rules. The specific name must agree in grammatical gender with the generic name, so its ending may change when a species is moved (*Fringilla montana* became *Passer montanus*). After its first mention a generic name may be abbreviated (*P. montanus*). A subgenus is written in parentheses between genus and species, as in the yellow-fever mosquito *Aedes* (*Stegomyia*) *aegypti*. A specimen identified only to genus is written *Rana* sp. (several species: spp.); "cf." marks a tentative identification and "aff." a probably new species close to a named one. A name published without a description is a *nomen nudum* and is not available. Names above the genus are capitalized and not italicized (Apidae, Insecta). Common names, in Chinese as in English, are not regulated by the Code, which is one reason why the same Chinese name can refer to different animals in different regions and why scientific names are used in this course for every species.

A famous case shows priority and synonymy at work. The sauropod dinosaur described by O. C. Marsh as *Apatosaurus* in 1877 and the one he named *Brontosaurus* in 1879 were long treated as the same genus, so the older name *Apatosaurus* had priority and *Brontosaurus* became a junior synonym, although it remained the more popular name. A large phylogenetic analysis in 2015 concluded that the two differ enough to be separate genera, and *Brontosaurus* was reinstated: the Code decides which name a taxon must bear, but whether two specimens belong to one taxon is a scientific judgment that can change.

### 1.4.3. Differences from the Botanical Code

Animals and plants are named under independent codes, so the same generic name may occur in both (*Aotus* is a genus of night monkeys and of Australian peas). The rules differ in many details ([7.12.2](/7.12.2#sec-2.4)).

| Point | Zoological code (ICZN) | Botanical code (ICN) |
|---|---|---|
| Starting date | 1 January 1758 | 1 May 1753 (*Species Plantarum*) for most groups |
| Ranks regulated | Family group, genus group, species group only | All ranks up to division; endings fixed for family and above (-aceae, -ales, -phyta) |
| Family ending | -idae; subfamily -inae, tribe -ini | -aceae; subfamily -oideae, tribe -eae |
| Tautonyms (identical genus and species names) | Allowed (*Gorilla gorilla*, *Bufo bufo*) | Forbidden |
| Language of the diagnosis | Any language | Latin or English (since 2012) |
| Terms | Available name; valid name | Validly published name; correct name |
| Citation after transfer to a new genus | Original author in parentheses: (Linnaeus, 1758) | Original author in parentheses followed by the author of the new combination: (L.) Mill. |
| Subspecific ranks | Subspecies only | Subspecies, variety, form |
| Conservation of names | Decisions of the Commission, Official Lists | Lists approved by International Botanical Congresses, appendices of the Code |

### 1.4.4. Species

The species is the basic unit of classification, and the only rank that most zoologists regard as a real entity in nature rather than a convention. Defining it has nevertheless proved difficult, and several species concepts coexist ([7.10](/7.10)):

| Concept | A species is | Strength | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phenetic species concept** (morphological) | A group of organisms distinguishable from others by a constant set of characters | Applicable to museum specimens and fossils | Sexual dimorphism, polymorphism and cryptic species |
| **Biological species concept** (Mayr 1942) | A group of interbreeding natural populations reproductively isolated from other such groups | Explains why species are discrete | Inapplicable to asexual organisms and fossils; hard to test for allopatric populations |
| Evolutionary species concept (Simpson, Wiley) | A lineage of ancestral and descendant populations with its own evolutionary tendencies and fate | Includes fossils and asexual lineages | Hard to apply operationally |
| **Phylogenetic species concept** (Cracraft) | The smallest diagnosable cluster of individuals with a pattern of ancestry and descent | Operational with molecular data | Tends to split populations into many species |
| **Ecological species concept** (Van Valen) | A lineage occupying a distinct adaptive zone | Explains coexistence of asexual lineages | Niches are hard to delimit |

Most working zoologists use these concepts as complementary criteria for one underlying idea, that a species is a separately evolving lineage of populations (the **general lineage concept** of de Queiroz 2007). In practice species are usually described from morphology, and the description is tested by other evidence. The willow and alder flycatchers of North America (*Empidonax traillii* and *E. alnorum*) are nearly identical in plumage and were long treated as one species, until it was found that they sing different songs, breed in different habitats and do not interbreed where their ranges meet; they were separated in 1973.

The only rank below the species recognized by the zoological Code is the subspecies, a geographical population that differs consistently from other populations of the species but interbreeds with them where they meet. Breeds of domestic animals are not taxa and are not named under the Code.

### 1.4.5. How Many Phyla

How many phyla there are depends on classification, and the number has changed with every new phylogeny. Chinese textbooks of the 2000s list 36 phyla; recent classifications recognize about 32 to 34. The differences arise from three kinds of change: groups removed from the animals, phyla absorbed into others when molecular trees placed them inside an existing phylum, and new phyla split off or discovered.

| Traditional phylum | Current treatment | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Protozoa | Not animals; protists in several supergroups | [7.11.20](/7.11.20) |
| Mesozoa | Two separate spiralian lineages, Dicyemida and Orthonectida | [7.17.12](/7.17.12) |
| Myxozoa | Highly reduced parasitic cnidarians | [7.17.9](/7.17.9) |
| Coelenterata (Cnidaria + Ctenophora) | Two unrelated phyla, Cnidaria and Ctenophora | [7.17.8](/7.17.8), [7.17.9](/7.17.9) |
| Acoela within Platyhelminthes | Phylum Xenacoelomorpha (with *Xenoturbella* and nemertodermatids) | [7.17.10](/7.17.10) |

| Acanthocephala | Parasitic rotifers, within Rotifera (Syndermata) | [7.17.12](/7.17.12) |

Phylum, like every rank above the species, is partly a convention. What the modern tree adds is that phyla are no longer simply "the largest groups with a distinct body plan": each is expected to be a clade, and the relationships between phyla, which morphology could not resolve, are now known for most of them ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)).

## 1.5. The Plan of This Course

Animals have no single morphological system comparable to the classical systems of plant classification, because the body plans of the phyla are too different for one anatomy to describe them all. The course therefore follows three intertwined threads. ## Summary

- **Metazoa** (Definition 1.1.1) is a clade of opisthokont eukaryotes, the sister group of the choanoflagellates within Holozoa; its synapomorphies are multicellularity with differentiated cells, epithelia on a collagenous extracellular matrix, ingestive heterotrophy, a gametic life cycle with flagellated sperm, and an embryo that develops through cleavage and, in most animals, gastrulation.
- The former "Protozoa" are protists scattered across the eukaryote tree; **invertebrates** (Definition 1.1.2) are a paraphyletic group defined by the absence of a backbone.
- About 1.45 million animal species are described: 82% arthropods, more than two thirds hexapods. Estimates of the total, by extrapolation from samples (Example 1.2.1), by ratios or by the higher-taxon method of Mora and colleagues, mostly lie between 5 and 10 million. The uncertainty of a product of factors is dominated by its most uncertain factor (Example 1.2.2, Eq. (1.1)).
- The kingdom systems (two, three, four, five, six and eight kingdoms) classified by grade; they have been replaced by three domains and the supergroups of eukaryotes, within which Animalia is synonymous with Metazoa.
- Zoology developed from Aristotle's description, Linnaeus's binomials and classes, Cuvier's body plans and comparative anatomy, through embryology, evolution, genetics and the Modern Synthesis and Hennig's phylogenetic systematics, to molecular phylogenetics; Chinese zoology has a long practical tradition, from the *Erya* to the first recorded biological control (304 CE) and the *Compendium of Materia Medica*.
- Description, comparison and experiment are the basic methods; DNA barcoding works where there is a barcode gap (Example 1.3.1). Zoology underpins conservation, pest control, parasitology and medicine, and the use of animal products and designs.
- The **International Code of Zoological Nomenclature** (Definition 1.4.1) regulates names of the family, genus and species groups: binominal names from 1 January 1758, availability, priority, homonymy, name-bearing types and coordination. It differs from the botanical code in its starting date, family endings, tolerance of tautonyms and citation of authors.
- Species are described mostly by morphology and tested by the biological, phylogenetic and other species concepts, which are complementary criteria for a separately evolving lineage; the subspecies is the only lower rank. Of the 36 phyla of older textbooks, several have been removed (Protozoa), merged into others (Echiura, Pogonophora, Sipuncula, Myxozoa, Acanthocephala) or split (Coelenterata, Mesozoa).
- The course reads the animal tree from the root upward ([7.17.6](/7.17.6)), develops the body-plan concepts in 7.17.2–7.17.5, and treats vertebrates first as groups and then system by system.

## Questions

1. Why are sponges included in the Metazoa although they lack nerves, muscles and, in most species, a basal lamina, whereas colonial choanoflagellates are excluded?

   **Answer** Metazoa is defined by ancestry, not by a checklist: it is the clade descended from the common ancestor of sponges and the other animals. Molecular phylogenies place sponges inside this clade, and sponges share with other animals collagen, epithelia joined by junctions, a gametic life cycle with flagellated sperm and eggs, and embryos that develop through cleavage. Their lack of nerves and muscles is either primitive or a loss. Colonial choanoflagellates are multicellular in a simple sense, but they lie outside the clade as its sister group: their colonies arise by incomplete division of a single cell type and have no embryo or germ line.

2. Using Erwin's figures, by how much does the final estimate change if only 5% of the herbivorous beetles are specialists of one tree species, all else being equal?

   **Answer** If host-specific beetles scale with the assumed fraction, 163 specialist species per tree species fall to roughly $163 \times 5/13.5 \approx 60$; the other guilds contributed part of Erwin's 163, so the reduction is somewhat smaller in reality. With 60 specialists, $60 \times 50{,}000 = 3$ million canopy beetles, $3/0.4 = 7.5$ million canopy arthropods and, adding half again for the ground fauna, about 11 million tropical arthropods instead of 30 million. The estimate is proportional to the specificity factor, which is why later studies of host specificity reduced it so much.

3. A beetle described in 1801 as *Carabus nitens* Smith was later transferred to the genus *Notiophilus*, and in 1810 another author described the same species as *Notiophilus splendens* Jones (hypothetical names). Which name is valid and how is it cited?

   **Answer** By the principle of priority the older available name wins, so the species is called *Notiophilus nitens*, and because it was originally described in another genus the author and year are put in parentheses: *Notiophilus nitens* (Smith, 1801). *N. splendens* Jones, 1810 is a junior subjective synonym. Under the botanical code the citation would additionally name the author who made the new combination.

4. Why does the higher-taxon method risk underestimating the number of species in groups such as mites or parasitic wasps?

   **Answer** The method assumes that the ratio between the numbers of taxa at successive ranks follows one regular relation across groups. In poorly studied hyperdiverse groups, families and genera are broad and few relative to the species they contain, and many genera are themselves still undiscovered, so the asymptotes of the higher ranks are underestimated and the extrapolation to species is too low. Cryptic species that differ only genetically add to the shortfall, because the method extrapolates the history of morphological description.

5. In the Monte Carlo analysis of Example 1.2.2, suppose that $\ln T$ has a standard deviation of 0.1, $\ln b$ and $\ln c$ of 0.2 each and $\ln s$ of 0.8. What fraction of the variance of $\ln N$ comes from host specificity, and by how much would the 90% interval of $N$ shrink if host specificity were known exactly?

   **Answer** The variances add: $0.01 + 0.04 + 0.04 + 0.64 = 0.73$, of which $0.64/0.73 \approx 88\%$ comes from $s$. The 90% interval of a log-normal variable spans a factor $\mathrm{e}^{2 \times 1.645\,\sigma}$. With $\sigma = \sqrt{0.73} \approx 0.85$ this factor is about 16; without the contribution of $s$, $\sigma = \sqrt{0.09} = 0.3$ and the factor is about 2.7. Measuring host specificity well is therefore worth far more than counting tree species more precisely.

6. Two populations of a beetle on neighboring islands differ by 4% in their *COI* barcodes but cannot be distinguished morphologically. Under which species concepts would they be separate species, and what further evidence would you seek?

   **Answer** Under the phylogenetic species concept they may be distinct species if each is diagnosable, for example by fixed differences in the barcode, and forms a monophyletic cluster. Under the phenetic concept they are one species, because they are morphologically indistinguishable. The biological species concept cannot be tested directly in allopatry; one would test whether they interbreed in the laboratory or where they meet, and look for differences in nuclear genes (mitochondrial divergence alone can reflect old geographic structure within one species), in behavior such as mating signals, and in ecology. If several independent lines of evidence agree, the populations are best described as cryptic species.
