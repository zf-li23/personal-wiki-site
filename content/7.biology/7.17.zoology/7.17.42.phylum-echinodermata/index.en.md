Echinoderms (sea lilies and feather stars, sea stars, brittle stars, sea urchins and sand dollars, sea cucumbers) are the only animals whose adults are built on a fivefold radial plan. They have about 7,300 living species, all marine, and a fossil record of some 13,000 species that begins in the early Cambrian; in parts of the deep sea they are the most abundant large animals on the bottom. Their body is assembled from features found nowhere else: a skeleton of porous plates each of which behaves as a single crystal of calcite, a system of seawater-filled canals that operates hundreds of hydraulic tube feet, and a connective tissue that can change from stiff to soft within seconds under nervous control. Yet echinoderms are deuterostomes, and their larvae are bilaterally symmetrical animals much like the tornaria of acorn worms ([7.17.41](/7.17.41)). How a bilateral larva turns itself into a pentaradial adult is the key to the whole phylum.

This chapter first describes the body plan (§42.1), the skeleton and the mutable connective tissue (§42.2) and the water vascular system (§42.3). Section 42.4 treats the other organ systems, §42.5 development, larvae and metamorphosis, and §42.6 the five living classes, with the sea star *Asterias* as the representative animal. The chapter ends with the fossil record and the origin of pentaradiality (§42.7), echinoderms in ecology and human use (§42.8) and a comparison with the traditional classification (§42.9). Pentaradial symmetry was introduced in [7.17.3](/7.17.3), the dipleurula larva in [7.17.5](/7.17.5); the sea urchin embryo as a model of development is treated in [7.5.5](/7.5.5#sec-5.4.1) and [7.5.6](/7.5.6#sec-6.5).

## 42.1. The Echinoderm Body Plan

**Definition 42.1.1 (Echinodermata)** The **Echinodermata** (echinoderms) are marine deuterostomes whose adults have a pentaradial symmetry derived from a bilaterally symmetrical larva, an endoskeleton of mesodermal ossicles made of stereom (a porous meshwork of magnesian calcite filled with living tissue), a water vascular system derived from the left mesocoel (hydrocoel) and expressed externally as tube feet, and mutable collagenous tissue in the body wall; they have no excretory organs and no centralized brain.

### 42.1.1. Pentaradial Symmetry

In an adult echinoderm the body is arranged around an oral–aboral axis. Five radii, the ambulacra, run from the mouth toward the aboral pole and carry the radial canals of the water vascular system, the radial nerves and the rows of tube feet; between them lie five interambulacra. In sea stars and brittle stars the ambulacra lie on the oral surface of the arms; in sea urchins they are drawn up over the globular test like the lines of longitude on a globe; in sea cucumbers the body is stretched along the oral–aboral axis, so that the animal lies on its side; in crinoids the mouth faces upward and the ambulacra run along the arms. Because the axes are not those of the larva, the words "dorsal" and "ventral" have no anatomical meaning for adult echinoderms and are used only for the side that faces up or down.

The radii are not identical. The madreporite, the external opening of the water vascular system, lies in one interambulacrum, and this asymmetry allows homologous rays to be identified in all echinoderms. In Lovén's system, viewed from the aboral side, the ambulacrum opposite the madreporite is labelled A and the others B to E counterclockwise, so that the madreporite always lies in the CD interradius. Many echinoderms show a secondary bilateral symmetry superimposed on the pentaradial plan: the irregular sea urchins (heart urchins, sand dollars), which move in one direction and have shifted the anus to the rear; and the sea cucumbers, which crawl on three ambulacra (the trivium, rays A, B and E) with two (the bivium, C and D) facing up.

**Pentaradial symmetry** is secondary in two senses. It appears late in development, in a larva that is first bilateral (§42.5), and it is derived within the deuterostomes, all other members of which are bilateral. An adaptive explanation often offered is that radial symmetry suits sessile or slow-moving animals that meet their environment equally from all sides, as the first echinoderms probably did (§42.7). Why the number should be five is not known; one suggestion is mechanical: with an odd number of rays, the sutures between the plates of the test never lie opposite each other, so that a pentaradial test has no straight plane of weakness.

### 42.1.2. Axial and Extraxial Regions

The extraxial–axial theory (EAT) of Mooi and David describes echinoderm bodies as composed of two kinds of body wall with different developmental origins:

| Region | Origin | Growth | What it forms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Axial | The rudiment on the left side of the larva (hydrocoel and left somatocoel) | Ordered: new plates and tube feet are added at the tip of each ray, just behind a terminal tentacle (the ocular plate rule), so that the oldest elements are near the mouth | The mouth, the water vascular system, tube feet and ambulacral plates |
| Extraxial, perforate | The rest of the larval body | Unordered: plates can be added anywhere | The body wall with the anus, gonopores and madreporite; most of the disc of sea stars |
| Extraxial, imperforate | The rest of the larval body | Unordered | A wall without openings: the cup and stem of crinoids, most of the body of many fossil groups |

The classes differ in the proportions of these regions. In sea urchins the extraxial region is reduced to the small apical system around the anus, and almost the whole test is axial; in crinoids the imperforate extraxial region forms most of the calyx and the stem. The theory explains why the ocular plates of sea urchins, the terminal tentacles of sea stars and the tips of crinoid arms correspond to each other, and it provides homologies between living and fossil echinoderms whose plates cannot otherwise be compared.

## 42.2. Skeleton and Connective Tissue

### 42.2.1. Stereom

The echinoderm endoskeleton lies in the dermis, beneath the epidermis, and is therefore mesodermal. It consists of ossicles: plates, spines, the teeth of sea urchins, the vertebrae of brittle star arms and the microscopic spicules of sea cucumbers. Each ossicle is made of *stereom*, a three-dimensional meshwork of calcite struts (trabeculae) whose pores, typically making up about half of the volume, are filled with living cells and collagen fibres, the stroma.

Stereom is one of the best-studied biominerals. It is magnesian calcite, $\ce{CaCO3}$ with a few to 15 mol % $\ce{MgCO3}$ in solid solution, which raises its hardness. It is deposited inside a syncytium of sclerocytes, first as amorphous calcium carbonate, which then crystallizes; a small amount of occluded protein is incorporated. As a result, each ossicle behaves optically as a single crystal of calcite, with all its microcrystals aligned along one crystallographic axis, but it does not cleave along the planes of a calcite crystal: it breaks with a conchoidal fracture like glass, because the occluded proteins deflect cracks. The porous structure saves material: a plate of a given stiffness needs less calcite than a solid one, and the living stroma inside allows ossicles to grow, be resorbed and be repaired. Stereom is recognizable in fossils by its texture and, because each plate is a single crystal, by its optics, and it identifies even the oddest Cambrian fossils as echinoderms.

Ossicles bear external appendages:

- spines, mounted on tubercles by a ball-and-socket joint and moved by muscles and held by catch connective tissue;
- *pedicellariae*, small pincers of two or three jaws on a stalk or set directly on the body surface, found in sea stars and sea urchins. They clean the surface of debris and settling larvae, defend against predators (the venomous globiferous pedicellariae of sea urchins) and, in some sea stars, catch small prey;
- in sea cucumbers, microscopic ossicles shaped like wheels, anchors, tables or buttons, whose forms are used to identify species.

### 42.2.2. Mutable Collagenous Tissue

**Definition 42.2.1 (Mutable Collagenous Tissue)** **Mutable collagenous tissue** (catch connective tissue) is a connective tissue of echinoderms whose mechanical properties (stiffness, viscosity, tensile strength) change rapidly, within about a second to minutes, under nervous control, without contraction of muscle; it consists of collagen fibrils in an extracellular matrix whose interfibrillar cohesion is regulated by proteins secreted by juxtaligamental cells.

Mutable collagenous tissue occurs in the ligaments between ossicles, in the catch apparatus at the base of sea urchin spines, in the body wall of sea cucumbers and in the arm ligaments of crinoids and brittle stars. It has three states: soft, normal and stiff. A sea urchin uses muscles to point its spines, then stiffens the catch collagen around their base to lock them; a sea star crawling over rocks softens its arms, moulds them to the surface and then stiffens them to hold the new posture; a sea cucumber picked up turns as hard as a rubber hose, and some species can liquefy parts of the body wall within minutes. The stiffness is changed by molecules that make the collagen fibrils cohere or slide relative to each other (stiffening factors such as tensilin and softening factors), released from juxtaligamental cells, whose secretory granules are controlled by nerves.

The tissue allows postures to be held without metabolic cost: muscles that maintain tension consume ATP continuously, whereas a stiffened ligament does not. This helps to explain how echinoderms live on very low metabolic rates. Mutable collagenous tissue also underlies autotomy: the ligaments at the base of a brittle star arm or a crinoid stem can be softened locally until the part breaks off.

## 42.3. The Water Vascular System

**Definition 42.3.1 (Water Vascular System)** The **water vascular system** (ambulacral system) of echinoderms is a system of canals derived from the left mesocoel (hydrocoel), lined by coelomic epithelium and filled with a fluid similar to seawater: a ring canal around the oesophagus, connected to the exterior through a stone canal and the madreporite, gives off five radial canals, which supply the tube feet through lateral canals and end in terminal tentacles.

**Definition 42.3.2 (Tube Foot)** A **tube foot** (podium) is a hollow, muscular, hydraulically operated appendage of the water vascular system that projects through the ambulacral skeleton; in sea stars and sea urchins it is connected to an internal muscular sac, the ampulla, and usually ends in an adhesive disc. Tube feet serve in locomotion, attachment, feeding, gas exchange and sensation.

### 42.3.1. Structure

In a sea star the system consists of the following parts, from the exterior inward (see the figure):

1. The *madreporite* (sieve plate), a calcareous plate on the aboral surface in the CD interradius, perforated by many fine pores lined by ciliated epithelium.
2. The *stone canal*, a canal with calcified walls running orally from the madreporite, often accompanied by the axial organ of the hemal system (§42.4.2).
3. The *ring canal* around the oesophagus, bearing interradial pouches: Tiedemann's bodies, which probably produce coelomocytes, and Polian vesicles, muscular reservoirs that probably regulate pressure.
4. Five *radial canals*, one in each ambulacrum, running in the floor of the ambulacral groove outside the skeleton, each ending in an unsuckered terminal tentacle, which is light-sensitive.
5. Short *lateral canals*, alternately long and short so that the tube feet appear in two or four rows, each with a one-way valve leading to a tube foot and its ampulla.

The fluid is seawater with added coelomocytes, proteins and a high concentration of potassium. It is moved slowly by cilia; the madreporite is not the intake of a flow-through system, but replaces fluid lost across the thin walls of the tube feet, as radioactive tracer experiments have shown.

### 42.3.2. How Tube Feet Work

Each tube foot and its ampulla form a closed hydraulic unit once the valve in the lateral canal is closed. The ampulla has circular muscle in its wall; the podium has longitudinal muscle and, around its lumen, a sheath of collagen fibres arranged in a crossed helix, which resists widening and so converts the incoming volume into lengthening (compare the fibre-wound worms of [7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.2.2)). A step has four phases: contraction of the ampulla extends the podium; the disc attaches to the substrate; the longitudinal muscles of the podium contract, shortening it and pulling the body forward while the fluid flows back into the relaxing ampulla; and the disc releases.

**Example 42.3.1 (Hydraulics of a Tube Foot)** Because the fluid is nearly incompressible, the volume $\Delta V$ expelled from the ampulla enters the podium. If the collagen sheath keeps the lumen radius $r$ constant, the podium lengthens by

$$
\Delta l = \frac{\Delta V}{\pi r^2}.
$$

With $r = 0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\Delta V = 0.1\,\mathrm{mm^3}$ (an ampulla about $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ long losing a fifth of its volume), $\Delta l = 0.1/(\pi \times 0.04) \approx 0.8\,\mathrm{mm}$. The pressure follows from the balance of forces at the tip: an extended podium pushing with force $F$ needs a pressure $p = F/(\pi r^2)$ above ambient, about $8\,\mathrm{kPa}$ for $F = 1\,\mathrm{mN}$. The adhesive disc, of the order of $1\,\mathrm{mm^2}$, attaches with a tenacity measured at a few tenths of a megapascal, so that one tube foot can hold some tenths of a newton. A sea star has many hundreds to a few thousand tube feet; even if only a fraction pull together, the animal can exert tens of newtons, enough to pull apart the valves of a mussel over a few hours, as the pull of the bivalve's tiring adductor muscle declines.

Adhesion is not suction. The disc secretes a protein-rich adhesive from one type of gland cell and releases itself with secretions of another, a "duo-gland" system; tube feet leave footprints of adhesive on glass. The coordination of hundreds of independently stepping tube feet is achieved without a brain: each radial nerve coordinates the feet of its arm, and one arm leads, its direction set by stimuli or by which arm is "dominant" at the moment.

|---|---|---|---|
| Crinoidea | None; many stone canals open into the coelom, and the coelom communicates with the exterior through ciliated pores | In groups of three on the pinnules, without ampullae or suckers | Suspension feeding, respiration |

## 42.4. Other Organ Systems

### 42.4.1. Digestive System

The gut is complete in most echinoderms but has lost the anus in brittle stars and in some sea stars. Its form varies with diet: a short gut with a huge eversible stomach in predatory sea stars, a blind sac in brittle stars, a long coiled tube with a siphon in grazing sea urchins, a long intestine in deposit-feeding sea cucumbers. Feeding methods range from suspension feeding by crinoids and many brittle stars and sea cucumbers, through grazing and scraping by sea urchins, to the extraoral digestion of sea stars (§42.6.2).

### 42.4.2. Coelom, Hemal System and Axial Organ

The large perivisceral coelom, derived from the paired somatocoels, is lined with ciliated peritoneum, and its fluid is the main medium of internal transport. It contains several kinds of coelomocytes, phagocytic amoebocytes among them, which clot wounds, carry wastes and, in some holothurians and brittle stars, contain haemoglobin.

The hemal system is a set of fluid-filled spaces without an endothelium, running beside the main canals of the water vascular system: an oral hemal ring with radial branches beside the radial canals, an aboral ring supplying the gonads, and in many groups a gastric ring. The hemal channels are enclosed in coelomic channels, the perihemal sinuses. Beside the stone canal lies the axial organ, a spongy tissue with a pulsating dorsal sac at its aboral end; together with the axial sinus it forms the axial complex, which has podocytes and is probably a filtration site comparable to the glomerulus of acorn worms ([7.17.41](/7.17.41#sec-41.3.3)). The hemal system is best developed in sea cucumbers, where it forms a network around the intestine and carries absorbed nutrients.

### 42.4.3. Gas Exchange, Excretion and Osmotic Balance

Echinoderms exchange gases across thin external surfaces bathed by countercurrent flows of water outside and coelomic or ambulacral fluid inside (**countercurrent exchange**). The surfaces differ between classes:

| Class | Gas-exchange surfaces |
|---|---|
| Crinoidea | Tube feet and thin parts of the body wall |
| Asteroidea | Tube feet and papulae (dermal branchiae), thin finger-like evaginations of the body wall containing coelomic fluid |
| Ophiuroidea | Ten bursae, pouches opening by slits beside the arm bases, ventilated by cilia or pumping |
| Echinoidea | Tube feet; in irregular urchins leaf-like respiratory tube feet in the petals. The five pairs of peristomial "gills" probably mainly accommodate pressure changes caused by the lantern |
| Holothuroidea | Respiratory trees: paired, branched outgrowths of the cloaca, filled and emptied by pumping water through the anus |

Echinoderms have no excretory organs. Ammonia diffuses out across the tube feet, papulae and respiratory trees, and particulate wastes are collected by coelomocytes, which leave through the papulae, tube feet or gut wall. Their body fluids are nearly isotonic with seawater: they are **osmoconformers** with little ionic regulation ([7.14.21](/7.14.21)). This, together with the lack of excretory organs, is generally held to be why no echinoderm has entered fresh water or land, although a few species tolerate brackish water (*Asterias rubens* in the Baltic at a salinity of about 8 ‰).

### 42.4.4. Nervous System and Sense Organs

The nervous system has no brain. It consists of three interconnected systems, named by their position:

1. The ectoneural (oral) system: a nerve ring around the mouth and five radial nerves beneath the epidermis of the ambulacra, with a subepidermal nerve plexus. It is mainly sensory and is the dominant system in all classes except crinoids. In sea stars the radial nerve is visible as a V-shaped ridge in the floor of the ambulacral groove.
2. The hyponeural (deep oral) system, lying deeper, against the perihemal sinuses, parallel to the ectoneural ring and radial nerves; it is mainly motor.
3. The entoneural (aboral) system, which innervates the aboral body wall; it is the main nervous system of crinoids, controlling the arms, and is absent in sea cucumbers.

Chinese textbooks state that the hyponeural and entoneural systems derive from mesoderm, a rare exception in the animal kingdom. Their embryonic origin is in fact poorly known; the names refer only to position, and recent developmental work traces echinoderm neurons to ectoderm.

Sense organs are simple: sensory cells scattered over the epidermis, especially on the tube feet, spines and pedicellariae; pigment-cup ocelli at the tips of sea star arms, at the bases of the terminal tentacles; statocysts in some sea cucumbers; and in sea urchins spherical sphaeridia that sense gravity. Photoreceptors are widespread in the skin. Some brittle stars (*Ophiocoma wendtii*) change colour between day and night and avoid light using a network of opsin-expressing cells over the arm surface; their calcite arm plates were once proposed to act as microlenses, but the evidence favours a dispersed photoreceptor system.

### 42.4.5. Regeneration, Autotomy and Asexual Reproduction

Echinoderms regenerate lost parts readily: sea stars regrow arms, brittle stars and crinoids cast off arms or the stem when attacked (autotomy) and regrow them, and sea cucumbers regenerate the gut after evisceration (§42.6.5). In most sea stars an arm can regenerate a whole animal only if part of the central disc remains, but in *Linckia* a single arm regrows the disc and the other arms, passing through a "comet" stage. Some sea stars, brittle stars and sea cucumbers reproduce asexually by fission of the disc or body; the six-armed brittle star *Ophiactis* splits into two halves of three arms each. The cellular mechanisms of regeneration are treated in [7.5.35](/7.5.35).

## 42.5. Reproduction, Development and Metamorphosis

### 42.5.1. Reproduction

Most echinoderms are gonochoristic and spawn into the sea, where fertilization takes place; spawning is often synchronized by photoperiod, temperature or pheromones, and many species aggregate and raise the body before spawning. The gonads are simple and lie in the interradii: ten in sea stars (a pair in each arm), five in regular sea urchins (opening through the genital plates of the apical system), many beside the bursae of brittle stars, one in sea cucumbers. Crinoids have no discrete gonads; gametes form in the genital pinnules and are released by rupture of the wall. Brooding is common in polar and deep-sea species, which have few large eggs.

### 42.5.2. Embryonic Development and the Fates of the Coeloms

The eggs of free-spawning species are small and isolecithal; cleavage is radial, holoblastic and regulative, and the blastopore becomes the anus. Sea urchins became the classical material of experimental embryology because their eggs are abundant, transparent and fertilized in a dish: Driesch's isolated blastomeres and the gene regulatory network of the skeletogenic micromeres are treated in [7.5.6](/7.5.6#sec-6.5). The coeloms form by **enterocoely**, as pouches of the archenteron that divide into three pairs:

| Embryonic coelom | Corresponds to | Adult fate |
|---|---|---|
| Left axocoel | Protocoel | Axial sinus; with the dorsal sac and hydropore, the madreporic complex |
| Right axocoel | Protocoel | Largely lost; dorsal sac |
| Left hydrocoel | Left mesocoel | The whole water vascular system: ring canal, radial canals, lining of tube feet |
| Right hydrocoel | Right mesocoel | Rudimentary or lost |
| Left somatocoel | Left metacoel | Oral perivisceral coelom; hyponeural sinus; genital sinus |
| Right somatocoel | Right metacoel | Aboral perivisceral coelom |

The strong asymmetry, in which the left mesocoel forms the water vascular system and the right one disappears, is the origin of the pentaradial adult.

### 42.5.3. Larvae

Planktotrophic echinoderm larvae are variants of the dipleurula plan: bilateral, with a through-gut and a ciliary band that loops around the mouth field and is drawn out on arms or lobes. In the larvae of sea urchins and brittle stars the arms are supported by calcite rods made by the skeletogenic mesenchyme.

**Definition 42.5.1 (Echinoderm Larvae)** The main larval types of echinoderms are the **bipinnaria** of sea stars, with separate preoral and postoral ciliary bands on several lobes and no skeleton, which in many species develops into a **brachiolaria** with three adhesive brachiolar arms and an adhesive disc for settlement; the **pluteus** of sea urchins (echinopluteus) and brittle stars (ophiopluteus), with long arms supported by skeletal rods and a ciliary band along the arms; the **auricularia** of sea cucumbers, with a single sinuous ciliary band; and the barrel-shaped **doliolaria** (vitellaria), a nonfeeding larva with transverse ciliary rings, of crinoids and the late larvae of sea cucumbers.

The similarity of the echinopluteus and the ophiopluteus is convergent: brittle stars are related to sea stars, not to sea urchins (§42.7.3). Lecithotrophic, nonfeeding larvae have evolved from planktotrophic ones many times, by the loss of the arms, skeleton and larval gut; the sea urchin *Heliocidaris erythrogramma*, with a large egg and a larva that metamorphoses in four days, is the best-studied case ([7.17.5](/7.17.5#sec-5.4.3)).

### 42.5.4. Metamorphosis

The adult develops from a rudiment on the left side of the larva. The left hydrocoel grows around the oesophagus as a crescent with five lobes, the primary lobes, which become the radial canals; the left somatocoel grows beneath it, and the two together form the rudiment. The right somatocoel stacks beneath the left one. At metamorphosis the larva settles (sea star larvae attach by their brachiolar arms), most of the larval body is resorbed, the mouth and anus close and reopen in new positions, and the rudiment grows into the juvenile. The left side of the larva thus becomes the oral side of the adult, the right side the aboral side, and the larval anteroposterior axis is turned through about 90° relative to the new oral–aboral axis.

Gene expression studies show that the adult axial region does not express the anteroposterior Hox code as a single axis: the Hox genes are expressed in a nested series along the somatocoels, and genes that pattern the anterior of other bilaterians are expressed around the rays and the oral surface, which suggests that much of the adult echinoderm corresponds to a bilaterian head with the trunk greatly reduced or "folded" into the radial plan (Formery et al. 2023). How a bilateral body plan was reorganized into five rays remains one of the main problems of evolutionary developmental biology.

## 42.6. The Five Living Classes

The living echinoderms belong to five classes in two lineages. Crinoids form the sister group of the Eleutherozoa, the free-living classes with the mouth facing the substrate; these comprise the Asterozoa (sea stars and brittle stars) and the Echinozoa (sea urchins and sea cucumbers).

![The five living classes of echinoderms and their relationships (after Telford et al. 2014; O'Hara et al. 2014), with characters mapped as numbered bars: (1) pentaradial adult, stereom skeleton, water vascular system; (2) mouth facing up, arms with pinnules, stalk in larva or adult, entoneural system dominant; (3) mouth facing the substrate, madreporite as a sieve plate, robust tube feet (Eleutherozoa); (4) stellate body with arms; (5) arm ossicles fused into vertebrae, arms sharply set off from the disc, no anus; (6) eversible stomach, papulae, ampullae and suckered tube feet; (7) peripharyngeal coelom, arms absent, ambulacra meeting at the aboral pole (Echinozoa); (8) rigid test, Aristotle's lantern; (9) skeleton reduced to microscopic ossicles, feeding tentacles, respiratory trees. Approximate numbers of living species are given beside the names.](./plots/echinoderm-classes.py)

### 42.6.1. Class Crinoidea

**Definition 42.6.1 (Crinoidea)** The **Crinoidea** (crinoids: sea lilies and feather stars) are echinoderms with the mouth and anus on the upper surface of a cup-shaped calyx, from which five (often branched) arms bearing pinnules radiate; the arms carry open ambulacral grooves with suckerless tube feet used for suspension feeding; the aboral side bears a jointed stem or, in feather stars, a ring of cirri.

Crinoids (about 650 living species) are the only living echinoderms with the mouth facing up, the ancestral orientation of the earliest echinoderms. Sea lilies (stalked crinoids, about 100 species) live mostly below 100 m, attached by a stem up to a metre long; feather stars (comatulids), which form the majority today, have a stalk only as juveniles and then detach, holding on with cirri, crawling with their arms and swimming by alternate strokes of them. Both feed by spreading their arms across a current: the tube feet flick particles into the ambulacral grooves, along which cilia carry them to the mouth. The entoneural system in the calyx and arms is the main nervous system. There is no madreporite: the water vascular system communicates with the coelom through many stone canals, and the coelom with the exterior through ciliated funnels. Crinoids dominated many Paleozoic seafloors, forming "crinoid meadows" whose ossicles make up entire limestones. Isocrinid sea lilies, which can crawl, escape predatory cidaroid sea urchins by dropping part of their stem; the retreat of stalked crinoids to deep water since the Mesozoic is attributed to the rise of such predators (the Mesozoic marine revolution).

### 42.6.2. Class Asteroidea and the Representative Animal *Asterias*

**Definition 42.6.2 (Asteroidea)** The **Asteroidea** (sea stars, starfish) are free-living echinoderms with a flattened, star-shaped body of five or more arms not sharply set off from the central disc; each arm contains extensions of the coelom and of the digestive glands and bears on its oral side an open ambulacral groove with two or four rows of tube feet with ampullae; the mouth faces the substrate and the madreporite is aboral.

Sea stars (about 1,900 species) are among the most important predators of the shore and shallow seas. *Asterias*, the representative animal of Chinese textbooks, with *A. amurensis* and *A. rollestoni* common on Chinese coasts, shows the class well:

| System | Structure in *Asterias* |
|---|---|
| External form | Central disc and five arms; oral surface with the mouth in a soft peristomial membrane and an ambulacral groove along each arm, guarded by movable spines; aboral surface with the small anus, the madreporite in the CD interradius, spines, pedicellariae and papulae; a terminal tentacle and red eyespot at each arm tip |
| Body wall | A cuticle over a ciliated epidermis with gland and sensory cells and a basiepidermal nerve plexus; a thick dermis containing the ossicles; thin circular and longitudinal muscle layers; ciliated peritoneum |
| Skeleton | Ossicles in a lattice joined by mutable ligaments: two rows of ambulacral plates in the groove, between which the tube feet pass to their ampullae; adambulacral plates with spines beside them; inferomarginal and superomarginal plates at the margins; carinal and dorsolateral plates aborally |
| Digestive system | Mouth, short oesophagus, large folded cardiac stomach that can be everted, pyloric stomach giving off a pair of pyloric ducts to the paired pyloric caeca in each arm, short intestine with rectal caeca, anus |
| Water vascular system | Madreporite, stone canal, ring canal with Tiedemann's bodies and Polian vesicles, five radial canals, lateral canals with valves, ampullae and suckered tube feet |
| Hemal and perihemal systems | Oral hemal ring, radial hemal channels, axial organ beside the stone canal with a pulsating dorsal sac, aboral and gastric rings; enclosed in perihemal sinuses (ring sinus, radial sinuses, axial sinus, genital sinus) |
| Gas exchange and excretion | Papulae and tube feet; ammonia by diffusion, particulate wastes carried out by coelomocytes through the papulae |
| Nervous system | Ectoneural nerve ring and V-shaped radial nerves; hyponeural system against the perihemal sinuses; weak entoneural system aborally |
| Reproduction | Gonochoristic; a pair of gonads in each arm, opening aborally between the arm bases; external fertilization; bipinnaria and brachiolaria larvae |

The feeding of *Asterias* on bivalves combines the water vascular system and the stomach. The sea star arches over the bivalve, attaches its tube feet to both valves and pulls; when the valves part by as little as $0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$, it everts its thin cardiac stomach through the gap, secretes enzymes from the pyloric caeca and digests the prey inside its own shell, withdrawing the stomach with the partly digested soup. Cushion stars spread the everted stomach over sponges, algal films or detritus; the crown-of-thorns sea star *Acanthaster* does the same on living corals (§42.8). Other sea stars are scavengers or suspension feeders. Orders include the Forcipulatida (*Asterias*, *Pisaster*, *Pycnopodia*, with crossed pedicellariae), Valvatida (*Asterina*, *Linckia*, *Culcita*), Paxillosida (*Astropecten*, *Luidia*: burrowing, with pointed tube feet and no anus), Spinulosida, Velatida and the deep-sea Brisingida; the tiny wood-dwelling *Xyloplax*, once made a separate class (Concentricycloidea), is a highly modified asteroid.

### 42.6.3. Class Ophiuroidea

**Definition 42.6.3 (Ophiuroidea)** The **Ophiuroidea** (brittle stars and basket stars) are echinoderms with a small, rounded central disc sharply set off from five long, slender, flexible arms, which are built of articulated vertebral ossicles (fused ambulacral plates) covered by four rows of shields; the ambulacral grooves are closed, the tube feet lack ampullae and suckers, the madreporite is on the oral surface and there is no anus.

With about 2,100 species, brittle stars are the largest class of living echinoderms and among the most abundant animals of the deep-sea floor, where they can carpet the bottom at hundreds per square metre. They move by rowing with their arms, which bend sideways like snakes (hence the name Ophiuroidea); the arms break easily and are regenerated. The gut is a blind sac confined to the disc, and indigestible remains leave through the mouth. Brittle stars feed as deposit feeders, scavengers, predators and suspension feeders; basket stars (Euryalida, *Gorgonocephalus*), with repeatedly branched arms, spread them into a net at night and catch small swimming crustaceans with microscopic hooks. Ten bursae open beside the arm bases and serve for gas exchange, excretion and brooding.

### 42.6.4. Class Echinoidea

**Definition 42.6.4 (Echinoidea)** The **Echinoidea** (sea urchins, sand dollars and heart urchins) are echinoderms without arms whose plates are sutured into a rigid test (globular to flattened), with five double rows of ambulacral plates pierced by pore pairs for the tube feet alternating with five interambulacral areas, movable spines on tubercles, pedicellariae, internal radial canals, and usually a jaw apparatus, Aristotle's lantern; the anus lies in an apical system of genital and ocular plates or, in irregular urchins, is shifted to the rear.

Sea urchins (about 1,000 species) are built like a sea star whose aboral surface has shrunk to a small apical system, so that the ambulacra converge on the aboral pole; the plates of the test lie outside the radial canals, which are therefore internal, and the tube feet pass through pore pairs to their ampullae. The apical system around the periproct consists of five genital plates, each with a gonopore and one of them perforated as the madreporite, and five ocular plates, through which the terminal tentacles protrude.

Regular urchins (cidaroids, with thick pencil spines, and most euechinoids such as *Strongylocentrotus*, *Hemicentrotus*, *Diadema*) graze algae with Aristotle's lantern, a complex of 40 calcite elements and muscles: five pyramids, each holding a continuously growing tooth that sharpens itself as it wears, linked by protractor and retractor muscles that open, close and rock the jaws. Irregular urchins have a secondary bilateral symmetry related to their burrowing: heart urchins (Spatangoida, *Echinocardium*) have lost the lantern and swallow sediment; sand dollars (Clypeasteroida, Scutelloida) keep a modified lantern as a grinding mill and collect particles with thousands of minute tube feet; leaf-shaped respiratory tube feet are concentrated in the five petals on their upper surface. The lunules (slits) of some sand dollars reduce lift in strong currents.

### 42.6.5. Class Holothuroidea

**Definition 42.6.5 (Holothuroidea)** The **Holothuroidea** (sea cucumbers) are echinoderms with a soft, elongate body stretched along the oral–aboral axis and lying on one side, a skeleton reduced to microscopic ossicles in a thick, muscular, mutable dermis, a ring of 10–30 feeding tentacles (modified oral tube feet) around the anterior mouth, a calcareous ring around the pharynx, an internal madreporite, a single gonad and usually a pair of respiratory trees opening into the cloaca.

Sea cucumbers (about 1,700 species) are mostly deposit feeders that shovel sediment into the mouth with their tentacles and digest its organic matter, or suspension feeders that hold out branched, sticky tentacles and wipe them off in the mouth one by one. In the deep sea they are the dominant animals of the abyssal plains, some (*Scotoplanes*, "sea pigs") walking on elongated tube feet and some swimming (*Enypniastes*). The body wall is mostly mutable collagenous tissue with five longitudinal muscle bands along the ambulacra; burrowing apodids (*Synapta*), without body tube feet, grip the sediment with anchor-shaped ossicles.

Two defences are peculiar to the class. In evisceration, the body wall ruptures and the gut, respiratory trees and sometimes the gonads are expelled; they are regenerated in weeks. In some Holothuriidae, the Cuvierian tubules, blind tubes at the base of the respiratory trees, are shot out through the anus when the animal is attacked and become long sticky threads that entangle the predator; they too are regenerated. Many sea cucumbers contain triterpene glycosides (saponins, such as holothurin), which are toxic to fishes.

Holothurians have been interpreted as paedomorphic echinozoans, "giant larvae" in which the rudiment contributes little and the larval bilaterality is partly retained: the tube feet along the body of most species arise from interradial canals that are not homologous with the radial canals of other echinoderms. In development the auricularia becomes a doliolaria and then a pentactula, a juvenile with five primary tentacles.

## 42.7. Fossils and the Origin of Pentaradiality

### 42.7.1. Paleozoic Diversity

Echinoderms appear in the fossil record in Cambrian Series 2, about 520 million years ago, already with stereom. Their Paleozoic diversity of body forms (some 20 classes are recognized by many authors) far exceeds that of the living classes, and most of these groups had become extinct by the end of the Permian. The main extinct groups are:

| Group | Age | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Helicoplacoids | Early Cambrian | Spindle-shaped, expandable, with three ambulacra spiralling around the body; triradial |
| Edrioasteroids | Cambrian–Permian | Discoidal, attached by the aboral side, mouth up, five curved ambulacra on the upper surface |
| Eocrinoids, "cystoids" (Rhombifera, Diploporita), blastoids (Blastozoa) | Cambrian–Permian | Stalked, with a globular theca bearing brachioles (slender feeding appendages without coelomic extensions, unlike true arms); pore structures in the theca (rhombs, diplopores, hydrospires) for gas exchange; blastoids pentaradial with hydrospire folds |
| Carpoids (Homalozoa: Stylophora, Ctenocystoidea, Cincta, Soluta) | Cambrian–Devonian | Flattened, asymmetrical or almost bilateral, with a theca and one appendage (the aulacophore of stylophorans); without obvious pentaradiality |
| Ophiocistioids | Ordovician–Triassic | Domed, with a jaw apparatus and large tube feet; possibly related to sea cucumbers |

Asteroids, ophiuroids and echinoids first appear in the Ordovician, holothurians in the late Ordovician, and crinoids in the early Ordovician (with possible Cambrian forerunners); by the end of the Ordovician all living classes existed.

### 42.7.2. Was the First Echinoderm Bilateral?

The carpoids are at the centre of an old debate. In the calcichordate hypothesis of Jefferies (1960s–1990s), stylophorans were interpreted as primitive deuterostomes with gill slits, a brain and a tail (the aulacophore), ancestral both to echinoderms and to chordates. This would make the first echinoderms bilateral and give chordates a calcite-plated ancestry. Most workers now reject it: the aulacophore is a feeding arm with an ambulacral groove and cover plates, the supposed gill openings are better interpreted as respiratory structures, and the theca contains no evidence of a notochord. The alternative, which the extraxial–axial theory supports, is that the first echinoderms were already sessile and radial (like the edrioasteroid-like *Stromatocystites* of the Cambrian), and that triradial helicoplacoids and asymmetrical carpoids are derived offshoots.

Some Cambrian forms do combine stereom with apparent bilateral features. The ctenocystoid *Ctenoimbricata* and the cinctans, which have a series of openings on one side of the theca interpreted by some as gill pores, have been placed near the base of the echinoderm tree (Zamora et al. 2012). If the openings are pharyngeal slits, echinoderms lost them only in the crown group, as the deuterostome phylogeny predicts ([7.17.41](/7.17.41#sec-41.1.1)). The evidence remains contested, and the transition from a bilateral, hemichordate-like ancestor to the first radial echinoderm has not been documented by fossils.

### 42.7.3. Relationships among the Living Classes

Morphology long favoured a grouping of sea stars, brittle stars and sea urchins with similar larvae (pluteus) or similar skeletons. Phylogenomic analyses have settled the question: crinoids are the sister group of the rest, and within Eleutherozoa brittle stars are the sister group of sea stars (Asterozoa), sea urchins the sister group of sea cucumbers (Echinozoa) (Telford et al. 2014; Reich et al. 2015). The pluteus larvae of sea urchins and brittle stars are therefore convergent, and the stellate body of sea stars and brittle stars is shared by descent. The Hox cluster of sea urchins is rearranged, with the anterior genes inverted and moved to the other end, a reorganization that may be related to the unusual body plan.

## 42.8. Echinoderms in Ecology and Human Use

Several echinoderms are keystone species whose activities shape whole communities:

- The ochre star *Pisaster ochraceus* was the subject of Paine's experiments (1966), in which its removal from the rocky shore of Washington allowed mussels to monopolize the space and the number of species to collapse, the origin of the keystone species concept.
- Sea urchins graze kelp. Where their predators (sea otters, lobsters, large fishes) are removed, urchin populations grow and turn kelp forests into "urchin barrens". The mass mortality of the Caribbean urchin *Diadema antillarum* in 1983–1984 was followed by the overgrowth of corals by algae.
- Since 2013 sea star wasting disease has killed billions of sea stars on the Pacific coast of North America; the sunflower star *Pycnopodia helianthoides* lost about 90% of its population, and the urchin populations it controlled increased.
- Outbreaks of the crown-of-thorns sea star *Acanthaster* destroy large areas of coral on the Great Barrier Reef and elsewhere; their causes, among them nutrient run-off that increases larval survival and the removal of predators, are debated.

Sea cucumbers are eaten dried (trepang, bêche-de-mer), especially in China, where the spiky sea cucumber *Apostichopus japonicus* is farmed on a large scale in ponds and sea ranches, and many tropical species are overfished. Sea urchin gonads are a delicacy. Echinoderm research has contributed fertilization biology, the first gene regulatory networks and a model of regeneration; the purple sea urchin *Strongylocentrotus purpuratus* was the first non-chordate deuterostome to have its genome sequenced (2006).

## Summary

- Echinoderms (Definition 42.1.1): about 7,300 living marine species; pentaradial adults built from a bilateral larva. Rays are identified by Lovén's system (madreporite in the CD interradius); the body consists of axial (rudiment-derived) and extraxial (larva-derived) regions.
- The skeleton is stereom: porous magnesian calcite in which each ossicle behaves as a single crystal, filled with living stroma. Spines and pedicellariae are skeletal appendages.
- Mutable collagenous tissue (Definition 42.2.1) changes stiffness under nervous control, allowing postures to be held at no metabolic cost and parts to be autotomized.
- The water vascular system (Definition 42.3.1), from the left hydrocoel: madreporite, stone canal, ring canal with Tiedemann's bodies and Polian vesicles, radial and lateral canals, ampullae and tube feet (Definition 42.3.2), which work as closed hydraulic units (Example 42.3.1) and adhere by secretions.
- Coelom and coelomocytes do most internal transport; hemal system and axial organ; gas exchange by tube feet, papulae, bursae or respiratory trees; no excretory organs; osmoconformers. Three nervous systems (ectoneural, hyponeural, entoneural), no brain.
- Development: radial cleavage, enterocoely, three pairs of coeloms with asymmetric fates; larvae (Definition 42.5.1): bipinnaria and brachiolaria, pluteus, auricularia, doliolaria; the left side of the larva becomes the oral side.
- Classes: Crinoidea (Definition 42.6.1), Asteroidea (Definition 42.6.2; *Asterias*), Ophiuroidea (Definition 42.6.3), Echinoidea (Definition 42.6.4; Aristotle's lantern), Holothuroidea (Definition 42.6.5; evisceration, Cuvierian tubules). Relationships: (Crinoidea, ((Asteroidea, Ophiuroidea), (Echinoidea, Holothuroidea))).
- Paleozoic diversity included helicoplacoids, edrioasteroids, blastozoans and carpoids; whether the first echinoderms were bilateral is debated.
- Keystone roles (*Pisaster*), urchin barrens, wasting disease and *Acanthaster* outbreaks; trepang and sea urchin roe.

## Questions

1. Why are "dorsal" and "ventral" not used in their usual anatomical sense for adult echinoderms, and how are homologous rays identified?

   **Answer** The adult body is organized around an oral–aboral axis that arises at metamorphosis from the left–right axis of the larva: the left side of the larva becomes the oral side and the right side the aboral side. The larval dorsal and ventral sides have no simple correspondence in the adult, so "dorsal" and "ventral" can only describe the side facing up or down, which differs between classes (crinoids mouth up, sea stars mouth down, sea cucumbers lying on their side). Homologous rays are identified by their relation to the madreporite, which always lies in one interradius: in Lovén's system the ray opposite it is A and the others B to E counterclockwise in aboral view, so the madreporite lies between C and D.
2. A sea star of arm radius $R$ walks with its tube feet. If each tube foot can extend by $\Delta l$ per step and steps $f$ times per second, what limits the speed of a sea star, and why do sea stars move so slowly?

   **Answer** In each step the body advances by roughly the stroke length of a tube foot, of the order of $\Delta l$ (about a millimetre), so the speed is of the order of $f\,\Delta l$. With step frequencies well below one per second (each step involves hydraulic extension, secretion of adhesive, a power stroke and chemical detachment), this gives speeds of a few to some tens of centimetres per minute, as observed. The number of tube feet increases the force and stability but not the speed, because they do not move in metachronal waves that would multiply the stride; the arm size $R$ hardly matters. Fast echinoderms use other means: brittle stars row with their arms, some sea stars (*Pycnopodia*) have long, fast-stepping tube feet.
3. Why is it advantageous for a sea star to use mutable collagenous tissue rather than muscle to hold its arms around a bivalve for hours?

   **Answer** A muscle that maintains tension consumes ATP continuously (cross-bridge cycling), and its energy cost increases with the duration of the contraction. Mutable collagenous tissue, once stiffened by the secretion of stiffening proteins, holds a posture passively at almost no metabolic cost. The sea star can therefore keep its arms in position and maintain a steady pull with tube feet for hours while the bivalve's adductor muscle tires. The same principle allows sea urchins to lock their spines in crevices and crinoids to hold their arms in a current.
4. Pluteus larvae occur in sea urchins and brittle stars. What does the phylogeny of the classes imply about this similarity, and what kind of evidence settled the relationships?

   **Answer** Phylogenomic data place brittle stars with sea stars (Asterozoa) and sea urchins with sea cucumbers (Echinozoa). Since sea stars and sea cucumbers have larvae without skeletal arms (bipinnaria, auricularia), the most parsimonious interpretation is that the larval skeleton and the long arms of the pluteus evolved independently in sea urchins and brittle stars, by convergence, perhaps by co-option of the adult skeletogenic genes into the larval mesenchyme in each lineage. Morphological characters were contradictory (larvae suggesting one tree, adult skeletons another); the question was settled by analyses of hundreds of genes (transcriptomes) and supported by microRNA and gene-order evidence.
5. Explain how a sea cucumber, a sea urchin and a sea star are variations of the same body plan, using the extraxial–axial theory.

   **Answer** All three have a rudiment-derived axial region (mouth, water vascular system, ambulacra with tube feet) and a larva-derived extraxial region. In a sea star the extraxial region forms the aboral disc and the upper surface of the arms, and the ambulacra lie on the oral surface. In a sea urchin the extraxial region is reduced to the small apical system around the anus, so the five ambulacra extend from mouth to apex over the whole test and the radial canals become internal. In a sea cucumber the body is elongated along the oral–aboral axis, the skeleton is reduced, and the axial region is truncated: the radial canals form only the oral tentacles, while the tube feet along the body arise from interradial canals, so that the animal resembles an enlarged larva with a small rudiment. The same parts, in different proportions, produce the three forms.
6. Why have no echinoderms colonized fresh water, although many other marine phyla have?

   **Answer** Echinoderms are osmoconformers: their coelomic fluid and the fluid of the water vascular system are nearly isotonic with seawater, they have no excretory organs that could produce a dilute urine to eliminate excess water, and their large, thin, permeable surfaces (tube feet, papulae, respiratory trees) exchange water and ions freely. The water vascular system itself, open to the outside through the madreporite in most classes, would be flooded with fresh water. Colonizing fresh water would require an osmoregulatory organ and impermeable surfaces, which are incompatible with their way of gas exchange and locomotion; a few species tolerate brackish water down to about 8 ‰ but none lives in fresh water.
