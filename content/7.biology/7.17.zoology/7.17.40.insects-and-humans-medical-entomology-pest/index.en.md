Insects affect people more than any other group of animals. They transmit the pathogens of malaria, dengue, sleeping sickness and plague, which together have killed more people than all wars; they destroy a substantial fraction of crops in the field and in storage; and at the same time they pollinate most crops and wild plants, recycle dead matter, feed fish and birds, produce silk, honey, lac and wax, and are the natural enemies that keep most potential pests rare. This last chapter of the entomology part of the course brings together the applied branches of entomology: medical and veterinary entomology, forensic entomology, agricultural and forest entomology, and insect conservation, ending with the practical methods by which insects are collected and identified.

The chapter applies the biology of the previous chapters. Mouthparts and feeding ([7.17.23](/7.17.23), [7.17.35](/7.17.35)) explain how pathogens are transmitted and which insecticides reach which pests; the **degree-day rule** ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)) is used to estimate the time since death and to forecast pest generations; sex **pheromones** ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)) are used for monitoring and mating disruption; the endocrinology of moulting ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)) underlies insect growth regulators; and host–parasitoid dynamics ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)) underlie biological control. The protists transmitted by insects are described in [7.11.20](/7.11.20), the epidemiology of infectious disease in [7.11.35](/7.11.35), and the population genetics of selection in [7.10.5](/7.10.5); this chapter treats the insect side.

## 40.1. Insects as Direct Causes of Harm

### 40.1.1. Bites, Stings and Venoms

Most insects that harm people directly do so by biting to feed on blood, by stinging in defence, or by contact with defensive hairs and secretions.

| Harm | Insects | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Blood feeding | Mosquitoes, black flies, sand flies, biting midges, horse flies, stable flies, tsetse, fleas, lice, bed bugs (*Cimex lectularius*, *C. hemipterus*), triatomine bugs | Bites cause itching and wheals through immune reactions to salivary proteins; the main medical importance is as vectors (Section 40.2) |
| Stings | Bees, wasps, hornets, ants | Venoms cause pain and local swelling; in sensitized people anaphylaxis, which can be fatal; mass attacks by *Vespa* hornets cause dozens of deaths in some years in China ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)); fire ant stings cause sterile pustules |
| Urticating hairs and spines | Caterpillars of tussock moths, lappet moths (pine caterpillars *Dendrolimus*), slug caterpillars, processionary moths | Contact dermatitis (lepidopterism); in southern China, contact with hairs of the pine caterpillar *Dendrolimus punctatus* causes "pine caterpillar disease", an arthritis of exposed joints |
| Vesicant secretions | Blister beetles (cantharidin), rove beetles of *Paederus* (pederin) | Blisters and linear dermatitis ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)) |
| Myiasis | Larvae of blow flies, flesh flies, bot flies and screwworms in living tissue | Common in livestock; the human bot fly and screwworms in the tropics ([7.17.35](/7.17.35)) |
| Allergy | Cockroaches, midges, mayflies, house flies; storage pests | Cockroach allergens (proteins in faeces and cast skins) are a major cause of asthma in children in cities |
| Nuisance and fear | Swarming midges, house flies, cockroaches | Entomophobia; delusional parasitosis, the conviction of being infested by insects that are not there |

### 40.1.2. How Insects Transmit Pathogens

**Definition 40.1.1 (Mechanical and Biological Transmission)** In **mechanical transmission**, a pathogen is carried on the mouthparts, legs or body of an insect, or passes unchanged through its gut, and the insect is only a contaminated vehicle. In **biological transmission**, the pathogen multiplies or develops in the insect, which becomes infective only after an *extrinsic incubation period*.

An insect that transmits a pathogen biologically is a *vector* in the strict sense; house flies, which carry the agents of diarrhoeal diseases, trachoma and the eggs of parasitic worms on their bodies and in regurgitated crop fluid, are mechanical carriers. Biological transmission takes several forms:

| Form | What happens in the vector | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Propagative | The pathogen multiplies without changing form | Arboviruses (dengue, yellow fever, Japanese encephalitis) in mosquitoes; *Yersinia pestis* in fleas |
| Cyclopropagative | The pathogen multiplies and changes form | *Plasmodium* in *Anopheles*: gametocytes → zygote → ookinete → oocyst → sporozoites ([7.11.20](/7.11.20)); *Trypanosoma* in tsetse and triatomines; *Leishmania* in sand flies |
| Cyclodevelopmental | The pathogen develops but does not multiply | Filarial worms: microfilariae develop into infective third-stage larvae in mosquitoes and black flies, one larva per ingested microfilaria |
| Vertical (transovarial) | The pathogen passes from the female to her eggs | Some arboviruses in *Aedes*; rickettsiae and viruses in ticks; it allows a pathogen to persist through seasons without vertebrate hosts |

The route of transmission follows from the insect's feeding. Mosquitoes and sand flies inject saliva containing the pathogen when they probe ([7.17.35](/7.17.35)), so salivary transmission is the rule in Nematocera. Triatomine bugs and lice do not transmit with their saliva: the trypanosomes of Chagas disease and the rickettsiae of epidemic typhus are shed in their faeces, which are rubbed into the bite wound or the mucous membranes (stercorarian transmission). The plague bacterium uses its vector's anatomy most ingeniously, as described in Section 40.2.2.

### 40.2.2. Two Cases: Malaria and Plague

The cycle of *Plasmodium* in the mosquito ([7.11.20](/7.11.20)) shows why only some mosquitoes are vectors. A female *Anopheles* ingests gametocytes with a blood meal; in the midgut, within minutes, male gametocytes exflagellate and fertilize female gametes; the motile zygote (ookinete) penetrates the midgut wall and forms an oocyst under the basal lamina, which grows for 10–14 days at 25 °C and releases thousands of sporozoites into the hemolymph; these invade the salivary glands, and the mosquito is infective at its next bite. The parasite must survive the mosquito's immune system (the complement-like protein TEP1 kills many ookinetes) and must develop within the mosquito's lifespan. The duration of development, the extrinsic incubation period, depends on temperature: about 111 degree-days above a threshold of about 16 °C for *P. falciparum* (Detinova 1962), so that the parasite cannot complete development in most mosquitoes below about 18 °C. This is why malaria transmission is concentrated in warm regions and seasons, and why climate warming can extend it to higher altitudes.

The plague bacterium exploits the flea's anatomy. *Yersinia pestis* forms a biofilm in the proventriculus of the flea, the valve with backward-pointing spines that separates the oesophagus from the midgut ([7.17.35](/7.17.35)). In some fleas, the biofilm blocks the proventriculus completely; the starving "blocked" flea bites repeatedly and tries to suck blood, which cannot pass the blockage, mixes with bacteria and is regurgitated into the bite wound (Bacot and Martin 1914). The biofilm depends on genes (*hms*) acquired by *Y. pestis* after it diverged from its gut-dwelling ancestor *Y. pseudotuberculosis*, and inactivation of the bacterium's *ymt* gene, which encodes a phospholipase D, prevents survival in the flea gut (Hinnebusch and coauthors 2002); the evolution of insect-borne transmission thus required only a few genetic changes. Unblocked fleas also transmit early after an infectious meal, which helps explain the speed of epidemics.

### 40.2.3. Vector Competence and Vectorial Capacity

**Definition 40.2.1 (Vector Competence)** **Vector competence** is the intrinsic ability of an arthropod to become infected by a pathogen, to support its development or multiplication, and to transmit it; it is measured experimentally as the proportion of insects that become infective after feeding on an infected host.

Competence is only part of what makes a vector dangerous. Its ecology and behaviour determine how much transmission actually occurs: how abundant it is, how often and on whom it bites, and how long it lives. These factors are combined in the **vectorial capacity** defined in [7.17.35](/7.17.35), $C = ma^2p^n/(-\ln p)$, the number of potentially infective bites arising from one infected person per day. *Anopheles gambiae* and *An. funestus* in Africa are highly anthropophilic (they take most blood meals on people), endophilic (they rest indoors after feeding) and long-lived, and they are both competent and abundant; the main Chinese vector *An. sinensis* is mostly zoophilic, biting cattle and pigs, and less competent for *P. falciparum*, which helps explain why vivax malaria, not falciparum, dominated the plains of China.

## 40.3. The Basic Reproduction Number of a Vector-Borne Infection

### 40.3.1. The Ross–Macdonald Formula

R. Ross, who discovered in 1897–1898 that mosquitoes transmit malaria, also founded the mathematical theory of vector-borne disease, which G. Macdonald extended in the 1950s. For a vector-borne infection, the **basic reproduction number** $R_0$ ([7.11.35](/7.11.35)) is the expected number of new human infections caused by one infected person introduced into a fully susceptible population, through all the vectors that bite him or her.

**Formula 40.3.1 (Ross–Macdonald $R_0$)** Let $m$ be the number of female vectors per person, $a$ the number of bites on people per vector per day (the biting rate times the proportion of blood meals taken on people, the *human blood index*), $b$ the probability that a bite by an infective vector infects a person, $c$ the probability that a vector becomes infected when it bites an infectious person, $p$ the daily survival probability of the vector, $n$ the extrinsic incubation period in days, and $r$ the daily recovery rate of infected people (so $1/r$ is the duration of infectiousness). Then

$$
R_0 = \frac{m\,a^2\,b\,c\,p^n}{r\,(-\ln p)}. \tag{40.1}
$$

The formula can be read as a chain of events. One infectious person is bitten $ma$ times a day by vectors, for $1/r$ days, and a fraction $c$ of these vectors become infected: $mac/r$ infected vectors. Each survives the incubation period with probability $p^n$ and then lives on average $1/(-\ln p)$ further days, during which it bites people $a$ times a day, each bite transmitting with probability $b$. The product gives Eq. (40.1). In terms of the vectorial capacity, $R_0 = C\,bc/r$. An infection persists in a population when $R_0 > 1$, and control aims to bring it below 1.

### 40.3.2. Sensitivity: Why Adult Vector Control Works Best

The effect of a control measure on $R_0$ depends on which parameter it changes. A convenient measure is the elasticity, the proportional change of $R_0$ for a small proportional change of a parameter $x$, $\partial\ln R_0/\partial\ln x$. From Eq. (40.1),

$$
\frac{\partial\ln R_0}{\partial\ln m} = 1,\quad \frac{\partial\ln R_0}{\partial\ln a} = 2,\quad \frac{\partial\ln R_0}{\partial\ln b} = \frac{\partial\ln R_0}{\partial\ln c} = 1,\quad \frac{\partial\ln R_0}{\partial\ln r} = -1,\quad \frac{\partial\ln R_0}{\partial\ln p} = n + \frac{1}{-\ln p}. \tag{40.2}
$$

The last expression follows from $\ln R_0 = \text{const} + n\ln p - \ln(-\ln p)$, whose derivative with respect to $\ln p$ is $n - 1/\ln p$. With $p = 0.9$ and $n = 10$, the elasticity of $R_0$ with respect to vector survival is about $10 + 9.5 = 19.5$, ten to twenty times larger than for the density of vectors. Reducing daily survival from 0.9 to 0.8 (an 11% reduction) reduces $R_0$ about sevenfold, whereas halving the number of vectors only halves $R_0$.

![Sensitivity of the Ross–Macdonald basic reproduction number, Eq. (40.1). A: R0 relative to its baseline value as each parameter is reduced by the fraction shown, with baseline values m = 10 vectors per person, a = 0.3 bites per day, b = c = 0.5, p = 0.9, n = 10 days and r = 0.01 per day (baseline R0 about 75). Reducing vector density m (for example by larval control) lowers R0 in proportion; reducing the human biting rate a (bed nets, repellents) acts quadratically; reducing daily survival p (insecticides that kill adult vectors) acts most strongly, because a vector must survive the incubation period before it can transmit. The dotted line marks R0 = 1 in relative terms for this baseline. B: elasticities (proportional change of R0 per proportional change of the parameter) from Eq. (40.2).](./plots/r0-elasticity.py)

This result, derived by Macdonald, guided the WHO Global Malaria Eradication Programme of 1955–1969, which relied on indoor residual spraying of DDT on the walls on which mosquitoes rest after feeding: the spray kills the old, potentially infective females and so attacks $p$. The programme eliminated malaria from Europe, much of Asia and the Americas, but failed in tropical Africa and was abandoned, partly because of insecticide resistance and the exophilic behaviour of some vectors. Since 2000, insecticide-treated bed nets, which reduce both $a$ (the bites on people) and $p$, and artemisinin-based drugs, which increase $r$, have reduced malaria deaths by about a third; a modelling study attributed most of the reduction of *P. falciparum* infection in Africa from 2000 to 2015 to bed nets (Bhatt and coauthors 2015). Larval control is effective where breeding sites are few and accessible, as with *Aedes aegypti* in containers in cities, but by Eq. (40.2) it must remove a large fraction of mosquitoes to have a large effect.

The formula also shows the importance of vector biology for the targets of new methods. Replacing the vector population with insects that cannot transmit reduces $b$ and $c$: releases of *Aedes aegypti* carrying the *Wolbachia* strain *w*Mel, which blocks the replication of dengue virus in the mosquito and spreads by **cytoplasmic incompatibility** ([7.17.28](/7.17.28)), reduced the incidence of virologically confirmed dengue by 77% in a cluster-randomized trial in Yogyakarta, Indonesia (Utarini and coauthors 2021). Suppression methods (sterile males, gene drives, Section 40.6.4) reduce $m$.

## 40.4. Forensic Entomology

**Definition 40.4.1 (Forensic Entomology)** **Forensic entomology** is the use of insects and other arthropods in legal investigations, most often to estimate the minimum time since death (postmortem interval) from the age of the oldest insects developing on a corpse, and also to infer whether a body has been moved, whether drugs were present, or how long a person has been neglected.

The first recorded case comes from China. Song Ci's *Collected Cases of Injustice Rectified* (*Xiyuan jilu*, 1247) describes the investigation of a murder by a sickle: the investigator had all villagers lay their sickles on the ground in the sun, and flies gathered on the one sickle that still carried invisible traces of blood, whose owner then confessed.

A corpse is colonized in a predictable succession. Blow flies (Calliphoridae: *Lucilia*, *Calliphora*, *Chrysomya*; in China *Chrysomya megacephala* and *Lucilia sericata* are among the commonest) arrive within minutes to hours of death and lay eggs in natural openings and wounds; flesh flies (Sarcophagidae) deposit first-instar larvae; later come muscid flies and predators of the larvae (rove beetles, histerid beetles); and in later stages of decay and drying, carrion beetles (Silphidae), skin beetles (Dermestidae), checkered beetles (Cleridae), cheese skippers (Piophilidae) and moths of the family Tineidae feed on the remains. The species present indicate the stage of decay and, for long intervals, the season of death.

For short intervals the age of fly larvae is estimated from their development, which, as in all insects, depends on temperature. Using the degree-day (or degree-hour) model of [7.17.29](/7.17.29), development from egg to a given stage requires an approximately constant sum of temperatures above a lower threshold.

**Example 40.4.1 (Estimating a Postmortem Interval)** Suppose that, in a species of blow fly, development from oviposition to the end of the third larval instar requires $K = 2500$ accumulated degree-hours above a threshold $T_0 = 10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (illustrative values of the order measured for *Lucilia sericata*), and that the oldest larvae on a body have just reached this stage. Temperatures at the scene, reconstructed from the nearest weather station corrected by measurements at the scene after discovery, averaged $22\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Each hour contributes $22 - 10 = 12$ degree-hours, so the larvae are about $2500/12 \approx 208$ hours, nearly nine days, old. Since eggs are usually laid within a day of death (except at night, in cold weather, or if the body was wrapped or buried), the minimum postmortem interval is about nine days. Uncertainties come from the temperature reconstruction, from the heat generated by masses of feeding larvae (which can raise the temperature of the maggot mass 10–20 °C above ambient), and from differences among populations of the same species, which is why development data for local populations are collected.

:::

Insects also serve as evidence in other ways: larvae feeding on tissues of people who took drugs contain the drugs (entomotoxicology), and cocaine accelerates the development of some fly larvae; the presence of species that do not occur at the place of discovery suggests that the body was moved; and fly larvae in the wounds or soiled clothing of living elderly people or infants indicate neglect.

## 40.5. Insects as Pests

### 40.5.1. What Makes an Insect a Pest

A pest is an organism in the wrong place at the wrong density from a human point of view; the term is economic, not biological. Fewer than 1% of insect species are pests, and most pest species are pests only occasionally or in some places. Insects become pests when crops provide large, uniform, unprotected resources, when people transport species to regions without their natural enemies, when natural enemies are killed by insecticides, and when the value of a crop is so high that even slight damage matters (cosmetic damage of fruit, contamination of grain). Pests are often classified as key pests, which occur every year at damaging densities and require regular control; occasional pests; secondary pests, which become damaging when their natural enemies are destroyed by the control of a key pest; and potential pests.

Losses are large. Estimates of global yield losses caused by pests and diseases of five major crops in the 2010s were 10–40%, with insects accounting for much of the losses of rice, maize and potato (Savary and coauthors 2019). China, with a fifth of the world's population and a tenth of its arable land, has many important pests; some of the most important are:

| Crop or resource | Main insect pests in China | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rice | Brown planthopper *Nilaparvata lugens*, white-backed planthopper *Sogatella furcifera*, rice leaf roller *Cnaphalocrocis medinalis*, striped stem borer *Chilo suppressalis*, yellow stem borer *Scirpophaga incertulas* | Planthoppers and the leaf roller migrate each year from Southeast Asia and southern China northward on monsoon winds ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)) |
| Maize | Asian corn borer *Ostrinia furnacalis*, fall armyworm *Spodoptera frugiperda* (invasive since 2019), oriental armyworm *Mythimna separata*, aphids | The fall armyworm spread through 26 provinces within a year of its arrival in Yunnan |
| Wheat | Grain aphids (*Sitobion miscanthi*, *Rhopalosiphum padi*), orange wheat blossom midge *Sitodiplosis mosellana* | Aphids also transmit yellow dwarf viruses |
| Cotton | Cotton bollworm *Helicoverpa armigera*, mirid bugs (*Apolygus lucorum*), cotton aphid, whitefly | Bt cotton since 1997 (Section 40.6.2) |
| Vegetables | Diamondback moth *Plutella xylostella*, beet armyworm *Spodoptera exigua*, whitefly *Bemisia tabaci*, thrips, leaf miners | The diamondback moth is resistant to almost all insecticides |
| Fruit | Oriental fruit fly *Bactrocera dorsalis*, oriental fruit moth *Grapholita molesta*, peach fruit moth, citrus psyllid *Diaphorina citri* (vector of huanglongbing, citrus greening) | Huanglongbing, caused by a phloem bacterium, is the most destructive disease of citrus in southern China |
| Forests | Pine caterpillars (*Dendrolimus*), fall webworm *Hyphantria cunea* (invasive since 1979), Japanese pine sawyer *Monochamus alternatus* (vector of the pinewood nematode), Asian longhorned beetle *Anoplophora glabripennis* | Pine wilt disease has killed tens of millions of pines since the 1980s |

The history of the oriental migratory locust shows how understanding the biology of a pest changed its control. Locust plagues are recorded in Chinese chronicles for more than two thousand years, on average every few years, originating in the flood plains and lake shores of the lower Yellow River, Huai River and Bohai coast, where fluctuating water levels created bare, moist soil favoured for egg laying. From the 1950s, ecological studies (notably by Ma Shijun) identified the breeding areas, and the combination of water management that stabilized water levels, the cultivation of breeding grounds, monitoring and targeted spraying ended the large plagues; since the 2000s, spraying has increasingly used the fungal **biopesticide** *Metarhizium acridum*.

### 40.5.2. The Economic Injury Level and the Economic Threshold

Since a pest is defined by economics, the decision to control it should be economic too. Stern, Smith, van den Bosch and Hagen (1959) introduced the concepts on which integrated pest management is built.

**Definition 40.5.1 (Economic Injury Level and Economic Threshold)** The **economic injury level** (EIL) is the lowest density of a pest that causes economic damage, that is, a loss of crop value equal to the cost of controlling the pest. The **economic threshold** (action threshold) is the density at which control must be applied to prevent the population from reaching the economic injury level; it is lower than the EIL by a margin that allows for the time needed for control to take effect and for continued population growth.

**Formula 40.5.1 (Economic Injury Level)** Pedigo and coauthors (1986) wrote the EIL, in pests per production unit (per plant, per square metre or per sample), as

$$
\mathrm{EIL} = \frac{C}{V\,I\,D\,K}, \tag{40.3}
$$

where $C$ is the cost of control per unit area (yuan per hectare), $V$ the market value of the product per unit (yuan per kilogram), $I$ the injury caused per pest (for example leaf area eaten per caterpillar), $D$ the loss of yield per unit of injury (kilograms per hectare per unit injury), and $K$ the proportion of the pest population killed by the control measure.

For example, if control costs $C = 450$ yuan per hectare, the crop is worth $V = 3$ yuan per kilogram, each pest per plant reduces the yield by $I D = 15$ kilograms per hectare and the insecticide kills $K = 80\%$ of the pests, then $\mathrm{EIL} = 450/(3 \times 15 \times 0.8) = 12.5$ pests per plant. Eq. (40.3) shows how the threshold responds to circumstances: when the price of the crop rises, the EIL falls and control becomes worthwhile at lower densities; when control is expensive or ineffective, it rises. The relationship between pest density and yield loss is usually not linear, being compensated by plants at low densities and saturating at high ones, so that $I D$ applies only to a range of densities and must be measured in field experiments for each crop and region.

![The economic injury level and economic threshold (schematic). The pest population (solid line) grows during the season; without control (dashed) it would exceed the economic injury level (EIL), at which the crop loss equals the cost of control. When sampling shows that the population has reached the economic threshold (ET), lower than the EIL, a control measure is applied and the population falls below the EIL. A second peak late in the season stays below the threshold and is not treated. The general equilibrium position (GEP) is the long-term mean density set by natural enemies and other factors; for a key pest the GEP lies close to or above the EIL, for an occasional pest far below it.](./plots/eil.py)

Economic thresholds require monitoring: sampling plans (counts per plant, sweeps, sticky traps, pheromone traps), often sequential, in which sampling stops as soon as the cumulative count shows with sufficient confidence whether the threshold has been exceeded. Thresholds are used for many pests in China, for example for rice planthoppers (numbers per hundred hills) and the cotton bollworm (eggs per hundred plants). The threshold can also be used for natural enemies: if the ratio of predators to pests is high, a treatment can be postponed.

## 40.6. Methods of Pest Control

### 40.6.1. Insecticides

Synthetic insecticides have been the main means of controlling insects since the 1940s. They are classified by mode of action, which also determines cross-resistance:

| Class | Target (mode of action) | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Organochlorines | Voltage-gated sodium channel (DDT); GABA-gated chloride channel (cyclodienes, lindane) | DDT, lindane, dieldrin | DDT's insecticidal activity was discovered by P. Müller (Nobel Prize 1948); persistent and accumulating in food chains (*Silent Spring*, Carson 1962); banned for agriculture in China in 1983, still permitted for indoor malaria control in some countries |
| Organophosphates and carbamates | Acetylcholinesterase (inhibition) | Malathion, chlorpyrifos; carbaryl, carbofuran | Highly toxic organophosphates (parathion, methamidophos) were banned in China in 2007 |

| Diamides | Ryanodine receptor (calcium release in muscle) | Chlorantraniliprole, flubendiamide | Highly selective for Lepidoptera; widely used in rice and maize in China |
| Spinosyns, avermectins | Nicotinic receptor (allosteric); glutamate-gated chloride channel | Spinosad; abamectin, emamectin | Fermentation products of soil actinobacteria; the discovery of avermectins earned S. Ōmura and W. C. Campbell the Nobel Prize in 2015 |
| Insect growth regulators | **Juvenile hormone** analogues; ecdysone agonists; chitin synthesis inhibitors | Methoprene, pyriproxyfen; tebufenozide; diflubenzuron | Selective, because the targets are absent in vertebrates ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)) |
| Microbial insecticides | Pore-forming Cry and Vip toxins in the gut; infection | *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt), baculoviruses, *Beauveria*, *Metarhizium* | Bt toxins are activated by alkaline gut proteases and bind specific receptors on the midgut epithelium |

How an insecticide reaches the insect depends on how the insect feeds. Stomach poisons, applied on foliage, are taken up by chewing insects that eat the leaf, but not by sucking insects that insert their stylets into the phloem beneath the surface; contact insecticides penetrate the cuticle of any insect they touch; systemic insecticides are taken up by the plant and transported in its sap, so that they reach aphids, planthoppers and borers inside the plant, but also nectar and pollen; fumigants reach insects in stored grain and timber. This is the reason for the classical question of Chinese textbooks about the relation between mouthpart type and control: the choice of product and application follows from the feeding habit described in [7.17.23](/7.17.23).

### 40.6.2. The Evolution of Insecticide Resistance

**Definition 40.6.1 (Insecticide Resistance)** **Insecticide resistance** is a heritable decrease in the sensitivity of a population to an insecticide, caused by selection by the insecticide, which leads to the repeated failure of a product to achieve the expected level of control when used as recommended.

Resistance was first reported in 1914 (San José scale to lime sulfur), became common after 1946 when house flies became resistant to DDT, and is now known in more than 600 species of arthropods, against every class of insecticide. It is a textbook case of natural selection observed in real time ([7.10.5](/7.10.5)). Its mechanisms are of four kinds:

| Mechanism | Molecular basis | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Target-site insensitivity | Point mutations in the target | *kdr* (knockdown resistance) substitutions such as L1014F in the voltage-gated sodium channel (pyrethroids, DDT) in house flies, mosquitoes and aphids; *ace-1* G119S in acetylcholinesterase (organophosphates) in *Culex* and *Anopheles*; *Rdl* A301S in the GABA receptor (cyclodienes); mutations in the ryanodine receptor of the diamondback moth (diamides) |
| Metabolic resistance | Overexpression or amplification of detoxifying enzymes | Amplified esterase genes in *Culex pipiens* and the peach-potato aphid *Myzus persicae*; overexpression of the cytochrome P450 *Cyp6g1* caused by the insertion of a transposable element (*Accord*) in *Drosophila melanogaster*, which spread worldwide (Daborn and coauthors 2002) |
| Reduced penetration | Thicker or altered cuticle | Cuticular hydrocarbon changes in *Anopheles gambiae* |
| Behavioural resistance | Avoidance | Mosquitoes that bite outdoors or earlier in the evening, avoiding treated nets |

Several genes of resistance were present at low frequency before insecticides were used, as standing variation, and they spread rapidly once selection began. The rate of spread can be modelled with the population genetics of [7.10.5](/7.10.5).

**Formula 40.6.1 (Resistance Evolution with a Refuge)** Consider a resistance allele R with frequency $q$ and a susceptible allele S. A proportion $1 - f$ of the population develops in treated fields, where the insecticide kills a fraction $s$ of SS individuals, $(1 - h)s$ of RS heterozygotes and none of RR, $h$ being the dominance of resistance ($h = 0$: resistance recessive, heterozygotes die like susceptible homozygotes; $h = 1$: dominant); a proportion $f$ develops in untreated refuges, where all genotypes survive equally. If adults from both mate at random, the genotypes have average fitnesses

$$
w_{\mathrm{RR}} = 1, \qquad w_{\mathrm{RS}} = 1 - (1 - f)(1 - h)s, \qquad w_{\mathrm{SS}} = 1 - (1 - f)s, \tag{40.4}
$$

and the allele frequency changes each generation as in the one-locus selection model, $q' = (q^2 w_{\mathrm{RR}} + pq\,w_{\mathrm{RS}})/\bar w$ with $p = 1 - q$. A rare R allele is almost always in heterozygotes, and it spreads at a rate determined mainly by their advantage over susceptible homozygotes, $w_{\mathrm{RS}} - w_{\mathrm{SS}} = (1 - f)hs$.

The model shows the logic of the **refuge strategy** (high-dose/refuge strategy, [7.13.29](/7.13.29)). If the insecticide or Bt toxin is applied at a dose high enough to kill heterozygotes ($h \approx 0$, resistance functionally recessive), a rare R allele, which is almost always in heterozygotes, is not selected, while the few RR survivors in treated fields mate mostly with SS insects from the refuges. In the model with $s = 0.95$ and an initial frequency of 0.001, partly dominant resistance ($h = 0.5$) reaches a frequency of 0.5 within about ten generations with or without a refuge, while recessive resistance takes about 60 generations without a refuge and more than 300 with a refuge of 20%.

![Evolution of insecticide resistance with a refuge (Formula 40.6.1), starting from a resistance allele frequency of 0.001 with mortality s = 0.95 of susceptible homozygotes in treated fields. A: allele frequency over generations without a refuge and with 20% refuge, for recessive (h = 0) and partly dominant (h = 0.5) resistance. B: number of generations until the resistance allele reaches a frequency of 0.5, as a function of the refuge fraction. A refuge delays resistance only modestly when resistance is partly dominant, but by decades when it is recessive, which is why the high dose, making heterozygotes susceptible, is the other half of the strategy. Curves are model calculations, not data.](./plots/resistance-refuge.py)

The Bt cotton of China illustrates both the success and the limits. Cotton expressing the Cry1Ac toxin of *Bacillus thuringiensis* was planted from 1997 and covered most of the cotton of northern China by the mid-2000s. Over ten years, it suppressed the cotton bollworm *Helicoverpa armigera* not only in cotton but also in maize, soybean, peanut and vegetables nearby, because the bollworm, a polyphagous migrant, was killed in the cotton that formed part of its habitat (Wu and coauthors 2008). Those other crops served as natural refuges, without a planted refuge, and resistance evolved slowly; but non-recessive resistance alleles, including a dominant mutation in a tetraspanin gene, increased in the 2010s (Jin and coauthors 2015), and Bt cotton has since been combined with additional toxins. Insecticide use on cotton fell, and the abundance of generalist predators (ladybirds, lacewings, spiders) rose in Bt cotton and spilled over into neighbouring crops (Lu and coauthors 2012); but mirid bugs, previously minor pests controlled incidentally by sprays against the bollworm, became key pests of cotton and fruit trees (Lu and coauthors 2010), a case of secondary pest outbreak.

### 40.6.3. Biological Control

**Definition 40.6.2 (Biological Control)** **Biological control** is the use of living natural enemies (predators, parasitoids, pathogens, competitors) to reduce the populations of pests. In *classical* biological control, natural enemies are introduced from the pest's region of origin to establish permanently; in *augmentative* control, natural enemies are released in large numbers (inundative release) or in small numbers early in the season to multiply (inoculative release); in *conservation* biological control, the environment is managed to protect and enhance natural enemies already present.

The founding success was the control of the cottony cushion scale *Icerya purchasi* in Californian citrus by the vedalia beetle *Novius cardinalis*, introduced from Australia in 1888–1889 ([7.17.32](/7.17.32)). Since then about 2000 species of natural enemies have been introduced against about 600 pest species, and around 10% of the introductions against insects have given complete control, often of introduced scale insects, mealybugs and whiteflies. The cassava mealybug programme in Africa and the control of the winter moth in Canada ([7.17.34](/7.17.34), [7.17.39](/7.17.39)) are among the classic successes; the release of the parasitoid *Torymus sinensis* against the chestnut gall wasp is a recent one.

China has the oldest record and one of the largest programmes of biological control. Weaver ants were used in citrus orchards in the 4th century ([7.17.34](/7.17.34)); today, *Trichogramma* egg parasitoids, reared on eggs of the Chinese oak silkmoth, are released over millions of hectares against the Asian corn borer, and predatory mites, *Orius* bugs and the parasitoid *Encarsia formosa* are released in greenhouses. Conservation biological control is used in rice: in the "ecological engineering" approach developed in Zhejiang and other provinces, flowering plants such as sesame are grown on the bunds between paddies to provide nectar for parasitoids of planthopper eggs and leaf rollers (*Anagrus*, *Trichogramma*), and vetiver grass is planted as a trap crop for stem borers, whose larvae fail to develop on it; together with reduced spraying, these measures maintain natural enemies and reduce insecticide use (Gurr and coauthors 2016).

Natural enemies can themselves become problems. The harlequin ladybird *Harmonia axyridis*, native to China and released as a biological control agent in North America and Europe, has become invasive, outcompeting native ladybirds and contaminating wine grapes; and some generalist agents introduced in the past, especially vertebrates such as the cane toad and mongooses, caused serious damage. Modern programmes test host specificity before release.

Plant breeding provides another biological method. Resistant cultivars reduce pest populations by antibiosis (adverse effects on the pest's survival, growth or reproduction), antixenosis (non-preference for feeding or oviposition) or tolerance (the ability of the plant to yield despite injury). Rice cultivars carrying resistance genes against the brown planthopper (*Bph3*, *Bph14*, *Bph15* and others) have been widely used in China, but planthopper populations adapt to single genes within a few years by forming virulent biotypes, as rust fungi overcome resistance genes ([7.13](/7.13)), so that genes are combined. Push–pull systems combine repellent intercrops and attractive trap plants: in East Africa, maize intercropped with the legume *Desmodium*, which repels stem borer moths, and surrounded by Napier grass, which attracts them and kills many of their larvae, suffers much less damage from stem borers and from the fall armyworm (Khan and coauthors).

### 40.6.4. Sterile Males, Gene Drives and Pheromones

The **sterile insect technique** ([7.17.35](/7.17.35)) releases large numbers of males sterilized by irradiation, which mate with wild females, whose eggs then fail to hatch. E. F. Knipling showed in the 1950s why it can eradicate a population, while killing the same number of insects would not.

**Formula 40.6.2 (Knipling's Model)** Let a wild population of $N_t$ females and as many males grow by a factor $\lambda$ per generation, and let $S$ sterile males be released each generation, competing equally with wild males. A female's mating is fertile with probability $N_t/(N_t + S)$, so

$$
N_{t+1} = \lambda N_t\,\frac{N_t}{N_t + S}. \tag{40.5}
$$

The population declines when $\lambda N_t/(N_t + S) < 1$, that is when $S > (\lambda - 1)N_t$. With a constant release, the ratio of sterile to wild males increases as the wild population declines, so that the decline accelerates: suppose $\lambda = 5$, $N_0 = 10^6$ and $S = 9 \times 10^6$ (an initial ratio of 9 : 1); then $N_1 = 5 \times 10^6 \times 0.1 = 5 \times 10^5$, $N_2 = 2.5 \times 10^6 \times 0.05 \approx 1.3 \times 10^5$, $N_3 \approx 9 \times 10^3$, and the population is extinct by the fifth generation. By contrast, an insecticide killing the same proportion of the population each generation (90%) would leave $\lambda \times 0.1 = 0.5$ times as many insects each generation, a constant rate of decline.

![Knipling's model of the sterile insect technique, Eq. (40.5), with population growth λ = 5 per generation and an initial wild population of one million females. Solid lines: constant releases of sterile males at initial ratios of 2 : 1, 4 : 1 and 9 : 1; at the critical ratio λ − 1 = 4 the population stays constant and below it grows, while above it the decline accelerates because the sterile-to-wild ratio rises as the wild population falls. Dashed line: an insecticide that kills 90% of the population each generation, which gives a constant proportional decline. Releases are most efficient after the population has been reduced by other means, and when females mate only once.](./plots/knipling.py)

The technique works best against species in which females mate once, against isolated populations, and after the population has been reduced by other methods. Its successes include the eradication of the New World screwworm from North and Central America, of the Mediterranean fruit fly from Mexico and parts of the United States, of the tsetse *Glossina austeni* from Zanzibar ([7.17.28](/7.17.28)), and the near-elimination of *Aedes albopictus* from river islands in Guangzhou by a combination of incompatible *Wolbachia* and irradiation ([7.17.35](/7.17.35)). Genetic variants release males that carry a dominant lethal gene, which kills their offspring (the RIDL method), or a female-specific lethal gene, so that only females die and males pass the gene on for several generations.

Gene drives go further. A gene drive is a genetic element that is inherited by more than half of the offspring of a heterozygote and can therefore spread through a population even if it reduces fitness, like the natural **selfish genetic elements** of [7.4.17](/7.4.17). In a CRISPR-based homing drive, the drive construct encodes the Cas9 nuclease and a guide RNA targeting the homologous wild-type chromosome at the site where the construct is inserted; in the germ line of a heterozygote, Cas9 cuts the wild-type allele, and the break is repaired by homology-directed repair using the drive chromosome as template, converting the heterozygote into a homozygote. If conversion occurs with efficiency $e$, a heterozygote transmits the drive to a proportion $(1 + e)/2$ of its gametes. A drive targeting a conserved region of the female-specific exon of the sex-determination gene *doublesex* in *Anopheles gambiae* made homozygous females sterile and spread in caged populations until they collapsed within 7–11 generations (Kyrou and coauthors 2018). The main technical obstacles are resistance alleles, created when the cut is repaired by end-joining with small insertions or deletions, and the main questions are ecological and ethical: a suppression drive released in the wild could in principle spread across the range of a species, and its use requires public consent and international governance.

Insect **pheromones** are used in three ways. Pheromone traps baited with synthetic sex pheromones, specific to a species, monitor the timing and density of adult flights and are the basis of forecasts and threshold decisions. Mass trapping removes males or, with aggregation pheromones, both sexes, and is effective mainly against low-density populations and some beetles. Mating disruption saturates the air in a crop with the female pheromone (from dispensers, sprayable microcapsules or puffers), so that males cannot find females, through competitive attraction to the dispensers, camouflage of the females' plumes and sensory adaptation; it is used over large areas against the codling moth, the oriental fruit moth in Chinese peach and pear orchards, the pink bollworm and the rice stem borers. Pheromones are non-toxic and specific, and they spare natural enemies, but they are expensive and work best over large, contiguous areas.

### 40.6.5. Integrated Pest Management

**Definition 40.6.3 (Integrated Pest Management)** **Integrated pest management** (IPM) is a decision-based strategy that combines several compatible methods (biological, cultural, physical, genetic and chemical) to keep pest populations below economically damaging levels, using monitoring and economic thresholds to decide when to intervene, and minimizing risks to people, natural enemies and the environment.

IPM arose in the 1950s and 1960s as a response to the failures of reliance on insecticides alone, summarized as the "three R's": resistance of pests, resurgence of pests after their natural enemies were killed, and residues in food and the environment. A classic example of resurgence is the brown planthopper in tropical Asia in the 1970s: frequent spraying killed the spiders and mirid predators that normally control it, and some insecticides even stimulated planthopper reproduction, so that outbreaks became more severe the more the crop was sprayed. China's plant protection policy has been "prevention first, integrated control" since 1975, and since 2006 it has emphasized "green" plant protection with ecological and biological methods.

An IPM programme follows a hierarchy of measures:

1. Prevention: quarantine against introduced pests; resistant cultivars; cultural practices that make the crop less suitable for pests (crop rotation, sowing dates that avoid the peak flight of a pest, destruction of crop residues in which pests overwinter, intercropping, trap crops).
2. Conservation of natural enemies: habitat management, selective insecticides, avoidance of unnecessary sprays.
3. Monitoring: pheromone and light traps, field sampling, forecasting with degree-day models and, for migratory pests, with radar and trajectory analysis (insect radar on the island of Beihuang in the Bohai Strait monitors the migration of moths between northeastern and northern China).
4. Intervention when thresholds are exceeded: biological, physical (light traps, barriers, nets, heat treatment of stored products), behavioural (pheromones) and, where necessary, chemical methods, chosen to minimize side effects and resistance.

Area-wide management, which treats a pest across its whole regional population rather than field by field, is needed for mobile pests and for methods such as the sterile insect technique and mating disruption.

## 40.8. Insect Declines and Conservation

### 40.8.1. The Evidence

Long-term monitoring has documented declines of many insects. In German nature reserves, the biomass of flying insects caught in Malaise traps fell by about 76% between 1989 and 2016 (Hallmann and coauthors 2017; [7.17.22](/7.17.22)); grassland and forest arthropods in Germany declined in biomass, abundance and species numbers between 2008 and 2017 (Seibold and coauthors 2019); butterflies in the United Kingdom, monitored by volunteers on fixed transects since 1976, have declined in abundance in about half of the species; and the eastern North American population of the monarch butterfly overwintering in Mexico fell by more than 80% from the 1990s to the 2010s. A global compilation of 166 long-term surveys (van Klink and coauthors 2020) found an average decline of terrestrial insect abundance of about 9% per decade, with increases in freshwater insects, which have benefited from improved water quality in some regions, and with large differences among regions.

The evidence has limits, and its interpretation has been controversial. Most data come from Europe and North America; few time series exist for the tropics, where most insects live; many series are short or start from different baselines; and some widely publicized syntheses, such as a review that projected the extinction of 40% of insect species within decades (Sánchez-Bayo and Wyckhuys 2019), were criticized for selecting studies that reported declines. The consensus is that many insect populations are declining, often substantially, in landscapes affected by people, that the declines are uneven, and that better monitoring, especially outside Europe and North America, is needed.

### 40.8.2. Causes and Conservation

The main causes are those of the decline of biodiversity in general: the loss, fragmentation and degradation of habitat, especially through agricultural intensification (larger fields, fewer hedgerows and margins, loss of grasslands, fertilizers); pesticides, including the sublethal effects of neonicotinoids on bees and the reduction of food for insectivorous birds; climate change, which shifts ranges and desynchronizes insects from their host plants; artificial light at night, which disrupts the behaviour of nocturnal insects and draws them to their death; invasive species and pathogens (such as the parasites spread with commercial bumble bees); and, for some species, overcollecting.

Insect conservation works at two levels. For individual species, it protects habitats and the specific resources of the life cycle: host plants, ant hosts for lycaenid butterflies such as the large blue, whose reintroduction in England succeeded only when the grazing regime that favours its host ant was restored (Thomas and coauthors 2009), dead wood for saproxylic beetles, and water bodies for dragonflies. In China, the golden Kaiser-i-Hind *Teinopalpus aureus*, a swallowtail of montane forests in the south, is protected under the first class of the national list of protected wild animals, and other butterflies, beetles and dragonflies were added in the revision of 2021. For insects in general, conservation means landscapes that retain semi-natural habitat, flowering field margins and hedgerows, reduced and targeted pesticide use (IPM), reduced light pollution, and the protection of large natural areas, especially tropical forests. The IUCN Red List has assessed fewer than 1% of described insect species, a measure of how little is known.

## 40.9. Collecting, Preserving and Identifying Insects

Entomology depends on specimens. Collecting methods are chosen for the target group and habitat:

| Method | Principle | Groups collected |
|---|---|---|
| Aerial net | Catching insects in flight or at flowers | Butterflies, dragonflies, bees, flies |
| Sweep net and beating tray | Sweeping vegetation or beating branches over a sheet or tray | Bugs, beetles, caterpillars, leafhoppers, spiders |
| Aquatic net and kick sampling | Disturbing stream beds upstream of a net | Nymphs of mayflies, stoneflies, dragonflies; caddis larvae |
| Light trap and light sheet | Attraction to ultraviolet and white light at night | Moths, beetles, Hemiptera, caddisflies, mayflies |
| Malaise trap | A tent-like interception trap that guides flying insects upward into a bottle of ethanol | Flies and Hymenoptera; standard for long-term monitoring |
| Pitfall trap | A cup sunk in the ground with preservative | Ground beetles, ants, springtails, spiders |
| Pan traps | Coloured bowls with soapy water | Bees, flies, small wasps |
| Berlese–Tullgren funnel | Heat and light drive small arthropods out of litter or soil into a collecting vial | Mites, springtails, small beetles |
| Baited and pheromone traps | Carrion, dung, fruit, pheromones | Carrion and dung beetles, fruit flies, moths of particular species |
| Rearing | Keeping immatures or host material until adults (and parasitoids) emerge | Leaf miners, gall makers, parasitoids, wood borers |

Specimens are preserved according to their structure. Hard-bodied adults are pinned, through the right side of the thorax (or the right elytron in beetles), with stainless insect pins of standard sizes, and the wings of butterflies, moths and many other insects are spread symmetrically on setting boards before drying; small insects are glued to card points. Soft-bodied insects, immatures and specimens for DNA are kept in ethanol (70–80% for morphology, 95% or more and cold storage for molecular work). Minute insects (thrips, aphids, scale insects, lice, parasitoid wasps) are cleared and mounted on microscope slides, and dissected genitalia, which are often needed for identification, are stored with the specimen. Every specimen must carry a label with the locality (with coordinates), date, collector and, where relevant, host plant or method; a specimen without data has little scientific value. Collected material is deposited in museums, where type specimens ([7.17.1](/7.17.1)) anchor species names.

Identification uses keys based on morphology, such as the key to orders in [7.17.31](/7.17.31) and keys to families, genera and species in faunal monographs, and increasingly DNA. **DNA barcoding** uses a standard fragment of 658 base pairs of the mitochondrial cytochrome *c* oxidase subunit I gene (COI) to assign specimens to species by comparison with a reference library (Hebert and coauthors 2003); in large surveys, mixtures of insects from traps are sequenced together (metabarcoding), giving rapid estimates of the composition of communities. Barcoding has revealed many cryptic species, such as the complex of at least ten species previously included in the skipper *Astraptes fulgerator*, distinguished by DNA, larval colour and host plants. Photographs submitted by naturalists to platforms such as iNaturalist, identified by specialists and by image-recognition software, have become an important source of distribution data. Collecting in protected areas, of protected species and across borders requires permits.

## Summary

- Insects harm people directly by bites, stings, urticating hairs, vesicants, myiasis and allergens, and indirectly as vectors. In mechanical transmission the insect carries the pathogen; in biological transmission (Definition 40.1.1) the pathogen multiplies or develops (propagative, cyclopropagative, cyclodevelopmental, vertical) after an extrinsic incubation period.
- Major vector-borne diseases: malaria (*Anopheles*), dengue and other arboviruses (*Aedes*), Japanese encephalitis (*Culex*), filariasis, onchocerciasis (*Simulium*), leishmaniasis (sand flies), sleeping sickness (tsetse), Chagas disease (triatomines, faecal transmission), plague (blocked fleas), epidemic typhus (body lice). The extrinsic incubation of *Plasmodium* takes about 111 degree-days above 16 °C.
- Vector competence (Definition 40.2.1) and vectorial capacity determine transmission. The Ross–Macdonald $R_0 = ma^2bcp^n/[r(-\ln p)]$ (Formula 40.3.1) has elasticity 1 to vector density, 2 to the biting rate and $n + 1/(-\ln p)$ (about 20) to daily survival: killing adult vectors (residual spraying, bed nets) is far more effective than reducing their numbers. *Wolbachia* replacement reduces competence.
- Forensic entomology (Definition 40.4.1) estimates the minimum postmortem interval from the succession of carrion insects and the degree-hours needed for blow fly development (Example 40.4.1); its first record is Song Ci's *Xiyuan jilu* (1247).
- Pest status is economic. The economic injury level $\mathrm{EIL} = C/(VIDK)$ and the lower economic threshold (Definition 40.5.1, Formula 40.5.1) guide control decisions based on monitoring.
- Insecticides act on sodium channels, acetylcholinesterase, nicotinic receptors, ryanodine receptors, chloride channels and hormonal systems; feeding habit determines whether stomach, contact or systemic products reach a pest. Resistance (Definition 40.6.1) evolves by target-site mutations (*kdr*, *ace-1*), metabolic enzymes, penetration and behaviour; the refuge strategy with high doses delays it (Formula 40.6.1). Bt cotton in China suppressed the bollworm regionally, increased predators, and caused mirid outbreaks.
- Biological control (Definition 40.6.2) is classical, augmentative or conservation-based; resistant cultivars and push–pull systems add plant-based control. The sterile insect technique works because the sterile-to-wild ratio rises as the population falls (Knipling's model, Formula 40.6.2); CRISPR homing gene drives can suppress mosquito populations in cages. Pheromones are used for monitoring, mass trapping and mating disruption. Integrated pest management (Definition 40.6.3) combines these methods with thresholds.
- Resource insects provide pollination, silk, honey, lac, white wax, dyes, food, feed and medicines. Insect declines are documented in Europe and North America and caused mainly by habitat loss, agricultural intensification, pesticides, climate change and light pollution; data elsewhere are scarce.
- Insects are collected with nets, traps (Malaise, pitfall, light, pan), funnels and rearing, preserved pinned, in ethanol or on slides with full labels, and identified with keys and DNA barcodes.

## Questions

1. A control programme can either reduce the density of mosquito larvae by 50% or reduce the daily survival of adult mosquitoes from 0.90 to 0.85. With an extrinsic incubation period of 10 days, which programme reduces $R_0$ more, and by how much?

   **Answer** By Eq. (40.1), $R_0$ is proportional to $m$, so halving the larval density, and hence $m$, halves $R_0$ (factor 0.5). The survival term $p^n/(-\ln p)$ is $0.9^{10}/0.105 = 0.349/0.105 \approx 3.31$ at $p = 0.90$ and $0.85^{10}/0.163 = 0.197/0.163 \approx 1.21$ at $p = 0.85$, a factor of about 0.37. The adult-control programme, which changes survival by only 5.6%, therefore reduces $R_0$ more (to 37% instead of 50%), because a mosquito must survive the incubation period before it can transmit and then lives a shorter infective life. (In practice the two can be combined, multiplying the effects.)

2. Explain why a female *Xenopsylla cheopis* whose proventriculus is blocked by *Yersinia pestis* is a more effective vector than an unblocked flea, and why this is an example of adaptation by the pathogen.

   **Answer** In a blocked flea, the biofilm of *Y. pestis* fills the proventriculus, so ingested blood cannot reach the midgut. The flea starves, bites repeatedly and attempts to feed; the blood it sucks contacts the bacterial mass and is regurgitated, with bacteria, into the bite wound, so each bite can transmit a large dose. Biofilm formation in the flea depends on genes (the *hms* locus) and flea gut survival on *ymt*, acquired or modified in the lineage of *Y. pestis* after its divergence from *Y. pseudotuberculosis*, which is transmitted by the faecal–oral route; these changes adapt the bacterium to flea-borne transmission. The behaviour of the starving flea is a side effect that benefits the pathogen.

3. The cost of controlling a caterpillar pest of cabbage is 300 yuan per hectare; cabbage is worth 1.5 yuan per kilogram; each caterpillar per plant reduces yield by 40 kilograms per hectare; and the insecticide kills 90% of the caterpillars. Calculate the economic injury level. How would it change if the price of cabbage doubled, and why is the economic threshold set lower?

   **Answer** $\mathrm{EIL} = C/(VIDK) = 300/(1.5 \times 40 \times 0.9) \approx 5.6$ caterpillars per plant. If the price doubled to 3 yuan per kilogram, the EIL would halve to about 2.8 caterpillars per plant, because each caterpillar then destroys twice as much value, so control pays at a lower density. The economic threshold is set below the EIL so that control can be applied before the population reaches the EIL, allowing for the time needed to sample, decide and apply the treatment, for the delay before the insecticide takes effect, and for the population growth that continues in the meantime.

4. Using Formula 40.6.1, explain why Bt crops that produce a high dose of toxin, combined with refuges of non-Bt plants, delay the evolution of resistance. What happens if resistance is not recessive?

   **Answer** A high dose kills heterozygotes as well as susceptible homozygotes, making resistance functionally recessive ($h \approx 0$). When the resistance allele is rare, almost all copies are in heterozygotes, which are killed in Bt fields, so the allele is selected only through the very rare resistant homozygotes; the refuge produces many susceptible insects, and the few homozygous survivors from Bt fields mate mostly with them, producing heterozygous (susceptible) offspring. Selection on the allele is then of order $(1 - f)hs \approx 0$ for heterozygotes, and the allele frequency increases extremely slowly. If resistance is partly dominant ($h > 0$), heterozygotes survive in Bt fields, the allele is selected directly at rate $(1 - f)hs$, and a refuge reduces this rate only in proportion to $f$, giving a modest delay, as observed for *Helicoverpa armigera* in China once non-recessive resistance alleles appeared.

5. A wild pest population of 100,000 females grows fivefold per generation. What minimum constant release of sterile males per generation will cause a decline? With a release of 1 million sterile males per generation, calculate the population for three generations by Eq. (40.5).

   **Answer** The population declines when $S > (\lambda - 1)N = 4 \times 100{,}000 = 400{,}000$ sterile males per generation. With $S = 10^6$: $N_1 = 5 \times 10^5 \times 10^5/(1.1 \times 10^6) \approx 45{,}500$; $N_2 = 5 \times 45{,}500 \times 45{,}500/(1{,}045{,}500) \approx 9{,}900$; $N_3 = 5 \times 9{,}900 \times 9{,}900/(1{,}009{,}900) \approx 490$. The decline accelerates (factors 0.45, 0.22, 0.05) because the sterile-to-wild ratio rises as the population shrinks; by the next generation the population would be close to extinction. This assumes sterile males compete equally with wild males and the population is closed to immigration.

6. (Open question) A gene drive that suppresses *Anopheles gambiae* has been shown to work in cages. What biological, ecological and social questions would need to be answered before a field release?

   **Answer** Biological: the rate of conversion (homing) in wild genetic backgrounds; the frequency of resistance alleles created by end-joining repair and pre-existing variation at the target site; fitness costs of the drive; whether the drive can spread to related species by hybridization (members of the *An. gambiae* complex hybridize). Ecological: the role of *An. gambiae* in food webs (as larvae and adults); whether other vectors would fill its niche; the effect of suppression on malaria transmission given other vectors (*An. funestus*). Social and regulatory: the consent of communities in the release area and in neighbouring countries into which the drive could spread; monitoring and the possibility of reversal (for example with a second "immunizing" drive); international governance, since a self-spreading drive does not respect borders; and comparison of benefits and risks with existing malaria control methods.
