About four of every five described insect species, and more than half of all described animals, belong to the Holometabola, the insects with a pupal stage. This chapter introduces their classification and treats three groups: the Neuropterida (dobsonflies, snakeflies, lacewings and antlions), an old lineage of mostly predatory insects; the beetles (Coleoptera), the largest order of animals; and the Strepsiptera, minute parasites of other insects that are the beetles' closest relatives. Hymenoptera follow in [7.17.34](/7.17.34), the Antliophora (scorpionflies, fleas and flies) in [7.17.35](/7.17.35), and the Amphiesmenoptera (caddisflies, moths and butterflies) in [7.17.36](/7.17.36).

The pupa, larval types and the hormonal control of **complete metamorphosis** are treated in [7.17.29](/7.17.29), and the fossil record and the origin of the Holometabola in [7.17.30](/7.17.30). The question why there are so many beetles is discussed in [7.17.22](/7.17.22); the bombardier beetle and other defences in [7.17.39](/7.17.39).

## 33.1. The Holometabola

The **Holometabola** (Endopterygota) comprise eleven orders. Their larvae differ from the adults in form and usually in food and habitat; the wings and many other adult structures develop internally from **imaginal discs** and appear only at the larval–pupal molt; larvae have simple eyes (stemmata) only, and the adult compound eyes form anew in the pupa. Their monophyly is among the best-supported results of insect phylogenetics. Phylogenomic studies (Misof and coauthors 2014; McKenna and coauthors 2019) agree on the main relationships:

![Relationships of the eleven holometabolous orders according to phylogenomic studies, with approximate numbers of described species. Hymenoptera are the sister group of all other holometabolans (Aparaglossata). These form two clades: Neuropteroidea (the three neuropterid orders and Coleopterida, beetles and Strepsiptera) and Mecopterida (Antliophora, with scorpionflies, fleas and flies, and Amphiesmenoptera, with caddisflies and moths). Fleas are shown as the sister group of Mecoptera; some analyses place them within Mecoptera. Marks give characters of four clades.](./plots/holometabola-tree.py)

- Hymenoptera are the sister group of all others. This was a surprise: on morphology they were often placed close to the Mecopterida. The remaining orders, Aparaglossata, share the loss of the paraglossae of the larval labium, among other characters.
- Neuropterida and Coleopterida (beetles and Strepsiptera) are sister groups, together Neuropteroidea.
- Antliophora (Mecoptera, Siphonaptera, Diptera), whose males have a sperm pump, and Amphiesmenoptera (Trichoptera, Lepidoptera) are sister groups, together Mecopterida (or Panorpida).

The larvae of the different orders are described by a few recurrent forms, related to habit rather than to ancestry ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)):

| Larval form | Characters | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Campodeiform | Prognathous, flattened, long legs and often cerci; active predator | Neuroptera, Megaloptera, Carabidae, Dytiscidae, Staphylinidae, first instars of Meloidae and Strepsiptera (triungulins) |
| Elateriform | Elongate, cylindrical, heavily sclerotized, short legs | Wireworms (Elateridae), mealworms (Tenebrionidae) |
| Scarabaeiform | C-shaped, fleshy, with a sclerotized head and thoracic legs; sluggish | White grubs (Scarabaeidae, Lucanidae) |
| Eruciform | Cylindrical caterpillar with thoracic legs and abdominal prolegs | Lepidoptera, sawflies (Symphyta), Mecoptera |
| Apodous (vermiform) | Legless; head capsule present or reduced | Weevils, longhorn and buprestid larvae (nearly legless), Apocrita, Siphonaptera, Diptera |

Pupae are classified by two independent characters. In *decticous* pupae the mandibles are articulated and moved by muscles, so that the pharate adult can bite its way out of a cocoon or cell (Neuropterida, Mecoptera, Trichoptera and the basal moths); in *adecticous* pupae the mandibles are immobile (beetles, Hymenoptera, flies and most Lepidoptera). Pupae whose appendages are free from the body are exarate (most Holometabola), those whose appendages are glued to the body by a molting secretion are obtect (most Lepidoptera and lower Diptera), and exarate pupae enclosed in the hardened last larval cuticle are coarctate (Cyclorrhapha, [7.17.35](/7.17.35)). All decticous pupae are exarate; adecticous pupae may be of any type. Strepsiptera, whose males pupate inside the host, and the hypermetamorphic Meloidae and Mantispidae show how far the larval series can be modified within one life cycle.

The success of the Holometabola is usually attributed to the separation of larval and adult life. A larva specialized for feeding and growth and an adult specialized for dispersal and reproduction can exploit different resources and evolve independently, the pupa providing the stage in which the body is rebuilt ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)). Four holometabolous orders, Coleoptera, Diptera, Hymenoptera and Lepidoptera, together contain about 70% of all described animal species.

## 33.2. Neuropterida

**Definition 33.2.1 (Neuropterida)** The **Neuropterida** are holometabolous insects comprising Megaloptera, Raphidioptera and Neuroptera, with chewing mouthparts in the adult, two pairs of similar membranous wings with many crossveins held roof-like over the abdomen at rest, five-segmented tarsi, no cerci, prognathous, mostly predatory larvae, and decticous exarate pupae, in which the mandibles are articulated and can be used to bite and to cut out of the cocoon.

The three orders are sometimes ranked as suborders of a single order Neuroptera in the broad sense. Each is monophyletic, and molecular data support Raphidioptera as the sister group of Megaloptera plus Neuroptera. Neuropterida are an old group, abundant in the Permian and Mesozoic, and many living families are relicts.

### 33.2.1. Megaloptera

**Definition 33.2.2 (Megaloptera)** The **Megaloptera** (dobsonflies, fishflies and alderflies) are large neuropterid insects with a square prothorax, hind wings broader at the base than the forewings with a folding anal area, longitudinal veins not forked near the wing margin, and aquatic predatory larvae with strong mandibles and lateral abdominal gills; about 380 species in two families.

The Corydalidae (dobsonflies and fishflies) have three ocelli; the males of dobsonflies, such as *Corydalus* in the Americas and *Acanthacorydalis* in Asia, have enormously elongate mandibles used in male–male contests. *Acanthacorydalis fruhstorferi* of southern China, with a wingspan up to about 21 cm, is the largest aquatic insect known. The Sialidae (alderflies) lack ocelli and are smaller. Megalopteran larvae (hellgrammites) are top invertebrate predators of streams, live for up to five years, leave the water to pupate in soil, and are used as fish bait. China is a centre of megalopteran diversity, with more than 100 species of *Protohermes*, *Neochauliodes*, *Acanthacorydalis*, *Neoneuromus* and other genera, and their larvae are a traditional medicine and food in parts of the south.

The larvae are elongate, flattened and prognathous, with long mandibles and eight pairs of lateral abdominal filaments that serve as gills; corydalid larvae also have tufts of tracheal gills at the bases of the filaments and end in a pair of anal prolegs with hooks, whereas alderfly larvae (*Sialis*) end in a single long median filament. They take a year (alderflies) to five years (dobsonflies) to develop through ten or more instars, then crawl ashore and pupate in a cell in moist soil or under stones; the decticous pupa can bite if disturbed. Females lay masses of up to several thousand eggs on vegetation or rocks overhanging water, and the hatching larvae drop into it. Because hellgrammites need clean, oxygen-rich streams, they are, with mayflies, stoneflies and caddisflies, indicators of water quality.

### 33.2.2. Raphidioptera

**Definition 33.2.3 (Raphidioptera)** The **Raphidioptera** (snakeflies) are neuropterid insects with a flattened, elongate head narrowed behind, an elongate neck-like prothorax, forelegs similar to the other legs, a pterostigma in both wings, a long slender ovipositor in the female, and terrestrial predatory larvae living under bark or in soil; about 260 species in two families.

Snakeflies raise the head and prothorax like a snake about to strike. The two families, Raphidiidae (with ocelli) and Inocelliidae (without), occur only in the Northern Hemisphere, mostly in temperate and Mediterranean regions; most species need a period of cold for pupation or adult development, which may explain why the order is absent from the lowland tropics. Larvae and adults eat aphids and other soft-bodied insects. Larvae live under bark of conifers and other trees or in soil and litter, have a large, flattened, prognathous head and a sclerotized prothorax, and develop over two or more years; pupation takes place in a cell without a cocoon, and the pupa is mobile. In China the order is represented by a few dozen species of *Inocellia* and *Mongoloraphidia*, mostly in the north and in mountains. The order was much more diverse and widespread in the Mesozoic, including the tropics, and the survival of only cold-adapted lineages has been linked to the end-Cretaceous extinction.

### 33.2.3. Neuroptera

**Definition 33.2.4 (Neuroptera)** The **Neuroptera** (lacewings, antlions and their relatives) are neuropterid insects with two pairs of similar wings in which many veins fork near the margin, terrestrial or (in one family) aquatic larvae whose mandible and maxilla on each side are grooved and fitted together to form a sucking tube for extra-oral digestion of prey, a larval midgut closed behind, and a cocoon spun from silk produced by the Malpighian tubules and extruded through the anus; about 6000 species.

The sucking jaws inject digestive secretions and suck up the liquefied contents of prey, and because the larval midgut does not connect with the hindgut, wastes accumulate until the adult emerges and voids them as a meconium. The main families are:

| Family | Characters and biology | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Chrysopidae (green lacewings) | Green, golden eyes; eggs laid on long silk stalks; larvae ("aphid lions") eat aphids, some carry debris on their backs as camouflage; adults of some species hear ultrasound through an organ in the wing | *Chrysoperla sinica* and *Chrysopa pallens*, mass-reared for biological control in China |
| Hemerobiidae (brown lacewings) | Brown, smaller; larvae without debris; active at low temperatures | *Micromus* |
| Myrmeleontidae (antlions, including owlflies) | Long abdomen; short clubbed antennae; larvae with huge sickle-shaped jaws; in many species the larva builds a conical pit in sand and flicks sand at ants that slip in; larvae walk backward | *Myrmeleon*, *Euroleon*; owlflies (formerly Ascalaphidae) with long clubbed antennae |
| Mantispidae (mantidflies) | Raptorial forelegs and elongate prothorax, convergent with mantises; hypermetamorphic: the active first instar enters a spider's egg sac (or wasp nest) and molts into a grub-like feeding larva | *Mantispa*; first instars of some species board a female spider and enter her egg sac as it is spun |
| Sisyridae (spongillaflies) | Larvae aquatic, feeding on freshwater sponges with long stylets | *Sisyra* |
| Coniopterygidae (dustywings) | Minute, covered with white wax; few veins | *Conwentzia* |
| Nemopteridae (spoonwings, threadwings) | Hind wings long, ribbon- or spoon-like | *Nemoptera* |
| Osmylidae (lance lacewings) | Large, with spotted wings and three ocelli | Larvae semiaquatic, under stones and moss at stream margins; *Osmylus* |
| Ithonidae (including moth lacewings and the former Polystoechotidae) | Robust, moth-like | Larvae scarabaeiform, in soil, possibly feeding on roots |
| Psychopsidae (silky lacewings) | Broad, hairy wings | Larvae under bark, predatory; relicts in Africa, Asia and Australia |
| Berothidae (beaded lacewings) | Small; scales on the wings of some | Larvae in termite nests, killing termites with a toxic secretion |
| Nymphidae (split-footed lacewings) | Long antennae | Australian; larvae with long jaws, camouflaged with debris |

Green lacewings are the most important neuropterans for agriculture. A larva of *Chrysoperla* may eat several hundred aphids during its two to three weeks of development, and the adults of many species feed only on honeydew and pollen, so that they can be maintained in crops with food sprays. Lacewing eggs are laid singly or in groups at the tips of silk stalks, which protect them from ants and from cannibalism by the first larvae to hatch. The larvae of many chrysopids and of some myrmeleontids carry a packet of debris, plant fibres, lichen or the empty skins of their prey on hooked setae, which disguises them from ants tending aphids. Adults of many green lacewings hear bat ultrasound with a tympanal organ in a swelling of the radial vein of the forewing (Miller 1970) and fold their wings and drop when a bat approaches.

The owlflies, formerly the family Ascalaphidae, are fast-flying aerial predators with long, clubbed antennae and large eyes divided into upper and lower halves, the upper half sensitive to ultraviolet; they hunt small insects on the wing at dusk, like dragonflies. Their larvae resemble antlion larvae but lie in wait on the ground or on vegetation, covered with debris, rather than in pits. Dustywings (Coniopterygidae), among the smallest neuropterans at 2–3 mm, are covered with a whitish wax secreted by abdominal glands and are important predators of mites and scale insects in orchards; spongillafly larvae (Sisyridae) pierce freshwater sponges and bryozoans with their long stylets and breathe with segmented gills.

The antlion pit trap is a well-studied example of a sit-and-wait foraging structure. The larva digs a conical pit in dry, loose sand by moving backward in a spiral, flicking sand outward with its head; the walls settle at the angle of repose of the sand, about 30–35°, so that an ant stepping on the slope triggers a small avalanche and slides to the bottom, where the larva, buried except for its jaws, seizes it, often after flicking sand to bring it down. The larva injects venom and digestive secretions produced by symbiotic bacteria as well as by its own glands, sucks out the contents and throws the empty carcass out of the pit. A simple geometric model shows why pit size is limited:

![The economics of an antlion pit (model with illustrative parameters). A conical pit of rim radius R whose walls lie at the angle of repose θ has depth $h = R\tan\theta$; the minimum work to lift its sand to the surface is $W = \rho g \pi R^4 \tan^2\theta / 12$ (ρ, the bulk density of sand, about 1500 kg/m³), shown for θ = 30° and 35°. The rate at which wandering ants encounter the pit grows only in proportion to R (and its area with R²; both scaled to the same maximum). The cost of digging rises much faster with pit size than the benefit. Values are a model, not measurements.](./plots/antlion-pit.py)

The work of digging grows as $R^4$, whereas the rate at which ants walking across the sand meet the pit grows only as $R$, so large pits pay only for large larvae, which can subdue large prey and have large reserves. In accordance with this, larvae of *Myrmeleon* and *Euroleon* build larger pits as they grow, relocate pits more often where prey are scarce, and many species survive months without food. Antlion larvae spend one to three years in the pit before pupating in a spherical cocoon of sand and silk.

### 33.2.4. Neuropterida in Time

Neuropterida are known from the Permian, and in the Jurassic and Cretaceous they were far more diverse than today. Among the most remarkable fossils are the Kalligrammatidae, large Middle Jurassic to Early Cretaceous lacewings from northeastern China and Kazakhstan whose wings bore eyespots and dense scales and whose adults had a long tubular proboscis; they were nectar or pollination-drop feeders and have been called "Jurassic butterflies" because of their convergence with butterflies tens of millions of years before butterflies existed (Labandeira and coauthors 2016). Their extinction, like the decline of many other neuropterid lineages, coincided with the rise of flowering plants and of the holometabolous orders that came to dominate them. The living neuropterids are therefore a small remnant of a once dominant group of predators and flower visitors.

## 33.3. Coleoptera

**Definition 33.3.1 (Coleoptera)** The **Coleoptera** (beetles) are holometabolous insects whose forewings are hardened **elytra** without functional venation, meeting in a straight midline suture and covering the membranous hind wings, which alone power flight and fold transversely and longitudinally beneath them at rest; they have chewing mouthparts, a large, free prothorax, a reduced mesothorax, antennae usually of 11 segments, sclerotized ventral abdominal sternites with the dorsal abdomen membranous under the elytra, retractable genitalia without an external ovipositor in most, and adecticous exarate pupae; about 390,000 described species.

### 33.3.1. The Beetle Body Plan

The beetle body is a compact, armoured box. The elytra and the overlapping sclerites protect the hind wings, the soft dorsal abdomen and the spiracles, which open into the subelytral space; this reduces water loss, allows life in soil, wood, dung, under bark and in leaf litter, and in water beetles holds an air store. The prothorax is joined to the rest of the body by a mobile joint, and much of the beetle's strength lies in its legs and head. The mesothorax is reduced (its scutellum often visible as a small triangle between the elytra), and the metathorax holds the flight muscles. The abdomen usually shows five to seven visible sternites. Wing folding is a complex origami of transverse and longitudinal folds, opened by elastic joints and the movements of the abdomen ([7.17.24](/7.17.24)). In flight most beetles hold the elytra raised and spread, contributing a little lift; the flower chafers (Cetoniinae) fly with the elytra closed, the hind wings protruding through a notch in their lateral margins.

Many details of the body plan are used in classification. The head is usually prognathous, with a gula, a ventral plate between the gular sutures behind the mouthparts; antennae are inserted on the side of the head or the frons, in weevils on the rostrum. The prothorax is the most conspicuous part of the thorax: its dorsal pronotum, the shape of the prosternal process between the fore coxae, and whether the cavities in which the fore coxae sit are closed behind by sclerites ("procoxal cavities closed") or open are family characters. The elytra usually have longitudinal grooves (striae) or rows of punctures, ancestrally ten on each elytron, and a turned-down lateral margin (epipleuron); in many flightless beetles the elytra are fused along the suture and the hind wings lost. The male genitalia are typically a trilobed aedeagus of a median lobe and two parameres, retracted into the abdomen; the female has a short ovipositor derived from the ninth segment, and many beetles lay eggs singly on or in their food.

Beetles range from the featherwing beetles (Ptiliidae), among the smallest free-living insects at 0.3–0.4 mm, to *Titanus giganteus* (Cerambycidae, about 17 cm) and the heavy goliath beetles. About a quarter of all described animals are beetles, and a single family, the weevils, has more species than all vertebrates together.

The Archostemata include *Micromalthus debilis* (Micromalthidae), a wood-boring beetle with one of the strangest life cycles known: larvae reproduce paedogenetically, some producing female larvae viviparously, others laying a single egg that develops into a male larva which eats its mother. The other archostematans (Cupedidae, Ommatidae) are wood-feeding relicts whose ancestors dominated the Permian and Triassic beetle faunas; the Myxophaga are minute beetles of wet rocks, stream margins and algal films. The Adephaga are mostly predators: ground beetles on land, and several families of aquatic beetles (Dytiscidae, Gyrinidae, Haliplidae, Noteridae) that reflect at least two invasions of water; adults of many have pygidial glands that secrete defensive compounds, including the explosive quinone spray of bombardier beetles ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)).

|---|---|---|

| Bostrichiformia | Bostrichoidea (Dermestidae, Bostrichidae, Ptinidae) | Dry-matter feeders, many stored-product pests |
| Cucujiformia | Cleroidea, Cucujoidea, Coccinelloidea, Tenebrionoidea, Chrysomeloidea, Curculionoidea | The largest series, with about 60% of beetle species; the plant-feeding Chrysomeloidea and Curculionoidea alone contain about half of all beetles |

The great diversity of the phytophagous Cucujiformia is associated with the radiation of flowering plants and, according to genomic evidence, with the acquisition by horizontal gene transfer from bacteria and fungi of genes for enzymes that degrade plant cell walls, such as pectinases and cellulases, which allowed leaf beetles, longhorns and weevils to digest plant tissues (McKenna and coauthors 2019).

### 33.3.3. Larvae, Life Histories and Parental Care

Beetle larvae have a sclerotized head with chewing mandibles, usually six stemmata or fewer on each side, three pairs of thoracic legs (reduced or lost in weevils, many longhorns and buprestids, and other borers) and no abdominal prolegs, although a pygopod on segment 10 helps many to crawl. Many have a pair of fixed or articulated processes on the ninth abdominal segment (urogomphi). The larval types of Section 33.1 correspond to habits: active campodeiform predators (ground beetles, diving beetles, rove beetles, ladybirds), elongate wireworms in soil, C-shaped white grubs in soil, dung and rotten wood, and legless grubs in plant tissue.

Most beetles have one generation a year, but development is slow in wood borers, whose food is poor in nitrogen: larvae of large longhorns and stag beetles take two to five years, and buprestid larvae in dry timber have emerged after more than 25 years. Adults of many species live for months, and some ground beetles and darkling beetles for several years. Hypermetamorphosis, a series of very different larval forms, occurs in Meloidae and Ripiphoridae: an active, legged first-instar triungulin searches for a host (a grasshopper egg pod or a bee nest), and is followed by grub-like feeding instars and, in Meloidae, a quiescent pseudopupa before the true pupa.

Parental care has evolved several times. Burying beetles (*Nicrophorus*, Staphylinidae: Silphinae) locate a small vertebrate carcass by smell, bury it, remove its fur or feathers and treat it with antimicrobial anal and oral secretions; both parents guard the brood and feed the larvae with regurgitated liquefied carrion, and larvae beg by touching the parents' mouthparts. Bess beetles (Passalidae) live as families in rotting logs, the adults chewing wood for the larvae and communicating by stridulation; dung beetles (Scarabaeinae) roll or bury dung balls in which a single larva develops, guarded in some species by the female; and the ambrosia beetles (Scolytinae, Platypodinae) cultivate fungi in their galleries, some species with cooperative breeding and sterile helpers.

### 33.3.4. Aquatic Beetles

Beetles have invaded fresh water at least ten times, as adults, larvae or both. Diving beetles (Dytiscidae) carry a bubble of air under the elytra, which they renew at the surface with the abdomen tip; it acts as a **physical gill** that extracts oxygen from the water until the nitrogen dissolves ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)). Water scavenger beetles (Hydrophilidae) hold an air film over the ventral surface and renew it with the antennae. Riffle beetles (Elmidae) and some weevils live permanently submerged with a **plastron**, a thin film of air held by a dense pile of hydrofuge hairs that never needs renewal in well-oxygenated water. Whirligig beetles (Gyrinidae) skate on the surface film; their paddle-shaped middle and hind legs beat at up to about 50 Hz and are among the most efficient swimming appendages known in animals. Many aquatic beetles are predators of fish fry and tadpoles; others graze algae or eat detritus.

### 33.3.5. Recognizing Beetle Families

Beetles are identified to family mainly by antennal type and number of segments, tarsal formula, the form of the prothorax and of the coxae, the shape and sculpture of the elytra, and the head (rostrum, eyes). The tarsal formula is the most useful single character. Two cases need care: in the large phytophagous families (Cerambycidae, Chrysomelidae, Curculionidae) the tarsi are five-segmented, but the fourth tarsomere is minute and hidden in the bilobed third, so they appear 4-4-4 (pseudotetramerous); in ladybirds the tarsi are four-segmented but appear three-segmented (pseudotrimerous). The heteromerous beetles (Tenebrionoidea, among them darkling and blister beetles) have 5-5-4 tarsi.

![Antennal types and tarsal formulae of some beetle families (schematic; scape, pedicel and flagellum coloured). Ground beetles have filiform antennae, click beetles serrate antennae, scarabs a lamellate club of movable plates, ladybirds a short clavate antenna, sap beetles a compact capitate club, darkling beetles moniliform antennae, longhorn beetles very long antennae often exceeding the body, and weevils geniculate antennae with a long scape and a terminal club. Tarsal formulae give the true number of tarsomeres on the fore, middle and hind legs, with the apparent number where one tarsomere is minute.](./plots/beetle-antennae.py)

Several pairs of families are regularly confused, and the characters above separate them. Black ground beetles and black darkling beetles look alike: ground beetles have 5-5-5 tarsi, filiform antennae inserted between the eye and the mandible, enlarged hind trochanters and are fast runners, while darkling beetles have 5-5-4 tarsi and antennae inserted under a ridge of the head, and many walk slowly and smell of quinones. Ladybirds and round leaf beetles are separated by the antennae (short and clubbed in ladybirds, longer and filiform in leaf beetles) and by the tarsi (apparently three-segmented in ladybirds, apparently four-segmented in leaf beetles); the plant-feeding ladybirds (Epilachninae) are hairy and dull. Soldier beetles and net-winged beetles have soft elytra and resemble each other in colour, but net-winged beetles have a network of raised ridges on the elytra. Rove beetles are recognized by their short elytra, but earwigs, which also have short forewings, have cerci forming forceps ([7.17.31](/7.17.31)).

Three of the largest families deserve further comment. The Carabidae (about 40,000 species) include the tiger beetles (Cicindelinae), formerly a separate family, which run down prey in sunlit open ground at speeds of up to about 2.5 m s⁻¹ for *Cicindela hudsoni*, among the fastest running animals relative to size, stopping repeatedly because their visual system cannot track prey at full speed; the large, flightless *Carabus* (including the brilliant *Carabus* of the Chinese mountains, many of them local endemics); and the bombardier beetles (Brachininae, Paussinae), such as *Pheropsophus jessoensis* in China, which spray a boiling quinone mixture ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)). Ground beetles are generalist predators of slugs, caterpillars and seeds of weeds, and their assemblages are standard indicators in studies of land use.

The Staphylinidae (about 64,000 species), the largest beetle family on some counts, owe much of their success to the short elytra that leave the abdomen free to bend, allowing them to move through litter, soil and narrow spaces; their hind wings fold under the short elytra with help from the abdomen. Many rove beetles are inquilines in ant and termite nests, mimicking their hosts in shape or chemistry and being fed by them. The pederin of *Paederus* species, produced by endosymbiotic bacteria, causes linear blisters when the beetles are crushed on the skin, and outbreaks of "Paederus dermatitis" are common in rice-growing regions of southern China in summer.

The weevils (Curculionidae and related families; over 60,000 species) are the most species-rich group of beetles. The rostrum allows the female to drill a hole into seeds, fruits, stems or wood and to deposit an egg inside it; in nut weevils (*Curculio*) the rostrum may be as long as the body and is bent like a bow as the female drills into an acorn or chestnut. The larvae thus develop protected within plant tissue, and many species are host-specific. The genome of weevils, like those of leaf beetles and longhorns, includes plant cell wall-degrading enzymes acquired from microorganisms, and many weevils also carry bacterial endosymbionts (*Nardonella*, *Sodalis*) that supply tyrosine for cuticle formation, the hard cuticle being a weevil hallmark.

The beetle families that are most important in agriculture are thus the plant feeders of the superfamilies Chrysomeloidea and Curculionoidea, whose species-richness reflects repeated radiations on flowering plants ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)), and the stored-product pests (Dermestidae, Bostrichidae, Tenebrionidae, Curculionidae), many of which have spread worldwide with commerce. Among predators, ladybirds and ground beetles are the most important natural enemies of pests ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)).

### 33.3.7. Selected Topics in Beetle Biology

#### 33.3.7.1. Weapons and Allometry

The horns of scarabs and the mandibles of stag beetles are sexually selected weapons used in contests between males for access to females, burrows or feeding sites. Their size scales with body size in unusual ways. In the dung beetle *Onthophagus taurus*, males above a threshold body size develop long head horns, and smaller males develop almost none; horn length plotted against body size is a sigmoid curve, and the population consists of two male forms. Hornless minor males do not fight but sneak into tunnels guarded by major males and mate with the female (Moczek and Emlen 2000). The threshold is set by larval nutrition through juvenile hormone and insulin signalling, an example of a developmental switch producing alternative phenotypes from one genotype (**polyphenism**). Stag beetle mandibles show a similar, steep **allometry**, and in many species the largest males have disproportionately large jaws.

#### 33.3.7.2. Desert Beetles

Darkling beetles are the dominant beetles of deserts. Many are flightless, with the elytra fused and a sealed subelytral cavity over the abdomen into which the spiracles open, reducing water loss; the cavity air is warmer and more humid than the outside air. In the coastal Namib Desert, *Onymacris unguicularis* climbs dune crests on foggy mornings and stands head-down facing the wind; fog condenses on its back and runs down to the mouth (fog basking), and other species dig trenches that collect fog. The textured elytra of such beetles have inspired designs of fog-harvesting surfaces.

#### 33.3.7.3. Dung Beetles

Dung beetles bury the dung of mammals, return nutrients to the soil, aerate it and reduce the breeding of pest flies and the survival of parasite eggs. When cattle were introduced to Australia, whose native dung beetles are adapted to the dry pellets of marsupials, cow pats accumulated on pastures and bush flies bred in them; the Australian Dung Beetle Project (1965–1985) introduced more than 40 species from Africa and Europe. Ball-rolling dung beetles roll away in straight lines, steering by the sun, the pattern of polarized light, and on moonless nights by the Milky Way (Dacke and coauthors 2013).

#### 33.3.7.4. Luminous Beetles

Bioluminescence evolved in Lampyridae (fireflies), Phengodidae (glow-worm beetles, whose larviform females glow along the body), Rhagophthalmidae and some Elateridae (the tropical *Pyrophorus*), probably more than once in the Elateroidea ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)). In fireflies, light is produced in abdominal photophores by the oxidation of luciferin by firefly luciferase, with ATP and oxygen; flashing is controlled by nervous regulation of oxygen supply to the photocytes through nitric oxide. Courtship codes are species-specific: in *Photinus* the flying male flashes a pattern and the female answers after a fixed delay. Females of *Photuris* imitate the answers of *Photinus* females, attract the males and eat them, gaining defensive lucibufagins they cannot make themselves (Lloyd 1965; Eisner and coauthors 1997). In mangroves of Southeast Asia, males of *Pteroptyx* gather in trees by thousands and flash in synchrony. Firefly larvae, which glow too, are predators of snails and earthworms, and the glow is thought to have evolved first as a warning signal of distastefulness.

#### 33.3.7.5. Ladybird Colour Polymorphism

The harlequin ladybird *Harmonia axyridis*, native to eastern Asia, is polymorphic: in China, red forms with up to 19 black spots (succinea) and black forms with two or four red spots (conspicua, spectabilis) occur together, the proportion of black forms increasing towards the north and west. The pattern forms are alleles at one locus, the transcription factor gene *pannier*, which controls the deposition of melanin in the elytra (Ando and coauthors 2018; Gautier and coauthors 2018). Ladybirds defend themselves by reflex bleeding of hemolymph containing alkaloids, and their colours are **aposematism** ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)). Released widely for biological control, *H. axyridis* has become invasive in Europe, the Americas and Africa, where it competes with and eats native ladybirds.

#### 33.3.7.6. Beetles and People

Beetles include major pests: white grubs and wireworms in soil; leaf beetles and flea beetles on vegetables; the Colorado potato beetle, which has evolved resistance to more than 50 insecticides; weevils, bark beetles and longhorns in crops, forests and urban trees; and the stored-product beetles, which with moths destroy a considerable part of stored grain in tropical countries. The red flour beetle *Tribolium castaneum* is a model organism in genetics, development and population ecology (its genome was sequenced in 2008, and gene function can be studied by systemic RNA interference); mealworms (*Tenebrio molitor*) and superworms (*Zophobas*) are farmed as animal feed and human food, and the larvae of palm weevils are eaten in tropical Asia and Africa. Many beetles are threatened by the loss of old trees and dead wood, which support saproxylic species such as the Rosalia longhorn and the stag beetles of Europe; the American burying beetle *Nicrophorus americanus* is one of the few insects that have been the subject of national recovery programmes.

## 33.4. Strepsiptera

**Definition 33.4.1 (Strepsiptera)** The **Strepsiptera** (twisted-wing parasites) are minute holometabolous insects with extreme sexual dimorphism: free-living, short-lived winged males with forewings reduced to club-shaped pseudohalteres, large fan-shaped hind wings with radiating veins, branched (flabellate) antennae and large eyes of few, separate lens units ("raspberry eyes"); and neotenic, larviform, wingless females that in all but one family remain as endoparasites inside the host; development is hypermetamorphic, with an active, legged first-instar triungulin and legless later instars; about 600 species.

In Stylopidia (all families except the free-living Mengenillidae), the female develops inside the host's abdomen, and only her fused head and thorax (cephalothorax) protrudes between the host's sclerites. The male, which lives for a few hours, locates her by a pheromone and mates by traumatic insemination through the cephalothorax. Thousands of triungulins hatch inside the mother's body cavity, leave through a brood canal, and are carried to new hosts, often by the parasitized host itself visiting flowers or nests; each triungulin enters the egg or larva of the host, molts into a legless endoparasitic larva and develops without killing the host, although parasitized ("stylopized") hosts are often sterile and show altered morphology and behaviour. Hosts belong to seven orders, mainly Hymenoptera (Stylopidae in bees, Xenidae in wasps) and Hemiptera (Halictophagidae and Elenchidae in leafhoppers and planthoppers, where they can contribute to the natural control of rice planthoppers), and also Orthoptera, Blattodea, Mantodea, Diptera and Zygentoma.

The position of Strepsiptera was long called "the Strepsiptera problem". Their pseudohalteres suggested a relationship with flies (with which they would share haltere-like organs, though on the other wing pair), their parasitic larvae a relationship with blister beetles (Meloidae) or Ripiphoridae, and the long branches caused by rapid molecular evolution created artefacts in early molecular analyses. Genome-scale data and morphology now agree that they are the sister group of beetles, forming Coleopterida; like beetles, they fly with the hind wings alone.

The forewing pseudohalteres are mechanosensors that, like the halteres of flies on the other wing pair, oscillate during flight and provide information about rotations of the body, a striking case of convergence. The eyes of males consist of up to about 100 large lenses, each with its own small retina that forms a partial image, so that the whole eye combines many low-resolution images; the design has been compared with the schizochroal eyes of some trilobites. Adult males live only a few hours and do not feed.

## Summary

- **Holometabola** (eleven orders, about 80% of insect species) have a pupa and develop wings and adult eyes from imaginal discs; Hymenoptera are the sister group of all others, which form Neuropteroidea (Neuropterida and Coleopterida) and Mecopterida (Antliophora and Amphiesmenoptera). Larval forms (campodeiform, elateriform, scarabaeiform, eruciform, apodous) reflect habit, not ancestry.
- **Neuropterida** (Definition 33.2.1) have decticous pupae and mostly predatory larvae (antlion pits cost work in proportion to $R^4$): **Megaloptera** (Definition 33.2.2; aquatic larvae with gills; giant dobsonflies), **Raphidioptera** (Definition 33.2.3; long prothorax, Northern Hemisphere) and **Neuroptera** (Definition 33.2.4; larvae with sucking jaws and a closed midgut; lacewings, antlions, mantidflies).
- **Coleoptera** (Definition 33.3.1) have elytra, fly with the hind wings, and have a free prothorax and exarate pupae. Adephaga have hind coxae that divide the first visible sternite and a notopleural suture; Polyphaga, nearly 90% of beetles, have movable hind coxae and no notopleural suture.
- Beetle families are recognized by antennae (filiform, serrate, lamellate, clavate, capitate, geniculate), tarsal formulae (5-5-5; 5-5-4 in heteromerous families; 4-4-4 appearing 3-3-3 in ladybirds; 5-5-5 appearing 4-4-4 in longhorns, leaf beetles and weevils), the prothorax and the head; the most species-rich and economically important families are the phytophagous Chrysomelidae, Cerambycidae and Curculionidae.
- Beetle larvae are campodeiform, elateriform, scarabaeiform or legless; wood borers develop for years; parental care evolved in burying beetles, bess beetles, dung beetles and ambrosia beetles; aquatic beetles breathe with air stores, physical gills and plastrons.
- **Strepsiptera** (Definition 33.4.1) are hypermetamorphic parasites with winged males bearing forewing pseudohalteres and neotenic endoparasitic females; they are the sister group of beetles.

## Questions

1. An elongate beetle with serrate antennae lies on its back, then flips into the air with a click. To which family does it belong, how does the mechanism work, and what is its larva called?

   **Answer** It is a click beetle (Elateridae). A spine on the prosternum is pressed against the edge of a pit in the mesosternum while the body is arched; when muscles pull it free, the spine slides into the pit and the sudden flexion of the body at the prothoracic joint, with elastic energy stored in the cuticle, strikes the ground with the base of the elytra and throws the beetle upward, so that it may land on its feet. The larvae are wireworms, elateriform larvae living in soil, some of them pests of roots and tubers.

2. A beetle has a rostrum, geniculate antennae and apparently four-segmented tarsi. Which family is it most likely to belong to, and why is the tarsal count misleading?

   **Answer** It is most likely a weevil (Curculionidae). The tarsi are in fact five-segmented, but the fourth tarsomere is minute and concealed in the bilobed third, so that they appear four-segmented (pseudotetramerous), a character shared with longhorn and leaf beetles. The geniculate, clubbed antennae arising from the rostrum distinguish true weevils from straight-snouted weevils (Brentidae), whose antennae are not elbowed.

3. Explain how a neuropteran larva feeds, and why its midgut can be closed behind.

   **Answer** On each side the mandible has a ventral groove into which the elongate maxilla fits, forming a closed channel; the larva pierces the prey, injects digestive fluids and sucks up the liquefied tissues, so that it takes in almost no indigestible solids. Since the liquid food is digested and absorbed nearly completely, the small amount of waste can be stored in the closed midgut until the adult emerges, when it is voided as a meconium; meanwhile the Malpighian tubules, still connected to the hindgut, are free to produce silk for the cocoon, which is spun through the anus.

4. What evidence placed Strepsiptera as the sister group of beetles, and why had their position been so difficult to determine?

   **Answer** Genome-scale analyses of many genes and of rare genomic changes, using models that account for unequal rates of evolution, consistently recover Strepsiptera with Coleoptera; morphological characters, including the use of the hind wings alone for flight and features of the wing base and of the larval head, support this. The difficulty arose from their extremely modified morphology (neotenic females, pseudohalteres on the forewings, unusual eyes) that obscured homologies, and from very fast molecular evolution, which produced long branches that attracted them to other fast-evolving groups (flies) in early analyses.

5. An antlion larva doubles the radius of its pit. By what factor does the minimum work of digging increase, and why might the larva nevertheless build a larger pit as it grows?

   **Answer** For a conical pit with walls at the angle of repose, the minimum work to raise the sand to the surface is $W = \rho g \pi R^4 \tan^2\theta/12$, so doubling the radius increases it by $2^4 = 16$ times, while the rate at which wandering prey encounter the rim only doubles. A larger larva can nevertheless profit: it digs with more power and over a longer time, it can subdue larger prey that a small pit would not hold (the slope and depth determine whether an ant can climb out), and it has larger reserves to survive the time spent digging and waiting. Pit size is therefore expected to scale with larval size and prey size and to be reduced where sand is hard to move.

6. Describe the parental care of burying beetles and explain why it may have evolved in a beetle that breeds on small carcasses.

   **Answer** A pair of *Nicrophorus* buries a small vertebrate carcass, strips and rounds it, and coats it with antimicrobial secretions; the female lays eggs nearby, and both parents guard the brood and feed the larvae with regurgitated, predigested carrion, the larvae begging by touching the parents' mouthparts. A carcass is a rich but rare and ephemeral resource contested by flies, bacteria, fungi and other beetles; burial and preservation protect it, and feeding the young speeds their development before the resource is spent or stolen. Parental care therefore increases the survival of offspring enough to outweigh its cost in future reproduction.

7. (Open question) Phytophagous beetle clades are often more species-rich than their non-phytophagous sister clades. How would you test whether plant feeding itself is the cause, and what alternative explanations should be considered?

   **Answer** One would compare species numbers of all independent pairs of sister clades that differ in plant feeding, using a phylogeny of beetles, and test whether the phytophagous member is richer more often than expected by chance (a sign test) or use models that estimate speciation and extinction rates as a function of the trait. Alternative explanations include the age of clades (older clades accumulate more species), correlated traits such as small size or wood-boring habits, the diversification of the host plants themselves (angiosperms), and incomplete taxonomy (better-known groups appear richer). Repeated, independent origins of phytophagy make the test stronger than a single comparison.
