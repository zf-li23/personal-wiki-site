Amniotes are the tetrapods whose embryos develop inside a set of membranes that the embryo itself produces: the amnion, chorion, allantois and yolk sac. Laid on land within a shell, or retained in the mother's body, this amniotic egg frees reproduction from water: there is no aquatic larva and no gill-breathing stage. With some 30 000 living species — about 12 000 reptiles in the narrow sense, 11 000 birds and 6 700 mammals — amniotes are the largest radiation of tetrapods and include every vertebrate that lives entirely away from water, as well as many that returned to it (sea turtles, ichthyosaurs, whales).

The earliest amniotes, small lizard-like insectivores of the Late Carboniferous, had already split into two lineages that have remained separate for more than 310 million years: the **synapsids**, the lineage of mammals, and the **sauropsids**, the lineage of turtles, lepidosaurs, crocodylians and birds. The two lineages are recognized by the openings in the side of the skull behind the eye, the temporal fenestrae, which give the groups their names and have long been the basis of amniote classification.

This chapter describes the characters of amniotes (§50.1) and the amniotic egg (§50.2), then the temporal fenestrae (§50.3), the divergence of synapsids and sauropsids and their different solutions to the problems of terrestrial life (§50.4), and the contrast between ectothermy and endothermy that runs through the rest of amniote history (§50.5). Section 50.6 surveys the extinct amniote radiations that are not treated elsewhere, and §50.7 relates the modern classification to the traditional class Reptilia. The tetrapods from which amniotes arose are in [7.17.48](/7.17.48); the living reptile groups follow in [7.17.51](/7.17.51) (lepidosaurs and turtles) and [7.17.52](/7.17.52) (archosaurs), birds in [7.17.53](/7.17.53), synapsids and mammals in [7.17.54](/7.17.54) and [7.17.55](/7.17.55). The development of the extraembryonic membranes is treated in [7.5.9](/7.5.9) and the skull in detail in [7.17.59](/7.17.59); the physiology of thermoregulation is in [7.14.31](/7.14.31).

## 50.1. What Is an Amniote

### 50.1.1. Definition and Earliest Fossils

**Definition 50.1.1 (Amniota)** The **Amniota** (amniotes) are the **crown group** of tetrapods comprising the last common ancestor of mammals and reptiles (including birds) and all its descendants. Their embryos develop within four extraembryonic membranes (amnion, chorion, allantois and yolk sac); further synapomorphies are a lateral flange of the pterygoid bone that gives origin to the pterygoideus muscle, an axis vertebra, an astragalus formed by fusion of proximal tarsal bones with a mesotarsal ankle joint, and two or more sacral vertebrae.

Because the amniotic egg itself rarely fossilizes, fossil amniotes are recognized by their skeletons. The oldest are *Hylonomus lyelli* and *Paleothyris acadiana* from the Late Carboniferous (Pennsylvanian) of Nova Scotia, about 312–318 million years old. Their skeletons were found inside the hollow stumps of fossil lycopsid trees (*Sigillaria*) at Joggins, where the animals had fallen or sheltered and were buried by sediment; *Hylonomus* was first described by J. W. Dawson in 1860. These were animals about 20 cm long with slender limbs, long clawed digits and small, sharp teeth, probably insect eaters on the floor of coal-swamp forests. The earliest synapsids are nearly as old (*Archaeothyris*, about 306 million years, and fragments from Joggins itself), so the split between the mammalian and reptilian lineages must have occurred very soon after the origin of amniotes, by about 318 million years ago.

The sister group of amniotes among Paleozoic tetrapods is debated. Several groups of large, terrestrial tetrapods of the Late Carboniferous and Permian are closer to amniotes than to amphibians and are grouped with them as Reptiliomorpha: the diadectomorphs (*Diadectes*, one of the first large terrestrial herbivores), the seymouriamorphs (*Seymouria*) and others ([7.17.48](/7.17.48#sec-48.5)). Diadectomorphs share many skeletal characters with amniotes, and it has been suggested that they laid amniotic eggs too; most analyses place them just outside Amniota. Because no fossil eggs or embryos are known from the earliest amniotes (the oldest amniote embryos, of mesosaurs from the Early Permian of Uruguay and Brazil, are about 286 million years old), the moment at which the amniotic egg evolved cannot be dated directly; it is inferred from its presence in all living amniotes.

### 50.1.2. Skeletal Synapomorphies

Four skeletal characters that can be seen in fossils diagnose amniotes. Each is related to a change in feeding or locomotion.

| Character | Description | Functional significance |
|---|---|---|
| Lateral flange of the pterygoid | A downward and outward process of the pterygoid bone of the palate, behind the eye | Origin of the pterygoideus, a jaw-closing muscle split off from the adductor mandibulae; allows a stronger bite at a wide gape and more varied jaw movements |
| Axis vertebra | The second cervical vertebra is enlarged, with a large neural spine, and forms with the atlas a specialized joint | Rotation and nodding of the head; feeding with a mobile head on a longer neck |
| Astragalus and mesotarsal joint | Three proximal tarsals of earlier tetrapods (tibiale, intermedium and a centrale) fuse into the astragalus, beside the calcaneus; the ankle bends between the proximal and distal tarsals | The foot works as a lever pushed by the thigh and shank muscles rather than as a holdfast around which the limb pivots; limb-driven locomotion |
| Two or more sacral vertebrae | The pelvic girdle is attached to the vertebral column by at least two sacral ribs | A stronger connection that transmits the thrust of the hindlimbs |

The pterygoid flange and pterygoideus muscle reflect a change in feeding. Most early tetrapods snapped at prey with a simple adductor muscle that pulled the jaw shut; in amniotes the jaw muscles became subdivided, and the pterygoideus, which runs from the palate to the inside of the back of the lower jaw, can hold the jaw closed against struggling prey and later, in many lineages, move it fore and aft. Subdivision of the jaw muscles is also the context in which the temporal fenestrae evolved (§50.3).

Other features of early amniotes are found in some of their relatives as well: a skull that is deeper and narrower than the flat skulls of temnospondyls, a single occipital condyle on the basioccipital, the loss of the intertemporal bone, a pectoral girdle with a new dermal element, the interclavicle, and a phalangeal formula of 2-3-4-5-3 in the hand and 2-3-4-5-4 in the foot. The ribs of early amniotes are long, curve ventrally and attach to a cartilaginous sternum, a structure associated with costal ventilation (§50.1.3).

### 50.1.3. Characters of Soft Tissues

Several characters that define living amniotes leave no direct trace in fossils but can be inferred by phylogenetic bracketing: if both synapsids and sauropsids have a character, their common ancestor probably had it.

- *Waterproof skin.* The epidermis is multilayered and heavily keratinized, and its outer layers contain lipids that limit evaporation; the skin is no longer a respiratory organ. Amniotes produce a great variety of hard **keratin** structures — scales, feathers, hair, claws, nails, beaks, horns — that develop from epidermal **placodes** sharing a common signalling network ([7.5.22](/7.5.22), [7.17.57](/7.17.57)). All amniotes have α-keratins; the harder β-keratins (corneous β-proteins) of scales, claws and feathers are found only in sauropsids, so the hard scaly skin of reptiles is a sauropsid feature, not an ancestral amniote one.
- *Costal ventilation.* Air is drawn into the lungs by expanding the rib cage with the intercostal and other axial muscles (aspiration breathing), rather than pushed in by the buccal pump of amphibians and lungfishes ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.3)). Aspiration allows much higher ventilation rates and the elimination of carbon dioxide through the lungs, so the skin is freed from gas exchange.
- *A long trachea with cartilaginous rings*, associated with a longer neck.
- *The metanephric kidney.* The adult kidney is the **metanephros**, a compact organ that develops from the posterior part of the intermediate mesoderm, induced by the ureteric bud, and drained by a new duct, the ureter ([7.5.25](/7.5.25), [7.17.66](/7.17.66)). The **pronephros** and **mesonephros** function only in the embryo; in males the archinephric duct becomes the vas deferens. Amphibians and fishes retain a functional mesonephros (opisthonephros) drained by the archinephric duct.
- *Internal fertilization.* Fertilization must precede the formation of the shell and the albumen, so all amniotes fertilize internally, and most have a copulatory organ. The single penis of turtles, crocodylians, ratites and mammals and the paired hemipenes of squamates are probably homologous, both developing from paired genital tubercles, whereas the intromittent organs of fishes and caecilians evolved independently.
- *No larva and no lateral line.* Amniote embryos never form functional gills, and the lateral line system, present in aquatic larvae and some adult amphibians, is absent.

## 50.2. The Amniotic Egg

### 50.2.1. The Extraembryonic Membranes

**Definition 50.2.1 (Amniotic egg)** The **amniotic egg** (cleidoic egg) is the egg of amniotes, in which the embryo is enclosed and supported by four extraembryonic membranes derived from its own germ layers: the amnion, which surrounds the embryo in a fluid-filled amniotic cavity; the chorion, which encloses the embryo and all other membranes; the allantois, an outgrowth of the hindgut that stores nitrogenous waste and, fused with the chorion, forms the respiratory surface; and the yolk sac, which encloses the yolk. In oviparous species the egg is enclosed in albumen, shell membranes and a calcareous or parchment-like shell.

The membranes are formed from two double sheets. The somatopleure (ectoderm with the somatic layer of lateral plate mesoderm) rises around the embryo in folds that meet and fuse above it; the inner layer of the fused fold is the **amnion**, the outer layer is the **chorion**, and between them lies the extraembryonic coelom. The splanchnopleure (endoderm with splanchnic mesoderm) spreads over the yolk as the **yolk sac** and buds from the hindgut as the **allantois**, which grows into the extraembryonic coelom. Because the mesoderm of these membranes forms blood vessels, the yolk sac and allantois are vascular: the vitelline circulation carries nutrients from the yolk, the allantoic circulation carries gases. The germ layers and functions are summarized in the figure.

![The amniotic egg of a bird in section (schematic, about day 5 of incubation), with the germ layers and functions of the four extraembryonic membranes. The embryo floats in the amniotic cavity enclosed by the amnion; the chorion lies under the shell membranes; the allantois, a sac from the hindgut, expands under the chorion and later fuses with it as the chorioallantoic membrane; the yolk sac grows over the yolk. The albumen fills the remaining space.](./plots/amniotic-egg.py)

The functions of the membranes are divided as follows:

- The **amnion** encloses the amniotic fluid, which keeps the embryo moist and cushions it against mechanical shocks and adhesion to the other membranes; the amnion of birds and reptiles contains smooth muscle that rocks the embryo.
- The **chorion** is the outermost membrane. In birds and reptiles it lies against the shell membrane; in therian mammals its outer cell layer, the trophoblast, is the fetal part of the placenta.
- The **allantois** receives the embryo's nitrogenous waste: in a closed egg the waste cannot be diluted and flushed away, and it is stored mainly as uric acid, which precipitates and is osmotically inert. As the allantois enlarges, its mesodermal wall fuses with the chorion to form the chorioallantoic membrane, a vascular sheet under the shell through which oxygen enters and carbon dioxide and water vapour leave. In a chicken egg it covers the whole inner surface of the shell by day 12 of the 21-day incubation.
- The **yolk sac** digests and absorbs the yolk; its blood islands are the first site of blood formation. In birds the remnant of the yolk sac is drawn into the body before hatching and nourishes the chick for its first days.

The rest of the egg is made by the mother's oviduct after fertilization: in a bird, the albumen in the magnum, the shell membranes in the isthmus and the shell in the shell gland (uterus), which in the chicken takes about 20 of the 24–26 hours that an egg spends in the oviduct. The albumen supplies water and protein and contains antimicrobial proteins (lysozyme, ovotransferrin); twisted strands of dense albumen, the chalazae, suspend the yolk so that the embryo stays uppermost. At the blunt end an air cell forms between the two shell membranes as the egg cools and loses water; the chick breaks into it with its beak a day or two before hatching and begins to breathe air with its lungs (internal pipping), then breaks the shell with the egg tooth, a keratinous knob on the beak (external pipping). Squamates have a true tooth on the premaxilla as egg tooth, other reptiles and birds a keratinous caruncle.

### 50.2.2. The Eggshell

Amniote eggshells are of two broad kinds:

| Kind | Structure | Who has it |
|---|---|---|
| Rigid, calcareous | A thick layer of calcium carbonate crystals on a thin organic shell membrane; calcite in birds and crocodylians, aragonite in turtles | Birds, crocodylians, many turtles, some geckos |
| Flexible (parchment, leathery) | A thick fibrous shell membrane with a thin, discontinuous mineral layer or none | Most squamates, many turtles, monotremes |

The bird eggshell is built from cones of calcite crystals (the mammillary layer) that nucleate on organic cores attached to the outer shell membrane and grow outward into columns (the palisade layer). Pores, narrow channels between the columns, pass through the shell; a chicken egg 0.3 mm thick has about 7 000–17 000 of them. The shell is also the calcium reserve of the embryo, which dissolves part of its inner surface to mineralize its skeleton.

Rigid shells resist water loss and mechanical load and allow large eggs; flexible shells take up water from the soil and swell during incubation, so eggs of many squamates must be laid in moist soil or rotting vegetation. The ancestral condition is uncertain. Fossil eggs are much younger than the earliest amniotes, and analyses of dinosaur eggs suggest that the earliest dinosaurs, such as the sauropodomorph *Mussaurus* and the ceratopsian *Protoceratops*, laid soft-shelled eggs, so that rigid calcareous shells evolved several times among archosaurs (Norell et al. 2020). A flexible shell is probably the ancestral amniote condition.

### 50.2.3. Gas Exchange through the Shell

An egg on land is a closed system for everything except gases and heat. Oxygen must diffuse in and carbon dioxide out through the pores at the rate the embryo needs, while water vapour escapes through the same pores, so the shell faces a trade-off between gas exchange and desiccation.

Diffusion through the pores obeys Fick's law ([7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.2.2)). For a gas $x$,

$$
\dot M_x = G_x\,\Delta p_x, \qquad G_x = \frac{D_x}{R\,T}\,\frac{A_{\mathrm{p}}}{L},
$$
where $\dot M_x$ is the molar flux, $\Delta p_x$ the partial-pressure difference across the shell, $D_x$ the diffusion coefficient of the gas in air, $A_{\mathrm{p}}$ the total cross-sectional area of the pores and $L$ their length (the shell thickness). The conductance $G$ is a property of the shell alone; the same pores conduct oxygen, carbon dioxide and water vapour in proportion to their diffusion coefficients. Hermann Rahn, Amos Ar and their colleagues measured $G$ for the water vapour of eggs of hundreds of bird species, by weighing eggs in a desiccator, and found that it scales with egg mass $W$ as $G = 0.432\,W^{0.780}$ (in mg of water per day per Torr, $W$ in g), while incubation time scales as $I = 12.03\,W^{0.217}$ days. The exponents sum to almost exactly 1, so the total water lost during incubation, $G\,\Delta p\,I$, is a nearly constant fraction of egg mass: about 15–18 % of the initial mass for eggs from 0.5 g (hummingbirds) to 1.5 kg (ostrich). The air cell forms from this lost water, and the gas tensions in it at the end of incubation are similar in all species (about 14 kPa of oxygen and 6 kPa of carbon dioxide).

![Model: allometry of eggshell gas exchange in birds. (A) The water-vapour conductance of the shell rises with egg mass with an exponent of 0.78 (Ar et al. 1974), and incubation time with an exponent of 0.22 (Rahn and Ar 1974). (B) Their product predicts that every egg loses about the same fraction of its mass as water during incubation, independent of size, close to the measured 15–18 %. A vapour-pressure difference of 34 Torr between egg and nest is assumed.](./plots/eggshell-conductance.py)

The design is adjusted where the environment differs. Eggs of birds nesting at high altitude have lower conductance, because water vapour diffuses faster in thin air; eggs buried in moist mounds (megapodes) or laid in wet nests (grebes) have higher conductance. A turtle or squamate egg buried in soil can absorb water, and its conductance is much higher than that of a bird egg of the same mass.

### 50.2.4. Why the Amniotic Egg Evolved

The amniotic egg is usually described as the adaptation that allowed tetrapods to reproduce on land. That is its effect, but its origin is less clear.

- *Eggs on land before amniotes.* Many amphibians lay eggs on land, and some (plethodontid salamanders, many frogs such as *Eleutherodactylus*) have direct development without a larva ([7.17.49](/7.17.49)). Their eggs are small, because an egg without membranes depends on diffusion through jelly and yolk, and the largest such eggs are about 1 cm in diameter. The membranes and shell allowed larger eggs: the chorioallantois brings blood vessels to the surface of the egg, and the shell supports a large yolk on land.
- *Size of the first amniotes.* Robert Carroll noted that the earliest amniotes were small (about 20 cm) and suggested that the amniotic egg evolved in small animals that laid relatively large eggs on land, giving rise to large, well-developed hatchlings.
- *The membranes before the shell.* An alternative view is that the extraembryonic membranes first evolved in eggs retained in the oviduct, perhaps in viviparous forms, and the shell later. There is no fossil evidence that can decide between these scenarios.

Whatever its origin, the amniotic egg permitted direct development on land, independence from bodies of water for breeding, and, by retaining the egg in the mother's body, the repeated evolution of viviparity. Viviparity has evolved more than 100 times in squamates alone, often in cold climates where a female basking in the sun incubates her embryos at a higher temperature than a nest could provide: on the Tibetan Plateau the toad-headed lizard *Phrynocephalus vlangalii* is viviparous at altitudes above 2 000 m, whereas most of its relatives lay eggs. In therian mammals the shell was lost and the chorion and allantois or yolk sac became the **placenta** ([7.14.38](/7.14.38#sec-38.9.2), [7.17.55](/7.17.55)). Ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs and mosasaurs, which could not come ashore, were viviparous; a Chinese fossil of the early ichthyosaur *Chaohusaurus* (about 248 million years ago, Anhui) preserves a mother giving birth to a young that emerges head first.

## 50.3. Temporal Fenestration

### 50.3.1. The Temporal Region of the Skull

The skull of early amniotes, like that of their tetrapod ancestors, was covered by a continuous roof of dermal bones over the braincase and jaw muscles ([7.17.58](/7.17.58)). Behind the orbit the roof enclosed the temporal fossa, the space occupied by the main jaw-closing muscle, the adductor mandibulae, which originated from the inner surface of the roof and inserted on the lower jaw. The dermal bones of the skull roof of an early amniote such as *Paleothyris* fall into five series:

1. the tooth-bearing marginal bones: premaxilla and maxilla;
2. the median roof bones: nasal, frontal, parietal and postparietal;
3. the circumorbital bones: lacrimal, prefrontal, postfrontal, postorbital and jugal;
4. the temporal series: supratemporal and tabular (the intertemporal of earlier tetrapods is lost);
5. the cheek bones: squamosal and quadratojugal.

The same bones, in the same colours, are traced through all vertebrates in the comparative chapters ([7.17.58](/7.17.58), [7.17.59](/7.17.59)).

### 50.3.2. Types of Fenestration

**Definition 50.3.1 (Temporal fenestra)** A **temporal fenestra** is an opening in the dermal roof of the skull behind the orbit, bounded by the bones of the circumorbital, temporal and cheek series, through which the jaw-closing muscles pass and from whose margins they originate. A lower (lateral, infratemporal) temporal fenestra lies low on the side of the skull, an upper (supratemporal) temporal fenestra on the dorsal surface beside the parietal.

The number and position of the fenestrae define four classical skull types (the names refer to the arches or bars of bone between the openings, Greek *apsis*, arch):

| Type | Fenestrae | Bones bordering the opening | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anapsid ("without arch") | None | — | Early amniotes (*Captorhinus*, *Paleothyris*), parareptiles; living turtles (secondarily) |
| Synapsid ("joined arch") | One lower fenestra | Postorbital and squamosal above, jugal and quadratojugal below | Synapsids: *Dimetrodon*, therapsids, mammals |
| Diapsid ("two arches") | Upper and lower fenestrae | Upper temporal bar (postorbital and squamosal) between them; lower temporal bar (jugal and quadratojugal) below | *Petrolacosaurus*, tuatara, archosaurs |
| Euryapsid ("broad arch") | One upper fenestra | Postorbital and squamosal below | Plesiosaurs, placodonts and other sauropterygians |

A fifth type, the parapsid skull, was distinguished for ichthyosaurs, which also have a single upper fenestra but bordered below by the postfrontal and supratemporal instead. Both the euryapsid and parapsid skulls are now interpreted as diapsid skulls in which the lower fenestra closed or the lower temporal bar was lost and the cheek filled in, and the groups that have them are diapsid reptiles.

### 50.3.3. Why Fenestrae Evolved

The fenestrae are related to the jaw muscles. Three functional explanations, not mutually exclusive, have been proposed:

1. *Space for contracting muscles.* A muscle that shortens thickens. In a closed temporal fossa the adductor mandibulae can bulge only inward; an opening lets the muscle bulge outward when it contracts, so that a larger muscle can be accommodated.
2. *Attachment surfaces.* Muscles attach more firmly to the edge of a bone than to a flat sheet, because the tendon fibres can be anchored at the margin. The rims of the fenestrae provide such edges, and in many lineages the jaw muscles spread from the inner surface of the skull roof onto its outer surface through the openings. The subdivision of the adductor into separate muscles (in mammals the temporalis and masseter, [7.17.54](/7.17.54)) depends on such new attachment areas.
3. *Removing unstressed bone.* In a skull loaded by biting, the stresses are carried by struts and arches, and some areas of the roof carry little load. Bone in such areas can be lost without weakening the skull, saving material and weight. Finite-element models of reptile skulls show that the temporal bars lie along the paths of high stress, and that fenestration does not reduce the strength of the skull much.

A pterygoid flange and a pterygoideus muscle were present before fenestration (§50.1.2), so jaw muscle subdivision had already begun when the openings appeared. The fenestrae evolved independently in synapsids and in diapsids, and later fenestrae (the antorbital fenestra of archosaurs, [7.17.52](/7.17.52)) evolved in front of the orbit as well. Fenestration was part of a general trend: in many amniote lineages the solid, plated skull of early tetrapods was transformed into a framework of struts.

### 50.3.4. Fenestration as a Classification Criterion

From the work of Henry Fairfield Osborn (1903) and Samuel Williston to Alfred Romer's *Vertebrate Paleontology* (1966), reptiles were divided by their temporal fenestrae into subclasses: Anapsida (early amniotes and turtles), Synapsida (the "mammal-like reptiles"), Euryapsida or Synaptosauria (plesiosaurs and placodonts), Ichthyopterygia or Parapsida (ichthyosaurs) and Diapsida (Lepidosauria and Archosauria). The scheme captured an important truth — synapsids and diapsids are both natural groups — but it fails in three ways:

- *Anapsid is ancestral.* A solid skull is the primitive condition of amniotes and of tetrapods in general, so "Anapsida" defined by the absence of fenestrae is a **paraphyletic group** of early amniotes that do not share a common derived character.
- *Fenestrae are modified within lineages.* Lizards have lost the lower temporal bar, so the lower fenestra is open below; snakes have lost the upper bar as well; the tuatara regained a lower bar secondarily, braced for its shearing bite ([7.17.51](/7.17.51)); birds have lost the postorbital bar and fused most skull bones; in mammals the orbit and the temporal fenestra have merged in many groups. The skull of a snake is "diapsid" only by descent.
- *Reversals occur.* Living turtles have no temporal fenestrae, and for a century they were taken to be the last anapsid reptiles. Molecular phylogenies consistently place turtles within the diapsids, as the **sister group** of archosaurs, and the Triassic stem turtles *Pappochelys* (Germany, 240 million years ago) and *Eorhynchochelys* (Guizhou, China, 228 million years ago) have both fenestrae. The turtle skull closed secondarily, as the back of the skull was emarginated from behind ([7.17.51](/7.17.51)). Some parareptiles, conversely, have a lower temporal opening, which evolved independently.

The lesson is general: a single character, however conspicuous, can be lost, regained or convergent, and a classification is reliable only when it is based on a phylogeny reconstructed from many characters ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)). The names Synapsida and Diapsida survive because they name clades, not because every member has the skull type.

## 50.4. Synapsids and Sauropsids

### 50.4.1. The Basal Split

**Definition 50.4.1 (Synapsida and Sauropsida)** The **Synapsida** (synapsids) are the clade of amniotes comprising mammals and all extinct amniotes more closely related to mammals than to reptiles; ancestrally they have a single lower temporal fenestra. The **Sauropsida** (sauropsids) are the clade comprising living reptiles, including birds, and all extinct amniotes more closely related to them than to mammals.

Within Sauropsida, the extinct Parareptilia (mesosaurs, procolophonids and the large herbivorous pareiasaurs of the Permian) branch first; all other sauropsids form the Eureptilia, including the early *Captorhinus* and *Hylonomus* and the Diapsida. All living reptiles belong to Sauria, the crown group of diapsids, which divides into the Lepidosauria (tuatara, lizards and snakes) and the Archelosauria, the clade of turtles and archosaurs (crocodylians and birds). The name Reptilia is used either for the crown group (Sauria) or for the total group of sauropsids; in both senses it includes birds.

### 50.4.2. Two Ways of Living on Land

Synapsids and sauropsids solved the problems of active terrestrial life independently, and their solutions differ in almost every organ system. The comparison shows that there is more than one way to become a fast, enduring, water-conserving land animal.

A central problem is the conflict between locomotion and breathing identified by David Carrier (1987). Early amniotes walked with lateral undulation of the trunk, like a salamander or lizard, and ventilated their lungs with the same intercostal muscles. As a lizard runs, the trunk bends to one side and then the other, compressing one lung while the other expands; air moves from lung to lung but little enters or leaves the trachea, so a running lizard cannot breathe, and its activity is limited to short bursts fuelled by anaerobic metabolism. Lizards stop frequently to breathe.

Synapsids solved this by holding the limbs under the body and bending the trunk up and down instead of from side to side; the movements of the viscera during a gallop then pump air in time with the stride (one breath per stride in many mammals), and a new muscle, the diaphragm, ventilates the lungs independently of the rib cage. Sauropsids kept a sprawling or semi-erect posture longer and evolved accessory pumps and lungs with one-way airflow. Monitor lizards use a gular pump; crocodylians pull the liver backward with a diaphragmaticus muscle (the hepatic piston); birds ventilate air sacs with their sternum; and in alligators, monitor lizards and iguanas, as in birds, air flows in one direction through the lung, suggesting that unidirectional airflow evolved early in sauropsids (Farmer and Sanders 2010; [7.14.17](/7.14.17#sec-17.7.4), [7.17.64](/7.17.64)).

| System | Synapsid solution (mammals) | Sauropsid solution (reptiles, birds) |
|---|---|---|
| Posture and locomotion | Limbs under the body; dorsoventral flexion of the spine | Sprawling (lepidosaurs, turtles), semi-erect (crocodylians), erect and bipedal (dinosaurs, birds) |
| Ventilation | Rib aspiration plus diaphragm | Rib aspiration plus gular pump, hepatic piston, abdominal muscles (turtles), air sacs (birds) |
| Lung | Branching bronchial tree ending in blind alveoli; tidal flow | Septate lungs with large chambers; unidirectional flow in archosaurs and some lizards; parabronchi in birds |
| Heart | Ventricle completely divided; left aortic arch | Ventricle incompletely divided with variable intracardiac shunts (lepidosaurs, turtles); completely divided in crocodylians and birds; right aortic arch in birds |
| Nitrogenous waste | Urea, dissolved in a concentrated urine | Uric acid, precipitated and excreted as a paste with little water |
| Kidney and urine | Loop of Henle; urine up to 4–25 times as concentrated as plasma | No loops (lepidosaurs, turtles, crocodylians) or few (birds); urine modified in the cloaca, colon or bladder |
| Skin | Glandular skin with hair; sweat, sebaceous and mammary glands | Few glands; scales and feathers with β-keratins |
| Red blood cells | Without nuclei | Nucleated |

The table is a summary; the details of each system are treated in the comparative chapters ([7.17.63](/7.17.63)–[7.17.66](/7.17.66)) and their physiology in 7.14 ([7.14.16](/7.14.16#sec-16.8), [7.14.24](/7.14.24#sec-24.7)). The uricotelic strategy of sauropsids (**uricotelic**) is linked to the egg: a closed egg cannot get rid of urea or ammonia, and an embryo that makes uric acid stores it harmlessly in the allantois. Mammals, whose embryos excrete through the placenta, kept urea.

### 50.4.3. "Reptiles" as a Paraphyletic Group

In the traditional classification still used in many textbooks, amniotes form three classes: Reptilia, Aves and Mammalia. "Reptilia" in this sense contains the living turtles, lepidosaurs and crocodylians together with the extinct "mammal-like reptiles" (pelycosaurs and therapsids), dinosaurs, pterosaurs and marine reptiles, but excludes birds and mammals. It is a **paraphyletic group**, defined by the absence of feathers, hair and endothermy, characters of its descendants. Crocodylians are more closely related to birds than to lizards, and *Dimetrodon* is more closely related to humans than to any reptile.

There are two consistent ways to restore a natural group. One is to use Sauropsida for the reptile lineage, including birds, and Synapsida for the mammal lineage, so that the "mammal-like reptiles" are simply early synapsids. The other is to redefine Reptilia as a clade that includes birds: birds are then reptiles in the same sense in which humans are mammals. This course uses both conventions: Sauropsida for the total group and "reptiles" for the living non-avian sauropsids when convenient, with the understanding that birds belong to the group.

## 50.5. Ectothermy and Endothermy

### 50.5.1. Two Energy Economies

The distinction between **ectotherms**, which obtain their body heat mainly from the environment, and **endotherms**, which produce it metabolically, cuts across the amniote tree: among living amniotes, birds and mammals are endotherms, whereas turtles, lepidosaurs and crocodylians are ectotherms. The physiological mechanisms are treated in [7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6); here the focus is on the consequences for the form and ecology of animals.

An endotherm has a resting metabolic rate five to ten times that of an ectotherm of the same mass at the same body temperature (Bennett and Ruben 1979). The extra energy buys independence from environmental temperature, sustained high activity (aerobic capacity) and fast growth. Its costs are large:

- *Food.* An endotherm needs roughly ten times as much food as an ectotherm of the same mass. A 20 g mouse and a 20 g lizard living in the same desert differ in energy budget by an order of magnitude.
- *Conversion efficiency.* An ectotherm converts a much larger part of its assimilated energy into growth and reproduction: about 30–50 % in lizards and snakes against 1–3 % in birds and mammals, most of whose energy is spent on heat. Ectotherms are therefore efficient converters of food into biomass, and in many ecosystems the biomass of lizards or salamanders exceeds that of birds and mammals.
- *Body size and shape.* Because heat production scales with volume and heat loss with surface, small endotherms lose heat rapidly; the smallest are about 2 g (the Etruscan shrew *Suncus etruscus*, the bee hummingbird *Mellisuga helenae*), whereas many ectothermic tetrapods weigh less than 1 g. Ectotherms can also be long and thin (snakes, caecilians, legless lizards), a shape with a surface too large for an endotherm.
- *Activity.* Ectotherms depend on sun and warm surfaces, regulating temperature by behaviour (basking, posture, shuttling between sun and shade, [7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.4)); they can survive long periods without food, at little cost, in cold or dry seasons.

Neither strategy is "better": they are different solutions, and ectotherms thrive wherever food is scarce or unpredictable. The categories are also extremes of a continuum. Large ectotherms such as the leatherback turtle (*Dermochelys coriacea*) keep their bodies warm in cold seas by their size and countercurrent heat exchangers (gigantothermy); brooding pythons raise their temperature by shivering contractions; the tegu lizard *Salvator merianae* is endothermic during its breeding season (Tattersall et al. 2016); and some mammals (hibernators, sloths, naked mole-rats) let their temperature fall to that of their surroundings.

### 50.5.2. Inferring Metabolism in Fossils

Because endothermy evolved independently in the synapsid and sauropsid lineages, a recurring question is when each lineage became endothermic. Soft tissues and metabolism do not fossilize, so palaeontologists use indirect evidence:

| Evidence | Reasoning | Results |
|---|---|---|
| Insulation | Hair and feathers are insulation, needed only by an animal producing its own heat (or brooding) | Hair in Jurassic mammaliaforms (*Castorocauda*); feathers in many Jurassic and Cretaceous dinosaurs ([7.17.52](/7.17.52)) |
| Bone histology | Fast growth produces richly vascularized fibrolamellar bone; slow growth produces lamellar bone with growth rings | Fibrolamellar bone in many therapsids, dinosaurs and pterosaurs; growth rings also present in many dinosaurs |
| Respiratory turbinates | Thin scrolls of bone in the nasal cavity of mammals and birds recover heat and water from exhaled air, needed at high ventilation rates (Hillenius 1992) | Ridges for turbinates in therocephalians and cynodonts ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)) |
| Posture and locomotion | Sustained erect locomotion requires high aerobic capacity | Erect posture in dinosaurs and cynodonts |
| Isotopes and clumped isotopes | Oxygen isotopes of bone phosphate and clumped isotopes of eggshell carbonate record body temperature | Elevated body temperatures in Late Permian dicynodonts and cynodonts (Rey et al. 2017) and in many dinosaurs |
| Inner ear | The size of the semicircular canals relative to body size correlates with body temperature through the viscosity of the endolymph | A shift to endothermy near the origin of Mammaliamorpha, about 233 million years ago (Araújo et al. 2022) |

The evidence suggests that both lineages became endothermic gradually, the synapsids beginning in the Late Permian and completing the transition in the Late Triassic, the archosaurs perhaps much earlier than birds (in the common ancestor of dinosaurs and pterosaurs, or even of archosaurs, whose living crocodylian members would then be secondarily ectothermic, an idea supported by their four-chambered heart and growth rates of Triassic crocodile-line archosaurs). Hypotheses for the selective advantages of endothermy — aerobic capacity for sustained activity, parental care that keeps eggs or young warm, the ability to process food faster — are discussed in [7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.2).

## 50.6. Extinct Amniote Radiations

Much of amniote diversity is extinct, and several major groups have no living members. The dinosaurs and pterosaurs are treated with the archosaurs in [7.17.52](/7.17.52) and the extinct synapsids in [7.17.54](/7.17.54); this section surveys the others.

*Parareptiles.* The sister group of the eureptiles, diverse in the Permian and Triassic. Mesosaurs (*Mesosaurus*, Early Permian) were slender, aquatic fish-eaters about 1 m long, known from South Africa and South America; because they lived in coastal waters and could not cross an ocean, their distribution on both continents was an early piece of evidence for continental drift. Pareiasaurs were large herbivores up to 3 m long and 600 kg, with heavy, bony-studded skulls and osteoderms; they were part of the Permian megafauna and died out in the end-Permian extinction. Pareiasaurs were long proposed as the ancestors of turtles because of their bony armour, a hypothesis now abandoned.

*Marine reptiles.* During the Mesozoic, several lineages of diapsids returned to the sea and became the dominant marine predators.

- *Ichthyosaurs* (Ichthyopterygia) evolved a dolphin-like, fully pelagic body with a crescent-shaped tail fin, a dorsal fin, paddle-like limbs with many extra phalanges and digits, enormous eyes (up to 26 cm in *Temnodontosaurus*) and live birth; they appeared in the Early Triassic and died out about 94 million years ago, before the end of the Cretaceous. Some of the earliest are Chinese: *Chaohusaurus* from Chaohu in Anhui (about 248 million years ago) still had a short, lizard-like body. The Late Triassic Guanling biota of Guizhou (about 230 million years ago) has produced complete skeletons of large ichthyosaurs such as *Guizhouichthyosaurus*, preserved together with crinoids attached to driftwood.
- *Sauropterygians* include the small pachypleurosaurs, the nothosaurs and the plesiosaurs. *Keichousaurus hui*, a pachypleurosaur about 20–30 cm long from the Middle Triassic Xingyi fauna of Guizhou (about 240 million years ago), is one of the most familiar Chinese fossils; it is found by the thousands, sometimes with embryos inside the mother, showing that it was viviparous. Plesiosaurs (Jurassic and Cretaceous) swam with four large flippers, used as hydrofoils; the long-necked plesiosauroids such as *Elasmosaurus*, with up to 72 cervical vertebrae, and the short-necked, large-headed pliosaurs, with skulls up to 2 m long, were the top predators of their seas. Placodonts were Triassic shellfish-eaters with flat crushing teeth, some armoured like turtles.
- *Other marine diapsids*: the hupehsuchians of the Early Triassic of Hubei, the long-necked tanystropheids and *Dinocephalosaurus* of the Middle Triassic of Yunnan and Guizhou, which gave birth to live young although it belongs to the archosauromorphs, a group otherwise oviparous (Liu et al. 2017), and the filter-feeding *Atopodentatus* of the Luoping biota of Yunnan. The mosasaurs of the Late Cretaceous were marine squamates, relatives of monitor lizards or snakes ([7.17.51](/7.17.51)). The marine crocodylomorphs are treated in [7.17.52](/7.17.52).

The Triassic marine reptile faunas of southern China (Chaohu, Luoping, Panxian, Xingyi, Guanling), spanning about 20 million years after the end-Permian extinction, document how quickly marine ecosystems recovered and how many reptile lineages independently adapted to the sea.

*Choristoderes.* Semi-aquatic diapsids of uncertain position that survived from the Jurassic to the Miocene. The long-necked *Hyphalosaurus* is one of the most abundant vertebrates of the Early Cretaceous Jehol biota of Liaoning, and crocodile-like champsosaurs survived the end-Cretaceous extinction.

*Other Triassic archosauromorphs* include the beaked, herbivorous rhynchosaurs, abundant in the Middle and Late Triassic, the gliding kuehneosaurs and the strange, chameleon-like drepanosaurs.

## 50.7. Comparison with Traditional Classifications

Chinese textbooks treat the amniotes as three classes, Reptilia, Aves and Mammalia, and divide the fossil reptiles by the type of temporal fenestration into five groups: anapsids (cotylosaurs and turtles), parapsids (ichthyosaurs), euryapsids (plesiosaurs), synapsids (pelycosaurs and therapsids) and diapsids (archosaurs and lepidosaurs). The correspondence is as follows:

| Traditional treatment | Current view |
|---|---|
| Class Reptilia, the first true terrestrial vertebrates, giving rise to birds and mammals | Reptilia is paraphyletic as traditionally defined. Amniota divides into Synapsida (with mammals) and Sauropsida (with birds); birds are archosaurian reptiles ([7.17.52](/7.17.52)) |
| Reptiles derived from cotylosaurs (Cotylosauria), the "stem reptiles", ancestors of all other reptiles, close to *Seymouria* | Cotylosauria is a paraphyletic assemblage of early amniotes (captorhinids) and amniote relatives (diadectomorphs); *Seymouria* is a reptiliomorph outside Amniota, not an ancestor |
| The oldest amniote skeleton, 338 million years old, from Scotland (*Westlothiana*) | *Westlothiana* is now considered a stem amniote or reptiliomorph; the oldest undoubted amniotes are *Hylonomus* and *Paleothyris* (about 312–318 million years) |
| Turtles as anapsids, the survivors of the earliest reptiles | Turtles are diapsids, sister group of archosaurs; their skull closed secondarily (§50.3.4) |
| Ichthyosaurs (parapsids) and plesiosaurs (euryapsids) as separate subclasses | Both are diapsid lineages that lost or modified the lower fenestra; their relationship to each other is uncertain |
| Synapsids as "mammal-like reptiles" (Pelycosauria, Therapsida), ancestors of mammals | Synapsids are not reptiles: they are the mammal lineage, and pelycosaurs and therapsids are paraphyletic grades of it ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)) |
| "Thecodonts" (Thecodontia), the Triassic stem archosaurs, as ancestors of dinosaurs, pterosaurs, crocodiles and birds | Thecodontia is paraphyletic; the Triassic archosaurs belong to the crocodile line (Pseudosuchia) or the bird line (Avemetatarsalia) ([7.17.52](/7.17.52)) |

The textbooks' account of the amniotic egg (the shell, the amnion and chorion formed by folds around the embryo, the extraembryonic coelom between them, the allantois from the hindgut as respiratory and excretory organ) agrees with §50.2. Their list of features of the reptile body that adapt it to life on land — keratinized skin with few glands, a well-ossified skeleton with a single occipital condyle and differentiated cervical vertebrae with atlas and axis, two or more sacral vertebrae, a rib cage and sternum (thorax) that ventilate the lungs, a metanephric kidney excreting uric acid, internal fertilization — corresponds to §50.1 and to the comparison with synapsids in §50.4.2. Their emphasis on the amniotic egg as the reason for the "age of reptiles" in the Mesozoic is a simplification: the dominance of dinosaurs and pterosaurs after the end-Triassic extinction depended on many other factors ([7.17.52](/7.17.52)).

The traditional terms for skull types remain useful as descriptions, but the classification of reptiles by skull type has been replaced by a phylogeny in which synapsids and sauropsids are the two primary branches of the amniotes.

## Summary

- Amniotes (Definition 50.1.1): the crown group of tetrapods with an amniotic egg; skeletal synapomorphies are the pterygoid flange (pterygoideus muscle), the axis vertebra, the astragalus with a mesotarsal ankle and two or more sacral vertebrae. The earliest are *Hylonomus* and *Paleothyris* (about 312–318 million years ago, Nova Scotia).
- Soft-tissue characters inferred by phylogenetic bracketing: keratinized, waterproof skin; costal ventilation; a long, ringed trachea; the metanephric kidney with a ureter; internal fertilization; no larva and no lateral line.
- The amniotic egg (Definition 50.2.1): amnion and chorion from the somatopleure, yolk sac and allantois from the splanchnopleure; the chorioallantoic membrane is the respiratory surface; the allantois stores uric acid. Shells are calcareous (birds, crocodylians, many turtles) or flexible (most squamates, monotremes, probably ancestral).
- Eggshell gas exchange follows Fick's law; conductance scales as $W^{0.78}$ and incubation time as $W^{0.22}$, so all bird eggs lose about 15–18 % of their mass as water.
- Temporal fenestrae (Definition 50.3.1): anapsid (none, ancestral), synapsid (one lower), diapsid (upper and lower), euryapsid and parapsid (one upper, modified diapsid). They provide space and attachment for jaw muscles and remove unstressed bone; turtles closed theirs secondarily, so fenestration alone is not a reliable classification.
- Synapsids and sauropsids (Definition 50.4.1) diverged by about 318 million years ago and solved the problems of land differently: posture, diaphragm versus accessory pumps and one-way lungs, divided versus shunting hearts, urea versus uric acid. Reptilia in the traditional sense is paraphyletic; birds are reptiles.
- Ectothermy and endothermy are two energy economies; endotherms pay a tenfold energy cost for activity and independence from ambient temperature. Endothermy evolved independently in synapsids and archosaurs, inferred from insulation, bone histology, turbinates, isotopes and the inner ear.
- Extinct radiations include parareptiles, the Mesozoic marine reptiles (ichthyosaurs, sauropterygians; the Triassic faunas of southern China with *Chaohusaurus* and *Keichousaurus*), choristoderes and other archosauromorphs.

## Questions

1. Why can the amniotic egg not be observed directly in the fossils of the earliest amniotes, and how do we know that they had one?

   **Answer** Eggs and embryos fossilize only under exceptional conditions; the oldest known amniote embryos (mesosaurs, about 286 million years old) are some 30 million years younger than the first amniote skeletons, and the oldest fossil eggshells are younger still. The amniotic egg is inferred by phylogenetic bracketing: all living amniotes, both synapsids (mammals, whose monotremes still lay eggs and whose therians retain the four membranes in the placenta) and sauropsids, have the four extraembryonic membranes, so the most parsimonious inference is that their last common ancestor had them. Early fossil amniotes are recognized by skeletal characters that are also synapomorphies of the living groups (pterygoid flange, axis, astragalus, two sacral vertebrae), and are assumed to have had the egg because they belong to the clade.
2. A chicken egg weighs 60 g and an ostrich egg 1.5 kg. Using $G = 0.432\,W^{0.78}$ and $I = 12.03\,W^{0.217}$ with a vapour-pressure difference of 34 Torr, calculate the water lost by each egg during incubation. What does the result imply about the design of eggs?

   **Answer** Chicken: $G = 0.432 \times 60^{0.78} \approx 10.5\,\mathrm{mg\,d^{-1}\,Torr^{-1}}$, $I = 12.03 \times 60^{0.217} \approx 29$ days (the observed 21 days is shorter than the regression predicts). Water lost $= 10.5 \times 34 \times 29 \approx 10\,300\,\mathrm{mg} \approx 10.4\,\mathrm{g}$, about 17 % of 60 g. Ostrich: $G = 0.432 \times 1500^{0.78} \approx 129$, $I = 12.03 \times 1500^{0.217} \approx 59$ days; water lost $\approx 129 \times 34 \times 59 \approx 259\,000\,\mathrm{mg} = 259\,\mathrm{g}$, again about 17 % of 1500 g. Because the exponents of conductance and incubation time sum to almost 1, larger eggs have relatively lower conductance per unit mass but incubate longer, and every egg loses about the same fraction of its water. The shell's pore area is evidently tuned so that the egg loses enough water to form the air cell and concentrate the contents, but not so much that the embryo dehydrates; any change in incubation time or nest humidity must be matched by a change in pore area.
3. Explain why the skull of a living turtle, which has no temporal fenestrae, is not evidence that turtles are primitive "anapsid" reptiles.

   **Answer** A character state that looks primitive can be secondarily derived. Molecular phylogenies place turtles within the diapsids, as the sister group of archosaurs. Fossil stem turtles such as *Pappochelys* and *Eorhynchochelys* have upper and lower temporal fenestrae, showing that the turtle lineage began with a diapsid skull. In later turtles the fenestrae were lost and the skull roof was instead emarginated from behind and below, leaving a solid roof with deep notches through which the jaw muscles pass. The absence of fenestrae in living turtles is therefore a reversal, not a retained ancestral condition, and classifying turtles with early amniotes on that basis groups them by a shared absence, an unreliable criterion.
4. Compare how synapsids and sauropsids resolved the conflict between running and breathing described by Carrier.

   **Answer** The conflict arises because early amniotes used the intercostal and other axial muscles both to bend the trunk laterally in walking and to expand the rib cage in breathing, and lateral bending compresses one lung while expanding the other, so little air enters or leaves. Synapsids moved the limbs under the body and bent the spine dorsoventrally, so that locomotion assists ventilation (one breath per stride in galloping mammals), and they evolved the diaphragm, a breathing muscle independent of the rib cage. Sauropsids evolved additional pumps and lungs with one-way airflow: the gular pump of monitor lizards, the hepatic piston of crocodylians, the air-sac system of birds, which ventilates the lung independently of the flight muscles, and unidirectional airflow through septate lungs, which may be ancestral for archosaurs and some lizards. Both groups achieved sustained aerobic activity by different structural means.
5. (Open) The amniotic egg is often called the key innovation that allowed vertebrates to conquer the land. Evaluate this claim.

   **Answer** Points to consider: (a) The egg allowed reproduction independent of water and direct development of large hatchlings, and every fully terrestrial vertebrate is an amniote. (b) But amniotes did not dominate terrestrial faunas immediately: for 30 million years after their origin they were small and few, and large terrestrial herbivores and predators of the Late Carboniferous included non-amniotes such as diadectomorphs; amniote dominance came in the Permian, alongside other changes (limb-based locomotion, costal ventilation, waterproof skin, efficient kidneys, feeding on plants). (c) Non-amniote tetrapods also breed on land (direct-developing frogs and salamanders), but only with small eggs; the amniotic egg removed the size limit. (d) The egg also opened new possibilities unrelated to land — viviparity, which allowed marine reptiles and mammals to live entirely in water. A **key innovation** in the strict sense ([7.10.31](/7.10.31#sec-31.5.2)) is one that increases diversification rate; testing that would require comparing diversification of amniotes with that of their sister group over the same time interval, which is difficult because of the poor early record. The egg was necessary but not sufficient for amniote success.
