Muscles are the motors of the vertebrate body. They make up 30–50 % of the body mass of most vertebrates, and in a fish more than half; every movement of the skeleton, of the jaws and gills, of the eyes and of the skin is the work of muscles. For comparative anatomy the muscles pose a special problem. Bones fossilize and keep their identity over hundreds of millions of years, but a muscle may split into several, fuse with its neighbours, move its attachments and change its function as a lineage evolves. A muscle named in human anatomy (the "pectoralis", the "deltoid") is therefore not necessarily the homologue of the muscle of the same name in a frog. The key that allows us to follow muscles through the vertebrates is their embryonic origin and their nerve supply: a muscle keeps its motor nerve even when it migrates far from its origin.

This chapter first reviews muscle tissue and the anatomy of a muscle organ (§62.1), then sets up the classification of skeletal muscles by origin and innervation (§62.2). The somatic muscles are followed through the vertebrates in three groups: the axial muscles of the trunk and tail (§62.3), and the hypobranchial, eye and appendicular muscles (§62.4). The branchiomeric muscles of the pharyngeal arches, which become the muscles of the jaws, face, throat and larynx in tetrapods, are treated arch by arch in §62.5; the integumentary muscles in §62.6. Section 62.7 turns to the mechanics of muscle architecture and levers, and §62.8 to electric organs, muscles converted into batteries. The physiology of contraction, from the neuromuscular junction to the Hill force–velocity relation and the types of muscle fibres, is in [7.14.8](/7.14.8)–[7.14.10](/7.14.10); the development of somites and their compartments in [7.5.23](/7.5.23). The skeleton on which the muscles act is the subject of [7.17.58](/7.17.58)–[7.17.61](/7.17.61), and the cranial nerves that supply the head muscles are tabulated in [7.17.67](/7.17.67).

## 62.1. Muscle Tissue and the Organization of a Muscle

### 62.1.1. Three Kinds of Muscle Tissue

Vertebrates have three kinds of muscle tissue, distinguished by their structure, their embryonic origin and their control ([7.17.2](/7.17.2); physiology in [7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.1)).

| | Skeletal muscle | Cardiac muscle | Smooth muscle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cells | Long multinucleate fibres (syncytia formed by fusion of myoblasts), nuclei peripheral | Branched cells with one or two central nuclei, joined end to end by intercalated discs | Spindle-shaped cells with one central nucleus |
| Striation | Striated (sarcomeres in register) | Striated | Not striated |
| Location | Attached to the skeleton, the skin or the eye; walls of the pharynx and larynx | Wall of the heart (and the muscular sleeves of the great veins) | Walls of the gut, blood vessels, airways, urogenital ducts; iris; arrector pili muscles |
| Origin | Paraxial mesoderm (somites and unsegmented head mesoderm) | Lateral plate mesoderm (splanchnic layer, heart fields) | Splanchnic mesoderm, local mesenchyme; iris muscles from neural ectoderm |
| Control | Somatic motor neurons; voluntary | Autonomic nerves modify an intrinsic rhythm | Autonomic nerves, hormones, local factors |

Comparative anatomy deals mostly with the skeletal muscles, which are individually named, attached to identifiable bones and supplied by identifiable nerves. Smooth and cardiac muscle are treated with the organs whose walls they form: the gut in [7.17.63](/7.17.63), the heart in [7.17.65](/7.17.65).

Skeletal muscle fibres differ in their metabolism and speed. Fibres rich in myoglobin and mitochondria and well supplied with capillaries look red; they contract slowly and resist fatigue. Pale (white) fibres have few mitochondria, rely on glycolysis, contract fast and fatigue quickly; intermediate fibres lie between ([7.14.9](/7.14.9#sec-9.9)). The proportions match the use of a muscle. The dark leg meat of a chicken, which walks all day, is red; its breast muscle, used in short bursts of flight, is white, whereas the flight muscles of pigeons and other long-distance fliers are red. In fishes the red fibres form a thin superficial wedge along the horizontal septum, used in sustained cruising, while the bulk of the myomeres is white muscle recruited in fast starts and bursts ([7.14.9](/7.14.9#sec-9.11.2)); in tunas the red muscle lies deep and warm near the vertebral column ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)).

### 62.1.2. The Muscle as an Organ

A skeletal muscle is an organ of muscle fibres and connective tissue. Each fibre is wrapped in endomysium, bundles of fibres (fascicles) in perimysium and the whole muscle in epimysium; these collagenous sheaths continue into the tendon, which transmits the force to the skeleton. A cord-like tendon goes with a round muscle belly, a flat sheet-like tendon (aponeurosis) with a flat muscle. Tendons are cheap tissue: they have little metabolism and let a short, metabolically expensive muscle belly act at a distance, as the long tendons of the forearm muscles move the fingertips of a primate or a raccoon. In running ungulates and kangaroos the tendons are also springs that store elastic energy in one stride and return it in the next.

Each muscle has an *origin*, the attachment that usually stays fixed, and an *insertion* on the bone that is moved; a muscle with several origins has several heads (the biceps, triceps and quadriceps). Origin and insertion can exchange roles: the sternocleidomastoid pulls the head down and turns it when the trunk is fixed, but raises the sternum in forced inspiration when the head is fixed.

Muscles never push: they only pull. Every movement is therefore controlled by at least two muscles or groups acting in opposite directions, the agonist (prime mover) and the antagonist, assisted by synergists that add force or stabilize a joint. Anatomists name muscles by their action:

| Action | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Flexor / extensor | Decreases / increases the angle of a joint | Biceps brachii / triceps brachii |
| Adductor / abductor | Moves a part toward / away from the midline | Adductors of the thigh / deltoid |
| Levator / depressor | Raises / lowers a part | Levator palatoquadrati / depressor mandibulae |
| Protractor / retractor | Moves a part forward / backward | Protractor and retractor of the lens in fishes |
| Rotator (pronator, supinator) | Turns a part about its long axis | Pronator teres, supinator |
| Constrictor (sphincter) / dilator | Narrows / widens an opening | Constrictors of the pharynx; pupillary sphincter |

Muscle names combine shape (deltoid, trapezius, serratus), fibre direction (rectus, obliquus, transversus), position (pectoralis, temporalis), number of heads (biceps, triceps), attachments (sternohyoid, from sternum to hyoid) and size or depth (major, minor, superficialis, profundus). Because most names were given in human anatomy and then transferred to other animals, the same name in two classes is a hypothesis of homology, not a proof.

## 62.2. Classification by Origin and Innervation

### 62.2.1. The Mesodermal Sources of Muscle

All skeletal muscles of the vertebrate body arise from mesoderm, but from two different populations.

1. *Somites.* The trunk paraxial mesoderm segments into **somites** ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)). Each somite gives a **sclerotome** (vertebrae and ribs) and a **dermomyotome**, whose **myotome** forms the segmental muscle blocks of the trunk ([7.5.23](/7.5.23#sec-23.10)). The epaxial (dorsomedial) lip of the dermomyotome gives the muscles above the vertebral column; the hypaxial (ventrolateral) lip gives the body-wall muscles and, by migrating precursor cells, the muscles of the limbs, the diaphragm and the tongue. Somites in front of the forelimb, the occipital somites, contribute the hypobranchial and tongue muscles.
2. *Cranial mesoderm.* In the head the paraxial mesoderm does not form somites (in amniotes; whether it is segmented into "somitomeres" is debated). Together with the prechordal mesoderm it supplies the extrinsic eye muscles, and, by entering the pharyngeal arches together with adjacent splanchnic (lateral) mesoderm, the muscles of the arches. These pharyngeal arch muscles share their progenitors with part of the heart (the second heart field), the "cardiopharyngeal field" that already exists in tunicates ([7.17.43](/7.17.43)).

Neural crest cells, which form the skeleton of the arches ([7.17.58](/7.17.58)), also form the tendons and connective tissue of the head muscles and guide where they attach; in the trunk the corresponding connective tissue comes from the somites and the lateral plate. The muscle cells and their connective tissue therefore have separate origins, and the muscle fibres find their attachments by following connective-tissue patterns laid down by other cells.

### 62.2.2. Somatic and Branchiomeric Muscles

On the basis of these origins, skeletal muscles fall into two great systems, with a third, the integumentary muscles, split off from both.

| System | Origin | Motor nerves | Groups | Section |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Somatic (somitic) muscles | Myotomes of the somites; eye muscles from prechordal and anterior paraxial head mesoderm | Ventral roots of spinal nerves; cranial nerves III, IV, VI (eye) and XII (tongue) | Axial muscles (epaxial and hypaxial); hypobranchial and tongue muscles; extrinsic eye muscles; appendicular muscles | 62.3–62.4 |
| Branchiomeric muscles | Cranial paraxial and splanchnic mesoderm of the pharyngeal arches | Cranial nerves V, VII, IX, X and XI (the branchiomotor nerves) | Muscles of the mandibular arch, hyoid arch and branchial arches; in tetrapods jaw, facial, pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles and the trapezius group | 62.5 |
| Integumentary muscles | Split off from somatic or branchiomeric muscles | The nerve of the parent muscle | Cutaneous trunci (panniculus carnosus) and sphincter colli, facial muscles of mammals | 62.6 |

Traditional anatomy divided muscles into "somatic" (of the body wall) and "visceral" (of the gut), and placed the branchiomeric muscles among the visceral muscles because they lie in the wall of the pharynx, the front of the gut. Branchiomeric muscles are indeed associated with the pharynx and serve feeding and breathing, but they are striated and voluntary, and their motor neurons lie in the brainstem in a separate column (the branchiomotor or special visceral efferent column, [7.17.67](/7.17.67)). The table follows the modern division by mesodermal source.

### 62.2.3. Innervation as the Criterion of Homology

Three kinds of evidence identify homologous muscles in different vertebrates: the position, attachments and relations of the muscle in the adult; the embryonic origin and the path of its migration; and its nerve supply. The third is the most useful, because a motor nerve enters a muscle primordium early and follows it wherever it migrates. Three examples make the point.

- *The diaphragm.* The mammalian diaphragm separates the thorax from the abdomen, but it is supplied by the phrenic nerve, which arises from cervical spinal nerves (C3–C5 in humans) and runs down through the thorax. The muscle precursors come from cervical somites and migrate back into the developing diaphragm, trailing their nerve (§62.3.4).
- *The hypobranchial muscles.* The muscles in the floor of a shark's pharynx lie in the head but are supplied by spinal nerves (the hypobranchial nerve from the first spinal nerves): their precursors grow forward from the anterior trunk myotomes below the gills (§62.4.1). In amniotes the corresponding tongue muscles are supplied by the hypoglossal nerve (XII), a cranial nerve formed from the occipital spinal nerves incorporated into the skull.
- *The trapezius.* The trapezius and sternocleidomastoid of mammals lie in the neck and shoulder, but they are supplied by the accessory nerve (XI), a branch split off from the vagus: they descend from the cucullaris, the most posterior levator of the gill arches of fishes (§62.5.4).

Innervation is not infallible. Nerves combine into plexuses before entering the limbs, so that one limb muscle may receive fibres from several spinal segments, and in a few cases a muscle has changed its nerve. Homologies are therefore accepted when position, development and innervation agree.

## 62.3. Axial Muscles

### 62.3.1. Myomeres

The trunk and tail muscles of fishes are the least modified somatic muscles: a series of muscle blocks, the **myomeres** (one per body segment), separated by sheets of connective tissue, the myosepta. The muscle fibres of a myomere run roughly longitudinally between neighbouring myosepta, and the myosepta transmit their force to the vertebral column and the skin. Contraction of the myomeres of one side in a wave running backward bends the body into the undulation of swimming ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)).

The myomeres of lancelets are V-shaped in side view, with the apex pointing forward, and those of the left and right sides alternate by half a segment, which suits side-to-side bending of the notochord ([7.17.43](/7.17.43)). In cyclostomes they are W-shaped, but there is no horizontal septum dividing them into upper and lower halves. In gnathostomes the myomeres are W-shaped with a forward-pointing middle cone and backward-pointing upper and lower cones, and each myomere is cone-shaped in three dimensions, so that in a cross section through the tail the myosepta appear as a series of concentric rings. Because each myomere spans several vertebrae and the fibres within it run at different angles at different depths, fibres deep and superficial shorten by similar amounts when the body bends, and all can work near the optimum of their length–tension curve ([7.14.9](/7.14.9#sec-9.3)).

### 62.3.2. Epaxial and Hypaxial Muscles

In gnathostomes a sheet of connective tissue, the horizontal septum, runs from the vertebral column to the skin at the level of the lateral line and divides the axial muscles into dorsal and ventral masses.

**Definition 62.3.1** The **epaxial muscles** are the axial muscles dorsal to the horizontal septum (dorsal to the transverse processes of the vertebrae in tetrapods), derived from the epaxial part of the myotomes and supplied by the dorsal rami of the spinal nerves. The **hypaxial muscles** are the axial muscles ventral to the horizontal septum, derived from the hypaxial part of the myotomes and supplied by the ventral rami of the spinal nerves; they form the lateral and ventral body wall.

The division is a gnathostome character ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.1)). It is linked to the spinal nerves: in lancelets and lampreys the dorsal (sensory) and ventral (motor) roots of a spinal nerve stay separate, and the muscles are supplied by ventral roots only; in gnathostomes the two roots join into a mixed spinal nerve that divides again into a dorsal ramus (epaxial muscles and dorsal skin) and a ventral ramus (hypaxial muscles, ventral skin and, in tetrapods, the limbs) ([7.17.67](/7.17.67)).

In fishes the epaxial muscles are large, often larger than the hypaxial ones, and little differentiated; they are the main swimming muscles together with the hypaxial muscles of the tail. The hypaxial muscles of the trunk are thinner because the body cavity lies within them. Even in fishes the beginnings of the tetrapod pattern can be seen: a thin superficial layer of oblique fibres on the flank, a longitudinal band beside the ventral midline (the precursor of the rectus abdominis) and deep fibres attached to the ribs. Left and right hypaxial muscles meet at a midventral connective-tissue line, the linea alba.

### 62.3.3. The Axial Muscles of Tetrapods

On land the trunk is no longer the main organ of propulsion. It must hold the viscera, stiffen the body between the limbs, support the head and, in amniotes, ventilate the lungs. The axial muscles change accordingly:

1. *The myosepta fade.* Neighbouring myomeres fuse into long muscles, and segmentation survives only in deep short muscles between vertebrae and in the tendinous intersections of the rectus abdominis (five in frogs, three or four in humans).
2. *The hypaxial body wall becomes layered.* The flank musculature divides into three sheets with crossing fibre directions, like plywood: the external oblique (fibres running ventrally and backward), the internal oblique (ventrally and forward) and the transversus abdominis (dorsoventral). The rectus abdominis runs longitudinally beside the linea alba from the pubis to the sternum. The crossed layers resist bending and twisting of the trunk and compress the abdomen.
3. *The epaxial muscles shrink relative to the hypaxial and limb muscles*, because the horizontal septum lies higher than in fishes; they become a set of longitudinal bundles that extend and bend the vertebral column and support the head.
4. *Limb muscles spread over the trunk.* The large extrinsic muscles of the limbs (latissimus dorsi, pectoralis, serratus, trapezius) cover much of the axial musculature from outside.

The changes build up from salamanders to mammals.

- *Amphibians.* Salamanders still show myosepta throughout, but the hypaxial muscle is already split into external oblique, internal oblique and transversus, with a rectus abdominis beside the linea alba; the epaxial muscle is a single segmented dorsalis trunci. Salamanders walk with a lateral undulation of the trunk, and the axial muscles still do most of the work. In frogs, whose trunk is short and rigid, the myosepta have disappeared except for the intersections of the rectus.
- *Reptiles.* The epaxial muscle divides into three longitudinal systems: the transversospinalis (medial; from transverse processes to spines), the longissimus (central and largest) and the iliocostalis (lateral, inserting on the ribs), with short intersegmental muscles in the depth (interspinales, intertransversarii). The oblique muscles of the thorax become external and internal intercostal muscles between the ribs, which move the ribs and ventilate the lungs (costal aspiration, [7.17.64](/7.17.64)); in front of the ribs the oblique layer becomes the scalene muscles of the neck. Snakes have hundreds of segments, each with a set of epaxial and hypaxial muscles and costocutaneous muscles running from the ribs to the ventral scales, which they use in rectilinear locomotion. Turtles, whose trunk vertebrae are fused to the carapace, have lost most trunk axial muscles; the remaining ones move the neck, tail and limb girdles and ventilate the lungs ([7.17.51](/7.17.51)).
- *Birds.* The trunk vertebrae are fused into the notarium and synsacrum, so the trunk epaxial muscles are vestigial; only the neck, with its many mobile vertebrae, and the tail, which moves the pygostyle and the tail feathers, have well developed axial muscles. The body wall is thin, and the large flight and leg muscles dominate ([7.17.53](/7.17.53)).
- *Mammals.* The epaxial muscles form the erector spinae (iliocostalis, longissimus, spinalis) and the deep transversospinal muscles (semispinalis, multifidus, rotatores), with interspinales and intertransversarii; the splenius of the neck is new. The thoracic hypaxial muscles give the external and internal intercostals and the serratus muscles; the lumbar hypaxial muscles give the psoas and quadratus lumborum (the "tenderloin"), which flex the vertebral column and the hip. The muscles of the tail are reduced in species without a tail, and part of them become the anal sphincter and the muscles of the pelvic floor.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Myomeres | V-shaped (lancelet), W-shaped (cyclostomes) | W-shaped, cone-shaped in 3D | Present in salamanders; lost in frogs except rectus intersections | Lost; deep short segmental muscles | Lost | Lost; rectus intersections, deep segmental muscles |
| Horizontal septum | Absent | Present; epaxial and hypaxial muscles | Present, high | Present (at transverse processes) | Present | Present |

| Hypaxial muscles | Not separated | Thin in trunk; oblique and rectus fibres beginning | External and internal oblique, transversus, rectus abdominis | As in amphibians, plus intercostals, scalenes | Thin body wall, intercostals | As in reptiles, plus serratus, psoas, quadratus lumborum; diaphragm |

### 62.3.4. The Diaphragm

The muscular diaphragm, a dome of skeletal muscle between the thoracic and abdominal cavities, is found only in mammals. Its muscle fibres arise from cervical somites (C3–C5 in humans), whose migratory hypaxial precursors invade the pleuroperitoneal folds; the developing diaphragm then descends, carrying the phrenic nerve with it. Contraction of the diaphragm flattens the dome and expands the thorax, so that ventilation becomes independent of the ribs ([7.17.64](/7.17.64); the separation of the body cavities in [7.17.63](/7.17.63)). The diaphragm is therefore a hypaxial muscle of the neck that has moved into the trunk; its innervation records the move. Crocodylians have a "diaphragmaticus" muscle that pulls the liver backward in breathing, but it runs from the pubis to the liver capsule and is not homologous with the mammalian diaphragm (convergence, [7.17.64](/7.17.64)).

## 62.4. Hypobranchial, Eye and Appendicular Muscles

### 62.4.1. Hypobranchial and Tongue Muscles

The *hypobranchial muscles* of fishes are longitudinal somatic muscles in the floor of the pharynx, between the pectoral girdle and the lower jaw and gill arches. In sharks they comprise the coracoarcuales (from the coracoid bar to the gill arches), the coracomandibularis (to the lower jaw) and the coracohyoideus (to the hyoid arch). They form the floor of the pharynx and the pericardial cavity, and by pulling the floor of the mouth and the arches backward they open the mouth and expand the pharynx in feeding and ventilation; in suction feeding fishes they help to expand the mouth cavity in a few milliseconds ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)). The embryo shows their origin: buds from the ventral ends of the occipital and anterior trunk myotomes grow forward beneath the gill region (the hypoglossal cord), which explains their supply by spinal nerves. Muscles cannot grow through the gill arches themselves, and the somatic muscles therefore reach the head only below and above the pharynx.

In tetrapods, where gills are lost, the hypobranchial muscles become the muscles of the hyoid apparatus and the tongue. The infrahyoid muscles of the neck (sternohyoid, sternothyroid, thyrohyoid, omohyoid) and the geniohyoid derive from them. The tongue is a new organ of tetrapods: a muscular tongue first appears in amphibians and is used to capture prey by projection in frogs, salamanders and chameleons. In mammals the extrinsic tongue muscles (genioglossus, hyoglossus, styloglossus) move the tongue as a whole and the intrinsic muscles, with longitudinal, transverse and vertical fibres and no bony attachment, change its shape; the tongue works as a muscular hydrostat. All these tongue muscles are supplied by the hypoglossal nerve (XII) and are homologous to the hypobranchial muscles of fishes.

### 62.4.2. Extrinsic Eye Muscles

Six extrinsic muscles move the eyeball in all vertebrates from lampreys to mammals, and their arrangement and nerve supply are among the most conservative features of vertebrate anatomy. Four rectus muscles (superior, inferior, medial and lateral) arise from the back of the orbit and insert on the eyeball above, below, in front of and behind its equator; two oblique muscles (superior and inferior) arise from the front of the orbit.

| Muscle | Nerve | Traditional source (preotic segment) | Main action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Superior rectus | Oculomotor (III) | First (premandibular) | Elevates the gaze |
| Inferior rectus | Oculomotor (III) | First | Depresses the gaze |
| Medial rectus | Oculomotor (III) | First | Turns the eye forward (adduction) |
| Inferior oblique | Oculomotor (III) | First | Rotates the eye, elevates |
| Superior oblique | Trochlear (IV) | Second (mandibular) | Rotates the eye, depresses |
| Lateral rectus | Abducens (VI) | Third (hyoid) | Turns the eye backward (abduction) |
| Retractor bulbi (amphibians, reptiles, many mammals) | Abducens (VI) | Third | Pulls the eye into the orbit |

Classical embryology, working on shark embryos, derived these muscles from three pairs of head cavities in front of the ear, the "preotic somites": the premandibular cavity gives the muscles supplied by III, the mandibular cavity the superior oblique (IV), the hyoid cavity the lateral rectus (VI). Amniote embryos have no comparable cavities, and fate mapping shows that the eye muscles come from the prechordal mesoderm and the most anterior unsegmented paraxial head mesoderm; the three nerve groups, however, are found in all gnathostomes, and the correspondence between nerves and muscle groups holds. Lampreys differ in detail: one of their rectus muscles, often called the inferior (or caudal) rectus, is supplied by the abducens rather than by the oculomotor nerve.

Accessory muscles derived from the same sources move the eyelids and the nictitating membrane: the levator palpebrae superioris of reptiles and mammals (supplied by III), and the pyramidalis and quadratus that draw the nictitating membrane across the eye of birds and reptiles (VI). The retractor bulbi, which first appears in amphibians, pulls the eyeball inward; frogs use it in swallowing, pushing the prey down the pharynx by pulling the eyes into the roof of the mouth. In eyeless cave fishes and in blind subterranean mammals the eye muscles regress together with the eye.

### 62.4.3. Appendicular Muscles

The muscles of fins and limbs are divided by their attachments into two groups. *Extrinsic* muscles arise outside the appendage, on the axial skeleton or the fascia of the trunk, and insert on the girdle or the proximal limb; they move the appendage as a whole. *Intrinsic* muscles arise on the girdle or the proximal limb bones and insert on more distal bones; they move parts of the appendage against each other. In every appendage the muscles form two primary masses, a dorsal mass, which gives extensors, abductors and levators, and a ventral mass, which gives flexors, adductors and depressors.

The appendicular muscles of fishes are simple. The median fins have many small muscles (inclinators, erectors and depressors of the fin rays) derived from the adjacent myomeres. The paired fins of sharks have a dorsal levator (abductor) and a ventral depressor (adductor) sheet, which arise from the girdle and the trunk fascia and move the fin as a unit; there are few intrinsic muscles, so a shark fin cannot bend locally. Ray-finned fishes have superficial and deep abductors and adductors that move individual fin rays; the lobed fins of sarcopterygians contain intrinsic muscles between the endoskeletal elements, a precondition for the tetrapod limb ([7.17.48](/7.17.48)).

Tetrapod limbs are levers that support the body and push against the ground, and their muscles are many and differentiated. The extrinsic muscles, which tie the limb to the trunk, are massive (latissimus dorsi, pectoralis, serratus, trapezius, rhomboids); the intrinsic muscles move every joint (flexion and extension of the elbow, wrist and fingers, pronation and supination, abduction and adduction). Two shifts in this table are instructive. In reptiles the supracoracoideus lies on the coracoid, below the shoulder joint; in mammals, whose coracoid is reduced to a process of the scapula, its homologues migrated onto the scapula, above and below the scapular spine (supra- and infraspinatus), where they stabilize the shoulder of a limb held under the body. And the caudofemoralis, the main retractor of the hindlimb in lizards, crocodylians and non-avian dinosaurs, running from the tail vertebrae to the femur, lost its importance in birds (whose tail is short) and in mammals (whose hindlimb is swung by the gluteal muscles and the hamstrings, [7.17.61](/7.17.61)). Its attachment, the fourth trochanter, can be traced on dinosaur femora.

Birds are a special case. Their two main flight muscles are both ventral muscles arising from the keeled sternum ([7.17.53](/7.17.53)). The pectoralis, which can make up 15–25 % of the body mass, inserts on the ventral side of the humerus and pulls the wing down in the power stroke. The supracoracoideus lies beneath it, also on the sternum; its tendon passes through the triosseal canal between the coracoid, scapula and furcula, which works as a pulley, and inserts on the dorsal side of the humerus, so that a ventral muscle raises the wing. The intrinsic wing muscles are small, and the hand is moved mainly by long tendons. In the hindlimb the muscles are concentrated in the thigh and drumstick, close to the body's centre of mass, and act on the toes through long tendons. When a perching bird bends its legs, the tendons of the toe flexors are tensioned around the ankle and the toes close; in many perching birds ridges on the flexor tendons engage folds of the tendon sheath and lock the grip passively (the tendon-locking mechanism). The ambiens muscle of the thigh, long credited with the perching reflex, is absent in many passerines, the most accomplished perchers.

Classical accounts, including some textbooks still in use, state that the limb muscles of tetrapods (unlike those of fishes) come from the somatic layer of the lateral plate mesoderm. Chick–quail grafting experiments in the 1970s showed that all limb muscle fibres arise from the somites: muscle precursors (expressing *Pax3* and *Lbx1*) delaminate from the hypaxial lip of the dermomyotome and migrate into the limb bud, whereas the lateral plate supplies the bones, tendons and connective tissue of the limb, which pattern the muscles ([7.5.23](/7.5.23#sec-23.10)). In fishes the fin muscles form in a similar way, partly by direct extension of the myotomes and partly by migrating precursors; the difference between fishes and tetrapods is one of degree.

## 62.5. Branchiomeric Muscles

### 62.5.1. Definition and Basic Pattern

**Definition 62.5.1 (Branchiomeric Muscle)** A **branchiomeric muscle** is a striated muscle of the pharyngeal arches, derived from the cranial mesoderm that fills the arches and supplied by the motor branch of the cranial nerve of its arch: the trigeminal (V) for the mandibular arch, the facial (VII) for the hyoid arch, the glossopharyngeal (IX) for the third arch and the vagus (X) for the arches behind it, with the accessory nerve (XI) for the posterior group derived from the cucullaris.

Each **pharyngeal arch** has a skeletal bar, an aortic arch, a nerve and a set of muscles ([7.5.30](/7.5.30#sec-30.3); [7.17.65](/7.17.65); [7.17.67](/7.17.67)). The muscles remain faithful to their arch and their nerve however much the skeleton changes, so that the jaw-closing muscle of a shark and the masseter of a human are both mandibular-arch muscles supplied by the trigeminal nerve, and the hyoid constrictor of a shark and the muscles of human facial expression both belong to the hyoid arch and the facial nerve.

In a shark each arch carries the same set of muscles ([7.17.46](/7.17.46)):

- *Constrictors.* Superficial sheets of fibres in the wall of each gill pouch, divided into dorsal and ventral constrictors, which compress the pharynx and force water out through the gill slits.
- *Levators.* Muscles from the braincase and the fascia above the gills to the upper elements of the arches, which raise the arches and the upper jaw.
- *Interarcuals.* Small deep muscles between the elements of the arches (interarcuales, adductores arcuum), which flex the arches.
- *The cucullaris.* The most posterior levator, a sheet from the fascia of the epaxial muscles to the last gill arch and the scapular process, which elevates the posterior arches and the pectoral girdle.

The hypobranchial muscles (§62.4.1) are not branchiomeric: they lie beneath the arches, are somatic and are supplied by spinal nerves. They expand the pharynx while the constrictors compress it, and the two systems together form the ventilation pump of fishes ([7.17.64](/7.17.64)).

### 62.5.2. Mandibular Arch Muscles

In fishes the mandibular arch muscles comprise the adductor mandibulae, a large muscle from the palatoquadrate to Meckel's cartilage that closes the jaws; the levator palatoquadrati and preorbitalis, which move the upper jaw; the spiracularis around the spiracle; and the intermandibularis, a transverse sheet between the two halves of the lower jaw that raises the floor of the mouth. In teleosts, whose jaws protrude, the adductor mandibulae is divided into several parts with tendons to the maxilla and the lower jaw, and the levator arcus palatini and dilator operculi move the suspensorium and the gill cover ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)).

In tetrapods the adductor mandibulae divides into external, internal (pseudotemporalis and pterygoideus) and posterior parts, which arise from the skull roof, the braincase and the palate and pass through the temporal region to the lower jaw. The temporal fenestrae of amniotes opened where the adductors bulge when they contract and where their fibres attach to the margins of the openings ([7.17.50](/7.17.50)). In mammals the adductor complex differentiates into four masticatory muscles: the temporalis from the side of the braincase to the coronoid process; the masseter from the zygomatic arch to the angle of the dentary; and the medial and lateral pterygoids from the pterygoid plates of the sphenoid to the medial side of the dentary and the jaw joint. These muscles move the dentary in the rotary and side-to-side movements of chewing, and their proportions match diet: carnivores have a huge temporalis that holds the jaws against a struggling prey, ungulates and rodents a large masseter and pterygoids that grind ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)).

Two small mammalian muscles tell the history of the middle ear. The tensor tympani, which dampens the vibrations of the malleus, is a mandibular-arch muscle supplied by the trigeminal nerve, as its insertion on the malleus (the former articular bone of the jaw joint) predicts; the tensor veli palatini of the soft palate is its neighbour. The intermandibularis becomes the mylohyoid and the anterior belly of the digastric, which lower the mandible.

### 62.5.3. Hyoid Arch Muscles

In sharks the hyoid arch carries a constrictor (the interhyoideus ventrally) and a levator of the hyomandibula. In bony fishes the hyoid muscles move the hyoid bar and the opercular apparatus. In non-mammalian tetrapods, in which the hyomandibula has become the columella of the middle ear, the main hyoid-arch muscle is the depressor mandibulae, which runs from the back of the skull to the retroarticular process of the lower jaw and opens the mouth; the sphincter colli, a constrictor of the neck, is also a hyoid muscle.

Mammals open the jaw differently. Their jaw joint is the new dentary–squamosal joint and the retroarticular process was lost with the reduction of the postdentary bones, so the depressor mandibulae has no lever; the jaw is lowered by the digastric, a muscle with two bellies joined by a tendon. Its posterior belly is a hyoid-arch muscle supplied by the facial nerve, its anterior belly a mandibular-arch muscle supplied by the trigeminal nerve: two muscles of different arches have combined into one functional unit. The other hyoid-arch muscles of mammals are the stylohyoid, the stapedius, which inserts on the stapes (the former hyomandibula) and protects the inner ear from loud sounds, and the muscles of facial expression, which spread from the neck over the face (§62.6).

### 62.5.4. Branchial Arch Muscles and the Cucullaris

The muscles of the third arch (the first gill arch of fishes) are supplied by the glossopharyngeal nerve; those of the fourth to sixth and any further arches by the vagus. When the gills are lost, these muscles become the muscles of the pharynx and the larynx. In mammals the stylopharyngeus (IX) raises the pharynx in swallowing; the pharyngeal constrictors, the muscles of the soft palate (except the tensor) and all intrinsic laryngeal muscles (cricothyroid, posterior and lateral cricoarytenoids, thyroarytenoid with the vocal folds) are branchial muscles of arches 4–6 supplied by vagal branches. The recurrent laryngeal nerve, which loops around the aortic arch (left) or the subclavian artery (right) before ascending to the larynx, preserves the relation between the nerve of the sixth arch and the artery of that arch ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)).

The cucullaris of fishes is the ancestor of the trapezius group. In tetrapods it divides into the trapezius, which runs from the skull and the neural spines of the neck and thorax to the scapula, and in mammals the sternocleidomastoid (often divided into cleidomastoid and sternomastoid), from the sternum and clavicle to the mastoid process. In amniotes both are supplied by the accessory nerve (XI), a branchiomotor nerve whose motor neurons extend from the medulla into the upper cervical spinal cord. The cucullaris is the only branchiomeric muscle that attaches to the postcranial skeleton; it connects the head with the shoulder girdle and was important when the girdle separated from the skull in tetrapods ([7.17.61](/7.17.61)). Its development is unusual: in amphibians and amniotes its precursors arise from the lateral mesoderm next to the occipital somites, at the border between head and trunk, rather than from the arches themselves. In fishes the cucullaris is supplied by the vagus and by spino-occipital nerves.

## 62.6. Integumentary Muscles

*Integumentary muscles* (skin muscles) insert into the dermis and move the skin. They split off in development from underlying skeletal muscles and keep the nerve of their parent muscle; many lose their bony attachment altogether.

Fishes have essentially none, since their skin is tightly bound to the myomeres. Amphibians have a few small ones, such as those that close the nostrils. Reptiles move scales and osteoderms with them, and snakes use the costocutaneous muscles in rectilinear crawling, a caterpillar-like progression in which waves of scale movement pass along the belly (§62.3.3). Birds have an elaborate set of feather muscles: smooth muscles raise or lower individual feathers (fluffing in the cold, display), and striated dermal muscles tension the feather tracts and the patagium of the wing.

Mammals have the most extensive integumentary muscles, in two groups:

- The *panniculus carnosus* (cutaneus trunci) is a thin sheet under the skin of the trunk, split off from the latissimus dorsi and the pectoralis and supplied by spinal nerves (via the pectoral nerves). It twitches the skin of a horse to shake off flies and lets a hedgehog roll into a ball under its spiny skin; it is reduced in primates, where only remnants (such as the palmaris brevis and, sometimes, the axillary arch) occur.
- The *sphincter colli* system is derived from the hyoid-arch musculature and supplied by the facial nerve. In mammals it spreads over the neck as the platysma and, in front, over the face as the muscles of facial expression: muscles around the eyes, lips, nostrils and ears, used in suckling (the orbicularis oris and buccinator form the cheeks that let a mammal suckle), feeding, moving the external ear and communication. Their richness in primates and especially in humans, where some 20 paired facial muscles produce the expressions read by others, is a branchiomeric heritage; a facial palsy (paralysis of the facial nerve) shows their common innervation.

## 62.7. Muscle Architecture and Mechanics

### 62.7.1. Parallel and Pennate Muscles

The force a muscle exerts depends on how many fibres act side by side, its excursion and speed on how long its fibres are. A muscle's architecture, the arrangement of its fibres relative to its line of action, sets the balance between the two. In a *parallel-fibred* (strap or fusiform) muscle, such as the sartorius or the biceps, the fibres run along the line of action and are long; such muscles shorten far and fast. In a *pennate* muscle the fibres are short and attach obliquely, at the pennation angle $\theta$, to a tendon or aponeurosis running through the muscle: on one side in a unipennate muscle, on both sides in a bipennate muscle, around several internal tendons in a multipennate muscle (the deltoid, the masseter).

The force of a muscle is proportional to its **physiological cross-sectional area** (PCSA), the total cross-sectional area of its fibres measured perpendicular to the fibres, $\mathrm{PCSA} = V/l_{\mathrm{f}}$ for a muscle of volume $V$ and fibre length $l_{\mathrm{f}}$; the maximum isometric stress of vertebrate skeletal muscle is about $20$–$30\,\mathrm{N\,cm^{-2}}$ in all vertebrates ([7.14.9](/7.14.9#sec-9.10.3)). Pennation packs many short fibres into a given volume: PCSA rises, at the cost of fibre length, and only the component $\cos\theta$ of each fibre's force acts along the tendon.

**Example 62.7.1 (A Pennate Muscle in a Fixed Envelope)** Consider a muscle that must fit into a space of length $L$ and thickness $t$ between two bones, and compare a parallel-fibred muscle (fibres of length $L$) with a unipennate muscle whose fibres run at the angle $\theta$ between two aponeuroses of length $L$ separated by $t$. Per unit depth the parallel muscle has $\mathrm{PCSA} = t$ and fibre length $L$. The pennate muscle has fibres of length $l_{\mathrm{f}} = t/\sin\theta$ and a PCSA of $L\sin\theta$ (the aponeurosis length times the component perpendicular to the fibres), and its force along the aponeurosis is

$$
F_{\mathrm{pennate}} = \sigma L \sin\theta\cos\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma L\sin 2\theta,
$$
where $\sigma$ is the stress of the fibres. Relative to the parallel muscle, $F_{\mathrm{pennate}}/F_{\mathrm{parallel}} = (L/t)\sin\theta\cos\theta$: for an envelope five times as long as it is thick, a pennation angle of $30^\circ$ more than doubles the force ($5 \times 0.433 = 2.2$), while the fibres are only $2t = 0.4L$ long, so the maximum excursion and the shortening velocity of the muscle fall to well below half. In this simple geometry the force is largest at $45^\circ$; real pennate muscles mostly have angles of $5$–$30^\circ$, because the fibres rotate to larger angles as they shorten and because the excursion would become too small.

![A model of pennation in a fixed envelope (Example 62.7.1). Left, the geometry: a parallel-fibred muscle (fibres of length L) and a unipennate muscle in the same envelope of length L and thickness t, fibres at the pennation angle θ between two aponeuroses. Right, force along the line of action (relative to the parallel muscle) and fibre length (relative to L) as functions of θ for L/t = 5; the shaded band marks the 5–30° range typical of vertebrate pennate muscles. Model, not data.](./plots/pennation.py)

Pennate muscles are therefore found where large forces over small distances are needed: the masseter, the calf muscles (gastrocnemius and soleus) of running mammals, whose short fibres work almost isometrically while their long tendons stretch and recoil, and the chewing muscles of herbivores. Parallel muscles serve large, fast excursions: the sartorius, the long hamstrings, the hyoid retractors of a frog or chameleon tongue.

### 62.7.2. Levers

Muscles move bones as levers about joints. Three classes of lever are distinguished by the order of the fulcrum (joint), the effort (the muscle insertion) and the load (resistance).

| Class | Arrangement | Mechanical advantage | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| First (balance lever) | Fulcrum between effort and load | Greater or less than 1 | Head on the atlanto-occipital joint, balanced by the neck extensors; triceps acting on the olecranon |
| Second (force lever) | Load between fulcrum and effort | Greater than 1 | Rising on the toes: the ball of the foot is the fulcrum, the calf muscles pull on the heel, body weight acts between |
| Third (speed lever) | Effort between fulcrum and load | Less than 1 | Flexing the elbow with the biceps; closing the jaw by the adductors in front of the jaw joint |

For a muscle force $F_{\mathrm{m}}$ applied at a distance $a$ from the joint (the in-lever, or moment arm of the muscle) and a load at a distance $b$ (the out-lever), static equilibrium of the moments gives the output force

$$
F_{\mathrm{out}} = F_{\mathrm{m}}\,\frac{a}{b},
$$
and the output point moves with velocity $v_{\mathrm{out}} = v_{\mathrm{m}}\,b/a$ when the muscle shortens with velocity $v_{\mathrm{m}}$. The ratio $a/b$ is the mechanical advantage. Most vertebrate limb and jaw muscles insert close to the joint and work as third-class levers with mechanical advantages of $0.05$–$0.5$: the skeleton amplifies speed and excursion at the expense of force. The jaw of a bony fish shows the trade-off clearly. Species that eat snails or hard-shelled prey have a high closing mechanical advantage (short jaw, long in-lever), species that capture evasive prey a low one, and the mechanical advantage of the lower jaw is a good predictor of diet in wrasses and cichlids; the same lever was examined for the bite of a shark in [7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.1.2). In limbs, diggers (moles, armadillos, badgers) have long olecranon processes and short forearms, which raises the mechanical advantage of the triceps; runners (horses, cheetahs, deer) have short in-levers and long distal segments, which raises the speed of the foot ([7.17.61](/7.17.61)).

### 62.7.3. The Force–Velocity Trade-off

The lever cannot be analysed without the muscle that drives it, because the force a muscle can exert falls as it shortens faster. The Hill force–velocity relation ([7.14.9](/7.14.9#sec-9.6)) is

$$
\frac{F_{\mathrm{m}}}{F_0} = \frac{1 - v_{\mathrm{m}}/v_{\max}}{1 + v_{\mathrm{m}}/(k\,v_{\max})},
$$
with $F_0$ the isometric force, $v_{\max}$ the maximum shortening velocity and $k \approx 0.25$ for vertebrate skeletal muscle. Combining it with the lever, the output force at an output velocity $v_{\mathrm{out}}$ is $F_{\mathrm{out}} = (a/b)\,F_{\mathrm{m}}(v_{\mathrm{out}}\,a/b)$.

**Example 62.7.2 (Choosing a Mechanical Advantage)** Normalize forces to $F_0$ and velocities to $v_{\max}b_0/a_0$ of a reference lever with $a_0/b_0 = 1$. A lever with mechanical advantage $m = a/b$ produces an output force $F_{\mathrm{out}} = m\,F_{\mathrm{m}}(m\,v_{\mathrm{out}})$, which is $m$ times larger at zero velocity but falls to zero at the output velocity $1/m$. A low mechanical advantage therefore reaches high speeds but exerts little force; a high one exerts large forces only at low speeds. The output power $P = F_{\mathrm{out}}v_{\mathrm{out}}$ has the same maximum for every $m$, about $0.1F_0v_{\max}$ for $k = 0.25$, reached when the muscle shortens at about $0.3v_{\max}$ ([7.14.9](/7.14.9#sec-9.6.2)): the lever cannot create power, only shift the velocity at which the muscle delivers its maximum power. A muscle–lever system is matched to its task when the velocity required at the load corresponds to about $0.3v_{\max}$ of the muscle; animals achieve this by choosing both the lever (the skeleton) and the fibre type (fast or slow muscle).

Right, output force against output velocity for mechanical advantages m = 0.25, 0.5 and 1, computed from the Hill relation with k = 0.25; dashed curves show the output power, which has the same maximum for all m. ### 62.7.4. Multiarticular Muscles

Many muscles cross two or more joints: the hamstrings cross hip and knee, the rectus femoris hip and knee, the gastrocnemius knee and ankle, and the long flexors and extensors of the digits wrist and finger joints. Such muscles couple the movements of the joints they cross. When a horse or a dog extends its hip, the two-joint muscles transfer power to the knee and ankle, so that proximal muscles near the centre of mass power the movement of the distal segments; distal limb segments can then be slender and light, with tendons instead of muscle bellies, which reduces the inertia of the swinging limb. The concentration of muscle mass near the body and the long tendons to the feet are common to running mammals, running birds and many dinosaurs, a convergence driven by the same mechanics ([7.17.61](/7.17.61)). Two-joint muscles also explain why a fully flexed wrist weakens the grip: the finger flexors are already shortened and work at the low end of their length–tension curve.

## 62.8. Electric Organs

The **electric organs** of fishes are stacks of modified muscle (or, in one family, nerve) cells, the electrocytes, which have lost most of their contractile apparatus and produce an electric discharge instead of a contraction ([7.14.8](/7.14.8#sec-8.8.1); biophysics in [7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.8)). Each electrocyte is a flat cell innervated on one face only. When the nerve fires, the innervated face depolarizes while the opposite face stays at the resting potential, so the cell generates a potential difference of about $0.1$–$0.15\,\mathrm{V}$ across its thickness; thousands of electrocytes stacked in series add their voltages, and many stacks in parallel add their currents.

Electric organs evolved independently at least six times, from different muscles:

| Group | Source of the electric organ | Discharge |
|---|---|---|
| Torpedo rays (Torpediniformes) | Branchiomeric muscles of the hyoid and branchial arches (supplied by VII, IX and X), kidney-shaped organs in the pectoral disc; electrocyte columns run dorsoventrally | Strong, about $50$–$200\,\mathrm{V}$ at high current, to stun prey |
| Skates (Rajidae) | Tail muscles | Weak, communication |
| Elephantfishes (Mormyridae, Africa) | Caudal axial muscles | Weak, electrolocation and communication |
| South American knifefishes (Gymnotiformes) | Hypaxial muscles of the tail; in the Apteronotidae the axons of spinal motor neurons (a neurogenic organ) | Weak, electrolocation and communication; in electric eels (*Electrophorus*) three organs, one strong (up to about $860\,\mathrm{V}$ in *E. voltai*) |
| Electric catfishes (Malapteruridae, Africa) | A sheet under the skin, probably derived from pectoral muscle | Strong, up to about $350\,\mathrm{V}$ |
| Stargazers (*Astroscopus*, Uranoscopidae) | Extrinsic eye muscles (superior rectus and others) behind the eyes | Moderate, up to about $50\,\mathrm{V}$ |

The geometry of the organs follows the electrical properties of the water. In a circuit of $n$ electrocytes in series (each with electromotive force $\mathcal{E}_1$ and internal resistance $r_1$) and $p$ such columns in parallel, discharging into an external resistance $R$, the current is

$$
I = \frac{n\mathcal{E}_1}{n r_1/p + R},
$$
and the power delivered to the water is greatest when the internal resistance $n r_1/p$ equals $R$. Sea water conducts well (low $R$), and marine torpedo rays have short columns of a few hundred electrocytes but hundreds of columns in parallel: low voltage, high current. Fresh water is a poor conductor (high $R$), and the electric eel has a few long columns of thousands of electrocytes each along its tail: high voltage, small current. The weak electric fishes use their discharges for active electrolocation and communication, sensing the distortions of their own field with electroreceptors ([7.17.68](/7.17.68); [7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5)); the convergent evolution of the organs in mormyrids and gymnotiforms involved the duplication and change of expression of the same sodium channel gene (*scn4a*) in both lineages.

## Summary

- Skeletal muscles are classified by origin and nerve supply, and innervation is the main criterion of muscle homology: muscles keep their motor nerve when they migrate (diaphragm, tongue, trapezius).

| System | Origin | Nerves | Fate in tetrapods |
|---|---|---|---|
| Epaxial muscles (Definition 62.3.1) | Epaxial myotome | Dorsal rami of spinal nerves | Longitudinal back muscles (transversospinal, longissimus, iliocostalis; erector spinae) |
| Hypaxial muscles (Definition 62.3.1) | Hypaxial myotome | Ventral rami | Three-layered body wall, rectus abdominis, intercostals, scalenes, serratus, psoas; diaphragm (mammals); limb muscles via migrating precursors |
| Hypobranchial muscles | Occipital and anterior trunk myotomes | Spinal (hypobranchial) nerves; XII in amniotes | Infrahyoid and tongue muscles |
| Extrinsic eye muscles | Prechordal and anterior head mesoderm | III, IV, VI | Unchanged six muscles; retractor bulbi, eyelid muscles |
| Appendicular muscles | Migrating somitic precursors | Ventral rami via plexuses | Extrinsic and intrinsic limb muscles in dorsal and ventral masses; flight muscles of birds |
| Branchiomeric muscles (Definition 62.5.1) | Cranial mesoderm of the arches | V, VII, IX, X, XI | Jaw muscles (V), facial and hyoid muscles (VII), pharynx and larynx (IX, X), trapezius and sternocleidomastoid (XI) |
| Integumentary muscles | Split from somatic or branchiomeric muscles | Parent nerve | Panniculus carnosus (spinal), platysma and facial muscles (VII) |

- Myomeres are V-shaped in lancelets and W-shaped in vertebrates; the horizontal septum and the division into epaxial and hypaxial muscles are gnathostome characters. On land the myosepta vanish, the body wall becomes three-layered and the limb muscles cover the trunk.
- The jaw-closing muscles of all gnathostomes are mandibular-arch muscles; the masseter, temporalis and pterygoids of mammals, the tensor tympani and the anterior digastric belong to the trigeminal nerve, the facial muscles and stapedius to the facial nerve.
- Muscle architecture and levers trade force for speed (Examples 62.7.1 and 62.7.2): pennation raises PCSA and force at the expense of excursion, a low mechanical advantage raises speed at the expense of force, and the lever cannot change the maximum power of the muscle.
- Electric organs evolved at least six times from different muscles (branchiomeric, axial, eye muscles) or nerves; series and parallel arrangements match the conductivity of fresh and sea water.

## Questions

1. Why is innervation a more reliable guide to muscle homology than the position or attachments of a muscle? Give an example where innervation reveals a homology that position would hide, and a situation in which innervation can mislead.

   **Answer** A motor nerve enters a muscle primordium early and is carried along when the primordium migrates, so the nerve records where the muscle came from, while attachments and position change with function. The diaphragm lies between thorax and abdomen but is supplied by the phrenic nerve from cervical segments, revealing its origin from cervical hypaxial muscle; the trapezius lies in the neck and shoulder but is supplied by the accessory nerve, revealing its descent from the cucullaris, a branchiomeric muscle. Innervation can mislead in the limbs, where spinal nerves exchange fibres in plexuses before reaching the muscles, so one muscle receives fibres from several segments, and in the rare cases where a muscle acquires a new nerve; homology is then judged from position, development and innervation together.

2. Distinguish branchiomeric and hypobranchial muscles by position, origin, innervation and function, and state what each becomes in mammals.

   **Answer** Branchiomeric muscles lie on the pharyngeal arches themselves (constrictors, levators, interarcuals), arise from the cranial mesoderm of the arches and are supplied by the branchiomotor cranial nerves V, VII, IX, X and XI; they close the jaws and compress the pharynx. Hypobranchial muscles lie beneath the arches, from the pectoral girdle to the jaw and hyoid, arise from occipital and anterior trunk myotomes that grow forward and are supplied by spinal nerves; they open the mouth and expand the pharynx. In mammals branchiomeric muscles become the masticatory muscles, facial muscles, pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles and the trapezius and sternocleidomastoid; hypobranchial muscles become the infrahyoid and tongue muscles supplied by the hypoglossal nerve.

3. A muscle 10 cm long and 1 cm thick must fit between two bones. Compare the force and fibre length of a parallel-fibred muscle and a unipennate muscle with $\theta = 20^\circ$ (Example 62.7.1).

   **Answer** Here $L/t = 10$. The parallel muscle has PCSA $t$ per unit depth and fibres $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ long. The pennate muscle has fibres of length $t/\sin 20^\circ = 1/0.342 = 2.9\,\mathrm{cm}$ and a force ratio $(L/t)\sin\theta\cos\theta = 10 \times 0.342 \times 0.940 = 3.2$. The pennate muscle is about three times as strong but its fibres, and hence its excursion and maximum shortening velocity, are less than a third as long.

4. The masseter and temporalis of a lion and of a sheep differ in size. Which muscle dominates in each, and why?

   **Answer** In carnivores such as the lion the temporalis is very large: it inserts on the tall coronoid process and pulls the jaw up and back, holding the jaws closed against a struggling prey and generating large force at the canines when the gape is wide. In herbivores such as the sheep the masseter and medial pterygoid dominate: they insert on the expanded angle of the dentary and move the jaw forward and sideways in the grinding strokes that break plant tissue between the molars, and their pennate architecture gives large force over small excursions.

5. Explain, using the circuit model of §62.8, why the electric eel has long columns of electrocytes in series while a torpedo ray has many short columns in parallel.

   **Answer** Power delivered to the water is maximal when the internal resistance of the organ, $nr_1/p$, equals the external resistance $R$. Fresh water has a high resistance, so the eel needs a high internal resistance and high voltage: many electrocytes in series (large $n$) in a few columns (small $p$). Sea water has a low resistance, so the ray needs a low internal resistance and high current: short columns (small $n$) and very many of them in parallel (large $p$).

6. (Open question) The diaphragm of mammals and the diaphragmaticus of crocodylians both ventilate the lungs. What evidence would you use to decide whether they are homologous?

   **Answer** Compare origin, attachments and innervation. The mammalian diaphragm arises from cervical somites, is supplied by the phrenic nerve from cervical spinal nerves and inserts on the ribs, sternum and lumbar vertebrae around a central tendon. The crocodylian diaphragmaticus runs from the pubis and posterior gastralia to the liver capsule, is supplied by posterior trunk nerves and acts by pulling the liver backward like a piston. Different origins, attachments and nerves, the absence of either muscle in the intervening groups (lizards, turtles, birds) and the phylogenetic distance between mammals and crocodylians all indicate convergence: two independent solutions to ventilating lungs without relying solely on the ribs.
