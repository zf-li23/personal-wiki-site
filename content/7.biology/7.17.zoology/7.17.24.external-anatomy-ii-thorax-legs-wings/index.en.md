This chapter continues the external anatomy of insects from [7.17.23](/7.17.23) with the two posterior tagmata. The thorax is the locomotory centre of the insect: it carries the three pairs of legs and, in the Pterygota, the two pairs of wings, together with the muscles that move them. The abdomen houses most of the viscera and carries the genitalia and, in many larvae, false legs. The vocabulary introduced here, the sclerites of the thorax, the parts of the leg, the veins and cells of the wing and the parts of the genitalia, is used throughout the chapters on insect orders ([7.17.31](/7.17.31)–[7.17.36](/7.17.36)). The mechanics of flight are treated separately in [7.17.25](/7.17.25).

## 24.1. The Thorax

### 24.1.1. The Three Thoracic Segments

The thorax consists of the prothorax, mesothorax and metathorax, each with a pair of legs; in winged insects the meso- and metathorax each bear a pair of wings and together form the *pterothorax*. The degree of development of each segment matches that of the appendages it carries. In insects that fly mainly with the forewings (flies, bees, wasps, moths) the mesothorax is large and the metathorax small; in beetles, which fly with the hindwings, the metathorax is larger; in Orthoptera and many Polyneoptera the two are similar. The head is connected to the prothorax by a membranous neck reinforced by small cervical sclerites.

The prothorax has no wings and its tergum, the pronotum, is a simple plate of very variable shape: saddle-shaped and covering the sides of the prothorax in locusts, a broad shield covering the head in cockroaches, large and often with processes in beetles and in treehoppers (Membracidae), where it can extend back over the whole body. Paranotal lobes of the pronotum in some Palaeozoic insects are part of the evidence on wing origin ([7.17.25](/7.17.25)).

### 24.1.2. The Notum of a Wing-Bearing Segment

The tergum of a wing-bearing segment is divided into two plates. The anterior *alinotum* carries the wings and is subdivided by sulci into a narrow prescutum, a large scutum and a posterior scutellum. The scutellum of the mesothorax is the triangular plate visible between the bases of the forewings of true bugs and beetles; in shield bugs it can cover most of the abdomen. Behind the alinotum lies the *postnotum*, which is the enlarged acrotergite of the following segment (secondary segmentation, [7.17.23](/7.17.23)); internally it bears a plate, the phragma, on which the dorsal longitudinal flight muscles insert. The lateral margins of the alinotum form the anterior and posterior notal wing processes, on which the first and fourth axillary sclerites of the wing articulate.

### 24.1.3. The Pleuron

The pleuron of each thoracic segment is a sclerotized plate between the notum and the leg base, strengthened by an internal *pleural ridge*, whose external line is the *pleural suture* (more correctly, the pleural sulcus). The pleural suture divides the pleuron into an anterior *episternum* and a posterior *epimeron*. At its upper end the pleural ridge forms the *pleural wing process*, the fulcrum on which the wing pivots; at its lower end it forms the *pleural coxal process*, on which the coxa articulates. In the membrane below the wing base lie small sclerites, the *basalare* in front of the pleural wing process and the *subalare* behind it, on which the direct flight muscles insert. In front of the coxa a small sclerite, the *trochantin*, gives a second articulation to the coxa in many insects. Thoracic spiracles lie in the membrane between segments, a mesothoracic spiracle between pro- and mesothorax and a metathoracic spiracle between meso- and metathorax.

### 24.1.4. The Sternum

The sternum of each thoracic segment is often divided into a basisternum in front and a furcasternum behind, the latter bearing internally a forked apophysis, the furca, to which the leg muscles and the ventral longitudinal muscles attach; a small spinasternum between segments bears a median apophysis, the spina. In many insects the sternum is partly fused with the pleura, forming bridges in front of (precoxal) and behind (postcoxal) the coxae. The pleural ridges and furcae together make an internal skeleton of the thorax, the endoskeleton that braces the box against the pull of the flight muscles.

## 24.2. Legs

### 24.2.1. Segments and Joints

**Definition 24.2.1 (Insect Leg)** The insect leg is a uniramous appendage of six articles: the **coxa**, articulated with the pleuron (and often with the trochantin); the small **trochanter**; the **femur**, usually the longest and strongest article; the slender **tibia**; the **tarsus**, divided in most insects into one to five tarsomeres; and the **pretarsus**, usually a pair of claws with an arolium or other pads between or beneath them.

The joints between the leg articles are of two kinds. Most are *dicondylic* hinges with two articulating points, which move in one plane: coxa–trochanter, femur–tibia (the "knee") and trochanter–femur where it is mobile (often the trochanter is fused to the femur). The coxa–body joint and the tibia–tarsus joint are typically *monocondylic* with one point, and allow rotation. The tarsomeres are not true segments: they lack muscles and are moved as a unit by muscles in the tibia, while the claws are flexed by a single long tendon, the unguitractor, that runs through the tarsus from a muscle in the femur and tibia. Antennal flagellomeres and tarsomeres are thus comparable: subdivisions without intrinsic muscles.

The number of tarsomeres is constant within many groups and is written as a tarsal formula giving the numbers for fore-, mid- and hindlegs: 5-5-5 in most beetles, 5-5-4 in darkling beetles (Tenebrionidae), 3-3-3 in locusts and grasshoppers (Acrididae), 4-4-4 in many leaf beetles and weevils where the small fourth tarsomere is hidden ("pseudotetramerous"). The tarsal formula is used extensively in the keys of [7.17.33](/7.17.33).

Insects attach to smooth surfaces with adhesive pads on the pretarsus or on the undersides of tarsomeres. *Smooth* pads, such as the arolium of cockroaches, bugs, bees and ants and the euplantulae of many Polyneoptera, are soft cuticle that conforms to the surface; *hairy* pads, such as the pulvilli of flies and the tarsal soles of beetles, carry thousands of tenent setae with flattened tips. Both secrete a thin film of fluid and work by capillary and van der Waals forces. Pads can be released by peeling, controlled by the direction of pull, which is why an insect can run upside down on glass without being stuck.

### 24.2.2. Walking

Insects walk with an *alternating tripod gait*: the fore- and hindleg of one side and the midleg of the other side move together, while the other three legs support the body. A tripod is a statically stable support, so slow insects can stop at any moment without falling. At high speeds the gait becomes dynamic and some cockroaches run on their hind legs alone at more than 1 m s⁻¹, about 50 body lengths per second. Coordination is largely decentralized: each leg has its own rhythm generator in the thoracic ganglia, coupled to its neighbours by sensory feedback from leg proprioceptors ([7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.6.3)).

### 24.2.3. Leg Types

The same six-article plan is modified for many functions:

| Type | Modification | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Cursorial (walking, running) | Slender, all articles similar | Cockroaches, ground beetles, fore- and midlegs of locusts |
| Saltatorial (jumping) | Hind femur greatly enlarged with the extensor muscle, the tibia long and spiny | Locusts, crickets, flea beetles; fleas jump with other mechanisms |
| Raptorial (grasping) | Elongate coxa; spiny femur and tibia that close on each other like a jack-knife | Mantises, mantispids, some predatory bugs |
| Fossorial (digging) | Short, broad femur and tibia, the tibia with strong teeth (dactyls) | Mole crickets, forelegs of scarabs and cicada nymphs |
| Natatorial (swimming) | Flattened tibia and tarsus with fringes of long setae, oar-like | Diving beetles, water boatmen, backswimmers |
| Corbiculate (pollen-carrying) | Hind tibia broad, concave and fringed (corbicula, pollen basket), basitarsus with rows of bristles (pollen brush) | Workers of honey bees and bumblebees |
| Clinging (scansorial) | Tibia, tarsus and a single large claw close on each other around a hair | Sucking lice |
| Clasping | Fore tarsi of males expanded with suction discs for holding the female | Male diving beetles |

Legs have many other special functions: cleaning (the antenna cleaner of bees and ants, a notch on the fore basitarsus closed by a tibial spur), hearing (tympanal organs on the fore tibiae of crickets and katydids, [7.17.27](/7.17.27)), sound production (the file on the hind femur of grasshoppers), tasting (chemosensilla on the tarsi of flies and butterflies) and walking on water (hydrophobic hairs on the long legs of water striders). Legs may also be reduced: the forelegs of nymphalid butterflies are small and are not used for walking.

The jump of a locust is a catapult. Before the jump the hind tibia is fully flexed and held by a locking mechanism; the large extensor tibiae muscle in the femur contracts slowly against the co-contracting flexor, storing energy in two semi-lunar processes of the femoro-tibial joint and in the extensor tendon. Relaxation of the flexor releases the tibia, which extends in about 30 ms and launches the locust at about 3 m s⁻¹. The principle is the same as in the springtail of [7.17.22](/7.17.22): muscle power is too low for a fast jump, but elastic recoil is not.

## 24.3. Wings

### 24.3.1. Structure and Articulation

An insect wing is a flat outgrowth of the body wall between the notum and the pleuron, in which the dorsal and ventral layers of cuticle are pressed together except along *veins*, tubes that contain hemolymph, tracheae and nerves. The veins stiffen the wing; their pattern, alternately raised (convex) and sunken (concave), gives the wing a corrugated, folded profile that is stiff along its length and flexible in torsion. Wing membranes often carry microtrichia, and the veins carry sensilla that monitor bending during flight.

The wing is joined to the thorax by a flexible membrane containing small *axillary sclerites*, which form a hinge with the notal wing processes and the pleural wing process. In Neoptera there are three axillary sclerites (and a fourth in some), plus median plates; the third axillary sclerite, moved by a dedicated muscle, folds the wing back over the abdomen. Mayflies and dragonflies (Palaeoptera) have a different articulation and cannot fold their wings ([7.17.25](/7.17.25), [7.17.30](/7.17.30)).

The wing has three margins (anterior or costal, outer or apical, and posterior or anal) and three angles (humeral at the base of the costa, apical at the tip and anal between the outer and anal margins). Folding lines divide it into regions: the main area supported by most veins, the *remigium*; the *clavus* or anal area behind the claval furrow, which in the hindwings of many Polyneoptera is greatly expanded into a fan-folded *vannus* (anal fan); and the small *jugum* at the base of the posterior margin, behind the jugal fold. Many wings also have a thickened, often pigmented spot near the tip of the costal margin, the *pterostigma*, whose inertia damps flutter of the wing tip.

### 24.3.2. Venation

**Definition 24.3.1 (Wing Venation)** **Wing venation** is the arrangement of veins in an insect wing; in the Comstock–Needham system, which homologizes venation across orders, the longitudinal veins are, from front to back, costa (C), subcosta (Sc), radius (R, with R1 and the radial sector Rs, branched into R2–R5), media (M, branched into M1–M4), cubitus (Cu, with an anterior branch CuA, itself branched into CuA1 and CuA2, and a posterior branch CuP), the anal veins (A1, A2, …) and the jugal veins (J); the longitudinal veins are connected by crossveins, and the areas of membrane enclosed by veins are cells.

Crossveins are named in lower case after the veins they join: humeral (h, between C and Sc near the base), radial (r), sectoral (s), radio-medial (r-m), medial (m), medio-cubital (m-cu) and cubito-anal (cu-a). Cells are named after the vein in front of them: the cell behind R1 is cell R1, the cell behind M2 is cell M2, and so on; a cell is *closed* if surrounded by veins and *open* if it reaches the wing margin. Some cells have special names, such as the discal cell of moths and butterflies or the triangle of dragonflies.

The Comstock–Needham system (1898) was built from comparisons of the venation of nymphal wing pads, where the veins follow tracheae, and is still used in identification. Later work by Kukalová-Peck and others on Palaeozoic fossils showed that each main vein primitively had an anterior convex and a posterior concave branch (for example MA and MP, CuA and CuP), and that the alternation of convex (+) and concave (−) veins (C+, Sc−, R+, Rs−, MA+, MP−, CuA+, CuP−, A+) is the most reliable guide to homology. Fossil and some living wings (mayflies, dragonflies, some Orthoptera) also have a fine network of irregular veins between the main veins, the archedictyon. In most orders the ground plan is simplified by loss and fusion of veins: flies and bees have few, Hymenoptera and Diptera many closed cells, small insects almost none.

### 24.3.3. Wing Types

Wings are classified by texture as well as by venation. The ancestral type is the *membranous* wing, thin and transparent (Hymenoptera, Diptera, Odonata, the hindwings of most orders). Forewings are often hardened and protect the hindwings, which provide most of the thrust when folded hindwings are flown.

**Definition 24.3.2 (Tegmen)** A **tegmen** (plural tegmina) is a leathery, more or less opaque forewing that retains a visible venation and covers the folded hindwing at rest, as in Orthoptera, Blattodea and Mantodea.

**Definition 24.3.3 (Elytron)** An **elytron** (plural elytra) is a strongly sclerotized forewing without functional venation, meeting its partner in a straight line along the midline (the elytral suture) and covering the folded hindwings and much of the abdomen, characteristic of beetles (Coleoptera).

Elytra are held up and spread in flight but usually contribute little lift (in some beetles, such as rose chafers, they stay closed and the hindwings are extended through lateral gaps). The space under the elytra traps air, which reduces water loss in desert beetles and serves as an air store in diving beetles. Earwigs have short, elytron-like forewings over an elaborate fan-folded hindwing.

**Definition 24.3.4 (Hemelytron)** A **hemelytron** (plural hemelytra) is a forewing whose basal part is thickened and leathery, divided into the corium and the clavus (sometimes also an embolium and a cuneus), and whose distal part is membranous, characteristic of true bugs (Heteroptera).

In Heteroptera the hemelytra are folded flat over the back with their membranous tips overlapping, which is the reason for the name of the suborder ("different wings"). Other Hemiptera (cicadas, aphids, leafhoppers) have forewings of uniform texture, membranous or slightly thickened, held roof-like over the body.

**Definition 24.3.5 (Halter)** A **halter** (haltere, plural halteres) is a hindwing reduced to a small club-shaped organ, with a basal swelling covered by fields of campaniform sensilla, a stalk and a terminal knob, that beats in antiphase to the forewing and acts as a gyroscopic sensor of body rotation, as in true flies (Diptera).

Halteres sense Coriolis forces acting on the vibrating knob when the body rotates, and their information is used to stabilize flight ([7.17.25](/7.17.25)). In males of Strepsiptera the forewings, not the hindwings, are reduced to similar club-shaped organs, so-called pseudohalteres, which are thought to have a similar function.

Other textures are the *scaled* wings of Lepidoptera and the *hairy* wings of Trichoptera, from which these orders are named (Greek *lepis*, scale; *thrix*, hair), and the *fringed* wings of thrips (Thysanoptera, "fringe wings"), narrow straps with long marginal setae that act like a membrane at the low Reynolds numbers of these tiny insects.

### 24.3.4. Wing Coupling and Folding

Many insects with two pairs of wings link them so that they beat as one surface (functional two-wingedness). The main coupling mechanisms are:

- *Hamuli*: in Hymenoptera a row of small hooks on the costal margin of the hindwing catches a fold on the hind margin of the forewing.
- Frenulum and retinaculum: in most moths one or more bristles on the base of the hindwing hook under a catch on the forewing.
- Jugum: in some primitive moths a lobe at the base of the forewing overlaps the hindwing.
- Amplexiform coupling: in butterflies and some moths a large humeral lobe of the hindwing simply lies under the forewing.
- In Hemiptera, grooves and folds on the forewing clavus and the hindwing margin interlock.

**Definition 24.3.6 (Frenulum)** A **frenulum** is a bristle or group of bristles at the humeral angle of the hindwing of many Lepidoptera that engages a hook or a tuft of scales (the retinaculum) under the forewing, coupling the two wings.

**Definition 24.3.7 (Jugum)** A **jugum** is the small posterior basal lobe of a wing behind the jugal fold; in jugate moths the jugum of the forewing projects back over the hindwing and couples the two wings.

The frenulum is single and strong in males and multiple in females of many moths; butterflies have lost it. Coupling mechanisms differ between orders, which shows that functional two-wingedness evolved several times; in flies it was achieved by losing the hindwings, in beetles by turning the forewings into elytra.

Neopteran wings are folded at rest. In Orthoptera, cockroaches and mantises the hindwing folds like a fan along radiating lines in the anal area; beetles and earwigs fold their hindwings transversely as well as longitudinally, an origami-like pattern in which elastic resilin-rich joints spring the wing open. The ladybird hindwing is folded under the elytra by movements of the abdomen and unfolds in a fraction of a second at take-off.

## 24.4. The Abdomen

### 24.4.1. Segments and Terminal Structures

The insect abdomen has eleven segments plus, in the embryo, a terminal telson; segment 11 is often reduced. Each segment has a tergum and a sternum joined by pleural membranes; eight pairs of spiracles usually open on segments 1–8. The segments are classified by their relation to the genitalia: the pregenital (visceral) segments 1–7, the genital segments 8 and 9 and the postgenital segments 10 and 11. The tergum of segment 11 is the epiproct, above the anus; its sternum is divided into a pair of paraprocts beside the anus.

**Definition 24.4.1 (Cercus)** A **cercus** (plural cerci) is one of a pair of segmented or unsegmented appendages of the eleventh abdominal segment of insects, usually sensory, rarely modified for defence or copulation.

Cerci are long and multi-segmented in bristletails, silverfish, mayflies, stoneflies and cockroaches, where they carry wind-sensitive filiform hairs that trigger escape from predators; they are short in locusts and mantises, transformed into strong forceps in earwigs and in some diplurans (in the latter not homologous with insect cerci in the strict sense), used as claspers by males of many species and absent in many Holometabola. Bristletails, silverfish and most mayflies also have a median caudal filament, an extension of the epiproct, between the cerci, giving three "tails".

### 24.4.2. Abdominal Appendages and Larval Prolegs

Adult pterygotes have no abdominal appendages except the genitalia and cerci. Apterygote insects and diplurans retain *styli* (small appendage remnants on the sterna of some segments) and eversible *vesicles* that absorb water. In the embryo, abdominal limb buds form on several segments and are then suppressed by the Hox genes *Ultrabithorax* and *abdominal-A*, which repress the limb gene *Distal-less* in the abdomen (**Hox genes**, [7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

Many larvae have secondarily evolved abdominal *prolegs*, fleshy unsegmented false legs. Caterpillars (Lepidoptera) usually have prolegs on segments 3–6 and 10, each ending in a sole armed with hooks (crochets), whereas sawfly larvae (Hymenoptera, Symphyta) usually have six or more pairs of prolegs, starting on segment 2, without crochets; this distinction is used to separate the two kinds of caterpillar-like larvae. Mayfly nymphs have abdominal gills on segments 1–7 that may be serial homologues of the leg exites. Whether larval prolegs are reactivated limbs or new structures that recruited the limb developmental program is still debated.

### 24.4.3. The Female Genitalia and the Ovipositor

**Definition 24.4.2 (Ovipositor)** The (appendicular) **ovipositor** is the egg-laying organ of female insects, formed by the appendages of abdominal segments 8 and 9: two pairs of basal plates, the first and second valvifers (gonocoxites of segments 8 and 9), and three pairs of elongate valves (valvulae), the first or ventral valvulae (gonapophyses of segment 8), the second or inner valvulae (gonapophyses of segment 9) and the third or dorsal valvulae (gonoplacs of segment 9).

The valvulae slide against one another along a tongue-and-groove joint (olistheter), so that the shaft can be pushed into soil or plant tissue by alternating movements while eggs pass down the channel between them. The structure varies greatly with the substrate:

- In grasshoppers (Caelifera) the dorsal and ventral valves are short, stout, hooked blades that open and close to dig a hole for the egg pod, into which the whole abdomen is extended (an expansion of the intersegmental membranes of up to about twenty times, [7.17.23](/7.17.23)); the inner valves are small.
- In katydids and crickets (Ensifera) all three pairs form a long, sabre- or needle-like ovipositor that inserts eggs into plants or soil.
- In cicadas, leafhoppers and sawflies the ovipositor saws or cuts into plant tissue.
- In parasitoid Hymenoptera the ovipositor drills into wood or into the body of a host; ichneumonids of the genus *Megarhyssa* drill through several centimetres of wood to reach woodwasp larvae, using an ovipositor reinforced at the tip with metals.
- In aculeate Hymenoptera (bees, wasps, ants) the ovipositor has become a sting connected to venom glands, and eggs are laid from the genital opening at its base.

In many orders, including most Lepidoptera, Diptera and Coleoptera, the appendicular ovipositor has been lost and replaced by a *substitutional ovipositor* formed by the terminal abdominal segments, which are slender and telescoped into each other and extended by hemolymph pressure.

The Chinese textbook names of the locust valves correspond as follows: the "dorsal valves" are the third valvulae, the "ventral valves" the first valvulae, and the small "inner valves" hidden within the dorsal ones the second valvulae. The female gonopore opens behind segment 7 or 8, on a genital chamber into which the duct of the spermatheca also opens ([7.17.26](/7.17.26)). A subgenital plate formed by the sternum of segment 7 or 8 often covers the base of the ovipositor.

### 24.4.4. The Male Genitalia

The male genitalia are derived from segment 9. The intromittent organ, the *phallus*, consists of a basal phallobase and a distal aedeagus, often with an eversible inner sac (endophallus) bearing spines and sclerites, through which the ejaculatory duct opens. Paired *parameres* beside the phallus, or claspers derived from gonocoxites and gonostyli of segment 9, grasp the female; in many species the cerci and the terminal terga help. In grasshoppers the ninth sternum forms a spoon-shaped subgenital plate that encloses the genitalia.

Male genitalia are among the most variable structures in animals, differing even between closely related species, and they are the main characters for identifying species in many groups. The explanations of this diversity, the lock-and-key hypothesis and sexual selection by cryptic female choice and sexual conflict, are discussed in [7.17.28](/7.17.28). Dragonflies and damselflies are unusual: the male transfers sperm from the gonopore on segment 9 to a secondary copulatory organ on segments 2–3 before mating.

## Questions

1. Explain why the wings of an insect move up when the tergum is pulled down. Which sclerites form the lever and the fulcrum?

   **Answer** The wing is a lever whose inner end articulates with the edge of the notum (through the axillary sclerites and notal wing processes) and which rests, a little further out, on the pleural wing process, the fulcrum at the top of the pleural ridge. Pulling the notum down lowers the inner end, so the part of the wing outside the fulcrum moves up, as a seesaw does. The mechanics of this indirect system are treated in [7.17.25](/7.17.25).

2. A beetle has the tarsal formula 5-5-4. Which legs have how many tarsomeres? Why are tarsomeres not considered true segments?

   **Answer** Fore- and midlegs have five tarsomeres, hindlegs four (darkling beetles, Tenebrionidae). Tarsomeres have no muscles of their own; the tarsus is moved as a unit by muscles in the tibia and the claws by the unguitractor tendon, just as the antennal flagellomeres are moved only from the scape. True leg segments (podomeres) are delimited by joints moved by intrinsic muscles.

3. A larva has five pairs of prolegs with crochets on abdominal segments 3–6 and 10; another has seven pairs without crochets starting on segment 2. To which orders do they belong? Why is this important in agriculture?

   **Answer** The first is a caterpillar (Lepidoptera), the second a sawfly larva (Hymenoptera, Symphyta). They look similar and feed on the same plants, but they belong to different orders and respond differently to control agents: for example, preparations of *Bacillus thuringiensis* used against caterpillars are not effective against sawfly larvae.

4. Compare the wings of a grasshopper, a beetle and a shield bug. In each case, which wing provides thrust in flight, and what is the protective role of the forewing?

   **Answer** Grasshopper: the forewing is a tegmen, leathery with veins, and contributes some lift; the large fan-folded hindwing provides most thrust. Beetle: the forewing is an elytron, hard and without venation, which protects the folded hindwings and abdomen and is held out in flight with little contribution; the transversely folded hindwings provide almost all thrust. Shield bug: the forewing is a hemelytron with leathery corium and clavus and a membranous tip, coupled in flight to the membranous hindwing; both contribute. In all three the forewing protects the folded hindwing at rest, which allowed these insects to live in dense vegetation, soil or crevices.

5. In the venation of a wing you see a vein that branches four times behind the radial sector and in front of a strongly concave vein that ends near the claval furrow. Identify both veins and justify the identification.

   **Answer** The four-branched vein is the media (M1–M4); the concave vein ending near the claval furrow is the posterior cubitus CuP, which lies in the claval furrow in the ground plan. In the convex–concave alternation the media (MA+ and MP−) lies between the concave radial sector and the convex CuA, and CuP is concave; position and corrugation are more reliable than the number of branches, which varies.

6. (Open question) The female of the woodwasp parasitoid *Megarhyssa* drills several centimetres into wood with an ovipositor less than a millimetre thick. Suggest how its structure allows this without buckling.

   **Answer** Hints from the anatomy: the ovipositor consists of three pairs of valvulae linked by tongue-and-groove joints, which slide relative to each other. By pushing one valve forward while the others are anchored by teeth in the wood, and pulling on the anchored valves, the insect keeps the shaft mostly in tension rather than compression, so it does not buckle (a reciprocating "push–pull" mechanism also used by wood wasps and by some engineered probes). The tip is hardened by incorporated metals (manganese, zinc), and the abdomen and membranes guide the shaft at its base.
