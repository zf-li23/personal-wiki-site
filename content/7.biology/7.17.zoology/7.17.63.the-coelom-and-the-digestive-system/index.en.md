The digestive system is the oldest organ system of the vertebrate body, and its basic layout, a tube from mouth to anus with a liver and a pancreas budding from it, is the same in a lamprey and a human. What varies among vertebrates is the equipment at the front end, where food is caught, held and broken (jaws, teeth, tongue, beak), the way food is stored and processed (stomach, crop, gizzard, fermentation chambers), and the way the absorptive surface is enlarged (spiral valves, caeca, long coiled intestines). The gut lies in the body cavity, the coelom, which in vertebrates is divided into separate compartments for the heart, the lungs and the abdominal viscera; the evolution of these partitions is linked to the evolution of breathing.

This chapter first describes the coelom and its partitions (§63.1) and the mesenteries that suspend the viscera (§63.2). It then follows the gut from front to back: its overall organization and embryonic origin (§63.3), the mouth with the palate, tongue and glands (§63.4), the teeth (§63.5), the oesophagus and stomach (§63.6), the intestine (§63.7), the cloaca (§63.8) and the liver and pancreas (§63.9). Section 63.10 summarizes the comparison class by class. The pharynx, as the site of the gills and the origin of the lungs, is treated with the respiratory system in [7.17.64](/7.17.64); the pharyngeal pouches and the endocrine glands derived from them in [7.17.67](/7.17.67); the jaws and their suspension in [7.17.58](/7.17.58) and [7.17.59](/7.17.59). The physiology of motility, secretion, absorption and the liver is in [7.14.26](/7.14.26)–[7.14.29](/7.14.29), and the development of the gut, liver, pancreas and lung in [7.5.30](/7.5.30).

## 63.1. The Coelom and Its Partitions

### 63.1.1. Origin of the Coelom

The **coelom** of vertebrates is the cavity within the lateral plate mesoderm ([7.17.3](/7.17.3); [7.17.5](/7.17.5)). In the embryo the mesoderm on each side of the neural tube divides into the paraxial mesoderm (somites), the intermediate mesoderm (kidneys and gonads, [7.17.66](/7.17.66)) and the lateral plate. The lateral plate splits into two layers: the somatic (parietal) mesoderm, which lines the body wall, and the splanchnic (visceral) mesoderm, which covers the gut and its derivatives. The space between them is the coelom. The left and right cavities meet beneath the gut, and the double layer of splanchnic mesoderm above and below the gut becomes the dorsal and ventral mesenteries.

The lining of the coelom is a serous membrane, the peritoneum (pleura in the thorax, pericardium around the heart): a single layer of flat mesothelial cells on connective tissue, which secretes a little serous fluid. The parietal layer lines the body wall, the visceral layer covers the organs, and the slippery fluid between them lets organs that change in shape and volume (heart, lungs, stomach and intestine) move without friction against each other and against the body wall. This is the main function of the coelom in vertebrates; it no longer serves as a hydrostatic skeleton, as in annelids, and its role in excretion is limited to the nephrostomes of the embryonic kidney ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)).

### 63.1.2. Partitions in Fishes and Amphibians

In all vertebrates the heart develops in the most anterior part of the coelom, beneath the pharynx, which becomes the *pericardial cavity*. A transverse partition, the *septum transversum*, separates it from the rest of the coelom, the *pleuroperitoneal cavity*, which contains the gut, the liver, the gonads and, in tetrapods, the lungs. The septum transversum carries the great veins (the common cardinal veins and the hepatic veins) from the body to the sinus venosus of the heart, and the liver grows into its posterior face; in the adult the liver remains attached to the septum by the coronary ligament.

In elasmobranchs the pericardial cavity is surrounded by cartilage of the pectoral girdle and the floor of the pharynx and is almost rigid. A pericardioperitoneal canal connects it with the pleuroperitoneal cavity, and the rigid chamber may help the heart fill by suction as the ventricle contracts. In hagfishes the pericardial and peritoneal cavities remain connected widely. In salamanders the septum transversum is still a transverse wall close to the pharynx; in frogs, whose heart has moved backward, the pericardial cavity lies ventral to the front part of the pleuroperitoneal cavity and the septum runs obliquely.

### 63.1.3. Partitions in Amniotes

In amniotes the lungs move out of the general cavity into separate compartments in several lineages, and they do so in different ways, which shows that the partitions evolved repeatedly with different ventilation mechanisms ([7.17.64](/7.17.64)).

- *Lepidosaurs and turtles.* In most lizards, snakes and turtles the lungs lie free in a single pleuroperitoneal cavity. Some lizards (tegus and other teiids) have a *post-hepatic septum*, a membrane behind the liver that separates the lungs and liver in front from the intestine behind.
- *Crocodylians.* A post-hepatic septum divides the cavity into an anterior pulmonary–hepatic compartment and a posterior intestinal peritoneal cavity. The diaphragmaticus muscle, running from the pubis to the septum around the liver, pulls the liver backward and expands the lungs: the hepatic piston ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.3.4); [7.17.64](/7.17.64)).
- *Birds.* The lungs are small, rigid and fixed to the ribs and vertebrae, and the pleural cavities are virtually obliterated by the attachment of the lungs to the body wall. A horizontal septum covers the ventral face of the lungs, and an oblique septum separates the thoracic and abdominal air sacs from the viscera; the peritoneal cavity is subdivided into several hepatic and intestinal compartments.
- *Mammals.* The coelom is divided into four cavities: one pericardial cavity, two pleural cavities and one peritoneal cavity. The thorax and abdomen are separated by the muscular diaphragm, which arises from four sources: the septum transversum (which forms the central tendon), the paired pleuroperitoneal folds, the mesentery of the oesophagus and an ingrowth of the body wall; its muscle fibres come from cervical somites ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.3.4)). The pericardial cavity is separated from the pleural cavities by the pleuropericardial membranes. The mammalian *diaphragm* (a dome of skeletal muscle between the thoracic and abdominal cavities) is the only complete muscular partition of the coelom among vertebrates.

## 63.2. Mesenteries

A *mesentery* is a double fold of peritoneum that suspends an organ from the body wall or connects two organs; blood vessels, lymphatics and nerves reach the organs through it. In the embryo the gut is suspended along its whole length by a dorsal mesentery and, in its anterior part, also by a ventral mesentery.

The dorsal mesentery persists in most vertebrates and takes different names along its length: the mesogaster (greater omentum in mammals) suspends the stomach, the mesoduodenum, mesentery proper and mesocolon suspend the intestine, and the mesorectum the rectum. The spleen develops in the dorsal mesogaster and remains connected to the stomach by the gastrosplenic ligament. In mammals the dorsal mesogaster grows into a large apron, the greater omentum, which hangs from the greater curvature of the stomach over the intestine; it stores fat, contains lymphoid tissue (milky spots) and can wall off inflamed organs. The ventral mesentery disappears except in the region of the liver, which grows into it: the part between the stomach and duodenum and the liver becomes the lesser omentum (hepatogastric and hepatoduodenal ligaments), and the part between the liver and the ventral body wall the falciform ligament, in whose free margin the obliterated umbilical vein runs as the round ligament of the liver ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)). In lampreys and some teleosts the dorsal mesentery is incomplete or absent in places. The gonads and their ducts have their own mesenteries (mesorchium, mesovarium, mesosalpinx), described with the urogenital system ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)).

During development the gut lengthens and rotates. In mammals the stomach rotates about its long axis so that its originally dorsal border becomes the greater curvature on the left, and the midgut loop herniates into the umbilical cord, rotates by $270^\circ$ counterclockwise (seen from the front) and returns into the abdomen, which brings the caecum to the lower right. The positions of the organs and of their mesenteries in the adult are explained by these rotations.

## 63.3. Organization of the Digestive System

### 63.3.1. The Archenteron and Its Derivatives

The gut develops from the archenteron, the endodermal tube formed in gastrulation ([7.17.5](/7.17.5); [7.5.30](/7.5.30)). At its two ends the endoderm meets ectoderm, which invaginates as the stomodeum (mouth) and the proctodeum (anal region); the oral and cloacal membranes between them break through. The lining of the front of the mouth and of the anal canal is therefore ectodermal, the rest of the lining endodermal, and the connective tissue and smooth muscle of the gut wall come from the splanchnic mesoderm. Neural crest cells colonize the gut and form the enteric nervous system ([7.14.26](/7.14.26#sec-26.3.1)).

### 63.3.2. Regions and Wall

The adult tract is divided into the mouth (oral or buccal cavity), the pharynx, the oesophagus, the stomach, the small intestine, the large intestine and the cloaca or rectum and anus. The divisions are clearest in mammals and birds and least distinct in cyclostomes. The wall has four layers throughout: the mucosa (epithelium, lamina propria with glands and a thin muscularis mucosae), the submucosa (connective tissue with vessels and nerve plexuses), the muscularis externa (inner circular and outer longitudinal smooth muscle; striated in the pharynx and the upper oesophagus of mammals) and the serosa or, outside the coelom, the adventitia ([7.14.26](/7.14.26#sec-26.1.4)).

Food is broken down mechanically (teeth, gizzard, muscular stomach), chemically (enzymes of the stomach, pancreas and intestine) and microbially (fermentation in specialized chambers of herbivores). The anatomy of a gut reflects which of these processes dominates: a carnivore's gut is short and simple because animal food is easily digested by its own enzymes, while a herbivore's gut is long and includes fermentation chambers because plant cell walls are broken down only by microbial enzymes ([7.14.28](/7.14.28#sec-28.8.1)).

## 63.4. The Mouth and Oral Cavity

### 63.4.1. Lips, Palate and the Separation of Air and Food

The mouth of jawless vertebrates is a rasping apparatus without jaws: lampreys have an oral disc with horny teeth and a piston-like tongue that rasps flesh, hagfishes a pair of tooth plates on a protrusible cartilage that pull food in ([7.17.45](/7.17.45)). In jawed vertebrates the mouth is bordered by the jaws ([7.17.58](/7.17.58)). Fleshy, muscular lips and cheeks are a mammalian character, made possible by the facial muscles; they seal the mouth around the teat during suckling and keep food between the teeth during chewing.

In fishes the mouth and pharynx are one chamber traversed by water. The olfactory sacs open to the outside only, except in lungfishes and early tetrapodomorphs, where the incurrent nostril has moved into the mouth as the **choana** ([7.17.48](/7.17.48)). In amphibians and most reptiles the choanae open into the front of the roof of the mouth, and air passes through the mouth cavity to the pharynx. A **secondary palate**, a shelf of bone (from the premaxilla, maxilla and palatine) below the original roof of the mouth, evolved independently in mammals (and their cynodont ancestors) and in crocodylians ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)); turtles and some lizards have partial versions. It moves the internal nostrils back to the pharynx, so that air flows through a separate nasal passage above the food. In mammals the bony hard palate is continued backward by the muscular soft palate, and the respiratory and alimentary routes cross in the pharynx, where the epiglottis and the soft palate close the larynx and the nasopharynx during swallowing ([7.17.64](/7.17.64)). The secondary palate allows a mammal to chew and suckle while breathing, and a crocodile to breathe with only its nostrils above water while holding prey in its mouth; a valve of the tongue and the palate (the gular valve) closes the back of a crocodile's mouth.

### 63.4.2. The Tongue

A tongue in the sense of a muscular organ appears only in tetrapods ([7.17.62](/7.17.62#sec-62.4.1)). Lampreys and hagfishes have a "tongue" that is a cartilaginous piston operated by special muscles, not homologous with the tongue of tetrapods. Fishes have a primary tongue, a fold of mucosa over the basihyal without intrinsic muscles, which moves only with the hyoid apparatus.

In tetrapods the tongue develops from the floor of the pharynx in front of the hyoid arch, and its muscles derive from the hypobranchial muscles. Amphibians use it to capture prey: the tongue of a frog is attached in front and flipped forward with the sticky back end; some plethodontid salamanders project the tongue several body lengths by shooting the hyobranchial skeleton forward, powered by elastic energy stored in collagen. Among reptiles, chameleons project the tongue by the same elastic catapult mechanism, while snakes and monitor lizards have a slender forked tongue that samples odour molecules and carries them to the vomeronasal organ ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)). Birds have a tongue supported by a long hyoid apparatus, used in handling food (woodpeckers have a tongue with hyoid horns that curve around the skull and can be protruded far). The mammalian tongue is a large muscular hydrostat that manipulates food during chewing and swallowing and bears the taste buds on its papillae (filiform papillae without taste buds, fungiform, foliate and circumvallate papillae with taste buds; [7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.4)).

### 63.4.3. Oral Glands

Fishes, living in water, have mucous cells in the oral epithelium but no multicellular oral glands; the "oral glands" of lampreys, which secrete an anticoagulant into the wound of the host, are an exception. Tetrapods, which must lubricate food in air, have oral glands from amphibians onward: an intermaxillary gland in the roof of the mouth of amphibians; labial, palatine, lingual and sublingual glands in reptiles. Venom glands are modified oral glands: the venom glands of snakes (Duvernoy's gland and the venom glands of front-fanged snakes) are derived from posterior supralabial glands of the upper jaw, those of helodermatid lizards (the Gila monster) from infralabial glands of the lower jaw. The poison of the platypus spur and of some shrews' saliva are independent inventions. Birds that eat dry seeds have large salivary glands that secrete mucus; swifts and swiftlets build nests with their saliva (the edible nests of *Aerodramus*). Mammals have three paired salivary glands, the parotid, submandibular and sublingual, which secrete saliva with amylase in many species ([7.14.27](/7.14.27#sec-27.2)); rabbits have an additional infraorbital (zygomatic) gland, and ruminants secrete large volumes of bicarbonate-rich saliva that buffers the rumen ([7.14.27](/7.14.27#sec-27.7.2)).

## 63.5. Teeth

### 63.5.1. Structure and Origin

A tooth is a mineralized structure of dentine with a pulp cavity containing blood vessels and nerves, covered on its crown by enamel or, in fishes, by enameloid, and attached to the jaw by bone or connective tissue. Teeth develop from an interaction of oral epithelium and neural-crest-derived mesenchyme: the epithelial enamel organ produces the enamel (ameloblasts), the mesenchymal dental papilla the dentine (odontoblasts) and the pulp, and the dental sac the cementum and periodontal ligament that anchor the root. This is the same developmental module, an epithelial–mesenchymal unit producing dentine and an enamel-like cap, that forms the placoid scales of sharks and the odontodes of the dermal armour of early vertebrates ([7.17.45](/7.17.45); [7.17.57](/7.17.57)).

The homology of teeth and placoid scales is supported by their identical tissues (dentine, enameloid, pulp), by the same gene network (*Shh*, *Bmp*, *Pitx2*, *Sox2*) in their development, and by the fossil record: in early chondrichthyans and in many sharks today the scales at the margin of the jaw grade into teeth. Whether teeth arose from odontodes that migrated into the mouth ("outside-in") or arose within the pharynx and spread outward ("inside-out") is still debated: some jawless fishes had tooth-like denticles in the pharynx, and the oldest teeth on jaws appear in placoderms and early osteichthyans ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.3)).

### 63.5.2. Tooth Attachment

**Definition 63.5.1** Teeth are classified by how they attach to the jaw. **Acrodont** teeth are fused (ankylosed) to the crest or rim of the jaw bone without sockets; **pleurodont** teeth are ankylosed to the inner (lingual) side of a high labial wall of the jaw, in a groove without separate sockets; **thecodont** teeth are set in individual sockets (alveoli) in the jaw bone, held by a periodontal ligament (mammals) or by bone and ligament.

In sharks the teeth are not attached to the jaw cartilage at all but embedded in a band of connective tissue, the dental lamina, which moves the rows of teeth forward like a conveyor belt ([7.17.46](/7.17.46)). Bony fishes and amphibians mostly have acrodont or pleurodont teeth ankylosed to the bones; amphibian teeth are *pedicellate*, with a crown separated from its base (pedicel) by a zone of uncalcified dentine, a character shared by frogs, salamanders and caecilians. Among reptiles the tuatara, agamids and chameleons have acrodont teeth, most other lizards (iguanas, skinks, geckos) pleurodont teeth; snakes have teeth fused to the rims of shallow sockets, a condition described as acrodont in some textbooks and as thecodont-like (ankylothecodont) in more recent studies. Crocodylians, many extinct archosaurs and mammals are thecodont. Teeth in deep sockets are the most firmly anchored, and a periodontal ligament in mammals allows slight movement that cushions the forces of chewing and allows teeth to drift as they wear.

### 63.5.3. Tooth Replacement

**Definition 63.5.2** Teeth are classified by the number of generations that replace each other at each tooth position. A **polyphyodont** dentition replaces teeth continuously throughout life; a **diphyodont** dentition has two generations, the deciduous (milk) teeth and the permanent teeth; a **monophyodont** dentition has a single generation.

Most non-mammalian vertebrates are polyphyodont. Sharks shed and replace teeth every few weeks; a lemon shark replaces each tooth about every 8 days. Crocodylians and lizards replace their teeth in waves that pass alternately along odd and even tooth positions, so that the jaw never has gaps at neighbouring positions; a new tooth forms in a resorption pit at the base of the old one. Mammals are diphyodont: incisors, canines and premolars are replaced once, and the molars, which erupt late at the back of the jaw, have only one generation (they belong to the first set but erupt with the permanent teeth). Diphyodonty goes with determinate growth and precise occlusion between upper and lower teeth: a jaw that stops growing can carry a fixed set of teeth whose cusps fit each other exactly, which continuous replacement would disturb.

Several mammals have modified this pattern. Toothed whales and armadillos are monophyodont; the platypus loses its teeth after weaning and replaces them by horny pads. Elephants have six molars per quadrant that erupt one after another from the back and move forward horizontally, so that only one or two are in use at a time; manatees replace their molars continuously in the same horizontal way ("marching molars"). Rodent incisors and the cheek teeth of some rodents, lagomorphs and wombats are ever-growing (hypselodont): open roots allow continuous growth that compensates for wear.

### 63.5.4. Homodont and Heterodont Dentitions

Most fishes, amphibians and reptiles are *homodont*: all teeth are of essentially the same conical or blade-like form and differ only in size; teeth may occur not only on the jaw margins (premaxilla, maxilla, dentary) but also on the vomer, palatine, pterygoid, parasphenoid and, in fishes, on the gill arches. Mammals are *heterodont*: the teeth are differentiated along the jaw into incisors (cropping, gnawing), canines (piercing, holding), premolars and molars (cutting and grinding). Cyprinid fishes such as the carp have no teeth on the jaws but grind food with pharyngeal teeth on the fifth ceratobranchial against a horny pad under the basioccipital; cichlids and wrasses have a second set of pharyngeal jaws, a functional duplication of the jaw apparatus that contributed to their adaptive radiation. Some reptiles were also heterodont, notably the synapsids that led to mammals, with enlarged canine-like teeth already in pelycosaurs and fully differentiated dentitions in cynodonts ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)).

The mammalian dentition is described by the **dental formula** ([7.17.55](/7.17.55)), the number of each tooth type in one half of the upper and lower jaw; the ancestral placental formula is $\frac{3.1.4.3}{3.1.4.3} = 44$, and those of the rabbit ($\frac{2.0.3.3}{1.0.2.3} = 28$), pig ($\frac{3.1.4.3}{3.1.4.3} = 44$), rat ($\frac{1.0.0.3}{1.0.0.3} = 16$) and human ($\frac{2.1.2.3}{2.1.2.3} = 32$) show the reductions in different lineages. The molars of early mammals were tribosphenic: an upper molar with three main cusps arranged in a triangle (the trigon, with the protocone) and a lower molar with a triangle of three cusps (the trigonid) and a basin behind it (the talonid), into which the protocone grinds like a pestle in a mortar. Shearing crests between the triangles cut food, the protocone and talonid crush it. From this pattern evolved the bunodont teeth of omnivores (low rounded cusps; pigs, primates), the lophodont teeth of many herbivores (cusps joined into transverse ridges; rhinoceroses, elephants), the selenodont teeth of ruminants (crescent-shaped ridges), and the carnassials of carnivorans (the last upper premolar and first lower molar as shearing blades). Herbivores that eat abrasive grasses evolved high-crowned (hypsodont) cheek teeth, which wear for years before reaching the root.

Living birds are toothless: tooth development was lost in the common ancestor of modern birds about 100 million years ago, and the genes for enamel and dentine matrix proteins (enamelin, amelogenin, dentin matrix protein) are pseudogenes in their genomes. Teeth were lost independently in turtles, in baleen whales, anteaters and pangolins; the beak or horny pads, and gizzards that grind food, take over their functions (§63.6.2). Hatchling lizards and snakes have an egg tooth, a true tooth on the premaxilla used to cut the eggshell; hatchling birds, crocodylians, turtles and monotremes have instead a horny egg caruncle on the tip of the snout or beak.

## 63.6. Oesophagus and Stomach

### 63.6.1. Oesophagus and the Stomach of Fishes and Reptiles

Cyclostomes have no stomach: in lampreys the pharynx divides into a dorsal oesophagus and a ventral respiratory tube, and the oesophagus leads directly into a straight intestine. A stomach appears with the gnathostomes, which can take large prey and must store it: it is a muscular dilation of the foregut with glands that secrete acid and pepsinogen ([7.14.27](/7.14.27#sec-27.3)). The stomach and its acid-secreting genes (*atp4a*, *pga*) have been lost secondarily many times: in chimaeras, lungfishes and about a quarter of teleost species (carp and other cyprinids, many gobies, wrasses, parrotfishes), and in monotremes. The oesophagus lengthens with the neck in amniotes.

In most vertebrates the stomach is J-shaped or U-shaped, with a cardiac part near the oesophagus and a pyloric part closed by a sphincter. Snakes, swallowing prey whole, have a long, straight, distensible stomach; crocodylians have a muscular gizzard-like stomach with swallowed stones (gastroliths) and a small pyloric chamber, similar to that of birds, their closest relatives.

### 63.6.2. Crop, Proventriculus and Gizzard of Birds

Birds lack teeth and must swallow food whole; their foregut compensates with three specializations ([7.14.26](/7.14.26#sec-26.10.3)).

- *Crop.* A dilation of the oesophagus, well developed in seed-eating birds (fowl, pigeons, finches), stores food so that a bird can eat quickly and digest later. In pigeons and doves both sexes produce "crop milk" from proliferating and sloughing crop epithelium to feed the young, under the control of prolactin. The hoatzin of South America uses its huge crop as a fermentation chamber for leaves, the only bird with foregut fermentation.
- *Proventriculus* (glandular stomach). The anterior, thin-walled chamber secretes acid and pepsinogen; it is large in fish-eating birds that store food there.
- **Gizzard** (ventriculus, muscular stomach). The posterior chamber has thick muscular walls and a tough lining of koilin (a carbohydrate–protein layer secreted by its glands) and usually contains grit. It grinds food, replacing the teeth; seed-eaters and herbivorous birds have large gizzards, raptors and fruit-eaters small ones. Owls regurgitate the indigestible fur and bones collected in the gizzard as pellets.

### 63.6.3. The Stomach of Mammals and the Ruminant Stomach

Most mammals have a simple stomach with gastric glands. Its mucosa is divided into a non-glandular part lined by stratified squamous epithelium (large in rodents and horses), a cardiac gland region, a fundic (proper gastric) gland region with parietal and chief cells, and a pyloric gland region. Herbivores that ferment plant food in the stomach (foregut fermenters) have a compartmented stomach in which a large non-glandular part houses microbes: kangaroos, colobine monkeys, sloths, hippopotamuses, peccaries and, most elaborately, the ruminants.

**Definition 63.6.1 (Ruminant Stomach)** The **ruminant stomach** is the four-chambered stomach of ruminant artiodactyls (Ruminantia: deer, giraffes, cattle, sheep, goats, antelopes and chevrotains). Three chambers develop from the non-glandular part of the stomach: the rumen (the largest, a fermentation vat with papillae; [7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.6)), the *reticulum* (with a honeycomb lining; together with the rumen the reticulorumen), and the *omasum* (with leaf-like folds that absorb water and volatile fatty acids); the fourth, the *abomasum*, is the glandular stomach that secretes acid and enzymes and corresponds to the stomach of other mammals.

Ingested plant food is fermented by bacteria, archaea, protists and fungi in the reticulorumen ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.6); [7.14.26](/7.14.26#sec-26.10.4)), which digest cellulose to volatile fatty acids absorbed through the rumen wall. Coarse particles are regurgitated and chewed again (rumination, chewing the cud); only particles that have been broken down small enough pass through the reticulo-omasal orifice. The abomasum then digests the microbes themselves, which supply most of the ruminant's protein. In young ruminants the reticular (oesophageal) groove closes into a tube when the calf suckles and carries milk directly to the abomasum, bypassing the rumen. Chevrotains (mouse deer, Tragulidae), the earliest branch of ruminants, have a rudimentary omasum.

Camels and llamas (Tylopoda) also ruminate but have a three-chambered stomach (compartments 1–3) that evolved its fermentation independently of the ruminants; they have no distinct omasum. The "water sacs" in the wall of their first compartment, long thought to store water for desert crossings, are patches of glandular mucosa; camels tolerate dehydration by other means ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)).

## 63.7. The Intestine

### 63.7.1. Enlarging the Absorptive Surface

The small intestine is the main site of chemical digestion and absorption ([7.14.28](/7.14.28)). Because absorption is proportional to surface area and food is processed more slowly in larger volumes, the internal surface of the intestine scales with body mass and diet, and vertebrates enlarged it in several ways that replaced each other in evolution.

1. *Typhlosole.* Lampreys and lancelets have a longitudinal fold of the intestinal wall that projects into the lumen, the typhlosole; in lampreys it is twisted slightly.
2. *Spiral valve.* In chondrichthyans, basal actinopterygians (bichirs, sturgeons, paddlefishes, gars and bowfins, in reduced form), coelacanths and lungfishes the short intestine contains a **spiral valve**, a fold of mucosa wound helically or rolled like a scroll, which forces food along a long path and greatly increases the surface ([7.17.46](/7.17.46)). Fossil coprolites with spiral marks show that placoderms and early sarcopterygians had it too. The spiral valve is a primitive gnathostome feature lost in teleosts and tetrapods.
3. *Pyloric caeca.* Many teleosts have finger-like blind outpocketings of the intestine just behind the stomach, from one (in some species) to more than 1,000 (in salmonids and mackerels); they increase the absorptive surface and secrete enzymes.
4. *Length and coiling.* Teleosts and tetrapods lengthen the intestine, which coils in the abdominal cavity. Relative intestinal length correlates with diet: in cats and dogs the intestine is roughly 3–6 times the body length, in rabbits about 10 times and in sheep and cattle more than 20 times. Tadpoles, which eat algae and detritus, have a long coiled gut that shortens by about three-quarters at metamorphosis as the frog becomes a carnivore; the change shows the link between diet and length within one life.
5. *Folds, villi and microvilli.* Circular folds of the submucosa, finger-like villi of the mucosa (in birds and mammals) and the microvilli of the epithelial cells increase the surface by factors of about 3, 10 and 20 ([7.14.28](/7.14.28#sec-28.1.2)).

### 63.7.2. Differentiation of the Intestine and Caeca

In fishes the intestine is not divided into small and large intestine; in amphibians a short large intestine (rectum) is distinguished. A *caecum* (blind sac) at the junction of the small and large intestine appears first in reptiles; it is single in many lizards and turtles, paired in most birds (large in herbivorous birds such as grouse and ostriches, minute or absent in parrots, raptors and passerines). Mammals have a small intestine of duodenum, jejunum and ileum, and a large intestine of caecum, colon and rectum; the colon is long and sacculated in herbivores. The appendix, a narrow lymphoid extension of the caecum, has evolved independently more than 30 times among mammals.

Herbivores that ferment in the hindgut (hindgut fermenters: horses, rhinoceroses, elephants, rabbits, many rodents, koalas, and many herbivorous lizards and birds) house their microbes in the caecum and colon, which are enormous: the caecum of a horse holds about $30\,\mathrm{L}$ and the colon about $80\,\mathrm{L}$. Hindgut fermentation recovers less of the microbial protein, which passes out with the feces; rabbits, hares and many rodents compensate by **coprophagy**, eating the soft caecal pellets (caecotrophs) produced at night to digest them a second time.

### 63.7.3. Fermentation and Body Size

The choice between fermentation, no fermentation and fermenting in the foregut or the hindgut depends strongly on body size. The capacity of the gut is roughly proportional to body mass $M$ in mammals (gut contents are about $10$–$15\,\%$ of body mass in large herbivores), while metabolic rate, and hence the rate of food intake, scales as $M^{3/4}$ ([7.14.30](/7.14.30)). The mean time that food remains in the gut is therefore

$$
t_{\mathrm{ret}} \propto \frac{\text{gut capacity}}{\text{intake rate}} \propto \frac{M^{1}}{M^{3/4}} = M^{1/4}.
$$
A large animal keeps each mouthful longer and can wait for its microbes to digest cell walls, which takes $1$–$2$ days for grass. This is the Jarman–Bell principle: large herbivores can live on abundant, poor food (grass, mature leaves), small ones must select rich, easily digested food (seeds, fruit, young shoots, insects).

**Example 63.7.1 (A Lower Size Limit for Fermentation)** Suppose that a herbivore must hold fibrous food for $t_{\mathrm{ret}} \geq t^\ast$ to digest a useful fraction of it, and that a $500\,\mathrm{kg}$ ungulate retains food for about $70\,\mathrm{h}$. Scaling by $M^{1/4}$, an animal of mass $M$ retains food for $70\,\mathrm{h}\times(M/500\,\mathrm{kg})^{1/4}$: about $40\,\mathrm{h}$ at $50\,\mathrm{kg}$, $22\,\mathrm{h}$ at $5\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $7\,\mathrm{h}$ at $50\,\mathrm{g}$. If cell walls need $t^\ast \approx 24\,\mathrm{h}$, animals below a few kilograms cannot rely on them. Small herbivores instead feed selectively, ferment the easily digested fraction in the caecum and recycle it by coprophagy, and the smallest ruminants (dik-diks, chevrotains, $2$–$5\,\mathrm{kg}$) are selective browsers of rich leaves and fruit. The model is crude, since retention times also depend on gut structure and food, but it explains why grass-eating ruminants are large and why no grazing mammals the size of mice exist.

## 63.8. Cloaca and Anus

A *cloaca* is the terminal chamber of the gut into which the urinary and genital ducts also open, so that feces, urine and gametes leave through a single opening. A cloaca is present in chondrichthyans, lungfishes and coelacanths, amphibians, reptiles, birds and monotremes, and in the embryos of all mammals; it is thus an ancestral gnathostome character. In lampreys, hagfishes and ray-finned fishes the anus opens separately in front of a urogenital opening (in many teleosts the urinary and genital ducts also open separately, behind the anus). Therian mammals divide the embryonic cloaca by the urorectal septum into a dorsal rectum and a ventral urogenital sinus, which opens through a urogenital opening (in males through the penis, in females in the vestibule); in many female placentals, including primates and rodents, the urethra and vagina open separately ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)).

The cloaca of birds has three chambers in series: the coprodeum (receiving the rectum), the urodeum (with the openings of the ureters and genital ducts) and the proctodeum (with the cloacal bursa, or bursa of Fabricius, in its dorsal wall: the primary lymphoid organ where B cells mature in birds, [7.15](/7.15)). Urine from the ureters is moved backward into the coprodeum and rectum by reverse peristalsis, where water and salts are reabsorbed before the uric acid is excreted as a white paste ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)).

## 63.9. Liver and Pancreas

### 63.9.1. Liver

The liver develops as a ventral outgrowth of the foregut, the hepatic diverticulum, which grows into the septum transversum and the ventral mesentery ([7.5.30](/7.5.30#sec-30.6)). The cords of hepatocytes interweave with the capillaries of the vitelline veins, which become the hepatic portal vein and the sinusoids ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)). The hepatic diverticulum of lancelets, a blind sac of the gut wall that absorbs food and produces enzymes, is often compared with the liver, but it lacks hepatocytes organized in cords around sinusoids and a portal blood supply; whether it is homologous is uncertain.

All vertebrates have a liver as a discrete organ. Its size and form vary: in sharks the oily liver may make up a quarter of body mass and provides buoyancy ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.5.1)); in many teleosts pancreatic tissue spreads into the liver along the portal veins, forming a hepatopancreas. A gall bladder stores bile in most vertebrates. It is absent in adult lampreys (which lose the bile ducts at metamorphosis), in many birds (pigeons, parrots, ostriches) and in many mammals (horses and other perissodactyls, deer, rats, whales), which secrete bile continuously.

### 63.9.2. Pancreas

The pancreas develops from two or three buds of the gut endoderm, a dorsal bud from the duodenum and one or two ventral buds from the base of the liver diverticulum, which fuse into one organ in most vertebrates; its ducts open into the duodenum together with or close to the bile duct ([7.5.30](/7.5.30#sec-30.8)). It has two components with different histories.

- *The exocrine pancreas* (acini secreting digestive enzymes and bicarbonate, [7.14.27](/7.14.27#sec-27.4)) is absent as a separate organ in cyclostomes, where exocrine cells lie scattered in the gut wall. Chondrichthyans have a compact pancreas; in many teleosts it is diffuse along the mesenteric vessels and in the liver.
- *The endocrine pancreas* (islets of Langerhans, with insulin-secreting β cells, glucagon-secreting α cells and others; [7.14.33](/7.14.33)) is older than the exocrine organ. In lancelets, insulin-like peptides are produced by gut cells; in lampreys and hagfishes islet cells form a separate islet organ around the bile duct and in the intestinal wall. In chondrichthyans islet cells cluster around the pancreatic ducts. In many teleosts most islet tissue is concentrated in one or a few large islets (Brockmann bodies) near the gall bladder or spleen. Tetrapods have islets scattered among the acini.

## Summary

- The coelom of the lateral plate is divided by the septum transversum into pericardial and pleuroperitoneal cavities. The lungs were separated from the abdominal cavity independently in mammals (pleural cavities and diaphragm), crocodylians (post-hepatic septum and hepatic piston) and birds (lungs fixed to the body wall, horizontal and oblique septa).
- The dorsal mesentery persists along the gut (greater omentum in mammals); the ventral mesentery survives only around the liver (lesser omentum, falciform ligament).
- The gut and its glands derive from the archenteron, with ectodermal stomodeum and proctodeum; the pharynx gives the gills, lungs and pharyngeal pouch derivatives ([7.17.64](/7.17.64), [7.17.67](/7.17.67)).
- Teeth are odontodes in the mouth. Their evolution in the lineage leading to mammals ran from homodont to heterodont, from polyphyodont to diphyodont (Definition 63.5.2), from acrodont or pleurodont to thecodont attachment (Definition 63.5.1), from many teeth on many bones to fewer teeth on the jaw margins.
- The stomach appeared with jaws and was lost many times. Birds compensate for the lack of teeth with crop and gizzard; ruminants ferment plant food in a four-chambered stomach (Definition 63.6.1), horses and rabbits in the caecum and colon.
- The intestinal surface was enlarged by a typhlosole (cyclostomes), a spiral valve (chondrichthyans and basal bony fishes), pyloric caeca and length (teleosts), and folds and villi (amniotes). Retention time scales as $M^{1/4}$, so fermentation of fibre is a strategy of large herbivores (Example 63.7.1).
- A cloaca is ancestral for gnathostomes; the liver is constant, the gall bladder and the exocrine pancreas variable, and the endocrine pancreatic cells are older than the exocrine organ.

## Questions

1. Compare the ways in which the lungs are separated from the abdominal viscera in mammals, crocodylians and birds. What do the differences imply about the evolution of these partitions?

   **Answer** Mammals divide the coelom into pleural, pericardial and peritoneal cavities with a muscular diaphragm formed from the septum transversum, pleuroperitoneal folds, oesophageal mesentery and body wall, with muscle from cervical somites. Crocodylians have a non-muscular post-hepatic septum behind the liver and a diaphragmaticus muscle from the pubis that pulls the liver backward. Birds fix their lungs to the dorsal body wall, obliterating the pleural cavities, and separate air sacs and viscera with horizontal and oblique septa. The different components and muscles show that the partitions are not homologous: each arose independently with a different ventilation mechanism (aspiration by the diaphragm, hepatic piston, air-sac bellows), while lizards and turtles retain a single pleuroperitoneal cavity.

2. Present the evidence that teeth and placoid scales are homologous. Why is the question of whether teeth evolved "outside-in" or "inside-out" still open?

   **Answer** Both have a pulp cavity surrounded by dentine and capped by enameloid, both develop from the interaction of epithelium and neural-crest mesenchyme with the same gene network, and in sharks and early chondrichthyans the scales at the margins of the jaws grade into teeth; both are odontodes. The direction is debated because some jawless vertebrates had tooth-like denticles inside the pharynx, far from the skin, which suggests that odontodes could arise in endoderm-lined regions independently of the skin, whereas in other lineages oral teeth appear to derive from marginal scales; the fossil record of the earliest jawed vertebrates is incomplete, and the epithelium forming teeth can be ectodermal or endodermal.

3. Why are mammals diphyodont while crocodiles are polyphyodont? Name mammals that deviate from diphyodonty and explain how.

   **Answer** Mammals grow to a determinate adult size and chew with precise occlusion of upper and lower teeth whose cusps interlock; a fixed adult set fitted to an adult jaw is compatible with this, whereas continuous replacement would disturb occlusion. Crocodiles grow throughout life and use teeth only for seizing, so continuous replacement keeps teeth matched to jaw size and replaces broken ones. Deviations: toothed whales and armadillos are monophyodont; elephants and manatees replace molars horizontally from behind; rodent incisors and some rodent and lagomorph cheek teeth grow continuously from open roots.

4. Using Example 63.7.1, estimate the retention time of a $20\,\mathrm{kg}$ antelope and explain why dik-diks are browsers of rich food rather than grazers.

   **Answer** $70\,\mathrm{h}\times(20/500)^{1/4} = 70 \times 0.447 = 31\,\mathrm{h}$, enough to ferment grass moderately. A dik-dik of $4\,\mathrm{kg}$ retains food for about $70 \times (4/500)^{1/4} = 70 \times 0.30 = 21\,\mathrm{h}$, below the time needed to digest fibrous grass, while its mass-specific energy demand is higher; it must therefore choose food with more soluble contents and less fibre (young leaves, shoots, fruit), which is digested quickly.

5. Explain the structure and function of the four chambers of the ruminant stomach and the role of the reticular groove.

   **Answer** The rumen and reticulum, non-glandular chambers with papillae and a honeycomb lining, form a fermentation vat where microbes digest cellulose to volatile fatty acids absorbed through the wall; coarse material is regurgitated for rechewing. The omasum, with leaf-like folds, absorbs water and remaining fatty acids and retains coarse particles. The abomasum is the glandular stomach that digests the microbes with acid and enzymes, supplying protein. In suckling young, the reticular groove closes into a tube that carries milk directly from the oesophagus to the abomasum, so milk is digested by the young animal's own enzymes rather than fermented.

6. Why is a stomach absent in lampreys, chimaeras and carp? What does the comparison suggest about the function of the stomach?

   **Answer** Lampreys lack it ancestrally: they are jawless and do not take large prey, and the stomach appeared with gnathostomes. Chimaeras, lungfishes, cyprinids such as the carp and many other teleosts lost it secondarily, with the genes for the proton pump and pepsinogen. These animals feed continuously on small or hard-shelled food that is crushed by tooth plates or pharyngeal teeth, so that storage and acid digestion of large prey are less important. The comparison suggests that the primary functions of the stomach are storing large meals and beginning their digestion with acid and pepsin, functions that can be dispensed with when food comes in small portions.
