Comparative anatomy compares the bodies of different animals in order to explain them. For vertebrates the comparison is unusually rewarding: from a lamprey to a human, almost every bone, muscle, nerve and vessel of one species can be matched with a part of another, and the differences between the matched parts record both the history of the lineage and the work each part does. The thirteen chapters that follow ([7.17.56](/7.17.56)–[7.17.68](/7.17.68)) take the organ systems one at a time, from the skin to the sense organs, and follow each through the vertebrate classes whose diversity was described in [7.17.45](/7.17.45)–[7.17.55](/7.17.55). This chapter supplies the concepts that the comparisons rely on.

Three questions run through every comparison. What is a structure made of and where does it come from in the embryo? Which structures of other animals is it the same as, that is, homologous with? And what does it do, and how does its form fit that function? The first question is answered by development ([7.5](/7.5)), the second by phylogeny ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)), the third by functional morphology and physiology ([7.14](/7.14)). Comparative anatomy is the place where the three answers are put together.

The chapter first sketches the history of the field, because many of its terms (homology, archetype, correlation of parts) were coined before evolution was accepted and changed their meaning afterwards (§56.1). It then treats homology and its criteria (§56.2), the analysis of form and function (§56.3), size and shape (§56.4) and the elementary mechanics that the later chapters use (§56.5). Section 56.6 introduces the skeletal tissues and the distinction between dermal bone and cartilage bone, which is the basic classification of the skeleton in the four chapters that follow, and §56.7 tabulates the embryonic sources of vertebrate structures. Section 56.8 explains how the comparative chapters are organized and how their "class" columns correspond to the modern phylogeny and to the Chinese textbooks.

## 56.1. Questions and History

### 56.1.1. What Comparative Anatomy Asks

Anatomy describes the parts of one body; comparative anatomy describes how the parts of different bodies correspond. A correspondence is not a resemblance. The wing of a bat and the wing of a fly resemble each other because both are flat airfoils; the wing of a bat and the flipper of a whale resemble each other very little, yet each contains a humerus, a radius and an ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges in the same order. Comparative anatomy is built on the second kind of correspondence, which persists in spite of changes of shape and function, and it uses the first kind as evidence about function.

Its practical program has changed little since the nineteenth century: dissect, describe, draw homologies, and look for regularities. What has changed is the explanation of the regularities. Before 1859 they were explained by a plan of nature or by conditions of existence; since Darwin, by common descent and natural selection; and since the 1980s also by the developmental mechanisms that build the parts.

### 56.1.2. Cuvier: Correlation of Parts and Conditions of Existence

Georges Cuvier (1769–1832), the founder of comparative anatomy as a discipline, held that an animal is an integrated whole whose parts must fit one another to permit it to live, the *conditions of existence*. From this followed his *principle of the correlation of parts*: a carnivore with teeth for cutting flesh must also have jaws for holding prey, claws for seizing it, limbs for pursuit and a gut for digesting meat. Cuvier claimed, with some exaggeration, that a single bone allowed him to reconstruct the whole animal, and he used the principle to describe fossil mammals from the gypsum of Montmartre and to establish that species become extinct.

Cuvier divided animals into four *embranchements* (vertebrates, molluscs, articulates and radiates), each built on a different plan, and denied that the plans could be connected. The idea of a body plan survives in the concept of the **body plan** ([7.17.3](/7.17.3)), and his functional reasoning survives in functional morphology. What did not survive is his belief that correlation is so strict that no part can change without all the others: the mosaic evolution of mammalian characters in the synapsids ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)) shows that parts can change separately.

### 56.1.3. Geoffroy and Owen: Unity of Plan, Archetype and Homology

Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire (1772–1844), Cuvier's colleague and opponent, emphasized unity of plan over function. His *principle of connections* held that a part is identified by its position relative to other parts, not by its shape or use; a bone keeps its neighbours however much it changes. This is still the most important criterion of homology (§56.2.3). Geoffroy went further and tried to derive the arthropod from the vertebrate plan by turning it upside down, which Cuvier ridiculed in the famous debate of 1830; the inversion was revived in molecular form 160 years later, when the dorsoventral patterning genes of flies and vertebrates were found to be used in opposite orientation ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

Richard Owen (1804–1892) gave the terms their classical definitions in 1843. An *analogue* is "a part or organ in one animal which has the same function as another part or organ in a different animal"; a *homologue* is "the same organ in different animals under every variety of form and function". Owen distinguished special homology (the same part in different species), general homology (the relation of a part to the ideal plan) and serial homology (repeated parts of one body), and he drew a vertebrate archetype, an idealized series of segments each with a vertebra, ribs and appendages, of which every real vertebrate was a modification.

Owen, like Goethe and Oken before him, believed the skull to be made of modified vertebrae (the *vertebral theory of the skull*). Thomas Henry Huxley refuted it in 1858 by showing that the skull develops from a continuous cartilaginous trough and from dermal bones, not from a series of vertebra-like segments. The question of segmentation of the head was nevertheless revived repeatedly and is answered today, in part, by the pharyngeal arches and the rhombomeres of the hindbrain ([7.17.58](/7.17.58), [7.5.20](/7.5.20)).

### 56.1.4. After Darwin

Darwin reinterpreted Owen's archetype as the ancestor: homologous parts are similar because they are inherited from a common ancestor and modified by descent. The comparative anatomy of the late nineteenth century, led by Carl Gegenbaur in Germany, used homologies to reconstruct phylogeny, and much of the descriptive anatomy in modern textbooks dates from that period. Ernst Haeckel's biogenetic law, that ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny, encouraged the search for ancestral states in embryos; the law in its strong form is false ([7.17.5](/7.17.5)), but embryos do retain many ancestral features, such as pharyngeal arches and a two-chambered heart tube, because development is built by modification of earlier developmental programs.

In the twentieth century three developments changed the field. Cladistic methods (Hennig 1950, 1966) made homology a testable hypothesis about shared derived characters on a **phylogenetic tree** ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)). Functional morphology, using cineradiography, electromyography, force measurements and engineering theory, turned the correlation of form and function into an experimental science. And evolutionary developmental biology (evo-devo), from the 1980s, discovered that structures as different as the eyes of flies and mice or the limbs of insects and tetrapods use shared genetic toolkits (**deep homology**, [7.5.39](/7.5.39)), and began to explain how changes in development produce changes in form.

| Period | Leading figures | Explanation of anatomical regularity | Lasting contributions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1790–1830 | Cuvier | Conditions of existence; function determines form | Correlation of parts, extinction, functional reasoning |
| 1800–1850 | Geoffroy, Goethe, Oken, Owen | Unity of plan; the archetype | Principle of connections, the terms homology and analogy, serial homology |
| 1859–1900 | Darwin, Huxley, Gegenbaur, Haeckel | Common descent with modification | Homology as inheritance, phylogenetic reconstruction from anatomy |
| 1900–1980 | Goodrich, de Beer, Romer, Hennig | Phylogeny and adaptation | Classical vertebrate anatomy, homology of development, cladistics |
| 1980– | Many | Phylogeny, adaptation and developmental mechanism | Evo-devo, deep homology, quantitative functional morphology, CT imaging of fossils |

## 56.2. Homology

### 56.2.1. Homology and Its Kinds

**Homology** was defined in [7.10.2](/7.10.2) as similarity due to common ancestry: two features are homologous when they derive from one feature in their most recent common ancestor. Homology is a relation between features of organisms, and it is always relative to a level: the wing of a bat and the wing of a bird are homologous as forelimbs, inherited from the forelimb of the first tetrapods, but not as wings, because their common ancestor had no wing. Similarity that is not due to common ancestry is **homoplasy**.

Comparative anatomy uses several kinds of homology, distinguished by what is being compared.

| Kind | Compared | Example | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Historical (phylogenetic, special) homology | The same part in different species | The stapes of a mammal and the hyomandibula of a fish | Descent from a part of a common ancestor |
| Serial homology | Repeated parts of one body | Vertebrae; ribs; the forelimb and hindlimb; the teeth of one jaw | Repetition of one developmental program at several sites |
| Developmental homology | Parts that arise from the same precursor or by the same developmental process | Elements derived from the same pharyngeal arch; the lens induced from a placode | Shared embryonic origin; partly a criterion for the first two |
| Deep homology | Non-homologous structures built with homologous genetic circuits | The eyes of flies and mice (Pax6); appendages of insects and tetrapods (Distal-less) | Inheritance of a regulatory network rather than of the structure |

**Definition 56.2.1 (Serial Homology)** **Serial homology** is the correspondence between repeated parts of a single organism that are built by the same developmental program at different positions along a body axis, such as the successive vertebrae and ribs, the pharyngeal arches, the teeth of a tooth row, or the forelimb and hindlimb. Parts of one series are serial homologues; the same element of a series in different species is in addition historically homologous.

Serial homologues differ in their positional identity, which in vertebrates is specified largely by **Hox genes** along the anteroposterior axis ([7.5.23](/7.5.23#sec-23.9)). The cervical, thoracic and lumbar vertebrae of a mammal are serial homologues that have been given different identities (§60), and the humerus and the femur are serial homologues because the forelimb and hindlimb buds express the same limb program, distinguished by *Tbx5* in the forelimb and *Tbx4* with *Pitx1* in the hindlimb ([7.5.31](/7.5.31)). Serial homology explains why a mutation can turn one member of a series into another (a homeotic transformation): a cervical vertebra that bears ribs, a lumbar vertebra that becomes sacral.

Within the limb, the correspondence between fore and hind elements is one to one: humerus and femur, radius and tibia, ulna and fibula, carpals and tarsals, metacarpals and metatarsals, phalanges of fingers and of toes. The proximal–distal regions themselves, **stylopod**, **zeugopod** and **autopod**, are defined by development ([7.5.31](/7.5.31)), and the same three-part scheme is used for all tetrapods in [7.17.61](/7.17.61).

### 56.2.2. Homoplasy

Homoplasy has several sources, and distinguishing them is part of reading any comparative table.

- Convergence (**convergent evolution**): similar features evolve from different starting points under similar demands, such as the streamlined bodies of sharks, ichthyosaurs and dolphins, or the wings of pterosaurs, birds and bats built from different digits.
- Parallelism (**parallel evolution**): similar features evolve independently from the same starting point, often by changes in the same developmental pathway, such as the repeated loss of pelvic spines in freshwater sticklebacks by mutations of the same *Pitx1* enhancer.
- Reversal: a derived feature reverts to the ancestral condition, such as the secondary closure of the temporal fenestrae in turtles ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.3.4)) or the loss of limbs in snakes, caecilians and many lizards.

Homoplasy and homology can coexist in one structure. The wings of birds and bats are convergent as wings and homologous as forelimbs; the middle ear of mammals, lizards and frogs is homologous in its stapes and convergent in its tympanum, which evolved independently in each lineage ([7.17.48](/7.17.48), [7.17.68](/7.17.68)). For this reason a comparative statement should always name the level at which homology is claimed.

### 56.2.3. Criteria of Homology

Adolf Remane (1952) formalized three main criteria by which a hypothesis of homology is proposed.

1. *Position.* Structures are homologous if they occupy the same position relative to other parts of a comparable system. This is Geoffroy's principle of connections. The mammalian incus is identified with the reptilian quadrate because it lies between the stapes and the element that was the articular, in the chain from the braincase to the old jaw joint, although it no longer forms a jaw joint.
2. *Special quality.* Structures that are complex and agree in many independent details are homologous even if they lie in different positions. The more independent details agree, the less likely is the agreement by chance. Teeth and the placoid scales of sharks share pulp, dentine, an enameloid cap and the same developmental gene network, and are therefore homologous as odontodes ([7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1.3)).
3. *Continuity through intermediates.* Dissimilar structures are homologous if a series of intermediate forms connects them, in fossils or in development. The dentary–squamosal jaw joint and the three ossicles of mammals are connected to the quadrate–articular joint of earlier synapsids by a series of fossils in which both joints coexisted ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)), and by the development of the malleus and incus from the back of the first arch in every mammalian embryo.

Remane's criteria generate hypotheses; phylogeny tests them. Colin Patterson (1982) listed three tests: similarity (Remane's criteria), conjunction (two structures proposed as homologous must not occur together in the same organism), and congruence (the proposed homology must agree with the phylogeny supported by other characters). Wings of birds and bats pass the first two tests as forelimbs and as wings; as wings they fail congruence, because the tree places many wingless mammals and reptiles between them.

**Example 56.2.1** The names of the skull-roof bones of fishes illustrate how the position criterion corrects tradition. Nineteenth-century anatomists called the large paired bones over the brain of osteolepiform fishes "frontals" and "parietals" by their resemblance to the bones of tetrapods. Westoll (1943) pointed out that the pineal foramen, which in tetrapods lies between the parietals, lies in these fishes between the bones called frontals, and that the bones behind them cover the otic region like the tetrapod postparietals. The fish "frontals" are therefore the parietals, and the fish "parietals" the postparietals; the fish bones in front, often called "nasals" or "postrostrals", correspond to the tetrapod frontals and nasals only in part. The renaming is now standard, and it is used throughout [7.17.58](/7.17.58) and [7.17.59](/7.17.59). The example also shows that homology between bones need not be one to one: a single bone in one taxon can correspond to two in another (by fusion or by division of an ossification center), and homology is then claimed for a field of bone, not for an individual element.

### 56.2.4. Homology and Development

It is tempting to define homology by embryonic origin, but the criterion fails in both directions. Homologous structures can develop in different ways: the lens of most vertebrates is induced by the optic cup, but in some frogs it forms without the cup; the vertebrate gut is endodermal, but the lining of the mouth and of the cloaca is ectodermal in different proportions in different species; the digits of salamanders form in anterior-to-posterior order, those of frogs and amniotes in the opposite order. And structures that develop from the same tissue need not be homologous: the many bones formed from cranial neural crest are not homologous with each other simply because they share their origin.

The modern view is that homology is a historical relation (identity by descent), and that development provides evidence for it and explains it. What is inherited is a developmental *character identity network*, a set of regulatory genes that gives a part its identity (Wagner 2014); the details of how the part is induced, where its cells come from and when it forms can drift without changing the identity. This explains why the ear ossicles of mammals remain identifiable as first- and second-arch elements in spite of their new function, and why **deep homology** can exist without structural homology.

## 56.3. Form, Function and Adaptation

### 56.3.1. Form, Function and Biological Role

Bock and von Wahlert (1965) distinguished three things that are often confused. The *form* of a feature is its structure: shape, size, material, connections. Its function is what it can do because of its form: the jaw of a cat can close with a certain force and speed, its claws can be retracted. The combination of a form and its functions is a *faculty*. The *biological role* is how the organism uses the faculty in its natural life: the cat uses the closing of its jaws to kill mice. A faculty can have several roles (the tongue of a frog captures food and helps swallow it), and a role can be performed by several faculties.

The distinction matters for comparative anatomy because selection acts on roles, through performance in the natural environment, while anatomy observes forms. A feature is adaptive for a role when it improves the performance of that role and has been shaped by selection for it. To show this, one needs (1) a functional analysis of how form produces performance, (2) evidence that performance matters for fitness in the field, and (3) a phylogenetic comparison showing that the form evolved together with the role. This is the program of ecomorphology and of the comparative method in evolutionary biology ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)).

### 56.3.2. Preadaptation, Exaptation and Tinkering

Most new structures are modified old ones. A **preadaptation** is a feature that, evolved for one role, happens to be suitable for another and makes the new role possible; the term implies no foresight. Gould and Vrba (1982) introduced **exaptation** for any feature that now serves a role for which it was not originally selected, keeping "adaptation" for features shaped by selection for their current role. Feathers, first evolved in small theropods probably for insulation and display, were exapted for flight; the lungs of early bony fishes were exapted as the swim bladder of teleosts ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)); the hyomandibula, a jaw prop in fishes, was exapted as a sound conductor, the stapes.

François Jacob (1977) compared evolution to a tinkerer rather than an engineer: it works with what is available, adapting an old part to a new use. The vertebrate skeleton shows this everywhere. The jaws are modified gill arches, the middle ear bones modified jaw elements, the limbs modified fins, the mammalian diaphragm a modified set of hypaxial muscles. Because each modification starts from a structure built for something else, the result is rarely optimal by engineering standards: the vertebrate retina is inverted, the recurrent laryngeal nerve of a giraffe loops down to the heart and back because its ancestral course passed behind the sixth aortic arch.

### 56.3.3. Constraints

The forms that evolve are limited not only by selection but also by constraints. A *phylogenetic constraint* is the limitation imposed by the inherited body plan: no tetrapod has evolved six limbs, and the wings of birds and bats had to be made from forelimbs. A **developmental constraint** is a bias in the variation that development can produce: the near invariance of seven cervical vertebrae in mammals, discussed in [7.17.60](/7.17.60), is thought to result from the coupling of vertebral identity with other developmental processes, so that changes are associated with harmful side effects. A *structural* or *mechanical constraint* follows from physics: animals cannot grow larger at constant shape without changing their skeletons (§56.4).

Constraints and selection are not alternatives. A trend can arise because selection favors it, because variation is biased, or both; the comparative evidence of many lineages is needed to tell them apart. Kemp's model of *correlated progression* in the origin of mammals ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)) stresses that many features changed together, each by small steps, because each change was functionally linked with others: the dentary could enlarge only as the jaw muscles were reorganized, and the postdentary bones could become ear bones only once they were relieved of their jaw-joint function.

### 56.3.4. Inferring Soft Parts in Fossils: the Extant Phylogenetic Bracket

Comparative anatomy is also the basis for reconstructing extinct animals, whose soft tissues are rarely preserved. Witmer (1995) formalized the method as the *extant phylogenetic bracket*. To infer a soft-tissue feature in a fossil, one looks at the two closest living groups that bracket it on the tree, one on either side, and at the osteological correlates of the feature in them, such as a muscle scar, a foramen for a nerve or a canal for a vessel. If both bracketing groups have the feature and its correlate, and the fossil has the correlate, the inference is strongest (level I); if only one bracketing group has it, the inference is weaker (level II); if neither does, it is speculative (level III).

For a non-avian dinosaur the bracket is formed by crocodylians and birds. Both have a four-chambered heart, so a four-chambered heart in dinosaurs is a level I inference, although the heart leaves no trace on the skeleton; both have air sacs connected to the lung, but only birds pneumatize the vertebrae with them, and the presence of pneumatic foramina in the vertebrae of sauropods and theropods makes air sacs there a level I inference with a skeletal correlate ([7.17.52](/7.17.52), [7.17.53](/7.17.53)). Similar reasoning reconstructs the jaw muscles of synapsids from the bracket of reptiles and mammals ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)), and the comparative chapters use the bracket whenever they attribute soft structures to fossil groups.

## 56.4. Size and Shape

### 56.4.1. Allometry

Animals of different size are rarely scaled copies of one another. **Allometry** ([7.5.34](/7.5.34#sec-34.6)) is the change of proportions with size, and is described by the power law

$$
y = a x^{b},
$$
where $y$ is a measurement of a part, $x$ a measure of overall size (often body mass $M$), $a$ the scaling coefficient and $b$ the scaling exponent. Taking logarithms gives the straight line $\log y = \log a + b\log x$, whose slope $b$ is estimated by regression of log-transformed data. If $y$ and $x$ are both lengths, $b = 1$ means isometry (constant shape), $b > 1$ positive allometry (the part becomes relatively larger in larger animals) and $b < 1$ negative allometry. If $x$ is mass, isometry of a length corresponds to $b = 1/3$, of an area to $b = 2/3$.

Three kinds of allometry are distinguished by the data compared. *Ontogenetic allometry* compares individuals of one species at different ages: the human head grows with negative allometry, so the newborn's head is a quarter of its length, the adult's an eighth. *Static allometry* compares adults of one species: the antlers of deer and the horns of beetles grow with strong positive allometry. *Evolutionary allometry* compares species: the brain of mammals scales approximately as $M^{0.75}$ ([7.14.46](/7.14.46)), the skeleton as $M^{1.09}$ (Prange et al. 1979, [7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.1.4)). Changes in the timing of growth (**heterochrony**) shift a species along an allometric line or change its slope, which is one of the most common ways in which skull proportions evolve ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)).

### 56.4.2. Galileo's Bones and Three Models of Similarity

Galileo, in his *Two New Sciences* (1638), drew the bone of a large animal beside the bone of a small one to show that a larger animal of the same proportions would be too weak: the weight to be supported grows as the cube of the linear dimensions, the strength of the bone as the square, so a larger animal needs relatively thicker bones. The argument can be stated as three alternative models for how the length $l$ and diameter $d$ of a long bone should scale with body mass $M$.

| Model | Condition kept constant | Length $l$ | Diameter $d$ | Relation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Geometric similarity | Shape | $M^{1/3}$ | $M^{1/3}$ | $d \propto l$ |
| Elastic similarity (McMahon 1973) | Relative deflection under the body's own weight | $M^{1/4}$ | $M^{3/8}$ | $d \propto l^{3/2}$ |
| Static stress similarity | Stress under the body's own weight | — | $M^{1/2}$ | $d^2 \propto M$ |

**Formula 56.4.1 (Elastic Similarity)** Treat a limb bone as a beam of length $l$ and diameter $d$ loaded in bending by a force proportional to body weight, $F \propto M g \propto \rho g l d^2$ (body mass taken proportional to the volume of a limb-shaped body, $l d^2$). The deflection $\delta$ of a cantilever is proportional to $F l^{3}/(E I)$, where $E$ is the **Young's modulus** of bone and $I \propto d^{4}$ the second moment of area of its cross section. The relative deflection is

$$
\frac{\delta}{l} \propto \frac{F l^{2}}{E d^{4}} \propto \frac{\rho g\, l^{3}}{E\, d^{2}} .
$$
Keeping $\delta/l$ constant across sizes with constant $\rho$ and $E$ requires $d^{2} \propto l^{3}$, that is $d \propto l^{3/2}$. With $M \propto l d^{2} \propto l^{4}$ this gives $l \propto M^{1/4}$ and $d \propto M^{3/8}$.

The same scaling follows from requiring that a column should not buckle under its own weight (Euler buckling), which is why McMahon also derived the proportions of trees from it. Elastic similarity predicts that larger animals have relatively short, thick limbs; geometric similarity that they are simply scaled up.

What do bones actually do? Across all mammals from shrews to elephants, Alexander and colleagues (1979) found that the lengths and diameters of the femur and humerus scale with exponents close to $0.35$ and $0.36$, near geometric similarity; within bovids, McMahon (1975) found proportions closer to elastic similarity. Neither model holds over the whole range. The reason, shown by Biewener (1989), is that large mammals do not keep the same posture: a mouse runs crouched, with flexed joints, a horse with nearly straight legs. Straightening the limb aligns the bones with the load and reduces the bending moments, and increases the effective mechanical advantage of the muscles (§56.5.1) roughly as $M^{0.26}$, so that peak bone stresses are similar (about 50–100 MPa during fast locomotion) from small to large mammals. The safety factor of limb bones, the ratio of failure stress to peak stress in normal life, is about 2–4 for most vertebrates.

The bones of birds add a twist. Because flight favors low mass, bird limb bones are thin-walled tubes, many filled with air (**pneumatic bones**, [7.17.53](/7.17.53)). For a given mass of bone, a hollow tube of larger diameter resists bending and buckling much better than a solid rod, because the second moment of area grows with the fourth power of the radius. The ratio of inner to outer diameter is about 0.5–0.6 in terrestrial mammals and up to 0.9 in the wing bones of large soaring birds and pterosaurs.

### 56.4.3. Transformation Grids and Morphometrics

D'Arcy Wentworth Thompson, in *On Growth and Form* (1917), drew the outline of one species on a rectangular grid of coordinates and showed that the outline of a related species could be obtained by deforming the grid in a simple way: a shear turns the hatchet fish *Argyropelecus* into *Sternoptyx*, a radial expansion of the posterior part turns the porcupine fish *Diodon* into the sunfish *Mola*. He argued that the differences between related forms are often not changes in individual parts but coordinated changes of the whole, such as might be produced by differential growth.

Thompson's method was qualitative, but it is the ancestor of modern *geometric morphometrics*. Corresponding points (landmarks) are digitized on each specimen; the configurations are superimposed by translating, rotating and scaling them to the best fit (Procrustes superimposition); and the remaining differences in shape are analyzed statistically, typically by principal component analysis, which defines a morphospace of shape variation. The deformation from one configuration to another can be displayed as a thin-plate spline, a smooth interpolation that produces exactly Thompson's deformed grid. These methods are now the standard way to quantify the evolution of skull shape ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)), for example in the allometric shape changes of mammalian skulls or the divergence of beak shapes in birds.

## 56.5. Elementary Biomechanics

The mechanics of support and movement in all animals is introduced in [7.17.4](/7.17.4); here are the concepts that the skeletal and muscular chapters use, in the form they take in vertebrates.

### 56.5.1. Force, Torque and Levers

A muscle pulls on a bone at its insertion and turns it about a joint. The turning effect is the **torque** (moment) of the force, the force multiplied by its perpendicular distance from the joint, the moment arm. A bone with a joint and two forces acting on it is a lever: the muscle force $F_{\mathrm{in}}$ acts at the in-lever $l_{\mathrm{in}}$, the force on the environment $F_{\mathrm{out}}$ (a bite, a push against the ground) at the out-lever $l_{\mathrm{out}}$. At equilibrium the torques are equal,

$$
F_{\mathrm{in}}\, l_{\mathrm{in}} = F_{\mathrm{out}}\, l_{\mathrm{out}},
$$
so the mechanical advantage is $F_{\mathrm{out}}/F_{\mathrm{in}} = l_{\mathrm{in}}/l_{\mathrm{out}}$. The velocity ratio is the inverse: the tip of the lever moves $l_{\mathrm{out}}/l_{\mathrm{in}}$ times faster than the insertion. A lever cannot amplify both force and speed; each lever system sits somewhere on the trade-off between the two.

Levers are classified by the positions of the joint (fulcrum), the applied force and the resistance. In a first-class lever the fulcrum lies between the two forces (the triceps extending the elbow, the head balanced on the atlas); in a second-class lever the resistance lies between the fulcrum and the applied force (rising on the toes, with the ball of the foot as fulcrum); in a third-class lever the applied force lies between the fulcrum and the resistance, as in most limb and jaw muscles, which insert close to the joint and give speed at the expense of force. The comparison of digging and running limbs in [7.17.61](/7.17.61) and of the jaws in [7.17.59](/7.17.59) and [7.17.62](/7.17.62) is an application of this rule: a mole's humerus has a long medial epicondyle and olecranon (large in-levers) and short distal segments; a horse's limb has short in-levers and long distal segments.

### 56.5.2. Stress, Strain and the Material of Bone

The *stress* in a loaded material is the force per unit area of cross section, $\sigma = F/A$; the *strain* is the relative change of length, $\varepsilon = \Delta l/l$; in the elastic range they are proportional, $\sigma = E\varepsilon$, with Young's modulus $E$. A bone can be loaded in tension, compression, shear, bending (tension on one side, compression on the other) and torsion, and in life usually in combinations. Compact bone is about $15$–$25\,\mathrm{GPa}$ stiff; its strength is greatest in compression (about $170$–$200\,\mathrm{MPa}$ along the grain), less in tension (about $100$–$150\,\mathrm{MPa}$) and least in shear (about $50$–$70\,\mathrm{MPa}$). The Chinese textbooks quote the same values as $1300$–$2100\,\mathrm{kg\,cm^{-2}}$.

Bone owes these properties to its composite structure. Hydroxyapatite crystals resist compression but are brittle; **collagen** fibres resist tension and stop cracks from spreading. The proportion of mineral is adjusted to the demand: the ear bones of whales, which transmit sound, contain about 86 % mineral and are very stiff but brittle; deer antlers, which must absorb impacts in fights, about 59 %, and are tough. Because bending stresses are greatest at the surface, long bones are tubes with compact walls around a marrow cavity; their ends, which receive loads from joints in many directions, consist of spongy bone whose trabeculae are aligned with the principal stresses.

### 56.5.3. Wolff's Law and Remodeling

In 1892 Julius Wolff summarized the observation that the architecture of bone corresponds to its loads, and that bone changes its form and internal structure when its loads change. This "Wolff's law" is a description, not a mechanism; the mechanism is **bone remodeling** ([7.5.24](/7.5.24#sec-24.11)), the coupled removal of bone by **osteoclasts** and its replacement by **osteoblasts**, regulated by osteocytes that sense strain in the matrix. Frost's *mechanostat* model states that bone is added where strains exceed an upper threshold (around $1500$–$3000$ microstrain) and removed where they fall below a lower one (around $100$–$300$ microstrain). The model explains why the bones of the playing arm of tennis players are thicker than those of the other arm, why astronauts and bedridden patients lose bone, and why the bones of birds that have lost flight thicken.

Remodeling also limits what bone can tell us. The shape of an adult bone reflects not only its genetic program but also the loads of the individual's life; crests and tuberosities where large muscles insert are larger in more active individuals. Paleontologists use such muscle scars to reconstruct musculature (§58, [7.17.62](/7.17.62)), but must allow for the effect of individual use.

### 56.5.4. Joints

Bones meet at joints, and the type of joint determines what movement the skeleton allows. Joints are classified by the tissue that connects the bones.

| Type | Connecting tissue | Movement | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suture | A thin layer of fibrous tissue between interlocking edges of dermal bones | None or very little; growth at the margins | Sutures of the skull roof; the junctions of turtle shell plates |
| Syndesmosis | A ligament or membrane | Slight | The distal tibia and fibula of many mammals; the interosseous membrane of the forearm |
| Synchondrosis | Hyaline cartilage, often temporary | None; a growth zone | The cartilage between the basioccipital and basisphenoid; the growth plates of long bones |
| Symphysis | Fibrocartilage | Slight | Intervertebral discs of mammals; the pubic symphysis; the mandibular symphysis of many mammals |
| Synovial joint (diarthrosis) | A capsule enclosing a cavity filled with synovial fluid; articular surfaces covered with cartilage | Free, limited by the shape of the surfaces and by ligaments | Joints of the limbs, the jaw joint, the zygapophyses |

Synovial joints are further described by their degrees of freedom: hinge joints (the elbow, the interphalangeal joints) allow rotation about one axis; pivot joints (the atlas on the dens of the axis, the radius on the ulna in pronation) rotation about the long axis; condyloid and saddle joints (the wrist, the carpometacarpal joint of the human thumb) two axes; ball-and-socket joints (shoulder and hip) three. Fusion of bones (synostosis) is the ultimate reduction of mobility and occurs repeatedly where stiffness is favoured: the synsacrum and pygostyle of birds, the cannon bone of ruminants, the fused cervical vertebrae of whales and jerboas. Conversely, new joints within former single elements make a skeleton kinetic, as in the skull of lizards, snakes and birds ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)).

## 56.6. The Skeletal Tissues

### 56.6.1. Cartilage and Bone

The vertebrate skeleton is built of a few tissues, described histologically in [7.17.2](/7.17.2#sec-2.4.2) and as mineralized tissues of the earliest vertebrates in [7.17.45](/7.17.45#sec-45.1.3). They are recapitulated here with the features that matter for comparison.

| Tissue | Cells | Matrix | Growth | Where it predominates |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Notochord** | Vacuolated cells under pressure, inside a fibrous sheath | Collagen sheath | Stiff but flexible rod | Axis of all vertebrate embryos; adult axis of cyclostomes, sturgeons, coelacanths, lungfishes |
| Hyaline cartilage | Chondrocytes in lacunae | Collagen II and proteoglycans, avascular | Interstitial and appositional | Embryonic skeleton; articular surfaces; skeleton of cyclostomes |
| Calcified cartilage | Chondrocytes | Cartilage impregnated with apatite, often as tesserae | Appositional | Skeleton of chondrichthyans |
| Fibrocartilage, elastic cartilage | Chondrocytes and fibroblasts | Collagen I bundles or elastic fibres | — | Intervertebral discs, pubic symphysis; external ear and epiglottis of mammals |
| Cellular bone | Osteoblasts, osteocytes, osteoclasts | Collagen I with hydroxyapatite, vascular | Appositional only; remodeled | Skeleton of most bony vertebrates |
| Acellular bone | Osteoblasts that retreat from the matrix | As cellular bone, without osteocytes | Appositional | Heterostracan armour (aspidin); skeleton of most advanced teleosts |
| Dentine, enamel or enameloid | Odontoblasts (neural crest), ameloblasts (epidermis) | Highly mineralized | Not remodeled | Teeth, odontodes, placoid scales, ganoine |

Cartilage grows by expanding from inside (interstitial growth), bone only by adding to its surfaces (appositional growth), because osteocytes are locked in a rigid matrix. A skeleton made of bone can therefore grow only if its parts are separated by growth zones of cartilage (the **growth plates** of long bones, the synchondroses of the skull base) or by sutures between dermal bones. This is one reason why the skeleton of all vertebrate embryos is first laid down in cartilage, and why fused bones in adults (the mammalian occipital, the avian skull) usually began as separate elements.

### 56.6.2. Dermal Bone and Cartilage Bone

Every bone of the vertebrate skeleton forms in one of two ways ([7.5.24](/7.5.24#sec-24.6)). The distinction, although it can be made only from development or from fossils with preserved growth stages, organizes the comparative anatomy of the skull, the girdles and the skin.

**Definition 56.6.1 (Dermal Bone and Cartilage Bone)** **Dermal bone** (membrane bone) is bone that forms by **intramembranous ossification**, directly in a condensation of mesenchyme in or below the dermis, without a cartilage precursor. **Cartilage bone** (endochondral or replacement bone) is bone that forms by **endochondral ossification**, replacing a cartilage model that develops first; it is usually accompanied by perichondral bone laid down on the surface of the cartilage.

The two kinds of bone differ in history as well as in development. Dermal bone is the older: it is the bone of the armour of ostracoderms, which consisted of **odontodes** set on plates of bone in the skin, and it is homologous, as a tissue, with the bony part of scales. Perichondral bone appears in osteostracans; true endochondral bone, which replaces the interior of the cartilage, appears only in the gnathostome stem and is extensive only in **Osteichthyes**. In chondrichthyans the endoskeleton is calcified cartilage throughout life, and dermal bone survives only in the bases of placoid scales and teeth; the absence of bone is a secondary loss, since the placoderms and the earliest chondrichthyan relatives had perichondral bone.

The vertebrate skeleton therefore consists of two systems, an exoskeleton of dermal origin and an endoskeleton of cartilage origin, which meet and interlock in the head and the shoulder.

| Region | Dermal skeleton (dermal bone) | Endoskeleton (cartilage or cartilage bone) | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skin | Scales of fishes, fin rays (lepidotrichia), osteoderms of crocodylians and armadillos, the shell plates of turtles, gastralia | — | [7.17.57](/7.17.57) |
| Skull | Dermatocranium: roof, cheek, palate, opercular series, tooth-bearing jaw bones | Chondrocranium (braincase and sense capsules) and splanchnocranium (pharyngeal arch skeleton) | [7.17.58](/7.17.58), [7.17.59](/7.17.59) |
| Teeth | Teeth (odontodes) and the bone of attachment | — | [7.17.63](/7.17.63) |
| Trunk | Gastralia (abdominal ribs) | Vertebrae, ribs, sternum | [7.17.60](/7.17.60) |
| Girdles | Cleithrum, clavicle, interclavicle, supracleithrum, posttemporal | Scapula, coracoid, procoracoid; ilium, ischium, pubis | [7.17.61](/7.17.61) |
| Limbs and fins | Dermal fin rays | Radials, basals, limb bones | [7.17.61](/7.17.61) |

Several complications must be kept in mind when the distinction is used.

- The two processes can contribute to one adult bone. The mammalian temporal bone combines the dermal squamosal and tympanic with the endochondral petrosal; the human clavicle, a dermal element, forms by intramembranous ossification but develops secondary cartilage at its ends.
- Some bones form in neither the dermal skeleton nor the cartilage skeleton: *sesamoid bones* ossify within tendons (the patella, the pisiform, the radial sesamoid that forms the "thumb" of the giant panda), and *heterotopic bones* form in other connective tissues, such as the os penis (baculum) of many mammals and the os cordis in the heart of cattle.
- Some anatomists distinguish dermal bone in the strict sense, which forms in contact with the epidermis and often bears odontodes, from membrane bone formed deeper, without a dermal association (for example some tendon bones).
- In the adult the two kinds cannot be told apart histologically once remodeling has replaced the primary bone; their identification rests on development and on homology with fossils.

### 56.6.3. Bone Histology and Growth Rate

Primary bone, the bone first laid down, differs in the arrangement of its collagen fibres, and the arrangement depends on how fast it was deposited. *Lamellar bone* consists of sheets of parallel fibres whose orientation alternates between lamellae; it is deposited slowly. *Woven bone* has randomly oriented fibres and is deposited fast, as in embryos and in the callus of a healing fracture. *Fibrolamellar bone*, typical of large fast-growing mammals, birds and dinosaurs, combines a scaffold of woven bone around many vascular canals with lamellar bone filling the canals as primary osteons.

Amprino's rule (1947) relates these tissues to growth rate: the faster bone is deposited, the more disorganized its fibres and the denser its vascularization. Measurements with fluorescent labels in growing birds give deposition rates of a few micrometres per day for lamellar bone and tens of micrometres per day, in king penguin chicks up to about 170 μm per day, for fibrolamellar bone (de Margerie et al. 2004). Slow-growing ectotherms such as most living reptiles and amphibians deposit lamellar-zonal bone interrupted by *lines of arrested growth* (LAGs), formed during an annual cessation of growth; counting LAGs (skeletochronology) gives the age of an individual, as tree rings do.

These relations allow paleontologists to infer growth rates and, indirectly, metabolic rates of extinct animals from thin sections of fossil bone. The fibrolamellar bone of dinosaurs and pterosaurs, with relatively few LAGs, indicates growth rates far above those of living reptiles, and was one of the first lines of evidence for elevated metabolic rates in archosaurs ([7.17.50](/7.17.50#sec-50.5.2), [7.17.52](/7.17.52)). Secondary remodeling replaces primary bone with *secondary osteons* (Haversian systems), which are abundant in large mammals and dinosaurs; their density increases with age and with the loading of a bone.

## 56.7. Embryonic Sources of Vertebrate Structures

### 56.7.1. The Four Sources

The vertebrate embryo builds its body from the three germ layers ([7.5.9](/7.5.9)) and from two tissues that arise at the border of the neural plate and are often called the "fourth germ layer": the **neural crest** ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)) and the cranial **placodes** ([7.5.22](/7.5.22)). Both are vertebrate innovations, and according to the **new head hypothesis** (Gans and Northcutt 1983, [7.17.45](/7.17.45)) the head of vertebrates, with its paired sense organs, braincase and muscular pharynx, was built largely from them.

The **mesoderm** is divided along the mediolateral axis into the paraxial mesoderm, which in the trunk forms the **somites** ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)) and in the head the unsegmented head mesoderm; the intermediate mesoderm, which forms the kidneys and gonadal ducts ([7.17.66](/7.17.66)); and the lateral plate mesoderm, which lines the coelom and forms the heart, the blood vessels, the limb skeleton and the girdles. Each somite divides into the ventromedial **sclerotome**, the source of the vertebrae and ribs, and the dorsolateral **dermomyotome**, which forms the dermis of the back and, through the **myotome**, the skeletal muscles of the trunk and limbs.

The following table lists the main derivatives of each source that appear in the comparative chapters. Three points from the table are used repeatedly in the following chapters. First, the boundary between neural crest and mesoderm in the skull: in the mouse the bones in front of a line through the middle of the skull roof (the frontal, nasal and most facial bones) derive from neural crest and the parietal from mesoderm (Jiang et al. 2002), and a similar boundary has been mapped in the chick and the axolotl. Second, the skeleton of the pharyngeal arches is entirely neural crest in origin in the parts that form jaws and ear ossicles, whereas the posterior laryngeal cartilages of mammals arise largely from mesoderm. Third, the scapula is a compound of somitic and lateral plate cells in birds, which complicates the homology of the parts of the shoulder girdle ([7.17.61](/7.17.61)).

### 56.7.3. Origins and Homology

Because embryonic origin is evidence for homology but not its definition (§56.2.4), a shift of origin does not by itself contradict a homology. The frontal bone is neural crest-derived in the mouse but partly mesodermal in the chick; it is still the frontal. Conversely, when two elements that were thought homologous turn out to arise from different arches, the homology must be reexamined: this was one reason for reconsidering whether the alisphenoid of mammals corresponds to the epipterygoid of reptiles, a homology now well supported because both derive from the dorsal part of the first arch.

## Summary

- Comparative anatomy asks three questions of each structure: its composition and embryonic origin (development), its correspondence with structures of other animals (homology and phylogeny), and its function. Cuvier (correlation of parts, conditions of existence), Geoffroy (principle of connections), Owen (homology, analogy, the archetype, serial homology) and Darwin (homology as descent) set the terms; cladistics, functional morphology and evo-devo transformed them.
- **Homology** is identity by descent and is claimed at a stated level; Definition 56.2.1 (serial homology) covers repeated parts of one body built by one developmental program, distinguished by positional (Hox) identity. Developmental homology is evidence for homology; **deep homology** is the sharing of genetic circuits by non-homologous structures.
- Homoplasy arises by convergence, parallelism and reversal; homology and homoplasy can coexist in one structure (wings of birds and bats). Remane's criteria of position, special quality and continuity propose homologies; Patterson's tests of similarity, conjunction and congruence test them (Example 56.2.1: the fish "frontal" is the parietal).
- Form, function (faculty) and biological role are distinct; adaptation, **preadaptation** and **exaptation** describe their history; evolution tinkers with existing parts under phylogenetic, developmental and mechanical constraints.
- **Allometry** $y = ax^{b}$ describes the change of proportion with size. Geometric similarity predicts bone length and diameter $\propto M^{1/3}$, elastic similarity (Formula 56.4.1) $l \propto M^{1/4}$, $d \propto M^{3/8}$; mammals are close to geometric similarity overall and maintain similar bone stresses by changing posture. Thompson's transformation grids are the ancestors of geometric morphometrics.
- Levers trade force against speed ($F_{\mathrm{out}}/F_{\mathrm{in}} = l_{\mathrm{in}}/l_{\mathrm{out}}$); bone is a composite of apatite and collagen, strongest in compression; Wolff's law describes and remodeling explains the adaptation of bone to load.
- The skeleton consists of **dermal bone** (intramembranous) and **cartilage bone** (endochondral) (Definition 56.6.1), an exoskeleton and an endoskeleton that interlock in the skull and the shoulder girdle. Dermal bone is the older; endochondral bone is an osteichthyan feature. Bone histology (lamellar, woven, fibrolamellar; LAGs) records growth rate.
- Vertebrate structures arise from ectoderm, neural crest, placodes, the subdivisions of mesoderm (head mesoderm, sclerotome, dermomyotome, intermediate and lateral plate) and endoderm; the neural crest forms the pharyngeal arch skeleton and most of the facial skeleton.
- The comparative chapters use seven "class" columns, of which bony fishes and reptiles in the traditional sense are paraphyletic, and a single homology colour scheme for the skeleton.

## Questions

1. The wings of a bat and of a pigeon are said to be both homologous and analogous. Explain at what level each statement is true, and which of Patterson's tests each passes or fails.

   **Answer** As forelimbs, the two wings are homologous: both contain a humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges in the same connections (position and special quality), they never occur together with another forelimb in one animal (conjunction), and the phylogeny places the forelimb in the common ancestor of all tetrapods (congruence). As wings, that is as flight surfaces, they are analogous and convergent: they pass the similarity test only superficially (the bat's wing is a skin membrane stretched between elongated digits II–V, the bird's a set of feathers on a fused carpometacarpus with reduced digits), and they fail congruence, because the common ancestor of bats and birds, an early amniote, had no wing and many wingless groups lie between them on the tree.

2. A long bone of a 30 g mouse is 15 mm long and 1.2 mm thick. Predict its length and diameter in a 3 t animal under geometric and under elastic similarity, and compare the predicted slenderness ratios. Why do real large mammals not need to be as stout as elastic similarity predicts?

   **Answer** The mass ratio is $3\times10^{6}/30 = 10^{5}$. Geometric similarity: both dimensions scale by $(10^{5})^{1/3} \approx 46.4$, giving $l \approx 0.70\,\mathrm{m}$ and $d \approx 56\,\mathrm{mm}$, slenderness $l/d = 12.5$ as in the mouse. Elastic similarity: $l$ scales by $(10^{5})^{1/4} \approx 17.8$ and $d$ by $(10^{5})^{3/8} \approx 75$, giving $l \approx 0.27\,\mathrm{m}$, $d \approx 90\,\mathrm{mm}$, slenderness about 3. Real large mammals have slenderness ratios between these values, closer to geometric similarity, because they hold their limbs straighter: an upright limb is loaded mainly in compression along its axis rather than in bending, and the larger effective mechanical advantage of the muscles reduces the forces they must exert, so peak stresses remain similar without disproportionately thick bones (Biewener 1989).

3. Why is embryonic origin not a sufficient criterion of homology? Give one example of homologous structures with different developmental origins and one of non-homologous structures with the same origin.

   **Answer** Homology is a relation of descent, and developmental processes themselves evolve: the inherited identity of a part can be kept while the way it is induced, the cells it comes from and the timing of its formation change. Examples of homologous structures with different origins: the lens, which forms by induction from the optic cup in most vertebrates but independently of it in some frogs; the frontal bone, from neural crest in the mouse but partly from mesoderm in the chick; digits that form in opposite orders in salamanders and amniotes. Non-homologous structures from the same origin: the many bones of the face that all arise from cranial neural crest are not homologous with each other; the scales, feathers and hairs arise from epidermal placodes, but a feather is not homologous with a hair as a structure (only, perhaps, the placode is).

4. Classify the following as dermal bone or cartilage bone and state how you would know in a living species: the frontal, the quadrate, the clavicle, the scapula, the dentary, the femur, the patella, the osteoderms of a crocodile.

   **Answer** Dermal: frontal, clavicle, dentary, osteoderms (all form by intramembranous ossification in mesenchyme without cartilage). Cartilage bone: quadrate (ossifies in the posterior end of the cartilaginous palatoquadrate), scapula and femur (replace cartilage models). The patella is a sesamoid bone, forming by endochondral ossification of a cartilage nodule within the tendon of the quadriceps, and belongs to neither the dermal skeleton nor the primary endoskeleton. In a living species the distinction is made by following development: staining embryos for cartilage (alcian blue) and bone (alizarin red) shows whether a bone first appears as cartilage; histology shows chondrocyte hypertrophy and invasion by vessels in endochondral ossification. In adults remodeling usually erases the difference.

5. (Open) Fibrolamellar bone with few lines of arrested growth is often taken as evidence that dinosaurs were endothermic. Evaluate this inference.

   **Answer** Points to consider: (a) Amprino's rule links fibrolamellar bone to fast deposition, and fast growth to large body size at maturity reached in a few years; fast growth requires a high rate of energy assimilation, which is easier for endotherms. (b) But fast growth and endothermy are not the same: some living ectotherms (large crocodylians and turtles under favourable conditions) can produce fibrolamellar bone, and growth rate depends on food supply and temperature. (c) The absence of LAGs indicates continuous growth, but many mammals, including endotherms, form LAGs in seasonal climates (e.g., ruminants in the Arctic), so LAGs do not prove ectothermy. (d) The inference is strongest when combined with independent evidence: isotopic paleothermometry, respiratory turbinates or their absence, insulation (feathers in theropods), predator–prey ratios and the structure of the inner ear. A balanced conclusion is that most dinosaurs grew at rates within the range of living endotherms and had elevated metabolic rates, with differences between groups, rather than that histology alone proves endothermy.
