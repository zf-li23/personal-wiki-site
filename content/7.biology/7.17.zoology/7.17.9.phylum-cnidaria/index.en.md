Cnidarians — sea anemones, corals, jellyfish, hydroids and their relatives — are the sister group of the bilaterian animals and the most diverse of the non-bilaterian phyla, with about 13,300 described species. They are built from two epithelia with a jelly between them, around a single gut cavity that opens through one mouth, yet they are efficient predators, build the largest biological structures on Earth (coral reefs), and include some of the most venomous animals known. All cnidarians possess cnidae, stinging or adhesive capsules discharged with one of the fastest movements in biology. Many alternate between a sessile polyp and a swimming medusa, two body forms so different that they were once classified in different groups.

This chapter first describes the body plan (§9.1), the cnidae (§9.2) and the life cycles (§9.3). It then surveys the three subphyla: Anthozoa (§9.4), Medusozoa (§9.5) and the parasitic Endocnidozoa (§9.6). It ends with the phylogeny of the phylum (§9.7) and a comparison with the old phylum Coelenterata (§9.8). The concepts of diploblasty, the mesoglea and the gastrovascular cavity were introduced in [7.17.2](/7.17.2)–[7.17.4](/7.17.4), the planula larva in [7.17.5](/7.17.5). Cnidarians are the outgroup that allows us to reconstruct the first bilaterians ([7.17.10](/7.17.10)).

## 9.1. The Cnidarian Body Plan

**Definition 9.1.1 (Cnidaria)** The **Cnidaria** are metazoans with cnidocytes, cells that produce cnidae (stinging or adhesive capsules); they are diploblastic, with an outer epidermis and an inner gastrodermis separated by a mesoglea, have a gastrovascular cavity with a single opening, a nerve net, and a primary radial or biradial symmetry about the oral–aboral axis, and their life cycle includes a polyp, a medusa, or both.

Cnidarians are organized at the tissue grade: they have well-defined epithelia, muscle in the form of epitheliomuscular cells, nerve cells and sensory cells, but few organs. Their **diploblasts** body has two germ layers, ectoderm and endoderm. The **mesoglea** between them ranges from a thin basement membrane in *Hydra* to a thick, fibrous jelly with scattered cells in large jellyfish. Food is digested in the **gastrovascular cavity** (coelenteron), which also distributes nutrients; the single opening serves as both mouth and anus, and in the embryo it derives from the blastopore.

The symmetry is radial in appearance, with the body parts repeated around the oral–aboral axis in multiples of four (most medusozoans) or six or eight (anthozoans). But anthozoans have a second axis at right angles to the first, defined by the slit-shaped mouth, the ciliated grooves (siphonoglyphs) and the arrangement of the mesenteries. This *directive axis* makes sea anemones biradial or even bilaterally symmetrical, and the genes that pattern it (*Hox* genes, *dpp* and *chordin* homologues) are expressed in a way that has been compared with the dorsoventral and anterior–posterior axes of bilaterians (Finnerty et al. 2004).

### 9.1.1. Polyp and Medusa

**Definition 9.1.2 (Polyp)** A **polyp** is the sessile cnidarian body form: a cylindrical column attached by an aboral pedal disc or stolon, with the mouth on an oral disc at the free end surrounded by tentacles, and usually a thin mesoglea.

**Definition 9.1.3 (Medusa)** A **medusa** (jellyfish) is the free-swimming cnidarian body form: a bell- or umbrella-shaped body whose convex aboral surface (exumbrella) faces up during swimming and whose concave oral surface (subumbrella) bears a central manubrium with the mouth; tentacles hang from the margin, and the mesoglea is usually thick.

A medusa is, topologically, a polyp turned upside down and flattened along its axis, with a greatly thickened mesoglea; the gastrovascular cavity is reduced to a central stomach with radial canals running to a ring canal at the margin. The two forms divide the labour of life. The polyp, fixed to the bottom, feeds and multiplies asexually; the medusa, swimming, carries the gonads and disperses the species. ### 9.1.2. Tissues and Cells

Both epithelia consist mostly of *epitheliomuscular cells*: columnar epithelial cells whose bases extend into contractile processes containing myofilaments. In the epidermis the processes run along the body axis and form a longitudinal muscle layer; in the gastrodermis they run around the body and form a circular layer. These two layers, antagonistic and acting on the fluid in the gastrovascular cavity, form a **hydrostatic skeleton** ([7.17.4](/7.17.4)): contraction of the circular layer lengthens the polyp, contraction of the longitudinal layer shortens it. In jellyfish the swimming muscles of the subumbrella are striated, and some anthozoans and medusae also have muscle cells separated from the epithelium.

Among the epithelial cells lie:

- *interstitial cells* (i-cells), small multipotent stem cells in hydrozoans that give rise to nematocytes, nerve cells, gland cells and gametes; other cnidarians have comparable but less well characterized stem cells;
- cnidocytes (§9.2), mostly in the epidermis of the tentacles;
- gland cells in the gastrodermis, which secrete enzymes into the gastrovascular cavity, and mucous cells around the mouth;
- sensory cells and nerve cells.

Digestion begins extracellularly in the gastrovascular cavity, where proteases break prey into fragments, and is completed intracellularly in gastrodermal cells that phagocytose the fragments. Indigestible remains are expelled through the mouth. Gas exchange and excretion (mostly of ammonia) take place across the body surface, which in all cnidarians is within a few cell layers of the water.

### 9.1.3. Nerve Net and Sense Organs

The nervous system is a **nerve net** of neurons beneath the epidermis and the gastrodermis, connected by both chemical (mostly peptidergic) and electrical synapses. It conducts in all directions, so a stimulus applied anywhere spreads over the body. The net is not uniform: it is condensed around the mouth of polyps and into two nerve rings at the bell margin of hydromedusae, which act as a central pattern generator for swimming. Cnidarian neurons use many neuropeptides and the classical transmitters; their evolution is discussed in [7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.1).

Medusae have specialized sense organs at the bell margin. Statocysts, vesicles containing a calcareous statolith that touches sensory cilia, detect the orientation of the bell. Ocelli, from simple pigment-cup eyes to lensed eyes, detect light. In scyphozoans and cubozoans these structures are grouped in club-shaped marginal *rhopalia*, each with a statocyst, ocelli and a pacemaker that sets the rhythm of the bell. The rhopalia of box jellyfish carry six eyes each, four simple and two camera-type eyes with a lens, a cornea and a retina; with four rhopalia a box jellyfish thus has 24 eyes. The lensed eyes of the mangrove box jellyfish *Tripedalia cystophora* look up through the water surface and use the mangrove canopy to keep the animal near the roots where it feeds (Garm et al. 2011).

## 9.2. Cnidae

### 9.2.1. Structure and Types

**Definition 9.2.1 (Cnidocyte)** A **cnidocyte** is the cell that produces and contains a cnida; in medusozoans it bears a mechanosensory cilium-based trigger (the cnidocil) at its surface, and it can discharge its cnida only once.

**Definition 9.2.2 (Cnida)** A **cnida** is the secretory capsule of a cnidocyte, produced inside a giant post-Golgi vacuole: a capsule wall of collagen-like proteins closed by an operculum or apical flaps, containing a long hollow tubule coiled inside it, which is everted explosively at discharge. Cnidae that penetrate prey and inject venom are **nematocysts**.

About 30 types of cnidae are distinguished, by the shape of the tubule, its spines and whether its tip is open or closed; their combination, the *cnidome*, is a taxonomic character. There are three functional classes:

| Class | Tubule | Function | Occurrence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nematocysts | Often armed with spines, open at the tip, injects venom | Prey capture, defence | All cnidarians (polar capsules in Myxozoa) |
| Spirocysts | Unarmed, sticky microfibrils | Entangle prey, attach | Hexacorallia only |
| Ptychocysts | Unarmed, folded | Build the felt-like tube | Ceriantharia only |

The cnidocil of hydrozoans and the ciliary cone of anthozoans are mechanoreceptors, but discharge also requires chemical stimulation: sugars and amino compounds from prey sensitize the cnidocytes, so that cnidae fire at contact with food but not at contact with a stone or with the animal's own tentacles. Clownfish, which live among the tentacles of sea anemones, are protected by a mucus that does not provoke discharge.

Nematocysts are among the most complex products of any single cell, and they are not easily replaced. A *Hydra* replaces discharged nematocysts continuously from i-cells that differentiate into nematocytes in the body column and migrate to the tentacles, where they are mounted in battery cells. Several sea slugs and the ctenophore *Haeckelia* eat cnidarians and store the undischarged nematocysts of their prey in their own tissues for defence.

### 9.2.2. The Physics of Discharge

A nematocyst capsule is a pressure vessel. During its development the capsule accumulates poly-γ-glutamate at a concentration of about 2 M together with its counterions, which gives an internal osmotic pressure of about 15 MPa (150 bar), and the minicollagen wall is strong enough to hold it. Triggering opens the capsule; water rushes in, and the stored elastic and osmotic energy drives discharge in two phases (Nüchter et al. 2006):

1. Stylet ejection (about 700 ns). In *Hydra* the elastic capsule wall contracts and expels the closed tip of the tubule, armed with a stylet of spines, at a speed of about 19 m s⁻¹ and an acceleration of up to $5.4 \times 10^6\,g$. The stylet hits the prey with a pressure estimated at 7 GPa, comparable to that of a bullet, which is enough to pierce the cuticle of a crustacean.
2. Tubule eversion (microseconds to milliseconds). The tubule, now inside the prey, turns itself inside out from the base, driven by the inflow of water, and delivers the venom from its open tip.

These numbers can be checked with simple mechanics. If the stylet reaches a speed $v$ from rest in a time $\tau$, its mean acceleration is

$$
\bar a = \frac{v}{\tau} = \frac{19\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}{700 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \approx 2.8 \times 10^{6}\,g,
$$

about half the peak value, as expected for a pulse that rises and falls. The osmotic pressure follows from the van 't Hoff relation $\Pi = c_{\mathrm{osm}} R T$: at $T = 293\,\mathrm{K}$, $15\,\mathrm{MPa}$ requires an osmolarity of $c_{\mathrm{osm}} = \Pi/RT \approx 6\,\mathrm{osmol\,L^{-1}}$, about twenty times that of seawater. The discharge of a nematocyst is among the fastest movements recorded in biology, and it is powered not by muscle but by a pre-loaded pressure vessel.

### 9.2.3. Venoms

Cnidarian venoms are mixtures of pore-forming proteins (actinoporins of sea anemones, the jellyfish toxins of box jellyfish), neurotoxic peptides that block sodium and potassium channels, and phospholipases. Most cnidarians are harmless to humans, but the Australian box jellyfish *Chironex fleckeri*, whose tentacles reach 3 m, can kill a person within minutes: its toxins form pores in cell membranes, causing a massive release of potassium that stops the heart. Irukandji syndrome, caused by small box jellyfish such as *Carukia barnesi*, produces severe pain and a dangerous surge of catecholamines. The Portuguese man-of-war *Physalia* and the fire corals *Millepora* are hydrozoans with painful stings. Sea anemone toxins are widely used in neurophysiology as tools to study ion channels, and one of them (ShK, from *Stichodactyla*) has led to drug candidates for autoimmune diseases.

## 9.3. Life Cycles

### 9.3.1. Metagenesis

When a cnidarian life cycle includes both a polyp and a medusa, they alternate: polyps produce medusae asexually by budding, and medusae produce the next generation of polyps sexually, via a larva.

**Definition 9.3.1 (Metagenesis)** **Metagenesis** is the alternation of an asexually reproducing generation with a sexually reproducing generation in a life cycle in which both generations are diploid; in cnidarians the polyp generation reproduces asexually and gives rise to medusae, and the medusa generation produces gametes.

Chinese textbooks call this "alternation of generations", the same term that is used for plants, but it is a different phenomenon. The **alternation of generations** of land plants and many algae alternates a haploid gametophyte with a diploid sporophyte and is tied to the alternation of meiosis and fertilization. In cnidarians both polyp and medusa are diploid; meiosis occurs only in the gonads of the medusa (or, in anthozoans, of the polyp), and the gametes are the only haploid cells. Metagenesis is an alternation of modes of reproduction, not of ploidy.

### 9.3.2. *Obelia*: A Hydrozoan Life Cycle

*Obelia* (Hydrozoa: Leptothecata) is a colonial hydroid common on seaweeds and pilings. The colony grows from a horizontal stolon (hydrorhiza) and branches (hydrocaulus), all covered by a chitinous perisarc and connected inside by a common tube of living tissue, the coenosarc, through which food is shared. Two kinds of polyps arise on the branches:

- feeding polyps (*hydranths* or gastrozooids), each with a mouth on a hypostome, a ring of solid tentacles, and a cup-shaped extension of the perisarc (*hydrotheca*) into which it can withdraw;
- reproductive polyps (*gonangia*), each a central stalk (blastostyle) without mouth or tentacles, enclosed in a vase-shaped gonotheca, on which medusae bud.

The small medusae swim out through the opening of the gonotheca. They are tetramerous, with four radial canals and four gonads, a velum and many marginal tentacles; males and females are separate and spawn into the water. The zygote develops into a ciliated **planula**, which settles, attaches by its aboral end and grows into the first polyp of a new colony. Asexual growth of the colony then produces hundreds of hydranths and gonangia.

### 9.3.3. *Aurelia*: A Scyphozoan Life Cycle

The moon jellyfish *Aurelia* has the reverse emphasis: the medusa, up to 40 cm across, is the conspicuous stage, and the polyp is small. Its life cycle runs as follows. The female broods the fertilized eggs on her oral arms until they hatch as planulae. A planula settles and becomes a small solitary polyp, the *scyphistoma*, 1–3 mm tall, with 16 tentacles. In autumn or spring, triggered by temperature, the scyphistoma divides transversely into a stack of discs (*strobilation*); the strobilating polyp is called a *strobila*. Each disc detaches as a tiny eight-armed medusa, the *ephyra*, which grows into the adult medusa over several months. A scyphistoma survives strobilation and can strobilate again, and it also buds new polyps, so one larva can produce many medusae.

![Two cnidarian life cycles. (A) *Obelia* (Hydrozoa): a branching colony of feeding polyps (hydranths) and reproductive polyps (gonangia) buds off small medusae with a velum; the dioecious medusae spawn, and the zygote develops into a planula that settles and grows into a new colony. (B) *Aurelia* (Scyphozoa): the large medusa is the dominant stage; planulae settle as small polyps (scyphistomae), which multiply by budding and strobilate, releasing ephyrae that grow into medusae. In both cycles the polyp reproduces asexually and the medusa sexually; both are diploid (metagenesis), and meiosis occurs only in the gonads.](./plots/cnidarian-life-cycles.py)

### 9.3.4. Variations

Many cnidarians have shortened their life cycles. Anthozoans have no medusa: the polyp reproduces both sexually and asexually (by fission, budding or pedal laceration). *Hydra* has lost the medusa, and its polyp forms gonads directly in the body wall. Many hydrozoans retain the medusa as a sessile, reduced gonophore that never leaves the colony; others, such as the trachyline hydromedusae and some scyphozoans (*Pelagia*), have lost the polyp, and the planula develops directly into a medusa. Cubozoan polyps transform entirely into a single medusa by metamorphosis, without strobilation. In the hydrozoan *Turritopsis dohrnii* a damaged or senescent medusa can settle and transform back into a polyp colony (reverse development), apparently indefinitely; it has been called the "immortal jellyfish".

## 9.4. Subphylum Anthozoa

**Definition 9.4.1 (Anthozoa)** The **Anthozoa** are cnidarians with no medusa stage, whose polyps have a tubular actinopharynx (stomodeum) lined by epidermis, a gastrovascular cavity partitioned by radial mesenteries bearing gastrodermal gonads, and usually one or more ciliated siphonoglyphs.

With about 7,200 species, anthozoans comprise sea anemones, corals, sea fans, sea pens and tube anemones. The anthozoan polyp is larger and more complex than the hydrozoan polyp. The mouth, a slit on the oral disc, leads into an actinopharynx, an ectodermal tube with one or two siphonoglyphs, ciliated grooves that drive water into the gut and keep the hydrostatic skeleton inflated even when the mouth is closed. The gastrovascular cavity is divided by *mesenteries*, radial sheets of gastrodermis and mesoglea that run from the body wall toward the pharynx; complete mesenteries reach the pharynx, incomplete ones do not. The free edges of the mesenteries carry the *mesenterial filaments*, which secrete enzymes and absorb food, and in many sea anemones end in threadlike acontia armed with nematocysts that can be extruded through the mouth or the body wall for defence. Mesenteries also bear strong longitudinal retractor muscles and the gonads, which in anthozoans, as in scyphozoans and cubozoans, are gastrodermal; in hydrozoans they are epidermal.

*Nematostella vectensis*, a small estuarine sea anemone, has become a model organism for the developmental biology and genomics of cnidarians. Its genome, the first cnidarian genome sequenced, showed that the gene content and even the order of many genes on chromosomes are remarkably similar to those of vertebrates, more so than in the fruit fly or the nematode (Putnam et al. 2007): the ancestor of cnidarians and bilaterians was genetically complex.

### 9.4.2. Corals and Coral Reefs

Reef-building corals are colonial scleractinians whose polyps sit in cup-shaped corallites of an aragonite skeleton. The skeleton is secreted beneath the basal epidermis (the calicodermis), which pumps calcium ions and removes protons to raise the saturation state of aragonite in a thin calcifying fluid. Radial ridges of the skeleton, the septa, lie between the mesenteries, and a colony grows as polyps bud and lay down new skeleton beneath them, at rates of millimetres to centimetres per year. Over thousands of years the skeletons, cemented by coralline red algae, build reefs; the Great Barrier Reef extends for 2,300 km.

Reef corals are mutualistic hosts of dinoflagellates of the family Symbiodiniaceae, the **zooxanthellae**, which live inside gastrodermal cells at densities of millions per square centimetre ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.5.4)). The algae transfer most of their photosynthetic products to the coral, mainly glycerol, glucose and lipids, and the coral provides inorganic nutrients and carbon dioxide; photosynthesis also raises the pH at the calcifying site and accelerates calcification. This partnership explains why reefs thrive in clear, nutrient-poor tropical waters: nutrients are recycled within the holobiont rather than taken from the water. It also restricts reef corals to the photic zone, usually above 50 m. Most corals spawn their gametes in synchronized mass spawnings, timed by water temperature and the lunar cycle, and their planulae acquire symbionts from the environment.

When water temperatures exceed the long-term summer maximum by about 1 °C for several weeks, the symbiosis breaks down: damaged algae produce reactive oxygen species, and the coral expels them. The coral loses its colour, revealing the white skeleton beneath the transparent tissue, which is **coral bleaching**. Bleached corals can recover if temperatures fall, but prolonged bleaching kills them. Global mass bleaching events (1998, 2010, 2014–2017, 2023–2025) have killed large parts of the world's reefs, and ocean acidification further reduces the saturation state of aragonite in seawater, which makes calcification more costly. Some corals tolerate heat better by hosting heat-tolerant symbionts (*Durusdinium*), a flexibility that may buy time but is unlikely to keep pace with warming.

Not all corals live in reefs. Deep-water scleractinians such as *Desmophyllum pertusum* (formerly *Lophelia*) form large mounds in cold, dark water without symbionts, feeding on plankton.

## 9.5. Subphylum Medusozoa

The Medusozoa (about 3,900 species) are united by a medusa stage, although it has been lost in some lineages, by a linear mitochondrial genome (anthozoans, like other animals, have a circular one) and the cnidocil. They comprise four classes. Hydrozoa is the sister group of the other three (Staurozoa, Cubozoa, Scyphozoa), which together are called Acraspeda.

### 9.5.1. Class Staurozoa

Stalked jellyfish, about 50 species of cold, shallow seas, are sessile medusae attached to seaweeds by a stalk on the aboral side. The bell has eight arms, each ending in a cluster of capitate tentacles. The planula is not ciliated and creeps; it grows into a small polyp, which transforms apically into the stauromedusa. Staurozoa were long treated as an order of Scyphozoa, but they are morphologically and molecularly distinct.

### 9.5.2. Class Cubozoa

**Definition 9.5.1 (Cubozoa)** The **Cubozoa** (box jellyfish) are medusozoans whose medusa has a nearly square bell, a tentacle or cluster of tentacles on a blade-like pedalium at each of the four corners, four rhopalia with complex eyes, and an inward fold of the bell margin (velarium) that narrows the subumbrellar opening; each polyp transforms entirely into one medusa.

Box jellyfish (about 50 species) are fast, agile swimmers of tropical and subtropical coasts, reaching speeds of several metres per minute and actively hunting fish and shrimps. The velarium, like the velum of hydromedusae, focuses the jet of water from the bell. Box jellyfish have elaborate courtship in some species, with males transferring a spermatophore to the female.

### 9.5.3. Class Scyphozoa

**Definition 9.5.2 (Scyphozoa)** The **Scyphozoa** (true jellyfish) are medusozoans with a large medusa without a velum, with rhopalia in marginal notches, gastric filaments in the stomach and gastrodermal partitions or a system of branched canals, and a small polyp (scyphistoma) that produces ephyrae by strobilation.

The scyphozoans (about 200 species) are the familiar large jellyfish. The bell is mostly mesoglea, more than 95% water, with a network of fibres that store elastic energy: the coronal muscle contracts the bell and the mesoglea springs it back. Large jellyfish swim with remarkable efficiency, the lowest cost of transport measured in any animal, by recapturing energy from the vortex shed in each stroke (Gemmell et al. 2013). The main orders are:

- Semaeostomeae, with four long frilled oral arms and marginal tentacles: *Aurelia*, *Cyanea* (the lion's mane, with a bell over 2 m and tentacles over 30 m long), *Chrysaora*, *Pelagia*.
- Rhizostomeae, without marginal tentacles; the oral arms are fused over the mouth, leaving thousands of small mouths on the arms (the order's name means "root mouth"). They include the edible jellyfish *Rhopilema esculentum*, harvested and cultured in China, and *Nemopilema nomurai*, whose medusae reach 2 m and 200 kg and periodically bloom in East Asian seas. The upside-down jellyfish *Cassiopea* lies on the bottom, oral arms up, and houses zooxanthellae.
- Coronatae, deep-sea jellyfish with a coronal groove around the bell.

Jellyfish blooms have increased in some coastal seas, apparently favoured by overfishing of competitors, eutrophication, warming and the multiplication of hard surfaces on which polyps settle. Blooms clog the cooling-water intakes of power plants and the nets of fishermen.

### 9.5.4. Class Hydrozoa

**Definition 9.5.3 (Hydrozoa)** The **Hydrozoa** are medusozoans whose medusae, when present, are small and have a velum and gonads on the subumbrella or manubrium, whose polyps lack mesenteries and an actinopharynx, and which often form colonies; the mesoglea is acellular.

With about 3,600 species, hydrozoans are the most diverse medusozoans and the only cnidarians that have entered fresh water in many lineages. Most are colonial hydroids with small medusae or reduced gonophores, but the class also includes *Hydra*, the fire corals, the stylasterid lace corals and the siphonophores.

**Definition 9.5.4 (Colony)** A **colony** is a group of genetically identical individuals (zooids) produced by asexual budding that remain physically connected and share tissues, so that food, signals or both pass between them; the zooids of a colony may be specialized for different functions.

Hydrozoan colonies show how far the division of labour among zooids can go. In the Leptothecata (*Obelia*, *Campanularia*) the perisarc forms cups around the hydranths; in the Anthoathecata (*Tubularia*, *Eudendrium*, *Hydractinia*) it does not. Many colonies have, besides feeding and reproductive polyps, defensive zooids (dactylozooids) armed with nematocysts.

The siphonophores carry division of labour to an extreme. A siphonophore colony develops from a single planula by budding of zooids along a stem and floats or swims in the open sea.

**Definition 9.5.5 (Siphonophore)** A **siphonophore** is a pelagic, colonial hydrozoan in which polypoid and medusoid zooids, specialized as floats (pneumatophores), swimming bells (nectophores), feeding polyps (gastrozooids), defensive and prey-capturing tentacles, protective bracts and reproductive zooids (gonophores), are budded in a fixed order along a stem and function together as an integrated individual.

The Portuguese man-of-war *Physalia physalis* has a gas-filled float up to 30 cm long that acts as a sail, and fishing tentacles many metres long; the deep-sea *Praya dubia* reaches 40 m and is among the longest animals. Siphonophores raise the question of what an individual is: each zooid is homologous to a free-living polyp or medusa, but none can live alone, and the colony behaves as one animal.

### 9.5.5. *Hydra*: The Representative Hydrozoan

*Hydra* is a solitary freshwater hydrozoan polyp, 2–20 mm long, that hangs from plants in ponds and has been a laboratory animal since Trembley's experiments of 1744. Its body has the following parts:

| Part | Structure and function |
|---|---|
| Hypostome | Conical oral end with the mouth; nerve ring around it |
| Tentacles | Usually 4–8, hollow, with batteries of nematocysts (four types: stenoteles, two kinds of isorhizas, desmonemes) |
| Body column | Epidermis and gastrodermis on a thin mesoglea; budding zone in its lower part |
| Peduncle and basal disc | Basal disc secretes mucus for attachment and can glide or release a gas bubble for floating |
| Gastrodermis | Digestive (epitheliomuscular) cells, gland cells; in green hydras (*Hydra viridissima*) symbiotic *Chlorella* algae |

*Hydra* lacks a medusa and a larva. It reproduces asexually by budding when food is abundant and sexually, often in autumn, by forming testes and ovaries as swellings of the body wall; the embryo encysts and overwinters. Its cells belong to three self-renewing lineages, ectodermal and endodermal epithelial cells and interstitial cells, which divide continuously in the column and are displaced into the tentacles and the basal disc, where they are shed. A *Hydra* thus renews all its cells in about three weeks and shows no sign of ageing in the laboratory. Its regeneration, including the organizer in the hypostome, the Wnt pathway and the activator–inhibitor model, is treated in [7.5.35](/7.5.35#sec-35.2).

Other freshwater hydrozoans include *Craspedacusta sowerbii*, a small limnomedusa of lakes and ponds, native to the Yangtze basin and now found on all continents except Antarctica.

## 9.6. Subphylum Endocnidozoa: Parasitic Cnidarians

**Definition 9.6.1 (Myxozoa)** The **Myxozoa** are microscopic, obligately parasitic cnidarians with a life cycle alternating between a fish (or other vertebrate) and an annelid or bryozoan host; their multicellular spores contain polar capsules, which are homologous to nematocysts and serve to attach the spore to the host.

Myxozoans (about 2,200 species) were long classified as protists ("Myxosporidia", among the sporozoans), because each consists of only a few cells; their spores, built of valve cells, polar capsules and an amoeboid infective sporoplasm, were the only clue to their multicellular nature. In 2015 a phylogenomic study placed them firmly within the Cnidaria as the sister group of *Polypodium* (Chang et al. 2015), and their polar capsules share with nematocysts the same structure and minicollagen genes.

The best-known myxozoan, *Myxobolus cerebralis*, causes whirling disease in salmonid fishes. Spores in the cartilage of an infected trout are released when the fish dies and are eaten by the oligochaete *Tubifex*, in whose gut they develop into a different, triradiate spore. These spores float in the water, attach to a fish by their polar capsules, inject the sporoplasm, and the parasite migrates to the cartilage of the head and spine, where it destroys tissue and causes the fish to swim in circles. Myxozoans have the smallest genomes of any animals (about 20 Mb) and have lost most genes for development and for the nervous system. *Henneguya salminicola* has even lost its mitochondrial genome and with it aerobic respiration (Yahalomi et al. 2020).

*Polypodium hydriforme*, the only species of its class, is a parasite of the eggs of sturgeons and paddlefishes. It lives inside the oocytes as a binucleate cell that grows into an inside-out stolon, with the gastrodermis outside and the epidermis inside, feeding on yolk. When the fish spawns, the stolon turns right side out and fragments into free-living, tentacled individuals that walk on the river bottom and infect new fish. It shows how far a cnidarian body can be modified by parasitism.

## 9.7. Phylogeny and Evolution

The relationships within the phylum are now well resolved. Anthozoa is the sister group of the rest, which comprise Endocnidozoa and Medusozoa; within Medusozoa, Hydrozoa is the sister group of Acraspeda, in which Staurozoa is the sister group of Cubozoa + Scyphozoa. This topology settles an old question. Classical zoology debated whether the first cnidarian was a medusa, with hydrozoans as the most primitive class and anthozoans as medusae that had lost the medusa, or a polyp, with anthozoans primitive and the medusa an addition. Because anthozoans, which lack a medusa, are the sister group of all other cnidarians, the most parsimonious interpretation is that the ancestral cnidarian was a polyp-like animal with a planula larva, and that the medusa evolved once, in the ancestor of Medusozoa, as a sexual stage budded from the polyp. The linear mitochondrial DNA of medusozoans is derived; anthozoans retain the circular mitochondrial genome of other animals.

Cnidarians have a long fossil record. Phosphatized embryos and polyps from the early Cambrian of southern China (about 535 million years ago) include medusozoans with tetraradial symmetry, some still enclosed in their egg membranes, and fossil medusae and scyphozoan-like polyps are known from the Cambrian onward. Many Ediacaran fossils, such as the frond-like *Charnia*, were once interpreted as sea pens, but most specialists no longer accept this. The stony corals appeared in the Middle Triassic; the Paleozoic reefs were built by two extinct orders, the rugose and tabulate corals, together with sponges.

The representative animals of the Chinese textbooks (*Hydra*, *Obelia*, *Aurelia*, sea anemones and corals) are all treated above, and their structures and life cycles are described in the same terms.

## Summary

- Cnidarians (Definition 9.1.1) are diploblastic animals with epidermis, gastrodermis and a mesoglea, a gastrovascular cavity with one opening, a nerve net and radial or biradial symmetry. Anthozoans have a directive axis.
- The polyp (Definition 9.1.2) is sessile and feeds and buds; the medusa (Definition 9.1.3) swims and reproduces sexually. Epitheliomuscular cells form longitudinal and circular muscle layers acting on a hydrostatic skeleton.
- Medusae bear statocysts and ocelli, grouped in rhopalia in scyphozoans and cubozoans; box jellyfish have 24 eyes, including camera eyes.
- Cnidocytes (Definition 9.2.1) produce cnidae (Definition 9.2.2): nematocysts, spirocysts, ptychocysts. A nematocyst stores an osmotic pressure of about 15 MPa and ejects its stylet in about 700 ns at accelerations up to $5 \times 10^6\,g$, then everts its tubule and injects venom.
- Metagenesis (Definition 9.3.1) alternates asexual polyps and sexual medusae, both diploid. *Obelia*: colonial hydroid with gonangia budding medusae; *Aurelia*: scyphistoma → strobila → ephyra → medusa.
- Anthozoa (Definition 9.4.1): no medusa; actinopharynx, siphonoglyphs, mesenteries; Octocorallia, Hexacorallia, Ceriantharia. Reef corals depend on zooxanthellae; heat stress causes bleaching.
- Medusozoa: Staurozoa (stalked jellyfish), Cubozoa (Definition 9.5.1), Scyphozoa (Definition 9.5.2), Hydrozoa (Definition 9.5.3), the last with colonies (Definition 9.5.4), siphonophores (Definition 9.5.5) and *Hydra*.
- Endocnidozoa: the Myxozoa (Definition 9.6.1), with polar capsules homologous to nematocysts and two-host life cycles, and *Polypodium*.
- Anthozoa is the sister group of the other cnidarians, so the ancestral cnidarian was polyp-like and the medusa evolved once in Medusozoa.

## Questions

1. A medusa and a polyp of the same hydrozoan species look completely different. In what sense are they the "same" body plan?

   **Answer** Both consist of an epidermis and a gastrodermis on either side of a mesoglea, around a gastrovascular cavity with a single mouth surrounded by tentacles, and both have radial symmetry about the oral–aboral axis. The medusa corresponds to a polyp flattened along its axis and turned mouth down, with a greatly thickened mesoglea and the gastrovascular cavity reduced to a stomach and canals; the tentacles of the polyp's oral disc correspond to the marginal tentacles of the bell. They are genetically identical stages of one life cycle that express different developmental programs.
2. Explain why the "alternation of generations" of *Obelia* is not the same phenomenon as the alternation of generations of a fern.

   **Answer** In a fern the sporophyte is diploid and produces haploid spores by meiosis; the spores grow into a haploid gametophyte, which produces gametes by mitosis; the two generations differ in ploidy, and the alternation is coupled to meiosis and fertilization. In *Obelia* both the polyp colony and the medusa are diploid. The polyps produce medusae asexually by budding; meiosis occurs only in the gonads of the medusa and produces gametes directly. The alternation is between asexual and sexual reproduction (metagenesis), not between haploid and diploid organisms.
3. Using the van 't Hoff relation, estimate the osmotic pressure in a nematocyst whose capsule contains $2\,\mathrm{M}$ poly-γ-glutamate residues with one monovalent counterion each, at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Why might the measured pressure be lower than this ideal estimate?

   **Answer** With $c_{\mathrm{osm}} \approx 4\,\mathrm{osmol\,L^{-1}} = 4000\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$, $\Pi = c_{\mathrm{osm}}RT \approx 4000 \times 8.314 \times 293 \approx 9.7\,\mathrm{MPa}$, about 100 bar; if the residues themselves also count, the estimate rises. The ideal relation overestimates the pressure of a concentrated polyelectrolyte, because many counterions are bound to the polymer (counterion condensation) and do not contribute fully to the osmotic activity, and because a polymer chain contributes far less than one osmole per residue. The order of magnitude, about $10\,\mathrm{MPa}$, agrees with measurements.
4. Why does the position of Anthozoa as the sister group of all other cnidarians suggest that the medusa evolved within Cnidaria?

   **Answer** Anthozoans have only polyps. If the medusa were ancestral, it would have had to be lost in Anthozoa and retained in Medusozoa (one loss); if the polyp alone were ancestral, the medusa would have arisen once in the ancestor of Medusozoa (one gain). The two are equally parsimonious when counting changes, but the medusa of all medusozoans develops from a polyp (by lateral budding, strobilation or metamorphosis) and shares derived features (linear mitochondrial DNA, cnidocil) absent from anthozoans; the planula always becomes a polyp first. Together these point to a polyp-like ancestor and a medusa added in Medusozoa.
5. Why are reef-building corals restricted to clear, shallow tropical waters, and why does warming threaten them even if the water never becomes too hot for the coral itself?

   **Answer** Most of their energy comes from photosynthesis by their symbiotic dinoflagellates, which needs light (hence clear water and the photic zone), and photosynthesis also raises the pH at the site of calcification; high temperature and aragonite saturation, found in tropical surface waters, favour rapid calcification. Warming threatens them through the symbiosis: a rise of about 1 °C above the usual summer maximum for several weeks damages the photosynthetic apparatus of the algae, which produce reactive oxygen species and are expelled. Without its symbionts the coral starves; the coral animal itself could survive the temperature, but not the loss of its partner.
