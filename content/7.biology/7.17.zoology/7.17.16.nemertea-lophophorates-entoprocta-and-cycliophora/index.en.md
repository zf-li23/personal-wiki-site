This chapter completes the Lophotrochozoa ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#def-6.5.3)) with five phyla that are small in species numbers but large in their lessons. The ribbon worms (Nemertea) are predators that capture prey with an eversible proboscis housed in a fluid-filled coelomic cavity. The three lophophorate phyla — phoronids, brachiopods and bryozoans — are sessile suspension feeders that filter water with a crown of hollow, ciliated tentacles, the lophophore; brachiopods were among the commonest animals of Palaeozoic seas and bryozoans are still among the commonest colonial animals of hard substrates. Two minute phyla, the Entoprocta and the Cycliophora, have a tentacle crown of a different kind, and the cycliophoran *Symbion*, discovered only in 1995 on the mouthparts of lobsters, has one of the most complicated life cycles known.

The Chinese textbooks treat nemerteans with the flatworms, as acoelomates "between flatworms and annelids", entoprocts among the pseudocoelomates, and the lophophorates as a transition between protostomes and deuterostomes. All three placements reflect the logic of grades: body cavity type and developmental features were read as steps on a single ladder ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#sec-3.6)). Molecular phylogenies place all five phyla in the Lophotrochozoa, together with molluscs ([7.17.13](/7.17.13), [7.17.14](/7.17.14)) and annelids ([7.17.15](/7.17.15)). We treat the nemerteans (§16.1), the lophophore (§16.2), the phoronids (§16.3), brachiopods (§16.4) and bryozoans (§16.5), the entoprocts (§16.6) and cycliophorans (§16.7), and then the relationships within the Lophotrochozoa (§16.8) and the comparison with the traditional classification (§16.9).

## 16.1. Phylum Nemertea

**Definition 16.1.1 (Nemertea)** The **Nemertea** (nemerteans, ribbon worms) are unsegmented, usually elongate and dorsoventrally flattened lophotrochozoan worms with a ciliated epidermis, a complete gut with a terminal anus, a closed circulatory system, protonephridia, and an eversible proboscis that lies above the gut in a fluid-filled, mesoderm-lined cavity, the rhynchocoel, and is everted by hydrostatic pressure to capture prey.

About 1,300 species are described, almost all marine, from the intertidal zone to the deep sea; a few live in fresh water (*Prostoma*), in moist soil and leaf litter on tropical islands (*Geonemertes*, *Argonemertes*), or as commensals in the mantle cavity of bivalves (*Malacobdella*) and among the eggs of crabs (*Carcinonemertes*). Most are a few centimetres long, but the bootlace worm *Lineus longissimus* of North Atlantic shores is reported to exceed 30 m in length (and claimed to reach 55 m) although only a few millimetres thick, which makes it a candidate for the longest animal known. Many are brightly coloured with stripes and bands.

### 16.1.1. Body Wall and Proboscis

The body wall consists of a ciliated, glandular epidermis on a thick dermis of connective tissue, and two or three layers of muscle. The order of the muscle layers distinguishes the main groups: palaeonemerteans have an outer circular and an inner longitudinal layer (often with an inner circular layer), heteronemerteans an outer longitudinal, a circular and an inner longitudinal layer, hoplonemerteans an outer circular and an inner longitudinal layer. The space between gut and body wall is filled with connective tissue and muscle (parenchyma), which is why nemerteans were long classified as acoelomates.

The proboscis is the defining organ of the phylum. It is a muscular, glandular tube that lies inverted in the *rhynchocoel*, a closed cavity lined by mesodermal epithelium, which is now accepted as a true coelom. When the muscles of the rhynchocoel wall contract, the pressure of the rhynchocoelic fluid everts the proboscis through the proboscis pore in a fraction of a second, often to a length greater than that of the body. The everted proboscis coils around prey, typically annelids and crustaceans, and holds it with adhesive and toxic secretions; in hoplonemerteans the middle of the proboscis carries a calcareous stylet on a basis, with reserve stylets in pouches beside it, which stabs the prey and introduces the venom. The retractor muscle draws the proboscis back. Nemertean toxins include tetrodotoxin, produced by associated bacteria and found in several species, and anabaseine, a pyridine alkaloid of hoplonemerteans that activates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and has been the starting point for drugs tested against cognitive decline.

In palaeonemerteans and heteronemerteans the proboscis pore and the mouth are separate, the mouth lying ventrally behind the brain; in most hoplonemerteans the proboscis opens into the foregut, so that mouth and proboscis share one opening.

### 16.1.2. Circulation, Excretion and Nervous System

Nemerteans have a **closed circulatory system** of two lateral vessels, often joined by a dorsal vessel and by connections at both ends, through which blood moves by contraction of the vessel walls and of the body. The vessels are lined by a mesodermal epithelium and, like the rhynchocoel, develop as cavities in the mesoderm; they are now interpreted as coelomic spaces. The blood is usually colourless, but some species have red cells with hemoglobin. The **protonephridia** lie close to the lateral vessels, often in the foregut region, and their flame cells may be pressed directly against the vessel wall, so that they filter the blood rather than the tissue fluid, a functional parallel to a nephron ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#def-4.6.1)).

The brain consists of four lobes, two dorsal and two ventral, forming a ring around the rhynchodaeum, from which two lateral nerve cords run backward. Cerebral organs, ciliated canals opening on the sides of the head and closely associated with the brain, are chemosensory and perhaps neurosecretory; there are also pigment-cup eyes, often many, and ciliated head slits or grooves.

Nemerteans can stretch to many times their contracted length without kinking or losing their round section. This is the case analysed by the Clark–Cowey model of helical fibre lattices ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.2.2)): their body volume is close to the maximum that the fibre lattice of their dermis can enclose, so they stay round over a wide range of fibre angles, whereas flatworms, with a volume far below the maximum, are flat at every length.

### 16.1.3. Reproduction and the Pilidium

Most nemerteans are gonochoristic, with numerous gonads arranged in a row between the lateral diverticula of the intestine, each opening by its own pore; the regular alternation of gut diverticula and gonads is a pseudometamerism, not segmentation ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#def-3.5.1)). Fertilization is usually external, in the water or in a mucous cocoon. Many species regenerate well, and *Lineus* can fragment into pieces each of which grows into a new worm.

Development follows three patterns. Palaeonemerteans and hoplonemerteans develop directly or through a ciliated larva that resembles a small adult. The heteronemerteans and the related Hubrechtiida, together called Pilidiophora, have the most remarkable larva of the phylum.

**Definition 16.1.2 (Pilidium)** The **pilidium** is the planktotrophic larva of pilidiophoran nemerteans: a helmet-shaped larva with an apical tuft and a band of cilia along two lateral lobes, with a blind gut, in which the juvenile worm is assembled from separate invaginations of the larval epidermis (imaginal discs) that surround the larval gut; at metamorphosis the juvenile breaks out of the larva and usually eats the larval body.

The pilidium is a primary larva in the sense of [7.17.5](/7.17.5#sec-5.4.4): almost none of its tissues contribute to the adult, and the juvenile body develops from rudiments set aside within it, like the imaginal discs of holometabolous insects ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)). Its metamorphosis, which takes minutes, is among the most drastic in the animal kingdom. Lecithotrophic variants occur: the Desor larva of *Lineus* develops inside the egg capsule.

|---|---|---|
| Palaeonemertea (monophyly uncertain) | Unarmed proboscis; nervous system in or just below the epidermis; mouth separate from proboscis pore; direct development | *Tubulanus*, *Cephalothrix* |
| Pilidiophora: Hubrechtiida and Heteronemertea | Unarmed proboscis; three muscle layers (heteronemerteans); pilidium larva | *Lineus*, *Cerebratulus*, *Baseodiscus* |

The Chinese textbooks place the nemerteans between flatworms and annelids, emphasizing their acoelomate body, flame cells and ciliated epidermis (flatworm-like) and their complete gut and blood vessels (annelid-like). Molecular phylogenies place them firmly in the Lophotrochozoa, far from flatworms; their closest relatives are uncertain, with some analyses grouping them with brachiopods and phoronids, others with annelids or molluscs. The recognition of the rhynchocoel and the vessels as coelomic cavities resolves the apparent paradox of an "acoelomate" with a circulatory system: nemerteans are coelomates whose coelom is specialized as a hydraulic organ for the proboscis and as blood vessels, while the body cavity of other lophotrochozoans has been filled with tissue.

## 16.2. The Lophophore and the Lophophorates

**Definition 16.2.1 (Lophophore)** A **lophophore** is a crown of ciliated, hollow tentacles that surrounds the mouth but not the anus, borne on a fold of the body wall and containing an extension of the coelom (the mesocoel) that runs into each tentacle; its ciliary bands drive a current of water through the crown and capture suspended food particles by the upstream-collecting mechanism.

**Definition 16.2.2 (Lophophorata)** The **Lophophorata** (lophophorates) are the clade of lophotrochozoans comprising the Phoronida, Brachiopoda and Bryozoa, which share a lophophore, a U-shaped gut with the anus outside the tentacle crown, and a body divided into a region with the lophophore (mesosome) and a trunk (metasome), each with its own coelomic compartment.

![U-shaped guts and tentacle crowns (schematic sagittal views, not to scale; mouth and tentacle crown up). Body wall grey, coelom green, gut yellow, hollow tentacles red with green lumen, shell or zooecium beige. (A) A phoronid in its chitinous tube: horseshoe-shaped lophophore, U-shaped gut, anus on a papilla outside the lophophore, spacious trunk coelom. (B) A bryozoan zooid with the polypide extended: the lophophore protrudes through the orifice of the zooecium on a tentacle sheath; the U-shaped gut ends with the anus outside the crown; the retractor muscle withdraws the polypide; the funiculus joins the stomach to the body wall. (C) A brachiopod in sagittal section, anterior to the left: dorsal (brachial) and ventral (pedicle) valves, the lophophore arms, seen here as coiled arms with their tentacles, fill the mantle cavity; the gut lies posteriorly and ends blindly in articulate brachiopods; the pedicle emerges posteriorly. (D) For contrast, an entoproct: the solid tentacles surround both mouth and anus, and the body cavity is filled with mesenchyme; the tentacle crown is not a lophophore](./plots/lophophorate-plans.py)

The lophophore is a feeding organ for life at low Reynolds number ([7.17.4](/7.17.4#sec-4.3.2)). In bryozoans and phoronids, water enters the crown from above, between the tips of the tentacles, and leaves between the tentacles at their sides, driven by the lateral cilia of each tentacle. Particles carried toward a tentacle are not sieved: the lateral cilia, which beat across the current, change their beat locally when a particle approaches, throwing it back toward the frontal surface of the tentacle (the side facing the centre of the crown), along which frontal cilia carry it to the mouth. Because particles are caught on the upstream side of the ciliary band that drives the current, this is called *upstream collecting*. Trochophore larvae, rotifers, entoprocts and the larvae and adults of many molluscs and annelids instead use *downstream collecting*: two opposed bands of compound cilia (prototroch and metatroch) catch particles between them on the downstream side of the main band. The two systems differ in their ciliary architecture and are one of the characters used to argue that the lophophore is not homologous with the tentacle crowns of entoprocts and cycliophorans.

**Example 16.2.1 (How Much Water Does a Lophophore Clear?)** A feeding bryozoan zooid with $n = 16$ tentacles $L = 0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ long drives water through the gaps between neighbouring tentacles. If each gap is about $w = 0.03\,\mathrm{mm}$ wide and the water passes through it at $u \approx 1\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$, the flow through the crown is

$$
Q = n L w u = 16 \times 0.5 \times 0.03 \times 1\,\mathrm{mm^3\,s^{-1}} \approx 0.24\,\mathrm{mm^3\,s^{-1}} \approx 0.9\,\mathrm{mL\,h^{-1}}.
$$
These are illustrative values; measured clearance rates of single zooids are of the same order, fractions of a millilitre per hour. A colony of $10^4$ zooids would therefore clear several litres an hour, comparable to a mussel, which is why bryozoan colonies are significant filters of coastal water. Because $Q$ is proportional to $n L$, the total length of the tentacles, and the volume of the zooid scales roughly with the cube of its size, large lophophorates need disproportionately long lophophores, which explains the spirally coiled lophophores of large phoronids and brachiopods.

:::

The traditional view that the lophophorate body is divided into three parts (protosome, mesosome and metasome), each with its own coelomic cavity like the bodies of hemichordates and echinoderms ([7.17.41](/7.17.41)), has been revised: the epistome, a flap over the mouth in phoronids and freshwater bryozoans, contains at most a small cavity that is not a separate coelom in most species, so the body is better described as two-part. Together with the fact that radial cleavage and enterocoely, once taken as deuterostome characters, occur only in some lophophorates, this removes most of the evidence that tied them to the deuterostomes (§16.8).

## 16.3. Phylum Phoronida

**Definition 16.3.1 (Phoronida)** The **Phoronida** (phoronids, horseshoe worms) are solitary, worm-shaped marine lophophorates living in secreted chitinous tubes, with a horseshoe-shaped or spirally coiled lophophore, a U-shaped gut, a trunk coelom divided by mesenteries, a closed circulatory system with hemoglobin in red blood cells, one pair of metanephridia that also serve as gonoducts, and usually an actinotroch larva.

Phoronids are the smallest phylum of lophophorates, with about 15 species in two genera, *Phoronis* and *Phoronopsis*. They are from a few millimetres to more than 20 cm long and live in tubes of chitin and mucus in sand and mud, encrusted with sand grains, or bored into rock and shells; some form dense mats, and *Phoronis australis* lives in the tube wall of cerianthid sea anemones. The lophophore is the only part normally extended from the tube. The trunk ends in a swollen ampulla that anchors the animal; the U-shaped gut runs down to a stomach in the ampulla and back, and the anus opens on a papilla just outside the lophophore, beside the paired nephridiopores. The blood circulates in two longitudinal vessels connected by a ring vessel at the base of the lophophore, with blind capillaries in the tentacles, and is propelled by contraction of the vessel walls; the blood cells contain hemoglobin. A nerve ring at the base of the lophophore gives off a giant fibre that coordinates rapid withdrawal into the tube.

Many species are hermaphrodites, others have separate sexes; gametes from the peritoneum around the stomach leave through the nephridia. Cleavage is radial. The planktotrophic *actinotroch* larva has a preoral hood, a collar of ciliated larval tentacles and a ring of cilia around the anus. After weeks in the plankton it undergoes a rapid metamorphosis in which a pouch of its ventral body wall, the metasomal sac, everts within seconds to form the adult trunk, and the gut is drawn into it to become U-shaped; the larval tentacles are lost and replaced by the adult lophophore. The U-shaped gut of the adult is thus the result of a dramatic ventral outgrowth of the body, so that mouth and anus come to lie close together at the top.

## 16.4. Phylum Brachiopoda

**Definition 16.4.1 (Brachiopoda)** The **Brachiopoda** (brachiopods, lamp shells) are solitary, benthic marine lophophorates enclosed in a bivalved shell whose valves are dorsal and ventral, secreted by two mantle lobes; the large lophophore occupies the mantle cavity in front of the body, which lies posteriorly; most are attached by a fleshy stalk, the pedicle, that emerges between or through the ventral valve, and the mantle edges bear chitinous chaetae.

About 400 living species are known, against more than 12,000 fossil species (some estimates reach 30,000): brachiopods are a phylum whose history is mostly in the past. They live from the intertidal zone to more than 5,000 m, mostly in cool water and on hard bottoms, in crevices, under ledges and in caves, attached by the pedicle; lingulids live in vertical burrows in sand and mud flats.

### 16.4.1. Shell, Mantle and Muscles

The two valves are not left and right, as in bivalves, but dorsal and ventral: the plane of bilateral symmetry passes through both valves, each of which is itself symmetrical, while in bivalves the plane passes between the valves, which are mirror images of each other. The similarity of the two shells is therefore a case of **convergent evolution** for the same function, protection of a sessile filter feeder.

(B) Transverse section of a bivalve: the valves are left and right, joined dorsally by the ligament and hinge, and the plane of bilateral symmetry of the body (dashed) runs between them, so that the two valves are mirror images. (C) Outside view of the ventral valve of a brachiopod: the valve is symmetrical about its midline (dashed), with the beak and pedicle opening at the posterior end. (D) Transverse section of a brachiopod: the valves are dorsal and ventral and differ in size and shape; the plane of symmetry passes through both valves](./plots/shell-symmetry.py)

The ventral valve is usually the larger, and in articulate brachiopods it carries at its posterior end a beak with an opening for the pedicle. The shell is secreted by the mantle lobes as in molluscs, with an outer organic periostracum and inner mineralized layers. Two kinds of mineral occur: calcium phosphate (apatite) interlayered with chitin and protein in the lingulids, and calcite in the other groups. Many calcitic shells are perforated by fine canals (punctae) containing extensions of the mantle (caeca), which store reserves and may deter boring organisms. The mantle edge bears chitinous chaetae, formed like those of annelids (§16.8), which act as a sensory grille that closes the shell when a shadow falls on it.

The valves are moved by muscles, not by a ligament. Adductor muscles close the shell; in articulate brachiopods diductor muscles, attached to a cardinal process of the dorsal valve behind the hinge axis, open it, working as a lever like the handles of a pair of scissors. Inarticulate brachiopods lack a hinge and use a more complex set of muscles to open, close and slide the valves against each other. The coelom extends into the mantle as branching mantle canals, through which coelomic fluid circulates under ciliary action; there is a small dorsal heart and an open blood system, and coelomocytes contain hemerythrin.

### 16.4.2. Lophophore, Gut and Reproduction

The lophophore of brachiopods is drawn out into two arms (brachia, hence the name) that are often coiled into spirals and, in terebratulids, supported by a calcareous loop, the brachidium, which projects from the dorsal valve. The cilia of the lophophore draw water into the shell from the sides and expel it in the front, a current that separates incoming from outgoing water as the mantle cavity of a bivalve does. The gut is U-shaped with an anus in the lingulids and craniids; in articulate brachiopods it ends blindly, and indigestible matter is expelled through the mouth as pellets. One or two pairs of metanephridia also serve as gonoducts.

The sexes are usually separate and fertilization external; some species brood their embryos in the mantle cavity or on the lophophore. Cleavage is radial, and the coelom is formed by enterocoely in some species and schizocoely in others. Lingulids have a planktotrophic larva that looks like a miniature adult with a shell and a feeding lophophore and gradually becomes heavier until it settles; articulate brachiopods have a short-lived, lecithotrophic, three-lobed larva that settles and metamorphoses within days, the mantle lobes turning back over the anterior lobe to form the valves.

### 16.4.4. Lingula and the Meaning of "Living Fossil"

*Lingula anatina*, the commonest brachiopod of the Chinese coast, lives in vertical burrows in intertidal mud flats, its long pedicle anchoring the lower end of the burrow and its shell at the surface; chaetae of the mantle edges form three funnels that separate the inhalant and exhalant currents. When disturbed, the pedicle contracts and pulls the animal down. It is collected for food in parts of southern China and Japan.

Lingulids are often called **living fossils**, because shells of lingulid shape and organophosphatic composition are known from the Cambrian and Ordovician, more than 480 million years ago. The phrase needs qualification. The living genus *Lingula* is known with certainty only from much younger rocks; Palaeozoic lingulids belong to other genera and families; and the genome of *L. anatina* (Luo et al. 2015) shows the normal amounts of change in gene sequences and an expansion of gene families involved in shell formation, with no sign that molecular evolution has been slower than in other animals. What has persisted is a body form and way of life, a burrowing suspension feeder in shallow, often brackish, mud flats, a habitat in which lingulids survived several mass extinctions. "Living fossil" describes the conservatism of a phenotype, not an arrest of evolution, as with the monoplacophorans ([7.17.13](/7.17.13#sec-13.5)).

### 16.4.5. Brachiopods in the History of Life

Brachiopods appear in the Early Cambrian, and their Cambrian relatives, the tommotiids and the shell-bearing *Halkieria*-like animals, document how a bivalved shell may have formed from a coat of sclerites. In the Palaeozoic, articulate brachiopods were the dominant shelled animals of the sea floor: thousands of genera, from tiny forms to spiriferids with wing-like shells and productids anchored in mud by spines. Their abundance and rapid evolution make them important index fossils of Palaeozoic rocks. The end-Permian **mass extinction**, about 252 million years ago, eliminated roughly 90% of brachiopod genera; afterwards bivalves became the dominant suspension feeders of most marine bottoms ([7.17.14](/7.17.14#sec-14.9)).

Whether bivalves outcompeted brachiopods or simply recovered faster from the extinction is a classic question of palaeobiology. Gould and Calloway (1980) showed that the two groups' diversity curves were not reciprocal over the Palaeozoic, as competitive replacement would predict, but changed abruptly at the Permian–Triassic boundary, and called them "clams and brachiopods, ships that pass in the night". Later analyses suggest that both biological differences and the extinction played a role: brachiopods have lower metabolic rates and less tissue per shell, which made them less vulnerable in times of food shortage but less able to resist the Mesozoic increase in predation and bioturbation.

## 16.5. Phylum Bryozoa

**Definition 16.5.1 (Bryozoa)** The **Bryozoa** (bryozoans, moss animals; also called Ectoprocta) are lophophorates that live in **colonies** of small, physically connected modules, the zooids, formed by asexual budding from a founding individual; each feeding zooid has a body wall (cystid) that usually secretes an exoskeleton, and a polypide comprising a circular or horseshoe-shaped lophophore, a U-shaped gut with the anus outside the lophophore, muscles and a ganglion, which can be protruded through an orifice and withdrawn; there are no circulatory or excretory organs.

**Definition 16.5.2 (Zooid)** A **zooid** is one of the individual modules of a colonial animal, produced by budding and remaining physically connected to the others; zooids may be all alike or differentiated for different functions (polymorphism).

About 6,000 living species are known and some 15,000 fossil species. Bryozoans are almost all marine, with about 90 species in fresh water. Their zooids are usually less than 1 mm long, but the colonies, from a few zooids to several million, form crusts on rocks, shells and seaweeds, bushy tufts, lace-like sheets and massive heads up to a metre across.

### 16.5.1. The Zooid

The cystid secretes the zooecium, an exoskeleton of chitin and, in most marine species, calcium carbonate, often with a lid (operculum) closing the orifice. The polypide is withdrawn by a large retractor muscle and protruded by hydrostatic pressure. Because the zooid is enclosed in a rigid box, the different groups have evolved different ways of raising the pressure in the coelom: in freshwater phylactolaemates the muscular body wall contracts; in the cheilostomes a flexible frontal membrane is pulled inward by parietal muscles, or water is drawn into a compensation sac (ascus) under a calcified frontal shield; in the cyclostomes a membranous sac surrounding the polypide is compressed. The protruded lophophore is carried on the tentacle sheath, an introvert that becomes the lining of the cavity into which the polypide is withdrawn.

The gut is U-shaped, with a ciliated pharynx, an oesophagus, a stomach with a caecum and an intestine opening by an anus just outside the lophophore on the tentacle sheath. A cord of tissue, the funiculus, connects the stomach with the body wall and, through pores in the zooid walls, with neighbouring zooids, carrying nutrients through the colony. The zooids have no blood vessels, gills or nephridia: gases diffuse through the thin tentacles and body wall, and wastes are accumulated in the cells of the stomach. Periodically the whole polypide degenerates into a compact *brown body*, a new polypide regenerates from the body wall, and the brown body is either expelled or retained in the cystid. A small ganglion between mouth and anus gives off nerves to the tentacles and the body. The mineralized bryozoan skeleton has a long and rich fossil record, and the degeneration–regeneration cycle is one of the few cases of recurrent organ renewal in an adult animal.

### 16.5.2. Colonies: Growth and Polymorphism

A colony begins with a single zooid, the ancestrula, formed by metamorphosis of a settled larva, and grows by budding. In encrusting species budding takes place only at the margin of the colony, so the colony's growth is limited by its perimeter, not by its total number of zooids. If the margin advances at a constant radial speed $v$, the area of a circular colony grows as $\pi (vt)^2$ and the number of zooids of area $a$ as

$$
N(t) \approx \frac{\pi v^2 t^2}{a},
\tag{16.1}
$$
a quadratic, not an exponential law. The figure shows this in a simple model of marginal budding.

Because the colony is a clone of connected individuals, it can divide labour among them. Marine bryozoans, especially cheilostomes, have several kinds of non-feeding zooids, the heterozooids, nourished through the funiculus by the feeding autozooids:

| Zooid | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Autozooid | Complete polypide with lophophore and gut | Feeding |
| Avicularium | Reduced or no polypide; the operculum enlarged into a mandible moved by strong muscles; in *Bugula* a stalked "bird's head" that snaps continuously | Defence against small animals and settling larvae |
| Vibraculum | The mandible drawn out into a long seta that sweeps over the colony | Cleaning; in free-living colonies, locomotion |
| Ovicell (ooecium) | A hood-like chamber formed by a zooid or its neighbour | Brooding of embryos |
| Kenozooid | Zooid reduced to a body wall | Spines, stolons, rootlets, attachment and support |
| Gonozooid | Enlarged zooid in cyclostomes | Brooding and polyembryony |

Polymorphism makes the bryozoan colony comparable to the colonies of hydrozoans and siphonophores ([7.17.9](/7.17.9)) and to the societies of social insects ([7.17.38](/7.17.38)): in each case the unit of selection and of function shifts toward the colony.

### 16.5.3. Reproduction and Larvae

Most bryozoans are hermaphrodites, with zooids that are male first and female later or colonies with separate male and female zooids. Sperm are released through pores at the tips of some tentacles and caught by the lophophores of other colonies; eggs are fertilized in the coelom and released through a pore between the tentacles, or brooded. Most marine bryozoans brood their embryos in ovicells or internal sacs and release short-lived, non-feeding (lecithotrophic) coronate larvae, which swim for hours and settle. A few, such as *Membranipora* and *Electra*, release small eggs that develop into the planktotrophic *cyphonautes* larva, triangular and laterally compressed, with a bivalved chitinous shell and a functional gut, which feeds for weeks in the plankton. In cyclostomes a single embryo divides repeatedly into many genetically identical secondary embryos (polyembryony), so that one fertilized egg gives rise to dozens of larvae.

Freshwater bryozoans produce *statoblasts*, asexual resting bodies formed on the funiculus: a mass of cells rich in yolk enclosed in two chitinous valves, often with a ring of air cells (floatoblasts) or hooked spines. Released when the colony dies in autumn, they survive freezing and drying, are dispersed by water and by birds, and germinate in spring into a new zooid. Statoblasts are to freshwater bryozoans what gemmules are to freshwater sponges ([7.17.7](/7.17.7)): a convergent solution to life in seasonal fresh waters. The freshwater *Plumatella* is widespread in Chinese lakes and reservoirs, where its colonies foul water intakes; *Cristatella* forms gelatinous colonies that creep slowly over plants, and *Pectinatella magnifica* forms gelatinous masses the size of a football.

### 16.5.4. Classification and Importance

| Class | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Phylactolaemata | Freshwater; horseshoe-shaped lophophore with an epistome; muscular, uncalcified body wall; coelom continuous between zooids; no polymorphism; statoblasts | *Plumatella*, *Fredericella*, *Cristatella*, *Pectinatella* |
| Stenolaemata | Marine; tubular, calcified zooids with a terminal orifice; membranous sac for protrusion; polyembryony; mostly extinct (the Palaeozoic trepostomes and fenestellids), today only the Cyclostomatida | *Crisia*, *Tubulipora* |
| Gymnolaemata: Ctenostomata | Marine and brackish; uncalcified zooids; orifice closed by a pleated collar | *Alcyonidium*, *Bowerbankia* |
| Gymnolaemata: Cheilostomata | Marine; box-shaped, calcified zooids with an operculum; extensive polymorphism; the dominant living group, radiating since the Cretaceous | *Bugula*, *Membranipora*, *Electra*, *Schizoporella*, *Flustra*, *Cupuladria* |

The earliest unquestioned bryozoans are from the Early Ordovician; a claimed Cambrian bryozoan from South China (*Protomelission*, 2021) has been reinterpreted by other authors as a different organism, so the phylum's origin remains uncertain. Bryozoans are among the main fouling organisms of ships, buoys and aquaculture cages, and introduced species such as *Watersipora* and *Bugula neritina* have spread along shipping routes. *Bugula neritina* harbours a bacterial symbiont that produces the bryostatins, defensive compounds of its larvae that modulate protein kinase C and have been tested as drugs against cancer and Alzheimer's disease.

## 16.6. Phylum Entoprocta

**Definition 16.6.1 (Entoprocta)** The **Entoprocta** (entoprocts, also called Kamptozoa) are minute, sessile, solitary or colonial animals with a cup-shaped body, the calyx, on a stalk; the calyx bears a crown of ciliated, solid tentacles that encloses both the mouth and the anus of a U-shaped gut, and the space between the body wall and the gut is filled with mesenchyme; they have a pair of protonephridia and feed by downstream-collecting ciliary bands.

About 180 species are known, all less than 5 mm long and almost all marine; *Urnatella gracilis* lives in fresh water. Solitary loxosomatids live attached to the body of polychaetes, sipunculans, bryozoans and sponges, often near the host's feeding current; colonial pedicellinids and barentsiids form stolons from which zooids arise. The tentacles roll inward to cover the calyx when the animal is disturbed, and the stalk, with its muscles, makes the characteristic nodding movements that give the group its alternative name (from the Greek for "bending"). Asexual budding is common; sexual reproduction leads, through spiral cleavage, to a ciliated larva that resembles a trochophore.

Liu and Zheng and Xu and Cheng both classify entoprocts with the pseudocoelomates. The body cavity, however, is not an open pseudocoel but a space filled with mesenchyme, and the "pseudocoelomates" are themselves a grade, not a clade ([7.17.12](/7.17.12#sec-12.7)). Entoprocts were once united with ectoprocts as Bryozoa; the different position of the anus, the solid tentacles, the absence of a coelom and the downstream feeding mechanism led to their separation, and molecular analyses place them with the cycliophorans.

## 16.7. Phylum Cycliophora

**Definition 16.7.1 (Cycliophora)** The **Cycliophora** (cycliophorans) are microscopic (less than 0.5 mm) marine animals that live attached to the mouthparts of lobsters; their sessile feeding stage has a bell-shaped buccal funnel with a ring of compound cilia, a U-shaped gut and an attachment disc, replaces its feeding apparatus by internal budding, and produces several kinds of free-swimming larvae in alternating asexual and sexual phases.

*Symbion pandora*, described by Funch and Kristensen in 1995 from the Norway lobster *Nephrops norvegicus*, was the first animal discovered in decades that required a new phylum; *S. americanus* lives on the American lobster. Its life cycle has the following stages:

1. The *feeding stage*, attached by a disc to the setae of the lobster's mouthparts, filters food particles from the scraps of the lobster's meals with its ciliated buccal funnel. Inside it, new buccal funnels with guts are budded to replace the old ones, which degenerate.
2. In the asexual phase, the feeding stage buds internally a *Pandora larva*, which leaves, settles on the same host and grows into a new feeding stage; populations on a host increase in this way.
3. In the sexual phase, which coincides with the approach of the host's moult, feeding stages produce either a *Prometheus larva*, which settles on another feeding stage and produces inside itself one to three tiny dwarf males, or a female. The dwarf male, about 30 µm long and with very few cells, fertilizes the female, which leaves its feeding stage, settles and produces a single *chordoid larva*.
4. The chordoid larva, the dispersal stage, swims to a new host and settles on its mouthparts, where it develops into a new feeding stage.

Because the lobster's moult removes all the cycliophorans from the old mouthparts, the sexual phase and the chordoid larva serve to reach a new host or the new cuticle; the asexual phase multiplies the population while the cuticle lasts. Cycliophorans are related to the entoprocts, with which they share features of the larvae and of the nervous system, a relationship confirmed by molecular data.

## 16.8. Relationships within the Lophotrochozoa

**Lophotrochozoa** was named by Halanych and colleagues (1995) for a clade revealed by ribosomal RNA, uniting the lophophorates with the "trochozoans", the phyla with trochophore larvae (annelids, molluscs, nemerteans, entoprocts). The name records the surprise of the discovery: the lophophorates, which the textbooks of the time placed near the deuterostomes, grouped with the spiralian protostomes. Later evidence confirmed it. Brachiopods and phoronids have the lophotrochozoan Hox genes *Lox5*, *Lox4* and *Lox2*, with signature peptides found only in this clade (de Rosa et al. 1999); brachiopod chaetae are formed like annelid chaetae; and in some brachiopods the blastopore becomes the mouth, as in protostomes. The deuterostome-like traits of some lophophorates, radial cleavage and enterocoely, are better interpreted as convergences or as variations within the protostomes.

Within the Lophotrochozoa, a few groupings are now well supported and others are disputed:

| Grouping | Members | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Lophophorata | Phoronida, Brachiopoda, Bryozoa | Supported by most recent phylogenomic analyses (e.g. Laumer et al. 2019); phoronids and brachiopods are sister groups (Brachiozoa) |
| Polyzoa | Bryozoa, Entoprocta, Cycliophora | Recovered in some early analyses, probably because of long-branch artefacts; now mostly rejected in favour of Lophophorata |
| Entoprocta + Cycliophora | Entoprocta, Cycliophora | Well supported |
| Kryptrochozoa | Brachiopoda (with phoronids), Nemertea | Recovered in some analyses; not stable |
| Tetraneuralia | Entoprocta, Mollusca | A morphological hypothesis based on the tetraneurous nervous system of entoproct larvae and molluscs; not supported by most molecular analyses |

The trochophore and the lophophore represent two different solutions to ciliary suspension feeding, downstream and upstream collecting, and their distribution on the tree suggests that the common ancestor of the Lophotrochozoa had a larva with downstream-collecting bands and that the lophophore evolved once in the ancestor of the Lophophorata. The relationships among the major lophotrochozoan phyla — molluscs, annelids, nemerteans, lophophorates, and the entoproct–cycliophoran pair — remain one of the least resolved parts of the animal tree ([7.17.6](/7.17.6#sec-6.5.6)).

The species numbers in the Chinese textbooks need updating: about 1,300 nemerteans (not 500–600), about 6,000 living bryozoans and about 400 living brachiopods. The representative and named species of the Chinese textbooks (*Lineus*, the phoronids, *Lingula*, *Terebratula* and its relatives, *Bugula*, *Plumatella*, *Fredericella*, *Cristatella*, *Schizoporella*, *Membranipora*, *Electra*, *Flustra*, *Cupuladria*, *Crisia*, *Urnatella* and *Symbion*) are treated in the sections above.

## Summary

- Nemerteans (Definition 16.1.1) are unsegmented lophotrochozoan worms with a complete gut, closed circulation, protonephridia next to the blood vessels and an eversible proboscis in a coelomic rhynchocoel, armed with stylets in hoplonemerteans. Their shape changes follow the Clark–Cowey lattice. Pilidiophorans have a pilidium larva (Definition 16.1.2), in which the juvenile is assembled from imaginal discs.
- The lophophore (Definition 16.2.1) is a crown of hollow, ciliated tentacles around the mouth but not the anus, feeding by upstream collecting; the Lophophorata (Definition 16.2.2) are phoronids, brachiopods and bryozoans. A lophophore's clearance rate scales with total tentacle length (Example 16.2.1).
- Phoronids (Definition 16.3.1) are tube-dwelling worms with a horseshoe lophophore, closed circulation with hemoglobin and an actinotroch larva whose metasomal sac everts at metamorphosis.
- Brachiopods (Definition 16.4.1) have dorsal and ventral valves (convergent with bivalves), a pedicle, a large lophophore supported in terebratulids by a brachidium, muscle-operated valves, mantle canals and hemerythrin. Linguliformea (phosphatic shells), Craniiformea and Rhynchonelliformea (articulates). Lingulids are living fossils in body form, not in molecular evolution; brachiopods dominated the Palaeozoic and lost about 90% of genera in the end-Permian extinction.
- Bryozoans (Definition 16.5.1) are colonies of zooids (Definition 16.5.2) with a cystid and a protrusible polypide, no circulatory or excretory organs, polypide recycling into brown bodies, marginal colony growth ($N \propto t^2$, Eq. (16.1)), polymorphism (avicularia, vibracula, ovicells, kenozooids), cyphonautes and coronate larvae, and statoblasts in fresh water; classes Phylactolaemata, Stenolaemata and Gymnolaemata.
- Entoprocts (Definition 16.6.1) have solid tentacles enclosing both mouth and anus, a mesenchyme-filled body and downstream feeding; cycliophorans (Definition 16.7.1) live on lobster mouthparts and alternate asexual Pandora larvae with a sexual cycle of Prometheus larvae, dwarf males, females and chordoid larvae. The two are sister groups.
- Lophotrochozoa unites the lophophorates with the trochozoans; Lophophorata and Entoprocta + Cycliophora are supported, Polyzoa and Tetraneuralia are not; the lophophorates are protostomes, not intermediates between protostomes and deuterostomes.

## Questions

1. How does a nemertean evert its proboscis, and why is the rhynchocoel considered a coelom?

   **Answer** The proboscis lies inverted in the rhynchocoel, a closed, fluid-filled cavity. When the muscles of the rhynchocoel wall contract, the pressure of the incompressible fluid rises and pushes the proboscis out through the proboscis pore, turning it inside out like the finger of a glove; the retractor muscle, attached to the rear of the proboscis and the rhynchocoel wall, pulls it back. The rhynchocoel arises as a cavity within the mesoderm and is lined by a mesodermal epithelium, which is the definition of a coelom ([7.17.3](/7.17.3#def-3.4.2)); the blood vessels of nemerteans develop in the same way. Nemerteans are therefore coelomates whose coelom serves as a hydraulic organ and as a circulatory system rather than as a general body cavity.
2. Compare the lophophore with the tentacle crown of an entoproct. Why are they not considered homologous?

   **Answer** Both are crowns of ciliated tentacles used for suspension feeding. The lophophore has hollow tentacles containing an extension of the coelom, surrounds only the mouth (the anus lies outside it) and feeds by upstream collecting: lateral cilia drive the current and particles are caught on the upstream side by local reversal of the ciliary beat. The entoproct crown has solid tentacles without coelom, encloses both mouth and anus, and feeds by downstream collecting with compound cilia, as trochophore larvae do. The differences in structure, in the relation to the anus and in the ciliary mechanism, together with molecular phylogenies that place entoprocts with cycliophorans rather than with bryozoans, indicate that the two crowns evolved independently.
3. Explain the statement that "brachiopods and bivalves have different planes of symmetry". What does this imply about the similarity of their shells?

   **Answer** A bivalve's valves are left and right: the plane of bilateral symmetry of the body passes between them, so the two valves are mirror images, and each valve is usually asymmetrical from front to back. A brachiopod's valves are dorsal and ventral: the plane of symmetry passes through both valves, each valve is symmetrical about its midline, and the two valves usually differ in size and shape. The two shells therefore cover the body from different sides and are not homologous; their overall similarity, a bivalved protective case for a sessile suspension feeder, is convergent. Other differences agree: brachiopod valves are opened by muscles, bivalve valves by an elastic ligament, and the brachiopod mantle cavity is filled by a lophophore rather than by ctenidia.
4. An encrusting bryozoan colony grows by budding only at its margin. Derive how the number of zooids increases with time, and explain why this differs from the growth of a population of free-living clones.

   **Answer** If the margin of a circular colony advances at constant radial speed $v$, the colony radius is $vt$ and its area $\pi v^2 t^2$; with each zooid occupying area $a$, the number of zooids is $N \approx \pi v^2 t^2/a$, a quadratic function of time (Eq. (16.1)). In a population of free-living clones every individual can reproduce, so the number grows exponentially, $N = N_0 \mathrm{e}^{rt}$. In the colony only zooids at the margin have free space to bud into; interior zooids are surrounded by neighbours. Growth is therefore limited by the perimeter, which grows linearly with time. Erect, branching colonies escape this limit partly, because every branch tip is a growing margin.
5. Is *Lingula* a living fossil? Discuss what the term can and cannot mean.

   **Answer** *Lingula* is called a living fossil because lingulid brachiopods with the same organophosphatic shell, body form and burrowing habit have existed since the Cambrian–Ordovician. But the genus *Lingula* itself is known with certainty only from much younger rocks; the Palaeozoic forms belong to other genera, and the genome of *L. anatina* shows ordinary rates of molecular evolution and expansions of gene families. The term can legitimately describe the long persistence of a body plan and way of life (morphological stasis in a stable habitat, perhaps combined with resistance to extinction), but it cannot mean that evolution has stopped or that the living species is identical to its ancient relatives.
6. Outline the life cycle of *Symbion pandora*. How is it adapted to life on the mouthparts of a lobster?

   **Answer** The sessile feeding stage, attached to the setae of the lobster's mouthparts, feeds on food particles from the lobster's meals and regularly replaces its buccal funnel and gut by internal budding. In the asexual phase it produces Pandora larvae that settle on the same host and form new feeding stages, multiplying the population. Near the host's moult, feeding stages produce Prometheus larvae, which settle on feeding stages and develop dwarf males, and females, which are fertilized, leave and settle to produce chordoid larvae. Chordoid larvae swim away and colonize new hosts or the new mouthparts after the moult. The cycle combines rapid asexual multiplication on a temporary substrate, the old cuticle, with a sexual, dispersing stage timed to the moult, which would otherwise destroy the whole population.
