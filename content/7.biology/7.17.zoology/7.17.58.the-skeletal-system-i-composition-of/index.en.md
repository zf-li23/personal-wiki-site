The skull is the most complex part of the vertebrate skeleton. It protects the brain and the paired sense organs, frames the mouth and pharynx, carries the teeth and the jaw muscles, and in fishes supports the gills. A human skull has 22 bones (29 with the ear ossicles and the hyoid), a dog's about 50, the skull of a carp more than 130. The number, names and shapes of these bones are daunting if they are memorized one by one, but they become intelligible once two principles are understood.

The first principle is that the skull is a composite of three parts with different embryonic and evolutionary origins: a braincase of cartilage, the chondrocranium; the skeleton of the pharyngeal arches, the splanchnocranium; and a covering of dermal bones derived from the armour of early fishes, the dermatocranium. The second principle is that the dermal bones are arranged in a few series that occupy constant positions around the openings of the skull (the nostrils, the orbits, the temporal region, the mouth), so that each bone can be identified by its position and its neighbours ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2.3)). This chapter explains both principles and follows each element of the pharyngeal arches through the vertebrates; [7.17.59](/7.17.59) then follows the skull as a whole from fishes to mammals.

The chapter first defines the three components and their origins (§58.1), then treats the chondrocranium (§58.2) and the splanchnocranium with the fate of each arch (§58.3). The origin of jaws, introduced in [7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.2), is reviewed from the point of view of the skull (§58.4), followed by the ways in which the jaws are attached to the braincase (§58.5). Section 58.6 sets out the dermatocranium series by series, §58.7 the hyoid apparatus, and §58.8 compares the composition of the skull across the vertebrate classes. The bones are drawn in the course-wide homology colours: the palatoquadrate, quadrate and incus gold, Meckel's cartilage, articular and malleus coral, the hyomandibula, columella and stapes magenta, and the dermal bones in the colours of their series.

## 58.1. Three Components

### 58.1.1. Definitions

**Definition 58.1.1 (Components of the Skull)** The vertebrate skull is composed of three parts:

- the **chondrocranium** (neurocranium in the narrow sense), the cartilaginous braincase that develops around the brain and encloses the nasal, optic and otic capsules, and the cartilage bones that replace it;
- the **splanchnocranium** (visceral skeleton), the skeleton of the pharyngeal arches, comprising the jaws, the hyoid arch and the branchial arches, in cartilage or cartilage bone;
- the **dermatocranium**, the dermal bones that cover the chondrocranium and the splanchnocranium from outside and line the roof of the mouth.

The terms are not always used in the same way. *Neurocranium* is often used for the whole braincase, including the dermal bones that roof it, while *chondrocranium* is kept for its cartilaginous part; *cranium* may mean the braincase alone or the whole skull without the lower jaw; and the *skull* in comparative anatomy usually includes the lower jaw and the hyoid. In this course the three components of Definition 58.1.1 are used whenever the origin of a bone matters.

### 58.1.2. Origins

The three components differ in their embryonic sources ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.7)) and in their mode of ossification ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.6.2)).

| Component | Embryonic source | Tissue | First appearance | Main derivatives in mammals |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chondrocranium | Anterior part (trabeculae, nasal capsules): cranial neural crest; posterior part (parachordals, much of the otic capsule, occipital arch): head mesoderm and the first somites | Cartilage, replaced by cartilage bone | Cartilaginous braincase in all vertebrates | Occipital (except interparietal), sphenoid, ethmoid with turbinals, petrous part of the temporal |
| Splanchnocranium | Cranial neural crest (most of it); the posterior laryngeal cartilages in mammals partly mesoderm | Cartilage, replaced by cartilage bone | Pharyngeal arch skeleton of all vertebrates | Malleus, incus, stapes, styloid process, hyoid, laryngeal cartilages; the alisphenoid |
| Dermatocranium | Neural crest (face and front of the skull) and mesoderm (back of the skull roof) | Dermal bone | Armour of ostracoderms | Nasal, frontal, parietal, interparietal, premaxilla, maxilla, lacrimal, jugal, squamosal, tympanic, vomer, palatine, pterygoid (part), dentary |

The relative contribution of the three components changes through evolution. In cyclostomes and chondrichthyans the skull consists of the chondrocranium and splanchnocranium in cartilage, and the dermatocranium is absent (cyclostomes) or reduced to scales and teeth (chondrichthyans). In bony fishes all three components are present and both cartilage and dermal elements are numerous. In tetrapods the splanchnocranium is reduced to the jaw joint, the ear ossicles and the hyoid, and in amniotes and especially in mammals the dermatocranium forms most of the braincase and the whole of the functional jaws.

## 58.2. The Chondrocranium

### 58.2.1. Development

The chondrocranium forms in all vertebrate embryos from a few paired cartilages that appear below and around the brain and then fuse (after de Beer 1937):

1. The *parachordal cartilages* develop on either side of the anterior end of the notochord and fuse to form the *basal plate*, the floor of the hindbrain region.
2. The *trabeculae cranii* (prechordal cartilages) develop in front of the notochord, below the forebrain, and fuse anteriorly into the *ethmoid plate*, which supports the nasal capsules. Between the trabeculae and the basal plate the *hypophyseal fenestra* leaves an opening for the pituitary.
3. Three pairs of capsules form around the sense organs: the *nasal (olfactory) capsules* around the olfactory organs, the *optic capsules* around the eyes, and the *otic capsules* around the inner ears. The otic capsules fuse with the basal plate; the nasal capsules with the ethmoid plate. The optic capsules remain separate, so that the eyes can move, and form the sclera, which in many fishes, reptiles and birds contains cartilage (sclerotic cartilage) and, in birds and many reptiles, a ring of dermal sclerotic ossicles.
4. The side walls are completed by the *orbital cartilages* (and in many vertebrates the *pila antotica* and other bars between the nerve foramina) and the back by the *occipital arch*, which develops from the sclerotomes of the first somites and encloses the foramen magnum; the occipital region of the skull is thus derived from vertebra-like segments, the one part of the vertebral theory of the skull that was correct ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.1.3)). The roof is formed only at the back, by the *synotic tectum* between the otic capsules; the rest of the roof remains open (as fontanelles) and is closed by dermal bones.

The chondrocranium thus forms a trough beneath the brain with walls at the sides and a roof only at the back. In chondrichthyans it grows into a complete box, roofed except for an anterior fontanelle; in other vertebrates the dermal roofing bones close the gap. The nerves leave the chondrocranium by foramina between its parts, and the positions of these foramina are among the most reliable landmarks for homology.

A further distinction concerns the space between the orbits. In some vertebrates the trabeculae remain widely separated, the braincase extends forward between the eyes and the floor of the braincase is broad (platybasic skull); in others the trabeculae fuse into a single median bar, the orbits approach each other and are separated only by a thin interorbital septum, and the braincase is confined to the back of the head (tropibasic skull). The distinction and its distribution among the classes are discussed in [7.17.59](/7.17.59).

### 58.2.2. Ossifications of the Chondrocranium

In bony fishes and tetrapods the chondrocranium ossifies from centres in fixed regions. The names of the bones are the same in all bony vertebrates, although not every bone is present in every group, and adjacent bones fuse in many lineages.

| Region | Cartilage | Bones (cartilage bone) | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Occipital | Basal plate, occipital arch, synotic tectum | Basioccipital (median, below the foramen magnum), exoccipitals (paired, at its sides), supraoccipital (median, above) | The four bones surround the foramen magnum and form the occipital condyle(s); fused into one occipital bone in mammals and birds |
| Otic | Otic capsule | Prootic (front), opisthotic (back), epiotic (top); in teleosts also the pterotic, sphenotic and intercalar | Fused into the petrosal (periotic) in mammals; enclose the inner ear |
| Sphenoid | Trabeculae and the orbital cartilages | Basisphenoid (floor, behind the pituitary), presphenoid (floor, in front), orbitosphenoids (side walls of the orbits); pleurosphenoids or laterosphenoids in some reptiles and birds | Fused in humans into one sphenoid; the alisphenoid of mammals, which forms much of the sphenoid's greater wing, is a splanchnocranial element (§58.3.4) |
| Ethmoid | Ethmoid plate, nasal capsules | Mesethmoid (median septum), lateral ethmoids, ectethmoids, turbinals (conchae) | Often unossified in reptiles; in frogs fused with the sphenoid region as the sphenethmoid; in mammals the turbinals form the scrolls of the nasal cavity |
| Optic | Optic capsule | No bone of the braincase; sclerotic cartilage | Not part of the braincase |

The number of separate cartilage bones is largest in bony fishes and decreases in tetrapods, mainly by fusion: the frog has one sphenethmoid instead of a sphenoid and an ethmoid series, and it lacks the basioccipital and supraoccipital, which remain cartilage, so that its two occipital condyles are formed by the exoccipitals alone. The occipital bone of mammals and birds, the petrosal of mammals and the sphenoid of humans are each a fusion of several bones that are separate in other vertebrates and in the embryo ([7.17.59](/7.17.59)).

### 58.2.3. Foramina and the Cranial Nerves

The nerves and vessels that leave or enter the braincase pass through foramina whose positions are constant within large groups, and they are among the most useful landmarks for identifying bones and for comparing skulls with fossils, in which the brain is not preserved but the foramina are. In fishes and amphibians the cranial nerves leave the chondrocranium through separate foramina in its side wall and floor; in amniotes, and especially in mammals, the enlargement of the brain and the fusion of bones gathered several nerves into shared openings.

| Opening (mammal) | Bones bounding it | Structures transmitted | Corresponding exits in non-mammals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Olfactory foramina (cribriform plate) | Ethmoid | Olfactory nerve fibres (I) | Olfactory canal through the ethmoid region |
| Optic canal | Orbitosphenoid (lesser wing of the sphenoid) | Optic nerve (II), ophthalmic artery | Optic foramen in the orbital cartilage |
| Orbital fissure (superior orbital fissure) | Between orbitosphenoid and alisphenoid | Oculomotor (III), trochlear (IV), abducens (VI), ophthalmic branch of V | Separate foramina for III, IV and VI in the side wall |
| Foramen rotundum | Alisphenoid | Maxillary branch of V | Common trigeminal foramen (prootic incisure) |
| Foramen ovale | Alisphenoid | Mandibular branch of V | Common trigeminal foramen |
| Internal acoustic meatus, stylomastoid foramen | Petrosal | Facial (VII) and vestibulocochlear (VIII) nerves | Foramina in the prootic and otic capsule |
| Jugular foramen | Between petrosal and occipital | Glossopharyngeal (IX), vagus (X), accessory (XI), internal jugular vein | Vagus foramen (metotic fissure) between otic capsule and occipital arch |

| Foramen magnum | Occipital | Spinal cord | Same |

Two points recur in later chapters. The alisphenoid of mammals, a splanchnocranial element (§58.3.4), lies outside the original side wall of the braincase; the space between it and the original wall, the cavum epiptericum, was incorporated into the cranial cavity as the brain expanded, and the trigeminal ganglion now lies inside the skull rather than outside it as in reptiles. And the hypoglossal canal marks the occipital segments, whose nerves became the hypoglossal nerve when the occipital region was added to the skull ([7.17.67](/7.17.67)).

## 58.3. The Splanchnocranium

### 58.3.1. The Pharyngeal Arches

The pharynx of every vertebrate embryo is subdivided by a series of **pharyngeal arches** ([7.5.20](/7.5.20#sec-20.7)), bars of tissue between the endodermal **pharyngeal pouches** inside and the ectodermal clefts outside. Each arch contains a skeletal element derived from neural crest, a muscle mass from the head mesoderm, a cranial nerve and an artery, the aortic arch ([7.17.65](/7.17.65)). The arches are serial homologues ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.2.1)), and the four components of each arch remain associated through all later transformations, so that the nerve and muscles of a structure reveal its arch of origin even when its skeleton has changed beyond recognition.

| Arch | Skeleton (shark) | Nerve | Muscles (branchiomeric) | Aortic arch | Pouch behind it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1, mandibular | Palatoquadrate (upper), Meckel's cartilage (lower) | Trigeminal (V), mandibular branch | Jaw muscles: adductor mandibulae, intermandibularis | 1 | Spiracle (fishes); middle ear cavity and Eustachian tube (tetrapods) |
| 2, hyoid | Hyomandibula, ceratohyal, basihyal | Facial (VII) | Hyoid constrictors, levator hyomandibulae; muscles of facial expression and the stapedius in mammals | 2 | First gill slit; palatine tonsil (mammals) |
| 3, first branchial | Pharyngo-, epi-, cerato-, hypobranchial, basibranchial | Glossopharyngeal (IX) | Branchial constrictors; stylopharyngeus | 3 (carotid) | Gill slit; thymus and inferior parathyroid (mammals) |
| 4, second branchial | As arch 3 | Vagus (X) | Branchial constrictors; pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles | 4 (systemic) | Gill slit; superior parathyroid, ultimobranchial body |
| 5–7, third to fifth branchial | As arch 3 | Vagus (X) | Branchial constrictors; laryngeal muscles; cucullaris (trapezius) | 5, 6 (pulmonary) | Gill slits (sharks); reduced or absent in tetrapods |

Sharks usually have seven arches, the mandibular, the hyoid and five branchial arches; sixgill and sevengill sharks have one or two more. The muscles of the arches, the branchiomeric muscles, are compared in [7.17.62](/7.17.62), the nerves in [7.17.67](/7.17.67).

### 58.3.2. The Elements of an Arch

The skeleton of a typical branchial arch of a shark consists, on each side, of four jointed elements, from dorsal to ventral the pharyngobranchial, epibranchial, ceratobranchial and hypobranchial, and a median basibranchial joining the arches of the two sides ventrally. The arch is bent forward at the joint between epibranchial and ceratobranchial, so that contraction of muscles across this joint closes the arch and compresses the pharynx. Gill rays project backward from the epibranchial and ceratobranchial and support the interbranchial septum.

The first two arches are modified.

**Definition 58.3.1 (Mandibular Arch, Hyoid Arch and Hyomandibula)** The **mandibular arch** is the first pharyngeal arch; its skeleton consists of a dorsal palatoquadrate, which forms the upper jaw in chondrichthyans, and a ventral Meckel's cartilage, which forms the lower jaw. The **hyoid arch** is the second pharyngeal arch; its skeleton consists of a dorsal **hyomandibula**, which in most fishes braces the jaw joint against the otic region of the braincase, and ventral elements (ceratohyal, hypohyal and the median basihyal) that support the floor of the mouth and the tongue.

The palatoquadrate and Meckel's cartilage are thought to correspond to the epibranchial and ceratobranchial of a gill arch, the hyomandibula and ceratohyal likewise, although whether the jaws contain also pharyngobranchial elements, or elements of other arches, is uncertain (§58.4). In bony fishes the hyoid arch acquires additional elements: an interhyal between the hyomandibula and the ceratohyal, an epihyal and the *symplectic*, a small bone between the hyomandibula and the quadrate that helps to suspend the jaws.

### 58.3.3. The Cyclostome Branchial Basket

Cyclostomes lack jaws, and their pharyngeal skeleton is a *branchial basket* of unjointed cartilage bars connected by longitudinal bars, lying outside the gill pouches, just below the skin. The mouth of lampreys is supported by a set of cartilages of the oral disc and the piston (the "tongue") that have no counterparts in gnathostomes. The skeleton of the first arch of lampreys forms the velum, a muscular flap that pumps water in the larva, and the lips.

The Chinese textbooks, following the classical view, state that the branchial basket is not homologous with the pharyngeal arches of gnathostomes because it lies lateral to the gills, while the gnathostome arches lie medial to them. Developmental studies have modified this view. In lamprey embryos the basket develops from neural crest that migrates into the pharyngeal arches as in gnathostomes, and the cartilages express several of the same genes; the bars therefore represent the skeleton of the same arches, but in a different position relative to the gill pouches and with a different, unjointed structure (Square et al. 2017 for the shared gene expression; [7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.2.1)). Whether the ancestral vertebrate had external or internal arch skeletons, or both, is unresolved.

### 58.3.4. The Fate of the Arches

When vertebrates came onto land, the gills were lost and the branchial arches lost their respiratory function, and the first two arches were transformed in the course of the evolution of the jaw joint and the middle ear. Yet every element of the splanchnocranium can be followed. The following table summarizes the derivatives of each arch, element by element; it corrects and extends the tables in the Chinese textbooks.

Several entries need comment.

- *The jaw joint and the ear.* The posterior palatoquadrate (quadrate) and the posterior Meckel's cartilage (articular) form the jaw joint of all gnathostomes except mammals. In mammals they become the incus and malleus of the middle ear, and the new jaw joint is formed by dermal bones, the dentary and squamosal. The evidence and the fossil sequence are treated in [7.17.54](/7.17.54) and [7.17.59](/7.17.59); the hearing function in [7.17.68](/7.17.68).
- *The hyomandibula and the stapes.* The hyomandibula, a jaw prop in fishes, became the stapes in tetrapods, at first a massive brace between braincase and palate and later a slender sound conductor ([7.17.48](/7.17.48#sec-48.4)). In sauropsids the stapes (also called columella) is continued by a cartilaginous extracolumella to the tympanum; in mammals the stapes connects the incus with the oval window.
- *The epipterygoid and alisphenoid.* The dorsal process of the palatoquadrate ossifies as the epipterygoid of reptiles, a rod between the pterygoid and the braincase; in mammals the homologous element, the alisphenoid, forms part of the side wall of the braincase in front of the ear, enclosing the enlarged brain. This is the only part of the mammalian braincase wall that comes from the splanchnocranium.
- *The laryngeal cartilages.* The correspondence of the arches behind the third with the elements of the larynx is the least certain part of the table. In human embryology only arches 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 are counted (the fifth is rudimentary): the thyroid cartilage derives from the fourth, the cricoid and arytenoids from the sixth. Fate-mapping in the mouse shows, moreover, that the laryngeal cartilages arise largely from lateral plate and somitic mesoderm rather than from neural crest, so their homology with branchial arch skeleton is questioned. The Chinese tables, which assign arch 5 to the "laryngeal cartilages" and arches 6–7 to "lost", should be read with this uncertainty in mind.
- *The frog.* Frogs also have an operculum in a different sense, a small cartilage in the oval window connected to the shoulder by the opercularis muscle and used in hearing ground vibrations; it is a derivative of the otic capsule, not of the pharyngeal arches.

### 58.3.5. Pharyngeal Jaws

The branchial arches of teleosts are not only respiratory. In many groups the dorsal pharyngobranchials and the ventral ceratobranchials of the last arch bear tooth plates that form a second set of jaws in the throat, the *pharyngeal jaws*. In cyprinids (carp and goldfish), which have no teeth in the mouth, the fifth ceratobranchials form the lower pharyngeal bones, each with one to three rows of large teeth that grind food against a horny pad (the chewing pad) beneath the basioccipital; the number and arrangement of these teeth are important characters for identifying cyprinid species.

In cichlids, wrasses and parrotfishes the left and right lower pharyngeal jaws are fused into a single plate, the upper jaws articulate directly with the braincase, and new muscles connect the jaws, so that the pharyngeal jaws can crush, shear or chew independently of the oral jaws. This *pharyngognathy* decouples food capture (oral jaws) from food processing (pharyngeal jaws) and is often cited as a key innovation of the rapid radiations of cichlids in the African Great Lakes, where some 2 000 species have evolved in the last few million years ([7.10.32](/7.10.32)), although the evidence for a causal role is debated. Moray eels have gone furthest: they protract their pharyngeal jaws into the mouth to seize prey held by the oral teeth and pull it into the oesophagus, a mechanism that compensates for the weak suction of their narrow heads (Mehta and Wainwright 2007).

## 58.4. Primary and Secondary Jaws

### 58.4.1. The Origin of Jaws Revisited

The origin of jaws from the first pharyngeal arch is discussed in [7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.2) from the perspectives of comparative anatomy, development (the *Hox*-free first arch, the *Dlx* code and the heterotopic shift of the nasal and hypophyseal placodes) and fossils. From the point of view of the skull, two versions of the hypothesis have been distinguished (Kardong 2019).

1. In the *serial theory* the jaws derive entirely from one arch, the first, which was once like the others; any additional elements of the jaws arose within that arch.
2. In the *composite theory* the jaws and the front of the braincase are composed of elements of several adjacent arches, including one or more "premandibular" arches that contributed to the trabeculae.

Neither theory is supported in its strong form. The trabeculae of the braincase derive from neural crest that migrates into the region in front of the mandibular arch, which in lampreys forms part of the upper lip; this suggests that the dorsal part of the ancestral first arch contributed to the front of the braincase, as the composite theory proposes, while the jaws themselves derive from the mandibular arch alone, as the serial theory proposes. The developmental genetic data favour this compromise (Kuratani 2012). In either case, the jaws are a pharyngeal arch that was specialized, not a respiratory gill arch converted to feeding.

### 58.4.2. Primary and Secondary Jaws

The jaws of chondrichthyans are the cartilaginous palatoquadrate and Meckel's cartilage themselves. In osteichthyans the functional jaws are dermal bones that sheathe these cartilages from outside: the premaxilla and maxilla in the upper jaw, the dentary and its accompanying bones in the lower jaw. The cartilages ossify only at a few points, mainly at the jaw joint, and their remaining parts are enclosed by dermal bone or resorbed.

**Definition 58.4.1 (Primary and Secondary Jaws)** The **primary jaws** are the cartilages of the mandibular arch, the palatoquadrate and Meckel's cartilage, and the cartilage bones that ossify in them; they form the functional jaws of chondrichthyans. The **secondary jaws** are the dermal bones that sheathe the primary jaws and bear the marginal teeth, chiefly the premaxilla, maxilla and dentary; they form the functional jaws of osteichthyans.

The origin of the secondary jaws was clarified by Silurian fossils from China. The placoderms were long thought to have only internal jaw plates, unlike the marginal jaw bones of osteichthyans. *Entelognathus primordialis* (about 423 million years ago, Yunnan) is a placoderm with a premaxilla, maxilla and dentary on the outside of the jaws, arranged as in osteichthyans (Zhu et al. 2013), and *Qilinyu* shows a partial version of the pattern ([7.17.46](/7.17.46#sec-46.3.2)). The dermal jaw bones of bony fishes and tetrapods, including the human maxilla and mandible, thus originated in the placoderm grade of stem gnathostomes.

In mammals the evolution of the jaws completed a curious reversal: the primary jaws, which were the functional jaws of the earliest gnathostomes, were left with no jaw function at all and became the middle ear, while one secondary jaw bone, the dentary, became the entire lower jaw.

## 58.5. Jaw Suspension

### 58.5.1. Types of Suspension

The lower jaw articulates with the upper jaw, and the upper jaw must be attached to the braincase. How this is done, the *jaw suspension* (suspensorium), determines how freely the jaws can move and how forces are transmitted to the skull. Several types are recognized, named after the element that provides the attachment (Greek *stylos*, pillar).

**Definition 58.5.1 (Types of Jaw Suspension)** The types of jaw suspension are:

- **paleostyly**: no pharyngeal arch is attached to the braincase (the condition of jawless vertebrates);
- **euautostyly**: the mandibular arch is attached to the braincase directly, without help from the hyoid arch (placoderms, acanthodians);
- **amphistylic suspension** (amphistyly): the palatoquadrate is attached to the braincase both anteriorly, by a ligament or articulation, and posteriorly, through the hyomandibula (early sharks, some osteichthyans and early sarcopterygians);
- **hyostylic suspension** (hyostyly): the jaws are attached to the braincase mainly through the hyomandibula, the anterior palatoquadrate being connected only by ligaments (most modern sharks and rays, most actinopterygians);
- **holostyly**: the palatoquadrate is fused to the braincase, and the hyomandibula takes no part (chimaeras);
- **autostylic suspension** (autostyly, metautostyly): the palatoquadrate or its derivative, the quadrate, articulates with or is fused to the braincase directly, and the hyomandibula is freed from the jaw (lungfishes, most amphibians, reptiles and birds);
- **craniostyly**: the whole upper jaw is incorporated into the braincase, and the lower jaw articulates with a dermal bone of the skull, the squamosal; the quadrate and articular are no longer part of the jaw apparatus (mammals).

The terminology differs between authors, and the differences matter for readers of several textbooks.

| Type | Kardong and most English textbooks | Chinese textbooks | Examples |

### 58.5.2. Function

Each type of suspension combines strength and mobility in a different way. In hyostylic sharks the palatoquadrate is free in front and is swung forward and down by the hyomandibula when the mouth opens, so that the upper jaw protrudes and the teeth are brought against the prey; the protrusion of the jaws of the great white shark can be watched in films of feeding. In teleosts a separate mechanism, with a mobile premaxilla that slides forward on the maxilla, produces a protrusible mouth for suction feeding ([7.17.47](/7.17.47)), and the hyomandibula and the bones of the suspensorium move laterally to expand the buccal cavity. In both cases the hyomandibula allows the jaws and the floor of the mouth to move relative to the braincase, an advantage for suction and for gill ventilation.

A direct attachment of the jaws to the braincase (autostyly) gives a strong, stable joint and frees the hyomandibula for other functions. In lungfishes, which crush hard prey between tooth plates, the palatoquadrate is fused to the braincase; in tetrapods the change was accompanied by the loss of the gill cover and of suction feeding as the main way of capturing food in air, and the freed hyomandibula became the stapes. In mammals the jaw joint moved again, to the dermal squamosal, which permitted precise occlusion of the teeth in chewing and the transfer of the old joint bones to the ear ([7.17.54](/7.17.54)).

### 58.5.3. The Jaw as a Lever

The force and speed of a bite depend on the geometry of the jaw, which can be modelled as a lever rotating about the jaw joint ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.5.1)). The jaw-closing muscle inserts at a distance $l_{\mathrm{in}}$ from the joint (the in-lever), and the tooth that bites lies at a distance $l_{\mathrm{out}}$ (the out-lever).

**Formula 58.5.1 (Bite Force)** If a muscle of force $F_{\mathrm{m}}$ pulls at an angle $\theta$ to the jaw, the static bite force at a tooth is

$$
F_{\mathrm{bite}} = F_{\mathrm{m}} \sin\theta \, \frac{l_{\mathrm{in}}}{l_{\mathrm{out}}},
$$
and the closing velocity at the tooth is $v_{\mathrm{tooth}} = v_{\mathrm{m}}\,l_{\mathrm{out}}/l_{\mathrm{in}}$, where $v_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the shortening velocity of the muscle. The ratio $l_{\mathrm{in}}/l_{\mathrm{out}}$ is the mechanical advantage of jaw closing. The force of a muscle is approximately proportional to its physiological cross-sectional area, with a specific tension of about $20$–$30\,\mathrm{N\,cm^{-2}}$ for vertebrate skeletal muscle.

**Example 58.5.1** A carnivoran temporalis muscle with a cross-sectional area of $10\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ exerts about $250\,\mathrm{N}$. If it inserts on the coronoid process $3\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the jaw joint and pulls at $70^\circ$ to the jaw, the bite force at a canine $12\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the joint is $250 \times \sin 70^\circ \times 3/12 \approx 59\,\mathrm{N}$, and at a carnassial $5\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the joint $250 \times 0.94 \times 3/5 \approx 141\,\mathrm{N}$. The same muscle produces more than twice the force at the back of the tooth row, which is why carnivorans shear meat and crack bone with the carnassials and the molars rather than with the canines, and why durophagous animals (hyenas, sea otters, crushing fishes) have short jaws and large in-levers.

:::

The model explains several trends in the evolution of the skull. In fishes that feed by suction, the lower jaw has a low mechanical advantage for opening and closing (about $0.1$–$0.2$), favouring speed; in fishes that bite hard prey, a high one (about $0.3$–$0.5$) (Westneat 2004). In synapsids the enlargement of the coronoid process of the dentary lengthened the in-lever of the temporalis, and the reorganization of the jaw muscles shifted the reaction force away from the jaw joint, a condition that allowed the postdentary bones and the quadrate to become smaller and eventually to leave the jaw ([7.17.54](/7.17.54), [7.17.59](/7.17.59)). The dynamics of the jaw muscles are treated in [7.17.62](/7.17.62).

## 58.6. The Dermatocranium

### 58.6.1. Origin

The dermatocranium arose from the armour of the head of early vertebrates. In heterostracans and osteostracans the head was covered by large plates of dermal bone with odontodes on their surface; in placoderms by a head shield of several plates; in osteichthyans by a pattern of bones that is recognizable from the earliest bony fishes to humans. Two kinds of evidence connect the skull bones with the armour: in living gars, bichirs and sturgeons the bones of the skull roof are covered by ganoine like the scales and continue into the scale cover of the body; and the canals of the lateral line ([7.17.68](/7.17.68)) run through the dermal bones of fishes and early tetrapods in constant positions, the supraorbital canal through the nasal, frontal and parietal series, the infraorbital canal through the circumorbital bones, and the mandibular canal through the lower jaw. The lateral-line canals are, in effect, labels that identify bones across taxa.

The Chinese textbooks state that the dermal bones first appeared in ganoid fishes and sank inward from their scales. The first part is not correct: dermal bone is older than the jawed vertebrates, and the pattern of skull-roof bones was established in stem osteichthyans and in placoderms such as *Entelognathus*. The second part, the origin of skull bones from superficial dermal plates that sank beneath the skin and came into contact with the chondrocranium, is the accepted view.

### 58.6.2. The Series

The dermal bones of an early tetrapod, or of an osteolepiform fish such as *Eusthenopteron*, can be arranged in series, each occupying a constant region of the skull. The series are named by their position; the bones of each series are coloured with one hue in all figures of this course.

|---|---|---|---|

| Roof (vault) | Nasal, frontal, parietal, postparietal (paired, in a median series) | Top of the skull from snout to occiput | Nasal, frontal, parietal; the postparietals become the interparietal, usually fused to the occipital |
| Circumorbital | Lacrimal, prefrontal, postfrontal, postorbital, jugal | Around the orbit | Lacrimal, jugal; prefrontal, postfrontal and postorbital lost |

| Cheek | Squamosal, quadratojugal | Side of the skull behind and below the orbit, over the jaw joint | Squamosal (part of the temporal bone, with the jaw joint); quadratojugal lost |
| Opercular (fishes) | Opercular, subopercular, preopercular, interopercular; branchiostegal rays; gular plates | Gill cover and throat | Lost with the gills (the preopercular persists in the earliest tetrapods) |
| Palatal | Vomer, palatine, ectopterygoid, pterygoid (dermal part), parasphenoid | Roof of the mouth | Vomer, palatine, pterygoid (as a small bone or a process of the sphenoid); parasphenoid lost or incorporated in the sphenoid |

Both groupings are in use; the important point is that these bones form the side wall of the skull behind the orbit, the region where temporal fenestrae open.

The series are easiest to learn by position. Around the external naris lie the premaxilla, maxilla, nasal and, inside, the septomaxilla. Around the orbit lie, clockwise from the front in a left lateral view, the lacrimal, prefrontal, frontal (in some taxa), postfrontal, postorbital and jugal. Behind the orbit, the squamosal and quadratojugal cover the cheek above the jaw joint, and the supratemporal, tabular and postparietal form the back of the skull table. The lower jaw has an outer row (dentary, angular, surangular) and an inner row (splenial, coronoids, prearticular) around the articular.

### 58.6.3. The Palate

The roof of the mouth of bony fishes and early tetrapods is a continuous floor of dermal bone beneath the braincase, the primary palate. Its bones are paired except the parasphenoid. From front to back, the vomers lie behind the premaxillae and medial to the internal nostrils (choanae) in tetrapods; the palatines lateral to the choanae; the ectopterygoids lateral to the pterygoids; and the large pterygoids extend back to the quadrate, forming with it the palatoquadrate complex. The median parasphenoid covers the floor of the braincase. The palatal bones of early tetrapods bore teeth, often large fangs on the vomer, palatine and ectopterygoid, and fields of small denticles on the pterygoid and parasphenoid; palatal teeth persist in many lizards and snakes and in salamanders, but are lost in mammals and birds.

In many tetrapods a second, more ventral shelf of bone develops below the primary palate and separates the nasal passage from the mouth: the **secondary palate**, formed by processes of the premaxilla, maxilla and palatine (and in crocodylians also the pterygoid). It evolved independently in mammals and crocodylians and in some turtles; its structure and function are treated in [7.17.59](/7.17.59).

### 58.6.4. The Lower Jaw

The lower jaw of osteichthyans and early tetrapods consists of a core of Meckel's cartilage, ossified at its back end as the articular, and of up to eleven dermal bones on each side. On the outer (lateral) side the dentary bears the marginal teeth and extends back to the angular and surangular; on the inner (lingual) side a row of splenials, coronoids and the large prearticular cover the cartilage; the Meckelian fossa, an opening on the inner side behind the tooth row, admits the jaw-closing muscles. The two halves of the jaw meet at the front in the mandibular symphysis.

The history of the lower jaw in synapsids is one of the best-documented transformations in vertebrate history: the dentary enlarged, the postdentary bones (angular, surangular, prearticular, articular) diminished, a coronoid process of the dentary developed for the attachment of the temporalis muscle, and eventually the dentary contacted the squamosal and formed a new joint ([7.17.54](/7.17.54), [7.17.59](/7.17.59)). In birds, by contrast, the bones of the lower jaw fuse into one unit in the adult, although the separate elements can be recognized in the embryo.

### 58.6.5. Growth of the Dermal Skull: Sutures

Dermal bones grow at their margins, and where two bones meet they are separated by a suture, a thin layer of fibrous tissue containing osteogenic cells that deposit bone on both edges as the skull enlarges. Sutures thus allow the braincase to grow with the brain while remaining rigid. Their shape reflects their loading: straight, overlapping (scarf) sutures where bones are compressed, interdigitating sutures where they are subject to bending and tension, as in the skull roof of mammals and the sutures between the nasals and frontals of pigs that resist the forces of rooting.

The fate of a suture depends on signals from the underlying dura and the expanding brain; FGF receptor signalling and the transcription factor Twist1 maintain the suture as an undifferentiated zone. Premature closure, craniosynostosis, distorts the shape of the skull because growth continues only at the open sutures, and in humans it is caused by mutations of *FGFR2* (Apert and Crouzon syndromes) and *TWIST1* (Saethre–Chotzen syndrome) among others ([7.5.24](/7.5.24)). Conversely, the late or incomplete closure of sutures in many vertebrates, the fontanelles of the newborn human and the open sutures of juvenile reptiles, is part of the evidence that a fossil specimen is young. In birds and mammals most sutures fuse in the adult, so that the separate bones of the dermatocranium can be distinguished only in juvenile skulls, which is why skull counts for these groups (§59) are usually taken from juveniles or embryos.

## 58.7. The Hyoid Apparatus

The ventral elements of the second and third arches, with the median basihyal and basibranchials, form in tetrapods the *hyoid apparatus*, a sling of cartilage or bone in the floor of the mouth that anchors the tongue and the muscles of the throat and larynx. It has a body (corpus), formed by the basihyal and basibranchials, and two or three pairs of horns (cornua), formed by the ceratohyals and the ceratobranchials of the first branchial arches.

The hyoid takes part in several specialized feeding mechanisms:

- Frogs and salamanders project the tongue: in plethodontid salamanders the elongated ceratobranchials and epibranchials of the hyoid are shot out of the mouth by a squeezing muscle, carrying the tongue pad up to 80 % of the body length within a few milliseconds ([7.17.49](/7.17.49)).
- Chameleons project the tongue over more than a body length on a long entoglossal process of the hyoid body, around which the accelerator muscle stores elastic energy in collagen sheaths and then releases it, reaching accelerations above $2000\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ in small species (Anderson 2016).
- Woodpeckers and hummingbirds have elongated hyoid horns that curve around the back of the skull and sometimes reach the nostril, allowing the tongue to be protruded far beyond the bill tip.
- In mammals the hyoid is suspended from the skull by the stylohyoid chain (Reichert's cartilage), which may be a chain of bones (in carnivorans and ungulates) or a ligament (in humans, where the hyoid is the only bone not articulated with another bone); it supports the tongue and the larynx, and in roaring cats an incompletely ossified hyoid and elongated vocal folds allow the larynx to descend and produce low-frequency roars.

### 58.8.2. Representative Skulls

The skulls of the representative animals illustrate the table. The dogfish has a single cartilaginous braincase with a rostrum, nasal capsules, orbits and otic capsules, and seven arches. The carp has more than 130 bones in the skull and pharyngeal skeleton, including the four opercular bones and three branchiostegal rays on each side and the lower pharyngeal bones with three rows of pharyngeal teeth that grind food against a horny pad under the basioccipital. The frog has a flat, broad skull with large orbits, a frontoparietal, a T-shaped parasphenoid, a sphenethmoid, two exoccipital condyles and a stapes in a tympanic middle ear. The lizard has a skull with an upper temporal fenestra and an incomplete lower temporal bar, a movable quadrate and a long epipterygoid. The pigeon has a thin, fused braincase with large orbits separated by an interorbital septum and a beak covering the premaxilla and dentary. The rabbit has a skull of about 30 bones, with a diastema between incisors and cheek teeth, a fenestrated maxilla, a large tympanic bulla and a dentary that articulates with the squamosal.

## Summary

- The skull (Definition 58.1.1) is composed of the **chondrocranium** (braincase and sense capsules, from neural crest anteriorly and mesoderm posteriorly), the **splanchnocranium** (pharyngeal arch skeleton, from neural crest) and the **dermatocranium** (dermal bones, from the armour of early vertebrates). Their relative contributions shift from cartilage in cyclostomes and sharks to dermal bone in mammals.
- The chondrocranium develops from parachordals, trabeculae, sense capsules, orbital cartilages and an occipital arch derived from somites; it ossifies in occipital, otic, sphenoid and ethmoid regions, whose bones fuse in many lineages.
- Each **pharyngeal arch** has a skeleton, branchiomeric muscles, a nerve (V, VII, IX, X) and an aortic arch. The **mandibular arch** (palatoquadrate and Meckel's cartilage) and the **hyoid arch** with its **hyomandibula** (Definition 58.3.1) are specialized arches; the cyclostome branchial basket is probably homologous with arch skeleton but lies lateral to the gills.
- Arch fates: palatoquadrate → quadrate → incus, and epipterygoid → alisphenoid; Meckel's cartilage → articular → malleus; hyomandibula → columella/stapes; ceratohyal → anterior hyoid horn, styloid; arch 3 → posterior hyoid horns; arches 4–6 → laryngeal cartilages (with uncertain homology).
- Primary jaws are the mandibular arch cartilages; secondary jaws (Definition 58.4.1) are the dermal premaxilla, maxilla and dentary, which originated in placoderms such as *Entelognathus*.
- Jaw suspension (Definition 58.5.1): paleostyly, euautostyly, **amphistylic**, **hyostylic**, holostyly, **autostylic** and craniostyly; they are not a single series, and each combines mobility and strength differently.
- The dermatocranium is organized in series by position: marginal, roof, circumorbital, temporal and cheek, opercular, palatal and mandibular; lateral-line canals label the bones. Mammals retain the premaxilla, maxilla, nasal, frontal, parietal, interparietal, lacrimal, jugal, squamosal, vomer, palatine, pterygoid and dentary, and convert the angular into the tympanic.
- The hyoid apparatus, from the ventral parts of arches 2 and 3, supports the tongue and larynx and is modified for tongue projection in salamanders, chameleons and woodpeckers.

## Questions

1. Name the arch, the nerve and the element of the shark from which each of the following derives: the human incus, the stapes, the styloid process, the greater horn of the hyoid, the thyroid cartilage, the alisphenoid.

   **Answer** Incus: first (mandibular) arch, trigeminal nerve (V), posterior palatoquadrate (via the quadrate). Stapes: second (hyoid) arch, facial nerve (VII), hyomandibula (in mammals the stapes forms mainly from neural crest of the second arch, with a small contribution of the otic capsule to the footplate). Styloid process: second arch, VII, the dorsal part of the ceratohyal–epihyal (Reichert's cartilage). Greater horn of the hyoid: third arch, glossopharyngeal (IX), ceratobranchial of the first branchial arch. Thyroid cartilage: fourth arch, vagus (X), branchial arch skeleton, but with the caveat that the laryngeal cartilages of mammals arise largely from mesoderm, so the homology is uncertain. Alisphenoid: first arch, V, the dorsal (ascending) process of the palatoquadrate, homologous with the reptilian epipterygoid.

2. Why is it misleading to say that the jaws of a carp and the jaws of a shark are "the same bones"?

   **Answer** Both jaws belong to the mandibular arch, but the functional elements differ. In the shark the jaws are the primary jaws, the cartilaginous palatoquadrate and Meckel's cartilage, which bear the teeth. In the carp the functional jaws are secondary jaws, the dermal premaxilla and maxilla above (in cyprinids toothless and protrusible) and the dentary below, while the primary jaw cartilages survive only as the quadrate and articular at the jaw joint and as unossified cores; the palatoquadrate also gives rise to palatal elements. The jaws are homologous as the mandibular arch apparatus, but the bones that bite are not homologous with the cartilages that bite in the shark.

3. Explain why the hyomandibula could become a sound-conducting bone in tetrapods but not in fishes with hyostylic suspension.

   **Answer** In hyostylic fishes the hyomandibula is the main support of the jaws, transmitting the forces of biting and of the movements of the suspensorium to the braincase, and it also supports the operculum. A bone with these mechanical functions must be massive and firmly attached, which is incompatible with the light, mobile ossicle needed to transmit small vibrations. In tetrapods (and already in lungfishes) the palatoquadrate attaches directly to the braincase (autostyly) and the operculum is lost, so the hyomandibula was relieved of both functions. It could then be reduced and come to connect the otic capsule with a tympanum, first as a massive stapes that braced the braincase and later, independently in several lineages, as a slender sound conductor. The change illustrates exaptation of a freed part ([7.17.56](/7.17.56#sec-56.3.2)).

4. Identify, by position, the bones that border the orbit and the external naris in an early tetrapod, and say which of them survive in a dog.

   **Answer** Orbit (from the front, clockwise in left lateral view): lacrimal (anterior), prefrontal (anterodorsal), frontal (in some, dorsal), postfrontal (posterodorsal), postorbital (posterior), jugal (ventral). Naris: premaxilla (anterior and ventral), maxilla (posteroventral), nasal (dorsal), septomaxilla (inside). In a dog: the lacrimal, frontal and jugal survive around the orbit, which is open behind (the postorbital bar is incomplete, closed by a ligament); the prefrontal, postfrontal and postorbital are lost. Around the naris the premaxilla (incisive bone), maxilla and nasal survive; the septomaxilla is lost.

5. (Open) The relative importance of the three skull components changed from cartilage to dermal bone in the evolution from sharks to mammals. Propose functional reasons and evaluate whether the change was driven by function or by something else.

   **Answer** Points to consider: (a) Functional reasons for more dermal bone: dermal bone can grow at sutures and enclose a growing brain, which was important for the enlargement of the brain in mammals; it forms rigid, light plates resistant to the forces of biting; dermal jaws bear teeth in sockets and permit precise occlusion. (b) Reasons for less splanchnocranium: the loss of gills removed the respiratory function of the branchial arches, and the loss of suction feeding and the operculum freed the hyoid arch. (c) The chondrocranium persists as the skull base, where it houses the sense organs and pituitary and provides growth centres (synchondroses). (d) Historical contingency: the dermal skull was inherited from armoured ancestors and was available for co-option; sharks lost bone secondarily. The change is best seen as a combination of selection on function, the developmental properties of the tissues (sutural growth, endochondral replacement) and inheritance from ancestors.
