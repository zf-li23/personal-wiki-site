"Nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution," wrote Theodosius Dobzhansky; François Jacob called the theory of evolution by far the most important of the few true theories in biology. A science that explains so much has practical uses and consequences for how we understand ourselves, and these are the subject of this last thematic chapter. They are also contested: many people, in some countries a majority, reject evolution, so the chapter begins with creationism, the nature of science and the evidence, briefly, because the evidence was presented in [7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.7) and throughout the course. It then turns to the applications of evolutionary biology in agriculture, conservation and medicine, which use the population genetics, phylogenetics and models of the preceding chapters, and ends with the most sensitive topic, the evolution of human behaviour and the misuse of evolutionary ideas.

## 34.1. Creationism and Science

### 34.1.1. Creationism and Public Acceptance of Evolution

**Creationism** ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.6)) takes several forms. The most extreme, "young-Earth" creationists, read every statement in the Bible literally: special creation of each species, an Earth less than 10 000 years old, a worldwide flood; they must reject most of geology and physics as well as biology. Others accept that mutation and natural selection occur, and even that similar species share an ancestor, but deny that higher taxa evolved from common ancestors and hold that humans were specially created. In opinion polls, more than half of the people of the United States deny or doubt evolution, and among 34 countries surveyed in 2005 (32 European countries, the United States and Japan) only Turkey showed lower acceptance; in most European countries a large majority accept it. Most disbelievers reject evolution because they think it conflicts with their religion. But many religious traditions read the creation stories as symbolic, and many deeply religious people, including scientists, accept evolution as the natural process by which creation proceeds (*theistic evolution*); Pope John Paul II affirmed in 1996 that evolution is more than a hypothesis, while reserving a divine origin for the human soul.

In the United States, courts have held that the constitutional prohibition of state support for religion excludes teaching explicitly religious accounts of origins in public schools, so activists have adopted other forms. **Intelligent design** argues, without naming the designer, that some biological structures are "irreducibly complex" and could only have been designed. In 2005, in *Kitzmiller v. Dover Area School District*, a federal judge ruled that intelligent design is not science but a disguised religious doctrine. Later campaigns invoked "academic freedom" or "critical analysis" of evolution, usually together with a few other socially controversial topics such as climate change; their effect is to sow doubt where, scientifically, there is little. Pressure on teachers has weakened science education where such campaigns are strong.

### 34.1.2. The Nature of Science

Science acquires understanding of natural phenomena by posing hypotheses and testing them against observation and experiment. Its hypotheses must be testable at least in principle; often the objects (atoms) or processes (DNA replication) are not observed directly but inferred by comparing observations with the predictions of competing hypotheses, which requires assuming that nature obeys laws. Supernatural agents are supposed to suspend natural laws, so a hypothesis that an omnipotent being exists or created something predicts no consistent pattern and cannot be tested; specific claims that do make predictions, such as a worldwide flood or an Earth younger than 10 000 years, have been tested and refuted (the age of the Earth before the *Origin* was published).

Science is provisional: the best-supported hypothesis is accepted until evidence or a better hypothesis displaces it. Mendel's laws were modified by the discoveries of linkage and meiotic drive, but particulate inheritance stands. Individual scientists may cling to ideas, but science as a social process questions authority and seeks the weak points of established views; ideas compete, and the body of knowledge grows in explanatory power, itself a kind of selection among ideas (evolutionary epistemology, associated with Karl Popper and David Hull). Creationism does not test its claims, does not let evidence shake them, and does not grow in its power to explain nature. Evolution is both a fact and a theory ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.1.2)): common descent with modification is a hypothesis supported by so much evidence over a century and a half that it is a fact, beyond reasonable if not every conceivable doubt; evolutionary theory, the body of statements about mutation, selection, drift, development and so on, explains it. It is chiefly the fact that creationists deny.

### 34.1.3. Answering Antievolutionary Arguments

The common arguments, and the replies to them, are few and recur constantly:

| Argument | Reply |
|---|---|
| Evolution cannot be observed or proved | Evolutionary change has been observed many times ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)); most of science tests hypotheses by their predictions rather than by direct observation, and no hypothesis in science is "proved" absolutely |
| Evolution is untestable | Many conceivable observations would refute it (mammal fossils in Precambrian rocks); it is creationism that can accommodate any observation |
| Order and adaptation show design | Order arises from natural causes (crystals); natural selection, a nonrandom process acting on random variation, explains apparent design |
| Complexity violates the second law of thermodynamics | The law applies to closed systems; order increases locally in open systems powered by energy from the Sun, as in every developing embryo |
| Life could not arise by chance | Some steps have been demonstrated in the laboratory ([7.10.27](/7.10.27)); many sequences may work, raising the probability; and common descent does not depend on how life began |
| Mutations are harmful; selection only eliminates | Most mutations are harmful, but some are beneficial; selection assembles combinations of beneficial alleles that would be improbable by chance, and new characters are modified old ones ([7.10.5](/7.10.5), [7.10.32](/7.10.32)) |
| Complex organs are irreducibly complex | Eyes, hemoglobins and many pathways show functional intermediate stages, and parts are often co-opted from other functions ([7.10.32](/7.10.32#sec-32.3.2)) |
| There are no transitional fossils | Many exist: tetrapods, birds, mammals, whales, hominins ([7.10.29](/7.10.29), [7.10.32](/7.10.32), [7.10.33](/7.10.33)) |
| Vestigial organs are functional | Imperfect, nonfunctional and even harmful features are expected from history, not from design |
| Biologists disagree, so Darwin was wrong | Disagreements about mechanisms are the fuel of research; there is no disagreement about common descent |
| "Teach both sides" | Science courses teach what is supported by evidence; no research program of "creation science" or intelligent design exists, and fairness would require teaching every creation myth and supernatural explanations in every science |

Some creationists charge that classic examples are fraudulent, for instance that H. B. D. Kettlewell's study of industrial melanism in the peppered moth was flawed because he released moths onto tree trunks. Later research with more natural methods confirmed his conclusion that bird predation drove the changes; and even if one example failed, natural selection and rapid evolution have been documented in so many species that the principles would stand. In the 1980s the astronomer Fred Hoyle and colleagues claimed that the feather impressions of the London specimen of *Archaeopteryx* had been forged; examination of the specimen showed hairline cracks and mineral features running continuously through the feathered areas and the surrounding rock, which could not have been faked (Charig and colleagues 1986), and the dozen skeletons found since, together with the many feathered dinosaurs of China, put the matter beyond doubt ([7.10.32](/7.10.32)). Hoyle also likened the origin of life by chance to a tornado assembling a Boeing 747 from a junkyard; the analogy assumes that a complex cell had to arise in one random step, whereas chemical evolution and natural selection accumulate improbable combinations step by step, each step retained because it works ([7.10.27](/7.10.27)).

### 34.1.4. The Failures of the Argument from Design

William Paley argued that as a watch implies a watchmaker, the adaptations of organisms imply a designer. Darwin made the argument unnecessary by supplying a natural mechanism of design, and the history of life is full of facts that are hard to reconcile with a beneficent, intelligent designer and that only history and selection explain. Vestigial organs and pseudogenes ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.7.3)) are relics of former functions. Many features are poorly engineered because they were modified from features with other functions: lower back pain and impacted wisdom teeth, the blind spot of the vertebrate eye, a panda's "thumb" that is a stiff wrist bone because the pentadactyl limb could not easily evolve a sixth digit. Adaptation depends on random mutation, which often fails to supply what is needed, and on the wasteful death of most individuals; sickle-cell disease protects some people against malaria at the cost of the death of others who are homozygous for the allele. More than 99 % of all species that have lived are extinct. No feature of any species is known to exist solely for the benefit of another species or of the "balance of nature". Selfish genetic elements, parasites that consume their hosts alive, sperm removal, infanticide and siblicide follow from selection on genes and individuals ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)) but would be inexplicable as products of benevolent design.

## 34.2. The Evidence Reviewed

The evidence for common descent comes from independent sources that agree ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.7)). The fossil record documents transitional forms, mosaic evolution and gradual change, and the order in which groups appear matches the order predicted by phylogenies of living organisms. Phylogenies from anatomy and from DNA, two independent kinds of data, recover the same nested groups; features shared by all life (the genetic code, L-amino acids, transcription and translation) show common ancestry of all known organisms, and homologous genes and developmental programs are shared by phyla that diverged more than half a billion years ago. Genomes are full of features that only history explains, pseudogenes, satellite DNA, transposable elements, and polymorphisms older than species, such as MHC alleles of humans more closely related to chimpanzee alleles than to other human alleles. Biogeography matches the history of land masses and the sequence in which islands formed ([7.10.30](/7.10.30)). And the processes have been observed: mutation, selection in nature and in the laboratory, rapid adaptation to pesticides, antibiotics and new environments, populations on their way to becoming species. Common ancestry is also the only reason that research on bacteria, yeast, nematodes, flies, zebrafish, mice and chimpanzees can teach us about human biology and health.

## 34.3. Evolutionary Science as a Tool

### 34.3.1. Methods

Methods developed for evolutionary questions are now used everywhere. The theory of linkage disequilibrium, developed by evolutionary geneticists, is the basis of mapping genes for human disease and **genome-wide association studies** ([7.4.25](/7.4.25)); population genetics underlies the interpretation of DNA evidence in forensics and paternity testing ([7.4.14](/7.4.14)); the tests for selection in genomes ([7.10.8](/7.10.8)) find functional variants; and phylogenetics traces the origin and spread of pathogens ([7.8.6](/7.8.6#sec-6.8.3)), and has been used in court to show that a defendant was the source of a victim's HIV infection. The idea of evolution by selection among random variants also inspired *evolutionary computation*: genetic algorithms that solve optimization problems by mutation, recombination and selection among candidate solutions.

### 34.3.2. Using and Directing Adaptations

Much of what humans would like to make has already been made by natural selection in some species. Penicillin is an adaptation of the fungus *Penicillium* for suppressing competing bacteria; the heat-stable DNA polymerase of *Thermus aquaticus*, a bacterium of hot springs, made the polymerase chain reaction practical; CRISPR–Cas9, which evolved in bacteria as a defense against viruses, has become the standard tool for editing genomes; artemisinin, now a front-line drug against malaria, comes from sweet wormwood (*Artemisia annua*), one of many thousands of plant compounds evolved as defenses against herbivores and parasites. Other adaptations inspire inventions (biomimetics): Velcro was modeled on the hooks of burrs, dry adhesives on the feet of geckos, underwater glues on the byssal threads of mussels. And evolution can be harnessed directly: in **directed evolution** ([7.6.15](/7.6.15#sec-15.8.1)) enzymes and other proteins are improved by repeated rounds of mutation and selection in the laboratory, an approach for which Frances Arnold shared the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The knowledge of these adaptations comes largely from taxonomists, naturalists and microbiologists, and testifies to the practical value of natural history.

## 34.4. Agriculture and Natural Resources

### 34.4.1. Genetic Diversity and Breeding

The improvement of crops and livestock is evolution by **artificial selection** ([7.10.9](/7.10.9)), and plant and animal breeding and evolutionary genetics have developed together for more than a century, from the theory of selection response to the mapping of quantitative trait loci ([7.4.24](/7.4.24), [7.4.25](/7.4.25)). Evolutionary biology also teaches that genetic diversity protects populations. In 1970 an epidemic of southern corn leaf blight destroyed much of the maize crop of the United States because most of the maize planted carried the same cytoplasm, which made it susceptible; in experiments, genetically mixed plantings of rice suffer much less disease than single varieties. Wild relatives are reservoirs of useful genes: at least 20 genes for disease resistance have been crossed into cultivated tomatoes from wild species. Genetic engineering can now move genes between any organisms, for example the genes for Bt toxin from the bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis*, which kill the larvae of moths such as the corn earworm, into maize and cotton. Evolutionary biology helps assess the risks of transgenic crops: phylogenies identify wild relatives with which a crop might hybridize, and population genetics estimates the chance that a transgene will spread into wild populations and its fitness effects there.

### 34.4.2. Resistance and the Refuge Strategy

Insects, weeds and pathogens cause enormous crop losses, and much of the loss arises when pests evolve resistance to insecticides and herbicides ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)): hundreds of species of insects are resistant to at least one insecticide, and resistance to glyphosate has evolved in many weed species since glyphosate-tolerant crops were planted widely. Resistance raises costs and leads to ever more chemicals sprayed on the landscape. Evolutionary models have shaped the regulations meant to slow it. For Bt crops, farmers in the United States are required to plant *refuges* of non-Bt plants. This *refuge strategy* works if resistance is recessive and the toxin dose is high enough to kill heterozygotes: the rare resistance alleles are then carried mostly in heterozygotes, which die on Bt plants, and the few resistant homozygotes that emerge from Bt fields mate mostly with susceptible insects from the refuge, so their offspring are heterozygous and die (figure below). Where these conditions failed, for instance where resistance was not fully recessive or refuges were not planted, field-evolved resistance to Bt crops appeared within years.

![The high-dose/refuge strategy, in a one-locus model with random mating across Bt fields and refuges. On Bt plants susceptible homozygotes have fitness 0.01 and resistant homozygotes 1; in the refuge all genotypes have fitness 1; the resistance allele starts at frequency 0.001. A: with recessive resistance (heterozygotes killed by the high dose), the time to resistance increases steeply with the refuge fraction. B: generations until the resistance allele exceeds 0.5, for recessive resistance and for partly dominant resistance (heterozygote fitness 0.5 on Bt plants): the refuge delays resistance greatly only when resistance is recessive.](./plots/refuge-strategy.py)

The same logic applies to wild resources. Fishing and hunting are selection: heavy fishing that removes large, old fish favours early maturation at small size, and many exploited fish stocks have evolved to mature earlier ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)); trophy hunting of bighorn sheep has selected for smaller horns. Such evolution may reduce yields and slow the recovery of stocks after fishing stops, so it should be considered in management. Biological control relies on phylogenetics too: when a new pest appears, systematics identifies it and its region of origin, where natural enemies are sought among relatives of enemies of related pests, and candidate control agents are tested for their ability to attack relatives of the target that are crops or native species.

## 34.5. Conservation

### 34.5.1. The Extinction Crisis

For the first time in the history of life, a single species has precipitated what may become a mass extinction ([7.10.31](/7.10.31)). The main cause, now and probably throughout this century, is the destruction of habitat, above all of tropical forests, which hold most of the world's species and are being cleared at a high rate; others are overexploitation, introduced species, pollution and, increasingly, climate change. About 29 % of North American freshwater fishes are endangered or already extinct; estimates that 10–25 % of tropical forest species could be lost within decades imply extinction rates that equal or exceed those of past mass extinctions. Climate change may be the greater long-term threat: for many species the rate at which their climatic niche would have to evolve to keep pace with projected warming is more than 10 000 times faster than niche evolution has typically been in the past, so they must shift their ranges, which is impossible for many mountain and Arctic species and for those without corridors of habitat; one modeling study estimated that 18–35 % of species could be "committed to extinction" within 50 years under midrange scenarios of warming. The reasons to care range from the utilitarian (foods, medicines, materials, pest control, ecotourism) to the aesthetic and ethical; most species have not even been described, let alone studied.

The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) assesses the risk of extinction of species for its Red List, assigning them to categories by quantitative criteria (the rate of population decline, the size and fragmentation of the range, the number of mature individuals, and quantitative estimates of the probability of extinction): Extinct, Extinct in the Wild, Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened and Least Concern, with Data Deficient for species too poorly known. Species in the three middle categories, Critically Endangered, Endangered and Vulnerable, are called threatened. The Philippine eagle (*Pithecophaga jefferyi*), the world's largest eagle, with only about 600 individuals left in the wild because of deforestation, is Critically Endangered.

Extinction can be complete or partial. A species is *extinct in the wild* when it survives only in captivity: Père David's deer (*Elaphurus davidianus*) had long disappeared from the wild when the French missionary Armand David saw the last herd in the imperial hunting park south of Beijing in 1865; that herd was destroyed around 1900, the species survived in European parks, and it was returned to China in 1985. Przewalski's horse, last seen in the wild in Mongolia in 1969, was saved by captive breeding from a few founders and has been reintroduced since the 1990s ([7.10.33](/7.10.33)). In *local extinction* (extirpation) a species disappears from part of its range: the saiga antelope (*Saiga tatarica*) vanished from Xinjiang by the mid-twentieth century, hunted for its horns, which are used in traditional medicine; elsewhere it survives in fragmented, fluctuating populations with low genetic diversity, and in 2015 more than half of the world's saigas died within weeks in central Kazakhstan, killed by a bacterial septicaemia (*Pasteurella multocida*) during unusually warm and humid weather. Subspecies can be lost while the species persists: of the tigers (*Panthera tigris*), the Bali, Javan and Caspian forms became extinct in the twentieth century. And a species can become *functionally* extinct before the last individual dies, when it is too rare to play its ecological role or to escape the effects of drift and inbreeding.

### 34.5.2. Genetics of Small Populations and Genetic Rescue

Protecting habitat is mostly a matter of ecology, law, politics and economics, but evolutionary biology contributes in specific ways. Phylogenies are used to choose reserves that protect the greatest amount of evolutionary history (phylogenetic diversity), and biogeography identifies regions with many endemic species, such as Madagascar and the Cape region of South Africa ([7.10.30](/7.10.30)). Genetic markers identify genetically distinct populations that deserve separate management, and detect illegal trade in endangered species.

Population genetics explains why small populations are vulnerable even when their habitat is protected ([7.10.6](/7.10.6)). In a population of effective size $N_{\mathrm{e}}$, heterozygosity declines by drift as

$$
H_t = H_0\left(1 - \frac{1}{2N_{\mathrm{e}}}\right)^{t} \approx H_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/(2N_{\mathrm{e}})},
$$
and the inbreeding coefficient rises correspondingly, $F_t = 1 - H_t/H_0$ ([7.4.22](/7.4.22)). Rising $F$ exposes recessive deleterious alleles, causing **inbreeding depression** ([7.4.24](/7.4.24)), and lost variation limits the capacity to adapt; slightly deleterious mutations also drift to fixation when $N_{\mathrm{e}}s$ is small, and lowered fitness can reduce the population further (a "mutational meltdown"). A small, isolated population can be rescued genetically by bringing in a few unrelated individuals (figure below).  The best-known case is the Florida panther, a population of the puma (*Puma concolor*) that by the early 1990s had dwindled to a few dozen animals with low genetic diversity, kinked tails, heart defects and poor sperm quality; after eight females from Texas were released in 1995, the population grew, heterozygosity rose and the frequency of these defects fell. The risk to be weighed is *outbreeding depression*, the breakdown of local adaptation or of coadapted gene complexes in hybrids between populations long separated ([7.10.11](/7.10.11)). Whether a declining population can adapt fast enough to a changing environment is the question of **evolutionary rescue** ([7.10.9](/7.10.9)).

Small numbers in the past leave their mark long after a population has recovered. The cheetah (*Acinonyx jubatus*) has extraordinarily low genetic variation: in the 1980s Stephen O'Brien and colleagues found almost no allozyme polymorphism, and reciprocal skin grafts between unrelated cheetahs were accepted as if the animals were identical twins, evidence of near-uniformity at the MHC; high juvenile mortality and abnormal sperm suggested inbreeding depression. Genomes confirm severe bottlenecks, the most recent at the end of the Pleistocene, when many other large mammals became extinct. Conversely, large numbers are no guarantee. The passenger pigeon (*Ectopistes migratorius*) of eastern North America probably numbered three to five billion in the nineteenth century, flying in flocks that took hours to pass; commercial hunting and the clearing of forests destroyed it within a few decades, the last wild birds were killed around 1900, and the last individual, Martha, died in the Cincinnati Zoo in 1914. Genomes from museum specimens show surprisingly low genetic diversity for so vast a population, which has been attributed to past fluctuations in population size (Hung and colleagues 2014) and, in a later analysis of whole genomes, to the efficiency of linked selection in a very large population, which removed variation near selected sites (**linked selection**, [7.10.8](/7.10.8); Murray and colleagues 2017). Whatever the cause, a species adapted to living in immense flocks proved unable to persist at the reduced numbers to which humans drove it.

## 34.6. Health and Medicine

The applications of evolutionary biology are probably most numerous and important in medicine and public health.

**Definition 34.6.1 (Evolutionary Medicine)** **Evolutionary medicine** (Darwinian medicine) is the application of evolutionary biology to health and disease: the explanation of human vulnerabilities to disease by their evolutionary (ultimate) causes, and the use of evolutionary principles and methods to understand, prevent and treat diseases, especially the evolution of pathogens and cancers.

The program was articulated by George C. Williams and Randolph Nesse in the 1990s. It complements the proximate explanations of most medical research ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.5.1)). Evolutionary theory supplies new questions (why do we age?), principles that practice must respect (selection for drug resistance), and methods (phylogenetic tracing of pathogens). It also reminds physicians that people differ genetically in susceptibility and in their responses to drugs, often with genotype-by-environment interactions: variation in *N*-acetyltransferases, which break down some toxins, affects the risk of cancer from smoking. Population labels are poor proxies for such differences. The analgesic codeine works only after the liver enzyme CYP2D6 converts it into morphine; roughly one in ten northern Europeans lacks the enzyme, a much higher proportion than in some other populations, but the useful information is the patient's own genotype, not the region of their ancestors ([7.10.33](/7.10.33#sec-33.3.3)).

### 34.6.1. Evolutionary Legacies

Like almost all eukaryotes, we age. The evolutionary theory of senescence ([7.5.37](/7.5.37), [7.10.15](/7.10.15)) explains it by the declining force of selection with age, through the accumulation of late-acting deleterious mutations and **antagonistic pleiotropy**, alleles that raise fitness early in life and lower it later. It predicts that many genes and many functions are involved, so that there is no single cause of senescence and probably no single "magic bullet" against it: curing one disease of old age would expose the next. Many defenses are adaptations that can go awry. Fever appears to be an adaptation that suppresses pathogens, but extreme fever is dangerous; pain, nausea, coughing and inflammation are protective but can become harmful or intolerable; nausea and vomiting in early pregnancy are most frequent when the embryo is most sensitive to toxins and are associated with fewer miscarriages, which suggests a protective function. Genetic conflicts within the body also cause disease: paternally expressed alleles that increase the extraction of nutrients by the fetus are opposed by maternally expressed alleles (**genomic imprinting**, [7.4.18](/7.4.18), [7.10.16](/7.10.16)), and imbalances underlie disorders such as Beckwith–Wiedemann and Prader–Willi syndromes.

### 34.6.2. Mismatch with Modern Environments

Much modern disease reflects **evolutionary mismatch** ([7.10.33](/7.10.33#sec-33.6.3)). Agriculture brought nutritional deficiencies, tooth decay and infectious diseases acquired from domesticated animals, and standing water around villages increased mosquitoes and malaria; industrial life brought "diseases of civilization", cardiovascular disease, obesity and type 2 diabetes, whose best prevention is exercise, moderate eating and avoidance of calorie-rich, nutrient-poor processed food. A diverse microbiome is needed for normal development of the immune system, and urban life and heavy use of antibiotics in early childhood increase the risk of inflammatory bowel disease, asthma and severe allergies; some members of the microbiome protect us directly, by suppressing pathogens such as *Clostridioides* (formerly *Clostridium*) *difficile*, which can take over the gut after antibiotic treatment and is treated by restoring the microbial community.

### 34.6.3. Genetic Disease

Most inherited diseases are caused by mutations; many are rare alleles maintained by **mutation–selection balance** ([7.4.23](/7.4.23#sec-23.4)), and most nonsynonymous mutations slightly reduce fitness ([7.10.4](/7.10.4)). Most new point mutations enter the population through sperm, and their number increases with the father's age, because spermatogonial stem cells divide throughout adult life. Some disease alleles are common because they have, or had, countervailing benefits: those that protect heterozygotes against malaria ([7.4.23](/7.4.23)). Others may have been favoured by antagonistic pleiotropy, with benefits early in life and costs at ages that few people reached in the past: it has been suggested that the *APOE* ε4 allele, which increases the risk of Alzheimer's disease late in life, may benefit cognitive development in children in some environments. As lifespans lengthen, such late effects are encountered more often.

### 34.6.4. Cancer as Somatic Evolution

A cancer is an evolutionary process within the body ([7.3.26](/7.3.26#sec-26.2)): somatic mutations create genetically different cell lineages, and selection among them favors those that divide faster and escape controls. By middle age, skin cells exposed to sunlight carry thousands of mutations, but cancers arise only when several "driver" mutations accumulate in one lineage. Because mutations continue as a tumor grows, phylogenies of the cells of a tumor reconstruct its history, like gene trees of a population ([7.9.13](/7.9.13#sec-13.4.2)); they show that driver mutations may occur long before a cancer is detected, and that cancers often have more than one origin. Selection within a tumor also explains why therapy fails: drugs that kill sensitive cells select for resistant ones, which were often present before treatment ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)). *Adaptive therapy* uses this logic: rather than trying to kill as many cancer cells as possible, which removes the sensitive cells that compete with resistant ones, it adjusts the dose to keep the tumor stable, preserving sensitive cells that suppress the resistant clone (figure below). Lower-dose adaptive treatment slowed tumor progression in mice compared with standard high-dose therapy, and the approach is being tested in clinical trials.

### 34.6.5. Infectious Disease and Drug Resistance

Pathogens range from those that infect humans only occasionally from animal reservoirs (rabies, anthrax), through those that spread among humans for a limited number of transmissions (Ebola, monkeypox), to those that are exclusively human (measles, smallpox, syphilis). The evolution of **virulence** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)) depends on the trade-off between virulence and transmission, on the mode of transmission (vertical transmission favours benign parasites) and on competition among strains within hosts; some old associations, such as those of several primates with their simian immunodeficiency viruses, cause little disease, probably because both host and virus have evolved. Four contributions of evolutionary biology are especially important.

- *Phylogenetic tracing.* Phylogenies of pathogen genomes, dated by the molecular clock, reveal where and when outbreaks began and how they spread (*phylodynamics*, [7.8.6](/7.8.6#sec-6.8.3), [7.10.24](/7.10.24)): the genomes of the West African Ebola epidemic of 2014–2016 showed sustained human-to-human transmission and rapid diversification during the outbreak.
- *Vaccine strains.* Influenza viruses evolve continually in their surface antigens, and new antigenic variants spread because existing immunity does not recognize them. Vaccines must be prepared months in advance, so the strains to be included are chosen by predicting which lineages will dominate; models that combine fitness estimates from mutations in epitopes with the shape of the viral phylogeny have improved these predictions.
- *Vector control by gene drive.* Genetic elements that are transmitted to more than half of the offspring, like meiotic drivers ([7.4.17](/7.4.17), [7.10.16](/7.10.16)), can spread a gene through a population even if it lowers fitness. Two approaches aim to make mosquitoes unable to transmit pathogens rather than to eliminate them: releasing mosquitoes infected with strains of the bacterium *Wolbachia*, which spread through populations by cytoplasmic incompatibility and block the transmission of dengue virus, and CRISPR–Cas9 gene drives; one experimental construct that impairs the transmission of malaria parasites was transmitted to 99.5 % of the offspring of carriers in laboratory populations.
- *Antibiotic resistance.* Above all, pathogens evolve resistance to the drugs used against them. Multidrug-resistant bacteria are among the gravest threats to public health: some common in hospitals, such as *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, have become resistant even to carbapenems, antibiotics of last resort. Resistance genes spread among bacterial species by **horizontal gene transfer** ([7.10.18](/7.10.18)) and so reach the worst pathogens. The most important measure is to reduce the selection pressure: antibiotics are often prescribed for viral infections, against which they are useless, and much of the antibiotic use in some countries has been in livestock, a large part of it to promote growth (a practice phased out in the European Union in 2006 and in the United States from 2017). Treatment itself can be designed with evolution in mind (figure below): combination therapy, standard for tuberculosis and HIV, works because the chance that a pathogen population already contains a variant resistant to all drugs at once is the product of the individual chances ([7.6.41](/7.6.41#sec-41.5)); negative correlations between resistances to different drugs, where they exist, can be exploited in drug sequences.

Public health also illustrates the conflict between individual and collective interest that recurs in evolutionary biology ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)). A farmer who uses antibiotics to promote growth may profit at a cost to everyone. If enough of a population is vaccinated, a pathogen cannot spread and the unvaccinated few are protected (herd immunity); in the simplest model the critical fraction is $1 - 1/R_0$, where $R_0$ is the number of new infections caused by one infected person in a fully susceptible population, about 90–95 % for measles with $R_0$ of 12–18. Outbreaks of childhood diseases have occurred where many parents refused vaccination.

## 34.7. Evolution and Human Behaviour

### 34.7.1. Social Darwinism and Eugenics

No topic in evolutionary biology is more controversial than the evolution of human behaviour. Many people are reluctant to see human abilities as extensions of those of other animals; behaviour varies greatly with learning and culture; and any hint of biological determinism recalls *Social Darwinism*. This political philosophy, developed by Herbert Spencer, who had begun writing on social evolution before the *Origin* and later coined the phrase "survival of the fittest" (1864), saw competition as the engine of improvement in nature and society and attributed poverty, illiteracy and crime to inherited inferiority rather than to social conditions; Darwin did not share it, but his name was attached to it. It became linked with *eugenics*, a movement founded by Darwin's cousin Francis Galton, which advocated encouraging "superior" people to have more children and discouraging or preventing "inferior" people from doing so. In the early twentieth century eugenic ideas were widely accepted by scientists and governments: many states of the United States, and several European countries, passed laws for the compulsory sterilization of people judged "unfit", and the United States restricted immigration on eugenic grounds; Nazi Germany carried compulsory sterilization much further and went on to the murder of disabled people. Eugenics rested on bad science as well as bad values: it treated complex traits as simple Mendelian characters, confused social disadvantage with heredity and assumed that groups could be ranked. The lesson is that scientific claims about human differences must be examined with special care, and that no description of nature dictates what we ought to do (the naturalistic fallacy, [7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.6)). Historians disagree about how far Nazi racial ideology drew on Darwinian ideas, but it drew heavily on racist ideologies with a long pre-Darwinian history. But evolution has been twisted to support claims that some populations are superior, and evolutionary biologists and geneticists have a responsibility to correct such misuses of their science.

### 34.7.2. Variation in Cognitive Traits and the Meaning of Heritability

Cognitive abilities measured by IQ tests are correlated with one another, and their common factor $g$ has a high **narrow-sense heritability** in studies of twins and adoptees ([7.4.24](/7.4.24)): about 0.7 in studies of 11-year-olds in Scotland and Minnesota, with a further 0.2–0.3 of the variance attributed to shared family environment; heritability increases with age, as the effects of the childhood environment fade. Genome-wide association studies have begun to find variants of small effect associated with test scores. But heritability is a property of a population in its environments, not of a trait ([7.4.24](/7.4.24#sec-24.8)). A high heritability does not mean that a trait is fixed: the heritability of height in industrial countries is about 0.8, yet mean height increased greatly within one or two generations with better nutrition, and children adopted into more stimulating homes score substantially higher on IQ tests. Nor does heritability within populations say anything about the causes of differences between populations that live in different environments. The gap in average IQ scores that separated European-Americans and African-Americans reflects their different social, economic and educational environments: it has narrowed by about a third in recent decades, and children of different ancestry reared in the same environment have similar scores. The evidence does not indicate genetic differences in cognitive ability among human populations. Other behavioural traits, such as sexual orientation, have been studied in the same way; their modest heritability and evolutionary explanations remain the subject of research.

### 34.7.3. Evolutionary Approaches to Human Behaviour

It would be contrary to everything we know about evolution to suppose that no trace of homology remains between the behaviour of humans and that of other primates, or that the human mind is a blank slate. Several research programs study human behaviour from an evolutionary perspective. *Human sociobiology*, announced by E. O. Wilson in 1975, interpreted many behaviours as adaptations shaped by kin selection and reciprocity, and was bitterly criticized by social scientists and by evolutionary biologists such as Gould and Lewontin, who objected to the assumption that every trait is an adaptation. *Human behavioural ecology* uses optimality models to predict behaviour and cultural norms, assuming not that specific behaviours are genetically determined but that humans evolved the capacity to respond adaptively to their circumstances: Monique Borgerhoff Mulder found that women in a polygynous society chose husbands according to the resources they could expect. *Evolutionary psychology*, developed by Leda Cosmides, John Tooby and others, proposes that specific cognitive mechanisms evolved in the Pleistocene to solve recurrent problems such as detecting cheaters in social exchange and choosing mates; it tests predictions with psychological experiments and cross-cultural surveys, but its assumptions of "massive modularity" and adaptation to a Pleistocene environment are controversial, and it is often difficult to decide whether a behaviour is a genetic adaptation, a cultural product or a rational response. Gene–culture coevolution ([7.10.33](/7.10.33#sec-33.7)) studies how culture changes genetic selection and how genetic predispositions shape culture, and may help to explain human cooperation, art, music and religion. All agree that whatever our evolved predispositions are, they permit an immense range of cultural expression and individual potential.

Understanding where we come from and how we came to be as we are is rewarding in itself, like other great achievements of science: our three-and-a-half-billion-year pedigree, the emergence of modern humans from Africa, the genetic unity and diversity of humankind. Evolutionary biologists do not set social policy, but they can speak out against misuses of their science, such as racist interpretations of human variation or the naturalistic fallacy, and they share with all scientists the obligation to defend evidence and reason.

## Summary

- **Creationism** ranges from young-Earth literalism to intelligent design; it is rejected by courts as religious doctrine in public schools and is not science, because it makes no testable predictions or makes predictions that have been refuted. Evolution is both a fact (common descent) and a theory (its mechanisms).
- The standard antievolutionary arguments have standard answers; the argument from design fails because natural selection explains adaptation and because organisms are full of imperfections, relics and selfish features explicable only by history and selection.
- Evolutionary methods (linkage disequilibrium, tests of selection, phylogenetics) are used in medicine, forensics and epidemiology; adaptations of organisms are used directly or imitated, and **directed evolution** creates new proteins.
- In agriculture, diversity protects crops, and models of resistance evolution underlie the high-dose/refuge strategy, which works when resistance is recessive; harvesting causes evolution of wild stocks.
- Conservation faces a human-caused extinction crisis; IUCN categories rank extinction risk; small populations lose heterozygosity at rate $1/2N_{\mathrm{e}}$ and suffer **inbreeding depression**, and genetic rescue (Florida panther) can restore them.
- **Evolutionary medicine** (Definition 34.6.1) explains vulnerability to disease by legacies (senescence, defenses, genetic conflict), mismatch and the evolution of genetic disease; cancers and pathogens evolve, which motivates adaptive therapy, phylodynamics, vaccine-strain prediction, gene drives, antibiotic stewardship and combination therapy.
- Social Darwinism and eugenics misused evolutionary ideas; heritability within populations says nothing about differences between them; evolutionary approaches to behaviour must disentangle genes, culture and rational choice.

## Questions

1. Why is "evolution is only a theory" a misunderstanding of how scientists use the words fact and theory?

   **Answer** In science a theory is a well-supported body of explanation that unifies many hypotheses (atomic theory, the theory of gravitation), not a guess. Common descent with modification is a fact in the scientific sense: a hypothesis confirmed by so much independent evidence that doubt is not reasonable. Evolutionary theory (mutation, selection, drift, gene flow, development) explains this fact. Debates about mechanisms do not cast doubt on the fact.

2. In the refuge model of §34.4.2, explain why a refuge has little effect if heterozygotes survive on Bt plants.

   **Answer** When resistance is rare, almost all resistance alleles are in heterozygotes. If the high dose kills heterozygotes, these alleles are selected only in the rare homozygotes, and the refuge supplies susceptible mates that turn the offspring of resistant survivors back into heterozygotes, so resistance spreads very slowly. If heterozygotes survive (partly dominant resistance), every resistance allele is favoured on Bt plants from the start, selection is strong while the allele is rare, and the dilution by the refuge only slightly reduces the rate of spread.

3. A captive population of an endangered species is kept at $N_{\mathrm{e}} = 25$. What fraction of its heterozygosity is expected to remain after 20 generations, and what is $F$? How large must $N_{\mathrm{e}}$ be to retain 90 % over 100 generations?

   **Answer** $H_{20}/H_0 = (1 - 1/50)^{20} = 0.98^{20} \approx 0.67$, so $F \approx 0.33$. For 90 % after 100 generations, $(1 - 1/2N_{\mathrm{e}})^{100} \ge 0.9$, i.e. $100/(2N_{\mathrm{e}}) \lesssim -\ln 0.9 \approx 0.105$, so $N_{\mathrm{e}} \gtrsim 475$.

4. If a pathogen population contains $10^{10}$ cells and resistance to each of two drugs arises by a single mutation with probability $10^{-8}$, what is the probability that a cell resistant to both is present at the start of treatment? To one drug alone?

   **Answer** One drug: $1 - \exp(-10^{10} \times 10^{-8}) = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-100} \approx 1$. Both: $1 - \exp(-10^{10} \times 10^{-16}) \approx 10^{-6}$. With one drug resistance is almost certainly present; with two it almost certainly is not, provided both drugs are taken together, so that cells resistant to one are still killed by the other.

5. The heritability of height within a population is about 0.8, yet mean height increased by several centimetres within two generations, and two populations may differ in mean height for entirely environmental reasons. Explain.

   **Answer** Heritability is the fraction of the variance among individuals within a population, in its present range of environments, that is due to additive genetic differences. It says nothing about the effect of a change in the environment shared by everyone (better nutrition shifts the whole distribution) and nothing about the cause of a difference in means between populations that live in different environments. A trait can be highly heritable within each of two populations while the difference between them is wholly environmental.
