The previous chapter took the existence of two sexes for granted. This chapter asks why sex exists at all. Sexual reproduction mixes genomes: segregation and recombination in meiosis, followed by the fusion of gametes from two parents, give offspring new combinations of alleles. An asexual female who simply copies her genome into all her offspring avoids the costs of sex, the most important of which is that half of a sexual female's offspring are males who bear no young. That almost all eukaryotes are sexual, and that asexual lineages are rare and short-lived, is therefore one of the central puzzles of evolutionary biology. The leading answers are all population-genetic: sex and recombination help selection by breaking up associations between alleles that arise from interactions with parasites, from drift and from selection itself.

The chapter relies on **linkage disequilibrium** and its decay ([7.4.22](/7.4.22#sec-22.3)), on **mutation–selection balance** and the **mutation load** ([7.4.23](/7.4.23#sec-23.4)), on drift and fixation (7.10.6) and on the coalescent with recombination (7.10.7). The same logic then explains the evolution of mating systems in hermaphrodites (selfing or outcrossing), of the degenerate Y chromosome ([7.4.6](/7.4.6#sec-6.2)) and of the genetic system itself: mutation and recombination rates. Host–parasite coevolution, one of the forces that maintains sex, is treated in 7.10.17.

## 14.1. The Paradox of Sex

### 14.1.1. Asexual Reproduction and the Twofold Cost

Bacteria and viruses exchange genes by conjugation, transformation, transduction or co-infection, mechanisms often called *parasexuality*, but they do not reproduce through meiosis and gamete fusion. In eukaryotes, sex and reproduction are usually coupled, and the exceptions are instructive.

**Definition 14.1.1 (Parthenogenesis)** **Parthenogenesis** is the development of offspring from unfertilized eggs. In *apomictic* parthenogenesis the eggs are produced by mitosis and the offspring are genetic clones of their mother; in *automictic* parthenogenesis meiosis occurs but the diploid state is restored within the female germ line, which makes the offspring partly homozygous. In plants, asexual reproduction through seeds is called *apomixis*, and many plants also reproduce vegetatively.

About 1 percent of plant species and 0.1 percent of animal species reproduce asexually: dandelions (*Taraxacum officinale*), some whiptail lizards (*Aspidoscelis*), and the bdelloid rotifers, which have persisted without males for more than 100 million years and diversified into hundreds of species. Most asexual species sit at the tips of the tree of life, their closest relatives sexual, which implies that asexual lineages arise often but rarely persist. Yet asexuality has an immediate advantage that should make it common.

**Theorem 14.1.1 (Twofold Cost of Males)** Consider a population in which sexual females produce $k$ offspring, half of them sons, and suppose that an asexual mutant female produces $k$ offspring, all daughters, which grow up to be asexual females with the same survival as sexual ones. Then the frequency $p$ of asexual females among females changes as
$$
p_{t+1} = \frac{2p_t}{1 + p_t},
$$
so the asexual clone has a relative fitness of 2 and goes to fixation within a few tens of generations. This is the *twofold cost of sex* (more precisely, the cost of males).

**Proof** Let $A_t$ and $S_t$ be the numbers of asexual and sexual females. Each produces $k$ surviving offspring; asexual females produce $kA_t$ daughters and sexual females $kS_t/2$ daughters (the other half are sons, who do not bear offspring). Hence $p_{t+1} = kA_t/(kA_t + kS_t/2) = 2p_t/(2p_t + 1 - p_t)$. In terms of $u_t = p_t/(1 - p_t)$ this reads $u_{t+1} = 2u_t$: the ratio of asexual to sexual females doubles every generation. $\square$

The cost arises because males contribute genes but no resources to the offspring; it disappears in isogamous species and is reduced when males provide care. In hermaphrodites a related *cost of meiosis* (or of genome dilution) arises: a sexual parent transmits only half its genome to each offspring. Sex has further costs. Mates must be found and courted, which is risky and sometimes impossible, so asexuality gives the reproductive assurance that lets a single dandelion seed found a population. Mating transmits diseases. Meiosis is slow. And recombination breaks up combinations of alleles that have survived selection, producing on average less fit offspring when epistasis favors the parental combinations (*recombination load*). To be maintained, sex must have a benefit that is, under natural conditions, comparable to a twofold advantage.

### 14.1.2. What Sex Does

Sex changes the genotypes of a population only by changing associations between alleles. Segregation randomizes alleles within a locus (it moves a population to **Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium** in one generation), and recombination randomizes alleles across loci, reducing the linkage disequilibrium $D$ by a factor $1 - r$ per generation ([Theorem 22.3.1](/7.4.22#thm-22.3.1)). If $D = 0$, recombination has no effect at all. A theory of sex must therefore explain why linkage disequilibria arise and why breaking them up is advantageous. Since the additive genetic variance in fitness determines the rate of increase of mean fitness (**fundamental theorem of natural selection**, 7.10.5), the key quantity is the sign of $D$ between alleles that affect fitness. If favorable alleles tend to be found together ($D > 0$), recombination reduces the variance in fitness and slows adaptation; if favorable alleles tend to be found with unfavorable ones ($D < 0$), recombination creates both the best and the worst combinations, increases the variance in fitness and speeds adaptation. The theories below differ in the source of the negative disequilibria.

A popular argument, repeated in many textbooks, is that sex is favored "because it increases genetic variation". The statement is incomplete: an individual that has survived selection probably carries a good combination of alleles, and mixing it with another genome usually produces worse offspring, like a card player who discards half of a winning hand. Increased variation is advantageous only if the rules of the game change, so that new combinations are better, or if the existing associations are on balance unfavorable.

## 14.2. Why Sex Is Maintained

### 14.2.1. The Red Queen

A changing environment can favor recombination if what is favored in one generation is disfavored a few generations later, so that combinations that were good become bad. Fluctuations in climate do this poorly: selection must change direction quickly, on a time scale of a few generations, and the theory shows that most fluctuating environments do not favor sex. Parasites and pathogens are much better candidates: they are numerous, short-lived and evolve fast, and they adapt to the commonest host genotypes.

**Definition 14.2.1 (Red Queen Hypothesis)** The **Red Queen hypothesis** holds that species must evolve continually to keep up with the evolution of the species with which they interact. Van Valen (1973) proposed it to explain the roughly constant probability of extinction of taxa through time; applied to sex (Jaenike 1978; Hamilton 1980), it holds that coevolving parasites, which track common host genotypes, create negative frequency-dependent and rapidly changing selection that favors the production of rare genotypic combinations by recombination.

The name refers to the Red Queen in Lewis Carroll's *Through the Looking-Glass*, who must run as fast as she can to stay in the same place. Several observations support the hypothesis. In the New Zealand mud snail *Potamopyrgus antipodarum*, populations of which contain both sexual and asexual females, the frequency of sexual individuals is higher where trematode parasites are more common, and in some years the fitness of sexual females relative to asexuals was more than twice as high, enough to overcome the cost of males (Lively and colleagues). In the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*, in which hermaphrodites can self or outcross with males, populations coevolving with the pathogenic bacterium *Serratia marcescens* maintained high outcrossing rates, while obligately selfing populations exposed to the coevolving pathogen went extinct (Morran et al. 2011). The cyclical parthenogenesis of water fleas (*Daphnia*), which reproduce asexually during the summer and sexually in the fall, before the conditions of the next year are known, suggests that sex is timed to moments of high environmental uncertainty. Parasites, too, benefit from recombination: the influenza virus genome consists of eight RNA segments, and reassortment of segments between strains that infect the same host can produce new genotypes against which human populations have little immunity. Matching-allele models of host–parasite coevolution, which generate the required cycles, are analyzed in 7.10.17.

### 14.2.2. Selective Interference

Alleles are not selected independently: the fate of an allele depends on the genome in which it finds itself. In the absence of recombination, selection at one locus interferes with selection at others, and adaptation is slowed. This is the second major class of explanations.

**Definition 14.2.2 (Hill–Robertson Effect)** The **Hill–Robertson effect** (selective interference) is the reduction in the efficacy of selection at a locus caused by selection at linked loci in a finite population: random linkage disequilibria generated by drift make the fate of an allele depend on the genetic background in which it arises, so that beneficial alleles fix less often and deleterious ones more often than if the loci evolved independently (Hill and Robertson 1966).

Drift and selection together generate, on average, negative linkage disequilibria between selected loci (Felsenstein 1974): when beneficial alleles at two loci are spreading, the combinations that contain both are lost faster by selection than they are produced, while chance associations of a good allele with a bad background persist. Modifiers that increase recombination break up these disequilibria and hitchhike with the improved combinations they create, so recombination is favored indirectly (Otto and Barton 2001). Selective interference takes three forms.

**Definition 14.2.3 (Clonal Interference)** **Clonal interference** is the competition between clones carrying different beneficial mutations in an asexual population: since the mutations cannot be combined, at most one of them can fix, and the others are lost unless they arise again in the winning clone (Gerrish and Lenski 1998).

This is the Fisher–Muller argument for sex (Fisher 1930; Muller 1932). In an asexual population a genotype with two beneficial mutations, $AB$, can arise only if the second mutation occurs in an individual that already carries the first; in a sexual population, recombination combines $A$ and $B$ that arose in different individuals. When beneficial mutations are rare, they sweep one at a time and sex makes no difference. When the population is large enough that several beneficial mutations spread at once, roughly when $N U_{\mathrm{b}} \ln(Ns) \gtrsim 1$ for a beneficial mutation rate $U_{\mathrm{b}}$, the rate of adaptation of an asexual population saturates while that of a sexual population keeps increasing with $N$ (the dynamical regimes are discussed in [7.6.41](/7.6.41#sec-41.4.4)).

A beneficial mutation that arises on a genome with deleterious mutations faces a second form of interference. If its advantage is small relative to the load of its background, it is lost even though it is beneficial; if it is large, it sweeps to fixation and drags the linked deleterious mutations with it, a hitchhiking that degrades other loci. Peck (1994) called the first fate the *ruby in the rubbish*. Recombination can move the beneficial allele onto a better background. In experimental populations of the yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* that were made to reproduce sexually or asexually, the sexual populations adapted about twice as much over 1000 generations; whole-population sequencing showed that in an asexual population eight beneficial mutations fixed and dragged five deleterious mutations to fixation with them, while in a sexual population recombination separated the beneficial mutations from their deleterious passengers, which were all eliminated (McDonald, Rice and Desai 2016).

The third form concerns deleterious mutations alone.

**Definition 14.2.4 (Muller's Ratchet)** **Muller's ratchet** is the irreversible accumulation of deleterious mutations in an asexual population: the class of genomes with the fewest mutations is lost by drift from time to time, and without recombination (or back mutation) it cannot be regenerated, so the minimum number of mutations per genome increases stepwise (Muller 1964; Felsenstein 1974).

**Formula 14.2.1 (The Least-Loaded Class)** In a large asexual population with deleterious mutations arising at rate $U$ per genome per generation, each multiplying fitness by $1 - s$, mutation–selection balance gives a Poisson distribution of the number $k$ of mutations per genome with mean $U/s$ (Haigh 1978). The number of individuals in the least-loaded class is therefore
$$
n_0 = N\mathrm{e}^{-U/s},
$$
and the ratchet clicks rapidly when $n_0$ is small (of order 10 or less), slowly when $n_0$ is in the hundreds or more. For example, with $U = 0.1$ and $s = 0.02$ a population of 1000 contains only $1000\,\mathrm{e}^{-5} \approx 7$ mutation-free genomes.

Each click lowers the mean fitness of the population by the factor $1 - s$. In a small asexual population the decline in fitness can reduce population size, which speeds the ratchet and leads to a positive feedback called *mutational meltdown* (Lynch et al. 1993). Even very large asexual populations cannot prevent the accumulation of mutations with small effects, roughly those with $s$ less than about $U/10$.

### 14.2.3. Kondrashov's Deterministic Mutation Hypothesis

Interference relies on drift and works most strongly in small populations or in genomic regions with little recombination. Kondrashov (1988) proposed a deterministic advantage that operates even in infinite populations.

**Theorem 14.2.1 (Mutation Load With and Without Sex)** Let deleterious mutations arise at rate $U$ per genome per generation at many loci, and let fitness depend only on the number $k$ of mutations per genome. (a) In an asexual population at equilibrium the mean fitness is $\bar w = \mathrm{e}^{-U}$, the load $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-U}$, whatever the fitness function, provided mutation-free genomes have fitness 1. (b) In a sexual population with free recombination and multiplicative fitness, $w(k) = (1 - s)^k$, the load is also $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-U}$. (c) In a sexual population with *synergistic epistasis*, in which each additional mutation reduces fitness more than the last ($\log w(k)$ concave in $k$), the load is smaller, and with strong synergism (approaching truncation selection) the population can tolerate $U$ well above 1.

**Proof** (a) At equilibrium the least-loaded class (with $k = 0$ at fitness 1) must replace itself, and only individuals that acquire no new mutation, a fraction $\mathrm{e}^{-U}$, remain in it; since its frequency is constant, $\bar w = \mathrm{e}^{-U}$ (the Haldane–Muller principle, [Theorem 23.4.2](/7.4.23#thm-23.4.2)). (b) With multiplicative fitness the loci do not interact, no linkage disequilibria arise, and each locus is at its own mutation–selection balance; the product of the single-locus loads is again $\mathrm{e}^{-U}$. (c) With synergistic epistasis, selection removes genotypes with many mutations disproportionately, generating negative linkage disequilibria between deleterious alleles and reducing the variance in $k$ below the Poisson value. Recombination restores the Poisson variance in each generation, so more mutations are exposed together in the worst genotypes and are removed with fewer selective deaths; the equilibrium mean fitness exceeds $\mathrm{e}^{-U}$. A numerical example is computed in the figure. $\square$

The hypothesis requires a genomic deleterious mutation rate $U$ of order 1 or more per generation and appreciable synergistic epistasis. In organisms with large functional genomes, such as mammals, $U$ may be high enough, but in most organisms the load is probably too small to cause strong selection for sex, and experiments with a variety of organisms have found little consistent evidence for synergistic epistasis between deleterious mutations. Most evolutionary geneticists now regard selective interference in finite populations and the Red Queen as the stronger forces, possibly acting together, rather than any single universal explanation.

With multiplicative fitness (w = 0.98ᵏ) the asexual and sexual loads coincide at 1 − e^(−U); with synergistic epistasis (log w = −0.01k − 0.002k²) the sexual load is much lower](./plots/kondrashov-load.py)

### 14.2.4. Evidence from Genomes and Experiments

Within sexual species, recombination rates vary along the genome, and the efficacy of selection varies with them. In *Drosophila melanogaster*, the estimated rate of adaptive amino acid substitution, obtained from polymorphism and divergence at protein-coding genes (7.10.8), is lower in genomic regions with low recombination, and neutral diversity is reduced there by **background selection** and **selective sweeps** (7.10.8). In artificial selection experiments on arbitrary traits, recombination rates tended to increase, as predicted if recombination modifiers hitchhike with favorable combinations. In the rotifer *Brachionus calyciflorus*, the rate of sex evolved to be higher in spatially heterogeneous than in homogeneous environments (Becks and Agrawal 2010). None of these observations alone proves why sex is maintained in nature, but together they show that recombination makes selection more effective, as the theories require.

## 14.3. Living Without Recombination

### 14.3.1. The Degeneration of the Y Chromosome

The best evidence for the long-term cost of losing recombination comes from **sex chromosomes**. Sex chromosomes probably evolved from an ordinary pair of autosomes in a hermaphroditic ancestor: a male-sterility mutation established a population of females and hermaphrodites, and a female-sterility mutation tightly linked to it on the homologous chromosome then produced males. Selection then favored suppression of recombination around the sex-determining region, which keeps sexually antagonistic alleles (7.10.13) in the sex where they are favored. Once a region of the Y stops recombining, it is inherited like an asexual genome, through males only, and all forms of selective interference act on it: Muller's ratchet, background selection and genetic hitchhiking reduce the efficacy of selection and fix deleterious mutations, and genes decay into **pseudogenes** or are lost.

The X and Y of therian mammals arose from an ordinary autosomal pair about 180 million years ago; recombination was suppressed in a series of steps, so that the X–Y divergence of gene pairs falls into several "strata" of different ages (Lahn and Page 1999). Of the roughly 800 protein-coding genes on the X, the male-specific region of the Y retains only a few dozen, many of them with functions in the testis, and it has lost more than 60 percent of the X's DNA; recombination with the X persists only in the small pseudoautosomal regions ([Definition 6.2.1](/7.4.6#def-6.2.1)). The same pattern of degeneration is seen in the W chromosomes of birds and in young sex chromosomes of plants such as *Silene latifolia*, and the mitochondrial genome, which also does not recombine in most animals, shows the reduced efficacy of selection expected of an asexual genome.

### 14.3.2. The Fate of Asexual Lineages

The phylogenetic distribution of asexuality, scattered at the tips of the tree, implies a high rate of extinction of asexual lineages relative to their sexual relatives. The bdelloid rotifers, the classic "ancient asexuals", have been studied as a test. Their genomes carry many genes acquired by **horizontal gene transfer** from bacteria, fungi and plants, and some genomic analyses suggest that genetic exchange between individuals also occurs; how they escape the problems of asexuality remains debated. Asexual lineages often arise from hybridization between sexual species (many asexual whiptail lizards are hybrid in origin) or are polyploid, which buffers them against the expression of new recessive mutations for a time.

## 14.4. Selfing and Outcrossing

### 14.4.1. Mating Systems of Hermaphrodites

Many hermaphrodites can fertilize themselves, a compromise between sexual and asexual reproduction: gametes are produced by meiosis, but both come from one parent.

**Definition 14.4.1 (Self-Fertilization, Outcrossing and Reproductive Assurance)** **Self-fertilization** (selfing) is the fusion of male and female gametes produced by the same individual; **outcrossing** is mating with another individual. **Reproductive assurance** is the ability to reproduce when mates or pollinators are scarce or absent, which favors selfing, parthenogenesis and hermaphroditism in colonizing species and in sparse populations.

Selfing is common in weedy and colonizing plants. Most hermaphroditic species, however, have mechanisms that reduce or prevent it. Pollen and stigmas can mature at different times (dichogamy) or be separated in space (herkogamy). Populations can be polymorphic for floral form: *heterostyly*, in which plants have either long styles and short stamens or the reverse and pollen transfer is efficient only between the morphs, has evolved independently many times. The most elaborate mechanism is **self-incompatibility**: in *gametophytic* self-incompatibility, the stigma compares the single allele at the S locus carried by a pollen grain with its own two alleles and arrests pollen tubes that carry a matching allele, while in *sporophytic* self-incompatibility the pollen phenotype is determined by the diploid genotype of the parent plant. Self-incompatibility is a powerful source of **negative frequency-dependent selection**: pollen carrying a rare S allele is accepted by almost all stigmas, so S loci maintain dozens of alleles and very old allelic lineages that are shared among species (balancing selection, 7.10.8).

### 14.4.2. Inbreeding Depression and the Automatic Advantage of Selfing

The main cost of selfing is **inbreeding depression** ([Definition 24.7.1](/7.4.24#def-24.7.1)), the reduced fitness of inbred offspring, caused mainly by the expression of partly recessive deleterious alleles that are rare and mostly heterozygous in outcrossing populations. Under selfing, every heterozygous locus becomes homozygous with probability $\tfrac12$ in each offspring. In the white campion (*Silene latifolia*), the germination success of offspring of the most closely related parents was reduced by more than 80 percent relative to the offspring of unrelated parents. Inbreeding depression is different from the **inbreeding load** of small populations (7.10.6), which results from the fixation of deleterious mutations by drift.

**Theorem 14.4.1 (Fisher's Automatic Selection Advantage of Selfing)** In a hermaphroditic population, let an allele cause its carriers to self-fertilize all their own ovules while still exporting the same amount of pollen to outcross the ovules of other plants. Let the selfed offspring have relative fitness $1 - \delta$ compared with outcrossed offspring ($\delta$ is the inbreeding depression). Then the selfing allele has a transmission advantage of $3:2$ when $\delta = 0$ and increases when rare if and only if
$$
\delta < \tfrac12 .
$$

**Proof** Count gene copies transmitted per plant. An outcrossing plant transmits one copy of a given gene through each of its ovules and one through each pollen grain that succeeds in fertilizing another plant; per unit of reproduction, two copies. A selfing plant transmits two copies through each selfed seed, which survive with relative probability $1 - \delta$, and, because its pollen output to other plants is unchanged, one copy through outcross pollen: in total $2(1 - \delta) + 1 = 3 - 2\delta$. The ratio of the selfer's to the outcrosser's transmission is $(3 - 2\delta)/2$, which is $\tfrac32$ at $\delta = 0$ and exceeds 1 if and only if $\delta < \tfrac12$. $\square$

If selfing reduces the pollen a plant exports (*pollen discounting*), the advantage is smaller. Inbreeding depression is not a constant: in a population that selfs regularly, recessive deleterious alleles are exposed in homozygotes and eliminated, so that $\delta$ declines, a process called *purging*. Lande and Schemske (1985) showed that this feedback should make mating systems bimodal: populations with high inbreeding depression evolve toward outcrossing, and populations that begin to self purge their load and evolve toward complete selfing; mixed mating would be unstable. The distribution of outcrossing rates among plant species is indeed bimodal, but a substantial minority of species maintain intermediate selfing rates stably, which pollination ecology (reproductive assurance when pollinators fail, delayed selfing at the end of a flower's life) and the purging of only part of the load help to explain.

**Formula 14.4.1 (Equilibrium Inbreeding Under Partial Selfing)** In a population that self-fertilizes a fraction $S$ of its offspring and outcrosses the rest at random, the inbreeding coefficient approaches
$$
\hat F = \frac{S}{2 - S}.
$$
This follows from the recursion $F_{t+1} = \tfrac{S}{2}(1 + F_t)$ (an outcrossed offspring has $F = 0$, a selfed one $F = \tfrac12(1 + F_t)$, [Formula 22.4.2](/7.4.22#fml-22.4.2)). A population that selfs half of its offspring has $\hat F = 1/3$, more than the offspring of a single mating between full siblings ($F = 1/4$).

Complete selfing is rare even among species with little inbreeding depression. Selfing populations, like asexual ones, suffer from reduced effective recombination and from selective interference, and the occasional outcross releases alleles from their genetic backgrounds. Selfing species are, like asexuals, concentrated at the tips of phylogenies, which suggests that they diversify less or go extinct sooner (7.10.31). Many selfers retain the showy flowers of their outcrossing ancestors, a sign that the transition is recent.

## 14.5. The Evolution of Genetic Systems

### 14.5.1. Mutation Rates

**Mutation rates** are under genetic control: mutator alleles, often defects in mismatch repair or proofreading, raise them, and antimutator alleles lower them. Why, then, are mutation rates not lower, given that most mutations are deleterious? Two factors oppose each other. Fidelity is costly: proofreading and repair require energy and slow replication. And the mutation load, $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-U}$ for an asexual population (Theorem 14.2.1), favors a lower genomic rate $U$. In an asexual population a modifier allele that changes the mutation rate stays linked to the mutations it causes, and the mutation rate should evolve toward the compromise that maximizes fitness. In a sexual population a mutator recombines away from most of the deleterious mutations it produces, the load is shared by the whole population, and selection against the mutator is much weaker. Selection on a modifier of mutation rate is therefore indirect and weak, roughly proportional to the change in the load it causes in its own lineage.

Mutators can even rise to high frequency in asexual populations that are adapting to a new environment, because they hitchhike with the beneficial mutations they cause: in the long-term evolution experiment with *Escherichia coli* in Lenski's laboratory, three of the twelve populations evolved defects in mismatch repair within the first 10 000 generations, and mutator strains had an advantage early in the colonization of germ-free mouse guts. Because indirect selection on mutation rate is weak, the level a population reaches is limited by drift: the **drift-barrier hypothesis** (Lynch 2010; 7.10.6) holds that selection lowers the mutation rate until further improvements in fidelity are too small to be "seen" by selection in a population of effective size $N_{\mathrm{e}}$. It explains why the mutation rate per nucleotide is lower in organisms with large effective population sizes, such as bacteria, than in those with small ones, such as mammals (7.10.4). At some loci, by contrast, selection favors high mutation rates: pathogenic bacteria have hypermutable "contingency loci" that switch surface proteins on and off at high rates, and vertebrate antibody genes are diversified by programmed somatic hypermutation, adaptations to arms races with hosts or pathogens.

### 14.5.2. Recombination Modifiers

Recombination rates also vary and evolve. They differ among species, among individuals of a species, between the sexes, and along chromosomes, where much recombination is concentrated in hotspots. A modifier allele that increases recombination is selected only indirectly, through the effect of recombination on the genotypes with which it is associated. Modifier theory (Feldman, Otto and Barton) shows that increased recombination is favored when selection generates negative linkage disequilibria, that is, under weak negative epistasis or when drift and directional selection act together (the Hill–Robertson effect), and that it is disfavored when epistasis is positive or fluctuates slowly. The same theory shows why, in a population at a stable equilibrium with epistasis, recombination tends to be reduced (Feldman's reduction principle): the existing associations are favorable, and recombination only breaks them. The evolution of sex is thus a special case of the evolution of recombination, and the evidence of Section 14.2.4 bears on both.

### 14.5.3. Evolvability

**Definition 14.5.1 (Evolvability)** **Evolvability** is the capacity of a population or a genetic system to generate heritable phenotypic variation on which selection can act, and so to adapt. Quantitatively, it can be measured by the additive genetic variance of a trait scaled by its mean, $I_A = V_A/\bar z^2$ (Houle 1992), which predicts the proportional response to a given strength of selection.

Mutation, recombination, the structure of development and the **genetic architecture** of traits all affect evolvability. Whether evolvability is itself an adaptation, shaped by selection for the ability to evolve, is controversial. Selection on future adaptability is indirect, acting only through the hitchhiking of the modifiers with the favorable variants they produce, and it is effective mainly in asexual populations, where the association between a modifier and its products persists. In sexual populations, features that increase evolvability, such as recombination itself, modular gene regulation or robustness that allows **cryptic genetic variation** to accumulate, are more often explained as by-products of selection for other functions. The developmental side of evolvability is discussed in 7.10.19.

## Questions

1. A sexual population has 1000 females; an asexual mutant female appears. Assuming the twofold cost with no compensating benefit, after how many generations do asexual females make up more than half of all females? How large a benefit of sex would be needed to stop the clone from spreading?

   **Answer** With $u_t = p_t/(1 - p_t)$ doubling each generation and $u_0 = 1/1000$, we need $2^t/1000 > 1$, that is $t \geq 10$ generations. The clone does not spread if the relative fitness of its offspring, $2 \times (\text{asexual viability}/\text{sexual viability})$, is at most 1, so sexual offspring must have at least twice the fitness of asexual offspring, as observed in some years in *Potamopyrgus*.

2. Why does recombination have no effect on a population in linkage equilibrium, and why is the sign of the linkage disequilibrium between beneficial alleles decisive for whether recombination accelerates adaptation?

   **Answer** Recombination changes haplotype frequencies only by the term $-rD$; if $D = 0$, the haplotype frequencies are already the products of the allele frequencies and recombination leaves them unchanged. If beneficial alleles are in negative disequilibrium (each tends to occur with the other locus's deleterious allele), recombination creates more double-beneficial and double-deleterious genotypes, which increases the variance in fitness and therefore the response to selection; if they are in positive disequilibrium, recombination breaks up the best genotypes and reduces the variance in fitness.

3. An asexual population of $N = 10^6$ has a deleterious mutation rate $U = 0.5$ per genome. For which selection coefficients $s$ does the least-loaded class contain fewer than 10 individuals, so that Muller's ratchet turns quickly?

   **Answer** We need $N\mathrm{e}^{-U/s} < 10$, that is $U/s > \ln(10^5) = 11.5$, or $s < 0.5/11.5 \approx 0.043$. Mutations with effects below about 4 percent accumulate quickly even in a population of a million; strongly deleterious mutations do not.

4. Explain why the mutation load of an asexual population, $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-U}$, does not depend on how deleterious the mutations are, and why this conclusion is the starting point of Kondrashov's hypothesis.

   **Answer** At equilibrium the mutation-free class must maintain itself; its members keep their class only if they acquire no new mutation, which happens with probability $\mathrm{e}^{-U}$, and since their fitness is the maximum, 1, the mean fitness of the population must equal $\mathrm{e}^{-U}$ for this class to remain at a constant frequency. The strength of selection determines how many mutations the average genome carries ($U/s$), but not the load. Since an asexual population cannot do better than $\mathrm{e}^{-U}$, any mechanism by which sex lowers the load gives sexual populations an advantage that grows with $U$; Kondrashov identified synergistic epistasis plus recombination as such a mechanism.

5. A plant population has inbreeding depression $\delta = 0.3$. Will a mutation causing complete selfing (without pollen discounting) spread? What if half of the plant's outcross pollen is lost because the pollen is used for selfing? What does purging do to the answer over time?

   **Answer** Without discounting, the selfer transmits $3 - 2\delta = 2.4$ copies against 2, so it spreads. With half the outcross pollen discounted, it transmits $2(1 - \delta) + 0.5 = 1.9 < 2$, and it does not spread. If selfing does become established, purging lowers $\delta$, which increases the advantage of selfing further, so the population tends to move toward complete selfing.
