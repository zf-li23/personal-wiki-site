Every species evolves in a biological environment made of other species: its resources, competitors, enemies and partners. In 1862, in his book on the pollination of orchids, Darwin described the Madagascan orchid *Angraecum sesquipedale*, whose nectar lies at the bottom of a spur up to 30 cm long, and predicted that a moth with a proboscis of similar length must exist; in 1903 such a sphinx moth was described and named *Xanthopan morganii praedicta*. Interactions of this kind exert some of the strongest and most persistent natural selection known, and when each species selects on the other, the two can evolve in response to each other. This chapter treats the evolution of interactions between species: antagonistic interactions between predators and prey, parasites and hosts, herbivores and plants; mutualisms; and competition.

The chapter applies the population genetics of **frequency-dependent selection** ([7.4.23](/7.4.23#sec-23.3.2), 7.10.5), the quantitative genetics of correlated traits (7.10.9), the theory of cooperation and conflict (7.10.16) and the dynamics of interacting populations, whose simplest case is the **Lotka–Volterra model** of predators and prey ([Definition 23.4.1](/7.5.23#def-23.4.1), [2.4.14](/2.4.14#ex-14.1.2)). Coevolving parasites are a leading explanation for the maintenance of sex (7.10.14); the long-term coevolution of endosymbionts and their hosts, which reduces endosymbiont genomes, is discussed in 7.10.18; the ecological side of community structure belongs to ecology (7.19), and the immunological side of host–pathogen interactions to immunology (7.15).

## 17.1. Coevolution

### 17.1.1. Kinds of Coevolution

Many adaptations to other species are not coevolved: the industrial melanism of the peppered moth evolved in response to predation by birds, but the birds did not evolve in response to the moths. The term is reserved for reciprocal change.

**Definition 17.1.1 (Coevolution)** **Coevolution** is reciprocal evolutionary change in interacting species, caused by the natural selection that each exerts on the other. In *specific* coevolution, two species evolve in response to each other; in *diffuse* (guild) coevolution, several species are involved and their effects are not independent; in *escape-and-radiate* coevolution, a species evolves a new defense, escapes its enemies, and diversifies, until other enemies adapt to the new clade.

Darwin's orchid and its moth are a candidate for specific coevolution. Diffuse coevolution arises, for example, if a host's resistance to two parasite species is genetically correlated, so that selection by one alters the host's interaction with the other. The strength and direction of the selection that species exert on one another vary from place to place with the presence of other species and the environment. In the Rocky Mountains and the Sierra Nevada, where tree squirrels (*Tamiasciurus*) harvest the seeds of the limber pine (*Pinus flexilis*), the pine's cones are wide, heavy and thick-scaled, which deters squirrels; in the Great Basin, where squirrels are absent, cones are adapted to seed dispersal by Clark's nutcracker, a bird on which the pine depends (Benkman). Thompson's *geographic mosaic theory of coevolution* holds that coevolution proceeds in a patchwork of "hot spots", where reciprocal selection is strong, and "cold spots", where it is weak or absent, connected by gene flow, so that the traits of interacting species are matched in some populations and mismatched in others.

### 17.1.2. Phylogenetic Patterns

Coevolution leaves a trace in phylogenies when interacting lineages speciate together.

**Definition 17.1.2 (Cophylogeny)** **Cophylogeny** is the relationship between the phylogeny of a group of hosts and that of their parasites or symbionts. Concordant phylogenies result from *cospeciation*, in which speciation of a host is accompanied by speciation of its associate; discordance results from *host switching*, duplication (speciation of the associate within one host lineage), extinction of associate lineages and failure of the associate to diverge.

The bacterium *Buchnera aphidicola*, which lives in specialized cells of aphids and supplies essential amino acids, has a phylogeny perfectly congruent with that of its aphid hosts, and the association is as old as the aphid lineages themselves, well over 100 million years (Moran and colleagues). The explanation is simple: *Buchnera* is transmitted from mother to offspring, like a mitochondrion, and never switches hosts. Concordant phylogenies are therefore not evidence of coevolution by themselves, which requires reciprocal adaptation; they show only that the associate rarely moves between host lineages. The phylogenies of free-living parasites and mutualists seldom match those of their hosts closely, because host switches are frequent; methods for reconciling host and parasite trees are discussed with the methods of comparative phylogenetics (7.10.26).

## 17.2. Enemies and Victims

### 17.2.1. Arms Races and Cycles

Predators and prey, parasites and hosts, and herbivores and plants are in conflict: an adaptation of one reduces the fitness of the other. Such interactions are often unstable: introduced foxes and cats drove many species of Australian marsupials to extinction, and a chytrid fungus has exterminated some species of frogs. Because selection cannot anticipate the future, the possibility of exterminating the victim does not cause enemies to evolve restraint; victims and enemies coexist only when ecological and evolutionary factors stabilize their interaction. Fisher (1930) remarked that "probably more important than the changes in climate will be the evolutionary changes in progress in associated organisms", the idea later named the **Red Queen hypothesis** (Definition 14.2.1): each species must keep evolving to keep up with its enemies, which keep evolving too.

**Definition 17.2.1 (Evolutionary Arms Race)** An **evolutionary arms race** (escalation) is coevolution in which a trait of a predator or parasite and a corresponding trait of its victim evolve repeatedly in the same direction, each change in one selecting for a further change in the other, until the costs of further escalation stop the process.

An arms race is expected when the success of the enemy depends on the *difference* between its trait and the victim's, for example on whether a predator is faster than its prey. The Japanese camellia (*Camellia japonica*) protects its seeds with a woody pericarp, and the camellia weevil (*Curculio camelliae*) bores through it with mandibles at the tip of a long rostrum to lay eggs on the seeds. Across Japan, pericarps are much thicker and rostra much longer in the south than in the north; in southern populations the plants are "ahead", with pericarps thick enough to keep the weevils' success below 50 percent, and in the north the weevils are ahead (Toju and Sota). The rough-skinned newt (*Taricha granulosa*) of western North America carries tetrodotoxin (TTX) in its skin, enough in one newt to kill thousands of mice. Garter snakes (*Thamnophis sirtalis*) from outside the newt's range have almost no resistance to TTX, but populations sympatric with toxic newts eat them and are resistant; resistance is caused by amino acid substitutions in the snake's skeletal-muscle sodium channel, the target of the toxin, which arose independently in different populations (Geffeney et al. 2002, 2005). The match is imperfect: some snake populations are far more resistant than the newts they eat are toxic, and there is then no selection for further resistance.

When the enemy's success depends instead on a close *match* between its trait and the victim's, for example a parasite that must recognize a host molecule, the victim is selected to move away from the enemy's trait and the enemy to follow, which can produce continuing cycles rather than escalation (Abrams 2000). Brood-parasitic birds illustrate matching. Each population of the common cuckoo (*Cuculus canorus*) contains genetic races (gentes) that parasitize different host species and lay eggs closely resembling those of their hosts. Host species whose eggs are mimicked reject artificial cuckoo eggs more often than species whose eggs are not mimicked, while species that are not parasitized do not reject them; and populations of two host species that reject cuckoo eggs in Britain accept them in Iceland, where cuckoos are absent (Davies and Brooke).

**Example 17.2.1 (A Quantitative Model of Coevolution)** Let a prey population have mean trait $x$ and a predator mean trait $y$, with additive genetic variances $G_x$, $G_y$, and let each trait have a cost $\tfrac12 kx^2$ or $\tfrac12 ky^2$. The rate of capture is $a(y - x)$, increasing in $y - x$, in the escalation model, and $a = \exp[-(x - y)^2/(2\sigma^2)]$ in the matching model. The traits evolve up the gradients of mean fitness,
$$
\dot x = G_x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[-a - \tfrac12 kx^2\right], \qquad \dot y = G_y\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[a - \tfrac12 ky^2\right].
$$
In the escalation model both traits increase together to a joint equilibrium where marginal costs equal marginal benefits. In the matching model the prey is selected away from the predator and the predator toward the prey. Linearizing at $x = y = 0$ gives the matrix $\begin{bmatrix} G_x(\sigma^{-2} - k) & -G_x\sigma^{-2} \\ G_y\sigma^{-2} & -G_y(\sigma^{-2} + k)\end{bmatrix}$, with determinant $G_xG_yk^2 > 0$; the equilibrium is unstable when the trace is positive, that is when the prey's escape, $G_x(\sigma^{-2} - k)$, outpaces the predator's response, $G_y(\sigma^{-2} + k)$, and the traits then oscillate around it ([2.4.11](/2.4.11)).

### 17.2.2. Warning Coloration and Mimicry

**Definition 17.2.2 (Aposematism)** **Aposematism** is the advertisement of unprofitability (toxicity, distastefulness, dangerous defenses) to predators by conspicuous signals, such as the bright colors of many bees, poison frogs and coral snakes. Predators learn, or have evolved, to avoid the signal, so both predator and prey benefit.

The evolution of aposematism is a puzzle. A warning signal is subject to **positive frequency-dependent selection**: predators learn to avoid the common pattern, and individuals that deviate from it, including the first carriers of a new warning pattern, are attacked. How new patterns arise is therefore unclear. Proposed solutions include genetic drift where predation is relaxed, the protection of distasteful prey that survive being sampled, and kin selection, if relatives cluster so that a predator's lesson protects the victim's kin, an argument first made by Fisher (1930).

**Definition 17.2.3 (Mimicry, Batesian and Müllerian)** **Mimicry** is the evolved resemblance of one species to another because the resemblance is advantageous, not because of common ancestry. In **Batesian mimicry** a palatable species (the mimic) resembles an unpalatable or dangerous one (the model); in **Müllerian mimicry** two or more unpalatable species resemble each other and share the cost of educating predators.

Batesian mimicry is parasitic on the model. A predator that has recently eaten a palatable butterfly with a given pattern is more likely to attack the next, so the protection conferred by a pattern declines as the palatable mimics become common relative to the model: selection on Batesian mimics is negatively frequency-dependent. A rare new form that resembles a different model has higher fitness, and polymorphic mimicry can be maintained, as in the African swallowtail *Papilio dardanus*, whose females come in several forms, each resembling a different distasteful butterfly, while the males are not mimetic. Müllerian mimicry, proposed by Fritz Müller in 1879 to explain the resemblance between distasteful butterflies, is positively frequency-dependent: the more common the shared pattern, the fewer individuals of each species are killed while predators learn. In experiments with *Heliconius cydno*, whose color morphs resemble two different distasteful co-models, *H. sapho* and *H. eleuchia*, marked butterflies survived better where their morph matched the locally more common co-model, whichever it was (Kapan 2001).

**Formula 17.2.1 (Müller's Model)** Suppose predators kill a fixed number $k$ of individuals of a given warning pattern each season before they learn to avoid it, whatever the abundance of the prey. If two distasteful species with abundances $n_1$ and $n_2$ have different patterns, their per capita mortalities are $k/n_1$ and $k/n_2$; if they share one pattern, both suffer $k/(n_1 + n_2)$. Sharing benefits both, but the rarer species gains more: its mortality falls by a factor $n_1/(n_1 + n_2)$.

### 17.2.3. Plants and Herbivores

Almost all plants synthesize secondary compounds, which play little or no role in primary metabolism. Many thousands are known, including drugs (salicylic acid), stimulants (caffeine) and condiments (capsaicin); plant families are often characterized by particular classes, such as cardiac glycosides in milkweeds and glucosinolates in the mustards (Brassicaceae). Many are toxic or repellent to animals, and herbivores select for chemical defenses. In wild parsnip (*Pastinaca sativa*), seed production is correlated with genetically variable resistance to the parsnip webworm (*Depressaria pastinacella*), based on the concentration of furanocoumarins in the seeds (Berenbaum); in common milkweed (*Asclepias syriaca*), families that produce more latex carry fewer herbivorous insects and have higher fitness (Agrawal).

Ehrlich and Raven (1964), comparing the host plants of butterflies, proposed that a plant lineage that evolves a new and effective chemical defense escapes many of its herbivores and radiates into many species sharing the defense; later, some insects from other hosts shift to these plants, adapt to the defense and radiate in turn (escape-and-radiate coevolution, Definition 17.1.1). Later work has supported the idea. The plant order Brassicales, including the mustards, evolved the synthesis of glucosinolates, the precursors of toxic mustard oils, about 92 million years ago. The butterflies of the subfamily Pierinae colonized these plants about 68 million years ago by evolving an enzyme, the nitrile-specifier protein, that diverts the breakdown of glucosinolates to less toxic products. New classes of glucosinolates later evolved in one lineage of Brassicales, and two butterfly lineages soon adapted to them through duplications of the gene for the detoxifying enzyme; these innovations were associated with increased rates of diversification in both plants and butterflies (Edger et al. 2015).

### 17.2.4. Parasites, Pathogens and Virulence

Evolutionary biologists include pathogenic microorganisms among parasites. A parasite faces two challenges: overcoming the host's defenses and getting from one host to the next, either vertically, from a host parent to its offspring, or horizontally, to unrelated hosts.

**Definition 17.2.4 (Virulence)** **Virulence** is the reduction in host survival or reproduction caused by a parasite; in models it is usually measured by the additional host mortality rate $\alpha$ due to infection.

Many parasites are virulent not because harming the host benefits them but because they must consume host resources to reproduce. The once-common view that parasites should evolve to become benign, because a host killed quickly cannot transmit them, is too simple. The *trade-off hypothesis* (Anderson and May 1982; Ewald) holds that faster replication within the host increases both the rate of transmission and the virulence, so the parasite's fitness is maximized at an intermediate virulence.

**Theorem 17.2.1 (Virulence That Maximizes $R_0$)** In an SIR-type model ([1.4.7](/1.4.7)), let infected hosts transmit at rate $\beta(\alpha)$, an increasing function of virulence $\alpha$, die from other causes at rate $\mu$ and recover at rate $\gamma$. The **basic reproduction number** of a parasite strain is
$$
R_0(\alpha) = \frac{\beta(\alpha)S}{\alpha + \mu + \gamma},
$$
where $S$ is the density of susceptible hosts. In a population of hosts infected by one strain at a time, the strain with the largest $R_0$ excludes the others, and the optimal virulence $\alpha^*$ satisfies
$$
\beta'(\alpha^*) = \frac{\beta(\alpha^*)}{\alpha^* + \mu + \gamma},
$$
that is, the line from the point $(-\mu - \gamma, 0)$ to $(\alpha^*, \beta(\alpha^*))$ is tangent to the curve $\beta$. If $\beta(\alpha) = c\sqrt\alpha$, then $\alpha^* = \mu + \gamma$.

**Proof** The expected duration of an infection is $1/(\alpha + \mu + \gamma)$, during which the host infects $\beta S$ others per unit time, giving $R_0$. A strain can invade a host population at the equilibrium set by another strain if and only if it has the larger $R_0$, since at that equilibrium the resident has an effective reproduction number of exactly 1 and the invader's is $R_0^{\text{inv}}/R_0^{\text{res}}$. Setting $\mathrm{d}\ln R_0/\mathrm{d}\alpha = \beta'/\beta - 1/(\alpha + \mu + \gamma) = 0$ gives the condition. For $\beta = c\sqrt\alpha$, $\beta'/\beta = 1/(2\alpha)$, so $2\alpha^* = \alpha^* + \mu + \gamma$. $\square$

A: the optimal virulence α* is where the line from (−(μ + γ), 0) is tangent to the transmission curve; here μ + γ = 1, so α* = 1. B: the basic reproduction number R₀(α) = β(α)/(α + μ + γ) (with S = 1) for three values of the background loss rate μ + γ; dots mark the maxima, which move to higher virulence as hosts die or recover faster](./plots/virulence-tradeoff.py)

The theorem predicts that virulence should be higher when hosts die quickly anyway (large $\mu$), when transmission opportunities are abundant, and when several strains infect the same host. Within-host competition among unrelated genotypes favors faster replication and higher virulence, since a prudent strain is outcompeted inside the host (a conflict of levels of selection, 7.10.16). Vertical transmission links the parasite's fitness to the host's reproduction and favors lower virulence: in experiments with filamentous phage of *Escherichia coli*, a phage genotype that slowed its host's growth declined, and a more benign one increased, when horizontal transmission was prevented. Medical interventions change the balance. An effective immune response or treatment that kills the parasite quickly shortens the infectious period, which favors faster reproduction; when chickens were vaccinated against Marek's disease virus with a vaccine that protected them from disease but did not prevent transmission, more virulent strains spread, because their hosts no longer died before transmitting them (Read et al. 2015).

The myxoma virus is the classic natural experiment. Released in Australia in 1950 to control introduced European rabbits (*Oryctolagus cuniculus*), it initially killed more than 99 percent of infected rabbits. In the following decades the rabbits evolved resistance and the virus evolved lower, but still high, virulence: strains of intermediate virulence, which keep rabbits alive and infectious longer, came to dominate, as the trade-off hypothesis predicts. The same happened after the virus was introduced in France in 1952. Virulence does not always decline, however. Microsporidian parasites (*Pleistophora intestinalis*) of the water flea *Daphnia magna* produce more spores and cause more mortality in hosts from their own or nearby populations than in hosts from distant ones: parasites are locally adapted and most virulent in the host populations with which they have coevolved (Ebert).

The genetics of resistance and infectivity determines the form of host–parasite coevolution. Two models bracket the range of possibilities.

**Definition 17.2.5 (Gene-for-Gene and Matching-Alleles Models)** In the **gene-for-gene model** (Flor), each host resistance locus has an allele that encodes a receptor recognizing the product of a corresponding parasite *avirulence* allele; infection fails if the host recognizes any of the parasite's products, so a parasite lacking a recognized product can infect all hosts at that locus, and a host lacking the receptor is susceptible to all. In the **matching-alleles model**, infection requires that the parasite match the host, like a key and a lock: each parasite genotype can infect only the host genotypes it matches, and no genotype is universally infective or universally resistant.

The gene-for-gene model was derived from plants and their fungal pathogens, such as flax and flax rust; it predicts arms races in which resistance and broad infectivity are favored until their costs balance, and with costs it can also produce cycles. The matching-alleles model generates the most intense negative frequency dependence: a host allele is favored when the parasite allele that matches it is rare, and the parasite allele that matches the commonest host allele is favored. The result is cycles or irregular fluctuations in allele frequencies, with the parasite lagging behind the host, exactly the conditions under which the Red Queen favors sex and recombination (7.10.14).

**Example 17.2.2 (Matching-Alleles Dynamics)** Let haploid hosts carry allele $H_1$ or $H_2$ (frequencies $h$, $1 - h$) and haploid parasites $P_1$ or $P_2$ (frequencies $q$, $1 - q$), with $P_i$ able to infect only $H_i$. If a host loses a fraction $s_{\mathrm{h}}$ of its fitness when it meets a matching parasite and a parasite loses a fraction $s_{\mathrm{p}}$ when it meets a host it cannot infect, then
$$
h' = \frac{h(1 - s_{\mathrm{h}}q)}{1 - s_{\mathrm{h}}[hq + (1 - h)(1 - q)]}, \qquad q' = \frac{q[1 - s_{\mathrm{p}}(1 - h)]}{1 - s_{\mathrm{p}}[q(1 - h) + (1 - q)h]}.
$$
The internal equilibrium $h = q = \tfrac12$ is surrounded by oscillations in which the parasite tracks the host with a lag of about a quarter of a cycle; without mutation or migration they grow until alleles are lost.

A "resurrection" study revealed such cycles in nature. The water flea *Daphnia magna* produces resting eggs that remain viable in pond sediments for decades, together with spores of its bacterial parasite *Pasteuria ramosa*. When Decaestecker et al. (2007) hatched *Daphnia* from different sediment layers and exposed them to bacteria from the same, earlier and later layers, the hosts were infected most often by contemporary parasites: the host population changed from year to year, and the parasite followed, as in matching-allele models. At the same time the average virulence of the bacteria increased over the years. Genetic studies confirm that the resistance of *D. magna* to genotypes of *P. ramosa* follows a matching-alleles pattern.

Genetic uniformity of hosts invites epidemics. In 1970, 85 percent of the hybrid corn planted in the United States carried the same cytoplasmic male-sterility factor, which was used to simplify the production of hybrid seed; it also made the plants susceptible to southern corn leaf blight (*Helminthosporium maydis*), which destroyed about 30 percent of the national crop that year. Emerging pathogens often enter humans from other species, and phylogenetic analyses routinely trace their origins (7.10.34). Canine parvovirus arose from a virus of cats and other carnivores and spread around the world among dogs in 1978; six amino acid changes in the capsid protein allowed it to bind the canine transferrin receptor, and further changes after its emergence improved binding to the dog receptor and abolished binding to the feline one.

## 17.3. Mutualism

**Definition 17.3.1 (Mutualism)** A **mutualism** is an interaction between individuals of different species in which each gains in fitness from the other. *Symbiosis*, intimate living together, may be mutualistic or parasitic; an *endosymbiont* lives inside the cells or body of its partner.

Mutualisms are not altruism but reciprocal exploitation: each partner obtains something from the other. Darwin challenged his readers to find a species modified solely for the good of another, "for such could not have been produced through natural selection", and no one has met the challenge. Some mutualisms arose from parasitism. Yuccas (*Yucca*) are pollinated only by female yucca moths (*Tegeticula* and *Parategeticula*), which actively pollinate a flower with specialized mouthparts and then lay eggs in its ovary, where the larvae eat some of the many seeds. The closest relatives of these moths simply eat developing seeds, and one of them incidentally pollinates the flowers in which it lays eggs, a plausible transitional step (Pellmyr).

As in cooperation within species (7.10.16), a partner that "cheats", taking benefits without paying the cost, is potentially favored, and mutualisms are stabilized by mechanisms that reduce the fitness of cheaters:

- Sanctions: legumes house nitrogen-fixing rhizobial bacteria in root nodules and supply them with photosynthate; the bacteria provide ammonium (**nitrogen fixation**, 7.1.27). When soybean nodules were placed in an atmosphere without $\mathrm{N_2}$, so that the rhizobia could not fix nitrogen, the plants reduced the oxygen supply to these nodules and the rhizobia multiplied much less (Kiers et al. 2003).
- Partner choice: one partner preferentially rewards or associates with the more beneficial individuals of the other; legumes supply more resources to more beneficial rhizobial strains.
- Aligned interests: selection favors cooperation when a partner's fitness depends on its host's, as with long-term associations, few alternatives, and especially vertical transmission: the vertically transmitted *Buchnera* of aphids is an obligate mutualist.

When the benefit disappears, so can the mutualism. Rhizobia isolated from plots that had been fertilized with nitrogen for 22 years were less beneficial to their host plants than rhizobia from unfertilized plots (Weese et al. 2015), presumably because well-fertilized plants no longer sanctioned cheaters or rewarded good partners. Many species cheat: many orchids offer no nectar to their pollinators, and some are pollinated by male insects that try to copulate with them; two lineages of yucca moths evolved from mutualistic ancestors no longer pollinate, and lay so many eggs that their larvae destroy most of the seeds.

Even mutualisms contain conflict, which can drive escalation. Darwin suggested that the extraordinarily long spur of *Angraecum* results from a race: the moth benefits from a proboscis long enough to reach the nectar, and the plant from a spur long enough that the moth must press its head into the flower and pick up pollen. Two studies confirmed this logic in other extreme pollination systems. In South Africa, flies (*Moegistorhynchus longirostris*) with longer proboscises obtained more nectar from the iris *Lapeirousia anceps*, and plants with longer floral tubes received more pollen (Pauw et al. 2009); in Ecuador, the bat *Anoura fistulata*, which can extend its tongue twice as far as other nectar-feeding bats, delivered and received more pollen at longer floral tubes (Muchhala and Thomson 2009).

Mutualisms are a source of evolutionary novelty and biochemical complexity. The most important are the endosymbioses that gave rise to mitochondria and plastids (7.10.28). After the union, the genomes of both partners change: chloroplasts retain fewer than 10 percent of the genes of free-living cyanobacteria, and many of the others were transferred to the nucleus, where they may account for as many as 18 percent of the protein-coding genes of *Arabidopsis* (Martin et al. 2002). Animals, which cannot synthesize several amino acids and vitamins, obtain them from microbial partners; sap-sucking insects such as aphids, cicadas and leafhoppers can harbor several coexisting obligate symbionts, each supplying different nutrients, whose genomes become drastically reduced (7.10.18).

## 17.4. Competition

Competition for resources occurs within species and between species that use the same resources. Two species that use exactly the same limiting resources cannot coexist indefinitely, the *competitive exclusion principle*: in the Lotka–Volterra competition equations,
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = r_iN_i\left(1 - \frac{N_i + \alpha_{ij}N_j}{K_i}\right),
$$
stable coexistence requires that each species limit itself more than it limits the other ($\alpha_{12}\alpha_{21} < 1$ when the carrying capacities are similar), which is the stability condition of the interior **equilibrium point** ([2.4.11](/2.4.11)). Competition also imposes selection. Darwin's "principle of divergence of character" held that the descendants of a common ancestor become more different because they then compete less and can occupy more places in the economy of nature.

**Definition 17.4.1 (Character Displacement)** **Character displacement** (ecological character displacement) is the evolutionary divergence of a character between two species in sympatry, caused by competition between them for resources, so that the species differ more where they coexist than where each occurs alone (Brown and Wilson 1956).

If individuals of two species that use the most similar resources compete most intensely, selection within each species favors individuals that use resources less used by the other. The Galápagos ground finches *Geospiza fortis* and *G. fuliginosa* differ more in beak depth, which determines the size and hardness of the seeds they eat, on Santa Cruz, where they coexist, than on islands where each occurs alone (Daphne Major and Los Hermanos). Losos and colleagues introduced the brown anole (*Anolis sagrei*) to some small islands in Florida and not to others: the native green anole (*A. carolinensis*) shifted to higher perches within a few years, and within about 15 years (some 20 generations) evolved larger toepads with more lamellae, adaptations for clinging to thin branches higher in trees; the difference persisted in lizards reared in a common environment, so it is genetic (Stuart et al. 2014).

The converse of character displacement is *ecological release*: a species that lives without its competitors uses a wider range of resources and becomes more variable. Pumpkinseed sunfish (*Lepomis gibbosus*) live in both shallow and open water in lakes where they are the only sunfish, but only in shallow water where another sunfish occupies the open water; open-water and shallow-water pumpkinseeds differ heritably in body shape, feeding structures and diet. Some species compete by interference rather than by depleting resources. American goldenrod (*Solidago canadensis*), accidentally introduced to China, evolved there an increased production of chemicals that inhibit the growth of a Chinese plant species; one explanation is that, freed from many of its natural enemies, the introduced plant reallocated resources from defense to competition.

## 17.5. Evolution and Community Structure

The species that live together in a community are a sample from the clades present in a region, and their evolutionary history affects which can live where. Traits that are retained over long evolutionary periods (**phylogenetic niche conservatism**, 7.10.26) filter species into habitats. All *Heliconius* butterflies require passionflowers as larval food plants and are absent where these plants are absent. In a forest in Borneo, trees growing together in small plots are more closely related than random samples from the whole forest, which suggests that close relatives share adaptations to the local conditions that differ among plots (phylogenetic clustering). Competition and shared enemies have the opposite effect: closely related species compete most strongly and share specialized enemies that kill seeds and seedlings near adults of the same or related species, so close neighbors may be less related than expected (phylogenetic overdispersion). Among 31 species of *Inga* trees in a Peruvian forest, neighboring trees differed in their chemical defenses more than random pairs, and the evolution of different defenses that expose each species to different enemies may contribute to the high diversity of tropical forests.

Evolutionary divergence that reduces competition also structures communities. Among the sympatric members of the weasel family in North America and in Israel, the diameters of the canine teeth, which are used to kill prey and reflect prey size, are spaced more regularly among species and sexes than expected by chance. Where environments are similar, whole communities can converge. Tropical rainforests throughout the world have tall canopy trees laden with epiphytes, and warm deserts have small-leaved shrubs and succulents, each growth form represented by many unrelated lineages (**convergent evolution**). The most striking case is the *Anolis* lizards of the Greater Antilles: on each of the four large islands, a monophyletic radiation has produced the same set of ecomorphs (crown giants, trunk-ground, twig anoles and others) with the same morphology and microhabitat, independently on each island (Losos). Convergence is not universal: the largest islands have unique ecomorphs, and some ecological roles are filled in one region but not in climatically similar ones: blood-feeding vampire bats live in the New World tropics but not in Africa, despite abundant ungulate prey, and sea snakes live in the Indian and Pacific Oceans but not the Atlantic.

## Questions

1. Why are concordant host and symbiont phylogenies, such as those of aphids and *Buchnera*, not sufficient evidence of coevolution? What additional evidence would be needed?

   **Answer** Concordance shows only that the symbiont has not moved between host lineages (here because it is transmitted vertically, from mother to offspring) and has diverged in parallel with its hosts. Coevolution requires reciprocal adaptation: evidence that traits of each partner evolved in response to selection imposed by the other, such as correlated evolution of interacting traits (amino acid supply by the symbiont and host cell specializations), experiments showing selection by each on the other, or signatures of adaptive evolution in genes mediating the interaction.

2. Explain why an arms race is expected when capture depends on the difference between predator and prey traits, but cycles are possible when it depends on their match.

   **Answer** With a difference-based interaction, the prey always gains by increasing its trait and the predator by increasing its own, whatever their current values, so both traits move in the same direction until costs balance the gains: escalation to an equilibrium. With a matching interaction, the prey gains by moving away from the predator's trait in either direction, and the predator by moving toward the prey's. If the prey has moved far, its trait becomes costly, and it may be favored to return; the predator follows with a lag. The lagged pursuit makes the equilibrium unstable when the predator evolves fast enough, and the traits cycle.

3. Two distasteful butterfly species have abundances 200 and 2000, and predators kill 100 individuals of each warning pattern per season while learning. Compare their per capita mortalities with and without Müllerian mimicry.

   **Answer** Without mimicry: $100/200 = 0.5$ for the rare species and $100/2000 = 0.05$ for the common one. With a shared pattern both suffer $100/2200 \approx 0.045$. The rare species' mortality falls elevenfold, the common species' by only about 9 percent: mimicry benefits both, but the rare species much more, which is why convergence is expected mostly in the rarer species.

4. Using Theorem 17.2.1 with $\beta(\alpha) = c\sqrt\alpha$, predict how the optimal virulence changes if the host's natural mortality $\mu$ doubles, and explain the biological reason.

   **Answer** $\alpha^* = \mu + \gamma$, so doubling $\mu$ increases $\alpha^*$ by $\mu$. When hosts die quickly anyway, a parasite gains little by keeping its host alive, so the benefit of faster transmission outweighs the cost of shortening the infection. The model therefore predicts more virulent parasites in hosts with high background mortality.

5. Describe the three mechanisms that stabilize mutualisms against cheaters, and give an example of each.

   **Answer** (i) Sanctions: one partner punishes cheaters, as legumes cut off oxygen to nodules containing non-fixing rhizobia. (ii) Partner choice: one partner preferentially rewards or associates with more beneficial partners, as legumes supply more resources to more beneficial strains. (iii) Aligned interests: when a partner's fitness depends on the other's, as with vertical transmission of *Buchnera* in aphids, the symbiont gains nothing by cheating. When these mechanisms weaken, for example when nitrogen fertilization makes plants independent of rhizobia, less beneficial rhizobia can evolve.
