Evolutionary biology asks two questions about the living world: what its history has been, and what has caused that history. The causes are a small set of processes acting on populations. **Mutation** supplies heritable variation, **natural selection** sorts it by its effect on survival and reproduction and is the only process that produces **adaptation**, **genetic drift** changes allele frequencies by chance in every finite population, and **gene flow** and recombination move and recombine variation in space and in the genome. Which process prevails is rarely decided by the size of one parameter alone; it is decided by its product with the **effective population size**, and the comparison of selection with drift that this implies, the subject of the **neutral theory** and the **nearly neutral theory**, runs through the whole course. The history is the **phylogeny** of all organisms back to the **last universal common ancestor**, with its branchings, its times, its radiations and extinctions. Neither the history nor most of the processes can be observed directly, so a large part of the modern subject is inference: reading genealogies, rates, times and the action of selection back from DNA sequences, the traits of living species and the fossil record. Whether the processes seen in populations over a few generations, **microevolution**, account for the patterns of **macroevolution** over hundreds of millions of years is itself one of its central questions.

The chapters follow that order, from processes to inference to history. After the concepts, history and evidence of the subject, the course reads trees, defines adaptation and asks where variation comes from. The theory of processes is then built up for genomes and traits: selection on one and on many loci, drift and the neutral theory, the **coalescent** as the genealogy of a sample, the tests that detect selection in genomes, the evolution of quantitative traits, and evolution in space under gene flow. With these processes in hand, the next chapters explain the units and the social and sexual life of organisms: species, **reproductive isolation** and **speciation**, **sexual selection** and sex ratios, why sex and recombination exist at all, the evolution of **life histories**, cooperation and conflict under **Hamilton's rule**, and **coevolution** among species; two chapters then follow the evolution of genes and genomes and of developmental programs. The inference block takes up the quantitative molecular phylogenetics that underlies most current work: Markov **substitution models**, the statistical properties of tree methods, Bayesian phylogenetics, **codon models** of adaptive protein evolution, the **molecular clock** and divergence times, the discordance between **gene trees** and **species trees** under the **multispecies coalescent** and through hybridization, and the **comparative method** on trees. The history of life is told last, from the origin of life and the prokaryotes through eukaryotes and multicellularity, the fossil record, biogeography and the dynamics of diversity with its **adaptive radiations** and **mass extinctions**, macroevolution, to the evolution of our own species, and a final thematic chapter turns to evolution in medicine, agriculture and public debate. The course summary sets the processes, their scaled parameters and the methods of inference side by side.

The course builds directly on the earlier biology. The single-locus theory of selection and drift and the quantitative genetics of heritability come from [genetics](/7.4), and are extended here rather than derived again; the algorithms of tree inference, distance methods, parsimony and likelihood, and the processing of population genomic data come from [bioinformatics](/7.8); the developmental genetics behind evo-devo comes from [developmental biology](/7.5); the genome, the RNA world and the cell come from [molecular biology](/7.2) and [cell biology](/7.3); and the models of fitness landscapes and directed evolution from [biophysics](/7.6). The mathematics is that of Markov chains, likelihood, the bootstrap and Markov chain Monte Carlo from [probability](/3.1), [statistical inference](/3.2), [statistical computing](/3.5) and [Bayesian statistics](/3.7), and of the stability of equilibria and diffusion from [ordinary](/2.4) and [partial differential equations](/2.5). In turn, evolution is the frame for the rest of the biology: the phylogenetic classification of the organismal courses, the comparative interpretation of physiology and behavior, and ecology.

I first studied evolution during olympiad training, under the Chinese name *jinhua shengwuxue*. I now resist that name and prefer *yanhua shengwuxue*: *jinhua* suggests advance toward something higher, which is exactly the misreading of evolution that the subject spends much of its effort correcting, while *yanhua* means only change unfolding over time. Besides the standard Chinese olympiad textbook, the book I used most in those years was the Chinese translation of Futuyma, and it was far clearer on molecular phylogenetics than anything else I had. That is the direction in which the field itself has moved. Much of what is now new in evolutionary biology, from dated phylogenies and the multispecies coalescent to scans for selection in genomes and ancient DNA, needs knowledge at the molecular level, and so these pages follow Futuyma as the main line, take Barton as the complement on genomes and development, and use Yang for the statistics of molecular phylogenetics. Tsinghua has no corresponding course; the lectures of the Peking University course PK01130780 served as the check on scope and, for the Chinese pages, on terminology.

Evolution is also where I think one has to be most careful with words. In the molecular and cellular courses before it, a term usually names a molecule or a mechanism that can be pointed to; once biology leaves that level, it deals in concepts that are easy to use loosely and hard to pin down, fitness, adaptation, species, homology, the level at which selection acts, and many of the old controversies of the subject were disputes about what such words meant. These pages therefore define each term where it first appears and hold to that meaning. I regard [microbiology](/7.11), which follows, as the starting point of a systematic understanding of biological diversity, and evolution is the theoretical framework for that understanding: the tree of life, the origin of the prokaryotes and of eukaryotes, and the processes that generate and sort variation are what turn the catalogue of organisms in the later courses into a history.
