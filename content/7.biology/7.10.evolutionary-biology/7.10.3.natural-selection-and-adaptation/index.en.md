A snake can swallow prey larger than its head, because its lower jaws are joined in front only by a stretchable ligament and the bones of both jaws can be moved independently to walk the prey into the throat; in a rattlesnake, the short maxilla carries a single hollow fang that is rotated forward when the mouth opens, a hypodermic needle connected to a modified salivary gland. Features like these look designed for a purpose, and for centuries the argument from design ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.2.1)) took them as evidence of a designer. Darwin's alternative was design by a mindless process: natural selection, which cannot have a goal, any more than erosion has the goal of carving a canyon. The apparent goal-directedness of organisms is the execution of a program, coded in DNA, that was itself shaped by selection.

This chapter explains what natural selection is and what it is not, at which levels it acts, what an adaptation is and how adaptations are recognized, why adaptations are imperfect, and how selection generates diversity. The population-genetic theory of selection, beginning from the one-locus recursion of genetics ([7.4.23](/7.4.23#sec-23.3)), is developed in the next chapters ([7.10.5](/7.10.5), selection on quantitative traits in [7.10.9](/7.10.9)); social behavior and the theory of kin selection in [7.10.16](/7.10.16); here the concepts come first.

## 3.1. Adaptive Evolution Observed

Darwin thought that evolution was too slow to be seen. Today hundreds of cases of adaptive evolution have been observed directly, most rapid where humans have introduced species into new environments or altered their environments.

- *New host plants.* The soapberry bug *Jadera haematoloma* feeds on the seeds of plants of the soapberry family by piercing the fruit with its beak. Within the last several decades, related Asian trees have been planted so widely that many bug populations now feed mainly on them. In Texas the introduced tree has larger fruits than the native host, and the bugs have evolved longer beaks; in Florida the introduced golden rain tree has much smaller fruits, and the bugs have evolved shorter beaks; museum specimens collected over the twentieth century document the change.
- *Resistance.* Hundreds of species of arthropods have evolved resistance to one or more classes of insecticides, many weeds have evolved resistance to herbicides within 10 to 20 years of exposure, and bacteria have evolved resistance to every widely used antibiotic ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)). Grasses and other plants have evolved tolerance of copper, zinc and other heavy metals on soils contaminated by mines less than a century old; in the absence of the metal the tolerant genotypes grow less well in competition than nontolerant ones, so the adaptation has a cost.
- *Harvesting.* Commercial fishing removes the largest fish, and many exploited populations have evolved earlier maturation at a smaller size; in Atlantic cod (*Gadus morhua*) the age at which half the fish mature declined, with a genetic basis, until the fishery was closed in the early 1990s. Trophy hunting of bighorn rams (*Ovis canadensis*) selected against large horns, and mean horn length declined.
- *Climate.* Many insects enter a dormant state (diapause) for the winter when the day length falls below a critical photoperiod; northern populations, which face early winters, respond at longer day lengths. Samples of the pitcher-plant mosquito *Wyeomyia smithii* from Florida to southern Canada between 1972 and 1996 showed that the critical photoperiod became shorter, so that the mosquitoes entered diapause later in autumn as winters grew milder, most in the north, where warming was greatest, and within as little as five years.

Evolution can be this fast because altered environments impose strong selection and because natural populations contain genetic variation in most characteristics ([7.10.4](/7.10.4)).

## 3.2. Natural Selection

### 3.2.1. The Meaning of Natural Selection

Darwin introduced natural selection by analogy with the selection practiced by breeders: if variations useful to an organism "in the great and complex battle of life" occur, and many more individuals are born than can survive, then individuals with any advantage will have the best chance of surviving and reproducing; this "preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection". **Natural selection** was defined for genotypes at a locus in [7.4.23](/7.4.23#def-23.3.2) as the differential survival and reproduction of individuals because of their heritable characteristics. In its most general form, natural selection is any consistent difference in **fitness** among different classes of biological entities, whether alleles, genotypes, individuals, groups or species (Section 3.3).

The simplest measure of the fitness of a type of individual is the average number of offspring it leaves in the next generation. If in an annual plant 1 of every 1000 seeds survives to reproduce, and survivors produce on average 3000 seeds, the fitness of that type is $0.001 \times 3000 = 3$. The **fitness components**, survival and reproduction (including mating success, the subject of **sexual selection**), multiply ([7.10.5](/7.10.5)). Evolution by natural selection then requires two conditions:

1. a consistent relationship between a phenotype and fitness, and
2. inheritance: phenotypic variation is correlated between parents and offspring.

**Formula 3.2.1 (Frequency of a Fitter Type)** If two asexual types A and B, with constant fitnesses $w_A$ and $w_B$ (offspring per individual per generation), start in numbers $N_A(0)$ and $N_B(0)$, then $N_A(t) = N_A(0)w_A^t$, $N_B(t) = N_B(0)w_B^t$, and the frequency of B is
$$
p_t = \frac{N_B(t)}{N_A(t) + N_B(t)} = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{1 - p_0}{p_0}\left(\dfrac{w_A}{w_B}\right)^t}.
$$

Only the ratio $w_A/w_B$, the **relative fitness**, appears in the frequency: what matters for evolution is not whether a population grows, but which types grow faster.

**Example 3.2.1** Genotypes A and B of an annual plant have fitnesses 3 and 4 (B is resistant to a herbicide, A susceptible), and each starts with 10 individuals. After 7 generations there are $10 \times 3^7 = 21\,870$ A and $10 \times 4^7 = 163\,840$ B plants, and the frequency of B has risen from $0.5$ to $1/(1 + 0.75^7) = 0.88$.

A: numbers of individuals on a log scale; both grow, B faster. B: the frequency of B rises from 0.5 to 0.88 in 7 generations (dots), as given by Formula 3.2.1 (lines)](./plots/genotype-growth.py)

In sexual organisms genotypes are broken up by segregation and recombination in each generation, whereas genes are copied; it is therefore often useful to speak of the fitness of an allele and of selection among genes (Section 3.3). Natural selection also works on traits inherited epigenetically or culturally, to the extent that they are transmitted ([7.10.4](/7.10.4#sec-4.7)), but it has no lasting effect without inheritance.

Natural selection is not an agent and has no purpose. Darwin, criticized for speaking of selection "as an active power or Deity", answered that this was a metaphor like the attraction of gravity "ruling" the planets. To say that selection "favors" a trait, or to call selection a "force", is shorthand: natural selection is a name for statistical differences in reproductive success, and nothing more.

### 3.2.2. Selection and Chance

Natural selection is not synonymous with evolution. Selection can occur without evolutionary change, when it keeps a population at an optimum by eliminating deviants (stabilizing selection, [7.10.9](/7.10.9)), and evolution can occur without selection, by **genetic drift**, random fluctuations of allele frequencies ([7.4.23](/7.4.23#sec-23.5), [7.10.6](/7.10.6)). When a neutral allele increases by drift, its carriers have, by chance, left more offspring, but there has been no selection, because there is no consistent difference: the other allele could equally have been the one to increase. Fitness differences are differences in expectation, biases in the probability of reproductive success. In this sense natural selection is the antithesis of chance, and the two can be distinguished only statistically, by measuring many individuals of each type and finding a consistent difference in reproductive success.

### 3.2.3. The Environment Depends on the Organism

The factors that impose selection depend on the organism itself. The branching structure of trees in a forest matters to a tree-nesting oriole but hardly to a partridge that nests on the ground; the viscosity of water, which changes with temperature, matters to a ciliate but hardly to a fish. Organisms construct their own niches, literally (a beaver's dam) or by their choice of habitat, and their history determines which aspects of the environment they depend on. Army ants rely almost entirely on chemical signals and have compound eyes reduced to a single facet; burrowing blind snakes perceive light but form no image; humans, who came to rely on vision, have lost the function of many olfactory receptor genes. When a function is no longer used, selection that maintained it is relaxed, and the feature degenerates.

## 3.3. Levels of Selection

### 3.3.1. A Hierarchy of Units

By natural selection, Darwin and most later biologists meant differences in fitness among individual organisms within populations. But the general definition applies to any entities that vary and change in number.

**Definition 3.3.1 (Levels of Selection)** The **levels of selection** are the levels of biological organization at which natural selection can act: genes, cells, individual organisms, groups of organisms, species. **Genic selection** is selection among alleles within an organism or a population by their effects on their own transmission; **individual selection** is selection among individual organisms within a population; **group selection** (interdemic selection) is the differential production or survival of groups that differ in genetic composition; **species selection** is the differential speciation or extinction of species that differ in a heritable characteristic.

Selection at different levels can act in different directions, and the outcome depends on their relative strengths. The usual argument for the primacy of genic and individual selection is quantitative: genes and individuals are far more numerous than groups or species and turn over much faster, so selection among them is much stronger.

### 3.3.2. Selfish Genes

Genic selection is seen most clearly in **selfish genetic elements**, which are transmitted at a higher rate than the rest of the genome and may be harmful to the organism ([7.4.17](/7.4.17#def-17.6.1)). Transposable elements multiply within genomes whether or not this helps their hosts. Alleles subject to **meiotic drive** are transmitted to more than half of a heterozygote's gametes: in a male house mouse heterozygous for a *t* haplotype, the *t* chromosome disables the sperm carrying the normal chromosome, so that more than 90% of functional sperm carry *t*, and *t* reaches high frequencies in wild populations although *t/t* homozygotes die or are sterile ([7.4.17](/7.4.17#def-17.6.2)). Such elements show that natural selection is nothing more than differential reproduction, here of genes: it need not produce adaptation or improvement in any sense, and genic selection can oppose individual selection and might even drive a population extinct.

In a wider sense every allele that has increased in frequency is a "selfish gene", in Richard Dawkins's phrase: an allele spreads if it leaves more copies of itself, whatever the means. The view explains behavior that seems unselfish. J. B. S. Haldane noted in 1932 that the workers of a beehive, which do not reproduce, carry the same genes as the young queens, so that a behavior of workers, "however suicidal", that benefits the hive can spread. An allele for altruism can increase if its beneficiaries are relatives who carry copies of the same allele by common descent; this is **kin selection**, of which parental care is the simplest example, and whose theory (Hamilton's rule) is developed in [7.10.16](/7.10.16).

### 3.3.3. Group Selection

Do oysters produce many eggs "to ensure the survival of the species"? Do lemmings drown themselves to relieve overpopulation, as a popular myth holds? If traits evolve by the replacement of less fit by fitter individuals, the future extinction of the species cannot affect the process, which has no foresight. A trait that benefits the population at a cost to its bearer is opposed by individual selection: within any group of altruists, a selfish mutant (a "cheater") leaves more offspring and increases.

Such a trait could evolve only by group selection, if groups of altruists survive or found new groups at a higher rate than groups of selfish individuals. V. C. Wynne-Edwards (1962) argued that many animals limit their reproduction for the good of the population. George C. Williams (*Adaptation and Natural Selection*, 1966) answered that the supposed adaptations for the good of the group are better explained by benefits to individuals or their genes, or are not adaptations at all (females lay fewer eggs at high density because they cannot form more, not to conserve food), and that individual selection, with its far higher turnover, generally prevails. Most evolutionary biologists accept Williams's view: few features have evolved because they benefit the population or the species, and cooperation is usually explained by kin selection or by benefits to the cooperators themselves.

The arithmetic of selection within and between groups can be made explicit. In a trait-group model, the population forms groups of $n$ individuals at random each generation; an altruist pays a cost $c$ in fitness and adds $b/n$ to the fitness of each member of its group, itself included; then all offspring mix and form new groups. Within every group the altruists lose ground, but groups with more altruists produce more offspring. Summing over the binomial distribution of group compositions shows that altruism spreads only if $b/n > c$, that is, if the benefit an altruist confers on itself through its own group exceeds its cost: in large groups it is lost (figure, panel A). The case $b/n > c$ also shows a statistical point, Simpson's paradox: the fraction of altruists can fall in every group and still rise in the population as a whole (panel B). The general partition of selection into within- and between-group components (the Price equation) and its relation to kin selection are developed in [7.10.16](/7.10.16).

### 3.3.4. Species Selection

Selection among species does not shape the adaptations of organisms, but it does change the distribution of characteristics among the species of a clade. If species with one state of a character speciate more often or go extinct less often than species with another, the proportion of species with the first state increases over time, even if no lineage has any tendency to change in that direction. A likely example is the predominance of sexual reproduction: many groups of plants and animals have given rise to asexual lineages, but most asexual lineages are young, closely related to sexual relatives, which implies that they go extinct faster ([7.10.14](/7.10.14)). Another hypothetical example is body size: if large-bodied lineages go extinct less often, the average body size of the species in a clade increases, as in the simulation below, without any selection for larger size within species. ## 3.4. The Nature of Adaptations

### 3.4.1. Adaptation

**Definition 3.4.1 (Adaptation)** **Adaptation** is the evolutionary process by which, over generations, organisms become improved with respect to features that affect survival or reproduction. *An* adaptation is a characteristic that evolved by natural selection for a particular function: a derived character state that conferred higher fitness than the ancestral state from which it evolved.

Both senses are hard to define precisely, but the definition contains the essentials: an adaptation is a product of selection, it has a function, and it is identified by comparison with the ancestral state it replaced. A feature that merely happens to be useful is not, by this definition, an adaptation for that use.

**Definition 3.4.2 (Preadaptation and Exaptation)** A **preadaptation** is a feature that, by chance, can serve a new function before it has been modified for it. A feature that has been co-opted for a new function during evolution is an **exaptation** for that function (the term was introduced by Stephen Jay Gould and Elisabeth Vrba in 1982).

When sheep were introduced into New Zealand, the kea (*Nestor notabilis*), a parrot whose strong, sharp beak evolved for feeding on seeds and fruits, began to tear the skin of living sheep and feed on their fat: its beak was preadapted for this. The wings of auks, used both to fly and to "fly" under water, are exaptations for swimming; the wings of penguins, which have been modified into stiff flippers that cannot support flight, are adaptations for swimming. Exaptation, the **co-option** of an existing structure ([7.5.39](/7.5.39#def-39.2.2)), is a very common first stage of a new adaptation, and it answers the old objection that the first steps toward a complex organ could have had no use ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.4.1)).

### 3.4.2. Selection of and Selection for

To call a feature adaptive is incomplete without saying what it is adaptive for: by what mechanism did it increase fitness? The philosopher Elliott Sober illustrated the distinction with a child's toy, a tube divided by partitions with holes that are smaller at each level. Balls of several sizes placed at the top fall through, and only the smallest reach the bottom. If the smallest balls are all red, the toy selects red balls, but it selects them *for* their small size, not for their color. There is selection *of* red balls and selection *for* small size.

Natural selection, likewise, selects for particular properties and incidentally selects other features correlated with them, through genetic linkage, **pleiotropy** or developmental connection. The function of a feature is the effect for which it was selected; its other consequences are effects, not functions. A fish may be selected for coloration that makes it inconspicuous to predators; the function of the coloration is predator avoidance, and if the population as a consequence becomes less likely to go extinct, that is an effect, not a cause of its evolution.

### 3.4.3. Cumulative Selection

A recurrent objection is that selection creates nothing new, but only sifts the variants already present. With more than a few genes the objection fails, because the number of possible combinations is astronomically larger than any population. If two populations differ at 20 loci, at each of which one allele slightly increases fitness, the fittest diploid genotype is a particular combination of 40 alleles and occurs in an F$_2$ with frequency $2^{-40}$, less than one in $10^{12}$. Selection does not pick out this combination from the start; it raises the frequency of each favorable allele, one generation at a time, and as they become common, combinations of many of them become common too. In the simulation below the fittest genotype starts at a frequency of about $10^{-26}$ and makes up more than half of the population within about 65 generations. The same holds when variation arises by mutation: as one favorable mutant lineage increases, further mutations arise within it. Cumulative selection explains how highly improbable, complex adaptations are assembled, and it is why selection, applied by engineers to molecules in vitro or to programs in evolutionary computation, often finds solutions that rational design does not.

In a haploid population, a favorable allele at each of 20 freely recombining loci starts at frequency 0.05 and multiplies fitness by 1 + s (linkage equilibrium assumed; three values of s). A: the frequency of the favorable allele at one locus, which follows p_t = p_0 e^{st}/(1 − p_0 + p_0 e^{st}). B: the frequency of the genotype that carries all 20 favorable alleles, p_t^20, on a log scale. Initially about 10^−26, a combination that would never be found by sampling any real population, it makes up more than half of the population within about 65 generations for s = 0.1](./plots/cumulative-selection.py)

The examples also show why natural selection is the only process that systematically produces adaptation. Mutation, recombination, migration and drift are random with respect to function and, on average, degrade what selection has built; complex structures can arise by physical self-organization (crystals, the self-assembly of a virus capsid), but they function to aid reproduction only to the extent that they have been shaped by selection.

## 3.5. Recognizing Adaptations

Not every feature is an adaptation. A characteristic may instead be:

1. *a consequence of physics or chemistry*: blood is red because of the structure of hemoglobin, not because redness was selected (although the color has been co-opted, for example in the red, bulging eyes that the Australian white-winged chough displays in aggression);
2. *the result of genetic drift* rather than selection ([7.10.6](/7.10.6));
3. *a correlated effect* of selection on another feature, through linkage or pleiotropy (Section 3.4.2);
4. *a consequence of phylogenetic history*: Darwin noted that the sutures of the skulls of young mammals help in birth, but occur also in young birds and reptiles, which only have to escape from an egg, so they did not evolve for that function; it is not sensible to ask why hummingbirds have four toes rather than five, because the ancestor of birds lost the fifth toe and no bird has regained it.

For these reasons many evolutionary biologists, following Williams, hold that a feature should not be called an adaptation unless the evidence favors it, while recognizing that a great many features, probably most, are adaptations. Several kinds of evidence are used.

- *Complexity.* Complex structures cannot arise except by selection, so a complex feature is suspected of having a function even before the function is known. The pecten, a highly vascularized structure that projects into the vitreous body of the bird eye, was the subject of about 30 hypotheses; it was finally shown to supply oxygen to the retina.
- *Design.* A feature that matches the design an engineer would use for a task, or the predictions of a model of its function, is probably an adaptation for that task. Plants of hot environments often have finely divided leaves, or leaves that tear along fracture lines (bananas); small surfaces shed the hot boundary layer of air more readily than large ones, so these leaves stay cooler. Optimality models of this kind are the method of functional morphology and ecological physiology, and of the theory of life histories and behavior ([7.10.15](/7.10.15), [7.10.16](/7.10.16)).
- *Experiments.* Many lineages of plants have shifted from bee pollination to hummingbird pollination and convergently evolved flowers with a reduced lower lip, protruding anthers and a narrow corolla tube. In an experiment, flowers of a bee-pollinated *Penstemon* were surgically altered to resemble those of hummingbird-pollinated species, and pollen transfer by bees and hummingbirds was measured: removing the lower lip, the bees' landing platform, discouraged bees; projecting anthers were also an "anti-bee" feature; and the narrow tube both made hummingbirds remove more pollen and kept bees from the nectar.
Comparison among species is the fourth, and most widely used, approach.

**Definition 3.5.1 (Comparative Method)** The **comparative method** infers the adaptive significance of a feature, or tests hypotheses about it, by comparing species: if a feature has evolved independently in many lineages exposed to the same selective factor, the correlation between the feature and the factor across lineages indicates the feature's function.

Fast-swimming predators of open water from many families have a streamlined body, a forked tail fin and a slender tail base, whereas fishes that maneuver among corals or vegetation have deep, compressed bodies. In species in which a female mates with several males, the males' sperm compete to fertilize her eggs, so males that produce more sperm should be favored; since sperm production is proportional to the size of the testes, polygamous primates should have relatively larger testes than monogamous ones. Paul Harvey and Mark Pagel compiled published data and found exactly this. The data had existed before; no one had compiled them until the hypothesis predicted what they would show. Two cautions apply. Species are not independent data points, because related species share features by descent; the comparison must count independent evolutionary origins, which requires a phylogeny (phylogenetic comparative methods, [7.10.26](/7.10.26)). And a correlation shows association, not the mechanism, which needs functional or experimental study.

The hypothetico-deductive method, of which Darwin was one of the first effective users, is central to all of these approaches: predictions of evolutionary theory are usually predictions of what we will find when we collect data, not of the future course of evolution ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.5.2)).

## 3.6. Imperfection and Constraints

"Natural selection will not produce absolute perfection", Darwin wrote. Selection can fix only the variants that are present, and those that arise are limited by several constraints.

**Definition 3.6.1 (Trade-off)** A **trade-off** is a negative relationship between two components of fitness or two traits, such that an improvement in one is possible only at the cost of the other, because they draw on the same limited resource or are coupled by genetics or development.

With a fixed amount of energy and nutrients, a plant can evolve more seeds only by making them smaller, and genotypes that differ in allocation show a negative correlation between seed number and seed size ([7.10.15](/7.10.15)). The metal-tolerant grasses of Section 3.1 grow less well than nontolerant ones on normal soils. Trade-offs mean that optimal features depend on the context and that no organism is best at everything.

Adaptation may also be limited by the absence of suitable mutations. In large samples of seeds from populations far from copper mines, a small proportion of copper-tolerant seedlings was found in every one of eight grass species that have evolved tolerance near mines, and none in seven species that have not: those species apparently lacked the variation needed. A lack of genetic variation can also explain phylogenetic constraints, features retained although they seem nonadaptive. Birds such as swans have more neck vertebrae than short-necked birds, as one would expect; but almost all mammals, from the giraffe to the dugong, have seven neck vertebrae (sloths and manatees are the exceptions). Mice and humans with an abnormal number of cervical vertebrae have skeletal defects and high rates of embryonic cancers, side effects that have probably prevented the change in other mammals. Such **developmental constraints** ([7.5.39](/7.5.39#def-39.6.1)) and the genetic correlations among traits ([7.10.9](/7.10.9)) limit and channel the direction of adaptive evolution.

## 3.7. Natural Selection and Diversity

The optimal state of a feature depends on the circumstances in which it functions, and because environments vary immensely, selection produces diversity. In many animals, darker populations live on darker rocks than paler populations, a difference in camouflage that in the rock pocket mouse is due to a single gene, *Mc1r* ([7.10.9](/7.10.9)). Different parts of the human body are different environments for bacteria, which support different communities of species.

Darwin also proposed that competition makes related species diverge: where related species coexist, individuals that use resources the others do not use suffer less competition, so the species diverge. Divergence caused by the interaction of species is now called **character displacement** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Peter and Rosemary Grant and their colleagues studied ground finches of the Galápagos for more than 35 years. Finches with larger, deeper bills feed more efficiently on large, hard seeds, and the species that coexist on each island differ in bill size in accordance with the seeds available. On Daphne Major, a drought in 1977 that made small seeds scarce killed mostly *Geospiza fortis* with small bills, and the mean bill size of the next generation increased. Years later the large ground finch *G. magnirostris*, with the large bill its name describes, colonized the island and increased until, in a drought in 2004, it depleted the large seeds; *G. fortis* then evolved a smaller mean bill, as Darwin would have predicted. Such divergence in resource use is the first stage of the **adaptive radiations** described in [7.10.2](/7.10.2#def-2.6.2).

## 3.8. What Not to Expect of Natural Selection

- *No foresight.* Selection acts on present differences in reproduction; it cannot prepare a population for future conditions or avoid extinction. Features that seem to anticipate the future (trees that drop their leaves before winter) evolved because, in past generations, individuals responding to present cues (day length) reproduced more.
- *Not for the good of the species.* Selection at the level of genes and individuals is inherently "selfish"; behaviors that harm members of the same species, from territorial defense to infanticide and parasitism, are common, and cooperation requires special explanation ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)).
- *Not for the good of other species.* "Natural selection cannot possibly produce any modification in a species exclusively for the good of another species" (Darwin). Bees pollinate flowers because they obtain food from them; male bees that try to copulate with flowers of the orchid *Ophrys*, which mimic a female bee's scent and appearance, are duped.
- *No balance of nature.* Predators and prey coexist not because predators restrain themselves but because prey are defended well enough to persist or predators are limited by other factors; the rapid recycling of nutrients in tropical forests is the aggregate effect of microorganisms selected to decompose litter quickly and of plants selected to capture the released nutrients, not a property for which ecosystems were selected.
- *No perfection and no progress.* Adaptations are limited by trade-offs, history and the available variation (Section 3.6), and selection does not lead toward greater complexity; parasites and cave animals lose features that are no longer needed.
- *No morality.* Natural selection is not moral or immoral, kind or cruel, any more than wind or erosion, and it provides no model for human ethics ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.6)).

## Summary

- Adaptive evolution has been observed many times, especially where humans change environments: soapberry bugs, pesticide and metal resistance (with costs), fisheries-induced evolution, a mosquito tracking climate change.
- Natural selection is any consistent difference in fitness among classes of entities; evolution by selection requires a phenotype–fitness relationship and inheritance. The frequency of the fitter type rises according to the ratio of fitnesses (Formula 3.2.1, Example 3.2.1). Selection is the antithesis of chance, not an agent, and not the same as evolution.
- Selection acts at several levels (Definition 3.3.1). Selfish genetic elements show genic selection opposing individual selection; kin selection explains altruism toward relatives; group selection is usually weak because groups turn over slowly, and in a trait-group model altruism spreads only if $b/n > c$; species selection changes the distribution of traits among species without any change within them.
- An adaptation (Definition 3.4.1) is a derived feature that evolved by selection for a function; a preadaptation is fortuitously useful, an exaptation co-opted (Definition 3.4.2). Selection is *for* properties and *of* the objects that have them; cumulative selection assembles improbable combinations step by step.
- Not all features are adaptations (physics, drift, correlated effects, history). Adaptations are recognized by complexity, design, experiment and the comparative method (Definition 3.5.1), which requires phylogenetic information.
- Adaptations are imperfect because of trade-offs (Definition 3.6.1), lack of variation and developmental and phylogenetic constraints.
- Because optimal features depend on context, selection generates diversity; competition causes character displacement, the beginning of adaptive radiation. Selection has no foresight, works for no species' or community's good, does not seek perfection or progress, and carries no moral content.

## Questions

1. A single mutation for insecticide resistance arises in a population of insects exposed to the insecticide. Is the mutation an adaptation? If most of the population is resistant some generations later, is resistance an adaptation?

   **Answer** The newly arisen mutation is not an adaptation: it arose by chance, independently of its usefulness ([7.10.4](/7.10.4#sec-4.5)), and has not yet been shaped by selection; at most it is a preadaptation. Once it has increased because resistant individuals left more offspring in the presence of the insecticide, resistance is an adaptation by Definition 3.4.1: a derived state that spread by natural selection for the function of surviving the insecticide.

2. Use Formula 3.2.1 to find how many generations it takes for a type with relative fitness $1.01$ to increase from a frequency of $0.01$ to $0.99$.

   **Answer** From Formula 3.2.1, $\dfrac{p_t}{1 - p_t} = \dfrac{p_0}{1 - p_0}\left(\dfrac{w_B}{w_A}\right)^t$, so $t = \dfrac{\ln\bigl(\frac{0.99}{0.01}\big/\frac{0.01}{0.99}\bigr)}{\ln 1.01} = \dfrac{2\ln 99}{0.00995} \approx 924$ generations. A 1% advantage, too small to measure in a field study, carries a type almost to fixation in under a thousand generations, a moment in geological time (compare [Formula 23.3.2 in 7.4.23](/7.4.23#fml-23.3.2)).

3. Why does the argument of Williams against group selection rely on the relative rates of turnover of individuals and groups? Under what conditions could group selection be effective?

   **Answer** An altruistic allele declines within each group at a rate set by individual selection, every generation; it can increase only through the extinction or proliferation of groups, which happens much more rarely, and migrants from selfish groups can invade altruistic ones. Group selection therefore prevails only if groups differ strongly in composition and turn over fast relative to the rate at which cheaters spread within them: small groups, little migration between them, strong differences in group productivity or extinction. In the trait-group model of Section 3.3.3 random grouping creates such differences only in very small groups ($b/n > c$); assortment of relatives into groups increases them, which is why group selection and kin selection are two descriptions of the same process in many models ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)).

4. Distinguish selection of and selection for in this case: in a population of moths, selection by birds favors darker individuals on soot-darkened trees, and the allele for dark coloration is closely linked to an allele that lowers fertility at high temperatures.

   **Answer** There is selection for dark coloration, the property that causes higher survival against bird predation. Because of linkage, there is also selection of the low-fertility allele, which hitchhikes with the coloration allele (genetic hitchhiking, [7.10.5](/7.10.5)); its increase is an effect of selection, not something selected for, and lowered fertility at high temperature is not a function. Recombination will gradually separate the alleles if the linkage is not complete.

5. Explain why species selection can change the mean body size of species in a clade even if, within every lineage, larger and smaller size are equally likely to evolve.

   **Answer** If extinction rates decrease (or speciation rates increase) with body size, large-bodied lineages persist longer and produce more descendant species, so the proportion of large-bodied species among the living ones increases over time, as in the simulation of Section 3.3.4. Each lineage changes size by unbiased random steps; the trend is in the distribution of species, caused by differential survival and multiplication of species, not by selection within species. The trend therefore does not imply that larger size is an adaptation of individuals.
