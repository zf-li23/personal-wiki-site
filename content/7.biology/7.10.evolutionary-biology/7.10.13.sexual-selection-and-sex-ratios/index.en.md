Most animals and plants reproduce sexually, and in most of them sex comes in two forms. This chapter asks two questions about the sexes as products of evolution. First, why do the sexes so often differ in appearance and behavior, and why are traits such as the peacock's train, which seem to reduce survival, so common? Darwin's answer was **sexual selection**, selection that arises from competition for mates; it explains much of the most conspicuous diversity of animals and many features of flowers. Second, in what proportions should the two sexes be produced? Fisher's argument that the sex ratio is held near equality by frequency-dependent selection, and Hamilton's extension to local competition between relatives, are among the most quantitatively successful predictions in evolutionary biology.

The chapter builds on single-locus selection and **frequency-dependent selection** ([7.4.23](/7.4.23#sec-23.3.2)), on **linkage disequilibrium** ([7.4.22](/7.4.22#sec-22.3)) and on the genetics of correlated traits (7.10.9). The mechanisms of **sex determination** are those of [7.4.6](/7.4.6#sec-6.1). The next chapter asks the prior question of why sex exists at all; kin selection, which reappears in the theory of sex ratios, is developed in 7.10.16, and the coevolution of hosts and parasites, which links sexual selection to disease, in 7.10.17.

## 13.1. Females and Males

### 13.1.1. Anisogamy

In plants, animals and many other eukaryotes, the gametes of a species come in two sizes: a large, immobile egg (or ovule) and a small, mobile sperm (or pollen grain). This dimorphism, rather than any trait of the adult, is what defines the two sexes.

**Definition 13.1.1 (Anisogamy)** **Anisogamy** is the production of gametes of two different sizes by the members of a species. Individuals (or gonads) that make the small gametes are *male*, and those that make the large gametes are *female*. Species whose gametes are all of one size are *isogamous*; they usually still have two or more *mating types*, and only gametes of different types fuse.

Why should gametes have diverged in size at all? A gamete can succeed in two ways: by being large and well provisioned, which gives a large zygote with a high chance of survival, or by being small, which allows many to be made from the same resources and so increases the chance of finding a partner. The theory of Parker, Baker and Smith (1972) shows that if zygote survival increases more than proportionally with zygote size (accelerating returns), selection on gamete size is disruptive: intermediate gametes are neither numerous enough nor large enough, and the population splits into producers of small and of large gametes. The small gametes then become a parasite on the provisioning of the large ones. The comparative evidence fits: among the colonial green algae of the Volvocales, species with small colonies tend to be isogamous, while those with large, differentiated colonies, which need a large zygote, have small motile sperm and large eggs.

Anisogamy is ancient and evolved independently in several eukaryote lineages; the "female" of a bird and of a flowering plant are not homologous, only analogous products of the same selective logic. Once the sexes exist, they may be combined in one individual or separated. Many plants and animals are hermaphrodites, producing both kinds of gametes, either simultaneously (the leopard slug *Limax maximus*, most flowering plants) or sequentially: the slipper shell *Crepidula fornicata* and the clownfishes (*Amphiprion*) change sex as they grow (7.10.15). An important advantage of hermaphroditism is **reproductive assurance** when mates are scarce, which is one reason why plants, which cannot search for a mate, are hermaphroditic more often than animals (7.10.14). In species with separate sexes, the sex of an embryo is set by sex chromosomes (XY in mammals, ZW in birds), by **haplodiploidy** (all Hymenoptera) or by the environment, as in the **temperature-dependent sex determination** of many reptiles ([7.4.6](/7.4.6#sec-6.1.3)).

### 13.1.2. Bateman's Principle and the Operational Sex Ratio

Because a male makes many small gametes, he can often fertilize many females, and his reproductive success rises with the number of his mates. A female can often fertilize all her eggs in a single mating; her success is limited by the resources she can put into eggs and offspring, not by the number of her partners.

**Definition 13.1.2 (Bateman's Principle)** **Bateman's principle** is the generalization that the reproductive success of males increases more steeply with the number of mates than does that of females, so that males have the greater variance in reproductive success and are under stronger sexual selection. The slope of the regression of reproductive success on mating success is called the *Bateman gradient*.

Bateman (1948) inferred the principle from experiments with *Drosophila melanogaster* in which the parents of each offspring could be identified by genetic markers: the number of offspring sired by a male increased with the number of females he mated, while the number produced by a female hardly depended on the number of her mates. The difference in variance is large in natural populations. In the red deer (*Cervus elaphus*) of the Isle of Rum, the variance in lifetime reproductive success is 22.0 among males and 6.4 among females; the difference comes mostly from breeding success among adults (variances 51.3 and 8.8). Because the variance in relative fitness sets an upper bound on the rate of evolution by selection (the *opportunity for selection*, $I = \operatorname{Var}(w)/\bar w^2$), the sex with the larger variance can evolve faster under sexual selection.

What matters is not the numbers of males and females present but the numbers ready to mate at a given moment.

**Definition 13.1.3 (Operational Sex Ratio)** The **operational sex ratio** is the ratio of sexually active males to fertilizable females in a population at a given time. The sex with the higher *potential reproductive rate* (the number of offspring an individual could produce per unit time if mates were unlimited) is in excess in the mating pool and competes for the other.

Females are often unavailable while they develop eggs, gestate or care for young; males can usually remate quickly. The operational sex ratio is therefore usually male-biased, even when the adult sex ratio is equal, and males compete for females. Social monogamy does not abolish the competition: in socially monogamous passerine birds more than 10 percent of the offspring are often sired by males other than the social mate, and extra-pair matings have been found in about 90 percent of the bird species examined.

The exceptions confirm the rule. In the red phalarope (*Phalaropus fulicarius*) the male incubates the eggs and raises the chicks; the female, freed from care, can lay a second clutch with another male. Females are larger and more brightly colored than males and compete for them. In seahorses the male broods the young in a pouch, and females court males. In such cases of *sex-role reversal* the female has the higher potential reproductive rate, Bateman's principle is reversed, and it is the females that carry the exaggerated traits.

## 13.2. Sexual Selection

### 13.2.1. Darwin's Theory

Primary sexual traits, the gonads and genitalia, are necessary for reproduction and pose no puzzle. Darwin noticed a second class of traits that differ between the sexes without a direct role in fertilization: the antlers of stags, the train of the peacock, the songs of birds and frogs. These secondary sexual traits are often costly, are expressed mostly in males and often only in the breeding season, and are used in contests with other males or in courtship. They are also among the fastest-evolving characters, and in many groups they are the only traits that reliably distinguish closely related species.

**Definition 13.2.1 (Sexual Selection)** **Sexual selection** is selection that arises from differences among individuals of the same sex in their success in obtaining mates or fertilizations. Darwin distinguished *intrasexual selection*, by competition between members of one sex (usually males) for access to the other, and *intersexual selection*, by the choice of mates by members of the other sex (usually females).

Sexual selection can favor traits that reduce survival if the gain in mating success compensates. Both halves of this argument have been tested. In the long-tailed widowbird (*Euplectes progne*) of the African savanna, Andersson (1982) shortened the tails of some territorial males and glued the clipped feathers onto the tails of others; males with lengthened tails attracted more than twice as many nesting females as controls or males with shortened tails. In the túngara frog (*Physalaemus pustulosus*), males add "chucks" to a basic whine call; females prefer calls with chucks, whose frequencies match the sensitivity of their inner ear, but the fringe-lipped bat (*Trachops cirrhosus*), which hunts by listening, also finds males that chuck more easily. When the strength of natural selection changes, so does the compromise: after a parasitoid fly (*Ormia ochracea*) that locates calling crickets arrived on Kauai, males of the field cricket *Teleogryllus oceanicus* evolved, within about 20 generations, wings that cannot produce song.

**Example 13.2.1 (A Compromise Between Survival and Mating Success)** Let a male trait $z$ have survival $S(z) = \exp[-(z - \theta)^2/(2\omega^2)]$, maximal at the naturally selected optimum $\theta$, and mating success $M(z) = \exp(\alpha z)$, which increases with $z$ when females prefer large values ($\alpha > 0$). Lifetime fitness $W = SM$ is maximal where $\mathrm{d}\ln W/\mathrm{d}z = -(z - \theta)/\omega^2 + \alpha = 0$, that is, at
$$
z^* = \theta + \alpha\omega^2 .
$$
The trait is exaggerated beyond the survival optimum by an amount that increases with the strength of the preference and with the width $\omega$ of the survival function (the weaker natural selection). If females preferred small values ($\alpha < 0$), the trait would evolve below $\theta$. This is the logic of the widowbird and túngara frog examples: the equilibrium is a balance, and it shifts when either kind of selection changes.

### 13.2.2. Competition Between Males

Males of many species fight for females or for the resources females need, and have evolved weapons for the purpose. Red deer stags use their antlers in dangerous fights; about 20 percent of stags suffer permanent injuries, but winners guard harems, and while nearly half of all males fail to reproduce in a given year, the most successful father many offspring. Male stag beetles (*Lucanus cervus*) defend sites where females lay eggs. The ecology of the resources determines how strong such selection is: when females or the resources they need are clumped in space or time, a few males can monopolize them, and sexual selection is intense.

There is often more than one way to win. *Alternative mating strategies* are different tactics used by males of the same species to obtain matings. In the marine isopod *Paracerceis sculpta*, males come in three genetically determined morphs: large alpha males guard harems in sponge cavities, beta males mimic females and slip into the harems, and tiny gamma males sneak past the guards. The three morphs have about equal reproductive success, a polymorphism maintained by **negative frequency-dependent selection**. Male side-blotched lizards (*Uta stansburiana*) have three throat-color morphs: orange males hold large territories, blue males guard their mates in small territories, and yellow males mimic females and sneak matings in orange males' territories. Orange beats blue, yellow beats orange, and blue beats yellow, a "rock–paper–scissors" relation; measured from genetic paternity, the morph frequencies cycled during the 1990s. Games of this kind are analyzed in 7.10.16.

Competition continues after mating.

**Definition 13.2.2 (Sperm Competition)** **Sperm competition** is the competition between the ejaculates of two or more males to fertilize the eggs of one female (in plants, *pollen competition* among pollen grains on one stigma).

Male damselflies (*Calopteryx*) have a scoop on the penis that removes the sperm of earlier mates before the male deposits his own; other males guard the female after mating, insert a copulatory plug, or simply produce more sperm. Across primates, species in which females mate with several males have larger testes for their body size than monogamous species or species with single-male groups. Among the most brutal outcomes of male–male competition is infanticide: when new males take over a pride of lions they often kill the cubs, which removes the offspring of their rivals and brings the females back into breeding condition sooner.

### 13.2.3. Mate Choice

Many male displays play no role in fights and work only by attracting females. In this context "choice" means any property of a female that makes her more likely to mate with some males than with others, whether or not a nervous system is involved; a preference can be as simple as the spectral sensitivity of the eye. That females do bias mating has been shown in many species, and genetic variation in mating preferences has been found by heritability experiments. Female preferences can evolve for several reasons, which differ in whether the preference itself is under selection.

The simplest reason is direct benefits. Males of many species give their mates food, nest sites, protection or parental care, and a female that chooses a male who provides more of these leaves more offspring. Male crickets and katydids transfer a spermatophore rich in lipids and carbohydrates, which the female eats and converts into eggs; in the Mormon cricket (*Anabrus simplex*) it is so large that females can live on it. After copulation, male redback spiders (*Latrodectus hasselti*) often somersault into the female's jaws, and males that are eaten fertilize more eggs than those that are not. Selection here acts directly on the genes for preference, through the female's own fecundity.

Second, preferences can be side effects of sensory systems that evolved for other functions.

**Definition 13.2.3 (Sensory Bias)** **Sensory bias** (perceptual bias) is a preexisting preference of the receiver, arising from selection on the sensory system in another context (foraging, predator detection) or from how signals propagate through the environment, which a new male signal can exploit.

The strongest evidence comes from phylogenies in which the preference is older than the trait. Male swordtails (*Xiphophorus*) carry a long sword on the tail that females prefer; females of *Priapella olmecae*, a related species whose males lack a sword, also prefer males to which a sword has been surgically added (Basolo), and the phylogeny shows that the preference arose before the sword. In the genus *Physalaemus*, females of *P. coloradorum* prefer calls with chucks although their own males do not produce them, so the preference evolved before the trait. Female guppies (*Poecilia reticulata*) prefer males with more orange, and both sexes peck at small orange objects, perhaps because guppies feed on orange fruit. Because colors transmit differently through water of different depth and turbidity, and sounds attenuate differently in forest and open habitat, the signals favored by sexual selection also depend on the habitat in which they are given.

Third, preferences can evolve through indirect benefits to the offspring. The most puzzling preferences are those of lekking species.

**Definition 13.2.4 (Lek)** A **lek** is an aggregation of males that display, often on small territories, at a traditional site that females visit only to mate; males provide no resources other than sperm.

Female sage grouse visit a lek and mate mostly with the few males at its center; female Andean cocks-of-the-rock (*Rupicola peruvianus*) choose among displaying males and raise the young alone. Since the female gains nothing for herself, the preference can only evolve through its effects on her offspring, that is, by indirect selection: preference genes become associated in **linkage disequilibrium** with genes for the preferred trait (because choosy females mate with males that carry it), and they change in frequency because the trait genes are selected. Two hypotheses differ in why the trait genes are selected.

**Definition 13.2.5 (Good Genes and the Handicap Principle)** The **good genes hypothesis** holds that females prefer male traits that indicate high heritable viability ("genetic quality"), so that their offspring inherit genes that improve survival or condition. The **handicap principle** (Zahavi 1975) holds that a signal of quality is reliable only if it is costly, and more costly for males of low quality than for males of high quality, so that lower-quality males cannot profitably fake it.

In three-spined sticklebacks (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) the young of brightly red fathers were more resistant to tapeworm infection than their half-siblings with dull red fathers (Barber et al. 2001); the red is made from carotenoids obtained in the diet, which also support the immune system. In great tits (*Parus major*), males with larger black breast stripes attract mates more easily, and in a cross-fostering experiment the offspring survived the winter better when their biological father had a large stripe, while the stripe of the foster father had no effect. Hamilton and Zuk (1982) proposed that coevolution with parasites maintains heritable variation in resistance and that bright plumage advertises it; the cross-species correlation they reported has not been found consistently in later surveys. Two problems make good genes difficult to establish. A signal must remain honest, which the handicap principle explains: Grafen (1990) showed that costly signaling with a cost that falls with quality is an evolutionarily stable strategy (7.10.16). And there must be enough additive genetic variance in fitness for the indirect benefit to be appreciable, although selection itself erodes this variance (7.10.5); mutation, migration and coevolving parasites can renew it. Correlations between attractiveness and offspring quality are expected anyway if vigor is heritable, so they show that preferences *could* evolve by good genes, not that they *did*.

Fisher (1930) pointed out that indirect selection does not require the trait to indicate anything beyond itself.

**Definition 13.2.6 (Fisherian Runaway)** In **Fisherian runaway** (runaway sexual selection), genes for a male trait and for the female preference for it become genetically correlated because choosy females mate with males that have the trait; selection on the trait then drags the preference with it, and the stronger preference intensifies sexual selection on the trait, a positive feedback that can exaggerate both until natural selection on the trait halts the process.

The idea was formalized only in the 1980s, by Lande (1981) for polygenic traits and Kirkpatrick (1982) for two loci. A simplified version of Lande's model shows the essential result.

**Theorem 13.2.1 (Line of Equilibria and Runaway)** Let $\bar z$ be the mean male trait and $\bar y$ the mean female preference, with additive genetic variance $G$ of the trait and additive genetic covariance $B$ between preference and trait. Suppose natural selection on the trait has gradient $-(\bar z - \theta)/\omega^2$, a female with preference $y$ favors males near $z = y$ so that sexual selection on the trait has gradient $(\bar y - \bar z)/\nu^2$, and the preference is under no direct selection. With each trait expressed in one sex,
$$
\Delta\bar z = \tfrac12 G\beta, \qquad \Delta\bar y = \tfrac12 B\beta, \qquad \beta = \frac{\bar y - \bar z}{\nu^2} - \frac{\bar z - \theta}{\omega^2}.
$$
Then (a) every point of the line $\bar y - \theta = (1 + \nu^2/\omega^2)(\bar z - \theta)$ is an equilibrium; (b) the population evolves along straight lines of slope $B/G$ in the $(\bar z, \bar y)$ plane; (c) the line of equilibria is approached if $B/G < 1 + \nu^2/\omega^2$, and the population runs away from it, with trait and preference exaggerated at an accelerating rate, if $B/G > 1 + \nu^2/\omega^2$.

**Proof** (a) $\beta = 0$ exactly on the stated line, and then $\Delta\bar z = \Delta\bar y = 0$. (b) $\Delta\bar y/\Delta\bar z = B/G$ whatever $\beta$. (c) From the definition of $\beta$,
$$
\Delta\beta = \frac{\Delta\bar y - \Delta\bar z}{\nu^2} - \frac{\Delta\bar z}{\omega^2} = \frac{\beta}{2}\left[\frac{B}{\nu^2} - G\left(\frac{1}{\nu^2} + \frac{1}{\omega^2}\right)\right],
$$
so $\beta$ changes by a constant factor each generation, $\beta_{t+1} = \beta_t\{1 + \tfrac{G}{2\nu^2}[B/G - (1 + \nu^2/\omega^2)]\}$. If $B/G < 1 + \nu^2/\omega^2$ the factor is less than one (for small $G/\nu^2$) and $\beta \to 0$; otherwise $\lvert\beta\rvert$ grows geometrically. $\square$

The genetic covariance $B$ is itself generated by assortative mating and grows with the strength of preference and the heritabilities, so runaway requires strong preferences and much genetic variation, while weak preferences lead to the line of equilibria. On the line, sexual and natural selection on the trait balance exactly, and any point is as stable as any other: drift or mutation can move the population along it, and which point is reached depends on history, so Fisher's process predicts arbitrary, possibly maladaptive, divergence among populations. This is a possible engine of speciation (7.10.12). The model has an important weakness. If choosiness carries even a small direct cost (time, predation risk), direct selection on the preference overwhelms the indirect selection, and the preference decays (Pomiankowski 1987; Pomiankowski, Iwasa and Nee 1991), unless something else, such as mutation biased toward reduced trait values, keeps the trait away from its naturally selected optimum. Theoretical analyses suggest that direct selection on preferences is often stronger than indirect selection, and well-documented cases of direct selection are more numerous than of indirect selection; the runaway itself has never been directly observed.

Female choice and male–male competition need not be alternatives. Weapons are tested in fights, and if winners carry good genes, females may evolve to prefer the weapons. Females also choose after mating.

**Remark 13.2.1 (Cryptic Female Choice)** *Cryptic female choice* is the biasing of fertilization among the sperm of different males by structures or processes in the female reproductive tract. In *Drosophila*, the ability of a male's sperm to resist displacement by a later mating varies among male genotypes but depends strongly on the genotype of the female: in a design with six male and six female genotypes there was no "best" male overall. Preferences of this kind favor genetic compatibility rather than a single best genotype, as do preferences for mates of the right species (**reinforcement**, 7.10.12) and for unrelated mates, which avoid **inbreeding depression** (7.10.14).

### 13.2.4. Sexual Selection in Flowering Plants

Plants have neither weapons nor nervous systems, but individuals of the same sexual function still compete for access to the other. Most flowering plants are hermaphrodites, and their operational sex ratio is usually male-biased: once its ovules are fertilized, a plant can go on exporting pollen. Large, showy flowers attract more pollinators and export more pollen; smaller and much cheaper flowers would usually suffice to have the ovules fertilized. Much of the floral display of animal-pollinated plants has therefore evolved as a male function, under sexual selection for pollen export. Bateman's principle applies to plants too: the fitness gained through pollen often increases more steeply with display size than the fitness gained through seeds. A second arena is the stigma, where pollen grains from different donors race to fertilize the ovules, the analog of sperm competition.

### 13.2.5. Sexual Conflict

Males and females must cooperate to produce offspring, but their evolutionary interests rarely coincide.

**Definition 13.2.7 (Sexual Conflict and Sexually Antagonistic Selection)** **Sexual conflict** is a difference between the evolutionary interests of males and females. *Interlocus* conflict concerns the outcome of interactions between the sexes (mating rate, parental investment) and involves different genes in each sex; *intralocus* conflict arises when a trait encoded by the same genes in both sexes has different optima in males and females. **Sexually antagonistic selection** is selection in which an allele or trait value that increases the fitness of one sex decreases the fitness of the other.

A male often gains by mating with a female who has already mated, while the female gains little from extra matings and may be injured or infected. Male bedbugs inseminate by piercing the female's abdominal wall, which reduces her survival; groups of male mallards sometimes drown females during forced copulations. In *Drosophila melanogaster*, the seminal fluid contains accessory gland proteins that make the female lay eggs faster and reduce her willingness to remate, which benefits the male if she later mates again, but shorten her life. Rice (1996) let males evolve for 30 generations against females that were prevented from evolving: the males' fitness rose, and females mated to them died sooner. Under normal conditions the sexes are locked in *sexually antagonistic coevolution*, a continual arms race in which male persistence and female resistance escalate together, often without visible change because each sex keeps pace with the other. Such races are a plausible explanation for the extraordinarily rapid evolution of genitalia and of seminal and reproductive proteins (7.10.23), and they can contribute to reproductive isolation (7.10.11).

Intralocus conflict is resolved, at least partly, by the evolution of sex-limited expression, which allows sexual dimorphism. When the **genetic correlation** between the sexes is high, the resolution is slow, and the fitness of both sexes remains compromised (7.10.9). Sexually antagonistic alleles are expected to accumulate on the X chromosome, where recessive alleles favorable to males are fully expressed in hemizygous males, and near the sex-determining region, where linkage to sex resolves the conflict and favors the suppression of recombination between the X and Y (7.10.14; [7.4.6](/7.4.6#sec-6.2)).

## 13.3. Sex Ratios

### 13.3.1. Fisher's Principle

**Definition 13.3.1 (Sex Ratio)** The **sex ratio** is the proportion of males (or the ratio of males to females) in a population or brood: the *primary* sex ratio at fertilization, the *secondary* sex ratio at birth and the adult sex ratio later; the *sex allocation* of a parent is the fraction of its reproductive resources spent on sons.

Chromosomal sex determination with fair segregation produces a 1:1 primary sex ratio, but this is not a constraint: females of the Seychelles warbler (*Acrocephalus sechellensis*) produce up to 87 percent daughters on good territories and only 23 percent on poor ones (Komdeur), apparently because daughters stay and help their parents when food is plentiful. Haplodiploid females choose the sex of each egg by fertilizing it or not, and in reptiles with temperature-dependent sex determination the sex ratio evolves through the choice of nest sites. Why, then, are most sex ratios close to equality, when a population with more females could grow faster?

**Theorem 13.3.1 (Fisher's Principle)** Suppose that every individual has one mother and one father, that parents spend equal resources on a son and on a daughter, and that mating is random in a large population. If $x$ is the proportion of males among the offspring of the population, a parent that produces a proportion $m$ of sons has fitness, measured by its expected number of grandchildren,
$$
W(m) \propto \frac{m}{x} + \frac{1 - m}{1 - x}.
$$
The sex ratio $x^* = \tfrac12$ is the only evolutionarily stable one: when $x < \tfrac12$ parents producing more sons are favored, and when $x > \tfrac12$ parents producing more daughters are favored.

**Proof** Let the next generation contain $N$ offspring, $Nx$ males and $N(1 - x)$ females. Since each of the grandchildren has exactly one father and one mother, the males as a group and the females as a group have equal total reproduction, say $K$ offspring each. The expected reproduction is therefore $K/(Nx)$ per son and $K/[N(1 - x)]$ per daughter. A parent with $n$ offspring, $nm$ of them sons, expects $nK[m/(Nx) + (1 - m)/(N(1 - x))]$ grandchildren, which is proportional to $W(m)$. The slope $\partial W/\partial m = 1/x - 1/(1 - x)$ is positive if $x < \tfrac12$ and negative if $x > \tfrac12$: the rarer sex has the higher reproductive value per individual, and parents biased toward it are favored. At $x = \tfrac12$ all values of $m$ have the same fitness, and no deviant strategy can invade a population producing equal numbers (any mutant that becomes common shifts $x$ against itself). $\square$

The argument is an instance of **negative frequency-dependent selection**: the advantage of producing a sex declines as that sex becomes common. If sons and daughters cost different amounts, $c_m$ and $c_f$, the same argument applied to the investment gives *equal total investment* in the two sexes: the ESS proportion of sons is $c_f/(c_m + c_f)$, so more of the cheaper sex is produced. If sons die more often before the end of parental care, the ESS is still equal investment at the end of care; the primary sex ratio is then male-biased, as it is slightly in humans. Fisher's principle concerns the allocation of resources between sexes, not population growth: as for most traits, the effect on the population is irrelevant to selection among individuals (7.10.3).

### 13.3.2. Local Mate Competition

Fisher's argument assumes that sons compete for mates in the population at large. Hamilton (1967) noticed that this fails when brothers compete with one another. In fig wasps, females enter a fig through a narrow opening, pollinate its flowers and lay their eggs in them. The wingless males hatch first, fight, often to the death, and mate with the females, many of whom are their sisters, before the females leave to find new figs. If a single female founds a fig, her sons compete only with each other for her daughters: one son would be enough to mate them all, and every extra son is wasted.

**Definition 13.3.2 (Local Mate Competition)** **Local mate competition** occurs when related males (usually brothers) compete with each other for mates, so that extra sons add less to a mother's fitness than they would in a randomly mating population.

**Theorem 13.3.2 (Hamilton's Sex Ratio Under Local Mate Competition)** Suppose $n$ mothers lay equal numbers of eggs in a patch, their offspring mate at random within the patch, and the mated daughters disperse to found new patches while the sons stay and die. In a diploid outbred population, the evolutionarily stable proportion of sons is
$$
x^* = \frac{n - 1}{2n}.
$$

**Proof** Measure fitness by the number of mated daughters of the patch to whose genes a mother contributes. Let a focal mother produce a proportion $x$ of sons and the other $n - 1$ mothers the resident proportion $x^*$, each mother producing one unit of offspring. Through her daughters she contributes $1 - x$; her sons father a share $x/[x + (n - 1)x^*]$ of all the daughters of the patch, $(1 - x) + (n - 1)(1 - x^*)$, and in a diploid each offspring carries equal shares of maternal and paternal genes, so the two routes have equal weight. Hence
$$
W(x) = (1 - x) + \frac{x}{x + (n - 1)x^*}\left[(1 - x) + (n - 1)(1 - x^*)\right].
$$
Setting $\partial W/\partial x = 0$ at $x = x^*$ gives
$$
-1 + \frac{(n - 1)x^*}{(nx^*)^2}\,n(1 - x^*) - \frac{1}{n} = 0 \iff \frac{(n - 1)(1 - x^*)}{x^*} = n + 1 \iff x^* = \frac{n - 1}{2n}. \qquad \square
$$

With one foundress the model predicts as few sons as possible (in practice, just enough to fertilize the sisters); as $n$ grows, $x^*$ rises toward Fisher's $\tfrac12$. In haplodiploid species, mating among siblings also inbreeds the population, and a mother's genes pass to the next generation more efficiently through her daughters, which carry both her own genes and those of a father who may be related to her; the ESS becomes $x^* = (n - 1)(2n - 1)/[n(4n - 1)]$, slightly more female-biased. Fig wasps fit these predictions remarkably well. Within species, figs founded by fewer mothers produce more daughters, so individual females sense the number of other foundresses and adjust the proportion of eggs they fertilize; and across species, those that usually have fewer foundresses, and are therefore more inbred, have more female-biased sex ratios. Parasitoid wasps such as *Nasonia vitripennis* adjust their sex ratios in the same way when they lay eggs in hosts already parasitized by another female.

### 13.3.3. Haplodiploidy, Condition and Environment

Sex allocation is a source of conflict in social insects. In a colony of ants, bees or wasps headed by a single queen who mated once, the workers are related to their sisters by $r = \tfrac34$ and to their brothers by $r = \tfrac14$, while the queen is related equally ($r = \tfrac12$) to her sons and daughters (7.10.16). Trivers and Hare (1976) predicted that the queen favors equal investment in reproductive males and females, but the workers, who rear the brood, favor a 3:1 investment ratio in favor of new queens. In colonies with several queens or a multiply mated queen, the workers' relatedness to the female brood is lower and their favored ratio is closer to 1:1. In ants, wasps and bees in which some colonies have a single, singly mated queen and others do not, a review found the predicted difference in sex allocation in 18 of 19 species, evidence that workers manipulate the sex ratio, for example by selectively rearing or killing larvae.

Allocation can also depend on the condition of the parent. Trivers and Willard (1973) argued that if the condition of a mother affects the reproductive success of sons more than that of daughters, as in polygynous mammals where only large, vigorous males win harems, mothers in good condition should produce sons and mothers in poor condition daughters. The *Trivers–Willard hypothesis* is supported in some species and not in others, partly because a mechanism for adjusting the sex ratio is not always available. The Seychelles warbler is a case of the related *local resource enhancement*: daughters help on the natal territory, so they are worth more where food can support them.

Finally, Charnov and Bull (1977) proposed that sex should be determined by the environment when an environmental factor affects the fitness of sons and daughters differently and offspring cannot choose their environment. In many turtles and crocodilians, the incubation temperature sets sex, and the population sex ratio can evolve through the choice of nest sites and the pivotal temperature ([7.4.6](/7.4.6#sec-6.1.3)); a fast change of climate may outpace this response and bias the sex ratios of such species. Sex-ratio distorters that cheat at meiosis, and selfish elements that convert females into males or males into females, are discussed in 7.10.16.

## Questions

1. Why does Bateman's principle predict that sexual selection is usually stronger on males, and why do species with male parental care, such as phalaropes, show the opposite pattern?

   **Answer** A male's reproductive success is limited mainly by the number of eggs he fertilizes, so it rises with the number of his mates; a female's is limited by the resources she can put into eggs and care, so extra mates add little. Males therefore vary more in reproductive success, the operational sex ratio is male-biased, and males compete for females. When males incubate the eggs and rear the young, a male's reproductive rate is limited by his capacity for care, while a female can lay further clutches for other males; females then have the higher potential reproductive rate, are in excess in the mating pool, and compete for males, so the females are larger and brighter.

2. In Example 13.2.1, let $\theta = 10$ cm (the aerodynamic optimum of a tail), $\omega = 2$ cm and $\alpha = 0.5\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. What tail length evolves, and how much survival does a male at this length lose relative to a male at the survival optimum?

   **Answer** $z^* = \theta + \alpha\omega^2 = 10 + 0.5 \cdot 4 = 12$ cm. Survival relative to the optimum is $\exp[-(z^* - \theta)^2/(2\omega^2)] = \exp(-4/8) = \mathrm{e}^{-0.5} \approx 0.61$: the male loses about 39 percent of his survival but gains a factor $\mathrm{e}^{\alpha(z^* - \theta)} = \mathrm{e}^{1} \approx 2.7$ in mating success relative to a male at $\theta$.

3. Distinguish direct and indirect selection on female preferences, and explain why a small direct cost of being choosy is a problem for the runaway and good-genes mechanisms.

   **Answer** Direct selection acts on preference genes through their effect on the female's own survival and fecundity, as with direct benefits or sensory bias. Indirect selection acts on them only through their linkage disequilibrium with genes for the preferred trait or for viability, which arises because choosy females mate with preferred males. The indirect force is second order: it is proportional to the genetic covariance between preference and trait (or viability), which is usually small, so even a weak direct cost of choosing (time, energy, predation) usually outweighs it, and the preference decays unless something, such as biased mutation or parasites, maintains a persistent indirect benefit.

4. Show that if a son costs twice as much to rear as a daughter, Fisher's argument predicts one son for every two daughters.

   **Answer** With costs $c_m = 2$, $c_f = 1$ and a budget $R$, a parent producing a fraction $q$ of its investment in sons rears $qR/2$ sons and $(1 - q)R$ daughters. Its fitness is proportional to $(qR/2)/M + (1 - q)R/F$, where $M$ and $F$ are the numbers of males and females in the population, and each sex as a whole has the same total reproduction. The slope in $q$ is $R/(2M) - R/F$, which vanishes when $F = 2M$. So at the ESS there are two daughters per son, and the investment is equal: $2 \cdot 1 = 1 \cdot 2$.

5. Using Theorem 13.3.2, predict the proportion of sons for two and for five foundresses in a diploid and in a haplodiploid species. Why do haplodiploid mothers produce fewer sons?

   **Answer** Diploid: $n = 2$ gives $1/4 = 0.25$, $n = 5$ gives $4/10 = 0.40$. Haplodiploid: $n = 2$ gives $1 \cdot 3/(2 \cdot 7) = 0.214$, $n = 5$ gives $4 \cdot 9/(5 \cdot 19) = 0.379$. In a haplodiploid a daughter carries her mother's genes and those of her father, and with sib-mating the father is often the mother's son or relative; a son carries only his mother's genes and transmits them only through his daughters. The inbreeding produced by local mating thus raises the value of daughters relative to sons, and the ESS is more female-biased.
