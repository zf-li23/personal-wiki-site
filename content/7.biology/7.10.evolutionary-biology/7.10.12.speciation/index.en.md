The previous chapter ([7.10.11](/7.10.11)) defined species by reproductive isolation and analysed the barriers that separate them. Here we ask how such barriers evolve. The question looks paradoxical: barriers reduce the chance that individuals mate or that their offspring survive, so how can selection build them? The usual answer is that reproductive isolation evolves as a by-product of divergence that happens for other reasons, most often while populations are kept apart by geography. The first part of this chapter treats the evolutionary forces that cause divergence and isolation, the second the geographic settings in which speciation happens, and the third what genome sequences reveal about it. Speciation links population genetics ([7.10.5](/7.10.5), [7.10.6](/7.10.6)) and the interaction of gene flow and selection ([7.10.10](/7.10.10)) to macroevolution: rates of speciation and their variation among clades are treated in [7.10.31](/7.10.31), and the statistical inference of divergence times and gene flow between species from genomic data in [7.10.25](/7.10.25).

## 12.1. The Causes of Speciation

**Definition 12.1.1 (Speciation)** **Speciation** is the origin of two or more species from one ancestral species; under the biological species concept, it is the evolution of reproductive isolation between populations of one species.

To start speciation, something must restrict free interbreeding between the diverging populations, because interbreeding erases emerging differences. Most often this is geographic separation: a barrier, such as a mountain range, separates two populations, which then evolve genetic and phenotypic differences, perhaps as they adapt to different conditions. Because the populations do not mix, there is no reason why their genes or their mating behaviour should remain compatible. If they come into contact again and the differences prevent interbreeding, two species have formed. The question then becomes which evolutionary forces produce the differences that cause isolation.

### 12.1.1. Ecological Speciation

**Definition 12.1.2 (Ecological Speciation)** **Ecological speciation** is the evolution of reproductive isolation as a consequence of divergent natural selection between populations in different environments or exploiting different resources.

The monkeyflowers *Mimulus lewisii* and *M. cardinalis* ([7.10.11](/7.10.11#sec-11.2.1)) are a plausible case. A reconstruction of ancestral characters on the phylogeny of the genus suggests that their common ancestor resembled *M. lewisii*: bee-pollinated and living at high elevation. The population that gave rise to *M. cardinalis* colonized lower elevations, where selection favoured traits that attract hummingbirds: red pigments, abundant nectar and petals extended into a long tube that excludes bees. The shift in elevation and pollinator itself strongly reduced the exchange of pollen with the ancestral population.

Reproductive isolation in ecological speciation evolves by **pleiotropy** or by linkage: selection acts on traits for their ecological function, and isolation is a side effect. Speciation, one of the most important outcomes of evolution, is commonly a consequence of adaptation but is typically not itself an adaptation. That divergent selection can produce isolation quickly has been shown in the laboratory.

**Example 12.1.1 (Speciation in the Laboratory)** Eight populations of *Drosophila pseudoobscura*, all founded from flies collected at one locality, were kept for about a year (some 20 generations) on two stressful larval media, four on a starch-based and four on a maltose-based medium; this was natural, not artificial, selection, because the flies themselves determined who reproduced. After one generation on standard food, to remove maternal effects, virgin females and males from pairs of populations were observed in mating chambers. For one pair adapted to different media the numbers of matings were

| | Female starch | Female maltose |
|---|---|---|
| Male starch | 19 | 7 |
| Male maltose | 8 | 22 |

The sexual isolation index, the excess of same-population over different-population matings as a fraction of all matings, is $I = (19 + 22 - 7 - 8)/56 = 0.46$. Females preferred males adapted to their own medium in all 16 pairs of populations adapted to different media, significantly so in 11, whereas no pair adapted to the same medium showed a significant preference. In about 20 generations, divergent selection had produced roughly half of complete sexual isolation. The genes responsible were not identified; either the genes for adaptation to the media also affected mating behaviour (pleiotropy) or selected alleles carried linked alleles affecting it.

Sticklebacks provide a natural example. In several lakes of British Columbia formed after the last glaciation, less than $10\,000$ years ago, two forms of the three-spined stickleback (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) coexist: a benthic form feeding near the shore and a limnetic form feeding in open water. They differ in morphology, feeding behaviour and mate preference; they hybridize, but hybrids grow more slowly than either parent in its own habitat. Nuclear gene genealogies show that the pairs did not originate within the lakes: the lakes were colonized twice from the sea; the first colonists evolved an intermediate form, and when a second invasion arrived, the residents specialized as benthics and the newcomers as limnetics, a case of ecological divergence driven by competition, **character displacement** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)).

### 12.1.2. Mutation-Order Speciation

Divergent selection is not required for incompatibilities to arise. Populations exposed to the *same* selective pressures may fix different favourable mutations, simply because different mutations happen to arise first in each; if these mutations are incompatible with one another, Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibilities ([7.10.11](/7.10.11#sec-11.4.1)) result. This process is called mutation-order speciation (Schluter 2009), in contrast to ecological speciation. Its clearest examples come from genetic conflict and from adaptive evolution of genes such as *Nup96*, in which recurrent positive selection, unrelated to the environment of the species, produced incompatibilities ([7.10.11](/7.10.11#sec-11.4.3)).

### 12.1.3. Genetic Conflict

Genetic conflict arises when an allele increases its own transmission at the expense of other alleles at the same or other loci ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)). Alleles that are transmitted to more than half of the gametes through **meiotic drive** (segregation distortion, [7.4.17](/7.4.17)) increase in frequency even if they reduce fertility, and they select for suppressors at other loci that restore normal segregation. If such an arms race has run in one population and not another, hybrids may express drivers without the matching suppressors. This is the basis of the strong isolation between the Bogotá and North American populations of *Drosophila pseudoobscura*: a gene, *Overdrive*, that spreads by segregation distortion in the Bogotá population causes sterility of hybrid males, where the restorer alleles of the Bogotá population are inadequate. Similar conflicts between nuclear and mitochondrial or chloroplast genes produce cytonuclear incompatibilities. **Sexual conflict** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)) acts in a similar way: in abalones, changes in egg-surface proteins that slow sperm entry are favoured because fertilization by more than one sperm kills the egg, and they impose selection on sperm proteins to penetrate faster; the resulting rapid coevolution of lysin and its egg receptor ([7.10.11](/7.10.11#sec-11.2.1)) can cause gametic isolation between populations.

### 12.1.4. Sexual Selection

In many rapidly speciating groups of animals, species differ more in secondary sexual traits, such as male coloration or song, than in ecologically important traits. Females (usually) choose mates on the basis of these traits, and **sexual selection** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)) can drive their rapid evolution; species that differ in male traits typically also differ in female preferences, so that females recognize and prefer males of their own species. Divergent sexual selection can therefore produce prezygotic isolation quickly. Groups with strong sexual selection, such as cichlid fishes and hummingbirds, have indeed speciated rapidly, and phylogenetic analyses of birds suggest that male coloration associated with sexual selection and species recognition evolves fastest in lineages with high speciation rates. In the Hawaiian cricket *Laupala cerasina*, the pulse rate of the male song and the pulse rate preferred by females vary together among local populations, to the point that females hardly respond to the songs of the most different population. Sexual isolation is the only known barrier between some ecologically indistinguishable species of darters. Why sexual selection should differ among populations is considered in [7.10.13](/7.10.13): direct benefits of preferences, pleiotropic effects of preference genes, "good genes", and ecological conditions that make different signals effective in different environments.

### 12.1.5. Drift and Founder Events

Some closely related species differ by underdominant chromosomal rearrangements, which reduce the fertility of heterozygotes ([7.10.11](/7.10.11#sec-11.6)). In the sunflowers *Helianthus annuus* and *H. petiolaris*, about half of the postzygotic barrier is due to such rearrangements. How can a rearrangement that is selected against when rare ever become fixed?

**Formula 12.1.1 (Fixation of an Underdominant Rearrangement)** Let a new rearrangement arise as a single copy in a deme of $N$ diploids, with heterozygote fitness $1 - s$ relative to both homozygotes. By the diffusion approximation ([7.10.6](/7.10.6)), its probability of fixation is

$$
u = \frac{\int_0^{1/2N}G(y)\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_0^1G(y)\,\mathrm{d}y}, \qquad G(y) = \mathrm{e}^{4Nsy(1 - y)}, \tag{12.1}
$$
which falls roughly as $\mathrm{e}^{-Ns}$ relative to the neutral value $1/(2N)$ for $Ns \gg 1$.

**Proof** With weak selection the expected change in frequency is $M(p) = sp(1 - p)(2p - 1)$ and the variance $V(p) = p(1 - p)/(2N)$. The fixation probability of the diffusion is $u(p) = \int_0^pG/\int_0^1G$ with $G(y) = \exp(-\int^y2M/V) = \exp(-4Ns\int^y(2z - 1)\,\mathrm{d}z) = \exp(4Nsy(1 - y))$. The denominator is dominated by $y$ near $\tfrac12$, where $G = \mathrm{e}^{Ns}$, which gives the order of magnitude. $\square$

A rearrangement thus has an appreciable chance of fixation only if $Ns$ is below about ten: with $s = 0.01$, in demes of about a thousand individuals or fewer. A species as a whole cannot be that small for long, but a rearrangement fixed in one small deme can spread through a subdivided species if demes go extinct and are recolonized from neighbours. The argument has a limitation: drift can establish only rearrangements of small effect, which contribute little isolation. Rearrangements may also spread by selection, through meiotic drive or by reducing recombination between favourable combinations of alleles (§12.3), and their role in speciation may lie mainly in that suppression of recombination.

![Fixation probability of a new underdominant chromosomal rearrangement, relative to the neutral probability 1/2N, against Ns (heterozygote fertility 1 − s, N diploids). Line: diffusion approximation (Formula 12.1.1); dots: Wright–Fisher simulations with N = 25 (200 000 replicates each); dashed: e^(−Ns). Fixation by drift is plausible only when Ns is below about 10, i.e. in small demes or for weakly underdominant rearrangements](./plots/underdominant-fixation.py)

Mayr proposed that drift in small founder populations triggers speciation more generally. In his hypothesis of *founder-effect* or *peripatric speciation*, a new population founded by a few individuals at the edge of the range has rare alleles fixed at some loci by drift; alleles at other loci that interact favourably with them then increase, producing a new, coadapted gene combination incompatible with the parental population. He cited island birds as examples: the paradise kingfishers *Tanysiptera riedelii* on Biak and *T. carolinae* on Numfor, small islands off New Guinea, are distinct species, whereas *T. galatea* is widespread on the New Guinea mainland. Founder-effect speciation is controversial on theoretical and empirical grounds. Laboratory populations of *Drosophila* passed through bottlenecks sometimes develop partial sexual isolation from the parental population, but it is often temporary, and theory suggests that founder events seldom produce strong isolation. Most evolutionary biologists regard it as rare.

### 12.1.6. Polyploidy

When the genome of a diploid is doubled, the resulting tetraploid has four copies of every chromosome. Tetraploids arise from the union of two unreduced gametes, each carrying a diploid set, which form when chromosomes occasionally fail to segregate in meiosis. A polyploid is an **autopolyploid** if the unreduced gametes come from one species and an **allopolyploid** if they come from different species ([7.4.16](/7.4.16)).

A new tetraploid is reproductively isolated from its diploid ancestors from the moment of its origin, because their hybrids are triploid: one chromosome set from the diploid and two from the tetraploid. Triploids are largely sterile, because their gametes receive unbalanced numbers of chromosomes. **Polyploidy** is thus one of the rare cases in which a single mutation of large effect founds a new species. In allopolyploids, which are more common among polyploid species, the diploid hybrid is usually sterile because the chromosome sets of the two parents do not pair properly at meiosis; doubling gives every chromosome a partner and restores regular meiosis. The fixed heterozygosity of an allopolyploid, which carries complete genomes of both parents, may give it a lasting advantage of hybrid vigour that Mendelian segregation cannot dissipate.

Polyploid speciation is rare in animals, which usually have separate sexes, so that at least two polyploid individuals must meet to found a population; even so, the gray tree frog *Hyla versicolor* (24 pairs of chromosomes) is a tetraploid derived from ancestors like *H. chrysoscelis* (12 pairs), with which it is morphologically indistinguishable but from which it differs in male call. In flowering plants polyploidy is common: it accounts for about $15\,\%$ of speciation events, and all flowering plants have polyploid ancestors somewhere in their history (whole-genome duplications, [7.4.16](/7.4.16), [7.10.18](/7.10.18)). Many crops are polyploid, including wheat, cotton, coffee, tobacco and sugarcane. Polyploid speciation has been observed in historical time. Three diploid species of goatsbeards (*Tragopogon*), introduced to North America from Europe, have hybridized there, and within the last century or so the allotetraploids *T. miscellus* (from *T. dubius* × *T. pratensis*) and *T. mirus* (from *T. dubius* × *T. porrifolius*) have arisen; each has twice the chromosome number of its diploid parents ($n = 12$ against $n = 6$). Allopolyploid species can be re-created: in 1930 Müntzing showed that artificially produced tetraploid hybrids of the mints *Galeopsis pubescens* and *G. speciosa* resemble the natural tetraploid *G. tetrahit* and interbreed with it. Plant breeders have created new allopolyploids such as triticale, from wheat and rye.

![Speciation by allopolyploidy in the goatsbeards Tragopogon (chromosome numbers from Futuyma and Kirkpatrick 2017). Each bar is one haploid set of six chromosomes, coloured by the parental genome of origin. The diploid F₁ hybrid of T. dubius and T. pratensis is largely sterile, because the two chromosome sets do not pair regularly; genome doubling produces the fertile allotetraploid T. miscellus. A cross of the tetraploid with a diploid parent gives a sterile triploid (dashed), so the tetraploid is reproductively isolated from both parents from its origin](./plots/allopolyploid-speciation.py)

A new tetraploid faces a demographic obstacle: it starts as one or a few individuals among many diploids, so most of its matings are with diploids and produce unfit triploids, pushing it towards extinction. Tetraploids are more likely to become established if they self-fertilize, reproduce vegetatively, are fitter than their diploid ancestors, or are segregated from them by habitat or flowering time. Many polyploid taxa do self-fertilize or propagate vegetatively, and most differ from their diploid relatives in habitat and distribution. Some of these differences arise immediately from genome duplication. In California, tetraploid and hexaploid forms of the yarrow *Achillea borealis* grow in wetter and drier habitats, respectively; newly formed hexaploids, produced from tetraploid parents and planted in dry dunes, survived better and flowered earlier than tetraploids, so they were partly isolated from their tetraploid parents by habitat and flowering time from their origin. That the natural hexaploids differed still more shows that further evolution followed.

### 12.1.7. Hybrid Speciation

Hybridization usually opposes divergence, but occasionally it creates a new species.

**Definition 12.1.3 (Hybrid Speciation)** **Hybrid speciation** is the origin of a new species from hybrids between two species. In allopolyploid speciation (§12.1.6) the hybrid species has the combined chromosome sets of both parents; in homoploid hybrid speciation it has the same chromosome number as the parents and must be isolated from them by other barriers.

Three species of sunflowers (*Helianthus anomalus*, *H. deserticola* and *H. paradoxus*) have originated by independent hybridization events between the same two parental species, *H. annuus* and *H. petiolaris*, without change in chromosome number. The hybrid species live in drier or saltier habitats than either parent, are genetically incompatible with them, and carry different combinations of parental genes. Their origin has been repeated experimentally: synthetic hybrids between the parents, after several generations, show some of the same distinctive adaptive traits and chromosome arrangements as the natural hybrid species. Hybridization thus produced new combinations of genes that allowed the colonization of new habitats, and the combination of ecological separation and genetic incompatibility isolated the hybrids from their parents.

### 12.1.8. Reinforcement

So far reproductive isolation has been a by-product. In one case selection can favour isolation directly.

**Definition 12.1.4 (Reinforcement)** **Reinforcement** is the evolution of stronger prezygotic isolation between populations by natural selection against the production of hybrids of low fitness.

If hybrids between two populations that have come into contact have reduced fitness, a female that mates with a male of her own population leaves more descendants than one that mates with the other: an allele that increases the tendency to mate within the population is transmitted to more surviving offspring. Only prezygotic barriers can be strengthened in this way. An allele that further lowers hybrid fitness cannot spread by natural selection (exceptions are possible when embryos compete for maternal resources: a mother may gain by aborting hybrid embryos early and investing in non-hybrid offspring). Reinforcement can operate only between populations that still interbreed after some postzygotic isolation has evolved, and it completes speciation that began in allopatry.

The clearest example is the wildflower *Phlox*. Allopatric populations of *P. drummondii* and *P. cuspidata* both have light blue flowers, but where the two species overlap in Texas, *P. drummondii* has dark red flowers, a difference determined by two loci. Hybrids have up to $90\,\%$ lower fertility than non-hybrids, and pollinators move less pollen between flowers of different colours. In common-garden experiments with all four colour genotypes of *P. drummondii* grown with *P. cuspidata*, the genotypes produced similar numbers of fruits but differed in the fraction of hybrid offspring, which was lowest for dark red.

Reinforcement predicts that sympatric pairs of closely related species should have stronger prezygotic isolation than allopatric pairs. *Drosophila* show exactly this pattern ([7.10.11](/7.10.11#sec-11.3)): among young species pairs, sympatric pairs have much stronger prezygotic isolation, while postzygotic isolation does not differ, and the strength of prezygotic isolation hardly overlaps between sympatric and allopatric pairs, which makes it unlikely that only well-isolated pairs could become sympatric. Where hybridization is asymmetric, the stronger sexual isolation is found in the direction of the cross that produces less fit hybrids. Pied flycatchers (*Ficedula hypoleuca*) and collared flycatchers (*F. albicollis*) overlap in central and eastern Europe; heterospecific pairs are much rarer than expected under random mating ($2.6\,\%$ observed against $13.8\,\%$ expected), and when one or both parents are hybrids nearly three-quarters of the eggs fail to hatch, against about $5\,\%$ otherwise. Outside the zone of overlap, males of both species have black-and-white plumage, and female pied flycatchers prefer such males; in the overlap zone, male pied flycatchers have dull brown, female-like plumage and the local females prefer them, a divergence in signal and preference that reduces hybridization.

The theory of reinforcement faces an obstacle similar to that of sympatric speciation (§12.2.2): gene flow and recombination break the association between alleles for mating preference and the genetic background in which they are favoured. Reinforcement is easiest when the species are already strongly isolated and overlap broadly, harder when they meet only in a narrow hybrid zone. A rare example within a narrow cline is the grass *Agrostis* at the edge of copper mines ([7.10.10](/7.10.10#sec-10.5.2)), where flowering time has diverged between tolerant and non-tolerant plants and self-fertilization has increased, both reducing maladaptive gene flow.

## 12.2. The Geography of Speciation

A critical issue is how gene flow between diverging populations is first reduced, because populations cannot diverge, and evolve reproductive isolation, if gene flow counteracts the divergence caused by selection or drift.

**Definition 12.2.1 (Geographic Modes of Speciation)** In **allopatric speciation** reproductive isolation evolves between populations that are geographically separated, so that gene flow between them is severely reduced by a physical barrier or distance; in **peripatric speciation**, a form of allopatric speciation, the isolated population is a small population founded at the periphery of the range. In **parapatric speciation** neighbouring populations diverge into species while continuing to exchange genes across their boundary. In **sympatric speciation** a single population splits into reproductively isolated groups without any geographic separation.

Sympatric and parapatric speciation are forms of speciation with gene flow; allopatric speciation is not. Real cases combine elements of the three idealized modes, and the geographic pattern may change during speciation. Allopatry is defined by the reduction of movement, not by distance: in organisms that disperse little or are faithful to a habitat, populations a few hundred metres apart, for example in patches of habitat along a lakeshore, may be "microgeographically" allopatric.

Peripatric: a small population founded at the edge of the range diverges. Parapatric: adjacent populations along an environmental gradient diverge while exchanging genes across a hybrid zone (double arrow). Sympatric: two reproductively isolated forms arise within a single area without any spatial separation](./plots/geography-of-speciation.py)

### 12.2.1. Allopatric Speciation

Allopatric populations originate in two ways. In **vicariance** ([7.10.30](/7.10.30)) a barrier appears and divides a widespread population; when the Isthmus of Panama rose from the sea several million years ago, it divided many marine species into Caribbean and Pacific populations, which have since evolved into distinct species. In dispersal, individuals colonize a new region, as has happened countless times on oceanic islands. Ranges change over time, and many sister species that are sympatric today speciated in allopatry and expanded their ranges later; present sympatry is not in itself evidence of sympatric speciation.

Evidence for the importance of allopatric speciation comes from several sources. Many species show partial reproductive isolation among geographic populations: in the dusky salamander *Desmognathus ochrophaeus*, sexual isolation between populations increases with the geographic distance between them, and allopatric darters in different river systems show sexual isolation. In the southeastern United States, populations of the fence lizard (*Sceloporus undulatus*) separated by rivers differ more morphologically than populations the same distance apart without a river between them. Islands show the role of area: no pair of sister species of birds occurs together on any isolated island smaller than about $10\,000\,\mathrm{km^2}$, which implies that birds do not speciate on land masses too small to provide geographic isolation within them; in groups with high gene flow, such as bats, in-situ speciation has occurred only on very large islands, and in groups with very low gene flow, such as snails, on small ones. On continents, distributions may be fragmented when species track particular climates or habitats: a species widespread at low elevation in a cool period may be split into populations on separate mountains when the climate warms. Allopatric sister species of salamanders occupy localities with similar climate separated by regions of different climate, and the numbers of species of birds and plants are very high in mountainous regions such as the Andes, where many species have small ranges separated by valleys from those of their sister species.

### 12.2.2. Secondary Contact and Hybrid Zones

In allopatric speciation, isolating barriers come into play only when the populations meet again.

**Definition 12.2.2 (Secondary Contact and Hybrid Zone)** **Secondary contact** is the meeting of populations that diverged in geographic isolation. A **hybrid zone** is a region where genetically distinct populations meet and interbreed to a limited extent, producing individuals of mixed ancestry, while partial barriers to gene exchange keep the populations distinct.

If reproductive isolation is incomplete at secondary contact, three outcomes are possible.

1. *Fusion.* The populations interbreed so freely that they merge again, and speciation fails. This happened to incipient species of three-spined sticklebacks in a lake when a change of habitat relaxed ecological selection against hybrids, and it is happening to cichlids in turbid parts of Lake Victoria ([7.10.11](/7.10.11#sec-11.3)).
2. *A stable hybrid zone.* Alleles mix across the zone and form clines ([7.10.10](/7.10.10#sec-10.5)), but selection keeps the populations distinct. The fire-bellied toad *Bombina bombina* of eastern Europe and the yellow-bellied toad *B. variegata* of western Europe meet in a narrow hybrid zone; along transects in Poland, the clines in allele frequency at six enzyme loci and in a morphological score based on seven characters are coincident, as expected for a zone formed by contact between populations that had diverged in allopatry. Parts of the genome under selection may remain differentiated indefinitely, whereas others become homogenized by gene flow (§12.3).
3. *Reinforcement.* When hybrids are unfit, selection may strengthen prezygotic isolation (§12.1.8).

Whether the new species can then coexist also depends on ecology. Sister species often use similar resources, and competition may prevent them from spreading into each other's ranges, leaving them in a parapatric distribution with a narrow contact zone, or may drive one to extinction, unless further ecological divergence allows coexistence ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Hybrid zones also allow genes to cross species boundaries.

**Definition 12.2.3 (Introgression)** **Introgression** (introgressive hybridization) is the incorporation of genes of one species into the gene pool of another through hybridization and repeated backcrossing.

Some introgressed genes enhance adaptation. *Heliconius* butterflies are distasteful to predators and carry warning colour patterns; alleles that determine parts of the wing pattern have spread among even distantly related species. The DNA sequences of two colour-pattern genes show that *H. timareta* acquired the "postman" hindwing pattern from *H. melpomene amaryllis*, and *H. elevatus* the "rayed" pattern from *H. melpomene aglaope*/*malleti*, while the rest of their genomes reflect their own species' history.

### 12.2.3. Sympatric Speciation

Sympatric speciation is the most extreme form of speciation with gene flow and has long been controversial, because interbreeding causes genetic mixing that prevents divergence. Consider a bird with disruptive selection on its bill: birds with long thin bills eat insects, birds with stout bills eat seeds, and intermediates do poorly. The birds also vary in colour and mate assortatively by colour. If all thin-billed birds were blue and all stout-billed birds red, the two groups would have both prezygotic isolation (colour) and postzygotic isolation (intermediate bills of hybrids). The difficulty is that this requires **linkage disequilibrium** between the colour and bill loci. Selection favours such disequilibrium, because pairs with the same bill type, more likely if bill type is associated with colour, produce fewer intermediate offspring; but every mating between birds with different combinations of colour and bill alleles erodes it, and recombination breaks it down. Unless disruptive selection and **assortative mating** are strong, the associations decay before two separate clusters can form, and the problem grows when mating depends on several loci.

The difficulty depends on the genetic basis of isolation. Felsenstein (1981) distinguished two kinds of mechanisms. In a *two-allele mechanism*, reproductive isolation requires different alleles to be fixed in the two incipient species at the loci for mate choice, and these must stay associated with the ecologically selected alleles, which recombination opposes. In a *one-allele mechanism*, isolation is increased by the spread of the same allele in both groups, for example an allele that makes individuals prefer mates similar to themselves, or that reduces dispersal from the natal habitat; recombination cannot break such an association, and speciation is much easier.

It is also much easier when the trait under divergent selection itself causes assortative mating. A trait under divergent ecological selection that also contributes to non-random mating has been called a *magic trait*, or speciation trait: the ecological difference and the isolation are then the same thing, and no linkage disequilibrium is needed. Host choice in insects that mate on their host plants is the classic case: selection favouring a preference for a particular host simultaneously increases mating within the group that prefers it. Populations at early and intermediate stages of this process are called host races. The apple maggot fly (*Rhagoletis pomonella*) originally laid its eggs only in the fruits of hawthorns in North America; in the nineteenth century, after apples were introduced by European settlers, it began to infest apple trees in the same areas. Apple and hawthorn races now coexist in the northeastern United States; the flies mate on their host, the races differ in host preference, which reduces interbreeding to about $5\,\%$, and in the timing of adult emergence, matched to the fruiting of their hosts (apples fruit several weeks earlier than hawthorns), which causes temporal isolation and makes flies that develop on the wrong host diapause at the wrong time. The races differ in allele frequency at loci in three regions of the genome, which turned out to carry three inversions that suppress recombination across roughly half the genome; the inversions found in the apple race derive from Mexican populations, so part of the variation probably arose in geographically distant populations before spreading into apple-growing regions. The case thus combines sympatric and geographic elements.

The best evidence of sympatric speciation comes from sister species confined to small, isolated areas with little opportunity for spatial separation.

- Lord Howe Island, a small ($15\,\mathrm{km^2}$), remote island between Australia and New Zealand, has two endemic sister species of palms, the kentia palm (*Howea forsteriana*) and the curly palm (*H. belmoreana*). They often grow close together and are wind-pollinated, so it is unlikely that a physical barrier ever separated them. Their main difference is a six-week difference in peak flowering time, which leaves only a short window for cross-pollination; the kentia palm grows more often on calcareous soils, and its flowering time is shifted when it grows on non-calcareous soil, suggesting that divergent selection on soil adaptation pleiotropically changed flowering time. Perhaps a third of the endemic plant species of the island may have arisen there by sympatric speciation.
- Two small crater lakes in Cameroon, Barombi Mbo ($4.2\,\mathrm{km^2}$) and Bermin ($0.6\,\mathrm{km^2}$), formed in homogeneous, conical volcanic craters isolated from the surrounding river system; they contain 11 and 9 endemic cichlid species, respectively, each set monophyletic, and in each lake the deepest split separates species feeding on plankton from those feeding on the bottom. In another small crater lake, Lake Ejagham, two forms of the cichlid *Tilapia* cf. *deckerti* associated with shallow and deep water interbreed but mate assortatively by size, suggesting incipient speciation in sympatry.
- Only one example is known in birds: the band-rumped storm-petrel (*Oceanodroma castro*), which has split into sympatric populations that breed at different seasons on the same islands.

Most evolutionary biologists believe that allopatric speciation is much more common than sympatric speciation, but many cases of sympatric speciation may go undetected, because a past episode of allopatry is difficult to rule out.

### 12.2.4. Parapatric Speciation

Parapatric speciation, in which adjacent populations diverge while continuing to interbreed, involves less gene flow than sympatric speciation and should be more common. Selection can maintain sharp differences between connected populations whenever the habitats differ over distances larger than the characteristic length $\sqrt{\sigma^2/2s}$ ([7.10.10](/7.10.10#sec-10.5.2)), and incompatibilities can accumulate in different parts of a large range and meet along narrow clines. The White Sands region of New Mexico consists of geologically young gypsum dunes, starkly white against the surrounding dark soils. In three lizard species living on both soil types, the dune populations differ in head shape, toe length and, most strikingly, colour, all thought to be adaptive. In two of them, the fence lizard *Sceloporus undulatus* and the earless lizard *Holbrookia maculata*, the frequencies of nuclear genetic markers differ strongly across the habitat boundary, implying that partial reproductive isolation has reduced gene flow; pale coloration is associated with differences in the colour of ventral patches displayed in sexual and social encounters, and partial sexual isolation has been demonstrated in at least one species. The whiptail lizard *Aspidoscelis inornata*, the most active species with the highest gene flow, shows the least genetic differentiation.

It is hard to tell whether present-day hybrid zones arose in situ, in parapatry, or by secondary contact. Where many clines coincide in one place, secondary contact is likely; in northern latitudes many hybrid zones must have formed after the last glaciation, although the differences evolved over far longer periods. Since most evolution takes place in widespread species, much divergence may originally have occurred in parapatry, and the present association between barriers and divergence may reflect the subsequent shuffling of ranges.

## 12.3. The Genomics of Speciation

Genome sequencing has made it possible to identify the number, identity and location of the loci involved in reproductive isolation, and to ask how divergence is distributed across the genome when populations diverge with some gene flow.

### 12.3.1. Islands of Divergence

When two populations or species are partly isolated but continue to hybridize, introgression is expected to be uneven across the genome. Neutral regions become more similar through gene flow, and a mutation favourable in both species can sweep through both and homogenize its region, as with the colour-pattern genes of *Heliconius*. Regions carrying loci that cause isolation (or are under divergent selection) resist gene flow and remain differentiated, and so do linked neutral regions.

**Formula 12.3.1 (Effective Migration Near a Barrier Locus)** Let a neutral site be at recombination fraction $r$ from a locus at which immigrant alleles are selected against with coefficient $s$. A neutral allele entering with a migrant persists only if it recombines onto a resident background before the linked immigrant allele is eliminated, which happens with probability about $r/(r + s)$. The effective migration rate at the site is therefore

$$
m_{\mathrm{e}} \approx m\,\frac{r}{r + s}, \tag{12.2}
$$
and its expected differentiation is $F_{\mathrm{ST}} \approx 1/(1 + 4Nm_{\mathrm{e}})$.

**Proof** Each generation, the linked immigrant allele survives selection with probability $1 - s$, and the neutral site recombines away from it with probability $r$. The probability that recombination happens before elimination is $\sum_{t\geq0}(1 - r)^t(1 - s)^tr \approx r/(r + s)$ for small $r$ and $s$. Replacing $m$ by $m_{\mathrm{e}}$ in the island model gives the second statement. $\square$

Regions with high differentiation against a background of low differentiation are called genomic islands of divergence (or "islands of speciation" when they contain loci for reproductive isolation). By Eq. (12.2), their width scales with $s$ in units of recombination: strong barrier loci produce broad islands, weak ones narrow peaks; and islands are wide in regions of low recombination, such as near centromeres or within inversions.

Mosquitoes of the *Anopheles gambiae* complex, the main vectors of malaria in Africa, include six species that diverged recently and still hybridize. Some genomic regions, particularly inversions, are similar between some species pairs because they have introgressed after hybridization, whereas other regions show unusually high divergence. The X chromosome in particular differs more than the autosomes and has deep gene trees, suggesting that X-linked genes were among the first to contribute to reproductive isolation, in line with the large-X effect ([7.10.11](/7.10.11#sec-11.5)).

The interpretation of islands requires care. Islands are predicted when speciation proceeds with gene flow, but also when populations that diverged in allopatry come back into contact: divergent selection then maintains the regions where adaptive differences were fixed, while neutral regions introgress. Distinguishing speciation with gene flow from secondary contact faces the same difficulty as distinguishing sympatric origin from secondary sympatry. A further complication is that relative measures of divergence such as $F_{\mathrm{ST}}$ are also elevated where diversity within populations is low, as in regions of low recombination affected by **linked selection** ([7.10.8](/7.10.8)), even without any barrier to gene flow; absolute measures of divergence between populations, such as the mean number of differences between sequences from the two populations ($d_{XY}$), help to distinguish reduced gene flow from reduced diversity. Gene trees add information: under incomplete lineage sorting or gene flow, genealogies vary along the genome, and the **multispecies coalescent** with migration (the **isolation-with-migration model**, [7.10.25](/7.10.25)) can estimate the timing and amount of gene flow from many loci.

### 12.3.2. Inversions and Coadapted Alleles

Chromosomal rearrangements contribute little to reproductive isolation through their direct effects on meiosis, yet rates of speciation across groups of vertebrates correlate with rates of chromosomal evolution: primates and horses have high rates of both, whereas whales are chromosomally conservative and less species-rich. One explanation is that rearrangements, especially **inversions** ([7.4.15](/7.4.15)), suppress recombination in heterozygotes and thereby protect combinations of alleles from gene flow.

*Drosophila pseudoobscura* and *D. persimilis* are found together along the west coast of North America and occasionally hybridize. They differ by inversions that cause little sterility by themselves but prevent recombination in hybrids. All the factors for reproductive isolation that have been mapped, including cuticular hydrocarbons and male wing vibrations used in courtship, female mating preferences, hybrid inviability and hybrid sterility, lie in or near the inverted regions. The genealogies of genes in these regions follow the species, whereas genes in collinear regions show evidence of gene exchange; only about one successful hybrid per generation is needed to shuffle genealogies in this way. *Drosophila* species pairs that do not differ by chromosomal rearrangements are almost never found in the same place, whereas pairs with chromosomal differences often are. The apple and hawthorn races of *Rhagoletis* show the same pattern (§12.2.3).

The mechanism (Noor et al. 2001; Rieseberg 2001; Kirkpatrick and Barton 2006) is that an inversion carrying a set of locally favoured alleles, or alleles for isolation, spreads because it keeps them together, and while gene flow homogenizes the rest of the genome, further incompatibilities and adaptations can accumulate within the protected region. A single inversion can also bind together the components of a complex adaptation, as in the supergene of *Heliconius numata* ([7.10.9](/7.10.9#sec-9.6.3)). In general, the evolution of species involves a reduction of recombination between diverging gene pools, which in turn accelerates divergence; the converse question, why most species maintain high levels of sex and recombination, is the subject of [7.10.14](/7.10.14).

## Questions

1. Why can reinforcement strengthen prezygotic but not postzygotic isolation? Describe one situation in which selection could favour lower viability of hybrid embryos.

   **Answer** An allele that reduces the chance of mating with the other population increases the number of fit (non-hybrid) offspring of its carriers, so it spreads. An allele that further reduces hybrid fitness only lowers its carriers' reproductive success, because the hybrid already exists and is the parent's offspring. The exception arises when offspring compete for limited parental resources, as embryos do in mammals and in plant ovaries: early abortion of a hybrid embryo with poor prospects can free resources for non-hybrid siblings, and an allele causing it can be favoured.

2. Using Formula 12.1.1, explain why chromosomal speciation by drift is expected to proceed through many rearrangements of small effect rather than one of large effect, and what this implies for the time needed.

   **Answer** The fixation probability falls roughly as $\mathrm{e}^{-Ns}$, so a rearrangement with large $s$ is essentially never fixed by drift, whereas one with $s$ of order $1/N$ has a reasonable chance. Each such rearrangement contributes only a small reduction of hybrid fertility, so strong isolation requires many fixations, each rare and slow; isolation by this route accumulates slowly, and in a subdivided species also requires extinction and recolonization for the rearrangement to spread beyond the deme.

3. A newly formed tetraploid plant is surrounded by its diploid progenitors. Under random mating with the diploids, what fraction of its offspring are tetraploid when the tetraploids are at frequency $q$? Which traits help the tetraploid to escape this minority disadvantage?

   **Answer** With random mating, a tetraploid's ovules are fertilized by tetraploid pollen with probability $q$ and by diploid pollen with probability $1 - q$; only the former give tetraploid offspring, the latter sterile triploids. When $q$ is small, almost all offspring are triploid, so the rare tetraploid has much lower fitness than the diploids (positive frequency dependence that eliminates it). Self-fertilization, vegetative reproduction, a long perennial life (more chances to meet another tetraploid) and differences in habitat or flowering time that reduce mating with diploids all help, and many polyploids show these traits.

4. Explain Felsenstein's distinction between one-allele and two-allele mechanisms, and classify (a) a preference for mates of one's own colour, with red and blue colours associated with different bill sizes, (b) an allele that makes individuals mate on the host plant on which they developed.

   **Answer** In a two-allele mechanism, isolation requires different alleles in the two groups at a mating locus, associated with the ecologically selected alleles; recombination between them destroys the association. In a one-allele mechanism, a single allele spreading through both groups increases isolation, so recombination does not oppose it. (a) is a two-allele mechanism: red must stay associated with stout bills and blue with thin ones. (b) is a one-allele mechanism: the same allele for mating on the natal host increases assortative mating in both host races, which is why host races of insects are among the most plausible cases of sympatric speciation.

5. A genome scan of two hybridizing species reveals a few regions with very high $F_{\mathrm{ST}}$. List alternative explanations besides "speciation genes", and how they could be distinguished.

   **Answer** (1) The regions carry loci under divergent selection that have nothing to do with isolation (local adaptation) but resist gene flow equally; testing hybrid fitness and mating with genotypes at these regions distinguishes this. (2) Low recombination combined with linked selection (background selection, sweeps) reduces diversity within populations and inflates $F_{\mathrm{ST}}$ without any barrier; absolute divergence ($d_{XY}$) is not elevated in that case, whereas a true barrier increases $d_{XY}$ because gene flow has been reduced there for a long time. (3) The pattern may result from secondary contact after allopatric divergence rather than divergence with gene flow; demographic models (isolation-with-migration, [7.10.25](/7.10.25)) fitted to many loci and the age of introgressed tracts help. (4) Inversions differing between species produce broad regions of high differentiation; cytogenetic or long-read assemblies reveal them.
