Humans are descended, like every other living thing, from the last universal common ancestor, and share with all organisms inheritance by nucleic acids, the genetic code and proteins of L-amino acids ([7.10.27](/7.10.27)). Along the path to *Homo sapiens* lie the origin of eukaryotes, of animals, of vertebrates, of tetrapods and amniotes ([7.10.28](/7.10.28), [7.10.29](/7.10.29)); primates appeared by about 70 Myr ago, the Old World monkeys and apes (catarrhines) about 35 Myr ago. This chapter treats the last few million years: where the human lineage fits among the apes, what the fossils of hominins show, how genomes, including those of extinct humans, have rewritten the story of our origin and dispersal, how the genetic variation of present-day humans is structured, and how selection, drift and culture have shaped us since agriculture. Darwin began this program in *The Descent of Man* (1871) by replacing origin myths with testable hypotheses; it now draws on paleontology, genomics and ancient DNA, whose methods are treated in [7.4.14](/7.4.14) and [7.8.13](/7.8.13). Every chapter of this course bears on it, and the social questions raised by human evolution follow in [7.10.34](/7.10.34).

## 33.1. Our Place among the Apes

### 33.1.1. Our Closest Living Relatives

Humans are apes, distinguished, like the other apes, from monkeys most visibly by the lack of an external tail. Among living apes the gibbons branched first, then the orangutans, then the gorillas; our closest relatives are the chimpanzee (*Pan troglodytes*) and the bonobo (*Pan paniscus*). In current classification the apes form the superfamily Hominoidea, with two families: the Hylobatidae (gibbons, the "lesser apes") and the Hominidae, which comprises the great apes and humans, divided into the subfamilies Ponginae (orangutans) and Homininae (gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos and humans). The tribe Hominini groups humans with the chimpanzees and bonobos, and the human lineage proper is the subtribe Hominina; in the older usage, now abandoned, Hominidae included only humans and their extinct relatives, which is why the human lineage is now called the hominins rather than the hominids. The human lineage diverged from the chimpanzee lineage about 7 Myr ago; estimates from fossils and molecular clocks differ by a few million years, because mutation rates are uncertain, because hybridization may have continued after the lineages began to diverge, and because a fossil's age shows when its lineage lived, not when it split from others.

Humans and chimpanzees differ at about 1 % of aligned nucleotide sites and by less than 2 % in the sequences of shared protein-coding genes; counting genes deleted or duplicated in one lineage, the difference rises to about 6 %. The speciation events were so close in time, relative to the ancestral population sizes, that the gene trees of different parts of the genome disagree: in a substantial fraction of the genome (roughly 30 % in genome-wide analyses) humans are more closely related to gorillas, or chimpanzees to gorillas, than humans to chimpanzees (**incomplete lineage sorting**, [7.10.25](/7.10.25)). At several loci humans and chimpanzees share allelic lineages older than the species, maintained by **balancing selection**, probably related to pathogens; at the MHC some human alleles are closer to chimpanzee alleles than to other human alleles ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.7.8)).

The other African apes are highly social, with males much larger than females, a consequence of sexual selection. Chimpanzees crack nuts on stone anvils, fish for termites with twigs and hunt monkeys cooperatively, and learn these skills by imitation; captive apes can learn sign language or symbol systems to a rudimentary degree. Their common ancestor with us surely had an opposable big toe, arms longer than legs, a projecting lower face, large canines, plentiful body hair and a small brain.

### 33.1.2. How Humans Differ from Other Apes

- *Bipedalism.* Humans are fully bipedal and adapted for walking and running. The pelvis is short and broad and anchors the muscles that stabilize the body; the legs are long and angled inward beneath the center of gravity; the lumbar spine is curved; the foramen magnum, through which the spinal cord leaves the skull, lies forward, so that the face points ahead when standing; the big toe is aligned with the others and the foot has a rigid arch. When bipedalism evolved, the African climate had become drier and hominins lived in open woodland; erect posture may have aided walking between patches, picking fruit and later running while hunting, and evaporative cooling by sweating probably selected for reduced body hair.
- *Hands and teeth.* Human fingers are shorter and straighter, the thumb longer and more opposable, allowing both power and precision grips; the canines are small and the face flat.
- *Brain.* Relative to body mass, the human brain is about three times as large as those of other primates and five times those of most mammals.
- *Life history.* Human babies are large but helpless and need a long childhood of care. Chimpanzee females care for an infant for about five years and do not conceive during that time, whereas human mothers can have more children while older ones are still dependent, so the potential growth rate of human populations is much greater (**life history**, [7.10.15](/7.10.15)). The costs are met by a higher metabolic rate and more body fat than any other ape (§33.5).

## 33.2. Hominins through Time

**Definition 33.2.1 (Hominin)** **Hominins** are the members of the clade that includes *Homo sapiens* and all species more closely related to it than to chimpanzees and bonobos.

Darwin predicted in *The Descent of Man* that, because living mammals in each region are related to the extinct species of the same region, our early ancestors probably lived in Africa, home of the gorilla and chimpanzee. More than fifty years later the first hominin fossils were found in South Africa: in 1925 Raymond Dart described the skull of a child from Taung as *Australopithecus africanus*, a small-brained, apparently upright "man-ape". It was dismissed for some twenty years, partly because most anthropologists expected the human ancestor to have evolved a large brain first and to have lived in Eurasia, an expectation reinforced by the "Piltdown man" from England, a modern human cranium combined with an orangutan jaw, which was exposed as a forgery only in 1953. The subsequent history of paleoanthropology has confirmed the prediction: the origin and most of the later evolution of hominins happened in Africa. After diverging from the chimpanzee lineage, hominins proliferated into many species, most of which were not our ancestors but relatives on side branches; several usually coexisted (figure below). Some are known from a few fragments, so the number of species and their relationships are uncertain, but the main features of the history are agreed.

- *The earliest hominins.* *Sahelanthropus tchadensis* (Chad, 6–7 Myr ago), *Orrorin tugenensis* (Kenya, about 6 Myr) and *Ardipithecus* (Ethiopia) are accepted as hominins by most anthropologists; if so, *Sahelanthropus* sets a minimum age for the split from the chimpanzee lineage. *Ardipithecus ramidus*, from deposits 4.4 Myr old in Ethiopia and described in detail from a partial skeleton in a set of eleven papers in 2009, combined an ape-sized brain and an opposable big toe for climbing with small canines (enlarged in male apes for fighting) and a pelvis adapted for upright walking.
- *Australopithecus.* The earliest species of the genus, *Australopithecus anamensis*, lived in Kenya and Ethiopia about 4.2–3.8 Myr ago. *Australopithecus afarensis*, about 3.9–2.9 Myr ago in eastern Africa, is represented by the unusually complete partial skeleton "Lucy" from Hadar, Ethiopia, found by Donald Johanson in 1974 (about 40 % of a skeleton), and by the skeleton of a three-year-old child from Dikika, Ethiopia, described in 2006. It kept a projecting face, large canines, long arms and a brain of about $400\,\mathrm{cm^3}$, but its limbs show that it walked upright as well as climbed, and fossil footprints in volcanic ash at Laetoli, Tanzania, about 3.6 Myr old, record bipedal walking. Bipedalism was the first distinctively human trait to evolve; a large brain came much later. Stone tools capable of butchering animals are known from about 3.3 Myr ago, before the earliest *Homo*. *Australopithecus africanus* of South Africa, thought to descend from *A. afarensis*, had a larger cranial capacity.
- *Paranthropus.* The "robust" australopithecines, with massive jaws and molars, lived from about 2.7 to 1 Myr ago and died out without contributing to our ancestry.
- *Early Homo.* The earliest fossil attributed to *Homo* is a jaw about 2.8 Myr old from Ethiopia. *Homo habilis* had a flatter face, a shorter tooth row, a more humanlike hand and a larger brain; its legs and feet show a nearly human gait, and it made stone tools and butchered animals.
- *Homo erectus.* Fossils from about 1.9 Myr ago until well under 200 000 years ago (in Java perhaps until about 110 000 years ago) are usually referred to *H. erectus* (its early African form is often called *H. ergaster*). It had a rounded skull, a less projecting face, smaller teeth and a brain of about $1000\,\mathrm{cm^3}$, and is generally considered our ancestor. It was the first hominin to leave Africa, reaching the Caucasus (Dmanisi, Georgia, about 1.8 Myr ago) and later China, Java and Europe, and made more sophisticated stone tools. In China it is known from Yuanmou and Lantian, more than a million years old, and from the cave of Zhoukoudian near Beijing, where "Peking man" (*H. erectus pekinensis*), excavated from 1927, is dated to roughly 0.8–0.4 Myr ago; the much younger modern humans of the same area, from Tianyuan Cave (about 40 000 years ago) and the Upper Cave of Zhoukoudian, are not its descendants but belong to the expansion of *H. sapiens* (§33.3), and the genome of the Tianyuan man is related to those of present-day East Asians and Native Americans. Fire was used in southern Africa about 1 Myr ago and widely by 500 000 years ago. On the Indonesian island of Flores lived *H. floresiensis*, about a meter tall with a tiny brain but stone tools, perhaps descended from *H. erectus* or an earlier hominin; in South Africa lived the small-brained *H. naledi* between about 335 000 and 236 000 years ago. Several kinds of hominins thus coexisted with early *H. sapiens*.
- *The archaic humans of Eurasia.* From about 600 000 years ago a second wave of hominins, often called *H. heidelbergensis*, spread across Europe and Asia. In Europe and western Asia their descendants were the Neanderthals.

**Definition 33.2.2 (Neanderthals and Denisovans)** **Neanderthals** (*Homo neanderthalensis*, or *H. sapiens neanderthalensis* for authors who treat them as a subspecies) are archaic humans who lived in Europe and western and central Asia from about 400 000 to about 40 000 years ago, named after the Neander Valley in Germany where their fossils were first recognized. **Denisovans** are an archaic group known mainly from DNA, first from a finger bone about 50 000 years old in Denisova Cave in the Altai Mountains of Siberia, whose genome showed them to be a sister group of the Neanderthals.

Neanderthals had dense bones, a thick skull and a projecting brow, and brains as large as or larger than ours (up to about $1500\,\mathrm{cm^3}$); they made sophisticated stone tools, controlled fire, buried their dead and made ornaments. They became extinct about 40 000 years ago, after tens of thousands of years of coexistence with modern humans in parts of their range; competition with our ancestors was probably a major cause. Through hominin history average brain size increased, and the teeth, face, pelvis, hands and feet changed, at different rates: mosaic evolution again ([7.10.32](/7.10.32)). Whatever details remain unresolved, it has been established that modern humans evolved from an apelike ancestor.

## 33.3. The Origin and Spread of *Homo sapiens*

### 33.3.1. Out of Africa or Multiregional?

The oldest fossils generally assigned to *Homo sapiens* come from Africa: skulls from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, about 300 000 years old, with a modern face and a still elongated braincase, and fossils from Omo Kibish, Ethiopia, more than 200 000 years old. Two hypotheses competed for the origin of modern humans. The *multiregional* model held that *H. erectus* populations that spread through the Old World more than 1.7 Myr ago evolved into modern humans in several regions at once, connected by gene flow; the *recent African origin* (out-of-Africa) model held that modern humans arose in Africa and replaced the archaic populations elsewhere.

Genetic data decided between them. Both models predict that the deepest split in the tree of human populations separates Africans from non-Africans, but they predict very different dates: more than a million years under the multiregional model, much less under the African origin. Estimates from many kinds of markers put the deepest splits well under 200 000 years ago. The **most recent common ancestor** of human mtDNA lived about 100 000–200 000 years ago and that of the Y chromosome at a broadly comparable time ([7.4.23](/7.4.23#sec-23.8.1)); no deeply divergent "archaic" lineages of mtDNA or Y chromosomes have been found in any population, as would be expected if they had descended from Asian or European *H. erectus*. Gene trees of mtDNA have their deepest branches in Africa, and all non-African lineages descend from one branch of the African tree. Neanderthal mtDNA, first sequenced from fossils in 1997, lies outside the variation of all living humans, diverging about 600 000 years ago. A single locus, however, is one realization of the coalescent ([7.10.7](/7.10.7)): with an effective size of about $10^4$, the expected time to the common ancestor of all copies of an autosomal locus is $4N_{\mathrm{e}}$ generations, roughly 800 000 years with a generation time of 20 years (about a million years with 25–30 years), and some loci have much deeper genealogies. The decisive evidence is genome-wide.

Modern humans spread out of Africa about 60 000 years ago; DNA sequence variation suggests that the founding population was small, perhaps a few thousand individuals, from which almost all of the billions of people living outside Africa descend. They reached Australia, Europe and East Asia between about 60 000 and 40 000 years ago, crossed into the Americas from Siberia when the Bering land bridge was dry, and spread through North and South America within a few thousand years: in less than 50 000 years one species colonized every continent except Antarctica. Each colonization of a new region by a small group was a genetic bottleneck, and the bottlenecks accumulated with distance: genetic diversity is highest, and linkage disequilibrium lowest, in African populations, and heterozygosity declines almost linearly with geographic distance from East Africa along likely routes of migration (the *serial founder* effect, figure in §33.3.3).

### 33.3.2. Interbreeding with Neanderthals and Denisovans

In 2010 the group of Svante Pääbo published a draft Neanderthal genome sequenced from fossil bones, and in the same year the genome of the Denisovan finger bone ([7.4.14](/7.4.14#sec-14.6)). The comparisons showed that the out-of-Africa model was right in its main outline but incomplete: as modern humans spread through Eurasia, they interbred with archaic humans, more than once (figure below).

![A simplified history of modern and archaic humans, after Futuyma and Kirkpatrick (2017), Fig. 21.14, with approximate times in thousands of years ago. The ancestors of Neanderthals and Denisovans separated from those of modern humans several hundred thousand years ago and split into a western (Neanderthal) and an eastern (Denisovan) lineage; modern humans left Africa about 60 000 years ago. Arrows: gene flow from Neanderthals into the ancestors of all non-Africans (with further, smaller pulses, for example into East Asians), and from Denisovans into the ancestors of present-day Oceanians.](./plots/archaic-admixture.py)

The evidence comes from statistics of shared derived alleles: the **D statistic** ([7.10.25](/7.10.25)) and the $f$-statistics ([7.8.13](/7.8.13#sec-13.3.3)) test whether a Neanderthal genome shares more derived alleles with one modern population than with another, which a tree without gene flow does not allow. Non-Africans share more derived alleles with Neanderthals than Africans do, and about 2 % of the genomes of present-day Europeans and Asians derive from Neanderthals, from interbreeding about 45 000–60 000 years ago, after the exodus from Africa, followed by smaller later pulses. Present-day Africans have little Neanderthal ancestry (some, acquired later by back-migration from Eurasia). Melanesians and other Oceanians carry several percent of Denisovan ancestry in addition, and smaller amounts are found in East and South Asians. Denisovans were not confined to Siberia: a mandible from Baishiya Karst Cave, at 3280 m on the Tibetan Plateau near Xiahe, Gansu, at least 160 000 years old, was identified as Denisovan from its ancient proteins (Chen and colleagues 2019), and Denisovan mitochondrial DNA was later recovered from the sediments of the cave (Zhang and colleagues 2020); and the Denisovan genome itself carries traces of gene flow from a still more divergent hominin. Ancient genomes of early modern humans from Europe and Siberia, some with Neanderthal ancestors only a few generations back, have since confirmed the timing: a jawbone from Peştera cu Oase in Romania, about 40 000 years old, carried 6–9 % Neanderthal DNA in long blocks, implying a Neanderthal ancestor four to six generations earlier, yet that population left little trace in later Europeans (Fu and colleagues 2015). In 51 Eurasian genomes from 45 000 to 7000 years ago, the proportion of Neanderthal ancestry declined from 3–6 % to about 2 %, consistent with selection against Neanderthal alleles (Fu and colleagues 2016). Gene flow also ran the other way, and earlier: the genome of a Neanderthal from the Altai carries DNA from modern humans who had left Africa about 100 000 years ago, a population that left no descendants among living people (Kuhlwilm and colleagues 2016); a bone fragment from Denisova Cave even belonged to a first-generation offspring of a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father.

Introgressed alleles have been both helpful and harmful. Some Neanderthal alleles affecting skin and the immune system rose in frequency under selection, and an allele of *EPAS1*, a gene involved in the response to low oxygen, that helps Tibetans live at high altitude was introgressed from Denisovans (**introgression**, [7.10.12](/7.10.12)). Other regions of the modern genome are significantly depleted of Neanderthal DNA, including much of the X chromosome and genes expressed in the testes, probably because selection removed archaic alleles that were deleterious in a modern genetic background or in the larger modern population ([7.10.6](/7.10.6)). The phylogeny of hominins, on which modern humans are one leaf, looks less like a tree than a tangled bush.

### 33.3.3. The Diversity of Human Populations

As humans colonized the planet, populations diverged genetically, by drift during founding and isolation and by local adaptation, producing differences in skin color, stature, face shape, body fat and many other traits. These visible differences give a misleading impression of genetic differences overall. Among East Asians, Yoruba from Nigeria and Europeans, $F_{\mathrm{ST}}$ ([7.10.10](/7.10.10)) is about 0.12: the differences among these populations account for only about 12 % of their total genetic variation, and 88 % is found within each of them; worldwide, $F_{\mathrm{ST}}$ is about 0.1–0.15, typical of animal species. Richard Lewontin first reported in 1972, from blood-group and protein polymorphisms, that about 85 % of human genetic variation lies within populations, and genomic data confirm the pattern. The low differentiation reflects the small number of generations since the expansion out of Africa and continuing gene flow; many other species are far more fragmented over smaller ranges.

Variation is mostly *clinal*: allele frequencies and phenotypes change gradually across geography, with sharper changes at major barriers (deserts, oceans) and, within continents, sometimes at linguistic boundaries, and the gradients of different genes and traits run in different directions (**cline**, **isolation by distance**, [7.10.10](/7.10.10)). The serial founder process produces such gradients directly (figure below). Individual genomes can nevertheless be assigned to their region of origin with high accuracy, because many small differences in frequency add up across thousands of loci (ancestry inference, [7.8.13](/7.8.13#sec-13.3.2)); this is a statistical aggregate, not evidence of discrete groups.

![The serial founder model of human expansion, simulated. A chain of 25 populations is founded one after another, each by 50 individuals from the previous one; populations then grow to 2000 and exchange migrants with their neighbors; 300 neutral loci. A: heterozygosity falls almost linearly with the order of founding, as human heterozygosity falls with distance from East Africa. B: for two populations at increasing distance along the chain, the fraction of their combined genetic variation found within populations remains high, and differences grow gradually with distance, without discrete boundaries.](./plots/serial-founder.py)

For these reasons the concept of human "races", groups with discrete boundaries and distinctive sets of traits, is not useful in biology. Traditional racial classifications, such as the division of humans into three, four or five great races by skin color and other visible traits, sort a continuous distribution into arbitrary categories; they rest on a few traits that are adaptations to local environments and are not concordant with the rest of the genome; they do not correspond to the actual history of populations, which is one of repeated splitting, mixing and expansion; and the greatest genetic diversity of all, that of Africans, would be lumped into one group. Many of the visible differences are adaptations, often convergent. Dark skin, ancestral in humans, protects against ultraviolet radiation, and lighter skin, which permits vitamin D synthesis under weak sunlight, evolved at high latitudes, by mutations at different loci in Europeans and East Asians; an allele of *SLC24A5* (the human homolog of a gene for pigmentation in zebrafish, in which a threonine replaces an alanine) explains a substantial part of the difference in pigmentation between Europeans and West Africans and lies in a region of reduced variation, the signature of a recent **selective sweep** ([7.10.8](/7.10.8)). Biologists therefore refer to the people of a region as a *population*, which connects humans with other species and avoids the baggage of the word "race". Ancestry can still matter in medicine, because some alleles affecting disease and drug response differ in frequency among populations, but the relevant variable is the genotype itself, not a racial label ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)).

## 33.4. Brain and Language

Our brain gives us cognitive abilities far beyond those of any other species, and with them language. Two hypotheses have been proposed for its enlargement. The ecological hypothesis holds that selection favored the ability to learn and function in complex environments, for example in foraging and hunting. Robin Dunbar's *social brain hypothesis* holds that life in complex groups, with alliances for hunting and resolving conflicts, selected for a large neocortex, the region of learning, memory and cognition; across primates the relative size of the neocortex is correlated with mean group size, and that of humans corresponds to groups of about 100–200 individuals, a plausible size for early human communities. Gene expression in the brain has changed faster in the human lineage than in that of chimpanzees, although brain-expressed genes are otherwise conservative across primates; the regulatory region of the prodynorphin gene *PDYN*, an endogenous opioid involved in pain, attachment and learning, shows several substitutions on the human lineage and drives higher expression in neural cells than the chimpanzee version. Brain size is not the whole story: Neanderthal brains were as large as ours, yet they may have differed in the production of neurons. In the transketolase-like gene *TKTL1*, expressed in the progenitor cells of the fetal neocortex, almost all modern humans carry arginine at position 317, where Neanderthals, Denisovans and other apes have lysine; in experiments in mouse and ferret embryos and human brain organoids, the modern human variant increases the number of basal radial glia, the progenitors that generate most neocortical neurons, whereas the archaic variant does not (Pinson and colleagues 2022). The functional significance of such differences for cognition is not yet known.

Language, according to Steven Pinker, rests on metaphorical abstraction (words as symbols for objects, actions and concepts) and combinatorial structure (meaning conveyed by the arrangement of words); it transmits information cheaply and lets it accumulate across individuals and generations. It probably evolved in steps, each useful: a set of symbols used beyond specific situations, an open class of words built by combining sounds, and then syntax, from word order to hierarchical phrases and inflection. Our vocal versatility depends on a larynx lower in the throat than in other apes, giving an L-shaped vocal tract; that this arrangement lets us choke on food is testimony to a strong advantage of speech. Captive apes show some of the cognitive ingredients: the bonobo Kanzi learned symbols spontaneously by watching attempts to teach his mother, came to use hundreds of them and formed requests about other individuals. So at least rudimentary cognitive capacities for language, though not the anatomy of speech, probably go back to our common ancestor with the chimpanzees. Tool use is not unique to humans either: New Caledonian crows (*Corvus moneduloides*) make hooked tools from twigs and leaves, and bonobos have learned to strike stone flakes to cut a string, as our ancestors were doing 3.3 Myr ago.

The gene *FOXP2* illustrates the difficulty of finding genes for human traits. Mutations in this transcription factor were found in a family with a severe disorder of articulation and grammar. Two amino acid substitutions arose on the human lineage after the split from chimpanzees, and early analyses of variation around the gene suggested a selective sweep within the last 200 000 years, prompting the idea that *FOXP2* had a role in the origin of language. But Neanderthals turned out to share both substitutions, and later analyses of larger and more diverse samples found no clear evidence of a recent sweep. A gene in which mutations disrupt speech is not necessarily a gene "for" language: *FOXP2* is also involved in the development of the lungs and of other neural circuits, and many genes show signatures of selection. Language, like other complex traits, is likely to depend on many genes of small effect.

## 33.5. Diet, Agriculture and Recent Selection

The large brain is expensive. It consumes about 20 % of an adult's basal metabolism and up to 60 % of an infant's, and takes about 18 years to grow, longer than in any other primate; the large head makes birth difficult. Humans are paradoxical primates: they reproduce more often, with larger babies, yet live longer, which the usual life-history trade-offs would not predict ([7.10.15](/7.10.15)). The solution was a larger energy budget: humans have a higher metabolic rate for their fat-free mass than the other great apes and much more body fat. This was made possible by a shift from fruit to meat and tubers, first by scavenging and later by hunting, which in turn selected for endurance running and throwing, and by slicing, pounding and cooking food. Humans became such effective hunters that their arrival on each continent was followed by the extinction of many large mammals and birds, the mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, giant ground sloths and, in New Zealand, the moas, among others ([7.10.31](/7.10.31)).

Agriculture appeared first in the Middle East at least 11 000 years ago and was invented independently over the next several thousand years in China, Mexico, New Guinea, the Andes, Africa and eastern North America. Many crops share convergent *domestication traits*: large seeds, loss of seed dormancy and seeds that stay on the plant rather than shattering, which harvesting and replanting select automatically. Domestication usually reduced the effective population size, letting slightly deleterious alleles drift to high frequency, a cost of domestication seen in many crops and livestock ([7.10.6](/7.10.6)). The best-studied example of the genetics of domestication, maize from teosinte, is treated in [7.10.19](/7.10.19).

*Domestication*, the conversion of a wild species into one whose reproduction is controlled by people, began with an animal before any plant. The dog descends from the gray wolf (*Canis lupus*), but where, when and from which wolves have been debated for two decades, with each new kind of data giving a different answer:

Two conclusions survive: dogs were domesticated by hunter-gatherers well before agriculture, at least 15 000 years ago, and the wolf population they came from no longer exists, so that no living wolf population is the "ancestor"; early dogs also mixed with wolves locally, which confuses the signal of origin. The history of horses shows the same complexity. Horses herded at Botai in northern Kazakhstan about 5500 years ago, long regarded as the first domestic horses, turned out from ancient genomes to be ancestors of the Przewalski's horse, not of domestic horses: Przewalski's horses, often called the last wild horses, are feral descendants of these early herds (Gaunitz and colleagues 2018), and modern domestic horses come from a later domestication, in the western Eurasian steppe about 4200 years ago, that rapidly replaced other lineages (Librado and colleagues 2021); the older date in the table above rests on the Botai evidence. The European wild horse, the tarpan, became extinct around 1900.

Agriculture spread worldwide only about 300 generations ago, but it changed the human environment so radically that it left marks across the genome. Ancient DNA now lets allele frequencies be followed through time directly. Iain Mathieson and colleagues compared genomes of 230 individuals who lived in Europe and western Asia between about 8500 and 2300 years ago with those of present-day people, and found large frequency changes, the signature of selection, at a dozen loci, four of them related to the agricultural diet. The strongest signal is at the allele that causes lactase persistence, which allows adults to digest milk; it was rare in Europe until a few thousand years ago and rose to high frequency in northern Europe, and different alleles with the same effect arose independently in pastoralist populations of Africa and the Middle East ([Example 23.8.1 in 7.4.23](/7.4.23#ex-23.8.1)). Another, at *SLC22A4*, increases absorption of ergothioneine, abundant in the pre-agricultural diet but scarce in wheat; others affect skin and eye pigmentation, immunity and stature. The salivary amylase gene *AMY1*, which digests starch, varies in copy number, and populations with traditionally starch-rich diets carry more copies on average than those with low-starch diets (**gene duplication**, [7.10.18](/7.10.18)). Dense, sedentary populations also brought new diseases: malaria became widespread, and more than 50 infectious diseases, among them influenza, tuberculosis and diphtheria, came from domesticated animals, imposing strong selection on resistance alleles such as those of sickle-cell hemoglobin ([7.4.23](/7.4.23)).

## 33.6. Natural Selection, Past and Present

### 33.6.1. Genetic Load and Relaxed Selection

Not all recent genetic change has been beneficial. Alleles linked to favored mutations hitchhike with them ([7.10.8](/7.10.8)): variants near the selected *SLC22A4* allele are associated with celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease. And for most of its history the human population was small: the effective size of the ancestral African population about 100 000 years ago was only about $10^4$, the size of an endangered species today, so drift was strong and many amino acid substitutions on the human lineage were probably fixed by drift rather than selection, some of them slightly deleterious (**nearly neutral theory**, [7.10.6](/7.10.6)). The serial bottlenecks of the expansion out of Africa made selection less efficient still in the populations farthest from Africa; such populations carry more deleterious variants in the homozygous state, although how much this affects fitness is debated, because the total number of deleterious alleles per genome differs little among populations.

Does modern medicine stop selection and so allow inherited diseases to increase? The answer depends on the mode of inheritance (figure below). A recessive deleterious allele at **mutation–selection balance** ([7.4.23](/7.4.23#sec-23.4)) is carried almost entirely by healthy heterozygotes, so removing selection against homozygotes raises its frequency only by the mutation rate per generation, an exceedingly slow change. A dominant allele is exposed in every carrier: if treatment lets carriers reproduce normally, its frequency, and the incidence of the disease, rises by the mutation rate each generation from a much lower starting value, doubling in about $1/s$ generations ($s$ the selection coefficient against carriers before treatment). With a genome-wide deleterious mutation rate $U$ that may exceed 1 per generation, complete relaxation of selection would lower fitness, in the absence of medical intervention, by about $U\bar s$ per generation, $\bar s$ the average effect; because most deleterious mutations have small effects, the decline would be spread over many generations.

A recessive lethal at mutation–selection balance has frequency $q = \sqrt{\mu} \approx 0.003$; a dominant allele with fitness $1 - s$ ($s = 0.1$) in carriers has $p = \mu/s$. After selection is removed both rise by $\mu$ per generation. A: allele frequencies. B: incidence relative to the start: the dominant disease (incidence $\approx 2p$) doubles in 10 generations and rises 11-fold in 100; the recessive disease ($q^2$) rises by about three-quarters in 100 generations.](./plots/relaxed-selection.py)

### 33.6.2. Selection Today

Selection has not stopped. In the Framingham Heart Study, which has followed three generations of Americans since 1948, women with low total blood cholesterol had more children over their lifetimes than those with high levels, a directional selection gradient acting now ([7.10.9](/7.10.9)); with a heritability of about 0.6, the trait should respond. In Finland between 1935 and 1967 stabilizing selection acted on women's height and directional selection for greater height on men's. Where health care is unavailable, as for much of the world's population, selection by infectious disease remains intense.

### 33.6.3. Evolutionary Mismatch

**Definition 33.6.1 (Evolutionary Mismatch)** An **evolutionary mismatch** is a disparity between a trait that was adaptive, or at least harmless, in the environments in which a population evolved and the different environment in which it now lives, such that the trait now reduces health or fitness.

Adaptation always lags behind environmental change, and for humans the lag is conspicuous: our bodies have had only a few hundred generations to adapt to agriculture and only a few to industrial life. Appetite, digestion and metabolism geared to scarce and fluctuating food, in which storing energy as fat was advantageous, now contribute to the epidemics of obesity and type 2 diabetes; more than 400 million people had diabetes in 2015. Some genetic adaptation has occurred (variants of *TCF7L2* that lower the risk of diabetes have spread in several populations), but full adaptation would require many generations of higher mortality, by which time diets will have changed again. Bones weakened by sedentary life (osteoporosis), myopia and many other conditions have been interpreted in the same way. The *hygiene hypothesis* attributes the recent rise of allergies, asthma and other immune disorders to reduced exposure, early in life, to the diverse microbes with which humans evolved and which are needed for normal development of the immune system; children who grow up on farms are less affected, and heavy use of antibiotics in infancy increases the risk. Mismatch is a central idea of **evolutionary medicine** ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)).

## 33.7. Cultural Evolution

A revolution in human culture is evident from about 50 000 years ago onward: increasingly sophisticated stonework, abstract and figurative art, ornaments, and flutes made of bird bone and mammoth ivory about 40 000 years old from Hohle Fels in Germany; cave paintings such as those of Lascaux in France date from about 17 000 years ago. The capacity for culture rests on evolved changes in the brain, but cultural elements themselves do not evolve by changes in allele frequencies.

**Definition 33.7.1 (Cultural Evolution)** **Cultural evolution** is the change over time in the frequencies of cultural variants (behaviors, beliefs, skills, technologies, words) in a population, by their transmission through social learning, imitation and teaching (**cultural inheritance**, [7.10.4](/7.10.4)) and by processes analogous to mutation, selection and drift. Richard Dawkins called the units of cultural transmission *memes*.

Cultural evolution differs from genetic evolution in three main ways. First, cultural variants are transmitted not only vertically, from parents to children, but horizontally, among individuals of the same generation, so they can spread through a population within a generation, more like an infection than a gene; the comparison is built into the phrase "going viral" (figure below). Second, variants spread not only because they increase fitness but because they are preferentially copied: for their content (they seem useful or are easy to remember), for their psychological rewards, because of who displays them (copying of prestigious or successful individuals), or because people tend to conform to the majority (*biased transmission*). Traits that reduce fitness can therefore spread, as smoking and heavy drinking have, because they are socially attractive and addictive. Third, cultural "mutations" are often intentional. There are also close analogies: cultural drift (a craft lost when its few practitioners die before training apprentices) and selection among groups, which is more effective for culture than for genes because conformity and the enforcement of norms keep groups internally homogeneous and different from each other (**group selection**, [7.10.16](/7.10.16)).

![Genetic and cultural transmission compared. A: a gene with a selective advantage of 5 % per generation (25 years) spreads only by vertical transmission and barely changes in 60 years; a cultural variant with a 5 % advantage per transmission event, copied horizontally with each individual choosing a model four times a year, sweeps through the population within years. B: conformist transmission, $\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}t = cp(1 - p)(2p - 1)$ with $c = 0.5$ per year: the majority variant goes to fixation from any starting frequency, keeping groups internally uniform.](./plots/cultural-transmission.py)

Culture also changes genetic evolution, in *gene–culture coevolution* (dual-inheritance theory): dairying created the selection for lactase persistence, cooking and agriculture changed selection on teeth, jaws and digestive genes, and the invention of clothing and shelter let humans live in climates to which they are not physiologically adapted. Peter Richerson and Robert Boyd argue that cultural group selection, by favoring groups with cooperative norms, helps explain the extraordinary scale of human cooperation, beyond what kin selection and reciprocity alone explain ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)). Evolutionary approaches also study cultural diversity with the **comparative method**: polygyny is more common in societies whose wealth is in cattle controlled by men, and monogamy in the agricultural societies of Eurasia, where land is divided among heirs. Human behavior is cultural influence laid over biological predispositions; whatever those predispositions are, they do not tell us what we must, still less what we should, do with our lives ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)).

## Summary

- Humans are African apes, closest to chimpanzees and bonobos (split about 7 Myr ago); incomplete lineage sorting makes many gene trees discordant. Humans differ in bipedalism, hands, a brain three times the primate norm, and a life history of helpless infants, short birth intervals and high energy use.
- **Hominins** (Definition 33.2.1) proliferated in Africa: *Sahelanthropus*, *Ardipithecus*, *Australopithecus* (bipedal with small brains), *Paranthropus*, and *Homo* from about 2.8 Myr ago. *H. erectus* first left Africa; **Neanderthals** and **Denisovans** (Definition 33.2.2) descended from a later wave. The hominin tree is a bush with one surviving branch.
- *H. sapiens* arose in Africa about 300 000 years ago and spread out of Africa about 60 000 years ago; shallow mtDNA and Y genealogies, the absence of archaic lineages and genome-wide data refute the multiregional model, but modern non-Africans carry about 2 % Neanderthal DNA and Oceanians also Denisovan DNA, some of it adaptive (*EPAS1*), some purged.
- Most human genetic variation (about 85–90 %) lies within populations; variation is clinal and shaped by serial founder effects, and racial classifications do not describe it. Visible differences such as skin color are local, often convergent adaptations.
- The large brain may reflect ecological and social demands; language evolved in steps, and *FOXP2* is not a "language gene". A higher metabolism and new diets paid for the brain.
- Agriculture caused strong recent selection (lactase persistence, *SLC22A4*, pigmentation, *AMY1*), documented directly by ancient DNA. Relaxed selection raises the incidence of dominant diseases much faster than recessive ones; selection continues today; many modern diseases reflect **evolutionary mismatch** (Definition 33.6.1).
- **Cultural evolution** (Definition 33.7.1) is faster than genetic evolution because of horizontal and biased transmission; genes and culture coevolve.

## Questions

1. Why do shallow mtDNA and Y-chromosome genealogies argue against the multiregional model, and why are they not by themselves decisive?

   **Answer** Under multiregional evolution, lineages in different continents would trace back to populations separated since *H. erectus* dispersed more than 1.7 Myr ago, so mtDNA and Y genealogies should contain deep regional lineages; instead their common ancestors lived only about 100 000–200 000 years ago and no archaic lineages are found. But each is a single locus, one random realization of the coalescent; a little gene flow or a selective sweep could make a single locus shallow even under multiregionalism. Genome-wide data, many independent loci with mostly shallow, African-rooted genealogies, are decisive.

2. A D statistic $D(\text{Yoruba}, \text{French}; \text{Neanderthal}, \text{chimpanzee})$ is significantly positive (more ABBA than BABA sites, with B the Neanderthal-derived allele shared with French). What does this imply, and what alternative must be excluded?

   **Answer** On a tree in which Yoruba and French are sisters and the Neanderthal an outgroup, incomplete lineage sorting makes ABBA and BABA equally frequent, so an excess of derived alleles shared by French and Neanderthals implies gene flow between Neanderthals and the ancestors of the French. The alternative is ancient population structure within Africa (the ancestors of non-Africans being closer to the Neanderthal ancestors before they split); it is excluded by the length of Neanderthal-derived haplotypes in modern genomes, which shows that the segments entered about 45 000–60 000 years ago.

3. If $F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.12$ among three human populations, what fraction of genetic variation lies within populations? Why can individuals nevertheless be assigned to their continent of origin?

   **Answer** About $1 - 0.12 = 88$ %. Assignment uses many loci at once: each locus differs only slightly in frequency, but the small likelihood ratios multiply over thousands of loci, so the combined evidence points clearly to a region. Accurate assignment by aggregate is compatible with the fact that, at almost every single locus, variation is mostly shared and changes gradually across geography.

4. With $\mu = 10^{-5}$, compare the increase over 10 generations in the incidence of a dominant disease (heterozygote fitness 0.9 before treatment) and of a recessive lethal disease, if treatment removes selection completely.

   **Answer** Dominant: $p_0 = \mu/s = 10^{-4}$; after 10 generations $p = 10^{-4} + 10 \times 10^{-5} = 2 \times 10^{-4}$, so incidence ($\approx 2p$) doubles. Recessive: $q_0 = \sqrt{\mu} \approx 3.16 \times 10^{-3}$; after 10 generations $q \approx 3.26 \times 10^{-3}$, incidence $q^2$ rises by only about 6 %.

5. Why can a cultural trait that lowers fitness spread, whereas a genetic variant with the same effect would decline?

   **Answer** A genetic variant spreads only if its carriers leave more offspring, so lower fitness makes it decline. A cultural variant spreads if it is copied more often than it is lost, and copying depends on content, rewards, prestige of the models and conformity, not on the reproductive success of its bearers; with horizontal transmission it can spread within one generation, faster than any reduction in survival or fertility can remove it (smoking is an example).
