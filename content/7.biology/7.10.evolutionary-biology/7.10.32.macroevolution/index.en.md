Bighorn sheep evolve smaller horns under trophy hunting, and the bill of a Galápagos finch grows deeper after a drought ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)): such changes are small, rapid and explained by selection on heritable variation within populations. Over long times, however, evolution has produced forms profoundly different from their ancestors: tetrapods from lobe-finned fishes, birds from dinosaurs, whales from terrestrial mammals. **Macroevolution** is often defined as evolution above the species level ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.1.3)): the origin of higher taxa and of novel characters, the rates and directions of change over millions of years, and the trends in the history of life. Before the evolutionary synthesis, some authors held that macroevolution required processes different from those of microevolution. George Gaylord Simpson, from the rates and directions of change in the fossil record, and Bernhard Rensch, from comparative morphology and embryology, argued that it is based on microevolutionary processes and differs only in scale; their argument has largely been accepted, but questions remain about how far the processes of populations account for patterns over geological time. This chapter examines these questions: how major new forms and characters arise (§32.1–32.3), how rates and modes of change seen over short times relate to those seen in the fossil record (§32.4), and whether long-term evolution has directions, is predictable, or progresses (§32.5). It builds on the history of life ([7.10.29](/7.10.29)), the genetics of phenotypic evolution ([7.10.9](/7.10.9)), evolution and development ([7.10.19](/7.10.19), [7.5.39](/7.5.39)), the dynamics of diversity ([7.10.31](/7.10.31)) and the comparative methods with which trait evolution is modeled on phylogenies ([7.10.26](/7.10.26)).

## 32.1. The Origin of Major New Forms

### 32.1.1. Intermediate Forms

Paleontologists have documented intermediate steps in the origin of many higher taxa. Because creationists often claim that no new "kinds" of organisms have been shown to evolve ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)), these cases matter beyond their scientific interest. Birds evolved from theropod dinosaurs ([7.10.29](/7.10.29), §32.1.3): many feathered theropods are known, with long clawed fingers, a long tail and legs like those of other theropods. Feathers almost certainly first served for insulation; the modifications for flight, as in *Archaeopteryx*, came later. Smaller size may have enabled other changes, such as more juvenile-looking (paedomorphic) skulls with relatively large eyes and brains. Nearly all the genes known to affect feather development are also present in crocodilians and therefore in all dinosaurs, so feathers required changes in the interactions among existing genes rather than new genes. Some features of modern birds (feathers, hollow limb bones) arose in theropods long before *Archaeopteryx*; others (the keeled breastbone, loss of teeth and of claws on the hand) evolved later. The transition from terrestrial artiodactyls to whales passed through amphibious forms such as *Ambulocetus* and *Rodhocetus*, which still had the distinctive ankle bones of artiodactyls, to *Dorudon*, which had most features of modern cetaceans but a small, nonfunctional pelvis and hindlimb; the nostrils moved to the top of the head and the teeth became more uniform. Toothless baleen whales evolved from toothed whales, through extinct forms with both baleen and reduced teeth.

### 32.1.2. The Origin of Mammals

The origin of mammals is one of the most fully documented transitions. Hair and mammary glands rarely fossilize, but the skeleton has been followed in detail. Soon after the first amniotes arose in the Carboniferous, they gave rise to the Synapsida, which diversified into many lineages; from among these arose the **crown group** Mammalia ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)), the descendants of the common ancestor of monotremes, marsupials (Metatheria) and placentals (Eutheria). Living mammals differ from other amniotes in four critical skeletal features:

| Feature | Other amniotes | Mammals |
|---|---|---|
| Lower jaw | dentary, articular and several other bones | dentary only |
| Jaw joint | articular–quadrate | dentary–squamosal |
| Middle ear | one bone, the stirrup (stapes) | stirrup, hammer (malleus) and anvil (incus) |
| Teeth | uniform, single-cusped | incisors, canines and multicusped premolars and molars |

Mammals also have an enlarged braincase, a large opening behind the eye socket (temporal fenestra) for the jaw muscles, and a secondary palate that separates the airway from the mouth. The early synapsids, such as *Haptodus*, already had a temporal fenestra into which the contracted jaw muscles could bulge; it enlarged in later forms. Permian therapsids, such as *Biarmosuchus*, had large canines, a recessed palate and hindlimbs held more upright. In the cynodonts, from the late Permian to the late Triassic, the dentary enlarged relative to the other jaw bones, the cheek teeth acquired a row of cusps, and a bony secondary palate formed; the quadrate became smaller and looser. In advanced cynodonts of the middle and late Triassic, such as *Probainognathus*, the cheek teeth gained a cusp on their inner side, the beginning of the complex chewing teeth of mammals, and in some late Triassic and Jurassic forms the dentary contacted the squamosal, so that the jaw had two joints. *Morganucodon*, of the latest Triassic and earliest Jurassic, had a weak articular–quadrate hinge beside a fully developed dentary–squamosal joint, with the articular and quadrate already sunk toward the ear region. In *Hadrocodium*, of the early Jurassic, the lower jaw consisted of the dentary alone and the articular and quadrate lay free in the middle ear, where, as the hammer and anvil, they transmit sound together with the stirrup (figure below). The brain enlarged in steps over the same sequence, especially the olfactory bulbs and the neocortex.

![The origin of mammals: some of the fossil genera on the stem lineage leading to crown Mammalia, after Futuyma and Kirkpatrick (2017), Fig. 20.1. Each fossil genus is a side branch; none need be a direct ancestor. Numbered bars mark where the listed characters evolved, from the temporal fenestra of early synapsids to the jaw formed by the dentary alone, with the former jaw-joint bones (articular and quadrate) in the middle ear.](./plots/mammal-origin.py)

The history illustrates themes common in the origin of higher taxa. Most mammalian characters (posture, tooth differentiation, the skull changes associated with the jaw muscles, the secondary palate, brain size, the reduction of the bones that became the middle ear) evolved gradually, and different characters evolved at different times: **mosaic evolution** ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)). Several changes in the jaw and ear occurred independently in different cynodont lineages. No new bones evolved; many were lost, and all that persist are modified from those of early amniotes and ultimately of lobe-finned fishes. Repeated elements became individualized, so that each molar has its own identity. And major changes in form accompanied changes in function, most strikingly when bones that form the jaw joint in all other tetrapods became the sound-transmitting bones of the mammalian ear. Because the transition was gradual over more than 130 Myr, there is no natural point at which synapsids become mammals: the boundary of "Mammalia" in a temporal series is arbitrary.

### 32.1.3. The Origin of Birds

Some 10 000 species of birds live today, and their origin is now one of the best-documented major transitions, largely through fossils found in China since the 1990s. Thomas Henry Huxley argued in 1868 that birds are close to dinosaurs, citing the similarity of *Archaeopteryx* to the small theropod *Compsognathus*. Gerhard Heilmann's *The Origin of Birds* (1926) set the idea aside for half a century: he agreed that birds and theropods were strikingly similar, but concluded that the similarity was convergent, because theropods seemed to lack clavicles, which birds have fused into the wishbone (furcula), and a lost structure was not expected to reappear. John Ostrom revived the dinosaurian hypothesis in the 1970s, after describing the agile dromaeosaur *Deinonychus*, by listing the many features of the skeleton shared by *Archaeopteryx* and such theropods. Furculae have since been found in many theropods, and cladistic analyses of skeletons place birds within the Theropoda, close to the dromaeosaurs and troodontids (together the Paraves). In the hand, for example, theropods progressively lost the fourth and fifth fingers and kept three, the arrangement of the avian wing.

*Archaeopteryx*, from the Late Jurassic (about 150 Mya) lithographic limestones of Solnhofen, Germany, was found in 1861, two years after the *Origin*: a feathered animal with wings and asymmetric flight feathers, but also with teeth, a long bony tail, clawed fingers and no ossified breastbone. About a dozen skeletons are now known. The decisive new evidence came from the Early Cretaceous Jehol Biota of northeastern China (about 135–120 Mya) and the older Middle to Late Jurassic Yanliao Biota (about 165–157 Mya), lake deposits whose fine volcanic ash preserved feathers and soft tissues. *Sinosauropteryx* (described in 1996), a small theropod outside the Paraves, was covered with simple filaments; oviraptorosaurs and paravians had vaned (pennaceous) feathers; and *Microraptor gui* (2003), a small dromaeosaur, had long flight feathers on both forelimbs and hindlimbs, a "four-winged" glider or flier. Feathers therefore evolved long before flight, presumably first for insulation and display, and were later co-opted for flight (**exaptation**, [7.10.3](/7.10.3)). Filamentous body coverings in some ornithischian dinosaurs and in pterosaurs suggest that feather-like integument may be older still, perhaps ancestral to the whole group that includes dinosaurs and pterosaurs, but whether these structures are homologous with feathers is debated.

The feathered dinosaurs of the Jehol Biota are younger than *Archaeopteryx*, an apparent paradox that creationists exploited ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)): descendants seemed older than their relatives. But a phylogeny predicts that the lineages leading to them existed earlier, so feathered paravians older than *Archaeopteryx* should be found. They were: *Anchiornis*, a feathered paravian from the Yanliao Biota about 160 Mya, older than *Archaeopteryx*, was described in 2009, a prediction of the kind that makes evolutionary theory testable. As more paravians have been found, *Archaeopteryx* has proved to be only one of many small feathered animals near the base of the bird lineage, and whether it lies just inside the Avialae (birds in the broad sense) or just outside is debated. Melanosomes, the pigment organelles preserved in fossil feathers, allow even colors to be reconstructed: spherical pheomelanosomes indicate reddish brown, elongated eumelanosomes black or gray; *Sinosauropteryx* had a banded reddish tail, *Anchiornis* a black and white wing and a reddish crest, and the arrangement of melanosomes in *Microraptor* indicates an iridescent sheen. Body size fell almost continuously along the stem lineage of birds for some 50 million years, while skeletal features evolved faster than in other theropods (Lee and colleagues 2014), a miniaturization that favored arboreal life and flight.

The flight apparatus of living birds was assembled piecemeal ([Definition 2.6.1](/7.10.2#def-2.6.1)), and the Jehol fossils show many intermediate stages (figure below). *Archaeopteryx*, *Anchiornis* and *Sapeornis* had no ossified sternum. In the long-tailed *Jeholornis* the tail was still longer than in *Archaeopteryx*; *Sapeornis* and the beaked, toothless *Confuciusornis* had a pygostyle, the fused tail vertebrae that support the fan of tail feathers. The two great Cretaceous radiations, the Enantiornithes, which dominated Cretaceous land birds and died out at the K/Pg boundary, and the Ornithuromorpha, to which living birds belong, had a keeled sternum, a strut-like coracoid and an alula (the small "bastard wing" on the first digit that controls airflow at low speed), and so probably flapping flight close to that of modern birds. In living birds the downstroke is powered by the pectoralis muscle, and the upstroke by the supracoracoideus, whose tendon passes over the shoulder joint like a rope over a pulley; both muscles attach to the keel, which is reduced again in the flightless ratites such as the ostrich and emu. Other avian features also appear in steps. Teeth were reduced and lost several times independently (in *Confuciusornis*, in some ornithuromorphs and in the ancestor of living birds). Crocodiles have two functional ovaries and oviraptorosaur fossils contain eggs in pairs, one from each oviduct, whereas fossil ovarian follicles show that early birds such as *Jeholornis* already had a single functional ovary, as living birds do, a reduction often interpreted as an adaptation to reduce weight in flight. A fossil embryo of an enantiornithine, already feathered inside the egg, suggests precocial young. Stomach contents and other remains document diets: seeds in the crop of *Sapeornis*, fish bones in the gut of the ornithuromorph *Yanornis*, and gastroliths in others. Long display feathers in the tails of *Confuciusornis*, *Jeholornis* and enantiornithines show that sexual selection acted on plumage from the beginning (**sexual selection**, [7.10.13](/7.10.13)).

## 32.2. Gradualism and Saltation

Darwin held that evolution proceeds by small steps; Thomas Henry Huxley cautioned that his theory would be just as valid if it proceeded by leaps.

**Definition 32.2.1 (Saltation)** **Saltation** is the origin of a new form, a new species or a major new character in a single large evolutionary step, by a mutation of large phenotypic effect, without intermediate stages.

The geneticist Richard Goldschmidt argued in *The Material Basis of Evolution* (1940) that species and higher taxa arise not from the genetic variation within species but by "systemic mutations", reorganizations of the chromosomes that produce highly altered creatures; most would perish, but a few would be "hopeful monsters" adapted to new ways of life. His genetic theory has been completely abandoned, but whether evolution sometimes proceeds by more modest jumps remains debated. Quite different species are often connected by intermediates, so that whether a complex is classified as one genus or two is arbitrary; but there are conspicuous gaps among higher taxa: no living species bridges cetaceans and other mammals. The most obvious explanation is the extinction of intermediates that once existed, as the fossil cetaceans show. The common ancestor of two quite different forms need not have looked intermediate between them: whales are most closely related to hippopotamuses among living animals, but the earliest members of the whale lineage were terrestrial and not particularly hippo-like.

Some features seem to demand a saltational origin because it is hard to imagine an advantageous intermediate. The carapace of turtles, formed largely of the vertebrae and expanded ribs, encloses the shoulder girdle, whereas in all other tetrapods the shoulder blade lies outside the rib cage. Development and fossils show how the change could have been gradual. In other amniotes the embryonic ribs grow outward and then down into the body wall beneath the muscle plate that forms the shoulder girdle; in turtles a novel embryonic structure, the carapacial ridge, arrests the ribs' downward growth, so they lie above the girdle. The late Triassic *Odontochelys semitestacea*, one of the oldest members of the turtle lineage, had a lower shell (plastron) but no carapace, and its anterior ribs were directed backward so that the shoulder girdle lay in front of the rib cage; if the ribs later turned forward, they would come to lie above it.

Goldschmidt could point to many mutations with large, discontinuous effects. Mutations of the Hox gene *Ultrabithorax* (*Ubx*) transform the third thoracic segment of *Drosophila*, with its halteres, into a copy of the second, producing a fly with two pairs of wings ([7.5.15](/7.5.15)). It is tempting to think that the reverse mutation turned the hindwings of the ancestor of flies into halteres. But the mutant does not restore real hindwings; it produces a second set of forewings, whereas hindwings differ from forewings in all four-winged insects. Knocking out a master control gene diverts development into a default pathway; that the system can be switched off in one step does not mean it was built in one step. And such large-effect mutations drastically reduce survival because they disrupt development so profoundly.

Mutations of fairly large, but not huge, effect do contribute to evolution. Quantitative trait loci of both small and large effect underlie variation in bristle number in *Drosophila* ([7.4.25](/7.4.25)); in *Heliconius* butterflies the evolution of one Müllerian mimetic pattern into another was probably initiated by a mutation large enough to produce a substantial resemblance to a new model, followed by alleles of smaller effect that refined it ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)), as Fisher's geometric model predicts when the population is far from the optimum ([7.10.19](/7.10.19)). Some large changes have simple genetic bases because they only extend or truncate a developmental trajectory without harmful side effects: a few genetic changes separate the metamorphosing tiger salamander (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) from its paedomorphic relative, the axolotl (*A. mexicanum*), but they truncate a complex pathway that itself evolved in many steps (**heterochrony**, [7.5.39](/7.5.39)). In other cases large differences accumulate gradually by successive mutations in a single "hotspot" gene with few pleiotropic effects. The larvae of *Drosophila sechellia* lack the dorsal hairs (trichomes) of their relatives; the loss is due to mutations in three cis-regulatory regions of *shavenbaby*, a gene that controls three downstream batteries of genes needed for trichome development and whose own upstream regulators all have multiple functions ([7.10.19](/7.10.19)).

## 32.3. The Evolution of Novelty

**Definition 32.3.1 (Evolutionary Novelty)** An **evolutionary novelty** is a new character: a structure, organ or function that is not homologous to any character of the ancestor (a new *character identity*), or, in a broader sense, a radically modified state of an existing character. Günter Wagner distinguishes character states (the tusk of an elephant is one state of the character "incisor") from *character identity* (the incisor itself, or the kneecap of mammals, which reptiles lack).

Two questions must be separated: what role natural selection played at each step, from the slightest initial alteration to the full complexity of later forms, and what genetic and developmental changes produced each step. Both bear on how complex characters, whose function depends on the mutual adjustment of many parts, can evolve at all.

### 32.3.1. Co-option, Permissive Conditions and Selection

Many features are modifications of ancestral structures shaped by selection for new functions, a principle Darwin recognized and one of the most important in macroevolution. A bee's sting is a modified ovipositor; the wings of penguins are entirely modified for underwater flight, those of auks used in both air and water; lens crystallins of the vertebrate eye are a heat-shock protein and other enzymes recruited with little or no change ([7.10.18](/7.10.18)). Some features begin as nonadaptive by-products of other adaptations: excreting nitrogen as crystalline uric acid saves water in insects, but its white color is incidental, and pierine butterflies such as the cabbage white (*Pieris rapae*) deposit white nitrogenous excretory compounds in their wing scales (mainly pterins, which were long mistaken for uric acid), where the white color functions in thermoregulation and probably in courtship. A feature co-opted for a new function is an **exaptation** for it ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)).

Behavior often leads. Aquatic mammals would not have begun to evolve adaptations for swimming unless their ancestors had chosen to live near water, and insects adapt to a plant's toxins only if some of them start to eat it. Behaviorally flexible animals often do things they are not adapted for: gulls that normally eat aquatic animals sometimes feed on swarms of flying ants. Other features of organisms permit or facilitate new characters. Decoupling the multiple functions of a structure releases it from constraints: the lungless salamanders (Plethodontidae), the largest family, no longer use the hyobranchial bones for pumping air, and these bones have been modified into a folding apparatus with which some species project the tongue over extraordinary lengths at extraordinary speed. Marc Kirschner and John Gerhart argued that core cellular and developmental processes are robust and adaptable, so that variation arises in ways that facilitate evolution ("facilitated variation"): muscles, nerves and blood vessels grow toward signals from developing bone and dermis, so a genetic change in the limb skeleton yields an altered but functional limb without separate changes in the soft tissues (**evolvability**, [7.10.19](/7.10.19)).

### 32.3.2. Complex Characters: The Eye

A common argument against evolution is "irreducible complexity": a complex feature cannot function without all its parts, which could not all have arisen at once ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)). Darwin himself confessed that the evolution of the eye by natural selection "seems, I freely confess, absurd in the highest possible degree", and then answered that if numerous gradations from a simple to a perfect eye exist, each useful to its possessor, and if the eye varies and the variations are inherited, the difficulty "can hardly be considered real". Later research has fully supported him. The eyes of living animals range from small groups of light-sensitive cells (in some flatworms and annelids) through cups and pinhole "camera" eyes (in cnidarians and molluscs) to closed eyes that form precise images, which evolved independently in cnidarians, snails, bivalves, polychaete worms, arthropods and vertebrates (figure below). Most of the stages occur among living gastropods alone, from the limpet *Patella* (an open cup) and the abalone *Haliotis* (a pinhole eye) to *Murex* (a closed eye with a lens). Each grade serves a function: simple photoreceptors and cups are most common in slow or burrowing animals, elaborate eyes in active ones. At the molecular level, the phototransduction cascade, screening pigments and lens crystallins were present in the common ancestors of animals, and most of them were independently recruited for vision in several lineages.

**Example 32.3.1 (How Long Does an Eye Take?)** Dan-Eric Nilsson and Susanne Pelger (1994) modeled the transformation of a flat patch of photoreceptors into a focused camera eye with a graded-index lens, a sequence of small changes in shape and refractive index, each improving spatial resolution. They found that the whole sequence requires about 1829 steps, each a change of 1 % in some dimension, a total change by a factor of $1.01^{1829} \approx 8 \times 10^{7}$. With deliberately pessimistic assumptions (heritability $h^2 = 0.5$, a coefficient of variation of 0.01 and a weak selection intensity of 0.01, so that the mean changes by $h^2 \times 0.01 \times 0.01 = 0.005\,\%$ per generation by the **breeder's equation**, [7.4.24](/7.4.24)), the number of generations is

$$
\frac{\ln(8 \times 10^{7})}{\ln 1.00005} \approx 3.6 \times 10^{5},
$$
a few hundred thousand years for small animals with annual generations, a geological instant compared with the half-billion years since the Cambrian.

Moreover, a part that was initially merely advantageous can become indispensable once other characters evolve to depend on it. Many annelids with lensless eyes do well without acuity, but an eagle, whose way of hunting became possible only through acute vision, could not survive without its lens. Dependence can also be lost: many cave and burrowing vertebrates have degenerate eyes ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.7.3)).

### 32.3.3. Homology and the Emergence of New Characters

New skeletal elements can arise from sesamoid bones, which form in connective tissue: the false "thumb" of the giant panda and the extra digit of moles are enlarged wrist sesamoids, and the mammalian kneecap is a sesamoid lacking in reptiles. The forelimbs of tetrapods (the one-toed leg of a horse, the flipper of a whale, the wings of birds and bats) are homologous among species, and the same structural plan recurs between forelimb and hindlimb (*serial homology*), which suggests that one genetic-developmental program is expressed in different parts of the body, just as it is in different species that inherited it. Wagner calls similarity based on a shared genetic-developmental program *biological homology*, a broader concept than the phylogenetic **homology** of systematics ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)), because a character can be biologically homologous in two species even if their common ancestor did not express it, as when a lost character is regained. Regulatory pathways inherited widely and deployed independently in different lineages constitute **deep homology** ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)): homologues of *Pax6* initiate eye development in arthropods, vertebrates and other animals, and a pathway including *Distal-less* underlies many outgrowths of the body wall, from the tube feet of sea urchins to the legs and the horns of scarab beetles.

Novel characters are thought to arise from new regulatory interactions among previously unconnected genes, which Wagner calls *character identity networks*. The sex combs of male *Drosophila*, rows of modified bristles on the foreleg used to grasp females, arose in the *melanogaster* and *obscura* groups when the male-specific form of the transcription factor *doublesex* (*dsx*) and the Hox gene *Sex combs reduced* (*Scr*) came to regulate each other in a positive feedback loop in part of the developing tarsus; *Scr* specifies the position and number of the modified bristles, *dsx* their male-specific form, and both regulate many downstream genes. An entire pathway may also be deployed in a new place (heterotopy, [7.5.39](/7.5.39)): in birds, the digits that develop in positions 2, 3 and 4 of the embryonic hand take on the identities of digits 1, 2 and 3 of their theropod ancestors, a frameshift in which the gene networks specifying digit identity are activated in different condensations. The molecular sources of new genes and functions (duplication, co-option, exon shuffling) are treated in [7.10.18](/7.10.18); here the point is that novelty usually means recombining old parts and old programs.

## 32.4. From Microevolution to Macroevolution

### 32.4.1. Rates of Evolution

Rates of morphological evolution are measured in two units. J. B. S. Haldane proposed the **darwin**, the change in the natural logarithm of a trait per million years,

$$
r_{\mathrm{d}} = \frac{\ln x_2 - \ln x_1}{\Delta t}\ \text{darwins} \quad (\Delta t \text{ in Myr}),
$$
so that 1 darwin is a change by a factor of $\mathrm{e}$ in a million years, or roughly one part per thousand per thousand years. Philip Gingerich proposed the **haldane**, the change in the mean in units of the within-population standard deviation per generation,

$$
r_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac{\bar z_2/s_p - \bar z_1/s_p}{g},
$$
with $s_p$ the pooled phenotypic standard deviation and $g$ the number of generations; it relates rates directly to the variation available to selection and to the response predicted by the breeder's equation.

Rates vary enormously among characters, lineages and times, and above all with the interval over which they are measured: the longer the interval, the lower the rate. Rates measured over a few generations in living populations are hundreds to millions of times faster than those measured between fossil samples millions of years apart. At the rates seen in some living populations, body mass could evolve from a 20-g mouse to a 2-million-g elephant in fewer than 100 000 generations. But such high rates are not sustained. Over long intervals, short episodes of rapid change in alternating directions average out into slow net change. In terrestrial vertebrates, the net change in body mass within lineages is about the same whether measured over a few years or over a million years; steadily greater differences accumulate only over intervals longer than about 1 Myr, perhaps when a lineage shifts to a substantially different ecological niche. The dependence of rate on interval is itself informative (figure below): for a random walk the expected net change grows as $\sqrt{t}$ and the rate falls as $t^{-1/2}$; for stasis the net change is bounded and the rate falls as $t^{-1}$; only sustained directional change gives rates independent of interval. These models are the **Brownian motion model** and the **Ornstein–Uhlenbeck model** of [7.10.26](/7.10.26).

![Why measured rates of evolution fall with the interval over which they are measured. A trait (in units of its phenotypic standard deviation) evolves for $10^5$ generations by a random walk, by fluctuation around a fixed optimum (stasis) or by sustained directional change, with the same short-term noise. A: trajectories. B: the median rate in haldanes for pairs of samples separated by $t$ generations: slope $-1/2$ for the random walk, $-1$ for stasis, approaching $0$ only for directional change.](./plots/rate-interval.py)

Even low rates produce large changes over long times: the body mass of the largest species of horses increased about tenfold over the last 25 Myr, and the fossil record shows that terrestrial mammals have increased in mass 100-fold in about 1.6 million generations and 5000-fold in 10 million. The long-term rates of morphological evolution, in fossils or among living species, are usually so low that mutation and drift alone could account for them. The problem is therefore not whether known processes are fast enough, but why evolution is so often so slow.

### 32.4.2. Stasis

**Definition 32.4.1 (Stasis)** **Stasis** is the persistence of a lineage, or of a character in a lineage, with little or no net change over a long period of geological time, often with fluctuations about a stable mean.

Stasis is common. Eight living lineages of bivalves with a fossil record show as much variation among present-day geographic populations as over 4 Myr of their history, and most fossil lineages fit a model of stasis or of random fluctuation better than one of directional change. At the extreme are "living fossils" such as the ginkgo, the horseshoe crab *Limulus* (similar to Triassic fossils) and the coelacanths, known from the Devonian onward and thought to have died out in the Cretaceous until the living *Latimeria chalumnae* was found in 1938. The synapomorphies of large clades are also conservative: almost all mammals have seven neck vertebrae, and almost no tetrapods since the earliest have had more than five digits.

Explanations fall into two groups. **Stabilizing selection**, the most widely supported, is often maintained by *habitat tracking*: a species shifts its range with the habitat to which it is adapted rather than adapting to new conditions (**phylogenetic niche conservatism**, [7.10.30](/7.10.30)). Diverse molluscs have occupied the same temperature regime throughout the Pleistocene climate changes by moving; the larvae of all butterflies of the tribe Heliconiini feed on passionflowers (Passifloraceae), as they apparently have since the tribe originated in the Oligocene. Gene flow between the populations in the ancestral environment and those in a new one also favors the ancestral state if there is a fitness trade-off, simply because most of the population lives in the ancestral environment. *Constraints* form the second group. Genes no longer used decay into pseudogenes: in a bird-pollinated clade of red-flowered morning glories (*Ipomoea*), the genes for the blue and purple pigments of other morning glories are no longer functional, which closes the way back. Some traits, such as tolerance of high temperature, have little genetic variation, and genetic correlations may permit change in some combinations of traits but not others (the **G matrix**, [7.10.9](/7.10.9)). In the Paleogene ostracod *Poseidonamicus*, shell shape evolved in several lineages mostly along the direction of greatest variation within populations, the **genetic line of least resistance**, deviating from it only over longer times; divergence in wing shape among *Drosophila* clades over tens of millions of years resembles the genetic covariance matrix of *D. melanogaster*.

### 32.4.3. Punctuated Equilibrium and Phyletic Gradualism

The fossil record of some groups shows gradual transitions, that of many others gaps between rather constant forms. Most paleontologists followed Darwin in attributing the gaps to the incompleteness of the record. In 1972 Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould proposed a different interpretation, in the essay "Punctuated equilibria: an alternative to phyletic gradualism" (in T. J. M. Schopf, ed., *Models in Paleobiology*).

**Definition 32.4.2 (Punctuated Equilibrium and Phyletic Gradualism)** **Punctuated equilibrium** is the hypothesis that species typically show long periods of little or no phenotypic change (stasis), interrupted by geologically rapid shifts to a new stable state that coincide with speciation, usually in small peripheral populations that then expand and replace or coexist with the ancestral species. **Phyletic gradualism** is the contrasting model of slow, more or less continuous change within lineages, not tied to speciation.

The "rapid" shifts of punctuated equilibrium are still gradual on the time scale of populations (thousands of generations); they appear sudden only in strata. Eldredge and Gould linked the punctuations to founder-effect speciation ([7.10.12](/7.10.12)), in which a new species evolves "offstage" and appears abruptly in the record when it spreads. In the Miocene bryozoan genus *Metrarabdotos*, species persisted with little change for several million years and new species appeared abruptly without evident intermediates. Gradual change also occurs: in eight lineages of Ordovician trilobites from central Wales, the number of ribs on the tail shield (pygidium) changed gradually and in parallel over about three million years, with intermediates connecting forms that had been classified as different species (Sheldon 1987); and in the excellent record of Cenozoic planktonic Foraminifera, although there, too, morphological change is almost always accompanied by speciation. Paleontologists agree that stasis is common; the hypothesis that morphological change requires speciation is not widely accepted, because selection can change populations whether or not they speciate.

Phylogenies of living species offer a test (figure below). If change happens mainly at speciation, the total amount of molecular or morphological change from the root to a living species (its *path length*) should increase with the number of speciation events (nodes) on the path; under gradualism, with all tips at the same time depth, it should not. Mark Pagel and colleagues found significant correlations between the number of nucleotide substitutions and the number of nodes in many phylogenies of animals, fungi and plants, and speciation accounts for more than two-thirds of the variance in body mass among species of mammals. Possible reasons are the adaptive changes in reproductive isolation and ecology that accompany speciation, or a mechanism proposed by Futuyma: populations of a widespread species adapt to local conditions, but as climates and habitats shift, divergent populations come into contact and the divergence is lost by interbreeding unless reproductive isolation has evolved. Speciation "captures" local adaptations and lets them persist, like a piton that keeps a climber from slipping back; a succession of speciation events, each capturing further change, could produce a long-term trend.

## 32.5. Trends, Predictability and Progress

For decades after the *Origin*, most who accepted evolution saw it as cosmic progress, with humans, the top of the former scala naturae, as its supreme product. Darwin distinguished himself by denying that evolution necessarily progresses. A *trend* can be described objectively, as a directional shift over time; *progress* implies improvement, which requires a criterion of what is better.

### 32.5.1. Passive and Driven Trends

A trend is a persistent directional change in the mean, maximum or minimum of a character in a clade over time; it may describe one clade, many, or all of life.

**Definition 32.5.1 (Passive and Driven Trends)** In a **passive trend**, lineages change in either direction with equal probability, but a boundary (such as a minimum viable body size) prevents change beyond it in one direction; as the variance among lineages expands, the mean and maximum move away from the boundary while many lineages remain near the ancestral state. In a **driven trend** (active trend), changes in one direction are more likely than in the other, so the whole distribution of character states among lineages, including its minimum, shifts.

![Passive and driven trends, simulated after the scheme of Futuyma and Kirkpatrick (2017), Fig. 20.21. A clade starts with one species of trait value 1 (for example log body size above a minimum); species change by random steps, speciate and go extinct. A: unbiased steps with a reflecting boundary at 0: the mean and maximum rise but the minimum stays near the boundary (passive trend). B: steps biased upward: the minimum also rises and the whole distribution shifts (driven trend).](./plots/trends.py)

Driven trends can arise from individual selection favoring the same direction in many lineages, from **species selection** ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)), when speciation or extinction rates are correlated with the character, or from asymmetries in the possibility of change (a "ratchet": the loss of a complex feature may be irreversible). Paleontologists long ago noticed that the maximum body size in many animal groups has increased over time.

**Definition 32.5.2 (Cope's Rule)** **Cope's rule** is the tendency of body size to increase over time within lineages or clades.

In 1534 species of late Cretaceous and Cenozoic North American mammals, small mammals were present throughout and the lower size limit has stayed nearly the same, while the mean and maximum increased, especially after the K/Pg extinction: a passive pattern at the level of the clade. Yet matched older and younger species of the same genera, likely ancestor–descendant pairs, increased in size more often than they decreased, which suggests that individual selection within lineages drove the changes. Species selection is the cause in other cases. In several Cenozoic clades of volutid snails, species without a planktotrophic larval stage had shorter durations than planktotrophic species but speciated faster, probably because low dispersal reduces gene flow, so their proportion increased. In the family Solanaceae, self-compatible lineages arise often from self-incompatible ones, and the reverse almost never happens (a genetic ratchet, often favored by individual selection for reproductive assurance, [7.10.14](/7.10.14)); yet self-incompatible species predominate, because self-compatible lineages have higher extinction rates and lower net diversification.

### 32.5.2. The Horse Family: A Bush, Not a Ladder

The fossil horses were long displayed as a ladder from a small, many-toed browser, *Hyracotherium* (the "dawn horse" of the Eocene), through *Mesohippus* and the grazer *Merychippus* to the large, one-toed grazer *Equus*, as if evolution had aimed at the modern horse. The fuller record shows a bush: many species are known across the Cenozoic, many lineages coexisted, browsers and grazers lived at the same time, and some lineages decreased in size while more increased, so that the average size of the family rose and the maximum increased about tenfold over 25 Myr. *Equus* is the one surviving twig of a once diverse bush, not the goal of a ladder; the same holds for the hominins ([7.10.33](/7.10.33)).

### 32.5.3. Are There Major Trends in the History of Life?

Many trends have been proposed for all of life; all have exceptions. Two candidates are efficiency and complexity. Efficiency of design has surely improved in many lineages (the feeding and locomotor apparatus of synapsids on the way to mammals), but adaptedness can be judged only relative to a way of life: a shark and a falcon are no more comparable than flat-head and Phillips-head screwdrivers. Darwin thought that revived extinct species would lose in competition with modern ones, but the fossil record shows more replacement after extinction than competitive displacement ([7.10.31](/7.10.31)), and the constant extinction rates of taxa of different ages show no gain in extinction resistance over time. John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry listed the **major evolutionary transitions** ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)), most of which increased hierarchical organization: replicating molecules in compartments, eukaryotic cells from associations of prokaryotes, multicellular organisms, and integrated societies. Each required the suppression of selection at the lower level, usually by passage through a stage of high relatedness such as the single-celled egg. But these were a few events in which most lineages did not take part. Complexity is hard to define and compare; Cambrian animals were arguably as complex as living ones; and many characters have been simplified or lost: lobe-finned fishes had more skull bones than early amniotes, and these more than mammals, whose lower jaw has one bone instead of six. Genome size varies 60 000-fold among eukaryotes, with no clear relation to complexity ([7.10.18](/7.10.18)). The conclusion is that all organisms together show a passive trend toward greater maximum complexity, starting from simple cells near a lower boundary, but no characteristic shows a consistent driven trend among most branches of the tree of life.

### 32.5.4. Predictability and Contingency

A history is predictable if lawlike principles determine its sequence of events; it is *contingent* if the outcome would have been different had any of many earlier events been different, even though each event has causes. Gould argued that if the "tape of life" were replayed from any point, the outcome would be very different (*Wonderful Life*, 1989); Simon Conway Morris argued that convergent evolution makes the broad outcome, including something like human intelligence, nearly inevitable.

There is certainly predictability. The selection equations of population genetics predict allele-frequency change in large populations; physical principles make the legs of elephants disproportionately thick; optimality models of life histories, behavior and form are often successful ([7.10.15](/7.10.15)), largely because **convergent evolution** is so common. But most evolutionary biologists emphasize contingency. Evolution can depend on rare mutations and combinations of interacting mutations and on the sequence of environmental changes; convergence between closely related lineages may be close, but between distant ones it is usually superficial. Experiments have tested replays directly. In Richard Lenski's long-term experiment, 12 populations of *Escherichia coli* founded from one ancestor have been propagated in a glucose medium since 1988 ([7.10.5](/7.10.5)). Fitness increased in parallel in all populations, but only one evolved the ability to grow aerobically on the citrate also present in the medium, after about 31 000 generations; replays started from frozen samples of that population showed that the innovation became possible only after earlier "potentiating" mutations had occurred in its history. Unique events abound: as far as is known life arose once, as did vertebrates, flowers, the amnion and feathers; mass extinctions cut short most lineages that ever lived, and nothing equivalent to trilobites, ammonites or dinosaurs replaced them. Among perhaps a billion species in Earth's history only one evolved human intelligence, after some 3 billion years of cellular life. Had any of our ancestors' lineages become extinct, intelligent hominins would probably never have evolved.

### 32.5.5. The Question of Progress

If progress means movement toward a goal, evolution cannot progress: mutation and selection have no goals. If it means betterment, it requires a value judgment, and there is no objective basis for calling human features better than those of other species; a reflective rattlesnake might measure progress by the elegance of venom delivery. Intelligence is an adaptation to certain ways of life, and most animal lineages, let alone plants and fungi, show no trend toward it. Adaptive improvement exists, but only relative to a particular environment and way of life. The most characteristic feature of evolutionary history is not progress but the unceasing proliferation of new forms and new ways of living, Darwin's "endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful".

## Summary

- Higher taxa evolved through documented intermediates: birds from theropods, whales from artiodactyls, and mammals from synapsids, in which the jaw joint shifted from articular–quadrate to dentary–squamosal and the old joint bones became the middle ear. Change was gradual and mosaic, with changes of function.
- **Saltation** (Definition 32.2.1) by hopeful monsters is rejected; mutations of large effect (homeotic mutants) are usually deleterious, but mutations of moderately large effect, heterochronic truncations and hotspot genes contribute.
- **Evolutionary novelties** (Definition 32.3.1) arise by co-option, often led by behavior and facilitated by decoupling of functions; complex organs such as the eye evolve through useful intermediate stages, quickly by geological standards (Example 32.3.1). New characters arise from new regulatory networks among old genes (character identity networks, deep homology).
- Rates (darwins, haldanes) fall with the interval of measurement; short-term rates far exceed long-term ones, and the puzzle is the prevalence of **stasis** (Definition 32.4.1), explained by stabilizing selection with habitat tracking and by constraints.
- **Punctuated equilibrium** contrasts with **phyletic gradualism** (Definition 32.4.2); stasis is common, and speciation is correlated with phenotypic and molecular change, perhaps because it stabilizes local adaptations.
- **Passive trends** and **driven trends** (Definition 32.5.1) differ in whether the minimum moves; **Cope's rule** (Definition 32.5.2) in mammals is passive at the clade level but driven within lineages; species selection explains trends in snails and Solanaceae. The horse family is a bush.
- No feature shows a driven trend across all life; complexity shows at most a passive trend. Long-term evolution is largely contingent; progress exists only as adaptation relative to a way of life.

## Questions

1. Why did the articular and quadrate bones not simply disappear when the dentary–squamosal joint evolved? What does this case illustrate about the origin of new structures?

   **Answer** During the transition the two joints coexisted (*Morganucodon*), so the old bones still had a mechanical role; they were small and already close to the stapes, and in synapsids the lower jaw probably already conducted vibrations to the ear. Once the new joint took over the load, selection for hearing could modify the freed bones into a lever system that transmits sound, rather than eliminating them. The case illustrates co-option and change of function: new structures are usually modified old ones, and a part freed from one function by redundancy can be shaped for another; it also shows that intermediate stages (a double joint) can be fully functional.

2. Using the breeder's equation, verify the order of magnitude of the Nilsson–Pelger estimate. How would the answer change if the selection intensity were ten times larger?

   **Answer** The response per generation is $R = h^2 S$; with $S$ equal to 0.01 standard deviations and the standard deviation equal to 1 % of the mean, $R = 0.5 \times 0.01 \times 0.01 = 5 \times 10^{-5}$ of the mean. The required total factor $8 \times 10^7$ is reached after $\ln(8\times10^7)/\ln(1 + 5\times10^{-5}) \approx 18.2/(5\times10^{-5}) \approx 3.6 \times 10^5$ generations. With ten times stronger selection the response is ten times larger and the time about $3.6 \times 10^4$ generations. The estimate assumes continuous availability of variation in the right directions and a smooth path in which each step improves vision, which the optical model establishes.

3. A fossil lineage changes in mean body length by 2 standard deviations over 1 Myr, with a generation time of 1 year. What is its rate in haldanes? Compare with a rate of 0.1 haldanes observed over 10 generations in a living population, and explain why the comparison does not show that fossil lineages evolved more slowly.

   **Answer** $2/10^6 = 2 \times 10^{-6}$ haldanes, about $5 \times 10^4$ times slower than 0.1 haldanes. But rates are not comparable across intervals: over long intervals periods of rapid change in alternating directions, and stasis, cancel out, so the net rate falls roughly as $t^{-1/2}$ or $t^{-1}$ (figure in §32.4.1). The fossil lineage may have experienced short episodes as fast as the living population; the measured rate reflects the net displacement, not the instantaneous speed.

4. How could you distinguish a passive from a driven trend toward larger body size in a clade, using fossils or a phylogeny?

   **Answer** Look at the minimum and at ancestor–descendant changes. In a passive trend the minimum stays near the boundary while the mean and maximum rise, and among ancestor–descendant pairs (or sister-species contrasts on a phylogeny) increases and decreases are equally frequent except near the boundary; in a driven trend the minimum also rises and increases outnumber decreases everywhere in the distribution, including far from the boundary. If changes within lineages are unbiased but large-bodied species speciate more or go extinct less, the trend is driven by species selection, detectable with trait-dependent diversification models ([7.10.31](/7.10.31)).

5. What is the difference between the claims that (a) stasis is common and (b) morphological change occurs mainly at speciation? Which is better supported, and why?

   **Answer** (a) is a description of the fossil record: many species show little net change over long times. (b) is a causal claim that change is tied to the origin of new species. (a) is widely accepted, from bivalves, bryozoans and many other lineages. (b) is not generally accepted as a necessity, because response to selection does not require speciation, and gradual change within lineages is documented (Foraminifera, many quantitative traits); but correlations between path length and number of nodes in phylogenies show that speciation is often associated with more change, perhaps because reproductive isolation lets local adaptations persist.
