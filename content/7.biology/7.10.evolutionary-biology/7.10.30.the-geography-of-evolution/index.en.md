Why are there apes in Africa but not in South America, sloths only in tropical America, and no native land mammals other than bats on most oceanic islands? Before Darwin, the distribution of organisms was a catalogue of facts with no explanation: a Creator could have placed each species anywhere. Darwin and Wallace turned it into one of the strongest arguments for evolution ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.7.5)), because distributions make sense if each species arose in one place and spread from there, diverging as it went. This chapter treats the geography of evolution at two scales. The first is history: which events, the splitting of continents, the rise of isthmuses, the ice ages, rare journeys across the sea, put lineages where they are, and how phylogenies dated with the molecular clock ([7.10.24](/7.10.24)) distinguish among them. The second is ecology: what limits the range of a species today, and why some regions hold many more species than others. Geographic variation within species and the evolution of range limits were treated in [7.10.10](/7.10.10), and the geography of speciation in [7.10.12](/7.10.12); the history of the continents and of life is in [7.10.29](/7.10.29), and the long-term dynamics of diversity, which the geographic patterns of this chapter partly reflect, in [7.10.31](/7.10.31).

## 30.1. Biogeography and the Evidence for Evolution

**Definition 30.1.1 (Biogeography)** **Biogeography** is the study of the geographic distributions of organisms and of the processes that produce them. *Historical biogeography* explains distributions by past events (the origin of taxa in particular places, their dispersal, extinction and the movement of land masses); *ecological biogeography* explains them by processes operating now (climate, resources, competitors, predators and the ability to disperse).

The two parts are complementary: the range of a species reflects both where its ancestors happened to be and which conditions it can tolerate. Naturalists began cataloguing the faunas and floras of different regions in the eighteenth century, but it was Darwin and Wallace who placed these facts in an evolutionary framework. Darwin argued, in two chapters of *On the Origin of Species*, that distributions make sense if a species (1) has a definite region of origin, (2) achieves a wider distribution by **dispersal** ([7.10.10](/7.10.10)), and (3) becomes modified and gives rise to descendant species in the regions it reaches. He emphasized three facts:

- Similar climates and habitats do not carry similar organisms. Deserts of the Old and New World both have leafless succulents with photosynthetic stems, but the New World ones are cacti (Cactaceae), almost confined to the Americas, and the Old World ones are spurges (*Euphorbia*) and members of other families that evolved the same growth form by **convergent evolution**. All New World monkeys belong to one clade (Platyrrhini) and all Old World monkeys to another (Catarrhini), although their habitats and diets are similar.
- Barriers to movement are associated with differences between biotas: the marine faunas of the Atlantic and Pacific coasts of South America differ greatly.
- The inhabitants of one continent or sea are related to one another even when they live in different habitats. The aquatic rodents of South America (coypu, capybara) are related to the rodents of the South American mountains and grasslands, not to the aquatic rodents (beaver, muskrat) of the Northern Hemisphere. Darwin called this bond, "throughout space and time, over the same areas of land and water", simply inheritance.

Oceanic islands gave the most compelling evidence that species are not created where they live. They have just those kinds of organisms that disperse well over long distances: on many islands the only native mammals are bats, and island species that disperse poorly, such as the dodo and other flightless birds, descend from strong fliers. Continental plants and animals introduced by humans often thrive on islands, so their absence before was a failure to arrive, not an unsuitable environment. Most island species are related to species on the nearest mainland, as almost all the birds and plants of the Galápagos are related to South American species; island plants often carry the marks of their ancestry, such as hooked seeds adapted for dispersal by mammals on islands without native mammals. And the proportion of species found only on an island is highest where the chance of dispersal to it is lowest. Wallace, who studied distributions above all on the islands of the Malay Archipelago and is often called the father of biogeography, reached the same conclusions; a century and a half of research, with the addition of continental drift and sea-level change, has not overturned any of them.

## 30.2. Major Patterns of Distribution

### 30.2.1. Endemism and Biogeographic Realms

Almost every species, and many higher taxa, occupy only part of the Earth. The salamander genus *Plethodon* is limited to North America, and *Plethodon caddoensis* to the Caddo Mountains of western Arkansas; the kiwi family (Apterygidae) lives only in New Zealand, whereas the pigeon family (Columbidae) is nearly cosmopolitan.

**Definition 30.2.1 (Endemic Species and Biogeographic Realms)** A taxon is **endemic** to a region if it occurs naturally there and nowhere else; an **endemic species** is a species restricted to a region, and *endemism* is the occurrence of such taxa. **Biogeographic realms** are major regions of the Earth, each with a characteristic set of taxa, within which the taxonomic composition of the biota is more uniform than between regions.

Wallace recognized that many higher taxa have roughly similar distributions and divided the land into six realms that are still widely used: the Palearctic (Eurasia and northern Africa), the Nearctic (North America), the Neotropical (Central and South America), the Ethiopian, or Afrotropical (Africa south of the Sahara), the Oriental (India and Southeast Asia) and the Australian (Australia, New Guinea, New Zealand and nearby islands). Each realm has many taxa that are much more diverse there than elsewhere or restricted to it. Endemic or nearly endemic to the Neotropical realm, for example, are the Xenarthra (anteaters, sloths and armadillos), the platyrrhine primates, most hummingbirds, a large radiation of suboscine birds such as antbirds and ovenbirds, many families of catfishes and, among plants, the bromeliads. The borders of realms are not sharp, because some taxa have infiltrated their neighbours: an armadillo, an opossum and Spanish moss (*Tillandsia usneoides*, a bromeliad) are North American representatives of Neotropical lineages.

The realms are more the result of Earth's history than of present climate or geography. The clearest case is a line Wallace drew through the Malay Archipelago.

**Definition 30.2.2 (Wallace's Line)** **Wallace's line** is the boundary between the Oriental and Australian faunas that runs through the Malay Archipelago, between Bali and Lombok and between Borneo and Sulawesi: west of it most vertebrates belong to Asian families and genera, east of it the fauna has Australian affinities.

The islands on either side are close and have similar climates. The difference is that the islands to the west stand on the Asian continental shelf and were joined to the mainland when sea level fell during the Pleistocene glaciations, whereas a deep-water channel along the line was never dry. Many groups of Asian mammals, for example, stop at the line; of the hoofed mammals (Cetartiodactyla), only a few, such as the wild pigs, babirusa and dwarf buffaloes (anoas) of Sulawesi, live just east of it.

### 30.2.2. Disjunct Distributions

**Definition 30.2.3 (Disjunct Distribution)** A taxon has a **disjunct distribution** if its range consists of two or more areas separated by a large gap in which it does not occur.

Disjunct higher taxa usually have different species or genera in each area. Many groups occur on two or more of the southern continents, among them lungfishes, marsupials, cichlid fishes and *Araucaria* pines. Another classic disjunction links eastern North America and temperate eastern Asia, with nothing in between: alligators (*Alligator*), skunk cabbages (*Symplocarpus*), tulip trees (*Liriodendron*) and lady-slipper orchids (*Cypripedium*) are among the many genera with this pattern. Boas (Boidae) live in tropical America and on islands of the southwestern Pacific. Explaining how taxa became disjunct has long been the central problem of historical biogeography.

## 30.3. Historical Processes: Extinction, Dispersal and Vicariance

Three historical processes shape the range of a taxon. A range shrinks by *extinction* of some of its populations, or of some species of a higher taxon. The horse family (Equidae) originated and diversified in North America but became extinct there; only the zebras of Africa and the wild asses and horses of Asia survived, until horses returned to the Americas with European colonists. Extinction also created the eastern Asia–eastern North America disjunction: during the warm, moist Paleogene, many plants and animals spread across the northern continents over land connections between North America and both Europe and Siberia; mountain uplift and a cooler, drier climate in the Neogene eliminated many of them from western North America, and the Pleistocene glaciations eliminated many from Europe.

A range expands by **dispersal**. Some species spread very fast: many European plants carried accidentally to New York and New England have spread across most of North America within the last two centuries, and the European starling (*Sturnus vulgaris*), introduced into New York City in 1890, spread across most of the continent within a century. Others cross major barriers on their own: the cattle egret (*Bubulcus ibis*), once restricted to warm parts of the Old World, reached South America about 140 years ago, apparently without human help, and has spread through the warmer parts of the New World.

**Definition 30.3.1 (Vicariance)** **Vicariance** is the separation of the populations of a widespread species, or of the species of a widespread clade, by a barrier that arises from changes in geology, climate or habitat, rather than by the movement of the organisms across a pre-existing barrier. The separated populations, *vicariant* populations, may then diverge into different subspecies, species or higher taxa.

The emergence of the Isthmus of Panama, completed about 3 Myr ago in the Pliocene, divided many marine species. In fishes, shrimps and other marine groups the closest relative of a species on the Pacific side is often a species on the Caribbean side, each pair descended from an ancestor sundered by the isthmus; the snapping shrimps *Alpheus nuttingi* (Atlantic) and *A. millsae* (Pacific) are an example. Such sister pairs on two sides of a dated barrier are also used as calibrations for the **molecular clock** ([7.10.24](/7.10.24)).

The three processes usually act together. During the Pleistocene glaciations, cold-adapted species of the north dispersed far to the south; when the climate warmed, they recolonized the north and their southern populations became extinct except on cold mountaintops. The arctic-alpine saxifrage *Saxifraga cernua* has a circumpolar northern range and isolated populations on high mountains farther south: dispersal extended the range, and extinction in the intervening lowlands left vicariant *relict* populations.

## 30.4. Inferring Biogeographic History

### 30.4.1. Evidence and Guidelines

Some guidelines for inferring history are sound. A distribution cannot be explained by an event that happened before the taxon existed: a genus that originated in the Miocene cannot owe its distribution to continental movements in the Cretaceous. Others are unreliable. The idea that a taxon originated where it is now most diverse fails for the horses, which now live wild only in Africa and Asia although their fossil record shows a North American origin; beetle species that lived in England during the Pleistocene now survive only in remote parts of Asia and Africa.

The fossil record can show that a taxon proliferated in one area before it appeared in another. Armadillos are known only from South America throughout the early Cenozoic and appear in North American deposits only in the Pliocene and Pleistocene, after the Isthmus of Panama formed, so they dispersed north. Such inferences must be cautious, because a taxon may be older, and may have lived longer in a region, than a sparse fossil record shows. Most modern historical biogeography is therefore phylogenetic: ancestral distributions are inferred on a phylogeny much as ancestral character states are (**ancestral state reconstruction**, [7.10.2](/7.10.2), [7.10.26](/7.10.26)). In model-based methods, the geographic range of each lineage is a discrete state that changes along branches by dispersal (adding an area) and local extinction (losing one), and is divided between the daughter lineages at speciation; the best known of these, the dispersal–extinction–cladogenesis (DEC) model, estimates the rates by maximum likelihood on a dated tree, like the **Mk model** of character evolution.

### 30.4.2. Testing Vicariance with Dated Phylogenies

The breakup of **Pangaea** into **Laurasia** and **Gondwana**, and of Gondwana into the southern continents ([7.10.29](/7.10.29)), long seemed the obvious explanation of the southern disjunctions. Cichlids live in fresh water in Africa, Madagascar and tropical America; *Araucaria* in South America, Australia, Norfolk Island and New Caledonia; southern beeches (*Nothofagus*) in southern South America, Australia, New Zealand and New Caledonia. In each case the hypothesis was that the ancestor was distributed across Gondwana and that its descendants were isolated on the fragments as they drifted apart. The hypothesis makes a testable prediction: a clade split by continental drift must be at least as old as the separation of the land masses it occupies, and the order of its splits should match the order of the rifts (figure below).

![Testing vicariance with a dated phylogeny (schematic, hypothetical ages). Three land masses A, B and C separate at two rifts (shaded): A from B + C 80 Myr ago and B from C 50 Myr ago. A: a clade whose branching order and divergence times match the rifts, as vicariance predicts. B: a clade with the same branching order whose lineages diverged long after the rifts; the land masses were already apart, so the lineages must have dispersed across the water between them.](./plots/vicariance-test.py)

Most of the fragmentation of Gondwana happened in the Mesozoic, before the end-Cretaceous mass extinction 66 Myr ago ([7.10.31](/7.10.31)), and dated phylogenies show that many supposedly Gondwanan clades are too young:

- The African–South American disjunction of cichlids would require the family to be more than 110 Myr old, but both fossils and DNA indicate that it is at most about 65 Myr old. These freshwater fishes must somehow have crossed the Atlantic when it was narrower than now.
- In *Nothofagus*, fossil-calibrated divergences of several genes show that two subgenera with species in both New Zealand and Australia diverged more than 30 Myr after these land masses separated.
- Flightless ratite birds (ostriches in Africa, rheas in South America, emus and cassowaries in Australia and New Guinea, kiwis and the extinct moas of New Zealand, the extinct elephant birds of Madagascar) were long the textbook example of Gondwanan vicariance. DNA sequences, including ancient DNA from moas and elephant birds, contradict it: the flying tinamous of South America are nested within the ratites, the kiwis are the sister group of the elephant birds rather than of the moas, and several divergences are younger than the separation of the land masses involved. The simplest explanation is that the ancestors could fly, dispersed between land masses, and that flightlessness evolved several times independently.

Some clades are old enough. Marsupials (opossums in tropical America; kangaroos, koalas and many others in Australia) appear to have spread between South America and Australia through Antarctica before these land masses separated, about 50 Myr ago. Cypresses (Cupressaceae) originated in the Triassic, when Pangaea was intact, and their two major clades split about 153 Myr ago, when Laurasia and Gondwana were separating; one is distributed mostly on the former Laurasian continents (Eurasia, North America), the other mostly on former Gondwanan ones, although some lineages later dispersed into Africa. The general conclusion is that vicariance explains some disjunctions, but dispersal appears to explain more.

### 30.4.3. Dispersal

Ordinary dispersal, generation after generation, carries species gradually through suitable habitat into new areas, where they may differentiate by **allopatric speciation** or parapatric speciation ([7.10.12](/7.10.12)). When a land bridge between North and South America formed in the late Miocene and Pliocene, North American deer, horses, camelids and cats spread deep into South America, and a few South American families, including armadillos, opossums and porcupines, colonized North America (the Great American Biotic Interchange). The plant family Malpighiaceae spread step by step from tropical America through North America and Europe into Africa during warm intervals of the Paleogene.

Organisms also disperse, rarely, over long distances of unsuitable habitat. Insects, lizards and the seeds of many plants reach oceanic islands on rafts of vegetation swept out to sea by floods, some as water-resistant eggs. Such rare colonizations often give rise to **adaptive radiations** ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)): the Galápagos finches descend from a South American grassquit. The caviomorph rodents of South America (porcupines, chinchillas, guinea pigs and their relatives) and the New World monkeys both descend from African ancestors that crossed the then narrower Atlantic, apparently on rafts, in the Eocene or early Oligocene. At least 110 genera of plants that occur on both sides of the tropical Atlantic are too young to have occupied Gondwana before it rifted and must have crossed the ocean, most by floating; even single species have done so, such as the kapok tree (*Ceiba pentandra*), a giant of rainforests in tropical America and West Africa.

The Hawaiian Islands show how geology and dispersal together shape a biota. The islands formed one after another as the Pacific plate moved northwest over a volcanic hot spot, so that they are progressively older toward the northwest: Kauai is about 5 Myr old, the "Big Island" of Hawaii less than 0.5 Myr. If a clade colonized each new island soon after it formed, rarely moved from a younger to an older island and suffered little extinction, its phylogeny should be a comb whose earliest-diverging lineages live on Kauai and whose youngest live on Hawaii (the *progression rule*). Phylogenies of the Hawaiian trees of the genus *Psychotria* and of the crickets of the genus *Laupala* show this pattern (figure below).

## 30.5. Phylogeography

**Definition 30.5.1 (Phylogeography)** **Phylogeography** is the description and analysis of the processes that govern the geographic distribution of lineages of genes, especially within species and among closely related species.

The term and the field were introduced by John Avise and colleagues in the 1980s, first with mitochondrial DNA. The **gene genealogy** of a sample of gene copies from across the range of a species ([7.10.7](/7.10.7)) records the movements of the organisms that carried them, and therefore the history of dispersal and vicariance that produced the present distribution. Analyses of populations of the tree *Symphonia globulifera* show, for example, that it dispersed from West Africa to South America more than 15 Myr ago, reached the West Indies and eastern South America, and later crossed the Andes and spread to coastal Ecuador and Central America.

Much phylogeographic work concerns the Pleistocene. During glacial periods species survived in *refugia*, regions where the climate remained suitable, and expanded from them when the ice retreated. The southeastern coastal plain of the United States provides a case of shared history: in the spotted sunfish (*Lepomis punctatus*), the bowfin (*Amia calva*) and a pair of sister species of mints (*Dicerandra*), the gene trees show a sharp division between eastern and western populations, which are thought to have been isolated in two refugia, perhaps separated by high sea level during interglacials. Agreement among species is the key evidence. A single gene tree is one realization of a random process: its deep split may reflect chance in the **coalescent** rather than a historical event. If many codistributed species, with different ecologies, show splits at the same place and of the same age, a common cause, such as a shared vicariant barrier, is likely (*comparative phylogeography*; figure below).

![Comparative phylogeography: a test for a shared vicariant event. In each of four codistributed species an eastern and a western population separated $T$ generations ago (time in units of $2N$ generations, $N$ the size of each population). Two gene copies sampled on opposite sides can coalesce only in the ancestral population, so their divergence time is $T$ plus an exponential waiting time with mean 1. B: the species were split at different times ($T = 0.3$, $1.0$, $2.0$, $3.5$), as expected if each crossed or met the barrier independently.](./plots/comparative-phylogeography.py)

Modern methods fit explicit models to multilocus data: population trees with migration, changes in population size, and divergence times shared or not among species, using the structured and multispecies coalescent ([7.10.7](/7.10.7), [7.10.25](/7.10.25)) and the description of continuous spatial structure by **isolation by distance** and clines ([7.10.10](/7.10.10)). The expansion from refugia leaves its own genetic signature: populations near the leading edge descend from a series of small founder groups and have lost genetic diversity relative to those near the refugium. Human populations show the same pattern on a global scale, with diversity declining with distance from Africa ([7.10.33](/7.10.33)).

## 30.6. Range Limits: Ecology and Evolution

The range of a species reflects not only the history of its ancestors but also present conditions, the subject of ecological biogeography. To expand its range a species must disperse to the new region; individuals of sexual species must find mates, which is hard at low density; and they must tolerate the physical conditions, find food and habitat, and contend with competitors, predators and parasites.

*Dispersal limitation.* The edge of a range is sometimes simply a barrier, as when a terrestrial species stops at the coast. Such a border may be temporary, since the species might thrive beyond the barrier if it ever crossed it, as the plants with trans-Atlantic distributions show. Finding mates can also limit spread. In 19 of 20 clades of flowering plants examined, species that reproduce by **self-fertilization** have larger geographic ranges than related species that require **outcrossing** ([7.10.14](/7.10.14)), probably because a single selfing colonist can found a population; the outcrossers may still be spreading, more slowly.

*Ecological niche.* A species can persist only where every relevant condition, such as the minimum winter temperature, water supply and food, lies within its tolerances. G. Evelyn Hutchinson defined the *fundamental niche* as the set of environmental conditions under which a population can grow; this is the **ecological niche** of [7.10.15](/7.10.15). A locality within the fundamental niche may still be unoccupied because competitors or predators exclude the species. The competitive exclusion principle holds that species too similar in their use of limiting resources cannot coexist indefinitely, and many species occupy a broader range of elevations or habitats where a related species is absent than where it is present. Competition also affects whether two species formed in allopatry can spread into each other's ranges: pairs of Neotropical ovenbirds and woodcreepers (Furnariidae) became sympatric much sooner after speciation if they differed in bill shape and foraging than if they were similar (secondary sympatry is the first step to **character displacement**, [7.10.17](/7.10.17)).

*Climate.* The ranges of many species are correlated with temperature and rainfall. For 35 species of amphibians and reptiles introduced into North America, the northern limit of the introduced range matches the northern limit of the native range: the tropical green iguana (*Iguana iguana*) has not spread north of Florida, whereas the European wall lizard (*Podarcis muralis*) lives in New York. Many species have shifted their latitudinal or altitudinal ranges in recent decades with human-caused climate change; in several butterflies both the northern and the southern range limits have moved poleward.

Why do species not evolve broader tolerances and expand indefinitely? Gene flow from the center of the range can swamp adaptation at the edge, and small, sparse edge populations have little genetic variation (**gene swamping**, [7.10.10](/7.10.10)). Whatever the cause, related species often share their ecological requirements, a pattern called **phylogenetic niche conservatism** ([7.10.26](/7.10.26)). Congeneric plants of eastern Asia and eastern North America have similar latitudinal and climatic distributions, as do genera shared by North America and Europe; many lineages of herbivorous insects have fed on the same genus or family of plants for more than 40 Myr; oaks (*Quercus*) and dogwoods (*Cornus*) are among the many temperate genera of the northern continents that have never adapted to the lowland tropics. Niche conservatism is also why many species shifted their ranges during the Pleistocene instead of adapting in place to the changing climate, which raises the question of how well species can adapt to present climate change ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)).

## 30.7. Geographic Patterns of Diversity

### 30.7.1. The Latitudinal Diversity Gradient

**Definition 30.7.1 (Latitudinal Diversity Gradient)** The **latitudinal diversity gradient** is the decline in the number of species, and of higher taxa such as genera and families, from the tropics toward the poles, found on land and in the sea and in most groups of organisms.

The number of species of vascular plants per $10\,000\,\mathrm{km^2}$ in the Americas, for example, falls more than tenfold from the equator to high latitudes, and most groups of terrestrial animals and plants are most diverse in wet tropical lowlands. Three kinds of explanation have been proposed (figure below):

1. *Ecological limits.* Tropical environments may allow more species to coexist, because high productivity from abundant solar energy supports more populations large enough to persist, or because resources are more finely partitioned; the number of species then approaches a higher equilibrium.
2. *Diversification rate.* The tropics may have been a "cradle" in which species arise faster, or a "museum" in which they go extinct more slowly, terms of G. Ledyard Stebbins; either gives a higher net **diversification rate** ([7.10.31](/7.10.31)). New genera of marine bivalves, for example, have arisen mostly in the tropics over the last 11 Myr and spread from there toward higher latitudes while persisting in the tropics ("out of the tropics").
3. *Time and area.* Lineages may diversify at similar rates everywhere, but tropical climates are older and were more extensive. Through the Cretaceous and the first 60 Myr of the Cenozoic the Earth was warmer than now and much of it tropical, so most lineages originated in tropical climates; the few that evolved tolerance of cold and seasonality are younger and have had less time to diversify. This hypothesis rests on phylogenetic niche conservatism.

![Three models of species accumulation that could produce the latitudinal diversity gradient, after the scheme of Futuyma and Kirkpatrick (2017), Fig. 18.19. Each region starts with one lineage and its richness $N$ grows as $\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}t = DN(1 - N/K)$, with $D$ the net diversification rate and $K$ the number of species the region can hold. A: the tropics hold more species ($K = 400$ vs $120$) at the same rate $D = 0.15$ per Myr. B: the tropics diversify faster ($D = 0.12$ vs $0.06$ per Myr) and neither region is near its limit. C: equal rates and no limit, but diversification began 60 Myr ago in the tropics and 25 Myr ago in the temperate zone. The present is on the right.](./plots/ldg-models.py)

Productivity matters, but many recent studies support the time-and-area hypothesis. All major lineages of tree frogs (Hylidae) and their common ancestors lived in tropical America; only three lineages invaded the temperate zone, all recently, and across regions the number of hylid species is strongly correlated with the time since the family began diversifying there (estimated by the molecular clock). The number of tree species in the tropical, temperate and boreal biomes of each continent is correlated with an index combining the area each biome has occupied and the time since the Miocene, Oligocene or Eocene, and a similar model accounts for much of the regional variation in vertebrate richness. Tropical environments have simply covered larger areas for longer, so that is where most genera and species arose.

### 30.7.2. Island Biogeography

Islands are natural replicates for the study of diversity: they differ in area and isolation, and the number of species on them varies in regular ways. Two regularities are general. Larger islands have more species, approximately as a power of area,

$$
S = cA^{z},
$$
where $S$ is the number of species of a group, $A$ the area, and $c$ and $z$ constants; for islands $z$ usually lies between about 0.2 and 0.35, so a tenfold increase in area roughly doubles the number of species. And remote islands have fewer species than islands of the same size near a mainland. Robert MacArthur and Edward O. Wilson explained both by a dynamic balance.

**Definition 30.7.2 (Theory of Island Biogeography)** The **theory of island biogeography** (the MacArthur–Wilson model) holds that the number of species on an island is a dynamic equilibrium between the immigration of species new to the island and the local extinction of species already present. With a mainland pool of $P$ species and $S$ species on the island, the simplest form takes the immigration rate $I(S) = I_0(1 - S/P)$, which decreases because fewer arrivals are new species, and the extinction rate $E(S) = E_0 S/P$, which increases because more species can go extinct and each has a smaller population:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t} = I_0\left(1 - \frac{S}{P}\right) - E_0\frac{S}{P}, \qquad S^* = \frac{I_0}{I_0 + E_0}\,P. \tag{30.1}
$$
Isolation lowers $I_0$ and small area raises $E_0$, so near, large islands have the most species at equilibrium.

The parameters have a direct biological meaning: $I_0$ is the rate at which propagules of all mainland species arrive and establish on an empty island, and $E_0/P$ the per-species rate of local extinction, which falls with population size and therefore with island area. Eq. (30.1) is linear, so $S(t)$ approaches $S^*$ exponentially at the rate $(I_0 + E_0)/P$, whatever the starting number (figure below). Two predictions go beyond the species–area curve. First, the composition of an island biota should turn over even when the number of species is constant. Second, an island stripped of its species should return to about the same number. Daniel Simberloff and Wilson tested this in the late 1960s by fumigating small mangrove islands in the Florida Keys to remove their arthropods: within about a year most islands had regained roughly their former number of species, though not the same species, and the nearest island recovered most. The recolonization of the volcanic island of Krakatau after its eruption in 1883 is a natural counterpart.

The intersections (dots) are the equilibria $S^* = PI_0/(I_0 + E_0)$. B: recolonization of a defaunated island approaches $S^*$ exponentially at the rate $(I_0 + E_0)/P$. C: the species–area relationship $S = cA^z$ with $z = 0.25$, a straight line on logarithmic axes.](./plots/island-biogeography.py)

The model ignores evolution: on remote or large islands new species arise by speciation within the island faster than they arrive. On the Caribbean islands, species of *Anolis* lizards have arisen in place only on islands larger than about $3000\,\mathrm{km^2}$; on smaller islands the species come from elsewhere, and the radiations of the Hawaiian Islands are almost entirely products of speciation within the archipelago. Speciation then adds a third term to the balance, and diversity on the largest and most remote islands is set by the rates of speciation and extinction that are the subject of [7.10.31](/7.10.31). The theory also applies to "islands" of habitat on continents, mountaintops and lakes and, importantly, to fragments of habitat left by human land use, where it predicts the loss of species as area shrinks and isolation grows ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)).

## Summary

- **Biogeography** (Definition 30.1.1) has a historical and an ecological part. Darwin and Wallace used distributions as evidence for evolution: similar habitats on different continents carry unrelated organisms, barriers separate different biotas, and the inhabitants of a region, and of oceanic islands, are related to one another and to the nearest mainland.
- Taxa are often **endemic** to regions; Wallace's biogeographic realms (Definition 30.2.1) and Wallace's line (Definition 30.2.2) reflect the history of land and sea more than present climate. Many taxa have **disjunct distributions** (Definition 30.2.3).
- Ranges change by extinction, dispersal and **vicariance** (Definition 30.3.1). Dated phylogenies test vicariance: a clade split by a barrier must be at least as old as the barrier. Many supposedly Gondwanan clades (cichlids, southern beeches, ratites) are too young and dispersed; marsupials and cypresses are old enough. Dispersal seems to explain more disjunctions than vicariance; the Hawaiian clades follow the progression rule.
- **Phylogeography** (Definition 30.5.1) reconstructs history from the geographic distribution of gene lineages: expansion from Pleistocene refugia, and shared vicariance shown by concordant splits in codistributed species.
- Range limits are set by dispersal, by the **ecological niche**, competitors and climate; species do not expand indefinitely, and related species tend to share their niches (phylogenetic niche conservatism).
- The **latitudinal diversity gradient** (Definition 30.7.1) may reflect higher ecological limits, higher diversification rates or the greater age and extent of tropical environments; the last (time and area) is supported by many phylogenetic studies.
- In the **theory of island biogeography** (Definition 30.7.2) richness is a balance of immigration and extinction, Eq. (30.1); it explains the effects of area ($S = cA^z$) and isolation and the recovery of defaunated islands. On large, remote islands speciation within the island becomes the main source of species.

## Questions

1. A new species of the tree family Dipterocarpaceae, previously thought to be restricted to tropical Asia, is found in a rainforest in Colombia. What hypotheses could explain its presence there, and how would you test them?

   **Answer** (a) Vicariance: the family is old enough to have been distributed across a former land connection (Gondwana, or a northern route in warm periods) and survives as a relict in South America; (b) long-distance dispersal across an ocean; (c) a former wider distribution, for example across the northern continents in the warm Paleogene, followed by extinction in between. Test them with a dated phylogeny of the family including the Colombian species: if it is the sister group of all Asian species and the split is older than the separation of the land masses concerned, vicariance is possible; if it is nested among Asian species and the divergence is recent, dispersal is required. Fossils (wood, pollen) of the family in Africa, North America or Europe would support a former wider range; the dispersal ability of the seeds (winged fruits in many dipterocarps, poorly suited to crossing oceans) bears on the plausibility of (b).

2. Apart from birds and bats, the native land vertebrates of New Zealand are a few frogs of a single genus (*Leiopelma*) and many skinks and geckos; there are no snakes and no native terrestrial mammals, and the native freshwater fishes are mostly galaxiids, whose young spend time at sea. Is this biota more likely to derive from vicariance or from dispersal? What is the significance of the missing groups?

   **Answer** Mostly from dispersal. New Zealand separated from the rest of Gondwana about 80 Myr ago, and if its fauna were a vicariant fragment of Gondwana we would expect a broad sample of the groups present then, including the ancestors of the mammals, snakes and strictly freshwater fishes of Australia and South America. The groups present are mostly those that cross water readily (flying birds and bats, lizards that raft, fishes with a marine stage) or their descendants (flightless birds descended from fliers), and their closest relatives elsewhere are often younger than the separation. The absence of whole groups that disperse poorly over sea is the classic signature of an oceanic, dispersal-derived biota; it has also been proposed that much of New Zealand was submerged in the Oligocene, which would have eliminated older lineages. A few groups (*Leiopelma* frogs, some insects and plants) may be old Gondwanan relicts, and only dated phylogenies can show which.

3. Using Eq. (30.1), derive the equilibrium number of species and the time scale of approach to it. An island has a pool of $P = 200$ species, $I_0 = 10$ and $E_0 = 30$ species per year. How many species are expected at equilibrium, and how long does a defaunated island take to regain half of them?

   **Answer** Setting $\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t = 0$: $I_0 - (I_0 + E_0)S/P = 0$, so $S^* = PI_0/(I_0 + E_0)$. Writing $u = S - S^*$ gives $\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = -[(I_0 + E_0)/P]\,u$, so $S(t) = S^* + (S_0 - S^*)\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$ with $k = (I_0 + E_0)/P$. Here $S^* = 200 \cdot 10/40 = 50$ species and $k = 40/200 = 0.2$ per year; from $S_0 = 0$ the half-way point is reached at $t = \ln 2/k \approx 3.5$ years.

4. Under the species–area relationship $S = cA^z$ with $z = 0.25$, what fraction of the species of a forest is eventually expected to be lost if 90 % of its area is cleared and the rest remains as one block? What assumptions does this estimate make?

   **Answer** $S_{\mathrm{new}}/S = (A_{\mathrm{new}}/A)^z = 0.1^{0.25} \approx 0.56$, so about 44 % of the species would eventually be lost. The estimate assumes that the remaining forest behaves like an island at equilibrium with the same $z$ (in reality, fragments are not islands in a sea of uninhabitable ocean, and some species use the cleared matrix), that loss is not immediate (the "extinction debt" is paid over time), and that the lost area contained a random sample of species; endemics concentrated in the cleared part, or edge effects and fragmentation into many small blocks, would make the loss greater.

5. Phylogenetic niche conservatism explains why many species shifted their ranges during the Pleistocene rather than adapting to the changing climate. Is this consistent with the many examples of rapid adaptation to human-altered environments ([7.10.3](/7.10.3))?

   **Answer** Largely yes. Rapid adaptation is usually documented for traits with abundant genetic variation and simple targets (resistance to a toxin, a change in beak length, timing of breeding), often in large populations under strong, consistent selection. Tolerance of a new climate typically involves many physiological traits at once, some with little genetic variation (upper thermal limits, for example) and genetic correlations that constrain joint change; at the edge of the range, gene flow from the center and small population size further hinder adaptation. When a suitable climate is available elsewhere, tracking it by dispersal is easier than adapting in place, so habitat tracking prevails. Niche conservatism is a statistical tendency over millions of years and coexists with occasional niche shifts, which are what the rapid-adaptation examples show on short time scales.
