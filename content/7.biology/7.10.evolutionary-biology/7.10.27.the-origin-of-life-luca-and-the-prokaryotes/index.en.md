For more than half of the history of life, the only organisms on Earth were bacteria and archaea. Everything that followed, eukaryotic cells, multicellular bodies, plants and animals, was built on the biochemistry they invented: the genetic code and the ribosome, the membrane and its pumps, photosynthesis and respiration, the cycles of carbon, nitrogen and sulfur on which ecosystems still depend. This chapter goes back to the beginning. It asks when and how life arose, what can be inferred about the last universal common ancestor of all living things, how the deepest branches of the tree of life are rooted, and how the prokaryotes diversified, in their metabolism and in their genomes, where lateral gene transfer blurs the tree into a partial network.

The questions are hard because the evidence is thin. No molecular fossils of the first replicators survive, rocks older than about 3.5 billion years are rare and altered, and phylogenetic signal decays over billions of years. The answers therefore combine geology, laboratory chemistry and the comparison of living genomes, and they differ greatly in certainty. Some of the molecular side is in other chapters: the RNA world hypothesis, ribozymes, the error threshold and protocells in [7.2.23](/7.2.23), the energetics of early cells in [7.1.1](/7.1.1), the cell biology of prokaryotes and the origin of eukaryotic cells in [7.3.1](/7.3.1), bacterial genetics in [7.4.10](/7.4.10), the methods of phylogenetics in [7.8.6](/7.8.6) and of metagenomics in [7.8.14](/7.8.14). The origin of eukaryotes and of multicellularity continues in [7.10.28](/7.10.28), and the geological time scale and the methods of dating are in [7.10.29](/7.10.29).

## 27.1. Before Life: The Early Earth

### 27.1.1. The Formation of the Earth

The solar system formed about 4.57 billion years ago (Ga), the age given by radiometric dating of the oldest meteorites; the Earth accreted from colliding bodies within the next few tens of millions of years, and its age is usually given as about 4.54 Ga ([7.10.29](/7.10.29) explains the dating). A collision with a body about the size of Mars is thought to have formed the Moon. The heat of accretion and of such impacts melted the surface, and large impacts may have evaporated oceans and sterilized the planet more than once. Mineral grains of zircon from the Jack Hills of Western Australia, about 4.4 Ga old, preserve oxygen isotope ratios that suggest that liquid water already existed at the surface; the oldest known rocks, gneisses from the Acasta region near Great Slave Lake in northwestern Canada, are about 4.0 Ga old. Rocks of the Hadean eon (before about 4.03 Ga) are almost entirely lost to recycling of the crust by plate tectonics.

The early atmosphere contained little or no free oxygen. It was long believed to have been strongly reducing, rich in methane, ammonia and hydrogen; most geochemists now think that the gases released from the mantle made it only weakly reducing, dominated by carbon dioxide, nitrogen and water vapor, although local or transient reducing conditions, for example after impacts, may have existed. Oceans of liquid water probably existed from very early, and small protocontinents grew and merged over the following billion years.

### 27.1.2. The Oldest Evidence of Life

Three kinds of evidence are used, and each has been disputed.

**Definition 27.1.1 (Stromatolite)** A **stromatolite** is a layered sedimentary structure formed by the trapping, binding and precipitation of sediment by microbial mats, mainly of photosynthetic bacteria such as cyanobacteria; living stromatolites grow today in a few warm, salty, shallow seas, as in Shark Bay, Western Australia.

- *Stromatolites.* Stromatolite-like structures about 3.5 Ga old occur in Western Australia, and conical stromatolites of the Strelley Pool Formation, about 3.43 Ga old, have the same structure as those built by living microbial mats. Layered mounds can also form abiotically, however, so the oldest ones are debated.
- *Microfossils.* Filaments in the 3.465-Ga Apex chert of Western Australia were described as the oldest fossil cyanobacteria; later analysis showed that many of them can be explained as artifacts of minerals, and their biological nature remains controversial.
- *Chemical fossils.* Enzymatic carbon fixation, especially by rubisco, prefers the lighter isotope $^{12}\mathrm{C}$, so organic carbon made by organisms is depleted in $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ relative to the carbonate from which it was made, a signal that survives in kerogen. Carbon with such a signature in rocks about 3.7–3.8 Ga old from Greenland has been claimed as evidence of life, and graphite inclusions in a 4.1-Ga zircon have been reported with a similar signature, but metamorphism and abiotic processes can mimic or reset the signal.

The safe conclusion is that of Futuyma and Kirkpatrick: the evidence of life is strong by 3.4 Ga and debated back to 3.5 Ga, and older claims are possible but unproven. Since the surface was probably habitable from about 4.4 Ga and certainly by 4 Ga, life arose within the first few hundred million years at which it could, a short time compared with its later history.

## 27.2. How Did Life Begin?

### 27.2.1. A Short History of Ideas

Until the nineteenth century most naturalists accepted spontaneous generation, the origin of living things from nonliving matter in a broth or in rotting meat. Francesco Redi (1668) showed that maggots do not appear in meat protected from flies, and Louis Pasteur's experiments with swan-necked flasks (1859–1862) showed that boiled broth remains sterile if microbes in the air cannot reach it: in today's world, life comes only from life. This left the question of how life arose at the beginning. Darwin wrote to Joseph Hooker (1871) of a "warm little pond, with all sorts of ammonia and phosphoric salts, light, heat, electricity", in which a protein might be formed, adding that such matter would now be "instantly devoured or absorbed", which would not have been the case before living creatures were formed.

In the 1920s Aleksandr Oparin and J. B. S. Haldane independently proposed that in a reducing early atmosphere, energy from lightning and ultraviolet light would have produced organic molecules that accumulated in the oceans as a "prebiotic soup", from which the first living systems formed; Oparin proposed that droplets of concentrated organic polymers (coacervates) were the first compartments. Panspermia, the idea that life arrived on Earth from elsewhere (Svante Arrhenius, 1903), does not explain the origin of life, only moves it; the delivery of organic molecules by meteorites and comets, which certainly happens, is a different matter.

### 27.2.2. Prebiotic Chemistry

The synthesis of urea from inorganic salts (Wöhler, 1828) and of sugars from formaldehyde in alkaline solution (the formose reaction, Butlerov, 1861) had already shown that the molecules of life need no living cells.

**Definition 27.2.1 (Chemical Evolution)** **Chemical evolution** (prebiotic chemistry) is the abiotic formation, transformation and accumulation of organic molecules, from simple compounds to polymers and networks of reactions, that preceded and led to the origin of life.

**Definition 27.2.2 (Miller–Urey Experiment)** In the **Miller–Urey experiment** (Stanley Miller in the laboratory of Harold Urey, 1953), a mixture of methane, ammonia, hydrogen and water vapor, circulating above boiling water, was exposed to electrical discharges simulating lightning; within days the condensed water contained amino acids, including several found in proteins, and simple compounds such as hydrogen cyanide and formaldehyde.

Later experiments of this kind produced most of the protein amino acids, sugars including ribose (by the formose reaction), and the base adenine (from hydrogen cyanide and ammonia, Joan Oró, 1960). The Murchison meteorite, which fell in Australia in 1969, contains dozens of amino acids, both protein and nonprotein, showing that such chemistry occurs in space. But the experiment has three limitations. In a weakly reducing atmosphere of carbon dioxide and nitrogen, yields fall drastically. Many products form in tiny amounts, and some, such as ribose, are unstable in water. And for a long time no prebiotic synthesis produced nucleotides at all; plausible routes to pyrimidine and purine ribonucleotides have been found only since 2009, by chemistry that avoids free ribose ([7.2.23](/7.2.23#sec-23.6.1)). Prebiotic syntheses also produce both mirror-image forms of chiral molecules, while life uses only one (**chirality**): L-amino acids and D-sugars. How a homochiral chemistry arose is unsolved.

### 27.2.3. Where Did It Happen?

Several settings have been proposed, each solving some problems and creating others:

| Setting | Attractions | Difficulties |
|---|---|---|
| Ocean surface, "primordial soup" | lightning, ultraviolet light, delivery from space | dilution; ultraviolet damage; weakly reducing atmosphere |
| Deep-sea hydrothermal vents | reduced compounds (H₂, H₂S), metal sulfides as catalysts, gradients of temperature, pH and redox; protected from radiation and impacts | high temperatures destroy RNA and many organics; dilution |
| Land: hot springs and evaporating pools | wet–dry cycles concentrate and polymerize nucleotides; minerals; ultraviolet light drives some syntheses | small and transient environments |
| Mineral surfaces and clays | concentrate and order molecules, catalyze polymerization and vesicle formation | the step from surface chemistry to free cells |

The discovery in 1977 of rich animal communities at deep-sea vents, based on chemosynthesis rather than sunlight, made vents a popular candidate; the alkaline vents of the kind found at the Lost City field, at lower temperatures and with natural proton gradients across mineral walls, have been proposed as the cradle of an energy metabolism resembling the proton-driven membranes of all cells. Terrestrial pools have gained support from the chemistry of nucleotides, which favors cycles of drying and wetting and ultraviolet light. There is no consensus.

### 27.2.4. Compartments

A replicating molecule in open solution cannot keep its products to itself: an improved catalyst helps every molecule nearby, parasitic sequences that are copied but contribute nothing outgrow the rest, and the link between genotype and phenotype is lost. Compartments solve these problems. Lipid membranes form spontaneously from amphipathic molecules ([7.1.2](/7.1.2)); vesicles of simple fatty acids, which may have been made prebiotically, grow by taking up more fatty acid, divide under mild shear, and are permeable to nucleotides, and clay particles catalyze their formation and the encapsulation of RNA adsorbed onto them. Such **protocells** ([Definition 23.6.1](/7.2.23#def-23.6.1)) can couple the replication of their contents to their own growth. Oparin's coacervates and the "proteinoid microspheres" that Sidney Fox made by heating amino acids are historical models of the same idea; they form compartments but have no means of heredity.

### 27.2.5. Replicators First or Metabolism First?

Barton and colleagues break the origin of life into seven steps: the formation of simple organic molecules; chemical evolution of more complex molecules and primitive metabolic networks; the origin of self-replication; compartmentalization; the linking of genotype and phenotype; the origin of the genetic code; and the takeover of replication by DNA. Their order is uncertain, and two broad views differ in what came first.

- *Replicator first.* Evolution by natural selection began with molecules that copied themselves, most plausibly RNA or a simpler predecessor, in an **RNA world** ([Definition 23.1.1](/7.2.23#def-23.1.1)) in which RNA was both genotype and phenotype; metabolism and proteins were added later. In vitro experiments show how such molecules evolve. Sol Spiegelman (1967) supplied the RNA genome of the bacteriophage Qβ with its replicase and nucleotides and transferred the products serially: after many transfers the population consisted of short RNAs, a small fraction of the original genome, that were copied fastest. Catalytic RNA ligases evolved greater efficiency in continuous automated culture.
- *Metabolism first.* Self-sustaining networks of reactions, catalyzed by mineral surfaces such as iron and nickel sulfides and driven by chemical disequilibria at vents, preceded genes (the "iron–sulfur world" of Günter Wächtershäuser). An autocatalytic network can grow and compete, but whether it can carry heritable variation, and so evolve, is doubtful.

The views are less opposed than they seem: replicators need a supply of activated monomers, which some form of geochemical metabolism had to provide, and metabolic networks need replicators to evolve far. Many current models combine a geochemical setting with RNA chemistry inside protocells.

### 27.2.6. Eigen's Paradox and the Hypercycle

Early replication was inaccurate. By Eigen's error threshold ([Theorem 23.6.1](/7.2.23#thm-23.6.1)), a replicator copied with error rate $\mu$ per nucleotide can keep its information only if its length is less than about $1/\mu$: around 100 nucleotides for nonenzymatic copying. A longer genome needs an accurate replicase, but an accurate replicase needs a longer genome (Eigen's paradox). Manfred Eigen and Peter Schuster (1977–1979) proposed a way out.

**Definition 27.2.3 (Hypercycle)** A **hypercycle** is a cyclic network of $n$ self-replicating molecules in which the replication of each member is catalyzed by the preceding one, so that the information of the whole cycle can exceed the error threshold of any single member. In the elementary model, with relative concentrations $x_i$ ($\sum_i x_i = 1$) and rate constants $k_i$,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = x_i\bigl(k_i x_{i-1} - \phi\bigr), \qquad \phi = \sum_j k_j x_j x_{j-1}, \qquad x_0 \equiv x_n.
$$
The term $\phi$ keeps the total constant, as in a flow reactor with dilution.

The members of a hypercycle coexist rather than competing: none can outgrow the others, because each depends on its predecessor. For $n \le 4$ the members approach a stable equilibrium (with damped oscillations for $n = 4$); for $n \ge 5$ they oscillate on a stable limit cycle. But hypercycles are vulnerable to parasites: a mutant that receives catalytic help from a member without helping the next gains at the cycle's expense, and in well-mixed solution it destroys it. Hypercycles therefore work only in space, on surfaces or in compartments, where parasites are kept with their victims and cycles that harbor them lose. Once replicators are enclosed in compartments that compete, selection acts on groups of cooperating molecules (**multilevel selection**, [7.10.16](/7.10.16)), and the specific hypercyclic coupling is no longer essential; the origin of cells is the first of the **major evolutionary transitions** ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)).

B: with n = 5 members they oscillate indefinitely on a limit cycle, each member peaking in turn. ### 27.2.7. The Genetic Code and the Takeover by DNA

The origin of translation and the code, and the takeover of information storage by DNA, are treated in [7.2.23](/7.2.23#sec-23.4). Two classes of explanation are proposed for the pattern of codon assignments. In the stereochemical view (Carl Woese), the code reflects chemical affinities between amino acids and their codons or anticodons: RNA aptamers selected in vitro to bind particular amino acids are enriched in the corresponding codons, for about half of the amino acids tested. In the "frozen accident" view (Francis Crick), the assignments were largely arbitrary but became fixed, because any change would alter many proteins at once. The code's structure, in which similar codons specify similar amino acids so that many errors are harmless, also suggests selection for robustness. Ribonucleotide cofactors such as ATP, NAD⁺ and coenzyme A, and the synthesis of deoxyribonucleotides from ribonucleotides, are taken as molecular fossils of an RNA world.

## 27.3. LUCA, the Last Universal Common Ancestor

### 27.3.1. One Origin

Living or almost-living systems may have arisen more than once, but all known organisms descend from a single ancestor. The evidence is a set of universal features that are arbitrary as far as we can tell, and so would not have arisen independently twice: all organisms use L-amino acids, although D-amino acids would serve equally well; they share the genetic code with minor variations; they store information in DNA, copied by base pairing; they transcribe it with homologous RNA polymerases and translate it on ribosomes made of homologous rRNAs and proteins, with tRNAs, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and elongation factors; they use ATP as energy currency; and they are bounded by membranes with homologous transporters and protein-insertion machinery.

**Definition 27.3.1 (Last Universal Common Ancestor)** The **last universal common ancestor** (LUCA) is the most recent population of organisms from which all living cellular organisms descend.

LUCA was not the first living thing: the lineage leading to it already had a long history, and it lived among other forms whose lineages have died out. If genes were transferred between lineages before and after LUCA, different genes may trace back to different ancestors, and LUCA is best understood as the ancestor of the core genes that have been transmitted mostly vertically ([27.6.3](/7.10.27#sec-27.6.3)).

### 27.3.2. What LUCA Was Like

Genes present in both Bacteria and Archaea, and inferred to have been inherited vertically, tell what LUCA had:

- **translation** in nearly modern form: small- and large-subunit rRNAs, many ribosomal proteins, tRNAs, several aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, the elongation factors EF-Tu and EF-G;
- **transcription** by a multisubunit RNA polymerase with homologs of the bacterial subunits RpoA, RpoB and RpoC;
- a DNA genome, some DNA repair and recombination (RecA), and protein insertion into membranes (SecY, signal recognition particle);
- ATP synthase driven by an ion gradient across a membrane.

Other systems are strikingly different between the domains. The core enzymes of DNA replication of Bacteria and of Archaea are largely unrelated, and so are the lipids of their membranes: ester-linked fatty acids on glycerol-3-phosphate in Bacteria, ether-linked isoprenoids on glycerol-1-phosphate in Archaea. Either LUCA had simpler or different systems that were replaced in one or both lineages, or these systems evolved separately after the split, and LUCA was a "progenote" (Woese) whose replication and membranes were still rudimentary, perhaps with an RNA genome. The deepest branches of rRNA trees were once dominated by hyperthermophiles, suggesting a hot LUCA; but the base composition of rRNA converges in thermophiles, which can bias trees, and reconstructions of ancestral proteins have given conflicting temperatures.

Genome-scale reconstructions go further, with the caveat that they depend on how lateral transfer is distinguished from vertical descent. Weiss and colleagues (2016) identified 355 protein families likely present in LUCA, which suggest an anaerobic organism that fixed carbon dioxide by the reductive acetyl-CoA pathway using hydrogen, in a hydrothermal setting. A later analysis using a molecular clock calibrated across the duplicated genes of the tree estimated that LUCA lived about 4.2 Ga and had a genome of a few megabases encoding some 2600 proteins (Moody and colleagues, 2024). On such views LUCA was already a complex cell, and the progenote stage lies before it.

### 27.3.3. Rooting the Tree of Life

A phylogeny is rooted by an outgroup ([7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.2.4)), but there is no outgroup to all of life. In 1989, Iwabe and colleagues and Gogarten and colleagues independently found a way around the problem: genes that were duplicated before LUCA. The elongation factors EF-Tu and EF-G are paralogs present in all organisms, as are the catalytic and regulatory subunits of the proton ATPases. A tree of the EF-Tu genes of all domains can be rooted with the EF-G genes, and vice versa: each paralog serves as the outgroup of the other. Both analyses placed the root between Bacteria on one side and Archaea plus eukaryotes on the other, implying that Archaea and eukaryotes are sister groups, and that the "prokaryotes" are a **paraphyletic group**.

![Rooting the tree of life with ancient paralogs (after Barton et al. 2007, Fig. 5.20). A gene duplicated before LUCA (square) gave two paralogs, here EF-Tu and EF-G, which every domain inherited. In the gene tree, each paralog subtree contains the three domains, and each is rooted by the other: the root of the EF-Tu subtree lies on the branch leading to the EF-G subtree, and vice versa. Both subtrees place the root between Bacteria (green) and Archaea plus eukaryotes (orange and blue).](./plots/paralog-rooting.py)

The bacterial rooting has been challenged. The paralogs diverged so long ago that the branch joining the two subtrees is very long, and a long branch attracts other fast-evolving lineages toward it (**long-branch attraction**, [7.8.6](/7.8.6)); if the bacterial genes evolved faster, they would be pulled to the root artifactually. Analyses with better models and more genes have mostly confirmed a root between Bacteria and Archaea, though not unanimously.

## 27.4. Three Domains or Two?

In 1977 Carl Woese and George Fox compared the small-subunit (16S) ribosomal RNA of many prokaryotes and found that the methanogens and some extremophiles formed a group as distinct from the other prokaryotes as from eukaryotes. Woese, Kandler and Wheelis (1990) made them one of three domains ([7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.7.3)).

**Definition 27.4.1 (Three-Domain System)** The **three-domain system** divides cellular life into three primary lineages of equal rank, the domains Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya (eukaryotes), in which the Archaea and Eukarya are sister groups and each domain is monophyletic.

Archaea resemble bacteria in cell size, the absence of a nucleus, circular chromosomes and operons; they resemble eukaryotes in their information-processing machinery (an RNA polymerase related to eukaryotic polymerase II, TATA-binding protein, histones in many species, translation factors); and they are unique in their ether-linked membrane lipids and in methanogenesis. Since James Lake's "eocyte hypothesis" (1984), some analyses suggested instead that eukaryotes arose *within* the Archaea, from relatives of the Crenarchaeota. The question was settled largely by metagenomics. From 2015 on, genomes assembled from sediment samples revealed the Asgard archaea, which encode many proteins previously known only from eukaryotes (homologs of actin and its regulators, ESCRT proteins of membrane remodeling, small GTPases); phylogenies of conserved genes place eukaryotes within the Asgard archaea, probably within or as sister to the Heimdallarchaeia, and in 2020 the first Asgard archaeon was grown in culture ([7.3.1](/7.3.1)).

The current consensus is therefore a two-domain tree: Bacteria and Archaea are the primary domains, and eukaryotes are a branch of the Archaea that acquired a bacterial endosymbiont, the mitochondrion ([7.10.28](/7.10.28)). Eukaryotes remain a natural group of cells with a unique organization; what has changed is that "Archaea" in the sense of the three-domain system is a **paraphyletic group**, like "reptiles" without birds.

## 27.5. The Diversification of Bacteria and Archaea

### 27.5.1. A Tree Revealed by Molecules

Prokaryotes have few morphological characters, and those they have, such as cell shape and staining, are prone to convergence. Their phylogeny was unknown until molecular sequences became available; the 16S rRNA, present in all cells, slowly evolving and easy to amplify, became the standard marker. It led to many reclassifications: the radiation-resistant *Micrococcus radiodurans*, renamed *Deinococcus radiodurans*, is a relative of the thermophile *Thermus aquaticus*, not of *Micrococcus*; *Rickettsia* is an alphaproteobacterium, not a relative of *Chlamydia*. Most bacteria and archaea cannot be grown in the laboratory: microscopic counts of cells in natural samples exceed the colonies that grow on plates by orders of magnitude (the "great plate count anomaly"). Since the 1980s, sequencing rRNA genes amplified directly from the environment, and later whole metagenomes ([7.8.14](/7.8.14)), has revealed that most bacterial and archaeal phyla are known only from their DNA, and that uncultured lineages are often abundant and ecologically important. Genome-based trees now include a large radiation of small bacteria with reduced genomes, the candidate phyla radiation, known almost entirely from metagenomes.

### 27.5.2. Metabolic Diversity

Prokaryotes use almost every source of energy, electrons and carbon available on Earth, and every combination of the three occurs among them.

| Criterion | Alternatives | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Energy source | **phototroph** (light) or **chemotroph** (chemical reactions) | cyanobacteria; methanogens |
| Electron donor | lithotroph (inorganic: H₂, H₂S, Fe²⁺, NH₃) or organotroph (organic compounds) | sulfur-oxidizing bacteria; *E. coli* |
| Carbon source | **autotroph** (CO₂) or heterotroph (organic carbon) | *Aquifex*; most pathogens |

At least six pathways of carbon fixation exist, among them the Calvin cycle (cyanobacteria, plants and many chemoautotrophs), the reductive citric acid cycle (green sulfur bacteria, *Aquifex*), the reductive acetyl-CoA (Wood–Ljungdahl) pathway (acetogens and methanogens) and the 3-hydroxypropionate cycle (*Chloroflexus*); the last three occur in deep-branching lineages and are candidates for the most ancient. Methanogenesis, the reduction of carbon dioxide or methyl compounds to methane, is found only in archaea. **Nitrogen fixation** by nitrogenase occurs only in bacteria and archaea, which supply the fixed nitrogen of all ecosystems. Chemoautotrophic bacteria that oxidize hydrogen sulfide feed the animal communities of hydrothermal vents as symbionts of tube worms and clams. Light-driven proton pumps related to the bacteriorhodopsin of halophilic archaea, the proteorhodopsins, are widespread among marine bacteria, a form of phototrophy discovered only from metagenomic sequences.

### 27.5.3. Photosynthesis and the Great Oxidation Event

Chlorophyll-based photosynthesis evolved in bacteria; eukaryotes acquired it by endosymbiosis, as plastids ([7.10.28](/7.10.28)). It occurs in several bacterial phyla: the cyanobacteria, green sulfur bacteria (Chlorobi), green nonsulfur bacteria (Chloroflexi), heliobacteria (Firmicutes), some proteobacteria and a few other groups. All but the cyanobacteria are anoxygenic: they use one type of photochemical reaction center and take electrons from donors such as hydrogen sulfide, hydrogen or ferrous iron, producing no oxygen. Cyanobacteria alone combine two reaction centers in series (**photosystem II** and **photosystem I**, [7.1.20](/7.1.20)), which provide enough energy to take electrons from water, releasing oxygen. The distribution of photosynthetic genes across phyla, which do not form a clade, indicates that photosynthesis spread among them in part by lateral transfer of genes.

**Definition 27.5.1 (Great Oxidation Event)** The **Great Oxidation Event** (GOE) is the first lasting rise of free oxygen in the Earth's atmosphere, about 2.4–2.3 Ga, from trace levels to a concentration of perhaps a percent or more of the present level.

The best evidence for it is the disappearance, at about 2.3–2.4 Ga, of an unusual fractionation of sulfur isotopes that is produced by ultraviolet photochemistry only in an atmosphere without oxygen; other evidence includes the disappearance of detrital minerals such as pyrite and uraninite that are unstable in the presence of oxygen, and the appearance of oxidized red beds on land. Oxygenic photosynthesis must be older than the GOE; how much older is debated (estimates range from shortly before 2.4 Ga to more than 3 Ga), because oxygen produced by early cyanobacteria may have been consumed by reduced volcanic gases and iron in the oceans for a long time, the banded iron formations being one record of that sink. The rise of oxygen made aerobic respiration, with its far higher yield of ATP ([7.1.1](/7.1.1)), and an ozone layer possible, and was toxic to many anaerobes, which retreated to anoxic habitats. A second rise of oxygen at the end of the Proterozoic preceded the radiation of animals ([7.10.29](/7.10.29)).

## 27.6. Prokaryotic Genomes and Lateral Gene Transfer

### 27.6.1. Small, Dense and Changeable Genomes

The genomes of bacteria and archaea are compact: most range from about 1 to 10 Mb, with a median of a few megabases; they contain about one gene per kilobase, few introns and little intergenic DNA, and many genes are organized in **operons** ([7.2.2](/7.2.2#sec-2.1.2)). Genes that are not maintained by selection are lost rapidly, so prokaryotes carry few pseudogenes and little decayed DNA; the smallest genomes belong to endosymbionts that have shrunk by drift and loss ([7.10.18](/7.10.18#sec-18.8.3)), the largest to soil bacteria such as the myxobacteria and actinomycetes, with more than 10 Mb and more genes than yeast. Within a species, gene content varies enormously. The *E. coli* strains K-12 (harmless) and O157:H7 (a pathogen) share about 4000 genes, but O157:H7 has more than 1000 genes that K-12 lacks, and K-12 about 500 that O157:H7 lacks, most of them clustered in genomic islands inserted into a conserved backbone of shared genes. Islands that carry virulence genes (pathogenicity islands) often differ in base composition and codon usage from the rest of the genome, a sign that they came from elsewhere.

### 27.6.2. Lateral Gene Transfer

**Horizontal gene transfer** ([Definition 10.2.1](/7.4.10#def-10.2.1)) occurs by **transformation**, the uptake of free DNA; by **conjugation**, transfer through a cell-to-cell connection, often driven by conjugative plasmids; and by transduction, transfer of host DNA packaged in phage particles ([7.4.10](/7.4.10)). Some bacteria also produce gene transfer agents, phage-like particles that package random fragments of the host genome. Unlike sex in eukaryotes, these processes are usually one-way, transfer short pieces, and can cross great phylogenetic distances, even between domains.

Several barriers limit what is transferred and kept: restriction enzymes destroy foreign DNA; mismatch repair blocks recombination between diverged sequences; foreign genes may lack suitable promoters or codons; and a gene that gives no advantage is soon lost again. Transferred genes that persist are gradually "ameliorated" toward the base composition of their new genome, so compositional signatures detect only recent transfers. Genes differ in how often they are transferred. Genes of information processing (replication, transcription, translation), whose products interact with many others, are rarely transferred successfully (the complexity hypothesis), whereas genes for metabolic functions, resistance and virulence, which can work on their own and are often clustered in operons that can move as a unit, are transferred often.

### 27.6.3. Pangenomes and the "Web of Life"

The set of all genes found in the genomes of a species is its **pangenome** ([Definition 14.2.2](/7.4.14#def-14.2.2)): the *core genome* shared by all strains, and the *accessory genome* present in some. In many bacterial species, each newly sequenced genome adds new genes, so that the pangenome keeps growing without apparent limit (an "open" pangenome), while the core shrinks toward a set of essential genes. Across bacteria as a whole, only a few dozen genes, mainly for translation, are present in all genomes.

If genes can be transferred between any lineages, is there a tree of life at all? In the 1990s, as whole genomes revealed the extent of transfer, W. Ford Doolittle and others argued that the history of prokaryotes is better described as a web in which the tree is an artifact of averaging over genes with different histories. Two findings moderate this view. First, transfer is pervasive but uneven: a core of genes, mostly for information processing, has been inherited mainly vertically, and trees of concatenated core genes are congruent with each other and with rRNA trees. In the gammaproteobacteria, a tree of 205 core proteins agreed with nearly all the individual gene trees. Second, the genes transferred are mostly accessory genes that are soon lost again, so most transfers are short-lived. The tree of life is therefore a useful description of the vertical history of cell lineages, overlaid by a network of gene exchange that is dense among close relatives and in metabolic genes and thinner across great distances; organelles and the origin of eukaryotes are the great exceptions in which whole genomes were merged ([7.10.28](/7.10.28)).

### 27.6.4. What Is a Prokaryotic Species?

The **biological species concept** ([7.10.11](/7.10.11)) relies on sexual reproduction, which prokaryotes do not have, and gene exchange crosses species lines. Prokaryotic species are therefore defined operationally, as clusters of genomes. A traditional criterion was 70 % DNA–DNA reassociation; it corresponds roughly to 97–98.7 % identity of 16S rRNA genes and to an average nucleotide identity (ANI) of about 95 % across shared genes. Large genomic surveys find a gap in the distribution of ANI values near 95 %: most pairs of genomes are either more similar or much less similar, suggesting that prokaryotic species are real clusters maintained by homologous recombination, ecological specialization or periodic selection, although which force dominates is still debated.

## 27.7. The Origin of Viruses

Viruses are not on the tree of cells: no gene is shared by all viruses, they evolve fast and exchange genes readily, and many lineages may have arisen independently. Three hypotheses, not mutually exclusive, are discussed:

- *The virus-first (relic) hypothesis*: viruses descend from replicators of a precellular world, which became parasites of the first cells; their distant homologs of cellular enzymes (polymerases, reverse transcriptases, the jelly-roll capsid proteins) would be ancient.
- *The escape hypothesis*: viruses arose from genetic elements of cells, such as plasmids or transposons, that acquired capsids and the ability to leave cells.
- *The reduction hypothesis*: viruses descend from parasitic cells that lost their cellular machinery, as obligate intracellular bacteria have lost much of theirs.

The giant viruses of amoebae, beginning with mimivirus, whose 1.2-Mb genome encodes more than 900 proteins, including some components of translation, revived the reduction hypothesis; analyses of their genes suggest instead that many were acquired from hosts. Current virus classification groups viruses into a few realms, each defined by a hallmark gene such as a type of capsid protein or replicase, consistent with several independent origins.

## 27.8. Life beyond Earth

Astrobiology asks whether life exists or existed elsewhere, which would show whether the origin of life is a probable or an exceptional event: with a single example, its probability cannot be estimated. What is established is limited.

- *Chemistry.* Amino acids, nucleobases and other organic molecules occur in carbonaceous meteorites, and organic molecules are detected in interstellar clouds and comets: the raw materials are widespread.
- *Mars.* Orbiters and rovers have shown that early Mars had lakes, rivers and probably groundwater, and that Martian rocks contain organic compounds. Whether Mars ever had life is unknown; the claim of fossil bacteria in the Martian meteorite ALH84001 (1996) has not been accepted.
- *Ocean worlds.* Jupiter's moon Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus have oceans of liquid water beneath ice crusts; plumes from Enceladus contain water, salts, organic molecules and molecular hydrogen, a possible source of chemical energy like that at hydrothermal vents.
- *Exoplanets.* Thousands of planets around other stars are known, including rocky planets in the zone where liquid water could exist on the surface; the search for life there relies on atmospheric biosignatures, such as oxygen together with methane, whose interpretation is uncertain.

No life has been found beyond Earth. The open questions are those of this chapter read in reverse: which steps in the origin of life are likely under plausible conditions, and which, if any, are extremely improbable.

## Questions

1. Why does the universality of L-amino acids and of the genetic code argue for a single common ancestor of all life, whereas the universality of water as a solvent does not?

   **Answer** Homology is inferred from shared features that are arbitrary, that is, that could equally well have been otherwise: D-amino acids would make functional proteins, and other codon assignments would work. Independent origins would be expected to make different arbitrary choices, so sharing them indicates common descent. Water is used by all life because of its physical properties: any independent origin on Earth would also have used it, so its universality says nothing about ancestry.

2. In a well-mixed hypercycle, a parasitic mutant receives catalytic help from a member of the cycle but helps no other member. Explain why it spreads, and why compartmentalization changes the outcome.

   **Answer** The parasite's growth depends on the member that catalyzes it, but its existence costs it nothing, so it can grow faster than the member it competes with for that catalytic help; the member that it diverts help from declines, the cycle loses a link and collapses. In compartments, a parasite reduces the growth of the compartment that contains it, so compartments with parasites divide more slowly and are outcompeted by compartments without them; selection between compartments counteracts selection within them, as in multilevel selection.

3. How does rooting with a pair of ancient paralogs work, and what is the main artifact that could mislead it?

   **Answer** A gene duplicated before LUCA is present in two copies in every domain; in the combined gene tree each set of orthologs forms a subtree that contains all domains, and each subtree can be rooted on the branch leading to the other subtree, its outgroup. If both subtrees give the same root position, the root of the species tree is inferred. The connecting branch is very long, so long-branch attraction can pull a fast-evolving group, such as bacteria if their genes evolved faster, toward the root artifactually; better substitution models and more genes are used to check the result.

4. What evidence indicates that the atmosphere became oxygenated about 2.4 Ga, and why need this not be the time when oxygenic photosynthesis evolved?

   **Answer** Mass-independent fractionation of sulfur isotopes, produced by photochemistry in an anoxic atmosphere, disappears from sedimentary rocks at about 2.4–2.3 Ga; detrital pyrite and uraninite, unstable in oxygen, disappear, and oxidized red beds appear. Oxygen produced by cyanobacteria would first have reacted with reduced iron in seawater, reduced volcanic gases and organic matter; only when the production of oxygen, together with the burial of organic carbon, exceeded these sinks could free oxygen accumulate, so oxygenic photosynthesis may be considerably older than the GOE.

5. Given that lateral gene transfer is common, why is it still meaningful to speak of a species tree of bacteria?

   **Answer** Transfer is uneven among genes. Genes of the core informational machinery are rarely transferred successfully and are inherited mainly vertically; trees built from many concatenated core genes are congruent with each other and with the rRNA tree, and they describe the history of cell lineages. Most transferred genes are accessory and short-lived. The tree is therefore a description of the vertical backbone of descent, with a network of transfers superimposed, rather than a complete history of every gene.
