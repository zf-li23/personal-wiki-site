No one knows how many species live on Earth, even to the nearest million. About 1.5 million species of eukaryotes have been named; recent estimates of the total number of living eukaryotic species are about 5 million, with a margin of error of several million, and this excludes the bacteria and archaea, of which any sample of soil or seawater contains thousands of unnamed genomes. Diversity is also very unevenly distributed. The pine family has about 220 species, the orchids about 18 000 and the sunflower family about 23 000; the insect order Embioptera (webspinners) has about 500 described species, the beetles (Coleoptera) more than 350 000; the order Tubulidentata has a single living species, the aardvark, and the rodents more than 2280. Living species are, moreover, probably less than 1 % of all that have ever lived. This chapter asks how diversity has changed over the history of life and why. Has the number of species increased steadily, or has it fluctuated? Why do some clades contain far more species than others? Is there a limit to the number of species, and has it been reached? Two kinds of evidence bear on these questions: the fossil record, which records past diversity directly but incompletely, and the phylogenies of living species, which record the branching history of surviving lineages. Both are interpreted with the same simple model, borrowed from population dynamics. The history of life itself is told in [7.10.29](/7.10.29), the geographic patterns of diversity in [7.10.30](/7.10.30), and the macroevolutionary patterns of characters, including species selection, in [7.10.32](/7.10.32).

## 31.1. Measuring and Modeling Diversity

### 31.1.1. The Birth–Death Framework

In evolutionary studies "diversity" usually means the number of taxa, such as species or genera (the number of species is its species richness). The number $N$ of species in a clade changes by speciation and extinction, which are analogous to the births and deaths of individuals in a population. Let $S$ be the rate of speciation, the probability per unit time that a species gives rise to a new one, and $E$ the rate of extinction per species. In a short interval $\mathrm{d}t$ about $SN\,\mathrm{d}t$ new species arise and $EN\,\mathrm{d}t$ become extinct, so on average

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = (S - E)N = DN. \tag{31.1}
$$

**Definition 31.1.1 (Diversification Rate)** The **diversification rate** (net diversification rate) of a clade is $D = S - E$, the per-taxon rate of speciation (or, for higher taxa, origination) minus the per-taxon rate of extinction. The *turnover* of the clade is measured by $E/S$, or by the sum $S + E$.

The same equations describe the number of genera or families, with $S$ the rate of origination of new taxa. If $D$ is constant, the number of species grows exponentially when $D > 0$ and declines when $D < 0$. Eq. (31.1) is the deterministic mean of a stochastic **birth–death process** ([7.10.22](/7.10.22), [7.6.32](/7.6.32)): clades with the same rates differ greatly by chance, and many go extinct while young. Just as competition for resources slows the growth of a population, speciation may slow or extinction increase as the number of competing species grows (*diversity-dependent* factors), and diversity may then approach an equilibrium $K$, as in the logistic model (figure in §31.6). The model is an oversimplification, because changes in the environment and in the organisms change the rates, but it provides the framework for every question in this chapter: two clades, or two regions, can differ in richness because they differ in age (time to diversify), in $D$, or in $K$ (compare [7.10.30](/7.10.30#sec-30.7.1)).

### 31.1.2. Diversity in the Fossil Record and Its Biases

Paleontological studies of diversity mostly count genera or families, whose fossil record is more complete than that of species, and express rates per taxon per unit of time. Because the record is a very incomplete sample of past life, raw counts are biased in several ways, and much of paleobiology consists of finding and correcting these biases:

- *Sampling intensity.* Rare species are more likely to be found in large samples, so samples of different sizes must be standardized, for example by subsampling the same number of specimens or occurrences from each interval (rarefaction).
- *Unequal intervals and rock.* Geological stages differ in duration, and younger rocks are exposed over greater areas and volumes, so counts must be adjusted for time and for the amount of fossiliferous rock.
- *Range truncation.* A taxon is usually recorded only at scattered horizons, so its true origination is earlier than its first record and its true extinction later than its last. If many taxa went extinct at the same moment, their last records are spread over the preceding interval, smearing a sudden extinction into an apparently gradual one (the *Signor–Lipps effect*); the same holds in reverse for originations.
- *Exceptional preservation.* Rare deposits with soft tissues, such as the Burgess Shale of the Cambrian, record many taxa that are otherwise unknown. Taxa known only from one stage (singletons) are especially common in such deposits and in poorly sampled intervals; they make an interval look rich and create a spurious correlation between origination and extinction, because they appear to originate and disappear in the same interval. Counting only the taxa that cross the boundary between two stages avoids this bias.

The best-known bias concerns the present.

**Definition 31.1.2 (Pull of the Recent)** The **pull of the Recent** is the artificial increase in apparent diversity toward the present that arises because living taxa are known far more completely than fossil ones: a living taxon whose last fossil is old is nevertheless counted as present in every interval up to the Recent (the Holocene), whereas an extinct taxon of the same age is counted only between its first and last fossil occurrences.

Because younger taxa are more likely to be still alive, the extension of living taxa to the present inflates the counts of recent intervals more than those of older ones, and diversity seems to rise toward the present even if it did not (figure below). The bias is reduced by counting each living taxon only where it has fossil occurrences.

![The pull of the Recent, simulated. Taxa originate and go extinct at the same constant rate (0.1 per taxon per Myr), so true diversity (grey) has no trend, apart from random fluctuations. Each taxon leaves fossils as a Poisson process at rate 0.3 per Myr while it lives. A: diversity per 5-Myr bin counted from the first to the last fossil occurrence of each taxon (blue), and with every living taxon extended from its last fossil to the present (orange). B: the fraction of true diversity recovered. These corrections illustrate a general feature of science: researchers consider how their data could mislead them, and devise ways of avoiding being misled.

## 31.2. Diversity through the Phanerozoic

The most complete fossil record is that of marine animals with mineralized skeletons. Jack Sepkoski compiled from the literature the stratigraphic ranges of more than 4000 families and 30 000 genera of such animals through the 541 Myr since the start of the Cambrian. His plot of the number of families, one of the most famous graphs in paleobiology, shows a rapid increase in the Cambrian and Ordovician, a plateau through the rest of the Paleozoic, and a steady, almost fourfold increase through the Mesozoic and Cenozoic, interrupted by drops at the mass extinctions; about 1900 marine animal families live today, including those rarely or never fossilized. Sepkoski also found that the record can be described by three successive *evolutionary faunas*, each a set of classes that dominated marine diversity for a long time: a Cambrian fauna (trilobites, inarticulate brachiopods and others), a Paleozoic fauna (articulate brachiopods, crinoids, corals, cephalopods, ostracods and others) and a Modern fauna (gastropods, bivalves, bony fishes, malacostracan crustaceans, echinoids and others), which rose to dominance after the end-Permian extinction.

Later analyses with corrections for sampling and for the pull of the Recent changed some details: they found a decline in diversity in the Devonian instead of a Paleozoic plateau, an increase in the Permian before the end-Permian extinction, and a less steep, though still pronounced, increase through the Mesozoic and Cenozoic. Much of the increase reflects new ways of life, not just more taxa. Marine animals today occupy many more combinations of habitat tier (from the open water to deep in the sediment), feeding mode and motility than in the Cambrian; on land, ecologically novel forms such as dinosaurs, snakes, birds and bats account for much of the increase in tetrapod diversity. Land has its own history: the end-Permian extinction was followed, after a delay of more than 15 Myr, by the diversification of dinosaurs, crocodilians and the synapsids that included the ancestors of mammals; flowering plants proliferated in the Cretaceous and largely replaced gymnosperms, and insects diversified enormously. About 85 % of described living species live on land, most of them insects, and life on land has become more diverse than in the sea.

## 31.3. Origination and Extinction

### 31.3.1. Rates and Their Decline

The increase of diversity in the Mesozoic and Cenozoic implies that the origination rate of marine taxa has on average exceeded the extinction rate, but both have fluctuated. Some lineages became extinct in every interval.

**Definition 31.3.1 (Background Extinction)** **Background extinction** is the ordinary, continual extinction of lineages that occurs in every interval of geological time, as opposed to the rare intervals of mass extinction (§31.4).

David Raup and Sepkoski found that the background extinction rate of marine families has declined through the Phanerozoic, and so has the rate of origination of new genera and families in many groups, although origination rose after each of the large mass extinctions, when diversity recovered, usually within 10–15 Myr. One might suppose that species became better adapted over time and so less vulnerable, but natural selection has no foresight: it cannot prepare species for new kinds of environmental change. Two other explanations are more plausible. Genera and families seem to contain more species in the Cenozoic than earlier, and a higher taxon goes extinct only when all its species do. And some clades are more volatile than others, with high rates of both origination and extinction: they tend to disappear early, leaving the less volatile clades with longer-lived taxa.

Origination and extinction rates are correlated across clades: trilobites, for example, turned over much faster than gastropods, and the orders of marine animals that are now extinct had, on average, higher rates of both than the living ones. Steven Stanley suggested that the same features of organisms raise both rates. Ecologically specialized species are more vulnerable to environmental change and also speciate more often, perhaps because their patchy distributions favor isolation; species with broad geographic ranges are less prone to extinction, because local catastrophes do not eliminate them, and speciate less often, probably because they disperse well. Groups also differ characteristically in the lifespan of their species: the average duration of a species is about 23 Myr in bivalves, 10 Myr in gastropods and 7 Myr in sea urchins.

### 31.3.2. Survivorship and the Red Queen

Do taxa become more resistant to extinction as they age, perhaps because older taxa have had more time to adapt? This is a different question from whether extinction rates have changed through geological time, and it is answered with *taxonomic survivorship curves*: the number of taxa (for example the genera of a family) that persist for at least $t$ million years, whenever they originated. If every taxon, young or old, has the same probability $E$ per unit time of going extinct, durations are exponentially distributed and the number surviving to age $t$ is

$$
n(t) = n_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-Et}, \qquad \ln n(t) = \ln n_0 - Et,
$$
a straight line on a logarithmic scale. If the risk declines with age, the curve is concave, with a long tail of old survivors (figure below).

![Taxonomic survivorship curves for 2000 simulated genera. A: with a constant extinction rate of 0.1 per Myr (blue), the number surviving to each age falls on a straight line on a logarithmic axis; if the risk declines with age (orange, a Weibull distribution of durations with shape 0.6 and the same mean), the curve is concave with a long tail. B: the extinction rate estimated in successive 5-Myr age classes is constant in the first case and declines in the second.](./plots/survivorship.py)

When Leigh Van Valen plotted survivorship curves for many groups in 1973, he found them to be roughly straight: the probability of extinction appeared to be independent of the age of a taxon (Van Valen's *law of constant extinction*). This is what one would expect if each taxon is continually assaulted by new changes in its environment, each carrying a risk of extinction. Van Valen proposed that the most important of these changes is the evolution of other species: because competitors, predators and parasites keep evolving, each species must evolve as fast as it can merely to persist, like the Red Queen in Lewis Carroll's *Through the Looking-Glass* who has to run to stay in the same place (the **Red Queen hypothesis**, [7.10.14](/7.10.14), [7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Not all data fit. In many clades, among them two groups of Paleozoic crinoids, older genera have lower extinction rates than younger ones, which would be expected if similar changes in the physical environment recur, so that lineages that survived one episode carry features that help them survive the next.

## 31.4. Mass Extinctions

**Definition 31.4.1 (Mass Extinction)** A **mass extinction** is an interval of geological time, short relative to the duration of most taxa, in which a large fraction of the species of many higher taxa, in many environments and regions, became extinct, at rates far above the background rate.

Five mass extinctions, the "big five", are generally recognized (figure below): at the end of the Ordovician, in the Late Devonian, at the end of the Permian, at the end of the Triassic and at the end of the Cretaceous (the K/Pg boundary between the Cretaceous and the Paleogene, formerly called the K/T boundary). They were responses to different global perturbations, sometimes acting together. An analysis in the 1980s suggested that mass extinctions recurred with a regular period of about 26 Myr, which was attributed to periodic showers of comets; the periodicity is not now generally accepted.

![The five largest mass extinctions of the Phanerozoic on the ICS geological time scale. The Late Devonian event is placed at the Frasnian–Famennian boundary, about 372 Myr ago. Causes and magnitudes as given by Futuyma and Kirkpatrick (2017) and Barton et al. (2007); estimates of magnitude differ among studies and measures.](./plots/big-five.py)

The end-Ordovician extinction, perhaps caused by a drop in temperature and sea level during a glaciation, was in proportional terms perhaps the second largest. The Late Devonian extinctions were prolonged, struck marine life above all, especially reef ecosystems, and have causes that are still debated. The end-Triassic extinction was associated with a massive release of carbon into the atmosphere and global warming; marine diversity fell by about half, ammonoids and bivalves were devastated, and the extinction cleared the way for the dominance of dinosaurs on land.

### 31.4.1. The End-Permian Extinction

The extinction at the end of the Permian, 252 Myr ago, was the most severe in the history of life. It eliminated at least 56 % of the genera and more than 80 % of the species of skeleton-bearing marine invertebrates, apparently within less than 200 000 years. Trilobites and the rugose and tabulate corals disappeared; crinoids (perhaps only one genus survived), brachiopods, bryozoans and ammonoids were decimated; on land several orders of insects and many families of amphibians and synapsids became extinct, and plant communities changed greatly. The likely trigger was the eruption of the Siberian Traps, flood basalts that covered several million square kilometres. The eruptions are thought to have released carbon dioxide, perhaps augmented by methane, and toxic gases such as hydrogen sulfide, causing global warming, aridity and acidification of the ocean, which hinders the formation of calcium carbonate skeletons; the oceans became widely depleted of oxygen (anoxic), as shown by pyrite and other indicators in boundary sediments, and on land there were wildfires, deforestation and soil erosion. Recovery was slow: in the sea the large, sessile animals living on the sea floor were replaced as dominants by bivalves and gastropods living in the sediment, and diversity took many millions of years to rebuild.

### 31.4.2. The K/Pg Extinction

The extinction at the end of the Cretaceous, 66 Myr ago, is less severe but better known: it ended the non-avian dinosaurs, the ammonites, the marine reptiles and many groups of plankton, plants and mammals, an estimated 40–76 % of species. In 1980 Luis and Walter Alvarez and colleagues reported that a thin clay layer at the boundary, first studied at Gubbio in Italy, contains iridium at many times its normal concentration; iridium is rare in the Earth's crust but common in meteorites, and they proposed that the extinction was caused by the impact of an asteroid. The Chicxulub crater, about 180 km across, buried beneath the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico and of the right age, was identified in 1991. The impact probably threw up a worldwide cloud of dust and vapor that blocked sunlight, halted photosynthesis and produced a long-lasting "impact winter", followed by warming. The great eruptions of the Deccan Traps in India occurred around the same time and may have contributed. The vindication of the impact hypothesis revived the idea, abandoned since Lyell, that rare catastrophes have shaped the history of life (*neocatastrophism*); it complements rather than replaces the uniformitarian view of gradual change in normal times ([7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.2.2)).

### 31.4.3. Selectivity and Consequences

Mass extinctions were selective, but often in different ways from background extinction. In the end-Permian extinction, gastropods survived better if they had wide geographic and ecological distributions and if their genera contained many species; survival was random with respect to other features, such as mode of feeding. In late Cretaceous bivalves and gastropods during normal times, survival was greater for taxa with planktotrophic larvae (which feed in the plankton and disperse widely) and for species-rich genera, especially those with broad ranges; during the K/Pg extinction, larval type and species richness had no effect, and only broad geographic range enhanced survival. Taxa with otherwise excellent adaptations perished because they happened to lack a feature that mattered in the catastrophe, and evolutionary trends begun in normal times were cut short. The ability to drill through bivalve shells evolved in a Triassic gastropod lineage that died out in the end-Triassic extinction; the same habit evolved again, 120 Myr later, in the lineage that gave rise to the diverse oyster drills.

Mass extinctions therefore "reset the stage". Stephen Jay Gould distinguished three tiers of evolutionary change: microevolution within populations; **species selection** ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)), the differential proliferation and extinction of species in normal times; and the reshaping of the biota by mass extinctions, which start histories largely decoupled from earlier ones. Richard Bambach and colleagues classified Phanerozoic marine genera as motile or not, predatory or not, and physiologically buffered (with well-developed gills and circulation) or not; the proportions remained stable for intervals as long as 200 Myr even as total diversity and composition changed, but shifted rapidly to new stable values at the end-Ordovician, end-Permian and K/Pg extinctions. After a mass extinction, previously subordinate groups often radiate: mammals after the K/Pg event ([7.10.29](/7.10.29)). Some groups continued to dwindle long after the main event, and others diversified for millions of years: the origination rate of bivalve genera rose after the K/Pg extinction and has stayed high since, mostly in the tropics, which has shaped the present latitudinal gradient ([7.10.30](/7.10.30)).

No truly massive extinction has occurred for 66 Myr; even the climatic oscillations of the Pleistocene had a relatively small effect on global diversity, although the extinction of most large mammals and birds outside Africa at the end of the Pleistocene, in which human hunting played a major role, was severe for that group. A major extinction caused by humans has, however, almost certainly begun ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)).

## 31.5. Phylogenetic Studies of Diversification

### 31.5.1. Birth–Death Trees and Lineage-through-Time Plots

The phylogenies of living species, dated with the molecular clock ([7.10.24](/7.10.24)), record the times of the speciation events that produced the surviving lineages. In a constant-rate **birth–death process** with speciation rate $\lambda$ and extinction rate $\mu$ (written $S$ and $E$ above), the expected number of lineages grows as $\mathrm{e}^{(\lambda - \mu)t}$. The phylogeny of the survivors, the *reconstructed tree*, contains only lineages with living descendants: lineages that went extinct leave no trace on it (figure below).

**Definition 31.5.1 (Lineage-through-Time Plot)** A **lineage-through-time plot** (LTT plot) of a dated phylogeny shows the number of lineages in the reconstructed tree, usually on a logarithmic scale, against time from the root to the present.

Under a pure-birth process (the **Yule process**, $\mu = 0$, [7.10.22](/7.10.22)) the expected logarithmic LTT curve is a straight line with slope $\lambda$. With extinction it is not: in the deeper past the curve rises with slope $\lambda - \mu$, but near the present it steepens toward slope $\lambda$, because lineages that arose recently have not yet had time to go extinct (the *pull of the present*, the phylogenetic counterpart of the pull of the Recent). In principle the bend of the curve allows $\lambda$ and $\mu$ to be estimated separately from a tree of living species. In practice the estimates of $\mu$ are unreliable, and it has been shown that for any set of time-varying rates there are infinitely many other combinations of $\lambda(t)$ and $\mu(t)$ that give exactly the same probability distribution of reconstructed trees, so that time-varying speciation and extinction rates cannot be identified from extant species alone. Fossils, which do record extinct lineages, are therefore indispensable; the fossilized birth–death process combines them with molecular data ([7.10.24](/7.10.24)). The fossil record of cetaceans shows, for example, that they were much more diverse in the Miocene than today, which could not be inferred from the phylogeny of living species.

![Birth–death trees and lineage-through-time plots. A: one simulated clade with speciation rate 0.5 and extinction rate 0.25 per lineage per Myr over 12 Myr. Lineages that went extinct (crosses) are grey; the dark branches form the reconstructed tree of the living species. B: the mean logarithm of the number of lineages in the reconstructed trees of 300 clades conditioned on survival, under a pure-birth (Yule) process with rate 0.25 (straight line), a birth–death process with rates 0.5 and 0.25 (the curve steepens toward the present, the pull of the present) and diversity-dependent speciation with rate $0.5(1 - N/12)$ and extinction 0.05 (the curve levels off).](./plots/birth-death-ltt.py)

Many clades show the opposite of the pull of the present: lineages accumulated rapidly early and more slowly later. In the American wood-warblers of the genus *Setophaga*, for example, most branch points of the mitochondrial phylogeny lie near its base, and the LTT plot rises steeply and then levels off, unlike the exponential growth expected at a constant rate. Relatively few clades show an increasing rate. Simulations show that such slowdowns are more likely to be caused by a decreasing speciation rate than by an increasing extinction rate, which suggests diversity-dependent speciation (§31.6), although incomplete sampling of species and the time taken for speciation to be completed can produce similar patterns. Indeed, many incipient species probably become extinct during or soon after the long process of speciation, so speciation and extinction may be hard to distinguish even conceptually.

### 31.5.2. Ecological Opportunity and Key Innovations

Biologists have long noticed that many clades radiated where they found *ecological opportunity*, many vacant ecological niches. Islands provide many examples of **adaptive radiation** ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)). The Hawaiian honeycreepers, derived from an Asian ancestor in the finch family (Fringillidae) related to rosefinches, comprised about 60 species, of which 18 survive; they are almost the only songbirds native to the archipelago, where they faced little competition and diversified into insect eaters with thin bills, seed and fruit eaters with thick bills and nectar feeders with long, curved bills. The diversification of many clades also accelerated when they expanded into new geographic regions.

**Definition 31.5.2 (Key Innovation)** A **key innovation** (key adaptation) is an evolutionary novelty that enables a lineage to interact with its environment in a new way or to use new resources, and thereby increases its rate of diversification, by raising speciation, lowering extinction or allowing more species to coexist.

In insects, rates of origination of families rose first with the evolution of wings and later with complete metamorphosis, which characterizes the immensely diverse beetles, wasps, flies and moths. Three orders of sea urchins increased greatly in diversity from the early Mesozoic: the Echinacea, with stronger jaws that allowed a wider diet, and the heart urchins and sand dollars, with a flattened form and modified tube feet that capture fine organic particles in sand. But a single event is hard to explain: flowering plants have several distinctive features (pollination by animals, closed carpels that protect the developing seeds, more efficient water transport in the leaves), any of which might explain their diversity. Stronger evidence comes when a feature has evolved independently in several clades, and in a *replicated sister-group comparison* each clade with the feature is compared with its sister group, which lacks it. Sister groups are equally old, so a consistent difference in richness cannot be due to age (compare [7.10.30](/7.10.30#sec-30.7.1)).

**Example 31.5.1 (Herbivory and Insect Diversity)** Feeding on the green tissues of plants has evolved at least 50 times in insects, usually from predatory or detritus-feeding ancestors. Charles Mitter and colleagues identified the nonherbivorous sister groups of 13 herbivorous clades; in 11 of the 13 pairs the herbivorous clade has more species. Under the null hypothesis that herbivory has no effect, each pair is equally likely to go either way, so the probability of at least 11 of 13 in favour of herbivores is

$$
\mathbb{P}(X \ge 11) = \frac{\binom{13}{11} + \binom{13}{12} + \binom{13}{13}}{2^{13}} = \frac{78 + 13 + 1}{8192} \approx 0.011
$$
(a one-sided sign test). The weevils and leaf beetles, with about 130 000 species, contrast with their fungus- and detritus-feeding relatives, with fewer than 10 000; the moths and butterflies (about 140 000 species) with their sister group, the caddisflies (about 7000). Later analyses supported the conclusion, in part because herbivorous species diverge in diet and specialize on different plants.

Plants show the converse: clades that evolved latex (milkweeds) or resin canals (pines), defenses against herbivorous insects, are more diverse than their sister groups, and diversification rates are twice as high in plant families with extrafloral nectaries, glands that attract ants and other protective arthropods, as in families without them. Some features increase diversity simply by promoting speciation. The nectar spurs of columbines (*Aquilegia*) let different species use pollinators of different tongue lengths, which also contributes to reproductive isolation, and the evolution of spurs was followed by a burst of speciation not seen in the spurless sister group; plant clades with bilaterally symmetrical flowers have speciated faster than those with radial symmetry. Several factors may act together: across the lakes of Africa, the number of cichlid species that arose within a lake depends both on lake depth and incident energy and on features related to **sexual selection** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)), such as sexual dichromatism and the egg spots on the fins of male haplochromines.

### 31.5.3. Trait-Dependent Diversification and Tree Shape

A consistent association between a character state and the rate at which the number of species increases is **species selection** in the broad sense ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)): it makes the state more common among species, whether or not it affects any individual's fitness ([7.10.32](/7.10.32)). Model-based methods test such associations on a single large phylogeny. In the binary-state speciation and extinction (BiSSE) model, a binary character evolves along the branches by a two-state Markov chain, as in the **Mk model** ([7.10.26](/7.10.26)), and each state has its own speciation and extinction rate; the likelihood of the tree and of the tip states is computed by integrating differential equations backward along each branch, and models in which the rates depend on the state are compared by likelihood ratio tests or information criteria ([3.2](/3.2), [3.4.11](/3.4.11)). Simulations later showed that such tests often find a "significant" effect of a neutral character when the rates vary across the tree for other reasons, so models with a hidden, unobserved character that also affects the rates are now used as the null hypothesis.

The shape of a phylogeny, independent of its branch lengths, also carries information. Under a constant-rate birth–death process all lineages at any time are equally likely to split next, which gives a characteristic distribution of *tree balance*, measured for example by the Colless index (the sum, over internal nodes, of the difference in the number of tips descending from the two daughters). Real phylogenies are, on average, more unbalanced than this model predicts, as expected if rates of diversification vary among lineages and are inherited.

## 31.6. Does Diversity Reach an Equilibrium?

Ecologists agree that some factors limit the number of coexisting species: space and energy are finite and can be divided among only so many populations large enough to persist. Whether the diversity of clades has actually approached such limits is debated. Paleontological data show diversity dependence. Michael Foote correlated the rates of origination, extinction and diversification of marine invertebrate genera from one stratigraphic stage to the next with the number of genera present at the start of each stage, and found that the diversification rate, and especially the origination rate, declined as diversity increased. John Alroy found that extinction rates were higher when diversity at the start of an interval was higher, and that high extinction in one interval was followed by high origination in the next. Such results imply that diversity tends toward an equilibrium, and the slowdown of LTT plots (§31.5.1) is usually interpreted the same way: the earliest species of a new clade adapt rapidly to different resources, and fewer later species can persist because fewer opportunities remain.

Competition is generally thought to be the main brake. The fossil record contains many cases in which the decline of one group was accompanied or followed by the rise of an ecologically similar one. Two processes can produce this pattern (figure below):

- *Competitive displacement*: the later clade drives the earlier one extinct by competition. The pattern is consistent with displacement if the two lived in the same places at the same times and used the same resources, if the earlier one was not decimated by a mass extinction, and if the later one increased as the earlier declined. By these criteria rodents seem to have displaced the multituberculates, a long-lived group of nonplacental mammals with rodent-like teeth, in North America; flowering plants increased in diversity and abundance at the expense of other vascular plants in the Cretaceous.
- *Incumbent replacement*: an established, incumbent clade prevents a similar clade from diversifying simply by occupying the resources, and only its extinction lets the second clade radiate. Replacement of the stem-group turtles that could not retract their heads (amphichelydians) by the two modern groups that fold the neck within or beside the shell happened four or five times in different regions, especially after the K/Pg extinction; the repetition makes release from incumbency likely. Incumbent replacement has probably been more common than competitive displacement.

Sepkoski and colleagues modeled two clades whose growth is inhibited by their own diversity and by that of the other:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N_1}{\mathrm{d}t} = r_1N_1\left(1 - \frac{N_1 + \alpha_{12}N_2}{K_1}\right), \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}N_2}{\mathrm{d}t} = r_2N_2\left(1 - \frac{N_2 + \alpha_{21}N_1}{K_2}\right),
$$
the Lotka–Volterra competition equations applied to numbers of genera, with $r_i$ the initial rates of diversification, $K_i$ the equilibrium diversities and $\alpha_{ij}$ the effect of clade $j$ on clade $i$. Applied to cyclostome and cheilostome bryozoans, sessile colonial animals in which cheilostomes generally overgrow cyclostomes where colonies meet, the model with cheilostomes as superior competitors and a perturbation reducing both clades at the end of the Cretaceous reproduced the observed history, in which cheilostomes have increased since the K/Pg extinction and cyclostomes have not recovered. The fit does not prove that competition determined the history, but it is consistent with it.

Yet diversity has continued to increase in the long run, even though the rate of increase slows. Throughout the history of life, innovations have let clades break into new ways of using resources, and diversity itself creates opportunity: a whole family of fishes (pearlfishes, Carapidae) lives inside sea squirts and sea cucumbers, and the long tubular flowers that many tropical plants evolved for hummingbird pollination are a resource for flowerpiercers (*Diglossa*), which rob the nectar by biting through the base of the flower. On a global scale, diversification was increased by the fragmentation of Pangaea into separate land masses and by the steeper temperature gradient between low and high latitudes that developed during the Cenozoic. Probably both sides of the debate are partly right: most clades, viewed individually, seem to approach a limit, but new clades with new ways of life keep arising and raising the ceiling.

## Summary

- The number of taxa changes at the net **diversification rate** $D = S - E$ (Definition 31.1.1, Eq. (31.1)); with diversity dependence it approaches an equilibrium. Clades and regions differ in richness because of age, diversification rate or equilibrium.
- Fossil counts must be corrected for sampling, interval length, range truncation and singletons; the **pull of the Recent** (Definition 31.1.2) inflates diversity toward the present.
- Marine diversity rose rapidly in the Cambrian–Ordovician, changed little (or fell) through the rest of the Paleozoic and increased, at varying rates, through the Mesozoic and Cenozoic; three evolutionary faunas succeeded one another. Land is now more diverse than the sea.
- **Background extinction** (Definition 31.3.1) and origination rates have declined through the Phanerozoic; volatile clades turn over fast and disappear early. Survivorship curves are often linear (constant extinction, Van Valen), which led to the Red Queen hypothesis.
- Five **mass extinctions** (Definition 31.4.1) dominate the record: end-Ordovician, Late Devonian, end-Permian (the largest; Siberian Traps, warming, anoxia), end-Triassic and K/Pg (asteroid impact). Their selectivity differed from that of background extinction, and they reset the course of evolution.
- In birth–death models the **lineage-through-time plot** (Definition 31.5.1) of a reconstructed tree is linear under pure birth and steepens near the present with extinction; many clades show slowdowns. Extinction rates cannot be reliably estimated from living species alone.
- **Key innovations** (Definition 31.5.2) and ecological opportunity raise diversification, as shown by replicated sister-group comparisons (Example 31.5.1) and trait-dependent models such as BiSSE; real trees are more unbalanced than constant-rate models predict.
- Diversity is diversity-dependent within clades; replacement of one clade by another occurs by competitive displacement or, more often, incumbent replacement after extinction. Overall diversity keeps rising as new ways of life evolve.

## Questions

1. Distinguish the speciation rate of a clade from its diversification rate. A clade has 1000 living species and is 20 Myr old. Assuming constant rates and one ancestral species, what net diversification rate does this imply? Could its speciation rate be much higher?

   **Answer** The speciation rate $S$ is the per-species rate at which new species arise; the diversification rate $D = S - E$ subtracts extinction. Ignoring stochasticity, $N = \mathrm{e}^{Dt}$ gives $D = \ln 1000/20 \approx 0.35$ per Myr. This is only the net rate: the same number of species could result from $S = 0.35$, $E = 0$, or from $S = 1.0$, $E = 0.65$, with much higher turnover. Distinguishing these requires fossils or, less reliably, the shape of the reconstructed tree (the pull of the present). (A more careful estimate conditions on survival of the clade and uses the crown age, but the point is the same.)

2. Explain why a compilation of fossil families that counts every living family as present since its first fossil appearance would show an increase of diversity toward the present even if diversity had been constant. How can the bias be reduced?

   **Answer** Fossil taxa are known only between their first and last fossil occurrences, and their true ranges extend beyond these; living taxa, in contrast, are known to exist now and are counted in every interval from their first fossil to the present, including intervals without fossils. Young intervals contain proportionally more taxa that are still alive, so they gain more from this extension; diversity therefore appears to rise toward the present (the pull of the Recent). The bias is reduced by counting living taxa only in intervals in which they have fossil occurrences, by sampling-standardized counts, and by counting only taxa that cross stage boundaries.

3. In a constant-rate birth–death process, why does the lineage-through-time plot of the reconstructed tree steepen toward the present? What would the LTT plot look like if speciation slowed as diversity increased?

   **Answer** A lineage appears in the reconstructed tree only if at least one of its descendants survives to the present. A lineage that arose long ago has had a long time in which all its descendants could die, so many old lineages are missing; a lineage that arose very recently has almost certainly not yet gone extinct. Near the present, the reconstructed tree therefore contains nearly all lineages, and the number grows at nearly the full speciation rate $\lambda$; in the deep past it grows at the net rate $\lambda - \mu$. With diversity-dependent speciation the rate of branching falls as the number of lineages grows, so the logarithmic LTT curve rises steeply at first and then flattens, as in *Setophaga*.

4. In a replicated sister-group comparison, 9 of 12 clades that evolved a particular trait are more species-rich than their sister groups. Is this significant at the 5 % level (one-sided)? Why are sister groups used rather than arbitrary clades without the trait?

   **Answer** Under the null hypothesis each comparison is a fair coin: $\mathbb{P}(X \ge 9) = [\binom{12}{9} + \binom{12}{10} + \binom{12}{11} + \binom{12}{12}]/2^{12} = (220 + 66 + 12 + 1)/4096 \approx 0.073$, not significant at 5 %. Sister groups are equally old, so a difference in richness cannot be due to one clade having had more time to diversify; they also share most other inherited features, and the comparisons are phylogenetically independent of each other, which a regression across arbitrary clades would not be.

5. Why were the characteristics that favored survival during the K/Pg extinction different from those that favored survival in normal times? What does this imply for adaptation and long-term trends?

   **Answer** Background extinction results from ordinary, local and gradual environmental changes, for which features such as species-rich genera or planktotrophic larvae (wide dispersal) reduce risk; during the K/Pg crisis the cause was global and sudden (an impact, collapse of photosynthesis), and only very broad geographic range helped, because some populations were likely to be in a less affected place. Survival then depended on features unrelated to, and sometimes opposite to, those favored by selection in normal times. Adaptations to normal environments therefore offer no insurance against rare catastrophes, and trends built up over millions of years can be cut off by chance, so the long-term history of clades is contingent ([7.10.32](/7.10.32)).
