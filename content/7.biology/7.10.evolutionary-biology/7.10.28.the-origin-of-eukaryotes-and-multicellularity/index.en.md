Eukaryotic cells differ from those of bacteria and archaea by a nucleus enclosed in a double membrane with pores, an endomembrane system, a cytoskeleton of actin and microtubules with motor proteins, mitosis and meiosis, linear chromosomes with telomeres, spliceosomal introns, and, in almost all lineages, mitochondria. No living intermediates between prokaryotes and eukaryotes exist, so their origin, eukaryogenesis, is reconstructed from phylogenies of genes, from the comparison of living eukaryotes and from a sparse fossil record. It was the second of the major evolutionary transitions (**major evolutionary transitions**, [Definition 16.6.1](/7.10.16#def-16.6.1)), the merger of an archaeal and a bacterial cell into a new kind of individual, and it made possible a third: the repeated evolution of multicellular organisms with differentiated cells.

The cell biology of this story is in [7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.6) (the time scale, the Asgard archaea and the models of eukaryogenesis), [7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.8) (the evidence for endosymbiosis, gene transfer to the nucleus, why organelles keep genomes, primary, secondary and tertiary plastids) and [7.3.16](/7.3.16#sec-16.7.2) (the origin of the nucleus). This chapter concentrates on the phylogenetic evidence, the evolutionary processes and the diversification of eukaryotes, and on the origins of multicellularity. It continues [7.10.27](/7.10.27), where the two-domain tree of life was introduced, and leads to the fossil history of multicellular life in [7.10.29](/7.10.29).

## 28.1. The Eukaryotic Cell and Its Ancestor

Comparative genomics reconstructs the **last eukaryotic common ancestor** (LECA, [Definition 1.6.1](/7.3.1#def-1.6.1)) as a complex cell. It had a nucleus with nuclear pores and nucleocytoplasmic transport, an endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus with vesicle traffic, an actin and tubulin cytoskeleton with myosin, kinesin and dynein motors, cilia, mitochondria, mitosis, meiosis and sex, and intron-rich genes spliced by a spliceosome. Many of its gene families had already been expanded by duplication. Everything that distinguishes eukaryotic cells from prokaryotic ones therefore evolved on the stem lineage between the separation from the Asgard archaea and LECA, which may have lasted for hundreds of millions of years, and none of the intermediate stages survives.

The fossil record of this period is meager. The oldest fossils accepted as eukaryotic are organic-walled microfossils (acritarchs) with ornamented or branched walls about 1.6–1.7 billion years old, which required a cytoskeleton and endomembranes to build; older candidates are disputed. Steranes, molecular fossils of sterols that are characteristic of eukaryotes, support the eukaryotic nature of some Proterozoic assemblages, although their oldest reported occurrences have been shown to be contamination. By 1.0–1.3 Ga there are fossils that can be assigned to living groups: the multicellular red alga *Bangiomorpha pubescens*, about 1.05 Ga old, with differentiated holdfast cells and apparently sexual reproduction, and green algae somewhat later. Molecular clocks give dates for LECA from about 2 to about 1.2 Ga, depending on the calibrations used. For more than a billion years eukaryotes remained mostly unicellular and inconspicuous in a world still dominated by prokaryotes.

## 28.2. Endosymbiosis: The Phylogenetic Evidence

### 28.2.1. Mitochondria

The **endosymbiotic theory** ([7.1.1](/7.1.1)) is supported by several kinds of evidence ([7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.8.1)), but the decisive one is phylogenetic: trees of the genes in mitochondrial genomes, and of nuclear genes of mitochondrial origin such as the chaperonin cpn60, place all mitochondria together within the Alphaproteobacteria. Mitochondria therefore arose once, from a single endosymbiotic lineage. Early analyses grouped them with the Rickettsiales, intracellular parasites with reduced genomes, which suggested a parasitic ancestor; analyses of complete genomes with models that correct for the compositional biases of reduced genomes have not confirmed this, and the exact position of mitochondria within, or as sister to, the Alphaproteobacteria remains unresolved.

The mitochondrial genome that most resembles a bacterial genome is that of the free-living protist *Reclinomonas americana*, with 97 genes, including genes for a bacterial-type RNA polymerase and an operon-like order of ribosomal protein genes; the order of ribosomal protein genes is conserved in the mitochondrial genomes of protists, plants and *Reclinomonas* in the same arrangement as in *E. coli* and the **rickettsias**, with some genes lost from the series but the order of the rest retained. At the other extreme, the mitochondria of animals keep 37 genes, those of *Plasmodium* 3 protein genes.

Some anaerobic eukaryotes were once thought to have diverged before the mitochondrion was acquired, because rRNA trees placed *Giardia*, *Trichomonas* and the microsporidia near the base of the eukaryotic tree. This "Archezoa" hypothesis failed. The microsporidia proved to be relatives of fungi, placed at the base by **long-branch attraction** ([7.8.6](/7.8.6)), and all three groups were found to have organelles derived from mitochondria, without genomes: the hydrogenosomes of *Trichomonas*, which produce ATP and hydrogen, and the mitosomes of *Giardia*, *Entamoeba* and microsporidia, which assemble iron–sulfur clusters. Their nuclear genomes contain genes of mitochondrial origin (for chaperonins and iron–sulfur proteins) whose products are targeted to these organelles. Mitochondria were thus present in LECA, and their loss is secondary: in the one known eukaryote without any mitochondrial organelle, the oxymonad *Monocercomonoides*, it happened recently ([7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.6.2)).

### 28.2.2. Primary Plastids

**Definition 28.2.1 (Primary Endosymbiosis)** A **primary endosymbiosis** is the acquisition of an organelle by a eukaryotic cell through the incorporation and reduction of a free-living prokaryote, as of the cyanobacterium that became the plastid of the Archaeplastida.

The plastids of land plants, green algae, red algae and glaucophytes (together the Archaeplastida) form a single clade in phylogenies of plastid genes, nested within the Cyanobacteria near the freshwater genus *Gloeomargarita*; nuclear genes of plastid origin and the shared protein import machinery of their envelopes (the TOC and TIC complexes) point to the same single origin. The plastids have two envelope membranes, the inner and outer membranes of the cyanobacterium, and the glaucophytes keep a remnant of the cyanobacterial peptidoglycan wall between them. The event is older than the oldest red algal fossils, at least about 1.05 Ga and perhaps much more. A second, independent primary endosymbiosis occurred much later in the amoeba *Paulinella*, about 100 million years ago, and the nitrogen-fixing "nitroplast" of a marine alga is a recent third case at an early stage ([7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.8.4)).

### 28.2.3. Secondary and Tertiary Endosymbiosis

**Definition 28.2.2 (Secondary Endosymbiosis)** A **secondary endosymbiosis** is the acquisition of a plastid by a eukaryote through the incorporation and reduction of a eukaryotic alga that already had a primary plastid; a *tertiary endosymbiosis* is the incorporation of an alga with a secondary plastid.

Three lines of evidence reveal secondary plastids. They are surrounded by three or four membranes instead of two: from inside out, the two cyanobacterial membranes, the plasma membrane of the engulfed alga (lost in euglenids and dinoflagellates) and the membrane of the host's food vacuole. In cryptophytes and chlorarachniophytes, a tiny remnant of the alga's nucleus, the *nucleomorph*, survives between the second and third membranes, with a degenerate genome of a few hundred kilobases. And the nuclear genome of the host and the genomes of the plastid and nucleomorph have different phylogenetic affinities: the nucleomorph and plastid of cryptophytes group with red algae, the host nucleus elsewhere. Cryptophytes and chlorarachniophytes thus keep four genomes in one cell (host nucleus and mitochondrion; symbiont nucleomorph and plastid, the symbiont's mitochondrion having been lost); the apicomplexans, such as *Plasmodium* and *Toxoplasma*, have kept only a nonphotosynthetic plastid, the apicoplast, which makes fatty acids and isoprenoids and is a target of antimalarial drugs.

Secondary endosymbiosis happened at least three times: twice with green algae (in euglenids and in chlorarachniophytes, independently) and at least once with a red alga, whose plastid is now found in cryptophytes, haptophytes, stramenopiles (diatoms, brown algae) and alveolates (dinoflagellates, apicomplexans). Whether the red plastid was acquired once by a common ancestor of these groups (the former "chromalveolate" hypothesis) or spread among them by a series of secondary and tertiary endosymbioses is unsettled; phylogenies of the host lineages, which do not form a clade, favor serial transfers. Dinoflagellates have repeatedly replaced their plastids with others from diatoms, haptophytes or green algae. Because all photosynthesis in eukaryotes descends from the cyanobacterial endosymbiont, these events spread photosynthesis across most of the eukaryotic tree.

### 28.2.4. Endosymbiotic Gene Transfer

**Definition 28.2.3 (Endosymbiotic Gene Transfer)** **Endosymbiotic gene transfer** (EGT) is the movement of genes from the genome of an endosymbiont or organelle to the nuclear genome of its host, where they are expressed and, if their products act in the organelle, acquire signals for targeting the proteins back to it.

The free-living ancestors of mitochondria and plastids had thousands of genes; their organelles keep tens to a few hundred ([7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.8.2)). Most of the genes were lost, but many were transferred: about 18 % of the nuclear protein-coding genes of *Arabidopsis thaliana* appear to be of cyanobacterial origin, and their products include many proteins that now work outside the plastid. Transfer happens continually: fragments of organelle DNA, released when organelles break down, are inserted into nuclear chromosomes at double-strand breaks during repair, in some cases at rates comparable to point mutation, so that nuclear genomes contain many fragments of mitochondrial and plastid DNA (NUMTs and NUPTs). Most insertions are nonfunctional. Rarely, a transferred gene acquires a promoter and a targeting sequence; its product is then imported, and the organelle copy becomes redundant and is lost. Because DNA moves almost exclusively from organelles to the nucleus and not back, the process acts as a ratchet that reduces organelle genomes over time.

Two kinds of explanation are offered for why genes move to the nucleus. Transfer may be largely neutral, the product of a one-way flow; or it may be favored, because organelle genomes are clonal, inherited from one parent and without recombination, and therefore suffer the accumulation of deleterious mutations (**Muller's ratchet**, [7.10.14](/7.10.14)) and the low efficiency of selection of a small nonrecombining population, while nuclear genes are exposed to recombination and selection in sexual populations. Why organelles keep any genes at all is discussed in [7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.8.3).

## 28.3. How Did the Eukaryotic Cell Arise?

### 28.3.1. Host and Symbiont

Eukaryotic genomes are chimeric: genes for information processing (replication, transcription, translation) are mostly archaeal, while many genes for metabolism and for membrane lipids are bacterial. Since the discovery of the Asgard archaea ([7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.4)), the host is known to have been an archaeon related to them, and the mitochondrial symbiont an alphaproteobacterium. What is disputed is the order of events and the nature of the partnership.

- *Phagocytosis first (mitochondria late).* The host was already a complex cell, with a cytoskeleton, endomembranes and perhaps a nucleus, which fed by engulfing bacteria; one of them escaped digestion and became the mitochondrion. This view, which goes back to the "serial endosymbiosis" of Lynn Margulis and was elaborated by Christian de Duve and Thomas Cavalier-Smith among others, explains how the symbiont got inside, but requires that the eukaryotic features evolved without the energy supply of mitochondria.
- *Symbiosis first (mitochondria early).* The partnership was metabolic and began between prokaryotes. In the hydrogen hypothesis (William Martin and Miklós Müller, 1998), the host was an anaerobic, hydrogen-dependent archaeon and the symbiont a facultatively anaerobic bacterium that released hydrogen as a waste product; dependence on that hydrogen drew host and symbiont together. Syntrophic models based on the Asgard archaea propose exchanges of hydrogen or other metabolites with bacteria attached to the host, whose membrane protrusions eventually enclosed the partner ([7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.6.2)). Nick Lane and William Martin (2010) argued that the energy per gene made available by many mitochondria, each with its own small genome controlling its respiratory chain, was what allowed the expansion of genome size and cellular complexity, which would make mitochondria a precondition rather than a consequence of eukaryotic complexity.

The Asgard archaea, small cells with long membrane protrusions and an actin-based cytoskeleton, living in syntrophic partnerships, fit the second class better, and phylogenomic dating suggests that many eukaryotic features arose after, not before, the mitochondrial genes entered; but the question is open, and intermediate scenarios (a host with simple dynamic membranes but without true phagocytosis) are also considered. Older gradualist (autogenous) scenarios, in which all eukaryotic compartments formed by infolding of the plasma membrane of a single lineage, cannot explain the bacterial genomes of mitochondria and plastids and are now only of historical interest.

### 28.3.2. The Nucleus and Introns

The origin of the nucleus is still unclear. In *karyogenic* (autogenous) models the nuclear envelope formed from internal membranes of the host, consistent with the continuity of the nuclear envelope with the ER and with the shared architecture of nuclear-pore proteins and vesicle coats ([7.3.16](/7.3.16#sec-16.7.2)). In *endokaryotic* models the nucleus descends from an endosymbiont, an archaeon inside a bacterial host or a virus; these models explain the archaeal character of nuclear functions but not the structure of the envelope and are not supported by phylogenies. One hypothesis links the nucleus to introns (William Martin and Eugene Koonin, 2006). Group II introns from the mitochondrial symbiont invaded the host genome and spread, the ancestors of spliceosomal introns ([7.2.16](/7.2.16#sec-16.8.2), [7.10.18](/7.10.18#sec-18.6)); because splicing is slow and translation fast, ribosomes would translate unspliced transcripts into faulty proteins, and a membrane that separated transcription and splicing from translation would have been favored. The hypothesis is consistent with the many introns of LECA, but it has not been tested directly.

## 28.4. The Diversification of Eukaryotes

Most eukaryotic diversity is unicellular: amoebae, flagellates, ciliates, diatoms, foraminifera and countless others, most of them traditionally lumped as "protists" or as "algae" and "protozoa", groups defined by grade rather than by descent. Classifications based on morphology could not resolve the deep branches. Phylogenies of rRNA brought the first molecular trees, which were distorted by long-branch attraction; phylogenomic analyses of hundreds of genes have since grouped most eukaryotes into a few large clades, the supergroups, while showing that some earlier supergroups were not monophyletic. The eight supergroups of Barton and colleagues (2007), for example, included "excavates" and "discicristates", which are no longer recognized in that form, and separate "heterokonts", "alveolates" and "rhizaria", which are now united in one clade (SAR).

Archaeplastida, SAR, Haptista and Cryptista together form a larger clade (Diaphoretickes), and the former "Excavata", grouped by a ventral feeding groove, has been split into Discoba, Metamonada and malawimonads. Where the root of the eukaryotic tree lies is unresolved: proposals place it between Amorphea and all other eukaryotes, or among the lineages once called excavates. The root matters for the inference of LECA's features, but because many of them are shared by the deepest lineages on any proposed rooting, the conclusion that LECA was a complex cell does not depend on it.

## 28.5. The Origins of Multicellularity

### 28.5.1. Many Independent Origins

**Definition 28.5.1 (Multicellularity)** **Multicellularity** is the condition of organisms that consist of many cells that adhere to each other, communicate and depend on each other for survival and reproduction; in *complex multicellularity* the cells are differentiated into several types in a reproducible spatial pattern, and only some of them reproduce the organism.

Multicellularity is a continuum, from colonies of identical cells to bodies with hundreds of cell types. Simple multicellularity has evolved at least 25 times, in bacteria (filamentous cyanobacteria, the fruiting bodies of myxobacteria) and in many eukaryotic lineages; complex multicellularity evolved independently in only a handful: animals, land plants (and their charophyte relatives), red algae, brown algae, and fungi (at least twice, in ascomycetes and basidiomycetes). The phylogeny of eukaryotes shows that each of these lineages is nested among unicellular relatives, and the closest unicellular relatives of animals are the choanoflagellates, whose cells resemble the feeding cells (choanocytes) of sponges. Choanoflagellates, some of which form colonies in response to bacterial signals, encode proteins once thought specific to animals, such as cadherins and receptor tyrosine kinases. Proteins that are central to multicellularity therefore evolved earlier, for other functions in unicellular ancestors, and were co-opted; the first steps in the origin of animals and plants seem to have been the evolution of new adhesion molecules, followed by new signaling molecules, transcription factors and connections between cells.

Two routes lead to multicellularity:

- *Clonal* multicellularity: cells remain attached after division, forming filaments, sheets or spheres. All the cells of an individual are descendants of one cell and genetically nearly identical. All complex multicellular lineages are clonal.
- *Aggregative* multicellularity: free-living cells come together, usually in response to starvation. In the slime mold *Dictyostelium discoideum*, amoebae aggregate along waves of cyclic AMP into a migrating slug of many thousands of cells; the front cells form a stalk and die, the rest become spores. Myxobacteria form fruiting bodies in a similar way. Because aggregates can contain cells of different genotypes, they are open to cheaters that contribute to spores rather than stalk, a reason why aggregative multicellularity has remained simple ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)).

Why become multicellular? Even undifferentiated clusters can escape predators that engulf single cells, sink or attach differently, and share secreted products. In experiments, simple multicellularity evolves quickly: in yeast selected for rapid settling, "snowflake" clusters of cells that remain attached after division arose within a few hundred generations, and later evolved a division of labor in which some cells die and break the cluster into propagules; in the alga *Chlamydomonas*, predation by a filter-feeding ciliate selected for multicellular forms. The advantage of complex multicellularity is above all division of labor.

### 28.5.2. The Volvocine Algae and the Origin of a Germ Line

The green algae of the order Volvocales form a series from unicells to organisms with differentiated cells, which has served as a model of how multicellularity evolves.

| Genus | Organization | Cells | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Chlamydomonas* | unicell with two flagella | 1 | none |
| *Gonium* | flat plate | 4–16 | none |
| *Pandorina* | sphere | 16 | none |
| *Pleodorina* (e.g., *P. californica*) | sphere | 64 or 128 | a fraction of small somatic cells at the anterior; the rest reproductive |
| *Volvox carteri* | sphere | about 2000 somatic cells, 16 gonidia | complete separation: flagellated somatic cells, nonflagellated reproductive gonidia set aside early in development |

In *Volvox carteri*, the somatic cells cannot divide and die after the juveniles are released; the gonidia make the next generation. The series is not a ladder of ancestors and descendants: phylogenies show that the members evolved within a single clade, that larger colonies and somatic cells arose more than once, and that some lineages reverted to smaller sizes. It shows instead how easily the transitions can occur.

Why specialize? A flagellated cell of a volvocine alga cannot divide while it is swimming, because the basal bodies of its flagella are needed as centrioles for mitosis; a cell must therefore trade motility, which keeps the colony in the light, against reproduction. A simple model in the spirit of those of Richard Michod and colleagues shows when the trade-off favors specialization.

**Formula 28.5.1 (Germ–Soma Specialization)** Let each of $N$ cells allocate a fraction $e_i \in [0, 1]$ of its resources to reproduction, gaining fecundity $b_i = e_i$ and viability $v_i = (1 - e_i)^{\alpha}$ for its contribution to the survival of the group, and let the fitness of the group be the product of its mean viability and mean fecundity,

$$
W = \Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i v_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i b_i\Bigr).
$$
If the trade-off is convex ($\alpha > 1$, so that cells lose viability faster than proportionally as they invest in reproduction), a group of specialized cells, a fraction $1 - e^*$ of the cells pure somatic ($e_i = 0$) and the rest pure reproductive ($e_i = 1$), has higher fitness than any group of identical generalist cells; if $\alpha < 1$, identical generalists do best.

**Proof** For identical cells with $e_i = e$, $W(e) = e(1 - e)^{\alpha}$, which is maximized at $e = 1/(1 + \alpha)$ with $W_{\mathrm{gen}} = \alpha^{\alpha}/(1 + \alpha)^{1 + \alpha}$. For a group in which a fraction $f$ of cells is fully reproductive and $1 - f$ fully somatic, mean fecundity is $f$ and mean viability $1 - f$, so $W = f(1 - f)$, maximized at $f = \tfrac12$ with $W_{\mathrm{spec}} = \tfrac14$. Now $W_{\mathrm{gen}} < \tfrac14$ for $\alpha > 1$ and $W_{\mathrm{gen}} > \tfrac14$ for $\alpha < 1$ (at $\alpha = 1$ both are $\tfrac14$). More generally, if $v$ is a convex function of $e$, mean viability for a given mean investment is maximized by putting the cells at the extremes, by Jensen's inequality; if $v$ is concave, by making them equal. $\square$

The model suggests that a convex trade-off, which is more likely in larger groups where somatic cells contribute to motility more efficiently, favored the separation of germ and soma. In *Volvox carteri* the switch is controlled by a single gene, *regA*, which encodes a transcriptional repressor expressed in somatic cells, where it represses genes for chloroplast biogenesis and so prevents growth and division; *regA* mutants have somatic cells that grow and become reproductive. *regA* belongs to a gene family present in the unicellular *Chlamydomonas*, where a related gene is expressed in response to environmental conditions: an environmentally induced switch between survival and reproduction in a unicell was apparently co-opted into a developmental program that divides labor between cells.

B: fitness of a group of identical generalist cells, maximized over e (solid), and of a group of fully specialized somatic and reproductive cells (dashed, W = 1/4), against the curvature α: specialization is favored when α > 1. ### 28.5.3. From Multicellularity to Body Plans

Multicellularity raised new problems that had to be solved for complex bodies to evolve: cells had to adhere in specific patterns, signal to each other, take on stable differentiated states and keep them through cell division, and cells that cheat by proliferating at the expense of the organism had to be suppressed ([7.10.16](/7.10.16#sec-16.6)). In animals, the solutions include a conserved set of adhesion proteins (cadherins, integrins), signaling pathways (Wnt, Hedgehog, TGF-β, Notch, receptor tyrosine kinases), transcription factor families such as the Hox genes, and an early-segregated germ line; most of them were present in the common ancestor of animals and were deployed in different ways in different lineages ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)). Cancer is a breakdown of this cooperation, the evolution of cell lineages that escape the controls of the organism ([7.3.26](/7.3.26)). The independent origins of complex multicellularity in animals, plants and fungi used different molecules for the same tasks, plants for example cell walls and plasmodesmata instead of junctions and migration. The first large multicellular organisms appear in the fossil record of the late Proterozoic, the Ediacaran biota, followed by the radiation of animals in the Cambrian ([7.10.29](/7.10.29)); the body plans of animals and their genetic basis are the subject of developmental biology ([7.5](/7.5)) and of the animal phyla in zoology.

## Questions

1. Why was the "Archezoa" hypothesis, that *Giardia*, *Trichomonas* and microsporidia diverged before the acquisition of mitochondria, abandoned?

   **Answer** Two kinds of evidence refuted it. Their deep position in rRNA trees was an artifact of long-branch attraction: with more genes and better models the microsporidia group with fungi and the others with other eukaryotic lineages, not at the base. And all of them have organelles derived from mitochondria (hydrogenosomes, mitosomes) and nuclear genes of mitochondrial origin whose products are imported into these organelles, so their ancestors had mitochondria.

2. A plastid is surrounded by four membranes, and the cell contains a small extra nucleus between the second and third. What can you infer about its origin, and how would you test the inference?

   **Answer** It is probably a secondary plastid: the inner two membranes come from the cyanobacterium, the third from the plasma membrane of an engulfed eukaryotic alga, the fourth from the host's food vacuole, and the small nucleus is a nucleomorph, the remnant of the alga's nucleus. The test is phylogenetic: genes of the nucleomorph and the plastid should group with a lineage of primary algae (red or green), while the host's nuclear genes group with a different eukaryotic lineage.

3. Why does endosymbiotic gene transfer reduce organelle genomes even if each transfer is selectively neutral?

   **Answer** Because DNA flows almost only from organelles to the nucleus. Once a functional copy exists in the nucleus with a targeting sequence, the organelle copy is redundant and can be lost without cost; the reverse movement, which would restore genes to the organelle, essentially never happens. A one-way process with absorbing loss reduces the organelle gene content over time, down to genes that cannot be expressed or imported from the nucleus.

4. In Formula 28.5.1 with $\alpha = 2$, compute the maximal fitness of a group of identical generalists and compare it with a fully specialized group.

   **Answer** For identical cells, $W(e) = e(1 - e)^2$ is maximized at $e = 1/3$, with $W = \frac13\cdot\frac49 = \frac{4}{27} \approx 0.148$. A specialized group with half somatic and half reproductive cells has $W = \frac14 = 0.25$, so specialization increases fitness by about 70 %.

5. Why might complex multicellularity have evolved only in clonal and never in aggregative lineages?

   **Answer** In clonal development all cells descend from one cell and are genetically nearly identical, so a cell that gives up reproduction (soma) still transmits its genes through the germ cells, and selection at the level of the organism dominates. In aggregates, cells of different genotypes come together, and a genotype that contributes more than its share to the reproductive cells (spores) and less to the somatic ones (stalk) is favored within the aggregate; such cheating limits the degree of differentiation and cooperation that can evolve. A single-cell bottleneck also makes the organism's fitness more heritable.
