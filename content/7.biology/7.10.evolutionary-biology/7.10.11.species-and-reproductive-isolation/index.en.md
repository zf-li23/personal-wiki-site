The branches of the tree of life ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)) are separated by speciation, the process by which one species gives rise to two. Darwin first came to believe in evolution when he realized that different islands of the Galápagos harbour different but similar forms of mockingbirds, most plausibly descended with modification from a common ancestor. This chapter and the next treat speciation. Here we ask what species are, by what biological barriers they are kept apart, how fast those barriers evolve, and what their genetic basis is; [7.10.12](/7.10.12) asks how the barriers arise: by which evolutionary forces, in which geographic settings, and with what signature in the genome. The material builds on population genetics ([7.4.23](/7.4.23)), on the interaction of gene flow and selection ([7.10.10](/7.10.10)), on chromosomal rearrangements ([7.4.15](/7.4.15)) and on the sex chromosomes ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)). The statistical delimitation of species from DNA sequences is treated in [7.10.25](/7.10.25).

## 11.1. What Are Species?

A definition is a convention, not a hypothesis, and probably no single definition of "species" (Latin for "kind") serves every context in which biologists use the word. For Linnaeus and other early taxonomists, species were simply groups of organisms that could be distinguished. That criterion fails in both directions. The eastern screech owl (*Megascops asio*) has a grey and a rufous form that look very different, yet they sound the same, interbreed, and may occur in one brood: the difference is a one-locus polymorphism, with rufous dominant. The grey form, conversely, is almost indistinguishable from the western screech owl (*M. kennicottii*), which has a very different voice; the two coexist in Texas but can be completely distinguished by their mitochondrial DNA, evidence that there is little or no gene flow between them. They are separate gene pools.

### 11.1.1. The Biological Species Concept

**Definition 11.1.1 (Biological Species Concept)** Under the **biological species concept** (BSC; Mayr 1942), **species** are groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups. **Reproductive isolation** means that genetically based biological differences between the groups greatly reduce gene exchange between them, even where they are not geographically separated.

Several points follow. The definition does not count geographic separation: populations separated by a river or a mountain range are not for that reason different species, because such barriers are temporary, whereas reproductive isolation caused by genetic differences is usually permanent. It refers to natural conditions: lions and tigers hybridize in zoos (the hybrid is a liger), but were never known to do so where their ranges overlapped in India. It does not require complete isolation: a little genetic leakage through hybridization is allowed. And hybrid sterility or inviability is only one of many possible barriers; a species need not be unable to form fertile hybrids. Genetic and phenotypic differences do not define biological species, but they are how we recognize them, because they mark the reduced gene flow. The fundamental idea is that a biological species is one gene pool: a favourable allele arising in one individual can in principle spread through the whole species, whereas genetic variation in one species cannot contribute to evolution in another.

The BSC accommodates variation within species (the colour forms of the screech owl, the sexes of species in which males and females look very different, larval and adult stages) because each line of descent passes through all these forms. It also recognizes *sibling species*, which are almost identical in appearance and are often discovered through differences in behaviour, ecology, chromosomes or genetic markers; the European mosquito *Anopheles maculipennis* turned out to be a cluster of nine sibling species, of which only some transmit human malaria. Sibling species should not be confused with sister species, which are each other's closest relatives (a **sister group** of two species, [7.8.6](/7.8.6)).

**Definition 11.1.2 (Cryptic Species)** **Cryptic species** are distinct species that are not, or hardly, distinguishable by morphology and have been classified as one species. Sibling species are cryptic species that are also each other's closest relatives.

The BSC is the most widely used definition and applies to most sexually reproducing organisms. Its limitations are the following.

- *Allopatric populations.* Whether geographically separated populations would interbreed if they met is often a judgement call. The pygmy nuthatch (*Sitta pygmaea*) of western North America and the brown-headed nuthatch (*S. pusilla*) of the southeastern United States are separated by hundreds of kilometres in which neither occurs; they differ in voice and slightly in colour pattern, and it is hard to tell whether they are different biological species. In practice, allopatric populations are often ranked as species if they differ as much as sympatric species of the same group do.
- *Gradual evolution of isolation.* Reproductive isolation evolves gradually (§11.3), so the distinction between interbreeding and isolated populations is not all-or-none. Nevertheless, countless closely related forms live in the same area and remain distinct.
- *Asexual organisms.* The BSC does not apply to organisms that do not reproduce sexually. Bacteria and archaea exchange genes sporadically, sometimes with distantly related lineages; most homologous recombination occurs within traditionally recognized species. Prokaryotic species were traditionally distinguished by metabolic capabilities and are now delimited by genomic similarity (a whole-genome average nucleotide identity of about $95\,\%$ is the usual working boundary; [7.10.27](/7.10.27)).
- *Fossils.* Paleontologists cannot observe the mating of extinct ammonites or dinosaurs and must classify by morphology; along a single lineage, the boundaries between successive species are arbitrary.

### 11.1.2. Other Species Concepts

**Definition 11.1.3 (Phylogenetic Species Concept)** Under the **phylogenetic species concept** (PSC), a species is an irreducible (basal) cluster of organisms, diagnosably distinct from other such clusters, within which there is a parental pattern of ancestry and descent (Cracraft 1983). In a related, genealogical version, two groups are distinct species if the gene trees of all or most loci show them as reciprocally monophyletic.

The PSC emphasizes species as products of a history of divergence and can be applied to allopatric populations, such as those on different islands, where reproductive isolation cannot be assessed; it is used by some systematists for classification. Most evolutionary biologists use a variant of the BSC, because they regard the evolution of reproductive isolation as the key event that allows sexual lineages to evolve independently: without it there would be one, or a few geographically separate, species of cichlid in each African lake, rather than hundreds. The PSC tends to recognize more species than the BSC, since any diagnosable population qualifies, which matters for estimates of biodiversity and for conservation law. Genealogical criteria have a further problem: gene trees of different loci differ, and reciprocal monophyly is reached late, because of **incomplete lineage sorting** ([7.8.6](/7.8.6)); for recently diverged species many loci are not yet monophyletic.

The oldest criterion, implicit in the work of Linnaeus and still the basis of most descriptions of new species, is the *phenetic species concept* (morphological or typological species concept): a species is a group of organisms that resemble one another in form more than they resemble members of other groups. It is practical, because specimens can be sorted without knowing how they breed, and it is the only criterion available for most fossils; but it is arbitrary, because there is no agreed standard of how different two groups must be, and it has no genetic or developmental basis. Its failures are those of the screech owls above. In the common Mormon swallowtail (*Papilio polytes*), the Philippine subspecies *P. p. alphenor* looks so much like the mainland *P. p. polytes* that both are placed in one species, yet genome-wide SNP data group *alphenor* with two species of Wallacea and New Guinea, *P. phestus* and *P. ambrax*, which look quite different (Zhang and colleagues 2017): the morphological species is not a single lineage.

The *ecological species concept* (Van Valen 1976) defines a species as a lineage that occupies an adaptive zone, a set of resources and habitats, different from that of any other lineage; it reflects the fact that species can coexist only if they use different resources, the principle of competitive exclusion that limits the number of biological species that can live together ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Habitat alone does not delimit species, however: populations of one species adapted to different habitats, such as the heavy-metal-tolerant populations of the grass *Agrostis capillaris* (formerly *A. tenuis*) on mine spoils, which differ in tolerance and growth form from the populations on normal soil a few metres away ([7.10.10](/7.10.10)), are usually called *ecotypes* rather than species, because they still exchange genes. These concepts were reconciled by de Queiroz (2007) in the *general lineage concept*: a species is a separately evolving metapopulation lineage, and the various criteria (diagnosability, monophyly, ecological divergence, reproductive isolation) are not competing definitions but properties that diverging lineages acquire at different times and in different orders. Disagreement among concepts is then confined to a grey zone during divergence, when some criteria are met and others not.

### 11.1.3. Difficult Cases

No species concept divides all organisms cleanly, because the properties that distinguish species evolve gradually.

- *Hybridizing taxa.* Oaks of North America, such as the white oaks *Quercus grisea* and *Q. gambelii* in New Mexico and Arizona, differ in habitat and in many adaptive traits and remain distinct at most genetic markers, yet hybridize frequently; their chloroplast genealogies follow geography rather than species. Such groups of distinct but interbreeding taxa are called syngameons by botanists. Mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes often cross the boundaries of otherwise good species, and in some butterflies alleles controlling wing pattern have crossed species boundaries by hybridization ([7.10.12](/7.10.12)). The red wolf (*Canis rufus*) of the southeastern United States shows how such cases matter in practice. As its populations collapsed in the twentieth century, the survivors hybridized with coyotes (*C. latrans*) that were spreading east; the last wild animals were captured in the 1970s, 14 of them judged to be "pure" red wolves founded a captive population, and the species, declared extinct in the wild in 1980, was reintroduced to North Carolina from 1987. Mitochondrial DNA (Wayne and Jenks 1991) and later whole genomes (vonHoldt and colleagues 2016) indicated that the red wolf, and the eastern wolf of the Great Lakes region, are largely admixtures of gray wolves and coyotes, whereas others argue for an older distinct lineage that has absorbed coyote genes. Whether a taxon of hybrid ancestry deserves protection as a species is a legal as well as a biological question.
- *Complex networks of populations.* Land snails of the genus *Partula* on the island of Moorea (French Polynesia) include several described species that differ in morphology and habitat; some pairs coexisted without interbreeding in some places but hybridized or were connected through intergrading populations elsewhere. Sadly, most *Partula* species of Moorea were driven extinct in the wild after the introduction of a predatory snail (*Euglandina rosea*); several survived only in captive breeding programmes, from which they are now being reintroduced.
- *Ring species.* In some cases two forms coexist without interbreeding at one place yet are connected by a continuous chain of interbreeding populations around a barrier.

**Definition 11.1.4 (Ring Species)** A **ring species** is a chain of interbreeding populations that encircles a geographic barrier and whose terminal populations, where they meet, coexist without interbreeding, behaving as distinct species.

The best-known example is the greenish warbler (*Phylloscopus trochiloides*): two distinct forms meet in central Siberia without interbreeding but are connected by populations that intergrade gradually around the south of the Tibetan Plateau. Ring species show in space the continuous divergence that normally can be reconstructed only in time. Genomic studies have shown that even classic ring species usually have a history of interrupted gene flow somewhere along the ring, so true unbroken rings are rare.

### 11.1.4. Diagnosing Species in Practice

Biological species are seldom recognized by testing whether they interbreed or form fertile hybrids. Phenotypic differences serve as markers of reduced gene flow: if a sample of sympatric organisms falls into two discrete clusters that differ in several independent characters, it probably contains two species. Genetic markers serve the same purpose: a polymorphic locus with almost no heterozygotes in a local sample deviates strongly from **Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium** and indicates that the sample contains more than one gene pool.

**Example 11.1.1 (A New Species of Leaf Beetle)** Each species of the leaf-beetle genus *Ophraella* feeds on one or a few related plants; *O. notulata* is known only from two species of *Iva* along the east coast of the United States. Beetles closely resembling it were found in Florida on ragweed (*Ambrosia artemisiifolia*). Enzyme electrophoresis of beetles from both hosts throughout Florida, including both hosts at the same localities, showed consistent differences in allele frequencies at three loci; at one, an allele had frequency $0.968$ in beetles from *Ambrosia* and was absent from beetles on *Iva*, where another allele had frequency $0.989$, and no individual had a heterozygous profile suggesting hybridization. Mitochondrial DNA differed as well. The genetic markers indicated two reproductively isolated gene pools; a careful search then revealed average differences in the shape of a mouthpart and in relative leg length. Adults and newly hatched larvae preferred their own host plant, and beetles mated preferentially with their own kind; in laboratory crosses, *Ambrosia* females mated with *Iva* males produced viable eggs but the reverse cross did not, few hybrid larvae survived to adulthood, and none of those laid viable eggs. The *Ambrosia* form was described as a new species, *Ophraella slobodkini*.

:::

Today DNA sequences are often the first evidence of species boundaries, from short barcoding sequences to genome-wide data analysed with the **multispecies coalescent** ([7.10.25](/7.10.25)). **Species delimitation** ([7.10.25](/7.10.25)) by such methods identifies independently evolving lineages, but whether those lineages are reproductively isolated remains a biological question that genetic data alone cannot always answer.

## 11.2. Barriers to Gene Exchange

Gene flow between biological species is prevented by genetically based differences called reproductive isolating barriers (or isolating mechanisms; the latter term is somewhat misleading, because it implies that the barriers evolved in order to reduce gene exchange, which is rarely the case, §11.4). Under the BSC, speciation is the evolution of such barriers.

**Definition 11.2.1 (Prezygotic and Postzygotic Barriers)** A **prezygotic barrier** reduces the formation of hybrid zygotes: the individuals of two species do not meet, do not mate, or mate without fertilization. A **postzygotic barrier** reduces gene exchange after hybrid zygotes have formed, through reduced viability or fertility of hybrids or their descendants. Postzygotic isolation is *intrinsic* if it is independent of the environment (developmental or genetic incompatibility) and *extrinsic* if it arises from the environment (hybrids are poorly suited to the ecological niches or the mating systems of the parents).

| Stage | Barrier | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Premating: mates do not meet | Temporal isolation | Breeding at different seasons or times of day |
| | Habitat (ecological) isolation | Mating in different habitats or on different host plants |
| | Immigrant inviability | Migrants between divergent populations die before mating |
| Premating: mates meet but do not mate | Sexual (behavioural) isolation | Preference for conspecific mates, based on signals |
| | Pollinator isolation | Different pollinators do not move pollen between species |
| Postmating, prezygotic | Mechanical isolation | Reproductive structures do not fit |
| | Copulatory isolation | Female not stimulated by heterospecific male |
| | Gametic isolation | Sperm or pollen fail to fertilize; conspecific sperm or pollen take precedence |
| Postzygotic, extrinsic | Ecological inviability | Hybrids poorly adapted to the parental habitats |
| | Behavioural sterility | Hybrids unsuccessful in obtaining mates |
| Postzygotic, intrinsic | Hybrid inviability | Developmental failure due to genetic incompatibility |
| | Hybrid sterility | Failure to produce viable gametes |
| | Hybrid breakdown | Reduced viability or fertility in F₂ or backcross generations |

The distinction between prezygotic and postzygotic barriers matters for two reasons. Prezygotic barriers act first, so they have the first opportunity to restrict gene flow. And they are subject to different selection: natural selection can strengthen prezygotic isolation directly (reinforcement, [7.10.12](/7.10.12)), but it cannot directly favour lower fitness of hybrids. Both kinds of barrier are often asymmetric: females of species A may be less inclined to mate with males of B than the reverse, and the viability of F₁ hybrids may depend on the direction of the cross.

### 11.2.1. Examples of Prezygotic Isolation

Species may be temporally isolated. The band-rumped storm-petrel (*Oceanodroma castro*) includes genetically different populations that breed at different times of year in the same place, and periodical cicadas (*Magicicada*) have 13- or 17-year life cycles, so that a 13-year and a 17-year brood in one area emerge together only every $13 \times 17 = 221$ years. Ecological isolation separates two Japanese species of herbivorous ladybird beetles (*Henosepilachna*): one feeds and mates on thistles (*Cirsium*), the other on blue cohosh (*Caulophyllum*), and this segregation appears to be the only barrier to gene exchange. Sexual isolation is the main barrier among many sympatric animals that meet but do not mate: female túngara frogs (*Physalaemus*) respond almost exclusively to the calls of their own species, two races of the European corn borer (*Ostrinia nubilalis*) are isolated by male responses to different female sex pheromones, and males of *Heliconius pachinus* recognize conspecific females by their wing pattern. Mate preferences may also be learned. In the strawberry poison frog (*Oophaga pumilio*), whose populations in Panama differ strikingly in colour, cross-fostering experiments showed that females prefer males of the colour of the mother that raised them (*sexual imprinting*; Yang and colleagues 2019); learned preferences of this kind can rapidly create sexual isolation between colour forms.

In insects, the genitalia of closely related species often differ in morphology. In *Drosophila sechellia*, *D. mauritiana* and *D. simulans* the shape of the male genital arch is almost the only morphological difference; there is evidence that females terminate copulation when the male genitalia do not provide the appropriate tactile stimulation. Gametic isolation is important in externally fertilizing marine invertebrates. In abalones (large marine gastropods), the sperm protein lysin dissolves the egg's vitelline envelope by binding to a receptor protein of the envelope; both proteins diverge unusually fast between species, and heterospecific gametes fail to unite.

Monkeyflowers show how several barriers act in sequence. *Mimulus lewisii*, with broad pink petals, is pollinated by bees and grows at higher elevations in the Sierra Nevada of California than *M. cardinalis*, with a narrow, red, tubular corolla pollinated by hummingbirds; they co-occur at intermediate elevations. They can easily be crossed and give viable, fertile hybrids, yet hybrids are almost never found in nature. A field experiment with a large population of F₂ hybrids, whose novel combinations of traits revealed which of 12 floral differences matter, combined with QTL mapping, showed that at least four traits affect which pollinator visits a flower, and that differences in some of these traits depend on as few as one to six QTL. Separation by elevation alone reduces gene exchange by $59\,\%$; among plants at the same elevation, pollinator fidelity is $98\,\%$ effective; when a flower receives pollen of both species, conspecific pollen fertilizes the ovules at least $70\,\%$ of the time; F₁ seed germinates $20\,\%$ less often, and hybrids produce fewer seeds and much less viable pollen.

**Formula 11.2.1 (Sequential Barriers)** If barriers $i = 1, \dots, k$ act in sequence and barrier $i$ alone would reduce gene flow by the fraction $RI_i$, the absolute contribution of barrier $i$ to total isolation is

$$
AC_i = RI_i\Bigl(1 - \sum_{j<i}AC_j\Bigr), \qquad RI_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_i AC_i = 1 - \prod_i(1 - RI_i). \tag{11.1}
$$

Barrier $i$ can act only on the gene flow that earlier barriers have left, which is $\prod_{j<i}(1 - RI_j)$; the two expressions for the total follow by induction. For the monkeyflowers, $AC_1 = 0.59$, $AC_2 = 0.98 \cdot 0.41 = 0.402$, $AC_3 = 0.70 \cdot 0.0082 = 0.0057$ and $AC_4 = 0.20 \cdot 0.0025 = 0.0005$: total isolation is $0.998$, and the early barriers account for almost all of it. Because isolation by elevation and by pollinators is so strong, the reduced fitness of hybrids hardly comes into play, although it would be a substantial barrier on its own.

![Sequential reproductive barriers between the monkeyflowers Mimulus lewisii and M. cardinalis (barrier strengths from Futuyma and Kirkpatrick (2017), after Schemske, Bradshaw and colleagues). A: the strength of each barrier on its own. B: absolute contributions in the order in which the barriers act (Formula 11.2.1): elevational separation and pollinator fidelity account for 0.992 of the total isolation of 0.998, leaving little for later barriers to prevent](./plots/sequential-barriers.py)

A barrier that contributes to isolation now may not have caused speciation. Differences continue to accumulate after species are fully isolated, and some barriers, especially late-acting ones, may have evolved only after earlier ones had already stopped gene flow. The genes and traits that caused speciation are best identified in populations that have become isolated only recently.

### 11.2.2. Examples of Postzygotic Isolation

Extrinsic postzygotic isolation often arises because parental species are adapted to different environments and the hybrid is adapted to neither. The butterflies *Heliconius melpomene* and *H. cydno* are distasteful to birds and have different warning colour patterns; birds learn to avoid common patterns but attack unfamiliar ones. Artificial butterflies with the colour pattern of the F₁ hybrid, placed in a tropical forest, were attacked significantly more often than models with either parental pattern. Intrinsic postzygotic isolation is the low viability or fertility of hybrids whatever the environment: mules, the F₁ hybrids of horse ($2n = 64$) and donkey ($2n = 62$), are healthy but sterile: with $2n = 63$, their chromosomes, which differ in number and arrangement, cannot all pair in meiosis. Lions and tigers likewise produce viable hybrids in captivity in both directions (the liger, from a male lion, and the tigon, from a male tiger), with reduced fertility, especially in males. Intrinsic isolation is based on interactions between genes from the two parents (§11.4) or on differences in chromosomes (§11.6), and is often more permanent than extrinsic isolation.

## 11.3. How Fast Does Reproductive Isolation Evolve?

The time needed for reproductive isolation to become complete varies enormously. A new species can arise by **polyploidy** in one or two generations ([7.10.12](/7.10.12)). The more than 450 species of cichlids of Lake Victoria, a lake whose basin was dry about $15\,000$ years ago, have been estimated to derive from just five ancestral species within that time. Going from 5 to 450 species takes $\log_2 90 \approx 6.5$ successive doublings, so each lineage must have split on average about every $15\,000/6.5 \approx 2300$ years, or more often if some species went extinct. At the other extreme, many species and even genera of birds that diverged more than five million years ago can still form fully viable and fertile hybrids when crossed, and bird populations that diverged $1.5$–$3$ million years ago may still form hybrid zones.

The best comparative data come from pairs of *Drosophila* species whose prezygotic isolation (measured as mate choice in the laboratory) and postzygotic isolation (viability and fertility of hybrids) have been compared with their genetic distance, a rough molecular clock (Coyne and Orr 1989, 1997). Three patterns emerge.

- Isolation increases steadily with divergence time. Allopatric pairs reach about half of complete isolation, prezygotic and postzygotic, at a divergence time of more than a million years; molecular-clock estimates are about $1.1$–$2.7$ million years for allopatric populations to become fully isolated.
- In many groups, prezygotic isolation evolves faster than intrinsic postzygotic isolation; the same is seen in darters (*Etheostoma*), small freshwater fishes in which 13 pairs of allopatric species were tested for both sexual isolation and survival of artificially produced hybrids.
- Closely related *sympatric* pairs have much stronger prezygotic isolation than allopatric pairs of the same genetic distance ($0.83$ against $0.29$ for pairs with Nei's $D < 0.5$), whereas postzygotic isolation does not differ; sympatric sister species are estimated to have needed only about $200\,000$ years. This pattern is the main comparative evidence for reinforcement ([7.10.12](/7.10.12)).

Where prezygotic isolation is fast and postzygotic isolation slow, as may be the rule in many groups, postzygotic isolation probably plays little role in speciation itself. It may still matter, because prezygotic barriers based on ecology or behaviour may be incomplete or may break down when conditions change. In Lake Victoria, increasing turbidity has interfered with the ability of female cichlids to see the male colours on which sexual isolation between some closely related species depends, and formerly isolated species now hybridize. Complete hybrid sterility, in contrast, is probably irreversible and makes the separation of species permanent.

In bacteria that take up DNA by transformation, isolation depends in a simple way on sequence divergence: in *Bacillus subtilis*, the rate at which DNA is incorporated by homologous recombination falls exponentially with the divergence between donor and recipient, because recombination requires a stable heteroduplex. Bacteria can still take up genes from donors an order of magnitude more divergent than eukaryotes can successfully cross with.

## 11.4. The Genetics of Postzygotic Isolation

Intrinsic hybrid inviability and sterility pose an evolutionary puzzle. If hybrids between two species are unfit, the genotypes that make them unfit must be bad combinations. How could evolution in either lineage pass through an unfit intermediate? A mutation that made heterozygotes sterile would itself be sterile when it first appeared.

### 11.4.1. The Dobzhansky–Muller Model

**Definition 11.4.1 (Dobzhansky–Muller Incompatibility)** A **Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibility** (DMI; also Bateson–Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibility) is a deleterious epistatic interaction between alleles at two or more loci that have been fixed in different lineages and that meet for the first time in hybrids.

Proposed by Dobzhansky (1937) and Muller (1942), and earlier by Bateson, the model explains how incompatibilities between populations evolve without any incompatibility within a population. Let the ancestor have genotype $aabb$, and let it be divided by a geographic barrier. In one population allele $A$ is fixed (perhaps by selection for local adaptation), giving $AAbb$; in the other $B$ is fixed, giving $aaBB$. Every genotype that occurs in either population, $aabb$, $Aabb$, $AAbb$ or $aaBb$, $aaBB$, works well; $A$ and $B$ have never been together in one genome, so selection has never tested whether they are compatible. If they are not, the hybrid $AaBb$ is unfit. In the language of the adaptive landscape, the populations move along ridges of high fitness that surround a hole into which only hybrids fall. Incompatibilities are typically asymmetric: the alternative recombinant $aabb$ is the ancestor and is fit.

The model fits the genetic analyses of hybrid incompatibility. A simple example comes from crosses between strains of *Arabidopsis thaliana* from different regions: both strains carry two duplicate (paralogous) copies of an essential gene; one strain has lost the function of the first copy, the other of the second. The F₁ is viable, but in the F₂ the recombinants homozygous for the nonfunctional alleles of both copies die. In the platyfish *Xiphophorus maculatus*, a sex-linked allele *Tu* that produces pigment spots is held in check by an autosomal suppressor *R*; the swordtail *X. helleri* lacks both. F₁ hybrids have exaggerated spots, and in backcrosses to *X. helleri* the offspring that inherit *Tu* without *R* develop malignant melanomas, a strong postzygotic barrier. The reciprocal recombinant, without *Tu* and with or without *R*, is normal, as predicted if it resembles the ancestor.

The causes of incompatibilities are diverse: mismatches between cis-regulatory sequences and the trans-acting factors that bind them, intragenomic conflict (§11.4.3), and cytonuclear incompatibility. In the copepod *Tigriopus californicus*, crosses between populations give F₁ hybrids of normal or slightly elevated survival, but F₂ and later hybrids, in which nuclear genes have recombined, have reduced survival and fecundity when their mitochondria (inherited from the mother) come from a different population than most of their nuclear genes: offspring of backcrosses in which most nuclear genes match the mitochondria survive well, those in which they do not, poorly.

### 11.4.2. The Snowball Effect

**Formula 11.4.1 (Accumulation of Incompatibilities)** Suppose two lineages have accumulated a total of $K$ substitutions since their split, and that each pair of alleles that has never been tested together in one genome is incompatible with a small probability $p$. Then the expected number of incompatibilities between the lineages is (Orr 1995)

$$
\mathbb{E}[I_K] = p\,\frac{K(K - 1)}{2} \approx \frac{pK^2}{2}. \tag{11.2}
$$

**Proof** Let the $k$th substitution occur in lineage 1, and let lineages 1 and 2 have accumulated $n_1$ and $n_2$ substitutions before it, $n_1 + n_2 = k - 1$. The new allele has been tested by selection against everything present in lineage 1, but not against the $n_2$ derived alleles of lineage 2, nor against the ancestral alleles that lineage 2 still carries at the $n_1$ loci already substituted in lineage 1. It thus creates $k - 1$ new untested pairs, each incompatible with probability $p$. Summing over $k = 1, \dots, K$ gives $p\sum_{k=1}^{K}(k - 1) = pK(K - 1)/2$. $\square$

Because the number of incompatibilities grows faster than linearly with divergence, with $K$ itself roughly proportional to time, Orr called this the snowball effect. Two consequences follow. Reproductive isolation can appear suddenly after a long period in which hybrids are nearly normal; and the incompatibilities between well-separated species are many, most of them having arisen after the species became isolated, so the genes found to cause hybrid problems between old species are not necessarily the ones that caused speciation. Introgression experiments in *Drosophila* and tomatoes (*Solanum*), counting incompatibilities between species pairs of increasing divergence, found the faster-than-linear increase predicted by Eq. (11.2).

B: simulation of the accumulation of incompatibilities between two lineages (300 replicates, probability 0.002 that an untested pair of alleles is incompatible): the mean number of incompatibilities grows as pK(K − 1)/2 (red), much faster than linearly (dashed). C: the probability that a hybrid carries no incompatibility, exp(−pK(K − 1)/2), stays near one for a long time and then drops: reproductive isolation appears abruptly](./plots/snowball.py)

Barton et al. put the same argument in terms of genotype space. Two species that separated about a million years ago, with $10\,000$ genes of $1000$ base pairs and a substitution rate of about $10^{-9}$ per base pair per year, differ at about $20\,000$ sites of their coding sequences; only about $20\,000$ genotypes, the successive states of each lineage, have ever been common, whereas the F₂ and backcrosses can produce an astronomical number of new combinations that selection has never tested. Seen this way, what needs explanation is not that hybrids are unfit but that diverged genomes remain compatible for so long; much of development and gene function is remarkably conserved (human genes can replace their counterparts in yeast).

### 11.4.3. Speciation Genes

Classical genetics showed that every chromosome of one *Drosophila* species usually has some effect on the fitness of hybrids. Modern analyses reveal both many and few genes. In a study of 87 lines of *D. simulans* carrying a single introgressed segment of *D. mauritiana* genome (each about $7\,\%$ of the genome), segments had little effect when heterozygous; when homozygous, about $5\,\%$ of autosomal segments caused inviability, $9\,\%$ maternally dependent inviability, $5\,\%$ female sterility and $50\,\%$ male sterility, and $75\,\%$ of the segments carried on the X chromosome of males caused sterility. These two species, which separated about $0.9$ million years ago, are therefore separated by incompatibilities throughout their genomes. In contrast, the Bogotá subspecies of *D. pseudoobscura*, isolated in Colombia from the main population of the United States perhaps $150\,000$–$230\,000$ years ago, shows an early stage: Bogotá females crossed with USA males give fertile daughters but completely sterile sons, the reverse cross gives fertile offspring of both sexes, and the sterility is due to complex interactions among relatively few loci on the X chromosome and two autosomes.

*Speciation genes*, loci at which divergence contributes to reproductive isolation, have now been identified at the molecular level. Hybrid males of *D. melanogaster* and *D. simulans*, which diverged about $5.4$ million years ago, normally die; screens using hybrid rescue mutations and deficiencies located an incompatibility in the gene *Nup96*, which encodes a component of the nuclear pore complex. Its sequence shows an excess of amino acid substitutions over synonymous ones, a signature of **positive selection** ([7.4.23](/7.4.23); [7.10.23](/7.10.23)), along both lineages: adaptive evolution of a highly conserved protein, for reasons unrelated to speciation, produced the incompatibility as a side effect. In mice, the gene *Prdm9*, which determines the positions of meiotic recombination hotspots, causes sterility of hybrid males between subspecies of the house mouse. Such genes are not genes "for" reproductive isolation: their normal functions (pigmentation, nuclear transport, recombination) have nothing to do with speciation, and isolation is an unpredictable by-product of their divergence.

Genetic conflict is a common cause of incompatibilities. Alleles that are transmitted to more than half of the gametes by **meiotic drive** ([7.4.17](/7.4.17)) spread even if they reduce fertility, and they select for suppressors at other loci. If the conflict between a driver and its suppressors has played out in one population but not another, hybrids may express the driver without its suppressors. The sterility of hybrid males between Bogotá and USA *D. pseudoobscura* is largely due to such a gene, *Overdrive*, which causes segregation distortion and male sterility in hybrids, where the suppressors that restore fertility within the Bogotá population are inadequate.

## 11.5. Haldane's Rule and the Large-X Effect

**Definition 11.5.1 (Haldane's Rule)** **Haldane's rule** (Haldane 1922): "When in the F₁ offspring of two different animal races one sex is absent, rare, or sterile, that sex is the heterozygous [heterogametic] sex."

In mammals and *Drosophila*, in which males are XY (the **heterogametic sex**, [7.4.6](/7.4.6)), sterile or inviable F₁ hybrids are usually males; male mules are sterile. In birds and butterflies, in which females are ZW, hybrid females suffer more. The rule is obeyed in the great majority of crosses in which one sex is more affected than the other, and it applies early in speciation: in the *Drosophila* survey of §11.3, species pairs in which only male hybrids are sterile or inviable have an average genetic distance of about $0.26$, whereas pairs in which females are also affected have about $0.88$. Hybrid incompatibility thus begins in the heterogametic sex and extends to the homogametic sex much later.

Several explanations contribute.

- *The dominance theory* (Muller; Turelli and Orr 1995). If the alleles involved in incompatibilities are on average partly recessive, incompatibilities between the X chromosome of one species and autosomes of the other are fully expressed in hemizygous F₁ males but largely masked in F₁ females, which carry one X of each species. Direct evidence comes from the introgression lines described in §11.4.3: segments of *D. mauritiana* genome have little effect when heterozygous but often cause sterility or inviability when homozygous, just as a whole X chromosome is exposed in males.
- *Faster male evolution.* Genes involved in male reproduction evolve fast, largely because of **sexual selection** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)), so incompatibilities affecting male fertility accumulate fastest. In groups with heterogametic females (birds, butterflies), this effect acts against Haldane's rule, which nevertheless holds there.
- *Faster X evolution.* Recessive favourable mutations on the X chromosome are exposed to selection in the hemizygous sex and may therefore be substituted faster than on autosomes, increasing the number of X-linked incompatibilities.
- *Meiotic drive.* Sex-chromosome drivers and their suppressors, which evolve through conflict, are disproportionately involved in hybrid male sterility.

Mosquitoes allow these explanations to be separated. *Anopheles* has a large X chromosome and a degenerate Y, as *Drosophila* does. In *Aedes*, sex is determined by a single locus, and the chromosomes that carry it, though called X and Y, are homologous and both carry a full set of genes: a recessive incompatibility on the X is covered by the Y in males, so the dominance theory does not apply, while sexual selection should act in both groups. In *Aedes*, hybrid sterility nevertheless affects only males in 11 crosses and only females in none, which must be due to faster evolution of male fertility; hybrid inviability, to which neither sexual selection nor the dominance theory applies in *Aedes*, almost never affects one sex only. *Anopheles* shows Haldane's rule for both sterility and inviability; its excess of male-only inviability can be explained only by the dominance theory.

The dominance theory also explains the *large-X effect*: in genetic analyses of hybrid sterility and inviability, the X chromosome has a much larger effect than expected from its size, in *Drosophila* and also in butterflies and mammals, although it has no unusual role in ordinary species differences or quantitative variation. Haldane's rule and the large-X effect together are strong evidence that the alleles causing postzygotic isolation are mostly recessive; why this should be so for alleles expressed only on a foreign genetic background is not yet understood.

## 11.6. Chromosomal Differences and Cytoplasmic Incompatibility

Many sister species differ by **chromosomal rearrangements** ([7.4.15](/7.4.15)), mainly inversions and translocations, including centric fusions. Heterozygotes for translocations and fusions often have reduced fertility, because irregular segregation at meiosis produces unbalanced gametes; heterozygotes for inversions produce unbalanced recombinant gametes when crossing over occurs within the inversion. Because heterozygotes are selected against (**underdominance**), populations with different arrangements are almost monomorphic, and where "chromosome races" meet they form narrow tension zones ([7.10.10](/7.10.10#sec-10.5.3)).

The common shrew (*Sorex araneus*) is divided across Europe and Asia into many chromosome races, each with a different combination of Robertsonian fusions of chromosome arms (**Robertsonian translocations**, [7.4.15](/7.4.15)). In Britain, the Oxford race carries the fusions $k/q$ and $n/o$ and the Hermitage race the fusion $k/o$; in F₁ hybrids the arms pair at meiosis into a chain of five chromosomes that segregates irregularly, and fertility is low. In Siberia the Novosibirsk and Tomsk races differ by fusions that form a chain of nine chromosomes in hybrids, and they are separated by a cline less than $9\,\mathrm{km}$ wide. Yet each fusion could have spread from an unfused ancestor without much loss of fitness, because a heterozygote for a single fusion pairs as a simple trivalent and segregates fairly regularly; the strong isolation between races arises only when different fusions involving the same arms are combined, a chromosomal analogue of the Dobzhansky–Muller model. Within the hybrid zones, the unfused arms have risen to high frequency, apparently because they reduce the incompatibility. The direct effects of most rearrangements on fertility are small, however, and their main role in speciation may be the suppression of recombination, which protects divergent genes from gene flow ([7.10.12](/7.10.12)).

Reproductive isolation can also be caused by endosymbionts. Bacteria of the genus *Wolbachia*, maternally inherited endosymbionts of many arthropods, cause **cytoplasmic incompatibility** ([7.4.11](/7.4.11)): sperm from infected males modify the paternal chromosomes so that embryos die unless the egg carries the same *Wolbachia* strain. When two populations carry different strains, incompatibility can be bidirectional, a strong postzygotic barrier. In the parasitoid wasps *Nasonia giraulti* and *N. longicornis*, incompatibility caused by *Wolbachia* is the main barrier between the species, and curing them of the bacteria with antibiotics allows them to produce hybrids. The evolutionary consequences of such symbionts for their hosts, including their spread by manipulating host reproduction, are discussed in [7.10.16](/7.10.16).

## Summary

- Species concepts: the biological species concept (Definition 11.1.1) defines species by reproductive isolation; the phylogenetic species concept (Definition 11.1.3) by diagnosability or monophyly; the ecological and general lineage concepts by adaptive zones and separately evolving lineages. The criteria are met at different stages of divergence, and they disagree in a grey zone. Cryptic species (Definition 11.1.2), hybridizing taxa and ring species (Definition 11.1.4) are difficult cases.
- Barriers: prezygotic barriers (temporal, ecological, sexual, pollinator, mechanical, gametic) and postzygotic barriers (extrinsic or intrinsic inviability and sterility, Definition 11.2.1) act in sequence; early barriers contribute most to total isolation (Formula 11.2.1).
- Rates: isolation accumulates over hundreds of thousands to millions of years in most groups, but within thousands of years in some cichlids and within one generation by polyploidy. Prezygotic isolation often evolves faster than postzygotic isolation, and it is stronger in sympatric than in allopatric pairs.
- Genetics: postzygotic isolation is caused by Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibilities (Definition 11.4.1), which evolve without fitness valleys and accumulate faster than linearly with divergence (Formula 11.4.1). Speciation genes such as *Nup96* diverged by selection unrelated to speciation; genetic conflict (meiotic drive) is a frequent cause.
- **Haldane's rule**: hybrid sterility and inviability first affect the heterogametic sex (Definition 11.5.1), explained mainly by recessive X-linked incompatibilities (dominance theory) and the faster evolution of male fertility genes; the X has a disproportionately large effect.
- Chromosomes and symbionts: underdominant rearrangements form narrow hybrid zones but usually contribute little directly; *Wolbachia* can cause strong cytoplasmic incompatibility.

## Questions

1. Why is "inability to produce fertile hybrids" neither necessary nor sufficient for two populations to be different biological species?

   **Answer** Not necessary: species may be isolated entirely by prezygotic barriers, such as the monkeyflowers that produce fertile hybrids by hand-pollination but almost never in nature. Not sufficient in the strict sense: what matters is gene exchange in nature; two populations that cannot meet (allopatric) are not distinguished by the BSC merely by a geographic barrier, and lineages that hybridize at a low rate may still be regarded as good species if gene flow is small enough that they evolve largely independently.

2. Three barriers act in sequence with individual strengths $0.5$, $0.5$ and $0.9$. Compute their absolute contributions and the total isolation. How does the answer change if the order of the first and third barriers is reversed?

   **Answer** In the order given: $AC_1 = 0.5$, $AC_2 = 0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.25$, $AC_3 = 0.9 \cdot 0.25 = 0.225$; total $0.975 = 1 - 0.5 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 0.1$. Reversed ($0.9$, $0.5$, $0.5$): $AC_1 = 0.9$, $AC_2 = 0.05$, $AC_3 = 0.025$; the total is the same, $0.975$, but almost all of it is now attributed to the first barrier. The total is independent of order; the contributions are not.

3. In the Dobzhansky–Muller model, why must at least one of the two lineages substitute a derived allele at each of the interacting loci? Could a DMI arise if only one lineage evolved?

   **Answer** An incompatibility requires a combination of alleles never tested together. If only lineage 1 evolves, substituting $A$ and then $B$, every intermediate ($Aabb$, $AAbb$, $AABb$, ...) was tested in lineage 1, including $A$ with $B$. A hybrid with the unchanged lineage 2 combines derived alleles of lineage 1 with ancestral alleles of lineage 2; incompatibility can still arise if the second substitution ($B$) was compatible with $A$ but not with $a$, as long as $a$ and $B$ never coexisted: the pair $a$–$B$ was untested, since $B$ arose after $A$ had replaced $a$ in lineage 1. So a DMI can arise with evolution in only one lineage, between a derived allele and an ancestral allele at another locus, but not between the derived alleles $A$ and $B$ of that lineage. This is why the count in Formula 11.4.1 includes derived–ancestral pairs.

4. Using Eq. (11.2), how many times more incompatibilities are expected between species that have diverged for 2 million years than for 1 million years? What does this imply for the search for the genes that caused speciation?

   **Answer** If $K$ is proportional to time, the ratio is about $(2/1)^2 = 4$. Most incompatibilities between old species arose after they had already become isolated; to find those that caused speciation, one should study recently diverged populations, such as the Bogotá subspecies of *D. pseudoobscura*, in which few loci are involved.

5. Explain why the dominance theory predicts Haldane's rule for both sterility and inviability, whereas faster male evolution predicts it only for sterility (in male-heterogametic taxa). How does the comparison between *Aedes* and *Anopheles* use this?

   **Answer** The dominance theory concerns the hemizygous exposure of recessive X-linked incompatibilities, which applies to any trait affected, viability as well as fertility, and only where the Y lacks the corresponding genes. Faster male evolution concerns genes for male reproduction, so it predicts excess male sterility but not inviability, and in any group regardless of the sex chromosomes. *Aedes* has homologous X and Y, so only faster male evolution acts: it shows male-only sterility but no male-only inviability. *Anopheles* has a degenerate Y, so both act: it shows male-biased sterility and inviability. The excess of male inviability in *Anopheles* is therefore evidence for the dominance theory.
