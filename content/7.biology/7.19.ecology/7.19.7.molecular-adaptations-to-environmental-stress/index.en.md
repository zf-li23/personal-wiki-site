The previous chapters described how organisms tolerate temperature, drought, salinity and hypoxia in terms of physiology and morphology. This chapter descends to the molecules that make such tolerance possible. *Molecular ecology* is the application of the principles and methods of molecular biology to the study of how organisms interact with their environment: it includes molecular adaptations to the environment, the effects of the environment on gene expression, the genetic structure of populations and the mechanisms of their divergence and speciation, phylogeography, behavioral and conservation genetics, ecological genomics, landscape genetics and the tracing of invasive species. Here we deal with the first two topics, the level of the individual: the proteins and regulatory circuits that protect cells against cold, heat, drought and lack of oxygen, and the ways in which environments switch genes on and off. Molecular ecology at the level of populations, genetic diversity and its measurement, is treated in [7.19.20](/7.19.20).

Many of the molecules discussed here have already appeared in other courses: thermogenin in thermoregulation ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)), cold shock and antifreeze proteins in microbiology ([7.11.9](/7.11.9)) and plant stress physiology ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)), and the hypoxia-inducible factor in biochemistry and respiratory physiology ([7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.6.2)). We do not repeat their biochemistry but ask the ecological questions: how the molecular machinery is distributed among species and habitats, what it costs, how it varies among populations, and how simple quantitative models explain its properties. Two models are worked out: the adsorption–inhibition model of antifreeze proteins and a feedback model of the heat shock response.

## 7.1. Cold

### 7.1.1. Nonshivering Thermogenesis and UCP1

Small mammals and birds in cold environments must produce heat continuously to keep their body temperature constant, and the smaller they are, the larger their heat loss per gram ([7.19.6](/7.19.6#sec-6.5.1)). Their main long-term source of extra heat is **nonshivering thermogenesis** in **brown adipose tissue**, where **thermogenin** (uncoupling protein 1, UCP1) lets protons return across the inner mitochondrial membrane without passing through ATP synthase, so that the energy of oxidation is released as heat ([7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.4.3)). During cold acclimation, and in many rodents in response to the short days of autumn even before it becomes cold, the mass of brown fat, its mitochondrial content and the expression of *Ucp1* increase several-fold; in the Brandt's vole (*Lasiopodomys brandtii*) of the Inner Mongolian grasslands and the striped hamster (*Cricetulus barabensis*) of northern China, winter-acclimatized animals have much higher nonshivering thermogenic capacity than summer animals, and the increase can be induced in the laboratory by cold or short photoperiod alone.

The distribution of UCP1 among mammals reflects body size. Thermogenic UCP1 is an innovation of eutherian mammals (marsupials and monotremes have homologous genes but little or no brown fat thermogenesis), and it has been inactivated independently in at least eight eutherian lineages, including pigs, horses, elephants, whales and sea cows, pangolins and armadillos (Gaudry et al. 2017). Most of these lineages are large-bodied, and many lost the gene at times of global cooling: in a large animal, with a low surface-to-volume ratio and a small newborn heat loss per gram, the costs of maintaining brown fat may exceed its benefits. A cost becomes visible in domestic piglets, which lack functional UCP1, have no brown fat and are notoriously sensitive to cold; they depend on shivering and on behavioral huddling. Birds have no UCP1 and produce nonshivering heat mainly in skeletal muscle.

### 7.1.2. Cold Shock Proteins

When bacteria are shifted suddenly to low temperature, their growth stops for a few hours (the acclimation phase) and then resumes at a lower rate. During this phase a set of proteins is strongly induced.

**Definition 7.1.1 (Cold Shock Protein)** A protein whose synthesis is strongly and transiently induced when cells are shifted to low temperature, and which helps them resume growth in the cold, is called a **cold shock protein** (CSP).

The best-studied is CspA of *Escherichia coli*, a small (70 amino acids) nucleic-acid-binding protein that can make up more than 10% of the proteins synthesized in the acclimation phase. At low temperature, RNA forms stable secondary structures that block translation and transcription termination; CspA binds single-stranded RNA without sequence specificity and melts these structures, acting as an RNA chaperone, so that the mRNAs can be translated (and, by the same token, are more accessible to ribonucleases and turned over). Its own expression is regulated by temperature at the level of the mRNA: the 5′ untranslated region of the *cspA* mRNA adopts a structure at low temperature that makes it stable and efficiently translated, so the mRNA itself acts as a thermosensor. Once the cells have acclimated and their translational apparatus is adjusted, CspA production falls to a low level, a transient response similar to that of heat shock proteins (Section 7.2). Cold shock domain proteins with the same fold occur in all three domains of life, including the Y-box proteins of animals and the glycine-rich RNA-binding proteins of plants, and psychrophilic bacteria express some of them constitutively ([7.11.9](/7.11.9)).

### 7.1.3. Antifreeze Proteins

Ectotherms in environments below 0 °C either avoid the freezing of their body fluids or tolerate extracellular ice ([7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.7)). **Antifreeze proteins** (AFPs, ice-binding proteins) are central to freeze avoidance in polar fishes and many insects and contribute to freeze tolerance in plants ([7.13.27](/7.13.27#sec-27.6.3)). They have three measurable effects on ice:

- thermal hysteresis: the lowering of the temperature at which an ice crystal grows below the temperature at which it melts;
- modification of crystal shape: instead of the flat discs that ice normally forms, crystals in AFP solutions are faceted (hexagonal bipyramids for many fish AFPs), because growth is blocked on the faces to which the protein binds;
- inhibition of recrystallization: the growth of large ice crystals at the expense of small ones in frozen tissue, which is the main source of damage during prolonged freezing and freeze–thaw cycles; this is the principal function of plant AFPs, whose hysteresis is small.

**Definition 7.1.2 (Thermal Hysteresis)** The difference between the melting temperature of ice in a solution and the lower temperature at which a seed crystal in it begins to grow is called **thermal hysteresis**.

In ordinary solutions the two temperatures coincide: both are lowered equally by solutes in proportion to their molal concentration (the colligative depression, $1.86\,\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$ for an ideal solute). AFPs lower the growth temperature by about 1 K at millimolar concentrations, about a thousand times more than colligative depression at the same concentration, while leaving the melting temperature practically unchanged. The diversity of AFPs is a classic case of **convergent evolution**: the antifreeze glycoproteins (AFGPs) of Antarctic notothenioid fishes, repeating Ala–Ala–Thr units with disaccharides, evolved from a trypsinogen gene and its noncoding sequence (Chen, DeVries and Cheng 1997); Arctic cods evolved almost identical AFGPs from unrelated noncoding DNA; and the type I (α-helical), II (lectin-like) and III (globular) AFPs of other fishes, the β-helical AFPs of beetles and moths and the ice-binding proteins of plants, bacteria and fungi have entirely different structures that share only a flat, relatively hydrophobic ice-binding surface with regularly spaced groups that match the spacing of water molecules in particular planes of the ice lattice.

How can a protein at millimolar concentration lower a freezing temperature by a degree? The accepted explanation is the adsorption–inhibition mechanism (Raymond and DeVries 1977). AFP molecules adsorb practically irreversibly to the ice surface. Between the adsorbed molecules the ice front can still advance, but only by bulging out into the liquid, and a curved ice surface has a lower equilibrium melting temperature than a flat one, by the **Gibbs–Thomson equation** ([6.3.19](/6.3.19)):

$$
\Delta T(r) = \frac{2\gamma T_{\mathrm{m}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}L_{\mathrm{f}}\,r}, \tag{7.1}
$$
where $r$ is the radius of curvature of the ice surface, $\gamma \approx 0.03\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}$ the ice–water interfacial energy, $T_{\mathrm{m}}$ the melting temperature, $\rho_{\mathrm{ice}} = 917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $L_{\mathrm{f}} = 334\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg^{-1}}$ the density and latent heat of fusion of ice. A bulge pinned between two adsorbed molecules a distance $d$ apart can be no more curved than a semicircle of radius $d/2$.

**Proposition 7.1.1 (Adsorption–Inhibition Model)** If AFP molecules adsorbed on the ice surface are spaced a distance $d$ apart, the ice front cannot grow until the supercooling exceeds

$$
\Delta T_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac{4\gamma T_{\mathrm{m}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}L_{\mathrm{f}}\,d}. \tag{7.2}
$$
If adsorption follows a Langmuir isotherm, so that the surface density of adsorbed molecules is $\sigma = \sigma_{\max}\theta$ with coverage $\theta = Kc/(1 + Kc)$ at solution concentration $c$, and $d = \sigma^{-1/2}$, then $\Delta T_{\mathrm{h}} \propto \sqrt{\theta}$: it rises as $\sqrt{c}$ at low concentration and saturates at high concentration.

**Proof** At a supercooling $\Delta T$, a pinned bulge of radius $r$ grows if $\Delta T > \Delta T(r)$ and melts back otherwise. As the bulge advances from a flat front, its radius decreases from infinity to a minimum of $d/2$ (a semicircle spanning the gap) and then increases again as it spreads beyond the pinning points. Growth past the pinning points therefore requires $\Delta T > \Delta T(d/2)$, which by Eq. (7.1) is Eq. (7.2). The melting point of the bulk ice is not changed, because melting proceeds without the need to create curvature. With $d = (\sigma_{\max}\theta)^{-1/2}$, $\Delta T_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac{4\gamma T_{\mathrm{m}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}L_{\mathrm{f}}}\sqrt{\sigma_{\max}}\,\sqrt{\theta}$; for $Kc \ll 1$, $\theta \approx Kc$ and $\Delta T_{\mathrm{h}} \propto \sqrt{c}$, while for $Kc \gg 1$, $\theta \to 1$. $\square$

With the values above, $4\gamma T_{\mathrm{m}}/(\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}L_{\mathrm{f}}) \approx 1.1\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{K\,m}$, so a hysteresis of 1 K requires a spacing of about 100 nm between adsorbed molecules, and 5 K about 20 nm: a sparse coverage of the ice surface suffices, which explains how micromolar to millimolar concentrations can have such large effects. The model also explains why the hyperactive AFPs of insects (spruce budworm, *Tenebrio* beetles, snow fleas), which reach hysteresis of 5–6 K, are more potent than fish AFPs (about 1–1.5 K): they bind the basal plane of ice as well as the prism planes, blocking growth in the direction in which fish AFPs cannot stop it, and they bind more strongly at lower concentration (figure below). The observed square-root dependence of hysteresis on concentration at low concentrations agrees with the model, although the adsorption of AFPs is effectively irreversible and the "Langmuir" coverage is better understood as a kinetic steady state of binding to a growing surface.

The ecological importance of AFGPs is shown by the history of the notothenioids. The Southern Ocean cooled to near freezing about 10–14 million years ago, and the notothenioids, the only group of fishes with AFGPs there, radiated into more than a hundred species that now make up most of the fish species and biomass of the Antarctic shelf; the evolution of the antifreeze is regarded as a key innovation that opened this habitat. The price is dependence: notothenioids cannot tolerate warm water, and many have lost the heat shock response (Section 7.2), which makes them vulnerable to warming of the Southern Ocean.

### 7.1.4. Cold-Regulated Genes in Plants

The molecular basis of **cold acclimation** in plants ([7.13.27](/7.13.27#sec-27.6.3)) is a regulon of hundreds of cold-regulated (*COR*) genes, many of whose products are hydrophilic, intrinsically disordered proteins with simple, repetitive amino acid compositions (dehydrins and other LEA proteins). COR15A, for example, accumulates in the chloroplast stroma and stabilizes the inner envelope membrane against the lamellar-to-hexagonal phase transitions that occur when freezing dehydrates the cell. The *COR* genes share a C-repeat/dehydration-responsive element (CRT/DRE) in their promoters, which is bound by the CBF (CRT-binding factor) transcription factors; *CBF* genes are induced within minutes of cooling, more strongly the lower the temperature, under the control of ICE1 and the circadian clock.

The CBF pathway provides a clear example of the molecular basis of **local adaptation**. *Arabidopsis thaliana* from a population in northern Sweden is much more freezing tolerant than a population from central Italy, and in reciprocal transplant experiments each population survives and reproduces better at home. The Italian population carries a loss-of-function mutation in *CBF2*, and the difference in *CBF2* explains a substantial part of the difference in freezing tolerance between them (Gehan et al. 2015): where winters are mild, the cost of acclimation (slower growth in the cold, Section 7.4) is not repaid, and loss of a component of the pathway is favored or at least tolerated.

## 7.2. Heat: Heat Shock Proteins

### 7.2.1. Functions and Properties

When cells are exposed to temperatures a few degrees above their normal range, they rapidly synthesize a set of highly conserved proteins, the **heat shock proteins** (HSPs), named for the families of their molecular mass (Hsp100, Hsp90, Hsp70, Hsp60, Hsp40 and the small HSPs). The same proteins are induced by other stresses that damage proteins: heavy metals, oxidative stress, ethanol, drought, high salt and cold ([7.13.27](/7.13.27#sec-27.3.3)). Their functions are:

- As **molecular chaperones**, they bind unfolded and misfolded proteins, prevent their aggregation, help them refold or target them for degradation, and so maintain proteostasis, the balance of the cell's protein population (Hsp70 binds exposed hydrophobic segments; Hsp60 provides a folding chamber; Hsp100 disaggregates; small HSPs hold denatured proteins in a refoldable state).
- They raise the tolerance of cells to heat and other stresses (acquired thermotolerance, below).
- In vertebrates, extracellular and membrane-bound HSPs act as danger signals in innate immunity and help present antigenic peptides, and intracellular HSPs protect tissues against inflammatory damage.
- Several HSPs have antioxidant effects, for example by maintaining glutathione levels, and small HSPs stabilize membranes.

The HSP response has three characteristic properties:

1. High conservation: HSPs of different organisms share 50–90% amino acid identity (Hsp70 of *E. coli*, DnaK, is about 50% identical to human Hsp70), indicating that the response is ancient and its molecular mechanism constrained.
2. Transience: synthesis begins within minutes of the shock, peaks within an hour or so and declines even if the high temperature persists, as soon as enough HSPs have accumulated.
3. Cross-tolerance: a mild heat shock increases tolerance not only to a later, more severe heat shock but also to other stresses (**cross-tolerance**, [7.13.27](/7.13.27#sec-27.3.5)).

### 7.2.2. A Feedback Model of the Heat Shock Response

The transience and acquired tolerance of the response follow from its regulation. In eukaryotes, HSP genes are activated by the heat shock transcription factor HSF1 (HSF in yeast and *Drosophila*), which in unstressed cells is kept inactive in complexes with chaperones, especially Hsp70 and Hsp90. When proteins unfold, they compete for the chaperones, HSF1 is released, trimerizes and activates the HSP genes; the newly made HSPs refold the client proteins and bind HSF1 again, switching the response off. This chaperone-titration mechanism is a negative feedback loop of the kind analyzed in [7.9.16](/7.9.16). A minimal model captures it with two variables, the fraction $U$ of client proteins that are unfolded and the chaperone level $H$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t} &= k_{\mathrm{d}}(T)(1 - U) - k_{\mathrm{r}}H_{\mathrm{f}}U,\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}H}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \beta_0 + \beta\frac{F^n}{K_{\mathrm{A}}^n + F^n} - \delta H,
\end{aligned}
$$
with free chaperone $H_{\mathrm{f}} = H K_{\mathrm{U}}/(K_{\mathrm{U}} + U)$ (unfolded proteins sequester chaperones) and free HSF $F = 1/(1 + H_{\mathrm{f}}/K_{\mathrm{F}})$ (free chaperones bind HSF). Here $k_{\mathrm{d}}(T)$ is the rate of unfolding, which rises steeply with temperature; $k_{\mathrm{r}}$ the rate of chaperone-assisted refolding; $\beta_0$ and $\beta$ the basal and maximal HSP synthesis rates; $K_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $n$ the activation constant and cooperativity of HSF on the HSP genes; and $\delta$ the rate of HSP degradation and dilution. Simulation (figure below) reproduces the main features:

- A heat shock unfolds proteins, which titrate the chaperones away from HSF; HSP synthesis jumps, and as HSPs accumulate they refold the proteins and recapture HSF, so synthesis falls again within the shock: transience is a consequence of the negative feedback.
- The HSPs made during a first shock decay slowly (here with a half-life of about 7 hours). A second shock a few hours later meets a higher chaperone level, and the fraction of unfolded proteins rises less than in naive cells: acquired thermotolerance, and, since other stresses also unfold proteins, cross-tolerance.
- The temperature at which the response is switched on depends on the balance between basal chaperone capacity and the rate of unfolding, which are both set by the organism's protein stability and HSP expression: the threshold is a tunable trait.

Heat increases the rate of protein unfolding; unfolded proteins sequester chaperones (HSPs), releasing the heat shock factor HSF from inhibition; free HSF activates the HSP genes; HSPs refold proteins and inactivate HSF again. B: fraction of proteins unfolded in cells given two heat shocks (2–4 h and 8–10 h, shaded) and in naive cells given only the second shock: the primed cells, which still carry the HSPs made during the first shock, suffer less unfolding in the second (acquired thermotolerance). C: HSP synthesis rate and HSP level in the primed cells: synthesis rises abruptly at the start of a shock and declines even while the high temperature lasts, because the accumulating HSPs switch HSF off.](./plots/hsp-feedback.py)

### 7.2.3. Ecology and Evolution of the Heat Shock Response

The temperature at which HSP synthesis begins ($T_{\mathrm{on}}$) and its maximum ($T_{\mathrm{max}}$) are correlated with the temperatures that a species experiences. Snails of the genus *Tegula* from the low intertidal and subtidal zones of the Pacific coast of North America induce Hsp70 at lower temperatures than species from the high intertidal, which heats up more at low tide (Tomanek and Somero 1999); desert ants and lizards have higher thresholds than temperate species; and Antarctic notothenioid fishes, which have lived for millions of years at a constant −1.9 °C, have lost the inducible response altogether and express Hsp70 constitutively. Within species, *Drosophila melanogaster* lines selected at high temperature evolve higher Hsp70 expression and greater thermotolerance, and extra copies of the *Hsp70* genes increase tolerance of heat in the larvae (Feder and Hofmann 1999).

The response has costs. HSP synthesis consumes energy and diverts the translation machinery from other proteins, and high HSP concentrations interfere with growth, development and signaling: *Drosophila* lines with extra *Hsp70* copies survive heat better as larvae but have lower fecundity and survival under normal temperatures (Krebs and Feder 1997). The level of expression is therefore a compromise between protection in rare stressful events and performance under normal conditions, and reef-building corals illustrate how this compromise varies within a species: colonies from naturally warm back-reef pools in American Samoa, which tolerate heat better, maintain high baseline expression ("frontloading") of HSPs and other stress genes, whereas colonies from cooler, more stable pools must induce them strongly when heated and are more likely to bleach (Barshis et al. 2013).

## 7.3. Drought and Hypoxia

### 7.3.1. Molecular Responses to Drought

Drought, salt and freezing all dehydrate cells, and plants respond to them with overlapping sets of genes ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)). The regulators fall into two pathways that both converge on the promoters of drought-induced genes:

- An **abscisic acid** (ABA)-dependent pathway: water deficit raises ABA, which through its receptors (PYR/PYL), the inhibition of type 2C phosphatases and the activation of SnRK2 kinases activates the bZIP transcription factors AREB/ABF; these bind ABA-responsive elements (ABRE) in the target promoters. NAC and MYB transcription factors act in parallel branches.
- An ABA-independent pathway, in which the DREB2 transcription factors, relatives of the CBFs of the cold pathway (DREB1), bind the same DRE/CRT element.

The functional genes they activate encode proteins that protect cells directly (LEA proteins and dehydrins, which shield proteins and membranes as water is lost; osmoprotectant-synthesizing enzymes such as Δ¹-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase for proline; aquaporins; detoxifying enzymes) and regulatory proteins (kinases, phosphatases and further transcription factors). The overlap of the DRE-binding pathways for cold and drought is the molecular basis of the cross-tolerance between freezing and drought.

### 7.3.2. Hypoxia and High-Altitude Adaptation

Animal cells sense oxygen through the stability of a transcription factor.

**Definition 7.3.1 (Hypoxia-Inducible Factor)** The heterodimeric transcription factor composed of an oxygen-regulated α subunit (HIF-1α, HIF-2α encoded by *EPAS1*, or HIF-3α) and a constitutive β subunit (ARNT), which accumulates when oxygen is low and activates genes of the response to hypoxia, is called the **hypoxia-inducible factor** (HIF).

In the presence of oxygen, prolyl hydroxylases (PHD1–3; PHD2 is encoded by *EGLN1*) hydroxylate two proline residues of HIF-α, using O₂ and 2-oxoglutarate as substrates; the von Hippel–Lindau protein (VHL) recognizes the hydroxylated prolines and targets HIF-α for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, so that the half-life of HIF-α in normal oxygen is only minutes. In hypoxia hydroxylation slows, HIF-α accumulates, enters the nucleus, dimerizes with ARNT and binds hypoxia-response elements, activating genes for erythropoietin, vascular endothelial growth factor, glucose transporters and glycolytic enzymes, and the switch from oxidative to glycolytic metabolism ([7.1.18](/7.1.18)). The work of Semenza, Ratcliffe and Kaelin on this mechanism was recognized by the Nobel Prize in 2019.

The PHDs are well suited to be sensors because their affinity for oxygen is low: the $K_{\mathrm{m}}$ of PHD2 for O₂ is about 250 μM in vitro, far above the 5–60 μM found in tissues. In a minimal model with constant synthesis of HIF-α at rate $k_{\mathrm{s}}$ and degradation at rate $k_0 + k_{\max}[\ce{O2}]/(K_{\mathrm{m}} + [\ce{O2}])$, the steady state is

$$
[\text{HIF-}\alpha] = \frac{k_{\mathrm{s}}}{k_0 + k_{\max}[\ce{O2}]/(K_{\mathrm{m}} + [\ce{O2}])},
$$
and because $[\ce{O2}] \ll K_{\mathrm{m}}$ in tissues, the degradation rate is nearly proportional to the oxygen concentration over the whole physiological range, so the HIF level responds to every change in oxygen rather than saturating (figure below). Changes in PHD activity shift the whole curve: weaker PHD activity raises HIF at a given oxygen level and so exaggerates the response to hypoxia, stronger activity dampens it.

At altitude the low inspired $P_{\ce{O2}}$ ([7.19.5](/7.19.5#sec-5.3)) activates this pathway. In lowlanders the result is acclimatization, including a rise in erythropoietin and hemoglobin concentration; but a high hematocrit increases blood viscosity, and chronic mountain sickness (excessive erythrocytosis) is common in long-term residents of the Andes. Tibetans, whose ancestors have lived on the plateau for many thousands of years, have hemoglobin concentrations close to those of lowlanders, and genome scans for selection found the strongest signals in two genes of the HIF pathway: *EPAS1* (HIF-2α) and *EGLN1* (PHD2) (Yi et al. 2010; Simonson et al. 2010). The Tibetan *EPAS1* haplotype, associated with lower hemoglobin, was introduced into the ancestors of Tibetans by **introgression** from Denisovans (Huerta-Sánchez et al. 2014; [7.10.33](/7.10.33)), and the Tibetan *EGLN1* variants increase PHD2 activity in hypoxia, damping HIF signaling (Lorenzo et al. 2014). The Tibetan solution is thus to blunt the hypoxic erythropoietic response, which in lowlanders is adaptive in the short term but harmful in the long term, rather than to amplify it.

The same pathway has been targeted repeatedly in other species living on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. Tibetan mastiffs carry an *EPAS1* haplotype introgressed from Tibetan wolves (Miao et al. 2017), and signals of selection on *EPAS1*, *EGLN1* or other HIF-pathway genes have been reported in yaks, Tibetan antelopes, Tibetan pigs, the ground tit and other birds of the plateau, a case of convergence at the level of a pathway rather than of particular mutations. Other adaptations to altitude involve the oxygen-transport proteins themselves: the high-affinity hemoglobins of bar-headed geese, Andean geese and high-altitude deer mice ([7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.6.2)).

Subterranean mammals face a different hypoxia: their burrows are low in O₂ and high in CO₂. The blind mole rats (*Spalax*) of the eastern Mediterranean, which tolerate 3% O₂ for hours, show constitutively high expression of hypoxia-responsive genes (erythropoietin, VEGF, myoglobin and neuroglobin) and an amino acid substitution in the tumor suppressor p53 (an arginine-to-lysine change at the position equivalent to human residue 174, similar to mutations found in human tumors) that shifts p53 from triggering apoptosis to triggering cell-cycle arrest and DNA repair under hypoxia (Avivi et al. 2007); naked mole rats (*Heterocephalus glaber*) survive minutes of complete anoxia by switching their brain metabolism to fructose. Insulin-like growth factors and their binding proteins are also among the genes regulated by hypoxia and differ between hypoxia-tolerant and intolerant rodents.

## 7.4. Environment and Gene Expression

### 7.4.1. Phenotypic Plasticity

The responses described so far are examples of a general property of organisms.

The ability of a single genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments is **phenotypic plasticity** ([7.5.38](/7.5.38)).

Plasticity is described by the **norm of reaction** of a genotype, its phenotype as a function of an environmental variable ([7.10.9](/7.10.9#sec-9.7)); when the reaction norms of different genotypes are not parallel there is **genotype–environment interaction**, and plasticity itself can evolve. Plasticity ranges from reversible physiological acclimation within hours or days (HSP induction, cold acclimation, the acclimatization of [7.19.2](/7.19.2)) to irreversible developmental switches that produce discrete morphs. Examples of the latter include the helmets and tail spines that *Daphnia* grow in response to **kairomones** of predatory fish or insect larvae, the carnivorous morph of spadefoot toad tadpoles, the winged and wingless morphs of aphids, and temperature-dependent sex determination in reptiles ([7.19.4](/7.19.4)). Plasticity is adaptive when the environment varies within the lifetime or between generations, when reliable cues predict the future environment, and when its costs (the machinery of sensing and regulation, the lag before a response, the production of a wrong phenotype after a misleading cue) are lower than the benefits; the cost of constitutive expression in the HSP example (Section 7.2.3) is the counterpart of the cost of plasticity.

### 7.4.2. Ecological Genomics

*Ecological genomics* is the use of genome-wide data to identify the genes and regulatory changes that underlie ecologically important traits and the responses of organisms to their natural environments. Its main approaches are:

- Transcriptomics: comparison of gene expression (RNA-seq, [7.9.2](/7.9.2)) among individuals exposed to different conditions in the laboratory or field, or among populations from different environments raised in a common garden. Constitutive differences in a common garden indicate genetic divergence, differences that appear only under stress indicate divergence in plasticity. The coral "frontloading" (Section 7.2.3) was found in this way.
- Reciprocal transplants and common gardens: growing genotypes from different environments in each other's environment, which tests for local adaptation (home-site advantage), as in the *Arabidopsis* *CBF2* study (Section 7.1.4).
- Genome scans for selection and genotype–environment association: identification of loci with unusual differentiation between populations or with allele frequencies correlated with environmental variables such as altitude or temperature ([7.10.8](/7.10.8)), as for *EPAS1* in Tibetans.
- Experimental evolution and functional tests (gene knockouts, transgenic replacement of alleles) to confirm the effect of candidate genes.

A striking result of these methods concerns the Atlantic killifish (*Fundulus heteroclitus*), small estuarine fish that live in some of the most polluted harbors of the eastern United States. At least four urban populations have independently evolved tolerance of polychlorinated biphenyls and dioxins thousands of times higher than that of nearby populations, within some tens of generations; the tolerant populations share deletions and other loss-of-function changes in genes of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor pathway, which mediates the toxicity of these compounds (Reid et al. 2016). Rapid adaptation was possible because killifish populations are very large and genetically diverse, so that the tolerance alleles were already present as standing variation.

## 7.5. Extreme Tolerance: Cryptobiosis

At the far end of tolerance lies **cryptobiosis** ([7.17.12](/7.17.12)), a reversible state in which metabolism is undetectable. Its commonest form is anhydrobiosis, survival of almost complete desiccation, found in seeds and pollen, resurrection plants ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.2)), lichens and mosses, bacterial and fungal spores, and among animals in bdelloid rotifers, nematodes, tardigrades, the encysted embryos of brine shrimp (*Artemia*) and the larvae of the "sleeping chironomid" *Polypedilum vanderplanki*, which survive in dried rock pools in Africa. Other forms are cryobiosis (freezing), anoxybiosis (lack of oxygen) and osmobiosis (high salt).

Tardigrades in the dried "tun" state, with their legs withdrawn and the body contracted, survive temperatures from near absolute zero to above 100 °C (for minutes), pressures of thousands of atmospheres, high doses of ionizing radiation and exposure to space. The molecular basis is being worked out:

- Vitrification: instead of crystallizing, the cytoplasm of an anhydrobiote dries into a glass, an amorphous solid of very high viscosity in which molecular motion and chemical reactions practically stop. In many anhydrobiotes (*Artemia* embryos, *Polypedilum*, nematodes, yeast) the nonreducing sugar trehalose accumulates to 15–20% of dry mass; it forms glasses with a high glass transition temperature and replaces the water hydrogen-bonded to membranes and proteins (the water replacement hypothesis).
- Intrinsically disordered protective proteins: tardigrades produce little trehalose but express tardigrade-specific intrinsically disordered proteins, cytoplasmic-abundant heat-soluble (CAHS) proteins that form gels and vitrify on drying and protect enzymes in vitro (Boothby et al. 2017); LEA proteins play similar roles in plants, nematodes and *Artemia*.
- Protection of DNA: the tardigrade protein Dsup binds nucleosomes and reduces DNA breaks caused by X-rays and hydroxyl radicals, and it increases radiation tolerance when expressed in human cultured cells (Hashimoto et al. 2016); after rehydration, efficient DNA repair removes the damage that accumulated in the dry state.

Cryptobiosis shows that "life" can be suspended as a structural state rather than a process, and it is a strategy of habitats that dry out unpredictably: mosses, lichens and ephemeral pools. The ecology of dormancy in general, including diapause and the seed bank, is treated in [7.19.19](/7.19.19).

## Summary

- Molecular ecology applies molecular methods to the interactions of organisms with their environment; at the level of the individual it studies molecular adaptations and the environmental control of gene expression.
- Cold: nonshivering thermogenesis by UCP1 in brown fat is increased in winter by cold and short days in small rodents, and UCP1 was lost repeatedly in large eutherians (pigs lack it and are cold-sensitive). Cold shock proteins (Definition 7.1.1) such as CspA act as RNA chaperones during acclimation. Antifreeze proteins produce thermal hysteresis (Definition 7.1.2), modify crystal shape and inhibit recrystallization; by the adsorption–inhibition model the hysteresis is $\Delta T_{\mathrm{h}} = 4\gamma T_{\mathrm{m}}/(\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}L_{\mathrm{f}}d)$, rising as the square root of coverage (Proposition 7.1.1); AFGPs were a key innovation of the Antarctic notothenioids. The CBF–COR regulon underlies plant cold acclimation, and a *CBF2* loss-of-function allele contributes to local adaptation of *Arabidopsis* in Italy versus Sweden.
- Heat: HSPs are molecular chaperones; the response is conserved, transient and confers cross-tolerance. A chaperone-titration model, in which unfolded proteins release HSF from chaperones, explains transience and acquired thermotolerance as consequences of negative feedback. Induction thresholds match habitat temperatures (*Tegula*), Antarctic fishes have lost the inducible response, and HSP expression has fitness costs (frontloading in heat-tolerant corals).
- Drought: ABA-dependent (AREB/ABF–ABRE) and ABA-independent (DREB2–DRE) pathways activate LEA proteins, osmoprotectant synthesis and aquaporins; sharing the DRE with the cold pathway underlies cross-tolerance.
- Hypoxia: HIF (Definition 7.3.1) is stabilized when oxygen-dependent hydroxylation by PHDs slows; the high $K_{\mathrm{m}}$ of PHD2 makes HIF a graded oxygen sensor. Tibetans carry selected *EPAS1* (Denisovan) and *EGLN1* alleles that damp the erythropoietic response; Tibetan mastiffs got *EPAS1* from wolves; mole rats have constitutive hypoxia responses and a modified p53.
- Phenotypic plasticity is described by reaction norms and can evolve; ecological genomics uses transcriptomics, common gardens and transplants, genome scans and functional tests (killifish tolerance of pollutants through loss of AHR-pathway function).
- Cryptobiosis rests on vitrification (trehalose, intrinsically disordered CAHS and LEA proteins) and protection and repair of DNA (Dsup).

## Questions

1. Use Eq. (7.2) to estimate the average spacing of adsorbed molecules on the ice surface of an Antarctic fish whose AFGPs provide 1.2 K of thermal hysteresis. Why does such a sparse coverage suffice, whereas colligative freezing-point depression of 1.2 K would require a solute concentration of about 0.65 mol/kg?

   **Answer** $d = 4\gamma T_{\mathrm{m}}/(\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}L_{\mathrm{f}}\Delta T_{\mathrm{h}}) \approx 1.07\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{K\,m}/1.2\,\mathrm{K} \approx 90\,\mathrm{nm}$, that is, about one adsorbed molecule per $8000\,\mathrm{nm^2}$ of ice surface. Colligative depression acts on the chemical potential of water in the whole solution and therefore needs a number of solute particles comparable to the number of water molecules (a mole fraction of about 1%); AFPs act only at the interface, by forcing the ice front to curve, and need to occupy only a minute fraction of the surface. The large molecular size of AFPs is not a disadvantage, because the effect depends on their spacing on the ice, not on their number in solution.

2. In the heat shock model, what would happen if the chaperones did not bind HSF (that is, $K_{\mathrm{F}} \to \infty$)? Which property of the natural response would be lost?

   **Answer** With $K_{\mathrm{F}} \to \infty$, $F = 1$ always, and HSP synthesis would be constant at $\beta_0 + \beta/(K_{\mathrm{A}}^n + 1)$, independent of unfolding: the cell would either express HSPs at a permanently high level (if $\beta$ is large), paying their cost all the time, or have no inducible response. The loop from unfolded proteins to HSF is what couples HSP production to need: synthesis rises only when chaperones are titrated away by unfolded proteins and falls when HSPs have accumulated. Transience, the economy of an inducible response and the setting of the induction threshold by the balance of chaperone capacity and protein damage would all be lost; this is roughly the situation of Antarctic notothenioids, which express Hsp70 constitutively.

3. Explain why a PHD with a $K_{\mathrm{m}}$ for O₂ far above tissue concentrations is a better oxygen sensor than one with a $K_{\mathrm{m}}$ of, say, 5 μM.

   **Answer** With $K_{\mathrm{m}} \gg [\ce{O2}]$, the hydroxylation rate is $k_{\max}[\ce{O2}]/K_{\mathrm{m}}$, proportional to oxygen concentration: a 20% fall in oxygen gives roughly a 20% fall in degradation and a corresponding rise in HIF-α, over the whole physiological range. With $K_{\mathrm{m}} = 5\,\mu\mathrm{M}$, the enzyme would be saturated at all normal tissue oxygen levels (5–60 μM), its rate nearly independent of oxygen, and HIF-α would respond only at severe hypoxia, as a switch rather than a graded sensor. The low affinity is thus the property that makes the enzyme report oxygen; the same principle (a sensor operating below saturation) applies to other metabolic sensors.

4. (Open question) Design a study to test whether populations of a lizard species along an altitudinal gradient on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau differ genetically in their heat tolerance and in the plasticity of their HSP expression.

   **Answer** Sample several populations at low, middle and high altitude; raise offspring (or acclimate adults for several weeks) in a common garden at two temperatures to remove most environmental effects. Measure critical thermal maximum and the induction of Hsp70 and other HSPs (by quantitative PCR or RNA-seq after controlled heat shocks at a range of temperatures), estimating $T_{\mathrm{on}}$ and the magnitude of induction; plot reaction norms of each population across the acclimation temperatures. Genetic differences in mean tolerance appear as differences between populations in the common garden; differences in plasticity appear as differences in the slopes of the reaction norms (genotype–environment interaction). Complement with genome scans for loci associated with altitude, a reciprocal transplant of eggs or juveniles if feasible, and control for confounding variables (body size, oxygen, UV) that also change with altitude.
