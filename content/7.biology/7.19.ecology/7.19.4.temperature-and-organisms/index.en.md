Temperature affects every organism, all the time. It sets the rate of every biochemical reaction, and therefore the rates of growth, development, movement and reproduction; it kills when it is too high or too low; and it acts as a signal that times germination, flowering and the end of diapause. Of all the conditions of 7.19.2, it is the one for which ecological effects are best understood and most widely relevant, and it is the condition most clearly changing at present, which makes the ecology of temperature central to predicting the effects of climate change.

This chapter treats temperature from the level of the individual to that of the species' range. We begin with the effect of temperature on rates, described by the thermal performance curve, and derive its consequences in variable environments; then development and the timing of the seasons; the thermal strategies of animals and their heat budgets; adaptations to cold and heat; temperature and the limits of distribution; and finally a striking case in which temperature decides the sex of an individual. The physiology of thermoregulation is treated in [7.14.31](/7.14.31), plant responses to cold and heat in [7.13.27](/7.13.27), the degree-day rule for insect development in [7.17.29](/7.17.29); here we build on them. The radiation and microclimates that determine the temperatures organisms experience were described in 7.19.3, and the effects of global warming are taken up in 7.19.47.

## 4.1. Temperature and the Rates of Life

### 4.1.1. $Q_{10}$ and the Arrhenius Relation

The rates of biological processes increase roughly exponentially with temperature over a wide range. The **temperature coefficient** $Q_{10}$ ([7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.3.1)) is the factor by which a rate increases for a rise of 10 °C; for most enzymatic processes and for the overall metabolic rate of animals, plants and microorganisms it lies between 2 and 3, so that a rise of 10 °C roughly doubles or triples the rate (Clarke 2017). The physical basis is the **Arrhenius equation** ([6.1.7](/6.1.7)): the rate constant of a reaction with activation energy $E$ is proportional to the Boltzmann factor $\mathrm{e}^{-E/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)}$, where $T$ is the absolute temperature. For two temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2 = T_1 + 10\,\mathrm{K}$,

$$
Q_{10} = \exp\left(\frac{10E}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_1T_2}\right).
$$
Metabolic rates of organisms from bacteria to mammals scale with temperature with an apparent activation energy averaging about 0.65 eV (Gillooly and colleagues 2001), which gives $Q_{10} = \exp[6.5/(8.617 \times 10^{-5} \times 288 \times 298)] \approx 2.4$ between 15 and 25 °C. The temperature dependence of metabolism is the basis of the metabolic theory of ecology (7.19.6), which combines it with the scaling of metabolism with body mass.

### 4.1.2. The Thermal Performance Curve

For an ecologist, the rate of a single reaction matters less than the performance of the whole organism: its rate of growth, sprint speed, feeding rate, fecundity or population growth rate. These all follow the same characteristic shape.

**Definition 4.1.1 (Thermal Performance Curve)** The function relating a measure of performance $r$ of an organism to its body temperature $T$ is its **thermal performance curve**. It is positive between a critical thermal minimum $\mathrm{CT}_{\min}$ and a critical thermal maximum $\mathrm{CT}_{\max}$, rises with temperature to a maximum $r_{\max}$ at the optimal temperature $T_{\mathrm{opt}}$ and then falls; the range of temperatures over which performance exceeds a given fraction of $r_{\max}$ (often 80%) is the performance breadth.

Thermal performance curves are almost always asymmetric (skewed to the left): performance rises slowly over a wide range below the optimum, approximately exponentially as the Arrhenius relation predicts, and falls steeply above it, typically reaching zero only a few degrees above $T_{\mathrm{opt}}$. This asymmetry has a mechanistic explanation. Above the optimum, the enzymes on which performance depends are increasingly inactivated (unfolded), and the inactivation itself is a reaction with a very high activation energy.

The Sharpe–Schoolfield model (Sharpe and DeMichele 1977; Schoolfield and colleagues 1981) makes this explicit. Suppose the rate-limiting enzyme exists in an active and an inactive state in rapid equilibrium, with equilibrium constant $K(T) = \exp[E_{\mathrm{h}}/k_{\mathrm{B}}\,(1/T_{\mathrm{h}} - 1/T)]$, where $E_{\mathrm{h}}$ is the enthalpy of inactivation and $T_{\mathrm{h}}$ the temperature at which half the enzyme is inactive. The fraction active is $1/(1 + K)$, and the active enzyme works at a rate given by the Arrhenius factor, so that

$$
r(T) = \frac{r_{\mathrm{ref}}\exp\left[\dfrac{E}{k_{\mathrm{B}}}\left(\dfrac{1}{T_{\mathrm{ref}}} - \dfrac{1}{T}\right)\right]}{1 + \exp\left[\dfrac{E_{\mathrm{h}}}{k_{\mathrm{B}}}\left(\dfrac{1}{T_{\mathrm{h}}} - \dfrac{1}{T}\right)\right]},
$$
with $r_{\mathrm{ref}}$ the rate at a reference temperature well below $T_{\mathrm{h}}$. (A second term in the denominator can be added for inactivation at low temperature.)

**Proposition 4.1.1 (Optimal Temperature in the Sharpe–Schoolfield Model)** If $E_{\mathrm{h}} > E > 0$, the function $r(T)$ has a unique maximum at

$$
\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{opt}}} = \frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{h}}} - \frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}}{E_{\mathrm{h}}}\ln\frac{E}{E_{\mathrm{h}} - E},
$$
at which the fraction of active enzyme is $1 - E/E_{\mathrm{h}}$. In particular $T_{\mathrm{opt}} < T_{\mathrm{h}}$ when $E < E_{\mathrm{h}}/2$.

**Proof** Let $u = 1/T$. Then $\ln r = \mathrm{const} - (E/k_{\mathrm{B}})u - \ln(1 + K)$ with $\ln K = (E_{\mathrm{h}}/k_{\mathrm{B}})(1/T_{\mathrm{h}} - u)$, so $\mathrm{d}K/\mathrm{d}u = -(E_{\mathrm{h}}/k_{\mathrm{B}})K$ and

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln r}{\mathrm{d}u} = -\frac{E}{k_{\mathrm{B}}} + \frac{E_{\mathrm{h}}}{k_{\mathrm{B}}}\frac{K}{1 + K}.
$$
The fraction $K/(1+K)$ decreases from 1 to 0 as $u$ increases from 0 to $\infty$ (as $T$ falls), so the derivative changes sign exactly once, at $K/(1+K) = E/E_{\mathrm{h}}$, that is at $K = E/(E_{\mathrm{h}} - E)$; since $u$ and $T$ are monotonically related, $r$ has a unique maximum there. Solving $\ln K = \ln[E/(E_{\mathrm{h}} - E)]$ for $u$ gives the formula, and the active fraction is $1/(1 + K) = 1 - E/E_{\mathrm{h}}$. If $E < E_{\mathrm{h}}/2$, the logarithm is negative and $1/T_{\mathrm{opt}} > 1/T_{\mathrm{h}}$. $\square$

For typical values $E = 0.65$ eV and $E_{\mathrm{h}} = 3.0$ eV, about 22% of the enzyme is already inactive at the optimum, and with $T_{\mathrm{h}} = 32$ °C the optimum is 28.6 °C. The large ratio $E_{\mathrm{h}}/E$ explains the steep fall above the optimum, and the proposition explains why optima lie only a few degrees below the temperatures at which performance collapses.

Thermal performance curves are measured by holding organisms at a series of constant temperatures and fitting a model such as this one by nonlinear least squares ([3.5](/3.5), [4.4.4](/4.4.4)); the asymmetric shape means that a quadratic or Gaussian curve fits poorly near the optimum.

### 4.1.3. Fluctuating Temperatures and Jensen's Inequality

In nature body temperature fluctuates, and the mean performance over a day is not the performance at the mean temperature. By **Jensen's inequality**, if $r$ is convex over the range of temperatures experienced, $\mathbb{E}[r(T)] \geq r(\mathbb{E}T)$, and if it is concave, the reverse. A second-order expansion around the mean $\bar T$ makes the effect quantitative:

$$
\mathbb{E}[r(T)] \approx r(\bar T) + \tfrac{1}{2}r''(\bar T)\operatorname{Var}(T).
$$
Since thermal performance curves are convex at low temperatures and concave near and above the optimum, fluctuations benefit an organism living on the cool side of its optimum and harm one living near or above it (figure above, panel B). For a sinusoidal daily cycle of amplitude $A$, $\operatorname{Var}(T) = A^2/2$. The same consideration makes development in fluctuating temperatures faster near the developmental threshold than at the constant mean (the Kaufmann effect, [7.17.29](/7.17.29)).

The consequences for climate change are direct. Deutsch and colleagues (2008) compared the thermal performance curves of insects, lizards and frogs with the temperatures of their habitats. At high latitudes, ectotherms live at temperatures well below their optima, so moderate warming increases their performance. In the tropics, where the seasonal temperature range is small, ectotherms have narrower thermal tolerances (Proposition 2.4.1 of [7.19.2](/7.19.2)) and live close to their optima; even the modest warming predicted for the tropics will take them above their optima, reducing fitness. The distance from the habitat temperature to the critical maximum, the *thermal safety margin* $\mathrm{CT}_{\max} - T_{\mathrm{hab}}$, is smallest in tropical lowland species, although the warming expected in the tropics is smaller than at high latitude. Increased temperature variability adds to the harm, because extreme heat events push organisms across the steep, concave part of the curve.

### 4.1.4. Temperature and Size

Growth and development respond differently to temperature. Development (progression through the stages of the life cycle) usually accelerates with temperature more than growth (increase in mass), so that ectotherms reared at higher temperatures mature earlier at a smaller size: the *temperature–size rule* (Atkinson 1994). It holds for more than 80% of the ectotherms studied, from protists to insects and fishes; in protists, final cell volume decreases by about 2.5% per °C of rearing temperature (Atkinson and colleagues 2003). The rule has consequences beyond the individual: in the Mediterranean, the maximum size of fishes declines along a gradient of sea surface temperature, most steeply in large, active species, and if the pattern is a plastic response to temperature, fishery yields will decline in a warmer sea (van Rijn and colleagues 2017). Together with the shifts in body size of endotherms (Section 4.4.1), decreasing body size has been called a third universal response to warming, alongside shifts in range and phenology (Gardner and colleagues 2011).

## 4.2. Development, Thermal Time and Phenology

### 4.2.1. Linear Development and Degree-Days

Over the middle part of the tolerance range, the rate of development $v = 1/N$, where $N$ is the duration of a stage in days, increases nearly linearly with temperature,

$$
v(T) = \frac{T - C}{K}, \qquad C < T < T_{\mathrm{opt}},
$$
so that the product $N(T - C) = K$ is constant: the **degree-day rule** ([7.17.29](/7.17.29)), also called the law of effective accumulated temperature. Here $C$ is the developmental threshold (biological zero) and $K$ the thermal constant of the stage in degree-days. For plants the same quantity is called **thermal time** ([7.13.17](/7.13.17)). Under varying temperatures the stage is completed when the accumulated degree-days $\sum_j \max(T_j - C, 0)$ reach $K$. Organisms thus do not require a certain time to develop but a combination of time and temperature, "physiological time". The predatory mite *Amblyseius californicus*, for example, with a threshold of 9.9 °C, took 24.22 days to develop from egg to adult at 15 °C and 8.18 days at 25 °C; both correspond to 123.5 degree-days ($24.22 \times 5.1 = 8.18 \times 15.1 = 123.5$; Hart and colleagues 2002). The estimation of $C$ and $K$ by regression, the nonlinear models that replace the linear rule near the threshold and the optimum, and the computation of degree-days from daily temperature cycles are treated in 7.17.29; the linear rule is the starting point for its ecological uses.

### 4.2.2. Ecological Uses of Thermal Time

If the degree-days above $C$ accumulated in a year at a site are $D$, an organism needing $K$ degree-days per generation can complete at most $D/K$ generations there. This simple ratio has four ecological applications (figure below).

- Voltinism. The number of generations per year of insects and other ectotherms increases towards the equator and with warming; the oriental migratory locust and the cotton bollworm complete more generations in southern than in northern China, and the potential number of generations is the first step in forecasting outbreaks.
- Geographic limits. Where $D < K$, a species cannot complete even one generation; the line where $D = K$ is a candidate for its poleward or upper altitudinal limit. Species may temporarily exceed it in warm years and during outbreaks, then retreat.
- Forecasting. From the observed date of one stage and a running sum of degree-days, the date of the next stage can be forecast, and control measures timed accordingly ([7.17.40](/7.17.40)).
- Agricultural zoning. The thermal time required by a crop cultivar from sowing to maturity is matched with the thermal time available at a site; in China, the accumulated temperature above 10 °C is the standard index for delimiting thermal zones of agriculture (7.13.17).

### 4.2.3. Phenology

*Phenology* is the study of the timing of recurrent biological events, such as budburst, flowering, leaf fall, the emergence of insects, the arrival and departure of migrants and the dates of breeding, and of their causes. It has a long tradition: Chinese farmers' calendars recorded the arrival of swallows and the flowering of plants for more than two thousand years, the meteorologist Zhu Kezhen founded the modern study of phenology in China (Zhu and Wan 1963), and in Europe phenological networks date from the 18th century. Hopkins (1918) formulated a "bioclimatic law" for North America: spring events are delayed by about four days for each degree of latitude northward, each five degrees of longitude eastward and each 120 m of altitude. The thermal-time model of the figure above reproduces a gradient of this order, because the date at which a degree-day sum is reached is set by the spring rise of temperature, which comes later at higher latitude.

Phenology is now one of the clearest indicators of climate change: across more than a thousand species, spring events have advanced by an average of 2.3 days per decade since the mid-20th century (Parmesan and Yohe 2003). Because species respond to different cues (temperature, day length) with different sensitivities, the advance is not the same for interacting species, and **phenological mismatch** ([7.18.20](/7.18.20)) between consumers and their food can result: a bird that times its breeding by day length or by conditions in its distant winter quarters may miss the peak of caterpillars, which advances with spring temperature. The population consequences of mismatch are discussed in 7.19.47.

## 4.3. Thermal Strategies of Animals

### 4.3.1. Ectotherms and Endotherms

Animals are classified by temperature in two ways, by the source of their body heat and by its constancy ([7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.1)). **Endotherms** produce most of their body heat by metabolism and regulate their body temperature with it; **ectotherms** obtain their body heat mainly from the environment. **Homeotherms** keep a nearly constant body temperature; **poikilotherms** let it vary with that of the environment. The popular terms "warm-blooded" and "cold-blooded" mix the two classifications and are misleading: a desert lizard basking in the sun may be warmer than a mammal, and a deep-sea fish is a poikilotherm with a perfectly constant body temperature because the deep sea is constant.

Neither classification is clear-cut, and the exceptions are ecologically informative.

- Endotherms that let their temperature fall. Many small birds and mammals enter **torpor**, an active lowering of metabolism and body temperature, to save energy. The black metaltail (*Metallura phoebe*), an Andean hummingbird with a normal body temperature near 39 °C, cools to as low as 3.3 °C on cold nights, the lowest recorded for a bird (Wolf and colleagues 2020). In **hibernation** body temperature approaches ambient temperature for weeks; arctic ground squirrels (*Urocitellus parryii*) supercool their body to −2.9 °C (Barnes 1989). Such animals are endotherms that become temporarily poikilothermic: their thermal strategy is **heterothermy**.
- Ectotherms that produce and retain heat. Bumblebees and many moths warm their flight muscles by shivering before take-off, and honeybees regulate the temperature of their brood within a degree; tunas and lamnid sharks keep their swimming muscles, eyes and brain warm by **countercurrent heat exchange**; brooding pythons raise their body temperature by shivering; thermogenic plants such as the eastern skunk cabbage and the sacred lotus warm their flowers. Large size alone conserves heat: the leatherback turtle (*Dermochelys coriacea*), with a mass of several hundred kilograms, keeps its body at about 25 °C in water of 7.5 °C (Frair and colleagues 1972) by its low surface-to-volume ratio, insulating fat and countercurrent exchangers in the flippers, whereas in tropical water its body is only 1–3 °C warmer than the water (**gigantothermy**).

Endothermy and ectothermy are two strategies with different costs and benefits. An endotherm maintains near-optimal performance over a wide range of environmental temperatures, at the cost of a high rate of energy expenditure: within its **thermoneutral zone** it metabolizes at its **basal metabolic rate**, and outside it more ([7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.3)). Even at rest, a mammal or bird uses roughly five to ten times as much energy as a reptile of the same mass at the same body temperature, and its field energy expenditure is more than an order of magnitude higher (Nagy 2005). The ecological consequence is a large food requirement: endotherms must eat far more than ectotherms of the same size, which limits their population densities and makes them less efficient converters of food into biomass (production efficiency, 7.19.43). Ectotherms, in turn, are inactive when it is cold and are restricted in the times and places of activity. Both strategies work: most environments, from hot deserts to the edge of the Antarctic ice, support both endotherms and ectotherms.

Thermal tolerance of endotherms also varies with climate. Khaliq and colleagues (2014) compiled the thermoneutral zones of hundreds of bird and mammal species and found, as the climate variability hypothesis predicts, that the breadth of the zone increases with the annual temperature range at the capture sites in birds; in mammals the relation was weak, possibly because many mammals use burrows and dens that buffer climate.

### 4.3.2. Heat Budgets and Operative Temperature

The body temperature of an organism is set by its heat budget. In steady state, the heat gained by absorbing radiation and by metabolism equals the heat lost by emitting radiation, by convection, by conduction to the substrate and by evaporation:

$$
R_{\mathrm{abs}} + M = \varepsilon\sigma T_{\mathrm{s}}^4 + h_{\mathrm{c}}(T_{\mathrm{s}} - T_{\mathrm{a}}) + G + \lambda E,
$$
per unit surface area, where $R_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is the absorbed solar and longwave radiation, $M$ the metabolic heat, $\varepsilon\sigma T_{\mathrm{s}}^4$ the emitted longwave radiation of a surface at temperature $T_{\mathrm{s}}$, $h_{\mathrm{c}}$ the convective heat transfer coefficient (which increases with wind speed and decreases with body size, roughly as $\sqrt{u/D}$ for wind speed $u$ and body diameter $D$), $T_{\mathrm{a}}$ the air temperature, $G$ the conduction to the substrate and $\lambda E$ the latent heat of evaporation. The budget is often written in the compact form $H_{\mathrm{s}} = H_{\mathrm{m}} \pm H_{\mathrm{cd}} \pm H_{\mathrm{cv}} \pm H_{\mathrm{r}} - H_{\mathrm{e}}$; the physics of the four routes is treated in [7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.2.1).

For a small ectotherm, metabolic heat and evaporation are negligible. Linearizing the radiative term around air temperature, $\varepsilon\sigma T_{\mathrm{s}}^4 \approx \varepsilon\sigma T_{\mathrm{a}}^4 + h_{\mathrm{r}}(T_{\mathrm{s}} - T_{\mathrm{a}})$ with $h_{\mathrm{r}} = 4\varepsilon\sigma T_{\mathrm{a}}^3 \approx 6\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$, and neglecting conduction, the steady body temperature is

$$
T_{\mathrm{e}} = T_{\mathrm{a}} + \frac{R_{\mathrm{abs}} - \varepsilon\sigma T_{\mathrm{a}}^4}{h_{\mathrm{r}} + h_{\mathrm{c}}}.
$$
This is the *operative temperature* (Bakken 1976): the temperature that an inanimate object of the same size, shape and color as the animal would reach at that place and time. It is measured with hollow copper models painted like the animal and placed in the field, and maps of operative temperature show which microhabitats are available to an ectotherm at each time of day. The operative temperature in full sun can exceed the air temperature by 10–20 °C, while in deep shade it is close to air temperature (figure below).

An ectotherm does not reach the operative temperature instantly. Its body temperature $T_{\mathrm{b}}$ approaches $T_{\mathrm{e}}$ according to Newton's law of cooling,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{b}}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{T_{\mathrm{e}} - T_{\mathrm{b}}}{\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{mc}{(h_{\mathrm{r}} + h_{\mathrm{c}})A},
$$
with mass $m$, specific heat $c$ and surface area $A$. Since $m/A \propto m^{1/3}$ and $h_{\mathrm{c}}$ decreases with size, the time constant $\tau$ increases roughly as $m^{1/2}$: a 10 g lizard heats or cools within minutes, a 100 kg tortoise or crocodile over many hours. Small ectotherms can therefore thermoregulate precisely by shuttling between sun and shade, while large ones integrate the environment over a day and maintain relatively constant temperatures by thermal inertia (gigantothermy, and probably the basis of the elevated body temperatures of large dinosaurs).

The model makes the value of **behavioral thermoregulation** quantitative. Hertz and colleagues (1993) proposed comparing the mean deviation $\bar d_{\mathrm{e}}$ of the operative temperatures available in a habitat from the preferred range with the mean deviation $\bar d_{\mathrm{b}}$ of the body temperatures actually achieved; the index $E = 1 - \bar d_{\mathrm{b}}/\bar d_{\mathrm{e}}$ is 0 for an animal that does not thermoregulate and approaches 1 for one that keeps its body temperature within the preferred range whatever the habitat offers. Lizards in open, sunny habitats have high values of $E$; species in forests, where the thermal environment is nearly uniform and close to the preferred range, often thermoregulate little.

Behavioral thermoregulation serves functions beyond performance. The clear-winged grasshopper (*Camnula pellucida*) of subalpine grasslands in Arizona basks to raise its body temperature to 38–40 °C, about 12 °C above air temperature; besides accelerating development, this high temperature inhibits the fungus *Entomophaga grylli*, which kills grasshoppers, and infected grasshoppers allowed to bask survive much better than those kept cool (Carruthers and colleagues 1992). Such behavioral fevers are widespread among ectotherms ([7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.4)).

## 4.4. Adaptations to Extreme Temperatures

### 4.4.1. Endotherms in the Cold: Bergmann's and Allen's Rules

Endotherms in cold climates reduce heat loss by insulation (fur, feathers, fat), by regional heterothermy and countercurrent exchange in the limbs, by behavior (huddling, burrows, migration, hibernation), and by increasing heat production (shivering and **nonshivering thermogenesis** in **brown adipose tissue**, driven by **thermogenin**, [7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.4.3)). Body shape also matters, and two biogeographic rules describe it.

**Bergmann's rule** (1847) states that within a group of related endotherms, species or populations in colder climates tend to be larger; **Allen's rule** (1877) that they tend to have shorter appendages (ears, tails, limbs, bills) ([7.14.31](/7.14.31#sec-31.6.8)). The tigers of the Russian Far East are much larger than the extinct Bali tigers, and the ears of the Arctic fox (*Vulpes lagopus*), the red fox (*Vulpes vulpes*) and the fennec (*Vulpes zerda*) of the Sahara increase in size in that order. The classical explanation is geometric: heat production scales with volume, heat loss with surface area, and the ratio of surface area to volume decreases with size as $L^{-1}$, so larger bodies and shorter extremities lose less heat per unit of heat production.

The rules are statistical tendencies, and their explanation is debated. Scholander (1955) objected that a change in insulation changes heat loss far more than a realistic change of size: the lower critical temperature of an animal depends much more on its fur than on its mass (7.14.31). Other explanations of larger size in the cold include greater fasting endurance (fat reserves scale with mass faster than metabolic rate), seasonality of food supply and the productivity of the environment. The rules are nevertheless supported by large comparative analyses. Across thousands of bird and mammal species, body mass increases with latitude in most orders, significantly so in three mammalian orders (armadillos, even-toed ungulates, primates) and five avian orders (game birds, pelicans and herons, owls, hawks and eagles, woodpeckers) (He and colleagues 2023); bills and limbs follow Allen's rule in many birds and mammals (Symonds and Tattersall 2010).

If the rules reflect thermal selection or plasticity, a warming climate should make endotherms smaller and their appendages longer, and this is being observed. Weeks and colleagues (2020) measured 70,716 specimens of 52 species of North American migratory birds killed by collisions with buildings in Chicago between 1978 and 2016. Over these four decades, tarsus length decreased by 2.4% and body mass by 2.6%, while wing length increased by 1.3%; the decline in size was best predicted by the rise in summer temperatures on the breeding grounds. Similar "shape-shifting", with bills, ears and tails becoming relatively longer, is reported for many species (Ryding and colleagues 2021). A related pattern, *Gloger's rule*, states that endotherms in warm, humid regions tend to be more darkly pigmented than those in cool, dry ones; darker plumage and fur protect against ultraviolet radiation and against feather-degrading bacteria, which are more abundant in humid climates.

### 4.4.2. Ectotherms and Plants in the Cold

At low temperatures above freezing, many tropical and subtropical organisms suffer **chilling injury** ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)), damage caused by the loss of membrane function and ionic homeostasis: bananas blacken, rainforest trees and many insects are injured at temperatures well above 0 °C. Below 0 °C, **freezing injury** threatens. Ice seldom forms at 0 °C, because water in small volumes can supercool, remaining liquid well below its freezing point until ice forms around a nucleating particle; once extracellular ice forms, the remaining solution becomes concentrated, water is drawn out of the cells, and the cells are damaged by dehydration. Ice forming inside cells is almost always lethal.

Ectotherms survive the cold by two main strategies.

- Freeze avoidance. The animal keeps its body fluids liquid by **supercooling**, removing ice-nucleating agents from its body, accumulating low-molecular-weight cryoprotectants such as glycerol and sorbitol, and producing **antifreeze proteins** that bind to the surface of small ice crystals and stop them from growing. Larvae of the goldenrod gall moth *Epiblema scudderiana* convert almost all their glycogen into glycerol in autumn, until it makes up over 19% of their body mass, and lower their supercooling point from −14 °C in late summer to −38 °C in midwinter (Rickards and colleagues 1987). Antarctic notothenioid fishes and Arctic cod live in seawater at −1.9 °C thanks to antifreeze glycoproteins; in the winter flounder (*Pseudopleuronectes americanus*), the well-known type I antifreeze protein lowers the freezing point of the blood only to about −1.5 °C, and a "hyperactive" antifreeze protein discovered later explains its survival in ice-laden water at −1.9 °C (Marshall and colleagues 2004).
- Freeze tolerance. The animal allows controlled freezing of its extracellular water, initiated by ice-nucleating proteins at a relatively high temperature, while protecting the cells with cryoprotectants. The North American wood frog survives with about two-thirds of its body water frozen, its heart stopped, by flooding its cells with glucose (7.14.31).

Two further strategies have been described: cryoprotective dehydration, in which soil invertebrates lose nearly all freezable water to the surrounding frozen soil, and vitrification, in which the body fluids of larvae of the Alaskan red flat bark beetle (*Cucujus clavipes*) become a glass, allowing survival to −100 °C (Sformo and colleagues 2010).

A quantitative check shows why antifreeze mechanisms are needed in addition to cryoprotectants. The colligative **freezing-point depression** of an aqueous solution is $\Delta T = K_{\mathrm{f}}b$, with $K_{\mathrm{f}} = 1.86\,\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$ for water and $b$ the molality. Glycerol making up 19% of the mass of a caterpillar whose body is about 60% water gives $b = (190\,\mathrm{g} / 92\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}})/0.6\,\mathrm{kg} \approx 3.4\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ per kilogram of body mass, and a depression of the freezing point of only about 6 K. The observed supercooling point of −38 °C is far below this; it results from the combined action of polyols, antifreeze proteins and the elimination of ice nucleators, which allow the fluids to remain supercooled far below their equilibrium freezing point.

Plants of cold climates protect their overwintering buds with bud scales, which leave scars on the twig when they fall in spring; they have waxy or hairy surfaces, cushion and prostrate growth forms that stay within the warmer boundary layer near the ground (7.19.3), and they acquire freezing tolerance by **cold acclimation** in autumn. Frost resistance differs among organs and seasons: in the woody plants of the Alps, summer frost kills reproductive shoots at about −4.6 °C and immature leaves at −5.0 °C, whereas in winter vegetative buds survive −30 to −50 °C and stems −30 to −70 °C (Neuner 2014). The most frost-sensitive organ in the growing season may set the upper altitudinal limit of a species.

### 4.4.3. Heat

Lethal high temperatures usually lie only a few degrees above the optimum (Proposition 4.1.1): enzymes are inactivated, membranes become too fluid, and in aquatic animals oxygen supply cannot keep pace with the rising demand (the oxygen- and capacity-limited thermal tolerance hypothesis of Pörtner 2001). Few animals survive body temperatures above about 45–47 °C; most mammals die at body temperatures of 42–44 °C, birds tolerate a few degrees more. Organisms cope with heat by:

- Morphology: reflective, hairy or waxy surfaces, spines that shade the body of cacti, vertical leaf orientation that reduces the radiation intercepted at midday, and the long appendages that dissipate heat in desert mammals (Allen's rule).
- Physiology: evaporative cooling, by transpiration in plants (the desert perennial *Tidestromia oblongifolia* in Death Valley keeps its leaves at 40–45 °C at an air temperature near 50 °C by rapid transpiration; Berry and Björkman 1980), sweating and panting in animals; tolerance of high tissue temperatures (desert succulents survive tissue temperatures above 60 °C); the synthesis of **heat shock proteins** ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)); and adaptive hyperthermia, storing heat by day as camels and oryxes do (7.19.2).
- Behavior: retreating into shade and burrows, nocturnal activity, aestivation.

The upper limit of life is held by microorganisms. **Hyperthermophiles** among the archaea grow optimally above 80 °C; *Pyrococcus furiosus* from geothermal sediments grows best at 100 °C, and *Methanopyrus kandleri* strain 116, from a deep-sea hydrothermal vent, grows at 122 °C under high pressure (Takai and colleagues 2008). Around the vents a rich eukaryotic fauna lives in the gradient between the 350 °C vent fluids and the 2 °C deep sea.

### 4.4.4. Periodic Temperature Changes

Many organisms are adapted not only to the level but also to the rhythm of temperature. The growth of many plants is better under alternating day and night temperatures than at a constant temperature (**thermoperiodism**, [7.13.17](/7.13.17)); many seeds require alternating temperatures to germinate; and many temperate plants require a prolonged period of cold before they flower (**vernalization**) or before their buds and seeds break dormancy, a physiological record that winter has passed. In the oases of Xinjiang, the large daily range of temperature, with hot sunny days and cool nights, contributes to the high yield and sugar content of crops such as grapes and melons: photosynthesis proceeds rapidly by day, while respiration, which consumes sugar, is slow at night. As Section 4.1.3 showed, the effect of fluctuating temperatures on performance depends on the position on the thermal performance curve, so that fluctuations help in some ranges and harm in others.

## 4.5. Temperature and Distribution

The ranges of many species coincide with isotherms. The northern limit of wild madder (*Rubia peregrina*) in Britain follows the January isotherm of 4.5 °C; the high-altitude limit of trees (the treeline) on mountains from the subarctic to the equator lies where the mean ground temperature of the growing season is about 6.7 ± 0.8 °C (Körner and Paulsen 2004), although the tree species differ completely; the commercial cultivation of coffee is limited by the 13 °C isotherm of the coldest month. In China, the cultivation of rubber trees (*Hevea brasiliensis*), a tropical species, at the northern edge of the tropics in southern Yunnan and Hainan is limited by the occasional cold waves that damage the trees.

Such correlations must be interpreted carefully (7.19.1). The isotherms on a map are computed from standard meteorological measurements, but organisms experience microclimates that may be several degrees warmer or colder; the prostrate shrub *Dryas octopetala* grows only above 650 m in north Wales, near its southern limit, but at sea level in northern Scotland. Many limits are set by rare extremes rather than means, because an individual needs to be killed only once: the saguaro cactus (*Carnegiea gigantea*) is killed if temperatures stay below freezing for 36 hours, and the northern and eastern edges of its range in Arizona correspond to the places where, on occasional days, it fails to thaw. Temperature also acts through other species: the rush moth *Coleophora alticolella* in England is little affected by the cold above 600 m, but its food plant, the rush *Juncus squarrosus*, fails to ripen seeds there, and the moth's caterpillars starve; two stream charrs on Hokkaido replace each other along an altitudinal gradient because temperature reverses the outcome of competition between them (7.19.22). Correlation becomes evidence of cause when the limits coincide with physiological thresholds: the warm range boundaries of nine Australian fishes correspond closely to their optimal temperatures for activity and growth (Payne and colleagues 2016).

At the global scale, the thermal tolerance breadth of terrestrial ectotherms increases with latitude, mostly because the critical minimum decreases: cold tolerance varies much more among species than heat tolerance, whose upper limits are similar from the tropics to the temperate zone (Sunday and colleagues 2011). Tropical species thus live closer to their limits and have less room to warm, which is the reason for their particular vulnerability to climate change (Section 4.1.3). Mechanistic models that combine thermal tolerances, operative temperatures and degree-day requirements to predict ranges are treated with species distribution models in 7.19.49.

## 4.6. Temperature-Dependent Sex Determination

### 4.6.1. The Pattern

In many turtles, all crocodilians, the tuatara and some lizards and fishes, the sex of an individual is not fixed by sex chromosomes but decided by the temperature at which the egg is incubated: **temperature-dependent sex determination** ([7.4.6](/7.4.6), [7.5.26](/7.5.26)). In most turtles low temperatures produce males and high temperatures females; in crocodilians and some turtles females develop at both extremes and males at intermediate temperatures. Temperature acts during a thermosensitive period in the middle third of incubation. The proportion of females as a function of constant incubation temperature, the **norm of reaction** of sex, is a steep sigmoid curve characterized by the pivotal temperature $T_{\mathrm{p}}$, which produces a 1:1 **sex ratio**, and the transitional range of temperatures over which both sexes are produced. A logistic model,

$$
p(T) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp[-(T - T_{\mathrm{p}})/s]},
$$
fits such data well ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)); the transitional range from 5% to 95% females is $T_{\mathrm{p}} \pm s\ln 19$. For green turtles the pivotal temperature is close to 29 °C in most populations studied, and the transitional range only one to two degrees on either side.

### 4.6.2. The Molecular Switch

How does temperature determine sex? In the red-eared slider (*Trachemys scripta elegans*), males develop at 26 °C and females at 32 °C. Ge and colleagues (2018) showed that the histone demethylase KDM6B is expressed more at the male-producing temperature; it removes the repressive mark of trimethylation of lysine 27 of histone H3 (H3K27me3) from the promoter of *Dmrt1*, a key male-determining gene, and thereby activates its transcription. Knocking down *Kdm6b* in embryos at 26 °C caused them to develop as females, and restoring *Dmrt1* expression rescued male development. Upstream of this, Weber and colleagues (2020) found that at the female-producing temperature, an influx of calcium ions into gonadal cells activates the transcription factor STAT3 by phosphorylation, and phosphorylated STAT3 represses *Kdm6b*. Temperature thus acts through a chain from calcium signaling to chromatin modification to a sex-determining gene, a mechanism in which the environment directly controls the epigenetic state of a developmental switch.

### 4.6.3. Fluctuating Nest Temperatures

In nature nest temperatures fluctuate daily, and the sex ratio depends on more than the mean temperature. Since embryos develop faster when warm, the warm part of each day contributes more development, and the temperature that determines sex is a development-weighted average (the "constant temperature equivalent" of Georges 1989).

**Proposition 4.6.1 (Development-Weighted Nest Temperature)** Let the nest temperature be $T(t) = m + A\sin\omega t$, with $m - A > C$, and let the development rate be linear, $v(T) = T - C$ above a threshold $C$. Then the mean temperature weighted by development over a cycle is

$$
T_{\mathrm{w}} = \frac{\int_0^{2\pi/\omega} T\,v(T)\,\mathrm{d}t}{\int_0^{2\pi/\omega} v(T)\,\mathrm{d}t} = m + \frac{A^2}{2(m - C)}.
$$

**Proof** Over a full cycle the means of $\sin\omega t$ and $\sin^2\omega t$ are $0$ and $1/2$. Hence the mean of $v(T) = (m - C) + A\sin\omega t$ is $m - C$, and the mean of $T\,v(T) = m(m - C) + A(2m - C)\sin\omega t + A^2\sin^2\omega t$ is $m(m - C) + A^2/2$. Dividing gives $T_{\mathrm{w}} = m + A^2/[2(m - C)]$. $\square$

The weighted temperature exceeds the mean, by an amount that grows with the square of the daily amplitude: with $m = 28.5$ °C, $C = 22$ °C and $A = 4$ °C, the shift is $16/13 \approx 1.2$ °C, enough to change the outcome from mostly male to mostly female for a species with a pivotal temperature of 29.3 °C (figure below). Nests in open, sunny sand with large daily ranges therefore produce more females than shaded nests with the same mean temperature, an instance of the general effect of nonlinearity (Section 4.1.3).

### 4.6.4. Sea Turtles in a Warming Climate

Because the transitional range is narrow, a warming of one or two degrees can turn a population from producing both sexes to producing almost only females. The green turtle (*Chelonia mydas*), listed as Endangered by the IUCN and a first-class protected species in China, shows that this is happening. Raine Island in the northern Great Barrier Reef is the largest green turtle rookery in the world. Jensen and colleagues (2018), combining endocrine and genetic methods to determine the sex and natal origin of turtles caught at foraging grounds, found that among turtles from the northern Great Barrier Reef rookeries, 99.1% of juveniles, 99.8% of subadults and 86.8% of adults were female: the rookery has produced almost only females for at least two decades, as sand temperatures have risen. Warm incubation also lowers hatching success and hatchling quality, and rising sea levels flood nests on low islands, so that the effects of warming go beyond the sex ratio (Blechschmidt and colleagues 2020).

Whether such populations will decline depends on several factors examined in 7.19.47: because males can mate with many females, a population with a strongly female-biased sex ratio may grow faster for a time, but at the extreme a lack of males limits fertilization; turtles may shift their nesting dates or sites; and the pivotal temperature itself may evolve, although slowly in animals with generation times of decades. Managers on Raine Island and elsewhere have begun to cool nesting beaches by shading and irrigation, and to relocate nests. The evolutionary question of why temperature-dependent sex determination exists at all, given these risks, is treated in [7.10.13](/7.10.13).

## Summary

- Rates of biological processes rise exponentially with temperature ($Q_{10} \approx 2$–$3$; Arrhenius with $E \approx 0.65$ eV). Whole-organism performance follows a left-skewed thermal performance curve (Definition 4.1.1); the Sharpe–Schoolfield model derives it from reversible enzyme inactivation, with a unique optimum a few degrees below collapse (Proposition 4.1.1).
- In fluctuating temperatures mean performance differs from performance at the mean (Jensen's inequality): fluctuations help on the convex, cool side of the curve and harm near and above the optimum. Tropical ectotherms live close to their optima and have small thermal safety margins. Ectotherms reared warmer are smaller (temperature–size rule).
- Development rate is nearly linear in temperature over the middle range, giving the degree-day rule $N(T - C) = K$; degree-days predict voltinism, range limits, the dates of stages and agricultural zones, and the latitudinal delay of spring events (Hopkins' law). Spring phenology has advanced by about 2.3 days per decade; uneven advances cause phenological mismatch.

| Strategy | Heat source | Body temperature | Examples and exceptions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Endotherm, homeotherm | Metabolism | Constant | Birds, mammals; torpor and hibernation (heterothermy) |
| Ectotherm, poikilotherm | Environment | Variable | Most fishes, amphibians, reptiles, invertebrates |
| Ectotherm, near-homeotherm | Environment (behavior, inertia) | Fairly constant | Shuttling lizards; leatherback turtle (gigantothermy) |
| Regional endotherm | Metabolism, countercurrent exchange | Warm core only | Tunas, lamnid sharks, bumblebees in flight |

- Heat budgets determine the operative temperature $T_{\mathrm{e}}$, the temperature an inanimate model would reach; body temperature approaches it with a time constant growing as about $m^{1/2}$. Small ectotherms thermoregulate by shuttling, large ones by inertia.
- Cold: Bergmann's and Allen's rules (supported by comparative analyses; birds are becoming smaller and longer-winged with warming), Gloger's rule; chilling and freezing injury; freeze avoidance (supercooling, glycerol, antifreeze proteins) and freeze tolerance, cryoprotective dehydration and vitrification; plant bud scales, growth forms and cold acclimation. Heat: lethal limits a few degrees above the optimum, evaporative cooling, heat shock proteins; hyperthermophiles grow up to 122 °C.
- Distributions follow isotherms, but limits are often set by extremes, microclimates and other species; ectotherm tolerance breadth increases with latitude mainly through cold tolerance.
- In temperature-dependent sex determination the sex ratio is a steep function of incubation temperature (pivotal temperature, transitional range); in the red-eared slider temperature acts through Ca²⁺–STAT3 signaling and the H3K27 demethylase KDM6B on *Dmrt1*. Daily fluctuations raise the effective temperature by $A^2/[2(m - C)]$ (Proposition 4.6.1). Northern Great Barrier Reef green turtles are now almost all female.

## Questions

1. The respiration rate of a beetle is 0.20 mL O₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹ at 12 °C and 0.46 at 22 °C. Compute $Q_{10}$ and the apparent activation energy. Predict the rate at 30 °C, and explain why the prediction may fail.

   **Answer** $Q_{10} = 0.46/0.20 = 2.3$. From $Q_{10} = \exp[10E/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T_1T_2)]$ with $T_1T_2 = 285.15 \times 295.15 = 84\,163\,\mathrm{K^2}$: $E = k_{\mathrm{B}}T_1T_2\ln Q_{10}/10 = 8.617 \times 10^{-5} \times 84\,163 \times 0.833/10 \approx 0.60$ eV. Extrapolating, the rate at 30 °C is $0.46 \times 2.3^{0.8} \approx 0.89$. The prediction may fail because 30 °C may be near or above the optimum of the beetle's thermal performance curve, where the high-temperature inactivation term of the Sharpe–Schoolfield model reduces the rate below the Arrhenius extrapolation; respiration of the Colorado potato beetle, for example, doubles per 10 °C up to 20 °C but increases more slowly above.

2. Using Proposition 4.1.1, show that increasing the enthalpy of inactivation $E_{\mathrm{h}}$ (more cooperative unfolding) at fixed $T_{\mathrm{h}}$ moves the optimum closer to $T_{\mathrm{h}}$, and interpret.

   **Answer** $1/T_{\mathrm{opt}} - 1/T_{\mathrm{h}} = -(k_{\mathrm{B}}/E_{\mathrm{h}})\ln[E/(E_{\mathrm{h}} - E)] = (k_{\mathrm{B}}/E_{\mathrm{h}})\ln[(E_{\mathrm{h}} - E)/E]$. As $E_{\mathrm{h}}$ increases, the logarithm grows only logarithmically while the prefactor falls as $1/E_{\mathrm{h}}$, so the difference tends to zero (for large $E_{\mathrm{h}}$ it behaves as $(k_{\mathrm{B}}/E_{\mathrm{h}})\ln(E_{\mathrm{h}}/E) \to 0$). A sharper (more cooperative) inactivation lets performance keep rising almost to $T_{\mathrm{h}}$ and then collapse abruptly: the curve becomes more asymmetric, and the margin between optimum and failure shrinks. Organisms with such curves gain performance from warming until shortly before a catastrophic loss, which makes them vulnerable to heat waves.

3. An insect pest has a developmental threshold of 10 °C and needs 350 degree-days per generation. At site X the accumulated degree-days above 10 °C are 1800 per year; at site Y 1100. How many generations are possible at each? If warming adds 1 °C to every day of a season in which the temperature exceeds 10 °C on 200 days, how many more generations result at Y?

   **Answer** Site X: $1800/350 \approx 5.1$, so about five generations; site Y: $1100/350 \approx 3.1$, three generations. Warming by 1 °C on 200 days adds about 200 degree-days (more, because some days previously below 10 °C now contribute), so Y has about 1300 degree-days, $1300/350 \approx 3.7$: a partial fourth generation becomes possible in favorable years. Whether it is realized depends on diapause induction by photoperiod (7.19.3): if the insect enters diapause at a fixed day length, the extra degree-days late in the season may not be used, or a fourth generation may be started that cannot complete before winter.

4. Why is a large ectotherm such as a Galápagos tortoise or a crocodile able to keep a relatively constant body temperature without much behavioral thermoregulation, whereas a small lizard must shuttle between sun and shade? Use the time constant $\tau$.

   **Answer** The time constant $\tau = mc/[(h_{\mathrm{r}} + h_{\mathrm{c}})A]$ increases with size, roughly as $m^{1/2}$: a 10 g lizard has $\tau$ of minutes, a 100 kg tortoise of many hours. With $\tau$ much shorter than the period of environmental change, the small lizard's body temperature follows the operative temperature of its microhabitat almost immediately, so it must choose microhabitats continually. With $\tau$ comparable to or longer than a day, the large animal's body temperature responds to the daily average of operative temperature, and its daily fluctuation is damped by a factor of about $1/\sqrt{1 + (\omega\tau)^2}$ (the response of a first-order system to sinusoidal forcing). Its body temperature is therefore nearly constant without behavior, though it cannot change quickly when needed.

5. Weeks and colleagues found that birds have become smaller but longer-winged over 40 years. Suggest two hypotheses for the change in body size and one for the change in wing length, and say how they could be distinguished.

   **Answer** Body size: (a) plastic response to warmer temperatures during growth (the temperature–size rule, or reduced food quality in heat), (b) natural selection for smaller size, which dissipates heat more easily in warmer summers (a Bergmann's-rule mechanism). Wing length: smaller birds may need relatively longer wings to maintain the flight efficiency required for long migrations, so selection on migratory performance compensates for decreasing mass. Plasticity and selection can be distinguished by common-garden or rearing experiments (do birds raised at different temperatures differ in size?), by quantitative-genetic analyses of long-term pedigreed populations (has the breeding value of size changed?), and by comparing the timing of the response with temperatures during the nestling period versus survival-related selection in adults.

6. A gardener notices that a hardy shrub survived −25 °C in January but its new shoots were killed by a frost of −5 °C in May. Explain.

   **Answer** Frost resistance is acquired by cold acclimation in autumn, as days shorten and temperatures fall: tissues lose water, accumulate sugars and protective proteins, and membranes change composition, so that in midwinter buds and stems tolerate very low temperatures. In spring the plant deacclimates as it resumes growth; young, expanding shoots full of water have very little frost resistance (often only to −3 to −5 °C), so a late frost kills them. The most sensitive stage of the year, not the coldest temperature of the year, often limits plants, which is why late spring frosts are so damaging to orchards.

7. Using Proposition 4.6.1, explain why turtle nests in open sand on a beach with large daily temperature ranges can produce more females than shaded nests of the same mean temperature. What does the proposition predict if the development threshold $C$ is close to the mean temperature?

   **Answer** Development proceeds faster when it is warm, so the warm hours of each day contribute more to development during the thermosensitive period than the cool hours; the effective temperature is the development-weighted mean, which exceeds the arithmetic mean by $A^2/[2(m - C)]$. A large daily amplitude $A$ thus raises the effective temperature above the pivotal temperature even when the mean is below it, producing females. If $C$ is close to $m$, the denominator is small and the shift is large: near the threshold almost all development occurs in the warm part of the day, so the effective temperature approaches the daily maximum. (The formula requires $m - A > C$; if temperatures fall below the threshold for part of the day, those hours contribute no development at all, and the effect is stronger still.)

8. (Open question) Evaluate the management options for the feminization of sea turtle populations: shading or irrigating nesting beaches, relocating nests to hatcheries, or doing nothing. What data would be needed to choose?

   **Answer** Points to consider. Shading and irrigation lower sand temperatures and can restore male production locally, as trials on Raine Island and elsewhere show, but they are labor-intensive, may change hatching success, and must be sustained indefinitely. Hatcheries allow control of temperature but risk producing too many males, may lower hatchling fitness and disrupt natural selection on nest-site choice. Doing nothing relies on the population's ability to adapt (nesting earlier or in cooler sites, evolution of the pivotal temperature) and on the fact that a female-biased sex ratio may not limit reproduction until males become very rare. Data needed: the actual operational sex ratio of breeding adults and whether females remain fertilized; sand temperatures and their projection under warming; hatching success and hatchling quality at different temperatures; the heritability of the pivotal temperature and of nesting behavior; and population models that combine these (7.19.14, 7.19.15). The choice involves trade-offs between short-term intervention and long-term adaptive capacity.
