Nearly all energy used by life comes from the Sun. Solar radiation drives photosynthesis, warms the surface of the Earth unequally and so drives the winds and ocean currents that determine climate, and its daily and seasonal rhythms provide organisms with the most reliable clock in their environment. This chapter follows solar energy from the top of the atmosphere to the organism: how much arrives at each latitude and season, how the unequal heating creates the climates of the Earth, how microclimates arise near the ground and in water, how light acts on plants and animals, and how day length is used as a seasonal signal.

The chapter is the first of four on the physical environment, after the general framework of 7.19.2. Temperature, the most important condition for organisms, follows in 7.19.4, and water and the other physical factors in 7.19.5. The physiology of photosynthesis and of plant responses to light is treated in plant physiology ([7.13.7](/7.13.7)–[7.13.9](/7.13.9), [7.13.16](/7.13.16), [7.13.21](/7.13.21)), the biological clock in animal behavior ([7.18.18](/7.18.18)); here we treat their ecological consequences. The climatic patterns described here explain the distribution of biomes (7.19.41), and the energy budget of the Earth is developed into a model of the greenhouse effect in 7.19.47.

## 3.1. Solar Radiation and the Energy Budget of the Earth

### 3.1.1. The Solar Constant

The Sun radiates approximately as a **black body** at about 5770 K. At the mean distance of the Earth from the Sun, the flux of solar radiation on a surface perpendicular to the rays outside the atmosphere, the total solar irradiance or solar constant, is $S_0 \approx 1361\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ (Kopp and Lean 2011); it varies by only about 0.1% over the 11-year sunspot cycle. The Earth intercepts this flux over its cross-section $\pi R^2$ but spreads it over its surface $4\pi R^2$, so the mean flux at the top of the atmosphere is $S_0/4 \approx 340\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. The fraction of the Sun's total output that reaches the Earth is

$$
\frac{\pi R^2}{4\pi d^2} = \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{6.37 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}}{1.50 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}}\right)^2 \approx 4.5 \times 10^{-10},
$$
about one part in two billion. Of the energy reaching the Earth, about 30% is reflected back to space by clouds, aerosols and the surface (the planetary albedo); about 23% is absorbed in the atmosphere and about 47% at the surface (Trenberth and colleagues 2009). Photosynthesis captures only about 0.1% of the solar energy reaching the Earth: global terrestrial and marine net primary production, about $1.0 \times 10^{17}$ g of carbon per year, stores about $4 \times 10^{21}$ J per year, against $5.5 \times 10^{24}$ J of solar radiation intercepted. On this small fraction all ecosystems depend (7.19.42).

The absorbed energy is returned to space as infrared radiation. A planet in radiative balance at the mean absorbed flux of $(1 - 0.30)S_0/4 \approx 240\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ has, by the **Stefan–Boltzmann law**, an effective radiating temperature of $T_{\mathrm{e}} = [(1-\alpha)S_0/(4\sigma)]^{1/4} \approx 255\,\mathrm{K}$, about 33 K below the observed mean surface temperature of about 288 K; the difference is the greenhouse effect of water vapor, carbon dioxide and other gases (7.19.47).

### 3.1.2. The Solar Spectrum

By **Wien's displacement law** the solar spectrum peaks near 500 nm, in the visible, whereas the Earth, at about 288 K, radiates mainly between 4 and 50 μm with a peak near 10 μm; solar radiation is therefore called shortwave and terrestrial radiation longwave, and the two barely overlap. Solar radiation is divided by wavelength into:

| Band | Wavelength | Share at the surface | Ecological significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ultraviolet C | 100–280 nm | None (absorbed by O₂ and ozone) | Lethal to cells |
| Ultraviolet B | 280–315 nm | Small (mostly absorbed by ozone) | Damages DNA and proteins; vitamin D synthesis |
| Ultraviolet A | 315–400 nm | A few percent | Seen by many insects, birds and fishes |
| Visible (photosynthetically active) | 400–700 nm | About 45% | Vision; photosynthesis |
| Infrared | > 700 nm | About 50% | Heats organisms and surroundings; far-red (700–800 nm) acts as a signal to phytochrome |

The band boundaries follow the CIE and ISO 21348 conventions (some textbooks use 380 and 760 nm for the limits of the visible). The band of **photosynthetically active radiation** ([7.13.7](/7.13.7)), 400–700 nm, coincides roughly with the range visible to humans; about 55% of the solar energy reaching the surface lies outside it (Begon and Townsend 2021). Ultraviolet radiation would be lethal to life on land if it were not absorbed by the ozone layer (7.19.47); its absorption was one of the consequences of the oxygenation of the atmosphere (7.19.2).

### 3.1.3. Insolation by Latitude and Season

Because the Earth's axis is tilted by 23.44° to the plane of its orbit, the latitude at which the Sun stands overhead at noon, the solar declination $\delta$, moves between the tropics through the year. A convenient approximation is

$$
\delta \approx 23.44^\circ \sin\left(\frac{2\pi(284 + n)}{365}\right),
$$
where $n$ is the day of the year. At latitude $\varphi$ the Sun's elevation at noon is $90^\circ - \lvert\varphi - \delta\rvert$, and the flux on a horizontal surface at the top of the atmosphere is $S_0\sin\beta$ when the Sun has elevation $\beta$. Integrating over the hours of daylight gives the daily mean insolation at the top of the atmosphere,

$$
Q = \frac{S_0}{\pi}\left(\frac{\bar d}{d}\right)^2\left(h_0\sin\varphi\sin\delta + \cos\varphi\cos\delta\sin h_0\right), \qquad \cos h_0 = -\tan\varphi\tan\delta,
$$
where $h_0$ is the hour angle of sunset (in radians) and $(\bar d/d)^2 \approx 1 + 0.033\cos(2\pi n/365)$ corrects for the eccentricity of the orbit. Two factors combine: the angle of the rays (the same beam is spread over a larger area when the Sun is low) and the length of the day. The figure below shows the result. The annual total decreases from the equator to the poles, but its seasonal amplitude increases: at the summer solstice the polar regions, lit for 24 hours, receive more energy per day at the top of the atmosphere than the equator does at any time. The near-symmetric pattern is modified at the surface, because low Sun angles mean long paths through the atmosphere (by the **Beer–Lambert law**, the transmitted fraction is $\tau^{m}$ with the optical air mass $m \approx 1/\sin\beta$), and because snow and ice reflect much of what arrives.

![Astronomical controls of radiation and day length (computed). A: daily mean insolation at the top of the atmosphere as a function of latitude and date (S₀ = 1361 W m⁻²); the dashed line is the latitude at which the Sun stands overhead at noon. Insolation is high and nearly constant through the year at the equator; at high latitudes it is strongly seasonal, with polar summer maxima above 500 W m⁻² and months of polar night. Local topography modifies the radiation received. A slope facing the equator receives more radiation than level ground, and a slope facing the pole less: at 30° N a south-facing slope receives more than level ground, which receives more than a north-facing slope, and the difference is largest in winter, when the Sun is low. Because the angle of the slope is equivalent to a change of latitude, a south-facing slope of 10° at 40° N receives roughly the radiation of level ground at 30° N. The contrast between equator- and pole-facing slopes shapes vegetation from the scale of a hill (forest on the shaded, moister northern slope and grassland or shrubland on the sunny southern slope of hills in northern China) to the scale of a mountain range. Radiation also increases with altitude, because the air above is thinner and drier, and ultraviolet radiation especially so.

## 3.2. The Formation of Climate

### 3.2.1. The General Circulation

The unequal heating of the globe produces a surplus of absorbed radiation in the tropics and a deficit at high latitudes; atmosphere and oceans carry the surplus poleward. In the tropics, warm moist air rises in the intertropical convergence zone (ITCZ), cools, and releases its moisture as heavy rain; this is why the equatorial belt is wet. The air, now dry, flows poleward aloft and sinks at about 30° N and S, where it is compressed and warmed; the subtropical high-pressure belts at these latitudes are dry, and the great deserts (Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari, Australian) lie there. The circulation between the equator and 30° is the Hadley cell. Between about 30° and 60°, the mid-latitude (Ferrel) circulation brings westerly winds and the polar front, where warm and cold air masses meet and cyclones produce abundant rain; near the poles, cold, dense air sinks, and the polar regions are dry deserts of ice. Because of the rotation of the Earth, the **Coriolis force** turns the surface flows into the north-east and south-east trade winds of the tropics and the westerlies of mid-latitudes.

The ITCZ follows the Sun, moving north in the northern summer and south in the southern summer, and produces the wet and dry seasons of the tropics. Over large continents the seasonal reversal of heating between land and sea adds monsoon circulations: in East and South Asia, moist air flows from the ocean onto the heated continent in summer, bringing most of the year's rain, and dry air flows out from the cold Siberian high in winter. The ocean currents redistribute heat in a similar way: warm western-boundary currents (the Gulf Stream, the Kuroshio) warm the coasts to their west and the lands downwind, and cold eastern-boundary currents with upwelling (California, Humboldt, Benguela) cool and dry the coasts they pass, producing coastal deserts such as the Atacama. On time scales of years, two oscillations modulate climate over large regions: the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, an alternation between warm (El Niño) and cold (La Niña) states of the tropical Pacific, and the North Atlantic Oscillation, an alternation of the pressure difference between the Azores and Iceland, both of which affect the abundance of plankton, fish, birds and the timing of plant growth far from where they originate (7.19.13).

### 3.2.2. Temperature: Latitude, Altitude and Continentality

Mean annual temperature falls from the equator to the poles, by roughly 0.5–0.7 °C per degree of latitude on average, but much faster in winter than in summer: in eastern China the January mean falls by about 1.5 °C per degree of latitude between Guangzhou and Harbin, while the July mean changes little. The annual range of temperature therefore increases with latitude.

Temperature also falls with altitude. A parcel of dry air that rises expands as the pressure falls and does work on its surroundings; if it exchanges no heat with them (an **adiabatic process**), the first law of thermodynamics gives $c_p\,\mathrm{d}T = \mathrm{d}p/\rho$, and with the hydrostatic relation $\mathrm{d}p = -\rho g\,\mathrm{d}z$,

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}z} = \frac{g}{c_p} = \frac{9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}}{1004\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}} \approx 9.8\,\mathrm{K\,km^{-1}},
$$
about 1 °C per 100 m: the dry adiabatic lapse rate. In saturated air the condensation of water vapor releases latent heat and reduces the cooling to the moist adiabatic lapse rate, between about 4 K km⁻¹ in warm, moist air and nearly the dry value in cold air. The mean lapse rate of the real atmosphere, the environmental lapse rate, is about 6.5 K km⁻¹, the value of the standard atmosphere: the familiar rule that temperature falls by about 0.6 °C per 100 m of altitude refers to this average, not to a particular process. On a clear night, the air near the ground may be colder than the air above it (an inversion), because the ground loses heat by radiation; cold air then drains into valleys (Section 3.3). Air that rises over a mountain range, cools at the moist rate and loses its moisture as rain on the windward side warms at the dry rate as it descends on the leeward side, arriving warmer and drier than it started: this is the foehn effect, and the dry leeward region is a rain shadow.

Water has a high specific heat (4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, against about 0.8 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for dry soil and rock), it mixes heat downward, and it consumes heat in evaporation. Oceans therefore warm and cool slowly, and coastal and island climates are maritime, with small daily and annual ranges; the interiors of continents are continental, with hot summers, cold winters and large ranges. Two measures describe the variation in time: the daily range, the difference between the daily maximum (usually at 13–14 h, after the peak of radiation, because the ground continues to heat the air) and the minimum (just before sunrise), and the annual range, the difference between the mean temperatures of the warmest and the coldest month.

### 3.2.3. Precipitation and Humidity

The amount of water vapor that air can hold increases steeply with temperature. By the **Clausius–Clapeyron equation** the saturation vapor pressure $e_{\mathrm{s}}$ obeys $\mathrm{d}\ln e_{\mathrm{s}}/\mathrm{d}T = L/(R_{\mathrm{v}}T^2)$, about 7% per kelvin near 15 °C; a convenient empirical form is the Magnus formula,

$$
e_{\mathrm{s}}(T) \approx 6.112\,\mathrm{hPa}\times\exp\left(\frac{17.62\,T}{243.12 + T}\right) \qquad (T \text{ in } {}^\circ\mathrm{C}).
$$
The relative humidity is the ratio of the actual vapor pressure $e$ to the saturation vapor pressure at the same temperature, $\mathrm{RH} = e/e_{\mathrm{s}}(T)$. For a fixed amount of water vapor the relative humidity therefore falls when temperature rises and rises when temperature falls; air cooled to its **dew point**, where $e_{\mathrm{s}}(T) = e$, becomes saturated and deposits dew or fog. For organisms that lose water by evaporation, the vapor pressure deficit $e_{\mathrm{s}}(T) - e$ is more informative than the relative humidity: at the same relative humidity of 50%, it is about 0.6 kPa at 10 °C but 2.1 kPa at 30 °C, so that the drying power of the air is more than three times greater (7.19.5).

Precipitation follows the circulation: high in the equatorial belt (the ITCZ), low near 20–35° N and S (subtropical highs), moderate in the mid-latitude belt of frontal cyclones and low near the poles. It decreases with distance from the sea, is higher on windward slopes than in rain shadows, and in monsoon regions falls mostly in summer. In China precipitation decreases from over 2000 mm per year on the south-eastern coast to less than 50 mm in the Tarim Basin, and the 400 mm isohyet, running roughly from the Greater Khingan Mountains to the eastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau, separates the forested and agricultural east from the grasslands and deserts of the north-west. Relative humidity in eastern China is low in winter, when dry continental air dominates, and high in summer, when moist air flows in from the ocean.

### 3.2.4. Climate Diagrams

For ecologists, the most useful summary of a climate is the seasonal course of temperature and precipitation together. In the climate diagrams of Walter and Lieth (1960–1967), the monthly mean temperature and monthly precipitation are plotted on the same graph with the scales chosen so that 10 °C corresponds to 20 mm of precipitation. This scale ratio is an empirical rule: when the precipitation curve lies above the temperature curve, precipitation exceeds the potential evaporation and the month is humid; when it lies below, the month is arid. Precipitation above 100 mm per month is drawn at a reduced scale. A single diagram thus shows the length of the warm season, the length of the dry season and their coincidence, which together largely determine the type of vegetation (7.19.41).

## 3.3. Microclimates and the Thermal Structure of Soil and Water

### 3.3.1. Microclimates Near the Ground

The macroclimate is measured in standardized conditions, a white louvred screen about 1.5–2 m above short grass; the microclimate (Definition 2.1.2 of [7.19.2](/7.19.2)) in which most organisms live can be very different. The layer of air near the ground has the largest extremes: by day the ground absorbs radiation and heats the air in contact with it, by night it radiates heat to the sky and cools it. On a summer day the soil surface of a bare field may reach 65 °C while the air at 1.2 m is 38 °C (Odum's example). Vegetation buffers the extremes: under a closed forest canopy the daily maximum is lower, the minimum higher and the humidity higher than in the open, and the reduction of maximum temperatures under forest canopies averages several degrees worldwide (De Frenne and colleagues 2019), which makes forests refuges for species sensitive to warming. Hollows collect cold air at night and become frost pockets, which is why frost-sensitive orchards are planted on slopes, not in valley bottoms. Cities form heat islands, several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside at night, because of their dark, heat-storing surfaces, reduced evaporation and waste heat.

### 3.3.2. The Daily and Annual Heat Wave in Soil

Below the surface, the daily and annual variation of temperature is damped and delayed. This can be derived exactly for a homogeneous soil. Heat flows down the temperature gradient, and conservation of energy gives the **heat equation** ([2.5.7](/2.5.7))

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \kappa\,\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z^2},
$$
where $z$ is depth and $\kappa$ the thermal diffusivity of the soil, about $2$–$8 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ for mineral soils (higher when moist, lower when dry or rich in organic matter).

**Proposition 3.3.1 (Periodic Heat Wave in Soil)** If the surface temperature varies periodically, $T(0, t) = T_{\mathrm{m}} + A\sin\omega t$, and the temperature remains bounded at depth, the periodic solution of the heat equation is

$$
T(z, t) = T_{\mathrm{m}} + A\,\mathrm{e}^{-z/d}\sin\left(\omega t - \frac{z}{d}\right), \qquad d = \sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{\omega}}.
$$
The amplitude decreases exponentially with depth, by a factor $\mathrm{e}$ for every damping depth $d$, and the time of the maximum is delayed by $z/(\omega d)$.

**Proof** Write $T = T_{\mathrm{m}} + \operatorname{Im}[u(z)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}]$. Substitution gives $\mathrm{i}\omega u = \kappa u''$, so $u = c_1\mathrm{e}^{\lambda z} + c_2\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda z}$ with $\lambda^2 = \mathrm{i}\omega/\kappa$, that is $\lambda = (1 + \mathrm{i})/d$ with $d = \sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}$. Boundedness as $z \to \infty$ requires $c_1 = 0$, and the surface condition gives $c_2 = A$. Hence $u = A\mathrm{e}^{-z/d}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}z/d}$ and $T = T_{\mathrm{m}} + A\mathrm{e}^{-z/d}\sin(\omega t - z/d)$, which satisfies the equation and both conditions. $\square$

Because $d \propto \omega^{-1/2}$, slow oscillations penetrate deeper than fast ones: the annual wave (period 365 days) penetrates $\sqrt{365} \approx 19$ times as deep as the daily wave. For $\kappa = 5 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ the daily damping depth is 12 cm and the annual one 2.2 m. The daily amplitude is reduced to 1% at $z = d\ln 100 \approx 4.6d \approx 54$ cm, and the annual amplitude at about 10 m; below these depths the soil has, respectively, no daily and practically no annual temperature variation, and its temperature is close to the annual mean of the surface. The delay is $z/d$ radians: at a depth of 10 cm the daily maximum arrives $0.85/(2\pi) \times 24 \approx 3.3$ h after the surface maximum, at about 16–17 h if the surface peaks at 13 h; at 2 m the annual maximum arrives about two months late. These are exactly the observations summarized in soil-temperature tables.

The ecological consequences are direct. Burrowing animals in deserts (rodents, lizards, scorpions) live a few tens of centimeters down, where the daily range is a few degrees, and emerge at the cooler times of day; hibernating mammals and overwintering insects choose depths below the frost line; the roots and seeds of plants experience much smaller variations than their shoots. Snow is an excellent insulator (its thermal diffusivity is lower than that of soil and it reflects most radiation), so that under a deep snow cover the soil surface stays near 0 °C even when the air is at −30 °C, and many small mammals and plants survive the winter in the subnivean space.

### 3.3.3. Thermal Stratification of Lakes

Water has a maximum density at 3.98 °C. As a consequence, a lake does not freeze from the bottom up: in winter, water at 4 °C sinks to the bottom while colder, lighter water and then ice form at the surface, and the ice insulates the water below. In summer, the surface water is warmed by the Sun and stays on top, because warm water is lighter, and wind mixes only the upper layer.

**Definition 3.3.1 (Thermal Stratification)** The layering of a body of water into zones of different temperature and density is called **thermal stratification**. In a stratified lake the warm, mixed upper layer is the epilimnion; the deep, cold layer is the hypolimnion; and the intermediate layer of rapid temperature change is the metalimnion, which contains the **thermocline**, the depth at which the temperature gradient is steepest.

![Thermal structure of a deep temperate lake. A: density of pure water against temperature, with its maximum at 3.98 °C; the density difference per degree increases with temperature, so that warm stratification is more stable than cool. B: idealized seasonal temperature profiles of a dimictic lake (schematic): inverse stratification under winter ice (0 °C at the surface, 4 °C at depth), complete mixing at about 4 °C in spring, summer stratification into epilimnion, metalimnion with the thermocline, and hypolimnion, and renewed mixing in autumn as the surface cools.](./plots/lake-stratification.py)

The seasonal cycle of a deep lake in a temperate climate, such as many lakes of northern China, Europe and North America, has four phases. In spring, as the ice melts and the surface warms to 4 °C, the whole water column has nearly the same temperature and density, and wind mixes it from top to bottom (spring overturn). In summer, the surface warms faster than wind can mix the heat downward, and a stable stratification forms. In autumn the surface cools, the density difference disappears and the lake mixes again (autumn overturn). In winter, ice forms and the lake is inversely stratified. Lakes that mix twice a year are dimictic; deep lakes in mild climates that never freeze mix once, in winter (warm monomictic); shallow lakes are mixed by wind repeatedly (polymictic); and some deep lakes with dense, saline bottom water never mix completely (meromictic).

Stratification has profound ecological consequences. The hypolimnion is cut off from the atmosphere during summer, and the decomposition of organic matter sinking from the productive epilimnion consumes its oxygen; in nutrient-rich lakes it may become anoxic, killing fish and releasing phosphate from the sediment. The epilimnion, in turn, is depleted of nutrients by phytoplankton, which sink out of it. Overturn restores oxygen to the depths and nutrients to the surface, and is followed by blooms of phytoplankton. These processes are central to the productivity and **eutrophication** of lakes (7.19.46). In the oceans, a permanent thermocline separates the warm surface layer from the cold deep water over most of the tropics and subtropics, which is why the open tropical ocean, nutrient-poor at the surface, is a biological desert except in regions of upwelling.

## 3.4. Ecological Effects of Light

### 3.4.1. Light Intensity and Plants

Light is a resource for plants (7.19.6), and its intensity varies over five orders of magnitude, from full sunlight (about 2000 μmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ of photosynthetically active radiation) to the floor of a tropical rainforest (often 1–2% of that). The rate of photosynthesis increases with light, linearly at first, then more slowly, and reaches a plateau at light saturation; at the **light compensation point** ([7.13.9](/7.13.9)), gross photosynthesis just balances respiration, and below it the plant loses carbon. These responses, with the non-rectangular hyperbola that describes them, are treated in 7.13.9; ecologically they define two strategies.

- **Sun plants** ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)), such as most crops, grasses of open habitats and pioneer trees, have high rates of photosynthesis and respiration, saturate at high intensity, and have high compensation points; they grow well only in full light. Their leaves are small, thick and often steeply inclined, which spreads the overhead light over more leaf area.
- **Shade plants**, such as understory herbs and the seedlings of late-successional trees, have low rates of respiration and low compensation points, saturate at low intensity, and use dim light more efficiently than sun plants, but cannot use bright light. Their leaves are thin, large, horizontal and rich in chlorophyll per unit dry mass. Shade-tolerant plants grow best in full light but survive moderate shade.

The difference matters at the level of the community: the order in which tree species replace one another in succession largely follows their shade tolerance (7.19.36), and the coexistence of sun and shade coffee illustrates the agricultural side: traditional shade-grown coffee, under a canopy of trees, supports far more birds and insects than modern sun-grown varieties (Perfecto and colleagues 1996). Within a single plant, leaves in the sun and in the shade of the crown develop as sun and shade leaves.

Light also controls development. Seedlings grown in darkness elongate rapidly, form no chlorophyll and remain yellow (**etiolation**, [7.13.16](/7.13.16)), a strategy for reaching the light from below the soil; the yellow chives (*Allium tuberosum*) of Chinese cuisine are produced by growing the plants under covers. The ratio of red to far-red light, sensed by **phytochrome**, tells a plant whether it is shaded by other leaves (which absorb red and transmit far-red) and triggers **shade avoidance** ([7.13.16](/7.13.16)), elongation towards the light at the expense of leaves and roots, a response that matters greatly for competition in dense stands.

### 3.4.2. Light Intensity and Animals

For animals, light is an information source rather than an energy source, and its intensity determines when they can see. Animals are diurnal (active by day), nocturnal (by night), crepuscular (at dawn and dusk) or cathemeral (at any time); the onset of activity of many birds and mammals is set by a threshold light intensity, so that it shifts with the season and the weather. Nocturnal species have large eyes relative to body size (owls, tarsiers, many moths) or rely on other senses; animals living permanently in darkness lose their eyes: the olm *Proteus anguinus*, a cave salamander of the Dinaric karst, has regressed eyes covered by skin but retains light-sensitive cells in the skin. Many deep-sea fishes have either very large, sensitive eyes or their own light organs. Light intensity also affects growth and development: juvenile Chinese softshell turtles (*Pelodiscus sinensis*), which live on the bottom of fresh waters, grow fastest at low light intensities (Zhou and colleagues 1998).

### 3.4.3. Light in Water

In water, light is absorbed and scattered much more strongly than in air. In a homogeneous water body the downward irradiance decreases exponentially with depth,

$$
E(z) = E(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-K_{\mathrm{d}}z},
$$
where $K_{\mathrm{d}}$ is the diffuse attenuation coefficient. The euphotic zone, in which net photosynthesis is possible, is conventionally taken to extend to the depth where 1% of the surface light remains,

$$
z_{\mathrm{eu}} = \frac{\ln 100}{K_{\mathrm{d}}} \approx \frac{4.6}{K_{\mathrm{d}}}.
$$
Below it lies the dysphotic zone, with too little light for photosynthesis but enough for vision, and below about 1000 m the aphotic zone, where the only light is produced by organisms. $K_{\mathrm{d}}$ ranges from about 0.03 m⁻¹ in the clearest ocean water (euphotic zone over 150 m deep) to over 2 m⁻¹ in turbid or eutrophic lakes (euphotic zone of 2 m or less). A simple field estimate uses the Secchi disc, a white disc lowered until it disappears: its depth $z_{\mathrm{SD}}$ is related to the attenuation by $K_{\mathrm{d}} \approx 1.7/z_{\mathrm{SD}}$ (Poole and Atkins 1929), so that the euphotic zone is roughly 2.7 times the Secchi depth.

The attenuation depends on wavelength. Pure water absorbs red light strongly (absorption coefficient 0.65 m⁻¹ at 700 nm) and blue light very weakly (about 0.01 m⁻¹ at 425–450 nm); infrared and ultraviolet radiation are absorbed within the first meters. In clear ocean water, therefore, the light remaining at depth is blue, as divers see; in coastal and lake waters, dissolved organic matter (which absorbs blue) and phytoplankton (which absorb blue and red) shift the most penetrating light to green or yellow.

The change of light quality with depth has long been invoked to explain the vertical zonation of seaweeds, with green algae in shallow water, brown algae deeper and **red algae** deepest, each with accessory pigments (phycobiliproteins in red algae) absorbing the light that penetrates to its depth (Engelmann's theory of complementary chromatic adaptation, 1883). The theory is only partly correct. Red algae do have the deepest records (a crustose coralline alga at 268 m off the Bahamas; Littler and colleagues 1985), but green algae also grow deep and many red algae grow in the intertidal zone, and the depth distribution of seaweeds is determined as much by grazing, competition, wave action and the total quantity of light as by its color (Saffo 1987). The pigment composition of individual algae and cyanobacteria does adjust to light quality (**chromatic acclimation**), which is a real but more limited phenomenon. Pigment differences do help explain the composition of phytoplankton: in clear, greenish lake water, cryptophytes with phycobiliproteins are favored, in yellowish humic water chrysophytes (Gerea and colleagues 2017).

### 3.4.4. Light Quality

Different wavelengths have different effects. Blue light is absorbed by chlorophyll and by the blue-light receptors that control phototropism and stomatal opening; red light drives photosynthesis and, with far-red, controls phytochrome responses; ultraviolet B damages DNA and photosynthetic proteins but also stimulates the synthesis of protective flavonoids, and alpine and tropical-mountain plants exposed to high UV are rich in them. Animals see different parts of the spectrum: most insects, many birds and fishes see ultraviolet light, and flowers have ultraviolet patterns (nectar guides) invisible to us but conspicuous to bees, whose vision spans about 300–650 nm but lacks red. The ecological meaning of light quality for animals is thus largely a matter of signals, treated in [7.18](/7.18).

## 3.5. Photoperiod

### 3.5.1. Day Length

The length of the day is a precise function of latitude and date. With the solar declination $\delta$ of Section 3.1.3, the hour angle of sunset satisfies

$$
\cos h_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{\sin h - \sin\varphi\sin\delta}{\cos\varphi\cos\delta},
$$
where $h = -0.833^\circ$ is the elevation of the center of the Sun at apparent sunset (allowing for refraction and the radius of the solar disc), and the day length is $N = 2h_{\mathrm{s}}/15^\circ$ hours. Neglecting $h$ gives the simpler $\cos h_{\mathrm{s}} = -\tan\varphi\tan\delta$. At the equinoxes ($\delta = 0$) the day lasts 12 h everywhere; at the equator it lasts 12 h all year; and the annual range increases with latitude, from about 2.5 h at 23.5° to 6 h at 40° and 24 h beyond the polar circles (figure in Section 3.1.3).

Day length is the most reliable seasonal signal available to organisms. Temperature also changes with the season, but it fluctuates from day to day and year to year: a warm spell in February does not mean that spring has come. Day length is free of such noise and tells the date exactly; its rate of change and direction (lengthening or shortening) distinguish spring from autumn. Organisms that must prepare in advance for the season, such as a plant that must flower when pollinators are present, an insect that must enter diapause before the frost, or a bird that must grow its gonads and migrate before the breeding season, therefore use day length, often in combination with temperature, as their calendar.

### 3.5.2. Photoperiodism in Plants

**Photoperiodism** ([7.13.21](/7.13.21)) is the response of organisms to the length of day and night. In plants it controls flowering, dormancy, the formation of storage organs and leaf fall. By their flowering response, plants are classified as **long-day plants**, which flower when the day exceeds a critical length (wheat, oats, barley, cabbage, carrot, *Arabidopsis*); **short-day plants**, which flower when the day is shorter than a critical length (soybean, rice, cocklebur, chrysanthemum, tropical maize); and **day-neutral plants**, whose flowering is independent of day length (tomato, cucumber, many temperate maize cultivars). What the plant actually measures is the length of the uninterrupted night: a short flash of light in the middle of a long night (a **night break**) prevents flowering of short-day plants and promotes that of long-day plants.

The ecological logic is that day length predicts the season. Long-day plants of high latitudes flower in early summer, short-day plants of the tropics and subtropics flower towards the end of the rainy season, and the critical day length of a species varies among its populations with latitude, so that each flowers at the right time locally; moving a cultivar to another latitude changes its flowering date, which is a central problem of plant breeding. Horticulturists exploit photoperiodism: chrysanthemums are made to flower at any time of year by covering them to shorten the day or lighting them to lengthen it, and the flowering of sugarcane, which reduces its yield of sugar, is suppressed by short periods of light in the middle of the night.

### 3.5.3. Photoperiodism in Animals

In animals, day length controls the timing of reproduction, moult, migration, fattening, hibernation and diapause. Many birds and small mammals of temperate regions are long-day breeders, whose gonads grow when days lengthen in spring; sheep, goats and deer, whose gestation lasts five months or more, are short-day breeders that mate in autumn so that their young are born in spring. In mammals the signal is transduced by the nightly secretion of melatonin by the pineal gland, whose duration reflects the length of the night; in birds deep-brain photoreceptors are involved. Mechanisms of photoperiodic time measurement and the role of the circadian clock (**circadian rhythm**) are treated in [7.18.18](/7.18.18).

In insects, the induction of **diapause** by short days is the best-studied photoperiodic response. Larvae of the oriental fruit moth *Grapholita molesta* enter diapause when the day falls below about 13–14 h, and in this way prepare for winter weeks before the first frost. The critical day length of insect populations increases with latitude, by about 1–1.5 h per 5° of latitude in several Lepidoptera (Danilevskii 1965), because at higher latitude the summer days are longer and winter comes earlier, so that diapause must be induced at a longer day length. Such clines are adaptive and evolve quickly: the pitcher-plant mosquito *Wyeomyia smithii* in North America has shifted its critical photoperiod towards shorter days within a few decades as the growing season lengthened with climate warming (Bradshaw and Holzapfel 2001), one of the first demonstrations of genetic adaptation to recent climate change.

### 3.5.4. Artificial Light at Night

For most of the history of life, nights were dark except for moonlight. Today about 23% of the land surface between 75° N and 60° S, and the night sky of more than 80% of the world's people, are polluted by artificial light (Falchi and colleagues 2016), and the lit area increases by about 2% per year. *Light pollution*, the alteration of natural light levels by artificial light at night, has three kinds of effects.

- Disruption of timing. Artificial light shifts the daily activity of animals (songbirds in cities begin singing earlier at dawn), advances reproduction and budburst, and suppresses melatonin; it can mislead photoperiodic responses that rely on the length of the night.
- Attraction and disorientation. Lights attract insects, which die of exhaustion or are eaten; hatchling sea turtles, which find the sea by moving towards the brighter horizon, crawl inland towards lit roads; nocturnally migrating birds are drawn to lit towers and buildings and collide with them. During the annual Tribute in Light memorial in New York, two vertical beams concentrated migrating birds at densities up to 150 times the background within a few hundred meters, and the effect disappeared within minutes when the lights were switched off (Van Doren and colleagues 2017).
- Changes in communities. Lit areas favor species that tolerate light or exploit insects attracted to it, and disfavor light-shy species such as many bats.

Cabrera-Cruz and colleagues (2018) mapped the light exposure of the ranges of 298 species of nocturnally migrating birds in the Americas and found that exposure is highest in the migration seasons, when the birds cross the lit landscapes between their breeding and wintering grounds. Mitigation is straightforward compared with most environmental problems: turning off unnecessary lights during migration peaks, shielding lamps so that they shine downward, and using lamps with less blue light.

## Summary

- The solar constant is about 1361 W m⁻²; the Earth receives on average 340 W m⁻² at the top of the atmosphere, reflects about 30%, and radiates the rest as infrared. Solar radiation is shortwave (peak near 500 nm), terrestrial radiation longwave (peak near 10 μm). About 45% of the solar energy at the surface is photosynthetically active (400–700 nm); photosynthesis captures about 0.1% of the solar energy reaching the Earth.
- Daily insolation depends on solar elevation and day length; its annual mean decreases poleward and its seasonal amplitude increases.
- The general circulation (Hadley, mid-latitude and polar cells), the Coriolis force, monsoons and ocean currents distribute heat and rain: wet tropics, dry subtropical belts near 30°, moist mid-latitudes, dry poles. Temperature falls with latitude and altitude (dry adiabatic lapse rate $g/c_p \approx 9.8$ K km⁻¹; environmental average about 6.5 K km⁻¹); continentality increases daily and annual ranges. Saturation vapor pressure rises by about 7% per kelvin, so relative humidity falls as air warms; Walter climate diagrams show humid and arid seasons at a glance.
- Microclimates near the ground have the largest extremes; vegetation and snow buffer them. In soil, a periodic surface temperature penetrates as a damped, delayed wave with damping depth $\sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}$ (Proposition 3.3.1): about 12 cm for the daily and 2 m for the annual wave.
- Because water is densest at 3.98 °C, deep temperate lakes stratify in summer (epilimnion, metalimnion with thermocline, hypolimnion; Definition 3.3.1), stratify inversely under winter ice and overturn in spring and autumn, with major consequences for oxygen and nutrients.
- Plants are sun or shade plants; light controls development (etiolation, shade avoidance). In water, light decreases exponentially ($z_{\mathrm{eu}} = 4.6/K_{\mathrm{d}}$), red light first; algal depth zonation is only partly explained by light color. For animals, light is information: activity rhythms, eye size, vision in the ultraviolet.
- Day length depends exactly on latitude and date and is the most reliable seasonal cue: photoperiodism controls flowering (long-day, short-day, day-neutral plants; the night is measured), animal reproduction and insect diapause, with adaptive latitudinal clines. Artificial light at night disrupts timing, attracts and disorients animals and changes communities.

## Questions

1. Compute the noon elevation of the Sun at the summer and winter solstices at Beijing (40° N) and at Guangzhou (23° N). Why are seasonal differences in temperature larger at Beijing?

   **Answer** Noon elevation is $90^\circ - \lvert\varphi - \delta\rvert$. Beijing: summer $90 - (40 - 23.4) = 73.4^\circ$, winter $90 - (40 + 23.4) = 26.6^\circ$. Guangzhou: summer $90 - 0.4 \approx 89.6^\circ$, winter $90 - 46.4 = 43.6^\circ$. The flux on a horizontal surface is proportional to $\sin\beta$ (before atmospheric losses): at Beijing it falls from 0.96 to 0.45 of the perpendicular flux between summer and winter, at Guangzhou from 1.00 to 0.69. In addition the winter day at Beijing is about 9.4 h against 10.7 h at Guangzhou, and in winter Beijing is under the cold, dry continental air of the Siberian high. All three effects increase the annual range at higher latitude.

2. A mountain rises from a plain at 500 m altitude with a mean temperature of 15 °C. Estimate the mean temperature at 3000 m. If moist air rising over the mountain is saturated from 1500 m upward, and the moist adiabatic lapse rate is 5 K km⁻¹, what is the temperature of the air when it descends to 500 m on the other side, compared with its starting temperature of 15 °C?

   **Answer** With the environmental lapse rate of about 6.5 K km⁻¹, the mean temperature at 3000 m is about $15 - 6.5 \times 2.5 \approx -1$ °C. The rising parcel cools at the dry rate from 500 to 1500 m, by 9.8 K, to 5.2 °C, then at the moist rate to 3000 m, by $5 \times 1.5 = 7.5$ K, to −2.3 °C, having lost much of its water as rain. Descending unsaturated, it warms at the dry rate over 2500 m, by 24.5 K, to about 22 °C: 7 °C warmer and much drier than at the start. This is the foehn effect, which makes leeward valleys warm and dry (rain shadows).

3. The thermal diffusivity of a dry sandy soil is about $2 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, that of a moist loam about $8 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. Compute the daily damping depth for each, and the depth at which a desert rodent's burrow experiences only 5% of the daily surface amplitude.

   **Answer** $d = \sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}$ with $\omega = 7.27 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Dry sand: $d = \sqrt{4 \times 10^{-7}/7.27 \times 10^{-5}} = 0.074$ m; moist loam: $d = \sqrt{1.6 \times 10^{-6}/7.27 \times 10^{-5}} = 0.15$ m. The amplitude is 5% at $z = d\ln 20 \approx 3.0d$: 22 cm in dry sand, 45 cm in moist loam. In dry sand a burrow of a few tens of centimeters already gives nearly constant temperature; desert rodents commonly dig to such depths. If the surface varies between 15 and 65 °C (amplitude 25 °C), the burrow at 22 cm varies by only ±1.3 °C around 40 °C, still hot but bearable, and deeper burrows are cooler only if the daily mean temperature is lower.

4. Why does a dimictic lake in summer often have an oxygen-rich epilimnion and an oxygen-poor hypolimnion, and why is the hypolimnion of an oligotrophic (nutrient-poor) lake often well oxygenated all summer?

   **Answer** The epilimnion exchanges gases with the atmosphere and contains photosynthesizing phytoplankton, so it stays near saturation. The hypolimnion is isolated from the atmosphere by the stable stratification and receives no light; its oxygen is consumed by the decomposition of organic matter sinking from above, and nothing replaces it until autumn overturn. The rate of consumption depends on the supply of organic matter, which depends on productivity: in an oligotrophic lake little organic matter sinks, and the large volume of cold water that was saturated at overturn (cold water holds more oxygen) suffices for the whole summer. In a eutrophic lake the sinking organic matter exhausts it, and the hypolimnion becomes anoxic (7.19.46).

5. A Secchi disc disappears at 4 m in a lake. Estimate the attenuation coefficient and the depth of the euphotic zone. After a phytoplankton bloom the Secchi depth falls to 1 m; what happens to the euphotic zone, and why may this be self-limiting for the bloom?

   **Answer** $K_{\mathrm{d}} \approx 1.7/4 = 0.43\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, and $z_{\mathrm{eu}} = 4.6/0.43 \approx 11$ m. After the bloom, $K_{\mathrm{d}} \approx 1.7\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and $z_{\mathrm{eu}} \approx 2.7$ m. The phytoplankton shade themselves: if the epilimnion is deeper than the euphotic zone, cells mixed through it spend much of their time below the compensation depth, so that net growth averaged over the mixed layer falls. This self-shading limits the density that a bloom can reach in a deep mixed layer (Sverdrup's critical depth, 7.19.42); in shallow lakes, where the whole water column is lit, blooms can become much denser.

6. At what latitude is the change of day length between two successive days largest at the spring equinox: at 20°, 40° or 60° N? What does this imply for organisms that use day length to time spring events?

   **Answer** Near the equinox, $\cos h_{\mathrm{s}} \approx -\tan\varphi\tan\delta$ with $\delta$ small and $h_{\mathrm{s}} \approx 90^\circ$, so $h_{\mathrm{s}} \approx 90^\circ + \tan\varphi\,\delta$ (in degrees). The day length changes at a rate proportional to $\tan\varphi$ times the rate of change of the declination (about 0.4° per day at the equinox), that is by about $2 \times 0.4\tan\varphi/15$ h per day: 1.2 min at 20°, 2.7 min at 40° and 5.5 min at 60°. At high latitude day length is therefore a sharper calendar, changing quickly in spring and autumn; at low latitude it changes so little that tropical organisms more often use rainfall, temperature or lunar cues, or need very sensitive photoperiodic mechanisms (some tropical plants respond to differences of 15–30 minutes).

7. Explain, in terms of photoperiodism, why the critical day length for diapause induction in an insect increases with latitude, and predict how a population moved from 30° N to 45° N would perform.

   **Answer** At higher latitude winter begins earlier while the days of late summer are still long, so diapause must be triggered at a longer day length to occur in time; selection therefore produces a cline of increasing critical photoperiod with latitude. A population moved from 30° to 45° N would continue to develop until the day length fell below its short critical value, which at 45° happens late in autumn; it would therefore begin diapause too late and many individuals would be caught by frost in a non-diapausing, cold-sensitive stage. Conversely a high-latitude population moved south would enter diapause too early and lose part of the growing season. Such mismatches are a reason why biological control agents and crops must be matched to the latitude where they are used.
