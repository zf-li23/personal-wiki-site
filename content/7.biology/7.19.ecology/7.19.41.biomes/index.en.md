The previous chapter described an **ecosystem** in the abstract: producers, consumers and decomposers linked by flows of energy and matter ([7.19.40](/7.19.40)). This chapter surveys the real ecosystems of the Earth. On land, the most visible fact of biogeography is that vegetation of similar appearance recurs wherever the climate is similar, even on continents whose floras share almost no species: evergreen sclerophyll shrubland around the Mediterranean, in California, central Chile, the Cape of South Africa and southwestern Australia; tall evergreen forest wherever it is warm and wet throughout the year. These recurrent formations are the biomes. We first ask how climate determines them (41.1) and how they are arranged along gradients of latitude, distance from the sea and altitude, with the mountains of China as examples (41.2). We then describe the terrestrial biomes one by one (41.3) and the aquatic ecosystems, which are classified not by vegetation but by the physical properties of the water (41.4), and close with the shifting of biomes under climate change (41.5). The climatic background (circulation, temperature, precipitation, climate diagrams, the stratification of lakes and the attenuation of light in water) is in [7.19.3](/7.19.3); plant life forms and the adaptations of plants to water, light and temperature are in botany ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)). The productivity of the biomes is the subject of the next chapter ([7.19.42](/7.19.42)).

## 41.1. Biomes and Climate

### 41.1.1. The Biome Concept

**Definition 41.1.1 (Biome)** A **biome** is a major regional ecosystem type characterized by the growth form of its dominant vegetation (its physiognomy) and by the climate that determines it, together with the animals and microorganisms adapted to it, such as tropical rain forest, boreal forest, temperate grassland or tundra.

A biome is defined by structure, not by species. The tropical rain forests of the Amazon, the Congo basin and Borneo have almost no tree species in common and are dominated by different families, yet all are tall, evergreen, many-layered forests rich in lianas and epiphytes, with buttressed trees and leaves of similar size and shape. This is **convergent evolution** at the level of whole communities: similar climates impose similar constraints on how a plant can capture light and water and survive the unfavourable season, and the same **life forms** (in Raunkiær's sense, classified by the position of the buds that survive the unfavourable season; [7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.1)) evolve repeatedly from different lineages. The biome is therefore the terrestrial equivalent of the vegetation type or formation of the plant ecologists (Clements' formation, the "vegetation type" of Chinese vegetation science); animals are included, but the boundaries are drawn by vegetation because plants are fixed in place and integrate the climate of a site.

Biomes are idealizations. At any one place the vegetation also depends on soil, fire, grazing and history, and biome boundaries are broad transition zones (**ecotones**) rather than lines. The number of biomes recognized depends on the purpose: Whittaker (1975) used nine terrestrial types, Walter (1985) nine zonobiomes, and global vegetation models typically distinguish about a dozen plant functional types.

### 41.1.2. The Whittaker Diagram

Whittaker (1975) plotted the major biomes in a space whose axes are the two climatic variables that matter most to plants, mean annual temperature and mean annual precipitation. Every place on land falls within a roughly triangular region of this space: cold climates cannot be very wet, because cold air holds little water vapour, while warm climates range from extremely dry to extremely wet.

![The Whittaker biome diagram (schematic, after Whittaker 1975 and Ricklefs 2008). Each region shows the biome expected for a combination of mean annual temperature and annual precipitation; the upper left boundary is the limit of observed climates. Points: approximate 1991–2020 climate normals of five Chinese cities. Ürümqi falls in grassland/desert, in agreement with the desert steppe and desert around it. The four eastern cities fall in drier biomes than their natural vegetation: Harbin and Beijing in woodland/shrubland (natural vegetation: temperate mixed and deciduous forest), Wuhan in temperate seasonal forest and Guangzhou in tropical seasonal forest/savanna (natural vegetation: subtropical evergreen broadleaf and monsoon forest). The diagram ignores the seasonal distribution of rain, which in the East Asian monsoon falls in the warm season, when plants can use it](./plots/whittaker-biomes.py)

The diagram shows three gradients at once. Along the temperature axis, at adequate precipitation, forest changes from boreal to temperate to tropical, with tundra beyond the cold limit of trees. Along the precipitation axis, at any temperature above the tundra, forest gives way to woodland and shrubland, then grassland and finally desert. And the dry boundary of forest moves to higher precipitation as temperature rises, because evaporation increases with temperature: 500 mm of rain supports forest in the boreal zone but only savanna or thorn scrub in the tropics.

The diagram has limits. Mean annual values hide seasonality, which often matters more than the totals: a Mediterranean climate (wet winters, dry summers) and a monsoon climate (dry winters, wet summers) can have the same means but carry sclerophyll shrubland and broadleaf forest respectively. Fire, soils and herbivores determine the vegetation where the climate allows more than one biome; the overlap between savanna, woodland and forest is especially large (41.3.2). And the diagram says nothing about the alpine tundra and forests of high mountains in the tropics, which have low mean temperatures but no winter.

### 41.1.3. Climate Diagrams and the Holdridge Life Zones

The climate diagrams of Walter and Lieth ([7.19.3](/7.19.3#sec-3.2.4)) remedy the first limitation by showing the course of temperature and precipitation through the year, scaled so that 10 °C corresponds to 20 mm of monthly rain: arid months are those in which the precipitation curve lies below the temperature curve. Walter used them to divide the land into nine zonobiomes, each defined by a type of annual climate:

| Zonobiome | Climate | Zonal vegetation |
|---|---|---|
| I | Equatorial, humid all year | Evergreen tropical rain forest |
| II | Tropical, summer rain and dry winter | Tropical deciduous forest, savanna |
| III | Subtropical arid | Hot desert |
| IV | Winter rain, summer drought (Mediterranean) | Sclerophyll woodland and shrubland |
| V | Warm temperate, humid (often summer rain) | Temperate evergreen forest, including the subtropical evergreen broadleaf forests of East Asia |
| VI | Temperate (nemoral), short cold winter | Deciduous broadleaf forest |
| VII | Continental, arid, cold winter | Steppe and cold desert |
| VIII | Cold temperate (boreal), long cold winter | Boreal coniferous forest (taiga) |
| IX | Arctic, short cool summer | Tundra |

The Holdridge (1947, 1967) life-zone system is designed to predict vegetation from climate alone, using three variables on logarithmic scales. The first is the mean annual biotemperature $T_{\mathrm{bio}}$, the mean of temperatures with values below 0 °C (and, in Holdridge's later work, above 30 °C) replaced by 0, on the grounds that plant growth stops outside this range. The second is the mean annual precipitation $P$. The third is the ratio of potential evapotranspiration to precipitation, where Holdridge estimated potential evapotranspiration as

$$
\mathrm{PET} = 58.93\,\mathrm{mm\,^\circ C^{-1}} \times T_{\mathrm{bio}},
$$
so that the PET ratio $\mathrm{PET}/P$ measures aridity: below 1 a site has a water surplus, above 1 a deficit. Because $\log(\mathrm{PET}/P) = \log 58.93 + \log T_{\mathrm{bio}} - \log P$, the three axes are not independent, and on logarithmic scales they form a triangular grid. The lines $T_{\mathrm{bio}} = 1.5, 3, 6, 12, 24\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (doubling at each step) separate the latitudinal regions (polar, subpolar, boreal, cool temperate, warm temperate and subtropical, tropical), the PET-ratio lines $0.125, 0.25, \dots, 32$ separate the humidity provinces, and each life zone is a hexagon of this grid named by its region and its humidity, such as "tropical moist forest" or "cool temperate steppe". The same divisions apply to altitudinal belts, which Holdridge named (lower montane, montane, subalpine, alpine) from the same biotemperature lines.

![The Holdridge life-zone system drawn on rectangular logarithmic axes. Horizontal lines of mean annual biotemperature separate the latitudinal regions, and diagonal lines of the potential evapotranspiration ratio (PET/P, with PET = 58.93 mm °C⁻¹ × biotemperature) separate the humidity provinces, from superhumid on the right (PET ratio below 0.25) to superarid at the lower left (above 16); the numbers along the bottom give the PET ratio of each diagonal line. Each cell is a life zone; Holdridge's hexagons are these cells with their corners trimmed by lines of constant precipitation. The labels name the main life zones of each region; the frost line (about 17 °C) dividing warm temperate from subtropical is omitted.](./plots/holdridge-life-zones.py)

The Holdridge system has been widely used in tropical land-use planning and as the simplest model of how biomes would shift under a changed climate. Its weaknesses are the crude estimate of evapotranspiration (which ignores radiation, humidity and wind) and, again, the neglect of seasonality.

### 41.1.4. Why Climate Determines Vegetation

The climatic boundaries of biomes correspond to physiological thresholds of the dominant plants, which is why biome maps can be predicted from climate.

- The cold limit of trees, the treeline, lies where the mean temperature of the growing season falls to about 6–7 °C (6.4 °C in a global survey of treelines; Körner and Paulsen 2004), independent of latitude. At lower temperatures the formation of new tissue (cell division and wall synthesis), not photosynthesis, is limiting: trees at the treeline are rich in sugars, not starved of them. Trees, whose crowns are coupled to the cold air, are limited before dwarf shrubs and herbs, whose leaves are warmed in the boundary layer near the ground ([7.19.3](/7.19.3#sec-3.3.1)).
- Minimum winter temperatures limit life forms. Evergreen broadleaf trees rarely tolerate temperatures below about −15 °C; many deciduous trees survive to about −40 °C because their xylem parenchyma avoids freezing by **supercooling**, which fails near −40 °C, the homogeneous nucleation temperature of water; only trees that tolerate extracellular freezing (larch, birch, some pines and spruces) extend into the coldest continental climates of Siberia.
- The balance of precipitation and evaporation sets the dry limits. Forest needs enough water in the soil during the growing season to keep a closed canopy transpiring; where water is shorter the canopy opens, and grasses, which can exploit the upper soil quickly after rain and survive drought and fire as buried buds, take over.
- The length of the growing season, set by both temperature and drought, determines whether evergreen or deciduous leaves are more profitable: a leaf must repay its construction cost within its lifetime, so long-lived, tough evergreen leaves are favoured where the season is short and nutrients are poor (boreal conifers, sclerophylls), and cheap, thin deciduous leaves where a long favourable season alternates with a harsh one.

## 41.2. Patterns of Distribution: Zonality

### 41.2.1. Latitudinal Zonality

Because the Earth's surface receives less solar radiation towards the poles ([7.19.3](/7.19.3#sec-3.1.3)), mean temperature falls by about 0.5–0.7 °C per degree of latitude (about 111 km), and biomes are arranged in belts roughly parallel to the lines of latitude.

**Definition 41.2.1 (Zonality)** **Zonality** is the arrangement of biomes in belts along the large-scale gradients of heat and moisture: latitudinal zonality follows latitude (the gradient of heat), longitudinal zonality follows the distance from the ocean (the gradient of moisture within a latitudinal zone), and the two together make up horizontal zonality; altitudinal zonation follows elevation on mountains. Vegetation determined by the regional climate is called zonal; vegetation determined by local conditions of soil or water, which can occur in several climatic zones, is azonal.

On an idealized continent, the sequence from the equator poleward on the humid eastern side is tropical rain forest, subtropical evergreen forest, temperate deciduous forest, boreal forest, tundra and polar desert, whereas on the western side and in the interior the subtropical high-pressure belts produce deserts at about 15–35° latitude, flanked by savanna towards the equator and by Mediterranean shrubland and steppe poleward. Eastern China is one of the few regions where the full humid sequence is developed over a single landmass, because the East Asian monsoon brings summer rain across latitudes that are desert in North Africa and Arabia. From south to north, over more than 30° of latitude, the zonal vegetation is:

| Zone | Approximate latitude | Zonal vegetation | Examples of dominants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tropical | south of about 22–23° N | Tropical monsoon forest and rain forest | Dipterocarps (*Parashorea chinensis* in Xishuangbanna), *Vatica* in Hainan |
| Subtropical | about 23°–33° N (to the Qinling–Huaihe line) | Evergreen broadleaf forest | *Castanopsis*, *Cyclobalanopsis*, *Lithocarpus* (Fagaceae), *Machilus*, *Cinnamomum* (Lauraceae), *Schima* (Theaceae) |
| Warm temperate | about 33°–42° N | Deciduous broadleaf forest | Deciduous oaks (*Quercus mongolica*, *Q. variabilis*), *Pinus tabuliformis* |
| Temperate | about 42°–50° N (Northeast) | Mixed coniferous–broadleaf forest | Korean pine (*Pinus koraiensis*) with *Tilia*, *Acer*, *Fraxinus* |
| Cold temperate | north of about 50° N (northern Da Hinggan Ling) | Boreal deciduous coniferous forest | Dahurian larch (*Larix gmelinii*) |

### 41.2.2. Longitudinal Zonality

Within the temperate zone of Eurasia and North America, precipitation decreases from the coasts towards the continental interiors, because the moist air from the oceans loses its water as it travels inland and crosses mountains (rain shadows). The vegetation changes accordingly from forest through grassland to desert. In northern China, the gradient runs from the forests of the Northeast through the meadow steppe of eastern Inner Mongolia (annual precipitation about 350–500 mm), the typical steppe (250–400 mm) and the desert steppe (150–250 mm) to the deserts of western Inner Mongolia, Gansu and Xinjiang (below 150 mm, in the Tarim basin locally below 20 mm). The Heihe–Tengchong line of population geography (the Hu Huanyong line) runs close to the 400 mm isohyet, which separates the forest and farmland of the east from the grassland, desert and high plateau of the west, and it is also a sharp boundary of primary production (41.3.7; [7.19.42](/7.19.42)).

### 41.2.3. Altitudinal Zonation

Air temperature falls with elevation by about 0.6 °C per 100 m (the environmental lapse rate), so that climbing a mountain resembles travelling poleward: a rough rule is that 100 m of elevation corresponds to about 100 km of latitude. Precipitation usually increases with elevation up to a zone of maximum precipitation, often at 1500–3000 m on humid mountains, and then decreases. The sequence of vegetation belts on a mountain is its altitudinal spectrum, and it begins with the zonal vegetation of the surrounding lowland. Three mountains illustrate how the spectrum depends on latitude and height.

![Altitudinal zonation of vegetation on three mountains in China (simplified; boundaries are approximate and vary with slope aspect). Changbai Shan (42° N, 2691 m) begins in the temperate forest zone and has a short spectrum ending in krummholz of Erman's birch and alpine tundra; Yushan in Taiwan (23.5° N, 3952 m) begins in tropical lowland forest and passes through evergreen broadleaf and coniferous belts to subalpine shrubland; the southern slope of Namcha Barwa (29.6° N, 7782 m), rising from the Yarlung Tsangpo gorge at about 600 m, has the most complete altitudinal spectrum on Earth, from tropical seasonal rain forest to permanent snow.](./plots/altitudinal-belts.py)

- Changbai Shan, on the border of Jilin and North Korea, rises from the temperate deciduous and mixed forest belt (below about 1100 m, Korean pine with broadleaf trees) through subalpine coniferous forest of spruce and fir (*Picea jezoensis*, *Abies nephrolepis*; about 1100–1800 m) and a krummholz of Erman's birch (*Betula ermanii*; about 1800–2100 m) to alpine tundra with dwarf shrubs (*Rhododendron aureum*, *Dryas*) above about 2100 m, the only alpine tundra in eastern China.
- Yushan, the highest peak of Taiwan, rises from tropical lowland forest (below about 600 m) through montane rain forest and evergreen broadleaf forest of Lauraceae and Fagaceae (to about 1800 m), mixed and coniferous forest of hemlock (*Tsuga chinensis*) and Taiwan cypresses (*Chamaecyparis*) (about 1800–3000 m) and subalpine forest of Taiwan fir (*Abies kawakamii*; about 3000–3600 m) to alpine shrubland of juniper and rhododendron near the summit.
- Namcha Barwa in southeastern Tibet, at the great bend of the Yarlung Tsangpo, spans more than 7000 m of relief: lowland tropical seasonal rain forest in the gorge (below about 1100 m), subtropical evergreen broadleaf forest (about 1100–2400 m), temperate coniferous and broadleaf forest (about 2400–2800 m), subalpine dark coniferous forest of fir (about 2800–3900 m), alpine shrubland and meadow (about 3900–4500 m), sparse alpine vegetation on scree, and permanent snow above about 5000 m. Moist air from the Bay of Bengal penetrates the gorge, which also allows tropical forest at Medog (about 29° N), the northernmost tropical forest in the world.

The altitudinal and latitudinal sequences correspond, and the corresponding belts are similar in physiognomy, but they differ in several ways. Altitudinal belts are much narrower (hundreds of metres rather than hundreds of kilometres) and discontinuous, because each mountain is an island; they differ in species composition; and mountain climates differ from polar ones in having strong radiation, low air pressure, daily rather than seasonal temperature cycles in the tropics, and no polar night. The elevation of the belts also depends on the size of the mountain mass: large massifs heat the air above them more than isolated peaks (the mass-elevation effect), so that on the southeastern Tibetan Plateau the treeline reaches about 4600 m and locally more, the highest in the Northern Hemisphere, whereas on isolated coastal mountains at the same latitude it is more than 1000 m lower.

### 41.2.4. Local Topography and Azonal Vegetation

Within a climatic zone, topography redistributes radiation and water. In the Northern Hemisphere, south-facing slopes receive more radiation and are warmer and drier than north-facing ones; on the Loess Plateau and in the mountains of northern China, forests often grow only on north-facing slopes while south-facing slopes carry grassland or shrubland, and the boundaries of altitudinal belts are higher on south-facing slopes. Steep slopes have thin, dry soils; valley bottoms collect water, nutrients and, on calm nights, cold air, so that frost-sensitive species may grow higher on the slopes than in the valleys (temperature inversion). Vegetation determined by such local conditions rather than by the regional climate is azonal (riparian forests, marshes, salt marshes, mangroves, dune vegetation) or intrazonal, if it is characteristic of a particular zone but confined to special soils, such as the saline meadows of the steppe zone.

## 41.3. Terrestrial Biomes

The following sections describe the main terrestrial biomes in the order of the Whittaker diagram, from warm and wet to cold. Their productivity is compared in [7.19.42](/7.19.42#sec-42.2).

### 41.3.1. Tropical Rain Forest

*Tropical rain forest* grows in the equatorial zone where it is hot (mean annual temperature above about 24 °C, every month above 18 °C, daily range 2–9 °C, greater than the annual range) and wet throughout the year (annual precipitation usually 2000–4000 mm, no month or only short periods with less than 60–100 mm). Rain often falls in afternoon thunderstorms after a sunny morning. The three great formations are the Neotropical rain forest of the Amazon and Orinoco basins and Central America, the African rain forest of the Congo basin and West Africa, and the Indo-Malayan rain forest of Southeast Asia and New Guinea, with smaller areas in northeastern Australia and Madagascar. In China, tropical rain forest and the more seasonal tropical monsoon forest occur in southern Yunnan (Xishuangbanna), on Hainan, in southern Taiwan and in the gorges of southeastern Tibet (Medog).

Structure. The forest is extraordinarily rich in species: a single hectare in the western Amazon may contain more than 250 tree species with a stem diameter of at least 10 cm, and a large Southeast Asian plot more than 1000. The canopy is tall (typically 30–45 m) with scattered emergents reaching 50–70 m (the tallest known tropical tree, a *Shorea faguetiana* in Sabah, is about 100 m), and below it lie lower tree layers, a layer of treelets and shrubs, and a sparse herb layer in deep shade; the layers are often indistinct because each species and each age class fills its own height range. Typical features of the trees are:

- buttresses, plank-like flanges at the base of the trunk that brace shallow-rooted trees;
- leaves of similar size and shape across unrelated species: evergreen, leathery, entire and often with drip tips that shed water quickly;
- cauliflory, the formation of flowers and fruits on the trunk and old branches, which makes them accessible to bats and other animals that cannot fly through the canopy;
- thin, smooth bark, and pollination and seed dispersal mostly by animals.

Climbing plants are a second defining feature: woody lianas, up to more than 200 m long, root in the soil and reach the canopy, where they may make up a large part of the leaves. **Epiphytes** (orchids, bromeliads, ferns, mosses and lichens) grow on the branches without contact with the soil, forming hanging gardens; hemiepiphytes such as strangler figs (*Ficus*) germinate on a host, send roots down to the ground and may eventually kill the host by encircling it. Seasonal rhythms are weak: leaves live about a year or longer and are shed and replaced continuously, and different species flower at different times.

Nutrients and energy. Most rain forest soils are old, deeply weathered and strongly leached (Oxisols and Ultisols, rich in iron and aluminium oxides, acidic, and poor in phosphorus and base cations). Most of the nutrients of the ecosystem are held in the living biomass, and litter decomposes within months ([7.19.44](/7.19.44)); a dense mat of fine roots and **mycorrhizas** near the surface takes up nutrients as they are released, so that the nutrient cycle is tight and almost closed. When the forest is cleared and burned, the nutrients in the ash are quickly lost by leaching and erosion, which is why shifting cultivation on cleared land is productive for only a few years. Tropical rain forests have the highest net primary production of any terrestrial biome (about 2000–2500 g dry matter m⁻² yr⁻¹, with an above-ground biomass of typically 300–400 t ha⁻¹), but also high respiration, so that their net ecosystem production is small ([7.19.42](/7.19.42)).

Importance. Tropical forests as a whole cover about 18 million km², about 45% of the world's forest area (FAO 2020), hold about half of the carbon in the world's vegetation, contain more than half of all terrestrial species, recycle much of their own rainfall (in the Amazon, a quarter to a half of the precipitation is transpired water), and supply timber, rubber, medicines and the wild ancestors of many crops. Most of their large animals are long-lived, slow-breeding species with narrow niches, which recover slowly once disturbed. Deforestation, now concentrated in the Amazon, the Congo basin and Southeast Asia, is the main threat to terrestrial biodiversity ([7.19.51](/7.19.51)).

### 41.3.2. Tropical Seasonal Forest and Savanna

Away from the equator the rainy season shortens and a dry season of increasing length appears. Where it lasts a few months, the rain forest is replaced by tropical seasonal (monsoon) forest, in which many trees shed their leaves in the dry season; where it is longer, by dry deciduous forest and thorn woodland.

*Savanna* is a tropical grassland with scattered trees or shrubs, covering about a fifth of the land surface in Africa, South America (the cerrado and llanos), India and northern Australia. Annual precipitation is typically 500–1500 mm, concentrated in a wet season of three to eight months. The grass layer is dominated by **C4 plants**, which are favoured by high temperature and light and dry out in the dry season to form abundant fuel; fires, often several times a decade, kill tree seedlings but not grasses, whose buds are at or below the ground. In Africa large herds of grazing and browsing mammals, from wildebeest and zebra to elephants and giraffes, and their predators are the most conspicuous feature of the biome; the annual migration of about 1.3 million wildebeest in the Serengeti–Mara ecosystem tracks the rains and the resulting flush of grass.

At precipitation between about 1000 and 2500 mm, savanna and forest occur side by side under the same climate. Satellite data show that tree cover in this range is bimodal, with peaks near 20% (savanna) and 80% (forest) and few sites in between (Staver et al. 2011; Hirota et al. 2011), the signature of alternative stable states maintained by a fire feedback: grassy vegetation burns, and fire keeps it open, whereas a closed forest canopy shades out grass, so that fire cannot spread ([7.19.39](/7.19.39)). Fire suppression, rising $\ce{CO2}$ (which favours C3 woody plants) and the loss of browsers are causing woody encroachment in many savannas.

### 41.3.3. Subtropical Evergreen Broadleaf Forest

On the eastern sides of the continents between the tropics and about 35° latitude, where summers are hot and wet and winters mild, the zonal vegetation is evergreen broadleaf forest. The largest and best-developed area on Earth is in China south of the Qinling–Huaihe line, where it once covered about 2.5 million km²; it extends to southern Japan and Korea, and similar laurel forests occur in Florida, southern Brazil, southeastern Australia and on the Canary Islands. The latitudes of southern China (20°–30° N) are occupied by desert in North Africa and Arabia; China is forested because the East Asian monsoon, strengthened by the Tibetan Plateau, brings summer rain, so that heat and water coincide ([7.19.3](/7.19.3#sec-3.2.4)).

The forest is dominated by evergreen Fagaceae (*Castanopsis*, *Cyclobalanopsis*, *Lithocarpus*), Lauraceae (*Machilus*, *Cinnamomum*, *Phoebe*), Theaceae (*Schima*, *Camellia*) and Magnoliaceae, with leaves that are leathery, glossy and oriented to reflect strong summer light (the "laurophyll" type). The canopy is lower (15–25 m) and the structure simpler than in tropical rain forest, with fewer lianas and epiphytes; there are four seasons, but the evergreen canopy changes little. Biomass and production are lower than in rain forest. Very little primary evergreen broadleaf forest remains in lowland China, which has been farmed for millennia; most of the area is now farmland, plantations of Chinese fir (*Cunninghamia lanceolata*) and masson pine (*Pinus massoniana*), bamboo and secondary forest, with remnants on mountains (Dinghushan in Guangdong, Wuyishan in Fujian, Gutianshan in Zhejiang).

Around the Mediterranean, in California, central Chile, the Cape region and southwestern Australia, winter rain and summer drought produce instead the sclerophyll woodlands and shrublands (maquis, chaparral, matorral, fynbos, kwongan), with small, hard, evergreen leaves, adaptations to fire such as resprouting from lignotubers and **serotiny**, and extraordinary plant diversity in the Cape and southwestern Australia.

### 41.3.4. Temperate Deciduous Forest

Deciduous broadleaf forest (summer-green forest) covers the humid temperate regions with a warm summer and a cold winter of three to five months: eastern North America, western and central Europe, and northeastern Asia, including northern China, Korea and Japan. Annual precipitation is 500–1500 mm (in northern China about 500–900 mm, concentrated in summer); in the warm-temperate zone of northern China the mean annual temperature is about 8–14 °C and the July mean 24–28 °C.

The dominant trees are deciduous species of beech (*Fagus*), oak (*Quercus*), maple (*Acer*), lime (*Tilia*), ash (*Fraxinus*), birch and hornbeam; the canopy, 20–35 m high, is often dominated by one or a few species. The forest has distinct layers of trees, shrubs and herbs, and a strong seasonal rhythm. Spring ephemerals flower and photosynthesize on the forest floor before the canopy closes; the canopy then intercepts 95–99% of the light. In autumn the trees resorb about half of their leaf nitrogen and phosphorus and shed the leaves, which form a litter layer that decomposes over a few years into a fertile, base-rich soil. Animals cope with the winter by hibernating, migrating or storing food (squirrels, jays), and much of the animal biomass is in the soil. In northern China the original oak forests have largely been replaced by farmland and secondary forest; the forests of the Northeast are mixed forests of Korean pine and broadleaf trees.

### 41.3.5. Boreal Forest

*Boreal forest* (taiga) is the largest terrestrial biome after deserts, a belt across North America and Eurasia between about 50° and 70° N covering about 12 million km² (more if open lichen woodland is included), about a quarter of the world's forest area and the largest source of industrial softwood. The climate is cold-temperate and continental: winters are long (six months or more) and severe, with minima below −40 °C in eastern Siberia and the record low of −68 °C at Oymyakon; summers are short and cool to warm; mean annual temperature is mostly between −5 and 5 °C and precipitation is 300–900 mm, but evaporation is low, so the forest is usually moist.

The forest is dominated by a few species of evergreen conifers (spruce *Picea*, fir *Abies*, pine *Pinus*) and, in eastern Siberia and the northern Da Hinggan Ling of China, by deciduous larch (*Larix gmelinii*), the only trees that tolerate the coldest continental climate with permafrost; birch, aspen and poplar dominate after disturbance. Stands are often formed by a single species, with sparse shrubs and a ground layer of evergreen dwarf shrubs (*Vaccinium*), mosses and lichens. The conical crowns shed snow and expose more leaf area to the low sun; the shallow roots are adapted to cold, wet soils and, in the east, to permafrost. Because decomposition is slow, a thick layer of acidic needle litter and moss accumulates (up to tens of tonnes per hectare), the soils are podzols with a bleached leached horizon, and peatlands are extensive; boreal soils and peat hold more carbon than the vegetation. Stand-replacing fires every 50–200 years ([7.19.5](/7.19.5#sec-5.6)), together with insect outbreaks, drive the dynamics of the forest. Animal activity is strongly seasonal: many birds migrate, mammals such as the snowshoe hare and lynx show population cycles ([7.19.26](/7.19.26)), and summer swarms of mosquitoes and blackflies are famous.

### 41.3.6. Temperate Grassland

Temperate grasslands occupy the interiors of the continents where precipitation (usually 250–750 mm) is too low for forest, winters are cold and summers warm: the steppes of Eurasia, from Hungary to Inner Mongolia and northeastern China, the prairies of North America, the pampas of Argentina and Uruguay, and the veld of South Africa. In North America the tallgrass prairie of the east (to about 1000 mm) grades into mixed-grass and shortgrass prairie towards the Rocky Mountains, a gradient that also appears in the precipitation dependence of production ([7.19.42](/7.19.42#sec-42.5.1)). In China the meadow steppe of *Leymus chinensis* and *Stipa baicalensis*, the typical steppe of *Stipa grandis* and *S. krylovii*, and the desert steppe of *S. klemenzii* follow the longitudinal moisture gradient (41.2.2).

Grasses and other herbs dominate; most of the biomass, often more than two thirds, is below ground in a dense mass of roots, and the deep, dark soils (chernozems or mollisols) are rich in humus, which makes temperate grasslands the world's most important regions for grain farming and, therefore, the most extensively converted of all biomes: little more than a few percent of the original tallgrass prairie survives. Grazing by large mammals (bison, wild horses, saiga, gazelles), by burrowing rodents (prairie dogs, marmots, pikas, zokors) and by insects, and periodic fires keep out woody plants. Overgrazing and the ploughing of marginal land degrade grasslands into desert; this is the main cause of desertification in northern China ([7.19.54](/7.19.54)).

### 41.3.7. Desert

Deserts are regions where potential evapotranspiration exceeds precipitation by a large factor; they usually receive less than 250 mm of rain a year (extreme deserts less than 50 mm), and the rain is highly variable. Hot deserts lie under the subtropical high-pressure belts at about 15–35° latitude (Sahara, Arabian, Thar, Sonoran, Australian deserts) and along coasts with cold currents (Atacama, Namib); cold deserts lie in continental interiors and rain shadows, with cold winters (Gobi, Taklimakan, the Great Basin). About a third of China's land area is arid or semi-arid.

Vegetation is sparse, and the plants are spaced out so that each exploits the water of a large volume of soil. The main strategies are drought escape (desert ephemerals that complete their life cycle within weeks after rain and survive as seeds), drought avoidance by deep roots reaching groundwater (phreatophytes such as *Prosopis* and the desert poplar *Populus euphratica* along the rivers of the Tarim basin) or by water storage (succulents with **crassulacean acid metabolism**, such as cacti and agaves), and drought tolerance (**xerophytes** such as creosote bush, saxaul *Haloxylon* and *Reaumuria*) ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.2)). Desert animals are mostly small, nocturnal or burrowing, and minimize water loss; some, like kangaroo rats, can live without drinking ([7.19.5](/7.19.5#sec-5.1.4)). Production in deserts is limited by water and increases nearly linearly with precipitation; it occurs in pulses after rain, which build up reserves (seeds, storage organs) that carry the ecosystem through the dry periods (the pulse–reserve paradigm; Noy-Meir 1973).

### 41.3.8. Tundra

*Tundra* is the treeless vegetation beyond the cold limit of trees: Arctic tundra in a belt north of the boreal forest, around the Arctic Ocean, and alpine tundra above the treeline on mountains. The Arctic growing season lasts only two to three months, with continuous daylight at midsummer and mean July temperatures below about 10 °C; precipitation is low (often below 250 mm), but evaporation is lower still, so the soils are often waterlogged. Arctic soils are underlain by *permafrost*, ground that remains at or below 0 °C for at least two consecutive years, which in places is hundreds of metres thick; only the active layer above it, a few decimetres to a metre or two deep, thaws each summer, and frost action sorts the soil into polygons, circles and stripes.

The vegetation consists of dwarf shrubs (willows, birches, heaths), sedges, grasses, cushion plants, mosses and lichens; most plants are perennials with large below-ground reserves, and many reproduce vegetatively. Net primary production is low (typically 100–400 g dry matter m⁻² yr⁻¹) and limited by temperature and by nitrogen, because decomposition and nitrogen mineralization are very slow; fertilization experiments double production. Animals include lemmings with population cycles, caribou and reindeer, musk oxen, arctic foxes and wolves; in summer, vast numbers of migratory birds breed on the tundra. Permafrost soils hold about 1300–1600 Pg of organic carbon, roughly twice as much as the atmosphere, and its thaw is a major positive feedback on warming ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.2.1)). The Arctic is warming several times faster than the global average, and shrubs are expanding into the tundra ("Arctic greening").

## 41.4. Aquatic Ecosystems

Water covers about 71% of the Earth's surface, and aquatic ecosystems are classified by physical variables: salinity (fresh, brackish, marine), movement (standing or lentic, and flowing or lotic), depth and light, and the relation to the bottom (the pelagic open water and the benthic bottom). Their organisms are grouped by habit: plankton drift with the water (phytoplankton and zooplankton), nekton swim against currents (fish, squid, whales), benthos live on or in the bottom, and periphyton grows attached to submerged surfaces.

### 41.4.1. Lakes

A lake has four zones. The littoral zone is the shallow margin where light reaches the bottom and rooted plants (emergent, floating-leaved and submerged **hydrophytes**) grow; the limnetic zone is the open water down to the depth at which photosynthesis just balances respiration (the compensation depth); the profundal zone is the water and bottom below it, which receives organic matter from above but has no photosynthesis; and the benthic zone is the bottom as a whole. Deep lakes of temperate regions show **thermal stratification** in summer, with a warm epilimnion separated from a cold hypolimnion by the metalimnion and its **thermocline**, and mix in spring and autumn ([7.19.3](/7.19.3#sec-3.3.3)); the mixing returns nutrients from the bottom to the surface and oxygen from the surface to the bottom.

Lakes are classified by their trophic state, the amount of nutrients and primary production. Oligotrophic lakes (typically deep, in nutrient-poor catchments, such as Lake Baikal or many alpine lakes) are clear, poor in nutrients and phytoplankton, and have a well-oxygenated hypolimnion with cold-water fish such as trout and char. Eutrophic lakes (typically shallow, in fertile lowland catchments) are rich in nutrients, turbid with phytoplankton, have a large littoral zone and, in summer, an oxygen-depleted hypolimnion; mesotrophic lakes are intermediate, and dystrophic lakes are stained brown by humic substances from bogs. Carlson (1977) combined the three standard measurements into a trophic state index on a scale from 0 to 100, in which each step of 10 corresponds to a halving of the Secchi depth:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{TSI}(\mathrm{SD}) &= 60 - 14.41\ln z_{\mathrm{SD}}, \\
\mathrm{TSI}(\mathrm{Chl}) &= 9.81\ln c_{\mathrm{Chl}} + 30.6, \\
\mathrm{TSI}(\mathrm{TP}) &= 14.42\ln c_{\mathrm{TP}} + 4.15,
\end{aligned}
$$
with the Secchi depth $z_{\mathrm{SD}}$ in metres and the concentrations of chlorophyll a and total phosphorus in $\mathrm{\mu g\,L^{-1}}$. Values below about 40 indicate oligotrophy, 40–50 mesotrophy, 50–70 eutrophy and above 70 hypereutrophy. For example, a lake with a Secchi depth of 1 m has $\mathrm{TSI} = 60$, and one with $c_{\mathrm{TP}} = 100\,\mathrm{\mu g\,L^{-1}}$ has $\mathrm{TSI} = 14.42 \times 4.61 + 4.15 \approx 70$. Lakes become more eutrophic naturally as they fill with sediment, but nutrient inputs from fertilizer and sewage have made cultural **eutrophication** the most widespread problem of fresh waters ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.3.3)): in Taihu, the third largest freshwater lake of China, a bloom of the cyanobacterium *Microcystis* in 2007 contaminated the drinking water of Wuxi. Shallow lakes can switch between a clear state dominated by submerged plants and a turbid state dominated by phytoplankton, a classic example of alternative stable states ([7.19.39](/7.19.39)).

### 41.4.2. Rivers and the River Continuum

Running waters change from source to mouth: headwater streams are narrow, steep, cold, well oxygenated and often shaded by trees; downstream the channel widens, the gradient decreases, the water warms and becomes more turbid, and the bed changes from rock and gravel to sand and silt. Stream ecologists number the segments of a network by stream order (Strahler): the smallest permanent tributaries are of order 1, and two segments of order $n$ join to form one of order $n + 1$; the Amazon is of order 12. The organisms of a stream classify their food by particle size: coarse particulate organic matter (CPOM, larger than 1 mm, mostly leaves and wood), fine particulate organic matter (FPOM, 0.45 µm to 1 mm) and dissolved organic matter.

**Definition 41.4.1 (Functional Feeding Group)** A **functional feeding group** is a class of stream invertebrates defined by how they obtain food rather than by taxonomy (Cummins 1974): shredders feed on coarse particulate organic matter, mainly leaves conditioned by fungi (caddisfly larvae with cases, stoneflies, amphipods); collectors feed on fine particulate organic matter, either gathering it from the bed (collector–gatherers, such as many mayfly and chironomid larvae) or filtering it from the water (collector–filterers, such as blackfly larvae and net-spinning caddisflies); grazers (scrapers) scrape the film of algae and microbes from stones (snails, heptageniid mayflies); and predators eat other animals.

**Definition 41.4.2 (River Continuum Concept)** The **river continuum concept** (Vannote et al. 1980) describes a river system as a continuous gradient of physical conditions from headwaters to mouth, to which the communities respond in a predictable sequence of energy sources and functional feeding groups:

- in shaded headwaters (orders 1–3), the energy base is allochthonous litter from the riparian forest, primary production is low, respiration exceeds production ($P/R < 1$, the community is heterotrophic), and shredders and collectors dominate;
- in middle reaches (orders 4–6), the channel is open to the sky, attached algae and macrophytes grow, production exceeds respiration ($P/R > 1$), and grazers and collectors dominate;
- in large rivers (orders above 6), the water is deep and turbid, the energy base is again mainly FPOM transported from upstream with some phytoplankton, $P/R < 1$, and collectors dominate the benthos; plankton-feeding fish appear.

The diversity of the community is highest in the middle reaches, where the variation of temperature and food is greatest.

![The river continuum concept (Vannote et al. 1980; schematic values). A: the ratio of gross primary production to community respiration along a river system: headwaters shaded by forest and large turbid rivers are heterotrophic (P/R < 1), middle reaches open to light are autotrophic (P/R > 1). B: the expected composition of the benthic invertebrates by functional feeding group: shredders decline downstream as the supply of coarse litter falls, grazers peak where attached algae are most productive, collectors dominate large rivers, and predators make up a roughly constant share.](./plots/river-continuum.py)

The river continuum concept was developed for forested temperate rivers in North America and has been qualified in several ways. Dams interrupt the continuum and reset conditions downstream (the serial discontinuity concept; Ward and Stanford 1983): below a reservoir the water is clear, cold and poor in particles, like that of a headwater stream. In large floodplain rivers, much of the production and the exchange of organic matter happens on the floodplain during the annual flood (the flood pulse concept; Junk et al. 1989). Rivers in deserts, in grasslands and in the tropics need not be shaded at the source, and the river ecosystem synthesis (Thorp et al. 2006) emphasizes that rivers consist of patches of different hydrogeomorphic types rather than a smooth gradient. The predictions about functional feeding groups have nevertheless been broadly confirmed in forested streams, and the concept remains the framework for comparing rivers.

### 41.4.3. Wetlands

Wetlands are ecosystems in which water saturates the soil or covers it at shallow depth for long enough to produce anoxic hydric soils and a vegetation of hydrophytes; the Ramsar Convention defines them broadly, including marine water no deeper than 6 m at low tide. The main types are marshes (dominated by herbaceous plants such as reeds, sedges and cattails), swamps (dominated by trees or shrubs, such as the cypress swamps of the southeastern United States), and peatlands, in which the production of plant matter exceeds its decomposition in waterlogged soil so that peat accumulates: bogs are acidic, fed only by rain and dominated by *Sphagnum* mosses, and fens are fed by groundwater and less acidic. Although peatlands cover only about 3% of the land, they store about 500–600 Pg of carbon, a third or more of all soil carbon, because decomposition under waterlogging is extremely slow ([7.19.44](/7.19.44)). Wetlands are among the most productive ecosystems (41.4.4; [7.19.42](/7.19.42)), regulate floods, filter nutrients and are breeding grounds for waterbirds; about a third or more of the world's wetlands have been lost since 1970. In China, the largest are the marshes of the Sanjiang Plain in the Northeast, the Zoigê (Ruoergai) peatlands on the eastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau and the lake wetlands of the middle Yangtze (Dongting, Poyang), wintering grounds of most of the world's Siberian cranes.

### 41.4.4. Estuaries, Salt Marshes and Mangroves

An *estuary* is a semi-enclosed coastal body of water where fresh water from a river mixes with sea water. Salinity varies with the tide and the river flow, which only euryhaline organisms tolerate, but estuaries are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth: rivers bring nutrients, the tide mixes the water and traps particles, and the shallow water and the surrounding intertidal flats support productive plants and algae. Many fish and crustaceans use estuaries as nurseries, and the intertidal flats of the Yellow Sea are the key stopover of the East Asian–Australasian flyway of migratory shorebirds.

The intertidal margins of sheltered coasts are occupied by two vegetation types. *Salt marshes* of salt-tolerant grasses, rushes and succulents (**halophytes**) dominate in temperate and boreal regions; in China, the smooth cordgrass *Spartina alterniflora*, introduced from North America in 1979 to stabilize mudflats, has become one of the most damaging invasive plants of the coast ([7.19.51](/7.19.51)). *Mangroves* are forests of salt-tolerant trees and shrubs (*Rhizophora*, *Avicennia*, *Kandelia*, *Bruguiera*, *Sonneratia*) on tropical and subtropical coasts, roughly between 30° N and 30° S, covering about 147 000 km² (Global Mangrove Watch, 2020). Their adaptations include aerial roots that supply oxygen to roots in anoxic mud (stilt roots of *Rhizophora*, pneumatophores of *Avicennia*), salt exclusion by the roots or excretion through salt glands, and vivipary: the seedlings of *Rhizophora* and *Kandelia* germinate on the parent tree and drop into the mud or float away. In China, mangroves grow naturally in Hainan, Guangdong, Guangxi, Fujian and Taiwan, the northernmost natural stands (*Kandelia obovata*) at about 27° N in Fujian. Salt marshes, mangroves and **seagrass** meadows bury organic carbon in their sediments at high rates per unit area ("blue carbon"), protect coasts from storms and erosion, and are being lost to aquaculture and land reclamation.

### 41.4.5. The Oceans

The ocean is divided horizontally into the intertidal zone between the tidal limits, the neritic zone over the continental shelf (to a depth of about 200 m) and the oceanic zone beyond the shelf, and vertically into the pelagic zones of the open water (epipelagic to 200 m, mesopelagic to 1000 m, bathypelagic to 4000 m, abyssopelagic to 6000 m and hadal in the trenches, to almost 11 000 m in the Mariana Trench) and the corresponding benthic zones of the bottom. Light defines the euphotic zone, where photosynthesis exceeds respiration (to at most about 150–200 m in the clearest water), the dysphotic zone, with light enough for vision, and the aphotic zone below about 1000 m ([7.19.3](/7.19.3#sec-3.4.3)). Temperature at depth is nearly constant at 2–4 °C, and pressure increases by about 1 atmosphere (0.1 MPa) for every 10 m.

The open ocean is limited by nutrients, not light: over most of the tropical and subtropical ocean a permanent thermocline separates the sunlit surface from the nutrient-rich deep water, and the central gyres are oceanic deserts. Production is high where nutrients reach the surface: in coastal upwelling zones (Peru, California, northwestern and southwestern Africa), where winds drive surface water offshore and deep water rises to replace it, which support a large fraction of the world's fish catch; at the equatorial divergence; in the seasonally mixed waters of high latitudes; and near the coasts. In some regions (the Southern Ocean, the subarctic Pacific) nitrate is abundant at the surface but production is limited by iron ([7.19.42](/7.19.42#sec-42.5.2)).

Several marine ecosystems are especially rich:

- Rocky intertidal shores show sharp vertical zonation of barnacles, mussels, seaweeds and their predators, set by tolerance of exposure at the upper limit and by competition and predation at the lower limit; they were the setting of classic experiments on competition and **keystone species** ([7.19.22](/7.19.22), [7.19.33](/7.19.33)).
- Kelp forests of giant brown algae (*Macrocystis*, *Laminaria*, *Ecklonia*; [7.12.8](/7.12.8)) grow on rocky bottoms in cool, nutrient-rich coastal waters, reach 30 m or more in height and have production rates among the highest on Earth; where sea otters have been lost, sea urchins graze them into barrens, a **trophic cascade** ([7.19.39](/7.19.39)).
- Coral reefs are built by scleractinian corals in clear, warm (above about 18–20 °C), shallow tropical waters that are poor in nutrients. Their high production in a nutrient-poor ocean (Darwin's paradox) depends on the mutualism between the coral animal and its photosynthetic dinoflagellates (**zooxanthellae**, family Symbiodiniaceae), which pass most of their photosynthate to the host, and on the tight recycling of nutrients within the reef ([7.19.29](/7.19.29)). Covering less than 0.1% of the ocean floor, reefs harbour about a quarter of all marine species. Marine heatwaves now cause repeated mass **coral bleaching**, the breakdown of the symbiosis, and ocean acidification threatens calcification ([7.19.47](/7.19.47)).
- The deep-sea floor is a heterotrophic ecosystem fed by the rain of organic particles from above ("marine snow") and occasionally by large falls such as whale carcasses. At hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, by contrast, chemolithoautotrophic bacteria oxidizing hydrogen sulfide or methane support dense communities of tube worms (*Riftia*), clams and mussels that harbour symbionts, an ecosystem independent of sunlight for its primary production ([7.11.29](/7.11.29#sec-29.9.4)).

## 41.5. Biomes and Climate Change

Since biome boundaries follow climatic thresholds, a change of climate moves them. The movements of the past are recorded in pollen: after the last glaciation, temperate trees spread northward across Europe and North America at rates of a few hundred metres per year, and the forest belts of northern China shifted by hundreds of kilometres between glacial and interglacial periods. Today the climatic zones are moving faster, poleward by several kilometres per decade and upward by several metres per decade. Observed responses include the advance of treelines (about half of the treelines studied have moved up or north since 1900; Harsch et al. 2009), shrub expansion in the Arctic tundra, the upward shift of alpine plants on European mountains, and woody encroachment in savannas. Models that couple climate with vegetation (from the Holdridge scheme to dynamic global vegetation models) predict further large shifts this century and warn of tipping points: the drying of the eastern Amazon could convert rain forest to savanna once deforestation and warming exceed a threshold, and thawing permafrost could release large amounts of carbon. Because plants migrate more slowly than climatic zones move, and because fragmented landscapes block their movement, many biome shifts lag behind the climate, and extinctions may follow ([7.19.47](/7.19.47), [7.19.49](/7.19.49)).

## Summary

- A **biome** (Definition 41.1.1) is a major ecosystem type defined by the growth form of its dominant vegetation; similar climates produce similar biomes on different continents by convergent evolution.
- Climate determines biomes through physiological thresholds: the treeline at a growing-season temperature of about 6–7 °C, the freezing limits of evergreen and deciduous trees, the water balance, and the length of the growing season. The Whittaker diagram (temperature against precipitation), Walter's climate diagrams and zonobiomes, and the Holdridge life zones (biotemperature, precipitation and the PET ratio on logarithmic axes) are three ways of mapping climate to vegetation; the first and last ignore seasonality.
- Zonality (Definition 41.2.1) arranges biomes by latitude (heat), by distance from the sea (moisture) and by altitude (0.6 °C per 100 m). Eastern China has the full humid sequence from tropical forest to boreal larch forest because of the monsoon; northern China shows the longitudinal sequence from forest to steppe to desert. Changbai Shan, Yushan and Namcha Barwa illustrate altitudinal spectra; altitudinal belts resemble latitudinal ones in structure but are narrower, discontinuous and differ in species.

- Aquatic ecosystems are classified by salinity, flow, depth and light. Lakes have littoral, limnetic, profundal and benthic zones and are classified by trophic state (the Carlson index). Rivers follow the river continuum concept (Definition 41.4.2): heterotrophic, shredder-dominated headwaters, autotrophic middle reaches with grazers, and collector-dominated large rivers, interrupted by dams and floodplains. Wetlands, estuaries, salt marshes, mangroves, kelp forests and coral reefs are among the most productive ecosystems; the open ocean is nutrient-limited except in upwelling regions; hydrothermal vents are powered by chemosynthesis.
- Biome boundaries are moving poleward and upward with climate change, with lags and possible tipping points.

## Questions

1. Wuhan (mean annual temperature about 17 °C, precipitation about 1300 mm) and Rome (about 16 °C, 800 mm) are not far apart in the Whittaker diagram, yet the natural vegetation is evergreen broadleaf forest around Wuhan and sclerophyll woodland around Rome. Explain the difference.

   **Answer** The Whittaker diagram uses annual means and ignores the seasonal distribution of precipitation. Wuhan has a monsoon climate in which most rain falls in the warm season, when plants can use it: heat and water coincide, so a broadleaf forest with large, thin leaves can transpire through the summer, and the mild winter allows evergreen leaves. Rome has a Mediterranean climate: rain falls in the cool winter and the hot summer is dry, so plants face drought precisely in the season of highest evaporative demand; small, hard, long-lived evergreen leaves (sclerophylls) that tolerate drought and fire are favoured. A Walter climate diagram shows the difference at once: an arid summer period in Rome, none in Wuhan.

2. Calculate the Holdridge PET ratio for a site with a biotemperature of 24 °C and annual precipitation of 2000 mm, and for one with 6 °C and 500 mm. Which humidity provinces are they in, and what vegetation do you expect?

   **Answer** PET = 58.93 × 24 ≈ 1414 mm, so the PET ratio is 1414/2000 ≈ 0.71, between 0.5 and 1, the humid province: at a biotemperature of 24 °C (the boundary of the tropical region) this is tropical (or subtropical) moist forest. For the second site, PET = 58.93 × 6 ≈ 354 mm and the ratio is 354/500 ≈ 0.71, also humid; at 6 °C (the boundary of the boreal and cool temperate regions) this is boreal moist forest or cool temperate moist forest. The example shows the logic of the scheme: the same precipitation is four times "wetter" in a climate whose biotemperature is four times lower.

3. On a mountain at 30° N the lowland vegetation is evergreen broadleaf forest at 500 m, where the mean annual temperature is 17 °C. Estimate the elevation of the treeline, assuming that the growing-season mean temperature at the treeline must be 6.5 °C, that the growing season at the treeline lasts from May to September with a mean temperature 6 °C above the annual mean, and a lapse rate of 0.6 °C per 100 m. What would change on a large plateau?

   **Answer** The treeline lies where the annual mean temperature is 6.5 − 6 = 0.5 °C, that is 17 − 0.5 = 16.5 °C colder than at 500 m, which requires an ascent of 16.5/0.6 × 100 ≈ 2750 m: the treeline would be at about 3250 m. On a large plateau, the mass-elevation effect warms the air relative to the free atmosphere at the same altitude, so the isotherms and the treeline are higher (on the southeastern Tibetan Plateau above 4500 m). The estimate is also sensitive to the assumed seasonal amplitude: the growing season is longer on low-latitude mountains, which raises the treeline further.

4. According to the river continuum concept, how should the ratio $P/R$ and the proportion of shredders change below a deep reservoir built on a fourth-order forest river? Explain.

   **Answer** The concept predicts $P/R > 1$ and few shredders for a fourth-order river. Below the dam, the reservoir traps coarse particles and releases water from the hypolimnion, which is cold, clear and poor in particulate organic matter: conditions resemble a headwater stream in temperature, but without the litter input. The supply of CPOM is cut off, so shredders remain scarce; the clear water and stable flow favour attached algae, so $P/R$ may rise rather than fall, and grazers and collector–filterers feeding on plankton exported from the reservoir increase. Further downstream, tributaries and the riparian zone gradually restore the gradient, so the dam shifts the continuum (the serial discontinuity concept).

5. (Open question) Why do the coral reefs of the tropical ocean, which is poor in nutrients, have some of the highest gross primary production on Earth? What does this imply for their vulnerability?

   **Answer** Points to include: (a) production per unit area is high because light and temperature are favourable and the reef is three-dimensional, with a large surface of photosynthetic tissue (zooxanthellae in corals, turf algae, coralline algae); (b) nutrients are scarce in the water but are recycled tightly within the reef: the coral host retains nitrogen and phosphorus from its own metabolism and from captured plankton and passes them to the symbionts, which return carbon; nitrogen fixation by cyanobacteria and imports by currents and fish complete the budget; (c) net production of the whole reef is small, because most gross production is respired, so the reef is a high-turnover, nearly closed system. The vulnerability follows: the system depends on a mutualism that breaks down above a narrow temperature threshold (bleaching), on clear water (which nutrient enrichment and sediment destroy by favouring macroalgae and plankton), and on herbivores that keep algae down; once corals die, reefs can shift to an alternative algae-dominated state.
