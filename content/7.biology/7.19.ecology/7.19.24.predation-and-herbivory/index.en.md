Predation links the populations of [7.19.8](/7.19.8)–[7.19.21](/7.19.21) into food chains, and through them into food webs ([7.19.38](/7.19.38)). Before its population dynamics can be modeled ([7.19.25](/7.19.25)–[7.19.27](/7.19.27)), the interaction itself must be understood: who eats whom, how much of each prey a consumer takes, how prey resist and tolerate being eaten, and how far the death or damage of individual prey translates into changes in prey abundance. This chapter treats these questions in turn: the kinds of predators and their diets (Section 24.1), the defences of animal prey and their coevolution with predators (Section 24.2), herbivory and the responses of plants (Section 24.3), and the effects of predation on prey populations (Section 24.4). The behavioral side of foraging, the prey model and the marginal value theorem, is developed in [7.18.22](/7.18.22)–[7.18.23](/7.18.23), and the defences of animals against predators in [7.18.24](/7.18.24); here we use their conclusions and concentrate on the consequences for populations. Parasites and pathogens are treated in [7.19.28](/7.19.28), parasitoids and their hosts in [7.19.27](/7.19.27).

## 24.1. Predators and Their Diets

### 24.1.1. Kinds of Predation

**Definition 24.1.1 (Predation)** In the broad sense used in ecology, **predation** is the consumption of all or part of one living organism (the prey) by another (the predator), the prey being alive when first attacked. In the narrow sense it is the killing and eating of animals by animals.

Dead organic matter is excluded: its consumers are detritivores and decomposers ([7.19.44](/7.19.44)). The broad definition covers several kinds of interaction that differ in how many prey a consumer attacks in its lifetime and whether the attack is lethal (Begon and Townsend 2021):

| Type | Prey attacked in a lifetime | Effect on each prey | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| True predators | many | killed, usually soon after attack and often eaten whole | wolves, eagles, spiders, carnivorous plants; also seed-eating rodents and ants, baleen whales |
| Grazers | many | part removed; harmful but rarely lethal in the short term | sheep, cattle, grasshoppers; blood-sucking flies and leeches |
| Parasites | one or very few, in intimate association | part consumed; harmful but rarely lethal in the short term | tapeworms, flukes, viruses, rusts and mistletoes; aphids and many caterpillars on their host plant |
| Parasitoids | one | eventually killed | wasps and flies whose larvae develop in or on a single insect host ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)) |

The **parasitoid** ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)) shares the intimacy of parasites and the inevitable lethality of true predators; with an estimated 10 % or more of all animal species, parasitoids are among the most important consumers of insects. Cannibalism, the consumption of members of the same species, is a special case of predation with important consequences for population regulation ([7.19.11](/7.19.11)).

**Definition 24.1.2 (Herbivory)** **Herbivory** is the consumption of living plant tissue (leaves, stems, roots, sap, pollen, nectar, seeds or fruits) by animals. Depending on the part eaten and the duration of the association, herbivores act as true predators (seed predators that kill seeds), grazers or browsers (removing part of many plants), or parasites (a caterpillar or aphid developing on one plant).

Because most plants are modular ([7.19.8](/7.19.8)) and most herbivores remove only part of each plant, herbivory is usually not lethal, and the plant's response to the loss of tissue becomes central (Section 24.4).

### 24.1.2. Classification by Diet

The traditional taxonomic classification divides consumers into carnivores, which eat animals, herbivores, which eat plants, and omnivores, which eat food from more than one trophic level. A second axis is the breadth of the diet.

**Definition 24.1.3 (Specialist and Generalist)** A **specialist** consumer feeds on one or a few prey types; a **generalist** feeds on many. For herbivorous insects and other consumers of plants, diet breadth is often described as monophagous (feeding on a single host species or genus), oligophagous (a few related hosts, usually within one family) or polyphagous (many hosts in several families) ([7.17.37](/7.17.37)).

The koala (*Phascolarctos cinereus*) eats almost only the leaves of *Eucalyptus*, the snail kite (*Rostrhamus sociabilis*) almost only apple snails of the genus *Pomacea*. Most true predators have relatively broad diets, whereas parasitoids and parasitic herbivores are usually specialists: in tropical forests, insects whose larvae develop inside seeds were the most specialized (99 % feeding within one plant family, 76 % on a single species), followed by leaf-miners, fruit-eaters, leaf-chewers and sap-suckers, wood-eaters and, least specialized, root-feeders (Novotny and Basset 2005). Specialization goes with intimacy: a consumer that lives on a single host can afford to adapt to its particular chemistry, but is then poorly adapted to others.

**Example 24.1.1 (The Diets of Chinese Mammal Orders)** Mapping the main diet of each order onto the phylogeny of Chinese mammals shows that diet is broadly conserved within orders but has changed repeatedly between them:

| Order | Example in China | Main diet |
|---|---|---|
| Proboscidea | Asian elephant (*Elephas maximus*) | plants (grasses, browse, fruit) |
| Sirenia | dugong (*Dugong dugon*) | seagrasses |
| Primates | humans (*Homo sapiens*), macaques | omnivorous; some leaf-eaters (snub-nosed monkeys) |
| Scandentia | northern treeshrew (*Tupaia belangeri*) | insects and fruit (omnivorous) |
| Rodentia | Eurasian red squirrel (*Sciurus vulgaris*) | mainly seeds and other plant parts |
| Lagomorpha | Tolai hare (*Lepus tolai*) | plants |
| Eulipotyphla | Amur hedgehog (*Erinaceus amurensis*) | invertebrates |
| Chiroptera | Asian particolored bat (*Vespertilio sinensis*) | insects (some fruit- and nectar-eating bats) |
| Pholidota | Chinese pangolin (*Manis pentadactyla*) | ants and termites |
| Perissodactyla | Przewalski's horse (*Equus ferus przewalskii*) | grasses |
| Artiodactyla (incl. Cetacea) | wild boar (*Sus scrofa*) | most herbivorous; wild boar omnivorous; whales and dolphins carnivorous |
| Carnivora | snow leopard (*Panthera uncia*) | mostly animals; red and giant pandas mainly bamboo |

The giant panda (*Ailuropoda melanoleuca*) is the most famous exception. It belongs to the bears, retains the short, simple gut of a carnivore and digests only about a fifth of the dry matter of the bamboo that forms more than 99 % of its diet, compensating by eating 10–20 kg a day and minimizing energy expenditure. Stable isotopes in bone collagen of fossil and historical pandas indicate that their diet was more varied in the past and that the extreme specialization on bamboo, together with the contraction of the range to the mountains of Sichuan, Shaanxi and Gansu, is relatively recent (Han et al. 2019). Wild pandas still occasionally eat meat and gnaw bones.

### 24.1.3. Food Preference and Switching

A consumer *prefers* a food type if the type forms a larger proportion of its diet than of the food available to it. Preference may reflect value (energy gained per unit handling time, the profitability of the prey model in [7.18.22](/7.18.22)), the need for a balanced diet (many insects regulate their intake of protein and carbohydrate to a target ratio, Behmer 2009), or the avoidance of toxins: the food intake of ringtail possums feeding on *Eucalyptus* fell with the concentration of the toxin sideroxylonal in the leaves, not with their nutrient content (Lawler et al. 2000).

Some consumers have fixed preferences. Others *switch*: they eat a prey type disproportionately often when it is common and disproportionately rarely when it is scarce (Murdoch 1969). Guppies offered fruit flies at the water surface and tubificid worms at the bottom concentrated on whichever was more abundant; the mirid bug *Macrolophus pygmaeus*, a biological control agent on tomatoes, switches between whiteflies and the invasive tomato leafminer *Tuta absoluta* (Jaworski et al. 2013). Switching arises when prey types live in different microhabitats and the consumer concentrates on the most profitable one, when predators form a **search image** ([7.18.24](/7.18.24)) for common prey, or when practice improves the capture and handling of a common type. Its population consequence is important: by attacking whichever prey is common, a switching predator imposes frequency-dependent mortality that favors rare prey species, which stabilizes their coexistence (apostatic selection at the level of species; [7.19.25](/7.19.25), [7.19.33](/7.19.33)).

### 24.1.4. Diet Width and the Optimal Diet

The **prey model** of **optimal foraging theory** ([7.18.22](/7.18.22)) predicts which prey types a forager should accept so as to maximize its long-term rate of energy intake: rank types by profitability $E_i/h_i$ and add them to the diet while the profitability of the next type exceeds the rate obtained without it ([Theorem 22.5.1](/7.18.22#thm-22.5.1) of 7.18.22). Its consequences for diet width are the ones relevant here.

- *Searchers should be generalists.* If search times are long relative to handling times, as for insectivorous birds gleaning small insects, almost any prey found is worth eating.
- *Handlers should be specialists.* If prey are easily found but handling is long and costly, as for lions, which live in sight of their prey, the predator should take only the most profitable prey (for lions, the young, the old and the sick).
- *Diets should be broader in unproductive environments.* When good prey are scarce, the search time saved by accepting poorer prey matters more. Bluegill sunfish and great tits had more specialized diets at high prey density in experiments, and bears fishing for salmon in Alaska ate only the most energy-rich parts (eggs, brains) of fish in years of abundance (Gende et al. 2001).
- *The inclusion of a prey type does not depend on its own abundance*, only on the abundance of better types.

The predictions are qualitatively supported, especially for foragers on immobile prey; predators of mobile prey are often limited more by differences in prey vulnerability than by their own choices (Sih and Christensen 2001). Predation risk also shapes diet and habitat use: small bluegills restricted their foraging to weed beds in the presence of largemouth bass, at the cost of a lower intake (Werner et al. 1983), so that the realized niche of a prey can be set by its predators as well as by its competitors ([7.19.23](/7.19.23)).

## 24.2. Prey Defences and Coevolution

### 24.2.1. Defences of Animal Prey

Prey resist predation at every stage of the predation sequence: encounter, detection, identification, approach, subjugation and consumption ([7.18.24](/7.18.24)). Three broad strategies can be distinguished, for animal and plant prey alike (Begon and Townsend 2021): deterrence (avoiding attack, by not being found or recognized or by warning), resistance (injuring the attacker or surviving the attack) and tolerance (compensating for the damage).

- *Avoiding detection.* **Crypsis** ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)), matching the background in color, pattern and behavior, is the first line of defence of green caterpillars and grasshoppers and of transparent plankton; masquerade, resembling an inedible object, is shown by caterpillars of the viceroy butterfly (*Limenitis archippus*) that look like bird droppings.
- *Warning.* Unpalatable or dangerous prey are often conspicuous: **aposematism** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Monarch caterpillars (*Danaus plexippus*) store the cardiac glycosides of their milkweed food plants, and a naive blue jay that eats an adult monarch vomits and thereafter rejects monarchs on sight; monarchs reared on cabbage are palatable (Brower 1969).
- *Mimicry.* Palatable species resembling unpalatable models (**Batesian mimicry**) and unpalatable species resembling each other (**Müllerian mimicry**, [7.10.17](/7.10.17)) exploit predators' learning.
- *Physical and chemical defences, escape and group defence.* Spines, shells and armor; secretions (sulfuric acid in some sea slugs, terpenoid glycosides in sponges); flight, burrows and death-feigning; and, in groups, vigilance, dilution and mobbing ([7.18.27](/7.18.27)). Like plant defences, animal defences can be constitutive or inducible: the snail *Nucella lamellosa* from shores where crabs are common has constitutively wider, squatter shells, and snails from all sites thicken the lip of the shell when exposed to the scent of crabs eating snails, more so when they come from high-risk sites (Bourdeau 2012); *Daphnia* grow helmets and spines in the presence of predator kairomones.

### 24.2.2. Coevolution and the Red Queen

Defences select for counter-adaptations in predators, which select for further defences: an **evolutionary arms race** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Predators evolve sharper teeth, claws and beaks, venom, lures and cooperative hunting; prey evolve crypsis, warning colors, mimicry, speed, alarm calls and group defence. The asymmetry of the **life–dinner principle** ([7.18.24](/7.18.24)), that the fox runs for its dinner but the rabbit for its life, suggests that selection on prey is usually stronger, so that prey tend to stay ahead. The **Red Queen hypothesis** ([7.10.14](/7.10.14)), named after the character in *Through the Looking-Glass* who must keep running to stay in the same place, captures the idea that interacting species must evolve continually merely to maintain their fitness relative to each other.

**Coevolution** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)) between particular pairs of species is, however, harder to demonstrate than adaptation of each to the other. The species-rich tree genus *Inga* in the Peruvian Amazon varies greatly in chemical, physical and ant-mediated defences. The phylogenies of the caterpillars that eat *Inga* do not match the phylogeny of the trees, but they do match a "defensogram" grouping the trees by similarity of defences: the herbivores have tracked defences to which they were already adapted, rather than cospeciating with their hosts (Endara et al. 2017).

### 24.2.3. Introduced Predators and Naive Prey

Prey that have evolved without a type of predator often lack effective defences against it, and introduced predators are among the most destructive invasive species. The brown tree snake (*Boiga irregularis*), introduced to Guam after the Second World War, eliminated most of the island's native forest birds; domestic cats introduced to islands have contributed to at least 14 % of the global extinctions of birds, mammals and reptiles (Medina et al. 2011); Nile perch (*Lates niloticus*) introduced to Lake Victoria in the 1950s contributed to the loss of a large part of the lake's endemic cichlid fishes; the American bullfrog (*Lithobates catesbeianus*), farmed and released in China, preys on and competes with native frogs. The naivety of prey, the failure to recognize a novel predator as a threat, is one reason for these impacts; another is the release of introduced predators from their own enemies and competitors. These cases are revisited in the context of biological invasions ([7.19.51](/7.19.51)).

## 24.3. Herbivory and Plant Defence

The effect of herbivory on a plant depends on which herbivore attacks which part of the plant and when, and above all on the response of the plant. Plants cannot run away, but they have three advantages over animal prey: they are modular, so that lost parts can be replaced; they can invest energy that animals spend on movement in structures and chemicals; and many herbivores remove only part of the plant.

### 24.3.1. Kinds of Defence

Plant defences are classified along two axes.

- *Constitutive or inducible.* A **constitutive defense** is produced regardless of attack; an **induced defense** ([7.13.26](/7.13.26)) is produced or increased in response to attack. Constitutive defences are always in place but always costly; inducible defences save costs when attack is absent but leave the plant vulnerable during the lag before they are effective.
- *Direct or indirect.* Direct defences harm or deter the herbivore; indirect defences attract its enemies, such as the **extrafloral nectary** that feeds protective ants, or the volatile compounds released by damaged leaves that attract parasitoids and predators of the herbivore.

Physical defences include spines and thorns (holly, acacias), tough or silica-rich leaves (grasses), hooked or glandular **trichome** ([7.12.4](/7.12.4)) hairs, and the hard shells of nuts. Chemical defences are mostly **secondary metabolite** compounds ([7.13.26](/7.13.26)), traditionally defined as compounds not required for growth and reproduction: cyanogenic glycosides, **glucosinolate** compounds of crucifers ([7.12.44](/7.12.44)), **alkaloid** compounds such as nicotine and caffeine, terpenoids, phenolics and tannins. The distinction between primary and secondary metabolism is less sharp than once thought: anthocyanins also regulate osmotic balance and seed dormancy, cyanogenic glycosides store and transport nitrogen (Nielsen et al. 2013).

### 24.3.2. Apparency and Optimal Defence

Why do plants differ so much in how they are defended? The most influential framework is the *optimal defence hypothesis*, which comprises four linked propositions: (1) organisms invest in defence in proportion to their risk of attack and in inverse proportion to its cost; (2) investment increases when the current risk increases (induction); (3) within the organism, the most valuable tissues are best defended; and (4) organisms with fewer resources invest less in defence, because defence competes with other functions.

*Apparency theory* (Feeny 1976; Rhoades and Cates 1976) applies the first proposition. *Qualitative* defences (toxins such as alkaloids, glucosinolates and cyanogenic glycosides) are effective in small amounts and cheap, but specialists can evolve to detoxify them. *Quantitative* defences (digestibility reducers such as tannins and other phenolics) act in proportion to their concentration, are expensive, but are hard to overcome. *Apparent* plants, large, long-lived and predictably found by herbivores (oaks), should invest in quantitative defences; *unapparent* plants, small, short-lived and patchy (many weeds), should rely on toxins, effective against the generalists that find them by chance. Young bracken fronds emerging in spring are rich in cyanogenic glycosides, while the tannin content rises to a maximum in mature leaves (Rhoades and Cates 1976). A meta-analysis of 158 studies found the predicted preponderance of quantitative defences in woody plants and qualitative defences in herbaceous plants, but in only 68 of 94 and 82 of 114 studies, respectively, and herbaceous plants were as well protected against specialists as against generalists, contrary to the theory (Smilanich et al. 2016). Apparency is a useful starting point rather than a law.

The third proposition is supported by within-plant patterns: wild radish (*Raphanus sativus*), pollinated by insects, has constitutively high glucosinolate concentrations in its petals, twice those of undamaged leaves, whereas the leaves rely on induction (Strauss et al. 2004). The fourth proposition has several versions. The *resource availability hypothesis* (Coley, Bryant and Chapin 1985) predicts that species adapted to resource-poor environments grow slowly, cannot easily replace lost tissue, and invest heavily in defence; tropical tree species of poor soils have tougher, more phenolic leaves than species of rich soils. The *carbon–nutrient balance* and *growth–differentiation balance* hypotheses concern the phenotypic response of individual plants: carbon-based defences should be highest when growth is limited more than photosynthesis, for example at intermediate nutrient supply, as in marula saplings in which flavonoid defences peaked at intermediate fertilization (Scogings 2018).

### 24.3.3. Induced Defences

Herbivore damage, and even the oral secretions or eggs of herbivores, triggers the synthesis of the hormone **jasmonic acid** ([7.13.15](/7.13.15)) and its derivatives, which de-repress genes for defensive compounds such as proteinase inhibitors, alkaloids and volatile signals ([7.13.26](/7.13.26)); **salicylic acid** signaling responds mainly to biotrophic pathogens and sap-sucking insects. Volatiles released by damaged leaves can induce defences in undamaged branches of the same plant and in neighbors. Whether induced defences benefit the plant has to be shown by field experiments that include their costs.

- Caterpillars of *Pieris rapae* feeding on wild radish induced higher glucosinolate concentrations and denser trichomes. Induced plants later suffered 50 % less damage from chewing herbivores and carried fewer aphids than undamaged controls or plants from which the same leaf area had been removed with scissors; their lifetime fitness (seeds × seed mass) was over 60 % higher than that of the controls, whereas clipped plants, which lost tissue without induction, had 38 % lower fitness (Agrawal 1998). In the absence of herbivores, induction brought no benefit and reduced fitness.
- Grazing by the snail *Littorina obtusata* induced phlorotannins in the seaweed *Ascophyllum nodosum* that deterred later grazing; clipping and grazing by a mobile isopod did not, presumably because the slow induced response is useful only against herbivores that stay (Pavia and Toth 2000).
- Many modern maize varieties have lost the ability, retained by about half of the traditional landraces tested and by teosinte, to release parasitoid-attracting volatiles when stem-borers lay eggs on them (Tamiru et al. 2015).

Costs of defence have been demonstrated directly. *Arabidopsis* mutants unable to make glucosinolates grew faster than wild-type plants in the absence of herbivores but more slowly once aphids attacked (Züst et al. 2011); glucosinolate synthesis has been estimated to increase the photosynthetic requirement of the plant by about 15 %. Theory predicts that inducibility is favored when attacks are variable and past attack predicts future risk, constitutive defence when attack is predictable and frequent, and no defence when attack is rare (Karban et al. 1999). ### 24.3.4. Tolerance and Compensation

**Definition 24.3.1 (Tolerance and Compensation)** The *tolerance* of a plant to herbivory is its ability to regrow and reproduce after damage, relative to an undamaged plant. A damaged plant *undercompensates* if its fitness is lower than that of an undamaged plant, *compensates exactly* if it is equal, and *overcompensates* if it is higher.

Plants compensate in many ways: removal of shaded leaves that respire more than they photosynthesize improves the carbon balance of the plant; reserves are mobilized and photosynthate redistributed; the photosynthetic rate of the remaining leaves increases; dormant buds are activated; and the death rate of remaining parts falls. Meta-analyses show that photosynthetic rates are often unaffected or even increased by herbivory, while growth and reproduction are reduced less than the immediate damage would suggest (Zvereva et al. 2010; Stephens and Westoby 2015). Grasses are the most tolerant of grazing: their meristems sit at ground level among the leaf sheaths, out of reach of most grazers, and they regrow leaves from reserves and produce new tillers. Their tolerance helps them in competition with less tolerant plants, which accounts in part for the dominance of grasses in heavily grazed habitats.

Overcompensation has been reported in a few cases. The biennial field gentian (*Gentianella campestris*) clipped to remove half its biomass between 1 and 20 July, when cattle normally graze, produced more fruits than unclipped plants, but plants clipped later produced fewer (Lennartsson et al. 1998). Whether herbivory can increase plant fitness is debated; what is clear is that the timing of damage relative to the plant's development is critical.

At the level of a whole sward, the *grazing optimization hypothesis* (McNaughton 1979) proposes that moderate grazing can increase net primary production above that of ungrazed vegetation, as McNaughton reported for Serengeti grasslands grazed by migrating ungulates. A simple canopy model shows how this can happen and why it is not universal.

**Example 24.3.1 (A Canopy Model of Grazing Optimization)** Let $L$ be the leaf area index of a sward, with canopy photosynthesis $f(L) = a(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-kL})$, which saturates because of self-shading (Beer–Lambert, [7.19.3](/7.19.3)), a maintenance cost $mL$, leaf senescence at rate $\mu$ and removal by grazers at rate $g$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}t} = f(L) - (m + \mu + g)L.
$$
The steady state $L^*(g)$ solves $f(L^*) = (m + \mu + g)L^*$ and decreases with $g$. Net primary production at the steady state is $\mathrm{NPP}(g) = f(L^*) - mL^*$, which as a function of $L$ is maximal at $L_{\mathrm{opt}}$, where $f'(L) = ak\,\mathrm{e}^{-kL} = m$:

$$
L_{\mathrm{opt}} = \frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{ak}{m}.
$$
If the ungrazed canopy is denser than $L_{\mathrm{opt}}$ (its lower leaves are shaded and barely pay their maintenance), grazing that reduces $L^*$ toward $L_{\mathrm{opt}}$ *increases* NPP; further grazing reduces it. With $a = 10$, $k = 0.5$, $m = 1$, $\mu = 0.3$ (Figure 2), the ungrazed canopy has $L^* = 7.5$, $L_{\mathrm{opt}} = 3.2$, and NPP more than doubles at a grazing rate of about 1.2. The offtake $gL^*$ by grazers is maximal at a slightly higher grazing rate, the analogue of the maximum sustainable yield of harvesting theory ([7.19.53](/7.19.53)).

The model makes clear the conditions for overcompensation at the sward level: a dense canopy with much self-shading, ample nutrients and water for regrowth (often recycled through dung and urine), and grazing of the right intensity and timing. Where regrowth is limited by nutrients or water, or where the canopy is sparse, grazing reduces production, which is the more common finding.

### 24.3.5. Effects on Plant Survival, Fecundity and Competition

Herbivores more often increase the susceptibility of plants to other causes of death than kill them outright, and competition is the most common second factor. In a model parameterized by experiments with two perennial herbs of eastern North America, *Solidago altissima* was the superior competitor of *Solanum carolinense*, but only in the presence of insect herbivores, which affected the growth rates, self-limitation and interspecific competition of both species, did its superiority lead to the suppression of *Solanum* (Kim et al. 2013). Herbivory therefore changes the outcome of plant competition, often in favor of the more tolerant or better defended species, and this is how grazers and browsers shape the composition of vegetation ([7.19.33](/7.19.33)). In the steppe of southern Ukraine, protected areas from which grazing was excluded for decades accumulated litter, lost much of their characteristic feather grasses (*Stipa*) and were invaded by other species, whereas moderate grazing maintained the steppe; overgrazing degraded it. Grazing at an appropriate intensity is therefore part of the management of many grasslands.

Effects on survival are nonlinear. In a glasshouse experiment with eight grassland species, one or two clippings had little effect on survival, but four or eight clippings had a disproportionately large one (del-Val and Crawley 2005). Small amounts of damage in the wrong place can kill: ring-barking by squirrels or porcupines cuts the phloem and kills trees while removing little tissue, and slugs that sever the meristems of grass seedlings destroy them. Herbivores that transmit pathogens can do more harm through what they give than through what they take: bark beetles carrying the fungus of Dutch elm disease virtually eliminated elms from southern England in the 1970s and 1980s.

Fecundity is affected indirectly, through reduced growth (smaller plants bear fewer seeds), and directly, through the destruction of flowers, fruits and seeds. Even when growth fully compensates, seed production may be reduced: *Brassica napus* seedlings regrew completely after 25–75 % defoliation but still produced fewer seeds (Gavloski and Lamb 2000). Herbivory frequently delays flowering, which in semelparous plants prolongs life: *Poa annua* mown weekly on a lawn can live almost indefinitely. Not all consumption of reproductive tissues is harmful: pollinators and seed dispersers are mutualists ([7.19.29](/7.19.29)), and scatter-hoarding squirrels and jays that bury seeds and fail to recover some of them disperse the plants whose seeds they eat.

## 24.4. Effects of Predation on Prey Populations

That predators kill or harm individual prey does not imply that they reduce prey abundance. Three reasons why the effect on the population may be smaller than the effect on individuals, and one reason why it may be larger, are considered here.

### 24.4.1. Demographically Unimportant Stages

Predation has little effect on prey abundance if it falls on a stage of the life cycle that does not limit the population. If plant recruitment is limited by sites for germination rather than by seed numbers, seed predators have little effect: sowing 1000 seeds of the nodding thistle *Carduus nutans* per square meter in southern France produced no more rosettes, so the weevil *Rhinocyllus conicus*, which destroyed over 90 % of the seeds, did not reduce recruitment (Crawley 1989). The weevil *Apion ulicis*, introduced to Chile to control gorse, ate up to 94 % of the seeds without affecting gorse invasiveness. In other systems, by contrast, seed predators do limit plant populations (Maron et al. 2002), and identifying the limiting stage is the first question in the biological control of weeds and the management of harvested species ([7.19.9](/7.19.9), key factor analysis).

### 24.4.2. Compensatory and Additive Mortality

The survivors of predation often benefit: they compete less for food, grow faster, survive better or reproduce more. Predation then replaces deaths that density-dependent processes would have caused anyway.

**Definition 24.4.1 (Compensatory and Additive Mortality)** Mortality from a cause (predation, harvest, disease) is *additive* if it adds to the mortality from other causes, so that total mortality increases with it, and *compensatory* if it is offset by reduced mortality (or increased reproduction) from other causes, so that total mortality does not change. With natural mortality $M_0$ and a fraction $h$ killed by the new cause, total mortality is $M = 1 - (1 - M_0)(1 - h)$ if the effect is fully additive, and $M = M_0$ for $h \le c$ if it is fully compensatory up to a threshold $c \le M_0$ (Anderson and Burnham 1976).

Errington (1946) argued from studies of muskrats and bobwhite quail that predators mostly remove a "doomed surplus" of animals that would have died anyway, of starvation, disease or emigration, because their habitat could not support them. Compensation requires density dependence that acts after the predation. A seasonal model shows that the timing of predation relative to density dependence decides how much is compensated (Figure 3).

**Example 24.4.1 (Timing of Predation and Compensation)** Let a population breed in summer, multiplying its spring density $N$ by $\lambda$; let predators (or hunters) remove a fraction $h$ in autumn; and let winter survival be density dependent, $s(N_{\mathrm{w}}) = s_0/(1 + bN_{\mathrm{w}})$, where $N_{\mathrm{w}}$ is the density entering winter. Then

$$
N_{t+1} = \frac{s_0(1 - h)\lambda N_t}{1 + b(1 - h)\lambda N_t},
\qquad
N^* = \frac{\lambda s_0(1 - h) - 1}{b\lambda(1 - h)}.
$$
If instead the same fraction is killed in spring, after the density-dependent winter, $N_{t+1} = (1 - h)s_0\lambda N_t/(1 + b\lambda N_t)$ and $N^* = (\lambda s_0(1 - h) - 1)/(b\lambda)$. The equilibrium with autumn predation is larger by the factor $1/(1 - h)$: the survivors of autumn predation enter a less crowded winter and survive better. Both populations collapse at the same $h = 1 - 1/(\lambda s_0)$, where even complete compensation can no longer make up the losses. In the first year of autumn predation, total mortality from autumn to spring rises less than additively (Figure 3A); at the new equilibrium, total mortality returns to $1 - 1/\lambda$ for any $h$, and the effect of predation appears as a lower density.

:::

Field experiments confirm that compensation depends on the strength of density dependence and therefore on resources. Grasshoppers (*Ageneotettix deorum*) in caged plots in a Nebraska prairie survived equally well with and without predatory wolf spiders when food was of normal quality, because removing spiders increased competition for food; on fertilized plots with better food, the absence of spiders greatly increased survival (Oedekoven and Joern 2000). For waterfowl and game birds, the extent to which hunting mortality is compensatory has been a central question of harvest management since the 1970s; for many species compensation is partial and varies among years ([7.19.53](/7.19.53)).

### 24.4.3. Selective Predation

Predators often take the individuals with the lowest reproductive value ([7.19.14](/7.19.14)): the young, the old, the sick and those without territories. In Kielder Forest on the England–Scotland border, northern goshawks preyed on tawny owls for three decades; juvenile owls, most of which would have died before breeding, were almost four times as likely as adults to be killed, and among adults the risk increased with age. In a matrix model of the owl population the observed pattern of selective predation reduced owl numbers almost as little as predation on juveniles alone, and owl numbers were statistically unrelated to goshawk numbers (Hoy et al. 2015). Wolves on Isle Royale kill mainly moose calves and old, arthritic or parasitized adults. Selective predation is therefore one reason why predators often regulate prey less than their kill rates suggest; it also means that predators can reduce the prevalence of disease in their prey, a point that matters for the management of predators ([7.19.28](/7.19.28)).

The same idea underlies the old notion of the "prudent predator" (Slobodkin 1961), which would take mainly prey of low reproductive value and so avoid overexploiting its prey. Natural selection on individual predators does not favor restraint for the good of the prey population (each predator gains by eating what it can catch); prudent-looking patterns arise instead because the vulnerable prey are the old, young and sick. Stories of prey populations that exploded after their predators were removed, such as the mule deer of the Kaibab Plateau in Arizona after predator control in the early twentieth century, are often cited in this context, but the Kaibab data are unreliable and the irruption had several causes, including the removal of livestock (Caughley 1970). The lesson that predators can regulate prey and that their removal can be followed by irruptions and overgrazing is better supported by experiments and by trophic cascades ([7.19.39](/7.19.39)).

### 24.4.4. Non-Consumptive Effects: the Ecology of Fear

Predators also affect prey that they do not kill. Prey respond to the risk of predation by changing their behavior (vigilance, habitat shifts, reduced foraging), morphology (spines, shells) and physiology (stress hormones). These *non-consumptive* or *trait-mediated* effects are costly, and their costs can reduce fecundity and growth at the population level. The spatial pattern of perceived risk is the **landscape of fear** ([7.18.23](/7.18.23)).

**Definition 24.4.2 (Non-Consumptive Effect)** A **non-consumptive effect** of a predator is a change in the survival, growth or reproduction of its prey caused not by killing but by the responses of the prey to predation risk.

- In the Yukon, excluding mammalian predators from large areas hardly changed the survival of arctic ground squirrels (*Urocitellus parryii*) but improved their body condition, increased the proportion of females weaning litters and the size of litters, and doubled population density; combined with food supplementation the increase was much larger (Karels et al. 2000). The stress response, the release of glucocorticoids that reduces reproduction, is one mechanism.
- Song sparrows (*Melospiza melodia*) on Gulf Islands of British Columbia, protected from actual predation by electric fences and nets but exposed for a breeding season to playbacks of predator calls, laid fewer eggs, fed their nestlings less and produced 40 % fewer offspring than birds exposed to playbacks of non-predators (Zanette et al. 2011).
- A meta-analysis of experiments found that non-consumptive effects on prey abundance were smaller on average than consumptive effects but large and significant, and that they contributed as much as consumptive effects to the total effect (Preisser et al. 2005).

A model makes the population consequence explicit (Figure 4). Let prey grow logistically with birth rate $b$ reduced by fear to $b/(1 + fP)$ in the presence of predators at density $P$, and be killed at rate $aP$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = N\Big(\frac{b}{1 + fP} - d - cN\Big) - aNP,
\qquad N^* = \frac{1}{c}\Big(\frac{b}{1 + fP} - d - aP\Big).
$$
Fear alone ($a = 0$) lowers the prey equilibrium, without a single prey being eaten. Fear and killing together drive the prey to extinction at a predator density well below that needed by killing alone. Fear effects also cascade through food webs: prey that avoid risky places reduce their consumption of plants there, a trait-mediated trophic cascade ([7.19.39](/7.19.39)). The extent to which the return of wolves to Yellowstone affected vegetation through fear, as opposed to through killing elk, remains debated.

## Summary

- **Predation** (Definition 24.1.1) includes true predation, grazing, parasitism and parasitoidism, which differ in the number of prey attacked and the lethality of attack; **herbivory** (Definition 24.1.2) is consumption of living plant tissue by any of these modes.
- Consumers are carnivores, herbivores or omnivores, and specialists or generalists (Definition 24.1.3; monophagous, oligophagous, polyphagous). Diet is conserved within mammal orders with striking exceptions such as the giant panda (Example 24.1.1).
- Preferences reflect profitability, balanced nutrition and toxins; switching predators take common prey disproportionately, which favors rare prey. The prey model predicts broad diets in searchers and in unproductive environments, narrow diets in handlers and in productive ones.
- Prey defend themselves by deterrence, resistance and tolerance: crypsis, aposematism, mimicry, physical, chemical and group defences, constitutive or inducible. Predators and prey are locked in arms races (Red Queen), but strict coevolution is less common than tracking of pre-existing defences; introduced predators devastate naive prey.
- Plant defences are constitutive or induced, direct or indirect, physical or chemical. Apparency theory and the optimal defence hypothesis explain part of their variation; induced defences raise fitness only when herbivores are present (wild radish), and defences have measurable costs (Figure 1).
- Plants tolerate herbivory by compensatory growth (Definition 24.3.1); grasses are especially tolerant. A canopy model shows when moderate grazing can increase net primary production (Example 24.3.1, Figure 2). Herbivory interacts with competition, is nonlinear in its effects on survival, and reduces fecundity even when growth is compensated.
- Predation may fall on demographically unimportant stages, may be compensatory rather than additive (Definition 24.4.1; its timing relative to density dependence decides, Example 24.4.1, Figure 3), and is often selective for prey of low reproductive value. Conversely, non-consumptive effects of fear (Definition 24.4.2) reduce prey fecundity without killing, and with killing can greatly increase the impact of predators (Figure 4).

## Questions

1. A predator has two prey types: type 1 with energy content 20 J and handling time 10 s, type 2 with 6 J and 6 s. Type 1 is encountered at a rate $\lambda_1$ per second of search. Using the prey model, for what values of $\lambda_1$ should the predator ignore type 2? What happens to the predicted diet as the habitat becomes poorer in type 1?

   **Answer** Profitabilities: $E_1/h_1 = 2$ J s⁻¹, $E_2/h_2 = 1$ J s⁻¹. Eating only type 1 gives the rate $R_1 = \lambda_1E_1/(1 + \lambda_1h_1) = 20\lambda_1/(1 + 10\lambda_1)$. Type 2 should be excluded if its profitability is below $R_1$: $1 < 20\lambda_1/(1 + 10\lambda_1)$, that is $1 + 10\lambda_1 < 20\lambda_1$, $\lambda_1 > 0.1$ s⁻¹. If type 1 is encountered less often than once every 10 s of search, the predator should take both; in a poorer habitat the diet broadens, whatever the abundance of type 2.

2. Explain why a switching predator can promote the coexistence of two competing prey species that would otherwise exclude each other.

   **Answer** A switching predator attacks the commoner prey disproportionately, so the per capita predation mortality of each prey species rises with its relative frequency. This negative frequency dependence gives each prey species an advantage when rare: if the competitively superior species becomes common, its predation mortality increases and the inferior species, attacked less, can increase. In the language of [7.19.23](/7.19.23) the predator adds a stabilizing mechanism (intraspecific "apparent" limitation exceeds interspecific limitation), and the invasion growth rates of both prey can become positive.

3. Wild radish plants induced by caterpillar feeding had higher fitness than undamaged plants in the field, while plants clipped with scissors had lower fitness. Why was the scissors treatment necessary, and why does this result not show that induction is always beneficial?

   **Answer** Induction is always accompanied by tissue loss, so comparing induced with undamaged plants confounds the benefit of induction with the cost of damage. The clipped plants lose the same tissue without the full induced response (clipping does not reproduce the herbivore's chemical cues), separating the two effects: the 38 % lower fitness of clipped plants measures the cost of damage, and the 60 % higher fitness of induced plants the net benefit of induction in the presence of later herbivores. Without herbivores, induced plants pay the cost of defence for no benefit; other experiments showed reduced fitness in that case. Induction is beneficial only when attacks are likely to follow, consistent with the fitness model of Figure 1.

4. In the canopy model of Example 24.3.1 with $a = 10$, $k = 0.5$, $m = 1$ and $\mu = 0.3$, would grazing increase net primary production if the maintenance cost $m$ were 3 instead of 1? Explain.

   **Answer** $L_{\mathrm{opt}} = \ln(ak/m)/k = \ln(5/3)/0.5 = 1.02$. The ungrazed steady state solves $10(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-0.5L}) = 3.3L$; at $L = 1.02$ the left side is $3.99$ and the right $3.37$, and at $L = 2$ they are $6.32$ and $6.6$, so $L^* \approx 1.8 > L_{\mathrm{opt}}$. Grazing still increases NPP slightly, until $L^*$ falls to $L_{\mathrm{opt}}$, but the gain is small because the ungrazed canopy is now close to the optimum: high maintenance costs keep the canopy thin. Overcompensation at the sward level requires a dense, self-shaded canopy.

5. Hunting of a duck population removes 20 % of the birds in autumn. Under what conditions will this reduce the breeding population next spring by much less than 20 %? What observations would distinguish compensatory from additive hunting mortality?

   **Answer** The reduction will be much less than 20 % if winter mortality (or spring reproduction) is strongly density dependent and acts after the hunting season, so that survivors face less competition for food or space (Example 24.4.1); it requires that the population be near the limit set by winter resources. Compensation is suggested if total annual survival does not decline with hunting mortality across years or areas (the slope of survival on hunting kill is near zero rather than near $-1$ in the Anderson–Burnham analysis), if non-hunting mortality declines when hunting increases, and if the effect disappears at low population densities, where density dependence is weak. Experimental changes in hunting regulations, with controls, are the strongest test.

6. (Open question) Arctic ground squirrels doubled in density when predators were excluded, although their survival hardly changed. Propose mechanisms and an experiment to separate them.

   **Answer** Mechanisms: in the presence of predators, squirrels spend more time vigilant and less foraging, forage in safer but poorer habitats, and have chronically elevated glucocorticoids; all reduce body condition and reproduction. To separate them: (1) measure foraging time, habitat use and fecal glucocorticoid metabolites inside and outside exclosures; (2) add a treatment in which predators are excluded but predator cues (playbacks of calls, scent) are presented, which should reproduce the reduction in reproduction if fear is responsible; (3) add food supplementation with and without predator cues to test whether better nutrition compensates for the behavioral costs; (4) experimentally manipulate stress hormone levels (e.g. implants) to test whether the physiological pathway alone reduces litter size.
