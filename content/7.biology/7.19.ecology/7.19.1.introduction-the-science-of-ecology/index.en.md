Why do red-eared sliders hatched from warm nests become females? Why is a tree line found at about the same soil temperature on mountains from the Arctic to the equator? Why do some animal populations cycle every ten years while others stay nearly constant, why do the tropics hold more species than the poles, and what limits the amount of plant matter that a hectare of land can produce in a year? These are ecological questions. They concern where organisms live, how many of them there are, and what processes, physical and biological, set these numbers.

Ecology is the last of the biology courses on this site, and it draws on nearly all of them. The physiology of 7.13 and 7.14 explains how individual plants and animals respond to temperature, water and light; evolutionary biology (7.10) explains why they have the traits they have; animal behavior (7.18) explains the decisions of individuals; microbiology (7.11) supplies the decomposers and the agents of nutrient cycles. Ecology asks what these properties of individuals imply for populations, communities and ecosystems, and it studies phenomena, such as population cycles, species diversity and the cycling of carbon, that exist only at these higher levels. Ecology is also a quantitative science: from the logistic equation to models of food webs and climate it uses the mathematics of [2.4](/2.4) and [2.5](/2.5) and the statistics of [3.1](/3.1)–[3.5](/3.5), and the models are developed in full throughout this course.

This chapter introduces the discipline: the definitions of ecology and their history, the levels of organization and the scales at which ecologists work, the subdisciplines, the methods of field work, experiment and theory, and the nature of ecological models. The course then follows the levels upward: the individual and its environment (7.19.2–7.19.7), populations (7.19.8–7.19.21), interactions between species (7.19.22–7.19.30), communities (7.19.31–7.19.39), ecosystems and the globe (7.19.40–7.19.47), landscapes (7.19.48–7.19.49) and applied ecology (7.19.50–7.19.55).

## 1.1. Definition and History

### 1.1.1. The Word

The word *Oecologie* was coined in 1866 by the German zoologist Ernst Haeckel (1834–1919) in his *Generelle Morphologie der Organismen*, from the Greek οἶκος (*oikos*, house or household) and -λογία (*-logia*, study). Ecology is thus the study of the household of nature, the same root as in "economy", the management of a household; Linnaeus had already spoken of an "economy of nature" in 1749. In 1895 the Japanese botanist Miyoshi Manabu (1862–1939) of the Imperial University of Tokyo rendered the word into Japanese with the characters that are still used in East Asia, and the term was brought to China by the botanist Zhang Ting (1884–1950), who had studied in Japan and later taught at Wuhan University.

### 1.1.2. Definitions

Definitions of ecology have changed with the questions ecologists asked.

| Year | Author | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 1866 | Ernst Haeckel | The comprehensive science of the relations of the organism to its environment, organic and inorganic |
| 1927 | Charles Elton (1900–1991) | Scientific natural history |
| 1953 | Eugene Odum (1913–2002) | The study of the structure and function of nature, later of ecosystems |
| 1954 | Herbert Andrewartha (1907–1992) | The scientific study of the distribution and abundance of organisms |
| 1972 | Charles Krebs (born 1936) | The scientific study of the interactions that determine the distribution and abundance of organisms |
| 1980 | Ma Shijun (1915–1991) | The science of the relations between living systems and environmental systems |
| 2021 | Fang Jingyun (born 1959) | The science of the structure, function and dynamics of macroscopic living systems, providing a theoretical basis and solutions for understanding, protecting and using nature |

Each definition emphasizes a different level. Haeckel's is physiological in spirit (the individual and its surroundings); Elton's places ecology in the tradition of field naturalists such as Gilbert White and Darwin; Andrewartha's, written by an entomologist who studied fluctuating insect populations, makes the numbers and ranges of organisms the central object; Odum's makes the ecosystem central. Krebs' revision keeps Andrewartha's object but adds the interactions that explain it, and does not need the word "environment" because the environment is exactly the set of things with which an organism interacts. Ma Shijun, the founder of modern Chinese ecology, extended the object to human society with the concept of the *social–economic–natural complex ecosystem* (Ma and Wang 1984), in which a city or a region is analysed as a coupled system of human society, its economy and the natural environment. Fang Jingyun's definition emphasizes the "macroscopic" levels, from populations to the biosphere, and the applied role of the discipline.

**Definition 1.1.1 (Ecology)** **Ecology** is the scientific study of the interactions between organisms and their environment, and in particular of the distribution and abundance of organisms and the interactions that determine them. The environment of an organism comprises all factors and phenomena outside it that influence it: physical and chemical factors (the abiotic environment) and other organisms (the biotic environment).

The definition has two halves that ecologists often put as two questions: *where* do organisms occur and *how many* are there (pattern), and *why* (process). Because organisms also change their environment (Section 2.3 of [7.19.2](/7.19.2)), the interactions run in both directions.

### 1.1.3. A Short History

Ecology grew out of natural history and became an independent science around 1900.

| Period | Developments |
|---|---|
| Antiquity to 18th century | Descriptions of animals and plants and their habitats (Aristotle, Theophrastus); Linnaeus' "economy of nature" (1749); Malthus on population growth (1798) |
| 19th century | Plant geography of Alexander von Humboldt, who related vegetation to climate along latitude and altitude (1807); Darwin's *Origin* (1859), whose "struggle for existence" is an ecological idea; Haeckel's word (1866); Möbius' "biocoenosis" in oyster banks (1877); Liebig's law of the minimum (1840) |
| 1895–1930 | Plant ecology as a discipline: Warming's *Oecology of Plants* (1895), Clements' theory of succession (1916) and Gleason's individualistic concept (1926); Shelford's law of tolerance (1913); Elton's *Animal Ecology* (1927), with food chains, the pyramid of numbers and the niche |
| 1925–1960 | Mathematical population ecology: the logistic equation (Pearl and Reed 1920), the competition and predation equations of Lotka (1925) and Volterra (1926), Gause's experiments (1934), Nicholson and Bailey (1935); the density-dependence debate between Nicholson and Andrewartha and Birch; Hutchinson's niche (1957) |
| 1935–1970 | Ecosystem ecology: Tansley's "ecosystem" (1935), Lindeman's trophic-dynamic study of a lake (1942), the Odum brothers' energy flow and systems ecology, radiotracers and the International Biological Programme (1964–1974) |
| 1960–1990 | Theoretical and evolutionary ecology: MacArthur and Wilson's island biogeography (1967), r- and K-selection, optimal foraging, May's work on stability and chaos (1973–1976), Tilman's resource competition (1982) |
| 1990–present | Macroecology, metapopulations and landscapes; biodiversity and ecosystem functioning; molecular ecology with DNA markers and environmental DNA; remote sensing; global change ecology, from the carbon cycle to the redistribution of species |

The development has been one of increasing integration of levels and methods. Early plant and animal ecology were largely descriptive; mathematical theory entered through population ecology, physics and chemistry through ecosystem ecology, genetics through evolutionary and molecular ecology. Ecology today is a strongly quantitative science of large data sets and explicit models, and at the same time the science on which environmental policy depends.

## 1.2. The Objects of Ecology: Levels, Emergence and Scale

### 1.2.1. Levels of Organization

The living world is organized as a hierarchy: molecules, cells, tissues, organs, organisms, and above the organism populations, communities, ecosystems, landscapes, biomes and the biosphere. Ecology deals mainly with the levels from the individual upward.

| Level | What it is | Typical ecological questions | Course chapters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual | A single organism | How do organisms respond to and tolerate temperature, water, light and other factors? (physiological ecology) | 7.19.2–7.19.7 |
| Population | Individuals of one species living in the same area at the same time | What determines abundance, its fluctuations and spatial distribution? | 7.19.8–7.19.21 |
| Interacting species | Pairs or small groups of species | How do competition, predation, parasitism and mutualism affect abundance and coexistence? | 7.19.22–7.19.30 |
| Community | All populations of different species (plants, animals, microorganisms) living in one area | What processes determine the composition, structure and dynamics of communities? | 7.19.31–7.19.39 |
| Ecosystem | A community together with its abiotic environment | How do energy flow and matter cycle through living and non-living components? | 7.19.40–7.19.46 |
| Landscape | A mosaic of interacting ecosystems over kilometres | How does spatial pattern affect ecological processes? | 7.19.48–7.19.49 |
| Biome, biosphere | Regional formations of similar vegetation; all life on Earth with the parts of the planet it inhabits | How do climate and life shape each other at global scale? | 7.19.41, 7.19.47 |

The *biosphere* is the part of the Earth in which life exists: the lower atmosphere, the whole hydrosphere and the upper lithosphere, extending from several kilometres up in the atmosphere to the **deep biosphere** of microorganisms kilometres below the sea floor. The term was coined by the geologist Eduard Suess (1875) and developed by Vladimir Vernadsky (1926), who emphasized life as a geological force. Below the individual, molecular ecology applies the methods of molecular biology to ecological questions: species identification by DNA, population genetic structure, phylogeography and the molecular basis of adaptation (7.19.20).

The precise definitions of population (7.19.8), community (7.19.31), ecosystem (7.19.40) and landscape (7.19.48) are given in the chapters that study these levels.

### 1.2.2. Emergent Properties and Hierarchy

Each level has properties that its components lack. A single animal is born and dies once; a population has a birth rate, a death rate, an age structure and a growth rate. A single plant species has a niche; a community has a species diversity and a food-web structure; an ecosystem has a productivity and nutrient cycles. Salt (1979) distinguished two kinds of such properties. *Collective properties* are sums or averages of the properties of components, such as the birth rate of a population (the sum of individual births). *Emergent properties* are new properties that result from interactions among the components and cannot be predicted from the components studied in isolation, such as the oscillations of a predator–prey system or the stability of a food web. **Emergence** in this sense is the reason why ecology cannot be reduced to physiology, just as physiology cannot be reduced to biochemistry; Odum called it the principle of emergent properties and took it as the foundation of a holistic ecology.

Hierarchy theory (Allen and Starr 1982; O'Neill and colleagues 1986) adds two general observations. First, higher levels are larger and change more slowly than lower ones: an individual leaf responds to a passing sunfleck in seconds, the canopy of a forest changes over years, the composition of the forest over centuries. Second, when a phenomenon is studied at one level, the level below supplies the mechanism and the level above the constraints. To understand why a population of voles fluctuates, one looks below at the births, deaths and movements of individuals, and above at the community of predators and the vegetation that set the context. The two broad strategies of ecological research follow from this. One builds from the level below, for example deriving population dynamics from individual birth and death rates; the other relates the properties of the level of interest directly to the environment, for example species diversity to climate. Both are used throughout this course.

### 1.2.3. Scale

The *scale* of an ecological phenomenon is the extent in space and time over which it occurs and the frequency or duration of its events. Three kinds of scale are distinguished: organizational (individual to biosphere), spatial (from the gut of an insect or the cell surface to the whole globe) and temporal (from seconds to millions of years). The three are correlated (figure below): processes that involve larger areas generally take longer, because they are made of many smaller, faster events. Oceanographers drew such space–time diagrams first (Stommel 1963), and Delcourt and colleagues (1983) used them for vegetation change.

![Space–time scales of ecological processes (schematic). Each ellipse spans the typical spatial extent (horizontal, from micrometres to thousands of kilometres) and characteristic time (vertical, from seconds to millions of years) of a class of processes, both on logarithmic axes; the dashed line shows the general tendency for larger processes to be slower. Physiological processes such as enzyme kinetics and photosynthesis are small and fast; individual thermoregulation, foraging, growth and development, population dynamics, dispersal, succession, nutrient cycling, biome shifts during glacial cycles and speciation follow at increasing scales. Disturbances (red diamonds) are exceptions: a treefall, a wildfire or a hurricane acts over a large area within hours to days.](./plots/space-time-scales.py)

Scale matters in three ways. First, patterns depend on the scale of observation: two bird species can be positively associated at the scale of a continent (they share the same climate) and negatively associated at the scale of a single wood (they compete for nest sites); the number of species found depends on the area searched (7.19.34). Levin (1992) called the relation between pattern and scale "the central problem in ecology". Second, results cannot simply be transferred between scales: a nonlinear process averaged over a heterogeneous area does not behave like the process at the average conditions, a consequence of **Jensen's inequality** that appears repeatedly in this course (7.19.4). Third, the scale of a study must match the question: an experiment in a 1 m² plot lasting one summer cannot answer a question about the recovery of a forest after fire. The spatial descriptors used in landscape ecology, grain and extent, are defined in 7.19.48.

## 1.3. Subdisciplines

Ecology is divided in several overlapping ways.

| Basis | Subdisciplines |
|---|---|
| Level of organization | Individual (physiological) ecology, population ecology, community ecology, ecosystem ecology, landscape ecology, global ecology; molecular ecology |
| Taxonomic group | Plant ecology, animal ecology, insect ecology, **microbial ecology**, human ecology |
| Habitat | Terrestrial ecology (forest, grassland, desert, tundra), freshwater ecology (limnology), marine ecology, wetland ecology, urban ecology |
| Neighbouring sciences | Mathematical (theoretical) ecology, chemical ecology, physiological ecology, evolutionary ecology, **behavioral ecology**, ecological genetics, paleoecology, ecological economics |
| Application | Conservation biology, restoration ecology, agroecology, fisheries and wildlife management, pest management, ecological engineering |

These divisions are conveniences. A study of how warming affects the timing of caterpillar emergence and the breeding success of tits is at once physiological, population, community and global ecology, and it uses methods from behavioral ecology and statistics. The chapters of this course are organized by level, because the same principles (limiting factors, density dependence, consumer–resource interactions, energy and mass balance) apply across taxa and habitats.

## 1.4. Methods of Ecology

### 1.4.1. Field Studies

Ecology begins with observation of organisms in nature: where they occur, how many there are, what they eat, when they breed. Field methods range from counting animals along transects and in quadrats, mark–recapture and camera traps (7.19.8), through measuring the growth of trees or the flux of carbon dioxide over a forest, to remote sensing from satellites. Increasingly, ecologists sample traces rather than organisms: faeces collected in Antarctic penguin colonies, for example, can be analysed by DNA metabarcoding to reconstruct the diet of the birds without disturbing them, and environmental DNA in a litre of river water reveals which fishes live upstream.

Long-term studies are particularly valuable, because many ecological processes are slow and many events rare. The Park Grass Experiment at Rothamsted, England, has recorded the effect of fertilizers on a hay meadow since 1856; the Hubbard Brook Experimental Forest in New Hampshire has measured the chemistry of rain and stream water since 1963; networks such as the US Long Term Ecological Research sites and the Chinese Ecosystem Research Network (CERN, since 1988) coordinate measurements across many sites. Field observation also exploits **natural experiments**, situations in which nature or human activity has changed one factor while others stay comparable: the eruption of a volcano, the arrival of an invasive predator on some islands but not others, or the reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone.

Observations reveal correlations, but correlations do not establish causes. Two variables can be correlated because both depend on a third, and the correlation of a species' range with an isotherm does not prove that temperature sets the range (7.19.4).

### 1.4.2. Experiments

Experiments test causal hypotheses by changing a factor deliberately and comparing the outcome with controls. They range from laboratory microcosms (Gause's protozoan cultures, 7.19.22) and greenhouse experiments, through field manipulations (removing a competitor, excluding grazers with fences, adding nutrients to a lake) to whole-ecosystem experiments, such as the clear-cutting of a catchment at Hubbard Brook. The principles of a **designed experiment** apply in ecology as elsewhere ([3.8](/3.8), [7.7](/7.7)): replication, randomization and controls. A frequent error is **pseudoreplication** (Hurlbert 1984): treating repeated measurements within a single experimental unit, such as several plots in one fenced exclosure, as independent replicates of the treatment.

**Example 1.4.1 (Egg Matching by Cuckoos)** The common cuckoo (*Cuculus canorus*) is an obligate brood parasite (**brood parasitism**, [7.18.32](/7.18.32)): females lay their eggs in the nests of other birds, which raise the cuckoo chick. In northeastern China one of its hosts is the Daurian redstart (*Phoenicurus auroreus*), a hole-nesting bird whose females lay either blue or pink eggs. Cuckoo eggs in redstart nests are blue. Measured reflectance spectra, analysed with a model of avian colour vision, show that cuckoo eggs are chromatically much closer to blue than to pink redstart eggs, and field records for 2018–2020 showed that nests with blue clutches were parasitized more often than nests with pink ones (Zhang and colleagues 2023). Two hypotheses can explain the pattern: the cuckoos may choose nests whose eggs match their own (egg matching), or they may parasitize nests at random while hosts with pink eggs reject the mismatched cuckoo eggs more often, so that fewer parasitic eggs are recorded there.

An observational study cannot separate the two, so the authors did an experiment. Next to active redstart nests they placed two unattended nest boxes, 0.5 m on either side, one containing a clutch of blue model eggs and the other a clutch of pink ones. The empty boxes had no host to reject eggs, so the eggs found in them reflect the choice of the cuckoo alone. When the host clutch was blue, cuckoos laid 9 eggs in the host nest, 4 in the blue box and none in the pink box; when the host clutch was pink, they laid 7 eggs in the blue box and only 1 each in the host nest and in the pink box (figure below). Cuckoos thus chose nests by egg colour, even preferring an unattended box with blue eggs over a real host nest with pink eggs: the result supports the egg-matching hypothesis.

![Nest-choice experiment with cuckoos. Each active Daurian redstart nest (centre) was flanked at 0.5 m by two unattended nest boxes holding a blue or a pink model clutch. Bars give the number of cuckoo eggs laid in each box. A: host clutch blue; cuckoos laid in the host nest and the blue box but never in the pink box. B: host clutch pink; most cuckoo eggs went to the unattended blue box. Data read from Zhang and colleagues (2023), Proceedings of the Royal Society B 290: 20222094.](./plots/cuckoo-egg-choice.py)

:::

The example illustrates the logic of ecological experiments: an observed pattern, alternative hypotheses that predict the same pattern, and a manipulation designed so that the hypotheses predict different outcomes. It also shows the limits: the samples are small (the counts are of single eggs from a few dozen trials), and the result concerns one host population.

### 1.4.3. Theory and Models

Theoretical ecology builds mathematical and computational models of ecological systems. Models make assumptions explicit, derive their consequences, suggest experiments and allow prediction. Some models are simple enough to be solved analytically, such as the logistic equation of 7.19.10 or the Lotka–Volterra equations of 7.19.22 and 7.19.25; others are large simulations, such as the Earth system models whose projections of global temperature under different emission scenarios underlie the assessments of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC, 7.19.47). Section 1.5 discusses what models are and how they are judged.

### 1.4.4. Statistical Thinking

Ecological data are variable: no two plots, years or individuals are alike, and the variation is part of what ecology studies. Ecologists therefore need probability ([3.1](/3.1)) to describe variation and chance events, estimation and hypothesis testing ([3.2](/3.2)) to draw conclusions from samples, regression ([3.3](/3.3)) to relate abundance to environmental variables, multivariate methods ([3.4](/3.4)) to summarize communities of many species, and computational methods ([3.5](/3.5)) to fit models to data. Observation, experiment and theory work together: theory suggests what to measure, observation reveals patterns that need explanation, experiments test the proposed causes, and statistics connects all three to the data.

## 1.5. Models in Ecology

### 1.5.1. What a Model Is

A model is a purposeful simplification of a real system. Like a map, it is useful precisely because it leaves out most details: a road map does not show every tree, and a model of a vole population does not follow every vole. The modeller decides which features are essential for the question asked (for example, that each vole produces offspring at a certain rate and that this rate falls as food becomes scarce) and writes them down in a form, usually equations or a computer program, whose consequences can be derived exactly. The value of the model lies in the derivation: it shows which consequences follow from the assumptions, and when the consequences disagree with observation, which assumptions must be wrong.

### 1.5.2. Kinds of Models

Models can be classified in several ways.

- Descriptive and mechanistic models. A descriptive (phenomenological, statistical) model summarizes data, for instance by a regression of species richness on rainfall or a polynomial fitted to a growth curve; it does not claim that its terms correspond to processes. A mechanistic model is built from assumed processes, such as births, deaths, consumption or diffusion, and its parameters have biological meaning. Descriptive models can predict well within the range of the data; mechanistic models are needed to predict outside it and to explain. The figure below compares the two on the same data.
- Deterministic and stochastic models. Deterministic models give one outcome for given parameters and initial conditions; stochastic models include chance, which matters for small populations (7.19.15).
- Discrete and continuous models, in time (difference equations for populations that breed once a year, differential equations for continuous breeding) and in space (patch models, lattices, partial differential equations).
- Analytical and simulation models. Simple models can be analysed with pencil and paper (equilibria, stability, bifurcations); complex ones are explored numerically.

![Descriptive versus mechanistic models (simulated data). Counts (dots) of a population growing logistically from 4 to about 100 individuals (dotted curve: the process that generated the data) are observed on days 0–11 (shaded) with about 8% random error. A cubic polynomial (red) and the logistic equation (blue) are both fitted by least squares. Within the observed range the two curves are indistinguishable; extrapolated beyond it, the polynomial turns down and predicts extinction within a week, whereas the logistic model levels off near its estimated carrying capacity K = 102.](./plots/descriptive-vs-mechanistic.py)

Two older philosophical positions correspond loosely to these strategies. Reductionism holds that a system is best understood by taking it apart and studying the components; in ecology it corresponds to building population and community models from the physiology and behavior of individuals. Holism holds that the whole has properties that cannot be understood from its parts (Section 1.2.2) and studies these properties directly, as systems ecology studies the energy budget of an ecosystem without following individual organisms. Each strategy is incomplete without the other: mechanisms come from the level below, and the questions worth asking come from the level of interest.

### 1.5.3. Generality, Realism and Precision

Richard Levins (1966) argued that a model of a complex system cannot simultaneously maximize generality (applying to many systems), realism (including the actual mechanisms) and precision (giving accurate quantitative predictions), and that modellers must choose which to sacrifice. Strategic models, such as the logistic equation or the Lotka–Volterra competition equations, sacrifice precision for generality and realism of mechanism: they predict qualitative behavior (the existence of a carrying capacity, the conditions for coexistence) across many systems. Tactical models, such as a fisheries model of one stock or a population viability analysis of one endangered species (7.19.15), sacrifice generality for precision in a particular case. Purely statistical models sacrifice realism for precision. The trade-off is not a theorem, but it explains why ecology needs both simple and complex models, and why the simple models of this course are worth studying even though no real population follows them exactly.

### 1.5.4. Judging a Model

"All models are wrong, but some are useful" (Box 1979). A model is never a complete description of nature; the question is whether it answers the question it was built for. Several criteria, often in conflict, are used to judge models.

| Criterion | Question | Conflict |
|---|---|---|
| Parsimony | Does the model contain only the mechanisms needed? | Too simple a model omits essential processes (**underfitting**); too complex a model fits noise and generalizes poorly (**overfitting**). Information criteria such as the **Akaike information criterion** formalize the balance |
| Interpretability | Do the variables and parameters have biological meaning? | A black-box statistical model may predict better than an interpretable mechanistic one |
| Robustness | Do the conclusions survive small changes in parameters and assumptions? | Precise conclusions often depend on precise but poorly known parameters |
| Testability | Does the model make predictions that new data could contradict? | Flexible models fit any data and therefore test nothing |
| Generality | Does it apply beyond the system it was built for? | Levins' trade-off |

When reading a model, whether someone else's or one's own, several questions should be asked. Are the assumptions stated, and how would the conclusions change if they were violated? Where do the parameters come from: from independent measurements, or from fitting the same data that the model is then said to explain? (Fitting and validating on the same data is circular; independent data or **cross-validation** are needed.) Is the uncertainty of the predictions quantified? Has the model been compared with simpler alternatives?

Good models also have an aesthetic quality that experienced modellers recognize. A few lines of mathematics can capture a striking phenomenon: the coupled equations of a predator and its prey generate oscillations without any external cause (7.19.25); a simple nonlinear difference equation for an insect population produces chaos (7.19.12); diffusion, which by itself smooths everything out, can create spatial patterns when combined with local interactions (7.19.30). Such results change how we see the natural phenomena, and that, more than any single prediction, is the purpose of theory.

## 1.6. Pure and Applied Ecology

Ecologists study not only natural ecosystems but also those made or changed by people (fields, plantations, cities, reservoirs, nature reserves) and the effects of human activity on nature: pollution, overharvesting, invasive species, the fragmentation of habitats and global climate change. Human influence is now so pervasive that geologists have proposed naming a new epoch, the Anthropocene, in which humanity is a major geological and ecological force; whatever its formal status, the term expresses that there is hardly an ecosystem left that is free of human influence.

Applied ecology uses ecological understanding to manage populations and ecosystems: to set sustainable harvests of fish (7.19.53), to control pests with minimal damage to other species (7.19.53), to design nature reserves and corridors (7.19.52), and to predict the consequences of climate change (7.19.47). Its success depends on the quality of the underlying science: a harvest quota computed from the wrong population model can drive a stock to collapse, and a reserve too small for the population it is meant to protect fails silently over decades. In this course applications are treated alongside the theory that supports them, and the final part (7.19.50–7.19.55) brings them together.

Ecology as a science should also be distinguished from environmentalism as a social movement. Ecology describes and explains; it can predict the consequences of different choices, for example how many species a given loss of forest will commit to extinction, but the choice itself involves values and interests as well as science. Many environmental questions of current public debate, such as the feeding of wild animals, the control of wild boar, the effects of free-ranging cats or the value of carbon offset trading, have ecological components that can be analysed with the tools of this course, and this course treats them in that way (7.19.52, 7.19.54).

## Summary

- **Ecology** (Definition 1.1.1) is the scientific study of the interactions between organisms and their environment that determine the distribution and abundance of organisms. Definitions have shifted from the individual and its environment (Haeckel 1866) through distribution and abundance (Andrewartha 1954, Krebs 1972) to ecosystems (Odum), the social–economic–natural complex ecosystem (Ma Shijun) and macroscopic living systems (Fang Jingyun).
- Ecology studies the levels from the individual to the biosphere. Each level has emergent properties that arise from interactions among its components; higher levels are larger and slower, the level below gives mechanisms and the level above gives constraints. Spatial, temporal and organizational scales are correlated, and patterns depend on the scale of observation.
- Methods: field observation (including long-term studies and natural experiments), experiments (replication, randomization, controls; Example 1.4.1 on egg matching by cuckoos), theory and models, connected by statistics.
- Models are purposeful simplifications. They are descriptive or mechanistic, deterministic or stochastic, discrete or continuous; mechanistic models are needed to extrapolate and explain. Generality, realism and precision cannot all be maximized (Levins); models are judged by parsimony, interpretability, robustness, testability and generality.
- Applied ecology brings ecological theory to bear on harvesting, pest control, conservation and global change; ecology informs but does not by itself decide environmental choices.

## Questions

1. Krebs' definition of ecology does not mention the environment, whereas Haeckel's does. Is anything lost?

   **Answer** Nothing essential is lost. Krebs defines ecology by its object (distribution and abundance) and by the interactions that determine it; the environment is by definition the set of factors outside the organism that influence it, so "the interactions that determine distribution and abundance" are interactions with the environment, biotic and abiotic. What Krebs' definition gains is precision about the object: ecology is not any study of organisms and their surroundings (which would include much of physiology), but the study of where organisms occur and how many there are. What it loses, if anything, is the explicit statement that organisms also change their environment, which modern ecology (ecosystem ecology, niche construction) emphasizes.

2. Give an example of a collective property and of an emergent property of a population and of a community.

   **Answer** Population: the number of births per year is a collective property (the sum of the births of individuals); the cycle period of a population that oscillates because of time-delayed density dependence or a predator is emergent, because no individual has a period. Community: total biomass is collective; the stability of a food web, or the coexistence of competitors that is possible only because a predator keeps the dominant competitor rare, is emergent.

3. Two bird species are positively correlated in abundance across 100 km × 100 km grid cells of a continent but negatively correlated across 1 ha plots within one forest. Suggest explanations.

   **Answer** At the continental scale both species respond to the same broad gradients (climate, forest cover), so both are common in forested, mild regions and rare elsewhere: the positive correlation reflects shared habitat requirements. Within one forest the habitat is broadly suitable for both, and local processes dominate: competition for nest holes or food, or territorial exclusion, makes them avoid each other, or they prefer different microhabitats (canopy versus shrub layer). The example shows that the sign of an association depends on scale and that an inference about interaction from a correlation must specify the scale.

4. In Example 1.4.1, why were the experimental boxes left unattended by any host, and what would have been lost if the experiment had used only real host nests with blue or pink clutches?

   **Answer** Host rejection is the alternative explanation of the observed pattern: if pink-egg hosts reject cuckoo eggs more often, fewer cuckoo eggs would be found in pink nests even if cuckoos laid at random. Boxes without a host cannot reject eggs, so the number of eggs found in them measures the cuckoos' choice alone. With only real nests, choice and rejection would remain confounded unless the nests were checked immediately after laying, which is difficult. The flanking design also controls for location: the three boxes were so close that site, nest density and the cuckoo's search area were the same.

5. A fisheries scientist fits a polynomial in time to 20 years of catch data and uses it to predict the catch for the next 10 years. Criticize the procedure and propose an alternative.

   **Answer** The polynomial is a descriptive model with no mechanism: it can fit the past data well, but outside the range of the data its behavior is determined by the highest-order term and is arbitrary (as in the figure in Section 1.5.2, where it predicts extinction). Moreover catch depends on fishing effort as well as on stock size, so a time trend in catch confounds the two. A better approach is a mechanistic model of the stock (for example a surplus-production model with logistic growth and harvest proportional to effort, 7.19.53, or an age-structured model), fitted to catch and effort data, with its uncertainty quantified, and with predictions made for alternative levels of effort.

6. (Open question) Choose one of the controversial topics mentioned in Section 1.6 and outline which ecological questions it raises, which data would be needed and which chapters of this course are relevant.

   **Answer** Example: supplementary feeding of wild birds in cities. Ecological questions: does feeding increase survival and breeding success (population ecology, 7.19.9, 7.19.13), does it change which species dominate, favouring a few generalists (community ecology, 7.19.31–7.19.33), does it spread disease at feeders (7.19.28), does it alter behaviour and migration (7.18)? Data needed: comparisons of fed and unfed sites, preferably experimental with replication and controls (Section 1.4.2), long-term counts, disease prevalence. The answer may differ between species, places and seasons, and the decision whether to feed also involves human values (the pleasure of watching birds, the welfare of individual animals), which ecology can inform but not settle.
