Why are there more species in some places than in others? Richness varies across the Earth in strikingly regular ways: it is high in the tropics and low near the poles, it changes with elevation and depth, it is high in productive but not too productive environments, and it rises during succession. Ecologists have proposed more explanations for these patterns than for almost any other phenomenon, and part of the difficulty is that many factors vary together along the same gradients. This chapter first builds a simple framework, MacArthur's model of species packed along a resource axis, which sorts the explanations by what they change (Section 35.1). It then describes the main spatial gradients (Section 35.2), examines the explanations one by one, with the metabolic theory and the mid-domain null model in quantitative form (Section 35.3), and turns to patterns in time (Section 35.4) and to the influence of the regional species pool (Section 35.5). Island area and isolation, which also shape richness, were treated in [7.19.34](/7.19.34); the evolutionary explanations of the **latitudinal diversity gradient**, diversification rates and the time-and-area hypothesis, are developed in [7.10.30](/7.10.30) and [7.10.31](/7.10.31) and are summarized here only so far as they bear on the ecological ones.

## 35.1. A Simple Model of Species Richness

MacArthur (1972) imagined the resources of a community as a single continuum of length $R$, for example the sizes of prey or the heights in the vegetation at which insects are taken. Each species uses a stretch of it, its niche breadth $n$, and adjacent species overlap by an amount $o$ ([7.19.23](/7.19.23) on resource utilization curves and limiting similarity).

**Proposition 35.1.1 (Species Packing on a Resource Axis)** If $S$ species with equal niche breadth $n$ and equal overlap $o < n$ between neighbors cover a fraction $f$ of a resource axis of length $R$, then

$$
S = \frac{fR - o}{n - o}. \tag{35.1}
$$
Richness therefore increases with the range of resources $R$, with the degree of saturation $f$ and with the overlap $o$, and decreases with niche breadth $n$.

**Proof** $S$ intervals of length $n$, each overlapping the next by $o$, cover a total length $Sn - (S - 1)o$, the union of the intervals. Setting this equal to the exploited length $fR$ gives $S(n - o) = fR - o$. The partial derivatives of $S$ with respect to $R$ and $f$ are positive; $\partial S/\partial n = -(fR - o)/(n - o)^2 < 0$; and $\partial S/\partial o = (fR - n)/(n - o)^2 > 0$ whenever the axis holds more than one species ($fR > n$). $\square$

The four routes to more species (Figure 1) organize the explanations of richness that follow. A community may hold more species because a greater range of resources is available (larger $R$), because species are more specialized (smaller $n$), because they overlap more (larger $o$) or because more of the axis is used (larger $f$). The effects of interactions fit into the same scheme. Intense competition keeps the axis saturated but limits overlap; predation can either leave parts of the axis unused, by eliminating prey species, or allow greater overlap, by holding prey below the densities at which they compete; facilitation allows narrower niches; and frequent, severe disturbance leaves communities unsaturated, while moderate disturbance acts like predation (Section 33.3 of [7.19.33](/7.19.33)).

Each species uses a stretch of a resource axis (utilization curves) and overlaps its neighbors; the black bar is the available range of resources R. From top: a reference community; a longer axis allows more species; more specialized species (smaller niche breadth n) pack more species into the same axis; greater overlap o allows more species; an unsaturated community leaves part of the axis (gray) unused and holds fewer species](./plots/resource-axis-model.py)

The model has two limitations. Real niches have many dimensions, and differentiation on one may be compensated by overlap on another (niche complementarity, Section 33.1.1). And it says nothing about the processes that determine $R$, $n$, $o$ and $f$, which is where the explanations of the gradients diverge.

## 35.2. Gradients of Species Richness

### 35.2.1. Latitude

From the poles to the equator, the number of species increases in most groups of organisms, on land, in the sea and in fresh water: trees, ants, butterflies, freshwater fishes, lizards, birds, mammals, marine bivalves, corals (the **latitudinal diversity gradient**, [Definition 30.7.1](/7.10.30#def-30.7.1) of 7.10.30). The number of species of woody angiosperms in plots of the Americas falls more than tenfold from the equator to latitudes of 50–60°, the number of swallowtail butterflies and of North American mammals in a similar way. The gradient is not universal. Penguins and seals are richest in polar regions, conifers and ichneumonid wasps in temperate regions, and aphids, many sawflies and soil nematodes do not follow it; marine species of the open ocean often peak at mid-latitudes, and the gradient in the deep sea differs from that in shallow seas (Section 35.2.3).

### 35.2.2. Elevation

Climbing a tropical mountain takes one through climates that resemble those from the equator to the poles, and richness often declines with elevation: breeding birds in the Nepalese Himalayas and plants in the Sierra de Manantlán in Mexico are examples. About half of all studies, however, find a peak at intermediate elevations (Rahbek 1995). Flowering plants of the Nepalese Himalayas, for example, are richest at 1500–2500 m, and ants in a canyon of the arid Spring Mountains of Nevada increase with elevation, because precipitation, and so productivity, rises with height in this desert range. Elevational gradients are not simply latitudinal gradients compressed: at high elevation the area of each elevation band is usually smaller and its habitats more isolated, so area and isolation (the island effects of [7.19.34](/7.19.34)) contribute to the decline, and a geometric null model (Section 35.3.8) predicts a mid-elevation peak.

### 35.2.3. Depth

In large lakes the cold, dark, oxygen-poor depths hold fewer species than the shallow, well-lit littoral zone. In the sea, photosynthesis is restricted to the euphotic zone, which reaches about 200 m in the clearest oceanic water but often only 10–30 m in turbid coastal water, and attached algae are rare below a few tens of meters; species richness in the water column falls rapidly with depth. The benthos is different. The abyssal plain, 3000–6000 m deep, dark, cold ($-0.5$ to $3\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) and dependent on the rain of organic particles from above, holds remarkably rich communities: about 50 species of polychaete worms per 150 individuals and more than 100 species of macrofauna in 0.25 m² of sediment have been reported, with high **beta diversity** among sites. Depth interacts with latitude: brittle stars (Ophiuroidea), compiled from 1614 expeditions, are richest near the equator in shallow seas but at intermediate latitudes in the deep sea, and their richness is related to temperature on the shelf and upper slope and to the flux of organic carbon in the deep sea (Woolley et al. 2016).

### 35.2.4. Peninsulas and Edges

The number of species often declines from the base of a peninsula toward its tip (the *peninsula effect*), for example for mammals and birds of Baja California and Florida, because the tip is narrower and more isolated from the continental source, and so behaves like an island. The peninsula effect is not universal; in some groups it reflects a gradient of climate rather than geometry. At a smaller scale, the boundary between two communities, an **ecotone**, often holds more species than either community, because it contains species of both and some of its own, the **edge effect** described in [7.19.32](/7.19.32); edges can also be poorer in species that need interior habitat, a point that matters for fragmented landscapes ([7.19.48](/7.19.48)).

## 35.3. Explanations

Many hypotheses have been proposed for the latitudinal gradient. Chinese textbooks group them in a list of seven: evolutionary time, ecological time, spatial heterogeneity, climatic stability, competition, predation and productivity. Two more have become prominent since, the direct effect of energy on metabolism and the geometric constraints and area of the domain. The table summarizes them; the subsections examine each.

| Hypothesis | Mechanism | Route in Eq. (35.1) | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evolutionary time (time and area) | Tropical climates are older and were more extensive; most lineages arose there and few adapted to cold (**phylogenetic niche conservatism**) | Saturation $f$ (temperate regions not yet filled) | Strong phylogenetic support |
| Ecological time | Species have not yet recolonized areas glaciated in the Pleistocene | $f$ | Explains regional anomalies, not the global pattern |
| Climatic stability | Stable climates allow specialization and narrow niches | Smaller $n$ | Correlational; supported by stable, rich deep-sea and gyre communities |
| Spatial heterogeneity | Complex habitats offer more niches | Larger $R$ | Strong at local scales; partly a consequence of richness |
| Competition | Biotic interactions, not physical stress, drive specialization in the tropics | Smaller $n$ | Plausible, hard to test |
| Predation | Predators and pathogens hold prey below competitive densities and act in a density-dependent way | Larger $o$ | Supported by Janzen–Connell effects; cannot be the root cause |
| Productivity | More resources support more populations | $R$ and $f$ | Relationship scale-dependent, often hump-shaped locally |
| Energy (metabolic theory) | Temperature speeds metabolism and evolution | — | Predicts a slope of about $-0.65\,\mathrm{eV}$; often but not always observed |
| Area and geometry | The tropics are the largest climatic zone; ranges placed at random overlap most in the middle of a domain | — | Area important; mid-domain effect a useful null model |

### 35.3.1. Evolutionary Time and History

The tropics have existed as warm, wet environments over long and relatively uninterrupted periods, whereas temperate and polar regions were glaciated repeatedly in the Pleistocene and became cool only in the last 30–40 million years of global cooling. The modern form of this argument is the time-and-area hypothesis, combined with **phylogenetic niche conservatism**: lineages tend to retain the climatic niche of their ancestors. In woody angiosperms of the Americas, temperate floras are phylogenetically less diverse than tropical floras of the same richness, most temperate lineages arose from tropical ancestors after global cooling began about 36 million years ago, and 94 % of divergences within tropical lineages and 90 % within temperate lineages remained in the same zone (Kerkhoff et al. 2014). The long-established richness of the tropics has spread poleward only slowly. History also explains differences between regions at the same latitude. The Arctic and Antarctic seas are both cold, seasonal and icy, but the Antarctic shelf fauna has existed since the Paleozoic, whereas the Arctic basin lost its fauna under thick ice in the last glaciation and is being recolonized: the Antarctic is rich, the Arctic poor. The models of species accumulation under these hypotheses are in [7.10.30](/7.10.30).

### 35.3.2. Ecological Time

On a shorter time scale, species may simply not yet have reached all suitable areas. Tree species of Europe, whose ranges contracted to southern refugia during the glaciations and were blocked by the east–west Alps and Mediterranean, are fewer than in eastern North America and eastern Asia, where north–south mountain chains and continuous lowlands allowed retreat and return; many European trees are still absent from parts of their potential climatic range (Svenning and Skov 2004). Such dispersal lags explain regional differences but not why the gradient is found almost everywhere.

### 35.3.3. Climatic Stability and Seasonality

In a seasonal environment species can share the year (temporal niche partitioning, as among annual plants or the plankton of temperate lakes), but specialization on resources that are available only part of the year is impossible: a long-lived specialist on fruit can exist only where some fruit is available all year, as in the tropics. Richness of birds, mammals and gastropods along the west coast of the Americas decreases as the annual range of monthly mean temperatures increases from Panama to Alaska (MacArthur 1975), but many other factors change along the same coast. More persuasive are rich communities in especially constant environments where productivity and energy are low, such as the abyssal benthos and the oligotrophic subtropical gyres, where a single trawl may bring up 50 species of fish (Barnett 1983).

### 35.3.4. Spatial Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity increases the range of resources $R$ (Section 33.4 of [7.19.33](/7.19.33)), and tropical forests are structurally more complex than boreal ones. But much of this structure is produced by the species themselves: more plant species create more structure for animals. Heterogeneity can amplify a gradient; whether it causes it is doubtful.

### 35.3.5. Competition and Predation

Dobzhansky (1950) argued that in harsh, variable environments natural selection is dominated by physical factors, whereas in benign tropical environments biotic interactions, competition above all, are the main force, leading to finer niche differentiation. The predation hypothesis argues instead that tropical predators, herbivores and pathogens are more numerous and more specialized, keep prey populations below their competitive limits and allow more overlap. Density- and distance-dependent attack by natural enemies near parent trees, the Janzen–Connell effect ([7.19.37](/7.19.37)), is well supported: in a meta-analysis of 154 rigorous tests, the survival of seeds and seedlings was lower at high density and near conspecific adults, at both tropical and temperate latitudes (Comita et al. 2014). But neither competition nor predation can be the root cause of the gradient, since they invite the question of what makes the competitors and predators more numerous. They are better seen as amplifiers: more energy supports more species, more species support more enemies, and more specialized enemies allow still more species.

### 35.3.6. Productivity

If greater productivity means a wider range of resources, or makes rare resources abundant enough to support specialists, richness should increase with productivity. It often does: fish species in North American lakes increase with phytoplankton productivity, seed-eating ants and rodents of the deserts of the southwestern USA with precipitation, and plants of large ecoregions worldwide with **net primary production** ([7.19.42](/7.19.42)). But richness often decreases with productivity, as in the fertilized plots of the Park Grass Experiment at Rothamsted, rich fens of England and Wales, and lakes and estuaries subject to **eutrophication**, where phytoplankton productivity rises while richness falls. Many studies find a hump: richness of lake phytoplankton, of desert rodents in Israel against rainfall, and of deep-sea molluscs against the flux of organic carbon peak at intermediate productivity. A compilation of published relationships found positive, negative, hump-shaped, U-shaped and nonsignificant relationships, positive ones being the most common (Gillman et al. 2015).

Two reasons account for much of this variety. First, a study may sample only part of the productivity range, the rising or the falling limb of a hump. Second, the relationship depends on scale. In ponds of Michigan and Pennsylvania, richness of both plants and animals within ponds (alpha diversity) was hump-shaped against productivity, but richness of whole catchments (gamma diversity) increased monotonically, because the dissimilarity among ponds (beta diversity) increased with productivity (Chase and Leibold 2002). A plausible mechanism is that at high productivity competitive exclusion within a pond is fast and leaves few species, but different ponds end up dominated by different species, through priority effects and chance (Figure 2C). The decline of richness at high productivity, sometimes described with Rosenzweig's (1971) phrase "paradox of enrichment" (coined for the destabilization of predator–prey systems by enrichment, [7.19.26](/7.19.26)), may thus be largely a local phenomenon.

### 35.3.7. Energy and the Metabolic Theory

The energy hypothesis holds that energy acts on richness directly, through the metabolism of organisms at all trophic levels, not only through primary production. At large scales, richness of birds and butterflies in North America is correlated with potential evapotranspiration, a measure of atmospheric energy, and the richness of vertebrates across China with mean annual temperature, though also with precipitation and with an index of vegetation productivity (Luo et al. 2012). The **metabolic theory of ecology** ([7.19.6](/7.19.6)) made the hypothesis quantitative.

**Proposition 35.3.1 (Temperature Dependence of Richness)** Suppose that (i) the total number of individuals $J$ in a region does not change with temperature, and (ii) the steady-state number of species supported by $J$ individuals is proportional to the rate of molecular evolution and diversification per individual, which, like the individual metabolic rate, depends on temperature through the Boltzmann factor $\mathrm{e}^{-E/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)}$ of Eq. (6.1) in [7.19.6](/7.19.6). Then

$$
\ln S = C - \frac{E}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}, \tag{35.2}
$$
where $C$ does not depend on temperature: a plot of $\ln S$ against $1/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$ is a straight line with slope $-E \approx -0.6$ to $-0.7\,\mathrm{eV}$ (Allen, Brown and Gillooly 2002).

**Proof** Under (ii), $S = aJ\,\mathrm{e}^{-E/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)}$ with $J$ the number of individuals and $a$ a constant; under (i), $J$ does not depend on $T$. Taking logarithms, $\ln S = \ln(aJ) - E/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$. $\square$

The assumptions are strong and were made to obtain a testable prediction. Over the range of environmental temperatures from 0 to 30 °C, $1/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$ falls from 42.5 to 38.3 eV⁻¹, so Eq. (35.2) with $E = 0.65\,\mathrm{eV}$ predicts a $\mathrm{e}^{0.65\times4.2} \approx 15$-fold increase of richness (Figure 2A). Allen and colleagues reported slopes close to this for North American trees and amphibians and for fish and prosobranch gastropods. Later tests found much more variation: slopes differed among groups and regions, the relationship was often curved, and in warm regions richness depends more on water than on temperature (Hawkins et al. 2007). Translated into a latitudinal profile, temperature alone predicts a flat gradient within about 20° of the equator, where mean temperatures change little, and a steep decline only at higher latitudes, whereas observed gradients are often steep in the tropics too (Figure 2B). Statistical analyses that separate energy from productivity find that both are associated with richness after accounting for the other, energy most strongly at the largest scales and productivity more uniformly across scales (Belmaker and Jetz 2011). Energy may set an upper limit on richness at global scales, while other factors determine where communities lie below it.

### 35.3.8. Area and the Mid-Domain Effect

The tropics are the largest climatic zone of the Earth: the zone between the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn is the largest of the latitudinal bands, and because the temperature changes little within it, the area of uniformly tropical climate is larger still. By the species–area relationship ([7.19.34](/7.19.34)), a larger area supports more species, larger ranges and larger populations, which lowers extinction and may increase speciation (Rosenzweig 1995). A related argument is that the climates most widespread on Earth support more species: for mammals and amphibians, the global prevalence of a climatic regime predicts richness better than the climate itself (Meyer and Pie 2018).

A second geometric argument concerns the boundaries of a domain. Species ranges cannot extend beyond the edges of a continent, the top of a mountain or the floor of a valley, and ranges placed at random within a bounded domain overlap most in its middle.

**Definition 35.3.1 (Mid-Domain Effect)** The **mid-domain effect** is the increase of species richness toward the center of a bounded geographic domain that arises when the ranges of species are placed at random within the domain, without any environmental gradient (Colwell and Hurtt 1994; Colwell and Lees 2000).

**Proposition 35.3.2 (Richness under Random Range Placement)** Let the domain be $[0, 1]$ and let each of $N$ species have a range $[U, V]$ whose end points are the minimum and maximum of two independent uniform points of the domain. Then the expected number of species whose ranges cover the point $x$ is

$$
\mathbb{E}[S(x)] = 2N\,x(1 - x), \tag{35.3}
$$
a parabola with maximum $N/2$ at the center and zero at the boundaries.

**Proof** A range covers $x$ if and only if one of the two random points lies below $x$ and the other above it. The probability of this is $\mathbb{P}(X_1 < x < X_2) + \mathbb{P}(X_2 < x < X_1) = 2x(1 - x)$ for independent uniform $X_1, X_2$. By linearity of expectation, $\mathbb{E}[S(x)] = N\cdot2x(1 - x)$. $\square$

Other ways of placing ranges at random, for example keeping the observed range sizes and placing their midpoints at random where they fit, give similar humps (Figure 3B). The mid-domain effect is a null model, in the sense of Section 33.1.2 of [7.19.33](/7.19.33): it shows that a peak in the middle of a gradient need not be explained by an optimal environment there. It has been applied to latitude (the tropics lie in the middle of the domain between the poles, or of the Americas between their northern and southern ends) and especially to elevation. For flowering plants of the Nepalese Himalayas, the best model combined the mid-domain effect with an underlying monotonic decline of richness with elevation (Grytnes and Vetaas 2002), which shifts the peak below mid-elevation (Figure 3C). In geometrid moths on 19 elevational gradients worldwide, most of which showed mid-elevation peaks, the factors with the strongest support were the area of each elevation band, productivity multiplied by area ("productive space") and the mid-domain effect; temperature and precipitation alone had little support (Beck et al. 2017). The geometric explanation is weakest where ranges are small relative to the domain, since short ranges produce little overlap effect, and its predictions depend on the assumed distribution of range sizes, so it is best used as a null expectation against which environmental effects are measured.

No single hypothesis accounts for all gradients, and the factors interact. The current view is that energy and water, acting on productivity and metabolism, set the broad pattern, that the age and area of tropical climates, together with niche conservatism, explain why that pattern has been filled unequally, and that biotic interactions amplify differences once they exist.

## 35.4. Patterns in Time

### 35.4.1. Richness during Succession

In a succession that runs its full course, the number of plant species usually rises in the early stages, as species colonize, and may fall again in late stages as competitive dominants exclude others ([7.19.36](/7.19.36)). The rise is partly a matter of accumulation: later stages are more completely colonized from surrounding communities (larger $f$). But succession also starts a cascade. The first plants add resources and heterogeneity that were absent before (resource-depletion zones in the soil, a variety of microhabitats, food for herbivores), increasing $R$; herbivores and predators that follow can promote further coexistence; and a closed canopy buffers temperature, humidity and wind, providing the constancy that allows specialists to persist. Animals usually follow the plants: bird richness increased during the recovery of abandoned open-cast mines in the Czech Republic (faster with natural than with uniform managed reclamation), amphibian richness increased after the abandonment of cornfields in Mexico, whereas in regrowing Atlantic forest in Brazil habitat-generalist small mammals declined as the forest matured.

### 35.4.2. Richness through Geological Time

Over the Phanerozoic, the number of marine families and genera increased in several phases, interrupted by the five mass extinctions, and the question of whether global diversity approaches an equilibrium, as on an island, or is still rising, remains open ([7.10.31](/7.10.31)). Present-day patterns carry this history: the regions and habitats that have existed longest and been least disturbed tend to be the richest, and the effects of the last glaciation are still visible in the flora and fauna of the northern continents.

## 35.5. Regional and Historical Influences on Local Richness

Every local community is assembled from a regional species pool, the set of species that could potentially reach and live in it, and local richness reflects both local processes and the size of the pool. Plotting local richness against regional richness for a set of regions tests their relative importance (Cornell and Lawton 1992). If local communities are unsaturated, local richness rises in proportion to regional richness (a "type I" relationship); if local interactions limit the number of species that can coexist in a site, local richness levels off as the regional pool increases ("type II"). Most published relationships are close to proportional, suggesting that many local communities are not saturated and that regional processes, speciation, dispersal and history, strongly influence local richness, though the method has statistical pitfalls (the pattern depends on the scale of "local" sampling and on pseudoreplication; Srivastava 1999). The relationship between local and regional processes is the subject of metacommunity theory ([7.19.37](/7.19.37)).

At the largest scale, richness and endemism are concentrated: 25 "hotspots" covering 1.4 % of the land surface hold 44 % of all vascular plant species and 35 % of all species of terrestrial vertebrates other than fishes as endemics (Myers et al. 2000). The concentration of **endemic species** in hotspots, many of them tropical mountains and Mediterranean-climate regions, reflects all the factors discussed in this chapter, and it is the basis for setting conservation priorities ([7.19.50](/7.19.50), [7.19.52](/7.19.52)).

## Summary

- MacArthur's model (Proposition 35.1.1): $S = (fR - o)/(n - o)$; more species with a wider range of resources, narrower niches, greater overlap or fuller saturation.
- Gradients: richness decreases from the tropics to the poles in most groups (with exceptions), often peaks at mid-elevations, decreases with depth in the water column but is high in the abyssal benthos, and may decline along peninsulas; ecotones may be richer than the adjoining communities.
- Explanations (table in Section 35.3): evolutionary time and area with **phylogenetic niche conservatism**; ecological time; climatic stability; heterogeneity; competition and predation as amplifiers (Janzen–Connell); productivity, with relationships that are positive, negative or hump-shaped and change from local to regional scale; energy, with the metabolic prediction $\ln S = C - E/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$ (Proposition 35.3.1); area and geometry, with the mid-domain null model $\mathbb{E}[S(x)] = 2Nx(1 - x)$ (Proposition 35.3.2).
- Time: richness rises during succession through accumulation and a cascade of heterogeneity, interactions and constancy; history shapes present patterns.
- Local richness depends on the regional pool; most local–regional relationships suggest unsaturated communities; hotspots concentrate richness and endemism.

## Questions

1. Using Eq. (35.1), compare two communities on an axis of length $R = 20$: in one, niches have breadth $n = 4$ and overlap $o = 1$; in the other, competition is weaker because of predation, so overlap increases to $o = 2$. Both are fully saturated. How many species does each hold?

   **Answer** First: $S = (20 - 1)/(4 - 1) = 6.3$, about six species. Second: $S = (20 - 2)/(4 - 2) = 9$ species. Allowing greater overlap, as predation may by holding prey below their competitive densities, increases richness by half in this example. This is the route by which predators can raise prey diversity in Section 33.2 of [7.19.33](/7.19.33).

2. Under the metabolic prediction with $E = 0.65\,\mathrm{eV}$, how much richer should a region with a mean annual temperature of 25 °C be than one at 5 °C? Why might observed differences be larger?

   **Answer** $1/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$ is $1/(8.617\times10^{-5}\times278.15) = 41.72\,\mathrm{eV^{-1}}$ at 5 °C and $1/(8.617\times10^{-5}\times298.15) = 38.92\,\mathrm{eV^{-1}}$ at 25 °C; the ratio of richness is $\mathrm{e}^{0.65\times2.80} = \mathrm{e}^{1.82} \approx 6.2$. Observed differences can be larger because the warm region may also be older and larger (time and area), more productive, less seasonal and less recently glaciated; and because the assumptions of Proposition 35.3.1, constant total abundance and diversification proportional to metabolism, need not hold.

3. Show that in the mid-domain model of Proposition 35.3.2 the mean range length is $1/3$ of the domain, and explain why a model in which all species have very short ranges produces almost no mid-domain effect.

   **Answer** The range length is $\lvert X_1 - X_2\rvert$ for independent uniform $X_1, X_2$, whose mean is $\int_0^1\int_0^1\lvert x - y\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y = 1/3$. With very short ranges of length $\ell$ placed at random where they fit, the probability of covering a point $x$ is about $\ell/(1 - \ell)$ for all $x$ farther than $\ell$ from the edges, so richness is nearly constant across the domain and drops only within $\ell$ of the boundaries. The hump arises because long ranges are forced to overlap the middle; the mid-domain effect is therefore strongest for groups with large ranges relative to the domain (birds on a small continent, plants on a single mountain).

4. In the pond study of Chase and Leibold, alpha diversity was hump-shaped and gamma diversity increased with productivity. What must be true of beta diversity, and what mechanism could produce it?

   **Answer** With multiplicative partitioning, $\beta = \gamma/\alpha$; if $\gamma$ increases while $\alpha$ declines at high productivity, $\beta$ must increase, so ponds become more different from one another as productivity rises. Possible mechanisms: at high productivity competitive exclusion is fast and the identity of the dominant species depends on the order of arrival (priority effects) or on chance, so different ponds converge on different dominant species (alternative stable states); at low productivity, harsh conditions filter the same few tolerant species into every pond. Predation that varies among ponds could also increase turnover.

5. (Open question) Choose one gradient of richness in China, such as the decline of tree species from the tropical forests of Xishuangbanna to the boreal forests of the Greater Khingan Mountains, or the elevational pattern on the Gongga Mountain, and outline how you would distinguish the energy, area and history hypotheses.

   **Answer** Possible approach for the latitudinal gradient of trees: (1) compile species lists from standardized plots (to control sampling area) and estimate richness with rarefaction or Hill numbers; (2) obtain climate (temperature, precipitation, potential evapotranspiration), productivity (satellite NPP) and the area of each climate type in China and adjacent regions; (3) test the metabolic prediction by regressing $\ln S$ on $1/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$ and comparing the slope with $-0.65\,\mathrm{eV}$, and fit models with water and energy together; (4) use a dated phylogeny of the woody flora to test time and niche conservatism: are northern floras composed of a few young clades derived from tropical ancestors, and does phylogenetic diversity fall faster than species richness? (5) examine the history of glaciation and refugia (southwestern China as a refugium) for regional anomalies; (6) for an elevational gradient, compare the observed pattern with a mid-domain null model that uses the observed range sizes, and include the area of each elevation band. No single analysis decides; agreement across independent lines of evidence is the goal.
