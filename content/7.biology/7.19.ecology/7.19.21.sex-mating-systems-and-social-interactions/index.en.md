Individuals of a population do not interact only by competing for resources ([7.19.11](/7.19.11)). They mate, choose mates and compete for them; they hold territories, form hierarchies and groups; plants poison their neighbors. Behavioral ecology asks why individuals behave in these ways, and [7.18](/7.18) answers that question for sexual selection ([7.18.28](/7.18.28)–[7.18.29](/7.18.29)), mating systems ([7.18.30](/7.18.30)), territoriality ([7.18.26](/7.18.26)), group living ([7.18.27](/7.18.27)) and cooperation ([7.18.33](/7.18.33)), and [7.10.13](/7.10.13)–[7.10.16](/7.10.16) for the evolution of sex, sex ratios and altruism. This chapter asks the population ecologist's question: what do these intraspecific relationships do to the size, growth, structure and persistence of populations? It treats in turn the mode of reproduction (Section 21.1), sex ratios (Section 21.2), mating systems (Section 21.3), territories and hierarchies (Section 21.4), allelopathy (Section 21.5) and aggregation and altruism (Section 21.6). Throughout, the evolutionary arguments are recalled briefly and linked; the emphasis is on demographic consequences, which connect to density dependence and regulation ([7.19.11](/7.19.11), [7.19.13](/7.19.13)), Allee effects ([7.19.10](/7.19.10)), effective population size ([7.19.20](/7.19.20)) and the distribution of individuals among habitats ([7.19.16](/7.19.16)).

## 21.1. Sexual and Asexual Reproduction

### 21.1.1. Modes of Reproduction

**Definition 21.1.1 (Sexual and Asexual Reproduction)** In **sexual reproduction** a new individual develops from a zygote formed by the fusion of two gametes, usually from two parents, each produced by meiosis, so that the offspring combine the genomes of the parents. In **asexual reproduction** new individuals arise from a single parent without the fusion of gametes, and are genetically identical to it except for new mutations.

Asexual reproduction takes several forms.

- *Vegetative reproduction.* Plants produce new individuals from roots, stems or leaves: **vegetative propagation** ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)) by tubers (potato), runners (strawberry), bulbs (daffodil), rhizomes (bamboo), and, with human help, cuttings (willow) and grafts. The products are ramets of one genet ([7.19.8](/7.19.8)).
- *Spores.* Many algae, fungi, mosses and ferns produce mitotic spores, each of which grows into a new individual; in the life cycles of land plants, meiotic spores alternate with gametes ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)).
- *Budding and fragmentation.* *Hydra* and many other cnidarians, sponges and colonial ascidians bud off new individuals; flatworms, annelids and many aquatic plants regenerate whole organisms from fragments.
- *Parthenogenesis.* An egg develops without fertilization (**parthenogenesis**, [7.10.14](/7.10.14)). It may be *occasional* (tychoparthenogenesis), as when unfertilized eggs of the silkworm *Bombyx mori* sometimes develop; *constitutive* in one sex, as in honeybees (*Apis*), in which unfertilized eggs develop into males (arrhenotoky, the basis of **haplodiploidy**, [7.4.6](/7.4.6)); *obligate*, in all-female species of some lizards, snails and weevils; or *cyclical*.

**Cyclical parthenogenesis** ([7.17.12](/7.17.12)), alternating parthenogenetic and sexual generations, is common in aphids (the cotton aphid *Aphis gossypii*), cladocerans (*Daphnia*) and rotifers, and shows most clearly the ecological roles of the two modes. In the sycamore aphid *Drepanosiphum platanoidis*, which lives on sycamore maple (*Acer pseudoplatanus*; Chinese textbooks mistranslate the host as the plane tree), winter eggs hatch in spring as the buds open, and several parthenogenetic generations of females exploit the rich sap of the growing leaves; populations grow rapidly in spring, stop reproducing in a summer diapause when the leaves mature and sap quality falls, resume briefly in autumn when the leaves senesce and amino acids are exported, and in autumn produce males and sexual females whose fertilized eggs overwinter (Dixon 1966). Parthenogenesis exploits a temporarily abundant resource; sex precedes the dispersal or dormancy that bridges an unpredictable change.

The unicellular green alga *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* shows the same logic. Haploid vegetative cells divide mitotically while conditions are favorable. When nitrogen is scarce, cells differentiate into gametes of two mating types, mt⁺ and mt⁻, which fuse in pairs; the diploid zygote develops a thick wall and becomes a dormant, resistant zygospore, which undergoes meiosis and releases four haploid cells when conditions improve.

### 21.1.2. The Cost of Sex and Its Ecology

Sex has a large immediate cost. In a species with separate sexes, a female that reproduced asexually would produce only daughters, each of which would again reproduce; a sexual female devotes half her offspring to sons, who produce no offspring themselves. Other things being equal, an asexual clone therefore increases twice as fast as a sexual population, the **twofold cost of sex** ([Theorem 14.1.1](/7.10.14#thm-14.1.1) of 7.10.14). Further costs include finding mates, the risk of sexually transmitted infection, and the breaking up of favorable gene combinations by recombination. Asexual reproduction also has ecological advantages: a single colonist can found a population, and a well-adapted genotype is copied intact.

Why, then, is sex so widespread? The evolutionary explanations ([7.10.14](/7.10.14)) are that sex accelerates adaptation and purges deleterious mutations (Muller's ratchet, the Hill–Robertson effect) and that it produces variable offspring in a variable environment. The ecological version of the last idea is the **Red Queen hypothesis** ([7.10.14](/7.10.14)): parasites evolve to attack the commonest host genotypes, so a sexual population, whose genotypes are continually reshuffled, keeps ahead of them (Hamilton 1980). The New Zealand freshwater snail *Potamopyrgus antipodarum* has both sexual and asexual populations; males, and hence sexual reproduction, are more frequent in lakes where the snails are heavily infected by castrating trematodes (Lively 1987).

The distribution of the two modes fits these ideas. *Geographic parthenogenesis* is the tendency of asexual forms of plants and animals to occur at higher latitudes and altitudes, on islands and in disturbed or marginal habitats, where they colonize readily and biotic interactions may be weaker, while their sexual relatives occupy the center of the range (Vandel 1928). Asexual reproduction is favored where rapid colonization of empty, temporary habitats matters (the parthenogenetic phase of aphids and *Daphnia*), sexual reproduction where the environment, especially the biotic environment, changes unpredictably.

## 21.2. Sex Ratios

### 21.2.1. Fisher's Principle

The **sex ratio** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)) of a population is the ratio of males to females, or the proportion of males. Ecologists distinguish the *primary* sex ratio (at fertilization), the *secondary* sex ratio (at birth or hatching) and the *tertiary* sex ratio (among adults), which change through differential mortality, and the **operational sex ratio** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)), the ratio of sexually active males to receptive females at a given time, which determines the intensity of competition for mates. In most species with separate sexes the primary sex ratio is close to 1:1.

Fisher (1930) explained why: if males are rarer than females, each male mates more often and leaves more offspring than each female, so parents that produce sons have more grandchildren, and the proportion of males increases; the reverse holds if males are commoner. The rarer sex always has an advantage, so the sex ratio is held at the point where both sexes are equally valuable. This **frequency-dependent selection** ([7.4.23](/7.4.23)) makes 1:1 an **evolutionarily stable strategy** ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)) (Fisher's principle, [Theorem 13.3.1](/7.10.13#thm-13.3.1) of 7.10.13). In the notation of Shaw and Mohler (1953), a rare mutant mother producing a proportion $s'$ of sons in a population in which mothers produce a proportion $s$ has fitness, counted in grandchildren and relative to a normal mother,

$$
W(s', s) = \frac12\left(\frac{1 - s'}{1 - s} + \frac{s'}{s}\right),
\tag{21.1}
$$
because all grandchildren have one father and one mother, so each son contributes $(1 - s)/s$ times as much as each daughter. $W$ increases with $s'$ if $s < 1/2$ and decreases if $s > 1/2$; only at $s = 1/2$ does no mutant do better (Figure 1A). The argument applies to *investment*, not numbers: if sons cost $c$ times as much as daughters to raise, the ESS is equal investment in the two sexes, a proportion $1/(1 + c)$ of sons (Figure 1B). Fisher's principle has been tested by perturbing sex ratios. Populations of the platyfish *Xiphophorus maculatus*, whose sex is determined by three sex chromosomes (W, X and Y) so that families of different genotypes have different sex ratios, were started with strongly female-biased or male-biased sex ratios; within a few generations the ratios returned toward 1:1 as families producing the rarer sex left more offspring (Basolo 1994).

### 21.2.2. Departures from 1:1

Fisher's argument assumes that sons and daughters cost the same, that mating is random in a large population, and that the parents control the sex ratio. When these assumptions fail, the sex ratio departs from equality in predictable ways; the general theory of **sex allocation** ([7.18.31](/7.18.31)) predicts the direction.

- *Differential mortality and cost.* In mammals more males than females are usually born, and more males die before maturity. In many birds and mammals in which sons are larger and costlier to raise, parents bias investment.
- *Local resource competition.* If offspring of one sex stay near the mother and compete with her and with each other for resources, that sex is less valuable to the mother and should be produced less. In many primates, daughters remain in the natal group and compete for food, while sons disperse; mothers then produce more sons, and the effect is stronger in low-ranking mothers and in poor years (Clark 1978; Silk 1983).
- *Local mate competition.* If brothers compete with each other for mates, sons are less valuable. When $n$ females lay eggs in a patch and their sons mate with the females emerging there before the daughters disperse, the ESS proportion of sons is $(n - 1)/(2n)$ (**local mate competition**, Hamilton 1967; [Definition 13.3.2](/7.10.13#def-13.3.2) of 7.10.13), from 0 for a single foundress to 1/2 for many (Figure 1C). Fig wasps, parasitoid wasps such as *Nasonia* and mites with sib-mating produce strongly female-biased broods, and in fig wasps and *Nasonia* females adjust their sex ratio to the number of other females laying in the same host.
- *Condition-dependent allocation.* The **Trivers–Willard hypothesis** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)) predicts that when a mother's condition affects the reproductive success of sons more than that of daughters (as in polygynous species, where a large son may father many offspring and a small one none), mothers in good condition should produce sons and mothers in poor condition daughters. Dominant red deer hinds on Rum produce more sons (Clutton-Brock et al. 1984). The kakapo (*Strigops habroptila*), a flightless, lekking parrot of New Zealand, provides a conservation example. Supplementary feeding, intended to help the critically endangered population (only 149 birds in 2018), made females produce more sons, the sex of lesser value for recovery; when food was given according to need, the bias disappeared (Clout et al. 2002). Seychelles warblers (*Acrocephalus sechellensis*) on high-quality territories, where daughters stay as helpers, produce up to 87 % daughters; on poor territories, where helpers reduce the parents' success, they produce mostly sons (Komdeur et al. 1997).
- *Endosymbionts and selfish genetic elements.* Maternally inherited bacteria favor the production of daughters, through which they are transmitted. *Wolbachia* and related endosymbionts kill male embryos, feminize genetic males, induce parthenogenesis or cause **cytoplasmic incompatibility** ([7.4.11](/7.4.11)). In the dwarf spider *Oedothorax retusus*, populations infected with *Wolbachia* and with **rickettsia** endosymbionts produce strongly female-biased broods (Vanthournout et al. 2014). Population sex ratios can then be far from 1:1, with consequences for mating competition and effective population size.
- *Environmental sex determination.* Where sex is determined by temperature, as in many reptiles (**temperature-dependent sex determination**, [7.4.6](/7.4.6), [7.19.4](/7.19.4)), the primary sex ratio depends on the environment, and climate warming is producing strongly female-biased hatchling ratios in some sea turtle populations ([7.19.47](/7.19.47)).

### 21.2.3. The Human Birth Sex Ratio

In human populations without strong sex selection the sex ratio at birth is about 105 boys per 100 girls (the normal range is usually given as 103–107); because boys and men have higher mortality at most ages, the sex ratio falls with age and is female-biased among the elderly. Where prenatal sex selection is practiced, the birth sex ratio rises. In China the sex ratio at birth recorded by the population censuses was 116.9 in 2000, 118.1 in 2010 and 111.3 in 2020; in the Republic of Korea it exceeded 115 around 1990 and returned to the normal range by the late 2000s. As in other animals, a skewed sex ratio among adults changes the operational sex ratio and the competition for mates, and the cohorts born with skewed ratios will carry it through their lives, a demographic consequence that can be projected with the age-structured methods of [7.19.9](/7.19.9) and [7.19.14](/7.19.14).

### 21.2.4. Sex Ratio and Effective Population Size

The sex ratio of breeders affects the genetic size of a population. If $N_{\mathrm{m}}$ males and $N_{\mathrm{f}}$ females breed at random, the **effective population size** ([7.4.23](/7.4.23), [7.19.20](/7.19.20)) is

$$
N_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{4N_{\mathrm{m}}N_{\mathrm{f}}}{N_{\mathrm{m}} + N_{\mathrm{f}}},
\tag{21.2}
$$
which equals $N = N_{\mathrm{m}} + N_{\mathrm{f}}$ for an equal sex ratio and is dominated by the rarer sex otherwise: half the genes of each generation come from the males, however few they are. With 5 breeding males and 50 breeding females, $N_{\mathrm{e}} = 18.2$, only 33 % of the 55 breeders (Figure 2A).

## 21.3. Mating Systems and Their Population Consequences

### 21.3.1. Sexual Selection and Mating Systems

Because females usually invest more in each offspring than males do (anisogamy, Bateman's principle; [7.10.13](/7.10.13)), the reproductive success of males is usually limited by access to females, and that of females by resources. This asymmetry drives **sexual selection** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13), [7.18.28](/7.18.28)–[7.18.29](/7.18.29)): competition among members of one sex, usually males, for mates (intrasexual selection: weapons, displays, song, sperm competition) and choice of mates by the other sex (intersexual selection, explained by direct benefits, good genes and the handicap principle, or **Fisherian runaway**). Where males care for the young and can raise fewer offspring than females can produce, the roles reverse, and females compete for males: in jacanas, phalaropes and pipefishes the operational sex ratio is female-biased. In some species both sexes are ornamented and choosy: crested auklets (*Aethia cristatella*) of both sexes respond strongly to models with long crests (Jones and Hunter 1993).

The mating system of a population ([7.18.30](/7.18.30#def-30.1.1)) describes the number of mates, the duration of pair bonds and the pattern of parental care. In birds, Lack (1968) estimated that about 92 % of species are socially monogamous, about 2 % polygynous, less than 1 % polyandrous and about 6 % promiscuous, with no lasting pair bond (including lekking species).

- *Monogamy.* A male and a female form a pair for a breeding season or longer and usually share parental care. Great hornbills (*Buceros bicornis*) seal the female into the nest cavity, where the male feeds her and the chicks for months. Laysan albatrosses (*Phoebastria immutabilis*) form lifelong pairs; the female known as Wisdom, ringed as an adult in 1956, was still breeding in the 2020s, at an age of more than 70 years, with a new partner after her long-term mate disappeared.
- **Polygyny** (one male, several females) is common in mammals, in which females nurse the young and males can contribute little care. Male black grouse (*Lyrurus tetrix*) display on leks and provide no care.
- **Polyandry** (one female, several males) occurs in pheasant-tailed jacanas (*Hydrophasianus chirurgus*), whose larger females defend territories containing several males, each of which incubates a clutch and raises the young.
- **Polygynandry**, in which both sexes have several mates, is shown by the greater rhea (*Rhea americana*): several females lay in the nest of one male, who incubates and guards the clutch, and each female moves on to lay for other males.

Genetic studies have shown that social monogamy rarely means genetic monogamy. **Extra-pair paternity** ([7.18.30](/7.18.30)) was found in about 90 % of socially monogamous bird species studied, and on average about 11 % of offspring were sired by males other than the social mate, with large variation among species (Griffith et al. 2002). Males gain additional offspring; females may gain fertility insurance, better or more compatible genes for their offspring, or resources.

### 21.3.2. Mating Systems and Effective Population Size

Mating systems change the variance in reproductive success, and through it the effective population size. If the number of offspring that individuals leave varies with variance $V_k$ in a stable population of $N$ individuals with an equal sex ratio, then

$$
N_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{4N - 2}{V_k + 2},
\tag{21.3}
$$
which equals $N$ for random (Poisson) variation, $V_k = 2$, exceeds it when reproduction is more even, and is much smaller when a few individuals produce most offspring (Figure 2B). In northern elephant seals (*Mirounga angustirostris*) a small fraction of males sire most of the pups in each season; in red deer, lekking grouse and many polygynous species the variance of male reproductive success is many times that of females. Polygyny and high variance in mating success thus reduce $N_{\mathrm{e}}$ well below the census size, accelerating the loss of genetic diversity in small populations ([7.19.20](/7.19.20)) and requiring larger census sizes in conservation targets ([7.19.15](/7.19.15)).

### 21.3.3. Mate Finding and Allee Effects

In sexual species, individuals at low density may fail to find mates. A simple description is that a female finds a mate with probability $N/(N + \theta)$, where $\theta$ is the density at which half the females are mated. If births are proportional to the number of mated females, the per capita growth rate becomes

$$
g(N) = b\,\frac{N}{N + \theta} - d - cN,
$$
which for $\theta > 0$ is negative at very low densities, rises to a maximum and declines through ordinary density dependence: a mate-finding **Allee effect** ([7.19.10](/7.19.10)) with a threshold below which the population declines (Figure 2C). The threshold increases with $\theta$, that is with how hard mates are to find. Mate-finding Allee effects have been documented in sessile and sparse species, such as wind-pollinated plants and broadcast-spawning sea urchins, whose fertilization rates fall steeply with distance between individuals, and they are used in pest management: releasing sterile males or disrupting mate-finding with sex pheromones reduces the effective density of mates below the threshold.

The mating system shapes these effects. In polygynous species a few males can fertilize many females, so a female-biased adult sex ratio need not reduce reproduction, until males become too rare. Selective hunting of males for their horns reduced adult males of the saiga antelope (*Saiga tatarica*) in Kalmykia to about 1 % of adults by 2000; the proportion of females that conceived then fell sharply, because the remaining males could not hold and mate harems (Milner-Gulland et al. 2003). Harvest strategies that remove one sex, trophy hunting and sex-biased fisheries can therefore turn the demographic advantage of polygyny into an Allee effect ([7.19.53](/7.19.53)).

### 21.3.4. Plant Sexual Systems

Most flowering plants are hermaphroditic, but the arrangement of the sexes varies (Renner 2014):

| System | Arrangement | Frequency (species) | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hermaphroditic (perfect flowers) | male and female organs in the same flower | about 70–75 % | lily, rose |
| **Monoecious** ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)) | separate male and female flowers on the same plant | about 5–10 % | maize, squash, oak |
| **Dioecious** ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)) | male and female flowers on different plants | about 6 % | ginkgo, willow, spinach, holly |
| Gynodioecious, androdioecious and other mixed systems | hermaphrodites with females or with males | a few % | thyme (gynodioecious) |

Hermaphrodites can fertilize themselves (**self-fertilization**, [7.10.14](/7.10.14)), which ensures reproduction at low density or in the absence of pollinators but risks **inbreeding depression** ([7.4.7](/7.4.7)); many species avoid it by **self-incompatibility** ([7.4.3](/7.4.3)), by separating the timing of male and female function, or by separating the sexes. Some species switch according to circumstances: violets (*Viola*) produce open, insect-pollinated flowers in spring and closed, self-fertilizing (cleistogamous) flowers in summer. Dioecy avoids inbreeding and separates the resource demands of the sexes, but halves the fraction of individuals that set seed and makes reproduction dependent on the presence of both sexes, a source of mate-finding Allee effects in sparse populations. Some animals are hermaphrodites too, either simultaneously (earthworms, many snails, which may self-fertilize after long isolation) or sequentially, changing sex with age and size (clownfish change from male to female, many wrasses from female to male).

## 21.4. Territoriality, Habitat Distribution and Dominance

### 21.4.1. Territories

A **territory** ([Definition 26.2.2](/7.18.26#def-26.2.2) of 7.18.26) is an area defended by an individual, a pair or a group against conspecifics; it is distinct from the **home range** ([7.18.26](/7.18.26)), the area used without exclusive defence. Territorial behavior (song, scent marks, displays, threats, fights) has a cost, and territories are expected where resources are economically defensible ([7.18.26](/7.18.26)). Several regularities of territory size are relevant to populations.

- Territory and home-range size increase with body mass, roughly as $M^{1}$ to $M^{1.4}$ across species, more steeply than metabolic requirements ($M^{3/4}$), and are larger for carnivores than for herbivores of the same mass, increasingly so for larger animals (McNab 1963; Schoener 1968), because the food of carnivores is sparser.
- Territories are smaller where food is richer: male lions in Zimbabwe held smaller territories where prey biomass was higher (Loveridge et al. 2015).
- Territorial behavior changes with the life cycle: many songbirds defend territories most strongly during nesting.
- Neighbors often settle into reduced aggression toward each other (the **dear enemy effect**, [7.18.26](/7.18.26)), lowering the cost of territorial defence.

### 21.4.2. Territoriality and Population Regulation

Territoriality turns competition for resources into a contest for space ([7.19.11](/7.19.11)): there are winners, which hold territories and breed, and losers, the *floaters*, which survive with low success or not at all and do not breed. If territories have a minimum size, the number of breeders is limited by the area of suitable habitat, whatever the total number of individuals, and the surplus is excluded from breeding. Territoriality is therefore a powerful mechanism of **population regulation** ([7.19.13](/7.19.13), Definition 13.2.1).

The existence of floaters has been shown by removal experiments. When territorial great tits (*Parus major*) were removed from Marley Wood near Oxford, their territories were reoccupied within days by birds from the surrounding hedgerows, a poorer habitat in which they had bred or not bred (Krebs 1971); removals of red grouse (*Lagopus lagopus scotica*) and of many other birds and mammals have given similar results. In some species floaters are mainly young birds waiting for a vacancy rather than inferior individuals: among black kites (*Milvus migrans*) in Doñana, floaters were not smaller or in poorer condition, but younger, and all territory holders belonged to the older age classes (Sergio et al. 2009). Wynne-Edwards (1962) proposed that territoriality had evolved because it prevented populations from overexploiting their food, a **group selection** ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)) argument now rejected: territoriality evolves because it benefits the individuals that defend territories, and population regulation is a consequence, not its function.

Regulation also arises when habitat varies in quality and individuals occupy the best sites first. Under *site-dependent regulation* (Rodenhouse et al. 1997), each territory has its own expected success, independent of density; as the population grows, additional individuals settle in progressively poorer sites, so that mean reproduction per individual declines with population size even though no individual is affected by crowding on its own territory (Figure 3A).

**Definition 21.4.1 (Buffer Effect)** The **buffer effect** (Brown 1969) is the pattern in which, as a population of a territorial species increases, the number of individuals in the preferred habitat stays nearly constant while the number in poorer habitats, and of non-breeding floaters, changes; poorer habitats thus buffer the preferred habitat against fluctuations in population size.

Kluyver and Tinbergen (1953) found that the numbers of great tits breeding in preferred mixed woodland in the Netherlands varied little from year to year, whereas numbers in poorer pine woods fluctuated widely. Black-tailed godwits (*Limosa limosa islandica*) wintering in Britain provide a large-scale example: as the Icelandic population grew over several decades, the numbers on the best estuaries, where food intake and survival were highest, remained nearly constant, while new birds spread to poorer sites with lower survival (Gill et al. 2001). Because the per capita success of the population declines as it spreads, the buffer effect is a form of density dependence acting through habitat quality, and it means that counts in the best habitat are poor indicators of population trends.

### 21.4.3. Ideal Free and Despotic Distributions

Fretwell and Lucas (1970) formalized the distribution of individuals among habitats. Let the *basic suitability* of habitat $i$ be the expected success of an individual settling there when it is empty, and let realized success decline with density. Under the **ideal free distribution** ([Definition 25.3.1](/7.18.25#def-25.3.1) of 7.18.25), individuals are free to settle anywhere and choose the habitat that gives them the highest success; the best habitat fills until its realized success falls to the basic suitability of the next, after which both are occupied with equal realized success. Under the **despotic distribution** ([Definition 25.3.2](/7.18.25#def-25.3.2) of 7.18.25), dominant or territorial individuals prevent others from settling in the best habitat once its territories are taken; later arrivals are forced into poorer habitat, where they do worse than the residents of the good habitat (Figure 4).

The two distributions have different population consequences.

- Under both, the mean success of the population declines as it grows, because more individuals live in poorer habitat: habitat selection produces density dependence at the population level.
- Under the ideal free distribution, densities track habitat quality, and the success of individuals is equal across habitats. Under the despotic distribution, densities in the best habitat are capped by territoriality and do not reflect its quality, while success differs between habitats.
- Under the despotic distribution, a large part of the population may live in habitats where it cannot replace itself, sustained by immigrants from the good habitat: a source–sink system (**source–sink dynamics**, [7.19.16](/7.19.16), Definition 16.5.1). Density is then a misleading indicator of habitat quality (Van Horne 1983), which matters for conservation: a habitat crowded with subordinate individuals may be a sink, or even an **ecological trap** ([7.18.26](/7.18.26)).

### 21.4.4. Dominance Hierarchies

**Definition 21.4.2 (Dominance Hierarchy)** A **dominance hierarchy** is the ordering of the members of a social group by their status, established through contests and thereafter maintained mostly by threats and submission, in which higher-ranking individuals have priority of access to food, mates or shelter.

Schjelderup-Ebbe (1922) described the pecking order of domestic hens, in which each hen pecks those below her and is pecked by those above. Hierarchies are classified by their structure: *despotic*, with one individual dominating all others, which are not ranked among themselves; *linear*, in which A dominates B, B dominates C, and so on; and with *cyclic* (intransitive) triads, in which A dominates B, B dominates C and C dominates A. Wolves live in family packs in which the breeding pair is dominant; savanna baboons (*Papio*) live in large multimale groups in which high-ranking males have priority of mating and feeding but males also form coalitions. The social organization of primates correlates with their ecology: species in poor, predator-scarce environments often live in small one-male groups, while species in rich, predator-rich environments form large multimale groups (Crook and Gartlan 1966), a generalization later refined by phylogenetic comparisons.

Hierarchies reduce the costs of repeated fighting and channel limited resources to dominant individuals. When resources are scarce, subordinates bear most of the shortfall: they eat less, breed less or not at all, and may emigrate or die. Like territoriality, this contest competition stabilizes the number of breeders and regulates the population. Social stress may add to the effect: Christian (1950) proposed that crowding raises adrenal activity and suppresses reproduction in rodents, a hypothesis of self-regulation that is supported in some laboratory and enclosed populations but has rarely been shown to regulate natural ones ([7.19.13](/7.19.13)). The most extreme reproductive division of labor is found in **eusociality** ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)), in which one or a few individuals reproduce and the others, usually sterile, work; in ants, bees and wasps all workers are females (in termites both sexes), and the colony, rather than the individual, behaves demographically as the unit of reproduction.

## 21.5. Allelopathy

**Allelopathy** ([7.13.26](/7.13.26)) is the inhibition of one plant by chemicals released by another, through volatilization from leaves, leaching by rain, decomposition of litter or exudation from roots. The compounds (allelochemicals) are mostly secondary metabolites: phenolic acids, quinones, terpenoids, alkaloids. Allelopathy is a form of interference competition; it acts both within species (autotoxicity) and between species ([7.19.22](/7.19.22)).

- *Soil sickness.* Many crops cannot be grown repeatedly on the same land without yield loss (the replant problem of continuous cropping), and autotoxic root exudates and residues are one cause, together with the build-up of specific soil pathogens. Rice roots exude phenolic acids such as *p*-coumaric (*p*-hydroxycinnamic) acid that inhibit rice seedlings; residues of red clover release isoflavonoids and their microbial breakdown products that inhibit germination.
- *Community composition.* The black walnut (*Juglans nigra*) stores the non-toxic hydrojuglone (1,4,5-trihydroxynaphthalene) in leaves, bark, husks and roots; when it is washed into the soil it is oxidized to juglone, which inhibits the growth of many plants, so that few herbs grow under walnuts.
- *Succession and patterns of vegetation.* Shrubs of *Salvia leucophylla* and *Artemisia californica* in Californian coastal sage scrub are surrounded by bare zones 1–2 m wide in the adjacent annual grassland. Muller (1964, 1966) attributed them to volatile terpenes (camphor, 1,8-cineole) released by the shrubs, which inhibited the germination and growth of the grassland annuals, and proposed that the shrubs expand into grassland in this way. Bartholomew (1970) showed that rodents, rabbits and birds sheltering in the shrubs also feed in the bare zones, and that annuals grew there when animals were excluded by cages: the pattern has at least two causes.

The difficulty of the *Salvia* case is typical. Chemicals with allelopathic effects can be extracted from many plants, but showing that they reach effective concentrations in the field, that they cause the observed pattern, and that they evolved for this function is difficult; many such compounds also defend against herbivores and pathogens or influence nutrient cycling (Inderjit et al. 2011). Allelopathy may matter most for invasive species whose chemicals are novel to the plants of the invaded community (the *novel weapons hypothesis*; Callaway and Ridenour 2004): spotted knapweed (*Centaurea stoebe*) from Eurasia releases the phenolic catechin, which inhibits North American grasses more than Eurasian ones, although the concentrations of catechin in field soils have been disputed.

## 21.6. Aggregation, Group Living and Altruism

### 21.6.1. Aggregation

Most organisms are aggregated in space ([7.19.8](/7.19.8), dispersion patterns), and many live in groups for part or all of their lives. Groups may be temporary (wintering flocks, migratory swarms, hibernation clusters) or permanent (colonies, family groups). Their causes are of two kinds: shared responses to the environment (individuals gather where food, shelter or suitable conditions are found, or are carried together by currents and wind; offspring remain near their parents), and social attraction, in which individuals are attracted to one another. Only the second produces groups in the behavioral sense.

### 21.6.2. Benefits and Costs of Groups

The benefits and costs of group living are analyzed in [7.18.27](/7.18.27). Benefits include protection from predators through the **dilution effect**, the **selfish herd**, the **many-eyes effect** and active group defence; better location and capture of food (cooperative hunting by wolves and lions, information sharing at colonies); modification of the microclimate (huddling of emperor penguins); and easier finding of mates. Goshawks attacking woodpigeons succeeded in about 80 % of attacks on single pigeons but in less than 10 % of attacks on flocks of more than 50, mainly because large flocks detected the hawk sooner (Kenward 1978). Costs include competition for food within the group, greater conspicuousness to predators, and higher transmission of parasites and disease ([7.19.28](/7.19.28)); the colonies of cliff swallows (*Petrochelidon pyrrhonota*) carry more blood-sucking swallow bugs, the larger they are (Brown and Brown 1986).

The balance of benefits and costs generally gives an intermediate group size that maximizes the success of each member; because individuals join a group as long as they do better than alone, observed groups tend to be larger than this optimum ([Proposition 27.5.1](/7.18.27#prop-27.5.1) of 7.18.27). For the population, these effects mean that per capita performance can rise with density at low density and fall at high density. Allee (1931), studying aggregations of animals from flatworms to fish, emphasized both "undercrowding" and "overcrowding": the principle that each species has an optimal density, below which its members suffer from too few companions. This is the origin of the Allee effect ([7.19.10](/7.19.10)), and it explains why species that depend on groups or colonies, such as colonial seabirds, social carnivores and schooling fish, can collapse when their numbers fall below the size needed for cooperative defence, hunting or breeding (the passenger pigeon is a much discussed example).

### 21.6.3. Altruism and Cooperation

**Altruism** ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)) is behavior that increases the fitness of others at a cost to the actor's own direct fitness: alarm calls that warn others but attract the predator; soldier termites blocking a breach in the nest; honeybee workers whose barbed sting kills them when they sting a vertebrate; helpers that raise the offspring of others. Its evolution is explained by **kin selection** ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)), when the beneficiaries are relatives (**Hamilton's rule**, $rb > c$), and by reciprocity and mutual benefit among non-relatives ([7.18.33](/7.18.33)). The demographic consequences are significant. **Cooperative breeding** ([7.18.35](/7.18.35)), in which nonbreeding helpers assist in raising young, occurs in up to 9 % of bird species and in many mammals; it often arises from **philopatry** ([7.18.26](/7.18.26)) when suitable breeding territories are saturated, so that young individuals do better by staying home and helping than by floating. Helpers increase the survival and reproduction of the breeders, which can raise the rate of increase and reduce the extinction risk of the group, but saturation of territories also caps the number of breeding units, linking cooperative breeding to the territorial regulation of Section 21.4.

## Summary

- Reproduction is sexual or asexual (Definition 21.1.1): vegetative propagation, spores, budding, fragmentation and parthenogenesis (occasional, arrhenotokous, obligate, cyclical). Cyclical parthenogens (aphids, *Daphnia*, *Chlamydomonas*) reproduce asexually to exploit abundant resources and sexually before dispersal or dormancy. Sex has a twofold cost; its maintenance is linked to parasites (Red Queen) and variable environments; asexual forms prevail in marginal and disturbed habitats (geographic parthenogenesis).
- Fisher's principle makes equal investment in the sexes an ESS (Eq. (21.1), Figure 1). Unequal costs, local resource competition, local mate competition ($(n - 1)/(2n)$), condition dependence (Trivers–Willard; kakapo, red deer, Seychelles warblers), endosymbionts and environmental sex determination shift the sex ratio. The normal human birth sex ratio is 103–107 males per 100 females.
- The **sex ratio** of breeders and the variance of reproductive success set the effective population size (Eqs. (21.2), (21.3)). Mating systems (monogamy, **polygyny**, **polyandry**, **polygynandry**; extra-pair paternity in most socially monogamous birds) therefore affect genetic diversity, and mate finding produces Allee effects that sex-selective harvest can trigger (saiga) (Figure 2). Plants are mostly hermaphroditic; dioecy (about 6 % of species) and self-incompatibility prevent selfing.
- **Territory** holding is a contest that excludes floaters from breeding and regulates populations; site-dependent regulation and the buffer effect (Definition 21.4.1) make per capita success decline as populations spread into poorer habitat (Figure 3). Ideal free and despotic distributions (Figure 4) differ in whether density reflects habitat quality; despotic settlement creates sources and sinks. Dominance hierarchies (Definition 21.4.2) channel scarce resources to dominants and stabilize the number of breeders.
- **Allelopathy** is chemical interference among plants (soil sickness, juglone, *Salvia* bare zones, novel weapons of invaders); its role in nature is often confounded with herbivory and other causes.
- Group living brings dilution, selfish-herd and many-eyes benefits and costs of competition and disease; it underlies Allee's principle of an optimal density. **Altruism**, explained by kin selection and reciprocity, and cooperative breeding affect the growth and regulation of populations.

## Questions

1. In a population with a primary sex ratio of 0.4 (40 % males), what is the relative fitness of a mutant mother who produces only sons, and of one who produces only daughters? Which way will the sex ratio evolve?

   **Answer** By Eq. (21.1) with $s = 0.4$: for $s' = 1$, $W = \frac12(0 + 1/0.4) = 1.25$; for $s' = 0$, $W = \frac12(1/0.6) = 0.83$. Mothers producing more sons than the population average leave more grandchildren, so the proportion of sons will increase toward 1/2. The reason is that each son, being rarer, fathers on average $0.6/0.4 = 1.5$ times as many offspring as each daughter produces.

2. A parasitoid wasp lays eggs in hosts; on average three females lay in each host, and their offspring mate inside the host before the females disperse. What sex ratio does local mate competition predict, and how should a female adjust her brood if she is the only female to lay in a host?

   **Answer** With $n = 3$, the ESS proportion of sons is $(n - 1)/(2n) = 2/6 = 1/3$. A female laying alone should produce only as many sons as are needed to mate with all her daughters (theoretically a proportion near 0, in practice one or a few sons), because her sons compete only with each other for her own daughters. Females of *Nasonia* and fig wasps do adjust their sex ratios to the number of other females using the same host in roughly this way. (For haplodiploid species, in which the relatedness of mothers to sons and daughters differs under inbreeding, the predicted proportion is slightly lower.)

3. A population of 200 deer has 20 breeding males and 100 breeding females; the rest do not breed. Compute the effective population size from the breeding sex ratio. How would $N_{\mathrm{e}}$ change if two of the 20 males sired 80 % of the offspring?

   **Answer** $N_{\mathrm{e}} = 4 \cdot 20 \cdot 100/(20 + 100) = 66.7$, a third of the census size. If two males sire most offspring, the variance in male reproductive success increases sharply and the number of males that contribute effectively is close to two to four; using an effective number of males of, say, 3, $N_{\mathrm{e}} \approx 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 100/103 = 11.7$. Polygyny with skewed mating success can thus reduce $N_{\mathrm{e}}$ to a few percent of the census number, with corresponding loss of genetic variation each generation.

4. Explain how the buffer effect can create density-dependent regulation in a population in which no individual's reproduction is affected by the density of its neighbors.

   **Answer** If territories differ in quality and individuals occupy the best available first, each new individual settles in a poorer site than the previous ones. Each individual's success depends only on the quality of its own site, but the mean success of the population declines as the population grows, because the added individuals occupy progressively poorer sites or become floaters. Mean per capita recruitment is therefore a decreasing function of population size, which is density dependence at the population level and yields a stable equilibrium where recruitment balances mortality (Figure 3A). The numbers in the best habitat stay constant; fluctuations are absorbed by the poorer habitats and the floaters.

5. Why is the density of a species in a habitat a misleading measure of habitat quality under a despotic distribution? Give an example of how this can mislead conservation.

   **Answer** Under a despotic distribution, the number of individuals in the best habitat is limited by territoriality, while subordinates are forced into poorer habitats in numbers that depend on the total population, not on the quality of those habitats. Poor habitat can therefore hold high densities of subordinate or young individuals that reproduce poorly or not at all, sustained by immigration from the good habitat (a sink). A survey that ranks habitats by density would rank such sinks as important; protecting them instead of the sources, or allowing the sources to be destroyed while sinks are preserved, would lead to decline (Van Horne 1983). Measures of reproduction and survival per individual, not density alone, are needed to identify sources.

6. (Open question) The kakapo program found that well-fed females produced more sons. Using the Trivers–Willard hypothesis, explain why, and propose how supplementary feeding should be managed in a recovery program that needs females.

   **Answer** Kakapo are lekking, polygynous birds in which a male in good condition can father many chicks while a female's reproductive output is limited; females in good condition gain more grandchildren from sons, and the hypothesis predicts that they bias their broods toward sons. Supplementary food improved female condition and thus shifted the sex ratio toward males, the sex of less value to a recovery program limited by the number of breeding females. Food should therefore be given to raise breeding frequency and chick survival without raising female condition above the level at which the bias appears: for example, rationing food according to body mass so that females stay at moderate condition, timing it to the chick-rearing period rather than before egg-laying, and monitoring the sex of chicks to adjust the program. The kakapo team did adjust feeding this way, and the bias disappeared.
