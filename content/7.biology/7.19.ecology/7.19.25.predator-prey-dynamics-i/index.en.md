Predators kill prey, and prey feed predators. The interaction is a feedback loop with a delay built into it: an increase of prey is followed by an increase of predators, which reduces the prey, which then starves the predators. Feedback with delay is the classic recipe for oscillation ([7.19.13](/7.19.13)), and the central question of the next three chapters is what the oscillations of consumer–resource systems look like, when they are damped, when they persist, and when they destroy the interaction.

This chapter builds the theory from its simplest model. Section 25.1 treats the **Lotka–Volterra model** ([Definition 23.4.1](/7.5.23#def-23.4.1) of 7.5.23, where it appears as a biochemical oscillator) as an ecological model, with a complete analysis of its closed orbits; Section 25.2 draws from it the one prediction that survives every refinement, the Volterra principle; Section 25.3 adds self-limitation of the prey. Sections 25.4 and 25.5 then replace the model's crudest assumption, that each predator eats prey at a rate proportional to prey density, by the functional responses that behavioral studies actually measure, and by consumption rates that depend on the density of predators too. The consequences of these refinements for stability, the paradox of enrichment and the cycles of natural populations follow in [7.19.26](/7.19.26); discrete-time host–parasitoid models are in [7.19.27](/7.19.27), and parasites and pathogens in [7.19.28](/7.19.28). The kinds of predators and the effects of predation on individual prey are described in [7.19.24](/7.19.24), and the foraging decisions behind consumption rates in [7.18.22](/7.18.22). The mathematics of planar systems (phase plane, linearization, limit cycles) is in [2.4.11](/2.4.11).

## 25.1. The Lotka–Volterra Predator–Prey Model

### 25.1.1. Assumptions and Equations

Lotka (1925) and Volterra (1926) independently wrote down the same pair of equations, Lotka by analogy with chemical kinetics and Volterra to explain the fish catches of the Adriatic (Section 25.2). Let $N(t)$ be the density of prey and $P(t)$ that of predators. The model rests on five assumptions:

1. In the absence of predators the prey grow exponentially at the per capita rate $r$ (food is unlimited).
2. In the absence of prey the predators die out exponentially at the per capita rate $m$ (the prey is their only food).
3. Each predator searches a fixed area per unit time and kills every prey it finds, so it kills $aN$ prey per unit time: consumption is linear in prey density, a type I **functional response** (Section 25.4), and the total kill rate $aNP$ follows the law of mass action.
4. Predators convert the prey they eat into new predators with a constant efficiency $c$, so the predator birth rate is $caNP$.
5. Both populations are homogeneous and well mixed: no age structure, no time delays other than those generated by the interaction itself, no spatial structure.

**Formula 25.1.1 (Lotka–Volterra Predator–Prey Equations)** Under these assumptions,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = rN - aNP, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = caNP - mP, \tag{25.1}
$$
where $r$ is the intrinsic rate of increase of the prey (per unit time), $a$ the attack rate (per predator per unit time per unit prey density, the area effectively searched), $c$ the number of predators produced per prey eaten, and $m$ the per capita death rate of predators.

Chinese textbooks write the same equations as $\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}t = r_1N - \varepsilon PN$ and $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}t = -r_2P + \theta PN$, so that $\varepsilon = a$, $\theta = ca$ and $r_2 = m$; the separation of $\theta$ into a capture rate and a conversion efficiency makes clear that the numerical response of the predator is driven by what it eats.

The four parameters reduce to one. With

$$
x = \frac{ca}{m}N, \qquad y = \frac{a}{r}P, \qquad \tau = rt, \qquad \alpha = \frac{m}{r},
$$
Eq. (25.1) becomes

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\tau} = x(1 - y), \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}\tau} = \alpha y(x - 1). \tag{25.2}
$$
Densities are now measured relative to their equilibrium values and time in units of the prey's doubling scale $1/r$; the single parameter $\alpha$ compares the time scales of predator decline and prey increase. Everything said below about Eq. (25.1) can be read off Eq. (25.2).

### 25.1.2. Zero Isoclines and Equilibria

The prey **zero net growth isocline** ([7.19.22](/7.19.22)) is the set where $\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}t = 0$ with $N > 0$, the horizontal line $P = r/a$: prey increase below it, where predators are scarce, and decrease above it. The predator isocline is the vertical line $N = m/(ca)$: predators increase to its right, where prey are plentiful, and decline to its left. The two lines divide the positive quadrant into four regions in which the flow points, in turn, to the upper right, upper left, lower left and lower right, so trajectories circulate anticlockwise around the intersection (Figure 1A). Along any such circuit the predator maximum comes a quarter turn after the prey maximum.

The equilibria are the origin and the coexistence point

$$
N^* = \frac{m}{ca}, \qquad P^* = \frac{r}{a}. \tag{25.3}
$$
Both values have a direct meaning: the equilibrium prey density is the density at which a predator just replaces itself ($caN^* = m$), and the equilibrium predator density is the density at which predation just balances prey reproduction. Neither depends on the parameters of its own species alone: $N^*$ is set by the predator's parameters and $P^*$ by the prey's growth rate, a first hint of the counterintuitive results of Section 25.2.

The Jacobian matrix of Eq. (25.1) is

$$
J(N, P) = \begin{bmatrix} r - aP & -aN \\ caP & caN - m \end{bmatrix}.
$$
At the origin $J = \operatorname{diag}(r, -m)$: a saddle, whose stable manifold is the $P$-axis (predators alone decline) and unstable manifold the $N$-axis (prey alone increase). At the coexistence point

$$
J(N^*, P^*) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -m/c \\ cr & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}J = 0, \qquad \det J = rm > 0,
$$
with eigenvalues $\pm\mathrm{i}\sqrt{rm}$. The linearization is a center: small deviations oscillate with angular frequency $\sqrt{rm}$, period $2\pi/\sqrt{rm}$, and an amplitude fixed by the initial condition. Solving the linear system, $N - N^* \propto \cos(\sqrt{rm}\,t)$ and $P - P^* \propto \sin(\sqrt{rm}\,t)$, so the predator lags the prey by a quarter period.

Linearization does not settle the nonlinear behavior: purely imaginary eigenvalues are exactly the case that Lyapunov's first method leaves open ([Theorem 11.3.1](/2.4.11#thm-11.3.1) of 2.4.11), and a nonlinear perturbation could turn the center into a stable or an unstable focus. For Eq. (25.1) the answer comes from a conserved quantity.

### 25.1.3. The Conserved Quantity and Closed Orbits

**Proposition 25.1.1 (Conserved Quantity)** The function

$$
V(N, P) = caN - m\ln N + aP - r\ln P \tag{25.4}
$$
is a **first integral** of Eq. (25.1) in the open positive quadrant $Q = \{N > 0, P > 0\}$: it is constant along every solution. $V$ is strictly convex on $Q$, has its unique minimum at $(N^*, P^*)$, and tends to $+\infty$ at the boundary of $Q$ and at infinity. Consequently every solution in $Q$ other than the equilibrium is periodic, and its orbit is the closed level curve of $V$ through its initial point.

**Proof** Along a solution,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = \Bigl(ca - \frac{m}{N}\Bigr)\dot N + \Bigl(a - \frac{r}{P}\Bigr)\dot P = (caN - m)(r - aP) + (aP - r)(caN - m) = 0.
$$
The Hessian of $V$ is $\operatorname{diag}(m/N^2,\ r/P^2)$, positive definite, so $V$ is strictly convex; its gradient vanishes only where $caN = m$ and $aP = r$. Each one-variable part, such as $caN - m\ln N$, tends to $+\infty$ as $N \to 0^+$ and as $N \to \infty$, so the sublevel sets $\{V \le v\}$ are compact convex subsets of $Q$, and for $v > V(N^*, P^*)$ the level set $\{V = v\}$ is a simple closed convex curve around the equilibrium. A solution starting on it stays on it, never leaves $Q$ (it stays in a compact subset of $Q$, so it exists for all time), and contains no equilibrium. Its speed $\lvert(\dot N, \dot P)\rvert$ is continuous and positive on the compact curve, hence bounded below by some $s > 0$, so the solution goes once around the curve, of finite length $L$, in time at most $L/s$ and then repeats: it is periodic. $\square$

The first integral was found by separating variables in $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}N$ ([Example 14.1.2](/2.4.14#ex-14.1.2) of 2.4.14); in the scaled variables of Eq. (25.2) it is $\alpha(x - \ln x) + (y - \ln y)$. $V$ is also a Lyapunov function in the sense of [Theorem 11.4.1](/2.4.11#thm-11.4.1) of 2.4.11 ($V - V^*$ is positive definite and $\dot V = 0$), which shows directly that the equilibrium is stable but not asymptotically stable: a perturbed population neither returns nor drifts away, it simply moves to another orbit. Such a family of closed orbits is a center in the terminology of planar systems, and the closed orbits are not **limit cycles**: none of them is isolated, and none attracts neighboring orbits. The distinction matters, because the limit cycles of [7.19.26](/7.19.26) are robust where these orbits are not.

Far from equilibrium the orbits are no longer ellipses (Figure 1). Large cycles have sharp prey peaks, long troughs in which both species are rare, and a longer period. Two properties hold for every orbit, however large.

**Theorem 25.1.1 (Averages over a Cycle)** For every periodic solution of Eq. (25.1), with period $T$,

$$
\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T N(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac{m}{ca} = N^*, \qquad \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T P(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac{r}{a} = P^*.
$$

**Proof** Divide the prey equation by $N > 0$: $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\ln N = r - aP$. Integrate over one period; since $N(T) = N(0)$ the left side integrates to $0$, so $0 = rT - a\int_0^TP\,\mathrm{d}t$, which gives the mean of $P$. The predator equation gives $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\ln P = caN - m$ and, in the same way, the mean of $N$. $\square$

The time averages are therefore independent of the amplitude: an observer who records the mean densities over many cycles sees the equilibrium, whatever the oscillation. This is what makes the model testable through means even though its amplitudes are arbitrary.

### 25.1.4. Structural Instability

The closed orbits are a knife-edge. An arbitrarily small change in the equations destroys them: they are a degenerate boundary between damped and growing oscillations, which is what structural instability means. Two examples:

- With a little self-limitation of the prey (Section 25.3) the divergence of the vector field multiplied by the Dulac function $B = 1/(NP)$ becomes $-r/(KP) < 0$ everywhere in $Q$, so by Dulac's criterion ([Theorem 11.6.2](/2.4.11#thm-11.6.2) and the remark after it) there is no closed orbit at all; trajectories spiral into the equilibrium.
- With a little saturation of the predator's feeding rate (Section 25.4) the equilibrium becomes an unstable focus and trajectories spiral outward ([7.19.26](/7.19.26)).

Stochastic disturbances have a similar effect even when the equations are exact. Each random perturbation moves the system to another orbit; because nothing pulls it back, $V$ performs a random walk, and sooner or later the system reaches an orbit whose troughs are so low that one population is lost. A model in which the amplitude is set by the history of disturbances cannot, by itself, explain the regular amplitude of observed cycles.

The lesson is not that the Lotka–Volterra model is useless, but which of its predictions to trust. The tendency of predator–prey systems to oscillate, the quarter-period lag and the values $N^* = m/(ca)$ and $P^* = r/a$ survive the refinements of the following sections; the neutral closed orbits do not.

## 25.2. The Volterra Principle

### 25.2.1. Fishing in the Adriatic

Volterra's interest in the model came from his son-in-law, the marine biologist Umberto D'Ancona, who had compiled the composition of fish landings in the ports of the northern Adriatic. During the First World War fishing almost stopped, and the proportion of predatory fish (selachians: sharks, skates and rays) in the catch rose markedly, falling again when fishing resumed (Figure 2B). Why should a reduction in fishing, which removes predators and prey alike, favor the predators?

**Proposition 25.2.1 (Volterra Principle)** Suppose that both species of Eq. (25.1) are removed at the same per capita rate $h$, with $0 < h < r$ (fishing or a broad-spectrum pesticide), so that

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = (r - h)N - aNP, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = caNP - (m + h)P.
$$
Then the cycle averages are

$$
\bar N = \frac{m + h}{ca}, \qquad \bar P = \frac{r - h}{a}:
$$
increasing the harvesting rate increases the average prey density and decreases the average predator density. Equivalently, reducing harvesting (as in wartime) favors the predator.

**Proof** The harvested system is again of the form (25.1), with $r$ replaced by $r - h > 0$ and $m$ by $m + h$. Theorem 25.1.1 applies and gives the averages. $\square$

The prey gain because harvesting acts on them twice: directly, which they can make up, and through the predators, whose loss reduces predation by more than the direct removal. The result is about averages, not about the shape of the cycles, and it survives prey self-limitation: with logistic prey (Section 25.3) the equilibrium prey density $(m + h)/(ca)$ still increases with $h$, and the equilibrium predator density $(r - h)(1 - N^*/K)/a$ decreases.

### 25.2.2. Pesticides and Natural Enemies

The same arithmetic applies to insecticides that kill a pest and its natural enemies. Spraying a broad-spectrum insecticide can raise the average density of the pest, the phenomenon of *pest resurgence*. The classic case is the cottony cushion scale *Icerya purchasi* in Californian citrus. It had been brought under control in 1889 by the vedalia beetle *Rodolia cardinalis* imported from Australia (the history of biological control is in [7.19.27](/7.19.27#sec-27.5)); when DDT was applied to citrus groves in the late 1940s, it killed the beetles more effectively than the scales, and the scale became a pest again until spraying was reduced. Pest resurgence and its cousin, secondary pest outbreaks (a species previously held down by enemies becomes a pest once the enemies are killed), are central arguments for integrated pest management ([7.19.53](/7.19.53), [7.17.40](/7.17.40)).

## 25.3. Prey Density Dependence

Real prey populations are limited by their own resources. Replacing exponential prey growth by logistic growth ([7.19.10](/7.19.10)) gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = rN\Bigl(1 - \frac{N}{K}\Bigr) - aNP, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = caNP - mP, \tag{25.5}
$$
where $K$ is the **carrying capacity** of the prey. No carrying capacity is written for the predator: in this model its only limiting resource is the prey, so it is limited through them. With $x = N/K$, $y = aP/r$, $\tau = rt$ the system becomes

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\tau} = x(1 - x - y), \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}\tau} = \alpha y(x - \beta), \qquad \alpha = \frac{caK}{r}, \quad \beta = \frac{m}{caK} = \frac{N^*}{K}. \tag{25.6}
$$
The parameter $\beta$ is the predator's break-even prey density as a fraction of the prey carrying capacity: a small $\beta$ means an efficient predator, or a productive prey.

The prey isocline is now the falling line $y = 1 - x$ and the predator isocline the vertical line $x = \beta$.

**Proposition 25.3.1 (Logistic Prey)** For Eq. (25.6):

1. If $\beta \ge 1$ the predator cannot persist: every solution with $x(0) > 0$ tends to $(1, 0)$.
2. If $\beta < 1$ the coexistence equilibrium $(x^*, y^*) = (\beta, 1 - \beta)$ is globally asymptotically stable in the open positive quadrant. It is a stable focus (damped oscillations) if $\beta < 4\alpha(1 - \beta)$ and a stable node otherwise.

**Proof** At $(1, 0)$ the Jacobian is $\begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 0 & \alpha(1 - \beta)\end{bmatrix}$, so rare predators increase if and only if $\beta < 1$. For $\beta \ge 1$ take $U(x, y) = \alpha(x - 1 - \ln x) + y$, which is positive except at $(1, 0)$ on $\{x > 0, y \ge 0\}$ and tends to infinity at its boundary and at infinity. Using $1 - x - y = -(x - 1) - y$,

$$
\dot U = \alpha(x - 1)(1 - x - y) + \alpha y(x - \beta) = -\alpha(x - 1)^2 - \alpha(\beta - 1)y \le 0.
$$
Where $\dot U = 0$ we have $x = 1$ and, if $\beta > 1$, $y = 0$; if $\beta = 1$, the line $x = 1$ is crossed by every trajectory with $y > 0$ (there $\dot x = -y$). In both cases the only invariant set in $\{\dot U = 0\}$ is $(1, 0)$, and LaSalle's principle gives convergence to it.

For $\beta < 1$ take the Volterra-type function

$$
V(x, y) = \alpha\bigl(x - x^* - x^*\ln(x/x^*)\bigr) + \bigl(y - y^* - y^*\ln(y/y^*)\bigr),
$$
which is positive definite about $(x^*, y^*)$ and tends to infinity at the boundary of the quadrant. Using $1 - x^* - y^* = 0$, so that $1 - x - y = -(x - x^*) - (y - y^*)$,

$$
\dot V = \alpha(x - x^*)(1 - x - y) + \alpha(y - y^*)(x - x^*) = -\alpha(x - x^*)^2 \le 0.
$$
The set where $\dot V = 0$ is the line $x = x^*$, on which $\dot x = x^*(y^* - y) \ne 0$ unless $y = y^*$, so it contains no whole trajectory other than the equilibrium; by LaSalle's invariance principle ([Theorem 11.4.3](/2.4.11#thm-11.4.3)) every solution in the quadrant converges to $(x^*, y^*)$. At the equilibrium

$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} -\beta & -\beta \\ \alpha(1 - \beta) & 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}J = -\beta < 0, \qquad \det J = \alpha\beta(1 - \beta) > 0,
$$
and the eigenvalues are complex, a focus, exactly when $(\operatorname{tr}J)^2 < 4\det J$, that is $\beta < 4\alpha(1 - \beta)$ ([Theorem 11.5.2](/2.4.11#thm-11.5.2) of 2.4.11). $\square$

Self-limitation of the prey thus converts the neutral cycles into damped oscillations. The rate of damping is $-\frac12\operatorname{tr}J = \beta/2$ in units of $r$: the more efficient the predator (the smaller $\beta = N^*/K$), the closer the trace is to zero and the more slowly the oscillations die out, because the prey at equilibrium are held so far below $K$ that their self-limitation is weak. In the limit $K \to \infty$ the model returns to the neutral Lotka–Volterra cycles. This observation is the germ of the paradox of enrichment ([7.19.26](/7.19.26)): once predators also saturate, enriching the prey's environment pushes the system past neutrality into growing oscillations.

## 25.4. Functional Responses

### 25.4.1. Functional and Numerical Responses

Solomon (1949) divided the response of predators to prey density into two parts, and Holling (1959) gave the first part its modern forms.

**Definition 25.4.1 (Functional and Numerical Response)** The **functional response** of a predator is its consumption rate, the number of prey eaten per predator per unit time, as a function $f(N)$ of prey density. The **numerical response** is the change in predator density with prey density, through reproduction, survival and immigration.

The total predation rate is $f(N)P$, and the per capita risk of a prey individual is $f(N)P/N$. In Eq. (25.1), $f(N) = aN$ and the numerical response is the predator birth rate $c f(N)$. A general consumer–resource model therefore has the form

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = g(N)N - f(N)P, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = \bigl(c f(N) - m\bigr)P, \tag{25.7}
$$
with prey growth rate $g(N)$, and most of the theory of [7.19.26](/7.19.26) is about how the shapes of $g$ and $f$ determine stability.

### 25.4.2. The Disc Equation

A predator that finds a prey must catch, subdue, eat and digest it before it can search again. Holling (1959) measured this with a blindfolded assistant who searched a table by touch for sandpaper discs, removing each disc found (the handling time) before tapping on; the number of discs found per minute rose with disc density and leveled off.

**Proposition 25.4.1 (Holling's Disc Equation)** Suppose a predator searches at rate $a$ (area per unit time) and spends a handling time $h$ on each prey caught, and that prey density $N$ stays constant (prey are replaced or few are eaten). Then the long-run consumption rate is

$$
f(N) = \frac{aN}{1 + ahN}. \tag{25.8}
$$

**Proof** In a period of length $T$ the predator catches $N_e$ prey and spends $hN_e$ handling them, so it searches for $T_s = T - hN_e$. While searching it finds prey at rate $aN$, so $N_e = aNT_s = aN(T - hN_e)$. Solving, $N_e/T = aN/(1 + ahN)$.

The same result holds exactly for a stochastic predator. Let the search intervals be independent and exponentially distributed with mean $1/(aN)$, and let each be followed by a handling bout of length $h$. The intervals between successive captures are then independent with mean $1/(aN) + h$, and by the renewal theorem the long-run number of captures per unit time is $1/\bigl(1/(aN) + h\bigr) = aN/(1 + ahN)$. Equivalently, in the two-state Markov chain "searching" $\to$ "handling" (rate $aN$) $\to$ "searching" (rate $1/h$), the stationary probability of searching is $1/(1 + ahN)$, and captures occur at rate $aN$ times that probability. $\square$

The disc equation has the form of the **Michaelis–Menten equation** of enzyme kinetics, and for the same reason: an enzyme, too, is "busy" with a substrate molecule for a while after binding it. Its three features are its initial slope $a$ (at low density consumption is limited by search), its asymptote $1/h$ (at high density, by handling), and its half-saturation density $1/(ah)$. A predator with a long handling time relative to its search efficiency saturates at low prey density.

In laboratory experiments the prey eaten are often not replaced, so prey density falls during the experiment. Then the instantaneous rate $\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}t = -aN/(1 + ahN)$ for one predator must be integrated over the experiment of length $T$: separating variables, $\ln(N/N_0) + ah(N - N_0) = -aT$, and with $N_e = N_0 - N$ eaten,

$$
N_e = N_0\Bigl[1 - \exp\bigl(a(hN_e - T)\bigr)\Bigr], \tag{25.9}
$$
Rogers' (1972) *random predator equation*. It is implicit in $N_e$ but can be solved with the Lambert $W$ function, $N_e = N_0 - W\bigl(ahN_0\mathrm{e}^{-a(T - hN_0)}\bigr)/(ah)$. Fitting Eq. (25.8) to depletion data underestimates the attack rate (Figure 5A).

### 25.4.3. Three Types

Holling distinguished three shapes (Figure 4):

| Type | Form | Mechanism | Examples | Per capita risk $f(N)/N$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | $f = aN$, up to a maximum | Search limits intake; handling takes no time from searching, until gut capacity is reached | Filter feeders such as *Daphnia* feeding on algae | Constant, then decreasing |
| II | $f = aN/(1 + ahN)$ | Handling time | Most insect predators and parasitoids, many vertebrates (wolves on moose) | Decreasing from the start: inverse density dependence |
| III | $f = bN^2/(1 + bhN^2)$, generally sigmoid | Attack rate rises with prey density: learning, a **search image**, switching between prey types, refuges that hold a fixed number of prey | Vertebrate predators of insects, generalists that switch | Increasing at low density, then decreasing |

A convenient family containing types II and III is $f = aN^q/(1 + ahN^q)$ (Real 1977), with $q = 1$ for type II and $q > 1$ for sigmoid responses; for type III the attack rate is itself proportional to density, $a(N) = bN$.

The shape of $f(N)/N$ decides whether predation is a stabilizing force. With a type II response each additional prey reduces the risk of all the others (predators are swamped), which is destabilizing and underlies the synchronized mass emergences of periodical cicadas and mast seeding of trees; with type III the risk rises with density over the range where the response is accelerating, so predators can regulate prey at low density, as birds regulate the spruce budworm between outbreaks ([7.19.13](/7.19.13#sec-13.2.5)).

Switching is the most important source of type III responses in nature. Murdoch (1969) offered predatory snails two kinds of prey in different proportions and found that the snails took the commoner prey more often than its proportion would predict; a generalist that switches to whichever prey is common has a sigmoid response to each prey species even though its total intake follows a type II curve. Switching connects the functional response to foraging theory ([7.18.22](/7.18.22)): an optimal forager drops a prey type from its diet when better prey are common, whatever its own abundance.

### 25.4.4. Estimating Attack Rate and Handling Time

Functional responses are measured by offering single predators different initial numbers $N_0$ of prey for a fixed time $T$ and counting the prey eaten. Three statistical points matter.

1. Fit the right equation. With replacement, the disc equation (25.8) applies; without replacement, Rogers' equation (25.9). Both are nonlinear in the parameters and are fitted by nonlinear least squares or, better, by maximum likelihood with a binomial distribution for the number eaten out of $N_0$ (the numerical methods are in [3.5.5](/3.5.5)). The old linearizations, such as plotting $1/N_e$ against $1/N_0$ (the Lineweaver–Burk plot of enzyme kinetics), weight the noisiest points most heavily and give biased estimates.
2. Decide the type before fitting it. Juliano (2001) proposed fitting a **logistic regression** of the proportion eaten $N_e/N_0$ on $N_0$, with a polynomial of degree two or three: a negative linear term (the proportion declines from the start) indicates type II, a positive linear term followed by a negative quadratic or cubic term (the proportion first rises) indicates type III (Figure 5B).
3. Laboratory arenas are small and simple. Attack rates measured in Petri dishes overestimate field values, and handling times exclude the time predators spend resting and digesting; in the field, consumption is estimated from kill rates of radio-tracked predators (wolves, lynx) or from gut contents and digestion rates.

### 25.4.5. Numerical and Aggregative Responses

The numerical response has two components. The reproductive response is the increase of predator births with food, modeled in Eq. (25.7) by $cf(N)$; because births lag behind feeding (gestation, maturation), it is the main source of delay in predator–prey interactions, and it is weak in long-lived predators that cannot breed faster than once a year. Snowy owls and long-tailed skuas in the Arctic barely breed at all in years of low lemming density and lay large clutches in peak years. The aggregative response is the movement of predators into patches of high prey density: predators that follow the **ideal free distribution** ([7.18.25](/7.18.25)) concentrate where intake is highest, which is how ladybirds arrive at aphid outbreaks and seabirds at fish shoals. Aggregation does not change the total number of predators, but it changes who is at risk, and its role in stabilizing host–parasitoid systems is the subject of [7.19.27](/7.19.27#sec-27.3).

## 25.5. Predator Interference and Ratio Dependence

The functional responses of Section 25.4 depend on prey density alone. Predators, however, also compete with and disturb one another: hummingbirds spend time defending flowers, gulls steal food, searching parasitoids leave patches where they meet others, and prey hide when predators are common. All of these reduce the intake per predator as predator density increases, a set of processes known as *mutual interference* (Hassell 1978).

Three forms are in use.

- Hassell–Varley (1969): the searching efficiency declines as a power of predator density, $a = QP^{-\mu}$, where $\mu$ is the *interference constant*, estimated as minus the slope of $\log a$ against $\log P$ in parasitoid experiments.
- Beddington–DeAngelis (Beddington 1975, DeAngelis et al. 1975): extend the time budget of Proposition 25.4.1 by a time $w$ lost in each encounter with another predator, and let encounters with predators occur at rate $a'P$ while searching. Then $T = T_s + hN_e + wa'PT_s$, and with $q = wa'$,

  $$
  f(N, P) = \frac{aN}{1 + ahN + qP}. \tag{25.10}
  $$
- Ratio-dependent (Arditi and Ginzburg 1989): intake depends only on the prey available per predator, $f = \tilde a(N/P)/\bigl(1 + \tilde ah(N/P)\bigr) = \tilde aN/(P + \tilde ahN)$, with $\tilde a$ now measured per unit time, not per unit density. This is the limit of Eq. (25.10) in which interference dominates the 1 in the denominator.

The effect on the phase plane is immediate. With prey-dependent predation the predator isocline is vertical, because the predator's growth rate depends on $N$ only. With interference the predator needs more prey to break even when predators are common: from $caN/(1 + ahN + qP) = m$, the isocline is the rising line $P = \bigl((ca - mah)N - m\bigr)/(mq)$. A tilted predator isocline gives the predator its own direct density dependence, which damps oscillations, and the equilibrium prey density now rises with the prey's carrying capacity (Figure 6C): enrichment benefits both levels of the food chain, as it often does in lakes and grasslands, whereas in prey-dependent models the extra production goes entirely into predators (the paradox of [7.19.26](/7.19.26) and the food-chain theory of [7.19.39](/7.19.39)).

Which description is closer to nature has been debated sharply. Arditi and Ginzburg (2012) argue that predators usually compete for prey, so that ratio dependence is the better default at the time scale of population dynamics; for wolves preying on moose on Isle Royale, kill rates were better explained by the moose-to-wolf ratio than by moose density alone (Vucetich, Peterson and Schaefer 2002). Abrams (2015) replies that ratio dependence is a special case without a mechanism, that it behaves badly at low densities (the ratio-dependent model can drive both species to extinction together, because the per capita risk of prey does not decline as predators become rare), and that the prey-dependent model with interference covers the data equally well. The practical position, as for the type of functional response, is to fit the predator dependence as a parameter, as Eq. (25.10) does.

## Summary

- The Lotka–Volterra model (Formula 25.1.1) assumes exponential prey growth, exponential predator decline, a linear functional response and constant conversion. It scales to a one-parameter system, Eq. (25.2). Its equilibrium $N^* = m/(ca)$ is set by the predator, $P^* = r/a$ by the prey's growth rate; linearization gives a center with frequency $\sqrt{rm}$ and a quarter-period lag of the predator.
- The first integral $V = caN - m\ln N + aP - r\ln P$ (Proposition 25.1.1) makes every orbit a closed curve; the averages over any cycle equal the equilibrium (Theorem 25.1.1). The closed orbits are neutral and structurally unstable: self-limitation of the prey or saturation of the predator destroys them, and noise makes the amplitude wander.
- Volterra principle (Proposition 25.2.1): harvesting both species at the same rate raises the average prey and lowers the average predator density; reduced fishing in wartime increased the share of predatory fish, and broad-spectrum insecticides can cause pest resurgence.
- Logistic prey (Proposition 25.3.1): the predator persists if and only if $\beta = N^*/K < 1$; the coexistence equilibrium is then globally stable (Lyapunov function with $\dot V = -\alpha(x - x^*)^2$), a focus or a node; damping weakens as the predator becomes more efficient relative to $K$.
- Functional responses (Definition 25.4.1): type I linear, type II the disc equation $aN/(1 + ahN)$ derived from the time budget or renewal theory (Proposition 25.4.1), type III sigmoid (learning, switching, refuges). Only type III gives density-dependent predation risk at low prey density. Without prey replacement, fit Rogers' equation (25.9); distinguish types by logistic regression of the proportion eaten.
- Numerical responses (reproductive and aggregative) supply the delay of predator–prey interactions and the spatial heterogeneity of risk.
- Interference (Hassell–Varley, Beddington–DeAngelis Eq. (25.10), ratio dependence) tilts the predator isocline, adds direct density dependence and makes prey equilibria rise with enrichment.

## Questions

1. In the Lotka–Volterra model, a disease raises the predator death rate $m$ by 20 %. What happens to the average densities of prey and predator, and to the period of small oscillations?

   **Answer** By Theorem 25.1.1 the averages are $\bar N = m/(ca)$ and $\bar P = r/a$. Increasing $m$ by 20 % increases the average prey density by 20 % and leaves the average predator density unchanged: the predators become rarer per prey but not in absolute number, because their average is fixed by the prey's growth rate. The period of small oscillations $2\pi/\sqrt{rm}$ decreases by the factor $1/\sqrt{1.2} \approx 0.91$, about 9 %.
2. Use Dulac's criterion with $B(N, P) = 1/(NP)$ to show that the model with logistic prey, Eq. (25.5), has no periodic solution in the positive quadrant, and that the criterion gives no conclusion for the Lotka–Volterra model (25.1).

   **Answer** For Eq. (25.5), $B\dot N = r(1 - N/K)/P - a$ and $B\dot P = ca - m/N$. Then $\partial(B\dot N)/\partial N + \partial(B\dot P)/\partial P = -r/(KP) + 0 < 0$ everywhere in the quadrant, which is simply connected, so there is no closed orbit ([Theorem 11.6.2](/2.4.11#thm-11.6.2) of 2.4.11 with Dulac's extension). For Eq. (25.1) the same computation gives $\partial(r/P - a)/\partial N + \partial(ca - m/N)/\partial P = 0$ identically: the criterion fails, as it must, since the model has a continuum of closed orbits. The divergence $-r/(KP)$ measures how fast the area of a patch of initial conditions shrinks in the weighted flow, so the strength of prey self-limitation is literally the rate at which the neutral cycles are damped.
3. A predator has a type II functional response with $a = 0.2$ per day (per unit prey density) and $h = 0.25$ day. Find its maximum intake, its half-saturation density, and the fraction of a prey population of density $N = 5$ and $N = 50$ eaten per predator per day.

   **Answer** Maximum $1/h = 4$ prey per day; half-saturation $1/(ah) = 20$. At $N = 5$: $f = 1/(1 + 0.25) = 0.8$ prey per day, a fraction $0.8/5 = 0.16$; at $N = 50$: $f = 10/(1 + 2.5) \approx 2.86$, a fraction $0.057$. The per capita risk falls almost threefold as prey become ten times more abundant: inverse density dependence.
4. In the logistic-prey model (25.5) with $r = 1$, $K = 100$, $a = 0.02$, $c = 0.5$, $m = 0.4$: will the predator persist? Find the equilibrium and say whether the approach to it is oscillatory.

   **Answer** $N^* = m/(ca) = 0.4/0.01 = 40 < K$, so $\beta = 0.4 < 1$ and the predator persists. $P^* = (r/a)(1 - N^*/K) = 50 \times 0.6 = 30$. In the scaled form $\alpha = caK/r = 1$; the focus condition $\beta < 4\alpha(1 - \beta)$ reads $0.4 < 2.4$, true, so the approach is by damped oscillations. The eigenvalues in units of $r$ are $-\beta/2 \pm \mathrm{i}\sqrt{\alpha\beta(1 - \beta) - \beta^2/4} = -0.2 \pm 0.44\,\mathrm{i}$: the oscillations decay by a factor $\mathrm{e}^{-0.2 \cdot 2\pi/0.44} \approx 0.06$ per cycle.
5. Explain, using the isoclines, why a predator with strong interference is more likely to coexist stably with its prey than one without, and why ratio dependence predicts that prey density increases with enrichment.

   **Answer** Without interference the predator's growth rate depends only on prey density, so its isocline is vertical at $N^*$; stability then depends on where this vertical line meets the prey isocline, and with a saturating response it may meet it on the rising limb of the hump, which is unstable (7.19.26). Interference makes the predator's per capita growth decline with its own density, so the predator isocline tilts upward to the right: the predator is self-limited, which adds a negative diagonal element to the Jacobian and damps oscillations, and the intersection moves toward the descending limb of the prey isocline. Under ratio dependence the predator's growth depends on $N/P$ alone, so its isocline is a line $P = \gamma N$ through the origin; the equilibrium lies where this line meets the prey isocline, and since the prey isocline stretches in proportion to $K$, the intersection does too: both $N^*$ and $P^*$ are proportional to $K$.
