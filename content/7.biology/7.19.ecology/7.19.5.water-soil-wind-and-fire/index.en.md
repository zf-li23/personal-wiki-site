The previous two chapters treated the energy environment of organisms: radiation and light ([7.19.3](/7.19.3)) and temperature ([7.19.4](/7.19.4)). This chapter turns to the material environment, the water, air and soil in which organisms live and from which they take the substances they are built of, and to two physical forces, wind and fire, that act on whole stands of vegetation. Water and soil are both conditions and resources in the sense of [7.19.6](/7.19.6): the salinity or pH of water is a condition that an organism tolerates, the water itself a resource that it uses up. We follow the same plan for each factor: its physical properties, how it varies in space and time, and the morphological, physiological and behavioral adaptations of plants and animals to it. The cellular mechanisms of water relations are treated in plant physiology ([7.13.3](/7.13.3)) and animal physiology ([7.14.21](/7.14.21)), the ecological adaptations of plant structure in botany ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)); here we emphasize budgets, gradients and the distribution of organisms, and we write simple models where a mechanism can be quantified: the water budget of a desert animal, the solubility and daily cycle of oxygen in water, the retention of water by soils, and the dynamics of fire regimes.

## 5.1. Water

### 5.1.1. The Ecological Roles of Water

Water plays four roles in the life of organisms. It is the internal medium of every organism and the bulk of its living matter (60–90% of the fresh mass of most active tissues); it is the solvent in which metabolism and transport take place, so that an organism that loses too much water stops functioning long before it runs out of anything else; it is the external medium, the habitat itself, of aquatic organisms, from which they also obtain oxygen and nutrients; and on land its abundance and seasonality are, together with temperature, the main determinants of the distribution of vegetation ([7.19.41](/7.19.41)).

The physical properties of water explain most of these roles:

| Property | Value | Ecological consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Polar molecule, hydrogen bonds | Dielectric constant about 80 | An excellent solvent for ions and polar molecules: nutrient transport in xylem, blood and soil solution |
| High specific heat capacity | $4.18\,\mathrm{J\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ | Water bodies and wet bodies change temperature slowly; coasts have mild climates |
| High latent heat of vaporization | $2.45\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}$ at 20 °C | Evaporation is the most powerful cooling mechanism of plants and animals |
| Density maximum at 4 °C; ice less dense than water | $1000$ vs. $917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ | Lakes freeze from the top; the ice cap insulates the water below, and the bottom water of deep lakes stays at about 4 °C in winter ([7.19.3](/7.19.3)) |
| High surface tension | $0.072\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ | Capillary retention of water in soil pores (Section 5.4.2) and cohesion of the xylem water column ([7.13.3](/7.13.3)) |
| Low solubility and slow diffusion of gases | O₂ about 1/30 of its content in air | Oxygen often limits aquatic life (Section 5.2.1) |

Of the water on the Earth about 97.5% is salt water; of the remaining 2.5% about two thirds is locked in ice caps and glaciers, and most of the rest is groundwater, so that lakes, rivers, soil moisture and the atmosphere together hold well under 1% of the fresh water. On land, the water available to organisms comes as precipitation, and its amount depends on latitude (the wet equatorial belt, the dry subtropical belts near 20°–30°, the cyclonic rain of the mid-latitudes), distance from the sea, the windward or leeward side of mountains, and season; these patterns and the humidity of the air (relative humidity and saturation deficit) are described with climate in [7.19.3](/7.19.3). What matters for an organism is the evaporative demand of the air, which rises with temperature at a given relative humidity, and the supply of liquid water in the soil, which depends on precipitation and on the soil's capacity to hold it (Section 5.4).

### 5.1.2. Water Budgets

No organism on land is watertight, and every terrestrial organism must balance its gains and losses of water. Writing them out as a budget makes the possible adaptations explicit.

**Formula 5.1.1 (Water Budget)** The internal water $W_{\mathrm{ia}}$ of a terrestrial animal and $W_{\mathrm{ip}}$ of a terrestrial plant change according to

$$
\Delta W_{\mathrm{ia}} = W_{\mathrm{d}} + W_{\mathrm{f}} + W_{\mathrm{m}} + W_{\mathrm{a}} - W_{\mathrm{e}} - W_{\mathrm{s}}, \qquad
\Delta W_{\mathrm{ip}} = W_{\mathrm{r}} + W_{\mathrm{a}} - W_{\mathrm{t}} - W_{\mathrm{s}},
$$
where for animals $W_{\mathrm{d}}$ is water drunk, $W_{\mathrm{f}}$ free water in food, $W_{\mathrm{m}}$ **metabolic water** produced by oxidizing food, $W_{\mathrm{a}}$ water absorbed from the air, $W_{\mathrm{e}}$ evaporative loss (from the skin and the respiratory tract) and $W_{\mathrm{s}}$ loss in urine, feces and secretions; for plants $W_{\mathrm{r}}$ is uptake by roots, $W_{\mathrm{t}}$ **transpiration** and $W_{\mathrm{s}}$ loss in secretions, nectar, fruits and seeds.

At steady state the gains equal the losses, and each term is a target of selection. The size of the metabolic term follows from stoichiometry: oxidizing 1 g of starch yields $0.56\,\mathrm{g}$ of water, 1 g of fat $1.07\,\mathrm{g}$ and 1 g of protein (excreted as urea) about $0.40\,\mathrm{g}$. Per litre of oxygen consumed, however, starch gives more water ($0.67\,\mathrm{g}$) than fat ($0.53\,\mathrm{g}$), because fat needs more oxygen per gram, and every litre of oxygen must be breathed in with air that carries water out.

**Example 5.1.1** Merriam's kangaroo rat (*Dipodomys merriami*) of the North American deserts can live indefinitely on dry seeds without drinking. Schmidt-Nielsen (1964) measured its budget on a diet of barley: per 100 g of barley eaten, the animal gains $54.0\,\mathrm{mL}$ of oxidation water and $6.0\,\mathrm{mL}$ of free water in the grain, and loses $43.9\,\mathrm{mL}$ by evaporation, $13.5\,\mathrm{mL}$ in urine and $2.6\,\mathrm{mL}$ in feces. Gains and losses balance at $60\,\mathrm{mL}$ with $W_{\mathrm{d}} = 0$. The balance depends on three adaptations: a kidney that produces urine of up to about $5000\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$ (four times as concentrated as human urine, [7.14.24](/7.14.24)), very dry feces, and a nasal **countercurrent exchange** that cools exhaled air below body temperature so that much of its water condenses in the nose and is recovered. Its burrow, where the air is cool and humid by day, reduces evaporation further.

The importance of the nasal mechanism can be seen in a minimal model. A small mammal breathes about $V = 25\,\mathrm{L}$ of air per litre of oxygen consumed; exhaled air is saturated at its exit temperature $T_{\mathrm{ex}}$, inhaled air at temperature $T_{\mathrm{a}}$ has relative humidity $h$. The respiratory loss per litre of oxygen is then

$$
w_{\mathrm{resp}} = V\left[\rho_{\mathrm{sat}}(T_{\mathrm{ex}}) - h\,\rho_{\mathrm{sat}}(T_{\mathrm{a}})\right],
$$
where $\rho_{\mathrm{sat}}$ is the saturation vapor density ($44\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-3}}$ at 37 °C, $26\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-3}}$ at 27 °C, $23\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-3}}$ at 25 °C). Without cooling ($T_{\mathrm{ex}} = 37\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) the loss in dry air is about $1.1\,\mathrm{g}$, more than the oxidation water of any food; cooling the exhaled air by 10 °C cuts it to $0.65\,\mathrm{g}$, below the oxidation water of starch at any humidity (figure below). Respiration alone can thus produce a net gain of water, which pays for the losses through the skin, urine and feces.

### 5.1.3. Water and Land Plants

By their relation to water, land plants are classified as **xerophytes**, which tolerate long drought; mesophytes, which grow best with a moderate water supply and include most crops and temperate trees; and hygrophytes, which grow in permanently moist habitats such as forest floors and marsh edges and wilt quickly when dry. Their leaf structure and the strategies of xerophytes (drought escape, water uptake, conservation, storage and tolerance) are described in [7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2). From the ecological point of view the central problem is a trade-off: CO₂ enters the leaf through the same stomata through which water leaves, so every gram of carbon fixed costs water. A plant can conserve water at the expense of photosynthesis now, or photosynthesize at the risk of running out of water later, and plants solve this dilemma by strategies (fixed in development) and by tactics (flexible responses):

- Short active lives: desert annuals live a few weeks after rain and pass the dry season as seeds, neither photosynthesizing nor transpiring.
- Seasonal leaves: many *Acacia* species shed their leaves in drought; the Israeli desert shrub *Teucrium polium* produces finely divided, thin-cuticled leaves in the wet season and replaces them by small, thick-cuticled leaves and finally by green spines as the soil dries, a seasonal sequence of ever more watertight organs.
- Long-lived, water-conserving leaves: evergreen desert shrubs have hairy, waxy leaves with sunken stomata that lose water slowly but also take up CO₂ slowly, so they photosynthesize slowly even when water is plentiful.
- Physiology: **C4 plants** have about twice the **water-use efficiency** (carbon fixed per unit water transpired) of **C3 plants**, and **CAM plants** open their stomata at night ([7.13.8](/7.13.8)).
- Tactics: stomatal conductance changes within minutes; the rain-forest-gap plant *Piper auritum* wilts at midday, reducing the leaf area exposed to the sun by about 55% and transpiration by 30–50% (Chiariello et al. 1987).

These alternatives can coexist in one community. In the seasonally dry savannas of northern Australia, evergreen, deciduous, semideciduous and brevideciduous trees occur in roughly equal numbers: the deciduous species avoid drought by losing their leaves and make no net carbon for about three months, whereas the evergreens keep a positive carbon balance through the dry season at the cost of much lower predawn water potentials (Eamus 1999).

Roots are the other side of the budget. Root systems forage for water: they branch profusely in moist patches and hardly at all in dry ones, and plants of dry climates invest more in roots, which may reach 90% of the biomass of desert and semiarid grassland plants and extend 10–30 m down to groundwater in mesquite (*Prosopis*). Shallow, wide root systems are the alternative: the roots of a 15 m saguaro cactus (*Carnegiea gigantea*) spread over more than $700\,\mathrm{m^2}$ just below the surface and take up the water of a brief shower within hours. The saguaro and the dromedary camel illustrate convergent solutions to the same budget (table below).

| Problem | Dromedary camel (*Camelus dromedarius*) | Saguaro cactus |
|---|---|---|
| Acquire water when available | Drinks up to a third of its body mass at once | Shallow root network absorbs rain within hours |
| Store water | Tolerates dehydration to 20–25% of body mass while keeping plasma volume (humans die at about 12%); stores water in tissues and gut | Stem and arms swell with stored water (the pleated stem expands) |
| Reduce heat gain | Thick hair insulates; faces the sun to reduce exposed area | Exposes mainly the tips of trunk and arms to the midday sun; spines and hairs shade the growing tips |
| Reduce evaporative loss | Lets body temperature rise by up to 6–7 °C during the day and unloads the stored heat at night, instead of sweating; very concentrated urine and dry feces | Stomata closed by day (CAM); internal temperature may exceed 50 °C |

The camel's hump is often said to be a water store. It is a fat store: although oxidizing fat yields more water per gram than any other food, the extra ventilation needed to supply the oxygen costs respiratory water, so in dry air the net gain is small (Schmidt-Nielsen 1964). The camel's real advantages are its tolerance of dehydration and its saving of evaporative water by heat storage, an example of an adaptive suite, a set of coordinated traits that together suit an organism to one environment ([7.19.2](/7.19.2)).

### 5.1.4. Water and Land Animals

Animals lose water by evaporation through the skin and the respiratory surfaces and in excretion; they gain it by drinking, in food, as metabolic water and in a few cases from the air (some ticks, mites and insect larvae absorb water vapor from humid air). Adaptations act on each term of Formula 5.1.1:

- Evaporative loss through the body surface is reduced by the waxy epicuticle of insects and spiders, the keratinized scales of reptiles and the skin lipids of birds and mammals; amphibians, with permeable skin, are confined to humid habitats or behave to avoid dryness. The Namib Desert tenebrionid beetle *Onymacris unguicularis* gains water from fog by standing head-down on dune crests so that droplets condense on its back and run to its mouth.
- Respiratory loss is reduced by nasal countercurrent exchange in mammals and birds (Example 5.1.1) and by closing the spiracles in insects.
- Excretory loss is reduced by concentrating urine (the length of the loop of Henle, [7.14.24](/7.14.24)), by reabsorbing water in the cloaca, rectum or Malpighian tubule system, and by excreting nitrogen as **uric acid**, which needs little water (birds, reptiles, insects and land snails; [7.14.21](/7.14.21#sec-21.4)).
- Behavior: desert animals are nocturnal or crepuscular and spend the day in burrows, where humidity is high and temperature moderate; they migrate or move to water; many pass the dry season in aestivation or **diapause** ([7.19.19](/7.19.19)).

Drought changes behavior and therefore the energy budget even of animals that do not live in deserts. In the extreme drought of 2022, the water level of Poyang Lake, China's largest freshwater lake and the main wintering site of the endangered Oriental stork (*Ciconia boyciana*), fell to its lowest level on record. Storks watched after the drought walked about twice as fast while searching (median about 38 versus 18 steps per minute) but took food only about half as often (about 3 versus 6 attempts per minute) as before it (Yang, Shao and Wang 2024): with the shallow pools gone, prey had become scarcer and more dispersed, and the birds paid more for less.

Humidity also affects development, survival and coloration. Hygrophilous insects survive and reproduce better as humidity rises, whereas xerophilous insects have an optimum humidity at which development is fastest, and both survival and fecundity decline away from it. Coloration, too, follows humidity: by **Gloger's rule** ([7.19.4](/7.19.4)), endotherms of warm, humid regions tend to be more darkly pigmented than their relatives in cool, dry regions. Among the explanations that involve humidity directly are crypsis in darker, more shaded humid habitats and the resistance of eumelanin-rich feathers to feather-degrading bacteria, which are more abundant in humid climates.

## 5.2. Life in Water

### 5.2.1. Dissolved Oxygen

The amount of a gas dissolved in water at equilibrium with air is given by **Henry's law** ([6.3.5](/6.3.5)): the concentration is proportional to the partial pressure of the gas, $c = k_{\mathrm{H}}\,p_{\ce{O2}}$, and the solubility constant $k_{\mathrm{H}}$ decreases with temperature and with salinity (the "salting-out" effect). Air-saturated fresh water holds $14.6\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$ of O₂ at 0 °C, $9.1$ at 20 °C and $7.6$ at 30 °C; seawater holds about 20% less ($11.4$, $7.4$ and $6.2\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$). Air at 20 °C contains about $280\,\mathrm{mg}$ of O₂ per litre, thirty times more than air-saturated water, and O₂ diffuses about $10^4$ times more slowly in water than in air. Because the partial pressure of oxygen falls with altitude (Section 5.3), saturated water at 4000 m contains only about 60% as much oxygen as at sea level; contrary to a common statement, the oxygen content of water does depend on altitude.

![Dissolved oxygen. A: concentration of oxygen in air-saturated water as a function of temperature (fit of Garcia and Gordon 1992 to the measurements of Benson and Krause) for fresh, brackish and sea water at sea level, and for fresh water at 2000 m and 4000 m. B: a one-box model of a pond at 25 °C, in which oxygen is produced by photosynthesis during the day (sine-shaped, 06:00–18:00), consumed by respiration of the whole community at a constant rate and exchanged with the air at a rate proportional to the deficit from saturation (re-aeration coefficient 0.08 per hour). In the eutrophic pond, where both production and respiration are higher, the water is supersaturated in the afternoon but falls below 2 mg/L before dawn.](./plots/dissolved-oxygen.py)

Oxygen in a water body is supplied by diffusion from the air (aided by turbulence, so that running water is better oxygenated than still water) and by photosynthesis, and is consumed by respiration of animals, plants and above all bacteria decomposing organic matter. It therefore decreases with depth from the surface, where it is supplied, towards the bottom, where it is consumed, and in a productive lake the deep water may become anoxic in summer when thermal stratification cuts it off from the air ([7.19.3](/7.19.3)). The daily course can be written as a one-box model:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}t} = P(t) - R + k\,(C_{\mathrm{s}} - C),
$$
where $C$ is the oxygen concentration, $P(t)$ the rate of photosynthetic production (zero at night), $R$ the community respiration, $C_{\mathrm{s}}$ the saturation concentration and $k$ the re-aeration coefficient. Averaging over a day at steady state gives $\bar C = C_{\mathrm{s}} + (\bar P - R)/k$: a balanced pond ($\bar P = R$) averages at saturation, but the amplitude of the daily cycle grows with the absolute rates of production and respiration. In a eutrophic pond the water is supersaturated in the afternoon and falls to near zero before dawn (figure above). This is why fish kills in fishponds occur in summer nights and early mornings, often after a calm, overcast day when photosynthesis was weak: the fish first gather at the surface gulping the oxygen-rich film (called "floating heads" by Chinese fish farmers) and then die. The same mechanism, on a larger scale, produces the hypoxic "dead zones" of **eutrophication** ([7.11.30](/7.11.30)).

Aquatic animals cope with low oxygen by ventilating their gills more, raising the oxygen affinity of their hemoglobin (lowering the **P50**, partly by reducing the ATP and GTP that bind to deoxyhemoglobin in fish erythrocytes), breathing air at the surface (many tropical swamp fishes, lungfishes), or switching to anaerobic metabolism. Crucian carp (*Carassius carassius*) and goldfish survive months without oxygen under the ice by converting lactate to ethanol, which they excrete across the gills, avoiding acidosis. Plants of waterlogged soils and of water have **aerenchyma**, continuous air channels from the shoots to the roots, and some swamp trees produce pneumatophores, root extensions that grow up into the air ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.3)).

### 5.2.2. Salinity

Salinity sets the osmotic problem of aquatic life, described physiologically in [7.14.21](/7.14.21#sec-21.5). Marine invertebrates and hagfish are mostly **osmoconformers**, their body fluids nearly isosmotic with seawater; most vertebrates are **osmoregulators**. Freshwater fishes are hyperosmotic to their medium, gain water and lose salts, and therefore produce copious dilute urine and take up ions actively at the gills; marine teleosts are hyposmotic, lose water and gain salts, and therefore drink seawater, excrete the salt through the gills and produce little urine; sharks and rays retain urea and trimethylamine *N*-oxide (TMAO) in their blood, which makes them slightly hyperosmotic to seawater, and excrete NaCl through the rectal gland. Fishes that migrate between fresh and salt water (salmon, which are born in rivers, grow in the sea and return to spawn, are anadromous; eels, which grow in rivers and migrate to the sea to spawn, are catadromous) switch the direction of ion transport in the gills under the control of cortisol, growth hormone and prolactin.

Tolerance of salinity determines distributions most sharply in estuaries, where marine and fresh water meet. **Euryhaline** species tolerate a wide range of salinity, **stenohaline** species only a narrow one. Two grass shrimps, *Palaemonetes pugio* and *P. vulgaris*, co-occur in the estuaries of the eastern United States, but *P. pugio* extends into fresher water: at low salinity its standard metabolic rate is markedly lower than that of *P. vulgaris*, which must spend much more energy simply on osmoregulation (Rowe 2002). On the seashore salinity acts together with exposure to air and wave action, producing the vertical zonation of rocky shores, whose upper limits are set by physical stress and whose lower limits are often set by competition and predation ([7.19.33](/7.19.33)).

### 5.2.3. Pressure, Density and pH

Water is about 800 times denser than air, which supports aquatic organisms but resists their movement. Pressure rises by about 1 atmosphere ($101\,\mathrm{kPa}$) for every 10 m of depth, so that the deep sea floor at 4000 m is at about 400 atm. Pelagic animals adjust their buoyancy with gas (the **swim bladder** of teleosts, the floats of siphonophores), with lipids of low density (shark livers, copepod oil sacs) or by replacing heavy ions with lighter ones; deep-sea fishes have no swim bladder, reduced skeletons and watery muscles. Proteins are compressed and destabilized at high pressure, and deep-sea animals counteract this with piezolytes: the TMAO content of fish muscle rises linearly with depth, and extrapolating the trend to the point where fish would become hyperosmotic to seawater gives about 8200 m, close to the greatest depth at which fish have been seen (Yancey et al. 2014). Diving mammals avoid decompression sickness and nitrogen narcosis by exhaling before diving and letting their flexible rib cage and lungs collapse at depth, which stops gas exchange in the alveoli ([7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.6.3)).

The pH of water and soil acts directly on osmoregulation, enzymes and gas exchange, and indirectly through the solubility of nutrients and toxic metals (Section 5.4.4). Most freshwater animals do not tolerate a pH below about 5; acidification by acid rain removed fish from thousands of lakes in Scandinavia and North America in the twentieth century, and its reversal after emission controls has produced chemical recovery but slower biological recovery ([7.19.46](/7.19.46)). Some archaea and bacteria grow at pH 0–2 (*Acidithiobacillus*) or at pH 10–11 in soda lakes (*Arthrospira*, formerly *Spirulina*). The absorption of anthropogenic CO₂ has lowered the pH of the surface ocean by about 0.1 units since the Industrial Revolution, with consequences for calcifying organisms ([7.19.47](/7.19.47)).

## 5.3. The Atmosphere: Oxygen and Hypoxia

Dry air contains 78.1% N₂, 20.9% O₂, 0.93% Ar and about 0.042% CO₂ (420 ppm in 2024). Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor of aerobic respiration and comes from photosynthesis; its rise in the Great Oxidation Event is the classic example of the reaction of organisms on their environment ([7.19.2](/7.19.2)). CO₂ is the carbon source of photosynthesis and is treated as a resource in [7.19.6](/7.19.6).

Above ground on land, oxygen is rarely limiting except at altitude. The fraction of oxygen in the air is constant to the edge of the stratosphere, but its partial pressure falls with total pressure, roughly exponentially with altitude: at 3650 m (Lhasa) the barometric pressure is about 64% of that at sea level, at 5000 m about 53%, and at the summit of Qomolangma (Everest, 8849 m) about 33%. The responses of mammals to this hypoxia are of two kinds ([7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.6.2), [7.14.20](/7.14.20#sec-20.6.3)):

- Acclimatization of lowland individuals: increased ventilation, more erythropoietin and hemoglobin, more 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate in the red cells, more capillaries and myoglobin in the muscles. The rise in 2,3-BPG shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the right (raises the P50), which helps unloading in the tissues at moderate altitude but becomes counterproductive at extreme altitude, where loading in the lung limits supply.
- Genetic adaptation of high-altitude species and populations: high-affinity hemoglobins (low P50) evolved independently in the bar-headed goose, the Andean goose, llamas and vicuñas, and high-altitude populations of deer mice; the yak and the plateau pika have large hearts and lungs and high capillary densities; and Tibetans have hemoglobin concentrations close to those of lowlanders, associated with alleles of genes in the hypoxia-inducible factor pathway ([7.19.7](/7.19.7#sec-7.3.2)).

Ectotherms, with their low metabolic rates, are less constrained by oxygen at altitude than by temperature, food and humidity. Subterranean mammals such as mole rats live in burrows where O₂ may fall below 10% and CO₂ rise above 5%; they have high-affinity blood, low metabolic rates and low sensitivity of respiration to CO₂. Soil air is treated in Section 5.4.3.

## 5.4. Soil

### 5.4.1. Soil as a Three-Phase System

Soil is the weathered surface layer of the land, made of mineral particles, organic matter, water and air, and inhabited by roots, microbes and animals. It provides plants with anchorage, water and mineral nutrients, and it is the site of decomposition, in which the elements of dead organisms are returned to inorganic form ([7.19.44](/7.19.44)). The solid phase consists of mineral particles of different sizes and of organic matter; the pores between them hold water and air in proportions that change after every rain.

*Soil texture* is the relative proportion by mass of the mineral particles of different size classes: sand (2–0.05 mm in the USDA system), silt (0.05–0.002 mm) and clay (< 0.002 mm). Sandy soils are loose and well aerated but hold little water and lose nutrients by leaching; clay soils hold much water and many nutrient cations (clay particles and humus carry negative charges and determine the **cation exchange capacity**) but are poorly aerated and drain slowly; loams, with a balanced mixture, combine the advantages of both. *Soil structure* is the arrangement of the particles into aggregates. A crumb structure, in which mineral particles are glued into water-stable aggregates of 0.25–10 mm by humus, fungal hyphae, bacterial polysaccharides and the casts of earthworms, gives a soil both large pores between the aggregates (for drainage and air) and small pores within them (for water storage); it is the best structure for plant growth and is destroyed by compaction and continuous tillage.

### 5.4.2. Soil Water

Water in soil is held in pores by capillary forces against gravity. By the **Young–Laplace equation** a pore of radius $r$ holds water at a suction (negative pressure) $\lvert\psi\rvert = 2\gamma\cos\theta/r$, where $\gamma = 0.072\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ is the **surface tension** of water and $\theta$ the contact angle (about 0 for mineral surfaces): the narrower the pore, the more strongly it holds its water. After rain the largest pores drain by gravity within a day or two. The water left is the **field capacity**, the upper limit of the water that a freely drained soil retains, conventionally taken as the water content at $\psi = -33\,\mathrm{kPa}$ (at this suction pores wider than about 9 μm in diameter are empty). As roots withdraw water, the remaining water is held in ever smaller pores, until the **permanent wilting point** is reached ([7.13.3](/7.13.3#sec-3.1)): the soil water content at which plants wilt and do not recover overnight, for most crops at a soil water potential of about $-1.5\,\mathrm{MPa}$ (pores narrower than about 0.2 μm).

The water held between field capacity and the wilting point is the available water capacity. It depends on texture in a nonmonotonic way: sand holds little water at any suction, clay holds much but most of it so tightly that it lies below the wilting point, and loams and silt loams store the most available water (figure below). Plants of arid regions extract water at much lower potentials (down to $-4$ to $-8\,\mathrm{MPa}$ in desert shrubs), which extends the available range. Water retention is described by the empirical curve of van Genuchten (1980),

$$
\theta(h) = \theta_{\mathrm{r}} + \frac{\theta_{\mathrm{s}} - \theta_{\mathrm{r}}}{\left[1 + (\alpha h)^n\right]^{1 - 1/n}},
$$
where $\theta$ is the volumetric water content, $h = \lvert\psi\rvert/(\rho g)$ the suction head, $\theta_{\mathrm{s}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{r}}$ the saturated and residual water contents, $1/\alpha$ roughly the suction at which the largest pores begin to empty, and $n$ a measure of the width of the pore-size distribution (large $n$: uniform pores, as in sand). The movement of water from soil to roots and through the plant to the air is the subject of the **soil–plant–atmosphere continuum** ([7.13.3](/7.13.3#sec-3.5)); a plant transpiring fast can wilt in moist soil because the soil around its roots dries and its hydraulic conductivity falls steeply.

### 5.4.3. Soil Air and Soil Temperature

Soil air occupies the pores not filled with water. Roots and microbes consume its oxygen and release CO₂, and gas exchange with the atmosphere is by diffusion through the connected air-filled pores, so that soil air contains less O₂ and 10 to more than 100 times more CO₂ than the atmosphere. In waterlogged soils, where gases must diffuse through water, oxygen falls to zero within centimeters, roots of most plants stop absorbing nutrients and die, and anaerobic microbes reduce nitrate, manganese, iron and sulfate, producing toxic Fe²⁺, H₂S and, in rice paddies and wetlands, methane. Good aeration speeds decomposition, but excessive aeration (in frequently tilled soils) burns organic matter faster than humus forms.

Soil temperature fluctuates most at the surface and is damped and delayed with depth: the daily cycle disappears below about 0.5–1 m and the annual cycle below about 10–20 m, and the annual extremes are reached later the deeper one goes ([7.19.3](/7.19.3)). Soil animals exploit this gradient by vertical migration, moving deeper in autumn and in the heat of summer days and returning to the surface in spring.

### 5.4.4. Soil Chemistry: Acidity, Organic Matter and Mineral Elements

Soil acidity has two components: the active acidity of the soil solution (its pH) and the reserve or potential acidity held on exchange sites (exchangeable H⁺ and Al³⁺), which determines how strongly the soil buffers a change in pH. Acid soils (pH < 5.5) form where rainfall leaches base cations, under coniferous litter and with acid deposition; alkaline and calcareous soils (pH > 7.5) form on limestone and in arid regions where salts accumulate. The pH acts mostly through the availability of nutrients and toxins:

- Below about pH 5, aluminum and manganese become soluble and toxic to roots, and P, Ca, Mg, K and Mo are deficient.
- Above about pH 7.5, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu and B, and phosphate (as calcium phosphates), are fixed in insoluble forms; calcifuge plants (those of acid soils, such as rhododendrons and tea) transplanted to calcareous soil show iron-deficiency chlorosis.
- Most nutrients are most available between pH 6 and 7, and chalk and limestone grasslands carry a richer flora than acid grasslands; the snail fauna of limestone areas is much richer than that of granite areas, because snails need calcium for their shells.

Plants confined to one type of soil serve as indicators: calcicoles grow on calcareous soils, calcifuges on acid soils (in China tea, Chinese red pine *Pinus massoniana* and many *Rhododendron* species indicate acid soil), and halophytes saline soils.

Soil organic matter consists of fresh and partly decomposed litter and of humus.

*Humus* is the dark, amorphous, relatively stable organic material formed by microbial decomposition and resynthesis of plant and animal remains in soil; it consists of humic substances (traditionally divided into humic acid, fulvic acid and humin) and decomposes slowly, with turnover times of decades to millennia. Humus binds aggregates, holds water and cations and slowly releases nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur. It is the largest store of organic carbon on land, larger than vegetation and the atmosphere combined ([7.19.45](/7.19.45)). Mineral elements are mostly bound in minerals or adsorbed on clay and humus colloids, and only a small fraction is in solution at any moment; they are discussed as resources in [7.19.6](/7.19.6#sec-6.2).

### 5.4.5. Soil Organisms

A gram of fertile soil contains $10^8$–$10^9$ bacteria, several meters of fungal hyphae, thousands of protists and nematodes, and a square meter holds tens of thousands of mites and springtails and up to hundreds of earthworms. Soil animals fragment litter, mix organic matter into the mineral soil and create pores; Darwin (1881) estimated that earthworms bring up several kilograms of soil per square meter per year in their casts. Their body forms reflect the resistance of the soil in which they move:

| Soil | Mode of movement | Adaptations | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loose, with large pores | Moving through existing spaces | Small, slender or flattened body, tough cuticle | Mites, springtails, nematodes, enchytraeids |
| Soft, moist | Peristaltic pushing | Hydrostatic skeleton, segmental muscles that alternately elongate and shorten the body | Earthworms |
| Firm | Scraping and shoveling | Strong claws on broad forelimbs | Moles, mole crickets |
| Hard | Chiseling with teeth or head | Procumbent incisors that lie outside the closed lips, or a wedge-shaped head | Mole rats, zokors, burrowing caecilians |

### 5.4.6. Plants of Special Soils

**Halophytes** grow on saline soils and in seawater by accumulating salt in vacuoles, excreting it through salt glands, or excluding it at the root ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.4)). Salt cedars (*Tamarix*) of the saline deserts and coasts of northern China secrete salt through glands on their leaves, which are often covered with white crystals; seepweeds (*Suaeda*) and glassworts (*Salicornia*) accumulate it in succulent leaves and stems; reeds (*Phragmites*) exclude it. Salinization of irrigated land in arid regions, where evaporation draws salty water to the surface, has destroyed much farmland since antiquity.

*Psammophytes* (sand plants) are plants adapted to mobile sand, which buries them, exposes their roots by wind erosion, dries rapidly and heats strongly. Their adaptations include rapid elongation of buried stems and the formation of **adventitious roots** on them (marram grass *Ammophila*, sand-binding *Calligonum* and *Artemisia* shrubs of Chinese deserts), very fast root growth after germination, root sheaths of sand grains cemented by mucilage and root hairs that protect the roots of desert grasses against abrasion and desiccation, and seeds or fruits that disperse with the wind and germinate only after enough rain. Psammophytes are used to stabilize dunes, which is the basis of the sand-control belts of northern China.

## 5.5. Wind

Wind is the movement of air driven by pressure differences, which arise from the uneven heating of the Earth's surface ([7.19.3](/7.19.3)). It acts on organisms in several ways:

- Through water balance and heat exchange: wind thins the boundary layer around leaves and bodies, increasing both transpiration and convective heat loss. Hot, dry winds (the "dry hot wind" of the North China Plain in late May and June) can kill the flowers and grains of wheat within days.
- Mechanically: trees on windy coasts and mountain ridges have flag-shaped crowns, with branches only on the leeward side, because buds on the windward side are killed by abrasion and desiccation; at the tree line trees grow as low, dense krummholz. Wind-exposed plants are short, with short internodes, thick stems and strong roots, a response to bending that can be induced experimentally by shaking plants (thigmomorphogenesis). Animals of windy habitats tend to have dense, short and close-lying fur or feathers.
- As a means of transport: wind carries the pollen of anemophilous plants (**anemophily**: grasses, conifers, many temperate trees), the seeds and spores of many plants, fungi and mosses, spiderlings ballooning on silk threads, and aphids and other small insects over hundreds of kilometers; migrating birds and insects use favorable winds ([7.18.20](/7.18.20)). Wind also limits the distribution of flying animals, and on oceanic islands many insects are flightless, perhaps because fliers are blown out to sea.
- Through erosion: when vegetation is removed, wind strips the topsoil. The great dust storm of 11–12 May 1934, during the "Dust Bowl" years of the North American Great Plains, carried an estimated 300 million tonnes of topsoil from the plowed plains as far as the Atlantic coast; the disaster led to soil conservation programs and to the planting of shelterbelts.

*Shelterbelts* (windbreaks) are rows of trees and shrubs planted across the prevailing wind to reduce wind speed, evaporation and erosion over the fields behind them. Their effect depends on their porosity, height $H$ and length. A dense belt stops the wind almost completely just behind it but creates strong turbulence and a short sheltered zone; a porous belt, with an optical porosity of about 20–40%, lets part of the air through, which suppresses the eddies and gives the longest effective shelter, wind speed being reduced by at least 20% to a distance of about 15–25 $H$ downwind; a ventilated belt (dense crowns over bare trunks) shelters well at a distance but accelerates the wind near the ground just behind the belt. Shelterbelts are the basis of the Three-North Shelter Forest Program in northern China.

Wind is one of the forces behind extreme events. Begon and Townsend (2021) distinguish hazards, which recur so often that organisms are adapted to withstand them (the daily force of waves on limpets, the storms that most trees survive); disasters, which recur at intervals long enough to be devastating but short enough to have shaped the evolution of the species they strike (fires in seasonally dry vegetation, Section 5.6); and catastrophes, so rare that no genetic memory of them remains, such as the eruption of Krakatau in 1883 or the Tōhoku tsunami of 2011. Intact ecosystems moderate disasters: coral reefs absorbed part of the energy of the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami along the coast of Sri Lanka, where illegal coral mining had increased the damage, and mangrove forests reduced both deaths and the intrusion of salt water into rice fields, an example of a regulating **ecosystem service** ([7.19.50](/7.19.50)).

## 5.6. Fire

### 5.6.1. Fire Regimes

Fire is a natural ecological factor in all vegetation that has a season dry enough for fuel to burn and enough production to accumulate fuel: savannas, grasslands, Mediterranean-type shrublands, and boreal and many temperate forests. Two main types of fire are distinguished by where they burn. Surface fires (ground fires) burn the litter, grass and shrubs beneath the canopy; they are of low to moderate intensity, kill seedlings and thin-barked species and spare large trees of fire-resistant species. Crown fires spread through the canopy, are of high intensity and kill most of the trees over large areas. Smoldering fires in peat and deep organic soils (sometimes also called ground fires) burn below the surface for months.

*Fire regime* is the characteristic pattern of fire in an area, described by the frequency (or mean interval) of fires, their intensity and severity, their season, their size and their type. The regime, not any single fire, is what organisms are adapted to: a pine of the frequently burned savannas of the southeastern United States and a pine of the boreal forest, where crown fires recur every century or two, have opposite adaptations.

The simplest model of a fire regime assumes that each patch of a landscape burns with a constant probability $\lambda = 1/F$ per year, independently of its age, where $F$ is the fire cycle (the time needed to burn an area equal to the whole landscape).

**Proposition 5.6.1 (Stand-Age Distribution)** With a constant fire hazard $\lambda = 1/F$, the fraction of the landscape that has not burned for at least $a$ years is $\mathrm{e}^{-a/F}$; time since fire is exponentially distributed with mean $F$.

**Proof** A patch reaches age $a$ without burning if it escapes fire in each of the $a$ years, with probability $(1 - \lambda)^a$, which for small $\lambda$ is $\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda a} = \mathrm{e}^{-a/F}$ (in continuous time, the survival function of a **Poisson process** of fires). At stationarity the landscape is a mixture of patches of all ages in these proportions; the mean of the exponential distribution is $1/\lambda = F$. $\square$

The exponential age distribution (Van Wagner 1978) is a good first description of boreal forests, and departures from it are informative: if the hazard increases with stand age, as fuel accumulates, old stands are rarer than the exponential predicts (a Weibull distribution fits better), and fire suppression shows up as a deficit of young stands.

### 5.6.2. Fire Adaptations

The ecological effects of fire are both destructive and creative:

- Fire mineralizes in hours the nutrients that decomposition would release over years, and the ash fertilizes the soil; but much nitrogen and sulfur is volatilized and lost, and on slopes the bare soil erodes and its water-holding capacity falls.
- Fire removes competitors and litter, opening space and light for seedlings, and it favors fire-tolerant species over fire-sensitive ones; the longleaf pine (*Pinus palustris*), whose seedlings pass several years in a fire-resistant "grass stage" with the bud protected by dense needles, dominated the southeastern United States as long as surface fires recurred every few years.
- Fire kills animals and reduces their populations for a time, but many animals escape underground or fly away, and some species depend on burned habitats (the black-backed woodpecker *Picoides arcticus* in recently burned boreal forest).

Plants have evolved several responses to recurrent fire:

| Response | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Resistance | Thick insulating bark, buds protected deep in the stem, self-pruning of lower branches | Longleaf and ponderosa pines, cork oak, giant sequoia |
| Resprouting | Buds in lignotubers, rhizomes or under thick bark produce new shoots after the shoot is killed | Many *Eucalyptus*, chaparral shrubs, savanna grasses |
| Fire-stimulated seed release | Serotiny (below) | Jack pine, lodgepole pine, *Banksia* |
| Fire-stimulated germination | Heat cracks hard seed coats; chemicals in smoke (karrikins) break dormancy | Legumes of fire-prone shrublands; chaparral and fynbos annuals |
| Fire-stimulated flowering | Flowering only in the season after a fire | Grass trees (*Xanthorrhoea*), many geophytes of the fynbos |

*Serotiny* is the retention of mature seeds in closed cones or fruits on the plant for years, with release triggered by an environmental cue, most commonly the heat of fire, which melts the resin that seals the cone. Serotinous species such as jack pine (*Pinus banksiana*) and lodgepole pine (*P. contorta*) store a canopy seed bank and release it onto the ash bed after a crown fire, when competition is minimal; after the Yellowstone fires of 1988 lodgepole pine seedlings reached densities of tens of thousands per hectare in burned stands.

Plants that are killed by fire and regenerate only from seed (obligate seeders, which include many serotinous species) are vulnerable to both too frequent and too rare fire. A fire that comes before the new generation has matured kills the plants before they have produced seed (immaturity risk); a fire that comes after the plants and their seed bank have died out comes too late. With a constant fire hazard we can quantify this window.

**Proposition 5.6.2 (Fire-Interval Window)** Let an obligate seeder start to produce seed at age $J$ and lose its seed bank (through senescence of the adults or decay of the stored seeds) at age $L > J$, and let fires occur with constant hazard $1/F$. The probability that the next fire falls within the window $(J, L)$, so that the population regenerates, is

$$
p(F) = \mathrm{e}^{-J/F} - \mathrm{e}^{-L/F},
$$
which tends to 0 both as $F \to 0$ and as $F \to \infty$ and is maximal at

$$
F^* = \frac{L - J}{\ln(L/J)}.
$$

**Proof** The interval to the next fire is exponential with mean $F$, so $\mathbb{P}(J < \text{interval} < L) = \mathrm{e}^{-J/F} - \mathrm{e}^{-L/F}$. As $F \to 0$ both terms tend to 0; as $F \to \infty$ both tend to 1, and their difference to 0. Differentiating, $p'(F) = F^{-2}\left(J\mathrm{e}^{-J/F} - L\mathrm{e}^{-L/F}\right)$, which vanishes when $\mathrm{e}^{(L - J)/F} = L/J$, that is at $F = F^*$; $p' > 0$ for $F < F^*$ and $p' < 0$ for $F > F^*$, so $F^*$ is the maximum. $\square$

For a short-lived shrub ($J = 5$, $L = 50$ years) the optimal mean interval is about 20 years and the population survives a fire cycle with probability 0.70; for a serotinous pine ($J = 15$, $L = 250$ years) the optimum is about 84 years (figure below). Even at the optimum the probability is well below 1, because exponential intervals are highly variable; real fire regimes are more regular than exponential, which widens the window. The model explains why an increase in fire frequency, for example through human ignitions or the spread of flammable invasive grasses, can eliminate obligate seeders from shrublands within a few decades, and why fire suppression lets serotinous pines be replaced by shade-tolerant species.

### 5.6.3. Fire Management

The suppression of all fires, practiced in North America for most of the twentieth century, allowed fuel to accumulate and shade-tolerant trees to invade fire-adapted forests, so that the fires that eventually escaped control were larger and more intense crown fires. Modern fire management treats fire as an ecological process to be managed rather than eliminated:

- *Prescribed burning*: the deliberate setting of low-intensity fires under chosen weather conditions to reduce fuel loads, maintain fire-dependent communities (prairies, longleaf pine savannas) and lower the risk of uncontrollable fires.
- Fuel management: thinning, removal of ladder fuels that carry surface fires into the crowns, and firebreaks.
- Biological firebreaks: belts of fire-resistant tree species, whose dense evergreen canopies keep the ground moist and shaded and whose leaves burn poorly; in southern China the schima tree (*Schima superba*, with thick, water-rich leaves) is the standard species of green firebreaks.
- Prevention, detection and suppression where human lives and property are at risk, especially at the expanding interface between settlements and wildland.

### 5.6.4. Snow Cover

At high latitudes and altitudes a stable winter snow cover changes the environment beneath it. Snow is an excellent insulator: under 50 cm of snow the soil surface stays near 0 °C while the air above may fall below −30 °C. Small mammals (voles, lemmings, shrews) live and even breed in the subnivean space between the snow and the ground, where they find shelter from cold and from predators and stored food; their winter survival is higher in years of deep, early snow, and the collapse of lemming cycles in parts of Norway has been attributed to warmer winters with rain-on-snow events that destroy this space. Snow protects overwintering plants from frost and desiccation and, in arid regions, is a reservoir of water released in spring. But deep or crusted snow hinders the movement of ungulates, increases their energy costs, covers their food and makes them vulnerable to wolves, whose lighter foot loading lets them run on crusted snow; snow depth sets the northern limit of several deer species.

## Summary

- Water is the internal medium, solvent and, for aquatic organisms, external medium of life; its heat capacity, latent heat, density maximum at 4 °C and surface tension explain its ecological roles.
- Water budgets (Formula 5.1.1): gains from drinking, food, metabolic water and the air balance evaporation and excretion. The kangaroo rat lives without drinking (Example 5.1.1); nasal countercurrent cooling can make respiration a net source of water. Camel and saguaro converge on the same budget: take up water fast, store it, reduce heat gain, tolerate heat storage rather than evaporate.
- Land plants are xerophytes, mesophytes and hygrophytes; strategies (short life, seasonal leaves, conservative leaves, C4 and CAM) and tactics (stomata, wilting) resolve the trade-off between photosynthesis and water loss; roots forage for water. Gloger's rule links pigmentation to humidity; drought alters foraging (Oriental storks at Poyang Lake).
- In water, oxygen solubility (Henry's law) falls with temperature, salinity and altitude; air-saturated fresh water holds 14.6 mg/L at 0 °C and 9.1 mg/L at 20 °C, thirty times less than air. The diel oxygen cycle $\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = P(t) - R + k(C_{\mathrm{s}} - C)$ explains dawn hypoxia and fish kills in productive water.
- Salinity divides aquatic animals into osmoconformers and osmoregulators, euryhaline and stenohaline species; pressure is countered by piezolytes (TMAO, limiting fish to about 8200 m); acidity acts through osmoregulation and metal toxicity.
- The partial pressure of oxygen falls with altitude (53% at 5000 m); lowlanders acclimatize, high-altitude species have evolved high-affinity hemoglobins and other genetic adaptations.
- Soil is a three-phase system; texture and structure (crumb structure, humus) determine water and air. Water is held by capillarity ($\lvert\psi\rvert = 2\gamma/r$); available water lies between field capacity (−33 kPa) and the permanent wilting point (−1.5 MPa) and is largest in loams. Soil pH controls the availability of nutrients and the toxicity of aluminum; humus is the largest store of organic carbon on land. Soil animals are shaped by the resistance of the soil; halophytes and psammophytes are adapted to salt and mobile sand.
- Wind affects water and heat exchange, form (flag trees, krummholz), dispersal and erosion; porous shelterbelts give the longest shelter. Extreme events are hazards, disasters or catastrophes according to how often they recur relative to generation time.
- Fire regimes: surface and crown fires; with a constant hazard the stand-age distribution is exponential (Proposition 5.6.1). Plants resist, resprout, release seed (serotiny) or germinate after fire; obligate seeders persist only within a window of fire intervals, optimal at $F^* = (L - J)/\ln(L/J)$ (Proposition 5.6.2). Fire management uses prescribed burning, fuel control and green firebreaks. Snow insulates the subnivean world but hinders ungulates.

## Questions

1. Why does a kangaroo rat gain more water per litre of oxygen from starch than from fat, although fat yields almost twice as much water per gram? Which diet would you expect a desert granivore to prefer in very dry air?

   **Answer** Per gram, fat yields 1.07 g of water against 0.56 g for starch, but fat needs about 2.0 L of oxygen per gram against 0.83 L for starch, so per litre of oxygen starch yields 0.67 g and fat 0.53 g. Since respiratory water loss is proportional to the volume of air breathed, and therefore to the oxygen consumed, the relevant measure for the water budget is water per litre of oxygen; carbohydrate-rich seeds are the better food in dry air. Kangaroo rats indeed prefer starchy seeds, and they avoid protein-rich seeds when water is scarce, because protein yields little metabolic water and its nitrogen must be excreted as urea, which costs urinary water.

2. Using the pond model $\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = P(t) - R + k(C_{\mathrm{s}} - C)$, explain why a calm, overcast day in midsummer is particularly dangerous for the fish of a eutrophic pond.

   **Answer** On an overcast day $P(t)$ is small, so the oxygen stored by evening is low; the warm water has a low saturation concentration $C_{\mathrm{s}}$; and on a calm day the re-aeration coefficient $k$ is small, so diffusion from the air cannot make up for respiration. Respiration $R$ is high in a eutrophic pond, especially at high temperature (bacteria decomposing abundant organic matter, dense algae respiring at night). During the night $\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t \approx -R + k(C_{\mathrm{s}} - C)$, and with small $k$ the concentration falls almost linearly at rate $R$; it reaches its minimum just before dawn. Aerators in fishponds raise $k$ for this reason.

3. By the Young–Laplace equation, what pore diameter corresponds to the permanent wilting point (−1.5 MPa)? How can desert shrubs extract water below this potential, and why does this matter more in loams and clays than in sand?

   **Answer** $d = 4\gamma/\lvert\psi\rvert = 4 \times 0.072/(1.5\times10^6) \approx 0.19\,\mu\mathrm{m}$: at the wilting point only pores narrower than about 0.2 μm still hold water (at field capacity, pores up to about 9 μm). Desert shrubs lower their leaf water potential to −4 to −8 MPa by accumulating solutes and having cavitation-resistant xylem, and so draw water from still narrower pores. Sand has few fine pores and holds almost no water below the wilting point, so a lower extraction limit gains little; loams and especially clays hold much water in fine pores (panel B of the soil-water figure: about 0.27 of the clay's volume lies below −1.5 MPa), so the ability to extract at lower potentials greatly increases the water available to the plant.

4. Prove that in Proposition 5.6.2 the optimal mean fire interval $F^*$ lies between $J$ and $L$, and discuss how the result changes if fire intervals are regular rather than exponential.

   **Answer** $F^* = (L - J)/(\ln L - \ln J)$ is the logarithmic mean of $J$ and $L$. By the mean value theorem $\ln L - \ln J = (L - J)/\xi$ for some $\xi \in (J, L)$, so $F^* = \xi \in (J, L)$; indeed the logarithmic mean lies between the geometric and the arithmetic mean. If intervals were perfectly regular with value $F$, the population would survive with probability 1 for every $F \in (J, L)$ and 0 otherwise; real regimes lie between this and the exponential case, and the more regular the intervals (for example, because fuel needs time to accumulate after a fire), the wider and higher the window. The exponential model is thus the most pessimistic case for obligate seeders.

5. (Open question) In many Chinese cities snow is removed from roads with salt. Using the concepts of this chapter, list the possible effects on roadside trees, soil and streams, and suggest how they could be measured.

   **Answer** Points to consider: sodium chloride raises the osmotic potential of the soil solution, causing physiological drought in roadside trees (the same problem as in saline soils), and Na⁺ and Cl⁻ accumulate in leaves to toxic levels (marginal leaf scorch, especially in conifers sprayed by passing traffic). Na⁺ displaces Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ from the exchange sites, dispersing clay and destroying soil structure (lower infiltration and aeration). Salt runoff raises the conductivity and chloride concentration of streams and ponds, affecting stenohaline freshwater invertebrates and amphibians; dense saline water can stratify small ponds and cause bottom-water hypoxia. Measurements: electrical conductivity and Na⁺/Cl⁻ of soil along transects at increasing distance from the road; leaf chloride and damage scores of trees; stream conductivity before and after snowfalls compared with upstream reference sites; survival of tadpoles in mesocosms with a range of chloride concentrations.
