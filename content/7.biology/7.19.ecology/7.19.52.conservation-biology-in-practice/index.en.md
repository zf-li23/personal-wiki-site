The two preceding chapters asked what biodiversity is worth ([7.19.50](/7.19.50)) and what threatens it ([7.19.51](/7.19.51)). This chapter treats what is done about it. Conservation biology ([7.19.50](/7.19.50)) acts at several levels at once: international agreements set goals and obligations (Section 52.1), protected areas safeguard places, chosen by methods of systematic planning that are, at heart, problems of combinatorial optimization (Section 52.2), species are managed directly, by captive breeding, reintroduction and genetic rescue, or indirectly through umbrella and flagship species (Section 52.3), and degraded ecosystems are restored (Section 52.4). Section 52.5 turns to the people on whom all of these depend. Several of the questions this raises are contested, and the chapter treats them, at the end, as open questions with a framework for analysis rather than a verdict. The population-level tools of conservation, population viability analysis and the minimum viable population, were developed in [7.19.15](/7.19.15), reserve design and corridors in [7.19.34](/7.19.34) and [7.19.49](/7.19.49), and harvesting in [7.19.53](/7.19.53).

## 52.1. International Frameworks

### 52.1.1. The Convention on Biological Diversity

The Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) is the main international treaty on biodiversity. Its history began in 1987, when the Governing Council of the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) asked its Executive Director to convene an ad hoc group of experts to consider whether an umbrella convention on biological diversity was needed. In 1989 the Council authorized negotiations on an international legal instrument; in 1991 the working group became the Intergovernmental Negotiating Committee for a Convention on Biological Diversity; and on 22 May 1992 the agreed text was adopted at a conference in Nairobi (the Nairobi Final Act). The Convention was opened for signature on 5 June 1992 at the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development (the Earth Summit) in Rio de Janeiro, and entered into force on 29 December 1993. It has 196 Parties; the United States has signed but not ratified it.

The Convention consists of a preamble, 42 articles and two annexes. Its three objectives (Article 1) are the conservation of biological diversity, the sustainable use of its components, and the fair and equitable sharing of the benefits arising from the use of genetic resources, including by appropriate access to genetic resources, transfer of technologies and funding. Its principle (Article 3) is that states have the sovereign right to exploit their own resources pursuant to their own environmental policies, and the responsibility to ensure that activities within their jurisdiction do not damage the environment of other states or of areas beyond national jurisdiction. Two protocols implement parts of it: the Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety (adopted 2000, in force 2003), on the transboundary movement of living modified organisms, and the Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit-Sharing (adopted 2010, in force 2014). The Convention works through national biodiversity strategies and action plans, national reports and the decisions of the Conference of the Parties (COP), whose first meeting was held in Nassau, the Bahamas, from 28 November to 9 December 1994.

The record of global targets is sobering. The target of the parties to achieve a significant reduction in the rate of biodiversity loss by 2010 was not met. The Strategic Plan for Biodiversity 2011–2020, adopted at COP10 in Nagoya, set 20 Aichi Biodiversity Targets; the fifth Global Biodiversity Outlook (2020) found that none had been fully achieved and only six partially, although the target for protected areas (17 % of land, 10 % of the oceans) came close.

COP15 was held under China's presidency in two parts: the first in Kunming from 11 to 15 October 2021, which adopted the Kunming Declaration and established a Kunming Biodiversity Fund, and the second in Montreal, Canada, from 7 to 19 December 2022, with the theme "Ecological Civilization: Building a Shared Future for All Life on Earth". It adopted the *Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework* (KMGBF), with four long-term goals for 2050 (the integrity of ecosystems and the halting of human-induced extinctions; the sustainable use of biodiversity; fair sharing of benefits; adequate means of implementation) and 23 targets for 2030, among them:

- Target 2: at least 30 % of degraded ecosystems under effective restoration;
- Target 3 ("30×30"): at least 30 % of land and inland waters and 30 % of coastal and marine areas effectively conserved and managed through protected areas and other effective area-based conservation measures (OECMs), recognizing the rights of indigenous peoples and local communities;
- Target 6: rates of introduction and establishment of invasive alien species reduced by at least 50 %;
- Target 7: nutrient losses to the environment and the overall risk from pesticides and highly hazardous chemicals at least halved, and plastic pollution reduced;
- Target 18: subsidies harmful to biodiversity reduced by at least US\$500 billion a year;
- Target 19: at least US\$200 billion a year mobilized for biodiversity, including international flows of at least US\$20 billion a year by 2025 and US\$30 billion by 2030.

COP16 met in Cali, Colombia, from 21 October to 1 November 2024. It established the "Cali Fund", to which companies using digital sequence information on genetic resources are expected to contribute, and a permanent subsidiary body for indigenous peoples and local communities; it was suspended for lack of a quorum before decisions on finance were taken, and its resumed session in Rome (February 2025) agreed a strategy for resource mobilization and the monitoring framework for the KMGBF. Other treaties cover particular aspects: the Ramsar Convention on Wetlands (1971), the World Heritage Convention (1972), CITES on trade in endangered species (1973), the Convention on Migratory Species (1979), and the agreement on marine biodiversity of areas beyond national jurisdiction (2023). IPBES ([7.19.50](/7.19.50)) provides the scientific assessments.

### 52.1.2. Protected Areas

Protected areas are the oldest and most widely used tool of conservation. The IUCN defines a protected area as a clearly defined geographical space, recognized, dedicated and managed, through legal or other effective means, to achieve the long-term conservation of nature with associated ecosystem services and cultural values, and classifies them into categories from strict nature reserves (Ia) to protected areas with sustainable use of natural resources (VI). By 2024 protected areas and OECMs covered about 17.6 % of land and inland waters and 8.4 % of the oceans (Protected Planet Report 2024); reaching 30×30 requires nearly doubling the former and more than tripling the latter.

China began establishing nature reserves in 1956 (Dinghushan in Guangdong was the first) and had about 2750 of them by the late 2010s, covering about 15 % of its land, together with many forest parks, geoparks, wetland parks and scenic areas under different agencies, often overlapping. A reform since 2019 is consolidating these into a single system of protected areas in which national parks are the main body, nature reserves the foundation and natural parks a supplement. After a decade of pilots, the first five national parks were established in 2021: Sanjiangyuan (the headwaters of the Yangtze, Yellow and Lancang rivers on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau), Giant Panda, Northeast China Tiger and Leopard, Hainan Tropical Rainforest, and Wuyishan. A plan published in 2022 designated 49 candidate national parks with a total area of about 1.1 million km². In addition, ecological conservation redlines, areas where development is restricted because of their ecological functions or vulnerability, cover more than 30 % of China's land area.

Whether protected areas work is an empirical question. Globally, monitored populations of birds and mammals in protected areas have on average been maintained (Barnes et al. 2016), with better outcomes in countries with higher development and lower corruption, and for large charismatic species that receive management attention; but many protected areas are "paper parks", legally designated and poorly managed, and protection is biased toward remote land of low economic value rather than toward the places of greatest biodiversity. The effectiveness of a protected area is measured against a counterfactual: what would have happened without protection. Studies that match protected and unprotected sites with similar remoteness, slope and land value find that protection reduces deforestation, but less than naive comparisons suggest.

## 52.2. Systematic Conservation Planning

### 52.2.1. Principles

Resources for conservation are limited, so which places should be protected? Early reserves were chosen opportunistically, for scenery or because land was available. *Systematic conservation planning* (Margules and Pressey 2000) makes the choice explicit, with quantitative targets (for example, 30 % of the range of each species and 10 % of each ecosystem type) and methods that achieve them efficiently. Its principles are:

- *Comprehensiveness and representativeness*: the reserve system should include examples of all species, ecosystems and environmental gradients of the region, not only the most spectacular.
- *Adequacy*: each feature should be represented well enough to persist, which requires enough area, viable populations and connectivity ([7.19.15](/7.19.15), [7.19.49](/7.19.49)).
- *Efficiency*: targets should be met at the lowest cost (area, money, foregone opportunities).
- Complementarity: each new site should add what the existing sites lack.
- *Irreplaceability*: the importance of a site is the likelihood that it is needed to meet the targets; a site with the only population of a species is fully irreplaceable.
- *Vulnerability*: sites at risk of imminent loss deserve priority.

The power of complementarity was shown along the coast of Western Australia, where the coastline was divided into 100 km sections: all 1855 species of coastal fishes could be represented in 26 sections, and more than 95 % of them in just six (Fox and Beckley 2005). For the Cape Floristic Region of South Africa, a global hotspot with more than 9000 plant species ([7.19.31](/7.19.31#sec-31.6)), Cowling and colleagues (2003) used irreplaceability analysis to design a 20-year plan that met targets for plant species, ecosystem types and populations of large mammals, and concluded that some 40 000 km² beyond existing reserves needed some level of protection.

### 52.2.2. The Reserve Selection Problem

Let there be $m$ candidate sites (planning units) and $n$ species, with $a_{ij} = 1$ if species $i$ occurs in site $j$ and $0$ otherwise, and let site $j$ cost $c_j$. Choosing reserves is a problem of integer programming.

**Definition 52.2.1 (Minimum-Set and Maximal-Coverage Problems)** With decision variables $x_j \in \{0, 1\}$ ($x_j = 1$ if site $j$ is selected), the *minimum-set problem* is

$$
\min\sum_{j=1}^mc_jx_j \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{j=1}^ma_{ij}x_j \ge t_i \quad (i = 1, \dots, n),
\tag{52.1}
$$
with $t_i = 1$ (each species represented at least once) or a larger target; the *maximal-coverage problem* is to maximize the number of species represented, $\sum_iy_i$ with $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ and $y_i \le \sum_ja_{ij}x_j$, subject to a budget $\sum_jc_jx_j \le B$.

With $t_i = 1$ and equal costs, the minimum-set problem is the set cover problem of computer science, and the maximal-coverage problem is the budgeted maximum coverage problem; both are NP-hard, so no algorithm is known that solves every instance in time polynomial in its size. In practice, instances with thousands of sites are solved exactly by integer linear programming solvers (branch and bound with linear-programming relaxations), and larger or more complex ones approximately. The simplest approximation is the *greedy algorithm*: start with no sites and repeatedly add the site that represents the most species not yet represented (per unit cost). It is the algorithmic form of complementarity, and its performance is guaranteed.

**Theorem 52.2.1 (Greedy Maximal Coverage)** For the maximal-coverage problem with equal costs and a budget of $k$ sites, the greedy algorithm represents at least $1 - (1 - 1/k)^k > 1 - 1/\mathrm{e} \approx 0.632$ times as many species as an optimal choice of $k$ sites.

**Proof** Let $W^*$ be the number of species represented by an optimal set $O$ of $k$ sites, and $c_s$ the number represented by the greedy algorithm after $s$ steps. At least $W^* - c_s$ species covered by $O$ are not yet covered after step $s$, and the $k$ sites of $O$ together cover them, so at least one site of $O$ covers at least $(W^* - c_s)/k$ of them. The greedy choice at step $s + 1$ adds at least as many, so $c_{s+1} - c_s \ge (W^* - c_s)/k$, that is, $W^* - c_{s+1} \le (1 - 1/k)(W^* - c_s)$. By induction from $c_0 = 0$, $W^* - c_k \le (1 - 1/k)^kW^* < W^*/\mathrm{e}$, since $(1 - 1/k)^k < \mathrm{e}^{-1}$. $\square$

The argument uses only one property of the number of species represented as a function of the set of sites: adding a site to a larger set never adds more new species than adding it to a smaller subset. Such *submodular* functions arise widely, and the same bound holds for maximizing any of them under a cardinality constraint (Nemhauser, Wolsey and Fisher 1978). For the minimum-set problem, the greedy algorithm uses at most $H(d) = 1 + 1/2 + \cdots + 1/d \le 1 + \ln d$ times the optimal number of sites, where $d$ is the largest number of species in one site (Johnson 1974; Chvátal 1979). In ecological data the greedy solution is usually within a few percent of optimal (Figure 1), but not always, and it gives no information about alternatives. Ranking sites by richness ("hotspots") is much worse: the richest sites tend to share the same widespread species, and adding them represents few new species.

Real planning adds costs that vary among sites (land prices, opportunity costs for agriculture), shapes (compact reserves have less edge, [7.19.49](/7.19.49)), connectivity, existing reserves, and the persistence of species, not just their presence. The widely used program Marxan minimizes the total cost of a reserve system plus a penalty proportional to the length of its boundary (which favors compact, clustered reserves) plus penalties for targets not met, using simulated annealing; reporting how often each site appears in many near-optimal solutions gives a measure of irreplaceability. Zonation ranks the whole landscape by iteratively removing the cells whose loss costs least. Including costs often changes priorities greatly: protecting cheap land in the right places can achieve the same targets at a fraction of the cost of protecting the richest land (Naidoo et al. 2006).

## 52.3. Species-Focused Conservation

### 52.3.1. In Situ and Ex Situ Conservation

*In situ* conservation protects species in their natural habitats, together with the interactions and evolutionary processes that sustain them; it is the foundation of conservation. *Ex situ* conservation maintains populations outside their natural habitats: in zoos, aquaria, breeding centers and botanical gardens, and as seeds, tissues and frozen gametes in genebanks. The Svalbard Global Seed Vault (2008) stores duplicates of crop collections from around the world; the Millennium Seed Bank at Kew and the Germplasm Bank of Wild Species at the Kunming Institute of Botany (2007) store seeds of tens of thousands of wild plant species. Ex situ populations are insurance against extinction in the wild and a source for reintroduction. They have saved several species: Père David's deer and Przewalski's horse ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)), the California condor (22 birds in 1982, all taken into captivity by 1987; more than 500 today, over half in the wild), the black-footed ferret and the Arabian oryx. The crested ibis (*Nipponia nippon*) was reduced to seven wild birds found in Yangxian, Shaanxi, in 1981; with protection of the remaining birds, captive breeding and reintroduction, it now numbers more than ten thousand. The giant panda's captive population, more than 700 animals, has become self-sustaining.

Ex situ populations have problems of their own. They are small, so they lose genetic diversity unless they are managed by studbooks that minimize kinship; they adapt to captivity, losing behavior and traits needed in the wild, so that the fitness of captive-bred animals in the wild declines with the number of generations in captivity; and they are expensive. Reintroductions, the release of individuals into an area from which the species has disappeared, often fail: in a review of 180 reintroduction projects, only about a quarter of those with known outcomes were successful (Fischer and Lindenmayer 2000). Success is higher when the causes of the original decline have been removed, when many individuals are released (propagule pressure, [7.19.51](/7.19.51)), when wild-caught rather than captive-bred animals are used, and into the core of the historical range. The IUCN guidelines (2013) distinguish reinforcement of an existing population, *reintroduction* within the indigenous range, and *conservation introductions* outside it, including *assisted colonization* of areas that climate change will make suitable.

### 52.3.2. Genetic Rescue

A small, isolated population loses genetic variation by drift, at the rate $1/(2N_{\mathrm{e}})$ per generation, and the resulting inbreeding reduces survival and reproduction (**inbreeding depression**, [7.10.34](/7.10.34)). If its numbers fall as a result, drift accelerates: a positive feedback that is one of the mechanisms of the **extinction vortex** ([7.19.15](/7.19.15)). The remedy is gene flow.

**Definition 52.3.1 (Genetic Rescue)** **Genetic rescue** is an increase in the fitness, and hence the growth rate, of a small population caused by the immigration of individuals carrying new alleles, beyond the increase expected from the added numbers alone (demographic rescue).

The Florida panther, an isolated population of the puma (*Puma concolor*) in southern Florida, numbered 20–30 adults in the early 1990s. It had low genetic diversity and a suite of traits attributed to inbreeding: kinked tails, cowlicks, undescended testes, heart defects and poor sperm quality. In 1995 eight female pumas from Texas, the nearest population of the subspecies' historical range, were released; five produced litters. Over the following decade the population roughly tripled, heterozygosity doubled, the frequency of the defects fell, and admixed kittens survived better than pure Florida kittens (Johnson et al. 2010). The wolves of Isle Royale in Lake Superior, founded by a few wolves that crossed the ice around 1950, illustrate both the benefit and its limits. A single male immigrant arriving in 1997 brought a period of higher reproduction, and his genes spread to more than half of the population within a decade; but without further immigration, as ice bridges became rare in warmer winters, inbreeding rose again, the population collapsed to two closely related animals by 2016, and new wolves had to be translocated in 2018–2019.

A model makes the dynamics explicit (Figure 2). Let inbreeding depression reduce the intrinsic growth rate, $r(F) = r_0 - \delta F$, so that above $F = r_0/\delta$ the population declines; and let drift increase $F$ by $1/(2N_{\mathrm{e}})$ per generation. If $q$ is the proportion of immigrants among the breeders of a generation, and immigrants are outbred and unrelated to residents, only a fraction $(1 - q)^2$ of the random matings are between residents, so that the inbreeding coefficient of the next generation is reduced to

$$
F' = (1 - q)^2\left[\frac{1}{2N_{\mathrm{e}}} + \left(1 - \frac{1}{2N_{\mathrm{e}}}\right)F\right].
\tag{52.2}
$$
A few immigrants into a population of tens of individuals make $q$ large enough to lower $F$ substantially, and the growth rate becomes positive. But drift then raises $F$ again; in a population that remains small and isolated, a single rescue buys time rather than securing persistence. Recurrent gene flow, about one effective migrant per generation as the classical rule of thumb suggests, maintains low inbreeding, which is why the restoration of natural connectivity ([7.19.49](/7.19.49)) is the lasting solution.

Genetic rescue was long avoided for fear of *outbreeding depression*, the loss of fitness in hybrids between populations adapted to different environments or with chromosomal differences ([7.10.34](/7.10.34)). A meta-analysis found that crosses between populations improved fitness in about 93 % of cases, often greatly and for several generations (Frankham 2015), and the risk of outbreeding depression is low when the populations belong to the same species, have no fixed chromosomal differences, have exchanged genes within the last 500 years and live in similar environments (Frankham et al. 2011). Many small populations that would benefit from rescue have not received it.

### 52.3.3. Umbrella and Flagship Species

Because most species cannot be studied or managed individually, conservation often works through a few species that stand for many.

**Definition 52.3.2 (Umbrella and Flagship Species)** An **umbrella species** is a species whose conservation, usually because it requires large areas of habitat of a particular type, is expected to protect many other species that share its habitat. A *flagship species* is a charismatic species used to attract public support and funding for conservation.

The two roles are distinct from each other and from that of a **keystone species** ([7.19.33](/7.19.33)), whose effects on its community are large relative to its abundance. A flagship need not be a good umbrella, and an umbrella need not be charismatic. The giant panda is the archetypal flagship and is often claimed to be an umbrella. The evidence has three parts. First, overlap: the panda's range in the mountains of southwestern China covers the ranges of about 70 % of China's endemic forest birds, 70 % of its endemic forest mammals and 31 % of its endemic forest amphibians (Li and Pimm 2016), so protecting panda habitat could protect many endemics. Second, the panda's status has improved: the fourth national survey (2011–2014) counted 1864 wild pandas, an increase of about 17 % over the third, and in 2016 the IUCN moved the species from Endangered to Vulnerable; the reserves covered about two thirds of the wild population, and the Giant Panda National Park, established in 2021, joins many of them. Third, whether the protection actually benefits co-occurring species: here the evidence is mixed. Camera-trap surveys and historical records showed that large carnivores have disappeared from most panda reserves since the 1950s–1960s: leopards from about 81 %, wolves from 77 %, dholes from 95 % and snow leopards from 38 % of them (Li et al. 2020), because carnivores need more space, more prey and protection from poaching and conflict than the forest protection designed for pandas provides. Species of other habitats (alpine meadows, low-elevation broad-leaved forest, rivers) and other elevations are not covered by panda reserves at all.

Figure 3 shows the logic in a model landscape: reserves designed for an umbrella species protect species whose habitat requirements are similar, and miss the others; a design based on complementarity, with the same area, meets the targets for many more species. Umbrella species are therefore useful where their requirements encompass those of many others and where data on the others are lacking, but they are a substitute for, not an equivalent of, planning for many species. The flagship role is a separate and real contribution: the money and public attention the panda attracts protect large areas of forest whose total value, including services such as water supply to the Sichuan Basin, is many times its cost ([7.19.50](/7.19.50)).

## 52.4. Protecting and Restoring Ecosystems

### 52.4.1. Ecosystem Restoration

Where ecosystems have been degraded, protection alone is not enough. Ecological restoration, defined by the Society for Ecological Restoration as "the process of assisting the recovery of an ecosystem that has been degraded, damaged, or destroyed", aims to return an ecosystem toward a reference state: its species composition, structure, functions and connections with the surrounding landscape. Its principles, developed further in [7.19.54](/7.19.54), include identifying reference ecosystems while recognizing that the climate and the species pool may have changed, removing the causes of degradation first, relying on natural regeneration where possible and assisting it where thresholds or **alternative stable states** prevent recovery ([7.19.39](/7.19.39)), restoring at the landscape scale, involving local people, and monitoring outcomes against goals. The United Nations declared 2021–2030 the Decade on Ecosystem Restoration, and the KMGBF set the target of effective restoration of 30 % of degraded ecosystems.

China's large programs illustrate both the potential and the pitfalls. The Natural Forest Protection Program (1998) banned logging of natural forests in the upper Yangtze and Yellow river basins after the floods of 1998, and the Grain for Green Program (1999) paid farmers to convert sloping cropland to forest and grassland; together with afforestation, they made China the largest contributor to the greening of the Earth, accounting for about a quarter of the global increase in leaf area in 2000–2017 with less than 7 % of the vegetated area (Chen et al. 2019). But many plantations are monocultures of a few tree species, poorer in biodiversity than natural forest; on the Loess Plateau, revegetation has approached the limit of what rainfall can support, depleting soil water (Feng et al. 2016); and tree planting in natural grasslands and savannas destroys ecosystems rather than restoring them.

### 52.4.2. The Ten-Year Fishing Ban on the Yangtze

The Yangtze, the third longest river in the world, holds more than 400 species of fishes, about 170 of them endemic. Its fisheries and its large animals declined for decades under overfishing, damming (the Gezhouba Dam from 1981, the Three Gorges Dam from 2003, and many dams on tributaries), sand mining, shipping, pollution and the hardening of banks. The annual catch fell from about 430 000 t in the 1950s to less than 100 000 t in the 2010s; the Chinese paddlefish became extinct and the baiji functionally extinct ([7.19.51](/7.19.51)); natural reproduction of the Chinese sturgeon below the Gezhouba Dam has not been detected in most years since 2013; and the abundance of larvae of the four major Chinese carps fell by about an order of magnitude. In response, fishing was banned in the 332 aquatic protected areas of the basin from 1 January 2020, and from 1 January 2021 in the whole main stem, the major tributaries and the Poyang and Dongting lakes, for ten years. About 111 000 fishing boats were withdrawn and about 231 000 fishers resettled, with compensation, retraining and social insurance.

Early monitoring reported signs of recovery: more native fish species recorded in surveys of the basin, increased biomass indices, the return of migratory fishes such as the Yangtze estuarine tapertail anchovy (*Coilia nasus*) to the middle reaches and Dongting Lake, and an increase in the Yangtze finless porpoise (*Neophocaena asiaeorientalis asiaeorientalis*) from 1012 animals in the survey of 2017 to 1249 in 2022, the first increase since surveys began. How to evaluate these results is itself a scientific problem, set out as an open question below.

### 52.4.3. Managing Invasive Species

Prevention, early detection and eradication, and control of invasive species were discussed in [7.19.51](/7.19.51#sec-51.4.5). In the KMGBF, Target 6 requires a halving of the rates of introduction and establishment; on islands, the eradication of invasive mammals is one of the most cost-effective conservation actions available, and in China the national campaign against *Spartina alterniflora* on the coasts aims to remove it from most of its range and restore the mudflats and native salt marshes used by migratory shorebirds.

## 52.5. People and Conservation

Conservation succeeds or fails through people. Protected areas imposed on local communities without their consent have often failed, through conflict and poaching, and have imposed costs on some of the poorest people; conservation that offers benefits, rights and a voice tends to last longer. Lands managed by indigenous peoples cover about a quarter of the world's land and overlap about 40 % of its protected areas (Garnett et al. 2018), and their recognition is now part of the KMGBF. Payments for ecosystem services, such as China's ecological compensation transfers to upstream regions that protect water sources, align local incentives with services enjoyed elsewhere. Human–wildlife conflict, when elephants raid crops in Yunnan or carnivores kill livestock, can turn local people against conservation; compensation schemes, public liability insurance for wildlife damage (introduced in Yunnan in 2010), fencing, early-warning systems and land-use planning reduce it.

Citizen science, in which volunteers collect scientific data, has become a major source of information on biodiversity. Birdwatchers' records in eBird and similar databases, observations in iNaturalist, standardized butterfly and bird counts, and local surveys such as the winter counts of crows roosting on the Tsinghua University campus ([7.19.8](/7.19.8)) provide data on distributions and trends at scales no professional program could cover, and they engage the public in the science. Their limits are those of presence-only data ([7.19.49](/7.19.49#sec-49.5)): uneven effort, biases toward conspicuous species and accessible places, and variation among observers, which statistical models must account for.

Individuals contribute in many ways, most of which follow from the threats of [7.19.51](/7.19.51): consuming less, and less of what drives habitat conversion and overexploitation (for example, choosing certified sustainable seafood and timber, avoiding products of the illegal wildlife trade); not releasing pets or "mercy-releasing" animals into the wild; supporting and participating in conservation, monitoring and education; and, for students of ecology, contributing the knowledge on which all these depend. Questions about stray and feral animals, the feeding of wildlife, the hardening of river channels, microplastics, carbon offsets and renewable energy are treated in [7.19.54](/7.19.54).

## Questions

1. Prove that the number of species represented by a set of sites is a submodular function of the set, and explain why this property, rather than anything specific to biology, is what makes the greedy algorithm work.

   **Answer** Let $f(S)$ be the number of species occurring in at least one site of $S$. For $S \subseteq T$ and a site $j \notin T$, the gain $f(S \cup \{j\}) - f(S)$ is the number of species in $j$ not present in $S$; since every species present in $S$ is also present in $T$, the species in $j$ not present in $T$ are a subset of those not present in $S$, so $f(T \cup \{j\}) - f(T) \le f(S \cup \{j\}) - f(S)$: diminishing returns. In the proof of Theorem 52.2.1, the step "some site of $O$ adds at least $(W^* - c_s)/k$ new species" uses only that the joint gain of the $k$ optimal sites, added to the greedy set, is at most the sum of their individual gains, which follows from submodularity; together with monotonicity, this gives the $1 - 1/\mathrm{e}$ bound for any monotone submodular function, such as expected species represented under uncertain occurrence, or phylogenetic diversity of the selected species. Objectives that are not submodular, for example requiring each species to be represented in two connected sites, lose the guarantee.
2. A small, isolated population of 30 animals has $F = 0.3$, and its growth rate is negative. Using Eq. (52.2), compute the inbreeding coefficient of the next generation if 6 unrelated immigrants join the breeders ($N_{\mathrm{e}} = 0.4N$). What else would you need to know before recommending the translocation?

   **Answer** Breeders $36$, $q = 6/36 = 1/6$, $N_{\mathrm{e}} = 14.4$, $1/(2N_{\mathrm{e}}) \approx 0.035$. Without immigrants $F' = 0.035 + 0.965 \times 0.3 \approx 0.324$; with them $F' = (5/6)^2 \times 0.324 \approx 0.225$. Before recommending: the genetic and environmental similarity of the source population (risk of outbreeding depression: same species, no chromosomal differences, recent gene flow, similar environment); the health of the source and the disease risk of translocation; whether the source can spare the animals; whether the cause of the initial decline has been removed and the habitat can support a larger population; social and legal factors; and a plan for recurrent gene flow or restored connectivity, since one rescue only resets inbreeding temporarily. Monitoring of fitness and genetics after release is needed to confirm rescue.
3. (Open question) Is the giant panda an effective umbrella species? Outline how the question should be framed and what evidence bears on it.

   **Answer** Framework: distinguish the flagship role (attracting funds and attention) from the umbrella role (protecting co-occurring species), and the umbrella role into potential (overlap of ranges and habitat requirements) and realized (whether populations of other species actually fare better because of panda-focused protection). Evidence on potential: high overlap with endemic forest birds and mammals (about 70 %), lower with amphibians (31 %); no coverage of other habitats and elevations. Evidence on realized effects: forest protection and logging bans benefit many forest species; but large carnivores have disappeared from most panda reserves, showing that protection designed for pandas does not meet their needs (space, prey, anti-poaching). Methods to strengthen the evidence: compare trends of many species inside and outside panda reserves with matched controls; camera-trap and acoustic monitoring of multiple taxa; systematic planning analyses that compare panda-based and complementarity-based designs. Considerations on value: the panda's flagship role may pay for protection of large forest areas whose services exceed costs. A reasonable conclusion is conditional: an effective umbrella for some forest species, an inadequate one for others, best combined with multi-species planning.
4. (Open question) How effective has the ten-year fishing ban on the Yangtze been? Design an evaluation.

   **Answer** Define outcomes: abundance and size structure of target and non-target fishes, recruitment (larvae of the four major carps), status of flagship species (finless porpoise, Chinese sturgeon), species richness, and socioeconomic outcomes for former fishers. Evidence: monitoring data before and after the ban, ideally with a before–after–control–impact (BACI) design or comparisons with rivers or reaches without bans; standardized survey effort (catch per unit effort, eDNA, hydroacoustics), since changes in sampling can mimic recovery. Confounders: concurrent changes in dam operations and ecological flow releases, water quality improvements, climate and hydrology, restocking and releases of hatchery fish, and the time lags of fish life histories. Interpretation: early increases in short-lived species and porpoises are consistent with reduced mortality, whereas long-lived migratory species blocked by dams (sturgeons) cannot recover without restoration of spawning habitat and passage; a ban addresses only one of several threats. Social side: compliance and illegal fishing, livelihoods of resettled fishers, and the question of what management should follow the ten years (permanent closure, regulated fisheries, protected areas).
5. (Open question) Wild boar populations are reported to have increased across much of China, causing crop damage and conflict, and in 2023 the species was removed from the national list of protected terrestrial wildlife of ecological, scientific or social value. Should their populations be controlled by hunting? Outline an analysis.

   **Answer** Framework: (a) establish the facts: abundance and trends by region (camera-trap occupancy and density estimates, distance sampling, harvest and damage records), since many reports are anecdotal; (b) identify the drivers: loss of large predators (tigers, leopards, wolves), forest recovery under Grain for Green and natural forest protection, abandonment of farmland and rural depopulation, restrictions on hunting and firearms, and abundant crops as food; (c) assess costs and benefits: crop losses and injuries, vehicle collisions, disease risk (African swine fever, which also kills wild boar), and the ecological roles of boar (soil disturbance, seed dispersal, prey for carnivores). Evidence on control: wild boar have high reproductive rates and compensate for mortality (density-dependent recruitment, [7.19.11](/7.19.11)), so in Europe intensive recreational hunting has generally not stopped population growth; culling can be effective locally if sustained, targeted at reproductive females and coordinated over large areas, with harvest rates computable from population models ([7.19.53](/7.19.53)). Alternatives and complements: fencing and deterrents, compensation and insurance, changes in crop placement, and the recovery of predators. The decision also involves animal welfare, the risks of firearms, the capacity to regulate hunting, and public attitudes; an adaptive management design with monitoring would test whether control reduces damage.
6. (Open question) Should the trade in exotic pets be allowed? Identify the ecological issues and the evidence needed.

   **Answer** Ecological issues: (a) pathways of invasion: released or escaped pets are a major source of invasive species (red-eared sliders in China and worldwide, Burmese pythons in the Everglades, aquarium fishes, whose propagule pressure is high, [7.19.51](/7.19.51)); (b) disease: the pet trade has spread chytrid fungi (including *B. salamandrivorans*), ranavirus and zoonoses (monkeypox in the United States in 2003 via imported rodents, salmonellosis from reptiles); (c) overexploitation of wild populations: many reptiles, amphibians, birds and fishes are wild-caught, sometimes laundered as captive-bred, and rarity can raise prices (Proposition 51.5.1); (d) welfare of animals with specialized needs. Possible benefits: captive breeding that reduces wild collection, livelihoods, public interest in nature. Evidence needed: which species are traded and in what numbers (customs, online markets), the proportion wild-caught, the invasion and disease risk of each species (climate match, history of invasion), and the effectiveness of regulations. Policy options range from bans to "positive lists" of species permitted because they are low-risk and captive-bred, with traceability, health screening and prohibition of release; the choice depends on the balance of risks, enforcement capacity and values.
7. (Open question) Do zoos and aquaria still have a role in conservation? Discuss the arguments and the evidence.

   **Answer** Arguments for: ex situ populations have prevented extinctions and supplied reintroductions (Przewalski's horse, Père David's deer, California condor, Arabian oryx, crested ibis); zoos fund field conservation (members of the World Association of Zoos and Aquariums spend hundreds of millions of dollars a year on it), host research on reproduction, nutrition and disease, and reach hundreds of millions of visitors, with some evidence of improved knowledge of biodiversity after visits. Arguments against or for caution: most animals in zoos are not threatened species or are not in managed breeding programs; captive populations are small and adapt to captivity; welfare problems are documented for wide-ranging and social species (elephants, cetaceans, large carnivores), shown by stereotypic behavior; the educational effect of visits is modest and hard to measure; and resources might achieve more in situ. Framework for evaluating a particular institution: the proportion of its collection in coordinated conservation programs, its contribution to reintroduction and field projects, standards of welfare, research output and measured educational outcomes. A plausible conclusion is that the role depends on how a zoo is run: as a conservation and research center integrated with in situ programs ("one plan approach"), or as entertainment.
