An organism lives in a particular place at a particular time, surrounded by air or water of a certain temperature, light that varies through the day, soil or sediment of a certain chemistry, and other organisms that feed on it, compete with it or provide for it. Which of these factors matter, how they act, and why a species is found in some places and not in others are the first questions of ecology at the level of the individual. This chapter provides the general concepts used throughout the course: the environment and its components, the ecological factors and their classification, the laws of limitation and tolerance associated with Liebig, Blackman and Shelford, and the ways in which organisms adjust their tolerance by acclimatization and homeostasis.

The concepts here are general; the following chapters treat the individual factors in turn: radiation, light and climate (7.19.3), temperature (7.19.4), water and other physical factors (7.19.5), resources and their stoichiometry (7.19.6) and soil (7.19.7). The physiology behind tolerance (thermoregulation, osmoregulation, photosynthesis) is treated in plant physiology (7.13) and animal physiology (7.14); here the emphasis is on its ecological consequences, the limits it places on where species live.

## 2.1. Environment and Habitat

### 2.1.1. The Environment

**Definition 2.1.1 (Environment)** The **environment** of an organism or of a group of organisms (the subject) is the totality of everything around it, including the space it occupies and all factors that directly or indirectly influence its survival, growth, reproduction and distribution.

The environment is a relative concept: it is defined with respect to a subject. For a single carp in a pond, the environment includes the other carp, the other fish, shrimp, water fleas and water plants, and the water, sediment and air; for the carp population, the other carp are part of the subject, and the environment consists of the other species and the abiotic surroundings; for the pond community, the environment is only the abiotic part. Environments also range in size from the whole universe (the Sun, cosmic radiation) to the immediate surroundings of a cell.

### 2.1.2. Macroenvironment and Microenvironment

**Definition 2.1.2 (Macroclimate and Microclimate)** The macroenvironment is the environment of a region, of the Earth or of the cosmos; its climate, the **macroclimate**, is the climate above the layer near the ground (conventionally measured at 1.5–2 m) and is determined by large-scale factors such as the general circulation of the atmosphere, latitude, distance from the sea and large landforms. The microenvironment is the immediate surroundings of an organism, a small and specific site within the macroenvironment; its climate, the **microclimate**, is the climate of a small area near the ground, which varies strongly over short distances and is modified by local topography, vegetation, soil and water.

The macroclimate determines which kinds of vegetation can grow in a region, and thereby the major types of communities, the biomes (tropical rainforest, temperate grassland, tundra and so on, 7.19.41); conversely, the biome of a region indicates its climate. Organisms, however, experience the microclimate, which may differ strikingly from the macroclimate. On a clear night the air in a valley bottom may be 30 °C colder than on a slope 100 m higher, because cold, dense air drains downhill; the south side of a tree trunk in winter sunshine may reach 30 °C while the air is below freezing; and under a forest canopy the daily temperature range is smaller and the humidity higher than in an adjacent field. Organisms also choose microclimates: a female hummingbird incubating at dawn in a nest under an overhanging branch loses less heat by radiation to the cold night sky than one in an exposed nest (Calder 1973). Microclimates are treated quantitatively in 7.19.3.

### 2.1.3. Habitat

**Definition 2.1.3 (Habitat)** The **habitat** of an organism, a population or a species is the place where it lives, characterized by the set of environmental factors acting there.

The habitat of a gut bacterium is the alimentary canal of its host, that of a woodpecker the woodland, that of a pike the littoral zone of a lake. A habitat is a place; the **ecological niche** ([7.10.15](/7.10.15)) is not. The niche is the set of conditions and resources that allow a species to maintain a population, and a single habitat (a lake) contains many niches, occupied by species with quite different ways of life (Begon and Townsend 2021). The niche is developed fully in 7.19.23.

## 2.2. Ecological Factors

### 2.2.1. Definition

**Definition 2.2.1 (Ecological Factor)** An **ecological factor** is any element of the environment that acts on an organism, such as temperature, light, water, oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, food and other organisms.

All ecological factors together constitute the ecological environment of an organism, and the ecological environment at a particular site is its habitat. Factors that an organism cannot do without are sometimes called conditions of existence: light, carbon dioxide, water and mineral nutrients for green plants; food, oxygen and a suitable temperature for animals.

### 2.2.2. Classification

Ecological factors are classified in several complementary ways.

| Criterion | Classes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Climatic | Temperature, light, water, wind, air pressure, lightning |
| | Edaphic (soil) | Soil structure, texture, chemistry, soil organisms |
| | Topographic | Altitude, slope, aspect, mountain ranges, land and sea |
| | Biotic | Interactions with other organisms: food, predators, parasites, competitors, mutualists |
| | Anthropogenic | Pollution, land use, introduction of species, harvesting |
| Living or not | Biotic and abiotic | |
| Effect on population size | Density-dependent | Food, predators, parasites, disease: the intensity of their effect on per capita birth or death rates changes with population density |
| | Density-independent | Weather, catastrophes: the per capita effect does not depend on density |
| Stability | Stable | Gravity, geomagnetism, the solar constant |
| | Periodically variable | Seasons, day and night, tides |
| | Non-periodically variable | Wind, fire, outbreaks of predators |
| Consumption | Conditions | Temperature, pH, salinity, humidity: they affect organisms but are not consumed |
| | Resources | Light, water, nutrients, food, space: they are consumed and can be depleted by other organisms |

Two of these distinctions are central to the rest of the course.

The distinction between density-dependent and density-independent factors (Smith 1935) concerns population regulation. A factor whose per capita effect increases with density, such as starvation when food is shared among more individuals, can stop a population from growing and hold it near a level; a factor that kills the same fraction of individuals whatever their number, such as a severe frost, can reduce a population but cannot by itself regulate it. Biotic factors are usually density-dependent and climatic factors density-independent, but there are exceptions: a frost is density-dependent in its effect if the number of sheltered sites is limited, so that individuals in excess of the shelters die. The ten-year cycles of snowshoe hares and lynx in the Canadian boreal forest are the textbook example of density-dependent biotic interactions. The debate in the 1950s between Nicholson, who held that populations are regulated by density-dependent factors, and Andrewartha and Birch, who emphasized the role of weather, is discussed with its modern resolution in 7.19.13.

The distinction between conditions and resources (Begon and Townsend 2021) concerns the kind of niche dimension. A condition, such as temperature, is not used up: one organism's experience of a temperature does not reduce what remains for another, although organisms can modify conditions (a forest canopy lowers the temperature beneath it). A resource is consumed, so organisms compete for it. Water is both: as a resource it is taken up by roots, as a condition (humidity) it affects evaporation. Conditions are the subject of 7.19.3–7.19.5 and resources of 7.19.6.

### 2.2.3. How Ecological Factors Act

The action of ecological factors has several general features.

- Combined action. Factors do not act in isolation: each is linked to others and a change in one changes others. A forest canopy changes light, temperature, humidity and wind together; a change in altitude changes temperature, pressure, radiation and precipitation.
- Principal (dominant) factor. Among the factors acting on a process, one is often decisive. The low temperatures of winter are the principal factor that allows winter wheat to flower (**vernalization**, [7.13.22](/7.13.22)); day length is the principal factor that induces diapause in many insects (7.19.3).
- Irreplaceability and compensation. Every essential factor is necessary, and none can be fully replaced by another; but when one factor is deficient, an increase in a related factor can partly compensate for it. Molluscs need calcium to build their shells; where strontium is abundant, some species incorporate strontium in place of part of the calcium (Odum's example). In plants, a high light intensity partly compensates for a low CO₂ concentration, and the reverse.
- Direct and indirect action. Light and temperature act directly on physiology; the slope, aspect and altitude of a site act indirectly, through their effect on light, temperature and water. Indirect factors are often the ones that are easiest to map and therefore appear in studies of distribution.
- Stage-specific action. Organisms need different factors, or different levels of a factor, at different stages of development. The reproductive stage is usually the most sensitive: seeds, eggs, larvae and seedlings have narrower tolerance limits than adults (Section 2.4.3), and many animals breed only in a part of their range.

## 2.3. Interactions between Organisms and Environment

### 2.3.1. Action, Reaction and Interaction

The relation between organism and environment runs in both directions. Three terms, introduced by Clements, describe it.

- Action: the effects of the abiotic environment on organisms, on their growth, development, reproduction, behavior, survival and distribution.
- Reaction: the effects of organisms on their abiotic environment. A forest creates its own microclimate, soil organisms change the structure and nutrient content of soil, and beavers convert streams into ponds.
- Interaction (coaction): the effects of organisms on one another. Between a predator and its prey or a parasite and its host it is not meaningful to say which is acting and which is reacting: the relation is mutual, and over evolutionary time it leads to **coevolution** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). The types of interaction between two species, classified by the sign of the effect on each (+, − or 0), are the subject of 7.19.22–7.19.29.

The largest reaction in Earth history was the oxygenation of the atmosphere by photosynthesis. Cyanobacteria, the only organisms performing oxygenic photosynthesis before the eukaryotic algae, released oxygen that was at first consumed by reduced iron and sulfur compounds; in the **Great Oxidation Event**, about 2.4–2.1 billion years ago, free oxygen accumulated in the atmosphere for the first time, from almost nothing to perhaps 1–10% of the present level ([7.10.27](/7.10.27)). The oxygen in turn made aerobic respiration and the ozone layer possible, and thus the later evolution of complex life. Today one species, our own, causes a reaction of comparable reach: the rise of carbon dioxide and global temperature since the industrial revolution (7.19.47).

### 2.3.2. Adaptation

Organisms are not passive. Through natural selection they acquire morphological, physiological and behavioral traits that reduce the limiting effect of their environment: **adaptation** ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)). Plants adapt mainly by morphology and physiology; animals also by behavior, for example by moving to a better place or time. All adaptations have a genetic basis, although phenotypic plasticity, the ability of one genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments, is itself heritable and adaptive.

A classic example is industrial melanism in the peppered moth *Biston betularia*: in soot-darkened woods of industrial England the dark (*carbonaria*) form, less visible to birds on dark bark, rose to over 90% in the 19th century; after clean-air legislation reduced smoke from the 1950s onward, its frequency fell again, closely tracking the decline in winter smoke concentration ([7.10.5](/7.10.5), [7.4.23](/7.4.23)).

Two patterns of adaptation are common. Different species living under similar conditions tend to converge on similar forms; such groups of species are called **life forms** ([7.12.32](/7.12.32)), for example the streamlined bodies of whales, seals and fishes, or the Raunkiær life forms of plants classified by the position of their perennating buds (7.19.32). Populations of one species living under different conditions tend to diverge into genetically distinct **ecotypes** ([7.10.11](/7.10.11)), for example climatic ecotypes of rice adapted to different day lengths and temperatures (the early, middle and late rices of Chinese agriculture), edaphic ecotypes such as upland and paddy rice, and biotic ecotypes such as disease-resistant and susceptible strains. Organisms can also escape adverse conditions in time, by dormancy and **diapause**, or in space, by **migration** (7.19.19, [7.18.20](/7.18.20)).

## 2.4. Laws of Limitation and Tolerance

### 2.4.1. Liebig's Law of the Minimum

The German agricultural chemist Justus von Liebig (1803–1873) observed in the 1840s that the yield of crops was often limited not by the nutrients needed in large amounts, such as carbon dioxide and water, which are usually plentiful, but by an element needed in small amounts that is scarce in the soil, such as boron, magnesium or iron. Carl Sprengel had made the same point in 1828, and the principle is sometimes called the Sprengel–Liebig law. It is usually illustrated by a barrel whose staves have different heights: water can only be filled to the height of the shortest stave.

The **law of the minimum** ([7.13.4](/7.13.4)) states that the growth of a plant is controlled by the essential factor that is present in the smallest amount relative to the requirement; more generally, a factor that is below the minimum required by a species is the decisive factor for its survival and distribution. Odum added two qualifications. First, the law applies strictly only in a steady state, when inflows and outflows of energy and matter are balanced; during transitions, many factors may change in quick succession and none is limiting for long. Second, factors interact: a high concentration of one factor may change the requirement for another (the compensation of Section 2.2.3), so that the minimum is not fixed.

In its simplest mathematical form, yield $y$ depends on the supplies $x_1, \dots, x_n$ of $n$ factors through

$$
y = \min\{f_1(x_1), f_2(x_2), \dots, f_n(x_n)\},
$$
where $f_i(x_i)$ is the yield that factor $i$ alone would allow. If soil nitrogen can support a yield of 250 kg, potassium 350 kg and phosphorus 500 kg, Liebig's law predicts a yield of about 250 kg, and adding potassium or phosphorus without nitrogen achieves nothing. The equal-yield curves (isoclines) of this model in the plane of two factors are right angles (figure below): along the vertical arm only the first factor matters, along the horizontal arm only the second.

Two alternatives are also used. In a multiplicative model each factor scales the effect of the other,

$$
y = y_{\max}\, g_1(x_1)\, g_2(x_2), \qquad g_i(x_i) = \frac{x_i}{k_i + x_i},
$$
where $g_i$ is a saturating (Monod or **Michaelis–Menten equation**-type) function with half-saturation constant $k_i$; and in an additive model the factors substitute for each other, $y = a_1 g_1(x_1) + a_2 g_2(x_2)$. The isoclines are hyperbola-like curves in both cases, and both factors affect yield at every point.

![Three models of limitation by two resources, nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P), with saturating responses N/(1 + N) and 1.5P/(1 + 1.5P) (illustrative parameters). Top: isoclines of equal yield. Under Liebig's law of the minimum the isoclines are right angles, so that only the scarcer resource matters; in the multiplicative model (each factor scales the effect of the other) and in the additive model (factors substitute for each other) they are smooth curves. Bottom: yield against nitrogen supply at a low (red) and a high (blue) phosphorus supply. Under Liebig's law yield increases with nitrogen until phosphorus becomes limiting and then stays at a plateau set by phosphorus; in the multiplicative model nitrogen always increases yield, and more so when phosphorus is abundant.](./plots/limitation-models.py)

The models make different predictions for a factorial fertilization experiment, in which plots receive nothing (control), nitrogen alone, phosphorus alone, or both. Under single limitation by nitrogen (Liebig), only the N and N+P treatments increase growth, by the same amount until phosphorus becomes limiting. Under multiplicative co-limitation, N and P alone each increase growth, and N+P increases it more than the sum of the two separate effects (synergy). A meta-analysis of 641 factorial N × P experiments in terrestrial, freshwater and marine systems (Harpole and colleagues 2011) found that the combined addition of N and P most often gave a much larger response than either alone; strict single limitation was less common than the simple form of Liebig's law suggests. Liebig's insight remains correct in the sense that an essential resource in short supply cannot be replaced, but at the scale of whole plants and communities several resources often limit simultaneously, because organisms adjust their allocation (a plant short of nitrogen invests in roots), and because communities contain species limited by different resources. Ecological stoichiometry (7.19.6) develops this theme.

### 2.4.2. Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors

The British plant physiologist Frederick Blackman (1866–1947) studied how the rate of photosynthesis depends on light, carbon dioxide and temperature. In his paper "Optima and limiting factors" (1905) he formulated the principle that when a process depends on several factors, its rate is limited by the factor that is in shortest supply (the "slowest" factor), and increasing that factor increases the rate until another factor becomes limiting. Thus photosynthesis increases linearly with light at low intensity and then reaches a plateau whose height is set by the carbon dioxide concentration: the Liebig curve in the lower left panel of the figure above. Blackman also showed that apparent optima can arise from the interplay of factors: at high temperature photosynthesis is initially fast but declines with time as the apparatus is damaged, so that the "optimum" temperature measured depends on the duration of the measurement. Real light-response curves of leaves have a rounded shoulder rather than Blackman's sharp corner, and are usually described by a non-rectangular hyperbola whose curvature parameter $\theta$ gives the Blackman curve in the limit $\theta \to 1$ ([7.13.9](/7.13.9)).

The ideas of Liebig and Blackman were later combined with the observation that a factor can limit by excess as well as by deficiency (too much heat, light or water). This gives the general concept of a limiting factor.

**Definition 2.4.1 (Limiting Factor)** A **limiting factor** is an ecological factor that, when it approaches or exceeds the limits of tolerance of an organism, restricts its survival, growth, reproduction or spread. A factor can be limiting by deficiency or by excess.

The concept is practical: to understand or manage a population, one looks for the factor whose change has the largest effect. When the survival of deer in a reserve was found to rise and their numbers to increase after supplementary food was provided in winter, winter food was identified as a limiting factor. The spread of the Africanized honey bee through the Americas, from its introduction in Brazil in 1956 to Texas in 1990, slowed and stopped near similar latitudes in South and North America, where cold winters limit colonies that do not store enough honey: low temperature limits its range at both ends.

### 2.4.3. Shelford's Law of Tolerance

The American ecologist Victor Shelford (1877–1968) generalized the limiting-factor concept to both ends of every gradient.

**Definition 2.4.2 (Law of Tolerance)** The **law of tolerance** (Shelford 1913) states that the presence and success of an organism are limited by both a deficiency and an excess of each ecological factor: when a factor approaches or reaches the limits of tolerance of the organism, quantitatively or qualitatively, the organism declines or cannot survive.

**Definition 2.4.3 (Ecological Amplitude)** For each ecological factor an organism has a lower and an upper limit of tolerance. The range between them is its range of tolerance, or **ecological amplitude**, for that factor. Within it lies a narrower optimal range, flanked by zones of physiological stress, and outside it a zone of intolerance.

The law is usually drawn as a bell-shaped tolerance curve along the gradient of a factor (figure below, panel A): performance or abundance is highest in the optimal range, low in the zones of stress, and zero beyond the limits of tolerance. Measured along a geographic gradient, the population is abundant in the optimal zone, rare in the zones of stress and absent outside. Not all life processes have the same range: organisms can usually survive over a wider range than allows them to grow, and grow over a wider range than allows them to reproduce (S, G and R in panel A). Toxins have a one-sided response (no effect at low concentrations, harm and death above a threshold), and micronutrients such as copper and zinc are essential at low and toxic at high concentrations.

The width of the amplitude is expressed by the prefixes eury- (wide) and steno- (narrow): eurythermal and stenothermal (temperature), euryhydric and stenohydric (water), **euryhaline** ([7.14.21](/7.14.21)) and stenohaline (salinity), euryphagous and stenophagous (food), euryphotic and stenophotic (light), euryoecious and stenoecious (habitat). Stenothermal species may be adapted to cold (oligothermal) or to warmth (polythermal). The Antarctic emerald rockcod *Trematomus bernacchii*, which lives at about −1.9 °C, dies when warmed to about 6 °C (Somero and DeVries 1967): it is extremely stenothermal. The goldfish, which tolerates temperatures from near freezing to about 40 °C depending on acclimation (Section 2.5.1), is one of the most eurythermal fishes known.

Later work developed Shelford's law in several directions (Odum 1971; Pianka 1978):

1. A species may have a wide range of tolerance for one factor and a narrow range for another.
2. Tolerance changes during development; the reproductive period is usually critical, with the narrowest limits, so that breeding is often restricted to part of the range.
3. Different species have different ranges of tolerance for the same factor; species with wide ranges for all factors are likely to be widely distributed.
4. When conditions are suboptimal for one factor, the limits of tolerance for other factors are reduced (Section 2.4.5).
5. Species frequently do not live where conditions are physiologically optimal, because other species exclude them from there (Section 2.4.6).

### 2.4.4. Tolerance as a Performance Function

The tolerance curve can be written as a function. Let $x$ be the level of a factor and $P(x) \geq 0$ the performance (growth rate, survival, fecundity) of an organism exposed to it. A convenient model is the Gaussian curve

$$
P(x) = P_{\max} \exp\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
$$
with optimum $\mu$, maximum $P_{\max}$ and tolerance breadth $\sigma$; the ecological amplitude, defined as the range over which $P$ exceeds a fraction $q$ of its maximum, is $\mu \pm \sigma\sqrt{-2\ln q}$. Thermal performance curves are asymmetric and are modelled differently (7.19.4), but the Gaussian captures the essential trade-off.

It is often observed that a species with a wide tolerance performs less well at any particular point than a specialist does at its optimum: "a jack of all trades is master of none". The simplest way to express this is to assume that the area under the tolerance curve is fixed, $\int P(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = P_{\max}\sigma\sqrt{2\pi} = c$, so that $P_{\max} = c/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$: doubling the breadth halves the peak (figure below, panel B). Herbivores with narrow diets and generalist omnivores are a familiar example. Which breadth is best then depends on how variable the environment is.

Performance along an environmental gradient (Gaussian curve); the optimal range (green) is flanked by zones of physiological stress and, beyond the lower and upper limits of tolerance, by zones of intolerance (red); the range between the limits is the ecological amplitude. Bars below the curve show that survival (S) is possible over a wider range than growth (G), and growth over a wider range than reproduction (R). B: The generalist–specialist trade-off: three tolerance curves with the same area; the stenotopic specialist (red) has a high, narrow peak, the eurytopic generalist (blue) a low, broad one.](./plots/tolerance-curves.py)

**Proposition 2.4.1 (Optimal Tolerance Breadth)** Let an individual with the Gaussian tolerance curve of fixed area $c$ experience a factor $X$ that varies from generation to generation with mean $m$ and variance $s^2$, and let the long-term growth of its lineage be measured by the expectation of $\ln P(X)$, the logarithm of performance. Then the expected log performance is

$$
\mathbb{E}[\ln P(X)] = \ln\frac{c}{\sqrt{2\pi}} - \ln\sigma - \frac{s^2 + (m - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2},
$$
and it is maximized by the tolerance breadth $\sigma^* = \sqrt{s^2 + (m - \mu)^2}$. In particular, if the optimum matches the mean environment ($\mu = m$), the best breadth equals the standard deviation of the environment, $\sigma^* = s$.

**Proof** From $\ln P(x) = \ln P_{\max} - (x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ with $P_{\max} = c/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$, and $\mathbb{E}[(X - \mu)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X) + (\mathbb{E}X - \mu)^2 = s^2 + (m - \mu)^2$, the expression follows. Writing $D^2 = s^2 + (m - \mu)^2$ and differentiating with respect to $\sigma$,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\sigma}\,\mathbb{E}[\ln P(X)] = -\frac{1}{\sigma} + \frac{D^2}{\sigma^3},
$$
which is positive for $\sigma < D$ and negative for $\sigma > D$; hence the maximum is at $\sigma = D$. $\square$

The logarithm is used because the growth of a lineage over many generations is multiplicative: after $T$ generations its size is proportional to $\prod_t P(X_t)$, whose $T$-th root (the geometric mean) converges to $\exp\mathbb{E}[\ln P(X)]$ by the law of large numbers. A specialist with a high peak does very well in average years but very badly in extreme ones, and a single very bad year (performance near zero) has a disproportionate effect on the geometric mean. The proposition expresses a general result of evolutionary ecology: environmental variability selects for broad tolerance, and stable environments for specialization. It also explains why tropical ectotherms, which experience little seasonal temperature variation, tend to have narrower thermal tolerances than temperate ones (7.19.4). The same logic of geometric-mean fitness underlies **bet hedging** (7.19.18).

### 2.4.5. Two Factors: Response Surfaces

Because factors interact, the tolerance for one factor depends on the level of others. Pianka (1978) illustrated this with an organism whose fitness depends on both relative humidity and temperature: the optimal temperature depends on humidity and the optimal humidity on temperature, and the area of high fitness is a region in the plane of the two factors rather than the product of two independent ranges. Haefner (1970) measured the survival of the sand shrimp *Crangon septemspinosa* at combinations of temperature and salinity; the zone of high survival was largest at intermediate values of both, and at temperatures far from the optimum the range of tolerated salinities was narrower. Two factors define a two-dimensional section of the niche, and many factors together define Hutchinson's $n$-dimensional niche (7.19.23).

A simple model of such an interaction is

$$
s(T, S) = \exp\left(-\frac{(T - T_0)^2}{2\sigma_T^2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{(S - S_0)^2}{2w(T)^2}\right), \qquad w(T) = w_0\exp\left(-\frac{(T - T_0)^2}{2\tau^2}\right),
$$
in which the salinity tolerance $w(T)$ is widest at the optimal temperature and narrows as temperature departs from it. If $w$ were constant the surface would factorize, $s(T, S) = s_T(T)\,s_S(S)$, and the response to each factor would be independent of the other; the dependence of $w$ on $T$ is what makes the factors interact, and it reproduces the fourth point of Section 2.4.3.

### 2.4.6. Physiological and Ecological Optima

The optimum measured in the laboratory, with one species grown alone, is the physiological optimum; the conditions where the species is most abundant in nature are its ecological optimum. They often differ, because competitors, predators and parasites exclude a species from parts of its physiological range. In Ellenberg's experiments (1953) several meadow grasses were grown along a gradient of water-table depth. Grown alone, all had their maximum growth at similar intermediate water tables; grown in mixture, each was displaced towards a different part of the gradient, some to the wet and some to the dry end, by the competitive superiority of others in the middle. Field surveys of herbs along gradients of soil pH show the same pattern: species that grow best at near-neutral pH in pure culture are found in the field on acid or on calcareous soils, where they are free from stronger competitors. The physiological range corresponds to the fundamental niche and the ecological range to the realized niche, concepts developed in 7.19.23.

### 2.4.7. Adaptive Suites

Adaptation to a demanding environment usually involves not a single trait but a coordinated set of morphological, physiological and behavioral traits, an adaptive suite. The dromedary camel (*Camelus dromedarius*) illustrates it for water shortage:

- It feeds on succulent plants and those wet with dew, and it can go without drinking for two weeks or more in winter.
- Its kidneys produce concentrated urine and its faeces are dry.
- It lets its body temperature rise from about 34 °C at dawn to about 41 °C by late afternoon when dehydrated, storing heat by day and losing it by conduction and radiation in the cool night instead of by sweating; this saves several litres of water a day (Schmidt-Nielsen 1964).
- It tolerates the loss of about a quarter of its body water, and the water is lost mainly from the tissues rather than from the blood, so that the blood does not become viscous; its oval red blood cells withstand the osmotic swelling when it drinks over 100 L within minutes.
- Its hump stores fat as an energy reserve. The metabolic water produced by oxidizing this fat is often cited as a water source, but it is largely offset by the extra respiratory water loss needed to obtain the oxygen for the oxidation, so the hump is an energy store, not a water store.

## 2.5. Adjusting the Limits of Tolerance

### 2.5.1. Acclimation and Acclimatization

Tolerance limits are not fixed. Over evolutionary time they shift through selection; within a lifetime they shift through physiological adjustment to recent conditions.

**Definition 2.5.1 (Acclimation and Acclimatization)** If an organism is kept for some time at conditions towards one side of its range, its tolerance curve shifts: the optimum and the lower and upper limits move in the direction of the conditions experienced. Such an adjustment induced experimentally, usually by changing a single factor, is called **acclimation**; the same process occurring in nature, in response to the combined seasonal or geographic change of many factors, is called **acclimatization**.

Frogs acclimated to 5 °C consume more oxygen at any test temperature than frogs acclimated to 25 °C, partially compensating for the slowing effect of cold on metabolism. Birch seedlings that die at −15 to −20 °C in summer survive −35 °C after hardening in autumn, and willow twigs that survive −15 °C in winter are killed by −5 °C in summer (**cold acclimation**, [7.13.27](/7.13.27)).

The effect of acclimation on thermal tolerance is summarized by a tolerance polygon (Fry 1947; figure below, panel A). Fish are acclimated at different temperatures $T_{\mathrm{a}}$, and their upper and lower lethal temperatures $U(T_{\mathrm{a}})$ and $L(T_{\mathrm{a}})$, at which half of the fish die within a fixed time, are measured. For the goldfish both rise nearly linearly with acclimation temperature: acclimated to 20 °C, goldfish tolerate 2.5–34 °C; acclimated to 30 °C, 9–38 °C. The upper limit eventually reaches a ceiling (about 41 °C for the goldfish) that no acclimation can raise, the ultimate upper lethal temperature, and the lower limit cannot fall below the freezing point of water. The area of the polygon,

$$
A = \int \max\{U(T_{\mathrm{a}}) - L(T_{\mathrm{a}}), 0\}\,\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{a}},
$$
in $^\circ\mathrm{C}^2$, is a measure of the overall thermal tolerance of a species: about 1200 °C² for the goldfish, among the largest known, and only a few hundred for stenothermal salmonids (Brett 1956).

### 2.5.2. Homeostasis: Regulators and Conformers

A second way of extending tolerance is to keep the internal environment constant while the external one varies, **homeostasis** ([7.14.1](/7.14.1)). Organisms can be classified by their response.

**Definition 2.5.2 (Regulators and Conformers)** With respect to a given environmental factor, an organism that maintains a relatively constant internal state (body temperature, osmotic concentration, ionic composition, pH) while the external state varies is called a **regulator** (a homeostatic organism); one whose internal state follows the external state is called a **conformer** (a non-homeostatic organism).

The terms refer to particular factors: a marine crab may be an **osmoconformer** ([7.14.21](/7.14.21)) and a thermal conformer at the same time, while a freshwater fish regulates its osmotic concentration but conforms in temperature. Many organisms are partial regulators (figure above, panel B): their internal state changes, but by less than the environment changes. If the internal state $y$ responds to the external state $x$ as $y = y^* + \beta(x - y^*)$ near the set point $y^*$, the coefficient $\beta$ ranges from 0 for a perfect regulator to 1 for a perfect conformer. Regulation is never unlimited: outside its range of regulation, a regulator conforms and then dies. Homeostasis thus widens the ecological amplitude but does not free an organism from environmental limits; regulators are typically eurytopic for the regulated factor.

Regulation has costs. An endotherm that keeps its body at 37 °C in an environment at 0 °C loses heat in proportion to the difference, and must replace it by metabolism (7.19.4); a freshwater fish that keeps its body fluids more concentrated than the water must pump ions against their gradients. The balance between the costs and benefits of regulation explains why conformers are common wherever the environment is stable, as in the deep sea and much of the open ocean.

Regulation need not be physiological. The simplest and most widespread mechanism is behavior: moving to a better microclimate. Desert lizards bask with their flanks perpendicular to the sun and pressed against warm rock in the morning, face the sun to minimize the exposed area and raise their bodies into the breeze at midday, or retreat into shade and burrows; in this way they hold their body temperature within a few degrees of a preferred value for much of the day (**behavioral thermoregulation**, 7.19.4). Plants, which cannot move, adjust the angle of their leaves, and many flowers track the sun or close at night.

## Summary

- The environment is everything around a subject that influences it (Definition 2.1.1); the macroclimate is set by large-scale factors, the microclimate by local topography, vegetation and soil (Definition 2.1.2). The habitat is the place where an organism lives (Definition 2.1.3), the niche the set of conditions and resources it needs.
- Ecological factors (Definition 2.2.1) are classified by nature (climatic, edaphic, topographic, biotic, anthropogenic), by their dependence on density (density-dependent factors can regulate populations, density-independent ones cannot) and as conditions (not consumed) or resources (consumed). They act in combination, with a principal factor, irreplaceably but with partial compensation, directly or indirectly, and differently at different stages of life.
- Organisms are acted on by the environment, react on it (the Great Oxidation Event, forest microclimates) and interact with each other; they adapt by convergence (life forms) and divergence (ecotypes).

| Law | Statement | Model |
|---|---|---|
| Liebig (1840) | The essential factor in shortest supply relative to need controls growth | $y = \min_i f_i(x_i)$, L-shaped isoclines |
| Blackman (1905) | A process is limited by its slowest factor until another becomes limiting | Linear rise to a plateau |
| Shelford (1913) | Deficiency and excess both limit (Definition 2.4.2) | Bell-shaped tolerance curve; ecological amplitude (Definition 2.4.3) |
| Co-limitation | Several factors limit simultaneously | $y = y_{\max}\prod_i g_i(x_i)$, smooth isoclines |

- A limiting factor (Definition 2.4.1) can limit by deficiency or excess. Species are eury- or stenotopic for each factor; broad tolerance costs peak performance, and under the equal-area trade-off the tolerance breadth that maximizes long-term (geometric-mean) performance equals the root-mean-square deviation of the environment from the optimum (Proposition 2.4.1).
- Factors interact (response surfaces); species often live away from their physiological optimum because of competitors (ecological versus physiological optimum); adaptation to a hard environment involves suites of traits (the camel).
- Tolerance limits shift by acclimation and acclimatization (Definition 2.5.1; the tolerance polygon); homeostasis distinguishes regulators from conformers (Definition 2.5.2), widens but does not abolish the limits, and costs energy; behavior is the simplest regulator.

## Questions

1. A crop on a certain soil receives enough nitrogen for a yield of 250 kg, potassium for 350 kg and phosphorus for 500 kg. What yield does Liebig's law predict, and what would a farmer gain by adding potassium alone? How would the answer differ if the factors co-limited multiplicatively?

   **Answer** Liebig's law predicts about 250 kg, the yield allowed by the most limiting nutrient, nitrogen; adding potassium alone gains nothing, and adding nitrogen raises the yield until potassium becomes limiting at 350 kg. Under multiplicative co-limitation each nutrient scales the effect of the others, so adding potassium alone would raise the yield somewhat, though much less than adding nitrogen, and adding both would give more than the sum of the separate gains. A factorial experiment (control, N, K, N + K) distinguishes the two cases.

2. Explain why a frost that kills all individuals not in shelters, when shelters are limited in number, is a density-dependent factor.

   **Answer** Suppose there are $H$ shelters and $N$ individuals, and only sheltered individuals survive. The fraction killed is $\max(0, 1 - H/N)$, which increases with $N$: at low density all find shelter and none die, at high density most die. The per capita mortality thus depends on density, so the frost (through the limited shelters) can regulate the population, although it is a climatic factor. Density dependence is a property of the effect, not of the kind of factor.

3. Using Proposition 2.4.1, compare the optimal thermal tolerance breadth of an insect in a tropical lowland, where the mean temperature of the active season varies with a standard deviation of 1 °C among generations, with one in a temperate region with a standard deviation of 4 °C. What happens if the temperate insect's optimum is 2 °C above the mean temperature?

   **Answer** With the optimum at the mean temperature, $\sigma^* = s$: 1 °C in the tropics, 4 °C in the temperate zone. If the temperate optimum is displaced by 2 °C, $\sigma^* = \sqrt{4^2 + 2^2} \approx 4.5$ °C: a mismatch between optimum and environment favours broader tolerance, just as variability does. The model predicts narrower thermal tolerance in the tropics, consistent with the observed latitudinal pattern (7.19.4), and implies that tropical specialists are more vulnerable to a shift in mean temperature.

4. Why is the reproductive stage often the one that limits the distribution of a species? Give an example.

   **Answer** Eggs, embryos, larvae and seedlings have narrower tolerance limits than adults (they are small, cannot move away, and development requires precise conditions), and reproduction requires more energy and better conditions than survival. A species may therefore survive as adults where it cannot breed. Examples: many fishes and amphibians whose eggs tolerate a narrower range of temperature and oxygen than the adults; sea turtles, whose embryos die above about 33–35 °C (7.19.4); plants that survive as adults beyond the range where they set seed, such as the rush *Juncus squarrosus* above 600 m in England, whose failure to ripen seeds in turn limits the rush moth that feeds on them.

5. In seawater of 1000 and 1100 mOsm kg⁻¹, the blood osmolality of crab A is 960 and 1050 mOsm kg⁻¹, and that of crab B 600 and 620 mOsm kg⁻¹. Estimate the coefficient $\beta$ for each and classify them. Which is more likely to live in an estuary?

   **Answer** $\beta_A = (1050 - 960)/(1100 - 1000) = 0.9$: crab A is nearly an osmoconformer. $\beta_B = (620 - 600)/100 = 0.2$: crab B is a partial regulator, holding its blood far below the external concentration. Conformers are typical of the stable open sea, where there is no need to regulate; partial or strong regulators are typical of estuaries, salt marshes and other environments where salinity fluctuates with tides and rainfall, because there regulation keeps the internal environment within tolerable limits. Crab B is the estuarine species.

6. Why can acclimation not raise the upper lethal temperature indefinitely?

   **Answer** Acclimation works by adjusting enzymes, membrane lipids, heat shock proteins and other components within the limits allowed by the genome, but the stability of proteins and membranes has an upper limit set by their chemistry: above a certain temperature essential proteins denature, membranes become too fluid and oxygen supply cannot meet demand (7.19.4). The upper ceiling of the tolerance polygon (about 41 °C for the goldfish) reflects these limits; raising it requires genetic change over generations, not acclimation.
