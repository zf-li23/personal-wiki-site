The last part of this course applies ecology to the problems that human activity creates. Its central object is biodiversity: the variety of genes, species and ecosystems whose structure and dynamics the previous chapters have analyzed. This chapter asks what biodiversity is, how much of it there is, and why it matters; the next two ask what threatens it ([7.19.51](/7.19.51)) and what can be done ([7.19.52](/7.19.52)), and the remaining chapters treat harvesting, pest management, agriculture and pollution ([7.19.53](/7.19.53)–[7.19.54](/7.19.54)). Section 50.1 defines the scope of applied ecology and conservation biology. Section 50.2 defines biodiversity and its levels, and discusses how the number of species on Earth is estimated. Section 50.3 classifies the values of biodiversity, from medicines and **ecosystem services** to the value of knowing that a species exists, and the methods used to put numbers on them; Section 50.4 summarizes the ethical arguments that do not depend on such numbers. Section 50.5 returns to theory: biodiversity is not only valued but also functional, and experiments and models show how the number of species affects the productivity and stability of ecosystems. The measurement of diversity within communities (**alpha diversity**, **beta diversity**, Hill numbers, global indicators) was treated in [7.19.31](/7.19.31), and the stabilizing effect of diversity on aggregate properties in [7.19.38](/7.19.38#sec-38.5).

## 50.1. Applied Ecology and Conservation Biology

*Applied ecology* uses the theory and principles of ecology to explain, guide and solve practical problems of society. Its classical branches serve the production of food and materials from living resources: agricultural ecology (agroecology), forest ecology, range ecology and fisheries science, each of which has moved from the individual and population levels toward the community and ecosystem levels as the indirect effects of management (pest resurgence, overfishing of food webs, soil degradation) became apparent. Newer branches address the sustainability of human society as a whole: pollution ecology, restoration ecology, ecological engineering, human ecology and ecological economics. Agroecology and pollution are the subject of [7.19.54](/7.19.54), harvesting and pest management of [7.19.53](/7.19.53).

**Definition 50.1.1 (Conservation Biology)** **Conservation biology** is the applied science that documents the diversity of life, studies the effects of human activities on species, communities and ecosystems, and develops practical approaches to prevent extinctions, maintain genetic diversity within species, and protect and restore biological communities and the ecosystems on which they depend.

Michael Soulé (1985) called conservation biology a *crisis discipline*: like medicine, it must often act before the evidence is complete, because waiting for certainty means accepting losses that cannot be reversed. It is synthetic, drawing on population and community ecology, genetics, biogeography, economics, law and the social sciences, and normative, because it starts from the value judgment that the diversity of life is worth keeping. Soulé listed its postulates: diversity of organisms is good; the untimely extinction of populations and species is bad; ecological complexity is good; evolution is good; and biological diversity has intrinsic value, regardless of its usefulness to people. The first four are partly empirical claims, which the following sections examine; the last is an ethical position (Section 50.4).

## 50.2. What Biodiversity Is

### 50.2.1. Definition and Levels

**Definition 50.2.1 (Biodiversity)** **Biodiversity**, or biological diversity, is "the variability among living organisms from all sources including, inter alia, terrestrial, marine and other aquatic ecosystems and the ecological complexes of which they are part; this includes diversity within species, between species and of ecosystems" (Convention on Biological Diversity, Article 2).

The term "biological diversity" was in use by about 1980; its contraction "biodiversity" was coined by Walter G. Rosen in 1985 while planning the 1986 National Forum on BioDiversity in Washington, whose proceedings, edited by E. O. Wilson (1988), made it widely known. The definition is deliberately broad: it covers variety at all levels of biological organization, together with the complexity of the habitats and ecological processes in which organisms live. Three levels are usually distinguished:

- *Genetic diversity*: the variation in genes within species, among the individuals of a population and among populations.
- *Species diversity*: the variety of species, in a community or a region or on Earth.
- *Ecosystem diversity*: the variety of communities and their physical environments in the biosphere, together with the ecological processes that link them.

Chinese textbooks often add *landscape diversity*, the variety of the spatial arrangement of ecosystems ([7.19.48](/7.19.48)). Other dimensions cut across the levels: *functional diversity*, the variety of ecological roles and traits (nitrogen fixers, deep-rooted and shallow-rooted plants, pollinators of different flower types), which matters for ecosystem functioning (Section 50.5); and *phylogenetic diversity*, the total branch length of the phylogeny connecting a set of species (Faith 1992), which measures the evolutionary history they represent. A region whose species belong to many old, distinct lineages has more phylogenetic diversity than one with as many species from a single recent radiation.

### 50.2.2. Genetic Diversity

Genetic diversity is measured by **heterozygosity**, **allelic diversity** and the proportion of polymorphic loci within populations, and by **genetic differentiation** among them ([7.19.20](/7.19.20)). It is the raw material of adaptation, and its loss through drift and inbreeding reduces the fitness of small populations ([7.19.15](/7.19.15), [7.19.52](/7.19.52)). Genetic diversity also includes the divergence among populations that are, or may be, distinct species, and it can be lost by mixing as well as by drift. Two Chinese cases illustrate both points.

The Chinese giant salamander (*Andrias davidianus*), the largest living amphibian, was long treated as one species distributed over much of central and southern China. Genetic analyses showed that it comprises at least five, and perhaps as many as eight, deeply divergent lineages that separated millions of years ago (Yan et al. 2018), and two of them have since been described as separate species, the South China giant salamander (*A. sligoi*) and, in 2022, the Jiangxi giant salamander (*A. jiangxiensis*). Meanwhile, wild populations had collapsed: a survey of 97 sites in 16 provinces in 2013–2016 found giant salamanders at only four sites, and the animals found were probably escaped or released farm animals (Turvey et al. 2018). Large-scale farming for food, with breeding stock moved across the country, and the release of farmed animals into rivers as a conservation measure, mixed the lineages and threatened to erase the very diversity that the releases were meant to protect. The discovery of genetically pure wild Jiangxi giant salamanders in 2022 therefore mattered for conservation: it showed that some native lineages survive, and that releases must be matched genetically to the lineage of each river basin.

The South China tiger (*Panthera tigris amoyensis*), once widespread south of the Yangtze, has not been confirmed in the wild for decades and is considered extinct there. The captive population, about two hundred animals in Chinese zoos and breeding centers, descends from six wild-caught founders captured in the 1950s–1970s, so that inbreeding is a central problem of its management. A genomic study published in 2023 found that the captive population retains a moderate level of genetic diversity and a distinct genetic identity, which supports the possibility of eventual reintroduction if enough habitat and prey can be restored.

### 50.2.3. Species Diversity and the Number of Species

Species diversity at the scale of communities is measured by **richness** and by indices that weight species by their abundance (**Shannon index**, **Simpson index**, Hill numbers), and it is partitioned among scales into alpha, beta and **gamma diversity** ([7.19.31](/7.19.31)). At the global scale the first question is simply how many species there are.

About 2.1–2.2 million living species have been described and named. Estimates of the total range more widely than estimates of almost any other basic quantity in biology, from about 3 million to over 100 million (May 2010); most published estimates of eukaryotes lie between 5 and 30 million. The uncertainty arises because the best-known groups (vertebrates, vascular plants, butterflies) are a small fraction of the total, and the groups that dominate richness (insects, mites, nematodes, fungi, marine invertebrates, unicellular eukaryotes) are poorly sampled, especially in the tropics and the deep sea. Several methods have been used:

- Extrapolation of discovery curves: the cumulative number of described species in a group against year of description approaches an asymptote, which estimates the total if sampling effort has been roughly constant.
- Ratios: if the ratio of known to unknown species in a well-studied region (Britain) holds elsewhere, or the ratio of insects to vascular plants holds everywhere, global totals follow from well-known groups. Erwin (1982) fogged the canopies of 19 trees of one species in Panama, counted the beetle species, and estimated how many were specific to that tree species; scaling up to all tropical tree species and all arthropods gave about 30 million tropical arthropods, an estimate later reduced to 5–7 million when host specificity was measured directly.
- Higher-taxonomy patterns: Mora and colleagues (2011) observed that the numbers of higher taxa (phyla, classes, orders, families, genera) are much better known than the number of species, because most higher taxa have already been discovered, and that within groups whose species are well known the number of taxa increases regularly with taxonomic rank. Fitting asymptotic curves to the discovery of higher taxa and extrapolating the relationship between number and rank to the species level, they estimated 8.7 million (standard error 1.3 million) eukaryotic species, about 6.5 million on land and 2.2 million in the oceans, of which 86 % of terrestrial and 91 % of marine species remain undescribed.

For prokaryotes the concept of species is itself uncertain ([7.11.2](/7.11.2)), and estimates based on the scaling of sequence diversity with sample size range up to $10^{12}$, which is disputed. China, with about 10 % of the world's described species, is one of the dozen or so megadiverse countries; the Catalogue of Life China lists well over a hundred thousand species and infraspecific taxa, and the number grows by several thousand each year.

### 50.2.4. Ecosystem Diversity

Ecosystem diversity is the variety of **biomes** ([7.19.41](/7.19.41)) and of the ecosystems within them, and it is the level at which conservation can protect whole sets of species and processes at once. It is the hardest level to quantify, because ecosystems grade into one another; in practice it is described by the number and extent of vegetation types or ecosystem types in a classification. The southern slope of Namcha Barwa (7782 m) in southeastern Tibet shows how much ecosystem diversity a single gradient can hold. Over a vertical distance of about 7000 m, from the gorge of the Yarlung Tsangpo near Medog to the summit, one passes through lowland tropical seasonal rain forest, subtropical evergreen broad-leaved forest, mixed coniferous and broad-leaved forest, subalpine dark coniferous forest of fir and spruce, alpine shrubland and meadow, alpine sparse vegetation and scree, and finally the nival zone of permanent snow and ice: a sequence of zones that, on the plain, would span thousands of kilometers of latitude ([7.19.41](/7.19.41)).

## 50.3. The Value of Biodiversity

### 50.3.1. A Classification of Values

Why should biodiversity be conserved? Arguments fall into two kinds. Instrumental arguments value biodiversity for what it does for people; intrinsic arguments value it for its own sake (Section 50.4). Economists classify instrumental values into a *total economic value*:

| Category | Kind | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Direct use value: consumptive use | Goods consumed locally, not sold | Firewood, wild meat, medicinal plants used by households |
| Direct use value: productive use | Goods harvested from the wild and sold | Timber, fish, rattan, wild-harvested medicines, genetic material for crop breeding |
| Indirect use value | Benefits of ecosystem functions, usually not marketed | Water purification, flood control, pollination, climate regulation, soil formation |
| Option value | The value of keeping the possibility of future use | Undiscovered medicines and genes; bioprospecting |
| Existence value | The value of knowing that a species or ecosystem exists | Willingness to pay for the survival of whales or pandas one will never see |
| Bequest value | The value of passing biodiversity on to future generations | Willingness to pay to preserve wilderness for descendants |

Use values (the first three rows) arise from use; existence and bequest values are non-use values. Option value lies between: it is the value of keeping open the possibility of use.

### 50.3.2. Direct Use Values

Direct use is the oldest relationship between people and other species, and in many places it is still the most important. Hundreds of millions of people depend on wild-harvested foods, fuel and medicines; wild meat supplies much of the animal protein of rural people in Central Africa and Amazonia; fisheries provide about a fifth of the animal protein of three billion people ([7.19.53](/7.19.53)). The productive use of wild species has supplied the founders of all crops and livestock, and their wild relatives remain sources of genes for disease resistance and stress tolerance.

The most cited direct values are pharmaceutical. *Paclitaxel* (Taxol) was found in the 1960s in a screening program of the US National Cancer Institute in the bark of the Pacific yew (*Taxus brevifolia*); its structure was published in 1971, and in 1979 it was shown to act by stabilizing microtubules, which arrests cells in mitosis. Approved for ovarian cancer in 1992 and later for breast and lung cancer, it became one of the most widely used anticancer drugs. Supply was a problem from the start: the bark contains about 0.01–0.02 % paclitaxel, so that one kilogram required the bark of thousands of slow-growing trees, and wild yews in Yunnan and elsewhere were stripped illegally. All Chinese yews are now nationally protected (Class I). The drug is now made semisynthetically from a precursor extracted from the renewable needles of cultivated yews, or by plant cell fermentation, and the long biosynthetic pathway has recently been largely reconstructed in heterologous hosts. The case shows both the value of biodiversity and the danger that discovery of value brings to the species concerned.

The blood of horseshoe crabs provides another. In the 1950s and 1960s Frederik Bang and Jack Levin found that the amebocytes of the Atlantic horseshoe crab (*Limulus polyphemus*) clot in the presence of the endotoxin of Gram-negative bacteria. *Limulus amebocyte lysate* (LAL), licensed in 1977, became the standard test for bacterial endotoxin in injectable drugs, vaccines and medical devices. Hundreds of thousands of crabs are caught each year on the Atlantic coast of North America, bled of about a third of their blood and returned to the sea, with a mortality estimated at 4–30 %; in Asia the lysate (TAL) comes from the tri-spine horseshoe crab (*Tachypleus tridentatus*), now listed as Endangered by the IUCN and protected in China (Class II) since 2021. Harvests for bait and lysate affect migratory shorebirds such as the red knot, which depend on horseshoe crab eggs during their spring stopover. A synthetic alternative, recombinant factor C, has been accepted by pharmacopoeias in recent years, which would remove most of the pressure on the crabs.

Basic research on obscure organisms has produced tools of incalculable value. *Taq* DNA polymerase, from the bacterium *Thermus aquaticus* isolated by Thomas Brock from a hot spring in Yellowstone in 1969, made the polymerase chain reaction practical. CRISPR–Cas systems, the adaptive immune systems of bacteria and archaea, were discovered by microbiologists studying sequence repeats in archaeal genomes and became, from 2012, the most versatile genome-editing tools ([7.2](/7.2)). No one could have predicted either use; this is the essence of option value (Section 50.3.4).

### 50.3.3. Indirect Use Values and Ecosystem Services

Most of the value of biodiversity does not pass through markets. Ecosystems purify water, regulate floods and climate, pollinate crops, control pests, form soil and recycle nutrients. These benefits rarely appear in national accounts such as GDP, but they are what makes the sustained use of natural resources possible.

**Definition 50.3.1 (Ecosystem Services)** **Ecosystem services** are the benefits that people obtain from ecosystems. The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005) classified them into *provisioning services* (food, fiber, fuel, fresh water, genetic resources, medicines), *regulating services* (regulation of climate, floods, disease and water quality, pollination, pest control), *cultural services* (spiritual, aesthetic, recreational and educational benefits) and *supporting services* (primary production, nutrient cycling, soil formation), which underlie all the others.

The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (MA), carried out in 2001–2005 by about 1360 experts at the request of the United Nations, was the first global assessment of the links between ecosystems and human well-being. It related ecosystem services to the constituents of well-being: security, the basic materials for a good life, health, good social relations, and freedom of choice and action. Its main findings were that over the preceding 50 years humans had changed ecosystems more rapidly and extensively than in any comparable period of history, that the gains in human well-being had been obtained at the cost of the degradation of about 60 % (15 of 24) of the ecosystem services it examined, and that these costs fell disproportionately on the poor. The Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES), founded in 2012, continues the assessments; it speaks of *nature's contributions to people*, a framework that recognizes that the same benefits are valued differently in different cultures.

Some services have been quantified with confidence. Animal pollination benefits about three quarters of the leading types of food crops; about 5–8 % of global crop production, worth US\$235–577 billion a year (2015 values), is directly attributable to it (IPBES 2016). New York City chose in the 1990s to protect the forested watersheds of the Catskill Mountains, which filter its drinking water, for about US\$1–1.5 billion, instead of building a filtration plant estimated at US\$6–8 billion with annual operating costs of hundreds of millions. Global totals are more problematic. Costanza and colleagues (1997) estimated the value of the world's ecosystem services at about US\$33 trillion a year, more than the global gross national product of the time, and updated it in 2014 to about US\$125 trillion a year. Such numbers communicate that the value is large, but their meaning is unclear: the economy cannot exist without ecosystem services, so their total value is not a price at which they could be traded, and valuation is meaningful mainly at the margin, for decisions about particular changes.

### 50.3.4. Option, Existence and Bequest Values

*Option value* is the value of keeping biodiversity available for future uses that are not yet known. Bioprospecting, the systematic search for useful compounds and genes in wild organisms, realizes it. Long-lived animals offer a current example. The bowhead whale (*Balaena mysticetus*) lives for more than 200 years and has thousands of times more cells than a human, yet it rarely develops cancer (Peto's paradox: cancer risk does not scale with body size and lifespan across species). A study published in 2025 found that bowhead whale cells repair DNA double-strand breaks faster and more accurately than human cells, by both homologous recombination and non-homologous end joining, and accumulate fewer mutations and chromosomal abnormalities after damage. A key factor is the cold-inducible RNA-binding protein CIRBP, present at much higher levels than in other mammals: expressing the whale protein in human cells improved their repair, and in fruit flies extended lifespan and increased survival after irradiation. Elephants solve the same problem differently: they carry about 20 copies of the tumor suppressor gene *TP53* (one ancestral copy and many retrogenes), and their cells respond to DNA damage by apoptosis more readily, eliminating damaged cells rather than repairing them. Both mechanisms are potential leads for human medicine that would be lost with the species.

*Existence value* is the value people place on knowing that a species or ecosystem exists, independently of any use; *bequest value* is the value of preserving it for future generations. They are measured by what people are willing to pay. For the giant panda, Wei and colleagues (2018) estimated the value of the ecosystem services of the panda and its reserves, including their cultural value, at US\$2.6–6.9 billion a year in 2010, about 10–27 times the cost of conservation; much of this was cultural value, the willingness of people within and outside China to pay for the existence of the species and its habitat.

### 50.3.5. Valuation Methods

Values that do not pass through markets must be estimated indirectly:

- *Market prices*, for goods that are traded (timber, fish), net of the costs of harvest.
- *Production function* and *replacement cost* methods value a service by its contribution to a marketed product (pollinators to crop yield) or by the cost of replacing it (a filtration plant for a watershed, artificial pollination).
- *Revealed preference* methods infer values from behavior: the travel costs that visitors incur to reach a park, or the premium that houses near green space command (hedonic pricing).
- *Stated preference* methods ask people what they would be willing to pay (contingent valuation) or have them choose among alternatives with different attributes and costs (choice experiments). They are the only way to estimate non-use values, and they are sensitive to the wording of questions and to the difference between willingness to pay and willingness to accept compensation.
- *Benefit transfer* applies values estimated in one place to another, which is cheap but often inaccurate.

The total economic value of a tropical wetland, for example, is the sum of its fisheries and timber, its flood regulation and water purification valued by replacement cost, its recreation valued by travel cost, and its existence value from surveys. Comparisons of land uses with such totals often show that intact ecosystems are worth more than the converted land, but that the benefits of conversion go to a few private owners while the benefits of conservation are spread over many people and future generations, which is why conversion proceeds.

Time enters through discounting. A benefit $B_t$ received $t$ years in the future is valued today at $B_t/(1 + r)^t$, where $r$ is the discount rate, and the present value of a stream of benefits is $\mathrm{PV} = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}B_t(1 + r)^{-t}$.

**Example 50.3.1** An ecosystem service worth $B$ per year forever has present value $\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}B(1 + r)^{-t} = B/r$. At $r = 3\,\%$ this is $33B$; at $r = 7\,\%$, $14B$. A benefit of $B$ received only after 100 years is worth $B(1.03)^{-100} \approx 0.05B$ at 3 % and $B(1.07)^{-100} \approx 0.001B$ at 7 %. If clearing a forest yields a one-time profit $V$ and destroys a service of $B$ per year, the forest is cleared under cost–benefit analysis whenever $V > B/r$: high discount rates make the long-term costs of biodiversity loss nearly invisible, and the choice of $r$, partly an ethical choice about the weight of future generations, often decides the outcome. The same logic makes the extinction of slow-growing harvested populations economically "optimal" when their growth rate is below the discount rate ([7.19.53](/7.19.53)).

### 50.3.6. Ecological Economics

*Ecological economics* treats the economy as a subsystem of the biosphere, dependent on flows of energy and materials from it and limited by its capacity to supply resources and absorb wastes (Daly, Costanza and others, from the 1980s). It aims to include the value of biodiversity and ecosystem services in economic accounting and decisions, so that their conservation can be argued for in the language in which decisions are made. A central concept is natural capital, the stock of ecosystems and resources that yields ecosystem services, analogous to manufactured capital; consuming natural capital and recording the proceeds as income, as GDP does, overstates the true income of a society.

The *genuine progress indicator* (GPI) is one attempt to correct this. It starts from personal consumption expenditure, adjusts it for the inequality of income distribution, adds the value of household and volunteer work and of public infrastructure, and subtracts costs such as crime, commuting, pollution, the loss of wetlands, farmland and forests, the depletion of non-renewable resources and long-term environmental damage (26 components in the usual formulation). Applied to 17 countries holding about half the world's population, GPI per capita peaked around 1978 and then stagnated or declined while GDP per capita continued to rise (Kubiszewski et al. 2013). China has developed the gross ecosystem product (GEP), the annual value of the final ecosystem services of a region, as a complement to GDP in assessing the performance of local governments (Ouyang et al. 2020). Related tools include the **ecological footprint** ([7.19.40](/7.19.40)) and the System of Environmental-Economic Accounting of the United Nations.

## 50.4. Environmental Ethics

Economic valuation has limits. Many people hold that species have value independently of human interests, and that their extinction is wrong even if no one would pay to prevent it. *Environmental ethics* examines these claims. Its main positions differ in what they regard as having moral standing:

- *Anthropocentrism*: only humans have moral standing, but human interests (including those of future generations, and aesthetic and spiritual interests) give strong reasons to conserve nature. Most economic arguments are of this kind.
- *Biocentrism*: all living organisms have moral standing, each pursuing its own good; Albert Schweitzer's "reverence for life" and Paul Taylor's respect for nature are examples.
- *Ecocentrism*: species, communities and ecosystems as wholes have value. Aldo Leopold's land ethic (1949) is its classical statement: "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. It is wrong when it tends otherwise."

Arguments commonly made in conservation biology include: each species has a right to exist; all species are interdependent; humans have a responsibility to future generations; respect for human life and interests is compatible with respect for biodiversity; the spiritual and aesthetic values of nature exceed its economic value; and biodiversity is needed to understand the origin and history of life. A more recent view emphasizes *relational values*: much of what people value in nature is neither instrumental nor intrinsic but lies in relationships, such as the identity, responsibility and care that communities feel toward particular places and species (Chan et al. 2016). In practice, conservation decisions combine these arguments: economic values can justify protection where markets ignore it, and ethical arguments justify protection where economic values are small or unknown, as for most of the millions of undescribed species.

## 50.5. Biodiversity and Ecosystem Functioning

### 50.5.1. Experiments

The values of Section 50.3 assume that biodiversity contributes to the functioning of ecosystems. Does it? Until the 1990s, ecologists mostly asked how the environment determines diversity ([7.19.35](/7.19.35)); since then, they have also asked how diversity affects ecosystem processes.

**Definition 50.5.1 (Biodiversity–Ecosystem Functioning)** **Biodiversity–ecosystem functioning** (BEF) research studies how the number, identity, traits and relative abundances of species affect ecosystem functions: the rates and magnitudes of processes such as primary production, nutrient uptake and retention, decomposition and their stability over time.

The first experiments assembled communities with different numbers of species drawn at random from a pool. In the Ecotron, a set of controlled-environment chambers in London, communities of plants and animals with more species took up more carbon dioxide (Naeem et al. 1994). At Cedar Creek, Minnesota, Tilman and colleagues sowed 168 plots of $9 \times 9$ m with 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 grassland species drawn at random from a pool of 18 perennials; above-ground biomass increased with richness, and the effect grew over time, until the 16-species plots produced about 2.7 times as much biomass as monocultures on average (Tilman et al. 2001). The European BIODEPTH experiment, replicated at eight sites from Sweden to Greece, found a log-linear decline of biomass with species loss, by about 80 g m⁻² for each halving of richness, and a further effect of the number of functional groups, especially the presence of nitrogen-fixing legumes (Hector et al. 1999). The Jena Experiment in Germany (since 2002) extended the design to 60 species and many functions. A synthesis of hundreds of experiments in terrestrial, freshwater and marine systems established the general pattern (Cardinale et al. 2011): species richness increases the production and resource use of a trophic level, with a saturating relationship, and effects strengthen with the duration of experiments.

### 50.5.2. Mechanisms: Sampling and Complementarity

Two mechanisms can produce a positive effect of richness. Under the *sampling effect*, also called the selection effect (Huston 1997; Tilman et al. 1997), a richer mixture is more likely to contain a particularly productive species, and if that species is also competitively dominant, it takes over the mixture. Under complementarity, species differ in their use of resources (rooting depth, timing of growth, nutrient forms, nitrogen fixation) or in their natural enemies, so that a mixture uses more of the available resources than any single species can, or species help one another (**facilitation**). The two mechanisms have different consequences: a sampling effect cannot make a mixture yield more than the best monoculture, whereas complementarity can, a result called *transgressive overyielding*. A simple model makes the sampling effect precise.

**Proposition 50.5.1 (Sampling Effect)** Suppose that monoculture yields $M_1, M_2, \dots$ of the species in a pool are independent with distribution function $F$, that a mixture of $S$ species drawn at random is taken over by its most productive species, and that this species yields its monoculture yield. Then the expected mixture yield is

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y(S)] = \mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i \le S}M_i\right] = \int_0^\infty\left(1 - F(y)^S\right)\mathrm{d}y,
\tag{50.1}
$$
which increases with $S$, with decreasing increments, toward the upper limit of $F$; and the mixture never yields more than its best monoculture.

**Proof** The maximum of $S$ independent draws is at most $y$ exactly when every draw is, so its distribution function is $F(y)^S$, and for a non-negative random variable $\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^\infty\mathbb{P}(X > y)\,\mathrm{d}y$. Since $F(y)^S$ decreases in $S$ for each $y$, the integrand increases with $S$. The increments are $\int_0^\infty F(y)^S(1 - F(y))\,\mathrm{d}y$, which decrease in $S$, so the curve is concave in $S$, and it converges to the supremum of the support of $F$ by monotone convergence. The last statement holds because the mixture yield equals the largest monoculture yield in the mixture. $\square$

If competitive dominance is unrelated to monoculture yield, the mixture yield is that of a randomly chosen species and the expected yield does not depend on $S$ at all (Figure 1A). The sampling effect therefore requires a correlation between competitive ability and productivity, which holds under resource competition: by the **R\* rule** ([7.19.23](/7.19.23)), the species that reduces the limiting resource to the lowest level excludes the others, and it is often the species that converts the resource into biomass most efficiently. Complementarity is modeled by letting species be best in different parts of a heterogeneous habitat (Tilman et al. 1997). In Figure 1B, each species performs best at its own position along a resource gradient; in a mixture, each point of the habitat is held by the species that performs best there, and the mixture yields more than its best monoculture, increasingly so as species fill the gradient.

### 50.5.3. Partitioning the Net Effect

Experiments measure the yields of species in mixture and in monoculture; how can the mechanisms be separated? Loreau and Hector (2001) proposed an additive partition based on relative yields, the ratio of the yield of a species in mixture to its yield in monoculture.

**Proposition 50.5.2 (Loreau–Hector Partition)** In a mixture of $N$ species sown in proportions $p_i$ ($\sum_ip_i = 1$), let $M_i$ be the monoculture yield of species $i$, $Y_{\mathrm{O},i}$ its observed yield in the mixture, $Y_{\mathrm{O}} = \sum_iY_{\mathrm{O},i}$, and let the expected yield under the null hypothesis that each species performs in mixture as in monoculture be $Y_{\mathrm{E}} = \sum_ip_iM_i$. Define the deviation in relative yield $\Delta\mathrm{RY}_i = Y_{\mathrm{O},i}/M_i - p_i$. Then the net biodiversity effect $\Delta Y = Y_{\mathrm{O}} - Y_{\mathrm{E}}$ is

$$
\Delta Y = \underbrace{N\,\overline{\Delta\mathrm{RY}}\;\bar M}_{\text{complementarity effect}} + \underbrace{N\operatorname{cov}(\Delta\mathrm{RY}, M)}_{\text{selection effect}},
\tag{50.2}
$$
where bars denote means over the $N$ species and the covariance is computed with divisor $N$.

**Proof** By definition $Y_{\mathrm{O},i} = (p_i + \Delta\mathrm{RY}_i)M_i$, so $\Delta Y = \sum_i(p_i + \Delta\mathrm{RY}_i)M_i - \sum_ip_iM_i = \sum_i\Delta\mathrm{RY}_iM_i$. For any two sequences, $\frac1N\sum_ia_ib_i = \bar a\bar b + \operatorname{cov}(a, b)$ with the covariance taken with divisor $N$. Multiplying by $N$ gives Eq. (50.2). $\square$

The complementarity effect (CE) is positive when species on average do better in mixture than expected from their monocultures, as with resource partitioning or facilitation; it is negative under interference. The selection effect (SE) is positive when the species with high monoculture yields are those that gain in mixture (dominance by productive species), and negative when low-yielding species dominate.

**Example 50.5.1** A two-species mixture is sown at $p_1 = p_2 = 0.5$. Monoculture yields are $M_1 = 500$ and $M_2 = 200$ g m⁻²; in mixture the species yield $Y_{\mathrm{O},1} = 400$ and $Y_{\mathrm{O},2} = 150$ g m⁻². Then $Y_{\mathrm{E}} = 0.5 \times 500 + 0.5 \times 200 = 350$, $Y_{\mathrm{O}} = 550$, and $\Delta Y = 200$ g m⁻². Relative yields are $0.8$ and $0.75$, so $\Delta\mathrm{RY} = (0.30, 0.25)$, with mean $0.275$; $\bar M = 350$. The complementarity effect is $2 \times 0.275 \times 350 = 192.5$. The covariance is $\frac12(0.30 \times 500 + 0.25 \times 200) - 0.275 \times 350 = 100 - 96.25 = 3.75$, so the selection effect is $2 \times 3.75 = 7.5$. The two add to $200$: the overyielding is almost entirely complementarity. Both species yield more than half their monoculture, and the mixture ($550$) exceeds the better monoculture ($500$), a case of transgressive overyielding.

:::

Applied to the BIODEPTH experiments, the partition showed that complementarity dominated the net effect, while the selection effect varied in sign among sites (Loreau and Hector 2001). In a meta-analysis of grassland experiments, about two thirds of the net effect was due to complementarity, almost half of the selection effects were negative, and complementarity increased with the duration of experiments while selection did not (Cardinale et al. 2007), consistent with the growing effect at Cedar Creek. Figure 2 shows how the partition recovers known mechanisms in simulated communities. The partition has limits: a positive CE does not prove niche partitioning (facilitation and the release from species-specific pathogens also produce it), and Fox (2005) refined it into three terms, separating dominance at the expense of other species from trait-independent complementarity.

### 50.5.4. Stability and the Insurance Effect

Diversity also affects the stability of ecosystem functions over time. The **portfolio effect** and the **insurance hypothesis** ([7.19.38](/7.19.38#sec-38.5.1)) express the statistical argument: if species fluctuate asynchronously, the sum of their biomasses fluctuates less than each. Yachi and Loreau (1999) made the biological mechanism explicit with a model in which species differ in their responses to a fluctuating environment. In each environmental state some species perform well, and competition shifts dominance toward them; a richer community is more likely to contain species suited to whatever conditions occur. Two effects follow (Figure 3): a *buffering effect*, a reduction in the temporal variance of ecosystem productivity, and a *performance-enhancing effect*, an increase in its temporal mean. Both require differences in species' responses to the environment; if all species respond alike, adding species adds nothing.

![The insurance effect in a model community. Environment $E_t$ autocorrelated in time; species have Gaussian responses of growth to $E$ with optima spread along the environmental axis, and compete through total biomass, with weak immigration from a regional pool. A: total biomass of a one-species and an eight-species community over 200 years, with the environment (grey): in the richer community, species replace one another as conditions change, and total biomass stays high. B: temporal mean of total biomass and C: its coefficient of variation against richness (60 random communities per level; bands: central 80 %). Diversity raises the mean (performance-enhancing effect) and lowers the variability (buffering effect).](./plots/insurance.py)

The insurance effect has been observed in the Cedar Creek grasslands, where total biomass varied less from year to year in richer plots, especially during a severe drought ([7.19.38](/7.19.38#sec-38.5.2)), and in a synthesis of 46 grassland experiments, richer communities were more resistant to climate extremes (Isbell et al. 2015). It becomes stronger at larger scales: different species maintain function in different years, at different places and for different functions, so that many more species are needed to sustain ecosystem multifunctionality across times and places than for any single function in one place and year (Isbell et al. 2011).

### 50.5.5. From Experiments to the Real World

How far do these results apply to natural ecosystems and to the losses now under way? Several qualifications matter. Experiments assemble random subsets of species, whereas real extinctions are not random: large, rare, specialized species and top predators are lost first, and losing them may affect function more or less than random loss would. In natural communities, the environment that determines diversity also determines productivity, and effects of diversity are weaker and more variable than in experiments, though often detectable after accounting for environmental gradients (van der Plas 2019). On the other hand, experiments mostly vary plant richness within one trophic level; losses of consumers can have larger effects through trophic cascades ([7.19.39](/7.19.39)). The comparison with other drivers is nonetheless sobering. In a meta-analysis of nearly 200 studies, Hooper and colleagues (2012) found that losses of 21–40 % of species reduced primary production as much as climate warming or increased ultraviolet radiation, and losses of 41–60 % as much as ozone, acidification, elevated carbon dioxide or nutrient pollution; at Cedar Creek, reductions of richness of the size caused by human activity had effects on biomass as large as those of nitrogen addition, drought, fire or herbivore exclusion (Tilman et al. 2012). Biodiversity is therefore not only something that ecosystem functions support, but also one of their controls.

## Questions

1. A pharmaceutical company finds a promising anticancer compound in the bark of a rare tree known from two valleys. Analyze the consequences for the species, using the history of paclitaxel, and propose measures that would let the discovery benefit both medicine and the tree.

   **Answer** Discovery of value can endanger a species: demand for raw material leads to overharvest, illegal stripping and rising prices that make the remaining trees more valuable (as with wild yews). Measures: protect the populations legally and in reserves at once; determine whether renewable tissues (leaves, twigs) contain the compound or a precursor; establish cultivation from seeds of many genotypes, to conserve genetic diversity and supply material; develop semisynthesis, cell culture or reconstruction of the biosynthetic pathway in microbes or yeast; and under the Nagoya Protocol, agree on benefit sharing with the country and communities that hold the resource, so that local people gain from conservation rather than from illegal harvest. Monitoring of the wild populations and genetic analysis of cultivated stock are needed to ensure that cultivation does not become a channel for laundering wild-harvested material.
2. In a two-species experiment, $M_1 = 600$, $M_2 = 300$ g m⁻²; the mixture, sown at equal proportions, yields $Y_{\mathrm{O},1} = 450$ and $Y_{\mathrm{O},2} = 60$ g m⁻². Compute the net, complementarity and selection effects, and interpret them.

   **Answer** $Y_{\mathrm{E}} = 0.5(600 + 300) = 450$, $Y_{\mathrm{O}} = 510$, $\Delta Y = 60$. Relative yields: $450/600 = 0.75$ and $60/300 = 0.2$, so $\Delta\mathrm{RY} = (0.25, -0.30)$, mean $-0.025$; $\bar M = 450$. CE $= 2 \times (-0.025) \times 450 = -22.5$. Covariance: $\frac12(0.25 \times 600 + (-0.30) \times 300) - (-0.025)(450) = \frac12(150 - 90) + 11.25 = 41.25$, so SE $= 82.5$. Check: $-22.5 + 82.5 = 60$. The mixture overyields because the more productive species dominates at the expense of the other (positive selection effect), while the species do slightly worse on average than expected from monocultures (small negative complementarity, as with interference competition). The mixture yields less than the better monoculture (510 < 600), as expected when selection dominates.
3. Explain why, in the sampling model of Proposition 50.5.1, the effect of richness on mean yield disappears if competitive ability is unrelated to productivity. What experimental evidence distinguishes sampling from complementarity?

   **Answer** If the winner of a mixture is chosen independently of the yields, its yield is a random draw from $F$ whatever the number of species, so the expected mixture yield is $\mathbb{E}[M]$ for all $S$; only the variance of outcomes changes. The richness effect in the sampling model comes entirely from the tendency of richer mixtures to include, and be taken over by, the most productive species. Evidence for complementarity: transgressive overyielding (mixtures exceeding their best monoculture), which sampling cannot produce; positive complementarity effects in the Loreau–Hector partition; increased total resource use (deeper soil water uptake, lower residual nitrate in mixtures); and the growth of the effect over time, as in Cedar Creek and the meta-analysis of Cardinale et al. (2007). Evidence for sampling: mixtures dominated by one species that is also the best monoculture, and positive selection effects.
4. A government compares converting a mangrove forest into shrimp ponds, which would yield a profit of 8000 yuan per hectare per year for 10 years (after which the ponds are abandoned), with keeping the mangrove, which provides fisheries, coastal protection and carbon storage worth 3000 yuan per hectare per year indefinitely. Compare the options at discount rates of 3 % and 10 %, and comment on what the calculation leaves out.

   **Answer** Shrimp ponds: $\mathrm{PV} = 8000\sum_{t=1}^{10}(1 + r)^{-t}$, which is $8000 \times 8.53 \approx 68\,200$ at 3 % and $8000 \times 6.14 \approx 49\,200$ at 10 %. Mangrove: $3000/r$, which is $100\,000$ at 3 % and $30\,000$ at 10 %. At 3 % the mangrove is worth more; at 10 % the ponds are. Omissions: the costs of restoring abandoned ponds and the lost services after abandonment (no recovery is assumed); risk, such as the value of coastal protection in storms, which may be large but rare; non-use values of the species; the distribution of benefits (profits to pond owners, services to fishing communities and coastal residents); irreversibility, which gives keeping the mangrove an option value; and the ethical question of how to weigh future generations, implicit in the discount rate.
5. (Open question) Some argue that putting monetary values on biodiversity is essential to protect it in a market economy; others that it reduces nature to a commodity and fails for most species. Discuss with examples.

   **Answer** Points for valuation: decisions are made by comparing costs and benefits, and values of zero are assumed for what is not valued (Catskills, pollination, mangroves for coastal protection show that valuation can change decisions); payments for ecosystem services and GEP accounting create incentives for local conservation. Points against: most species have no measurable instrumental value, and values estimated by stated preferences are unstable; valuation invites the conclusion that biodiversity may be destroyed whenever its price is exceeded, and discounting makes long-term losses negligible; values depend on who is asked and on income; some losses are irreversible and of uncertain consequence, for which a precautionary rule is more appropriate than a price. A balanced position uses valuation where services are well understood and markets fail, sets safe minimum standards or legal protection where losses are irreversible, and recognizes ethical and relational values that are not commensurable with money.
