Human activity now changes the Earth system as a whole. The carbon added to the atmosphere by burning fossil fuels ([7.19.45](/7.19.45#sec-45.5)) and the doubling of reactive nitrogen ([7.19.46](/7.19.46#sec-46.1.2)) are global perturbations of biogeochemical cycles; chlorofluorocarbons, compounds that did not exist before 1930, thinned the ozone layer over the whole planet. This chapter treats the physical basis and the ecological consequences of these changes. We begin with the energy balance of the Earth, which explains the greenhouse effect quantitatively, from the simplest one-layer model to the feedbacks that set the sensitivity of climate and to the ice-albedo feedback, which can make the climate bistable (47.1). We then summarize the observed changes in climate (47.2) and the responses of organisms and ecosystems: shifts of ranges at a speed set by the velocity of climate change, changes of phenology and body size, the feminization of sea turtle populations, the bleaching of coral reefs and the effects of ocean acidification (47.3). The ozone layer is the case in which science identified a global problem, and international action solved it (47.4). The chapter closes with the framework of planetary boundaries, which places these and other changes in a single picture (47.5). The ecological threats to biodiversity as a whole are the subject of [7.19.51](/7.19.51).

## 47.1. The Greenhouse Effect

### 47.1.1. Radiative Balance and the Effective Temperature

The Earth receives solar radiation with a flux of $S_0 \approx 1361\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ at the top of the atmosphere (the solar constant, [7.19.3](/7.19.3#sec-3.1.1)). Averaged over the sphere, four times the area of the intercepted disc, this is $S_0/4 \approx 340\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, of which a fraction $\alpha \approx 0.30$ (the planetary albedo) is reflected. In equilibrium the planet must radiate the absorbed energy as infrared radiation; by the **Stefan–Boltzmann law** a black body at temperature $T$ emits $\sigma T^4$, so the effective radiating temperature is

$$
T_{\mathrm{e}} = \left[\frac{(1 - \alpha)S_0}{4\sigma}\right]^{1/4} \approx 255\,\mathrm{K} \tag{47.1}
$$
with $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}$. The observed mean surface temperature is about $288\,\mathrm{K}$ ($15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$). The difference of 33 K is the greenhouse effect.

**Definition 47.1.1 (Greenhouse Effect)** The **greenhouse effect** is the warming of a planetary surface above its effective radiating temperature, because gases and clouds in the atmosphere, nearly transparent to the shortwave radiation of the Sun, absorb the longwave radiation emitted by the surface and re-emit it, partly back to the surface, from colder layers higher up.

**Definition 47.1.2 (Greenhouse Gas)** A **greenhouse gas** is an atmospheric constituent, natural or anthropogenic, that absorbs and emits infrared radiation within the range of wavelengths emitted by the Earth's surface and atmosphere; the main ones are water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, ozone and the halocarbons.

The name comes from a misleading analogy: a glass greenhouse stays warm mainly because it prevents convection, not by absorbing infrared radiation. The atmospheric effect depends on molecular physics: $\ce{N2}$ and $\ce{O2}$, symmetric diatomic molecules without a dipole moment that can change in vibration, do not absorb infrared radiation, whereas $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{CH4}$ have vibrational and rotational transitions in the thermal infrared ([5.5.5](/5.5.5)). $\ce{CO2}$ absorbs strongly near $15\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, close to the peak of the Earth's emission. Water vapour causes about half of the present greenhouse effect and clouds about a quarter, but the amount of water vapour is controlled by temperature (it condenses out of a cooler atmosphere), so it acts as a feedback rather than a driver; the non-condensing gases, above all $\ce{CO2}$, set the level around which water vapour adjusts (Lacis et al. 2010).

### 47.1.2. Layer Models

The simplest model of the greenhouse effect represents the atmosphere as one layer, transparent to sunlight and absorbing a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the infrared radiation from the surface; by Kirchhoff's law it then emits a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the black-body flux at its temperature, equally upwards and downwards.

**Proposition 47.1.1 (Layer Models of the Greenhouse Effect)** Let $T_{\mathrm{e}}$ be the effective temperature (47.1).

1. With one atmospheric layer of infrared absorptivity $\varepsilon$, the surface and atmospheric temperatures in radiative equilibrium are
$$
T_{\mathrm{s}} = \left(\frac{2}{2 - \varepsilon}\right)^{1/4}T_{\mathrm{e}}, \qquad T_{\mathrm{a}} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1/4}T_{\mathrm{s}}.
$$
2. With $n$ layers that are opaque in the infrared ($\varepsilon = 1$), the surface temperature is $T_{\mathrm{s}} = (n + 1)^{1/4}T_{\mathrm{e}}$.

**Proof** (1) Let $Q = (1 - \alpha)S_0/4 = \sigma T_{\mathrm{e}}^4$ be the absorbed solar flux. At the top of the atmosphere, the outgoing flux is the transmitted surface emission plus the upward emission of the layer: $(1 - \varepsilon)\sigma T_{\mathrm{s}}^4 + \varepsilon\sigma T_{\mathrm{a}}^4 = Q$. For the layer, absorption equals emission in both directions: $\varepsilon\sigma T_{\mathrm{s}}^4 = 2\varepsilon\sigma T_{\mathrm{a}}^4$, so $T_{\mathrm{a}}^4 = T_{\mathrm{s}}^4/2$. Substituting, $\sigma T_{\mathrm{s}}^4(1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/2) = Q$, i.e. $T_{\mathrm{s}}^4 = 2T_{\mathrm{e}}^4/(2 - \varepsilon)$. (2) Number the layers $1, \dots, n$ from the top, with temperatures $T_k$, and write $E_k = \sigma T_k^4$ and $E_{n+1} = \sigma T_{\mathrm{s}}^4$. The top layer emits $E_1$ to space, so $E_1 = Q$. Each opaque layer absorbs from its neighbours and emits both ways: $E_{k-1} + E_{k+1} = 2E_k$ for $k = 1, \dots, n$ (with $E_0 = 0$ for the downward flux from space, the first equation reads $E_2 = 2E_1$). Hence $E_k$ is linear in $k$ with $E_k = kQ$, and at the surface $E_{n+1} = (n + 1)Q$. $\square$

For $\varepsilon = 1$ the single-layer model gives $T_{\mathrm{s}} = 2^{1/4} \times 255 \approx 303\,\mathrm{K}$, more than observed; the observed 288 K corresponds to $\varepsilon \approx 0.77$, an atmosphere that lets about a quarter of the surface radiation escape directly through the "window" between 8 and 13 µm. The model explains the essential point: adding absorbers makes the layer from which radiation escapes to space higher and colder, so the surface must warm to restore the balance. It also predicts that the upper atmosphere cools as the greenhouse effect strengthens, which is observed in the stratosphere, whereas a warming caused by a brighter Sun would warm all layers; this "fingerprint" is one of the lines of evidence that attribute the warming to greenhouse gases. The model omits convection, which carries much of the heat from the surface to the atmosphere and makes real temperature profiles less steep than pure radiative equilibrium would.

The three terrestrial planets illustrate the range of the effect (panel A of the figure in 47.1.3). Mars, with a thin atmosphere of about 0.6 kPa of $\ce{CO2}$, has a surface temperature (about 210 K) close to its effective temperature. Venus, with a dense $\ce{CO2}$ atmosphere of about 9 MPa and clouds of sulfuric acid that reflect three quarters of the sunlight, has an effective temperature (about 230 K) below that of the Earth, but a surface temperature of about 740 K, the equivalent of an atmosphere many layers thick.

### 47.1.3. Radiative Forcing and Climate Sensitivity

A perturbation of the energy balance is measured by the radiative forcing $F$, the change in the net downward flux at the top of the atmosphere (in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$) after the stratosphere has adjusted and before the surface temperature responds. For $\ce{CO2}$ the forcing increases approximately with the logarithm of the concentration, because the centre of its absorption band is saturated and additional absorption occurs in the wings: $F \approx 5.35\ln(C/C_0)$, about $3.9\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ for a doubling (Forster et al. 2021). Relative to 1750, the total anthropogenic effective radiative forcing in 2019 was about $+2.7\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$: $\ce{CO2}$ contributed $2.2$, methane $0.5$, halocarbons $0.4$ and nitrous oxide $0.2$, partly offset by aerosols, about $-1.1\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$.

The response of the global mean temperature $T$ (anomaly from equilibrium) to a forcing is described by the energy balance

$$
C\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = F - \lambda T, \tag{47.2}
$$
where $C$ is the heat capacity per unit area (mostly that of the ocean mixed layer) and $\lambda > 0$ the climate feedback parameter, the increase in outgoing radiation per kelvin of warming. In equilibrium $T = F/\lambda$.

**Definition 47.1.3 (Equilibrium Climate Sensitivity)** The **equilibrium climate sensitivity** (ECS) is the equilibrium increase of global mean surface temperature caused by a doubling of the atmospheric concentration of $\ce{CO2}$, $\mathrm{ECS} = F_{2\times}/\lambda$.

If only the temperature of the emitting layer changed, $\lambda$ would be the Planck response $\lambda_0 = \mathrm{d}(\sigma T_{\mathrm{e}}^4)/\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{e}} = 4\sigma T_{\mathrm{e}}^3 \approx 3.8\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$ (about $3.2$ when the vertical structure of the atmosphere is taken into account), and a doubling of $\ce{CO2}$ would warm the Earth by only about 1.2 K. Feedbacks change this. Writing $\lambda = \lambda_0 - \sum_i \lambda_i$, the positive feedbacks $\lambda_i$ are the increase of water vapour with temperature (about 7% per kelvin, by the **Clausius–Clapeyron equation**; partly offset by the change of the lapse rate), the decrease of albedo as snow and sea ice melt, and the net effect of changes in clouds. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report estimated the net feedback parameter at about $1.2\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$ and assessed the ECS at 3 K, very likely between 2 and 5 K (Forster et al. 2021).

Because the deep ocean takes up heat slowly, the warming at any time is less than the equilibrium warming. A two-layer model represents this with a mixed layer at temperature $T$ and a deep ocean at $T_D$, exchanging heat at rate $\gamma(T - T_D)$:

$$
C\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = F - \lambda T - \gamma(T - T_D), \qquad C_D\frac{\mathrm{d}T_D}{\mathrm{d}t} = \gamma(T - T_D). \tag{47.3}
$$

**Proposition 47.1.2 (Equilibrium and Transient Response)** In the two-layer model with constant $F$, $T \to F/\lambda$. If $C_D \gg C$ and $F$ changes slowly compared with the time scale $C/(\lambda + \gamma)$ but fast compared with $C_D/\gamma$, then $T \approx F/(\lambda + \gamma)$.

**Proof** The system is linear with matrix $\begin{bmatrix} -(\lambda + \gamma)/C & \gamma/C \\ \gamma/C_D & -\gamma/C_D \end{bmatrix}$, whose trace is negative and whose determinant $\lambda\gamma/(CC_D)$ is positive, so both eigenvalues have negative real parts and the unique equilibrium, $T = T_D = F/\lambda$, is globally stable. On time scales short compared with $C_D/\gamma$, $T_D$ remains close to its initial value $0$; the mixed layer, with its short time scale, follows the quasi-equilibrium of its own equation with $T_D = 0$, $0 \approx F - (\lambda + \gamma)T$. $\square$

The transient climate response (TCR), the warming at the time of doubling when $\ce{CO2}$ increases by 1% per year (after 70 years), is therefore about $F_{2\times}/(\lambda + \gamma)$, assessed at 1.8 K. The deep ocean, which takes up about 90% of the extra heat, delays the warming by decades to centuries, and it also explains a remarkable property of the coupled carbon–climate system: after emissions stop, the slow decline of the atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$ excess ([7.19.45](/7.19.45#sec-45.5.4)) is nearly balanced by the slow decline of ocean heat uptake, so that the temperature remains approximately constant. Warming is then proportional to cumulative emissions, about $1.65\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ per $1000\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$ (the transient climate response to cumulative emissions), and the remaining carbon budget for a given temperature limit is a fixed quantity. In 2024 the budget remaining for a 50% chance of limiting warming to $1.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ was about $200\,\mathrm{Gt\,CO_2}$, five years of current emissions (Forster et al. 2024).

### 47.1.4. Ice-Albedo Feedback and Multiple Climates

When the albedo depends on temperature, the energy balance can have more than one equilibrium. In the zero-dimensional version of the models of Budyko (1969) and Sellers (1969), outgoing longwave radiation is approximated by $A + BT$ (with $T$ in °C, $A \approx 203\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, $B \approx 2.1\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$), and the albedo rises from about 0.3 for an ice-free planet to about 0.6 for an ice-covered one as the temperature falls:

$$
C\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mu\frac{S_0}{4}\bigl(1 - \alpha(T)\bigr) - (A + BT), \tag{47.4}
$$
where $\mu$ is the solar constant relative to today. An equilibrium $T^*$ is stable if outgoing radiation increases faster with temperature than absorbed radiation, $\mu(S_0/4)\lvert\alpha'(T^*)\rvert < B$; where the albedo changes steeply with temperature, the absorbed radiation increases faster, and the equilibrium is unstable. The equilibria therefore form an S-shaped curve with a warm, partly ice-covered branch and a cold, completely ice-covered "snowball" branch, joined by an unstable branch and two saddle-node folds, the same structure as in the lake model of [7.19.46](/7.19.46#sec-46.2.5). Simple models of this kind exaggerate the sensitivity of the present climate (the warm branch ends only a few percent below the present solar constant), but the mechanism is real. Geological evidence of glaciers near the equator in the Cryogenian, about 720–635 million years ago, indicates that the Earth did enter snowball states, from which it escaped, consistent with the hysteresis of the model, only after volcanic $\ce{CO2}$ had accumulated for millions of years in an atmosphere without rock weathering to remove it. Tipping elements of the present climate, such as the ice sheets of Greenland and West Antarctica, the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation and the Amazon rain forest, are thought to have similar thresholds (Armstrong McKay et al. 2022).

### 47.1.5. Greenhouse Gases

The Kyoto Protocol (1997) regulated six greenhouse gases or groups: carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), perfluorocarbons (PFCs) and sulfur hexafluoride; nitrogen trifluoride was added in 2012. They differ in concentration, lifetime and radiative efficiency, which are combined in the global warming potential (GWP), the time-integrated forcing of a pulse emission of 1 kg of a gas relative to that of 1 kg of $\ce{CO2}$, usually over 100 years.

| Gas | Concentration in 2023 | Preindustrial | Main sources | Lifetime | GWP (100 years) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CO2}$ | 420 ppm | 278 ppm | Fossil fuels, cement, deforestation | Not a single value (centuries to millennia for part of a pulse) | 1 |
| $\ce{CH4}$ | 1934 ppb | 729 ppb | Wetlands, livestock, rice, fossil fuel extraction, landfills | About 12 years | About 28–30 |
| $\ce{N2O}$ | 337 ppb | 270 ppb | Fertilized soils, manure, industry | About 110 years | About 270 |
| $\ce{SF6}$ | About 11 ppt | 0 | Electrical equipment | About 3200 years | About 25 000 |

Concentrations are global means from the WMO Global Atmosphere Watch network (WMO 2024); GWP values from IPCC AR6. Methane rose especially fast after 2007 and again after 2020, mainly from microbial sources (wetlands, agriculture and waste), as shown by its isotopic composition. In China, the Mount Waliguan station in Qinghai (3816 m), the only global baseline station of the network in the Eurasian interior, records concentrations close to the Northern Hemisphere means, with the same rising trends. The emissions of all three main gases reached record levels in 2023.

## 47.2. Observed Changes in Climate

### 47.2.1. Temperature

Global mean surface temperature in 2011–2020 was about $1.1\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ above that of 1850–1900, $1.6\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ over land and $0.9\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ over the oceans, and the IPCC concluded that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land (IPCC 2021). Each of the last four decades has been warmer than any preceding decade since 1850. The year 2024 was the warmest on record and the first calendar year more than $1.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ above the preindustrial level (about $1.55\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$; WMO 2025), though the limits of the Paris Agreement refer to averages over two decades. Warming is not uniform: land warms faster than oceans, and the Arctic has warmed nearly four times as fast as the global mean since 1979 (Rantanen et al. 2022), because of the ice-albedo feedback and changes in the vertical structure of the atmosphere.

### 47.2.2. Extremes

A shift of the mean temperature produces a disproportionate increase in the frequency of extremes, because extremes lie in the tail of the distribution. The IPCC estimated that a hot extreme that occurred once in ten years in the climate of 1850–1900 now occurs about 2.8 times as often, and would occur 4.1, 5.6 and 9.4 times as often at global warming levels of 1.5, 2 and $4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$; a once-in-fifty-years heat extreme 4.8 times as often now and 8.6, 13.9 and 39.2 times at those levels. Ten-year heavy precipitation events become about 1.3, 1.5, 1.7 and 2.7 times as frequent at 1, 1.5, 2 and $4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and ten-year agricultural droughts in drying regions 1.7, 2.0, 2.4 and 4.1 times (IPCC 2021). Heat waves, droughts, heavy rainfall and fire weather have all increased, and the probability of many individual events can now be attributed: several recent heat waves would have been virtually impossible without anthropogenic warming.

### 47.2.3. Precipitation, Ice and Sea Level

The water cycle has intensified ([7.19.45](/7.19.45#sec-45.4.3)): precipitation has increased at high latitudes and the variability of precipitation has increased over most land. Glaciers are retreating almost everywhere, including on the Tibetan Plateau and in the Himalaya, whose glaciers feed the great rivers of Asia; Arctic sea ice in September has declined by about 40% in area since 1979; and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are losing mass. Global mean sea level rose by about 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018, at an accelerating rate, 3.7 mm per year in 2006–2018, from thermal expansion of seawater and melting land ice. By 2100 it is projected to rise by about 0.3–0.6 m relative to 1995–2014 under very low emissions and 0.6–1.0 m under very high emissions, with a possible larger rise if the Antarctic ice sheet becomes unstable, and it will continue to rise for centuries.

### 47.2.4. Hazards and Health

Climate change increases the frequency and severity of weather-related hazards. Records of disasters compiled by the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction showed 3254 major floods in 2000–2019, against 1389 in 1980–1999, and 2043 storms against 1457 (UNDRR 2020), although improved reporting and growing exposure of people and property contribute to such trends. Heat is the deadliest weather hazard: heat-related mortality, especially of the elderly, has risen with the frequency of heat waves. Warming also expands the ranges and transmission seasons of vectors of disease, such as mosquitoes transmitting dengue, and affects food production, water supply and labour productivity; analyses of variation in climate across places and years show that temperature and precipitation influence mortality, agricultural yields, economic output and the risk of conflict (Carleton and Hsiang 2016). The ecological effects are the subject of the next section.

## 47.3. Ecological Responses

### 47.3.1. Range Shifts and the Velocity of Climate Change

Species track the climate to which they are adapted. A meta-analysis of more than 1000 range shifts found that species had moved their distributions towards the poles at a median rate of about 17 km per decade and upwards by about 11 m per decade, faster where warming was stronger (Chen et al. 2011). How fast must a species move to keep pace?

**Definition 47.3.1 (Velocity of Climate Change)** The **velocity of climate change** at a place is the speed at which an isotherm (or another climatic value) moves across the surface, $v = \dfrac{\partial T/\partial t}{\lvert\nabla T\rvert}$, the rate of change of temperature divided by its spatial gradient.

The velocity is high where the climate is spatially uniform: on flat lowlands the latitudinal gradient of temperature is only about $0.5$–$1\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ per 100 km, so a warming of $0.3\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ per decade moves the isotherms by several kilometres per year. In mountains the gradient is set by the lapse rate, about $6.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ per km of elevation, and a short climb compensates a large warming; velocities there are a hundred times lower. Loarie et al. (2009) estimated a global mean velocity of about 0.4 km per year for the second half of the twenty-first century under intermediate emissions, more than 1 km per year in flooded grasslands, mangroves and deserts and less than 0.1 km per year in montane forests. Reaction–diffusion theory gives the criterion for keeping pace: a species whose climate envelope moves at speed $q$ persists only if its asymptotic spreading speed $2\sqrt{rD}$ exceeds $q$ ([7.19.17](/7.19.17#sec-17.6.2)). Mountains are therefore refuges in a warming climate, but they offer less and less area towards their summits, and species already at the top have nowhere to go. Fragmentation of habitat reduces the effective dispersal of species and so their ability to track the climate ([7.19.49](/7.19.49)).

Range shifts reshuffle communities, because species move at different rates; they bring species into contact that did not co-occur before and separate predators from prey and parasites from hosts. In the eastern United States, models combining projected changes of habitat suitability with the dispersal capacity of 40 tree species and the fragmentation of the landscape predict that northern and mountain species are the most vulnerable and that most species will not be able to migrate fast enough without assistance (Rogers et al. 2017). In the Alps, native plants have spread upwards by more than 10 m per year in recent decades, and non-native species even faster (Dainese et al. 2017). In the Southern Ocean, the foraging grounds of king penguins along the Antarctic Polar Front are moving south, away from the islands on which the colonies breed (Cristofari et al. 2018).

### 47.3.2. Phenology and Mismatch

Spring events have advanced by about 2.3 days per decade on average (Parmesan and Yohe 2003; [7.19.4](/7.19.4)), but at different rates in different species, so that a **phenological mismatch** ([7.18.20](/7.18.20)) develops between consumers and their resources. In temperate European forests the peak of caterpillars, the main food of the nestlings of many songbirds, has advanced by 9–20 days, more than the arrival of long-distance migrants, which respond partly to day length in their African wintering grounds. In the Netherlands, populations of the pied flycatcher (*Ficedula hypoleuca*) declined by about 90% over two decades in areas with the earliest caterpillar peaks, but not in areas with late peaks (Both et al. 2006). Migrants have shortened their stopovers, but an analysis of tracking studies suggests that they cannot gain more than a few days in this way (Schmaljohann and Both 2017); evolution of the timing of migration and breeding is needed, at a rate that is unknown.

### 47.3.3. Body Size

Warming tends to reduce body size, a response so common that it has been called the third universal response to warming after range shifts and phenology (Gardner et al. 2011). In ectotherms it follows the **temperature–size rule** ([7.19.4](/7.19.4)): development is faster at higher temperature, and maturation at a smaller size. In aquatic ectotherms, the oxygen demand of large bodies rises with temperature faster than the supply through the gills, and Atlantic cod (*Gadus morhua*) are projected to reach smaller maximum sizes in the southern part of their range and larger ones at its northern margin (Butzin and Pörtner 2016). Among endotherms, birds of 52 species migrating through Chicago, killed by collisions with buildings and measured from 1978 to 2016 (more than 70 000 specimens), declined in body mass and in the length of the tarsus, while their wings became longer, consistent with **Bergmann's rule** in time and with selection for flight efficiency (Weeks et al. 2020).

### 47.3.4. Sea Turtles and Temperature-Dependent Sex Determination

In species with **temperature-dependent sex determination**, warming changes the sex ratio directly. Green turtle rookeries of the northern Great Barrier Reef now produce almost only females ([7.19.4](/7.19.4#sec-4.6.4)). Whether this threatens the populations depends on demography: because one male can fertilize many females, a female-biased population may at first grow faster; but turtles mature only after decades, so the hatchling sex ratio of today becomes the adult sex ratio of thirty years later, and when males become too rare, fertilization fails.

A simple scenario model shows these consequences. Let the incubation temperature be $T(t)$, rising linearly with interannual fluctuations, and the proportion of females among hatchlings be $p(T) = [1 + \mathrm{e}^{-(T - T_{\mathrm{p}})/s}]^{-1}$ with $T_{\mathrm{p}} = 29.3\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $s = 0.4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (the reaction norm of [7.19.4](/7.19.4#sec-4.6.1)). Adult females $F$ and males $M$ survive with probability $s_a$ per year, and hatchlings produced at time $t$ become adults at $t + \ell$:

$$
F_{t+1} = s_aF_t + p(T_{t-\ell})B_{t-\ell}, \qquad M_{t+1} = s_aM_t + \bigl(1 - p(T_{t-\ell})\bigr)B_{t-\ell}, \qquad B_t = cF_t\bigl(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-hM_t/F_t}\bigr),
$$
where the last factor is the fraction of females whose eggs are fertilized, which is close to 1 as long as males are not much rarer than $1/h$ of the females (each male can mate with several females). With $s_a = 0.95$, $\ell = 30$ years and $h = 10$, and $c$ chosen so that the population is stationary at the temperatures of 1990, the model predicts that warming of $0.025\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ per year makes more than 95% of hatchlings female by about 2050. The population first grows, as the extra females breed, but once adult males fall below about a tenth of the females (around 2100) reproduction fails and the population declines. Faster warming makes the boom shorter and the collapse earlier. Shading half of the nests by $1.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, a management measure tested on several nesting beaches, delays the loss of males by decades. The model ignores adaptive responses that could slow feminization: nesting earlier in the season or in cooler sites, and evolution of the pivotal temperature, which is heritable but slow to evolve in a species with a generation time of decades. It also ignores the direct effects of high temperatures on hatching success and of sea level rise on nesting beaches, which are likely to become limiting before the sex ratio does (Hays et al. 2017).

### 47.3.5. Coral Bleaching and Ocean Acidification

Reef-building corals live within 1–2 °C of their upper thermal limit. When the water stays warmer than the usual summer maximum for weeks, the symbiosis with their dinoflagellate algae (Symbiodiniaceae) breaks down and the corals expel them, **coral bleaching** ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#def-31.5.2)); prolonged bleaching kills the corals by starvation. The accumulated heat stress is measured in degree heating weeks (the sum of weekly temperature excesses above the maximum monthly mean over 12 weeks): significant bleaching is expected above 4 °C-weeks and widespread mortality above 8. Mass bleaching, unknown before the 1980s, has become frequent: the Great Barrier Reef bleached severely in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022 and 2024, and the marine heat wave of 2016 alone killed about 30% of its corals (Hughes et al. 2018). A fourth global bleaching event, declared in 2024, affected more than 80% of the world's reef area. The intervals between bleaching events are now shorter than the 10–15 years needed for reefs to recover, and the IPCC projected losses of 70–90% of warm-water corals at $1.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ of warming and more than 99% at $2\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

Ocean acidification ([7.19.45](/7.19.45#sec-45.5.2)) adds a second stress for organisms that build skeletons and shells of calcium carbonate, because it lowers the saturation state of seawater. Effects are documented on several groups:

- Pteropods (planktonic snails with aragonite shells, an important food of fish and whales) in the Southern Ocean and the California Current show shell dissolution where waters are undersaturated (Bednaršek et al. 2012).
- Oyster hatcheries in the Pacific Northwest of the United States suffered mass mortality of larvae in 2007–2009 when upwelled water, naturally rich in $\ce{CO2}$ and made more corrosive by anthropogenic carbon, entered the hatcheries; monitoring and buffering of the intake water restored production (Barton et al. 2012).
- Corals, coralline algae and sea urchins calcify less in experiments with elevated $\ce{CO2}$, and reefs near natural $\ce{CO2}$ seeps in Papua New Guinea have fewer structurally complex corals (Fabricius et al. 2011).
- Effects are not uniform: some phytoplankton and seagrasses benefit from more $\ce{CO2}$, and the responses of coccolithophores vary among strains. Reports of severe effects of acidification on the behaviour of reef fishes could not be replicated (Clark et al. 2020).

Interactions among stressors often matter more than single factors: in an experimental food web, elevated $\ce{CO2}$ increased production at all trophic levels, but warming cancelled the gain for herbivores and fish, because predators ate more (Goldenberg et al. 2017).

### 47.3.6. Shifting Biomes

At the scale of biomes ([7.19.41](/7.19.41)), warming moves the boundaries between vegetation types. Alpine and arctic treelines are advancing in many regions, shrubs are spreading into the tundra, and the boreal forest is suffering more fires and insect outbreaks; fires in boreal forests have released carbon at a rate equivalent to most of the biome's net uptake in some recent years. In the tropics, the combination of warming, drought and deforestation may convert parts of the Amazon rain forest to savanna, a possible tipping element. Models of Latin America under high emissions predict that about 15% of the area will change biome by 2100, half as much under low emissions, with shifts from closed to open forest and from forest to cropland (Boit et al. 2016). Because the biomes of the Anthropocene include croplands, pastures and cities, land use and climate change cannot be separated in such projections ([7.19.51](/7.19.51)).

## 47.4. The Ozone Layer

### 47.4.1. The Chapman Mechanism

About 90% of the ozone of the atmosphere is in the stratosphere, between about 15 and 35 km, with the highest concentration at 20–25 km. Although its mixing ratio never exceeds about 10 ppm, the ozone layer absorbs nearly all solar ultraviolet radiation of wavelengths below about 290 nm (UV-C and most UV-B), which damages DNA and proteins ([7.19.3](/7.19.3#sec-3.1.2)); life on land became possible only after oxygen, and with it ozone, had accumulated. The total ozone column is measured in Dobson units (1 DU corresponds to a layer of pure ozone 0.01 mm thick at standard temperature and pressure); the global mean is about 300 DU. Near the surface, ozone formed from nitrogen oxides and hydrocarbons in sunlight is a pollutant harmful to people and plants.

Chapman (1930) proposed that ozone is formed and destroyed by four reactions involving only oxygen:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{O2 + h\nu ->[\lambda < 242\,nm] 2O} &&(J_1)\\
&\ce{O + O2 + M -> O3 + M} &&(k_2)\\
&\ce{O3 + h\nu ->[\lambda < 320\,nm] O2 + O} &&(J_3)\\
&\ce{O + O3 -> 2O2} &&(k_4)
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\mathrm{M}$ is any third molecule ($\ce{N2}$ or $\ce{O2}$) that carries away the energy of the new bond.

**Proposition 47.4.1 (Chapman Steady State)** If the exchange between O and $\ce{O3}$ (reactions 2 and 3) is fast compared with the production and loss of odd oxygen (reactions 1 and 4), the steady-state concentrations satisfy

$$
\frac{[\ce{O}]}{[\ce{O3}]} = \frac{J_3}{k_2[\ce{O2}][\mathrm{M}]}, \qquad [\ce{O3}] = [\ce{O2}]\sqrt{\frac{J_1k_2[\mathrm{M}]}{J_3k_4}}.
$$

**Proof** Odd oxygen $\mathrm{O_x} = \ce{O} + \ce{O3}$ is produced by reaction 1 at rate $2J_1[\ce{O2}]$ and destroyed by reaction 4 at rate $2k_4[\ce{O}][\ce{O3}]$; reactions 2 and 3 only interconvert its two forms. Fast interconversion gives the quasi-steady state $k_2[\ce{O}][\ce{O2}][\mathrm{M}] = J_3[\ce{O3}]$, the first formula. In the steady state of odd oxygen, $J_1[\ce{O2}] = k_4[\ce{O}][\ce{O3}] = k_4[\ce{O3}]^2J_3/(k_2[\ce{O2}][\mathrm{M}])$, and solving for $[\ce{O3}]$ gives the second. $\square$

The steady state explains the existence of a layer: higher up, $J_1$ is larger but $[\ce{O2}][\mathrm{M}]$, and hence the formation of ozone, falls; lower down, the short wavelengths that dissociate $\ce{O2}$ have been absorbed. Quantitatively, however, the Chapman mechanism predicts about twice as much ozone as is observed. The missing loss is catalytic: a radical X destroys ozone in a chain and is regenerated,

$$
\ce{X + O3 -> XO + O2}, \qquad \ce{XO + O -> X + O2}, \qquad \text{net: } \ce{O + O3 -> 2O2},
$$
where X can be $\ce{OH}$ (from water vapour and methane), $\ce{NO}$ (from $\ce{N2O}$, [7.19.46](/7.19.46#sec-46.1.3)), $\ce{Cl}$ or $\ce{Br}$. Because the catalyst is not consumed, one chlorine atom can destroy of the order of $10^5$ ozone molecules before it is captured in an inactive reservoir compound such as $\ce{HCl}$ or $\ce{ClONO2}$ ([6.3](/6.3) treats chain reactions and catalysis).

### 47.4.2. Chlorofluorocarbons and the Antarctic Ozone Hole

Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), introduced in the 1930s as non-toxic, non-flammable refrigerants and later used as aerosol propellants and foam-blowing agents, are so stable that they are not removed in the troposphere; they mix into the stratosphere, where hard ultraviolet radiation releases their chlorine. Molina and Rowland (1974) predicted that this chlorine would deplete the ozone layer; with Crutzen, who had shown the role of nitrogen oxides, they received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1995.

The depletion turned out to be far more dramatic than predicted, and in an unexpected place. Farman, Gardiner and Shanklin (1985) of the British Antarctic Survey reported that the total ozone over Halley Bay in the Antarctic spring (October) had fallen by about a third since the 1960s; satellite data showed a region of depleted ozone, the ozone hole (total column below 220 DU), covering most of Antarctica each September to November, with a largest area of about 29 million km² in 2000 and 2006. The explanation involves heterogeneous chemistry. In the extreme cold of the polar winter vortex, below about −78 °C, polar stratospheric clouds form, and on their particles the reservoir compounds react to release chlorine, $\ce{ClONO2 + HCl -> Cl2 + HNO3}$, while the nitric acid is removed by sedimentation. When the Sun returns in spring, $\ce{Cl2}$ is photolyzed, and a catalytic cycle through the ClO dimer, which requires no atomic oxygen, destroys ozone rapidly (Molina and Molina 1987):

$$
\ce{2ClO + M -> Cl2O2 + M}, \quad \ce{Cl2O2 + h\nu -> Cl + ClOO}, \quad \ce{ClOO + M -> Cl + O2 + M}, \quad 2(\ce{Cl + O3 -> ClO + O2}),
$$
with net reaction $\ce{2O3 -> 3O2}$. Bromine, from halons and methyl bromide, adds a similar coupled cycle and is about 60 times more effective per atom than chlorine. The Arctic stratosphere is warmer and its vortex less stable, so Arctic depletion is smaller and variable, but in cold winters (2011, 2020) it has also been severe. The low summer ozone over the Tibetan Plateau, sometimes called the third pole, is a regional minimum caused by the elevation of the terrain and atmospheric circulation, not a hole of the Antarctic kind.

Ozone depletion increases the ultraviolet radiation reaching the surface. Under the Antarctic ozone hole, UV-B in spring doubled at times; it reduced the photosynthesis of phytoplankton near the surface and damaged the DNA of fish eggs and larvae, although the overall effect on the productivity of the Southern Ocean was a few percent. For people, more UV-B increases skin cancers and cataracts.

### 47.4.3. The Montreal Protocol and the Recovery of the Ozone Layer

The Vienna Convention (1985) and the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer (1987), strengthened by amendments in London (1990), Copenhagen (1992) and later, phased out the production of CFCs, halons, carbon tetrachloride and methyl chloroform in industrialized countries by 1996 and globally by 2010; their replacements, the HCFCs, are being phased out by 2030–2040. The Protocol is ratified by every country of the world. Because the CFCs have long atmospheric lifetimes, about 52 years for CFC-11 and 102 years for CFC-12, their concentrations decline slowly: by the compartment logic of [7.19.45](/7.19.45#sec-45.2.1), each is a single well-mixed box with mean residence time $\tau$, $\mathrm{d}B/\mathrm{d}t = E - B/\tau$, whose burden decays with time constant $\tau$ once emissions stop. Stratospheric chlorine peaked in the late 1990s, and the WMO/UNEP assessment of 2022 projected that total ozone will return to its 1980 values around 2040 at mid-latitudes, 2045 in the Arctic and 2066 over Antarctica. Signs of healing of the Antarctic ozone hole are now detectable (Solomon et al. 2016). A rise of CFC-11 emissions from eastern China after 2012, detected from atmospheric measurements, was traced to illegal foam production and stopped after 2018 (Montzka et al. 2018; Rigby et al. 2019), showing the value of independent monitoring.

![One-box models of the two main chlorofluorocarbons, dB/dt = E − B/τ, with atmospheric lifetimes of 52 years (CFC-11) and 102 years (CFC-12) and stylized emissions: growth from 1950, a plateau in the 1970s and 1980s, phase-out after the Montreal Protocol (1987) to small leakage from banks of old equipment and foams, and a "world avoided" (dotted) in which emissions kept growing by 3% per year. Because CFCs are also powerful greenhouse gases, the Montreal Protocol has been the most effective climate treaty so far: without it, model studies suggest an additional warming of more than 2 °C by 2100, part of it through the damage of increased UV radiation to plants and hence to the land carbon sink (Young et al. 2021). The Kigali Amendment (2016) extended the Protocol to HFCs, substitutes for CFCs that do not deplete ozone but are strong greenhouse gases. The success of the ozone regime rested on clear science, a small number of producers, available substitutes, and a protocol designed to be tightened as knowledge improved.

## 47.5. Planetary Boundaries

The Holocene, the last 11 700 years, has had an unusually stable climate, and it is the only state of the Earth system known to support agricultural and industrial societies. Rockström et al. (2009) proposed to define, for the main processes that regulate this state, limits within which humanity can operate safely.

**Definition 47.5.1 (Planetary Boundaries)** **Planetary boundaries** are proposed limits for nine Earth-system processes, each expressed by a control variable, set at a safe distance from thresholds or zones of increasing risk beyond which human perturbation could change the state of the Earth system in ways harmful to humans; the region within all boundaries is the safe operating space for humanity.

| Process | Control variable | Boundary | Current value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Climate change | Atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$ | 350 ppm | About 420 ppm |
| Biosphere integrity | Extinction rate (genetic); appropriation of primary production (functional) | Fewer than 10 extinctions per million species-years; 10% of preindustrial NPP | More than 100; about 30% |
| Land-system change | Forest area as % of original | 75% | About 60% |
| Freshwater change | Area with deviations of river flow (blue) and soil moisture (green) | About 10–11% of land | 16–18% |
| Biogeochemical flows | P from fresh water to ocean; industrial and intentional N fixation | 11 Tg P per year; 62 Tg N per year | 22 Tg P; 190 Tg N |
| Ocean acidification | Aragonite saturation as % of preindustrial | 80% | About 80% |
| Atmospheric aerosol loading | Difference in aerosol optical depth between hemispheres | 0.1 | About 0.08 |
| Stratospheric ozone | Total column ozone | 276 DU | About 285 DU |
| Novel entities | Chemicals and engineered materials released without adequate safety testing | Zero | Exceeded |

Values from Richardson et al. (2023). By that assessment six of the nine boundaries were transgressed: climate change, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater change, biogeochemical flows and novel entities; ocean acidification was at its boundary and a later assessment (2025) placed it beyond. Stratospheric ozone is within its boundary, thanks to the Montreal Protocol, and aerosol loading is within it globally though not in several regions. The framework has been criticized: the boundaries are partly arbitrary, several processes act regionally rather than globally (phosphorus, freshwater, aerosols), and the interactions among them are large (land-system change and climate change both affect biosphere integrity). Its value lies in making explicit that the biogeochemical changes of the previous chapters and the loss of biodiversity of the next chapters are parts of one problem, and that they must be addressed together.

## Summary

- Energy balance: effective temperature $T_{\mathrm{e}} = [(1 - \alpha)S_0/(4\sigma)]^{1/4} \approx 255\,\mathrm{K}$ against 288 K at the surface; the greenhouse effect (Definition 47.1.1) is due to infrared-absorbing gases (Definition 47.1.2) and clouds. Layer models: $T_{\mathrm{s}} = [2/(2 - \varepsilon)]^{1/4}T_{\mathrm{e}}$, $T_{\mathrm{s}} = (n + 1)^{1/4}T_{\mathrm{e}}$ (Proposition 47.1.1); Mars about 0 K, Earth 33 K, Venus more than 500 K of greenhouse warming.
- Forcing $F \approx 5.35\ln(C/C_0)$, $3.9\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ per doubling; total anthropogenic forcing about $2.7\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. Feedback parameter $\lambda \approx 1.2\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$ (Planck response minus water vapour, albedo and cloud feedbacks); ECS 3 K (2–5 K; Definition 47.1.3); TCR $\approx F_{2\times}/(\lambda + \gamma) \approx 1.8$ K (Proposition 47.1.2); warming proportional to cumulative emissions. Ice-albedo feedback can make the climate bistable (snowball Earth).
- Greenhouse gases (2023): $\ce{CO2}$ 420 ppm, $\ce{CH4}$ 1934 ppb (GWP about 28), $\ce{N2O}$ 337 ppb (GWP about 270).
- Observed: about 1.1 °C warming in 2011–2020, 2024 the warmest year; Arctic warming four times faster; more frequent hot extremes (2.8 times for a ten-year event now), heavy precipitation and droughts; sea level +0.20 m since 1901.
- Ecological responses: range shifts (17 km poleward and 11 m upward per decade) at the velocity of climate change $v = \partial_tT/\lvert\nabla T\rvert$ (Definition 47.3.1), which is high on plains and low in mountains, with persistence requiring $2\sqrt{rD} > v$; phenological mismatch (pied flycatcher); smaller body size; feminization of sea turtle populations, with a delayed population collapse in a scenario model; coral bleaching (degree heating weeks; frequent mass bleaching; 70–90% loss at 1.5 °C); effects of ocean acidification on pteropods, oyster larvae and corals; moving treelines and biomes.
- Ozone: Chapman mechanism and steady state $[\ce{O3}] = [\ce{O2}]\sqrt{J_1k_2[\mathrm{M}]/(J_3k_4)}$ (Proposition 47.4.1); catalytic destruction by $\ce{OH}$, $\ce{NO}$, $\ce{Cl}$, $\ce{Br}$; the Antarctic ozone hole by heterogeneous chemistry on polar stratospheric clouds; Montreal Protocol (1987), recovery over Antarctica around 2066 because of CFC lifetimes of 50–100 years.
- Planetary boundaries (Definition 47.5.1): nine processes; six (now seven) transgressed, ozone recovering.

## Questions

1. Compute the effective temperature of the Earth if its albedo fell from 0.30 to 0.28, and the corresponding change of surface temperature in the single-layer model with $\varepsilon = 0.77$. Compare the radiative forcing of this albedo change with that of doubling $\ce{CO2}$.

   **Answer** $T_{\mathrm{e}} \propto (1 - \alpha)^{1/4}$, so $T_{\mathrm{e}}' = 255 \times (0.72/0.70)^{1/4} = 255 \times 1.00707 \approx 256.8\,\mathrm{K}$, an increase of 1.8 K; in the single-layer model $T_{\mathrm{s}} = 1.13\,T_{\mathrm{e}}$, so the surface warms by about 2.0 K. The forcing is $\Delta\alpha\,S_0/4 = 0.02 \times 340 = 6.8\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, nearly twice the $3.9\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ of a doubling of $\ce{CO2}$. The comparison shows why albedo changes (sea ice, snow, clouds, aerosols) matter so much, and why the no-feedback warming from the layer model (here about 0.3 K per $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$) is smaller than the observed sensitivity, which includes feedbacks.

2. In the two-layer model with $\lambda = 1.3$, $\gamma = 0.7\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$, compute ECS and the approximate TCR for $F_{2\times} = 3.9\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. Why does the ratio TCR/ECS matter for policy?

   **Answer** ECS $= 3.9/1.3 = 3.0$ K; TCR $\approx 3.9/(1.3 + 0.7) = 1.95$ K, a ratio of 0.65. The difference, about 1 K, is warming "in the pipeline" that would occur over centuries if the concentration were held at the doubled value, because the deep ocean continues to warm. Holding concentrations constant therefore does not stop warming; stopping emissions roughly does, because the concentration then falls as the ocean takes up carbon, offsetting the delayed warming. The ratio also tells us how much of the long-term warming will be felt within a human lifetime.

3. Using Definition 47.3.1, estimate the climate velocity for a warming of 0.3 °C per decade (a) on a plain where temperature falls by 0.6 °C per 100 km northward, (b) on a mountain slope rising 1000 m over 10 km. If a plant species spreads 50 m per year, where can it keep pace?

   **Answer** (a) $v = 0.03\,^\circ\mathrm{C\,yr^{-1}}/(0.006\,^\circ\mathrm{C\,km^{-1}}) = 5\,\mathrm{km\,yr^{-1}}$. (b) The temperature gradient along the slope is about $6.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ per km of elevation × 0.1 km of elevation per km of distance $= 0.65\,^\circ\mathrm{C\,km^{-1}}$, so $v = 0.03/0.65 \approx 0.046\,\mathrm{km\,yr^{-1}}$, about 46 m per year. The plant cannot keep pace on the plain (it would fall behind by about 5 km each year) but can just keep pace on the slope, provided suitable habitat continues upwards and is not fragmented. Its persistence on the plain depends on refuges of cooler microclimate and on assisted migration.

4. In the turtle model, explain why the population first increases when the proportion of female hatchlings rises, and why the decline begins decades after the sex ratio of hatchlings has become extremely biased. Which parameters of the model would most change the timing of the collapse?

   **Answer** Reproduction is limited by the number of females as long as there are enough males to fertilize them (the factor $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-hM/F}$ is close to 1 for $M/F$ well above $1/h$); producing more female hatchlings therefore increases future reproduction, and the population grows. Hatchlings join the adults only after the maturation delay $\ell$ of 30 years, and adults survive for decades ($1/(1 - s_a) = 20$ years on average), so the adult sex ratio lags the hatchling sex ratio by several decades. The collapse comes when males fall below about $1/h$ of the females, so the timing depends on the rate of warming, the pivotal temperature and the width $s$ of the reaction norm, the mating capacity $h$ of males, the delay $\ell$ and adult survival $s_a$, and on adaptive responses (shifts of nesting time or place, evolution of $T_{\mathrm{p}}$) and management (shading, relocation) that are not in the model.

5. The Chapman steady state gives $[\ce{O3}] \propto [\ce{O2}]\sqrt{J_1[\mathrm{M}]/J_3}$. Explain why a catalyst that adds a loss reaction $\ce{O + O3 -> 2O2}$ with rate constant $k_{\mathrm{X}}[\mathrm{X}]$ (per unit $[\ce{O}][\ce{O3}]$) reduces ozone, and by how much if the catalytic rate equals the Chapman rate $k_4$.

   **Answer** The catalytic cycle has the same net reaction as reaction 4, so it acts as an increase of the loss coefficient from $k_4$ to $k_4 + k_{\mathrm{X}}[\mathrm{X}]$ (if X is regenerated much faster than it is removed). In the derivation of Proposition 47.4.1, $k_4$ is replaced by this sum, so $[\ce{O3}] \propto (k_4 + k_{\mathrm{X}}[\mathrm{X}])^{-1/2}$. If the catalytic term equals $k_4$, the loss coefficient doubles and ozone falls by a factor $1/\sqrt 2$, about 29%. This is roughly why the Chapman mechanism alone overestimates stratospheric ozone by about a factor of two: natural catalysts ($\ce{NO_x}$, $\ce{HO_x}$) remove more odd oxygen than reaction 4 itself. Adding chlorine from CFCs increases the loss further; in the polar spring the ClO dimer cycle, which does not need atomic oxygen, destroys ozone much faster still.
