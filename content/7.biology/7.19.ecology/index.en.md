**Ecology** is the scientific study of the distribution and abundance of organisms and of the interactions that determine them. Its questions are deceptively simple. Why does a species live here and not there? Why are there so many individuals of it, and why does their number stay nearly constant in one population and cycle every ten years in another? When can two competitors coexist, why do the tropics hold more species than the poles, and how much of the sunlight that falls on a lake ends up in its fish? The answers require two things at once: knowledge of the organisms themselves, their tolerances, resources, life cycles and enemies, and a way of reasoning about many individuals and many species together, whose collective behavior cannot be read off any one of them. Ecology supplies the second by working at a hierarchy of levels, the **population**, the **ecological community**, the **ecosystem**, the **landscape** and the **biosphere**, each defined so that its state can be measured and its change described by a small number of quantities: numbers and rates for a population, diversity and composition for a community, pools and fluxes of energy and matter for an ecosystem.

The course climbs this hierarchy, and at every level the argument is carried by mathematical models, each derived in full and tested against data. An introduction sets out the definitions and history of the discipline, its levels and scales, and its methods of observation, experiment and theory. The individual comes first: ecological factors and the **law of tolerance**, radiation, temperature, water, soil and fire, resources and **ecological stoichiometry**, the **metabolic theory of ecology**, and the molecular adaptations to stress. The population part, the longest, begins with sampling and demography and builds up the theory of a single species step by step: exponential and **logistic growth**, density dependence, discrete-time models with their cycles and chaos, time delays and the detection of regulation in time series, matrix models, stochastic extinction and **population viability analysis**, metapopulations and reaction–diffusion models of spread, followed by life histories, dormancy and migration, genetic variation and the social structure of populations. The chapters on species interactions treat competition from the **Lotka–Volterra model** to resource competition and coexistence, predation from functional responses to limit cycles and the **paradox of enrichment**, hosts and parasitoids, parasites and disease with the **basic reproduction number**, mutualism and facilitation, and finally interactions in space, where Turing instability produces pattern. The community part measures diversity, describes structure along gradients and by ordination, and asks which processes assemble communities, from competition, predation and disturbance to island biogeography, latitudinal gradients, succession, metacommunities and neutral theory, food webs and their stability, trophic cascades and regime shifts. The ecosystem part follows energy and matter through the biomes of the world: primary and secondary production, ecological efficiencies, decomposition, the cycles of water, carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur as compartment models, and the ecology of global change. Two chapters of landscape ecology treat spatial pattern, connectivity and fragmentation, and the applied part turns the whole theory to biodiversity and its threats, conservation practice, harvesting and pest management, and agroecology. A course summary reads the course once more as eight families of models and the mathematics they share.

Ecology is the last of the biology courses on this site and draws on nearly all of them, linking rather than repeating. The physiology of tolerance and metabolism comes from [plant physiology](/7.13) and [animal physiology](/7.14), the organisms themselves from [botany](/7.12) and [zoology](/7.17), and the decomposers, symbionts and microbial agents of the nutrient cycles from [microbiology](/7.11). [Evolutionary biology](/7.10) supplies the theory of life histories, niches, speciation and biogeography, and the relation runs in both directions, since character displacement, invasion fronts and the evolution of virulence are ecological and evolutionary at once; the decisions of individuals, foraging, territories and the **ideal free distribution**, come from [animal behavior](/7.18), and genetic variation within populations from [population genetics](/7.4.23). The mathematics is taken from the mathematics courses and applied in full: the phase plane, stability and bifurcations of [ordinary differential equations](/2.4), the diffusion equation and traveling fronts of [partial differential equations](/2.5), the eigenvalues of [linear algebra](/2.1) for matrix models and community matrices, Markov chains and branching processes from [probability](/3.1), epidemics on networks from [network science](/1.4.7), and regression, ordination and Monte Carlo methods from [linear regression](/3.3), [multivariate analysis](/3.4) and [statistical computing](/3.5). Several of these models, the Lotka–Volterra oscillator and the **Fisher–KPP equation**, were first met as mechanisms of patterning in [developmental biology](/7.5).

Ecology is, to me, the most logically coherent branch of biology, which may sound surprising for a subject so often taught as a catalog of examples. The reason is that, once the organisms are known, the objects of ecology are defined by the ecologist. A population, a community, an ecosystem or a landscape is not given by nature with sharp boundaries; it is delimited for the purpose of a study, and the definitions are deliberately simple, so that each level can be described by a few state variables and the rules by which they change. From the population upward almost everything can therefore be written as a model, and the models of the different levels are recognizably related: the logistic term returns in competition, predation and harvesting, the projection matrix returns as the community matrix and the compartment system, and diffusion returns in invasions, metacommunities and landscapes. This quantitative view is what anyone who wants to learn ecology systematically has to acquire. Models are not the whole of ecology, and they are only as good as the measurements against which they are tested, but without them the subject remains a collection of cases. The other half of the subject is organismal. The zoology and botany I studied are, at bottom, natural history, and in the modern world natural history leads almost inevitably to **conservation biology**. In that sense ecology is a specialized discipline of macrobiology that rests on a solid knowledge of animals and plants: a model of competition or predation means little unless one knows what the organisms in it actually do.

These pages are arranged accordingly. I first learned ecology from the *Foundations in Ecology* of Cuijuan Niu and coauthors; Ruyong Sun's *Principles of Animal Ecology* treats the mathematical models in much more detail, and I used the Chinese translation of Molles's *Ecology* as a reference book. Olympiad training covered the subject broadly, while the university course, within its limited hours, covered less ground but raised new questions. Here the classic English textbooks form the main line, Begon and Townsend above all; the models are not stated as formulas to be remembered but derived in full, with their equilibria, stability conditions and ecological meaning, and with simulations where analysis stops; and the content of the Chinese textbooks and of both courses is covered within this framework, so that every topic a reader may have met there has its place in the hierarchy of levels.
