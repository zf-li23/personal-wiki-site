Carbon enters ecosystems from the atmosphere in unlimited supply; the elements that most often limit production are nitrogen and phosphorus, and in some places iron, silicon or sulfur. Their cycles differ in a way that decides how humans have changed them. Nitrogen has an enormous atmospheric reservoir of $\ce{N2}$ that only a few microorganisms and an industrial process can tap; phosphorus has no gaseous form and comes only from rocks; sulfur has both atmospheric and lithospheric phases. This chapter follows these three cycles at the ecosystem and global scale, using the compartment language of [7.19.45](/7.19.45#sec-45.2). The microbial transformations themselves have been described in microbiology ([7.11.30](/7.11.30)), and the definitions of the **nitrogen cycle** and **nitrogen fixation** in biochemistry ([7.1.27](/7.1.27)); here the emphasis is on budgets, the human alteration of the cycles, and their consequences. We treat nitrogen (46.1), then phosphorus with two models of lake eutrophication, Vollenweider's loading model and Carpenter's model of internal loading, whose bistability explains why lakes are so hard to restore (46.2), then sulfur and acid rain (46.3), and finally the coupling of the cycles through the stoichiometry of organisms (46.4). Their global limits are part of the planetary boundaries discussed in [7.19.47](/7.19.47#sec-47.5).

## 46.1. The Nitrogen Cycle

### 46.1.1. Pools and Transformations

Nitrogen is an element of every amino acid and nucleotide, and plants need more of it than of any other mineral element ([7.13.6](/7.13.6)). The atmosphere, 78% $\ce{N2}$ by volume, is by far the largest reservoir, about $4 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{Tg\,N}$, but the triple bond of $\ce{N2}$ makes it unavailable to all organisms except diazotrophic bacteria and archaea. Nitrogen in any other form (ammonium, nitrate, nitrite, nitrogen oxides, organic nitrogen) is called reactive nitrogen.

**Definition 46.1.1 (Reactive Nitrogen)** **Reactive nitrogen** (Nr) comprises all nitrogen compounds except $\ce{N2}$: reduced inorganic forms ($\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{NH4+}$), oxidized inorganic forms ($\ce{NO}$, $\ce{NO2}$, $\ce{HNO3}$, $\ce{N2O}$, $\ce{NO3-}$, $\ce{NO2-}$) and organic compounds (urea, amines, proteins).

The cycle consists of a few transformations ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.3.1)):

- Fixation converts $\ce{N2}$ to ammonia: biologically, by **nitrogenase** in free-living bacteria and in the symbioses of legumes with rhizobia, of alders and other actinorhizal plants with *Frankia* and of cycads, *Azolla* and lichens with cyanobacteria; by lightning, which produces nitrogen oxides (a few percent of natural fixation); and industrially.
- Assimilation incorporates ammonium and nitrate into organic matter.
- Ammonification (mineralization) releases ammonium from organic nitrogen during decomposition.
- **Nitrification**, by ammonia-oxidizing archaea and bacteria and nitrite-oxidizing bacteria, oxidizes ammonium via nitrite to nitrate in aerobic soils and waters. Nitrate is mobile and leaches; ammonium is held by soil colloids.
- **Denitrification** in anoxic soils, sediments and water reduces nitrate via $\ce{N2O}$ to $\ce{N2}$; **anammox** oxidizes ammonium with nitrite to $\ce{N2}$. Both return reactive nitrogen to the atmosphere and close the cycle.

Within an ecosystem the internal cycle of uptake, litter and mineralization carries most of the nitrogen, and inputs and outputs are small (the Hubbard Brook budget of [7.19.45](/7.19.45#sec-45.3.2)); globally, natural fixation is balanced by denitrification.

### 46.1.2. The Human Alteration of the Nitrogen Cycle

Before the twentieth century, the supply of fixed nitrogen limited agriculture, and farmers depended on legumes, manure and imported guano and Chilean saltpetre. The Haber–Bosch process, introduced in 1913, synthesizes ammonia from $\ce{N2}$ and $\ce{H2}$ over an iron catalyst at about 400–500 °C and 15–30 MPa. It now fixes about $120\,\mathrm{Tg\,N}$ per year, cultivated legumes and rice another $60$, and combustion produces about $30\,\mathrm{Tg\,N}$ per year of nitrogen oxides: human-made fixation, about $210\,\mathrm{Tg\,N}$ per year, now exceeds natural fixation on land (about 60) and is comparable to all natural fixation including the ocean (Fowler et al. 2013; see the figure in [7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.3.1)). The fertilizer nitrogen of the Haber–Bosch process supports roughly half of the world's population (Erisman et al. 2008).

Only part of this nitrogen ends in food. Of the nitrogen applied to cropland worldwide, about 40% is harvested in crops (the **nitrogen use efficiency**, which was about 25% in China around 2010 and has risen since; Zhang et al. 2015); the rest is lost by leaching of nitrate, volatilization of ammonia, and denitrification, and the nitrogen in food is released again in sewage and manure. Once fixed, a nitrogen atom can pass through a sequence of forms and environments before it is denitrified: ammonia emitted from a pig farm is deposited in a forest, nitrified, leached as nitrate into a river, carried to an estuary, and partly emitted as $\ce{N2O}$ on the way, causing a different problem at each step. Galloway et al. (2003) called this sequence the nitrogen cascade.

### 46.1.3. Consequences of Excess Reactive Nitrogen

- Drinking water. Nitrate in groundwater and wells can cause methemoglobinemia in infants ("blue baby syndrome"): nitrate is reduced to nitrite in the gut, and nitrite oxidizes the iron of hemoglobin to the ferric state, so that methemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen, rises from below 1% to 20% or more, causing cyanosis; levels above about 50% can be fatal. The guideline value of the World Health Organization is $50\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$ of nitrate ($11\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$ of nitrate-nitrogen).
- **Eutrophication** of fresh and coastal waters ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.3.3)), with blooms of cyanobacteria in lakes, harmful algal blooms (red tides) in coastal seas, and hypoxic dead zones, of which more than 400 are known worldwide (Diaz and Rosenberg 2008). In lakes phosphorus is usually the primary limiting element (46.2), in estuaries and coastal seas nitrogen more often, and in many waters both.
- Soil acidification. Nitrification produces two protons per ammonium oxidized, and nitrate leaching removes base cations with it. In the main croplands of China, topsoil pH fell by about 0.5 units between the 1980s and the 2000s, mainly because of heavy nitrogen fertilization (Guo et al. 2010).
- Nitrous oxide. $\ce{N2O}$, from nitrification and incomplete denitrification in fertilized soils, is a greenhouse gas with a global warming potential of about 270 over 100 years and, since the phase-out of chlorofluorocarbons, the largest ozone-depleting substance emitted (Ravishankara et al. 2009; [7.19.47](/7.19.47#sec-47.4)).
- Acid deposition. Nitrogen oxides from combustion are oxidized to nitric acid, and ammonia from agriculture is deposited as ammonium, which acidifies soil when it is nitrified; together they now cause a large share of acid deposition (46.3).
- Loss of plant diversity. Nitrogen deposition fertilizes ecosystems adapted to low nitrogen. Fast-growing nitrophilous grasses and herbs outcompete slow-growing species of heathlands, bogs, nutrient-poor grasslands and forest floors; across British acid grasslands, species richness declined by about one species per $4\,\mathrm{m^2}$ quadrat for every $2.5\,\mathrm{kg\,N\,ha^{-1}\,yr^{-1}}$ of chronic deposition (Stevens et al. 2004). The deposition below which no harmful effects are expected is the critical load, about $5$–$15\,\mathrm{kg\,N\,ha^{-1}\,yr^{-1}}$ for the most sensitive ecosystems (Bobbink et al. 2010). In China, nitrogen deposition increased from about $13$ to $21\,\mathrm{kg\,N\,ha^{-1}\,yr^{-1}}$ between the 1980s and the 2000s (Liu et al. 2013) and has since stabilized as emissions of nitrogen oxides were controlled.

### 46.1.4. Nitrogen Deposition, Lichens and Snub-Nosed Monkeys

Epiphytic lichens obtain all their nutrients from rain, fog and dust, and many are extremely sensitive to nitrogen and sulfur pollution; they are classic bioindicators of air quality. The long beard lichen *Usnea longissima*, which hangs in long strands from the branches of old conifers in humid montane forests, is among the most nitrogen-sensitive, and in experiments simulated nitrogen deposition reduced the growth of its strands and the survival of its propagules (Jia et al. 2019). Beard lichens of the genus *Usnea* and related genera are a staple winter food of the Sichuan snub-nosed monkey (*Rhinopithecus roxellana*), when leaves are scarce in its high-altitude forests, and lichens are the main food of the Yunnan snub-nosed monkey (*R. bieti*) for much of the year. The example shows how a change in a biogeochemical cycle can travel through a food chain: increased nitrogen deposition could reduce a food resource of an endangered primate, in addition to the effects of logging of the old trees on which the lichens grow.

## 46.2. The Phosphorus Cycle

### 46.2.1. An Open, Sedimentary Cycle

Phosphorus makes up only about 1% of the mass of organisms, but it is part of nucleic acids, phospholipids, ATP and, in vertebrates, bone; in many freshwater and some terrestrial ecosystems it limits production. Phosphorus has no significant gaseous compound and no redox reactions in ecosystems: it cycles as phosphate. Its sources are the weathering of apatite and other phosphate minerals, and, over large distances, mineral dust. In soils, phosphate is strongly sorbed to iron and aluminium oxides in acid soils and precipitated with calcium in alkaline soils, so it is most available to plants at pH 6–7 ([7.19.5](/7.19.5#sec-5.4.4)); mycorrhizal fungi and root exudates help plants to obtain it ([7.13.6](/7.13.6)).

The global cycle is open: phosphorus moves from land to the sea in rivers, mostly bound to particles, and is buried in marine sediments, from which it returns to land only by tectonic uplift over tens of millions of years. Within the ocean, a phosphorus atom is taken up by phytoplankton near the surface, sinks with organic particles, is remineralized at depth and returns by upwelling and mixing, many times over; its residence time in the ocean, the oceanic stock divided by the river input or the burial, is about $10^4$–$10^5$ years (Ruttenberg 2003). Within an ecosystem, the internal cycle between plants, litter and soil is relatively tight, but on long timescales the phosphorus of a soil is depleted by leaching and converted into occluded forms unavailable to plants (46.4.3).

Human activity has roughly tripled the flux of phosphorus through the biosphere. About $20\,\mathrm{Tg\,P}$ per year are mined as phosphate rock, mostly for fertilizer, and erosion of farmland, sewage and manure carry much of it to rivers and lakes, where it causes eutrophication. Only a small reverse flux returns phosphorus from the sea to the land, in fish catches and the guano of seabirds.

### 46.2.2. Phosphate Rock, Guano and Nauru

Seabird colonies on dry tropical islands accumulate guano, rich in nitrogen and phosphorus, and over geological time guano reacting with the underlying coral limestone forms phosphate rock. Guano from the islands off Peru was the main fertilizer of nineteenth-century Europe and North America. The island of Nauru in the central Pacific, about $21\,\mathrm{km^2}$, was covered by such phosphate deposits. They were mined from 1906, first under colonial administrations and after independence in 1968 by Nauru itself, whose income per person became, for a time in the 1970s, among the highest in the world. By the 2000s the deposits were largely exhausted, about 80% of the island had been stripped to a landscape of coral pinnacles unsuitable for farming or forest, and the national economy collapsed. Nauru is an extreme example of a general point: phosphate rock is a finite, non-renewable resource, concentrated geographically (Morocco and Western Sahara hold about 70% of the known reserves), and unlike nitrogen it cannot be fixed from the air. Recovering phosphorus from sewage and manure, and keeping it on farmland instead of losing it to waters, is both an economic and an ecological necessity.

### 46.2.3. Phosphorus and the Productivity of Lakes

In most lakes phosphorus is the element that limits phytoplankton production. The classic demonstration is Schindler's whole-lake experiment in the Experimental Lakes Area of Ontario, where Lake 226 was divided by a curtain and both halves received carbon and nitrogen but only one phosphorus; only that half developed a cyanobacterial bloom ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.3.3)). When nitrogen is scarce relative to phosphorus, nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria can make up the deficit (46.4.1), so in the long run phosphorus supply controls the productivity of the lake. Phosphorus also accumulates in sediments, and from there it can be released again when the bottom water is anoxic, a process that makes eutrophication self-reinforcing (46.2.5).

Lakes are classified by trophic state according to their nutrient concentrations and productivity: oligotrophic (clear water, deep oxygenated hypolimnion, total phosphorus below about $10\,\mu\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$), mesotrophic ($10$–$35$), eutrophic ($35$–$100$) and hypereutrophic (above $100\,\mu\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$; OECD 1982). Eutrophication in the sense of nutrient enrichment by human activity is often called cultural eutrophication, to distinguish it from the slow natural enrichment of lakes as their basins fill.

### 46.2.4. The Vollenweider Loading Model

How much phosphorus can a lake receive before it becomes eutrophic? Vollenweider (1968, 1975) answered with a one-compartment mass balance of the total phosphorus in the water. Let $V$ be the volume of the lake, $A_0$ its surface area, $z = V/A_0$ its mean depth, $Q$ the outflow and $\tau_w = V/Q$ the water residence time; let $L$ be the areal loading of phosphorus (mass per unit lake area per year), $P$ the concentration of total phosphorus in the water, and assume that phosphorus settles to the sediments at a rate $\sigma P$ per unit volume. Mass balance gives $V\,\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}t = LA_0 - QP - \sigma VP$, or, dividing by $V$,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{L}{z} - (\rho + \sigma)P, \qquad \rho = \frac{1}{\tau_w}. \tag{46.1}
$$

**Proposition 46.2.1 (Vollenweider Model)** Equation (46.1) with constant parameters has the globally stable steady state

$$
P^* = \frac{L}{z(\rho + \sigma)},
$$
and after a change of loading the concentration approaches the new steady state exponentially with response time $T = 1/(\rho + \sigma)$; half of the change is completed in $T\ln 2$.

**Proof** The equation is linear with constant coefficients and negative rate constant $-(\rho + \sigma)$; its solution is $P(t) = P^* + (P(0) - P^*)\mathrm{e}^{-(\rho + \sigma)t}$. $\square$

The sedimentation coefficient $\sigma$ cannot be measured easily, and Vollenweider used empirical relations. In his 1975 form, $\sigma \approx 10/z$ per year, so that

$$
P^* = \frac{L}{10 + q_s}, \qquad q_s = \frac{z}{\tau_w},
$$
where $q_s$ (m per year) is the areal hydraulic load. In the OECD form (Vollenweider 1976), $\sigma \approx 1/\sqrt{\tau_w}$, giving $P^* = (L/q_s)/(1 + \sqrt{\tau_w})$: the inflow concentration $L/q_s$ reduced by retention in the lake. Taking $P^* = 10$ and $20\,\mathrm{mg\,m^{-3}}$ as the limits between "permissible" and "dangerous" loading, Vollenweider drew a loading diagram on which any lake can be placed and the loading reduction needed to make it mesotrophic read off. It became the basis of the phosphate bans in detergents and of tertiary sewage treatment in Europe and North America from the 1970s. Lake Washington near Seattle, whose sewage was diverted between 1963 and 1968, recovered within a few years, as expected for a lake with a residence time of about three years (Edmondson 1991).

### 46.2.5. Internal Loading and Alternative States

Many lakes did not recover as Eq. (46.1) predicts: after the external loading was reduced, phosphorus concentrations stayed high for years or decades. The reason is internal loading. In a eutrophic lake the decomposition of sinking algae consumes the oxygen of the hypolimnion; under anoxia, iron(III) oxyhydroxides in the sediment surface are reduced and release the phosphate bound to them, and the lake fertilizes itself. Carpenter, Ludwig and Brock (1999) added this positive feedback to the mass balance, as a recycling term that switches on steeply as $P$ rises:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = L - sP + r\frac{P^q}{m^q + P^q}, \tag{46.2}
$$
where $L$ is the external loading (per unit volume), $s$ the rate constant of loss by outflow and permanent burial, $r$ the maximum rate of recycling, $m$ the concentration at which recycling is half-maximal and $q \ge 1$ the steepness of the switch. Microbiology introduced this model and its hysteresis ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.3.3)); here we analyze it completely. With $x = P/m$, $\tau = st$, $a = L/(sm)$ and $\hat r = r/(sm)$ (and writing $r$ for $\hat r$ from now on), Eq. (46.2) becomes

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\tau} = a - x + rf(x), \qquad f(x) = \frac{x^q}{1 + x^q}. \tag{46.3}
$$
Equilibria are the intersections of the loss line $x$ with the supply curve $a + rf(x)$; an equilibrium is stable if the supply curve crosses the line from above, that is, if $rf'(x^*) < 1$, since the derivative of the right-hand side is $-1 + rf'(x)$. Equivalently, the equilibria are the minima of the potential $V(x) = -\int_0^x [a - y + rf(y)]\,\mathrm{d}y$, a "stability landscape" in which the state rolls downhill.

**Proposition 46.2.2 (Bistability in the Carpenter Model)** For $q > 1$, Eq. (46.3) has two stable equilibria for some loading $a$ if and only if

$$
r > r_{\mathrm{c}}(q) = \frac{4q}{(q + 1)^2}\left(\frac{q + 1}{q - 1}\right)^{(q - 1)/q}.
$$
Then bistability holds for $a$ between two fold values $a_- < a_+$ at which a stable and an unstable equilibrium merge in a **saddle-node bifurcation**. For $q \le 1$ the positive equilibrium is unique for every $a > 0$.

**Proof** The equilibria satisfy $a = g(x) = x - rf(x)$. There are three equilibria for some $a$ exactly when $g$ is not monotonic, i.e. when $g'(x) = 1 - rf'(x) < 0$ somewhere, i.e. when $r\max_x f'(x) > 1$. For $q \le 1$ the function $f$ is concave on $x > 0$, so $g$ is convex with $g(0) = 0$, and for every $a > 0$ the equation $a = g(x)$ has exactly one positive root, which is stable. For $q > 1$, $f'(x) = qx^{q-1}/(1 + x^q)^2$, and setting the derivative of $\ln f'(x) = \ln q + (q - 1)\ln x - 2\ln(1 + x^q)$ to zero gives $(q - 1)/x = 2qx^{q-1}/(1 + x^q)$, so the maximum of $f'$ is at $x^q = (q - 1)/(q + 1)$. There $1 + x^q = 2q/(q + 1)$, and

$$
\max f' = \frac{q\,x^{q-1}}{(1 + x^q)^2} = \frac{(q + 1)^2}{4q}\left(\frac{q - 1}{q + 1}\right)^{(q - 1)/q}.
$$
The condition $r\max f' > 1$ is $r > r_{\mathrm{c}}(q)$. When it holds, $g$ has a local maximum and a local minimum; their values are $a_+$ and $a_-$, and at each of them $g' = 0$, i.e. $rf'(x) = 1$, the double root of a saddle-node bifurcation; between them $a = g(x)$ has three solutions, of which the outer two have $g' > 0$ (stable) and the middle one $g' < 0$ (unstable). $\square$

For $q = 2$ the threshold is $r_{\mathrm{c}} \approx 1.54$, for $q = 8$ about $0.49$: the steeper the switch, the weaker the recycling needed to make the lake bistable. With $r = 1$ and $q = 8$, the lake has a clear and a turbid stable state for $0.39 < a < 0.66$. If the loading rises slowly past $a_+$ the lake jumps to the turbid state; reducing the loading again does not restore clear water until $a$ falls below $a_-$, far below the loading at which the lake flipped. This is **hysteresis**, the signature of **bistability** analyzed for gene circuits in [7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.2.2) and for ecosystems as a **regime shift** in [7.19.39](/7.19.39).

**Corollary 46.2.1 (Irreversible Eutrophication)** If

$$
r > q(q - 1)^{(1 - q)/q},
$$
then the turbid state persists even at zero external loading ($a_- < 0$): once eutrophic, the lake cannot be restored by reducing the external loading alone.

**Proof** At $a = 0$ a positive equilibrium satisfies $x = rf(x)$, i.e. $r = h(x) = x(1 + x^q)/x^q = x + x^{1-q}$. The function $h$ has its minimum where $h'(x) = 1 - (q - 1)x^{-q} = 0$, at $x = (q - 1)^{1/q}$, with value $(q - 1)^{1/q} + (q - 1)^{(1-q)/q} = q(q - 1)^{(1-q)/q}$. If $r$ exceeds this minimum, positive equilibria exist at $a = 0$, the upper of which is stable; the turbid branch thus extends to $a < 0$. $\square$

For $q = 8$ the threshold is about $1.46$. Lakes in this regime, typically shallow, warm lakes with phosphorus-rich sediments, need measures that act on the internal loading itself: removal of phosphorus-rich sediment by dredging, capping or precipitating phosphate with aluminium or iron salts, oxygenation of the hypolimnion, or biomanipulation of the food web ([7.19.39](/7.19.39)). Near the fold the restoring force $1 - rf'(x^*)$ approaches zero, so that the lake returns ever more slowly from small disturbances, an early-warning signal of the approaching shift. Lake Taihu, whose cyanobacterial bloom in 2007 cut off the drinking water of the city of Wuxi, and Lake Dianchi near Kunming are Chinese lakes in which internal loading has slowed recovery despite large reductions of external loading.

## 46.3. The Sulfur Cycle and Acid Rain

### 46.3.1. The Sulfur Cycle

Sulfur is part of the amino acids cysteine and methionine, of coenzymes and of iron–sulfur proteins; plants take it up as sulfate ([7.13.6](/7.13.6)). The **sulfur cycle** ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#def-30.4.1)) has atmospheric and lithospheric phases of similar magnitude. Rocks (gypsum, pyrite) and seawater sulfate are the large reservoirs; weathering supplies about half of the sulfur in rivers. Three natural processes release sulfur to the atmosphere: the production of dimethyl sulfide (DMS) from the dimethylsulfoniopropionate of marine phytoplankton, which accounts for more than 90% of the roughly $20\,\mathrm{Tg\,S}$ per year released by organisms; the release of hydrogen sulfide by sulfate-reducing bacteria in marshes, tidal flats and bogs; and volcanism, about $10\,\mathrm{Tg\,S}$ per year. In the atmosphere these compounds are oxidized to sulfate within days and return in wet and dry deposition; sulfate aerosols reflect sunlight and act as cloud condensation nuclei, so that sulfur emissions cool the climate. The CLAW hypothesis (Charlson et al. 1987) proposed that phytoplankton regulate climate through DMS and cloud formation, but the strength of this feedback has not been confirmed (Quinn and Bates 2011). In marine sediments, sulfide reacts with iron to form iron sulfides, which bury sulfur.

The human perturbation of the cycle is the combustion of coal (1–5% sulfur) and oil and the smelting of sulfide ores, which emit sulfur dioxide. Anthropogenic emissions, concentrated in industrial regions, have at times exceeded natural emissions to the atmosphere.

### 46.3.2. Acid Rain

Even unpolluted rain is acidic, because it is in equilibrium with atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$.

**Proposition 46.3.1 (pH of Unpolluted Rain)** Pure water in equilibrium with air containing $\ce{CO2}$ at partial pressure $p$ has

$$
[\ce{H+}]^2 = K_1K_0p + K_{\mathrm{w}},
$$
where $K_0$ is the Henry constant of $\ce{CO2}$ and $K_1$ the first dissociation constant of carbonic acid. At 25 °C ($K_0 = 0.0334\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,atm^{-1}}$, $\mathrm{p}K_1 = 6.35$), $p = 420 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{atm}$ gives $\mathrm{pH} = 5.60$.

**Proof** $[\ce{CO2(aq)}] = K_0p$ by **Henry's law**, and $[\ce{HCO3-}] = K_1K_0p/[\ce{H+}]$; carbonate is negligible at this pH. Electroneutrality, $[\ce{H+}] = [\ce{HCO3-}] + [\ce{OH-}] = (K_1K_0p + K_{\mathrm{w}})/[\ce{H+}]$, gives the formula. Numerically $K_1K_0p = 4.47 \times 10^{-7} \times 0.0334 \times 4.2 \times 10^{-4} = 6.27 \times 10^{-12}$, so $[\ce{H+}] = 2.5 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{pH} = 5.60$. $\square$

(At the preindustrial 280 ppm the value is 5.69; rising $\ce{CO2}$ alone has lowered the pH of clean rain by less than 0.1.)

**Definition 46.3.1 (Acid Rain)** **Acid rain** (acid precipitation) is rain, snow, fog or hail with a pH below 5.6, the value for water in equilibrium with atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$, made acidic mainly by sulfuric and nitric acids formed from emitted $\ce{SO2}$ and nitrogen oxides. Acid deposition includes the dry deposition of these gases and of acidic particles.

$\ce{SO2}$ is oxidized in the gas phase by hydroxyl radicals and in cloud droplets by hydrogen peroxide and ozone to sulfuric acid; $\ce{NO2}$ is oxidized to nitric acid. Because they are strong acids, even small concentrations dominate the pH: $10\,\mu\mathrm{eq\,L^{-1}}$ of strong acid lowers the pH of rain to about 5.0, $50\,\mu\mathrm{eq\,L^{-1}}$ to about 4.3. In the 1970s and 1980s, annual mean pH of about 4.2–4.5 was common in Scandinavia, central Europe and northeastern North America, and individual events below 3 were recorded. Acidification was first recognized in Scandinavian lakes downwind of British and central European industry, and the problem became international because acid precipitation falls hundreds of kilometres from its sources.

### 46.3.3. Effects and Recovery

Acid deposition harms ecosystems through the chemistry of soils and waters as much as through acidity itself.

- Lakes and streams on granite and other poorly buffered bedrock lost their alkalinity; when pH fell below about 5, fish populations failed, first through failed reproduction, and thousands of lakes in southern Norway and Sweden and in the Adirondack Mountains lost their fish. The proximate cause is often aluminium, which is mobilized from soil minerals at low pH (panel B of the figure) and precipitates on the gills of fish; snails, crustaceans and mayflies are among the most sensitive invertebrates.
- Soils lose base cations (calcium, magnesium), because the mobile sulfate and nitrate anions carry them away in drainage water, and aluminium replaces them on exchange sites; trees suffer from calcium deficiency and aluminium toxicity, and in central Europe and the eastern United States acid deposition contributed to the decline of spruce and sugar maple forests.
- Lichens and mosses disappear near sources of $\ce{SO2}$ (the "lichen deserts" of industrial cities); conversely, the return of epiphytic lichens to cities is a sign of recovery.

International agreements (the Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution, 1979, with its protocols) and national laws (the US Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990, with emissions trading) reduced sulfur emissions in Europe and North America by 70–90% since 1980. Rain has become much less acidic, and the chemistry of many lakes has recovered, but biological recovery lags: soils depleted of base cations recover only as fast as weathering supplies new cations, sulfate stored in soils is released for decades, and acid-sensitive species must recolonize. At Hubbard Brook, calcium addition was needed to restore the health of the forest ([7.19.45](/7.19.45#sec-45.3.2)).

In China, acid rain became severe in the 1980s and 1990s in the south and southwest, where high-sulfur coal was burned, and in the 1990s acid rain areas covered about 30–40% of the country. Since about 2006 the installation of flue-gas desulfurization in power plants, followed by controls on nitrogen oxides from 2012, reduced $\ce{SO2}$ emissions by more than 70%, and the frequency and area of acid rain fell steeply (Duan et al. 2016): in recent years acid rain areas (annual mean pH below 5.6) have covered less than 5% of the land area, mainly south of the Yangtze River. As sulfur emissions fell faster than nitrogen emissions, the ratio of sulfate to nitrate in Chinese rain has decreased, and acid rain has changed from a sulfate type to a mixed type in which nitrate and ammonium play a growing role. Soil acidification continues because of nitrogen fertilization (46.1.3), and dust rich in calcium, which neutralizes much acidity in northern China, would no longer do so if dust were controlled.

## 46.4. Interactions Among Element Cycles

### 46.4.1. Stoichiometric Coupling

The cycles of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur are coupled because organisms build their biomass in roughly fixed proportions. In the ocean, phytoplankton, and the dissolved nitrate and phosphate of deep water, have on average the atomic ratio $\mathrm{C}:\mathrm{N}:\mathrm{P} = 106:16:1$, the **Redfield ratio** ([7.19.6](/7.19.6#def-6.4.2)), which **ecological stoichiometry** extends to consumers and decomposers. Redfield (1958) asked why the ratio of nitrate to phosphate in the deep sea matches that of plankton, and suggested that the organisms set the ratio of the water, not the reverse.

The mechanism is nitrogen fixation. Denitrification in oxygen-poor waters and sediments removes nitrogen, so the deep water upwelling to the surface tends to have N:P below 16. Where nitrogen is in deficit, nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria (*Trichodesmium* and unicellular diazotrophs), which need only phosphorus from the water, have an advantage over other phytoplankton, although they grow more slowly; as they grow and are remineralized, they add nitrogen in the Redfield proportion until the deficit is nearly made up. Phosphorus, which cannot be fixed, therefore controls the total production of the ocean over long timescales (the ultimate limiting nutrient), whereas nitrogen limits production at any given moment over much of the ocean (the proximate limiting nutrient; Tyrrell 1999). The same argument explains why, in lakes, nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria bloom when phosphorus is abundant and nitrogen scarce, and why reducing nitrogen loading alone does not cure eutrophication.

### 46.4.2. Single and Co-Limitation

Liebig's **law of the minimum** states that growth is limited by the resource in shortest supply relative to need; adding any other resource has no effect. The law is a good first approximation, and the Miami model of [7.19.42](/7.19.42#sec-42.5.1) uses it. But nutrient addition experiments show that limitation is often shared. A meta-analysis of more than 600 experiments in freshwater, marine and terrestrial ecosystems found that nitrogen and phosphorus limitation were about equally common, and that adding both together increased production much more than either alone, often more than the sum of their separate effects (Elser et al. 2007; Harpole et al. 2011). Co-limitation can arise because different species of a community are limited by different elements, because one element is needed to acquire another (phosphatases that release phosphate from organic matter are proteins rich in nitrogen; nitrogenase requires iron and molybdenum), or because the supplies of the two are close to the ratio of demand. In coastal seas and many lakes, sustained reduction of both nitrogen and phosphorus is now recommended (Conley et al. 2009).

### 46.4.3. Nutrient Retention and Soil Development

Terrestrial ecosystems retain nutrients through several mechanisms that couple the cycles: plants resorb about half of the nitrogen and phosphorus of their leaves before abscission; mycorrhizal fungi ([7.13.6](/7.13.6)) take up phosphate and organic nitrogen from a large soil volume; litter of high C:N ratio immobilizes mineral nitrogen in microbial biomass ([7.19.44](/7.19.44)); and the root mats of tropical rain forests on old, leached soils capture nutrients released from litter before they can be leached, so that most of the nutrient capital of these forests is in the biomass rather than the soil ([7.19.41](/7.19.41)).

Over the development of a soil the limiting element changes. A new substrate, such as a lava flow or a glacial moraine, contains phosphorus in primary minerals but no nitrogen; nitrogen accumulates from fixation and deposition, while phosphorus is gradually leached and converted to occluded forms bound in iron and aluminium oxides (Walker and Syers 1976). On the Hawaiian chronosequence of volcanic soils from 300 years to 4.1 million years old, fertilization experiments showed that forest growth was limited by nitrogen on the youngest site, by phosphorus on the oldest, and by both on intermediate sites (Vitousek 2004). Very old landscapes, such as much of Australia, southern Africa and the Amazon basin, are therefore phosphorus-poor, and their vegetation has evolved remarkable adaptations for acquiring and conserving phosphorus (cluster roots of Proteaceae, long leaf lifespans).

### 46.4.4. Planetary Boundaries for Nitrogen and Phosphorus

The planetary boundary framework ([7.19.47](/7.19.47#sec-47.5)) includes the perturbation of the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles among the processes that regulate the state of the Earth system. For nitrogen the control variable is the industrial and intentional biological fixation of $\ce{N2}$, with a proposed boundary of $62\,\mathrm{Tg\,N}$ per year against a current value of about $190$; for phosphorus, the flow from fresh water into the ocean, with a boundary of $11\,\mathrm{Tg\,P}$ per year (to avoid widespread anoxia of the ocean) against about $22$, and a regional boundary for the flow of fertilizer phosphorus to erodible soils (Steffen et al. 2015; Richardson et al. 2023). Both boundaries are transgressed by factors of two to three. The boundaries for nitrogen and phosphorus are not set by their global availability, which is ample, but by the regional damage of their losses: eutrophication, hypoxia and the loss of biodiversity. Reducing them requires closing the cycles that agriculture has opened, by raising nitrogen use efficiency, recycling manure and sewage, and reducing waste of food.

## Summary

- Nitrogen: a huge atmospheric $\ce{N2}$ reservoir accessible only by fixation; transformations: fixation, assimilation, ammonification, nitrification, denitrification and anammox. Human fixation (Haber–Bosch about 120, crops 60, combustion 30 Tg N per year) exceeds natural terrestrial fixation; reactive nitrogen (Definition 46.1.1) cascades through air, soil and water. Consequences: nitrate in drinking water (methemoglobinemia), eutrophication and dead zones, soil acidification (topsoil pH −0.5 in Chinese croplands), $\ce{N2O}$ (greenhouse gas and ozone depletion), acid deposition, and loss of plant diversity above critical loads; nitrogen-sensitive lichens such as *Usnea longissima* link deposition to the winter food of snub-nosed monkeys.
- Phosphorus: an open sedimentary cycle without a gas phase; oceanic residence time $10^4$–$10^5$ years; mined phosphate rock is finite and concentrated (Nauru, Morocco). In lakes phosphorus usually limits production.
- Vollenweider model (Proposition 46.2.1): $\dot P = L/z - (\rho + \sigma)P$, $P^* = L/[z(\rho + \sigma)]$, response time $1/(\rho + \sigma)$; loading diagram $P^* = L/(10 + q_s)$; trophic categories by total phosphorus (10, 35, 100 µg L⁻¹).
- Carpenter model: $\dot x = a - x + rx^q/(1 + x^q)$; bistable for $r > r_{\mathrm{c}}(q)$ between two saddle-node folds (Proposition 46.2.2), with hysteresis; irreversible eutrophication if $r > q(q - 1)^{(1-q)/q}$ (Corollary 46.2.1); restoration then needs control of internal loading.
- Sulfur: mixed cycle (DMS, $\ce{H2S}$, volcanoes, rocks); unpolluted rain has pH 5.6 (Proposition 46.3.1); acid rain (Definition 46.3.1) from $\ce{SO2}$ and $\ce{NO_x}$ acidifies poorly buffered lakes and soils, mobilizes aluminium and depletes base cations; emissions controls in Europe, North America and, since 2006, China brought chemical recovery, with slower biological recovery and a shift to nitrate-dominated acidity.
- Coupling: Redfield ratio 106:16:1; nitrogen fixers keep oceanic N:P near 16, so phosphorus is the ultimate and nitrogen the proximate limiting nutrient; co-limitation by N and P is common; soils shift from N to P limitation as they age; the planetary boundaries for N and P are exceeded two- to threefold.

## Questions

1. A lake of mean depth $8\,\mathrm{m}$ has a water residence time of 2 years and receives $0.6\,\mathrm{g\,P\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$. Using the OECD form of Vollenweider's model ($\sigma = 1/\sqrt{\tau_w}$), calculate the steady-state total phosphorus and the trophic state. By how much must the loading be reduced to reach $20\,\mu\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$, and how long will it take for half of the change to occur, if the sediments do not release phosphorus?

   **Answer** $q_s = z/\tau_w = 4\,\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}$, so the inflow concentration is $L/q_s = 0.6/4 = 0.15\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-3}} = 150\,\mu\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$, and $P^* = 150/(1 + \sqrt 2) = 150/2.414 \approx 62\,\mu\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$: eutrophic. For $P^* = 20$ the loading must be $20/62 \approx 0.32$ of the present value, a reduction of about 68%, to $0.19\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}$. The response rate is $\rho + \sigma = 1/2 + 1/\sqrt 2 = 1.21\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, the response time $0.83$ years and the half-time $0.83 \ln 2 \approx 0.57$ years. If internal loading occurs, recovery will be much slower than this.

2. In the Carpenter model with $q = 2$, show that a lake with $r = 1$ responds smoothly to loading, and find the value of $r$ above which hysteresis occurs. What property of real lakes corresponds to a large $q$?

   **Answer** For $q = 2$, $r_{\mathrm{c}} = \frac{8}{9}\left(\frac{3}{1}\right)^{1/2} = \frac{8\sqrt 3}{9} \approx 1.54$. With $r = 1 < r_{\mathrm{c}}$, $g(x) = x - f(x)$ is increasing (its derivative $1 - f'(x) \ge 1 - \max f' = 1 - 0.65 > 0$), so each loading gives exactly one equilibrium, which moves continuously with $a$: no jumps, no hysteresis. Hysteresis requires $r > 1.54$. A large $q$ means that recycling switches on abruptly over a narrow range of phosphorus concentration, as happens when a small increase in productivity tips the hypolimnion from oxic to anoxic and releases iron-bound phosphate from the sediment; shallow lakes in which a switch between macrophyte-dominated clear water and phytoplankton-dominated turbid water involves several reinforcing feedbacks behave similarly.

3. Explain why rain in northern China, despite high $\ce{SO2}$ emissions in the past, was often less acidic than rain in southern China, and why the soils of southern China were more vulnerable to acid deposition.

   **Answer** Rain pH reflects the balance of acids and bases. In northern China, airborne dust from loess and deserts is rich in calcium carbonate, and agricultural ammonia is abundant; both neutralize sulfuric and nitric acids in the atmosphere, so rain pH stays near or above 5.6 even with high acid emissions (though the deposited ammonium acidifies soil when nitrified). In the south, there is little alkaline dust, high-sulfur coal was burned in the southwest, and heavy rainfall washes the acids out. Southern soils (red and yellow soils, highly weathered under a humid subtropical climate) are already acidic and poor in base cations, with low buffering capacity, so additional acid input readily mobilizes aluminium and depletes the remaining calcium and magnesium, whereas the calcareous soils of the north buffer acids effectively.

4. Using the mechanism of 46.4.1, predict what happens to the N:P ratio and to the plankton of a lake when (a) only nitrogen loading is reduced, (b) only phosphorus loading is reduced.

   **Answer** (a) Reducing nitrogen alone lowers the N:P of the supply; nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria (heterocystous species such as *Dolichospermum* and *Aphanizomenon*) gain an advantage, fix atmospheric nitrogen and make up much of the deficit, so total production, still set by phosphorus, declines little, and the plankton may shift toward bloom-forming, sometimes toxic, fixers. (b) Reducing phosphorus lowers the total production that the lake can support, including that of the fixers; the N:P of the supply rises, cyanobacterial fixers lose their advantage, and the plankton shift toward diatoms and green algae. This is why phosphorus control has been the primary strategy for lakes, although nitrogen reduction is also needed to protect downstream estuaries and coastal seas, which are more often nitrogen-limited.

5. (Open question) Phosphorus is mined, used once and largely lost to waters and sediments, whereas nitrogen can always be fixed again from the air. Discuss which of the two elements poses the greater long-term risk to food security and which the greater risk to ecosystems, and how their cycles could be closed.

   **Answer** Points to consider: nitrogen is not scarce (fixation costs energy, about 1–2% of world energy use, but the raw material is unlimited), so its risk is mainly ecological (eutrophication, $\ce{N2O}$, acidification, biodiversity loss) and climatic; phosphorus has no substitute in biology and its high-grade reserves are finite and geographically concentrated, so supply and price shocks (as in 2008) threaten food security, especially for countries without reserves, although estimates of the time to depletion range from a century to several centuries. Ecologically, phosphorus losses drive freshwater eutrophication, nitrogen losses coastal eutrophication. Closing the cycles: for both, raise use efficiency (precision fertilization, matching supply to crop demand), recycle manure and human excreta (struvite precipitation from wastewater recovers phosphorus and some nitrogen), reduce erosion (phosphorus moves with soil) and food waste, and shift diets toward less animal protein, which requires several times more nitrogen and phosphorus per unit of protein. Planetary boundaries (46.4.4) suggest that both flows must be reduced by roughly half.
