Most net primary production is never eaten alive: in forests more than 95% of it, and even in plankton communities a large part, dies and enters the pool of dead organic matter, together with the feces and bodies of consumers ([7.19.43](/7.19.43#sec-43.5.3)). Decomposition returns the carbon of this material to the atmosphere as $\ce{CO2}$ and releases its nitrogen, phosphorus and other elements in inorganic forms that plants can take up again, so that it closes the cycles of matter that photosynthesis opens. It also builds the soil: the residues of decomposition and the products of microbial resynthesis form **humus**, the largest store of organic carbon on land ([7.19.5](/7.19.5#sec-5.4.4)). This chapter describes the process (44.1), the organisms that carry it out (44.2), and the two factors that control its rate, the quality of the resource (44.3) and the physical and chemical environment (44.4). We then develop the mathematics of decomposition, from Olson's single exponential to models with many pools and a continuum of qualities, which connect the rate of decomposition to the storage of carbon in soils (44.5), and close with the soil profile (44.6) and the question of when "degradable" plastics degrade (44.7). The biochemistry of microbial decomposition and the microbial guilds are in microbiology ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.6.1), [7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.2.1)); the cycling of the released elements is the subject of [7.19.45](/7.19.45) and [7.19.46](/7.19.46).

## 44.1. The Nature of Decomposition

### 44.1.1. Definitions

When a plant takes up an inorganic nutrient and incorporates it into organic matter, the nutrient is immobilized; the reverse conversion, from organic to inorganic form, is mineralization.

**Definition 44.1.1 (Decomposition)** **Decomposition** is the gradual breakdown of dead organic matter by physical and biological agents, in which complex, energy-rich molecules are converted by decomposers and detritivores into carbon dioxide, water and inorganic nutrients, with the release of energy as heat. It combines three overlapping processes:

- fragmentation (comminution): the physical and biological breaking of dead material into smaller particles, by detritivores, freezing and thawing, wetting and drying, and abrasion;
- catabolism: the enzymatic breakdown of organic compounds, from polymers to monomers (cellulose to glucose, proteins to amino acids) and on to inorganic products ($\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{NH4+}$, phosphate), carried out mainly by bacteria and fungi;
- leaching: the purely physical removal of soluble substances (sugars, amino acids, phenolics, potassium) by water.

The three processes act together and reinforce each other: fragmentation increases the surface available to microbes, microbial activity softens tissues for detritivores, and leaching removes much of the soluble fraction of fresh litter within days. Decomposition is the counterpart of primary production: photosynthesis fixes solar energy and immobilizes nutrients; decomposition dissipates the energy as heat and mineralizes the nutrients, and a given atom of nitrogen may pass through many cycles of immobilization and mineralization.

The resources of decomposition are diverse: the bodies of dead plants and animals; parts shed during life (leaves, which form the largest single input in most terrestrial ecosystems, roots, bark, cast skins, feathers and hair); root exudates and the sloughed cells of the rhizosphere; and the feces of all animals. In soils, much of the organic matter that is decomposed was never visible litter: dead fine roots and exudates contribute at least as much as above-ground litter in many ecosystems.

### 44.1.2. Donor Control and Recycling

Organisms that use dead organic matter (saprotrophs) differ from herbivores, predators and parasites in one fundamental respect: they do not affect the rate at which their resource is produced. A lion that eats a gazelle reduces the production of gazelles; a beetle that eats a dead leaf does not affect the rate at which leaves fall. The interaction is donor-controlled (Pimm 1982): the donor (the dead matter) controls the density of the recipient, not the reverse. There is an indirect positive feedback, since decomposers release the nutrients on which plant production, and thus the supply of litter, depends; this is the most important role of decomposers in ecosystems.

The decomposer subsystem has the same trophic structure as the grazer subsystem, with consumers of decomposers (microbivores), predators and their predators, but it is a recycling system. The feces, dead bodies and secretions of decomposers and detritivores return to the pool of dead organic matter at its base and are processed again, so that, unless material is exported, buried or protected, all the energy that enters dead organic matter is eventually dissipated as heat after one or several passages (Proposition 43.5.1 of [7.19.43](/7.19.43#sec-43.5.3)). Where decomposition is incomplete because of cold, waterlogging or acidity, organic matter accumulates: as peat in bogs, as thick litter layers in boreal forests, and, over geological time, as coal and oil.

## 44.2. Decomposer Organisms

### 44.2.1. Bacteria, Archaea and Fungi

Bacteria, archaea and fungi carry out most of the catabolism, because they produce the extracellular enzymes that depolymerize cellulose, hemicellulose, chitin, proteins and, in the case of fungi, lignin ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.6.1)). Freshly fallen litter is colonized first by fungi and bacteria that use soluble sugars and amino acids (often already present on the living leaf), then by species that degrade cellulose and hemicellulose, and finally by basidiomycete white-rot fungi that degrade lignin by oxidative enzymes; this sequence is a microbial succession. Fungi dominate the decomposition of plant litter in terrestrial and acid soils, because their hyphae penetrate tissues and bridge air-filled pores and they can translocate nitrogen from the soil into nitrogen-poor litter; bacteria dominate in moist, near-neutral soils, in aquatic sediments and in the decomposition of animal tissue. Under anoxic conditions, in waterlogged soils, sediments and guts, organic matter is degraded by a chain of fermenting bacteria, syntrophs and methanogens, more slowly and with methane as an end product ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.2.2)).

### 44.2.2. Detritivores

**Definition 44.2.1 (Detritivore)** A **detritivore** is an animal that feeds on dead organic matter (detritus) together with the microorganisms growing on it. Decomposers in the narrow sense are the bacteria, archaea and fungi that digest dead matter externally; microbivores are small animals that feed selectively on these microorganisms rather than on the detritus.

On land, detritivores are classified by body width, which determines whether they can move through litter and soil pores and fragment tissues (Swift et al. 1979):

| Size class | Body width | Examples | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microfauna | below 100 µm | Protozoa, nematodes, rotifers | Live in water films; mainly microbivores, regulate microbial populations |
| Mesofauna | 100 µm–2 mm | Mites, springtails, enchytraeid pot worms | Feed on fungal hyphae and partly decomposed litter; process the feces of larger animals |
| Macrofauna and megafauna | 2–20 mm and above | Woodlice, millipedes, earthworms, termites, snails, fly and beetle larvae | Fragment litter, mix it into the soil and build soil structure |

Detritivores are astonishingly numerous: a square meter of temperate woodland soil may contain a thousand species of animals, more than ten million nematodes and protozoa, about a hundred thousand mites and springtails and fifty thousand other invertebrates. Earthworms are the most important macrofauna in temperate soils. Darwin (1881) estimated that in pastures near his home they brought more than 50 t of soil per hectare to the surface each year as casts; they bury and mix litter, create burrows that aerate and drain the soil, and accelerate the conversion of litter into humus and the mineralization of nitrogen. In a meta-analysis of 58 studies, the presence of earthworms increased crop yields by 25% on average, most where crop residues were returned to the soil (van Groenigen et al. 2014). Earthworms are absent from very acid soils and dry regions; in the tropics and savannas termites take over the role, decomposing a large fraction of wood and litter with the help of gut symbionts or, in the fungus-growing Macrotermitinae, of cultivated *Termitomyces* fungi ([7.19.29](/7.19.29)).

In streams and lakes, invertebrate detritivores are classified by how they feed, into the functional feeding groups of [7.19.41](/7.19.41#sec-41.4.2): shredders fragment coarse particulate organic matter (leaves), collectors gather or filter fine particles, and grazers scrape the organic film on stones. Leaves that fall into a stream lose a quarter or more of their mass by leaching within days, are colonized by aquatic hyphomycete fungi that make them more nutritious ("conditioning"), and are then shredded; the feces and fragments of the shredders feed the collectors downstream. The seabed has its own shredders (amphipods and other crustaceans) and many filter and deposit feeders.

The relative importance of microbes and animals is measured with litter bags of different mesh size: fine mesh excludes the macrofauna, coarse mesh admits it, and the difference in mass loss is attributed to the animals. Such experiments show that detritivores increase decomposition rates by roughly a third on average, and much more in wet tropical forests and in streams with abundant shredders; the effect of the soil fauna is largest in warm, humid climates (García-Palacios et al. 2013). The relative contributions of the size classes also change with latitude: macrofauna dominate in tropical forests, mesofauna in temperate forests, and microfauna in tundra and boreal soils, where large animals are scarce and contribute little to decomposition (Swift et al. 1979).

### 44.2.3. Feces and Carrion

The feces of herbivores and carnivores are rich resources that are quickly exploited by specialists. Dung beetles (Scarabaeidae) bury or roll balls of dung, in which they lay their eggs; in African savannas a fresh pile of elephant dung may attract thousands of beetles within hours. When cattle were introduced to Australia, whose native dung beetles are adapted to the dry, fibrous pellets of marsupials, cattle pats remained undecomposed on the pastures for months, smothering the grass and breeding pestilent bush flies. From 1965 to 1985 the Australian Dung Beetle Project introduced about 50 species of dung beetles from Africa and Europe, of which about 23 became established and now bury dung and reduce fly breeding in much of the country.

Carrion, the bodies of dead animals, is consumed first by scavengers where they are present (vultures, hyenas, crows, carrion beetles), and otherwise by a succession of insects and microbes. Blowflies (Calliphoridae) arrive within minutes to hours and lay eggs; their larvae, together with those of flesh flies (Sarcophagidae) and house flies, consume most of the soft tissue within days to weeks in warm weather. Predatory rove beetles (Staphylinidae) and clown beetles (Histeridae) arrive to feed on the larvae; burying beetles (*Nicrophorus*, Silphidae) bury small carcasses as food for their brood, which both parents tend ([7.18.31](/7.18.31)); and in the dry stages hide beetles (Dermestidae) and clothes moths (Tineidae), which can digest keratin, remove skin, hair and feathers. Because each stage follows the last predictably and insect development depends on temperature, the stage of succession and the age of the oldest larvae, converted with accumulated degree-days, allow the time since death to be estimated, the basis of **forensic entomology**.

## 44.3. Resource Quality

### 44.3.1. Chemical and Physical Composition

The rate at which litter decomposes depends first on its chemistry. The components of plant tissue decompose at very different rates: sugars, starch and proteins in days to weeks (simple sugars lose about 99% of their mass in a year); hemicellulose and cellulose in months to a few years; lignin, a complex aromatic polymer that only some fungi can degrade, over years to decades. Litter rich in lignin decomposes slowly, and lignin also slows the decomposition of the cellulose it encrusts. Nitrogen and phosphorus are needed by the decomposers, so litter poor in nutrients, with a high C:N ratio (wheat straw about 80–100, pine needles 60–100, sawdust above 400), decomposes more slowly than nutrient-rich litter (legume leaves 15–25). Across many litters, the ratio of lignin to nitrogen at the start of decomposition is a good single predictor of the decomposition constant: the higher the lignin:N ratio, the lower $k$ (Melillo et al. 1982). Secondary compounds that defend leaves against herbivores, such as tannins and other **phenolics**, also slow decomposition by inhibiting microbial enzymes and binding proteins. Physically, tough leaves with thick cuticles and much sclerenchyma resist fragmentation and colonization.

These properties are inherited from the living plant: species of fertile habitats with fast growth and short-lived, nutrient-rich leaves produce litter that decomposes quickly, whereas species of poor habitats with long-lived, tough, defended leaves produce slowly decomposing litter, so that plant traits, decomposition and soil fertility reinforce each other. Litter also tends to decompose faster in the habitat where it was produced than elsewhere ("home-field advantage"), presumably because the local decomposer community is adapted to it.

### 44.3.2. Nitrogen Immobilization and the Critical C:N Ratio

Microorganisms have a C:N ratio of about 5–10, much lower than most plant litter. When they decompose nitrogen-poor litter, they need more nitrogen than the litter supplies and take up inorganic nitrogen from the soil, immobilizing it; nitrogen is released (net mineralization) only when enough carbon has been respired that the C:N ratio of the remaining material has fallen. A simple stoichiometric model makes this precise. Suppose decomposers consume litter carbon at the rate $F$, incorporate a fraction $e$ of it into their biomass (the carbon use efficiency) and respire the rest, and that their biomass has the C:N ratio $r_{\mathrm{m}}$ while the litter has the C:N ratio $r_{\mathrm{L}}$.

**Proposition 44.3.1 (Critical C:N Ratio)** The net rate of nitrogen mineralization is

$$
M = F\left(\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{L}}} - \frac{e}{r_{\mathrm{m}}}\right),
$$
which is positive (net mineralization) if $r_{\mathrm{L}} < r^* = r_{\mathrm{m}}/e$ and negative (net immobilization of soil nitrogen) if $r_{\mathrm{L}} > r^*$.

**Proof** The litter consumed supplies nitrogen at the rate $F/r_{\mathrm{L}}$. The decomposers build biomass carbon at the rate $eF$, which requires nitrogen at the rate $eF/r_{\mathrm{m}}$. If the decomposer biomass keeps its composition, the difference between supply and requirement is released as inorganic nitrogen (if positive) or taken from the soil (if negative). The sign changes at $1/r_{\mathrm{L}} = e/r_{\mathrm{m}}$. $\square$

With $r_{\mathrm{m}} = 8$ and $e = 0.3$–$0.4$, the critical C:N ratio is about 20–27, in agreement with the empirical rule that litter with C:N above about 25–30 immobilizes nitrogen. This is why plowing in straw can cause temporary nitrogen deficiency in a crop, why the nitrogen content of decomposing litter at first rises (immobilization) before it falls, and why the C:N ratio of soil organic matter converges towards about 10–15. The same reasoning applies to phosphorus, and it is the decomposer version of the threshold elemental ratio of consumers ([7.19.6](/7.19.6#sec-6.4.3)).

## 44.4. The Physicochemical Environment

Decomposition is a biological process and responds to the same conditions as other metabolism. It increases with temperature, roughly doubling with each 10 °C rise (a **temperature coefficient** $Q_{10}$ of about 2, often higher at low temperatures), as long as there is enough moisture. It is limited by drought, because microbial activity stops as water films in the soil break, and by waterlogging, because oxygen diffuses $10^4$ times more slowly in water than in air and anaerobic decomposition is slow; it is therefore fastest at intermediate moisture. It is slow in very acid soils, where bacteria and earthworms are suppressed, and on infertile soils. Repeated wetting and drying and freezing and thawing promote fragmentation and leaching. Climate and litter quality together account for most of the variation in decomposition rates across the world: in a ten-year experiment with standard litters at 28 sites in North and Central America, from tundra to tropical forest, mass loss was predicted well by a climate decomposition index combining temperature and moisture, together with initial litter chemistry (Gholz et al. 2000; Parton et al. 2007).

The resulting latitudinal pattern is striking. From the tropics to the tundra, the rate of litter decomposition falls by two orders of magnitude, while the amount of organic matter that accumulates on and in the soil rises. A classic comparison of the main biomes (Swift et al. 1979), in which the decomposition constant is estimated as the ratio of annual litter input to the litter on the ground (44.5.2), shows:

| Ecosystem | Litter input $L$ / $\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}\,yr^{-1}}$ | Litter on the ground $X_{\mathrm{ss}}$ / $\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ | $k = L/X_{\mathrm{ss}}$ / $\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ | Time to 95% decomposition $3/k$ / yr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tundra | 1.5 | 44 | 0.03 | 100 |

The values are rounded and come from early studies; in particular, litter input in tropical rain forests is now usually measured at about $8$–$15\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}\,yr^{-1}}$, which with a similar litter layer gives $k$ of about 2–4 per year. The order of magnitude, and the pattern, are robust: in a tropical rain forest the litter of one year is largely gone within a year, nutrients are returned to the roots almost as soon as they are released, and the soil holds relatively little organic matter; in the tundra and boreal forest, litter accumulates for decades or centuries, nutrients are locked in organic matter, and production is limited by the slow release of nitrogen ([7.19.41](/7.19.41#sec-41.3.5)).

## 44.5. The Mathematics of Decomposition

### 44.5.1. Olson's Negative Exponential Model

The simplest assumption, introduced by Jenny et al. (1949) and Olson (1963), is that a constant fraction of the remaining dead matter is decomposed per unit time. For a cohort of litter of initial mass $X_0$,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t} = -kX, \qquad X(t) = X_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-kt},
$$
where $k$ ($\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$) is the decomposition constant. The time to lose half of the mass is $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k \approx 0.693/k$, and the time to lose 95% is

$$
t_{0.95} = \frac{\ln 20}{k} \approx \frac{3}{k},
$$
the "3/k rule". The constant is estimated from litter bags: bags of a known mass of litter are placed in the field and retrieved at intervals, and $k$ is the slope of $\ln(X/X_0)$ against time. Typical values range from more than 2 per year for leaves in tropical forests to below 0.05 per year for conifer needles and wood in cold climates.

### 44.5.2. Accumulation and Steady State

Suppose litter falls at a constant rate $L$ (mass per area per year) onto a forest floor that already holds $X_0$. Then

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t} = L - kX.
$$

**Proposition 44.5.1** The solution is

$$
X(t) = \frac{L}{k} + \left(X_0 - \frac{L}{k}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-kt},
$$
which converges monotonically to the steady state $X_{\mathrm{ss}} = L/k$. Starting from $X_0 = 0$, the litter layer reaches 95% of its steady state after $3/k$ years. At steady state, the mean residence time of litter is $X_{\mathrm{ss}}/L = 1/k$, and the ages of the litter present are exponentially distributed with mean $1/k$.

**Proof** The equation is linear with constant coefficients; the function $Y = X - L/k$ satisfies $\mathrm{d}Y/\mathrm{d}t = -kY$, so $Y(t) = Y_0\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$, which gives the solution and its monotone convergence. With $X_0 = 0$, $X(t)/X_{\mathrm{ss}} = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-kt} = 0.95$ when $\mathrm{e}^{-kt} = 0.05$, that is $t = \ln 20/k \approx 3/k$. At steady state the stock is $L/k$ and the throughput $L$, so the residence time is $1/k$. Litter that fell $a$ years ago has decayed to the fraction $\mathrm{e}^{-ka}$, so the mass of litter of age $a$ to $a + \mathrm{d}a$ is $L\mathrm{e}^{-ka}\,\mathrm{d}a$; normalized by $X_{\mathrm{ss}} = L/k$, this is the exponential density $k\mathrm{e}^{-ka}$, with mean $1/k$. $\square$

The proposition gives Olson's method for estimating $k$ without litter bags: in a forest at steady state, $k = L/X_{\mathrm{ss}}$, the ratio of annual litterfall to the litter on the ground, used in the table of 44.4. It also shows that the stock of dead organic matter is proportional to the input and to the residence time: a tropical forest with high litterfall but $k \approx 3$ has a thin litter layer, a boreal forest with modest litterfall but $k \approx 0.1$ a thick one. The concepts of residence time and steady state of a pool are generalized to the compartments of the biogeochemical cycles in [7.19.45](/7.19.45).

### 44.5.3. Several Pools

Litter bag data rarely follow a single exponential: mass is lost quickly in the first weeks, as soluble compounds are leached and labile compounds consumed, and slowly afterwards. A two-pool model treats litter as a labile fraction $f$ decomposing at rate $k_1$ and a recalcitrant fraction $1 - f$ at rate $k_2 \ll k_1$:

$$
X(t) = X_0\left[f\,\mathrm{e}^{-k_1t} + (1 - f)\,\mathrm{e}^{-k_2t}\right].
$$
On a logarithmic scale the curve has two straight segments, steep at first and shallow later. Soil organic matter is modeled in the same way with more pools and with transfers between them. In the CENTURY and RothC models, plant residues are split into metabolic and structural fractions, which feed an active pool of microbial biomass and labile compounds (turnover times of months to a few years), a slow pool of physically and chemically protected material (decades) and a passive pool of highly stabilized material bound to minerals (centuries to millennia). The radiocarbon age of soil organic matter, often hundreds to thousands of years in deep horizons, shows that the passive pool is real, while most of the annual flux of $\ce{CO2}$ from soil comes from the active pool. Modern views attribute the persistence of soil carbon less to intrinsic chemical recalcitrance than to physical protection inside aggregates and association with clay and iron and aluminum oxides, which make organic matter inaccessible to microbes and their enzymes.

### 44.5.4. A Continuum of Qualities

Instead of a few pools, the litter can be thought of as a mixture of compounds with a continuous distribution of decomposition constants (Bosatta and Ågren 1991). If each component decays exponentially at its own rate $k$, the mass remaining is the average of $\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$ over the distribution of $k$.

**Proposition 44.5.2** If the decomposition constants of the components of a litter have a gamma distribution with shape $\alpha > 0$ and rate $\beta > 0$ (mean $\bar{k} = \alpha/\beta$), the mass remaining is

$$
\frac{X(t)}{X_0} = \left(1 + \frac{t}{\beta}\right)^{-\alpha},
$$
and the apparent decomposition constant $k_{\mathrm{app}}(t) = -\mathrm{d}\ln X/\mathrm{d}t = \alpha/(\beta + t)$ decreases with time, from the mean $\bar{k}$ at $t = 0$ towards zero.

**Proof** The mass remaining is $X_0\int_0^\infty \mathrm{e}^{-kt}\,\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}k^{\alpha - 1}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta k}\,\mathrm{d}k = X_0\,\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^\infty k^{\alpha - 1}\mathrm{e}^{-(\beta + t)k}\,\mathrm{d}k = X_0\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta + t}\right)^\alpha$, using $\int_0^\infty k^{\alpha - 1}\mathrm{e}^{-sk}\,\mathrm{d}k = \Gamma(\alpha)/s^\alpha$. Then $\ln X = \ln X_0 - \alpha\ln(1 + t/\beta)$ and $k_{\mathrm{app}} = \alpha/(\beta + t)$, which equals $\alpha/\beta$ at $t = 0$ and decreases monotonically. $\square$

The model explains why decomposition slows as litter ages, without assuming discrete pools: the fast components are used up first, and the material that remains is dominated by ever slower components. The decay follows a power law at long times ($X \propto t^{-\alpha}$), so that a small fraction of the litter persists for a very long time. When the variance of $k$ is small ($\alpha \to \infty$ with $\alpha/\beta$ fixed), the formula tends to Olson's exponential $\mathrm{e}^{-\bar{k}t}$. The same reasoning underlies the "reactivity continuum" models of organic matter decay in marine sediments.

### 44.5.5. Temperature and the Soil Carbon Feedback

If decomposition constants depend on temperature as $k(T) = k_{\mathrm{ref}}\,Q_{10}^{(T - T_{\mathrm{ref}})/10}$, warming by $\Delta T$ multiplies all of them by $q = Q_{10}^{\Delta T/10}$. By Proposition 44.5.1, the steady-state stock of a pool with constant input falls from $L/k$ to $L/(qk)$, a loss of the fraction $1 - 1/q$; for $Q_{10} = 2$ and $\Delta T = 2\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $q = 1.15$ and the eventual loss is 13% of the soil carbon, although the loss from the slow and passive pools takes decades to centuries (panel C of the figure in 44.5.4). With about 1500 Pg of organic carbon in the top meter of the world's soils, even a few percent is a large release relative to the atmosphere's 880 Pg, which is why the temperature sensitivity of decomposition is one of the key uncertainties of climate projections ([7.19.47](/7.19.47)). Whether stable soil carbon is as temperature-sensitive as labile carbon, and how much the response is offset by increased plant production and litter input, remains debated; the release from thawing permafrost is a special case in which the effective $k$ jumps from nearly zero to a finite value ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.2.1)).

### 44.5.6. The Priming Effect

Pools do not always decompose independently. The addition of fresh, energy-rich organic matter (root exudates, litter, sugars) often accelerates the decomposition of older soil organic matter, a phenomenon called the priming effect (Kuzyakov et al. 2000). Microbes stimulated by the fresh carbon produce enzymes that also attack old organic matter, and, when nitrogen is scarce, they "mine" the old organic matter for nitrogen. Priming can be measured with isotopically labeled additions, which separate the $\ce{CO2}$ derived from the added material from that derived from the soil; it may increase the decomposition of native soil carbon by tens of percent. The effect means that the input $L$ and the decomposition constants of slow pools are not independent, as the linear models above assume: higher plant production under elevated $\ce{CO2}$, by increasing root exudation, may accelerate the loss of old soil carbon, and models that include microbial biomass explicitly can reproduce such behavior.

## 44.6. The Soil Profile and Humus

The progress of decomposition can be read in an undisturbed soil profile. On top lies the organic horizon O, subdivided into a litter layer of recognizable recently fallen leaves (L or Oi), a fermentation layer of fragmented, partly decomposed litter permeated by fungal hyphae (F or Oe), and a humified layer of amorphous organic matter (H or Oa). Below lie the mineral horizons: the A horizon, a dark topsoil in which humus is mixed with mineral particles; in acid, leached soils an eluvial E horizon from which clay, iron and humus have been washed out; the B horizon, in which they accumulate; the C horizon of weathered parent material; and the bedrock R ([7.11.29](/7.11.29#sec-29.7.1)).

The form of the humus layer reflects the decomposer community. Mull, typical of deciduous forests and grasslands on base-rich soils with abundant earthworms, has a thin or seasonal litter layer and organic matter thoroughly mixed into a crumbly A horizon; mor, typical of boreal and heathland soils that are acid and poor in nutrients, where fungi dominate and earthworms are absent, has a thick, sharply delimited layer of F and H material resting on a bleached E horizon; moder is intermediate, its organic layer consisting largely of the feces of mites and springtails. Humus itself, the stable product of decomposition and microbial resynthesis ([7.19.5](/7.19.5#sec-5.4.4)), holds water and cations and releases nutrients slowly; its formation depends on the same factors as decomposition, and the balance between input and decomposition (Proposition 44.5.1) explains why grassland and boreal soils, with their slow decomposition, hold more organic carbon per unit area than tropical soils.

## 44.7. When Do "Degradable" Plastics Degrade?

The same principles apply to synthetic materials. Conventional plastics (polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, PET) are not decomposed at significant rates by any organisms, because their carbon–carbon backbones and high crystallinity offer no point of attack to extracellular enzymes; they break up physically into microplastics, which persist and spread through food webs ([7.19.54](/7.19.54)). "Biodegradable" plastics are polymers that microorganisms can in principle mineralize, but their decomposition constant, like that of litter, depends on the environment.

- Polylactic acid (PLA), the most widely used bio-based plastic, degrades quickly only in industrial composting, at about 58 °C and high humidity, above its glass transition temperature (about 55–60 °C), where hydrolysis makes the chains accessible to microbes. Standards for compostable packaging (such as EN 13432) require 90% conversion to $\ce{CO2}$ within six months under these conditions. In soil, fresh water or the sea at ambient temperature, PLA can persist for years.
- Blends of PLA with polybutylene adipate terephthalate (PBAT), common in shopping bags, behave similarly; polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA), produced by bacteria as storage compounds ([7.11.3](/7.11.3)), are among the few that degrade in soil and seawater.
- "Oxo-degradable" plastics, conventional polymers with additives that make them fragment in light and heat, do not biodegrade but simply produce microplastics faster; the European Union banned them in its 2019 directive on single-use plastics, with effect from 2021.

The lesson is that "degradable" is not a property of the material alone but of the material together with the decomposer community, temperature, moisture and oxygen, as for any dead organic matter. A plastic that is compostable in an industrial facility behaves, if it ends up in a cold ocean, more like the litter of the tundra than that of a tropical forest.

## Summary

- Decomposition (Definition 44.1.1) combines fragmentation, catabolism and leaching; it mineralizes nutrients that photosynthesis immobilized and dissipates the energy of dead organic matter as heat. Decomposer–resource interactions are donor-controlled, and the decomposer subsystem recycles its own feces and bodies until the energy is used up; incomplete decomposition builds peat and soil organic matter.
- Decomposers (bacteria, archaea, fungi) carry out most catabolism; detritivores (Definition 44.2.1) fragment and mix litter: microfauna, mesofauna and macrofauna on land, functional feeding groups in water. Earthworms and termites are key ecosystem engineers of soils; dung beetles and carrion insects form specialized communities, the succession on carrion being used in forensic entomology.
- Resource quality: sugars > hemicellulose, cellulose >> lignin; high C:N, lignin:N and phenolics slow decomposition. Decomposers immobilize soil nitrogen when the litter C:N exceeds the critical ratio $r_{\mathrm{m}}/e \approx 20$–$30$ (Proposition 44.3.1).
- Environment: decomposition increases with temperature ($Q_{10} \approx 2$) and is fastest at intermediate moisture; the time to 95% decomposition increases from about half a year in tropical rain forest to about a century in tundra.
- Mathematics:

| Model | Equation | Key results |
|---|---|---|
| Olson, single cohort | $\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = -kX$ | $X = X_0\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$; $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k$; $t_{0.95} \approx 3/k$ |
| Constant input | $\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = L - kX$ | $X_{\mathrm{ss}} = L/k$; $k = L/X_{\mathrm{ss}}$; residence time $1/k$ (Proposition 44.5.1) |
| Two or more pools | $X = X_0[f\mathrm{e}^{-k_1t} + (1 - f)\mathrm{e}^{-k_2t}]$ | Fast initial loss, slow persistent fraction; soil carbon pools with turnover from years to millennia |
| Continuum (gamma) | $X/X_0 = (1 + t/\beta)^{-\alpha}$ | Apparent $k = \alpha/(\beta + t)$ declines with age; power-law tail (Proposition 44.5.2) |
| Warming | $k \to qk$, $q = Q_{10}^{\Delta T/10}$ | Steady-state stock falls by the fraction $1 - 1/q$ |

- Fresh inputs can accelerate the decomposition of old soil organic matter (priming). The O, A, E, B and C horizons and the mull, moder and mor humus forms record the progress of decomposition. "Degradable" plastics degrade only where the conditions allow, PLA mainly in industrial composting.

## Questions

1. Litter bags with 10.0 g of oak leaves retain 7.4 g after 6 months and 5.5 g after one year. Estimate $k$ by Olson's model, the half-life and the time to 95% decomposition. Does a single exponential fit the data?

   **Answer** From the first interval, $k = \ln(10.0/7.4)/0.5 = 0.301/0.5 = 0.60\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$; from the whole year, $k = \ln(10.0/5.5)/1 = 0.60\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$. The two estimates agree, so a single exponential fits these two points (more points, especially in the first weeks and after several years, would probably reveal a fast initial and a slow late phase). Half-life $= 0.693/0.60 \approx 1.2$ years; time to 95% loss $\approx 3/0.60 = 5$ years.

2. In a boreal forest, annual litterfall is $3.0\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ and the forest floor holds $45\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ of organic matter. Estimate $k$, the mean residence time of the organic matter and the time a new forest floor would take to reach 95% of this amount after a fire that burned it completely. Why is this an overestimate of the decomposition rate of the litter itself?

   **Answer** $k = L/X_{\mathrm{ss}} = 3.0/45 = 0.067\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, mean residence time $1/k = 15$ years, and 95% of the steady state is reached after $3/k = 45$ years (assuming constant litterfall, which in reality is low in the young stand after the fire, so the recovery is slower). The estimate treats the forest floor as a single pool. In fact fresh litter decomposes faster than the humified material of the F and H layers, which has a much lower $k$; the single $k$ is an average weighted by stock. Moreover, part of the litter input is not decomposed on the forest floor but transported into the mineral soil (by animals and leaching) or burned, so the true decomposition constant of the material remaining on the floor differs from $L/X_{\mathrm{ss}}$.

3. Microorganisms with a C:N ratio of 8 and a carbon use efficiency of 0.35 decompose wheat straw (C:N 90) and clover leaves (C:N 15). For each, compute the net N mineralization per gram of litter carbon decomposed and explain the practical consequences for a farmer who plows the residues into the soil.

   **Answer** By Proposition 44.3.1, $M/F = 1/r_{\mathrm{L}} - e/r_{\mathrm{m}} = 1/r_{\mathrm{L}} - 0.35/8 = 1/r_{\mathrm{L}} - 0.0438$. Straw: $1/90 - 0.0438 = 0.0111 - 0.0438 = -0.033\,\mathrm{g\,N}$ per gram of carbon decomposed: the microbes immobilize about 33 mg of soil nitrogen per gram of straw carbon. Clover: $1/15 - 0.0438 = 0.0667 - 0.0438 = +0.023$: about 23 mg N is released per gram of carbon. The critical C:N is $8/0.35 \approx 23$. Plowing in straw can therefore deprive the next crop of nitrogen for weeks to months unless nitrogen fertilizer is added; plowing in clover (a green manure) supplies nitrogen. Over the longer term, as the straw carbon is respired, the C:N of the residue falls below the critical value and the immobilized nitrogen is released again.

4. Show that for the gamma-continuum model the time to lose half of the litter is $t_{1/2} = \beta(2^{1/\alpha} - 1)$, and compare it with $\ln 2/\bar{k}$ for $\alpha = 0.8$, $\beta = 0.6\,\mathrm{yr}$. What does the comparison say about using early decay rates to predict long-term storage?

   **Answer** Setting $(1 + t/\beta)^{-\alpha} = 1/2$ gives $1 + t/\beta = 2^{1/\alpha}$, so $t_{1/2} = \beta(2^{1/\alpha} - 1)$. For $\alpha = 0.8$, $\beta = 0.6$: $2^{1.25} = 2.378$, $t_{1/2} = 0.6 \times 1.378 = 0.83$ years. The initial mean decomposition constant is $\bar{k} = \alpha/\beta = 1.33\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, which in a single exponential would give $t_{1/2} = 0.693/1.33 = 0.52$ years. The time to lose 95% is even more different: the continuum gives $\beta(20^{1/0.8} - 1) = 0.6 \times (42.3 - 1) \approx 25$ years, the exponential $3/1.33 = 2.3$ years. Early decay rates, dominated by the fast components, greatly overestimate the rate at which the last fractions disappear, and so underestimate long-term storage of carbon.

5. (Open question) A company advertises a "fully biodegradable" PLA cup. Using the concepts of this chapter, explain under which conditions the claim is true, what happens to the cup in a city park, a landfill and the sea, and how you would test the claim experimentally.

   **Answer** Points to include: (a) PLA is mineralized by microbes only after hydrolysis, which is fast only above its glass transition temperature (about 55–60 °C) and in moist conditions, as in industrial composting; there it meets standards such as 90% conversion to $\ce{CO2}$ in six months; (b) in a park soil at 10–25 °C, the effective $k$ is very small and the cup persists for years, slowly fragmenting; in a landfill, which is anoxic and often dry, decomposition is extremely slow and any degradation yields methane; in the sea, cold, nutrient-poor and with few adapted microbes, PLA behaves almost like a conventional plastic; (c) "biodegradable" is a property of material plus environment, like litter quality plus climate; (d) a test: incubate the material in the relevant environments (soil, seawater, compost) at realistic temperatures and moisture, measure $\ce{CO2}$ evolution (ideally with ¹³C-labeled polymer to distinguish polymer carbon from soil carbon, as in priming studies) and mass loss over time, fit decomposition models (Olson or two-pool) to estimate $k$ and the time to 95% mineralization, and check for microplastic residues.
