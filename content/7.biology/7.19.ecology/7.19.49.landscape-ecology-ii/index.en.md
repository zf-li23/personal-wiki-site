The previous chapter ([7.19.48](/7.19.48)) described the structure of landscapes: **patches**, **corridors** and the **landscape matrix**, their sizes, shapes and arrangement, and the indices that quantify pattern. Structure matters because it controls function: the movement of animals and plants among patches, the spread of fire and disease, the flows of water and nutrients. This chapter asks how. Section 49.1 states the problem. Section 49.2 introduces the simplest model of how habitat amount controls connection, percolation on random maps, and uses it to frame the debate over whether **habitat fragmentation** itself, as distinct from habitat loss, harms populations. Section 49.3 develops the methods used to measure connectivity for a particular species: resistance surfaces, least-cost paths and circuit theory, which treats the landscape as an electrical network. Section 49.4 turns to disturbance, modeling fire spread as a cellular automaton and asking when a landscape subject to disturbance is in equilibrium. Section 49.5 treats species distribution models, the statistical tools that predict where species can live and that underlie most corridor plans, and Section 49.6 closes with scale, hierarchy and the tools of remote sensing. The metapopulation models of [7.19.16](/7.19.16), the island theory and reserve design of [7.19.34](/7.19.34), the graph theory of [1.4](/1.4) and the regression models of [3.3](/3.3) are used throughout.

## 49.1. Landscape Function

A landscape is not only a pattern but a system through which things move. Energy, water, nutrients, sediment, seeds, animals and people flow among its elements, carried by wind, water, gravity, animals and human transport, and the structure of the landscape speeds, slows or redirects these flows. This is what landscape ecologists call *landscape function* or *landscape processes*. The size, number, shape and isolation of habitat patches determine how often animals move between suitable patches, and hence the dynamics, persistence and extinction rates of their populations, as the **metapopulation** models of [7.19.16](/7.19.16) showed in their simplest form. Riparian vegetation intercepts nutrients flowing from fields to streams; hedgerows and forest edges channel the movement of birds and the seeds they carry; a road divides a population of amphibians into two.

Pattern and process are linked in both directions. Patterns constrain processes: a continuous forest carries a fire that a patchwork of forest and fields stops. Processes create patterns: the fire, in turn, leaves a mosaic of burned and unburned stands of different ages, which conditions the next fire. Understanding a landscape therefore requires models in which pattern and process interact, at the scales relevant to the organisms and processes concerned. The rest of this chapter develops such models, from the most abstract (random maps) to the most applied (habitat suitability maps used to plan corridors).

## 49.2. Percolation and Neutral Landscapes

### 49.2.1. Site Percolation

The simplest question one can ask about a landscape is whether its habitat is connected. Consider an organism that can move only through habitat and only between adjacent cells of a grid map, and suppose that each cell is habitat independently with probability $p$. Such random maps are *neutral landscape models* (Gardner, Milne, Turner and O'Neill 1987): they contain no ecological process that creates pattern, and they serve as null models against which real landscapes can be compared, much as the random placement model of [7.19.34](/7.19.34) is a null model for species–area curves. Connectedness on a random map is the classical problem of **percolation** ([1.4.7](/1.4.7#sec-7.8.1)).

**Definition 49.2.1 (Percolation Threshold)** In *site percolation* on a lattice, each site is occupied (habitat) independently with probability $p$, and occupied sites that are neighbors under a fixed rule (on a square grid, the four edge-sharing cells or the eight cells that share an edge or a corner) form clusters. The **percolation threshold** $p_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the critical value such that, on an infinite lattice, there is with probability $1$ no infinite cluster for $p < p_{\mathrm{c}}$ and an infinite cluster for $p > p_{\mathrm{c}}$. On a finite map of side $L$, the threshold appears as the value of $p$ near which a cluster spanning the map first appears.

For the square lattice the threshold is not known in closed form; numerically, $p_{\mathrm{c}} \approx 0.5927$ with the 4-neighbor rule and $p_{\mathrm{c}} \approx 0.4073$ with the 8-neighbor rule. (For *bond* percolation on the square lattice, in which edges rather than sites are kept with probability $p$, Kesten proved in 1980 that $p_{\mathrm{c}} = 1/2$ exactly.) Near the threshold, percolation behaves like a phase transition: the probability $P_\infty(p)$ that a site belongs to the infinite cluster rises from $0$ as $(p - p_{\mathrm{c}})^{\beta}$ with $\beta = 5/36$ in two dimensions, so steeply that the transition looks almost discontinuous, and the mean size of finite clusters diverges. One case can be solved exactly, and it shows where the threshold comes from.

**Proposition 49.2.1 (Threshold on a Tree)** On the infinite regular tree in which every vertex has $z$ neighbors (the Bethe lattice), site percolation has threshold $p_{\mathrm{c}} = 1/(z - 1)$.

**Proof** Start from an occupied vertex and explore its cluster outward. Every vertex reached, except the first, has $z - 1$ neighbors further from the start, and because a tree has no cycles, these are all new vertices, each occupied independently with probability $p$. The numbers of occupied vertices at successive distances therefore form a Galton–Watson **branching process** ([7.19.15](/7.19.15)) with mean offspring number $(z - 1)p$. A branching process survives forever with positive probability if and only if its mean offspring number exceeds $1$, so the cluster is infinite with positive probability if and only if $p > 1/(z - 1)$. $\square$

For $z = 4$ the tree threshold is $1/3$, far below the value $0.593$ of the square lattice with four neighbors: on a lattice, the neighbors of a cluster overlap, so that each new site adds fewer new neighbors than on a tree, and more habitat is needed for the cluster to grow without bound. The same branching-process argument gives the giant-component threshold of random networks in [1.4.7](/1.4.7#sec-7.8.1). Clusters themselves are found with a disjoint-set (union–find) algorithm ([4.3.11](/4.3.11)).

![Site percolation on random neutral landscapes. Below the threshold the largest cluster holds a few percent of the habitat; at $p = 0.59$ a tortuous cluster with many holes spans the map; at $p = 0.68$ almost all habitat is connected. The ecological reading of Figure 1 is the main lesson of neutral landscape models. If habitat is lost at random, its connectivity changes little at first, then collapses over a narrow range of habitat amount near $p_{\mathrm{c}}$: a small additional loss can break a connected landscape into many isolated fragments. The threshold depends on the organism. A species that can cross gaps of one non-habitat cell perceives the landscape with a larger neighborhood (the 8-neighbor rule, or larger still) and has a lower threshold; a species that cannot cross even narrow gaps has a higher one (With and Crist 1995). The threshold also depends on the arrangement of habitat. Real habitat is spatially correlated, clumped by topography, soils and land use. On fractal neutral landscapes, generated by filtering random noise so that nearby cells are similar, habitat remains in large connected blocks at much lower amounts, and the transition is smooth rather than abrupt (Figure 1D–E). In the limit of very smooth correlated surfaces, the threshold of a level set approaches $p_{\mathrm{c}} = 1/2$, by the symmetry between habitat and non-habitat.

### 49.2.2. Habitat Loss and Fragmentation per se

When forest is cleared, two things happen at once: there is less forest (*habitat loss*), and what remains is broken into more, smaller and more isolated pieces with more edge (*fragmentation*). Fahrig (2003) argued that the two must be separated, and defined *fragmentation per se* as the breaking apart of habitat at a constant total amount. Most studies that report "effects of fragmentation" measure patch size or isolation, which are correlated with the amount of habitat in the surrounding landscape, and therefore confound the two.

The theory gives a clear expectation. In the Levins model with habitat destruction ([7.19.16](/7.19.16)), the equilibrium fraction of all sites occupied is $\hat p = 1 - D - e/c$, where $D$ is the fraction of sites destroyed: the amount of habitat matters, and its arrangement does not, because the model has no space. When dispersal is local, arrangement matters. In the lattice model of Figure 2 (a contact process, [7.19.30](/7.19.30)), propagules reach only adjacent cells and are lost if they land outside habitat. With habitat at random, the population disappears at a habitat amount well above the mean-field threshold, close to the amount at which habitat stops percolating; with the same amount of habitat clumped, the population persists. Configuration matters most where habitat is scarce. Andrén (1994), reviewing studies of birds and mammals, suggested that effects of patch size and isolation beyond those expected from habitat loss appear mainly when suitable habitat falls below about 10–30 % of the landscape, consistent with this picture.

![Habitat amount and configuration in a lattice metapopulation (80 × 80 periodic grid; local extinction at rate 1, colonization of empty habitat cells from occupied neighbors at rate $\lambda/4$ per neighbor, $\lambda = 3$; propagules landing outside habitat are lost). A: proportion of the habitat occupied at equilibrium against habitat amount, for random (green) and clumped, fractal (red) habitat, with the mean-field Levins prediction $1 - e/(\lambda h)$ (dashed). With local dispersal the population on random habitat goes extinct near $h \approx 0.5$, close to the percolation threshold, while clumped habitat of the same amount supports it. B, C: snapshots at $h = 0.45$ (gray: habitat; green: occupied).](./plots/habitat-amount-configuration.py)

The empirical question is more contested than the theory. In a review of 118 studies that measured fragmentation independently of habitat amount, Fahrig (2017) found that of 381 significant responses of species or communities to fragmentation per se, 76 % were positive: at a fixed amount of habitat, more and smaller patches were associated with more species or individuals more often than with fewer. She proposed the *habitat amount hypothesis*: the number of species at a site is determined by the amount of habitat in the surrounding landscape, regardless of how it is divided into patches. Several explanations of positive effects are plausible: many small patches spread risk among independent populations, contain more different habitat types, increase the access of edge species and of species that use both habitat and matrix, and reduce the spread of predators and disease. Fletcher and colleagues (2018) replied that many of these studies are short, measure the wrong response (richness rather than the persistence of the species of concern), ignore the time lags of extinction debt, and use landscapes in which fragmentation is too weak to have effects. Experiments in which fragmentation is manipulated directly, such as the Biological Dynamics of Forest Fragments Project near Manaus, Brazil (since 1979), show strong and long-lasting negative effects in small fragments: in a synthesis of seven such experiments on five continents, habitat fragmentation and loss reduced biodiversity by 13–75 % and impaired ecosystem functions, with effects that increased over time (Haddad et al. 2015).

The two sides agree that habitat amount is the dominant variable and that the effects of configuration depend on the scale of measurement (patch or landscape), on the species (generalists or interior specialists), and on time since fragmentation. The disagreement is over the sign and size of the remaining effect of configuration, and it has practical consequences: if fragmentation per se is usually weak or positive, small patches are worth protecting and the SLOSS question of [7.19.34](/7.19.34#sec-34.5.1) shifts toward several small reserves. What is not disputed is that edges change conditions inside patches. The **edge effect** ([7.19.32](/7.19.32)) shrinks the interior habitat of small patches disproportionately.

**Example 49.2.1** Suppose that conditions within $d = 100$ m of a forest edge (higher light, wind and temperature, more nest predators) are unsuitable for an interior species. A square fragment of side $1$ km has core area $(1 - 2 \times 0.1)^2 = 0.64\ \mathrm{km}^2$, 64 % of its area. The same square divided into four squares of side $0.5$ km has core area $4 \times (0.5 - 0.2)^2 = 0.36\ \mathrm{km}^2$, and sixteen squares of side $0.25$ km have $16 \times 0.05^2 = 0.04\ \mathrm{km}^2$. At constant habitat amount, fragmentation removes 94 % of the habitat available to this species, even though it may increase the habitat available to edge species.

## 49.3. Measuring Connectivity

### 49.3.1. Structural and Functional Connectivity

Connectivity, as Taylor and colleagues (1993) defined it, is "the degree to which the landscape facilitates or impedes movement among resource patches". The definition has two parts. Structural measures describe the physical continuity of habitat: the number and size of patches, their distances, the presence of corridors; they can be computed from a map alone. Functional measures describe how a particular organism responds to that structure: a hedgerow connects woodland patches for a dormouse but not for a deer, and a road that a bird flies over may be an absolute barrier for a salamander. Functional connectivity is the quantity of interest, and it requires information on movement.

The standard representation of functional connectivity is a *resistance surface*: a map in which each cell is assigned a cost of movement, reflecting the reluctance of the animal to enter it, the energy it spends or the risk of death. Resistance values come from expert opinion, from the inverse of habitat suitability (often estimated with the species distribution models of Section 49.5), or, best, from data on movement (telemetry tracks compared with available routes) or on gene flow (Section 49.3.4). Two families of methods then turn a resistance surface into measures of connection between patches.

### 49.3.2. Least-Cost Paths

The *cost distance* between two patches is the minimum, over all paths through the grid from one to the other, of the accumulated cost along the path, each step costing the mean resistance of the two cells times the step length. Treating cells as vertices of a weighted grid graph, cost distances are shortest-path distances, computed by **Dijkstra's algorithm** ([1.4.2](/1.4.2)). The path that attains the minimum is the *least-cost path*. Because an animal does not know the optimal route and the resistance values are uncertain, it is more useful to map a *least-cost corridor*: the set of cells through which the cheapest path is within a given margin of the optimum, obtained by adding, for every cell, the cost distances from the two patches (Figure 3A).

Least-cost methods are intuitive and widely used for corridor planning, but they assume that the animal knows the whole landscape and takes the optimal route, and they ignore alternatives: a single optimal route and a hundred nearly optimal ones receive the same cost distance.

### 49.3.3. Circuit Theory

McRae (2006) proposed to treat the landscape as an electrical circuit. Each cell is a node, adjacent cells are joined by resistors with resistance equal to the movement cost, and a current is passed from one patch to another. The connection is the reason this works: current in an electrical network is related to the movement of a random walker. A walker on a graph with edge conductances $g_{ij}$ (inverse resistances) that moves from $i$ to a neighbor $j$ with probability $g_{ij}/\sum_k g_{ik}$ is a **random walk** ([7.6.5](/7.6.5)) whose probabilities of reaching one set of nodes before another are the potentials of the circuit, and whose net passage through an edge is proportional to the current in it.

**Proposition 49.3.1 (Effective Resistance)** Let $G$ be a connected graph with edge conductances $g_{ij} > 0$ and weighted **Laplacian matrix** $L$ ([1.4.3](/1.4.3)), $L_{ii} = \sum_j g_{ij}$, $L_{ij} = -g_{ij}$. If a unit current is injected at node $a$ and withdrawn at node $b$, the node potentials $\boldsymbol{v}$ satisfy $L\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{e}_a - \boldsymbol{e}_b$, and the effective resistance between $a$ and $b$ is

$$
R_{ab} = v_a - v_b = (\boldsymbol{e}_a - \boldsymbol{e}_b)^{\mathsf T}L^{+}(\boldsymbol{e}_a - \boldsymbol{e}_b),
\tag{49.1}
$$
where $L^{+}$ is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of $L$.

**Proof** By Ohm's law the current from $i$ to $j$ is $g_{ij}(v_i - v_j)$, and Kirchhoff's current law says that the net current leaving node $i$ equals the current injected there: $\sum_j g_{ij}(v_i - v_j) = (L\boldsymbol{v})_i = (\boldsymbol{e}_a - \boldsymbol{e}_b)_i$. Since $G$ is connected, $L$ is symmetric positive semidefinite with null space spanned by $\boldsymbol{1}$. The right-hand side is orthogonal to $\boldsymbol{1}$, so it lies in the range of $L$; the solutions are $\boldsymbol{v} = L^{+}(\boldsymbol{e}_a - \boldsymbol{e}_b) + c\boldsymbol{1}$, and the constant cancels in $v_a - v_b$. With a unit current, the effective resistance is the potential difference, $v_a - v_b = (\boldsymbol{e}_a - \boldsymbol{e}_b)^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{v}$, which is Eq. (49.1). $\square$

Effective resistance has the properties that make it a good measure of connection. Resistances in series add, so long, costly routes have high resistance; resistances in parallel combine as $1/R = \sum_k 1/R_k$, so several alternative routes connect better than one; and by Rayleigh's monotonicity law, adding habitat or lowering the resistance of any cell can never increase the effective resistance. Effective resistance is also proportional to the expected *commute time* of a random walker, the mean time to go from $a$ to $b$ and back: $\mathbb{E}[C_{ab}] = \operatorname{vol}(G)\,R_{ab}$, where $\operatorname{vol}(G) = \sum_{i,j} g_{ij}$ (Chandra et al. 1989). The current density at each cell shows where movement is concentrated; cells where current is funneled through narrow strips are *pinch points*, priorities for protection (Figure 3B). In practice the potentials are computed by solving the sparse linear system with the source patch held at one potential and the target at another, as in the software Circuitscape.

C: adding crossings (1 to 6) leaves the least-cost distance unchanged once the best crossing exists, while the effective resistance keeps falling, because circuit theory counts parallel routes.](./plots/connectivity.py)

Patches can also be treated as the vertices of a graph ([1.4.1](/1.4.1)), joined by an edge when they are within the dispersal distance of the species or weighted by a dispersal probability that declines with cost distance. The connected components of this graph are groups of patches between which the species can move; a patch whose removal splits a component is a cut vertex and deserves protection. Indices of connectivity sum over pairs of patches: the *probability of connectivity* of Saura and Pascual-Hortal (2007),

$$
\mathrm{PC} = \frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{L}}^2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j} a_i a_j p^*_{ij},
$$
where $a_i$ is the area (or quality-weighted area) of patch $i$, $A_{\mathrm{L}}$ the area of the landscape and $p^*_{ij}$ the largest product of dispersal probabilities along any path between $i$ and $j$, measures the probability that two points placed at random in the landscape fall in habitat patches that are connected. The importance of a patch is the percentage decrease of PC when it is removed, which counts its own area and its role as a stepping stone.

### 49.3.4. Landscape Genetics

Movement leaves a record in genes. Individuals that disperse and breed elsewhere carry their alleles with them, so that the genetic differentiation between populations reflects the **gene flow** between them, integrated over many generations ([7.19.20](/7.19.20)). In a uniform landscape, differentiation increases with geographic distance (**isolation by distance**, [7.10.10](/7.10.10)). In a heterogeneous landscape it should increase with the difficulty of movement.

**Definition 49.3.1 (Landscape Genetics)** **Landscape genetics** is the study of how landscape features (land cover, topography, roads, rivers) shape gene flow and the spatial genetic structure of populations, by relating genetic distances among individuals or populations to landscape distances (Manel et al. 2003).

The typical analysis computes, for each pair of sampling sites, a genetic distance (such as $F_{\mathrm{ST}}/(1 - F_{\mathrm{ST}})$ or the proportion of shared alleles) and several landscape distances: Euclidean distance, cost distance and effective resistance under alternative resistance surfaces. The surface whose distances best explain the genetic distances, by Mantel tests, mixed models for pairwise data or optimization of the resistance values themselves, is taken as the best description of functional connectivity. McRae (2006) called the relationship between genetic differentiation and effective resistance *isolation by resistance* and showed that, for an island model on a lattice, equilibrium differentiation is approximately proportional to effective resistance, so that multiple routes reduce differentiation as they reduce resistance. In tests on wolverines and on the big-leaf mahogany, effective resistance explained genetic distances better than Euclidean or least-cost distances (McRae and Beier 2007). Genetic data integrate dispersal and successful breeding over generations, which telemetry cannot, but they respond to landscape change with a lag of tens of generations and so describe the landscape of the past as much as that of the present.

### 49.3.5. Corridors

Corridors and wildlife crossings, their evidence and Chinese examples (the tunnel of National Road 108 in the Qinling Mountains, corridors for elephants, leopards and spotted seals) were discussed with reserve design in [7.19.34](/7.19.34#sec-34.5.2). The strongest experimental evidence comes from the Savannah River Site in South Carolina, where replicated clearings in pine plantation were created in sets of a central patch connected by a 150 m corridor to one peripheral patch and unconnected to others of equal area. Butterflies, bird-dispersed seeds and pollen moved more between connected patches (Tewksbury et al. 2002), and plant species richness was higher in connected patches, with the difference growing over 18 years as colonization accumulated and extinction slowed (Damschen et al. 2006, 2019). The methods of this section are how corridors are located: a resistance surface derived from habitat suitability, least-cost corridors or current maps between core patches, and pinch points or barriers (cells whose restoration would most reduce resistance) as priorities for action.

## 49.4. Disturbance in Landscapes

### 49.4.1. Fire Spread as a Cellular Automaton

Fire is the most studied landscape process ([7.19.5](/7.19.5)), because it is both shaped by and shapes landscape pattern. Its spread can be modeled by a *cellular automaton*: each cell of a grid is in one of a few states (non-flammable, unburned fuel, burning, burned), and the state of each cell at the next step depends on its own state and those of its neighbors. In the simplest version, each cell carries fuel with probability $p$; a burning cell ignites each neighbor that carries unburned fuel with probability $q$ and then burns out. When $q = 1$, the area burned from an ignition is exactly the percolation cluster containing it, so fire spreads across the landscape only if $p$ exceeds the percolation threshold (Figure 4). When $q < 1$ (moist fuel, weak wind), each edge between fuel cells transmits fire independently with probability $q$, a combination of site and bond percolation, and more fuel is needed for spread.

![Fire spread as a cellular automaton on 150 × 150 grids ignited along the left edge. A, B: burned area (black) and unburned fuel (green) for fuel densities $p = 0.55$ and $0.65$ with certain spread to adjacent fuel ($q = 1$): below the percolation threshold the fire dies near the ignition line, above it the fire sweeps across the map. C: proportion of fuel burned against $p$ for spread probabilities $q = 1$, $0.9$ and $0.8$ (means of 20 grids): with $q = 1$ the transition is at $p_{\mathrm{c}} = 0.593$, and smaller $q$ shifts it to higher fuel densities. D: duration of the fire (steps until it stops) with $q = 1$, which peaks at the threshold, where the burned cluster is tortuous and the front advances slowly.](./plots/fire-spread.py)

The model explains two observations. First, landscape pattern matters most at intermediate fuel continuity and under moderate weather: in very dry, windy conditions (effectively $q \approx 1$ and long-distance spotting by embers), fires spread across almost any landscape, and in moist conditions they spread across none. The fires of 1988 in Yellowstone National Park, which burned about a third of the park, spread under extreme drought and wind through stands of all ages, and the role of landscape pattern in limiting them was small (Turner and Romme 1994). Second, the suppression of small fires allows fuel to accumulate and become continuous, raising $p$ toward and beyond the threshold, so that the eventual fires are larger and more intense: a positive feedback that underlies the use of **prescribed burning** in fire-adapted ecosystems ([7.19.5](/7.19.5)). A variant in which trees regrow slowly and lightning strikes rarely (the Drossel–Schwabl forest-fire model) organizes itself into a state near the threshold, with fire sizes following a power law over several orders of magnitude, which resembles the heavy-tailed size distributions of real fires.

### 49.4.2. Disturbance Regimes and Landscape Equilibrium

A **disturbance** ([7.19.33](/7.19.33)) at the landscape scale is characterized by its regime: the distribution of event sizes, their frequency or return interval, intensity and severity, seasonality and spatial pattern (for fire, the **fire regime**). Under a steady regime, a landscape can be in equilibrium even though no stand is: patches are disturbed and recover continually, and the proportions of the landscape in each stage of recovery remain constant, a *shifting-mosaic steady state* (Bormann and Likens 1979).

**Proposition 49.4.1 (Age Distribution under a Constant Disturbance Rate)** Suppose that every part of a landscape is disturbed at rate $\lambda$ per year independently of its age (time since the last disturbance), and that disturbed area returns to age $0$. Then the stationary age distribution of the landscape is exponential,

$$
f(a) = \lambda\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda a}, \quad a \ge 0,
\tag{49.2}
$$
the proportion of the landscape older than $a$ is $\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda a}$, and the mean age equals the *disturbance cycle* $1/\lambda$, the time needed to disturb an area equal to the whole landscape.

**Proof** Let $n(a, t)$ be the density of area of age $a$ at time $t$, with $\int_0^\infty n\,\mathrm{d}a = 1$. Area ages at unit rate and is lost to disturbance at rate $\lambda$, so $n$ satisfies the **McKendrick–von Foerster equation** ([7.19.14](/7.19.14)) $\partial n/\partial t + \partial n/\partial a = -\lambda n$, with the boundary condition $n(0, t) = \lambda\int_0^\infty n\,\mathrm{d}a = \lambda$, since all disturbed area re-enters at age $0$. At stationarity $\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}a = -\lambda n$ and $n(0) = \lambda$, so $n(a) = \lambda\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda a}$, which integrates to $1$, has survival function $\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda a}$ and mean $1/\lambda$. $\square$

The negative exponential distribution (Van Wagner 1978) is used to estimate fire cycles from the ages of stands in boreal forests. If flammability increases with age, the hazard is an increasing function $\lambda(a)$ and the distribution becomes a Weibull or similar, with fewer young and very old stands; the same equation applies with $\lambda$ replaced by $\lambda(a)$, giving $f(a) \propto \exp[-\int_0^a\lambda(s)\,\mathrm{d}s]$.

Whether a real landscape behaves like a shifting mosaic depends on two ratios (Turner et al. 1993): the size of disturbances relative to the size of the landscape, and the interval between disturbances relative to the time needed for recovery. When disturbances are small relative to the landscape and infrequent relative to recovery, the landscape is in equilibrium with low variance in its composition, as in old-growth forests with small gaps. When disturbances are large relative to the landscape, the composition fluctuates widely even if recovery is fast: Yellowstone, where single fire years burn a large part of the park, is a non-equilibrium landscape. When disturbances recur faster than recovery, the system shifts to a different state, as when frequent fires convert forest to shrubland or grassland (**regime shift**, [7.19.39](/7.19.39)). The equilibrium concept therefore depends on scale: the same disturbance regime produces an equilibrium landscape at a large extent and a non-equilibrium one at a small extent, which is why protected areas must be large relative to the disturbances they contain.

## 49.5. Species Distribution Models

### 49.5.1. What They Estimate

**Definition 49.5.1 (Species Distribution Model)** A **species distribution model** (SDM) is a statistical model that relates observations of a species (presence, presence and absence, or abundance) at known locations to environmental variables at those locations, and uses the fitted relationship to predict the probability of occurrence, or the relative suitability of habitat, at other places or times. Models built mainly on climate and used to project into new regions or future climates are also called *ecological niche models* or climate envelope models.

The idea is Hutchinson's: the **ecological niche** ([7.10.15](/7.10.15)) is a region of environmental space, and the geographic distribution is its projection onto the map. What an SDM estimates is not obvious. Soberón and Peterson (2005) summarized the problem in the BAM diagram: a species occurs where the abiotic conditions are suitable (A, the **fundamental niche** in Hutchinson's sense, [7.10.30](/7.10.30)), where the biotic environment allows it (B: food, mutualists, absence of excluding competitors and predators), and where it has been able to disperse (M, movement). Occurrence data come from the intersection of all three, so an SDM fitted to them estimates something between the **realized niche** ([7.19.23](/7.19.23)) and the fundamental niche, and its projections are reliable only where the biotic and dispersal constraints are similar to those in the data.

Data come in two kinds. Systematic surveys record both presences and absences at sampled sites. Much more abundant are presence-only records: museum and herbarium specimens, citizen-science observations, camera-trap detections, compiled in databases such as GBIF. Predictors are gridded maps of climate (temperature, precipitation and their seasonality), topography, soils, land cover and human influence (distance to roads, population density).

### 49.5.2. Presence–Absence Data: Logistic Regression

With presence–absence data $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ at sites with environmental vectors $\boldsymbol{x}_i$, the natural model is **logistic regression** ([3.4.12](/3.4.12)), a **generalized linear model** ([3.3.6](/3.3.6)) with Bernoulli response and logit link,

$$
\log\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{x})}{1 - \pi(\boldsymbol{x})} = \beta_0 + \boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{x}),
$$
fitted by maximum likelihood, where $\boldsymbol{h}$ contains the predictors and transformations of them. Niches are unimodal: a species does best at intermediate temperatures, not at the highest or lowest. A quadratic term captures this, and its coefficients have a direct ecological meaning. Completing the square, a model linear and quadratic in one predictor $x$ with $\beta_2 < 0$ is

$$
\beta_0 + \beta_1x + \beta_2x^2 = a - \frac{(x - u)^2}{2t^2}, \qquad u = -\frac{\beta_1}{2\beta_2}, \quad t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-2\beta_2}},
\tag{49.3}
$$
the Gaussian response of gradient analysis ([7.19.32](/7.19.32)) on the logit scale, with optimum $u$, tolerance $t$ and maximum $a = \beta_0 - \beta_1^2/(4\beta_2)$. A positive $\beta_2$ indicates a minimum, usually a sign that the sampled range covers only one side of the niche. Figure 5 shows the procedure for a virtual species whose true niche is known: 300 random surveys recover the optima and tolerances closely, and the fitted map reproduces the true distribution.

### 49.5.3. Presence-Only Data: MaxEnt

Presence-only data cannot estimate the probability of occurrence, because nothing records where the species is absent. They can estimate how the density of records varies across the landscape. MaxEnt (Phillips, Anderson and Schapire 2006), the most widely used SDM program, applies the **maximum entropy principle**: among all probability distributions $\pi$ over the cells $x$ of the study region that reproduce the mean of each environmental feature $f_k$ (predictors, their squares, products, thresholds) observed at the presence records, choose the one closest to uniform, that is, of maximum entropy.

**Proposition 49.5.1 (MaxEnt and Maximum Likelihood)** Let $\bar f_k$ be the mean of feature $f_k$ over $m$ presence records in a region of finitely many cells. The distribution that maximizes the entropy $H(\pi) = -\sum_x\pi(x)\log\pi(x)$ subject to $\sum_x\pi(x)f_k(x) = \bar f_k$ for all $k$ and $\sum_x\pi(x) = 1$, if it exists, is the Gibbs distribution

$$
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(x) = \frac{\exp\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{f}(x)\right)}{Z(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}, \qquad Z(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \sum_x\exp\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{f}(x)\right),
\tag{49.4}
$$
and its parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ are the maximum likelihood estimates of the exponential family (49.4) given the presence records.

**Proof** The Lagrangian $-\sum\pi\log\pi + \sum_k\lambda_k(\sum\pi f_k - \bar f_k) + \mu(\sum\pi - 1)$ has stationary points where $-\log\pi(x) - 1 + \boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{f}(x) + \mu = 0$, so $\pi(x) \propto \exp(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{f}(x))$; since entropy is strictly concave and the constraints are linear, this stationary point is the unique maximum. For the second statement, the log-likelihood of the records $x_1, \dots, x_m$ under Eq. (49.4) is $\ell(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \sum_i\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{f}(x_i) - m\log Z(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) = m[\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathsf T}\bar{\boldsymbol{f}} - \log Z(\boldsymbol{\lambda})]$. It is concave, and its gradient is $m[\bar{\boldsymbol{f}} - \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\boldsymbol{f}]$, because $\partial\log Z/\partial\lambda_k = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}f_k$. The likelihood is therefore maximized exactly where the model reproduces the empirical feature means, which are the constraints of the entropy problem. $\square$

MaxEnt adds a penalty on $\sum_k\lvert\lambda_k\rvert$ (an $\ell_1$, lasso-type regularization) so that the fitted distribution matches the feature means only within their sampling error, which avoids overfitting with many features. Statistically, MaxEnt is equivalent to fitting an inhomogeneous Poisson point process to the presence records (Renner and Warton 2013), and approximately to logistic regression of presences against a large sample of background points (Fithian and Hastie 2013). Its raw output is a *relative* occurrence rate; transforming it into a probability of presence (MaxEnt's "logistic" or "cloglog" outputs) requires an assumption about the prevalence of the species, which presence-only data cannot supply.

### 49.5.4. Evaluation and Pitfalls

Models are evaluated by their ability to discriminate presences from absences (or from background points) in data not used for fitting. The usual statistic is the area under the **ROC curve**, the AUC: the probability that a randomly chosen presence site receives a higher predicted value than a randomly chosen absence site, $0.5$ for a useless model and $1$ for perfect discrimination. AUC values must be read with care. With background points instead of true absences, the maximum attainable AUC is below $1$ and depends on the range of the species relative to the study area: a narrowly distributed species in a large study area gets a high AUC easily. Test data that are spatially close to training data overstate performance, because of spatial autocorrelation; spatial block cross-validation, which withholds whole regions, is more honest.

The main pitfalls are ecological rather than statistical:

- Sampling bias: presence records cluster near roads, cities and research stations, so a model can learn the distribution of observers rather than of the species; background points should be drawn with the same bias (target-group background) or the bias modeled explicitly.
- Non-equilibrium: an expanding invader or a species recovering from persecution does not yet occupy all suitable habitat, so absence does not mean unsuitability.
- Biotic interactions and dispersal limits are ignored; predictions of the fundamental niche are confounded with the realized niche.
- Extrapolation: projections to future climates or new continents involve combinations of conditions absent from the training data, where the shape of the fitted response is arbitrary. Projections should be accompanied by maps of novelty (multivariate environmental similarity surfaces).
- Correlation is not causation: a predictor that correlates with the true driver in the training region may not do so elsewhere.

Because different methods (GLM, generalized additive models, boosted regression trees, random forests, MaxEnt) give different predictions, ensembles of models are common. Mechanistic models, which predict distributions from physiology (thermal tolerances, energy and water budgets, [7.19.4](/7.19.4)), are a complement that does not depend on current distributions.

### 49.5.5. Applications

SDMs are used to map habitat for conservation planning, to predict the spread of invasive species, to project range shifts under climate change ([7.19.47](/7.19.47)), and to provide the resistance surfaces of connectivity analyses. Two Chinese applications illustrate the pipeline from suitability to corridors.

Asian elephants (*Elephas maximus*) in China, about three hundred animals, live in southern Yunnan (Xishuangbanna, Pu'er, Lincang), in forest that has been fragmented by rubber and tea plantations, farmland and roads. In 2020–2021 a herd of about fifteen elephants left Xishuangbanna and walked about 500 km north to the outskirts of Kunming before being guided back, attracting worldwide attention to the loss and fragmentation of their habitat. Models of habitat suitability built from occurrence records, vegetation, terrain and human disturbance identify the remaining suitable patches and the gaps between them, and corridors are planned along the routes of least resistance between them (Chen et al. 2023). For the North China leopard (*Panthera pardus japonensis*), whose remaining populations are scattered in the mountains of central North China, Wang and colleagues (2024) combined several SDM algorithms into an ensemble model of habitat suitability, delineated core habitat patches, and used resistance surfaces derived from suitability to map connections among them and potential dispersal corridors: the same sequence as in Figures 3 and 5.

Climate projections illustrate the uncertainty. Beaumont and Hughes (2002) projected that even under a conservative climate scenario, the climatically suitable ranges of 13 of 24 Australian butterfly species would shrink by more than 20 % by 2050, and that much of the future range of the most threatened species lay outside its current range, so that reserves may be in the wrong place in a changing world. Climate-matching models with MaxEnt predicted the potential range of the northern Pacific sea star (*Asterias amurensis*), introduced to Tasmania in ballast water, including New Zealand and sub-Antarctic islands it has not yet reached (Byrne et al. 2016): a prediction useful for biosecurity precisely because it identifies places to watch before invasion ([7.19.51](/7.19.51)).

## 49.6. Scale, Hierarchy and Tools

Every pattern and process in this chapter depends on scale: on the grain (the size of the smallest unit, such as a map cell) and the extent (the area studied) of [7.19.48](/7.19.48). Percolation thresholds depend on the neighborhood an organism perceives; the equilibrium of a disturbance regime depends on the extent of the landscape relative to the disturbances; habitat suitability averaged over 10 km cells differs from that over 100 m cells. *Hierarchy theory* (Allen and Starr 1982; O'Neill et al. 1986) provides a framework: ecological systems are organized in levels that differ in their characteristic spatial and temporal scales, so that a level is constrained by the slower, larger level above it (the climate constrains the vegetation of a landscape) and its behavior is explained by the faster, smaller level below it (the vegetation by the demography of trees). To explain a phenomenon one considers three levels: the level of the phenomenon, the level below for mechanisms and the level above for constraints. *Scaling* (extrapolating information across scales) is difficult because nonlinear relationships do not average: the mean of a function of temperature is not the function of the mean temperature, so models fitted at one grain give biased predictions at another unless the variability within cells is accounted for.

The data of landscape ecology come mostly from remote sensing. The Landsat satellites have imaged the whole land surface at 30 m resolution since 1972 (with earlier sensors at 80 m), making it possible to measure half a century of land-cover change; Sentinel-2 provides 10 m imagery every few days, and MODIS daily coverage at 250 m–1 km. Vegetation is detected by its spectral signature: chlorophyll absorbs red light and leaf tissue reflects near-infrared, so the normalized difference vegetation index

$$
\mathrm{NDVI} = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}} - \rho_{\mathrm{red}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}} + \rho_{\mathrm{red}}},
$$
where $\rho$ are reflectances, ranges from near $0$ over bare soil to about $0.8$ over dense green canopy and serves as a proxy for green biomass and primary production ([7.19.42](/7.19.42)). Airborne and satellite lidar measure canopy height and structure in three dimensions, and radar sees through cloud. Geographic information systems (GIS) overlay these layers with maps of soils, roads and protected areas and with field data, and perform the calculations of this chapter: cluster labeling, cost distances, current maps and model predictions. GPS telemetry, camera traps and acoustic recorders ([7.19.8](/7.19.8)) provide the data on movement and occurrence that make these maps functional rather than merely structural.

## Questions

1. A landscape has 70 % forest scattered at random in 1 ha cells, and a forest bird moves only through forest cells that share an edge. Forest is being cleared at random at 1 % of the landscape per year. Describe how the connectivity of its habitat will change over the next 20 years, and how your answer changes if the bird can cross one cleared cell.

   **Answer** At 70 % forest the habitat percolates and almost all forest is in one cluster (Figure 1). Connectivity changes little for about ten years, then collapses as forest cover passes the threshold of about 59 % for the 4-neighbor rule: between roughly 62 % and 55 % cover the largest cluster breaks into many isolated pieces. If the bird can cross one cleared cell, its effective neighborhood is at least the 8-neighbor rule, with a threshold near 41 %, so its habitat stays connected for the whole 20 years (cover falls to 50 %). The answer assumes random clearing; real clearing usually proceeds from roads and settlements and leaves clumped forest, which keeps the remnants internally connected longer but separates them by wide gaps.
2. Explain why least-cost distance and effective resistance can rank the same pair of patches differently. Which would you use to place a single wildlife overpass, and which to judge whether a whole network of patches is well connected?

   **Answer** Least-cost distance depends only on the single cheapest route, whereas effective resistance combines all routes, decreasing with each parallel route as resistances in parallel do (Proposition 49.3.1, Figure 3C). Two patches joined by one good route and two patches joined by many slightly worse routes can therefore be ranked in opposite orders. For placing one overpass, the least-cost corridor shows where most animals with good knowledge of the landscape would cross, and the current map shows where movement of animals that wander is funneled (pinch points); both are useful, and they often agree at a road crossing. For the connectivity of a network, effective resistance (or graph indices with dispersal probabilities) is better because redundancy among routes makes a network robust to the loss of any one route, and random-walk movement is a better approximation for dispersing juveniles that do not know the landscape.
3. In a region, bird species richness of forest patches increases with patch area. Does this show that fragmentation is harmful? Design an analysis that separates the effects of habitat amount and fragmentation per se.

   **Answer** No. Larger patches are usually surrounded by more forest, so patch area confounds the amount of habitat in the landscape with its configuration; the species–area relationship within patches is also expected from sampling alone. Following Fahrig, choose landscapes (for example circles of a few kilometers radius, scaled to the movement of the birds) that vary independently in total forest cover and in the number of patches or edge density at a given cover, ideally in a factorial design; measure richness or the abundance of focal species at sample points of equal effort in each landscape; then model the response with both habitat amount and configuration as predictors and check that they are not strongly correlated. Separate interior specialists from edge and generalist species, since configuration may affect them in opposite directions, and if possible use long-term data to allow for extinction debt.
4. Fire suppression has been practiced for a century in a forest where fires used to be frequent and small. Use the cellular automaton and Proposition 49.4.1 to explain the consequences, and say what the age distribution of stands would look like before and after.

   **Answer** Frequent small fires kept fuel discontinuous (low effective $p$), below the threshold at which a fire spreads across the landscape, so each fire burned a small area. Suppression lets fuel accumulate and become continuous, raising $p$ above the threshold, so that when a fire escapes under dry, windy conditions it spreads widely and burns intensely (Figure 4C). Before suppression, with a roughly constant hazard $\lambda$, stand ages would follow the exponential distribution with mean $1/\lambda$, short for a frequent regime, with few old stands. Suppression lowers $\lambda$ temporarily, so the age distribution shifts toward older stands with no young ones, the landscape becomes homogeneous, and the eventual large fires reset large areas at once, producing a non-equilibrium landscape dominated by a few age classes. This is the rationale for prescribed burning and fuel reduction.
5. A MaxEnt model of an endangered plant fitted to herbarium records has an AUC of 0.95. A colleague concludes that the model is excellent and can be used to choose sites for reintroduction under future climate. What are your concerns?

   **Answer** A high AUC from presence–background data mainly shows that presences differ from the average background, which is easy when the species is rare and the study area large; it says little about calibration. Herbarium records are biased toward accessible places, so the model may partly describe collectors' behavior; spatially independent test data are needed. The model estimates relative suitability, not probability of occurrence, so it cannot say whether a site is suitable enough. The distribution may be limited by dispersal, pollinators or past habitat destruction rather than climate, so absences in the background need not be unsuitable, and the realized niche may underestimate the fundamental niche. Future climates may include combinations of conditions outside the training data, where responses are extrapolated arbitrarily. The model should be complemented by experiments (transplants, germination tests), by mechanistic knowledge of tolerances, and by maps of environmental novelty, and reintroduction sites should be chosen across a range of predicted suitability and monitored.
