[7.15.9](/7.15.9) explained that the V region of an antibody recognizes and the C region calls up effector functions; complement is one of the main effector systems that is called up, and it is also the most ancient humoral effector system of innate immunity. It gives another answer to the question "what effectors eliminate the threat?" on the path of knowledge ([7.15.1](/7.15.1)). A series of plasma proteins are activated in turn and form an enzymatic amplification cascade on the surface of the pathogen, with three results: the pathogen is tagged for removal by phagocytes, chemotactic and inflammatory signals are generated, and pores are punched in the membrane of some target cells. Complement links innate immunity (lectins that recognize carbohydrate patterns, and the alternative pathway, which is independent of antibody) with adaptive immunity (the classical pathway activated by antibody; complement fragments in turn lower the activation threshold of B cells), and it is also the main executor of the opsonization and inflammation described in [7.15.7](/7.15.7).

The complement system consists of more than thirty proteins with a bewildering array of names, but its skeleton is a single line: C3 is the center, each of the three pathways generates its own C3 convertase, the convertase cleaves C3, and C3b is deposited covalently on the surface, where it both tags the surface and generates more convertase, and finally the sequence runs on to the membrane attack complex. This main line, together with regulation (how host surfaces avoid attack), is the content of this chapter.

## 10.1. Components and Properties of Complement

**Definition 10.1.1 (Complement)** **Complement** is a group of proteins (more than thirty) present in plasma and tissue fluid, some of them also expressed on cell membranes. They normally exist as inactive precursors and are activated in sequence on the surface of pathogens or on antigen–antibody complexes, forming an enzymatic cascade that carries out lysis, opsonization, inflammation and other effects.

Complement was first found by Bordet at the end of the 19th century as a heat-labile component of serum (inactivated at $56\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ for 30 min) that "complements" the bacteriolytic action of antibody, and Ehrlich therefore named it complement. Complement proteins make up about 5% of the plasma globulins and are synthesized mainly by hepatocytes; monocytes, macrophages and epithelial cells also synthesize them locally. Plasma concentrations of the components vary widely, and C3 is the most abundant, at about $1\ \mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$.

The nomenclature of complement bears traces of history:

- The components named with C plus a number, C1–C9, are numbered in the order of discovery rather than the order of reaction: C4 reacts before C2.
- When a protein is cleaved on activation, the small fragment is given the letter a (C3a, C4a, C5a) and the large fragment the letter b (C3b, C4b, C5b). The naming of the fragments of C2 has not been uniform in the literature; the large fragment is now called C2b (formerly C2a), so that C4b2b is the formerly named C4b2a.
- Components of the alternative pathway are named with letters: factor B, factor D, factor P (properdin), factor H, factor I.
- Complexes that acquire activity after cleavage or binding are written with the component numbers run together (C4b2b, C3bBb).

| Class | Components | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Classical pathway | C1 (C1q, C1r, C1s), C4, C2 | Recognize antibody and generate the C3 convertase C4b2b |
| Lectin pathway | Mannose-binding lectin (MBL), ficolins, MASP-1, MASP-2 | Recognize microbial carbohydrates and generate C4b2b |
| Alternative pathway | C3, factor B, factor D, factor P | Spontaneous hydrolysis and amplification, generating C3bBb |
| Terminal pathway | C5, C6, C7, C8, C9 | Form the membrane attack complex |
| Regulatory proteins | C1 inhibitor, C4-binding protein, factor H, factor I, DAF (CD55), MCP (CD46), CR1 (CD35), CD59 | Limit activation and protect self (§10.5) |
| Receptors | CR1, CR2 (CD21), CR3 (CD11b/CD18), CR4 (CD11c/CD18), CRIg, C3aR, C5aR | Transmit the signals of complement fragments (§10.4) |

## 10.2. The Three Pathways of Activation and the Terminal Pathway

### 10.2.1. Initiation of the Three Pathways

The three pathways differ in how recognition occurs and in who first generates the C3 convertase.

**Definition 10.2.1 (The Three Pathways of Complement Activation)** The pathway initiated by complexes formed by binding of antibody to antigen is called the **classical pathway**; the pathway initiated by binding of recognition molecules to carbohydrates on the surface of microbes is called the **lectin pathway**; and the pathway that is independent of antibody and lectin and is initiated by spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 and its deposition on the surface of microbes is called the **alternative pathway**. The recognition molecules of the lectin pathway are mainly **mannose-binding lectin** (MBL) and the ficolins.

The classical pathway begins with C1. C1 is a complex made of C1q, with its six heads, together with two C1r and two C1s. The globular heads of C1q bind the $\mathrm{C_H2}$ (for IgM, $\mathrm{C_H3}$) of the Fc of IgM or IgG (mainly IgG3 and IgG1, IgG2 more weakly). Binding of a single IgG is too weak; it requires the Fc regions of at least two IgG molecules bound close together on the same antigen surface, and an IgM pentamer bound to a surface itself provides such closely spaced Fc regions, which is why IgM is the strongest activator of complement. C1q can also bind C-reactive protein (CRP), apoptotic cells and the surfaces of some bacteria directly. A conformational change of C1q causes autoactivation of C1r, activated C1r cleaves C1s, and C1s cleaves C4 and C2: C4 is cut into C4a and C4b, and the exposed thioester bond of C4b binds covalently to the nearby surface; C2 then binds C4b and is cleaved by C1s, the large fragment C2b remaining on C4b to form C4b2b.

The lectin pathway joins the classical pathway at C4 and C2. MBL and the ficolins bind mannose, fucose and $N$-acetylglucosamine arranged repetitively on the microbial surface (the termini of carbohydrate chains on mammalian cell surfaces are sialic acid and galactose and are not recognized), and the serine protease MASP-2 bound to them cleaves C4 and C2, forming the same C4b2b. This pathway needs no antibody and acts before antibodies appear; it is part of innate immunity (pattern recognition, see [7.15.6](/7.15.6)).

The alternative pathway needs no recognition molecule. C3 in plasma contains an internal thioester bond and hydrolyzes spontaneously at a very low rate ("tick-over"); the hydrolyzed C3 ($\mathrm{C3(H_2O)}$) binds factor B, factor D cleaves B, and the fluid-phase C3 convertase formed cleaves C3 to give C3b. Once its thioester bond is exposed, C3b can bind covalently to amino or hydroxyl groups on a nearby surface; on the surface it binds factor B again, factor D cleaves B, and the surface C3bBb is formed, bound and stabilized by factor P (properdin; the half-life is prolonged several-fold from about 90 s). On a microbial surface lacking regulatory proteins, once this process begins it amplifies itself (§10.3.2); on a host cell surface, regulatory proteins quickly terminate it.

### 10.2.2. C3 Convertases

**Definition 10.2.2 (C3 Convertase)** An enzyme complex that cleaves C3 into C3a and C3b is called a **C3 convertase**: C4b2b of the classical and lectin pathways, and C3bBb of the alternative pathway.

The two convertases have similar structures: each consists of a large fragment covalently bound to the surface (C4b or C3b) and a fragment with protease activity (C2b or Bb), the enzymatic active site being in the latter. They are very unstable (half-life less than 2 minutes), so complement activation occurs only in the neighborhood where a convertase has formed and also terminates itself quickly unless stabilized.

### 10.2.3. The Common Terminal Pathway and the Membrane Attack Complex

C3 convertases produce large amounts of C3b, some of which binds the convertase itself to form C4b2b3b or C3bBb3b; these are the C5 convertases, which cleave C5 to produce C5a (soluble, a potent anaphylatoxin and chemotactic factor) and C5b. C5b is transiently stable and binds C6 and then C7; a conformational change of C7 exposes a hydrophobic region of the complex, which inserts into the nearby membrane; C8 binds and inserts deeper into the membrane; finally C8 recruits multiple C9.

**Definition 10.2.3 (Membrane Attack Complex)** The transmembrane pore assembled in the membrane of the target cell from C5b, C6, C7, C8 and multiple (10–16) C9 is called the **membrane attack complex** (MAC, $\mathrm{C5b\text{–}9}$).

C9 polymerizes into a ring and forms a hollow pore with an inner diameter of about $10\ \mathrm{nm}$, through which ions and water pass freely, and the cell lyses from osmotic imbalance. The domains of C6–C9 belong to the same membrane-attack protein family as perforin ([7.15.21](/7.15.21)) and bacterial pore-forming toxins. The membrane attack complex kills Gram-negative bacteria (the pore passes through the outer membrane and damages the inner membrane) and is also effective against enveloped viruses, some parasites and some tumor cells; the thick peptidoglycan layer of Gram-positive bacteria prevents the pore from reaching the membrane, and the action of complement on them is mainly opsonization. Nucleated host cells have some resistance to the membrane attack complex: CD59 prevents the polymerization of C9 (§10.5), and pores can be removed by endocytosis or shedding. In people lacking terminal components (C5–C9), the main problem is recurrent *Neisseria* infection (*N. meningitidis* and *N. gonorrhoeae*), showing that the bactericidal action of the membrane attack complex is especially important against these Gram-negative cocci, whereas for other bacteria opsonization is sufficient (§10.6).

## 10.3. The Central Position of C3 and the Amplification Loop

### 10.3.1. The Fates of C3 and C3b

The three pathways converge on C3 because C3 is both the most concentrated component and the source of all effector functions. C3 is a protein of two chains, α and β (about $185\ \mathrm{kDa}$), and the α chain contains an internal thioester bond formed between cysteine and glutamine side chains. When it is cleaved by a convertase, C3a (about $9\ \mathrm{kDa}$) is released from the N terminus of the α chain, and a conformational change in the rest exposes the thioester bond, which becomes a powerful electrophile: within about $100\ \mathrm{\mu s}$ it either reacts with water (C3b is inactivated) or binds covalently to a nucleophilic group on a nearby surface. This extremely short lifetime limits the diffusion of C3b, so that it is deposited only within a few tens of nanometers around the convertase, that is, on the surface where complement has been activated.

C3b deposited on a surface has three fates: (1) as an opsonin it is recognized by receptors such as CR1 and CR3 on phagocytes (§10.4); (2) it binds the convertase to form a C5 convertase and drives the terminal pathway; (3) it binds factor B to generate new C3bBb, so that deposition amplifies itself. Finally, factor I, assisted by cofactors such as factor H, cleaves C3b to the inactive iC3b; iC3b is still a ligand of CR3 and CR4 and continues to promote phagocytosis, and is then degraded to C3dg and C3d, the latter a ligand of CR2 (CD21) on B cells, linking complement to adaptive immunity.

### 10.3.2. A Model: Amplification and Threshold in the Alternative Pathway

The amplification loop makes complement a system with a threshold: once a microbial surface crosses the threshold, C3b deposition bursts rapidly, whereas a host surface is kept at a low level by regulatory proteins. A simple equation shows this. Let the density of C3b on the surface be $x$ (in units of the density that gives half-maximal amplification); then

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = s + a\,\frac{x^2}{1 + x^2} - \delta x. \tag{10.1}
$$

The three terms are: $s$, the basal deposition supplied by tick-over of C3; $a\,x^2/(1+x^2)$, convertase-driven deposition, in which the amount of convertase increases with $x$, and the clustering of C3b on the surface and its stabilization by properdin make this positive feedback cooperative (represented by a Hill exponent of 2) and limited above (by the available receptor sites on the surface and factor B); and $\delta x$, the rate at which factor I and cofactors (factor H, CR1, MCP) inactivate C3b, the size of $\delta$ depending on the amount of regulatory proteins on the surface. This is a typical example of the one-dimensional autonomous ordinary differential equations of [2.4](/2.4); the competition between positive feedback and inactivation is discussed in general in [7.9.15](/7.9.15) and [7.9.16](/7.9.16).

Neglecting $s$ ($s \ll a$), the nonzero steady states satisfy $\delta x^2 - a x + \delta = 0$, that is,

$$
x_\pm = \frac{a \pm \sqrt{a^2 - 4\delta^2}}{2\delta}. \tag{10.2}
$$

They exist only when $\delta \le a/2$: $x_-$ is the unstable threshold and $x_+$ is the stable high-deposition state; $x = 0$ (in fact $x \approx s/\delta$) is the stable low-deposition state. When $\delta > a/2$ only the low state remains, and any initial deposition decays; when $\delta$ is too small the low state $x \approx s/\delta$ also ceases to exist (this requires $\delta^2 \ge 4as$, that is, $\delta \ge 2\sqrt{as}$), because basal deposition alone can trigger the burst. There are therefore three regions:

| Range of $\delta$ | State | Corresponding surface |
|---|---|---|
| $\delta > a/2$ | Only the low-deposition state; any perturbation dies away | Normal host cells (rich in regulatory proteins) |
| $2\sqrt{as} < \delta < a/2$ | Bistable: low and high states coexist, and a burst occurs if the initial dose crosses the threshold $x_-$ | Damaged cells or poorly regulated surfaces |
| $\delta < 2\sqrt{as}$ | Only the high-deposition state; tick-over alone can trigger a burst | Microbes lacking regulatory proteins |

Take $a = 3$ and $s = 0.02$; the two boundary values are $\delta = 1.5$ and $\delta = 2\sqrt{0.06} \approx 0.49$ (the turning points in panel B). For $\delta = 1$, by Eq. (10.2), the threshold is $x_- = (3 - \sqrt{5})/2 \approx 0.38$ and the high state is $x_+ = (3 + \sqrt{5})/2 \approx 2.62$: a trajectory starting from $x_0 = 0.3$ decays back to the low state, whereas one starting from 0.6 jumps to the high state (the dashed and solid lines in panel A).

This model is a qualitative simplification: it merges the consumption of C3 and factor B and the geometry of the surface into a single saturating term, does not distinguish fluid-phase from surface reactions, and does not include the selective binding of factor H to host surfaces. But it captures the essence: complement's "judgment" of a surface does not rest on recognizing a molecule of the pathogen but on the presence or absence of regulatory proteins on the surface, which makes the gain of the positive feedback greater or smaller than inactivation (§10.5).

## 10.4. Biological Functions of Complement

Each product of complement activation has its own actions, and the functions can be summarized by product:

| Product | Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Membrane attack complex | Lysis of target cells | Forms pores in the membrane (§10.2.3) |
| C3b, iC3b, C4b | Opsonization | Recognized by complement receptors on phagocytes, promoting phagocytosis ([7.15.7](/7.15.7)) |
| C3a, C5a (C4a very weak) | Inflammation | Anaphylatoxins, activating mast cells, endothelial cells and smooth muscle; C5a is also a chemoattractant for neutrophils |
| C3b bound to immune complexes | Clearance of immune complexes | CR1 on erythrocytes carries the complexes to the liver and spleen, where macrophages remove them |
| C3d | Enhancement of the B-cell response | Binds CR2 (CD21), lowering the activation threshold of B cells |
| C1q | Clearance of apoptotic cells | Directly recognizes the surface of apoptotic cells, promoting efferocytosis |

**Definition 10.4.1 (Complement Receptor)** Cell-surface molecules that bind activated complement fragments and mediate the biological effects of complement are called **complement receptors**: CR1 (CD35) binds C3b and C4b; CR2 (CD21) binds C3d; CR3 (CD11b/CD18) and CR4 (CD11c/CD18) are integrins that bind iC3b; CRIg binds C3b on liver macrophages; C3aR and C5aR are G-protein-coupled receptors that bind C3a and C5a.

**Definition 10.4.2 (Anaphylatoxin)** The small fragments C3a, C4a and C5a produced by complement activation can cause smooth-muscle contraction, increased vascular permeability and mast-cell degranulation, and are called **anaphylatoxins**; C5a is the most potent.

Opsonization is the most important function of complement in the body: CR1 and CR3 on the surface of phagocytes bind C3b and iC3b, so that microbes coated with them are readily ingested (on CR3, the integrin signal also needs other receptors acting at the same time to trigger phagocytosis). C3b produced by the lectin and alternative pathways covers the pathogen before antibodies appear, so complement is the main humoral component of opsonization in innate immunity. C5a and C3a activate endothelium and leukocytes through C5aR and C3aR and are important participants in acute inflammation; excessive inflammatory action of them is one cause of shock and organ damage ([7.15.7](/7.15.7)). When immune complexes are deposited, C3b is carried by erythrocytes through CR1 to the liver and spleen; people deficient in early components (C1q, C2, C4) cannot clear immune complexes and readily develop systemic lupus erythematosus, showing how important this function is in avoiding autoimmunity.

Complement also contributes to adaptive immunity. CR2 (CD21) on B cells forms the coreceptor complex with CD19 and CD81, and C3d covalently bound to antigen cross-links CR2 and the BCR at the same time, lowering the activation threshold of B cells about 1000-fold ([7.15.19](/7.15.19), [7.15.20](/7.15.20)); the actions of C3a and C5a on dendritic cells and T cells also influence the direction of adaptive immunity. In the nervous system, C1q and C3 take part in the pruning of synapses by microglia, one of the nonimmune functions of complement.

## 10.5. Regulation of Complement

The killing and inflammatory actions of the complement system are powerful, and its initiation has no selectivity of recognition molecules: C3b of the alternative pathway binds covalently to any surface it meets, host cells included. Strict regulation is therefore needed so that complement proceeds only on the surfaces where it is required.

**Definition 10.5.1 (Complement Regulatory Protein)** Proteins that inhibit the individual steps of complement activation and protect the host's cells and tissues from complement damage are called **complement regulatory proteins**.

| Regulatory protein | Location | Step acted on | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 inhibitor (C1-INH) | Plasma | C1, MASP | Serine protease inhibitor; inactivates C1r, C1s and MASP and dissociates them from the complex |
| C4-binding protein | Plasma | C4b2b | Accelerates dissociation of C4b2b; cofactor for cleavage of C4b by factor I |
| Factor H | Plasma, binds host surfaces | C3bBb | Competes with factor B for binding C3b and accelerates dissociation of C3bBb; cofactor for cleavage of C3b by factor I |
| Factor I | Plasma | C3b, C4b | Serine protease; cleaves C3b and C4b with the aid of cofactors (factor H, C4-binding protein, MCP, CR1) |
| DAF (CD55) | Cell membrane | C3 convertases | Accelerates dissociation of C4b2b and C3bBb |
| MCP (CD46) | Cell membrane | C3b, C4b | Cofactor for factor I |
| CR1 (CD35) | Cell membrane | C3b, C4b | Accelerates dissociation; also a cofactor |
| CD59 | Cell membrane | Membrane attack complex | Binds C8 and C9 and prevents C9 polymerization |

Regulation works by three mechanisms: dissociating the enzyme complex (decay acceleration), inactivating the enzyme substrate (cleavage by factor I, which requires cofactors), and preventing assembly of the terminal complex. Host cells protect themselves with DAF, MCP, CR1 and CD59 on the membrane and, with sialic acid and glycosaminoglycans on the surface, bind factor H from plasma; pathogens lack these components, and their surfaces are the target of complement activation. This is also the answer to how the alternative pathway "distinguishes self from nonself": it does not recognize a particular molecule of the pathogen but recognizes the lack of protection on the surface. The size of $\delta$ in Eq. (10.1) means exactly this.

## 10.6. Complement and Disease

### 10.6.1. Deficiency and Overactivation

Both deficiency and overactivation of complement cause disease, and the deficient component points to its main function in the body:

| Deficient component | Consequence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| C1q, C4, C2 (early components) | Immune-complex disease, systemic lupus erythematosus | Cannot clear immune complexes and apoptotic cells |
| C3 | Severe recurrent pyogenic bacterial infections | Loss of opsonization; the most severe |
| C5–C9, properdin | Recurrent *Neisseria* infection | The membrane attack complex is essential for killing *Neisseria* |
| MBL | Mild; increased infections in infants and young children | Deficiency of the lectin pathway; common polymorphism |
| C1 inhibitor | Hereditary angioedema | C1-INH also inhibits the kinin system, and excess bradykinin increases vascular permeability |
| Factor H, factor I, or MCP mutations | Atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, C3 glomerulopathy; accelerated age-related macular degeneration | C3 convertase on host surfaces is unregulated, damaging endothelium and glomeruli |
| DAF, CD59 (loss of the GPI anchor) | Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria | Erythrocytes lose protection and are lysed by complement |

Overactivation occurs in immune-complex disease ([7.15.27](/7.15.27)), ischemia–reperfusion injury, transplant rejection (in hyperacute rejection, preexisting antibodies of the recipient activate complement, [7.15.29](/7.15.29)) and the uncontrolled inflammation of some infections. Drugs that target complement include anti-C5 monoclonal antibody (blocking the substrate of the C5 convertase; used in paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, and therefore requiring prophylaxis against *Neisseria* infection), C3 inhibitors, and C1 inhibitor replacement therapy.

### 10.6.2. Complement Evasion by Pathogens

Complement is a powerful selective pressure on pathogens, and many have therefore evolved evasion mechanisms, most of them hijacking the host's regulatory proteins or directly inhibiting the enzymes of complement ([7.11.34](/7.11.34)):

- Recruiting host regulatory proteins: the M protein of group A *Streptococcus*, the factor H-binding protein of *Neisseria meningitidis* and the surface proteins of some spirochetes bind factor H; some bacteria coat themselves with sialic acid (the capsules of *N. meningitidis* and group B *Streptococcus*), mimicking the binding sites of host surfaces.
- Inhibiting complement enzymes: proteins of *Staphylococcus aureus* bind and inhibit the C3 convertase or disable the C5a receptor.
- Borrowing host membrane regulatory proteins: HIV and some other viruses carry DAF and CD59 into their envelopes when they bud; poxviruses encode homologs of complement control proteins.
- Preventing the terminal pathway: the cell wall of Gram-positive bacteria and the long chains of LPS of Gram-negative bacteria both keep the membrane attack complex from reaching the membrane.

### 10.6.3. Evolutionary Origin

Complement is one of the most ancient components of humoral immunity. C3 and its protease, factor B, are already present in cnidarians and echinoderms, so the alternative pathway (perhaps together with the lectin pathway) is the most ancient, and antibody-independent complement opsonization is a basic immune mechanism of invertebrates and jawless vertebrates; the lamprey has the lectin pathway and C3 opsonization but no antibodies. The classical pathway appeared together with the antibody system of jawed vertebrates and gave complement specific recognition: C1q is not a collectin (it belongs to the C1q/TNF superfamily), but like MBL it has a collagen-like tail and globular heads, and the two are structurally similar and functionally equivalent. The protein family relationships of complement also show this: C3, C4 and C5 belong to the α2-macroglobulin family, C6–C9 belong to the membrane attack complex/perforin (MACPF) family, and C2 and factor B are homologous proteases.

## Questions

1. Why does the complement cascade use the structure of "enzymatic amplification plus a cascade"? What are its advantages and risks?

   **Answer** Each activated enzyme molecule can cleave many substrates, and several cascade steps multiply, so that very few initiating events produce large numbers of effector molecules in a very short time; at the same time, covalent deposition of C3b concentrates the effects on the surface of the pathogen. The risk is that the amplification is not selective: once initiated on a host surface, the damage is amplified too, so multiple layers of regulation are needed. The convertases are themselves unstable, C3b has an extremely short lifetime, host surfaces are rich in regulatory proteins, and the alternative pathway recognizes "lack of protection" rather than a molecule of the pathogen.

2. A boy has had recurrent meningococcal meningitis but few other bacterial infections. Which complement components should be examined first? Why does this deficiency not cause severe infections with other bacteria?

   **Answer** The terminal components C5–C9 and properdin should be examined (total complement activity CH50 is reduced while the alternative pathway may be normal; in people lacking C3 the spectrum of infection is broader). The action of complement against most bacteria depends mainly on opsonization (C3b and iC3b with phagocytes), and this route is intact when terminal components are deficient. *Neisseria* are Gram-negative cocci that are killed mainly by the membrane attack complex and, once phagocytosed, can resist intracellular killing, so they are especially sensitive to a deficiency of the membrane attack complex.

3. In Eq. (10.1) take $a = 3$ and $s = 0.02$. For what range of $\delta$ is the system bistable? For $\delta = 1.2$, what are the unstable threshold $x_-$ and the high steady state $x_+$? What does this result suggest for factor H deficiency?

   **Answer** The bistable range is $2\sqrt{as} < \delta < a/2$, that is, about $0.49 < \delta < 1.5$. For $\delta = 1.2$, by Eq. (10.2), $x_\pm = (3 \pm \sqrt{9 - 5.76})/2.4 = (3 \pm 1.8)/2.4$, that is, $x_- = 0.5$ and $x_+ = 2.0$. Deficiency of factor H (or MCP, etc.) lowers $\delta$ on host surfaces and moves the system into the bistable region or even the monostable high-state region: small perturbations (infection, ischemia) that were originally below threshold and suppressed can now cause explosive deposition of C3b, which is the mechanism of atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome and C3 glomerulopathy.

4. Anti-C5 antibody is effective in paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria; why must patients still be vaccinated against meningococcus? Would an anti-C3 drug for the same disease remove this concern?

   **Answer** The lysis of erythrocytes in paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria is caused by the membrane attack complex downstream of C5. Blocking C5 protects the erythrocytes, but patients lose the bactericidal action of the MAC and their susceptibility to *Neisseria* increases, so prophylaxis is needed. A C3 inhibitor blocks further upstream and so also affects opsonization, giving a broader susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria; but it can at the same time prevent the extravascular hemolysis caused by C3b deposition on erythrocyte surfaces (erythrocytes opsonized with C3b are cleared by macrophages of the liver and spleen), so the indications and infection risks of the two classes of drugs differ.

5. (Open question) The complement system is called "a bridge between innate and adaptive immunity." Explain this using the mechanisms of this chapter.

   **Answer** Key points: (1) the classical pathway is initiated by antibody, and adaptive immunity obtains effector function through complement; (2) the lectin and alternative pathways are independent of antibody and belong to innate immunity, but the C3b they produce can likewise opsonize; (3) C3d and CR2 on B cells cause antigen and BCR to be cross-linked together, turning complement deposition into a "danger signal" for adaptive immunity; (4) C3a and C5a influence dendritic cells and T cells; (5) complement clears immune complexes and apoptotic cells and prevents autoimmunity, and its deficiency is associated with lupus.
