The preceding chapter ([7.15.26](/7.15.26)) described Type I hypersensitivity, carried out by IgE and mast cells. This chapter covers the other three types, following a single thread, the effector molecule: in Type II, antibody binds to antigen on cells or matrix; in Type III, complexes of antibody with soluble antigen are deposited in tissue; in Type IV, the effector is the **T cell**. These three pathways use effector mechanisms already introduced in earlier chapters: complement ([7.15.10](/7.15.10)), Fc receptors with phagocytes and NK cells ([7.15.21](/7.15.21)), activation of Th1 cells and macrophages ([7.15.17](/7.15.17)), and CTL killing. This chapter therefore introduces no new molecules, only effectors directed at the wrong target.

For each type there is only one question, which differs in the antigenic setting: what is the target of the antibody or T cell, and can the target be cleared? If the target is foreign and one-off (a transfusion, an injection), the reaction is self-limiting; if the target is self and persistent (red cells, glomerular basement membrane, self DNA), the reaction becomes chronic and becomes autoimmune disease ([7.15.28](/7.15.28)); inflammation in which the effectors can never be switched off and yet cannot clear the target is the chronic inflammation treated at the end of this chapter.

## 27.1. Type II: Action of Antibody on Cellular and Matrix Antigens

Type II hypersensitivity is caused by IgG or IgM binding to antigens on cell surfaces or in the extracellular matrix. The antigen may be an alloantigen (the ABO and Rh antigens of red cells, HLA), a self antigen whose structure has been altered, a drug hapten adsorbed to cells, or a self antigen (autoimmune disease). After antibody binding, harm or functional change results through five mechanisms:

| Mechanism | Process | Typical targets and examples |
|---|---|---|
| Complement-mediated lysis | IgM or IgG activates the classical pathway, and C5b–9 forms the membrane attack complex; C3a and C5a recruit inflammatory cells | Red cells (intravascular hemolysis) |
| **Opsonization** and phagocytosis | C3b and IgG are recognized by **complement receptors** and **Fc receptors** of macrophages and phagocytosed in the spleen and liver | Red cells and platelets (extravascular hemolysis) |
| **ADCC** | NK cells and others kill IgG-coated cells through FcγRIIIA | Antibody-coated cells |
| Stimulation or blockade of receptors | The antibody does not kill the cell but changes the function of the receptor | Graves disease (stimulates the thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor), myasthenia gravis (blocks and degrades the acetylcholine receptor) |
| Inflammation of matrix and basement membrane | Antibody deposited on the basement membrane; complement and neutrophils cause damage | Anti-glomerular basement membrane disease (Goodpasture syndrome; the target is the α3 chain of collagen IV) |

### 27.1.1. Transfusion Reactions and Blood Groups

The ABO **blood group** antigens are terminal sugars of glycans on the surface of red cells (and of epithelial and endothelial cells): the A and B antigens are made by different glycosyltransferases that add N-acetylgalactosamine or galactose to the H antigen; type O individuals have no active glycosyltransferase and only the H antigen. Everyone naturally has IgM antibodies against the A or B antigen that they lack: these arise as cross-reactions from the response to similar glycans on the surface of intestinal bacteria and need no stimulation by transfusion or pregnancy (for the inheritance of blood groups see [7.4](/7.4)).

| Blood group | Red-cell antigen | Natural antibodies in serum | Red cells that can be received |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | A | Anti-B | A, O |
| B | B | Anti-A | B, O |
| AB | A, B | None | A, B, AB, O |
| O | None (H) | Anti-A, anti-B | O |

When incompatible red cells are transfused, the IgM of the recipient binds at once and activates complement, and the red cells lyse in the vessels; the free hemoglobin released damages the kidney, and complement anaphylatoxins and cytokines cause fever, hypotension and coagulation abnormalities. Blood typing and cross-matching are therefore performed before transfusion: serum of the recipient is mixed with red cells of the donor and agglutination is looked for (for the principle of agglutination see [7.15.34](/7.15.34)). The same mechanism causes hyperacute rejection in organ transplantation ([7.15.29](/7.15.29)).

### 27.1.2. Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn

**Definition 27.1.1 (Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn)** The disease in which IgG antibodies produced by the mother against antigens of fetal red cells cross the placenta into the fetus, destroying the red cells of the fetus or newborn and causing hemolytic anemia and jaundice, is called **hemolytic disease of the newborn** (HDN).

The typical cause is incompatibility of the D antigen of the Rh blood group: an Rh-negative mother (lacking the D antigen) carrying an Rh-positive fetus. In the first pregnancy only very few fetal red cells enter the maternal circulation; at delivery the placenta detaches and many fetal red cells enter the mother, activating D-specific B cells, which produce IgM (which does not cross the placenta) and form memory B cells. In the next pregnancy the memory B cells are reactivated, and the IgG produced after class switching crosses the placenta through the neonatal Fc receptor ([7.15.21](/7.15.21)), coats the fetal red cells and causes their removal in the fetal spleen.

The principle of prevention is "suppressing the response with antibody": anti-D IgG given to the mother around delivery binds the fetal red cells that have entered her body and clears them rapidly, so that these antigens have no time to be recognized by B cells, preventing primary sensitization and formation of memory (suppression of B-cell activation by antibody also depends on FcγRIIB, see 25.6). Hemolytic disease of the newborn due to ABO incompatibility is more common but milder: an O mother naturally has IgG antibodies against the A or B antigen, so no prior sensitization is needed and even the first child may be affected; but the density of A and B antigens on red cells is low and these antigens are widely distributed on other tissues, so they are "diluted" and the damage is milder.

### 27.1.3. Drug-Induced Cytopenias and Altered Receptor Function

Some drugs bind covalently or noncovalently to membrane proteins of red cells, granulocytes or platelets and form hapten–carrier complexes (for the hapten–carrier effect see [7.15.8](/7.15.8)), inducing antibodies; the antibodies bind the drug-modified cells and cause hemolytic anemia, granulocytopenia or thrombocytopenia, and the reaction disappears when the drug is stopped. Antibodies may also, without killing cells, only change the function of a receptor: in Graves disease, IgG against the thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor mimics the ligand and continuously stimulates the thyroid to synthesize hormone, outside the control of negative feedback; in myasthenia gravis, antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor block the receptor, accelerate its internalization and degradation and activate complement, destroying the end plate (the mechanisms of these two diseases are in [7.15.28](/7.15.28)).

## 27.2. Type III: Immune Complexes

Binding of antigen to antibody forms an **immune complex**, a normal step in humoral immunity; the complexes are ordinarily cleared by phagocytes: complexes bound to C3b attach to CR1 on the surface of red cells, are carried to the spleen and liver and removed by macrophages. Type III hypersensitivity occurs when complexes are deposited because they are cleared too slowly. The conditions are:

- Antigen is persistently present in large amounts, or the antibody-to-antigen ratio produces soluble complexes of intermediate size (see 27.2.2).
- The complexes have an affinity for tissue, or carry strong charge, and are not easily phagocytosed.
- The clearance system is impaired (deficiency of early complement components, so that complexes cannot be solubilized and transported; abnormal Fc receptor function; reduced splenic function).
- Features of the local vessels: sites with high blood pressure and filtration (glomeruli, synovium of joints, small vessels of the skin) favor deposition of complexes.

The chain of injury after deposition is as follows. Complexes bind Fc receptors and complement in the vessel wall; mast cells and platelets release vasoactive amines, which cause endothelial cells to contract and vascular permeability to increase so that complexes enter the vessel wall; C3a and C5a (anaphylatoxins) generated by complement activation and cytokines recruit **neutrophils**; neutrophils recognize the complexes through FcγR and complement receptors, but complexes deposited on basement membrane cannot be ingested, so the neutrophils undergo "frustrated phagocytosis" and release proteases (elastase, collagenase) and oxygen radicals outside the cell, damaging basement membrane and surrounding tissue; platelet activation causes microthrombi. Depending on the site, the result is called vasculitis, glomerulonephritis or arthritis.

### 27.2.1. Arthus Reaction and Serum Sickness

**Definition 27.2.1 (Arthus Reaction)** The vasculitic reaction in which an individual who already has large amounts of specific IgG is reinjected subcutaneously or intradermally with the same antigen, and at the injection site the antigen forms large precipitating immune complexes with antibody near the vessel wall, causing local edema, hemorrhage and necrosis within 4–10 hours, is called the **Arthus reaction**.

It was first found in rabbits repeatedly injected subcutaneously with horse serum; the corresponding human situations are local or pulmonary Arthus-like reactions on repeated injection of insulin or on inhalation of environmental organic dusts (such as "farmer's lung" caused by actinomycetes in moldy hay). Its characteristics are that antibody is already present (so it appears within hours, faster than Type IV), the antigen is introduced locally, and with a high antibody concentration the antigen forms large complexes with antibody on the spot, deposited in the walls of small venules.

**Definition 27.2.2 (Serum Sickness)** The self-limiting systemic disease in which, about 1–2 weeks after a single large injection of a foreign serum protein (or other foreign antigen), antibody appears while antigen has not yet been cleared, circulating immune complexes form and are deposited at multiple sites, causing fever, rash, joint pain and glomerular injury, is called **serum sickness**.

The disease first appeared in the early twentieth century in patients treated for diphtheria with antitoxin in horse serum. Its time course explains the mechanism: a large amount of antigen enters at once and is gradually metabolized; antibody production begins on days 5–7, and the antibody forms complexes with the remaining antigen; the complexes are deposited, complement is consumed and symptoms appear; antibody continues to increase and the antigen is cleared, and the complexes shift to a large, readily phagocytosed type, so that the disease remits. Modern analogues are seen in patients treated with murine or chimeric monoclonal antibodies who respond to the antibody (one reason therapeutic antibodies are "humanized", [7.15.9](/7.15.9)), and in reactions caused by drugs (penicillin, sulfonamides) acting as haptens. If the antigen is a self antigen that cannot be cleared, such as the nuclear antigens in systemic lupus erythematosus or the denatured IgG in rheumatoid arthritis, complexes are produced and deposited continually and a chronic Type III disease results ([7.15.28](/7.15.28)).

### 27.2.2. Size of Complexes: the Precipitin Curve

The size of the complexes is determined by the ratio of antibody to antigen. Bivalent (or multivalent) antibody and multivalent antigen can link alternately to form a lattice. In antibody excess, each antigen molecule is capped by many antibodies and only small complexes form; in antigen excess, each of the two arms of an antibody binds one antigen and again only small complexes form; at equivalence the lattice can extend without limit and forms a large, insoluble precipitate. This is the precipitin curve of Heidelberger and Kendall (the amount of precipitate first rises with antigen, peaks in the equivalence zone and then falls, the precipitate redissolving in antigen excess).

**Example 27.2.1 (Lattice Model with Random Bonding)** Let the antigen have $f$ identical sites (with $n_{\mathrm{Ag}}$ molecules, a total of $F = f n_{\mathrm{Ag}}$ sites) and the antibody 2 sites (a total of $A = 2n_{\mathrm{Ab}}$ sites). Suppose binding is so strong that all sites of the scarcer kind are bound, forming $\min(F, A)$ bonds between randomly chosen sites of the two kinds, with no rings and no steric hindrance. This is a Flory–Stockmayer-type random graph: with reacted fractions $p_{\mathrm{A}}$ (antigen sites) and $p_{\mathrm{B}}$ (antibody sites), a giant complex (gel) spanning the whole system appears when

$$
p_{\mathrm{A}}\,p_{\mathrm{B}} > \frac{1}{f - 1}.
$$

In antibody excess ($F < A$) all antigen sites are bound, so $p_{\mathrm{A}} = 1$, $p_{\mathrm{B}} = F/A$ and the product is $F/A$; likewise in antigen excess the product is $A/F$. The gel therefore appears only in the "equivalence zone"

$$
\frac{1}{f - 1} < \frac{F}{A} < f - 1
$$

(for the relation between probability and random graphs see [3.1](/3.1)). Pairing randomly by the above rule using the Monte Carlo method ($n_{\mathrm{Ag}} = 1000$), we count how many antigens the largest complex contains:

![Relation between the antigen-to-antibody ratio and the size of immune complexes (lattice model with random bonding, $n_{\mathrm{Ag}} = 1000$ antigen molecules, $f = 4$ and 8). A: fraction of antigen molecules contained in the largest complex as a function of the ratio $F/A$ of antigen sites to antibody sites; the vertical dotted lines are the mean-field boundaries of the equivalence zone, $1/(f-1)$ and $f-1$, and the dashed line marks $F/A = 1$. B: weight-average size (in number of molecules) of the remaining (soluble) complexes apart from the largest; its peak lies on the two sides of the equivalence zone, and near $F/A = 1$ there are no soluble complexes, where the curve is interrupted.](./plots/precipitin-curve.py)

The equivalence zone given by the model widens as $f$ increases ($1/3 < F/A < 3$ for $f = 4$ and $1/7 < F/A < 7$ for $f = 8$): the higher the valence of the antigen, the more easily a large network forms over a wide range of ratios. Real precipitin curves are narrower than this model because affinity is finite and there is steric hindrance and ring formation. The result in panel B is related to the pathogenesis of Type III disease: on both sides of the equivalence zone, the largest soluble complexes appear in great numbers, that is, complexes of intermediate size, large enough to deposit on vessel walls yet not readily cleared by phagocytes; the large complexes formed within the equivalence zone are rapidly cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system and so are less harmful, which is why the peak of serum sickness occurs in the stage of mild antigen excess.

## 27.3. Type IV: Delayed-Type Hypersensitivity

**Definition 27.3.1 (Delayed-Type Hypersensitivity)** The inflammatory reaction mediated by antigen-specific effector T cells (mainly Th1 and Th17 cells, but also CD8⁺ T cells) and macrophages, which appears only 24–72 hours after re-exposure to antigen and is characterized by infiltration of mononuclear cells, is called **delayed-type hypersensitivity** (DTH).

Type IV is the only hypersensitivity that needs no antibody, and it is also the protective immune mechanism against intracellular pathogens that antibody cannot reach. It has two stages:

1. Sensitization phase. Antigen is taken up by Langerhans cells or dendritic cells and migrates to the draining lymph node, where it activates naive CD4⁺ T cells, which differentiate into effector Th1 cells (sometimes Th17 cells) and memory cells; this takes 1–2 weeks.
2. Effector phase. On re-exposure to antigen, memory or effector T cells are activated locally by antigen-presenting cells (macrophages, dendritic cells, and in humans endothelial cells also express MHC class II molecules) and secrete IFN-γ, TNF and chemokines; IFN-γ activates macrophages (for the effects of **Th1 cells** see [7.15.17](/7.15.17)), and macrophages release lysosomal enzymes, reactive oxygen species and cytokines and recruit more monocytes. The inflammation appears after 24 hours and peaks at 48–72 hours because cytokine-induced cell infiltration takes time.

At the peak of DTH only about 5% of the cells at the site are antigen-specific T cells; the rest are recruited macrophages and other innate cells, so the T cells supply specificity while macrophages amplify and cause most of the damage (as in Type III, "specific initiation, nonspecific amplification"). **Th17 cells** recruit neutrophils and are another component of some DTH (such as some inflammation of skin and joints).

DTH has three common forms:

- Tuberculin type: in a person who has been infected with *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, protein components of the bacterium injected intradermally cause a local induration after 48–72 hours; this reaction reads whether the individual has memory Th1 cells against *M. tuberculosis* and is an in vivo test of T-cell memory (it is a sign of immunity and does not equal active disease).
- Contact hypersensitivity: small molecules (nickel salts, urushiol of poison ivy, dyes, some drugs) penetrate the skin and bind self proteins, becoming hapten-modified proteins presented to T cells by Langerhans cells of the skin; CD8⁺ T cells directly kill the modified keratinocytes and CD4⁺ T cells assist, so the main effector cells are CD8⁺ T cells, accompanied by the innate "danger" signal supplied by the hapten. This is an example of a **hapten** causing a T-cell-mediated (not antibody-mediated) reaction.
- T-cell-mediated tissue damage: autoimmune diseases mediated by Th1 and Th17 cells (rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease) and type 1 diabetes mediated by CTLs (β cells are killed, see [7.15.28](/7.15.28) and [7.14](/7.14)).

### 27.3.1. Granulomas

**Definition 27.3.2 (Granuloma)** A chronic inflammatory nodule formed when antigen (a pathogen or foreign body) cannot be cleared, consisting mainly of activated macrophages (epithelioid cells and multinucleated giant cells) surrounded by lymphocytes and fibroblasts, is called a **granuloma**.

If in a DTH reaction the antigen cannot be cleared (as *M. tuberculosis* can resist killing in the phagosome and some foreign bodies such as talc and beryllium salts cannot be degraded), the cycle between macrophages and Th1 cells continues (Th1 cells activate macrophages with IFN-γ, and macrophages reactivate Th1 cells with IL-12 and antigen presentation): macrophages differentiate into "epithelioid" cells and fuse into multinucleated giant cells, and a shell of lymphocytes and fibroblasts forms around them, confining the antigen locally. The granuloma is therefore protective (limiting spread of infection) and also destructive: the caseous necrosis at the center of a tuberculous granuloma and the fibrosis of lung tissue impair organ function. Maintenance of a granuloma depends on TNF and IFN-γ, so treatment that inhibits TNF can reactivate latent tuberculosis, a mechanistic cost of that treatment (27.4). Sarcoidosis is a noninfectious granulomatous disease of unknown cause, showing that the same structure can be initiated by factors other than persistent antigen stimulation.

## 27.4. Chronic Inflammation

**Definition 27.4.1 (Chronic Inflammation)** A state in which an inflammatory response persists for more than several weeks because the inciting factor persists or because the inflammation itself cannot be switched off, characterized by infiltration of monocytes, macrophages and lymphocytes with tissue destruction and repair (fibrosis, angiogenesis) coexisting, is called **chronic inflammation**.

Each of the outcomes described above may progress to chronic inflammation: persistent infection (tuberculosis, chronic viral hepatitis), persistent self antigen (autoimmune disease), indigestible foreign bodies (silica, asbestos), recurrent Type I reactions (airway remodeling in asthma), and metabolic stimuli: in the adipose tissue of obesity, macrophages infiltrate and engulf dead adipocytes, and saturated fatty acids, cholesterol crystals and urate crystals produce IL-1β through TLRs and the **inflammasome** ([7.15.6](/7.15.6)), forming a "low-grade inflammation" associated with insulin resistance and atherosclerosis.

Chronic inflammation sustains itself through a self-perpetuating network:

- The macrophage–T-cell cycle: IFN-γ and IL-17 from Th1 and Th17 cells activate macrophages, and macrophages reactivate T cells with IL-12, IL-23 and antigen presentation (a positive-feedback network motif, [7.9.14](/7.9.14)).
- Cytokine amplification: TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 cause endothelial cells to express adhesion molecules, recruiting more leukocytes, and induce the acute-phase response ([7.15.7](/7.15.7)).
- Runaway repair: TGF-β and PDGF cause fibroblasts to proliferate and deposit collagen, and excessive repair becomes fibrosis (loss of organ function); unlike wound healing, there is no stop signal (for repair and regeneration see [7.14](/7.14)).
- New lymphoid tissue: persistent inflammation causes **tertiary lymphoid structures** resembling lymph nodes to appear in the tissue ([7.15.22](/7.15.22)), locally sustaining self-reactive B-cell and T-cell responses.

Interventions in chronic inflammation also reflect its network character: suppressing one cytokine (such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1, IL-17 or IL-23) may cause the whole network to subside, which is why cytokine-targeted drugs ([7.15.11](/7.15.11)) are effective in many chronic inflammatory diseases; and their side effects are precisely the function that is suppressed: TNF is required to resist intracellular infection, so the risk of infection (especially reactivation of granulomatous infections) rises.

## Summary

- The three types differ in effector molecule: Type II is antibody against cellular and matrix antigens, causing disease through complement, phagocytosis, ADCC or altered receptor function; Type III is immune complexes that cause disease after deposition through complement and neutrophils; Type IV is mediated by T cells and macrophages and appears at 24–72 hours.
- Hemolytic disease of the newborn (Definition 27.1.1): IgG crosses the placenta through FcRn; primary sensitization occurs at delivery, and antibody can be given to prevent sensitization; ABO incompatibility is more common but milder.
- Type III disease depends on complexes being cleared too slowly and deposited; the Arthus reaction (Definition 27.2.1) is a local reaction with preexisting antibody, and serum sickness (Definition 27.2.2) is a self-limiting reaction 1–2 weeks after a single large dose of antigen; the size of complexes depends on the antigen-to-antibody ratio (Example 27.2.1, equivalence zone $1/(f-1) < F/A < f-1$).
- The specificity of delayed-type hypersensitivity (Definition 27.3.1) comes from T cells, and amplification and damage from macrophages; when antigen cannot be cleared a granuloma forms (Definition 27.3.2), both protective and destructive.
- Chronic inflammation (Definition 27.4.1) perpetuates itself through the macrophage–T-cell cycle, cytokine amplification and runaway repair; cytokine drugs aimed at nodes of the network are effective, and their side effect is suppression of normal defense.

## Questions

1. Why is the first Rh-positive child usually unaffected by hemolytic disease of the newborn while later children are? Why is ABO incompatibility an exception?

   **Answer** In the first pregnancy few fetal red cells enter the mother, and it is only at delivery, when the placenta detaches, that many enter; only then does the primary response of D-specific B cells occur, the antibody produced being mainly IgM (which does not cross the placenta), and memory B cells form. In the next pregnancy memory B cells rapidly produce class-switched IgG, which crosses the placenta through FcRn and causes fetal hemolysis. ABO incompatibility needs no sensitization: an O mother naturally has antibodies against the A and B antigens, and the IgG portion can cross the placenta already in the first pregnancy; but the A and B antigens on red cells are few and are also expressed in tissues, which disperses the antibody, so the damage is milder.

2. Explain why serum sickness occurs 1–2 weeks after injection and remits spontaneously.

   **Answer** After the first injection of a foreign protein, B cells need several days to complete activation and secrete antibody, which appears on days 5–7. At this time the antigen has not yet been cleared, and in the region of mild antigen excess soluble complexes of intermediate size form, which deposit in glomeruli, joints and small vessels and activate complement and neutrophils, causing symptoms. Antibody continues to increase, the antigen is cleared and the situation turns to antibody excess, the complexes grow larger and are cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system, until the antigen is exhausted, so the reaction is self-limiting. Repeated injection of the same foreign serum causes a faster and more severe reaction (Arthus type) because of the preexisting antibody.

3. The tuberculin test usually shows no obvious reaction within 24 hours and is most pronounced at 72 hours. Why? What does it show and what does it not show?

   **Answer** The reaction results from memory Th1 cells being activated locally by antigen and secreting IFN-γ, TNF and chemokines, which recruit monocytes through endothelial adhesion molecules and activate macrophages, and this takes tens of hours. It shows that the individual has memory T cells against antigens of *M. tuberculosis*, that is, has had contact (infection or vaccination); it cannot distinguish active from latent infection, a cured infection or a response caused by a vaccine, so it serves only as immunological evidence of exposure.
