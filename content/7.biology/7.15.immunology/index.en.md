Immunology studies how the body recognizes and eliminates pathogens, abnormal cells and tissue damage without harming itself. The **immune system** faces three difficulties: pathogens are endlessly diverse and constantly changing, while the host has only a finite set of genes; the stronger its ability to recognize nonself, the greater the risk of attacking self; and the more vigorous its effector responses, the more tissue damage they cause. The solution has two layers. **Innate immunity** uses germline-encoded **pattern recognition receptors** to detect molecular patterns shared by whole classes of microbes and responds within minutes to days. **Adaptive immunity** generates an almost unlimited range of specificities from randomly rearranged antigen receptors; according to the **clonal selection theory**, only the few clones that recognize the antigen expand, and they leave behind **immunological memory**, while **immune tolerance** and layer upon layer of negative feedback keep the system from attacking self. Immunology therefore has a reputation for having too many names: hundreds of CD molecules, dozens of interleukins, more than ten T-cell subsets. Yet all of these names serve five questions along one response pathway: who recognizes and what is recognized, how the system decides after recognition, which effectors eliminate the threat, how it is remembered, and how the response is shut down and kept in check. The answer to each question is in turn split, according to where the pathogen lives, into three effector modules, **type 1 immunity**, **type 2 immunity** and **type 3 immunity**, each with its own matching set of innate lymphoid cells, T-cell subsets, cytokines and antibody classes. The course is also as quantitative as possible: naive lymphocytes specific for a given antigen make up only $10^{-6}$–$10^{-5}$ of the repertoire, and in a response a clone expands about $10^4$-fold and then contracts by more than 90%; the search for antigen in a lymph node, the affinity window of thymic selection, the bistable switch between helper T-cell subsets, the expansion and contraction of T-cell responses, and the race between interferon and cytotoxic T cells are all written as models and simulations.

The course follows this pathway. Chapter 1 is a map of the whole course, introducing the two kinds of immune response, clonal selection, the five questions and the three effector modules. Chapters 2–4 are the parts list: the differentiation of immune cells from hematopoietic stem cells and their lineages, the central and peripheral lymphoid organs where they develop and meet, together with lymphocyte recirculation, and the CD molecules, adhesion molecules and chemokines that let cells recognize one another and reach the right place. Chapters 5–7 cover innate immunity: barriers and innate cells, pattern recognition receptors and their shared downstream pathways, and the innate effector mechanisms of inflammation, phagocytosis and the acute-phase response. Chapters 8–12 lay the molecular foundations of adaptive recognition: antigens, antibody structure and function, complement, **cytokines** and their receptor families, and how antigen receptor genes generate diversity through rearrangement, somatic hypermutation and class switching. Chapters 13–14 describe what T cells see: the genes, structure and polymorphism of the **major histocompatibility complex**, and how antigens are loaded onto MHC molecules by the two processing pathways and by cross-presentation. Chapters 15–18 deal with T cells: positive and negative selection in the thymus, activation by the integration of three signals, differentiation into helper subsets and cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and the expansion, contraction, memory and exhaustion of the response. Chapters 19–21 follow the same line on the B-cell side: development and receptor checkpoints, **affinity maturation** and class switching in the **germinal center**, and the effector functions antibodies recruit through their Fc region. Chapters 22–25 integrate these steps: how a primary response unfolds in space and time across several organs, mucosal and barrier immunity, where tolerance is the default state, central and peripheral tolerance, and the brakes formed by inhibitory receptors, **immune checkpoints** and regulatory T cells. Chapters 26–33 ask where the pathway goes wrong and how it can be exploited: the four types of **hypersensitivity**, **autoimmune diseases**, transplantation immunology, tumor immunology, immunodeficiency diseases (including HIV infection), immunity to infection, which matches effector modules to the location of the pathogen, and the principles of vaccination and immunotherapy. Chapters 34–35 are about methods: the readout principles of experimental techniques in immunology, and the analysis of the **immunophenotype**, which uses combinations of markers to determine a cell's identity and state, together with a marker atlas organized by lineage. The course summary introduces no new material; it places the first 35 chapters back into a grid of five questions by three modules and reads diseases as break points along the pathway.

Immunology is a meeting point of nearly every level of biology. Antibodies and protein–ligand binding come from [biochemistry](/7.1), the molecular mechanism of **V(D)J recombination** from [molecular biology](/7.2), and receptor signaling, apoptosis and inflammasomes, hematopoiesis and cancer from [cell biology](/7.3). The haplotypes and linkage disequilibrium of the MHC connect with [genetics](/7.4), and its balancing selection and the coevolution of hosts and pathogens with [evolutionary biology](/7.10). The physiology of white blood cells, the vascular response in inflammation, lymph circulation and fever is treated in [animal physiology](/7.14), and here we discuss only their immunological mechanisms; the biology of the pathogens themselves, how viruses are restricted by interferons, vaccine platforms, the human microbiome and epidemiology are covered in [microbiology](/7.11), and plant immunity in [plant physiology](/7.13) can be read alongside innate immunity for comparison. The general theory of feedback, bistability and network motifs comes from [systems biology](/7.9), while single-cell and [spatial omics](/7.9.6) are today the main tools for reading the states of immune cells and the tissue microenvironment; the reading of markers in the last two chapters of the course is prepared precisely for such data.

The biology olympiad does not cover immunology, so I had never studied it before university. For a long time afterward my impression of the field was that it meant memorizing countless names of signaling molecules and immune factors. This put me off so much that I did not really look into it until my third year. What finally made me study it systematically was research: in my work on spatial proteomics, most of the pathological markers on tissue sections are immunological molecules, and without understanding their place in the immune response there was no way to interpret the data. When I went back to learn the subject, I found that the difficulty lay not in the names themselves but in the lack of a framework to put them in. Once I had worked out the pathway of who recognizes, how the system decides, what eliminates the threat, how it is remembered and how the response ends, and had grouped everything by the three effector modules, most of the names found a definite place, and for any new molecule I could first ask which question it answers. For this reason I think immunology is well worth studying as a course in its own right. Its knowledge is scattered across biochemistry, cell biology, physiology and microbiology, and encountered piecemeal it is only a string of terms; only when it is learned as a whole does it become clear that it is a control system that strikes a precise balance between recognition, amplification and restraint, and this systems view is exactly what one needs to read pathological markers and the immune microenvironment of a tissue.
