The previous chapter ([7.15.22](/7.15.22)) described how a response unfolds in space and time across the body; this chapter looks at the place where it occurs most often. The epithelia of the skin, gut, respiratory tract and urogenital tract separate the body from the outside world, and the total area of human mucosae is several tens of square meters, most of it in the small intestine. These surfaces are exposed at every moment to large numbers of microbes and food antigens, the great majority of them harmless or even beneficial. The "decision" problem faced by barrier tissues is therefore different from that at other sites: the default state of the immune system here is tolerance, and only when the barrier is breached and danger signals appear does it turn to defense, and it must choose the right effector module (**type 1 immunity**, **type 2 immunity**, **type 3 immunity**, [7.15.1](/7.15.1)). The anatomy of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue was given in [7.15.3](/7.15.3), the transport mechanism of secretory IgA is in [7.15.21](/7.15.21), and the composition and ecology of the **microbiome** itself are in [7.11.32](/7.11.32).

## 23.1. Common Themes of Barrier Immunity

Barrier tissues are broadly similar in structure and all consist of four layers of defense:

| Layer | Components | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Physical and chemical barrier | Epithelial cells and their **tight junctions**; mucus layer; **antimicrobial peptides** (**defensins**, cathelicidin, the REG3 family); lysozyme; low pH; ciliary movement | Keep microbes from touching or crossing the epithelium |
| Sensing by the epithelium itself | Epithelial cells express TLRs, NOD-like receptors and so on, and secrete "alarmins" such as IL-25, IL-33 and TSLP | Convert damage and invasion into cytokine signals |
| Resident immune cells | Macrophages, DCs, ILCs, **intraepithelial lymphocytes**, **tissue-resident memory T cells**, Tregs, IgA plasma cells | Local surveillance and immediate effector action |
| Organized lymphoid tissue and draining lymph nodes | **Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue** (Peyer's patches, isolated lymphoid follicles, tonsils and so on) and draining lymph nodes | Induction of adaptive responses |

Several common rules follow:

1. Inductive sites are separate from effector sites. Organized lymphoid tissues such as Peyer's patches are the "inductive sites" where antigen is sampled and lymphocytes are activated; activated cells circulate through lymph and blood and, with mucosa-specific homing molecules, return to the widely distributed lamina propria and epithelium, the "effector sites".
2. The common mucosal immune system. A fraction of the lymphocytes activated at one mucosa can home to other mucosae (for example IgA plasma cells activated in the gut appear in the mammary gland, so that milk contains secretory IgA against the mother's gut microbes), so vaccination at a mucosal site can sometimes produce protection at distant mucosae.
3. Secretory IgA predominates. The mucosae secrete more IgA each day than all other antibodies in the body combined; it acts outside the epithelium and causes no inflammation ([7.15.21](/7.15.21)).
4. Tolerance is the default state. At steady state barrier tissues are dominated by TGF-β, IL-10, retinoic acid, Tregs and IgA; only when the epithelium is damaged or invasion is sensed do they turn to inflammatory responses (§23.4, §23.5).

## 23.2. Structure of Gut Immunity

The gut epithelium is a simple columnar epithelium that is renewed every 3–5 days and maintained by stem cells at the base of the crypts. Several of its differentiated cell types take part in immunity:

| Cell | Location | Immune function |
|---|---|---|
| Absorptive enterocyte | Villus | Expresses pIgR and transports IgA; secretes antimicrobial proteins such as REG3γ; expresses PRRs |
| Goblet cell | Villi and crypts | Secretes mucus (MUC2); can also deliver small luminal antigens to underlying DCs through goblet cell-associated passages |
| Paneth cell | Base of small intestinal crypts | Secretes α-defensins and lysozyme, protecting the stem cell niche |
| Tuft cell | Scattered | Senses parasites and so on, secretes IL-25 and starts a type 2 response |
| M cell | Follicle-associated epithelium above Peyer's patches and isolated lymphoid follicles | Transports particulate antigen from the lumen to beneath the epithelium (see below) |

The mucus layer of the small intestine is thin and loose; the colon has two layers: an inner layer that is dense and almost free of bacteria, and a loose outer layer that is the habitat of bacteria. This spatial segregation means that at steady state the epithelial surface is hardly in direct contact with bacteria.

**Definition 23.2.1 (Peyer's Patch)** An organized lymphoid tissue in the mucosa and submucosa of the small intestine (especially the ileum), formed by an aggregation of several lymphoid follicles, covered by follicle-associated epithelium, lacking afferent lymphatics, sampling antigen directly from the lumen and inducing mucosal immune responses is called a **Peyer's patch**.

Each Peyer's patch consists of a group of B-cell follicles containing germinal centers, T-cell zones between the follicles, and a subepithelial dome region, rich in DCs, above the follicles. There are about 100–200 in the human small intestine, most of them in the terminal ileum. In their germinal centers, B cells switch in large numbers to IgA under the action of TGF-β, retinoic acid and T cells ([7.15.20](/7.15.20)).

**Definition 23.2.2 (M Cell)** A specialized epithelial cell that lies in the follicle-associated epithelium above mucosal lymphoid tissue, lacks regular microvilli and a thick glycocalyx on its surface, has a basal surface invaginated into a "pocket" that holds lymphocytes and DCs, and transports particles, microbes and macromolecules from the lumen to beneath the epithelium by transcytosis is called an **M cell** (microfold cell).

M cells do not process antigen; they merely "hand" it intact to DCs and macrophages of the dome region, which process it and present it to T cells in the Peyer's patch, and unprocessed antigen can also reach B cells in the follicle directly. M cells are the main portal of active sampling in the gut, which also makes them a gateway through which some pathogens cross the epithelium. Besides M cells, luminal antigen can also be sampled through goblet cell-associated passages, through processes that CX3CR1⁺ macrophages in the lamina propria extend between epithelial cells, and by reverse transport through receptors such as FcRn after binding IgA or IgG.

**Definition 23.2.3 (Intraepithelial Lymphocyte)** A lymphocyte that lies between mucosal epithelial cells (above the basement membrane), consists mainly of T cells, and resides long term and monitors the state of the epithelium is called an **intraepithelial lymphocyte** (IEL).

In the gut there is one IEL for every 5–10 epithelial cells, and they are one of the largest T-cell populations in the body. Most IELs are CD8⁺ T cells, including "induced" IELs that express the classical TCRαβ and CD8αβ (derived from peripheral memory cells) and "natural" IELs that express the CD8αα homodimer, many of which are **γδ T cells**; they express CD103 (integrin αEβ7, which binds epithelial E-cadherin), recognize molecules such as MICA/B on the surface of stressed or damaged epithelial cells (through NKG2D), rapidly kill these cells and secrete factors that promote epithelial repair.

The lamina propria is the connective tissue beneath the epithelium and the main effector site of gut immunity; it contains large numbers of IgA plasma cells, effector and memory T cells (mostly Th17 cells and Tregs), ILC3s, macrophages and DCs.

## 23.3. Secretory IgA: The Antibody That Shapes the Microbiota

Gut IgA has two sources:

- T-cell-dependent IgA: produced in the germinal centers of Peyer's patches, somatically mutated and of relatively high affinity, mainly directed against pathogens and a few specific commensals;
- T-cell-independent IgA: in isolated lymphoid follicles and the lamina propria, BAFF and APRIL secreted by epithelium and DCs together with TGF-β drive B cells (including B-1 cells) to switch to IgA; this IgA is less mutated and more polyreactive and coats commensals broadly.

**Secretory IgA** acts on microbes in several ways: agglutination, so that bacteria are trapped in mucus and expelled by peristalsis ("immune exclusion"); masking of bacterial adhesins and motility organs; and neutralization, in the lamina propria and within the epithelium, of antigens that have already crossed the barrier, carrying them back into the lumen. Because it does not activate complement, none of these actions causes inflammation. IgA deficiency is the most common primary immunodeficiency in humans (about 1 in 600 in European populations), and most affected people have no symptoms, because IgM can also be secreted through pIgR and partly compensates; this shows that mucosal defense is redundant at several levels.

IgA does not merely hold bacteria back; it also shapes the composition of the microbiota: in mice lacking IgA, or lacking somatically mutated IgA, bacteria that adhere closely to the epithelium, such as segmented filamentous bacteria, proliferate greatly in the small intestine, accompanied by systemic immune activation. That IgA coats some bacteria and not others amounts to applying selection pressure to the microbiota.

## 23.4. Steady State: Tolerance Is the Default

What exists in the healthy gut is a kind of "controlled physiological inflammation": the lamina propria is full of immune cells but there is no tissue damage. This state is maintained by a set of interactions among epithelium, DCs, macrophages and lymphocytes:

| Cell | Signal | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Epithelial cells (stimulated by commensals) | TGF-β, retinoic acid, TSLP | Keep DCs and macrophages in a tolerogenic phenotype |
| CD103⁺ conventional DCs | Carry antigen to mesenteric lymph nodes; produce retinoic acid and activated TGF-β | Induce peripherally derived Tregs (pTregs); imprint α4β7 and CCR9 on T and B cells; promote IgA switching |
| CX3CR1⁺ resident macrophages | Strongly phagocytic but "unresponsive" to TLR stimulation; secrete IL-10 | Clear bacteria that cross the epithelium without causing inflammation; maintain Tregs |
| Tregs (FOXP3⁺) | IL-10, TGF-β | Suppress effector responses to microbiota and food antigens |
| ILC3s, Th17 cells | IL-22, IL-17 | IL-22 acts on the epithelium, promotes secretion of antimicrobial peptides and epithelial repair and reinforces the barrier |
| IgA plasma cells | Secretory IgA | Immune exclusion |

Two points deserve notice:

- Regulation and defense coexist. Th17 cells and ILC3s are usually regarded as inflammatory cells, but in the steady-state gut their IL-22 is used mainly to maintain the barrier; the number of Th17 cells in the mouse gut depends on particular commensals (such as segmented filamentous bacteria) and they are almost absent in **germ-free animals**. Germ-free mice have small Peyer's patches, little IgA and few lamina propria T cells, and colonization with a single species can restore part of these, which shows that the development of the gut immune system itself depends on the microbiota ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)).
- A firewall. Even when commensals in the gut are carried away by DCs, they reach only the mesenteric lymph nodes, where they are confined and do not enter the systemic circulation; the response to commensals therefore stays local (IgA and Tregs), and the systemic immune system hardly "sees" them.

Tolerance to food antigens is called **oral tolerance**: protein entering by mouth induces antigen-specific pTregs and T-cell anergy, so that the response is weaker when the same antigen is later encountered by other routes. Its mechanism and clinical use are described in [7.15.24](/7.15.24).

## 23.5. From Tolerance to Defense

When a pathogen breaches the epithelium, or a microbe appears where it should not (for example close to the epithelium or in the lamina propria), the state of the barrier tissue changes. What triggers the switch is not "foreignness" itself but damage and position: dying epithelium releases **damage-associated molecular patterns**, intracellular or basal PRRs are activated, inflammasomes produce IL-1β and IL-18, and DCs change from a tolerogenic to an inflammatory phenotype. The response after the switch selects an effector module according to the type of invader:

| Type of invader | Signals from epithelium and innate cells | Effector module | Main effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extracellular bacteria and fungi that cross the epithelium | DCs and macrophages secrete IL-23, IL-1β, IL-6 | Type 3: ILC3, Th17 | IL-17 recruits neutrophils, IL-22 promotes secretion of antimicrobial peptides and epithelial regeneration |
| Intracellular pathogens, viruses | IL-12, type I and type III interferons | Type 1: Th1, CTLs, NK cells, resident CD8⁺ T cells | Killing of infected epithelial cells, IFN-γ activation of macrophages |
| Intestinal helminths | IL-25 from tuft cells, IL-33 and TSLP from the epithelium | Type 2: ILC2, Th2 | IL-13 causes goblet cell hyperplasia and copious mucus secretion and stronger smooth muscle contraction ("weep and sweep"); IL-5 recruits eosinophils; IgE and mast cells |

After the infection is cleared, the barrier tissue must return to the tolerant state: Tregs, IL-10 and tissue repair programs resolve inflammation and leave tissue-resident memory cells behind. A barrier that cannot return is chronic inflammation.

## 23.6. Barrier Imbalance: Dysbiosis, Inflammatory Bowel Disease and Celiac Disease

**Dysbiosis** ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)) means that the composition or function of the microbiome deviates from the healthy state, for example by a fall in diversity, a decrease in bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids, and an increase in Proteobacteria close to the epithelium. Short-chain fatty acids (especially butyrate) are an energy source of the colonic epithelium and promote Treg differentiation by inhibiting histone deacetylases, so the composition of the microbiota can directly change the balance of immunity. Dysbiosis is associated with many immune-mediated diseases, but in most cases it is hard to tell whether it is cause or consequence.

**Definition 23.6.1 (Inflammatory Bowel Disease)** A group of chronic relapsing inflammatory diseases of the digestive tract, of unknown cause and driven by an imbalance of immune tolerance to gut commensals in genetically susceptible individuals, is called **inflammatory bowel disease** (IBD); it comprises mainly Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis.

| | Crohn disease | Ulcerative colitis |
|---|---|---|
| Site | Any part from mouth to anus, most often the terminal ileum, with skip lesions | Starts in the rectum and spreads continuously proximally, confined to the colon |
| Depth | Transmural inflammation, can form granulomas, strictures and fistulas | Confined to mucosa and submucosa |
| Dominant type of immunity | Types 1 and 3 (IFN-γ, IL-17, the IL-23 axis) | Mixed, partly with type 2 features (IL-13) |
| Genetic clues (examples) | *NOD2* (intracellular sensor of bacterial peptidoglycan), *ATG16L1* (autophagy), *IL23R* | *IL23R*, genes related to the epithelial barrier |

Genetics gives a clear mechanistic framework: the known susceptibility genes cluster in three kinds of functions, namely intracellular sensing and clearance of microbes (*NOD2*, autophagy genes), the epithelial barrier, and regulation by the IL-23–Th17 axis and IL-10. Loss-of-function mutations in *IL10* or *IL10R* cause severe colitis already in infancy, which shows directly that IL-10-mediated tolerance is indispensable to the gut. The principles of treatment also correspond to these pathways: anti-TNF antibodies, antibodies against the common p40 subunit of IL-12/IL-23 or the p19 subunit of IL-23, JAK inhibitors, and antibodies that block the binding of integrin α4β7 to MAdCAM-1 and prevent effector cells from homing to the gut ([7.15.22](/7.15.22)).

Celiac disease is a disease of the small intestine caused by a T-cell response to dietary gluten (the prolamins in wheat and other cereals), and its mechanism is one of the few examples that have been fully elucidated:

1. Gluten is rich in proline, and some of its peptides resist digestive enzymes and reach the small intestinal lamina propria as relatively long fragments;
2. Tissue transglutaminase 2 (TG2) deamidates glutamine in these peptides to glutamic acid, introducing negative charge;
3. The negatively charged peptides bind with high affinity to the peptide-binding groove of HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8 (whose anchor positions prefer negatively charged residues), and almost all patients carry one of these two alleles (for the association of the **major histocompatibility complex** with disease see [7.15.13](/7.15.13));
4. Gluten-specific CD4⁺ T cells are activated and secrete IFN-γ and IL-21; under the action of IL-15 the epithelium expresses NKG2D ligands, IELs are "licensed" to kill epithelial cells, and villous atrophy and crypt hyperplasia result;
5. B cells produce autoantibodies against TG2 (TG2 forms a complex with gluten peptides, B cells recognize TG2 and T cells recognize the gluten peptides, an example of linked recognition, [7.15.20](/7.15.20)), and anti-TG2 IgA is the main serological marker for diagnosis.

After gluten is removed from the diet, T cells lose their antigen, the lesions resolve and antibody levels fall; this is why the model of "environmental antigen + specific HLA + autoantigen" can be tested directly.

## 23.7. Other Barrier Tissues

| Barrier | Epithelium | Characteristic immune components | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skin | Stratified squamous epithelium (epidermis) and stratum corneum; dermis | Langerhans cells and resident CD8⁺ memory T cells in the epidermis; DC subsets, macrophages, mast cells, large numbers of Tregs and circulating CD4⁺ memory T cells in the dermis; γδ T cells (dendritic epidermal T cells in the mouse epidermis) | The thickest physical barrier; keratinocytes secrete antimicrobial peptides and IL-1 family cytokines; skin commensals can induce protective T cells without inflammation |
| Respiratory tract | Pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium in the upper airways; extremely thin type I and type II alveolar epithelium in the alveoli | Nasal-associated lymphoid tissue and tonsils; alveolar macrophages; inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT, a kind of TLS) that can form after infection | **Mucociliary clearance** is the main physical defense; the alveoli must suppress inflammation while preserving gas exchange, and alveolar macrophages have a high threshold |
| Urogenital tract | Stratified squamous epithelium in the vagina, columnar epithelium in the uterus and urethra | DCs and T cells in the mucosa; IgG and IgA coexist in the female genital tract (IgG transported by FcRn) | Regulated by the sex hormone cycle; vaginal lactobacilli maintain a low pH |
| Ocular surface | Corneal and conjunctival epithelium | Conjunctiva-associated lymphoid tissue; secretory IgA and lysozyme in tears | The anterior chamber is a site of **immune privilege** ([7.15.24](/7.15.24)) |

At all these sites tolerance predominates while the barrier is healthy, and type 1, 2 or 3 responses are selected according to the type of invader at times of damage or invasion; the immune responses of different barriers also influence one another, for example a barrier defect in the skin is associated with sensitization to allergens, which is one explanation for why eczema often precedes food allergy and asthma in the "atopic march" ([7.15.26](/7.15.26)).

## Summary

- Four layers of defense: physical and chemical barrier (epithelium, tight junctions, mucus, antimicrobial peptides), sensing by the epithelium, resident immune cells and organized lymphoid tissue; inductive sites are separate from effector sites, and the common mucosal immune system connects the mucosae of different sites.
- Structure of the gut: the **Peyer's patch** (Definition 23.2.1) is the inductive site, the **M cell** (Definition 23.2.2) hands luminal antigen to dome DCs, **intraepithelial lymphocytes** (Definition 23.2.3) monitor the epithelium, and the lamina propria is the effector site.
- **Secretory IgA**: T-cell-dependent and T-cell-independent sources; agglutination, immune exclusion and neutralization without causing inflammation, and shaping the composition of the microbiota.
- **Steady state**: TGF-β and retinoic acid from the epithelium, induction of pTregs and IgA by CD103⁺ DCs, IL-10 from CX3CR1⁺ macrophages, IL-22 from ILC3s and Th17 cells; the mesenteric lymph nodes act as a firewall; oral tolerance to food antigens.
- Switch to defense: triggered by damage and position; extracellular bacteria → type 3 (IL-23, IL-17, IL-22), intracellular pathogens → type 1, helminths → type 2 (tuft cells, IL-25, ILC2, IL-13).
- Imbalance: **dysbiosis**; the susceptibility genes of **inflammatory bowel disease** (Definition 23.6.1) cluster in microbial sensing and autophagy, the epithelial barrier, the IL-23–Th17 axis and IL-10; celiac disease is a fully described mechanism of TG2-deamidated gluten peptides + HLA-DQ2/DQ8 + CD4⁺ T cells + killing by IELs.

## Questions

1. Why does the gut immune system need a structure in which inductive sites are separate from effector sites? What would be the problem if every piece of lamina propria could activate naive lymphocytes independently?

   **Answer** Activation of naive lymphocytes requires bringing together rare specific precursors, antigen-bearing DCs and helper cells ([7.15.22](/7.15.22)), which only organized structures such as Peyer's patches and mesenteric lymph nodes can do efficiently; at the same time, the controlled entry of antigen into these sites through M cells and DCs determines the type of response (mostly IgA and Tregs). If every piece of lamina propria could activate naive cells, the large amounts of commensal and food antigens would provoke responses everywhere without unified regulation, and tolerance would be hard to maintain; distribution of the activated effector cells by homing throughout the gut then ensures sampling at one site and protection of the whole length.

2. People with IgA deficiency mostly have no symptoms, yet mice deficient in pIgR show marked barrier defects and systemic immune activation. How can the difference be explained?

   **Answer** When humans lack IgA, pentameric IgM also contains the J chain and can be transported to the mucosal surface by pIgR, partly compensating for the function of IgA; pIgR deficiency, by contrast, prevents transport of both IgA and IgM, so the mucosal surface loses all secretory antibody, commensals contact the epithelium more and cross the barrier, and systemic immune activation results. The comparison shows that what is truly irreplaceable is the function of "delivering polymeric antibody to the surface" and not any one isotype.

3. Almost all patients with celiac disease carry HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8, yet only a small fraction of people who carry these alleles become ill. In light of the mechanism in this chapter, what other conditions are needed?

   **Answer** HLA-DQ2/DQ8 is a necessary condition, since it allows deamidated gluten peptides to be presented efficiently; disease also requires: (1) dietary gluten (the environmental antigen); (2) activation of TG2 and deamidation, which are enhanced during tissue damage or inflammation, for example after a gut infection; (3) stress of the epithelium and raised IL-15, which give IELs the ability to kill epithelium; (4) breakdown of oral tolerance to gluten, that is, Tregs and tolerogenic DCs are insufficient to suppress gluten-specific CD4⁺ T cells; and other non-HLA susceptibility genes. HLA thus explains "who may become ill", and the environment and failure of tolerance determine "who actually becomes ill".

4. (Open question) An antibody against integrin α4β7 can treat inflammatory bowel disease, whereas an antibody against integrin α4 (blocking both α4β1 and α4β7) is also effective but increases the risk of central nervous system infection with long-term use. Explain this difference with the homing mechanism and discuss the significance of "tissue selectivity" in immunotherapy.

   **Answer** Line of thought: α4β7 binds MAdCAM-1 on gut endothelium and is the specific signal for homing of effector cells to the gut, so blocking it reduces only the cells entering the gut and has little effect on immune surveillance in other tissues; α4β1 (VLA-4) binds VCAM-1 and takes part in lymphocyte entry into many tissues, including the central nervous system, and blocking it as well weakens immune surveillance in the brain and lets latent infections that were under control reactivate. The point is that the tissue specificity of homing molecules offers a way to "suppress immunity only in one organ": the higher the tissue selectivity, the smaller the systemic side effects, at the possible price of a narrower range of efficacy.
