The previous two chapters described who immune cells are and where they live; this chapter describes the molecules with which they "recognize" one another and the means by which they get where they need to go. This is the common foundation for all the questions on the knowledge path: recognition (question 1) relies on surface receptors, decision (question 2) on costimulatory and coinhibitory molecules, and effector action (question 3) presupposes that effector cells reach the site of infection, all of which is written in molecules on the cell surface. The great majority of these molecules have been assigned CD numbers, whose names reveal nothing of function, which is the main source of the "too many names" problem in immunology. This chapter therefore first explains how CD names arose and then groups the molecules by function (§4.1); it next treats two classes of molecules that determine cell position, **adhesion molecules** (§4.2) and **chemokines** (§4.3); and it ends with their clinical uses (§4.4). A marker atlas organized by cell lineage is in [7.15.35](/7.15.35); the general structure of **integrins**, **selectins**, and **cadherins** as cell adhesion molecules was covered in [7.3.5](/7.3.5) and elsewhere, and the physiology of leukocyte extravasation is in [7.14.11](/7.14.11).

## 4.1. Leukocyte Differentiation Antigens and CD Nomenclature

### 4.1.1. Clusters of Differentiation

When hematopoietic stem cells differentiate into different lineages, when each lineage differentiates to different stages, and when mature cells are activated, certain molecules appear or disappear on the cell surface; these are collectively called leukocyte differentiation antigens. After the appearance of hybridoma technology in the 1970s, laboratories used mouse monoclonal antibodies to recognize molecules on the surface of human leukocytes, and the same molecule recognized by antibodies from different laboratories was given different names. Beginning in 1982, the International Workshop on Human Leukocyte Differentiation Antigens (HLDA) periodically compared these antibodies and the molecules they recognize, grouped antibodies recognizing the same molecule into one cluster, and gave it a number.

**Definition 4.1.1 (Cluster of Differentiation)** A leukocyte surface molecule recognized by a group of monoclonal antibodies is named according to the uniform numbering of the international workshops as a **cluster of differentiation** (CD), written as CD followed by a number (for example CD4); the CD number is also used to refer to the molecule itself.

By the tenth workshop in 2014, human CD molecules had been numbered up to CD371. Several conventions apply when reading CD names:

- The number reflects only the order of confirmation and has nothing to do with function: CD4 and CD8 are coreceptors of T cells, whereas CD5 and CD6 are members of the scavenger receptor cysteine-rich family that modulate TCR signaling. This is the root cause of "names that reveal nothing of function", which is why the molecules must be regrouped by function (§4.1.3).
- Different chains of the same complex are distinguished by letters: the γ, δ, and ε chains of CD3 are CD3g, CD3d, and CD3e; CD79a and CD79b are Igα and Igβ of the BCR. Some molecules retain customary names as well: CD25 (IL-2 receptor α chain), CD62L (L-selectin), CD279 (PD-1).
- Expression is written as a superscript: CD4⁺ means expressed, CD8⁻ means not expressed, and CD56bright and the like indicate expression level (written hi/lo in flow cytometry); a combination such as "CD45RA⁺CCR7⁺" is called a phenotype ([7.15.35](/7.15.35)).
- "CD" was originally used only for leukocytes, but many CD molecules are also expressed on endothelial, epithelial, and stromal cells (such as CD31, CD34, CD54), and today it is the general numbering for surface molecules.

### 4.1.2. Biochemical Properties and Structural Families

Most CD molecules are transmembrane glycoproteins with an extracellular domain, a transmembrane domain, and a cytoplasmic domain; some are anchored to the membrane by glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI; CD14, CD16b, CD55, CD59) and can be cleaved off by phospholipases; a few are carbohydrates, such as CD15 (the Lewis X trisaccharide, a classical marker of neutrophils) and CD15s (sialylated Lewis X, a selectin ligand). According to the structure of the extracellular domain, CD molecules belong to several families:

| Structural family | Structural features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Immunoglobulin superfamily | Contains Ig-like domains (V-like or C-like) | CD2, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD28, CD80/86, CD54 (ICAM-1), CD106 (VCAM-1), CD279 (*PD-1*) |
| Integrin family | Heterodimers of noncovalently linked α and β chains | CD11a–c/CD18, CD49d/CD29, CD103 |
| C-type lectin superfamily | Calcium-dependent carbohydrate-recognition domain | CD62E/L/P (selectins), CD69, CD94, CD206 (mannose receptor), CD209 (DC-SIGN) |
| Cytokine receptor family | Conserved cysteines and the WSXWS motif | CD25, CD122, CD127, CD132 |
| Tumor necrosis factor superfamily / receptor superfamily | Ligands are trimers; receptors are cysteine-rich | CD40L (CD154), FasL (CD178); CD40, Fas (CD95), OX40 (CD134) |
| Leucine-rich repeat | Horseshoe-shaped LRR domain | CD14, Toll-like receptors (CD281–CD290) |
| Seven-transmembrane receptors | G protein-coupled receptors | Chemokine receptors CD183 (CXCR3), CD184 (CXCR4), CD195 (CCR5), CD197 (CCR7) |
| Tetraspanins | Organize other receptors in the membrane | CD9, CD37, CD81 |

### 4.1.4. How to Read a CD Name

Faced with an unfamiliar CD molecule, one can ask three questions in turn: to which structural family does it belong (which determines with whom it binds and how it transmits signals); on which cells is it found (lineage marker or state marker); and at which step of the response does it act (which row of the table in §4.1.3). For example, CD103 is the integrin αE, which forms a heterodimer with β7 and whose ligand is epithelial E-cadherin; it therefore anchors cells in the epithelium, is expressed on tissue-resident memory T cells and some dendritic cells, and is a marker of "tissue residence". In this way a number is put back onto the knowledge path.

## 4.2. Adhesion Molecules

### 4.2.1. Concept

**Definition 4.2.1 (Adhesion Molecule)** Membrane molecules that mediate adhesion between cells, or between cells and the extracellular matrix, by receptor–ligand binding are called **adhesion molecules**.

Adhesion has special significance in the immune system. Immune cells are for the most part free-swimming cells, and they must adhere rapidly and reversibly when needed: grasping the endothelium in blood vessels, forming contacts lasting hours with dendritic cells in lymph nodes, and closely apposing target cells during killing. The adhesion molecules of immune cells are therefore mostly in a "switchable" state, with their affinity or expression regulated by chemical signals.

### 4.2.2. The Four Families

| Family | Structure | Mode of binding | Representatives in immunity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Immunoglobulin superfamily | One or more Ig-like domains | Usually bind integrins or another IgSF molecule | ICAM-1 (CD54), ICAM-2, VCAM-1 (CD106), MAdCAM-1, CD2, CD58 (LFA-3), PECAM-1 (CD31) |
| Integrins | α, β heterodimers; 18 α and 8 β chains form 24 combinations | Bind IgSF molecules or extracellular matrix (fibronectin, collagen); require $\ce{Mg^2+}$ / $\ce{Mn^2+}$ | β2 group: LFA-1 (αLβ2), Mac-1 (αMβ2, i.e., CR3); β1 group: VLA-4 (α4β1); β7 group: α4β7, αEβ7 |
| Selectins | C-type lectin domain, EGF-like domain, and several complement regulatory protein repeats | Recognize sialylated, fucosylated glycans (such as sLeX); fast binding and fast dissociation | L-selectin (CD62L, leukocytes), E-selectin (CD62E, activated endothelium), P-selectin (CD62P, platelets and activated endothelium) |
| Cadherins | Five extracellular cadherin repeats, $\ce{Ca^2+}$-dependent | Homophilic binding (E to E) | E-cadherin (epithelium, the ligand of αEβ7) |

**Definition 4.2.2 (Immunoglobulin Superfamily)** Proteins whose extracellular region contains one or more domains similar to the V or C region of immunoglobulins (the Ig fold, a "sandwich" of two β sheets of about 100 amino acids stabilized by a disulfide bond) are collectively called the **immunoglobulin superfamily** (IgSF).

The IgSF is one of the largest protein families in vertebrate genomes. Antibodies, TCRs, MHC molecules, CD4, CD8, costimulatory molecules, and many adhesion molecules all belong to it; their common ancestor may have been an adhesion molecule mediating cell recognition, and the stability and variable loops of the Ig fold make it particularly suited to the evolution of diverse binding surfaces (the structure of the Ig fold is in [7.1.7](/7.1.7)).

Integrins are characterized by inside-out activation. LFA-1 on resting leukocytes is in a bent, low-affinity conformation; signals from chemokine receptors or the TCR act through the small G protein Rap1 and the cytoplasmic proteins talin and kindlin on the cytoplasmic tail of the β chain to extend the integrin and open the ligand-binding site, raising affinity about $10^{3}$–$10^{4}$-fold in less than a second; clustering of multiple molecules at the contact surface raises avidity further. This mechanism lets leukocytes adhere firmly only at the moment they see a "signal" (§4.2.3).

The table below lists the most important pairs of adhesion molecules in immunity:

| On the leukocyte | Ligand (location) | Action |
|---|---|---|
| L-selectin (CD62L) | PNAd: sulfated sLeX of GlyCAM-1, CD34, and others (HEV) | Rolling of naive lymphocytes on HEVs |
| sLeX on PSGL-1 (CD162) | P-selectin, E-selectin (activated endothelium) | Rolling of leukocytes on inflamed vessels |
| LFA-1 (CD11a/CD18) | ICAM-1, ICAM-2 (endothelium, APCs) | Firm adhesion; contact of T cells with APCs |
| Mac-1 (CD11b/CD18) | ICAM-1; complement fragment iC3b | Adhesion and phagocytosis by neutrophils and monocytes |
| VLA-4 (α4β1, CD49d/CD29) | VCAM-1 (activated endothelium) | Entry of effector T cells and monocytes into inflamed tissue |
| α4β7 | MAdCAM-1 (intestinal mucosal vessels) | Homing of lymphocytes to the gut |
| αEβ7 (CD103) | E-cadherin (epithelium) | Retention of intraepithelial lymphocytes in the epithelium |
| CD2 | CD58 (human) / CD48 (mouse) (APCs, target cells) | Adhesion of T cells to APCs, NK cells to targets |

**Remark 4.2.1** Defects of adhesion molecules present directly as immunodeficiency. Leukocyte adhesion deficiency type I is caused by mutations in CD18, the common chain of β2 integrins: neutrophils cannot adhere firmly or extravasate, so the blood neutrophil count is very high while there is no pus at sites of infection, and patients suffer recurrent severe bacterial infections and delayed umbilical cord separation. Type II is a defect of fucosylation: leukocytes lack the selectin ligand sLeX and cannot roll. These two diseases show, respectively, that both the "firm adhesion" and the "rolling" steps of the cascade are indispensable.

### 4.2.3. The Leukocyte Adhesion Cascade

**Definition 4.2.3 (Leukocyte Adhesion Cascade)** The multistep process by which leukocytes in the blood cross the endothelium into tissue, mediated in turn by different adhesion molecules and chemokines, is called the **leukocyte adhesion cascade**: (1) selectin-mediated tethering and rolling; (2) integrin activation by chemokines on the endothelial surface; (3) firm adhesion mediated by integrins and IgSF ligands; (4) crawling and transendothelial migration (extravasation).

![The leukocyte adhesion cascade. A leukocyte in the blood flow (left to right) is first briefly tethered by selectins on the endothelium and their glycan ligands and slows as it rolls along the vessel wall (1); chemokines displayed on glycosaminoglycans on the endothelial surface activate the leukocyte's G protein-coupled receptors, which converts integrins from the inside out to the high-affinity conformation (2); activated integrins (LFA-1, VLA-4) bind endothelial ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, and the leukocyte stops and spreads (3); it then crawls along the endothelium to cell junctions, squeezes out of the vessel between endothelial cells (in a few cases through them), and moves along chemokine gradients in the tissue toward the site of infection (4). Each step is carried out by a different molecular pair, and together they determine which class of leukocytes leaves the blood where](./plots/adhesion-cascade.py)

Each step of the cascade is an "AND gate": only a leukocyte that simultaneously has the appropriate selectin ligand, the appropriate chemokine receptor, and the appropriate integrin can leave the blood at a given vessel segment. The selectin–ligand bond is a "catch bond": within a certain range, the greater the shear force of the blood flow, the longer the lifetime of the bond, so leukocytes can roll stably only under flow. The combinations differ among leukocyte classes, which is the molecular basis for neutrophils arriving first and monocytes and effector T cells later at the same site of inflammation ([7.15.7](/7.15.7)).

### 4.2.4. Lymphocyte Homing

**Definition 4.2.4 (Lymphocyte Homing)** The process by which lymphocytes selectively enter and settle in particular secondary lymphoid organs or tissues by means of homing receptors on their surface and addressins (and the corresponding chemokines) on the vascular endothelium of specific tissues is called **lymphocyte homing**.

Homing is the specialization of the adhesion cascade for lymphocytes: different organs use different "address codes", and lymphocytes are "written" with the address they are to go to when they are activated.

| Destination | Addressin on endothelium | Chemokine → receptor | Homing receptor on the lymphocyte | Typical cells |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peripheral lymph node (HEV) | PNAd | CCL21, CCL19 → CCR7 | L-selectin, LFA-1 | Naive T and B cells, central memory T cells |
| Peyer's patches and intestinal lamina propria | MAdCAM-1 | CCL25 → CCR9 (small intestine); CCL28 → CCR10 (large intestine) | α4β7 | Gut-homing effector and memory cells, IgA plasma cells |
| Skin | E-selectin, ICAM-1 | CCL17 → CCR4; CCL27 → CCR10 | Cutaneous lymphocyte-associated antigen (CLA, a glycosylated form of PSGL-1) | Skin-homing T cells |
| Inflamed tissue | E/P-selectin, VCAM-1, ICAM-1 | CXCL9, CXCL10 → CXCR3 (type 1 inflammation); CCL17, CCL22 → CCR4 (type 2); CCL20 → CCR6 (type 3) | VLA-4, LFA-1 | Effector T cells |

How is the address written? Dendritic cells presenting antigen in mesenteric lymph nodes can metabolize dietary vitamin A to retinoic acid, which induces the T cells they activate to express α4β7 and CCR9; in skin-draining lymph nodes, metabolites of vitamin D3 induce CCR10. Effector cells thereby return to the kind of tissue where they first met antigen, which is one reason mucosal vaccines usually need to be administered at mucosal sites ([7.15.23](/7.15.23), [7.15.33](/7.15.33)).

## 4.3. Chemokines

### 4.3.1. Structure, Nomenclature, and Receptors

**Definition 4.3.1 (Chemokine)** **Chemokines** are secreted small cytokines of about 8–12 kDa with a conserved structure (a three-stranded antiparallel β sheet plus a C-terminal α helix, stabilized by disulfide bonds) that induce directed migration of cells expressing the corresponding receptors; their actions are mediated by G protein-coupled **chemokine receptors**.

**Definition 4.3.2 (Chemokine Receptor)** Seven-transmembrane receptors that bind chemokines, transmit signals through Gαi proteins, and cause cell polarization, integrin activation, and directed movement are called **chemokine receptors**.

According to the number of amino acids between the first two conserved N-terminal cysteines, chemokines are divided into four subfamilies; ligands are written "L" and receptors "R", and the numbers follow the order of discovery:

| Subfamily | Cysteine motif | Number of human members | Receptors | Representatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CC | Two adjacent C | About 27 | CCR1–CCR10 | CCL2 (MCP-1), CCL5 (RANTES), CCL11 (eotaxin), CCL19, CCL21 |
| CXC | One amino acid between | About 16 | CXCR1–CXCR6 | CXCL8 (IL-8), CXCL10, CXCL12 (SDF-1), CXCL13 |
| CX3C | Three amino acids between | 1 | CX3CR1 | CX3CL1 (fractalkine, membrane-bound, can be cleaved off) |
| XC | Only one (the second and fourth) cysteine | 2 | XCR1 | XCL1 (lymphotactin) |

The CXC subfamily is further divided into two groups according to whether the tripeptide Glu–Leu–Arg (ELR) precedes the first cysteine: ELR⁺ chemokines (such as CXCL8) attract neutrophils and promote angiogenesis, whereas ELR⁻ chemokines (such as CXCL9 and CXCL10) attract lymphocytes and inhibit angiogenesis. Humans have about 45 chemokines and 18 signaling receptors, as well as 4 "atypical chemokine receptors" (ACKRs) that do not transmit G protein signals; they bind and internalize chemokines and act as scavengers or transporters (for example ACKR1 on erythrocytes, the Duffy antigen). The relationship between ligands and receptors is many-to-many: one receptor often binds several ligands and one ligand often binds several receptors, but the most important pairs are quite specific (such as CXCL12–CXCR4 and CXCL13–CXCR5).

Chemokine receptors are also exploited by pathogens: besides CD4, entry of HIV-1 into cells requires the coreceptor CCR5 or CXCR4, and individuals homozygous for the 32-bp deletion (Δ32) in the *CCR5* gene are naturally resistant to virus strains that use CCR5 ([7.11.27](/7.11.27)).

### 4.3.2. Homeostatic and Inflammatory Chemokines: The "Position Code" of Cells

By mode of expression, chemokines are divided into two classes: homeostatic chemokines are constitutively expressed by stromal cells and maintain the structure of immune organs and the daily traffic of cells; inflammatory chemokines are induced by stimulated tissue cells and leukocytes during infection and recruit effector cells to the scene. The table below is a key to understanding "why the cells are here" in a tissue section:

| Chemokine → receptor | Source | Cells attracted | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| CCL19, CCL21 → CCR7 | Stromal cells of the lymph node T-cell zone, HEVs, lymphatic vessels | Naive T and B cells, mature dendritic cells | Entry into the lymph node T-cell zone |
| CXCL13 → CXCR5 | Follicular stromal cells, follicular dendritic cells | B cells, Tfh cells | Entry into the follicle |
| CXCL12 → CXCR4 | Bone marrow stromal cells and others | Hematopoietic stem cells, plasma cells, B-cell precursors | Retention in bone marrow; germinal center dark zone |
| CCL25 → CCR9 | Small intestinal epithelium, thymus | Gut-homing T cells, thymocytes | Positioning in gut and thymus |
| CXCL8 → CXCR1, CXCR2 | Macrophages, epithelium, endothelium | Neutrophils | Neutrophil recruitment in acute inflammation (type 3) |
| CCL2 → CCR2 | Inflamed tissue | Classical monocytes | Exit of monocytes from bone marrow and entry into tissue |
| CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11 → CXCR3 | IFN-γ-stimulated cells | Th1, CTLs, NK cells | Type 1 inflammation |
| CCL11 (eotaxin) → CCR3 | Epithelium, fibroblasts (induced by IL-4, IL-13) | Eosinophils, Th2 | Type 2 inflammation |
| CCL17, CCL22 → CCR4 | Dendritic cells, skin | Th2, Treg, skin-homing T cells | |
| CCL20 → CCR6 | Epithelium (above Peyer's patches, inflamed skin) | Th17, immature dendritic cells, B cells | Type 3 inflammation; mucosal inductive sites |
| CX3CL1 → CX3CR1 | Endothelium, neurons | Nonclassical monocytes, NK cells, microglia | Patrolling and adhesion |

The migration of dendritic cells is an example driven by receptor switching: immature dendritic cells express inflammatory receptors such as CCR6 and CCR1 and stay in peripheral tissue; after sensing a pathogen they mature, downregulate these receptors and upregulate CCR7, and so enter lymphatic vessels along CCL21 secreted by the lymphatic endothelium and finally reach the T-cell zone of the lymph node ([7.15.14](/7.15.14)).

### 4.3.3. Formation and Reading of Gradients

For a chemokine to guide cells it must form a concentration gradient. The simplest model is that the chemokine is produced at a constant rate at a point source (for example the boundary of inflamed tissue beside a segment of blood vessel), diffuses in the tissue with diffusion coefficient $D$, and is cleared with rate constant $k$ (internalized by receptors, removed by atypical receptors, or degraded by proteases). The one-dimensional reaction–diffusion equation ([2.5](/2.5); written as a network dynamics model in [7.9.14](/7.9.14)) is

$$
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2} - kc .
$$

**Example 4.3.1** At steady state $D c'' = kc$, and with the boundary conditions $c(0) = c_0$ at the source and $c \to 0$ far away, the solution is

$$
c(x) = c_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-x/\ell}, \qquad \ell = \sqrt{D/k} .
$$

$\ell$ is the characteristic length of the gradient: fast diffusion makes the gradient extend farther, and fast clearance makes it steep and short, which is the same logic as for **morphogen** gradients in development. The diffusion coefficient of a chemokine of about 8 kDa in tissue interstitium is about $D \approx 50$–$100\,\mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$; if the clearance time scale is several to a dozen minutes ($k \approx 10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$), then $\ell \approx 70$–$300\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, that is, several to a few dozen cell diameters, comparable to the distance from a blood vessel to a focus of infection.

How do cells "read" the gradient? For a cell of length $L$, the relative difference in concentration between its two ends is $\Delta c / c = L/\ell$, which for $L = 10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ is only about 3%–15%. What a cell senses is not the concentration itself but the receptor occupancy $\theta = c/(c + K_{\mathrm{d}})$; the difference in occupancy between the two ends,

$$
\Delta\theta \approx \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}c}\,\Delta c = \theta(1 - \theta)\,\frac{L}{\ell}
$$

is greatest at $c = K_{\mathrm{d}}$ ($\theta = 1/2$), where it is $L/(4\ell)$; far from the source ($c \ll K_{\mathrm{d}}$) the receptors are almost empty, and near the source ($c \gg K_{\mathrm{d}}$) the receptors are saturated, and in both cases the cell cannot tell direction. An exponential gradient is therefore readable only over a distance in which $c$ and $K_{\mathrm{d}}$ differ by less than about tenfold, with a width of about $2\ell\ln 10 \approx 4.6\ell$.

This simple calculation explains several arrangements of the immune system. First, chemokines in tissue are mostly bound to glycosaminoglycans of the extracellular matrix and the endothelial surface, forming fixed "surface gradients" that both reduce washout by blood flow or interstitial fluid and allow cells to crawl along the surface and read them (haptotaxis). Second, cells often respond in sequence to several chemokines of different affinity or origin, completing long-distance navigation in segments like a relay; neutrophils first respond to CXCL8 near the vessel and, on approaching the focus of infection, switch to responding preferentially to bacterial formyl peptides and the complement fragment C5a. Third, cells continually "reset" their sensitivity through receptor desensitization and internalization, so they can keep their sense of direction over a very wide range of concentrations, a general principle of adaptation in signal transduction ([7.9.16](/7.9.16)).

## 4.4. Clinical Applications

CD molecules and adhesion molecules are ready-made targets for diagnosis and therapy.

| Application | Molecule | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| Monitoring immune status | CD4 | When the absolute peripheral blood CD4⁺ T-cell count of an HIV-infected person (normally about $500$–$1500\,\mathrm{\mu L^{-1}}$) falls below $200\,\mathrm{\mu L^{-1}}$, the risk of opportunistic infection rises steeply, which is one of the defining criteria of AIDS |
| Classification of leukemias and lymphomas | CD19, CD20, CD3, CD34, CD117, CD33, CD13, and others | The lineage and stage of differentiation of tumor cells are judged by CD phenotype |
| Depletion of B cells | CD20 (rituximab); CD19 (CAR-T, CD19×CD3 bispecific antibodies) | B-cell lymphoma and leukemia, autoantibody-mediated diseases |
| Suppression of T cells | CD3 (anti-CD3 antibody), CD25 (basiliximab), CD52 (alemtuzumab) | Prevention and treatment of transplant rejection; anti-CD3 antibody can delay the onset of type 1 diabetes |
| Plasma cell tumors | CD38 (daratumumab) | Multiple myeloma |
| Blockade of costimulation or coinhibition | CTLA-4–Ig (abatacept, blocks CD28–B7); anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1 | Treatment of rheumatoid arthritis; tumor immunotherapy ([7.15.25](/7.15.25)) |
| Blockade of homing | α4 integrin (natalizumab); α4β7 (vedolizumab) | Natalizumab prevents lymphocytes from entering the central nervous system and treats **multiple sclerosis**; vedolizumab blocks only gut homing and treats **inflammatory bowel disease** |
| Blockade of chemokine receptors | CCR5 (maraviroc); CXCR4 (plerixafor) | Prevent HIV entry; mobilize hematopoietic stem cells out of the bone marrow for collection |

The contrast between natalizumab and vedolizumab illustrates the practical meaning of homing "address codes": the former blocks α4 (affecting both α4β1 and α4β7), inhibiting lymphocyte entry into the brain and also weakening immune surveillance of viruses in the central nervous system, so that in a few patients reactivation of latent JC virus has caused progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy; the latter blocks only the gut-specific α4β7–MAdCAM-1 and carries no such risk. All these drugs are designed on the molecular logic of this chapter; the structure and engineering of antibody drugs themselves are in [7.15.9](/7.15.9).

## Summary

- CD numbers come from the clustering of monoclonal antibodies (Definition 4.1.1) and reflect only the order of confirmation, not function; CD molecules are grouped by function into antigen receptors and coreceptors, pattern recognition receptors, cytokine receptors, complement receptors, Fc receptors, NK receptors, costimulation and coinhibition, adhesion and homing, death receptors, enzymes, and lineage and state markers (§4.1.3). To read a CD name: structural family → expressing cells → which step of the response.
- **Adhesion molecules** (Definition 4.2.1) fall into four families:

  | Family | Binding | Representatives | In the cascade |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | **Selectins** | Glycans (sLeX), fast binding and fast dissociation | L-, E-, P-selectin | Rolling |
  | **Integrins** | IgSF ligands, matrix; inside-out activation | LFA-1, Mac-1, VLA-4, α4β7, αEβ7 | Firm adhesion |
  | **Immunoglobulin superfamily** | Integrins or IgSF | ICAM-1, VCAM-1, MAdCAM-1, CD2 | Endothelial side of adhesion; cell contact |
  | **Cadherins** | Homophilic binding | E-cadherin | Residence in epithelium |

- The **leukocyte adhesion cascade** (Definition 4.2.3): rolling → integrin activation by chemokines → firm adhesion → extravasation; each step is an "AND gate". **Lymphocyte homing** (Definition 4.2.4) encodes the destination by the combination of "homing receptor + addressin + chemokine": lymph node (L-selectin/PNAd, CCR7), gut (α4β7/MAdCAM-1, CCR9), skin (CLA/E-selectin, CCR4, CCR10).
- **Chemokines** (Definition 4.3.1) are divided by cysteine motif into CC, CXC, CX3C, and XC and act through G protein-coupled **chemokine receptors** (Definition 4.3.2); homeostatic chemokines maintain organ structure (CCL19/21–CCR7, CXCL13–CXCR5, CXCL12–CXCR4), and inflammatory chemokines correspond to the effector modules (CXCR3: type 1; CCR3, CCR4: type 2; CXCR1/2, CCR6: type 3).
- The reaction–diffusion model gives the gradient length $\ell = \sqrt{D/k}$ (tens to hundreds of micrometers); the difference in receptor occupancy between the two ends of a cell, $\theta(1-\theta)L/\ell$, is greatest at $c \approx K_{\mathrm{d}}$, and the gradient is readable only over a range of about $4.6\ell$ (Example 4.3.1).

## Questions

1. A monoclonal antibody is assigned by the international workshop to CD62L. From the name alone, what properties of this molecule can you infer? What else must you look up?

   **Answer** The number "62" carries no information by itself, but the suffix "L" and this number correspond by convention to L-selectin (CD62E and CD62P are E- and P-selectin), from which one can infer that it belongs to the C-type lectin family, recognizes sLeX-type glycans, and mediates leukocyte rolling on endothelium. One still needs to look up its expressing cells (naive and central memory lymphocytes, neutrophils) and the fact that after activation it is cleaved from the membrane by metalloproteases (which is why effector T cells are mostly CD62L⁻). This shows that CD numbers must be remembered together with family, expression, and function.

2. The blood neutrophil count of patients with leukocyte adhesion deficiency type I is often several times higher than normal, yet there is no pus at sites of infection. Explain both phenomena using the adhesion cascade.

   **Answer** Loss of CD18 eliminates all β2 integrins (LFA-1, Mac-1); neutrophils can roll but cannot adhere firmly or extravasate, so they remain in the blood (the marginal pool is also reduced) and the blood count rises; at the same time they cannot reach infected tissue, no neutrophils accumulate in the tissue, and pus therefore does not form. Mac-1 is also the complement receptor CR3, and its loss further impairs phagocytosis of bacteria coated with iC3b.

3. Use the model of Example 4.3.1: if a drug increases the clearance rate of a chemokine in tissue 4-fold, how do the gradient length and the peak of the occupancy difference between the two ends of the cell change?

   **Answer** $\ell = \sqrt{D/k}$, so when $k$ increases 4-fold $\ell$ is halved; the peak $\Delta\theta_{\max} = L/(4\ell)$ doubles, that is, the gradient becomes steeper and the directional signal stronger, but the readable range (about $4.6\ell$) is halved, and cells farther from the source cannot sense the gradient. Clearance therefore does not simply "weaken" the signal but changes the trade-off between "carrying far" and "reading clearly".

4. Why is vedolizumab considered "safer" than natalizumab? What does this suggest for the design of drugs targeting other tissues?

   **Answer** Natalizumab blocks the α4 chain and so inhibits both α4β1–VCAM-1 (entry into the brain and many other tissues) and α4β7–MAdCAM-1 (entry into the gut), weakening immune surveillance of the central nervous system and allowing reactivation of JC virus; vedolizumab recognizes only the α4β7 heterodimer, and MAdCAM-1 is expressed mainly on intestinal mucosal vessels, so it blocks only gut homing. The lesson is that by exploiting tissue-specific "address codes" (particular integrin heterodimers, particular addressins or chemokine receptors), one can block the entry of lymphocytes into a single organ while preserving surveillance throughout the body.

5. (Open question) In spatial proteomics of a tumor section you see many CXCR3⁺ CD8⁺ T cells aggregated in a region of high CXCL10 expression, while CCR7⁺ T cells are concentrated in a tertiary lymphoid structure. According to the "position codes" of this chapter, in what states might these two groups of cells be?

   **Answer** CXCR3 is the chemokine receptor of type 1 inflammation, and its ligands CXCL9 and CXCL10 are induced by IFN-γ; the aggregation of CXCR3⁺ CD8⁺ T cells in the CXCL10 region suggests that these are recruited effector or effector memory T cells and that an IFN-γ-driven response is under way locally (often associated with better response to immunotherapy). CCR7 is the receptor of the homeostatic chemokines CCL19/21, and the concentration of CCR7⁺ T cells in a tertiary lymphoid structure suggests that they are naive or central memory T cells retained by CCL19/21 from stromal cells in the structure and may be activated on site there. The assessment should be combined with state markers such as CD45RA/RO, PD-1, and Ki-67 ([7.15.35](/7.15.35)).
