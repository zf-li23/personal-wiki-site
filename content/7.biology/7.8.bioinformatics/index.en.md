Bioinformatics answers biological questions from the data of molecular biology, above all the sequences of DNA, RNA and protein, by means of algorithms, statistical models and software. Its objects in this course are linear and semi-linear: a sequence is a string, and almost everything else treated here is built directly on strings, an alignment of two or many of them, a tree relating them, a graph assembled from their overlaps, a list of positions in which one genome differs from another, the nested base pairs of an RNA, the fold of a protein chain. One distinction organizes the whole subject. A *model* states how the data are thought to arise, substitutions along a tree, errors in a sequencing read, states of a hidden chain that switch between exon and intron, and so defines what the best answer is; an algorithm, very often **dynamic programming** or an index of a genome, finds that answer in reasonable time. Most methods of the field are such a pair, and the recurring questions are whether the model is right, whether the algorithm is fast enough for the data, and how significant the result is when it was found by searching an enormous space.

The chapters follow the data from one sequence to a population and then to three dimensions. After the formats, coordinates, computing environment and databases that every analysis begins and ends with, the comparison of sequences is built up in four steps: **sequence alignment** of two sequences by dynamic programming under a **substitution matrix**, database search with **BLAST** and the statistics of its **E-value**, **multiple sequence alignment** with profiles and **profile hidden Markov models**, and the inference of a **phylogenetic tree** from an alignment. Statistical models of sequence come next: Markov chains and **hidden Markov models** for **gene prediction**, and **sequence motifs** for the short sites that proteins read. The longest part goes from sequencing **reads** to genomes and their variation, following the dry-lab path of a resequencing study: quality control, **read mapping** with the **FM-index**, **genome assembly** with overlap and **de Bruijn graphs**, variant calling and the interpretation of variants for a patient, the computation behind population genomic data, and the analysis of mixtures of genomes in **metagenomics**. The last three chapters turn to learning and structure: deep networks and language models that read sequences, the dynamic programs of RNA folding and covariance models, and the comparison, prediction and use of protein structures. A small set of examples runs through the chapters, the lactase-persistence variant rs4988235 in *MCM6*, the benchmark genome HG002, the genome of SARS-CoV-2 and the rare disease NGLY1 deficiency, and the course summary sets the methods side by side and shows that a handful of structures, dynamic programming, log-odds scores, hidden Markov models and grammars, latent variables, indexes and seeds, and significance in large searches, carry most of the field.

Bioinformatics stands between several subjects whose boundaries are not sharp. Statistics is its foundation: the probability of [3.1](/3.1) and [3.2](/3.2), and the testing, count models and multiple comparisons of [biostatistics](/7.7). The algorithms and data structures are those of [4.3](/4.3), the learning methods those of [4.6](/4.6) and [4.7](/4.7), and the graph theory of assembly that of [1.4](/1.4). On the biological side the course takes its genome biology from [molecular biology](/7.2), its variants, association studies and population genetics from [genetics](/7.4), and the physics of folding, binding and structure prediction from [biophysics](/7.6), and does not derive them again. The course stops where the structures stop being linear: the omics assays, single-cell and spatial data, interactomes and regulatory networks, whose reads are mapped and counted with the tools of this course, belong to [systems biology](/7.9), and the general mathematics of networks to [network science](/1.4.7). Trees and molecular clocks continue in [evolutionary biology](/7.10), and the metagenomic methods in [microbiology](/7.11) and [ecology](/7.19).

During olympiad training I learned bioinformatics unsystematically; here it is laid out as one system. Three courses go into it, and each supplies something the others do not. The bioinformatics course itself sets out the standard range of the field. The course on intelligent biocomputing, offered by the department of electronic engineering, explains how the algorithms underneath the basic tools were developed: why BLAST seeds and extends, where the extreme-value statistics of its scores come from, how a profile hidden Markov model or an RNA folding algorithm is built, how AlphaFold is put together. The dry-lab half of the fundamental laboratory course, which follows one sample from raw reads through quality control, mapping and variant calling to population comparison and a tree, gives the clearest guidance I know on how the tools are actually used, and much of the order of the middle chapters follows it. Of the textbooks, Pevsner's remains the classic and is still the best place to check the principles of alignment, search and phylogeny; Mount and Zvelebil are worth consulting beside it, above all for derivations. In Chinese, the new textbook edited by Ming Chen for the national 101 Plan is, I think, the best available.

Bioinformatics textbooks age faster than those of almost any other part of biology. A book from ten years ago describes sequencing platforms that no longer exist, an older build of the human genome, and aligners and databases that have since been replaced, while the field has since gained accurate long reads, the complete telomere-to-telomere genome and the pangenome, and learned models that predict protein structure. These pages are therefore written mainly from current knowledge, with the textbooks as the check on the principles, which age much more slowly than the tools. I also do not see a clear line between biostatistics, bioinformatics and systems biology. I prefer to regard statistics as the foundation for learning bioinformatics, and to keep bioinformatics to the linear and semi-linear structures, the part a dry-lab course covers from reads to variants and trees; the newer omics assays and the networks built from them I leave to systems biology, because what they produce is knowledge from which networks are constructed.
