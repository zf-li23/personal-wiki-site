The previous chapter ([7.13.4](/7.13.4)) described which elements plants need and how soil supplies them to the root surface. This chapter describes how ions enter root cells and cross the root to the xylem, and how they are transported and redistributed within the plant. The central problem is energy. The concentrations of many nutrients in the soil solution are in the micromolar range, whereas in the cytosol they are in the millimolar range, so roots must accumulate them against a concentration gradient; at the same time $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ must be excluded from the cytosol. Deciding whether the transport of an ion runs "downhill" or "uphill" requires considering both the concentration difference and the membrane potential, which is the electrochemical potential. Plant cells use proton pumps in the plasma membrane to establish an electrochemical gradient of protons, and then use it to drive the active transport of almost all other solutes; in this they differ from animal cells, which are organized around the sodium–potassium pump. The general principles of membrane transport and the structure of transport proteins are covered in biochemistry [7.1.13](/7.1.13), cell biology [7.3.5](/7.3.5) and biophysics [7.6.30](/7.6.30); here we treat only what is specific to plant cells, together with quantitative tests. For root anatomy see [7.12.20](/7.12.20), and for the movement of water along the same pathways see [7.13.3](/7.13.3).

## 5.1. Passive and Active Transport

### 5.1.1. Electrochemical Potential

Diffusion of a substance always proceeds spontaneously in the direction of decreasing free energy (chemical potential) until equilibrium is reached (**Fick's law**, [2.5.1](/2.5.1)). For a solute $j$ in solution, the various forces driving its movement can be combined into a single quantity, the **electrochemical potential** ([7.6.3](/7.6.3#sec-3.1.5)):

$$
\tilde\mu_j = \mu_j^\circ + RT\ln c_j + z_jF\varphi + \bar V_jp, \tag{5.1}
$$

where $c_j$ is the concentration (strictly, the activity), $z_j$ is the charge number of the ion, $F = 96\,485\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}$ is the Faraday constant, $\varphi$ is the electric potential of the solution, $\bar V_j$ is the partial molar volume and $p$ is the hydrostatic pressure. The last term can be neglected except for the osmotic movement of water; for an uncharged solute (such as sucrose) only the concentration term remains.

The free energy change when the solute moves from outside (o) to inside (i) the membrane is

$$
\Delta\tilde\mu_j = \tilde\mu_j^{\mathrm{i}} - \tilde\mu_j^{\mathrm{o}} = RT\ln\frac{c_j^{\mathrm{i}}}{c_j^{\mathrm{o}}} + z_jFV_{\mathrm{m}},
$$

where $V_{\mathrm{m}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}} - \varphi_{\mathrm{o}}$ is the membrane potential (inside relative to outside; to avoid confusion with the water potential $\Psi$, this chapter does not use $\Delta\Psi$ for the membrane potential). When $\Delta\tilde\mu_j < 0$ the solute can move inward spontaneously, which is **passive transport**; movement against the electrochemical potential gradient ($\Delta\tilde\mu_j > 0$) is called **active transport**; it cannot proceed spontaneously and must be coupled to a process with a more negative free energy change (usually ATP hydrolysis). An important corollary of the equation above is that a charged ion can move passively against its concentration gradient when driven by the electric field: concentration alone is not enough to decide whether ion transport is active or passive.

### 5.1.2. Diffusion Potentials and the Membrane Potential

When two $\ce{KCl}$ solutions of different concentration are separated by a membrane, $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ each diffuse down their own gradient. If the membrane is more permeable to $\ce{K+}$ than to $\ce{Cl-}$ (as biological membranes usually are), $\ce{K+}$ runs ahead, causing a slight charge separation that immediately generates a transmembrane potential; this potential slows $\ce{K+}$ and speeds $\ce{Cl-}$ so that the two diffuse at the same rate. A potential produced by diffusion in this way is called a diffusion potential. The amount of charge separation is chemically negligible: the $-100\,\mathrm{mV}$ potential common across plant plasma membranes requires only one extra cation for every hundred thousand anions in the cell, and the excess charges lie right at the membrane surface, so the bulk solution remains electroneutral.

When several ions are permeant at once, the diffusion potential is determined jointly by their concentration gradients and permeabilities and can be calculated with the **Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation** ([7.3.5](/7.3.5#sec-5.6.2)). In most eukaryotic cells the intracellular concentration and the permeability of $\ce{K+}$ are both highest, so the diffusion potential is close to the equilibrium potential of $\ce{K+}$; the resting potential of animal neurons is also close to this. But the membrane potentials measured in plant and fungal cells (often $-100$ to $-200\,\mathrm{mV}$) are much more negative than the diffusion potential calculated from the Goldman equation (usually only $-50$ to $-80\,\mathrm{mV}$). The excess comes from the electrogenic $\ce{H+}$ pump in the plasma membrane, which in each cycle moves one $\ce{H+}$ out of the cell with no countermovement of charge, and so pushes the membrane potential away from the Goldman value. The evidence is that when pea cells are treated with cyanide ($\ce{CN-}$, which poisons mitochondria and depletes ATP), the membrane potential falls within minutes from about $-140\,\mathrm{mV}$ to the level of the diffusion potential, while the pH of the external solution rises and the cytosol acidifies; when the cyanide is washed out ATP synthesis gradually recovers and the membrane potential recovers with it. The membrane potential of a plant cell therefore has two components: the diffusion potential and the potential contributed by electrogenic ion pumps.

### 5.1.3. Using the Nernst Equation to Identify Active Transport

If a membrane is permeable to only one ion, the ion diffuses across until the concentration gradient is exactly balanced by the potential difference; setting $\Delta\tilde\mu_j = 0$ gives the equilibrium potential of that ion, the **Nernst equation**:

$$
E_j = \frac{RT}{z_jF}\ln\frac{c_j^{\mathrm{o}}}{c_j^{\mathrm{i}}} = \frac{59.2\,\mathrm{mV}}{z_j}\lg\frac{c_j^{\mathrm{o}}}{c_j^{\mathrm{i}}} \quad (25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}). \tag{5.2}
$$

A tenfold concentration difference for a monovalent ion corresponds to about $59\,\mathrm{mV}$. Given the membrane potential, Eq. (5.2) can be inverted to predict the internal concentration at electrochemical equilibrium, $c_j^{\mathrm{i}} = c_j^{\mathrm{o}}\,10^{-z_jV_{\mathrm{m}}/59.2\,\mathrm{mV}}$. If the measured internal concentration is higher than predicted, the ion is actively taken up; if it is lower, the ion enters passively and is then actively pumped out. Note the difference between equilibrium and steady state: a steady state is one in which influx equals efflux and the concentration does not change with time; because of active transport, many ions are in a steady state far from equilibrium.

**Example 5.1.1** Higinbotham et al. (1967) bathed pea root segments in dilute nutrient solution until they reached a steady state and measured a membrane potential $V_{\mathrm{m}} = -110\,\mathrm{mV}$. Taking $\ce{K+}$ as an example, with $1\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ outside, the predicted equilibrium internal concentration is $1 \times 10^{110/59.2} = 72\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and the measured value is $75\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, so $\ce{K+}$ is close to equilibrium and can accumulate passively. For the cations $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ the driving force points inward while the measured concentrations are far below the equilibrium values, which shows that they are continually pumped out. This example is simplified. A plant cell has several compartments; the large central vacuole of a mature cell occupies more than 90% of the cell volume, and the cytosol is only a thin layer against the plasma membrane, so the concentrations measured in hot-water extracts of root segments mainly reflect the vacuole. Measurements of cytosol and vacuole separately in green algae with very large cells, such as *Chara* and *Nitella*, led to the following conclusions. $\ce{K+}$ is accumulated passively by cytosol and vacuole, and actively taken up when the external $\ce{K+}$ is very low. $\ce{Na+}$ is actively pumped from the cytosol out of the cell and into the vacuole. The excess $\ce{H+}$ produced by metabolism is actively expelled from the cytosol, keeping cytosolic pH near neutral, while the vacuole and the outside of the cell are 1–2 pH units more acidic than the cytosol. Anions are actively transported into the cytosol. $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is actively transported out of the cytosol at both the plasma membrane and the vacuolar membrane (tonoplast).

### 5.1.4. The Proton-Motive Force

The proton electrochemical gradient established by the plasma membrane $\ce{H+}$ pump stores free energy. The free energy change for protons flowing back from outside to inside (Eq. (5.1) with $z = +1$) is

$$
\Delta\tilde\mu_{\ce{H+}} = FV_{\mathrm{m}} - 2.303\,RT\,(\mathrm{pH}_{\mathrm{i}} - \mathrm{pH}_{\mathrm{o}}), \tag{5.3}
$$

and dividing by $F$ gives the **proton-motive force** expressed as a potential, $\Delta p = V_{\mathrm{m}} - 59.2\,\mathrm{mV}\cdot\Delta\mathrm{pH}$ ($25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$; the same as [Eq. (3.17) of 7.6.3](/7.6.3#eq-3.17)).

**Example 5.1.2** For a root epidermal cell with $V_{\mathrm{m}} = -150\,\mathrm{mV}$, cytosolic pH 7.2 and apoplastic pH 5.5, $\Delta\mathrm{pH} = 1.7$. From Eq. (5.3), $\Delta p = -150 - 59.2 \times 1.7 = -251\,\mathrm{mV}$ and $\Delta\tilde\mu_{\ce{H+}} = F\Delta p = -24.2\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$: each mole of protons flowing back into the cytosol can release about 24 kJ of free energy, 60% of it from the potential and 40% from the pH gradient. The free energy of ATP hydrolysis in the cytosol is about $-50\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, enough for the plasma membrane $\ce{H+}$-ATPase to pump one proton per ATP hydrolyzed and establish such a gradient; conversely, the mitochondrial ATP synthase needs more than three protons to flow back per ATP synthesized, so a gradient of the same size drives ATP synthesis there.

## 5.2. Membrane Transport Proteins

Pure phospholipid bilayers have permeabilities to small nonpolar or uncharged molecules such as $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{CO2}$ and glycerol similar to those of biological membranes, but their permeabilities to ions, sugars and water are one to several orders of magnitude lower than those of biological membranes: biological membranes contain specific **transporter** proteins. Of the roughly 34 000 protein-coding genes of *Arabidopsis*, as many as 1800 may encode transporters. By mechanism, transporters fall into three classes: channels, carriers and pumps.

| Type | Mechanism | Rate | Direction of transport | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ion channel** | Transmembrane pore; ions pass down their electrochemical gradient when it is open | about $10^8$ ions $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ | Passive only | Inward and outward $\ce{K+}$ channels, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels, anion channels, aquaporins |
| Carrier | Conformational change after substrate binding alternately exposes the substrate to the two sides of the membrane | $10^2$–$10^3$ $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ | Passive (uniport) or secondary active (symport, antiport) | $\ce{H+}$–$\ce{NO3-}$ symporter, $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ antiporter |
| Pump | Uses the energy of ATP, pyrophosphate and the like directly to transport against the gradient | Slower | Primary active | Plasma membrane $\ce{H+}$-ATPase, V-ATPase, $\ce{H+}$-pyrophosphatase, $\ce{Ca^2+}$-ATPase, ABC transporters |

### 5.2.1. Channels

The selectivity of a channel depends mainly on the size of the pore and the distribution of charge on its wall, not on specific binding. When a channel is open, ions pass at very high speed, but channels are not always open: gating structures open and close in response to changes in membrane potential, ligands, hormones, light, phosphorylation or mechanical force. Patch clamping can record the current through a single channel. Higinbotham's data show that most ions are far from equilibrium, so the channels of most ions are usually closed.

- $\ce{K+}$ channels are the best understood. Those that open only when the membrane potential is more negative than $E_{\ce{K}}$ and mediate $\ce{K+}$ influx are called inward-rectifying $\ce{K+}$ channels, such as AKT1, expressed in root epidermis, cortex and endodermis and responsible for $\ce{K+}$ uptake from the soil, and KAT1 in guard cells. Those that open only when the membrane potential is more positive than $E_{\ce{K}}$ are called outward-rectifying $\ce{K+}$ channels, such as SKOR in the stele, which releases $\ce{K+}$ into the xylem, and GORK in guard cells. *Arabidopsis* has nine Shaker-type $\ce{K+}$ channels, homologous in structure to the voltage-gated $\ce{K+}$ channels of animals (four subunits, each with six transmembrane helices, the fourth helix carrying positive charges that sense voltage). As long as the membrane potential is sufficiently negative, Shaker channels in roots can take up $\ce{K+}$ passively even when external $\ce{K+}$ is only micromolar.
- $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels are tightly regulated and open essentially only during signal transduction, letting $\ce{Ca^2+}$ flow into the cytosol; cyclic nucleotide-gated channels and glutamate receptor-like channels are less selective cation channels that can pass $\ce{Ca^2+}$.
- Anion channels: the electrochemical gradient of anions always points out of the cell, so when anion channels open anions flow out (for example, anion efflux from guard cells when stomata close; see [7.13.10](/7.13.10)).
- Vacuolar channels: the slow vacuolar channel (SV, that is TPC1, permeable to monovalent and divalent cations and activated by $\ce{Ca^2+}$), the fast vacuolar channel (FV, passing only monovalent cations), the $\ce{Ca^2+}$-activated, highly selective $\ce{K+}$ channel TPK (VK), and anion channels that admit $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{NO3-}$ and malate to the vacuole.
- **Aquaporins**: there are about 35 in *Arabidopsis*; besides water, some transport boric acid, silicic acid, $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O2}$. Because of structural similarity, the arsenite produced by reduction of arsenate also enters rice roots through silicon channels, causing the problem of arsenic accumulation in rice grain.

### 5.2.2. Carriers and Cotransport

A carrier has no pore through the membrane: the substrate first binds to a specific site on the carrier, and a conformational change exposes it to the other side of the membrane and releases it. Because every molecule transported requires a conformational change, carriers are several orders of magnitude slower than channels. When a carrier mediates transport down the electrochemical gradient it is called a **uniporter** (historically this was also called facilitated diffusion); when it is coupled to the downhill movement of another solute and transports its substrate uphill, this is secondary active transport.

In plant cells, transport powered directly by pumps using the energy of ATP is called primary active transport, and the transport of other solutes driven by the transmembrane proton electrochemical gradient established by primary active transport is called secondary active transport. Secondary active transport is cotransport: two solutes are carried across the membrane together, and neither can move without the other. When both move in the same direction the carrier is a **symporter**, and when they move in opposite directions it is an **antiporter**. Because the proton gradient points into the cell, $\ce{H+}$-coupled symporters generally carry substrates into the cytosol and $\ce{H+}$-coupled antiporters generally carry substrates out of it. In plants, $\ce{NO3-}$, $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{H2PO4-}$, $\ce{SO4^2-}$, sucrose, amino acids and peptides all enter the cell through specific $\ce{H+}$ symporters; $\ce{Na+}$ is extruded by $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ antiporters; and $\ce{K+}$ enters passively through channels when the external concentration is high, and is taken up by high-affinity carriers of the HAK family (probably $\ce{H+}$–$\ce{K+}$ symport) when the external concentration is very low. Even passive influx through channels is ultimately driven by the $\ce{H+}$-ATPase, which keeps the membrane potential more negative than $E_{\ce{K}}$.

How high a concentration can cotransport accumulate a substrate to? Setting the free energy change of the whole transport cycle to zero, for a substrate of charge $z$ transported inward together with $n$ $\ce{H+}$,

$$
\lg\frac{c^{\mathrm{i}}}{c^{\mathrm{o}}} = -\frac{(z + n)V_{\mathrm{m}}}{59.2\,\mathrm{mV}} + n\,(\mathrm{pH}_{\mathrm{i}} - \mathrm{pH}_{\mathrm{o}}). \tag{5.4}
$$

**Example 5.2.1** Take $V_{\mathrm{m}} = -150\,\mathrm{mV}$ and $\Delta\mathrm{pH} = 1.7$. (a) If $\ce{NO3-}$ is transported through a channel ($z = -1$, $n = 0$), $\lg(c^{\mathrm{i}}/c^{\mathrm{o}}) = -150/59.2 = -2.5$: the internal concentration can be at most $1/340$ of the external one, so passive uptake is impossible. (b) Electroneutral symport of $1\ce{H+} : 1\ce{NO3-}$ ($z + n = 0$) uses only the pH gradient: $\lg(c^{\mathrm{i}}/c^{\mathrm{o}}) = 1.7$, an accumulation of at most 50-fold. (c) Electrogenic symport of $2\ce{H+} : 1\ce{NO3-}$ (one net positive charge moved inward per cycle): $\lg(c^{\mathrm{i}}/c^{\mathrm{o}}) = 150/59.2 + 2 \times 1.7 = 5.9$, an accumulation of about $10^6$-fold, enough to take up nitrate from a micromolar soil solution. Experiments show that the $\ce{H+}/\ce{NO3-}$ ratio of the nitrate transporter NRT1.1 is greater than 1, consistent with this.

![Equilibrium accumulation ratio $c_{\mathrm{in}}/c_{\mathrm{out}}$ attainable by cotransport as a function of membrane potential (Eq. (5.4); apoplast pH 5.5, cytosol pH 7.2, $25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$). The curves are, in order, a $\ce{K+}$ channel ($z = +1$, $n = 0$), a $\ce{NO3-}$ channel ($z = -1$, $n = 0$, which can only make anions flow out), $\ce{H+}$–sucrose symport (uncharged substrate, 1 $\ce{H+}$), electroneutral $1\ce{H+} : 1\ce{NO3-}$ symport (independent of membrane potential) and electrogenic $2\ce{H+} : 1\ce{NO3-}$ symport. ### 5.2.3. Ion Pumps

**Definition 5.2.1 (Plasma Membrane H⁺-ATPase)** The **plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase** is a P-type ATPase in the plasma membrane of plants and fungi that pumps one $\ce{H+}$ from the cytosol to the outside per ATP hydrolyzed, with phosphorylation of an aspartate residue during the catalytic cycle; it establishes the proton electrochemical gradient across the plasma membrane, drives the secondary active transport of the great majority of solutes, and regulates cytosolic pH and turgor.

The plasma membrane $\ce{H+}$-ATPase is a single polypeptide of about 100 kDa with ten transmembrane segments and a catalytic domain on the cytosolic side, with $\ce{Mg^2+}$-ATP as substrate; vanadate is a specific inhibitor (a property shared by **P-type ATPases**, [7.1.13](/7.1.13#sec-13.5.2)). *Arabidopsis* has about a dozen encoding genes (the AHA family), some highly expressed in guard cells (driving solute uptake during stomatal opening) and some in the root endodermis and in transfer cells around seeds; loss of all function is lethal. Its C terminus is an autoinhibitory domain: when a threonine near the C terminus is phosphorylated by a protein kinase, it binds a 14-3-3 protein, which relieves the autoinhibition and activates the pump. The fungal toxin fusicoccin stabilizes the binding of 14-3-3 and strongly activates the $\ce{H+}$ pump, which can irreversibly open stomata and cause wilting or even death. Light (blue light), auxin and pathogen signals all regulate pump activity through this mechanism. Because it is the energy source for almost all transport at the plasma membrane, it has been called the "master enzyme" of the plasma membrane; auxin-induced wall acidification and cell elongation ([7.13.17](/7.13.17)) also depend on it.

The vacuolar membrane has two proton pumps, which together acidify the vacuole:

- The vacuolar $\ce{H+}$-ATPase (V-ATPase), homologous to the F-type ATPases of mitochondria and chloroplasts, is a multisubunit rotary motor of about 750 kDa, made of a peripheral $\mathrm{V_1}$ complex that hydrolyzes ATP and an integral membrane $\mathrm{V_0}$ complex that transports protons; it transports about 2 $\ce{H+}$ per ATP and forms no phosphorylated intermediate. It is activated by $\ce{Cl-}$ and inhibited by $\ce{NO3-}$ and bafilomycin. It is also present on the Golgi and other endomembranes.
- **Definition 5.2.2 (H⁺-Pyrophosphatase)** The **H⁺-pyrophosphatase** (V-PPase) is a single-chain proton pump in the vacuolar membrane and other endomembranes that uses the free energy released by hydrolysis of inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi) to pump one $\ce{H+}$ into the vacuole per PPi.

  Hydrolysis of PPi releases less free energy than that of ATP, but the V-PPase moves only one proton per PPi whereas the V-ATPase moves two per ATP, so the energy available per proton transported is similar for the two and they can establish comparable gradients. PPi is a by-product of the synthesis of sucrose, cellulose, nucleic acids and proteins, and the V-PPase uses it instead of ATP, which is especially important when the ATP supply is limited, as in hypoxia or at low temperature. The enzyme is absent from animals and yeast and is found in plants and some bacteria and protists.

The two pumps pump protons into the vacuole, making it 20–30 mV more positive than the cytosol (though still negative relative to the outside of the cell); anions such as $\ce{Cl-}$ and malate follow through channels into the vacuole, neutralizing the charge so that the pumps can establish a larger pH gradient. Vacuolar pH is usually about 5.5, and the vacuoles of lemon juice sac cells can acidify to below pH 2.5 (hyperacidification, which requires a vacuolar membrane with very low proton permeability, a more efficient specialized V-ATPase and buffering by organic acids such as citric acid). The proton gradient of the vacuolar membrane drives the $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ antiporter (NHX), the $\ce{Ca^2+}/\ce{H+}$ antiporter (CAX), the $\ce{H+}$ antiporters for hexoses and sucrose and others that load solutes into the vacuole.

The $\ce{Ca^2+}$-ATPase is also a P-type ATPase, located in the plasma membrane, endoplasmic reticulum and vacuolar membrane, pumping $\ce{Ca^2+}$ out of the cytosol (possibly with countertransport of $\ce{H+}$ inward). One class is released from autoinhibition by binding of $\ce{Ca^2+}$–**calmodulin**, so that a rise in cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ accelerates its own removal through calmodulin, a negative feedback. No $\ce{K+}$ pump or $\ce{Na+}$ pump like the $\ce{Na+}/\ce{K+}$-ATPase has been found in plants. **ABC transporters** use the energy of ATP hydrolysis directly to transport large molecules, for example loading secondary metabolites such as anthocyanins and flavonoids into the vacuole, or transporting abscisic acid.

### 5.2.4. Carrier Kinetics and Biphasic Uptake

Carrier-mediated transport shows saturation kinetics just like enzymatic reactions: the rate of transport rises with substrate concentration toward a maximum $V_{\max}$ and can be described by the **Michaelis–Menten equation**. $V_{\max}$ reflects the number of functional carrier molecules in the membrane, and the **Michaelis constant** $K_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the substrate concentration at which $V_{\max}/2$ is reached and reflects the affinity of the binding site: the lower $K_{\mathrm{m}}$, the higher the affinity. Simple diffusion through an open channel is, by contrast, proportional to concentration (for ions, to the electrochemical potential difference) and does not saturate.

Measuring the rate of ion uptake by excised roots as a function of external concentration often gives complex curves. Epstein and his coworkers in the 1960s, studying barley roots with radioactive $\ce{^{86}Rb+}$ (an analog of $\ce{K+}$), found that uptake showed Michaelis–Menten saturation in the low concentration range of 0–0.2 mM and a second saturating phase in the high concentration range of 1–50 mM. They called the former mechanism I and the latter mechanism II, and proposed that roots have two transport systems of different affinity.

**Definition 5.2.3 (Biphasic Uptake)** The rate of uptake of an ion by roots as a function of external concentration shows two phases: at low concentrations a high-affinity transport system (HATS, $K_{\mathrm{m}}$ in the micromolar range) dominates and saturates, and at high concentrations the contribution of a low-affinity transport system (LATS, $K_{\mathrm{m}}$ in the millimolar range, or unsaturable) becomes apparent. An uptake isotherm made up of the two systems in this way is called **biphasic uptake** (dual isotherm):

$$
v = \frac{V_1[\mathrm{S}]}{K_1 + [\mathrm{S}]} + \frac{V_2[\mathrm{S}]}{K_2 + [\mathrm{S}]}. \tag{5.5}
$$

It is now known that the two systems are encoded by different genes:

| Nutrient | High-affinity system | Low-affinity system |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{K+}$ | $K_{\mathrm{m}} \approx 5$–$30\,\mu\mathrm{M}$; carriers such as HAK5 ($\ce{H+}$-coupled), with the AKT1 channel also contributing; induced by potassium deficiency, strongly selective for $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Rb+}$ | Channels (AKT1 and others), less selective against $\ce{Na+}$, inhibited by tetraethylammonium |
| $\ce{NO3-}$ | $K_{\mathrm{m}} \approx 50\,\mu\mathrm{M}$; NRT2 family (requires the NAR2 protein), induced by nitrate | $K_{\mathrm{m}} \approx 5\,\mathrm{mM}$; NRT1 family |
| $\ce{H2PO4-}$ | $K_{\mathrm{m}} \approx 1$–$5\,\mu\mathrm{M}$; PHT1 family, mainly in root epidermis and root hairs, induced by phosphorus deficiency | Low-affinity PHT1 members |
| $\ce{NH4+}$ | AMT1 family, transport down the gradient | Not yet identified |

The nitrate transporter NRT1.1 (CHL1, first identified from chlorate-resistant mutants: chlorate is an analog of nitrate that nitrate reductase reduces to toxic chlorite) is dual-affinity: it is a high-affinity transporter when its threonine 101 is phosphorylated and a low-affinity transporter when dephosphorylated, and the external nitrate concentration triggers this switch; it is also a nitrate sensor and regulates nitrate-induced gene expression. Sucrose uptake has a similar two-phase pattern: protoplasts from soybean cotyledons show saturation kinetics of $\ce{H+}$–sucrose symport at low concentrations and an unsaturable linear component at high concentrations, and metabolic inhibitors inhibit only the former.

## 5.3. Ion Uptake by Roots

### 5.3.1. Exchange Adsorption of Ions

The first step in uptake is the arrival of ions at the root surface and their adsorption on the cell surface. The root cell wall and plasma membrane surface carry exchangeable ions, mainly the $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$ dissociated from carbonic acid formed when respiratory $\ce{CO2}$ dissolves in water, and $\ce{H+}$ secreted by the root; these exchange with cations and anions of the soil solution, respectively, on the basis of equivalence of charge, so that nutrient ions become adsorbed on the root surface. When the root is in close contact with soil particles, the $\ce{H+}$ on the root surface can also exchange directly with the cations adsorbed on clay surfaces (contact exchange), without passing through the soil solution. Exchange adsorption consumes no metabolic energy and is fast.

### 5.3.2. Apparent Free Space and Donnan Equilibrium

Part of the volume of root tissue is a region that ions can enter and leave by free diffusion, rapidly reaching equilibrium with the external solution; when root segments are transferred from a radioactively labeled solution to an unlabeled one, this fraction of ions is washed out within minutes, whereas ions that have entered the cytosol and vacuole are released slowly over hours or longer. This part is the cell walls and intercellular spaces of the apoplast.

**Definition 5.3.1 (Apparent Free Space)** The part of the volume of root tissue that ions can enter and leave freely and that rapidly equilibrates with the external solution is called the **apparent free space** (AFS). It is expressed as the relative free space:

$$
\mathrm{RFS} = \frac{\text{volume of free space}}{\text{total tissue volume}} \times 100\,\% = \frac{\text{amount of solute in the free space}}{\text{external solution concentration} \times \text{total tissue volume}} \times 100\,\%.
$$

It is called "apparent" because the values measured with different ions differ. The pectin of the cell wall contains many dissociated carboxyl groups, which are fixed negative charges; cations are enriched in the wall and anions are excluded, so the apparent free space measured with cations is too large and that measured with anions too small. The free space is therefore divided into two parts: the "water free space", whose solution is the same as the external one, and the "Donnan free space", which is affected by the fixed charges. Cell walls generally make up 5%–20% of the volume of plant tissue, and the relative free space of roots is mostly in this range.

The distribution of ions caused by fixed charges obeys **Donnan equilibrium**: diffusible ions reach electrochemical equilibrium between the two phases, while the nondiffusible charged components (proteins, wall carboxyl groups) remain on one side. For a monovalent salt $\ce{KCl}$ the equilibrium condition is that the product of the concentrations of diffusible anion and cation is equal on both sides,

$$
[\ce{K+}]_{\mathrm{i}}[\ce{Cl-}]_{\mathrm{i}} = [\ce{K+}]_{\mathrm{o}}[\ce{Cl-}]_{\mathrm{o}}, \tag{5.6}
$$

with each phase remaining electroneutral. A Donnan potential $E = (RT/F)\ln([\ce{K+}]_{\mathrm{o}}/[\ce{K+}]_{\mathrm{i}})$ arises between the two phases.

**Example 5.3.1** (a) A semipermeable membrane divides a container into two parts, A and B, of equal volume. A contains 1 L of a protein solution in which the $\ce{K+}$ balancing the protein anion $\ce{R-}$ is $0.24\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$; B contains 1 L of $0.08\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ $\ce{KCl}$. At equilibrium let $x\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ of $\ce{KCl}$ have passed from B into A: in A, $[\ce{K+}] = 0.24 + x$ and $[\ce{Cl-}] = x$; in B, $[\ce{K+}] = [\ce{Cl-}] = 0.08 - x$. From Eq. (5.6), $(0.24 + x)x = (0.08 - x)^2$, which gives $0.40x = 0.0064$, $x = 0.016$. Hence in A, $[\ce{K+}] = 0.256\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and $[\ce{Cl-}] = 0.016\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, and in B both are $0.064\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$: $\ce{K+}$ is enriched 4-fold in A and $\ce{Cl-}$ is excluded to 1/4.

(b) Let the concentration of fixed negative charge in the cell wall be $0.5\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ (in the aqueous phase of the wall) and the external solution $1\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ $\ce{KCl}$. In the wall $[\ce{K+}]_{\mathrm{w}} = [\ce{Cl-}]_{\mathrm{w}} + 0.5$ and $[\ce{K+}]_{\mathrm{w}}[\ce{Cl-}]_{\mathrm{w}} = 10^{-6}$, giving $[\ce{Cl-}]_{\mathrm{w}} \approx 2 \times 10^{-6}$ and $[\ce{K+}]_{\mathrm{w}} \approx 0.5\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$: $\ce{K+}$ is enriched 500-fold in the wall, $\ce{Cl-}$ is only 1/500 of the external concentration, and the Donnan potential is about $-59 \times \lg 500 = -160\,\mathrm{mV}$. The divalent $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is attracted still more strongly by the fixed charges, so the cell wall is an important reservoir of calcium. This shows that the large "adsorption" of cations in the cell wall requires no metabolic energy.

### 5.3.3. Radial Transport: The Apoplastic and Symplastic Pathways

There are two pathways for ions from the root surface to the xylem vessels ([7.12.20](/7.12.20#sec-20.3.2)). In the **apoplast** pathway, ions diffuse (or move with the water flow) in the continuous space formed by cell walls and intercellular spaces, without entering living cells; in the **symplast** pathway, ions cross the plasma membrane at epidermal or cortical cells into the cytoplasm and diffuse from cell to cell through **plasmodesmata**. Plasmodesmata are cytoplasmic channels $20$–$60\,\mathrm{nm}$ in diameter containing continuous endoplasmic reticulum (the desmotubule), at densities of up to 15 per square micrometer in tissues with active intercellular transport; movement of ions and small molecules through plasmodesmata is by simple diffusion.

The apoplast pathway ends at the endodermis: the **Casparian strip** on the radial and transverse walls of endodermal cells is a lignified and suberized hydrophobic structure that blocks the pathway of ions along the cell wall into the stele and forces all ions to cross a plasma membrane at least once to enter the stele. This has two consequences: uptake of ions by the root is selective and under metabolic control; and ions that have entered the stele cannot leak back to the soil through the apoplast, so the ion concentration of the xylem sap can exceed that of the soil solution. In the root tip, where the Casparian strip is not yet fully developed, and at sites where lateral roots emerge from the pericycle and break through the endodermis, a small amount of ions can enter the stele directly through the apoplast.

### 5.3.4. Xylem Loading

After entering the stele, ions diffuse in the symplast of the pericycle and xylem parenchyma cells and are finally released from the living cells into the dead vessels and tracheids, whose lumens are continuous with the apoplast.

**Definition 5.3.2 (Xylem Loading)** The process by which ions leave the symplast of xylem parenchyma cells (and pericycle cells) and enter the xylem vessels or tracheids is called **xylem loading**.

In the mid-twentieth century there was a debate over whether ions enter the vessels passively, leaking out of stelar cells that are short of oxygen, or are actively secreted by living cells (the "double pump" model of Crafts and Broyer, 1938, held that cortical cells actively absorb and stelar cells passively release). It is now known that xylem loading is tightly regulated: the plasma membrane of xylem parenchyma cells contains an $\ce{H+}$-ATPase that maintains a negative membrane potential, along with aquaporins and a variety of specialized efflux channels and carriers. In *Arabidopsis* the outward-rectifying $\ce{K+}$ channel SKOR, expressed in pericycle and xylem parenchyma cells, releases $\ce{K+}$ into the vessels, and mutants lacking SKOR have greatly reduced $\ce{K+}$ transport from root to shoot; several classes of anion channels are responsible for efflux of $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$; and boric acid, $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and phosphate each have specific loading proteins. Drought, abscisic acid and elevated cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ all lower the activity of SKOR and anion channels, which may help roots conserve water in drought. Under salt stress, HKT1 in the plasma membrane of xylem parenchyma cells retrieves $\ce{Na+}$ from the transpiration stream into the cells, reducing the entry of $\ce{Na+}$ into leaves; introducing an HKT1-like gene into durum wheat markedly increased grain yield on saline soil. Ions accumulating in the vessels lower the water potential of the xylem sap, so that water enters the vessels and generates **root pressure** ([7.13.3](/7.13.3)); the ions are then carried to the shoot with the water flow produced by **transpiration**.

## 5.4. Characteristics of Uptake and Influencing Factors

### 5.4.1. Water Uptake and Mineral Uptake

Mineral uptake and water uptake by roots are both related and relatively independent. They are related in that mineral elements must be dissolved in water to be absorbed; ion uptake lowers the water potential of cells and promotes water uptake; and the transpiration stream drives mass flow, affecting the rate at which nutrients reach the root surface ([7.13.4](/7.13.4)). They are independent in that they cross the membrane through different transport proteins (aquaporins and ion transporters); the amounts taken up are not proportional, since ions are taken up neither in proportion to water nor in proportion to one another; the driving forces differ, water uptake being mainly a passive process caused by transpirational pull while mineral uptake is mainly active uptake that consumes metabolic energy and shows saturation; and the destinations differ, most of the water going to the leaves to be transpired while the mineral elements go mainly to the growth centers.

### 5.4.2. Selective Absorption and Physiological Acidity or Alkalinity

**Definition 5.4.1 (Selective Absorption)** The property whereby a plant takes up different ions in the same solution, or the anion and cation of the same salt, in proportions different from their proportions in the solution is called **selective absorption**.

For example, tomato takes up $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$ faster than water and hardly takes up silicic acid, whereas rice takes up $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$ more slowly than water but takes up silicic acid in large amounts. The basis of selective absorption is that each ion has its own transport proteins, whose number and activity are regulated by genetics and nutritional status. When the anion and cation of the same salt are taken up unequally, to maintain electroneutrality the root must simultaneously release or take up $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ (or $\ce{HCO3-}$), which changes the pH of the medium:

**Definition 5.4.2 (Physiologically Acid and Alkaline Salts)** A salt of which the plant takes up more cation than anion, so that the medium becomes acidic, is called a **physiologically acid salt**, such as $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$ and most ammonium salts; a salt of which the plant takes up more anion than cation, so that the medium becomes alkaline, is called a **physiologically alkaline salt**, such as $\ce{NaNO3}$ and $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$; a salt whose anion and cation are taken up in similar amounts and which hardly changes the pH of the medium is called a physiologically neutral salt, such as $\ce{NH4NO3}$.

The mechanism can be understood from charge balance and assimilation reactions. When roots take up $\ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{H+}$ is expelled by the $\ce{H+}$-ATPase to compensate for the positive charge flowing in, and assimilation of ammonium into amino acids in the cell also releases protons, so ammonium nutrition acidifies the rhizosphere. When roots take up $\ce{NO3-}$ by $\ce{H+}$ symport, external $\ce{H+}$ is consumed, and the reduction of nitrate to ammonium and its assimilation into amino acids again consume $\ce{H+}$ on balance (producing $\ce{OH-}$, partly excreted or stored as $\ce{HCO3-}$ or organic anions), so nitrate nutrition alkalinizes the rhizosphere. Chinese textbooks often explain physiological alkalinity by "exchange of $\ce{NO3-}$ with $\ce{HCO3-}$ at the root surface", which describes only one form of charge compensation.

**Example 5.4.1** Let 1 L of unbuffered nutrient solution contain $0.5\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$, and let the plant take up all of the $1\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{NH4+}$ in one day while taking up almost no $\ce{SO4^2-}$. In the charge-balance approximation one $\ce{H+}$ is expelled per $\ce{NH4+}$ taken up, so the solution gains $1\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ of $\ce{H+}$ and the pH falls to about 3.0. Real nutrient solutions have some buffering and the pH does not fall so much, but this shows why in hydroponics the use of ammonium salts alone or nitrate salts alone makes the pH deviate quickly, and why the two nitrogen sources are often combined ([7.13.4](/7.13.4#sec-4.6.1)). In the field, long-term heavy use of ammonium sulfate acidifies soil, especially soils of low buffering capacity.

### 5.4.3. Single-Salt Toxicity and Ion Antagonism

**Definition 5.4.3 (Single-Salt Toxicity and Ion Antagonism)** When a plant is grown in a salt solution containing only one metal ion, even at a very low concentration (below the concentration that would be harmful under normal conditions), it soon becomes abnormal and dies; this phenomenon is called **single-salt toxicity**. Adding a small amount of other metal ions to a solution that causes single-salt toxicity weakens or eliminates the toxicity, and this interaction among ions is called **ion antagonism**.

In a classical experiment on wheat roots grown in different salt solutions (total root length), roots reached 59 mm in $\ce{NaCl}$ and 70 mm in $\ce{CaCl2}$, with poor growth in both; 254 mm in $\ce{NaCl + CaCl2}$; and 324 mm in $\ce{NaCl + CaCl2 + KCl}$, with normal growth. A solution made up of several essential elements in suitable concentrations and proportions that allows plants to grow well is called a balanced solution; for marine plants seawater is a balanced solution, and for land plants the soil solution is generally a balanced solution.

Chinese textbooks often explain single-salt toxicity and antagonism by the viscosity of protoplasmic colloids ($\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Na+}$ lower viscosity and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ raises it). The current understanding is at the level of the membrane: $\ce{Ca^2+}$ bridges the negative charges of phospholipids, stabilizing the structure of the plasma membrane and reducing the entry of $\ce{Na+}$ into cells through nonselective cation channels; high concentrations of $\ce{Na+}$, in turn, displace $\ce{Ca^2+}$ from the membrane, increasing membrane permeability and causing $\ce{K+}$ leakage, and compete with $\ce{K+}$ for transport proteins, creating an imbalance of $\ce{K+}/\ce{Na+}$ in the cell. Antagonism therefore occurs mostly between ions of different valence ($\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$), and the alleviation of salt injury by calcium is the physiological basis for applying gypsum to reclaim saline soils ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)). Ions of the same valence also show competitive antagonism, such as $\ce{K+}$ with $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ with $\ce{NH4+}$ (calcium can reduce the damage caused by excess ammonium in the soil solution).

### 5.4.4. Environmental Factors Affecting Root Uptake

- Temperature: within a certain range, the rate of uptake rises with soil temperature, and beyond a certain temperature it falls. For example, between 10 and 30 °C the rate of $\ce{K+}$ uptake by roots increases with temperature and declines at about 40 °C. Low temperature weakens respiration and slows active uptake, and changes in membrane fluidity and cytoplasmic viscosity also make it harder for ions to enter; high temperature inactivates enzymes and increases membrane permeability, so that ions leak out passively and net uptake decreases. The temperature coefficient $Q_{10}$ of the uptake process is often greater than 2, clearly higher than for simple diffusion (about 1.3), which is one piece of evidence that uptake depends on metabolism.
- Aeration: with good aeration the soil contains much oxygen and little $\ce{CO2}$, and root respiration is vigorous, supplying ATP for active uptake; in flooded or compacted soil the roots lack oxygen and uptake is inhibited, and accumulated $\ce{CO2}$ also inhibits respiration.
- Concentration of the soil solution: within a certain range, uptake increases with concentration, and at higher concentrations it no longer increases proportionally because the transport proteins saturate (Eq. (5.5)); too high a concentration also lowers soil water potential, causing difficulty in water uptake and even "burning" of seedlings.
- pH: in general, the rate of cation uptake increases with rising pH of the medium and that of anions decreases (for example, $\ce{K+}$ uptake rises with pH between 3 and 7, while $\ce{NO3-}$ uptake falls with rising pH above 4). One direct reason is that apoplastic pH changes the proton-motive force: a rise in pH reduces $\Delta\mathrm{pH}$, so that $\ce{H+}$-coupled anion symport loses part of its driving force; the dissociation state of the amino acids of plasma membrane proteins was also invoked in earlier explanations. Indirectly, pH affects the availability of nutrients through their solubility and through soil microorganisms ([7.13.4](/7.13.4#sec-4.4.2)).
- Interactions among ions: competition, antagonism and synergism (for example, a suitable amount of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ promotes $\ce{K+}$ uptake).

## 5.5. Uptake of Mineral Elements by Aerial Parts

The aerial parts of plants, mainly the leaves, can also absorb mineral nutrients and small organic molecules such as urea; this is called foliar nutrition, and its application in agriculture is foliar fertilization. Solutions enter the leaf mainly through the cuticle and stomata. The cuticle is a mixture of cutin and wax and is not readily permeable to water, but it contains hydrophilic polar channels through which solutions can pass slowly, then reach the plasma membrane through the outer wall of epidermal cells and be transported into the cell; stomata are the necessary route for gaseous substances such as $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{SO2}$, and some ionic nutrients also enter through stomata. The "ectodesmata" described in Chinese textbooks (thread-like structures in the outer wall of epidermal cells extending from the plasma membrane to the surface) are generally thought to be regions of the outer wall that favor the passage of solutions, not true channels connecting the inside and outside of the cell.

For foliar fertilization to be effective, the solution should form a film on the leaf surface, and surfactants (such as Tween 80 or organosilicone surfactants) are often added to lower the surface tension. Young leaves have a thin cuticle and absorb faster than mature leaves. Environmental conditions affect how long the solution stays on the leaf, so spraying is best done on a still evening or an overcast day, to avoid high-temperature evaporation that causes salts to accumulate on the leaf and scorch it; adding lime can lower the solubility of many nutrients and reduce toxicity. The advantages of foliar fertilization are that nutrients can be supplied rapidly late in the crop's life, when the uptake capacity of the roots declines, or in the nutritional critical period; that elements such as iron, manganese and copper, which are easily adsorbed and fixed in soil, are applied to leaves with high efficiency and small amounts; and that micronutrient deficiencies can be corrected quickly. It has been most successful in fruit trees and grapes and is also used on cereals: foliar nitrogen applied to wheat late in the season can raise the protein content of the grain. But the amount that leaves can absorb is limited, and foliar fertilization can only supplement fertilization of the roots.

## 5.6. Transport and Reutilization of Mineral Elements in the Plant

Form of transport: most mineral elements absorbed by roots are transported as ions, and some are first converted into organic compounds in the root. For nitrogen it depends on the plant and the nitrogen supply ([7.13.6](/7.13.6)): xylem sap contains $\ce{NO3-}$ as well as amino acids synthesized in the root (aspartate, a little alanine, methionine, valine) and amides (asparagine, glutamine), and tropical legumes also transport ureides. Phosphorus is transported mainly as phosphate, with a little as organic phosphorus compounds (phosphorylcholine and others); sulfur is transported mainly as $\ce{SO4^2-}$, with a little as methionine and glutathione; metal ions are mostly chelated by organic compounds in the xylem, for example iron is transported as a citrate complex and in the phloem is bound to nicotianamine; the rest, $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$ and others, are transported as ions.

Route of transport: mineral elements absorbed by roots are transported upward in the xylem with the transpiration stream and can also be transferred laterally to the phloem; mineral elements absorbed by leaves or remobilized there are transported in the phloem from source to sink, upward or downward, generally together with the transport of assimilates ([7.13.11](/7.13.11)). Classical evidence comes from the radiotracer experiment of Stout and Hoagland (1939): in a segment of willow stem, the bark was separated from the xylem with waxed paper between them, and $\ce{^{42}K+}$ was supplied at the roots; after five hours almost all the radioactive potassium was in the xylem and very little in the separated bark, whereas when they were not separated, a large amount of the potassium in the xylem moved laterally into the phloem.

Reutilization: some elements, once in the shoot, can be transported from one organ to another and used repeatedly; these are called reutilizable (mobile) elements, such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium and chlorine. Other elements (calcium, sulfur, iron, manganese, boron and others) form stable compounds in cells (calcium with pectin and oxalate, iron precipitated as insoluble forms) or can hardly enter the phloem, and are immobile; they are called nonreutilizable elements. The degree of reutilization determines where deficiency symptoms appear ([7.13.4](/7.13.4#sec-4.2.3)). During leaf senescence large amounts of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are remobilized to young leaves, seeds or storage organs: before leaf fall in perennial plants and during grain filling in annual plants, proteins in the leaves are degraded and nitrogen is exported through the phloem as amino acids and amides ([7.13.24](/7.13.24)); nonreutilizable elements such as calcium remain in old leaves and leave the plant with the fallen leaves.

Through channels ($n = 0$): $\lg(c^{\mathrm{i}}/c^{\mathrm{o}}) = 120/59.2 = 2.03$, a maximum accumulation of about 107-fold, so external $\ce{K+}$ must be at least $100/107 \approx 0.93\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. The $\ce{K+}$ in soil solution is often below this value (tens to hundreds of micromolar); then channels could only let $\ce{K+}$ flow out, and uptake must depend on $\ce{H+}$-coupled high-affinity carriers (HAK5 and others); this agrees with the high-affinity system acting in the micromolar range in biphasic uptake.

3. Why is the plasma membrane $\ce{H+}$-ATPase of plant cells said to be equivalent to the $\ce{Na+}/\ce{K+}$-ATPase of animal cells? How do the two differ?

   **Answer** Both are P-type ATPases and primary active transport systems in the plasma membrane; the ion electrochemical gradients they establish provide the energy for secondary active transport of almost all other solutes and maintain the membrane potential and ionic homeostasis of the cell. They differ in that animals use the $\ce{Na+}$ gradient as the "currency" (3 $\ce{Na+}$ out, 2 $\ce{K+}$ in), whereas plants, fungi and bacteria use the $\ce{H+}$ gradient (1 $\ce{H+}$ per ATP, no counterion, more strongly electrogenic); as a result the membrane potential of plants (−100 to −200 mV) is much more negative than that of animals (about −70 mV), and the apoplast is acidic (pH about 5.5). Plants have no $\ce{Na+}/\ce{K+}$-ATPase, and $\ce{Na+}$ is extruded by $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ antiport. The plasma membrane $\ce{H+}$-ATPase also takes part in cell elongation (acid growth) and stomatal movement through acidification of the apoplast, which is a function specific to plants.

4. (Calculation) The concentration of fixed negative charge of cell wall pectin is $0.3\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and the external solution is $5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ $\ce{KCl}$. Find the concentrations of $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ in the wall at Donnan equilibrium and the Donnan potential.

   **Answer** Let $[\ce{Cl-}] = y$ in the wall, so $[\ce{K+}] = y + 0.3$; from Eq. (5.6), $y(y + 0.3) = (0.005)^2 = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}$, $y \approx 8.3 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ (exact solution $y = 8.31 \times 10^{-5}$). So $[\ce{K+}]_{\mathrm{w}} \approx 0.300\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ (60 times the external value) and $[\ce{Cl-}]_{\mathrm{w}} \approx 0.083\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ (1/60 of the external value). The Donnan potential is $E = -59.2 \times \lg(0.300/0.005) = -59.2 \times 1.78 = -105\,\mathrm{mV}$ (the wall is negative relative to the external solution).

5. Design an experiment to distinguish whether the uptake of an ion by roots is passive diffusion through the apoplast or active transmembrane uptake, and explain how to measure the apparent free space.

   **Answer** Idea: treat excised root segments with a solution of the radioisotope-labeled ion for a period, transfer them to unlabeled solution of the same ion, and measure the radioactivity washed out at intervals. The fast phase of the efflux curve (completed within minutes, with a rate largely independent of temperature and metabolic inhibitors) comes from the free space; the slow phase comes from the cytosol and vacuole. One can also compare uptake at low temperature (2 °C) or with metabolic inhibitors ($\ce{CN-}$, uncouplers) with uptake without them: the amount in the free space is hardly affected, whereas the transmembrane accumulation is strongly inhibited and shows saturation kinetics and $Q_{10} > 2$. The apparent free space is obtained by dividing the amount of solute washed out in the fast phase by the external concentration and then by the tissue volume; measuring separately with a cation and an anion and comparing the two allows the contribution of the Donnan free space (fixed charge) to be estimated.
