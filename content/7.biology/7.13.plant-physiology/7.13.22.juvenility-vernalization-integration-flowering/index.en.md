The previous chapter discussed how a single environmental signal, photoperiod, is measured by the leaf and carried to the shoot apex in the form of florigen ([7.13.21](/7.13.21)). Whether a plant flowers, however, depends on more factors: whether it has "grown up" to an age at which it can flower, whether it has experienced winter cold, the state of its endogenous hormones and carbohydrates, and whether the ambient temperature is suitable. The genetics of *Arabidopsis* groups these factors into several flowering pathways, which converge on a few "integrator" genes; the integrators decide when the shoot apical meristem changes into an inflorescence and flowers.

This chapter first describes the phases of flowering and the juvenile phase (Sections 22.1 and 22.2), then discusses vernalization: its physiological conditions, site and transmission, and its molecular mechanism as an epigenetic memory (Section 22.3). Section 22.4 introduces the remaining pathways and Section 22.5 integrates them. The last two sections discuss the changes that occur at the shoot apex after flowering is induced: flower bud differentiation and the formation of floral organs (Section 22.6) and sex differentiation (Section 22.7). The ABC, ABCDE and tetramer models of floral organ identity have already been covered in [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.1.2), so only their upstream regulation is discussed here; the epigenetic silencing of *FLC* uses the Polycomb repressive complex and models of epigenetic memory ([7.2.15](/7.2.15#sec-15.6.3)) and the dynamics of bistable switches ([7.6.33](/7.6.33)).

## 22.1. Phases of Flowering

Flower bud differentiation marks the transition of a plant from vegetative to reproductive growth. From seed germination to flowering, a plant must meet two kinds of conditions, internal and external:

- Internal conditions: reaching a certain age (passing the juvenile phase) and physiological state (sufficient accumulation of nutrients, appropriate hormone levels). The physiological state a plant must reach before it can perceive external stimuli and flower is called *ripeness to flower*.
- External conditions: suitable photoperiod, low temperature (for plants that require vernalization), temperature, water and nutrition.

The process of flowering is usually divided into three phases:

1. *Floral induction*: the plant is induced by internal and external signals, specific genes are switched on, the course of development is changed, and the plant enters the floral-determined state. Photoperiodic induction and vernalization occur in this phase.
2. *Floral evocation*: the series of changes in the shoot apical meristem before floral primordia form, and the whole process of differentiating recognizable floral primordia, also called floral initiation.
3. *Floral development*: the formation and maturation of floral organs.

Requirements for flowering are either "qualitative" or "quantitative" ([7.13.21](/7.13.21#sec-21.2.1)): if a condition is lacking and the plant never flowers, the requirement is obligate; if flowering is delayed but eventually occurs, it is facultative. Most plants have facultative requirements for many conditions, which makes flowering time a quantitative trait determined jointly by many factors.

## 22.2. Juvenility and Phase Change

### 22.2.1. The Juvenile Phase

During the life of a plant its shoot apical meristem passes successively through three developmental phases: the juvenile vegetative phase, the adult vegetative phase and the reproductive phase.

**Definition 22.2.1 (Juvenile Phase)** The vegetative growth stage before a plant reaches ripeness to flower, that is, the period during which no treatment can induce flowering, is called the **juvenile phase**. The change of the shoot apical meristem from the juvenile phase to the adult phase (and from the vegetative phase to the reproductive phase) is called **phase change**.

The juvenile phase of herbaceous plants is very short, only a few days or weeks; some plants already have floral primordia in the seed and almost no juvenile phase (such as peanut). The juvenile phase of woody plants lasts from several years to several decades:

| Plant | Length of juvenile phase |
|---|---|
| Japanese morning glory | 2–3 d |
| Henbane | 10–30 d |
| Rose (hybrid tea) | 20–30 d |
| Grape | 1 yr |
| Apple | 4–8 yr |
| Ivy | 5–10 yr |
| Redwood | 5–15 yr |
| Maple | 15–20 yr |
| Moso bamboo and other bamboos | 30–40 yr or longer |
| European beech | 30–40 yr |

The juvenile and adult phases differ not only in the ability to flower but often also clearly in morphology (heteroblasty). The classic example is ivy (*Hedera helix*):

| Trait | Juvenile form | Adult form |
|---|---|---|
| Leaf shape | 3–5 shallow lobes | Entire, ovate |
| Phyllotaxis | Alternate (distichous) | Spiral |
| Plastochron | About 1 week | About 2 weeks |
| Growth habit | Climbing, creeping, oblique growth | Erect, shrubby |
| Anthocyanin | Present in young leaves and stem | Absent |
| Hairs on stem | Present | Smooth |
| Aerial roots | Present | Absent |
| Rooting of cuttings | Easy | Poor |
| Flowering | Does not flower | Flowers and sets seed |

In addition, juvenile tissues generally grow fast, respire strongly, have vigorous nucleic acid metabolism and protein synthesis and contain more auxin, and cuttings root easily. In many trees juvenility also appears as retention of dead leaves through winter (the basal branches of oak and beech).

On one tree, juvenile and adult regions are spatially distributed. The shoot apical meristem matures progressively as it grows, so the base of the trunk, the earliest-formed part, is juvenile, the upper crown, the last-formed part, is adult, and the middle is transitional, giving an overall cone-shaped "juvenile zone." This state is maintained stably in vegetative propagation: adult shoots taken from the top of the crown and used as scions flower soon after grafting, whereas juvenile shoots taken from the base retain juvenile traits for a long time after grafting. In horticulture, juvenile shoots from the base are used for cuttings (they root easily) and adult shoots from the crown as scions (early fruiting). Once a plant has matured it is very stable, and it generally returns to the juvenile phase only through sexual reproduction, in the embryo.

### 22.2.2. The Age Timer: miR156 and SPL

How does a plant "know" its age? Studies in *Arabidopsis* and maize show that the timer is a microRNA. The level of miR156 in the shoot apex is very high in the seedling stage and then decreases gradually as leaves are produced. The targets of miR156 are a class of SBP-box transcription factor genes (*SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN-LIKE*, *SPL*): as miR156 decreases, SPL protein increases, and when it reaches a threshold adult traits appear (in *Arabidopsis*, trichomes begin to form on the abaxial side of leaves and the leaf margin becomes serrated) and the shoot apex acquires competence to receive the flowering signal (Figure 1).

- In maize, the *Corngrass1* mutation causes overexpression of miR156 and the plant keeps juvenile traits for a long time; in *Arabidopsis*, overexpression of miR156 prolongs the juvenile phase and delays flowering, whereas expression of an SPL not regulated by miR156 shortens the juvenile phase. This mechanism is highly conserved among angiosperms and is also present in woody plants: in perennial trees (such as poplar and eucalyptus) the level of miR156 likewise decreases with age.
- SPL9 and SPL10 activate transcription of another microRNA, miR172. The targets of miR172 are a group of AP2-type transcription factors (such as TOE1 and SMZ), which repress *FT* in the leaf. As age increases, therefore, miR156 falls and miR172 rises, and the repression of flowering is relieved. SPL3–5 directly activate *FUL*, *LFY* and *AP1* in the shoot apex (Section 22.5).
- Why does miR156 decrease with age? One important signal is sugar: sucrose produced by the leaves represses expression of the *MIR156* genes, and in seedlings from which leaves have been removed or which grow in weak light, miR156 declines more slowly and phase change is delayed (Yang et al., 2013; Yu et al., 2013). Another sugar signal is trehalose-6-phosphate: *Arabidopsis* lacking it flowers very late even under inductive conditions, and it promotes flowering pathways in both leaf and shoot apex. This links "age" to the size and carbon nutrition of the plant: the shoot apex must reach a certain size and obtain sufficient carbohydrate supply before it can form floral primordia.

Sugar represses expression of *MIR156*; in plants whose leaves have been removed or whose sugar supply is insufficient (dashed line) miR156 declines more slowly and phase change is delayed](./plots/age-timer.py)

### 22.2.3. Factors Affecting Phase Change and Shortening the Juvenile Phase

The transition from the juvenile to the adult phase is affected by nutrition, hormones, the environment and the expression of specific genes. Conditions that promote rapid growth of the shoot apex and bring it faster to a certain size generally shorten the juvenile phase; if growth is inhibited (poor nutrition, low light) the juvenile phase is prolonged. Shortening the juvenile phase of woody plants is important in breeding because it determines the length of a breeding cycle. Common methods are:

- Accelerating growth: long days, adequate water and fertilizer and suitable temperature bring seedlings to a certain size as soon as possible.
- Grafting: using shoots of adult plants as scions, or grafting seedlings onto adult plants (drawing on the nutrition of the adult stock), brings fruiting earlier.
- Hormones: the effect of gibberellin varies among plants. Applying $\mathrm{GA_3}$ to ivy can restore juvenile traits to adult shoots; in many fruit trees gibberellin prolongs the juvenile phase and inhibits flowering; but in many conifers (Cupressaceae, Taxodiaceae) gibberellin (especially a $\mathrm{GA_{4/7}}$ mixture) can induce young plants to form cones.
- Transgenes: overexpression of *Arabidopsis* *LEAFY* (*LFY*) in poplar makes very young plants flower within a few months (Weigel and Nilsson, 1995); overexpression of *FT*-type genes or *BpMADS4* in apple and plum can shorten the flowering cycle to less than a year and is used in "fast breeding."

## 22.3. Vernalization

### 22.3.1. Discovery and Concept

It has long been known that autumn-sown crops such as winter wheat, winter rye and winter rapeseed overwinter as seedlings and flower in early summer of the next year; if they are spring-sown they fail to flower or flower much later. In the nineteenth century it was found that if germinating winter wheat seeds are held at low temperature for some time and then sown in spring, they head the same summer. G. Gassner (1918) confirmed the low-temperature requirement for flowering in winter cereals by comparing winter and spring rye.

**Definition 22.3.1 (Vernalization)** The induction or promotion of flowering of plants by low temperature is called **vernalization**.

Two main kinds of plants require vernalization. The first are winter annuals (winter wheat, winter rye, winter barley, winter rapeseed and winter-annual *Arabidopsis*), which germinate in autumn, overwinter as seedlings and flower the following year. The second are biennials (radish, carrot, sugar beet, celery, Chinese cabbage, cabbage and the biennial strains of henbane), which form a vegetative body in the first year (often a rosette, a storage root or a head) and bolt and flower in the second year after overwintering. In addition, many perennials (some chrysanthemum cultivars, primrose, stock) require vernalization every year.

The low-temperature requirement likewise has qualitative and quantitative forms. For many biennials it is obligate; for winter cereals it is mostly facultative, and they will head very late even without cold. The biological significance of vernalization is that it delays flowering until winter has passed: winter annuals exploit cool, moist autumn conditions to germinate and grow, overwinter safely as a vegetative body (flowers are especially sensitive to frost) and flower and set seed rapidly in spring. The main groups of angiosperms originated in warm climates, and vernalization evolved independently several times as different groups adapted to temperate regions, so the mechanisms of vernalization differ among plants (Section 22.3.6).

Wheat is classified by its vernalization requirement into winter, semi-winter and spring types:

| Type | Vernalization temperature range / °C | Days required / d | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Winter | 0–3 | 40–45 | Strongly winter cultivars of the northern winter-wheat region |
| Semi-winter | 3–6 | 10–15 | Cultivars of the Huang-Huai wheat region |
| Spring | 8–15 | 5–8 | Spring cultivars of the spring-wheat region and those sown in winter in the south |

### 22.3.2. Conditions for Vernalization

First, low temperature and time. The effective temperature for vernalization is generally 0–10 °C and the optimum is 1–7 °C; below freezing metabolism stops and vernalization cannot proceed; above 10–15 °C the effect decreases rapidly. The duration of cold needed depends on the plant and cultivar, from a few days to two or three months: winter *Arabidopsis* needs about 40 d at 4 °C and winter rye about 6–8 weeks. Within a certain range, the longer the cold, the earlier the flowering, until saturation is approached (Figure 2B).

A common quantitative description converts the temperature of each day into "effective vernalization days." Let the vernalization efficiency at temperature $T$ be $r(T)$ ($0 \le r \le 1$, equal to 1 at the optimum temperature); the accumulated vernalization and the number of days to flowering are then

$$
V = \sum_{i} r(T_i)\,\Delta t, \qquad
D = D_{\min} + (D_0 - D_{\min})\,\mathrm{e}^{-V/V_{\mathrm{s}}},
\tag{22.1}
$$

where $D_0$ is the number of days to flowering without any cold, $D_{\min}$ is the minimum number of days determined by photoperiod and temperature after full vernalization, and $V_{\mathrm{s}}$ is the vernalization requirement of the cultivar (large for winter cultivars, small for spring cultivars). The accumulation of vernalization days used in crop models is similar.

**Example 22.3.1** A winter wheat cultivar has $D_0 = 160\ \mathrm{d}$, $D_{\min} = 55\ \mathrm{d}$ and $V_{\mathrm{s}} = 15\ \mathrm{d}$; $r(T)$ is 1 between 1 and 7 °C, decreases linearly to 0 between 7 and 12 °C and is 0 below $-2$ °C (Figure 2A). During winter it experiences 30 d at 2 °C, 20 d at 9 °C and 10 d at $-5$ °C. Find the number of days to flowering, and compare with spring sowing in which it experiences only 10 effective vernalization days.

$r(2) = 1$, $r(9) = (12 - 9)/5 = 0.6$, $r(-5) = 0$, so $V = 30 + 12 + 0 = 42\ \mathrm{d}$. $D = 55 + 105\,\mathrm{e}^{-42/15} = 55 + 105 \times 0.061 = 61\ \mathrm{d}$. With spring sowing $V = 10\ \mathrm{d}$ and $D = 55 + 105\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.67} = 55 + 54 = 109\ \mathrm{d}$; heading is delayed by about 7 weeks and often meets high temperature and drought, so the yield is very low. This is why winter wheat cannot be spring-sown.

:::

Besides low temperature, vernalization also requires oxygen, water and sugar. Vernalization is an active metabolic process: in the absence of oxygen, cold is ineffective; only fully imbibed seeds can be vernalized, and dry seeds cannot pass through vernalization at low temperature (the water content of the seed usually has to be above 40%–50%); vernalization also cannot be completed if respiratory substrate is insufficient, and isolated embryos can be vernalized only on a sugar-containing medium. Vernalization also requires cell division and DNA replication (Section 22.3.3).

Light also has an effect. Abundant light before vernalization lets the plant accumulate nutrients and favors vernalization. Most plants also require long days after vernalization in order to flower, such as winter wheat, winter rapeseed, sugar beet and henbane; these plants therefore flower when day length increases in spring, and the dual requirement of "cold plus long days" reliably signals that winter has passed. There are also short-day plants that require vernalization (some chrysanthemum cultivars) and day-neutral plants that do (some cultivars of faba bean).

The effect of vernalization is not stable before it is complete. If plants are transferred to a higher temperature (such as 30–35 °C) before the end of the vernalization process, the effect of the cold is weakened or eliminated, a phenomenon called *devernalization*. Devernalized plants can be vernalized again. The longer the cold treatment, the more stable the vernalization and the less easily it is reversed by heat: Purvis and Gregory (1952) treated imbibed winter rye seeds at 5 °C for different times and immediately gave a 3 d treatment at 35 °C; the longer the cold treatment, the higher the proportion of plants that retained the vernalized state (Figure 2C). After vernalization is complete, high temperature can no longer reverse it.

Applications of vernalization in production:

- Vernalization treatment: artificial cold treatment of germinating seeds so that the plant completes vernalization. Chinese farmers have long had experience with this, for example putting germinated winter wheat in jars buried in the ground at 0–5 °C for 40–50 d (the "stuffed wheat" or "jar-burying" method) and then sowing in spring; in the "Seven-Nine wheat" method, starting from the winter solstice, seeds are soaked in well water and taken out the next day to dry in the shade, once every nine days, seven times in all. These methods can be used to sow winter wheat in spring as a replacement crop.
- Accelerating breeding: vernalizing winter material together with long days allows several generations per year.
- Introduction: winter temperatures in the south are high, and winter cultivars from the north introduced to the south may fail to head because they do not receive enough cold ([7.13.21](/7.13.21#sec-21.7.2)).
- Controlling flowering time and bolting: biennial vegetables harvested for their vegetative organs (Chinese cabbage, cabbage, radish, onion) "bolt prematurely" if they meet cold after early-spring sowing, and the head or fleshy root cannot develop; cultivars with a strong winter habit should be chosen and the sowing date watched. The roots of medicinal plants such as Chinese angelica become lignified after bolting, so vernalization must also be avoided.

### 22.3.3. Site of Vernalization and Transmission of the Stimulus

The site that perceives cold is the shoot apical meristem or, more generally, tissue capable of cell division. If celery is grown in a greenhouse and only its shoot tip is cooled with a cold-water pipe, the plant flowers; conversely, if celery is placed in the cold with its shoot tip kept warm, it does not flower. Isolated shoot tips and fragments of embryos containing only the shoot tip can be vernalized. Using *Lunaria*, Wellensiek (1960s) showed that vernalization occurs only in dividing cells: of leaf cuttings that had been chilled, only plants regenerated from cells dividing at the base of the petiole flowered. Vernalization is therefore usually carried out after seed germination or in the seedling stage: winter wheat and winter rye can be vernalized from seed imbibition to the seedling stage, fastest at the three-leaf stage; cabbage, evening primrose and carrot can pass through vernalization only as seedlings that have reached a certain size (for example evening primrose at the 6–7 true-leaf stage).

Can the effect of vernalization be transmitted? In the 1930s G. Melchers performed a series of grafting experiments with henbane:

- A vernalized biennial henbane grafted to an unvernalized biennial henbane made the latter flower under long days;
- A vernalized henbane grafted onto tobacco or petunia made the latter flower;
- A biennial henbane without cold, grafted to an annual henbane or to the long-day plant tobacco, also flowered under long days.

Melchers inferred that a transmissible flowering stimulus is produced in vernalized plants and called it "vernalin," but it was never isolated. Melchers and Lang later proposed a reaction-sequence hypothesis: a precursor is converted in the cold into an unstable intermediate, which is converted in the cold into a stable end product, completing vernalization; if heat is encountered before vernalization is complete the intermediate is broken down, which appears as devernalization.

Today these experiments are understood as follows. Vernalization itself is a cell-autonomous epigenetic change that in *Arabidopsis* occurs at the *FLC* locus of each cell that experiences cold (Section 22.3.5), and it does not move; what can be transmitted by grafting is the florigen produced in the leaf after vernalization has relieved repression, namely the FT protein ([7.13.21](/7.13.21#sec-21.6.2)). That is, "vernalin" does not exist, and what is transmitted in the grafting experiments is the flowering signal and not the "vernalized" state. The three-step Melchers–Lang sequence also has a molecular counterpart: early in the cold the transcription of *FLC* is reversibly reduced (the unstable intermediate), and after a long cold stable Polycomb silencing is established at the *FLC* locus (the stable end state).

### 22.3.4. Physiological and Biochemical Changes during Vernalization

Classical physiological studies found that carbohydrate oxidation and energy metabolism are vigorous early in vernalization, nucleic acid metabolism is critical in the middle, and changes in proteins dominate in the middle and late stages; vernalization is also accompanied by DNA demethylation of certain genes. These observations show that vernalization is completed by several metabolic steps in sequence and is regulated by many genes, but by themselves they do not reveal the mechanism.

**Gibberellin** can replace low temperature and make some plants that require vernalization flower without cold, such as henbane, Chinese cabbage, carrot and sugar beet (Lang, 1957). But the two act differently: after gibberellin treatment the stem elongates first (bolting) and flower buds form afterward, whereas after vernalization flower bud formation and stem elongation appear almost simultaneously. Gibberellin acts mainly on the bolting of rosette long-day plants and promotes expression of flowering genes (*SOC1*, *LFY*); it bypasses the requirement for vernalization rather than being an intermediate of vernalization.

### 22.3.5. *Arabidopsis*: Epigenetic Silencing of *FLC*

The molecular mechanism of vernalization is best understood in *Arabidopsis*. There are two kinds of *Arabidopsis* ecotypes: the ecotypes commonly used in laboratories (Col, Ler) are summer annuals (rapid-cycling types) that do not require vernalization, whereas most ecotypes in the wild are winter annuals that flower very late without cold. The difference between the two lies mainly in two genes:

- *FLOWERING LOCUS C* (*FLC*) encodes a MADS-box transcription factor and is a repressor of flowering. In the leaf it directly represses transcription of *FT*, and in the shoot apex it represses *SOC1* and *FD*, so that the plant is insensitive to florigen and does not flower.
- *FRIGIDA* (*FRI*) encodes a scaffold protein that forms a transcriptional activation complex with other proteins and raises *FLC* expression. Winter ecotypes carry a functional *FRI* (and a functional *FLC* allele) and *FLC* is highly expressed; rapid-cycling types mostly have loss-of-function alleles of *FRI* (Johanson et al., 2000) or a weak *FLC* allele.

The action of vernalization is to shut off *FLC*. In the shoot apex of winter ecotypes *FLC* is strongly expressed before cold; after cold treatment the level of *FLC* expression falls, the lower the longer the treatment, and this silencing is maintained for the rest of the life cycle after return to warm conditions, even though the cells have divided many times. This is an epigenetic memory: the expression state of the gene has changed, the DNA sequence has not, and the state can be passed to daughter cells through mitosis. Its molecular process has several steps:

1. Early cold: the antisense long noncoding RNA COOLAIR of *FLC* is rapidly induced by cold and is associated with reduced transcription of *FLC* (Swiezewski et al., 2009). Repression in this phase is reversible.
2. Prolonged cold: a PHD-finger protein, VIN3 (*VERNALIZATION INSENSITIVE 3*), is induced only after cold lasting several weeks (Sung and Amasino, 2004); it binds Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2, containing the histone methyltransferase CLF/SWN and subunits such as VRN2) and, at a short "nucleation region" (a few nucleosomes) near the first intron of *FLC*, writes trimethylation of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27me3). The biochemistry of PRC2 and H3K27me3 is described in [7.2.15](/7.2.15#sec-15.6.3).
3. After return to warmth: H3K27me3 spreads from the nucleation region over the whole *FLC* locus and is maintained by proteins such as LHP1 and VRN1; the chromatin becomes facultative heterochromatin and silencing is stable. The *vrn1* and *vrn2* mutants can transiently reduce *FLC* in the cold, but after return to warmth *FLC* is expressed again, and they cannot "remember" winter; in *vin3* mutants cold fails to establish H3K27me3 at the nucleation region, and *FLC* is not stably silenced.
4. Resetting in the next generation: during gametogenesis and embryogenesis the silencing of *FLC* is erased and the gene is reactivated, so winter plants of every generation need to be vernalized again.

The effect of vernalization increases gradually with the duration of cold (Figure 2B). How is this "quantitative" memory implemented? One possibility is that *FLC* expression in each cell decreases gradually in proportion (analog); another is that the *FLC* locus of each cell has only two states, "on" and "off," and cold causes more and more cells to flip randomly and irreversibly from "on" to "off" (digital), so that the population mean expression decreases gradually. Angel et al. (2011) proposed and supported the latter with a mathematical model and reporter-gene expression in individual cells, and single-cell imaging by Berry et al. (2015) confirmed it: in root tip cells an *FLC*–fluorescent protein fusion gene is expressed in an "all-or-none" manner, and the longer the cold, the higher the proportion of cells that are switched off; the state of each *FLC* allele is independent within a cell. The core of the model is positive feedback in histone modification: H3K27me3 recruits PRC2, PRC2 writes more H3K27me3 on neighboring nucleosomes, and active marks exclude PRC2; with sufficiently strong positive feedback a locus has only two stable states, that is, it is **bistable**, and random fluctuations occasionally push it from one state to the other. This is an instance of the epigenetic memory models of [7.2.15](/7.2.15#sec-15.6.4).

An advantage of digital memory is robustness: a brief cold snap in autumn switches off only a few cells, and after warming some of the unstable switching-off is reversed; only when sufficiently long cold has accumulated are most cells switched off stably. The plant thus "measures" the length of winter, rather than merely "sensing" cold.

### 22.3.6. Cereals and Other Plants

Cereals use different genes for vernalization. Wheat and barley have three main *VRN* genes:

- *VRN1* encodes a MADS-box protein homologous to *AP1* and *FUL*; it is induced gradually in the cold, the extent of induction increasing with duration of cold, and it remains induced after warming (chromatin modifications also occur at its locus).
- *VRN2* encodes a zinc-finger protein with a CCT domain that represses *VRN3* under long days; it is the flowering repressor that plays the role of FLC in cereals and is unrelated to FLC.
- *VRN3* is the wheat *FT* homolog (*FT1*).

Winter cold induces *VRN1*, and VRN1 represses *VRN2* in the leaf; when day length increases in spring *VRN3* (FT) is no longer repressed and is strongly expressed under long days, and florigen moves to the shoot apex and further activates *VRN1*, so the plant heads. Spring wheats mostly carry dominant alleles with a deletion in the *VRN1* promoter or first intron (expressed without cold) or alleles with a deletion of *VRN2*. Differentiation of spring types from winter ancestors during domestication of wheat relied on exactly these mutations, which allow wheat to be grown at different latitudes and sowing seasons.

Thus in both dicot *Arabidopsis* and monocot cereals vernalization consists of "cold relieving the repression of *FT* by a flowering repressor," but the repressors (FLC and VRN2) and the components of memory are different, a result of convergent evolution. The mechanisms of vernalization in other plants such as sugar beet and chrysanthemum differ again.

## 22.4. Other Pathways to Flowering

The flowering-time mutants of *Arabidopsis* fall into several groups corresponding to several relatively independent flowering pathways (photoperiod, vernalization, autonomous, gibberellin and age pathways, and the ambient-temperature pathway). The photoperiod pathway was discussed in the previous chapter, the vernalization pathway is in Section 22.3 and the age pathway in Section 22.2.2; the others are described below.

### 22.4.1. The Autonomous Pathway

A group of late-flowering mutants (*fca*, *fy*, *fpa*, *fld*, *fve*, *ld*, *flk* and others) flower late under both long and short days, but flower normally after vernalization, and their late flowering depends on a functional *FLC*. The pathway they define is called the *autonomous pathway*: independent of environmental signals such as photoperiod, it lowers *FLC* expression as the plant develops. Most members of the autonomous pathway are RNA-binding proteins, RNA 3′-end processing factors and chromatin-modifying enzymes (such as the histone demethylase FLD); they promote premature termination of COOLAIR and removal of active histone modifications at the *FLC* locus, keeping *FLC* at a low level. Therefore in rapid-cycling *Arabidopsis*, even with a functional *FLC*, as long as *FRI* is inactive the autonomous pathway can hold *FLC* down and the plant does not need vernalization.

### 22.4.2. The Gibberellin Pathway

Gibberellin has the largest effect on flowering. It can make many rosette long-day plants (henbane, spinach, sugar beet) bolt and flower under short days and can replace the requirement for low temperature in many plants (Section 22.3.4). In *Arabidopsis* gibberellin is required for flowering under short days: the gibberellin-synthesis-deficient mutant *ga1-3* flowers essentially normally under long days but never flowers under short days. By degrading **DELLA proteins** (signaling mechanism in [7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.5.2)), gibberellin relieves DELLA repression of transcription factors such as SPL and promotes expression of *SOC1* and *LFY*; in the leaf gibberellin also promotes *FT* expression under long days.

The effect of gibberellin varies among plants and it does not always promote flowering. In many fruit trees (apple, pear, citrus, grape, peach) gibberellin inhibits flower bud differentiation: in years when an apple tree bears a heavy crop, developing seeds produce a large amount of gibberellin that inhibits flower bud formation on the shoots of that year, so that the next year has few flowers and little fruit, one cause of alternate bearing. In production the gibberellin synthesis inhibitor paclobutrazol (PP333) is used to promote flower bud differentiation in fruit trees. In short-day plants the effect of gibberellin on flowering is generally weak or inhibitory.

### 22.4.3. The Ambient-Temperature Pathway

Within the temperature range in which *Arabidopsis* grows normally (about 12–28 °C), higher temperature promotes flowering: under short days flowering at 27 °C is much earlier than at 22 °C and later still at 16 °C. Besides sensing of temperature by phytochrome B and ELF3 and activation of *FT* by PIF4 ([7.13.21](/7.13.21#sec-21.5.4)), temperature also acts through MADS-box repressors: SHORT VEGETATIVE PHASE (SVP) and FLOWERING LOCUS M (FLM) form a complex that represses *FT* and *SOC1*; at low temperature the pre-mRNA of *FLM* is spliced mainly into FLM-β, which can bind SVP, and when temperature rises the splicing pattern changes, FLM-β decreases and SVP protein becomes less stable, so repression is relieved (Posé et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2013). In this way the plant does not flower easily at 16 °C and flowers readily at 27 °C.

Temperature and photoperiod often interact, and a lower night temperature can change the day-length requirement of long-day or short-day plants ([7.13.21](/7.13.21#sec-21.5.4)).

### 22.4.4. Nutrition and the Carbon/Nitrogen Ratio Theory

Nutritional status is closely related to flowering. Early in the twentieth century G. Klebs proposed the carbon/nitrogen ratio theory on the basis of the effect of nutrient conditions on flowering: when the ratio of carbohydrates to nitrogenous compounds in the plant (aboveground part) is high the plant flowers, and when the ratio is low it grows vegetatively. The theory has some basis in practice: in fruit trees, girdling, scoring the branches or bending shoots downward reduces downward transport of photosynthate, raises the carbohydrate content of the shoots and promotes flower bud formation; with too much nitrogen fertilizer crops grow rank and green and flowering is delayed.

The carbon/nitrogen ratio theory is now only of historical interest. It cannot explain the two most important kinds of flowering phenomena, photoperiod and vernalization: under different photoperiods the C/N ratio of a plant can be the same while flowering differs completely; nor can it explain why florigen can be transmitted by grafting. The effects of carbon and nitrogen on flowering are real, but they are not its direct cause; they act through signals: sucrose and trehalose-6-phosphate, as signals of carbon status, take part in the age pathway (Section 22.2.2) and in the expression of *FT* and *SOC1*; nitrate affects flowering genes through NLP-type transcription factors; and the effect of girdling is also related to changes in the transport of hormones (gibberellin, cytokinin).

### 22.4.5. Hormones and Flowering

All hormones affect flowering, but the effects vary among plants and are often related to photoperiod:

| Hormone | Effect on flowering |
|---|---|
| Gibberellin | Promotes flowering of many long-day plants (especially rosette plants) under short days, replaces cold; inhibits flower bud differentiation of many fruit trees |
| Auxin | Inhibits flowering of short-day plants (cocklebur cuttings immersed in IAA solution have photoperiodic induction suppressed; treatment with polar auxin transport inhibitors promotes flowering), promotes flowering of some long-day plants; strongly promotes flowering in Bromeliaceae (pineapple and others), possibly via ethylene |
| Cytokinin | Promotes flowering of the short-day plants *Chenopodium*, *Pharbitis* (morning glory) and duckweed, and of the long-day plant *Arabidopsis*; in *Pharbitis* the increase in cytokinin appears after photoperiodic induction; in white mustard cytokinin causes the cells of the shoot tip to divide mitotically as under long days |
| Ethylene | Induces flowering of pineapple and related genera (in production ethephon or calcium carbide water is used to induce flowering so that pineapple matures in every month); inhibits flowering of most plants such as mango |
| Abscisic acid | Can replace short days and make some short-day plants (black currant, Japanese morning glory, strawberry, *Chenopodium*) flower under long days; inhibits flowering of long-day plants such as darnel and spinach, acting at the shoot apex |
| Polyamines | May promote flower bud differentiation in some plants (putrescine, spermine) |

It is generally considered that flowering proceeds in stages and that different hormones may dominate at different stages, so flowering is not regulated by any single hormone but by the combined regulation of many hormones in space (site of action) and time (induction and developmental stages).

It has been reported that the animal hormone estradiol promotes flowering of cabbage, chicory, duckweed and others, that the content of estrogen-like substances increases in plants induced by a suitable photoperiod, and that sterol synthesis inhibitors also inhibit flower bud formation. Substances with estrogenic activity do exist in plants (phytoestrogens, such as the isoflavones of soybean), but there is no evidence that they are plant hormones. The true steroid hormones of plants are the **brassinosteroids** ([7.13.15](/7.13.15)); *Arabidopsis* mutants defective in their synthesis or signaling flower late, partly because *FLC* expression is raised.

## 22.5. Integration of Flowering Signals

### 22.5.1. Floral Pathway Integrators

The several flowering pathways converge on a few genes, called *floral pathway integrators*, mainly *FT* and *SOC1* and the downstream floral-meristem-identity genes *LFY* and *AP1* (Figure 4):

- In the leaf, *FT* integrates signals from photoperiod (CO), ambient temperature (PIF4, SVP–FLM), the vernalization and autonomous pathways (FLC) and age (miR172–AP2 type).
- *SOC1* (*SUPPRESSOR OF OVEREXPRESSION OF CONSTANS 1*, also called *AGL20*) is a MADS-box gene that in the shoot apex integrates signals from the FT–FD complex, repression by FLC, gibberellin and SPL. SOC1 forms a complex with another MADS protein, AGL24, and activates *LFY*.
- *LFY* (*LEAFY*) encodes a plant-specific transcription factor and *AP1* (*APETALA1*) encodes a MADS-box protein; both are floral-meristem-identity genes: in *lfy* mutants shoots with bracts form where flowers should be, and in *ap1* mutants secondary flowers arise in the flowers. LFY and AP1 activate each other, forming a positive feedback loop; at the same time AP1 represses *SOC1*, *AGL24* and *SVP* in the floral meristem, so that the floral meristem no longer shows inflorescence character.

The LFY–AP1 positive feedback makes the decision to flower irreversible once made: the shoot apex either remains vegetative or enters the reproductive state completely, without stopping in between. On the other hand, *TERMINAL FLOWER 1* (*TFL1*, a member of the same PEBP family as *FT* with the opposite action) is expressed in the center of the inflorescence meristem and represses *LFY* and *AP1*, so that the inflorescence apex keeps growing indeterminately; in *tfl1* mutants the inflorescence apex forms a terminal flower and the inflorescence becomes determinate ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.7.4)). FT and TFL1 compete for the same binding partner FD, and their ratio determines the fate of the meristem: in tomato the dosage balance of *SFT* (FT homolog) and *SP* (TFL1 homolog) determines the pattern of sympodial branching and plant architecture, and the *sp* mutation makes tomato determinate in growth, which is convenient for mechanical harvest.

The significance of integration is that the plant flowers only when multiple conditions are met at the same time. Even if winter *Arabidopsis* meets an occasional long day in autumn, FLC prevents expression of FT and SOC1; after winter FLC is shut off, the long days and rising temperature of spring cause strong expression of *FT*, and together with competence of the shoot apex already conferred by the age pathway, flowering occurs.

![Flowering pathways and integrators in *Arabidopsis* (SBGN activity-flow sketch, simplified). Left (leaf companion cell): long days via CO and higher temperature via PIF4 promote *FT*; FLC and SVP–FLM repress *FT*. Right (shoot apical meristem): FT moves through the phloem to the shoot apex and forms a complex with FD; prolonged cold (vernalization) and the autonomous pathway repress FLC, FRI activates FLC, and FLC represses FT–FD and SOC1; gibberellin and the age pathway (miR156 represses SPL) act on SOC1; SOC1 activates LFY, LFY and AP1 activate each other (positive feedback), and FT–FD activates AP1; TFL1 represses LFY so that the inflorescence keeps growing indeterminately; AP1 and others initiate flower formation](./plots/flowering-network.py)

### 22.5.2. A Light–Temperature Model of Flowering Time

For crops, flowering time is a quantitative trait and the most important environmental variables are temperature and photoperiod. Roberts and Summerfield (1980s) found in soybean, cowpea, wheat and other crops that within a certain range the reciprocal of the number of days $f$ from sowing to flowering (the development rate) is linearly related to both mean temperature $T$ and photoperiod $P$:

$$
\frac{1}{f} = a + bT + cP,
\tag{22.2}
$$

where $b > 0$ (higher temperature accelerates development) and $c$ is negative for short-day plants and positive for long-day plants; outside the photoperiod-sensitive range (photoperiod below a lower limit for short-day plants, above an upper limit for long-day plants) $c = 0$ and the development rate is determined by temperature alone. The parameters of Eq. (22.2) can be estimated by multiple linear regression from trials with several sowing dates or several environments and used to predict flowering time of a cultivar in a new region; the model is related to the concept of thermal time ([7.13.17](/7.13.17)).

**Example 22.5.1** In the photoperiod-sensitive range a soybean cultivar has $a = 0.030\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$, $b = 0.0010\ \mathrm{d^{-1}\,{}^\circ C^{-1}}$ and $c = -0.0020\ \mathrm{d^{-1}\,h^{-1}}$. Find the number of days to flowering at (a) $T = 25\ {}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $P = 12\ \mathrm{h}$; (b) $T = 25\ {}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $P = 14\ \mathrm{h}$; (c) $T = 20\ {}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $P = 14\ \mathrm{h}$.

(a) $1/f = 0.030 + 0.025 - 0.024 = 0.031\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$, $f = 32\ \mathrm{d}$; (b) $1/f = 0.027\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$, $f = 37\ \mathrm{d}$; (c) $1/f = 0.022\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$, $f = 45\ \mathrm{d}$. A 2 h increase in day length delays flowering by 5 d, and a further 5 °C decrease in temperature delays it by another 8 d: at high latitudes the two factors of long days and low temperature add up, and flowering of late-maturing short-day cultivars is greatly delayed.

## 22.6. Flower Bud Differentiation and Formation of Floral Organs

### 22.6.1. Flower Bud Differentiation

**Definition 22.6.1 (Flower Bud Differentiation)** The process of formation of floral primordia and of differentiation and maturation of the parts of the flower bud is called the formation of floral organs, or **flower bud differentiation**.

After flower bud differentiation begins, the shoot apex changes markedly in morphology. A vegetative shoot apex is narrow, smooth and not prominent, and regularly produces leaf primordia on its flanks; after induction, cell division in the central zone of the apex accelerates and the cells enlarge, and the growth cone swells and rises into a hemisphere; globular protuberances then form around its base and are gradually pushed upward, forming the primordia of individual small flowers (the changes at the shoot apex of cocklebur after short-day induction are classic material for observation). In fruit trees, leaf buds and flower buds can be clearly distinguished at this time: the growing point of a leaf bud is narrow, whereas that of a flower bud is plump and raised. If environmental conditions are unsuitable the bud stays vegetative and does not differentiate into a flower bud. Flower buds of temperate fruit trees such as apple, pear and peach differentiate in summer and autumn of the previous year and flower after overwintering, so the cultivation management of the current year determines the amount of flowering of the next year.

Physiological and biochemical changes accompany the morphological changes: after flower bud differentiation begins, the metabolic level of the cells rises, organic matter is transformed vigorously, the content of soluble sugars, amino acids and proteins increases and nucleic acid synthesis accelerates. Treating buds with inhibitors of RNA and protein synthesis can block the transition of the vegetative growth cone to a reproductive growth cone, which shows that flower bud differentiation requires new gene expression.

Conditions affecting flower bud differentiation:

| Condition | Effect |
|---|---|
| Nutrition | Sufficient carbohydrate is the material basis of flower bud formation; moderate nitrogen (differentiation is slow and flowers few when it is deficient, and plants grow rank and green and flowers develop poorly when it is excessive); polyamines such as arginine and spermine and phosphorus-containing compounds favor differentiation; combined application of phosphorus and potassium fertilizers with appropriate micronutrients speeds differentiation and increases flower number |
| Hormones | Cytokinin, ethylene and abscisic acid generally promote flower bud differentiation; gibberellin inhibits flower bud differentiation in many fruit trees; auxin promotes at low concentration and inhibits at high concentration |
| Light | Photoperiodic induction; photosynthesis supplies organic matter; stamens are especially sensitive to insufficient light, and shading easily leads to pollen abortion |
| Temperature | Vernalization induces; at suitable temperature, high temperature shortens the differentiation process, and low temperature delays or even stops it; low temperature can also damage pollen mother cells and make the tapetum swell so that it cannot transport nutrients |
| Water | The stages of pistil and stamen differentiation and meiosis are especially sensitive to water deficit; insufficient water delays young panicle differentiation and degenerates spikelets |
| Cultivation | Excessive planting density increases degenerate flowers; spikelets at the top of cereal ears differentiate early and have strong growth vigor (dominant flowers), whereas those at the bottom differentiate late and have weak growth vigor (inferior flowers) |

Measures used in production to promote flower bud differentiation include girdling, branch bending, nitrogen control and spraying growth retardants such as paclobutrazol in fruit trees; using photoperiod and vernalization treatments to control flowering time; and ensuring water and fertilizer supply during young panicle differentiation in cereals.

### 22.6.2. Determination of Floral Organ Identity

After the floral primordium forms, floral organs develop from the outside inward in the order sepals, petals, stamens and carpels (and the ovules in the carpels), and the identity of each whorl is determined by combinations of ABC (ABCDE) homeotic genes, most of which encode MADS-box transcription factors that bind DNA as tetramers ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.1.2), [7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.1.3)). From the standpoint of physiology it is worth adding how these organ-identity genes are switched on by flowering signals:

- LFY is the hub linking "flowering" and "floral organs." In the floral primordium LFY, together with the F-box protein UFO, activates the B-class gene *AP3* in whorls 2 and 3; LFY together with WUSCHEL (WUS) activates the C-class gene *AG* in the center of the flower.
- AG in turn terminates the stem cells of the floral meristem: AG represses *WUS* through KNUCKLES and others, so that the flower stops growing after producing carpels (determinacy; the WUS–CLV feedback is described in [7.12.4](/7.12.4)). The *ag* mutant flower repeatedly produces perianth organs, precisely because this feedback is missing.
- Expression of the E-class genes *SEP* also depends on LFY and AP1, and they provide the common component of the tetramers for all classes of organ identity.

The outputs of the flowering pathways (LFY, AP1) thus directly start the developmental program of floral organs, and from "when to flower" to "what kind of flower to make" is a continuous transcriptional cascade.

## 22.7. Sex Differentiation

### 22.7.1. Types of Sex Expression in Plants

Most angiosperms have bisexual flowers ([7.12.27](/7.12.27#sec-27.3.1)). Plants with unisexual flowers undergo sex differentiation at the same time as flower bud differentiation, and the sex differences are expressed mainly in the floral organs. Sex differentiation in plants has three characteristics: the types are diverse; it is unstable and very easily affected by the environment and by chemicals; and flowers of different sex can occur on the same plant.

| Type of sex expression | Flowers on the same plant | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hermaphroditic (bisexual-flowered) | Bisexual flowers | Wheat, tomato, *Arabidopsis* |
| Monoecious | Male and female flowers | Maize, cucumber, squash |
| Dioecious | Male plants have only male flowers, female plants only female flowers | Spinach, hemp, poplar, willow, asparagus, ginkgo |
| Gynomonoecious | Female and bisexual flowers | Pot marigold, oakleaf goosefoot |
| Gynodioecious | Female or bisexual flowers | Field thistle |
| Andromonoecious | Male and bisexual flowers | Horse nettle, maple, Shandong maple, many melon cultivars |
| Androdioecious | Male or bisexual flowers | Persimmon |
| Trimonoecious or trioecious | Female, male and bisexual flowers | Papaya |

### 22.7.2. Genetic Determination of Sex

In **dioecious** plants sex is usually controlled by sex chromosomes or sex-determining loci. Most sex-determination systems are of the XY type (the male is the heterogametic sex), as in white campion (*Silene latifolia*, with morphologically distinguishable X and Y chromosomes), papaya, asparagus, spinach and hemp; there are also ZW systems (as in some species of *Fragaria*). Dioecy in angiosperms arose independently in many families, and the sex chromosomes are also very "young," most having only a small nonrecombining region. Generally two genes are required: one suppressing the pistil and one promoting the stamens (the two-gene model). The example of *Diospyros* shows how simple the mechanism can be: in diploid date plum (*Diospyros lotus*) *OGI* on the Y chromosome produces a small RNA that represses the autosomal feminizing gene *MeGI*, making the plant male (Akagi et al., 2014). Sex determination in dioecious plants is compared with that of animals in [7.4.6](/7.4.6).

In monoecious or hermaphroditic plants sex expression is controlled by a group of modifier genes, most of which are related to hormone synthesis and signaling (Section 22.7.4).

### 22.7.3. Age and Environment

- Age: in monoecious plants male flowers often appear early in development (at the lower nodes of the plant) and female flowers appear later (at upper nodes), with a transitional zone of mixed male and female flowers in between (Figure 5A).
- Photoperiod: photoperiod not only regulates flowering but also affects sex expression and fertility. In general, continued exposure to the inductive photoperiod increases female flowers: cucumber produces more female flowers under short days; short days cause maize to form female flowers on the tassel.
- Temperature: lower night temperature and a larger diurnal temperature range favor female flower development in many plants, and low night temperature during the seedling stage of cucumber and squash raises the proportion of female flowers.
- Nutrition and water: sufficient water and more nitrogen fertilizer increase female flower differentiation, whereas drought and nitrogen deficiency increase male flowers.
- Other: smoke increases female flowers, the active component being mainly ethylene in the smoke; mechanical wounding may also shift male to female by inducing ethylene.

### 22.7.4. Hormones and Sex Differentiation

The effect of hormones on sex differentiation has been studied most in cucumber:

| Hormone | Effect on sex of cucumber | Other plants |
|---|---|---|
| Ethylene | Promotes female flowers: the ethylene content of the shoot tip of gynoecious lines is higher than that of monoecious lines; ethephon on seedlings greatly increases female flowers, and inhibitors of ethylene synthesis or action (AVG, silver thiosulfate) increase male flowers | Melon: ethylene likewise promotes femaleness |
| Auxin | Promotes female flowers, possibly by inducing ethylene | — |
| Gibberellin | Promotes male flowers, and can make gynoecious lines produce male flowers (used for seed multiplication of gynoecious lines) | Maize: gibberellin promotes femaleness, and gibberellin-deficient dwarf mutants form anthers on the ear |
| Cytokinin | Promotes female flowers | Grape: makes male flowers of male plants produce pistils |

The molecular genetic basis of sex in cucumber is ethylene synthesis. The dominant *F* locus (gynoecious lines) is an extra copy of an ACC synthase gene, *CsACS1G*, which makes the shoot tip produce more ethylene, and female flowers appear from the nodes at the base of the plant; the *M* locus encodes another ACC synthase, CsACS2, which is expressed in female flower primordia and inhibits stamen development, and the *m* mutation turns what would be female flowers into bisexual flowers (andromonoecy). In melon loss of function of *CmACS7* likewise produces andromonoecy (Boualem et al., 2008), and the transcription factor *CmWIP1* represses carpel development and determines male flowers (Martin et al., 2009); cucumber has the corresponding homologs. The role of ethylene here is local and at the organ level: in a flower primordium it inhibits the development of stamens (or promotes carpels), rather than changing the "sex" of the whole plant. The situation in maize is different: masculinization of the tassel requires jasmonic acid, and *tasselseed1* (*ts1*) encodes a lipoxygenase in the jasmonic acid synthesis pathway; *ts1* mutants form female flowers on the tassel (Acosta et al., 2009), showing that abortion of pistils in the tassel is programmed cell death controlled by jasmonic acid ([7.13.24](/7.13.24)).

In production, ethephon (usually sprayed at the 1–3 true-leaf stage) increases female flowers of cucumber, squash and zucchini, advances fruiting and increases yield; gibberellin can induce male flowers in gynoecious cucumber lines to allow seed production of the gynoecious lines.

## Questions

1. With the model and parameters of Example 22.3.1, if a warm winter gives winter wheat only 15 d at 2 °C and 30 d at 10 °C, how many days to flowering are needed? What is the minimum number of effective vernalization days needed for the number of days to flowering not to exceed 65 d?

   **Answer** $r(10) = (12 - 10)/5 = 0.4$, $V = 15 + 30 \times 0.4 = 27\ \mathrm{d}$, $D = 55 + 105\,\mathrm{e}^{-27/15} = 55 + 105 \times 0.165 = 72\ \mathrm{d}$, about 11 d later than in a normal year (61 d). For $D \le 65\ \mathrm{d}$ we need $105\,\mathrm{e}^{-V/15} \le 10$, $V \ge 15\ln 10.5 = 35\ \mathrm{d}$, that is, at least 35 effective vernalization days.

2. Winter *Arabidopsis* is treated at 4 °C for 2 weeks or 6 weeks and then moved to long days; the former flowers late and the latter flowers normally. Explain this result with the digital switch model of epigenetic silencing of FLC, and explain why a brief cold snap in autumn does not make the plant flower in autumn.

   **Answer** In the digital switch model, during cold the *FLC* locus of each cell flips from "on" to "off" with a certain probability and is held by the positive feedback of PRC2 and H3K27me3; the proportion of switched-off cells increases with the duration of cold. After 2 weeks of cold only some cells have switched off *FLC*, VIN3 has not yet been fully induced, and the silencing of many loci is still in the reversible early stage and is re-expressed after warming; population *FLC* is still high, *FT* and *SOC1* are repressed and flowering is delayed. After 6 weeks the vast majority of cells have *FLC* stably switched off, repression is relieved and *FT* is expressed normally under long days. A cold snap in autumn usually lasts only a few days, can switch off very few cells, and the early repression is reversed after warming, so what the plant "remembers" is the accumulated duration of cold and it flowers only after a sufficiently long winter.

3. Using the parameters of Example 22.5.1, calculate the number of days to flowering of this soybean cultivar at a mean temperature of 22 °C and photoperiods of 13 h and 15 h. If this were a northern cultivar introduced to a southern region with shorter day length, how would flowering time and yield change?

   **Answer** $P = 13\ \mathrm{h}$: $1/f = 0.030 + 0.022 - 0.026 = 0.026\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$, $f = 38\ \mathrm{d}$; $P = 15\ \mathrm{h}$: $1/f = 0.022\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$, $f = 45\ \mathrm{d}$. Shorter day length advances flowering. A northern cultivar introduced to the south meets shorter day length and higher temperature in the growing season, both of which accelerate development, so flowering is much earlier, the vegetative period is shortened, plants are small with few pods and yield falls; short-day crops moved from north to south should therefore be late-maturing cultivars (more photoperiod-sensitive, requiring shorter day length) ([7.13.21](/7.13.21#sec-21.7.2)).

4. Melchers found that vernalized henbane could make unvernalized henbane grafted onto it flower. Some concluded from this that "vernalin" exists. Reinterpret this experiment with modern molecular knowledge and design an experiment to test your interpretation.

   **Answer** In *Arabidopsis* and other plants vernalization is a cell-autonomous epigenetic change (Polycomb silencing of *FLC*) and cannot move through a graft; in the vernalized donor *FLC* (or its functional counterpart) is switched off, the leaf produces a large amount of FT protein under long days, and FT enters the unvernalized recipient through the phloem at the graft union and induces flowering at the recipient's shoot apex. What is transmitted is therefore florigen and not the "vernalized" state. Tests: (a) examine the recipient: after the recipient's shoot apex flowers, its own *FLC* should still be highly expressed, without enrichment of H3K27me3; new shoots of the recipient that are not connected to the donor, or plants grown from its seeds, should still require vernalization. (b) Graft with a vernalized donor unable to produce FT (an *ft* mutant or a plant with *FT* silenced): if the recipient no longer flowers, the flowering stimulus is FT. (c) Perform the same graft under short days (when FT is not expressed): even though the donor is vernalized it cannot make the recipient flower.

5. How can female flowers of cucumber be increased in production? Explain the physiological basis of each measure.

   **Answer** (a) Spraying ethephon at the seedling stage: the ethylene released promotes carpel development and inhibits stamens in the flower primordium and increases female flowers (both the *F* and *M* loci are ethylene synthesis genes). (b) Giving short days and lower night temperature (a larger diurnal range) during seedling raising: both conditions promote female expression, possibly also related to changes in ethylene and auxin levels. (c) Ensuring adequate water and applying somewhat more nitrogen fertilizer: abundant nutrition favors female flower differentiation, whereas drought and nitrogen deficiency increase male flowers. (d) Choosing gynoecious lines or strongly female cultivars: cultivars carrying the *F* locus (*CsACS1G*) open female flowers from the lower nodes; when gynoecious lines are multiplied, gibberellin or silver thiosulfate is used to induce male flowers. (e) Avoiding excessive use of gibberellin and other substances that promote maleness.
