Sexual reproduction in angiosperms unfolds on two scales. Morphologically, male and female gametophytes form in the anthers and ovules, pollen is carried to the stigma, the pollen tube delivers the sperm cells into the embryo sac to complete double fertilization, the ovule develops into a seed and the ovary into a fruit; these steps have been covered in the botany courses ([7.12.28](/7.12.28)–[7.12.31](/7.12.31)). This chapter goes through the process again from the standpoint of physiology, concentrating on the materials, energy and signals of each step: why pollen lives for such a short time, how the stigma distinguishes "self" from "other," why the pollen tube can travel through the style at millimeters or even centimeters per hour and find the ovule accurately, how a seed stays alive after losing more than 90% of its water, and why a fruit suddenly begins to ripen at a certain moment and cannot be stopped once it has begun.

The chapter discusses in turn pollen and the stigma (Sections 23.1–23.3), self-incompatibility (Section 23.4), growth, guidance and reception of the pollen tube (Sections 23.5 and 23.6), changes after fertilization (Section 23.7), development and maturation of the seed (Section 23.8), and growth and ripening of the fruit (Sections 23.9 and 23.10). Hormone synthesis and signaling are in [7.13.13](/7.13.13)–[7.13.15](/7.13.15), the transport of assimilates into seeds and fruits and sink strength in [7.13.11](/7.13.11), seed dormancy and germination in [7.13.20](/7.13.20), and the postharvest physiology and storage of fruits and vegetables in the next chapter, [7.13.24](/7.13.24).

## 23.1. Composition and Viability of Pollen

### 23.1.1. Chemical Composition of Pollen

Mature **pollen** is a male gametophyte: a vegetative cell enclosing one generative cell (bicellular pollen) or two sperm cells (tricellular pollen), surrounded by two wall layers. Its chemical composition is directly related to its physiological functions:

| Component | Content | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Exine** | Sporopollenin (a highly cross-linked polymer derived from fatty acids and phenolic compounds), cellulose, protein; the cavities and surface of the exine carry pollenkitt | Resistance to UV, desiccation and microbial degradation; exine proteins are synthesized by the **tapetum** (sporophytic origin), are mostly glycoproteins and take part in pollen–stigma recognition |
| Intine | Pectic substances, cellulose, **callose**, protein | Thicker at the germination aperture, where it forms the wall of the pollen tube; intine proteins are synthesized by the pollen itself (gametophytic origin), are mainly hydrolases and are related to the passage of the pollen tube through the stigma and style |
| Carbohydrates and lipids | Starch, sucrose; lipid droplets | Energy source for germination and early pollen tube growth; pollen of wind-pollinated flowers is mostly starchy, that of insect-pollinated flowers mostly lipid-rich (with many exceptions) |
| Amino acids and proteins | A high proportion of proline among free amino acids | Osmoprotection, maintaining pollen viability; food for pollinators |
| Pigments | Carotenoids, flavonoids | Protect pollen from UV damage; attract insects; pollen of maize and petunia lacking flavonoids cannot germinate normally |
| Enzymes, hormones, vitamins | Various hydrolases and invertases; high auxin content; relatively many B vitamins | Metabolism for germination and pollen tube growth; supply of auxin to the pistil after pollination (Section 23.7) |
| Mineral elements | K, P, Ca, B and others | Ca²⁺ and B are especially important for germination and pollen tube growth |

### 23.1.2. Pollen Viability

**Definition 23.1.1 (Pollen Viability)** The property of pollen to retain the ability to germinate and fertilize after leaving the anther is called **pollen viability**; it is usually measured by germination percentage or by seed set after pollination. The time over which viability is retained is called the longevity of the pollen.

The longevity of pollen differs greatly among plants:

| Plant | Longevity under natural conditions |
|---|---|
| Rice | 10–15 min; seed set after pollination 5 min after anther dehiscence has already fallen below 50% |
| Wheat | Viability begins to fall within a few hours; seed set after pollination 5 h after anther dehiscence is about 6.4% |
| Maize | Only 1–2 h under hot, dry field conditions; one or two days when cool and humid |
| Fruit trees (apple, pear, etc.) | Weeks to months |
| Sunflower | Up to 1 year |
| Some fruit trees of the Rosaceae | Several years under suitable storage |

Pollen of grasses is short-lived for two reasons: it is tricellular, with vigorous metabolism and few reserves; and its water content at shedding is high (about 35%–60% in grasses, whereas most bicellular pollen has only 10%–20% at shedding), so it does not tolerate desiccation and dies quickly from water loss in air. Bicellular pollen is already dehydrated before shedding and its metabolism has fallen to a very low level, so it lives much longer.

The decay of pollen viability can be approximated by an exponential process over a period of time:

$$
V(t) = V_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\ln 2/t_{1/2}},
\tag{23.1}
$$

where $t_{1/2}$ is the time for viability to halve. From the wheat data (down to 6.4% after 5 h), $t_{1/2} = 5\ln 2/\ln(1/0.064) = 1.3\ \mathrm{h}$; for rice $t_{1/2}$ is only about 5 min, and after 15 min only about one eighth remains. In cross breeding, therefore, artificial pollination of rice and wheat must be carried out immediately after anther dehiscence.

Favorable conditions for storing pollen are dryness, low temperature, low oxygen, high $\ce{CO2}$ and darkness: all of these reduce respiration and metabolism of the pollen and so reduce the consumption of reserves, the decline of enzyme activity and the loss of pantothenic acid (a component of coenzyme A) and similar compounds. Common methods include low-temperature dry storage, vacuum and freeze-dry storage, ultralow-temperature storage (liquid nitrogen, $-196$ °C) and keeping pollen immersed in organic solvents such as acetone and ether at 0–5 °C in the dark.

## 23.2. Physiology and Receptivity of the Stigma

The surface of the **stigma** carries many papillar cells. Wet stigmas (tobacco, petunia, lily, apple, legumes and others) secrete at maturity a liquid containing sugars, lipids, phenolics and proteins; dry stigmas (Brassicaceae, Caryophyllaceae, Poaceae, pineapple, castor bean, rose and others) have no flowing secretion on the surface but only a hydrophilic proteinaceous pellicle ([7.12.27](/7.12.27)). The physiological functions of the stigma are to adhere pollen, to supply nutrients for pollen germination and pollen tube growth, to regulate water uptake by the pollen through its water potential, and to recognize pollen (Section 23.3).

**Definition 23.2.1 (Stigma Receptivity)** The ability of the stigma to accept pollen, let it germinate normally and let the pollen tube enter the style is called **stigma receptivity**, also called the viability of the stigma.

The duration of stigma receptivity varies among plants and is directly related to the success of fertilization. The stigma of rice generally remains receptive for 6–7 d, strongest on the day of flowering and declining afterward; the silks of maize (stigma and style) remain receptive longer, rice < wheat < maize. This is used in production to arrange cross-pollination: in the seed production of hybrid rice the stigmas of the female parent are exserted and receptive for a long time, which gives the pollen of the male parent a chance to land on them.

Effects of external conditions on pollination:

- Temperature: low temperature prevents anther dehiscence and most florets cannot be pollinated; high temperature lowers pollen viability and the styles dry up after the glumes open (when rice meets temperatures above 35 °C at heading and flowering, seed set declines markedly).
- Rain: rain washes away the stigma secretion, and pollen takes up too much water and bursts; when the silks of a maize ear receive no pollen they continue to elongate, droop to the side and cover the silks below, causing "bald tips" and "missing kernels."
- Wind: no wind or strong wind are both unfavorable for pollination of wind-pollinated flowers.
- Nutrition: insufficient nutrition affects pollination, and deficiency of phosphorus fertilizer hinders cell division and affects emergence of the silks.

## 23.3. Pollen–Stigma Recognition

### 23.3.1. The Process of Recognition

After pollen lands on the stigma it passes in turn through adhesion, hydration, germination and penetration of the stigma, and every step is accompanied by exchange of information between pollen and stigma. Recognition means that one kind of cell, in the course of contact with another, reacts specifically through physical and chemical exchange of information. The molecules involved in recognition include proteins in the pollen exine and pollenkitt (mostly glycoproteins) and proteins on the surface of stigma papillar cells.

Within a few minutes of landing, the pollen releases exine proteins that interact with proteins on the stigma surface. If the two are compatible, the pollen takes up water and germinates, the tip of the pollen tube (intine) produces cutinase that dissolves the cuticle of the stigma, and the tube penetrates the stigma and enters the style; if they are incompatible, the papillar cells of the stigma produce callose that seals the tip of the pollen tube against the stigma surface, preventing the tube from entering, and fertilization fails. The significance of recognition is to prevent fertilization between individuals that are too different genetically (distant relatives) or too similar (selfing). Incompatibility in distant crosses is very common; self-incompatibility occurs in more than about 40% of angiosperms (Section 23.4).

On a dry stigma hydration of the pollen is the first controlled step: pollen must obtain water from the papillar cell, lipids and proteins of the pollenkitt (such as GRP17 and other oleosin-type proteins in Brassicaceae) form a "foot" at the contact site, and the stigma cell secretes in a directed manner toward the contact point before the pollen can hydrate. A dry stigma can therefore reject incompatible pollen before germination (self-incompatibility in the Brassicaceae occurs at this step), at the cost of slower germination of the pollen.

### 23.3.2. The "Lock" of the Stigma and the "Key" of the Pollen

Studies in *Arabidopsis* have revealed how the stigma prevents pollen tubes of distantly related species from penetrating. The surface of the stigma papillar cells carries a group of receptor-like kinases (FERONIA, ANJEA, HERK1 and others, of the CrRLK1L family); several RALF-type peptides secreted by the papillar cells themselves (sRALF1, 22, 23, 33) and the cell wall proteins LRX3–5 together with them form a "lock" that keeps the reactive oxygen species level of the papillar cell high and prevents pollen tubes from penetrating the stigma. Pollen of the same species and of close relatives carries another set of RALF peptides (pRALF10, 11, 12, 13, 25, 26, 30), which compete with the stigma sRALFs for the receptors and are the "key" that opens the "lock": after pollen lands, the key lowers reactive oxygen species at the contact site so that the pollen tube can penetrate. Pollen of distant species has no key that can be recognized and is kept outside the stigma (Lan et al., 2023, *Science*). This mechanism explains why the stigma is the first barrier to interspecific hybridization.

Germination and pollen tube elongation also show a "population effect": the denser the pollen on the stigma or in the medium, the higher the germination percentage and the faster the pollen tubes grow. One reason is that Ca²⁺ in the pollen wall is released into the surroundings and used for pollen germination and pollen tube growth (Brewbaker and Kwack, 1963: adding Ca²⁺ and boric acid to the medium for in vitro germination eliminates the population effect); another is that the more pollen there is, the more auxin and other substances are released. Boron markedly promotes pollen germination and pollen tube elongation, and with boron deficiency rapeseed "flowers without setting seed" and wheat "heads without setting grain" ([7.13.4](/7.13.4)).

## 23.4. Self-Incompatibility

### 23.4.1. Types

The types of **self-incompatibility**, representative plants of each type and the recognition molecules have already been compared in a table in [7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.2.3), which also shows the genetic logic of the gametophytic and sporophytic types. Here we add the definitions of the two main kinds of homomorphic self-incompatibility, their quantitative genetic consequences and their molecular mechanisms.

**Definition 23.4.1 (Gametophytic and Sporophytic Self-Incompatibility)** Self-incompatibility in which the incompatibility phenotype of the pollen is determined by the S allele of the pollen itself (the haploid male gametophyte) is called **gametophytic self-incompatibility** (GSI); that in which the phenotype of the pollen is determined jointly by the two S alleles of the parent plant that produced the pollen (the diploid sporophyte) is called **sporophytic self-incompatibility** (SSI).

Both systems are controlled by the same multiallelic S locus, which is in fact a group of tightly linked, nonrecombining genes (an S haplotype), at least one of which is expressed in the pistil and one in the pollen. Comparison of the two:

| Property | Gametophytic | Sporophytic |
|---|---|---|
| Phenotype of the pollen | One S allele of the pollen itself | The two S alleles of the parent (dominance relations possible) |
| Site of inhibition | In the style (the pollen tube stops midway or bursts); at the stigma in the poppy type | Stigma surface (pollen does not hydrate, does not germinate, or forms a very short tube) |
| Pollen and stigma | Often bicellular pollen and wet stigma (Solanaceae, Rosaceae, Plantaginaceae, Liliaceae); tricellular pollen in grasses | Often tricellular pollen and dry stigma (Brassicaceae, Asteraceae, Convolvulaceae) |
| Cross S₁S₂ ♀ × S₁S₃ ♂ | S₃ pollen compatible, S₁ pollen incompatible: half of the pollen can fertilize | With codominance all pollen is incompatible; if S₃ is dominant over S₁ in the pollen, all is compatible |
| Possible homozygotes | No offspring homozygous at the S locus can be produced | Recessive alleles can form homozygotes |

The quantitative consequence of GSI can be illustrated by a simple calculation. Suppose a population has $n$ S alleles of equal frequency. A random pollen grain lands on the stigma of a random plant; the probability that its allele is the same as one of the two alleles of the pistil, and that it is rejected, is $2/n$. For $n = 3$ more than half of the pollen is rejected; but natural populations often have dozens of S alleles (about 30–40 in some Solanaceae), and for $n = 30$ only about 7% of the pollen is rejected. A rare S allele has a small probability of being rejected and leaves offspring more easily; this negative frequency-dependent selection keeps extremely high allelic diversity at the S locus, and S alleles can be older than species (trans-species polymorphism, [7.10.14](/7.10.14)).

### 23.4.2. Molecular Mechanisms

First consider S-RNase-type gametophytic self-incompatibility in Solanaceae, Rosaceae and Plantaginaceae. The S determinant of the pistil is S-RNase secreted by the transmitting tissue of the style, a glycoprotein ribonuclease of about 30 kDa; the S determinant of the pollen is a group of F-box proteins, SLF (S-locus F-box, SFB in *Prunus*). All S-RNases enter all pollen tubes, so recognition takes place in the cytoplasm of the pollen tube. In Solanaceae each S haplotype carries a dozen or so different *SLF* genes, each SLF recognizes a part of the "nonself" S-RNases, and all the SLFs together can recognize all S-RNases except the self one. A recognized nonself S-RNase is tagged by the SCF^SLF^ ubiquitin ligase and sent to the 26S **proteasome** for degradation (or sequestered in the vacuole), and the pollen tube grows normally; the self S-RNase is not recognized, remains active, degrades the rRNA of the pollen tube and the pollen tube stops growing (Figure 1A). This "collaborative nonself recognition" (Kubo et al., 2010) shows that what is actually "recognized" in self-incompatibility is nonself, and "self" is rejected because it is not recognized.

Sporophytic self-incompatibility in the Brassicaceae is determined by SCR and SRK. The S determinant of the pollen is a small cysteine-rich peptide, SCR (S-locus cysteine-rich protein, also called SP11), synthesized in the tapetum of the anther and deposited in the pollenkitt, so that each pollen grain carries the SCR of both S haplotypes of the parent; the S determinant of the pistil is the S-locus receptor kinase SRK on the plasma membrane of stigma papillar cells. When pollen lands on the stigma, SCR binds the SRK of the same haplotype and causes autophosphorylation of the receptor; with the participation of the membrane-anchored kinase MLPK the E3 ubiquitin ligase ARC1 is activated; ARC1 causes degradation of proteins that promote compatibility, such as Exo70A1, the papillar cell stops secreting water and other substances toward the pollen contact site, and the pollen cannot hydrate or germinate (Figure 1B). Dominance relations are implemented by small RNAs in the anther: small RNAs produced by a dominant haplotype methylate the *SCR* promoter of the recessive haplotype and silence it (Tarutani et al., 2010).

The mechanism of gametophytic self-incompatibility in poppy is entirely different: when PrsS, a small protein secreted by the stigma, matches the pollen transmembrane protein PrpS, it causes massive Ca²⁺ influx into the pollen tube, an increase in reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide and depolymerization of the actin cytoskeleton, and finally **programmed cell death** of the self pollen tube ([7.13.24](/7.13.24)).

### 23.4.3. Overcoming Self-Incompatibility

In breeding it is often necessary to overcome self-incompatibility in order to obtain inbred lines or make distant crosses:

| Method | Principle | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Bud pollination | The stigma 2–4 d before flowering does not yet express enough SRK (or there is little S-RNase in the style) | Propagation of self-incompatible lines of cabbage and Chinese cabbage |
| Raising $\ce{CO2}$ concentration | Treating pollinated flowers with 3%–5% $\ce{CO2}$ inhibits the incompatibility reaction of the stigma | Brassica vegetables |
| Salt water or chemical treatment | Spraying NaCl solution and the like interferes with recognition at the stigma surface | Brassicaceae |
| Mixed (mentor) pollination | Compatible pollen killed with methanol, repeated freezing or radiation but retaining recognition proteins is mixed into the incompatible pollen so that the stigma cannot recognize the incompatible pollen | Distant crosses of poplar and apple |
| Cutting off the stigma, shortening the style, intraovarian pollination | Bypass the site where recognition occurs | Lily, tobacco |
| Bridge cross | First cross the distant parent with a more closely related species, then cross the hybrid with the other parent | Wheat and wheatgrass (*Elytrigia*), etc. |
| Physical and chemical methods | Temperature change (heat treatment of the style), radiation, growth regulators | Many plants |

In application, self-incompatible lines can replace male-sterile lines in the production of hybrid seed (cabbage, Chinese cabbage); in self-incompatible fruit trees such as apple, pear and cherry, orchards must include compatible pollinizer cultivars and rely on bees for pollination.

## 23.5. Growth and Guidance of the Pollen Tube

### 23.5.1. Tip Growth

After the pollen is hydrated, Ca²⁺ enters the vegetative cell, and the cytoskeleton and secretory vesicles gather below the aperture from which germination will occur and the cell becomes polarized; the intine then protrudes from the aperture, forming the pollen tube. The pollen tube is a typical tip-growing cell: new plasma membrane and wall material are added only in a region a few micrometers in size at the tube tip, and the rest of the tube no longer elongates. Pollen tube growth is very fast: lily pollen tubes elongate in vitro at about $0.1$–$0.4\ \mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and maize pollen tubes can elongate about 1 cm per hour in the silk and reach 40 cm (the length of the maize silk); by comparison, tip growth of root hairs is only a few tens of nanometers per second. Pollen tubes of angiosperms are about 1000 times faster than those of gymnosperms ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.4.2)).

The structure of the pollen tube serves this rapid polar growth:

- The tube tip is a "clear zone" filled with secretory vesicles from the Golgi apparatus, carrying materials for the synthesis of pectin and cellulose and new membrane; large organelles are excluded, and cytoplasmic streaming behind it drives the transport of materials to the tip.
- The cytoplasm, vegetative nucleus and sperm cells (or generative cell) are concentrated at the front, the rear is occupied by a large vacuole, and at intervals a **callose** plug forms that seals off the already empty part, so that the front keeps turgor (about 0.1–0.4 MPa).
- The wall at the tube tip is mainly highly methyl-esterified pectin, which is soft and extensible; a little farther back, pectin methylesterase removes the methyl esters, the carboxyl groups are cross-linked by Ca²⁺ and the wall stiffens. Only the tube tip can thus expand under turgor, and the mechanics of the wall are the same as those of expansion of ordinary cells ([7.13.17](/7.13.17)).

The pollen tube tip has pronounced ion gradients (Figure 2A): cytosolic free Ca²⁺ is as high as 3–10 μM at the very tip and falls to a basal level of 0.2–0.3 μM within about 20 μm of the tip; cytosolic pH is slightly acidic at the tip (about 6.8), with an alkaline band (about 7.5) at the base of the clear zone. The Ca²⁺ gradient is maintained by influx through Ca²⁺ channels in the plasma membrane at the tip (such as the CNGC type) and efflux farther back; it promotes fusion of secretory vesicles with the plasma membrane and regulates actin assembly.

Growth is not uniform: in many pollen tubes the growth rate oscillates with a period of ten to a few tens of seconds, and Ca²⁺ and pH at the tip oscillate with the same period but a different phase: the peak of growth rate comes first and the peak of intracellular Ca²⁺ lags by a few seconds (Holdaway-Clarke et al., 1997). This shows that the rise in Ca²⁺ is not the cause of growth but more like its result and a negative feedback signal. The core of the oscillation is now thought to be the small GTPase ROP1: ROP1 is activated at the plasma membrane of the tip (regulated by receptor-like kinases and GEFs) and promotes actin assembly, exocytosis and production of reactive oxygen species by NADPH oxidases (RBOHH/J), and reactive oxygen species in turn promote Ca²⁺ influx; Ca²⁺ accumulating at the tip in turn inhibits ROP1, for example by activating GAPs. This is a negative feedback loop with delay and can be described by two equations (Figure 2B):

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{1}{1 + (C/K)^h} - bR,
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}t} = s\,v(t - \tau) - dC,
\qquad v = v_0 + gR,
\tag{23.2}
$$

where $R$ is active ROP, $C$ is tip Ca²⁺, $v$ is the growth rate and $\tau$ is the delay with which growth opens Ca²⁺ channels (for example through membrane tension or newly inserted channels). When the negative feedback is strong enough ($h$ large) and the delay long enough, the steady state becomes unstable and the system shows **limit cycle** oscillation (Hopf bifurcation, [7.6.33](/7.6.33)); the peak of Ca²⁺ necessarily lags the peak of growth, in agreement with observation.

### 23.5.2. Guidance of the Pollen Tube

After passing through the stigma the pollen tube enters the style. In a solid style it grows in the extracellular matrix between the cells of the transmitting tract, in a hollow style along the secretion on the surface of the stylar canal; arabinogalactan proteins (such as TTS of tobacco) and proline-rich glycoproteins in the extracellular matrix provide attachment and nutrition for the pollen tube. The pollen tube travels in the pistil a distance of a few millimeters to tens of centimeters and finally enters the **micropyle** of the ovule accurately, which requires guidance signals.

**Definition 23.5.1 (Pollen Tube Guidance)** The process in which pistil tissues and the embryo sac guide the pollen tube by chemical signals from the stigma through the style and ovary to the ovule and into the embryo sac is called **pollen tube guidance**. Guidance by diploid sporophytic tissue in the style and ovary is called sporophytic guidance; guidance by the embryo sac (the female gametophyte) near the ovule is called gametophytic guidance.

Molecular signals of guidance:

- Sporophytic guidance: the TTS protein in the transmitting tissue of tobacco forms a gradient of glycosylation in the style; γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) in *Arabidopsis* has a concentration gradient between the style and the micropyle, and the *pop2* mutant has abnormal guidance because GABA degradation is blocked; SCA (a lipid transfer protein) secreted by the transmitting tissue of lily acts together with chemocyanin to promote attachment and directed growth of the pollen tube. Chemotropism of the pollen tube has traditionally been thought to be related to gradients of Ca²⁺ and auxin from the stigma to the placenta.
- Gametophytic guidance: laser ablation experiments in *Torenia* showed that the attractant comes from the **synergids**, and the defensin-like peptide LURE was identified ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.4.3)). In maize the small peptide ZmEA1 secreted by the egg apparatus acts in the last stage of pollen tube guidance (Márton et al., 2005). In *Arabidopsis* AtLURE1 secreted by the synergid cells is recognized by the receptor-like kinase PRK6 at the pollen tube tip (Takeuchi and Higashiyama, 2016, *Nature*), and another pair of receptors, MDIS1–MIK1, is also involved in recognition.

Surprisingly, after knocking out all the *AtLURE1* genes or their receptors in *Arabidopsis*, pollen tubes can still find the ovule, complete fertilization and produce normal seeds. The reason is that there is another set of attractant peptides without species specificity, XIUQIU: they attract the pollen tubes of *Arabidopsis* and also those of related species of the Brassicaceae. The role of AtLURE1 is to give the pollen tubes of the plant's own species an advantage in the competition to emerge from the septum and reach the funiculus, which promotes reproductive isolation from related species (Zhong et al., 2019, *Science*). Gametophytic guidance is therefore a layered system: general attractant signals ensure fertilization and species-specific signals provide selection among species.

## 23.6. Reception of the Pollen Tube and Signals of Double Fertilization

During growth the pollen tube must remain intact, and on reaching the embryo sac it must burst at the right place and release the sperm cells. These two opposite events are controlled by the same group of receptors:

- Maintaining integrity: the receptor-like kinases ANXUR1/2 (ANX) and BUPS1/2 on the plasma membrane of the pollen tube form heteromeric complexes that recognize the small peptides RALF4 and RALF19 secreted by the pollen tube itself and maintain the integrity and growth of the cell wall. In the *anx1 anx2* or *bups1 bups2* double mutants and the *ralf4 ralf19* double mutant the pollen tube bursts prematurely right after germination and the sperm cells cannot be delivered to the embryo sac (Ge et al., 2017, *Science*).
- Bursting at the embryo sac: another small peptide expressed in the ovule, RALF34, competes with RALF4/19 for the BUPS–ANX receptor complex; when the pollen tube reaches the embryo sac RALF34 replaces RALF4/19 and the pollen tube bursts. When RALF34 is added to pollen tubes growing in vitro, they burst within a very short time.
- Reception by the synergid: the receptor-like kinase FERONIA (of the same CrRLK1L family as ANX) on the filiform apparatus of the synergid, together with the glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored protein LORELEI and the MLO-type protein NORTIA, "receives" the pollen tube; FERONIA induces reactive oxygen species at the filiform apparatus and causes oscillations of Ca²⁺ in the synergid, and these signals are coordinated with bursting of the pollen tube and degeneration of the synergid. In *feronia* mutants the pollen tube does not burst after entering the embryo sac but continues to grow ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.4.3)). FERONIA is named after the goddess of fertility in ancient Roman mythology.
- Fusion of sperm and egg: HAP2/GCS1 on the surface of the sperm cell and the EC1 peptides secreted by the egg cell take part in recognition and fusion of the gametes ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.5.2)). After an ovule has received one pollen tube, FERONIA-dependent signals (demethylesterification of pectin at the filiform apparatus and accumulation of nitric oxide) prevent other pollen tubes from entering (polytubey block).

After fertilization the two products of **double fertilization** develop into the embryo and the **endosperm**, and the vegetative nucleus, the two synergids and the antipodal cells disappear.

## 23.7. Metabolic Changes in the Pistil after Fertilization

Pollination and fertilization cause drastic changes in the metabolism of the pistil:

1. The respiration rate increases. After fertilization the respiration rate of the pistil is several times higher than before, often with two rises: one when sperm and egg make contact and one when free-nuclear division of the endosperm is vigorous.
2. The ability to take up water and inorganic salts is enhanced, the contents of nitrogen and phosphorus increase markedly while their concentrations in the perianth fall; transpiration is sharply enhanced and the perianth withers.
3. Carbohydrate and protein metabolism accelerates.
4. The auxin content increases greatly. Pollen itself contains auxin, which diffuses into the pistil tissues after pollination, but the amount is limited; more important, pollen contains enzyme systems that convert tryptophan to indoleacetic acid, which cause large amounts of auxin to form in the style and ovary. The auxin peaks after pollination appear in turn at the top of the style, the base of the style and in the ovary, in step with the advance of the pollen tube.
5. Transport of nutrients to the reproductive organs is enhanced. Large amounts of auxin and gibberellin make the ovary a strong **sink** that "attracts" nutrients from the vegetative organs to the ovary ([7.13.11](/7.13.11)).

At the same time other parts of the flower begin to senesce. In many plants (petunia, carnation, orchid) ethylene is produced within a few hours after pollination and causes wilting and abscission of the petals ([7.13.24](/7.13.24)); unpollinated orchid flowers can stay open for weeks, whereas after pollination the perianth wilts and changes color within a day or two, a signal that likewise begins with growth of the pollen tube on the stigma and the start of ethylene synthesis.

## 23.8. Development and Maturation of the Seed

### 23.8.1. Stages of Seed Development

Seed development can be divided into three stages (Figure 3A; morphology in [7.12.30](/7.12.30#sec-30.4)):

1. Tissue differentiation (embryogenesis): from fertilization to the preliminary establishment of the embryo form; embryo and endosperm cells divide rapidly, the water content of the seed is very high and dry weight increases little.
2. Reserve accumulation (seed filling): the embryo, endosperm or cotyledons grow rapidly, assimilates are imported from the mother plant in large amounts, starch, storage proteins and oils are synthesized and dry weight increases rapidly. In cereals this stage passes in turn through the milk-ripe and dough-ripe stages.
3. Maturation drying: the vascular connection between the seed and the mother plant is cut off, water content falls rapidly to 10%–15%, metabolism drops to a very low level and the embryo enters quiescence or **seed dormancy** ([7.13.20](/7.13.20)).

By the kind of reserve, seeds can be divided into starch seeds (wheat, maize, rice, pea, faba bean, kidney bean), oil seeds (soybean, peanut, rapeseed, sunflower, castor bean) and protein seeds (legumes such as soybean, some nuts). The synthetic pathways and storage sites of the reserves differ among seed types (amyloplasts, oil bodies, protein storage vacuoles), but the process of accumulation is controlled in all of them jointly by assimilate supply, sink strength and hormones.

### 23.8.2. Accumulation of Reserves

- Carbohydrates: as starch seeds mature, soluble sugar content gradually decreases and starch content rises. In the rice caryopsis the early rates of accumulation of soluble sugar and starch are close; soluble sugar peaks about 9 d after flowering, after which soluble sugar is converted to starch and decreases, and starch accumulation essentially stops after the milk-ripe stage.
- Nitrogenous substances: nonprotein nitrogen (amino acids, amides) gradually decreases and protein nitrogen increases, with little change in total nitrogen: imported amino acids and amides are synthesized into storage proteins.
- Lipids: as oil seeds mature, fat content rises and starch and soluble sugar contents gradually fall, which shows that fat is converted from carbohydrate ([7.13.12](/7.13.12)). Oil formation has two characteristics: more free fatty acids are formed early, and triacylglycerols are gradually synthesized later so that the acid value falls; saturated fatty acids are synthesized first and later converted by desaturases into unsaturated fatty acids, so that the iodine value rises. The acid value is the number of milligrams of KOH needed to neutralize the free fatty acids in 1 g of oil, and the iodine value is the number of grams of iodine absorbed by 100 g of oil (reflecting the degree of unsaturation).
- Legume seeds first accumulate sugars, mainly sucrose, which are then converted to protein and starch; in the later stage starch accumulation decreases and protein remains at a high level.

The respiration rate of the seed shows a single-peaked curve during development: when organic matter accumulates rapidly respiration is also vigorous (strongest at the milk-ripe stage in cereals) and gradually decreases near maturity.

Grain filling can be described quantitatively. The change of dry weight of a cereal grain with the number of days $t$ after flowering can be described by the logistic equation ([7.11.8](/7.11.8)):

$$
W(t) = \frac{W_{\max}}{1 + \mathrm{e}^{-k(t - t_{\mathrm{m}})}},
\qquad
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{\max} = \frac{kW_{\max}}{4},
\tag{23.3}
$$

The maximum grain-filling rate occurs at $t = t_{\mathrm{m}}$, when dry weight reaches half. Dividing the final grain weight by the maximum filling rate defines the "effective filling period" $W_{\max}/(kW_{\max}/4) = 4/k$. Grain weight is determined jointly by the filling rate and the duration of filling.

**Example 23.8.1** A wheat cultivar has grain weight $W_{\max} = 45\ \mathrm{mg}$, $k = 0.22\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$ and $t_{\mathrm{m}} = 20\ \mathrm{d}$. Find the maximum filling rate and the effective filling period; if a dry-hot wind after flowering forces filling to stop on day 26, what is the grain weight?

Maximum filling rate $= 0.22 \times 45/4 = 2.5\ \mathrm{mg\,d^{-1}}$, effective filling period $= 4/0.22 = 18\ \mathrm{d}$. Grain weight on day 26 is $W = 45/(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-0.22 \times 6}) = 45/(1 + 0.267) = 35.5\ \mathrm{mg}$, about 21% less than normal, and the thousand-grain weight falls from 45 g to about 36 g. This is the quantitative meaning of "wind and drought leave grain unfilled" and of yield loss from dry-hot wind.

### 23.8.3. Hormone Dynamics

The peaks of the various endogenous hormones appear in sequence during seed development (Figure 3B):

- Cytokinin: very low before fertilization, highest at the end of fertilization and then decreasing. It promotes division of the primary endosperm nucleus and the zygote, enlarges the storage space in the seed and takes part in establishing sink strength.
- Gibberellin: there is a small peak from heading to before fertilization (possibly related to heading), a rapid increase after fertilization and then a decrease.
- Auxin: increases slightly at fertilization, then decreases, increases again when the grain grows rapidly and peaks before harvest. Gibberellin and auxin regulate the transport and accumulation of assimilates in the grain; the period from one week after fertilization to one week before harvest is when the seed grows fastest and accumulates dry matter fastest.
- **Abscisic acid**: increases greatly in the maturation stage. It promotes synthesis of storage proteins and LEA proteins, induces desiccation tolerance and primary dormancy, and inhibits precocious germination of the embryo. In many seeds abscisic acid has two sources: early mainly from maternal tissue and later synthesized by the embryo itself.

### 23.8.4. Desiccation Tolerance

The water content of the mature seed is the opposite of accumulation of organic matter and gradually decreases with maturation, by which the seed acquires the ability to germinate and desiccation tolerance. Most seeds lose more than 90% of their water at maturity, and the water content can be as low as 0.1 g of water per gram of dry weight, corresponding to a water potential below $-100\ \mathrm{MPa}$; under such conditions the protoplasm changes from a sol to a gel state, the cytoplasm enters a glassy state of extremely high viscosity, metabolism almost stops, and biological macromolecules and membranes are "fixed" without damage. Seeds that can tolerate desiccation are called orthodox seeds; those that do not dry and lose viability soon after leaving the mother plant are recalcitrant seeds (litchi, longan, mango, chestnut, cacao; [7.12.19](/7.12.19)).

Desiccation tolerance is acquired in the late maturation stage and depends on several classes of protective substances:

- LEA proteins (late embryogenesis abundant proteins): first found in the late maturation stage of cotton seeds (Dure et al., early 1980s), highly hydrophilic, mostly without a fixed structure in aqueous solution; on desiccation they form helices and bind membranes and other proteins, acting as molecular shields, ion chelators and water replacement. In recalcitrant seeds the seed-specific LEA proteins are missing or scarce.
- Nonreducing sugars: oligosaccharides such as sucrose, raffinose and stachyose accumulate, promote formation of the glassy state together with LEA proteins, and replace water molecules in forming hydrogen bonds with the headgroups of membrane phospholipids and with proteins during desiccation.
- Heat-shock proteins, antioxidant systems and preparation for DNA repair.

These processes are controlled by a group of transcription factors, LEC1, LEC2, FUS3 and ABI3 (the "LAFL" network), and ABI5, which under the action of abscisic acid activate genes for storage proteins, LEA proteins and desiccation tolerance. Seeds of strong *abi3* mutants of *Arabidopsis* cannot dry, remain green at maturity and die quickly after leaving the mother plant; the maize *vp1* (a homolog of *ABI3*) and abscisic-acid-deficient mutants germinate precociously on the ear (vivipary). Preharvest sprouting of cereals when rain falls at maturity is also a result of interruption of the desiccation and dormancy programs ([7.13.20](/7.13.20)).

A: dry weight (as a percentage of final dry weight, logistic curve) and water content (as a percentage of fresh weight, from about 80% to about 12% at harvest); the three stages are tissue differentiation, reserve accumulation and maturation drying. B: sequential peaks of endogenous hormones: cytokinin (end of fertilization and endosperm cell division), gibberellin and auxin (rapid grain filling), abscisic acid (maturation, desiccation tolerance and dormancy)](./plots/seed-maturation.py)

| Condition | Effect |
|---|---|
| Water | Water deficit hinders transport of photosynthate to the grain, filling is insufficient and grains are small and shriveled; dryness and hot wind (dry-hot wind) enhance hydrolase activity and hinder accumulation of reserves, causing unfilled grain even when water supply is restored. ## 23.9. Fruit Set and Fruit Growth

### 23.9.1. Fruit Set

**Definition 23.9.1 (Fruit Set)** The process in which, after pollination and fertilization (or another stimulus), the ovary no longer abscises but begins sustained growth and develops into a young fruit is called **fruit set**.

Pollination and fertilization are the main signals for fruit set: after fertilization the developing seeds produce auxin and gibberellin, which switch the ovary from "quiescence" to growth ([7.13.13](/7.13.13#sec-13.5.5)). In tomato, in the unpollinated ovary the Aux/IAA protein IAA9 and the transcription factor ARF8, together with DELLA, the repressor of gibberellin signaling, keep the ovary quiescent; after pollination auxin and gibberellin release these "brakes." Reduced function of *IAA9* or *ARF8* and mutation of DELLA (*procera*) all make tomato set fruit without pollination, forming fruit by parthenocarpy. Exogenous auxin or gibberellin can likewise induce **parthenocarpy** (seedless tomato, seedless grape, [7.12.31](/7.12.31#sec-31.1.3)).

Not every flower can set fruit. Fruit trees and many crops produce many more flowers than fruits that finally mature, and unfertilized flowers and some young fruits abscise within a few weeks after flowering ("physiological fruit drop" of apple and citrus, such as June drop), by which the plant regulates the number of fruits according to its capacity to supply assimilates ([7.13.24](/7.13.24)).

### 23.9.2. Fruit Growth Curves

Fruit growth is first dominated by cell division and later by cell expansion. Cell division in tomato fruit is essentially complete within one or two weeks of flowering, after which enlargement of the fruit is almost entirely by cell expansion; pericarp cells often undergo several rounds of endoreduplication, reaching ploidy of hundreds of times, and cell volume increases accordingly. The change of fruit volume or fresh weight with time takes two typical forms (Figure 4):

- Single sigmoid curve (slow–fast–slow): most fleshy fruits such as apple, pear, tomato, pineapple, strawberry and banana. It can be described by a logistic equation (Eq. 23.3).
- Double sigmoid curve (slow–fast–slow–fast–slow): stone fruits such as peach, apricot, plum, cherry and olive, and also grape, fig, blueberry and others. Growth has three stages: in stage I the fruit enlarges rapidly (cell division and expansion); in stage II enlargement of the flesh almost stops, the endocarp lignifies to form the "stone" and the embryo grows rapidly (stage II of grape corresponds to seed development); in stage III the flesh cells expand rapidly and the fruit enlarges rapidly a second time until maturity.

The double sigmoid curve can be written as the sum of two logistic equations:

$$
W(t) = \frac{W_1}{1 + \mathrm{e}^{-k_1(t - t_1)}} + \frac{W_2}{1 + \mathrm{e}^{-k_2(t - t_2)}}.
\tag{23.4}
$$

**Example 23.9.1** A peach cultivar has $W_1 = 50\ \mathrm{g}$, $k_1 = 0.15\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$, $t_1 = 25\ \mathrm{d}$; $W_2 = 110\ \mathrm{g}$, $k_2 = 0.16\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$, $t_2 = 95\ \mathrm{d}$. Find the maximum growth rates of the two rapid growth periods and the final fruit weight.

The two stages are far apart, and the maximum rate of each is approximately $k_iW_i/4$: stage I $0.15 \times 50/4 = 1.9\ \mathrm{g\,d^{-1}}$ (25 d after flowering), stage III $0.16 \times 110/4 = 4.4\ \mathrm{g\,d^{-1}}$ (95 d after flowering); the growth rate in stage II (about 50–70 d after flowering) is only about $0.1$–$0.3\ \mathrm{g\,d^{-1}}$. The final fruit weight is about $50 + 110 = 160\ \mathrm{g}$. Stage III contributes most to fruit weight; the fruit is then a very strong sink, and fruit thinning, an adequate leaf-to-fruit ratio and water supply are most critical for yield; when growth stops in stage II the fruit is still accumulating energy and building the endocarp, and drought at this time mainly affects the stone.

## 23.10. Fruit Ripening

*Ripening* is the series of changes from completion of development to edibility: color, texture, flavor and aroma all change, making the fruit attractive to animals that disperse the seeds. Ripening is followed by senescence, in which the fruit softens and rots. The discussion below is mainly of fleshy fruits.

### 23.10.1. The Climacteric

In many fruits the respiration rate suddenly rises at the onset of ripening. Kidd and West (1920s) first described this phenomenon in studies of apple storage.

**Definition 23.10.1 (Climacteric, Climacteric Fruit and Non-Climacteric Fruit)** The phenomenon in which, at a certain stage of ripening, the respiration rate first falls to a minimum, then suddenly rises, and falls again after reaching a peak is called the **climacteric**. Fruits that show a climacteric during ripening, accompanied by a peak of ethylene release, are called **climacteric fruits**, such as apple, pear, banana, tomato, peach, apricot, plum, mango, papaya, avocado and kiwifruit; fruits without an evident respiratory climacteric and ethylene peak during ripening are called **non-climacteric fruits**, such as citrus, grape, strawberry, pineapple, cherry, litchi and cucumber.

Climacteric fruits mostly contain complex reserves (starch) that are strongly hydrolyzed after harvest, respiration increases and ripening is fairly rapid, so they can "ripen after harvest"; ripening of non-climacteric fruits is slower and most cannot improve markedly in quality after harvest, so they must be harvested when fully ripe. The distinction is not absolute: melon can be climacteric or non-climacteric depending on cultivar; although citrus is non-climacteric, exogenous ethylene can still degreen the peel and turn it yellow.

**Ethylene** is the substance that triggers the climacteric. In climacteric fruits internal ethylene concentration rises rapidly from below $0.1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ to several to several hundred $\mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ before or at the same time as the onset of the climacteric; treating climacteric fruits that have reached ripening competence with ethylene advances the climacteric and ripening and induces the fruit to produce large amounts of ethylene itself. The response of non-climacteric fruits to ethylene is that respiration increases gradually with ethylene concentration and recovers after the ethylene is removed, but endogenous ethylene production is not induced and ripening is not triggered. Part of the respiratory increase during the climacteric comes from enhancement of the alternative oxidase pathway ([7.13.12](/7.13.12)), and the respiratory quotient also changes.

### 23.10.2. System 1 and System 2 Ethylene

Climacteric fruits have two ethylene synthesis systems:

- System 1: in unripe fruit (and vegetative tissues) a small amount of basal ethylene is produced; it is subject to negative feedback inhibition by ethylene (autoinhibition), so unripe fruit responds little to ethylene treatment. In tomato it is carried out by ACC synthase genes such as *ACS1A* and *ACS6*.
- System 2: starts at the onset of ripening, ethylene induces its own synthesis (autocatalysis) and large amounts of ethylene are produced. In tomato it is carried out by *ACS2* and *ACS4* and the ACC oxidases *ACO1* and *ACO4*, whose expression is induced by ethylene.

The positive feedback of system 2 ensures that once ripening has begun, the whole fruit (and other fruits at the same place) completes it rapidly and synchronously; this is the physiological basis of "one bad apple spoils the barrel." In the simplest model ([7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.2.2)):

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_0 + k_1\frac{E^n}{K^n + E^n} - k_2E,
\tag{23.5}
$$

$E$ is the level of internal ethylene (or ethylene signal), $k_0$ is the basal synthesis of system 1, the second term is the autocatalysis of system 2 (the Hill coefficient $n$ reflects the cooperativity of induction of ACS and ACO by ethylene) and $k_2E$ is the loss by diffusion and metabolism. When autocatalysis is strong enough ($k_1/(Kk_2)$ large enough, $n > 1$), over a certain range of $k_0$ the equation has three steady states: two stable states, low ethylene (unripe) and high ethylene (ripe), and an unstable state between them; the system is **bistable**. As the fruit develops, $k_0$ (and the sensitivity to ethylene) gradually increases, and when $k_0$ crosses the **saddle-node bifurcation** point the low steady state disappears and ethylene jumps to the high steady state (Figure 5). If the other bifurcation point lies in the region $k_0 < 0$, the system cannot return to the low steady state even when the triggering stimulus is removed: ripening is irreversible, which is the extreme case of **hysteresis**.

This model explains several phenomena in practice:

- Unripe climacteric fruit responds little to ethylene treatment (it has not yet entered the bistable region, or the negative feedback of system 1 dominates), and ripening competence increases with fruit age.
- The ethylene action inhibitor 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) binds the ethylene receptors irreversibly and blocks autocatalysis (equivalent to $k_1 \to 0$); treatment before the onset of the climacteric can delay ripening for a long time (dashed line in Figure 5A); tomato with antisense suppression of *ACS2* does not ripen in air and ripens normally when exogenous ethylene is supplied (Oeller et al., 1991).
- Removal of ethylene (ventilation, absorption by potassium permanganate) and low-temperature and low-oxygen storage all delay the climacteric by lowering $k_0$ or the activity of system 2 ([7.13.12](/7.13.12#sec-12.12), [7.13.24](/7.13.24)).

A: time course after harvest: the basal synthesis $k_0$ of system 1 increases slowly as the fruit ripens, after it crosses a threshold system 2 starts and ethylene rises rapidly, and respiration shows a climacteric peak accordingly (falling later because of limitation by substrate consumption); non-climacteric fruit ($k_1 = 0$) shows no ethylene or respiration peak; blocking the receptors with 1-MCP on day 2 prevents the respiratory climacteric. ### 23.10.3. Transcriptional Control of Ripening

Several spontaneous single-gene mutations in tomato prevent normal ripening of the fruit: *ripening inhibitor* (*rin*), *non-ripening* (*nor*) and *Colorless non-ripening* (*Cnr*). *RIN* encodes a SEPALLATA-type MADS-box transcription factor that is induced at the onset of ripening and binds directly to the promoters of ripening-related genes such as *ACS2*, *ACS4*, *PSY1* (phytoene synthase) and polygalacturonase; *NOR* encodes a NAC-type transcription factor; *CNR* encodes an SBP-box transcription factor, and the *Cnr* mutation is not a change in DNA sequence but spontaneous hypermethylation of its promoter region (an epiallele, Manning et al., 2006). During ripening many loci in tomato fruit undergo DNA demethylation, and fruit lacking the demethylase DML2 cannot ripen normally, showing that epigenetic changes are also part of the ripening program.

The understanding of *rin* has been revised in recent years: the *rin* mutation produces a fusion protein of RIN and the neighboring gene *MC*, which can bind DNA but cannot activate transcription and is a gain-of-function repressor; fruit in which *RIN* is completely knocked out by genome editing can still ripen partially (Ito et al., 2017). The *nor* mutation is similar and produces a truncated, dominant-negative NOR protein. RIN and NOR are thus important for ripening but are not the "master switches" once thought. The tomato ripening network also includes transcription factors such as AP2a, FUL1/2 and TAGL1, of which TAGL1 is homologous to *SHATTERPROOF*, which controls silique dehiscence in *Arabidopsis*: the same class of MADS-box genes controls dehiscence of dry fruit and ripening of fleshy fruit, which shows that the two are related in evolution ([7.12.31](/7.12.31)).

In non-climacteric fruits **abscisic acid** plays a more important role: abscisic acid content rises at the onset of ripening in strawberry and grape, exogenous abscisic acid promotes ripening, and strawberry in which abscisic acid synthesis is inhibited (for example by silencing *FaNCED1*) does not color normally. In climacteric fruits abscisic acid accumulates before ethylene rises and may act as the primary initiating signal. The overall change in hormones during fruit ripening is that young fruit has high contents of growth-promoting hormones (auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin); at ripening ethylene increases in climacteric fruits and abscisic acid increases in non-climacteric fruits.

### 23.10.4. Changes in Color, Aroma, Flavor and Texture

Most of the organic matter in ripening fruit comes from assimilates imported from the vegetative organs, and part is made by the chloroplasts in the pericarp itself. The main changes are:

| Change | Process | Examples and notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sweetening | Starch is hydrolyzed to soluble sugars (sucrose, glucose, fructose), which accumulate in the vacuole | The starch of banana falls during the climacteric from about 20% of the fresh weight of the pulp to 1%–2%; apple, banana and mango become sweeter after harvest. Grape and citrus store no starch and sugar is imported from the plant during ripening, so they do not become sweeter after harvest |
| Reduced sourness | Part of the organic acids in the vacuole (citric acid in citrus, malic acid in apple, tartaric acid in grape) is converted to sugar, part is oxidized by respiration and part combines with K⁺ and Ca²⁺ to form salts | The sugar/acid ratio determines flavor |
| Loss of astringency | Tannins (condensed tannins) are oxidized or polymerized into water-insoluble macromolecules that no longer bind proteins in the mouth | Astringent persimmon is "deastringed" with warm water, alcohol or high $\ce{CO2}$: acetaldehyde produced by anaerobic respiration polymerizes tannins |
| Aroma formation | Synthesis and release of volatile substances: esters, aldehydes, terpenes, apocarotenoids and others | The characteristic aroma of banana is esters such as isoamyl acetate; the peel oil of citrus contains limonene and citral; the main flavor volatiles of tomato come from hexanal and *cis*-3-hexenal produced from fatty acids by the lipoxygenase pathway, together with amino-acid-derived 2-phenylethanol and methyl salicylate, and β-damascenone from cleavage of carotenoids |
| Softening | Degradation and remodeling of the cell wall: pectin of the **middle lamella** dissolves, and pectin methylesterase, polygalacturonase (PG), pectate lyase, β-galactosidase, xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase (XTH) and **expansins** loosen the wall and separate cells; starch grains disappear; cell turgor also falls | In the "Flavr Savr" tomato (marketed in 1994) with antisense suppression of PG, PG activity fell below 1% but softening slowed only slightly, showing that PG is not the main factor in softening; tomato with silenced pectate lyase softens markedly more slowly (Uluisik et al., 2016) |
| Color change | Chlorophyll degrades and the existing carotenoids are revealed, or new carotenoids (lycopene in tomato, capsanthin in pepper) and anthocyanins (apple, grape, strawberry) are synthesized | Strong light, ample oxygen, higher temperature and a large diurnal temperature range favor anthocyanin synthesis and sugar accumulation, so the sun-facing side of a fruit has better color and is sweeter; chloroplasts change into chromoplasts |

Artificial ripening methods used in production include raising temperature and oxygen concentration, applying ethephon or ethylene gas (ripening of banana, tomato and persimmon), and soaking in warm water or smoking (acting mainly through the ethylene in the smoke). Storage and preservation of fruit after harvest are described in the next chapter ([7.13.24](/7.13.24)).

## Questions

1. The germination percentage of pollen of a cultivar is 50% 2 h after shedding and 12.5% after 6 h. Assuming that viability decays according to Eq. (23.1), find the germination percentage at shedding and the time for viability to halve; if artificial pollination requires a germination percentage of at least 30%, how long at most can the pollen be kept?

   **Answer** From $V(2)/V(6) = 4 = \mathrm{e}^{4\lambda}$, $\lambda = \ln 4/4 = 0.347\ \mathrm{h^{-1}}$, $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/\lambda = 2.0\ \mathrm{h}$. $V_0 = 50\,\mathrm{e}^{2\lambda} = 50 \times 2 = 100\%$. The time for the germination percentage to fall to 30% is $t = \ln(100/30)/\lambda = 1.20/0.347 = 3.5\ \mathrm{h}$, so the pollen can be kept for about 3.5 h at most. Actual decay often shows a plateau first and then accelerated decline, so extrapolation with the exponential form is only approximate.
2. In a natural population a plant has gametophytic self-incompatibility with 20 S alleles of equal frequency. (a) What is the probability that a random pollen grain landing on the stigma of a random plant is rejected? (b) In reciprocal crosses of an S₁S₂ plant and an S₂S₃ plant, what proportion of the pollen can fertilize? What genotypes occur among the offspring? (c) If this were sporophytic and all alleles codominant, what would the results of (b) be?

   **Answer** (a) $2/n = 2/20 = 10\%$. (b) S₁S₂ ♀ × S₂S₃ ♂: S₂ pollen is rejected and S₃ pollen is compatible, half of the pollen can fertilize, and the offspring are half S₁S₃ and half S₂S₃; in the reciprocal cross S₂S₃ ♀ × S₁S₂ ♂: S₂ pollen is rejected and S₁ pollen is compatible, and the offspring are half S₁S₂ and half S₁S₃. No S₂S₂ homozygotes arise. (c) With sporophytic control and codominance, every pollen grain of an S₂S₃ (or S₁S₂) plant carries the product of S₂, which is the same as S₂ of the pistil, so all pollen is rejected and neither cross sets seed.

3. Using Eq. (23.5), explain the following phenomena: (a) unripe tomato treated with ethylene does not ripen immediately; (b) one ripening apple accelerates ripening of other apples in the same box; (c) apples treated with 1-MCP before the onset of the climacteric can be stored for a long time, whereas treatment after the climacteric peak is much less effective.

   **Answer** (a) In unripe fruit ethylene synthesis is mainly system 1 and ethylene feeds back negatively on its own synthesis; the autocatalysis of system 2 ($k_1$) and the sensitivity to ethylene are still very low, there is no bistability in the model, and after a brief exogenous ethylene treatment is removed the system returns to the low steady state; once the fruit has reached ripening competence, $k_1$ increases, the system enters the bistable region and ethylene can push it over the threshold. (b) Ethylene released by the ripe apple diffuses to its surroundings, which is equivalent to increasing $k_0$ of the other apples (an external input), so that they cross the saddle-node bifurcation point and enter the high-ethylene state, system 2 is started and ripening occurs synchronously. (c) 1-MCP blocks the receptors, equivalent to $k_1 \to 0$; when treated before the climacteric the system stays in the low steady state; after the climacteric, ripening-related genes have already been strongly expressed, processes such as cell wall degradation have already started, and newly synthesized receptors gradually recover their response to ethylene, so blocking autocatalysis can no longer reverse the ripening changes that have occurred.

4. With the parameters of Example 23.8.1, calculate the filling rate on day 15 and day 30 after flowering. If photosynthesis is hindered during filling so that $k$ is unchanged and $W_{\max}$ falls to 38 mg, by how much is the thousand-grain weight reduced? Discuss from which two aspects grain weight should be raised.

   **Answer** $\mathrm{d}W/\mathrm{d}t = kW(1 - W/W_{\max})$. Day 15: $W = 45/(1 + \mathrm{e}^{1.1}) = 45/4.00 = 11.2\ \mathrm{mg}$, rate $= 0.22 \times 11.2 \times (1 - 0.25) = 1.85\ \mathrm{mg\,d^{-1}}$; day 30: $W = 45/(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-2.2}) = 40.5\ \mathrm{mg}$, rate $= 0.22 \times 40.5 \times 0.100 = 0.89\ \mathrm{mg\,d^{-1}}$. When $W_{\max}$ falls to 38 mg the thousand-grain weight is reduced by 7 g (about 16%). Grain weight is determined jointly by the filling rate ($kW_{\max}/4$, depending on assimilate supply and the sink strength of the grain, such as endosperm cell number and starch synthase activity) and the duration of filling (about $4/k$, depending on the timing of leaf senescence, temperature and water), so one must both maintain the photosynthetic capacity and duration of functional leaves such as the flag leaf after flowering (avoiding premature senescence and dry-hot wind) and form a sufficiently large sink capacity around flowering.

5. Compare tip growth of the pollen tube with the expansion growth of ordinary cells, and explain why pollen tubes can grow as fast as more than a millimeter per hour and what the callose plugs do.

   **Answer** Expansion of ordinary cells occurs over the whole cell wall, wall loosening by acid growth, expansins and the like proceeds over a longer time, and the rate is limited by the yielding properties of the whole wall (Lockhart equation, [7.13.17](/7.13.17)). The pollen tube grows only in a region of a few micrometers at the tube tip: secretory vesicles continually deliver highly methyl-esterified, soft pectin and new membrane to the tip, and turgor only has to stretch this small patch of soft wall; the wall a little farther back stiffens through the action of pectin methylesterase and cross-linking by Ca²⁺ and no longer consumes turgor. The increase in volume and surface area is thus concentrated in a small region, and together with vigorous respiration (the respiration rate of pollen tubes is very high) and nutrients from the style the tube can elongate at more than 0.1 μm/s. The callose plugs seal off the already empty rear segments of the pollen tube, concentrating cytoplasm and turgor at the front, so that the whole ever-longer tube does not have to be filled with cytoplasm and maintain turgor; they also limit "dilution" of the cytoplasm as the tube lengthens.
