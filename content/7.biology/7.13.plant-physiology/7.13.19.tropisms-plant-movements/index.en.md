Plants live fixed in place, yet they are not motionless: stems bend toward light, roots burrow down into the soil, tendrils coil around supports, the leaflets of *Mimosa* fold at a touch, the leaves of many legumes droop at night, and the Venus flytrap closes in a tenth of a second. Plants have no muscles, and these movements have only two sources of power: unequal growth on the two sides of an organ, which is slow and irreversible, and changes in the turgor of specialized cells, which are rapid and reversible. This chapter classifies movements by their dependence on the direction of the stimulus: the direction of a tropic movement is determined by the direction of the stimulus, the direction of a nastic movement by the structure of the organ itself, and circadian movements are driven by an endogenous biological clock.

The core of tropic movements is the lateral redistribution of auxin (the Cholodny–Went hypothesis). For **polar auxin transport** and auxin signal transduction see [7.13.13](/7.13.13), for photoreceptor molecules see [7.13.16](/7.13.16) and [7.6.18](/7.6.18), and for the mechanics of cell expansion see [7.13.17](/7.13.17). The structure of the root cap and statocytes is in [7.12.20](/7.12.20), the pulvinus of *Mimosa* and the trap leaf of the Venus flytrap are in [7.12.23](/7.12.23), [7.12.24](/7.12.24) and [7.12.32](/7.12.32); the physics of amyloplast sedimentation is in [7.6.22](/7.6.22#sec-22.6), and the general principles of the action potential are in neuroscience and biophysics ([7.6](/7.6)).

## 19.1. Types of Plant Movement

**Definition 19.1.1 (Tropism and Nastic Movement)** A directed movement of a plant organ in response to a unidirectional external stimulus, whose direction is related to the direction of the stimulus, is called a **tropism**; a movement caused by an external stimulus that has no direction (or whose direction is unrelated to the direction of movement), with the direction of movement determined by the structure of the organ itself, is called a **nastic movement**.

All tropisms are growth movements: the growth rates on the two sides of the organ differ, causing it to bend irreversibly. By the relation of the direction of movement to that of the stimulus, they are classified as positive (toward the stimulus), negative (away from the stimulus) and transverse (at a right angle or some other angle to the stimulus, as in the transverse gravitropism of rhizomes). All tropisms involve three steps: perception of the stimulus, signal transduction, and the response that causes asymmetric growth. Some nastic movements are growth movements (such as opening and closing of flowers, and epinasty) and others are turgor movements (caused by turgor changes of motor cells in the pulvinus, reversible and rapid, as in the seismonastic movement of *Mimosa*). There are also endogenous movements that need no external stimulus: shoot tips, root tips and tendrils swing in a spiral while growing, called circumnutation (described in detail by Darwin 1880), and circadian movements driven by the biological clock.

| Type | Stimulus | Driving force | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phototropism | Unilateral light (blue) | Differential growth | Stems and coleoptiles bend toward light; leaf mosaic; roots bend away from light |
| Gravitropism | Gravity | Differential growth | Roots downward, shoots upward; set-point angles of lateral roots and branches |
| Thigmotropism | Unilateral contact | Differential growth | Tendril coiling; roots going around obstacles |
| Hydrotropism | Water potential gradient | Differential growth | Roots toward moist soil |
| Chemotropism | Gradient of a chemical | Differential growth or tip growth | Roots toward fertilizer; pollen tubes toward the ovule |
| Seismonasty | Touch, vibration, heat, electricity | Turgor (pulvinus) | *Mimosa*; Venus flytrap (turgor and elastic instability) |
| Nyctinasty | Day–night light–dark change (plus the clock) | Turgor (pulvinus) or growth | "Sleep movements" of legume leaves; opening and closing of dandelion heads |
| Thermonasty | Temperature change | Differential growth | Flowering of tulip and crocus |
| Epinasty and hyponasty | Ethylene, auxin, gibberellin and others (no direction) | Differential growth | Petiole drooping of flooded tomato |
| Circadian movements | Endogenous biological clock | Turgor or growth | Periodic movement of bean leaves under constant conditions |

## 19.2. Phototropism

**Definition 19.2.1 (Phototropism)** The directed growth of a plant organ under unilateral light is called **phototropism**.

Stems, coleoptiles and hypocotyls are generally positively phototropic, the roots of many plants are negatively phototropic (or insensitive to light), and twisting of the petiole turns the leaf blade perpendicular to the incident light (transverse phototropism), thereby forming a **leaf mosaic** in which leaves do not shade one another. Phototropism puts photosynthetic organs in the most favorable position for intercepting light and also quickly orients newly emerged seedlings toward the light source.

### 19.2.1. Classical Experiments and the Cholodny–Went Hypothesis

The study of phototropism led directly to the discovery of auxin.

1. Darwin and his son (1880): grass coleoptiles bend toward light under unilateral illumination; if the tip is covered, they do not bend, and if the elongation zone below the tip is covered, they still bend. Light is perceived by the tip and bending occurs in the elongation zone below, so some "influence" is transmitted from the tip to the elongation zone.
2. Boysen-Jensen (1913): when a gelatin block is inserted between the tip and the lower part, bending still occurs; when an impermeable mica plate is inserted on the shaded side, there is no bending. The influence is a diffusible substance transported downward on the shaded side.
3. Paál (1919): when a cut tip is placed back on one side of the cut surface in darkness, the coleoptile bends toward the other side: a substance from the tip promotes growth below it.
4. Went (1926, 1928): when oat coleoptile tips are placed on agar blocks, an agar block placed asymmetrically on a decapitated coleoptile also causes bending, and over a certain range the degree of bending is proportional to the amount of substance in the block, which established the oat curvature test (see [7.13.13](/7.13.13)); the substance was later identified as indole-3-acetic acid.

Cholodny (1927) and Went (1928) independently proposed a unified explanation: unilateral stimulation causes lateral transport of auxin in the organ; in phototropism auxin is transported to the shaded side, the shaded side grows faster, and the organ bends toward the light; in gravitropism auxin is transported to the lower side, promoting growth of the lower side in the shoot (bending upward) and inhibiting growth of the lower side in the root because the root is extremely sensitive to auxin (bending downward). This is the Cholodny–Went hypothesis. The key test was the experiment of Briggs et al. (1957) on maize coleoptile tips: the total amount of auxin diffusing from the tip into agar blocks was the same in darkness and under unilateral light, showing that light does not destroy auxin; when the tip was divided longitudinally by a mica plate and the agar block below was also divided into two halves, the shaded half collected about twice as much auxin as the lit half under unilateral light; when the mica plate cut the tip completely through to the top, this difference disappeared. Unilateral light therefore causes lateral transport of auxin in the tip rather than photooxidation of auxin on the lit side. The explanation in Chinese textbooks that "under unilateral light the tip produces a transverse electric potential difference and negatively charged IAA⁻ moves toward the positively charged shaded side" was an early speculation and has now been replaced by carrier-mediated active transport (Section 19.2.3).

### 19.2.2. Photoreceptors

The action spectrum of phototropism is effective in the blue region (400–500 nm, with peaks at about 450 nm and 475 nm) and the near-UV region (about 370 nm), and red light is almost ineffective; this agrees with the absorption spectrum of flavoproteins and differs from carotenoids (which have no near-UV peak). The photoreceptors of phototropism are **phototropins** (Huala et al. 1997; Briggs et al.): *Arabidopsis* has phot1 and phot2; phot1 is the main phototropic receptor and acts under both weak and strong light, whereas phot2 acts only under strong light; the *phot1 phot2* double mutant lacks phototropism at all light intensities. Phototropins are plasma membrane-bound light-activated protein kinases; the two N-terminal LOV domains use **flavin mononucleotide** as the chromophore, and blue light causes the chromophore to form a covalent adduct with a cysteine in LOV2, relieving the inhibition of the C-terminal kinase domain by LOV2 (molecular mechanism in [7.13.16](/7.13.16)). Phytochromes and cryptochromes modulate the sensitivity of phototropism but are not required receptors.

The fluence–response relation of phototropism has two phases. With very weak light pulses, the curvature first increases and then decreases with fluence (intensity × time), forming the "first positive curvature", and in this phase the Bunsen–Roscoe reciprocity law holds (the response depends only on total fluence and not on how intensity and duration are combined); at greater fluence, "negative curvature" or no curvature occurs; under continuous illumination a "second positive curvature" appears that increases with the duration of illumination, does not obey reciprocity, and is the main form under natural conditions.

### 19.2.3. Signal Transduction and Lateral Redistribution

In *Arabidopsis* etiolated hypocotyls, the events caused by unilateral blue light occur in the following order (Christie et al. 2011; Taiz et al.).

1. Within 1 min, new longitudinal cortical microtubules appear in cells on the lit side, and the original transverse microtubules depolymerize.
2. At about 3 min, phot1 autophosphorylates and partly leaves the plasma membrane; activated phot1 phosphorylates and inhibits the auxin efflux carrier ABCB19, so that auxin transport down from the stem apex pauses and hypocotyl elongation stops. Phot1 then enters the cell by clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and NPH3 (an adaptor of a ubiquitin ligase, first identified from the nonbending *nph3* mutant) is dephosphorylated by blue light and binds phot1, mediating ubiquitination of phot1, which is required for bending.
3. After about 15 min, PIN3 in cells on the shaded side relocates to the outward-facing plasma membrane, auxin accumulates on the shaded side of the upper hypocotyl, and auxin then reaches the elongation zone by polar transport.
4. At about 2 h, elongation of shaded-side cells speeds up and that of lit-side cells slows down, and the hypocotyl begins to bend toward the light.

The apoplast on the shaded side is more acidic than on the lit side, which both promotes auxin entry into cells and chemiosmotic efflux and promotes elongation through acid growth ([7.13.17](/7.13.17#sec-17.4)). In oat and maize coleoptiles auxin accumulates in the upper 1–2 mm of the tip, and the region of light perception and lateral transport lies within 5 mm of the tip; Iino and Briggs (1984) found that after a unilateral blue light pulse elongation on the shaded side speeds up and on the lit side slows down, the changes on the two sides being roughly symmetric, as expected if the total amount of auxin is unchanged and only redistributed. Results with the auxin response reporter (*DR5*), the *pin3* mutant (reduced phototropism) and the auxin signaling mutant *nph4* (*arf7*, nonbending) all support the Cholodny–Went hypothesis.

### 19.2.4. The Growth Inhibitor Hypothesis

In the 1970s–1990s, Bruinsma, Hasegawa and others used physicochemical methods (gas chromatography–mass spectrometry) to measure auxin in sunflower and radish hypocotyls and etiolated oat coleoptiles and reported that the IAA content of the two sides showed no marked difference during phototropic bending, whereas more growth inhibitors accumulated on the lit side, such as raphanusanin in radish and benzoxazolinones (such as MBOA) in grasses; they therefore proposed that phototropism results from inhibition of growth on the lit side by inhibitors (the growth inhibitor hypothesis). The problems with this hypothesis are as follows.

- The early oat curvature test and the physicochemical determinations measure different auxin pools (diffusible, transported auxin versus total auxin in the tissue), and bending depends on the former; comparing the two sides by total content may mask differences in the transport stream.
- Genetic evidence from *Arabidopsis* and grasses (the *phot1*, *nph3*, *pin3* and *nph4/arf7* mutants, and asymmetric expression of *DR5* and auxin-responsive genes on the shaded side) directly links phototropism to auxin redistribution and signal transduction.
- Inhibitors may indeed participate in some species, as a supplement to the auxin mechanism.

The current mainstream view is that, at least in coleoptiles and *Arabidopsis* hypocotyls, lateral redistribution of auxin is the main mechanism of phototropism; the inhibitor hypothesis may reflect special cases in certain species or differences in measurement methods, and the debate continues.

### 19.2.5. Quantitative Relation Between Differential Growth and Curvature

Let the organ diameter be $d$, the length of the elongation zone $L$, and the relative elongation rates (strain rates) of the shaded and lit sides $\dot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{s}}$ and $\dot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{l}}$. From geometry, the rate of change of curvature of the elongation zone and the angular rate of bending are

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\kappa}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\dot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{s}} - \dot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{l}}}{d}, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{L}{d}\,(\dot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{s}} - \dot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{l}}).
\tag{19.1}
$$

The thinner the organ and the longer the elongation zone, the faster the bending caused by the same growth difference.

**Example 19.2.1** An oat coleoptile has a diameter of $1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ and an elongation zone $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ long, and in darkness both sides elongate at 10 % per hour. After unilateral light the shaded side speeds up to 11.2 % per hour and the lit side slows to 8.4 % per hour (corresponding to 68 % of the auxin on the shaded side and a saturating dose–response curve; panel A of the figure below). By Eq. (19.1), $\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}t = (10/1.5) \times 0.028\,\mathrm{h^{-1}} = 0.187\,\mathrm{rad\,h^{-1}} = 10.7^\circ\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$; if the auxin redistribution reaches the elongation zone after about half an hour, the bending angle after 2.5 h is about $10.7 \times 2 \approx 21^\circ$. At an auxin ratio of 2:1 the ratio of growth rates is only 1.33:1, because the dose–response is already close to saturation near the endogenous concentration; if auxin and growth rate were logarithmically related over this range, the conclusion would be the same.

## 19.3. Gravitropism

**Definition 19.3.1 (Gravitropism)** The property of plant organs of growing in a definite direction under the influence of gravity is called **gravitropism**.

The primary root is positively gravitropic (downward), the main stem is negatively gravitropic (upward), and rhizomes, stolons and many lateral branches and lateral roots are transversely or obliquely gravitropic. Gravitropism ensures that whatever the orientation in which a seed falls into the soil, the seedling grows root down and shoot up; lodged crops also recover upright growth by means of it.

### 19.3.1. The Set-Point Angle

The angle that an organ maintains relative to gravity is called the gravitropic set-point angle (Digby and Firn 1995). By convention, a primary root pointing straight down is $0^\circ$ and a main stem pointing straight up is $180^\circ$, with lateral roots and branches between the two. After an organ is pushed away from its set-point angle (whether upward or downward), it bends back to the set-point angle, so obliquely growing lateral roots sometimes bend upward against gravity and lateral branches sometimes bend downward; this shows that oblique growth is not the result of "weaker" gravitropism but of a separate mechanism that maintains the angle (Taiz et al.). The set-point angle determines the depth of the root system and the shape of the tree crown; the rice gene *DRO1* makes the root growth angle steeper and the root system deeper and increases drought resistance (Uga et al. 2013).

### 19.3.2. Perception: The Starch–Statolith Hypothesis

The simplest physical way to sense gravity is to detect the motion of a sinking body. In plants, such bodies are the large, heavy amyloplasts in statocytes (statoliths, [7.12.20](/7.12.20#sec-20.2.1)). The starch–statolith hypothesis proposed by Haberlandt and Němec (1900) is supported in several ways.

- Location: the statocytes in roots are the columella cells in the center of the root cap; a root with the cap removed still grows (even slightly faster) but loses gravitropism; an upright root with half of its cap removed bends toward the side where the cap remains, because the inhibitor supplied by the cap (later shown to be auxin) acts on one side only (Shaw and Wilkins 1973). The statocytes in stems and hypocotyls are the starch sheath outside the vascular bundles (continuous with the endodermis of the root but containing mobile amyloplasts); *Arabidopsis* *scr* (*sgr1*) and *shr* mutants lack the starch sheath and the stem and inflorescence have no gravitropism, whereas the roots are normal (Taiz et al.).
- Timing: amyloplasts are the only organelles in columella cells that consistently sediment, and the time they take to sediment is close to the time needed to perceive a gravity stimulus (a few minutes).
- Genetics: starch-deficient mutants (such as *Arabidopsis* *pgm*, lacking plastid phosphoglucomutase) have a markedly slower and weaker gravitropic response but retain some residual gravitropism, showing that starch is required for the normal response but that there may be starch-independent mechanisms of perception (such as the pressure of the weight of the whole protoplast on the plasma membrane).

Sedimentation of amyloplasts can be estimated with Stokes' law ([Eq. (22.10) of 7.6.22](/7.6.22#eq-22.10)): the sedimentation velocity of a sphere of radius $a$ with a density excess $\Delta\rho$ over the cytoplasm is

$$
v = \frac{2\,\Delta\rho\,g\,a^2}{9\eta}.
\tag{19.2}
$$

**Example 19.3.1** Amyloplasts in columella cells are about $2$–$3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter (take $a = 1.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), the density of starch is about $1.5\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, and the difference from cytoplasm is taken as $\Delta\rho = 450\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. If the viscosity of the cytoplasm is the same as that of water ($\eta = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$), $v = 2 \times 450 \times 9.8 \times (1.2\times10^{-6})^2/(9 \times 10^{-3}) = 1.4\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and crossing a columella cell about $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ tall takes only 7 s. The measured sedimentation takes several minutes after the root is turned (about 6–10 min in *Arabidopsis* columella cells as shown by Taiz et al.), corresponding to an apparent viscosity tens of times that of water (panel C of the figure quantifying gravitropism in Section 19.3.4): the actin network and endoplasmic reticulum in the cytoplasm hinder sedimentation, and amyloplasts fall in jumps through the network. The size and density of amyloplasts ensure that their sedimentation far exceeds thermal motion: by the criterion of [7.6.22](/7.6.22#sec-22.6.2), only particles with a radius above about $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and a density difference above about $100\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ sediment reliably under gravity, and organelles much smaller than amyloplasts (such as ribosomes and mitochondria) cannot act as statoliths.

:::

How sedimentation of amyloplasts is converted into a signal is not fully clear. One hypothesis holds that amyloplasts sedimented on the lower side of the cell press on the endoplasmic reticulum (columella cells have a special "nodal ER") or stretch the plasma membrane attached to the cytoskeleton, opening mechanosensitive ion channels; it was recently found that LAZY family proteins are enriched near the plasma membrane on the new lower side of the cell as amyloplasts sediment and recruit RLD proteins, causing PIN3 to localize polarly to the lower side, which provides a molecular pathway for "how sedimentation determines polarity".

### 19.3.3. Signal Transduction and Differential Growth

The sequence of events after an *Arabidopsis* root is placed horizontally (the time line of Taiz et al.; see also the columella-cell diagram in Section 19.3.4) is as follows.

| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| Seconds to minutes | Amyloplasts begin to sediment and reach the new lower side after about 6–10 min |
| About 2 min | Cytosolic pH of columella cells rises from 7.2 to 7.6 and apoplast pH falls from 5.5 to 4.5 (Fasano et al. 2001), about 10 min before visible bending |
| Minutes | Cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ signal; PIN3 (and PIN7) change from uniform distribution to concentration at the plasma membrane on the new lower side of columella cells (Kleine-Vehn et al. 2010) |
| Tens of minutes | In the root cap more auxin is transported to the lower side; it is carried through the lateral root cap and epidermis (AUX1, PIN2) to the lower side of the elongation zone, and the *DR5* reporter shows increased auxin on the lower side |
| About 1 to several hours | Elongation of the lower side of the elongation zone is inhibited, the upper side continues to elongate, and the root bends downward; near vertical, auxin becomes symmetric again |

The role of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ has long been known (an experiment often cited in competitions and Chinese textbooks): when an agar block containing the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ chelator EGTA is placed on the root cap, the root loses its gravitropic response, and it is restored when an agar block containing $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is substituted; inhibitors of calmodulin in the root cap also abolish the gravitropic response; and $^{45}\ce{Ca^2+}$, like auxin, is preferentially transported to the lower half of a gravistimulated root cap. The roots of the auxin transport mutants *aux1* and *pin2* have no gravitropism, and the *pin3* mutant has reduced but not abolished gravitropism, which shows that there are other asymmetric events besides the relocation of PIN3, for example asymmetric acidification of the apoplast, which changes the chemiosmotic gradient and directs auxin flow in a new direction (which in turn further polarizes PIN3, forming a canalization-like positive feedback).

In shoot gravitropism, PIN3 in starch sheath cells, together with ABCB19, confines auxin to the vascular cylinder; after the stem is placed horizontally, PIN3 in starch sheath cells relocates and carries auxin to the outer cortex and epidermis on the lower side, where it promotes elongation, and the stem bends upward. The stems of grass crops have swollen leaf sheath bases at the nodes (the "pulvinus" or node pulvinus), in which statocytes sense the direction of gravity after lodging, and elongation of cells on the lower side bends the stem upward from the node, which is why cereals can partly recover upright growth after lodging.

### 19.3.4. The Sine Law and the Time Course of the Response

When an organ deviates from its set-point by an angle $\theta$, the initial strength of the gravitropic response is approximately proportional to $\sin\theta$ (Sachs' sine law): the component of gravity along the organ axis carries no directional information, and only the component perpendicular to the axis, $g\sin\theta$, drives statoliths to sediment onto the side wall (panel A of the figure quantifying gravitropism below). In this sense the response is maximal at $\theta = 90^\circ$ (horizontal). The inverted position ($\theta = 180^\circ$) is in theory an unstable equilibrium and in practice is broken by circumnutation or any asymmetry. The stimulus must last a certain time to cause visible bending, and this minimum time is called the presentation time; within a certain range the response depends on the "dose" of the stimulus (the product of $g\sin\theta$ and stimulus duration), similar to the reciprocity law in phototropism, which is also the basis of studying gravitropism with centrifuges or clinostats.

If the bending rate obeys the sine law $\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}t = -k\sin\theta$ ($k$ is the gravitropic sensitivity of the organ), separating variables and integrating gives

$$
\tan\frac{\theta(t)}{2} = \tan\frac{\theta_0}{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-kt}.
\tag{19.3}
$$

At small angles $\theta$ returns to the set-point angle exponentially as $\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$; starting from horizontal ($\theta_0 = 90^\circ$), returning to $10^\circ$ takes $t = \ln[\tan 45^\circ/\tan 5^\circ]/k = 2.44/k$ (panel B of the figure below). Real organs also sense their own curvature (proprioception): a model with gravisensing only would make the organ overshoot the vertical and oscillate back and forth, whereas adding a proprioceptive term that tends to straighten the already bent part makes bending converge stably to the vertical; the ratio of the two forms a dimensionless "balance number" that can describe gravitropic form from seedlings to tree trunks in a unified way (Bastien et al. 2013).

## 19.4. Thigmotropism, Hydrotropism and Chemotropism

**Definition 19.4.1 (Thigmotropism, Hydrotropism and Chemotropism)** The directed growth of a plant organ in response to unidirectional contact is called **thigmotropism**; the tendency of roots to grow toward moister regions when soil water is unevenly distributed is called **hydrotropism**; directed growth caused by uneven distribution of a chemical around the plant or its unidirectional action is called **chemotropism**.

### 19.4.1. Thigmotropism

Tendrils are the organs showing the most obvious thigmotropism. After the tendril of pea or cucumber touches a support, growth slows on the contact side and speeds up on the opposite side, bending begins within minutes, and the tendril coils several turns around the support within hours; the contact signal also propagates along the tendril and makes the untouched part coil into a spring-like helix. The helix of a cucumber tendril is driven by a gelatinous fiber ribbon that contracts on the inner side of the tendril; because both ends of the tendril are fixed, the helix must reverse its handedness in the middle (the "perversion point"), and the whole tendril thus becomes a spring that can buffer the pull of wind on the plant (Gerbode et al. 2012). A root tip that meets an obstacle also bends around it; contact and gravitropic signals are integrated so that the root slides along the surface of the obstacle until it can grow downward again.

Related to thigmotropism is thigmomorphogenesis: plants that are frequently touched, shaken or blown by wind have shorter internodes, thicker stems and delayed flowering (Jaffe 1973). Within tens of minutes after touch in *Arabidopsis*, expression of *TCH* genes (encoding calmodulin and calmodulin-like proteins, xyloglucan endotransglucosylase and others) rises sharply (Braam and Davis 1990), and jasmonic acid takes part in this response. Rolling and "seedling pulling" in production make use of this effect of making plants short and sturdy.

### 19.4.2. Hydrotropism

Roots bend toward the moister side in a moisture gradient. Hydrotropism is hard to separate from gravitropism on the ground, and studies mostly use conditions without gravity bias (clinostats, space experiments) or mutants lacking gravitropism. Hydrotropism in *Arabidopsis* has the following features.

- It requires the *MIZ1* (*MIZU-KUSSEI1*) gene (Kobayashi et al. 2007), and *miz1* mutants have no hydrotropism at all but normal gravitropism.
- It requires **abscisic acid** signaling and acts mainly in the cortical cells of the elongation zone: restoring ABA signaling in the cortex alone restores hydrotropism in a nonbending mutant (Dietrich et al. 2017).
- In *Arabidopsis* it does not appear to depend on lateral auxin redistribution (auxin transport inhibitors do not prevent it), unlike phototropism and gravitropism; but in species such as pea auxin may take part.
- Early experiments found that removing the root cap weakens hydrotropism, so the root cap may also take part in perception; hydrotropism and gravitropism antagonize each other, and in drought gravitropism weakens and hydrotropism strengthens.

### 19.4.3. Chemotropism

Chemotropism of roots makes them grow toward regions richer in nutrients; in production, deep placement of fertilizer can induce the root system to grow deeper and enlarge the range of absorption (together with local proliferation of roots in nutrient patches, see [7.13.18](/7.13.18#sec-18.7)). Growth of the **pollen tube** is tip growth ([7.13.17](/7.13.17#sec-17.2.4)), and its guidance in the pistil is a typical example of chemotropism. Chinese textbooks often say that "the ovule secretes $\ce{Ca^2+}$ to guide the pollen tube"; calcium is indeed required for tip growth of the pollen tube, but the main signals for short-range guidance are the cysteine-rich small peptides LURE secreted by synergid cells (Okuda et al. 2009, discovered in *Torenia*), which are recognized by receptor kinases such as PRK6 at the pollen tube tip (Takeuchi and Higashiyama 2016) and are species-specific. Pollen tube guidance and fertilization are covered in [7.13.23](/7.13.23).

## 19.5. Nastic Movements

### 19.5.1. Seismonasty: *Mimosa*

**Definition 19.5.1 (Seismonasty)** A nastic movement caused by mechanical or other stimuli such as touch, vibration, sudden cold, sudden heat or electricity is called **seismonasty**.

The bipinnately compound leaf of *Mimosa pudica* has three levels of pulvini: the leaflet pulvinus at the base of each leaflet, the secondary pulvinus at the base of each pinna and the main pulvinus at the base of the petiole. When one leaflet is touched lightly, that pair of leaflets folds upward within about 1 s; with a stronger stimulus, the stimulus propagates along the leaf, the leaflets of the pinnae fold in succession, the petiole droops, and the stimulus may even reach other leaves of the same plant. After the stimulus ends, the leaf recovers within 10–30 min.

The driving force of the movement is the turgor change of motor cells in the pulvinus (see the figure below). In the main pulvinus the vascular bundle is at the center, surrounded by large, thin-walled parenchymatous motor cells; the cells in the lower half (extensor cells) have thin walls and large intercellular spaces, and the cells in the upper half (flexor cells) are the opposite. When the stimulus arrives, the permeability of the plasma membrane of lower-half motor cells to $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ suddenly increases, ions flow into the intercellular space (apoplast), water follows out through aquaporins, the lower-half cells lose turgor and shrink, and the petiole droops under its own weight and the turgor of the upper half; the upper–lower structure of the leaflet pulvinus is the reverse of that of the main pulvinus, so the leaflets fold upward. The vacuoles of motor cells also contain small tannin-rich vacuoles, and release of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ from them during stimulation may take part. Recovery is an active process: the proton gradient established by the plasma membrane $\ce{H+}$-ATPase drives $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ back into the motor cells.

Two kinds of electrical signals transmit the stimulus. The action potential propagates in the phloem of the petiole and the parenchyma cells around the protoxylem at about $2$–$3\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$ (Sibaoka), with all-or-none character and a refractory period; injury (such as burning) causes a variation potential, a depolarization caused by changes of hydraulic pressure in the xylem, which propagates more slowly and whose amplitude decreases with distance. The action potential of plants differs from that of animals: depolarization is mainly caused by $\ce{Cl-}$ efflux activated by $\ce{Ca^2+}$ influx and repolarization by $\ce{K+}$ efflux, and the duration is of the order of seconds, much slower than the millisecond scale of nerves (for a comparison of plant electrical signals see the relevant chapters of [7.6](/7.6)).

**Example 19.5.1 (Volume Change of a Motor Cell)** Let an extensor motor cell at rest have volume $V_0$, solute potential $\Psi_{\mathrm{s}} = -1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}$ and turgor $0.8\,\mathrm{MPa}$, in equilibrium with an apoplast of water potential $-0.4\,\mathrm{MPa}$; after stimulation it loses 40 % of its osmotically active solute (mainly KCl) and then re-equilibrates with the apoplast in water potential. Assume that the wall is linearly elastic in this range with volumetric elastic modulus $\varepsilon = 2\,\mathrm{MPa}$ (the walls of motor cells are soft), and write $x = V/V_0$. With the amount of solute reduced to 0.6 times and the volume $x$ times, the osmometer approximation gives $\Psi_{\mathrm{s}} = -0.6 \times 1.2/x = -0.72/x\,\mathrm{MPa}$ ([7.13.2](/7.13.2)); water potential equilibrium requires $P = -0.4 + 0.72/x$; the elastic relation requires $P = 0.8 - \varepsilon(1 - x) = 2x - 1.2$. Combining gives $2x^2 - 0.8x - 0.72 = 0$, $x = (0.8 + \sqrt{0.64 + 5.76})/4 = 0.83$ and $P = 0.47\,\mathrm{MPa}$. That is, loss of 40 % of the solute reduces the volume of the extensor cell by about 17 % and its turgor by about 40 %; if the wall were as stiff as that of an ordinary parenchyma cell ($\varepsilon = 10\,\mathrm{MPa}$), the same solute loss would reduce the volume by only about 4 %. The soft wall allows motor cells to produce a large volume change with a moderate ion flux.

### 19.5.2. The Venus Flytrap: Counting and Rapid Closure

Each half of the trap leaf of the Venus flytrap (*Dionaea muscipula*, [7.12.24](/7.12.24#sec-24.4.3)) has three sensory hairs on its inner surface. Touching one sensory hair produces one action potential, but the leaf does not close; when a second touch (of the same or another hair) occurs within about 30 s, the leaf closes within about 0.1 s. This "counting to two" mechanism prevents a single accidental stimulus such as a raindrop or falling leaf from wasting the energy of closure. After closure, the struggling captured insect keeps touching the sensory hairs: action potentials from the third onward induce jasmonate signaling, and the leaf closes tightly into a "stomach"; about five or more induce secretion of digestive enzymes and expression of transporters such as $\ce{Na+}$ transporters needed for nutrient uptake (Böhm et al. 2016); the flytrap thus decides its investment in a graded manner according to the number of stimuli.

The physical basis of counting is accumulation of cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ (Suda et al. 2020): each stimulus raises the cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ of leaf cells by a step $c_0$, which then decays with time constant $\tau$; the threshold $\theta$ for closure lies between $c_0$ and $2c_0$. When two stimuli are separated by $\Delta t$, the peak after the second stimulus is $c_0(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-\Delta t/\tau})$, and the condition for exceeding the threshold gives the time window for closure,

$$
\Delta t < \Delta t_{\max} = \tau\ln\frac{c_0}{\theta - c_0}.
\tag{19.4}
$$

When two stimuli are far apart, if a third stimulus follows quickly, the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ accumulated over three stimuli may still exceed the threshold, which agrees with experimental observation: stimuli with longer intervals need more repetitions to trigger closure (see the figure below).

The leaf closes in 0.1 s, far faster than can be achieved by water flow: the leaf is about $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick, and the time for water to be redistributed diffusively in the tissue (poroelastic relaxation) grows as $L^2$ and is at least of the order of seconds. The trap leaf of the flytrap, when open, is an outward-bulging hyperboloid in a metastable "prestressed" state; after stimulation a small active change in leaf curvature (possibly caused by turgor changes in motor cells and wall acidification) carries it past the instability point, and the leaf flips with a "snap" into the inward-bulging closed shape as elastic energy is released (snap-buckling, Forterre et al. 2005). The same principle (storing elastic energy and triggering an instability) is also used in the suction of bladderwort traps and the ejection of fruits of squirting cucumber and impatiens. This comparison shows that the maximum speed of plant movement is determined by the source of power: growth movements take hours, turgor movements seconds, and elastic instabilities milliseconds.

### 19.5.3. Nyctinasty and Thermonasty

**Definition 19.5.2 (Nyctinasty and Thermonasty)** A nastic movement caused by the day–night light–dark change is called **nyctinasty**; a nastic movement caused by temperature change is called **thermonasty**.

The leaves of legumes (soybean, bean, peanut, silk tree, *Mimosa*) and wood sorrel open during the day and fold or droop at night, called "sleep movements". They are driven by turgor changes of motor cells in the pulvinus. The best-studied cases are *Albizia* and rain tree (*Samanea saman*): at night the extensor cells lose $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ and shrink while the flexor cells take up ions and swell, and by day the reverse occurs; opening of $\ce{K+}$ channels in the plasma membrane of motor cells is rhythmically regulated by the biological clock and modulated by input from **phytochrome** (the disappearance of the red light signal) and blue light receptors. The explanation in Chinese sources that "auxin synthesized by leaves in the daytime is transported to the lower half of the petiole, with potassium ions transported along with it" lacks evidence, and it is now generally held that the ion flux is controlled directly by the clock and photoreceptors acting on the ion channels and proton pumps of motor cells. The adaptive significance of sleep movements is still debated and may be related to reducing nocturnal heat loss by radiation, avoiding interference of moonlight with photoperiod measurement or reducing nocturnal herbivory.

Opening and closing of flowers are mostly growth movements. In the perianth of tulip and crocus, as temperature rises the inner (adaxial) side grows faster than the outer side and the flower opens, and as temperature falls the outer side grows faster and the flower closes; this is thermonasty, and after several openings and closings the perianth becomes larger from accumulated growth. The capitulum of dandelion closes at night and opens by day, and the flowers of tobacco and four-o'clock open in the evening and close by day, which are nyctinasty (also regulated by the clock). Opening of flowers at suitable temperatures and times coordinates with the active times of pollinators.

### 19.5.4. Epinasty and Hyponasty

**Definition 19.5.3 (Epinasty and Hyponasty)** The bending downward of bilaterally symmetric (dorsiventral) organs such as leaves, petioles and petals because the upper (adaxial) side grows faster than the lower side is called **epinasty**; the bending upward because the lower side grows faster than the upper side is called *hyponasty*.

The direction of epinasty and hyponasty is determined by the dorsiventral structure of the organ and is unrelated to the direction of the stimulus, so they are nastic movements. **Ethylene** is the main hormone that causes epinasty: when tomato and other plants are flooded, the oxygen-deficient roots accumulate the ethylene precursor ACC, ACC is transported through the xylem to the shoot, and in the aerobic petiole it is converted to ethylene and causes epinastic drooping of the petiole (Bradford and Yang 1980), a typical symptom for diagnosing flooding and ethylene pollution; high concentrations of auxin also cause epinasty by inducing ethylene. Conversely, in flood-tolerant plants such as marsh dock (*Rumex palustris*), ethylene causes hyponasty of the petiole during flooding, so that the leaf lifts up and quickly emerges above the water; high temperature and shading also make *Arabidopsis* leaves lift hyponastically (part of thermomorphogenesis and shade avoidance, [7.13.16](/7.13.16)), which favors leaf cooling and light interception.

## 19.6. Circadian Movements

In 1729 de Mairan placed a *Mimosa* in darkness and found that its leaves still opened and closed with a day–night rhythm, the first record of an endogenous rhythm. In the first half of the twentieth century Bünning and others studied this rhythm with the movement of bean leaves and found that under constant conditions its period is close to but not equal to 24 h, that the period differs genetically among lines, and that it can be reset ("entrained") by light–dark cycles.

**Definition 19.6.1 (Circadian Movement)** A movement of a plant organ that does not depend entirely on the day–night change of the environment but continues under constant conditions with a period close to 24 h is called a *circadian movement*; it is driven by an endogenous biological clock and entrained by environmental light–dark and temperature cycles.

The molecular structure of the plant clock is the same as in other organisms, consisting of transcription–translation negative feedback loops ([7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.6)): CCA1 and LHY, expressed in the morning, repress evening genes such as *TOC1*, and TOC1 in turn represses *CCA1* and *LHY*, forming the core oscillator; light (through phytochromes and cryptochromes) and temperature act as inputs that synchronize it with the outside world (**entrainment**); output pathways control a large number of genes, so that leaf movement, stomatal aperture, hypocotyl elongation (the clock makes PIF4 and PIF5 peak before dawn, and the hypocotyl grows fastest before dawn) and release of fragrance all occur at the appropriate time of day. The relation of the clock to photoperiod measurement is in [7.13.21](/7.13.21).

Sun tracking by sunflower is a growth movement gated by the biological clock (Atamian et al. 2016): in young plants the stem apex turns from east to west with the sun during the day and back to the east at night, the result of alternating growth of the east and west sides of the stem, with the east side growing faster by day and the west side faster at night; under constant light this movement continues for several days with a period of about 24 h, showing that it is regulated by the clock. After flowering the stem stops growing and the flower head is fixed facing east; an east-facing head warms faster in the morning and attracts several times more pollinating insects than a head artificially turned to face west. The leaves of some plants (soybean, cotton, alfalfa) track the sun by means of pulvini, keeping the leaf blade perpendicular to the sunlight (diaheliotropism) or, under strong light and water deficit, parallel to the sunlight (paraheliotropism), the latter reducing photoinhibition and transpiration.

## Questions

1. In Briggs' experiment the total auxin of the maize coleoptile tip is unchanged under unilateral light, and the ratio of shaded to lit side is about 2:1. (a) If the growth rate is proportional to auxin concentration, calculate the ratio of elongation rates of the two sides. (b) If the growth rate is a saturating function of auxin, $r = r_{\max}A/(A + K)$ with $K = 0.6$ (taking the concentration in darkness as 1), what is the ratio of the two sides? (c) Explain why the coleoptile is so sensitive to auxin asymmetry and yet needs only a small growth difference to bend.

   **Answer** (a) Shaded side $A_{\mathrm{s}} = 2 \times 2/3 = 1.33$, lit side $0.67$, ratio of rates $2.0$. (b) $r_{\mathrm{s}}/r_{\mathrm{l}} = [1.33/1.93]/[0.67/1.27] = 0.690/0.528 = 1.31$. (c) By Eq. (19.1), the bending rate is proportional to the difference of strain rates of the two sides multiplied by $L/d$; the coleoptile is long and thin, $L/d \approx 7$, so a strain rate difference of 2.8 % per hour (Example 19.2.1) produces bending of about $11^\circ$ per hour. The change in auxin ratio is "compressed" by the saturating dose–response into a smaller growth difference, and the geometric amplification makes it a conspicuous bend.

2. A horizontally placed root returns to vertical according to the sine law with $k = 0.02\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$. (a) How long does it take to return from $90^\circ$ to $30^\circ$ and to $5^\circ$? (b) If the root is inverted at $175^\circ$, how long does it take to return to $90^\circ$? Explain why the response is so slow near inversion.

   **Answer** By Eq. (19.3), $t = \ln[\tan(\theta_0/2)/\tan(\theta/2)]/k$. (a) $90^\circ \to 30^\circ$: $\ln(1/0.2679)/0.02 = 1.317/0.02 = 66\,\mathrm{min}$; $\to 5^\circ$: $\ln(1/0.04366)/0.02 = 3.13/0.02 = 157\,\mathrm{min}$. (b) $\tan 87.5^\circ = 22.9$, $t = \ln 22.9/0.02 = 3.13/0.02 = 157\,\mathrm{min}$. Near $180^\circ$, $\sin\theta \to 0$: gravity is almost along the root axis, the component perpendicular to the axis is very small, and statoliths cannot reliably establish an asymmetry on the side wall, so the initial response is slow; in practice circumnutation or any small deviation is amplified.

3. Use Stokes' law to estimate how far amyloplasts in columella cells could sediment in 10 min if a plant were in the microgravity of the International Space Station (about $10^{-4}g$) (take apparent viscosity $0.05\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, $a = 1.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, $\Delta\rho = 450\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$). Compared with thermal motion, could such sedimentation be perceived?

   **Answer** $v = 2 \times 450 \times 9.8\times10^{-4} \times (1.2\times10^{-6})^2/(9 \times 0.05) = 2.8\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, so the sedimentation in 10 min is $1.7\,\mathrm{nm}$. In the same time, from the Stokes–Einstein relation $D = k_{\mathrm{B}}T/6\pi\eta a = 4.1\times10^{-21}/(6\pi \times 0.05 \times 1.2\times10^{-6}) = 3.6\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, the diffusive displacement is $\sqrt{2Dt} = \sqrt{2 \times 3.6\times10^{-15} \times 600} \approx 2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, three orders of magnitude larger than the sedimentation. Under microgravity amyloplasts therefore do not sediment directionally, and roots orient by other signals such as phototropism and hydrotropism; in space experiments the bending of plant roots is indeed mainly affected by light and circumnutation.

4. A Venus flytrap has a $\ce{Ca^2+}$ decay time constant $\tau = 30\,\mathrm{s}$ and a closure threshold $\theta = 1.45c_0$. (a) Within how long after the first touch must the second touch occur for the leaf to close? (b) If two touches are $40\,\mathrm{s}$ apart and the leaf does not close, within how long after the second touch can a third touch make the leaf close? (c) If low temperature increases $\tau$ to $50\,\mathrm{s}$, how does the time window change?

   **Answer** (a) By Eq. (19.4), $\Delta t_{\max} = 30\ln(1/0.45) = 30 \times 0.799 = 24\,\mathrm{s}$. (b) After the second touch $\ce{Ca^2+} = c_0(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-40/30}) = 1.264c_0$; the peak after the third touch is $c_0 + 1.264c_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-s/30} > 1.45c_0$, that is, $\mathrm{e}^{-s/30} > 0.356$, $s < 30\ln 2.81 = 31\,\mathrm{s}$. (c) $\Delta t_{\max} = 50 \times 0.799 = 40\,\mathrm{s}$; the time window lengthens in proportion to $\tau$: in the model low temperature makes the leaf more "forgiving", although in reality low temperature also slows the action potential and closure.

5. Design a set of experiments to distinguish two explanations of "sun tracking" in sunflower seedlings: (a) it is a direct phototropic response to the direction of the sun; (b) it is a growth rhythm driven by the biological clock and synchronized by light.

   **Answer** Key points (after Atamian et al. 2016): (1) Move sunflowers indoors under constant light (overhead source, no direction); if the stem apex still swings east–west with a period of about 24 h for several days, this supports (b); if it stops immediately, this supports (a). (2) Outdoors, rotate the pots by 180° each day (so that the east side faces west); if the stem apex at first still moves according to the "original east" for a day or two and then resynchronizes, the movement has an endogenous rhythmic component. (3) Under artificial light–dark cycles (for example a 30 h period), if the movement cannot follow periods that are too long or too short, it is limited by the period of the endogenous clock (the range of entrainment of the clock). (4) Measure the elongation rates of the east and west sides of the stem (tracking of marked points) and the auxin and growth-related gene expression of the two sides, and see whether growth of the east side by day and the west side at night is correlated with the rhythm of clock output genes. (5) Test with clock-disrupting treatments or mutants (if available). Conclusion: sun tracking requires both blue light phototropism (directional information by day) and the clock (turning back east at night, anticipation of sunrise).
