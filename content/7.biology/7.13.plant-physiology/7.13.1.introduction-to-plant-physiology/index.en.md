Plants have no heart, lungs or nervous system, yet they must carry out the same basic tasks as animals: exchanging gases, transporting materials, and sensing and integrating information. They do so in ways quite different from animals, and they do so without being able to move. Plant physiology studies how plants accomplish these tasks: how water and mineral nutrients move from the soil into the plant and up to the leaves, how light energy is converted into chemical energy and distributed among organs, how plants sense light, gravity, temperature and other organisms, and how they use this information to decide when to germinate, grow, flower and senesce. This chapter introduces the whole course. We first define the discipline (1.1), then outline the distinctive features of plant life and their physiological consequences (1.2), describe the roles of plants in the Earth system and in human life (1.3), review the history of the field (1.4), and finally present the structure of the course and the quantitative methods used throughout (1.5).

The course builds on earlier ones. For plant morphology, anatomy and phylogeny see botany ([7.12](/7.12)); for the biochemistry of photosynthesis and respiration see [7.1.24](/7.1.24), [7.1.25](/7.1.25), [7.1.20](/7.1.20) and [7.1.23](/7.1.23); for the physics of water potential, chemical potential and photoreceptors see biophysics ([7.6.3](/7.6.3), [7.6.18](/7.6.18)); for membrane transport and cell signaling see cell biology ([7.3](/7.3)). ## 1.1. What Plant Physiology Studies

**Definition 1.1.1 (Plant Physiology)** The science that investigates the laws and mechanisms of plant life processes and applies them to plant production is called **plant physiology**. It studies how plants grow, develop and function in interaction with their abiotic and biotic environments.

The content of plant physiology can be grouped into three interrelated aspects:

| Aspect | Content | Chapters of this course |
|---|---|---|
| Transformation of matter and energy | Uptake, transport and assimilation of water and mineral nutrients; photosynthesis; transport and partitioning of assimilates; respiration | 2–12 |
| Information transfer and signal transduction | Plant hormones; light signals; perception of and responses to the environment and other organisms | 13–16, 25–27 |
| Growth, differentiation and development | Cell expansion and growth, organogenesis, tropisms, germination, flowering, reproduction, senescence | 17–24 |

Growth is the irreversible increase in volume and mass resulting from an increase in cell number and cell size; differentiation is the structural and functional specialization of cells; development is the overall progression of growth and differentiation in time and space, expressed as morphogenesis, that is, the formation of tissues, organs and the whole plant. The transformation of matter and energy (metabolism) is the basis of growth and development, whereas signal transduction determines when and where metabolism and development take place and is therefore the key to how plants adapt to their environment.

Plant physiology overlaps with botany, biochemistry, biophysics and ecology. Its division of labor with botany is that botany describes structure and its evolution (what a leaf looks like and where it came from), whereas plant physiology explains function (how the leaf takes up $\ce{CO2}$ without losing too much water). Function cannot be separated from structure: from the mutual recognition of molecules to the unfolding of a leaf to the shading of neighbors by a whole plant, structure and function are two aspects of one biological whole at every scale. This course therefore returns often to anatomical structures introduced in botany, but each time it asks the same question: how does this structure work, and why does it work that way?

## 1.2. Features of Plant Life and Their Physiological Consequences

### 1.2.1. Unifying Principles

Plants vary enormously in size and form, from duckweeds less than 1 cm across to coast redwoods taller than 100 m, yet all of them carry out essentially the same processes and are built on the same structural plan. This is also why plant physiologists can study a few **model organisms** (*Arabidopsis*, rice, maize, tobacco, the moss *Physcomitrium patens*) and extend the conclusions to other plants. Plant life can be summarized in the following principles:

1. Plants are the primary producers of the Earth and the ultimate collectors of solar energy: through **photosynthesis** they convert light energy into chemical energy stored in carbohydrates synthesized from $\ce{CO2}$ and water.
2. Except for a few reproductive cells, plants cannot move; they are sessile. Instead, they grow throughout their lives toward resources such as light, water and mineral nutrients.
3. Plants have structural support that lets them grow toward sunlight against gravity.
4. Plants have mechanisms for transporting water and minerals from the soil to the sites of photosynthesis and growth, and for transporting photosynthetic products to non-photosynthetic organs.
5. Plants continually lose water by evaporation and have evolved mechanisms for avoiding desiccation.
6. Plants develop from embryos, which receive nutrients from the mother plant; this extra provision allows plants to build large, self-supporting structures on land.

### 1.2.2. Features of Plant Cells and the Plant Body

Compared with animals, plant life has the following features, each of which has far-reaching physiological consequences:

| Feature | Structural basis | Physiological consequences | Related chapters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autotrophy | **Chloroplasts**; derived from the endosymbiosis of cyanobacteria (**endosymbiotic theory**) | Self-sufficiency in carbon and energy; but taking up $\ce{CO2}$ inevitably entails losing water, a leaf losing several hundred water molecules for each $\ce{CO2}$ gained | 3, 7–9 |
| Sessility | Roots anchor the plant in the soil | Unable to escape adverse environments, plants show high developmental plasticity, sensitive environmental perception and signaling systems, a rich secondary metabolism (chemical defense) and mechanisms of stress tolerance | 16, 25–27 |
| Modular construction and indeterminate growth | Apical and lateral **meristems** retain the capacity for division throughout life; the plant body is made up of repeated modules (nodes, internodes, leaves, axillary buds) | Plant form is shaped by the environment; organs can be lost without fatal consequences (herbivory, abscission); competition and coordination among modules (**apical dominance**) are regulated by hormones | 17–18, 24 |
| **Totipotency** | Differentiated cells can dedifferentiate and regenerate a whole plant | Vegetative propagation, tissue culture and genetic transformation become possible | 18, 29 |
| **Cell wall** and central **vacuole** | A wall made of cellulose microfibrils; the vacuole can occupy 90% of the volume of a mature cell | Cells can build up a **turgor pressure** of several atmospheres and enlarge by taking up water rather than by synthesizing protoplasm, which makes growth "cheap"; growth is controlled by cell wall loosening; wall-less animal cells have none of this | 2, 17 |
| **Plasmodesmata** | Membrane channels through the cell wall connect the cytoplasm of adjacent cells into a symplast | Water, sugars, signaling molecules and even proteins and RNAs can move directly between cells; viruses also spread this way | 2–5, 11, 25 |
| **Alternation of generations** | Alternation of a diploid sporophyte and a haploid gametophyte | Pollen and the embryo sac are themselves multicellular gametophytes, so reproductive physiology differs from that of animals | 23 |

Animals assign gas exchange, nutrient uptake, transport, support and sensing to specialized organ systems (lungs, gut, circulatory system, skeleton, nervous system). Plants perform the same functions in a more distributed way: gas exchange takes place through thousands of **stomata**, photosynthesis occurs throughout green tissues, nutrients are transported from cell to cell and through vascular tissue, water and nutrients are taken up mainly by roots (sometimes also by stems and leaves), support comes from cell walls and hydrostatic pressure, and light, pathogens and other cues are sensed by receptors present in most cells. This "distributed" organization suits the modular plant body, and it also shapes the methods of plant physiology: many questions must be studied both in cells and in whole plants, or even in populations.

## 1.3. The Role of Plants in the Earth System

### 1.3.1. Oxygen Production and the Evolution of the Atmosphere

The atmosphere of the early Earth contained no free oxygen; it consisted mainly of $\ce{CO2}$ released by volcanoes, water vapor and nitrogen. After cyanobacteria evolved oxygenic photosynthesis, oxygen began to accumulate in the atmosphere. The **Great Oxidation Event**, about 2.4 billion years ago, raised atmospheric oxygen markedly for the first time, and the ozone layer that gradually formed in the upper atmosphere screened out solar ultraviolet radiation and transformed the whole surface environment. The geological evidence includes the following: in an anoxic atmosphere, dissolved $\ce{Fe^2+}$ could be carried along by rivers; once oxygen appeared, it was oxidized to insoluble $\ce{Fe^3+}$ and precipitated, forming banded iron formations and iron-oxide-rich red beds. Land plants appeared about 470 million years ago, and their subsequent large-scale expansion (in particular the forests of the Devonian) raised atmospheric oxygen further and lowered $\ce{CO2}$ substantially, through photosynthesis, the burial of organic carbon and the rock weathering accelerated by roots. The roughly 21% oxygen in today's atmosphere is the result of the long accumulation of photosynthetic organisms; without photosynthesis there would be no aerobic biosphere and no humans.

### 1.3.2. The Carbon Cycle and Primary Production

Plants are the main link in the natural **carbon cycle** that converts inorganic carbon into organic carbon. In ecology, the total carbon assimilated by photosynthetic organisms per unit time is called *gross primary production* (GPP), and the part remaining after subtracting their own respiration is called *net primary production* (NPP), which sets the upper limit of organic matter available to all heterotrophs. Terrestrial plants have a gross primary production of about $120\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$ per year ($1\,\mathrm{Pg} = 10^{15}\,\mathrm{g}$; Beer et al., 2010) and global net primary production is about $105\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$, with land plants and marine phytoplankton contributing roughly half each (56 and $49\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$; Field et al., 1998; [7.12.1](/7.12.1)). The carbon stored in terrestrial vegetation is about $450$–$650\,\mathrm{Pg}$, and more is stored in soils; their annual exchange with the atmosphere is more than ten times the annual emission from human fossil fuel use.

Human activities have perturbed this cycle. According to the Global Carbon Budget 2023 (Friedlingstein et al., 2023), over 2013–2022 fossil fuel and cement emissions averaged $9.6\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$ per year and land-use change (mainly deforestation) $1.3\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$ per year; of this, about $5.2\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$ remained in the atmosphere, the ocean took up about $2.9\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$, and terrestrial ecosystems about $3.3\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$. This net land sink arises mainly from enhanced photosynthesis and forest regrowth; it absorbs about a quarter of anthropogenic emissions, but its size fluctuates strongly from year to year and is highly sensitive to drought, heat and forest fires. Atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$ has risen from about 280 ppm before industrialization to about 419 ppm in 2023 (each 1 ppm corresponds to $2.12\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$ in the atmosphere). How elevated $\ce{CO2}$ affects photosynthesis, stomata and water-use efficiency is one of the central questions of plant physiology in climate change research ([7.13.9](/7.13.9)).

Plants also take part in the cycles of nitrogen, oxygen and other elements. Nitrogen-fixing microbes (including the **rhizobia** that live symbiotically with legumes) convert atmospheric nitrogen to ammonium that plants can take up, and plants then assimilate it into proteins and nucleic acids ([7.13.6](/7.13.6)); through roots, litter and exudates plants contribute to soil formation, turning weathered rock parent material into structured, fertile soil that provides habitat for other organisms.

### 1.3.3. Food, Energy and Medicine

Human food comes, directly or indirectly, from plants. The world population was about 2.5 billion in 1950 and exceeded 8 billion in 2022; the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations estimates that global food production will have to increase by about 60%–70% from 2005–2007 levels by 2050, while arable land is limited and shrinking, and climate anomalies and pests aggravate yield fluctuations. In 2023 about 700 million people worldwide were still chronically undernourished, and "hidden hunger", typified by deficiencies of iron, zinc and vitamin A, affects even more. Improving crop yield and quality is the most direct applied goal of plant physiology.

Crop yield can be decomposed physiologically into several factors. The economic yield $Y$ is the product of the biological yield (total aboveground dry matter) $B$ and the *harvest index* $\mathrm{HI}$, and the biological yield in turn equals the product of the radiation intercepted during the growing season and the efficiency with which it is converted into dry matter (Monteith, 1977):

$$
Y = \mathrm{HI}\times B, \qquad B = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}} \int f_{\mathrm{int}}(t)\,S(t)\,\mathrm{d}t, \tag{1.1}
$$

where $S$ is the incident solar radiation, $f_{\mathrm{int}}$ is the fraction of radiation intercepted by the canopy, and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the **light-use efficiency** (grams of dry matter formed per MJ of radiation intercepted). Eq. (1.1) divides the routes to higher yield into three classes: increasing interception (leaf area index, canopy structure, a longer growing season), raising the conversion efficiency (the efficiency of photosynthesis and respiratory losses, [7.13.9](/7.13.9)), and raising the harvest index (partitioning of assimilates to the harvested organ, [7.13.11](/7.13.11)). The **Green Revolution** of the 1960s relied mainly on the third: semidwarf wheat and rice varieties resist lodging, tolerate fertilizer and allocate more assimilate to grain, raising the harvest index from about 0.3 to about 0.5 ([7.13.28](/7.13.28)). Because the harvest index is now close to its upper limit, further gains depend increasingly on raising photosynthetic efficiency: in theory the maximum efficiencies of converting solar energy to biomass are about 4.6% for C3 and 6.0% for C4 plants, whereas the actual efficiency of field crops over a whole growing season is usually only 1%–2% (Zhu, Long and Ort, 2008).

**Example 1.3.1 (Yield components of wheat)** A wheat cultivar intercepts $1200\,\mathrm{MJ\,m^{-2}}$ of solar radiation during the growing season, with a light-use efficiency of $1.3\,\mathrm{g\,MJ^{-1}}$ and a harvest index of 0.45. By Eq. (1.1), the biological yield is $B = 1.3 \times 1200 = 1560\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}$, that is, $15.6\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$, and the grain yield is $Y = 0.45 \times 15.6 = 7.0\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$. Improving the canopy so that interception increases by 10%, or raising the light-use efficiency by 10%, increases yield by about $0.7\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ in either case; to obtain the same gain through the harvest index, it would have to rise to 0.50.

Plants are also a source of renewable energy. The coal, oil and natural gas used today are mainly the geologically transformed remains of ancient photosynthetic organisms. So-called energy plants are those that produce hydrocarbons close to petroleum in composition, or are rich in fermentable sugars and oils, and can replace fossil fuels; they fall roughly into three groups: hydrocarbon-rich plants (such as *Euphorbia lathyris*, *Euphorbia tirucalli* and the rubber tree), plants rich in carbohydrates used for fermentation to fuel ethanol (sugarcane, sugar beet, cassava, maize, sweet sorghum), and plants rich in oils used for biodiesel (soybean, oilseed rape, oil palm, *Jatropha*, and others). The main problems of biofuels are competition with food crops for land and the high energy input and cost of production; the use of cellulosic feedstocks such as straw, and of microalgae, are directions under study.

Plants also synthesize an extremely diverse array of compounds, many of which are important drugs. Artemisinin, a sesquiterpene lactone isolated from sweet wormwood (*Artemisia annua*), is a first-line drug against malaria, and Tu Youyou received the 2011 Lasker Clinical Medical Research Award and the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for its discovery; paclitaxel, a diterpenoid isolated from yews (*Taxus*), is an important chemotherapeutic for ovarian and breast cancer, among others; harringtonine and homoharringtonine from plum yews (*Cephalotaxus*) are used to treat leukemia. The active ingredients of medicinal plants are mainly secondary metabolites such as alkaloids (ephedrine, hyoscyamine), glycosides (cardiac glycosides, ginsenosides), essential oils and tannins, whose role in the plant is mostly defense ([7.13.26](/7.13.26)). Because the content of these compounds in plants is often very low, tissue culture, metabolic engineering and synthetic biology are used to increase their production. Plants can also serve as bioreactors for pharmaceutical proteins: in 1986 human growth hormone was first expressed in tobacco and sunflower tissue, and in 2012 glucocerebrosidase produced in carrot cells became the first plant-made recombinant protein drug approved for marketing ([7.13.29](/7.13.29)).

### 1.3.4. Plants and Fundamental Discoveries in Biology

The study of plants has also led to many fundamental discoveries in biology. Robert Hooke first saw and named "cells" in thin slices of cork in 1665, and Schleiden (1838) and Schwann (1839) subsequently proposed the **cell theory**; Mendel discovered the laws of inheritance from crosses of peas (1865); tobacco mosaic virus was the first virus to be discovered (1892–1898) and the first to be purified and crystallized (1935); transposons were first discovered by McClintock in maize; and many mechanisms of RNA silencing were first elucidated in plants. These discoveries show that plants are not only useful but also a window onto the general principles of life.

## 1.4. A Brief History of the Discipline

The development of plant physiology can be divided roughly into four periods:

| Period | Characteristics | Representative work |
|---|---|---|
| Gestation (16th–17th centuries) | Quantitative experiments explore the source of plant nutrition | van Helmont's willow experiment (published 1648): over 5 years the willow gained about 74 kg while the soil lost only about 57 g, from which he concluded that the substance of the plant comes from water |
| Foundation and growth (18th–19th centuries) | Discovery and measurement of basic phenomena such as photosynthesis, mineral nutrition and water transport; the disciplinary framework takes shape | Hales, *Vegetable Staticks* (1727), measured transpiration and root pressure; Priestley (1771) found that plants can "restore" air "injured" by animals; Ingenhousz (1779) showed that this requires light and the green parts; Senebier (1782) and de Saussure (1804) showed that plants take up $\ce{CO2}$ and that water takes part in the formation of matter; Liebig's (1840) mineral theory of plant nutrition; solution culture by Sachs and Knop (1860s); Pfeffer (1877) measured osmotic pressure, providing the data for van 't Hoff's law of osmotic pressure; Darwin and Darwin, *The Power of Movement in Plants* (1880); Dixon and Joly's cohesion theory (1894). The textbooks of Sachs and Pfeffer marked plant physiology as an independent discipline |
| Early modern development (20th century) | Discovery of hormones, photoperiodism and photoreceptors; the biochemistry of photosynthesis and respiration; application of biophysical methods | Garner and Allard discovered photoperiodism (1920); Went demonstrated the existence of auxin (1928); Münch's pressure-flow hypothesis (1930); Calvin and colleagues elucidated the Calvin cycle (1950s); Borthwick and Hendricks discovered the red/far-red reversibility (1952), and phytochrome was subsequently isolated (1959); Miller and Skoog discovered kinetin (1955); Mitchell's chemiosmotic theory (1961); Hatch and Slack elucidated the C4 pathway (1966); Scholander's pressure chamber (1965) |
| Rapid development (late 20th century to the present) | Molecular genetics and genomics take research down to genes and proteins; multiscale integration from molecules to ecosystems | Completion of the *Arabidopsis* genome sequence (2000); identification of the auxin receptor TIR1 (2005), the gibberellin receptor GID1 (2005) and the abscisic acid receptors PYR/PYL (2009); confirmation of FT protein as florigen (2007); application of genome editing to crop improvement |

Plant physiology over the past three or four decades has developed four salient characteristics: the levels of study have become richer, from molecules and cells to individuals, populations and ecosystems; disciplines have interpenetrated, so that the field now intersects deeply with molecular biology, biophysics, ecology and computational science; theory is linked with practice, serving high-yielding, high-quality and stress-tolerant crops; and research methods have been modernized, with genomics, imaging, single-cell technologies and mathematical modeling becoming routine tools.

## 1.5. Structure of the Course and Quantitative Methods

### 1.5.1. Structure of the Course

The course has 30 chapters, arranged in the order "acquisition and transformation of resources → signals and development → plants and the environment → agricultural applications":

1. Water and minerals (chapters 2–6): water relations of cells and whole plants, mineral nutrition, transmembrane solute transport, and the assimilation of nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus and iron.
2. Photosynthesis and carbon metabolism (chapters 7–12): light absorption and photoprotection, carbon assimilation in C3, C4 and CAM plants, the physiological ecology of photosynthesis, stomatal biology, phloem transport and the partitioning of assimilates, and respiration.
3. Hormones and light signals (chapters 13–16): synthesis, transport and physiological effects of plant hormones; photoreceptors and photomorphogenesis.
4. Growth and development (chapters 17–24): cell expansion, organogenesis, tropisms, seed dormancy and germination, floral induction, pollination, fertilization and fruit development, senescence and abscission.
5. Plants and the environment (chapters 25–27): interactions of plants with pathogenic microbes, with animals and other organisms, and abiotic stress.
6. Crop science (chapters 28–29): crop domestication and breeding, plant biotechnology and the biosafety of transgenic organisms; chapter 30 summarizes the whole course.

### 1.5.2. Fluxes, Driving Forces and Resistances

A basic view of this course is that many processes in plant physiology can be written as equations, described quantitatively with measured parameters, and predicted by numerical computation. The most frequent type of relation is "flux = conductance × driving force = driving force / resistance", a generalization of **Ohm's law** to physiology:

$$
J = g\,\Delta X = \frac{\Delta X}{r}. \tag{1.2}
$$

Diffusion under **Fick's law**, Hagen–Poiseuille flow in pipes, osmotic water flow across membranes, leaf transpiration and $\ce{CO2}$ uptake, and the current of ions through channels all take this form; resistances in series add and conductances in parallel add, so a complicated pathway can be divided into segments and the limitation each segment imposes on the total flux can be analyzed. We shall use this framework repeatedly from the next chapter on.

![Four kinds of material transport in plants, all of which can be written as "flux = conductance × driving force" (schematic). Water: from the soil through the root, xylem and leaf to the atmosphere; the driving force is the water potential difference in the liquid phase and the water vapor concentration difference in the gas phase (chapters 2, 3, 10). $\ce{CO2}$: from the atmosphere through the boundary layer, stomata and mesophyll to the chloroplast; the driving force is the concentration difference (chapters 9, 10). Sucrose: from the source leaf through phloem loading, sieve tubes and unloading to the sink; the driving force is the osmotically generated pressure difference (chapter 11). Ions: from the soil solution through channels, carriers and pumps into the cytoplasm; the driving force is the electrochemical potential difference (chapters 4, 5). The resistances of the individual segments (zigzag lines) are in series, and the total flux is limited by the largest resistance](./plots/flux-framework.py)

## Questions

1. A C3 crop assimilates $\ce{CO2}$ over the whole growing season equivalent to $1.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ of dry matter per square meter (as $\ce{CH2O}$, molar mass $30\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$), and its transpiration ratio is 400. Estimate the amount of water transpired per square meter during the growing season (expressed as mm of precipitation equivalent).

   **Answer** The carbon assimilated is $1500/30 = 50\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}}$ of $\ce{CO2}$, and the water transpired is $400 \times 50 = 2\times10^4\,\mathrm{mol} = 360\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}}$, that is, $0.36\,\mathrm{m^3\,m^{-2}}$, equivalent to 360 mm of precipitation. This shows why water is so often the first limit on crop yield: in regions where annual precipitation is only a few hundred millimeters, transpiration alone can exhaust a season's rainfall (without even counting soil evaporation).

2. Use Eq. (1.1) to analyze the following: a rice cultivar has a harvest index of 0.5 and a biological yield of $18\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$. A breeder has two options: (a) raise the harvest index to 0.55; (b) raise the light-use efficiency by 15% with the harvest index unchanged. Which gives the larger increase in yield? Which is easier to achieve?

   **Answer** The original yield is $0.5 \times 18 = 9.0\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$. (a) $0.55 \times 18 = 9.9$, a gain of $0.9\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$; (b) $0.5 \times 18 \times 1.15 = 10.35$, a gain of $1.35\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$. Option (b) gives the larger gain. But the harvest index of modern rice is already close to its physiological limit (the stem needs enough biomass to support the panicle and resist lodging), so there is little room to raise it further; raising the light-use efficiency requires improving photosynthesis itself (for example reducing photorespiration, speeding the relaxation of photoprotection, or introducing C4 or $\ce{CO2}$-concentrating mechanisms), which is difficult, but it is currently the main direction of research on crop yield.

3. What is the connection between the "sessility" of plants and their "modular construction and indeterminate growth"? Give two examples of a physiological process that is influenced by both features.

   **Answer** Sessile plants cannot escape the environment and can only adapt by changing their own growth: where to produce new roots, leaves and branches, how many, and when to flower. Modular construction and indeterminate growth make this possible, because the plant body consists of repeated modules that can be added continually or discarded. Examples: (1) the shade-avoidance response: a plant shaded by neighbors senses a reduced red to far-red ratio, promotes stem elongation and suppresses branching, "investing" modules at greater height ([7.13.16](/7.13.16)); (2) root system architecture: roots proliferate lateral roots in nutrient-rich soil patches and reduce branching in poor soil, so that limited carbon is spent where the return is highest ([7.13.4](/7.13.4)); leaf shedding in drought and the release of apical dominance are further examples.

4. In 2013–2022 anthropogenic emissions were about $10.9\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$ per year and the atmosphere gained about $5.2\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$ per year. (a) By how many ppm per year does this raise atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$? (b) The gross primary production of terrestrial plants is about $120\,\mathrm{Pg\,C\,yr^{-1}}$, yet the net land carbon sink is only about $3.3\,\mathrm{Pg\,C\,yr^{-1}}$. Why do these two numbers differ so greatly?

   **Answer** (a) $5.2/2.12 \approx 2.5\,\mathrm{ppm}$ per year. (b) About half of gross primary production is consumed by the plants' own respiration, and the remaining net primary production (about $56\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$) is returned to the atmosphere almost entirely by heterotrophic respiration of animals, fungi and microbes, by the decomposition of litter and soil organic matter, and by fire; the net land carbon sink is a very small difference between these two enormous fluxes, so it is highly sensitive to changes in climate (drought lowering photosynthesis, warming speeding decomposition).

5. (Open question) Choose a physiological process in this course, such as water uptake by roots, stomatal opening and closing, or floral induction, and explain why it requires study at both the molecular level and the whole-plant or population level.

   **Answer** Take stomatal movement as an example. At the molecular level one needs to know how the guard-cell blue-light receptors, plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase, ion channels and abscisic acid signaling change the osmotic potential and turgor of the guard cells ([7.13.10](/7.13.10)). At the level of the leaf and whole plant one needs to know how stomatal conductance simultaneously determines photosynthesis and transpiration and how it is coordinated with xylem hydraulic limitation and root signals so that the leaf does not lose turgor or the xylem embolize ([7.13.3](/7.13.3)). At the canopy and ecosystem level, stomatal behavior determines water-use efficiency and the response of terrestrial carbon and water fluxes to elevated $\ce{CO2}$ and drought ([7.13.9](/7.13.9)). Studying only molecules cannot predict how a plant performs in the field; studying only populations cannot identify the genes and mechanisms that could be improved. The task of plant physiology is precisely to connect these scales.
