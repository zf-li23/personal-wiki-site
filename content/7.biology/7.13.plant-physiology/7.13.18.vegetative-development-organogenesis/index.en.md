The previous chapter dealt with how cells expand; this chapter deals with where cells are and by what pattern new organs are produced. The plant embryo is only an axis with two meristems: the root and shoot apical meristems keep producing roots, leaves, buds and lateral roots throughout the life of the plant, and plant form is the sum of the number, position and growth rate of these organs. The signals that determine all this can be reduced to a few principles. Polar transport of auxin creates concentration maxima in tissues, and the maxima specify the sites of organ initiation. The antagonism between cytokinin and auxin determines the size of meristems. Existing organs inhibit the initiation (phyllotaxis) or growth (apical dominance) of neighboring organs by consuming or exporting auxin. Environmental signals such as nutrients and light change the synthesis and transport of these hormones, so that organ architecture varies plastically with the environment.

The structure and development of meristems, roots, stems and leaves (tunica–corpus, root tip zonation, endogenous origin of lateral roots, the mathematics of phyllotaxis) have been covered in botany ([7.12.4](/7.12.4), [7.12.20](/7.12.20), [7.12.21](/7.12.21), [7.12.23](/7.12.23)); embryo development is in [7.12.30](/7.12.30) and the WUS–CLV3 feedback in [7.12.4](/7.12.4#sec-4.2.5). Here we cover only the physiological and molecular mechanisms that regulate these processes and the models that describe them quantitatively. The chemiosmotic model of **polar auxin transport** and auxin signal transduction are in [7.13.13](/7.13.13), and cytokinin and strigolactone are in [7.13.14](/7.13.14) and [7.13.15](/7.13.15). The general theory of morphogens and self-organized patterns in animal embryos is in developmental biology ([7.5.11](/7.5.11), [7.5.12](/7.5.12)).

## 18.1. Establishment of Polarity in Embryogenesis

### 18.1.1. Polarity

**Definition 18.1.1 (Polarity)** The existence of gradients in morphological structure and physiological and biochemical properties along different axes of a plant organ, tissue or even a single cell is called **polarity**.

Polarity is the first step of differentiation. The angiosperm zygote divides unequally at its first division into a small apical cell and a large basal cell ([7.12.30](/7.12.30)); the apical cell gives rise to most of the embryo proper, and the basal cell gives rise to the suspensor and the hypophysis. Once established, polarity is hard to reverse and is eventually expressed in two basic patterns of the whole plant body.

- The apical–basal axial pattern: shoot apical meristem, cotyledons, hypocotyl, root, root apical meristem, which is maintained from the embryo to the adult plant.
- The radial pattern: in a cross section of root or stem, the epidermis, cortex, endodermis, pericycle and vascular tissue are arranged in concentric rings.

The three morphological stages of *Arabidopsis* embryo development mark the degree of completion of these two patterns. At the globular stage the apical and basal poles have been established and the protoderm (the first layer of the radial pattern) appears; at the heart stage the axial structure has formed and two cotyledon primordia protrude; at the torpedo stage both radial and axial structures have formed, and the regions of the embryo correspond one to one with the parts of the seedling.

### 18.1.2. Auxin Flow Establishes the Apical–Basal Axis

The material basis of polarity in the embryo is the directional flow of auxin (Friml et al. 2003).

1. From the two-cell stage to the early globular embryo: in the basal cell and the suspensor cells derived from it, the auxin efflux carrier PIN7 is located at the apical plasma membrane and pumps auxin from the suspensor toward the embryo proper; an auxin response maximum appears at the apex of the embryo proper and specifies its identity.
2. At about the 32-cell stage: PIN7 shifts to the basal side of the suspensor cells, and PIN1 in the embryo proper also shifts to the basal side of the cells; the auxin flow reverses, from the embryo proper toward the hypophysis, forming a maximum at the future root pole and specifying the position of the root apical meristem and quiescent center.
3. Heart stage: auxin accumulates at the tips of the two cotyledon primordia, and the channels transporting it downward from there become the procambium of the cotyledons and hypocotyl.

The genes involved are as follows. *GNOM* encodes a guanine nucleotide exchange factor for ARF-type small GTPases and is responsible for the directional recycling of PIN1-containing vesicles at the plasma membrane; the embryos of *gnom* mutants lose apical–basal polarity and form globular seedlings lacking both ends. *MONOPTEROS* (*MP*, that is, *ARF5*) encodes an auxin-responsive transcription factor, and *mp* mutants lack the radicle and hypocotyl and have discontinuous vascular tissue. Mutation of *BODENLOS* (*IAA12*) makes it insensitive to auxin-induced degradation, so that it continually inhibits MP and gives a phenotype identical to *mp*. When cultured embryos are treated with the auxin transport inhibitor NPA, or in *pin* multiple mutants, the axial pattern of the embryo is disorganized and the cotyledons fuse into a cup.

### 18.1.3. Polarity of Organs and Cuttings

In adult plants, polarity appears as the difference between the morphological upper and lower ends of an organ. When hardwood cuttings of willow or grape are hung upside down in a moist environment, adventitious roots always form at the morphological lower end and buds at the upper end, irrespective of the direction of gravity; a bamboo segment placed upside down also produces roots from its morphological base. The reason is that polar transport of auxin in the stem is not affected by gravity: auxin is transported from the upper end to the lower end and accumulates there, the auxin-to-cytokinin ratio is higher at the lower end and promotes rooting, and the ratio is lower at the upper end and favors bud formation. Therefore in cuttage, grafting and tissue culture the morphological lower end of the explant must be oriented downward. Root formation requires auxin, and in production the bases of cuttings are treated with IBA or NAA to promote rooting.

## 18.2. The Root Apical Meristem and Root Growth

### 18.2.1. The Quiescent Center and Initial Cells

The root tip of *Arabidopsis* is extremely regular: the **quiescent center** has only 4 cells and is surrounded by four types of initial cells (stem cells), which produce the columella root cap, the epidermis and lateral root cap, the cortex and endodermis, and the vascular cylinder and pericycle, respectively ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)). Experiments in which cells were killed one by one with a laser (van den Berg et al. 1995, 1997) revealed the signaling logic of this system.

- When the quiescent center is killed, the initial cells in contact with it stop dividing and differentiate prematurely: the quiescent center produces a short-range signal that keeps the surrounding initial cells in the stem cell state.
- When a cortical initial cell is killed, a neighboring cell of the vascular cylinder moves into its position and differentiates into cortex according to its new position: cell fate is determined by position rather than lineage.
- The identity of the daughter cells produced by initial cells depends on signals from more mature cells of the same type.

This is similar to the WUS–CLV3 system of the shoot apex: the homeobox gene *WOX5* expressed in the quiescent center is homologous to *WUS* and maintains the undifferentiated state of the distal (columella) initial cells; the small peptide CLE40 secreted by differentiated columella cells limits the expression domain of *WOX5* through the receptor kinase ACR4, forming a negative feedback.

### 18.2.2. The Auxin Maximum and Radial Pattern

The root tip is one of the places with the highest auxin concentration (panel A of the auxin-flow figure in Section 18.2.4). Most auxin comes from the shoot and reaches the root tip by rootward transport through PIN1 in the vascular cylinder; in the columella cells PIN3, PIN4 and PIN7 distribute it laterally to the lateral root cap; uptake by AUX1 and efflux by PIN2 in the lateral root cap and epidermis carry it back toward the elongation zone, and some auxin returns to the vascular cylinder through the cortex. This "reverse fountain" circulation maintains a stable auxin maximum at the quiescent center and the columella cells (Blilou et al. 2005) and allows the root tip to continue growing for some time independently of the supply from the shoot.

The auxin maximum positions the quiescent center: when a chemical treatment shifts the maximum, the quiescent center shifts with it, and when the maximum is eliminated the quiescent center disappears. Auxin concentration is read by a set of transcription factors (the model of Taiz et al.).

1. Auxin-dependent MP (ARF5) and NPH4 (ARF7) induce *PLETHORA* (*PLT1*–*PLT4*, AP2-type transcription factors) expression at the root base; PLT proteins form a gradient decreasing upward from the quiescent center in the root tip (Galinha et al. 2007), with high concentrations maintaining stem cells, intermediate concentrations allowing division and low concentrations allowing differentiation. This is a "French flag"-type system that reads position from a gradient (**positional information**, [7.5.11](/7.5.11)).
2. PLT, together with two other transcription factors, SHORT-ROOT (SHR) and SCARECROW (SCR), determines the position of the quiescent center and induces *WOX5*.

SHR and SCR also establish the radial pattern of the root (Helariutta et al. 2000). Both *shr* and *scr* mutants lack the periclinal division that separates cortex from endodermis and have only a single layer of "ground tissue": the layer in *scr* has properties of both cortex and endodermis, whereas the layer in *shr* has only cortex properties and no Casparian strip. The reason is that SHR mRNA is transcribed only in the vascular cylinder, while SHR protein moves through **plasmodesmata** into the adjacent outer layer of cells, where it induces *SCR*; SHR and SCR form a heterodimer that promotes that periclinal division and starts the endodermal developmental program, and SCR also sequesters SHR in the nucleus so that it moves no farther out. The layer "adjacent to the vascular cylinder that receives SHR" thus becomes the endodermis and the next layer outside becomes the cortex: a mobile transcription factor defines a tissue boundary with single-cell-layer precision.

### 18.2.3. Size of the Meristematic Zone: Cytokinin and Auxin

The meristematic zone (division zone) of the root has a definite length, and the position where cells leave the meristematic zone and enter the elongation zone is called the transition zone. The size of the meristematic zone is determined by the antagonism of two hormones (Dello Ioio et al. 2007, 2008): in the transition zone cytokinin induces *SHY2* (*IAA3*) through the type-B response regulators ARR1 and ARR12, SHY2 inhibits the expression of *PIN* genes and reduces auxin flow to the root tip, promoting cell differentiation; auxin promotes the degradation of SHY2 and maintains division. Applying cytokinin or enhancing its signaling shortens the meristematic zone and the root, whereas overexpression of the cytokinin-degrading CKX lengthens the meristematic zone and makes the root longer. Cytokinin thus promotes differentiation in the root but maintains the meristem at the shoot apex (Section 18.4): the same hormone has opposite effects in the two meristems. Gibberellin promotes division of meristematic zone cells by degrading DELLA proteins, and ethylene inhibits the elongation of cells in the elongation zone.

### 18.2.4. Kinematics of Root Growth

A steadily growing root can be regarded as a stream of cells "flowing" from the meristematic zone through the elongation zone. In coordinates with the root tip as origin and upward positive, the velocity $v(x)$ of each point in the tissue relative to the root tip is close to zero in the meristematic zone, rises rapidly in the elongation zone and reaches the root elongation rate $v_{\max}$ in the mature zone. The gradient of velocity along the root,

$$
\mathrm{REGR}(x) = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}
\tag{18.1}
$$
is called the relative elemental growth rate (in $\mathrm{h^{-1}}$); it gives the local relative elongation rate of tissue at each position and is maximal in the middle of the elongation zone (Erickson and Sax 1956; Silk). At steady state the root elongation rate equals the product of the flux of cells produced and the mature cell length:

$$
v_{\max} = F\,l_{\mathrm{mat}},
\tag{18.2}
$$

where $F$ is the number of cells leaving the meristematic zone per cell file per hour and $l_{\mathrm{mat}}$ is the mature cell length. Equation (18.2) decomposes root growth into two factors that can be regulated separately: cytokinin shortens the meristematic zone and reduces $F$, whereas ethylene, phosphorus deficiency and others mainly reduce $l_{\mathrm{mat}}$.

**Example 18.2.1** With ample water, the elongation rate of the maize primary root is about $3\,\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}$ and the elongation zone is about $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ long (Sharp et al. 1988). If mature cortical cells are $150\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long, by Eq. (18.2) each cortical cell file must produce $F = 3000/150 = 20$ cells per hour. If REGR is approximately constant within the elongation zone, its mean value is $v_{\max}/L_{\mathrm{el}} = 3/10 = 0.3\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$, that is, the tissue in the elongation zone lengthens by 30 % per hour, far higher than diffuse growth of stems; the time for a cell to cross the elongation zone is about $\ln(l_{\mathrm{mat}}/l_0)/\mathrm{REGR}$, and if the cell elongates from about $15\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ to $150\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, it takes $\ln 10/0.3 \approx 7.7\,\mathrm{h}$.

## 18.3. Initiation of Lateral Roots

**Lateral roots** are the main way in which the root system increases its absorptive area. They originate from the pericycle of the mature zone of the parent root, opposite the protoxylem poles in most angiosperm groups ([7.12.20](/7.12.20#sec-20.4)). The development of the *Arabidopsis* lateral root primordium is divided into 8 stages (Malamy and Benfey 1997): two adjacent pericycle "founder cells" first divide asymmetrically anticlinally (stage I), then divide periclinally to form two and three layers (stages II–IV), the primordium becomes dome-shaped and passes through the endodermis, cortex and epidermis (stages V–VII), and finally emerges from the parent root (stage VIII), after which it activates its own meristem.

### 18.3.1. Auxin and the Root Clock

Every step of lateral root initiation requires auxin. Local auxin signaling in pericycle cells releases ARF7 and ARF19 through degradation of SOLITARY-ROOT (SLR/IAA14), and these induce transcription factors such as LBD16 and LBD29 to start the asymmetric division; *slr* (IAA14 that cannot be degraded) and the *arf7 arf19* double mutant have almost no lateral roots. Auxin from the shoot mainly affects the initiation of lateral roots, whereas auxin synthesized by the root itself is more important for the emergence of the primordium. When the primordium emerges, the overlying endodermal cells must make room (the endodermis is locked in place by the Casparian strip and must change shape), while the outer cortical and epidermal cells express influx carriers such as LAX3 and wall-degrading enzymes in response to auxin, so that the cells separate from one another (Swarup et al. 2008).

Lateral roots are regularly arranged along the parent root. In the "oscillation zone" near the root tip of *Arabidopsis*, expression of the auxin response reporter (*DR5*) oscillates with a period of about 6 h (Moreno-Risueño et al. 2010; Taiz et al.); each oscillation peak leaves a "prebranch site" at a position farther up the oscillation zone, where a lateral root primordium later arises. This "root clock" is logically the same as the clock-and-wavefront model of vertebrate somite formation ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)): a periodic clock combined with advancing growth converts a period in time into a period in space.

**Example 18.3.1** From the clock-and-wavefront relation, the spacing of prebranch sites is $\lambda = vT$. If the elongation rate of the *Arabidopsis* primary root is $0.2\,\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}$ and the oscillation period is $T = 6\,\mathrm{h}$, then $\lambda = 1.2\,\mathrm{mm}$; if low temperature halves the elongation rate while the period is unchanged, the spacing is halved and the number of prebranch sites per unit root length doubles. The actual distribution of lateral roots is also regulated by bending and nutrients, as discussed below.

### 18.3.2. Other Regulation of Lateral Root Formation

- Mechanical bending: when roots bend in gravitropism or are bent artificially, lateral roots always form on the outer side of the bend, and the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ spike and local auxin accumulation caused by bending start initiation.
- Hormones: ethylene and cytokinin inhibit initiation of lateral roots, and abscisic acid inhibits emergence of primordia that have already been initiated (lateral root development pauses in drought).
- Inhibition by the primary root: lateral roots always form beyond a certain distance from the root tip, and cutting the primary root promotes the formation and growth of lateral roots; "pot shifting" in vegetable seedling production and cutting the taproot at transplanting both make use of this.
- Nutrients: local nitrate and phosphate (Section 18.7).

## 18.4. The Shoot Apical Meristem

The composition of the shoot apical meristem and the WUS–CLV3 feedback have been described in [7.12.4](/7.12.4). Stem cells of the central zone (CZ) express *CLV3*, the organizing center beneath expresses *WUS*, WUS protein moves through plasmodesmata into the central zone and induces *CLV3*, and the CLV3 peptide inhibits *WUS* through receptors such as CLV1, keeping the size of the stem cell pool constant; cells of the peripheral zone (PZ) divide rapidly and produce leaf primordia and axillary buds; the rib zone (RZ) produces the internal tissues of the stem; by cell layer the meristem is divided into L1, L2 and L3. Here we add the distribution and actions of hormones in the shoot apex (Besnard et al. 2011; Taiz et al.).

| Hormone | Distribution in the shoot apex | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Cytokinin** | Signal strongest in and around the organizing center | WUS directly represses type-A response regulators (*ARR5*, *ARR7* and others, negative feedback elements of cytokinin signaling), raising the sensitivity of the central zone to cytokinin; cytokinin in turn promotes *WUS* expression, forming a positive feedback; *KNOX*-type homeobox genes (such as *STM*) induce cytokinin synthesis |
| **Gibberellin** | Low in the central zone and organizing center, high in leaf primordia | KNOX proteins repress *GA20ox* and induce *GA2ox*, keeping gibberellin low in the shoot apex and cells undifferentiated; in leaf primordia *KNOX* is switched off, gibberellin rises, and cells begin to differentiate and expand |
| **Auxin** | Low in the central zone, with maxima at convergence points in the peripheral zone | Specifies the positions of leaf primordia (Section 18.5); at the boundary between primordium and shoot apex auxin is low and *CUC* genes are expressed, separating the organs |

Cytokinin maintains the meristem at the shoot apex but promotes differentiation in the root (Section 18.2.3); this opposition makes growth of root and shoot apex respond oppositely to the same signal: when cytokinin is high the plant tends to develop the shoot, and when it is low the root system, consistent with the functional balance of the root–shoot ratio ([7.13.17](/7.13.17#sec-17.8)); for example, under nitrogen deficiency cytokinin synthesis in roots decreases, which favors root growth.

Initiation of leaf primordia also requires light: the shoot apices of tomato and *Arabidopsis* stop producing new leaf primordia in darkness, accompanied by decreased auxin synthesis and loss of polar localization of PIN1; recovery requires the simultaneous application of auxin and cytokinin, and **phytochrome** B is the main photoreceptor (Taiz et al.).

## 18.5. Leaf Primordium Initiation and Phyllotaxis

### 18.5.1. Auxin Maxima Specify Primordium Position

New leaf primordia arise in the peripheral zone of the shoot apex at positions far from existing primordia (Hofmeister's rule), and the resulting spiral phyllotaxis has a divergence angle close to the golden angle $137.5^\circ$ (for the mathematics of **phyllotaxis** see [7.12.21](/7.12.21#sec-21.4)). It is auxin that determines primordium position (Reinhardt et al. 2000, 2003).

- In tomato shoot apices cultured on medium containing the auxin transport inhibitor NPA, and in the inflorescence apex of *pin1* mutants, the peripheral zone no longer produces organs and a naked, needle-like apex forms ("pin-formed").
- When a small drop of auxin (IAA in lanolin) is placed on the side of such a naked apex, a primordium (leaf or flower) grows out at the drop within a day or two, and its size is related to the amount of auxin.
- Observed with a PIN1 fluorescent fusion protein, PIN1 in L1 layer cells all points toward one neighboring point, where auxin accumulates; with an auxin response reporter the auxin maximum is seen to precede morphological outgrowth of the primordium (Heisler et al. 2005).
- Once a primordium has formed, it acts as a sink, draws away the surrounding auxin and conducts it downward into the procambium, creating a zone of auxin depletion around itself in which no new maximum can form for a time (panel B of the auxin-flow figure in Section 18.2.4).

These results translate the "inhibitory field" of Hofmeister's rule into molecular terms: the inhibition of neighboring regions by an existing primordium is auxin depletion. There are two types of model for how the polarity of PIN1 in the L1 layer comes to point toward the convergence point. One assumes that each cell places PIN1 on the side facing the neighbor with the higher auxin concentration ("up-the-gradient" transport; Jönsson et al. 2006; Smith et al. 2006), so that any small concentration fluctuation is amplified by positive feedback and evenly spaced maxima form spontaneously, equivalent to a Turing-type local activation and long-range inhibition (**lateral inhibition**, [7.5.12](/7.5.12)). The other assumes that PIN1 is placed on the side with the largest auxin outflow ("with-the-flux", that is, canalization, Section 18.5.3). The real shoot apex may use both mechanisms: the surface layer uses up-the-gradient transport to form maxima, and the inner layers use with-the-flux transport to form channels leading to the vasculature. Cytokinin also contributes to the timing of primordia: auxin in the primordium induces diffusion of AHP6, an inhibitor of cytokinin signaling, to its surroundings, forming a second inhibitory field that makes primordium initiation more regular in time (Besnard et al. 2014).

### 18.5.2. The Inhibitory Field Model and the Golden Angle

Douady and Couder (1996) used a discrete model with only two rules to show how the golden angle arises from mutual inhibition among primordia (see the figure below).

1. At every time interval $T$ (the plastochron) a new primordium is produced on the circumference at the edge of a shoot apex of radius $R_0$; existing primordia move radially outward as the apex grows, and the radius increases by a factor of $\mathrm{e}^{G}$ in each plastochron.
2. The new primordium appears where the inhibition caused by existing primordia is smallest, that is, at the angle $\theta$ that minimizes

$$
E(\theta) = \sum_j \frac{1}{d_j(\theta)^{3}},
\tag{18.3}
$$

where $d_j$ is the distance from this point on the circumference to the $j$th existing primordium.

The only parameter in the model is the dimensionless $G$, which measures the growth of the apex in one plastochron relative to the spacing of primordia (large $G$ means slow primordium initiation or a fast-growing apex; small $G$ means frequent, crowded initiation). Numerical simulation (panel A of the figure below) gives the following.

- For $G$ greater than about 1, a new primordium always appears opposite the previous one, with a divergence angle of $180^\circ$, that is, distichous alternate phyllotaxis (grasses).
- As $G$ decreases, the $180^\circ$ solution becomes unstable, the divergence angle decreases continuously, and the system enters spiral phyllotaxis through a bifurcation. From different initial arrangements the system falls onto different branches: the main branch tends to $137.5^\circ$ (successive parastichy numbers are Fibonacci numbers), and the secondary branch tends to about $99.5^\circ$ (parastichies of the Lucas numbers 1, 3, 4, 7, 11, …, rare in nature).
- The more crowded the primordia (the smaller $G$), the larger the numbers of visible parastichy pairs ($(3, 5) \to (5, 8) \to (8, 13)$ …), which is exactly why the number of parastichies changes from the center to the edge of a sunflower head.

If two primordia are produced at the same time in each plastochron, the same rules give decussate opposite phyllotaxis. The type of phyllotaxis therefore depends on the ratio of primordium size, apex size and rate of primordium initiation, which is supported by mutants: the maize *abphyl1* (*abph1*) mutant has an enlarged shoot apex and its phyllotaxis changes from distichous alternate to decussate opposite (Jackson and Hake 1999), and *ABPH1* encodes a cytokinin-induced type-A response regulator; in *Arabidopsis* *clv* mutants the shoot apex is enlarged, and flowers on the inflorescence are arranged more densely with more parastichies.

### 18.5.3. Canalization of Vasculature

After a leaf primordium has formed, the auxin it exports downward induces differentiation of procambium, and the leaf trace connects to the existing vascular bundles in the stem. Sachs (1981), on the basis of experiments on vascular regeneration at wounds, proposed that auxin flowing through a tissue promotes and polarizes its own transport, and the transport is gradually "canalized" into a few cell files that later differentiate into vascular tissue.

**Definition 18.5.1 (Auxin Canalization)** The process in which auxin, through positive feedback, concentrates its own transport into narrow cell files and induces these files to differentiate into vascular tissue is called **auxin canalization**; this explanation is called the canalization hypothesis.

The evidence is as follows. When a vascular bundle in a cucumber or pea stem is severed and auxin is applied above the cut, new vascular tissue grows downward from the application site as a narrow strand rather than a broad sheet and bypasses the wound to connect with the vascular bundle below. In developing leaves, the distribution of PIN1 foreshadows the position and direction of future veins (Scarpella et al. 2006). Existing vascular bundles act as auxin sinks that guide the direction of new leaf traces: when the lower leaf primordium is removed, the new leaf trace connects instead to a vascular bundle on the other side of the stem (Taiz et al.). Canalization together with up-the-gradient transport in the shoot apex can explain most patterns from phyllotaxis to leaf venation. Note that "canalization" in the canalization hypothesis is a different concept from developmental **canalization** in Waddington's sense (buffering of the phenotype against genetic and environmental perturbation).

## 18.6. Branching and Apical Dominance

**Apical dominance** ([7.12.24](/7.12.24#sec-24.6)) keeps the axillary buds of a plant dormant and determines plant architecture. In plants with strong apical dominance (sunflower, maize, sorghum, jute) there is generally no branching; in those with fairly strong dominance (cedar, dawn redwood) the lateral branches nearer the apex are inhibited more strongly, producing a pagoda-shaped crown; in those with weak dominance (willow, shrubs, cereals at the tillering stage) branching is abundant. The fate of axillary buds is decided in two steps: first the formation of the axillary meristem (which requires genes such as *LAS*; most leaf axils of the tomato *lateral suppressor* mutant have no axillary bud), and then whether an already formed bud "grows or not", which is decided by the signaling network below (see the network diagram in Section 18.6.4).

### 18.6.1. Auxin: The Classical Experiments and Their Difficulty

Thimann and Skoog (1933–1934) demonstrated the action of auxin: after decapitation of broad bean, the axillary buds sprout, and applying auxin to the cut surface in place of the apical bud keeps the axillary buds inhibited; applying an auxin transport inhibitor to the stem releases the buds below it from apical dominance. Mutants with reduced rootward transport branch more.

But auxin does not act directly on the bud: radiolabeled auxin applied to the stem apex does not enter the axillary bud, the auxin concentration in the bud actually rises after decapitation, and the auxin transported downward acts on the stem and not on the bud. The "hormone hypothesis" of Chinese textbooks (auxin synthesized in the apical bud is transported to the lateral buds, and the excess auxin in the lateral buds inhibits them) therefore needs correction: auxin in the stem controls axillary buds indirectly through second messengers. Another old explanation is the "nutrient hypothesis" (Goebel 1900): the apical bud is a strong metabolic sink and monopolizes nutrients. This hypothesis was long considered outdated and is now regaining attention in the form of sugar signaling (Section 18.6.3).

### 18.6.2. Second Messengers: Strigolactone and Cytokinin

**Strigolactones** are branching-inhibiting hormones discovered in the study of highly branched mutants (Gomez-Roldan et al. 2008; Umehara et al. 2008). The pea *rms*, *Arabidopsis* *max* (*more axillary growth*), rice *d* (dwarf, high tillering) and petunia *dad* mutants branch profusely; they either cannot synthesize strigolactone (MAX3/CCD7, MAX4/CCD8, D27, MAX1) or cannot perceive it (the receptor D14 and the F-box protein MAX2). Grafting experiments (Taiz et al.) show that when the shoot of a synthesis mutant is grafted onto wild-type roots, branching returns to normal, so this signal can be transported upward from the root; but a wild-type shoot grafted onto the root of a synthesis mutant also branches normally, so the strigolactone that inhibits axillary buds usually comes from the shoot itself; roots of the perception mutant *max2* restore the shoot of a synthesis mutant, showing that they produce the signal but cannot perceive it.

**Cytokinin** applied directly to axillary buds promotes their growth and is the signal that releases apical dominance. After decapitation, expression of the cytokinin synthesis genes *IPT1* and *IPT2* at pea nodes rises within hours; excised stem segments express them only in the absence of auxin; and when the auxin transport inhibitor TIBA is applied to an internode, *IPT* expression below it rises (Tanaka et al. 2006). The cytokinin that releases apical dominance is therefore synthesized locally at the node, and auxin in the stem constantly inhibits its synthesis.

The two pathways converge on the transcription factor BRANCHED1 in the axillary bud (BRC1; TB1 in maize, FC1 in rice): auxin promotes strigolactone synthesis at the node (inducing *MAX4*), strigolactone induces *BRC1* and inhibits cytokinin synthesis; cytokinin inhibits *BRC1*; and BRC1 keeps the axillary bud dormant (Aguilar-Martínez et al. 2007). In the domestication of maize, upregulated expression of *TB1* turned the highly branched teosinte into modern maize with a single main stalk ([7.13.28](/7.13.28)).

In addition there is the "auxin export competition" model (Bennett et al. 2006; Prusinkiewicz et al. 2009): for an axillary bud to start growing, it must export the auxin it produces into the main transport stream of the stem through canalization; when the auxin flow in the stem is already saturated, the bud cannot establish an export channel and so remains dormant; strigolactone lowers transport capacity by promoting endocytosis of PIN1 from the plasma membrane, which intensifies this competition. The two models are not mutually exclusive.

### 18.6.3. Sugar: The Signal That Triggers Bud Outgrowth

How soon after decapitation do axillary buds begin to grow? In pea, buds begin to grow about 2.5 h after decapitation, about 24 h before a detectable decline in auxin concentration appears in the stem adjacent to the bud; and within 2 h after decapitation, leaf photosynthate labeled with $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ has already decreased in the stem near the bud because it has been taken up by the bud (Mason et al. 2014; Taiz et al.). What triggers bud outgrowth is therefore an increase in sugar supply: decapitation removes the strongest sink, and sucrose that would otherwise flow to the stem tip quickly flows into the axillary bud; auxin depletion comes more slowly and mainly affects whether the bud can keep growing, especially buds farther from the apex. In this sense apical dominance is a source–sink problem (**sink strength**, [7.13.11](/7.13.11)), and the central idea of the nutrient hypothesis thus gains molecular evidence. Sucrose also inhibits expression of *BRC1* and counteracts the action of strigolactone.

### 18.6.4. Environmental and Practical Control

- Light quality: shading lowers the R:FR ratio and inhibits branching through phytochrome B and *BRC1*, which is part of **shade avoidance** ([7.13.16](/7.13.16)); crops at high density tiller less.
- Nutrients: under nitrogen or phosphorus deficiency strigolactone synthesis in roots and shoots increases and branching decreases, concentrating resources on the main stem and existing branches; strigolactone is also exuded by roots into the rhizosphere and promotes establishment of **arbuscular mycorrhiza** ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)), and both actions serve nutrient acquisition.
- Production: topping and pruning of cotton and tomato, pinching of melons (promoting lateral vines and female flowers), "pinching" of chrysanthemum to fill out the plant; pruning of fruit trees changes the apical dominance of branches; timber forests protect the leader and fiber crops are planted densely to maintain apical dominance; tiller number of cereals is an important component of yield, and rice *MOC1* and *IPA1* (ideal plant architecture gene) regulate tillering, the latter reducing unproductive tillers and thickening stems through *OsTB1* (Jiao et al. 2010).

## 18.7. Plasticity of Root System Architecture

### 18.7.1. Root System Architecture

**Definition 18.7.1 (Root System Architecture)** The geometric arrangement of the root system in the three-dimensional space of the soil, that is, the sum of the number, length, angle, branching order and density of the various root types, is called **root system architecture**.

Root system architecture differs between monocots and dicots ([7.12.20](/7.12.20)). The root system of a maize seedling consists of the primary root formed from the radicle, seminal roots on the scutellar node, and crown roots (nodal roots) emerging later from the nodes of the stem base; the root system of the adult plant is mainly crown roots. The root system of a dicot consists of the primary root (taproot) and its lateral roots of various orders, basal roots, and adventitious roots on the hypocotyl or underground stem. Even in the same habitat, the root system architectures of different species differ greatly; for example, in the North American tallgrass prairie some grasses root deeper than 2 m and some forbs have shallow, dense root systems, and they share resources in the soil profile. Root system architecture is determined by the initiation and growth of lateral roots and by the growth rate and angle of roots (the gravitropic set-point angle, [7.13.19](/7.13.19)), all of which are highly plastic with respect to the distribution of nutrients and water.

### 18.7.2. Nutrient Patches and Lateral Root Proliferation

Nutrients are very unevenly distributed in the soil, and roots proliferate locally in nutrient-rich patches. Drew (1975) divided the root system of barley into upper, middle and lower segments and supplied high concentrations of nitrate, phosphate or ammonium to the middle segment only; the number and length of lateral roots in the middle segment increased greatly, lateral root growth in the upper and lower segments was inhibited, and growth of the whole plant was similar to that with uniform supply (see the figure below). In *Arabidopsis* this local effect of nitrate is mediated by several components.

- NRT1.1 (NPF6.3) is both a dual-affinity nitrate transporter and a nitrate sensor (a "transceptor"): at low nitrate it transports auxin and moves auxin out of the lateral root primordium, inhibiting primordium growth; at high nitrate this auxin transport is inhibited, auxin accumulates in the primordium, and the lateral root grows (Krouk et al. 2010).
- The transcription factor ANR1 (MADS box) mediates lateral root elongation in nitrate patches (Zhang and Forde 1998).
- Systemic signals: when the whole plant is nitrogen-sufficient, lateral root growth is generally inhibited; the nitrogen-deficient part of the root system reports "starvation" to the shoot through the small peptide CEP, and the shoot then, through a phloem-mobile signal (CEPD), makes the roots in the nitrogen-rich part increase nitrate uptake, so that the plant divides labor at the root level in "foraging".

The changes caused by phosphorus deficiency are different (Taiz et al.; Lynch): primary root elongation is inhibited (the meristem is exhausted when the root tip meets the combination of free iron and low phosphorus), the number and length of lateral roots increase, root hairs become denser and longer, the growth angle of basal roots becomes shallower, and more roots are distributed in the topsoil where phosphorus is most abundant ("topsoil foraging"); at the same time roots secrete organic acids and phosphatases, increase phosphate transporters and depend on mycorrhiza. The systemic signals of phosphorus deficiency include miR399 transported to roots in the phloem, which inhibits a ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (PHO2) that degrades phosphate transporters and thereby enhances phosphorus uptake by roots. "Phosphorus-efficient" bean and soybean cultivars bred on the basis of these findings obtain more phosphorus in low-phosphorus soils. Nutrient uptake and transport are covered in [7.13.4](/7.13.4) and [7.13.5](/7.13.5).

Water also has local effects on root system architecture: the side of a root surface in contact with moist soil tends to initiate lateral roots ("hydropatterning"), root tips pause branching while crossing dry soil gaps ("xerobranching"), and roots also bend toward moisture (hydrotropism, [7.13.19](/7.13.19)).

## 18.8. Correlations of Growth

The parts of a plant are interdependent and mutually constraining through the supply of and competition for nutrients (downward transport of leaf photosynthate, upward transport of water and minerals from the roots) and through the transmission of signaling substances.

**Definition 18.8.1 (Correlations of Growth)** The phenomenon of mutual constraint and coordination of growth among the parts of a plant is called **growth correlation**.

Growth correlations appear mainly in three respects.

1. Correlation between the underground and aboveground parts: see the root–shoot ratio and functional balance ([7.13.17](/7.13.17#sec-17.8)); in drought, abscisic acid synthesized in roots is carried through the xylem to the shoot, reduces stomatal aperture and inhibits leaf expansion, a typical example of communication between root and shoot.
2. Correlation between the main stem and lateral branches: apical dominance (Section 18.6); there are similar phenomena in the root system, where lateral roots form only at a certain distance from the root tip and growth of the primary root inhibits lateral roots.
3. Correlation between vegetative growth and reproductive growth (below).

**Definition 18.8.2 (Vegetative Growth and Reproductive Growth)** The growth of vegetative organs such as roots, stems and leaves is called **vegetative growth**; the growth of reproductive organs such as flowers, fruits and seeds is called **reproductive growth**.

Vegetative and reproductive growth are interdependent: most of the nutrients required for growth of reproductive organs come from vegetative organs, and if vegetative organs grow poorly, flowers and fruits are necessarily few and small; the fertilized ovary and developing seeds produce auxin, gibberellin and other hormones, which also affect the metabolism of vegetative organs. The two are also mutually constraining.

- Excessive vegetative growth consumes too many nutrients, delays the reproductive phase, and reduces flowers and fruits and makes them liable to drop; the crop "stays green and matures late", as in the shedding of cotton squares and bolls and in fruit trees that grow only branches and leaves and bear no fruit.
- Excessive reproductive growth inhibits vegetative growth and accelerates senescence of the vegetative body: in annual crops the vegetative organs gradually die after flowering and fruiting, and removal of flowers and pods of soybean markedly delays leaf senescence ([7.13.24](/7.13.24)); when a fruit tree bears too heavily in one year, nutrient accumulation that year is insufficient, flower buds are few the next year, and "biennial bearing" (alternate bearing) results, and in production flowers and fruits are thinned to keep the load moderate each year.
- In perennial monocarpic plants such as bamboo and agave, flowering and fruiting occur in one burst after many years of vegetative growth, after which the whole plant dies, an extreme form of the relation between vegetative and reproductive growth.

The principle of regulation is to coordinate the two according to the harvested organ. For leafy vegetables, bast-fiber crops and sugarcane, harvested for vegetative organs, vegetative growth is promoted and flowering delayed or suppressed; for crops harvested for fruits or seeds, enough vegetative growth is needed as a basis in the early stage, and rank growth is prevented later so that assimilates are allocated to the grain (harvest index). For the rules of assimilate partitioning between source and sink see [7.13.11](/7.13.11).

## 18.9. Totipotency, Regeneration and the Physiology of Tissue Culture

### 18.9.1. Totipotency and Regeneration

The **totipotency** and **dedifferentiation** of plant cells ([7.3.24](/7.3.24), [7.12.3](/7.12.3)) give plants a capacity for regeneration that animals lack: a begonia leaf, a willow twig or a piece of carrot root phloem can grow into a complete plant. There are three main routes of regeneration.

| Route | Process | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue repair | Cells at the wound divide and restore the local tissue | Graft union, regeneration of vasculature (Section 18.5.3) |
| Organ regeneration (organogenesis) | Adventitious roots and shoots form directly or through **callus** | Rooting of cuttings, leaf cuttings, shoot and root formation in tissue culture |
| Somatic embryogenesis | Somatic cells pass through globular, heart and torpedo stages like a zygotic embryo and form embryoids | Carrot suspension cells (Steward 1958), seedling production of many forest trees and of coffee |

The direction of organ regeneration in tissue culture is determined by the ratio of auxin to cytokinin (Skoog and Miller 1957, [7.13.14](/7.13.14)): roots form at a high ratio, shoots at a low ratio, and at an intermediate ratio callus only proliferates without differentiating. This empirical rule now has a molecular explanation (Sugimoto et al. 2010 and later work).

1. On callus induction medium containing high auxin, callus is not a "disorganized, dedifferentiated" mass of cells but originates from pericycle or pericycle-like cells and forms along the lateral root developmental program, expressing genes of the root apical meristem (*PLT*, *SCR*, *WOX5*), so that even callus induced from leaves or petals has "root identity"; regenerative capacity (competence) is acquired at this stage.
2. After transfer to shoot induction medium with high cytokinin, type-B response regulators (ARR1, ARR10, ARR12) directly bind and activate the *WUS* promoter, establishing the organizing center of a shoot apical meristem in the callus and producing adventitious shoots.
3. The wound itself is a signal: the wound-induced transcription factor WIND1 promotes cytokinin signaling and callus formation; jasmonic acid and local auxin accumulation at the wound also take part.

This also explains why, in some species that regenerate poorly (most cereals and legumes), expressing developmental regulators (such as BABY BOOM and WUS2, or the GRF4–GIF1 fusion protein) markedly increases the efficiency of regeneration and genetic transformation ([7.13.29](/7.13.29)).

### 18.9.2. Conditions and Applications of Tissue Culture

*Plant tissue culture* (for its definition and micropropagation see [7.12.24](/7.12.24#sec-24.7.4)) is the technique of inoculating isolated organs, tissues or cells (explants) onto artificial medium under sterile conditions so that they proliferate, differentiate and regenerate into plants. The process is: explant → dedifferentiation → callus → redifferentiation → shoots, roots and other organs → plantlet → regenerated plant. The composition of the medium is as follows.

| Component | Content | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Inorganic nutrients | All macro- and micronutrients (MS medium has high ammonium and nitrate content) | Mineral nutrition |
| Carbon source | Generally sucrose, about $30\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ | Explants mostly cannot photosynthesize adequately; also adjusts osmotic potential |
| Vitamins | Vitamins $\mathrm{B_1}$, $\mathrm{B_6}$, nicotinic acid, inositol and others | Coenzymes |
| Plant growth regulators | Auxins (IAA, NAA, 2,4-D) and cytokinins (kinetin, 6-BA, zeatin) | Determine the direction of proliferation and differentiation |
| Organic supplements | Amino acids, casein hydrolysate, coconut water and others | Supply organic nitrogen and unknown growth factors |
| Gelling agent | Agar or gellan gum (omitted in liquid culture) | Supports the explant |

The main applications are as follows: rapid propagation (orchids, banana, potato, strawberry, many ornamentals and forest trees); production of virus-free plants by shoot tip culture; artificial seeds (somatic embryos encapsulated in nutrient gel); haploid breeding (anther or microspore culture, with doubling to give homozygous diploids); somatic hybridization (**protoplast** fusion) and selection of somaclonal variants; rescue of endangered plants and in vitro preservation of germplasm; cell culture production of medicinal plants and secondary metabolites (such as paclitaxel from yew cell culture); and use as a regeneration platform for genetic transformation ([7.13.29](/7.13.29)).

## Questions

1. The *shr* mutant has a single layer of ground tissue and no Casparian strip, and the layer in the *scr* mutant has properties of both cortex and endodermis. (a) Explain the difference between the two phenotypes in terms of the movement of SHR protein between cells. (b) Predict how the radial pattern of the root would change if SHR were expressed in all cells and SCR could be induced in any cell.

   **Answer** (a) SHR is transcribed in the vascular cylinder, and the protein moves through plasmodesmata into the adjacent layer and induces *SCR*; SHR–SCR promotes the periclinal division that separates cortex from endodermis, and SHR itself starts the endodermal program (Casparian strip and so on). In *scr* SHR still reaches that layer and can start some endodermal features, but without SCR the division does not occur, so a single layer has both properties; in *shr* neither the induction of division nor the endodermal program occurs, and that layer shows only the default cortex properties. (b) When SHR is everywhere and can induce SCR, SCR would again sequester SHR in the nucleus without limiting its range; multiple extra periclinal divisions and several layers of cells with endodermal features would be expected (in experiments, broadening SHR expression in the ground tissue did produce multiple endodermis-like layers), which shows that the single-layer precision comes from the unidirectional movement of SHR and its sequestration by SCR.

2. A maize root elongates at $2.4\,\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}$ and mature cortical cells are $120\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long. (a) Find the rate of cell production per cortical cell file. (b) Under moderate drought the root elongation rate falls to $1.2\,\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}$ and mature cell length falls only to $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$; how much do cell production and cell elongation each contribute? (c) If the elongation zone is also measured to shorten from $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ to $5\,\mathrm{mm}$, how does the mean REGR in the elongation zone change?

   **Answer** (a) By Eq. (18.2), $F = 2400/120 = 20$ cells per hour. (b) Under drought $F = 1200/100 = 12\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$; the ratio of elongation rates is $0.5 = (12/20) \times (100/120) = 0.60 \times 0.833$: cell production falls to 60 % and cell length to 83 %, and the former contributes more (taking logarithms, $\ln 0.6/\ln 0.5 = 74\,\%$). (c) Mean REGR $= v_{\max}/L_{\mathrm{el}}$: $2.4/10 = 0.24\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$ becomes $1.2/5 = 0.24\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$, unchanged. This is exactly the measured result (Sharp et al.): at low water potential elongation near the root tip is maintained, and what decreases is mainly elongation in the distal part of the elongation zone, so the elongation zone shortens.

3. Using the Douady–Couder model, explain (a) why mutants with an enlarged shoot apex (such as maize *abph1*) have altered phyllotaxis, and (b) why the pair of parastichy numbers on a sunflower head changes from $(21, 34)$ to $(34, 55)$ and $(55, 89)$ from the center outward while the divergence angle stays the same.

   **Answer** (a) What determines phyllotaxis in the model is the ratio of primordium spacing to apex size (the parameter $G$ and the size of primordia relative to $R_0$). An enlarged apex with unchanged primordium size makes primordia relatively smaller and more crowded along the edge of the apex, which can accommodate two positions of weakest mutual inhibition at once, and the system jumps from distichous alternate phyllotaxis, with one primordium per time, to decussate opposite phyllotaxis, with two primordia per time; *ABPH1* encodes a cytokinin-induced type-A response regulator, and in the mutant cytokinin signaling is enhanced and the apex is enlarged. (b) The rate of floret primordium initiation and the enlargement of the head make primordia relatively smaller and smaller with respect to the radius at which they lie (equivalent to a smaller and smaller $G$), and the divergence angle stays near the golden angle, but as the relative density of primordia increases, the closest neighbors change to primordia whose numbers differ by larger Fibonacci numbers, so the visible parastichy pairs shift toward larger Fibonacci numbers (Eq. (21.2) of [7.12.21](/7.12.21)).

4. Design an experiment to distinguish two explanations of apical dominance: axillary buds sprout after decapitation because of (a) depletion of auxin in the stem or (b) an increase in sugar flowing to the bud. State the predictions of each.

   **Answer** Outline (after Mason et al. 2014): decapitate pea plants and measure simultaneously, with high time resolution, (1) the growth of the axillary bud (digital measurement of length every 30 min); (2) the auxin content (mass spectrometry) and auxin response reporter in the stem segment adjacent to the bud; and (3) after labeling the leaves with $^{14}\mathrm{CO_2}$, the partitioning of radioactive sugar to the bud and the stem near the bud. If (a) is correct, bud outgrowth should come later than the fall of auxin in the stem, and later in buds farther from the cut (auxin depletion advances downward at the speed of polar transport, of the order of $1\,\mathrm{cm\,h^{-1}}$); if (b) is correct, bud outgrowth should coincide with the increase in sugar partitioning and precede the fall of auxin. Supplementary experiments: apply auxin to the cut immediately after decapitation (if it cannot prevent the early outgrowth, this supports (b)); without decapitation, supply sucrose directly to the axillary bud or increase its sugar supply by girdling leaves (if this induces outgrowth, it supports (b)); remove leaves (reducing the source of sugar) before decapitating (if outgrowth is delayed, this supports (b)). The actual results support (b) as responsible for initiation and (a) for sustained growth.

5. A leaf explant in tissue culture forms callus on high-auxin medium and grows adventitious shoots after transfer to high-cytokinin medium. Someone says, "Callus is completely dedifferentiated cells, so it can grow into any organ." Evaluate this statement in light of this chapter.

   **Answer** The statement is not accurate. Callus induction proceeds molecularly along the lateral root developmental pathway, originating from pericycle or pericycle-like cells and expressing genes of the root apical meristem such as *PLT*, *SCR* and *WOX5*; it carries the identity of a "root primordium" rather than being a mass of cells with no identity, and it is because it is already in a state resembling a lateral root primordium that it is competent for regeneration. After transfer to high-cytokinin medium, type-B ARRs directly activate *WUS* and establish the shoot organizing center in the callus, so that shoots form. Regeneration in tissue culture is therefore "borrowing developmental programs": high auxin starts the root program and high cytokinin starts the shoot apex program, and the Skoog–Miller ratio is the switch between the two programs; the differences in regenerative capacity among species and explants also lie in whether they can enter these programs (for example whether they retain pericycle-like cells that can respond).
