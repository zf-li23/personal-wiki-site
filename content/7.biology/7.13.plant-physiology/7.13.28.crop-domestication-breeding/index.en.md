Almost all of the food people eat today comes from a handful of plants domesticated over the past ten thousand years. Domestication turned wild plants into cultivated plants that depend on humans for sowing and harvesting: seeds no longer scatter at maturity, germinate uniformly, the plants are upright and compact, grains and fruits are much larger, and bitterness and toxicity are gone. Each of these changes has a physiological basis, and most can be traced to specific genes: shattering is a problem of abscission zone development, dormancy is a problem of the balance between ABA and GA, changes in flowering time are a problem of the photoperiod pathway, and semidwarfism is a problem of gibberellin. This chapter discusses domestication and breeding from the viewpoint of plant physiology. We ask how cultivated plants differ from their wild ancestors (Section 28.1), where crops originated (Section 28.2), what the physiological and genetic basis of domestication traits is (Section 28.3) and how it is described quantitatively in population genetics (Section 28.4), and which genes were involved in the domestication of rice (Section 28.5). We then turn to the major breakthroughs in crop breeding since the twentieth century: the Green Revolution (Section 28.6), heterosis and hybrid rice (Section 28.7), and wide hybridization and chromosome engineering (Section 28.8). The chapter closes with germplasm resources (Section 28.9), the evolution of breeding technology (Section 28.10) and the domestication of new crops (Section 28.11).

## 28.1. Cultivated Plants and Their Wild Ancestors

### 28.1.1. Domestication and Artificial Selection

**Definition 28.1.1 (Cultivated Plant)** A plant that is sown (or propagated vegetatively), managed and harvested by humans, has acquired heritable traits suited to human needs under human selection, and usually can no longer survive independently in nature for long, is called a **cultivated plant**. A population within a cultivated species that has been bred artificially and is relatively uniform and stable in its traits is called a cultivar.

**Definition 28.1.2 (Wild Progenitor)** The wild species or wild population from which a cultivated plant was derived at the beginning of its domestication is called its **wild progenitor** (also ancestral species). Wild progenitors can usually be crossed with the cultivated type to give fertile offspring, and many taxonomic treatments place the two in the same species.

**Domestication** is the process by which wild plants gradually become cultivated plants under human cultivation. It began with the agricultural age about ten thousand years ago, and at first it was not purposeful breeding. Gatherers always harvested the seeds that were still on the plant at maturity and sowed those that germinated quickly and uniformly, so non-shattering, non-dormant individuals increased automatically through generations of harvesting and sowing. This is *unconscious selection*, that is, selection caused as a side effect of human activity; it is the same in nature as **natural selection**, except that the "environment" is the field and the harvesting method. Later people deliberately chose plants with large panicles, large grains, or desirable color or taste for seed, which is artificial selection in the narrow sense. After domestication was complete, cultivated plants spread with humans to new regions and diversified into a large number of landraces under different climates and uses, which is a further stage of the evolution of cultivated plants.

Domestication differs from modern breeding mainly in time scale and in how genetic variation is used. Domestication took thousands of years and used variation already present, or arising by chance, in wild populations. Modern breeding has a history of only a little over a hundred years and actively creates and combines variation through planned crossing, mutagenesis, molecular markers and genetic engineering, but it still draws on the genetic base left by domestication (Section 28.9).

### 28.1.2. Wild Progenitors of the Major Crops

| Crop | Wild progenitor | Region of domestication | Main changes under domestication |

| Foxtail millet (*Setaria italica*) | Green foxtail (*S. viridis*) | Yellow River valley, China, more than 8000 years ago | Large panicle, non-shattering, reduced tillering |
| Proso millet (*Panicum miliaceum*) | Wild type still uncertain | Northern China, about 10,000 years ago | Larger grains, non-shattering |
| Bread wheat (*Triticum aestivum*) | Natural hybrid of cultivated emmer wheat and *Aegilops tauschii* ([7.12.39](/7.12.39)) | Fertile Crescent of western Asia and south of the Caspian Sea | Non-shattering (tough rachis), free-threshing, hexaploid |
| Soybean (*Glycine max*) | Wild soybean (*G. soja*) | China, cultivated for at least 5000 years | Upright, pod-indehiscent, large seeds, loss of twining |
| Maize (*Zea mays*) | Balsas teosinte (*Z. mays* subsp. *parviglumis*) | Southern Mexico, about 9000 years ago | Reduced branching, softened fruitcase, large ear ([7.10.19](/7.10.19)) |
| Tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*) | Currant tomato (*S. pimpinellifolium*) | Western South America and Mesoamerica | Fruit enlarged more than a hundredfold |
| Potato (*S. tuberosum*) | Wild species of the *S. brevicaule* complex of southern Peru | Andes | Tubers enlarged tens of times, regular shape, reduced glycoalkaloids |
| Cucumber (*Cucumis sativus*) | Wild cucumber (*C. sativus* var. *hardwickii*) | South Asia (India) | Fruit changed from oval to elongated, bitterness lost |
| Sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*) | Wild relatives in Central and South America (possibly related to *I. trifida*) | Central and South America | Enlarged storage roots |

This table shows several things. First, the wild progenitor of a crop may still grow in its region of origin: common wild rice is distributed in southern China and South Asia and wild soybean throughout China, so these wild species are irreplaceable genetic resources (wild soybean is listed as a second-class nationally protected plant in China, not because it is rare but because it is the ancestor of a crop). Second, the wild progenitor is not always obvious: the ear of maize is unlike that of any wild grass, and its ancestor was a mystery for a long time; bread wheat has no single wild ancestor at all but is the product of repeated natural hybridizations among several species. Third, once a crop leaves its region of origin, the main production areas can shift completely: soybean was introduced to the United States in 1804, and in the 2016/17 season the United States produced about 116 million tonnes of soybean, whereas China produced about 13 million tonnes in 2016; potato and sweet potato reached China after the Ming dynasty (the Chinese name of the sweet potato, "fan shu", literally "foreign tuber", reflects this), and China is now the largest producer of both.

A common misconception is that primitive or wild food is more natural and better. In fact, wild progenitors often contain more defensive secondary metabolites ([7.13.26](/7.13.26)): the fruit of wild cucumber is extremely bitter because of cucurbitacins, wild potato tubers have a high content of glycoalkaloids, and wild soybean and other legumes have high contents of trypsin inhibitors and lectins. Domestication is to a large extent the process of removing these defenses.

### 28.1.3. Main Differences Between Cultivated Plants and Wild Ancestors

In domestication, different crops independently underwent a similar set of changes, which is called the **domestication syndrome**. Chinese textbooks often summarize the differences between cultivated plants and their wild ancestors in eight points:

| Difference | Manifestation | Physiological and genetic basis (this chapter and related chapters) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Gigantism | Harvested organs (grains, fruits, tubers, storage roots) are markedly enlarged; often accompanied by **polyploidy** (bread wheat, potato, sweet potato) | Sink capacity and sink strength ([7.13.11](/7.13.11)); cell number and size (tomato *fw2.2*, Section 28.3.5) |
| 2. Allometric growth of organs | The organs that are used grow preferentially relative to other organs | Altered assimilate partitioning, increased **harvest index** (Section 28.6) |
| 3. Degeneration or loss of natural dispersal | Non-shattering, non-dehiscent, seeds remain on the plant; awns of cereals degenerate | Abscission zone absent or incompletely developed (Section 28.3.1) |
| 4. Loss of protective functions | Fewer thorns, hard glumes, bitter substances and toxins | Shutdown of secondary metabolism (cucumber *Bt*); softened glumes (maize *tga1*, wheat *Q*) |
| 5. Altered chemical composition | Altered content and composition of starch, protein and oil (waxy, sweet) | Mutations in storage product synthesis pathways (*Waxy*, *su1*, *sh2*, Section 28.3.5) |
| 6. Weakened or lost seed dormancy, uniform maturity | Uniform germination after sowing; seeds on one plant mature together | ABA/GA balance, seed coat pigments (Section 28.3.2) |
| 7. Altered life cycle | Perennial to annual; altered requirements for daylength and low temperature for flowering; outcrossing to self-pollination | Photoperiod and vernalization pathways (Section 28.3.3) |
| 8. Diversity of types | A single crop diverges into many cultivars and use types (flint, dent, sweet and waxy maize) | Divergent selection by people in different regions and for different uses |

These changes are often caused by mutations in homologous genes in different crops ([7.12.39](/7.12.39)): the same selection pressure acting repeatedly on a few developmental regulators in different cereals is an example of **convergent evolution**. On the other hand, most domestication traits are loss-of-function mutations that lower fitness in nature (seeds cannot disperse, there is no dormancy) and are advantageous only under human protection, which is also why cultivated plants rarely become feral.

## 28.2. Centers of Origin and Crops of China

In the 1920s and 1930s the Soviet botanist N. I. Vavilov organized expeditions across five continents and collected several hundred thousand crop accessions. He found that the genetic diversity of each crop is concentrated in certain regions and proposed the theory of the *center of origin*: the region where the variant types of a crop are richest and where its wild relatives occur is its place of origin. In 1935 he summarized eight main centers of origin: China, India (including the Indo-Malayan region), Central Asia, the Near East, the Mediterranean, Ethiopia, southern Mexico and Central America, and South America. The Chinese center is the first he listed and one of the centers with the most crops of origin (foxtail millet, proso millet, soybean, many vegetables and fruit trees, tea, mulberry).

Later research modified Vavilov's theory. The place of greatest genetic diversity is not necessarily the place of origin: after a crop is introduced to a new region it may diversify into more types (secondary centers), and diversity at the place of origin may also be lost through long cultivation. J. R. Harlan pointed out that some crops were domesticated dispersedly over wide areas and have no clearly defined center. Archaeobotany (charred seeds, phytoliths, starch grains) and molecular phylogenetics place domestication as events that occurred independently in at least a dozen regions of the world. The figure gives approximate dates of domestication of the main crops:

![Dates of domestication (thousands of years ago) of the main crops in several independent centers of domestication. Each horizontal bar extends from the earliest evidence of cultivation to the appearance of the domesticated type; values are approximate, taken from archaeobotanical reviews (for example Larson et al. 2014), with an uncertainty of about 1000 years. The cereals and pulses of the Fertile Crescent of western Asia are the earliest; proso and foxtail millet in northern China and rice in the south began to be cultivated about 10,000 years ago, and the domestication of rice (predominance of non-shattering types) lasted about three thousand years; squash and maize in Mesoamerica, potato and cassava in South America, and taro and banana in New Guinea are roughly contemporaneous; sorghum, pearl millet and African rice in the African Sahel are later](./plots/domestication-timeline.py)

Ancient China referred to the main food crops collectively as the "Five Grains", commonly given as rice, proso millet, foxtail millet ("ji", generally taken to be *Setaria*), wheat and soybean ("shu"). Except for wheat, all of them originated in China: in the north, the Yellow River valley relied mainly on the dryland crops foxtail and proso millet, and in the south the Yangtze valley relied mainly on rice. The Hemudu site in Yuyao, Zhejiang (about 7000 years ago) yielded a large quantity of rice grains, about half of them awned (a trait of wild types) and half awnless, which reflects domestication still in progress. The domestication of rice continued for several thousand years in the archaeological record of the middle and lower Yangtze: the morphology of the spikelet base (after a wild rice spikelet falls it leaves a smooth abscission scar, whereas cultivated rice leaves a rough, broken surface) shows that the proportion of non-shattering types among excavated grains rose gradually (Fuller et al. 2009).

Knowledge of centers of origin directly serves breeding: wild relatives and landraces of a crop are concentrated in its place of origin, so expeditions to find new genes for disease resistance, stress tolerance and the like point there first. Vavilov himself was persecuted in the period when Lysenkoism prevailed (T. D. Lysenko) and died in prison in 1943; the seed bank he established in Leningrad was preserved by researchers through the siege of the Second World War and is one of the earliest large crop gene banks still in existence (Section 28.9).

## 28.3. Physiological Basis of Domestication Traits

### 28.3.1. Shattering and the Abscission Layer

Seeds of wild grasses fall from the plant at maturity. This depends on an **abscission zone** that differentiates at the base of the spikelet (rice, sorghum) or at the nodes of the rachis (wheat, barley): one or two layers of small, thin-walled cells form the **abscission layer**, whose middle lamella is degraded by cell wall hydrolases at maturity, so that seeds fall under the slightest external force (the development of the abscission zone and the physiology of abscission are in [7.13.24](/7.13.24#sec-24.5)). Domestication selected non-shattering in two ways: the abscission layer does not form or forms incompletely (rice *sh4* and *qSH1*, Section 28.5.3; sorghum *Sh1*), or the rachis does not break at the abscission zone (the *Br*/*Btr* genes of wheat and barley, whose wild type makes the rachis break up node by node at maturity).

Shattering is a measurable quantitative trait: with a tension gauge, the force needed to pull a grain off its pedicel (breaking tensile strength) is measured. Wild rice needs only a few tenths of a newton or even drops spontaneously at maturity, whereas cultivated rice generally needs more than 1.5 N. If the breaking strength is too high, threshing becomes difficult, so the ideal cultivar is "non-shattering but easy to thresh": japonica rice is generally hard to shatter, whereas some indica and hybrid rice cultivars still suffer some shattering loss before harvest.

### 28.3.2. Loss of Dormancy

Seeds of wild plants are dormant, which spreads germination over different times so as to avoid unfavorable seasons; this is a risk-spreading strategy of "not putting all the eggs in one basket" ([7.13.20](/7.13.20#sec-20.2)). For the sower, dormancy means uneven emergence and weediness in the field, so domestication selected types with shallow dormancy. The depth of dormancy is determined by the balance between ABA and GA in the seed and is also related to the seed coat. The red pericarp and seed coat of wild rice contain proanthocyanidins; the region of the red-pericarp gene *Rc* is also a major quantitative trait locus for seed dormancy, and the white-grain *rc* allele weakens dormancy (Section 28.5.4). Red-grained wheat is more deeply dormant than white-grained wheat and more resistant to **preharvest sprouting**.

Complete loss of dormancy also has a cost: if continuous rain arrives before harvest, cultivars with shallow dormancy germinate on the ear, causing yield loss and reduced quality ([7.13.20](/7.13.20#sec-20.7)). The breeding aim is therefore "moderate dormancy": a short dormancy at maturity that is released quickly after harvest.

### 28.3.3. Altered Photoperiod and Vernalization Responses

As crops spread from their regions of origin to different latitudes, they had to change the daylength and low-temperature requirements of flowering; otherwise in the new region they either could not flower or flowered too early with low yield (photoperiod responses and florigen are in [7.13.21](/7.13.21), vernalization in [7.13.22](/7.13.22)). What was selected in domestication and spread was often a loss of function or a change in regulation of one gene in these pathways:

| Crop | Original response | Selected allele | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rice | Short-day plant | Weakened or loss-of-function alleles of *Hd1* (a *CO* homolog) and *Ghd7* | Heading is no longer strongly delayed under long days, which allowed rice cultivation to extend to high latitudes such as northeastern China |
| Wheat, barley | Long-day plants, winter habit | Wheat *Ppd-D1a* (photoperiod insensitive), barley *ppd-H1*; dominant spring alleles of wheat *Vrn-A1* | Timely heading under short days at low latitudes or under spring sowing |
| Maize | Short-day plant (teosinte) | Insensitive alleles of *ZmCCT* and others | Spread to temperate regions |
| Soybean | Short-day plant | Loss of function of *E1*; *J* alleles with a long juvenile period | Extension toward both high latitudes and low tropical latitudes |

For genes like *Hd1* and *Ghd7*, whether "later or earlier" is favorable depends on latitude and the length of the growing season, so one crop forms ecotypes with different growth durations in different regions (the four ecological groups of indica rice, early, middle, late and winter indica, are an example, Section 28.5.1).

### 28.3.4. Plant Architecture

Wild plants are often much branched or highly tillered and prostrate or twining, which lets them occupy space and compete with neighbors for light; in a densely planted field, compact, upright individuals favor the yield of the stand. A typical example in domestication is maize *tb1*: its high-expression allele strengthens the apical-dominance-like suppression of axillary buds, turning the several long branches of teosinte into the single stalk of maize ([7.10.19](/7.10.19); hormonal regulation of axillary bud growth is in [7.13.18](/7.13.18)). The change of rice from prostrate to upright is controlled by *PROG1* (Section 28.5.4). Modern breeding goes further in pursuit of an "ideal plant architecture": the favorable allele of rice *IPA1* (*OsSPL14*) reduces unproductive tillers, makes the culm sturdy and gives large panicles with many grains.

Plant height is another architectural trait. Tall stems are advantageous for competition under wild conditions but lead to lodging in fertilized fields; the semidwarf breeding of the twentieth century was the Green Revolution (Section 28.6).

### 28.3.5. Grains, Fruits and Storage Products

Enlargement of the harvested organs results from an expanded sink ([7.13.11](/7.13.11)): more florets and grains in the inflorescence, and more cells and larger cell volume in each grain or fruit. Tomato *fw2.2* was the first cloned QTL for fruit size (Frary et al. 2000). It encodes a negative regulator of cell division; the large-fruit allele has reduced expression early in fruit development, so carpel cells divide more and fruit weight can differ by about 30%. Grain number and grain weight in cereals are likewise regulated by a few major genes, often linked to hormones: rice *Gn1a* encodes the cytokinin oxidase OsCKX2, which degrades **cytokinin**, and the allele with reduced expression allows cytokinin to accumulate in the inflorescence meristem and increases the number of grains per panicle (Ashikari et al. 2005).

The composition of storage products is the main determinant of quality. Cereal endosperm starch consists of **amylose** and **amylopectin**; amylose is synthesized by granule-bound starch synthase I (GBSSI, encoded by *Waxy*). Waxy types with loss of *Waxy* function contain almost only amylopectin and have been selected independently in rice, maize, foxtail millet, proso millet and sorghum in East Asia for sticky foods and brewing. Sweet maize is a starch synthesis mutant: *su1* (deficient in the starch debranching enzyme, accumulating water-soluble polysaccharide) and *sh2* (deficient in the large subunit of ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase, with high sugar and little starch in the grain, which shrivels at maturity). The uses of ancient crops are still expanding: high-lysine maize (*opaque2*, which reduces zeins and raises lysine content), high-oil maize, and anthocyanin-rich black rice and purple sweet potato are all selections on the composition of storage products.

## 28.4. Quantifying Domestication: Selection Response and Genetic Diversity

Domestication traits have two kinds of genetic architecture: quantitative traits such as grain size, panicle length and plant height are controlled by many genes of small effect, whereas the key changes in shattering, dormancy and plant architecture are often caused by one or a few major genes. The responses of the two to selection can be described by quantitative genetics and population genetics respectively.

For a quantitative trait, the response to selection in one generation is given by the **breeder's equation** ([Theorem 24.5.1](/7.4.24#thm-24.5.1)):

$$
R = h^2S, \qquad \frac{R}{L} = \frac{i\,h\,\sigma_A}{L}, \tag{28.1}
$$

where $S$ is the selection differential, $h^2$ is the narrow-sense heritability, $i$ is the selection intensity, $\sigma_A$ is the additive genetic standard deviation, and $L$ is the generation interval in years (for an annual crop $L = 1\,\mathrm{a}$). Early farmers did not weigh seeds but merely tended to sow larger ones, so the selection intensity was small; but as long as selection continued for hundreds of generations, the accumulated response was still considerable.

**Example 28.4.1** Suppose a wild grass population has a mean thousand-grain weight of $10\,\mathrm{g}$, a phenotypic standard deviation $\sigma_P = 1.5\,\mathrm{g}$ and $h^2 = 0.3$. If the seeds sown are on average only $0.2\sigma_P$ larger than the population ($i = 0.2$, equivalent to very weak unconscious selection), the response per generation is $R = 0.3 \times 0.2 \times 1.5 = 0.09\,\mathrm{g}$. Assuming the additive variance stays constant, reaching a thousand-grain weight of $20\,\mathrm{g}$ takes about $10/0.09 \approx 110$ generations; if one deliberately keeps only the largest 20% of seeds ($i = 1.40$), the response per generation is $0.63\,\mathrm{g}$ and about 16 generations suffice. (The values are illustrative.) The archaeological record does show grain enlargement continuing over one or two thousand years, which indicates that early selection was weak, or that the additive variance was small in the small cultivated populations.

For a major gene, let the frequency of the non-shattering allele $a$ be $q$. Harvesting and resowing make non-shattering plants contribute more to the next season's seed than shattering plants, by a factor $1 + s$ relative to $1$. If $a$ is recessive and the population has inbreeding coefficient $F$ ($F = 0$ for an outcrossing population, $F$ close to 1 for a predominantly selfing crop), the zygotic genotype frequencies are $AA = p^2(1 - F) + pF$, $Aa = 2pq(1 - F)$ and $aa = q^2(1 - F) + qF$, and the frequency in the next generation is

$$
q' = \frac{(1 + s)\,[\,q^2(1 - F) + qF\,] + pq(1 - F)}{1 + s\,[\,q^2(1 - F) + qF\,]}. \tag{28.2}
$$

For $F = 0$ this is the formula for selection on a recessive in [7.4.23](/7.4.23): when $q$ is small, $\Delta q \approx sq^2$, and a rare recessive allele hidden in heterozygotes is almost unaffected by selection. Selfing exposes it in homozygotes: for $F = 1$ the population consists of homozygous lines, selection is equivalent to gene selection in a haploid, and the ratio $q/p$ is multiplied by $1 + s$ each generation. Rice, wheat, barley and soybean are all predominantly selfing, which may be one reason why their major domestication alleles could spread relatively quickly.

Domestication also had a population genetic cost: only a few individuals of the wild population were initially cultivated, and subsequent selection kept eliminating undesired types, so the cultivated population passed through a **population bottleneck**. If the effective size during the bottleneck is $N_{\mathrm{b}}$ and it lasts $d$ generations, the fraction of **heterozygosity** retained at neutral loci is (a basic result for **genetic drift**, [7.10.6](/7.10.6))

$$
\frac{H_d}{H_0} = \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{2N_{\mathrm{b}}}\Bigr)^{d} \approx \mathrm{e}^{-d/(2N_{\mathrm{b}})}. \tag{28.3}
$$

The loss of diversity depends only on the ratio $d/N_{\mathrm{b}}$: a bottleneck of 3000 generations with $N_{\mathrm{b}} = 2000$ loses the same proportion as a bottleneck of 300 generations with $N_{\mathrm{b}} = 200$ ($\mathrm{e}^{-0.75} = 0.47$). Whole-genome sequencing shows that the **nucleotide diversity** of cultivated rice is markedly lower than that of common wild rice, lowest in temperate japonica; near genes under domestication selection, the reduction of diversity is far more severe than the genome average, which is the signature of a **selective sweep** and is often used to look for domestication genes in the genome.

![Two population genetic aspects of domestication (deterministic models). A: spread of a non-shattering allele under harvest selection (Eq. 28.2, s = 0.03, initial frequency 0.01). Dominant and additive alleles rise to high frequency within two to three hundred generations; a recessive allele in an outcrossing population almost stalls and is still below 10% after three thousand generations; in a predominantly selfing population (F = 0.9), the same recessive allele spreads as fast as the dominant allele. B: fraction of neutral heterozygosity retained in a domestication bottleneck (Eq. 28.3) as a function of bottleneck size N_b and duration d in generations: retaining half the heterozygosity requires N_b ≈ 0.72 d](./plots/selection-bottleneck.py)

**Example 28.4.2** A cultivated population has an effective size of about 1000 during the first 3000 years of domestication. By Eq. (28.3), neutral heterozygosity retained is $\mathrm{e}^{-3000/2000} = 0.22$, a loss of about 80%. If the effective size in the same period were 5000, the retained fraction would be $\mathrm{e}^{-0.3} = 0.74$. For a selfing crop, the effective population size must further be reduced by the inbreeding coefficient ($N_{\mathrm{e}} \approx N/(1 + F)$), and the loss is greater. Modern cultivars then went through a second bottleneck after the domestication bottleneck: a few elite parents were used repeatedly in breeding, which narrowed the genetic base among cultivars, and this is precisely why germplasm resources (Section 28.9) are important.

## 28.5. The Domestication of Rice

### 28.5.1. The Genus *Oryza* and the Classification of Asian Cultivated Rice

The genus *Oryza* has about 24 species, divided by genome type into AA, BB, CC, BBCC, CCDD, EE, FF, GG and others, distributed in the tropics and subtropics of Asia, Africa, the Americas and Oceania (morphology in [7.12.39](/7.12.39)). Both cultivated species belong to the AA genome: Asian cultivated rice (*O. sativa*) was domesticated from common wild rice (*O. rufipogon*, including the annual form *O. nivara*) and is grown worldwide; African cultivated rice (*O. glaberrima*) was domesticated independently from *O. barthii* about 3000 years ago in the inland delta of the Niger River in West Africa and is now grown in small amounts only in West Africa. Rice was one of the first crops domesticated by humans and is still the staple food of more than half of the world's population.

Asian cultivated rice is divided into two subspecies, *indica* and *japonica*. In China, Xu Shen's *Shuowen Jiezi* (about AD 100) of the Eastern Han dynasty already distinguished the two by grain quality, stickiness and grain shape. The modern scientific classification began with the Japanese scientist Shigeo Kato and colleagues (1928), who, on the basis of the seed set (compatibility) of hybrids combined with morphological and physiological and biochemical characteristics, divided the world's rice into two subspecies, Japanese type (*japonica*) and Indian type (*indica*). The main differences between the two subspecies are as follows:

| Trait | indica | japonica |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution | Hot, humid regions at low latitude and low altitude | High latitude or high altitude, more cold tolerant |
| Grain shape | Slender, with short, sparse hairs on the lemma | Short and round, with long, dense hairs on the lemma |
| Leaves | Light green, broader and drooping | Dark green, narrower and erect |
| Grain quality | Higher amylose content (about 25%–30%), cooked rice firm and not sticky | Lower amylose content (about 15%–20%), cooked rice soft and sticky |
| Shattering | More prone to shattering | Harder to shatter |
| Cold tolerance | Weak | Strong |
| Phenol reaction (color change of the husk with phenol) | Mostly positive | Mostly negative (loss of function of *Phr1*) |

Japonica rice can be further divided into temperate japonica (ordinary japonica of northeastern and northern China, the Taihu Lake region, Japan and the Korean peninsula) and tropical japonica (the Javanica rice of the islands of Southeast Asia, and others). Indica rice is divided by sowing and harvest season into four ecological groups: early indica (March–July, early crop of double-cropping), middle indica (May–September, single middle-season crop), late indica (June–November, single or double late crop) and winter indica (November to May of the next year, double or triple winter crop in southern China and Southeast Asia); the difference among them lies mainly in the response to daylength (Section 28.3.3). Population genetic studies of whole genomes divide Asian cultivated rice into five groups: indica, aus, temperate japonica, tropical japonica and aromatic (basmati-type).

The origin of indica and japonica was long debated. The evidence now favors the following: japonica was first domesticated from common wild rice in southern China (the middle and lower Yangtze, with some studies placing it in the Pearl River basin), and the main domestication genes (such as *sh4* and *PROG1*) were selected first in japonica; these alleles then entered wild rice or early cultivated populations of South and Southeast Asia through hybridization and **introgression**, giving rise to indica. Indica and japonica therefore share many domestication alleles but retain different wild rice origins in the rest of the genome.

### 28.5.2. Domestication Traits and Genes

The changes from common wild rice to cultivated rice can be summarized in nine points: growth habit changed from prostrate to upright; from easy shattering to hard shattering; increased panicle number and a panicle changed from loose to compact; more grains per panicle and larger grains; awns changed from long to short or absent; hull color changed from black through brown to yellow (straw color); grain (pericarp) color changed from red to white; from perennial to annual; and from partial outcrossing to strict self-pollination. ### 28.5.3. The Shattering Genes *sh4* and *qSH1*

Shattering is the most critical trait in domestication: as long as seeds still scatter before harvest, large-scale harvesting and sowing are impossible. In wild rice, *SH4* is expressed in the abscission zone at the spikelet base and promotes the differentiation of abscission layer cells and cell wall degradation at maturity. In cultivated rice, a G→T substitution in the coding region of *sh4* changes a lysine (AAG) in the DNA-binding domain to asparagine (AAT); the function of the protein is weakened, the abscission layer develops incompletely, and the grains no longer fall spontaneously but can still be threshed. This mutation of *sh4* is present in all of the nearly one hundred indica and more than one hundred japonica cultivars examined but absent in wild rice, which indicates that it was fixed before the divergence of indica and japonica (or entered indica by introgression after selection in japonica) and is one of the earliest domestication mutations to be selected.

*qSH1* is another major QTL for shattering. It encodes a BEL1-type homeodomain protein, and a SNP in the regulatory region about 12 kb upstream prevents its expression in the abscission zone, so the abscission layer does not form at all. This allele is found only in temperate japonica, which is why temperate japonica is particularly hard to shatter. The different effects of the two genes show that shattering is determined jointly by several genes and their modifiers: the mutation of *sh4* was a necessary step of domestication, while *qSH1* and others further strengthened non-shattering in some groups.

The cloning of *sh4* is a classic example of fine mapping of a **quantitative trait locus** ([7.4.25](/7.4.25)): in the progeny of a cross between wild and cultivated rice, the shattering gene was mapped to chromosome 4 with molecular markers; large segregating populations were then used to find recombinants between markers and phenotype, narrowing the interval step by step to one or two candidate genes in a bacterial artificial chromosome clone, and the result was finally verified by a transgenic complementation experiment.

### 28.5.4. Plant Architecture, Seed Coat and Starch

The angle between the tillers and the main culm of common wild rice is large, and the plants are prostrate or oblique, which helps them spread in marshes but takes up space and shades heavily when planted densely. *PROG1* (*PROSTRATE GROWTH 1*) is expressed at the base of tillers in wild rice and maintains prostrate growth; the *prog1* allele of cultivated rice has lost this function, so the plants are upright and the number of grains per panicle also increases. Because an upright, compact architecture improves light interception and ventilation of the stand (canopy photosynthesis is in [7.13.9](/7.13.9)), *prog1* is also a yield trait.

The pericarp and seed coat of common wild rice are red, the result of proanthocyanidin accumulation in the seed coat; *Rc* encodes a bHLH transcription factor regulating proanthocyanidin synthesis, and in white-grained cultivars a 14 bp deletion in exon 7 truncates the protein so that proanthocyanidins cannot be synthesized. Red seed coat and seed dormancy are genetically linked: the *Rc* region is itself a major locus for dormancy, and proanthocyanidins and their oxidation products make the seed coat less permeable to oxygen and strengthen the action of ABA. Selection for white grain therefore also weakened dormancy (Section 28.3.2). The reasons why people prefer white rice may include appearance, taste and the ease with which white rice is processed.

The stickiness of rice depends on the proportion of amylose in the endosperm: amylose is a linear molecule, and the gel it forms retrogrades and hardens on cooling; amylopectin is a highly branched molecule that is sticky after gelatinization and does not readily retrograde. Glutinous rice has about 98% amylopectin and about 2% amylose; japonica rice has about 15%–20% amylose; indica rice about 25%–30%. These differences are caused mainly by alleles of *Waxy*: indica mostly carries $\mathit{Wx}^{\mathrm{a}}$, with high expression and more amylose; japonica mostly carries $\mathit{Wx}^{\mathrm{b}}$, in which the G→T mutation at the 5′ splice site of intron 1 lowers the splicing efficiency of the pre-mRNA, so the mature mRNA and GBSSI protein are reduced and the amylose content is lower; the *wx* allele of glutinous rice is a loss-of-function allele. Amylose content also varies with temperature during grain filling: at low temperature the splicing efficiency of $\mathit{Wx}^{\mathrm{b}}$ is lower, so the same japonica cultivar gives softer, stickier rice when grown in a cooler climate.

### 28.5.5. Introgression Lines and Gene Cloning

Wild rice preserves many favorable alleles that have been lost in cultivated rice (disease resistance, stress tolerance, large panicle), but the progeny of a direct cross with wild rice carry many undesirable traits such as shattering and prostrate growth. A method commonly used in breeding and genetics is to construct an *introgression line*: a cultivar is used as the recurrent parent and crossed with wild rice, followed by consecutive **backcrosses**, with marker selection in each generation, so that each progeny carries only one or a few small chromosome segments of wild rice, and finally selfing gives homozygous lines. For example, with the indica cultivar Teqing as the recipient and common wild rice from Yuanjiang, Yunnan as the donor, three backcrosses (BC₃) and selfing (BC₃F₂, BC₃F₃) gave a set of introgression lines covering all parts of the wild rice genome; among them, lines with the prostrate growth trait were used to map and clone *PROG1*.

After $t$ backcrosses, the expected proportion of the donor genome in unselected regions is $(1/2)^{t+1}$ (construction of **near-isogenic lines**, [7.4.25](/7.4.25)): 25% for BC₁, 12.5% for BC₂ and 6.25% for BC₃.

**Example 28.5.1** The rice genome is about $3.9\times10^8$ bp, and the total length of the genetic map is about 1500 cM. (a) How much wild rice DNA does a BC₃F₁ individual carry on average? (b) In fine mapping, only 4 recombinants between a marker and *sha1* were found among 3136 gametes; what are the genetic and physical distances between marker and gene?

**Solution** (a) $0.0625\times3.9\times10^8 \approx 2.4\times10^7\ \mathrm{bp}$, that is, about 24 Mb, distributed over several segments (in the heterozygous state). (b) The recombination fraction is $4/3136 = 0.13\%$, that is, 0.13 cM; averaged over the genome, each centimorgan corresponds to about $3.9\times10^8/1500 \approx 2.6\times10^5\ \mathrm{bp}$, so the physical distance is about $0.13 \times 260\ \mathrm{kb} \approx 33\ \mathrm{kb}$, which has room for only a few genes. The actual recombination rate is very uneven along chromosomes, so this is only an order-of-magnitude estimate.

## 28.6. The Green Revolution

**Definition 28.6.1 (Green Revolution)** The change in agricultural technology of the 1960s and 1970s that centered on semidwarf, fertilizer-responsive, lodging-resistant, high-harvest-index wheat and rice cultivars and, together with heavy use of chemical fertilizer, irrigation and pesticides, doubled grain yields per unit area in many developing countries within one or two decades is called the **Green Revolution**.

The name was coined by W. Gaud, Administrator of the United States Agency for International Development, on 8 March 1968. Semidwarf breeding of wheat is represented by the work of Norman Borlaug (1914–2009) in Mexico: he combined the dwarfing genes *Rht-B1b* and *Rht-D1b* from the Japanese cultivar Norin 10 with rust-resistant material and in the early 1960s bred Mexican semidwarf rust-resistant wheat; introduced to India and Pakistan in the mid-1960s, it turned the two countries within a few years from grain importers into nearly self-sufficient countries, for which Borlaug received the 1970 Nobel Peace Prize. The International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT) established in Mexico as a result and the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) in the Philippines were the two centers of the Green Revolution. IRRI bred the "miracle rice" IR8 in 1966; its dwarfing source was the Taiwan landrace Dee-geo-woo-gen, and the dwarfing gene was *sd1*. In China, Huang Yaoxiang bred the fertilizer-responsive, lodging-resistant, high-yielding indica semidwarf cultivar "Guangchangai" in 1959, seven years earlier than IR8; "Zhenzhu'ai" and more than a dozen other semidwarf cultivars that he bred in the 1960s became the main cultivars of the southern China rice region and were grown for more than a quarter of a century. The dwarfing source of Guangchangai was the Guangdong landrace Aizizhan, which was later shown to be a mutant allele of the same *sd1*.

The mechanisms of action of the two dwarfing genes (*Rht* is a **DELLA protein** that is not degraded in response to gibberellin; *sd1* is a loss-of-function allele of the gibberellin biosynthetic enzyme *OsGA20ox2*) are in [7.13.14](/7.13.14#sec-14.3.2). From the viewpoint of yield physiology, the effect of semidwarfing can be understood from Eq. (1.1) ([7.13.1](/7.13.1)), $Y = \mathrm{HI}\times B$: after heavy nitrogen application, traditional tall cultivars grow too vegetatively, their stems become taller, and after heading they lodge in wind and rain, so the increase of biomass $B$ cannot be converted into grain; semidwarf cultivars have short, sturdy stems and allocate more assimilate to the panicle, raising the **harvest index** from about 0.3 to about 0.5, and they can withstand higher rates of fertilization.

The mechanics of lodging resistance can be estimated roughly. Treat the stem as a cantilever beam fixed at the ground, with the weight $W$ of the panicle and upper leaves acting at height $h$; when the stem is displaced from the vertical by an angle $\theta$, the bending moment at the stem base is

$$
M = W h\sin\theta, \tag{28.4}
$$

and when $M$ exceeds the breaking moment of the basal internode, the stem breaks or lodges. If the plant height is halved, the bending moment at the base is also halved for the same panicle weight and inclination angle; panicle weight increases with yield, so only by lowering plant height and thickening the basal internodes can large panicles be carried without lodging.

**Example 28.6.1** The yield of Mexican wheat was about 110 jin per mu in the early 1950s, about 380 jin in the early 1970s and about 561 jin in the late 1970s. Convert to $\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ (1 jin = 0.5 kg, 1 mu = 666.7 m²) and compare with FAOSTAT data.

**Solution** $1\ \text{jin/mu} = 0.5\ \mathrm{kg}/666.7\ \mathrm{m^2} = 7.5\ \mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$, so the three values are $0.83$, $2.85$ and $4.21\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$, a yield increase of about fivefold in 25 years. FAOSTAT gives Mexican wheat yields of $1.68\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ in 1961, $2.64$ in 1972 and $3.91\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ in 1979, in rough agreement.

![Cereal yields since 1961 (FAOSTAT data compiled by Our World in Data; rice is paddy rice). A: world yields of rice, wheat and maize; shading marks the period of adoption of semidwarf cultivars, and dashed lines are linear fits for 1961–2024, whose slopes are the mean annual increments (about 49 for rice, 40 for wheat and 65 kg/ha for maize). B: wheat in India and Mexico and paddy rice in China; dashed lines mark the release of IR8 (1966) and the start of the adoption of hybrid rice in China (1976)](./plots/yield-trends.py)

World cereal yields have grown roughly linearly since 1961: rice from $1.9$ to $4.8\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$, wheat from $1.1$ to $3.6\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$. Linear growth means that the relative growth rate gradually declines (about 2.5% a⁻¹ in the 1960s, about 1% a⁻¹ now), which has raised concern about the future food supply ([7.13.1](/7.13.1)).

The costs of the Green Revolution are also obvious: the increase in yield depends on large inputs of fertilizer, irrigation and pesticides, which has brought eutrophication of waters, soil degradation and falling groundwater tables; planting a few semidwarf cultivars over large areas has narrowed the genetic base; and accumulation of DELLA proteins also lowers the ability of the plant to take up and assimilate nitrogen, so semidwarf cultivars have low nitrogen use efficiency. A 2018 study found that the transcription factor GRF4 and DELLA antagonistically regulate carbon and nitrogen metabolism, and that raising the expression of *GRF4* can increase nitrogen use efficiency while keeping the semidwarf stature, which is seen as one direction for a "new Green Revolution": maintaining high yield with fewer inputs.

## 28.7. Heterosis and Hybrid Rice

### 28.7.1. Measures of Heterosis

**Heterosis** is the superiority of the F₁ hybrid over its parents in vigor, biomass, yield or stress resistance ([Definition 24.7.1](/7.4.24#def-24.7.1)); its genetic explanations (the dominance, overdominance and epistasis hypotheses) are in [7.4.24](/7.4.24#sec-24.7). Breeding uses several indices to measure it:

**Definition 28.7.1 (Mid-Parent Heterosis and Better-Parent Heterosis)** Let the values of a trait in the two parents be $P_1$ and $P_2$, the better parent be $P_{\mathrm{h}} = \max(P_1, P_2)$, the mid-parent value be $\mathrm{MP} = (P_1 + P_2)/2$, and the F₁ hybrid be $F_1$. The **mid-parent heterosis** and **better-parent heterosis** are respectively

$$
H_{\mathrm{MP}} = \frac{F_1 - \mathrm{MP}}{\mathrm{MP}}\times100\%, \qquad H_{\mathrm{BP}} = \frac{F_1 - P_{\mathrm{h}}}{P_{\mathrm{h}}}\times100\%. \tag{28.5}
$$

Production is more concerned with competitive (standard) heterosis, that is, the percentage yield increase of the $F_1$ over a locally released conventional check cultivar; only combinations with significant competitive heterosis are worth using.

By [Remark 24.7.1 of 7.4.24](/7.4.24#rem-24.7.1), when the $F_1$ of a cross of two pure lines is selfed for one generation, heterosis is lost by half on average: $F_2 = \mathrm{MP} + \tfrac12(F_1 - \mathrm{MP})$. The use of heterosis therefore requires fresh production of hybrid seed every season. For a crop such as maize, which is cross-pollinated with separate male and female flowers, it suffices to remove the tassels of the female parent in seed production; for crops such as rice and wheat, which have perfect flowers and self-pollinate, manual emasculation cannot be carried out on a large scale, and a way must be found to induce **male sterility** in the female parent.

**Example 28.7.1** Two rice parents yield $6.0$ and $7.0\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$, the F₁ hybrid yields $8.4\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ and the conventional check cultivar yields $7.2\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$. Find the mid-parent, better-parent and competitive heterosis, and the expected yield if a farmer saves $F_2$ seed.

**Solution** $\mathrm{MP} = 6.5$, $H_{\mathrm{MP}} = 1.9/6.5 = 29\%$; $H_{\mathrm{BP}} = 1.4/7.0 = 20\%$; competitive heterosis $1.2/7.2 = 17\%$. The expected $F_2$ yield is $6.5 + 1.9/2 = 7.45\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$, 11% lower than the $F_1$, and the population segregates for plant height and growth duration and is not uniform, so seed of hybrid rice cannot be saved.

### 28.7.2. Three-Line Hybrid Rice

**Definition 28.7.2 (Three-Line Hybrid Rice)** The system for exploiting heterosis in rice that uses nuclear-cytoplasmic interaction in **cytoplasmic male sterility** and produces hybrid seed with three matched lines is called **three-line hybrid rice** (the three-line system). The three lines are: the **male-sterile line** (A line), with sterile cytoplasm and no restorer gene in the nucleus, genotype $S(rf\,rf)$, whose pollen aborts while the pistil is normal, used as the female parent in seed production; the **maintainer line** (B line), with the same nuclear genes as the male-sterile line but normal cytoplasm, genotype $N(rf\,rf)$, used to pollinate the male-sterile line so as to multiply it; and the **restorer line** (R line), carrying a dominant **restorer gene**, genotype $N(Rf\,Rf)$ or $S(Rf\,Rf)$, which when crossed with the male-sterile line gives the fertility-restored hybrid $S(Rf\,rf)$.

The male-sterile line and the maintainer line are a pair of "isonuclear alloplasmic" lines: the male-sterile line is obtained from the maintainer line by repeated backcrossing (nuclear substitution). Two fields are needed in production: in the multiplication field, the male-sterile and maintainer lines are planted in alternate rows, and what is harvested from the male-sterile line is again male-sterile line; in the seed production field, the male-sterile and restorer lines are planted in alternate rows, and what is harvested from the male-sterile line is the hybrid. The maintainer and restorer lines both propagate by selfing.

![Two seed production systems for hybrid rice (schematic). A: three-line system. The maintainer line (B line, N(rf rf)) pollinates the male-sterile line (A line, S(rf rf)) to multiply the male-sterile line; the restorer line (R line, carrying dominant Rf) pollinates the male-sterile line to give the fertility-restored hybrid F1 (S(Rf rf)) for field planting, with fresh seed production each season; the maintainer and restorer lines propagate by selfing. B: two-line system. The photoperiod- and thermo-sensitive genic male-sterile line is sterile under long days or high temperature and is crossed with any fertile male parent for seed production; it is fertile under short days or low temperature and propagates by selfing, with no maintainer line needed](./plots/hybrid-rice-systems.py)

The key to this system is finding a suitable sterile cytoplasm. Yuan Longping began research on male sterility in rice in 1964; in 1970 Li Bihu and Feng Keshan found a pollen-aborted plant of common wild rice ("wild abortive", WA) in Sanya, Hainan, and with it as the source of sterile cytoplasm the three-line system was completed in 1973 and widely adopted from 1976 (history in [7.12.28](/7.12.28)). Since then, hybrid rice in China has long accounted for about half of the rice area and has generally yielded about 10%–20% more than contemporary conventional cultivars (the yield of paddy rice in China in panel B of the figure rose fastest in 1976–1985, which also includes contributions from improved fertilization and crop management).

The molecular mechanism of WA-type sterility was elucidated in 2013: the mitochondrial genome of the WA cytoplasm contains a chimeric open reading frame produced by recombination, *WA352*; its transmembrane protein accumulates in the **tapetum** early in microspore development and interacts with the nuclear-encoded mitochondrial protein COX11, inhibiting its hydrogen peroxide-scavenging function and causing a burst of **reactive oxygen species**, which leads to premature **programmed cell death** in the tapetum, so that microspores abort for lack of nutrients (Luo et al. 2013). The two main restorer genes in restorer lines, *Rf4* and *Rf3*, respectively reduce the mRNA of *WA352* post-transcriptionally and prevent accumulation of its protein at the translational level; *Rf4* encodes a pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) protein, the most common protein family among plant fertility restorer genes. CMS is a typical nuclear-cytoplasmic conflict ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)): the mitochondrial genome is transmitted maternally and loses nothing by sacrificing male function, whereas the nuclear restorer genes restore male function.

Hybrid seed production raises many physiological problems. The first is synchrony of flowering: the parents differ in growth duration, so the sowing time difference ("sowing interval") must be calculated from their responses to light and temperature and adjusted by fertilization, irrigation or hormone sprays so that the male-sterile and restorer lines flower at the same time. The second is panicle enclosure in the male-sterile line: in WA-type male-sterile lines the neck internode of the panicle elongates insufficiently, so part of the panicle remains enclosed in the flag leaf sheath, which impairs outcrossing seed set; spraying gibberellin (GA₃, known in China as "920") at early heading to promote elongation of the neck internode, so that the panicle emerges completely and flowering is advanced and concentrated, is a key technique of hybrid rice seed production (the mechanism by which gibberellin promotes internode elongation is in [7.13.14](/7.13.14)). The third is the outcrossing rate: rice is a self-pollinating crop with small anthers, little pollen and a pollen life of only a few minutes, and the glume opening angle and stigma exsertion rate of the male-sterile line determine its ability to receive foreign pollen; in seed production fields, pollen is often driven by pulling ropes or bamboo poles over the plants at full flowering (supplementary hand pollination).

### 28.7.3. Two-Line Hybrid Rice

**Definition 28.7.3 (Two-Line Hybrid Rice)** The system in which hybrids are produced by crossing a **photoperiod- and thermo-sensitive genic male-sterile** line, that is, a nuclear male-sterile line whose fertility is controlled by daylength or temperature, with a fertile male parent is called **two-line hybrid rice** (the two-line system). A photoperiod- and thermo-sensitive genic male-sterile line is sterile under long days or high temperature, when it is used for seed production, and its fertility is restored under short days or low temperature, when it can be multiplied by selfing.

In 1973 Shi Mingsong found photoperiod-sensitive genic sterile plants (Nongken 58S) in the late japonica cultivar Nongken 58 in Hubei; they are sterile under long days and fertile under short days. Later, temperature-sensitive genic male-sterile lines controlled mainly by temperature (such as Annong S-1) were also bred. Their sterility genes have been cloned: the *pms3* of Nongken 58S is a point mutation in a long noncoding RNA gene (*LDMAR*), whose promoter is methylated under long days so that transcription of this RNA decreases and premature programmed cell death occurs in anther development; the *tms5* of Annong S-1 encodes a ribonuclease, RNase Z$^{\mathrm{S1}}$, and when its function is lost, some mRNAs accumulate excessively at high temperature and cause sterility.

The advantages of the two-line system are that the fertility of the male-sterile line is controlled by nuclear genes, so almost any conventional cultivar can serve as restorer line (male parent), combinations are free and combinations with strong heterosis are easy to find, and it also avoids the risk of a single sterile cytoplasm (in the 1970s, maize in the United States suffered a southern corn leaf blight epidemic because T-type sterile cytoplasm was in widespread use, [7.12.28](/7.12.28)). Its difficulties are also physiological: the critical temperature of fertility transition varies with the line and the environment, and if there is persistent low temperature in the seed production season, the male-sterile line becomes partly fertile and sets seed by selfing, lowering the purity of the hybrid seed; if the temperature is too high in the multiplication season, the male-sterile line sets too little seed. The two-line system therefore requires a male-sterile line with a sufficiently low and stable critical temperature, and seed production in regions and seasons with stable climate.

### 28.7.4. Fixation of Heterosis

Hybrid seed has to be produced afresh every season, which is costly. If the F₁ hybrid could be propagated clonally through seed, heterosis could be "fixed". Some plants in nature have **apomixis**, that is, formation of an embryo without meiosis and fertilization, so that the offspring have the same genotype as the mother. In 2019 two research groups synthesized apomixis in hybrid rice by genome editing: three meiotic genes were knocked out simultaneously so that meiosis becomes a mitosis-like division (MiMe), giving diploid gametes with the same genotype as the mother; then either the *MTL* gene, which is required for normal sperm development, was knocked out (inducing parthenogenesis), or the embryogenesis gene *BBM1* was expressed in the egg cell, so that unfertilized eggs develop into embryos. The seeds so obtained are clones of the F₁ hybrid, and heterosis is maintained in the progeny. The initial efficiency was low, and subsequent improvements raised the proportion of clonal seed markedly, but the technique has not yet entered production.

## 28.8. Wide Hybridization and Chromosome Engineering

**Definition 28.8.1 (Wide Hybridization)** Crossing between plants of different species, genera or more distant relationship is called **wide hybridization**. It can introduce traits such as disease resistance and stress tolerance from wild relatives into cultivated crops, or create new species.

Wide hybridization faces three barriers. The first is cross-incompatibility: pollen cannot germinate on the stigma of a foreign species, or the pollen tube cannot enter the embryo sac (the physiology of fertilization is in [7.13.23](/7.13.23)). The second is poor development of the hybrid embryo or endosperm and early abortion, which requires removing the young embryo before it aborts and culturing it in vitro (embryo rescue, [7.13.29](/7.13.29)). The third is hybrid sterility: the genomes of the two parents are not homologous, chromosomes cannot pair normally at meiosis and the gametes are sterile, and colchicine treatment is needed to double the chromosomes and form a fertile **allopolyploid**. Bread wheat in nature was formed in this way ([7.12.39](/7.12.39)); the first successful example of an artificially synthesized crop species is triticale.

Rye (*Secale cereale*, RR, 2n = 14) is cold hardy and tolerant of poor soil, one of the most cold-tolerant cereal crops, and cold-acclimated winter rye seedlings can survive temperatures of about −30 °C (the physiology of cold acclimation is in [7.13.27](/7.13.27)). Crossing bread wheat (AABBDD, 2n = 42) with rye gives a hybrid with the four genomes ABDR (2n = 28), which is sterile; after chromosome doubling, octoploid triticale (AABBDDRR, 2n = 56) is obtained. Bao Wenkui studied triticale at the Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences from 1951 and in 1973 bred octoploid triticale cultivars that could be released for cultivation in the cold highlands of southwestern China. The triticale widely grown in the world now is hexaploid triticale (AABBRR), used mainly as feed.

The wide hybridization work in wheat led by Li Zhensheng lasted about twenty years: he crossed bread wheat with tall wheatgrass (*Thinopyrum ponticum*, 2n = 70) and transferred the drought tolerance, tolerance of hot dry winds and resistance to several diseases of the wheatgrass into wheat, breeding the Xiaoyan series of cultivars, among which Xiaoyan 6 has long been a backbone parent in the Yellow-Huai wheat region; for this he received China's State Preeminent Science and Technology Award for 2006. This work created four kinds of new germplasm: octoploid *Trititrigia* (partial amphidiploids, 2n = 56), alien addition lines, alien substitution lines and translocation lines, and also produced the blue-grained monosomic wheat used in chromosome engineering.

**Definition 28.8.2 (Chromosome Engineering)** The technique of deliberately adding, replacing or transferring alien chromosomes or chromosome segments according to breeding needs is called **chromosome engineering**. Its main products are: the **alien addition line**, in which a pair of alien chromosomes is added to the full wheat chromosome set (2n = 44 in wheat); the **alien substitution line**, in which a pair of alien chromosomes replaces a pair of homoeologous wheat chromosomes (2n = 42); and the **translocation line**, in which a segment of an alien chromosome is translocated onto a wheat chromosome (2n = 42), which is genetically the most stable and has the highest breeding value.

Alien chromosomes usually do not pair with wheat chromosomes, because the *Ph1* gene on wheat chromosome 5B prevents pairing between homoeologous chromosomes ([7.12.39](/7.12.39)). In a *ph1* mutant background, or by inducing chromosome breakage with radiation and tissue culture, alien segments can exchange with wheat chromosomes to give translocation lines with small segments. The most successful example is the 1BL/1RS translocation line: the short arm of rye chromosome 1R replaces the short arm of wheat chromosome 1B, bringing genes for resistance to powdery mildew, stripe rust and leaf rust, and it has been used in a large number of wheat cultivars worldwide. Artificially synthesized allopolyploids also often show large-scale variation in chromosome number and structure and altered gene expression, which is one reason why the progeny of wide hybrids are unstable in their traits and require several generations of selection.

**Example 28.8.1** (a) Bread wheat (2n = 6x = 42) is crossed with tall wheatgrass (2n = 10x = 70); what is the somatic chromosome number of the $F_1$? (b) A wheat–wheatgrass alien addition line is crossed with bread wheat; what is the chromosome number of a monosomic addition individual carrying that alien chromosome in the progeny?

**Solution** (a) The gametes of the two parents carry 21 and 35 chromosomes respectively, so the $F_1$ has $21 + 35 = 56$ chromosomes, and having no homologous pairing partners it is highly sterile. (b) The alien addition line has 2n = 44 (42 + 2) and its gametes have 21 + 1 = 22 chromosomes; crossing with bread wheat (gametes of 21) gives 2n = 43, that is, a monosomic addition line carrying one alien chromosome.

## 28.9. Plant Genetic Resources

**Definition 28.9.1 (Plant Genetic Resources)** All genetic material that carries genetic information and can be used in crop breeding and research, including landraces, bred cultivars, intermediate breeding materials, genetic stocks (mutants, genetic populations) and wild relatives of crops, is collectively called **plant genetic resources** (also germplasm resources).

Germplasm resources are the basis of breeding. The genetic base of modern cultivars is very narrow (Section 28.4), and most new genes for disease resistance, stress tolerance and quality have to be sought in landraces and wild relatives: the WA-type sterile cytoplasm came from common wild rice, the disease resistance of *Trititrigia* came from wheatgrass, and the submergence-tolerance gene *SUB1A* came from an Indian landrace ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)). Germplasm resources are also a national interest: the "seed contest" is essentially a contest over germplasm. In international law, the International Convention for the Protection of New Varieties of Plants (UPOV), signed in 1961 and in force in 1968, protects the rights of breeders over bred new varieties but not germplasm itself; the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture, adopted in 2001, established a multilateral system for access and benefit sharing for the genetic resources of the main crops and recognizes the contribution of farmers in conserving genetic resources.

There are two ways to conserve germplasm. *In situ* conservation protects wild relatives in their original habitats (such as reserves for wild rice and wild soybean) or has farmers continue growing landraces so that they can keep evolving; *ex situ* conservation keeps seeds, plants or tissues in gene banks, field gene banks and test tubes. Gene banks mainly store orthodox seeds: following the Ellis–Roberts equation for seed longevity ([7.12.19](/7.12.19#sec-19.4.2), [7.13.20](/7.13.20#sec-20.1.2)), seeds are dried to a water content of about 3%–7% and stored sealed at −18 °C, with a longevity of decades to more than a century, but viability must still be tested periodically and the seeds regenerated. Recalcitrant seeds that cannot be dried (many tropical fruit trees) and vegetatively propagated crops (potato, sweet potato, fruit trees) are kept in field gene banks or cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen as shoot tips or embryos ([7.13.29](/7.13.29)).

The number of crop gene banks in the world and the amount of material they hold have varied greatly over time. Statistics from the 1990s counted more than 1300 gene banks holding about 6.1 million accessions, with the United States (about 430,000), the Soviet Union (about 320,000) and China (about 300,000) the largest; a 2010 report of the Food and Agriculture Organization counted about 1750 gene banks and 7.4 million accessions (including many duplicates). China established the Institute of Crop Germplasm Resources of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences in 1978, and the National Crop Genebank now holds more than 500,000 accessions. The Svalbard Global Seed Vault, built in 2008 in the permafrost of the Norwegian island of Spitsbergen, provides safety backup for the gene banks of countries and holds more than a million samples.

## 28.10. The Development of Breeding: From 1.0 to 4.0

The history of crop breeding is often summarized in four stages:

| Stage | Period | Main methods | Basis of selection | Typical achievements |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Breeding 1.0 | About 10,000 years ago to the nineteenth century | Domestication, farmers' selection of seed | Phenotype, by experience | Cultivated plants and landraces |
| Breeding 2.0 | From the early twentieth century | Systematic selection, cross breeding, **mutation breeding**; statistics of field trials and quantitative genetics | Phenotype and pedigree, statistical design | Hybrid maize, semidwarf wheat and rice, hybrid rice |
| Breeding 3.0 | From the 1980s | Marker-assisted selection, transgenesis | Genotype (markers) and phenotype | Insect-resistant cotton, herbicide-tolerant soybean, submergence-tolerant rice with introduced *Sub1* |
| Breeding 4.0 | From the 2010s | Genomic selection, **genome editing**, synthetic biology, high-throughput phenotyping, artificial intelligence | Genome-wide information and prediction models | Edited disease-resistant and high-quality cultivars; de novo domestication |

The progress of breeding 2.0 was to turn breeding into a science that could be planned: the rediscovery of Mendel's laws made the segregation of hybrid progeny predictable, randomized blocks and analysis of variance made cultivar comparisons reliable ([3.3.14](/3.3.14)), and quantitative genetics made the response to selection computable (Eq. 28.1). Its limitation is the long cycle: from cross to cultivar registration generally takes eight to ten years or more, and selection is inefficient for traits of low heritability.

The core of breeding 3.0 is *marker-assisted selection* (MAS): using DNA markers tightly linked to or inside the target gene to identify the genotype at the seedling or even seed stage, instead of time-consuming phenotyping that is affected by the environment. It is especially effective in backcross conversion: foreground selection can be made for the target gene and background selection for the genetic background, speeding the recovery of the recurrent parent genome. The submergence-tolerance locus *Sub1* was introduced into the main cultivars of South Asia in just this way within three or four backcrosses ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)). Transgenesis allows breeding to use genes from any species ([7.13.29](/7.13.29)).

In breeding 4.0, genomic selection uses markers covering the whole genome to estimate the breeding value of each individual without first finding specific genes, and suits yield traits controlled by many genes of small effect ([7.4.25](/7.4.25)); genome editing can "write" a known domestication or elite allele directly into a cultivar without bringing along linked undesirable segments ([7.2.24](/7.2.24)). Most of these methods work by shortening the generation interval $L$ in Eq. (28.1) or by raising the accuracy of selection (equivalent to raising $h$). *Speed breeding* shortens $L$ from the standpoint of plant physiology: under about 22 h of light per day and controlled temperature, flowering of long-day plants such as wheat, barley and pea is greatly advanced, and 4–6 generations can be raised per year, compared with 2–3 in a conventional greenhouse (Watson et al. 2018; photoperiodic induction of flowering is in [7.13.21](/7.13.21)); combined with early harvest and germination of immature seeds, this greatly accelerates the development of inbred lines.

**Example 28.10.1** For a wheat trait, $h^2 = 0.25$ and $\sigma_P = 4$ (units omitted), and the best 10% are selected ($i = 1.75$). Compare the annual response of conventional breeding ($L = 1\ \mathrm{a}$) with speed breeding ($L = 0.2\ \mathrm{a}$, the same $i$, but with heritability reduced to 0.15 because evaluation is done in a controlled-environment chamber).

**Solution** By Eq. (28.1), $R = i h^2\sigma_P$. Conventional: $R = 1.75 \times 0.25 \times 4 = 1.75$ per year; speed breeding: $R = 1.75 \times 0.15 \times 4 = 1.05$ per generation, so $1.05/0.2 = 5.25$ per year, three times that of conventional breeding. Evidently the gain from shortening the generation interval often exceeds the loss from reduced selection accuracy; in practice speed breeding is mostly used for rapid homozygosity and backcrossing, and yield traits still need to be evaluated in the field.

The bottlenecks of conventional breeding are reduced genetic diversity, dependence on experience, long cycles and difficulty in obtaining breakthrough cultivars quickly; yet humanity's food problem has only grown: abnormal climate and frequent pests and diseases, shrinking arable land, a world population approaching ten billion by 2050, and an environmental cost of pesticides and fertilizers that can no longer be increased. This is the question that breeding 4.0 has to answer.

## 28.11. New Crops and De Novo Domestication

By how early they became cultivated plants, cultivated plants can be divided into four classes: ancient cultivated plants (rice, wheat, maize, foxtail millet, persimmon, Chinese cabbage, tea and others, domesticated several thousand years ago); secondary cultivated plants (rye, oat, rape, pea and others, at first weeds in the fields of ancient crops, which became secondary crops when the ancient crops spread to new environments such as the north); cultivated plants of modern origin (domesticated in recent centuries); and cultivated plants now being domesticated.

There are many examples of cultivated plants of modern origin. The rubber of the Pará rubber tree (*Hevea brasiliensis*) was originally tapped from wild trees in the Amazon; demand rose sharply with vulcanized rubber in the nineteenth century and the later development of the automobile industry, and in the 1870s the British took the seeds to Kew Gardens in London and then introduced the seedlings to Southeast Asia, after which Malaysia and other places became the largest producers of rubber. Seeds of the Chinese gooseberry (*Actinidia chinensis*) were taken from Yichang, Hubei to New Zealand at the beginning of the twentieth century, where it was bred into a commercial fruit and sold throughout the world as the kiwifruit. When tomato reached Europe in the sixteenth century it was first an ornamental, and it became a common vegetable only in the eighteenth century.

Plants now being domesticated include grain amaranth (*Amaranthus*, a C4 plant, with 16%–19% protein in the grain, about twice that of rice, and tolerant of drought, poor soil and salinity), stevia (*Stevia rebaudiana*, whose steviol glycosides in the leaves are about 200–300 times as sweet as sucrose and are used as a low-calorie sweetener), plants used as biomass energy sources, and perennial cereals (such as Kernza, domesticated from intermediate wheatgrass *Thinopyrum intermedium*, whose perennial habit can reduce tillage and soil erosion). By traditional methods the domestication of these plants takes decades.

**Definition 28.11.1 (De Novo Domestication)** The use of knowledge of domestication genes, with techniques such as genome editing, to introduce mutations for a few key domestication traits directly into wild (or semidomesticated) plants and obtain within a few generations a new crop with cultivated traits is called **de novo domestication**.

The idea of de novo domestication is that the genetic basis of domestication traits is mostly loss of function in a few genes (Section 28.1.3), whereas wild plants retain disease resistance, stress tolerance and other traits that were lost from crops in the domestication bottleneck. In 2018 several groups edited, in currant tomato, genes controlling plant architecture (*SP*), fruit number and size (*CLV3*, *WUS*, *FW2.2*, *MULT*), fruit shape (*OVATE*) and vitamin C content (*GGP1*) simultaneously, and in one generation obtained compact plants with fruit several times larger and lycopene content higher than in cultivated tomato, while retaining the stress tolerance of the wild species; similar work was done in the same year in groundcherry (*Physalis*). In 2021 a Chinese group took the allotetraploid tall wild rice (*Oryza alta*, CCDD) as the object, established tissue culture and genetic transformation systems, edited homologous domestication genes for shattering, awn, grain length, plant height and heading date, and proposed domesticating it into a new tetraploid crop with large biomass and stress tolerance.

The prerequisites of de novo domestication are a genome sequence of the target species, an efficient genetic transformation and regeneration system ([7.13.29](/7.13.29)), and knowledge of domestication genes, the last being exactly what the earlier sections of this chapter have summarized. It also has limitations: many domestication traits are quantitative and controlled by many genes of small effect, and editing a few major genes yields only "semidomesticated" plants; a new crop must also undergo long-term evaluation of yield, quality and safety, and corresponding cultivation and processing systems must be established.

## Questions

1. Using the approach of Eq. (28.2), estimate: a recessive non-shattering allele has initial frequency $q_0 = 0.01$ and harvest selection advantage $s = 0.03$. (a) In a completely selfing population ($F = 1$), how many generations are needed for its frequency to reach 0.99? (b) In a completely outcrossing population ($F = 0$), about how many generations are needed for the frequency to rise from 0.01 to 0.02? Explain from this the effect of mating system on the speed of domestication.

   **Answer** (a) For $F = 1$ the population consists of homozygous lines, and the ratio of $aa$ to $AA$ is multiplied by $1 + s$ each generation, so $t = \ln\bigl[(0.99/0.01)/(0.01/0.99)\bigr]/\ln 1.03 = \ln 9801/0.02956 = 9.19/0.02956 \approx 311$ generations. (b) For $F = 0$ and very small $q$, $\Delta q \approx sq^2$, that is, $\mathrm{d}q/\mathrm{d}t = sq^2$, and integration gives $t = (1/q_0 - 1/q_t)/s = (100 - 50)/0.03 \approx 1700$ generations; merely doubling the frequency takes more than a thousand years (consistent with panel A of the figure). In an outcrossing population nearly all rare recessive mutations are in the heterozygous state, do not show the trait, and selection has nothing to act on; selfing makes them appear quickly as homozygotes. The domestication alleles of selfing crops such as rice, wheat and barley can therefore spread relatively fast, whereas for outcrossing maize the domestication alleles are mostly dominant or partially dominant regulatory mutations (such as the high-expression allele of *tb1*), which agrees with this prediction.

2. In a three-line hybrid rice combination, the male-sterile line yields $5.5$, the restorer line $6.5$ and the F₁ hybrid $8.0\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$. (a) Calculate the mid-parent and better-parent heterosis. (b) If a farmer sows seed harvested from this hybrid rice for another season, what are the expected yield and the behavior of the population? (c) If the ratio of rows of male-sterile to restorer line in the seed production field is 2 : 1 (by area), the restorer line serves only as male parent, the outcrossing seed set of the male-sterile line is 40%, and the male-sterile rows would yield $6\ \mathrm{t}$ per hectare at normal seed set, how much hybrid seed can be harvested per hectare of seed production field?

   **Answer** (a) $\mathrm{MP} = 6.0$, $H_{\mathrm{MP}} = 2.0/6.0 = 33\%$; $H_{\mathrm{BP}} = 1.5/6.5 = 23\%$. (b) On average $F_2$ retains only half of the heterosis, with an expected yield of about $6.0 + 1.0 = 7.0\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$; in addition, about a quarter of the plants in the $F_2$ are $S(rf\,rf)$ and male sterile, so seed set falls, and plant height and growth duration also segregate, so the actual yield is lower still; hybrid rice must therefore be bought as new seed every season. (c) The male-sterile line occupies 2/3 of the area and yields $\tfrac23 \times 6 \times 0.40 = 1.6\ \mathrm{t}$ of hybrid seed; field planting uses about $15$–$22\ \mathrm{kg}$ of seed per hectare, so one hectare of seed production field can supply about 70–100 hectares of field. Raising the outcrossing seed set (synchrony of flowering, GA₃ sprays, pollen driving) is the key to lowering the cost of seed production.

3. Using $N$/$S$ and $Rf$/$rf$, write the genotypes and fertility of the progeny of the following crosses: (a) male-sterile line × maintainer line; (b) male-sterile line × a cultivar of genotype $N(Rf\,rf)$; (c) an $S(Rf\,Rf)$ restorer line × maintainer line (restorer line as the female parent). Why must the male-sterile line be multiplied with the maintainer line, and not with a restorer line or any ordinary cultivar?

   **Answer** (a) $S(rf\,rf) \times N(rf\,rf) \to S(rf\,rf)$, all sterile, which maintains the male-sterile line. (b) $S(rf\,rf) \times N(Rf\,rf) \to \tfrac12 S(Rf\,rf)$ (fertile) $+ \tfrac12 S(rf\,rf)$ (sterile), so fertility segregates; such a cultivar can serve neither as maintainer nor as restorer. (c) $S(Rf\,Rf) \times N(rf\,rf) \to S(Rf\,rf)$, fertile (the cytoplasm comes from the female parent $S$, but there is one restorer gene). The cytoplasm of the progeny always comes from the female parent, and all progeny of the male-sterile line carry $S$ cytoplasm; only a male parent with all $rf$ in the nucleus leaves the progeny sterile, and such a male parent itself must carry $N$ cytoplasm to be fertile, which is exactly the definition of the maintainer line $N(rf\,rf)$. If the restorer line or an ordinary cultivar containing $Rf$ is used for pollination, fertility is restored or segregates in the progeny, and the male-sterile line is lost.

4. A tall rice cultivar is $1.5\ \mathrm{m}$ high and a semidwarf cultivar $0.9\ \mathrm{m}$; at maturity each panicle with the upper stem and leaves weighs $0.05\ \mathrm{N}$ (tall) and $0.07\ \mathrm{N}$ (semidwarf, with a larger panicle). When the stem leans $20^\circ$ in the wind, what is the bending moment at the stem base of each? Combined with the harvest index, explain why Green Revolution cultivars can tolerate more nitrogen fertilizer.

   **Answer** By Eq. (28.4), for the tall cultivar $M = 0.05 \times 1.5 \times \sin20^\circ = 0.05 \times 1.5 \times 0.342 = 0.026\ \mathrm{N\,m}$; for the semidwarf $M = 0.07 \times 0.9 \times 0.342 = 0.022\ \mathrm{N\,m}$. The panicle weight of the semidwarf cultivar is 40% greater, yet the bending moment at the base is about 15% lower; its basal internodes are also usually thicker with thicker walls and so have a greater breaking moment, so it lodges less readily. Nitrogen application makes the tall cultivar taller and its panicle heavier, the bending moment rises with the product of the two, and after lodging grain filling is impeded, so the added nitrogen cannot be converted into yield; the stem elongation of the semidwarf cultivar is limited by the gibberellin pathway ([7.13.14](/7.13.14)), the added nitrogen is used mainly to increase panicle and grain number, the harvest index stays at about 0.5, and yield continues to rise with nitrogen rate to a higher level.

5. (Open question) Suppose a salt-tolerant, disease-resistant wild grass is to be domesticated de novo into a food crop. Based on this chapter, list the kinds of traits and candidate genes that you would edit first, explain the physiological basis of each, and discuss the limitations of this approach.

   **Answer** Key points: (1) Shattering: edit homologs of *SH4* and *qSH1* to suppress the differentiation of the abscission zone at the spikelet base (Sections 28.3.1, 28.5.3). (2) Dormancy and uniform germination: reduce ABA synthesis or sensitivity in the seed, or edit *Rc*-like genes involved in seed coat proanthocyanidins, taking care to retain moderate dormancy to avoid preharvest sprouting (Section 28.3.2). (3) Plant architecture: edit homologs of *PROG1* and *tb1* to make the plant upright with moderate tillering, and if necessary edit gibberellin biosynthesis genes (*sd1*-like) to obtain a semidwarf (Sections 28.3.4, 28.6). (4) Grain size and number: edit homologs of *GS3*, *GW2* and *Gn1a* to enlarge sink capacity (Section 28.3.5). (5) Flowering time: edit photoperiod genes of the *Hd1* and *Ghd7* type so that the plant suits the daylength of the target region (Section 28.3.3). (6) Removal of awns and toxic components. The prerequisites are a genome sequence of the species and a genetic transformation and regeneration system ([7.13.29](/7.13.29)); for a polyploid, several homeologous copies must be edited together. Limitations: yield is a quantitative trait controlled by many genes of small effect, so editing a few major genes yields only a semidomesticated plant that still requires long-term selection in the field (Eq. 28.1); the genetic diversity of the edited wild species is limited and crossing with other populations may be needed; and the quality, nutrition and safety of the new crop must be evaluated, cultivation and processing systems established, and the regulations for genome-edited crops satisfied.
